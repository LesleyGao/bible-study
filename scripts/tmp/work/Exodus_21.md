# Exodus 21: An Eye for an Eye

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Exodus 21 opens the *sēper habbĕrît*, the "Book of the Covenant" (named from its later reference in Exodus 24:7), which runs from 20:22 through 23:33 and constitutes the oldest substantial law collection in the Hebrew Bible. Most scholars, including those skeptical of early dating elsewhere in the Pentateuch, agree that the Covenant Code preserves genuinely archaic legal material. Albrecht Alt's *Origins of Israelite Law* (1934) is the foundational form-critical study; its distinction between apodictic and casuistic law, while contested in detail, remains the starting point. The Covenant Code is predominantly casuistic, using the *kî/ʾim* protasis ("when… if… then") conditional form that is the classical ANE legal vocabulary.

The material-cultural context is dense with parallels. The *Code of Hammurabi* (c. 1750 BC), the *Laws of Eshnunna* (c. 1770 BC), the *Laws of Lipit-Ishtar* (c. 1930 BC), the *Laws of Ur-Nammu* (c. 2100 BC), the *Hittite Laws* (fourteenth–thirteenth c. BC), and the *Middle Assyrian Laws* (c. 1076 BC) all share formal features and often treat the same cases. The Exodus 21 ox laws (vv. 28–36) have their closest parallel in Hammurabi §§ 250–252 and *Laws of Eshnunna* §§ 53–55. The slave-release laws (vv. 2–11) parallel *Laws of Hammurabi* §§ 117–119 and *Laws of Eshnunna* § 23. The striking of a pregnant woman (vv. 22–25) is paralleled in *Laws of Hammurabi* §§ 209–214, *Middle Assyrian Laws* A §§ 21, 50–52, and *Hittite Laws* §§ 17–18. These parallels are not coincidence: the Covenant Code draws on the shared legal culture of the ANE, while modifying it in theologically significant ways. David Wright's *Inventing God's Law* argues, controversially, for direct literary dependence on Hammurabi; most scholars argue for shared legal tradition rather than textual borrowing.

What distinguishes the Israelite version is not the form but the theology. Moshe Greenberg's classic 1960 essay "Some Postulates of Biblical Criminal Law" identified three differentiating principles: (1) the incommensurability of human life and property (biblical law never authorizes the death penalty for property crimes, whereas Hammurabi frequently does); (2) the individual responsibility of the offender (biblical law does not permit vicarious punishment of children for parents' crimes in property cases, whereas Hammurabi §§ 229–230 famously puts the builder's son to death for a collapsed house that killed the owner's son); and (3) the principle that the human person is made in the image of God (Gen 9:6), which elevates human life above property in a way ANE law generally does not. Jacob Milgrom extended these principles into detailed readings of the slave laws. Moshe Weinfeld in *The Place of the Law in the Religion of Ancient Israel* refined the thesis. Critics — Raymond Westbrook in particular — have argued that the differences are smaller than Greenberg claimed and that the ANE codes also contain humanitarian principles; the debate continues in the pages of *Biblica*, *JBL*, and the *Zeitschrift für die Alttestamentliche Wissenschaft*.

The lex talionis ("life for life, eye for eye," vv. 23–25) is the most famous and most misunderstood formula. Its ANE context is retaliatory limitation: in a blood-feud culture, the principle "one life for one life" *restricts* vengeance rather than authorizing it. Hammurabi §§ 196–201 apply talionic principle to members of the same social class (noble-to-noble) while commuting to monetary damages for cross-class offenses (noble-against-commoner). The biblical formula applies to all free members of the covenant community, which is a flattening of the social hierarchy — though as Greenberg and Milgrom have emphasized, it still leaves the slave in a distinct category (vv. 20–21, 26–27, 32). Jewish tradition, uniformly from the Mekhilta onward, reads the formula as mandating monetary compensation, not literal mutilation — a reading defended on philological, legal, and moral grounds in Bavli Bava Kamma 83b–84a.

Archaeological and textual context: the Covenant Code is preserved with high textual stability in MT, SP, and LXX, with only minor variants. 4QpaleoExod-m has expansive readings elsewhere in Exodus but tracks the Covenant Code closely. The *Nash Papyrus* does not include this material. The *Temple Scroll* (11Q19) legislates on similar topics at greater length. Rabbinic expansions in the Mishnah tractate Bava Kamma ("the first gate," on torts) formalize the Covenant Code's principles into a detailed halakhic system.

On authorship and dating, the classical JEDP reconstruction assigns the Covenant Code to a J/E core with redactional adjustments. Martin Noth treated it as a pre-literary collection inserted into the Sinai narrative. William Propp's Anchor Bible offers the most detailed recent treatment. Bernard Jackson's *Wisdom-Laws* and *Studies in the Semiotics of Biblical Law* argue for a "self-executing" wisdom-law character. Ludger Schwienhorst-Schönberger's and Eckart Otto's studies (in German) have reshaped continental scholarship on the Covenant Code's composition. Most scholars date the Covenant Code's core to the early monarchy or earlier; a minority (Van Seters) argues for exilic composition.

Key Hebrew vocabulary:
- **מִשְׁפָּטִים** (*mišpāṭîm*) — "rules, judgments," the technical term for case law.
- **עֶבֶד עִבְרִי** (*ʿebed ʿibrî*) — "Hebrew slave," a category whose ethnic identification is contested; some scholars (Mendenhall) relate it to the *ʿapiru* of ANE texts.
- **עַיִן תַּחַת עַיִן** (*ʿayin taḥat ʿayin*) — "eye for an eye," the classical lex talionis formula.
- **רָצוֹן** / **רָצַח** (*rāṣaḥ*) — intentional killing, which 21:12 distinguishes from the accidental killing in v. 13.
- **מִקְלָט** — implied in v. 13, the "place" of refuge, formalized in Num 35 and Deut 19.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Exodus 21 is casuistic law, composed in the classic ANE legal form: conditional protasis ("when/if…") followed by prescriptive apodosis ("then…"). Alt's distinction between this casuistic style and the apodictic Decalogue is the structuring observation of modern form criticism. The Covenant Code's law is "case law": it presents hypothetical cases and prescribes appropriate responses, by which judges are to reason analogically to novel situations. This form is diagnostic of a legal culture in which case-by-case reasoning rather than statutory deduction drives adjudication.

Structurally, Exodus 21 opens with the slave laws (vv. 2–11) as its programmatic opening. The placement is significant. Israel has just been redeemed from slavery; the first law addresses what kind of slavery Israel will accept among its own. The text thus implicitly acknowledges that slavery persists — and works to humanize it, limit it, and orient it toward emancipation. The six-year limit on male Hebrew servitude (v. 2), with release "for nothing" in the seventh year, enacts the sabbatical principle. The female-slave laws (vv. 7–11) address concubinage, providing protections that are revolutionary in ANE context (though inadequate by modern standards; the ethical tension is real). Milgrom and Chris Wright have argued that the Covenant Code regulates existing institutions while reshaping them — a pastoral realism that modern ethics must contextualize rather than retrofit.

The capital offenses (vv. 12–17) are listed in apodictic form, embedded within casuistic context. The four capital offenses named — intentional homicide, striking parents, kidnapping, and cursing parents — share a common theme: violations of the foundational social bonds (the body, the parent, the free person, the honored parent). The insertion of a refuge provision (v. 13) for unintentional homicide establishes the legal distinction between intentional and accidental killing, a distinction that will be formalized in the cities-of-refuge legislation in Num 35 and Deut 19. This is one of the Covenant Code's most important contributions to legal history: the formal recognition of intent as a legal category.

The personal-injury laws (vv. 18–27) culminate in the famous lex talionis. The structure is notable: the first injury case (vv. 18–19) treats free men in a fight, with compensation for lost time and medical costs; then slave injuries (vv. 20–21, 26–27); then the pregnant-woman case (vv. 22–25) with the talionic formula; then the ox laws (vv. 28–36). The talionic formula is positioned between cases involving a pregnant woman and cases involving livestock — a placement that has generated substantial debate about scope. Is the talionic principle general (applicable to all injuries) or specific (to the pregnant-woman case)? Jewish tradition uniformly generalizes the principle but monetizes its application.

The ox laws (vv. 28–36) display the Covenant Code's logical structure. A goring ox is treated differently depending on whether its owner knew of its aggressive history (the *shor muʿad* of rabbinic tradition). Negligence is a distinct legal category: the warned owner who fails to restrain his beast is held to a different standard than the owner whose ox suddenly behaves violently. The introduction of the doctrine of prior warning (vv. 29, 36) — what Anglo-American common law calls "scienter" — is one of the world's earliest articulations of the negligence principle. Raymond Westbrook's *Studies in Biblical and Cuneiform Law* traces the principle across ANE codes and argues for a shared legal culture.

Textual variants are minor. The LXX reads the pregnant-woman case (vv. 22–23) with a participial phrase *exeikonismenon* ("formed") that distinguishes a "formed" from an "unformed" fetus, leading to a distinction with substantial later theological consequences (Augustine, Aquinas, early Christian abortion ethics). The MT is more ambiguous, using the phrase "her children come out" with "no harm" or "harm." The difference between a "formed" reading and an "unformed" reading structures two millennia of Christian moral debate about abortion. The Samaritan Pentateuch agrees with MT. The Peshitta renders with yet another interpretive gloss.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Covenant Code is the first canonical legal deposit of Israel. Its place in the canonical story is programmatic: Israel has been redeemed, has received the Decalogue's constitutional core, and now receives detailed case law by which the covenantal community will be governed. Every subsequent legal collection in the Pentateuch — the Holiness Code (Leviticus 17–26), the Priestly Code (largely in Leviticus 1–16 and Numbers), the Deuteronomic Code (Deuteronomy 12–26) — stands in some relation to the Covenant Code, whether as supplement, expansion, or reform.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** Second Temple treatments include Philo's *De Specialibus Legibus* 1–4, which organizes Mosaic law under the ten commandments and draws extensively on the Covenant Code material. Josephus's *Antiquities* 4.198–302 presents Mosaic law topically. *Jubilees* 49–50 expands the Sabbath and festival laws. The *Temple Scroll* (11Q19) rewrites the Covenant Code in expanded form. Qumran sectarian legal texts (*Damascus Document*, *4QMMT*) engage the Covenant Code's slave and injury laws.

Rabbinic tradition made the Covenant Code the foundation of the halakhic system. The Mishnah tractate *Bava Kamma* ("First Gate"), *Bava Metziʿa* ("Middle Gate"), and *Bava Batra* ("Last Gate") together develop a comprehensive civil law from the seeds planted in Exodus 21–23. The Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael's *Mishpatim* section is a sustained commentary on Exodus 21. Bavli Bava Kamma 83b–84a settles the talionic formula as monetary: "What is the reason? It is taught, 'an eye for an eye' — could this mean a real eye? If a blind man blinded his neighbor, what would you do to him? If someone with one eye blinded another, could you take his only eye? Therefore the Torah teaches monetary compensation." The argument is philological and moral, and it shaped Jewish halakhic practice continuously from the rabbinic period.

Maimonides in *Mishneh Torah* devoted entire books to the material of Exodus 21: *Hilchot Avadim* on slaves, *Hilchot Chovel u-Mazik* on torts, *Hilchot Nizkei Mamon* on property damage, *Hilchot Rotzeach u-Shmirat ha-Nefesh* on homicide. His codification organizes the Covenant Code's seeds into a comprehensive legal system. Rashi's commentary on Exodus 21 is a primer in rabbinic legal reasoning, line by line. Ibn Ezra offers philological readings. Ramban provides covenantal-theological synthesis. Modern Jewish scholarship on the Covenant Code includes Nahum Sarna's JPS *Exodus*, Moshe Greenberg's seminal essays, Jacob Milgrom's studies, David Weiss Halivni's *Peshat and Derash*, Bernard Jackson's *Wisdom-Laws*, and Jonathan Sacks's *Covenant and Conversation*. The rabbinic trajectory is marked by two signatures: the humanizing interpretation of the slave laws (the Mekhilta's readings push consistently toward emancipation and dignity) and the monetization of lex talionis (which removed the potential for physical retaliation from Jewish legal practice entirely).

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** The Covenant Code's reception in Christian tradition is dominated by the tripartite distinction — moral, judicial, ceremonial — that Aquinas articulates in *Summa Theologiae* I-II qq. 98–105. On this reading, the moral law (the Decalogue) binds universally; the judicial law (the Covenant Code, much of Deuteronomy 12–26) applied to Israel's political life and is instructive but not directly binding on Christians; the ceremonial law (sacrificial regulations, purity codes) is fulfilled in Christ and abrogated. Aquinas treats Exodus 21 primarily under judicial law. Calvin's *Commentary on the Four Last Books of Moses* treats the Covenant Code systematically under the rubric of the Decalogue, using the judicial laws to illuminate the moral meaning of each commandment. Luther's treatments (e.g., *How Christians Should Regard Moses*) treat the judicial law as "the Jews' Sachsenspiegel" — a particular civil code for a particular people, not binding on Christians but instructive as a window onto wisdom.

Patristic treatment of Exodus 21 was less systematic. Origen's *Homilies on Exodus* 10–11 allegorizes the slave laws (the slave is the soul, the master is Christ, the awl piercing the ear is hearing the word). Augustine's *Questions on the Heptateuch* 80 addresses the pregnant-woman case at length, drawing on the LXX's "formed/unformed" distinction to argue that the abortion of an "unformed" fetus is punishable by fine rather than by the full lex talionis — a reading that influenced Catholic moral theology well into the medieval period and was only decisively rejected in the nineteenth century. Aquinas in *Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 64 a. 8 engages the pregnant-woman passage in his treatment of homicide. Tertullian in *On the Soul* 37 argues for the full humanity of the embryo from conception.

Reformed and Puritan tradition engaged the Covenant Code in debates over theonomy and the application of judicial law to civil government. The Westminster Confession 19.4 stated that the judicial laws of the Old Covenant "have all expired, together with the State of that people; not obliging any other now, further than the general equity thereof may require." The phrase "general equity" became the hinge of subsequent Reformed political theology: which principles of the judicial law are general and binding, and which are specific to Israel's particular situation? Contemporary theonomists (Rushdoony's *The Institutes of Biblical Law*, Bahnsen's *Theonomy in Christian Ethics*) press toward a more comprehensive application; mainstream Reformed theology (Horton, VanDrunen) keeps the judicial specificity of Israel's law in focus while extracting general principles.

Modern critical scholarship: Mendenhall's *Law and Covenant in Israel and the Ancient Near East* (1954), Weinfeld's *Deuteronomy* (1972) and *The Place of the Law* (2004), Childs's *Exodus* (1974), Dozeman's *Exodus* (2009), Houtman's three-volume *Exodus* (HCOT, 1993–2000), Durham's *Exodus* (WBC, 1987), Propp's Anchor Bible *Exodus 19–40* (2006). Christopher J. H. Wright's *Old Testament Ethics for the People of God* (2004) is the standard evangelical ethical treatment of the Covenant Code, arguing for a "paradigmatic" application in which Israel's laws instruct Christian ethics by exhibiting God's character and intentions rather than by direct transfer.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Jewish tradition has treated the Covenant Code as binding and has developed it through halakhic reasoning into a complete civil and criminal law. Christian tradition has treated it as paradigmatic but not directly binding, with its principles abstracted through the moral-judicial-ceremonial distinction and the "general equity" clause. Both traditions converge on the text's moral seriousness and on the humanizing trajectory of its slave laws; they diverge on the question of continuing legal force. Critical scholarship has contextualized the Code in ANE legal culture, partly relativizing claims of unique revelation while also sharpening appreciation for the distinctive theological moves the text makes.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The slave laws — moral scandal or moral progress?** Exodus 21:2–11 and 20–21 regulate slavery rather than abolishing it. Is the Covenant Code's toleration of slavery a moral failure of the revelation, or a pastoral accommodation that contains the seeds of its own overcoming?

- **Catholic (Aquinas, ST II-II q. 57 a. 3; CCC 2414):** Slavery is contrary to natural law in its modern chattel form, though the ancient institution was regulated as a form of labor contract. The Covenant Code's regulation reflects the general character of ANE legal culture; its redemptive trajectory points toward the eventual abolition that Christian civilization has accomplished.
- **Reformed (Calvin, *Commentary on Exodus*; Westminster Confession 19):** The Covenant Code's slave laws are judicial law specific to Israel's context; their "general equity" principles (limited term, humane treatment, release compensation in Deut 15) retain force. The historic Reformed tolerance of New World slavery was a contextual failure of Christian ethics, not a faithful application of the biblical text — Jonathan Edwards's and Samuel Hopkins's arguments against slavery drew on Reformed principles.
- **Lutheran (Luther, *On Secular Authority*):** The two-kingdoms framing treats slavery as a civil institution whose permissibility depends on civil law, not on direct biblical prescription. Lutheran theology has historically been contextual on this question.
- **Wesleyan (Wesley, *Thoughts Upon Slavery*, 1774):** Wesley's tract is one of the earliest systematic Christian arguments against slavery, arguing that even regulated slavery violates the image of God in every person. The Wesleyan tradition carries an abolitionist inheritance.
- **Anabaptist (Yoder; historic peace churches' abolitionism):** The Sermon on the Mount's ethic relativizes the Covenant Code; discipleship of Christ excludes holding slaves regardless of what Exodus permits.
- **Jewish (Mekhilta on Ex 21:2; Maimonides *Hilchot Avadim* ch. 9; Sacks):** The slave laws are the beginning of an emancipatory trajectory, not an endorsement of slavery as a permanent institution. The Mekhilta's readings consistently push toward the slave's dignity and release. Halakhic development limited and humanized the institution.
- **Critical (Greenberg, Milgrom, Chris Wright):** The Covenant Code's laws are progressive within ANE context — limiting term, requiring release, protecting from permanent injury — while not transcending their context. The text's theological voice pushes against its own legal form. Brueggemann and Childs both note that the female-slave laws (vv. 7–11) treat women differently from men: the protections in vv. 10–11 (food, clothing, marital rights) are real, and substantively unusual in ANE context, but the woman has no parallel six-year automatic release, and her status as a concubine or daughter-in-law is built into the structure. The Covenant Code thus regulates an institution it does not abolish, and within that regulation maintains a differential between male and female bondservice that later halakhic and Christian moral development has had to work against from the text's own emancipatory premises.

*Steelman the critical concern.* The argument is that the Covenant Code's differential treatment of female slaves is not a minor irregularity to be explained away but a structural feature of the text's legal order. Male slaves are released in the seventh year; female slaves are bound by a different release mechanism tied to designated marital arrangements. The protections offered are real but presuppose the woman has no autonomous legal agency separate from her male owner. The steelman says that any reading of the text as "progressive" must reckon honestly with the fact that it treats women as a different category from men, and that the later Jewish and Christian traditions have had to develop resources from elsewhere in Scripture (the image-of-God theology of Genesis 1, the prophetic critique, Galatians 3:28) to work against the differential the text codifies.

**Where this leaves us:** the slave laws are both moral progress within ANE context and morally inadequate by the standards that later Jewish and Christian ethics would develop. The text contains the seeds of its own critique — the redemption from Egypt as the paradigm of freedom, the image-of-God theology of Gen 1 and 9, the prophetic critique of oppression — but it does not complete the trajectory. Faithful reading must honor both what the text does and what it fails to do.

**Tension 2: The pregnancy injury case (vv. 22–25) and the ethics of abortion.** The case describes two men fighting who strike a pregnant woman; the text prescribes different penalties for "no harm" and "harm." The LXX translates with *exeikonismenon* ("formed"), distinguishing between the abortion of a formed and an unformed fetus; the MT is more ambiguous about whose "harm" is in view (the woman's or the fetus's).

- **Catholic (Aquinas, ST II-II q. 64 a. 8 — who allowed the formed/unformed distinction; CCC 2270–2275; *Donum Vitae*; *Evangelium Vitae*):** The modern magisterium has converged on the position that direct abortion at any stage is the killing of innocent human life. The LXX's formed/unformed distinction, accepted in the medieval period, has been superseded by contemporary embryology and magisterial teaching. Exodus 21:22–25 is read as protecting fetal life at some stage, with the precise stage debated in the tradition.
- **Reformed (Westminster Larger Catechism Q. 135–136; John Frame; Wayne Grudem):** The passage presupposes that fetal life has moral and legal weight, and the MT's ambiguity is usually read as including the fetus within "life for life." Historically, Reformed ethics have opposed abortion on these and related grounds.
- **Lutheran (Missouri Synod statements; Helmut Thielicke):** The passage supports a pro-life reading, with attention to the nuance of degree of protection in the MT.
- **Wesleyan:** Generally pro-life, reading the passage through Wesley's broader valuation of life and natural law.
- **Jewish (Mekhilta; Rashi; Maimonides, *Hilchot Rotzeach* ch. 1):** The passage distinguishes the fetus from full personhood. The rabbinic reading is that "no harm" means harm to the woman — the miscarriage itself is treated as a property/monetary matter, not as homicide. This reading grounds Jewish halakhic positions that generally permit abortion to save the mother's life and may permit it in other specific cases, while prohibiting abortion-on-demand. Heschel and Sacks write within this framework.
- **Critical (Moshe Greenberg):** The MT ambiguity reflects genuine uncertainty about legal status of the fetus in ANE law; comparative study with Hammurabi and Middle Assyrian Laws shows the Covenant Code treats miscarriage as a distinct category from homicide.
- **Anabaptist / consistent life ethic (Hays, *Moral Vision of the New Testament*):** The passage must be read under the NT's ethic of life; Hays argues for a consistent pro-life position that includes opposition to capital punishment and war.

*Steelman the rabbinic Jewish reading.* The rabbinic reading is philologically serious. The MT "her children come out but no harm" most naturally reads the "harm" as pertaining to the woman (whose harm would entail "life for life" if she died). The fetus's loss is treated as a monetary matter. This is not a low view of fetal life; it is a legal distinction between the fetus and the full person. Rabbinic law takes fetal life seriously (permitting abortion only in narrowly defined circumstances) but draws the line of homicide at birth. The steelman: anyone reading the text in Hebrew, without the later Christian theological frame, is likely to arrive at something like the rabbinic reading on grounds of grammar and context.

**Where this leaves us:** the text distinguishes fetal from full-person status legally but does not devalue fetal life. Subsequent traditions have read the distinction differently based on broader theological commitments: Catholic and Reformed traditions have tended to read the passage as establishing fetal protection that approaches or equals full protection; Jewish tradition has read it as distinguishing fetal from full protection while still valuing fetal life. The disagreement is irreducible absent agreement on whether the decisive moral line is at conception, formation, viability, or birth.

**Unresolved question:** If the Covenant Code regulates slavery while the exodus narrative presupposes slavery's wrongness, what does the tension tell us about the relation between law (which accommodates fallen structures) and gospel (which transforms them)? The question is old — it shapes Jewish debates over halakhic development, Christian debates over judicial law's binding force, and contemporary debates over how biblical ethics should engage structural injustice — and it has not been settled.

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references tie Exodus 21 to the wider canon and to later ethics.

*First,* the slave-release law of Ex 21:2 ("six years… in the seventh he shall go out free") is the first instance of the sabbatical-release principle that will be elaborated in Lev 25 (the seventh year for the land, the fiftieth year for the jubilee) and applied prophetically in Jer 34:8–22 (where Zedekiah's failed release is cited as grounds for divine judgment). Jesus's inaugural sermon in Luke 4:18–19 — "to proclaim liberty to the captives" — quotes Isa 61:1–2 and through it invokes the jubilee tradition, which stands in direct continuity with Exodus 21's emancipatory thread. The passage is thus the root of the biblical theology of liberty, which flowers in the prophets and in the messianic proclamation.

*Second,* the doorpost ritual for the slave who chooses to remain (v. 6, "his master shall bring him to God, and he shall bring him to the door or the doorpost, and his master shall bore his ear through with an awl") has striking echoes with the Passover doorpost ritual (Ex 12:7, 22) and with the ear-piercing imagery of Psalm 40:6 ("but you have given me an open ear" — literally "dug through," using a verb cognate to *rāṣaʿ* in Ex 21:6). Hebrews 10:5–7 applies Psalm 40 to Christ's voluntary servitude: Christ is the servant who chooses to remain, ear pierced, committed to the master's house. The typology is not arbitrary; it reads the Exodus 21 ritual as foreshadowing a voluntary, love-motivated bondservanthood that Christian tradition has seen fulfilled in Christ's own incarnation.

*Third,* the lex talionis formula of Ex 21:23–25 is explicitly cited by Jesus in Matt 5:38–42 ("you have heard that it was said, 'an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth.' But I say to you, do not resist the one who is evil"). Crucially, Jesus presupposes the rabbinic interpretive tradition that reads the formula as just compensation rather than literal retaliation, and then intensifies it by calling for forgoing even legitimate compensation. The Sermon on the Mount is not abrogating the Covenant Code; it is pressing the Code's own trajectory (from retribution to compensation to, finally, forgoing one's rights) one step further. This is the interpretive structure that undergirds the Christian doctrine of the Law's fulfillment — not abolition, but teleological completion.
