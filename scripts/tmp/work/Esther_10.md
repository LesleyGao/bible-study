# Esther 10: Three Verses, One Empire, and the Silence as Final Word

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The book's epilogue is three verses long, and every clause is freighted with imperial information. "King Ahasuerus imposed tax on the land and on the coastlands of the sea" (10:1) — the Hebrew *va-yasem ha-melekh Achashverosh mas al ha-aretz ve-iyei ha-yam* (וַיָּשֶׂם הַמֶּלֶךְ אֲחַשְׁוֵרוֹשׁ מַס עַל הָאָרֶץ וְאִיֵּי הַיָּם) is a Persian fiscal-administrative formula whose specifics map cleanly onto Achaemenid practice. *Mas* (מַס) is the corvée or general tribute attested in ancient Near Eastern usage from Joshua 16:10 forward; under the Persian system it referred to provincial tribute payable in coin or kind, the *baji* of Old Persian inscriptions. "The coastlands of the sea" (Heb. *iyei ha-yam*, אִיֵּי הַיָּם) names the Aegean and Mediterranean tributary regions — Ionia, Caria, Lydia, Cyprus, the Phoenician coast — the very regions Xerxes had failed to subjugate fully in his Greek campaign of 480–479 BC. Herodotus 3.89–96 gives the standard Persian satrapal tribute list, with Ionia paying 400 talents annually, Phoenicia and Palestine paying 350 talents, and the *iyei ha-yam* designation grouping the maritime tributaries. The verse's seemingly throwaway opening is a reminder that the Persian fiscal apparatus continued operating regardless of any one provincial drama; the killing of 75,000 in Adar of year twelve did not interrupt the tax cycle of year thirteen.

The reign remains Xerxes I (Heb. *Achashverosh*, אֲחַשְׁוֵרוֹשׁ; Old Persian *Khshayārshā*, 485–465 BC). The historical Xerxes had roughly a decade left after the events of Esther's twelfth year, before his assassination in 465 BC by his courtier Artabanus (Ctesias *Persica* fr. 13; Justin 3.1; Diodorus Siculus 11.69). The chapter does not reach for that ending. Instead, it appeals to imperial archive: "the full account… are they not written in the Book of the Chronicles of the kings of Media and Persia?" (Heb. *sefer divrei ha-yamim le-malkhei Maday u-Pharas*, סֵפֶר דִּבְרֵי הַיָּמִים לְמַלְכֵי מָדַי וּפָרָס, 10:2). This formula matches verbatim the closing notices of 1 and 2 Kings ("Are they not written in the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Israel/Judah?") and points to the Persian royal annals attested by Herodotus 8.85–90, Ctesias's *Persica*, and the Behistun inscription's own self-archival mode. The reference is also a literary inclusio with Esther 6:1, the chapter's structural pivot, where these same chronicles were read in the king's sleepless night. The book that began with a chronicle being read ends by deferring to a chronicle yet unwritten.

The phrase "second in rank to King Ahasuerus" (Heb. *mishneh la-melekh Achashverosh*, מִשְׁנֶה לַמֶּלֶךְ אֲחַשְׁוֵרוֹשׁ, 10:3) names a specific Persian court position. The *hazarapatish* — "chiliarch" or "commander of a thousand," the Greek *chiliarchos* of Aelian *Varia Historia* 1.21 — was the most senior official in the king's personal entourage, and Greek sources name several historical chiliarchs of Xerxes' reign (Aristides *De Quattuor* identifies Mardonius and later figures). Whether Mordecai is described as occupying this exact role or a generalized "second-to-the-king" status (cf. Joseph in Genesis 41:43) is debated. Bickerman, Berlin (JPS), and Bush (WBC) read the formula as the standard ancient Near Eastern designation of a vizier or grand chamberlain; Yamauchi has speculated about the historical *Marduka* of the Persepolis Fortification Tablets, but the identification remains unverifiable.

Authorship is anonymous; the dating is broadly post-Persian, fourth to third century BC. The same scholarly consensus (Bickerman, Moore Anchor, Bush WBC, Fox *Character and Ideology*, Berlin JPS, Levenson OTL) holds. The *mishneh la-melekh* designation, the imperial-tax framing, the chronicle citation, and the closing sentence's careful prose all reflect a writer working within a developed scribal tradition that had inherited both the Deuteronomistic chronicle-formula and Persian administrative idioms. The Late Biblical Hebrew register and Persian loanwords anchor Polzin's and Berlin's linguistic dating.

The textual situation is significantly more complex here than in the preceding chapters. The MT ends at 10:3, with no explicit theology and no naming of God. The LXX's Addition F follows immediately after — a long theological epilogue in which Mordecai recalls a dream from Addition A, interprets it as covenantal-providential, names YHWH explicitly, and dates the Greek translation to "the fourth year of the reign of Ptolemy and Cleopatra" (LXX Esther F:11), which Bickerman dated to 114 BC. Catholic and Orthodox Bibles preserve Addition F; Protestant Bibles, following Jerome's *hebraica veritas* (Vulgate prologue to Esther), end at MT 10:3. The single most consequential textual decision in the entire book is whether to end with the silence of MT 10:3 or with the explicit theological resolution of LXX Addition F.

Key terms: *mas* (מַס, tribute/tax), *iyei ha-yam* (אִיֵּי הַיָּם, "coastlands of the sea"), *mishneh la-melekh* (מִשְׁנֶה לַמֶּלֶךְ, "second to the king"), *shalom* (שָׁלוֹם, "peace/welfare," 10:3 — the book's last word), and *zera Yisrael* (זֶרַע יִשְׂרָאֵל, "seed of Israel," 10:3 — Mordecai's lineage frame).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Esther 10 is a chronicle-style epilogue in three verses — a deliberately compressed and almost dismissive ending, in striking contrast to the elaborate festival institution of chapter 9. The genre is the deuteronomistic-style closing notice familiar from Kings: a brief account of the king's reign, a deferral to the chronicle, and a final assessment of the protagonist. The closest formal parallels are 2 Chronicles 36:22–23 (the chronicler's brief Cyrus epilogue), Nehemiah 13:31, and Daniel 6:28 ("So this Daniel prospered during the reign of Darius and the reign of Cyrus the Persian"). All of these are diaspora power-broker endings, and all have the same elliptical-conclusive register.

The most striking literary feature of Esther 10 is what is *not* there. The book that began with Vashti's deposition, ran through Esther's elevation, the decree of annihilation, the king's sleepless night, the queen's denunciation, Haman's impalement, the counter-decree, the slaughter of 75,000, and the institution of Purim — ends with three verses about taxation and Mordecai's social standing. There is no theophany, no covenantal speech, no thanksgiving psalm, no recapitulation of providence. The leitwort *yhudim* ("Jews") appears twice in the final verse, the leitwort *shalom* appears once at the very end, and the divine name does not appear at all. Beal's *The Book of Hiding* reads this terminal silence as the book's defining theological act: the canonical decision to end without God being named is itself the chapter's content. The Hebrew *baqesh tov le-amo ve-dover shalom le-khol zar'o* (בִּקֵּשׁ טוֹב לְעַמּוֹ וְדֹבֵר שָׁלוֹם לְכָל זַרְעוֹ, "he sought the welfare of his people and spoke peace to all his seed/offspring") closes the book with the language of Jeremiah 29:7's diaspora command — "seek the welfare (*shalom*) of the city" — and inverts the recipient: the welfare sought is now *of his people*, by a Jew who is also second to the king of an empire that nearly destroyed them.

The closing inclusio with chapter 1 is exact and deliberate. The book opened with the imperial reach: "in the days of Ahasuerus, the Ahasuerus who reigned from India to Cush over 127 provinces" (1:1). It closes with the imperial reach unchanged: "King Ahasuerus imposed tax on the land and on the coastlands of the sea" (10:1). The empire continues; the king is the same king; the tax is collected. What has changed is who stands at his right hand. The Persian imperial frame has not been disrupted, only inhabited differently. Berlin and Levenson both read this as the book's deliberate political theology: the diaspora does not end the empire; the diaspora finds Jewish flourishing inside the empire's persistence.

The literary device of *sefer divrei ha-yamim* (10:2) repeats the same chronicle-citation form that punctuates 1 and 2 Kings, but with the difference that the chronicles being deferred to are Persian, not Israelite — the diaspora has no Israelite chronicle to cite, only the imperial archive into which Mordecai's "high honor" has been written. The author is making a precise point about whose archive now carries Jewish memory. The forward reach of this gesture is profound: when later Jewish writers (Josephus, the rabbis, the medieval chroniclers) want to record Jewish history, they will increasingly do so within and against gentile-imperial archives, and Esther 10:2 is the canonical model.

Textual variants are decisive. The MT ends at 10:3 with the *shalom* of communal welfare. LXX Addition F adds 11 verses of explicit theological epilogue: Mordecai recalls his prophetic dream from Addition A, identifies the two dragons of the dream as himself and Haman, identifies the river that became sea-flood as Esther, and names YHWH ("the Lord has done these things… these things have come from God"). Addition F also includes a colophon dating the Greek translation. The Alpha-text gives a different and shorter epilogue. Josephus *Antiquities* 11.295–296 incorporates parts of Addition F's theological frame. The single literary fact is that the most theologically explicit version of Esther's ending — the LXX's Addition F — is the version Christian tradition (Catholic and Orthodox) inherited, and the most theologically silent version — the MT's three verses — is the version Jewish tradition canonized.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Esther 10 sits at the end of the diaspora court tale arc that runs from Joseph (Genesis 50) through Daniel 6:28 and Nehemiah 13:31. Each of these closing notices stages a Jewish-protagonist-as-imperial-official frame: Joseph dies as Pharaoh's vizier, having "made his bones" subject to a future exodus (Gen 50:25); Daniel "prospered during the reign of Darius" (Dan 6:28); Nehemiah ends with the cautious self-vindication "Remember me, O my God, for good" (Neh 13:31). Esther 10:3 closes the genre with a diaspora-flourishing ending that is, alone among them, *non-eschatological* — there is no exodus to come, no divine remembering invoked, no return to the land. The diaspora is the form, and the form is the home.

The Saul/Agag arc that has run through the entire book reaches its quiet completion. The Benjamite descendant of Kish, who would have been the failed king's heir, is not the king he ought to have been — he is the *mishneh la-melekh* of a foreign empire. The completion of the *cherem* (Deut 25:17–19; 1 Sam 15) is achieved not by an Israelite king on Israelite soil but by a Jewish vizier in a Persian capital, and the closing assessment — "he sought the welfare of his people and spoke peace to all his seed" — frames Mordecai as the Saul-redeemed figure who has done what the original Saul could not: protected the people without taking the spoil, completed the obligation against Amalek, and remained alive at the end. Levenson's reading: the book's quiet final note is that the Saul-counterstory has succeeded, but at the cost of accepting that Jewish kingship over Jewish land is no longer the relevant frame.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** Second-Temple readings dramatically expand the chapter. The LXX's Addition F supplies the prophetic-providential epilogue the MT withholds. Josephus *Antiquities* 11.295–296 follows the LXX. Rabbinic engagement (b. Megillah 16b–17a; Esther Rabbah 10:14–15) reads 10:3's closing assessment with celebration: Mordecai is the model diaspora leader, *baqesh tov le-amo* ("seeking good for his people") becomes a halakhic ideal of communal leadership, and the book's lack of explicit divine reference is read as God's hidden hand requiring no naming. Maimonides's *Hilkhot Megillah* 2:18 — that all the prophetic books and the Writings will be annulled in the messianic age except the Megillah of Esther — depends on Esther 10:3's permanent canonicity. Medieval commentators (Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Ralbag, Malbim) read the chapter as the book's deliberate ending in diaspora rather than restoration: Rashi notes that Mordecai did not return to the land, Ibn Ezra emphasizes the *mishneh la-melekh* status as a halakhic precedent for Jewish-gentile political cooperation, Malbim reads *shalom* as the book's defining word. Modern Jewish scholarship: Berg on the inclusio with chapter 1, Fox on the moral ambiguity of an ending without God, Berlin on the Jeremiah 29:7 echo, Levenson on the diaspora's constitutional claim, Hazony's *God and Politics in Esther* on the chapter as the diaspora's permanent political theology, Koller on rabbinic centrality, Niditch on the trickster pattern's resolution, Gruen on diaspora flourishing as a sustainable Jewish form, Crawford on the textual divergences. Elie Wiesel's post-Holocaust readings dwell on the silence: the book ends with peace and welfare, but God is not named — and the post-Holocaust Jewish reader is precisely the one who knows this kind of *shalom*.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** Patristic engagement with chapter 10 specifically is sparse, and most early Christian readers worked from the LXX-with-Additions, where Addition F supplied what the MT withheld. Origen's bilingual editions and Jerome's Vulgate preserved the Additions while marking them as later. Rabanus Maurus's Carolingian *Expositio* allegorizes Mordecai's elevation as the church's eschatological exaltation. Bede and the Glossa Ordinaria continue. Reformation engagement is again absent — Luther's *Table Talk* §475 dismissal; Calvin no commentary. The Council of Trent (1546) reaffirmed the LXX-with-Additions for Catholic use. Lewis Paton (ICC 1908) reads the chapter as anticlimactic — Mordecai "a Jewish official… caring only for his own people." Bickerman's *Four Strange Books* (1967) recovered it for serious engagement. Contemporary readings have substantially shifted: Moore (Anchor) takes the literary art seriously; Bush (WBC) reads the closing as deliberate inclusio; Jobes (NIVAC) and Duguid read the MT silence as providential reticence; Greidanus reads Mordecai's *mishneh la-melekh* status as foreshadowing Christ at the right hand. Brevard Childs's canonical reading (*Introduction to the Old Testament as Scripture*) reads MT 10:3 as the canonical decision: God is not named because the book is teaching that God's presence does not require explicit naming. Timothy Beal and Adele Berlin both note that Esther herself does not appear in chapter 10 — the book that bears her name closes with Mordecai alone, the queen who saved the people written out of its last word.

**Convergence and divergence.** The most theologically explicit version of Esther 10 is the LXX's; the most theologically silent is the MT's. Christian tradition has historically inherited the explicit version and the silent version uncomfortably side by side, with Catholic and Orthodox readers having Addition F as canonical and Protestant readers ending at MT 10:3. Jewish tradition has only ever had the MT silence. The post-*Nostra Aetate* (1965) era has increasingly drawn Christian readers toward the MT silence as the load-bearing version, but the canonical disagreement is itself the most honest interpretive frame.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: diaspora theology — is minority flourishing under foreign empire a sustainable mode of Jewish existence, or a temporary expedient pending eschatological return?** The diaspora reading (Jeremiah 29:7's "seek the welfare of the city," Erich Gruen's *Diaspora*, Yoram Hazony's *God and Politics in Esther*, Levenson's OTL) treats Esther 10:3 as the canonical claim that diaspora can be the permanent home, not exile-as-punishment. Mordecai is *mishneh la-melekh*, not a returnee; he seeks the welfare of his people inside Persian imperial structure, not at the temple in Jerusalem. The Reformed providential reading (Jobes, Duguid) tends to read this as one legitimate mode among others — God's people can be faithfully present in any sphere — without elevating diaspora as normative. The Catholic just-war and natural-law tradition reads Mordecai's vizier-role as an instance of the *participatio* of the believer in legitimate temporal authority, comparable to Christian magistrates serving in Roman or medieval Christian polities. The Anabaptist reading (Yoder's *Politics of Jesus*, *For the Nations*) is most distinctive: Yoder finds in Esther 10 (and Jeremiah 29:7) a positive theology of *Galut* — diaspora as the church's actual condition, not a temporary problem to be solved by Christian political establishment. The Eastern Orthodox tradition has emphasized synodal and imperial co-existence rather than diaspora per se. The Jewish position is itself divided: classical rabbinic Judaism reads Esther 10 as the diaspora's working model, awaiting messianic restoration; modern religious Zionism (Rav Kook, Hazony in his *Philosophy of Hebrew Scripture*) reads the diaspora as instrumentally essential but eschatologically incomplete, with the book's avoidance of return to Yehud a telling silence; secular Zionism reads Esther 10 as the model the diaspora must finally outgrow. The position least native to most contemporary Christian readers is the Yoder-Hazony convergent reading: Esther 10's *shalom*-seeking under empire may be the canonical model not only for Jewish but for Christian existence, and Christian theologies that assume "the Christian sphere" as a normative category may be reading against the canonical grain. Where this leaves us: the chapter's refusal to anticipate restoration is the diaspora's permanent theological signature, and any reading that domesticates Esther 10 into a temporary phase before eschatological homecoming may be missing what the text canonically claims.

**Tension 2: the silence of God — does the absence of the divine name in 10:3 teach that God hides and the faithful must act, that God is genuinely absent and the text refuses theological resolution, or that the canonical decision to include Esther signals God's commitment to be present even in apparently secular chapters of his people's life?** The Hazony-Levenson-Reformed providential position holds that the silence is the chapter's deepest claim about how God works in the diaspora: without prophet, without temple, without theophany, the faithful must read providence in administrative coincidence and political reversal, and the book is teaching the diaspora to read its own life this way. The canonical reading (Brevard Childs, *Introduction to the OT as Scripture*) takes the canonical *decision* to include the MT version as itself a theological act: by canonizing a book in which God is unnamed, the canon is making a claim about the kinds of stories God is willing to be present in. The Catholic LXX-tradition reading does not face the silence at all — Addition F supplies the theological closure, and the canonical Esther for Catholic and Orthodox readers ends with explicit divine reference. The Reformed reading (Jobes, Duguid) accepts the MT's silence but reads it providentially: God is hidden, but unmistakably present in everything but vocabulary. The position least native to most Christian readers, and the one that most rewards steelmanning, is Beal's. *The Book of Hiding* argues that the silence is not coyness or providential reticence but theological *content*: the book is deliberately *post-theological*, in a way Christian readers find hard to sit with. Esther refuses to give the reader the consolation of explicit divine presence; the *shalom* of 10:3 is a *shalom* without God being named, and the canonical decision to include such a book is, on Beal's reading, a permission for readers to live in the same kind of *shalom* — quiet, ordinary, post-religious, without theophany — and to find it canonically permitted. To steelman this fairly: a tradition that includes a book without God in its name is preserving the insight that not all faithful living requires explicit naming of the divine, and that the diaspora — and by extension, the modern reader's ordinary post-religious life — may be canonically validated as a place where faith looks like *shalom*-seeking without theophany. Where this leaves us: the silence of Esther 10 is not the absence of God but the canonical question about what counts as God's presence, and Christian readers who require explicit naming may find that the book has anticipated their requirement and refused it.

**The unresolved question:** if Esther 10 ends without God being named, with a Jewish vizier seeking *shalom* inside a foreign empire that nearly destroyed his people, with a tax being collected on coastlands the empire could not finally subdue — what does that ending say to your own ordinary, post-religious, *shalom*-seeking life, in which prayers are answered or unanswered without theophany, and in which the silence may be the only form God's presence is canonically willing to take?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 50:20–26** — Joseph's diaspora-vizier ending. Joseph names God explicitly ("you meant evil… God meant it for good") and dies awaiting an exodus; Mordecai names no God and seeks no return. The two diaspora endings frame the theological poles the canon holds together.
- **Daniel 6:28** — "So this Daniel prospered during the reign of Darius and the reign of Cyrus the Persian." The closest formal parallel to Esther 10:3, and the inverse: Daniel's God is named and acts overtly, Esther's is unnamed and acts through what looks like coincidence. The two books read each other.
- **Jeremiah 29:7** — "Seek the welfare (*shalom*) of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf." Esther 10:3's "spoke *shalom* to all his people" is Jeremiah 29:7's diaspora command lived out in administrative form. The verbal echo is exact, the recipient inverted, and the prayer-to-the-LORD half is conspicuously not repeated.
- **2 Maccabees 15:36** — Nicanor's Day instituted "the day before Mordecai's Day." The forward reach of Esther 10 into Hasmonean and rabbinic festival time is the evidence that the book's diaspora theology was actually liturgically inhabited; the closing silence of MT 10:3 became the canonical ground for centuries of diaspora *shalom*-seeking, with no eschatological homecoming required to make the book usable.
