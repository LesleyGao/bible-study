# Genesis 1 — The Seven-Day Creation

A first chapter is never really first. By the time Genesis 1 opens with *bereshit*, Israel already had kings, exiles, temples, and hymns. This chapter is doing something deliberate with ancient material — and to read it well, we have to see what it answers.

---

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political situation and compositional setting.** While the *narrative* is set "in the beginning," the *text* in its present form is widely dated by critical scholars to the exilic or early post-exilic period (6th century BC), attributed to the Priestly source (P). This matters enormously. Israel is in Babylon. The temple is rubble. Judeans are surrounded by a Mesopotamian religious imagination in which the world was made through cosmic combat — Marduk slicing Tiamat's corpse in the *Enuma Elish*, humanity fashioned from the blood of the rebel god Kingu to be slave labor for the gods.

Genesis 1 is written into that air. It is not primarily a proto-scientific document; it is a confessional and polemical document composed by priests for a dislocated people who needed to know whose world they were in.

That said, the theological *traditions* behind the chapter are old. The seven-day structure, the Sabbath logic, the creation-by-word motif all have deep roots in earlier Israelite worship. The conservative position (Kenneth Kitchen, Gordon Wenham with qualifications) holds that P preserves ancient liturgical material even if the final redaction is late.

**Material culture.** The cosmology is not modern. The "earth" is a flat disk; above it is a solid *rāqîaʿ* (beaten dome); above the dome, cosmic waters held back; below, the subterranean deep (*tehom*). Sun, moon, and stars are set *in* the dome like lamps on a ceiling. This is the standard Ancient Near Eastern picture, visible also in Egyptian tomb paintings (Nut arching over Geb) and Babylonian astronomical texts. The original audience would have recognized the furniture of the cosmos and noticed how strangely Israel's God moves through it.

**Archaeological and textual evidence.**
- The *Enuma Elish* (7 tablets, recited at the Babylonian New Year festival) is the closest comparative text. Genesis 1 seems to know this kind of material and systematically *demythologizes* it.
- The *Atrahasis Epic* (c. 1700 BC) gives humanity a purpose: to relieve the gods of labor. Genesis inverts this.
- The Memphite Theology (Egyptian, preserved on the Shabaka Stone, c. 700 BC but drawing on older traditions) has Ptah creating by speech — an interesting parallel to *wayyōmer Elohim* ("And God said").
- Ugaritic texts from Ras Shamra (14th century BC) describe Baal's combat with Yamm (Sea) and Lotan (the twisting serpent, cognate to Leviathan). Echoes surface in Psalm 74 and Isaiah 27, but Genesis 1 conspicuously *refuses* combat.
- At Qumran, 4QGen-b preserves an early Hebrew text of Genesis 1 that is largely identical to the Masoretic Text, suggesting remarkable textual stability.

**Key terms.**
- **בְּרֵאשִׁית** *bereshit* ("in the beginning"). Grammatically ambiguous. Rashi and many moderns (including the NRSV footnote) read it as a construct: "When God began to create…" — making verse 2 a parenthesis describing the pre-creation state. The traditional reading ("In the beginning God created…") supports *creatio ex nihilo*; the construct reading leaves the chaotic waters pre-existing. The Hebrew itself does not decide this.
- **תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ** *tohu wa-bohu* ("without form and void"). Not "nothing" but formless, uninhabitable, wild. The same pair describes post-judgment devastation in Jeremiah 4:23. Creation is the ordering of chaos into habitation.
- **תְהוֹם** *tehom* ("the deep"). Cognate with Babylonian *Tiamat*, the chaos-goddess. But notice: in Genesis she is not a character. She is water. This is deliberate deflation.
- **רָקִיעַ** *rāqîaʿ* ("expanse"). From a root meaning "to beat out" (as a metalsmith beats copper). A solid hammered dome. The Vulgate's *firmamentum* captured this; modern translations soften it.
- **צֶלֶם אֱלֹהִים** *tselem Elohim* ("image of God"). *Tselem* elsewhere often means a cultic statue or royal image. The term is drawn from ANE royal ideology (more below).
- **אֱלֹהִים** *Elohim*. Grammatically plural, syntactically singular when used of Israel's God. Verse 26's "let us make" has generated two millennia of debate.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

**Genre.** Genesis 1:1–2:4a is a **liturgical cosmogony** in highly stylized prose — closer to a temple hymn than to narrative. It is not apocalyptic, not myth in the Mesopotamian sense (no divine biography), not scientific report. Claus Westermann called it "priestly prose," structured for recitation.

**Structure.** The architecture is the argument. Days 1–3 *form* (separate and name realms); days 4–6 *fill* those realms.

| Forming | Filling |
|---|---|
| Day 1: light / darkness | Day 4: sun, moon, stars |
| Day 2: sky / sea | Day 5: birds, fish |
| Day 3: land / vegetation | Day 6: animals, humans |

Day 7 stands outside this pattern — it is both climax and interruption.

**Literary devices.**
- **Numerology of seven.** Seven days, but also: the first verse has 7 Hebrew words; verse 2 has 14 (7×2); "God" appears 35 times (7×5); "earth" 21 times (7×3); the seventh-day section has three consecutive seven-word clauses. Umberto Cassuto documented this in the 1940s; it's not speculative. This is sacred geometry in prose.
- **Refrain.** "And God said… and it was so… and God saw that it was good… and there was evening and morning." The repetition is liturgical. It builds toward the breaking of the pattern: humans get extra words, and day 7 gets no "evening and morning" at all.
- **Word-count escalation.** The creation of humans in 1:26–28 uses three successive verbs of creating (*bara*) in verse 27 — the most emphatic concentration in the chapter.
- **Inclusio.** "Heavens and earth" opens (1:1) and closes the unit (2:1).

**Location in the book's argument.** Genesis 1:1–2:3 is one of two creation accounts; 2:4b–25 (the Yahwist/J account) follows immediately and differs substantially in vocabulary, style, order, and theology. The redactor did not harmonize them. Reading them as a layered diptych — cosmic and intimate — is the ancient approach; reading them as a single seamless sequence is modern harmonization.

**Textual variants.** Minor. The Samaritan Pentateuch and the Septuagint have some smoothing (the LXX adds "and it was so" at 1:9 to complete the pattern), but the Hebrew text is remarkably stable. The big interpretive choices happen not at the textual level but at the translational level — especially *bereshit*.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Canonical arc.** Genesis 1 sits at the fountainhead of everything. Creation → fall → covenant → kingdom → exile → return → new creation. Isaiah 65:17 promises "new heavens and a new earth" in language deliberately drawn from Genesis 1:1. Revelation 21 closes the canon by returning to the language of 1:1 and inverting 1:2: no more sea, no more darkness. The Bible's shape is Genesis 1 answered.

### Jewish interpretive trajectory

**Second Temple period.** Already contested. The book of *Jubilees* (2nd century BC) retells Genesis 1 to emphasize the Sabbath and the solar calendar. Philo of Alexandria (1st century AD), reading through Platonic lenses, treats the six days as a logical rather than temporal sequence — God creates all at once, but the narrative unfolds in ordered time so humans can grasp it. Philo also distinguishes the ideal human of Genesis 1 from the earthly human of Genesis 2. The Qumran community (4Q Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice) draws heavily on Genesis 1 for angelology and liturgy.

**Rabbinic period.** *Bereshit Rabbah* (5th century AD) is the great rabbinic reflection. It preserves stunning debates: Rabbi Jonah says God created many worlds and destroyed them before this one; others explore why the Torah begins with *bet* (ב) rather than *alef* (א). The rabbis were remarkably unbothered by multiple creation stories or by the strangeness of "let us make." They read it as God consulting the angels, or as the royal "we," or as a deliberate invitation to humility (so the proud cannot claim God acts alone). The Mishnah (*Chagigah* 2:1) actually *forbids* public speculation about "what is above, what is below, what is before, what is after" — creation theology was considered esoteric.

**Medieval.** Rashi (11th century) famously opens his Torah commentary by arguing *bereshit* is construct: "When God began to create…" He's defending grammar, not doctrine. Maimonides (*Guide of the Perplexed* II.25) argues Aristotelian eternal-matter cosmology *could* be reconciled with Torah, but the plain sense of creation ex nihilo should be preferred — though not because it is philosophically demonstrable. Nachmanides reads the days as containing vast hidden periods, anticipating modern concordist readings by centuries.

**Modern Jewish thought.** Martin Buber and Franz Rosenzweig read Genesis 1 as proclaiming the dialogical structure of reality — creation is God's address. Abraham Joshua Heschel (*The Sabbath*, 1951) sees the chapter as architecture leading to day 7: Judaism builds "a cathedral in time," and Genesis 1 is its blueprint. Jon Levenson (*Creation and the Persistence of Evil*, 1988) crucially argues that biblical creation is *not* ex nihilo in the later philosophical sense — it is the imposition of order on pre-existing chaos, and that chaos is never fully eliminated. Avivah Zornberg (*The Beginning of Desire*) reads the chapter psychoanalytically, attending to what the text represses. James Kugel notes how thoroughly the chapter disenchants Mesopotamian cosmology while keeping its shape.

### Christian interpretive trajectory

**Patristic.** Origen (3rd century) argued the "days" cannot be literal since the sun isn't made until day 4 — they are logical/allegorical. Basil of Caesarea's *Hexaemeron* (9 homilies on the 6 days, 4th century) takes a more literal approach but is rich with science and wonder. Augustine's *De Genesi ad Litteram* spent 15 years on Genesis and concluded the six days represent a single instantaneous creation unfolded pedagogically — closer to Philo than to modern literalists. Augustine *warned* Christians against embarrassing themselves by using Scripture to contradict what pagans knew about nature (*Literal Meaning* I.19) — a passage Galileo would later quote.

**Medieval.** Aquinas (*Summa* I.65–74) carefully distinguishes questions of *faith* (that God created) from questions of *natural philosophy* (how and when) — and holds that Scripture permits multiple readings on the latter.

**Reformation.** Luther rejected Augustine's allegorizing and insisted on six 24-hour days — a significant hardening. Calvin introduced the crucial notion of *accommodation*: God "lisps" to us like a nurse to an infant, using the cosmology his audience understood. This allows Calvin to treat Genesis 1 as theologically true without requiring the dome to be physically real.

**Critical.** Julius Wellhausen (1878) identified P as a late exilic priestly document, distinct from J in Genesis 2. Gerhard von Rad read Genesis 1 as mature theological reflection, not primitive myth. Brevard Childs pushed beyond source criticism toward canonical reading. John Walton (*The Lost World of Genesis One*, 2009) argues Genesis 1 describes *functional* origins — God assigning roles in a cosmic temple — not material origins, reframing the whole science debate.

**Contemporary.** Walter Brueggemann reads Genesis 1 as doxological resistance to empire — the exiles declaring Babylonian chaos is not ultimate. N.T. Wright sees it as temple-cosmology: the world is built as God's dwelling, with humans as image-bearing priests, and he insists "male and female he created them" gives the image to humanity as a whole, not to a privileged subset. Goldingay stresses that the repeated "very good" is a material affirmation — the stuff of the world, not just its spiritual meaning, is blessed.

### Where Jewish and Christian readings converge and diverge

**Converge:** Both traditions read Genesis 1 as polemic against polytheism and as affirming the goodness of material creation. Both largely resist naive literalism in their best interpreters. Both see humans as uniquely dignified.

**Diverge:**
- **"Let us make."** Christian tradition, especially after Nicaea, often reads this Trinitarianly. Jewish tradition universally rejects this reading, finding it a retrojection; the rabbis offer the divine council, royal plural, or angelic consultation. Both readings face textual problems.
- **Creation ex nihilo.** Christian dogmatic tradition hardened this; Jewish tradition is more comfortable with pre-existing chaos (see Levenson).
- **Christological typology.** Christians read the Spirit hovering (1:2), the divine Word creating, the light before the sun, and the seventh-day rest through the lens of Christ, the Logos (John 1), and eschatological Sabbath (Hebrews 4). Jewish readings find these moves unwarranted impositions.
- **The Sabbath.** For Judaism, Genesis 1 climaxes in Genesis 2:1–3 — the Sabbath is the whole point. Most Christian readings treat day 7 as an epilogue.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the priestly author believe God was doing? Three things, interlocked.

**First, God is ordering cosmos into temple.** Recent scholarship (Walton, Beale, Levenson, Wright) has converged on this: the seven-day structure parallels ancient temple-building patterns. Solomon's temple took seven years to build (1 Kings 6:38); its dedication was a seven-day feast; the tabernacle instructions in Exodus come in seven speeches each beginning "And the LORD said to Moses." In ANE thought, the completion of a temple was when the god entered and *rested*. Genesis 1 is saying: the cosmos is God's temple, and he has taken up residence.

**Second, humans are the cult images.** Here Walton, Middleton (*The Liberating Image*), and Wright are persuasive: in the ANE, kings were called "images" of gods; statues of deities were placed in their temples. Genesis 1 does two subversive things — it extends image-bearing to *all* humans (not just kings), and it places image-bearers, not statues, in God's cosmic temple. This is democratizing royal theology and iconoclastic priestly theology in one move.

**Third, rest is the point.** The climax is not humans but Sabbath. God's *menuchah* (rest) is the world's proper end. The Priestly vision is that work serves rest, not the reverse — a direct inversion of Atrahasis, where humans exist to work so gods can rest.

### Theological tension 1: What does "image of God" mean?

- **Substantive view** (dominant in patristic and medieval Christianity — Aquinas, much Protestant tradition): image = some faculty humans possess, especially rationality or soul. Problem: the text never says this.
- **Functional/royal view** (Middleton, Walton, Wright, much current scholarship): image = representative vocation. Humans function as God's royal-priestly stewards. Strongest textual support.
- **Relational view** (Barth, some Catholic personalists): image consists in relationship — "male and female he created them" is immediately juxtaposed. God is self-giving communion; humans image that.
- **Jewish readings** (Maimonides): image is intellect/*tselem* as capacity for God-knowledge. Rashi: the capacity to understand and discern. Levenson: royal-representative, akin to Middleton.
- **Catholic (CCC 355–361):** integrates substantive and relational — rational soul + communion, the foundation of human dignity.

**Steelman the substantive view** (the one I find least persuasive): the functional reading can flatten into mere vocation, losing the *ontological* claim that there is something in human being itself — not just doing — that images God. If image is only function, then humans in persistent vegetative states, or the unborn, or those whose "dominion" capacities are impaired, would seem to image God less. The substantive view protects something the functional view can lose: that image-bearing is a gift prior to capacity.

### Theological tension 2: Creation and violence

Genesis 1 is famously non-violent. No Tiamat sliced in half. No blood of a slain god. Creation is by speech, and it is "good."

But Jon Levenson has pressed the question: is chaos *really* defeated? The waters above are held back by a dome. The *tehom* is pushed aside, not eliminated. In the flood (Genesis 7), the windows of heaven open and the fountains of the deep burst up — chaos returns. Creation in Genesis 1 is an *ongoing* act of restraint, not a completed conquest.

- **Classical Christian view:** creation is complete and very good; evil is a later privation (Augustine). Problem: this struggles with evolutionary death, natural disaster, and the persistence of chaos in the biblical witness itself.
- **Levenson's view:** biblical creation is the establishment of order against chaos that is restrained but not eradicated. Evil is not merely absence but persistent threat. This matches the text better but complicates divine omnipotence.
- **Process theology (Whitehead, Cobb):** God lures creation toward order from eternal chaos. Coheres with Levenson, but most traditions find it heterodox.
- **Orthodox view (Lossky, Bulgakov):** creation is an act of divine *kenosis* (self-limiting); God makes room. Chaos is the "meonic" potential God continually orders.
- **Reformed view (Calvin, later Kuyper):** creation is complete and good; the persistence of chaos reflects the fall, not creation's unfinishedness.

**Where this leaves us:** The text is theologically *pre*-modern. It is neither a scientific account to be reconciled with Big Bang cosmology nor a pure myth to be demythologized away. It is a polemical, liturgical, priestly declaration that *this* world — the one with darkness and deep and wildness in it — belongs to a God who orders by speech, who blesses matter, who makes humans his image, and whose proper end is rest, not work. When this is forgotten, Genesis 1 gets conscripted into battles it was never fighting.

### The unresolved question

If the cosmos is built as God's temple and you are shaped to be its living image, then the question is not whether you believe Genesis 1 — it's whether you can stand inside it. What would it cost you, today, to work as if the seventh day were the point?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 2:4b–25** — the very next unit. A different creation account, different order, different name for God (*YHWH Elohim*), different concerns (soil, loneliness, naming). Read them as a diptych, not a sequence. The redactor wanted both.
- **Exodus 20:8–11** — the Sabbath commandment grounds itself in Genesis 1. The *reason* to rest is that God rested. Genesis 1 is the theological foundation of the Decalogue's fourth word.
- **Psalm 104** — a creation hymn that covers almost the same ground as Genesis 1 but in exuberant poetry, with Leviathan as God's *pet* (v. 26). Notice how differently the same theology sounds in a different genre.
- **John 1:1–5** — "In the beginning was the Word… and the light shines in the darkness." John deliberately writes a new Genesis 1 with Jesus as the creative Word. When you get there, watch how every phrase of Genesis 1 is being rewritten.
- **Revelation 21:1–5** — "A new heaven and a new earth… and the sea was no more." The Bible ends by returning to Genesis 1 and finishing the work: chaos finally eliminated, God finally dwelling with humans as the temple-cosmos was always meant to be.
