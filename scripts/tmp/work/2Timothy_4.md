# 2 Timothy 4: The Cloak, the Parchments, and the Crown

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The final chapter of 2 Timothy is the most concrete chapter in the Pauline corpus. Eleven persons are named in twenty-two verses; an article of clothing, a stack of books, and a winter deadline appear; a city of imprisonment, a place of arrest, and a route of travel are implied. If the letter is genuinely Pauline — and 2 Timothy 4 is the chapter where the case for authenticity hangs heaviest — the historical setting is Rome in the mid-60s during or just after the Neronian persecution Tacitus describes in *Annals* 15.44. Paul writes as a man expecting imminent execution; tradition records his beheading on the Ostian Way under Nero, attested by Eusebius's *Ecclesiastical History* 2.22 (drawing on earlier sources, including Dionysius of Corinth around 170 AD), Tertullian's *Prescription Against Heretics* 36, and the *Liber Pontificalis*. The traditional date is 64-67 AD.

If the letter is pseudonymous, dating to the late first or early second century, the chapter functions as a literary Pauline epitaph constructed to anchor the post-apostolic communities in apostolic authority. P.N. Harrison's *Problem of the Pastoral Epistles* (1921) and James Miller's *The Pastoral Letters as Composite Documents* (1997) take a middle position: this chapter, with its biographical density, is the strongest candidate within the Pastorals for incorporating authentic Pauline fragments — possibly genuine Pauline notes preserved by a later editor and woven into the letter's wider frame. Hans Conzelmann conceded as much. Bart Ehrman in *Forged* maintains the pseudonymity case but acknowledges the chapter's distinctiveness.

Material culture is everywhere. The φαιλόνης *phailonēs* of 4:13, cognate to Latin *paenula*, was a heavy traveler's cloak — a one-piece woolen garment with a hood, designed for harsh weather. Suetonius and Quintilian describe it; archaeological depictions on funerary stelae confirm the form. Paul wants it before winter (4:21) because Roman prisons were unheated and the imprisonment would extend through the cold months. The τὰ βιβλία *ta biblia* — books, plural — are most likely papyrus scrolls or codices; the τὰς μεμβράνας *tas membranas* — Latin loanword for parchment notebooks — were the more durable writing material. T.C. Skeat's classic article "'Especially the Parchments': A Note on 2 Timothy IV.13" (1979) argues that *membranai* refers specifically to parchment notebooks (the Latin *membranae* used for personal note-taking), possibly Paul's own collection of dominical sayings, Old Testament excerpts, or letter copies. Larry Hurtado's *The Earliest Christian Artifacts* and Harry Gamble's *Books and Readers in the Early Church* document how this verse opens a window into early Christian literacy: the apostle traveled with books, lost them, missed them. The codex revolution that defined Christian textual culture from the second century onward had its precursor here.

The personal greetings reconstruct a network. Demas, mentioned positively in Colossians 4:14 and Philemon 24, has now "deserted" (4:10) — the verb *enkataleipō* is the same Mark uses at the cross (15:34). Crescens to Galatia and Titus to Dalmatia are dispersing the apostolic team; Luke alone remains. Mark — the same Mark whose departure caused the Acts 15 split with Barnabas — is now "very useful" (4:11). Tychicus, the bearer of Ephesians and Colossians (Eph 6:21; Col 4:7), has gone to Ephesus. Trophimus is ill at Miletus (4:20) — the same Trophimus from Acts 21:29 whose presence with Paul in Jerusalem precipitated the riot that began Paul's chain of imprisonments. Erastus remains at Corinth (4:20) — possibly the same Erastus named as city treasurer at Romans 16:23 and attested in the so-called "Erastus inscription" discovered at Corinth in 1929 (a Latin pavement inscription naming an *Erastus pro aedilitate*). Linus (4:21) appears in Irenaeus's list of Roman bishops (*Against Heresies* 3.3.3) as the second bishop of Rome after Peter, an early extra-NT cross-reference. Pudens and Claudia have been speculatively linked to figures in Martial's *Epigrams* but the identification is contested.

Alexander the coppersmith (4:14) is most likely distinct from the Alexander of 1 Timothy 1:20 (Mounce, Marshall). The Greek term χαλκεύς *chalkeus* names a metalworker. The "first defense" *prōtē apologia* of 4:16 raises a genuine historical question: which trial? Pauline interpreters take it as the first stage of his current Roman case, with execution still ahead; pseudonymous interpreters read it as an archaizing literary device.

Greek key terms anchor the chapter: ἀγῶνα *agōna* (fight, contest, 4:7); δρόμον *dromon* (race, 4:7); στέφανον *stephanon* (crown, 4:8); φαιλόνην *phailonēn* (cloak, 4:13); μεμβράνας *membranas* (parchments, 4:13); ἀνάλυσις *analusis* (departure, 4:6 — euphemism for death, with cognate at Phil 1:23); ἀπολογία *apologia* (defense, 4:16).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 4 brings the testamentary letter to its formal climax. Greco-Roman testaments and farewell discourses regularly conclude with three elements: a final charge to the successor, a self-vindication looking back over the dying figure's life, and personal greetings to a network of associates. The Pastoral author follows the form precisely: the charge in 4:1-5 ("preach the word… in season and out of season"), the self-epitaph in 4:6-8 ("I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith"), and the greetings in 4:9-22. Margaret Mitchell, Loveday Alexander, Benjamin Fiore, and Abraham Malherbe have all documented the pattern. Moses' farewell in Deuteronomy 32-34 follows it; so do Jacob in Genesis 49 and Paul's Miletus address in Acts 20:17-38. The genre signal is unmistakable.

The literary set piece of 4:6-8 is structured around three athletic-cultic metaphors stacked together. First, the libation: σπένδομαι *spendomai*, "I am being poured out as a drink offering," uses the same verb Paul deploys at Philippians 2:17 and that the Septuagint uses for the libation poured at Israelite sacrifices (Numbers 28:7). The image makes Paul's death a sacrificial completion of his ministry. Second, the contest: τὸν καλὸν ἀγῶνα ἠγώνισμαι *ton kalon agōna ēgōnismai*, "I have fought the good fight" — the cognate accusative is athletic-rhetorical. Third, the race: τὸν δρόμον τετέλεκα *ton dromon teteleka*, "I have finished the race." Then the verdict: τὴν πίστιν τετήρηκα *tēn pistin tetērēka*, "I have kept the faith" — the verb *tēreō* echoes the *parathēkē*-guarding language of chapters 1 and 2. The climactic image of the *stephanos tēs dikaiosynēs* — the crown of righteousness — is the athletic prize, an olive or laurel wreath, not a royal diadem (διάδημα *diadēma*). The Pastoral author's literary instinct is exact: Paul finishes as a successful contestant, not as a king.

The vice prediction of 4:3-4 — "people will not endure sound teaching, but having itching ears (κνηθόμενοι τὴν ἀκοήν *knēthomenoi tēn akoēn*) they will accumulate for themselves teachers to suit their own passions" — is one of the most vivid metaphors in the Pastorals. The ear-itching image appears in Plutarch (*De Recta Audiendi Ratione*) and Lucian; the Pastoral author adapts it for ecclesial use. The "myths" *mythoi* in 4:4 echo 1 Tim 1:4, 4:7, and Tit 1:14, suggesting a corpus-level battle against speculative teaching, possibly proto-Gnostic or Jewish-apocalyptic.

The biographical greetings in 4:9-22 are themselves a literary form. Compare Romans 16's roster of Roman believers, Colossians 4:7-18's network of Asia Minor associates, Philemon 23-24's smaller list. The Pastoral author preserves or constructs a final Pauline rolodex. Verses 4:13's request for the cloak and parchments are particularly hard to read as pseudonymous fabrication — what would the rhetorical purpose be of inventing a forgotten cloak at a forgotten host's house in a remembered city? The mundane particularity is the strongest internal argument for authenticity.

Textual variants are minor. Some manuscripts at 4:10 read "Gallia" (Gaul) for "Galatia," reflecting the historical ambiguity between Galatia in Asia Minor and Gallia in modern France; Codex Sinaiticus א and most witnesses read "Galatia." At 4:22, some early witnesses end with simple "Grace be with you" while others add "Amen"; the variant is liturgically generated.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical NT arc, 2 Timothy 4 is the apostle's death scene transcribed in advance. Israel's covenant, Jesus' messianic enactment, the Spirit's outpouring, the church's mission — and now the apostolic generation, dying with the gospel firmly committed to the next. The chapter ends not with despair but with confident expectation: "the Lord will rescue me from every evil deed and bring me safely into his heavenly kingdom" (4:18). The literary effect — particularly if read as Paul's actual last words — has shaped Christian death imagination for two millennia.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic readers turned 4:6-8 into Paul's epitaph. Eusebius's *Ecclesiastical History* 2.22 quotes the verse to authenticate his account of Paul's beheading. Augustine uses 4:7-8 in *Confessions* 9 and the *Sermons* on the death of friends to articulate Christian dying-well. John Chrysostom's *Homilies on 2 Timothy* (Homilies 9-10) preach the chapter as a model of pastoral steadfastness in face of death. The apocryphal *Acts of Paul* (mid-2nd c.) elaborates the death narrative; the *Acts of Paul and Thecla* makes Paul a martyr-protagonist in Christian imagination.

The medieval *ars moriendi* tradition — the *Art of Dying*, with its Latin and German illustrated versions from the early 15th century — drew on 2 Timothy 4:6-8 as the canonical model of Christian death. Erasmus's *De Praeparatione ad Mortem* (1534) and Robert Bellarmine's *De Arte Bene Moriendi* (1620) extended the tradition. The verse appears in burial liturgies across Christian traditions; it is read at countless funerals; Brahms's *German Requiem* incorporates verbal echoes; Newman's *Lead Kindly Light* and the *Te Deum* draw on its theological mood.

Reformation readers preached 4:6-8 with characteristic differences. Luther emphasized the gift-character of the crown — it is *given*, not earned, by the *iustus iudex* who is also *Salvator*. Calvin's commentary on 2 Timothy treats 4:7's "I have kept the faith" as compatible with sola gratia, since the perseverance is itself God's gift. Wesleyan tradition emphasized the empirical evidence of "happy deaths" — John Wesley collected death-bed accounts of believers as proof of sanctifying grace, and the tradition runs through Methodist hagiography into the nineteenth century.

Critical scholarship has read the chapter at two levels. F.C. Baur and the Tübingen School treated 4:6-8 as a literary construct of post-Pauline community looking back. Martin Dibelius and Hans Conzelmann's Hermeneia commentary on the Pastorals reads the personal greetings as either Pauline fragments preserved by a later author or as careful pseudonymous construction. James Miller's *The Pastoral Letters as Composite Documents* offers a sophisticated middle position. Bart Ehrman's *Forged* maintains the pseudonymity case. William Mounce, I. Howard Marshall, Philip Towner, George Knight, Jerome Quinn, and Luke Timothy Johnson defend full Pauline authenticity, with the biographical concreteness of chapter 4 as central evidence. Daniel Wallace and Bruce Metzger note that the textual transmission is ancient and stable, complicating arguments for late composition.

Modern theological retrievals of dying have engaged the chapter substantively. Allen Verhey's *The Christian Art of Dying* (2011) recovers the *ars moriendi* tradition for contemporary practice. Lydia Dugdale's *The Lost Art of Dying* (2020) draws on the same tradition from a medical-humanities standpoint. Richard Bauckham's work on early Christology in the worship of the Lamb has linked 4:8's "all who have loved his appearing" to a wider eschatological expectation in Pauline communities.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** The chapter's farewell form has strong Jewish parallels — Moses' farewell in Deuteronomy 32-34, Jacob's blessing in Genesis 49, the *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs*, and rabbinic ethical wills (the *zava'ah* tradition extending into medieval Jewish piety, with Eleazar of Worms and others producing classic ethical wills). George Foot Moore's *Judaism in the First Centuries of the Christian Era* and Jacob Neusner's work on rabbinic literature have both noted the deep continuity of the form. The Pastoral author's deployment of athletic and sacrificial imagery in 4:6-8 borrows partly from Greco-Roman culture but also reflects the Septuagintal libation language of Numbers and the apocalyptic crown imagery of 4 Ezra and Wisdom of Solomon.

Where modern Jewish scholarship has pushed back is on the supersessionist tendency of some Christian readings of 4:8 — the "righteous Judge" who awards the crown — when this is read as a verdict against Israel rather than a Pauline expectation rooted within Israel's covenantal eschatology. Krister Stendahl, Mark Nanos, and Paula Fredriksen have argued that Paul's apocalyptic expectation in 4:8 is not anti-Jewish but Jewish-apocalyptic, anticipating the same divine judgment 4 Ezra and 2 Baruch describe. Daniel Boyarin's *A Radical Jew* and Pamela Eisenbaum's *Paul Was Not a Christian* place the Pastoral author's Pauline imagination inside, not outside, late Second-Temple Jewish hope.

**Where this leaves us.** Christian and Jewish traditions share a common form of farewell discourse and a common imaginative universe of righteous judgment, sacrificial completion, and post-mortem reward. The shared vocabulary opens space for reading 2 Timothy 4 not as a Christian replacement of Jewish hope but as an apostolic instance of it, distinguished by its Christological focus on "his appearing."

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: The Christian art of dying.** Paul's serene confidence in 4:6-8 — "I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith. Henceforth there is laid up for me the crown of righteousness" — is one face of the New Testament's portrayal of Christian death. The other face is Jesus' Gethsemane prayer ("if it is possible, let this cup pass from me," Matt 26:39) and his cry of dereliction from the cross ("My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" Mark 15:34). Stephen's death in Acts 7:54-60 is closer to Pauline serenity ("Lord Jesus, receive my spirit"), but the New Testament does not simply harmonize the two postures.

The Catholic *ars moriendi* tradition, codified in the early 15th century anonymous *Tractatus de arte bene moriendi* and the illustrated woodblock *Bilder-ars-moriendi*, integrates both — the dying Christian must struggle against five temptations (faithlessness, despair, impatience, vanity, avarice) but trust the saving offices of the Church. Erasmus's *De Praeparatione ad Mortem* and Bellarmine's *De Arte Bene Moriendi* developed the tradition. Modern Catholic theology has retrieved it through the hospice movement (Cicely Saunders's founding of St. Christopher's Hospice in 1967) and writers like Henri Nouwen (*Our Greatest Gift*) and the magisterial document *Iura et Bona* (1980).

Protestant Wesleyan theology developed a distinctive "happy death" tradition; Methodist hagiographies of the 18th and 19th centuries recorded the dying testimonies of believers as empirical evidence of sanctification. Reformed theology emphasized the *blessed assurance* of perseverance grounded in election (Westminster Confession 17, 33). Lutheran theology has historically held the *simul iustus et peccator* dialectic into death — even the dying believer is simultaneously justified and sinner, and confident in the gospel's verdict despite remaining unworthiness (Forde, *Theology Is for Proclamation*; Bayer, *Living by Faith*). Eastern Orthodox monastic tradition cultivates *memento mori* through the daily *prayer at the parting of the soul* in the Trebnik and through the funeral rites' direct address to the deceased.

Secular critique has pressed hard. Atul Gawande's *Being Mortal* (2014) argues that contemporary Western (including Christian) communities have lost competence in dying, defaulting to medicalized denial. Sherwin Nuland's *How We Die* (1994) details the physiological collapse most deaths actually involve, against any sentimentalized picture. Ernest Becker's *The Denial of Death* (1973) frames religious confidence in death as a culturally adaptive but ultimately illusory defense mechanism. The challenge to Christian tradition: does Pauline confidence in 4:6-8 risk denial of the actual terror of dying? Does the *ars moriendi* paper over Gethsemane?

The steelman of the Pauline-confidence reading: Paul writes 4:6-8 as a man with months of imprisonment behind him, with execution imminent, with his closest companions abandoning him; the serenity is hard-won, not naive, and the faith vocabulary ("kept the faith") leaves space for the Gethsemane dimension to have been already worked through in the body of the apostle's prayer life. The steelman of the Gethsemane-honesty reading: the New Testament refuses to make Pauline serenity normative for every Christian death; Jesus' own dying combines forsakenness (Mark 15:34) and entrustment (Luke 23:46), and the theological tradition errs when it forces every dying Christian into the Pauline mold against the Markan witness.

N.T. Wright, in *Paul for Everyone: The Pastoral Letters* and again in *Surprised by Hope*, reads 4:6-8 against his characteristic future-and-bodily framework: the "crown of righteousness" the "righteous judge" will award is not a private reward in a disembodied heaven but the public vindication of those who belong to the Messiah on the day God puts the whole world right — justification's verdict declared at the end as it has been declared in advance through faith. For Wright the telltale phrase is "all who have loved his appearing" (4:8): the hope is not escape from the world but Christ's *epiphaneia*, his appearing to set up God's kingdom on a renewed earth, and Paul dies as a man who has poured himself out (4:6) for precisely that future. He notes too that the man who could write "I have finished the race" still wanted his books and his cloak — the gospel's most confident witness is, to the end, an embodied human being who feels the cold.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter authorizes Christian confidence in dying without canonizing serenity as the only authentic posture; both Paul's "I have finished the race" and Jesus' "let this cup pass from me" are inside the New Testament's working theology of how Christians die.

**Tension Two: The materiality of the canon.** The cloak and the parchments of 4:13 are a small theological revolution. Christianity has been described as a religion of the book; 4:13 is its founding domestic detail. The apostle traveled with books, lost them, asked for them back. He wrote letters that became Scripture. The Christian movement preserved them in physical media — papyrus scrolls, parchment notebooks, bound codices. Larry Hurtado's *The Earliest Christian Artifacts* and Harry Gamble's *Books and Readers in the Early Church* argue that Christianity's adoption of the codex (over the scroll) by the second century was a distinctive material commitment that shaped the religion's textual practice for centuries. Stanley Porter has written extensively on Paul's library; T.C. Skeat's analysis of the *membranai* opened a generation of scholarship on early Christian textual technology.

Catholic and Orthodox theology has read 4:13 as authorizing a sacramental view of the Word as both incarnate-and-textual. Henri de Lubac's *Medieval Exegesis: The Four Senses of Scripture* (4 vols) traces how the medieval exegetical tradition treated the physical book as the sacrament of the divine speech. Alexander Schmemann's *For the Life of the World* extends the sacramental theology to liturgical practice. The *kissing of the Gospel book*, the elevation of the codex in liturgical procession, the iconography of evangelists writing their books — all reflect a theological imagination in which the divine Word is genuinely present through physical mediation.

Protestant theology has historically been more austere about the materiality but no less committed to the textual locus of revelation. The Reformation's printing-press alliance (Gutenberg's 1456 Bible, Luther's 1522 New Testament, Tyndale's 1526 English New Testament, the Geneva Bible 1560) was a material-cultural revolution as much as a theological one. John Webster's *Holy Scripture: A Dogmatic Sketch* and Kevin Vanhoozer's *The Drama of Doctrine* recover the high-textual vision in dogmatic terms.

Critical-historical theology has pressed the contingency of the canon's emergence. Bruce Metzger's *The Canon of the New Testament*, Lee McDonald's *The Biblical Canon*, and Eldon Jay Epp's textual-critical work have shown how slowly and unevenly the boundaries of the New Testament were drawn. The 27-book NT canon was not formally listed in the form we now have it until Athanasius's Festal Letter 39 in 367 AD, was confirmed at the regional councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397), and was definitively closed in the West only at Trent in 1546. The Eastern Church preserved Revelation's marginal status into the medieval period. The cloak detail of 4:13 is a window into how contingent and embodied the canonization process was — actual scrolls in actual cities surviving actual fires and floods.

The steelman of the sacramental-textual position: God genuinely speaks through physical media, and the materiality of Christian Scripture is not incidental but theological — the Word becomes flesh in Jesus and continues to address us through ink and parchment. To read 4:13 as merely autobiographical is to miss its incarnational logic. The steelman of the contingency position: the canon's particular shape is the product of specific historical accidents — which manuscripts survived, which churches preserved which letters, which councils achieved consensus — and a healthy doctrine of Scripture must reckon with that contingency rather than baptize it as inevitable. **Where this leaves us:** the cloak and parchments remind us that Christianity's textual life is embodied and historically embedded. The doctrine of inspiration in 3:16 and the materiality of 4:13 are two faces of one reality: God speaks through specific words preserved in specific physical media by specific communities across specific centuries.

**Unresolved question.** Paul says "I have finished the race" three years before some scholars think these words were written, in a cell where he is asking for his cloak before winter — if the apostolic generation could not finally distinguish between what would be remembered as Scripture and what would be lost as a forgotten cloak at Carpus's house in Troas, why do later generations imagine they can?

## The Thread

- **Philippians 1:21-23** — "For me to live is Christ, and to die is gain… My desire is to depart (ἀναλῦσαι *analusai*) and be with Christ." The same verb for "departure" used in 2 Tim 4:6 (*analusis*). Read together, the two passages form Paul's developed theology of dying-as-departure, with Philippians written from an earlier imprisonment and 2 Timothy at the end. The thread is the *eschatological homecoming* register.

- **Acts 20:24** (earlier in canonical reading order) — Paul's Miletus address: "I do not account my life of any value nor as precious to myself, if only I may finish my course (τὸν δρόμον μου *ton dromon mou*) and the ministry that I received from the Lord Jesus." The same race image deployed in 2 Tim 4:7. The Acts speech is the literary precedent for 2 Timothy's farewell form.

- **2 Peter 1:13-15** (later in canonical reading order) — Peter, also writing on the eve of his death ("the putting off of my body will be soon"), uses the same testamentary form. The *apostolic farewell* genre is Petrine as well as Pauline, and the parallel deepens the historical plausibility that 2 Tim 4 reflects an actual late-stage apostolic literary practice.

- **Numbers 28:7 / Philippians 2:17** — The drink-offering libation (σπονδή *spondē*) of the Septuagint sacrificial system, and Paul's earlier deployment of it at Phil 2:17 ("even if I am to be poured out as a drink offering"). The same liturgical image appears in 2 Tim 4:6, but where Phil 2:17 was conditional ("even if"), 2 Tim 4:6 is realized ("I am already being poured out"). The hinge from Phil to 2 Tim is the hinge from apostolic mission still in progress to apostolic mission completed.
