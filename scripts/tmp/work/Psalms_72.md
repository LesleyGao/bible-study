# Psalm 72 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 72 carries the brief superscription *li-Shlomoh* (לִשְׁלֹמֹה, "of/for/concerning Solomon") — the only psalm in Book II so attributed and one of only two in the entire Psalter (the other being Psalm 127). The Hebrew preposition *le-* is famously ambiguous: it can mean "by" (Solomonic authorship), "for" (a prayer offered for Solomon, perhaps by David his father), "concerning" (a psalm about a king like Solomon), or "in the manner of" (Solomonic-style royal psalm). The closing colophon at v. 20 — *kalu tefillot David ben-Yishai* (כָּלּוּ תְפִלּוֹת דָּוִד בֶּן־יִשָׁי, "the prayers of David, son of Jesse, are ended") — adds a further interpretive complication. If the colophon belongs to Psalm 72, and the psalm is "of/for Solomon," the most natural reading is that this is David's final prayer offered for his son and successor — the dying king's prayer for the next king. The Talmud (*Pesachim* 117a) and *Midrash Tehillim* read it precisely this way: David composes Psalm 72 in his final days as a benediction over Solomon's accession.

The form-critical genre, on Hermann Gunkel's framework (*Einleitung in die Psalmen*, 1933), is *Königspsalm* — royal psalm — a category Gunkel defined to cover the small group of psalms (Pss 2, 18, 20, 21, 45, 72, 89, 101, 110, 132, 144) directly concerned with the Davidic king. Sigmund Mowinckel pressed further and argued that royal psalms had a *Sitz im Leben* in actual coronation or anniversary-of-enthronement liturgies in the Jerusalem temple — Psalm 72 in particular reads as a coronation prayer or anniversary-of-accession blessing. The psalm's structure (petitions for justice, prosperity, dominion, longevity, intercession on behalf of the king) fits ancient Near Eastern coronation liturgies. The intercessory structure ("may the king...") distinguishes Psalm 72 from psalms in which the king himself speaks (Pss 18, 101, 110); here the king is third-person subject of petition.

The royal-ideal vision is striking and specific. The king is to *yadin amcha be-tzedeq va-aniyei-cha be-mishpat* ("judge your people in righteousness and your poor with justice," v. 2). He is to *yishpot aniyei-am yoshia li-vnei evyon* (יִשְׁפֹּט עֲנִיֵּי־עָם יוֹשִׁיעַ לִבְנֵי אֶבְיוֹן, "judge the poor of the people, give salvation to the children of the needy," v. 4) and *vidakkei oshek* ("crush the oppressor"). He will *yatzil evyon meshave'a ve-ani ve-ein ozer lo* ("rescue the needy when he cries, and the poor and him who has no helper," v. 12). His blood-redeeming care — *mi-tokh u-me-chamas yig'al nafsham ve-yeqar damam be-einav* ("from oppression and violence he redeems their life, and precious is their blood in his sight," v. 14) — places the protection of the poor and oppressed at the center of royal vocation. This is the strongest articulation in the Psalter of the king's ethical responsibility to society's most vulnerable.

The cosmic and natural imagery is equally distinctive. Verse 6 contains one of the Psalter's most arresting similes: *yarad ke-matar al-gez* (יֵרֵד כְּמָטָר עַל־גֵּז, "may he descend like rain on the mown grass") — the king as life-giving rain on freshly-cut fields. The image is agricultural and gentle, in striking contrast to the imperial dominion language a few verses later. Verse 8: *ve-yerd mi-yam ad-yam u-mi-nahar ad-afsei-aretz* ("may he have dominion from sea to sea, and from the River to the ends of the earth") — a phrase echoing Zechariah 9:10 and used in later Jewish messianic and Christian post-millennial expectation alike. Verse 10's catalog of nations bringing tribute — *malkei Tarshish ve-iyim minchah yashivu malkei Sheba u-Seva eshkar yaqrivu* ("kings of Tarshish and the coastlands shall return tribute, kings of Sheba and Seba shall bring gifts") — gathers the trading horizons of the ancient Mediterranean and Arabian worlds. Sheba, in particular, evokes the visit of the queen of Sheba to Solomon (1 Kings 10) and gives the psalm's Solomonic attribution literary depth.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels are extensive. Egyptian royal hymns (the *Hymn to Sesostris III*, the coronation texts for Hatshepsut) similarly invoke the king's role in cosmic justice, fertility, and dominion. Mesopotamian royal hymns (Šulgi hymns, the *Hymn to Marduk* with its blessings on the Babylonian king) follow the same pattern: petition for the king's just rule, his agricultural prosperity, his international dominion, his longevity. What Psalm 72 distinctively shares with the Hebrew prophetic tradition (Isa 11; Jer 23; Mic 4) and what it does *not* share with neighboring royal hymns is the framing of royal dominion in covenantal-ethical terms grounded in *tzedaqah* (righteousness) and the protection of the *aniyim* and *evyonim* (the poor and needy). The king's authority rests not on power as such but on the just exercise of power for those who lack it.

The Hebrew text is well-preserved. The Dead Sea Scrolls (4QPsa, 11QPsa) preserve fragments. The Septuagint (Psalm 71 LXX) translates faithfully. There is little textual instability.

The most consequential historical-critical observation about Psalm 72 is its place in the editorial structure of the Psalter — specifically, the closing colophon *kalu tefillot David ben-Yishai* ("the prayers of David, son of Jesse, are ended," v. 20). This statement, taken at face value, marks the end of a "Davidic prayers" collection. But the canonical Psalter contains many further psalms attributed to David (Pss 86, 101, 103, 108-110, 122, 124, 131, 133, 138-145). The cleanest explanation, advanced by Gerald Wilson in *The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* (1985) and developed by David Mitchell (*The Message of the Psalter*, 1997), Susan Gillingham (*Psalms Through the Centuries*), and others, is that v. 20 is the colophon of an earlier, smaller "Prayers of David" collection — possibly Pss 3-72 or some subset — that was incorporated into the larger canonical Psalter without removing the colophon. This makes Psalm 72 not just the close of Book II but the close of an early Davidic-Solomonic collection, with the placement of Psalm 72 (a psalm "for Solomon" understood as David's prayer over his son) deliberately marking a generational handoff that closes the Davidic original.

Wilson's larger argument extends the editorial story: Books I-III of the Psalter (Pss 1-89), he proposed, represent a collection focused on the Davidic-messianic kingdom, whose theological narrative arrives at a crisis by Book III's end (Pss 88 and 89, with Ps 89's covenant-failure lament: "you have renounced the covenant with your servant"). Books IV-V (Pss 90-150), Wilson argued, respond with a *Yahweh-malak* ("the Lord reigns") theology in which the failure of Davidic kingship gives way to the eternal kingship of Yahweh. This canonical reading places Psalm 72 at a crucial juncture: it is the high-water mark of Davidic-monarchical hope in the Psalter. Everything after it in Book III moves toward crisis; everything in Books IV-V responds to that crisis with a different theological frame.

Book II's editorial closure thus has weight. The doxology at vv. 18-19 — *Baruch Yahweh Elohim Elohei Yisrael oseh nifla'ot levaddo u-baruch shem kevodo le-olam* ("Blessed be the Lord God, the God of Israel, who alone does wondrous things; blessed be his glorious name forever") — is one of four such doxologies marking the Psalter's books (Pss 41:13, 72:18-19, 89:52, 106:48). These doxologies are widely recognized as editorial — added to mark book boundaries — and the Book II doxology is the most expansive.

## Lens 2: Literary Architecture

Lowth's parallelism opens up Psalm 72's careful poetic architecture. The opening petition (vv. 1-2) sets the keynote in dense synonymous parallelism: *Elohim mishpatecha la-melekh ten ve-tzidqatecha le-ven-melekh* ("O God, give the king your justice / and your righteousness to the royal son"). The two divine attributes — *mishpat* (justice/judgment) and *tzedaqah* (righteousness) — are paired and chiastically reflected in the king's coming work: *yadin amcha be-tzedeq va-aniyei-cha be-mishpat* ("may he judge your people in righteousness and your poor with justice"). The psalm's two structural keywords (*tzedaqah* and *mishpat*) are introduced, balanced, and made the criterion of royal action.

The strophic structure resolves naturally into five movements:

- vv. 1-4: petition for the king's just rule, with focus on the poor
- vv. 5-7: petition for the king's enduring reign, with cosmic-natural imagery
- vv. 8-11: petition for the king's universal dominion
- vv. 12-14: ground for the petition — the king actually delivers the needy
- vv. 15-17: petition for the king's longevity, prosperity of the land, blessing through him

The closing doxology (vv. 18-19) and the colophon (v. 20) are editorial overlay rather than part of the original psalm structure. Within the body of the psalm, the structural turn at v. 12 is critical. After ten verses of petition ("may he..."), v. 12 shifts to indicative ground: *ki-yatzil evyon meshave'a* ("for he delivers the needy when he calls"). The *ki* ("for") gives the basis for the preceding petitions: the reason the psalmist asks God to grant the king dominion and prosperity is *because* the king's actual work is the rescue of the poor. The king's universal dominion is justified by his solidarity with the most vulnerable — a remarkable theological move that subordinates imperial reach to ethical care.

Several inclusios bind the psalm. Verse 5 (*im-shemesh ve-li-fnei yareach dor dorim*, "as long as the sun, before the moon, generation of generations") is echoed in v. 17 (*yehi shemo le-olam li-fnei-shemesh yinnon shemo*, "may his name endure forever, before the sun may his fame continue"). The sun-and-moon longevity frame holds the petitions together. Verse 4's *aniyei-am* and *bnei-evyon* (poor of the people; children of the needy) recur at v. 12 (*evyon... ani ve-ein ozer lo*) and v. 13 (*dal ve-evyon nafshot evyonim*) — the poor are the structural center of the psalm. *Tzedaqah* (righteousness) opens at v. 1 and closes at v. 7, framing the first half of the psalm.

The rain-on-mown-grass simile of v. 6 — *yarad ke-matar al-gez ki-revivim zarzif aretz* — is a prosodic gem. The Hebrew *gez* (mown grass) is rare; *zarzif* (a rare onomatopoeic hapax meaning "watering" or perhaps "drizzling") is even more so. The image is gentle — not the storm-king who terrifies, but the descending rain that quietly revives the earth. The verse rhythm slows; the king's authority is figured as a gift of life rather than a threat of force.

The dominion language of vv. 8-11, by contrast, accelerates and expands: *mi-yam ad-yam* (sea to sea), *mi-nahar ad-afsei-aretz* (river to ends of earth), tribute from Tarshish, gifts from Sheba and Seba, all kings bowing, all nations serving. The geographical scope reaches from the Mediterranean (Tarshish — possibly Spain) to the Arabian peninsula (Sheba) to Africa (Seba — perhaps Sudan/Ethiopia) to the desert tribes (*tziyim*). The horizon is the known world.

There is no *selah* in Psalm 72. The psalm closes with the doxology (vv. 18-19) and colophon (v. 20), both editorial, marking the close of Book II.

## Lens 3: Within the Larger Story — Reception

Jewish reception of Psalm 72 is rich and stratified. *Midrash Tehillim* (Buber) reads the psalm Davidically — David composes it as his prayer for Solomon at the end of his life. Targum Psalms expands the messianic dimension explicitly, rendering the king as *malka meshicha* (the Messianic King) and reading the universal dominion language as eschatological. The Babylonian Talmud (*Pesachim* 117a, *Sotah* 48a) treats the psalm as eschatological-messianic in its scope. *Rashi* (eleventh century) reads it Davidically-messianically: David prays for Solomon, but the petitions exceed Solomon's reign and look forward to the *melekh ha-mashiach* (the king-messiah). *Ibn Ezra* is more philological, attentive to the *li-Shlomoh* superscription and reading the psalm as a coronation prayer. *Radak* (Kimhi) develops the messianic reading more fully, taking the universal-dominion language as definitively eschatological. *Ramban* (Nachmanides) reads the psalm as both historical (about Solomon's coronation) and messianic (about the future kingdom).

Modern Jewish scholarship continues these threads. Martin Buber, in his discussions of biblical kingship, finds in Psalm 72 the "primal vision" of just rule that prophets later turn into critique. Abraham Heschel, in *The Prophets*, draws on the psalm's poor-and-oppressed motifs as the source from which prophetic ethics flow. Jon Levenson treats Psalm 72 as a key text in his work on royal theology and the temple. Nahum Sarna emphasizes the psalm's place in the editorial structure of the Psalter, reading the colophon as evidence of an earlier collection. Liturgically, Psalm 72 is part of *Hallel ha-Gadol* in some traditions, and its messianic dimensions are activated in *Tisha B'Av* and Passover *seder* discussions of redemption.

Christian reception of Psalm 72 is dominated by the Epiphany tradition. The visit of the Magi in Matthew 2:1-12 — kings from the East bringing gold, frankincense, and myrrh — was read by the early church as the fulfillment of Ps 72:10-11 ("kings of Sheba and Seba shall bring gifts… all kings shall fall down before him"). This reading is patristic and pre-Constantinian: Justin Martyr (*Dialogue with Trypho* 78), Tertullian (*Adversus Marcionem*), and Origen all treat Ps 72 as a messianic-Christological prophecy fulfilled in Christ. The Epiphany liturgy of Catholic, Anglican, and Orthodox traditions reads Psalm 72 alongside Matthew 2 and Isaiah 60 as the triadic Epiphany lectionary. Christian medieval iconography of the three kings drew directly on Ps 72:10's "kings of Sheba and Seba" — by the medieval period the Magi had been numbered three (one for each gift), named (Caspar, Melchior, Balthasar), and mapped to the continents (Europe, Asia, Africa) — a tradition explicitly linked to the universal-dominion vision of Ps 72.

Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on this psalm is one of his most expansive Christological readings: the psalm is wholly about Christ, who is the true king, the *rex iustus* who delivers the poor and rules from sea to sea. Athanasius treats it as an Epiphany psalm. Cassiodorus (*Expositio Psalmorum*) develops the messianic-typological reading at length. Aquinas in his commentary on the Psalms reads vv. 1-4 as Christ's just judgment, vv. 5-11 as his eternal and universal reign, vv. 12-14 as the redemption work of the cross, and vv. 15-17 as the eschatological consummation. Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* (1519-21) reads the psalm Christologically with a strong emphasis on v. 12-14: Christ's kingship is defined by his solidarity with the poor and oppressed, the very pattern by which Luther reframes royal-Christian power. Calvin's 1557 commentary, while affirming the Christological dimension, also recovers the historical reference: the psalm is genuinely about Solomon, and *through* Solomon points to Christ. Calvin uses the psalm as a basis for political theology — earthly rulers are judged by the criterion of Ps 72.

Modern critical scholarship has produced a substantial literature. Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 72 as the paradigmatic *Königspsalm*. Sigmund Mowinckel argued for its setting in actual Jerusalem coronation liturgy. Claus Westermann placed it in the trajectory of royal psalms moving toward eschatological reading. Hans-Joachim Kraus (BKAT) read it as a coronation prayer with strong eschatological reception. Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger (Hermeneia) place special weight on its editorial position closing Book II and read its universal-blessing language as a deliberate echo of the Abrahamic blessing of Gen 12:3 (cf. Ps 72:17, *ve-yitbarechu vo kol goyim*, "may all nations be blessed in him"). John Goldingay (BCOTWP) develops the political-theological reading. Marvin Tate (WBC) emphasizes the ANE royal-hymn parallels. Beth Tanner (NICOT) and W. Dennis Tucker treat its canonical placement carefully. Walter Brueggemann's *The Message of the Psalms* highlights the radical ethical core: a royal ideology that subordinates power to the poor.

The most consequential recent development is the editorial-canonical reading. Gerald Wilson's 1985 *The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* argued that Books I-III represent an earlier Psalter built around Davidic-messianic hopes, with Psalm 72 as the high-water mark — the closing prayer of the original Davidic collection. Books IV-V respond to Davidic failure (signaled by Pss 88 and 89's covenant lament) with *Yahweh-malak* theology. David Mitchell's *The Message of the Psalter* takes Wilson further into eschatological reading. Susan Gillingham's *Psalms Through the Centuries* charts the reception history. The result is that contemporary scholarship reads Psalm 72 as both an actual coronation prayer (probably Solomonic in its earliest layer) and a theologically-loaded canonical capstone — the moment before the Davidic kingdom enters its long crisis.

The New Testament uses Psalm 72 in multiple registers. Matthew 2:1-12 echoes its Sheba imagery. Hebrews and the broader New Testament identification of Christ as *Christos* (anointed king) draws on the entire royal-psalm tradition. The Book of Revelation's "kings of the earth shall bring their glory and honor into it" (Rev 21:24, 26) directly echoes Psalm 72.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — What's at Stake

**Tension 1: Royal psalm in non-monarchical context — what do we do with kingship language now?** Psalm 72 is the paradigmatic royal psalm. But there has been no Davidic king since the sixth century BCE. Christian and Jewish communities have prayed this psalm for two and a half millennia in monarchical, non-monarchical, democratic, and authoritarian political contexts. *Position one — Christological-messianic:* The mainstream patristic-medieval reading (Augustine, Cassiodorus, Aquinas, Luther, Calvin) and Christian-orthodox tradition reads the psalm as fulfilled in Christ. The earthly Davidic-Solomonic reference points forward to the eternal kingship of Christ; the universal dominion is realized eschatologically; the rescue of the poor is the work of the cross. This reading has been the dominant Christian one for nearly two thousand years. *Position two — political-prophetic-applicational:* A second tradition (running from the prophets' own use of royal-justice language through Calvin's political theology to Catholic Social Teaching documents like *Populorum Progressio* and *Caritas in Veritate*) reads Ps 72 as a critical political-theological standard: any earthly ruler is to be judged by whether he protects the poor, crushes the oppressor, and rules in *tzedaqah*. The psalm becomes a tool of prophetic critique against rulers who fail this standard. *Position three — eschatological-reservation:* A third position, especially developed in recent canonical-criticism scholarship (Wilson, Mitchell), holds that the Davidic kingdom of Pss 1-72 *failed* by Ps 89 and that the canonical Psalter's response is to defer fulfillment to God's eternal kingship. On this view, Ps 72 is ironically the high-water mark of a hope that the Psalter itself goes on to deconstruct. Steelmanning each: the Christological view honors how the church has actually prayed this psalm and Christ's distinctive fulfillment of its ideals; the political-applicational view honors the psalm's ethical core (the protection of the poor) and refuses to allow Christology to vacate ethical demand on rulers; the eschatological-reservation view honors the editorial structure of the Psalter and resists triumphalist royal readings. *Where this leaves us:* the three readings need each other. A Christological reading without political-ethical application becomes pious deflection from the demand the psalm makes on rulers. A political-ethical reading without Christological grounding becomes a generic royal-justice text indistinguishable from any moral-monarchical ideal. An eschatological-reservation without confidence in Christ's actual kingship becomes despair. The psalm is best held in all three registers at once — Christ is the true king, his kingship is realized eschatologically and partially-now, and rulers in this age are judged by Ps 72's standard.

**Tension 2: The just-ruler vision and contemporary political theology.** Psalm 72:12-14 — the king as protector of the poor, redeemer of the oppressed, the one for whom *yeqar damam be-einav* ("precious is their blood in his sight") — articulates a political theology. Modern application is contested. *Position one — Catholic Social Teaching:* From *Rerum Novarum* (Leo XIII, 1891) through *Centesimus Annus* (John Paul II, 1991) to *Laudato Si'* and *Fratelli Tutti* (Francis, 2015 and 2020), Catholic Social Teaching draws extensively on Ps 72-style royal-justice imagery to articulate the church's social doctrine: the preferential option for the poor, the universal destination of goods, solidarity with the oppressed. *Position two — N. T. Wright's kingdom reading:* Wright (*Surprised by Hope*, *How God Became King*, *The Day the Revolution Began*) treats Psalm 72 as one of the texts that defines what "kingdom of God" meant for a first-century Jew — God's rule enacted through an anointed king whose first business is *justice for the poor and the crushing of the oppressor*, not private spiritual comfort. For Wright the psalm is messianic in shape and the New Testament's claim is that Jesus is the king it describes; "heaven coming to earth" is not escape from the world but the establishing on earth of exactly the Psalm 72 order — which is why, on Wright's reading, the church that prays this psalm is committed to working for that justice now, in anticipation. *Position three — classical-conservative reading:* A more classical reading (drawing on Augustine's two-cities tradition, Aquinas's natural law, the Reformed political-theology tradition through Calvin and Bavinck) affirms Ps 72's ethical demands but resists collapsing the psalm into modern political programs; the king's role is to maintain justice and order, not to instantiate utopian visions, and Christological fulfillment is the proper resolution of the psalm's eschatological pressure. Steelmanning each: Catholic Social Teaching has produced a body of social doctrine grounded in scripture and intellectually serious; Wright's reading recovers the protection-of-the-poor as central rather than peripheral and ties it to the church's eschatological hope; the classical-conservative reading has resisted utopian political programs that have historically produced violence. *Where this leaves us:* Psalm 72 makes a real ethical demand on real rulers. *Yashpot aniyei-am yoshia li-vnei evyon* is not metaphor. The blood of the poor is *yeqar* (precious) to the true king, and any ruler who treats the blood of the poor as cheap stands judged by this psalm. How exactly that judgment translates into modern political program is genuinely contested, and Christians of good faith hold different views. But the demand itself — that rulers be judged by their treatment of the poor and oppressed — is not negotiable in this psalm. The closing question is how American Christians, in particular, who pray this psalm in worship and live in the most powerful empire in human history, hold its political-ethical claim against the rulers we elect.

**Closing question:** If you laid the rulers of your country, your church, and your community next to Psalm 72:12-14 — *he delivers the needy when he calls, the poor and him who has no helper* — how would they measure? And what does the answer reveal about whether the church is still praying this psalm, or merely reciting it?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 12:3** — *and in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed.* Psalm 72:17's *ve-yitbarechu vo kol goyim* echoes the Abrahamic promise; the king is the one through whom the Abrahamic blessing reaches the nations.
- **Isaiah 11:1-9** — *the wolf shall dwell with the lamb… they shall not hurt or destroy in all my holy mountain.* The closest prophetic echo of Ps 72's just-king vision, with the same poor-and-oppressed center.
- **Matthew 2:11** — *they fell down and worshiped him; opening their treasures, they offered him gifts, gold and frankincense and myrrh.* The Magi's gifts as the Christian liturgical fulfillment of Ps 72:10-11, sealed in the Epiphany lectionary.
- **Revelation 21:24-26** — *the kings of the earth will bring their glory into it… they will bring into it the glory and the honor of the nations.* The eschatological consummation of Psalm 72's universal-tribute vision, in the new Jerusalem.
