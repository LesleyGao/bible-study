# Acts 28: Malta, Rome, and the Open Door

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens on a beach in late autumn, on an island whose name the survivors do not yet know. The Greek text identifies it almost immediately—**Melitē** (Μελίτη, 28:1)—and the overwhelming weight of tradition, archaeology, and seamanship locates this as the modern island of **Malta**, sixty miles south of Sicily. **James Smith** of Jordanhill, in *The Voyage and Shipwreck of St. Paul* (1848), traced the drift trajectory from Cauda along the prevailing wind direction over fourteen days and produced a calculation that landed precisely on Malta's northeastern coast, in what is still called *San Pawl il-Baħar* (St. Paul's Bay). Smith's identification has been the dominant scholarly position for nearly two centuries. A minority alternative—proposed by **Heinz Warnecke** in 1987 and revived occasionally—argues for **Mljet** (Italian *Meleda*), an island off the Croatian Dalmatian coast. The Mljet hypothesis requires reading the ancient *Adrian* of 27:27 as the modern Adriatic and rests on a different drift calculation. Most contemporary commentators (Bruce, Fitzmyer, Schnabel, Keener) accept Maltese identification on the strength of Smith's seamanship analysis, the absence of Adriatic vipers (Mljet has no native venomous snakes), the linguistic data (Maltese tradition preserves *Pawlu* in place names), and the convergence of the route with the standard Alexandrian-grain westbound track to Italy via Syracuse and Rhegium.

The natives are called **hoi barbaroi** (οἱ βάρβαροι, 28:2, 4). The translation "barbarians" is misleading. In first-century Greek, *barbaroi* designated non-Greek-speakers—anyone whose speech sounded like "*bar-bar-bar*" to Hellenic ears—without the modern connotation of "uncivilized." Maltese natives of the period spoke a Punic-derived dialect (Phoenician colonists had settled the island around the eighth century BCE), with Latin and Greek as administrative second languages used by the Roman colonial overlay. The Maltese inscriptional record from the period—a bilingual Punic-Greek dedication to Melqart-Heracles found at Marsaxlokk and now in the Louvre—confirms that the islanders' first language was a Phoenician dialect, not a Greco-Roman language. Luke's *barbaroi* is technically precise. Their "*ou tēn tychousan philanthrōpian*"—"unusual kindness" (literally "no ordinary humanity")—is a Lukan litotes that shows up frequently in Hellenistic ethnography for praising peoples on the periphery of the Greco-Roman world.

The **viper episode** (28:3-6) has prompted much commentary. Modern Malta has no native venomous snakes (the only resident species is the western whip snake, *Coluber viridiflavus*, non-venomous). This has produced two interpretive responses. The first (Casson, Hemer): two thousand years of human pressure have driven the Maltese viper to extinction, and a small population of vipers in late autumn could plausibly have inhabited the island at the time of the shipwreck. The second (Pervo, more skeptical commentators): Luke is reproducing a snake-bite topos common in ancient adventure literature (cf. the snake-bite of Philoctetes; the serpent-encounter of Heracles; the Lucanian narrative tradition) and the species identification is not the point. **Bruce NICNT** notes a third option: Maltese *barbaroi* would have known the local snake species and would not have expected swelling and sudden death from a non-venomous bite, so Luke's narrative either preserves a real venomous-snake encounter from a population since extinct or relies on a legendary embellishment. The Maltese liturgical tradition treats the event as historical: the **Feast of the Shipwreck of St. Paul** (*Festa tan-Nawfraġju ta' San Pawl*) is celebrated on February 10 every year and has been since at least the early modern period, with documentary attestation back to the medieval era.

**Publius** (28:7) is named *prōtos tēs nēsou*—"the chief man of the island." A Latin inscription found on Malta in the nineteenth century, reading "*L. Castricius Cylenius prochegos Melitensium*"—uses *prochegos* (Greek for "chief") in apparent administrative parallel to Luke's *prōtos*, though the inscription is much later and the relationship is contested. **Schnabel** treats the term as a recognized Maltese-Roman administrative title, not just a casual designation. The healing of Publius's father from "fever and dysentery" (*pyretois kai dysenteriō*, 28:8)—a fairly precise medical description—has prompted comment from **Cadbury**, who in his classic studies of Lukan medical vocabulary noted that Luke's medical terminology is consistently more technical than that of the other Gospel writers. The term *dysenteria* (in this plural form) is the technical Hippocratic designation for what we still call dysentery; the chronic combination of fever and dysentery in Mediterranean climates was widely associated with what we now know as Mediterranean fever (brucellosis), endemic to the region.

The voyage from Malta to Italy (28:11-16) is precisely traceable. The vessel that wintered at Malta was another **Alexandrian grain freighter**, this one identified by its figurehead—the **Dioskouroi** (*Dioskourois*, 28:11), the twin gods Castor and Pollux, patrons of sailors and protectors against storm. Hellenistic and Roman maritime tradition credited the *Dioskouroi* with appearing in the rigging during storms as the corposants (St. Elmo's fire); ships were routinely consecrated to them. **Casson** documents the practice. The route—**Syracuse** (three days), **Rhegium** (Calabrian toe), **Puteoli** (the principal Roman grain port on the Bay of Naples)—is the standard late-season Alexandrian itinerary. **Puteoli** in 60-61 CE was a thriving cosmopolitan port; **Peter Lampe**'s *From Paul to Valentinus: Christians at Rome in the First Two Centuries* documents the early presence of a Jewish-Christian community there (Josephus, *Antiquities* 17.12.1, mentions Jews at Puteoli a generation earlier). The "brothers" (*adelphoi*) Paul finds (28:14) constitute the earliest archaeologically and epigraphically attested Italian Christian community outside Rome itself.

The final stretch into Rome on the *Via Appia* names the **Forum of Appius** (*Appiou Phorou*, 28:15)—a market town about 43 Roman miles from Rome, mentioned by Horace (*Satires* 1.5.3-4) as a typical road-stop—and **Three Taverns** (*Triōn Tabernōn*, 28:15)—a station 33 miles from Rome on the same road. These are administratively recorded *mansiones* and *mutationes* of the imperial cursus. Roman Christians coming this far out to meet Paul represents real social-political risk and substantial logistical effort, suggesting both the size and the organization of the Roman community by 60-61 CE. Lampe estimates that the Roman Christian community at the time of Paul's arrival was several hundred to perhaps a thousand persons, divided into multiple house-church cells across the *insulae* of Trastevere and the Aventine—the same community to whom Paul had written the letter to the Romans some four years earlier.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Acts 28 is the conclusion of the **third "we" passage** (which ends at 28:16) and the conclusion of the entire book. Its literary architecture is multi-layered: the Maltese interlude (1-10), the voyage to Rome (11-16), the encounter with Roman Jewry (17-28), and the famous "open ending" (30-31). Each unit is structured to bring the narrative to a climax that is also a non-ending—a rhetorical strategy unique to Lukan compositional practice.

The **Maltese interlude** (1-10) is structured as a chiasm of misperception and revelation. Paul is initially perceived as a murderer because the snake fastens on his hand (28:4); when he survives, the natives "*metaballomenoi elegon auton einai theon*"—"changing their minds, said he was a god" (28:6). This narrative pattern—mistaken-for-divine—reverses the Lystra episode of 14:8-18, where Paul and Barnabas were similarly mistaken for gods and rebuked the worshipers. **Tannehill** in *The Narrative Unity of Luke-Acts* notes that Luke does not record Paul's reaction to the Maltese deification, in contrast to the explicit Lystra refusal; the silence is significant. Some commentators (Pervo) read the silence as Lukan literary economy; others (Marguerat) read it as Lukan irony—the natives are partially right about Paul (he is the bearer of divine power) even as they are theologically wrong about Paul (he is not a god). The healing of Publius's father and the subsequent island-wide healing (28:9-10) confirm the divine power without explicitly correcting the deification.

The **voyage to Rome** (11-16) is unusually compressed for Luke. After thirty-seven verses of storm and shipwreck in chapter 27, the final leg of Paul's journey is dispatched in six verses. The compression is intentional: the dramatic arc of the journey is over; what remains is the Roman arrival itself. **Marguerat** in *The First Christian Historian* notes that the meeting with the Roman brethren at the Forum of Appius is the emotional peak of the entire travel narrative—"On seeing them, Paul thanked God and took courage" (*idōn ho Paulos eucharistēsas tō theō elabe tharsos*, 28:15). The verb *elabe tharsos* (received courage) appears nowhere else in Acts; Luke is signaling that the long journey from Caesarea has cost the apostle something, and the sight of Roman Christians has restored him.

The **Roman encounter with Jewry** (17-28) is structured as a two-stage diptych: a preliminary meeting (17-22) in which Paul presents his case and the Roman Jewish leaders profess curiosity but no hostility, and a longer second meeting (23-28) in which Paul "from morning till evening" expounds the kingdom of God. The verb *peithō* (convince) is used: "*hoi men epeithonto tois legomenois, hoi de ēpistoun*"—"some were convinced by what he said, but others disbelieved" (28:24). This is the canonical Lukan formula for divided Jewish response to the gospel (cf. 14:1-2; 17:4-5; 18:6; 19:8-9). The chapter then offers the **Isaiah 6:9-10 citation** (28:26-27) as Lukan-apostolic interpretation of the divided response. This is the chapter's most theologically charged moment.

The **citation of Isaiah 6:9-10** is one of the most cited prophetic texts in the entire New Testament—appearing also at Mark 4:12; Matthew 13:14-15; Luke 8:10; John 12:40; Romans 11:8. It is the prophetic-hardening text *par excellence*. Luke's use here is theologically programmatic: the rejection of the gospel by some Jews is not a contingent failure but a fulfillment of the same prophetic word that announced the gospel itself. **Tannehill**, **Marguerat**, **Eisenbaum**, and **Fredriksen** all engage this citation as the chapter's interpretive key, with very different conclusions about its meaning.

The **open ending** (30-31) is one of the most discussed conclusions in the New Testament. The two verses occupy fifty-three Greek words; they end with a single adverb—**akolyto*s*** (ἀκωλύτως, "without hindrance")—which is the final word of the entire two-volume Luke-Acts. **Mikeal Parsons** has noted that *akolyto*s* is a Lukan hapax legomenon in Luke-Acts; it appears nowhere else, and its placement at the very end of the work is a deliberate authorial signature. The verses report no death, no trial outcome, no martyrdom, no return journey. Paul lives "two whole years" (*dietian holēn*) at his own expense, welcomes all who come to him, proclaims the kingdom of God, teaches concerning the Lord Jesus Christ "with all boldness" (*meta pasēs parrēsias*) and "without hindrance" (*akolyto*s*). The book ends.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The interpretive history of Acts 28's open ending is enormous. **F. F. Bruce** (NICNT) defended early dating of Acts (pre-65 CE) partly on the strength of the open ending: if Acts had been written after Paul's death, why would Luke have omitted it? The early-dating position was held by **I. Howard Marshall** (TNTC), **Colin Hemer** (*The Book of Acts in the Setting of Hellenistic History*), and **John Wenham**. Most contemporary scholarship has moved away from early dating, however, preferring an 80-90 CE composition (Conzelmann, Haenchen, Pervo, Marguerat, Keener, Fitzmyer), which means the open ending must be explained on grounds other than ignorance.

The major positions on the ending:

**(1) Literary-theological / programmatic open ending** (Tannehill, Marguerat, Pervo, Mikeal Parsons): the ending is a deliberate Lukan literary device. The gospel that began with shepherds at Bethlehem ends with the apostle in chains at Rome, "without hindrance." The kingdom is unhindered even when its herald is bound. The ending is open because the gospel is open—the next chapter is the reader's own. This reading dominates contemporary commentary.

**(2) Apologetic** (Conzelmann, Haenchen, partially Marguerat): the Lukan-Acts narrative is shaped to demonstrate Christianity's compatibility with Roman law; ending with Paul's execution under Nero would undermine the apologetic, so Luke truncates the narrative before the unfortunate political turn. **Robert Maddox** developed this argument in *The Purpose of Luke-Acts*.

**(3) Source-availability / historical-limit** (some older commentators): Luke wrote what he knew; he had no information about the trial outcome or post-trial events; the ending reflects the limit of his sources.

**(4) Sequel / unfinished work** (C. H. Talbert and others): Luke intended a third volume (a "Third Treatise" addressed to Theophilus) covering Paul's last years and the early post-apostolic church; the open ending anticipates a continuation that was never written or has been lost. This is a minority view with limited textual evidence.

**(5) Eschatological** (some patristic and devotional readings): the ending leaves the door open because the church's mission is not yet complete; the gospel continues "without hindrance" through every age until the parousia.

**N. T. Wright**, in *Paul: A Biography*, treats the ending as theologically deliberate and historically open: Wright reads the Acts text as not foreclosing on Paul's eventual death but also not narrating it, and he reconstructs the historical Paul's last years from 1 Clement 5:7 (c. 96 CE: Paul "having reached the limits of the West, and having borne witness before rulers, departed from the world"), Eusebius (*Hist. Eccl.* 2.25), and the Pastoral Epistles (especially 2 Timothy 4:6-7), suggesting Paul was released after the two-year custody, traveled westward (possibly to Spain, fulfilling Romans 15:24), was rearrested in the mid-60s during the Neronian persecution, and was executed by beheading at Rome (Roman citizens were not crucified). **F. F. Bruce**'s *Paul: Apostle of the Heart Set Free* offers a similar reconstruction. **Helmut Koester**'s *Introduction to the New Testament* is more cautious about the post-Acts reconstruction but agrees on the basic outlines: release, further travel, rearrest, execution.

The **Tacitus passage** (*Annals* 15.44), describing Nero's persecution of Christians at Rome in the aftermath of the great fire of 64 CE, is the most important non-Christian source for the historical context of Paul's likely death: "*ergo abolendo rumori Nero subdidit reos et quaesitissimis poenis adfecit, quos per flagitia invisos vulgus Christianos appellabat*"—"to scotch the rumor [that he had set the fire], Nero substituted as culprits, and punished with the utmost refinements of cruelty, a class of men, loathed for their vices, whom the crowd styled Christians." Whether Paul's execution occurred in this 64 CE persecution or in a separate proceeding is debated, but the persecution provides the historical horizon for the silence at Acts 28.

The Jewish-interpretive trajectory engages chapter 28 with particular intensity because of the Isaiah 6 citation. **Adele Reinhartz** has argued that the Lukan use of Isaiah 6:9-10 to interpret the partial Jewish rejection is precisely the kind of move that Christianized Israel's prophetic tradition into an anti-Jewish weapon: the prophet who speaks against the Jews is appropriated by the gospel writer to indict the Jews. **Daniel Boyarin**'s broader work on the parting of the ways treats Acts 28 as a key data point in the gradual Jewish-Christian separation. **Pamela Eisenbaum**, **Matthew Thiessen**, and **Paula Fredriksen** read 28:28 ("this salvation of God has been sent to the Gentiles; they will listen") more carefully: the *kai autoi akousontai* ("they will listen") does not entail the abolition of the Jewish covenant; it announces the Gentile inclusion that has been part of Israel's prophetic hope from Isaiah 49 onward. **Tannehill** argues this is the Lukan view as well: the Jewish mission is not closed at 28:28 (Acts has narrated multiple "we turn to the Gentiles" moments before, all followed by continued Jewish ministry); the ending is rhetorical, not finally supersessionist.

Patristic interpretation focused most often on Paul's house-arrest hospitality. **Chrysostom** in his *Homilies on Acts* (Hom. 55, the final homily) read the ending as a deliberate triumph: the apostle in chains is more effective than the apostle in freedom; the gospel's progress is independent of the apostle's circumstances. Bede follows. **Calvin** in his *Commentary on Acts* treats *akolyto*s* as the chapter's hermeneutic key: nothing—neither chains, nor distance, nor imperial power, nor Jewish opposition—finally hinders the gospel.

Modern commentary has converged on the *akolyto*s* reading. **Bruce NICNT**, **Gaventa Abingdon**, **Johnson Sacra Pagina**, **Witherington III**, **Schnabel ZECNT**, **Peterson Pillar** all read the final adverb as the programmatic Lukan signature. **Marguerat** treats it as the climactic Lukan claim about the gospel's unhindered progress. **Keener** offers extensive reception-historical engagement. **Pervo** reads the ending more skeptically as Lukan literary cleverness rather than theological substance.

**Peter Lampe**'s *From Paul to Valentinus: Christians at Rome in the First Two Centuries* is essential here for understanding the Roman church Paul entered. Lampe's prosopographical and archaeological reconstruction shows a community concentrated in two main neighborhoods (Trastevere and the Aventine), spanning multiple house-church units, predominantly Greek-speaking, with significant Jewish-Christian and Gentile-Christian elements in tension—precisely the community Romans 14-15 addresses.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Does Acts 28:28 close the door on the Jewish mission?** Three positions. (1) The *terminus / supersessionist* reading (much patristic, Reformation, and traditional Catholic and Protestant exegesis): the chapter records the final, definitive turn from Israel to the Gentiles; the Jewish phase of the mission is closed; the gospel goes to the nations because Israel has finally rejected it. (2) The *partial / rhetorical-pivot* reading (Tannehill, Marguerat, Marshall): Paul has made similar declarations before (13:46; 18:6) and continued Jewish ministry afterward; the 28:28 statement is rhetorical conclusion to the immediate scene, not theological closure on the Jewish mission; the gospel continues to be offered to Israel. (3) The *Pauline-letter / continuing-Israel* reading (Eisenbaum, Thiessen, Fredriksen, drawing on Romans 9-11): Paul's own theology in Romans 11 holds that "all Israel will be saved" and the Gentile inclusion is *the means* of Israel's eventual reawakening; Acts 28:28 must be read against this Pauline framework, not against later Christian supersessionism.

Steelman: the partial / rhetorical-pivot reading does the most justice to the Lukan narrative pattern (the prior 13:46 and 18:6 turns) without flattening the sharpness of the 28:28 statement. The Pauline-letter reading is the most theologically generous but requires importing Romans 9-11 into the Lukan voice.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter's force is not finally about which ethnic group receives the gospel; it is about the gospel's unhindered progress wherever it goes. The Jewish mission and the Gentile mission are not zero-sum. Luke's pastoral concern is for the gospel's continuation, not for its terminal redirection.

**Tension 2: Why does Luke end without narrating Paul's death?** Three positions. (1) The *programmatic open ending* (Tannehill, Marguerat, Parsons, dominant contemporary view): the open ending is the theological climax; the gospel continues "without hindrance" beyond the apostle, and the reader inherits the unfinished mission. (2) The *apologetic-political* (Conzelmann, Haenchen, Maddox): the ending obscures the political-legal reality of Paul's execution to preserve the Lukan apologetic for Roman tolerance of Christianity. (3) The *historical-realist* (Bruce, Marshall, in earlier scholarship): Luke ends where his narrative material ends; nothing more should be read into the silence.

Steelman: the programmatic-open-ending reading has the strongest internal evidence—the deliberate placement of *akolyto*s*, the parallel structure with the Caesarean two-year detention, the unmistakable sense of arrival-as-beginning—and is the dominant contemporary view.

**Where this leaves us:** Luke ends with a door open. The kingdom of God is being proclaimed in Rome, the imperial capital, by an apostle in chains, with full boldness and without hindrance. Whatever happens to Paul next, the gospel's progress is not contingent on him. This is the chapter's core theological claim, and it is also the canonical bridge into the Pauline letters and the New Testament epistles, which carry the post-Acts story forward.

**One unresolved question:** Did Paul reach Spain? Romans 15:24, 28 expresses his intention—"I will set out by way of you to Spain"—and 1 Clement 5:7 enigmatically reports that he reached "the limits of the West" (*to terma tēs duseōs*), which Eastern readers might have understood as Rome itself but which Roman readers (including Clement, writing from Rome around 96) likely meant as the Iberian peninsula. The Pastoral Epistles and the Muratorian Fragment (c. 170 CE: "the journey of Paul as he went from the city to Spain") preserve hints. The historical question remains genuinely open.

## The Thread

The "two whole years" of 28:30 deliberately mirrors the "two whole years" of 24:27 (Caesarean detention), framing Paul's last four public years in symmetric biennial confinement. The "without hindrance" (*akolyto*s*) of 28:31 picks up the Acts 1:8 programmatic word ("you will be my witnesses... to the end of the earth") and finalizes it: the gospel has reached the imperial capital and is preached unhindered. Cross-reference Romans 15:14-33 for Paul's pre-Roman travel intentions; cross-reference Philippians 1:12-18 for Paul's later self-interpretation of the Roman house arrest ("what has happened to me has really served to advance the gospel"); cross-reference 2 Timothy 4:6-7 for the traditional pre-execution voice of Paul; cross-reference 1 Clement 5:7, Tacitus *Annals* 15.44, and Eusebius *Hist. Eccl.* 2.25 for the historical horizon of Paul's likely death under Nero. Flag the Isaiah 6:9-10 citation as the most-cited prophetic text in the New Testament (Mark 4:12; Matthew 13:14-15; Luke 8:10; John 12:40; Romans 11:8)—Luke's deployment in 28:26-27 is the climactic Christian use.
