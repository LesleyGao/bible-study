# Psalm 100 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 100 stands as one of the shortest yet most universally beloved psalms in the Psalter, occupying a singular position both in the canonical structure of Book IV and in the liturgical life of synagogue and church. Its superscription, sparse and famously distinctive, reads *mizmor le-todah* (מִזְמוֹר לְתוֹדָה), conventionally rendered "A psalm. For thanksgiving" or "A psalm for the thank offering." This is the only superscription in the entire Psalter that bears this designation, marking Psalm 100 as a uniquely cultic composition tied — at least in its earliest liturgical setting — to the *todah* sacrifice prescribed in Leviticus 7:11–15. Hermann Gunkel, in his foundational *Einleitung in die Psalmen* (1933), classified Psalm 100 unambiguously as a *Hymnus* — a hymn of praise — and more specifically as a processional hymn (*Prozessionslied*), citing the imperatival summons to "enter his gates" (*bo'u she'arav be-todah*, בֹּאוּ שְׁעָרָיו בְּתוֹדָה, v. 4) as evidence of a liturgical procession into the temple courts. Sigmund Mowinckel, building on Gunkel in *The Psalms in Israel's Worship* (1962), pressed further, situating Psalm 100 within the cultic life of the autumn New Year festival and reading it as a doxological capstone to the *Yahweh-malak* enthronement cycle (Psalms 93, 95–99).

The placement matters. Psalm 100 closes the cluster of "the LORD reigns" psalms (*Yahweh malakh*, יְהוָה מָלָךְ) that dominates the central section of Book IV. Where Psalms 93–99 announce divine kingship in increasingly cosmic and historical registers — culminating in Psalm 99's threefold *qadosh hu* ("holy is he") — Psalm 100 functions as the responsive doxology, the people's answer to the proclamation of God's reign. James L. Mays, in *The Lord Reigns* (1994), called this the "doxological *amen*" of the kingship cluster. Erhard Gerstenberger, in his *Forms of the Old Testament Literature* commentary (2001), reads it as a communal thanksgiving liturgy with a clear processional Sitz im Leben.

Dating is contested. The vocabulary is archaic and the form simple, suggesting pre-exilic temple use; but the universal scope ("all the earth," *kol-ha'aretz*, כָּל־הָאָרֶץ, v. 1) and the placement within Book IV — which Gerald Wilson's seminal *The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* (1985) demonstrated was redactionally shaped as a post-exilic response to the failure of the Davidic monarchy — point to a final form crystallized after the return from Babylon. Goldingay (*Psalms*, vol. 3, 2008) and Hossfeld–Zenger (*Psalmen 51–100*, 2000) both treat the present text as a post-exilic composition or redaction that draws on older cultic material. Tate's *Word Biblical Commentary* (1990) splits the difference, treating the core as ancient and the framing as later editorial.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels are illuminating. Mesopotamian *šuilla* hymns and Egyptian temple processionals share the imperatival-praise pattern, and the universal summons "all the earth" finds parallel in the Akkadian *Enuma Elish* and in royal hymns to Marduk. But the Israelite distinctive — that the universal call is grounded not in Yahweh's military victory but in his creative making (*hu asanu*, הוּא עָשָׂנוּ, "it is he who made us," v. 3) and covenantal faithfulness (*emunato*, אֱמוּנָתוֹ, v. 5) — sets the psalm apart. The Hebrew terms reward attention. *Hari'u la-Yahweh kol-ha'aretz* (הָרִיעוּ לַיהוָה כָּל־הָאָרֶץ) opens with a verb of acclamatory shouting, the same root used for the war-shout (*teru'ah*) and for the blowing of the shofar at coronations. *Ivdu et-Yahweh be-simchah* (עִבְדוּ אֶת־יְהוָה בְּשִׂמְחָה, v. 2) deploys *avad*, the verb of cultic service that elsewhere describes priestly duty and covenantal obligation. *Tzon mar'ito* (צֹאן מַרְעִיתוֹ, v. 3, "sheep of his pasture") evokes the shepherd-king imagery of Psalm 23 and the royal pastoral idiom found across the ANE.

Textually, the Masoretic Text is stable. The Septuagint (LXX) renders the psalm faithfully, with one significant variant at v. 3: where the MT *Ketiv* reads *velo anachnu* (וְלֹא אֲנַחְנוּ, "and not we ourselves") and the *Qere* reads *velo anachnu* (וְלוֹ אֲנַחְנוּ, "and we are his"), the LXX (*kai ouch hēmeis*, "and not we") follows the *Ketiv*. This famous crux generated centuries of theological commentary. The Dead Sea Scrolls evidence is fragmentary; 11QPs-a, the great Qumran psalms scroll, does not preserve Psalm 100 in extant form, though the Qumran community's broader liturgical use of the Psalter strongly suggests its inclusion. The *Targum* expands "all the earth" to "all the inhabitants of the earth," reinforcing the universal-missional reading already implicit in the Hebrew.

## Lens 2: The Literature in Front of Us

The five-verse structure of Psalm 100 is a small marvel of Hebrew poetic craft. Robert Lowth's eighteenth-century categories — synonymous, antithetic, and synthetic parallelism — illuminate the texture, but the psalm rewards more recent close-reading approaches as well. James Kugel's emphasis on the "A, what's more B" structure of Hebrew parallelism is on full display: each colon advances the previous rather than merely repeating it.

The psalm divides into two strophes of unequal length, each marked by imperatival clusters. Strophe one (vv. 1–3) summons the worshipers with three imperatives — *hari'u* ("make a joyful noise"), *ivdu* ("serve"), *bo'u* ("come") — and grounds the summons in the theological declaration of v. 3: *de'u ki Yahweh hu Elohim* (דְּעוּ כִּי־יְהוָה הוּא אֱלֹהִים, "know that the LORD, he is God"). Strophe two (vv. 4–5) repeats the imperatival pattern with *bo'u* ("enter"), *hodu* ("give thanks"), *barakhu* ("bless"), and grounds these in the covenantal triad of v. 5: *tov Yahweh, le-olam chasdo, ve-ad dor va-dor emunato* (טוֹב יְהוָה לְעוֹלָם חַסְדּוֹ וְעַד דֹּר וָדֹר אֱמוּנָתוֹ, "the LORD is good; his steadfast love endures forever, and his faithfulness to all generations"). The seven imperatives total — a number not incidental in Hebrew composition — frame two declarative cores. There is no *selah*. The brevity is the point.

Inclusio operates subtly: the opening *kol-ha'aretz* ("all the earth," v. 1) finds completion in the closing *dor va-dor* ("generation to generation," v. 5) — universal in space, universal in time. The middle declaration *hu asanu ve-lo anachnu* ("it is he who made us, and we are his") sits at the structural center. Wilfred Watson's *Classical Hebrew Poetry* (1984) identifies the chiastic possibility: praise → reason → praise → reason, with the central pivot on identity ("we are his people").

The triadic ending — *tov / chesed / emunah* (good / steadfast love / faithfulness) — is one of the densest theological compressions in the Psalter, echoing the Exodus 34:6 attribute formula in compressed form. Goldingay notes that this triad recurs as a kind of liturgical signature across Book IV (compare Psalm 89:1–2; 92:2; 103:8). The psalm's brevity is not poverty but distillation.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story — Jewish and Christian Trajectories

Within Book IV's redactional logic, Psalm 100 functions as the doxological seal of the *Yahweh-malak* cluster. Wilson's thesis — that Book IV (Psalms 90–106) responds to the failure of the Davidic covenant lamented at the end of Book III (Psalm 89) by relocating Israel's hope from the Davidic king to Yahweh's eternal kingship — gives Psalm 100 its canonical weight. Where Book III ends in covenantal crisis, Book IV answers: *Yahweh malakh*, the LORD reigns; and Psalm 100 is the people's *amen*.

In the Jewish trajectory, Psalm 100 acquired exceptional liturgical prominence. The *Mishnah* (Shevu'ot 15b) connects it to the *todah* sacrifice. *Midrash Tehillim* (Buber edition) preserves homiletic traditions reading the psalm as the song the Levites sang when the *todah* offering was brought, and as a song that will alone survive in the messianic age — a tradition Rashi cites, drawing on *Vayikra Rabbah* 9:7. Rashi's commentary glosses *hu asanu ve-lo anachnu* by following the *Ketiv* ("not we ourselves") and reading it as a polemic against self-made identity. Ibn Ezra prefers the *Qere* ("we are his"), reading the psalm as a covenantal confession. Radak (David Kimhi) harmonizes both readings, suggesting the deliberate ambiguity functions homiletically. In modern Jewish reception, Martin Buber (*Right and Wrong*, 1952) read the psalm as the paradigmatic moment of I-Thou encounter in collective worship; Abraham Joshua Heschel (*Man's Quest for God*, 1954) cited it as the model of liturgical "wonder." Jon Levenson (*Sinai and Zion*, 1985) situates the universal call within Israel's particular covenantal vocation. Nahum Sarna's *On the Book of Psalms* (1993) emphasizes the *todah* connection and the way the psalm invites all nations into Israel's distinctive worship.

Liturgically, Psalm 100 is recited in the *Pesukei de-Zimrah* of the daily *Shacharit* service in the Ashkenazi rite (omitted on Shabbat, festivals, and the eve of Passover and Yom Kippur — precisely because the *todah* offering was not brought on those days, a beautiful liturgical-theological consistency). It is, alongside *Ashrei*, one of the most-recited psalms in observant Jewish life.

The Christian trajectory is equally rich. Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 99 (LXX = MT 100) reads the universal call missionally, as the gospel summons to all nations, and dwells extensively on *ipse fecit nos et non ipsi nos* ("he made us and not we ourselves") as a foundational anti-Pelagian text — a verse Augustine returns to repeatedly in *De Civitate Dei* and the anti-Pelagian writings. Athanasius, in his *Letter to Marcellinus*, prescribes Psalm 100 for thanksgiving and processional use. Cassiodorus reads it as the church's universal hymn. Aquinas, in his *Postilla super Psalmos*, treats v. 3 as a key text for the doctrine of creation and divine providence.

The Reformation gave Psalm 100 perhaps its most enduring afterlife. Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* and his German psalm paraphrases set the tone for Reformed metrical psalmody. But the decisive moment came in 1561, when the Scotsman William Kethe, working with the Marian exiles in Geneva, produced the metrical paraphrase "All people that on earth do dwell" for the Anglo-Genevan Psalter. Set to the tune *Old Hundredth* — composed by Louis Bourgeois for Théodore de Bèze's French metrical psalter — Kethe's version became the most enduring metrical psalm in the English-speaking Reformed tradition. The tune *Old Hundredth* itself acquired iconic status, eventually becoming the standard musical setting for Thomas Ken's seventeenth-century doxology ("Praise God, from whom all blessings flow"), so that the very name of Psalm 100 became metonymous with the doxological act in Protestant worship. Calvin's 1557 commentary on the Psalms reads Psalm 100 as a hymn of grateful covenantal acknowledgment, emphasizing v. 3 as the doctrinal heart: God made us; we did not make ourselves; we are therefore his.

The Geneva Psalter, the Scottish Psalter (1650), the Bay Psalm Book (1640), and the Dutch *Genevan Psalter* tradition all gave Psalm 100 prominent place. The shaping effect on Reformed worship is incalculable — and worth being honest about. Reformed metrical psalmody simultaneously democratized the Psalter (giving it back to the congregation in the vernacular and in singable form) and constrained it (subjecting Hebrew poetry to the Procrustean bed of common-meter and long-meter English versification). What was gained in accessibility was sometimes lost in poetic texture; what was preserved in theological substance was sometimes attenuated in liturgical particularity. The *Old Hundredth* tradition is one of Christian worship's great treasures and one of its great trade-offs.

Modern critical scholarship on Psalm 100 has been substantial despite its brevity. Westermann (*Praise and Lament in the Psalms*, 1981) classified it as a "descriptive psalm of praise" and emphasized its place in his typology. Hans-Joachim Kraus (*Psalms 60–150*, 1978) treats it as a processional hymn for the autumn festival. Hossfeld–Zenger (*Psalmen 51–100*, 2000) emphasize the post-exilic redactional placement and the universalist trajectory. Goldingay reads the psalm as a "summary statement of Israel's faith." Walter Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*, 1984; *The Psalms and the Life of Faith*, 1995) places it in his typology of "psalms of orientation" and notes how its unembarrassed praise sits in productive tension with the laments that surround it in Book IV. Patrick Miller (*Interpreting the Psalms*, 1986) emphasizes its catechetical force. William Brown (*Psalms*, 2010; *Seeing the Psalms*, 2002) explores the "sheep of his pasture" imagery as a sustained pastoral metaphor. Beth Tanner (in *The Book of Psalms*, NICOT, 2014, with deClaissé-Walford and Jacobson) treats it as the doxological response to the *Yahweh-malak* claim.

NT use is allusive rather than direct. There is no formal NT citation of Psalm 100, but its universal-missional summons echoes through Pauline mission theology (Romans 15:9–12) and through the universal worship visions of Revelation 7:9–10 and 15:3–4.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — Where the Tension Lives

The first tension Psalm 100 raises is the tension of the universal call. *Hari'u la-Yahweh kol-ha'aretz* — "make a joyful noise to the LORD, all the earth." This is not a parochial summons. It addresses the nations. But what does it mean for "all the earth" to praise *Yahweh*, the particular God of Israel?

One position, advanced robustly by Christian missional readers from Augustine through David Bosch (*Transforming Mission*, 1991) and Christopher Wright (*The Mission of God*, 2006), reads the universal call as the gospel summons in nuce: all nations are invited into the worship of the one true God, and that invitation is fulfilled in Christ and the church's mission. The steelman: the psalm itself is unembarrassedly universal in scope; the redactional placement in Book IV (post-exilic) presupposes a vision of Yahweh as creator-king of the whole earth; and the NT echoes (Romans 15, Revelation 7) confirm that Israel's scriptures themselves anticipate the gathering of the nations.

A second position, articulated by Jewish scholars like Levenson and by Christian scholars working in interfaith register (Walter Brueggemann's later work; Ellen Davis), insists that the universal call does not erase Israel's particularity. The nations are summoned to praise *Yahweh* — not a generic deity — and they enter the worship of Israel's God on Israel's terms. Levenson's *Sinai and Zion* makes the case forcefully: universalism without particularity becomes imperial; particularity without universalism becomes tribal. The psalm holds both.

A third consideration, which any honest reader has to weigh, is the question of *use*: whose voice issued the universal summons in practice, and whose worship got normalized as the standard. The reception history of Christian metrical psalmody is not innocent — *Old Hundredth* was sung in the chapels of slave plantations, in colonial outposts, in residential schools, and the universal call was at times instrumentalized to mask cultural conquest. The psalm did not authorize that; but reading Psalm 100 honestly requires reckoning with how it has been put to work, and noticing that the psalm's own grounding — "he made us," not "we made you" — cuts against every such instrumentalization.

The second tension is the tension of brevity and depth. Psalm 100 is five verses. It contains no lament, no enemies, no curses, no historical recital, no extended theological argument. It is a doxology, full stop. Some readers — Brueggemann among them, in his more critical moods — worry that a Christianity over-shaped by Psalm 100 and the *Old Hundredth* doxology becomes a Christianity that cannot lament, that flattens the Psalter's full emotional and theological range into perpetual praise. The steelman: the Psalter itself contains far more lament than praise (over a third are laments by Westermann's count), and a worshiping community that knows only Psalm 100 is a community that has not yet learned to sit with Psalm 88.

The counter-position — articulated by Patrick Miller and by the broader Reformed tradition — is that Psalm 100's brevity is not poverty but distillation. The triad *tov / chesed / emunah* in v. 5 compresses the entire covenantal theology of the Hebrew Bible into seven Hebrew words. The brief doxological psalms (100, 117, 150) function as theological anchors, not as substitutes for the laments. The *Old Hundredth* tradition has not displaced the laments; it has framed them.

Where this leaves us is something like this: Psalm 100 is the Psalter's small invitation to the largest possible worship. It opens its arms to "all the earth" and grounds the invitation not in coercion but in a confession — *hu asanu*, "he made us." The making precedes the praising. The covenantal triad of v. 5 is the warrant: God is good, his *chesed* endures forever, his *emunah* runs from generation to generation. The metrical-psalmody tradition has carried this confession across five centuries of Reformed worship, with all the gains and trade-offs that carrying entails. The psalm remains an unembarrassed gift, and an unembarrassed challenge.

The closing question is not whether to sing the *Old Hundredth* — Christians and Jews who pray this psalm should sing it freely — but how to sing it without forgetting the laments that surround it in Book IV, the histories that have weaponized it, and the particularity of the God whose universal praise it invokes. The summons is to all the earth. The grounding is in the One who made us. The praise is the people's answer.
