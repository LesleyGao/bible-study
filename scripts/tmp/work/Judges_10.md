# Judges 10: God's Soul Grew Short

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Judges 10 is a hinge chapter — a chapter that does very little and means a great deal. It opens with two short notices about minor judges (Tola and Jair), pivots through an extended apostasy report, gives us one of the most theologically extraordinary passages in the Hebrew Bible (vv. 13–16), and ends with the assembly that will summon Jephthah in chapter 11. The historical world it sketches is the world of the late Iron I Transjordan and central highlands, with a coalition of Ammonite raiders pressing across the Jordan into Gilead and threatening Judah, Benjamin, and Ephraim.

The Ammonites in question are the kingdom centered at Rabbah of the Ammonites (modern Amman), descendants in the Genesis 19 genealogy of Lot through his younger daughter, persistent regional rivals of Israel from the time of the conquest narratives onward. Archaeologically the Ammonite polity is documented by the citadel of Amman (Jebel al-Qalʿa), by the Ammonite royal inscriptions of the ninth and eighth centuries BCE (the Amman Citadel inscription, the Amman Theatre inscription, the Tell Siran bottle inscription), and by the cluster of fortified Iron Age sites that protected the Ammonite plateau. The historical depth of Ammonite-Israelite conflict is thus well-attested; the Iron I context for the Judges 10–11 events is more difficult to pin down archaeologically because Iron I Transjordan settlement is sparse, but the political pattern (Ammonite expansion against Gileadite tribal territory) is consistent with what Larry Herr and Ø. LaBianca have documented for the Madaba Plains region.

The "eighteen years" of oppression in v. 8 is a specifically Transjordan figure — "all the people of Israel who were beyond the Jordan in the land of the Amorites, which is in Gilead." The text then in v. 9 escalates: the Ammonites cross the Jordan to fight against Judah, Benjamin, and Ephraim, putting Israel in "severe distress." The narrator is preserving a historically plausible memory of an Ammonite expansion that began as Transjordan raiding and grew into a Cisjordan threat. The cumulative pressure of this oppression is what produces the Israelite cry to YHWH.

The minor-judge notices (vv. 1–5) are formally distinct from the major-judge narratives. Tola the son of Puah, son of Dodo (a Manasseh tribesman residing in Ephraim) "judged" twenty-three years; Jair the Gileadite judged twenty-two years and had thirty sons who rode thirty donkeys and held thirty cities (the *Havvoth-Jair*). These are dynastic-administrative notices, not deliverance narratives. Susan Niditch's work on the minor-judge lists argues that they preserve actual administrative memory of regional headmen whose role was juridical-political rather than military; the formulaic pattern (name, location, years served, burial site) is the editorial signature of an old list incorporated into the deuteronomistic framework. The detail of Jair's "thirty sons riding thirty donkeys with thirty cities" is realistic Iron I tribal-elite material culture: donkey-riding is the prestige transport before mounted cavalry becomes feasible (cf. Judges 5:10's "you who ride on white donkeys" in the Song of Deborah), and the *havvoth* (tent-villages or unwalled settlements) are the small dependent communities clustered around a tribal-elite household.

The list of foreign gods Israel served in v. 6 is the most exhaustive in Judges and is geographically organized: Baal and Ashtaroth (the generic Canaanite paired deities), the gods of Syria (probably Hadad and Atargatis), the gods of Sidon (Baal-Shamem, Eshmun, Astarte), the gods of Moab (Chemosh), the gods of the Ammonites (Milcom/Molech), and the gods of the Philistines (Dagon, with Astarte). The list is nearly a gazetteer of the Levantine pantheon and signals the editor's escalating rhetoric: this is not a single act of apostasy but a comprehensive failure across every direction.

The location names that frame the chapter are precise. Shamir in the hill country of Ephraim (v. 1) is unidentified but probably in the southern Samaria range. Kamon (v. 5), where Jair is buried, has been tentatively identified with Qamm in northern Transjordan. Mizpah of Gilead (v. 17) is distinct from the Benjaminite Mizpah; it is probably Tell Maṣfa or one of the other Mizpah candidates in northern Transjordan, the same Mizpah where Jacob and Laban set up their *galeed* in Genesis 31:49. The narrator is moving across a real political geography.

A handful of Hebrew terms repay attention. **שֹׁפֵט (shophet)** — "judge"; the verb in vv. 2 and 3 (*va-yishpot*) carries the broader sense of "to govern, to lead, to render judicial decisions" — the office is administrative-juridical, not exclusively military. **בְּעָלִים (Beʿalim)**, plural — the Baals, treated as a collective category of foreign deities. **עַשְׁתָּרוֹת (ʿAshtarot)**, plural — the Astartes, similarly collective. **וַתִּקְצַר נַפְשׁוֹ (va-tiqtsar naphsho)** in v. 16 — "and his soul grew short" — is the Hebrew idiom for impatience, exhaustion, the inability to bear something further. The literal "short of soul" is the chapter's most theologically loaded phrase. **חָוֹת יָאִיר (Havvoth-Yair)** — "tent-villages of Jair" — the place-name preserves the toponymic memory of the dynastic notice. **בְּנֵי עַמּוֹן (Bene ʿAmmon)** — "sons of Ammon" — the standard biblical designation for the Ammonites, preserving the genealogical frame from Genesis 19.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Judges 10 is structurally a transition chapter, but its three components — the minor-judge list (vv. 1–5), the apostasy and divine refusal scene (vv. 6–16), and the assembly at Mizpah (vv. 17–18) — are doing distinct literary work. Daniel Block reads the chapter as the pivot from the Gideon-Abimelech cycle to the Jephthah cycle; Yairah Amit reads it as the editor's deliberate slowing of pace before the Jephthah-Samson disaster sequence; Barry Webb reads it as the moment the deuteronomistic framework breaks open and reveals the pathos beneath.

The minor-judge list's literary function is not merely chronological filler. Susan Niditch's *Judges: A Commentary* argues that the lists serve as structural breakwaters between the major narratives, marking the passage of time and signaling the editor's claim that the period of the judges was longer and more variegated than the major narratives alone could convey. The lists also serve theologically: they show that Israel's life continued during periods when no dramatic deliverance was needed, and they remind the reader that the deuteronomistic cycle (sin → oppression → cry → judge → peace → relapse) is an editorial pattern overlaid on a more diverse historical reality.

The apostasy-and-refusal scene of vv. 6–16 is the chapter's literary center and one of the most extraordinary passages in the Hebrew Bible. The pattern up to v. 12 is standard deuteronomistic: Israel sins, Israel is oppressed, Israel cries, Israel confesses. But at v. 13 the pattern breaks. YHWH does not, as in chapters 3 and 6, send a deliverer or a prophet. He says, in effect: I have saved you from the Egyptians, the Amorites, the Ammonites, the Philistines, the Sidonians, the Amalekites, and the Maonites; you have forsaken me and served other gods; therefore I will save you no more. Go cry to the gods you chose; let them save you in your distress. Robert Alter calls this "the most stunning refusal in the Hebrew Bible." The God who has been the deliverer of every cycle in Judges suddenly refuses to be deliverer; he points Israel back to the gods Israel has actually been worshiping and tells them to ask those gods for help.

What follows is more extraordinary still. Israel does not argue the case or invoke the covenant; they say "we have sinned; do to us whatever seems good to you. Only please deliver us this day." They put away their foreign gods and serve YHWH. And then v. 16: *va-tiqtsar naphsho ba-ʿamal Yisrael*, "and his soul grew short over the misery of Israel." The Hebrew idiom is impatience, exhaustion, the inability to bear something any longer; the King James "his soul was grieved" and the ESV "he became impatient" are both attempts to render an idiom that has no clean English equivalent. The literal sense is that God's *nephesh* (life-breath, soul, the seat of feeling) is *shortened* — running out of capacity — by Israel's misery. The grammatical subject is YHWH; the verb is the same one used in Numbers 21:4 of Israel's "soul grew short on the way" and in Judges 16:16 of Samson's "soul was vexed unto death" by Delilah's nagging. God's emotional capacity is being represented in the same idiomatic register as a tired traveler's or an exhausted lover's.

This is the chapter's literary climax. The deuteronomistic editor has set up the cycle with mechanical precision through nine chapters; in the tenth he stops and lets us see God's interior. The refusal in v. 13 and the impatience in v. 16 are not contradictory — they are two moments of the same divine struggle. God refuses, and then God cannot bear the misery of those he has refused. The chapter does not resolve the tension; it simply lets it stand.

The assembly at Mizpah in vv. 17–18 is structurally the setup for chapter 11. The leaders of Gilead pose the question — *who is the man who will begin to fight against the Ammonites?* — and the question hangs unanswered at chapter break. Jephthah will be the answer, but the editor wants us to feel the silence first.

Textually, Judges 10 is comparatively stable. LXX A and LXX B differ at minor points; in v. 16 LXX renders the *va-tiqtsar naphsho* idiom variously, with some witnesses softening the anthropopathism and some preserving it. The Vulgate's *taeduit eum super miseriis* ("he was wearied over the miseries") preserves the emotional sense; English translations have varied widely (KJV "his soul was grieved," NIV "he could bear Israel's misery no longer," NRSV "he could no longer bear to see Israel suffer," ESV "he became impatient"). The translation history reveals translator discomfort with attributing exhaustion to God; the Hebrew is more direct than most English versions allow.

In the larger argument of Judges, chapter 10 is the moment the cycle starts to fail. Gideon's deliverance produced Abimelech; Jephthah's will produce his daughter's death; Samson's will produce destruction without deliverance. The chapter's apostasy list and divine refusal signal that the cycle is no longer working as designed: the apostasy is more comprehensive, the divine response is more strained, and the deliverer the chapter is about to summon will be more compromised. The book is moving toward its own collapse, and chapter 10 is where the editor lets us see the strain in the system.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc that Judges 10 most directly contributes to is the Hebrew Bible's developing theology of divine pathos — the proposition that God is genuinely affected by Israel's covenant fidelity and infidelity, not as an external observer but as a participant. Abraham Heschel's *The Prophets* (1962) made this the central theme of his Jewish theology of revelation, and Heschel cited Judges 10:16 as one of the foundational data points; Terence Fretheim's *The Suffering of God* (1984) built a Christian theology of divine vulnerability on the same passage. The chapter is in conversation with Hosea 11:8 ("How can I give you up, Ephraim?... my heart recoils within me, my compassion grows warm and tender"), Jeremiah 31:20 ("I do remember him still; therefore my heart yearns for him"), Genesis 6:6 ("the LORD was sorry that he had made man on the earth"), and Exodus 32:14 ("the LORD relented from the disaster he had spoken of bringing on his people"). These passages together form a strand within the Hebrew Bible that the philosophical and Hellenistic traditions found difficult to integrate.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Pseudo-Philo (*LAB* ch. 39) treats the divine refusal scene with a long expansion: the *LAB* gives both Israel and YHWH extended speeches, and the divine relenting is presented as the moment Israel's covenant memory is renewed. Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.7.7) softens the divine refusal — Josephus's God is more philosophically respectable and less prone to rhetorical exasperation, and Josephus moves quickly past v. 13 to the Israelite repentance. The Talmud (*Yoma* 86b, *Megillah* 11a) cites Judges 10:16 as evidence that *teshuvah* (repentance) genuinely changes the divine disposition; the rabbinic principle that "great is *teshuvah* that it reaches the throne of glory" finds its scriptural anchor here. Rashi on 10:16 reads the *va-tiqtsar naphsho* as a deliberate divine accommodation: God's "soul" is shortened in a metaphorical sense to model the proper human response to suffering. Radak follows Rashi but more philosophically; Maimonidean negative theology requires that the anthropopathism be read as figurative, but the figurative reading still does theological work. Ralbag treats the passage as a precise theological statement about divine providence: God's "impatience" is the orderly working of providence in response to Israel's repentance, expressed in human idiom because the prophetic medium requires it. Heschel, drawing on the rabbinic tradition but moving past Maimonides, takes the *va-tiqtsar naphsho* literally as evidence that God experiences human suffering: the "divine pathos" is not an anthropomorphism to be explained away but a primary datum of biblical revelation. Levenson's *Sinai and Zion* and his *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* extend Heschel's reading: covenant means that God has bound himself to a people in such a way that their suffering becomes his, and the impassibility doctrine inherited from Greek philosophy is a foreign overlay on biblical revelation. Yair Zakovitch and Yairah Amit treat the passage literarily as the editorial high point of the deuteronomistic theology: the editor has rehearsed the cycle so many times that he can show God himself wearying of it.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Judges* devotes Homily 7 to the apostasy and refusal scene; Origen reads the divine refusal as pedagogical (God's "no" is meant to drive Israel to deeper repentance) and the impatience of v. 16 as figurative. Augustine, in *Confessions* and in his sermons, accepts the figurative reading of divine emotion as required by his doctrine of impassibility but uses Judges 10:16 to explore the depth of the human response God's accommodation invites. The Cappadocian Fathers (Gregory of Nazianzus, Gregory of Nyssa) develop the doctrine of *theopaschism* in carefully circumscribed ways: God in his essence does not suffer, but God in his economy of relation with creation accommodates himself to suffering. Theodoret of Cyrus reads Judges 10:16 within this Cappadocian frame. Aquinas in the *Summa* (I, q. 19, a. 11; q. 21) develops the classical doctrine of divine impassibility with great care; Aquinas would read *va-tiqtsar naphsho* as a *modus loquendi*, a manner of speaking, that signifies the effect of God's eternal will rather than a real change in God. The Reformation tradition is divided: Calvin in his *Institutes* and his sermons preserves a strong doctrine of divine impassibility but allows that scripture's anthropopathic language has real theological content; Luther's reading is more dynamic, with God's "hidden" and "revealed" wills standing in genuine tension. Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* (II/1) reframes the whole question: God's freedom includes the freedom to be affected by what God loves, and the impassibility doctrine inherited from Greek philosophy must be reformed in light of biblical revelation. Jürgen Moltmann's *The Crucified God* (1972) builds a theology of divine suffering that takes Judges 10:16 (along with the cross) as foundational; Moltmann is one of the most explicit modern Christian readers of the chapter. Terence Fretheim's *The Suffering of God* extends Moltmann's reading into the Old Testament more systematically. Walter Brueggemann's *Theology of the Old Testament* makes the divine pathos one of the central motifs of his "testimony and countertestimony" framework; Judges 10:13–16 is a key text. Daniel Block (NICOT) and Barry Webb (NICOT) read the passage more conservatively but acknowledge its theological weight. Both Block and Webb note that the chapter cannot be read apart from the Jephthah-daughter narrative that follows: the divine impatience that produces the Jephthah deliverance also produces the death of Jephthah's daughter, and the chapter's divine pathos is shadowed by the human cost.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Both Jewish and Christian traditions have wrestled with the chapter's anthropopathic language, and the convergence is on the proposition that God is genuinely related to his people in a way that is not merely cognitive. The deepest divergence is on the metaphysics: the rabbinic tradition (Heschel, Levenson) and the modern theological traditions of divine pathos (Moltmann, Fretheim, Brueggemann) take the language as a primary datum of revelation; the classical Christian tradition (Augustine through Aquinas) and parts of the Maimonidean Jewish tradition take the language as figurative within a metaphysical frame that includes impassibility. The Reformed tradition is internally divided. The Orthodox tradition's distinction between God's essence (impassible) and God's energies (in real relation with creation) offers a third path that the Western traditions have not fully appropriated.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What the deuteronomistic editor believed God was doing in chapter 10 is the chapter's central theological question, and the chapter does not give us a tidy answer. It gives us instead a refusal followed by an inability to maintain the refusal — a divine motion the editor has set down as carefully as he can without resolving.

**Tension One: Divine impassibility and divine pathos.** The chapter's *va-tiqtsar naphsho* is the most direct statement in the Hebrew Bible that God's emotional capacity is finite — not absolutely (God is not exhausted in any cosmic sense) but in the sense that God cannot maintain his refusal indefinitely in the face of his people's suffering. The classical Christian tradition (Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin in his more philosophical mood) reads this as figurative: God in his essence is unchanging and impassible; the language of impatience signifies the effect of God's eternal decree as it unfolds in time, not a real change in God's interior life. The Heschel-Moltmann tradition (with Brueggemann, Fretheim, and much modern theology) reads it as a primary datum: God really is affected by what he loves, and the impassibility doctrine is a Greek philosophical overlay that the Hebrew Bible does not authorize. The Orthodox tradition's energies/essence distinction (Maximus the Confessor, Gregory Palamas) offers a third position: God in his essence is impassible, but God in his energies of relation is genuinely affected. The Maimonidean Jewish tradition agrees with the classical Christian reading at the level of metaphysics but allows the figurative language to do real theological work in the life of devotion. The position least intuitive to a modern reader is probably the classical Aquinas-Maimonides one — that the language of divine pathos is figurative and that God's essence is impassible. Steelmanned: a God who could be exhausted by human suffering would not be a God to whom one could finally pray, because his capacity to respond would be limited; the impassibility doctrine protects the proposition that God's love for his people is not a finite resource that can run out. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter does not finally adjudicate between these positions, but it makes one thing impossible to deny — whatever the metaphysics, the Hebrew Bible's God is not a God who watches Israel suffer with detached equanimity, and any theology that produces such a God has lost contact with the text.

**Tension Two: Divine sovereignty and the moral status of divine relenting.** YHWH says in v. 13, "I will save you no more." Israel repents; God's soul grows short; the chapter ends with the assembly at Mizpah looking for a deliverer, and chapter 11 will give them Jephthah. So God did, in fact, save them again. Was the divine refusal a real refusal that was reversed by repentance, or was it a rhetorical move within a sovereign plan that always included the eventual relenting? The Reformed tradition (Calvin, the later Reformed Orthodox) tends toward the second reading: God's sovereignty is absolute, and the appearance of divine relenting is an accommodation to human time-bound experience. The Arminian and Wesleyan traditions, and much of modern open-theist theology (Boyd, Pinnock), read the relenting as genuine: the future is genuinely open, and God's responses are genuinely responsive. The Catholic tradition (Aquinas, the Thomist tradition) preserves divine sovereignty within a doctrine of secondary causation: God's eternal decree includes the response to free human acts, and the relenting is real at the temporal level even though no change occurs in God. The Orthodox tradition broadly aligns with the Catholic position but with greater emphasis on the synergistic dimension of divine-human cooperation. The Jewish tradition (rabbinic, then Heschel, Levenson) preserves the genuine reality of *teshuvah*'s effect on God without committing to a metaphysical theory; the rabbinic principle is that *teshuvah* genuinely changes the divine response. The critical reading (Brettler, Brueggemann) treats the chapter as one of the Hebrew Bible's strongest cases for the proposition that God's responses to Israel are genuinely conditioned by Israel's covenant fidelity. The position least intuitive to a strict Reformed reader is the open-theist one — that God's "I will save you no more" was a real prediction that God then chose to revise in light of Israel's repentance. Steelmanned: a God whose responses are not genuinely conditioned by his people's responses is a God who is not in a real covenant; the covenant frame requires that *teshuvah* matter, and matter in a way that affects what God actually does. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter is comfortable with a tension that systematic theology has spent centuries trying to resolve, and the editor's willingness to leave the tension open is itself part of his theology — he is not interested in solving the problem of divine sovereignty and human freedom, he is interested in showing us a God whose people's misery he cannot bear.

The chapter ends with a question hanging in the air at Mizpah: who will fight the Ammonites and become head of Gilead? The question is military, but it is also theological. If God will not save Israel, who will? And if God's soul has grown short over Israel's misery, what kind of deliverer is he about to send?

## The Thread

The *va-tiqtsar naphsho* idiom in v. 16 recurs in Judges 16:16 of Samson — "his soul was vexed unto death" — and in Numbers 21:4 of Israel — "the soul of the people grew short on the way." When you reach the Samson narrative and feel the verbal echo, remember that the chapter that produced Jephthah began with God himself wearied to the point of soul-shortening; the human and divine exhaustion are described in the same words.

The list of foreign gods in v. 6 (Baals, Ashtaroth, gods of Syria, Sidon, Moab, Ammon, Philistines) is the most exhaustive in Judges and is taken up almost verbatim in Solomon's apostasy in 1 Kings 11:5–7 (where Solomon serves Ashtoreth of Sidon, Milcom of Ammon, Chemosh of Moab). When you read 1 Kings 11, hold Judges 10 next to it; the deuteronomistic historian is signaling that Solomon's apostasy is structurally identical to the apostasy that produced the worst chapter of the judges era, and that the cycle the editor showed failing in chapter 10 will resume failing in the monarchy.

When you get to Hosea 11:8 — "How can I give you up, Ephraim?... my heart recoils within me, my compassion grows warm and tender" — the prophet is articulating in covenant-renewal language what Judges 10:16 articulated in the editorial idiom of the deuteronomistic historian. The Hosea passage is the chapter's theology made into a love poem, and the chapter is the Hosea passage made into political history.

When you get to Romans 9–11 and Paul's anguished argument about Israel's election and possible rejection, the question Paul is wrestling with — has God cast off his people? — is the question Judges 10 raises and refuses to answer cleanly. Paul's *me genoito* ("by no means!") in Romans 11:1 is, at one level, a citation of the deuteronomistic editor's theology: God's soul is too short over his people's misery to abandon them finally, even when his "I will save you no more" is genuinely meant. The chapter is doing more work in the New Testament than its quiet placement in the book of Judges suggests.
