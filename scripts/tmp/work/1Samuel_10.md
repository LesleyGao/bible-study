# 1 Samuel 10: The King Hidden Among the Baggage

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter unfolds in two distinct settings — the private anointing in the outskirts of Ramah (10:1, continuing from 9:27) and the public selection at Mizpah (10:17–27) — and the doubling itself is a historical clue. Halpern (*The Constitution of the Monarchy*) and McCarter (AB) read the two scenes as preserving two distinct legitimation moments characteristic of early Israelite (and broader West Semitic) kingship: a sacral-prophetic anointing that confers *nagid* status, and a public-tribal acclamation that confers *melekh* status. Both are required; neither alone is sufficient. The Mari archives (eighteenth century BCE) and Hittite royal protocols both attest to this double pattern — divine designation followed by communal recognition — and the chapter is showing Israel adopting an ANE constitutional template even as the dtr remains uneasy with the institution.

The geography of the three signs (10:2–6) traces a route from Rachel's tomb through Bethel-area pilgrimage traffic to Gibeath-elohim. קְבֻרַת רָחֵל *qevurat raḥel* (10:2) — Rachel's tomb — is here located בִּגְבוּל בִּנְיָמִן בְּצֶלְצַח *bigvul binyamin bə-tseltsaḥ*, "in the territory of Benjamin at Zelzah." The traditional location of Rachel's tomb on the road to Bethlehem (Gen 35:19, "on the way to Ephrath, that is, Bethlehem") sits in Judah, not Benjamin, and creates a long-running geographical puzzle. McCarter, Klein, and Tsumura all argue that the older tradition placed Rachel's tomb in Benjamin (Jer 31:15 also locates "Rachel weeping" at Ramah, a Benjaminite town), and the Bethlehem identification is a later harmonization. The chapter preserves the older geography. Zelzah's exact location remains unknown.

The Tabor-oak pilgrimage scene (10:3) shows pre-monarchic religious geography in motion: men going up to God at Bethel — one of the central sanctuaries of Iron Age I and pre-Josianic Israel — carrying three young goats, three loaves, and a skin of wine. These are the standard sacrificial elements (cf. Lev 23 festival offerings in miniature), and the detail captures the everyday rhythm of pre-Josianic worship before centralization. גִּבְעַת אֱלֹהִים *Givʿat Elohim* (10:5) — "the hill of God" — is most plausibly a reference to Saul's hometown Gibeah (Tell el-Ful), here designated by its sacral function: a hill with a sanctuary, where the Philistines have stationed a *netsiv* (נְצִיב, garrison or prefect, 10:5). The detail is telling: Saul's anointing as deliverer happens in his own hometown, which is currently under Philistine occupation. The chapter takes the question of Philistine pressure with full historical seriousness.

The Hebrew terms are loaded. צָלַח *tsalaḥ* (10:6, 10:10) — "rush" or "fall upon" — describes the action of the רוּחַ *ruaḥ*, the spirit of YHWH. The verb is used in the Samson narrative (Judg 14:6, 14:19, 15:14) for the same charismatic seizure, and in Saul's later episodes (1 Sam 11:6, 16:13, 18:10). The pre-classical form *tsalḥah* preserved in 10:6 is striking — older syntax for an older theology. The "spirit-rushing" of pre-classical prophecy is ecstatic, involuntary, and observable; it is not the indwelling spirit of later prophecy or Pauline pneumatology. McCarter, Tsumura, and especially Joseph Blenkinsopp (*A History of Prophecy in Israel*) and Robert Wilson (*Prophecy and Society*) treat this as evidence of an older "prophetic guild" tradition with ANE parallels in Mari prophecy texts and broader West Semitic ecstatic religion.

The instruments listed in 10:5 — נֵבֶל *nevel* (harp/lute), תֹּף *tof* (tambourine), חָלִיל *ḥalil* (flute or pipe), כִּנּוֹר *kinnor* (lyre) — give material specificity to a *band of prophets* whose practice involved music-induced ecstatic possession. This is corroborated by 2 Kings 3:15, where Elisha calls for a musician and the hand of YHWH comes upon him through music. The sociology is clear: in pre-classical Israel, prophecy could be a guild practice, transmitted through music and group ecstasy, recognizable to bystanders.

בַּכֵּלִים *bakkelim* (10:22) — "among the baggage" or "among the equipment" — is the chapter's most pointed literary detail and one of the more historically interesting words. *Kelim* covers any vessels or gear; here it is the assembled mass of supplies, packs, and weapons of an Iron Age tribal assembly. Saul has hidden among the *stuff*. The detail is not flattering. Robert Alter notes that "the chosen one hides among the baggage" is one of the chapter's defining literary images and a deliberate inversion of the kingly archetype.

בְּלִיָּעַל *bəliyyaʿal* (10:27) — "worthless," literally "without-worth" — is a term that will become the dtr's standard slur for political dissidents (cf. 1 Sam 2:12 of Eli's sons; 1 Kings 21:10, 13 of Naboth's accusers). The term may etymologically relate to "swallowing" or "destruction" and in later Second Temple usage it becomes a name for the satanic adversary (cf. *Belial* in 2 Cor 6:15). At this stage, however, it is simply a term of moral disqualification. The narrator is on Saul's side at 10:27 — but as the narrative will show, the *bəney bəliyyaʿal* may have been right.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: this chapter combines installation narrative, sign-fulfillment sequence, and assembly-by-lot ritual. The structure is carefully engineered. The opening unit (10:1–8) is Samuel's private investiture of Saul, with three predictive signs and a final command to wait at Gilgal. The second unit (10:9–13) reports the fulfillment of the third and most dramatic sign and its public reception. The third unit (10:14–16) returns to Saul's domestic life and his uncle's question, and Saul's significant *evasion*. The fourth unit (10:17–27) is the public selection at Mizpah by lot and the people's acclamation, with a final note of dissent.

The literary devices are dense and pointed. The anointing itself (10:1) — Samuel takes a פַּךְ הַשֶּׁמֶן *pakh ha-shemen*, a small vial of oil, and pours it on Saul's head and kisses him — is liturgically minimal compared to David's later anointing (1 Sam 16:13, where the horn-of-oil is used) and to the elaborate priestly investiture of Lev 8. The *kiss* is a covenantal-political gesture (cf. Ps 2:12, "kiss the son lest he be angry"), familiar from ANE vassal treaties. The smallness of the vial is itself a literary signal: the gesture is provisional. Robert Alter notes that the Hebrew uses *pakh* (a small flask) rather than *qeren* (a horn) — a deliberate diminution, in contrast to David's later horn-flask. The dtr is signaling that Saul's kingship is contingent.

The threefold sign sequence (10:2–6) is structurally pedagogical: the first sign confirms knowledge (the donkeys are found; YHWH is omniscient), the second sign confers provision (the men give Saul two loaves; YHWH is providential), the third sign produces transformation (the Spirit rushes; YHWH is empowering). Each sign comes true (10:9 explicitly summarizes "all these signs came to pass that day"). The careful staging echoes Moses' three signs at Sinai (Exod 4:1–9) — the rod becoming a serpent, the leprous hand, the water becoming blood — and Gideon's signs (Judg 6:36–40). The dtr is establishing that Saul is in the *moshiaʿ*-charismatic lineage.

The proverb "Is Saul also among the prophets?" (הֲגַם שָׁאוּל בַּנְּבִיאִים, 10:11–12) is the chapter's most famous line and the most literarily reflective. The narrator pauses to mark the saying as proverbial — the etiology is explicit. The same proverb recurs in 1 Sam 19:24, where Saul's prophesying is no longer charismatic empowerment but a stripping, naked seizure that ends his pursuit of David. The double use of the proverb, once at the chapter's coronation moment and once at Saul's nadir, is one of the dtr's most pointed literary inclusio devices. The proverb begins as a marker of divine empowerment and ends as a marker of divine derangement.

The lot-selection at Mizpah (10:20–22) is the chapter's other defining scene. The procedure is the same as Joshua 7's exposure of Achan: tribes by lot, then clans, then families, then individuals. The narrative parallel is pointed and ironic. Achan was selected by lot to be *executed*; Saul is selected by lot to be *crowned*. The dtr is staging Saul's elevation in the same procedural form as Achan's destruction — and the typological echo is not accidental. Polzin and Brueggemann both read the parallel as foreboding: the man chosen by the same procedure that exposed the nation's first sinner is the man whose kingship will end in his destruction at Gilboa.

The hiding of Saul among the baggage (10:22) is the chapter's defining image. The chosen one hides. The verse stages an irony that will dominate Saul's reign — divinely elevated, personally evasive. Bar-Efrat reads the *bakkelim* moment as the chapter's psychological key: Saul is not a self-confident charismatic; he is reluctant, perhaps terrified. His public emergence at 10:23 is mediated by others ("they ran and took him from there") rather than initiated by himself. The "tall from his shoulders upward" detail is repeated from 9:2 — the chapter is reminding us that Saul *looks* like a king while behaving like a man hiding among supplies.

Textually the chapter is significantly enriched by the LXX and 4QSam-a. The LXX of 10:1 includes a much longer anointing speech with explicit Philistine deliverance and a longer sign-list. McCarter (AB) and Frank Moore Cross both treated the longer Greek reading as plausibly original, with the MT preserving a haplographic loss (the scribe's eye skipped from one similar phrase to another). 4QSam-a preserves part of the longer reading. This is one of the most significant text-critical loci in 1 Samuel — the MT may be a shortened text. There is also a notable LXX expansion before 11:1 (the Nahash-Ammonite background, which 4QSam-a includes) that materially affects the chapter 11 narrative.

Within the book's argument, chapter 10 completes the institution of monarchy. Chapter 8 was the demand, chapter 9 was the providential meeting, chapter 10 is the formal investiture. Chapter 11 (Saul's victory over Ammon at Jabesh-gilead) will be the public confirmation. Chapter 12 (Samuel's farewell speech) closes the installation cycle. The dtr has carefully woven together pro-monarchic and anti-monarchic elements through these five chapters; the contradictions remain unresolved at the literary surface and that is the point.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Canonically, 1 Samuel 10 is the chapter that institutes Israelite monarchy and therefore stands as the constitutional precedent for the whole Davidic-messianic tradition that follows. The chapter looks backward to Joshua 7 (lot exposure of Achan), Judges 6–8 (Gideon's reluctant deliverance), and the Mosaic king-law of Deut 17. It looks forward to 1 Sam 16 (David's anointing), 2 Sam 7 (Davidic covenant), Ps 2 ("the LORD's anointed"), Isa 11 (the messianic shoot), and ultimately the Christian and Jewish messianic horizons — the *christos* and *mashiaḥ* traditions that take their name from precisely this kind of oil-anointing.

The chapter also sits at the headwaters of the *prophetic guild* tradition that will run through the schools of Elisha and Elijah, into the classical writing prophets, and finally into the New Testament's "spirit-rushing" pneumatology of Acts 2 (where the disciples speak in tongues and bystanders ask "What does this mean?" — a question whose original form is "Is Saul also among the prophets?"). The anthropological pattern of ecstatic prophetic empowerment is, in the canonical arc, a single tradition that begins with the band at Gibeath-elohim and ends with the upper room at Pentecost.

**Jewish trajectory.** Josephus (*Antiquities* 6.4.1, 6.5.1) gives the chapter a Hellenistic narrative polish, smoothing the ecstatic prophesying into something more decorous and treating the lot-selection as orderly constitutional procedure. The rabbis of *b. Megillah* 14a and *b. Berakhot* 12b debate Saul's prophesying and the proverb's meaning, generally treating Saul-of-chapter-10 as genuinely empowered and the chapter 19 recurrence as evidence of how the same charism can be perverted. Rashi reads "God gave him another heart" (10:9) as the genuine spiritual transformation that monarchy required, and reads the *bəney bəliyyaʿal* as straightforward dissidents whose later judgment (Saul's amnesty in 11:13) reveals Saul's early generosity. Radak develops a more political reading: the chapter shows how a king's authority must be confirmed by both divine designation and popular consent, and the dissenters at 10:27 reveal that the consent was not unanimous. Ralbag reads the spirit-rushing rationally as an unusual heightening of natural prophetic faculty, accessible to one prepared by divine providence. Don Isaac Abarbanel gives the chapter careful political-theological attention: he reads the lot-selection as YHWH's deliberate procedural transparency, ensuring that the king's selection cannot be attributed to Samuel's preference or the elders' politics; even the choice within the demanded institution is divinely guarded. Yair Zakovitch and Moshe Garsiel attend to the literary artistry of the proverb's double use and the ironic elevation-by-the-same-procedure that destroyed Achan. Robert Alter's *The David Story* gives the *bakkelim* image and the proverb extended literary analysis. Avivah Zornberg reads the chapter's central image — Saul hiding among the supplies — as the psychological emblem of the entire reign: a man elected to a role he is not psychologically equipped to inhabit, and who knows it.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic readers tended to read Saul's anointing typologically. Origen reads the oil as figure of the Spirit's gift, anticipating Christian baptismal anointing. Augustine in *City of God* 17.5–7 treats Saul's anointing as the inferior typological precedent that David and ultimately Christ will fulfill — Saul is the *christos* who fails so that the *Christos* who succeeds may be recognized. Chrysostom's homilies dwell on Saul's modesty in 10:22 and read the hiding-among-the-baggage as evidence of an initial humility later abandoned. Gregory the Great in *Pastoral Rule* uses the *bakkelim* image to warn pastors against the temptation to flee responsibility once divine appointment has been received — the right kind of humility serves the office, the wrong kind hides from it. Aquinas (*ST* II-II.184) cites the chapter on the institution of legitimate authority through divine designation and popular ratification. Calvin's sermons on 1 Samuel 10 are theologically dense: he reads the spirit-rushing as genuine but provisional empowerment that did not regenerate Saul's heart, and he treats the *bakkelim* hiding with surprising sympathy — Saul's reluctance is not ignoble, but it foreshadows the incapacity that will destroy his reign. Luther's marginal glosses are interested in the proverb and the lot procedure; he treats the chapter as evidence that even valid divine appointment requires grace continuously renewed, which Saul will not receive. Among critical scholars, Wellhausen treated the chapter as composite of pro-monarchic source (10:1–16) and anti-monarchic source (10:17–27 with its repeated indictment "you have rejected your God"). McCarter's Anchor Bible commentary remains the standard critical treatment, with detailed text-critical work on the LXX/4QSam-a longer reading at 10:1. Klein (WBC), Hertzberg (OTL), and Tsumura (NICOT) provide standard commentary. Halpern (*Constitution of the Monarchy*) develops the dual-legitimation argument. Brueggemann reads the chapter as the canon's most carefully balanced installation of an institution God simultaneously gives and withholds blessing from. Polzin reads it as the dtr's deliberate construction of constitutional ambivalence. Eugene Peterson treats the *bakkelim* moment as a pastoral parable of vocational fear. Ellen Davis and John Goldingay attend to the chapter's careful ritual sequence. Several literary readers (Polzin, Brueggemann, Alter) note the near-total absence of women from this chapter — only the maidens at Ramah's well in 9:11 mediate the preceding encounter — and read it as the moment Israel's political structure becomes formally centralized and male-presided in a way the more diffuse pre-monarchic order, with its village shrines and local cult traffic, had not been.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is divine selection by lot a confession of human inability to discern, or a divine endorsement of randomness, or a sacred procedure that converts contingency into providence?** The chapter's central act of public selection is by lot (10:20–21), the same mechanism that exposed Achan and that the New Testament will use to choose Matthias (Acts 1:26).

A *traditional Catholic* position would treat the lot as a sacred procedure within which divine providence operates — not random in any deep sense, because YHWH governs the dice. The lot is the ritualized humility of the community that confesses its inability to choose without divine guidance; it is precisely *not* randomness, but the form humility takes when it submits to providence.

A *Reformed* position with Calvin would emphasize the same point in a different register: the lot is providential ordering of contingencies, and the procedure publicly declares that the king is YHWH's choice, not the elders' politics or Samuel's preference. The lot is both ritual and confession.

A *Jewish rationalist* position with Ralbag and Maimonides would distinguish: the lot is a legitimate procedural instrument, and YHWH's providence operates through the natural causal order including the falling of the lots. There is no special suspension of nature; the procedure is sacralized but not magical.

A *critical-anthropological* position would read the lot as a standard ANE divinatory technique (cf. the Mari archives, Mesopotamian extispicy) Israelitized and theologized: the procedure is the same, the framing is YHWH-centric. The chapter sacralizes a common practice.

A *contemporary skeptical or political-theology* position would press: if the lot is random, divine choice is a post-hoc framing; if the lot is rigged by Samuel's prior knowledge (Saul has been anointed privately the day before), the public selection is theatrical. The chapter is constructively ambiguous on which it is.

A *narrative-theological* position (Polzin, Brueggemann) would say the chapter wants both: a real divine choice and a real public ratification, with the lot as the ritual that makes both visible. The narrative does not need to resolve the metaphysics. *Where this leaves us:* the lot's theology is a particular modesty — a procedure that confesses human inability to choose without endorsing a determinism that swallows freedom. The chapter teaches a politics in which legitimate authority is neither self-asserted nor democratically chosen but received through procedures that make divine providence and communal recognition simultaneously visible.

**Tension 2: Was Saul's transformation real?** The text says explicitly that "God gave him another heart" (וַיַּהֲפָךְ־לוֹ אֱלֹהִים לֵב אַחֵר, 10:9) and that "the Spirit of God rushed upon him" (10:10). Yet by chapter 13 he disobeys, by chapter 15 he is rejected, by chapter 16 the Spirit has departed (16:14), and by chapter 31 he dies on Gilboa. What kind of transformation is given and then withdrawn?

A *traditional Catholic* position would distinguish *charismatic* gift from *sanctifying* grace: the Spirit's rushing on Saul was a charism for office, not a regeneration of his soul. The gift was real but functional. Aquinas's distinction between *gratia gratis data* (charism for the church's benefit) and *gratia gratum faciens* (sanctifying grace) is precisely calibrated to this kind of biblical case.

A *Reformed* position with Calvin would similarly distinguish but more sharply: Saul received a *common operation* of the Spirit empowering him for office, but never the *saving operation* that regenerates the elect. The chapter's transformation language is real but limited; it does not constitute true conversion. This Calvinist reading is one of the chapter's most influential theological deployments.

An *Arminian* or *Wesleyan* position would read the gift as genuinely saving and Saul's later failures as resistance to grace continually offered — the Spirit's later departure reflects Saul's persistent refusal, not a mere withdrawal of an instrumental empowerment.

A *Jewish* position would generally affirm the genuine character of the divine gift and read Saul's later failures as moral choices for which he is responsible, not as evidence that the gift was inadequate. Abarbanel treats Saul's transformation as real and his later rejection as just judgment on real culpability.

An *Eastern Orthodox* position would emphasize *theosis* and the synergistic character of grace: the gift was given, the participation was required, the participation failed. The Spirit does not coerce.

A *critical-historical* position (Wellhausen, McCarter) would read the chapter's transformation language as the pro-monarchic source's high theology of charismatic kingship, and the later rejection narrative as the anti-monarchic source's correction. The contradiction is canonical because the editing is. *Where this leaves us:* the chapter teaches that genuine divine empowerment can be given to a person who will not finally be saved by it — and any reader must therefore distinguish between role-grace and saving-grace, between empowerment for service and the deeper work of conversion. The terror of Saul's story is precisely that the gifts were real.

*Unresolved question:* If the same Spirit who rushed on Saul at 10:10 is the same Spirit poured out at Pentecost, and if Saul's empowerment ended in Gilboa, what does it mean to receive a gift whose continued operation depends on a fidelity we cannot guarantee — and how should anyone, having read 1 Samuel, ever be casual about the line "the Spirit of the LORD departed"?

## The Thread

- **Joshua 7:14–18** — Achan exposed by lot through tribes, clans, and households. Saul is selected by the same procedure that destroyed Israel's first thief. The dtr is staging the chapter as ironic typology.
- **1 Samuel 16:13–14** — David anointed; the Spirit rushes on him; the Spirit departs from Saul. The deliberate juxtaposition makes 1 Sam 10 the antechamber of 1 Sam 16, and the proverb "Is Saul also among the prophets?" reverberates against the contrast.
- **1 Samuel 19:24** — The proverb's second use, when Saul is stripped naked and lies prophesying all night in pursuit of David. The same words mean the opposite. The dtr's most efficient irony.
- **Acts 2:1–13** — Pentecost: the Spirit rushes on disciples in a band, music attends them (in form of "tongues like fire"), bystanders ask, "What does this mean?" The flagged later passage where Gibeath-elohim's prophetic guild scene is restaged eschatologically — and where, unlike Saul, the recipients are not rejected, because the Spirit's gift is now ontologically tied to the resurrected Lord whose kingship cannot be revoked.
