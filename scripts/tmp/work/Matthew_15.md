# Matthew 15: The Tradition of the Elders, the Canaanite Woman, and Bread for the Outsiders

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Matthew 15 stages a confrontation about ritual purity that has roots deep in Second Temple Jewish practice and reaches forward into the formative debates that produced rabbinic Judaism a generation later. The Pharisees and scribes arrive "from Jerusalem" (15:1) — a detail Matthew preserves from Mark, marking these as senior interlocutors, not the local Galilean Pharisees Jesus had been disputing with for chapters. ***E.P. Sanders*** (*Jewish Law from Jesus to the Mishnah*, 1990) and ***Jacob Neusner*** (*The Rabbinic Traditions about the Pharisees before 70*, 1971) demonstrated that pre-70 Pharisaic halakhah was already extensive, oral, and contested, with ritual hand-washing (***netilat yadayim***, נטילת ידים) developing as a piety extension of the priestly purity laws of Leviticus 22 to ordinary tablefellowship. The relevant tractate is ***Mishnah Yadayim***, dedicated entirely to the laws of hand-washing, codified in the late second century but reflecting earlier Pharisaic practice. The "tradition of the elders" (***paradosis tōn presbyterōn***, παράδοσις τῶν πρεσβυτέρων, 15:2) is precisely what the Mishnah's own preface in ***m. Avot*** 1:1 claims: "Moses received the Torah from Sinai and transmitted it to Joshua, and Joshua to the elders, and the elders to the prophets, and the prophets to the Men of the Great Assembly."

***Qorban*** (קָרְבָּן) — the Aramaic-Hebrew loanword Mark preserves explicitly (Mark 7:11) and Matthew translates into the Greek paraphrase "what you would have gained from me is given to God" (15:5) — refers to a vow dedicating property to the Temple. The dedicated property could not then be used for ordinary purposes, including the support of one's parents. ***Mishnah Nedarim*** 9 and the ***Tosefta Nedarim*** preserve extensive rabbinic discussion of how such vows could be released, particularly when they harmed parents — and crucially, the rabbis themselves came to share Jesus' concern, ruling that vows in violation of the fifth commandment could be annulled by a sage. ***Davies/Allison*** (ICC, vol. 2) treat the dispute as Jesus joining an *internal* halakhic argument that was already in motion and would later largely vindicate Jesus' side. ***Saldarini*** (*Matthew's Christian-Jewish Community*, 1994) emphasizes that Jesus is not opposing the Torah but a particular halakhic ruling that subordinated the commandment.

The geography then shifts dramatically: Jesus withdraws to "the district of Tyre and Sidon" (15:21), Phoenician territory north of the Galilee. Tyre and Sidon are the great Phoenician port cities, deeply Hellenized in the first century, and theologically loaded as ancient Canaanite-Phoenician centers — the homeland of Jezebel (1 Kings 16:31) and the rivals against whom the Hebrew prophets thundered (Isaiah 23, Ezekiel 26-28, Amos 1:9-10). Archaeological evidence at Tyre confirms a wealthy, cosmopolitan, multi-ethnic city in the first century, with Roman-era harbor structures and the famous Herodian-era hippodrome. ***Mark*** calls the woman a "Syrophoenician" (Mark 7:26), the standard Greco-Roman ethnographic term distinguishing Phoenicians of Syria from Phoenicians of Carthage (Libo-Phoenicians); ***Matthew*** distinctively calls her ***Chananaia*** (Χαναναία, "Canaanite," 15:22) — a deliberately archaizing term that reaches back to Israel's deuteronomic enemies, the people whom the Israelites under Joshua were commanded to dispossess. ***Davies/Allison***, ***Boyarin***, and ***Levine*** all treat this lexical choice as theologically loaded: the woman is, in Matthean perspective, the very category against whom the conquest narrative was directed.

The "children's bread" / "dogs" exchange (15:26-27) plays on a Greco-Roman household scene. The diminutive ***kynaria*** (κυνάρια, "little dogs," "house-dogs") distinguishes Jesus' word from the contemptuous *kynes* used elsewhere as a slur for outsiders; the woman's reply takes up the imagery and presses it. The history of interpretation divides over how to take the harshness of Jesus' words: ***Origen*** and ***Chrysostom*** read them as a deliberate test designed to draw out and exhibit the woman's faith; ***Ben Witherington*** and many modern commentators (***Davies/Allison***, ***France***, ***Keener***) similarly treat the exchange as a probing of faith rather than a real rebuff, noting that the woman's quick wit meets Jesus on his own ground and that he immediately grants her request and praises her "great faith."

The second feeding (15:32-39) takes place in what is most likely the Decapolis region on the eastern shore of the Sea of Galilee — predominantly Gentile territory. The seven loaves, the seven baskets (***spyridas***, σπυρίδας — distinct from the *kophinoi* of 14:20), and the four thousand fed have invited symbolic-numerological readings: seven as the number of completion (the seven nations dispossessed by Joshua, the seven Gentile-context deacons of Acts 6), four thousand as the four corners of the earth.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Matthew 15 is the structural pivot that opens the Gospel's Gentile horizon. The chapter takes Mark 7 as its base text but re-shapes it in two crucial ways. First, Matthew preserves but in Greek paraphrase the *qorban* discussion, omitting Mark's editorial gloss "thus he declared all foods clean" (Mark 7:19). ***Saldarini***, ***Davies/Allison***, and ***France*** all read this omission as theologically deliberate: Matthew's distinctively Jewish-Christian community continued to keep the food laws, and the evangelist will not let Mark's Gentile-friendly redaction compromise that practice. Whatever Jesus' inner-purity teaching means, Matthew refuses to read it as the abolition of kashrut. The point is sharpened by the chapter's own logic: if Jesus had abolished the food laws here, the subsequent debate at Acts 10-15 over Gentile food practices would be incomprehensible.

Second, Matthew's labeling of the woman as "Canaanite" rather than "Syrophoenician" (the only New Testament use of *Chananaia*) draws the reader's attention to Israel's foundational territorial-theological narrative. ***Davies/Allison*** treat this as a deliberate Matthean evocation of Joshua and the conquest: this is the encounter with the *original Other*, the Other Israel was supposed to dispossess, now coming to David's son seeking healing.

The Greek of 15:24 — "I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" — uses the same restrictive language as 10:5-6, where Jesus charges the Twelve to go "nowhere among the Gentiles." This creates one of the great Matthean tensions: between the explicit Israel-only mandate of chapters 10 and 15, and the Great Commission of 28:19 ("make disciples of all nations"). ***Kingsbury*** (*Matthew: Structure, Christology, Kingdom*, 1975) reads this as a Matthean salvation-history sequence: the Israel-priority of Jesus' earthly ministry gives way after the resurrection to the universal mission. ***Davies/Allison*** treat the Canaanite woman as the *exception* that proves the rule — Jesus' ministry remains Israel-focused, but the kingdom is already breaking out at the edges, prefiguring the post-resurrection mission.

The vocabulary of "great faith" (***megalē sou hē pistis***, μεγάλη σου ἡ πίστις, 15:28) is almost exactly inverted from the Matthean *oligopistia* (little faith) vocabulary. In Matthew, the disciples are repeatedly *oligopistoi*, while *great faith* is twice attributed to Gentiles — the centurion (8:10) and the Canaanite woman (15:28). ***France*** and ***Hagner*** treat this as a Matthean polemical-pastoral move: the insiders waver, the outsiders trust.

The second feeding (15:32-39) raises the famous "doublet" question. ***Rudolf Bultmann*** (*History of the Synoptic Tradition*, 1921, ET 1963) argued that the two feedings (5,000 in chapter 14, 4,000 in chapter 15) are doublets of a single tradition, with the variant numbers reflecting different community memories. ***Davies/Allison*** push back, arguing for two separate events distinguished by setting (Jewish wilderness in chapter 14, Gentile region in chapter 15), basket-vocabulary (*kophinoi* vs. *spyridas*), and numerology (twelve tribes vs. seven nations / completeness). ***France*** and ***Keener*** follow this two-event reading. The harder question is theological rather than historical: even if the events are separate, Matthew's deliberate placement creates a typological pairing — Jewish abundance (twelve baskets) and Gentile abundance (seven baskets) — that signals the Gospel's missional shape.

Textual variants: 15:6 has variants in some manuscripts adding "or his mother" after "father," likely a scribal harmonization with 15:5; 15:8 has variants in the Isaiah quotation reflecting different textual traditions of LXX Isaiah 29:13.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The patristic tradition struggled with the harshness of Jesus' "dogs" remark. ***Origen*** (*Commentary on Matthew* 11.16-17) read it as a deliberate test, designed to elicit and exhibit the woman's faith. ***Chrysostom*** (*90 Homilies on Matthew*, Homily 52) developed a homiletic reading: Jesus' apparent rejection is pedagogical, drawing out the woman's persistence so that her faith becomes a public model. ***Augustine*** read the woman typologically as the church-from-the-Gentiles, persisting in faith despite apparent divine rejection. ***Aquinas's Catena Aurea*** stitches these readings together with characteristic balance.

The Reformation read the passage as a paradigm of justifying faith. ***Luther*** preached one of his most famous sermons on the Canaanite woman (1525, on Reminiscere Sunday): she becomes the model of *fides ex auditu*, faith that clings to the word of promise even when every appearance contradicts it. Luther's reading — that the woman heard in Jesus' "no" a deeper "yes," and clung to it against all evidence — became canonical for Lutheran preaching on faith. ***Calvin*** in his *Harmony* read her similarly, emphasizing her perseverance as the mark of true faith. The Lutheran-Reformed reading of the passage has been pastorally durable: faith is precisely what persists when God seems to refuse.

A strand of modern interpretation has emphasized the dialogue's genuine give-and-take — the woman's argument actually *answers* Jesus and the narrative records her as the one who carries the exchange — and some readers press this toward the idea that Jesus' stated mission-horizon is, in the scene, drawn open wider. Others (most mainstream commentators) hold that Matthew presents the encounter as a prefiguration rather than a redirection: the Israel-first mandate stands, but the kingdom is already spilling over the borders, and the woman's faith is the first Gentile fruit of that overflow. ***Amy-Jill Levine*** (*The Misunderstood Jew*, 2006; *Short Stories by Jesus*) cautions against readings that turn the woman into an anti-Jewish figure — Matthew's labeling of her as Canaanite is *internal* to Israel's narrative, drawing on the conquest tradition, not a polemic against Judaism.

Jewish readings have engaged the chapter substantially. ***Daniel Boyarin*** (*The Jewish Gospels*) reads the *qorban* dispute as classic Pharisaic intra-debate; Jesus is making a halakhic argument that the later rabbis themselves would substantially endorse. ***Vermes***, ***Lapide***, and ***Levine*** all emphasize that the dispute is internal Jewish argumentation, not a Christian rejection of Jewish ritual. ***Levine*** in particular reads "this people honors me with their lips" (Isaiah 29:13, Matthew 15:8-9) as a critique that Jewish prophets routinely made of their own people — Jesus is not a Christian critiquing Jews but a Jew speaking the prophetic critique tradition.

The contemporary commentary tradition is robust: ***Davies/Allison*** (ICC, vol. 2), ***Luz*** (*Hermeneia*, ET 2001), ***France*** (NICNT, 2007), ***Keener*** (NICNT, 1999), ***Hagner*** (WBC, 1995), ***Nolland*** (NIGTC, 2005), ***Harrington*** (Sacra Pagina, 1991), ***Bauckham*** on the canonical-narrative shape of the Gentile inclusion. ***Bonhoeffer*** in *Discipleship* read the Canaanite woman as the model of the *costly grace* that knows itself unworthy yet refuses to depart.

***N.T. Wright*** (*Matthew for Everyone* vol. 1; *Jesus and the Victory of God*, 1996; *How God Became King*) reads Matthew 15 as a chapter about what Israel's God's kingdom is finally for. The *qorban* dispute (15:1-20) is, on Wright's account, Jesus the loyal Israelite insisting that the heart of Torah — honour your father and mother, love God and neighbour — must not be hollowed out by clever traditions that let people off the hook; "you nullify the word of God for the sake of your tradition" (15:6) is prophetic critique from the inside, the kind Isaiah and Jeremiah made. The withdrawal to Tyre and Sidon and the encounter with the Canaanite woman (15:21-28) then dramatizes, in Wright's reading, the next phase of the kingdom-story: Jesus' mission is "to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" first (15:24) because Israel is the people through whom God always intended to bless the world (Genesis 12), and so the woman's faith — she will take even the crumbs that fall from Israel's table — is not the abolition of Israel's priority but its fulfilment: the nations beginning to come to the light. And the second feeding in Gentile territory (15:32-39), set against the first feeding in Jewish wilderness, is for Wright Matthew showing the bread of the kingdom now being broken for Israel *and* for the nations — a quiet preview of the Great Commission, heaven's table being laid for the whole world.

The two feedings have been read together in Christian liturgical and ecclesial reflection. ***Augustine***, ***Bede***, and the Western tradition through the medieval period read the two feedings as Jewish and Gentile prefigurations of the one Eucharist; ***Luz*** documents the reception history in detail. The Eastern Orthodox tradition similarly reads the doublet as the church's universality.

***Joachim Jeremias*** (*Jesus' Promise to the Nations*, 1956) gathered the Synoptic material on Gentile-inclusion and argued that Jesus' Israel-priority was historically real but structurally directed toward eschatological Gentile pilgrimage to Zion (Isaiah 2, 56, 60); the Canaanite woman is the prefigurative first-fruit of that pilgrimage. ***Krister Stendahl*** (*Paul Among Jews and Gentiles*, 1976) extended the argument: the question of how Gentiles are included was *the* dominant theological question of the early church, and Matthew 15 sits at the hinge. ***Richard Bauckham*** in *The Bible and Mission* reads the Canaanite-woman scene as the moment at which the trajectory becomes narratively visible without yet being structurally enacted.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Inner versus outer purity — abrogation, intensification, or relocation?** First, the *abrogation* reading (Mark's gloss "thus he declared all foods clean," Mark 7:19): Jesus eliminates the ritual purity system. Second, the *intensification* reading (some Reformed and patristic): Jesus does not abolish the food laws but makes them secondary to the heart-laws, intensifying the inner demand without removing the outer. Third, the *relocation* reading (Matthew's distinctive omission of Mark's gloss; ***Saldarini***, ***Davies/Allison***): Jesus relocates the *primary* concern from outer purity to inner purity without abrogating the food laws for his Jewish-Christian followers; the Gentile mission of Acts 10-15 will resolve the food-law question pastorally, but Matthew himself does not. Steelmanned, the third reading fits Matthew's text best; the first reading fits Mark's; the second is a synthesis. **Where this leaves us:** Matthew is more conservative than Mark on kashrut and more radical than the Pharisees on the heart. The chapter is a critique of *replacing* commandments with traditions, not a critique of tradition as such.

**Tension 2: The Israel-priority of Jesus' mission versus the universal Great Commission.** First, the *salvation-historical* reading (***Kingsbury***, ***Davies/Allison***, ***Wright***, much Reformed): Jesus' earthly ministry was Israel-focused as a matter of covenant priority; the resurrection commissions a universal mission. Second, the *progressive-revelation* reading: Jesus himself is genuinely drawn beyond his stated mission-horizon by encounters like the Canaanite woman. Third, the *unified-mission* reading: Jesus always intended Gentile inclusion, but according to a sequence — Israel first, then nations through Israel — that the Canaanite woman exemplifies as exception within rule. Steelmanned, the first is the dominant historical reading; the second takes the dialogue's genuine give-and-take seriously; the third holds them together. **Where this leaves us:** the Canaanite woman is neither a contradiction of Jesus' Israel-priority nor a moment of Jesus' moral failure but a prefiguration: the kingdom belongs to Israel first and through Israel to the world, and her faith is the kind that the Gentile mission will discover and elicit.

**Unresolved question:** What does it mean for Christian mission today that Matthew preserves a sharp tension between "I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" and "make disciples of all nations"? Is the Israel-priority a temporary economic arrangement now ended, or a permanent theological priority for the church? The Pauline tradition (Romans 9-11) wrestles with the same question without resolution.

## The Thread

The "blind guides" image (15:14) recurs in the woes against the scribes and Pharisees in 23:16, 24 — flag the deepening polemic across the Gospel. The "out of the heart come evil thoughts" catalogue (15:19) echoes the Decalogue and prefigures the Sermon-on-the-Mount-style heart-ethics that Matthew will return to in 23:25-28. The "great faith" of the Canaanite woman (15:28) pairs with the centurion's "great faith" (8:10) and the bleeding woman's quiet faith (9:22) to form a Matthean theology of outsider-faith that prefigures the Gentile mission of 28:19. The two feedings (14:13-21 and 15:32-39) form the typological backbone of the Bread-of-Life cycle and recur explicitly in 16:9-10 when Jesus reminds the disciples of both feedings in the boat — flag for chapter 16 the eucharistic-typological logic that ties chapters 14, 15, and 16 into a single arc.
