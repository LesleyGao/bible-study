# Acts 2: Pentecost, Tongues, and the First Apostolic Speech

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The day of Pentecost in Acts 2 is the Jewish festival of **Shavuot** (Hebrew "weeks"; Greek ***pentēkostē***, πεντηκοστή, "fiftieth"), one of the three pilgrimage festivals (along with Passover and Sukkot) that drew Jews from across the Roman world to Jerusalem. By the first century, the city's permanent population of perhaps 25,000–40,000 (Jeremias's figure in *Jerusalem in the Time of Jesus*) swelled several times over during the festivals; the surviving mikvaot near the southern Temple Mount entrances, and the monumental staircase uncovered by Benjamin Mazar's excavations south of the Hulda Gates, attest the infrastructure required to handle this throng. The Caiaphas ossuary (1990) and Eilat Mazar's continuing City of David excavations have refined our picture of the priestly aristocracy and the urban geography into which Pentecost's "devout men from every nation under heaven" (2:5) poured.

Shavuot was originally an agricultural firstfruits festival, the wheat-harvest counterpart to Passover's barley (Lev 23:15–22; Deut 16:9–12). By the late Second Temple period, however, **a parallel tradition associating Shavuot with the giving of the Torah at Sinai** was emerging. The earliest extant witness is **Jubilees 6:17–21** (second century BCE), which links the festival to Noah's covenant; later rabbinic sources, including the Mishnah and **Babylonian Talmud Pesachim 68b**, make the Sinai-Torah connection explicit. **Targum Pseudo-Jonathan to Exodus 19** depicts the Sinai theophany with imagery of fire and divided languages — the voice of God going forth in the seventy languages of the nations, an image strikingly resonant with Acts 2's tongues of fire and multilingual speech. Whether this Sinai-Pentecost association was already operative for Luke is debated: Bruce, Marshall, and Witherington argue it was; Conzelmann and Haenchen are more skeptical; Keener's encyclopedic commentary lays out the evidence on both sides. **Philo's** *De Decalogo* (33–35, 46) describes the giving of the Torah as a divine voice issuing in articulate fire, accessible to all hearers in their own understanding — a remarkably close parallel to Acts 2.

The "**mighty rushing wind**" (***pnoē biaia***, πνοὴ βιαία, 2:2) and "**divided tongues as of fire**" (***glōssai hōsei pyros***, γλῶσσαι ὡσεὶ πυρός, 2:3) deliberately echo the Sinai theophany of Exodus 19:16–19, where the mountain trembled and was wrapped in fire and smoke; the imagery is also at home in Qumran (1QHa column 11 uses fire imagery for prophetic possession) and in the broader apocalyptic tradition. The "third hour of the day" (2:15) is roughly 9 a.m., aligning with the morning Tamid offering at the temple — a precise temporal note characteristic of Luke's documentary style.

The catalogue of nations in 2:9–11 — Parthians, Medes, Elamites, Mesopotamia, Judea, Cappadocia, Pontus, Asia, Phrygia, Pamphylia, Egypt, Libya around Cyrene, Rome, Cretans, Arabians — has been the subject of extensive geographical analysis. **James Scott's** *Paul and the Nations* and other scholars have argued the list draws on the **Genesis 10 Table of Nations**, structuring the empire-spanning Jewish diaspora as a programmatic map of the Gentile world to be reached. Scholars from Bruce and Conzelmann onward have noted the list's curious omissions (Galilee, Syria, Anatolia's western coast) and irregular order; some have proposed an underlying astrological list of nations under the twelve zodiacal signs, but this remains speculative.

Textually, Acts 2 has comparatively minor Western expansions, but Codex Bezae's reading at 2:1 (giving Pentecost a slightly different temporal sense) is one of the disputed points discussed in **Bruce Metzger's** *Textual Commentary* and **Eldon Epp's** work on the theological tendency of D.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Acts 2 is the chapter where Lukan literary architecture becomes visible at full scale. The Pentecost narrative (2:1–13), the kerygmatic speech (2:14–36), the response and altar-call (2:37–41), and the **first summary statement** (2:42–47) together constitute the structural template Tannehill identifies in *The Narrative Unity of Luke-Acts* vol. 2 for the panels of Acts: Spirit-event, speech, response, summary. The five summary statements (2:42–47; 4:32–35; 5:12–16; 6:7; 9:31, with variants) are Luke's signature structural device.

Peter's Pentecost speech is the first and template-setting kerygmatic address in Acts, and its construction has been the test case for the **Cadbury–Dibelius–Marshall–Witherington** debate over Lukan speech-composition. Cadbury argued in *Making of Luke-Acts* that Luke followed the Thucydidean convention (Thucydides 1.22) of composing speeches that fit the speaker and occasion. **Martin Dibelius** in *Studies in the Acts of the Apostles* extended this with the more radical claim that the speeches are essentially Lukan compositions illustrating Lukan theology. **C.H. Dodd's** *The Apostolic Preaching and Its Developments* pushed back, arguing that Peter's speech preserves a primitive **kerygma** — a fixed pattern of apostolic proclamation centered on Christ's death, resurrection, exaltation, and call to repentance. **Marshall** and **Witherington** broadly side with Dodd, holding that Luke had access to traditional material; **Conzelmann** sides with Dibelius. The Christological titles in 2:36 — "**Lord and Christ**" (***kyrion kai christon***, κύριον καὶ χριστόν) — and the resurrection-as-vindication structure are arguably pre-Lukan; the elaborate exegesis of Psalm 16 and Psalm 110 may reflect later catechetical development.

The chapter's **citations of Joel 2:28–32, Psalm 16, and Psalm 110:1** model what scholars call **Lukan promise-fulfillment hermeneutics** (engage Conzelmann's *Theology of St. Luke*). Joel's "in the last days" (2:17 — Luke alters the LXX's "after these things" to **eschatological-temporal** "in the last days") frames the Spirit-outpouring as the inauguration of the eschaton; Psalm 16 supplies the **non-corruption-of-the-Holy-One** argument that requires resurrection; Psalm 110 supplies the **session at the right hand** that requires exaltation. This three-text pattern — prophetic frame, Davidic resurrection text, Davidic enthronement text — recurs (with variations) in Peter's speeches in chapters 3, 4, and 10.

The "**we passages**" do not appear in Acts 2, but the chapter's documentary precision — the catalogue of nations, the count of three thousand baptized, the time-stamp of the third hour — fits what Marguerat in *The First Christian Historian* calls Luke's apologetic-historiographical aesthetic. Whether the count of three thousand (2:41) is historically precise or stylized (cf. Exodus 32:28's three thousand who fell for the golden calf — a possible inverted Sinai allusion) remains debated; Pervo's late-dating Hermeneia treats the figure as novelistic, while Bruce and Keener defend its plausibility.

The **Western Text** of Acts 2 contains several expansions, the most discussed being at 2:17–18 (where D adds material from elsewhere in the prophetic corpus) and at 2:30 (an expansion glossing the Davidic descent). **Eldon Epp's** thesis that the Western Text reflects an early second-century anti-Judaic theological tendency finds some traction in Acts 2's Bezan readings.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Acts 2 sits at the heart of the canonical arc as the Spirit-counterpart to Sinai and the inauguration of the eschatological community Joel had foreseen.

**Jewish interpretive trajectories**. The Sinai-Pentecost association embedded in Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, Mishnah Pesachim, and the broader rabbinic tradition (engage **Lawrence Schiffman** *Who Was a Jew?*) makes Acts 2 doubly contested ground. For early Jewish readers, the Christian claim that the Spirit had been poured out apart from the Sinai-Torah was a direct theological challenge; Adele Reinhartz, Daniel Boyarin (*Border Lines*), and Amy-Jill Levine (*Jewish Annotated New Testament*) have urged contemporary Christian readers to see Pentecost not as the Spirit's *replacement* of Torah but as a development within Jewish eschatological hope (Joel itself stands within Israel's own prophetic tradition). The **birkat ha-minim** synagogue prayer-curse, often associated with the late first or early second century separation, addresses the very community Acts 2 inaugurates. Pamela Eisenbaum and Matthew Thiessen (*A Jewish Paul*) extend this corrective: the Pentecost outpouring "on all flesh" (2:17) is the inclusion of Gentiles within a continuing Jewish framework, not the supersession of Israel.

**Christian patristic reception**. **John Chrysostom's** Homilies 4–7 on Acts (c. 400 CE) treat Pentecost at length, emphasizing the apostles' transformation from cowardice to boldness, the universal scope of the outpouring, and the Spirit's role as continuing presence in the church's life. **Origen** in *Contra Celsum* uses Acts 2 to defend the Spirit's universal availability against pagan critics; **Augustine's** *Tractates on John* and his sermons on Pentecost develop the Sinai-Pentecost typology — the law on Sinai written on stone with finger of God, the Spirit at Pentecost written on hearts (cf. 2 Cor 3) — as a hermeneutical key to the new covenant. **Bede** in *Expositio Actuum* offers the most extensive medieval treatment, working the Sinai parallels and the Babel reversal carefully. **Jerome** preserves the tradition of the upper room location.

**The Babel reversal trope**. From at least the time of Augustine, Christian interpretation has read Acts 2 as a deliberate reversal of Genesis 11's confusion of languages — the curse of Babel undone in the Spirit's gift of comprehensible multilingual proclamation. This reading is preserved in the patristic and medieval lectionary tradition and developed by Calvin, Luther, and modern commentators including Bruce, Keener, and Gaventa. Some recent scholarship (Marguerat, Pervo) has cautioned against pressing the parallel too hard — Acts 2 does not undo linguistic diversity but uses it — but the reading remains generative.

**Reformation**. **Calvin's** *Commentary on Acts* on chapter 2 emphasizes the Spirit's work in producing intelligible speech (Calvin reads Pentecost as **xenolalia**, foreign-language speech) and in confirming the church's mission; he argues against any restriction of Spirit-work to extraordinary signs. **Luther's** Pentecost sermons emphasize the Spirit's binding of believers to Christ through the word.

**N.T. Wright**, in *Acts for Everyone* and across *Surprised by Hope* and *Simply Christian*, reads Pentecost as the moment the Spirit launches the renewed-Israel mission to the nations and constitutes the church as the new-covenant, new-Temple people — the place where God's presence now dwells. He works the Sinai parallel hard (fifty days after Passover, fire and divided languages, the people of God formed at the holy mountain — now the people of God formed in Jerusalem and immediately scattered outward) and the Babel reversal (the nations' confusion at Babel answered by intelligible proclamation to "every nation under heaven"). On Wright's reading Peter's speech is the public announcement that "the last days" have begun: the crucified Jesus has been raised and enthroned as Lord and Messiah, Israel's God has returned to his people in the Spirit, and the proper response is to repent, be baptized, and join the movement that will carry this news to the end of the earth. Pentecost, for Wright, is not the church's birthday party so much as its commissioning and equipping for the kingdom-task Acts 1:8 set out.

**Pentecostal and charismatic** scholarship has made Acts 2 the foundation of a distinct Lukan pneumatology. **Roger Stronstad's** *Charismatic Theology of St. Luke* argues that Luke develops a pneumatology of prophetic empowerment for mission distinct from the Pauline pneumatology of soteriological inclusion. **Robert Menzies's** *Empowered for Witness* extends this thesis, arguing Spirit-baptism in Luke is subsequent and additional to conversion. **Gordon Fee's** *God's Empowering Presence* offers an evangelical mediating reading, granting the Lukan distinctness while resisting strict subsequence. The classical Pentecostal reading (Assemblies of God, Church of God in Christ) takes Acts 2 as paradigmatic for ongoing Spirit-baptism with **tongues as initial physical evidence**; this position is defended in Stronstad and Menzies, modified in continuationist evangelical readings (Wayne Grudem's *Systematic Theology*, Sam Storms), and rejected in classical Reformed cessationism (B.B. Warfield's *Counterfeit Miracles*, Sinclair Ferguson, John MacArthur's *Charismatic Chaos*).

**Contemporary commentaries**. F.F. Bruce's NICNT, C.K. Barrett's ICC, Joseph Fitzmyer's Anchor Bible, Beverly Gaventa's Abingdon, Luke Timothy Johnson's Sacra Pagina, Craig Keener's encyclopedic four volumes (the volume on Acts 1–2 alone is 800 pages on Pentecost-related material), Ben Witherington's socio-rhetorical commentary, Eckhard Schnabel's ZECNT, Hans Conzelmann's Hermeneia, Ernst Haenchen's classic, Richard Pervo's late-dating Hermeneia, David Peterson's Pillar, Daniel Marguerat's Continental commentary, and I. Howard Marshall's TNTC anchor the contemporary conversation.

Peter's citation of Joel runs further than the bare promise of the Spirit: "your sons and your daughters shall prophesy … even on my male servants and female servants in those days I will pour out my Spirit, and they shall prophesy" (2:17–18). Commentators across the spectrum — Bruce, Marshall, Witherington, Keener, Gaventa, Luke Timothy Johnson — note that Luke has retained Joel's leveling sweep: the Spirit falls on young and old, male and female, slave and free, which both grounds Luke's recurring male-female pairings (the Gospel's Zechariah/Mary, Simeon/Anna; Acts' soon-to-come Aeneas/Tabitha, Aquila/Priscilla) and anticipates the prophesying daughters of Philip in Acts 21:9. Keener in particular stresses that the Joel citation is one of the load-bearing texts for the early church's sense that the age of the Spirit broke the ordinary social grammar of who could speak for God.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The nature of the tongues.** The chapter's central interpretive crux is whether the **glossolalia** (***glōssais lalein***, γλώσσαις λαλεῖν, 2:4) of Pentecost is the same phenomenon as the glossolalia of 1 Corinthians 12–14, and what either implies for ongoing Christian experience. Three positions:

*Xenolalia / one-time inaugural*: Acts 2 narrates the supernatural speaking of actual foreign languages comprehensible to the hearers (the narrator emphasizes hearing in own dialect, 2:6, 2:8, 2:11); 1 Corinthians describes a different phenomenon (ecstatic non-language speech requiring interpretation); cessationist Reformed reading (Warfield, Ferguson, MacArthur) holds the apostolic-era miraculous gifts have ceased with the foundation-laying period.

*Continuing Spirit-baptism with tongues / classical Pentecostal*: Acts 2 is paradigmatic; tongues continue as initial evidence of Spirit-baptism subsequent to conversion; engage Stronstad, Menzies, classical Pentecostal denominations.

*Continuationist mediating*: the Spirit's gifts continue without strict subsequence or initial-evidence requirement; tongues are one gift among many; engage Wayne Grudem, Sam Storms, the broader charismatic-renewal tradition.

The strongest version of each: the cessationist case rests on the unique inaugural framing of Acts 2 (the unrepeatable Sinai-correspondence, the ingathering of all-flesh-witness in one location); the classical Pentecostal case rests on the four "Pentecost" episodes in Acts (chapters 2, 8, 10, 19) and the apparent normativity of Spirit-receiving as a discrete experience; the continuationist case rests on Paul's own treatment of tongues as a continuing if regulated gift in the Corinthian church.

*Where this leaves us*: Luke's narrative is ambiguous about repeatability. He emphasizes Pentecost's uniqueness (the rushing wind and tongues of fire are not narrated to recur) while also patterning later Spirit-events on it. The text supports neither cessation as a categorical claim nor classical-Pentecostal subsequence as a normative requirement; it does support the Spirit's continuing missional empowerment in forms that may include but are not exhausted by the phenomenon at Pentecost.

**Tension 2: Communal possession in 2:42–47.** The first summary statement — "**they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and the koinōnia, to the breaking of bread and the prayers** … and all who believed were together and **had all things in common**" — has been one of the most contested texts in Christian social ethics. Three positions:

*Catholic sacramental-traditional*: the four marks of 2:42 (apostolic teaching, *koinōnia*, breaking of bread, prayers) constitute the church's normative life; the communalism of 2:44–45 is an aspirational ideal honored in monastic and religious communities; engage the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* on the apostolic life and Catholic social teaching from *Rerum Novarum* through *Sollicitudo Rei Socialis* and *Laudato Si'*.

*Anabaptist-radical*: 2:44–45 is normative for the gathered church; private property is incompatible with the apostolic life; engage the **Hutterite tradition**, the Schleitheim Confession, the **Bruderhof** movement, and contemporary new-monastic communities (Shane Claiborne and the New Friars).

*Reformed-evangelical*: the communalism of 2:44–45 was a voluntary, contextual response to the eschatological excitement of the early Jerusalem church and the influx of pilgrim-converts; it is described, not prescribed; private property is preserved (cf. Acts 5:4 — "while it remained, was it not your own?"); engage Calvin and the broader Reformed tradition; **Justo González's** *Faith and Wealth* offers a more critical Reformed-adjacent reading.

The strongest version of each: the Catholic position takes seriously both the text's idealism and the historical fact that voluntary religious communities have honored it without requiring it of all; the Anabaptist position takes seriously the text's plain force and the danger of explaining away its economic radicalism; the Reformed position takes seriously the textual fact that Acts 5:4 preserves the legitimacy of private property even within the early Jerusalem community.

*Where this leaves us*: Luke describes the Jerusalem community's economic practice with admiration but without explicit prescription. The four marks of 2:42 are normative; the specific economic form of 2:44–45 is a faithful but situational outworking. The challenge for any reading is to refuse both the literalist requirement that the text's specific form is binding and the evasive hermeneutic that empties the text of economic claim.

*One unresolved question*: Pentecost is depicted as the inauguration of a new age, but the disciples' question in 1:6 — "Lord, will you at this time restore the kingdom to Israel?" — has not yet been answered when chapter 2 ends. Joel's outpouring "on all flesh" suggests the eschaton has begun; Peter's call to "save yourselves from this crooked generation" (2:40) suggests final judgment is still pending. Luke leaves the eschatological tension intact: Pentecost is both inaugurated and not-yet-consummated, and the church lives in that interval.

## The Thread

The Spirit-promise of **Acts 1:5, 1:8** is fulfilled here; the four Pentecost-pattern episodes recur at **Acts 8:14–17** (Samaria), **Acts 10:44–48** (Cornelius), and **Acts 19:1–7** (Ephesus). The Joel citation reaches forward to **Romans 10:13** (Paul citing Joel 2:32 in identical context — "everyone who calls on the name of the Lord shall be saved"). The Davidic argument from Psalms 16 and 110 reaches forward to **Acts 13:32–37** (Paul's Antioch speech, repeating the Psalm 16 argument) and back to **Hebrews 1:13** (where Psalm 110 anchors the high-priestly Christology). The communalism of 2:44–45 reaches forward to **Acts 4:32–37** (Barnabas's gift) and **Acts 5:1–11** (Ananias and Sapphira) — the second summary's positive and shadow sides. Flag the Pentecost-Babel reversal trope and the question of whether Luke intends the Sinai parallel as load-bearing for chapters 7 (Stephen on Sinai) and 15 (the Jerusalem council on Torah obligation for Gentiles).
