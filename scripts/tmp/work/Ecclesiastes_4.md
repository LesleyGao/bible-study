# Ecclesiastes 4: The Tears of the Oppressed

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 4 opens with what is arguably the bleakest social observation in the Hebrew Bible: "Again I saw all the oppressions that are done under the sun. And behold, the tears of the oppressed, and they had no one to comfort them! On the side of their oppressors there was power, and there was no one to comfort them" (4:1). Choon-Leong Seow (Anchor, 1997) reads the chapter as a window into the social reality of Persian-period Yehud — a small temple-state inside a vast imperial bureaucracy where oppression was structurally, not incidentally, unrelieved. The verb *'ashuq* (עָשׁוּק, "oppressed/extorted") and its cognates are the same vocabulary that prophetic literature uses for systemic exploitation (Amos 4:1, Jer 22:17), and the doubled "they had no one to comfort them" (twice in v. 1) is unique in the canon — Qohelet uses the most common rhetorical tool of Hebrew poetry, repetition, to make precisely the point that there is *no* echo, *no* answering voice, *no* comforter. The verse's literary form mimics its content: a sound made twice into emptiness.

The political situation of the implied composition is therefore not a moment of national crisis but a steady-state of administered injustice. 5:8's commentary on the chapter's themes ("if you see in a province the oppression of the poor and the violation of justice and righteousness, do not be amazed at the matter, for the high official is watched by a higher, and there are yet higher ones over them") is the explicit identification of the bureaucratic mechanism: corruption is *recursive* up the satrapal chain. Persian-period Yehud was one administrative subdivision of the larger satrapy of *Eber-nari* ("Beyond the River"), and the Achaemenid system delivered both stability and extractive injustice. Seow notes that the *medinah* vocabulary (used at 5:8) is itself Persian administrative terminology that postdates the united monarchy by centuries; chapter 4's social world is post-exilic.

The economic vocabulary of 4:4–8 — *kishron ha-ma'aseh* (4:4, "skill in work"), *qin'at-ish me-re'ehu* (4:4, "envy of a man from his neighbor") and the lone miser of 4:8 *en-lo sheni gam ben va-ah en-lo* (4:8, "no other person, neither son nor brother") — describes what Crenshaw and Murphy both call the *atomized economic agent* of Persian-period commerce: the individual whose wealth-accumulation has become detached from kinship and community. The "poor and wise youth" who replaces "the old and foolish king" of 4:13–14 has been variously identified historically (Joseph and Pharaoh; David and Saul; Cyrus the Great in some Hellenistic readings; specific Hellenistic-period dynasts in others), but Seow's view is that the passage is paradigmatic, not historical-specific: it is a generalized observation about the cyclical instability of political power, perhaps loosely modeled on memories of court intrigue but not encoding a particular regime change.

Material culture: the *cord of three strands* (4:12, *ha-hut ha-meshullash*) is a textile-technology reference; ANE rope-making used twisted strands for tensile strength, and the threefold construction was practical engineering knowledge — Seow notes that the *Gilgamesh Epic* (Tablet I, in some recensions) uses the threefold cord image of Enkidu and Gilgamesh's friendship, suggesting Qohelet knew the proverb from a circulating ANE wisdom tradition.

Key Hebrew terms: *'oshqim* (עֲשֻׁקִים, "oppressed ones," 4:1) — passive participle, those who are *being* oppressed, not merely "the oppressed" as a category; *menahem* (מְנַחֵם, "comforter," 4:1) — the same word that haunts Lamentations ("she has no comforter," Lam 1:2, 1:9, 1:17, 1:21) and that the prophets use of YHWH's eschatological role (Isa 40:1, *nahamu nahamu 'ammi*); *qin'ah* (קִנְאָה, "envy/jealousy," 4:4) — the same word used for divine jealousy (Exod 20:5) and for Cain's relation to Abel (subtext in Gen 4); *'amel* (עָמֵל, "toiling one," 4:8) — the participle form of the chapter's signature verb; and *hevel* (הֶבֶל, the book's leitwort, returning at 4:4, 4:7, 4:8, 4:16).

Textual base: Qumran preserves portions of chapter 4 in 4QQoh-a, agreeing with MT. The LXX renders literally; the Targum to chapter 4 expands theologically (the "comforter" in 4:1 becomes in the Targum "no redeemer" — supplying the messianic frame Qohelet's Hebrew refuses).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 4 is structured as five discrete observational units, each beginning with a perception verb (*ra'ah*, "I saw," at 4:1, 4:4, 4:7, 4:15). The five units form a developing meditation on the social pathologies of *hevel*: oppression (4:1–3), competitive envy as the engine of work (4:4–6), the lone accumulator (4:7–8), the consolations of companionship (4:9–12), and the political instability of acclaim (4:13–16).

The first unit (4:1–3) is among the most rhetorically violent passages in the Hebrew Bible. Qohelet does not merely observe oppression; he draws the conclusion that the dead are *more fortunate* (literally *meshubbah*, "praised, congratulated") than the living, and the unborn — *asher 'aden lo' hayah* — are better off than both. James Crenshaw (OTL) notes that this is one of only a handful of OT passages that comes close to *anti-natalism*: Qohelet judges existence itself, in a world of unrelieved oppression, as a worse condition than non-existence. The passage shocked the rabbis (the *Bavli* preserves a debate at *Eruvin 13b* on the related "is it better to have been created than not" question, with Hillel and Shammai's schools dividing — the school of Shammai winning the vote that "it would have been better had man not been created"). Job 3 is the closest canonical analogue, and the parallels are striking: both texts wish for non-existence in the face of unrelievable suffering.

The second unit (4:4–6) connects work to envy. *Kol-'amal ve-khol kishron ha-ma'aseh ki hi' qin'at-ish me-re'ehu* — "all toil and all skill in work, that it is the envy of a man from his neighbor." Fox (*A Time to Tear Down*) reads this as Qohelet's diagnostic of *labor as competitive display*: humans work not for need but for status. The fool's folded hands (4:5) and the better-handful-than-two-handfuls (4:6) are paired proverbs — Seow argues 4:5 is a *quoted* proverb (the conventional wisdom Qohelet undercuts) and 4:6 is Qohelet's *better-than* response — a literary technique scholars call "quotation and rebuttal" (cf. 9:17–10:1 for similar structure).

The third unit (4:7–8) is the lone miser. The man "without son or brother" yet endlessly toiling, never asking *for whom?* Seow notes the literary precision: Qohelet does not say the miser has no family; he says the miser has *no other*, *en-lo sheni* — no second person, no companion, no one for whom the labor matters. The unit sets up the chapter's most famous passage.

The fourth unit (4:9–12) is the *companionship poem* — *tovim ha-shenayim min ha-echad*, "two are better than one." This is the closest thing to consolation in the entire book. The poem develops in three images: lifting after a fall (4:10), warmth in shared sleep (4:11), and resistance to attack (4:12), culminating in the *threefold cord*. The structural climax is so theologically warm that critics from Crenshaw to Fox have wondered whether the passage is genuinely Qohelet's or a pious interpolation; Seow defends authenticity, arguing that Qohelet's pessimism does not foreclose particular goods — companionship is one of them — and the poem fits the chapter's pattern of finding *limited* goods inside an *unrelieved* social world.

The fifth unit (4:13–16) is the political-cycle parable. A poor wise youth replaces an old foolish king; he is acclaimed by "all the living who move about under the sun"; and yet "those who come later will not rejoice in him." The unit's literary effect is that *acclaim is itself a hevel*: the political *kairos* of acclamation passes, the next generation forgets, the cycle resumes. Seow reads the passage as paradigmatic, not biographical — Qohelet is not encoding a specific royal succession but observing the structural instability of charismatic political authority.

Devices: the *better-than* (*tov... min*) construction governs the chapter (4:3, 4:6, 4:9, 4:13) — the wisdom genre's signature comparative form, but Qohelet uses it to make pessimistic comparisons (the unborn is better than the living) rather than the conventional virtue-comparisons of Proverbs. The *en-mi* construction ("there was no one") doubles in 4:1 and recurs in 4:8, 4:10. The *gam-zeh hevel* refrain closes 4:4, 4:8, 4:16, structuring the chapter's three judgments of *hevel*.

Textual variant of note: 4:8 — *u-le-mi 'ani 'amel* ("for whom am I toiling?"). Some manuscripts and the LXX read *u-le-mi anoki 'amel* (more emphatic first-person). The variant is minor but illustrates Qohelet's habit of voicing rhetorical questions in the first person; the miser's failure to ask the question is itself the chapter's point.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Chapter 4 has had an extraordinarily varied reception history because its sub-units are theologically distinct and have been read independently.

The Jewish trajectory engages 4:1–3 with notable seriousness. *Qohelet Rabbah* and the *Bavli* preserve the rabbinic debate about whether non-existence is preferable to existence under oppression; *Eruvin 13b* records that "for two and a half years the schools of Shammai and Hillel disputed, the former saying it would have been more pleasant for man not to have been created than to have been created, and the latter saying it is more pleasant for man to have been created than not to have been created. They voted and concluded: it would have been more pleasant for man not to have been created, but now that he has been created, let him examine his deeds." This is one of the most striking moments in rabbinic anthropology, and it is in dialogue with Eccl 4:2–3 (and Job 3). Maimonides (*Guide* 3.51) folds the passage into his theory of human limitation — the oppression of the world is among the conditions the wise person learns to transcend through intellectual perfection. Robert Gordis (*Koheleth*, 1951) reads 4:1–3 as the book's most uncompromising witness against the easy theodicy of Proverbs: oppression is real and unanswered, and any biblical theology that does not reckon with it is false. The companionship poem (4:9–12) is read in Jewish tradition through *havruta* — the rabbinic conviction that Torah study requires a partner, and the "threefold cord" is sometimes glossed as student-teacher-Torah.

The Christian patristic reception is dominated by allegorical readings of 4:9–12. Gregory of Nyssa (*Homilies on Ecclesiastes*) does not survive into chapter 4, but later patristic commentary (especially Jerome's, 388) reads the threefold cord as the *Trinity* (a reading that becomes standard in medieval exegesis and that the Glossa Ordinaria fixes for Latin Christianity). Hugh of St. Victor expands the trinitarian gloss; Aquinas cites 4:12 in *ST* III q. 24 (incidentally, on the unity of the divine persons). The trinitarian allegory is exegetically unfounded — Qohelet means literal cord-making — but the reading was so dominant that Reformation commentators had to actively dispute it. Luther's *Notes on Ecclesiastes* (1526/1532) reads 4:9–12 plainly: it is a praise of human friendship and partnership, with no trinitarian reference; Luther's *de servo arbitrio* anti-allegorical hermeneutics insists on the literal sense.

The most theologically active modern reading takes 4:1–3 as the Old Testament's most uncompromising statement on social violence — the verse refuses any easy resolution, and the "tears of the oppressed" without comforter stands as a permanent indictment of any theological system that domesticates oppression. Walter Brueggemann's *Theology of the Old Testament* (1997) reads Eccl 4:1–3 as a *counter-testimony* that refuses theodicy; Brevard Childs treats it as the canon's deliberate inclusion of an unanswered protest. Ellen Davis emphasizes the verse's force: Scripture itself contains the *unanswered* suffering, and any reading that softens this is unfaithful to the canon. Christian readings that move toward resolution typically do so by reading 4:1's diagnostic absence as the point the gospel answers — God *becomes* the comforter in Christ's solidarity with the suffering (Mt 5:4, *makarioi hoi penthountes*, "blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted") — but that move imports later canonical material onto Qohelet's deliberately unfinished sentence.

The political-cycle parable of 4:13–16 has its own reception. Jewish tradition speculatively identified the youth and king (*Qohelet Rabbah* names Joseph and Potiphar; the Targum supplies "Abraham and Nimrod"); medieval Christian commentary identified them with David and Saul or with Christ as the wise youth; modern critical scholarship (Seow, Murphy) reads the unit as paradigmatic. The Reformation reads political wisdom: Luther uses 4:13 in his political theology to argue that *all* rulers are eventually displaced and forgotten, and the church should not invest its hope in any temporal regime.

The companionship poem (4:9–12) has had pastoral reception out of proportion to its literary weight: it is one of the most cited passages at weddings in both Jewish and Christian liturgy. Seow notes the irony — a poem in a book about *hevel* has become the canonical text of the most enduring human covenant.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The problem of unrelieved evil — Qohelet's refusal to theodice.** Chapter 4:1–3 is canonically distinct in that it observes systematic oppression and *does not respond*. There is no answering divine voice (as in Job's whirlwind speeches); no eschatological vindication (as in the prophets); no covenantal *hesed* asserted (as in Lamentations 3:22). Qohelet observes; Qohelet judges existence as worse than non-existence; and Qohelet moves on to envy and competition (4:4–6) as if the question of oppression had already exhausted itself. This is the most theologically exposed moment in the book.

The Catholic reading (Murphy, the Aquinas tradition) treats unrelieved evil as a *Cross-precursor*: the OT's witness to oppression without comfort prepares the canon for Christ's solidarity, in which God himself enters the *menahem*-less condition (Christ on the cross has no comforter, *eli eli lema sabachthani*) and from inside it transforms it. The eschatological promise of comfort (Mt 5:4; Rev 21:4, "he will wipe away every tear") fulfills Eccl 4:1's diagnostic absence. The reading is theologically generative but does require importing later canonical material.

The Reformed/Calvinist reading reads 4:1–3 as a witness to the *non-utopian* character of fallen creation. Reformed political theology (Kuyperian and Augustinian alike) accepts that no human political arrangement will eliminate oppression *under the sun*; the Christian's hope is eschatological, and the present task is faithful witness in a *hevel* world. Iain Provan (NIVAC) reads the passage as the chapter's call to humility about political projects.

The Anabaptist non-resistant reading (extending from the Schleitheim Confession through the Mennonite peace tradition) treats 4:1–3 as the warrant for *non-participation* in coercive power. The oppressors have power and no comforter, the oppressed have tears and no comforter — Qohelet describes a system the church must refuse to join. The reading aligns with Christ's "blessed are those who mourn" rather than with any reformist program.

A more activist trajectory reads the same passage as a warrant for solidarity: scripture itself is testifying to oppression that has *no* comforter, and the church must therefore *become* the comforter — turning Qohelet's diagnostic of 4:1 into the ethics of Romans 12:15 ("weep with those who weep"). On this view Qohelet's refusal to theodice is itself a refusal of the comfortable, and the absence of a comforter in the text is a summons.

The Jewish reading (Gordis, the *Eruvin 13b* tradition) treats 4:1–3 as the canon's permission to acknowledge that creation is *not* obviously good in every condition, that the rabbinic vote on whether human creation was a good idea was *won by the negative*, and that biblical anthropology is honest enough to include this. The Christian readings, on this Jewish view, tend to soften too quickly toward eschatology; the Hebrew Bible holds the unanswered observation longer.

The genuine steelman: Qohelet may be the *most theologically honest* book in the canon precisely because it refuses to do what most theology cannot resist — turn unrelieved evil into a chapter in a vindication narrative. The book observes, names *hevel*, and stops. The canon has chosen to include this voice. *Where this leaves us*: any theology that finds Eccl 4:1–3 *resolvable* by importing later canonical material has not yet listened to what Qohelet is doing; the text's refusal of comfort is itself the text's contribution.

**Tension 2: Companionship in a *hevel* world — limited good or another *hevel*?** The companionship poem of 4:9–12 makes the book's most positive claim about human relationship: two are better than one, partners can lift each other after falls, can share warmth, can resist attack; a threefold cord is not quickly broken. Is this poem a genuine *hevel*-defying good — a real consolation — or is it itself part of the *hevel*-pattern, another temporary good that will dissolve?

Iain Provan (NIVAC) and the Murphy/Catholic tradition read the poem as a real and durable good: companionship is part of God's gift in creation, named so by Qohelet himself, not subject to the same dissolution as wealth, wisdom, or political acclaim. The threefold cord stands. The reading aligns with Genesis 2:18 ("it is not good that the man should be alone") and treats human companionship as a created good that survives the *hevel* condition.

Fox (*A Time to Tear Down*) and Crenshaw (OTL) read the poem more soberly. Yes, two are better than one for the *practical* purposes Qohelet enumerates (lifting, warmth, defense), but the comparative "better than" is functional, not metaphysical — companionship makes the *hevel* condition more bearable, but it does not exempt it from *hevel*. The lone miser of 4:7–8 is *hevel*; the companion-pair of 4:9–12 is *less hevel*; but both die in chapter 12. On this reading the poem is not a discovery of a *hevel*-defying good but a discovery of a *hevel*-mitigating one.

The Reformed reading (Provan, with Reformed creation theology) tends toward the Murphy position: covenantal companionship is a real created good. The Lutheran reading (extending Luther's 1526/1532 lectures) reads the poem as *gospel*: companionship is *Gabe*, gift, which the gospel intensifies in the body of Christ. The Anabaptist reading reads the poem as *ecclesiological*: the threefold cord is the church, the *havruta* of the redeemed community.

The Jewish reading (Gordis) treats the poem as praise of *havruta* in a more quotidian sense — partnership in study, work, marriage — without inflating the metaphysics. And the chapter's own structure is suggestive: 4:9–12 follows directly on the lone-miser passage and the comfortless-oppressed passage, which hints that companionship is the partial answer to the social atomism Qohelet has just diagnosed. *Where this leaves us*: 4:9–12 is one of the few places in Ecclesiastes where the *hevel* verdict is suspended, and the suspension is theologically significant — but the chapter also ends (4:13–16) with the political acclamation that *does* dissolve into *hevel*, so even the consolations the chapter offers are bounded.

**One unresolved question.** If Qohelet observes that the oppressed have no comforter and that the dead are better off than the living and the unborn better than both (4:1–3), and then, two paragraphs later, says that two are better than one and a threefold cord is not quickly broken (4:12) — is the companionship poem an *answer* to the comfortless-oppression problem, an *unrelated observation* in a chapter that does not cohere theologically, or a *deliberate juxtaposition* in which Qohelet means to say that the only comforter available is the one beside you, and that this is both insufficient and better than nothing?

## The Thread

The non-obvious cross-references work both backward and forward. *Lamentations 1:2, 1:9, 1:17, 1:21* — *en lah menahem*, "she has no comforter," repeated four times in Lamentations 1 — is the closest canonical parallel to Eccl 4:1's doubled "no comforter." Qohelet is using the technical vocabulary of Lamentations' *qinah* (lament), and Seow argues that Eccl 4:1–3 is in deliberate dialogue with the post-exilic *qinah* tradition — Qohelet refuses the prophetic resolution Lamentations holds out at 3:22 and stays in the unanswered observation. *Job 3:11–13* — Job's wish to have died at birth, to be "as those who never were" — is the strongest internal-canonical parallel to Eccl 4:2–3; both texts come from the wisdom corpus, both refuse easy theodicy, and Seow suggests they may share a common literary substrate. The forward echo to *Matthew 5:4* — *makarioi hoi penthountes, hoti autoi paraklēthēsontai* ("blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted") — uses the future passive of *parakaleō*, the LXX's standard rendering of Hebrew *naham*; Jesus's beatitude is the eschatological answer to Eccl 4:1's diagnostic absence, and the linguistic precision of the echo (Qohelet's *menahem*-less ones, Christ's coming *paraklēsis*) is one of the canon's strongest intertextual links. Finally *Sirach 7:34* ("do not avoid those who weep, but mourn with those who mourn") and *Romans 12:15* ("rejoice with those who rejoice, weep with those who weep") both prescribe *being* the comforter Qohelet's oppressed lacked — turning the diagnostic of 4:1 into the ethics of solidarity. The threefold cord of 4:12, finally, has a forward echo into the rabbinic *havruta* tradition and the Christian doctrine of the body of Christ (1 Cor 12) — both are answers to the lone miser of 4:7–8, the man with *no other* who has forgotten to ask *for whom*.
