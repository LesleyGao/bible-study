# 1 Samuel 28: The Woman at Endor

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter shifts the geographic stage decisively north. The Philistines, having mustered for an offensive deeper into Israelite territory than they had previously attempted, encamp at **Shunem** — a town on the south slope of the **Hill of Moreh**, identified with Solem/Sulam, surveyed by the German archaeologist Gottlieb Schumacher in 1903 and revisited in regional surveys by Yohanan Aharoni and more recently Adam Zertal. Saul gathers Israel opposite them on the slopes of **Mount Gilboa**, the long ridge running northwest-southeast at the eastern end of the Jezreel Valley. Between the two armies lies the broad alluvial floor of the Jezreel — the most strategic agricultural and military axis in the southern Levant, the corridor that connects the coastal Via Maris to the Jordan Valley and Damascus.

**Endor** (עֵין-דֹּר, *'Ein Dor*, "spring of the dwelling" or "spring of the generation") sits on the **north** side of the Hill of Moreh, behind the Philistine lines. The location is debated. Eusebius's *Onomasticon* placed it four Roman miles south of Tabor; medieval pilgrims followed this. The leading modern candidates are **Khirbet Safuriyeh** (sometimes spelled Tzafriyeh, near Mt. Tabor), and **Indur** (just north of the Hill of Moreh, preserving the toponym). Recent Israeli regional survey work — Zvi Gal and others — has favored an Indur identification but recognized that the site has not produced unambiguous Iron Age remains. What matters narratively is the geography: Endor lies *behind* the Philistine encampment at Shunem, in territory that should have been allotted to **Issachar** but was traditionally Manassite holding (cf. **Joshua 17:11**, where Endor and its dependent villages are listed among the towns Manasseh held within Issachar's portion). Saul has to skirt the Philistine line at night — a journey of perhaps fifteen kilometers, through enemy-controlled terrain — to consult the medium. The risk of the journey itself is part of the chapter's portrait of Saul's desperation.

The chapter's institutional world is one of late-tribal-monarchic Israel under acute external pressure. The Philistines have moved north out of the coastal plain into the Jezreel — beyond their previous operational zone. Aren Maeir's Tell es-Safi excavations have shown the late-eleventh-century Philistine pentapolis at the height of its power, and the **bichrome and monochrome pottery** of Iron I Philistine sites has been traced into the Jezreel and Beth-Shean regions, archaeologically corroborating the chapter's picture of Philistine military penetration of the north. The chapter's strategic setting is not literary embellishment; it tracks the actual Iron I military situation.

The chapter's central institution is **necromancy** — divination by consulting the dead. The Hebrew terminology is technical and contested. The medium is a ***ba'alat 'ov*** (בַּעֲלַת אוֹב, "mistress of an *'ov*"). The noun ***'ov*** (אוֹב) is etymologically debated. Three main proposals: (1) a **necromantic pit** or hole into which sacrifices were poured to summon the dead — Theodore J. Lewis (*Cults of the Dead in Ancient Israel and Ugarit*, 1989) argues this on the strength of cognates and Mesopotamian parallels; (2) the **ancestor spirit** itself — Brian Schmidt (*Israel's Beneficent Dead*, 1994) presses this, with Ugaritic *Rapi'uma* (the deified dead) as background; (3) a physical **vessel or chest** used in ventriloquist necromancy — the LXX renders *'ov* as *engastrimythos* ("ventriloquist," literally "belly-speaker"), and Josephus follows. The same root in Job 32:19 means "wineskin," and some have read the term as describing a cult object resembling a skin bag. **Lewis's** pit-and-spirit reading is currently dominant among scholars. The parallel term ***yidde'oni*** (יִדְּעֹנִי, "knowing one," from *yada*, "to know") refers to a familiar spirit or its medium.

The institutional context is **Levitical-Deuteronomic prohibition** of necromancy: **Lev 19:31, 20:6, 20:27; Deut 18:10–11**. The Deuteronomic list of banned divinatory practices (*ḥover ḥaver*, *sho'el 'ov ve-yidde'oni*, *doresh el ha-metim*) is comprehensive. Saul himself had enforced this prohibition (28:3, 9), making his consultation of the medium a transgression of his own royal policy. **The 'ov practice has ANE parallels**: Mesopotamian *kispum* rites (regular offerings to ancestors with provision for divinatory contact, well-documented at Mari, Emar, and Ur III period), Hittite *ḫešta*-house rituals, and the Ugaritic Rapi'uma texts (KTU 1.20–22). Egyptian Books of the Dead and the broader Egyptian funerary tradition share the cosmological assumption that the dead are accessible. Israel's prohibition is the regional outlier, not the global norm.

Five Hebrew terms warrant attention. **'Ov** (אוֹב) — discussed above. **Yidde'oni** (יִדְּעֹנִי, "familiar spirit"). **Elohim** (אֱלֹהִים, in v. 13 the medium says "I see *elohim* coming up out of the earth") — the noun is plural in form and notoriously ambiguous; here it likely means "a divine being" or "a numinous figure," not "God" or "gods" in the technical theological sense. **Karet** (כָּרֵת, "to cut off," used in v. 9 of Saul's purge of mediums) — the standard verb of Levitical exclusion. **Me'il** (מְעִיל, "robe," v. 14) — Samuel's identifying garment, the same robe he tore in the confrontation of **1 Sam 15:27** and the same garment Hannah brought him annually as a child (1 Sam 2:19); the word recurs as a leitmotif. The **fattened calf** (***'egel marbeq***, עֵגֶל מַרְבֵּק, v. 24) is the king's-portion sacrifice — the woman of Endor breaks her household economy to host a man she now knows is the king who tried to kill her.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is heroic prose narrative within the **History of David's Rise**, but the chapter is generically distinctive — it is the only sustained necromantic episode in the Hebrew Bible, and one of the most closely studied passages in biblical narrative for its theological, psychological, and dramatic compression.

The chapter is often read as the **tragic peripeteia** of Saul's story. **David Gunn**, in *The Fate of King Saul* (1980), made this the central reading: Saul has been moving, since chapter 13, toward a single inescapable end, and the night at Endor is the chapter where the hero finally turns to face it. Gunn reads the chapter alongside Greek tragedy — the disguised hero, the consultation of the oracle, the hearing of doom, the final meal — and finds Saul more dignified, more pitiable, more theologically serious than centuries of Christian and Jewish interpretation have allowed.

The structure is a tight diptych. Verses 1–2 are the **frame**: David's predicament with Achish, which the chapter sets up but defers. Verses 3–25 are the **Endor narrative**, itself in three movements: (a) Saul's silence from YHWH and decision to consult the medium (vv. 3–14); (b) Samuel's appearance and oracle of doom (vv. 15–19); (c) Saul's collapse and the medium's hospitality (vv. 20–25).

**Devices.** The **threefold silence** of v. 6 (dreams, Urim, prophets) is the chapter's most chilling formal element — the systematic exhaustion of legitimate channels. **Robert Alter** observes that this list is itself an internal reference to the legitimate divinatory institutions Saul has access to, all of which fail simultaneously. **Polzin** (*Samuel and the Deuteronomist*) reads the silence as judicial: YHWH does not answer because Saul stands under judgment.

The **disguise motif** (v. 8) is one of biblical narrative's most ironic. Saul disguises himself to escape recognition, but the medium recognizes him at the moment Samuel appears (v. 12). **Bar-Efrat** notes that disguise in biblical narrative typically signals moral inversion: Tamar (Gen 38), Jeroboam's wife (1 Kgs 14), Ahab (1 Kgs 22). Saul's disguise places him in a sequence of figures whose attempted concealment is exposed by divine action.

**The robe** (*me'il*, v. 14) is the chapter's most studied detail. **Lyle Eslinger**, **Polzin**, and **Alter** have all noted the metonymic chain: Hannah brings child Samuel a *me'il* annually (1 Sam 2:19); adult Samuel tears Saul's *me'il* in declaring the kingdom torn (1 Sam 15:27–28); David takes the corner of Saul's *me'il* in the cave (1 Sam 24:4–5); and now Samuel reappears in the same *me'il*, the garment in which he pronounced Saul's rejection, to confirm the rejection in the realm of the dead. The robe is the chapter's grammar of doom.

**The meal at the end** (vv. 21–25) is one of the most moving and disputed scenes in 1 Samuel. The *ba'alat 'ov*, who began as a transgressive figure under the king's sentence of death, ends as the figure who shows Saul his last hospitality. The **fattened calf** and **unleavened bread** are sacral terms (compare Abraham's hosting at Mamre, Gen 18:6–7, where the same vocabulary appears). **Adele Berlin** has read the meal as a darkly inverted royal meal — the king is fed by the criminal he hunted, in a non-sanctuary, on the eve of his death. **Polzin** reads it as a quasi-Eucharistic moment, a feeding for the journey to Sheol. The pathos is unmistakable.

**Textual variants** are significant in this chapter. **4QSam-a**, where preserved, shows several differences from MT — the most discussed in v. 1 ("In those days") and v. 4 (with possible variants in the encampment site name) — but the most important variant is in **the LXX of v. 19**, which has minor wording differences in Samuel's oracle. **Emanuel Tov** (*Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible*) and **Eugene Ulrich** treat the chapter as illustrating the still-fluid state of the Samuel text in the late Second Temple period. **P. Kyle McCarter**'s Anchor Bible volume gives a careful textual apparatus. None of the variants change the chapter's thrust, but they illustrate that the necromantic episode circulated in multiple textual forms.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The chapter's place in the canonical arc is enormous: it is Saul's last interview with Samuel, Saul's last theophany of any kind, and the public hinge between the rejection of the first king and the elevation of the second. In Christian interpretive history, the chapter became a battleground for the question of what the dead can do — a question with consequences for the cult of the saints, the doctrine of purgatory, the legitimacy of mediumship, and the ontology of post-mortem existence.

**Jewish interpretation.** **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 6.14.2–4) handles the chapter with surprising directness. He praises the medium of Endor for her hospitality (one of the rare biblical women he eulogizes), and treats Samuel's appearance as a real apparition — the soul of Samuel really did come up. He moralizes Saul's death as the inevitable consequence of disobedience, but the chapter also models, for Josephus, the virtue of generosity in the unlikely (the medium fed the king who would have killed her). **Pseudo-Philo**'s *LAB* (chs. 64–65) embellishes the episode with apocryphal detail: the medium recognizes Saul because, in this telling, dead prophets ascend rather than descend, and only a king's request could prompt that anomaly.

The classical rabbis are divided. **b. Berakhot 12b** and parallels treat Samuel's appearance as real, with the medium as a genuine necromancer whose practice was prohibited but operative. **Pirqei deRabbi Eliezer** 33 develops the apparition with apocalyptic detail. **Rashi** holds the apparition was real and Samuel really spoke. **Radak (Kimhi)** is more cautious and surveys multiple opinions, including the demonic-impersonation reading (which he ultimately rejects). **Ralbag (Gersonides)**, with his characteristic Aristotelian rationalism, takes the boldest path: the apparition was a kind of vivid prophetic vision, not a literal raising of the dead — the woman's craft was deception, but YHWH used the moment to communicate truth to Saul through Saul's own faculties. **Abarbanel** comes down between Rashi and Ralbag: the apparition was real but not a genuine necromantic raising; YHWH made an exception, sending Samuel for this single occasion to confirm Saul's doom.

**Yair Zakovitch** and **Avivah Zornberg** in modern Jewish scholarship both treat the chapter as the canon's most psychologically penetrating portrait of a man losing himself. Zornberg's reading (in *The Murmuring Deep* and elsewhere) emphasizes Saul's hunger — physical, theological, relational — as the chapter's organizing motif. **Moshe Garsiel** treats the chapter's parallels (disguise, recognition, doom) within his comparative-structure framework.

**Christian interpretation.** Patristic engagement with this chapter was intense and divided, and **Origen** stands at the center. Origen's *Homily 5 on 1 Samuel* (preserved in Latin via Rufinus, with substantial Greek fragments) takes the boldest stance: **Samuel really did appear** and really spoke. Origen treats this as a strong claim about the realm of the dead — the prophet is in the place of the dead, accessible to YHWH's call, and the medium's craft, however illegitimate, did not invent the appearance. Origen's reading was contested in his own day and condemned later. **Eustathius of Antioch** (early fourth century) wrote a polemical treatise *On the Belly-Speaker* (*Peri tēs engastrimythou*) refuting Origen at length, arguing that what appeared was a **demon impersonating Samuel** — the Devil cannot truly raise the dead but can produce a deceiving image. **Tertullian** (*De Anima* 56–57) had already taken a similar position.

**Augustine**'s view evolved. In earlier writings (*De Diversis Quaestionibus ad Simplicianum* 2.3) he leaned toward the Eustathian demonic-impersonation reading; in later works (*De Cura pro Mortuis Gerenda* 15) he allowed that, by special divine permission, Samuel may genuinely have appeared, while the medium remained ignorant of what was happening. Augustine's mature position became the more common Latin Christian reading: a real appearance, divinely permitted, mediated through the unlawful craft. **Jerome** stays closer to demonic impersonation. **John Chrysostom** (in homilies on the Old Testament) emphasizes Saul's despair and the moral lesson of forsaking YHWH.

**Gregory the Great** (*Moralia in Job*) allegorizes the chapter; **Pierre Maraval** and **John Reider** have written on the patristic reception of Endor as the test case for Christian theology of necromancy and the post-mortem state. The chapter shaped Patristic and Medieval debates on whether the dead can be communicated with — debates that fed directly into the development of the cult of the saints, the doctrine of intercession, and ultimately the prohibitions of the Council of Trent.

The medieval consolidation belongs to **Aquinas**, who in *Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 174, a. 5 ad 4, follows Augustine's mature position: Samuel did appear, by divine permission. Aquinas distinguishes carefully between what the medium *attempted* (illicit necromancy) and what *occurred* (a genuine, divinely authorized apparition).

The Reformation sharpened the question. **Calvin** (sermons on 1 Samuel, 1559) is unambiguous: the apparition was **demonic**, not Samuel. The theological cost of allowing genuine necromancy was, for Calvin, too great — it would seem to validate the medium's craft and Roman Catholic invocations of saints in one stroke. Calvin's reading is rigorously polemical and fits his anti-superstition program. **Martin Luther**, surprisingly, takes a more nuanced position: in his *Lectures on Genesis* and other places, he allows that the appearance may have been Samuel under exceptional divine permission, while sharply condemning all ordinary necromantic practice. Luther's position was less common in later Protestantism but reflects his characteristic willingness to follow the text against his polemical interests. The Lutheran orthodoxy that followed often hewed closer to Calvin.

**Critical scholarship**. **Wellhausen**, **S.R. Driver**, and the early historical critics treated the chapter as preserving a pre-Deuteronomistic folkloric narrative, perhaps from a "northern" source, embedded in the larger Deuteronomistic frame. **Frank Moore Cross**'s schema places it in Dtr1, the pro-Davidic redaction. **P. Kyle McCarter** (*I Samuel*, AB) treats it as part of the History of David's Rise apologetic — the chapter exonerates David from Gilboa by establishing he was elsewhere (forced out by Philistine commanders in ch. 29). **Ralph Klein** (WBC), **Hans Wilhelm Hertzberg** (OTL), and **David Tsumura** (NICOT) provide standard commentary frameworks. **Theodore Lewis** has done the most influential work on the necromantic backdrop.

**Contemporary** readings. **Walter Brueggemann** treats the chapter as the canon's most powerful portrait of a man seeking a word and being given the word that destroys him. The medium's hospitality, for Brueggemann, opens a theological space the official tradition could not — grace from outside the cult. **Robert Polzin** (*Samuel and the Deuteronomist*) reads the chapter as the climax of the narrator's ironic engagement with kingship. **Eugene Peterson** (*Leap Over a Wall*) treats Endor as the dark mirror chapter to David's wilderness chapters — Saul, deprived of the divine word, will substitute any voice for silence. **Ellen Davis** highlights the medium's ministry of food as a counter-image of priesthood. **David Gunn**'s *The Fate of King Saul* remains the indispensable literary-tragic reading.

The medium of Endor has drawn sustained attention. The chapter's portrait is, against expectation, the portrait of a competent figure — she practices a craft the king's policy has criminalized, she recognizes the disguised king and her own danger, she nonetheless does the chapter's only act of unforced kindness, feeding the man who hunted her in a non-sanctuary on the eve of his death. The meal scene is one of the rare biblical scenes in which a woman is named hostess to a king (the vocabulary echoes Abraham's hosting at Mamre), and several commentators have read it, as Berlin does, as a darkly inverted royal meal: the king fed by the criminal he pursued, outside the cult, on the last night of his reign.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions structure the chapter; both have shaped two millennia of theological argument.

**First tension: did Samuel really appear?** The exegetical question is also a metaphysical one — what is the relation between the dead, the divine, and the unlicensed practitioner?

*Position 1 (real appearance, demonic permission):* The figure who came up was not Samuel but a **demon impersonating** him. **Tertullian, Eustathius, Jerome, Calvin** hold this. The argument: necromancy is prohibited because it cannot succeed in legitimate terms; what appears is always demonic deception. Samuel cannot have been at the medium's disposal. Steelman: the canon's prohibitions (Lev 20, Deut 18) presuppose that necromantic outcomes are not real consultations of the dead but engagements with deceiving spirits. To grant a real Samuel is to undercut the prohibition. The chapter's silence on the *mechanism* of appearance is theologically deliberate — the narrator does not say Samuel rose; he says "the woman saw."

*Position 2 (real appearance, divine permission):* Samuel really came, by YHWH's exceptional sanction. **Origen, Augustine (mature), Aquinas, Luther, Rashi, Abarbanel** in various forms. The argument: the chapter speaks of "Samuel" without qualification through verses 12–20; the doom Samuel pronounces is fulfilled exactly; the canon's later figures (notably Sirach 46:20, the Greek text, "Even after his sleep he prophesied") read it as a genuine post-mortem prophecy. YHWH used the unlicensed setting to deliver a final word. Steelman: the theological prohibition of necromancy is moral, not metaphysical — necromancy is forbidden because it attempts to use the dead, not because it is impossible. YHWH's exceptional intervention here neither validates the practice nor denies the principle.

*Position 3 (vision-only, no real appearance):* What occurred was a prophetic vision granted to Saul (and possibly to the medium), with no actual raising. **Ralbag (Gersonides)** holds something like this. The medium's craft is empty; YHWH used the moment to communicate. Steelman: this position preserves both the prohibition's rationale and the chapter's truthfulness without committing to a metaphysics of soul-recall.

*Where this leaves us:* The chapter is theologically maximally productive precisely because it does not adjudicate. The narrator describes appearance, words, and doom; he does not specify mechanism. Two thousand years of debate have made the chapter into a Rorschach for the reader's prior theology of death and divine economy.

**Second tension: divine silence and human violation.** YHWH does not answer Saul (v. 6); Saul violates the prohibition he himself enforced. Is the silence itself the cause of the violation, or is the violation the cause of the silence?

*Position 1 (judgment-first):* The silence is YHWH's judgment for Saul's prior disobedience (1 Sam 15) — a settled rejection finally taking external form. Saul's violation in turning to the medium is the symptom, not the cause; he has been on this trajectory since Amalek. **Polzin, Brueggemann, Calvin** read this way. *Position 2 (existential-tragic):* The silence is itself the test, and Saul fails it. A different king (David in ch. 30, when YHWH does answer) responds to silence with patience; Saul responds to silence by going elsewhere. The chapter is moral, not metaphysical. **Gunn, Eugene Peterson** read this way. *Position 3 (relational-tragic):* The silence and the violation are two sides of one collapsed relationship. YHWH and Saul have been drifting apart since Gilgal (ch. 13), and the chapter is the moment that drift becomes a chasm. **Brueggemann** captures this with characteristic ambiguity. Steelman the existential reading: the chapter explicitly makes Saul's hunger for a word the engine of his violation, and the chapter explicitly contrasts him with David, who in the next campaign-narrative will face the same crisis (1 Sam 30) and will respond by *consulting the ephod* and by *receiving an answer*. The contrast is the chapter's pedagogical structure.

*Where this leaves us:* The chapter places silence where one expects voice. It refuses to provide a doctrine of why YHWH falls silent or a method for restoring the voice. It tells a story in which a king's failure to bear silence well is part of what makes him no longer king.

**One unresolved question:** The medium of Endor — by the chapter's account a sympathetic figure who fed the king who hunted her — operates on the wrong side of the canon's prohibitions and yet shows the chapter's only act of unforced kindness. Is the narrator critiquing the prohibitions, complicating them, or simply leaving the human face of the prohibited as a problem the canon does not resolve? The chapter does not say.

## The Thread

Several threads. **Backward:** Samuel's *me'il* (v. 14) carries the entire history of the prophet-king relationship — Hannah's annual robe (1 Sam 2:19), the torn robe at Saul's rejection (1 Sam 15:27), the corner of Saul's robe taken by David (1 Sam 24:4–5). The robe enters the realm of the dead and reappears with the same word. **Backward farther:** the prohibition Saul enforced and now violates (Lev 19:31, 20:6, 20:27; Deut 18:10–11) places him under his own decree. **Forward immediately:** the doom — "tomorrow you and your sons shall be with me" (v. 19) — is fulfilled in 1 Sam 31; the chapter is the explicit oracle that 1 Sam 31 enacts. **Forward farther:** David's contrasting consultation in 1 Sam 30:7–8 (where the ephod yields an answer) is the chapter's deliberate counterpoint. **Outward:** the necromantic prohibition recurs in 2 Kgs 21:6 (Manasseh) and 2 Kgs 23:24 (Josiah's reform). **Into Christian canon:** Sirach 46:20 cites Samuel's post-mortem prophecy as evidence of the prophet's enduring authority, and the chapter shapes patristic and medieval theology of the dead, the saints, and the post-mortem state for over a millennium. **Forward into the gospel narrative:** the disturbance of the dead and the appearance of the dead become motifs the gospels handle without anxiety (Moses and Elijah at the Transfiguration; the dead rising at Matthew 27:52–53), but those scenes are theologically continuous with, and theologically unlike, this one — and reading the gospels with 1 Samuel 28 in view is part of why patristic interpreters made Endor a hinge of their theology.
