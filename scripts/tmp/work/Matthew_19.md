# Matthew 19: Marriage, Eunuchs, and the Eye of the Needle

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Matthew 19 marks Jesus's geographical pivot — "**when Jesus had finished these sayings, he went away from Galilee and entered the region of Judea beyond the Jordan**" — and the start of the Jerusalem-bound journey. The phrase "**Judea beyond the Jordan**" describes **Peraea** (Hebrew *Ever Hayyarden*), the territory east of the Jordan ruled by **Herod Antipas** along with Galilee, a heavily Jewish region with cities like Pella and Gadara on its eastern margin. Jesus's route from Galilee to Jerusalem along Peraea is the standard Galilean-pilgrim route, avoiding Samaria, crossing the Jordan back into Judea proper at Jericho. This is where John the Baptist had operated and where Antipas had killed him; the geography is heavy with prophetic-political memory.

The chapter's central pericope is a halakhic dispute on **divorce**, and to read it historically is to be thrown into one of the liveliest controversies of first-century Jewish legal scholarship. **Deuteronomy 24:1-4** — the foundational text — permits a man to divorce his wife if he finds in her *erwat dabar* (עֶרְוַת דָּבָר), variously translated "indecency," "shameful matter," or literally "nakedness of a thing." The phrase was famously contested in the late Second Temple and early rabbinic period.

The **House of Shammai** read *erwat dabar* narrowly: the man may divorce his wife only for grave sexual misconduct (*erwat* taken as the dominant noun). The **House of Hillel** read it broadly: divorce is permissible for "any cause" (*dabar* taken as the dominant noun) — even, in the most permissive Hillelite glosses, for spoiling a meal. **m. Gittin 9:10** records the dispute directly: "**Beth Shammai say: a man may not divorce his wife unless he has found unchastity in her... Beth Hillel say: even if she spoiled a dish for him... Rabbi Akiba says: even if he found another fairer than she.**" This is exactly the legal landscape into which the Pharisees walk when they ask Jesus, "**is it lawful to divorce one's wife for any cause?**" The phrasing "**for any cause**" (Greek *kata pasan aitian*) is the technical formula of the Hillelite position. **David Instone-Brewer** in *Divorce and Remarriage in the Bible* makes this absolutely clear and is the leading recent scholar on the topic; **William Loader's** five-volume *Sexuality in the New Testament* and adjacent literatures provides extensive parallel rabbinic and Qumran material.

The Qumran community took yet a third, even stricter position. **CD 4:20-21** ("**they are caught in two snares: in fornication, by taking two wives in their lives, although the principle of creation is 'male and female he created them'**") and **11QTemple 57:17-19** appear to forbid divorce-with-remarriage altogether — possibly the closest pre-Christian analogue to Jesus's reading of Genesis 1-2 against Deuteronomy 24.

A Jewish woman in this period had limited divorce-initiation rights. The **ketubah** (marriage contract) was a major financial protection: divorce required the husband to pay out the dowry, which created a real economic disincentive. **Babatha's archive** from the early second century, discovered in the Cave of Letters at Nahal Hever, provides extraordinary documentary evidence of Jewish marriage and divorce practice at the edges of Roman Judaea. **Tal Ilan**'s *Jewish Women in Greco-Roman Palestine* and **Ross Kraemer**'s work are essential.

The Greco-Roman context is also relevant. Roman divorce was easier than Jewish — either party could initiate, and by the late Republic and early Empire, no-fault divorce was practically routine among the elite. **Augustus's** *lex Julia de adulteriis* (18 BCE) attempted to regulate but did not abolish ease of dissolution. **Suzanne Dixon**'s *The Roman Family* is the standard reference. Jesus's teaching, then, is sharper than even the most rigorous Jewish school and far sharper than Roman convention.

A few Greek terms anchor the chapter. **porneia** (πορνεία) — usually translated "sexual immorality" — is the contested noun in Jesus's exception clause at 19:9. The Greek covers a wide semantic range: prostitution, fornication, incest, adultery, idolatry-as-sexual-metaphor, sometimes specifically marriage within prohibited degrees of consanguinity (the LXX usage in Lev 18). **Joseph Fitzmyer** argued the *porneia* in the exception clause referred specifically to incestuous marriages forbidden by Leviticus 18 — relevant to Gentile converts who had married within prohibited degrees by Jewish law. Other scholars read *porneia* broadly as adultery. **eunouchos** (εὐνοῦχος) is the term for "eunuch" at 19:12 — both literal and figurative; the LXX usage (Esth 2:14, 4:5) covers physical castrates and high-ranking court officials. **kamēlos** (κάμηλος) and **rhaphidos** (ῥαφίδος) — camel and needle — anchor the famous hyperbole. **teleios** (τέλειος) — "perfect" or "complete" — is what Jesus says the rich young man would be if he sold his possessions; the term echoes 5:48 and is heavily contested in the two-tier-ethics debate.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

In Bacon's five-discourse architecture, Matthew 19 opens the third major narrative bridge — between the Community Discourse (chapter 18) and the Olivet Discourse (chapters 24-25). The closing formula at 19:1 ("**when Jesus had finished these sayings**") marks the discourse's end. The chapter's narrative function is to bring Jesus into Judea-bound proximity, raise the disciples' increasingly pointed questions about possessions and reward, and prepare the Triumphal Entry of chapter 21.

Synoptic relations are dense. **19:1-9** (the divorce question) parallels Mark 10:1-12, with two significant Matthean modifications: Mark has no exception clause; Matthew's "**except for sexual immorality**" is uniquely Matthean. Mark also has Jesus stating the prohibition symmetrically — "**whoever divorces his wife and marries another commits adultery against her, and if she divorces her husband and marries another, she commits adultery**" — assuming the Roman possibility of female divorce. Matthew's version, addressing a Jewish-Christian community, frames the prohibition asymmetrically. **Davies/Allison ICC** and **Luz Hermeneia** treat the relationship between Mark, Matthew, Luke 16:18, and 1 Cor 7:10-11 carefully.

**19:10-12** (the eunuch saying) is uniquely Matthean. Source-critically it is M material; **Davies/Allison** treat it as authentic Jesus tradition with a long history of difficult patristic and modern exegesis.

**19:13-15** (children blessed) parallels Mark 10:13-16 and Luke 18:15-17. **19:16-22** (the rich young man) parallels Mark 10:17-22 and Luke 18:18-23, with a famous Christological textual variation: Mark's "**why do you call me good? No one is good but God alone**" is modified in Matthew to "**why do you ask me about what is good? There is only one who is good**" — shifting the focus from Jesus's relation to divine goodness onto the abstract object of inquiry. **Bruce Metzger's** *Textual Commentary* notes this as Matthean redaction, possibly motivated by Christological sensitivity (the Markan formula could be read as denying Christ's deity). **Bart Ehrman's** *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* makes a closely related argument across many such variants.

**19:23-30** parallels Mark 10:23-31 and Luke 18:24-30, with one striking Matthean addition: **19:28**'s "**you who have followed me will also sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel**" — the *palingenesia* (παλιγγενεσία, "regeneration" or "new world") logion, partially paralleled at Luke 22:30 but with the regeneration language only in Matthew. **N.T. Wright** in *Jesus and the Victory of God* makes the case that this saying is fundamental to a properly Jewish reading of Jesus's mission: the regathering of Israel under the twelve apostles as eschatological judges of the restored twelve tribes.

The **camel through eye of needle** image (19:24) deserves a textual note. There is a long-standing but **wholly unfounded** popular interpretation that "eye of the needle" referred to a small Jerusalem gate through which a camel could pass on its knees. No first- or second-temple-period source attests such a gate; the interpretation appears to originate in a ninth-century commentary by **Theophylact of Ohrid** as a moralizing gloss and was popularized in the Victorian period. Modern critical commentaries — **Davies/Allison**, **France NICNT**, **Hagner WBC**, **Luz Hermeneia** — uniformly reject the gate-interpretation. The **literal hyperbole** is the point: the largest-known Palestinian animal cannot pass through the smallest-known household opening; the saying is an absolute impossibility. (Some manuscripts read *kamilon* — a thick rope — for *kamēlon* — camel; this is a transparent later attempt to soften the hyperbole and is rejected by all critical editions.)

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Matthew 19's reception history is one of the most consequential in Christian moral and social thought.

The **divorce question** has shaped two thousand years of Christian doctrine and practice. The **Patristic** tradition was generally strict. **Hermas's Mandates** (early second century) prohibits remarriage after divorce except in the case of the spouse's death. **Athenagoras of Athens** (*Plea* 33) calls a second marriage "decent adultery." **Tertullian** in *Ad Uxorem* and *De Monogamia* (the latter from his Montanist phase) is even stricter. **Origen** (*Commentary on Matthew* 14.16-23) is more nuanced, recognizing pastoral exigencies. **Chrysostom**'s *Homilies on Matthew* 62 treats the strict reading as binding. **Augustine** in *De Bono Conjugali* and *De Adulterinis Conjugiis* developed what became the classical Western reading: marriage as indissoluble *ratum et consummatum* sacrament, with separation possible but remarriage forbidden while the spouse lives.

The **Catholic** position formalized this Augustinian reading at the Council of Florence (1439), the Council of Trent (Session 24, 1563), and the **Code of Canon Law** (1983). Marriage is a sacrament (CIC §1055-1057), indissoluble (§1141), and what God has joined cannot be put asunder. The *porneia* of Matthew 19:9 has been read as: (a) marriage within prohibited degrees of consanguinity, with Pauline-privilege development for Gentile converts (Fitzmyer, the Catholic dogmatic tradition); (b) physical separation permitted (with no remarriage during the spouse's life); (c) basis for **annulment** (declaration of nullity, that the marriage was never sacramentally valid). **John Paul II's** *Familiaris Consortio* (1981) and **Francis's** *Amoris Laetitia* (2016) extend the pastoral discussion, especially around communion for the divorced-and-remarried, but do not modify the doctrinal core.

The **Eastern Orthodox** tradition has practiced *oikonomia* — pastoral economy — allowing remarriage after divorce in cases of adultery, abandonment, attempted murder, prolonged absence, and a few other grounds, with second marriages performed in a penitential rite distinct from the first marriage's rite. The development is patristic, conciliar, and canon-legal across centuries; **John Meyendorff's** *Marriage: An Orthodox Perspective* is the standard treatment.

The **Reformed** tradition followed Calvin's reading: divorce and remarriage are permitted for adultery and willful desertion (1 Cor 7:15 supplying the second exception in the Erasmian-Reformed tradition). The Westminster Confession 24.5-6 codifies this. **William Heth and Gordon Wenham**'s *Jesus and Divorce* surveys the Protestant exegetical tradition; **Heth** later modified his earlier strict-no-remarriage position. **Instone-Brewer** has argued forcefully that Jesus's exception clause is a rejection specifically of the Hillelite "any cause" reading, not a rejection of all divorce, and that the Deuteronomy 24 grounds (sexual unfaithfulness) and the Exodus 21 grounds (abandonment, neglect of food/clothing/conjugal rights) remain valid.

The **Anabaptist** tradition has been the strictest of Protestant readings; **Schleitheim** and Mennonite practice generally forbid remarriage during the spouse's life.

The **eunuch saying** (19:12) has shaped the entire history of Christian celibacy. **Origen** is the most striking — and tragic — early reader: per Eusebius's *Ecclesiastical History* 6.8, Origen took the eunuch saying so literally that he castrated himself, an act he later regretted and which the Council of Nicaea (325 CE, Canon 1) explicitly prohibited for the priesthood. **Athanasius**, **Basil**, and **Gregory of Nyssa** developed the saying as the foundation of monastic celibacy. **Augustine** in *De Bono Viduitatis* and *De Sancta Virginitate* developed it further. The Catholic priestly celibacy discipline (formalized at the Second Lateran Council, 1139) draws explicitly on 19:12; the Reformation rejected mandatory celibacy as having no scriptural warrant beyond charisma. **Karen Jo Torjesen's** *When Women Were Priests* and **Peter Brown's** *The Body and Society* are the major modern historical treatments.

The **rich young man** pericope and the "**eye of the needle**" saying have been the central Christian text on wealth and discipleship. **The two-tier ethics** reading — taken up systematically by Aquinas in *Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 184-189 — distinguishes the **commandments** (binding on all: keep the Decalogue, love God and neighbor) from the **counsels of perfection** (poverty, chastity, obedience — binding only on those called to religious life). The text supports this distinction: Jesus tells the man "**if you would enter life, keep the commandments**" and only then says "**if you would be perfect**" (*ei theleis teleios einai*) sell what you have. **Athanasius's** *Life of Antony*, **Cassian's** *Conferences*, **Benedict's Rule**, and the entire monastic tradition build on this two-tier reading. The **Catechism of the Catholic Church** §§915-933 codifies it.

The **Reformation** rejected the two-tier reading. **Luther's** *Babylonian Captivity* and **Calvin's** *Institutes* IV.13 treat the counsels-of-perfection as a Catholic corruption that creates two classes of Christian — the religious (perfect) and the laity (mediocre). For Luther, every Christian is called to the same radical discipleship in his or her vocation; for Calvin, every believer is called to use possessions for God's glory and the neighbor's good without compulsory poverty. **Bonhoeffer's** *Cost of Discipleship* turns the saying back into a call universally binding on every disciple, refusing any two-tier distinction — but doing so by intensifying the demand for all rather than relaxing it for any.

A strong **social-justice reading** — continuous with the Hebrew prophets' insistence on righteousness toward the poor and with Catholic social teaching on the universal destination of goods (from *Rerum Novarum* through *Populorum Progressio* and *Sollicitudo Rei Socialis*) — treats the rich-young-man pericope as a foundational text on the economic claims the gospel makes. **Ched Myers's** *Binding the Strong Man*, a politically attentive reading of the Markan parallel, takes the "go, sell what you possess and give to the poor" not as exceptional counsel but as gospel norm; the broader prophetic-ethics tradition reads it as a warning that wealth is, structurally and not merely personally, an obstacle to the kingdom.

The **Jewish-historical** reading is illuminating. **Geza Vermes**'s *The Religion of Jesus the Jew* situates the entire chapter within recognizable Jewish moral debates of the first century. **Levine** in *Short Stories by Jesus* and *The Misunderstood Jew* warns Christian readers against using the rich-young-man pericope to construct a "Christianity of grace versus Judaism of works" foil; the Decalogue listing is straightforwardly Jewish, and the call to almsgiving is at the heart of rabbinic *tzedakah* tradition. **Sanders**'s *Jesus and Judaism* sets the divorce dispute squarely within the Hillel-Shammai legal world.

Modern critical commentaries are essential. **Davies/Allison ICC** is foundational; **Luz Hermeneia** is comprehensive on reception; **France NICNT** balanced Protestant; **Keener NICNT** strong on Jewish background; **Hagner WBC** technical; **Nolland NIGTC** on Greek and Aramaic substrate; **Harrington Sacra Pagina** Catholic-critical. **N.T. Wright's** *Jesus and the Victory of God* is essential on the regathering-of-Israel reading at 19:28.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: How are we to read the divorce teaching?**

At least four positions deserve to be steel-manned.

The **Catholic indissolubility position** holds that marriage is an indissoluble sacramental bond, that Jesus's "**what God has joined together, let not man separate**" is absolute, and that the *porneia* exception is to be read as either (a) marriage within prohibited degrees of consanguinity (Fitzmyer's reading), (b) basis for declaration of nullity (the marriage was never sacramentally valid), or (c) ground for separation without remarriage. Steel-manned, this reading insists that the exception clause cannot be read as licensing remarriage without dissolving the entire sacramental theology of marriage and that Jesus's appeal to Genesis 1-2's "from the beginning it was not so" is the controlling hermeneutic.

The **Eastern Orthodox** *oikonomia* position holds that the Church can pastorally permit remarriage in cases of adultery, abandonment, and a small number of other grave grounds, while preserving the sacramental ideal. Steel-manned, this reading holds that Christian marriage is genuinely sacramental but that fallen humanity sometimes requires the Church's pastoral mercy without surrender of the underlying ideal.

The **Reformed / Erasmian** position reads the *porneia* exception as licensing remarriage after adultery and (on 1 Cor 7:15 grounds) after willful desertion. Steel-manned, this reading insists that any reading that denies the plain force of the exception clause is doing exegetical violence to Matthew, that Paul's apostolic guidance in 1 Cor 7 supplies a complementary exception, and that Christ's mercy toward the wronged spouse must inform the church's pastoral practice.

The **Instone-Brewer / contextual-Jewish** position reads the dispute as a polemic against the Hillelite "any cause" school, with Jesus siding broadly with Shammai (sexual unfaithfulness as the only legitimate grounds for divorce) but adding additional Old Testament grounds for divorce (Exodus 21:10-11 — neglect of food, clothing, conjugal rights) that were universally recognized in first-century Jewish practice. Steel-manned, this reading insists that the Pharisees' question is a specific halakhic question demanding a specific halakhic answer, and that reading Jesus as rejecting all divorce strips the question of its first-century texture.

**Where this leaves us:** the text is sharper than any first-century Jewish school except possibly Qumran, and substantially sharper than Roman convention. The exception clause is a real exception, but its meaning is contested. Christians who marry are entering a covenantal-sacramental bond that the Lord himself has guarded with extraordinary severity, and any tradition's pastoral practice should be tested against the stringency of the Lord's own words rather than the looseness of late-modern divorce culture.

**Tension Two: Wealth, perfection, and the call of every disciple.**

The rich young man pericope has been the central text of Christian thought on wealth, vocation, and discipleship.

The **two-tier reading** (Aquinas, the medieval Catholic tradition, the religious orders) holds that the Decalogue is binding on all Christians and that the "if you would be perfect" sale of possessions is a counsel for those called to consecrated religious life — a higher way, not the only way. Steel-manned, this reading insists that the gospel itself distinguishes commandments from counsels, that monastic life is a real charism, and that the Catholic Church's preservation of the religious life is a faithful following of the chapter's structure.

The **universal-demand reading** (Luther, Calvin, Bonhoeffer, the radical Protestant tradition) holds that the call to the rich young man is the call to every disciple, that the two-tier system is a Catholic invention without scriptural warrant, and that every Christian's possessions are held in trust for God and the neighbor. Steel-manned, this reading insists that the gospel cannot tolerate two classes of Christian and that the camel-needle saying is a genuine warning to every wealthy follower.

The **prophetic-ethics / social-justice reading** (the Hebrew prophets' tradition, Catholic social teaching, Ched Myers) holds that the pericope is a foundational text on the gospel's economic claims — that wealth is, structurally and not just personally, an obstacle to the kingdom, and that Christian economic ethics begins from solidarity with those left out. Steel-manned, this reading insists that the privatization of the call ("just my personal stewardship") empties it of the structural-social demand the chapter actually makes.

A fourth, **prosperity-gospel** reading (which deserves to be named only to be rejected) holds that 19:29's "**a hundredfold**" return is a divine economic principle for present material reward — a reading that requires inverting nearly every other emphasis of the chapter and that the Christian tradition uniformly rejects. The "hundredfold" is an eschatological-ecclesial promise (homes, brothers, sisters in the new community of faith), not a return-on-investment formula.

**Where this leaves us:** the camel cannot pass through the eye of the needle. Jesus's own diagnosis is unambiguous: "**with man this is impossible.**" Whatever theology we settle on, the chapter forbids any reading that domesticates the saying. The hyperbole is not exaggeration; it is reality. The rich young man **goes away sorrowful** — and that ending, with no recovery scene, with no recall and reconciliation, is one of the most painful in the Gospels. **Bonhoeffer** is right that the call comes unmediated to every disciple, and the question of what we do with possessions is not a question that any of us can outsource to a religious order.

**The unresolved question:** Peter's reply at 19:27 — "**we have left everything and followed you. What then will we have?**" — and Jesus's startling promise at 19:28 of twelve thrones over the twelve tribes of Israel raise an unresolved eschatological question. **Wright** in *Jesus and the Victory of God* reads the saying as a literal promise of the regathering of Israel under apostolic judgment. The Catholic tradition has read it as anticipating apostolic episcopal authority. The Reformed tradition has read it as eschatologically realized in the new creation. What is the *palingenesia*, the regeneration, the new world? The chapter sets the question and does not answer it, and the rest of Matthew (chapters 24-25, especially) will return to it.

## The Thread

The cross-references for Matthew 19 reach in every canonical direction. **Genesis 1:27** ("**male and female he created them**") and **Genesis 2:24** ("**a man shall leave his father and his mother and hold fast to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh**") are explicitly quoted by Jesus as the theological substrate of the divorce teaching. **Deuteronomy 24:1-4** is the contested text. **Malachi 2:13-16** ("**I hate divorce, says the Lord**") is the prophetic substrate of the strictness. **Isaiah 56:3-5** — the eunuchs given a name better than sons and daughters — is a likely substrate of the eunuch saying; **Acts 8:26-40** — the Ethiopian eunuch baptized by Philip — is its NT echo.

Look forward to **Matthew 22:23-33** — the Sadducees' question on resurrection-marriage — where Jesus's "**they neither marry nor are given in marriage**" further illuminates marriage's penultimate-not-ultimate place in the economy of the kingdom. Look forward to **Matthew 25:31-46** — the sheep-and-goats judgment — where the "go, sell what you have and give to the poor" of 19:21 is universalized as the criterion of judgment. Look forward to **1 Corinthians 7** — Paul's extended pastoral handling of marriage, divorce, and celibacy as charisms — where the apostolic-Pauline development of Jesus's teaching is worked out in the early church.

Flag forward to **Acts 4:32-37** — the early Jerusalem church's voluntary common-purse — and to **Acts 5:1-11** — Ananias and Sapphira — where the rich-young-man pericope's economic theology is being lived out and contested in the apostolic generation. The chapter is not ancient curiosity; it is the constitutional document of every Christian household and every Christian's relationship to possessions.
