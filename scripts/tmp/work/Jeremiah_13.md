# Jeremiah 13: The Spoiled Waistcloth and the Skirts Pulled Over the Face

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jeremiah 13 sits within the long, slow collapse of the Judean monarchy under the last four kings of David's line: Josiah (640–609 BC), whose reform of 622 had purged the high places and centralized worship in Jerusalem; Jehoahaz (deposed by Pharaoh Neco after three months in 609); Jehoiakim (609–598), the Egyptian client who became a Babylonian vassal after the Battle of Carchemish in 605, then rebelled; Jehoiachin (598–597), the eighteen-year-old who reigned three months and three days before being marched to Babylon in the deportation of 597; and Zedekiah (597–586), who would see the city burn. The chapter's lament over "the king and the queen mother" (13:18) is widely read by Lundbom (Anchor), Holladay (Hermeneia), and Bright as datable to the spring of 597 — the Babylonian Chronicle (BM 21946) records Nebuchadnezzar's seizure of the city on the second of Adar (16 March 597), and 2 Kings 24:8–17 names both the young king Jehoiachin and the *gebirah* Nehushta daughter of Elnathan among those carried off. The "cities of the Negeb shut up" (13:19) tracks the steady Edomite and Babylonian pressure on Judah's southern flank during the same window; the Lachish Ostraca, written within a decade of this oracle, will later show that flank collapsing in real time.

The material culture of this period is Late Iron II Judah: a dense settlement landscape from Lachish to Mizpah, a literate scribal bureaucracy, and a court whose internal politics we can now reconstruct from clay. The bullae of Baruch ben Neriah (Jeremiah's scribe), Gemariah ben Shaphan (the official whose chamber Baruch reads from in Jer 36), Jehucal ben Shelemiah (Jer 37:3), and Pashhur ben Immer (Jer 20) — recovered respectively from the antiquities market and from controlled excavations in the City of David — anchor Jeremiah's narrative in named, fingerprint-marked individuals. The household waistcloth itself has a material life: an *ezor* (אֵזוֹר) of *pishtim* (linen) was the inner garment worn directly against the loins, the most intimate piece of clothing a man wore. It is precisely *not* the priestly *ephod* nor the outer *simlah*; it is the cloth that touches skin. The choice is freighted: priests wore linen breeches "to cover the flesh of their nakedness" (Ex 28:42), and Jeremiah, of priestly stock, would have known the texture and the symbolism.

Authorship and dating are unusually well-anchored for a prophetic book. Jeremiah ben Hilkiah was of the Anathoth priestly line (likely Abiatharide, exiled north under Solomon's purge — 1 Kgs 2:26), and Baruch ben Neriah was his scribal partner. The book's textual history, however, is famously double: the Masoretic Text (MT) is roughly one-eighth longer than the Septuagint (LXX), and the Qumran fragment 4QJer-b matches the shorter LXX *Vorlage*, demonstrating that two distinct Hebrew editions circulated at Qumran in the late Second Temple period. Tov, McKane (ICC), and Stulman treat the LXX as the earlier edition and MT as expansionist; Lundbom defends MT priority on a per-passage basis. For chapter 13 the divergences are minor but real: LXX's verse 13:7 lacks the doubled "Euphrates," and the wineskin oracle (13:12–14) has small reorderings.

Archaeological and textual evidence converges on the chapter's polemic. The "abominations on the hills in the field" (13:27) — the Asherah-cult and high-place worship Jeremiah denounces — are not literary fictions. The Khirbet el-Qom inscription (Tomb 3, ca. 750 BC, now in the Israel Museum) reads "Blessed be Uriyahu by YHWH . . . and by his Asherah," and the Kuntillet Ajrud pithoi (8th c. BC, Sinai) repeat the formula "YHWH of Samaria and his Asherah / YHWH of Teman and his Asherah." These texts, a century older than Jeremiah, document precisely the syncretism the prophet has spent his career denouncing as covenant betrayal. When Jeremiah accuses Judah of "going after other gods" (13:10), the archaeological record gives the accusation teeth.

Key Hebrew terms for this chapter: *ezor* (אֵזוֹר), the linen waistcloth or loincloth, worn against the skin (13:1, 4, 6, 7, 10, 11); *Perath* (פְּרָת), conventionally rendered "Euphrates" but the same consonants name a wadi (Wadi Farah / Parah) three miles northeast of Anathoth — Ibn Ezra and many moderns (Aharoni, Bright as a possibility) take this as the actual location; *davaq* (דָּבַק), "to cling" (13:11), the same verb used in Genesis 2:24 of marriage and in Deuteronomy of Israel's covenant attachment to YHWH; *qina* (קִינָה), the lament-meter underlying 13:17–27; and *shulayim* (שׁוּלַיִם), "skirts" or "hems" (13:22, 26), which in 13:26 YHWH himself lifts over the woman's face — the gesture of ritualized public shaming attested in Hosea 2:3, Nahum 3:5, and Ezekiel 16:37.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Jeremiah 13 is constructed as a triptych: a sign-act narrative in prose (13:1–11), a parable-saying in prose (13:12–14), and a long judgment poem in *qina*-meter (13:15–27). The chapter sits at a literary hinge in the book. Chapters 1–10 have established the prophet's call, the temple sermon, and the broken-covenant lawsuit; chapters 11–12 introduced the first of the so-called "confessions" with the conspiracy at Anathoth. Chapter 13 inaugurates the major **sign-act** sequence that will run through the book — the linen waistcloth here, the unbroken pottery flask in 19, the yoke in 27–28, the field at Anathoth in 32, the stones buried at Tahpanhes in 43. Friebel's *Jeremiah's and Ezekiel's Sign-Acts as Nonverbal Communication* (1999) reads these not as illustrative drama but as performative speech-acts: the sign *enacts* what it announces, and YHWH's word is operative through the prophet's body.

The waistcloth narrative is structured by a fourfold *and-the-word-of-the-LORD-came-to-me* framework: command to buy (13:1), command to bury (13:3–4), command to retrieve (13:6), and the interpretive oracle (13:8–11). The interpretive verse is built on the Hebrew verb *davaq*, "cling": "as the loincloth clings to the waist of a man, so I made the whole house of Israel and the whole house of Judah cling to me" (13:11). This is leitwort technique. *Davaq* is the marriage verb of Genesis 2:24 and the covenant verb of Deuteronomy 10:20 ("you shall fear the LORD your God; you shall serve him and *cling* to him"). Israel was meant to be YHWH's intimate cloth — not the public ephod, not the outer cloak, but the cloth pressed to the body. The horror of the spoiled waistcloth is the horror of a marriage relic gone to rot.

The geography of *Perath* is one of the great interpretive cruxes of the chapter. Holladay considers a literal Euphrates trip (some seven hundred miles round-trip) historically implausible and reads the text as either symbolic or as referring to Wadi Parah near Anathoth. Lundbom defends the literal reading, citing Ezekiel's later sign-act journeys and the rhetorical force of the Babylonian destination. Carroll (OTL) treats the whole pericope as a Deuteronomistic *literary* sign-act — never literally performed, composed retrospectively to interpret the exile. The wordplay is unmistakable in either direction: *Perath* puns on Parah (the wadi) and on Euphrates (the river of exile), and the burial of the cloth in a cleft of the rock prefigures the burial of the people in Babylon. McKane reads the wordplay as the chapter's hermeneutical hinge.

The wineskin parable (13:12–14) is constructed as a wisdom-saying turned sour: an apparently innocuous proverb ("every jar shall be filled with wine") becomes, on YHWH's lips, a horror — the jars are the people, and the wine is *shikkaron*, drunkenness sent from heaven, that they may dash one against another. The motif appears again at 25:15–29 (the cup of wrath) and in apocalyptic literature (Rev 14:10, 16:19).

The poem of 13:15–27 is in classical *qina* (3+2) limp-meter, the meter of Lamentations. It oscillates between exhortation (13:15–17) — "give glory to the LORD your God / before he brings darkness" — and judgment (13:18–27). The "Ethiopian / leopard" couplet (13:23) is one of Jeremiah's most quoted verses; in Hebrew the meter is tight and the imagery exotic. The closing imagery (13:22, 26) — "your skirts are lifted up . . . I myself will lift up your skirts over your face" — borrows the formal vocabulary of adulteress-shaming and is, by modern lights, deeply troubling. We will take it up under Lens 4. Textually, the LXX preserves a slightly shorter form of 13:22; the meaning-affecting variant is minor.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, Jeremiah 13 is the moment the book first *enacts* what it has been *saying*. The temple sermon (ch. 7) and the broken-covenant lawsuit (ch. 11) had warned of judgment in words; the waistcloth makes it material. The sign-act tradition runs forward: Hosea is commanded to marry Gomer (Hos 1, 3), Isaiah walks naked and barefoot for three years (Isa 20), Ezekiel lies on his side and bakes bread over dung (Ezek 4), and ultimately, in Christian reading, Jesus' table-acts and the cross itself are read as the eschatological sign-act in which sign and reality fully coincide.

**Jewish trajectory.** Sirach 49:6–7 already places Jeremiah among the great pre-exilic prophets ("they handed Jerusalem over to her enemies . . . for they had afflicted Jeremiah"), and Bavli Megillah 14b counts him among the eight named prophets descended from Rahab. Targum Jonathan paraphrases 13:1–11 with characteristic interpretive expansion, glossing the *ezor* as the "girdle of glory" stripped from Israel. Rashi reads *Perath* literally as the Euphrates and treats the buried cloth as a typological prefiguration of exile; Radak, characteristically, prefers the symbolic reading and notes the sign-act category. **Ibn Ezra** is the locus classicus for the Wadi Parah reading — "it is impossible that he went to the great river Euphrates; rather it is a place near Anathoth named Parah" — and most modern commentators who take this view cite him directly. Abrabanel, writing after the Spanish expulsion of 1492, reads chapter 13 with raw recognition: he is himself the queen-mother stripped of the crown, and he expounds 13:18–19 with autobiographical force. Pesikta Rabbati (medieval homiletic midrash on the festivals) embeds Jeremiah in a wider lament tradition tied to Tisha B'Av.

**Christian trajectory.** The patristic engagement is led by **Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah***, fourteen of which survive in Greek and an additional fourteen in Jerome's Latin translation; Origen's allegorical method reads the *ezor* as the soul that should cling to the Logos and is spoiled when it descends to the river of Babylon (the world). **Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah***, written at Bethlehem in the late 410s and unfinished at his death, treats chapter 13 with characteristic philological rigor — Jerome had access to Hebrew informants, and his discussion of the *ezor* and the toponym of Perath is often the first stop for medieval Latin commentators. Rupert of Deutz (12th c.) develops the marital allegory; Aquinas, in his Jeremiah lectures, treats the sign-act under his broader theology of prophetic speech (*ST* II-II.171–174). **Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah*** (delivered 1560–63 in Geneva, published posthumously 1563) approach chapter 13 with characteristic sobriety: Calvin reads the waistcloth as a parable of election and apostasy and notes that "even what is most intimate to God can become abominable when it refuses Him."

The critical period brings Duhm (1901), who in his commentary excised most of 13 as Deuteronomistic redaction; Mowinckel sorted the chapter into his Sources A (poetic), B (biographical prose), and C (Deuteronomistic prose); Skinner's *Prophecy and Religion* (1922) read the chapter as a window into Jeremiah's interior development. **Holladay** (Hermeneia, 1986) defends the unity of the chapter on rhetorical-critical grounds; **Lundbom** (Anchor, 1999) reads it as a single composition from the early reign of Jehoiakim, with the closing poem updated to fit the 597 deportation. **Carroll** (OTL, 1986) treats the sign-act as a literary fiction and the chapter as exilic theological reflection. **Brueggemann** reads chapter 13 as a sustained meditation on covenant intimacy violated. **Kathleen O'Connor**, reading the book as exilic disaster literature, takes seriously the violence of the closing imagery and treats the chapter as testimony wrung out of catastrophe rather than cool didactic allegory; **Leslie Allen** (NICOT) and **Patrick Miller** (NIB) give the steady historical-exegetical reading, and Christopher J. H. Wright (*The Message of Jeremiah*, BST) and J. A. Thompson (NICOT) provide the standard evangelical commentaries.

**Where they diverge.** Christian readings — patristic and medieval — almost universally treat the sign-act typologically: the buried cloth prefigures the buried Christ, the spoiled cloth the unredeemed soul, the marital intimacy the church-as-bride. Jewish readings treat the sign-act mimetically: it actually happened, or actually didn't, and either way it tells us something about the *peshat* (plain sense) of exile. Critical readings bracket the typology and ask after redactional layers and the social location of the editors. The trauma-theology turn (O'Connor, Stulman) reads the chapter as testimony rather than as didactic allegory.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: the metaphysics of the prophetic sign-act.** Is the linen waistcloth narrative *efficacious* (the burial enacts what it announces, and YHWH's word goes forth and accomplishes), *illustrative* (the action is a vivid sermon, but the announcement does the work), or *testimonial* (the action testifies to a reality but does not constitute it)?

The Catholic and Orthodox traditions broadly read prophetic sign-acts within a sacramental ontology — the sign participates in what it signifies. The buried *ezor* is not merely a picture of exile but a kind of forerunner-sacrament, and the typological line runs from sign-act through eucharistic sign to the *res et sacramentum* tradition (cf. Aquinas *ST* III.60–65). On this reading, when Christ takes bread, the entire prophetic-sign tradition — Hosea's marriage, Isaiah's nakedness, Jeremiah's waistcloth, Ezekiel's bricks — is what makes the gesture intelligible.

The Reformed tradition, following Calvin, distinguishes sharply between God's word (which is efficacious) and the sign that accompanies it (which is illustrative — *signum* not *res*). Calvin's lectures on Jeremiah 13 emphasize that the waistcloth does not *cause* the exile; YHWH's decree does. The sign is a help to faith, not a vehicle of grace. The Anabaptist tradition, picking up an emphasis from the Schleitheim Confession, reads the sign-act as testimony — Jeremiah is a witness, and the church's calling is also testimonial; signs do not effect, they declare.

Steelman the Reformed position fairly. Friebel's argument that sign-acts are *nonverbal communication* is, in a sense, a critical reformulation of the Reformed instinct. The signs are speech-acts, and like all speech-acts they convey information; they do not constitute reality. The Catholic temptation, on this account, is to over-realize the sign and to lose the asymmetry between divine word and human action. The Reformed read protects the freedom of God against any prophetic instrumentalism.

Where this leaves us: the chapter does not resolve the question, but its grammar leans toward the realist end. The waistcloth is *bought*, *worn*, *buried*, and *retrieved*; the verbs are full-bodied. To call this merely illustrative is to flatten what the text does. But to call the cloth itself sacramental risks reading anachronistically. Perhaps the best provisional reading is that prophetic sign-acts are *bodily testimony enacted within the field of God's already-operative word* — efficacious not in themselves but as the prophet's body becomes a place where the word is staged.

**Tension 2: 13:23 and the afterlife of the verse.** "Can the Ethiopian change his skin or the leopard his spots? Then also you can do good who are accustomed to do evil." Augustine reads this verse in *De Spiritu et Littera* (4.6) as Scripture's testimony to the post-fall inability of the human will to do good apart from grace — a foundational text for his anti-Pelagian theology. Calvin extends it in the *Institutes* (2.3.5) to a full doctrine of total depravity. The Wesleyan tradition resists: the verse describes what is *naturally* impossible, but prevenient grace is precisely what God provides to make the impossible possible (Wesley, "On Working Out Our Own Salvation," sermon 85).

The harder question is what to do with the verse's American afterlife. From the early nineteenth century, the "Ethiopian / leopard" couplet was extracted from its Hebrew context and pressed into service in slaveholder hermeneutics — used to argue that black Americans could not change their nature, and therefore could not be free. **Esau McCaulley's *Reading While Black*** (2020) confronts this history directly: the verse names not racial fixity but moral habituation; the *kushi* of the Hebrew (the Ethiopian) is descriptive, not prescriptive, and the argument is one of *a fortiori* moral analogy, not anthropology. The verse asks whether a people steeped in covenant betrayal can, by their own resources, undo what habit has set; it does not say a black-skinned person is morally fixed.

The Catholic tradition (Aquinas *ST* I-II.78 on habitual sin) reads the verse as a description of *habitus* — the way repeated acts shape the agent. The Jewish tradition (Yoma 86b on *teshuvah*) reads it against the grain — even the most habituated sinner can return, because *teshuvah* is built into creation. Both traditions converge against the slaveholder reading.

Steelman the Augustinian position: human moral inability under sin is not a comfortable doctrine, and Jeremiah's rhetoric is genuinely severe. To soften the verse is to soften the prophet. But severity is not the same as fatalism, and the verse's force is to drive Israel toward dependence on YHWH (cf. the new-covenant promise of Jer 31, where the law is written on the heart precisely *because* the heart cannot, in itself, be reformed).

Where this leaves us: 13:23 is a verse about moral habituation under covenant, and reading it well requires both the theological and the historical layer — the Augustinian gravity *and* the awareness that the verse has been weaponized. To read it well after that history is to read it slowly, with attention to what its rhetoric actually does and to whose freedom it has been used to deny.

**Unresolved question.** The closing imagery of 13:22, 26 — YHWH lifting the woman's skirts over her face — is, for many readers, the chapter's real difficulty. The rhetoric belongs to a prophetic vocabulary of female-personified judgment (Hos 2, Isa 47, Ezek 16, 23, Nah 3) that is by modern lights unmistakably violent. Holladay and McKane note that the gesture is conventional shaming language (cf. Nah 3:5), not an endorsement of actual practice; Brueggemann calls it the *raw edge* of prophetic speech; O'Connor's trauma reading hears it as the disclosure of a community's wreckage rather than a model for behavior. The unresolved question is not whether the metaphor is harsh — it plainly is — but how a community committed to Scripture as authoritative reads, names, and refuses the mimetic risk of such language without dismissing what the prophet is trying to say about covenant betrayal.

## The Thread

Jeremiah 13's sign-act stands within a chain. Hosea 1 marries the prostitute and names the children *Lo-Ruhamah* and *Lo-Ammi* — the family becomes the sign. Isaiah 20 walks naked and barefoot for three years to announce Egypt's captivity. Ezekiel 4 lies on his side for hundreds of days and bakes bread over dung. The prophet's body becomes the medium because the word alone has not been heard. The Christian sacramental tradition, reading forward, sees in the institution of the Eucharist the moment the chain reaches its center: bread and wine taken, broken, given. The Reformed tradition, reading backward, sees in Jeremiah 13 a warning against treating any sign as automatically efficacious. Either way, the *ezor* clinging to the body remains the chapter's haunting figure: what God once chose for intimacy can be unmade by what is done with it.
