# Hebrews 10 — A Single Offering, A Living Way, A Fearful Warning

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Hebrews 10 is the climax of the cultic argument that began at chapter 7 and the launching point for the great parade of faith in chapter 11. It comes from the same anonymous author Origen confessed only "God knows" (Eusebius, *HE* 6.25.14) — a Hellenistic-Jewish Christian of high rhetorical training, fluent in Septuagint citation and *synkrisis*, addressing what most modern scholars (William L. Lane WBC, Barnabas Lindars, Harold Attridge Hermeneia, Craig Koester Anchor Yale) take to be a Roman Jewish-Christian community whose former boldness had been worn down by social marginalization, property loss, imprisonment of fellow believers (10:32-34), and now perhaps the lure of returning to Jewish-synagogue worship under the cover of *religio licita*. The genre marker is again *λόγος παρακλήσεως* — synagogue-style word of exhortation (13:22).

Dating is sharp here too. The present-tense verbs of 10:1-3 — sacrifices that "*are continually offered* every year," the reminder of sins offered "*every year*" (κατ᾽ ἐνιαυτόν) — and 10:11's "every priest *stands daily* (ἕστηκεν καθ᾽ ἡμέραν) at his service, offering repeatedly the same sacrifices" — read with peculiar force if Herod's temple is still functioning as the author writes. Lane and Cockerill (NICNT) read this as decisive evidence of pre-70 CE composition. Attridge and Koester reply that the author argues from Levitical *scriptural* sacrifice rather than contemporary temple practice; that the Diaspora Jewish-Christian audience would not have witnessed the Jerusalem cult anyway; that the standing-priest contrast (10:11-12) recalls the tabernacle of Exodus more than Herod's temple. What is striking is that 10:32-34 references concrete sufferings the audience has already endured — public reproach, imprisonment, plundered property — fitting either the Claudian expulsion of 49 CE (when "Chrestus"-related disturbances pushed Jewish-Christians out of Rome, per Suetonius *Claudius* 25.4) or the Neronian persecutions of 64-65. Either fits the chapter's encouragement: hold on, the worst is not yet over, but you have been here before.

The chapter's two structural pillars are scriptural citations. The first, at 10:5-7, is **Psalm 40:6-8** (LXX 39:7-9). The second, at 10:37-38, is **Habakkuk 2:3-4** in a longer-LXX form. Both citations carry distinctive textual histories that the author exploits.

The Psalm 40 citation contains one of the most consequential LXX-vs-MT divergences in the New Testament. The Masoretic Hebrew of Psalm 40:6 reads *'oznayim karita li* — literally "ears you have dug for me" or "ears you have hollowed out for me," an idiom probably meaning "ears you have prepared/opened for me to obey." The Septuagint translates *σῶμα δὲ κατηρτίσω μοι* — "*a body* you have prepared for me." Hebrews follows the LXX exactly, and the entire Christological argument of 10:5-10 — that Christ entered the world saying "a body you have prepared for me," and offered that body once for all — depends on the LXX rendering, not the Hebrew. The 4QPsa fragment from Qumran does not preserve this verse, so we cannot trace the textual history with certainty. Several explanations have been proposed: an early LXX scribal change (perhaps reading *gewiyyah* "body" or a similar word for *'oznayim* "ears"); a metaphorical paraphrase ("ear-digging" understood as "preparing for embodied obedience"); or an intentional Christianizing alteration. The earliest LXX Greek manuscripts (Vaticanus, Sinaiticus) preserve "body," so this reading predates Hebrews. Whatever its origin, the author of Hebrews builds his argument on it without apology — and the question of which textual form is *Scripture* opens out into the canonical and inspirational debates Lens 4 will treat.

The Habakkuk 2:3-4 citation at 10:37-38 also follows a longer LXX reading. The MT reads "behold, his soul is puffed up; it is not upright in him; but the righteous shall live by his faithfulness (*be'emunato*)." The LXX (and 1QpHab from Qumran) preserves a longer reading: "if he shrinks back (*ὑποστείληται*), my soul has no pleasure in him; but my righteous one shall live by faith." Romans 1:17 and Galatians 3:11 quote the shorter formula ("the righteous shall live by faith"), but Hebrews 10:38 quotes the longer LXX form because the "shrink back" clause fits the warning context exactly. Lane WBC and Cockerill NICNT both note this: Heb 10's use of Hab 2:4 is theologically distinct from Paul's, working not toward justification but toward perseverance.

The Greek manuscript tradition for Hebrews 10 is robust: P46 (c. 200 CE), Sinaiticus א, Vaticanus B, Alexandrinus A, Ephraemi C all preserve the chapter without significant variation in the load-bearing terms. The most discussed textual variant is at 10:34, where some witnesses read "you had compassion on *me in my* bonds" and others "you had compassion on *those in prison*" — a difference that affects whether the author identifies with the imprisoned or stands at one remove. ESV reads "those in prison."

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter unfolds in four distinct movements. Verses 1-18 conclude the cultic argument: the law is *σκιά* (shadow, 10:1) of the *εἰκών* (image — note the upgraded contrast vs. 8:5's *typos*); repeated sacrifices cannot perfect; Christ's single offering by his *body* prepared by the Father has perfected forever those being sanctified; "where there is forgiveness, there is no longer any offering for sin." Verses 19-25 transition into a hortatory crescendo: three "let us" subjunctives (*προσερχώμεθα* "let us draw near," *κατέχωμεν* "let us hold fast," *κατανοῶμεν* "let us consider") all anchored to the great Christological-liturgical claim — we have confidence to enter the holy places "by a new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain, that is, through his flesh." Verses 26-31 pivot into the second great warning passage of the epistle. Verses 32-39 close with endurance encouragement and the Habakkuk citation.

The Greek registers are dense. *Σκιά* (shadow) at 10:1 picks up the chapter 8 vocabulary, but here pairs with *εἰκών* (image) — a more technical Platonic-Philonic term denoting the participatory likeness, not merely the silhouette. *Σῶμα* (body) at 10:5, 10 names what Christ offered. *Ἐφάπαξ* and *μία προσφορά* ("a single offering") at 10:10, 12, 14 drum the once-for-all theme. *Συνείδησις* (conscience) recurs at 10:22 — the same word used at 9:9, 14, here resolved by sprinkling. *Καταπέτασμα* (curtain) at 10:20 returns from chapter 9 with a stunning reinterpretation: the curtain *is* Christ's flesh. *Παρρησία* (boldness, confidence) at 10:19, 35 names the disposition the new way produces. *Παροξυσμός* at 10:24 — "stirring up" — is an arresting word; the same root yields the *paroxysmos* of Acts 15:39 (the Paul-Barnabas dispute) and gives English "paroxysm." The author chooses an intense word for mutual encouragement: not gentle nudging but vigorous mutual provocation toward love and good works. *Ἑκουσίως* at 10:26 — "willfully, deliberately, with full knowledge" — is the warning verse's most freighted word. *Φοβερόν* at 10:27, 31 — "fearful" — bookends the warning passage.

The **10:19-20 reinterpretation of the curtain** is one of the most striking moves in the New Testament. Heb 9:3 and 9:8 had named the *katapetasma* as the inner curtain shielding the Most Holy Place — the boundary the high priest crossed only on Yom Kippur. 10:20 now identifies that curtain with *Christ's flesh*: "by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain, *that is, through his flesh*" (διὰ τοῦ καταπετάσματος, τοῦτ᾽ ἔστιν τῆς σαρκὸς αὐτοῦ). The grammar is debated: does the appositive "his flesh" modify "the curtain" (Christ's flesh *is* the curtain through which we enter) or "the way" (the new way is *through* his flesh *and through* the curtain)? Most modern commentators (Lane, Attridge, Cockerill, O'Brien) take the former: Christ's flesh, torn on the cross, *is* the curtain whose tearing opens access. The synoptic gospels' temple-curtain rending at Christ's death (Mark 15:38; Matt 27:51; Luke 23:45) sits underneath this reading.

The **10:5-10 Christological-incarnational argument** is also remarkable. The author has Christ *speaking the LXX of Psalm 40 as he enters the world* — the entry being either his incarnation or his self-presentation to do the Father's will. "Sacrifices and offerings you did not desire, but a body you have prepared for me. ... Then I said, 'Behold, I have come to do your will, O God.'" The argument compresses three claims: (1) the cultic sacrifices the law required were never God's deepest will (Ps 40 says so); (2) the Father has prepared a body for the Son's offering; (3) Christ enters the world precisely to offer that prepared body in obedience. The "by that will we have been sanctified" of 10:10 anchors sanctification not in repeated cultic acts but in the singular obedient offering of the Son's body.

The **10:14 paradox** is the chapter's most discussed soteriological line: "for by a single offering he has perfected for all time (*τετελείωκεν εἰς τὸ διηνεκές*) those who are being sanctified (*τοὺς ἁγιαζομένους*)." Perfect tense ("has perfected") plus present passive participle ("are being sanctified") — completed perfection plus ongoing sanctification — has scaffolded major theological structures (Reformed *definitive sanctification*, Wesleyan *progressive sanctification*, Catholic *infused justification with growth in grace*, Eastern Orthodox *theosis*).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Two reception-historical streams converge on Hebrews 10, and both are still alive today.

**The Septuagint question.** That Heb 10:5-7 builds its Christology on the LXX rendering of Psalm 40 ("a body you have prepared for me") rather than the MT ("ears you have dug for me") puts the chapter at the center of a long debate about the canonical status of the Greek Old Testament.

The early church received the Greek OT as Scripture. The Letter of Aristeas (2nd c. BCE) had narrated the Septuagint's miraculous translation by seventy-two scholars. Justin Martyr's *Dialogue with Trypho* 71-73 accused the Jews of "removing" passages favorable to Christianity and treated the LXX as the authentic text. Augustine in *De Doctrina Christiana* 2.15.22 argued for LXX inspiration: "the same Spirit that was in the prophets when they spoke these things was also in the Seventy when they translated them." The Eastern Orthodox Church canonically uses the LXX as its Old Testament to this day.

The Reformation pivoted decisively to *Hebraica veritas* — "Hebrew truth." Jerome had begun this turn with the Vulgate (replacing the Old Latin's LXX-base with translations from Hebrew for most OT books). Reuchlin's Hebrew grammar and the Christian-Hebraist Renaissance equipped Reformers to challenge LXX renderings. Luther and Calvin both preferred MT readings where MT and LXX diverged. The result: most Protestant Bibles translate the Old Testament from the Masoretic Text, footnoting LXX divergences only as alternatives.

This creates a peculiar tension at Heb 10:5. Modern Protestant Bibles translate Ps 40:6 from the Hebrew ("my ears you have opened" or similar), while at Heb 10:5 they translate the New Testament text-form, which preserves the LXX ("a body you have prepared for me"). Within the same Bible, the prophet's Psalm reads differently depending on whether you encounter it in the OT or in the NT citation. Brevard Childs's canonical-criticism (*The Theology of the Old and New Testaments*, 1992) treated this not as a problem to be solved but as a feature of the Christian canon — both forms are received Scripture. N.T. Wright has made similar points: the LXX is the Bible *the apostles read*, and apostolic citation patterns presuppose LXX inspiration in some functional sense.

The honest exegetical conclusion is this: the author of Hebrews does not regard himself as exploiting a translation error. He regards the LXX rendering as Spirit-given and Christ-revealing. Whether modern Christians can affirm that view depends on how strong a doctrine of providential textual transmission they hold.

**The second great warning passage.** Heb 10:26-31 sits in canonical-historical conversation with Heb 6:4-8, and together these two passages — *the* warning passages of Hebrews — have generated the most consequential debates over post-baptismal sin in church history.

The **Donatist** controversy in 4th-century North Africa hung on these warnings. The Donatists, led by Donatus of Casae Nigrae, refused communion with bishops who had handed over Scriptures during Diocletian's persecution (the *traditores*). Their argument quoted Heb 10:26-29 directly: those who had sinned willfully (*hekousiōs*) after receiving the knowledge of the truth had no further sacrifice; the church that received them back was profaning the blood of the covenant. **Cyprian of Carthage** had earlier (*De Lapsis*, 251 CE) taken a moderately rigorist line on the *lapsi* — those who had sacrificed to pagan gods during Decian persecution — but had eventually allowed restoration after extended penance. The **Novatian** schism in mid-3rd century Rome had taken a harder line, insisting that the lapsed could not be restored at all; Novatian and his followers read 10:26 as absolute. **Augustine** answered the Donatists in *Contra Donatistas* and the *Enchiridion*: the church on earth is a *corpus permixtum*, holy in its sacraments not its members; Heb 10:26-31 warns against deliberate apostasy, not against repentable sin.

The Reformation reopened these debates. **Calvin** in *Institutes* 3.3.21-25 read Heb 10:26 as referring to the sin of *final, irrevocable apostasy* — not all post-baptismal sin, but the sin of "rejecting the Gospel itself" with full deliberation. The elect, he argued, cannot fall this way, so the warning functions instrumentally to preserve perseverance. The **Westminster Confession 17** systematized this: those whom God has effectually called and sanctified can never totally fall from a state of grace. **Arminian and Wesleyan** traditions read the same passage differently: real apostasy is possible for genuine believers; Heb 10:26-29 warns concrete Christians against concrete apostasy. The **Anabaptist** *Schleitheim Confession* 2 (1527) read 10:25-29 to mandate church discipline by the *Bann* (ban): those who fall into willful sin and refuse correction are to be excluded from the community. The **Lutheran** Augsburg Confession 12 affirmed the possibility of restoration through repentance for post-baptismal sin against the rigorist tradition.

Contemporary debates continue. **Russell Moore** has written extensively on Southern Baptist church-discipline failures in the wake of clergy-abuse revelations. **Beth Allison Barr** in *The Making of Biblical Womanhood* (2021) has documented how the SBC's discipline mechanisms collapsed precisely where they were needed. The **Catholic Church's** post-2002 reform efforts — the Dallas Charter, the Vatican's *Vos Estis Lux Mundi* (2019, revised 2023) — represent a long, contested attempt to take Heb 10:26's *hekousiōs* seriously without falling into Novatian rigorism. The Protestant world's reckoning with high-profile abuse cases (Mark Driscoll, Ravi Zacharias, the Hillsong scandal, the SBC sex-abuse investigations) has reopened the same questions: when does institutional protection of abusers become the deliberate sin Heb 10:26 names?

Steelmanning the rigorist Donatist-Novatian reading honestly: their exegesis is not absurd. Heb 10:26 says what it says — *if we go on sinning deliberately after receiving the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins*. The author chose *hekousiōs*, deliberate, knowing willfulness. The author chose *no longer remains* — not "and yet there remains another way." The Augustinian moderation is exegetically defensible (the warning is hyperbolic; *hekousiōs* names final apostasy, not stumbling), but it is not the only available reading, and the Donatist objection — that a church which absorbs and protects deliberate apostates is itself profaning the blood of the covenant — keeps returning to haunt the church whenever institutional self-protection eclipses repentance.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

The author intends to land the cultic argument decisively (10:1-18), to convert the doctrinal accomplishment into actionable encouragement (10:19-25), to warn against the catastrophe of deliberate apostasy (10:26-31), and to call the audience to endurance with the hope that the coming one will not delay (10:32-39). Two tensions remain.

**Tension 1: The LXX-vs-MT divergence at Psalm 40 and what inspiration of textual transmission looks like.** Heb 10:5's "a body you have prepared for me" depends on the LXX. If the MT reads "ears you have dug for me," is the body-Christology the apostle builds *in* Scripture or *out of* a translation? The Catholic-Orthodox answer has been that the LXX is itself canonical Scripture for the church, that the providential history of the text includes its translation, and that the Spirit's inspiration extends to the form of the text the apostles received. The Reformation answer (Hebraica veritas) was that the original languages are normative and that LXX divergences are translation choices, sometimes felicitous and sometimes not. The contemporary canonical-critical answer (Childs, Wright) is that both text-forms are received in the Christian canon and that we should read the NT citation as Scripture even when the source-text differs from the modern critical reconstruction. None of these answers eliminates the question; what does the author of Hebrews think he is doing? The honest reading is: he thinks he is reading Scripture, and the form of Scripture he reads is the LXX. To say that is not to settle whether MT or LXX is more "original"; it is to notice that Christological argument in the NT *presupposes* LXX-form Scripture.

**Tension 2: The second warning and the church's response to public failure.** *Hekousiōs* in 10:26 means deliberate, knowing, willful — and the warning is concrete: "no sacrifice for sins remains, but a fearful expectation of judgment." Across two thousand years the church has answered the question "what counts as *hekousiōs*?" in three ways. The Donatist-Novatian rigorists answered: any deliberate post-baptismal sin against the gospel constitutes apostasy. The Augustinian moderation answered: only final, unrepentant rejection of the gospel constitutes the *hekousiōs* sin Hebrews names; ordinary post-baptismal sin remains repentable. The Calvinist-Reformed answer: the warning functions for perseverance; the elect cannot fall this way, so the warning is real but cannot effect what it warns against. The contemporary church now faces the question with fresh urgency: what does it mean for institutions, not only individuals, to sin *hekousiōs*? When a denomination knowingly protects abusers, when a hierarchy cultivates a culture of cover-up, when leaders preach grace while practicing predation — has the institution profaned "the blood of the covenant by which it was sanctified"? Heb 10:29's three verbs — *trampled* (καταπατήσας), *profaned* (κοινὸν ἡγησάμενος), *outraged* (ἐνυβρίσας) — name a posture, not just an act. Russell Moore, Beth Allison Barr, Catholic post-2002 reformers, and survivors of every tradition are pressing the chapter's question: at what point does institutional self-protection become the deliberate sin Hebrews names?

**Closing question:** Heb 10:31 says "*it is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God*." Is this fear the fear of a covenant relationship gone wrong (the Augustinian-Reformed reading: God's judgment falls on covenant-breakers as a function of his faithfulness) or the fear of an absolute boundary that can be crossed once and never recrossed (the Donatist-rigorist reading)? The chapter does not tell us; it calls us instead to *not throw away* the *parrhēsia* we have, to *endure*, and to remember that the coming one will not delay.

## The Thread

Hebrews 10 has finished the cultic argument, opened the new and living way through Christ's flesh, sounded the second great warning, and called the audience back to the endurance they once practiced. The thread from chapters 8-10 is one continuous argument: a better covenant (8), a better sanctuary entered by a better sacrifice (9), and a single offering that has perfected forever those being sanctified (10:14) — all converging on the call to draw near, hold fast, stir up, and not shrink back. Chapter 11 will turn from priestly typology to the long parade of Israel's faithful, whose endurance proved the same God's promises across the same long ages, and chapter 12 will plant the audience inside that cloud of witnesses with the cross as their forward horizon.
