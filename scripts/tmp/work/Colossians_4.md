# Colossians 4: The Network of Names and the Letter That Was Lost

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter that closes Paul's letter to the Colossians is, on its surface, the most ordinary section of the epistle: a final exhortation about prayer and speech, a string of personal greetings, a one-line instruction about a parallel letter, and an autograph. Yet historically, this is the densest single passage in the New Testament for reconstructing the social shape of the early Pauline mission, and the technical questions that hover over almost every line — provenance, authorship, identity of named persons, status of a vanished document — have generated more scholarly literature per verse than the great Christological hymn of chapter 1.

The setting is Pauline imprisonment. The chapter speaks of "my chains" (*desma mou*, δεσμά μου, 4:18) and names Aristarchus as "my fellow prisoner" (*synaichmalōtos mou*, συναιχμάλωτος μου, 4:10). Three locations have been proposed. The traditional view, defended by Eusebius and reaffirmed by J. B. Lightfoot's 1875 commentary, is Roman provenance — Paul writing during the Acts 28 house arrest, around AD 60-62. Lohmeyer and Bo Reicke argued for Caesarean captivity on the grounds that the Lycus Valley earthquake of AD 60/61, attested by Tacitus (*Annals* 14.27) and discussed in modern Colossae work by Alan Cadwallader, would have made a destination address in Colossae implausible after AD 61. The modern majority view for Philippians and Philemon follows G. S. Duncan and proposes a hypothetical Ephesian imprisonment, which places Paul within roughly 120 miles of Colossae and explains the rapid messenger traffic the chapter implies.

What sets Colossians 4 apart is its named individuals. Eleven persons appear in twelve verses, and almost every name is contested or interlocked with another New Testament document.

- **Tychicus** (4:7) — a Pauline messenger, mentioned also at Eph 6:21, 2 Tim 4:12, and Titus 3:12. He is the carrier of this letter.
- **Onesimus** (4:9) — "the faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you." This is the runaway slave of Philemon, returning to Colossae alongside Tychicus, carrying both Colossians and the letter to Philemon. The interlock with Philemon — same bearer, same destination, same time — is the strongest single argument for Colossians' authenticity, since a pseudepigrapher would have to coordinate the fiction with the smaller and better-attested Philemon letter.
- **Aristarchus** (4:10) — a Macedonian companion of Paul; Acts 19:29, 20:4, 27:2.
- **Mark, the cousin of Barnabas** (4:10) — John Mark of Acts 12:12, 12:25, 13:13, and the figure over whom Paul and Barnabas split in Acts 15:37-39. Now reconciled and present with Paul. Also at 2 Tim 4:11, Phlm 24, and 1 Pet 5:13. Crucially, this Mark is the figure traditionally identified by Papias (in the fragment preserved by Eusebius, *Hist. Eccl.* 3.39.15) as the author of the Gospel of Mark, working from Peter's preaching.
- **Jesus called Justus** (4:11) — appears nowhere else. He, Aristarchus, and Mark are described as "the only men of the circumcision among my fellow workers for the kingdom of God."
- **Epaphras** (4:12-13) — likely the founder of the Lycus Valley churches at Colossae, Laodicea, and Hierapolis (cf. 1:7-8, Phlm 23).
- **Luke, the beloved physician** (4:14) — the strongest New Testament identification of Luke the evangelist. The physician designation is not corroborated elsewhere. Also 2 Tim 4:11, Phlm 24.
- **Demas** (4:14) — present here without comment; in 2 Tim 4:10 he has defected: "Demas, in love with this present world, has deserted me."
- **Nympha** (4:15) — host of a house-church. The textual variant on the gender of the possessive pronoun (*autēs* vs. *autou*) is meaning-affecting and will be treated below.
- **Archippus** (4:17) — also Phlm 2; recipient of the enigmatic charge to "fulfill the ministry that you have received in the Lord." Origen, Theodore of Mopsuestia, and Theodoret all speculate on what this ministry was, with no consensus.

Three Greek terms anchor the chapter beyond the personal names. *Halati ērtymenos* (ἅλατι ἠρτυμένος, 4:6), "seasoned with salt," draws on Greco-Roman rhetorical convention in which salt signified wit and gracious sharpness in conversation — Plutarch uses the metaphor in *Moralia*. *Epistolē tēs ek Laodikeias* (4:16), "the letter from Laodicea," names a document the church no longer possesses. *En kyriō* (4:7, 17), "in the Lord," is the standard Pauline ecclesial identity marker, but stacked here against named individuals it functions as a recognition formula for who counts as inside the network.

The named greeting list is the strongest single argument for the letter's authenticity. As Lightfoot observed, would a pseudepigrapher invent so specific a network of mostly minor figures? Margaret MacDonald and Andrew Lincoln respond that verisimilitude is itself a pseudepigraphical technique, on display in the Pastorals. The interlock with Philemon — undisputed as authentic — gives the names of Col 4 a stronger pedigree than any other contested Pauline closing.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Generically, Colossians 4 is an epistolary closing, conforming to a Pauline pattern: commendation of the bearer, greetings from those with the apostle, greetings to specific persons in the receiving community, autograph signature, benediction. The closest parallel within the Pauline corpus is Romans 16, with two important differences. Romans 16 sends greetings *to* a long list of named persons in Rome, weighted heavily toward women and freedpersons; Colossians 4 sends greetings *from* a list of persons with Paul to a community where only three persons are individually named. The asymmetry is informative: at the receiving end of Colossians 4, only Nympha, Archippus, and the unnamed "brothers at Laodicea" are addressed; the rest are senders.

The chapter's most striking literary feature is the cross-letter instruction at 4:16: "When this letter has been read among you, have it also read in the church of the Laodiceans; and see that you also read the letter from Laodicea." This is one of the earliest pieces of evidence for Pauline letter circulation between congregations and is regularly cited by Harry Gamble (*Books and Readers in the Early Church*) and David Trobisch (*Paul's Letter Collection*) as a moment when the formation of a Pauline corpus is anticipated within the corpus itself.

The autograph signature at 4:18 — "I, Paul, write this greeting with my own hand. Remember my chains. Grace be with you." — follows a Pauline convention seen at Gal 6:11, 1 Cor 16:21, and 2 Thess 3:17. Papyrological parallels show that dictated letters in the Greco-Roman world frequently changed hands at the signature, with the principal writing the closing in his own script as authentication. Galen describes the practice. The "remember my chains" appended to the autograph is unique among Pauline closings — neither pure formula nor pure pathos, but a self-identifying tag attached to the signature itself.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Colossians 4 is the chapter where this letter intersects most densely with the rest of the New Testament — and with the post-canonical history of the early church. The cosmic Christology of chapter 1, the polemic against the "philosophy" of chapter 2, the eschatological identity of chapter 3, all eventuate in named bodies traveling along Roman roads.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

Patristic interest concentrated on two figures and one missing document. Mark's identification as the Gospel writer was treated as settled by Papias, Eusebius, Jerome (*De Viris Illustribus*), and Augustine. The reconciliation with Paul presupposed in 4:10 — "if he comes to you, welcome him" — became, in patristic and medieval reading, a model for the restoration of estranged ministers, with 2 Tim 4:11 ("Get Mark and bring him with you, for he is very useful to me for ministry") supplying the closing arc.

The "letter from Laodicea" of 4:16 generated three streams of interpretation. Marcion in the second century claimed Ephesians was the lost Laodicean letter; Tertullian rebutted him in *Against Marcion* 5.11 and 5.17, though the connection has resurfaced in modern scholarship that treats Ephesians as a circular letter possibly copied to Laodicea. Second, a pseudonymous Latin "Epistle to the Laodiceans," likely fourth-century and largely a pastiche of Pauline phrases, circulated in some manuscripts; the Muratorian Canon (late second century) explicitly rejects it as a forgery, and Calvin in his commentary on Colossians dispatches the legend with characteristic bluntness. Third, and most likely, the Laodicean letter was a genuine Pauline composition lost to history, joining the "previous letter" referenced at 1 Cor 5:9 as among the missing items in the apostolic correspondence.

Medieval reception expanded the personal greetings into legendary apostolic biographies. The "Acts of Paul" tradition picked up Onesimus and made him a bishop; Ignatius of Antioch in his *Letter to the Ephesians* 1.3 refers to a contemporary Bishop Onesimus, possibly though not certainly the same person, as John Knox argued in *Philemon Among the Letters of Paul*. The Reformation reworked the chapter for two opposed purposes: Catholic commentators stressed the chain of named coworkers as evidence for apostolic succession, while Calvin emphasized Paul's affection and practical pastoral concern in the greetings, treating them as a model for ministerial fellowship.

Critical scholarship since the nineteenth century has read the Pauline closings as a window on the formation of the canon. Harry Gamble's work on the Pauline letter collection and David Trobisch's argument that the corpus was edited and published as a unit both lean heavily on instructions like 4:16. Bruce Metzger's textual-critical work and Bart Ehrman's *Misquoting Jesus* and *Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* both treat the Nympha/Nymphas variant (discussed below) as a paradigm case of theologically-motivated scribal alteration.

N.T. Wright, in his Tyndale commentary *Colossians and Philemon* (1986) and again in *Paul for Everyone: The Prison Letters* (2002), treats the chapter as proof that for Paul the gospel is never an abstraction: the cosmic Lord of 1:15-20 reaches the Lycus Valley because Tychicus carries a scroll there, because Onesimus walks back to face his master, because Epaphras "wrestles in prayer" (4:12) for three towns at once, because Nympha opens her house and a congregation meets in it. Wright reads 4:5-6 — "walk in wisdom toward outsiders... let your speech always be gracious, seasoned with salt" — as the practical edge of the whole letter: the new humanity of chapter 3 is to be visible in how Christians talk to the people who are not yet inside, neither bland nor abrasive but winsome and pointed. And he takes the network of named co-workers (Tychicus, Onesimus, Aristarchus, Mark, Justus, Epaphras, Luke, Demas, Nympha, Archippus) plus the letter-exchange with Laodicea (4:16) as a snapshot of the concrete shape of the gospel's spread — a movement carried by ordinary people travelling Roman roads, copying and circulating letters, charging one another to "fulfil the ministry you received in the Lord" (4:17).

Contemporary social-historical reading has perhaps done the most with Col 4. Wayne Meeks's *The First Urban Christians* (1983) used passages of this kind to reconstruct the demographic and economic profile of early Pauline communities — predominantly urban, mixed-status, networked across the empire by traveling messengers. Edwin Judge's earlier work on the social pattern of the New Testament era and Bruce Malina's cultural-anthropological readings sit alongside Meeks. Roger Gehring's *House Church and Mission* and David Balch's work on early Christian household structure use the Nympha reference (4:15) as evidence for women's hosting of house churches. Carolyn Osiek and Margaret MacDonald in *A Woman's Place: House Churches in Earliest Christianity* (2006) treat the Nympha textual variant on social-historical grounds as a case for the prominence of women patrons in the early movement.

### Jewish Scholarly Engagement

The note at 4:11 that Aristarchus, Mark, and Jesus Justus were "the only men of the circumcision among my fellow workers for the kingdom of God" is the chapter's most direct datum on the Jewish-Gentile composition of Paul's mission at this moment. The Paul-within-Judaism school — Mark Nanos, Paula Fredriksen, Magnus Zetterholm — finds the verse striking but contested. It implies a Pauline mission whose center of gravity by this period was overwhelmingly Gentile, with Jewish coworkers a self-conscious minority. Whether this maps cleanly onto a "Jewish Paul" who never abandoned Torah is debated; Fredriksen reads it as compatible, Nanos with more difficulty.

The instruction at 4:16 about exchanging letters between congregations parallels Jewish-diaspora and rabbinic practices of inter-community correspondence. The *iggeret* tradition — letters circulating among diaspora communities, attested in the Elephantine archive and later rabbinic materials — is the relevant comparandum. Catherine Hezser's work on Jewish literacy and Steven Fraade on rabbinic textual culture both note the parallel.

The Onesimus question (4:9) — described by Paul as "one of you," meaning a Colossian — does not specify ethnicity. Jewish slave-status under Roman law would have been unusual but not unheard-of; most reconstructions treat Onesimus as a Gentile household slave, with implications for how the Philemon situation is read. Contemporary Jewish-Christian dialogue tends to engage Col 4 less for theological content than for what it reveals about the social shape of the early movement: a mixed community in transition, neither idealized as fully integrated nor reduced to confrontation.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The theological vision of Colossians has, by this final chapter, descended from the cosmic Christ of 1:15-20 into the bodies of named persons walking the empire's roads. This is not anticlimax but completion. The mission of God moves through ordinary networks: Tychicus on the road, Onesimus walking back to his master, Mark reconciled, Nympha hosting a house-church, Archippus charged to finish his ministry. The Christology of chapter 1 is real because Tychicus carries the letter that says so.

### Tension 1: The Lost Letter (4:16) — what does it mean that scripture testifies to scripture we do not have?

- **Inerrantist / providentialist** (Warfield-tradition; Wayne Grudem): God preserved what was needed. The loss of the Laodicean letter is not a problem because the canon as it stands is sufficient. The corollary is that the canon is providentially exact — not in the sense that nothing else was inspired, but in the sense that what was needed for the church was kept.
- **Catholic** (*Dei Verbum* 9; CCC §§80-83): tradition complements scripture. A written letter can be lost without the substance of revelation being lost, because the apostolic deposit lives in the tradition received from those who knew the apostles.
- **Critical / historical** (Bruce Metzger, Harry Gamble, David Trobisch): the canonical scriptures are the surviving documents of a much larger early-Christian textual production. Survival, transmission, and institutional decision shape what the church now reads. Any theology of "the Bible" must reckon with the contingency of what survived.
- **The pseudonymous Laodiceans**: a useful test case in the other direction. The church did not simply receive whatever bore Paul's name; the Muratorian Canon explicitly rejects the forgery. Canon-formation was discerning, not accidental.
- **Steelman**: if you came in providentialist, take Trobisch's case for contingency seriously. If you came in critical, take seriously the providentialist observation that the canon's coherence and pastoral usefulness suggests guiding intelligence at work in the discernment process. Brevard Childs's middle position — the canon is what it is, and theology operates from inside it — accepts both pressures without resolving them.

Where this leaves us: 4:16 is the verse that breaks any simple "the Bible is all of revelation" story. It forces a richer doctrine of scripture, tradition, and the church's discerning role.

### Tension 2: Naming and the Church's Politics — who is remembered and who is erased? (4:7-17)

The chapter names eleven people. One is a woman — Nympha — and her name's gender was actively contested in the manuscript tradition. Codex Vaticanus (B) and most modern critical editions read *autēs* (her), making the house-church host female. The Byzantine majority and many medieval manuscripts read *autou* (his), making the host a man named Nymphas. A few witnesses read *autōn* (their). Bart Ehrman has treated this variant as a paradigm case of the orthodox-corruption pattern: scribes uncomfortable with female leadership shifted the pronoun. The slaves of the household codes of 3:22, by contrast, are unnamed. Onesimus is named only because of the parallel letter to Philemon.

- **The egalitarian reading** (Ben Witherington, Lucy Peppiatt; with the social-historical reconstruction of Osiek and MacDonald): Nympha's textual fate, on this reading, is of a piece with the other named women of the Pauline mission — Junia (Rom 16:7), Phoebe (Rom 16:1-2), Priscilla (Rom 16:3-5; Acts 18) — and hosting a congregation in one's house carried real leadership weight in the early movement, so the scribal shift to "Nymphas" matters.
- **The textual-critical observation** (Bruce Metzger, Bart Ehrman, *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture*): independent of any theological program, the Nympha/Nymphas variant is a likely case of scribes harmonizing the text toward later expectations; the named/unnamed asymmetry within the letter — named coworkers in 4:7-17, unnamed slaves in 3:22 — is a datum the literary occasion only partly explains.
- **The complementarian response** (Wayne Grudem, Tom Schreiner, Andreas Köstenberger): the textual evidence for Nympha versus Nymphas is itself contested, and even granting Nympha, hosting a house-church does not by itself prove leadership in the modern teaching-elder sense. The named/unnamed distinction is a function of literary occasion — who happens to be relevant to this letter — not of theological status before God.
- **Catholic**: the communion of saints (CCC §§946-962). Every name preserved in scripture is a member of the church remembered before God; the lost names are equally remembered by God, even if not by the canon. The doctrine relativizes the named/unnamed distinction without erasing it.

Where this leaves us: read carefully, the chapter both registers the early movement's social breadth — women hosts, freed and enslaved persons, Jews and Gentiles working together — and the limits of the record, including the namelessness of the enslaved in the household codes and the unstable transmission of Nympha's name.

**Closing question**: If you were one of the names in this list, which would you be, and which would you want to be — and what is the gap between those two?

## The Thread

- **Philemon** — the companion letter, traveling with Onesimus on the same journey. Read them together; the interlock is the strongest internal evidence for both letters' authenticity.
- **Romans 16** — the parallel name-list, weighted toward women and addressed to a community Paul has not yet visited. Comparison with Col 4 illuminates the social shape of the Pauline mission.
- **2 Timothy 4:9-21** — the late-Paul name-list. Demas has now defected; Mark is loved and called for; Luke remains.
- **1 Peter 5:13** — Mark "my son" with Peter in "Babylon." The Papias tradition links the three Markan attestations (Col 4, 2 Tim 4, 1 Pet 5) to the Gospel of Mark.
