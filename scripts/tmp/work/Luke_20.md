# Luke 20: Authority Contested in the Temple Courts

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Luke 20 unfolds entirely within the Herodian Temple complex, the largest sacred precinct in the ancient Mediterranean world. Herod the Great's expansion, begun around 20 BCE and not finally completed until the 60s CE (only a few years before its destruction in 70 CE), produced a platform of roughly 144,000 square meters — large enough to contain twenty-four football fields. Josephus describes its scale with the awe of a man who knew it was already lost (*War* 5.184–227; *Antiquities* 15.380–425). The teaching depicted in Luke 20 would have taken place in the Court of the Gentiles, on the Royal Stoa or beneath the colonnaded porticoes of Solomon's Portico. Modern excavations along the southern wall (the Robinson's Arch and Hulda Gate excavations directed by Benjamin Mazar in the late 1960s, continued by Ronny Reich and Eli Shukron) have exposed the monumental staircases pilgrims would have ascended to reach the platform where Jesus is teaching.

The interlocutors in Luke 20 belong to three distinct social formations. The "chief priests, scribes, and elders" (20:1) constituted the standing Sanhedrin: a body composed of the priestly aristocracy (largely Sadducean), Pharisaic and other lay scholars, and lay aristocrats. The high priest in this period was **Joseph Caiaphas**, who served from approximately 18 to 36 CE under the Roman prefect Pontius Pilate. The 1990 discovery of the **Caiaphas ossuary** in Peace Forest, south of the Old City of Jerusalem — an ornately carved limestone bone-box bearing the inscription "Yehosef bar Qayafa" ("Joseph son of Caiaphas") and containing the bones of a man of approximately 60 years — has been widely accepted (Zvi Greenhut's original report, with cautious endorsement by Ronny Reich and Émile Puech) as belonging to the high priest of the Gospels, though dissenters such as William Horbury have pressed for caution. Whether or not this is the same Caiaphas, the ossuary illuminates the priestly aristocracy's wealth and Hellenized burial customs.

The Sadducees who appear in 20:27 controlled the temple cult and rejected the resurrection of the dead, the existence of angels and demons, and the authority of any scripture beyond the Torah (Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.16; *War* 2.165). Their levirate question rests on Deuteronomy 25:5–10, the legal mechanism by which a deceased man's brother takes his widow to "raise up seed" for him — a custom rooted in patrilineal land tenure rather than romantic theology.

The denarius in 20:24 is a remarkable artifact for archaeology: surviving examples of Tiberius's silver denarii (the so-called "Tribute Penny," minted at Lugdunum from approximately 14–37 CE) bear his laureate head and the inscription **TI CAESAR DIVI AVG F AVGVSTVS** ("Tiberius Caesar, son of the divine Augustus, Augustus") on the obverse, with PONTIF MAXIM and a seated female figure on the reverse. The implication that pious Jews carried in their belts a coin proclaiming the deification of Augustus has been pressed by **Joel Green** (NICNT) and **Bock** (BECNT) as the sharper edge of Jesus's question.

Several Greek terms repay attention. ***Exousia*** (ἐξουσία, "authority") in 20:2, 8 carries the political-juridical weight of delegated power; the Sanhedrin asks not abstractly about Jesus's competence but about his *license*. ***Paradidōmi*** (παραδίδωμι, "hand over / deliver up"), already programmatic in Luke (cf. 9:44; 18:32), surfaces in 20:20 as the spies' explicit aim — to "deliver him up to the *archē* and *exousia* of the *hēgemōn*" (the prefect, Pilate). ***Akrogōniaios*** (ἀκρογωνιαῖος, "cornerstone" or "capstone") in 20:17 quotes Psalm 118:22 LXX. ***Anastasis*** (ἀνάστασις, "resurrection") in 20:27, 33, 35, 36 dominates the Sadducean exchange. And ***isangeloi*** (ἰσάγγελοι, "equal to angels") in 20:36 is a Lukan hapax that triggered enormous patristic and medieval debate about the resurrection body.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Luke 20 is the central panel of Luke's Jerusalem temple-controversy block (19:45–21:38). Luke organizes the chapter as a sequence of formal questions, each one functioning as a theological trap: the question of authority (1–8), the parable of the wicked tenants as Jesus's counter-question (9–19), the tribute question (20–26), the resurrection question (27–40), Jesus's own counter-question about David's son (41–44), and the warning against the scribes (45–47). **Charles Talbert** has shown how Luke uses these scholastic dialogues — *chreiai* in classical rhetorical terminology — to portray Jesus as the wisdom-teacher whose answers silence his interlocutors (*Reading Luke*).

Luke's redaction of his Markan source is consistently sharper. Where Mark 12:13 says the Pharisees and Herodians sought to "trap" Jesus, Luke 20:20 reframes the move in juridical language: they send "spies, who pretended to be sincere, that they might catch him in something he said, **so as to deliver him up to the authority and jurisdiction of the governor**." This is Lukan distinctive: the political edge of the Roman trial is anticipated already here. **Joseph Fitzmyer** (Anchor Bible) and **Joel Green** (NICNT) both note that Luke is preparing the reader for the trial scenes in chapters 22–23, where the charge is precisely that Jesus forbade tribute (23:2) — the very accusation his answer in 20:25 refutes.

The Sadducean exchange contains one of the most striking Lukan additions in the entire passion-week material: in 20:35–36, Luke alone has Jesus speak of "those who are considered worthy to attain to that age and to the resurrection from the dead" who "**neither marry nor are given in marriage, for they cannot die anymore, because they are equal to angels**." Mark and Matthew lack the language of "considered worthy," lack the explicit reasoning about deathlessness, and lack ***isangeloi***. This is a Lukan eschatological supplement that opens onto a substantial later Christian discourse about celibacy as anticipatory of the resurrection life — a reading developed by **Origen**, weaponized by **Jerome** in *Adversus Jovinianum*, embraced by Eastern monasticism, and ratified at the Second Council of Constantinople. Commentators have also noted what the saying does to the *premise* of the Sadducees' question: the levirate institution treats the woman's identity as a function of her successive marriages, and Jesus's reply quietly dismantles that premise by speaking of resurrection persons who are "children of God" rather than property of any household.

The Davidic question (20:41–44) quotes Psalm 110:1 — the most-cited Old Testament verse in the New Testament. Luke's wording follows the LXX closely; the play turns on the Hebrew *adonai* / *adoni* distinction collapsed in Greek to *kyrios*. The cornerstone citation (20:17) likewise comes from the LXX of Psalm 117:22 (MT 118:22), a psalm that already appeared in Luke 19:38 at the triumphal entry and will return implicitly in Acts 4:11 and 1 Peter 2:7. Luke is constructing a Christological psalm-mosaic across the two volumes.

A textual note: 20:36's ***isangeloi*** is well-attested across the major uncials (P75, Vaticanus, Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus). No major textual variant disturbs the chapter, though the Old Latin and some Western witnesses harmonize 20:34 toward parallel synoptic readings.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The wicked tenants parable (20:9–19) draws on **Isaiah 5:1–7**, the song of the unfruitful vineyard, which in its original setting was an indictment of Judah for injustice. Luke's Jesus retools the allegory: the vineyard is now Israel, the tenants are the leadership, the servants are the prophets, and the "beloved son" is unmistakably himself (echoing the baptismal voice of 3:22). The parable thus encodes a salvation-historical claim: the leadership's rejection of Jesus is the climax of a long pattern, and "the vineyard will be given to others" — a phrase whose force in the post-70 Lukan church is hard to miss. **Krister Stendahl**, **Lloyd Gaston**, and **N.T. Wright** have all engaged the question of whether this parable supports a supersessionist reading or an internal-Jewish prophetic critique. **Amy-Jill Levine** has cautioned against reading the parable as Christianity-replaces-Israel; she argues, with **E.P. Sanders**, that the targets are the Jerusalem leadership specifically, not "the Jews" as such. **Daniel Boyarin** complicates the picture further by reading the parable within the broader rabbinic-Christian shared discourse of "wicked tenant" stories.

On the resurrection question, the patristic tradition is rich. **Origen** (*De Principiis* 2.10; *Contra Celsum* 5.18–24) drew on Luke 20:35–36's *isangeloi* to argue for a spiritualized resurrection body, a view that **Methodius of Olympus** later attacked. **Augustine** (*City of God* 22.17–21) worked through the angel-comparison carefully to preserve the materiality of the body while affirming the transformation of its mode. **Cyril of Alexandria**'s *Commentary on Luke* (Homilies 136–139) anchors the passage to a defense of bodily resurrection against various heterodox readings. **Aquinas** (*Catena Aurea* on Luke 20; *Summa Theologiae* III Suppl. q. 81–86) systematized the medieval consensus: the resurrection body is *idem numero* (numerically the same body), but with new properties of impassibility, subtlety, agility, and clarity.

The Reformation reading is largely continuous with the medieval one. **Calvin**'s *Harmony of the Evangelists* on Luke 20 emphasizes Jesus's wisdom in evading the political trap of the tribute question while refusing to legitimate Roman idolatry. Calvin reads "render to Caesar" not as a doctrine of two kingdoms in the later Lutheran sense but as a careful subordination of civil obedience to the higher claim of God. **Luther**'s sermons on the Passion week (especially the 1525 series) drew on the wicked tenants parable as a pattern of God's long-suffering patience and final judgment.

In modern Lukan scholarship, **Hans Conzelmann**'s *Theology of St. Luke* (originally *Die Mitte der Zeit*, 1954) read Luke's Jerusalem material as a mid-period of salvation history — the time of Jesus, between the time of Israel and the time of the Church. The Sanhedrin's interrogations, on Conzelmann's reading, are part of Luke's careful political apologetic: the demonstration that the Jewish-Roman authorities, when they deal with Jesus on the merits, cannot find against him. **C.H. Talbert** built on Conzelmann to read Luke 20 as scholastic dialogue. **Joseph Fitzmyer** (Anchor Bible) is masterful on the Lukan redactional fingerprints. **Joel Green** (NICNT) emphasizes the Lukan political-economy reading: Jesus's answer about Caesar implicitly relativizes Caesar by the prior question of who bears whose image. **John Nolland** (Word) is patient on the levirate background. **Darrell Bock** (BECNT) defends a more conservative Christological reading. **I. Howard Marshall** (NIGTC) gives the Greek careful philological attention. **Luke Timothy Johnson** (Sacra Pagina) reads the chapter as a Lukan portrait of Jesus's prophetic confrontation with religious power. **François Bovon** (Hermeneia) is invaluable on the patristic reception.

**Karl Barth** (*Church Dogmatics* IV/2 §65–67) drew on the cornerstone citation as a programmatic statement of God's electing reversal. **Bonhoeffer**'s *Discipleship* engages 20:25 obliquely, arguing that the rendering-to-Caesar-and-God formula does not authorize a sphere of state autonomy from divine claim.

**N.T. Wright**, in *Jesus and the Victory of God*, *Luke for Everyone*, and *How God Became King*, reads the whole of Luke 20 as Jesus's prophetic showdown with Israel's leadership in the moment the kingdom of God is being established. The parable of the wicked tenants is, on Wright's reading, the chapter's heart: Jesus retells Israel's story — the vineyard, the long line of mistreated prophets, and now the "beloved son" — to announce that the leadership's rejection of him is the climactic act of that story, and that the vineyard's management is being handed to others (which for Wright means a renewed Israel gathered around Jesus, not the abolition of Israel). The "render to Caesar" exchange is Jesus refusing both the Zealot and the collaborationist paths, throwing the question back so that it becomes a question about what bears God's image and who has the final claim on a human life. And the Davidic-son puzzle (Psalm 110, the most-quoted OT text in the NT) is Jesus pressing his interlocutors toward the recognition that the Messiah is not merely David's descendant restoring a national kingdom but David's *Lord* — enthroned at God's right hand, which is exactly the claim Luke will have Peter make at Pentecost. For Wright, these are not isolated debating points but Jesus systematically redefining what Israel's hope, kingship, and God's reign actually mean, in the temple courts, days before the cross.

The Sadducean question has also been engaged by Jewish scholarship. **Geza Vermes** (*Jesus the Jew*) reads Jesus's answer as Pharisaic in shape — citing Exodus's "I am the God of Abraham" as evidence for resurrection is a midrashic move at home in Pharisaic exegesis. **Jacob Neusner** (*A Rabbi Talks with Jesus*) and **Levine** broadly concur that Jesus here aligns with the Pharisaic majority against the Sadducean priestly elite.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What does it mean to "render to Caesar what is Caesar's, and to God what is God's"?** A first reading, congenial to Reformation two-kingdoms theology and especially to the Lutheran tradition, takes the saying as an authorization of distinct spheres: civil obedience to the political order, religious obedience to God, with each sphere having its own legitimacy. **Calvin** softens this by insisting the spheres are not coequal: civil authority is itself derived from God's rule. A second, more politically pointed reading — pressed by **John Howard Yoder** in *The Politics of Jesus* and by liberation theologians like **Justo González** — insists that Jesus's answer is sharper than it sounds. Since the image and superscription of God is borne not by coins but by human beings (Genesis 1:27), and since *everything* belongs to God in some final sense, the saying actually withdraws from Caesar the totalizing claim he makes on the denarius itself. Jesus does not endorse paying tribute; he reframes the question so that the tribute's extent is bounded by what does not belong to God — and there is precious little of that. A third reading, developed by **N.T. Wright** in *Jesus and the Victory of God*, sees the saying as deliberately ambiguous: it refuses both the Zealot path (refuse tribute and revolt) and the Herodian path (collaborate fully), and instead invites the questioner to ask what does belong to God — at which point the answer becomes the questioner's own life and loyalty. Steelmanning each: the two-kingdoms reading takes seriously Romans 13's later canonical witness to legitimate civil authority; the liberation reading takes seriously the prophetic critique of imperial idolatry; Wright's reading takes seriously Jesus's habitual refusal to be cornered. *Where this leaves us:* the saying functions less as a doctrine of church-state relations than as a Christological provocation — a question hurled back at the questioner, demanding that they think about what bears God's image, and at what cost.

**Tension 2: What does Jesus mean by "they neither marry nor are given in marriage" and "equal to angels"?** A first position, dominant in the Eastern monastic tradition and the Latin patristic synthesis, took the resurrection life as ontologically transcending marriage; this licensed the elevation of celibacy as anticipatory and the gradual development of mandatory clerical celibacy in the Western church. **Origen, Jerome, Aquinas** all stand in this stream. A second position, developed by Reformers like **Luther** in *On Monastic Vows* and by Anglican divines, denied that Luke 20 supports a hierarchy of vocations: the saying speaks of the resurrection state itself, not of a special calling within history. The reformed reading restores marriage to its goodness without making celibacy second-rate, but also without making it superior. A third position attends to what the saying does to the logic of the Sadducees' question itself: the levirate scenario presupposes that the woman's identity is a function of her successive marriages to a string of brothers, and Jesus's reply dissolves that presupposition by speaking of resurrection persons who are children of God rather than the property of any household. This observation is registered across the mainstream commentaries (Fitzmyer, Green, Bovon). Steelmanning each: the patristic reading takes seriously the eschatological orientation of Christian sexual ethics; the Reformation reading takes seriously the goodness of created marriage; the third reading takes seriously the way the question itself was a trap that reduced the woman to a chattel passed down a line of men. *Where this leaves us:* Luke 20:35–36 is best read as eschatological re-description rather than as an ethical program for the present age. The resurrection life is *isangelic* not because angels are sexless beings but because angels do not die, and where there is no death there is no need for the propagation of the species through marriage. The saying neither denigrates marriage nor canonizes celibacy; it relativizes both by gesturing toward a mode of communion that is no longer organized around mortality.

One unresolved question: if the resurrection life is "equal to angels," what continuity does it have with the embodied histories — including the marital and sexual histories — of the people who lived through them? The patristic answer (that all such relationships are taken up and transfigured) is theologically coherent but phenomenologically opaque. We cannot say what it means.

## The Thread

The cornerstone citation in Luke 20:17 echoes Luke 19:38 (the triumphal entry's quotation of Psalm 118) and reaches forward into Acts 4:11 and 1 Peter 2:7, becoming a programmatic Christological summary of God's reversal in raising the rejected one. The Davidic-son question of 20:41–44 reaches back to the angelic announcement in Luke 1:32 ("the throne of his father David") and forward to Acts 2:34–36, where Peter at Pentecost cites the same psalm to argue that the resurrection enthrones Jesus as Lord. The "image and superscription" of 20:24 implicitly invokes Genesis 1:27 — humans bear God's *eikōn* — and the question of what bears whose image will return at the cross, where the Son who is "the image of the invisible God" (Colossians 1:15) will be handed over to Caesar's authority. Watch the ***paradidōmi*** verb in 20:20: it will return at 22:4, 22:6, 22:21, 22:22, 22:48, 23:25, and 24:7, becoming the load-bearing word of the Lukan passion.
