# 2 Samuel 2: Hebron, Mahanaim, and the Pool of Gibeon

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 2 opens with David's relocation from Ziklag to Hebron and his anointing as king of Judah. The geography is decisive. Hebron — biblical Kiryat-Arba, modern Tel Rumeida overlooking the contemporary West Bank city — is the highest city in the southern Judean hill country, sitting at roughly 930 meters above sea level. It commands the central ridge route from the Negev to Jerusalem and the Bethlehem corridor. Avi Ofer's excavations at Tel Rumeida in the 1980s and 1990s, building on Philip Hammond's earlier work, identified an Iron Age I town of modest size (around 2.5 hectares within the wall circuit) but with a long Bronze Age pedigree — a site already venerable when David made it his capital. The walls, much-rebuilt, include Middle Bronze cyclopean construction reused in later phases. David's choice of Hebron is not accidental: it is Judah's old patriarchal seat (Genesis 23: Abraham's purchase of Machpelah), a Levitical city of refuge (Joshua 21:13), and the inheritance of Caleb (Joshua 14). To be anointed king at Hebron is to take the throne in the most ancestrally Judahite town possible.

The Saulide capital, by contrast, is **Mahanaim** — a Transjordanian site whose precise identification remains debated. The leading candidates are Tell edh-Dhahab al-Gharbi (the western mound) and Tell edh-Dhahab al-Sharqi (the eastern), both on the Jabbok River system in modern Jordan. Israel Finkelstein, Thomas Römer, and Erez Ben-Yosef have argued for the western mound based on Iron IIA ceramics and fortifications; the older identification with Tell el-Hajjaj has lost ground. The choice of Mahanaim by Abner is itself revealing: it is across the Jordan, well east of the Philistine zone of operation, defensible, and historically associated with Jacob's encounter with the angels (Genesis 32:2 — *mahanayim* meaning "two camps"). Saul's house is, in effect, governing in exile, occupying a refuge rather than a capital. The political geography of the chapter is therefore asymmetric from the first: David in the central hill country, anointed by his own tribe in his own ancestral seat; Ish-bosheth east of the Jordan, propped up by his late father's general.

The chapter also stages the famous **Pool of Gibeon** confrontation. Gibeon (el-Jib, eight kilometers northwest of Jerusalem) was excavated by James B. Pritchard between 1956 and 1962, and the site has become one of the great Iron Age water systems of the Levant. Pritchard documented an enormous rock-cut cylindrical pool — twenty-five meters deep, twelve meters across — with a spiral staircase descending its inner wall. The pool was almost certainly the *berekat Giv'on* of 2 Samuel 2:13. Whether Pritchard's pool is the original Iron I feature or a later refurbishment has been debated; recent reassessments (Brad Kelle, Carl Ehrlich) place its main use in Iron IIA, plausibly the period of David's wars. The contest at the pool — twelve to twelve, mutual stabbing, all twenty-four falling — is staged at a literal cistern of national significance.

Five Hebrew terms drive the chapter:

1. **חֶבְרוֹן** (*Hevron*) — Hebron, root *h-b-r* meaning "join, ally, confederate." The name itself signals the city's function as a meeting place of tribal alliance. The men of Judah "joined" themselves to David here.

2. **מַחֲנַיִם** (*Mahanayim*) — "two camps," dual form. The placename's Jacob-resonance (Gen 32) is in the air throughout the chapter.

3. **חֶלְקַת הַצֻּרִים** (*Helkat ha-tsurim*) — "field of sword-edges" or "field of flints" or "field of the sides." The etymology is contested; the LXX renders *meris ton epiboulon* ("portion of the plotters"). The placename memorializes the bloody contest.

4. **רַחַשׁ** (*rakhash*) is not in this chapter, but the politically charged term **בָּחוּר** (*bachur*) — "young man, chosen warrior" (2:14, 21) — is. It denotes elite combatants, not raw recruits.

5. **חֶסֶד וֶאֱמֶת** (*chesed ve-emet*) — "steadfast love and faithfulness" (2:6). David's blessing on the men of Jabesh-gilead invokes the formal language of covenant loyalty, the same vocabulary that governs the David-Jonathan bond and the later Davidic covenant.

The chapter's archaeological backdrop also includes Jabesh-gilead, identified with reasonable confidence at Tell Abu Kharaz on the eastern bank of the Jordan (Peter Fischer's Swedish excavations, 1989–2017). Jabesh's loyalty to Saul — they had recovered his body from the walls of Beth-Shean and given it honorable burial (1 Sam 31:11–13) — makes them a prime target of David's diplomacy. By blessing them and inviting their continued loyalty (2:5–7), David signals that his accession does not require disloyalty to Saul.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 2 is a tightly constructed political narrative, executed in three movements: the southern coronation (1–7), the northern counter-coronation (8–11), and the military encounter at Gibeon (12–32). The structure mirrors the political reality: two rival claims, then their first armed test.

The first movement opens with David's characteristic *inquiring of the LORD* (*sha'al ba-YHWH*, 2:1) — the verb that has structured David's entire rise (1 Sam 23:2, 23:4, 30:8). Robert Alter notes that the chapter places the verb in its richest position yet: David, on the cusp of kingship, does not act on his own counsel even when the path looks obvious. The double inquiry — *Shall I go up?* then *To which city?* — is liturgically formal. McCarter argues that the inquiry presupposes the use of the *ephod* with Urim and Thummim, the binary lot system associated with the Aaronide priesthood.

The reference to Ahinoam of Jezreel and Abigail the widow of Nabal (2:2) is more than a domestic note. Both women carry political weight. Ahinoam shares a name with Saul's wife (1 Sam 14:50); Jon Levenson and Baruch Halpern have argued that this Ahinoam *is* Saul's widow, married by David in a calculated move with regal-political resonance (taking the predecessor's wife as a claim on the throne; cf. 2 Sam 12:8 and the Absalom episode in 16:21–22). Abigail brings the Calebite estate of Carmel into David's household, securing southern Judahite territorial backing. The "two wives" notice is a marriage-and-territory ledger.

The second movement (8–11) introduces Abner and Ish-bosheth. The textual situation here is delicate. The MT reads *Ish-bosheth* — "man of shame" — but 1 Chronicles 8:33 and 9:39 preserve the original *Esh-baal* — "man of Baal" or perhaps "fire of Baal." This is a clear case of the Deuteronomist's later substitution of *boshet* for *baal* in proper names, a scribal censorship driven by the post-exilic horror of Baal-theophoric naming. The same operation produces *Mephibosheth* from *Merib-baal* (cf. 1 Chron 8:34, 9:40). The 2012 publication by Yosef Garfinkel and his team of the **Khirbet Qeiyafa ostracon** — an early Iron IIA inscription found in a Judahite border fortress dated to the late eleventh or early tenth century — included the name *Eshbaal ben Beda*, providing extrabiblical confirmation that "Eshbaal" was a real Judahite name in approximately the period of Saul. The ostracon does not name Saul's son specifically, but it establishes the linguistic plausibility of the Chronicler's tradition. The MT's *boshet* is a theological gloss; the underlying name was *Eshbaal*.

The list of territories Abner secures for Ish-bosheth (2:9) — Gilead, the Ashurites, Jezreel, Ephraim, Benjamin, "all Israel" — is geographically northern, eastern, and central. Some scholars (McCarter, Anderson) read "the Ashurites" as a textual corruption of "the Geshurites" or "the Asherites," since the Assyrian "Ashur" is anachronistic for this period. The political point survives any of these readings: Abner controls the north, David the south.

The third movement, the contest at the pool (12–32), is one of the strangest combat narratives in the Hebrew Bible. The proposal — *let the young men arise and play* (*yesachaqu*, 2:14) — is jarring. The verb *sachaq* normally means "play, sport, laugh," and McCarter, with Hertzberg, reads it as a euphemistic register: ritualized combat presented as sport. Saul Olyan and others have compared the scene to the *epic-cycle* duels of the Greek and Levantine traditions — paired champions fighting representational combats whose outcome was meant to determine the larger conflict. The narrative twist is that the contest fails: all twenty-four kill each other simultaneously (2:16). The decision-by-champions doctrine breaks down; full battle follows.

The Asahel-Abner episode (18–23) is structured as a chase-narrative with three escalating warnings. Abner's warnings are both protective and politically calculating: killing Joab's brother will forever bar his own integration into David's command structure. Asahel's refusal — narrated with the curt repetition *ve-lo avah lasur* ("but he refused to turn aside," 2:21, 23) — is the tragic pivot. Bar-Efrat (*Narrative Art in the Bible*) analyzes the scene as a study in refused offers, where each refusal accelerates the disaster. Abner's killing of Asahel with the butt-end of the spear (the spear comes out his back, 2:23) is depicted with grim economy. Fokkelman notes the verb of those who arrive afterward: *they stood still* (2:23). The narrator marks the moment as a stunned freeze in the middle of a chase.

Within the larger argument of the book, chapter 2 inaugurates what 3:1 will summarize as "the long war between the house of Saul and the house of David." The chapter is the opening campaign of a civil war that the Deuteronomist is at pains to depict as defensive on David's part and aggressive on the Saulide side: Abner, not Joab, calls for the contest; Asahel, not Abner, refuses to disengage. McCarter's apologetic frame is at full force.

Textually, the LXX shows several minor variants from MT, mostly in the troop numbers and the geography of the pursuit. 4QSam-a is fragmentary in this chapter but appears to support a Vorlage close to LXX in places. The scribal censorship of *Eshbaal* to *Ish-bosheth* is the most theologically loaded textual issue.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical arc, chapter 2 begins what Rost called the bridge between the History of David's Rise and the consolidation in chapters 5 and 7. The chapter establishes the legal mechanism of David's accession — popular anointing by Judah, on the basis of inquiry and divine approval — that will be repeated in 5:3 when all-Israel anoints him at Hebron. The chapter is the prologue to the Davidic-covenant city.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** Josephus (*Ant.* 7.1–2) compresses the narrative but emphasizes David's piety and Abner's political miscalculation. The rabbinic tradition is sharply divided on Abner. *Sanhedrin* 20a treats Abner as righteous in some respects but censures him for not having opposed Saul more vigorously when Saul pursued David — and especially for not having stopped Saul from killing the priests of Nob (1 Sam 22). His death in chapter 3 is read as judicial punishment for these failings. Rashi follows this line. Radak treats Abner's killing of Asahel as legitimate self-defense once Asahel refused to turn aside, citing the threefold warning as moral exoneration. Ralbag emphasizes the chapter's lesson in political prudence: David inquired before acting; Abner did not. Abarbanel develops a sustained reading of the chapter as a contrast between two modes of kingship — David's, derived from divine election and tribal consent; Ish-bosheth's, derived from dynastic continuity through a general's sponsorship. Yair Zakovitch's literary reading attends to the doubled coronation as a structural device. Moshe Garsiel's work on the Saul-David typology emphasizes the inversion: Saul's house is now in the position David occupied during 1 Samuel 18–30, hunted and shrinking. Avivah Zornberg has written more meditatively on the contest at the pool as a parable of failed substitution — the attempt to settle a national question by twelve representatives that produces only twenty-four corpses.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** Patristic readings vary. Origen's fragmentary homilies on Samuel treat the contest at the pool typologically as the futility of civil strife within the people of God. Augustine in *City of God* book 17 treats the chapter primarily as a stage in the political drama of David's accession; he is more interested in the messianic implications of the seven-and-a-half years at Hebron (a number with patristic typological weight) than in the tactical details. Ambrose in *De Officiis* uses Abner as a complex moral case: a competent man caught on the wrong side of providence. Chrysostom dwells on David's blessing of Jabesh-gilead as a model of magnanimity — rewarding loyalty to a former rival. Gregory the Great in the *Moralia* reads Asahel's refusal to turn aside as a study in the spiritual danger of zeal without prudence. Aquinas, in his treatment of just war (*Summa* II-II q. 40), uses the Abner-Asahel exchange as an illustration of self-defense within combat: Abner's repeated warnings establish his moral standing.

Calvin's sermons on 2 Samuel — preserved in the Bibliothèque de Genève manuscripts and partly published in the *Supplementa Calviniana* — treat the chapter as a study in lawful and unlawful kingship. Calvin reads David's inquiry as exemplary and contrasts it sharply with Abner's unilateral installation of Ish-bosheth. The Reformer is particularly interested in the legitimacy question: David is anointed by Judah's elders in response to a divine word; Ish-bosheth is installed by a general in response to political necessity. For Calvin this difference is the seed of all that follows. Luther's marginal notes are sparser but emphasize the chapter's depiction of the inevitable consequences of disordered political life.

Critical scholarship: Wellhausen identified the chapter as part of the apologetic source. McCarter's Anchor Bible commentary treats the apologetic shape carefully. A. A. Anderson (WBC) gives the most thorough text-critical and source-critical analysis; he is especially valuable on the Eshbaal/Ish-bosheth issue and on the geography of the Saulide kingdom. Hertzberg's Old Testament Library volume is the classic mid-twentieth-century treatment. Brueggemann reads the chapter as a study in the political costs of leadership, including David's. Halpern (*David's Secret Demons*) is sharp on the apologetic mechanics: David inquired, David was anointed by request, David did not initiate the contest at the pool, David's general (not David himself) killed Asahel — but David benefits at every turn. McKenzie (*King David: A Biography*) follows a similar line. Polzin (*David and the Deuteronomist* vol. 3) emphasizes the Deuteronomist's careful structuring of the doubled coronation. Fokkelman's detailed literary analysis remains valuable for the Asahel-Abner exchange. Bar-Efrat's narrative-art monograph treats the same scene as a model of refused-warnings narrative.

It is worth noting how little narrative space the chapter gives Ahinoam and Abigail: as Brueggemann and Alter both observe, the "two wives" of 2:2 appear only as items on the marriage-and-territory ledger, named for the alliances they secure rather than for anything they do — the political accounting crowds out everything else just as the stakes rise.

The Eshbaal/Ish-bosheth scribal censorship has been a major focus of recent scholarship. Garfinkel's Khirbet Qeiyafa ostracon publication (BASOR, 2012) is the touchstone. The scribal change preserves the theological discomfort with Saul's family's onomastic Baalism while leaving an evidentiary trail in Chronicles. The history of the gloss illuminates how Israelite tradition handled its own past.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Why two anointings?** Chapter 2 stages David's coronation by Judah alone, with Israel's coronation deferred until 5:3. Why does the Deuteronomist preserve this seven-and-a-half-year split?

*Position A: The split is historical fact preserved without theological smoothing.* This is McKenzie's and Halpern's preferred reading. David was a Judahite chieftain who had to consolidate the south before the north would consider him. The split is not theological architecture; it is what happened.

*Position B: The split functions theologically as a typology of kingship-by-stages.* This is the Christian patristic reading developed by Origen and Augustine: David's reign begins narrow and grows wide, as Christ's kingdom begins with Israel before extending to the nations. The seven-and-a-half years carry numerological weight (seven plus the sabbath portion).

*Position C: The split dramatizes the political theology of consent.* Brueggemann and Eugene Peterson read this way: David does not impose himself on the north; he waits to be invited (cf. 5:1 — "we are your bone and flesh"). The chapter establishes that Davidic kingship is not seizure but reception. The split is essential to that doctrine.

The steelman of position A is strong: the chapter contains too many specific local details (Mahanaim, the Ashurites, the seven-and-six-month figure) to be tidy theological architecture. The steelman of C is stronger still: even granting historical reality, the Deuteronomist's preservation of the split serves a doctrine of consent. The two are compatible.

*Where this leaves us:* The chapter records and theologizes simultaneously. The historical fact of the split becomes the basis for a political theology that constrains David's heirs as much as it justifies David himself. Solomon will inherit the same logic — and his violation of it (the corvée labor in the north) will eventually split the kingdom in 1 Kings 12.

**Tension 2: What is the moral standing of Abner?** The chapter introduces him as the architect of an unjustified counter-claim, but his treatment of Asahel is morally restrained, and his later defection (chapter 3) suggests a political realist of considerable sophistication.

*Position A: Abner is straightforwardly culpable.* The rabbinic tradition (Sanhedrin 20a) and the apologetic frame in McCarter both treat Abner as a man whose competence cannot save him from being on the wrong side of providence. He installed an illegitimate king; he led an aggressive campaign; he killed a Davidic warrior; his eventual death is just.

*Position B: Abner is the chapter's most morally complex figure.* This is Brueggemann's reading. Abner warns Asahel three times; Abner is the first to call for de-escalation (2:26 — "shall the sword devour forever?"); Abner will be the one to broker reunification in chapter 3. The narrator gives him more interior life than he gives Joab.

*Position C: Abner is a tragic figure caught between loyalties to Saul's house and recognition of David's legitimacy.* Halpern reads this way. Abner served the Saulide line out of obligation rather than conviction; his eventual defection is the resolution of a tension he had been carrying from the start. His death is a political assassination, not a divine judgment.

*Where this leaves us:* Position B and C together produce the most adequate reading. Abner is competent, restrained where he can be, and finally killable because his political utility runs out. The chapter does not flatten him into a villain, and the eventual royal lament for him in 3:33 confirms that the narrator wants him remembered as a "prince and a great man."

**Unresolved question:** What does the contest-by-champions episode mean for the Deuteronomist's theology of war? The contest *fails* — twelve plus twelve fall together. Is the failure a critique of the contest-tradition itself, an indictment of the legitimacy of either side's claim, or simply a narrative pivot to full battle? The episode resists clean theological reading; the Deuteronomist preserves it without comment.

## The Thread

The Davidic inquiry of the LORD that opens chapter 2 picks up the thread from 1 Samuel 23:2, 23:4, 30:7–8 — the verb *sha'al ba-YHWH* has structured David's military and political decisions throughout 1 Samuel, and it returns now in the more weighty register of dynastic foundation. The thread will run through the rest of David's reign until it conspicuously *stops*, in the preparations for the Bathsheba episode and after; the absence of inquiry in the later chapters is part of the moral architecture.

The ritualized combat at the Pool of Gibeon foreshadows the contest at the same location in later national memory. Gibeon will return as a sacrificial site (1 Kings 3:4 — Solomon's dream), as a battleground (Joshua 10), and as the site of Saulide blood-vengeance (2 Sam 21). The pool itself is a literary as well as topographic anchor.

The Asahel-Abner exchange seeds the blood-feud that drives chapter 3 to its catastrophe. The Deuteronomist plants the spear-stroke at 2:23 and harvests it at 3:27. Joab's killing of Abner cannot be read without the chapter 2 backstory.

Flag forward: Abner's appeal in 2:26 — "shall the sword devour forever?" — will be inverted by Joab himself in the long civil-war chapters of David's reign, and the question will recur thematically until it becomes the structural problem of the Davidic dynasty's later tragedy. The chapter's most haunting line is Abner's, and the book never quite answers him.
