# Judges 15: The Jawbone and the Spring

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter unfolds across a tight Shephelah geography: Timnah (Tel Batash), the cleft of the rock of Etam, Lehi, and Ramath-lehi. Etam's location is debated; some place it in the Judean hill country south of Bethlehem (where 1 Chronicles 4:32 mentions an Etam), others in the Shephelah closer to the Sorek Valley. Lehi (Hebrew *Leḥi*, לְחִי, "jaw") is the place name on which the chapter's central wordplay turns. The setting is the Iron I-IIA frontier between Philistine and Israelite settlement, the same contested zone that frames the entire Samson cycle.

The agricultural detail is precise. Samson goes to visit his wife at the time of wheat harvest (15:1), which in the southern Levant runs from late April through early June. The standing grain still in the fields, the cut grain stacked for threshing, and the olive orchards together represent the Philistine villagers' annual food security. To burn these is not merely vandalism but a calculated attack on the agricultural base of the local economy. Lawrence Stager's work on the early Israelite economy and Carol Meyers's analysis of household agriculture in *Discovering Eve* both emphasize that the loss of a season's wheat and the destruction of olive trees (which take twenty to thirty years to replace) would have produced acute economic distress for the affected villages.

The 300 foxes (Hebrew *shuʿalim*, שׁוּעָלִים) episode has provoked extensive commentary. The Hebrew *shuʿal* can refer to either a fox (*Vulpes vulpes*) or a jackal (*Canis aureus*); foxes are solitary and hard to capture in numbers, while jackals are pack animals more plausibly trapped in groups. G. R. Driver and several commentators have argued that "jackals" is the better translation here. The folkloric character of the number (300, like Gideon's 300 in chapter 7) and the difficulty of the technical execution have led many scholars (Niditch, Mobley, Boling) to read the episode as a folk-heroic exaggeration. There are also ANE parallels: a Roman agricultural rite at the Cerealia involved letting foxes loose with torches tied to their tails (Ovid, *Fasti* 4.679–712), which Frazer and others noted as comparative material; whether the Israelite story is independent or part of a wider Mediterranean folkloric pattern is debated.

The political dynamic of 15:9–13 is one of the most quietly devastating passages in Judges. The Philistines come to bind Samson, and the men of Judah — not Dan, but Judah — bring him out themselves. Three thousand Judahite men come down to the rock of Etam. Their question — "do you not know that the Philistines are rulers over us?" — is the chapter's most matter-of-fact statement of Israelite political subjection. Judah has accepted Philistine hegemony; Samson is the threat to a workable accommodation. Yairah Amit and Marc Brettler have read this scene as deliberately preserved by the deuteronomistic narrator to expose how far Israelite tribal identity has decayed under sustained foreign rule. Daniel Block notes that Samson's negotiation with Judah ("swear to me that you will not attack me yourselves") is the Hebrew Bible's earliest attestation of inter-tribal Israelite political negotiation under foreign occupation.

The "hip and thigh" expression at 15:8 is Hebrew *shoq ʿal-yarekh* (שׁוֹק עַל-יָרֵךְ), literally "leg upon thigh." It appears nowhere else in this exact form, but the cognate vocabulary suggests a wrestling idiom — a leg-over-thigh hold or a comprehensive defeat. Robert Boling and Daniel Block both treat it as a wrestling metaphor preserved in Hebrew idiom. Some commentators have argued it is a euphemism for genital mutilation; this reading has less textual support but is occasionally proposed (Niditch).

The jawbone of the donkey (Hebrew *leḥi ha-ḥamor ha-tariyah*, לְחִי הַחֲמוֹר הַטְּרִיָּה — "the fresh/moist jawbone of the donkey") is the chapter's central material object. The adjective *tariyah*, "fresh" or "moist," is significant: a fresh jawbone has more weight and structural integrity than a dried one, but ritually it is also a corpse-contact object — another Nazirite violation. The donkey's jawbone is a plausible improvised weapon; ANE archaeology has produced numerous examples of bone weapons and tools from periods predating the widespread availability of iron. The play between *leḥi* (jawbone) and *Leḥi* (the place name) is the chapter's central wordplay; Hebrew place-name etiologies frequently turn on such assonance, and the chapter offers two: Ramath-lehi ("height of the jawbone") and En-hakkore ("spring of the caller").

The donkey-jawbone victory poem in 15:16 is a piece of preserved poetry, possibly older than its prose framework. The Hebrew has dense alliteration: *bilḥi ha-ḥamor ḥamor ḥamoratayim* (בִּלְחִי הַחֲמוֹר חֲמוֹר חֲמוֹרָתָיִם) — "with the jawbone of a donkey, a heap, two heaps" — turning on the homophony of *ḥamor* (donkey) and *ḥomer* (heap). The wordplay does not survive translation. Robert Alter and Adele Berlin have both treated this as exemplary of biblical Hebrew poetic compression.

Five Hebrew terms repay attention. *Leḥi* (לְחִי), "jawbone" / place name, is the chapter's central wordplay. *Ḥomer* (חֹמֶר), "heap," puns with *ḥamor*, "donkey." *Shoq ʿal-yarekh* (שׁוֹק עַל-יָרֵךְ), "hip and thigh," is the wrestling idiom for total defeat. *En-hakkore* (עֵין הַקּוֹרֵא), "spring of the caller," is the etiology for the chapter's miraculous water. *Tariyah* (טְרִיָּה), "fresh" or "moist," describes the jawbone and quietly marks the corpse contact.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Judges 15 is folkloric narrative at its densest. The chapter compresses three escalating cycles of revenge — Samson against the Philistines (vv. 1–8), the Philistines against Judah and Samson (vv. 9–13), Samson against the Philistines at Lehi (vv. 14–17) — each driven by *talion* logic ("as they did to me, so I have done to them," 15:11). Susan Niditch (*Folklore and the Hebrew Bible*) and Gregory Mobley (*The Empty Men*) read the chapter as exemplary of the trickster-hero tradition, where escalating violence and improvisational weapon-use mark the genre. Mobley specifically compares the jawbone scene to the Mediterranean tradition of the underdog hero with an improvised weapon (David and the sling, Shamgar and the oxgoad in Judges 3:31).

The literary structure is built on the verb of descent and ascent. The Philistines "come up" (15:6, 9, 10), the Judahites "go down" (15:11) and "bring up" Samson (15:13), Samson "goes down" (15:8) and is "brought up." The vertical vocabulary is geographical (the rock of Etam is in the highlands, Lehi is lower) and theological (Samson moves through cycles of defeat and ascent). Robert Alter has noted the chapter's careful use of these directional verbs.

The two etiologies — Ramath-lehi at 15:17 and En-hakkore at 15:19 — are characteristic of the chapter's folkloric stratum. ANE folktales frequently include place-name etiologies that purport to explain the names of locally known features; the Hebrew Bible preserves dozens of such etiologies (Bethel, Beersheba, Mizpah, Peniel). The double etiology in Judges 15 is unusual and suggests that the chapter incorporates two originally separate traditions — the jawbone story and the spring story — bound together in the present narrative.

The poetic insertion at 15:16 is a brief victory song in the genre of the broader Hebrew Bible's victory poetry (compare Exodus 15, Judges 5, 1 Samuel 18:7). It is one of the shortest preserved examples and is conspicuously focused on the weapon rather than on God — Samson celebrates his own act, not the divine deliverance, a distinction the narrator immediately corrects in 15:18 when Samson does pray to God for water.

The chapter's place in the Samson cycle is as the second stage of his recorded adult action and the chapter that establishes him as a public deliverer rather than a private brawler. The closing notice at 15:20 — "and he judged Israel in the days of the Philistines twenty years" — is the standard deuteronomistic judge formula, and most commentators (Boling, Block, Webb) treat it as an editorial seam that originally closed the Samson cycle. The chapter 16 material would then be a later expansion. Susan Niditch and Lawson Younger develop this redactional argument at length.

Textual variants are significant. The LXX A and B recensions diverge in several details, particularly in the spelling of Etam and Lehi, in the casualty figure, and in the wording of the victory poem. The Old Latin tradition preserves additional variants. The 4QJudg-b fragment from Qumran preserves a small portion of Judges 15 but not enough to resolve any major variant.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc of Judges 15 establishes Samson as a deliverer in the technical sense — the closing notice that he "judged Israel twenty years" (15:20) places him alongside Othniel, Ehud, Deborah, Gideon, and Jephthah in the deuteronomistic list. But the chapter's mode of deliverance is uniquely individual: there is no Israelite army, no tribal coalition, no holy war assembly. Samson's deliverance is one man's revenge cycle escalating against a foreign power. The book's argument has shifted: deliverance, which began with Othniel raising up an army, has become the solitary act of a single charismatic figure whose moral character is increasingly compromised. Yairah Amit and Daniel Block both treat this trajectory as deliberate; the deuteronomist is showing the decay of the deliverer institution itself.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Pseudo-Philo (*LAB* 43) gives a brief retelling that emphasizes the divine source of Samson's strength. Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.8.7–9) renders the chapter with Hellenistic narrative care, treating the jawbone scene as exemplary of divine empowerment. The Talmud (*Sotah* 9b–10a) develops sustained Samson aggadah; *Sotah* 10a discusses the spring at En-hakkore at length, with one tradition holding that the water came from Samson's own jawbone (a hyperliteral reading of the Hebrew) and another insisting on a separate miraculous source. The midrashic tradition (*Bereshit Rabbah*, *Numbers Rabbah*) treats the foxes-and-firebrands episode as evidence of Samson's strategic intelligence as well as his strength. Rashi reads the chapter literally with detailed glosses on the Hebrew wordplay. Radak (Kimhi) develops the linguistic analysis of the Lehi etiologies. Maimonides cites Samson in the *Guide* as an example of a figure whose physical powers were divinely granted but whose moral judgment was independent. The Jewish liturgical tradition does not commemorate the Samson story in the way it does Hannah, Esther, or Ruth; this absence reflects the rabbinic ambivalence about a hero whose exploits cannot easily be presented as exemplary.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** The patristic tradition was characteristically allegorical. Augustine, in his sermons and in *Quaestiones in Heptateuchum*, reads the foxes and firebrands as figures for false teachers spreading destructive doctrine; the jawbone of the donkey becomes a figure for the simplicity of apostolic preaching that nonetheless overcomes the strong; the spring at En-hakkore becomes a figure for baptism or for the living water of John 4. Origen develops similar allegorical readings in his homilies on Judges. Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job* picks up the jawbone image. Bede's commentary on Judges, the *Glossa Ordinaria*, and Hugh of Saint-Cher all systematize the patristic allegories. The medieval visual tradition is rich: Samson with the jawbone appears in countless medieval manuscript illuminations and in major Renaissance and Baroque paintings (notably Guido Reni's "Samson Victorious" of 1611–1612). The Reformation moved away from the systematic allegory but maintained the typological readings. Luther in his lectures on Judges treats Samson as an example of how God's power works through unlikely instruments. Calvin's *Sermons on Judges* are critical of Samson's revenge ethic but defend the legitimacy of his role as deliverer. Critical scholarship has been extensive: Wellhausen treated the chapter as folkloric and pre-deuteronomic, Boling and Soggin developed the source-critical analysis, Block and Webb provide synchronic readings. James Crenshaw's *Samson: A Secret Betrayed, A Vow Ignored* gives the chapter sustained literary attention. Crenshaw, Block, and Webb all foreground the moral hinge at 15:6 — the woman of Timnah is burned to death along with her father by the same Philistines who had earlier threatened her, an act of violence the narrator records without commentary and that becomes the proximate cause of Samson's escalation. John Milton's *Samson Agonistes* (1671) is the great Christian literary engagement with the cycle, treating Samson as a tragic hero whose blindness and final death (chapter 16) provide the play's climax; chapter 15 supplies the backstory of revenge that frames the tragedy. Hebrews 11:32 lists Samson among the heroes of faith.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Both Jewish and Christian traditions treat Samson as a genuine deliverer despite his moral compromises and read 15:14–17 as a clear instance of divine empowerment. Both traditions are uneasy about the ethics of the foxes-and-firebrands episode and the broader revenge cycle, with the rabbinic and patristic readers offering different framings of Samson's moral status. They diverge on typology: the Christian tradition reaches consistently for allegory (jawbone = apostolic preaching, spring = baptism), while the Jewish tradition stays closer to the historical-literary analysis. The Christian visual and literary tradition (medieval art, Milton) treats Samson as a tragic hero; the Jewish tradition is more willing to register him as a problematic figure whose exploits cannot quite be assimilated into the canon of exemplary deliverers. Contemporary commentary (Crenshaw, Block, Webb) has insisted on the centrality of the woman of Timnah's death in 15:6 as the moral hinge of the chapter — a structural reading neither earlier tradition systematically developed.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The first tension is the theology of escalating revenge. Samson's *talion* logic — "as they did to me, so I have done to them" (15:11) — is the chapter's explicit moral framework. But the talion principle of Exodus 21 and Deuteronomy 19 is a principle of judicial proportionality, not of personal vendetta; it is a constraint on revenge, not a license for it. Catholic moral theology has historically distinguished between just war (which requires proper authority, just cause, and proportional means) and private vengeance (which is forbidden). On this distinction, Samson's individual revenge cycle fails the just war criteria and is morally suspect even when it accomplishes a divinely intended outcome. Reformed theology, with its stronger doctrine of providence, would treat the divine instrumentality more aggressively — God uses Samson's revenge for divine purposes — while still distinguishing between divine purpose and human moral status. Eastern Orthodoxy, with its developed theology of the passions, would read Samson's escalation as an instance of the passions overtaking the will — a tragedy of moral psychology more than of divine instrumentality. The Jewish tradition (*Sotah* 10a) treats Samson's revenge as morally compromised but acknowledges its role in the broader history of Israel's struggle with the Philistines. Critical scholarship (Niditch, Mobley) treats the revenge cycle as folkloric inevitability — the trickster-hero genre demands escalating violence — and treats the deuteronomistic moral framework as later overlay. The Mennonite and pacifist Christian traditions read the chapter as a critical case study in why personal vengeance must be renounced even when it is divinely instrumentalized. Where this leaves us: the chapter wants us to see both that Samson's revenge accomplishes divine deliverance and that the mode of his deliverance falls short of the moral standards that the Hebrew Bible itself sets — and the unresolved tension between these is the chapter's theological signature.

Steelmanning the position I find least intuitive — the patristic allegorical reading in which the jawbone becomes apostolic preaching — requires recognizing that the patristic readers were not engaged in escapism but in an effort to find moral and theological purchase in a narrative whose surface ethics they found difficult. The allegory was a way of treating the canonical text as polyvalent without rejecting it. The jawbone-as-apostolic-preaching reading captures something true about the broader biblical pattern that God's deliverance comes through unexpected and unimpressive means (cf. Paul's "we have this treasure in jars of clay," 2 Corinthians 4:7). Where this leaves us: the allegorical reading is not exegetically defensible as a reading of the literal sense, but it preserves a theological insight (divine deliverance through unimpressive instruments) that the literal-historical reading by itself does not yield.

The second tension is the spring at En-hakkore and the question of divine response to Samson's prayer. Samson's prayer at 15:18 is one of the chapter's most arresting moments: after the great victory, he prays to God in fear of dying of thirst and falling into the hands of the uncircumcised. The prayer is theologically remarkable — it acknowledges that the deliverance was God's ("you have granted this great salvation by the hand of your servant") and pleads for the personal completion of the divine work. God's response is immediate and concrete: a spring opens at Lehi. Catholic sacramental theology and the Eastern Orthodox tradition have read En-hakkore as a type of the Eucharist or of baptism — the divine response that sustains the soul after spiritual battle. Reformed theology has read it as evidence that even the morally compromised servant can be heard in genuine prayer. The Jewish tradition (Talmud *Sotah* 10a) reads the spring as a clear divine endorsement of Samson's role despite his moral failures, while the rabbinic ambivalence about Samson's character keeps the endorsement qualified. Critical scholarship treats the spring etiology as an originally separate folkloric tradition incorporated into the chapter — a local water source whose origin myth was attached to the Samson cycle in the redactional process. The skeptical reading does not necessarily diminish the theological force: the chapter, as we have it, presents the spring as God's response to Samson's prayer, and the text wants us to take that response seriously. Where this leaves us: the chapter holds two things together — the moral compromise of the servant and the genuine divine response to his prayer — and asks the reader whether such a combination can be theologically coherent.

The unresolved question that lands like a punch: when the Spirit of the LORD rushed upon Samson at Lehi and he killed a thousand men with the jawbone of a donkey, was he the fulfillment of Israel's calling — or its grotesque parody?

## The Thread

When you reach 1 Samuel 17, where David kills Goliath with a sling and a stone, remember Samson at Lehi with the jawbone — the Hebrew Bible loves the improvised weapon as a sign of divine deliverance, and the pattern is older than David. When you reach 2 Samuel 23:11–12, where Shammah son of Agee defends a plot of lentils alone against the Philistines, remember the agricultural focus of Samson's revenge — wheat and olives are not just food but the Israelite economic base under foreign pressure. When you reach Hebrews 11:32 listing Samson among the faithful, remember the chapter where he killed a thousand men in revenge for a wife who was burned to death because of his actions. And when you reach the Sermon on the Mount — "you have heard that it was said, an eye for an eye, but I say to you, do not resist the one who is evil" — remember Samson at Etam saying "as they did to me, so I have done to them," and ask whether the long arc of biblical theology has been moving away from the talion principle since the moment it was formally established.
