# Matthew 20: Equal Wages, the Cup, and the Two Blind Men of Jericho

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

To read Matthew 20 historically is to feel the rough texture of first-century Galilean and Judean labor markets, of imperial road-discipline along the pilgrim route to Jerusalem, and of the political theology embedded in the demand for prime seats in a coming kingdom.

The **vineyard parable** opens with a *oikodespotēs* — a "master of a house," a landowner — going out **early in the morning** (literally "at the first hour," meaning at sunrise) to hire **day laborers** in the marketplace. The institution Jesus invokes is well-attested in the rabbinic and Greco-Roman record. **m. Bava Metzia 7** lays out the legal framework for the *po'el* (פּוֹעֵל), the day laborer hired for one day's wage, with strict rules about prompt payment ("**you shall not oppress your neighbor or rob him; the wages of a hired worker shall not remain with you all night until the morning**" — Lev 19:13, with parallel at Deut 24:14-15). The marketplace where workers waited at sunrise — and at the third, sixth, ninth, and eleventh hours — was called the *agora* in the Greek city, the *suq* in Aramaic-speaking towns, and the existence of such hiring stations is attested both archaeologically (the standing-pavements of central agorae in Sepphoris, Tiberias, Caesarea) and literarily.

A **denarius** (Greek *dēnarion*, Latin *denarius*) was the standard daily wage of an unskilled rural laborer in first-century Palestine. **Tacitus** (*Annales* 1.17) records the same rate as standard for Roman legionnaires; **Cicero** (*Pro Roscio* 28) treats it as standard agricultural pay. A first-century vineyard during the harvest crush was a high-pressure work environment — grapes had to be picked and pressed within days of ripeness — and Galilean and Judean landowners frequently scrambled to recruit additional labor. The narrative detail of the master returning to the marketplace at four additional hours during one day reflects exactly this kind of harvest-pressure recruitment.

A **scorching heat** at the third quarter of the day — the *kausōn* (καύσων) of v. 12 — is a real Mediterranean phenomenon, the dry hot wind off the eastern desert that can push afternoon temperatures over 40°C. The grumbling laborers' complaint that they have "**borne the burden of the day and the scorching heat**" is grounded in actual climatic conditions. **Joachim Jeremias** in *The Parables of Jesus* and **Klyne Snodgrass** in *Stories with Intent* both treat the parable's economic and climatic realism carefully.

The chapter's geography then turns to the **Jericho road**. Jericho — the lowest city on earth, at -258 meters below sea level, an ancient oasis city that **Herod the Great** had rebuilt as a winter palace city beginning around 35 BCE — sat at the foot of the climb to Jerusalem. The Roman road from Jericho up to Jerusalem rose 1,000 meters over roughly 27 kilometers, a steep, switchback route through the **Wadi Qelt** desert that the Mishnah (m. Bikkurim 3) describes as the standard pilgrim path. The route was famously dangerous — the parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:30) presupposes its bandits — and the period between Jericho and the walls of Jerusalem was where festival pilgrims either gathered into safer caravans or pressed forward despite the risk. **Matthew 20:29** ("**as they went out of Jericho**") puts Jesus and his disciples on this road in the run-up to Passover, in a stream of pilgrims, with all the political-religious electricity of a Galilean prophet ascending to the festival city under the Roman procurator's eye.

A few Greek terms anchor the chapter. **dēnarion** (δηνάριον) is the Latin loan-word for the daily denarius. **misthos** (μισθός, "wage") is one of the chapter's load-bearing terms. **lytron** (λύτρον, "ransom") is the famous noun in Jesus's saying at 20:28 — "**a ransom for many**" — and is the technical Septuagint term for the redemption-price paid to free a slave or to ransom a forfeit life (Lev 25:51-52, Exod 21:30, Num 18:15). **diakonos** (διάκονος, "servant") and **doulos** (δοῦλος, "slave") are the two terms Jesus uses to describe what greatness looks like among his disciples — and the social distance between them is real: a *diakonos* was a household servant, often free; a *doulos* was a chattel slave with no civil standing. **hyios Dauid** (υἱὸς Δαυίδ, "Son of David") is the messianic title the two blind men cry out at 20:30-31, which in this chapter receives its first sustained public use just before the Triumphal Entry.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

In Bacon's five-discourse architecture, Matthew 20 sits in the long narrative bridge between the Community Discourse (chapter 18) and the Olivet Discourse (chapters 24-25). The chapter contains four units: the vineyard parable (20:1-16), the third passion prediction (20:17-19), the request of the mother of the sons of Zebedee (20:20-28), and the healing of the two blind men at Jericho (20:29-34).

**20:1-16** — the **parable of the vineyard workers** — is **uniquely Matthean**, with no parallel in Mark or Luke. It is bracketed at front and back by the same logion: 19:30's "**many who are first will be last, and the last first**" and 20:16's "**so the last will be first, and the first last**" — a Matthean *inclusio* that frames the parable as the illustration of the saying. **Davies/Allison ICC** treat the source-critical question carefully and judge the parable as M material, possibly Matthean composition with dominical core, possibly pre-Matthean tradition. The chapter division at 19/20 obscures the literary unity: 19:30 + 20:1-16 + 20:16 is a single rhetorical unit.

**20:17-19** — the **third passion prediction** — parallels Mark 10:32-34 and Luke 18:31-34. Matthew's version is the most detailed of the three, naming the **delivery to the Gentiles** (which neither the first prediction at 16:21 nor the second at 17:22-23 had specified) and the **flogging and crucifixion**. The progressive intensification of the passion predictions across Matthew is a major literary feature; **France NICNT** treats it as deliberately preparatory for the Passion narrative.

**20:20-28** — the **request of the mother of the sons of Zebedee** and Jesus's response — parallels Mark 10:35-45, with one striking Matthean modification: in Mark, **James and John themselves** make the request; in Matthew, their **mother** is the petitioner. **Davies/Allison** treat this as a Matthean redaction softening the apostolic-shame of the request — perhaps deferring to the developing reverence for James and John in the post-apostolic generation. (Mark 15:40 and 16:1 identify a "Salome" among the women at the cross and tomb, traditionally identified — perhaps including by Matthew — as the mother of James and John; Matthew 27:56 has "**the mother of the sons of Zebedee**" in the same position, suggesting Matthean editorial consistency.) The Markan version is generally regarded as more original; the Matthean version exhibits the redactional "doubling" that characterizes other Matthean modifications.

The **ransom saying** at 20:28 — "**the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many**" — parallels Mark 10:45 word for word in the Greek. This is one of the most theologically important sayings in the synoptic record. **Joachim Jeremias** argued for its authentic dominical origin and its Aramaic background; **Bultmann** read it as a post-Easter community formation. The "**many**" (*pollōn*, πολλῶν) is widely read as a Semitic idiom for "all" rather than a strict numerical limitation — drawing on **Isaiah 53:11-12** ("**by his knowledge shall the righteous one, my servant, make many to be accounted righteous**" / "**he bore the sin of many**"), which is the major Old Testament substrate. **Morna Hooker's** *Jesus and the Servant* and **Peter Stuhlmacher's** work on Isaiah 53 in NT theology are essential here.

**20:29-34** — the **healing of the blind men at Jericho** — parallels Mark 10:46-52 (where there is one blind man, named Bartimaeus son of Timaeus) and Luke 18:35-43 (where there is one blind man, unnamed, healed *before* Jericho rather than after). Matthew has **two** blind men, no name given, healed *as they leave* Jericho. The Matthean **doubling** is one of the gospel's well-attested editorial habits — also seen at Matt 8:28-34 (two demoniacs at the Gadarenes vs. Mark's one) and Matt 9:27-31 (two blind men at Galilee). **Davies/Allison** treat the doubling pattern carefully; **France NICNT** suggests Matthew may be combining the Bartimaeus tradition with another local-Jericho healing tradition. **Hagner WBC** notes that the doubling may also reflect the legal-witness principle of Deut 19:15 — that two witnesses establish a matter — embedded narratively.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The reception history of Matthew 20 turns on three texts: the vineyard parable's economic theology, the ransom saying's atonement theology, and the seats-at-the-right-and-left's ecclesiology of leadership.

The **vineyard parable** has been read variously across the tradition. **Patristic** readers — **Origen** (*Commentary on Matthew* 15.32-37), **Chrysostom** (*Homily 64 on Matthew*), **Augustine** (*Sermons 87, 88*) — frequently allegorized the five hires (first hour, third, sixth, ninth, eleventh) as the five ages of humanity from Adam to Christ, with the eleventh-hour workers being the Gentiles called late and yet receiving the same wage. **Aquinas's** *Catena Aurea* records this allegorical tradition. **Calvin** in his *Harmony* explicitly rejects the five-ages allegory as overreading: the parable is a single image, the equal denarius is divine sovereignty in grace, and the laborers' grumbling is the standing temptation of the religiously diligent.

The **Reformation** read the parable as a foundational text of free grace against any economic-meritocratic conception of salvation. **Luther's** sermons on the parable (Septuagesima Sunday in the historic lectionary) make this explicit: God's giving is sheer gift; the equal wage is justification by grace alone; the grumbling of the all-day workers is the Pharisaic-religious temptation that always shadows works-righteousness. **Calvin's** treatment in the *Harmony* makes the same point. **Bonhoeffer** in *Cost of Discipleship* uses the parable as illustration of the gospel's refusal of any quantitative-merit calculus.

**Joachim Jeremias** in *The Parables of Jesus* offered the now-classical historical-critical reading: the parable is a defense of Jesus's table fellowship with sinners against the Pharisees' grumbling. The "eleventh-hour workers" are the *am ha'aretz* — the "people of the land," the religiously observant-failing population — and the parable defends Jesus's gospel of grace against rabbinic strictness. **Klyne Snodgrass** in *Stories with Intent* extends this reading carefully.

**Amy-Jill Levine** in *Short Stories by Jesus* offers a pointed counter-reading. Levine argues that the parable does not contrast Christianity with Judaism (a foil that has long deformed Christian preaching) but offers a Jewish reading of God's economy of generosity that fits squarely within rabbinic-prophetic tradition. Set against the realities of first-century day labor — workers hired at sunrise for a single day's denarius, with no security of further employment — the parable, she notes, is *also* readable as describing a **just living wage**: the eleventh-hour workers receive a full denarius because a full denarius is what a household needed to eat, not because they had earned twelve hours' worth of grapes. The economic realism of the day-labor market, attested in m. Bava Metzia 7 and elsewhere, gives the parable a hard social texture that the spiritualizing patristic readings tend to smooth over.

The **ransom saying** at 20:28 has been the central text of Christian atonement theology. The major positions:

The **classic ransom theory**, dominant in patristic theology — **Origen**, **Gregory of Nyssa**, **Athanasius**, **Augustine** in some moods — held that Christ's life was paid as a ransom to the **devil**, who had acquired humanity in the Fall. Origen's *Commentary on Matthew* 16.8 develops the version most influentially. **Gustaf Aulén's** *Christus Victor* (1931) revived this trajectory, reformulating it as **Christus Victor** atonement: not a transaction with the devil, but Christ's victory over the powers of sin, death, and hell. The strong version is dramatic: the cross is the climactic moment of cosmic conflict in which Christ defeats the devil through apparent defeat.

The **satisfaction theory**, articulated by **Anselm** in *Cur Deus Homo* (1098), argued that Christ's death satisfies the divine honor offended by sin — a transaction that need not be with the devil but is rendered to the divine justice. The Reformation modified this into a **penal substitution** reading: Christ bears the penalty of sin in our place, the wrath of God is propitiated, and "**a ransom for many**" is an exchange. **Calvin's** *Institutes* II.16 develops this. **Charles Hodge**, **Louis Berkhof**, and the modern Reformed tradition continue it.

The **moral-influence reading**, articulated by **Peter Abelard** and revived in nineteenth-century liberal theology, treats the cross as the supreme exhibition of divine love that moves us to repentance — a reading that is generally regarded as *part* of the truth (the cross does so move us) but inadequate as the whole. Steel-manned, this reading insists that any transactional theology of the cross misreads its grammar of love.

The **N.T. Wright** reading, in *Jesus and the Victory of God* and *The Day the Revolution Began*, integrates Christus Victor, vicarious-Israel theology, and a covenantal-faithfulness reading: Jesus is the faithful Israelite — Israel's Messiah — who absorbs Israel's exile-curse and Adam's death-sentence so that the covenant can be renewed, the powers (sin, death, the idols, the empires) can be defeated, and the new creation can begin. The ransom saying, on Wright's reading, is shorthand for this whole drama: Jesus the Servant of Isaiah 53 giving his life "for many," not as a payment to an offended deity nor to the devil, but as the act by which heaven's rule is established on earth. Wright also stresses that 20:25-28 makes this cruciform pattern the constitution of the kingdom-community: the church that follows the ransoming Servant cannot be organized like the empires it replaces.

The **Isaiah 53 substrate** is the controlling Old Testament text for any reading of "**a ransom for many**." **Morna Hooker's** *Jesus and the Servant* and **Peter Stuhlmacher's** *Reconciliation, Law, and Righteousness* are the major modern treatments. The "many" (*rabbim*, *pollōn*) is the technical Isaiah 53 term — not a numerical limit but a Semitic idiom for the multitude.

The **seats at the right and left** has been the central NT text on leadership-as-service. **Patristic** and **medieval** readings — **Chrysostom** *Homily 65*, **Augustine**, **Bernard of Clairvaux** — develop the contrast between **dominium** (Gentile-imperial lordship) and **diakonia** (Christ-pattern service). **Aquinas** in *Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 184 on the religious life draws on this. **Pope Gregory the Great's** title *Servus Servorum Dei* — "servant of the servants of God" — is a deliberate appropriation of the Matthean-Markan logion as papal self-description. **Joseph Ratzinger / Benedict XVI**'s *Called to Communion* and his Petrine commentary in *Jesus of Nazareth* return to the same logion.

The **Reformation** read the passage as fundamentally undermining hierarchical-monarchical ecclesiology. **Luther** in *Babylonian Captivity* and *Address to the Christian Nobility*, and **Calvin** in *Institutes* IV.7, use Matthew 20:25-28 as a constitutional text against papal-imperial pretension. **Bonhoeffer's** *Life Together* (*Gemeinsames Leben*) is the modern devotional reading.

The **Jewish-historical** reading is again illuminating. **Geza Vermes** in *The Religion of Jesus the Jew* situates the servant-saying within the broader Jewish prophetic-suffering tradition. **Levine** consistently warns against any reading that uses Jesus's "**not so among you**" to caricature Jewish or Roman leadership while ignoring the same temptations within Christian institutions.

The major modern critical commentaries are essential. **Davies/Allison ICC**; **Luz Hermeneia**; **France NICNT**; **Keener NICNT**; **Hagner WBC**; **Nolland NIGTC**; **Harrington Sacra Pagina**. **Snodgrass's** *Stories with Intent* and **Jeremias's** *Parables of Jesus* are the major parable studies. **Levine's** *Short Stories by Jesus* is essential for the Jewish-historical reading. **Wright's** *Jesus and the Victory of God* and *The Day the Revolution Began* are essential for the ransom saying. **Aulén's** *Christus Victor* is the classical Christus Victor study.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: How does grace meet justice in the vineyard parable?**

The parable's irreducible problem is that the all-day workers' complaint is, on the face of it, just. They have worked twelve hours in the scorching heat. The eleventh-hour workers have worked one. To pay both the same denarius is — as a meritocratic principle — manifestly unfair. The master's reply, "**am I not allowed to do what I choose with what belongs to me? Or do you begrudge my generosity?**" does not deny the inequity; it acknowledges that the master is doing what he chooses with what is his. Steel-manned positions:

The **free-grace reading** (Luther, Calvin, the Reformation tradition) holds that the parable is the gospel: salvation is not earned, the kingdom is gift, and any grumbling at the late-comer's reception is the fundamental religious sin — the elder-brother sin of Luke 15. Steel-manned, this reading insists that any reading that softens the parable's offense to merit-thinking has lost the gospel.

The **just-living-wage reading** (Levine, and in a different key the social-historical commentators) holds that the parable is *also* about a real economic situation: all the workers needed a full denarius to feed their families. The eleventh-hour workers were not "lazy" or "late"; they had been waiting all day for hire. The master's "**why do you stand here idle all day?**" gets the answer "**because no one has hired us**" — and the parable presents the late-hired not as undeserving but as the unemployed of an unforgiving labor market. The denarius given to all is a subsistence wage, not a meritocratic-equal-effort wage. Steel-manned, this reading insists that Christian readers who read past the labor conditions are reading from the position of the all-day workers — the comfortable religiously-diligent — and missing the hard social texture the parable's first hearers would have felt.

The **eschatological-equity reading** (the patristic five-ages allegory in modified form) holds that the parable is about the equal share of all the elect in the eschatological kingdom regardless of when they were called. The first-called Israel and the late-called Gentiles, the early martyr and the deathbed convert, the lifelong monk and the eleventh-hour penitent — all receive the kingdom in equal measure because the kingdom is qualitative, not quantitative. Steel-manned, this reading insists that the parable has explicit eschatological framing (it begins "**the kingdom of heaven is like**") and cannot be flattened into either pure soteriology or pure economic justice.

A fourth, **divine-sovereignty reading** (Reformed theology in stricter form) holds that the parable's whole engine is the master's "**I choose**" — divine election, sovereign grace, the inscrutable freedom of God to give as he wills. Steel-manned, this reading insists that the parable refuses any human-claim on God: there is no claim, only gift.

**Where this leaves us:** all four readings draw real water from the parable. The text supports a reading that simultaneously refuses meritocratic earning (free grace), registers the harsh realities of subsistence labor (just-living-wage), affirms the equal eschatological share of all the called (eschatological equity), and confesses divine sovereignty (election). What the text does not support is the all-day workers' complaint, which is the position of every religiously diligent reader who hears the parable and resents it.

**Tension Two: Greatness as service — and the cup.**

The mother of the Zebedees' sons asks for prime seats — the cabinet positions in the coming kingdom. Jesus replies with two questions and a redirection. First: "**you do not know what you are asking**." Second: "**are you able to drink the cup that I am to drink?**" Then: "**you will drink my cup, but to sit at my right hand and at my left is not mine to grant.**" Then to all twelve: "**it shall not be so among you. But whoever would be great among you must be your servant... even as the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many.**"

The **ecclesial-service reading** — universal in Christian tradition — holds that this passage is the New Testament's constitutional text on Christian leadership. Whatever institutional structures are built (papal, episcopal, presbyterial, congregational), they must answer to "**not so among you.**" Steel-manned in its strongest form, this reading is constitutionally anti-monarchical: Christian leadership is structurally diakonal, and any "lordship" pattern is structurally Gentile, structurally Roman-imperial, structurally pre-gospel.

The **atonement-Christology reading** holds that the chapter's deepest theological weight is on the **lytron** — the ransom-life given for many. The ransom saying is the Synoptic anchor of substitutionary, ransom, and Christus Victor atonement theologies. Steel-manned, this reading insists that any reading of Christian leadership-as-service that detaches itself from the Christological ground (Christ's actual ransom, not just Christ's example) flattens the chapter into a moral lesson and severs it from the gospel.

A **political-ecclesial reading** holds that "**you know that the rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them**" is a real political analysis with a real political alternative; the *ekklēsia* is to be the alternative society in which power is not coercive. Steel-manned, this reading (Yoder's *Politics of Jesus*, Hauerwas, and in his own register Wright) insists that Christian institutions that mirror imperial-corporate structures have lost the chapter's witness.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter forbids any Christian institution from organizing itself by Gentile-imperial logic, and forbids any disciple from seeking the seats at the right and left as career advancement. The cup of the Lord — drunk by the Lord himself, drunk also by James (who would be the first apostolic martyr, beheaded by Herod Agrippa I in Acts 12:2) and John (who, on the consensus of patristic tradition, drank a different cup, dying in old age after long service) — is given to those who follow. The seats are not for sale, not even by the most fervent maternal advocacy.

**The unresolved question:** if "**not so among you**" is the constitutional norm, what does Christian institutional power actually look like in practice, in any actual Christian institution, including those that today wear papal tiaras and those that bear elder-board titles and those that publish quarterly reports? The chapter sets a measuring rod that Christian history has rarely lived up to. The unresolved question is whether the *ekklēsia* in any actual century has been able to carry the saying, or whether the ransom-Christology of 20:28 must always do double duty — providing both the example and the forgiveness for failure.

## The Thread

The cross-references for Matthew 20 reach in several canonical directions. **Isaiah 5:1-7** — the song of the vineyard — is the prophetic substrate for the vineyard imagery. **Isaiah 53:11-12** — the Servant who "**bore the sin of many**" — is the controlling Old Testament substrate for the **lytron** saying at 20:28. **Leviticus 25:51-52** and **Exodus 21:30** are the Old Testament substrate for the *lytron* term itself — the ransom-price paid to redeem a slave or a forfeit life. **Jeremiah 25:15** and **Lamentations 4:21** — the cup of YHWH's wrath — are the Old Testament substrate for the cup saying.

Look forward to **Matthew 21:1-11** — the Triumphal Entry — where the "Son of David" cry of the two blind men at Jericho is taken up by the festival crowds at Jerusalem. Look forward to **Matthew 26:36-46** — Gethsemane — where Jesus's "**let this cup pass from me**" is the dark counterpart to "**are you able to drink the cup that I am to drink?**" Look forward to **Matthew 27:38** — the two thieves crucified at Jesus's right and left — which is the dark fulfillment of "**at your right hand and at your left**"; the seats are not occupied by James and John but by two condemned bandits, and the kingdom is inaugurated through the cross they share.

Flag forward to **Acts 12:1-2** — the martyrdom of James, son of Zebedee, beheaded by Herod Agrippa I — where the "**you will drink my cup**" of 20:23 is fulfilled in the first apostolic martyrdom. Flag forward to **1 Peter 5:1-4** — Peter's pastoral instruction that elders shepherd "**not domineering over those in your charge, but being examples to the flock**" — where the "**not so among you**" of 20:25-28 is being lived out as ecclesial constitution in the apostolic generation.
