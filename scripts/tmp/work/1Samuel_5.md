# 1 Samuel 5: The God Who Won the War in His Enemy's House

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter takes us into the Philistine pentapolis — the five-city league of Philistine power along the coastal plain. Three of those cities appear here in succession: **Ashdod**, **Gath**, and **Ekron**. The other two (Ashkelon and Gaza) are not visited, presumably because the lords of the Philistines stop the ark before it reaches them. All three cities have been excavated; all three confirm the broad picture of Philistine material culture in the eleventh century BCE; and one of them — Ekron — has produced perhaps the single most important Philistine inscription ever discovered.

**Ashdod** (Tel Ashdod) was excavated by Moshe Dothan and others from the 1960s through the 1970s. The Iron I-IIA strata revealed a Philistine temple complex with a distinct architectural form — a long hall with two rows of pillars dividing it into nave and side aisles, with a raised platform at the back. Trude and Moshe Dothan in *People of the Sea* documented the Aegean affinities of Philistine cult architecture (parallels with Mycenaean and Cypriot temples), confirming the broader thesis that the Philistines were Sea Peoples whose cultural homeland lay in the Aegean. The Ashdod excavations also produced cult statuettes — including the famous "Ashdoda" figure, a seated female deity probably reflecting the Philistines' Aegean Mother-Goddess inheritance.

**Gath** is identified with **Tell es-Safi**, excavated since 1996 by Aren Maeir of Bar-Ilan University. Maeir's project has documented massive Iron Age fortifications, a destruction layer attributable to Hazael of Damascus's late-ninth-century campaign, and material culture confirming Gath as the largest Philistine city — possibly the seat of the *seranim* (Philistine lords). The Goliath ostracon found at Gath (a sherd with the Philistine name *alwt/wlt*, possibly a cognate of Goliath) provided the first epigraphic confirmation of the kind of name 1 Samuel attributes to Philistine warriors.

**Ekron** is identified with **Tel Miqne**, jointly excavated by Trude Dothan and **Seymour Gitin** from 1981 to 1996. The Ekron excavations are paradigmatic. Three pillared temples have been excavated, confirming the architectural form mentioned in 1 Samuel 5 (and in the Samson narrative of Judges 16). Most spectacularly, in 1996 the excavators recovered the **Ekron Royal Inscription**, a five-line Phoenician-script dedication on a limestone block in a seventh-century temple, naming the city, the king, the king's father, and the goddess to whom the temple was dedicated (*Ptgyh*, possibly Pythogaia). The Ekron Inscription is the first extrabiblical textual confirmation of Philistine royal nomenclature and cultic practice; the names it preserves (*Ikausu* = *Achish*, biblical and royal) confirm the Aegean origin of Philistine elite culture even six centuries after the migration. Gitin's many publications synthesize the material.

The temple of **Dagon** at Ashdod is the chapter's central setting. Dagon is a venerable West Semitic deity attested at Ebla and Mari in the third millennium BCE — long before the Philistines arrived. The Philistines, on settling in Canaan, adopted the local Canaanite-Semitic deity Dagon and gave him pride of place. **Tell Qasile**, excavated by **Amihai Mazar**, produced an Iron I Philistine temple with a clear stratigraphic sequence and architectural form (long hall with two pillars supporting the roof — exactly the architecture Samson collapses in Judges 16). The temple complex Mazar excavated at Qasile is the closest archaeological analogue to the Dagon temple of 1 Samuel 5. The "threshold" mentioned in v. 5 ("the priests of Dagon do not tread on the threshold of Dagon in Ashdod to this day") would have been a raised stone sill at the entrance to the temple cella — well-attested architecturally and ritually in Near Eastern temples. Threshold avoidance as a ritual taboo is documented in the cult of Marduk in Mesopotamia (Walter Burkert and others have noted the cross-cultural pattern).

The plague mentioned in vv. 6, 9, and 12 — Hebrew **עֳפָלִים** (*ofalim*) — has been the subject of an extended medical-historical debate. The Masoretic text reads *ofalim*, but the *Ketib*/*Qere* tradition (the Masoretes' record of variant readings) reads *teḥorim* in the *Qere*, and the LXX renders it variously. Most Jewish translation traditions render "hemorrhoids" or "tumors in the secret parts." The 1611 KJV split the difference with "emerods." Modern medical-historical scholarship — particularly work in the 1960s and 1970s by S. Krauss and later by W. Boecker — has argued that the description (sudden onset, mortality, tumors, association with mice in chapter 6) is most consistent with **bubonic plague**: the *ofalim* are the painful inguinal and axillary lymph node swellings (buboes) characteristic of *Yersinia pestis* infection. The connection with mice, made explicit in chapter 6 (the golden mice as guilt-offering), strengthens the bubonic-plague reading, since rodents are the natural reservoir for the disease. The NEB rendering "hemorrhoids" is probably wrong; "plague-tumors" or "buboes" captures the medical-historical sense. The chapter is thus, on one reading, the earliest recorded bubonic-plague outbreak in human history.

A few Hebrew terms repay attention. **דָּגוֹן** (*Dagon*) is a venerable name with a contested etymology — possibly from *dagan* (grain, suggesting a fertility deity) or from *dag* (fish, the medieval rabbinic etymology, which gave us the popular "fish-god" image of Dagon). The Eblaite and Mari evidence supports the grain etymology; Dagon was probably a deity of cultivation, agriculture, and storm. **גְּזוּעוֹ** (his "trunk," v. 4) is a striking rare term — the word denotes the stump of a tree cut down (cf. Isaiah 11:1, "a shoot from the stump of Jesse"). The narrator is using a forestry metaphor: Dagon has been reduced to a tree-stump. **כָּבְדָה יַד יְהוָה** (*kavedah yad YHWH*, "the hand of YHWH was heavy," v. 6) plays on the same root *kvd* as the *kavod* (glory) that departed in chapter 4. The glory has departed Israel, but the heaviness — the *kavod* — has come to rest on Philistia. The chapter is about where divine *kavod* lands when it cannot dwell in its proper place.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The literary genre of the chapter is **theological satire** — a mode the Hebrew Bible deploys when narrating divine victory over foreign gods. The closest parallels are in 1 Kings 18 (Elijah versus the prophets of Baal) and the various polemical passages in Isaiah 40-48 (the satire on idol-makers). The ark narrative of 1 Sam 5 plays for theological humor as well as terror. Yahweh has been "captured." His people have been slaughtered. His ark has been deposited as a trophy in the temple of the conquering god. And then, with no human agent, the conquering god falls on his face before the supposedly defeated ark — once, twice, until his hands and head are cut off and only the stump remains. **Polemical humor** is too weak a phrase; the scene is closer to vaudeville. Yahweh defeats Dagon in Dagon's own house, with no Israelite present, no priest, no warrior, no prophet. The chapter's argument is that YHWH's sovereignty is not contingent on the political-military success of his people.

The literary structure is a three-act escalation. First Ashdod: Dagon falls, then is mutilated, then the city is plagued. Second Gath: the city is plagued the moment the ark arrives. Third Ekron: the city is plagued before the ark even fully enters. The pattern is one of accelerating divine response. The Philistine lords' decision-making is also escalated: at Ashdod they convene to decide; at Gath they convene to decide; at Ekron the people preempt them with panic. Bar-Efrat calls this technique "narrative ratcheting" — each scene tightens the noose, with diminishing time between threat and response.

The chapter is also organized around **threshold motifs**. Dagon is decapitated *on the threshold* (v. 4), and an etiology is offered for an actual cult practice ("this is why the priests of Dagon do not tread on the threshold to this day"). Threshold avoidance later appears as a marker of paganism in Zephaniah 1:9 ("on that day I will punish everyone who leaps over the threshold"), where the practice is explicitly condemned. The Samuel narrator preserves the etiology with what reads as wry detachment — it is the narrator's only direct address to the reader in the entire chapter, and it functions as a kind of footnote: *if you have ever wondered why Philistine priests avoid the threshold of the Dagon temple, here is the story*. McCarter notes that this etiological aside is one of the clearest signs that the Ark Narrative comes from the period when the practice was still observable — placing the source firmly in the early monarchy.

The textual situation is, as always in Samuel, complex. The MT of v. 6 is short; the LXX preserves a longer reading: "and mice (or rats) appeared in their land, and there was a deathly tumult in the city." The mice are mentioned only in the LXX of chapter 5 and explicitly only in the MT of chapter 6 (the golden mice of the guilt offering). McCarter, Klein, and most critical commentators accept the longer LXX reading of 5:6, on the grounds that 6:4-5 (the golden mice) presupposes a plague of mice that the MT of chapter 5 does not narrate. 4QSam-a is fragmentary at this point but supports the longer reading where preserved. The implication is significant: the original ark narrative described a plague accompanied by a rodent infestation, which strengthens the bubonic-plague identification.

The literary placement of the chapter within the Ark Narrative is structural. Chapter 4 narrates the loss of the ark; chapter 5 narrates the ark's offensive operation in enemy territory; chapter 6 narrates the ark's return; 2 Samuel 6 narrates the ark's installation in Jerusalem. The whole Ark Narrative is structured as a journey: Shiloh → Aphek/Ebenezer → Ashdod → Gath → Ekron → Beth-shemesh → Kiriath-jearim → (interlude) → Jerusalem. The ark is a wandering protagonist, and the cities it visits are the chapter divisions of the source. Chapter 5 is the wartime episode of the journey: the ark in enemy hands.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish readings.** Josephus (*Antiquities* 6.1) renders the chapter with characteristic embellishment, expanding the Dagon scene and the medical description of the plague. The rabbis (Sanhedrin 105a) treat the chapter as the paradigmatic case of YHWH's self-vindication when his people have failed: Israel is in disgrace, but YHWH himself takes the field. Rashi works through the text closely and notes the threshold etiology. Radak engages the textual variants and offers reasoned guesses about the *ofalim* (he treats them as hemorrhoids, following the medieval medical understanding). Ralbag treats the chapter as a study in providential intervention — natural causes (plague) deployed for theological purposes. **Abarbanel** is again most penetrating: he reads the chapter as the answer to the question chapter 4 raised. Where has the *kavod* gone? It has gone to humiliate Dagon. The departure from Israel was not abandonment but redeployment. For Abarbanel, the political-theological lesson is that divine sovereignty operates by indirection: God's apparent absence from his own people is sometimes the necessary precondition for his presence among the nations as judge. Avivah Zornberg reads the chapter as Yahweh's solo war: a story in which there are no Israelite human characters at all, because the human Israelites have so completely failed that YHWH must now act without them. Yair Zakovitch on the literary structure of the ark narrative is essential. Moshe Garsiel on the wordplay around *kavod* and *yad* is illuminating.

**Christian readings.** The patristic and medieval tradition read the chapter typologically as a figure of the gospel's victory over paganism. Origen treats the falling of Dagon as the fall of all idols before Christ; this typology becomes standard. Augustine in *City of God* 17.5 reads the chapter as a precise figuration of the gospel's progress through the pagan world: Christ enters territories where he is not worshipped, and the local idols fall before him. Chrysostom's homilies on the chapter develop the polemical reading at length: pagans cannot stop the gospel by capturing its symbols. Gregory the Great's *Moralia* uses the chapter as a meditation on apparent divine defeat being the precondition for deeper victory. Aquinas treats the chapter philosophically as a study in providence working through what appears to be its opposite. Calvin's sermons on 1 Sam 5 are characteristically vigorous: the chapter is a refutation of any human attempt to control or manipulate the divine, and a demonstration that YHWH does not require human aid to vindicate himself. Luther on the chapter develops the *theologia crucis* reading: God's strength shows precisely where his weakness seems greatest. Eugene Peterson treats the chapter as a parable about the church's tendency to assume that institutional defeats are theological defeats; they are not. Ellen Davis reads the chapter as a study in divine humor and serious theology coexisting in a single literary moment.

The critical tradition has done extensive work. Rost's source-critical analysis identifies chapter 5 as the central section of the Ark Narrative. **Antony Campbell**'s *The Ark Narrative* and **P. D. Miller and J. J. M. Roberts**'s *The Hand of the Lord* are the two essential English-language monographs. Miller and Roberts argue that the Ark Narrative was originally an apologetic for the Jerusalem cult, designed to prove that YHWH cannot be defeated and that the ark belongs in Jerusalem; chapter 5 is the heart of the apologetic. Brueggemann's commentary reads the chapter as the answer to the chapter-4 question about the relationship between divine sovereignty and political success: YHWH's sovereignty is independent of Israel's military fortunes. McCarter, Klein, and Hertzberg all develop the ark-narrative reading.

**A note on character absence.** The chapter has no Israelite human characters and no women at all — a striking contrast to chapter 4, where the unnamed mother delivered the theological judgment. Brueggemann and Frymer-Kensky both treat the contrast as deliberate: chapter 4 stages the theological catastrophe in the domestic naming-scene, chapter 5 stages the divine response in the public-political theatre of foreign temples and city councils, and the two halves of the Ark Narrative are meant to be read against each other.

Where do the traditions converge? On the chapter as a vindication of divine sovereignty independent of human cooperation. Where do they diverge? On the question of whether the divine action in the chapter is primarily polemical (anti-Dagon, anti-idolatry) or primarily disciplinary (against the Philistines as historical actors). The text supports both readings.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author believe God was doing? Two genuine tensions.

**Tension 1: A God who fights without his people.** The chapter has no Israelite characters at all. The ark sits silent; Dagon falls; the Philistines suffer; the lords of the Philistines deliberate; the cities pass the ark from one to the next. Israel does nothing. Catholic theology has tended to read this as a model of divine providence operating through secondary causes (plague, panic, idol-collapse) without requiring direct human cooperation; the Catholic doctrine of providence is robust enough to accommodate this. Reformed theology has been particularly drawn to the chapter as a demonstration of *sola gratia* — God acts where his people cannot, and the human contribution is reduced to zero. Orthodox theology emphasizes the synergistic dimension less here than usual; even Orthodox readers tend to acknowledge that this is a chapter about divine action without human cooperation. Jewish theology accepts the chapter at face value: when Israel fails, YHWH acts alone, and the action vindicates the covenant by demonstrating that the covenant partner is more than equal to his obligations even when Israel is not. The position I find least intuitive — and which I think is most theologically serious — is the Orthodox concern: a theology in which God does not need human cooperation tends to drift toward a theology in which human cooperation is irrelevant. The chapter walks the line. It does not say the cooperation is irrelevant; it says, this time, it was not forthcoming, and YHWH acted anyway. Where this leaves us: a doctrine of divine sovereignty that does not abolish human responsibility but does demonstrate that responsibility's failure is not the last word.

**Tension 2: The use of plague as divine instrument.** The narrator describes the *ofalim* (plague-tumors) and the rodent infestation (LXX) as *yad YHWH* — the hand of YHWH. The plague is divine action. Catholic theology (Aquinas, Augustine) has historically been comfortable with the language of divine instrumentality through natural causes — plagues, famines, defeats — without crossing into a theology that makes God the author of evil. Reformed theology has held a stronger version of the same: the plague is the act of God, full stop. Orthodox theology resists the strong reading; the plague is a permitted consequence of pagan idolatry, not a direct divine action. Jewish theology, again, accepts the text's plain sense: YHWH afflicts the Philistines, and the affliction is just because they captured his ark and humiliated his glory. The position I find least intuitive — and the one I think the text actually supports — is the Reformed one: the narrator means what he says, the plague is divine action, and any theology of God that preserves divine goodness must do so without softening this. Where this leaves us: a portrait of the divine sovereign as one whose response to humiliation includes the deployment of natural disaster as judicial action — and the question of whether that is a moral problem or a moral necessity is left for the reader.

One unresolved question to leave with: if YHWH could act this freely outside of Israelite political life, why did he ever require Israelite political life at all?

## The Thread

When you get to **1 Samuel 6**, watch how the Philistines return the ark — the chapter you are reading is the setup, and chapter 6 is the conclusion of the unit. When you get to **1 Kings 18**, Elijah at Mount Carmel against the prophets of Baal, remember Dagon: the literary template for "YHWH defeats foreign god in foreign god's place" is laid down here. When you get to **Daniel 5**, Belshazzar's feast, where the Babylonian king uses the temple vessels for a drunken party and is destroyed in a single night, remember the Philistines using the ark as a trophy. The structural parallel is exact: capture the holy, display it for triumph, suffer divine response. And when you get to **Colossians 2:15** ("He disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him") — Paul is using the imagery of Dagon falling on the threshold, even if he never says so. The gospel claim that Christ disarms the powers and humiliates them publicly is built on a theological foundation laid here, in the temple at Ashdod, on the morning the priests of Dagon found their god face down on the floor.
