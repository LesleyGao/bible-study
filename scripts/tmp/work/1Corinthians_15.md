# 1 Corinthians 15 — The Resurrection of the Dead

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Some Corinthians, Paul has been told, are saying that "there is no resurrection of the dead" (v. 12). The denial is almost certainly not a denial of Jesus's resurrection — Paul can take that for granted as common ground in v. 11 — but a denial of the future bodily resurrection of believers. To understand why a young Christian community at Roman Corinth around AD 53–54 would reach for that denial, the relevant context is the Greco-Roman intellectual landscape into which Paul's gospel had been planted.

Three philosophical currents run through mid-first-century Achaia. Platonic dualism treats the body as the prison of the soul (*Phaedo* 81e–82b); salvation consists in the soul's escape to the realm of pure forms. Stoicism teaches that the rational soul is divine fire on loan from the cosmos, returning at death to be reabsorbed into the *logos*. Epicureanism makes the soul a configuration of fine atoms that disperses at death — Epicurus's letter to Menoeceus famously argues that "death is nothing to us." None of these traditions can imagine a redeemed *bodily* future. As Larry Hurtado and N.T. Wright have stressed, Greco-Roman writers from Homer to Plutarch use *anastasis* (ἀνάστασις) of corpses standing up only to ridicule the idea — Acts 17:32 records exactly this reaction at the Areopagus.

Dale Martin's *The Corinthian Body* (1995) reads the Corinthian denial through ancient body theory: the educated Greek mind ranked *pneuma* (spirit) over *psychē* (soul) over *sarx* (flesh), and the goal of salvation was the upward migration of the higher elements out of the lower. Some of the Corinthian elite, who had been over-realizing their eschatology — already reigning, already wise, already rich (cf. 4:8) — had concluded that resurrection had already happened spiritually and that no future *bodily* event was either expected or desirable. 2 Timothy 2:18 attributes a similar position to Hymenaeus and Philetus, who say "the resurrection has already happened." Anthony Thiselton (*NIGTC*, 2000) and David Garland (*BECNT*, 2003) read 1 Corinthians 15 as Paul's frontal assault on this over-realized, body-denigrating spirituality.

The Jewish setting is utterly different. Bodily resurrection was a Pharisaic distinctive. Daniel 12:2 — "many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life and some to shame and everlasting contempt" — became the bedrock text. 2 Maccabees 7 narrates the seven martyred sons declaring their confidence that God will raise their dismembered bodies, and the mother of 4 Maccabees gives a more Hellenized, immortality-of-soul rendering of the same family. Josephus (*Antiquities* 18.14, *Jewish War* 2.163) describes the Pharisees as believing in the resurrection of the righteous; the Sadducees, who confined themselves to the Pentateuch, denied it (Acts 23:8: "the Sadducees say that there is no resurrection, nor angel, nor spirit"). Qumran's *Hodayot* (1QH) and *4Q521* contain resurrection language. The Mishnah (*Sanhedrin* 10:1) lists those who deny the resurrection from the Torah as having no portion in the world to come, and Maimonides codifies belief in resurrection as the thirteenth of his Thirteen Principles. As Paula Fredriksen (*Paul: The Pagan's Apostle*, 2017) and Daniel Boyarin (*A Radical Jew*, 1994) have insisted, Paul thinks as a Pharisee on this question: the resurrection of the body is a Jewish-apocalyptic conviction, and Paul's gospel announces that it has begun in Jesus.

The pre-Pauline creed of 15:3–5 is, on the consensus of critical scholarship, the earliest preserved Christian text. Joachim Jeremias (*The Eucharistic Words of Jesus*, 1966; *New Testament Theology*, 1971) and Martin Hengel (*The Atonement*, 1981) argued from four features that the formula predates Paul's letter by some twenty years and originates in Aramaic-speaking Jerusalem Christianity within five years or less of the crucifixion: the use of "Cephas" (the Aramaic name) rather than the Greek "Peter"; the Semitic parallelism of the four *hoti* clauses (died — buried — raised — appeared); the formula's introduction with technical rabbinic transmission language ("delivered" *paredōka* / "received" *parelabon*, the Greek equivalents of the rabbinic *masar* and *qibbel*); and the high-Christological titles used in a fixed liturgical pattern. Gerd Lüdemann, despite drawing the opposite theological conclusion, agrees that the creed is datable to within two to three years of the crucifixion. This is the closest the historian gets to first-century resurrection belief: a fixed confessional formula in circulation in Jerusalem within months or a small number of years of the events it claims to report. Modern apologetic scholarship — Gary Habermas's "minimal facts" approach, Michael Licona's *The Resurrection of Jesus* (2010), William Lane Craig's work — has built the historical case for resurrection chiefly on this passage.

## Lens 2: Literature and Argument

1 Corinthians 15 is the longest sustained argument in the Pauline corpus on a single doctrinal topic, and it is built with extraordinary rhetorical economy. The chapter divides into four major movements.

**15:1–11 — The gospel as agreed common ground.** Paul reminds the Corinthians of the *euangelion* he preached and they received, in which they stand and by which they are being saved. He cites the pre-Pauline creed (vv. 3–5) and supplements it with an expanded list of resurrection appearances: to Cephas, to the Twelve, to "more than five hundred brothers at one time, most of whom are still alive" (the "go ask them" parenthesis is forensically striking — Paul is staking his case on testable testimony), to James, to all the apostles, and last of all to Paul himself "as to one untimely born" (*ektrōma*, ἔκτρωμα). The verb tense matters: *egēgertai* (ἐγήγερται) in vv. 4, 12, 13, 14, 16, 17, 20 is perfect passive — "has been raised and remains raised." The state continues. The appearances (*ōphthē*, ὤφθη) are described in the divine-passive aorist that the Septuagint uses for theophanies.

**15:12–34 — The consequences of denying resurrection.** Verses 12–19 form a tight reductio ad absurdum: if the dead are not raised, Christ has not been raised; if Christ has not been raised, the apostolic preaching is empty (*kenon*, κενόν), faith is empty, the witnesses are false witnesses against God, those who have died in Christ have perished, and "if in Christ we have hope in this life only, we are of all people most to be pitied" (v. 19). Verses 20–28 reverse the picture: Christ *has* been raised as the *aparchē* (ἀπαρχή, firstfruits), inaugurating an Adam–Christ typology in which Christ undoes Adam's death-bringing trespass and reigns until "the last enemy to be destroyed is death" (v. 26), at which point the Son hands the kingdom to the Father "that God may be all in all" (v. 28). Verses 29–34 turn ad hominem: if there is no resurrection, why are people being baptized "for the dead" (v. 29)? Why does Paul fight with beasts at Ephesus (v. 32, almost certainly metaphorical for hostile opponents)? Why not adopt the Epicurean motto Paul quotes from Isaiah 22:13: "let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die"? The chapter's social-ethical claim is sharpened in v. 33 with the citation of Menander's *Thais*: "bad company ruins good morals." Paul's ethics depend on his eschatology.

**15:35–49 — The nature of the resurrection body.** The Corinthian objection in v. 35 — "How are the dead raised? With what kind of body do they come?" — is the philosophical heart of the chapter. Paul answers with the analogy of the seed (vv. 36–38): the seed sown in the ground "dies" and rises as a different body, given by God. He then catalogs the different glories of different bodies — humans, animals, birds, fish, sun, moon, stars (vv. 39–41) — to establish that "body" (*sōma*, σῶμα) is a flexible category that admits of qualitatively different modes of existence. The four parallel contrasts in vv. 42–44 ("sown in *X*, raised in *Y*") culminate in the most contested phrase in the chapter: "It is sown a *sōma psychikon*, raised a *sōma pneumatikon*" (σῶμα ψυχικόν, σῶμα πνευματικόν). Paul then maps the contrast onto the Adam–Christ typology of vv. 45–49: the first Adam became a *psychē zōsa* (a living being, citing Genesis 2:7 LXX); the last Adam became a *pneuma zōopoioun* (a life-giving spirit). The first man is *ek gēs choikos* (from earth, dusty); the second man is *ex ouranou* (from heaven). "Just as we have borne the image of the man of dust, we shall also bear the image of the man of heaven" (v. 49).

**15:50–58 — The transformation and final victory.** The famous declaration that "flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God" (v. 50) introduces the *mystērion* (μυστήριον) of v. 51: "we shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed" (*allagēsometha*, ἀλλαγησόμεθα), in a moment, *en atomō*, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet. The perishable puts on imperishability (*aphtharsia*, ἀφθαρσία); the mortal puts on immortality. Paul then weaves Isaiah 25:8 ("death is swallowed up in victory") with Hosea 13:14 ("O death, where is your sting?") into an antiphonal taunt-poem against death. The chapter closes (v. 58) not with abstraction but with ethical exhortation: be steadfast, immovable, abounding in the work of the Lord, "knowing that in the Lord your labor is not in vain (*ou kenos*)" — picking up the *kenos* word that ran through vv. 14 and 17 and reversing it.

The Greek vocabulary across the chapter rewards careful attention. *Anastasis* (resurrection, lit. "standing up again"). *Egēgertai* — perfect passive, repeatedly. *Aparchē* — firstfruits, the temple offering of the first sheaf of barley at Passover (Leviticus 23:10–11), now used Christologically. *Sōma psychikon* / *sōma pneumatikon* — the most disputed pair in the chapter; the *–ikos* suffix in Greek typically denotes "characterized by" or "animated by," not "made of," so Wright (*The Resurrection of the Son of God*, 2003) renders these as "soul-animated body" and "Spirit-animated body" rather than "natural" and "spiritual." *Aphtharsia* — incorruptibility. *Allagēsometha* — we shall be changed, the same root as Paul's claim about Moses's veil in 2 Cor 3:18 where believers are "transformed (*metamorphoumetha*) from glory to glory."

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

The chapter sits at a junction in the larger story of God's people where two currents converge and where their later trajectories permanently diverge.

The Jewish current runs from Daniel 12 through Maccabean martyr literature into Pharisaic conviction and rabbinic codification. The Eighteen Benedictions (*Amidah*), recited daily, contain the second blessing praising God who "revives the dead" (*mehayeh ha-metim*) — a formula whose origins probably predate the Christian era. The Pharisaic-rabbinic tradition that became normative Judaism after AD 70 built bodily resurrection into the daily liturgy. Paula Fredriksen and Mark Nanos (the "Paul Within Judaism" movement) emphasize that Paul's argument is not the introduction of a Jewish doctrine to Greeks but the recalibration of a Jewish doctrine around a Messiah who has already been raised. Boyarin (*Border Lines*, 2004) reads the early Christian and rabbinic traditions as siblings, both inheriting the Pharisaic-apocalyptic conviction and developing it in different directions.

The Christian trajectory after Paul has to manage a permanent tension. The *creedal* affirmation of bodily resurrection is fixed by the second century — the Apostles' Creed confesses *carnis resurrectionem* ("the resurrection of the flesh"), and the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed of AD 381 says *prosdokōmen anastasin nekrōn* ("we look for the resurrection of the dead"). But the *philosophical* problem of how to articulate resurrection in a Hellenistic intellectual world produces a multi-century debate. Justin Martyr (*Dial.* 80; *First Apology* 18) and Athenagoras (*De Resurrectione*) argue for bodily resurrection against pagan philosophical objections. Tertullian's *De Resurrectione Carnis* (c. AD 210) is the most forceful patristic defense of the resurrection of the literal flesh — Tertullian's famous insistence that *caro salutis est cardo* ("the flesh is the hinge of salvation") — against Marcionite and Gnostic spiritualizations. Origen (*De Principiis* 2.10), at the other end of the patristic spectrum, argued for a more spiritualized resurrection body, distinct from the present body in continuity but transformed in substance, an *eidos* (form) preserved through transformation. The fourth-century bishop Methodius of Olympus (*De Resurrectione*) wrote a sustained polemic against Origen's view, defending the substantial continuity of the same flesh; this Origen–Methodius debate of the third and fourth centuries set the parameters of patristic discussion.

Augustine's *City of God* book 22 attempts a synthesis: the resurrection body is the same body in the sense of continuous identity, but transformed and glorified — gendered (against those who proposed all would be male), perfected in age (Augustine speculates around thirty), missing nothing essential, possessing the four "dowries" of subtlety, agility, impassibility, and clarity that medieval theology would systematize. Aquinas in the *Summa Theologiae* Supplement (qq. 75–86) develops the medieval scholastic doctrine of the glorified body in conversation with Aristotelian metaphysics. The Reformation continued this confession largely unchanged: Calvin's *Institutes* III.25 affirms bodily resurrection emphatically against any spiritualizing tendency, with explicit reliance on 1 Corinthians 15.

The modern critical period reopens the question. Albert Schweitzer's *The Mystery of the Kingdom of God* (1901) and *The Quest of the Historical Jesus* (1906) read Paul's resurrection theology as an integral piece of an apocalyptic worldview that modern people cannot share. Rudolf Bultmann's program of *Entmythologisierung* (demythologization, 1941) argued that resurrection language, taken literally, belongs to a first-century mythological cosmology and must be translated into existential categories — "the Easter faith of the disciples" rather than an event behind it. Wolfhart Pannenberg, in *Jesus — God and Man* (1968), reverses Bultmann: the resurrection is a historical event, an *anticipation* of the universal end of history that allows God's identity to be confirmed historically; for Pannenberg, the resurrection is the foundation of theology because it is publicly historical. Karl Barth (*Church Dogmatics* IV/1) confessed bodily resurrection as the act of God in history without conceding it to the historian's methods.

The most influential late-twentieth- and early-twenty-first-century treatment is N.T. Wright's *The Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003), the third volume of his *Christian Origins and the Question of God* series. Wright argues, against Lüdemann's subjective-vision hypothesis (*The Resurrection of Jesus*, 1994) and Crossan's metaphorical-apparition hypothesis (*The Birth of Christianity*, 1998), that the early Christian belief in a *bodily* resurrection — and the specific shape that belief took, including the empty tomb, the role of women as first witnesses, the appearances narrated as physically interactive but qualitatively transformed — is best explained by what the texts say happened: the embodied raising of Jesus. Wright's reading of *sōma pneumatikon* — that the *–ikos* suffix denotes the animating principle, not the substance — has reframed the philological discussion. Pheme Perkins (*Resurrection*, 1984), Murray Harris (*Raised Immortal*, 1983), James D.G. Dunn, and Dale Allison (*Resurrecting Jesus*, 2005) have all contributed major treatments; Allison in particular surveys the historical evidence with skeptical sympathy, concluding that something extraordinary lies behind the texts even if its nature cannot be fully recovered.

Two interpretive cruxes deserve flagging. The first is 15:29 — "those who are baptized on behalf of the dead." The Latter-day Saints have built proxy baptism into their temple practice on the strength of this verse. The majority of Christian interpretation has resisted, offering alternatives: that *hyper* (ὑπέρ) means "with reference to" one's own coming death (Tertullian, F.F. Bruce, Wright); baptism over the graves of the dead (Foschini); or Paul citing without endorsing a Corinthian practice he uses ad hominem to expose their inconsistency (Garland, Thiselton, Fee — the majority position). The text is genuinely opaque, and the chapter does not depend on resolving it.

The second crux is the nature of the resurrection body itself. The "spiritual body" (*sōma pneumatikon*) reading splits roughly three ways. The traditional patristic-medieval-Reformation reading insists on substantial continuity — the same matter, transformed and glorified (Tertullian, Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin). The Origen-influenced reading allows for greater discontinuity, a transformed *eidos* or pattern (Origen, parts of the Eastern tradition, modern theological liberalism). The Wright reading — which has gained considerable ground — argues philologically that *pneumatikon* means "animated by the Spirit" rather than "made of spirit-stuff," so that "spiritual body" is in fact a thoroughly *physical*, transformed body, energized by God's Spirit as the present body is energized by *psychē* (the breath/soul of natural human life). The three readings yield meaningfully different eschatological pictures and different anthropologies.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

What is Paul claiming, and what is he refusing?

He is refusing, first, any version of Christianity in which the body is dispensable. The Corinthian over-realized eschatology — the boast that they had already become rich and were already reigning (4:8), the willingness to use the body for sexual immorality on the assumption that "food is for the stomach and the stomach for food, and God will destroy them both" (6:13), and now the denial of bodily resurrection — is one continuous mistake. Paul's gospel insists that what God redeems is the embodied human, not a soul fleeing the body. This is why the chapter ends with ethical exhortation: "be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord" (v. 58). Bodies do work. If bodies are not raised, the work does not last, and the ethics evaporates. The connection between resurrection and ethics is not ornamental; it is structural.

He is claiming, second, that the resurrection of Jesus is the *aparchē* — the first installment of a harvest that the Corinthians belong to. The temple imagery of firstfruits matters: at Passover the priest waved the first sheaf of the barley harvest, and the wave-offering both consecrated and *guaranteed* the rest of the crop. Christ's resurrection is not an isolated marvel but an inaugurating event. Pannenberg's emphasis on *prolepsis* — anticipation — is exegetically faithful here. The end of the world has happened in advance to one human being, and what happened to him publicly will happen to those who belong to him at his coming.

He is claiming, third, that the gospel he preaches is a public, testable, historical claim. The "more than five hundred brothers, most of whom are still alive" in v. 6 is not piety; it is forensic. Paul is staking his case on testimony that could be checked. Joachim Jeremias's argument for the early dating of 15:3–5 is consequential here: the fact that a fixed confessional formula about Christ's death, burial, resurrection, and appearances was already in liturgical circulation in Jerusalem within a small number of years of the events makes the alternative explanations — late legendary embellishment, Hellenistic mystery-religion borrowing, gradual mythologization — historically difficult. Whatever a reader's final theological judgment, the text is not the product of slow accretion. It is an unusually early piece of evidence.

He is claiming, fourth, that the final state is not the abolition of the body but its transformation. *Sōma pneumatikon* is the chapter's pivot. Paul refuses both the Hellenistic disembodied-soul reading (where salvation is the soul's escape) and the over-literal reading (where the resurrection body is simply the present body resuscitated). The picture is of bodily continuity (this body put on imperishability, v. 53) within radical transformation (changed in the twinkling of an eye, v. 52). Wright's philology is helpful but not the only way to honor this dual emphasis; the patristic-medieval tradition with its language of glorification preserves the same balance.

He is claiming, finally, that death is an enemy. This is one of the chapter's most important pastoral notes and one of its most counter-cultural. The Stoic and the Epicurean both, in different ways, taught that death is *not* really an enemy — that the wise person learns to receive it with equanimity. Paul refuses. Death is the *eschatos echthros*, the last enemy, to be destroyed (v. 26). Christian hope is not the hope that death is fine; it is the hope that death will be undone. That refusal of resignation, paired with the grief language Paul uses elsewhere ("we do not grieve as those who have no hope," 1 Thess 4:13 — *grieve*, but not *as those who have no hope*), is the chapter's distinctive pastoral grammar. The mourner is not asked to pretend; the mourner is told that the enemy is real and is doomed.

The unresolved tensions remain. How precisely the resurrection body relates to the present body, what to make of 15:29, how the Adam–Christ typology of vv. 21–22 ("as in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive") relates to debates about universal restoration, and how to weigh Paul's apocalyptic temporal map ("at the last trumpet") against centuries of Christian waiting — all of these remain live questions. But the chapter's central claim is harder to evade than the cruxes it leaves on the table: that something happened to a particular dead body in Jerusalem in the spring of about AD 30, and that what happened to that body is the leading edge of what God intends for the cosmos. "Therefore, my beloved brothers, be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, knowing that in the Lord your labor is not in vain." If Paul is right, that is the only sentence with which an honest reading of the chapter can leave us.
