# 1 Corinthians 11 — Going Deeper

## Historical World

Roman Corinth in the early 50s AD was a Julian colony refounded by Julius Caesar in 44 BC on the ruins of the Greek city Lucius Mummius had razed in 146 BC. By the time Paul wrote from Ephesus around AD 53–54, Corinth had been the capital of the senatorial province of Achaia for nearly a century, hosting the proconsul, the bi-annual Isthmian games, and a transit economy fed by two harbors (Cenchreae east, Lechaion west) connected by the diolkos. The population was a mix of freedmen colonists, descendants of Greek inhabitants who returned, and a large slave population, with a sizable Jewish community (Acts 18:4 mentions the synagogue). Jerome Murphy-O'Connor's *St. Paul's Corinth* documents the inscriptional evidence for a city saturated with patron-client networks, status rivalry, and the boasting culture (*philotimia*) endemic to Roman urban elites.

Two specific social practices frame chapter 11. First, Greco-Roman dining. The wealthy host's *triclinium*, the formal dining room with three couches arrayed around a low table, accommodated nine to twelve persons of higher status. Lower-status clients, freedmen, and slaves were seated in the larger *atrium* and received markedly inferior food and wine. Pliny the Younger, *Letters* 2.6, complains that he attended a dinner where the host served himself and chosen friends "elegant" portions while the rest received "cheap and paltry" fare from a separate jug. Martial's *Epigrams* 3.60 and 4.85 mock the same custom. Gerd Theissen's *The Social Setting of Pauline Christianity* (1982) showed that this is precisely the situation behind 11:17–22: the Lord's Supper at Corinth, hosted in the *domus* of a wealthy patron-believer, replicated the standard Roman ranked meal. The wealthier members ate their *idion deipnon* ("his own meal," 11:21) ahead of those who arrived later (slaves, day laborers who could not leave work until evening), so "one goes hungry, another gets drunk."

Second, head coverings. Roman religious practice required men of standing to cover their heads with the toga pulled forward (*capite velato*) when leading sacrifice — visible on the Augustan Ara Pacis and on countless coins. David W. J. Gill ("The Importance of Roman Portraiture for Head-coverings in 1 Corinthians 11:2–16," *Tyndale Bulletin* 1990) argued this Roman elite custom is the foreground for 11:4 ("every man who prays or prophesies with his head covered dishonors his head"): high-status Corinthian men were carrying pagan religious deportment into Christian worship. For women, Bruce Winter (*After Paul Left Corinth*, 2001) marshals evidence for the "new Roman woman" — wealthy women adopting hairstyles and uncovered-head fashions associated with sexual availability and elite female emancipation, particularly tied to the cult of Isis and Dionysus. Mishnah *Ketubot* 7:6 and *Sotah* 1:5 confirm that for first-century Jewish women uncovering the head in public was scandalous; Numbers 5 prescribes uncovering the head of the suspected adulteress. Antoinette Wire's *The Corinthian Women Prophets* (1990) offers a contested reconstruction along different lines: that charismatic women prophets in Corinth were claiming a new eschatological status that included shedding cultural markers of feminine subordination, with Paul pushing back; the reconstruction has not won general assent, and most commentators (Thiselton, Garland, Fee) treat the disturbance simply as some Corinthian believers — likely including women prophets — abandoning the customary covering, without Wire's fuller social reconstruction.

The Pauline authorship of the letter is uncritically accepted. P46 (c. AD 200) preserves substantial portions of 1 Corinthians; the words of institution in 11:23–26 are textually stable across the manuscript tradition and represent the earliest written account of the Last Supper, predating Mark by roughly fifteen years.

## Literature

The chapter sits at the hinge of the letter's worship section (chapters 11–14). Paul addresses three liturgical disorders in sequence: head coverings (11:2–16), the Lord's Supper (11:17–34), and spiritual gifts (12–14). The structural pivot is verbal. In 11:2 he writes "I commend you" (*epainō*); in 11:17 he reverses, "I do not commend you." Margaret Mitchell's *Paul and the Rhetoric of Reconciliation* (1991) reads the entire letter as deliberative rhetoric urging concord (*homonoia*), and 11 enacts that mode in miniature: praise where possible, censure where necessary, both serving the goal of unified assembly.

The head-covering passage is unusually dense in argument types — appeal to creation order (11:7–9), appeal to angels (11:10), appeal to nature (11:14), appeal to universal church practice (11:16). Paul piles on warrants, which suggests he expects resistance. Lucy Peppiatt (*Women and Worship at Corinth*, 2015) and Philip Payne (*Man and Woman, One in Christ*, 2009) have argued that 11:4–10 contains material Paul is *quoting* from the Corinthian men's position, with 11:11–12 ("Nevertheless, in the Lord woman is not independent of man nor man of woman") functioning as Paul's correction. The argument is unprovable from punctuation alone (Greek manuscripts have no quotation marks) but explains the passage's internal tension between hierarchical and reciprocal language.

The Eucharistic narrative (11:23–26) is framed by formal tradition language — *parelabon* ("I received") and *paredōka* ("I delivered") are technical terms for rabbinic chain-of-tradition transmission (*qabbalah* and *masorah*), the same vocabulary Paul uses in 15:3 for the resurrection kerygma. This signals Paul did not invent these words but received them, almost certainly from Antioch or Jerusalem within two decades of the crucifixion.

The closing unit (11:27–34) intensifies the warning by linking unworthy participation to physical illness and death in the community ("many of you are weak and ill, some have died") — a sober claim few modern interpreters take comfort in.

## The Larger Story

**Jewish trajectory.** The cup over which Jesus pronounced the new covenant (*hē kainē diathēkē*, 11:25) sits in the Passover seder. Joachim Jeremias, *The Eucharistic Words of Jesus* (1949, ET 1966), argued the institution words come from a Passover meal in which the third cup, the *kos shel berakhah* ("cup of blessing," echoed in 10:16), is identified by Jesus with his blood. The Greek of 11:25, *to potērion tēs kainēs diathēkēs en tō emō haimati*, deliberately echoes Exodus 24:8 ("the blood of the covenant") and Jeremiah 31:31 ("a new covenant"). Daniel Boyarin's *A Radical Jew* (1994) and Mark Nanos's *Reading Paul Within Judaism* (2017) press the point that nothing in 11:23–26 requires Paul to have stepped outside the Jewish liturgical world; the Eucharist is initially a *haggadah* of the Christ-event sung within the synagogue's grammar of memorial, covenant, and meal-as-sign. The matzah broken and named *afikoman* in later Jewish tradition is a contested but suggestive parallel for the broken bread; the four cups, the *hallel* psalms, and the eschatological orientation of Passover ("next year in Jerusalem") provide the entire imaginative architecture.

The head-covering material draws on a deeper biblical pattern. In Numbers 5:18 the priest uncovers the head of the suspected adulteress before God. The Sotah ritual encodes the cultural symbol Paul invokes: a married woman's covered head signifies her honor and her husband's; uncovering signals shame or accusation. Qumran's *Rule of the Congregation* (1QSa 2:8–9) excludes those with bodily defects from the eschatological assembly "because the angels of holiness are with their congregation" — a striking parallel to Paul's "because of the angels" in 11:10 and the leading explanation Joseph Fitzmyer (*First Corinthians*, Anchor Yale, 2008) prefers. 1 Enoch 6–15 narrates the Watchers seduced by women, providing another angelic-propriety-at-worship background that C. K. Barrett finds plausible.

**Christian trajectory.** The Eucharistic words generated the longest theological argument in Christian history. Justin Martyr's *First Apology* 65–67 (c. 155) gives the earliest non-canonical description of the rite: bread and water-mixed-wine "over which thanksgiving has been given" become "the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh." Ignatius of Antioch (*Smyrnaeans* 7) calls it "the medicine of immortality." Cyril of Jerusalem (*Mystagogical Catecheses* 4–5, c. 350) speaks of metabolic transformation. Augustine's preaching to the newly baptized (*Sermon* 272) said "you are receiving what you are." Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* III qq. 73–83) systematized transubstantiation using Aristotelian substance-accident metaphysics. The Council of Trent, Session 13 (1551), defined that "by the consecration of the bread and wine, a conversion takes place of the whole substance of the bread into the substance of the body of Christ our Lord, and of the whole substance of the wine into the substance of his blood. This conversion the holy Catholic Church most fittingly calls transubstantiation" (canon 2, with chapters 4–5).

The Reformation fractured this consensus. Luther insisted on real corporeal presence "in, with, and under" the elements (sacramental union, *Small Catechism* 1529; the Marburg Colloquy of October 1529 broke down precisely on this point with Zwingli's memorialism). Calvin's *Institutes* 4.17 articulated a third position: the believer is lifted by the Spirit to feed on the ascended Christ (pneumatic real presence). Anglican formulation in the 39 Articles (28–31) preserved deliberate latitude; Eastern Orthodoxy frames the change through the *epiklesis* (the invocation of the Spirit) using the term *metabolē* rather than transubstantiation. The Lutheran-Catholic dialogue *The Eucharist* (1978), the Anglican-Catholic ARCIC documents, and the Vatican-Reformed dialogues of the late twentieth century have narrowed but not closed the gap. Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification (1999) demonstrated that some Reformation-era anathemas no longer apply; the Eucharistic question proved harder.

The head-covering passage produced a smaller but persistent tradition. Tertullian's *On the Veiling of Virgins* (c. 200) extends the practice. Chrysostom's *Homilies on 1 Corinthians* 26 reads it culturally but morally binding. The Anabaptist and Plymouth Brethren traditions kept literal coverings; the practice survives in conservative Mennonite, Hutterite, and some Reformed Presbyterian congregations. Vatican II's relaxation in 1969 (omitting the previous canonical requirement) ended general Catholic women's veiling, though it survives in Latin Mass communities.

## Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — Head coverings, gender, and worship.** The passage is read in at least three ways.

*Position A — Universal binding gender hierarchy with cultural symbol.* Wayne Grudem (*Evangelical Feminism and Biblical Truth*, 2004) and the broader complementarian tradition read *kephalē* in 11:3 as "authority over." The hierarchy (Christ → man → woman, with the Father over Christ) is creational and trans-cultural. The specific symbol (a fabric covering) is cultural; the reality (visible recognition of male headship in worship) is permanent. The "because of the angels" clause is read as angelic witnesses to right ordering of creation in the church.

*Position B — Cultural-only application.* Most modern complementarians and nearly all egalitarians read the symbol as historically situated. Gordon Fee (NICNT, 1987) treats the head-covering specifics as addressing a Corinthian disturbance — likely women prophets shedding the customary covering as a sign of new-creation freedom — that does not directly bind cultures where head coverings carry no equivalent meaning. The lasting principle is that worship should be conducted in a manner that does not gratuitously offend the gospel's witness to creation and to public propriety.

*Position C — Peppiatt's quotation reading.* Lucy Peppiatt argues 11:4–6 and the hierarchical material in 11:7–9 are quotations of the Corinthian men's position. Paul's own theology emerges in 11:11–12 ("in the Lord woman is not independent of man nor man of woman") and 11:13–15 (with its hint of irony, "judge for yourselves" — an invitation to see the cultural relativity of "nature"). On this reading, Paul is *correcting* a hierarchical Corinthian male position rather than imposing one.

N. T. Wright (*Paul for Everyone: 1 Corinthians*) emphasizes a point common to readings B and C: whatever 11:2–16 does about coverings, it plainly *presupposes* that women pray and prophesy aloud in the assembly (11:5), and so the passage cannot be made into a blanket prohibition of women's vocal ministry. Wright reads the covering instruction as a culturally specific way of marking the difference between male and female in public worship — a difference he takes to be real and creational — without that difference silencing or subordinating the women who are exercising the Spirit's gifts.

The least intuitive position to most modern readers is the strongest universalist version (A). Steelmanned: Paul anchors the argument in creation (11:7–9, citing Genesis 2), not in Corinthian custom; the angelic witness clause invokes the eschatological assembly (1QSa); the appeal to "nature" (11:14) and to universal church practice (11:16) deliberately push beyond local culture. If Paul wanted a culturally local argument, he would have stopped with "this is how Corinthian women dress." That he piles on creation, angels, nature, and ecclesial universality suggests he sees something genuinely permanent here — the visible differentiation of male and female bodies in worship as a sign that redemption does not erase creation. The position has internal coherence whether one accepts it or not.

The translation crisis matters. *Kephalē* in classical Greek can mean "head" in the sense of physical head, or metaphorically "source/origin" (as in the head of a river), or "ruler/authority." LSJ lists both. Stephen Bedale's 1954 article in JTS and Murphy-O'Connor's later work pressed the "source" reading; Grudem's response defended "authority." Cynthia Long Westfall (*Paul and Gender*, 2016) marshals lexical evidence for "prominent/source" and argues "authority" is anachronistic. Whichever direction one lands, the reading of 11:3 changes substantially.

**Tension 2 — Eucharistic presence.** Paul's "this is my body" (*touto mou estin to sōma*) generated a millennium of disagreement.

*Position A — Catholic transubstantiation (Trent).* The substance of bread and wine is converted into the substance of Christ's body and blood; the accidents (taste, appearance, chemistry) remain. This grounds Eucharistic adoration, the reservation of the elements, and the Mass as making present the one sacrifice of Calvary. Aquinas's metaphysical machinery is used to defend, not generate, the doctrine; the doctrine itself rests on the historic teaching that what is received is, in fact, Christ.

*Position B — Lutheran sacramental union and Reformed pneumatic real presence.* Luther agrees with Trent that the believer truly eats Christ's body but rejects the substance-accident framework as philosophically over-specified. Calvin agrees with Luther that the reception is real but locates the reality differently: Christ's body remains in heaven, and the Spirit raises the believer to feed on him. Both reject mere memorialism.

*Position C — Zwinglian memorialism, modernized in much evangelical practice.* The bread and cup are signs that direct the believer's memory and faith to the once-for-all sacrifice. The "is" in "this is my body" is metaphorical, like "I am the door."

*Position D — Eastern Orthodox metabole.* The transformation is real but not specified by Western metaphysics; it is invoked through the *epiklesis* of the Spirit. The mystery is preserved over the explanation.

What did Paul mean? Joachim Jeremias and I. Howard Marshall (*Last Supper and Lord's Supper*, 1980) argue Paul received a tradition that already understood the bread and cup as more than mere symbol but less than philosophically defined transformation. *Anamnesis* (11:24–25, "in remembrance of me") in Jewish liturgical usage is not bare cognition; the Passover *zikkaron* makes the past event present to the participants. Frank Senn (*Christian Liturgy*, 1997) traces how the Pauline text was already being read sacramentally in the second century. The least defensible position historically is bare memorialism — but the most defensible Catholic position is not the most metaphysically detailed one. Steelman the memorialist position: the Greek copula carries no metaphysical weight; the entire Passover frame is dense with symbol-as-memorial; Paul says "until he comes" (11:26), locating Christ's bodily presence in the parousia, not the elements; what gives the meal its weight is the proclamation, not a substance-change.

## Where this leaves us

Chapter 11 forces two kinds of reading at once. The head-covering passage requires us to distinguish what Paul thinks is universal from what he assumes as local custom — a distinction every interpretive tradition has had to make somewhere, and which the chapter itself does not resolve. The Eucharistic passage requires us to ask what *anamnesis* actually does. Both questions are about the relationship between sign and reality, between body and meaning, between what worship signals to "the angels" and what it does in the bodies of those who participate. Dale Martin's *The Corinthian Body* (1995) reads the entire letter as a quarrel about embodiment — whose bodies count, how they are arranged, and what they mean. Chapter 11 confirms his thesis: a covered or uncovered head is a body-sign; a meal where the rich eat first is a body-arrangement; a bread broken and named is a body-claim. Paul does not allow worship to be disembodied.

## One unresolved question

If the institution words (11:23–26) are the earliest written record of the Last Supper and predate the Synoptic Gospels by fifteen years or more, why does Paul say so little about the historical Jesus elsewhere? The same letter that preserves the Eucharistic chain of tradition almost never quotes Jesus' teachings. The asymmetry — preserving the meal-words verbatim while leaving the Sermon on the Mount unquoted — has not been adequately explained.

## The Thread

Worship is where Corinth's social fractures become visible. The triclinium ranking, the gendered head, the broken bread, the cup of the new covenant — these are bodies in arrangement, and Paul refuses to let any of them be disordered. He reaches for creation, for angels, for the night Jesus was betrayed, for the universal church. The body of Christ in the elements and the body of Christ in the assembly are not two questions but one: what kind of body the church is, and whose body is being broken.
