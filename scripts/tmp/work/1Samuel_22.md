# 1 Samuel 22: Adullam, the Tamarisk, and the *Herem* Inverted

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter spans three landscapes that map onto distinct political ecologies of the late 11th-century Levant. The cave of Adullam sits in the Shephelah (the foothills between the Judean hill country and the coastal plain), traditionally identified with Khirbet esh-Shuweikeh near modern Adullam Forest. Surveys by Yehuda Dagan and others have documented Iron I–II occupation in this zone; the cave system in the limestone escarpment provides defensible refuge with multiple egress points, a topographic fact that makes the choice tactically intelligible. Adullam appears in the city list of Joshua 15:35 and in the Karnak topographical list of Sheshonq I (Shishak), confirming continuity of habitation across the period.

Mizpeh of Moab — David's escape route for his parents — sits across the Dead Sea, plausibly identified with Khirbet el-Medeyineh or another of the Iron Age fortified sites on the Moabite plateau (Bruce Routledge's work on Iron Age Moab is the standard reference). The political move is intelligible only if we hold the Ruth tradition in view: David's great-grandmother Ruth was Moabite, and Moab is plausibly territory where the Bethlehemite line retained kinship ties. That David trusts a Moabite king with his parents' lives, while fleeing an Israelite king, registers the foreign-policy texture of the period — kinship and patronage networks crossed national lines.

Gibeah of Saul (Tell el-Ful, excavated by W. F. Albright in the 1920s and 30s, with subsequent re-evaluation by Patrick Arnold and Israel Finkelstein) is the third site. Albright's Iron I "fortress" identification has been challenged; the site is modest but does show Iron I–II occupation consistent with a Saulide tribal capital. The image of Saul "sitting under the tamarisk on the height with his spear in his hand" (v. 6) is iconographically loaded — the tamarisk (*eshel*) is a tree associated with patriarchal sanctuaries (Abraham at Beersheba, Gen 21:33), and Saul's spear is the recurring symbol of his unstable kingship (the spear he hurls at David in 18:11, 19:10; the spear by his head at 26:7).

Nob, the priestly town that becomes the chapter's slaughterhouse, almost certainly housed displaced Elide priests after Shiloh's destruction (Finkelstein's Iron I burn layer at Khirbet Seilun). The "eighty-five who wore the linen ephod" is a sociologically realistic number for a priestly settlement of perhaps 300–500 total population.

Hebrew terms:

- **מָר נֶפֶשׁ / מָרֵי נֶפֶשׁ** (*mar nefesh* / *marei nefesh*, "bitter of soul," v. 2). The phrase is rare and charged. It echoes Judges 18:25 (the Danites, "bitter of soul," who threaten Micah's family) and signals a class of socially desperate men — the destitute, the indebted, the embittered — who form David's first standing force. McCarter notes the deliberate echo: the Adullam band is the same demographic as Judges' Danite raiders, and the narrator is not flattering them.
- **חֶרֶב** (*cherev*, "sword," v. 19) and **לְפִי־חָרֶב** (*lefi-charev*, "to the mouth of the sword," vv. 18–19). The idiom for total slaughter; it is the same phrase used for the *cherem* warfare against Canaanite cities (Josh 6:21, 8:24).
- **חֵרֶם** (*cherem*, the ban; not used explicitly in the chapter but structurally invoked). Saul fails to *cherem* the Amalekites in ch 15; he completes a *cherem* against the priests of YHWH in ch 22. The omission is the loudest word in the chapter.
- **אֵפוֹד בָּד** (*ephod bad*, "linen ephod," v. 18). The priestly garment, distinguished from the high-priestly ephod with its breastplate; the men slaughtered are functioning priests, not laity.
- **מְצוּדָה** (*metsudah*, "stronghold," vv. 4–5). The same noun appears in 2 Sam 5:7 for the *metsudat tziyon*, the stronghold of Zion that becomes the City of David. The lexical thread is deliberate: David's first stronghold is a Moabite hilltop; his last is Jerusalem.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is a tightly constructed diptych. Verses 1–5 build David's nascent court in exile: family, the bitter and broke, a Moabite alliance, and a prophet (Gad) who issues movement orders. Verses 6–23 destroy Saul's court from within: Saul's paranoid soliloquy, Doeg's testimony, Ahimelech's defense, the Israelite soldiers' refusal, the Edomite's compliance, and Abiathar's escape. The two scenes mirror each other formally — both are court scenes around a leader who must judge — and morally invert each other. David's court grows by gathering the marginal; Saul's court shrinks by killing the holy.

Devices: Saul's speech in vv. 7–8 is a rhetorical masterpiece of paranoia. He addresses his servants as "people of Benjamin," invoking tribal solidarity to imply that David, a Judahite, will reward only Judahites. He poses three rhetorical questions in escalating implausibility (will the son of Jesse give you fields, vineyards, commands?), and concludes with a self-pitying "no one is sorry for me." Polzin reads the speech as a deuteronomistic indictment: Saul has become Samuel's prophecy of the king (8:11–17) — taking, taking, taking — and projects his own logic onto David.

The repeated phrase "the son of Jesse" (*ben Yishai*) — five times in the chapter, never David's name — is a deliberate refusal of recognition. Saul cannot speak David's name; he will not bring himself to it. Alter notes that this is the first occurrence of the patronymic-as-slur pattern that runs through the rest of 1 Samuel and into 2 Sam 20:1 (Sheba's "we have no portion in the son of Jesse").

The Israelite soldiers' refusal in v. 17 is the chapter's moral pivot. The royal guard "would not put out their hand to strike the priests of YHWH" — a passive resistance, the only one in 1–2 Samuel where royal subordinates simply decline a direct order. The narrator does not editorialize, but the structural placement is loud: the army of Israel will not kill the priests of Israel; the king must outsource the deed to a foreigner.

Doeg the Edomite is then the chapter's most disturbing literary figure. He is named four times. He is identified by ethnicity each time. He is the only character in 1 Samuel who acts on a king's order to kill priests, and he does so without recorded speech — Doeg never speaks again after his denunciation in vv. 9–10. Brueggemann calls him "the perfect functionary," and the silence of his violence is more chilling than any speech could be.

The chiastic pairing with 1 Samuel 15 is the chapter's most powerful literary structure, and it is a structure the narrator never points to but the reader cannot miss. In ch 15, Saul is commanded to *cherem* the Amalekites (man, woman, child, infant, ox, donkey, sheep — Deut 25:17–19; 1 Sam 15:3). He fails: spares Agag, spares the best of the livestock, loses the kingdom. In ch 22, Saul executes a *cherem* against Nob: "man and woman, child and infant, ox, donkey and sheep" (v. 19, with the verbal parallel to 15:3 exact). The word list is identical. The target has shifted from the Amalekites — Israel's archetypal enemies — to the priests of YHWH. Polzin and Brueggemann both treat this as the deuteronomistic narrator's most devastating irony: Saul finally completes a *cherem*, and it is against his own people's sanctuary.

Source-critically, the chapter is generally assigned to the History of David's Rise (Rost), but McCarter sees evidence of dtr editorial framing in vv. 6–8 (Saul's tribal-rhetoric speech) and in the climactic v. 19 where the *cherem* echo is too pointed for the source-narrator alone. The Qumran evidence (4QSamᵃ for parts of 1 Sam 22) shows generally MT-aligned text with minor variants — the chapter is textually stable.

The introduction of Gad the prophet in v. 5 is also a literary marker. David, in exile, now has a court prophet; this is the first appearance of Gad, who will reappear at 2 Sam 24 as the prophet who delivers David's punishment for the census. The mention here is brief but structural: David's exile court is being assembled with the same components as a future royal court — warriors, family, prophet, and shortly (when Abiathar arrives in v. 20) a priest with the ephod.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish trajectory.** Josephus (*Ant.* 6.244–268) handles ch 22 with measured disgust at Saul and explicit horror at Doeg. He elaborates the speech of Ahimelech (giving the priest a longer, more rhetorical defense) and underscores Saul's transition into something monstrous. Josephus is also careful to distance the priesthood from David — he stresses that Ahimelech genuinely did not know of the conflict, against any reading that would implicate the priests in rebellion.

Rabbinic tradition reads Doeg as a paradigmatic villain — second only to Ahithophel as the rabbinic exemplar of the Torah scholar gone wrong. In b. Sanhedrin 90a and 106b, Doeg is figured as a learned man whose knowledge becomes a weapon: he is one of the few biblical figures whom the rabbis declare excluded from the World to Come. Several psalms are read in his shadow — Psalm 52 superscription explicitly names "when Doeg the Edomite came and told Saul, 'David has gone to the house of Ahimelech'" — and the rabbis read Psalms 12 and 35 as also responding to Doeg.

Rashi, on v. 18, asks why Doeg succeeded in killing 85 priests when ordinary soldiers refused; he answers that Doeg's heart was already corrupt and the act came naturally. Radak focuses on David's confession in v. 22 ("I have occasioned the death of all the persons of your father's house") — Radak treats this as genuine moral acknowledgment, not just regret, and contrasts it with Saul's incapacity to confess across the surrounding chapters. Ralbag systematizes the chapter as a study in *middah keneged middah* (measure for measure): Saul's failure to honor priestly mediation in ch 13 (offering sacrifice himself) and ch 15 (defying Samuel's word) culminates in his slaughter of the priesthood here.

**Abarbanel** offers the most politically penetrating reading. He treats Saul's tribal-rhetoric speech (vv. 7–8) as the moment Saul becomes a tyrant in the Aristotelian sense — a king ruling for his own faction rather than the whole. The Benjaminite servants are bought with "fields and vineyards" (Saul's own implicit confession that he has been distributing royal patronage along tribal lines). Abarbanel reads the Israelite soldiers' refusal as the people's last residual loyalty to YHWH's order; once Doeg crosses the line, the Saulide regime has lost its legitimacy.

Modern Jewish readers (Zakovitch, Garsiel, Zornberg) read Doeg's foreignness sociologically. Zakovitch notes that the dtr historian places foreigners at moral pivot points throughout Samuel-Kings — Doeg the Edomite, Uriah the Hittite, Naaman the Aramean — sometimes as villains, sometimes as exemplars; the foreigner-as-mirror is a deliberate device. Zornberg dwells on David's confession to Abiathar in v. 22: David's "I knew" (*yadati*) is the chapter's only moral self-recognition, and it is offered to the survivor whose family David has unwittingly destroyed.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic reception focused on Doeg as a type of the betrayer (sometimes paired with Judas) and on the priests of Nob as types of Christian martyrs. Augustine, in the *Enarrationes in Psalmos* (especially on Ps 52), treats Doeg as the slanderer whose tongue is "a sharpened razor"; the psalm becomes a meditation on the violence of speech. Chrysostom uses the Nob massacre in his sermons on injustice as evidence that earthly power, untethered from God, becomes lethal to those nearest it.

Gregory the Great, in *Moralia*, allegorizes the priests' refusal to flee as the perseverance of the faithful under persecution — they came when Saul summoned them because they were innocent and did not anticipate evil. Aquinas (*ST* II-II q. 64) cites the chapter as a case study in unjust killing on royal command and in the duty of subordinates to refuse manifestly unjust orders, anchoring this in the soldiers' refusal in v. 17 — a passage with significant later resonance in just-war and conscientious-objection traditions (Vitoria, Suárez, and ultimately the Nuremberg defense).

**Calvin's sermons on 1 Samuel** treat the Nob massacre as the most chilling moment in the book and refuse all softening. Calvin reads Saul's descent as a warning to magistrates: when a ruler turns against the church, he turns against God; when subordinates obey, they share the guilt. The Geneva sermons on this chapter were preached in a period when Calvin was actively counseling French Protestants under Catholic persecution, and the application is direct. Luther, by contrast, reads David's confession in v. 22 typologically — the suffering Christ acknowledges that the suffering of his people is occasioned by him, and remains responsible.

Critical scholarship: Wellhausen treated the chapter as History of David's Rise material with light dtr framing; McCarter's commentary follows this but emphasizes the Qumran textual stability. Brueggemann reads ch 22 as the book's structural low-point for Saul: "Saul's kingdom is now reduced to the slaughter of those who serve YHWH." Polzin treats vv. 17–19 as the deuteronomistic historian's anticipation of the Babylonian destruction of the temple — Saul prefigures the foreign destroyers because, when he turns against the priests, he becomes functionally foreign. **Baruch Halpern** in *David's Secret Demons* presses the political reading: David's outlaw band, growing at Adullam, is an emerging shadow-state that is now joined by the surviving heir of the displaced Elide priesthood, giving David ritual legitimacy he previously lacked. Halpern's argument — that the chapter is not just tragedy but political consolidation — reframes Abiathar's arrival as the founding moment of David's parallel court.

Contemporary readers: Eugene Peterson (*Leap over a Wall*) reads Adullam as the place where God assembles a community out of failures — the bitter, indebted, distressed — and treats it as the hidden seedbed of the kingdom. Walter Brueggemann emphasizes the soldiers' refusal as scriptural warrant for civil disobedience. John Goldingay, in *Old Testament Theology*, treats the chapter as the limit case of the dtr theology of kingship: even the legitimate king (Saul, anointed by Samuel) can become an enemy of YHWH, and the people retain a right and duty to refuse. The women and children of Nob are massacred in a single clause: the *cherem* formula's grim inventory — "man and woman, child and infant" — is the only acknowledgment the chapter gives that they existed at all, and several commentators have noted how that compression is itself part of the horror.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The complicity of David.** David confesses in v. 22, "I have occasioned (*sabboti*, lit. 'I have caused around') the death of all the persons of your father's house." Is David morally responsible for the Nob massacre, or is the responsibility entirely Saul's?

*Position A (juridical, Augustinian tradition):* Moral responsibility is tied to direct agency. David did not order the killing; Saul did. David's lie was a contributing cause but not a proximate cause; the proximate cause is Saul's order and Doeg's compliance. David's confession is admirable acknowledgment of consequence but not of guilt.

*Position B (rabbinic, especially Radak):* David is genuinely culpable. He saw Doeg at Nob and knew (his own words: "I knew") that disaster would follow. To proceed with the deception while foreseeing the harm is to incur moral responsibility for the foreseeable consequence. The text places the confession in David's mouth precisely because the narrator wants the responsibility acknowledged.

*Position C (literary-theological, Brueggemann/Polzin):* The chapter stages a tragic structure in which moral responsibility is genuinely diffused — Saul, Doeg, David, the system of arbitrary kingship that produces such horrors — and refuses to assign single-agent guilt because the reality is structural. David's confession is true but partial; Saul's guilt is total but does not exhaust the field; Doeg is the proximate agent but a functionary; the institution of monarchy is the deeper culprit. Steelmanned, this is the deuteronomistic theology that *systems* of unrighteous power produce *outcomes* of unrighteous violence, and the prophets (Gad in this chapter, Samuel before, Nathan to come) name this.

*Where this leaves us:* The juridical reading is morally clean but too narrow — the text gives David a confession, not a denial, and to overrule the text is poor exegesis. The rabbinic reading honors David's words but risks reducing tragedy to individual fault. The structural reading is the most theologically demanding because it implicates the reader: monarchy itself, the system the people demanded in ch 8, has produced this. Methodologically I lean toward the third because it accounts for the most data, but it is the hardest to preach.

**Tension 2: The legitimacy of disobedience.** Saul's royal guards refuse to kill the priests of YHWH (v. 17). Is their refusal vindicated by the text? On what grounds?

*Position A (divine command):* The soldiers refuse because they recognize that no human king can command an act in violation of YHWH's prior command. The priests are YHWH's; the king is YHWH's servant; the king may not order the destruction of YHWH's. This is the position implicit in the dtr framing and explicit in Aquinas.

*Position B (moral conscience):* The soldiers refuse on what we would call grounds of conscience — the immediate intuition that killing innocent priests is monstrous, regardless of order. The text does not give them a theological speech; their refusal is silent and immediate. On this reading the refusal is a kind of bodily moral knowledge that runs prior to and deeper than any legal reasoning.

*Position C (institutional loyalty):* The soldiers refuse because they recognize the priests as a parallel institution to which they also owe allegiance. This is not divine command (which would require a prophetic word) nor private conscience (which would be unstable as a category in this period) but a sociological recognition that Israelite soldiers and Israelite priests belong to overlapping orders, and the king cannot weaponize one against the other. Steelmanned, this is the most historically realistic reading of how such a refusal would have been experienced in an Iron II tribal monarchy.

*Where this leaves us:* All three readings cohere with the text's silence about the soldiers' reasoning, and the silence is the point. The narrator wants us to register the refusal without theorizing it. For modern application, the divine-command reading is the most theologically secure but risks fundamentalism; the conscience reading is the most universally accessible but risks anachronism; the institutional reading is the most historically anchored but the least exportable. The chapter is content to leave us with the bare datum: the army of Israel said no.

**Unresolved question:** Why did Ahimelech come to Saul? The priests of Nob were summoned (v. 11) and they came. Did they come because they were innocent and did not fear (Gregory the Great), because they could not refuse a royal summons (Halpern's political reading), because they were testing Saul (no one quite says this but it lurks in the rabbinic readings of Ahimelech's bold defense), or because they did not anticipate the violence? The text says nothing. Their coming is the chapter's unanswered hinge.

## The Thread

Cross-references: **1 Samuel 15:3** (the *cherem* formula Saul fails to execute against Amalek; ch 22's massacre completes the same formula against priests of YHWH); **Judges 18:25** (the "bitter of soul" idiom for the Danite raiders, applied to David's Adullam band — the comparison is unflattering); **Psalm 52** (superscribed to Doeg's denunciation, making the chapter generative of psalmic tradition); **2 Samuel 8:17 and 20:25** (Abiathar's later service as priest in David's Jerusalem, fulfilling the safekeeping promise of 22:23); **1 Kings 2:26–27** (Abiathar's eventual exile by Solomon, completing the prophecy against Eli's house — the Nob massacre fulfilled the curse partially, Solomon's act completes it).

Flag forward: chapter 23's ephod oracle becomes operational because Abiathar arrived in this chapter with the ephod; chapter 24's "anointed" speech by David is deepened by his fresh awareness, post-Nob, of what kings become when they harm those YHWH has set apart; chapter 25's near-massacre of Nabal's household by David is the chapter David's restraint must be read against — having just caused (by his own confession) the slaughter of 85 priests, he is on the verge of slaughtering Nabal's household over an insult; the Abigail intervention saves him from becoming Saul. The bitter logic of 1 Samuel is that David carries Nob with him; the only question is whether he learns from it.
