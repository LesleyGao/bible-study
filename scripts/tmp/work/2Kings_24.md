# 2 Kings 24: The First Deportation

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 24 covers approximately seventeen years of cascading catastrophe: from the **Battle of Carchemish in 605 BCE** that ended Egyptian domination of the Levant, through the **first Babylonian deportation of 597 BCE** that emptied Jerusalem of its political-economic-craft elite, to the accession of Zedekiah and his rebellion that triggers the final destruction of chapter 25. The events are extrabiblically attested at remarkable precision and serve as one of the most secure synchronisms between biblical narrative and Mesopotamian sources.

The political context: in 609 BCE Pharaoh **Necho II** had marched north through Megiddo (where he killed Josiah) to support the remnant of Assyrian resistance against Babylon and the Medes at Harran. Egyptian forces remained in the Levant, with **Carchemish** (Tell Karkemish, on the upper Euphrates at the modern Turkey-Syria border) as their forward base. Necho appointed Eliakim as Jehoiakim (changing his name as a vassalage gesture; cf. 23:34) and demanded heavy tribute. In 605 BCE, **Crown Prince Nebuchadnezzar** — son of Nabopolassar — defeated the Egyptian army at Carchemish and pursued them south through Syria. The **Babylonian Chronicle ABC 5** (the Chronicle of the Early Years of Nebuchadnezzar, BM 21946) records: "in the twenty-first year of Nabopolassar... he gave to Nebuchadnezzar his eldest son the troops, and sent him forth to Carchemish... He crossed the river to go against the Egyptian army which lay in Carchemish... they fought with each other and the Egyptian army withdrew before him. He accomplished their defeat and to non-existence beat them." Nabopolassar died shortly after; Nebuchadnezzar returned to Babylon for accession; the **Neo-Babylonian Empire** was effectively founded in this campaign. Jeremiah 46 is the prophetic counterpart to ABC 5's Carchemish report.

Verse 7 — "the king of Egypt did not come again out of his land, for the king of Babylon had taken all that belonged to the king of Egypt from the Brook of Egypt to the river Euphrates" — is a precise summary of the post-Carchemish geopolitical reality. The "Brook of Egypt" is the **Wadi el-Arish** in northern Sinai, the traditional southern boundary of the Levant. Babylonian dominance from Wadi el-Arish to the Euphrates is the territorial scope ABC 5 documents.

Jehoiakim's three years of vassalage (24:1) before rebellion correspond to roughly 604-601 BCE. The Babylonian Chronicle records Nebuchadnezzar campaigning in "the Hatti-land" (the Levantine corridor) in his accession year and through his fourth year, with Jehoiakim's vassalage attested by tribute lists. **Babylonian Chronicle ABC 5** records that in Nebuchadnezzar's fourth year (601 BCE), he marched against Egypt and "in open battle they fought with each other and inflicted great damage on each other. The king of Akkad turned back to his land." This Babylonian setback at the Egyptian frontier — a draw, possibly even a Babylonian retreat — is almost certainly what emboldened Jehoiakim to revolt: the appearance of Babylonian weakness.

The "**bands of Chaldeans and bands of Syrians and bands of Moabites and bands of Ammonites**" sent against Judah (24:2) reflect Nebuchadnezzar's response: rather than commit the Babylonian field army, he authorized vassal raiding from neighboring territories. The Moabites, Ammonites, and Aramaeans were Babylonian vassals who could be unleashed against a rebellious neighbor.

Jehoiakim's death (24:6) is narrated obliquely — "slept with his fathers." **Jeremiah 22:18-19** preserves a strikingly different report: "they shall not lament for him... he shall be buried with the burial of a donkey, dragged and cast forth beyond the gates of Jerusalem." **Jeremiah 36:30** prophesies that Jehoiakim's body shall be "cast out to the heat by day and to the frost by night" and that "he shall have none to sit upon the throne of David." The Kings author's euphemism conceals what Jeremiah explicitly states: Jehoiakim died in disgrace, possibly assassinated, possibly killed during the Babylonian advance, his body dishonored. This is the most stark canonical tension in the chapter.

The **597 BCE deportation** is the chapter's central event. Nebuchadnezzar's eighth year corresponds to 597 BCE; **Babylonian Chronicle ABC 5** records: "In the seventh year, the month of Kislev, the king of Akkad mustered his troops, marched to Hatti-land, encamped against the city of Judah, and on the second day of the month of Adar he captured the city and seized its king. He appointed in it a king of his own choice, received its heavy tribute, and sent them to Babylon." (Note the chronicle dates the city's fall to the seventh year while 2 Kgs 24:12 dates Jehoiachin's surrender to the eighth year — a discrepancy resolved by recognizing the Babylonian chronicler counts from the accession year while the biblical author counts inclusive of Nebuchadnezzar's first regnal year, a common ancient calendrical difference.) **The 2 Adar date** corresponds to **March 16, 597 BCE** by the Julian calendar — one of the most precisely datable events in the entire Hebrew Bible.

The deportation's scale and composition: 24:14 reports 10,000 captives "all the officials and all the mighty men of valor, all the craftsmen and the smiths"; 24:16 specifies 7,000 fighting men and 1,000 craftsmen and metalworkers (*ḥārāš* and *masgēr*). The numbers are conventionally rounded but the policy is exact: the Babylonians removed the **productive class** — the artisans, soldiers, and political leadership — leaving "the poorest people of the land" (24:14). This is consistent with documented Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian deportation policy: the empire extracted human capital while preserving agricultural productivity. **Bustenay Oded's** *Mass Deportations and Deportees in the Neo-Assyrian Empire* documents the institutional pattern. The deportees included **Ezekiel** (Ezek 1:1-3 dates Ezekiel's call to "the fifth year of the exile of King Jehoiachin"), the **young Daniel** (per Daniel 1:1-6, though the dating is debated), and the families that became the core of the Babylonian Jewish community.

Three Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. *ʿebed* (עֶבֶד), "servant" (24:1), is the technical term for vassal status in ancient Near Eastern political vocabulary. *Wayyignəbû* (וַיִּגָּנְבוּ) — Babylonian raiders — and *wayyiglâ* (וַיֶּגֶל), "he carried away into exile" (24:14, 15), are the institutional vocabulary of imperial deportation. *Ḥārāš ûmasgēr* (חָרָשׁ וּמַסְגֵּר) (24:14, 16), "craftsmen and smiths," is the technical pair for the metalworking and skilled craft class targeted for removal.

Late-monarchic bullae from the **Bullae House** in the City of David and **Eilat Mazar's** Ophel excavations confirm the institutional context: bullae of *yhwkl bn šlmyhw* (Jehucal son of Shelemiah, cf. Jer 37:3; 38:1) and *gdlyhw bn pšḥwr* (cf. Jer 38:1) attest the named officials of Zedekiah's court. The **Lachish Letters** (Lachish Ostraca) — recovered from the Iron Age VI guard tower at Tel Lachish during James Starkey's excavations — date from Zedekiah's reign and provide late-monarchic Hebrew correspondence from the kingdom's last days.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: rapid-pace historical chronicle compressing nearly two decades into thirty-nine verses. The pace contrasts sharply with the leisurely narrative of Josiah's reform in chapter 23 — the literary effect is acceleration toward catastrophe.

The structure: verses 1-7 cover Jehoiakim's submission, rebellion, and death; verses 8-17 cover Jehoiachin's brief reign and the 597 deportation; verses 18-20 introduce Zedekiah and his rebellion. The chapter's literary shape is three accelerating arcs, each shorter than the last: Jehoiakim (eleven years compressed into seven verses), Jehoiachin (three months in ten verses), Zedekiah (eleven years compressed into three verses, with the destruction reserved for chapter 25).

The most significant literary feature is the chapter's theological commentary at vv. 3-4, attributing the catastrophe to "the sins of Manasseh, according to all that he had done, and also for the innocent blood that he had shed." This explicit citation of 2 Kgs 21:10-15 closes the rhetorical loop the dtr opened there: the prophecy of 21:10-15 is the cause; the deportation of 24 is the effect. **Frank Moore Cross** reads vv. 3-4 as Dtr2 redaction — the exilic editor's connecting tissue, retrojecting the Manasseh-theology as the theological structure of the catastrophe. **Steven McKenzie** in *The Trouble with Kings* notes the explicit citation of 21:11-15 ("for the sins of Manasseh") suggests editorial unity across the closing chapters: whoever composed 24:3-4 had 21:10-15 in mind. The dtr is constructing a tight causal-theological narrative.

**Polzin** in *Moses and the Deuteronomist* and **Walsh** in his FOTL commentary note the chapter's deliberate suppression of Jehoiakim's death-narrative. Compare the rich verbal-violence vocabulary of Jeremiah 22:18-19 ("buried with the burial of a donkey") with the Kings author's bland "slept with his fathers" — the Kings author is preserving narrative dignity for the Davidic line even as the dynasty disintegrates. **Brueggemann** reads this as the dtr's restraint: the catastrophe is rendered theologically rather than viscerally, with the king's body protected from explicit dishonor.

The chapter's final verse — "And Zedekiah rebelled against the king of Babylon" (24:20b) — is a hinge: the chapter ends mid-action, with the rebellion as the trigger that the next chapter's siege answers. **Robert Alter** notes the suspended-action ending as one of the chapter's strongest literary devices: the destruction is set in motion but withheld for one more chapter, producing the narrative tension that makes 25's siege account so heavy.

A textual variant: **2 Chronicles 36** parallels this chapter with significant compression and reorganization. The Chronicler eliminates much of the political-military detail and emphasizes the Sabbath-of-the-land theology (36:21, citing Lev 26:34-35) — the seventy years of exile match the unobserved Sabbatical years of the land. **Jeremiah 22, 36, 37, 39, 52** preserve parallel material with substantial overlap and significant variation. The Jeremiah parallels are essential canonical context for the period.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Jewish interpretive trajectory has wrestled most intensely with two questions: how to reconcile the Kings/Jeremiah accounts of Jehoiakim's death, and how to understand the moral structure of corporate punishment for ancestral sin. **Josephus** in *Antiquities* 10.6-7 paraphrases the chapter with elaboration drawing on both Kings and Jeremiah, harmonizing the divergent reports of Jehoiakim's death by suggesting Nebuchadnezzar killed Jehoiakim during the Babylonian advance and ordered the body left unburied. Josephus's narrative is essentially a synthesis of the canonical sources.

**Seder Olam Rabbah** (the early rabbinic chronological compendium) calculates the exile's length and integrates the regnal chronologies; the Seder Olam tradition becomes foundational for medieval Jewish historiography. **b. Sanhedrin 103a** discusses Jehoiakim's death and reconciles Kings/Jeremiah by suggesting the king's body was "thrown" outside Jerusalem after a brief burial.

**Rashi** on 24:6 cites Jeremiah's report as the historical reality the Kings author has dignified. **Radak** elaborates that Jehoiakim's evasive "slept with his fathers" formula is deployed for the Davidic dynasty even where the historical reality contradicts the formula. **Ralbag** engages the moral problem of vv. 3-4 — the destruction is for Manasseh's sins committed three generations earlier — and argues that the principle of corporate covenantal responsibility (Exod 20:5; Deut 5:9) operates over generations precisely because the people continued in Manasseh's sins despite Josiah's reform. **Abarbanel** on 24:3-4 develops this at length: Manasseh's sin was particularly the shedding of innocent blood, which under biblical legal categories cannot be expiated by ordinary atonement (Num 35:33: "blood pollutes the land, and no atonement can be made for the land for the blood that is shed in it, except by the blood of him who shed it"). The land itself is polluted, requiring purgation through the exile.

**Yair Zakovitch** in his Hebrew commentary observes the tight literary connection between 21:10-15 and 24:3-4 as the dtr's most explicit demonstration of prophecy-fulfillment over historical distance — the same vocabulary, the same theological frame, the same architectural argument. **Avivah Zornberg** explores the psychology of the deportation as collective trauma: the productive class removed, the poorest left, the cultural-institutional fabric of Judah dismantled in a single season.

The Christian interpretive tradition has produced the most extensive reflection on the Babylonian captivity, which becomes the Christian Old Testament's central typological resource for theology of suffering, exile, and return. **Origen** in his Homilies on Jeremiah uses the captivity as the type of the soul's exile from divine presence; the return becomes the type of conversion. **Augustine** in *City of God* 18 treats the Babylonian captivity as the historical-theological pivot of Old Testament history: Jerusalem's fall reveals that the earthly city cannot be the city of God; only the heavenly city, anticipated in the church, can fulfill the promise. Augustine's treatment of 587 (and by extension 597) shapes the entire Western Christian theology of history.

**Theodoret of Cyrus** in his *Quaestiones in Reges* (mid-5th century) treats the captivity at length, with sustained engagement with the question of corporate punishment for ancestral sin. Theodoret argues that the principle is just because the descendants share in the ancestors' guilt by continuing in their patterns; the individual righteousness of a Daniel or Ezekiel does not exempt them from sharing in the corporate punishment because they are the people. **Chrysostom** in his Homilies on the Statues uses the captivity extensively in his preaching to Antioch during the imperial crisis of 387 CE; Chrysostom reads the captivity as the type of God's pedagogical use of suffering to produce repentance. **Gregory the Great** in his *Moralia in Job* and his Pastoral Rule treats Babylon as the type of the fallen world from which the soul must be exiled to find its true home in God.

**Aquinas** treats the captivity in the *Summa* (e.g., IIa-IIae q. 12 a. 2) as evidence of legitimate divine punishment of corporate apostasy. **Calvin's sermons on 2 Kings 24** read the chapter as a stark demonstration of how God uses pagan empires as the rod of his discipline against his own people; Calvin's sustained engagement with the chapter (echoed in his Jeremiah commentary) becomes a foundational resource for Reformed theology of providence. **Calvin's** sermons on Jeremiah 25, 27, and the corresponding 2 Kings material were preached extensively in the 1550s and 1560s and are preserved in the Strasbourg edition; they engage at length with the difficulty of accepting that pagan Babylon is the instrument of divine purpose. **Luther** in his lectures on Jeremiah engages the same material with similar conclusions, emphasizing the pedagogical function of suffering. **Both Reformers** wrote extensively on the destruction of Jerusalem; the Reformation produced a vast theological literature on the captivity as the model of exile from which only divine grace can deliver.

Critical scholarship has been dominated by archaeological and chronological synchronism with the Babylonian sources. **Donald Wiseman's** *Chronicles of Chaldaean Kings* (1956) published the Babylonian Chronicle texts that documented Carchemish and the 597 deportation, transforming the historical study of the period. **Mordechai Cogan and Hayim Tadmor** in the AB commentary provide the standard treatment, with detailed engagement of the Babylonian Chronicle synchronisms. **Marvin Sweeney's** OTL commentary engages the redactional layers and the relationship to Jeremiah. **T. R. Hobbs** in the Word commentary takes a more conservative redactional stance. **Cross**, **Nelson**, and **McKenzie** treat vv. 3-4 as Dtr2 expansion. **Iain Provan** engages the moral problem of vv. 3-4 with particular attention to the Jeremiah parallels.

**Oded Lipschits's** *The Fall and Rise of Jerusalem* provides the standard archaeological and demographic synthesis of the deportation period. Lipschits argues the demographic disruption of 597 was substantial but not total — the Negev, Benjamin, and parts of the Shephelah remained inhabited — preparing the picture of the post-587 remnant that the next chapter narrates. **Ephraim Stern's** *Archaeology of the Land of the Bible* volume on the Babylonian and Persian periods documents the material culture transition. **Israel Finkelstein** has been more skeptical about deportation scale, arguing the Kings figures are inflated, but the basic outline is widely accepted.

The chapter's use of foreign nations as instruments of YHWH's wrath (24:2) is a pivotal moment in the biblical theology of war. The queen-mother Nehushta (24:8, 12, 15) is named among the deportees, so the Davidic *gebirah* institution goes into exile alongside the king; the chapter otherwise says nothing of the women carried off. The prose is deliberately flat — the bureaucratic precision of 7,000 men of valor and 1,000 craftsmen — and refuses lament; that work is left to the Psalms (137) and Lamentations.

**Walter Brueggemann** in his *1 & 2 Kings* engages the chapter as the dtr's most precise statement of how covenant violation produces consequences in real political-economic life — not as abstract theology but as concrete demographic disaster. Brueggemann's *Theology of the Old Testament* uses 597 BCE as a paradigmatic moment: the loss of land, monarchy, Temple, productive population. **Ellen Davis** in her work on biblical theology of land has noted the chapter's contrast between the deportation of the productive class and the retention of the agricultural poor — the agrarian social order continued, transformed, with new occupants on old fields.

**Jon Levenson's** *Sinai and Zion* engages the Davidic-Zion theology in tension with the deportation: the chapter records the failure of the Zion theology that promised Jerusalem's inviolability (Pss 46, 48) and forces the post-deportation community to develop new theological resources. The closing chapters of Kings are the working-out of this Zion-theology crisis.

**N. T. Wright** has made the Babylonian exile inaugurated here the structural center of his account of Second-Temple Jewish hope and of Jesus' own mission. In *The New Testament and the People of God* and *Jesus and the Victory of God* he argues that the deportation of 597 (completed in 587) began an "exile" Israel never regarded as truly over — even after the geographic return under Cyrus, the people still lived under foreign domination, the promises of Isaiah 40–55 still unfulfilled, the glory not yet returned to the Temple. On Wright's reading 2 Kings 24–25 opens the long story that the Gospels present Jesus as bringing to its climax: "the exile is over, the kingdom is breaking in." Whatever one makes of the thesis, it has made the chapter's deportation a load-bearing element in a major contemporary reading of the whole canon.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**First tension: corporate punishment for ancestral sin.** The chapter explicitly attributes the deportation to "the sins of Manasseh" (24:3) — a king dead more than fifty years. Three positions on what this means. Position one (covenantal-corporate, traditional Reformed): the people share covenantally in the ancestors' sins not as inheritors of guilt but as continuers of the same patterns; the deportation falls on those who, while not personally responsible for Manasseh's specific acts, have continued the apostasy he established. Steelman: the Manasseh-frame at 24:3-4 is paired with notes on each successor king's own evil (Jehoiakim 23:37, Jehoiachin 24:9, Zedekiah 24:19), showing the dtr's argument is corporate-cumulative not strictly inherited. Position two (redaction-critical, Cross/Nelson): vv. 3-4 are exilic editorial theology, retrojecting onto the historical events a theological frame the events themselves did not require; the historical deportation was geopolitical, not theological. Steelman: the diction of vv. 3-4 reads like later reflection, and the bare historical chronicle would be intelligible without it. Position three (moral-philosophical, Ralbag, Abarbanel, contemporary): the principle of corporate inherited consequence is real because corporate institutions persist across generations — the temple, the dynasty, the political structures — and individuals participate in those structures. The deportation falls on persons who lived in institutions Manasseh's reign had shaped. Steelman: this honors the genuine moral problem while preserving the dtr's claim, by locating responsibility in continued participation rather than direct inheritance.

Where this leaves us: the chapter forces engagement with the moral structure of inherited consequence. The dtr does not treat individuals as guilty of Manasseh's specific deeds but does treat the people as bearing the consequences of corporate institutional patterns Manasseh established. This is the model of corporate covenantal responsibility the Hebrew Bible repeatedly invokes (Exod 20:5; Deut 5:9; Jer 31:29-30 then begins to challenge it). The chapter is a pivotal moment in the biblical theology of guilt and consequence.

**Second tension: the suppression of Jehoiakim's death.** The Kings author writes "Jehoiakim slept with his fathers" (24:6). Jeremiah 22 and 36 record dishonored, unburied disposal. Three positions. Position one (literary-critical, conservative): the dtr is using the Davidic-dynasty formula even where its surface meaning does not fit, preserving narrative dignity for the line. Steelman: the formula is consistently applied to Davidic kings; its formal application here may not be making a claim about peaceful death. Position two (canonical-tension, traditional rabbinic): Jeremiah preserves the historical detail; Kings preserves the institutional formula; both are true at different levels. Steelman: this canonical-comparison reading works without forcing harmonization; the two texts illuminate different dimensions. Position three (theological-suppression): the dtr refuses to record the dishonor because the Davidic line itself, even in its corruption, retains theological significance; Jehoiakim's body must be protected because the dynasty-promise must be protected. Steelman: this preserves the chapter's theological architecture and explains why the dtr would systematically prefer the formula over the report.

Where this leaves us: the chapter's silence about Jehoiakim's death is theologically deliberate. The dtr is engaged in a project of preserving Davidic-dynastic continuity even as he narrates its destruction. This will become the structural problem of the book's closing — what Jehoiachin's release in chapter 25 is for.

**Unresolved question:** Why does the chapter end mid-rebellion? "And Zedekiah rebelled against the king of Babylon" (24:20b) leaves the action suspended for the next chapter. The dtr could have included the destruction here. The narrative architecture of two chapters for the catastrophe — first deportation in 24, final destruction in 25 — is deliberate but its purpose is not stated. Possibly: the dtr is showing that 597 was already catastrophe enough, and the final 587 destruction is its extension rather than its completion. Possibly: the dtr is preserving the Davidic-Davidic interval (Jehoiachin in Babylonian prison, Zedekiah in Jerusalem) as the theological space the book's closing will reopen. The structure suggests these are the right questions but the text does not answer them.

## The Thread

Cross-references that matter. **2 Kgs 21:10-15** is the prophecy fulfilled here at 24:3-4 — the chapter explicitly cites the Manasseh-judgment frame. **Deut 28:36, 49-50** prescribes deportation as the covenant curse for unfaithfulness — the chapter is the curse coming to pass. **Jer 22:18-19** preserves Jehoiakim's dishonored death; **Jer 22:24-30** records Jeremiah's prophecy that Jehoiachin's line would be cut off from the throne — a problem the genealogy of Christ in **Matthew 1** later engages by tracing the Davidic line through Jehoiachin/Jeconiah despite the prophecy. **Jer 25:11-12 and 29:10** prophesy seventy years of exile; **2 Chr 36:21** cites Lev 26:34-35 to interpret the exile as the land's Sabbath rest.

Forward references this chapter sets up: the **siege and destruction in 25:1-21** is the consequence of Zedekiah's rebellion at 24:20b; the **closing of 25:27-30** — Jehoiachin's release in Babylon thirty-seven years later — is the first hopeful note for the deportee community whose deportation is narrated here. **Ezekiel 1:1-3** dates Ezekiel's call by "the fifth year of the exile of King Jehoiachin" — Ezekiel writes among the 597 deportees, providing the prophetic counterpart to the deportation chapter narrates. **Daniel 1:1-6** locates Daniel's deportation in the period 605-597 (the dating depends on regnal-year reckoning); Daniel's life as a Babylonian official is the working-out of one deportee's trajectory. The **Babylonian Chronicle ABC 5** records this chapter's events with greater precision than any other chapter in the Hebrew Bible — the most secure synchronism in Old Testament history-writing.
