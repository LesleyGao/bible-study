# 2 Kings 17: The Fall of Samaria and the Deuteronomist's Great Apologia

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 17 stands at the geopolitical pivot of the entire Hebrew Bible's monarchical history: the **destruction of the Northern Kingdom of Israel by Assyria in 722 BCE** and the deportation that produces what later tradition will mythologize as "the ten lost tribes." The Assyriological dossier is unusually rich and unusually fraught. The siege of Samaria spans two reigns: **Shalmaneser V** (727–722 BCE) initiates it; **Sargon II** (722–705 BCE) completes it. The biblical text in v. 6 names only "the king of Assyria," strategically opaque on identity. Sargon's own **Khorsabad inscriptions** (the Annals, the Display Inscription, the Nimrud Prism) take credit for the conquest in his accession year: *"I besieged and conquered Samaria (Samerina), led away as booty 27,290 inhabitants of it… I rebuilt the city better than it was before and settled there people from countries which I myself had conquered."* The chronological tension — Shalmaneser dying mid-siege and Sargon claiming the conquest — has been worked through carefully by Hayim Tadmor ("The Campaigns of Sargon II of Assur," *JCS* 12, 1958), Mordechai Cogan and Hayim Tadmor (AB *II Kings*, 1988), Bob Becking (*The Fall of Samaria*, 1992), Gershon Galil (*The Chronology of the Kings of Israel and Judah*, 1996), and most recently K. Lawson Younger Jr. (*A Political History of the Arameans*, 2016, with extensive treatment of the Samaria question). The likeliest reconstruction: Shalmaneser took the city in 722 just before his death; Sargon executed the deportation and resettlement in 720 after suppressing a wider Levantine revolt led by Yau-bi'di of Hamath.

The figure of "**So king of Egypt**" (v. 4) is one of the great onomastic puzzles of the chapter. Three candidates dominate: **Tefnakht** of the 24th Dynasty (Sais), **Osorkon IV** of the residual 22nd Dynasty (Tanis), or — Donald Redford's preference — a place-name (Sais itself, *Sa'u*) rather than a personal name. Cogan-Tadmor lean Osorkon IV; Galil leans Tefnakht; the matter is unresolved. What is certain is that Hoshea's pivot toward Egypt was a calamitous misjudgment: late-period Egypt could not project power into the Levant to break an Assyrian siege.

The deportation destinations are precisely identifiable: **Halah** (Akkadian *Ḫalaḫḫu*, north of Nineveh), **the Habor** (modern Khabur river), **Gozan** (Tell Halaf — excavated by Max von Oppenheim from 1899; mid-20th-century cuneiform finds include the **Tell Halaf tablets** with West Semitic onomastica indicating Israelite deportee presence), and **the cities of the Medes** (the Zagros frontier). Bustenay Oded's *Mass Deportations and Deportees in the Neo-Assyrian Empire* (1979) remains the standard study of the demographic engineering involved. Israel Finkelstein and Neil Asher Silberman (*The Bible Unearthed*, 2001) press the demographic point: not all Israelites were deported; large rural populations remained, and significant numbers fled south to Judah, swelling Jerusalem's population — a finding consistent with archaeological surveys showing Jerusalem's late-8th-century expansion under Hezekiah (the **Broad Wall** in the Avigad excavations).

The reverse population movement (vv. 24–41) — settlers from **Babylon, Cuthah, Avvâ, Hamath, and Sepharvaim** — is also documented in Sargon's annals. Cuthah is securely identified with Tell Ibrahim east of Babylon; Sepharvaim is debated (perhaps Sippar, perhaps a Syrian site). The deity-list in vv. 30–31 — **Succoth-benoth, Nergal, Ashima, Nibhaz, Tartak, Adrammelech, Anammelech** — preserves authentic Mesopotamian and West Semitic theonymic data: Nergal is the well-known underworld deity of Cuthah (his cult center); Adrammelech may stand for *Adad-milki* or possibly the Aramean Hadad-Melek; Ashima is attested at Hamath in Aramaic inscriptions and reappears in the late biblical text Amos 8:14 ("the *ashima* of Samaria"). The detail of **child sacrifice to Adrammelech and Anammelech** (v. 31) is one of the few extrabiblical-corroborated references to ANE child sacrifice outside the Phoenician-Punic west.

Three Hebrew terms anchor the historical reading. **גָּלָה** (*gālāh*, "to be exiled"; vv. 6, 23) is the verbal root that will become a master-category for the rest of the Hebrew Bible's theological grammar. **גּוֹיִם** (*gôyîm*, "nations"; vv. 8, 11, 15) — the chapter uses the term programmatically: Israel's sin is precisely to walk in the *ḥuqqôt ha-gôyîm*, "the customs of the nations." **יָרֵא אֶת יְהוָה** (*yārē' 'et YHWH*, "to fear YHWH"; vv. 25–41) — this verb is iterated obsessively in the closing pericope, structuring the indictment of the new Samarian population's syncretism: they "feared YHWH" (in cult-technical sense, observed his rites) yet also served their own gods. The chapter weaponizes the polysemy of *yārē'*.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is bipartite by genre: a compressed regnal narrative (vv. 1–6) followed by the **dtr's most extended theological apologia in the entire Deuteronomistic History** (vv. 7–23), and a closing ethnographic-polemical pericope on the new Samarian community (vv. 24–41). Frank Moore Cross's classic two-edition theory of the dtr (*Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*, 1973) reads vv. 7–23 as the **central justifying sermon of the pre-exilic edition (Dtr1)** — the explanation of why the Northern Kingdom fell, designed to motivate Josianic reform in the southern kingdom. Richard Nelson (*The Double Redaction of the Deuteronomistic History*, 1981) refines the analysis. Martin Noth's foundational 1943 thesis (*Überlieferungsgeschichtliche Studien*) had already identified the chapter as the dtr's theological hinge.

The apologia itself (vv. 7–23) is a masterclass in dtr rhetoric. Its structure is recursive and prosecutorial: **(1) thesis (v. 7a, "this occurred because")**; **(2) historical preamble** invoking the Exodus (v. 7b); **(3) catalog of cultic offenses** (vv. 8–12); **(4) prophetic warning ignored** (vv. 13–14); **(5) full reiteration with theological intensification** (vv. 15–18); **(6) parenthesis on Judah** (vv. 19–20); **(7) the Jeroboam etiology** (vv. 21–23). The catalog of offenses — *high places, pillars, Asherim, Baals, host of heaven, divination and omens, child sacrifice, mediums and necromancers, calves of Jeroboam* — is the dtr's complete bestiary of Israelite apostasy, gathered in one place. Norbert Lohfink, Moshe Weinfeld (*Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School*, 1972), and Hans Walter Wolff identified the characteristic dtr lexicon here in its most concentrated form.

Devices: the chapter exploits **prosecutorial repetition** with relentless force. The phrase "they would not listen" (v. 14) is the dtr's signature verdict (compare Jer 7:25–26, 25:4 — almost certainly drawn from the same theological school); "as their fathers had been" turns generational continuity itself into an indictment. The **Jeroboam etiology** in vv. 21–23 closes the loop with the regnal frame stories of 1 Kings 12 and following — the dtr's relentless refrain "the sins of Jeroboam son of Nebat by which he made Israel to sin" finally bears its full weight here. Robert Polzin (*Moses and the Deuteronomist*, 1980; *Samuel and the Deuteronomist*, 1989) reads the chapter as the dtr's voice at its most exposed and most theologically anxious — the historian explicating his own historiography.

The closing ethnographic pericope (vv. 24–41) shifts genre into something like an **anti-Samaritan polemic**, though scholarly opinion on dating varies sharply. The phrase "**to this day**" (vv. 23, 34, 41) marks editorial layering: the chapter has been updated, perhaps multiple times, for an audience well after 722. The polemical edge ("they fear YHWH and also serve their own gods," v. 33) is foundational for the later Judean–Samaritan rift, but **Reinhard Pummer** (*The Samaritans*, 2016) and **Magnar Kartveit** (*The Origin of the Samaritans*, 2009) have argued forcefully that vv. 24–41 should not be read as an accurate ethnography of the later Samaritans (whose origins lie in the Persian and Hellenistic periods) but as a Judean polemical construction of "northern syncretism." Yitzhak Magen's archaeological work on **Mount Gerizim** (the temple complex constructed in the Persian period, 5th–4th c. BCE) confirms that the historical Samaritans were thoroughly Yahwistic, not the syncretists 2 Kgs 17 caricatures.

**Textual-critical note**: 4QKgs (4Q54), though fragmentary, preserves portions of 2 Kgs 7–9 and a few traces relevant to the wider Kings text. For ch 17 specifically, the LXX preserves some readings older than MT — notably in v. 31, where Greek manuscript variation around the deity names (*Anamelech/Adrammelech*) suggests early scribal uncertainty. The Lucianic recension (the Antiochene text) sometimes preserves valuable variants here. The scholarly view (Tov, Trebolle Barrera, Schenker) is that 2 Kings 17 was textually stable in its core but accumulated scribal expansion in the deity-list and the closing parenetic verses (vv. 34–40, which some treat as a later expansion).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish trajectory.** Josephus (*Ant.* 9.277–291) treats the fall of Samaria as the great moral exemplum of the Northern Kingdom's deserved end, and his account elaborates the Cuthaean settlement (the *Kuthim* of rabbinic literature). The rabbinic tradition is voluminous: **m. Berakhot 7:1**, **m. Demai 3:4**, **m. Sheviit 8:10**, and tractate **Kuthim** (a minor extra-canonical tractate) all wrestle with the legal status of Samaritans — whether they are *gerim 'arayot* ("converts under duress") or genuine Yahwists. **b. Sanhedrin 85b** debates whether Cuthaean conversion was sincere; **b. Yebamot 16a** preserves Rabbi Akiba's stricter view that they remain gentile. **Rashi** on 17:24 emphasizes the lions as theological pedagogy — God teaching the new settlers that the land's deity demands its own worship. **Radak** (Kimḥi) reads vv. 7–23 as a sustained legal indictment, structured to demonstrate that the exile was juridically due — not arbitrary. **Ralbag** (Gersonides) characteristically treats the apologia as a philosophical demonstration that historical causation (deportation) follows from moral causation (apostasy). **Abarbanel**, writing after the Spanish expulsion, finds in the chapter a contemporary trauma: the deportation is for him not historical distance but lived analogue, and his commentary on the prophetic warnings ignored (vv. 13–14) becomes a lament. **Yair Zakovitch** in modern Israeli scholarship has emphasized the chapter's literary artistry — its prosecutorial structure as biblical historiography at its most theologically self-aware. **Avivah Zornberg**, in her psycho-spiritual readings, sees the dtr's catalog of offenses as a kind of grief work, the historian narrating loss by enumerating its causes.

**Christian trajectory.** The patristic interest is dual: the chapter is read both as **historical apologetic for divine judgment** and as **ecclesiological warning** against the church becoming what Israel became. **Origen** (*Homilies on Numbers*, *Commentary on Romans*) treats the deportation as a type of the spiritual exile of those who abandon the law. **Augustine** (*City of God* 17.7, 18.27) reads the chapter as evidence that the visible kingdom of Israel has been removed precisely so that the invisible *civitas Dei* can be recognized as the true continuity. **Theodoret of Cyrus**'s *Quaestiones in Reges* gives detailed historical commentary, identifying the Mesopotamian deities and reading the dtr's apologia as theology done in the prosecutorial mode. **Chrysostom** preached on the chapter as warning to Constantinople: the great city, like Samaria, can fall when its people forget the God who delivered them. **Aquinas** (*ST* II-II q. 94 a. 4) cites 2 Kgs 17 as the prime biblical case of the wages of idolatry. **Calvin's** sermons on Kings (Geneva, 1561) made the chapter central to his political theology — the kingdom of Israel falls because its kings led it into idolatry; rulers bear theological weight; covenant infidelity has historical consequence. **Luther** treated the chapter as a paradigm of *deus absconditus* working through pagan empire.

Critical scholarship: **Cogan and Tadmor's AB volume** is indispensable for the historical reconstruction, including their treatment of the Sargon/Shalmaneser problem and the Cuthaean resettlement. **Bob Becking's *The Fall of Samaria***  (1992) is the monographic treatment. **Marvin Sweeney** (OTL *I & II Kings*) reads the chapter as the dtr's theological keystone. **Mordechai Cogan's *The Raging Torrent*** anthologizes the Sargon and Shalmaneser inscriptions in accessible form. **K. Lawson Younger** has written extensively on the deportation-and-resettlement question. **Brueggemann** (*1 & 2 Kings*) reads vv. 7–23 as the dtr's most theologically exposed moment — the historian risking everything to make a single argument: *covenant violation produces historical catastrophe*. **Davis**, **Provan**, **Goldingay** treat the chapter as the canonical center of the Northern Kingdom's tragedy. **Marvin Sweeney**'s *King Josiah of Judah* (2001) frames vv. 7–23 as the dtr's prosecutorial brief that justifies the Josianic reform program: this is the apologetic ground from which the Josiah narrative will be launched.

The "ten lost tribes" tradition has its own reception arc — Eldad ha-Dani in the 9th c., Benjamin of Tudela in the 12th, the medieval Sambation legends, the Bnei Menashe of northeastern India, the Lemba of southern Africa. None of this is what the dtr is doing in 2 Kings 17, but it is what the chapter became in popular Jewish, Christian, and even Mormon imagination.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is the dtr's apologia a theodicy that preserves divine justice, or is it a victim-blaming narrative that absolves YHWH of catastrophic failure?**

*Position A (the dtr is doing necessary theodicy):* For the historian writing in or near the 7th c. (Cross's Dtr1), the question is acute: how do you maintain the theological coherence of YHWH's covenant after the obvious historical failure of the Northern Kingdom? The answer is to relocate the failure: not in the covenant or the deity, but in covenant violation by the people. Brueggemann reads this as theodicy doing its proper work — the historian preserves the moral coherence of history.

*Position B (the dtr is theologically violent):* Post-Holocaust readers have pressed the question of whether the dtr's apologia is itself a kind of theological violence — making the destroyed people responsible for their own destruction, narrating Assyrian imperial violence as deserved. Walter Brueggemann, in *Theology of the Old Testament* (1997), distinguishes between the dtr's "core testimony" (covenant grounds judgment) and the "counter-testimony" (Lamentations, the laments) that protests the calculus.

*Position C (the dtr is doing both):* Many recent readers (Polzin, Sweeney, Carolyn Sharp) argue the dtr knows what he is doing — he is constructing an argument designed to discipline behavior in the surviving southern kingdom, and he is willing to absorb the moral cost. The chapter's parenthesis on Judah (vv. 19–20) reveals the agenda: the Northern apologia is a Southern warning.

**Steelman of the most uncomfortable position:** Position B. There is something disquieting about a text that catalogs an empire's violence and then assigns responsibility to its victims. The verbs throughout vv. 7–23 are causal in only one direction: *because* Israel sinned, *therefore* Assyria came. Assyrian agency is theologically erased. Sargon and Shalmaneser become instruments, not actors. If we read the chapter alongside the **Lachish reliefs** (Sennacherib's pictorial celebration of Levantine destruction) or the deportation depictions in Assyrian palace art, the asymmetry is acute: the chapter does not protest, it explains.

**Where this leaves us:** The dtr's apologia is doing covenant-theology under duress. It is theology that explains, not theology that protests. The Hebrew Bible eventually finds its protest voice in Lamentations and Job. But 2 Kings 17 is not Lamentations. It is the historian's brief — and the question of whether that brief honors the dead or buries them remains canonically open.

**Tension 2: What is the theological status of the new Samarian community in vv. 24–41 — and is the chapter's verdict on syncretism fair?**

*Position A (the dtr's verdict stands):* The closing pericope's diagnosis — "they feared YHWH and also served their own gods" — is the dtr's diagnosis of all unfaithfulness, not just Samarian. The principle is theological: covenant fidelity is exclusive or it is not real. Calvin pressed this hard; the chapter's logic is the logic of the first commandment.

*Position B (the dtr's verdict is polemical, not historical):* Pummer, Kartveit, and the recent archaeology of Gerizim have shown that the historical Samaritans were Yahwistic monotheists, not the syncretists vv. 24–41 portray. The pericope is **anti-Samaritan polemic** retrojected as origin story. To read it as ethnography is to participate in its polemic.

*Position C (the dtr is making a theological, not ethnographic, point):* The pericope may be using the new Samarian population as a literary screen for a theological question that applies to all peoples: what does it mean to "fear YHWH" while also serving other gods? Read this way, the chapter is anthropological rather than ethnographic — it diagnoses a human pattern.

**Steelman of the uncomfortable position:** Position B. The pericope generates a stereotype that has had centuries of theological mileage and arguable harm. The Samaritan community is a real historical community with its own scriptural tradition (the Samaritan Pentateuch), its own priestly lineage, and its own continuous worship at Gerizim. The dtr's portrait — multiple gods, lions, ad hoc priesthood — does not match the historical record of that community. The fairness question is real.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter is doing two things at once. Vv. 7–23 is theology of catastrophe that we must take with full seriousness. Vv. 24–41 is polemic that we must read with critical care. To collapse them — to treat the polemic as ethnography or the theology as polemic — is to miss what the chapter is doing.

**Unresolved question:** Why does the dtr include the parenthesis on Judah (vv. 19–20) — "Judah also did not keep the commandments" — given that Judah does not yet, at the chapter's narrative moment, fall? Is this a later editorial gloss from the exilic period (Cross's Dtr2) reading Judah's later fate back into the Samarian apologia? Or is it the pre-exilic dtr's prophetic warning that the Northern fate could become Judah's? The verse is the chapter's most theologically exposed moment, and its dating remains contested.

## The Thread

Read 2 Kings 17 against **Deuteronomy 28** (the covenant curses): the chapter is the historical realization of the covenant's predicted catastrophes, written as if Deuteronomy 28 is the prosecutor's manual. Read it forward into **2 Kings 18:9–12**, which restates the fall of Samaria *immediately* before the Hezekiah narrative — the dtr is structurally pairing the Northern catastrophe with the Southern's brush with the same fate. Read it against **Hosea 4–14** for the prophetic voice that the dtr's catalog is summarizing — Hosea is the source-tradition for much of the dtr's Northern critique. Read it against **Lamentations** for the protest voice the dtr does not give us. And read it against the later **Ezra 4** dispute over the Samarians' place in temple-rebuilding — the polemical seed planted in 2 Kgs 17 grows into the canonical Jewish-Samaritan rift, with consequences felt at **John 4** and through to Mount Gerizim today.
