# Song of Solomon 6 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Song of Solomon 6 sits at the structural midpoint of the *Shir HaShirim* (שִׁיר הַשִּׁירִים, "Song of Songs"), the Hebrew title meaning "the greatest of songs" (a superlative of the construct *shir + shirim*, like *qodesh qodashim*, "Holy of Holies"). The book's opening superscription, *asher li-Shlomoh* (אֲשֶׁר לִשְׁלֹמֹה, 1:1), traditionally attributed authorship to Solomon. Modern critical scholarship — Marvin Pope (*Anchor Bible*), Roland Murphy (*Hermeneia*), Othmar Keel (*Continental Commentary*), Richard Hess (*BECNT*), Tremper Longman (*NICOT*), Michael Fox (*The Song of Songs and the Ancient Egyptian Love Songs*) — generally reads the final form as a post-exilic anthology of older love poetry, redacted into a near-dialogic shape, perhaps in the Persian or Hellenistic period (fifth to third century BC). The presence of Persian *pardes* (פַּרְדֵּס) and possibly Greek-period syntactic features supports later dating, while the substance of the poetry may draw on pre-exilic and even Solomonic-era material.

Chapter 6 contains the chapter's most famous and most enigmatic clue to the woman's identity: *shulammit* (שׁוּלַמִּית, 6:13 in English; 7:1 in the Hebrew numbering). The name appears nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible. Three main proposals exist. First, geographic — *Shulammite* as a feminine gentilic from Shulam or Shunem, the village in the Jezreel Valley associated with Abishag the Shunammite who warmed the dying David (1 Kings 1:3). The Hebrew letters *lamed* and *nun* interchange in some dialects, and Pope, Keel, and Murphy generally accept this. Second, onomastic — *Shulammit* as a feminine form of *Shlomoh* (Solomon), making her "Mrs. Solomon" or the female Solomon. Third, eponymic-mythological — Pope provocatively linked the name to Shulmanitu, an Ishtar-type goddess; most scholars reject this. Robert Alter, Murphy, and Hess lean toward the geographic reading.

The chapter's reference to *shishim hemmah melakhot u-shemonim pilagshim ve-alamot ein mispar* (שִׁשִּׁים הֵמָּה מְלָכוֹת וּשְׁמֹנִים פִּילַגְשִׁים וַעֲלָמוֹת אֵין מִסְפָּר, "sixty queens and eighty concubines, and young women without number," 6:8) has been read by traditional scholarship as a Solomonic harem reference (1 Kings 11:3 famously gives Solomon 700 wives and 300 concubines), though the numbers do not match. Critical scholarship reads it as a poetic foil — a hyperbolic *court-of-women* against which the beloved stands singular and incomparable (*achat hi yonati tammati*, אַחַת הִיא יוֹנָתִי תַמָּתִי, "my dove, my perfect one, is one alone," 6:9).

The mention of *Tirzah* (תִּרְצָה, 6:4) is a chronological marker scholars exploit. Tirzah was the capital of the northern kingdom of Israel from Jeroboam I until Omri founded Samaria around 880 BC (1 Kings 14:17, 16:23-24). Pairing Tirzah with Jerusalem suggests a poet writing when both cities held political and aesthetic prestige — pointing to the divided-monarchy period or, more likely, to a later poet using both names as iconic beautiful cities without political weight. The Egyptian *Love Songs of the New Kingdom* (Chester Beatty Papyri, c. 1300 BC) and Mesopotamian Inanna-Dumuzi sacred-marriage poetry both use city-imagery for the beloved's body — an ANE convention Michael Fox documented thoroughly.

Major scholars whose work intersects this chapter: Marvin Pope, Roland Murphy, Robert Alter, Richard Hess, Tremper Longman, Othmar Keel, Michael Fox, Ellen Davis.

## Lens 2: The Literature

The chapter opens with the daughters of Jerusalem (*B'not Yerushalayim*) responding to the woman's praise-poem of 5:10-16: *anah halakh dodekh ha-yafah ba-nashim* (אָנָה הָלַךְ דּוֹדֵךְ הַיָּפָה בַּנָּשִׁים, "where has your beloved gone, O most beautiful among women," 6:1). The chorus serves as both literary mirror and audience — they sing back the praise the woman has just delivered. Robert Alter calls this device the chorus-as-amplifier: the daughters' question creates space for the woman to locate her beloved (6:2-3) and articulate the chapter's most quoted formula.

That formula — *ani le-dodi ve-dodi li ha-ro'eh ba-shoshanim* (אֲנִי לְדוֹדִי וְדוֹדִי לִי הָרֹעֶה בַּשּׁוֹשַׁנִּים, "I am my beloved's and my beloved is mine, who grazes among the lilies," 6:3) — is the third and most balanced occurrence of a refrain that runs through the book. The first version (*dodi li va-ani lo*, "my beloved is mine, and I am his," 2:16) puts him first. The third version (*ani le-dodi ve-alai teshuqato*, "I am my beloved's, and his desire is for me," 7:10) drops the reciprocal entirely. The 6:3 formulation is the chiastic balance point: *ani le-dodi // ve-dodi li*. Murphy and Longman both read this progression as the Song's deliberate movement toward perfect mutuality, with 6:3 as the equilibrium pivot — and note that across all three versions of the refrain it is the *woman* who speaks it.

The man's *waṣf* (وصف, the descriptive love-poem genre) at 6:4-7 partly reprises 4:1-3 with one shift: where chapter 4 began with eyes (*einayikh yonim*), chapter 6 hits the eyes after the cities, and uses a striking command — *hasebbi einayikh mi-negdi she-hem hir-hivuni* (הָסֵבִּי עֵינַיִךְ מִנֶּגְדִּי שֶׁהֵם הִרְהִיבֻנִי, "turn away your eyes from me, for they overwhelm me," 6:5). The verb *hir-hiv* is rare; it can mean "make proud," "embolden," or "throw into confusion." The man is undone by her gaze — a striking inversion of the gendered gaze conventions of ancient love poetry, where the male typically looks and the female is looked at. Pope, Murphy, and Longman all flag this reversal as of a piece with the Song's larger habit of giving the woman the active part.

The numerical climax (6:8-9) uses *shishim... shemonim... ein mispar* (60... 80... countless) in the rhetorical pattern *x // x+1 // hyperbole* — a graded numerical sequence common in Hebrew wisdom literature (cf. Proverbs 30, Amos 1-2). The point is not arithmetic but contrast: against any harem of any size, *achat hi* — she is one.

The address to the Shulammite (6:13) uses the verb *shuvi* (שׁוּבִי, "return") four times — *shuvi shuvi ha-shulammit shuvi shuvi ve-nehezeh bakh*. The fourfold imperative is rhetorically intense; the play between *Shulammit* and *shuvi* (both built on roots that *sound* connected even if etymologically distinct — *shalem* vs *shuv*) is a classic Hebrew sound-pun. The closing image — *ki-mecholat ha-machanayim* (כִּמְחֹלַת הַמַּחֲנָיִם, "as upon the dance of two camps/armies") — is one of the most opaque phrases in the book. Possibilities range from a specific dance form to a place name (Mahanaim, Gen 32:2) to an angelic-army image to a wedding processional dance. Murphy and Pope concede the obscurity; Keel proposes a sword-dance.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

The Song's canonical status was secured against late first-century opposition by Rabbi Akiva. Mishnah *Yadayim* 3:5 records his ruling: *kol ha-ketuvim qodesh ve-Shir HaShirim qodesh qodashim* — "all the writings are holy, but the Song of Songs is the Holy of Holies." Akiva's defense rested on the Song's allegorical reading as covenant-love between Yahweh and Israel. The *Targum Shir HaShirim* (probably seventh-eighth century AD) systematized this reading, mapping the Song onto Israel's history from Exodus through messianic redemption. *Shir HaShirim Rabbah*, the great midrashic compilation, allegorized verse by verse. Rashi, in the eleventh century, produced an influential commentary treating the Song as Israel-in-exile longing for God.

The phrase *ani le-dodi ve-dodi li* (6:3) became, and remains, the most popular Jewish liturgical and devotional inscription. The acronym *Elul* (אלול) is read in Jewish tradition as alluding to the verse: *Ani Le-dodi Ve-dodi Li* — making the month of Elul (preceding Rosh Hashanah) a season of returning to the divine beloved. The phrase is engraved on countless Jewish wedding rings, ketubot, and ritual objects. Maimonides cited the Song's longing-language as the highest model of the soul's love for God (*Mishneh Torah, Hilkhot Teshuvah* 10). The *Zohar* and later kabbalistic tradition read the lover-beloved unity as the union of *Tiferet* and *Shekhinah*, the masculine and feminine aspects of divine emanation.

In modern Jewish reception, Ariel and Chana Bloch's 1995 translation with literary commentary, Robert Alter's translation in *The Hebrew Bible* (2018), and Avivah Zornberg's literary readings have variously recovered the Song as both human love poetry and sacred text — refusing to flatten either pole.

The Christian allegorical trajectory begins with Hippolytus of Rome and reaches systematic form in Origen's *Commentary on the Song of Songs* and *Homilies on the Song* (mid-third century). Origen read the Shulammite as either the Church (corporate allegory) or the soul (individual mystical allegory). Gregory of Nyssa's fifteen homilies (c. 380) made the chapter central to his doctrine of *epektasis*, the soul's endless straining. Bernard of Clairvaux's eighty-six *Sermons on the Song of Songs* (1135-1153) built the great medieval mystical reading; Bernard's reading of *ani le-dodi ve-dodi li* became the template for Christian devotional literature on the soul's union with Christ. William of St. Thierry, Teresa of Avila, John of the Cross's *Spiritual Canticle*, and Hadewijch of Antwerp all extended this trajectory.

The reformers softened allegory but did not abandon it. Luther preferred a political reading. Calvin avoided the Song. Pope John Paul II's *Theology of the Body* (Wednesday catecheses, 1979-1984) and Christopher West's popularizations recovered a literal-theological reading: human eros as iconic of trinitarian self-gift. Modern Christian commentary — Ellen Davis (*Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Songs*), Tremper Longman (NICOT), Richard Hess (BECNT), Marvin Pope (Anchor) — generally reads the Song literally while acknowledging the legitimate allegorical trajectory.

NT echoes: Ephesians 5:22-33 (Christ and the Church as bridegroom and bride); Revelation 19:7-9 and 21:2 (the marriage supper of the Lamb); John 3:29 (John the Baptist as friend of the bridegroom). The covenantal *I am his and he is mine* formula echoes throughout the prophets — *I will be your God, and you shall be my people* (Jer 31:33, Ezek 36:28, Hos 2:23) — making 6:3 a deliberate or accidental resonance with the covenant formulary itself.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The principal tension in chapter 6 is the meaning of *ani le-dodi ve-dodi li* (6:3) and the identity of the *Shulammite* (6:13).

**Position one** — the dominant Jewish-traditional and Christian-mystical reading — treats 6:3 as a covenantal-mystical formula. The reciprocal pronouns describe the soul's union with God, mirroring the covenant formulary of the Sinai tradition: *I will be theirs, and they shall be mine*. On this reading, the Shulammite's identity is theologically rich: she is *Israel*, or *Ecclesia*, or the *anima* of the believer. The numerical contrast in 6:8-9 (one beloved against sixty queens) figures the singularity of God's election or of the soul's chosen object. The Shulammite as *Solomonic* feminine — *Shulammit* as the female *Shlomoh* — suggests perfect peace (*shalom*) embodied in covenant union. Akiva, Bernard, and John of the Cross stand together on this reading, as does the modern devotional inheritor — the *Ani le-dodi* of every Jewish wedding band.

**Position two** — modern critical scholarship — treats the chapter as human love poetry. The Shulammite is a young woman from Shunem (or a poetic feminine form). The 6:8-9 numerics are a hyperbolic court-of-women trope, not a Solomonic harem. The 6:3 formula is the language of mutual erotic possession, beautiful in its own right and not requiring theological encoding. Pope, Murphy, Longman, Hess, and Fox converge here, with varying tolerance for downstream allegorical use.

**Position three** — the *both/and* reading — argues the human-erotic and the theological-covenantal are not rivals. Ellen Davis and Pope John Paul II, from very different traditions, articulate versions of this. The covenant formulary echoes in 6:3 not because the Song is *really* about God but because the Song's human eros is *itself* a created participation in the kind of self-gift that names God's relation to Israel. Marriage is sacramental, not in some thin metaphor sense, but because human covenant-love is the ground from which the prophets *learned* how to speak about Yahweh's love. The Shulammite is a real woman *and* every soul *and* Israel *and* the Church — the levels do not compete but cohere.

Steelman position one: the canonical placement (Holy of Holies, *Megillah* read at Passover), the universal allegorical reading by both Jewish and Christian classical traditions, the covenant-formulary echo, and the immense liturgical-devotional life of *ani le-dodi* all suggest the text's intended canonical work is theological. To read the Song *only* as human love poetry is to flatten what Akiva and Bernard discerned.

Steelman position two: the literal sense is not a husk to be discarded for allegorical kernel. Eroticism in scripture is a gift, not a problem. The history of Christian unease with the body — Origen literally castrating himself, the Marcionite suspicion of the flesh, the puritan hostility to pleasure — is corrected, not enabled, by reading the Song as exactly what it appears to be. The *I am his* formula is a model of mutual erotic personhood that does theological work *because* it is first true of bodies.

**Where this leaves us**: chapter 6 stages the woman's location of her absent beloved (6:2-3), the man's overwhelmed gaze (6:5), and the daughters' fourfold call to *return*. The chapter's signature line, *ani le-dodi ve-dodi li*, has functioned as covenantal Hebrew, as wedding-ring inscription, as monastic prayer, and as erotic affirmation across two and a half millennia. Its survival across these uses is itself evidence of the both/and reading: the line works because human love and divine love share grammar. The Shulammite stays partly enigmatic — Shunemite woman, Solomonic feminine, dancing figure, returning beloved — and the canon does not resolve the ambiguity.

**Closing question**: when the chorus calls *return, return*, what kind of return is being asked for — geographic, erotic, covenantal? And whose voice is it that needs to be summoned back to be seen?

## The Thread

The chapter pivots on mutuality. The woman articulates the most balanced form of the possession refrain (6:3), and the man immediately replies that her gaze undoes him (6:5). Both lovers are subject and object; both look and are overwhelmed. Against the sixty queens and eighty concubines of any imperial court, against the four-times-summoned crowd that wants to *look upon* the Shulammite, the chapter insists on the singularity of the one beloved. The thread is the asymmetry within mutuality — *ani le-dodi*, but also *only one*.
