# Ephesians 4: One Body, Many Gifts, and the Mystery of the Descent

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Ephesus in the second half of the first century was the third- or fourth-largest city of the Roman Empire, the de facto capital of the senatorial province of Asia, and the principal port through which goods from the Anatolian interior reached the Aegean. If the letter is Pauline, the political horizon is the early reign of Nero (c. 60-62), when imperial cult activity in Asia was already entrenched but had not yet become aggressively Christianizing pressure; if the letter is post-Pauline, the horizon shifts toward Domitian or even Trajan (c. 80-95), when the imperial cult in Ephesus expanded with the Temple of the Sebastoi (dedicated 89/90 AD) and pressure on minority associations intensified. In either case, the city's defining institution was the Artemision — the temple of Artemis Ephesia, one of the seven wonders, four times the footprint of the Parthenon — whose silversmiths Acts 19 already shows resisting Pauline preaching. Civic identity was bound up with the goddess; the Ephesian *neōkoros* ("temple-keeper") title for the city was a source of intense pride.

The material culture relevant to chapter 4 is the cosmopolitan Asian household, the magical milieu of the so-called *Ephesia grammata* (the Ephesian Letters — six nonsense syllables believed to ward off demons, attested by Plutarch and the Suda), and a substantial Jewish diaspora community whose rights Josephus catalogues at length (*Antiquities* 14.225-264, where decrees from Roman officials protect Jewish Sabbath observance, dietary laws, and exemption from military service in Asian cities). Chapter 4's vocabulary of "putting off" the old self and "putting on" the new (4:22, 24) draws on the everyday gesture of changing a garment, but readers steeped in the local magical culture would also have heard echoes of ritual transformation; Clinton Arnold's *Ephesians: Power and Magic* (1989) demonstrates how saturated Asia Minor was with magical-protective formulae, which makes the letter's repeated insistence on the supremacy of Christ over every *archē* and *exousia* a contextually pointed claim.

The authorship debate hovers over every line. Critical scholars from C.L. Mitton's landmark *The Epistle to the Ephesians* (1951) through Andrew Lincoln's Word Biblical Commentary (1990) and Rudolf Schnackenburg's commentary (ET 1991) argue for pseudonymity on grounds of vocabulary (more than 80 *hapax legomena*), the long periodic sentences, the developed universal ecclesiology, the close literary dependence on Colossians, and the impersonal tone toward a city Paul knew intimately. Conservative defenders — Harold Hoehner's exhaustive 930-page commentary (2002), Peter O'Brien (PNTC), Frank Thielman (BECNT), F.F. Bruce, Markus Barth (Anchor Bible), and Clinton Arnold (ZECNT) — answer each point and retain Pauline authorship. A middle position, going back to Erasmus and reaffirmed by figures like J.D.G. Dunn, posits Pauline content mediated through a secretary or close disciple. Margaret MacDonald (*Colossians and Ephesians*, Sacra Pagina) argues for a deutero-Pauline letter that intentionally consolidates and universalizes Pauline theology for a second-generation church. The textual destination is itself unstable: "in Ephesus" at 1:1 is absent from P46 (c. 200), Sinaiticus א*, and Vaticanus B*, leading most modern scholars to treat the letter as an encyclical to Asian congregations — Marcion already called it the letter "to the Laodiceans."

The principal textual witnesses for chapter 4 are P46 (the earliest substantial witness, where Ephesians stands between Romans and Galatians), Sinaiticus א, Vaticanus B (which, unlike its treatment of the Pastorals, includes Ephesians), and Alexandrinus A. Five Greek terms anchor the chapter: ἑνότης (*henotēs*, "unity," appearing in the New Testament only in 4:3 and 4:13); χάρις (*charis*, "grace") and δωρεά (*dōrea*, "gift") in 4:7; ἀνέβη / κατέβη (*anebē* / *katebē*, "he ascended" / "he descended") in 4:8-10; τέλειος (*teleios*, "mature, complete") in 4:13; and the paired παλαιὸν ἄνθρωπον / καινὸν ἄνθρωπον (*palaion anthrōpon* / *kainon anthrōpon*, "old self" / "new self") in 4:22-24.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Ephesians 4 marks the hinge of the letter, where doxological-indicative theology (chapters 1-3) turns to imperatival paraenesis (chapters 4-6). The opening "I therefore urge you" (*parakalō oun hymas*) is the standard Pauline transitional formula, here expanded by a participial cascade typical of the letter's prolix style. The genre of 4:1-16 is community-formation paraenesis, but it carries embedded liturgical material. Verses 4-6 — "one body, one Spirit, one hope, one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father" — read as a sevenfold confession with a clear triadic substructure (Spirit / Lord / Father), almost certainly a baptismal acclamation already in circulation. Markus Barth, Lincoln, and Schnackenburg all treat it as pre-formed liturgical tradition. The asyndetic, rhythmic enumeration is a signature of early Christian acclamatory speech.

The literary centerpiece of the chapter is 4:7-16, a midrashic exposition of Psalm 68:18 [LXX 67:19] that functions almost as a small rabbinic *pesher*. The textual problem here is among the most discussed in the Pauline corpus. The Hebrew text and the Septuagint both read ἔλαβες δόματα ἐν ἀνθρώποις (*elabes domata en anthrōpois*, "you received gifts among/from men") — a Yahweh victory psalm in which the divine warrior receives tribute from the conquered. Ephesians 4:8 instead reads ἔδωκεν δόματα τοῖς ἀνθρώποις (*edōken domata tois anthrōpois*, "he gave gifts to men"). Three options dominate scholarship. First, the Targumic-Peshitta solution: the Aramaic Targum on Psalms at 68:18 reads "you gave gifts to the sons of men," and the Syriac Peshitta agrees; this is no idiosyncratic Christian alteration but reflects an ancient Jewish-Aramaic exegetical tradition that read the psalm's Moses-on-Sinai background through the lens of Moses receiving Torah and giving it to Israel. Lincoln (WBC 242-244) and Ernest Best (ICC) endorse this reading. Second, a creative Christological re-reading in which the verb is changed to fit the new Christ-event referent (Hoehner, Thielman). Third, a paraphrastic exegetical move characteristic of Second Temple midrashic technique, where the change is itself the interpretive act (Richard Hays, *Echoes of Scripture*). The Targumic explanation has gained ground because it shows the author working within a recognized Jewish hermeneutical stream rather than improvising.

Verses 9-10 contain the descensus parenthesis: εἰς τὰ κατώτερα μέρη τῆς γῆς (*eis ta katōtera merē tēs gēs*, "into the lower regions of the earth") — a genitive of apposition ("the lower regions, namely the earth," a contrast to "above all the heavens" in v. 10) or a partitive genitive ("the regions lower than the earth," i.e., Hades). Both readings have ancient pedigree, and the choice carries enormous downstream theological weight. Verse 11 lists what tradition calls APEPT or "fivefold ministry" — apostles, prophets, evangelists, shepherds-and-teachers — the last pair governed by a single article (τοὺς δὲ ποιμένας καὶ διδασκάλους, *tous de poimenas kai didaskalous*) which most commentators read as a Granville Sharp-style construction designating one office or two closely related ones. Verse 13 sets the goal as εἰς ἄνδρα τέλειον (*eis andra teleion*, "to mature manhood"), with *teleios* signaling completeness rather than gendered adulthood.

The latter half of the chapter (4:17-32) deploys the "put off / put on" baptismal metaphor (cf. Col 3:9-10, Rom 13:14), almost certainly catechetical in origin. The vice-virtue lists (4:25-32) follow Hellenistic-Jewish moral conventions but are theologized by being grounded in pneumatology ("do not grieve the Holy Spirit," 4:30) and christology ("as God in Christ forgave you," 4:32). In the architecture of the letter, chapter 4 begins the working-out of the cosmic reconciliation announced in chapters 1-3: the new humanity formed in Christ (2:15) must now actually behave like one body, with concrete speech, work, and forgiveness practices.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical New Testament, Ephesians 4 sits at the intersection of three trajectories: the Pauline ecclesiology of the body (1 Cor 12, Rom 12), the Lukan ascension narrative (Luke 24, Acts 1-2), and the descent-of-Christ tradition that runs through 1 Peter 3:18-20 and into the Apostles' Creed. The chapter's claim that the ascended Christ "gave gifts to men" reframes Pentecost: where Acts 2 narrates the Spirit poured out, Ephesians 4 narrates the *gifts* poured out — and the gifts are people (apostles, prophets, etc.), not merely abilities.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Origen's commentary (extant in Greek fragments and Jerome's Latin) reads 4:9-10 as a literal harrowing: Christ descended into Hades to liberate the patriarchs and OT righteous. The *Gospel of Nicodemus* / *Acts of Pilate* (4th c.) elaborates this scene into a full apocalypse. Chrysostom's twenty-four homilies on Ephesians (c. 392 in Antioch, then revisited at Constantinople) emphasize the moral-pastoral force of 4:1-16, treating "speak the truth in love" (v. 15) as the integrative virtue of church life. Jerome's commentary, drawing heavily on Origen, transmits the Greek exegesis to the Latin West. Marius Victorinus produced the first Latin commentary (mid-4th c.), notable for its Neoplatonic Christology. Augustine, in *Letter 164* to Evodius, develops the descensus carefully: Christ truly went to the realm of the dead and preached to the OT saints; this becomes the standard Latin patristic reading.

In the medieval period, Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* III.52) systematizes the descensus into four "effects" — liberating the holy fathers, manifesting Christ's power, comforting souls in purgatory, and showing the saints in hell their condemnation. Bernard of Clairvaux's bridal mysticism, while focused more on the Song of Songs, draws on Ephesians' nuptial imagery to read the church-Christ relationship as the model for the soul's union. Aquinas also reads 4:11 sacramentally: the apostolic office continues in the episcopate.

The Reformation reread the whole apparatus. Calvin (*Institutes* II.16.10) refused the literal harrowing as fanciful and treated the descensus as figurative for Christ's bearing of divine wrath on the cross; the symbolic reality, not the geographical journey, is the point. Luther retained the harrowing as victorious — Christ smashing the gates of hell — but reframed it as a preaching of victory rather than a rescue from limbo. The Anabaptist tradition, especially the Schleitheim Confession (1527) and later Menno Simons, read 4:1-6's unity through nonresistance: the "bond of peace" is constitutive, and any sword-bearing arm of the church violates it. On 4:11, Calvin distinguished the *foundational* apostles and prophets (no longer needed) from the *continuing* evangelists, pastors, and teachers — the seed of cessationist polity.

Critical scholarship from F.C. Baur and the Tübingen school treated Ephesians' high ecclesiology as evidence of "early catholicism" — the routinization of charisma into office. H.J. Holtzmann's *Kritik der Epheser- und Kolosserbriefe* (1872) developed the dependence-on-Colossians thesis; Mitton (1951) consolidated it. Käsemann's "Ephesians and Acts" (1966) argued the letter's ecclesiology already presupposes the apostolic age as past. Lincoln's WBC commentary (1990) and Schnackenburg synthesize this stream. Contemporary conservative work — Hoehner, O'Brien, Bruce, Thielman, M. Barth, Arnold — fights position by position.

The "fivefold" question has become a major contemporary battleground. The New Apostolic Reformation (NAR), associated with C. Peter Wagner's *Apostles Today* (2006), insists 4:11 mandates the restoration of contemporary apostles and prophets; this has shaped large swaths of charismatic-Pentecostal practice. J.D. Walt and the Wesleyan-charismatic stream take a softer restorationist line. Cessationist responses come from confessional Reformed (Westminster traditions), strict dispensational, and some Lutheran quarters: foundational gifts (apostles, NT prophets) ceased with the closing of the canon, while pastors, teachers, and evangelists continue. The Catholic position (*Lumen Gentium* 18-29) sees the apostolic charism as continuing through the episcopate in apostolic succession, so 4:11 is fulfilled, not pending. Mainline Protestant ecclesiology is mixed but generally retains a threefold or fourfold ordained ministry alongside lay charisms.

The descensus has had its own twentieth-century renaissance. Hans Urs von Balthasar's *Mysterium Paschale* (1969) reread Eph 4:9 alongside 1 Pet 3:18-20 to develop a theology of Holy Saturday: Christ's descent is solidarity with the dead in their abandonment, not a triumphal harrowing. Adrienne von Speyr's mystical visions shaped this. Alyssa Lyra Pitstick's *Light in Darkness* (2007) leveled a sharp Thomist critique: Balthasar's account, she argues, contradicts the magisterial tradition that distinguishes the *limbus patrum* (where Christ went) from the hell of the damned. The dispute is now a live faultline in Catholic theology.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** Modern Paul-within-Judaism scholarship has reshaped how this chapter reads. Krister Stendahl's *Paul Among Jews and Gentiles* (1976) and E.P. Sanders' *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977) dismantled the older Lutheran "Paul vs. Judaism" frame. Daniel Boyarin (*A Radical Jew*, 1994), Mark Nanos, Paula Fredriksen (*Paul: The Pagans' Apostle*, 2017), and Pamela Eisenbaum (*Paul Was Not a Christian*, 2009) read Pauline ethical paraenesis as continuous with diaspora-synagogue moral teaching rather than as displacement of Torah. The vice-virtue lists of 4:25-32 fit this perfectly: every item — truth-telling, control of anger, generosity to the poor, sealed speech — has direct parallels in rabbinic ethics (*Pirkei Avot*, the *Derekh Eretz* tractates). David Daube's *The New Testament and Rabbinic Judaism* (1956) argued for direct rabbinic-form influence on NT ethical material. Where Jewish scholarship still pushes back: against the universalizing church-as-new-humanity move (2:15, picked up in 4:13), which is read as effacing the particularity of Israel; against any reading of the Targumic shift in 4:8 that would suggest Christian supersession of Jewish exegetical authority; and against the descensus tradition as a foreign, mythological accretion.

**Where this leaves us.** The chapter is being read better today than at any point in the modern era because the historical-critical machinery, the Jewish hermeneutical context, and the theological tradition are finally in conversation. The fights — over fivefold ministry, over the harrowing, over what "unity of the Spirit" demands — are unresolved, but they are at least honest.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What does Ephesians 4:11-13 demand of contemporary church polity?** Take "the gifts" — apostles, prophets, evangelists, shepherds-and-teachers — given "until we all attain to the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God." The "until" (μέχρι, *mechri*) is the pressure point: are these offices ongoing, or did the foundational ones cease? The Catholic position grounded in *Lumen Gentium* 18-29 holds that the apostolic gift continues through the episcopate by apostolic succession; the church already lives in the answer to 4:11. The Reformed cessationist reading (Sinclair Ferguson, Richard Gaffin) distinguishes foundational from edificational gifts: apostles and NT prophets ceased with the canon, pastors-teachers continue. The restorationist Pentecostal-charismatic reading (C. Peter Wagner's NAR, the Fivefold movement) insists every office in the list is continuous and that the contemporary church is malformed where any of the five is missing. The Anabaptist reading historically downplayed offices in favor of communal discernment.

To steelman the position I find least intuitive — the strong restorationist NAR reading: the text says Christ *gave* (aorist, completed action with continuing effect) these five categories of persons "for the equipping of the saints, until we all attain unity." The "until" clause has a clear terminus (full unity in Christ), and the church manifestly has not reached it. If the offices were given "until X" and X has not occurred, the offices remain. Cessationism has to do exegetical work to introduce a category distinction (foundational vs. edificational) that the text itself does not draw. The restorationist insists the most natural reading is the continuing one. **Where this leaves us:** The text underdetermines polity; what it overdetermines is that gifts are given for unity, and any polity that fragments the body has failed the verse regardless of which offices it formally recognizes.

**Tension 2: What does it mean theologically that Christ descended?** The descensus tradition based on 4:9-10 (with 1 Pet 3:18-20) generates four major options. The Catholic-Orthodox literal harrowing: Christ in soul descended to the realm of the righteous dead and led them out (Aquinas, ST III.52; Catechism §§632-635 distinguishes this from the hell of the damned). Calvin's symbolic reading: the descensus names Christ's bearing of divine wrath on the cross, the spiritual reality the geographic image points to (*Institutes* II.16.10). Balthasar's Holy Saturday: Christ's descent is into godforsaken solidarity with the dead, a passive abandonment rather than active harrowing. Pitstick's Thomist counter-Balthasar: Balthasar's account is a serious deviation from magisterial tradition and dissolves the distinction between Christ's experience and the damned's. The Lutheran tradition, following Luther's *Torgau Sermon*, kept the harrowing as victorious proclamation.

Steelmanning Balthasar against my Reformed instincts: if the cross is the climax of God's self-giving and the descent is its extension, then any reading that empties the descent of content (Calvin: it's just a metaphor for the cross) loses the theological weight of the *triduum*. Balthasar refuses to collapse Friday, Saturday, and Sunday into a single moment. Saturday has to mean something, and what it means in his account is that God-in-Christ goes to the place where God is not — the place of total abandonment — and even there constitutes a presence. This is a serious answer to the problem of how Christ's death encompasses the godforsaken. Pitstick's worry — that this trespasses on the hell of the damned — is real, but Balthasar's defenders (Edward Oakes, Aidan Nichols) argue he carefully distinguishes Christ's experience from the damned's. **Where this leaves us:** the descensus is a doctrine the church has never fully agreed on, and the current Catholic dispute (Balthasar vs. Pitstick) is the most serious intra-tradition argument about it in centuries; Protestants who skip the doctrine because the Apostles' Creed is awkward are missing a real datum about what the cross accomplished.

**One unresolved question:** If the gifts of 4:11 are given "until we all attain to the unity of the faith," and the visible church is more fractured now than at any point in two millennia, is the deficit in our reception of the gifts, in our recognition of them, or in the very meaning of "unity" we are looking for?

## The Thread

- **Psalm 68:18 (LXX 67:19)** — the source text whose alteration in Eph 4:8 is the chapter's central exegetical puzzle; reading the psalm in Hebrew, LXX, and Targum side by side reveals that the author is not bending Scripture but participating in an existing Jewish-Aramaic interpretive tradition.
- **1 Peter 3:18-20** — the parallel descent passage; together with Eph 4:9-10 it forms the scriptural backbone of the descensus clause in the Apostles' Creed and of the Holy Saturday tradition.
- **Colossians 3:9-10** — the closest parallel to the "put off / put on" baptismal language of Eph 4:22-24; the literary dependence question between Colossians and Ephesians is fought across this seam.
- **Numbers 11:16-17 and 24-29** (Moses asks for elders and the Spirit is distributed) — a non-obvious Old Testament resonance for "he gave gifts" as the distribution of empowerment for service; Targum Psalms 68:18 makes the Sinai-Moses connection explicit.
