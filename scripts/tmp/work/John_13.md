# John 13: The Foot-Washing, the Betrayer, and the New Commandment

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

John 13 opens what Raymond Brown's Anchor Bible commentary famously named the "Book of Glory" (chs. 13–20), and it does so with a scene whose social texture would have struck a first-century Mediterranean reader with shocking force. Foot-washing was unambiguously the work of slaves. Greco-Roman household codes assigned the task to the lowest-ranked servants in the *familia*; in Jewish tradition, the *Mekhilta* on Exodus and the Babylonian Talmud (*Ketubbot* 96a) explicitly state that a Hebrew slave should *not* be required to wash his master's feet because the act was so degrading that it was reserved for Gentile slaves and women. Kenneth Bailey's *Jesus Through Middle Eastern Eyes* and Andreas Köstenberger's BECNT commentary both stress the cultural scandal: a free male teacher stripping (the verb in 13:4 is *tithēsin ta himatia*, τίθησιν τὰ ἱμάτια — "lays aside the garments," the same verb used for laying down one's life in 10:11), girding himself with a *lention* (λέντιον, the linen towel of a household slave — a Latin loan-word, *linteum*), and stooping to wash thirteen pairs of dust-caked feet would have been culturally transgressive in a way that the modernized liturgical reproduction can scarcely communicate.

The dining posture itself is historically loaded. The disciples are reclining (the Beloved Disciple is *anakeimenos en tō kolpō tou Iēsou*, ἀνακείμενος ἐν τῷ κόλπῳ τοῦ Ἰησοῦ — 13:23, "reclining at the bosom of Jesus") on triclinium couches in the Greco-Roman fashion, which had penetrated Jewish festival meals by the late Second Temple period; this is why Jesus can wash their feet at all (feet extended away from the table, head toward it). The chronological note "before the Feast of the Passover" (*pro de tēs heortēs tou pascha*) opens what is one of the most discussed historical-critical problems in the Gospels: the Johannine Passion chronology. In John, Jesus dies on 14 Nisan as the Passover lambs are slaughtered (18:28; 19:14); in the Synoptics, the Last Supper *is* the Passover meal. Annie Jaubert's *The Date of the Last Supper* proposed a solar-calendar (Qumran) reconciliation; Joachim Jeremias in *Eucharistic Words of Jesus* argued the Synoptic chronology against John; Brown in *Death of the Messiah* leaves the historical question open while emphasizing the Johannine theological point — Jesus *is* the Passover Lamb.

Three further Greek terms repay attention. *Hyperetein* and its semantic field do not appear here, but *doulos* (δοῦλος, "slave") explicitly does at 13:16 — the disciples are slaves whose master has just done slave-work. *Hypodeigma* (ὑπόδειγμα, 13:15, "example/pattern") is not the bland word for moral imitation; in 2 Maccabees 6:28, 31 it describes a martyr's death as a *hypodeigma* of nobility, suggesting Jesus' act prefigures the cross. And *deipnou ginomenou* (δείπνου γινομένου, 13:2, "during supper" or, with strong manuscripts, "supper having taken place") subtly affects whether Jesus washes feet during or after the meal — a textual-critical issue Bruce Metzger discusses in his *Textual Commentary*.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

John 13 inaugurates the **Farewell Discourse** (German: *Abschiedsreden*) that runs through chapter 17 and constitutes one of the most extended discourse units in the New Testament. The genre is Greco-Roman *testamentum* fused with Jewish farewell-tradition: Jacob's blessings of his sons in Genesis 49, Moses' final song and blessing in Deuteronomy 31–33, Joshua 23–24, David's last words in 1 Kings 2 and 1 Chronicles 28–29, and the post-canonical *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs*. Brown's Anchor Bible commentary catalogues the standard features of the Jewish farewell speech (announcement of imminent death, exhortation, prediction of trials, blessing or final commission, prayer) and shows how John 13–17 instantiates each. Käsemann's *The Testament of Jesus*, the foundational 1968 reading of John 17 as the theological summit of the entire Gospel, treats the whole farewell unit as a deliberate testamentary composition pointing toward chapter 17 as its climax.

The chapter's literary architecture is symmetrical. The opening prologue (13:1–3) telescopes Jesus' divine origin, destination, and intention with a triple knowing-clause (Jesus *knew* his hour had come, the Father had given all things into his hands, he had come from God and was going to God) — Marianne Meye Thompson in her NTL commentary calls this Johannine "stage-setting at altitude." Two narrative panels follow: the foot-washing (13:4–20) and the identification of the betrayer (13:21–30), each closing with a saying about reception or sending. The discursive section (13:31–38) then introduces the Farewell Discourse proper with three programmatic items: the glorification declaration (13:31–32), the new commandment (13:34–35), and Peter's denial foretold (13:36–38).

A source-critical seam lurks ahead at 14:31 ("Rise, let us go from here"), after which chapters 15–17 continue without movement; Bultmann's hypothesis of a misplaced or doubled discourse, with chapters 15–17 as an earlier or alternate version preserved by the redactor, is treated seriously by Brown but rejected by Carson and Köstenberger, who prefer a literary reading that treats 14:31 as a dramatic transition spoken in place. Either way, John 13 is the gateway, the moment when the Johannine narrative pivots from public signs to private discourse.

The chapter contains the **first explicit appearance of the Beloved Disciple as a named character in the narrative** (13:23). Bauckham's *The Testimony of the Beloved Disciple* and *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* argue this figure is John the Elder, an eyewitness distinct from John of Zebedee; Brown locates him in a sectarian Johannine community as its founding witness; Culpepper's *Anatomy of the Fourth Gospel* reads him as a literary device of authoritative witness. The "in the bosom" image (*en tō kolpō*) deliberately mirrors 1:18, where the Son is "in the bosom of the Father" — a literary inclusio that has the Beloved Disciple stand to Jesus as the Son stands to the Father.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The foot-washing episode has been read across two and a half millennia as a hinge between humility and sacrament. Origen's *Commentary on John* and Augustine's *Tractates on the Gospel of John* (Tract. 55–58) emphasize Christ's *humilitas* and the daily cleansing from venial sin that the foot-washing typifies after the once-for-all bath of baptism. Chrysostom's 88 Homilies on John (Hom. 70–71) treats Peter's protest as the standard pattern of human resistance to grace and the foot-washing as the model of pastoral self-emptying — a text Chrysostom returned to in his own ascetic practice. Aquinas's *Lectura super Joannem* and the *Catena Aurea* harvest these patristic readings and add a sacramental schema in which the bath that "makes you completely clean" (13:10) is baptism, the foot-washing is the washing of post-baptismal sins, and the betrayal of Judas reveals the limits of external rite without inward faith. The Latin word *mandatum* — from "I give you a *new commandment*" (13:34, *entolēn kainēn didōmi hymin*) — gave the Western liturgical day its name: **Maundy Thursday**, when the Roman Rite, the Anglican Communion, and Eastern liturgies all celebrate the *Mandatum* with the priest or bishop washing twelve men's feet.

The Reformation reception is divided. Calvin in his *Commentary on John* (one of his finest, completed in 1553) reads the foot-washing as a moral and christological example, not a sacrament — there are only two sacraments, and turning this episode into a third is, for Calvin, exactly the kind of Roman over-reading he resists. Luther in his John 14–17 sermons (delivered 1537–38, published in *WA* 45–46) likewise treats the foot-washing as exemplary humility and a rebuke of clerical pride. The **Anabaptist tradition**, however, took a different turn. The *Schleitheim Confession* (1527) does not list foot-washing among the ordinances, but later Mennonite, Brethren, and some Baptist confessions explicitly enumerated foot-washing as a third ordinance alongside baptism and the Lord's Supper. To this day, congregations in the Mennonite Church USA, the Church of the Brethren, the Old German Baptist Brethren, the Free Will Baptists, and many Pentecostal Holiness traditions practice ritual foot-washing — a sacramental witness Ron Sider, John Howard Yoder, and Stuart Murray argue keeps the radical-discipleship reading of John 13 visibly embodied. Pope Francis's choice, beginning in 2013, to wash the feet of women, Muslims, and prisoners on Holy Thursday — formally permitted by the *Decree on the Washing of Feet* in 2016 — provoked controversy within Catholic traditionalist circles precisely because it pressed the universalism of the foot-washing past the older convention of only twelve baptized males.

The Jewish interpretive trajectory has had less to say about John 13 specifically (since the foot-washing is uniquely Johannine and the chapter contains the dark "and it was night" of Judas's exit), but Daniel Boyarin's *The Jewish Gospels* and Adele Reinhartz's *Befriending the Beloved Disciple* read the chapter as a polemic-saturated text whose anti-Judaic resonances (especially in the Judas pericope and the prediction "where I am going you cannot come" repeating language used "to the Jews") need to be heard with care. Amy-Jill Levine in *The Jewish Annotated New Testament* notes that the love-command of 13:34 is rooted in Leviticus 19:18; what is "new" is not the content but the measure ("as I have loved you") and the missional function ("by this all people will know"). John Ashton's *Understanding the Fourth Gospel* highlights the dualistic light/darkness coding ("and it was night") as resonant with Qumran's *Two Spirits* tractate (1QS III–IV), and James Charlesworth's *John and Qumran* extends this in detail.

The 20th-century theological reception of John 13 has been concentrated in two areas: Christology and discipleship. Karl Barth in *Church Dogmatics* IV/2 §64 reads the foot-washing as the moment where the *kenōsis* of the Son is most concretely embodied — Christ "the Lord as servant" enacted at table, not on cross. Hans Urs von Balthasar's *Theo-Drama* IV treats Jesus' "having loved his own to the end" (*eis telos*, 13:1) as the dramatic key to the Passion: the *telos* is both temporal (the end of life) and qualitative (love's perfection). Bonhoeffer's *Cost of Discipleship* (German: *Nachfolge*) makes the foot-washing the visible content of "costly grace." Lesslie Newbigin's *The Light Has Come* underscores the missional reading of 13:35: the *world* is the audience of the Church's mutual love, not its judge. N.T. Wright, in *John for Everyone* and across *How God Became King* and *The Day the Revolution Began*, reads the foot-washing as a deliberate dramatic enactment of the cross: Jesus, knowing "the Father had given all things into his hands" (13:3), uses that total authority not to seize a throne in any conventional sense but to take the slave's towel — the kingdom of God redefined as self-giving love, the same redefinition the crucifixion will make absolute. For Wright the "new commandment" is the badge of the renewed-covenant community: just as circumcision and Torah-keeping marked the old covenant people, cruciform love for one another marks the people in whom Israel's God has come to dwell, and "by this all people will know" makes that love the sign to the watching world that the new creation has begun. Among contemporary commentators, Brown (AB), Barrett (CGTC), Carson (PNTC), Köstenberger (BECNT), Keener, Beasley-Murray (WBC), Schnackenburg, Marianne Meye Thompson (NTL), Moloney (Sacra Pagina), and O'Day (NIB) all give John 13 substantial treatment.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is the foot-washing primarily symbolic act, sacrament, or moral example?**

The Catholic tradition reads the foot-washing as a *sacramental* gesture pointing past itself to baptism (the bath that cleanses entirely) and the daily forgiveness extended through the Eucharist and reconciliation. The Anglican and Lutheran traditions retain the *Mandatum* liturgically without granting it sacramental status. Calvin's Reformed reading limits the act to a dramatic example of humility, with no sacramental content. The Anabaptist tradition makes it a third ordinance, a literal command requiring continuing congregational practice, and reads Peter's "you shall never wash my feet" as the cultural pride that the Church must keep dismantling. Eastern Orthodox liturgical practice retains the *Niptēr* rite (especially in Jerusalem and Mount Athos) without giving it equal weight with the seven mysteries. The Pentecostal-charismatic tradition has often retained foot-washing as ordinance among the holiness-rooted churches and dropped it among the prosperity-influenced ones.

Steelman the Anabaptist case: Jesus says "you also ought to wash one another's feet" with the same explicit imperative grammar as "do this in remembrance of me." If liturgical embodiment of the Supper is required, why should embodiment of the foot-washing not be? The Anabaptist witness to ordinances kept Christianity from sliding into purely symbolic readings of all of Jesus' table actions. The Catholic case in turn has the strength of patristic continuity and the recognition that 13:10 distinguishes a one-time bath (*leloumenos*) from recurrent foot-washing (*nipsasthai*) — exactly the baptismal-versus-daily-cleansing distinction the liturgy enacts.

**Where this leaves us:** The text does not need to be either sacrament or example exclusively; it discloses the shape of all Christian ritual action. Whatever the foot-washing is — third ordinance, *Mandatum*, or moral exemplum — its content is the same: the Lord becomes slave so that his slaves can become friends, and the community's truthfulness is measured by whether it can keep doing the same.

**Tension 2: What makes the "new commandment" (13:34) actually new?**

Brown's AB commentary catalogues four answers. (1) **The measure is new** ("as I have loved you") — Jesus' self-giving redefines the standard of love, making Levitical neighbor-love into cruciform discipleship. This is the dominant Reformed and evangelical reading (Carson, Köstenberger). (2) **The audience is new** — Leviticus 19:18 commanded love of fellow Israelite; this commandment establishes intra-Christian love as the foundational community marker. The Jewish-historical reading (Reinhartz, Levine) notes that Leviticus 19:34 already extended love to the *ger* (resident alien), so this answer needs qualification. (3) **The eschatological context is new** — in the messianic age, love takes on the character of participation in the divine life of the Trinity (the Eastern Orthodox reading; Vladimir Lossky). (4) **The missional function is new** — "by this all people will know that you are my disciples" makes love the apologetic of the Church to the world (Newbigin, Bosch).

Steelman each: the cruciform-measure reading honors the chapter's literary placement (just-washed feet, just-predicted betrayal, just-spoken glorification) and its echo in 15:13. The intra-community reading honors the actual referent ("one another," *allēlous*) and resists the modern flattening that turns Christian love into generic humanitarian benevolence without ecclesial shape. The Trinitarian-participatory reading honors 17:21 ("that they may be one as we are one") as the same theme reaching its climax. The missional reading honors the explicit purpose clause "so that all people may know."

**Where this leaves us:** All four are textually warranted and operate together. The newness is the cruciform measure embodied in the ecclesial community, participating in the Triune life of God, and visible to the world as evangelical witness. N.T. Wright would add a fifth angle that absorbs the others: the commandment is "new" in the way the *covenant* is new — it belongs to the age that has dawned with Jesus' death and resurrection, so that mutual love is not merely an ethic but the lived form of the new creation breaking into the present. The contemporary Church's particular failure has not been on any one of these fronts but in pulling them apart — making love either inwardly sentimental (no missional edge), or missionally instrumentalized (no cruciform measure), or theologically rarefied (no community-shaped practice).

**Unresolved question:** If "by this all people will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another" (13:35) is the Church's primary apologetic, what does it mean that the visible disunity and mutual hostility of the global Church across communions has been one of the most consistent features of Christian history since at least the third century? Is the verse a description (which would falsify Christianity by its own standard), a prescription (which leaves us with continuous failure), or an eschatological promise (which postpones the test to a horizon we cannot verify)? The chapter does not answer this; chapter 17 will press it harder.

## The Thread

John 13 establishes language and motifs that fan out across the rest of the Gospel and the New Testament. *Eis telos* ("to the end," 13:1) reaches its terminus at *tetelestai* ("it is finished," 19:30) — the same root, *telos*, framing the Passion. The foot-washing's *hypodeigma* (13:15) anticipates 1 Peter 2:21, where Christ leaves an *hypogrammos* (writing-pattern) for suffering. The new commandment of 13:34 is reformulated at 15:12, 15:17, and 1 John 3:11–18, 4:7–21 — the Johannine ethic in its fullest register. The Beloved Disciple's first appearance "in the bosom" (13:23) recurs at the cross (19:26–27, where he receives Mary), the empty tomb (20:2–10, where he believes), and the lakeside (21:7, 20–24, where he is identified as the witness). And "Where I am going you cannot come now, but you will follow afterward" (13:36) is answered at 21:18–19, where Peter's martyrdom is predicted in the same idiom.

Flag forward: chapter 14 will open with "Let not your hearts be troubled" — a direct response to 13:21 and 13:33's distress. Chapter 17:21–23 will return to the love-witnesses-the-world theme of 13:35. And the *aposynagōgos* warning in 16:2 reaches back through 9:22 and forward through Martyn's reconstruction of the Johannine community's late-first-century crisis.
