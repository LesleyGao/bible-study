# 1 Corinthians 9 — Going Deeper

## Historical World

Chapter 9 is not a digression. The Corinthians had read chapter 8 — or its substance — as Paul's claim that the strong must yield. They reply, in effect: Paul, you have not been yielding. You take support from us, you exercise apostolic authority over us, you are no model of the freedom-renounced-in-love you preach. Chapter 9 is Paul's defense (apologia, 9:3) — and, more importantly, his self-presentation as an example of the strong-becoming-weak that he just commended.

Roman Corinth was a patron-client society. Friendship (philia) ran on benefaction; financial support carried a chain of obligations. To accept payment from a patron was to enter that patron's clientela, with attendant duties of public deference. Bruce Winter (After Paul Left Corinth, 2001) and Stephen Joubert (Paul as Benefactor, 2000) have shown how thickly first-century social life was structured around these reciprocity obligations. Pauline ministry deliberately broke that grammar. By refusing payment from the Corinthians while accepting it from the Macedonians (2 Cor 11:7-9, Phil 4:14-18), Paul refused to enter the Corinthian patronage economy. Ronald Hock (The Social Context of Paul's Ministry, 1980) reconstructed Paul's tentmaking (skēnopoios, Acts 18:3) as the artisan-trade base of his self-support — work culturally beneath the dignity of itinerant philosophers and rhetors who lived from honoraria.

The athletic imagery in 9:24-27 lights up against Corinth's geography. The Isthmian Games were held every two years at Isthmia, a few miles from the city; they ranked second only to the Olympic Games among the four panhellenic festivals. The pine-wreath crown (the perishable wreath of 9:25) was the prize. Murphy-O'Connor argued that Paul probably timed his Corinth ministry to overlap the spring AD 51 Isthmian Games, when crowds and tentmakers' work both spiked. Pfitzner (Paul and the Agon Motif, 1967) traced how athletic-contest imagery saturated Hellenistic moral philosophy: Stoic, Cynic, and Hellenistic-Jewish writers (4 Macc 6:10, 17:11-16) used the agōn metaphor for moral discipline. Paul's wreath/crown contrast — perishable wreath versus imperishable — repurposes a culturally legible image.

The opening question "Have I not seen Jesus our Lord?" (9:1) is an apostolic credential, not biographical reminiscence. The criterion of 1 Cor 15:7-9 is being in view: an apostolos in the technical Pauline sense is a witness commissioned by the risen Christ. Galatians 1:11-17 fills out the contested standing: Paul's apostleship was disputed in some early Christian circles precisely because it bypassed the Twelve.

## Literature

The chapter divides into four movements. (1) 9:1-12a: a defense of Paul's apostolic rights, layered through rhetorical questions, common-life analogies (soldier, vinedresser, shepherd), Torah citation (Deut 25:4), and dominical tradition. (2) 9:12b-18: Paul's voluntary refusal of those rights, anchored in necessity (anankē) and stewardship (oikonomia). (3) 9:19-23: the missionary principle of becoming all things to all people. (4) 9:24-27: the athletic peroration on self-discipline.

Margaret Mitchell read the whole letter as deliberative rhetoric for concord; chapter 9 functions as the personal example (paradeigma) — the orator pointing to himself as the embodiment of the conduct he urges. The structural logic is: in chapter 8 I told you the strong must yield; in chapter 9 watch me yield; in chapter 10 here is what happens to those who do not (the wilderness generation).

The opening cascade of rhetorical questions (9:1) — four in succession — is a textbook diatribe move. So is the chain of analogies in 9:7 (soldier, vinedresser, shepherd), each of which expects the same answer: of course the worker is sustained by the work. The Deuteronomy 25:4 citation in 9:9 — "you shall not muzzle an ox when it treads out the grain" — is treated by Paul with a startling exegetical move: "Is it for oxen that God is concerned?" The verse's literal subject is set aside in favor of an a fortiori argument from the gemur principle of rabbinic kal va-homer (the lighter and the heavier): if God commands provision for the threshing ox, how much more for the apostle?

The pivot at 9:15 — "But I have made no use of any of these rights" — flips the elaborate defense on its head. Paul has spent fourteen verses establishing rights he refuses to exercise. The rhetorical purpose is now visible: only someone with a defensible right can demonstrate the meaning of forgoing it. The principle is announced at 9:12b: "we endure anything rather than put an obstacle in the way of the gospel." Anthony Thiselton (NIGTC 2000) and Gordon Fee (NICNT) both flag the unusual word enkopēn — a "cutting in" or hindrance — as the rhetorical key.

The closing athletic peroration is structured as a four-beat exhortation. The Greek participle hypōpiazō in 9:27 ("I beat my body black-and-blue") is a boxer's word — a punch under the eye. The verb doulagōgō ("bring into slavery") completes the imagery: Paul's body is a contestant being trained to obedience. The final word, adokimos ("disqualified"), was the technical Greek term for an athlete disqualified by the games' judges (the Hellanodikai at Olympia, the analogous officials at the Isthmian Games).

## The Larger Story

### The Jewish trajectory

Paul's argument in 9:8-12 self-consciously locates itself within Jewish exegetical method. The kal va-homer move on Deut 25:4 was standard rabbinic reasoning (codified later in Hillel's seven middot but already widespread in Second Temple practice). The principle of laborer's wages — "the laborer is worthy of his hire" — is rooted in Lev 19:13, Deut 24:14-15, and is developed in rabbinic tradition (Mishnah Bava Metzia treats laborer-disputes at length). The dominical word Paul cites in 9:14 ("the Lord commanded those who proclaim the gospel should get their living by the gospel") echoes the saying preserved in Luke 10:7: "the laborer deserves his wages."

The most contested Jewish question is 9:20: "to the Jews I became as a Jew, to those under the law I became as one under the law (though not being myself under the law)." Older Christian readings (and parts of Lutheran tradition) took the parenthesis as evidence Paul had ceased to be a Jew or to keep Torah. The "Paul within Judaism" school — Mark Nanos (Paul Within Judaism, 2015), Pamela Eisenbaum (Paul Was Not a Christian, 2009), Magnus Zetterholm — reads it differently: Paul's "becoming" language is strategic-accommodation language, not identity rejection. He remains a Torah-observant Jew (cf. his vows in Acts 18:18, 21:23-26) but practices flexibility around boundary-marker observances when ministering to Gentiles. Daniel Boyarin sees the move as a complex Jewish identity navigating the Gentile mission — both retaining and translating.

The athletic imagery has a Hellenistic-Jewish background as well as a Greco-Roman one. 4 Maccabees 6 and 17 use agon language for the Maccabean martyrs' contest of endurance. Philo (Legum Allegoriae 3.190) describes the soul's struggle as athletic. Paul stands within a Hellenistic-Jewish stream that had already domesticated the athletic metaphor for moral and religious endurance.

### The Christian trajectory

The patristic period read 9:5 — "the right to take along a believing wife" — with growing embarrassment. The Greek adelphēn gynaika literally means "a sister, a wife" (a wife who is a sister in Christ); the natural reading is that Peter, the brothers of the Lord, and the other apostles were married. In the Latin Vulgate's mulierem sororem and in the Greek tradition, the rendering was contested; some manuscripts and patristic citations (Tertullian, Jerome, Augustine in his stricter moods) leaned toward "a sister-woman" — meaning a female assistant or fellow-traveler, not a wife — supporting clerical celibacy. Modern Catholic NT scholarship has largely conceded the natural reading: Peter was married, the other apostles probably were too, and Paul's defense in 9:5 includes the right to marriage on the road. Trent's discipline of clerical celibacy is therefore disciplinary, not exegetical.

Apostolic poverty became a rallying point for Franciscan reform; 9:15-18 was a foundational text. Augustine's Sermon 46 and his De opere monachorum took up the tension: Paul models voluntary refusal, but the church may not require ministers to be unsupported. The Reformation generalized the worker-deserves-his-wages principle into the parish or diocesan support structure of Reformed and Anglican ecclesiology, while Anabaptist tradition (Schleitheim 1527, the Hutterites) followed the voluntary-refusal pattern more literally with bivocational lay ministry.

The "all things to all people" of 9:22 has driven the modern missiological tradition. Matteo Ricci's accommodation in Ming-dynasty China (the Chinese Rites question) and Roberto de Nobili's adoption of Brahmin practice in seventeenth-century Madurai both invoked Paul's principle. The Protestant translatability tradition (Andrew Walls, Lamin Sanneh, Whose Religion is Christianity?) reads it as the theological warrant for vernacular Bible translation and indigenous church planting. The Lausanne Covenant (1974) and the contextualization debates that followed worked the Pauline text hard. Stanley Hauerwas (Resident Aliens, 1989, with Willimon) pushed back: "all things to all people" is not the Constantinian church baptizing whatever the dominant culture serves; it is a missionary tactic from a position of weakness, and it stops short of what Paul names "the law of Christ" (9:21). D.A. Carson (Becoming Conversant with the Emerging Church, 2005) similarly warned against using 9:22 as warrant for unchecked cultural accommodation.

## Theological Intent

### Tension 1: Apostolic and ministerial pay

Should Christian ministers be paid?

**The institutional-support reading.** Paul affirms a right (exousia) — Torah, common practice, and dominical tradition all establish it. The Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran, and Reformed traditions have all built parish and diocesan structures around the principle that those who proclaim the gospel may legitimately live from it. The voluntary refusal Paul models is exemplary, not normative. To require all ministers to be unsupported is to impose Paul's personal vow on the universal church. Steelman: this reading takes seriously the chain of warrant Paul builds (vv. 7-14) and notices that Paul nowhere universalizes his refusal — he says "I have not made use of this right," not "no one should."

**The Anabaptist bivocational reading.** Paul's refusal is not just exemplary — it is paradigmatic. The hireling-pastor tradition (John 10:12-13 in patristic and Reformation polemic) and the Anabaptist communal economy both cite chapter 9 as warrant for lay ministry without paid clergy. The point of the personal example is to be imitated. Steelman: the chapter is structured as paradeigma (rhetorical example for imitation); Paul does not commend his refusal as eccentric but as gospel-shaped (9:23, "I do it all for the sake of the gospel").

**The mediating reading (most parishes in practice).** Both the right and the refusal stand. Paid ministry is normative; voluntary refusal is the standing critique that prevents paid ministry from becoming the patronage economy Paul is rejecting. The bivocational tentmaker is not the rule but the conscience-figure who keeps the rule honest. Most contemporary Western churches operate this way without articulating the theology.

The chapter's pressure runs against the prosperity-gospel abuses (paid minister as elite patron-figure) on one side and against the contempt-for-paid-ministry tradition on the other. Paul defends the right precisely so that the refusal of it can mean something. A right that is not real is not really being given up.

### Tension 2: Contextualization and the law of Christ

Where is the line between cultural translation and gospel betrayal?

**The strategic-accommodation reading (most missiology).** "Becoming all things" is the missionary tactic. The form of the gospel translates; its content does not. Walls, Sanneh, and the Lausanne movement read 9:19-23 as charter: cross every cultural boundary; baptize whatever may be baptized; translate the gospel into the host language. Steelman: this reading takes seriously how thoroughly Paul varied his practice (uncircumcised Titus in Galatians 2:3; circumcised Timothy in Acts 16:3) and how the New Testament itself dramatizes the early church's struggle with the Gentile question.

**The Hauerwasian critique.** "All things to all people" presupposes the church's cultural marginality, not its centrality. When the church accommodates from a position of cultural dominance — when it "becomes" something not for missionary winnowing but for institutional comfort — it inverts the Pauline logic. Steelman: Paul's "though not being myself under the law" (9:20) and "being under the law of Christ" (9:21) are the limits. The accommodation is bounded, not unbounded. There is something Paul will not become.

**The Boyarin reading.** Paul's strategic accommodation operates from within Jewish identity, not from above it. He does not imagine himself as the cosmopolitan free of all cultures, picking and choosing among them. He imagines himself as a Jew exercising a Jewishly-warranted flexibility for the sake of the Gentile mission. Steelman: this reading explains the parenthetical disclaimers (9:20, 9:21) better than the "Paul transcends Judaism" reading does. Paul never claims neutrality; he claims a specific kind of mission.

The chapter's own anchor at 9:21 — "not being outside the law of God but under the law of Christ" (ennomos Christou) — is the standing limit. There is something the becoming cannot reach. What that "law of Christ" specifies (cruciform love? the Sermon on the Mount? the Torah read Christologically?) is contested across the traditions. But Paul names a limit, and the chapter does not work without it.

N.T. Wright (*Paul for Everyone: 1 Corinthians*; *Paul and the Faithfulness of God*; *Paul: A Biography*) reads chapter 9 as Paul's living demonstration of the gospel he has just preached: the apostle who has every right — to support, to the company of a believing wife, to be honoured as a witness of the risen Lord — lays it all down "for the sake of the gospel" (9:23), and that self-emptying is precisely the cruciform shape of the Messiah being worked out in his own ministry. On Wright's reading "all things to all people" is missionary flexibility from below, not Constantinian accommodation from above; Paul becomes "weak" so that the "weak" may be won, and the bounded clause "under the Messiah's law" (9:21) keeps that flexibility tethered to the crucified Lord. The athletic peroration (9:24-27), for Wright, is the eschatological note: the present age is the training-ground, the "imperishable wreath" is the resurrection life of the age to come, and even the apostle runs as one who has not yet finished the race.

### Where this leaves us

The Corinthians had charged Paul with inconsistency: he tells the strong to yield while exercising his own apostolic authority. Paul's answer is to widen the frame. Yes, he has rights — multiple rights, Torah-warranted, Lord-commanded. He has refused them all. The refusal is not legalism (he is free); it is not weakness (he is an apostle); it is the apostolic shape of cruciformity. He is an example, in his refusal, of what he asked the strong-with-knowledge to be in chapter 8.

The athletic peroration is the second answer to the Corinthian charge. The Christian life is not the spectator's enjoyment of liberty. It is the contestant's discipline of the body. Paul's hypōpiazō — the boxer's punch under the eye — is shocking on purpose. The same apostle who proclaims freedom from law and freedom from earned merit also disciplines his body lest he himself be disqualified. The chapter holds together what later Christian traditions tried to hold separately: free grace and rigorous discipline; gospel privilege and the possibility of disqualification.

### One unresolved question

Adokimos in 9:27. Paul fears being disqualified after preaching to others. The Reformed tradition has wrestled with this verse for five centuries: how does the apostle's fear of disqualification fit perseverance of the saints? The Wesleyan and Catholic traditions have read it more naturally — yes, the apostle could fall. The Calvinist reading parses adokimos as a hypothetical impossible-in-fact, or as a fear about reward not salvation, or as the pastor's existential vigilance not a real possibility. The text gives us a fearful possibility that does not fit any of the systems neatly. Where exactly does Paul locate himself in his own salvation?

### The Thread

The strong must yield. Watch me yield. The whole chapter is exhibit A for chapter 8 — Paul writing his own apostolic autobiography to show what cruciform freedom looks like in practice. The pattern is rights known, rights refused, gospel served. The athletic image at the end pulls the lesson forward into the church's life. We are not free spectators savoring a show. We are contestants in a contest in which the prize is not perishable, the discipline is real, and even the apostle does not coast.
