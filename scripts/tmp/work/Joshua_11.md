# Joshua 11: The Burning of Hazor and the Limits of Total Victory

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Joshua 11 narrates the northern campaign against a coalition led by Jabin king of Hazor, a political confrontation that by any honest historical reckoning would have been the defining military event of Late Bronze Age Canaan. Hazor (Hebrew *Ḥāṣôr*, חָצוֹר, meaning "enclosure" or "fortified settlement") was not merely one city among many. At roughly eighty hectares — dwarfing Megiddo, Lachish, and every other Canaanite urban center — it supported a population estimated at fifteen to twenty thousand, making it the largest tell site in the southern Levant. The Amarna Letters (EA 227 and 228, fourteenth century BCE) preserve correspondence between "Abdi-Tirshi, king of Hazor" and the Egyptian pharaoh, and archives recovered from Hazor itself contain Akkadian administrative tablets confirming its role as a regional hegemon tied into the wider ANE diplomatic system. When the text calls Hazor "the head of all those kingdoms" (11:10), this is not hyperbole but a straightforward political description.

Yigael Yadin's excavations from 1955 to 1958 identified a dramatic Late Bronze IIB destruction layer dating roughly 1230–1200 BCE, with evidence of intense conflagration — mud-brick walls vitrified by heat reaching 1300°C. Amnon Ben-Tor's ongoing excavations at Tel Hazor since 1990 have confirmed and extended Yadin's picture. Ben-Tor's team found decapitated and mutilated royal statues of Canaanite and Egyptian origin in the destruction layer, evidence the excavators read as iconoclasm — someone who knew what the statues represented had taken pains to desecrate them before burning the palace. Ben-Tor has argued publicly that neither Egyptians nor Sea Peoples nor a rival Canaanite power fits the destruction pattern; Israelites remain for him the best candidate, though he concedes the evidence is circumstantial. The destruction of Hazor is the one item in Joshua's conquest narrative where the archaeological record is at its most suggestive, which is why the chapter carries disproportionate weight in debates over the conquest's historicity.

The "waters of Merom" (mê Mērôm, מֵי מֵרוֹם, 11:5, 7) where the battle was joined remain geographically disputed; most scholars identify the site with the Meiron region in Upper Galilee, the marshy terrain west of the Jordan sources. The chariot-and-horse coalition described in 11:4 — "very many horses and chariots" — fits the LB military milieu, where chariotry was the decisive arm. Joshua's response (11:6, 9) — to hamstring (ʿāqar, עָקַר) the horses and burn the chariots — is militarily puzzling. Hamstrung horses are useless as cavalry but could have been redistributed as draft animals; the act reads instead as ritual elimination. It also anticipates Deuteronomy 17:16's restriction against a king "multiplying horses," positioning Joshua as the anti-monarch who refuses to convert victory into the apparatus of empire.

Five Hebrew terms organize the chapter's theological freight. *Ḥāram* (חָרַם, "devote to destruction," "place under the ban," used in 11:11, 12, 20, 21) saturates the narrative and has no adequate English equivalent. It means something closer to "transfer absolutely to the sphere of the divine by destruction," implying an ancient Near Eastern practice attested also in the Mesha Stele (9th c. BCE), where King Mesha of Moab records *ḥrm* against the Israelite town of Nebo for Chemosh. *Šārap bāʾēš* (שָׂרַף בָּאֵשׁ, "burn with fire," 11:11, 13) is the technical formula for total destruction of sacred or impure objects. *Mamlākôt* (מַמְלָכוֹת, "kingdoms," 11:10), plural, underscores Hazor's paramount status. *ʿĂnāqîm* (עֲנָקִים, 11:21–22), the Anakim giants, connects to the scouts' report in Numbers 13:33 and to the Rephaim/Nephilim cluster (Gen 6; Deut 2–3) — a constellation English translations flatten into the generic "giants." *Qāšāh lēb* (הִקְשָׁה אֶת־לִבָּם, "harden their hearts," 11:20) deliberately echoes Pharaoh in Exodus, making the Canaanite kings into Egypt redivivus and retroactively extending the exodus logic into the land.

Authorship and dating are inseparable from the Deuteronomistic History (DtrH) hypothesis. Martin Noth (1943) argued that Joshua–2 Kings forms a single exilic composition shaped by Deuteronomic theology. Frank Moore Cross (1973) refined this into a dual redaction — Dtr1, a Josianic edition celebrating conquest as legitimation for Josiah's reforms, and Dtr2, an exilic supplement accommodating the fall of Judah. Joshua 11's triumphalism sits comfortably in Dtr1; its concessions (Gibeon excepted, Anakim survivals in Philistine cities) suggest earlier source material Dtr1 could not entirely suppress.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is theologized military narrative within the larger conquest cycle, shaped around a battle report that mirrors the southern campaign of chapter 10. The mirroring is deliberate: in chapter 10 Joshua defeats a southern coalition at Gibeon; in chapter 11 he defeats a northern coalition at Merom. Together they form a complete north-south typology of victory, whereupon the summary formula (11:16–23) closes the conquest narrative so the distribution material (chs. 13–21) can begin.

Several literary devices structure the chapter. The coalition list in 11:1–3 uses anaphora ("and to the king of… and to the kings who were in…") to build up scale — Jabin draws in Madon, Shimron, Achshaph, the northern hill country, the Arabah south of Chinneroth, the lowland, Naphoth-dor, Canaanites, Amorites, Hittites, Perizzites, Jebusites, and Hivites. The catalogue is both ethnographically comprehensive and rhetorically overwhelming, preparing for the reversal at verse 6. The simile "in number like the sand that is on the seashore" (11:4) is a signature Deuteronomistic metaphor used elsewhere for Israel's own multiplication (Gen 22:17; Josh 11:4 now inverts it toward the enemy). The oracle-fulfillment pattern in 11:6–9 — YHWH speaks, Joshua acts, the narrator confirms "just as the LORD said to him" — is the formal signature of the obedient Mosaic successor. Verse 15 makes this explicit with a triple-obedience formula: "Just as the LORD had commanded Moses his servant, so Moses commanded Joshua, and so Joshua did."

The summary in 11:16–23 contains the chapter's deepest literary problem. Verse 23 asserts that "Joshua took the whole land" and "the land had rest from war," a totalizing claim immediately complicated by Judges 1, which opens by listing in detail what remains unconquered. Richard Nelson and Trent Butler both read the summary as a deliberately artificial closure that the book's later chapters (especially 13:1) and Judges will unsettle. L. Daniel Hawk, in *Every Promise Fulfilled* (1991), argues the narrator knows this and uses the tension productively — the summary is not naive but honest about the theological claim over against the military fact. Gottwald, more skeptically, reads it as an ideological construct masking a much messier process of peasant-revolt emergence.

Textual variants are worth flagging. The LXX of Joshua is significantly shorter than the MT — Emanuel Tov estimates a 4–5% reduction overall — and the shorter readings often predate MT's expansions. 4QJosh^a from Qumran reorders material in the conquest section (most famously placing the Ebal altar earlier, at 8:30–35 in MT) and suggests fluidity in the text's transmission well into the Second Temple period. For Joshua 11 specifically, no major Qumran manuscript reorders the chapter, but the LXX's compactness in the coalition list hints that the Hebrew tradition was elaborated rather than condensed over time.

The chapter sits at the hinge of the book's two halves. Joshua 1–11 narrates conquest; Joshua 12–24 narrates distribution, closure, and covenant renewal. Chapter 11 is the last battle. Chapter 12 is its archive (the list of defeated kings). The literary function of 11's totalizing summary is to transform the narrative from verb to noun — from "conquering" to "conquered" — so that inheritance can begin.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canon Joshua 11 participates in the larger trajectory that begins with Abraham's land promise (Gen 12; 15) and ends provisionally here, with a land taken but not fully possessed. It is the pivot between the exodus-wilderness narrative (coming *toward* the land) and the judges-kings narrative (living *in* the land, poorly). The Anakim coda in 11:21–22 deliberately closes the loop with Numbers 13–14, where scouts' fear of these same giants condemned the wilderness generation to death. Their defeat here is the visible sign that the exodus has completed its arc.

**Jewish reading trajectory.** The Second Temple period saw productive expansion of the Joshua material. The Qumran *Apocryphon of Joshua* (4Q378–379) rewrites scenes with liturgical framing, emphasizing Joshua's covenantal role; it preserves fragments of speeches and prayers not in MT. Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.62–69) smooths the narrative for Greco-Roman readers, downplays *ḥerem* violence, and casts Joshua as a virtuous general in the Hellenistic mold. Philo allegorizes the Canaanites as vices to be extirpated from the soul, a move with enormous downstream influence on Christian readings. Ben Sira (Sir 46:1–6) frames Joshua as "great as his name," aligning him with Moses and the prophets.

Rabbinic tradition works the chapter's tensions carefully. *Mishnah Yadayim* and later *Megillah 6a–b* discuss the geographical categories of the chapter in the context of tithing and sabbatical-year halakhah — which territory counts as "the land" for religious obligations? The Babylonian Talmud (*Sanhedrin* 90b–91a) preserves a tradition that the Girgashites (not mentioned in Joshua 11's coalition but part of the fuller Canaanite list) voluntarily emigrated to Africa rather than fight, a midrashic softening of the *ḥerem*. Rashi on 11:20 takes the divine hardening seriously as an act of justice, arguing the Canaanites had been given centuries to repent (Gen 15:16) and refused. Ibn Ezra handles 11:22 (Anakim surviving in Gaza, Gath, Ashdod) with philological precision, noting that the text itself preserves what conquest could not eliminate — a realist strain within Jewish commentary. Ramban (Nachmanides) on the Torah's parallel commands argues the *ḥerem* was specifically tied to land-taking and does not translate into a general warrant; the mitzvah ended with the conquest. Radak reads Hazor's special treatment (the only city burned) as a measured response to its paramount political threat. Abarbanel, writing in the shadow of the Spanish Expulsion (1492), reads the chapter politically, interested in the mechanics of coalition warfare and what failed at Hazor. Maimonides' treatment in *Mishneh Torah* (*Hilkhot Melakhim* 6) codifies the seven Noahide laws as the minimum Canaanites could have accepted to be spared — a Jewish theology of moral responsibility that mitigates the apparent unconditionality of the *ḥerem*.

Modern Jewish readings run in several directions. Martin Buber (*Kingship of God*, 1932) read the conquest as a theocratic-political project against Canaanite kingship itself — not against Canaanites as a people but against the royal ideology Hazor represented. Jon Levenson (*The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*, 1993, and elsewhere) places the *ḥerem* within the larger ANE sacrificial economy and refuses to explain it away. James Kugel (*How to Read the Bible*, 2007) foregrounds the critical gap between what the ancient authors asserted and what actually happened. Moshe Weinfeld's *Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School* (1972) situates Joshua 11's rhetoric in the Josianic reform context. Nahum Sarna and Avivah Zornberg bring close literary readings to adjacent texts. Baruch Halpern (*The First Historians*, 1988) argues Dtr used older annalistic sources for the core of the conquest, including the Hazor destruction, even as the overall narrative is heavily shaped. Rachel Havrelock (*River Jordan*, 2011) reads the boundary rhetoric against modern Israeli-Palestinian territorial claims, showing how the chapter's totalizing language gets mobilized in contemporary politics.

**Christian reading trajectory.** Origen's twenty-six *Homilies on Joshua* (c. 240 CE) set the patristic template by reading the conquest as allegory for the soul's mortification of sin. Joshua 11's "devoting to destruction" becomes the radical uprooting of passions; Hazor's burning is the cleansing of the central vice. "Joshua-Jesus" — the name is identical in Greek — leads the soul into rest. Jerome follows Origen on the allegory while providing the Latin text that Western Christianity would receive. Augustine, in *Questions on the Heptateuch* and *City of God*, defends the *ḥerem* on the grounds that God, as author of life, may rescind it, and that the Canaanites' wickedness justified their judgment; he is already uncomfortable with the plain reading. John Chrysostom's preaching on Joshua emphasizes obedience (11:15) as the virtue modeled for the baptized. Theodoret of Cyrus offers a more grammatical reading that respects the historical sense without abandoning the christological.

Medieval Christian reading is dominated by Thomas Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, Q. 40 on war; Q. 100 on divine command) — his treatment of the *ḥerem* argues that God as dominus vitae may command killing that would otherwise be murder, but he treats this as a singular historical warrant, not a template. The *Glossa Ordinaria* preserves the Origenist allegory. Luther's Joshua preaching stresses law-gospel dialectic: the conquest shows law's severity, Christ fulfills it. Calvin's *Commentaries on Joshua* are more text-critical; he accepts the difficulty of 11:20's divine hardening and argues it does not vitiate human responsibility — the Canaanites' sin preceded and grounded the judgment. Menno Simons and the Anabaptists took a different path: because Christ's kingdom is not of this world, the conquest texts cannot be normative for Christian political action, which must be nonviolent.

Modern critical scholarship on Joshua 11 splits along models. The classical conquest school (W. F. Albright, G. E. Wright, Yigael Yadin) read the Hazor destruction as confirmation of the biblical account. The peaceful infiltration model (Albrecht Alt, Martin Noth) saw seminomadic Israelite settlement rather than military conquest. The peasant revolt model (George Mendenhall 1962; Norman Gottwald *The Tribes of Yahweh*, 1979) read the conquest as internal Canaanite social upheaval mythologized as external invasion. The indigenous emergence model (Israel Finkelstein *The Archaeology of the Israelite Settlement*, 1988; William Dever *Who Were the Early Israelites?*, 2003) argues Israel emerged from Canaanite highland populations through a socio-economic process, with destructions like Hazor's explained by other causes. Richard Nelson's *Joshua* commentary (1997) reads the book as Josianic-era ideology. Thomas Dozeman's *Joshua 1–12* (2015) gives the most thorough recent literary-historical treatment. Trent Butler's *Joshua* (Word, rev. ed. 2014) balances literary and historical concerns.

Walter Brueggemann (*The Land*, 1977 and 2002; *Divine Presence Amid Violence*, 2009) reads Joshua as land-theology that must be held alongside exile and return. Christopher Wright (*Old Testament Ethics for the People of God*, 2004) defends the historical specificity of the *ḥerem* against its use as ongoing warrant. John Goldingay (*Old Testament Theology*, vol. 1, 2003) and Richard Hess (*Joshua*, TOTC 1996) work hard at historical contextualization. Gordon Wenham and David Howard hold conservative-evangelical positions that affirm the narrative's historical core. Terence Fretheim emphasizes the moral complexity divine action creates. Ellen Davis's *Scripture, Culture, and Agriculture* (2008) reads the land chapters agrarian-ethically.

The reception history of the conquest narrative is itself part of its interpretation: from the Puritan settlement of New England onward, Joshua's campaigns have been invoked to underwrite the dispossession of indigenous peoples, a use that Brueggemann and Christopher Wright both flag as a misappropriation the text's own historical specificity should preclude. Lori Rowlett (*Joshua and the Rhetoric of Violence*, 1996) reads the chapter as Josianic propaganda that functioned to define internal Israelite identity through external opposition — the Canaanites were in part a literary construct.

Convergence and divergence: both Jewish and Christian traditions have long recognized that the *ḥerem* cannot simply be replicated as normative practice. Jewish interpretation tends to historicize the command as completed with the conquest; Christian interpretation has oscillated between allegorizing (Origen) and historicizing (Aquinas). They diverge over christology — Joshua as type of Christ has no analog in Jewish tradition — and over whether the chapter's totalizing geography supports any modern political project (Rashi's careful limitation versus dispensationalist maximalism).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Divine hardening and human responsibility (11:20).** The text asserts that it was YHWH's doing to harden the Canaanite kings' hearts so they would fight rather than seek peace and thus be destroyed. This is Pharaoh-language redeployed against the peoples of the land.

The Reformed position (Calvin, modern Calvinism) takes 11:20 as straightforwardly compatibilist. God's hardening does not override human agency but expresses prior human culpability (cf. Rom 1; Gen 15:16 on the Amorite iniquity "not yet complete"). The hardening is judicial, not arbitrary. Where this leaves us: serious moral agency in a universe finally governed by God's sovereign decree — and a conquest narrative that can be taken at its word.

The Catholic position (CCC §396–405 on the drama of sin; Aquinas on divine permission and command) distinguishes between God's antecedent will (that all be saved) and consequent will (that justice be done given actual choices). God does not positively cause sin but permits resistance and uses it. The *ḥerem* is a singular historical command, not a moral norm; the analogy of being (and of grace) prevents collapsing divine command into anything the state may now do. Where this leaves us: a canon with hard edges but bounded; the New Covenant's logic of mercy cannot be undone by Joshua's particular warrant.

The critical-historicist position (Nelson, Dozeman, Collins) steelmanned: 11:20 is not metaphysical reporting but ideological rhetoric. Dtr needs the Canaanite resistance to be total because he needs the destruction to be total; attributing their resistance to YHWH's hardening insulates the narrative from any suggestion that they might have made peace (as Gibeon did) or that Israel should have accepted such peace. The verse is less about theodicy than about closing the narrative against objections the text itself has raised. Where this leaves us: a chapter whose rhetoric reveals its own anxieties. The insistence that the hardening was divine admits that, without such hardening, the slaughter might not have been warranted — a concession embedded in the assertion.

**Tension 2: Total victory and persistent remnant (11:21–22 vs. 11:23, and the whole summary vs. Judges 1).** The chapter ends asserting that Joshua took the whole land while the same chapter notes Anakim remaining in Gaza, Gath, and Ashdod, and the next chapters will pile up "what remains."

The Wesleyan-Arminian reading (John Wesley's Notes; modern holiness theology) takes the tension as pedagogical: complete victory is both a real gift and a continuing task, anticipating the Christian life's tension between justification and sanctification. The Anakim remaining are the besetting sins that require ongoing engagement. Where this leaves us: a theology of progressive appropriation — the land is given in principle and taken in practice over time.

The Anabaptist reading (Menno Simons; John Howard Yoder, *The Politics of Jesus*, 1972; Yoder's late work on the original revolution in Joshua) finds in the incompleteness a divine check on any triumphalism. The summary's totalizing claim is chastened by the text's own acknowledgments; the kingdom Jesus inaugurates similarly refuses the logic of total military victory. Conquest cannot be the template for the church. Where this leaves us: a reading that privileges the chapter's concessions over its claims, and that refuses to make Joshua normative for Christian politics.

The Jewish rabbinic reading (Maimonides, Ramban, Abarbanel) treats the gap between promise and possession as Torah's realism. The land was given in principle but occupied in stages; the tribes had to do their own work (a point Judges makes explicit). The rabbinic tradition resists both the maximalist claim ("we have it all") and the minimalist denial ("we have nothing"); the land is real, bounded, and bears obligations. Where this leaves us: a sober middle path that takes the text's limits as seriously as its claims.

The Eastern Orthodox reading (following Chrysostom and the Cappadocians, forward through Zizioulas and Florovsky) treats the material more liturgically than polemically. The conquest is typological preparation for the eucharistic taking of the kingdom — a participation that is real but not yet consummated. Anakim remaining are eschatological residue. Where this leaves us: the tension is proleptic; what the text promises is what the liturgy rehearses and the eschaton completes.

Steelmanning the least intuitive reading: the critical one. Modern readers recoil from 11:20's divine hardening because it seems to strip the Canaanites of moral agency for the sake of the narrative's tidiness. But the alternative — that the Canaanites rationally chose to fight an overwhelming army whose God had dried up the Jordan and flattened Jericho — is even harder. The narrator's insistence on hardening is, paradoxically, a moral acknowledgment: absent some such explanation, the story of Gibeon (where the Hivites made peace) shows peace was available. The text itself names the cost of denying it.

*The ethical difficulty is real and does not resolve.* Paul Copan and Matthew Flannagan (*Did God Really Command Genocide?*, 2014) argue the language is ANE hyperbole, that "none left who breathed" is formulaic (cf. Egyptian and Assyrian royal inscriptions), and that demographic realities preclude literal reading — the same cities keep reappearing as populated. Eric Seibert (*Disturbing Divine Behavior*, 2009), Jerome Creach (*Violence in Scripture*, 2013), and John Collins (*Does the Bible Justify Violence?*, 2005) argue that even rhetorical hyperbole is morally damaging and that the text cannot be domesticated — it must be read *against* itself, with the New Testament's mercy trajectory functioning as canonical critique. Both positions deserve weight; neither makes the chapter comfortable.

**Unresolved question:** If the Canaanite kings' hearts were hardened by YHWH specifically so they would fight and be destroyed — rather than, like Gibeon, seek peace and live — what theological work is being done by the text's simultaneous commendation of Gibeon's peacemaking (Josh 9) as a covenant Israel must honor? Does the chapter ask us to read its own outcome as the best possible one, or to notice, with Gibeon in the rear-view mirror, that another outcome was theologically thinkable and the narrator knows it?

## The Thread

**Deuteronomy 7:1–6** — The *ḥerem* command Joshua 11:15 claims to fulfill. Reading Joshua 11 without Deuteronomy 7 makes the slaughter seem arbitrary; reading Deuteronomy 7 without Joshua 11 makes the command seem hypothetical. The two texts together form a single problem the canon refuses to dissolve.

**Judges 1:1–36** — The counter-inventory of what Joshua 11:23 claims was already done. The canonical juxtaposition forces the reader to hold the summary's theological claim alongside the historical particulars; neither erases the other.

**Numbers 13:27–33** — The Anakim report that paralyzed the wilderness generation. Joshua 11:21–22 closes that loop: the giants the spies feared are defeated, but not eliminated — they persist in the Philistine cities, setting up Goliath of Gath in 1 Samuel 17.

**Hebrews 4:8–11** — "If Joshua had given them rest, God would not have spoken of another day later on." The New Testament author reads Joshua 11:23's "rest from war" as penultimate — real but not final, a sign of a rest still to come.
