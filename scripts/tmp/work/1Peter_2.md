# 1 Peter 2: Living Stones, Royal Priesthood, and the Politics of the Sojourner

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 2 sharpens what chapter 1 established. The recipients — already named as elect exiles of the diaspora across Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia (1:1) — are now told that their corporate identity is *chosen race, royal priesthood, holy nation, a people for God's own possession* (2:9). And in the same chapter they are told to be subject to the emperor (2:13, 17), to honor governors (2:14), and — if they are slaves — to submit even to harsh masters (2:18). The juxtaposition is the chapter's theological pressure point and its historical signature.

Politically, the recipients live under provincial Roman administration that combined imperial cult, civic religion, and household-based social order. The Pliny-Trajan correspondence (Pliny, *Letters* 10.96-97, c. AD 112), addressed to the same region the letter targets, provides the clearest external picture of how Roman provincial governance interacted with Christian communities. Pliny was uncertain how to handle Christians; his procedure was to ask them three times whether they were Christians, and to execute those who confessed obstinately. Trajan's reply formalized a policy of *do not seek them out but punish those formally accused*. The friction is local, civic, and ad hoc — exactly the friction 2:12 ("when they speak against you as evildoers") and 2:15 ("ignorance of foolish people") presuppose.

Materially, the recipients are *paroikoi* (παροίκους, 2:11) — resident aliens, the legal-sociological category John H. Elliott analyzed in *A Home for the Homeless* (1981). *Paroikoi* in Greco-Roman cities had restricted civic privileges: limited rights to own land, restricted access to civic offices, heavier liturgical obligations. Stephen Mitchell's *Anatolia: Land, Men, and Gods in Asia Minor* (1993) documented the layered hierarchies of citizens, freedpersons, slaves, metics, and rural populations; Mark Wilson's work on the seven churches supplies the regional context. Peter's letter inscribes that already-marginal social status into a positive theological identity rather than promising escape from it.

David Balch's *Let Wives Be Submissive* (1981) argued that the household code starting at 2:13 reads as apologetic accommodation: by demonstrating Christian slaves, wives, and subjects met Greco-Roman civic standards, the community defused pagan slander. Elliott pushed back: the code is not assimilation but a *paradoxical* accommodation that preserves resident-alien identity. They disagree on whether the work is defensive or constitutive.

Authorship and dating remain contested in the same three-way split as for chapter 1:

- **Authentic Petrine** (Richard Bauckham, I. Howard Marshall, Thomas Schreiner, Karen Jobes, Donald Guthrie): Simon Peter the apostle writes through Silvanus (5:12) before AD 65; the social pressure is Neronian or pre-Neronian.
- **Pseudonymous** (Paul Achtemeier, *Hermeneia* 1996; John H. Elliott, *Anchor Bible*; Leonhard Goppelt; Ernest Best): the polished Greek, the developed ecclesiology, the "Babylon" code (5:13) read most naturally after AD 70, and the diffuse civic pressure matches a Pliny-era setting in the AD 80s-90s.
- **Mediating** (Peter Davids): a Petrine circle preserving the apostle's authority into a later stylistic dress.

The choice matters most for chapter 2 in interpreting 2:13-17. If Peter writes under Nero, "honor the emperor" (2:17) is breathtakingly costly counsel. If a Petrine circle writes in the Domitianic or post-Domitianic period, the same text reads differently in tone, though not in content.

Five Greek terms anchor chapter 2:

- **λίθος ζῶν** (*lithos zōn*, "living stone," 2:4-5) — the paradoxical genitive: a stone that lives. Christ is the living stone (2:4), and believers are *living stones* (plural, 2:5) being built into a spiritual house. The metaphor fuses the inert (stone) with the animate (life), and makes the temple a corporate body.
- **βασίλειον ἱεράτευμα** (*basileion hierateuma*, "royal priesthood," 2:9) — directly from LXX Exodus 19:6. The phrase is an Israel-defining title; Peter applies it to the predominantly Gentile diaspora recipients.
- **παροίκους** (*paroikous*, "sojourners," 2:11) — the resident-alien terminology, paired here with *parepidēmous* (exiles, sojourners-passing-through). The doubled language is sociologically dense.
- **ὑποτάγητε** (*hypotagēte*, "be subject," 2:13) — the aorist imperative passive of *hypotassō*, a key word for the household code (returning at 2:18, 3:1, 3:5, 5:5). It is voluntary self-ordering under, not coerced subjugation by.
- **ὑπογραμμόν** (*hypogrammon*, "example," 2:21) — literally a writing-copy or stencil, the model letters a child traces over to learn handwriting. Christ is the *hypogrammon* of suffering for righteousness; the believer traces over Christ's lines.

Archaeologically and textually: Pliny *Ep.* 10.96-97 on Bithynian Christians; Tacitus *Annals* 15.44 on the Neronian persecution and the Christians as scapegoats for the fire of Rome; Suetonius *Nero* 16 on the same. These do not mention 1 Peter directly but form the ambient documentary frame for "honor the emperor" and "suffering for doing good."

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The literary spine of chapter 2 is a *florilegium* — a chain or anthology of scriptural quotations stitched together to drive a single argument. 2:6-10 is one of the most concentrated examples of catena in the New Testament outside of Romans 9-11 and Hebrews. The chain is:

- **Isaiah 28:16** (cornerstone) — "Behold, I am laying in Zion a stone, a cornerstone chosen and precious" (2:6).
- **Psalm 118:22** (rejected stone) — "The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone" (2:7).
- **Isaiah 8:14** (stumbling stone) — "A stone of stumbling and a rock of offense" (2:8).
- **Exodus 19:6** (royal priesthood / holy nation) — "you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation" (2:9), with overlay from LXX Isaiah 43:20-21.
- **Hosea 2:23 / 1:9-10** (people / not-people) — "Once you were not a people, but now you are God's people; once you had not received mercy, but now you have received mercy" (2:10).

Each quotation is doing distinct work. The Isaiah 28 cornerstone establishes Christ's identity. Psalm 118 narrates rejection-then-vindication. Isaiah 8 turns the stone metaphor against unbelievers. Exodus 19:6 transfers Israel's foundational identity to the believing community. Hosea reverses status: Gentile non-people become God's people. The cumulative effect is to redefine the recipients — socially marginal, civically alien, ethnically mixed — as the eschatological people of God in continuity with Israel's covenant grammar.

Markus Bockmuehl and Richard Bauckham have shown that this catena is unlikely to be Peter's free composition; rather, it draws on an early Christian *testimonia* tradition — collections of christologically charged Old Testament passages that circulated in catechetical contexts. The stones-cluster appears with overlapping vocabulary in Romans 9:33 (Paul cites Isaiah 28:16 and 8:14 together) and at Qumran (4QFlor uses cornerstone imagery for the eschatological community). Peter is participating in a shared early-church practice of scriptural argumentation.

The chapter's second major literary movement (2:21-25) applies Isaiah 53 to Christ. The verbal echoes are dense: *hamartian ouk epoiēsen* (2:22) tracks LXX Isaiah 53:9; *tas hamartias hēmōn autos anēnegken en tō sōmati* (2:24) echoes Isaiah 53:12; *tō mōlōpi iathēte* (2:24, "by his wounds you have been healed") quotes LXX Isaiah 53:5 verbatim. Markus Bockmuehl, Richard Bauckham, and N. T. Wright have all noted that 1 Peter 2:21-25 is one of the New Testament's most extended applications of the Servant Song to Jesus — and it is doing so to ground a particular ethical posture (enduring unjust suffering by following Christ's *hypogrammon*).

Genre-wise, the household code beginning at 2:13 belongs to the *Haustafel* tradition shared with Ephesians 5-6, Colossians 3-4, the Pastoral Epistles, and Greco-Roman moralists like Aristotle (*Politics* I.1253b) and the Stoic Hierocles. The Christian Haustafeln are not invented from nothing; they engage a recognizable cultural form and modify it. Older liturgical hypotheses (Perdelwitz, Preisker, Cross) read 1:3-4:11 as a baptismal homily; most contemporary scholars (Achtemeier, Elliott, Jobes) reject the strong form but acknowledge the catechetical-paraenetic tone, especially in chapter 2's pivot from identity (2:1-10) to conduct (2:11-25).

Within the book's argument, chapter 1 established personal-and-corporate identity through new birth. Chapter 2 articulates the corporate identity in Israel-language (2:9-10) and immediately orients that identity outward into pagan civic order through 2:11-17 (general subjection, honor the emperor) and 2:18-25 (slaves and the suffering Christ). Chapter 3 will extend the household code to wives and husbands; chapter 4 returns to suffering as the dominant theme.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

**Patristic.** Polycarp's *To the Philippians* quotes 2:21-24 directly — Servant-Song Christology applied to suffering Christians. The *Martyrdom of Polycarp* echoes 2:21's "leaving you an example." Origen and Eusebius accepted Petrine authorship. Augustine read 2:21 (Christ as *hypogrammon*) as moral exemplar Christology while insisting — against the Pelagians — that the example presupposes grace.

**Medieval.** 2:9 ("royal priesthood") was used minimally. The medieval emphasis fell on the priesthood of Christ and the ordained priesthood. Thomas Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* III, q. 63) integrated the baptismal priesthood with sacramental orders, but the integration was hierarchical: lay priesthood is real but ordered toward and dependent on the ordained.

**Reformation.** Martin Luther's *Address to the Christian Nobility* (1520) seized on 2:9 to articulate the priesthood of all believers. *Every* baptized Christian is a priest; no sacerdotal mediator stands between believer and God for ordinary access. Luther retained pastoral office but denied that ordained clergy possess a different *kind* of access. John Calvin (*Institutes* IV.19.28) made the same move. The Catholic counter-articulation came at Vatican II: *Lumen Gentium* §10 distinguished baptismal and ministerial priesthood as differing "in essence and not only in degree." The Anglican via media held the two together with greater fluidity. Anabaptist traditions pressed Luther's logic into radical congregationalism. Eastern Orthodox theology articulates the royal priesthood eucharistically: the laity's *amen* in the Divine Liturgy is constitutive priestly act.

**Critical.** Achtemeier's *Hermeneia* (1996) supplies the field's most thorough socio-historical reading. Elliott's *Anchor Bible* commentary made *paroikoi* the master category. Goppelt's commentary (1978) emphasized the apocalyptic-eschatological frame. Feldmeier reads sojourner identity as organizing metaphor. Karen Jobes (*Baker Exegetical*, 2005) defends preserved-Petrine authorship. The letter has also become a frequent touchstone for theologies of diaspora and minority faithfulness — a faith that lives under empire by inhabiting its alien status rather than by mirroring imperial power.

**2:13-17 in Christian political ethics.** "Be subject... to the emperor" (2:13) has been wielded by Romans-13 quietists to underwrite non-resistance and by active resisters to mark its limits. Dietrich Bonhoeffer's *Ethics* and the Confessing Church's Barmen Declaration (1934) drew the Christological line: the church owes the state honor and obedience but never worship; where the state demands worship, obedience becomes idolatry. Martin Luther King Jr.'s *Letter from Birmingham Jail* (1963) developed parallel logic: unjust laws must be disobeyed, with willing acceptance of penalty as preserving witness. Catholic social teaching on conscience (CCC §§1782, 2242) holds the citizen "obliged in conscience not to follow directives... contrary to the demands of the moral order." The text has not yielded a single ethic; it has been the field on which Christian political ethics gets fought.

### Jewish Scholarly Engagement

The application of Israel's titles in 2:9-10 is the chapter's most theologically charged move for Jewish-Christian dialogue. *Chosen race* (LXX Isaiah 43:20-21), *royal priesthood* and *holy nation* (Exodus 19:6), *people for his own possession* (Deuteronomy 7:6), *people / not-people* (Hosea 2:23) — these are Israel-defining titles, transferred wholesale to a predominantly Gentile readership without any explicit theology of Israel's continuing covenant.

Daniel Boyarin (*Border Lines*, 2004), Pamela Eisenbaum, and Magnus Zetterholm have argued the late first century did not yet know a clean separation of Christianity from Judaism. 1 Peter 2:9-10 is among the texts pushing the parting in a supersessionist direction — the church *is* the chosen race, royal priesthood, holy nation, and what this means for ethnic Israel is left unstated. Some Jewish scholars read this as supersessionism par excellence; others, in dialogue with Christian scholarship reading Paul (especially Romans 9-11) as preserving Israel's irrevocable election, find a participational reading possible: Gentiles enter Israel's covenant grammar without displacing Israel's covenant.

Rabbinic diaspora ethics provide a parallel for 2:13-17. The principle *dina d'malkhuta dina* — "the law of the kingdom is law" (Babylonian Talmud *Bava Kamma* 113a) — articulated Jewish accommodation to Gentile rule through detailed casuistry over centuries. Peter's terse "honor the emperor" shares the underlying problem: how does a covenant minority live faithfully under a sovereignty not its own?

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What God is doing in chapter 2: God has called a people out of darkness into marvelous light (2:9), built them as living stones into a spiritual house with Christ as the cornerstone (2:4-5), made them a royal priesthood whose sacrifices are spiritual rather than animal (2:5), and oriented their corporate witness outward — into pagan civic order, into the Greco-Roman household, into the workplace of a slave under an unjust master — such that even there, by following the *hypogrammon* of the suffering Christ (2:21), they make God's mercy visible.

### Tension 1 — Royal priesthood: what does it mean and how does it relate to ordained ministry?

The single phrase *basileion hierateuma* (2:9) has done more work in dividing and shaping the Christian tradition than perhaps any other phrase in the letter. The word "priesthood" is loaded. It can mean: a status of sacrificial mediation, a status of access-to-God, a status of corporate worship, an office, a sacrament, a vocation. Different traditions hear different things in the phrase.

- **Reformation: priesthood of all believers.** Luther and Calvin read 2:9 as the constitutional charter of a Christian people in which every baptized believer has direct priestly access to God. Ordained ministry is functional, not ontological — Luther kept pastors, Calvin kept the fourfold office.
- **Catholic: ministerial vs. baptismal priesthood differ essentially.** *Lumen Gentium* §10 holds that the common priesthood of the faithful and the ministerial priesthood "differ from one another in essence and not only in degree." Both participate in Christ's priesthood; the ordained priest acts *in persona Christi capitis* in the eucharist in a way the baptized do not.
- **Anglican via media.** The Thirty-Nine Articles and historic Anglican episcopate hold priesthood of all believers and the three-fold ordained ministry without forcing a Roman-or-Reformed dichotomy.
- **Anabaptist radical congregationalism.** The radical Reformation pressed the logic further: every believer a priest, every congregation a complete church, no clerical hierarchy theologically necessary.
- **Eastern Orthodox eucharistic priesthood.** Orthodox theology articulates royal priesthood eucharistically — corporate, liturgical, constituted by the laity's *amen* in the Divine Liturgy.
- **Steelmanned: the Catholic ministerial-distinct position.** It is easy for Protestants to dismiss the Catholic distinction as residual clericalism. But the Catholic position responds to something real: the apostles ordain elders (Acts 14:23, 1 Timothy 4:14), and the early church's eucharistic and disciplinary practice — visible already in Ignatius and the *Didache* — operates with a recognizable office. If "every Christian is a priest" flattens to "every Christian is functionally interchangeable in liturgical roles," the New Testament's own pattern of ordered ministry is lost. The distinction-in-essence preserves both the universal priesthood and the ordered ministry.

**Where this leaves us:** The text affirms that the corporate community is a royal priesthood. It does not by itself adjudicate whether or how this priesthood relates to ordained ministry. Five hundred years of Reformation polemic and counter-polemic on this single phrase suggest the text is doing more theological work than any one tradition's reading captures.

### Tension 2 — "Be subject to every human institution" (2:13): when does Christian obedience to civil authority become complicity?

The instruction to be subject to the emperor (2:13, 17) and to honor governors (2:14) is, on its face, one of the New Testament's most direct affirmations of civil obedience. It sits alongside Romans 13:1-7 in the so-called "obedience texts." But the chapter itself supplies counter-pressure: 2:16's *as servants of God*, 2:11's *sojourners and exiles* who do not finally belong to the imperial order, and the entire frame of 2:21-25 in which the unjustly suffering Christ is the *hypogrammon*, not the unjustly punishing governor.

- **Romans 13 quietist reading.** A long tradition reads 2:13-17 with Romans 13 as a flat command of obedience. Some Lutheran "two kingdoms" theology, strands of evangelical political theology, and historical Christian establishments lean here: civil authority is from God; resistance is rebellion.
- **Bonhoeffer / Barmen / King.** Bonhoeffer's involvement in the plot against Hitler, the Confessing Church's Barmen Declaration (1934), and Martin Luther King Jr.'s *Letter from Birmingham Jail* draw the line: civil obedience is real, but Christological priority is more real. When the state demands what only God may receive, obedience becomes idolatry.
- **Catholic social teaching on conscience.** CCC §§1782 and 2242 hold that the citizen is obliged in conscience not to follow directives contrary to the moral order, and that armed resistance to oppressive authority can in extreme cases be legitimate.
- **Anabaptist nonresistance.** The Anabaptist tradition reads 2:13-17 with the Sermon on the Mount as warrant for nonresistance — neither active rebellion nor participation in coercive state functions. The witness is alterity, not engagement.
- **Theonomic / reconstructionist.** A small contemporary stream (Rushdoony, Bahnsen) reads obedience texts within biblical-law normativity: obedience is owed *only insofar as* civil authority conforms to biblical law.
- **Steelmanned: Anabaptist nonresistance.** Easy to caricature as politically irresponsible, the Anabaptist position deserves a fairer reading. Its insight: 2:21-25's *hypogrammon* — Christ who when reviled did not revile in return — is the immediate Christological context for "honor the emperor." If the suffering Christ is the model, Christian political ethics cannot terminate in the seizure of coercive power, because that path was explicitly *not* Christ's. The refusal to participate in the magistrate's sword is not flight from public life but preservation of a witness the world cannot otherwise see — a community whose loyalty cannot be coerced because its hope is elsewhere. To the Anabaptist, the political engagement that loses the *hypogrammon* loses the gospel.

**Where this leaves us:** "Honor the emperor" and "as servants of God" are both in the text, in the same chapter, and the Christological pressure of 2:21-25 will not let any of the political readings rest comfortably. The chapter does not authorize Christian quietism; it does not authorize Christian seizure of power; it does not even cleanly authorize Christian civil disobedience as a program. It plants a contradiction in the ground and waits for the church to live in it.

### Closing question

If your social location — slave, sojourner, alien, marginal — is precisely what makes you a *living stone* in the spiritual house God is building, then what does it mean that the cultural and political programs most readily on offer to you (assimilation, withdrawal, counter-empire) all promise to remove the very condition the chapter says is constitutive of your priesthood?

## The Thread

- **Exodus 19:5-6** — the priesthood-and-holy-nation foundation Peter quotes in 2:9. The original context is Sinai, the covenant constitution of Israel. Peter's transposition of these titles to a Gentile-majority diaspora is the chapter's most theologically charged move and the engine of two thousand years of Jewish-Christian and intra-Christian argument.
- **Isaiah 53** — the suffering servant Christology Peter applies to Jesus in 2:21-25. The verbal density of the echo (he committed no sin, he bore our sins, by his wounds you have been healed) makes 1 Peter 2 one of the New Testament's most extended servant-song readings of Christ.
- **Romans 13:1-7** — the parallel and contested civic-obedience passage. Paul's "the authorities that exist have been instituted by God" sits beside Peter's "be subject to every human institution." Read together, they have shaped Christian political ethics; read against each other, they expose the texts' refusal to yield a single program.
- **Ephesians 2:19-22** — the parallel "household of God" / cornerstone metaphor: "you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God, built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the cornerstone." Ephesians and 1 Peter share the architectural metaphor; the Petrine version sharpens it with the *living* stones plural and the priestly function of the structure.
