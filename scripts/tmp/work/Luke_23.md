# Luke 23: The Trial, the Cross, and the Innocent King

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The trial of Jesus moves through three Roman-period spaces in Jerusalem: Pilate's residence (the **Praetorium**), Herod Antipas's lodging while in the city, and Golgotha — the execution ground outside the walls. The location of the Praetorium is genuinely disputed. The traditional Christian site since the Byzantine period is the **Antonia Fortress** at the northwestern corner of the Temple Mount, where the Convent of the Sisters of Zion preserves a striking pavement (the "Lithostrotos") with games scratched into the flagstones — though **Pierre Benoit**'s 1952 study and subsequent excavations dated this pavement to the Hadrianic Aelia Capitolina rebuild (135 CE), not to Pilate's time. The competing identification, defended by **Shimon Gibson** (*The Final Days of Jesus*, 2009), **Joan Taylor**, and now broadly accepted by archaeology, places Pilate's Jerusalem residence at the **Herodian Palace** on the western hill, near today's Citadel and Jaffa Gate. Josephus tells us the Roman prefects regularly stayed in Herod's old palace when in Jerusalem (*War* 2.301; *Antiquities* 18.55–59). Recent excavations beneath the Tower of David have exposed Herodian-era walls and foundations consistent with this identification.

**Pontius Pilate** served as prefect of Judea from 26 to 36 CE under Tiberius. The 1961 discovery of the **Pilate Inscription** at Caesarea Maritima — a limestone block with the partial Latin inscription "[—]TIBERIEUM [—PO]NTIUS PILATUS [PRAEF]ECTUS IUDA[EA]E" — was the first direct epigraphic confirmation of Pilate's existence and corrected the title from "procurator" (Tacitus's later usage) to "prefect" (Pilate's actual title). A bronze ring excavated at Herodion in the 1960s and recently re-read in 2018 by Israeli scholars bears the Greek "of Pilatus" — possibly a sealing ring belonging to the prefect or to one of his agents. **Philo** (*Legation to Gaius* 38) and **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 18.55–89; *War* 2.169–177) both portray Pilate as harsh and provocative — a portrait that complicates the Lukan portrayal of a Pilate who repeatedly tries to release Jesus. Modern scholars have read the Lukan portrait as either historically grounded (Pilate's caution toward Jesus reflecting genuine political hesitation) or as Lukan apologetic (deflecting Roman blame onto the Judean leadership for an audience that included Roman readers).

**Herod Antipas** ruled Galilee and Perea as tetrarch from 4 BCE to 39 CE — he is the same Antipas who beheaded John the Baptist (Luke 9:9) and the only Herod who appears in Luke's passion. His Jerusalem lodging during festivals was almost certainly the old Hasmonean palace, southwest of the Temple Mount. The unique Lukan trial-before-Antipas (23:6–12) has been doubted as historical by some scholars (Goguel, Bultmann) but defended by others (Marshall, Bock, Joel Green) on the plausibility of Roman jurisdictional courtesy.

**Golgotha** — Aramaic *gulgaltā*, "place of the skull" — is identified by the strongest scholarly consensus with the site now occupied by the **Church of the Holy Sepulchre**. The site lay outside the city walls of Jesus's day (the so-called "Second Wall" ran east of it; the "Third Wall" extending the city northward was built by Agrippa I in 41–44 CE). Helena's church complex of the 320s–330s preserved a tradition that the site had been venerated by Jewish-Christians from the first century and intentionally desecrated by Hadrian when he built a Venus temple over it in the 130s. The 1960s excavations directed by **Virgilio Corbo** and the more recent restoration work confirm a first-century quarry, with rock-cut tombs of the period and a small outcrop that may have been the execution mound. The competing **Garden Tomb** site north of Damascus Gate, popularized by General Charles Gordon in 1883, has no early Christian tradition behind it; archaeology dates its tombs to the Iron Age, centuries before Jesus.

The mechanics of crucifixion are illuminated by the 1968 discovery at **Giv'at ha-Mivtar** of the heel bone (calcaneum) of Yehohanan ben Hagkol, pierced by an iron nail with olive-wood fragments still attached. This remains the only direct osteoarchaeological evidence of a first-century crucifixion victim. Studies by **Nicu Haas** and the revised analysis by **Joseph Zias** and **Eliezer Sekeles** showed that the nail had been driven sideways through both heel bones, with arms either bound or nailed through the wrists. The victim's legs had not been broken (suggesting he died of suffocation or blood loss before the *crurifragium*), but the bones suggested a young man in his twenties from a Jerusalem family of some means — a sobering parallel to the Lukan narrative.

Greek terms central to the chapter: ***basileus tōn Ioudaiōn*** (βασιλεὺς τῶν Ἰουδαίων, "King of the Jews," 23:3, 37, 38) — the *titulus* of the cross. ***Anaitios*** (ἀναίτιος, "innocent / not guilty") in 23:47 — the centurion's verdict; ***dikaios*** (δίκαιος, "righteous") is the parallel reading in some manuscripts. ***Paradeisos*** (παράδεισος, "paradise," 23:43) — Persian loan-word, from Old Persian *pairi-daēza* (an enclosed garden); used in the LXX of Eden (Genesis 2:8), in 2 Corinthians 12:4, and in Revelation 2:7. ***Eis cheiras sou paratithemai to pneuma mou*** (εἰς χεῖράς σου παρατίθεμαι τὸ πνεῦμά μου, "into your hands I commit my spirit," 23:46) — citing Psalm 31:5 LXX.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Luke 23 contains the highest concentration of Lukan distinctive material in the entire passion. Five major distinctives shape the chapter.

**First, the trial before Herod Antipas (23:6–12)** is found in no other Gospel. Pilate, hearing that Jesus is a Galilean, sends him to Antipas's jurisdiction — a gesture of jurisdictional propriety that may also be Pilate's attempt to deflect responsibility. Antipas, who has long wanted to see Jesus (cf. 9:7–9), questions him; Jesus is silent (in deliberate echo of Isa 53:7); Antipas's soldiers mock and dress him in *esthēta lampran* — splendid / shining clothing — and send him back. The closing comment — "Herod and Pilate became friends with each other that very day, for before this they had been at enmity" (23:12) — has been read by **Joel Green** (NICNT) as a Lukan-Acts thematic anticipation of the apostolic preaching that "Herod and Pontius Pilate, with the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel, gathered together" against Jesus (Acts 4:27, citing Psalm 2). The passage also fulfills the chronologically prior anticipation in Luke 13:31–33 (Herod's threat) and 9:9 (Herod's curiosity).

**Second, Pilate declares Jesus innocent three times** (23:4, 14, 22). This is uniquely Lukan emphasis: each declaration grows in formality and explicitness. The first ("I find no guilt in this man") follows the initial questioning; the second ("Neither I nor Herod found him guilty of any of your charges") follows Antipas's referral; the third ("Why? What evil has he done? I have found in him no guilt deserving death") immediately precedes the verdict. **Raymond Brown** in *The Death of the Messiah* analyzed this triple-declaration as Lukan-apologetic (deflecting Roman blame for a Roman audience) and as Lukan-theological (underscoring Jesus's innocence as the central Lukan-passion truth, finally vindicated by the centurion in 23:47). The Lukan emphasis on innocence creates the chapter's peculiar tone: Jesus is killed in full juridical exposure of his innocence.

**Third, the daughters of Jerusalem (23:27–31)** — uniquely Lukan — extends Jesus's ministry of prophetic word to the very route of execution. "**Weep not for me, but weep for yourselves and for your children... if they do these things when the wood is green, what will happen when it is dry?**" The saying is dense with Old Testament allusion: Hosea 10:8 ("they shall say to the mountains, 'Cover us'") underlies 23:30. **Joel Green** reads the saying as a final Lukan prophetic warning about the coming destruction of Jerusalem (the "wood when it is dry" interpreted as the city itself in 70 CE). The juxtaposition — women lament *Jesus*, Jesus laments *the city* — is theologically programmatic: the cross is not a private tragedy but the indictment of a system bearing toward judgment.

**Fourth, the two criminals (23:39–43)** is uniquely Lukan. Mark 15:32 and Matthew 27:44 have *both* criminals revile Jesus. Luke alone records the second criminal's rebuke ("we are receiving the due reward of our deeds; but this man has done nothing wrong") and his request ("Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom") — and Jesus's promise: "**Truly, I say to you, today you will be with me in paradise**" (23:43). The phrase "***sēmeron met' emou esē en tō paradeisō***" (Σήμερον μετ' ἐμοῦ ἔσῃ ἐν τῷ παραδείσῳ) is distinctively Lukan in its theological compression: ***sēmeron*** (today) — Lukan keyword (cf. 2:11; 4:21; 19:9) — locates salvation in the immediate present; ***paradeisos*** invokes the Persian royal-garden image, the LXX Eden, and the Jewish apocalyptic intermediate state of the righteous (1 Enoch 60:8, 23; 4 Ezra 7:36; 2 Cor 12:4; Rev 2:7).

**Fifth, the dying word "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit" (23:46)** is uniquely Lukan, displacing the Markan-Matthean cry of dereliction ("My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" — Mark 15:34; Matt 27:46). Luke's quotation of Psalm 31:5 LXX positions Jesus's death as confident filial trust rather than abandoned anguish. The Christological implications are profound and contested — see Lens 4. The other Lukan distinctive at the cross — "**Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do**" (23:34) — is **textually disputed**: omitted from P75, Vaticanus, Sinaiticus (original hand), Codex Bezae, Old Syriac; included in the corrector of Sinaiticus, Codex Alexandrinus, the majority text, Old Latin, and most patristic citations. **Bart Ehrman** has argued the words are a second-century addition expressing emerging Christian universalism; **Joel Green** and **Bovon** are more cautiously inclined to retain them; **Metzger**'s *Textual Commentary* gave the omission an "A" rating, considering them probably not original. Modern critical editions (NA28, ESV) print the words with double brackets.

The centurion's confession (23:47) is itself Lukan-distinctive in wording: where Mark 15:39 and Matthew 27:54 read "Truly this was the Son of God," Luke has "Certainly this man was *dikaios*" (***dikaios*** can mean "righteous" or "innocent"). The Lukan substitution rounds out the chapter's innocence-theme: Pilate, Herod, and now the centurion all testify that Jesus dies an innocent man. **Raymond Brown** treated this as deliberate Lukan composition; **Fitzmyer** as theological-judicial vindication.

**Joseph of Arimathea (23:50–53)** receives a uniquely Lukan characterization: "a good and righteous man, who had not consented to their decision and action, and was looking for the kingdom of God." Luke alone explicitly dissociates Joseph from the Sanhedrin's verdict — preserving the conscience of one righteous insider.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Lukan passion threads together half a dozen Old Testament textures. Psalm 22 underlies the casting of lots (23:34, citing Ps 22:18) and the mocking onlookers (23:35–37 echoes Ps 22:7–8). Psalm 31:5 supplies the dying word (23:46). Isaiah 53 underlies the silence before Antipas (23:9 and Isa 53:7), the numbering with transgressors (Luke 22:37 / Isa 53:12), and the centurion's verdict of innocence. Hosea 10:8 underlies the daughters-of-Jerusalem oracle (23:30). Psalm 2 underlies the gathering of "Herod and Pilate, the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel" (Acts 4:25–27 reads the Lukan trial scene through Psalm 2).

The sun's failing (23:45) and the temple veil's tearing (23:45) participate in the apocalyptic-prophetic image-bank: cosmic darkness as the sign of divine judgment (Amos 8:9; Joel 2:10, 31), the temple's loss of separating-mediation (cf. Mark's reading, where the *katapetasma* may be the inner veil before the Holy of Holies; **Daniel Gurtner**, *The Torn Veil*, has argued for this against the older outer-veil reading).

Patristic readings of the cross are extensive. **Origen** (*Commentary on Matthew* 12.25; fragments on Luke) read the seven last words as a single dramatic-theological texture, emphasizing the prayer for the executioners as proof of Christ's divine love. **Ambrose** in *Exposition of Luke* 10.105–129 worked the daughters-of-Jerusalem oracle into a typology of the church as the new Jerusalem. **John Chrysostom**'s *Homilies on Matthew* 87–88 (parallel material) developed the contrast between the two criminals as a paradigm of the two responses to Christ. **Cyril of Alexandria**'s *Commentary on Luke* (Homilies 151–154) read "today you will be with me in paradise" as foundational for the Christian doctrine of the intermediate state. **Augustine** preached on this saying repeatedly; in *Sermon* 232 and *Letter* 187 he developed the theme of the good thief as the first beneficiary of Christ's redemption, having stolen even paradise. **Aquinas** (*Catena Aurea*; *Summa* III q. 46–52) systematized the medieval theology of the cross, the descent to the dead, and the resurrection.

The Reformation engagement is particularly rich. **Luther**'s sermons on the Passion (especially the 1525 Holy Week series and the *Operationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 22) wrestled with the contrast between Mark/Matthew's cry of dereliction and Luke's "Father, into your hands" — Luther refused to soften the dereliction, but found in the Lukan saying the deeper trust that *holds even within* the dereliction. **Calvin**'s *Harmony of the Evangelists* on Luke 23 is masterful at the same point, refusing to harmonize the four passion-Christologies into a smooth surface.

In modern theology, the cross-narrative has carried enormous freight. **Karl Barth** (*Church Dogmatics* IV/1 §59, "The Judge Judged in Our Place") reads the trial scene as the central Christological event: the innocent one stands in the place of the guilty, Pilate's verdict ("I find no guilt") is the divine verdict on humanity-in-Christ, and the cross is the carrying-out of the substitutionary judgment. **Hans Urs von Balthasar**'s *Mysterium Paschale* reads Holy Saturday as the descent into the radical absence of God, with the Lukan filial trust ("into your hands") and the Markan dereliction ("why have you forsaken me") as two sides of the single dramatic abyss. **Jürgen Moltmann**'s *The Crucified God* (1972) takes Mark's dereliction as the foundational event but engages the Lukan saying as the pneumatological hinge — the Spirit who is "committed" to the Father is the Spirit who will be poured out at Pentecost. **N.T. Wright**'s *The Day the Revolution Began* (2016) reads the Lukan passion as the inauguration of the new exodus, with the criminal-on-the-cross as the first inhabitant of the new creation.

Among major Lukan commentators: **Joseph Fitzmyer** (Anchor Bible) is the gold standard. **Joel Green** (NICNT) is theologically rich, especially on the daughters-of-Jerusalem and the Lukan innocence-emphasis. **John Nolland** (Word) is meticulous. **Darrell Bock** (BECNT) defends a conservative-evangelical reading. **I. Howard Marshall** (NIGTC) is philologically careful. **Luke Timothy Johnson** (Sacra Pagina) reads the chapter as Lukan compositional craft. **François Bovon** (Hermeneia) is invaluable on patristic reception. **Raymond Brown**'s *The Death of the Messiah* (1994) — two volumes, 1,608 pages — is the foundational comparative study of all four passion narratives. **Markus Bockmuehl**'s *Simon Peter in Scripture and Memory* engages the Petrine layers.

Jewish readings: **Geza Vermes** (*The Passion*) and **Amy-Jill Levine** have repeatedly cautioned against reading the Lukan passion as anti-Jewish. The Lukan emphasis on the leadership's responsibility, **Levine** notes, has been weaponized in anti-Jewish polemic for two millennia, but the text itself preserves multiple righteous Jewish characters (Joseph of Arimathea, the daughters of Jerusalem, the women from Galilee). **E.P. Sanders** (*Jesus and Judaism*; *The Historical Figure of Jesus*) defends the basic historical core: Jesus's temple action and apocalyptic claims made him a credible threat to the Jerusalem leadership; the Roman prefect made the final decision; the trial scenes are theologized but not fabricated. **Pinchas Lapide**'s *The Resurrection of Jesus: A Jewish Perspective* and **David Flusser**'s work on the trial both read the Lukan account as broadly historically anchored but theologically shaped.

Mediterranean and contextual readings: **Kenneth Bailey**'s *The Cross and the Prodigal* recovers the Mediterranean honor-shame texture of the public mockery and the centurion's startling acclamation. **Justo González**'s Lukan commentary reads the cross within Luke's pervasive theme of divine reversal — the executed criminal, not the chief priests, ends the chapter bound to the King's destiny.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Why does Luke replace Mark's cry of dereliction with "Father, into your hands"?** A first position holds that Luke is theologically softening: smoothing the offense of a forsaken Christ for an audience uncomfortable with the dereliction. **Bultmann** read the Lukan substitution as part of his broader thesis that Luke is the most "Hellenized" of the Synoptics, retreating from Markan apocalyptic. A second position, the dominant patristic and Reformed reading, holds that Luke is theologically deepening, not softening: the Lukan saying preserves the filial trust that runs through the deepest abyss; it does not deny the dereliction but holds it within the Father-Son communion. **Calvin** on Luke 23 makes precisely this move. A third position, developed by **Hans Urs von Balthasar** in *Mysterium Paschale*, reads the four Gospel passions as four legitimate angles on a single mystery: Mark gives the abandonment, Matthew the cosmic judgment, Luke the filial trust, John the consummated love. None is reducible to the others; each is needed to glimpse the whole. Steelmanning each: the softening reading takes seriously the genuine Lukan literary-theological distinctives; the deepening reading takes seriously the Lukan-Petrine and Lukan-Stephen parallels (Acts 7:59, "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit") that show Luke as the evangelist of confident Christian dying; the dramatic-multiplicity reading takes seriously the canonical fact of four irreducibly different Gospels. *Where this leaves us:* Luke's substitution is theologically substantive, not merely cosmetic. The Lukan Christ does not flinch from the cross, but he meets it as the obedient son who has prayed in Gethsemane "not my will, but yours" and now enacts that prayer in the dying breath. The Markan dereliction and the Lukan trust are not contradictories but complementaries: the former shows what is borne, the latter shows how it is borne.

**Tension 2: What is "paradise" in 23:43, and what is the state of the dead between death and resurrection?** A first position, the Catholic doctrine of the intermediate state developed by **Aquinas** (*Summa* III Suppl. q. 69; *Summa Contra Gentiles* IV.91) and articulated at the Council of Florence (1439) and Trent, distinguishes paradise (the state of the saved souls), purgatory (the state of those undergoing purification), and limbo (the state of the unbaptized infants and Old Testament righteous prior to Christ's descent). The good thief, on this reading, goes immediately to paradise because his faith is perfect at the moment of death. A second position, the Eastern Orthodox doctrine, sees paradise as the place where the souls of the righteous await the general resurrection in the presence of God, but with the consummation still future at the parousia. A third position, the Reformed reading (Calvin, *Institutes* III.25 and *Psychopannychia*), holds that the saved go immediately to a conscious presence-with-Christ at death — "today you will be with me" being read as conclusive against soul-sleep theories. A fourth position, the soul-sleep view defended by some Anabaptists, by Luther occasionally in his early period, and by modern Seventh-day Adventists, takes "today" as referring to the moment of the criminal's awareness rather than to a continuous conscious state, with the next conscious moment being the resurrection. A fifth position, the contemporary "post-mortem encounter with Christ" view developed by **N.T. Wright** in *Surprised by Hope*, reads paradise as a temporary intermediate state of conscious presence with Christ awaiting the final bodily resurrection — broadly aligned with the Reformed reading but with stronger emphasis on the future consummation. Steelmanning each: Catholic theology takes seriously the church's lived practice of prayer for the dead; Orthodox theology takes seriously the resurrection-as-consummation; Reformed theology takes seriously the immediacy of the Lukan "today"; soul-sleep takes seriously the Pauline language of "those who have fallen asleep"; Wright's reading takes seriously both the immediacy of the saying and the futurity of bodily resurrection. *Where this leaves us:* the Lukan promise binds the criminal to Christ's own destiny — he will be where Christ is, and that is enough. The fine-grained metaphysics of the intermediate state is underdetermined by the saying itself; Luke is making a Christological promise more than a metaphysical proposition.

One unresolved question: if the textual status of "Father, forgive them" (23:34a) is genuinely uncertain, what does it mean that the church has read this saying as foundational to Christian forgiveness theology for nearly two millennia, even if it may not be Lukan-original? The reception history is theologically real even if the textual base is fragile. The saying expresses something true about the Lukan Jesus that may or may not have been written by Luke himself.

## The Thread

The verb ***paradidōmi*** that has run through the passion (22:4, 6, 21, 22, 48; 23:25 — "he delivered Jesus over to their will") will return at 24:7, 20 in the women's and Cleopas's recounting, completing the chain. The Lukan innocence-theme (23:4, 14, 22, 47) anticipates the apostolic preaching in Acts that will repeatedly insist on Jesus's righteousness (Acts 3:14; 7:52; 22:14 — *the Righteous One*, ***ho dikaios***). The dying prayer "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit" anticipates Stephen's "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit" at Acts 7:59 — Luke's deliberate Stephen-Christ parallelism. And the women from Galilee who stand watching at a distance (23:49) and then prepare spices (23:55–56) become the first witnesses to the resurrection in the very next scene — the chapter that proclaims the temple-veil torn ends with the Sabbath rest "according to the commandment," waiting for the morning that will undo all of it.
