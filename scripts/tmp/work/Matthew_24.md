# Matthew 24: The Olivet Discourse and the Problem of Time

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Matthew 24 opens on the **Mount of Olives**, looking westward across the Kidron Valley to the Temple Mount. The disciples have just emerged from the Temple courts; they pause on the western slope of the Mount and turn back to admire what is widely considered the architectural wonder of the Greco-Roman East. Josephus (*Wars* 5.5.6) describes the Temple as "covered on every side with massive plates of gold" that, when caught by the rising sun, "shone with a fiery splendor that those who tried to look upon it had to turn their eyes away." Some of the foundation stones in the southwestern corner — recovered from the destruction debris excavated by Benjamin Mazar in the 1960s — measure over forty feet long and weigh more than five hundred tons. They are still in situ. Jesus's prediction that "not one stone will be left upon another" (v. 2) is, as a literal description of what would happen forty years later, devastatingly precise: when the Romans under Titus took the city in August 70 CE and sacked the Temple, the Tenth Legion (Fretensis) systematically dismantled the platform structures and pushed the stones into the streets below. Those tumbled stones, lying in heaps at the base of the southern Temple wall, are visible today.

The disciples' question (v. 3) is **doubled** — distinctively Matthean compared to Mark 13:4. Mark has only "when will these things be, and what will be the sign?" Matthew adds "of your *coming* (***parousia***, παρουσία) and of the *end of the age* (***synteleia tou aiōnos***, συντέλεια τοῦ αἰῶνος)." This Matthean expansion is decisive for the chapter's interpretation: it explicitly links the Temple's destruction with eschatological consummation, and it introduces *parousia* — the Greek term for an emperor's official "arrival" or "presence" at a city — as Matthew's signature word for Christ's return (occurring four times in this chapter alone: vv. 3, 27, 37, 39). The Mount of Olives setting itself carries eschatological weight: **Zechariah 14:4** prophesied that on the Day of YHWH "his feet shall stand on the Mount of Olives... and the Mount of Olives shall be split in two."

The historical setting of the prediction's fulfillment matters. The **First Jewish-Roman War** (66-73 CE) began with revolts in Caesarea and Jerusalem, escalated through Vespasian's campaigns in Galilee in 67-68, and culminated in Titus's siege of Jerusalem in spring-summer 70. Josephus (*Wars* 5-6) — an eyewitness — provides the detailed account: the destruction of the Temple on the 9th or 10th of Av (August 4-5, 70 CE), the burning of the upper city, the slaughter of the population. The **fall of Masada** in 73 CE closed the war. **Most modern critical scholarship dates Matthew between 80 and 90 CE**, meaning the gospel was composed *after* the Temple's destruction — a context that bears directly on chapter 24's interpretation.

The **abomination of desolation** (v. 15) — Greek ***bdélygma tēs erēmōseōs*** (βδέλυγμα τῆς ἐρημώσεως) — translates Hebrew *šiqqûṣ šōmēm* from **Daniel 9:27, 11:31, 12:11**. The phrase originally referred to the desecration of the Temple by **Antiochus IV Epiphanes** in 167 BCE, who erected an altar to Olympian Zeus over the altar of burnt offering and sacrificed pigs upon it (1 Maccabees 1:54-59; Josephus, *Antiquities* 12.5.4). For first-century Jews the phrase had been reactivated in 39-41 CE when **Caligula** ordered his statue erected in the Temple — a crisis only averted by his assassination (Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.8). By the time of Matthew's writing, the question was whether the prophecy now referred to Roman desecration in 66-70 CE (the Zealot occupation of the Temple, Titus's army standing on the platform, the imperial standards bearing pagan images) or to a future eschatological figure.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter. ***Parousia*** (παρουσία) — "coming/arrival/presence," appearing in Matt 24:3, 27, 37, 39 and elsewhere only in this gospel. The technical sense in Hellenistic Greek was the formal arrival of a king, emperor, or god at a city. ***Synteleia tou aiōnos*** (συντέλεια τοῦ αἰῶνος) — "end/consummation of the age" — Matthew's signature eschatological idiom, occurring at 13:39, 13:40, 13:49, 24:3, and 28:20. ***Bdélygma tēs erēmōseōs*** (βδέλυγμα τῆς ἐρημώσεως) — "abomination of desolation," from Daniel. ***Hē genea hautē*** (ἡ γενεὰ αὕτη, v. 34) — "this generation" — the most contested phrase in the chapter, meaning either the contemporary generation (40-year duration) or the human race or a specific eschatological generation. ***Grēgoreite*** (γρηγορεῖτε, v. 42) — "stay awake/watch" — the ethical imperative that grounds the chapter's parenetic conclusion.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Matthew 24 opens the **fifth and final** of Matthew's structured discourses on B. W. Bacon's classic five-discourse hypothesis (*Studies in Matthew*, 1930). Each Matthean discourse closes with the formula "when Jesus had finished" — appearing here at 26:1 to close the entire Olivet sermon (chs. 24-25). The five discourses (Sermon on the Mount, ch. 5-7; Mission Discourse, ch. 10; Parables Discourse, ch. 13; Community Discourse, ch. 18; Olivet Discourse, chs. 24-25) deliberately mirror the Pentateuch and present Jesus as the new Moses delivering definitive teaching. The Olivet Discourse closes the structure as Deuteronomy closes the Torah — with eschatological prophecy and ethical exhortation before the speaker's death.

The chapter's source-critical relationship to Mark 13 is straightforward: Matthew expands Mark substantially. Major Matthean additions include the doubled disciples' question with *parousia* and *synteleia* (v. 3), the lightning-east-to-west saying (v. 27), the corpse-and-vultures saying (v. 28), the explicit appearance of "the sign of the Son of Man in heaven" (v. 30), the Noahic-flood comparison (vv. 37-39), the two-in-the-field/two-at-the-mill (vv. 40-41), the thief-in-the-night saying (vv. 43-44), and the faithful-and-wicked-servant parable (vv. 45-51). Davies/Allison (ICC vol. 3) carefully document the redactional layer, observing that Matthew expands Mark's apocalyptic toward greater eschatological elaboration.

The chapter's structure can be analyzed in three movements. **Verses 1-14** describe the *prelude* — false Christs, wars, famines, persecutions, the proclamation of the gospel — explicitly designated "the beginning of the birth pains" (v. 8). **Verses 15-28** describe the *crisis* — the abomination of desolation, the great tribulation, the imperative to flee, the warnings against false eschatological speculation. **Verses 29-31** describe the *parousia* itself — cosmic phenomena, the sign of the Son of Man, the gathering of the elect. **Verses 32-51** offer *parenetic* application — the lesson of the fig tree, the timing problem ("this generation"), the ignorance saying, the Noahic warning, the imperative to watchfulness, and the parable of the two servants.

The transition between the chapter's two halves is the most contested editorial seam. Verse 29's ***eutheōs*** (εὐθέως, "immediately") seems to bind the cosmic *parousia* directly to the tribulation-flight described in vv. 15-22 — supporting either a tightly preterist reading (all of this happened in 70 CE in apocalyptic-symbolic terms) or a "nearness" reading that would create the timing problem of v. 34. Modern critical commentaries are divided on whether this is original Matthean composition or reflects an earlier oracle Matthew has incorporated.

The **timing problem** of v. 34 — "this generation will not pass away until all these things take place" — is the chapter's most famous interpretive crux. Combined with v. 36's "no one knows the day or hour... not even the Son" and the entire chapter's apparent imminence, the verse generates a dilemma: either Jesus predicted his own *parousia* within the lifetime of his hearers (and was therefore wrong, on a critical-scholarly reading running from H. S. Reimarus through Albert Schweitzer), or the verse refers only to the events of 70 CE (preterist reading), or "this generation" means something other than the chronological cohort (the human race, the Jewish people, the wicked, the church-age generation, etc.).

**The kenotic Christological problem** of v. 36 — "no one knows... not even the Son, but only the Father" — is one of the most theologically exposed verses in the gospels. Some early manuscripts (notably Sinaiticus before correction) preserve "nor the Son" against later harmonization that drops it. The verse was a major battleground in fourth-century Christological controversy: Arius cited it for the Son's subordination; the orthodox party (Athanasius, Cyril of Alexandria) developed sophisticated readings to preserve full divinity while accommodating the verse.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**The interpretive landscape on the Olivet Discourse** is the most contested in NT studies. Four broad positions deserve full presentation.

**(1) Futurist / dispensationalist reading.** Classical premillennialism (held by John Walvoord, J. Dwight Pentecost, Charles Ryrie, and the Dallas Seminary tradition) reads almost the entire chapter as referring to a future seven-year tribulation period, with the abomination of desolation (v. 15) being a future Antichrist desecrating a rebuilt Jerusalem temple. The "this generation" of v. 34 refers to the future generation that witnesses these eschatological events. This reading is the popular reading of much American evangelicalism (especially through the *Left Behind* franchise and the Scofield Reference Bible) but is held by relatively few academic NT scholars today.

**(2) Preterist reading.** Held in its rigorous form by **R. T. France** (NICNT) and **N. T. Wright** (*Jesus and the Victory of God*, 1996), this reading takes virtually the entire chapter as referring to the events of 66-70 CE: the false messianic claimants Josephus describes, the Roman armies, the Zealot occupation of the Temple as the abomination of desolation, the cosmic-language passages (vv. 29-31) as apocalyptic-symbolic descriptions of the destruction of the Temple-cosmos rather than literal cosmic events. On Wright's reading, Jesus's predictions all came true within forty years exactly as he said. The "coming of the Son of Man on clouds" in v. 30 is, on this reading, his *enthronement* (echoing Daniel 7:13's ascension to the Ancient of Days, *not* a descent to earth) — Jesus is vindicated by the destruction of the institution that condemned him. The "this generation" of v. 34 means precisely what it says: the contemporary cohort.

**(3) Layered / partial-preterist reading.** Held by most modern critical commentators (**Davies/Allison** ICC, **Ulrich Luz** *Hermeneia*, **Donald Hagner** WBC, **John Nolland** NIGTC) and many evangelical scholars (**D. A. Carson**, **Craig Blomberg**), this reading sees the chapter as deliberately layering the 70 CE destruction with the eschatological *parousia*. The "abomination of desolation" has a near-historical referent (the Roman desecration in 70 CE) and a far-eschatological referent (a final desecration before Christ's return). The chapter's apparent compression reflects the prophetic compression of near and far events — a phenomenon paralleled in the Hebrew prophets (e.g., Isaiah's compression of Cyrus's return-from-exile and a far eschatological restoration).

**(4) Consistent eschatological reading.** **Albert Schweitzer**'s *Quest of the Historical Jesus* (1906) argued that Jesus genuinely expected the imminent end of the age within his disciples' lifetimes and was wrong; the apparent delay is the early church's painful adjustment to the *parousia*'s non-arrival. Schweitzer's position, though no longer dominant, retains influence in some critical scholarship. **C. H. Dodd**'s *Parables of the Kingdom* (1935) developed the partial counter-position of *realized eschatology* — Jesus proclaimed that the kingdom had already arrived in his ministry, and the apocalyptic future-tense language is secondary. **Joachim Jeremias** (*New Testament Theology*, 1971) and **George Beasley-Murray** (*Jesus and the Last Days*, 1993) sought middle paths — *inaugurated eschatology* — preserving both the present arrival and future consummation of the kingdom.

**Patristic and medieval readings.** **Origen**'s *Commentary on Matthew*, **Chrysostom**'s *Homilies* (Hom. 75-77), and **Jerome** read the chapter as combining literal-historical fulfillment in 70 CE with future-eschatological fulfillment at the *parousia* — a fundamentally layered reading that anticipates modern partial-preterism. **Augustine**'s *City of God* (especially Books 18-22) and his *Letter 199 to Hesychius* engage the timing problem directly: Augustine refuses to set dates and reads "this generation" as the human race or the church-age generation. **Aquinas**'s *Catena Aurea* compiles the patristic chorus; *Summa* III q. 10 a. 2 addresses v. 36's "not even the Son" by distinguishing Christ's divine knowledge (which knows all) from his human knowledge in its mode of acquisition.

**Reformation readings.** **Calvin**'s *Harmony* reads the chapter as primarily about Jerusalem's destruction with secondary application to the church-age and final return. **Luther** reads similarly but with greater apocalyptic urgency about the present age.

**Twentieth-century readings.** **Karl Barth**'s *Church Dogmatics* (especially III/2 §47 and IV/3.1) treats the Olivet Discourse as authentically eschatological proclamation — Jesus genuinely announced the eschaton's nearness, but the eschaton is itself a complex theological reality that includes both historical events (Jerusalem's fall) and the ultimate consummation. The "delay of the *parousia*" is not a problem to be solved but a theological reality of the time of the church. **Adela Yarbro Collins** (*Cosmology and Eschatology in Jewish and Christian Apocalypticism*, 1996) and **John Collins** (*The Apocalyptic Imagination*, 1998) have shown the chapter's deep continuity with Second Temple Jewish apocalyptic — Daniel, 1 Enoch, 4 Ezra, 2 Baruch — emphasizing that the genre's symbolic conventions resist literal-cosmic decoding.

**Jewish-Christian dialogue.** **Amy-Jill Levine** notes that the chapter's apocalyptic vocabulary is thoroughly Jewish — drawn from Daniel, Joel, Zechariah, the prophets — and resists a Christian-vs-Jewish framing. The early Christian apocalyptic imagination is Jewish apocalyptic continued by Christ-following Jews. The painful question is whether the chapter's prediction of Temple destruction, fulfilled in 70 CE, was understood (then and now) as theological judgment on Israel — and how that question lands in light of post-Holocaust Christian-Jewish dialogue (*Nostra Aetate*, PCBC 2001).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: How do we hold "this generation will not pass away" (v. 34) together with the chapter's apparent extension toward final eschatology?** Three positions deserve steelmanning. **(1) Hard-preterist resolution** (Wright, France) — "this generation" means exactly the contemporary cohort, "all these things" refers to the 70 CE events including the *parousia* understood as Christ's vindication-enthronement, and Jesus was right and on time. The cosmic language of vv. 29-31 is apocalyptic-symbolic, not literal-future. **(2) Generational-flexibility resolution** (much evangelical scholarship including Carson, Blomberg) — *genea* can mean "race" (the Jewish people will not pass away) or "kind" (this kind of evil generation will persist) or "the generation that sees these signs" (the eschatological generation), allowing the verse to refer to a future cohort. **(3) Apocalyptic-prophetic-foreshortening resolution** (most modern critical scholarship) — Jesus and Matthew operate within the prophetic convention of compressing near and far events; "this generation" is genuinely the contemporary cohort, but "all these things" refers specifically to the near events (the prelude and 70 CE destruction), not the *parousia*; the chapter's structure deliberately layers both. **Where this leaves us:** the resolution one chooses commits to a larger framework. Hard-preterism saves Jesus's prophetic accuracy at the cost of de-literalizing the *parousia*; generational flexibility preserves a future *parousia* at the cost of straining *genea*; apocalyptic-foreshortening preserves both at the cost of admitting Matthew (and Jesus, on the critical reading) operated within a worldview that compressed time differently than ours. Each costs something. The honest response is to name what each reading costs and choose deliberately, not to pretend the verse settles the question.

**Tension 2: What does "not even the Son" (v. 36) imply about Christ's nature?** Three positions. **(1) Two-natures distinction** (Cyril of Alexandria, Aquinas, classical Chalcedonian Christology) — Christ in his divine nature knows all things; Christ in his human nature, by the mode of his incarnate self-emptying, did not know the day. The verse describes Christ's human knowledge, not his divine. The two natures hold without confusion or change. **(2) Kenotic Christology** (Gottfried Thomasius, P. T. Forsyth, Hugh Ross Mackintosh, much twentieth-century Reformed theology) — Christ in the incarnation genuinely emptied himself (Phil 2:7) of certain divine attributes including comprehensive knowledge; the verse is a window into the genuine self-limitation of incarnation. **(3) Subordinationist reading** (Arius and his successors, Jehovah's Witnesses, Mormon theology) — the verse demonstrates Christ's ontological subordination to the Father; he is divine but lesser, and his ignorance is metaphysical. This reading has been definitively rejected by ecumenical Christianity since Nicaea (325) but recurs in various heterodox movements. **Where this leaves us:** Chalcedonian orthodoxy holds positions (1) or (2) as legitimate options within the bounds of the two-natures formula; both refuse position (3). The kenotic reading enjoys broad sympathy in modern Reformed and evangelical theology because it takes the verse at face value; the two-natures reading preserves the strongest classical metaphysical commitments. Both refuse to soften what the verse says: the incarnate Son did not know the day.

**Unresolved question:** Is the *parousia* in Matthew 24 essentially the same event as Christ's enthronement at the right hand of the Father (Wright's reading, citing Daniel 7:13's ascension), or is it a distinct future descent (the traditional reading, citing 1 Thess 4:16's "the Lord himself will descend")? This question is genuinely undecided in contemporary scholarship and divides preterist from non-preterist readings down the center.

## The Thread

Matthew 24 weaves Daniel 7:13 (Son of Man coming on clouds), Daniel 9:27 / 11:31 / 12:11 (abomination of desolation), Isaiah 13:10 (sun darkened, stars falling — language used of Babylon's fall), Joel 2:10, 30-31 (cosmic disturbance), Zechariah 12:10-12 (the tribes of the earth mourning), Genesis 6-9 (the days of Noah). The cosmic-darkness language has Hebrew Bible precedent in oracles against the nations — flag this for the preterist argument that it is apocalyptic-symbolic rather than cosmological. Forward in the NT, the *parousia* vocabulary returns at 1 Thess 4:15-17, 1 Cor 15:23, James 5:7, 2 Peter 3:4-12 (where the timing problem is engaged directly), and 1 John 2:28; the Son of Man on clouds returns at Mark 14:62, Acts 1:11, Rev 1:7, 14:14. Forward in Matthew, the chapter's parenetic close ("watch... be ready") flows directly into chapter 25's three eschatological parables (ten virgins, talents, sheep and goats), each illustrating watchful readiness. Flag this: the timing problem of v. 34 returns explicitly at 2 Peter 3:8-9 — "with the Lord one day is as a thousand years" — the early church's first formal engagement with the apparent delay.
