# Psalm 109 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 109 carries the superscription *la-menatzeach le-David mizmor* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ לְדָוִד מִזְמוֹר), "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of David." This is the standard Davidic-psalm formula familiar from the first book of the Psalter, and Ps 109 belongs to the small Davidic cluster (Pss 108-110) that Book V's editor placed after the opening thanksgiving of Ps 107. The Davidic superscription makes a canonical claim — this is the prayer of the messianic king under deadly accusation — without necessarily implying historical Davidic authorship.

In **Gunkel**'s form-critical taxonomy, Ps 109 is a **Klagelied des Einzelnen** — an individual lament — and specifically a **Klagelied der Angeklagten**, the lament of one falsely accused. This subtype is well-attested (Pss 7, 17, 35) and reflects a recognizable Sitz im Leben: the person hauled before the assembly under malicious accusation, seeking the temple as place of asylum and divine vindication. Hans Schmid's classic work on the *Klagelieder der Angeklagten* situated these psalms in a specific juridical-cultic process: the accused fled to the sanctuary, prayed for divine acquittal, and could expect either an oracle of acquittal (the *Heilsorakel*) or further suffering. Psalm 109 fits this Sitz precisely — the speaker is encircled by lying tongues (109:2-3), accused without cause (109:3), and pleads for YHWH's intervention as judge.

The composition's date is contested but most modern scholars (Hossfeld-Zenger, Allen, Goldingay) place it in the late pre-exilic or early exilic period, with editorial placement in Book V reflecting later canonical concerns. Some elements are linguistically older (the ANE imprecation patterns, the juridical setting); the canonical placement is later.

The psalm contains **the most concentrated imprecation in the Psalter**. Verses 6-19 form an extended sequence of curses: an accuser appointed against the enemy, judgment against him, his days cut short, his children orphaned, his wife widowed, his property seized, his name blotted out, his iniquity remembered, his memory cut off. The Hebrew *yhi-yamav me'atim* (יִהְיוּ־יָמָיו מְעַטִּים), "let his days be few" (109:8), is the verse that will become decisive in NT use. The imprecations draw on a broad ANE curse-tradition: the cuneiform *šurpu* and *maqlû* incantation series include similar calls for divine destruction of an enemy's name, posterity, and property. Egyptian execration texts curse named enemies similarly. What is distinctive in Ps 109 is not the curse-vocabulary but the *forensic* framing: the curses are addressed to YHWH as judge, not invoked as autonomous magical performance.

The pivotal interpretive question of the psalm is **whether vv. 6-19 are the speaker's own curse or a quotation of the enemy's curse against the speaker**. The Hebrew of v. 6, *hafqed alav rasha* (הַפְקֵד עָלָיו רָשָׁע), can be read either as imperative ("appoint a wicked man against him") in the speaker's own voice, or as the *opening of reported speech* — the curse that the speaker's accusers have been hurling at him. **Allen** and **Goldingay** advocate the reported-speech reading: vv. 6-19 are the *enemy's* curse, which the speaker reports back to God in v. 20 ("may *this* be the reward of my accusers from the LORD"). The textual signal for this reading is v. 20's *zo't pe'ulat sotnay me-et Yahweh* (זֹאת פְּעֻלַּת שֹׂטְנַי מֵאֵת יְהוָה), which can mean "let this [curse just quoted] return upon my accusers" — the *lex talionis* turning of the curse back onto the curser.

Mainstream textual readings vary. Most translations (KJV, NIV, ESV, NRSV) render vv. 6-19 in the speaker's voice — as his imprecation. But a significant minority (the NEB, CEB, several modern translators) follows the reported-speech reading. The Hebrew is genuinely ambiguous. The interpretive consequences are large: on the first reading, the speaker is cursing his enemy with extraordinary vehemence; on the second, the speaker is reporting the enemy's curse and asking God to turn it back. We will return to this in Lens 4.

The second key Hebrew term is *satan* (שָׂטָן) and its derivative verb. V. 6: *ve-satan ya'amod al-yemino* (וְשָׂטָן יַעֲמֹד עַל־יְמִינוֹ), "let an accuser stand at his right hand." V. 20: *sotnay* (שֹׂטְנַי), "my accusers." V. 29: *kemo me'il-bushtam* (כְּמוֹ מְעִיל בָּשְׁתָּם), "as in a cloak [of shame]." The *satan* here is forensic — the prosecuting accuser at a trial — not yet the demonic figure of later development. (The figure in Job 1-2 and Zech 3 is the same forensic role; the personalization into a single supernatural being comes later.) Ps 109 is one of the OT's clearest examples of *satan* as a forensic role.

There is no DSS scroll preserving Ps 109 directly (11Q5 begins later). The LXX (Psalm 108) translates the imprecations literally, with no softening — and it is from the LXX text that Acts 1:20 will quote.

## Lens 2: The Literature

**Lowth's** parallelism in Ps 109 is overwhelmingly synonymous in the lament-and-petition portions ("wicked and deceitful mouths are opened against me, / speaking against me with lying tongues," 109:2) and synthetic in the imprecation portions, where the second line intensifies and extends the first ("may his children be fatherless / and his wife a widow," 109:9 — the extension reaches further into the consequences of the curse).

The **strophic structure** of Ps 109 has been read in several ways; the most persuasive (following Hossfeld-Zenger and Allen) is a five-part structure:

- **Strophe I (vv. 1-5)** — opening cry and description of accusation. *Be not silent, O God of my praise!* (v. 1) The speaker is encircled by lying tongues; in return for love he is accused.
- **Strophe II (vv. 6-15)** — the imprecation proper (whether speaker's own or quoted). The accuser appointed, the trial, the days cut short, the children orphaned, the property seized, the name blotted out.
- **Strophe III (vv. 16-20)** — the *reason* for the curse (he showed no kindness, pursued the poor) and the talionic turn ("may *this* be the reward of my accusers").
- **Strophe IV (vv. 21-29)** — the speaker's own situation and renewed petition. *I am poor and needy* (v. 22). *Help me, O LORD my God!* (v. 26).
- **Strophe V (vv. 30-31)** — the closing thanksgiving-vow. *With my mouth I will give great thanks to the LORD* (v. 30). *He stands at the right hand of the needy one* (v. 31).

The **chiastic** structure of strophes I and V is striking: strophe I begins with the speaker's plea against the silent God, strophe V closes with the speaker's vow of thanksgiving. The accuser stood at the enemy's *right hand* in v. 6; YHWH stands at the *needy one's right hand* in v. 31. The imagery of the *right hand* — the place of the prosecuting accuser, the place of the divine helper — frames the entire composition.

There is also a powerful **inclusio** by way of the speech-mouth imagery. The wicked open their *mouths* against the speaker (v. 2); the speaker closes by giving great thanks with his *mouth* (v. 30). The trial of words is finally answered by praise — the right speech displaces the wrong.

There is no *selah* in Ps 109 — Book V again declining the marker. Prosody is mostly 3+3 stress in the laments and 4+3 in the imprecations, where the longer second colon delivers the extended curse-content.

The clothing imagery of vv. 18-19 and v. 29 is one of the Psalter's most arresting figural sequences. The cursed enemy "clothed himself with cursing as his coat" (109:18) — the curse becomes his garment, soaks into his body like water and oil. Then the talionic turn: "may my accusers be clothed with dishonor; / may they be wrapped in their own shame as in a cloak" (109:29). The curse-as-garment becomes the punishment-as-garment. The image insists on a reciprocal moral order: what one wears, one becomes.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Jewish Reception.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Ps 109 reads it as David's prayer against the false accusers who slandered him before Saul — Doeg the Edomite is the typical referent. The midrash treats the imprecations as the speaker's own and unproblematic, given the malice of the slander. The rabbinic tradition is generally less troubled by imprecation than the Christian tradition has been; the Jewish liturgy has not omitted Ps 109 from its psalmic repertoire as the Christian liturgies have.

**Rashi** reads vv. 6-19 in the speaker's voice and interprets the curses as a just recompense for the accusers' malice. **Ibn Ezra** notes the difficulty of the imprecations and offers a partial reading toward the reported-speech interpretation, suggesting that some of the curses may report the enemies' words. **Radak** continues the speaker's-voice reading and emphasizes that the imprecations are addressed to YHWH as judge, not enacted by the speaker — the speaker hands vengeance to God rather than enacting it.

**Nachmanides** treats the psalm as Davidic and reads the imprecations within the framework of public justice: the accusers are not private enemies but public threats to the community, and their destruction is necessary for the community's safety.

In modern Jewish reception, **Buber** read Ps 109 as the *I-Thou* in extremity — the speaker is at the edge of speech, and what he says to God is the edge-speech of the betrayed. **Heschel** (in *The Prophets*) read the imprecations as continuous with prophetic *pathos*: God shares the suffering of the wronged, and the imprecations are the protest-speech of those whom God hears. **Levenson** has insisted that Ps 109 must be read against the background of ANE imprecatory tradition — what looks extreme by modern standards is normal speech in its ancient setting, where the curse was the only weapon of the powerless against the powerful.

**Erich Zenger**'s monograph *A God of Vengeance? Understanding the Psalms of Divine Wrath* (German 1994, ET 1996) is the most important modern Jewish-Christian engagement. Zenger argues that the imprecatory psalms are *protest theology*: by handing vengeance to God, the speaker refuses to enact it himself, and the act of speaking the curse to God is itself a renunciation of personal violence. The imprecations are a discipline that prevents the rage from becoming murder. To omit them from liturgy, Zenger argues, is to deny the oppressed their voice and to leave the rage with no place to go but private retaliation.

**Christian Reception.** The Christian tradition has been deeply uncomfortable with Ps 109. **Augustine** (*Enarrationes* on Ps 108 LXX) reads the imprecations Christologically — they are spoken by Christ against Judas, the betrayer. This is in part because Acts 1:20 had already applied Ps 109:8 to Judas, making the Christological-imprecation reading available. Augustine softens the personal-vengeance dimension by reading the curses as prophetic announcements of divine judgment rather than personal calls for retaliation. **Cassiodorus** continues the Judas-Christological reading; **Aquinas** does likewise. **Luther** (*Operationes*) reads the imprecations as righteous indignation against God's enemies, but with characteristic intensity acknowledges that the modern Christian must struggle with the speech.

**Calvin** (1557) is the first major Christian commentator to wrestle visibly with the imprecations. He reads vv. 6-19 in the speaker's voice and argues that the imprecations are *proper* when (a) they reflect divine zeal rather than personal malice, (b) they are directed at obstinately unrepentant enemies of God's people, and (c) they are addressed to God as judge rather than enacted personally. Calvin sets the framework that the Reformed tradition will largely follow.

The Catholic **Liturgy of the Hours** in its post-Vatican-II reform omits Ps 109 entirely, along with portions of Pss 58, 83, 137. This was a deliberate pastoral decision (1971) on the grounds that these texts present "psychological difficulties" for modern liturgical use. The decision has been controversial. The **Anglican Book of Common Prayer** has historically retained the full psalm in the daily office; some recent BCP revisions have abridged it.

In modern critical scholarship, **Gunkel** and **Mowinckel** classed Ps 109 as a lament of the falsely accused, with the imprecations as integral to the form. **Westermann** included Ps 109 among the laments and noted the *talionic* function of the curse. **Kraus** detailed the juridical Sitz. **Hossfeld-Zenger** developed the protest-theology reading. **Allen** and **Goldingay** advocated the reported-speech reading of vv. 6-19. **Brueggemann**'s essay "The Costly Loss of Lament" (1986) and his book *The Message of the Psalms* argue that the church's omission of imprecations is a costly evasion: lament and imprecation are how the oppressed speak truthfully to God, and the church that forbids these speech-forms abandons the suffering to silence.

**C. S. Lewis** in *Reflections on the Psalms* (1958) wrestles honestly with Ps 109 and the imprecatory psalms generally. Lewis cannot endorse the curses but refuses to dismiss them; he reads them as the unfiltered cry of human pain, given to God as the only place such pain can go. Lewis's chapter is one of the most-read modern Christian engagements with imprecation. In a kindred vein, Brueggemann presses the point that the imprecations are the speech of the powerless — the betrayed, the dehumanized, the crushed — and that a Christianity insulated from real malice has too easily dismissed as embarrassing what was always the only weapon of those at the receiving end of it: handing the matter, intact, to God.

**NT Use.** Acts 1:20 cites Ps 109:8 in Peter's speech to the assembled disciples after Judas Iscariot's betrayal and death: *let his office be taken by another* (Hebrew *pequdato yiqach acher*, פְּקֻדָּתוֹ יִקַּח אַחֵר). Peter applies this verse to Judas's vacated apostolic position, justifying the selection of Matthias as Judas's replacement. The citation is consequential: it makes Ps 109 a Christologically-keyed psalm, reads Judas as the antitype of the wicked accuser, and uses the imprecation against the apostate as warrant for ecclesial replacement. The application is not the speaker's-voice imprecation against just any enemy — it is the application of one specific verse (109:8) to one specific apostate. The NT does not endorse the entire imprecation sequence; it cites the eighth verse as fulfilled prophecy in Judas's case. This is the standard pesher-style use of the Psalter in Acts.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Whose voice is vv. 6-19?**

The first interpretive question is whether the long imprecation is the speaker's own curse or a report of the enemy's curse.

*Position A: The traditional speaker's-voice reading.* Most translations and the patristic tradition read vv. 6-19 as the speaker's own imprecation. On this reading, the psalm is the Psalter's most concentrated imprecation, and the church must reckon with what it means to pray such curses. The strength of this reading is that it follows the natural flow of the Hebrew without requiring an unmarked introduction of reported speech. Its difficulty is the theological burden: the church must explain how Christians can pray verses that call for an enemy's children to be orphaned and his name blotted out.

*Position B: The reported-speech reading (Allen, Goldingay).* On this reading, vv. 6-19 are the curses the enemies have been hurling at the speaker, which he reports to God in v. 20: "may *this* [curse just quoted] be the reward of my accusers." The strength of this reading is that it makes excellent sense of v. 20 (which on the speaker's-voice reading is somewhat redundant) and explains the abrupt singular ("him") in vv. 6-19 amid the plural enemies elsewhere. Its difficulty is the absence of an unambiguous "they said" introducing the reported speech.

*Position C: The hybrid — talionic turning of the enemy's curse.* The psalm reports the enemy's curse pattern (vv. 6-19) and prays it back as *lex talionis* — what the wicked have wished on the righteous, may God return on the wicked. This combines elements of both readings: the curses are the enemy's content, but the speaker prays them back as recompense.

**Steelman:** The reported-speech and hybrid readings are textually plausible and theologically attractive (they ease the imprecatory burden) but rest on a Hebrew construction that is genuinely ambiguous. The traditional reading is grammatically simpler. Honest engagement requires that we hold the question open — and that we do not rush to the reported-speech reading simply because it is more comfortable. Even if vv. 6-19 are reported speech, v. 20 still asks God to enact the curse on the accusers; the imprecation is not eliminated, only relocated.

N.T. Wright touches Ps 109 in the wider argument of *The Case for the Psalms* and his lectures on the Psalter: the imprecatory psalms, on his reading, are not sub-Christian leftovers to be quarantined but part of the prayer book Jesus himself prayed — and in praying them Israel (and the church after her) hands the longing for justice over to the God who, in the cross, takes the world's violence into himself rather than dispensing it. The curse-speech is real, but it is speech *to* God, not licence for the pray-er; the resolution Wright points to is not the omission of the psalm but the resurrection that vindicates the wronged and disarms the wrongdoer.

**Tension 2: Imprecation in Christian liturgy.**

*Position A: Omission as pastoral wisdom.* The post-Vatican-II Liturgy of the Hours omitted Ps 109 (along with Pss 58, 83, parts of 137) on the judgment that these texts cause psychological difficulty for modern Christians and risk teaching a vengeful posture inappropriate to the Christian gospel. The decision reflects pastoral concern for ordinary worshipers.

*Position B: Omission as evasion (Brueggemann, Zenger).* The omission of imprecation from the Christian liturgy is a *costly loss*. It denies the wronged their voice. It teaches that Christian prayer is only the prayer of the comfortable — those whose enemies are not real, whose suffering is theoretical. It evades the protest-theology function of imprecation: handing vengeance to God so the speaker need not enact it. By forbidding the imprecation, the church does not eliminate the rage; it pushes it underground, where it festers. Brueggemann argues the omission has done real harm to the church's pastoral capacity to address abuse, injustice, and trauma.

*Position C: Honest but troubled engagement (Lewis, mainstream pastoral practice).* The middle path: pray the imprecations, but with honesty about their difficulty. Acknowledge that they reflect a stage of revelation that the gospel transforms (Matt 5:44, "love your enemies"). Do not endorse the curses uncritically, but do not omit them either. Use them in pastoral contexts where the speaker has actually been wronged, where the rage is real, and where God is the only safe addressee.

**Steelman:** All three positions have weight. The omission position is right that the imprecations are difficult; the protest position is right that omission has done harm; the troubled-engagement position is right that they cannot be uncritically endorsed and cannot be safely silenced. The best practice is probably: retain the texts in the canonical lectionary; teach them honestly; use them pastorally with those who have suffered real malice; refuse the false comfort of pretending the church's prayer can only be benediction.

**Tension 3: NT use and the Judas application.**

Acts 1:20 applies Ps 109:8 to Judas. This canonical use raises a question: does the NT endorse imprecation, or does it merely apply one verse as fulfilled prophecy? Most readers (Calvin, Brueggemann, mainstream commentary) hold that the NT use is *narrowly applicational* — Peter cites the verse as fulfilled in Judas's specific apostasy, not as a model for Christian imprecation. The Christian is still bound by Matt 5:44 in personal practice; the canonical use applies to a particular figure. The Judas application provides Christological warrant for reading Ps 109 as having a specific fulfillment-context, not for adopting imprecation as Christian devotional practice.

**Where this leaves us.** Psalm 109 is the Psalter's hardest text on imprecation. It teaches that the speech of the wronged includes curse — and that the curse is rightly addressed to God as judge rather than enacted by the speaker. It teaches that the church's omission of such speech is not pastoral wisdom but cultural evasion that has cost real ground in pastoral capacity. It teaches that the NT's narrow application to Judas is not endorsement of personal imprecation but identification of one verse's specific fulfillment. It teaches that the talionic logic — what the wicked wish, may they wear — is older than the gospel and present in the canonical text as honest record of the cry of the wronged. And it teaches that the speaker who finishes with thanksgiving (vv. 30-31) — *with my mouth I will give great thanks to the LORD* — has handed vengeance to God and walked away with his own mouth filled with praise rather than blood.

**Closing question.** If imprecation is the protest-speech of the powerless that the church has too often silenced, what would it mean for our liturgies to make room again — not for cursing as Christian discipleship, but for the honest speech of those who have been crushed, brought to God who alone can be trusted with vengeance?
