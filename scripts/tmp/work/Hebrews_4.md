# Hebrews 4 — Sabbath Rest, the Living Word, and the Great High Priest

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The Epistle to the Hebrews is, by the unanimous verdict of modern scholarship and the candor of the best ancient readers, anonymous. The Greek text bears no authorial superscription; the document opens not with the typical Pauline address but with a soaring Greek period — *Πολυμερῶς καὶ πολυτρόπως* (1:1) — that reads like a synagogue homily rather than a letter. Origen's famous concession in the early third century, preserved by Eusebius (*HE* 6.25.14), captures the situation: τίς δὲ ὁ γράψας τὴν ἐπιστολήν, τὸ μὲν ἀληθὲς θεὸς οἶδεν — "but who wrote the epistle, in truth God knows." The Eastern church gradually attached the work to Paul; the Western church hesitated until the synods of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) ratified its place in the canon. Modern proposals — Apollos (Luther's preferred guess), Barnabas (Tertullian's), Priscilla (Adolf von Harnack's striking 1900 thesis explaining the absent self-naming), Silas, Clement of Rome, Luke, and Aquila — all share one feature: a Hellenistic-Jewish Christian author of formidable rhetorical training, soaked in the Septuagint, fluent in the synkrisis-driven argument that ancient progymnasmata taught.

The dating is a 60s-90s window. Those who place it before 70 AD lean on the present-tense temple cult language scattered through chapters 7-10 (the priests "offer," not "offered"); those who place it after lean on the rhetorical idealization of cult patterns that need not depend on a still-functioning temple, and on the fact that 1 Clement (c. 96 AD) already quotes Hebrews 1 (1 Clem 36) — fixing a terminus ante quem. The most likely audience, defended by William Lane (WBC) and Barnabas Lindars, is a Roman Jewish-Christian house-church community: 13:24's *οἱ ἀπὸ τῆς Ἰταλίας* "those from Italy" reads most naturally as Italian believers abroad sending greetings home, and 1 Clement's Roman provenance plus its dependence on Hebrews points the same direction. The genre is clarified by the author himself in 13:22 — *λόγος παρακλήσεως*, "word of exhortation" — a technical term Hartwig Thyen and Lawrence Wills (*The Form of the Sermon in Hellenistic Judaism and Early Christianity*, 1984) have identified as the Diaspora-synagogue homiletical form, here closed with a brief epistolary postscript.

Chapter 4 sits inside the second great movement of the homily. Chapter 3 has just opened the comparison with Moses and pivoted to Psalm 95's "Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts" — the wilderness generation that died short of the promised rest. Chapter 4 sustains and intensifies that midrash. The historical setting matters: a community under social pressure (10:32-34 reminisces about earlier persecution and confiscation of property), tempted to retreat into the safer harbor of synagogue identity, hearing a homily designed to make that retreat unthinkable. The material conditions of Roman Jewish-Christianity in the 60s-80s — Claudius's expulsion edict of 49, Nero's pogrom of 64, the Flavian war — form the human texture behind the Sabbath argument. To a community whose neighbors observed Sabbath in synagogues now under suspicion, the question "what does Sabbath mean for us?" was not abstract.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The Greek of Hebrews 4 is among the most polished in the New Testament. The argument unfolds as a chain-midrash on Psalm 95:11 (*ὥμοσα ἐν τῇ ὀργῇ μου, εἰ εἰσελεύσονται εἰς τὴν κατάπαυσίν μου*) braided with Genesis 2:2 LXX (*κατέπαυσεν ὁ θεός… ἀπὸ πάντων τῶν ἔργων αὐτοῦ*) — a *gezerah shavah* style linkage by the shared verbal root *καταπαύω*. The author's quotations are uniformly Septuagintal, never reflecting the Masoretic Hebrew where the two diverge.

The chapter pivots on a single coined or near-coined noun. In 4:9 the author writes ἀπολείπεται ἄρα σαββατισμὸς τῷ λαῷ τοῦ θεοῦ — "there remains, then, a *sabbatismos* for the people of God." *σαββατισμός* is a hapax legomenon in the New Testament and exceptionally rare in any earlier Greek (Plutarch *De superstitione* 166a uses the verbal *σαββατίζω*; the noun appears in later patristic texts that may themselves be drawing from Hebrews). Many commentators — Attridge, Lane, Cockerill — judge it likely an authorial coinage, distinguishing the technical "Sabbath-keeping" or "Sabbath-festal-state" from the broader noun *κατάπαυσις* "rest" used eight times in the chapter from Psalm 95. The lexical move signals that the eschatological rest is not merely a continuation of weekly cessation but a Sabbath quality of life with God.

A second rhetorical trick is the wordplay at 4:8: *εἰ γὰρ αὐτοὺς Ἰησοῦς κατέπαυσεν* — "for if Joshua had given them rest." In Greek, Joshua and Jesus are the same name: Ἰησοῦς. The Septuagint of Joshua names the conqueror Ἰησοῦς; the author exploits this homonymy so that a hearer trained on the LXX will catch the shimmer — Joshua led Israel into Canaan but did not give the rest; another Ἰησοῦς is needed (Cockerill, Lane). The catchword Σήμερον "today" from Psalm 95:7, repeated at 4:7, presses the point: the rest is futural and present at once.

Verses 12-13 then erupt in one of the most rhetorically charged passages in the homily. The Word of God is *ζῶν… καὶ ἐνεργής*, "living and active," sharper than any *μάχαιρα δίστομος* "two-edged sword" (a Hebraic idiom literally "two-mouthed"), piercing *ἄχρι μερισμοῦ ψυχῆς καὶ πνεύματος, ἁρμῶν τε καὶ μυελῶν* — "to the dividing of soul and spirit, of joints and marrow." Albert Vanhoye's rhetorical analysis identifies this as a transitional periodic crescendo before the chapter culminates at 4:14-16 in the announcement of the *μέγας ἀρχιερεύς* "great high priest." The structural function is decisive: 4:14-16 inaugurates the central exposition of the *ἀρχιερεύς* theme that occupies the homily's belly (chapters 5-10), and the famous invitation *προσερχώμεθα… μετὰ παρρησίας* "let us draw near with confidence" (4:16) sets the keyword *παρρησία* — Greek civic boldness, here transposed into priestly access — that will recur at 10:19, 10:35.

The text-critical situation is stable. The major early witnesses — P46 (which strikingly places Hebrews directly after Romans, treating it as a genuinely Pauline document), Codex Sinaiticus (א), Codex Vaticanus (B), Codex Alexandrinus (A) — agree on the chapter's substance with only minor orthographic and word-order variants.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Hebrews 4 has been the seam along which Christian Sabbath theology has been stitched and re-stitched for two thousand years. The Catholic tradition, articulated systematically by Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* II-II q.122 a.4) and crystallized in the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* (CCC 2174-2188) and in John Paul II's apostolic letter *Dies Domini* (1998), reads the Sabbath commandment as fulfilled in Christ and transposed typologically onto the Lord's Day, the eighth day of the new creation. The Catholic position is not that the Sabbath was abolished but that it was elevated and relocated: the seventh-day rest of creation finds its eschatological consummation in the Sunday of resurrection. *Dies Domini* §59 identifies the Eucharist as the heart of Sunday observance and reads Heb 4:9's *sabbatismos* as the ecclesial-eschatological Sabbath already inaugurated in the liturgy.

The Reformed sabbatarian tradition draws a stricter line. Westminster Confession 21.7-8 holds the moral substance of the fourth commandment to be perpetual and obligates Christians to a "Christian Sabbath" of public and private worship and rest from common employments on the Lord's Day. Puritan exemplars — Nicholas Bownd's *Doctrine of the Sabbath* (1595), Bunyan's pastoral writings — built an entire pietistic culture on this reading. The Heidelberg Catechism Q. 103 places the Sabbath under the rubric of attendance at the assembly and rest from one's "evil works," softer than the Puritan formulation but still binding. Lutheranism diverges from both Catholic and Reformed positions: Augsburg Confession 28 and Luther's *Treatise on Good Works* (1520) insist the Mosaic Sabbath has been abrogated as ceremonial law and Sunday observance is *bona ordinatio*, good order rather than divine command. The Anabaptist-Mennonite tradition takes a similar non-prescriptive stance, observing Sunday as customary but resisting any binding day-keeping.

The Seventh-Day Adventist tradition reads Hebrews 4 with great seriousness in the opposite direction: Saturday Sabbath observance is held to be permanent moral law; *sabbatismos* in 4:9 is taken (e.g., by the Andrews University Seminary tradition; Ellen G. White's *Great Controversy*; SDA Fundamental Beliefs §20) as referring to literal Sabbath-keeping that "remains" precisely because it has not been transferred. This reading deserves a fair steelman: the verb *ἀπολείπεται* "remains" is naturally read as continuity rather than transfer; nothing in the chapter explicitly transfers the day to the first; the Genesis 2 grounding is creational, not Mosaic; and the early-church Sabbath/Sunday transition was historically gradual (witnessed by Ignatius *Magnesians* 9 but not normative until well into the second century, as A.T. Lincoln documented in *From Sabbath to Lord's Day*, 1982). The Eastern Orthodox tradition integrates Sabbath and Sunday differently: Saturday retains liturgical density (the Saturday of the Dead, Lazarus Saturday, Holy Saturday) while Sunday is the *kyriake hēmera* of Resurrection — the eighth day, the day of new creation. Karl Barth's theological reading in *Church Dogmatics* III/4 §53 reframes the entire question Christologically: the Sabbath is not first a precept about a day but God's invitation to share His rest, fulfilled in Christ and given to humanity as freedom from the tyranny of work.

The Jewish trajectory is its own dense world. The rabbinic literature on Sabbath — Mekilta de-Rabbi Ishmael on Exodus 31; the Mishnaic tractate Shabbat with its 39 categories of forbidden work; Sifre on Numbers; the rich Talmudic discussions of *oneg shabbat* "Sabbath delight" and *menuchah* "rest" — develops the Sabbath as the central sign of the covenant. Philo's *De Allegoria* and *De Plantatione* read the Sabbath philosophically as participation in the divine rest, the rational soul's contemplation of the Maker — a reading that sits remarkably close to Hebrews' eschatological *sabbatismos* and may share Hellenistic-Jewish soil. The Qumran *Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice* (4Q400-407, 11Q17, Mas1k) imagine angelic Sabbath worship in heaven that human worshipers join — a heavenly-Sabbath theme that resonates with Hebrews 12's heavenly-Zion liturgy. The post-70 rabbinic intensification of Sabbath as the central Jewish identity-marker (Heschel's *The Sabbath* would later memorialize it as the "cathedral in time") proceeds along a parallel track to the Christian sabbatismos debate, the two trajectories diverging from a shared Second-Temple matrix.

The patristic reception of Hebrews 4 is also instructive. John Chrysostom's homilies on Hebrews (Homily 6) read 4:9-11 as an exhortation to the moral and eschatological rest of the kingdom, identifying the *katapausis* with the future life rather than with present sabbatical observance, and using the chapter as a goad against sloth (*rhathymia*) in the Christian race. Ambrose's *De fuga saeculi* uses 4:11's *spoudasōmen* "let us strive" to underwrite the ascetic life. Augustine, more striking still, closes *Confessions* book 13 and *De civitate Dei* book 22 with explicit appeals to the seventh-day rest as the eschatological consummation of redemption — *ipse sabbatum erimus*, "we ourselves shall be the Sabbath" — folding the Hebrews 4 *sabbatismos* into the architecture of his theology of history. This Augustinian reading proved decisive for the medieval West and helps account for the Catholic tradition's relative comfort with both a real Sunday observance and an eschatological Sabbath that exceeds it.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions sit at the center of Hebrews 4 and refuse to be dissolved.

The first is the question of what *sabbatismos* requires of the Christian life now. The Catholic tradition answers with a sacramental Sunday, the Reformed with a strict Lord's Day, the Lutheran with ordered freedom, the Anabaptist with charitable indifference, the Adventist with continued seventh-day observance, the Orthodox with liturgical eighth-day eschatology. Each reading can claim a piece of the chapter's logic. The Adventist steelman has real force: the verb *ἀπολείπεται* (4:9) is most naturally read as "continues to remain" rather than "is transferred"; the Genesis 2 grounding is creational rather than Mosaic, which would seem to exempt the seventh-day pattern from the ceremonial-law abrogation logic Lutherans deploy; and the historical Sabbath-to-Sunday shift cannot, by the strictest historical-critical reading, be located in the New Testament documents themselves. The Reformed counter is equally strong: Hebrews 4 deliberately distinguishes *katapausis* (the Psalm-rest) from *sabbatismos* (the new noun) precisely to mark a transposition; the chapter's grammar of "today" (*σήμερον*) suggests a present participation in the rest that cannot be reduced to weekly day-keeping. The Catholic synthesis — Sunday as the eschatological eighth day — offers perhaps the most coherent reading of the homily's eschatological pressure, while leaving the SDA insistence on continuity not fully answered. Hebrews 4 in itself does not settle the question. It announces a Sabbath quality of life with God that "remains" and presses the hearer toward it without legislating its calendrical form.

The second tension concerns the "living word." When 4:12 says *ζῶν γὰρ ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ καὶ ἐνεργής*, what does that mean? The Catholic and Orthodox sacramental reading insists the Word is first Christ — the eternal *Logos* of John 1 — and only derivatively the canonical text and the proclaimed sermon; Henri de Lubac's *Catholicism* and the conciliar text *Dei Verbum* §21 hold that Scripture is venerated alongside the Eucharist as the "one table of God's Word and Christ's Body," so that the "living and active" word here is no mere book but the personal-presence economy of revelation. The Reformed Word-and-Spirit tradition (Calvin *Institutes* I.7-9; Bavinck; John Webster's *Holy Scripture*) holds the canonical text to be the *principium cognoscendi* of theology, the instrument by which the Spirit pierces — the "living and active" predicate properly applies to Scripture-in-the-Spirit's-hand. Karl Barth's *CD* I/2 §19 famously offers a threefold form: the Word of God as Christ revealed, as Scripture written, and as proclamation preached — each form genuinely the Word in mutual indwelling, none reducible to the others. Each tradition can plausibly read 4:12 in its own register; the verse's force is that whatever the Word is, it is not inert.

N.T. Wright, in *Hebrews for Everyone*, cuts across the calendrical debate by reading the "rest" eschatologically: the *katapausis* / *sabbatismos* that "remains" is God's renewed creation, the age to come, into which Jesus — the great high priest who has "passed through the heavens" (4:14) — has already entered as the pioneer, so that the community's task is to keep moving toward it through the wilderness rather than settling back. On this reading the weekly Sabbath was always a signpost; what it pointed to is the world set right, and the "today" of Psalm 95 is the running invitation to stay on the road. Wright also takes the "living and active word" of 4:12 as God's address in Christ and scripture together — the searchlight that lays the heart bare — and he reads 4:16's "let us draw near with confidence" as the homily's recurring move: because there is a great high priest who has been where we are, the exposure of 4:13 ends not in terror but at the throne of grace.

A closing question: if Hebrews 4 invites us to "strive to enter that rest" (4:11), what kind of striving belongs to a Sabbath?

## The Thread

The chapter binds two images that, on first hearing, seem unrelated: a *sabbatismos* that remains and a sword-sharp word that pierces. The link is the verb of motion. The hearers are urged to "enter" (*εἰσέρχομαι*, repeated from 3:11 through 4:11) and to "draw near" (*προσερχώμεθα*, 4:16). The Sabbath rest is not a state to be achieved but a country to be entered, and the door is the great high priest who has himself "passed through the heavens" (*διεληλυθότα τοὺς οὐρανούς*, 4:14). The word of God is the surgical instrument that opens the hearer to that country — naked and exposed before the eyes of the One to whom we must give account (*λόγον ἀποδώσομεν*, 4:13) — and the high priest's *parrhēsia* (4:16) is what makes the exposure bearable rather than terrifying. Hebrews 4 is therefore the door-passage of the homily: the wilderness generation's failure to enter is set against the new community's open invitation, the law's silent exposure is set against the throne of grace, and the Sabbath quality of life with God is offered not as a calendrical observance but as a future already breaking in upon those who have heard the voice that says, today.
