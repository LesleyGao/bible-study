# Acts 9: Damascus Road, Call versus Conversion, and Peter on the Coast

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Saul's Damascus-road experience is conventionally dated to roughly 33–36 CE, with most modern scholarship narrowing to 34–35 — Bruce, Schnabel, and Riesner work backward from the chronological anchor at 2 Corinthians 11:32–33, where Paul recounts his escape from Damascus "**under King Aretas**" (Aretas IV of Nabatea, who controlled or had influence over Damascus from approximately 37 CE until his death in 40 CE), giving the escape episode a hard upper bound. Paul's retrospective fourteen-year and three-year reckonings in Galatians 1–2 then push the original Damascus encounter to the mid-30s. The chronology is consequential because it places Saul's call within roughly two to four years of the resurrection — meaning the "things he received" (1 Cor 15:3) from the Jerusalem community and his own Damascus encounter were essentially contemporaneous events in the foundational decade of the Jesus movement.

**Damascus** in the 30s CE was a major commercial city in the Roman province of Syria, with a long-established Jewish population and multiple synagogue communities (Saul carries letters "to the **synagogues**" — plural — of Damascus). Josephus (*Jewish War* 2.20.2 / 7.8.7) reports a Jewish population of perhaps 10,000–18,000 in Damascus by the 60s CE, and pre-70 Damascus had a recognized institutional Jewish presence with formal relations to the Jerusalem priestly authorities. The "**street called Straight**" (9:11) — Greek *en rhumē tē kaloumenē Eutheia* — refers to the Roman *via recta* still traceable in modern Damascus's Old City as the *Bab Sharqi*–*Bab al-Jabiya* east-west axis, with Roman period substructure surviving beneath the Ottoman streetscape; archaeological investigation in Damascus has been politically constrained but the colonnaded street's Hellenistic-Roman pattern is not in doubt. The wall through which Saul escapes "in a basket" (9:25, paralleling 2 Cor 11:33) is the late Hellenistic-Roman defensive perimeter; the *Bab Kaisan* in southeastern Damascus preserves a Christian tradition (commemorated by the *Chapel of St. Paul*) of being the site of the escape, though the specific identification is medieval rather than archaeologically established.

The **high priest's authority over Damascus synagogues** that Saul invokes in 9:1–2 is historically interesting. The priestly high court in Jerusalem held a recognized but limited extra-territorial influence over diaspora synagogues, exercised through letters of authority for the apprehension of fugitives or the management of disputed religious questions; **E. P. Sanders's** *Judaism: Practice and Belief* and **Martin Hengel's** *The Pre-Christian Paul* document the institutional pattern. Saul's mission was not formally a Roman judicial procedure but a recognized intra-Jewish disciplinary mechanism — which is why the suddenness of Aretas's hostility to Saul in 9:23 and 2 Cor 11:32 makes sense: once Saul had switched sides, his presence in Damascus became politically useful to the local Jewish leadership in ways that drew Nabatean attention.

Five Greek terms structure the chapter. ***Saoul Saoul*** (Σαοὺλ Σαούλ, 9:4) — the **Hebrew vocative form** of Saul's name (rather than the Greek *Saulos* used in narration), preserving the patriarchal-namesake form (King Saul, son of Kish, fellow Benjaminite), and appearing only in this Christophanic moment and its parallels at 22:7 and 26:14. ***Hodos*** (ὁδός, 9:2) — "the Way" — Luke's preferred self-designation for the early Jesus movement, recurring at 19:9, 19:23, 22:4, 24:14, 24:22; the term may render an Aramaic *halakha*-cognate self-description. ***Skeuos eklogēs*** (σκεῦος ἐκλογῆς, 9:15) — "chosen instrument," literally "vessel of choice" — Christ's designation of Saul to Ananias, programmatic for the Pauline self-understanding (cf. 2 Cor 4:7's "treasure in jars of clay"). ***Kalōs lepidoi*** (ὡς λεπίδες, 9:18) — "like scales" — Luke's vivid metaphor for Saul's restored sight; *lepis* in classical Greek refers to fish-scales or flakes of metal. ***Tabitha koum*** — implied in 9:40's "Tabitha, arise" — preserves an Aramaic substrate (Tabitha = *tavyetha* / "gazelle") that explicitly mirrors Jesus's "Talitha koum" at Mark 5:41, marking Peter's resurrection-miracle as Christological imitation.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Acts 9:1–19 is the **first** of three accounts of Saul's Damascus experience in Acts; the parallels at 22:6–21 (Saul's defense before the Jerusalem mob) and 26:12–18 (Saul before Agrippa) repeat the core narrative with significant variations. The variations have been scrutinized in detail. **Tannehill's** *Narrative Unity* and **Pervo** (Hermeneia) document the differences: the men with Saul "hear the voice but see no one" in 9:7 versus "see the light but do not hear the voice" in 22:9, a classic apparent contradiction often resolved as a hearing-of-sound versus comprehension-of-meaning distinction; the commission to the Gentiles is mediated through Ananias in chapter 9, given indirectly through Ananias and a temple vision in chapter 22, and given directly by Christ on the road in chapter 26; the reference to Saul's persecution-zeal is more developed in the later accounts; and the kicking-against-the-goads proverb (a Greek and Latin commonplace from Pindar to Euripides to Terence) appears only in the chapter 26 account. **Marguerat's** narrative analysis and **Gaventa's** *From Darkness to Light* read the three retellings as a Lukan rhetorical strategy: each version is shaped to its narrative audience (church readers in chapter 9, Jerusalem mob in chapter 22, Roman-Herodian elite in chapter 26), with Luke's compositional artistry visible in the variations rather than evidence of conflicting sources.

The chapter's structure pairs **Saul's Damascus-road encounter** (9:1–22, an "outsider" brought in) with **Peter's coastal-plain ministry** (9:32–43, the apostolic insider extending his reach), separated by the bridge of Saul's escape from Damascus and his Jerusalem visit (9:23–31). The pairing is deliberate. **Tannehill** notes that Luke is keeping two narrative threads alive simultaneously — the Pauline thread that will dominate chapters 13–28, and the Petrine thread that will climax in the Cornelius narrative of chapter 10. Peter's healing of Aeneas (9:32–35) and raising of Tabitha/Dorcas (9:36–43) prepare for chapter 10 by placing Peter on the coastal plain at Joppa, in the house of Simon the tanner — a tradesman whose work with animal hides made him ritually questionable, foreshadowing the food-purity-and-table-fellowship questions of chapter 10. Tabitha is the only person in Acts explicitly called *mathētria* (μαθήτρια, "disciple," feminine form, 9:36), a detail commentators (Barrett, Gaventa, Keener) regularly note: Luke is signaling that her death and resurrection are not narrative ornament but a deliberate placement of a named woman disciple, supported by the local widows, at the seam between the Saul and Cornelius narratives.

The Pauline-letter parallels to Acts 9 must be read alongside the Lukan account. **Galatians 1:11–24** is Paul's own first-person account: he was called by a revelation of God's Son (1:15–16, with deliberate echoes of Isaiah 49:1 and Jeremiah 1:5), did *not* immediately confer with Jerusalem but went to Arabia and back to Damascus, and only after three years went up to Jerusalem to see Cephas and James for fifteen days. The Lukan account in Acts 9 compresses the Arabian sojourn (which Luke does not mention) and structures the Jerusalem visit through Barnabas's mediation. **John Knox's** *Chapters in a Life of Paul* (1950) and **Stanley Porter's** more recent work argued that the Lukan and Pauline chronologies cannot be flatly harmonized but reflect compositionally distinct sources working with overlapping but not identical traditions. The compositional question is whether Luke had access to Pauline letters; the consensus (Pervo, Marguerat, Marshall) is that Luke knew Pauline tradition and possibly some letters but composed independently from a travel-itinerary source and oral traditions.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Jewish interpretive trajectory on Acts 9 is dominated by the modern **Paul Within Judaism** movement, which has fundamentally reframed the chapter. **Krister Stendahl's** foundational essay "**Call rather than Conversion**" in *Paul Among Jews and Gentiles* (1976) argued that the Damascus-road event should not be read through the Augustinian or Lutheran category of "conversion" — implying a shift from one religion to another, from a guilty conscience to a saved one — but through the prophetic-call category of Isaiah 6, Jeremiah 1, and Ezekiel 1–3. Paul, on Stendahl's reading, did not convert from Judaism to Christianity (categories that would be anachronistic in the 30s CE); he received a *call* from one Jewish vocation (Pharisaic Torah-zealot) to another (apostle to the Gentiles), within a continuous Jewish framework. **E. P. Sanders's** *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977) provided the broader scholarly groundwork by demonstrating that first-century Judaism was not the legalistic foil Lutheran exegesis had constructed, freeing Pauline interpretation from the Augustinian-Lutheran reading frame. **Pamela Eisenbaum's** *Paul Was Not a Christian* (2009), **Matthew Thiessen's** *A Jewish Paul* (2023), and **Paula Fredriksen's** *Paul: The Pagan's Apostle* (2017) developed the Paul-Within-Judaism school: Paul never left Judaism, never abandoned Torah for himself, and understood his mission as the eschatological turning of Gentiles to the God of Israel without their conversion to Judaism. **N. T. Wright's** *Paul: A Biography* (2018) gives a more mediating reading — Paul did experience the Damascus event as a decisive reorganization of his understanding, but the reorganization happened within Jewish-messianic categories, not against them.

The classical Jewish reception of Paul has been less developed because Paul became central to Christian theology rather than a figure of Jewish reflection. **Daniel Boyarin's** *A Radical Jew: Paul and the Politics of Identity* (1994) is a notable exception — Boyarin reads Paul as a Jewish thinker grappling with the universalization of Israel's particular vocation, and argues that the Christian-Jewish boundary is itself a later patristic-rabbinic construction Paul himself does not yet operate within. **Mark Nanos's** edited *Paul Within Judaism* (2015) and **Kathy Ehrensperger's** work continue this trajectory.

The Christian interpretive trajectory is dominated by the **Augustinian-Lutheran** reading of Damascus as the paradigm of conversion. **Augustine's** *Confessions* explicitly models his own conversion narrative on Saul's, and **Augustine's** anti-Pelagian writings make Saul's experience the prototype of unmerited grace overcoming a violently resisting will. **Martin Luther's** *Lectures on Galatians* (1535) reads the Damascus event through Luther's own Anfechtung-and-resolution categories, making Saul-Paul the prototype of the believer freed from law-righteousness into faith-righteousness. **John Calvin's** *Commentary on Acts* preserves the conversion frame while emphasizing the chosen-instrument designation (9:15) as evidence of God's electing call. Patristic readings — **Chrysostom's** *Homilies on Acts* (Homilies 19–21 cover chapter 9), **Augustine's** *Sermons on Paul*, **Bede's** *Expositio Actuum* — read the chapter as the model of God's transformative grace and as the foundational moment for Pauline apostolic authority. **John Wesley's** sermons on the Damascus road frame Methodist conversion theology around the chapter. The conversion-paradigm reading dominated Christian theology and biography until the late twentieth century, producing such canonical texts as Bunyan's *Grace Abounding* and the broader evangelical-conversion narrative tradition.

Among the major contemporary Acts commentaries, **Bruce** (NICNT), **Barrett** (ICC), **Fitzmyer** (Anchor Bible), **Gaventa** (Abingdon — especially *From Darkness to Light* on Acts 9), **Johnson** (Sacra Pagina), **Keener** (4 vols), **Witherington III** (socio-rhetorical), **Schnabel** (ZECNT), **Conzelmann** (Hermeneia), **Pervo** (Hermeneia), **Peterson** (Pillar), **Marguerat**, and **Marshall** (TNTC) all give the chapter substantial treatment. Pentecostal scholarship (**Stronstad**, **Menzies**) reads Saul's filling with the Spirit (9:17) as a paradigm for Spirit-baptism distinct from initial conversion. **Justo González**'s *Acts (Belief)* emphasizes that Saul's transformation involves explicit reorientation of social-political loyalties — the persecutor becomes the persecuted, and the institutional insider becomes the marginal outsider. **Beverly Gaventa's** narrative-critical work (*From Darkness to Light*) draws particular attention to the chapter's two halves — Saul's reversal and Peter's coastal ministry — and to the named woman disciple Tabitha and the unnamed widows whose grief at her death drives the Joppa scene forward.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions structure the chapter. **First**, was Damascus a **call or a conversion**? The **traditional Christian reading** (Augustine, Luther, Calvin, the broader evangelical tradition) treats Damascus as a paradigm of *conversion* — Paul moves from one religion (Judaism) or religious posture (works-righteousness) to another (faith in Christ), and the chapter is the foundational Christian story of unmerited grace. The **Stendahl/Sanders/Paul-Within-Judaism reading** treats it as a *call* — Paul moves from one vocation within Judaism (Pharisaic Torah-zealot) to another within Judaism (apostle to the Gentiles), with no conversion of religious framework. The **mediating reading** (N. T. Wright, Beverly Gaventa) holds that Damascus *was* a decisive cognitive-religious reorganization for Paul (not merely a vocational shift) but the reorganization happened *within* Second Temple Jewish-messianic categories, not against them. The steel-man for the conversion reading is that Paul himself describes a fundamental reordering of his evaluations (Phil 3:7–9: "whatever gain I had, I counted as loss"), and the Acts narrative emphasizes the discontinuity between Saul-the-persecutor and Saul-the-preacher. The steel-man for the call reading is that Paul himself uses prophetic-call language (Gal 1:15, "set me apart before I was born and called me through his grace," echoing Isa 49:1, Jer 1:5) and never describes himself as having left Judaism. The steel-man for the mediating reading is that both elements — discontinuity and continuity — are textually grounded and the choice is theological, not exegetical.

**Where this leaves us:** the conversion-versus-call binary itself is a category error. Damascus was a *messianic-eschatological recognition* — Paul came to recognize the crucified Jesus as the resurrected Messiah whose vindication required a fundamental reordering of his understanding of Torah, Israel, and the Gentiles — that does not fit either modern category cleanly. The Augustinian-Lutheran reading captured the depth of the reorganization but mislocated its content (importing later Christian-against-Jewish framing into a first-century Jewish-messianic event). The Stendahl/Paul-Within-Judaism reading captured the continuity but sometimes underplays the depth of Paul's experiential and theological transformation. Both are correctives to each other; neither is the final word.

**Second**, the **role of Ananias** in Saul's call. Why does Christ commission Saul through a vision but require **a Damascus disciple** to lay hands on him for the restoration of sight, baptism, and Spirit-filling? The **ecclesial reading** (Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican) treats Ananias's role as a programmatic affirmation that the apostolic commission is mediated through the church — even Paul, the great apostle, is brought into the church through the laying-on of an ordinary disciple. The **charismatic reading** (Pentecostal, Stronstad) emphasizes that the Spirit-filling at 9:17 is mediated by an obscure local believer, programmatic for the Spirit's distribution beyond apostolic gatekeeping. The **Lukan-pattern reading** (Tannehill, Marguerat) reads Ananias as part of Luke's broader pattern of pairing visions across two characters (Cornelius and Peter in chapter 10, the Macedonian and Paul in chapter 16) to confirm divine agency. The steel-man for the ecclesial reading is the parallel to baptism — even the apostle Paul is baptized by an ordinary disciple, refusing self-authentication. The steel-man for the charismatic reading is that Luke deliberately routes Spirit-bestowal through obscure figures. The steel-man for the Lukan-pattern reading is that Luke is doing both at once. **Where this leaves us:** Ananias's role is theologically rich precisely because Luke is refusing apostolic self-sufficiency. The greatest apostle is mediated into the church by a disciple whose only narrative function is this episode — programmatic for the church's reception of charismatic leadership through ordinary means.

**Unresolved question**: did Paul understand the Damascus encounter at the time as a *resurrection appearance* equivalent to those of the original apostles (his claim in 1 Cor 15:8, "last of all, as to one untimely born, he appeared also to me"), and how does that claim relate to the Lukan distinction between the forty-day appearances and Paul's later vision? The question is consequential for the structure of apostolic authority in the early church and for the question of whether the apostolic office is a closed historical category or remains open through Christophanic experience.

## The Thread

The "chosen instrument" designation of 9:15 — "to carry my name before the Gentiles and kings and the children of Israel" — programs the entire second half of Acts: chapters 13–28 will narrate the Gentile mission (Gentiles), the trials before Felix, Festus, and Agrippa II (kings), and the synagogue confrontations (the children of Israel) in exactly the order specified. Saul's Damascus-road experience parallels structurally with Cornelius's vision in chapter 10 — both are double-vision narratives in which God works on two figures simultaneously to bring them together. Saul's basket-escape from Damascus (9:25) is corroborated by Paul's own first-person account at 2 Cor 11:32–33, providing rare cross-verification between Acts and the Pauline letters. Tabitha's resurrection (9:40) deliberately echoes Jesus's "Talitha koum" at Mark 5:41 and Elijah-Elisha resurrection narratives at 1 Kings 17 and 2 Kings 4 — Luke is constructing a programmatic prophet-typology around Peter that parallels his programmatic Moses-typology around Stephen. Finally, Peter's lodging "with one Simon, a tanner" at 9:43 — a tradesman whose work made him constantly handling animal hides and therefore in a state of recurrent ritual question — is a deliberate narrative setup for the food-purity vision that opens chapter 10.
