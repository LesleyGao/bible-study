# Isaiah 42 — Go Deeper

If Isaiah 40 announces consolation and Isaiah 41 stages the courtroom, Isaiah 42 introduces the figure who will dominate the rest of Second Isaiah and, for Christian readers, the rest of the Bible: the *servant of Yahweh*. The first nine verses of this chapter are the **First Servant Song**, identified as such by Bernhard Duhm in his epoch-making 1892 commentary. *Behold my servant, whom I uphold, my chosen, in whom my soul delights; I have put my Spirit upon him; he will bring forth justice to the nations.* Few sentences in the Hebrew Bible have generated more commentary, more theological argument, and more interfaith conversation than these. Matthew quotes the song in full as fulfilled in Jesus; the Targum reads it as messianic; modern Jewish readers read the servant as Israel; modern critical scholars debate whether the songs even form a distinct unit. The chapter then turns surprisingly: from the gentle servant of vv. 1–9, the text moves to a Yahweh going forth like a *man of war* (v. 13), and finally to a stark reminder that the empirical servant — Israel itself — is in fact blind, deaf, plundered, and burned by Yahweh's own anger.

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The setting remains Babylonian exile, around 545–540 BC, with the anonymous prophet Second Isaiah watching Cyrus the Persian rise to power and announcing that the God of Israel is the one moving these geopolitical tectonic plates. The mainstream-critical consensus (Westermann's *OTL Isaiah 40–66*, Blenkinsopp's *AB*, Goldingay's *ICC*, Roy Melugin, Patricia Tull) places Isaiah 40–55 in this exilic setting; conservative-evangelical scholars (Motyer, Oswalt, E.J. Young) defend eighth-century Isaianic authorship; Brevard Childs's canonical-criticism brackets the historical question and reads the book as canonically unified. For Isaiah 42, the question of dating matters less than for chapter 44 (where Cyrus is named), but the language of *prisoners*, *dungeon*, *plundered and looted*, *trapped in holes and hidden in prisons* (vv. 7, 22) presupposes the exile as present reality, not future threat.

The First Servant Song's literary form was first systematically isolated by Bernhard Duhm in 1892. Duhm identified four songs — 42:1–4 (sometimes extended to 42:1–9), 49:1–6 (extended to 49:1–13), 50:4–9 (extended to 50:4–11), and 52:13–53:12 — and argued they stood apart from their surrounding context as a distinct collection that had been later inserted into Second Isaiah. Subsequent scholarship has both refined and complicated Duhm's thesis. Some scholars (Mowinckel, North) accept the four-song demarcation but read the songs as integral to Second Isaiah's argument. Others (Mettinger, *A Farewell to the Servant Songs*, 1983) argue the *songs* are not a separate genre at all but simply emerge from continuous reading of the surrounding material. Most contemporary commentators take a middle position: the songs are recognizable units that contribute to Second Isaiah's overall argument, and Duhm's grouping remains useful even if his original *separate-collection* thesis is overstated.

The ANE parallel that bears most directly on Isaiah 42 is the **Cyrus Cylinder**, particularly the line in which Cyrus claims to have been called by Marduk to set the world right. Second Isaiah's claim — *I am the Lord; I have called you in righteousness; I will take you by the hand and keep you* (42:6) — uses the same imagery of divine commissioning and hand-grasping that appears in royal Mesopotamian inscriptions where a god takes the king by the hand. The prophet is consciously deploying the imperial commissioning vocabulary, but applying it to the servant who is the opposite of an emperor — the one who will *not cry aloud, not lift up his voice, not break a bruised reed*.

The Hebrew terms anchor the chapter's theology. *Hēn ʿabdî ʾetmāḵ-bô bəḥîrî rāṣətâ napšî nātattî rûḥî ʿālāyw* (הֵן עַבְדִּי אֶתְמָךְ־בּוֹ בְּחִירִי רָצְתָה נַפְשִׁי נָתַתִּי רוּחִי עָלָיו), *behold my servant, whom I uphold, my chosen, in whom my soul delights; I have put my Spirit upon him* (42:1). The verb *ʾetmāḵ* (uphold, sustain) and the phrase *rāṣətâ napšî* (my soul delights, my whole being takes pleasure) signal Yahweh's deepest favor; the noun *rûaḥ* (Spirit) connects the servant to the prophets, the kings, and the spirit-of-creation in Genesis 1. *Qāneh rāṣûṣ lōʾ yišbôr* (קָנֶה רָצוּץ לֹא יִשְׁבּוֹר), *a bruised reed he will not break* (42:3), uses *qāneh* for the marsh-reed of the Jordan and Egyptian deltas and the participle *rāṣûṣ* for *crushed* or *bruised*; the image is of the most fragile life imaginable, and the servant's tenderness toward it. *Ûpištâ kēhâ lōʾ yəḵabbennâ* (וּפִשְׁתָּה כֵהָה לֹא יְכַבֶּנָּה), *a faintly burning wick he will not quench* (42:3), pictures a flax-wick about to extinguish itself in an oil-lamp. *Bərît ʿām ləʾôr gôyim* (בְּרִית עָם לְאוֹר גּוֹיִם), *a covenant for the people, a light for the nations* (42:6) — the universalist scope of the servant's mission. *Šîrû la-YHWH šîr ḥādāš* (שִׁירוּ לַיהוָה שִׁיר חָדָשׁ), *sing to the Lord a new song* (42:10) — a refrain that runs through Psalms 33, 96, 98, 144, 149 and Revelation 5:9, 14:3.

## Lens 2 — Literature

Isaiah 42 contains several distinct units. The **First Servant Song** proper (vv. 1–4) is a divine speech presenting the servant to the heavenly council in language reminiscent of royal commissioning. Verses 5–9 extend the song with a direct address to the servant in covenantal-messianic language (*I am the Lord, I have called you in righteousness*). Verses 10–13 are a **hymnic call to praise**, structured as a *new song* with refrains echoed across Second Isaiah and the Psalms. Verses 14–17 give the divine perspective: Yahweh as the *woman in labor* who has restrained herself and now goes forth in delivering action — one of the most striking feminine images of God in the Hebrew Bible. Verses 18–25 turn back to Israel as the empirical *servant* who is *blind* and *deaf*, plundered and looted by Yahweh's own judgment.

The literary contrast within the chapter is deliberate. The servant of vv. 1–4 is gentle, quiet, attentive to the bruised reed. The servant of vv. 18–25 is blind, deaf, refractory, judged. The chapter does not explicitly resolve this tension; readers from antiquity onward have asked whether the *servant* of v. 1 and the *servant* of v. 19 are the same figure. One reading holds that the chapter is dialectical: Yahweh's vision for the servant (gentle, justice-bearing, light to the nations) confronts the empirical reality (blind, deaf, plundered) — and this gap will be the engine driving the rest of Second Isaiah's argument. Another reading separates the figures: the v. 1 servant is the ideal, perhaps individuated; the v. 19 servant is the corporate empirical Israel. A third reading (Westermann) holds that the song-servant has an individual character distinct from the surrounding corporate-Israel servant material.

The poetic imagery of vv. 13–14 deploys two extraordinary metaphors for Yahweh in succession: the *man of war* and the *woman in labor* (*kayyôlēdâ*, "like a woman in childbirth"). The juxtaposition is intentional, and commentators have wrestled with both. Westermann, Goldingay, and Brueggemann all note that the labor image is not decoration but argument: the violence of bringing-forth is the same violence as the warrior's, but the goal is birth — Yahweh has "kept still" and "restrained" himself (v. 14a) and now cannot hold back the new thing he is delivering. The maternal language here belongs to the same cluster as 49:15 ("Can a woman forget her nursing child?") and 66:13 ("As one whom his mother comforts, so I will comfort you"), and mainstream Isaiah scholarship treats it as part of Second and Third Isaiah's deliberate stretching of the divine-image vocabulary, not as an isolated curiosity.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

For Jewish tradition, the First Servant Song has a long messianic interpretation alongside the corporate-Israel reading. The Targum Jonathan (the Aramaic paraphrase used in synagogue worship) opens 42:1 with *behold my servant Messiah*, making the messianic reading explicit. The Talmud (b. Sanhedrin 98b) and several midrashic traditions read the servant as messianic. Rashi reads the servant as Israel; Ibn Ezra, the prophet himself; Radak, mostly the Messiah; Abarbanel, Israel collectively. The medieval Jewish tradition is therefore not monolithic on the servant's identity. Modern Jewish scholarship has tended to emphasize the corporate-Israel reading (Levenson, Boyarin, Sarna), partly as a response to Christian Christological readings, but Boyarin in particular has argued (*The Jewish Gospels*, 2012) that pre-Christian Jewish messianism included a suffering-servant-Messiah figure, and that Christian Christological readings draw on pre-existing Jewish interpretive options.

For Christian tradition, Isaiah 42:1–4 is one of the most important Old Testament texts in the New. Matthew 12:18–21 quotes the entire song in full as fulfilled in Jesus, framing it with *this was to fulfill what was spoken by the prophet Isaiah*. The voice from heaven at Jesus' baptism — *this is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased* (Matthew 3:17) — echoes the *bəḥîrî rāṣətâ napšî* of 42:1, *my chosen, in whom my soul delights*. Luke's Simeon canticle (Luke 2:32, *a light for revelation to the Gentiles*) draws on 42:6's *light for the nations*. Acts 13:47 applies the *light for the nations* to Paul's mission. The **Suffering Servant** identification of Jesus, which becomes central in Acts 8:32–35 (Philip and the Ethiopian eunuch reading Isaiah 53), is anchored in the song-tradition that begins here in chapter 42. Justin Martyr's *Dialogue with Trypho* (mid-second century) makes the servant-equals-Christ identification central to the Christian-Jewish argument; Origen, Jerome, Aquinas, Calvin all develop it. Modern critical Christian commentary (Westermann, Blenkinsopp, Goldingay, N.T. Wright) reads the song first in its sixth-century BC setting and treats the New Testament's Christological reading as theological appropriation that extends rather than erases the original sense.

The **bruised reed and faintly burning wick** image has had an enormous pastoral and devotional reception. Richard Sibbes's *The Bruised Reed and Smoking Flax* (1630), one of the great Puritan devotional works, takes its title from 42:3 (King James Version's translation of *qāneh rāṣûṣ* and *pištâ kēhâ*). The image continues to anchor pastoral counsel in Christian mental-health and trauma-care contexts: the servant-Messiah does not despise the broken, but tends them. Modern hymnody draws extensively on the chapter; *Sing a New Song* and similar contemporary worship songs work from 42:10.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

Two tensions run through Isaiah 42 and must be named.

The first is the **identity of the servant**. Three positions deserve hearing. The mainstream-Jewish reading (Rashi, modern Levenson, Boyarin in his classical mode) holds that the servant is corporate Israel throughout Second Isaiah; the song's individual features are figurative, applying corporate Israel's vocation to the people in personalized language. The mainstream-Christian reading (Matthew 12 onward, through Justin, Origen, Calvin) holds that the servant is fluidly both Israel and the coming Messiah, and that Christ fulfills, embodies, and extends Israel's servant vocation; the New Testament's Christological reading is constitutive of Christian faith. N. T. Wright works this terrain in *The New Testament and the People of God* and *Jesus and the Victory of God*: for him Isaiah 40–55 tells the controlling story of exile-and-return, and the Servant is "Israel reduced to one" — the figure who carries Israel's vocation when Israel itself proves blind and deaf (exactly the move Isaiah 42 makes between v. 1 and v. 19). Jesus, on Wright's reading, deliberately took this Servant-script onto himself; the "arm of YHWH revealed" (52:10, 53:1) is the announcement that in this figure YHWH himself is returning to act. The mainstream-critical reading (Westermann, Blenkinsopp, Goldingay) holds that the song's servant has an individual character that may refer to the prophet himself, to a coming royal figure, or to an idealized representative of the people; the corporate-Israel framing established in 41:8 remains the controlling backdrop, but the songs admit individual features that resist pure-corporate reading.

Steelmanned: a thoughtful Jewish reader can grant that the song-servant has individual features without abandoning the corporate framing; a thoughtful Christian reader can grant the priority of Israel-as-servant without surrendering Christology, because the Christian claim is that Christ embodies Israel's vocation rather than replacing Israel; a thoughtful critical scholar can grant both the historical-exilic setting and the legitimacy of subsequent religious appropriation.

The second tension is the **bruised reed image** and its modern devotional appropriation. Christian pastoral practice has used 42:3 powerfully for two thousand years to assure the broken that the Messiah does not despise them. The image is real and the comfort is real. But the chapter as a whole is more complex: the servant who does not break the bruised reed is the same Yahweh who in vv. 13–17 goes forth as a warrior and a woman in labor and lays waste the mountains, and who in vv. 22–25 *poured on him the heat of his anger* against the empirical servant Israel. The pastoral image cannot be detached from the larger theology. Modern critical scholarship (Brueggemann, *Theology of the Old Testament*) has emphasized that Second Isaiah's God is both consoler and warrior, both gentle with the bruised reed and unflinching in judgment, and that any reading that takes only one half cannot be the canonical reading.

Where this leaves us: a chapter that introduces the figure who will dominate the rest of Second Isaiah, presents him in the gentlest possible terms, and then immediately reminds the reader that the empirical servant — the people of Israel — is in fact blind, deaf, and burning. The gap between vision and reality is the engine of the rest of the book.

Closing question: If the servant of v. 1 is gentle with bruised reeds, and the servant of v. 19 is himself a bruised reed — blind, deaf, plundered — what does it mean to be both the one who consoles and the one who needs consolation, the one who brings light to the nations and the one who sits in the dungeon?
