# Psalm 10: Why Do You Stand Far Off?

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Psalm 10 has no superscription in the Masoretic Text — the only psalm in Book I (Pss 1-41) without one besides the deliberate prologue Psalm 1. This absence is not an accident. It is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that **Psalm 10 was originally the second half of a single composition with Psalm 9**, an alphabetic acrostic split somewhere in the textual tradition. The Septuagint and Vulgate preserve the unity (LXX Psalm 9 = MT Psalms 9-10), and the Catholic and Orthodox numbering tradition has followed the LXX throughout: Catholic Ps 9 covers what Protestants split into 9 and 10. (See the extended treatment of the unity question in the Lens 1 of Psalm 9; here we treat Ps 10 in its own register, but the historical fact of the original unity is foundational to everything that follows.)

The acrostic continues from Psalm 9's *aleph* through *teth* into Psalm 10's *lamed, mem, nun, samekh, ayin, pe, tsade, qoph, resh, shin, tav* — covering the bulk of the alphabet with damage and dislocation in the middle (the *yod, kaph* region around the seam between the two psalms is where the disruption is most severe). Frank Moore Cross and David Noel Freedman's reconstructive work, taken up by Patrick D. Miller, Erich Zenger, and Hossfeld-Zenger (*Die Psalmen*), treats the damage as transmission corruption rather than original irregularity, though some scholars (Beth Tanner, deClaissé-Walford) note the theological resonance of a broken acrostic in a poem about a broken world.

Form-critically, Psalm 10 alone is a paradigm *individual lament* (*Klagelied des Einzelnen* in Gunkel's *Einleitung in die Psalmen*) with extended *description-of-the-wicked* as its central middle section — what some commentators (Hossfeld-Zenger) call a *Feindbeschreibung*, an enemy-description of unusual length and concentration. Verses 2-11 contain the most concentrated portrayal of the wicked oppressor anywhere in the Psalter; the wicked is the structural subject of the middle section before the speaker turns to plead at v. 12.

Mowinckel (*The Psalms in Israel's Worship*) located such psalms in a sanctuary procedure for the falsely accused or socially-oppressed, with the description-of-the-wicked functioning as the speaker's evidence in his case before YHWH. The poor man enters the temple, recites the wicked man's deeds, and asks God to act.

ANE backdrop: Mesopotamian *šuilla* prayers and the *dingir.shà.dib.ba* lament-prayers similarly place an extended portrayal of the oppressor before the petition for divine intervention. Egyptian execration texts and *Wisdom of Amenemope* offer parallels for the social-justice register. The image of the wicked as a *lion* in ambush (v. 9) draws on a stock ANE poetic figure for predatory power that recurs across Ugaritic, Akkadian, and Egyptian literature.

Three Hebrew terms anchor the psalm. *Resha* (רָשָׁע, "wicked") sounds eight times — a concentrated density unmatched in any other Psalter chapter. The wicked is the psalm's structural protagonist. *Yatom* (יָתוֹם, "fatherless," v. 14, 18) and *dak* (דַּךְ, "oppressed/crushed," v. 18) are the social-justice terms that root this psalm in the legal-prophetic tradition: Exod 22:21-24 ("you shall not wrong a sojourner... a widow or fatherless child"), Deut 10:18 ("he executes justice for the fatherless and the widow"), Isa 1:17 ("seek justice, correct oppression, defend the fatherless, plead the widow's cause"). The Psalter and the Torah and the Prophets converge on this vocabulary; God is *the* defender of the *yatom*.

The wicked's signature speech is *ein elohim* (אֵין אֱלֹהִים, "there is no God," v. 4). This formulation appears with structural significance only here, at Psalm 14:1, and at Psalm 53:1 — and in all three places it characterizes the *naval*, the fool, by his denial. The denial is not metaphysical atheism in the modern philosophical sense; it is *practical* atheism — God will not call to account, will not see, will not act. The wicked man's statement in v. 11 makes this explicit: "God has forgotten; he has hidden his face; he will never see."

Textually, Psalm 10 is preserved at Qumran in 4QPsa and other fragmentary witnesses; the Qumran witnesses agree with the MT in its split form, making the split a pre-Christian fact. There is significant text-critical difficulty in vv. 3-5 and v. 10, where the Hebrew is awkward and the LXX diverges; some commentators (Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger) propose emendations, but no consensus reading has emerged.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Psalm 10 has eighteen verses in three structural movements:

- (vv. 1-11) The opening lament-question and the extended *Feindbeschreibung* of the wicked
- (vv. 12-15) The petition for God to arise and act
- (vv. 16-18) The closing confidence: the LORD is king, the *yatom* and *dak* will receive justice

The opening question — *lamah YHWH ta'amod be-rachoq* (לָמָה יְהוָה תַּעֲמֹד בְּרָחוֹק, "why, O LORD, do you stand far off?") — is one of the great theological openings in the Psalter. The verb *amad* (to stand) paired with *rachoq* (far off) creates a vivid image of a God who is *present in posture but distant in proximity* — visible but unreachable. Walter Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*) treats this opening as the paradigm utterance of *disorientation*: the speaker knows God exists, knows God is here, and asks why God's hereness does nothing.

Hebrew parallelism organizes the lines. The wicked's interior monologue is reported through synonymous-intensifying parallelism — vv. 6 ("I shall not be moved / throughout all generations I shall not meet adversity") and v. 11 ("God has forgotten / he has hidden his face / he will never see") both stack three parallel clauses that escalate the wicked's confidence. Robert Alter's principle of "intensification" is operating: the second and third clauses do not echo the first; they push it further into self-assurance.

The lion-in-ambush imagery of vv. 8-10 is the longest-sustained predator metaphor in the Psalter, running across three verses. The wicked sits, watches, lurks, draws into the net, crushes. James Kugel (*The Idea of Biblical Poetry*) and Adele Berlin (*The Dynamics of Biblical Parallelism*) both treat extended metaphors like this as signs of poetic seriousness — the poet is not just decorating but constructing a sustained moral image.

The structural climax falls at v. 12 (*qumah YHWH*, "Arise, O LORD") — the same warrior-arousal formula as Numbers 10:35 (the Ark) and Psalm 7:6, with deep Ugaritic and ANE resonance. After the long enemy-description, this is the speaker's first imperative. The poem has spent eleven verses portraying the wicked; now it turns and demands God act.

The closing imprecation — *shevor zeroa rasha* (שְׁבֹר זְרוֹעַ רָשָׁע, "break the arm of the wicked," v. 15) — uses the metaphor of the *zeroa* (arm), which in biblical Hebrew is the seat of physical and political power; "breaking the arm" is breaking the *capacity* for violence. This is the same body-politics that runs through 1 Sam 2:31 (Eli's house) and Job 38:15 ("the high arm is broken").

Westermann's lament structure is fully present: invocation (v. 1, posed as question), complaint about both God (v. 1) and enemies (vv. 2-11), petition (vv. 12, 15), motivation (vv. 13-14), and closing confidence (vv. 16-18). The *Stimmungsumschwung* — the turn from lament to confidence — falls in vv. 14-16, more gradual than the abrupt turn in Psalm 6 or Psalm 7.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Psalm 10 sits in Book I (Pss 1-41) as the second half of the Pss 9-10 unity. Its placement within Wilson's editorial structure (*The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter*) puts it in the early-Davidic lament cluster that gives way to the praise-and-trust psalms of 11-14 and beyond. Read as the second half of the Pss 9-10 unity, it functions as the *lament half* of an alternating composition whose first half was thanksgiving — and the alternation is theologically generative: the same speaker who praises God's enthroned justice (Ps 9) is the speaker who cries out at God's apparent absence (Ps 10).

The Jewish interpretive trajectory begins with the LXX preservation of the unity. *Midrash Tehillim* on (LXX) Psalm 9 expands the wicked's portrait into a typology of every imperial oppressor of Israel — Pharaoh, Sennacherib, Nebuchadnezzar, the Roman emperors. Rashi reads the wicked as historical political enemies of David's era; Ibn Ezra treats the wicked as a generic moral type without specific historical referent; Radak develops the eschatological reading of v. 18 ("man who is of the earth may strike terror no more") as messianic. Maimonides cites v. 4 ("there is no God") in the *Guide of the Perplexed* as evidence that the Bible's "atheism" is always practical, never metaphysical — the wicked do not deny God's existence as a logical proposition but deny God's *active rule*. Modern Jewish thought (Heschel, *The Prophets*; Levenson, *Creation and the Persistence of Evil*) treats the psalm as one of the great biblical articulations of God-as-defender-of-the-orphan; the social-justice vocabulary connects directly to the prophetic tradition.

The Christian interpretive trajectory inherited the LXX/Vulgate unity through Augustine, Cassiodorus, Aquinas, and the medieval Latin Western tradition (Glossa Ordinaria, Bernard, Bonaventure). Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on (LXX) Psalm 9 reads the wicked allegorically as Antichrist — the figure who says "there is no God" is the eschatological enemy of the church. The "lion in ambush" becomes Satan; the "fatherless" become the church under persecution. This allegorical reading dominated medieval Christian commentary.

The Reformation reasserted the literal sense. Luther read Psalm 10 as the cry of the oppressed peasant against the godless prince (anticipating his complicated stance toward the Peasants' War of 1525). Calvin's commentary treats the psalm as protest against actual social-political oppression and reads the speaker as the *afflicted faithful* in any era who suffer under unjust power. Calvin pushed back against the allegorizing tradition by insisting on the historical-literal sense first.

Modern critical scholarship (Gunkel, Mowinckel, Kraus *BKAT*, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Allen *WBC*, Tate, deClaissé-Walford-Jacobson-Tanner) reads Psalm 10 as one of the great social-justice psalms — the *anavim* (poor) tradition that runs through the Psalter and finds its New Testament echo in the Magnificat (Luke 1:46-55) and the Beatitudes (Matt 5:3-12). James L. Mays's *Psalms* commentary (Interpretation series) treats the psalm as central to what he calls the "theology of God's reign," in which YHWH's kingship is most decisively manifested in defense of the powerless. Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*) reads Psalm 10 as paradigm-disorientation moving toward new orientation; in his later work (*Theology of the Old Testament*) he treats the psalm as the voice of the oppressed within the canonical witness — the Bible's own internal protest against its own occasional silences.

The God who hears the *yatom* and the *dak* is, on the social-justice reading shared by Mays, Brueggemann, and the contemporary commentary tradition, the God of the powerless, and the psalm's prayer to "break the arm of the wicked" is the prayer of those whom the wicked have crushed.

The New Testament does not directly quote Psalm 10, but its theological substrate is everywhere. Romans 3:14 ("their mouth is full of curses and bitterness") quotes a phrase that appears at Psalm 10:7 in the LXX. The "fool says in his heart, there is no God" of Psalms 14:1 and 53:1 — verbally identical to Psalm 10:4 — is part of Paul's catena of psalm citations on universal human sinfulness in Romans 3:10-18. The Magnificat's "he has scattered the proud / brought down the mighty / lifted up the lowly" (Luke 1:51-52) is the Magnificat's reprise of the Psalter's *anavim* theology, of which Psalm 10 is a foundational text.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author's theological vision is that *God is not absent — God is paying attention even to what the wicked claims God does not see*. The psalm braids three movements: the *complaint at apparent divine distance* (v. 1), the *catalog of the wicked's confident violence* (vv. 2-11), and the *assertion of God's seeing* (v. 14: "but you do see"). The pivot is at v. 14 — *ki attah ra'itah* ("for you do see") — which directly contradicts the wicked's claim at v. 11 ("he will never see"). The psalm stages a confrontation between the wicked's epistemology of God ("God has forgotten, has hidden, will not see") and the speaker's contrary claim. What God is doing — even when standing far off — is *seeing* and *taking into hand*. Vision is the theological operator.

The first major theological tension is the violent prayer "break the arm of the wicked" (v. 15) and the Christian ethic of non-retaliation ("love your enemies," Matt 5:44; "do not repay evil for evil," Rom 12:17).

- The classical Christian allegorical reading (Augustine, Cassiodorus, Aquinas) directs the curse against the spiritual enemy — sin, the devil, Antichrist — preserving the psalm's prayability while removing its literal target. This reading has shaped almost all Christian liturgical use of the imprecatory psalms.
- The Reformed reading (Calvin, Bonhoeffer's *Psalms: The Prayer Book of the Bible*) accepts the literal sense but locates the imprecation in the *covenantal* role of the speaker as one who appeals to God's just government rather than acting on private vengeance. Calvin specifically argued that the psalm's curses would be illegitimate from a private actor but legitimate as protest of the covenant violated.
- The protest reading (Erich Zenger, *A God of Vengeance?*; Brueggemann's late work; Beth Tanner; James L. Mays) restores the prayer to the powerless. The *yatom* and the *dak* have nowhere else to go; their prayer to break the wicked's arm is the protest of those whose own arms have been broken. To censor the imprecation is to silence the violated. This is the position that has gained the most ground in contemporary scholarship.
- The Eastern Orthodox ascetic reading treats the wicked as the *logismoi* (intrusive thoughts) and the imprecation as interior spiritual warfare against the passions.
- The Jewish reading (Levenson, Heschel, Sarna, Mays) treats imprecation as part of the covenantal back-and-forth — God is not protected from the violated's anger; the prayer is *to* God, asking him to act, and the speaker's posture is submission to God's judgment rather than self-execution of vengeance. The petitioner asks God to act; the petitioner does not act.

The least-intuitive position to steelman is the protest reading with its social-location qualification. It says: the imprecation in Psalm 10 is not something for the church to spiritualize or transcend; it is something for the church to *make audible* in the prayers of the powerless and *refuse to put in the mouths* of the powerful. The same words are righteous protest in one social location and self-justifying violence in another. The psalm is right; the question is who gets to pray it.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 10 is the prayer of the *yatom*, the *dak*, the *ani*. Read in their voice, it is righteous protest; God hears it, takes it into hand, and acts. Read in the voice of the powerful, it becomes an instrument of the very oppression it protests. Christian liturgy has historically allegorized to make the psalm prayable for everyone; a contemporary, more honest reading would let the powerless pray it literally and ask the powerful to listen rather than to recite.

The second tension is the question of God's apparent absence in v. 1. Why *does* God stand far off? The psalm does not answer the question; it only asserts that the absence is not what it seems — God sees (v. 14), God hears (v. 17), God will act (v. 18). This is not a theodicy but a *trust-in-the-face-of* posture. Patrick D. Miller (*They Cried to the Lord*) calls this "the prayer of trust without resolution" — the speaker holds the absence and the assurance simultaneously, refuses to dissolve either, and waits.

- The classical theistic reading (Augustine, Aquinas) treats the divine "distance" as appearance only — God is metaphysically near but pedagogically silent for the believer's growth.
- The covenantal reading (Brueggemann) treats divine distance as a real feature of covenant life — God genuinely allows space, and the believer's protest at that space is part of the covenant relationship.
- The Jewish reading (Heschel, *The Prophets*; Levenson) treats divine distance as the structural condition of human freedom, including the wicked's freedom to do harm; God's hiddenness is the cost of the cosmos God made.
- The Holocaust-theology reading (Rubenstein, Berkovits, Eliezer Berkovits's *Faith After the Holocaust*) takes Psalm 10's "why do you stand far off?" as the foundational question of Jewish theology after Auschwitz; the psalm's refusal to answer the question is itself the most honest theological response.

**Closing question:** When the speaker says "you do see" (v. 14) immediately after the wicked has said "he will never see" (v. 11), the speaker is not reasoning from evidence — he is contradicting the wicked. What gives the speaker the standing to issue that contradiction, and what does it cost when the wicked's claim looks, on its face, more empirically supported?

## The Thread

- Psalm 9 — the first half of the original Pss 9-10 acrostic; the thanksgiving frame within which Ps 10's lament unfolds.
- Exodus 22:21-24 / Deuteronomy 10:18 — the Torah's foundational legislation for the *yatom* and the widow that Psalm 10 invokes.
- Romans 3:10-18 — Paul's catena of psalm citations on universal human sinfulness, including a phrase from Ps 10:7.
- Luke 1:46-55 — the Magnificat, which inherits and amplifies Psalm 10's *anavim* theology of the God who lifts up the lowly.
