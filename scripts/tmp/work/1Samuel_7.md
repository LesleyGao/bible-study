# 1 Samuel 7: The Stone Called "Until Here"

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter functions as the Deuteronomistic Historian's portrait of the last *judge* — the constitutional mode of pre-monarchic Israel — and is therefore staged with unusual ideological care. The setting is Iron Age I, late eleventh century BCE, after the Aphek catastrophe of 1 Samuel 4. The ark has been parked at Kiriath-jearim ("city of forests"), a settlement on the Judah–Benjamin border now generally identified with Deir el-ʿAzar near modern Abu Ghosh, where the recent Israel Antiquities/Tel Aviv excavation directed by Israel Finkelstein and Christophe Nicolle (2017–2019) has exposed an unusually large eighth–seventh-century podium that the excavators provocatively interpret as evidence of the site's lasting cultic memory. The ark sits there, in the house of Abinadab on the hill (בַּגִּבְעָה *bag-givʿah*, 7:1), under the charge of his consecrated son Eleazar — a private custodianship that lasts twenty years and ends only when David retrieves the ark in 2 Sam 6.

The geography of Samuel's circuit (7:16–17) is itself a historical claim. Bethel, Gilgal, Mizpah, and Ramah form a tight quadrilateral in the central highlands of Benjamin and southern Ephraim — exactly the territory archaeologically densest with Iron Age I four-room houses, collared-rim jars, and unfortified hilltop villages, the material signature Lawrence Stager identified as "early Israelite." Mizpah is most plausibly Tell en-Nasbeh, excavated by William Frederic Badè from 1926 to 1935 in one of American archaeology's earliest scientific Palestinian digs; the site shows continuous occupation through the Iron Age and a strategic position on the watershed road. Ramah (Samuel's home) is most likely er-Ram north of Jerusalem; Gilgal's location remains debated; Bethel is Beitin, with the Albright-Kelso excavation showing destruction layers that fit Iron I disruption.

The ritual at Mizpah deserves close historical attention because it is highly unusual. The people "drew water and poured it out before the LORD" (וַיִּשְׁאֲבוּ־מַיִם וַיִּשְׁפְּכוּ לִפְנֵי יְהוָה, 7:6). This water libation has no clear cultic prescription anywhere in the Pentateuch. Josephus (*Antiquities* 6.2.2) reads it euphemistically as the symbolic exhaustion of fasting. The Targum reads it as a metaphor for pouring out the heart in repentance (cf. Lam 2:19, "Pour out your heart like water"). McCarter and Tsumura (NICOT) note ANE parallels in Mesopotamian and Hittite purification rites where libation marks ritual self-emptying. A more daring reading — common in older comparative scholarship and revived recently by Susan Niditch and Saul Olyan — is that this is an imitative-magic rain rite, drought-breaking ceremony tied to the autumn agricultural calendar; the later Sukkot water libation at the Second Temple (m. Sukkah 4:9) may be a domesticated descendant. The chapter declines to explain it, which is itself a clue that the practice was once well known.

Three Hebrew terms repay attention. הָאַשְׁתָּרוֹת *ha-ashtarot* (7:3, 7:4) — Astartes, plural — denotes consort goddesses of the Levantine pantheon, attested in Ugaritic and Phoenician inscriptions and in the figurine record at virtually every Iron Age Israelite site (the JPF, "Judean Pillar Figurines," catalogued by Raz Kletter, are the late eighth-century cousins). הָרְעֵם *hirʿem* (7:10) — "thundered" — invokes YHWH as storm-warrior, the same theological vocabulary as Ps 18:13–14 and Ugaritic Baal cycle epithets reapplied to Israel's God. אֶבֶן הָעֵזֶר *Even ha-Ezer* (7:12) — "Stone of Help" — is etiology in its purest form; the chapter's most famous sentence, עַד־הֵנָּה עֲזָרָנוּ יְהוָה ʿad-hennah ʿazaranu YHWH, "until here YHWH has helped us," names the moment, not the destination.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre-wise, the chapter is a deliberate composite: covenant-renewal liturgy (7:3–6), holy-war narrative (7:7–11), monumental etiology (7:12), and idealized regnal summary (7:13–17). McCarter and Halpern both treat the chapter as a key piece of the Deuteronomistic Historian's stylized portrait of the *moshia*ʿ-judge — the redactor's deliberate construction of pre-monarchic Israel as it ought to have been: penitent, prayerful, victorious through divine intervention rather than royal apparatus, governed by an itinerant prophet-judge whose authority derives from intercession rather than office. The chapter is not naïve about its own purpose. It exists to make the demand for a king in chapter 8 morally inexplicable. The narrative reasoning is: *here is what was working; what could possibly justify abandoning it?*

The literary devices reinforce this. The structural inversion at 7:3–4 ("if you are returning … so the people of Israel put away") gives the people exact obedience — almost too exact, the kind of compliance the dtr longs for and rarely depicts. The Philistine attack at 7:7 interrupts the assembly *during* the covenant ritual, which is the dtr's favorite irony: the moment Israel becomes obedient is the moment external threat surfaces, and the moment external threat surfaces is the moment YHWH thunders. Samuel's intercession (7:8–9) — a nursing lamb (טָלֵה חָלָב *ṭaleh ḥalav*) offered as עֹלָה ʿolah, whole burnt offering — is the literary mirror of the Philistine guilt offering of 6:14, and the contrast is pointed: outsiders sacrifice cattle to placate; insiders sacrifice a suckling lamb to intercede. Polzin's *Samuel and the Deuteronomist* reads this Mizpah scene as the dtr's own theological self-portrait — the prophet whose voice intercedes is the dtr's own voice, addressing the exilic readership.

Bar-Efrat and Fokkelman both note the chapter's careful chiastic balance: ark to Kiriath-jearim (7:1) → repentance call (7:3–4) → assembly at Mizpah (7:5–6) → Philistine attack and divine deliverance (7:7–11) → monument (7:12) → permanent peace (7:13–14) → Samuel's circuit (7:15–17). The center is the stone, and the stone names the structure: *until here YHWH has helped us*.

Textually the chapter is comparatively stable. 4QSam-a is fragmentary at 7:1 and 7:9–12 but where preserved generally agrees with MT against the longer LXX expansions. The most consequential variant is at 7:6 where some LXX manuscripts add a longer description of the water rite, hinting that the practice was puzzling enough to invite scribal explanation. McCarter (AB) treats the addition as secondary midrash. The numbers at 7:2 — "twenty years" — are also textually stable, but they generate exegetical perplexity because the chronology elsewhere implies a much longer ark sojourn at Kiriath-jearim. The dtr framework prizes round numbers more than absolute chronology.

Within the book's argument, chapter 7 is the dtr's "before" picture. Samuel-as-judge is portrayed without flaw, his sons not yet introduced (they appear in 8:1 to introduce the chapter 8 crisis). The Philistines are not merely defeated but "subdued" (וַיִּכָּנְעוּ *vayyikkaneʿu*, 7:13) and recede from the territory for the rest of Samuel's life — a claim the rest of the book quietly contradicts (the Philistines are still threatening through Saul's reign and David's). The narrator and the redactor are willing to live with this contradiction because chapter 7 is not history; it is theology against the monarchy.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, 1 Samuel 7 is the chapter that completes the Judges-pattern (sin → oppression → cry → deliverance → peace) one final time, and then declines to continue it. The dtr places it as the literary capstone of the era of Judges before the era of kings begins; it is the deliberate bridge between Judges and Samuel, and it makes the chapter 8 demand for a king look like apostasy from a working order.

The chapter's covenant-renewal liturgy at Mizpah looks back to Joshua 24 at Shechem (the original covenant assembly with the same call to "put away foreign gods") and forward to 2 Kings 23 (Josiah's covenant renewal in Jerusalem). Brevard Childs and Frank Moore Cross both read these scenes as the dtr's signature — covenant renewal as theological reset button, always available, always identical in shape: confession, idol-removal, divine intervention, monument. The Eben-ezer stone is one of a long series of stone monuments — Jacob at Bethel (Gen 28, 35), Joshua at Shechem (Josh 24:26–27), Samuel here, the twelve stones at the Jordan (Josh 4) — that Israel's narrators use to mark the geography of memory.

**Jewish trajectory.** Josephus expands the chapter into a polished Greek narrative, emphasizing Samuel's prayerfulness and downplaying the cultic strangeness of the water libation. The rabbis of *b. Megillah* 14a list Samuel as one of the seven prophets who prophesied to seventy nations and treat his Mizpah intercession as paradigm for prayer that diverts judgment. *b. Taʿanit* 2a connects the water libation to rain liturgy, supporting Niditch's later anthropological reading. Rashi reads "Until here YHWH has helped us" as Samuel's confession that the help is past help — a humble retrospective, not a triumphalist claim. Radak focuses on the chronology of the twenty years and the meaning of the people's lament. Ralbag treats the Philistine thunder as a natural meteorological event providentially timed — characteristic Gersonidean rationalism. Abarbanel gives Samuel sustained attention as the model of *non-monarchic* governance: the prophet-judge as the constitutional ideal Israel betrayed in chapter 8. He reads Samuel's circuit (7:16–17) as deliberate refusal of court-style centralization — a portable, distributed authority whose power lay in moral persuasion and intercession, not in fortifications. Yair Zakovitch and Moshe Garsiel attend to the etiological wordplay (Eben-ezer, Mizpah, Shen) and the chapter's concentration of stone-and-place names as part of the dtr's geography of memory. Avivah Zornberg reads Samuel's intercessory voice as the prophet's defining act — the one who *cries out* on behalf of those who can no longer cry adequately for themselves.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic readers typologize. Origen reads Samuel's intercession as a figure of Christ's high-priestly prayer; Augustine in *City of God* 17.7 treats Samuel as the last figure of the old order before the institution of kingship that will eventually figure Christ. Chrysostom's homilies emphasize Samuel's tearful prayer as model for the Christian intercessor. Gregory the Great in *Pastoral Rule* uses Samuel's circuit as a type of the bishop's visitation. Aquinas (*ST* II-II) cites Mizpah as evidence that prayer can defer judgment without canceling justice. Bernard of Clairvaux invokes "until here YHWH has helped us" as the contemplative's retrospective gratitude. Calvin's sermons on 1 Samuel 7 are particularly substantial — he reads the chapter as the model of covenant renewal in any reforming community, and he draws explicit lines to sixteenth-century Geneva: the call to "put away foreign gods" becomes a sermon against Catholic relics, the water libation becomes a figure of public confession, the Eben-ezer becomes a confession that reform is divine gift not human achievement. Luther focuses on Samuel's prayer as evidence that intercession is itself a divine gift and uses the chapter polemically against works-righteousness. Among critical scholars, Wellhausen treated chapter 7 as theologically tendentious — a late dtr fiction designed to contrast with the older pro-monarchic source in chapters 9–10. Noth and Cross developed this into the standard double-redaction account: Mizpah is the Josianic dtr's idealized portrait. Brueggemann reads the chapter's achievement and the chapter 8 betrayal as the canonical Bible's clearest articulation of *theological tragedy* — Israel had what it needed and chose otherwise. Polzin reads the chapter as the dtr's ironic self-presentation. Eugene Peterson treats Samuel's circuit as the Bible's most underrated picture of pastoral leadership: presence, not headquarters. Ellen Davis and John Goldingay read pastorally the discipline of naming the stone — the practice of looking back to mark mercy. Several literary readers (Polzin, Brueggemann, Alter) note the absence of women from the chapter's covenant assembly — a striking shift from the gendered intimacy of chapter 1 — and treat it as evidence of the dtr's stylization of pre-monarchic public ritual as a male-presided liturgical event, in continuity with the assembly forms of Joshua 24 and Deuteronomy 31.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Did the Mizpah victory actually happen, or is it the dtr's theological postcard from an Israel that never was?** The Philistines, by the chapter's own account, are subdued for the rest of Samuel's life (7:13) — yet the rest of the book has the Philistines as the chronic existential threat that drives the demand for a king (chapter 8), kills Saul (chapter 31), and shadows David's career.

A *traditional Catholic* position would harmonize: the "subduing" is genuine but partial, providing Israel a generation's breathing room; renewed Philistine pressure under Saul is a separate phase reflecting Israel's later infidelity. The chapter is not exaggeration but theological framing of a real respite.

A *Reformed* position with Calvin would say the chapter records what happens whenever Israel actually returns to YHWH — and the contradictions in later chapters indicate that Mizpah's repentance did not last. The chapter is paradigmatic, not chronological.

A *Jewish rationalist* position with Ralbag and Abarbanel would read the chapter as historically minimal — a real but limited military success at one location — and the dtr's expansive language as homiletical idealization. Abarbanel especially is willing to treat the chapter as the dtr's preferred portrait of the constitutional alternative to monarchy, polemically heightened.

A *critical* position (Wellhausen, Noth, McCarter, Klein) would treat the chapter as the dtr's theological fiction projected back onto the pre-monarchic period — a foil for chapter 8 rather than a historical report. The Philistine pressure was relentless from Aphek through Gilboa; the dtr inserted a brief divinely-given pause to make the king-demand morally indefensible.

A *narrative-canonical* position (Polzin, Brueggemann, Childs) would say the historical question is the wrong question. The chapter's truth-claim is theological: when Israel actually does what 7:3 says — puts away foreign gods, returns whole-heartedly — then deliverance follows. The chapter functions as covenantal possibility, not chronological log. *Where this leaves us:* the chapter's historicity is genuinely uncertain, but its theological argument is unambiguous: pre-monarchic Israel had a working alternative to monarchy, and the rest of the book is the story of Israel choosing otherwise. Whether the alternative ever actually worked at the scale the chapter describes is a question the dtr is willing to leave open.

**Tension 2: Is intercessory prayer the cause of deliverance, the occasion of deliverance, or just the marker of deliverance?** Samuel "cried out to the LORD for Israel, and the LORD answered him" (7:9), and immediately the Philistine attack collapses (7:10).

A *Catholic* position, in continuity with the doctrine of intercessory prayer (the communion of saints, Marian intercession), would treat Samuel's prayer as genuinely causal in some real sense — the prophet's mediation makes a difference to divine action. This is the basis of the entire intercessory tradition.

A *Reformed* position, anchored in Calvin's commentary, would resist any human causation but affirm the *occasionality* — God ordains the prayer as the means by which deliverance is given. Samuel's prayer is real but not autonomous; it is itself God's gift. The deliverance is sovereignly given, not procured.

An *Orthodox* position, with the patristic and Hesychast traditions, would treat the prayer as participation rather than cause — Samuel is drawn into the divine action that is already in motion. The thunder is not produced by the prayer; the prayer and the thunder are two faces of the same divine event.

A *Jewish* position, especially in rabbinic and Hasidic strands, would emphasize the genuine agency of human petition: the *tzaddik*'s prayer can and does avert decree. The Mizpah scene is paradigm. Abarbanel reads Samuel's intercession as evidence that the prophet-judge's authority is not military but petitionary — a constitutional model that monarchy will betray.

A *critical* position would observe that the dtr is theologizing post hoc: the deliverance happened (or didn't), and the prayer narrative is the interpretive frame imposed on it to assert YHWH's agency. The question of causation is meaningless within the framework; what matters is that the chapter teaches Israel to read history through the lens of intercession.

A *contemporary skeptical* position (process theology, and the pastoral worry Brueggemann himself voices) would press hard: prayers are obviously not always answered with thunder; what does Mizpah teach us about the unanswered prayers of those whose Philistines do prevail? The chapter is not pastorally innocent. *Where this leaves us:* the chapter affirms that intercession matters without explaining how, and refuses to assure that intercession always works the way Mizpah did. The Eben-ezer is named *until here* — past help, not guaranteed future help.

*Unresolved question:* If the dtr has constructed Mizpah as the picture of an Israel that *could* have continued without a king, what does it mean that the canon preserves both this picture and the messianic-monarchic hope of Psalm 2 and Isaiah 9 — and which of these two visions is the Bible asking us, finally, to want?

## The Thread

- **Joshua 24:14–15** — "Put away the gods that your fathers served … choose this day whom you will serve." The covenant-renewal template Samuel invokes at Mizpah, almost word for word.
- **2 Kings 23:1–3** — Josiah's covenant renewal in Jerusalem after the discovery of the law-book. The dtr's Josianic redaction makes Mizpah and Josiah's reform deliberate parallels — same liturgy, same shape, framing kingship's bookends.
- **Psalm 99:6** — "Moses and Aaron were among his priests, Samuel also was among those who called upon his name." The Psalter's canonical placement of Samuel as paradigmatic intercessor.
- **Hebrews 7:25** — Christ "always lives to make intercession for them." The flagged later passage where the Mizpah pattern of prophetic intercession becomes ontologized in a high priest whose prayer is not occasional but eternal — and where "until here YHWH has helped us" finds its eschatological completion.
