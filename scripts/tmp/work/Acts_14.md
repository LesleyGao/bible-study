# Acts 14: Iconium, Lystra, Derbe — Stones, Garlands, and Elders

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Acts 14 traces the heart of the First Missionary Journey through southern Asia Minor in roughly 47–48 CE. The geography is precise: from Pisidian Antioch (where chapter 13 ended with the apostles being driven from the city) southeast some 90 miles to **Iconium** (modern Konya, Turkey), then south some 25 miles to **Lystra** (Hatunsaray, near modern Hatunsaray-Akören), then east some 60 miles to **Derbe** (likely Kerti Hüyük near Karaman), and back through the same cities in reverse before descending the Pisidian highlands through Perga to the port of **Attalia** (modern Antalya) and sailing back to Syrian Antioch. The route follows the **via Sebaste**, the Roman imperial road system that Augustus had constructed to link the new colonies of southern Galatia.

**Stephen Mitchell**'s *Anatolia: Land, Men, and Gods in Asia Minor* (Oxford, 1993, 2 vols.) remains the foundational regional history. Mitchell traces the layered identity of these cities: **Iconium** was a Phrygian-speaking city in the early Roman period, with a Greek-speaking elite; **Lystra** had been refounded as a Roman colony (*Colonia Iulia Felix Gemina Lustra*) by Augustus around 6 BCE, settled with Italian veterans, but still surrounded by a **Lycaonian-speaking** rural hinterland (note v. 11: "saying in *Lykaonisti*" — Luke's terminologically precise note that the crowd shifted from administrative Greek to local Lycaonian dialect). **Derbe** was the easternmost city of the Roman province of Galatia, a frontier town adjoining the kingdom of Antiochus IV of Commagene. The combination — Roman colony at Lystra, Hellenistic city at Iconium, frontier town at Derbe — gives the chapter its specific cultural texture.

The archaeological recovery of these cities is uneven. **Iconium** lies under modern Konya and is largely inaccessible to excavation. **Lystra** was identified by **J. R. S. Sterrett** in 1885 through a remarkable discovery: an inscribed altar from Hatunsaray bearing a Latin dedication that included the formula "*Colonia Iulia Felix Gemina Lustra*," confirming the site beyond doubt. Sterrett also documented inscriptions from the surrounding region attesting to the **cult of Zeus and Hermes** in the Lycaonian countryside — including an inscription from Sedasa near Lystra mentioning priests dedicated to "Zeus before the city" (Zeus Propolis) and to Hermes. **Sir William Mitchell Ramsay**'s *St. Paul the Traveller and the Roman Citizen* (1896) and *The Cities of St. Paul* (1907) drew this archaeological evidence into direct dialogue with Acts 14:11–13, arguing that the Lycaonian crowd's identification of Barnabas as Zeus and Paul as Hermes drew on a known local cult-pattern. The Ovidian story of Baucis and Philemon (*Metamorphoses* 8.611–724), in which Zeus and Hermes wander the Phrygian countryside in disguise and are received only by an old peasant couple, suggests the same regional Anatolian tradition — Luke's Lycaonian crowd was working out of a deeply-grooved cultural script in which the gods are expected to visit in human form.

**Derbe**'s identification has been more contested. The site was located in 1956 by **Michael Ballance** at Kerti Hüyük through an inscription, and re-examined in 1962. **Cilliers Breytenbach**'s *Paulus und Barnabas in der Provinz Galatien* (Brill, 1996) provides the standard recent treatment of the entire southern Galatian itinerary.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter. First, **Λυκαονιστί** (*Lykaonisti*, v. 11) — "in Lycaonian" — Luke's adverbial form attesting to the local non-Greek dialect of the region, a detail with no plausible explanation other than authentic Lukan-source memory. Second, **τὸν Δία... τὸν Ἑρμῆν** (*ton Dia... ton Hermēn*, v. 12) — "Zeus... Hermes" — the divine pairing attested archaeologically at Lystra and in the Ovidian Baucis-Philemon tradition. Third, **διέρρηξαν τὰ ἱμάτια αὐτῶν** (*dierrēxan ta himatia autōn*, v. 14) — "they tore their garments" — the Jewish ritual response to blasphemy (cf. Mark 14:63), here transposed to the Greco-Roman setting. Fourth, **χειροτονήσαντες πρεσβυτέρους** (*cheirotonēsantes presbyterous*, v. 23) — "having appointed elders" — the verb *cheirotoneō* originally meant "to elect by stretching out the hand," though by the first century it had broadened to mean simply "appoint." Fifth, **θύραν πίστεως** (*thyran pisteōs*, v. 27) — "door of faith" — Paul's image of the Gentile mission as a divinely-opened threshold, recurring in Pauline letters (1 Cor 16:9; 2 Cor 2:12; Col 4:3).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 14 is structured as a journey-out and journey-back. The outward leg (Iconium → Lystra → Derbe, vv. 1–20) takes the apostles eastward to the frontier; the return leg (Derbe → Lystra → Iconium → Pisidian Antioch → Perga → Attalia → Antioch, vv. 21–28) retraces the route, with the consolidating work of strengthening churches and appointing elders giving the return its distinct theological weight. **Robert Tannehill**'s *Narrative Unity of Luke-Acts* observes that this consolidating return is essential to the Lukan portrayal of Pauline mission as church-planting rather than mere itinerant evangelism: Paul does not move on from a city without leaving behind structured community.

The **Lystra speech** (14:15–17) is one of two speeches Paul delivers in Acts to a primarily pagan-Gentile audience, the other being the Areopagus speech of 17:22–31. The two are deliberately paired by Luke: the Lystra speech is the brief, rural, Lycaonian-peasant version; the Athens speech is the elaborated, urban, Hellenistic-philosophical version. Both speeches share a common structure — turn from idols to the living Creator, natural revelation as preparatory witness, no Christological content — and both deliberately omit the Old Testament citations that dominate Paul's synagogue speech in chapter 13. **Marion Soards**'s *The Speeches in Acts* (Westminster John Knox, 1994) and **Eckhard Schnabel**'s ZECNT commentary treat the pairing as deliberate Lukan typology of cross-cultural preaching: the kerygma must be inflected for its audience, but its theological substance — turn from idols to the living God — is constant.

The Lystra speech also raises a Pauline-letter question. **F. F. Bruce**'s NICNT and **Witherington**'s socio-rhetorical commentary note the close kinship between Acts 14:15–17 and **Romans 1:18–25** and **1 Thessalonians 1:9–10** ("you turned to God from idols to serve a living and true God"). The verbal-conceptual overlap is strong enough to suggest authentic Pauline kerygmatic substance preserved in the Lukan composition. **Pervo**'s Hermeneia commentary, by contrast, treats the speech as fully Lukan composition modeled on Hellenistic-Jewish anti-idol polemic.

The **stoning of Paul** at Lystra (14:19–20) corresponds to **2 Corinthians 11:25** ("once I was stoned"), one of the few external corroborations of Paul's catalog of sufferings. The narrative compression — Paul stoned, dragged outside the city, presumed dead, surrounded by disciples, rises up, enters the city, departs the next day for Derbe — has been variously read as miraculous revival (so the patristic and much modern devotional tradition) or as remarkable physical resilience (so most modern historical commentary). Luke leaves the question open, and the laconic narration is itself a literary feature.

The **appointing of elders** at v. 23 is significant for what it does and does not say. The verb *cheirotoneō* and its narrative context — "in every church, with prayer and fasting they committed them to the Lord" — establishes a pattern but not a constitutional theory. **C. K. Barrett**'s ICC observes that Luke does not specify how the elders were chosen (by apostolic appointment? congregational election? local elder consensus?), leaving subsequent traditions to read their own ecclesial structures into the silence. The verse becomes a major site of Catholic-Protestant-Orthodox debate over ordination, succession, and the relationship between apostolic authority and local ecclesial structure.

The chapter closes (v. 27) with a **report-back to the sending church** at Antioch, completing the missionary cycle that began at 13:1–3. The phrase "opened a door of faith to the Gentiles" sets the agenda for chapter 15: if the door has been opened, on what terms must the Gentiles enter?

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The **Lystra scene** has had a long and varied interpretive history. **Chrysostom**'s *Homilies on Acts* (Homilies 30–31 cover the chapter) read the apostles' tearing of their garments as the model of Christian humility against creature-worship — a contrast to imperial-cult acclamations and to the misdirected enthusiasm of pagan crowds. **Bede** read the Lystra healing as typological of Christian baptism (the lame man rises, walks, enters new life). **Calvin**'s *Commentary* drew a Reformed conclusion: pagan religion is not a partial truth to be perfected but a "vain thing" (v. 15) to be turned from, even as natural revelation (rains, fruitful seasons, v. 17) provides the universal witness against which idolatry stands condemned.

The **natural-theology** dimension of the Lystra speech became a major site of Protestant-Catholic and Reformed-Lutheran debate. **Karl Barth**'s *Church Dogmatics* (II/1, "The Knowledge of God") famously refused any natural-theology reading of Acts 14:17, arguing that the verse describes God's preserving providence rather than a knowledge-of-God available apart from revelation. **Emil Brunner**'s *Natur und Gnade* (1934) and **Wolfhart Pannenberg**'s *Systematic Theology* defended a more positive reading of natural revelation as authentic anticipation of saving knowledge. **N. T. Wright**'s *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* takes the Lystra and Athens speeches together as evidence of Paul's robust theology of creation as penultimate witness — not natural theology in the Thomist sense, but a Jewish-monotheistic insistence that creation testifies to its Creator even where the testimony is misread.

The **appointing of elders** at v. 23 has been a contested ecclesial text since the Reformation. The Catholic tradition reads the verse as evidence of apostolic ordination by laying-on-of-hands and prayer, with the elders constituting a local presbyteral structure under apostolic-episcopal authority — a reading developed in **Yves Congar**'s *Lay People in the Church* and the Vatican II *Decree on the Ministry and Life of Priests*. The Reformed and Presbyterian tradition (Calvin, the *Form of Presbyterial Church Government* of 1645, **Edward Schillebeeckx**'s *Ministry: Leadership in the Community of Jesus Christ*) reads the same verse as evidence of plural-elder governance with congregational discernment, against monepiscopal hierarchical structures. The Episcopal-Anglican tradition reads the verse as evidence of apostolic appointment with subsequent local discernment. **Gerhard Lohfink**'s *Jesus and Community* (Fortress, 1984) treats the appointing as illustrative of the dynamic between charismatic ministry and structured leadership in the early communities.

The **stoning of Paul** at Lystra has connected to the broader Pauline-suffering-and-apostolic-credentials tradition. **Karl Barth**'s exegesis of 2 Corinthians and the broader theology of *theologia crucis* take Paul's sufferings — including the Lystra stoning — as the visible sacrament of authentic apostolic vocation. **Justo González**'s liberation reading takes the same sufferings as the cost of border-crossing mission in a world organized around imperial and tribal violence.

The **contemporary commentary tradition** — **Bruce NICNT**, **Barrett ICC**, **Fitzmyer Anchor Bible**, **Gaventa Abingdon**, **Johnson Sacra Pagina**, **Keener** four-volume Hendrickson, **Witherington Socio-Rhetorical**, **Schnabel ZECNT**, **Pervo Hermeneia**, **Conzelmann Hermeneia**, **Peterson Pillar**, **Marguerat**, **Marshall TNTC** — gives substantial attention to the cross-cultural preaching question, the archaeological background of the Zeus-Hermes cult, and the ecclesial implications of v. 23. **Schnabel**'s *Early Christian Mission* (IVP, 2004, 2 vols.) draws the chapter into his broader reconstruction of Pauline missionary practice. **Cilliers Breytenbach**'s *Paulus und Barnabas in der Provinz Galatien* is the standard regional study.

The **Jewish interpretive trajectory** approaches the chapter in continuity with the broader Paul-within-Judaism reading. **Pamela Eisenbaum**'s *Paul Was Not a Christian*, **Matthew Thiessen**'s *A Jewish Paul*, and **Paula Fredriksen**'s *Paul: The Pagan's Apostle* take Paul's preaching at Lystra as an instance of the Jewish-eschatological gathering of the Gentiles: the Gentiles are not being converted to Judaism and not being made into a "third race," but are being summoned, as Gentiles, to turn from idols to the one God of Israel, in fulfillment of Jewish-prophetic expectation (Isa 2:2–4; Mic 4:1–5; Zech 8:20–23). The Lystra speech's distinctive omission of any reference to circumcision or Torah-observance is on this reading not Pauline supersessionism but Pauline strategic clarity about what the Gentile turn requires.

**E. P. Sanders**, **Krister Stendahl**, and **N. T. Wright** all develop the broader implication: Paul's mission practice — preaching first to Jews and God-fearers in synagogues, then to broader Gentile audiences with adapted kerygma — operates on a coherent theological framework in which Israel's God claims the nations through Israel's Messiah without dissolving Israel into the church.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: How does the gospel relate to non-Jewish religion and culture?** The Lystra scene poses the question with maximum sharpness. The crowd's response is not malicious — they are honoring what they take to be a divine epiphany — but it is fundamentally misdirected. Paul and Barnabas's response (tearing garments, refusing the sacrifices, redirecting toward the living God) establishes a clear pattern, but the question of how to evaluate the underlying religious instinct remains contested. Three positions have shaped the tradition.

The **Calvinist-Reformed position** (developed in Calvin's *Institutes* I.3–5, in **Cornelius Van Til**'s presuppositional apologetics, and in **G. C. Berkouwer**'s *General Revelation*) reads the chapter as evidence that pagan religion is fundamentally idolatrous: the religious instinct is real and universal, but it is corrupted, suppressed, and misdirected, such that all pagan religion stands under judgment as "vain things" (v. 15). Natural revelation testifies sufficiently to leave humanity without excuse but does not save. The **Catholic-Thomist position** (developed in Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* I-II q.94, in **Jacques Maritain**'s *Approaches to God*, and in Vatican II's *Nostra Aetate*) reads the chapter more sympathetically: pagan religion contains real fragments of truth that the gospel fulfills, and the apostolic preaching does not destroy but completes. Verse 17 ("he did not leave himself without witness") is read as a positive-natural-theology text. The **post-liberal / cultural-fulfillment position** (developed in **Lesslie Newbigin**'s *The Open Secret*, **Andrew Walls**'s *The Missionary Movement in Christian History*, **Lamin Sanneh**'s *Translating the Message*) reads the chapter as evidence that the gospel must always come to a people in their own cultural-religious idiom (note Luke's careful narration of the Lycaonian-language acclamation), confronting and converting that idiom from within rather than from outside.

Steelmanned: the Calvinist reading takes seriously Paul's sharp "vain things" language and the apostles' physical refusal of the sacrifices. The Catholic reading takes seriously v. 17's affirmation that God has been bearing witness through creation. The post-liberal reading takes seriously the Lukan attention to local language and cultural particularity. **Where this leaves us**: Acts 14 holds the affirmation of a universal divine witness in creation (v. 17) together with the rejection of the religious systems that misread it (v. 15). Any theology of religions that loses either pole is reading against the chapter.

**Tension 2: What is the relationship between mission, suffering, and church order?** The chapter narrates a sequence — preach, suffer, plant, structure — that has been read in incompatible ways. Three positions.

The **martyrological-charismatic position** (Tertullian's *Apology* 50, Irenaeus's *Against Heresies* 4.33.9, the Pentecostal tradition, and contemporary persecuted-church theology) reads the chapter as evidence that authentic mission produces suffering, that suffering legitimates apostolic witness, and that church structure (v. 23's appointing of elders) emerges from Spirit-given charismatic gift rather than from prior institutional authority. The **institutional-apostolic position** (developed in patristic episcopal theology, in Catholic canon law, and in Reformed presbyterian polity) reads the chapter as evidence that apostolic mission necessarily produces ordered communities under appointed leaders, with the laying-on-of-hands and prayer constituting a sacramental act of authorization. The **process-pastoral position** (developed in **Roland Allen**'s *Missionary Methods*, **Lesslie Newbigin**'s *The Household of God*, and contemporary missional ecclesiology) reads the chapter as evidence of a developmental sequence: mission produces community, community matures into structured leadership, leadership stabilizes in elder-form — all as natural pneumatic-organic process rather than externally-imposed institutional template.

Steelmanned: the martyrological-charismatic reading takes seriously the Pauline pattern of suffering-apostle (v. 22 — "through many tribulations"). The institutional-apostolic reading takes seriously the formal sacramental act of v. 23 (the laying-on-of-hands, prayer, and fasting). The process-pastoral reading takes seriously the developmental arc of the chapter — preaching to suffering to consolidating to structuring. **Where this leaves us**: the chapter narrates a sequence in which charismatic mission, suffering witness, and ordered community are inseparable rather than alternative ecclesiologies. *Unresolved question*: When v. 23 says the apostles "appointed elders for them in every church," does the Lukan absence of any specification of process leave the question of ordination genuinely open across traditions, or does the mere fact of apostolic agency require some form of authorized succession to be theologically coherent?

## The Thread

Acts 14 reaches across the canon in several directions. The **Lystra speech** of vv. 15–17 anticipates and is paralleled by **Paul's Areopagus speech** at **Acts 17:22–31** — the same theology of creation-as-witness developed for a more sophisticated audience — and is closely related to **Romans 1:18–25** (idolatry as suppression of creation's testimony) and **1 Thessalonians 1:9–10** ("you turned to God from idols"). The **stoning at Lystra** corresponds to **2 Corinthians 11:25** ("once I was stoned"), one of the few external corroborations of Paul's sufferings catalog. The **appointing of elders** of v. 23 grounds the Pauline-pastoral ecclesiology of **1 Timothy 3:1–13**, **Titus 1:5–9** ("appoint elders in every town as I directed you"), and **1 Peter 5:1–4**. The **"door of faith opened to the Gentiles"** of v. 27 anticipates the **Council debate of Acts 15** — having affirmed the Gentile mission, the church must now decide on what terms Gentiles enter — and is the immediate prelude to the chapter that will become the structural and theological climax of Acts. And the **suffering-as-kingdom-entry** theme of v. 22 echoes **Luke 9:23–27** (taking up the cross), **Romans 8:17** (suffering with Christ), and **2 Timothy 3:12** ("all who desire to live a godly life in Christ Jesus will be persecuted").
