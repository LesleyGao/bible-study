# Genesis 21 — The Laughter, the Expulsion, and the Well

---

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political and geographic situation.** The chapter moves Abraham's household into the Negev, specifically to Beersheba, the southernmost well-watered threshold between settled agricultural land and the nomadic wilderness stretching toward Sinai. In the Middle Bronze Age (c. 2000–1700 BC), this was genuine frontier territory — beyond the reach of Egyptian administration, populated by semi-nomadic herders whose wealth was measured in small cattle (sheep, goats) and, critically, in access to wells. The text's "land of the Philistines" (v. 32, 34) is a famous anachronism: the Philistines (Peleset of the Sea Peoples) do not arrive in Canaan until c. 1175 BC, after the Bronze Age collapse. This signals a later editor (likely the J/JE strand in its final form, or a Deuteronomistic updating) translating an archaic memory of "people on the coastal plain" into vocabulary their audience would recognize. Abimelech ("my father is king") and Phicol are likely dynastic or functional titles rather than personal names — the same Abimelech or his successor appears with Isaac in Genesis 26.

**Material culture.** Several details ring archaeologically true. *Circumcision on the eighth day* (v. 4) — Egyptian evidence (the Ankh-Mahor tomb at Saqqara, c. 2300 BC) shows circumcision was practiced in the region, but the specific eighth-day infant ritual is distinctively Abrahamic. *Weaning feast* (v. 8) — infants in the ancient Near East typically nursed until 2–3 years old (corroborated by 2 Maccabees 7:27 and Mesopotamian adoption contracts). The feast marks the first moment a child was likely to survive infancy; in a world of perhaps 30% infant mortality, it was a legitimate celebration. *Skin of water* (v. 14) — a goatskin water bag, standard nomadic kit, holds perhaps 3 gallons, enough for two people for a day or two in desert conditions. *Bow hunting* (v. 20) — Ishmael becomes a *roveh qashat*, a steppe-dweller's occupation, matching what we know of Bronze Age Shasu and proto-Arab peoples of the Paran wilderness. *Tamarisk tree* (v. 33, Hebrew *eshel* אֵשֶׁל) — a desert tree with deep taproot, casting wide shade; planting one is a claim to settled presence, a living boundary marker.

**The well dispute and covenant form.** Verses 22–34 preserve an archaic well-treaty that bears striking resemblance to Bronze Age parity treaties (e.g., the Mari texts). The exchange of animals, the oath formula, and the place-name etiology follow a recognizable pattern. Nuzi tablets (15th century but reflecting older customs) show that wells were transferable property requiring formal witness; Abraham's seven ewe lambs (*sheva kvasot*) function as legal witnesses — hence the name *Be'er-sheva* punning on both "well of seven" and "well of the oath" (*sheva*/*shava*).

**Key Hebrew terms:**

- **יִצְחָק (*Yitzchak*)** — "he laughs." The name has now been spoken four times in the Abraham cycle (17:17 Abraham laughs; 18:12 Sarah laughs in disbelief; 21:6 Sarah laughs in joy; 21:9 Ishmael *metsacheq* — "laughing / Isaac-ing"). The name is a pun-machine. English cannot convey that verse 9's "laughing" uses the same verbal root as Isaac's name — Ishmael is literally "Isaac-ing," playing the role of the heir.
- **מְצַחֵק (*metsacheq*)** — the piel participle of *tsachaq*, v. 9. This is the chapter's interpretive crux. It can mean innocent play, mocking, sexual impropriety (Gen 26:8, Isaac "*metsacheq*" with Rebekah), or idolatrous revelry (Exod 32:6). The ESV's "laughing" is minimalist; LXX adds "with her son Isaac" (παίζοντα μετὰ Ισαακ), which Paul follows in Galatians 4:29 as "persecuted."
- **אָמָה (*amah*)** — "slave woman" (v. 10, 12, 13). A different term than *shifchah* (used in Gen 16). *Amah* implies higher status — a concubine-slave whose children have potential inheritance rights. Sarah's demand overrides this customary right.
- **שָׁמַע (*shama*)** — "heard" (v. 17). God *hears* the boy. This is the etymology of Ishmael (*Yishma-el*, "God hears"). The narrative is activating his name theologically at the precise moment he seems abandoned.
- **אֵל עוֹלָם (*El Olam*)** — "the Everlasting God" (v. 33). A Canaanite divine epithet attested at Ugarit (*'il 'lm*). Abraham appropriates regional divine language for YHWH — evidence of the text's archaic stratum, where patriarchal religion engages local high-god concepts without the later anti-Canaanite polemic.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

**Genre.** This is patriarchal narrative with three distinct units welded together: (a) birth announcement and naming (vv. 1–7), often analyzed as a P-framed passage with older poetic material in Sarah's utterances; (b) the expulsion of Hagar and Ishmael (vv. 8–21), traditionally assigned to E, paralleling the J version in Genesis 16; (c) the Beersheba well-covenant etiology (vv. 22–34), likely J or a composite. The doublet of Hagar's expulsion is a classic Documentary Hypothesis case (Wellhausen, von Rad): Gen 16 has Hagar flee while pregnant, receive a promise, and return; Gen 21 has her expelled with a boy, receive a promise, and not return. Sarah's poetic couplet in vv. 6–7 reads as an embedded older hymn.

**Literary devices.**

- **The laughter motif as inclusio and pun-web.** The Abraham cycle's entire second half is stitched by *tsachaq*. Chapter 21 resolves the motif: Sarah's disbelieving laugh (18:12) becomes her triumphant laugh (21:6), and the son whose name embodies the joke is finally present. But v. 9's *metsacheq* twists the knife — Ishmael is doing what Isaac is named to do.
- **Parallelism between the two sons.** Robert Alter highlights the deliberate structural echo: both sons receive divine promises of nationhood (v. 12, v. 13, 18); both are associated with wells/water as life-source; both are almost-lost-then-saved (Ishmael here, Isaac in ch. 22). Chapters 21 and 22 form a diptych: Abraham sends away one son in ch. 21 and nearly sacrifices the other in ch. 22. Both sons are rescued at the last moment by the *mal'akh* (angel) calling from heaven, with almost identical language ("fear not," "I will make… a great nation" / "I will multiply your offspring"). This is not coincidence; it is chiastic theology.
- **Eye-opening motif.** "God opened her eyes, and she saw a well" (v. 19). The well was already there. The miracle is perceptual, not hydrological. Compare Elisha's servant (2 Kgs 6:17), the disciples at Emmaus (Luke 24:31). This is a recurrent Hebrew narrative pattern: revelation as unveiling what is present.
- **Numerical wordplay.** *Sheva* (seven) / *shava* (to swear) pun drives the etiology. Seven ewes, seven-fold oath, Beersheba. Ancient Semitic oaths often invoked the number seven (Herodotus 3.8 on Arab blood-oaths involving seven stones).

**Location in the book's argument.** Genesis 12–25 is the Abraham cycle. Chapter 17 instituted circumcision and renamed Sarai. Chapter 18 promised Isaac. Chapters 19–20 detoured through Sodom and Gerar. Chapter 21 delivers the long-delayed promise and clears the narrative space — by removing Ishmael — for chapter 22's terrible test, where Isaac becomes the *only* son ("your son, your only son, whom you love"). Ch. 21 also closes the Abimelech subplot opened in ch. 20.

**Textual variants.** The LXX of v. 9 reads "playing with Isaac her son" (παίζοντα μετὰ Ισαακ τοῦ υἱοῦ αὐτῆς), adding information the MT lacks. The Samaritan Pentateuch agrees with the LXX here. This addition drives much later interpretation. The MT's bare *metsacheq* is deliberately ambiguous.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Canonical arc.** This chapter sits at the hinge of the promise. God has promised land, seed, and blessing to the nations (Gen 12:1–3). Chapter 21 is the partial fulfillment of the seed promise — and raises the question of *which* seed. The answer here ("through Isaac shall your offspring be named," v. 12) becomes the narrowing principle of election that threads through Genesis (Jacob over Esau, Joseph over his brothers), through the monarchy (David over his brothers), and into the New Testament's claims about Jesus. Hagar and Ishmael's story, meanwhile, inaugurates a counter-theme: God's care for those outside the elect line. Ishmael is *not* cursed. He is blessed, promised nationhood, and accompanied ("God was with the boy," v. 20).

### Jewish trajectory

**Second Temple.** *Jubilees* 17 softens the expulsion, presenting Sarah as righteously protecting Isaac from genuinely wicked behavior by Ishmael. Josephus (*Antiquities* 1.12.3) similarly defends Sarah. Philo (*On the Cherubim*) allegorizes: Hagar is preliminary education (*enkyklios paideia*), Sarah is wisdom (*sophia*) — the soul must leave Hagar behind for true understanding. Notably, Paul will invert Philo's valences in Galatians 4.

**Rabbinic.** *Bereshit Rabbah* 53–54 wrestles with *metsacheq*. Rabbi Akiva sees innocent play. Rabbi Ishmael (ironically) reads it as idolatry (citing Exod 32:6). Rabbi Eliezer reads it as sexual immorality. Rabbi Yose reads it as attempted murder — Ishmael shooting arrows at Isaac pretending to play, grounded in v. 20's bow reference. These are not whimsical; each rabbi is justifying Sarah's harsh demand against the plain reading that Ishmael was merely playing. The Talmud (*Sotah* 6b) explores the legal status of Hagar's expulsion.

**Medieval.** Rashi follows the darkest rabbinic readings — Ishmael was idolatrous, sexually immoral, and murderous. Ibn Ezra, more sober, reads *metsacheq* as Ishmael mocking the weaning feast, perhaps claiming firstborn rights. Ramban (Nachmanides) highlights God's tender concern for Hagar and reads the whole episode as Sarah's prophetic insight superseding Abraham's paternal tenderness. Maimonides (*Guide* 3.29) treats Ishmael as ancestor of the Arabs with genuine divine relationship — written in an Islamic milieu where such claims mattered politically.

**Modern.** Nehama Leibowitz reads Sarah's harshness as a moral problem the text refuses to tidy. Avivah Zornberg (*The Beginning of Desire*) is brilliant here: Sarah's triumphant laughter in v. 6 is *ambivalent* — "everyone will laugh *at* me" is as legitimate a translation as "laugh *with* me." Sarah remains haunted by the absurdity of her own body. Jon Levenson (*The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*) reads 21 and 22 as a unit: both sons are given up, both restored; the "beloved son" motif is doubled. Levenson and others also note the way the narrator parallels Hagar's wilderness scene with the patriarchal narratives — she is the first person in Scripture to name God (Gen 16:13, *El Roi*), the first to run from servitude, and a woman driven out twice, an Exodus figure in miniature, except that here she is cast out *by* the covenant family rather than rescued from outsiders.

### Christian trajectory

**Patristic.** Paul's allegory in Galatians 4:21–31 is the seed: Hagar = Sinai covenant = earthly Jerusalem = slavery; Sarah = promise = heavenly Jerusalem = freedom. "Cast out the slave woman and her son" becomes a Christian charter-text. This is an extraordinary reversal — Paul, an ethnic Jew, identifies ethnic Israel with Hagar-Ishmael and Gentile believers with Sarah-Isaac. Origen (*Homilies on Genesis* 7) extends the allegory: Hagar is the letter, Sarah is the spirit. Augustine (*City of God* 15.2) makes Hagar/Sarah = earthly/heavenly cities. Chrysostom more pastorally emphasizes Abraham's grief (v. 11) as evidence of his fatherly love, countering any triumphalism.

**Medieval.** Aquinas (*ST* I-II.102) treats circumcision on the eighth day (v. 4) as type of baptism. The *Glossa Ordinaria* systematizes the Pauline allegory. Luther's *Lectures on Genesis* is remarkably extended here, reading Sarah's demand as harsh but Spirit-led, and Hagar's desert experience as a paradigm of *Anfechtung* (faith-trial). Calvin (*Commentary on Genesis*) is notably restrained: he refuses to demonize Hagar, emphasizes God's compassion, and warns against using the allegory to despise Jews.

**Critical.** Wellhausen saw the doublet with ch. 16 as a textbook source-critical case. Von Rad (*Genesis*) read the E version (ch. 21) as more theologically refined — the promise to Ishmael is more developed, God's care more explicit. Gunkel analyzed the etiological layers (why Ishmaelites live in Paran; why Beersheba is named thus).

**Contemporary.** Brueggemann (*Genesis*, Interpretation) names the scandal: the chosen line is established by casting out a woman and child. N.T. Wright, reading Galatians 4 in *Paul and the Faithfulness of God*, insists that Paul's allegory does not write Hagar and Ishmael out of God's care — Ishmael's blessing (vv. 13, 20) makes any simple "elect vs. damned" reading impossible — and that the larger Abrahamic story is precisely the family through whom God means to bless *all* families, the outsiders included. Goldingay notes that the chapter refuses to let us pick sides cleanly. Moberly recovers Hagar as the matriarch of the outcast whom God nonetheless meets and hears in the wilderness.

### Where Jewish and Christian readings converge and diverge

**Converge:** Both traditions recognize Sarah's words as divinely ratified ("whatever Sarah says to you, do," v. 12); both affirm that Ishmael receives real blessing; both are troubled by the ethics and produce elaborate justifications.

**Diverge fundamentally:** Jewish tradition reads Isaac as the elect *ancestor* — the line continues biologically to the Jewish people, and Ishmael's descendants (in later midrash and in Islamic tradition) are the Arabs. The covenant is a *family* covenant. Christian tradition, via Paul, reads Isaac as a *type* — pointing past biological descent to spiritual descent through faith. The "offspring" promised through Isaac is ultimately Christ (Gal 3:16), and the true children of Abraham are believers of any ethnicity. This is the deepest divergence in all of Christian-Jewish reading: the Pauline de-ethnicization of the promise, which Jewish readers (from Trypho to Levenson) have seen as a hostile takeover of their ancestor. After *Nostra Aetate* and post-Holocaust rereading (Krister Stendahl, Pamela Eisenbaum, Mark Nanos), some Christian scholars now read Galatians 4 not as supersession but as Paul arguing against forcing *Gentiles* into Torah-observance, with the allegory being occasional rather than ontological. This remains contested.

**Islamic trajectory** (briefly, because Ishmael matters here). The Qur'an (Surah 2:127, 14:37) places Abraham and Ishmael at Mecca, building the Ka'bah. Hagar's desperate running between Safa and Marwa becomes the ritual of *sa'i* during Hajj. The well God opens (v. 19) is identified with Zamzam. In Islamic reading, Ishmael, not Isaac, is the son nearly sacrificed in chapter 22. Reading Genesis 21 with awareness that three faiths trace themselves to this wilderness scene — and claim different sons as the bearer of promise — is unavoidable after the 20th century.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author's vision here is *the particularity and the pain of election*. God keeps promises (v. 1: "The LORD visited Sarah as he had said") — but the keeping is costly, narrow, and creates casualties. The narrator refuses to let either truth eclipse the other: Isaac is *the* promise, *and* God hears Ishmael's cry. The text holds tensions rather than resolving them.

### Tension 1: Election and the rejected

**Catholic.** The CCC (§59–64, §705–706) reads Abrahamic election as preparatory and expansive — Israel is chosen *for* the nations, fulfilled in Christ, extended in the Church. Ishmael is not abandoned; he prefigures the gathering of peoples. De Lubac and Balthasar emphasize election as mission, not privilege.

**Reformed (Calvin, later Barth).** Calvin reads Isaac/Ishmael as evidence of unconditional election — God's sovereign freedom chooses one and not the other, and the creature has no claim. Barth radicalizes: in Christ, the rejected one is taken up — Jesus is *both* Isaac (the beloved son) *and* Ishmael (the one cast out), so rejection is contained within election. This is stunning theology but controversial.

**Lutheran.** Luther emphasizes the *law/gospel* reading via Galatians 4: Hagar = the covenant that cannot save, Sarah = the gospel. But Luther also insists on Abraham's paternal grief as real, refusing to make the text coldly doctrinal.

**Wesleyan/Arminian.** Election is corporate and purposive, not individually deterministic. Ishmael's blessing (v. 13, 20) proves God does not damn the non-elect.

**Jewish (Levenson, Sarna).** Election is not about personal salvation but about vocation — a family chosen to carry a promise, with real obligations and real costs. Ishmael is not damned; he is simply not the covenant-bearer. Levenson warns Christian readers against the move that turns Jewish particularity into a problem to be solved.

**Critical.** The text is an etiology of Israelite-Ishmaelite (later Arab) relations — explaining why these peoples are kin but distinct, why Israel inhabits Canaan while Ishmaelites inhabit the steppe. Theology rationalizes geography.

**Steelman the Barthian view** (likely least intuitive). Barth argues that election in isolation from Christ is incoherent and cruel — but in Christ, the rejected Ishmael is not left in the wilderness; he is taken into the Isaac who is himself rejected, cast out of Jerusalem, abandoned to death. The chapter's scandal becomes the template for the gospel's deeper scandal: God identifies with the cast-out. You may reject this as christological overreach on a text that knows nothing of Jesus. But it takes seriously what the text itself insists: God was *with* Ishmael (v. 20). Election does not determine divine presence. That is genuinely radical and resists any supersessionist triumphalism.

### Tension 2: The ethics of Sarah's demand and God's endorsement

The hardest verse is 12: God tells Abraham to obey Sarah's harsh demand. A mother and child are sent into a wilderness where the water runs out and the child is left to die under a bush. Whatever the rabbinic justifications (Ishmael was violent, idolatrous, etc.), the plain sense is disturbing.

**Classical Jewish.** Sarah possessed prophetic insight superior to Abraham's (*Bereshit Rabbah* 47:1; Rashi on 21:12: "we learn that Abraham was secondary to Sarah in prophecy"). The endorsement is of her spiritual discernment, not of cruelty.

**Augustinian/Reformed.** God's will sometimes proceeds through morally troubling human actions; providence uses the crooked to make straight. This preserves divine sovereignty but is pastorally cold.

**Contemporary critical (Brueggemann, Moberly).** The moral difficulty is real and should be named as such. Hagar is a woman used and discarded — first by Sarah (ch. 16), then by both Sarah and Abraham (ch. 21). The text's endorsement of Sarah's *demand* (v. 12) does not require the reader to endorse the cruelty of its execution. But — and this is the chapter's countermove — God meets Hagar in the wilderness, hears the boy, and opens her eyes to water already there. The text does not abandon her even as the covenant family does; her story is one of survival and continued blessing rather than rescue-into-the-covenant.

**Contemporary Catholic (Levering, Gregory).** Moral difficulty is real; the text displays, does not resolve, the costs of covenant history. God's accommodating use of flawed human action is pedagogical, not exemplary.

**Where this leaves us.** The chapter will not let us be comfortable. God keeps his word — and a woman and child are nearly killed in the keeping of it. God hears them, saves them, blesses them — and still, they go into the wilderness and do not come back. Election generates outsiders; the text does not hide this, but it also insists the outsiders are heard. Any reading that resolves the tension cleanly (either "Sarah was right and Ishmael deserved it" or "the text is just patriarchal propaganda") is flinching from what is actually on the page.

---

**The unresolved question:**

If God hears the cry of the one cast out by the very family through whom he is working — if Ishmael under the bush is not abandoned even when he has been abandoned — then what do you do with the people your own "promises," "callings," and "certainties" have cost? Do you know their names, or only your own Isaac?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 16** — Hagar's first flight. Compare carefully: in 16 she runs pregnant and returns; in 21 she is expelled with a child and does not. Between them, she becomes the only character in the Torah to name God (*El Roi*, "God who sees me," 16:13) — and in 21:19 God *opens her eyes* to see. The seeing is mutual and chiastic.

- **Genesis 22** — Read 21 and 22 as a diptych. Both sons nearly die; both are saved by an *angel calling from heaven* with almost identical phrasing; both receive the promise of multiplication. Abraham loses both sons in two consecutive chapters — one by expulsion, one by knife — and gets both back. The Akedah's unbearable weight is set up by chapter 21's expulsion: when God says "your son, your only son" in 22:2, we have just watched the other son leave.

- **Galatians 4:21–31** — Paul's allegorical reading that has shaped (and distorted) Christian interpretation for two millennia. When you get there, you'll need to decide: is Paul offering an ontological claim about Judaism, or a pastoral argument to Gentile Galatians against forced Torah-observance? The answer reshapes Christian-Jewish relations.

- **Exodus 2:23–25** — "God heard their groaning… God saw… God knew." The verbs used of Hagar and Ishmael in 21:17 (*God heard*) are the same verbs that launch the Exodus. The God who hears the slave woman in Genesis 21 is the God who hears the slave nation in Exodus 2. When you get to Exodus, remember that the hearing-God was first revealed not in Moses's burning bush but in Hagar's dry bush.
