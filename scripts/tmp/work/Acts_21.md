# Acts 21: Agabus, the Temple Riot, and the Antonia Steps

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Acts 21 traces a tightening geographical noose. The "we" narrator carries Paul along the eastern Mediterranean coast — Cos, Rhodes, Patara, then a Phoenician cargo ship to Tyre, then Ptolemais (modern Akko/Acre), Caesarea Maritima, and finally up the Roman road to Jerusalem. Each stop has been heavily excavated. **Caesarea Maritima** — Herod's grand harbor city, dedicated 10 BCE — stood as the seat of Roman provincial administration for Judaea. The harbor (*Sebastos*), the largest artificial harbor in the ancient Mediterranean, used hydraulic concrete (*pulvis puteolanus*) whose composition the Israeli underwater excavations under Avner Raban and Robert Hohlfelder (the Combined Caesarea Expeditions, 1980s–2000s) have documented in detail. The **Pilate Inscription**, discovered in 1961 by Antonio Frova in the secondary use of a step in Caesarea's theater, is the single epigraphic confirmation of Pontius Pilate as *praefectus Iudaeae* — the very office Felix and Festus will hold by Acts 23–24. The praetorium where Paul will be detained (Acts 23:35) has been tentatively identified with Herod's promontory palace, excavated by Ehud Netzer; the theater, hippodrome, and Crusader-era reuse of the Roman city are now the Caesarea National Park.

In Jerusalem, two structures dominate Acts 21. The **Temple Mount** had been rebuilt by Herod from c. 20 BCE; the perimeter retaining walls (Western Wall, the Robinson's Arch monumental staircase, the southern Hulda Gate stairs excavated by Benjamin Mazar in the 1968–78 Temple Mount excavations) attest its imperial scale. Inside the outer Court of the Gentiles stood the ***soreg***, a low balustrade, and at intervals along it Greek warning inscriptions in capital letters threatening death to any non-Jew who entered the inner courts. Two such inscriptions have been recovered: the first by **Charles Clermont-Ganneau** in 1871, now in Istanbul Archaeological Museum (CIIP I.2 no. 2); a second fragment in 1936, now in the Israel Museum. The text reads: ΜΗΘΕΝΑ ΑΛΛΟΓΕΝΗ ΕΙΣΠΟΡΕΥΕΣΘΑΙ ΕΝΤΟΣ ΤΟΥ ΠΕΡΙ ΤΟ ΙΕΡΟΝ ΤΡΥΦΑΚΤΟΥ ΚΑΙ ΠΕΡΙΒΟΛΟΥ ΟΣ Δ' ΑΝ ΛΗΦΘΗΙ ΕΑΥΤΩΙ ΑΙΤΙΟΣ ΕΣΤΑΙ ΔΙΑ ΤΟ ΕΞΑΚΟΛΟΥΘΕΙΝ ΘΑΝΑΤΟΝ ("Let no foreigner enter within the screen and enclosure surrounding the sanctuary; whoever is caught will be himself responsible for the death that follows"). Josephus describes the same inscription twice (*War* 5.5.2 §193–194; *Ant.* 15.11.5 §417). The accusation in Acts 21:28 — that Paul brought Trophimus the Ephesian past the soreg — was thus a capital charge under Jewish law, with Roman provincial authorization to enforce. The **Antonia Fortress**, at the northwestern corner of the Temple Mount, was Herod's reworking of an earlier Hasmonean fortress and served as the cohort's barracks. Josephus (*War* 5.5.8 §238–245) describes its four corner towers and its staircase descending into the Temple precinct — the very steps from which Paul addresses the crowd at the chapter's close (21:40). The exact location is disputed (the traditional Sisters of Zion convent identification has been challenged by Pierre Benoit and others arguing for a more eastward location), but the steps-into-the-precinct topography is secure.

The chapter sits inside the **second "we" passage** (20:5–21:18), which here ends as the narrator falls silent in Jerusalem and resumes only at 27:1. Greek terms anchor the chapter. ***prophēteuō*** (προφητεύω, 21:9) describes Philip's daughters' activity. ***zēlōtai tou nomou*** (ζηλωταὶ τοῦ νόμου, 21:20) — "zealous for the law" — describes the myriads of Jewish believers in Jerusalem and is a self-designation, not a political-Zealot affiliation. ***hagnisthēti*** (ἁγνίσθητι, 21:24) — "purify yourself" — is the Nazirite-vow ritual language of Numbers 6 LXX. ***bebēlōken*** (βεβήλωκεν, 21:28) — "has profaned" — is the technical Septuagintal term for Temple-defilement. ***Hebraïdi dialektō*** (Ἑβραΐδι διαλέκτῳ, 21:40) — the language Paul uses on the Antonia steps — is the textual seam between chapters 21 and 22.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Luke constructs Acts 21 as a deliberately slow tightening of the trap. The chapter unfolds in three movements: (1) journey to Jerusalem with prophetic warnings (21:1–16); (2) meeting with James and the Nazirite-vow strategy (21:17–26); (3) the Temple riot and Roman intervention (21:27–40). The pacing is deliberately unhurried until verse 27, then catastrophic.

**Tannehill** (*Narrative Unity*) reads the chapter as the climactic instance of the **Pauline-Jesus parallelism**. Paul's resolved face toward Jerusalem (21:13: "I am ready not only to be imprisoned but even to die in Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus") echoes Luke 9:51 ("he set his face to go to Jerusalem"); the disciples' "let the will of the Lord be done" (21:14) echoes Gethsemane (Luke 22:42). The pattern is structural: as Jesus's passion is foretold by Spirit-prompted prophecy, so is Paul's; as Jesus is delivered by Jews into Gentile hands (Luke 18:32), so will Paul be (Agabus's prediction at 21:11). **Marguerat** sharpens this: Luke is constructing not biographical history alone but a *paradigm of the Spirit-led witness who suffers* — a paradigm reusable for the post-apostolic church.

The chapter is also Luke's most prominent use of **prophetic sign-acts**, a Hebrew-Bible genre. Agabus binding his own feet and hands with Paul's belt (21:11) belongs to the genre of Ahijah tearing his cloak (1 Kgs 11:29–31), Isaiah walking naked and barefoot (Isa 20:1–6), Jeremiah's yoke and clay jar (Jer 19, 27), and Ezekiel's brick-and-iron-pan miniature siege (Ezek 4–5). **Christopher Hays** has shown the genre dependence is explicit: Agabus's "Thus says the Holy Spirit" (21:11) substitutes for the prophetic "Thus says the Lord" of the Hebrew Bible — Luke is making a programmatic claim about the Spirit as the post-resurrection prophetic voice. **Pervo** (Hermeneia) reads this as Lukan composition; **Bruce** (NICNT) and **Schnabel** treat it as a remembered tradition Luke shapes.

**Philip the evangelist's four unmarried prophesying daughters** (21:8–9) is one of the most striking notices in Acts. It is uniquely Lukan, embedded almost as throwaway, and commentators (Barrett, Gaventa, Keener) regularly read it alongside Anna in Luke 2, the women at the empty tomb (Luke 24), and 1 Cor 11:5 as a plain indication that women exercising the gift of prophecy was an unremarkable feature of the early communities, not an exception requiring comment. **Eusebius** (*HE* 3.31.3, 3.39.9) treats Philip's daughters as a major early-church tradition, citing Polycrates of Ephesus claiming two of them were buried at Hierapolis and one at Ephesus, and Papias claiming the daughters were a source for early-church traditions about resurrection. The notice is therefore historically significant precisely in its quietness.

The **Western Text** (Codex Bezae) lengthens 21:16 with a detail about the journey from Caesarea to Jerusalem and adds a vivid expansion at 21:25; Bezae's tendency to expand "we" passages is one of the textual signals that the "we" tradition was understood early as eyewitness testimony. **Marguerat**'s textual work suggests Bezae preserves a second-century interpretive amplification rather than original readings.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The chapter's most theologically charged moment is the encounter with **James and the Jerusalem elders** (21:17–26). James reports "**how many thousands there are among the Jews of those who have believed, and they are all zealous for the law**" (21:20). This single verse is foundational evidence for the **Jewish-Christian community in Jerusalem** as a distinct, law-observant body alongside the Pauline-Gentile mission. **Richard Bauckham** (*The Restoration of Israel*) reads the verse as proof that the Jerusalem church understood itself as the eschatological-Israelite remnant, fulfilling rather than abrogating Torah. **A.J.M. Wedderburn** (*A History of the First Christians*) reads it as evidence of a binary church structure that the Pauline mission and the Jerusalem church each recognized as legitimate. **Robert Brawley** has shown how Luke deliberately constructs Paul as a faithful Torah-observant Jew (Nazirite vow, 21:23–26; Pharisee self-identification, 23:6; circumcision of Timothy, 16:3) precisely to undercut the accusation that Paul taught Jews to abandon Moses (21:21).

The **Nazirite vow** is the structural key. Numbers 6 prescribes the vow's terms: hair-growth, abstention from grape products, avoidance of corpse-defilement, terminated by a shaving and offering at the Temple. Paying the expenses of others' vow-completion was an ancient Jewish form of *tzedakah* (Josephus *Ant.* 19.6.1 §294 records Agrippa I doing the same, m. *Naz.* 5:6). Paul's participation (21:23–26) is therefore not a strategic dissimulation but a public Jewish-piety act. **Pamela Eisenbaum** and **Matthew Thiessen** (*A Jewish Paul*) read this as confirming that Paul never ceased Jewish observance for himself, even while teaching Gentiles that they need not adopt it. **Paula Fredriksen** (*Paul: The Pagan's Apostle*) sharpens: the Pauline mission is fundamentally about *Gentile* eschatological inclusion as Gentiles, not about the displacement of Israel's Torah. Acts 21 is the Lukan corroboration that this is also how the Jerusalem church saw it.

**Jewish interpretive trajectories** here are unusually generative. **Adele Reinhartz** has cautioned against Christian readings of 21:27–36 that flatten "the Jews from Asia" into a generic anti-Jewish villain; the Asian-Jewish accusers are a specific subgroup with a specific (and erroneous) charge. **Daniel Boyarin** (*Border Lines*) reads Paul's Nazirite vow as evidence that the parting-of-the-ways was not yet underway in the 50s CE — Paul still operated entirely within Second Temple Jewish ritual repertoire. **Amy-Jill Levine** has argued that Christian preachers of Acts 21 must explicitly note that Paul did not, in fact, teach Jews to abandon Torah, and that the accusation in 21:21 is Lukan-acknowledged false witness. **Lawrence Schiffman** has noted that the Asian-Jewish charge — that Paul brought a Gentile past the soreg — was, if true, a capital matter under Second Temple halakhah, with Roman authorization to enforce; the seriousness of the riot is not religious overreaction but legal alarm.

**Christian interpretive trajectories**: **Chrysostom**'s *Homilies on Acts* (homily 45) reads James's strategy with Paul as wisdom in pastoral accommodation. **Calvin** uses 21:23–26 to argue that Christian liberty includes the freedom to undertake voluntary observances for the sake of weaker brothers (1 Cor 9:20). **Bede** treats the Agabus prediction as a paradigm of how Spirit-given foreknowledge does not abolish obedience: Paul knows what awaits and goes anyway. **Karl Barth** (*CD* IV/3.2) reads 21:13–14 as the model of vocation under God's hand: human resistance to divine direction softens into "let the will of the Lord be done." **Richard Hays** has read the Nazirite-vow accommodation as a paradigm of Pauline missiological flexibility within fixed theological commitments. **N. T. Wright**, in *Acts for Everyone* and *Paul: A Biography*, sees the chapter as the deliberate convergence of his recurring themes: Paul walks toward Jerusalem with the same set face Luke gave Jesus (Luke 9:51), submits to the Nazirite vow not as cynical cover but because he genuinely remains a loyal son of Israel's covenant — his quarrel was only ever with the demand that *Gentiles* become Jews — and the temple riot is, in Wright's reading, the tragic collision between a renewed-Israel movement that believes the Messiah has come and a guardianship of the sanctuary that cannot hear it. For Wright the "thousands zealous for the law" (21:20) and the apostle to the Gentiles standing in the temple paying for vows are not an awkward inconsistency but the single shape of the one people of God Paul has been building all along.

The major contemporary commentary tradition — **Bruce** (NICNT), **Barrett** (ICC), **Fitzmyer** (AB), **Gaventa** (Abingdon), **Johnson** (Sacra Pagina), **Keener** (4 vols), **Witherington III**, **Schnabel** (ZECNT), **Conzelmann** (Hermeneia), **Haenchen**, **Pervo** (Hermeneia), **Peterson** (Pillar), **Marguerat** — divides on one major point: how to harmonize the Nazirite-vow accommodation with Paul's epistolary insistence (Galatians) that he never compromised the gospel for Jewish ritual demands. **Haenchen** read it as Lukan invention contradicting genuine Paul. **Bruce**, **Witherington**, and **Keener** read it as fully consistent with 1 Cor 9:20 ("to the Jews I became as a Jew"). **Marguerat** holds the middle: Luke is making a theological point about Pauline-Jewish continuity that may overstate the historical Pauline practice.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: How to receive Spirit-prompted prophecy that contradicts the Spirit's own constraint.** At Tyre the disciples "through the Spirit" tell Paul not to go on to Jerusalem (21:4); at Caesarea Agabus says "Thus says the Holy Spirit" that Paul will be bound (21:11); the disciples and the narrator beg Paul not to go (21:12). Yet Paul, "constrained by the Spirit" (20:22), goes anyway. Three readings of how to navigate this.

The first reading (older Pentecostal-charismatic, **Roger Stronstad**) treats the warnings as Spirit-given information about the cost but not as Spirit-given prohibition; Paul rightly distinguishes Spirit-revelation of facts from Spirit-revealed will. The second reading (**Bruce**, **Marshall**) treats the prophets as accurately reporting Spirit-given facts but the disciples as wrongly inferring a prohibition; Paul corrects the misinference. The third reading (**Pervo**, more skeptical) treats the contradiction as Lukan literary tension that accumulates dread without resolving the prophetic-discernment problem.

Steelmanning each: the first preserves the warnings' Spirit-origin without making them prescriptive; the second is the cleanest exegesis (Agabus does not say "do not go," only what will happen); the third is honest about the literary effect, which is precisely the Gethsemane-like atmosphere of impending unavoidable suffering.

**Where this leaves us:** Acts 21 teaches that prophetic gifting includes accurate revelation of cost, and that vocational discernment requires distinguishing "this will hurt" from "do not go." The Spirit's deepest will may be precisely that the witness go *into* the prophesied suffering. This has paradigmatic weight for any Christian reading of vocation under threat.

**Tension 2: Whether Paul's Nazirite accommodation is consistent with Pauline gospel theology.** James's strategy (21:23–26) is for Paul to publicly demonstrate he himself observes the law. Three positions.

The first (older Lutheran reading, **Bultmann** in pieces, **Haenchen**) treats it as Lukan whitewashing — a narrative softening of the actual Pauline rupture with Torah, contradicting the Galatian Paul who would not be circumcised for Titus's sake (Gal 2:3). The second (**Bruce**, **Marshall**, **Witherington**, **Keener**) treats the accommodation as fully Pauline — 1 Cor 9:20 ("to the Jews I became as a Jew") is the explicit Pauline charter for exactly such accommodation, and the issue at stake (no Gentile circumcision) is preserved (21:25). The third (**Eisenbaum**, **Thiessen**, **Fredriksen** — the "Paul-within-Judaism" school) treats it as no accommodation at all but as Paul's normal Jewish piety; the assumption that Paul *would* have abandoned Torah for himself is a Christian-supersessionist projection onto the text.

Steelmanning each: the first takes seriously Galatians' polemical edge but reads Paul too monolithically; the second preserves Pauline coherence by distinguishing what Paul required of Gentiles (no circumcision) from what he practiced as a Jew (continuing Torah observance); the third is exegetically the most parsimonious — it accounts for Acts 21, 16:3 (circumcising Timothy), and 23:6 (Pharisee self-identification) without strain.

**Where this leaves us:** the Nazirite accommodation is best read as Paul-within-Judaism, not Paul-as-formerly-Jewish. The gospel's universalism is about Gentile eschatological inclusion as Gentiles, not about Jewish abandonment of Torah. This reading reframes the chapter's ecclesial geometry: the Jerusalem church is not a more conservative wing of one Christian community, it is the Israelite-eschatological half of a single bipartite *ekklēsia* — which is essentially N. T. Wright's reading as well, with the added stress that the unity of that one people, Jew and Gentile around the Messiah, is the very thing Paul has spent his career fighting for.

**One unresolved question:** Was James's strategy a wise pastoral accommodation that nonetheless backfired catastrophically (the Asian Jews see Paul in the Temple and assume the worst), or was it a misreading of the situation that Paul should have refused? Luke does not editorialize. The narrative simply reports James's plan, Paul's compliance, and the ensuing riot.

## The Thread

Acts 21 ties forward to **Acts 22–28** as the long unfolding of Agabus's prophecy: Paul will indeed be bound and delivered to the Gentiles. The Asian-Jewish accusation about Trophimus (21:29) ties back to **Acts 19** (Trophimus's Ephesian origin) and forward to **Acts 24:5–6** (the formal accusation before Felix). The Antonia steps where Paul addresses the crowd in Hebrew (21:40) become the literary platform for the second of Paul's three Acts conversion narratives (chapter 22). And James's report of the Jewish-Christian "thousands zealous for the law" (21:20) is the most specific Lukan datum about the **Jerusalem church's ongoing Torah-observant identity**, a datum that will need to inform every subsequent reading of Pauline-Jewish relations across the New Testament canon.
