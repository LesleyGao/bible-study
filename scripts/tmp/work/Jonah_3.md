# Jonah 3: The City That Repented

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jonah 3 is one of the most theologically remarkable chapters in the Hebrew Bible. The chapter narrates the prophet's reluctant compliance with the LORD's renewed commission, his minimal sermon (a single Hebrew sentence: "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown!"), the city's surprisingly comprehensive repentance, and the LORD's relenting. The chapter argues that the LORD's mercy extends to the most theologically improbable recipient — the imperial capital of the empire that, in the historical-political imagination, embodied cruelty and aggression.

Verses 1-2 begin with "the word of the LORD came to Jonah the second time." The Hebrew formula *vayhi devar-YHWH... shenit* (וַיְהִי דְבַר־יְהוָה... שֵׁנִית) marks the renewed commission. The same imperative as 1:2 — *qum lekh el-Nineveh ha'ir hagdolah* (arise, go to Nineveh, that great city) — repeats with one alteration: instead of "call out against it" (1:2's *qera' aleyha*), the renewed commission says "call out... the message that I tell you" (3:2's *qera' eleyha et-haqri'ah asher anokhi dover eleykha*). Some commentators read the alteration as significant: the second commission is more open-ended, perhaps allowing for repentance as outcome. The verb *qera' aleyha* (against) and *qera' eleyha* (to / toward) differ by a single Hebrew letter.

Verse 3's description of Nineveh as "an exceedingly great city, three days' journey in breadth" has occasioned much discussion. The Hebrew *ir gedolah le-elohim* (עִיר גְּדוֹלָה לֵאלֹהִים) is sometimes rendered "exceedingly great city" (treating *le-elohim* as a Hebrew superlative idiom — "great even by God's standards") and sometimes "a city great to God" (with theological resonance). The "three days' journey" measurement has been variously interpreted: a literal three-day walk through the city's streets; the three-day diplomatic protocol for visiting a foreign capital (one day to enter, one day for audience, one day to depart); or hyperbolic-symbolic measurement. Archaeological excavation of Nineveh (Layard 1840s, subsequent expeditions) showed the city's actual size — the inner walls enclosed about 7.5 square kilometers, the outer walls perhaps three times that — substantial but not three days' walk in any literal sense. The book's description should be read as the post-exilic narrator's depiction of the legendary great city, not as careful historical-geographical measurement.

Jonah's sermon in v. 4 is one Hebrew sentence: *od arba'im yom veNineveh nehpaket* (עוֹד אַרְבָּעִים יוֹם וְנִינְוֵה נֶהְפָּכֶת) — "yet forty days and Nineveh overturned." The Hebrew *nehpaket* (overturned) is the same root as *mahpekhah* — the technical term for the Sodom-and-Gomorrah destruction (Genesis 19:25, 29; Deuteronomy 29:23; Isaiah 13:19). The vocabulary positions Nineveh's coming fate as Sodom-equivalent. Forty days is a stock biblical timeframe (Genesis 7:4, Exodus 24:18, Numbers 14:34, 1 Samuel 17:16, 1 Kings 19:8, Matthew 4:2, Acts 1:3) — the duration of preparation, testing, or transition. The sermon's brevity is significant: five Hebrew words. Jonah delivers the minimum possible message; whatever the prophet's continuing reluctance, the message itself functions powerfully.

Verses 5-9 describe the city's response. The structure is a fivefold escalation: the people believe God; they call for a fast; they put on sackcloth; the word reaches the king; the king joins the response and issues a comprehensive proclamation. The repentance moves from popular to royal — bottom-up rather than top-down. The king's actions are particularly striking: he rises from his throne, removes his royal robe, covers himself with sackcloth, sits in ashes. The Mesopotamian royal protocol made the king's body the focus of cosmic-cultic significance; the king's self-humiliation is the city's deepest sign of repentance.

Verse 7's "by the decree of the king and his nobles" (Hebrew *mita'am hammelekh ugdolaw*) uses the technical term *ta'am* (decree) — an Aramaic-influenced word also appearing in Daniel 3-6 and Ezra 4-7. The vocabulary is one of the linguistic features that suggests the book's post-exilic composition.

The proclamation's inclusion of animals is theologically remarkable. *"Let neither man nor beast, herd nor flock, taste anything. Let them not feed or drink water, but let man and beast be covered with sackcloth, and let them call out mightily to God"* (vv. 7-8). The animals fast; the animals wear sackcloth; the animals (presumably the human animals on their behalf) cry out. The image is comically excessive — and theologically pointed. The repentance extends beyond human consciousness into the animal-creation as participants in the city's penitential turn. Modern readers might find the image absurd; ancient readers might have found it theologically generous, extending the *teshuvah* category to all creation.

Verse 9's "Who knows? God may turn and relent and turn from his fierce anger, so that we may not perish" uses the *mi yodea* (who knows) formula characteristic of Joel 2:14's parallel passage. The king does not claim certainty that God will relent; he hopes. The theological humility is the chapter's pivot — the king acknowledges that *teshuvah* is not transactional but pleading.

Verse 10's *"God relented of the disaster that he had said he would do to them, and he did not do it"* uses the verb *vayinachem* (וַיִּנָּחֶם, "he relented / had compassion") — the same root as the divine relenting at Hosea 11:8 and the discussions of divine "repentance" throughout the prophetic tradition. The verse names the LORD's response without resolving the metaphysical questions about divine mutability.

Five Hebrew terms govern the chapter. *Qum lekh* (קוּם לֵךְ, vv. 2, 3), "arise, go" — the renewed commission's verbs. *Nehpeket* (נֶהְפָּכֶת, v. 4), "overturned" — the Sodom-vocabulary in Jonah's sermon. *Mi yodea* (מִי יוֹדֵעַ, v. 9), "who knows" — the king's humility. *Vayinachem* (וַיִּנָּחֶם, v. 10), "and he relented" — the divine response. *Sheniyt* (שֵׁנִית, v. 1), "second time" — naming the renewal of the prophet's commission after his flight.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter's literary structure is concentric. Verses 1-3a: the renewed commission and Jonah's compliance. Verses 3b-4: the city's size and Jonah's sermon. Verse 5: the popular response. Verses 6-9: the royal response. Verse 10: the divine response.

The chapter's most striking literary feature is the disproportion between the prophet's effort and the city's response. Jonah preaches one short Hebrew sentence; the entire city repents from the king's throne to the animals in the stables. The disproportion is the chapter's argument: the LORD's mercy is not contingent on the prophet's eloquence, the audience's prior preparation, or any of the standard variables. A reluctant five-word sermon produces comprehensive repentance.

The fivefold escalation of the response (people believe / fast / sackcloth / king's response / royal proclamation) compounds the chapter's portrait of comprehensive *teshuvah*. The verbs trace the spread: *vaya'aminu* (they believed) / *vayyiqra'u* (they called) / *vayyilbeshu* (they put on) / *vayyiga'* (it reached) / *vayyaqom* (he arose) / *vaya'avor* (he proclaimed). Each verb adds another dimension; the cumulative effect is overwhelming.

The king's proclamation in vv. 7-9 is the chapter's most rhetorically remarkable speech. The Mesopotamian-style royal proclamation form is preserved (the formal *ta'am hammelekh* — "decree of the king" — is the technical term for Persian-period royal proclamations, attested in Ezra 6:1, Daniel 3:10), with content theologically extraordinary. The king commands fasting that includes animals; sackcloth that covers beasts; outcry to God by the entire city; turning from "evil ways" and "violence in their hands"; and the humble *mi yodea* — "who knows? God may turn." The proclamation is theologically precise: turning from evil action, sustained outcry, hope without certainty.

The chapter's theological argument operates through reversal. The pagan king performs the *teshuvah* the prophet had refused. The "violence in their hands" of v. 8 is the standard prophetic vocabulary for imperial-political violence (cf. Habakkuk 2:8, 17 — Nineveh / Babylon is elsewhere indicted for "the violence done to Lebanon"). The Ninevites are precisely those whom the prophetic tradition treats as paradigmatic of imperial cruelty. Their repentance turning from "violence in their hands" is the theological centerpiece — the empire's representative-figures are turning from imperial violence, the very vocational identity that defined them.

The "forty days" of v. 4 has had elaborate reception. The Septuagint translates the period as "three days" rather than "forty days" — *eti treis hēmerai*. The variant has occasioned much discussion: was the Greek translator working from a different Hebrew Vorlage, or did the translator confuse the book's three-day-fish with three-day-warning? Most scholarship treats the LXX variant as translation error or alternative tradition; the MT's "forty days" is generally accepted as original.

The Hebrew text of Jonah 3 is generally stable. The vocabulary's linguistic features (Aramaic-influenced *ta'am*, post-exilic Hebrew patterns) inform the dating discussion.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Jonah 3's reception history has clustered around Nineveh's repentance, the king's exemplary response, and the divine relenting.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

The Yom Kippur afternoon haftarah reading of Jonah includes chapter 3 as the book's theological climax. The placement is liturgically powerful: on the day of Jewish national-personal *teshuvah*, the community hears the story of the foreign city's *teshuvah*. The contrast and the parallel are both significant: if Nineveh — the symbol of imperial cruelty — could repent and be received, what about the community gathered for Yom Kippur?

The Talmud (Ta'anit 16a-b, Berakhot 12b) cites Jonah 3 in discussions of communal fasting and *teshuvah*. The Ninevites' practices (fasting, sackcloth, outcry, turning from evil action) became foundational for the rabbinic theology of public fasting. Maimonides (*Hilkhot Ta'aniyot* 1:17) explicitly cites Jonah 3 in his exposition of public fast practice: the verse "let everyone turn from his evil way and from the violence that is in his hands" (v. 8) is foundational. The verse names the criterion by which fasting is judged authentic: not the fasting itself but the turning from evil that accompanies it.

Rashi reads chapter 3 with attention to the king's specific actions: rising from the throne, removing the robe, sitting in ashes. The royal humiliation is taken as a model of how the powerful must repent — by physically demonstrating their humility. Ibn Ezra develops the philological precision of the chapter's vocabulary. Radak emphasizes the chapter's theological argument: even the most foreign and improbable city can repent and be received.

The medieval Jewish reading of Nineveh's repentance was theologically generative for diaspora-era reflection. The story preserves the doctrine that the LORD's mercy is genuinely available to non-Israelite peoples; this preserved Jewish hope that surrounding empires might also turn and that Jewish communities might also be spared from coming catastrophe.

The post-Holocaust Jewish reading of chapter 3 has been complex. Some readers (Berkovits, Greenberg) have wrestled with whether the chapter's structure — the LORD's relenting in response to the foreign city's *teshuvah* — can be sustained when twentieth-century atrocities occurred without analogous relenting. The careful contemporary reading preserves both the chapter's theological possibility and the historical questions it raises.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

Matthew 12:41 and Luke 11:32 have Jesus citing Nineveh's repentance against his own generation: *"The men of Nineveh will rise up at the judgment with this generation and condemn it, for they repented at the preaching of Jonah, and behold, something greater than Jonah is here."* The Synoptic Jesus uses Nineveh as the moral comparison: the foreign city repented at minimal preaching; the religious-covenant community refuses to repent at the greater preaching of Jesus. The hermeneutical move makes Nineveh's *teshuvah* theologically positive — a witness against later resistance. N.T. Wright (*Jesus and the Victory of God*) reads this saying within Jesus's prophetic summons to first-century Israel: Jonah's word "yet forty days and Nineveh overturned" becomes the template for Jesus's warning that an unrepentant Jerusalem faces its own "overturning" — for Wright, the catastrophe of AD 70 — while a Nineveh-like turning would mean rescue; the comparison is not a generic moral lesson but a concrete eschatological alternative set before Jesus's own generation.

Patristic readings developed Nineveh's repentance as foundational for Christian theology of universal mission and conversion. Augustine (*City of God* 18.30) and Chrysostom both cite the chapter as evidence that God's mercy reaches beyond Israel to all peoples. The chapter joined the broader prophetic-canonical tradition warranting Christian Gentile mission.

Calvin's *Commentary on Jonah* gives chapter 3 a careful Reformed reading. Calvin emphasizes the king's humility as a model of how political power should respond to prophetic critique: the king who removes his robe and sits in ashes is the pattern for any sovereign confronted with the Word of God. Calvin's broader political theology (the magistrate accountable to the divine word) draws partly on this lineage.

The Reformation tradition's use of Nineveh's repentance was pastoral-evangelistic. Reformation preaching frequently cited Jonah 3 as evidence that God's grace reaches the apparently most-resistant. Wesleyan-evangelical revival preaching extended this: Nineveh's repentance was foundational for the conviction that even the most pagan culture could be reached.

The contemporary critical Christian commentary (James Limburg, Jack Sasson, Leslie Allen, John Goldingay) has read the chapter for its narrative-theological achievement, with particular attention to its theological proportionality: minimal sermon, maximum response, divine relenting.

The animal-fasting detail (vv. 7-8) has had remarkable cultural-theological reception. Medieval Christian art and devotional literature extended the Ninevite-fast image into broader theology of creation participating in *teshuvah*; contemporary commentators (Limburg, Allen) note that the detail draws all creation, not human consciousness alone, into the city's penitential turn.

### Where the Traditions Converge and Diverge

Both traditions take Jonah 3 as foundational for theology of *teshuvah*-and-mercy extending to those outside the covenant community. Both develop the chapter as evidence of the LORD's universal redemptive will. Both engage the king's exemplary humility as a model for political-religious leadership.

They diverge less on chapter 3 than on the chapter's theological-typological reception. Christian tradition has used the chapter especially for missionary-evangelistic theology (the foreign city evangelized by minimal preaching); Jewish tradition has used the chapter for Yom Kippur theology (the foreign city as comparative-paradigm for the community's own *teshuvah*).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author's vision is the LORD's mercy extending beyond all reasonable theological expectation. Chapter 3 enacts what the entire book has been preparing: the prophet who fled because he feared the LORD would extend mercy to Nineveh now sees that mercy extended. The chapter forces the reader to ask whether the LORD's mercy is consistent with what the reader had expected.

Two genuine tensions emerge.

**Tension 1: The relenting of God.**

- *Catholic / classical theist* (Aquinas, John of Damascus): The LORD's "relenting" is anthropopathic — human-emotion language describing divine action that, from the eternal perspective, was always integrated with the city's repentance. The metaphysical question is preserved within the language.
- *Reformed* (Calvin, Edwards): The relenting describes the changed circumstances (Nineveh's repentance) that warranted a different divine response. The Reformed integration of providence and contingent circumstances draws on this lineage.
- *Open theism / process theology*: The verse describes genuine divine responsiveness. The future is not pre-decided; the city's repentance genuinely reshaped the divine action.
- *Lutheran*: The relenting is theological mystery preserved without resolution. The hidden God's response to *teshuvah* is the gospel's foundational pattern.
- *Jewish (Maimonides, Hasidic)*: The relenting is foundational for Jewish theology of prayer-and-*teshuvah*. The doctrine that *teshuvah* genuinely changes the LORD's response shapes Jewish religious life.
- *Critical*: The chapter reflects ancient Israelite theology of divine relationality. Whether the relenting is metaphysical reality or theological convention is left open by the text.

**Steelmanning the position I find least intuitive**: the strict classical-theist reading. The temptation in our relational-theology moment is to dismiss classical theism as cold. But the careful classical reading is rigorous: the LORD's relenting describes divine action from inside the temporal-creaturely perspective, not metaphysical change in the divine essence. From the LORD's eternal perspective, the integrated whole (Jonah's mission, Nineveh's repentance, divine relenting) was always one act of providence; from the historical-narrative perspective, the relenting was real and responsive. The classical reading preserves both the chapter's narrative force and the doctrine of divine simplicity.

**Where this leaves us**: The chapter does not resolve the metaphysical question. It performs *teshuvah-met-by-relenting* as the texture of covenant life.

**Tension 2: The repentance of Nineveh.**

- *Catholic / Reformed*: The repentance is genuine and extends to "violence in their hands" — turning from imperial violence is the substance of the *teshuvah*. The chapter does not name the specific outcome of the repentance (whether Nineveh's violence permanently ceased or returned), but the immediate response is real. What makes it theologically arresting (Allen, Goldingay) is precisely that it is the imperial city repenting of the violence that constituted its public identity.
- *Critical (historical-skeptical)*: The chapter's depiction is theologically idealized; actual eighth-century or post-exilic Nineveh did not historically engage in such repentance. The chapter is parable-with-historical-frame, not historical record.
- *Jewish*: The chapter preserves the theological possibility of imperial *teshuvah*. Whether or not the historical Nineveh did this, the canonical text holds the possibility open.
- *Anabaptist*: The chapter warrants hope that even imperial-violent communities can turn. The repentance is genuine and lasting if it persists in the practice of non-violence.

**Where this leaves us**: The chapter does not resolve the historical question. The reader can hold the chapter's theological force without committing to specific historical claims about ancient Nineveh.

The unresolved question the author leaves: *Will the prophet be glad?* The chapter ends with the LORD's relenting and Nineveh's preservation. Chapter 4 will narrate Jonah's response. But chapter 3 itself ends with the question open: how will the prophet, who fled because he feared this exact outcome, respond when the outcome occurs?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 19** — Sodom and Gomorrah's destruction. Jonah's sermon ("yet forty days and Nineveh overturned") uses the same Hebrew root (*mahpekhah*) as the Sodom destruction. The Sodom-vocabulary positions Nineveh as Sodom-equivalent — yet Nineveh is spared. Read together to feel the theological reversal.
- **Joel 2:13-14** — "Return to the LORD your God, for he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger... Who knows whether he will not turn and relent?" Joel and Jonah share the *teshuvah*-and-relenting theology. The prophets share vocabulary; chapter 4 will develop the connection further.
- **Matthew 12:41; Luke 11:32** — Jesus's "men of Nineveh will rise up at the judgment" sayings. The Synoptic Jesus uses Nineveh's repentance as moral-eschatological comparison.
- **Acts 17:30-31** — Paul on the Areopagus: "The times of ignorance God overlooked, but now he commands all people everywhere to repent." The early Christian theology of universal repentance descends from the prophetic tradition Jonah 3 represents. When you reach Acts 17, remember Nineveh.
