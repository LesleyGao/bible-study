# Ruth 1: Empty Returning to Empty

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The opening verse plants the story "in the days when the judges ruled" — a phrase that does double work. It locates Ruth in the chaos of the Judges era (roughly 1200-1020 BCE in conventional chronology), the period of inter-tribal warfare, Philistine pressure, and the religious anarchy that closes the book of Judges with the refrain that "everyone did what was right in his own eyes." But the phrase also signals distance: the narrator is looking back across centuries. Most critical scholars — Edward Campbell in the Anchor Bible commentary, Kirsten Nielsen, Jack Sasson — date the composition itself much later, with strong arguments for either a late monarchic origin (under Josiah, when the Davidic line was being theologically consolidated) or a post-exilic setting (5th-4th c. BCE). The post-exilic dating gains force from the book's apparent counter-polemic against Ezra-Nehemiah's expulsion of foreign wives (Ezra 9-10, Neh. 13:23-31). If Ruth was composed or finalized in that climate, her unembarrassed inclusion as a Moabite woman in David's pedigree functions as a quiet but devastating intervention in a contemporary debate. Frederic Bush in his Word Biblical Commentary, by contrast, dates the language to the early monarchy on the basis of the Hebrew, which sits between Standard and Late Biblical Hebrew. The truth may be that an older story was preserved and reworked.

Bethlehem and Moab are concrete places with concrete relations. Bethlehem (Hebrew *beth-lechem* בֵּית-לֶחֶם, "house of bread") sits about six miles south of Jerusalem in the Judean hill country; archaeology at sites like Tel Beit Shemesh and the limited excavations near Bethlehem confirm a small Iron Age I-II agricultural settlement, dependent on terraced grain and barley cultivation in the rain-shadowed hills. Famines were periodic and politically devastating. Moab lay across the Jordan and Dead Sea, in what is now western Jordan, occupying the high plateau between the Arnon and the Zered. The famous Mesha Stele (discovered 1868 at Dhiban, dating to c. 840 BCE) gives us Moabite from the Moabite king's own hand — a Northwest Semitic dialect closely cognate to Hebrew, with Chemosh (כְּמוֹשׁ) as the chief deity and a remarkably similar covenantal theology of land and divine warfare. The relationship between Israel and Moab is bitterly framed in the Hebrew Bible: Moab descends from Lot's incestuous daughter (Gen. 19:30-38), Moabite kings hire Balaam against Israel (Numbers 22-24), Moabite women seduce Israel into Baal of Peor worship (Numbers 25), and Deuteronomy 23:3 famously prohibits Moabites from entering the assembly of YHWH "even to the tenth generation." Against that legal and narrative backdrop, the matter-of-fact opening — Elimelech's family settles in Moab, the sons take Moabite wives — is already loaded.

The names are deliberately legible. Elimelech (אֱלִימֶלֶךְ, "my God is king") is a theologically charged name in a book where YHWH's hidden providence will be the central argument. Naomi (נָעֳמִי) means "pleasantness," "sweetness." Mahlon (מַחְלוֹן) and Chilion (כִלְיוֹן) are widely read by Jewish commentators (already in *Ruth Rabbah* 2:5 and Rashi) as ominous: from roots meaning "sickness" and "destruction/wasting away." Whether the names are etymological back-formations from the plot or genuinely archaic, they signal the family's foredoomed trajectory. Orpah (עָרְפָּה) is sometimes connected to *oreph*, "back of the neck" — the one who turns her back. Ruth (רוּת) is most often linked to *re'ut*, "friendship," "companionship," though etymological certainty is impossible.

The material culture of the chapter is the small-scale Iron Age agricultural household: dependent on the barley harvest (April-May, the *Aviv* of the Hebrew calendar), the wheat harvest that follows, and the precarious bond between extended family, land tenure (*nahalah*, the inalienable patrimonial inheritance), and male protectors. The widow without a male relative — no husband, no son, no brother-in-law (the institution of *yibbum*, the levirate marriage of Deut. 25:5-10, requires a surviving brother of the deceased husband) — has no claim on land, no economic security, no social standing. Naomi's situation is not a feeling. It is structural destitution. The chapter's grim arithmetic — three women, three dead men, no sons — makes that visible.

A few Hebrew terms transform the chapter when you can hear them. ***Hesed*** (חֶסֶד) is the word Naomi invokes when she releases her daughters-in-law: "May YHWH deal *hesed* with you, as you have dealt with the dead and with me" (1:8). *Hesed* is notoriously hard to translate: covenant loyalty, steadfast lovingkindness, the kind of faithfulness that exceeds obligation. It is the book's theological keyword (recurring at 2:20 and 3:10), and Naomi here applies it both to YHWH and, retrospectively, to her Moabite daughters-in-law — a striking attribution. ***Shuv*** (שׁוּב), "to return," "to turn back," is the chapter's most repeated verb, occurring twelve times in twenty-two verses. It is also the standard prophetic term for repentance. Every character is choosing what to return to: Naomi to her land, Orpah to her people and her gods, Ruth to a destination she has chosen rather than received. ***Davaq*** (דָּבַק), "to cling," is the verb used of Ruth at 1:14 — the same verb Genesis 2:24 uses of a man "clinging" to his wife, and Deuteronomy 10:20 uses of Israel clinging to YHWH. The narrator is borrowing covenant vocabulary for a Moabite woman's loyalty to her mother-in-law. ***Mara*** (מָרָה), "bitter," is what Naomi renames herself: not Pleasantness but Bitterness, an act of self-renaming that recalls Jacob's refusal to be comforted (Gen. 37:35) and that forces the town to confront her grief in language. ***Shaddai*** (שַׁדַּי) is the divine name Naomi uses when accusing God: not the covenantal YHWH but the older patriarchal name, often translated "Almighty" but possibly meaning "the One of the Mountain" or "of the Breasts" (etymological debate; see Frank Cross). She is using a more remote name for the God who has, she believes, struck her down.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Ruth's genre is contested in productive ways. Hermann Gunkel classified it as a *Novelle*, a short literary novella with romantic and folkloric features. Edward Campbell argued it is a "Hebrew short story," a genre with shared formal features (compact plot, dialogue-driven scenes, careful framing, ironic providence) that includes Joseph, Esther, Jonah, and Tobit. Robert Hubbard, in the NICOT commentary, treats it as a comedy in the technical sense — a narrative that moves from emptiness and grief to fullness and resolution — and Frederic Bush in the WBC develops the same formal analysis with close attention to its scene structure. Robert Alter, in *The Art of Biblical Narrative*, treats it as a masterclass in biblical short fiction, with attention to type-scenes (the "betrothal at the well" pattern subverted in chapter 2) and to dialogue as the bearer of theological meaning.

The chapter's structure is a tight chiasm of movement. It opens with departure (Bethlehem to Moab) and ends with return (Moab to Bethlehem). The center is the encounter on the road — Naomi releasing, Orpah kissing and going, Ruth clinging and pledging. Within that frame, the chapter is shaped by triplets and reversals: three deaths, three women, three speeches by Naomi, two daughters-in-law and two responses. The famine that opens the chapter (1:1) is reversed by the harvest that closes it (1:22) — but the reversal is hollow, because Naomi returns "empty." That word *empty* (*reqam*, רֵיקָם) at 1:21 echoes the language of Exodus 3:21 (Israel did not leave Egypt empty) and inverts it. Naomi has had her exodus run backward.

Ruth's pledge in 1:16-17 is the chapter's literary high point. The Hebrew is a tightly built poetic structure of five paired commitments — where you go, where you lodge, your people, your God, where you die — capped by a self-imprecation oath (*koh ya'aseh YHWH li v'koh yosif*, "may YHWH do so to me and more also"). The form is the strongest oath formula in biblical Hebrew, used elsewhere by Saul, David, Jonathan, and Solomon. A Moabite woman is invoking YHWH by the most binding oath in YHWH's covenant vocabulary. The literary effect is that Ruth is more theologically articulate, and more covenantally committed, than any of the male Israelites in the surrounding book of Judges.

The narrator's restraint is its own technique. Three deaths in two verses (1:3-5), with no funeral, no lament, no theological commentary. Ruth's pledge is given without comment from the narrator. Naomi's bitter speech in Bethlehem is allowed to stand without correction. Meir Sternberg, in *The Poetics of Biblical Narrative*, notes that this kind of narratorial silence forces the reader to do the theological work — to decide whether Naomi is wrong, whether Ruth's pledge is a conversion, whether YHWH is in fact responsible for the bitterness. The book refuses to relieve the reader of that responsibility.

The chapter sits at a specific seam in the canon. In the Hebrew Bible's three-part structure (Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim), Ruth is in the Ketuvim, often grouped with the *Megillot* (the five scrolls) and read at Shavuot, the festival of the wheat harvest and traditionally of the giving of the Torah. In the Christian Old Testament, following the Septuagint's order, Ruth is placed between Judges and 1 Samuel — making it the bridge between the chaos of the judges and the rise of the monarchy, with the book's closing genealogy of David functioning as a structural pivot. The Septuagint placement is interpretive: it makes Ruth's story David's pre-history. The Hebrew placement, by contrast, lets Ruth stand as a discrete literary work independent of the Davidic apologetic.

There are no major textual variants of significance in Ruth 1. The DSS preserve fragments of Ruth (4QRuth-a and 4QRuth-b from Cave 4), but they are small and consistent with the Masoretic text. The LXX translates the chapter closely, with a few interpretive choices worth noting: Naomi's *Shaddai* at 1:20-21 is rendered *ho hikanos* ("the Sufficient One") in some witnesses and *pantokrator* ("Almighty") in others, reflecting the LXX's ongoing struggle with the divine epithet.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, Ruth occupies a pivotal seam. It looks backward to the patriarchal narratives (Naomi's exile and return mirror Abraham's descent into Egypt under famine, Genesis 12; Ruth's pledge echoes Rebekah's "I will go" of Genesis 24:58 and the matriarchal pattern of women whose risky choices secure the future of the covenant line). It looks forward to David and, in the New Testament reception, to Jesus (Matthew 1:5 names Ruth as one of four women in Jesus's genealogy, alongside Tamar, Rahab, and "the wife of Uriah"). It threads the question of the foreigner inside the people of God through a single woman's loyalty, and it does so without polemic — the book's argument is its narrative.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Second Temple Judaism barely engaged Ruth as a discrete book; Josephus retells it briefly in *Antiquities* 5.9 as part of the Judges-to-Davidic narrative. The major Jewish reception begins with the rabbis. *Ruth Rabbah*, a midrash compiled probably in the 6th-7th c. CE in Palestine, treats Ruth as the paradigmatic righteous convert (*ger tzedek*). The midrash glosses 1:16 word-by-word as a conversion catechism: Naomi explains the Sabbath, the prohibitions, the boundaries of life as a Jew, and Ruth answers each with assent. Ruth's pledge becomes the Bavli's model for sincere conversion (Yevamot 47b). The Talmud also confronts the Deuteronomy 23:3 prohibition against Moabites in the assembly head-on: Yevamot 76b-77a develops the principle "*Ammoni v'lo Ammonit, Moavi v'lo Moavit*" — the prohibition applies only to Moabite *men*, not to Moabite *women* — a halakhic move that retroactively legitimates Ruth's marriage to Boaz and David's lineage. Rashi (11th c., Troyes) follows this rabbinic harmonization closely; he reads Mahlon and Chilion's deaths as punishment for marrying Moabite women, and Ruth's survival as the reward of her conversion. Ibn Ezra (12th c.) is more textually attentive and less moralizing; he resists overreading the names. The Zohar treats Ruth as a vehicle of divine descent and reads the *davaq* of 1:14 as mystical clinging. In the modern period, Avivah Zornberg (whose readings of Genesis and Exodus are major) treats Ruth as a story about the labor of grief and the reconstruction of self after catastrophic loss, attentive to Naomi's bitter renaming as a refusal of cheap comfort. Yair Zakovitch's commentary attends carefully to its intertextual play with Genesis and Judges, and Jon Levenson's work on the family and covenant in the Hebrew Bible reads Ruth and Naomi's bond as an extension of covenant kinship beyond the bloodline.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic Christianity read Ruth almost exclusively typologically. Origen (3rd c.) and Jerome (4th-5th c.) treated her as a type of the Gentile church grafted into Israel. Ambrose, in *De Officiis* and elsewhere, made Ruth a model of *pietas* and chastity. Augustine in *City of God* 16.31 noted Ruth as a Gentile incorporated into the Davidic line and read her inclusion as a foreshadowing of Christ's universal kingdom. Theodoret of Cyrus wrote a brief commentary in the same vein. The medieval *Glossa Ordinaria* compiled patristic readings. Bernard of Clairvaux (12th c.) developed Ruth as a contemplative figure of the soul clinging to Christ. Thomas Aquinas treated her sparingly, mainly in genealogical context. The Reformation shifted register: Luther's prefaces to Ruth treated her as a model of faith and as evidence that salvation extends beyond Israel; Calvin's sermons on Ruth (delivered at Geneva, 1554-55) developed her as a paradigm of providence working through ordinary kinship loyalty rather than through miracle. Calvin is also unusually attentive to Naomi's grief and to her theological speech as honest accusation rather than pious resignation — a striking reading for the 16th century. The critical period (Wellhausen, Gunkel) brought Ruth into form-critical and source-critical analysis without much theological payoff. The major modern commentaries — Edward Campbell's Anchor Bible, Robert Hubbard's NICOT, Frederic Bush's WBC, Kirsten Nielsen's OTL — have transformed the field, attending to the book's literary architecture, its *hesed* theology, and its likely Sitz im Leben. Carol Meyers's work on the Israelite household economy supplies the material-culture frame for the widow's destitution and the gleaning laws. Brueggemann reads it as a story of the politics of inclusion. André LaCocque's *Ruth: A Continental Commentary* and Nielsen's commentary develop the post-exilic anti-Ezra reading. Robert Alter's translation and brief commentary attend to the literary craft. Ellen Davis in *Who Are You, My Daughter?* (with Margaret Adams Parker) reads Ruth pastorally without losing the critical apparatus.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Both traditions recognize Ruth's pledge as theologically extraordinary and treat her as a paradigm of incorporation into the people of God. They diverge sharply on the legal question of Moabite inclusion: Jewish interpretation works through halakhic harmonization (the gendered exegesis of Deut. 23:3), while Christian interpretation either ignores the legal problem (the New Testament's universalizing logic absorbs it) or reads the tension as proto-Christian (the Gentile church anticipated). Both traditions have, until the modern commentary tradition, often eclipsed Naomi behind Ruth — turning her bitterness into a setup for resolution rather than a theological statement in its own right. The recovery of Naomi as a co-protagonist, whose grief and accusation carry their own theological weight, runs through Campbell, Hubbard, and Bush and is one of the durable gains of recent scholarship.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author of Ruth believe God was doing in this story? The book's theological method is reticence: YHWH acts almost entirely off-stage, named in greetings, blessings, and accusations, but never appearing as a direct character. This is unusual in the Hebrew Bible and is the book's central theological move. Providence in Ruth is hidden in the small choices of women — to leave, to return, to cling, to glean — and made visible only retrospectively in the genealogy at the end. The theology is one of divine action through ordinary loyalty. There is no theophany, no oracle, no prophet. There is only *hesed*, exchanged among humans, and the harvest that arrives at the chapter's end as a quiet sign that the famine is over.

**Tension One: Naomi's accusation against God — pious lament or theological error?** Naomi says, plainly, that "the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me," "the LORD has brought me back empty," and "the LORD has testified against me" (1:20-21). She names YHWH as the agent of her suffering. The narrator never corrects her, and Ruth never contradicts her.

The Catholic interpretive tradition, mediated through Aquinas and Augustine, has generally read Naomi's speech as the lament of a faithful sufferer whose theology is incomplete but whose grief is honored. *Lamentations Rabbah* and the Catholic understanding of the *grief tradition* (the Joban tradition, Lamentations, the lament psalms) treat such accusation as authorized speech to God, not against God. The Catechism of the Catholic Church (§§2629-2630) acknowledges lament as a form of prayer.

The Reformed Protestant tradition, especially in Calvin's sermons on Ruth, takes Naomi's speech as honest confession of how providence appears under suffering — not as accurate theology, but as honest theology, and therefore acceptable to God. Calvin defends her right to speak this way without flattening it into resignation. Wesleyan and Lutheran readings have generally followed similar lines.

The Jewish reading, especially in *Ruth Rabbah* and later in Zornberg, reads Naomi's renaming as a refusal of premature comfort. Mara is not a complaint to be answered but a name to be inhabited until something genuinely changes. Walter Brueggemann's *The Message of the Psalms* and his work on lament generalize this: the biblical tradition not only permits but requires the speech of bitter accusation. Ellen Davis in pastoral mode has emphasized that the women who greet Naomi do not try to talk her out of her grief.

The critical reading (Campbell, Hubbard, LaCocque) sometimes pushes further: Naomi's accusation is theologically true to her experience of the deuteronomistic system. Within the framework she has inherited — that obedience brings blessing, that calamity signals divine displeasure — her family's deaths must be charged to YHWH. The book's quiet theological revolution is not to deny her speech but to add a different mode of divine presence (hidden, agential through *hesed*) without erasing her experience of divine absence.

The position that requires the most sympathetic engagement is the deuteronomistic reading Naomi herself voices: that her family has been struck down for the disobedience of leaving the land, marrying Moabites, and abandoning the covenant community in famine. This reading is not absurd. It is what the surrounding canon would lead a faithful Israelite to think. The book's response is not to refute it but to construct a counter-story alongside it.

**Where this leaves us:** Naomi's bitter speech is given the dignity of being uncorrected. The book does not require her to repent of her theology; it requires her circumstances to change. That is a model of theological pastoral care that most religious traditions struggle to honor.

**Tension Two: What does Ruth's pledge actually mean?** Is 1:16-17 a religious conversion, a kinship oath, a romantic vow, or something irreducible to those categories?

The Jewish rabbinic position, codified in Yevamot 47b and Ruth Rabbah 2:22-24, reads the pledge as a formal conversion. Naomi catechizes; Ruth assents; her pledge becomes the model for the legitimate convert (*ger tzedek*). This reading retroactively solves the Deut. 23:3 problem and grounds her place in David's lineage.

The Catholic typological reading, going back to Origen and Augustine, treats Ruth's pledge as a foreshadowing of the Gentile incorporation into the people of God — not a personal conversion in the modern Protestant sense, but an ecclesial sign.

The Reformation Protestant reading, in Calvin's sermons especially, treats it as a personal commitment of faith — Ruth choosing YHWH over Chemosh, the prototype of the believer who freely chooses the covenant.

A contemporary reading running through Campbell, Hubbard, and LaCocque resists those frames as too tidy. Ruth's pledge is not first a religious conversion to a creed — it is a commitment to a person, Naomi, and through Naomi to a people and a God. The order is theologically significant: religious belonging follows kinship loyalty rather than the reverse. LaCocque pushes this further: the book is challenging the very theology of religious purity and ethnic boundary that Ezra-Nehemiah is enforcing.

The position to steelman is the rabbinic conversion reading. It is sometimes dismissed as harmonization, but it takes seriously what the text actually says: Ruth invokes YHWH by the strongest oath formula in Hebrew. She is not just choosing Naomi; she is choosing Naomi's God by name and binding herself with YHWH's name. The rabbinic reading honors the theological specificity of her speech rather than reducing it to interpersonal loyalty.

**Where this leaves us:** Ruth's pledge is doing more than one kind of work at once — kinship, ethnic, religious, political — and the book's refusal to specify which is itself the theological argument. The boundaries of belonging are not adjudicated by which category the pledge occupies. They are reset by the act of pledging itself.

The unresolved question this chapter leaves us with: When the text refuses to correct Naomi's accusation that God has treated her bitterly, what does it mean to honor that speech in a pastoral life — to sit with the bereaved who say *the LORD has brought me back empty* without rushing to defend God or to fix the grief?

## The Thread

**Genesis 19:30-38.** Moab is born of Lot's incest with his daughters, fleeing the destruction of Sodom. Ruth's matter-of-fact identity as a Moabite woman and her ultimate place in David's lineage rewrites that origin story without erasing it. When you reach the genealogy in Ruth 4:18-22, remember that the line of David runs through both Tamar's deception of Judah (Genesis 38) and Lot's daughters' desperate pregnancy in the cave.

**Deuteronomy 23:3.** The legal prohibition that no Moabite shall enter the assembly of the LORD, even to the tenth generation, is the legal background that the book of Ruth is quietly working against. The book never quotes the law and never argues with it; it simply tells a story in which a Moabite woman is the great-grandmother of the king through whom the messianic line will run.

**1 Samuel 22:3-4.** When David is fleeing Saul, he takes his parents to Mizpah of Moab and asks the king of Moab to shelter them — citing kinship. The Moabite connection is not erased by his accession; it remains operative in his political life. Remember Ruth when David asks the Moabite king for refuge.

**Matthew 1:5.** "Salmon the father of Boaz by Rahab, and Boaz the father of Obed by Ruth." Matthew's genealogy of Jesus names four women before Mary — Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, Bathsheba — three of whom are foreigners and all four of whom enter the line through irregular or scandalous circumstances. The genealogy is making a theological claim by selection. When you read Matthew 1, remember that this is the same Ruth who cleaved to Naomi on the road from Moab.
