# Acts 24: Felix, Tertullus, and the Two Lost Years

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The hearing that opens Acts 24 takes place in Caesarea Maritima, the administrative seat of the Roman procurators of Judea since the days of Coponius (6 CE). Joseph Patrich's excavations at the site—particularly the work on the praetorium complex once thought to be Herod's promontory palace—have demonstrated that the Roman governors took over Herod's seaside residence and transformed it into the *praetorium* (Acts 23:35) where Paul was held. Lee Levine's *Caesarea Under Roman Rule* documents the Roman administrative apparatus that grew up around this seaside palace: a hippodrome, an inner harbor (*Sebastos*), a theater seating four thousand, and the strange dual identity of a Greco-Roman polis that nonetheless served as the political center of a Jewish province. The "five days" of 24:1 are the brief window the Sanhedrin needed to send a delegation by the Roman road from Jerusalem—roughly sixty-five miles, two days at administrative pace—and to retain a *rhētōr* fluent enough in Latin courtroom convention to plead the case.

That advocate is **Tertullus**, whose rhetorical opening (24:2-4) is a textbook *captatio benevolentiae*. Helen Bond's work on Roman governors in the gospels and Acts emphasizes how thoroughly the conventions of forensic speech regulated provincial hearings. The flattery of "much peace" Tertullus credits to Felix is the standard *exordium* of a Roman *causa*, but it is also bitterly ironic given what Josephus tells us. **Antonius Felix** was procurator from approximately 52 to 60 CE. He was a freedman—the brother of Pallas, Nero's powerful financial minister—elevated to a governorship rare for a former slave. **Tacitus** (*Annals* 12.54; *Histories* 5.9) skewers him in a famous line: "*per omnem saevitiam ac libidinem ius regium servili ingenio exercuit*"—"he exercised the prerogatives of a king with the temperament of a slave." **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 20.7-8; *War* 2.13) records that under Felix the *sicarii* assassins multiplied, that Felix arranged the murder of the high priest Jonathan, and that the social fabric of Judea began to fray in ways that would lead to the revolt of 66. The "much peace" of Tertullus is a piece of forensic mendacity audible to anyone in the room.

The charges Tertullus levels (24:5-6) are precisely calibrated to Roman categories. Paul is called a *loimos*—"plague" or "pestilence"—a term used in Roman political invective for those who threaten public order (Cicero uses it of Catiline). He is then called the *prōtostatēs* (ringleader, literally "one who stands at the front rank") of the *haireseōs tōn Nazōraiōn* (αἱρέσεως τῶν Ναζωραίων), the "sect of the Nazarenes." The Greek term ***haireseōs*** (αἱρέσεως) is critical. In first-century usage it is neutral. **Josephus** uses *hairesis* of the Pharisees (*War* 2.119; *Antiquities* 13.171), Sadducees, and Essenes as "schools of thought." Only later Christian theological development—chiefly second-century onward, in writers like Justin and Irenaeus—shifts the word toward the pejorative "heresy." Tertullus is calling Paul the head of a faction; the word does not yet carry doctrinal opprobrium. Finally, the temple-profanation charge (24:6) attempts to establish *seditio* under Roman provincial law, since the Jews retained their famous extraordinary jurisdiction to execute even Roman citizens for breaching the inner court—an exception **A. N. Sherwin-White** treats at length in *Roman Society and Roman Law in the New Testament* as one of the few extra-territorial exemptions Rome granted any subject people.

Paul's defense (24:10-21) reframes everything in terms of "**the Way**" (***tēs hodou***, τῆς ὁδοῦ), the Lukan self-designation already used at 9:2, 19:9, 19:23, 22:4, and now twice in 24:14, 22. This is, in Bruce's NICNT phrasing, "the earliest Christian self-name attested in the New Testament," and its Hebraic resonances—the Qumran *derekh*—suggest the term may have functioned among Aramaic-speaking believers before Luke memorialized it in Greek. Paul also invokes "*anastasis nekrōn*" (resurrection of the dead) at 24:15 and again at 24:21, deliberately reframing the controversy as an inner-Jewish theological dispute, not a political crime—a strategic move A. N. Sherwin-White recognized as a sophisticated piece of Roman jurisprudence: *res religionis* between Jewish parties were below the dignity of a Roman governor.

The chapter's most disturbing historical detail is the appearance of **Drusilla** (24:24), described by Luke simply as "Jewish" but identified by Josephus (*Antiquities* 20.7.1-2) as the youngest daughter of Agrippa I—and therefore the sister of Agrippa II and Bernice, both of whom will appear in chapter 25. Drusilla had been married to Azizus of Emesa; Felix, captivated by her beauty, sent the Cypriot magus Atomos to seduce her away from her husband. She was perhaps eighteen when this hearing occurred. Their son Marcus Antonius Agrippa would die in the eruption of Vesuvius in 79 CE. Daniel Schwartz's work on the Herodian dynasty makes clear how thoroughly Felix's marriage to Drusilla bound him into the tangled politics of Roman client kingship—and how scandalous, even by Herodian standards, the match was. When Felix listens "about righteousness and self-control and the coming judgment" (24:25), the historical layering of that listening is acute: a Roman freedman who has stolen a king's daughter is being preached to about *enkrateia* (self-control). His "I will summon you when I have an opportunity" is one of the most famous deferrals in literature.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Acts 24 inaugurates what Robert Tannehill, in *The Narrative Unity of Luke-Acts*, calls Paul's "**three-stage trial sequence**"—Felix (chapter 24), Festus (25), and Agrippa II (26)—a literary architecture deliberately patterned after the trial of Jesus before three authorities (Pilate, Herod, Pilate again) in Luke 23. The parallel is intentional and structural: in both volumes Luke presents the central protagonist before successive Roman, royal-Jewish, and finally appellate authorities, with each declaring innocence and yet none releasing him. Marguerat, in *The First Christian Historian*, calls this the "Lukan defense-trilogy" and reads it as an apologia *pro ecclesia* aimed at securing Roman recognition of Christianity as a *religio licita* under the umbrella of Judaism.

The chapter has its own internal architecture: the prosecution speech (1-9), the defense speech (10-21), the governor's deferral (22-23), and a strange epilogue—the private hearing with Drusilla and the two-year drift into custody (24-27). The prosecution and defense speeches are matched in length and stylized as forensic *agones*. Witherington III, in *The Acts of the Apostles*, points out that Luke's Greek in Tertullus's *exordium* shifts register noticeably—it is more Atticizing, more periodic, more rhetorically self-conscious than the surrounding narrative—precisely because Luke is portraying a professional Roman *rhētōr* in his native idiom. Conzelmann (Hermeneia) reads the same data more skeptically: Luke is showing off his Hellenistic education by writing a Tertullus who sounds like the schoolroom rhetors of the *progymnasmata*.

The most consequential literary feature of the chapter is the **"two whole years"** notice at 24:27 (*dietias plērōthēsēs*). Luke has used this kind of plenary chronological summary before (cf. 19:10), but here it functions as a hinge in the macro-structure of Acts. Pervo's Hermeneia commentary observes that the two-year detention at Caesarea is exactly mirrored by the "two whole years" of Roman detention that closes the book at 28:30, suggesting an intentional Lukan symmetry: Paul's last four years of public life are framed as two pairs of biennial confinements. The first biennium produces no evangelical narrative—Luke pointedly tells us only that Felix "sent for him often and conversed with him" hoping for a bribe. The second biennium produces the Roman ministry that closes the book "with all boldness and without hindrance." The contrast is not accidental.

There is also the small narrative oddity that **Luke shows us Paul's defense but not its outcome**. Felix neither acquits nor convicts; he defers. This is, in narrative terms, a kind of literary suspension—what Pervo calls "the procedural vanishing point"—and it is one of Luke's signature devices. Tannehill notes that the deferral creates dramatic tension that the reader expects to resolve in chapter 25, only to have Festus enact a different deferral (the appeal to Caesar), only to have Agrippa II enact a third deferral (the verdict that Paul "could have been set free"). The trilogy is structurally a sequence of *non-judgments*. Luke's point is that Roman justice cannot or will not produce a verdict; the verdict must come elsewhere.

A final literary observation: the chapter contains the **fifth occurrence of "the Way"** as a self-designation, and Paul accepts the term *kata tēn hodon hēn legousin hairesin* (24:14)—"according to the Way, which they call a sect." Luke is doing something subtle here. Paul does not deny the term *hairesis*; he embraces it (in its first-century neutral sense) while reserving the more theological term ("the Way") for Christian self-naming. This is one of the rare moments in Acts where the Lukan narrator allows two different vocabularies for the same movement to coexist on the page, and Johnson (Sacra Pagina) reads it as a deliberate display of perspectival relativity: what looks like a *hairesis* from the outside looks like *hē hodos* from within.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Read alongside the Pauline letters, Acts 24 produces some of the most acute interpretive friction in the New Testament. **N. T. Wright**, in *Paul: A Biography*, argues that the Caesarean detention may have been when Paul wrote some or all of the captivity epistles (Philippians, Philemon, Colossians, Ephesians)—a position with a long pedigree (B. F. Westcott, J. A. T. Robinson) but increasingly contested by those who place these letters in Rome. The Caesarean hypothesis has the virtue of explaining how Onesimus might have reached Paul (Caesarea is closer to Colossae than Rome) and how Paul might have anticipated visiting Philemon (Phlm 22) more easily from Caesarea than from Rome. **F. F. Bruce**'s *Paul: Apostle of the Heart Set Free* surveys both options and lands cautiously on Roman provenance, but he is careful to note that the two years at Caesarea cannot have been narratively or theologically empty.

The Jewish-interpretive trajectory finds Acts 24 a complicated text. **Pamela Eisenbaum**, in *Paul Was Not a Christian*, and **Matthew Thiessen**, in *A Jewish Paul*, both point to 24:14-15 as a text that complicates supersessionist readings: Paul affirms "everything laid down by the Law and written in the Prophets" and grounds his hope in a resurrection that "these men themselves accept." This is not a Paul who has left Judaism for a new religion; this is a Paul whose self-understanding is intra-Jewish. **Paula Fredriksen**, in *Paul: The Pagan's Apostle*, reads the same verses as evidence that the historical Paul thought of himself as an apocalyptic Pharisee whose mission to the Gentiles was the eschatological inclusion of the nations into the people of Israel, not the abolition of Israelite distinctiveness. Adele Reinhartz's work on Christian self-definition complicates this by noting how easily Lukan rhetoric—Tertullus on one side, Paul on the other—can be retrojected as evidence of a fully formed Jewish-Christian split, when in fact the boundaries in 56 CE were still profoundly porous.

Patristic interpretation of the chapter focused obsessively on **Felix's deferral** (24:25). **Chrysostom**, in his *Homilies on Acts* (Hom. 51), turns the moment into a homiletic centerpiece on the danger of procrastination: "*epheis tou kairou kai apōlesa*"—"he yielded the moment and lost it." Chrysostom reads Felix's "convenient season" as the universal posture of the unconverted, and he weaves into his commentary the line that became proverbial in later Western preaching: "the road to hell is paved with good intentions." Bede, drawing on Chrysostom, expanded the Felix passage into a meditation on *trembling without conversion* that influenced medieval Latin homiletics. **Calvin**, in his *Commentary on Acts*, reads Felix's trembling as evidence of natural conscience uncoupled from regenerate faith—what later Reformed theology would systematize as the doctrine of *common grace*. Luther in his *Lectures on Acts* fragments turns Drusilla into a moral exemplum: a Jewish woman who heard the gospel preached and, like her husband, deferred.

Modern commentary has converged on several questions. **Bruce's NICNT** focuses on the chronology: dating the hearing to roughly 57-59 CE depending on how one fixes the procuratorial transition. **Barrett's ICC** adopts a slightly later date (58-60). **Fitzmyer** in the Anchor Bible reads the chapter as the fulfillment of the dominical prophecy in Luke 21:12-13 ("you will be brought before kings and governors for my name's sake; this will be your opportunity to bear witness"), making Paul's appearances before Felix, Festus, and Agrippa II the explicit fulfillment of Jesus' words. **Gaventa** (Abingdon) emphasizes how Luke uses the chapter to subvert Roman categories of *gravitas*: Tertullus's flattering rhetoric is hollow; Paul's plain-spoken defense is true; the procurator who is supposed to embody Roman justice is a former slave hoping for a bribe. **Keener**, in his four-volume Acts commentary, devotes nearly two hundred pages to Acts 24-26 alone, with extensive engagement with the Tacitean and Josephan source material on Felix.

**Brian Rapske**, in *The Book of Acts and Paul in Roman Custody*, has perhaps the most thorough recent monograph on the chapter's *Sitz im Leben*. Rapske argues that the "some liberty" Paul received under *custodia militaris* (24:23) was a recognized legal status: not house arrest in the technical sense, but a custody form that allowed visitors, correspondence, and provisioning by friends. This explains how Paul could have continued ministry through the two-year detention (the captivity letters debate) and why Luke is unembarrassed by the long quiet stretch.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: How should we evaluate Felix's "trembling" (24:25)?** Three positions. (1) The *natural-conscience* reading (Calvin, Reformed tradition): Felix's trembling is the universal experience of the moral law written on the heart confronting the lived reality of sin; it is not regenerate faith and produces no fruit, but it is theologically real and serves as evidence that judgment is built into the structure of the moral universe. (2) The *almost-Christian* reading (Wesleyan, evangelical preaching tradition): Felix is presented as on the edge of conversion, separated from salvation only by his unwillingness to surrender the woman beside him and the bribe before him; the chapter functions as a warning about the seriousness of deferring conversion. (3) The *Lukan-political* reading (Pervo, Marguerat): Felix's trembling is part of Luke's larger apologetic strategy showing that Roman power is morally hollow when confronted with the gospel; the trembling is a *political* sign, not a soteriological one, and the deferral demonstrates that Roman justice is unable to render verdict on the truth.

Steelman: the natural-conscience reading does the best with Luke's careful word choice (*emphobos genomenos*—"having become afraid"), which avoids the technical vocabulary of conversion (*epistrephō*, *metanoeō*); Felix is afraid, not repentant, and Luke's restraint is the point.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter is more interested in showing what *fails* to happen than in adjudicating Felix's interior state. Paul preached *dikaiosynē*, *enkrateia*, *krima*; Felix trembled and adjourned. Luke gives us a portrait of moral perception without moral action—of conscience without conversion. The theological force is not a doctrine of common grace but a narrative warning: hearing without responding is a kind of self-deception.

**Tension 2: What is the theological significance of the two empty years (24:27)?** Three positions. (1) The *providential-formation* reading (much patristic and Reformation interpretation): the two years are God's preparation of Paul for Rome, a desert season in which the apostle was formed for the climactic ministry; nothing is wasted in providence. (2) The *Lukan-redactional* reading (Conzelmann, Haenchen): Luke had no source material for the two years and so summarized; the silence is not theological but historiographical. (3) The *literary-frame* reading (Tannehill, Marguerat, Pervo): the two years at Caesarea are deliberately mirrored by the two years at Rome (28:30), and the silence at the front of the diptych is meant to throw the eloquence at the back into relief; the gospel that goes nowhere under Felix goes "without hindrance" under house arrest in Rome.

Steelman: the literary-frame reading explains the most data—the precise verbal echo, the symmetric chronological notice, the contrast between Felix's hoped-for bribe and Paul's open hospitality in Rome.

**Where this leaves us:** Acts is willing to record long stretches in which the protagonist does nothing visible. The narrative theology is that the work of the gospel is not coterminous with the activity of the apostle. The kingdom advances even when the witness is detained. This is itself a theological claim about agency: the protagonist of Acts is not Paul.

**One unresolved question:** Did Drusilla hear Paul a second time? Luke records Felix sending for Paul "often" (24:26) but specifies no further appearance of Drusilla. The silence is suggestive—she would have been roughly twenty when the family disappeared from Caesarean society, eventually to Pompeii, eventually to the ash of 79 CE. The text leaves her interior unrendered.

## The Thread

Felix's deferral picks up an old thread from Luke 9:59-62 (the would-be disciples whose excuses postpone discipleship) and 12:19-20 (the rich fool whose "many years" become "this night"). It will be picked up again in 26:28 ("in a short time would you persuade me?") and most pointedly in 2 Corinthians 6:2 ("now is the favorable time; now is the day of salvation"), which Paul will write within a few years of this scene. Flag also the resonance with Hebrews 3:7-15's exhortation against hardening the heart "today"—a passage that early patristic readers (Chrysostom in particular) read alongside Acts 24:25 as a single biblical witness against deferral. The two-year frame at 24:27 must be read as paired with 28:30 to grasp what Luke is doing structurally.
