# Galatians 1: The Apostle Who Refused to Negotiate

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The Galatians who first heard this letter read aloud lived in a strange overlay of identities. The province of Galatia under Claudius (AD 41–54) was Roman in administration, Greek in culture, and stubbornly Celtic in the rural highlands of the central Anatolian plateau. The Galatai were descendants of Gauls who had migrated east in the third century BC, sacked Delphi in 279 BC, and finally been pinned into central Asia Minor by Attalid Pergamum. By Paul's day they spoke Greek in the marketplaces and worshipped a syncretic mix of Phrygian Cybele, the Roman imperial cult, and (in the south) the local cults of Men, Sabazios, and the Lukan deities at Lystra who took Paul and Barnabas for Hermes and Zeus (Acts 14:11–13).

Where Paul's letter actually went is contested. The North Galatian theory (championed by J.B. Lightfoot in 1865 and revived by J. Louis Martyn in his 1997 Anchor Yale commentary) places the recipients in ethnic Galatia proper — Pessinus, Ancyra, Tavium — visited on the second and third missionary journeys (Acts 16:6; 18:23). The South Galatian theory (William Ramsay 1893, F.F. Bruce, N.T. Wright, Richard Longenecker, Douglas Moo) places them in the southern Roman province cities Paul evangelized on the first journey: Pisidian Antioch, Iconium, Lystra, and Derbe. The South Galatian view tightens the dating to roughly AD 48–49, just before the Jerusalem Council of Acts 15. The North Galatian view pushes the letter to the mid-50s, after the Council. The whole question turns on whether Galatians 2:1–10 is the Acts 15 visit or an earlier visit (the Acts 11:30 famine-relief trip is one alternative). Most contemporary commentators favor the South Galatian destination because it fits Paul's missionary itinerary in Acts 13–14 and explains why the agitators' message landed: these were communities with significant Jewish populations (the Pisidian Antioch synagogue inscription, and Acts 13:14–43, attest the synagogue's role), which made circumcision an intelligible boundary marker.

The material world of these believers was the city. Pisidian Antioch was a Roman colony (colonia Caesarea) founded by Augustus around 25 BC for veterans of the Legio V and Legio VII; the Sergius Paulus inscription from there (CIL III 6687, on the Sergii family) is plausibly linked to the proconsul of Cyprus in Acts 13:7. The forum was dominated by the Augusteum and its imperial-cult propylon. In such a city the Greco-Roman religious world was not optional — civic festivals, patron-client banquets, and the imperial cult were the texture of daily life. Synagogues sat alongside as parallel communities; God-fearers like Cornelius (Acts 10) circulated in both. To enter "the church of God" without circumcision was to occupy a third, undefined category — neither pagan nor Jew. The agitators in Galatia were offering a way to resolve that vertigo: become Jews and the question disappears.

Circumcision in the ancient world was not neutral. Egyptian priests practiced a partial form (Herodotus 2.36–37). Greek athletes considered the foreskin essential to bodily integrity; epispasm (surgical reversal) was practiced by some Hellenizing Jews in the Maccabean era (1 Maccabees 1:15; Josephus, Antiquities 12.241). Philo (Spec. Laws 1.1–11) defends circumcision allegorically as removing pleasure and pride. Roman writers (Tacitus, Histories 5.5; Juvenal, Satires 14.99) viewed Jewish circumcision as a marker of separateness, sometimes mocked, sometimes admired. Hadrian's later ban (c. AD 132) shows how seriously the empire treated it as identity. For Paul to insist that Gentile believers must NOT be circumcised was to insist that the church remain visibly mixed — a point Paula Fredriksen has pressed in Paul: The Pagans' Apostle (2017): Paul's gospel is for ex-pagans, not a replacement religion for Jews.

The authorship of Galatians is universally accepted. It is one of the seven undisputed Pauline letters (with Romans, 1–2 Corinthians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, Philemon). Our earliest substantial manuscript witness is P46, the Chester Beatty II papyrus codex (c. AD 175–225), which preserves the Pauline corpus including most of Galatians. Codex Sinaiticus (ℵ, mid-fourth century) and Codex Vaticanus (B, mid-fourth century) are the major uncial witnesses. The text is remarkably stable; significant variants are few, though some matter — the Western text (D, F, G) at Galatians 2:5 omits "to whom" so that Paul appears to have yielded to the false brothers, an unmistakable reversal of the Alexandrian reading.

The autobiography of chapter 1 is one of two primary sources for Paul's life — Acts is the other. Where they conflict, most critical scholars (Gerd Lüdemann, Martin Hengel, Dunn) trust Galatians as a first-person source written under oath ("before God, I do not lie!" 1:20). The number of Jerusalem visits Paul reports (1:18 and 2:1) does not match Acts straightforwardly (Acts 9:26; 11:30; 15:2), which feeds the South vs. North Galatian timing debate.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter. **Euangelion** (εὐαγγέλιον, "good news / gospel") was political vocabulary in the Greco-Roman world — Augustus's birth was announced as euangelion in the Priene Calendar Inscription (9 BC). Paul's appropriation is polemical. **Apokalypsis** (ἀποκάλυψις, "unveiling, revelation," 1:12, 16) signals what J. Louis Martyn called the "apocalyptic" character of Paul's gospel: God's invasive disclosure, not human discovery. **Ioudaismos** (Ἰουδαϊσμός, 1:13–14) is the earliest attested use of "Judaism" as an abstract noun in extant Greek; it appears earlier only in 2 and 4 Maccabees (e.g., 2 Macc 2:21; 8:1) where it names the Maccabean cause against Hellenization. **Anathema** (ἀνάθεμα, 1:8–9) translates the Hebrew herem — devoted to destruction. And **kata anthrōpon** (κατὰ ἄνθρωπον, 1:11, "according to a human being") frames the central claim: the gospel is not of human origin.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Galatians is a polemical letter, but ancient letters were not free-form. They followed conventions, and Paul deploys them with violence. Hans Dieter Betz, in his 1979 Hermeneia commentary, argued that Galatians is structured as forensic (judicial) rhetoric: an exordium establishing the speaker's character (1:6–11), a narratio recounting the facts (1:12–2:14), a propositio stating the thesis (2:15–21), a probatio defending it (3:1–4:31), an exhortatio applying it (5:1–6:10), and a peroratio summing up (6:11–18). Betz's reconstruction has been hugely influential but also contested. George Kennedy and Richard Longenecker pushed back that Galatians more closely resembles deliberative rhetoric — persuading the audience to a future course of action — rather than forensic defense. Ben Witherington (Grace in Galatia, 1998) argued it is a hybrid with epideictic features. The disagreement matters because forensic rhetoric implies Paul is on trial; deliberative rhetoric implies he is calling the Galatians to decide.

Whatever genre label one prefers, chapter 1 is unmistakably the opening salvo. The salutation (1:1–5) is unusually combative — Paul defines his apostleship by negation ("not from men nor through man") before he has even named his audience. There is no thanksgiving section. Every other Pauline letter to a church begins with thanks (compare 1 Cor 1:4; Phil 1:3; 1 Thess 1:2). The absence here is rhetorical shock. By 1:6 Paul says, "I am astonished" (thaumazō, θαυμάζω) — a verb sometimes used in ancient letters as polite irony but here weaponized.

Paul's twin curse formulas in 1:8–9 deploy the anathema convention familiar from Greek inscriptions, Jewish herem texts (Deuteronomy 7:26; Joshua 6:17), and the Qumran community's exclusion formulas (1QS). The doubling is the signature: "as we have said before, so now I say again" creates the binding force of testimony. Combined with the oath in 1:20 ("before God, I do not lie!"), the chapter takes on the legal register of a sworn deposition.

The narratio (1:12–2:14) is autobiography in service of an argument. Paul's origin story is structured to defeat one charge: that he received his gospel from the Jerusalem apostles and is now distorting it. He counters with a temporal sequence — revelation, then Arabia and Damascus, three years before Jerusalem, only fifteen days with Cephas, only James seen besides — designed to demonstrate apostolic independence. Beverly Gaventa (From Darkness to Light, 1986) read this as a narrative of "cosmic transformation" rather than conversion in the modern psychological sense. Krister Stendahl's 1963 Harvard Theological Review essay "The Apostle Paul and the Introspective Conscience of the West" had already argued that Paul's experience on the Damascus road was a calling (compare Jeremiah 1:5; Isaiah 49:1) to the Gentiles, not an Augustinian breakdown of guilt.

The textual variant at Galatians 2:5 is the most consequential in the letter — though it lies just beyond chapter 1, it casts back on the autobiographical argument. Codex Bezae (D) and the Old Latin omit the relative pronoun, making Paul concede to the false brothers; the Alexandrian reading has him refuse. The Alexandrian text is almost universally accepted as original; the Western variant is plausibly an early scribal attempt to harmonize Paul with Acts.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** The patristic engagement with Galatians 1 was already vigorous. Marius Victorinus produced the first surviving Latin commentary on Galatians (c. AD 362), reading Paul's call as a model of philosophical conversion. Jerome's commentary (c. AD 387) leaned heavily on Origen's lost work and emphasized the polemical edge. Augustine's commentary (c. AD 394–395) was more pastoral but his correspondence with Jerome (Letters 28, 40, 75, 82) over Galatians 2:11–14 became one of the great patristic disputes — though the dispute is on the next chapter, it is set up here in chapter 1 by Paul's claim of apostolic independence: if Paul is independent of Peter, can he confront him? John Chrysostom's homilies on Galatians (delivered in Antioch, late 380s) defended Paul's harshness as physician's medicine. Theodore of Mopsuestia (early 5th c.) read the autobiography historically and was sober about its polemical edge.

Medieval interpretation domesticated the letter. Thomas Aquinas's Galatians lectures (Reportatio of Reginald of Piperno, c. 1265–73) integrated Paul's gospel with the system of grace and merit. The Glossa Ordinaria assembled a chain of patristic comments around the text. But the explosive reading came in the Reformation. Luther's 1535 Galatians commentary (his second, after the 1519 lectures) is one of the great polemical theological works of the West. Luther famously called Galatians "my Katie von Bora" — his own marriage, his beloved — because in it he found the doctrine on which the church stands or falls. Luther read 1:8–9 as the foundation of sola scriptura against papal authority: even an angel from heaven cannot bind the conscience against the gospel. Calvin's commentary (1548) shared the substance but was more measured rhetorically. Anabaptist readings (Hubmaier, Menno) emphasized the visible church and discipline.

Modern critical scholarship begins with F.C. Baur's Tübingen reconstruction (Paulus, 1845), which read all of Acts and the Pauline corpus through the lens of a Pauline-Petrine antithesis: a Gentile, law-free Pauline party against a Jewish-Christian, law-keeping Petrine party, with Acts as a later harmonization. J.B. Lightfoot (1865) defended a more historical reading and argued the South-North destination question. In the 20th century Hans Dieter Betz's Hermeneia (1979) brought rhetorical criticism; Richard Longenecker's WBC (1990) brought careful exegesis; F.F. Bruce's NIGTC (1982) brought historical-critical sobriety; J. Louis Martyn's Anchor Yale Galatians (1997) brought the apocalyptic reading. Douglas Moo's BECNT (2013) and Thomas Schreiner's ZECNT (2010) represent the contemporary Reformed mainstream.

The watershed in modern reading is Krister Stendahl's 1963 essay, which argued that the post-Augustinian, Lutheran reading of Paul mistook a missionary calling for a guilty conscience. E.P. Sanders's Paul and Palestinian Judaism (1977) demolished the caricature of Second Temple Judaism as legalistic merit-religion, replacing it with "covenantal nomism" — Israel kept the Law to maintain covenant standing already given by grace. James D.G. Dunn's 1982 Manson Lecture coined "the New Perspective on Paul" and argued that "works of the law" in Paul means specifically the boundary markers (circumcision, food laws, Sabbath) that separated Jew from Gentile. N.T. Wright extended this with a redemptive-historical frame. The Lutheran-Reformed counterresponse — Stephen Westerholm (Perspectives Old and New on Paul, 2004), Thomas Schreiner, Frank Thielman, Simon Gathercole — argued that Sanders softened Second Temple Judaism too much and that "works of the law" includes all human effort.

The apocalyptic Paul school (Martyn, Beverly Gaventa, Douglas Campbell, Martinus de Boer) reads chapter 1's apokalypsis as a cosmic invasion: God breaks into the present evil age. The "Paul Within Judaism" school (Mark Nanos, Pamela Eisenbaum, Magnus Zetterholm, Caroline Johnson Hodge) reads Paul as a Torah-observant Jewish apostle whose gospel was for Gentiles only — Galatians is not anti-Torah, it is anti-circumcision-of-Gentiles.

**Jewish scholarly critique and its evolution.** Early Jewish polemic against Paul echoes Justin's Trypho-style charges that Paul abolished Torah. In modernity, Joseph Klausner's From Jesus to Paul (1939) cast Paul as the Hellenizer who broke the Jewish Jesus from his Jewish setting. Hans Joachim Schoeps (Paul, 1959) argued Paul read Torah through a Hellenistic lens his rabbis would not have recognized. Daniel Boyarin's A Radical Jew (1994) reversed this — reading Paul as a thoroughly Jewish thinker, allegorical reader, and Hellenistic universalist within Judaism. Paula Fredriksen has insisted that Paul never left Judaism. Pamela Eisenbaum's Paul Was Not a Christian (2009) reframes the autobiographical material of chapter 1 as the call of a Jewish prophet. Amy-Jill Levine has documented how Galatians (especially chapters 3–4) has been weaponized against Judaism, and how post-Holocaust Christian scholarship — Nostra Aetate (1965), the WCC and mainline Protestant statements of the 1980s — has had to reckon with that history.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** All trajectories agree that Paul's call (1:15–16) deploys prophetic-call language from Jeremiah and Second Isaiah. They diverge on whether it constitutes a break from Judaism (older Christian reading), a calling within Judaism (Stendahl, New Perspective, Paul Within Judaism), or a cosmic apocalyptic event that relativizes the question (Martyn). They diverge on whether 1:8–9's anathema authorizes private revelation against ecclesial tradition (Luther) or whether it presumes a public, apostolic gospel that Paul is defending against innovation.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did Paul believe God was doing in this moment? He believed the God of Israel had unveiled (apokalypsis, 1:12, 16) the crucified and risen Messiah Jesus, that this unveiling carried with it a commission to preach Christ "among the Gentiles" (1:16) without making them Jews first, and that any modification of this gospel — even angelic — was anathema. The act of God Paul announces is the inclusion of the nations into the Abrahamic promise (an argument he develops in chapter 3) on the basis of the faithfulness of Christ and the gift of the Spirit, not on the basis of ethnic boundary markers. The world has been split by an invasive event that creates a third people who are neither Jew nor pagan in the ordinary sense.

**Tension 1: Apostolic authority versus ecclesial tradition.** Paul claims his gospel is "not from man" (1:11–12). What does this mean for the relation between revelation and the church?

The Catholic position (codified at Trent, Session IV, 1546, and reiterated in Dei Verbum at Vatican II, 1965) reads Paul's claim within a unified deposit of faith: Paul's revelation is not in tension with the apostolic college because it is the same Holy Spirit who confirms it (note Paul's later trip to Jerusalem to verify, 2:1–2). The authority of Scripture and the authority of the church's living tradition are co-equal expressions of the one apostolic gospel. The Catechism (CCC 80–83) sees Paul's confrontation with Peter (2:11–14) as an internal correction within the apostolic college, not a precedent for individual conscience against the church.

The Lutheran reading (Luther's 1535 Galatians commentary) takes 1:8–9 as the foundational text against ecclesiastical authority: even an angel — even a pope — preaching a different gospel is anathema. Sola scriptura emerges from this verse. The conscience captured by the word of God can stand against the church when the church preaches a different gospel. The Reformed tradition (Calvin) shares the substance but locates the test more in the church's confessional standards than in the individual conscience.

The Orthodox reading (Vladimir Lossky, Georges Florovsky) emphasizes that revelation is given to the church, not the isolated apostle, and that Paul submits his gospel to Peter and James (Gal 2:2) as a proper synodal act. The Spirit speaks through the conciliar mind. Personal revelation must be received within the church's noetic life or it lapses into Gnostic pretension.

The Paul Within Judaism reading (Nanos) sees the issue not as authority but as halakhic disagreement within an early Jewish movement: Paul's apokalypsis authorizes a halakhic ruling on Gentile inclusion, not a new religion.

The steelman of the Catholic-Orthodox position: Paul himself goes to Jerusalem (2:1–2) and submits his gospel. He fights for its acceptance synodally, not against synodality. The "individual conscience against the church" reading flattens the texture of the chapter. Where this leaves us: Paul's claim of independence is not a doctrine of private revelation but a defense that his apostolic commissioning is itself original, not derivative — a structural claim about apostolicity that the church has had to negotiate ever since.

**Tension 2: Continuity versus rupture with Judaism.** Paul speaks of his "former life in Judaism" (1:13). Does this language imply he has left Judaism, or that he has moved within it from the Pharisaic-zealot stream into a new sectarian configuration?

The traditional Christian reading (Augustine through Luther through 20th-century Lutheran scholarship): Paul converted from Judaism to Christianity. His old life and his new life are discontinuous. The gospel is the fulfillment of Israel's hope but the church is the new Israel.

The New Perspective (Sanders, Dunn, Wright): Paul did not leave Judaism in a religious sense; he was called to a specific mission within the eschatological purposes of Israel's God. "Judaism" in 1:13 names the zealous Pharisaic stream he has left behind, not the religion of Israel as such. N.T. Wright (Paul and the Faithfulness of God, 2013; Paul: A Biography, 2018) reads the Damascus event as the moment Paul discovered that the single-family promise to Abraham had been fulfilled in the crucified-and-risen Messiah, and that his own zealous "Judaism" — modeled on Phinehas and the Maccabees — had been aimed in the wrong direction; the gospel he now preaches is the announcement that in the Messiah the one God has formed the one worldwide family promised to Abraham, with no second-tier membership for the uncircumcised.

The Paul Within Judaism school (Nanos, Eisenbaum, Fredriksen): Paul never left Judaism at all. He remained Torah-observant. His mission was to Gentiles, who were not to become Jews but to remain Gentiles in Christ. The "former life" is the persecution-zealot phase, not Judaism.

The apocalyptic Paul reading (Martyn, Gaventa, de Boer): the question of continuity vs. rupture is the wrong question. The cross is a cosmic apocalyptic event that creates a new creation in which the old categories — including Jew/Gentile — are relativized. Paul is not within Judaism nor outside it; he is in the new creation.

The Jewish reading (Boyarin): Paul is a Hellenistic Jew who allegorically universalizes Israel's election. He remains within Jewish thought-forms even as he reconfigures them.

The steelman of the Paul Within Judaism position: Paul's language in 1:13–14 is precisely the Maccabean language for a sectarian conflict within Judaism (compare 2 Macc 8:1; 14:38). Paul never says he has left Judaism. He says he has been called to a mission. The traditional reading imports later Christian-Jewish separation back into Paul. Where this leaves us: the texture of Paul's self-description is more Jewish than the Reformation could see, but the apocalyptic intensity of his apokalypsis language pulls against any reduction of the gospel to a halakhic ruling. Paul is harder to place than any single school admits.

**One unresolved question.** If Paul's gospel comes by direct revelation and not from human tradition, what prevents the next person who claims direct revelation from displacing Paul's gospel — and what test, internal to Paul's own argument, distinguishes apostolic apokalypsis from sectarian innovation?

## The Thread

**Genesis 12:1–3.** When Paul says God "set me apart before I was born" (1:15), he is using prophetic-call language but also reaching back to the Abrahamic call: God's strategy of blessing the nations through a chosen one is being recapitulated in Paul's commissioning to the Gentiles. When you get to Galatians 3:8 ("the Scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the Gentiles by faith, preached the gospel beforehand to Abraham"), remember 1:15.

**Jeremiah 1:5 and Isaiah 49:1.** Paul's "set me apart from my mother's womb" deliberately echoes Jeremiah's call ("before I formed you in the womb I knew you") and the Servant of Isaiah 49 ("the LORD called me from the womb"). Paul reads his apostolic commission through the prophetic-Servant matrix — not as a generic Christian conversion but as a specific eschatological sending to the nations. This is the key Stendahl saw.

**Acts 9:1–19; 22:6–16; 26:12–18.** The three Lukan accounts of Paul's Damascus encounter parallel and complicate the autobiographical claim of Galatians 1. When the accounts disagree (number of Jerusalem visits, role of Ananias, duration in Damascus), critical scholars give priority to Paul's first-person testimony in Galatians. The relationship between Acts and Paul's letters remains a permanent question for Pauline studies.

**Romans 1:1–7.** Paul's later letter opens with the same self-definition — "set apart for the gospel of God" — but in a calmer register, without polemical edge. When you get to Romans, remember that the polemical Galatians self-portrait is the same person who, writing to a church he has not founded and whose gospel he has not had to defend, can present his apostleship without the curse formulas. The gospel is the same; the tone reflects the threat.
