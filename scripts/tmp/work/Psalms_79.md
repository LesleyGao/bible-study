# Psalm 79 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1 — The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 79 is a *mizmor le-Asaph* (מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף, "a psalm of Asaph") — the seventh psalm in the Asaphite cluster (Pss 73-83) gathered in Book III of the Psalter, in the heart of the **Elohistic Psalter** (Pss 42-83), where the divine name *YHWH* is largely replaced by *Elohim*. The Asaphites were Levitical singers descended, the Chronicler tells us, from David's chief musician at the ark (1 Chr 16:4-7); their guild composed and curated psalms for the Jerusalem cult across centuries. Psalm 79 carries the Asaphite signature in its concern for sanctuary, covenant, and the question of how Israel sings when the sanctuary itself has been profaned.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 79 as a **communal lament** (*Volksklage*) — the genre Israel reaches for when catastrophe strikes the nation as a whole. The form-critical anatomy is textbook: an opening complaint addressed to God (79:1-4), a "how long?" outcry (79:5), petitions for divine vengeance and forgiveness (79:6-12), and a closing vow of praise (79:13). Sigmund Mowinckel placed such laments in liturgies of national mourning; Claus Westermann distinguished communal from individual laments by their plural pronouns and their grounding in collective trauma rather than personal affliction. Psalm 79's plurals are everywhere: *nachalatkha* ("your inheritance"), *avadekha* ("your servants"), *chasidekha* ("your faithful"). The "we" who pray are the surviving remnant of a people, not an individual sufferer.

The historical occasion is named almost without disguise. "O God, the nations have come into your inheritance; they have defiled your holy temple; they have laid Jerusalem in ruins" (79:1). The mainstream critical and traditional reading places the psalm in the wake of the **Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 587 BCE** — Nebuchadnezzar II's sack of the city, the burning of the First Temple, and the deportation that opened the Babylonian exile (2 Kgs 25; Jer 52). The vivid detail of corpses left unburied, given to "the birds of the heavens for food, the flesh of your faithful to the beasts of the earth" (79:2), echoes Jeremiah's prediction of exactly this fate (Jer 7:33; 16:4; 19:7) — a covenant-curse drawn from Deuteronomy 28:26. A minority view, going back to Theodore of Mopsuestia and revived by some critical scholars, dates the psalm to the **Maccabean crisis** of 167 BCE, when Antiochus IV Epiphanes desecrated the Second Temple and slaughtered the faithful (1 Macc 1:37, 7:16-17 — the latter quoting 79:2-3 explicitly as fulfilled scripture). Most modern commentators (Hossfeld-Zenger, Tate, Goldingay, Kraus) prefer the 587 BCE setting as the original Sitz im Leben, while granting that the psalm was reused liturgically during the Maccabean period and beyond. The double resonance — a psalm written for one catastrophe and recited at another — is itself a feature of Israel's lament tradition.

Hebrew anchors the psalm's grief. The temple is defiled — *timme'u et heikhal qodshekha* (טִמְּאוּ אֶת־הֵיכַל קָדְשֶׁךָ, 79:1) — and the verb *timme* is the precise priestly term for cultic defilement, the inverse of *qiddesh* ("to sanctify"). Jerusalem is reduced to *iyyim* (עִיִּים, "ruin-heaps," 79:1), the same word Micah 3:12 uses prophetically of Zion's coming desolation. The psalm's most haunting line — *shafkhu damam ka-mayim* (שָׁפְכוּ דָמָם כַּמַּיִם, "they have poured out their blood like water," 79:3) — uses the verb *shafakh*, the technical sacrificial term for pouring out blood at the altar, but here the blood is the people's own, spilled not in offering but in slaughter. The simile *ka-mayim* ("like water") strips even the residual dignity of sacrificial blood from the violence: this blood is wasted, profligate, unclean. The psalmist is writing in the categories of a Levitical priesthood, but those categories have been violated by the very fact the psalm laments.

Dead Sea Scrolls fragments of Psalm 79 survive in 4QPsf and partial witnesses elsewhere; the text is stable. The Septuagint renders the psalm faithfully as Psalm 78 LXX. Ancient Near Eastern parallels include the Sumerian "Lament for the Destruction of Ur," whose vocabulary of unburied corpses, defiled shrines, and abandoned sanctuaries shares so much with Psalm 79 that it would be strange if the genre conventions were not, at some level, shared inheritance. The difference: the Sumerian lament weeps that the city's gods departed; Psalm 79 weeps that Israel's God has not yet returned.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Lowth's parallelism organizes Psalm 79's grief from the first verse. *Synonymous parallelism* opens the psalm in a triple beat: "the nations have come into your inheritance; / they have defiled your holy temple; / they have laid Jerusalem in ruins" (79:1) — three movements of profanation, each escalating: invasion, defilement, destruction. *Synthetic parallelism* dominates the petitions ("Help us, O God of our salvation, for the glory of your name; / deliver us, and atone for our sins, for your name's sake," 79:9), where the second line completes and extends the first. *Antithetic parallelism* surfaces in the closing vow: the dead servants of v. 2 give way to the surviving "sheep of your pasture" of v. 13 — death is set against ongoing praise.

The psalm's strophic structure tracks the lament form's classic stations. Strophe one (79:1-4) is **complaint**: the nations have done this. Strophe two (79:5-7) is the **how-long question** and the petition for vengeance against the nations. Strophe three (79:8-10) is **petition for forgiveness and vindication**, fusing the people's confession of sin with a demand that God answer the nations' taunt "Where is their God?" Strophe four (79:11-12) is **petition for the prisoners and sevenfold return** of the taunt. Strophe five (79:13) is the **vow of praise**.

Inclusio frames the psalm. The opening word — *Elohim* (אֱלֹהִים, "O God," 79:1) — and the closing word — *tehillatekha* (תְּהִלָּתֶךָ, "your praise," 79:13) — bracket the lament's emotional arc. The psalm begins by addressing God amid the unspeakable; it ends by promising to address God in praise across generations. The shift from corpses (79:2) to "we your people, the sheep of your pasture" (79:13) is a quiet act of theological resilience: the psalm does not deny the slaughter, but it refuses to let the slaughter be the final word about who Israel is.

A delicate chiastic structure organizes vv. 5-12. The opening petition (v. 6 — "pour out your anger on the nations") is mirrored by the closing petition (v. 12 — "return sevenfold into the lap of our neighbors"). Between them, vv. 8-10 turn inward: "do not remember against us our former iniquities" (v. 8) confesses Israel's own complicity. The psalm refuses the simple binary of innocent-Israel-against-evil-nations. The lament includes a confession that its own people have sinned, and the request for forgiveness is laced into the request for vengeance. This is theologically unusual and morally strenuous — Israel asks God to be both her judge and her avenger in the same breath.

There is no *selah* in Psalm 79 — striking for an Asaphite lament. The psalm reads as a single sustained cry, without liturgical pause. Hossfeld-Zenger suggest the omission keeps the lament unbroken, refusing to grant the kind of ritual cadence that might soften the rawness.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story (Jewish and Christian Trajectories)

Psalm 79 sits in the heart of **Book III**, which Gerald Wilson reads as the canonical lament over the failure of the Davidic covenant. Where Psalm 78 supplies the historical ground for God's choice of Zion, Psalm 79 cries out at the apparent unmaking of that choice: the temple defiled, the city in ruins, the chosen people slaughtered. Book III asks a single anguished question — has God abandoned the covenant? — and Psalm 79 is one of its sharpest forms of that question.

The **Jewish trajectory** of Psalm 79 is one of the deepest in the Psalter. The psalm became, and remains, a fixed liturgical text for **Tisha B'Av** (the ninth of Av), the annual fast commemorating the destruction of both temples — 587 BCE Babylonian and 70 CE Roman. On Tisha B'Av the psalm is read in the synagogue along with the book of Lamentations and the *kinnot* (medieval elegies for catastrophes); it is also chanted in some traditions at the Western Wall. *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 79 dwells especially on the question "Why should the nations say, 'Where is their God?'" (79:10) — the rabbis read the verse as Israel's appeal to God's own honor, the argument that the nations' mockery of Israel is also mockery of Israel's God. Rashi reads the psalm as straightforwardly historical — the destruction of the First Temple — but reads its application as ongoing, applicable to every catastrophe Israel has endured. Ibn Ezra and Radak similarly anchor the psalm in 587 BCE while honoring its liturgical reuse. Nachmanides links the psalm's "atone for our sins, for your name's sake" (79:9) to the doctrine that exile itself is atonement.

The Holocaust gave Psalm 79 a new and almost unbearable hermeneutic weight. Emil Fackenheim's "**614th commandment**" — formulated in *To Mend the World* and *God's Presence in History* — argues that after Auschwitz Jews are commanded not to grant Hitler a posthumous victory, not to despair of God or of the world, not to abandon the covenant. Psalm 79's refusal to stop praying despite massacre, its insistence that "we your people, the sheep of your pasture, will give thanks to you forever" (79:13), is one of the texts Fackenheim's reading depends on. Dorothee Sölle, in *Suffering*, reads the psalm in a similar key: it is the prayer of those who refuse silence after catastrophe, neither denying the horror nor abandoning the address to God. Levenson and Sarna read the psalm as exemplary of biblical lament's refusal to be edified out of grief; Buber emphasizes its dialogical address; Heschel hears in it the prophetic-poetic confrontation of God with God's own people's suffering. Avivah Zornberg reads the psalm psychoanalytically as a prayer that holds together rage, confession, and the determination to praise.

The **Christian trajectory** has been quieter — partly because the psalm's specifically Jewish historical referents (temple, Jerusalem, the inheritance) make Christian appropriation harder, and partly because the violence of the imprecation has embarrassed many Christian readers. Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on 79 reads the destroyed Jerusalem as a type of the church under persecution and the unburied bodies as the martyrs whose blood, in the famous Tertullianic phrase, becomes the seed of the church. Athanasius and Cassiodorus follow similar typological paths. Aquinas reads the psalm's request for divine vindication as fully compatible with the gospel's logic: God's name is glorified when injustice is exposed and corrected. Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* emphasizes the psalm's confession-of-sin element: "do not remember against us our former iniquities" (79:8) is, for Luther, the pivot of the whole prayer, the psalm's recognition that catastrophe and complicity are entangled. Calvin's 1557 commentary handles the imprecation carefully — the request for sevenfold return (79:12) is read not as personal vindictiveness but as a desire that God be God, that injustice not be the world's last word.

Modern critical scholarship has produced rich engagement. Gunkel set the form-critical category; Mowinckel located the psalm in cultic lament; Westermann gave the most theologically rigorous treatment of the lament form's structure; Kraus and Hossfeld-Zenger anchored the 587 BCE dating and traced the editorial placement in Book III. Goldingay's commentary handles the imprecation theologically, refusing to apologize for it while refusing also to celebrate it. Tate, Wenham, Brueggemann (especially in *Spirituality of the Psalms* and the seminal essay "The Costly Loss of Lament"), Patrick Miller, William Brown, and Beth Tanner have all argued that the contemporary church has impoverished its prayer by removing imprecatory psalms from its liturgies. Brueggemann's argument is sharpest: the alternative to imprecation is not forgiveness but denial, and a church that cannot pray Psalm 79 cannot honestly grieve. Erich Zenger's *A God of Vengeance?* is the standard modern Christian engagement with imprecation and reads Psalm 79 as morally serious, not morally embarrassing.

NT use of Psalm 79 is partial but real. **Revelation 6:10** — "How long before you will judge and avenge our blood on those who dwell on the earth?" — the cry of the martyrs under the altar, draws directly on Psalm 79:5 and 79:10. **Revelation 11:2 and 16:6** echo the psalm's imagery of profaned holy ground and outpoured blood. **Luke 21:24** ("Jerusalem will be trampled underfoot by the Gentiles") echoes 79:1's vocabulary of nations entering the inheritance. The psalm is in the New Testament's bloodstream even when it is not directly quoted.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions are unavoidable, and pretending otherwise lets the psalm off too easily.

**Tension one: the imprecation in 79:6, 10, 12 — vengeance after genocide.** "Pour out your anger on the nations." "Let the avenging of the outpoured blood of your servants be known among the nations." "Return sevenfold into the lap of our neighbors the taunts with which they have taunted you." Modern readers, especially Christian readers shaped by the Sermon on the Mount, recoil. Three positions on the field. (1) **Imprecation-as-honest-prayer reading** (Brueggemann, Zenger, much modern lament theology): the psalm names the unspeakable, hands the demand for justice over to God, and refuses to take revenge into human hands — the imprecation is a transfer of vengeance to God precisely so the petitioner does not enact it. (2) **Imprecation-as-superseded reading** (much patristic and Reformation moralism, some modern Christian discomfort): the imprecation belongs to an older covenant and has been replaced by Jesus's command to love enemies; the psalm is preserved for memory but not for prayer. (3) **Imprecation-as-survivor-speech reading** (Fackenheim, Sölle, Holocaust-era Jewish theology): the psalm is the prayer of victims, and only victims have the moral standing to pray it; for the comfortable to dismiss it is to dismiss the dignity of the slaughtered. Steelman across all three: the imprecation is morally serious, not casual; it is addressed *to God* and not enacted *by humans*; it confesses sin (79:8) at the very moment it asks for vengeance, refusing the binary of innocent-victim and pure-evil-enemy. **Where this leaves us:** the psalm is a survivor's prayer, and faithful reading begins by asking who has the standing to pray it now. For those who have buried their children, the psalm is permission. For those who have not, the psalm is an education in what catastrophe does to language. To remove the imprecation is to lie about what suffering sounds like; to enact the imprecation is to misread its grammatical structure, which hands the action to God.

**Tension two: confession of sin alongside protest of injustice (79:8-9 inside the lament).** The psalm asks God to forgive Israel's "former iniquities" in the same breath that it protests the nations' atrocities. Three positions. (1) **Theodicy-of-judgment reading** (much patristic and traditional Jewish exegesis): Israel's suffering is divinely permitted because of Israel's sin, and the psalm honestly confesses that — exile is deserved, even as the means of exile (Babylon's brutality) was not. (2) **Anti-blame-the-victim reading** (Brueggemann, Holocaust-era Jewish theology): the psalm's confession does not justify the slaughter; it merely acknowledges that suffering people are also sinful people, while refusing the move from "sinful" to "deserved this." (3) **Both-and reading** (Levenson, Hossfeld-Zenger, Wenham): the psalm holds together honest confession and honest protest because both are true, and theology that erases either falsifies the situation. Steelman: the psalm refuses to choose between "we have sinned" and "this is unjust" — both are spoken in the same prayer, addressed to the same God, by the same people. **Where this leaves us:** Psalm 79 is one of scripture's clearest models of how to pray when you are both guilty and wronged — a posture that almost everyone occupies but almost no one can articulate. The psalm gives the words.

A closing question. The psalm ends with the surviving remnant promising to praise God "from generation to generation" (79:13). Across the centuries Jews have done exactly that — praying Psalm 79 on Tisha B'Av through the destruction of the Second Temple, the medieval massacres, the expulsions from England and Spain, the Khmelnytsky pogroms, the Shoah. The psalm worked. The promise to praise from generation to generation has not been broken. What does it mean for Christian readers, who do not share this lineage of catastrophe, to inherit a psalm that has already done its work for someone else? Perhaps it means listening before speaking — learning the prayer's shape before presuming to pray it on our own terms.

## The Thread

- **Revelation 6:9-10** — the martyrs under the altar cry "How long, O Lord?... will you judge and avenge our blood?" — drawing directly on Psalm 79:5 and 79:10.
- **Jeremiah 10:25** quotes 79:6-7 nearly verbatim, the tradition of the petition for divine wrath against the nations cross-flowing between psalm and prophet.
- **Lamentations 1-2** is Psalm 79's closest scriptural neighbor in genre and trauma — read together they form the canon's most sustained reckoning with the 587 BCE catastrophe.
- **Luke 21:20-24** echoes 79:1's vocabulary as Jesus prophesies a second destruction of Jerusalem, a layer of typological-historical resonance the early church heard immediately.
