# 2 Kings 23: The Reform That Was Not Enough

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The eighteenth year of Josiah — 622 BCE — runs from the book's discovery in chapter 22 directly into the most extensive religious reform recorded in the Hebrew Bible. The chapter narrates a sequence of cultic interventions across Judah, the territory of the former northern kingdom, and the central highlands, ending with the king's death at Megiddo in 609 and the brief reigns of his sons Jehoahaz and Jehoiakim under Egyptian and then Babylonian overlordship.

The political window for the reform is narrow and deliberate. Ashurbanipal died around 631; the Assyrian core was under Babylonian and Median pressure through the 620s; Asshur fell in 614, Nineveh in 612. Josiah's reform happens during precisely the decade when Assyrian power has retreated from the Levant but Babylonian power has not yet replaced it. **Mordechai Cogan and Hayim Tadmor** in their AB commentary stress this geopolitical opening: the destruction of altars to "the host of heaven" and "the constellations" (23:5) that the kings of Judah had ordained — the Mesopotamian astral cult Manasseh had introduced under Assyrian vassalage — was politically possible only after the Assyrian state retreated. The reform's extension into the cities of Samaria (23:15-20) presupposes Josiah was operating in territory the Assyrians had ceased to control. **Marvin Sweeney's** *King Josiah of Judah* argues that the reform also had a territorial-political dimension: Josiah was attempting to reconstitute a "Greater Israel" extending into the former northern kingdom, anticipating the Davidic-Solomonic ideal, with the cultic reform as the symbolic and legal vehicle.

The material details are richly attested. The **Topheth** of the Valley of Hinnom (Gehenna; 23:10) is the child-sacrifice site Manasseh had used (cf. 21:6); **Lawrence Stager and Samuel Wolff's excavations of the Carthaginian Tophet** at Salammbo (Tunisia) recovered hundreds of cremation urns of infants and children with associated stelae dedicated to Tanit and Baal Hammon, providing the strongest archaeological documentation of the Phoenician-Punic *molk* sacrifice the Hebrew Bible repeatedly condemns. The Carthage Tophet's existence and scale (Iron II through the Hellenistic period) confirms the institutional reality of the practice the Hebrew Bible reports. Stager and Wolff's Israelite Tophet research argues the Hinnom Valley site was its Levantine counterpart, deliberately defiled by Josiah.

**Solomon's high places** "east of Jerusalem, to the south of the mount of corruption" (23:13) — the *har hammashḥît*, the Mount of Corruption, a deliberate distortion of *har hammishḥâ*, the Mount of Anointing or Mount of Olives — were the cultic sites Solomon built for Ashtoreth (Astarte/Ishtar), Chemosh, and Milcom (1 Kgs 11:7). Their location on the southern Mount of Olives is geographically specific; pilgrimage and survey work has identified Iron II cultic remnants in the eastern slopes consistent with the description.

**Bethel** (23:15-20), where Jeroboam I had built the rival royal sanctuary at the time of the kingdom's division (1 Kgs 12:28-33), receives extended treatment. **Albright's** 1934 excavations at Tell Beitin and **James Kelso's** subsequent work documented Iron II destruction layers consistent with seventh-century cultic disruption, though attribution specifically to Josiah is debated. **Israel Finkelstein** has argued that Bethel's archaeological profile suggests the major site survived through the Babylonian and Persian periods, complicating the picture of total destruction the dtr suggests.

The administrative geography of Josiah's reform — "from Geba to Beersheba" (23:8) — defines a kingdom roughly coterminous with late-monarchic Judah's boundaries: from the northern town in Benjamin near the Bethel border down to the southern Negev frontier. **Eilat Mazar's** Ophel excavations and the **City of David** late-monarchic strata provide the Jerusalem context; **Ramat Rachel** under Yohanan Aharoni's excavations preserved seventh-century royal-administrative architecture (lmlk-jar handles, palace remains) that may reflect Josianic-era expansion of state administration. The "house of the cult personnel" (*haqqədēšîm*; 23:7) "in the house of YHWH, where the women wove hangings for the Asherah" — the textile-production attached to the Temple's Asherah cult — is one of the most concrete descriptions in the Hebrew Bible of late-monarchic Temple religious life as it actually was, and the dtr's horror at it reads as a belated polemic against an integrated YHWH-Asherah cult that the **Kuntillet 'Ajrud** and **Khirbet el-Qom** inscriptions ("YHWH and his Asherah") confirm was widespread.

Three Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. *Bərît* (בְּרִית), "covenant" (23:2-3), is the formal political-theological category Josiah invokes — the public covenant ceremony at the Temple is constitutional in nature. *Shiqquṣ* (שִׁקּוּץ), "abomination" (23:13, applied to Solomon's high places), is the dtr's strongest cultic condemnation, a term reserved for foreign deities and cultic practices viewed as ritually polluting. *Wəṭimmēʾ* (וְטִמֵּא), "he defiled" (23:8, 10, 13, 16), is the technical inversion of holiness — Josiah uses ritual pollution (especially human bones, the strongest Levitical pollutant) as the instrument of cultic destruction. The bones-on-altar tactic at Bethel (23:16) and at the high places (23:14) is theologically calibrated: a defiled altar cannot be reconsecrated.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: extended narrative of cultic reform, framed by covenant ceremony (vv. 1-3) and Passover celebration (vv. 21-23), with regnal evaluation (vv. 25-27) and death-of-king material (vv. 28-30) followed by the brief regnal accounts of Jehoahaz (vv. 31-35) and Jehoiakim's opening (vv. 36-37). The chapter is the longest in 2 Kings and contains the densest cluster of place-names and cultic objects in the entire book.

The structural movement is geographic and theological at once. Verses 1-3: covenant ceremony at the Temple in Jerusalem. Verses 4-14: cultic destruction in Jerusalem and Judah. Verses 15-20: extension into the former northern kingdom (Bethel and Samaria). Verses 21-23: centralized Passover. Verses 24-25: summary evaluation of Josiah. Verses 26-27: the qualifying note — wrath not turned. Verses 28-30: Josiah's death. Verses 31-37: Jehoahaz and Jehoiakim. The sequence enacts the dtr's theology: covenant first, destruction of false worship second, restoration of legitimate worship third, evaluation fourth, the unresolvable problem fifth, the failure of dynasty sixth.

**Robert Alter** notes the chapter's heavy use of the verb *šābar* ("to break, smash") and *śārap* ("to burn") in repetitive parallel structures — the prose accumulates physical violence against cultic objects in a way that mimics the iconoclasm itself. The reform is rendered as kinetic action.

The most theologically loaded literary moment is the prophecy fulfillment at Bethel (23:15-18). 1 Kings 13 had recorded an anonymous "man of God" coming from Judah to Jeroboam's altar at Bethel and prophesying: "O altar, altar, thus says the LORD: Behold, a son shall be born to the house of David, Josiah by name, and he shall sacrifice on you the priests of the high places who burn incense on you, and human bones shall be burned on you" (1 Kgs 13:2). Josiah is named three centuries before his birth; the chapter narrates the prophecy's fulfillment with Josiah taking the bones from nearby tombs and burning them on the altar (23:16). **Polzin** in *Moses and the Deuteronomist* and **Walsh** in his FOTL commentary read the 1 Kings 13 / 2 Kings 23 inclusio as the dtr's most explicit demonstration of prophetic word-fulfillment as the engine of the History — a long arc of verbal anticipation finds its concrete realization. The fact that 1 Kgs 13 names Josiah suggests the unit was either composed late (after Josiah's reform) or is a vaticinium ex eventu retrojection. Most critical scholars (Cross, Nelson, Sweeney) read it as the latter.

Place in the book argument: 2 Kings 23 is the climax of Dtr1 on **Frank Moore Cross's** model. The pre-exilic edition culminated here: Josiah is the new David, the Davidic covenant is secure, the prophecy of 1 Kgs 13 is fulfilled, the dynasty endures. Verses 26-27 are Cross's clearest example of Dtr2 redaction — the exilic editor's qualifying note that explains why, despite the climactic reform, the catastrophe still came. The language of "I will remove Judah also out of my sight, as I have removed Israel" (23:27) directly contradicts the confidence of vv. 1-25 and reads as theological reckoning after the fact. **Richard Nelson's** *Double Redaction* extends this analysis. **Steven McKenzie's** *Trouble with Kings* questions the cleanness of the Dtr1/Dtr2 division but accepts vv. 26-27 as redactionally distinct.

Textual variants: the LXX preserves at 23:8 the difficult phrase "the high places of the gates" with slightly different syntax. **4QKgs** preserves fragments of this chapter consistent with MT. **2 Chronicles 34-35** parallels the chapter with major reorganization: the Chronicler places much of the iconoclasm before the book's discovery (in Josiah's twelfth year, 34:3-7), narrates a much expanded Passover account (35:1-19), and gives substantial detail to Josiah's death and the prophet-warning Necho gave him (35:20-25). The Chronicler-Kings divergence is theologically pointed: the Chronicler smooths the apparent contradiction between Josiah's righteousness and his violent death by inserting the warning Josiah ignored — the Chronicler's Josiah falls because of one disobedience, while the Kings author leaves the death as theological scandal.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Jewish interpretive trajectory has wrestled most intensely with two questions: how the reform's "no such Passover since the days of the judges" (23:22) can be reconciled with earlier Passover celebrations, and how the most righteous king can die at Megiddo. **Josephus** in *Antiquities* 10.4-5 paraphrases the chapter with elaboration, narrating Josiah's death as a tragic miscalculation: Josiah marched out against Necho thinking to defend Babylonian interests against Egyptian aid to Assyria, was killed by an Egyptian arrow, and was mourned across the kingdom. Josephus emphasizes the public grief and the prophet Jeremiah composing laments — drawing on 2 Chronicles 35:25's note that "Jeremiah uttered a lament for Josiah."

**b. Taʿanit 22b** records the rabbinic tradition that Josiah's death was the single moment that made the destruction of the Temple inevitable, since Josiah was the last righteous king who could have averted it. **Lamentations Rabbah** treats the lament-tradition for Josiah as the prototype of the lament for Jerusalem itself.

**Rashi** on 23:22 explains the "no such Passover" by distinguishing centralization: there had been Passovers under earlier kings, but never with the entire population gathered in Jerusalem under a single covenanted leadership. **Radak** elaborates that the verse refers to the structural completeness of the celebration — covenant + iconoclasm + Passover all occurring simultaneously. **Ralbag** notes that the Hebrew construction *ʾāsâ* with the technical Passover formula matches Deuteronomy 16's prescription, making this the first Deuteronomic Passover. **Abarbanel** engages the scandal of Josiah's death at length, arguing that the reform was incomplete in two senses: it was imposed top-down rather than emerging from the people's hearts, and it did not extend long enough for full institutional embedding before Josiah's death cut it off.

**Yair Zakovitch** in his Hebrew commentary observes the chapter's literary architecture as deliberate: the centralization of worship at Jerusalem, prescribed by Deuteronomy 12, is enacted; but the Deuteronomic blessing on those who keep the covenant is precisely what is denied at vv. 26-27. The narrative both validates and qualifies the Deuteronomic theology. **Avivah Zornberg** explores Josiah's fate as a meditation on the limits of intentional historical action — the most thoroughgoing reform cannot reverse the accumulated weight of the past.

The Christian interpretive tradition uses Josiah primarily as the type of the righteous reformer. **Origen** in his Homilies on Jeremiah cites Josiah's iconoclasm as the type of Christian moral purification — the soul as Temple to be cleansed of idols. **Augustine** in *City of God* 18 mentions Josiah briefly as the last good king before the catastrophe; Augustine's larger argument requires Josiah as the proof that even thorough reform cannot save a city whose collective moral debt is too great. **Theodoret of Cyrus** in his *Quaestiones in Reges* engages the central scandal — why the most righteous king dies violently — and argues that the death is a mercy: Josiah is spared seeing the destruction his people have earned. **Chrysostom** in his Homilies on the Statues uses Josiah's covenant ceremony (23:1-3) as a model of public penitence; he reads the chapter at length in his civic-religious preaching to Antioch. **Gregory the Great** in the *Pastoral Rule* uses Josiah as the type of the ruler who actively pursues righteousness rather than passively avoiding evil.

**Aquinas** in the *Summa* uses Josiah's reform briefly as evidence of legitimate political authority over religious abuses (IIa-IIae q. 12 a. 2). **Calvin's sermons on 2 Kings** (Supplementa Calviniana) read the chapter as a paradigm of magisterial reform — the king responds to the recovered word, executes the reform, restores legitimate worship. Calvin uses the chapter explicitly to defend Reformed civil-magisterial intervention against Roman cultic practices; the parallel between Josiah's destruction of the Asherah and the Reformation's destruction of altars and images is not subtle. **Calvin's** treatment of Josiah's death is theologically careful: Calvin argues Josiah's righteousness was real, his reward was real (the dynasty briefly continued), but the larger judgment had to fall because the people did not match the king. **Luther** treats Josiah in his lectures on Deuteronomy as exemplary of how scripture-shaped governance should look. **Both Luther and Calvin** wrote at length on Josiah; the destruction of Jerusalem produced extensive Reformation reflection on whether reform can avert judgment.

Critical scholarship on chapter 23 is dominated by the question of how much of the reform is historical and how much is literary construction. **Cogan and Tadmor** in the AB commentary accept substantial historicity for the cultic interventions in Judah while questioning the extension into Samaria as possibly idealized. **Marvin Sweeney's** *King Josiah of Judah* argues for substantial historicity of the entire reform program, including the territorial expansion. **Ernst Würthwein** and earlier German scholarship had been more skeptical, treating much of the chapter as dtr literary construction. **Oded Lipschits** in *The Fall and Rise of Jerusalem* has provided the standard archaeological frame — Judah's settlement profile through the late-monarchic period is consistent with substantial royal-administrative reach. **Israel Finkelstein** has been the most prominent archaeological skeptic, arguing that Josiah's territorial expansion into the north was minimal and that the Bethel destruction is not clearly attested in seventh-century strata. **Lawrence Stager's** late-Iron archaeological synthesis in *Forging an Identity* supports a more substantial Josianic reach.

**Bernard Levinson's** *Deuteronomy and the Hermeneutics of Legal Innovation* analyzes how chapter 23's iconoclasm aligns with Deuteronomic legal demands and argues that the reform programmatically enacts Deuteronomy 12-26. **Eckart Otto's** *Deuteronomium* commentary works through the legal-historical correspondences in detail. **William Schniedewind** in *How the Bible Became a Book* situates the reform within the late-monarchic literary explosion that produced (or revised) much of the Pentateuch and Deuteronomistic History.

The destruction of the Asherah and the women weaving for it (23:7) terminates what was evidently a substantial institutionalized cult, and the violence at 23:20 — Josiah sacrificing the priests of the high places on their own altars, anticipated as a tactic at 1 Kgs 13:2 — is a moral problem the chapter's celebratory tone does not fully suppress. There is also an unaddressed irony in the larger arc: Huldah, the prophet consulted in chapter 22, authorizes a reform that in chapter 23 dismantles the Temple's Asherah cult and its weavers. Historians of Israelite religion (Mark Smith, Rainer Albertz) read the reform as a decisive step toward monotheistic exclusivism.

**Walter Brueggemann** in his *1 & 2 Kings* (Smyth & Helwys) argues the chapter is the dtr's tragic high point: the most thorough reform conceivable is enacted, and it changes nothing. Brueggemann's *Theology of the Old Testament* engages 23:25-27 as the dtr's most honest theological moment — the recognition that righteousness does not produce the outcomes righteousness should produce. **Iain Provan** in his commentary engages the question of whether the dtr is endorsing or critiquing magisterial reform — the violence at Bethel and against the priests of the high places sits uneasily with the broader Deuteronomic ethic. **Ellen Davis** in her contemporary readings engages the chapter's environmental dimensions: the destruction at the Kidron, the bones-on-altars, the geographical reach of the iconoclasm as a ritual transformation of landscape.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**First tension: the most righteous king dies a violent death.** Josiah is evaluated more highly than any king of Judah: "before him there was no king like him, who turned to the LORD with all his heart and with all his soul and with all his might, according to all the Law of Moses, nor did any like him arise after him" (23:25) — language drawing directly on the Shema (Deut 6:5). Yet at 23:29, Pharaoh Necho kills him at Megiddo. Three positions on what this means. Position one (Sweeney, conservative): the death fulfills Huldah's "gathered to your grave in peace" (22:20) by ensuring Josiah dies before the catastrophe; the violence of the death is incidental to its theological meaning, which is temporal-priority. Steelman: this reading preserves the integrity of both the prophecy and the evaluation, and it accounts for the fact that the dtr does not treat Josiah's death as judgment. Position two (Brueggemann, theological): the death is the dtr's confession that righteousness does not produce the rewards righteousness should produce; the chapter is the most honest moment in the History because it acknowledges the disjunction between obedience and outcome. Steelman: this honors the moral seriousness of the dtr's project, which would be trivial if righteousness always produced flourishing. Position three (Chronicler-influenced): Josiah's death was a punishment for one disobedience — refusing Necho's prophet-warning at Megiddo (cf. 2 Chr 35:21-22) — and the Kings author has compressed the narrative without removing the cause. Steelman: this preserves a strict deuteronomic economy of obedience and reward, but at the cost of importing material the Kings author chose not to include.

Where this leaves us: the Kings author leaves Josiah's death theologically open. The chapter neither explains nor justifies it. The dtr's silence on causation — combined with the immediately preceding "no king like him" — produces a tension the text refuses to resolve. The most plausible reading is that the dtr is acknowledging, at the climax of his hopeful arc, that the catastrophe his theology promised reform would avert is in fact already underway.

**Second tension: the reform's effectiveness.** Verses 1-25 portray a thoroughgoing, comprehensive, possibly unprecedented reform. Verses 26-27 declare that nonetheless YHWH did not turn from his wrath. Three positions. Position one (covenantal, classical Reformed): the reform was real and right but insufficient because it was royal-political rather than fully participatory; the people did not change their hearts at the depth the king did, and corporate covenant requires corporate response. Steelman: 23:3's note that "all the people joined in the covenant" sits uneasily with this, but the larger pattern of Kings — repeated reforms followed by relapse — supports the diagnosis of insufficient depth of popular response. Position two (Cross, redaction-critical): vv. 26-27 are Dtr2's exilic insertion explaining why the dtr's confident pre-exilic theology had to be revised; the reform's failure is the editor's interpretation rather than the original meaning. Steelman: the language of 26-27 does read like later theological reckoning, and the chapter's structure is more coherent without these verses. Position three (theological-tragic, Brueggemann): the reform was effective in itself but came too late; some accumulations of corporate sin cannot be reversed even by maximal righteousness, and the chapter is acknowledging this. Steelman: this preserves both the reality of the reform and the inevitability of judgment, at the cost of accepting a theology in which obedience does not always produce its prescribed outcomes.

Where this leaves us: 2 Kings 23 portrays the most thorough reform conceivable failing to avert catastrophe. This is the dtr's hardest theological position. The chapter does not explain what would have been sufficient. It implies that nothing would have been.

**Unresolved question:** What does the dtr think about the violence at 23:20 — Josiah sacrificing the priests of the high places on their own altars? The chapter narrates this without comment; the reader is left to decide whether this is exemplary or scandalous. The Chronicler's parallel does not include it. The Christian interpretive tradition has largely passed over it. The text leaves the moral assessment to the reader.

## The Thread

Cross-references that matter. **Deut 6:5** ("with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your might") is cited verbatim at 23:25 — Josiah is constructed as the embodied fulfillment of the Shema. **Deut 12:1-14** prescribes the centralization of worship at one place; 23:8-9 enacts it. **Deut 16:1-8** prescribes the centralized Passover; 23:21-23 enacts it. **1 Kgs 13:1-3** prophesies Josiah's destruction of the Bethel altar three centuries in advance; 23:15-18 fulfills it.

Forward references: Josiah's death (23:29) recurs in **2 Chr 35:20-25** with the warning-from-Necho elaboration; Jehoahaz's deposition and exile to Egypt (23:31-34) is the first time a Davidic king dies in foreign exile and sets the pattern for what 24-25 will narrate; Jehoiakim's tribute-extraction (23:35) anticipates the reign of resentment that produces the first Babylonian intervention. **Jer 22:10-12** preserves Jeremiah's lament for Jehoahaz (Shallum) as the king who will not return — the Egyptian exile is the irreversible threshold the chapter crosses. The reform's failure to avert the catastrophe sets up the entire theological problem that the closing **2 Kgs 25:27-30** will reopen — if Josiah's reform was insufficient, what does Jehoiachin's release in Babylon thirty-seven years later signify? The dtr leaves the question for the reader to carry forward.
