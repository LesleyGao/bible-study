# Matthew 9: Mercy, Not Sacrifice

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Matthew 9 returns Jesus from the Decapolis to "his own city" (9:1) — Capernaum, the lakeside fishing village that Matthew identifies as Jesus's adopted base after the move from Nazareth (4:13). The chapter's setting is densely material. The paralytic of 9:1-8 is carried on a ***klinē*** (κλίνη) — a low pallet or stretcher of woven matting on a wooden frame, the standard sleeping arrangement of Galilean peasant homes excavated by Loffreda at Capernaum and by Strange and Meyers at Nazareth. The basalt foundations of those houses, with shared courtyards (***insulae***) and roofs of beaten earth laid over palm-frond matting, are what make Mark's parallel detail of the friends "digging through the roof" (Mark 2:4) materially intelligible — though Matthew compresses past that detail.

The "tax booth" (***telōnion***, τελώνιον) where Matthew sits in 9:9 is a specific institution. Capernaum stood astride the *Via Maris*, the ancient trade artery running from Egypt through Galilee toward Damascus, and at the boundary between Herod Antipas's tetrarchy of Galilee and Philip's tetrarchy of Gaulanitis (across the Jordan a few miles east). A toll station collected duty on goods crossing that boundary. Roman archaeologist **John Donahue** has documented the tax-farming system: the *publicani* contracted with Rome for the right to collect, while local *telōnai* (often Jewish) actually sat at the booths, taking customs (*portoria*) on transit goods. The work was lucrative and despised — collaborationist by definition, and usually corrupt. Mishnah *Nedarim* 3:4 and *Tohorot* 7:6 group tax collectors with thieves and Gentiles for purposes of impurity and oath-validity. **E. P. Sanders** (*Jesus and Judaism*) cautions against overreading the social stigma — the rabbinic texts are later — but the New Testament's consistent pairing of "tax collectors and sinners" (Matt 9:10, 11:19, 21:31; Luke 5:30, 7:34, 15:1) reflects a real first-century social classification.

The hemorrhaging woman of 9:20-22 lived under the Levitical *zavah* protocols (Lev 15:25-30): a woman with a chronic discharge of blood was ritually unclean, and so was anyone who touched her or what she sat on. Twelve years of this condition (the same length of life Jairus's daughter has lived, in a deliberate Markan pairing Matthew preserves) meant twelve years of exclusion from temple worship, from sexual relationship, from much of ordinary social contact. Her touch of Jesus's ***kraspedon*** (κράσπεδον, "fringe" or "tassel," 9:20) refers to the *tzitzit* required by Numbers 15:38-40 — the knotted blue cords on the four corners of a Jewish man's outer garment. The woman is not grasping randomly; she is touching the most observant and visible marker of Jesus's Torah piety.

The ruler whose daughter has died (9:18) is unnamed in Matthew — Mark and Luke give him the name *Jairus* and the office of ***archisynagōgos*** (synagogue ruler). The flute players and mourning crowd of 9:23 reflect well-attested first-century Jewish funeral practice; Mishnah *Ketubot* 4:4 specifies that even the poorest in Israel must hire at least two flutes and one wailing woman. **Joachim Jeremias** (*Jerusalem in the Time of Jesus*) details the speed of Jewish burial — typically the same day in Galilee's hot climate — which makes the urgency of Jairus's request and the rapidity of the mourners' assembly historically credible.

The "two blind men" of 9:27-31 cry out "**Son of David**" (***huie Dauid***, υἱὲ Δαυίδ), the gospel's first messianic-Davidic title from the healing crowds. The Davidic shape of Jesus's miracle ministry has Old Testament resonance with Solomon, traditionally regarded as a healer-exorcist (cf. Josephus, *Antiquities* 8.45-49); first-century texts such as the *Testament of Solomon* and the *Pseudepigraphic Psalms of Solomon* frame the Davidic Messiah as restorer of Israel's bodily and political wholeness. The crowd's title is theologically loaded: the man healing them is the legitimate king.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Matthew 9 completes the second triad of the miracle cycle (paralytic / Gadarene demoniacs from chapter 8 / paralytic continues here, then bridges to the third triad: Jairus's daughter and the hemorrhaging woman, two blind men, mute demoniac). **Davies and Allison's** ICC structural analysis treats 8:1-9:34 as a unified "ten miracles in three triads with bridging discipleship/mission material" — and chapter 9 contains both the third triad and two crucial bridge passages: the call of Matthew (9:9-13) and the question about fasting (9:14-17).

The chapter is dense with Matthean redactional fingerprints. The most famous is the citation of **Hosea 6:6** in 9:13: "Go and learn what this means: 'I desire mercy, and not sacrifice.'" Only Matthew uses this Hosea text, and he uses it twice — here and in 12:7 (the cornfield Sabbath dispute). **Donald Hagner** (WBC) and **R. T. France** (NICNT) both note that the citation is programmatic for Matthean theology: it frames Jesus's relationship to Pharisaic observance not as antinomian rejection but as a *prophetic* re-prioritization. The verb ***mathete*** (μάθετε, "learn") is rabbinic in shape; Jesus is meeting his interlocutors on their own terrain, sending them back to the prophets to do the kind of *midrash* they themselves practice. Matthew is the most rabbinic of the gospel writers, and 9:13 is one of his clearest signatures.

The call of Matthew (9:9) presents the gospel's most famous synoptic harmonization problem. Mark 2:14 and Luke 5:27 call the man **Levi** son of Alphaeus; Matthew 9:9 calls him **Matthew**, and the lists of the Twelve identify him as "Matthew the tax collector" (10:3). Three positions on the discrepancy. **Bauckham** (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*) defends the traditional identification: Levi was the man's birth name, *Matthias / Matthew* his post-call name (a not-uncommon practice; cf. Simon → Cephas, Saul → Paul). **Davies/Allison** are more cautious, surveying the possibility that Mark's Levi and Matthew's Matthew are different historical persons whom the Matthean tradition has conflated. **France** prefers the Bauckham reading and notes that the early church's unanimous identification of the apostle Matthew as the gospel's source is hard to dislodge without specific contrary evidence. The Matthean evangelist's choice to put *himself* in the call narrative — if he is indeed the source — is read by patristic interpreters from **Jerome** onward as humility (the writer naming his own scandalous past) and by modern source critics as a probable fingerprint of authentic apostolic memory.

Matthean compression is again striking. Mark 5:21-43 tells the Jairus / hemorrhaging woman story in twenty-three verses; Matthew (9:18-26) gives it nine. The girl is already dead when the ruler approaches (Matthew tightens Mark's two-stage narrative); the woman's twelve years of suffering and her interaction with the doctors (Mark 5:25-26) drop out; the disciples' confusion about who touched Jesus (Mark 5:31) drops out. **Ulrich Luz** (Hermeneia) reads this as Matthew's consistent pattern: distill to the Christological core, eliminate narrative texture that does not bear the weight of declaration.

Matthew's signature *doubling* recurs in the chapter — two blind men (9:27-31) where Mark has Bartimaeus alone (10:46-52), and the Jericho doubling will return in Matthew 20:30. The mute demoniac of 9:32-34 has no Markan parallel and overlaps with the longer Beelzebul controversy of 12:22-32 — many scholars (Hagner, Davies/Allison) read 9:32-34 as a Matthean foreshadowing of the conflict that 12:22-32 narrates in full.

Three Greek terms anchor the chapter. ***Exousia*** (ἐξουσία, "authority") returns at 9:6 — Jesus's authority "on earth to forgive sins" — and at 9:8 in a telling Matthean addition: "they glorified God, who had given such authority *to men*" (plural ***anthrōpois***, ἀνθρώποις). Matthew's plural prepares for the delegation of that authority to the Twelve in 10:1. ***Splanchnizomai*** (σπλαγχνίζομαι, "to be moved with compassion," literally "to feel in the bowels"), introduced at 9:36 as Jesus looks on the harassed crowds, is a strong Matthean term used at key narrative junctures (cf. 14:14, 15:32, 20:34). ***Therismos*** (θερισμός, "harvest") appears in 9:37-38 — "the harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few" — and binds the chapter's miracle ministry directly to the Mission Discourse in chapter 10.

Textually the chapter is mostly stable. Notable variants: 9:8 (some manuscripts read "they marveled" rather than "they were afraid"), 9:13 (some manuscripts add "to repentance" after "but sinners," a likely Lukan harmonization at 5:32), and 9:34 (D and several Old Latin witnesses omit the verse entirely; **Bart Ehrman** has argued this could be original, but the majority reading is preferred by NA28).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Read canonically, Matthew 9 is a sustained meditation on the *grammar of mercy*. The chapter's opening words to the paralytic — "your sins are forgiven" (9:2) — connect physical healing to atonement in a way that the Old Testament has rehearsed (Ps 103:3, "who forgives all your iniquity, who heals all your diseases") but never collapsed onto the actions of a single prophetic agent. The chapter's central citation of Hosea 6:6 lifts the prophet's critique of mechanical cult and applies it to a question of table fellowship; its closing image of harassed sheep awaiting a shepherd echoes Numbers 27:17 (Moses's prayer for a successor) and Ezekiel 34 (YHWH himself shepherding his scattered flock).

**Jewish interpretive trajectories** read this chapter with particular nuance. **Amy-Jill Levine** (*The Misunderstood Jew*; *Jewish Annotated NT*) repeatedly cautions against the standard Christian framing in which Jesus's "mercy not sacrifice" is read as opposing Jewish ritual. The Hosea citation is *internal* to the prophetic tradition; it appears on Jesus's lips not as a Jewish-Christian polemic but as a rabbinic move (cf. Yochanan ben Zakkai's later use of the same Hosea verse at the temple's destruction, recorded in *Avot de-Rabbi Nathan* 4: "We have an atonement equal to the temple — *gemilut hasadim*, deeds of loving-kindness"). **Daniel Boyarin** (*The Jewish Gospels*) reads Jesus's authority to forgive sins as a high-Christological claim that nevertheless emerges from within Jewish messianic categories — the Danielic Son of Man who receives ***exousia*** (Dan 7:14) is precisely a heavenly-divine figure who can do what only God can do. **Geza Vermes** notes that the call of a tax collector to a Galilean sage's table is unusual but not unprecedented; the Talmud preserves narratives of charismatic *hasidim* extending fellowship to *am ha-aretz* outsiders. **David Flusser** stressed the continuity of Jesus's table fellowship with the prophetic tradition's solidarity with the marginalized.

**Christian interpretive trajectories** are dense across the chapter. **Origen** allegorized the paralytic's friends as the *virtues* that carry the soul to Christ; the unshrunk patch on the old garment as the inadequacy of the law to contain gospel grace. **John Chrysostom**, in homilies 29-32 of his *Ninety Homilies on Matthew*, reads the "mercy not sacrifice" saying as the entirety of Christian ethics — every other commandment depends on it — and the call of Matthew as the model for clerical vocation. **Augustine** in *Sermon 86* and *Quaestiones Evangeliorum* 1.13 took the Matthean tax-booth conversion as the paradigm of grace defeating works: the man does nothing to deserve the call, and yet he rises and follows. **Jerome** in his *Commentary on Matthew* mounted what would become the dominant patristic harmonization: Levi and Matthew are the same man, the evangelist humbly identifying himself.

**Aquinas** in the *Catena Aurea* assembles the patristic comments verse by verse and in his *Lectura super Matthaeum* gives the chapter's theology a Thomistic shape: the paralytic narrative demonstrates the *causal connection* between forgiveness of sins and the healing of bodily disorder (sin and disease are not identical, but disease is a consequence of the Fall, and the same divine power addresses both). The "mercy not sacrifice" saying enters Aquinas's discussion of the *new law* in *Summa* I-II.107.

**Calvin** in his *Harmony of the Evangelists* reads Matthew's call as the model of *vocatio efficax* — effectual calling; the paralytic narrative as evidence that ***exousia*** to forgive sins belongs to Christ alone, against the medieval claim that the priesthood inherits and exercises that authority sacramentally. **Luther** preached the unshrunk-patch and new-wineskins sayings as a manifesto for the Reformation: gospel cannot be patched onto a self-justifying religious system but requires a wholly new container.

In the twentieth century, **Bonhoeffer** in *Discipleship* takes the call of Matthew as paradigmatic: "And he rose and followed him." There is no preparation, no deliberation, no negotiation; the call creates the obedience it demands. **Bultmann's** form-critical analysis classified the controversy stories (9:1-8, 10-13, 14-17) as *apophthegmata* — pronouncement stories — whose narrative frames are kerygmatic settings for the dominical sayings they preserve.

**Davies and Allison's** ICC volume 2 remains the most comprehensive technical treatment; **Ulrich Luz's** Hermeneia traces the reception of "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" through Augustine, Aquinas, Luther, and Wesleyan ethics into modern social-gospel readings. **R. T. France** (NICNT) is incisive on narrative structure; **Craig Keener** (NICNT) rich on Greco-Roman backgrounds; **Daniel Harrington** (Sacra Pagina) economical and Catholic; **John Nolland** (NIGTC) careful on Greek; **Donald Hagner** (WBC) judicious; **D. A. Carson** (Expositor's Bible) the leading evangelical conservative voice.

**Literary criticism** since **Jack Dean Kingsbury's** *Matthew as Story* has read Matthew 9 as the chapter where the gospel's *opposition* — the Pharisaic-scribal antagonist whose role will only deepen — first comes into sustained focus. The "scribes" of 9:3 (the paralytic narrative), the "Pharisees" of 9:11 (table fellowship) and 9:34 (Beelzebul accusation), and the disciples of John in 9:14 — three distinct groups questioning Jesus's authority and practice — form a coordinated literary front.

**Political readings** read Matthew's tax-booth call as a quiet but pointed gesture: Jesus reclaims a man whose work was emblematic of Roman extraction and integrates him into a community ordered by mercy, not by tribute. **Warren Carter** (*Matthew and Empire*) develops this at length.

Commentators across the spectrum note how thoroughly Matthew has *compressed* the Markan account of the hemorrhaging woman (9:20-22): Mark's woman tells Jesus "the whole truth" (Mark 5:33), but the Matthean woman is silent — she touches the *tzitzit* without permission and is healed before she speaks a word. **Levine** in the *Jewish Annotated NT* contests the standard Christian framing that her purity barrier was uniquely Jewish — Greco-Roman cultic codes had analogous menstrual purity rules — and pushes back against readings that treat first-century Judaism as uniquely oppressive of women; **Bauckham** (*Gospel Women*) and **Davies/Allison** treat her, with Jairus's daughter, as a deliberate Markan-Matthean pairing (twelve years of bleeding, a twelve-year-old girl) about the reach of Jesus's restoring power across the boundaries of impurity and death.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions structure the theological reading.

**First tension: the relation of forgiveness to healing.** Matthew 9:1-8 stages a controversy in which Jesus connects sin and disease without identifying them, and forgiveness and healing without subsuming one to the other. Three positions on what this connection means. **(1) Causal-individual reading**: the paralytic's particular paralysis was caused by his particular sin, and the forgiveness must precede and produce the healing. This is a strand in some patristic moral readings (Origen at moments, some medieval *exempla*) and in modern faith-healing communities. Steelmanned, it points to the narrative sequence — Jesus speaks forgiveness *before* healing — and to Old Testament passages connecting individual sin to individual disease (Ps 38, Ps 41:4). **(2) Cosmological-corporate reading**: disease as such is a consequence of the *Fall*, not of the patient's particular sin; Jesus's forgiveness and healing are two aspects of a single eschatological reversal, neither caused by nor causing the other. This is the Augustinian, Thomistic, and Reformation mainstream. Steelmanned, it accounts for John 9:2-3 ("Neither this man nor his parents sinned"), for Job, and for the gospel's own pattern of healing without forgiveness elsewhere. **(3) Sacramental-ecclesial reading**: in the church's life, forgiveness and healing are sacramentally linked (cf. James 5:14-16's prayer-anointing-confession); the paralytic narrative authorizes the church's continuing practice of binding pardon to bodily care. This is the patristic-medieval-Catholic reading developed by Aquinas and codified in the Council of Trent's discussion of unction. Steelmanned, it preserves the connection without imposing causal individualism. *Where this leaves us:* (2) is exegetically strongest as a reading of *the historical event*; (3) is theologically strongest as a reading of *the church's practice*; (1) is too narrow but preserves a real biblical concern (some illness is a consequence of moral disorder; cf. 1 Cor 11:30) that the cosmological reading too quickly dismisses.

**Second tension: mercy and sacrifice.** Jesus's citation of Hosea 6:6 (9:13) cuts both ways in Christian history. Three positions. **(1) Antithesis reading**: mercy *replaces* sacrifice; Jesus is announcing the abolition of cultic religion in favor of ethical mercy. This is the dominant Marcionite-tending impulse of much liberal Protestantism, and (in different form) some modern social-gospel readings. Steelmanned, it takes "I desire mercy *and not* sacrifice" at face value and reads Matthew's twin uses of the citation (9:13, 12:7) as programmatic for the gospel's ethical center. **(2) Priority reading**: mercy *outranks* sacrifice; Hebrew rhetorical idiom regularly uses "not...but..." as "not so much...as...". Sacrifice is not abolished but subordinated. This is the patristic-Reformation-Catholic mainstream. Steelmanned, it accounts for Hosea's own continuing affirmation of cult elsewhere in the prophets (cf. Mic 6:6-8, where the same rhetorical structure clearly subordinates without abolishing) and for Jesus's own attendance at temple festivals throughout the gospels. **(3) Internalization reading**: mercy *is* the true sacrifice; the cultic apparatus was always meant to symbolize and enact what mercy embodies, and Jesus's claim relocates sacrifice from the temple altar to the merciful act. This is **N. T. Wright's** preferred reading and traces back to the prophetic tradition's interiorization of sacrifice (Ps 51:17, "the sacrifices of God are a broken spirit"). Steelmanned, it preserves both the cult's legitimacy and the gospel's prophetic edge. *Where this leaves us:* (2) and (3) together. The Hosea citation does not abolish sacrifice; it relocates and reinterprets it. Christian readings that have used 9:13 as a rejection of Jewish cultic religion have misread both Hosea and Matthew.

**Unresolved question.** What does the warning the two blind men violate (9:30-31, "see that no one knows about it") actually mean? Why does Jesus repeatedly insist on silence about his miracles (cf. 8:4, 9:30, 12:16, 16:20) when the gospel's own theology seems to demand public proclamation? **William Wrede's** "messianic secret" hypothesis remains the standard scholarly framing, but Matthew's distinctive use of the motif — softer than Mark's, more pastoral — has resisted full explanation.

## The Thread

The chapter's ***exousia*** to forgive sins (9:6, 8) returns at 18:18 (binding and loosing delegated to the church) and at 28:18 ("all authority in heaven and on earth"). The "mercy not sacrifice" citation of 9:13 will return verbatim at 12:7. The harvest saying of 9:37-38 launches the Mission Discourse of chapter 10 directly. The "Son of David" cry of the two blind men (9:27) prefigures the same cry at 15:22 (Canaanite woman), 20:30-31 (Jericho blind men), and 21:9 ("Hosanna to the Son of David," at the triumphal entry). The Beelzebul accusation of 9:34 returns in full at 12:22-32. The *splanchnizomai* / shepherdless-sheep image of 9:36 returns at 14:14 (feeding of the five thousand) and binds Jesus's compassion to Ezekiel 34's promise of YHWH-as-shepherd come to gather the scattered flock.
