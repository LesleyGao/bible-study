# Ephesians 2: The Wall That Came Down

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The plausibility of every line of Ephesians 2 depends on a wall that actually stood in Jerusalem. The Herodian temple complex, completed in stages from 19 BC into the 60s AD, was organized as a series of nested courts of decreasing accessibility: outermost, the Court of the Gentiles, where any human being could enter; then a low stone parapet roughly four and a half feet high called the *soreg*, beyond which Gentile entry was forbidden on pain of death. Josephus describes it in *Antiquities* 15.417 and again in *Jewish War* 5.193-194: a balustrade of finely worked stone, equipped at intervals with pillars bearing inscriptions in Greek and Latin warning that any foreigner crossing the line would have only himself to blame for the death that would follow. Two of those warning stones have actually been recovered: the first by Charles Clermont-Ganneau in 1871, now in the Istanbul Archaeological Museum; the second, fragmentary, by J.H. Iliffe in 1935, now in the Israel Museum. The Greek of the Clermont-Ganneau stone reads, in part, *MĒTHENA ALLOGENĒ EISPOREUESTHAI… HOS D' AN LĒPHTHĒ HEAUTŌI AITIOS ESTAI DIA TO EXAKOLOUTHEIN THANATON* — "no foreigner is to enter… whoever is caught will have himself to blame for the death that follows." When Ephesians 2:14 says Christ "has broken down the dividing wall of hostility" (τὸ μεσότοιχον τοῦ φραγμοῦ, *to mesotoichon tou phragmou*), the metaphor lands on a stone any first-century reader could see.

Ephesus itself, the likely first stop on the encyclical's circuit, sat in a multicultural port economy. The political situation under Nero, the most plausible Pauline window (c. 60-62), included a brittle peace between the imperial cult, the Artemis cult, and a Jewish community whose privileges Josephus carefully documents (*Antiquities* 14.225-264, including the right to keep Sabbath, send the temple tax, and be exempted from oath-bound civic duties). The early Christian *ekklēsia* in such a city was demographically mixed: Gentile God-fearers who had attended the synagogue, Diaspora Jews, slaves, freedmen, and a thin layer of well-off patrons (Lydia in Philippi is the type). The "you" of 2:11 ("you Gentiles in the flesh") is sociologically real. Material culture matters here as well: civic association inscriptions across Asia Minor show how communal identity was organized — guilds of silversmiths, *thiasoi* (cult societies), professional collegia — each with admission rites, dues, and patronage networks. Christianity offered an *ekklēsia* (the term itself was a civic word for the citizen assembly) that crossed those divides, which is why the household-of-God language (2:19) and the temple-building language (2:20-22) hit a nerve in Asian urban readers.

Authorship and dating sit where chapter 1 left them. Critical scholars (Mitton, Lincoln, Schnackenburg, Pokorný, MacDonald, Käsemann) read 2:11-22 as *evidence* for post-Pauline composition: the developed ecclesiology — the universal Church as a temple, the apostles already a "foundation" (2:20) suggesting backward-looking veneration — feels more late-first-century than mid-first. Conservative defenders (Hoehner, O'Brien, Bruce, Thielman, M. Barth, Arnold) counter that 1 Cor 3:11 already calls Christ a foundation in undisputed Paul, and that the temple imagery is consistent with 1 Cor 3:16-17 and 2 Cor 6:16. A secretarial-Pauline middle position (Tychicus, Luke, or another Pauline associate writing under apostolic supervision) keeps surfacing as the most economical explanation of both stylistic difference and theological continuity. The textual witnesses — P46 (c. 200), Sinaiticus א, Vaticanus B (Ephesians is fully present in B, unlike the Pastorals), Alexandrinus A — give us a stable Greek text for chapter 2 with only minor variants; the contested address at 1:1 does not affect this chapter's substance.

Five Greek key terms are load-bearing: χάρις (*charis*, "grace"), the Pauline word that does in 2:5, 8 the work of "covenant loyalty" plus "gift" plus "favor"; πίστις (*pistis*, "faith/faithfulness"), whose ambiguity between human trust and divine faithfulness Apocalyptic-Paul scholars exploit; ποίημα (*poiēma*, "workmanship, made thing") in 2:10, a striking word that surfaces in Hellenistic poetry; μεσότοιχον (*mesotoichon*, "dividing wall"), a New Testament hapax legomenon; ἀκρογωνιαῖος (*akrogōniaios*, "cornerstone" or, debated, "capstone") in 2:20, with deep roots in Isa 28:16 LXX and Ps 118:22 LXX; and καινὸς ἄνθρωπος (*kainos anthrōpos*, "new humanity, new man") in 2:15, where *kainos* connotes qualitative not merely chronological newness.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 2 has two long Greek sentences that mirror each other. Verses 1-7 form one period: "And you were dead… but God, being rich in mercy… made us alive… and raised us up… and seated us…" — the structure stretches from death to enthronement in a single grammatical breath, with the *Theos de plousios ōn en eleei* ("but God, being rich in mercy") of 2:4 as the load-bearing pivot. Verses 11-22 form a second extended argument: a *therefore-remember* construction (vv. 11-12) followed by a *but-now* declaration (v. 13) and the *autos gar estin hē eirēnē hēmōn* ("for he himself is our peace") in v. 14 — the language of Mic 5:5 LXX — which then spills into the building metaphor of vv. 19-22. The genre stays epideictic, hymnic, and may incorporate pre-existing liturgical material. Some scholars (Käsemann, Wengst) argue that 2:14-18 is a redacted Christ-hymn comparable to Phil 2:6-11 and Col 1:15-20; others (O'Brien) see prose carefully shaped to hymnic cadences without an underlying source. Either way, the rhetorical effect is liturgical.

Literary devices include the "once / now" antithesis that structures both halves (*pote* in 2:2, 11, 13; *nyni de* in 2:13, 19). The repeated *en Christō / en autō / en hō* — eight times in the chapter — performs the doctrine grammatically: every saving act happens "in him." Heaped genitives recur ("the prince of the power of the air, the spirit now at work in the sons of disobedience," 2:2, with five genitive-construct chains in one verse). The temple-building metaphor in 2:19-22 layers participles (*synarmologoumenē*, "being joined together"; *synoikodomeisthe*, "being built together") to picture an organic, ongoing construction.

Locating chapter 2 in the book's argument: chapter 1 announced what God has done in Christ; chapter 2 narrates what that means for the readers — first individually (vv. 1-10, the death-to-life movement) and then corporately (vv. 11-22, the Jew-Gentile reconciliation movement). Chapter 3 will then explain Paul's place in stewarding this *mystērion*, and chapters 4-6 will draw the imperative consequences. Variant-wise, 2:5 ("by grace you have been saved" as a parenthetical) is sometimes bracketed; some MSS read "your trespasses" instead of "the trespasses" in 2:1 — neither change is meaning-bearing.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Canonically, Ephesians 2 is the New Testament's clearest meditation on the Jew-Gentile question after Romans 9-11. Israel's prior covenants (the Abrahamic, Mosaic, Davidic — "the covenants of promise" of 2:12) are not erased; Gentiles, formerly "alienated from the commonwealth of Israel," are brought near. The temple imagery extends Old Testament temple theology (Solomon's prayer at the dedication in 1 Kings 8 already imagined Gentile prayer in the temple; Isa 56:7 promised "my house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples"). The cornerstone language draws on Isa 28:16 and Ps 118:22, which Jesus had already applied to himself (Mark 12:10 par.).

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic readings of 2:8-9 generally took the verse as anti-Pelagian fuel before Pelagius existed: Origen (in fragments) emphasized the priority of grace; Chrysostom's homilies on the chapter underline that *charis* removes any boasting basis. Augustine's anti-Pelagian writings (*De gratia et libero arbitrio*; *De natura et gratia*; the late *De praedestinatione sanctorum*) made 2:8-10 a primary text against the proposition that grace responds to merit. Aquinas's commentary (*Super Epistolam ad Ephesios* II) carefully distinguished the gift of justifying grace from the works that follow, anticipating a distinction Trent would formalize. Bernard of Clairvaux read 2:14-18 through a peace-mysticism lens. Marius Victorinus's Latin commentary, Theodore of Mopsuestia's Antiochene reading, and Jerome's commentary all preserved the basic Augustinian-trajectory reading of 2:8-10 while disagreeing on the mechanics.

The Reformation made this chapter into a hinge. Luther's discovery of the *iustitia Dei* as gift in his 1515 Romans lectures (and the famous 1519 *Tower Experience* recollection) drew on Ephesians 2:8-9 alongside Romans 1:17 and 3:21-26. The 1522 Preface to Romans canonized *sola fide* hermeneutically. Calvin's commentary on Ephesians (1548) is, with his Romans, the most rigorous Reformed reading. The Council of Trent's Session 6 (*Decretum de iustificatione*, 1547) responded with a careful structure: justification is by grace (Canon 1), unmerited (Canon 1-3), but living faith works through love (Canon 24, citing Gal 5:6) and is genuinely *increased* by good works (Canon 32). The 1999 *Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification* between the Lutheran World Federation and the Catholic Church, joined later by the World Methodist Council (2006), the World Communion of Reformed Churches (2017), and the Anglican Communion (2017), formally claimed sufficient consensus on what 2:8-10 means for the legitimacy of the historic anathemas to be lifted in their original force.

The New Perspective on Paul reframed the question. Krister Stendahl's 1963 essay "Paul and the Introspective Conscience of the West" argued the Lutheran reading projected medieval Western anxieties onto Paul. E.P. Sanders's 1977 *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* recovered Second Temple Judaism as covenantal nomism — election by grace, response by Torah-keeping — which means Paul is not opposing legalism but boundary-marking. James Dunn's 1983 essay "The New Perspective on Paul" coined the label and read "works of the law" as ethnic covenant markers (circumcision, food laws, Sabbath). N.T. Wright's *What Saint Paul Really Said* (1997) and *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* (2013) push the corporate-covenantal reading further. Apocalyptic Paul — J.L. Martyn's *Galatians* in the Anchor Bible (1997), Beverly Gaventa, Susan Eastman, Douglas Campbell's *The Deliverance of God* (2009) — reads Paul's gospel as cosmic invasion: God acts to liberate slaves of the powers, and "faith" is best read as the *pistis Christou* (faithfulness *of* Christ) genitive. John Barclay's *Paul and the Gift* (2015) cuts through both readings by mapping six "perfections" of grace and arguing that Paul (and Ephesians) emphasizes *incongruity* — grace given to the unworthy — without committing to every other Reformed perfection. Stephen Fowl's theological commentary on Ephesians, Markus Bockmuehl's work on revelation, and Frank Thielman's exegetical commentary all sit in this contemporary mix.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** Jewish reception of Ephesians 2:11-22 has historically been the sharpest critique of any Pauline passage. The "abolishing the law of commandments expressed in ordinances" of 2:15, read at maximum strength, has fed centuries of supersessionist Christian readings — the Church as the new Israel, replacing the old. Augustine's *Tractates on John*, Justin's *Dialogue with Trypho*, Melito of Sardis's *Peri Pascha*, the medieval adversus-Iudaeos tradition, and on into the disputations of Paris (1240) and Barcelona (1263) all read in this trajectory. Daniel Boyarin (*A Radical Jew*, 1994) reread Paul as a Jewish thinker pressing universalism but in a way that risks erasing Israel's particularity. Mark Nanos (*The Mystery of Romans*, 1996) and Pamela Eisenbaum (*Paul Was Not a Christian*, 2009) argue Paul never abandoned Judaism, addressing his letters to Gentile auditors. Paula Fredriksen's *Paul: The Pagans' Apostle* (2017) reads "one new humanity" as the eschatological inclusion of Gentiles into the worship of Israel's God without erasing Israel.

Post-Holocaust Jewish-Christian dialogue has reshaped the field. *Nostra Aetate* §4 (Vatican II, 1965) explicitly repudiated collective Jewish guilt for the death of Jesus and affirmed the abiding election of Israel (citing Rom 9-11). Franz Rosenzweig's *Star of Redemption* (1921) had earlier proposed a two-covenant model in which Jews and Christians take parallel paths to the same God. R. Kendall Soulen's *The God of Israel and Christian Theology* (1996) named "structural supersessionism" as the deeper problem and argued for a recovery of Israel's continuing role. Mark Kinzer's *Postmissionary Messianic Judaism* (2005) defended a continuing distinct Jewish identity within the body of Christ. Michael Wyschogrod's *The Body of Faith* developed the carnal election of Israel as an ongoing reality. Where contemporary Jewish scholarship still pushes back is on the rhetorical totality of "one new man in place of the two": does this language eliminate Jewish-Gentile distinction, or does it create a peace that preserves them? The answer matters because the Belhar Confession (1986), drafted by the Dutch Reformed Mission Church under Allan Boesak in response to apartheid theology built on Reformed ethnic-particularist exegesis (the *Drie Formuliere van Enigheid* applied as theological scaffolding for racial separation), grounded itself precisely in Ephesians 2:11-22's anti-ethnic-supremacy logic.

**Where this leaves us.** Most contemporary Christian readers, post-Holocaust, post-NPP, post-Belhar, read 2:11-22 as eschatological inclusion that does not erase Israel's particularity (cf. Rom 9-11). The dialogue is no longer a dispute between caricatures.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What is the relationship between 2:8-9 and 2:10?** The Tridentine Catholic reading (Trent Session 6) holds that justification is by grace through living faith that works through love, and that genuine merit follows justification (a *meritum de condigno* possible only because of prior grace). Lutheran sola-fide insists that 2:8-9 is the gospel-key: faith alone justifies, works follow as fruit but contribute nothing to the *iustitia coram Deo*; the formula *simul iustus et peccator* preserves this. Reformed theology agrees with Lutherans on sola fide but adds covenant-of-redemption framing (Westminster Larger Catechism Q. 30-31). Wesleyan-Arminian theology accepts justification by faith alone but presses sanctification as the genuine *transformation* without which the eschatological inheritance is forfeit (Wesley's sermons "On Working Out Our Own Salvation" and "The Witness of the Spirit"). Eastern Orthodox theology reads 2:8-10 within the synergy of *theōsis*: grace initiates, the human response is genuine, and *energeia* (divine-human cooperation) leads to deification. The New Perspective reads 2:8-9 not against legalism in general but against ethnic boundary-marking: "no one may boast" of Jewish covenantal privilege. Apocalyptic Paul (Martyn, Campbell) reads it as anti-anthropological-religion as such — God acts cosmically, and human "faith" is the participation in Christ's faithfulness that the act produces.

The position I find least intuitive is the Apocalyptic-Paul reading, so let me steelman it. Martyn's argument is that Paul never uses *pistis* as a generic religious quality; the genitive constructions (*pistis Iēsou Christou*) and the verbal forms throughout Galatians and Romans treat faith as Christ's own faithfulness in which believers participate. Read this way, 2:8 says: "by grace you have been saved through *Christ's faithfulness* — and this (the whole event) is not from you; it is the gift of God." This dissolves the appearance of a transactional structure and makes the salvation entirely God's act, with human "faith" describing participation rather than condition. The strength is theological: grace remains incongruous and effective through and through. The weakness is grammatical: in Eph 2:8 *dia pisteōs* lacks a genitive object, and the natural Greek reading is human faith. **Where this leaves us:** the text gives genuine priority to grace (vv. 4-7), denies any merit-based ground (v. 9), and orients the saved life toward good works that God himself prepared (v. 10) — a structure all the major positions can read in their own categories without forcing the text.

**Tension 2: Has Israel been absorbed, or does "one new humanity" preserve Jewish-Gentile distinction within peace?** The traditional supersessionist reading (Augustine on John; the medieval *adversus Iudaeos* tradition) held that 2:15's "abolishing the law of commandments" abrogates Israel's covenant; the Church is the new Israel. The two-covenant reading (Rosenzweig; some post-*Nostra Aetate* Catholics) holds that Israel and the Church walk parallel paths to the same God. The Pauline-Jewish-restoration reading (Wright, Fredriksen, Eisenbaum, Nanos) holds that Christ fulfills Israel's vocation and Gentiles are grafted in without erasing the difference (Rom 11:17-24). The Messianic-Jewish reading (Mark Kinzer's *Postmissionary Messianic Judaism*; Boaz Michael's *Tent of David*) holds that Jewish identity in Christ remains a distinct calling within the *ekklēsia*. Catholic teaching since *Nostra Aetate* §4 and the 2015 Pontifical Commission document *The Gifts and the Calling of God Are Irrevocable* affirms Israel's abiding election and rejects organized Catholic mission to Jews specifically.

The position I find least intuitive is Kinzer's postmissionary Messianic Judaism, so let me steelman it. Kinzer argues that if Israel's election remains (Rom 11:29), and if 2:15's "abolishing" is best read as abolishing the *function* of the law as a wall (not its goodness as Torah for Jewish life), then Jewish believers in Yeshua should retain Jewish identity and practice as part of how the *kainos anthrōpos* concretely exists. The "one new humanity" is not ethnic erasure but the eschatological space in which Jew and Gentile, retaining their callings, share table fellowship in the Messiah. This reading takes Romans 11 with full seriousness, takes the Acts 21:20-26 picture of Jewish believers continuing temple observance with full seriousness, and refuses to let Paul's anti-boundary-marking polemic become an anti-Jewish-particularity polemic. **Where this leaves us:** the rhetorical totality of "one new man" is real, but it is a christological peace that creates room for difference rather than erasing it; the Belhar Confession's anti-apartheid use of this passage and Kinzer's pro-Jewish-particularity use are not in contradiction but together name the same logic.

**Unresolved question:** If the *mesotoichon* between Jew and Gentile has been broken, why does the Christian Church still spend most of its energy building new walls — and what would it cost a contemporary congregation to actually let the cross do to its hostilities what the text says it did to that one?

## The Thread

- **Isaiah 57:19** — "peace, peace, to the far and to the near" sits behind 2:13 and 2:17 directly; the LXX vocabulary is what Paul reaches for. This is non-obvious because most readers hear 2:17 as Pauline summary and miss that he is quoting Isaiah's Servant-of-the-Lord vocabulary.
- **1 Kings 8:41-43** — Solomon's prayer at the temple dedication explicitly imagines the foreigner who comes to pray; Eph 2:19-22 fulfills this in reverse, building Gentiles *into* the temple itself.
- **Acts 21:27-29** — the riot that puts Paul in Roman custody is triggered by the false rumor that he had brought Trophimus (a Gentile from Ephesus, of all places) past the *soreg*. The chapter's wall-imagery is autobiographical. This is the most important non-obvious cross-reference.
- **Revelation 21:22-26** (later in reading order) — "I saw no temple in the city, for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb… and the kings of the earth will bring their glory into it" completes Eph 2:19-22's trajectory: the temple-being-built becomes, eschatologically, the city itself.
