# 2 Chronicles 35 — The Great Passover and the Death at Megiddo

## Lens 1 — The Historical World Behind the Text

Second Chronicles 35 narrates two of the most consequential moments of the late seventh century BC: Josiah's celebration of Passover in 622 BC — described as the greatest since the days of Samuel (35:18) — and Josiah's death at Megiddo in 609 BC at the hands of Pharaoh Neco II of Egypt. The chapter parallels 2 Kings 23:21-30 but with distinctive Chronicler expansions and reframings. Where Kings devotes three verses to the Passover (2 Kgs 23:21-23) and four verses to Josiah's death (2 Kgs 23:28-30), the Chronicler devotes nineteen verses to the Passover (35:1-19) and six verses to Josiah's death (35:20-25). The proportions reveal the Chronicler's interest: cult-renewal expanded, royal-death given just enough space to make its theological point.

The Passover celebration is dated specifically to "the eighteenth year of the reign of Josiah" (35:19) — the same year as the discovery of the law book in chapter 34. The Chronicler is collapsing the temple repair, book discovery, Huldah oracle, covenant renewal, and Passover into a single year of intense reform — 622 BC. This timeline tracks with Kings (which gives the same date, 2 Kgs 23:23) and is broadly accepted by historians.

The historical-political context is critical. By 622 BC, the Assyrian Empire was visibly declining. Ashurbanipal (669-631 BC) had been the last great Assyrian king; under his successors Ashur-etil-ilani and Sin-shar-ishkun, Assyrian control over the western provinces was collapsing. The Medes under Cyaxares and the Babylonians under Nabopolassar were squeezing Assyria from the east. By 612 BC, Nineveh would fall; by 609 BC, the last Assyrian remnant under Ashur-uballit II would be cornered at Harran. Josiah's reform — purging Assyrian-imposed astral cults, asserting Judahite religious independence — was politically possible because Assyria could no longer enforce its policies in the west. The 622 BC Passover, organized to include "all Judah and Israel who were present" (35:18), reaches into territory that for a century had been under Assyrian provincial administration; Josiah is asserting de facto Judahite-Davidic claims over former-northern-kingdom regions.

The death at Megiddo in 609 BC is one of the best-attested events in the chapter from extra-biblical sources. Egyptian and Babylonian records confirm the larger campaign:

The **Babylonian Chronicle** (specifically the *Fall of Nineveh Chronicle*, BM 21901, in the British Museum, published by C.J. Gadd 1923 and re-edited by A.K. Grayson, *Assyrian and Babylonian Chronicles*, 1975) records that in 609 BC Pharaoh Neco II marched north to support the last Assyrian remnant under Ashur-uballit II in the attempt to retake Harran from the Babylonians and Medes. The chronicle does not mention Josiah specifically but does establish that Egyptian forces were moving north along the Levant in that year — the geopolitical setting the Chronicler describes.

**Egyptian sources** confirm Neco II's reign (610-595 BC) and his northern campaigns. Herodotus (II.158-159) mentions Neco's northern wars, including a battle at "Magdolus" — possibly a reference to Megiddo, though the toponym is debated.

The **archaeology of Megiddo** (Tel Megiddo, excavations by Schumacher, the University of Chicago Oriental Institute under Guy and Engberg, then Yadin, then Finkelstein and Ussishkin in the modern era) confirms a major late-seventh-century occupation phase but no specific Josianic destruction layer — Josiah went *to* Megiddo to fight Neco, was wounded there, and was carried back to Jerusalem to die. Megiddo was not destroyed in the encounter; it was the setting of the encounter.

The encounter itself is historically puzzling. Why did Josiah oppose Neco? 2 Kings simply records that Josiah went to meet him and was killed (2 Kgs 23:29). The Chronicler adds a remarkable detail: Neco sent envoys to Josiah saying "*What have we to do with each other, king of Judah? I am not coming against you this day, but against the house with which I am at war. And God has commanded me to hurry. Cease opposing God, who is with me, lest he destroy you*" (35:21). The Chronicler then notes (35:22) that Josiah "did not listen to the words of Neco *from the mouth of God*" (*mi-pi elohim*, מִפִּי אֱלֹהִים).

This is theologically extraordinary. The Chronicler is claiming that *Pharaoh Neco* — a pagan Egyptian king worshiping foreign gods — spoke a true oracle from YHWH, and that Josiah's failure was failing to recognize it. The pattern echoes Cyrus in 2 Chr 36 (the chapter immediately following), where another foreign king is presented as YHWH's instrument. The Chronicler is willing to recognize that YHWH speaks through unexpected mouths — Pharaohs and emperors as well as prophets and priests.

The historical-political question is harder. Why did Josiah oppose Neco's march to support Assyria? Several reconstructions are plausible. (1) **Anti-Assyrian alliance**: Josiah may have aligned with the Babylonians against Assyria, hoping to prevent Egyptian reinforcement. (2) **Independence assertion**: Josiah may have been asserting Judahite control over the strategic Megiddo pass and the Jezreel valley, treating Neco as an intruder regardless of his ultimate target. (3) **Theological motive**: the Chronicler frames it as Josiah failing to recognize a divine oracle, but the underlying historical motive was probably political.

The Chronicler's distinctive theological move is unique to him: Josiah, the most faithful king since David, dies young in battle because he failed to listen to the word of God *from a Pharaoh's mouth*. Huldah had promised Josiah he would be "gathered to your grave in peace, and your eyes shall not see all the disaster that I will bring upon this place" (34:28). He was indeed buried before Babylon's destruction of Jerusalem in 587 BC — Huldah's promise about the disaster held — but he died in battle, not in his bed, and the *be-shalom* (in peace) of Huldah's oracle is in significant tension with the arrows at Megiddo.

Sara Japhet (*I & II Chronicles*) reads the Chronicler's framing as theological smoothing: by introducing the Neco-as-divine-mouthpiece detail, the Chronicler can attribute Josiah's death not to a flaw in YHWH's promise (which Huldah delivered) but to a *specific act of disobedience* by Josiah in this one episode. The immediate-retribution theology requires an explanation; the Chronicler provides one. H.G.M. Williamson agrees, noting that the Chronicler is consistently uneasy with cases where faithful kings suffer or die badly, and consistently finds an explanation. Ralph Klein notes that Josiah's *disguising himself* (35:22, *vayithanchaph*, וַיִּתְחַפֵּשׂ) echoes Ahab's disguise before death at Ramoth-Gilead (1 Kgs 22:30, 2 Chr 18:29) — an unflattering parallel that subtly indicts Josiah's last act.

The figure of **Jeremiah** appears at 35:25, lamenting for Josiah. This is one of the few direct intersections between Chronicles and the Jeremiah tradition. Jeremiah began his prophetic ministry in Josiah's thirteenth year (Jer 1:2 — 627 BC), so Jeremiah was active for fifteen years before Josiah's death. The Chronicler refers to "the Laments" (*ha-qinot*, הַקִּינוֹת, 35:25) — a collection of lament-poems for Josiah, distinct from the canonical book of Lamentations (which laments the fall of Jerusalem in 587 BC). The book of Lamentations was traditionally ascribed to Jeremiah but its subject is the destruction of Jerusalem, not Josiah's death; the *qinot* of 35:25 are a separate, lost collection. The historical reality of post-Josiah lamentation is corroborated by Zechariah 12:11, which speaks of "the mourning for Hadad-Rimmon in the plain of Megiddo" — possibly a long-remembered Israelite mourning tradition tied to Josiah's death.

Megiddo itself becomes a place of theological resonance. The Hebrew *Har Megiddo* (הַר מְגִדּוֹ, "Mount of Megiddo") becomes, in Greek, *Harmagedōn* — *Armageddon*. Revelation 16:16 names the place of final eschatological battle as *Harmagedōn*. The connection between Josiah's death at Megiddo and the apocalyptic *Armageddon* tradition is direct: Megiddo was the place of national tragedy in Israelite memory, and the apocalyptic imagination read the place of historical catastrophe as the place of eschatological climax.

## Lens 2 — The Literature of the Text

The chapter divides into two clearly demarcated sections: the great Passover (35:1-19) and the death at Megiddo (35:20-27).

The Passover section is the most detailed cultic narrative in Chronicles, exceeding even the great Passover of Hezekiah (2 Chr 30). The Chronicler organizes it into administrative-cultic stages: Josiah's instructions to the Levites (35:2-6), the contributions of livestock (35:7-9), the actual sacrifice and distribution (35:10-15), the summary statement of unparalleled excellence (35:16-19).

The Levitical instructions (35:2-6) are theologically loaded. Josiah tells the Levites to *put the holy ark in the house that Solomon built* (35:3) — a remarkable instruction that has puzzled commentators. Why did the ark need to be put back? Some scholars (Williamson, Klein) suggest the ark had been removed during Manasseh's apostasy and was now being restored. Others (Japhet) suggest Josiah is reorganizing Levitical duties: under the older mobile-tabernacle structure, the Levites carried the ark; in the temple, they no longer needed to do so, and Josiah is releasing them from that duty so they can serve in other roles. The phrase *lo-lachem masa ba-katef* (לֹא־לָכֶם מַשָּׂא בַּכָּתֵף, "no burden on the shoulder for you," 35:3) — the Levites no longer carry the ark — frees them for the cultic-musical-administrative roles the Chronicler emphasizes.

The numerical scale of the Passover offerings is staggering. Josiah contributes 30,000 lambs and 3,000 bulls from the royal flocks (35:7); his officials add 2,600 lambs and 300 bulls (35:8); the Levite chiefs add 5,000 lambs and 500 bulls (35:9). The total — over 37,000 sacrificial animals in a single Passover — far exceeds the Hezekian Passover (2 Chr 30:24, 1,000 bulls and 7,000 sheep from Hezekiah; 1,000 bulls and 10,000 sheep from the princes). The numbers may be Chronistic exaggeration, but they may also reflect the reality of a much-expanded population participating: the Chronicler emphasizes that representatives from the former northern territories are present (35:18, "all Israel who were present").

The Chronicler's signature *kasher* (כָּשֵׁר) language appears throughout — *vayikkonu* (וַיִּכּוֹנוּ, "they prepared," 35:10), *uvashlu* (וּבַשְּׁלוּ, "they boiled," 35:13). The Hebrew phrase for "proper Passover" is implied throughout: this is *Pesach kasher*, properly observed according to the *katuv* (כָּתוּב, "what is written") of the Book of Moses (35:12). The Chronicler is showing his post-exilic readers what proper Passover looks like: priestly-and-Levitical hierarchy, public assembly, written-Torah authority, and abundant joyful sacrifice.

The summary statement is the literary climax of the Passover section. *Lo-na'asah pesach kamohu be-yisra'el mimei shemu'el ha-navi* (לֹא־נַעֲשָׂה פֶסַח כָּמֹהוּ בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל מִימֵי שְׁמוּאֵל הַנָּבִיא, "no Passover like it had been kept in Israel since the days of Samuel the prophet," 35:18). The reference to Samuel is intriguing — Samuel is presented in 1 Samuel as judge and prophet, not as Passover-organizer. The Chronicler is reaching back past the entire monarchic period to a pre-monarchic era when Israel was unified under prophet-judge leadership. The implication is that *no Davidic king before Josiah*, including Solomon, including Hezekiah (2 Chr 30) — kept a Passover like this. It is the highest superlative the Chronicler can offer.

The transition to the Megiddo episode is sharp: *acharei kol-zot asher hekhin Yoshiyahu et-ha-bayit* (אַחֲרֵי כָל־זֹאת אֲשֶׁר הֵכִין יֹאשִׁיָּהוּ אֶת־הַבָּיִת, "after all this, when Josiah had prepared the temple," 35:20). The Chronicler then narrates Neco's march, the envoy's oracle, Josiah's refusal, Josiah's disguise, Josiah's wounding, and Josiah's death. The whole episode takes only six verses; the speed is itself theologically expressive — the death of the most faithful king is brief, almost unceremonious.

The closing note about Jeremiah's lament (35:25) is the Chronicler's gesture of historical respect. Jeremiah is a major prophetic figure; his book is in the canon; his lament for Josiah is canonized in some now-lost collection. The Chronicler is signaling that Josiah's death was a national trauma, properly mourned. The phrase *vayittenum le-choq be-yisra'el* (וַיִּתְּנוּם לְחֹק בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל, "they made these a rule in Israel," 35:25) suggests an annual mourning observance for Josiah's death — possibly the historical kernel behind the Zech 12:11 "mourning for Hadad-Rimmon in the plain of Megiddo."

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Josiah's Passover and death have had layered afterlives.

The **Jewish trajectory** preserves Josiah's Passover as the high point of pre-exilic cultic faithfulness. The rabbinic tradition treats Josiah as a model king (*Ta'anit* 22b — the rabbis include Josiah among the kings who repented appropriately). The annual Passover Seder, in some traditions, contains echoes of Josianic-period centralization (the requirement that Passover be eaten in Jerusalem — a Deuteronomic and Josianic emphasis — survives in the diaspora as the spiritual orientation toward Jerusalem during Seder).

Josiah's death has been read in the Jewish tradition as a deeply tragic moment but not as a theological scandal. The rabbis (in *Ta'anit* 22b and elsewhere) read Josiah's death as resulting from his failure to listen to Jeremiah and Neco — both of whom (in the rabbinic reading) warned him not to oppose Egypt. The Chronicler's framing has shaped this rabbinic reading.

The **Megiddo / Armageddon trajectory** runs through both Jewish and Christian apocalyptic literature. Zechariah 12:10-11 (post-exilic, late sixth or fifth century BC) speaks of mourning "for him whom they have pierced … as the mourning for Hadad-Rimmon in the plain of Megiddo." Christian tradition (since John the Revelator) has read this Zechariah passage Christologically — the pierced one is Christ — but the historical referent of the *Megiddo* mourning is almost certainly Josiah's death. Revelation 16:16 then geographically locates the climactic eschatological battle at *Harmagedōn* / *Har Megiddo*, drawing on the deep Israelite cultural memory of Megiddo as the place of national catastrophe. Modern dispensationalist eschatology has elaborated *Armageddon* into specific scenarios (Israeli-Arab wars, Russian-American clashes, etc.), but the textual root in Revelation 16:16 is brief and the geographical-symbolic logic runs through Josiah's death.

The **Christian trajectory** has read Josiah's reform Christologically and ecclesiologically. Origen, Augustine, Bede all treat Josiah as a type of Christ — the king who purifies the temple, recovers the law, leads covenant renewal. Calvin's commentary on 2 Chr 35 reads Josiah's Passover as a type of the proper celebration of the Lord's Supper: properly ordered, liturgically full, abundant. The Reformation's emphasis on weekly communion (Calvin's preference, opposed by other Reformers) drew on this kind of typology.

Josiah's death has been read more difficultly. Augustine (*City of God* 17.5) treats it as the necessary consequence of disobedience even by the righteous, illustrating that no human is perfect. Calvin treats it similarly. Socially-oriented modern readers (Walter Brueggemann, Norman Gottwald) read Josiah's death as the moment when the Davidic-monarchic project failed under the weight of imperial geopolitics — even the most faithful king cannot hold history together when the empires are moving.

The **Jeremiah-lament tradition** has resonated through Jewish and Christian liturgical mourning. Jeremiah's lost lament for Josiah was likely a model for the canonical book of Lamentations (which laments Jerusalem's destruction in 587 BC, half a generation after Josiah's death). The annual reading of Lamentations on *Tisha B'Av* (the ninth of Av), observing the destructions of both temples, draws on the broader Israelite tradition of public lament — the very tradition the Chronicler invokes at 35:25.

The **NT** does not cite 2 Chr 35 directly. Christ's Passover at the Last Supper occurs under Roman occupation in a Jerusalem stripped of Davidic kingship; the contrast with Josiah's Passover — Davidic king, full temple, all Israel — is striking and possibly intentional in the synoptic framing. Whether Mark or Luke specifically had 2 Chr 35 in mind is unprovable, but the structural resonance is real.

## Lens 4 — The Chronicler's Theological Intent

The agenda: *the great Passover is the proper end of reform; the death at Megiddo is the consequence of one specific act of disobedience; the Chronicler's immediate-retribution theology holds even for the most faithful king, but it operates with discriminating precision — Josiah's overall reign is faithful and rewarded, his death is the consequence of his last failure, his promised peace at the grave is preserved by the timing of his death (before Babylon's destruction).*

This is a sophisticated theological move. The Chronicler is not abandoning his retribution framework; he is refining it. Josiah lived faithfully and was rewarded with the greatest reform and Passover in Israel's history. He died because of one specific failure — refusing the divine oracle that came through Pharaoh Neco's mouth. His death was tragic but not catastrophic; he was buried before Babylon came; Huldah's promise held in the most important sense.

**Tension 1: the Josiah-death problem.** Huldah promised peace at the grave; Josiah died with arrows in him at Megiddo. How does the Chronicler resolve this?

The Chronicler's solution (the Neco-as-divine-mouthpiece detail) is theologically creative but raises its own questions. The mainstream-critical position (Williamson, Japhet, Klein) reads this as the Chronicler's attempt to save his immediate-retribution theology by introducing a specific disobedience as the explanation for Josiah's bad death. The reading is plausible but uncomfortable: it suggests that the Chronicler is somewhat *forcing* his framework onto a tradition that resists it.

The conservative position (Daniel Block, Andrew Hill) accepts the Chronicler's framing at face value: YHWH does sometimes speak through unexpected channels, including foreign rulers; Josiah's failure to recognize the divine warning is a real failure; the consequence is his death. This is consistent with the broader biblical pattern (Cyrus, Nebuchadnezzar as YHWH's "servant," the Assyrians as YHWH's "rod").

The honest engagement is that both readings have force. The Chronicler is doing real theological work; whether he is *forcing* a framework or *recognizing* a real pattern is a judgment call. What is clear is that he refuses to let Josiah's tragic death undermine the Davidic-faithful pattern; he insists, with whatever rhetorical pressure is required, that the pattern holds.

**Tension 2: Pharaoh as YHWH's mouthpiece.** Can a foreign king genuinely speak God's word?

The Chronicler answers yes. In 2 Chr 35 it is Neco; in 2 Chr 36 it will be Cyrus. This is theologically bold. Most ancient Near Eastern theologies confined divine speech to native priests, prophets, and kings. The Chronicler's willingness to recognize divine voice in foreign mouths is part of his post-exilic theological sophistication: living under Persian rule, his community must reckon with the possibility that the gentile world is also under YHWH's sovereignty and can sometimes serve as YHWH's instrument.

Christian theology has generally affirmed this pattern (general revelation, common grace, Romans 1-2's recognition that God speaks through creation and conscience to those without the special revelation of Israel). The Chronicler's anticipation of this trajectory is striking.

**Tension 3: Megiddo as place of trauma and apocalyptic.** The historical Megiddo of 609 BC becomes the *Armageddon* of Revelation 16:16 — the symbolic place of final battle. Modern dispensational eschatology has elaborated this into specific geopolitical scenarios; mainstream eschatology treats it more symbolically.

The honest engagement is that the connection between Josiah's death and Revelation's *Armageddon* runs through the Israelite cultural memory of Megiddo as the place of national catastrophe. Whether one reads Revelation 16:16 dispensationally or symbolically, the textual root is in places like 2 Chr 35:22 and Zech 12:11 — Megiddo as the place where the most faithful king fell. The eschatological imagination then projected that historical wound onto the cosmic future battle.

**Where this leaves us:** Josiah's chapter teaches us that even the greatest reform is not final. The Passover was unparalleled; the king was the most faithful; the people were gathered; the law was read. And then arrows. The Chronicler is honest enough to record the death and theological enough to find a frame for it. He does not let the death undo the reform, and he does not let the reform pretend the death did not happen.

**Closing question:** What does it mean that the most faithful king of Judah died for failing to recognize God's voice in an unexpected mouthpiece? Where, in your life, might God be speaking from a Pharaoh — a source you have presumed could not be carrying His word?

## The Thread

Chapter 35 is the last great moment of Davidic faithfulness in Chronicles. The Passover is unparalleled; the death is unforeseen; the lament is bitter. What follows in chapter 36 is descent: four kings in twenty-two years, each worse than the last, until Babylon comes and the temple burns. The Chronicler will compress the descent dramatically — and then close his book, and the entire Hebrew Bible, with a Persian king's decree that opens the door to return.
