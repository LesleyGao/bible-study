# Joshua 6: The Liturgical Siege

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jericho (Tell es-Sultan, Arabic *Tall al-Sulṭān*) sits at the lowest point of the Jordan valley, 258 meters below sea level, around the spring of ʿAin es-Sultan, the water source that has made the mound continuously habitable since the Pre-Pottery Neolithic. It is one of the oldest excavated settlements on earth: Kathleen Kenyon's stratigraphy (1952-58) identified twenty-three occupation levels from PPNA (c. 9000 BCE) through Iron Age. For Joshua 6, the relevant strata are MB IIC (mid-17th to mid-16th c. BCE) and LB I-II (c. 1550-1200 BCE).

The archaeology is the crux. The chapter describes a fortified city with walls that collapse catastrophically; the excavation tells a different story. John Garstang's 1930-36 dig reported a fallen "City IV" wall he dated to c. 1400 BCE, matching the high biblical date and making headlines. Kenyon's stratigraphic work overturned this: the collapsed Middle Bronze wall Garstang saw belonged to City IV, which ended around 1550 BCE (destroyed, she argued, by the Hyksos expulsion or Egyptian campaigns). Kenyon's LB levels were sparse — some pottery, a small structure she called the "Middle Building," and a single tomb. Her conclusion: Jericho was small and largely unwalled through the Late Bronze Age, and by c. 1400-1200 BCE there was essentially no city to destroy.

Bryant Wood's 1990 *Biblical Archaeology Review* article reopened the case, redating Kenyon's City IV pottery (on the basis of local imitation Cypriot bichrome ware and absence of certain Mycenaean imports) to c. 1400 BCE and arguing for a conquest-compatible destruction. Piotr Bienkowski's immediate rebuttal (*BAR* 1990) defended Kenyon: Wood's ceramic revisions were methodologically unsound, and the radiocarbon dating of charred grain from the destruction (Bruins and van der Plicht, *Radiocarbon* 1995; further work in *Nature* 2000) clustered around 1617-1530 BCE. Mainstream archaeology has sided with Bienkowski. Amihai Mazar, Israel Finkelstein, and William Dever all treat the MB destruction as the only major event at Jericho in the relevant era and see the Joshua 6 narrative as reflecting either dim memory of much earlier ruins or a literary-theological construction.

Authorship sits within the Deuteronomistic History (Noth 1943; Cross 1973 Dtr1/Dtr2). The *ḥērem* terminology and the Ark-centered cultic procession point to Priestly and Dtr hands working on older material. Dozeman (Yale Anchor Bible, 2015) argues that chapter 6 preserves a pre-Dtr cult-liturgical core (the seven-day ritual) with Dtr frame and P-influenced ban terminology. The Rahab narrative (6:22-25, 17b, 25) is a secondary insertion by most source-critical readings, its seams visible in the awkward re-narration.

Material culture: the *šôpār* (rams' horn, יוֹבֵל and שׁוֹפָר both appear in 6:4-5) is a signaling instrument, not a wind instrument in the modern sense — tonally limited but acoustically penetrating; used throughout the ANE for military and cultic signals. The Ark (*ʾărôn habbĕrît*) is portable, palanquin-carried, a typical ANE divine throne/chest; parallels include Egyptian portable barques of Amun and Hittite divine standards (Othmar Keel's iconography work). The "treasury of the house of YHWH" (6:19, 24) is an anachronism for the pre-Temple period that many take as Dtr redaction (C. F. Burney, George Adam Smith).

Key terms:

- ***ḥērem*** (חֵרֶם, 6:17-18, 21), "devoted to destruction" / "ban." Not simply extermination; the semantic field is devotion/consecration taken to an absolute limit — the thing is given to God by being removed permanently from human use. Cognate with Arabic *ḥarām* (forbidden/sacred) and *ḥarīm* (sacred enclosure). Philip Stern (*The Biblical Herem*, 1991) and Walter Moberly (*Old Testament Theology*, 2013) have worked the semantics; Jeffrey Tigay distinguishes annihilation-herem from dedicatory-herem. Mesha Stele (c. 840 BCE, Louvre AO 5066, line 17) uses the identical root: Mesha devotes (*ḥrm*) Nebo to Ashtar-Chemosh. The vocabulary is not uniquely Israelite; the theological framing is.

- ***šôpār*** / ***yôvēl*** (שׁוֹפָר, יוֹבֵל, 6:4-5), rams' horn. *Yôvēl* doubles as "jubilee" (Lev 25) — the horn that inaugurates release. The chapter's use of the jubilee-horn for siege is theologically pointed in ways English flattens.

- ***ʿākar*** (עָכַר, 6:18), "bring trouble upon." The root connects this chapter directly to chapter 7's Achan (*ʿākār*) and the Valley of Achor (*ʿākôr*). The narrator is flagging what is coming.

- ***gibbôrê heḥāyil*** (גִּבּוֹרֵי הֶחַיִל, 6:2), "mighty men of valor." Standard military term; used ironically since the city's might is about to be irrelevant.

- ***ʾôṣar bêt YHWH*** (אוֹצַר בֵּית־יְהוָה, 6:19, 24), "treasury of YHWH's house." Presupposes a central sanctuary structure — Dtr's vocabulary retrojected.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: ritualized-conquest narrative with cultic-liturgical core, closing in etiological curse (6:26). Lawson Younger's *Ancient Conquest Accounts* (1990) catalogues ANE parallels (Hittite, Assyrian, Egyptian) where sieges are narrated in ritualized sequences; the form is genre-convention, not reportage. The chapter is unusually repetitive even for Hebrew narrative — the sevenfold pattern of seven days, seven priests, seven trumpets, seven circuits is not stylistic; it is the point.

Literary devices:

The number seven saturates the chapter: seven days, seven priests, seven trumpets, seven circuits on the seventh day. Counting verse by verse: *šebaʿ* or forms of it appear in 6:4 (three times), 6:6, 6:8, 6:13, 6:15 (twice), 6:16 — densest cluster of seven-terminology anywhere in Joshua. The seven-day cultic pattern mirrors creation week (Gen 1) and tabernacle construction (Exod 24:15-18) — Moshe Weinfeld and Michael Fishbane both read the siege as creation-by-un-creation: by the same ritual logic that builds world and sanctuary, Jericho is unmade.

Repetition as device: the verbs "encircle/go around" (*sbb/sābab*) and "march" are pounded in verses 3-15 to the point of monotony. Robert Alter notes that the monotony is mimetic — the reader experiences the ritual's duration. The shift at 6:20 from static ritual to catastrophic collapse is structurally climactic; the wall falls in a single clause after a chapter of circling.

Silence as narrative device: 6:10's commanded silence (*lōʾ tārîʿû*) interrupts the trumpet-blowing narration and sets up the contrast with the "great shout" (*tĕrûʿâ gĕdōlâ*, 6:20). The trumpets sound; the people are silent; then the people shout. The ordered sonic architecture matches the temporal architecture.

Inclusio and framing: 6:1 ("Jericho was shut up") and 6:20 ("the wall fell down flat") frame the liturgical core. 6:27 ("the LORD was with Joshua") echoes the book's opening promise (1:5, 9), closing the conquest's first phase.

The Rahab excursus (6:17b, 22-25) is narratively abrupt. Her rescue interrupts the ban-execution; her continuing presence "to this day" (*ʿad hayyôm hazzeh*, 6:25) is an etiological tag. As Brueggemann and L. Daniel Hawk both observe, her narrative placement generates the chapter's most persistent ethical wrench: the ban's absoluteness and its explicit exception are narrated in the same paragraph.

The curse on Jericho (6:26) is in poetic parallelism — "at the cost of his firstborn / shall he lay its foundation / and at the cost of his youngest son / shall he set up its gates." This is realized in 1 Kings 16:34 (Hiel of Bethel), which many critics take as Dtr back-reference constructing the curse's fulfillment.

Textual variants: LXX Joshua 6 runs about 15% shorter than MT, omitting elements of the Rahab material and compressing the ritual instructions. This is one of the places where LXX's shorter form is most pronounced, and where the debate about Vorlage vs abbreviation is sharpest. Emanuel Tov argues LXX preserves the earlier edition; Lawrence Greenspoon and Leonard Greenspoon have qualified this chapter by chapter. 4QJosh^a famously relocates the altar-building of MT 8:30-35 to earlier in the conquest; its implications for chapter 6 are indirect but important — they suggest the conquest sequence was editorially fluid in late Second Temple tradition.

Location in book's argument: Jericho is the first city, the paradigm city, and the ritual city. The chapter's ritual intensity is set against Ai's subsequent narrative ordinariness (chapter 7-8); the contrast is deliberate. Chapter 6 shows conquest as liturgy; chapter 7 shows conquest as ethics.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Canonically, Jericho is the threshold event. Crossing the Jordan opened the land; taking Jericho breaches it. The chapter is referenced later in Psalm 44 (implicitly, as paradigm of YHWH's victory-without-sword), Hebrews 11:30 ("by faith the walls of Jericho fell"), and, in a darker register, the prophetic critiques of Israel's military triumphalism (Amos, Micah).

**Jewish trajectories:**

*Second Temple*: Qumran's 4Q378-379 (Apocryphon of Joshua) includes a curse-expansion on whoever rebuilds Jericho, paralleling 6:26 and tying it to eschatological expectation. 4Q522 mentions Jericho. Ben Sira 46:6 praises Joshua's memorial-stone achievements but doesn't dwell on Jericho. Philo (*On Dreams* 2.170) allegorizes Jericho as the lunar/earthly life that falls before divine splendor — a standard Hellenistic allegorization. Josephus (*Ant.* 5.22-30) retells the siege emphasizing divine causation and downplays the ban's brutality.

*Rabbinic*: Sanhedrin 44a pairs Jericho and Achan as the paradigm of cherem-violation. Megillah 14b and Zevachim 116b identify Rahab as a proselyte and trace prophetic descent from her — eight prophets including Jeremiah. Mishnah Rosh Hashanah 3:2 links the shofar of Jericho to the shofar of jubilee and Rosh Hashanah. Halakhic discussion of cherem (Arakhin 28b-29a) distinguishes persons, animals, and property; most authorities concluded the biblical cherem no longer applies since the Canaanite nations can no longer be identified (Sennacherib argument, Berakhot 28a).

*Medieval*: Rashi on 6:17 restricts the ban to this specific divine command, not a general permission. Radak distributes agency: YHWH grants, Israel executes. Ramban treats the circuits as genuine theurgy — the wall falls as covenantal response. Abarbanel, post-Reconquista, reads the ban's restriction as essential Jewish political theology. Maimonides (*Mishneh Torah*, Melakhim 5-6) codifies cherem warfare as historically bounded.

*Modern*: Cassuto on the ritual character; Uffenheimer on sacral-war; Moshe Greenberg on cherem as a category that demands moral engagement. Milgrom's Anchor Bible Leviticus commentary distinguishes consecrational from exterminatory herem. Levenson's *The Universal Horizon of Biblical Particularism* wrestles without flinching. Shaye Cohen on the Rahab conversion traditions. Moshe Weinfeld's *The Promise of the Land* (1993) reads the conquest chapters as political-theological construct.

**Christian trajectories:**

*Patristic*: Origen's *Homilies on Joshua* 6-7 is the densest early Christian treatment. The walls are the "walls of wickedness" in the soul; each trumpet a spiritual gift; the priestly circuit the Church's proclamation; Rahab's scarlet cord (Josh 2) prefigures Christ's blood. Hebrews 11:30-31 pairs wall-fall with Rahab's faith. Jerome and Ambrose follow Origen. Augustine (*Quaestiones in Heptateuchum*) treats Rahab as type of the Gentile church. Theodoret (*Questions on Joshua* 10-12) handles the ban with notable caution.

*Medieval*: Aquinas (*Summa* I-II.100.8; II-II.10.8) treats the Jericho ban as a particular divine command not generalizable, a position that would be foundational for later just-war thought. The Glossa Ordinaria anthologizes Origen. Aquinas also engages Rahab's lie (Josh 2) in the moral theology; she figures in the Rahab-exception discussions across the tradition.

*Reformation*: Calvin's commentary on Joshua 6 is striking for its moral seriousness. He acknowledges the ethical difficulty of the ban, argues it was uniquely commanded for this history, and refuses to allow it to ground any contemporary Christian warfare — a hedge that later Puritan readings sometimes ignored. Luther treats the walls' fall as paradigm of gospel-power breaking human resistance. Menno Simons and the Anabaptists read the chapter as strictly typological; any literal-military application is categorically rejected for the New Testament church.

*Critical*: Noth reads the chapter as cult-etiology joined to conquest-narrative. Von Rad's *Holy War in Ancient Israel* places Jericho at the center of sacral-war. Nelson, Butler, Dozeman, and Robert Boling (Anchor Bible 1982) are the standard critical commentaries. Hess and Howard defend conservative-evangelical readings. Rowlett reads the chapter as power-legitimation; Hawk reads Rahab's inclusion as internal protest against the chapter's own ideology.

*Contemporary*: Brueggemann's *Divine Presence amid Violence* (2009) is a touchstone. Christopher Wright defends the conquest as bounded divine judgment (*The God I Don't Understand*, 2008). Copan and Flannagan (*Did God Really Command Genocide?*, 2014) argue the rhetoric is hyperbolic ANE convention. Seibert, Creach, and Collins push back: the violence is not hyperbole-neutralizable. Douglas Earl (*The Joshua Delusion?*, 2010) applies Divine Command Theory with qualifications. Regina Schwartz (*The Curse of Cain*) and the broader history-of-effects scholarship trace the chapter's use as warrant for later conquest and dispossession. Ellen Davis reads with moral candor. These lines do not reconcile.

**Convergence and divergence**: Both traditions acknowledge the chapter's liturgical shape and its theological claim that the victory belongs to YHWH. Both have historically insisted that the ban is historically bounded and not transferable (Maimonides on the Jewish side, Calvin and Aquinas on the Christian). Divergence: Rahab is absorbed into Christian salvation-history (as ancestor of David and Christ in Matthew 1) in a way the Jewish tradition does not fully parallel, though the rabbinic proselyte traditions are strong. The allegorical-typological Christian reading has no real Jewish analogue; Jewish tradition stays closer to the narrative's plain sense. On the ethics of *ḥērem*, contemporary critical scholarship converges across traditions in treating the command as a theological problem, though with different proposed resolutions.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What is God doing? Two things are going on at once: YHWH claims Jericho as his own by removing it from the ordinary economy of human use, and YHWH incorporates the outsider (Rahab) into the covenant people by the same narrative act that destroys the insiders (Jericho's population). The liturgical form makes the destruction something other than war in any ordinary sense; the Rahab exception makes the inclusion something other than ethnic in any ordinary sense.

**Tension 1: The ethics of *ḥērem***

- *Catholic (CCC 2258-2317; Dei Verbum 11-12)*: Divine revelation is given progressively; the ban belongs to an earlier pedagogical stage. Current moral theology fully repudiates annihilation of populations, but the text retains revelatory status as instruction in YHWH's holiness and the seriousness of idolatry. Vatican II's *Dei Verbum* 11 is often read as authorizing this kind of developmental hermeneutic.

- *Reformed*: Two sub-positions. Traditional Reformed (Calvin, Turretin): the ban was a unique divine command for a unique theological moment, not generalizable; it reveals God's sovereign right over life. Post-critical Reformed (Brueggemann, Goldingay): the text is honest theological wrestling, not prescriptive ethics, and Christians must read it as witness to the problem of divine violence rather than model for it.

- *Lutheran*: Two-kingdoms reading: the ban belongs to the kingdom of the left hand under specific divine mandate; no transfer to contemporary political ethics. Bonhoeffer's ethics press toward reading any such command through the cruciform lens — the God who commanded the ban is the God crucified.

- *Wesleyan/Holiness*: Moral government theology resists the literal ban's moral status; typically resolved by typological/spiritual reading in which the ban figures holy war against sin.

- *Anabaptist*: The ban is the clearest Old Testament text the New Testament church must read through Christ. The Jesus of Luke 9:51-56 forbids calling fire on villages; the Jesus of Matthew 5:44 commands enemy-love. Whatever Joshua 6 says theologically, it cannot govern Christian practice. Yoder (*The Politics of Jesus*) and Eric Seibert (*Disturbing Divine Behavior*) represent the strong form.

- *Orthodox*: The Fathers' allegorical reading (Jericho as the soul's strongholds of sin, walls as vices, trumpets as the sacraments) is not merely literary device but the tradition's way of preserving theological meaning without endorsing violence. Synergia-theology resists the monarchical-command framing.

- *Jewish rabbinic*: The ban is restricted by halakhic tradition to this specific historical moment. Maimonides, Nahmanides, and the Talmud (Berakhot 28a, on Sennacherib's conquest mixing the nations so the original Canaanites can no longer be identified) have effectively deactivated the ban as a live category. This is rabbinic interpretation performing ethical work through legal restriction.

- *Critical*: The Dtr text reflects post-exilic identity-construction rather than describing historical practice; actual archaeological data (Finkelstein, Dever) shows no evidence of systematic destruction of Canaanite populations in the relevant period. The ban is a literary theologoumenon about covenant-fidelity whose original-historical ethics are not the ethics of actual warfare. This does not dissolve the problem but relocates it: the text still shapes readers.

*Steelman Copan-Flannagan (hyperbole reading)*: ANE conquest rhetoric was routinely hyperbolic — Thutmose III's campaigns, the Assyrian royal annals, Egyptian campaign inscriptions all use "I annihilated all" formulas not to be read literally. The Joshua 6 ban language uses the same conventions. Joshua 15-17 and Judges 1 show Canaanites still in the land; the ban-rhetoric is hyperbolic triumph, not report of genocide. If this is right, the moral problem is with our over-literal reading, not with the text. This is the least intuitive position to moderns who take the text's concrete violence seriously, but it has genuine ANE-comparative evidence behind it.

*Steelman the history-of-effects critique (Schwartz, Collins)*: Whatever the text's original intent, its effect as scripture in the hands of colonial powers has been catastrophic — from the Spanish *requerimiento* through Puritan New England to apartheid South Africa. If scripture is constituted partly by its reception, the ban-texts are not neutralizable by qualification alone. The honest theological response, on this reading, is to name the text's danger and read it against the New Testament witness rather than defend it as-is.

*Where this leaves us*: Every live theological tradition has found a way to prevent the ban from being operational as ethics. What the traditions disagree about is whether that defensive work vindicates the text, convicts the text, or simply survives the text.

**Tension 2: The Rahab exception — boundary as both firm and porous**

- *Catholic*: Rahab exemplifies the *votum fidei*, implicit faith that extends saving grace across boundaries the institutional church visibly marks. The exception is theological rule — election operates freely. Matt 1:5 gives her in Jesus' genealogy.

- *Reformed*: Election is not ethnic. Rahab is the paradigm Gentile included by grace through faith (Heb 11:31, Jas 2:25). The exception reveals the rule: the covenant was never about Israelite blood but about YHWH's saving choice. Calvin's commentary on Heb 11 presses this.

- *Lutheran*: Sola gratia reading of Rahab — her faith saves where her ethnicity and her profession do not. Luther's treatment emphasizes the freedom of the gospel against ritual-ethnic exclusion.

- *Wesleyan*: Prevenient grace reaches the outsider who responds; Rahab's response makes her a model of saving faith across the ethnic line.

- *Anabaptist*: Rahab represents the voluntary covenant's porousness; she chooses Israel, is grafted in. The community is constituted by confession, not birth.

- *Orthodox*: Rahab is read as a type of the Gentile church; her household's salvation figures the household-salvation language later in Acts.

- *Jewish rabbinic (Megillah 14b)*: Rahab is a full convert (*giyoret ṣedeq*); eight prophets descend from her. The exception is metabolized into the halakhic category of conversion; the boundary is real but permeable through formal religious decision.

- *Critical (Hawk, Rowlett)*: The Rahab narrative is the text's own deconstructive moment — the narrator who just commanded total destruction pauses to preserve one household by the same ritual authority. This is either theological sophistication (the covenant was never what you thought) or narrative bad faith (the exception alibis the rule). The two readings cannot both be right.

*Steelman critical-deconstructive reading*: The Rahab inclusion functions as the narrative's ethical alibi. By naming an exception, the story performs the appearance of moral complexity while the ban proceeds. This reading is the least comfortable for confessional traditions because it implies that the theological use of the Rahab story has been self-serving — an escape valve that lets the reader feel okay about the chapter without actually confronting what else is in it.

*Where this leaves us*: The Rahab narrative is either the chapter's most important theological move or its most troubling literary device. There is no way to choose between these readings without a prior commitment about what kind of text this is.

**An unresolved question**: If the ritual is the substance — if the siege works by liturgy and not by force — then what is the theological difference between worship that builds and worship that unmakes, and who is licensed to perform which?

## The Thread

- **Hebrews 11:30-31** — "By faith the walls of Jericho fell down, after they had been encircled for seven days. By faith Rahab the prostitute did not perish with those who were disobedient." The New Testament pairs wall-fall and Rahab-rescue as single faith-event. The author refuses to let the chapter split into triumph and exception; they are one.

- **1 Kings 16:34** — "In his days Hiel of Bethel built Jericho. He laid its foundation at the cost of Abiram his firstborn, and set up its gates at the cost of his youngest son Segub." Joshua's curse (6:26) is reported fulfilled centuries later. Whether this is Dtr narrative construction or historical memory, the canonical effect is: the chapter's last word reaches forward and binds.

- **Lev 25:8-13** — Jubilee, announced by the *šôpār*/*yôvēl*. The instrument of release is the instrument of siege. In Leviticus, the horn unmakes debts; in Joshua, it unmakes walls. The theological grammar is the same: something held is given back to God.

- **Matthew 1:5** — "Salmon the father of Boaz by Rahab." Rahab stands in the genealogy of Christ. The chapter that narrates ḥērem is also the chapter without which the messianic line, as the Gospel of Matthew tells it, does not exist.
