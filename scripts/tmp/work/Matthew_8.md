# Matthew 8: The Authority That Crosses Boundaries

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Matthew 8 plants Jesus's first sustained miracle cycle in the rugged volcanic basin around the Sea of Galilee, a body of water roughly thirteen miles north to south, eight miles east to west, and seven hundred feet below sea level. The lake's geography is decisive for the chapter's climactic storm scene: cold air spilling from Mount Hermon and the Golan plateau collides with warm air rising off the Jordan rift, producing the violent squalls that fishermen — and Mendel Nun's twentieth-century studies of ancient Galilean boats and harbors — have catalogued as a regular hazard. The 1986 discovery of the so-called "Jesus Boat" at Ginosar (a twenty-six-foot first-century cedar-and-oak fishing vessel preserved in lake mud) has given concrete material substance to the kind of craft Matthew 8:23-27 envisions; archaeologists Shelley Wachsmann and Orna Cohen documented its construction, and its capacity (perhaps fifteen men) maps neatly onto the boat that Jesus and the Twelve cross with.

Capernaum, the setting for verses 5-17, has been excavated continuously since the Franciscan campaigns of Virgilio Corbo and Stanislao Loffreda beginning in 1968. Their work uncovered the basalt foundations of a first-century domestic complex — the so-called *insula sacra* — beneath a fifth-century octagonal Byzantine church, and beneath a fourth-century *domus ecclesia* that had been venerated as a Christian holy site since the late first century. Loffreda's stratigraphic analysis identified one room (the *venerated room*) as architecturally distinct from the surrounding *insulae* by the late first century: plastered walls, graffiti invoking Jesus and Peter, no domestic refuse. James Strange and Hershel Shanks's reading of the evidence makes a credible case that this is the house of Peter referenced in 8:14. Just to the north stands the famous limestone synagogue, built in the fourth or fifth century atop a black basalt foundation that excavators date to the first century — the synagogue, or its predecessor, that Luke 7:5 says the centurion of Matthew 8:5-13 financed.

That centurion (***hekatontarchos***, ἑκατόνταρχος) presents a careful historical puzzle. No Roman legion was stationed in Galilee until after 70 CE; Galilee was Herod Antipas's tetrarchy, not a Roman province. The most economical reconstruction, defended by **Warren Carter** and **Craig Keener**, is that this man commanded an auxiliary cohort raised from Syrian or Phoenician populations and seconded to Antipas's service — the kind of "soldiers under me" (8:9) who guarded tax routes and royal estates without belonging to a legionary unit. His self-deprecating "I am not worthy to have you come under my roof" (8:8) tracks ancient purity concerns: a Jewish teacher entering a Gentile household incurred ritual defilement (cf. Acts 10:28), and the centurion seems to know this.

The leper of 8:1-4 lived under the **Levitical 13-14 protocols**, a regime of priestly inspection, quarantine, and shaved-head reentry rituals exhaustively spelled out in Mishnah *Negaim*. The Greek term ***lepros*** (λεπρός) covers a range of skin conditions broader than modern Hansen's disease — psoriasis, vitiligo, fungal infections — but the social consequence was uniform: exclusion from the camp, the village, the temple. Jesus's act of *touching* the man (***hēpsato***, ἥψατο) is the evangelist's deliberate offense; under standard purity logic, contact transmits impurity to the toucher. Matthew lets the verb stand without explanation.

The country of the Gadarenes (8:28) refers to the territory of Gadara (modern Umm Qais), one of the cities of the Decapolis, perched on a high ridge overlooking the southeast corner of the lake. The textual variants are real and ancient: some manuscripts read *Gerasenes* (after Gerasa/Jerash, thirty-seven miles inland — geographically implausible), some read *Gergesenes* (after the village of Gergesa/Kursi on the eastern shore, where a steep escarpment plunges directly into the lake — geographically perfect for the pigs' rush). Origen, writing in the third century, knew Kursi as the traditional site and argued for *Gergesenes*. Excavations at Kursi in the 1970s under Vassilios Tzaferis uncovered a substantial Byzantine pilgrimage complex — a fifth-century basilica, a chapel built around a tomb, a small cave on the hillside — venerating exactly this story. The pigs (8:30) are the chapter's quiet ethnographic marker: this is Gentile country.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Matthew 8-9 is the first installment of what B. W. Bacon (*Studies in Matthew*, 1918) identified as Matthew's deliberate alternation of *narrative* and *discourse* — the ten chapters of action that follow the Sermon on the Mount and precede the Mission Discourse of chapter 10. **W. D. Davies and Dale Allison**, in their three-volume **International Critical Commentary**, gave the structural reading of these chapters its definitive form: ten miracle stories arranged in three triads, each triad followed by transitional material on discipleship and mission. Triad one (8:1-17) is leper, centurion's servant, Peter's mother-in-law (with summary healings). Triad two (8:23-9:8) is storm-stilling, Gadarene demoniacs, paralytic. Triad three (9:18-34) is the bracketed Jairus's daughter / hemorrhaging woman, two blind men, mute demoniac. Between triads one and two, Matthew inserts the discipleship sayings of 8:18-22; between triads two and three, the call of Matthew, the question about fasting, and the new wine sayings of 9:9-17.

The triadic structure is not Markan. Mark scatters these miracles across several chapters in a different sequence; **R. T. France** (NICNT) and **Donald Hagner** (WBC) both note that Matthew has *thematized* what in Mark is *geographical*. Where Mark organizes around journey, Matthew organizes around the demonstration of Jesus's ***exousia*** (ἐξουσία, "authority") — the same word the crowds applied to him at the close of the Sermon on the Mount (7:29). Authority over disease, authority over Gentiles' faith, authority over fever, authority over the elements, authority over demons, authority to forgive sins. The chapter is a sustained ***exousia*** demonstration, and the term will return programmatically when Jesus delegates that authority to the Twelve in 10:1.

Matthew's compression is striking. The Gadarene episode in Mark 5:1-20 runs twenty verses; Matthew tells it in seven (8:28-34). The hemorrhaging woman in Mark gets ten verses; Matthew gives her three. **Ulrich Luz** (Hermeneia) reads this consistently throughout: Matthew prunes Markan narrative detail to foreground Christological declaration. What Mark embeds in story, Matthew distills to dictum.

A second Matthean signature is the pattern of *doubling*. Matthew has two demoniacs at Gadara (8:28) where Mark has one; later he will have two blind men at Jericho (20:30) where Mark has Bartimaeus alone (10:46). Davies and Allison survey the explanations — that Matthew is conflating two Markan stories, that he is meeting the Deuteronomic two-witness requirement, that he is simply preserving an alternate tradition — without committing decisively. **John Nolland** (NIGTC) prefers the witness-multiplication reading; **France** thinks Matthew is preserving an independent tradition.

The fulfillment formula citation in 8:17 — Isaiah 53:4, "He took our illnesses and bore our diseases" — is the fourth of Matthew's ten programmatic *plērōthē* citations and the only one in this chapter. The application is striking: Matthew uses the Servant Song's vicarious-suffering language not for the cross but for the *healing ministry*. Davies and Allison argue this is a deliberate Matthean Christology — the Servant's burden-bearing begins with Jesus's empathic absorption of human disease and culminates at Calvary. It is the closest the Synoptics come to articulating an atonement theology of the healings.

Three Greek terms anchor the chapter. ***Exousia*** (ἐξουσία) we have already met. ***Pistis*** (πίστις, "faith") appears twice: in 8:10 (the centurion's faith greater than any in Israel) and 8:26 (the disciples' "little faith," ***oligopistoi***, ὀλιγόπιστοι — a distinctively Matthean term, used five times in the gospel and once in Luke). ***Akolouthēō*** (ἀκολουθέω, "to follow") appears five times in the chapter (8:1, 10, 19, 22, 23) — Matthew's primary verb for discipleship, and the verb that frames the discipleship sayings of 8:18-22.

Textually the chapter is stable. The most consequential variant is the city name in 8:28 (Gadarenes / Gerasenes / Gergesenes), where the Nestle-Aland 28 prints *Gadarēnōn* on the strength of א and B but acknowledges the ancient reception of *Gergesēnōn* through Origen.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Read canonically, Matthew 8 sits at the threshold of a problem the whole gospel is working out: how can a Davidic Messiah sent first to "the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (10:6, 15:24) finally commission his disciples to "make disciples of all nations" (28:19)? The chapter's centurion narrative is the gospel's first serious answer. A Gentile demonstrates faith greater than Israel's; Jesus declares that "many will come from east and west" to recline at Abraham's table while "the sons of the kingdom" are cast out (8:11-12). **Krister Stendahl** (*The School of St. Matthew*) and **Anthony Saldarini** (*Matthew's Christian-Jewish Community*) read this as the evangelist's careful staging: salvation goes to Israel first in the narrative, but the Gentile turn is already prefigured in the ministry itself. The reader is being prepared for the closing commission.

**Jewish interpretive trajectories** approach this material with particular care because it has so often been weaponized against Jews. **Amy-Jill Levine** (*The Misunderstood Jew*; *The Jewish Annotated New Testament*) insists that the contrast between the centurion's faith and the "sons of the kingdom" cannot be flattened into anti-Jewish polemic; it is an *intra-Jewish* prophetic critique on the model of Amos and Jeremiah, indicting complacent insiders, not the Jewish people as such. **Daniel Boyarin** (*The Jewish Gospels*) argues that the high Christology already evident in Matthew 8 — Jesus rebuking the sea (a divine prerogative throughout the Hebrew Bible: Psalm 89:9, Psalm 107:23-32, Job 38:8-11) and demons confessing him as "Son of God" — was not a foreign Hellenistic intrusion onto Judaism but a development *within* Second Temple Jewish messianic expectation, traceable to Daniel 7's "one like a son of man" coming on the clouds. **Geza Vermes** (*Jesus the Jew*) reads the leper's healing and the centurion's reception as continuous with charismatic Galilean *hasidim* such as Hanina ben Dosa, whose long-distance healings the Talmud preserves (*b. Berakhot* 34b). **David Flusser** stressed that Jesus's "I have not found such faith in Israel" is hyperbolic prophetic praise, not a generalization about Jewish unbelief.

**Christian interpretive trajectories** form a deep and interlocking conversation. The patristic readings begin with **Origen**, who allegorized the storm as the church's tribulation and the leper as the human soul defiled by sin. **John Chrysostom**, in his *Ninety Homilies on Matthew* (homilies 25-28 cover this chapter), reads the centurion's "I am not worthy" as the model of eucharistic approach — a phrase that entered the Roman Mass and remains there. **Augustine** in *Sermon 62* and his *Harmony of the Gospels* worked through the apparent discrepancies between Matthew's centurion (who comes himself) and Luke's (who sends elders), proposing that the man both sent emissaries and later came in person. **Jerome** read the leper as a figure for fallen humanity that Christ does not disdain to touch. **Hilary of Poitiers** found in the calmed sea a figure of the church preserved from the world's tempests. **Aquinas** in the *Catena Aurea* assembles the patristic comments verse by verse, and in his *Lectura super Matthaeum* presses the *exousia* theme: each miracle reveals a divine attribute proper to Christ's deity.

The Reformation reading sharpens differently. **Calvin**, in his *Harmony of the Evangelists*, reads the centurion's faith as a model of *fiducia* — trust in the bare word of Christ apart from sight — and stresses that the leper's "if you will, you can" is the right grammar of prayer, submitting to divine will while confessing divine power. **Luther** preached the storm pericope as a parable of the buffeted conscience that wakes Christ by prayer.

In the twentieth century **Dietrich Bonhoeffer** in *Discipleship* (the book later translated as *The Cost of Discipleship*) made 8:18-22 — "let the dead bury their own dead" — the cornerstone of his doctrine of *Nachfolge*: the call of Christ severs natural pieties and replaces them with absolute obedience. **Rudolf Bultmann** in his *History of the Synoptic Tradition* classified the miracle stories form-critically as Hellenistic *aretalogies* and demythologized their content; the existential meaning, for Bultmann, was the encounter with eschatological possibility, not the suspension of natural law.

Contemporary scholarship has produced a remarkable density of treatment. **Davies and Allison's** ICC volume 2 remains the most exhaustive philological and structural reading. **Luz's** Hermeneia is unique in its sustained reception-history (*Wirkungsgeschichte*) — for instance, his pages on 8:8 trace the centurion's words through medieval mystical writers, Trent, the *Domine non sum dignus* of the Mass, and modern hymnody. **R. T. France** (NICNT) is the most readable on narrative flow; **Craig Keener** (NICNT) the richest in Greco-Roman background; **Daniel Harrington** (Sacra Pagina) the most economical Catholic reading; **John Nolland** (NIGTC) the most careful on Greek syntax; **Donald Hagner** (WBC) balanced and judicious.

**Literary criticism** has reshaped the conversation since **Jack Dean Kingsbury's** *Matthew as Story* (1986) showed that the gospel's character development — Jesus's identity unfolding through plotted episodes — repays narrative-critical attention. The Matthean disciples in 8:23-27 are not simply terrified but *of little faith*; the storm is the gospel's first test of the discipleship that the Sermon has just demanded. Commentators across the spectrum (Davies/Allison, France, Luz, Bauckham in *Gospel Women*) note the spare handling of Peter's mother-in-law in 8:14-15 — she is unnamed, given no speech, and healed precisely so that she may *serve* (***diēkonei***, διηκόνει) — and observe that Matthew's later use of the same verb for the women who "ministered" to Jesus and followed him to the cross (27:55) lets her brief act stand as an early instance of discipleship-as-service. Levine's contribution to the *Jewish Annotated New Testament* repeatedly cautions against readings of "the sons of the kingdom" that detach from Jewish prophetic tradition.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions structure the theological reading.

**First tension: the boundary-crossing of authority.** Matthew 8 narrates Jesus reaching across every major boundary that organized first-century Jewish life — the leper's impurity, the Gentile's foreignness, the woman's domestic confinement, the demoniac's defilement, the dead man's claim on his son. Three positions on what this crossing means. **(1) Replacement / supersessionist reading**: the centurion's faith and the casting out of "the sons of the kingdom" inaugurate the displacement of Israel by a Gentile-inclusive church. This reading runs from Chrysostom through some Reformation polemics into the twentieth century. Steelmanned, it points to 8:11-12's plain sense (many from east and west *will* come; sons of the kingdom *will* be cast out) and to Matthew's eventual commissioning of the nations. **(2) Expansion / inclusion reading**: the centurion does not displace Israel but *enlarges* the people of God; "the sons of the kingdom" cast out are the unfaithful within Israel, not Israel as such. This is Levine's, Saldarini's, and Davies/Allison's view. Steelmanned, it reads Matthew's continuing concern for Israel (10:6, 15:24, 23:37-39) as decisive and refuses to let 8:11-12 carry more weight than the gospel's own internal tensions allow. **(3) Eschatological-pilgrim reading**: drawing on the Isaianic streaming of nations to Zion (Isa 2:2-4, 25:6-8, 60), the chapter prefigures the *messianic banquet* in which Gentiles arrive *as Gentiles* to share in Israel's covenant blessings. This is N. T. Wright's preferred frame; steelmanned, it preserves both Israel's enduring election and the Gentile turn without forcing one to displace the other. *Where this leaves us:* the strongest reading combines (2) and (3) — Israel is not displaced but *enlarged* through the messianic banquet's eschatological staging. The gospel's closing commission to "all nations" (28:19) is then not the abolition of Israel's priority but the fulfillment of Israel's vocation to be a light to the nations (Isa 49:6).

**Second tension: the cost of following.** The discipleship sayings in 8:18-22 are the gospel's first hard words about what the call entails. Three positions. **(1) Maximalist reading**: Jesus literally severs natural family obligations and demands a homeless itinerancy. This is Bonhoeffer's reading — *Nachfolge* is total dispossession, and the disciple who clings to even the most pious obligation (burying one's father) has not yet understood the call. Steelmanned, the maximalist reading takes the saying's shock at face value and refuses to domesticate it. **(2) Vocational reading**: the sayings address particular would-be disciples in particular moments and articulate the *kind* of priority Jesus must have, not the literal renunciation of every familial duty. This is the dominant patristic and Reformation reading; Calvin distinguishes between the *commandments* of the Decalogue (which include honoring father and mother) and the *call* to particular vocations that may temporarily relativize family claims. Steelmanned, the vocational reading honors the rest of the canon's pro-family ethic (Mark 7:9-13's critique of *corban*; Eph 6:1-4) and reads 8:21-22 as situational. **(3) Eschatological-urgency reading**: the sayings reflect the apocalyptic compression of the in-breaking kingdom; ordinary obligations are not abolished but *postponed* by the eschatological hour. This is roughly Schweitzer's, with modern refinements in **Dale Allison's** *The New Moses*. Steelmanned, it preserves the saying's edge without making it a permanent ethic. *Where this leaves us:* (3) probably best accounts for the saying's original setting, but (1) names the way the saying functions for the *post-Easter* church for whom every hour is the eschatological hour. Bonhoeffer's reading is overstated as a permanent literal ethic but indispensable as a corrective against domesticated discipleship.

**Unresolved question.** What does it mean that Matthew applies Isaiah 53 — the suffering-servant text the early church associated above all with the cross — to Jesus's *healing* ministry rather than to his death (8:17)? Is Matthew telling us that the atonement begins long before Calvary, that every healing is already a vicarious bearing of human disease? Or is he making a more limited typological gesture, signaling that the same Servant who would later die was already at work in Galilee? The text does not adjudicate, and the history of interpretation has divided sharply.

## The Thread

The chapter's ***exousia*** runs forward into 9:6 (authority to forgive sins) and 10:1 (authority delegated to the Twelve). The centurion's faith greater than Israel's prefigures the Canaanite woman of 15:21-28 — the gospel's other great Gentile-faith pericope — and the Roman centurion at the cross who confesses "Truly this was the Son of God" (27:54). The "sons of the kingdom cast out" (8:12) returns at 22:13 (the wedding garment) and 25:30 (the worthless servant). The storm-stilling in 8:23-27 will be doubled at 14:22-33, where Peter himself walks on the water, and the same disciples will again be called ***oligopistoi***. The cost-of-discipleship sayings in 8:18-22 anticipate the cross-saying of 16:24 ("If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross") and the renunciation logia of 19:27-29.
