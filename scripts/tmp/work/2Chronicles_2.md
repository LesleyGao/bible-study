# 2 Chronicles 2 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Chapter 2 narrates Solomon's preparations to build the temple: the conscription of laborers, the diplomatic correspondence with Hiram of Tyre, and the census of resident aliens. The parallel is 1 Kings 5 (Hebrew 5:1–18; English 5:15–32 in some editions), and the differences between the two accounts are theologically loaded.

The chapter opens with Solomon's resolution: *Now Solomon purposed to build a temple for the name of the LORD, and a royal palace for himself* (verse 1). Hebrew *vayyo'mer Shlomoh livnot bayit le-shem Yahweh u-vayit le-malkhuto* (וַיֹּאמֶר שְׁלֹמֹה לִבְנוֹת בַּיִת לְשֵׁם יְהוָה וּבַיִת לְמַלְכוּתוֹ). The phrase *bayit le-shem Yahweh* — "a house for the name of Yahweh" — is the Deuteronomistic name-theology that the Chronicler inherits. The temple is not Yahweh's residence in any literal sense; it is the place where Yahweh's *name* dwells. This is sophisticated post-exilic theology that the Chronicler shares with Deuteronomy 12 and Solomon's later prayer in chapter 6.

Verses 2 and 17–18 form a literary inclusio around the chapter, both stating the same labor numbers: 70,000 burden-bearers, 80,000 quarrymen in the hill country, 3,600 overseers. Total: 153,600 men. The Chronicler's emphasis on these numbers — opening and closing the chapter with them — signals theological weight. Sara Japhet, in her *Old Testament Library* commentary, treats the numbers as stylized but historically grounded: a state-mobilization for a major monumental project. Gary Knoppers, in his *Anchor Bible* commentary, compares them to ancient Near Eastern records of temple construction (the building inscriptions of Mesopotamian kings routinely cite tens of thousands of conscripted workers).

The most significant difference between Chronicles and Kings concerns *who* these laborers are. 1 Kings 5:13–18 says Solomon raised a forced levy *out of all Israel* — 30,000 Israelite men were conscripted for rotational service in Lebanon, plus the 70,000/80,000/3,300 of the resident workforce. The Chronicler removes the levy on Israelites entirely. In his version, the 153,600 are *resident aliens* (verse 17, Hebrew *gerim*, גֵּרִים) — the non-Israelite population living in the land. Verse 18 makes this explicit: the 70,000/80,000/3,600 are conscripted from this *ger* population alone. Hugh Williamson, in his *New Century Bible Commentary*, calls this one of the clearest theological adjustments in Chronicles. The Chronicler is defending Solomon against the implicit Kings-critique that a king who enslaves his own people for a building project is dangerously close to Pharaoh. Williamson notes the verbal echo: 1 Kings 5:13 uses the verb *vayya'al ha-melekh Shlomoh mas mi-kol-Yisrael* (וַיַּעַל הַמֶּלֶךְ שְׁלֹמֹה מַס מִכָּל־יִשְׂרָאֵל, "King Solomon raised a levy from all Israel") — the same word *mas* (forced labor/corvée) that Exodus 1:11 uses of Pharaoh's enslavement of Israel. The Kings narrator may be drawing the parallel deliberately. The Chronicler, recognizing the same parallel, severs it: in his account, no Israelite is conscripted, only *gerim*.

Whether this is historical revision or theological correction is debated. Mark Boda, Steven McKenzie, and Louis Jonker tend to read it as the Chronicler's deliberate reworking — the historical Solomon almost certainly did conscript Israelites, and Kings is closer to memory. Knoppers is more cautious, noting that the Chronicler had access to administrative traditions Kings did not preserve, and that *ger*-conscription for state projects is documented in ancient Near Eastern parallels. Either way, the Chronicler is making a theological claim: in the idealized Davidic-Solomonic monarchy, no Israelite is enslaved.

But this raises an uncomfortable question that the Chronicler does not address: is it morally acceptable to conscript 153,600 *non-Israelite* residents for state labor? The text is silent on this. The temple's construction rests on coerced labor, even if the coerced are "resident aliens" rather than Israelites. Walter Brueggemann, in *Israel's Praise: Doxology against Idolatry and Ideology*, presses the point about the moral cost of monumental religious architecture: the temple as a symbol of Yahweh's presence is also a state-building project that extracts labor from the powerless. The honest reader has to hold both — the theological grandeur of the temple and the human cost of its construction.

Verses 3–10 record Solomon's letter to Hiram of Tyre. The Chronicler calls him *Huram* throughout (verse 3, etc.), a variant spelling of the same name (Hebrew *Churam*, חוּרָם, vs. Kings's *Chiram*, חִירָם). The diplomatic exchange is reworked. In Kings, Hiram initiates contact (1 Kings 5:1); in Chronicles, Solomon initiates. The Chronicler's Solomon explains the temple's purpose at length (verses 4–6) — for the burning of incense, the regular showbread, the daily and Sabbath and festal burnt offerings — emphasizing the *cultic continuity* with the Mosaic system. He also offers a striking theological reflection: *who is able to build him a house, since heaven, even highest heaven, cannot contain him?* (verse 6). Hebrew *u-mi ya'atzor-koach livnot lo bayit ki ha-shamayim u-shme ha-shamayim lo yekhalkluhu* (וּמִי יַעֲצָר־כֹּחַ לִבְנוֹת לוֹ בַיִת כִּי הַשָּׁמַיִם וּשְׁמֵי הַשָּׁמַיִם לֹא יְכַלְכְּלֻהוּ). This is the same theology Solomon will articulate in his dedication prayer (6:18): the temple is not a *containment* but a *meeting place*. The Chronicler has Solomon name this directly, even to a foreign king.

Solomon requests a master craftsman (verse 7), describing the requirements in detail: skilled in gold, silver, bronze, iron, purple, crimson, blue fabrics, and engraving. Hiram responds (verses 11–16) with a striking diplomatic blessing: *Because the LORD loves his people, he has made you king over them* (verse 11). Hebrew *be-ahavat Yahweh et-amo natanu aleihem melekh* (בְּאַהֲבַת יְהוָה אֶת־עַמּוֹ נְתָנוֹ עֲלֵיהֶם מֶלֶךְ). Hiram, a Phoenician king worshiping Melqart, addresses Yahweh by name and confesses Yahweh's love for Israel. The Chronicler is making a point: even foreign kings recognize Yahweh's election of Israel and the legitimacy of Solomon's reign. Sara Japhet reads this as the Chronicler's universalizing impulse: Israel's God is acknowledged by the nations through their dealings with Solomon's temple-project.

Hiram sends Huram-abi (verses 13–14), a craftsman whose mother is from the tribe of Dan and whose father is a Tyrian. The genealogy is theologically pointed. In 1 Kings 7:14, Huram's mother is from Naphtali; in Chronicles, she is from Dan. Williamson and Klein both note the change. The reason is intertextual: the original tabernacle was built by Bezalel of Judah and Oholiab of Dan (Exodus 31:6, 35:34, 38:23). By making Huram-abi's mother a Danite, the Chronicler is creating a typological link between the tabernacle and the temple — both built by craftsmen with Danite ancestry. The temple is not a new sanctuary; it is the continuation of the tabernacle-project.

But there is a wrinkle: Huram-abi is half-Tyrian, the son of a foreigner, brought in from outside Israel. The tabernacle was built entirely by Israelites; the temple is built with foreign expertise. Knoppers reads this as a deliberate theological signal — the temple, while continuous with the tabernacle, is also a *trans-national* project that incorporates the skill of the nations. The Chronicler is preparing his post-exilic reader for a vision of the temple that is not narrowly ethnic but wider.

The chapter closes with the census of resident aliens (verses 17–18) — restating the labor numbers from verse 2 and naming their source. The verb in verse 18 is striking: *3,600 as overseers to make the people work* (Hebrew *le-ha'avid et-ha'am*, לְהַעֲבִיד אֶת־הָעָם). The hiphil of *avad* — "to cause to serve" — is the same verbal form Exodus 1:13 uses of Pharaoh's enslavement of Israel. Whether the Chronicler deliberately preserves the Pharaonic echo (as a residual sign of cost) or simply uses standard administrative vocabulary is debated, but the verb is there.

## Lens 2: The Literature

The chapter is structured as an inclusio: labor numbers (verses 1–2), diplomatic correspondence (verses 3–16), labor numbers (verses 17–18). The inner section is itself bipartite: Solomon's letter (verses 3–10) and Hiram's reply (verses 11–16). The literary form is *royal correspondence*, well attested in ancient Near Eastern archives (the Amarna letters, the Mari archives) where kings exchanged formal letters about diplomatic and commercial matters. The Chronicler is presenting his temple-project in the recognized literary form of state-to-state diplomacy, lending it administrative credibility.

The letters are theologically weighted in both directions. Solomon's letter (verses 4–6) is essentially a sermon on the temple's purpose — for the *cultus*, for the worship of the God who cannot be contained. Hiram's reply (verse 11) is a confession of Yahweh's love for Israel by a foreign king. The literary effect is that the temple is framed not as a private royal project but as a publicly-recognized international religious institution.

Hebrew literary features include the parallel descriptions of Huram-abi's skills in verses 7 and 13–14 — a chiastic mirroring in which what Solomon requests Hiram fulfills exactly. The numbers are stylized round figures (70,000 / 80,000 / 3,600 — multiples of ten and dozen), which Boda and Williamson treat as theological rhetoric rather than auditable accounts.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

The Jewish reception of 2 Chronicles 2 has focused on three points: the theology of the *bayit le-shem* (house for the Name), the legitimacy of foreign craftsmanship in Israel's holy work, and the moral status of the resident-alien labor.

Rashi (eleventh century) reads Solomon's "who is able to build him a house" (verse 6) as the foundational rabbinic insight that the temple is a meeting-place not a residence. The Talmud tractate *Berakhot* 63b draws on this verse for the principle that prayer-toward-the-temple is acceptable from anywhere because Yahweh is not contained there. Radak (David Kimchi, twelfth–thirteenth century) treats Hiram's confession of Yahweh as proof that even Gentile rulers had access to true theology in Solomon's day, an insight that medieval Jewish thinkers used to argue for the dignity of the Noahide tradition. Abarbanel (fifteenth century) treats the resident-alien labor as a model of how non-Israelites can serve the temple-project without compromising its sanctity — a reading that, in the Spanish-Portuguese Jewish context where Abarbanel wrote, had practical resonance for Jewish communities relying on non-Jewish labor and craftsmanship. Modern Jewish scholars — Sara Japhet, Isaac Kalimi, Ehud Ben Zvi — have emphasized the chapter's universalizing impulse: the temple is built with Phoenician timber and Tyrian craftsmanship, and a foreign king blesses Yahweh in connection with it. The temple, even before it is built, has a transnational dimension.

The Christian reception has had more layers. Jerome's Vulgate translates *bayit le-shem Yahweh* as *domum nomini Domini* — "a house for the name of the Lord" — a phrase that medieval theology (Aquinas, the *Glossa Ordinaria*) used to articulate the difference between God's essence and his manifest presence in worship. Cassiodorus and Bede both took the chapter as a charter for Christian church-building: any house dedicated to God's name participates by grace in the theology Solomon names in verse 6. John Calvin, in his 1561 commentary, treated Hiram's confession as evidence that God's revelation extends beyond Israel — a Reformed argument for natural theology and general revelation. Calvin was less comfortable with the labor-conscription, treating it briefly as a fact of monarchical administration rather than a model.

Modern Christian scholarship has been more willing to name the moral cost. Walter Brueggemann's *Israel's Praise* and *Theology of the Old Testament* read the temple's construction as an exercise in royal ideology — beautiful, grand, and built on coerced labor; the temple's grandeur cannot be celebrated without acknowledging the *gerim* whose labor built it. Jon Levenson, in *Sinai and Zion*, treats the temple as theologically central but is honest about its material costs. The honest Christian reader of 2 Chronicles 2 has to hold both — the theological vision of a house for Yahweh's name, and the moral weight of 153,600 conscripted laborers.

The New Testament does not quote 2 Chronicles 2 directly but the chapter's name-theology runs through the Gospels and Acts. Jesus's saying about the temple in John 2:19–22 ("destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up... but he was speaking about the temple of his body") presupposes the same logic Solomon names in verse 6 — Yahweh cannot be contained. Stephen's speech in Acts 7:48–50 quotes Isaiah 66:1–2 ("the Most High does not dwell in houses made by human hands") to make the same point. The New Testament writers are inheriting and extending the Chronicler's theology: the temple is not a containment but a meeting; the locus of divine presence is finally relational, not architectural.

The foreign-craftsman tradition, with Huram-abi as the half-Tyrian master craftsman echoing Bezalel of Judah and Oholiab of Dan, becomes typologically important in some Christian readings. Bede's commentary on Chronicles takes Huram-abi as a figure of the Gentile contribution to the building of the church — a typology that ran through medieval theology as a charter for the inclusion of non-Jewish believers. Modern scholarship is more cautious about typological readings, but Williamson, Knoppers, and Boda all note that the Chronicler is at least open to a transnational temple-project.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The Chronicler's agenda in chapter 2 is to establish the temple's preparation in a way that demonstrates Solomon's wisdom (he plans, corresponds, recruits, organizes), preserves the cultic continuity with the Mosaic tabernacle (Huram-abi's Danite mother, the materials lists), and protects Solomon from the implicit Kings-critique of Pharaoh-like enslavement (the resident-alien labor force). Three tensions deserve careful naming.

**The first tension: the conscripted labor and the moral cost of monumental theology.** The temple's construction rests on the coerced labor of 153,600 resident aliens. The Chronicler removes the Israelite levy from Kings, but the *ger* labor remains. How should the modern reader hold this?

**Position 1: Apologetic-historical reading.** The conscripted labor was a normal feature of ancient Near Eastern monarchical administration. Egypt, Assyria, Babylon, and Persia all conscripted labor on a similar or larger scale. Holding 2 Chronicles 2 to a modern moral standard is anachronistic. The Chronicler's actual move — protecting Israelites from forced labor while acknowledging *ger* conscription — represents a moral *advance* over the standard Near Eastern practice. This is the position of more conservative commentators and apologetic readers.

**Position 2: Ideology-critical reading.** The temple's construction rests on the labor of the powerless, and the Chronicler's removal of the Israelite levy makes this *worse*, not better. By excluding Israelites from the labor force, the Chronicler ensures that the burden falls on the most marginal — the resident aliens, the people without political voice. This is a textbook case of the powerful preserving their own standing while extracting labor from those least able to refuse. Walter Brueggemann presses something close to this in his work on royal ideology in the monarchy narratives.

**Position 3: Tragic-theological reading.** The temple is theologically grand and morally costly. Both are true, and the text does not resolve the tension. The Chronicler's silence about the *ger* laborers' experience is itself a theological problem. The honest reader holds the grandeur and the cost together, recognizing that monumental religious architecture has *always* been built on labor that was less than fully voluntary, and that the church's own history of cathedral-building, missionary-station-construction, and institutional expansion has often had a similar shadow. Jon Levenson, Mark Boda, and many mainline scholars take roughly this view.

**Steelman of Position 2.** The strongest case for the ideology-critical reading is that the Chronicler's adjustment is a textbook move. He sees the ethical problem in Kings — Solomon enslaves Israel, echoing Pharaoh — and his solution is not to question the monumental project but to redirect the burden onto a less-protected population. The *gerim*, in Israelite legal tradition, are supposed to be *protected*: Exodus 22:21, Leviticus 19:34, and Deuteronomy 10:19 all command Israel not to oppress the *ger* because Israel was a *ger* in Egypt. Conscripting the *gerim* for state labor is precisely the kind of oppression the Torah forbids. The Chronicler does not address this. The silence is the problem. A reader who notices it has noticed something the text passes over.

**Where this leaves us on this tension.** The honest answer is: the temple is theologically magnificent and morally compromised. Both. The Chronicler's removal of the Israelite levy is real ethical progress and also ideological cover. The resident-alien laborers are not heard from in the text and they need to be named in any honest contemporary reading. The temple's beauty was paid for in suffering the text does not fully acknowledge, and that recognition does not destroy the temple's theological meaning — it complicates it. The post-exilic community that received Chronicles was itself a community whose people had been gerim in Babylon. The Chronicler is writing for people who knew exactly what conscripted labor felt like. His silence about the gerim of his Solomon-narrative is therefore strange, and worth pressing.

**The second tension: the foreign craftsman.** Huram-abi is half-Tyrian, the son of a foreigner, and he is brought in to do the most sacred work — engraving the temple furnishings, working in gold and bronze, producing the very artifacts that will fill the holy place. The tabernacle was built entirely by Israelites. The temple is not. What is the theological status of the foreign contribution?

**Position 1: Strict-cultic reading.** The temple's holiness requires that Israelites alone build it. Huram-abi's involvement is a compromise the Chronicler tolerates because Phoenician craftsmanship was unmatched, but the principle remains: holy work belongs to the holy people. Some traditional Jewish readings (echoes of this in later medieval halakhic discussion) take this view in modified forms.

**Position 2: Universalizing reading.** The Chronicler is deliberately establishing that the temple is a *transnational* project that incorporates the skill of the nations. Huram-abi's Danite mother creates continuity with the tabernacle (Oholiab of Dan in Exodus 31), and his Tyrian father signals that Yahweh's house is built with the contributions of those outside Israel as well. Hiram's confession of Yahweh in verse 11 confirms the universalizing reading. Sara Japhet, Williamson, and Knoppers take this view.

**Position 3: Pragmatic reading.** Solomon needed the best craftsmen and Phoenicia had them. The Chronicler is reporting an administrative fact, not making a theological argument about Gentile inclusion. The Danite-mother detail is genealogical bookkeeping, not typological.

**Steelman of Position 2.** The Chronicler's deliberate adjustment of Huram-abi's mother from Naphtali (in Kings) to Dan (in Chronicles) is hard to explain except as an intentional typological move. The tabernacle was built by Bezalel-of-Judah and Oholiab-of-Dan; the temple is built by Huram-abi-half-of-Dan, with Solomon-of-Judah as the patron. The pattern is too tight to be accidental. The Chronicler is saying: the temple is the tabernacle continued, but with a transnational expansion. The Phoenician craftsmen are not contaminating; they are participating. This is a major theological move, and it prepares the ground for Solomon's foreigner-petition in chapter 6 (where he asks Yahweh to hear the prayers of foreigners who come to the temple).

**Where this leaves us.** The temple, even in its preparation, is wider than Israel alone. The Chronicler is signaling that Yahweh's house has a transnational dimension — Phoenician timber, Tyrian craftsmanship, foreign-king blessing, half-Israelite master craftsman. This is preparation for the universalizing prayer of chapter 6. The post-exilic reader, living under Persian rule in a land where Israelites and *gerim* lived together, is being told that the temple-vision is large enough to include the contribution of the nations.

**The third tension: the "house for the Name" theology.** Solomon's question in verse 6 — *who is able to build him a house, since heaven, even highest heaven, cannot contain him?* — articulates a theology that is profound and also raises a question about the entire temple-project. If Yahweh cannot be contained, why build a house at all? The Chronicler's answer (and Solomon's answer in chapter 6) is that the temple is not a containment but a *meeting place*, a location where the *name* of Yahweh dwells and where prayers are heard. This is sophisticated theology, and it sets up the New Testament's appropriation of the temple-tradition. Jesus in John 2:19–22, Stephen in Acts 7:48–50, Paul in 1 Corinthians 3:16–17 and 6:19, Hebrews 9–10 — all are working out what it means that the God who cannot be contained nevertheless meets his people in concrete places.

**Where this leaves us.** The chapter is a charter for theological architecture: build for God's name, not as if God were contained; recognize that the work is collaborative, transnational, and costly; acknowledge the human price even while celebrating the divine purpose. The temple is a real place where the immeasurable God meets a measurable people, and the meeting requires both stones and theology, both labor and prayer.

**Closing question.** The Chronicler protects Solomon by reassigning the labor burden from Israelites to resident aliens. But does the protection of Solomon become at the expense of the *gerim*? If the only way to make a king look righteous is to redirect the cost onto a more invisible population, what kind of righteousness is that? The post-exilic community received this text and built their second temple with their own hands, not with conscripted aliens. They had to. The first temple's architectural grandeur was beyond them. But maybe their constraint was also their grace: the smaller temple was built without the moral shadow of the larger one. What does that suggest about the architecture we build today?

## The Thread

Solomon resolves to build the temple, conscripts 153,600 resident aliens for the labor, writes to Hiram of Tyre for cedar and a master craftsman, and receives back both the materials and Huram-abi — a half-Tyrian craftsman whose Danite mother links him typologically to Oholiab of the tabernacle. Hiram blesses Yahweh in connection with Solomon's accession. Solomon names the deep theology of the project — *who is able to build him a house, since heaven cannot contain him?* The temple is a house for the name, a meeting place, a transnational project, and a monument whose construction rests on coerced labor the text does not fully acknowledge. The Chronicler is preparing the ground.
