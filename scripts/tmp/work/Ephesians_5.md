# Ephesians 5: Light, Hymn, and the Mystery of the Two Made One

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Roman Asia in the second half of the first century was a society organized around the household, and the household was a hierarchical economic unit before it was a sentimental one. The *paterfamilias* held *patria potestas* — formal legal authority over wife, children, slaves, and freedmen — supplemented by *manus* (legal authority over a wife who had passed under his hand) in the older form of marriage and by various forms of *potestas* over slaves whose lives were, in classical Roman law, his to take. By the first century, Roman marriage had largely shifted from *cum manu* to *sine manu* forms, in which the wife remained legally under her father's *potestas* rather than her husband's, but the cultural expectation of male headship persisted across both. The Greek philosophical tradition systematized this in Aristotle's *Politics* I.2-13, which produced the canonical triad of household relationships — master/slave, husband/wife, father/child — that would shape Stoic ethical writing (Hierocles' *On Duties*; Seneca's letters), Hellenistic-Jewish apologetics (Philo's *Hypothetica* and *On the Special Laws*; Josephus' *Against Apion* 2.199-208), and ultimately the Christian *Haustafeln* embedded in Eph 5:22-6:9, Col 3:18-4:1, 1 Pet 2:18-3:7, 1 Tim 2:8-15, Tit 2:1-10, and *Didache* 4.9-11.

The material culture of marriage in Asian cities is preserved in funerary inscriptions, the wedding contracts of the Egyptian papyri (which, while Egyptian, reflect broader Roman provincial practice), and the household frescoes of Pompeii — which, though earlier and Italian, give the closest surviving visual evidence of the spatial and ritual setting of the *Haustafel* world: the *atrium*, the *lararium* (household shrine), the dining couches arranged by status. Local to Ephesus, the cult of Artemis Ephesia, the maternal-virginal-protective goddess, supplied a non-domestic image of female power; the city was also home to a substantial Jewish community whose marriage practices Josephus repeatedly defends in *Against Apion* and *Antiquities* 14.225-264, and whose synagogue ethics included a strong familial-relational stream more egalitarian in some respects than its Roman context.

The authorship and dating debate remains live for chapter 5. Critical scholarship — C.L. Mitton (1951), Andrew Lincoln (WBC 1990), Rudolf Schnackenburg, Margaret MacDonald, Petr Pokorný, Ernst Käsemann — locates Ephesians in the deutero-Pauline period, c. 70-90, in part because its Haustafel is more developed than anything indisputably Pauline, and because its ecclesiology presupposes a settling-in to civic society. Conservative defenders — Hoehner, O'Brien, Bruce, Thielman, M. Barth, Arnold — date the letter to Pauline imprisonment (c. 60-62) and explain the Haustafel features through Pauline development rather than pseudonymity. The "in Ephesus" of 1:1, absent from P46, Sinaiticus א*, and Vaticanus B*, supports the encyclical thesis: chapter 5's instructions are addressed to a network of Asian house-churches.

Five Greek key terms anchor the chapter: μιμηταί (*mimētai*, "imitators," 5:1, only NT use of this exact form for God-imitation); φωτὸς / σκότους (*phōtos* / *skotous*, "of light" / "of darkness," 5:8-13); ὑποτασσόμενοι (*hypotassomenoi*, "submitting," the participle of 5:21 that grammatically governs 5:22); κεφαλή (*kephalē*, "head," 5:23); and μυστήριον (*mystērion*, "mystery," 5:32). Two textual witnesses are decisive: P46 (c. 200) and Vaticanus B*, both of which lack the verb in 5:22, requiring the reader to supply ὑποτασσόμενοι from 5:21.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Ephesians 5 is paraenesis from start to finish, but with three distinguishable units: the light/darkness exhortation (5:1-14), the Spirit-filled community ethics (5:15-21), and the household code proper (5:22-33). The light section deploys baptismal-catechetical material throughout. Verse 14 — "Awake, O sleeper, and arise from the dead, and Christ will shine on you" — is introduced by *διὸ λέγει* (*dio legei*, "therefore it says"), the Pauline citation formula, but no extant Old Testament text reads this way. Echoes of Isaiah 26:19, 51:17, 60:1 are real but partial; the consensus from Lincoln (WBC 318-320) through O'Brien (PNTC) through Markus Barth is that this is an early Christian baptismal hymn quoted as if it were Scripture — perhaps the earliest surviving fragment of post-apostolic Christian liturgical poetry. The triadic structure (awake / arise / shine) and the rhythm in Greek both support the hymn hypothesis.

Verse 18-21 is itself liturgical-formative material, repurposing the Dionysian "do not get drunk" topos (cf. Prov 23:31 LXX, but also pagan symposium ethics) as a contrast with Spirit-filling. The four participial phrases that follow — "addressing one another," "singing and making melody," "giving thanks," "submitting" — are all dependent on the imperative "be filled" (πληροῦσθε, *plērousthe*) in v. 18. This is grammatically crucial. The fourth participle, ὑποτασσόμενοι ἀλλήλοις (*hypotassomenoi allēlois*, "submitting to one another"), is the immediate antecedent of v. 22. There is no second imperative verb in 5:22 in the earliest manuscripts.

This is the chapter's central textual problem. P46, Vaticanus B*, the minuscule 6, and the important medieval witness 1739 all lack any verb in 5:22 — the verse depends on the participle in v. 21. Sinaiticus א and Alexandrinus A read ὑποτασσέσθωσαν (*hypotassesthōsan*, "let them submit"), a third-person imperative; later Byzantine witnesses read ὑποτάσσεσθε (*hypotassesthe*, "submit," second person), the form that became standard in printed Greek New Testaments and in most translations. Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* (2nd ed., 1994, ad loc.) judges the shorter reading (no verb) earliest and the verb a later harmonizing addition. Hoehner agrees on the textual judgment while resisting some of the implications. Lincoln (WBC 350-352) draws the implication out: in the earliest text, "Wives to your own husbands as to the Lord" is a sentence fragment whose verb must be supplied from the immediately preceding participle "submitting to one another" — and that participle is governed by the universal imperative "be filled with the Spirit" addressed to the whole community. The mutual-submission readers (Beth Allison Barr, Scot McKnight, Cynthia Long Westfall) lean heavily on this textual fact.

The household code that follows runs to v. 33 and is structured chiastically: wives (5:22-24) — husbands (5:25-30) — Genesis citation (5:31) — mystery declaration (5:32) — summary (5:33). The Genesis 2:24 citation is given according to the LXX; the "mystery" verse, *τὸ μυστήριον τοῦτο μέγα ἐστίν* (*to mystērion touto mega estin*, "this mystery is great"), is the one Jerome's Vulgate famously translated as *sacramentum hoc magnum est* — the verbal hinge on which the medieval and Tridentine doctrine of marriage as sacrament hangs. The Greek *mystērion* in Ephesians (used here and at 1:9, 3:3-9, 6:19) consistently means a previously hidden divine purpose now revealed in Christ; the Latin *sacramentum* picked up a far more specific cultic-ritual freight.

The literary-rhetorical engine of 5:25-30 is the Christ-church analogy, but it runs in both directions. Christ's love is the model for husbands; the church's submission is the model for wives. Within the letter's argument, the chapter completes the move begun in 2:11-22 (the new humanity) and 4:1-16 (the body grown into the head): the church-as-bride is now the lens through which Christian household life is reconceived.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the New Testament canon, Ephesians 5 sits in conversation with at least four trajectories: the marriage logia of Jesus (Matt 19:3-9, Mark 10:2-12, the Genesis 2:24 citation common to both); the household codes of Col 3:18-4:1, 1 Pet 2:18-3:7, and the Pastorals; the bridal imagery of Revelation 19:7-9 and 21:2-9; and the Pauline marriage casuistry of 1 Cor 7. The Christ-church-as-marriage figure is not exclusive to Ephesians — 2 Cor 11:2 already presents the church as a chaste virgin betrothed to Christ — but Ephesians 5 develops it most fully.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Origen's commentary fragments treat 5:22-33 typologically: the soul, like the church, is the bride of the Logos. Chrysostom's Homily 20 on Ephesians is a remarkable pastoral text — he insists husbands love their wives more than themselves, he condemns men who beat their wives, and he reads the Christ-analogy as a constraint on male behavior more than a license for it; whatever Chrysostom's broader patriarchalism, his actual reading is striking. Jerome's commentary, characteristically ascetic, reads the chapter through the lens of his celibacy advocacy. Augustine, in *On the Good of Marriage* (*De Bono Coniugali*, 401) and *On Marriage and Concupiscence*, established the Western framework: the three goods of marriage are *proles* (offspring), *fides* (fidelity), and *sacramentum* (the sacramental bond) — the third grounded explicitly in Eph 5:32.

The medieval West developed the bridal mysticism that runs from Bernard of Clairvaux's *Sermons on the Song of Songs* (c. 1135-1153) through the Victorines, the Rhineland mystics (Hildegard, Mechthild, Eckhart), and into John of the Cross. The *Sponsus-sponsa* tradition reads Ephesians 5 alongside the Song to construct a theology of mystical union; the church corporately and the soul individually are the bride. Aquinas (ST Suppl. Q. 42 a. 1, drawing on lectures since lost) defended marriage as a true sacrament — one of the seven — citing Eph 5:32 as the decisive scriptural warrant. The Council of Trent Session 24 (1563) made this dogmatic against the Reformers: *Si quis dixerit, matrimonium non esse vere et proprie unum ex septem legis evangelicae sacramentis... anathema sit*.

The Reformers split the question. Luther's *Estate of Marriage* (1522) called marriage a worldly-divine vocation but explicitly denied it sacramental status (*Babylonian Captivity*, 1520), arguing that the Vulgate's *sacramentum* mistranslated *mystērion* and that no instituting word of Christ established marriage as a sign-conferring rite. Calvin agreed (*Institutes* IV.19.34-37). Anabaptist tradition (Schleitheim, the Hutterite Chronicle, later Menno Simons) treated marriage as a covenantal-civil bond distinct from the spiritual community. Eastern Orthodoxy never developed the Western seven-sacraments scheme along the same lines; its *Mystery of Crowning* (the marriage rite) is sacramental but framed as a martyrological-eschatological coronation, not a juridical sign-act.

Critical scholarship since F.C. Baur and the Tübingen school treated the Haustafel as evidence of "early catholic" social conservatism — David Balch's *Let Wives Be Submissive: The Domestic Code in 1 Peter* (1981) gave this its mature form: the household codes function as Christian apologetic acculturation, demonstrating to suspicious Roman observers that the new movement did not subvert household order. James Dunn (*The Theology of Paul the Apostle*, 1998) developed parallel arguments for the Pauline trajectory. This thesis remains contested but has become a baseline in the literature.

The contemporary gender battleground is dominated by three positions. **Complementarian:** Wayne Grudem and John Piper, *Recovering Biblical Manhood and Womanhood* (1991), and the Council on Biblical Manhood and Womanhood's Danvers Statement (1987); Andreas Köstenberger, Bruce Ware, and the broader Southern Baptist confessional tradition; Catholic complementarity in John Paul II's *Mulieris Dignitatem* (1988) and the *Theology of the Body* Wednesday audiences (1979-1984). The complementarian reading takes *kephalē* in 5:23 as "authority over" (Grudem's lexicographical work in TJ 1985 and JETS 1990) and treats male headship as an order-of-creation reality reframed but not abolished by Christ. **Egalitarian:** Christians for Biblical Equality (CBE), Linda Belleville (*Two Views on Women in Ministry*), Cynthia Long Westfall (*Paul and Gender*, 2016), Aída Besançon Spencer, Carolyn Custis James. Egalitarians read *kephalē* as "source/origin" (Belleville surveying classical and patristic usage), point to the participle structure of 5:21-22, and read mutual submission as the controlling frame. **Mutual-submission / "biblical womanhood is a recent invention":** Beth Allison Barr (*The Making of Biblical Womanhood*, 2021), Scot McKnight (*The Blue Parakeet*, 2008), Cynthia Long Westfall (*Paul and Gender*, 2016). Barr's historical argument is that "biblical womanhood" as currently marketed is largely a twentieth-century American cultural construction projected back into the text.

N.T. Wright, in *Paul for Everyone: The Prison Letters* (2002) and in *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* (2013), reads chapter 5 as the practical edge of the whole letter's vision: "be imitators of God... walk in love, as the Messiah loved us and gave himself for us" (5:1-2) is the controlling command, and everything that follows — the renunciation of the works of "darkness," the call to "be filled with the Spirit" (5:18) and to overflow in psalms and thanksgiving — is the shape of a community that has been raised with Christ and is meant to be light in the world. On 5:21-33 Wright is emphatic that the household material is introduced by 5:21, "submit to one another out of reverence for the Messiah," so that what follows is not a one-way subordination borrowed from Roman household law but a mutual self-giving worked out in the asymmetrical roles of the day. He reads the husband's "headship" as defined entirely by the cross — the husband is to love "as the Messiah loved the church and gave himself for her" (5:25), which is the language of self-sacrifice, not domination — and he takes the *mystērion* of 5:32 in the Ephesians sense it bears throughout the letter: a hidden purpose now disclosed, namely that human marriage is a living sign pointing to the union of the Messiah and his people, the one-flesh of Genesis 2:24 read as a parable of the church.

A fourth voice that cannot be ignored: the pastoral-forensic literature on domestic violence in the church — Catherine Clark Kroeger and Nancy Nason-Clark, *No Place for Abuse: Biblical and Practical Resources to Counteract Domestic Violence* (rev. 2010), and Ruth Tucker's *Black and White Bible, Black and Blue Wife* (2016). Their concern is not exegetical primarily but pastoral: submission language drawn from Eph 5:22 has been weaponized in some Christian households to silence women experiencing abuse, and the church's reception of the verse cannot be evaluated apart from this history.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** Jewish scholarship has approached the Haustafel from two angles. The first compares the form to Hellenistic-Jewish ethical literature — Philo's *Hypothetica*, Josephus' *Against Apion* 2.199-208, *Pseudo-Phocylides* — and finds the Christian Haustafeln continuous with diaspora apologetic moralism. David Daube's *The New Testament and Rabbinic Judaism* (1956) demonstrated the rabbinic-form roots of much NT ethical paraenesis. Mishnaic-rabbinic marriage ethics, while patriarchal in their own register (the *ketubah* as legal contract, the asymmetry of *get* divorce), are differently structured from the Greco-Roman *Haustafel*: rabbinic discourse is casuistic and case-based rather than honor-coded and abstract. The rabbis legislate husband-wife obligations in detail (the famous list in *Mishnah Ketubot* 5:5-9) in a way that creates legal-mutual obligations — the husband is legally required to provide food, clothing, and conjugal rights; failure renders the marriage unsustainable. Modern Jewish scholars have noted that the rabbinic system, patriarchal in its own register, is differently structured from the Christian Haustafel, which imports Roman household-honor categories that rabbinic Judaism never fully adopted. Where modern Jewish scholarship still pushes back: against the Christ-church-as-marriage figure (5:32) which has historically been used in supersessionist readings (the church as "true Israel/bride" displacing rejected Israel), and against any reading of Genesis 2:24 in 5:31 that would obscure the Jewish exegetical history of the verse.

**Where this leaves us.** The exegetical-textual case for reading 5:22 as governed by 5:21's mutual submission is strong; the historical-cultural case for the chapter as Christianized Haustafel is strong; and the pastoral-forensic case that the verse has been weaponized is unanswerable. Whether complementarian readings can be held with integrity given all of this is the live question.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Marriage hierarchy and gender.** Take the textual fact: P46, Vaticanus B*, and other early witnesses lack a verb in 5:22; the verse depends on the participle ὑποτασσόμενοι (*hypotassomenoi*) in 5:21, which is governed by "be filled with the Spirit" addressed to the whole community. Take *kephalē* in 5:23: Grudem's massive lexicographical defense of "authority over" appeals to a wide sweep of Greek usage; Belleville and Westfall counter with a comparable sweep arguing for "source/origin" or even "prominent member." Steelmanning the complementarian reading I find least intuitive: the Christ-church analogy of 5:23 is not symmetrical. Christ is not "equal partner" with the church; Christ is its Savior. If the analogy holds at all, it carries some asymmetry — and asymmetry is what the complementarian reading names. Mutual-submission readings have to do real work to explain why the analogy is to a distinctly hierarchical relationship (Christ over church) if no hierarchy is in view. Grudem's argument is that the egalitarian reading flattens the analogy until it cannot do work.

The egalitarian counter is that Christ's headship is exercised exclusively as self-giving sacrifice (5:25), so any "headship" the husband exercises must be defined entirely by self-emptying, not by authority claims; in practice, this empties the hierarchy of any operational content. The mutual-submission reader (Bessey, Barr, McKnight) goes further: the controlling logic of the passage is 5:21's *allēlois* (one another), which is reciprocal by definition; *Haustafel* form is being deployed to subvert *Haustafel* content. **Where this leaves us:** the text underdetermines a polity of marriage; what it overdetermines is that any husband whose self-understanding involves authority not exercised as Christ-shaped self-giving has misread the chapter. Whether the residue of "headship" still names something operative or has been wholly consumed by the cruciform redefinition is the question complementarians and egalitarians cannot settle — and meanwhile, the pastoral-abuse data documented by Kroeger, Nason-Clark, and others suggest that the doctrinal abstractions are not the most urgent question.

**Tension 2: Marriage as sacrament.** Eph 5:32's *mystērion* / Vulgate *sacramentum* is the verbal pivot of one of the deepest divides in Christian theology of marriage. The Catholic position, formulated against the Reformers at Trent Session 24 (1563) and elaborated in CCC §§1601-1666, *Familiaris Consortio* (1981), and *Mulieris Dignitatem* (1988), holds that marriage between baptized persons is a true sacrament instituted by Christ, an efficacious sign that confers grace and makes the marriage indissoluble. The Eastern Orthodox tradition holds marriage as a *Mystery* — the Crowning — but frames it eschatologically and martyrologically rather than juridically; the matter of sacramental theology is differently structured. The Reformed position (Calvin, *Institutes* IV.19.34-37; Westminster Confession 24) calls marriage a covenant ordained by God for civil and personal flourishing but explicitly denies sacramental status; the Vulgate's *sacramentum* is a translation error. The Lutheran position (Luther, *Estate of Marriage*; *Babylonian Captivity*) is similar: marriage is a divinely-given estate but not a means of grace. The Anabaptist civil-distinct view (Schleitheim, Menno Simons) treats marriage as covenant, not sacrament, and was historically suspicious of state-church marriage forms.

Steelmanning the Catholic sacramental position against my Protestant instincts: the Reformers' move depends on a hard distinction between *mystērion* (Greek, broad) and *sacramentum* (Latin, technical) that is itself partly a 16th-century reading-back. In Tertullian and Cyprian, *sacramentum* already covered a wide range of sacred-sign realities. Augustine's *De Bono Coniugali* did not invent the marriage-as-sacrament reading from a Vulgate misprint; it grew naturally from the patristic conviction that the Christ-church bond is the *real* of which human marriage is the *image*, and that what makes Christian marriage Christian is its participation in that real — which is the operative grammar of sacrament. The Catholic position is not merely a translation accident; it is a theology of how grace runs through the ordinary. **Where this leaves us:** Protestants who dismiss the Catholic position as "Vulgate-error" are doing bad history; the real question is whether participation in the Christ-church mystery requires sacramental theology to be intelligible, and the Reformed-Lutheran tradition has not decisively answered this.

**One unresolved question:** If "this mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church," then the *primary* referent of 5:32 is the divine relationship and the *secondary* referent is human marriage — but every English Bible reader, every wedding sermon, every Christian household reverses this and reads the passage as primarily about marriage with Christ-church as illustration. Has the church spent two thousand years reading the verse backward, and what would it look like to read it forward?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 2:24** — quoted in 5:31; the verse Jesus also cites in Matt 19:3-9 / Mark 10:2-12; the entire Christian theology of marriage runs through how this OT verse is read, and Eph 5:32 makes the audacious move of saying its primary referent is Christ-church.
- **Song of Songs 4-5** — the OT bridal poetry that grounds the medieval *Sponsus-sponsa* tradition; reading Bernard's sermons alongside Eph 5 shows how the chapter generated mystical theology, not just marital ethics.
- **Revelation 19:7-9 and 21:2** — the marriage-of-the-Lamb and the New-Jerusalem-as-bride passages; the eschatological completion of the Eph 5 figure, and the reason marriage in this age is provisional and signifying rather than ultimate.
- **1 Corinthians 7:1-16** — Paul's own marriage casuistry, with its strikingly mutual rhythm ("the husband does not have authority over his own body, but the wife does," 7:4); this is the closest indisputably Pauline parallel and reads notably more mutual than Eph 5 in its surface form, sharpening the Pauline-vs-deutero-Pauline question.
