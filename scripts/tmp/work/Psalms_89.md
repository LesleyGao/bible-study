# Psalm 89 — Going Deeper

Psalm 89 is the structural hinge of the entire Psalter. It closes Book III in a manner that has stunned readers since antiquity: thirty-seven verses of soaring praise to God's covenant with David — the most extended Davidic-covenant theology in the Hebrew Bible — followed by an abrupt collapse into accusation. "But now you have cast off and rejected; you are full of wrath against your anointed. You have renounced the covenant with your servant; you have defiled his crown in the dust." The psalm ends in unanswered petition. The Davidic king has fallen, the throne lies in ruins, and the covenant that God swore by his own holiness — the covenant the psalmist has just spent thirty-seven verses praising — appears to have failed. Then a single editorial line, "Blessed be the LORD forever! Amen and Amen," closes Book III and waits.

## Lens 1: Historical World

**Superscription.** *Maskil le-Eitan ha-Ezrachi* (מַשְׂכִּיל לְאֵיתָן הָאֶזְרָחִי) — "A Maskil of Ethan the Ezrahite." Ethan is paired with Heman (the named author of Ps 88) in 1 Kgs 4:31 as one of the wisdom figures whose wisdom Solomon surpassed. 1 Chr 6:44 and 1 Chr 15:17–19 list Ethan as one of David's three Levitical chief musicians (alongside Asaph and Heman). The pairing of Pss 88 (Heman) and 89 (Ethan) at the close of Book III is editorial: the two named wisdom-musicians close the book in unresolved lament. Whether the historical Ethan composed Ps 89 is contested (most modern critics treat the attribution as a literary device); what matters for the canonical reading is that the editor placed an Ethan-psalm at this hinge-point.

**Gunkel's form-critical genre.** Hermann Gunkel's classification is famously divided. The first section (vv. 1–37) is a **hymn of praise** combined with a **royal psalm**; the second section (vv. 38–51) is a **communal lament**. The psalm thus crosses two of Gunkel's primary genres. Some early form critics tried to resolve the tension by proposing two originally independent compositions later joined; the contemporary consensus (Kraus, Tate, Hossfeld–Zenger, Goldingay, Mitchell) treats the unity as deliberate. The genre-clash is the theological point. The psalm is built to *break*. It piles up the royal-covenant theology so that the reader feels its full weight, then collapses it so that the reader feels the full weight of its failure.

**Date.** Most commentators place the psalm in the post-587 BC period — after the destruction of Jerusalem and the exile of the Davidic king. The descriptions of vv. 38–45 (the throne cast to the ground, the walls breached, the king covered in shame, his crown defiled in the dust) cohere most naturally with the events of 587 BC and the exile of Jehoiachin or Zedekiah. Hossfeld and Zenger (*Psalms 2*, Hermeneia, 2005) place it in the early exilic or early post-exilic period. A minority view (Tate, in part) allows for an earlier date during one of the monarchic crises (perhaps the Assyrian assault on Hezekiah, or the Egyptian assault on Josiah), but the language of "renounced covenant" fits the catastrophe of 587 better than any earlier crisis.

**ANE parallels.** The royal-covenant theology of vv. 19–37 has close parallels in ANE royal ideology — the king as "son" of the deity, the king's throne established forever, the king as the firstborn over the kings of the earth. Egyptian royal hymns and the Mesopotamian royal cult both speak of the king in such terms. What distinguishes Israelite royal theology is its anchoring in a *covenant* (Hebrew *berit*, בְּרִית) rather than in mere divinization, and in the explicit conditional clause of vv. 30–32 ("if his children forsake my law… I will punish their transgression with the rod") balanced by the unconditional clause of vv. 33–37 ("but I will not remove from him my steadfast love… I will not violate my covenant"). The lament-half of the psalm has parallels in the ANE city-laments (the Sumerian "Lament for Ur," "Lament for Sumer and Ur," "Lament for the Destruction of Eridu") which mourn the fall of cities and the abandonment by the patron deity. Ps 89 stands in that tradition while transforming it — the lament is not over a city's destruction but over the failure of a divine *promise*.

**Hebrew terms.** Several words carry the theological freight.

- *Chesed* (חֶסֶד) — "steadfast love," "covenant loyalty." The word appears seven times (vv. 1, 2, 14, 24, 28, 33, 49), framing the entire psalm. The opening "I will sing of the *chesed* of the LORD forever" (v. 1) and the closing "where is your *chesed* of old?" (v. 49) form an inclusio that traps the entire psalm inside the question of God's covenant loyalty.
- *Emunah* (אֱמוּנָה) — "faithfulness." Paired with *chesed* throughout (vv. 1, 2, 5, 8, 14, 24, 33, 49). The two words together evoke the formula of covenant fidelity (Exod 34:6).
- *Berit* (בְּרִית, vv. 3, 28, 34, 39) — "covenant." The covenant with David is the spine of the psalm.
- *Mashiach* (מָשִׁיחַ, vv. 38, 51) — "anointed one," "messiah." The king is twice called *mashiach*, and the lament's accusation focuses on what God has done to his *mashiach*.
- *Naar* (נֵאַרְתָּה, v. 39) — "to spurn," "to renounce." The verb is rare and shocking in its application: "you have *renounced* the covenant with your servant." This is covenant-breach language used *of God*. The psalmist is accusing God of having broken his own covenant.
- *Mahalaltzta nizro la-arets* (חִלַּלְתָּ לָאָרֶץ נִזְרוֹ, v. 39) — "you have defiled his crown in the dust" / "to the ground." *Chillalta* ("you have profaned") is sacrilege language; the king's crown was holy, and God has profaned it.
- *Bekhor* (בְּכוֹר, v. 27) — "firstborn." "I will make him the firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth." This term is picked up by Heb 1:6 and Rev 1:5.
- *Eitan* (אֵיתָן) — the name of the alleged author means "perpetual," "ever-flowing." There is wordplay between the author's name and the *forever*-language of the covenant.

**DSS / LXX.** Ps 89 is preserved in 4QPsa, 4QPsx, and other Qumran fragments, mostly in agreement with the MT. The LXX (Ps 88 in its numbering) preserves the structure intact. The closing doxology of v. 52 ("Blessed be the LORD forever! Amen and Amen") is the editorial close of Book III, paralleled at the close of Books I (Ps 41:13), II (Ps 72:18–19), and IV (Ps 106:48). All textual witnesses preserve this closing line, confirming its function as a book-marker.

## Lens 2: The Literature

**Lowth's parallelism.** Ps 89 displays all three Lowthian categories at virtuosic length. The hymn-section (vv. 5–18) deploys synonymous parallelism in stacking praise (v. 6: "Who in the skies can be compared to the LORD? Who among the heavenly beings is like the LORD?"). The covenant-oracle (vv. 19–37) uses synthetic parallelism to extend and intensify (vv. 26–27: "He shall cry to me, 'You are my Father…' And I will make him the firstborn…"). The lament-section (vv. 38–45) deploys antithetic parallelism by *implication*: each line of the lament is the antithesis of a corresponding line in the covenant-promise. Where v. 23 promised "I will crush his foes before him," v. 42 mourns "you have exalted the right hand of his foes." Where v. 28 promised "my covenant will stand firm for him," v. 39 mourns "you have renounced the covenant with your servant." The lament does not break with the hymn formally; it negates it term by term.

**Strophic structure.** Most commentators divide Ps 89 into five strophes plus a closing doxology:

1. **Strophe 1 (vv. 1–4):** Opening declaration of *chesed* and the Davidic covenant.
2. **Strophe 2 (vv. 5–18):** Hymn to God's cosmic kingship.
3. **Strophe 3 (vv. 19–37):** The covenant-oracle to David — the most extended Davidic-covenant text in the Hebrew Bible, echoing 2 Sam 7.
4. **Strophe 4 (vv. 38–45):** The lament — the abrupt collapse, the throne in the dust.
5. **Strophe 5 (vv. 46–51):** The petition — "How long, O LORD?"
6. **Doxology (v. 52):** Editorial close of Book III.

The four *selah* markers (vv. 4, 37, 45, 48) reinforce the strophic division. The placement of the third *selah* at the close of v. 45 — at the bottom of the lament's descent — is structurally devastating: the music holds at the moment the throne hits the ground.

**Inclusio.** The psalm opens with *chesed* (v. 1) and closes the petition-section with the question "Lord, where is your *chesed* of old?" (v. 49). The opening boast and the closing question frame the psalm inside the same word, but the word has changed from confession to accusation. The doxology (v. 52) sits outside this inclusio and belongs to the editorial frame of the Psalter as a whole.

**Chiasmus.** Within the covenant-oracle (vv. 19–37), commentators have noted a roughly chiastic structure organized around the unconditional core (vv. 33–35):

- A "I have granted help…" (vv. 19–22)
- B "I will crush his foes…" (vv. 23–25)
- C "I will make him the firstborn…" (vv. 26–29)
- D "If his children forsake my law…" — the conditional clause (vv. 30–32)
- D' "But I will not remove my steadfast love…" — the unconditional clause (vv. 33–35)
- C' "His offspring shall endure forever…" (vv. 36–37)

The chiasm centers on the conditional/unconditional pivot — the most theologically loaded moment of the oracle. The lament that follows (vv. 38–45) takes its accusation directly from this pivot: God has done what vv. 33–35 said he would never do.

**Selah.** Four *selah* markers (vv. 4, 37, 45, 48) — more than in any other psalm except Ps 3. Each marks a structural division and invites the reader to *hold* the moment before moving on.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Book III closes here.** This is the structural claim that Gerald Wilson's *The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* (1985) made central to modern Psalms scholarship. The five-book division of the Psalter is editorial — each book ends with a doxology (Pss 41:13; 72:18–19; 89:52; 106:48; 150). Books I–III plot the rise and tragic collapse of the Davidic-messianic kingdom: Ps 2 ("you are my Son… I will give you the nations"), Ps 18 (the king's victory), Ps 72 (the king's universal reign and the close of Book II), and then through Book III the gathering crisis until Ps 89's lament: "you have renounced the covenant with your servant; you have defiled his crown in the dust." The doxology of v. 52 closes the book on this unresolved cry. David Mitchell's *The Message of the Psalter* (1997) extends Wilson's reading eschatologically: Books IV–V are the canonical answer to Book III's question, and the answer begins immediately with Ps 90 (a Mosaic prayer redirecting from David back to YHWH himself) and Pss 93–99 (the *Yahweh-malak* psalms — "the LORD reigns") which proclaim God's direct kingship as the answer to the failure of Davidic kingship. On this reading, Ps 89 is not the Psalter's despair; it is the Psalter's pivot.

**Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Ps 89 reads the covenant-oracle as the foundation for messianic hope and the lament as the voice of Israel in exile. Rashi reads vv. 38–45 historically as the destruction of the First Temple and the exile of the Davidic king, and reads the petition (vv. 46–51) as the cry of post-exilic Israel awaiting the restoration. Ibn Ezra emphasizes the textual claim that the covenant is *unconditional* and reads the lament as a temporary suspension awaiting messianic fulfillment. Radak (David Kimchi) develops the messianic reading further — the *mashiach* of v. 38 is the historical Davidic king, but the *mashiach* of v. 51 ("the footsteps of your anointed") points eschatologically to the messianic age. Modern Jewish thinkers — Jon Levenson on covenant theology (*Sinai and Zion*, 1985), Walter Brueggemann engaging Levenson on David in *David's Truth* (1985), Nahum Sarna on the structure, Martin Buber on the dialogical "I-Thou," Abraham Joshua Heschel on the prophetic tradition of confronting God — converge on the recognition that Ps 89's accusation against God is the most extreme form of covenant theology, not its abandonment. Liturgically, Ps 89 is read in some Jewish traditions on Tisha B'Av and during periods of mourning the Temple.

**Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Ps 89 (Ps 88 in his Vulgate numbering) reads the entire psalm Christologically. The covenant-oracle of vv. 19–37 is the Father's promise about the Son; the lament of vv. 38–45 is the voice of the Church (or of Christ in the passion) crying out under affliction; the petition (vv. 46–51) is the prayer for Christ's return. Augustine resolves the apparent contradiction between covenant-promise and lament by reading the covenant as fulfilled in Christ rather than in the historical Davidic line. Cassiodorus and Aquinas extend this reading. Luther preached on Ps 89 with characteristic attention to the Christological reading of the covenant-oracle, which he heard as a prophecy of Christ. Calvin (*Commentary on the Psalms*) read the lament historically as the voice of post-exilic Israel and the covenant as fulfilled in Christ. Among twentieth-and-twenty-first-century commentators, Gunkel and Mowinckel (form-critical), Westermann (theological), Kraus (historical), Hossfeld and Zenger (canonical), Goldingay (literary-theological), Tate (Word Biblical Commentary), Wenham (canonical), Brueggemann (the prophetic protest tradition; *David's Truth*), Patrick Miller (theology of the Psalms), William Brown (poetic and pastoral), Beth Tanner (NICOT), and especially David Mitchell (*The Message of the Psalter*) and Gerald Wilson (*The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter*) have made the canonical-shape reading of Ps 89 one of the most generative areas of recent Psalms scholarship.

N. T. Wright treats Psalm 89 as one of the texts that holds the whole problem of Israel's story in a single frame: God's irrevocable promise to David, the catastrophe of exile, and the unanswered "how long?" that the rest of the Old Testament — and, on Wright's reading, the entire Second-Temple period — is waiting to have answered. For Wright (*Jesus and the Victory of God*, *Paul and the Faithfulness of God*) the New Testament's claim is that the resurrection of Jesus is precisely the answer to Psalm 89: the covenant that "seemed renounced" (v. 39) has been kept after all, not by restoring a king in Jerusalem but by raising Israel's Messiah from the dead and enthroning him as the "firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth" (v. 27) — which is why that verse surfaces, transformed, in Romans, Colossians, Hebrews, and Revelation. **NT use.** The covenant-oracle of vv. 19–37 is one of the most frequently cited OT passages in the NT, after Pss 2, 22, and 110. Hebrews 1:5 ("To which of the angels did God ever say, 'You are my Son…'?") draws on Ps 89:26–27 as well as Ps 2:7 and 2 Sam 7:14. Revelation 1:5 calls Christ "the firstborn from the dead, and the ruler of the kings of the earth," directly echoing Ps 89:27 ("I will make him the firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth"). Acts 13:22–23 cites the Davidic covenant tradition that includes Ps 89 in its background. The NT reads the covenant-oracle of Ps 89 as fulfilled in the resurrection of Christ — the firstborn from the dead — and in his enthronement at the right hand of the Father. The lament of Ps 89:38–51, on the NT reading, is the voice of the people awaiting the resurrection that will reverse the throne-cast-to-the-ground.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The Davidic covenant as unconditional vs. conditional.** The internal tension of the psalm itself is between the covenant-oracle's apparent unconditionality (vv. 33–37: "I will not remove from him my steadfast love… I will not violate my covenant… Once for all I have sworn by my holiness; I will not lie to David") and the lament's claim that God has, in fact, renounced the covenant (v. 39). 2 Samuel 7, the originating text of the Davidic covenant, contains the same tension: vv. 14–15 promise that even when David's son sins, "I will not take my steadfast love from him." Ps 89 is built on top of 2 Sam 7's apparent unconditionality and then watches that unconditionality collide with history.

- *The unconditional position* (much classical Christian theology, Augustine, Aquinas, the Reformed covenant tradition) holds that the Davidic covenant was always unconditional in the form God swore it, but its fulfillment was deferred from the historical Davidic line to the eternal Son of David, Jesus Christ. The lament-section of Ps 89 is the cry of those waiting for the fulfillment.
- *The conditional position* (some historical-critical readings, some Jewish readings of the Sinai tradition's primacy over the Davidic) holds that all biblical covenants are conditional in the end — Israel breaks Sinai, the Davidic kings break their conditional clause (vv. 30–32), and the lament is the honest acknowledgment that the conditions were not met.
- *The dialectical position* (Levenson's *Sinai and Zion*, 1985; Brueggemann's *David's Truth*, 1985; much recent scholarship) holds that the canonical witness preserves both the unconditional promise *and* the conditional reality without dissolving either. Ps 89 is the canonical text that holds both at once and lets the tension stand.

The steelman for the dialectical position is that the canon does not resolve the tension. Ps 89 in its canonical form contains both the unconditional oracle and the lament that the covenant has been renounced. The doxology of v. 52 ("Blessed be the LORD forever") does not resolve the tension; it simply closes Book III and waits for Book IV's response. The theological move that the canon makes is not to choose between unconditionality and conditionality but to hold them in eschatological tension.

**Tension 2: Canonical-shape reading vs. historical-critical reading.** Ps 89 is the textbook test-case for the difference between historical-critical and canonical-shape methods.

- *The historical-critical position* treats Ps 89 primarily as a post-587 BC composition mourning the fall of the Davidic line and accuses God of covenant-breach. The psalm's theological content is, on this reading, the cry of a particular historical community at a particular historical moment.
- *The canonical-shape position* (Wilson, Mitchell, Hossfeld–Zenger) treats Ps 89's placement at the close of Book III as theologically decisive. The psalm is not just a historical lament; it is the editorially-positioned hinge that sets up Books IV–V's *Yahweh-malak* response. The historical content is preserved but reframed by the canonical placement.
- *The both-and position* (Brueggemann, Goldingay, much contemporary scholarship) holds that the historical-critical and canonical readings are complementary. The historical situation generated the lament; the editorial placement generated the canonical meaning. Both are operative.

The steelman for the canonical-shape reading is that the editor of the Psalter did something *with* the historical lament. He placed it at the end of Book III. He followed it with the doxology of v. 52. He set up Pss 90–99 as the response. To read Ps 89 only as a historical lament is to miss what the editor did. The strongest argument for the canonical-shape reading is that the order of the Psalter is meaningful, and the order tells a story.

**Tension 3: The unresolved character of Ps 89 alongside Ps 88.** Pss 88 and 89 sit next to each other and together close Book III in unresolved lament. Both end without the customary turn-to-praise; both leave their petitions hanging; both press the limits of biblical lament.

- *The despair-reading position* would treat the canonical placement as the canon's confession that the Davidic-covenant project has failed and the people of God are simply in mourning.
- *The hinge-reading position* (Wilson, Mitchell, the canonical-shape consensus) treats the unresolved character as deliberate setup for the response in Books IV–V. The canon does not despair; it withholds. The withholding is the point.
- *The both-and position* holds that the unresolved character of Pss 88–89 is honored on its own terms *and* prepares for the response. The canon's response is not denial of the lament but a different kind of answer — not the restoration of the Davidic king but the reassertion of YHWH's direct kingship (Pss 93–99) and the return to Mosaic prayer (Ps 90).

The steelman for the hinge-reading is that the Psalter does not stop at Ps 89. The book continues. The doxology of v. 52 is a hinge, not a conclusion. The canonical answer to "you have renounced the covenant" is "the LORD reigns" — and beyond that, in Christian reading, the resurrection of the firstborn from the dead.

**Where this leaves us.** Psalm 89 is the canon's permission to confront God with the apparent failure of God's own promises. It is the theological warrant for honesty. The psalmist has spent thirty-seven verses praising God for the Davidic covenant; he then spends fourteen verses accusing God of having renounced that covenant; he ends in unanswered petition. And the canon receives the prayer, marks it with a closing doxology, and turns the page to Book IV, where the answer begins to unfold — slowly, indirectly, eschatologically. The Christian reader hears in Ps 89 the cry of the church between the times: the covenant that seems renounced *was* renewed in the resurrection of the firstborn-from-the-dead, and the throne that was cast to the ground *was* re-established in heaven, but we are still in the verses between v. 51 and the unwritten resolution. We are in the doxology that closes one book before the next book begins.

**Closing question.** If Ps 89 is the question that Books IV–V are written to answer, and if the Christian reading hears in the resurrection of Christ the canonical answer to that question, what does it mean to live in the long pause between the petition of v. 51 ("they mock the footsteps of your anointed") and the doxology of v. 52 ("Blessed be the LORD forever") — between the lament that God has renounced the covenant and the trust that he has not?
