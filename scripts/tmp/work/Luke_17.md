# Luke 17: The Samaritan Leper and the Days of the Son of Man

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Luke 17 stitches together five distinct units — disciple-teaching sayings on sin and forgiveness (17:1–4), faith and the mustard seed (17:5–6), unworthy servants (17:7–10), the Ten Lepers (17:11–19), and the eschatological discourse on the kingdom and the days of the Son of Man (17:20–37). The historical-cultural background varies sharply across the chapter.

The opening sayings sit in a recognizable rabbinic teaching environment. ***Skandalon*** (σκάνδαλον, "stumbling block / temptation to sin") in 17:1 is a hellenized form of a concept that already had a developed Jewish ethical life — a *mikhshol* in rabbinic Hebrew — referring to anything that causes another to fall into sin. The image of the *millstone* (***mylos onikos***, μύλος ὀνικός, 17:2 — literally "donkey-millstone," the large upper grindstone driven by a donkey rather than the small handheld mill used for daily flour) reflects the heavy rotary mills familiar from village agricultural life; archaeological remains of such mills survive at Capernaum, Chorazin, and across Galilee. The forgiveness saying — "if he sins against you seven times in the day, and turns to you seven times … you must forgive him" (17:3–4) — sets a number that rabbinic tradition recognized as already generous; m. Yoma 5:9 records the dispute over how many times one is required to forgive, with Rabbi Yose b. Hanina's three-times rule the most often cited. Jesus' seven-times exceeds the standard, and Matthew's "seventy times seven" (Matt 18:22) raises it further.

The unworthy-servants saying (17:7–10) draws directly on the everyday economics of the Galilean village. A peasant farmer who owned a single slave (Luke's audience could imagine this; the wealthier hearers could; the small landowners would recognize it as the lifestyle of those just above them) would not invite the slave to recline at table. The Greek ***doulos*** (δοῦλος) here is properly "slave," not "servant" — Joel Green (NICNT) and Fitzmyer (AB) both note that the ESV's softening obscures the social texture. The phrase "we are unworthy servants; we have only done what was our duty" (***achreioi douloi***, ἀχρεῖοι δοῦλοι, 17:10) uses a striking adjective — ***achreios*** literally means "useless," "good for nothing" — which has fueled a long debate (does it mean "unprofitable" in the sense of "not generating extra credit" or "worthless" in the sense of self-deprecating? Fitzmyer favors the former; Bovon notes the term is intentionally jarring).

The Ten Lepers scene (17:11–19) sits in a specific cultural geography. "Between Samaria and Galilee" (17:11) is geographically odd for a Jerusalem-bound traveler — Samaria lies south of Galilee, so to be "between" them while moving toward Jerusalem suggests an east-west path along the border, perhaps via the Beth Shean / Scythopolis corridor. Joel Green and many critical commentators take the geography as Lukan literary-theological framing rather than precise topography — Luke is interested in the Samaritan-Jewish boundary as the parable's stage, and he positions the encounter at exactly the threshold where that boundary becomes acute. ***Lepra*** (λέπρα) in the Septuagint and the Gospels does not denote what we now call Hansen's disease but a range of skin conditions — Levitical *tsara'at* (Lev 13–14) — that produced ritual exclusion from the community. The lepers "stood at a distance" (17:12) because Levitical law required this (m. Negaim 13:7 specifies four cubits, or seventy-two feet upwind). Going to "show yourselves to the priests" (17:14) is the Levitical procedure for re-admission to the community after cleansing (Lev 14:1–32) — the priests did not heal, they certified.

The Pharisees' question about "when the kingdom of God would come" (17:20) reflects a recognized Second Temple debate over the timing and signs of the eschaton — visible across 1 Enoch, the Qumran scrolls (especially the War Scroll, 1QM, with its scripted apocalyptic timetable), and the popular messianism associated with figures like Theudas and the Egyptian (Acts 5:36, 21:38; Josephus *Ant.* 20.5.1, *War* 2.13.5). The "days of the Son of Man" discourse uses a stock pair of judgment images — the flood (Gen 6–8) and the destruction of Sodom (Gen 19) — that were already linked in Jewish apocalyptic literature (Wisdom of Solomon 10:4–8; 3 Maccabees 2:4–5).

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter. ***Basileia tou theou entos hymōn*** (βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ ἐντὸς ὑμῶν, 17:21) — "the kingdom of God within / among / in the midst of you," the most disputed phrase in the chapter (and arguably in Lukan eschatology). ***Apolesthai / sōzein tēn psychēn*** (ἀπολέσθαι / σῴζειν τὴν ψυχήν, 17:33) — "lose / save the life," a central Lukan paradox. ***Eucharistōn*** (εὐχαριστῶν, 17:16) — "giving thanks," the participle that names the Samaritan leper's act and which gives the church its term Eucharist. ***Allogenēs*** (ἀλλογενής, 17:18) — "foreigner," the same term used on the Temple Soreg inscription (the warning stone forbidding Gentiles from entering the inner court, found archaeologically by Clermont-Ganneau in 1871) — a charged word in Jesus' mouth applied to a Samaritan. ***Astrapē*** (ἀστραπή, 17:24) — "lightning," the apocalyptic image of the suddenness of the Son of Man's day.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Luke 17 reads at first as a loose anthology, and many older commentators (most famously Conzelmann) treated it as Lukan compositional padding between the climactic Lost Parables of chapter 15 and the Jerusalem-approach material of chapters 18–19. More recent narrative-critical work (Tannehill, Joel Green, Bovon) has argued for a tighter coherence: the chapter moves from the *interior life of the disciple community* (forgiveness, faith, humility) through a *boundary-crossing healing* (the Samaritan leper) to a *cosmic eschatological horizon* (the days of the Son of Man) — a deliberate widening of frame that mirrors the Travel Narrative's larger pattern of zooming out from individual to community to nation to cosmos.

The Ten Lepers narrative (17:11–19) is uniquely Lukan and is one of the most artfully constructed healing stories in the Gospel tradition. Joel Green notes its symmetry with Naaman the Syrian in 2 Kings 5 — a foreigner cleansed of leprosy, returning to give thanks to the prophet — a typological resonance Luke himself surfaces in 4:27 ("there were many lepers in Israel in the time of the prophet Elisha, and none of them was cleansed, but only Naaman the Syrian"). The Samaritan leper is the chapter's *Naaman moment*. Robert Tannehill's *Narrative Unity of Luke-Acts* reads the scene as one of the Gospel's "foreigner-faith" pearls — paired with the Roman centurion in 7:1–10 and looking forward to the explicit Gentile mission of Acts.

The kingdom saying (17:20–21) is a textual and translational crux. The phrase ***entos hymōn*** is grammatically ambiguous between three readings: (1) "within you" — the kingdom as inward, spiritual reality (the older mystical-Lutheran reading; Tolstoy gave this its most famous title in *The Kingdom of God Is Within You*); (2) "among you" / "in the midst of you" — the kingdom as the present reality of Jesus' ministry (favored by most modern critical commentators — Fitzmyer, Bock, Joel Green, Bovon, N.T. Wright); (3) "within your reach / accessible to you" (Bailey, Joachim Jeremias). The grammar tilts toward (2): Jesus is addressing Pharisees, and "within you" as inward-spiritual is uncomfortable for an audience he elsewhere criticizes. Modern translations have moved decisively toward "in the midst of you" (NRSV, NIV, ESV), but the older "within you" persists in the KJV-tradition and in popular spirituality.

The eschatological discourse (17:22–37) has Synoptic parallels in Matthew 24 and Mark 13, but Luke's redactional choices are distinctive. Luke alone preserves "remember Lot's wife" (17:32) and the doublet of Noah and Lot (Matthew has only Noah). The famous "one will be taken and the other left" (17:34–35) is the textual basis for the modern dispensationalist *rapture* doctrine in its popular form (the *Left Behind* tradition stemming from John Nelson Darby, C.I. Scofield, and Hal Lindsey's *Late Great Planet Earth*). Mainstream critical scholarship — Fitzmyer, Joel Green, N.T. Wright, Dale Allison — reads "taken" as taken in *judgment* (the flood "took them all," 17:27, uses the same verb), making the saying parallel to the destruction-of-the-wicked motif rather than the rescue-of-the-righteous. Snodgrass and Bock allow the rescue reading on contextual grounds. The interpretation matters because it determines whether the verses underwrite a rapture or a judgment-discrimination eschatology.

Textually, 17:36 ("two men in the field; one will be taken and the other left") is omitted in the best manuscripts and is bracketed or footnoted in modern critical editions — it is a harmonization with Matthew 24:40.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish readings.** Amy-Jill Levine's *The Misunderstood Jew* and *Short Stories by Jesus* both push back on the conventional Christian reading of the Ten Lepers as "Jews-vs-Samaritans-vs-Jesus." Her point: nine out of ten healed lepers — presumably Jewish — *did go to the priests as Jesus instructed*, which was the Torah-prescribed response. They were doing the right thing. The Samaritan, *unable* to go to a Jerusalem priest, had to come back to Jesus instead. The narrative is not a slam on Jewish piety but a recognition that the Samaritan's marginal social location made his thanksgiving especially visible. E.P. Sanders and Geza Vermes both read Jesus' attitude toward Samaritans as continuous with prophetic tradition rather than a break from it. On the kingdom-saying, Joachim Jeremias and Fitzmyer note that the rabbinic concept of *malkhut shamayim* (the Kingdom of Heaven) was already a present-tense reality in the rabbinic understanding — accepted through the recitation of the Shema and Torah obedience — and Jesus' "in the midst of you" is intelligible within that framework.

**Christian readings.** The patristic tradition handled the Ten Lepers heavily through allegory. Augustine, in his Sermon 176, read the ten lepers as humanity, the nine as the unbelieving, the Samaritan as the Gentile church. Cyril of Alexandria in his *Commentary on Luke* developed similar typological readings. The chapter's eschatological discourse received massive patristic treatment — Eusebius read it through the lens of the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE; Gregory the Great applied it pastorally; Bede transmitted patristic readings to medieval Europe.

The Reformation pivoted. Calvin in his *Harmony* read the Samaritan's thanksgiving as the Reformation distinctive of *gratitude as the proper response to grace* — the formal structure of the Heidelberg Catechism (guilt, grace, gratitude) is partly informed by such readings. Luther preached on the Ten Lepers many times, treating the Samaritan as the model of justifying faith — receiving healing not at the priests' hand but by direct turn to Christ.

The kingdom-saying (17:21) has a singular interpretive trajectory in the modern period. *The Kingdom of God Is Within You* (Tolstoy, 1894) made it the foundational text of Christian non-resistance, an interior-spiritual reading that shaped Gandhi's satyagraha and Martin Luther King's *agape* politics. Albert Schweitzer in *The Quest of the Historical Jesus* (1906) read Jesus through a thoroughgoing apocalyptic future-kingdom lens, bracketing 17:21 as either a Lukan creation or an exception. C.H. Dodd in *The Parables of the Kingdom* (1935) used 17:21 as the centerpiece of his "realized eschatology" — the kingdom is *already* present in Jesus' ministry. Joachim Jeremias modified Dodd's view to "eschatology in process of realization." Hans Conzelmann's three-epoch scheme made Luke 17 the textual center of Lukan present-kingdom emphasis. N.T. Wright in *Jesus and the Victory of God* synthesizes — the kingdom is genuinely present in Jesus' ministry but has not yet arrived in its fullness.

The eschatological discourse has driven the modern *rapture* tradition. John Nelson Darby in the 1830s, the Scofield Reference Bible (1909), and Hal Lindsey's *Late Great Planet Earth* (1970) and Tim LaHaye's *Left Behind* series (1995–2007) have made 17:34–35 (with 1 Thess 4:17) a load-bearing passage for dispensational pre-tribulational rapture doctrine. George Eldon Ladd in *The Blessed Hope* and N.T. Wright in *Surprised by Hope* have argued from mainstream evangelical and Anglican positions, respectively, that the rapture reading misreads "taken" — and that the more consistent biblical pattern is bodily resurrection at Christ's return rather than a two-stage rapture-then-second-coming.

The Samaritan leper has had unique reception in the Eastern Orthodox tradition as the model of *eucharistia* (thanksgiving) — the verbal root of *Eucharist* itself. The *Philokalia* and the broader hesychast tradition have treated the Samaritan's return as the structural pattern of the spiritual life: receive mercy, return in thanksgiving. Henri Nouwen and Eugene Peterson have both written on this Samaritan as the pattern of doxological living.

Liberation and Mediterranean readings — Justo González (*Luke*, Belief), Kenneth Bailey (*Through Peasant Eyes*), and Virgil Elizondo (*Galilean Journey*) — emphasize the chapter's pattern of foreigner-faith disrupting insider-piety, reading the Samaritan as the displaced and despised whose return to give thanks indicts the silence of the included.

Among contemporary commentators: Fitzmyer (AB) is the standard philological reference; Joel Green (NICNT) on Lukan theology of inclusion and reversal; Nolland (WBC) on redaction; Bock (BECNT) for evangelical reading; Marshall (NIGTC) for grammar; Johnson (Sacra Pagina) for literary artistry; Bovon (Hermeneia) for European integration. Snodgrass surveys the parable units. N.T. Wright's *Luke for Everyone* and his *Jesus and the Victory of God* anchor the eschatological reading.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What does ***entos hymōn*** mean — "within you" or "among you" — and what does the kingdom's mode of presence imply for Christian life?** Three positions:

*Position A — Inward / spiritualized reading.* The kingdom is an interior reality, accessed by the soul's turning to God. Tolstoy, much of pietist and mystical Christianity, and the inward-turn of modern liberal Protestantism all hold this. The reading underwrites a contemplative spirituality and a politics of non-resistance.

*Position B — Among / present-in-Jesus' ministry.* The kingdom is in the midst of the hearers because Jesus and his works are in their midst. This is the dominant modern critical reading (Fitzmyer, Joel Green, Bock, Bovon, Wright) and underwrites a Christological present-eschatology — the kingdom is not yet fully consummated but is genuinely present where Jesus is, and where the church's life witnesses to him.

*Position C — Within reach / accessible.* Bailey's reading: ***entos*** in the Greek papyri sometimes means "within reach of." The kingdom is not interiorized but offered — disposed for response — and the question is whether the hearer will reach for it.

*Steelmanning:* The inward reading rightly registers that the kingdom is not merely external pageantry. The "among you" reading rightly registers that the kingdom is bound to Jesus' bodily ministry and to the church's embodied life. The "accessible" reading rightly registers the active disposition of God's reign as offer rather than imposition. *Where this leaves us:* the kingdom is genuinely present where Jesus is present, and its mode of presence is neither merely external nor merely internal — it is the disclosure, in Jesus' person and acts, of God's reign as both already-here and not-yet-fully-arrived. The Pharisees' question — "when?" — is the wrong question; the right question is "where, and in whom?"

**Tension 2: How should Christians read the eschatological discourse — as judgment-discrimination, rapture, or apocalyptic warning?** Three positions:

*Position A — Rapture / dispensationalist.* "One taken, one left" describes the believer caught up to be with Christ before the tribulation. Darby, Scofield, LaHaye, popular American evangelicalism. The reading underwrites a strong missionary urgency, a particular geopolitics of Israel, and a robust theology of imminent return.

*Position B — Judgment-discrimination.* "Taken" is taken in judgment, as in the flood (17:27); "left" is left as the remnant. Mainstream critical scholarship (Fitzmyer, Joel Green, Wright, Allison, Dale Allison's *The Historical Christ and the Theological Jesus* and *Constructing Jesus*). The reading underwrites a sober expectancy without rapture-machinery.

*Position C — Realized / inaugurated.* The discourse describes the crisis already present in Jesus' ministry and its first-century outcome (the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE), with the "days of the Son of Man" understood as the unfolding of God's reign through Jesus' death, resurrection, and the church's mission. R.T. France, N.T. Wright in *Jesus and the Victory of God*, much of the post-Schweitzer Anglican tradition.

*Steelmanning:* The rapture reading rightly registers that the New Testament does anticipate Christ's return. The judgment reading rightly registers the textual logic of "taken" parallel to the flood. The realized reading rightly registers that 17:25 ("first he must suffer many things and be rejected by this generation") locates the discourse's primary horizon in Jesus' own cross and the immediate aftermath. *Where this leaves us:* the chapter resists the rapture's two-stage scheme, takes seriously a real future consummation, and locates the gravity of the discourse in the Cross-shaped pattern that "whoever seeks to preserve his life will lose it" (17:33). The eschaton is shaped, not delayed, by the Cross.

**Unresolved question:** Why does Luke pair the days of Noah and the days of Lot — and why does Lot's wife receive a one-line warning (17:32) that has no Synoptic parallel? Is it a Lukan emphasis on the danger of looking back, on the immediacy of judgment, or on something else?

## The Thread

Luke 17 looks back to 9:51 (the start of the Travel Narrative — "he set his face to go to Jerusalem"), back to 10:25–37 (the Good Samaritan — paired structurally with the Samaritan leper here as the chapter's foreign exemplar of mercy and faith), back to 4:27 (the Naaman reference — the Samaritan leper as Naaman's New Testament fulfillment), and back to 6:36–38 (the Lukan teaching on forgiveness and mercy). The chapter looks forward to 18:1–8 (the persistent widow and the Son of Man's coming — the eschatological frame extends across the chapter break), forward to 19:11–27 (the Pounds parable, with its "the kingdom of God was to appear immediately" framing — the chapter pair forms an inclusio on the question of kingdom-timing), and forward to 21:5–36 (Luke's longer eschatological discourse). The chapter also looks outward to Genesis 6–8 and 19, and to the prophetic tradition of "remember" (zakhar) — Lot's wife is the negative inversion of the prophetic call to memory.
