# Mark 2: Authority on Earth

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Mark 2 unfolds in Capernaum and along the Sea of Galilee under the rule of Herod Antipas, against the backdrop of a Roman fiscal apparatus that reached down to a tax booth on the lakeside (2:14). Capernaum sat on the Via Maris between Antipas's Galilee and Philip's tetrarchy of Gaulanitis (the Golan); commerce moving across that border generated tolls (*telōnion*, the "tax booth") collected by sub-contractors of the publican system. These men — *telōnai* — were Jewish collaborators with the imperial fiscal machine, despised both for the moral compromise of working with Rome (or with Antipas's parallel system) and for the structural opportunity for over-collection. Cicero (*Against Verres* 2.3) and Philo (*De Specialibus Legibus* 2.93) describe the publicani as a hated class throughout the empire. Levi son of Alphaeus, sitting at his booth in 2:14, occupies a position of social and ritual marginality.

The "house" of 2:1 is most plausibly Simon Peter's — the same house at 1:29 — and the courtyard-house archaeology of first-century Capernaum is critical to understanding the roof scene. Stanislao Loffreda and Virgilio Corbo's Franciscan excavations from 1968 onward exposed the *insula* of black basalt courtyard houses immediately beneath the later octagonal Byzantine church. The construction is documented at Capernaum, Korazim, and Bethsaida: walls of dressed basalt about a meter thick, low single-story rooms surrounding an open courtyard, and a flat roof made of wooden beams (palm or sycamore) overlaid with a lattice of branches and reeds and packed with mud. James Strange and Hershel Shanks have written extensively on first-century Galilean village architecture; the reed-and-mud roof would have been "dug through" (Mark's verb at 2:4 is *exoryxantes*, ἐξορύξαντες — "having dug through, excavated") in the literal sense. A patched roof was a routine repair after winter rains. Luke 5:19 — written for a Hellenistic urban audience — translates the scene into tile-roof imagery; Mark preserves the Galilean village original.

The political geography of chapter 2 also includes Sabbath grainfields (2:23). The wheat harvest in Galilee runs roughly from late April to early June, depending on elevation; the disciples plucking heads of grain (*stachyas*, στάχυας) are practicing the gleaner's right of Deut 23:25 ("If you go into your neighbor's standing grain, you may pluck the ears with your hand"). Pharisees objected not to the gleaning but to the Sabbath labor — plucking, rubbing, winnowing — which Mishnah Shabbat 7:2 will later codify as among the thirty-nine forbidden categories of work (*melakhot*). The historical Pharisaic movement of the 30s CE is best reconstructed through Josephus (*War* 2.162; *Antiquities* 13.171, 18.12), the Dead Sea Scrolls (which polemicize against "seekers of smooth things" — likely Pharisees), and rabbinic sources working backward from the Yavneh academy after 70 CE. E. P. Sanders (*Jewish Law from Jesus to the Mishnah*) has argued that pre-70 Pharisaism was probably less stringent and less dominant than later rabbinic memory suggests; Jacob Neusner (*From Politics to Piety*) traced their evolution from a political party to a piety movement.

Material culture in the chapter is dense. The "bed" (2:4, 11) is a *krabattos* (κράβαττος, a Latin loanword *grabatus* — a poor man's pallet, not a bedstead). Wineskins (2:22) were made from goatskin, sewn shut and tarred at the seams; old skins lost elasticity and burst when fermenting new wine produced CO₂. Garment patching (2:21) reflects the linen-and-wool weaving culture of first-century Galilee preserved in textile finds at Masada and Murabba'at. The "bread of the Presence" (2:26) refers to the twelve loaves laid out on the table in the Holy Place (Lev 24:5–9; Exod 25:30) — a ritual that continued in the Second Temple until 70 CE.

Textual evidence: P45 contains portions of Mark 2; Codex Vaticanus and Sinaiticus give the chapter in continuous form. The famous textual problem of 2:26 — "in the time of Abiathar the high priest" — is not a manuscript variant but a historical-critical crux: 1 Sam 21 names Ahimelech, Abiathar's father, as the high priest who gave David the bread. Some manuscripts in the synoptic parallels omit the name; Mark's text is consistent. Solutions range from "in the section of Scripture about Abiathar" (so France, Marcus, attributing the genitive *epi Abiathar archiereōs* a referential rather than chronological force) to acknowledgment of historical inaccuracy (Hooker, Yarbro Collins). Greek terms worth carrying: ***exousia*** (ἐξουσία, "authority"); ***huios tou anthrōpou*** (υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου, "Son of Man"); ***blasphēmei*** (βλασφημεῖ, "he blasphemes"); ***telōnēs*** (τελώνης, "tax collector"); and ***Sabbaton*** (σάββατον, "Sabbath").

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Mark 2:1 begins what Joanna Dewey demonstrated in her 1973 article (and 1980 monograph *Markan Public Debate*) is a deliberately constructed chiasm: five controversy stories running from 2:1 to 3:6 and forming an A-B-C-B′-A′ structure. (A) The paralytic — forgiveness, healing, the verb "rise" (2:1–12). (B) Levi at the tax booth — eating with sinners (2:13–17). (C) The fasting question — bridegroom, garment, wineskins (2:18–22). (B′) Sabbath grainfields — eating (2:23–28). (A′) The withered hand — healing, the verb "rise," and the plot to kill Jesus (3:1–6). The center (the fasting controversy) is the theological heart: a new age has arrived; old containers cannot hold it. The outer panels are healing stories tied to the verb *egeire* ("rise") — the resurrection-vocabulary Mark plants here will detonate at 16:6.

***Euthys*** continues to drive the prose — at 2:8 ("immediately Jesus, perceiving in his spirit") and 2:12 ("immediately picked up his bed"). Historical present tense is heavy: "they come bringing" (2:3, *erchontai pherontes*), "he says" (2:5, *legei*), "the Pharisees were saying" (2:24). Mark's vivid, kinetic style turns each controversy into a moment witnessed.

The most charged literary feature of chapter 2 is the introduction of the ***Son of Man*** (*huios tou anthrōpou*, υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου) at 2:10 and 2:28 — Mark's first two uses, programmatic. The phrase will appear fourteen times in Mark, always on Jesus's lips, in three semantic clusters: present authority (2:10, 2:28), suffering and death (8:31, 9:31, 10:33–34, 9:9, 9:12, 10:45, 14:21 [twice], 14:41), and apocalyptic vindication (8:38, 13:26, 14:62). The Aramaic background ***bar enasha*** (בר אנשא) can mean simply "human being" or "one like me" or — drawing on Daniel 7:13–14, where "one like a son of man" comes with the clouds and is given everlasting dominion — a transcendent eschatological figure. Maurice Casey (*The Solution to the "Son of Man" Problem*, 2007) argued for an originally generic Aramaic idiom that later Christology elevated; Adela Yarbro Collins (*King and Messiah as Son of God*) and N. T. Wright (*Jesus and the Victory of God*) read the Markan usage as deliberately Daniel-7 inflected; Joel Marcus reads it as a self-designation that resists translation precisely because it draws together humanity, suffering, and divine authority. The fact that Mark's first Son-of-Man saying claims authority "*on earth*" (*epi tēs gēs*, 2:10) deliberately echoes Daniel 7's transferred dominion.

Calling Levi (2:14) is a doublet of the Simon-Andrew-James-John call in 1:16–20: "follow me" — "and rising, he followed him." The repetition is part of Mark's discipleship pattern. A literary curiosity: Mark calls him **Levi son of Alphaeus**; Matt 9:9 calls him **Matthew**. Most modern commentators (Marcus, France, Hooker) accept they are the same person under two names — common in the period — though some (Pesch) keep them separate. Mark 3:18 lists Matthew but not Levi among the Twelve, leaving the question open.

The bridegroom-fasting saying (2:19–20) is the only place in chapter 2 where Jesus speaks of his coming death — "the days will come when the bridegroom is taken away from them" (*hotan aparthē ap' autōn*) — and the verb *apairō* (ἀπαίρω, "to be taken away") carries violent overtones echoing Isaiah 53:8. Even at this early point, Mark plants the cross.

The garment-and-wineskins parable (2:21–22) functions as a meta-comment on the entire chapter: the new and the old cannot coexist; the new wine must have new skins. This is Mark's first parable, and the seed of his entire parabolic theology that flowers in chapter 4.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc behind Mark 2 reaches into the deepest layers of Israel's law and prophecy. The forgiveness of sins by a human figure on earth (2:10) explodes into the priestly system of Lev 16 (the Day of Atonement) and the prophetic promise of Jer 31:34 ("I will forgive their iniquity, and remember their sin no more"). The Sabbath controversy (2:23–28) reaches back to Gen 2:2–3 and Exod 20:8–11 and forward to Heb 4. The bread of the Presence (2:26) draws on 1 Sam 21 and Lev 24, and Jesus's argument is rabbinic *qal va-chomer*: if David ate the consecrated bread in extremity, how much more may the disciples pluck grain.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** The rabbinic Sabbath tradition that develops in the Mishnah (Shabbat) and the Babylonian and Jerusalem Talmuds (with extensive *halakhic* discussion of *pikuach nefesh* — saving life overrides Sabbath) is in dialogue with the issues Jesus raises in 2:23–28. The principle "the Sabbath is given to you, not you to the Sabbath" appears almost verbatim in *Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael* on Exod 31:14 (attributed to R. Shimon ben Menasya): "*lakhem Shabbat mesura ve-lo atem mesurim la-Shabbat*" — "the Sabbath is given over to you, and you are not given over to the Sabbath." Mark 2:27 sits inside a recognizable Jewish halakhic conversation, not against it. Geza Vermes (*Jesus the Jew*) read 2:23–28 as a Jesus operating within Pharisaic-style debate, not breaking from Judaism. Daniel Boyarin (*The Jewish Gospels*) reads the "Son of Man" claim of 2:28 as drawing on Jewish apocalyptic Christology that was already a live option in Second Temple Judaism (he points to the Similitudes of 1 Enoch 37–71). Amy-Jill Levine (*The Misunderstood Jew*; *Jewish Annotated NT*) cautions against the still-common Christian reading that pits "Pharisaic legalism" against "Jesus's grace" — the Pharisees of Mark 2 are not legalists in the Lutheran sense but Jewish renewal advocates with whom Jesus is in dispute *as a Jew*. Abraham Joshua Heschel's *The Sabbath* (1951) rendered the Jewish theology of Sabbath as "a palace in time" — not a burden but a sanctuary — and Heschel's account is the deepest contemporary frame for hearing 2:27.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** Bede's *Commentary on Mark* read the paralytic (2:5) as the soul carried by the four evangelists; the typological-medieval tradition extended this — the four bearers were the four virtues, the roof was the carnal mind to be opened. Origen and Augustine emphasized 2:10 as proof of Jesus's divinity (only God can forgive sins). The Reformation made 2:7 a key Christological text: Calvin (*Harmony*) reads "who can forgive sins but God alone?" as the scribes' inadvertent confession of Jesus's deity. The Reformed tradition through Cranfield reads 2:7 with full force; the Catholic tradition (Aquinas, *ST* III q. 8 a. 1; III q. 84) develops it as the foundation of the sacrament of penance — the priest forgives by Christ's authority extended to the apostles.

Modern critical commentators have read the chiastic structure (Dewey) as evidence of pre-Markan tradition gathered editorially around the central conflict pattern. R. T. France (*NIGTC*) emphasizes the escalating Christology — forgiveness, table fellowship, fasting, Sabbath — each controversy raises the stakes until the Pharisees and Herodians plot to destroy Jesus (3:6). Joel Marcus (*Anchor Bible*) traces the apocalyptic background — the new age has come, and the old structures cannot contain it. Adela Yarbro Collins (*Hermeneia*) emphasizes the Hellenistic-philosophical resonance of Jesus as a teacher with *exousia* contrasted with the *grammateis*. Morna Hooker reads the section as Mark's argument that the gospel is structurally incompatible with the Pharisaic-scribal program.

**Sabbath theology** has produced a long Christian denominational debate. Reformed sabbatarianism (the Westminster Confession 21.7–8) reads the Lord's Day as the Christian Sabbath with full Mosaic-decalogue continuity. Catholic doctrine (*Dies Domini*, John Paul II 1998) emphasizes Sunday as the Lord's Day with proper rest and worship but distinguishes it from Mosaic Sabbath. Orthodox theology preserves both Sabbath (Saturday) and Lord's Day (Sunday) as the "eighth day" of new creation. Seventh-day Adventists (drawing on Ellen White) read the Sabbath as continuing Saturday observance for Christians. The Lutheran tradition reads 2:27–28 as freeing Christians from Mosaic Sabbath altogether (Luther's "Christian Liberty"). Wesleyan-Methodist tradition emphasizes Sabbath rest as a means of grace.

**Anti-imperial readings.** Ched Myers (*Binding the Strong Man*) reads chapter 2 as Mark's narrative of Jesus's strategic confrontation with the "scribal-priestly establishment" — the chiasm climaxes in 3:6 with the political plot, demonstrating that Jesus's program of forgiveness, table fellowship with the marginalized, and Sabbath redefinition is inherently subversive of the temple-and-Torah order. Richard Horsley reads Levi's call from the tax booth as a renunciation of the imperial-collaborator system.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Forgiveness and authority — who has the right?** The scribes' question, "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (2:7) is theologically correct in Second Temple Judaism — sins are forgiven by God, mediated through the Day of Atonement and the temple sacrificial system, not by individual humans. The Catholic position (Aquinas; Council of Trent on penance): Jesus's claim to forgive sins on earth is a claim to divinity; he extends this authority to the apostles (John 20:23) and through them to the priesthood — Mark 2:10 is the foundation of sacramental absolution. The Reformed position (Calvin, *Institutes* III.4.10): Jesus's authority to forgive is divine and not transferable as an institutional power; pastors *announce* forgiveness; God forgives. The Orthodox position: forgiveness is mediated through the church's mystery of confession, not as juridical absolution but as therapy of the soul. The Wesleyan position: forgiveness is received in personal faith; the assurance of pardon is the witness of the Spirit. The Jewish-historical position (Sanders): Jesus is not claiming divinity but acting as a charismatic prophet pronouncing what God has done — comparable to Nathan declaring forgiveness to David (2 Sam 12:13). The anti-imperial reading (Myers): Jesus bypasses the temple-and-priestly mediation system, breaking a monopoly. (One may also note simply with the text that the forgiveness is relational — the paralytic is addressed as "son," *teknon* — not framed as a juridical transaction.) **Where this leaves us:** Mark refuses to soften the offense. The Son of Man — using Daniel 7's apocalyptic title — claims authority *on earth* to forgive, and validates it by visible healing. The reader is meant to be in the position of the scribes for a moment: this is either blasphemy or a divine in-breaking. Steelman the Jewish-historical reading (Sanders, Vermes), and the Christian claim sharpens: not that prophets cannot announce forgiveness, but that this prophet, on this authority, in this proximity to the marginalized, is doing something that exceeds the prophetic category.

**Tension 2: Sabbath — for whom and to what end?** The Pharisees' challenge in 2:24 ("why are they doing what is not lawful on the Sabbath?") and Jesus's response in 2:27–28 ("the Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath; so the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath") have generated centuries of ethical and theological argument. The Reformed sabbatarian position (Westminster): Sabbath remains a moral law of creation; the Lord's Day is its Christian expression with required rest and worship. The Catholic position (*Dies Domini*): Sunday is the Lord's Day, ordered to worship and rest, but not Mosaic Sabbath. The Orthodox position: both Sabbath and Lord's Day are observed, the latter as eschatological "eighth day." The Seventh-day Adventist position: the Sabbath remains binding on Saturday. The Wesleyan position: Sabbath is a means of grace, not legal obligation. The Jewish-rabbinic position (Mekhilta, Heschel): Sabbath is the gift of time to humanity; *pikuach nefesh* (saving life) suspends it; Mark 2:27 is recognizably Jewish. The anti-imperial position (Myers): Jesus reasserts the original purpose of Sabbath as a release from the alienating economic system, against its instrumentalization by elite religious gatekeepers — and the immediate occasion (hungry disciples gleaning) keeps the dispute grounded in the basic question of who is allowed to eat. **Where this leaves us:** Mark uses Sabbath to disclose Jesus's identity — "the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath." If the Sabbath is the deepest gift of God to creation (Gen 2:2–3), and Jesus is its lord, the Christological claim is staggering. Heschel's "palace in time" and Myers's "strategic disengagement from the alienating system" are both true; Mark's Jesus inhabits the palace and reorders its gates.

**Unresolved question:** If the bridegroom is taken away (2:20) and the disciples will then fast — when is "then"? Christian tradition has read 2:20 as predictive of the church age between ascension and parousia. But Mark's structure suggests the bridegroom returns at the resurrection (16:6), or perhaps at the parousia (13:26). Are we, as readers of Mark, fasting now or feasting now — and on what calendar?

## The Thread

Three Markan brackets and one prospective tension are planted in chapter 2. **2:5 / 2:9 / 2:11 — the verb *egeire* ("rise")** — anticipates the resurrection vocabulary at **16:6 (*ēgerthē*, "he is risen")**. The paralytic rising and walking is Mark's first staged enactment of the gospel's central promise. **2:10 — the first Son of Man saying** — initiates the Son-of-Man arc that runs through **8:31 (the suffering Son of Man), 13:26 (the apocalyptic Son of Man), and 14:62 (the Son of Man at the right hand)**. Watch that arc converge at the trial scene. **2:20 — "the bridegroom is taken away"** — plants the passion seed; pairs with **14:7 ("you will not always have me"), 14:24 ("my blood of the covenant"), and 15:37 (Jesus's death)**. **3:6 — the plot to destroy Jesus** is set up by chapter 2's escalating controversies; the Pharisees and Herodians (a strikingly improbable coalition — religious traditionalists with Antipas's political operatives) will reappear at 12:13 and ultimately at the cross. The new wine of 2:22 demands new wineskins; the gospel's question by chapter 16 will be whether anyone — disciples, women, readers — has skins flexible enough to hold what is happening.
