# Ruth 2: The Field That Happened to Be Boaz's

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens by introducing Boaz with a phrase loaded with social meaning: he is *ish gibbor hayil* (אִישׁ גִּבּוֹר חַיִל) — most often translated "a worthy man" or "a man of substance," but the phrase normally describes a warrior of valor (the same construction is used of Gideon's army in Judges 6:12 and of David's mighty men). Applied here to a landowner in peacetime Bethlehem, it signals capability, standing, and economic substance — the kind of man who can give protection and command labor. The narrator's framing tells us before the action begins that the encounter on the road we are about to witness is structurally weighted in his favor. The relational identifier — *moda* (מוֹדָע), "relative" of Naomi's husband, "of the clan of Elimelech" — is the legal hook on which the rest of the book will hang. Israelite kinship in the patrilineal Iron Age was organized in concentric circles: *bayt-av* (father's house, the immediate household), *mishpachah* (clan, the extended kinship group), *shevet* (tribe). The clan was the operative unit for land redemption, levirate marriage, and blood vengeance — duties that fell on adult male relatives in a graded order of nearness.

The chapter's central institution is **gleaning**. Israelite agricultural law in Leviticus 19:9-10 and 23:22, repeated and extended in Deuteronomy 24:19-22, prescribes that a landowner must not reap the corners of his field, must not gather what falls during reaping (*leket*), must not return for forgotten sheaves (*shikhechah*), and must not strip the vineyard or olive grove bare (*peret* and *olelot*). What is left belongs to the poor, the widow, the orphan, and the *ger* (גֵּר, the resident foreigner). This is not charity in the modern sense — it is a structural redistribution legally embedded in the agricultural calendar, a tax on the land paid in the form of unharvested margin. Carol Meyers, in her work on Israelite household economy, has shown how the labor itself fell into the picture: gleaning was difficult, low-status, and often dangerous, performed disproportionately by those without a household to draw on for protection — widows, orphans, and resident foreigners. The legal protection in Deuteronomy 24:19 ("when you reap your harvest in your field and forget a sheaf in the field, you shall not go back to get it") presumed that the poor would actually be there to take it, but enforcement was social and uneven. A widow gleaning in a stranger's field was visibly vulnerable.

Boaz's instructions to his young men in 2:9 — "Have I not charged the young men not to touch you?" — make the danger explicit. The Hebrew verb *naga* (נָגַע), "to touch," is a euphemism that ranges from harmful contact to sexual assault (Genesis 20:6, Proverbs 6:29). Naomi's later warning at 2:22 — "lest in another field you be *paga* (פָּגַע) you" — uses a different verb that more directly means "fall upon, assault." The threat is named, not implied. The chapter's quiet drama is the construction of a circle of protection around a foreign woman who, on her own, would have no recourse. Ellen Davis emphasizes the way the legal institution and the personal generosity of Boaz are doing different but overlapping work.

The harvest itself is concrete. Barley harvest in the Judean hill country runs from late April through early May (the Hebrew month of *Aviv*/*Nisan* into *Iyar*); wheat harvest follows through May and into June. The total harvest season lasts roughly seven weeks, the period from Passover to Shavuot — and it is not coincidental that Ruth is read at Shavuot in the Jewish liturgical calendar. Reaping was done with an iron sickle (Iron Age I-II bronze had largely given way to iron tools by the period the story is set in), the cut grain bundled into sheaves, then carted to the threshing floor. The "roasted grain" (*qali*, קָלִי) Boaz passes to Ruth at 2:14 is a specific delicacy: barley parched over hot stones, eaten as a high-energy field food (cf. 1 Samuel 17:17, 25:18, 2 Samuel 17:28). The "vinegar" (*chometz*, חֹמֶץ) into which she dips her morsel is sour wine — a working-class drink, the same mixture later offered to the dying Jesus. The "ephah" of barley Ruth carries home (2:17) is approximately 22 liters or 13-15 kilograms — a striking amount for a single day's gleaning, possibly two weeks' worth of food, signaling Boaz's deliberate intervention beyond legal minimum.

The material culture of gender, labor, and shelter is also legible. Ruth works in the open field through the heat of the day with only "a short rest" (2:7); the unmarried young women (*na'arot*, נְעָרוֹת) under Boaz's authority work in the same field but with informal protection. Water was drawn from a spring or well by Boaz's young men into vessels — and Boaz's offer that Ruth drink from those vessels (2:9) inverts the betrothal type-scene at the well (Genesis 24, Jacob and Rachel at Genesis 29, Moses and the daughters of Jethro at Exodus 2): in those scenes, the woman draws water for the man. Here, the man arranges water for the woman. Robert Alter has emphasized this inversion as one of the chapter's most pointed literary signals.

A few Hebrew terms transform the chapter when you can hear them. ***Hesed*** (חֶסֶד), again, returns at 2:20, when Naomi blesses Boaz: "May he be blessed by YHWH, whose *hesed* has not forsaken the living or the dead." The grammatical ambiguity is theological: is the *hesed* Boaz's, or YHWH's? Most commentators (Bush, Campbell, Hubbard) read it as deliberately doubled — Boaz's *hesed* is the medium through which YHWH's *hesed* is enacted. ***Go'el*** (גֹּאֵל), "redeemer" or "kinsman-redeemer," is the chapter's other key term, introduced at 2:20 as Naomi's first explicit naming of the legal possibility that will drive the rest of the book. The *go'el* in Israelite law was a near male relative obligated to (1) buy back land that had been alienated from the clan (Lev. 25:25-28), (2) buy back a relative who had sold himself into debt slavery (Lev. 25:47-49), (3) execute blood vengeance for a murdered relative (Numbers 35:19-21), and in some traditions (4) provide an heir for a deceased relative (the levirate, *yibbum*, of Deut. 25:5-10, though formally a different institution). Ruth's pivot at 2:20 fuses *go'el* and *yibbum* in a way the legal codes don't quite anticipate; the chapter is preparing the reader for a legal innovation. ***Kanaph*** (כָּנָף), "wing" or "skirt" or "corner of the garment," appears at 2:12 in Boaz's blessing — "under whose wings (*kenafav*) you have come to take refuge" — invoking the maternal-divine image of YHWH as the bird sheltering its young (cf. Deuteronomy 32:11, Psalm 17:8, 36:7, 91:4, Matthew 23:37). The same word will return at 3:9 when Ruth asks Boaz to spread the corner of his own garment over her — the human and divine shelter knit together by a single Hebrew word. ***Hen*** (חֵן), "favor" or "grace," is the word Ruth uses three times in the chapter (2:2, 2:10, 2:13) — she is constantly aware that her standing is contingent on the goodwill of others. ***Nokhriyah*** (נָכְרִיָּה), "foreigner," is the word she uses of herself at 2:10 — a more distancing term than *ger*; she names her own outsider status before Boaz can.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Ruth 2 is structurally a single scene flanked by two short conversations — Ruth and Naomi at the start, Ruth and Naomi at the end. The center of the chapter is the encounter in the field. Within that encounter, the conversation between Boaz and Ruth (2:8-13) sits at the literary heart, framed by his arrival (2:4-7) and the meal (2:14-16). The chapter's pacing is unhurried, almost daily; the narrator slows the action to let the texture of Ruth's day register.

The chapter is best read as a deliberate inversion of the **biblical betrothal type-scene** identified by Alter in *The Art of Biblical Narrative*. The classic pattern (Genesis 24, 29, Exodus 2): a man journeys to a foreign land, meets a young woman at a well, water is drawn, news is brought home to the family, a betrothal follows. Ruth 2 systematically rearranges every element. The foreign one is the woman, not the man. The setting is a field, not a well. Water is offered by the man to the woman. The "betrothal" is not announced; the chapter ends with Ruth simply living with her mother-in-law. The reader who knows the type-scene feels the weight of expectation deferred; the formal incompleteness is itself the storytelling. The fulfillment will not come for two more chapters.

Within the conversation, the **dialogue is finely structured**. Boaz initiates with deference ("my daughter," 2:8), grounds his protection in legal authority ("have I not charged the young men"), invokes YHWH's blessing on Ruth ("the LORD repay you," 2:12), and offers her shelter under YHWH's wings. Ruth responds with self-naming as a foreigner (2:10), then rebalances at 2:13 with a striking move: "you have spoken to the heart of (*al-lev*, עַל-לֵב) your servant, though I am not one of your servants." The Hebrew "to the heart" is a phrase used elsewhere of comforting speech to a grieving or disgraced person (Genesis 34:3 of Shechem to Dinah, with chilling resonance; Genesis 50:21 of Joseph to his brothers; Hosea 2:14 of YHWH to Israel). Ruth is naming what Boaz has done as a specific act of speech, and gently insisting on the gap between his framing of her as already incorporated and the legal reality that she is not.

The narrator's wordplay around Ruth's "happening" upon Boaz's field at 2:3 is famous. The Hebrew is *vayiqer miqreha* (וַיִּקֶר מִקְרֶהָ) — "her chance chanced upon" — a tautological idiom that the narrator deploys with manifest irony. Edward Campbell's commentary, Frederic Bush, and Jack Sasson all note the same point: the doubled language of accident calls attention to itself precisely because the reader knows it cannot be accident. The book's theological method — providence operating through what looks like contingency — is here at its most explicit. Robert Alter calls it "studied artlessness." The Talmud (Bava Batra 91a-b) reads the same passage as evidence of divine providence concealed in apparent randomness.

The chapter sits at a precise structural position in the book. Chapter 1 ends with arrival; chapter 4 will end with redemption and genealogy. Chapter 2 introduces Boaz and the legal possibility of redemption; chapter 3 will press that possibility to its limit. The book's four chapters are tightly chiastic: chapters 1 and 4 frame departures and arrivals across thresholds; chapters 2 and 3 stage encounters between Ruth and Boaz, the first by daylight in the field and the second by night at the threshing floor. Stephen Bertman in 1965 (*JBL*) identified the chiastic structure formally; later commentators have refined it. Ruth 2 and Ruth 3 are the inner panels of the diptych.

There are no major textual variants of significance in the chapter. The DSS Ruth fragments (4QRuth-a, 4QRuth-b) preserve scraps of chapters 1, 2, and 4, with no major divergence from the Masoretic text. The Targum on Ruth (composed in Aramaic, probably 7th-8th c. CE in Palestine) substantially expands Boaz's speech and reads Ruth's gleaning as a religious test orchestrated by heaven.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, Ruth 2 sits at the precise narrative hinge between exile and restoration: the famine has ended, the harvest has come, but the means by which Naomi's family will be restored to land and progeny is not yet visible. The chapter introduces the legal mechanism (the *go'el*) that will resolve the plot, but it does so in a single deferred clause at 2:20. Most of the chapter is about something quieter: a woman finding food. The narrator is using the agricultural calendar as the pacing of grace.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** *Ruth Rabbah*, the rabbinic midrash, expands the chapter substantially. *Ruth Rabbah* 4 reads Boaz as a model of righteousness: his greeting to the reapers ("the LORD be with you") becomes the basis for the rabbinic ruling that one must use the divine name in greetings (Berakhot 9:5 in the Mishnah; cf. Yerushalmi Berakhot 9:5). The midrash also dramatizes Ruth's gleaning as a series of small tests — she gleans modestly, sits modestly, eats modestly — each of which Boaz observes and approves. Rashi follows this midrashic line closely, adding philological notes (he glosses *qali* as roasted barley specifically). Ibn Ezra is more textually focused, attentive to the legal background of *go'el* and to the precise meaning of the rare verbs. The medieval Jewish exegete David Kimhi (Radak, 12th-13th c. Provence) emphasizes the legal precision of the chapter's vocabulary. The Zohar (medieval kabbalistic) reads the *kanaph* (wing/skirt) at 2:12 as a sefirotic image: Ruth taking refuge under the wings of the Shekhinah, a feminine divine presence. Avivah Zornberg in modern Jewish reading attends to the way the chapter slowly reconstructs Naomi's capacity to bless — by 2:20 she can name YHWH's *hesed* again. The chapter is also visibly driven by Ruth's own initiative ("Let me go to the field," 2:2): Ruth acts while Naomi, still in her grief, is largely passive, and the chapter ends with Naomi's first imperative voice returning at 2:22.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic Christianity read Boaz typologically as a figure of Christ — already in Origen's homilies on Joshua (where the genealogical line is traced) and developed in Augustine's *Quaestiones in Heptateuchum* and Jerome's letters. Ambrose in *De Officiis* extolled Boaz as a model of Christian *liberalitas* (generosity beyond legal minimum). The medieval *Glossa Ordinaria* compiles these typological readings. Bernard of Clairvaux's sermons developed Boaz as a figure of divine condescension, and Ruth's "happening upon" his field as the soul's encounter with Christ. Aquinas treated the chapter sparingly, mainly as legal example for almsgiving. The Reformation shifted the emphasis. Luther's prefaces praised the chapter as evidence of God's care for the poor through ordinary kinship structures rather than miraculous intervention. Calvin's sermons on Ruth (1554-55) gave sustained attention to chapter 2, treating gleaning as a paradigm of how civil law and personal *pietas* together create social welfare. Calvin's Geneva had instituted poor relief structures (the *bourse française*) that drew on similar logic; his sermons on Ruth were partly an indirect commentary on contemporary social policy. The critical period (Wellhausen, Gunkel, Hermann Gunkel's form-critical work) treated the chapter mainly as ethnographic-legal evidence about Iron Age Israelite gleaning. The contemporary turn has been largely economic and agrarian: Norman Gottwald and Roland Boer on the political economy of gleaning, and Carol Meyers on the household labor that the legal margin presumes. Brueggemann's *Ruth: A Conversation* foregrounds the chapter's quiet politics. Ellen Davis in *Scripture, Culture, and Agriculture* treats Ruth 2 as a paradigm of biblical agrarian theology. André LaCocque's *Ruth: A Continental Commentary* situates the chapter in the post-exilic anti-Ezra reading.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Both traditions recognize Boaz's *hesed* as exceeding legal minimum and read the chapter as a paradigm of how generosity transforms law. They diverge on what to do with Ruth's foreignness. Christian typological reading often subsumes her foreignness into Gentile incorporation; Jewish reading more often holds her foreignness in productive tension with her Israelite loyalty, reading her as the proselyte who chooses rather than the Gentile who is grafted. Both traditions have been challenged in the late 20th century to attend to the economic and material dimensions of gleaning that earlier readings often spiritualized. The shift is not a rejection of theological reading but a refusal to let theological reading erase the material.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author of Ruth believe God was doing in this chapter? The theological argument is again indirect. YHWH does not act. YHWH is invoked — by the reapers' greeting, by Boaz's blessing of Ruth, by Naomi's blessing of Boaz — but the agency in the chapter is human, and the providence is hidden inside what looks like coincidence. The narrator's *vayiqer miqreha* ("her chance chanced") at 2:3 is the theological method named: the divine works through what cannot be proven to be more than accident. The book is making an argument about how God acts that runs against the more public theology of theophany, oracle, and miracle that dominates much of the Hebrew Bible. The argument is that *hesed* is the medium of providence, and that *hesed* is something humans do.

**Tension One: Is providence the same as coincidence properly interpreted?** The chapter forces the question. Ruth's gleaning happens to land her in the field of the one man who is both kin and capable of redeeming. The narrator names the chance and lets the reader weigh it.

The Catholic reading, especially through Aquinas's account of providence (*ST* I q. 22), treats divine providence as the universal rational ordering by which God moves all secondary causes to their ends without violating their proper causality. Ruth's "chance" is genuine secondary causation — she did walk into a particular field — and is also genuinely providential, because God's wisdom orders the contingent. The two descriptions do not compete. Catholic teaching on grace and freedom (the De Auxiliis controversy of the 16th-17th centuries between Banez and Molina) developed sophisticated frameworks for holding the two together.

The Reformed Protestant reading, especially in Calvin's *Institutes* I.16-18 and in his Ruth sermons specifically, treats every "chance" as the immediate operation of divine governance. There is no contingency to God; what looks accidental is in fact directly willed. The chapter is, on Calvin's reading, evidence of the meticulous providence that orders even the steps of a foreign widow gleaning in a field. The Westminster Confession (1647) follows: God's providence "ordereth them to fall out, according to the nature of second causes, either necessarily, freely, or contingently" (V.2).

The Wesleyan and Lutheran readings stand somewhere between, with Wesleyan synergism resisting Reformed monergism and Lutheran two-kingdoms theology distinguishing God's hidden and revealed wills.

The Jewish tradition reads more variously. The Talmudic tradition (Bava Batra 91a-b) treats the *vayiqer* as evidence of divine action concealed in apparent accident, with a strong sense that pious behavior creates the conditions for providence to act — Ruth's *hesed* makes her *miqreh* possible. Maimonides in *Guide* III.17-18 develops a more philosophically restrained account: divine providence attaches to the rational soul, and the more the soul is perfected, the more it falls under direct divine care. Ruth's care for Naomi makes her a recipient of divine providence in Maimonides' framework. The kabbalistic tradition reads the *miqreh* through the language of *yichudim*, the unifications of divine attributes that righteous human action enables.

The contemporary critical reading often resists strong providentialism. Brueggemann reads the chapter as a story about how human agency under conditions of vulnerability can sometimes make space for grace; the narrator's *miqreh* is irony that gestures at providence without claiming it as a system. LaCocque emphasizes that the providence in Ruth is "without revelation" — there is no oracle, no prophet, no miracle; the theology has to be reconstructed from the human acts.

The position to steelman is Calvin's. It is the strongest claim and the one most resisted by modern sensibilities. But Calvin's reading honors what the narrator has actually done with *vayiqer miqreha*: the doubled language of chance is used precisely because the chapter is *not* describing chance. To read the providence as merely a literary trope and not a metaphysical claim is to soften what the text is doing. Calvin sees that the narrator is making a theological argument, and he refuses to make it less than it is.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter's theology of providence is harder to soften than it looks. The narrator's wordplay forces the reader to choose: either the doubled language of chance is ironic, in which case the chapter is making a theological claim about hidden divine action, or the chance is genuinely chance, in which case the narrator's wordplay is empty. The text doesn't allow the third option of a comfortable middle.

**Tension Two: Boaz's protection — paternalism, structural welfare, or both?** Boaz's intervention exceeds the legal minimum of gleaning. He instructs his men not to "touch" Ruth, has them deliberately leave additional grain for her, feeds her at the workers' meal, and identifies her as under his patronage in front of his entire workforce. The chapter is, depending on how you frame it, either a moving account of voluntary generosity exceeding obligation, or a study in how a protective structure simultaneously shelters and subordinates the one it protects.

The traditional reading (Jewish, patristic Christian, Reformation Protestant) takes Boaz's *hesed* as straightforwardly admirable. He is the model patron, the *gibbor hayil* who uses his standing to extend protection beyond what law requires. The vocabulary of *hesed* in 2:20 makes clear that the narrator approves.

A second reading — pressed by commentators attentive to the chapter's social mechanics, including Brueggemann and André LaCocque — does not deny the goodness of Boaz's action but insists on naming what it is: the protection a powerful man extends to a vulnerable woman who has no other recourse. The structure of the chapter — Ruth must be touched only with permission, drink only what Boaz authorizes, glean only in the field he designates — is one of dependent inclusion. The chapter solves Ruth's immediate problem without changing the conditions that make her dependent. Her foreignness, on this reading, is incorporated only through the patronage of the local powerful, and the cost — already paid in chapter 1 — is the loss of her own people and gods.

A third position, developed by Ellen Davis and Carol Meyers, reads the chapter as a study in how legal structure and personal *hesed* are mutually dependent. Without the law of gleaning, Boaz's generosity would be optional and arbitrary; without Boaz's *hesed*, the law would be enforced only to its minimum and the actual outcome for a Moabite widow would be hunger. The chapter is the case study of why both — formal redistributive structure and informal generosity — are needed, and why neither alone is sufficient.

The position to steelman is the second one. It is sometimes dismissed as anachronistic, but it is asking the right question: what does the chapter actually achieve for Ruth, and at what cost? The answer is honest. Ruth survives the harvest season; she does not become a free agent. She has food; she does not have land. The book will go on to address the unfinished business in chapters 3 and 4, but chapter 2 itself does not.

**Where this leaves us:** Boaz's *hesed* is real, and so is the asymmetry it operates within. The chapter does not resolve the tension between voluntary generosity and structural justice; it lets both visible forms of provision — the law and the man — work together without claiming either is sufficient. That is itself a theological claim.

The unresolved question this chapter leaves us with: When the providence we are asked to recognize operates entirely through the agency of someone who already holds power, what work is the language of providence doing — recognizing grace, or naturalizing the asymmetry through which grace happens to flow?

## The Thread

**Leviticus 19:9-10 and Deuteronomy 24:19-22.** The gleaning laws are the legal undergirding without which Ruth 2 cannot happen. Israelite agricultural law treats the margin of the field as belonging by right to the poor, the widow, the orphan, and the foreigner. Ruth 2 is the narrative case study of those laws actually working — and of the human generosity that turns minimum compliance into genuine welcome.

**Genesis 24.** The classic betrothal type-scene: Abraham's servant journeys to Aram, meets Rebekah at the well, water is drawn, news is brought home, a betrothal follows. Ruth 2 is the deliberate inversion. When you reread Genesis 24 with Ruth in mind, notice how thoroughly the elements have been reorganized: foreigner now female, well now field, water offered now reversed.

**Psalm 91:4 (and 17:8, 36:7, 57:1, 61:4, 63:7).** "He will cover you with his pinions, and under his wings you will find refuge." The image of YHWH as the bird sheltering the vulnerable — *kanaph* — is the same word Boaz uses to bless Ruth at 2:12 and that Ruth will use to ask Boaz to spread his garment over her at 3:9. The human and divine sheltering are knit together by a single Hebrew word; when you read the wing-of-God psalms, you are reading the vocabulary Ruth's marriage will rest on.

**Matthew 23:37 and Luke 13:34.** "How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!" Jesus invokes the same *kanaph* image of YHWH sheltering Israel — now of himself — in lament over Jerusalem. Ruth 2:12 is a quiet Hebrew Bible source for one of the New Testament's most maternal images of divine longing.
