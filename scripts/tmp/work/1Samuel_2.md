# 1 Samuel 2: The Song That Inverts Everything, and the Priests Who Forgot

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter divides into two unequal halves: a poetic hymn placed in Hannah's mouth (vv. 1-10) and a prose narrative about the corruption of Eli's sons and the prophecy against his house (vv. 11-36). Both halves are deeply embedded in the material life of the late tribal period — the sacrificial economy of Shiloh, the priestly perquisites that made the priesthood a lucrative inheritance, and the political theology of the Iron I sanctuary that was about to collapse.

Hannah's Song is widely dated by linguistic and prosodic markers as one of the oldest extended poems in the Hebrew Bible. The vocabulary is archaic — *qarni* ("my horn"), *tsuri* ("my rock"), *yarem qeren mashiḥo* ("he will exalt the horn of his anointed") — and the parallelism is the tight bicola of early Hebrew verse rather than the more developed forms of post-exilic poetry. David Noel Freedman, in studies of early Hebrew poetry, placed it alongside the Song of the Sea (Exod. 15), the Song of Deborah (Judg. 5), and David's psalm in 2 Samuel 22 as a corpus of pre-monarchic or early-monarchic Hebrew song. Frank Cross dated it similarly. The mention of "his king, his anointed" in v. 10 — without naming a specific king — has been read either as a sign that the poem antedates monarchy and prophesies it, or as a sign that the poem is a generic royal hymn later attributed to Hannah by the editor who wanted a programmatic opening to the book of Samuel. McCarter (Anchor Bible) takes the second view: the song was an existing royal psalm pressed into Hannah's mouth, and the editor's brilliance was in recognizing that the inversion-theology of the song matched Hannah's situation perfectly.

The prose narrative gives us a remarkable window onto sacrificial economy. Verses 13-16 describe a "custom of the priests" at Shiloh: while the offerer's meat is boiling, the priest's servant comes with a three-pronged fork (*mazleg shelosh ha-shinayim*) and takes whatever the fork brings up. This violates the Levitical regulation (Lev. 7:31-34) that the priest receives only the breast and right thigh, after the fat is burned to YHWH first. The Eli-priesthood at Shiloh was taking meat *before the fat went up* and taking it raw rather than after it had been properly offered. This is not a minor liturgical violation; it is a breach of the central principle that YHWH eats first. The narrator's phrase *na'atsu ha-anashim et minḥat YHWH* ("the men treated the offering of the LORD with contempt," v. 17) is the same verb used of the most serious cultic offenses elsewhere.

Worse, v. 22 adds that Hophni and Phinehas "lay with the women who were serving at the entrance to the tent of meeting." This single verse is missing entirely from 4QSam-a and from some LXX manuscripts, and McCarter, Klein, and most critical commentators argue it is a later moralizing expansion. The shorter text (no sexual scandal) preserves only the ritual offense — which is itself sufficient. The serving women at the entrance to the *ohel mo'ed* are mentioned again in Exod. 38:8, where they donate their bronze mirrors to make the laver — a dignified cultic role that the MT reading of 1 Sam 2:22 sexualizes as the site of priestly predation. Whether one accepts the verse as original or sees it as an anti-Elide expansion, its function in the present text is clear: it puts Hophni and Phinehas in the same priestly-sexual offense category as the apostates of Numbers 25 (Phinehas grandson of Aaron, ironically, was the one who *prevented* such an offense).

The "man of God" who comes to Eli (vv. 27-36) speaks the longest prophetic oracle in the early chapters of Samuel. He invokes the Egypt-revelation tradition ("did I indeed reveal myself to the house of your father when they were in Egypt"), implying that the Elide priesthood traces its legitimacy through Aaron — a connection the text never makes explicit elsewhere but which Cross and Halpern have argued is the historical reality: Eli's house at Shiloh was Aaronide. The promise of "a faithful priest" (*kohen ne'eman*) in v. 35 has been read variously as referring to Samuel (who is functioning priestly in the next chapters), to Zadok (who replaces Abiathar — the surviving Elide — under Solomon, 1 Kgs. 2:26-27, 35), or messianically to a future eschatological priest. Most critical scholars take it as deuteronomistic foreshadowing of the Zadokite priesthood that the Jerusalem editors favored.

A few Hebrew terms worth lingering over. **קֶרֶן** (*qeren*, "horn") in vv. 1 and 10 frames the song with an animal-strength metaphor that becomes the key metaphor of royal anointing throughout the Bible — Samuel will anoint Saul and David from a horn (*qeren shemen*), and the messianic horn (*qeren yeshu'a*, "horn of salvation") echoes through the Psalms and Luke 1:69. **מָשִׁיחַ** (*mashiaḥ*, "anointed") in v. 10 is the first occurrence of this term in the Former Prophets in the royal sense — the song is laying down the technical vocabulary of monarchy before there is a king. **בְּלִיַּעַל** (*beliyya'al*, v. 12, "worthless men," literally "sons of *belial*") is the strongest pejorative in the early Hebrew lexicon, applied later to the most heinous offenders (the men of Gibeah in Judg. 19, Nabal, Sheba son of Bichri); the narrator's deployment of it for the priests of Shiloh is an act of literary violence.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The literary architecture of the chapter is one of the great set-pieces of biblical narrative. Robert Polzin, in *Samuel and the Deuteronomist*, made the central observation: Hannah's Song announces the theology of inversion ("the bows of the mighty are broken, but the feeble bind on strength… the barren has borne seven, but she who has many children is forlorn") and the prose chapter that follows enacts it. The mighty house of Eli — three generations of priestly inheritance, fattened on the choicest parts of every offering — will be broken. The "feeble" outsider, Hannah's borrowed son, will rise. The chapter is a structural diptych: the song is the program, the prose is the demonstration.

The song itself is a carefully composed inclusio. It opens with Hannah's "horn" (*qarni*) being exalted (v. 1) and closes with the "horn of his anointed" (*qeren mashiḥo*) being exalted (v. 10) — a private joy reframed as a national-eschatological hope. The intervening verses move through a series of reversals: bows broken / weak strengthened, full hired out / hungry filled, barren bears seven / fertile forlorn, kills / brings to life, Sheol / raises up, poor / rich, low / exalts, dust / princes. The pattern is so insistent that it has become the structural template for later inversion-songs in the Bible. Most importantly, **Mary's *Magnificat*** in Luke 1:46-55 reproduces the form so faithfully that no responsible reading can treat it as coincidence. Mary sings: "He has scattered the proud… He has brought down the mighty from their thrones and exalted those of humble estate; he has filled the hungry with good things, and the rich he has sent away empty." Luke is rewriting Hannah. Joel Green's commentary on Luke and Raymond Brown's *Birth of the Messiah* both treat the Magnificat as a deliberate Hannah-allusion; Brown stresses that Luke wants Mary placed in the matriarchal line of Hannah specifically.

Equally important — and less often noticed — is the bracket that Hannah's Song forms with **David's Song** in 2 Samuel 22 (= Psalm 18). Both are long royal-theological poems placed in the mouth of a single character; both end with the language of *mashiaḥ* and *qeren*; together they bracket the entire book of Samuel. Brueggemann (*First and Second Samuel*) and Polzin both argue that the editor of Samuel framed his book with these two songs intentionally: Hannah's song opens by declaring the inversion-God who will raise up an anointed king, David's song closes by giving thanks as that anointed king. The book is held inside the inversion theology.

The prose section uses a different literary device: the **counterposed character pair**. Verses 12, 17, and 22 about the wicked sons of Eli are interleaved with vv. 11, 18, 21, and 26 about the growing Samuel. The technique is what Bar-Efrat calls "interlaced narration": two character arcs deliberately spliced so that the reader is forced to watch them diverge. Each Samuel verse echoes Genesis 39:2 (Joseph) — "the LORD was with him," "he grew in favor with the LORD and with man." Verse 26 ("the boy Samuel continued to grow both in stature and in favor with the LORD and also with man") is the verse Luke 2:52 lifts almost verbatim for Jesus. The literary genealogy runs Hannah → Samuel → Mary → Jesus, and the editors of both testaments knew it.

The "man of God" oracle (vv. 27-36) is a classic dtr judgment speech: invocation of past divine favor, indictment of present offense, announcement of judgment, sign of the judgment's certainty (the death of Hophni and Phinehas on the same day), and a forward-looking promise (the faithful priest). McCarter notes that the speech contains both pre-deuteronomistic material (the older indictment of Eli's sons) and clearly deuteronomistic editorial framing (the "faithful priest" promise that anticipates the Zadokite settlement of 1 Kings 2). The textual situation is again messy: 4QSam-a has notable variants in vv. 31-33 that affect the precise scope of the curse.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish readings.** Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.10.4-5) treats Hannah's Song as a prophecy of the monarchy and downplays its inversion theology — characteristic of Josephus's interest in dignifying biblical figures for a Greco-Roman audience. The rabbis, by contrast, embraced the inversion theology and extended it. The Talmud (Berakhot 31b) reads Hannah's song as the second half of her prayer-paradigm: first the silent petition, then the public hymn of praise. Pesikta de-Rav Kahana 27 sees in v. 6 ("the LORD kills and brings to life") a proof-text for resurrection. Rashi reads the song line-by-line with rabbinic literalism; Radak attends to its archaic Hebrew; Ralbag the philosopher notes how unusual it is for a woman in Israel to deliver a public theological hymn. Abarbanel — characteristically — focuses on the prose section, reading the corruption of Eli's sons as a paradigm of how priestly-political corruption metastasizes when those in power refuse correction. He treats Eli's mild "no, my sons; it is no good report" (v. 24) as the failure of leadership: the rebuke is too gentle, and the gentleness is itself the indictment. Avivah Zornberg reads the chapter as Hannah's transition from interior to exterior speech — the woman who could not be heard is now heard by everyone, and the song is in some sense her revenge on Peninnah and Eli both. Yair Zakovitch on the song's poetic structure remains a standard reference; Moshe Garsiel on the wordplay between *na'ar* (the boy Samuel) and *na'ot* (the women at the tent — though again a contested reading) is provocative.

**Christian readings.** The patristic and medieval Christian engagement with Hannah's Song was almost entirely typological. Augustine in *City of God* 17.4 treats it as the first explicit prophecy of Christ in the Former Prophets, with the "anointed" of v. 10 being Jesus and the inversion theology being the gospel program in nuce. He notices the Magnificat connection and develops it at length. Origen reads the song allegorically as the Church singing of her own elevation. Chrysostom's homilies on Hannah make her a figure of the Church and treat her transition from silent prayer to public song as the Church's transition from hidden faith to public confession. Gregory the Great's *Moralia* uses v. 6 ("the LORD kills and brings to life") as a foundation for his theology of providence. Aquinas treats the song's theology of divine reversal as compatible with his account of providence ordering all things to the good. Calvin's sermons on 1 Samuel 2 are substantial — he reads the song as a model of post-deliverance praise, contrasts Hannah's faithful song with the unfaithful priests, and uses the prose section as a stern warning against clerical corruption (a topic on which his pastoral concern is particularly vivid). Luther's lectures on the song treat it as a paradigm of *theologia crucis*: God works through reversal, weakness, the unexpected.

The critical tradition has done detailed work on the song. Wellhausen treated it as a late insertion. Hugo Gressmann gave it a careful form-critical analysis as a royal hymn of thanksgiving. Cross and Freedman dated it early. McCarter's Anchor commentary remains the most thorough treatment of the textual history. Brueggemann's commentary reads the song as the theological key to all of Samuel — every later episode in the book either confirms or strains the inversion-theology Hannah announces.

**Contemporary literary work on the song** has flagged a structural ambivalence the text itself raises: Hannah's song is placed only after she has handed over the child, so the same narrative that gives her the most extended prophetic voice in the early chapters also brackets that voice with her surrender of the son who will succeed her as the chapter's protagonist. Tikva Frymer-Kensky reads the song as Hannah's full theological maturation — from petition to praise to prophecy. Brueggemann notes that the prophetic voice the song carries is then immediately handed off to the royal/messianic line of v. 10 — the song is hers, but the future it announces belongs to the *mashiaḥ*.

Where do the traditions converge? On the song as the theological center of gravity for the book of Samuel and as the template for all later inversion-theology, including the Magnificat. Where do they diverge? On who the "anointed" of v. 10 is. The Christian tradition naturally reads it Christologically; the Jewish tradition reads it as Davidic with messianic resonance; the critical tradition reads it as either a generic royal reference or a specific reference to Saul or David. None of these readings is wrong; they are reading the same text from different vantages.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author believe God was doing? Two genuine tensions.

**Tension 1: The God who kills and brings to life (v. 6).** Hannah's song attributes both killing and resurrection to YHWH directly, with no demonic intermediary, no human agent absorbing blame. This is one of the strongest monistic statements in the Hebrew Bible: the LORD does both. Catholic theology (Aquinas, Aquinas-influenced) tends to read this as YHWH's universal sovereignty over life and death, mediated by secondary causes. Reformed theology (Calvin, Edwards) reads it more directly: God is the author of both blessings and judgments, and the believer's task is to bless God in both. Orthodox theology has historically been uncomfortable with the formulation, preferring to attribute death to human sin and the demonic; Vladimir Lossky and modern Orthodox writers like David Bentley Hart push back hard against any reading that makes God the agent of evil. Jewish theology accepts the formulation as the rabbis did: God is one, all comes from God, and the human task is to bless both *baruch dayan ha-emet* (blessed is the true judge — said upon hearing of a death) and *baruch ha-tov ve-ha-meitiv* (blessed is he who is good and does good). The position I find least intuitive is the Orthodox one, but it is the strongest moral reading: a God who only does life is a God who can be loved without reserve, and the cost of that reading is the reduction of v. 6 to rhetorical hyperbole rather than literal claim. Where this leaves us: the song means what it says, and any theology of God that preserves divine goodness must do so without softening the text.

**Tension 2: "It was the will of the LORD to put them to death" (v. 25).** The narrator's parenthetical comment on Hophni and Phinehas's refusal to listen to Eli is one of the most unsettling sentences in the Hebrew Bible. They would not listen — *because* YHWH wanted them dead. This is divine hardening, applied to specific named individuals, with the same theological structure as Pharaoh in Exodus. Catholic moral theology (Aquinas, Banez, Molina) developed elaborate machinery to preserve human freedom under divine sovereignty; the Reformed tradition (Calvin, Westminster) has historically embraced double predestination as the necessary corollary of divine sovereignty. Orthodox theology again resists, treating divine "willing" of death as permissive rather than active. Jewish theology absorbs the verse into the broader doctrine of *gezerah* (decree) and the limits of human freedom under divine providence — the rabbis in Yoma 38b note that those who choose evil are eventually given over to that choice. The position I find least intuitive — and the one I think the text actually requires — is the Reformed one: the narrator means what he says, the divine will is the prior cause of the brothers' refusal to repent, and the moral horror is part of the text's argument. Where this leaves us: a God whose judgments include foreclosing the possibility of repentance for those already deeply embedded in offense.

One unresolved question to leave with: if the LORD willed Hophni and Phinehas's death, did he also will their corruption?

## The Thread

When you get to **Luke 1:46-55**, remember Hannah's song — Mary is rewriting Hannah verse by verse, and the rewriting is the point. The Magnificat is the New Testament's announcement that the inversion-theology of Hannah is the structure of the gospel. When you get to **Luke 2:52** ("Jesus increased in wisdom and in stature and in favor with God and man"), remember 1 Sam 2:26 ("the boy Samuel continued to grow both in stature and in favor with the LORD and also with man") — Luke is placing Jesus in the Samuel-trajectory. When you get to **1 Kings 2:26-27**, where Solomon banishes Abiathar (the last surviving Elide) to Anathoth and the narrator comments "thus Solomon expelled Abiathar from being priest to the LORD, *fulfilling the word of the LORD that he had spoken concerning the house of Eli*" — the dtr editor is closing the loop on the man of God's prophecy in 1 Sam 2:31-36. Pay attention to that "fulfilling" phrase: it is the editor showing his hand, telling us this entire section was about Zadok all along. And finally, when you get to **2 Samuel 22** (= Psalm 18), David's song, notice the *qeren mashiaḥ* — David's horn — that closes the bracket Hannah opened.
