# Luke 13: The Tower, the Bent Woman, and the Hen Over Jerusalem

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Luke 13 opens with two news-of-the-day reports: Galileans whose blood Pilate had mingled with their sacrifices, and eighteen victims of a tower's collapse at Siloam. Neither incident is attested outside Luke. Pilate's procuratorship (26–36 CE) is well documented in Josephus, Philo, and Tacitus, with several incidents of brutality against Jewish populations recorded — the slaughter of Samaritans on Mount Gerizim (Josephus, *Ant.* 18.85-89), the ensigns-in-Jerusalem affair (*War* 2.169-174), the aqueduct-fund seizure and resulting massacre (*War* 2.175-177). The incident in Luke 13:1 is plausibly one of several minor frictions between Galilean pilgrims and Pilate's troops in the Jerusalem temple precincts. Galileans were notoriously prone to messianic-political agitation; pilgrims at festival times were a security concern.

The tower of Siloam (*pyrgos en tō Silōam*) was probably part of the southeastern fortification system overlooking the Pool of Siloam, in the lower city. Excavations by Ronny Reich and Eli Shukron in the early 2000s uncovered the Second Temple period Pool of Siloam at the southern end of the City of David, with substantial associated stairs and architectural remains. The "tower" was likely a defensive structure or an aqueduct-related installation. Whether it was the same construction project for which Pilate had seized temple funds (and against which the protesters in *War* 2.175-177 had rioted) is impossible to determine with certainty, though the suggestion has been made by some commentators (Bock, Marshall, Bovon) and is intriguing.

The Sabbath healing of the bent-over woman (13:10-17) presupposes a synagogue context. First-century synagogues are well attested archaeologically across Galilee — the basalt structure at Capernaum, the Magdala synagogue (excavated since 2009), the Gamla synagogue, the Migdal synagogue's stone with its menorah carving. The synagogue at Magdala, which can be visited today, has a *bema* (raised reading platform) and tiered stone benches around three walls — exactly the architecture implied by Luke's narrative. The "ruler of the synagogue" (*archisynagōgos*) was a recognized first-century Jewish lay office, not a priest; the *archisynagōgos* presided over synagogue order and decided who would read and teach.

The woman's eighteen-year affliction (note the Lukan number-resonance with the eighteen victims of the tower in 13:4) is described as a *pneuma astheneias* ("spirit of weakness"); Jesus calls it a binding by Satan (13:16). The medical specifics are debated — modern diagnoses have suggested ankylosing spondylitis, scoliosis, or psychogenic dystonia — but the cultural framework is the binding/loosing language of first-century Jewish demonology. The *archisynagōgos*'s objection cites the Sabbath commandment (Exodus 20:8-11; Deut 5:12-15); Jesus's reply uses the standard rabbinic *qal vahomer* logic that even an ox or donkey is loosed (untied) on the Sabbath for water — citing the very Sabbath commandment's recognition that animals also rest. The Mishnah tractate *Shabbat* preserves the rabbinic discussion of what kinds of work are permitted on the Sabbath, including the loosing of animals.

The mustard-seed and leaven parables (13:18-21) are paired around an agricultural and a domestic image. First-century Galilean households measured flour in *sata* (Greek *sata*; Hebrew *seʾah*), each *seah* roughly 13 liters; "three measures" is about 40 liters of flour, enough to make bread for over a hundred people. This is festival-quantity baking. The mustard plant in question (*Brassica nigra*, black mustard) does grow in Galilee to perhaps 8-10 feet under ideal conditions — large enough for birds to nest in lower branches but not actually a "tree" in the strict botanical sense. The exaggeration is part of the parable's force.

The "narrow door" (13:24) is *thyra stenē*, "narrow door" — not the Matthean *pulē* (gate). The image is a household door rather than a city gate. Reclining at table (*anaklithēsontai*) in 13:29 is the eschatological banquet of Isaiah 25:6-8. The proverb "the first will be last" (13:30) appears at three points in Luke (here, 14:8-11, and implicitly in 18:14) and parallels Mark 10:31, Matthew 19:30 and 20:16.

The Herod-the-fox saying (13:31-33) is uniquely Lukan. *Alōpex* ("fox") in first-century Mediterranean cultural usage signified not so much cunning as *insignificance* — a small, scavenging predator, not a noble lion. Calling Herod Antipas a fox was an insult of his political stature, not just his character. The "today, tomorrow, and the third day" (*sēmeron kai aurion kai tē tritē*) idiom is plausibly Aramaic-substrate — Joachim Jeremias suggested the underlying Aramaic was *yom umakhar uveyom hashlishi* — and the "third day" already echoes the resurrection foreshadowing.

Greek terms:
- ***metanoeō*** (μετανοέω), 13:3, 5 — "repent," literally "change one's mind"; the Lukan distinctive emphasis (cf. 24:47, Acts 2:38, etc.).
- ***thygater Abraam*** (θυγάτηρ Ἀβραάμ), 13:16 — "daughter of Abraham," Lukan-distinctive inclusion of women in covenantal vocabulary (cf. Zacchaeus as "son of Abraham," 19:9).
- ***zumē*** (ζύμη), 13:21 — leaven, contrasted with its negative use at 12:1.
- ***alōpex*** (ἀλώπηξ), 13:32 — fox.
- ***ornis*** (ὄρνις), 13:34 — hen/bird; the "hen gathering brood" image (the Lukan *ornis* is generic; the *epi tas pterygas* — "under the wings" — picks up Psalm 91:4, Ruth 2:12).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Luke 13 is dense with L material. The Pilate-and-tower questions and Jesus's repent-or-perish reply (13:1-5) are uniquely Lukan; the fig tree parable (13:6-9) is uniquely Lukan; the Sabbath healing of the bent woman (13:10-17) is uniquely Lukan; the Herod-fox saying (13:31-33) is uniquely Lukan. Q material includes the mustard seed and leaven (parallels Matt 13:31-33), the narrow door / first-and-last sayings (parallels Matt 7:13-14, 25:10-12, 8:11-12, 19:30), and the Jerusalem lament (13:34-35, parallel Matt 23:37-39). The chapter's *placement* of these Q sayings is, however, distinctively Lukan — the Jerusalem lament occurs in the middle of the Travel Narrative rather than (as in Matthew) at the end of the woes-discourse, where it forms a conclusion to Jesus's Jerusalem ministry.

The Jerusalem lament's Lukan placement is a signature Lukan editorial decision. Robert Tannehill's *Narrative Unity* and Joel Green's NICNT both argue that Luke wants the lament to *anticipate* Jerusalem rather than to *conclude* it. Jesus is moving toward Jerusalem; the lament is uttered en route as a preview of what awaits. The Matthean placement makes the lament a verdict on Jerusalem after the temple confrontations; the Lukan placement makes it a *grief* that will continue to drive Jesus toward the city.

The fig tree parable of 13:6-9 has a structural and thematic relationship to the Markan and Matthean cursing of the fig tree (Mark 11:12-14, 20-25; Matthew 21:18-22). Luke, alone among the Synoptics, *omits* the cursing of the fig tree from the Jerusalem entry sequence. Many commentators (Fitzmyer, Marshall, Bock) argue that the Lukan parable in 13:6-9 *replaces* the Markan/Matthean cursing — taking the same fig-tree judgment image but reframing it as parable rather than enacted prophetic sign, and *introducing the gardener's plea for one more year*. The Lukan move shifts the symbolism from finality to deferred judgment with intercession. C.H. Dodd's *Parables of the Kingdom* read the parable as eschatological deferral.

The mustard seed and leaven parables (13:18-21) are paired in Q and preserved by both Matthew and Luke. Joachim Jeremias in *The Parables of Jesus* read the leaven parable's surprising element — leaven was associated in Jewish thought with corruption (cf. Exodus 12:15-20 on Passover, and the Lukan use at 12:1) — as part of its parabolic shock. The kingdom is like leaven, *not* like the unleavened bread of holiness. Amy-Jill Levine's *Short Stories by Jesus* extends the point, noting that the woman who hides the leaven in three measures of flour (festival quantity) is doing what kosher kitchens explicitly prohibited at Passover. The kingdom is figuratively rendered through an image of *transgressive* hospitality.

C.F. Evans's Deuteronomy-typology would link Luke 13 with Deuteronomy 16-21 (the festivals, the king-warning, the prophet promise), with thematic resonance around festival, the prophet, and the call to choose. The Lukan Jesus's prophetic self-identification ("a prophet should not perish away from Jerusalem," 13:33) and the Jerusalem-lament's prophetic-mother imagery cohere with Deuteronomic theology.

Beverly Gaventa's apocalyptic readings emphasize 13:10-17 as a spiritual-warfare narrative: Satan binds the woman; Jesus looses her. The chapter participates in the broader Lukan drama of the kingdom-of-God displacing Satan's hold (cf. 10:18, 11:20).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc of Luke 13 runs from the prophetic tradition (Hosea, Jeremiah, Isaiah's vineyard-fig-tree imagery) through the Sabbath theology of Genesis-Exodus-Deuteronomy, into the eschatological banquet of Isaiah 25 and the gathering of the nations of Isaiah 2 and 60.

**Jewish interpretive trajectories.** Géza Vermes and David Flusser both place Jesus's teaching on suffering (13:1-5) inside the rabbinic discussion of theodicy. The dominant rabbinic position rejected the strict suffering-as-punishment-for-individual-sin equation; the Talmudic tractate *Berakhot* preserves Rabbi Akiva's discussion of "afflictions of love" (*yissurin shel ahavah*) and the rejection of any neat retributive calculus. Jesus's "do you think they were worse sinners?" (13:2, 4) participates in this Jewish theodicy conversation, refusing the simple equation while also pressing the urgency of repentance.

Amy-Jill Levine's *Short Stories by Jesus*, alongside her work on first-century Jewish context, draws out the Lukan vocabulary of "daughter of Abraham" (13:16) — found nowhere else in the Gospels — as a pointed move: the phrase claims for this woman the covenantal status that "son of Abraham" claims for men, so that she is not merely an anonymous sufferer but an heir of the promise whom Satan has bound and the Sabbath of all days is the right day to loose. Levine also treats Jesus's Sabbath argument as recognizably rabbinic — *qal vahomer* logic from animal to human, a standard mode of halakhic reasoning. Pinchas Lapide's work on Jesus's halakhah makes the same point: Jesus's Sabbath disputes are intra-Jewish disputes about the *correct* application of Sabbath law, not rejections of Sabbath observance.

E.P. Sanders's *Jesus and Judaism* contextualizes the Jerusalem lament inside the Jewish prophetic tradition's lament over Jerusalem — Isaiah 1, Jeremiah 8, Lamentations. The Lukan Jesus speaks as a prophet in the line of those who had wept over the city. Daniel Boyarin's work on Jewish-Christian boundaries argues that the lament's "I would have gathered your children" image (the maternal hen) is recognizably within Jewish prophetic vocabulary (Psalm 17:8, 36:7, 57:1, 91:4, all using wing imagery for divine protection; Ruth 2:12; Boaz's blessing on Ruth uses *kanaph*).

**Christian interpretive trajectories.** Origen's *Homilies on Luke* and Cyril of Alexandria's *Commentary on Luke* read 13:1-5 as a corrective to the "blessed are those who escape disaster" assumption; the disaster victims are not the worse sinners, but disaster ought to function as a memento mori for all. Augustine's reading of the parable of the fig tree in *Sermon 254* takes the gardener as Christ interceding with the Father and the manure as the cross's humbling — the patristic allegory is rich. Aquinas in the *Catena Aurea* preserves the patristic readings; Calvin in his *Harmony* reads the parable's three years as God's patience with the Jewish nation prior to the church's expansion.

The narrow-door saying (13:22-30) has been a focal point of soteriological controversy since the Reformation. Calvin reads it as confirmation of election's narrowness — many seek but cannot enter, because their seeking is not the saving faith of the elect. Arminius and the later Arminian tradition read the saying as a call to *strive* (*agōnizesthe*) — the door is genuinely available but enters only those who undertake the effort. Wesley's reading is harder still on the urgency of striving while emphasizing the door's universal availability. Universalist readings (Origen, Gregory of Nyssa in some readings, modern voices like Robin Parry, David Bentley Hart) read the saying inside the broader Lukan-Pauline trajectory toward universal reconciliation: the door is narrow now but not finally shut. Steelmanned positions on this question are taken up in Lens 4.

The Jerusalem lament has had a profound place in Christian worship and reflection. Augustine's *City of God* (especially books 17-18) reads Jerusalem typologically; Bernard of Clairvaux's sermons return to the lament repeatedly; Luther's *Lectures on Genesis* (when reaching Genesis 49 and the prophecies of Israel) cross-reference the Lukan lament; Bonhoeffer's *Discipleship* and his Berlin sermons cite the Jerusalem-lament image of the gathering hen. N.T. Wright, in *Luke for Everyone* and *Jesus and the Victory of God*, reads the lament — and the whole chapter — inside the Second Temple expectation of God's return to Zion. For Wright, "I would have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood" is the voice of the covenant God who has *now* come back to his city in the person of Jesus, only to find Jerusalem set on a different course — the path of armed resistance to Rome that will, Jesus warns, bring the city down (the "you will all likewise perish" of 13:3, 5, and the wreckage foreseen in 13:34-35). The Herod-the-fox saying and the "I must go on my way today and tomorrow… for it cannot be that a prophet should perish away from Jerusalem" (13:32-33) are, on Wright's reading, Jesus consciously taking the prophetic road into the city to do what only Israel's God could do — which is exactly the journey the Travel Narrative is dramatizing. The maternal-hen image at 13:34 also attributes to Jesus a feminine, mothering posture that Christian iconography and homiletic have long noted: Julian of Norwich's "Christ our Mother" tradition picks it up, as do Anselm's prayers and (earlier) Gregory of Nyssa, and the underlying *kanaph* / "under the wings" imagery runs through Psalm 17:8, 36:7, 57:1, 91:4, and Boaz's blessing on Ruth.

Justo González's *Luke* (Belief Commentary) reads the Pilate-and-tower passage as Jesus's refusal to *spiritualize* political and structural violence: the Galileans died at Pilate's hand, the eighteen at Siloam died from a building's collapse, and Jesus neither blames the victims nor explains the tragedy away — he calls the whole society to *metanoia*. Kenneth Bailey's *Jesus Through Middle Eastern Eyes* reads the bent-woman healing inside Mediterranean honor-shame culture: the woman's eighteen-year disability had pushed her to the social margin; Jesus's calling her forward and naming her covenantally is a public act of restoration, not just a medical cure.

Contemporary commentary — Fitzmyer, Green, Nolland, Bock, Marshall, Johnson, Bovon — finds Luke 13 thematically unified by the urgency of the present moment. Repent, be loosed, enter the door, recognize what is happening. The chapter ends with Jesus's resolute setting of his face toward Jerusalem under death threat. The Travel Narrative's pace quickens.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The narrow door (13:22-30) — predestinarian, Arminian, or universalist?** Calvinist readings emphasize the door's narrowness and the "I do not know where you come from" verdict as confirmation of double predestination: those outside knock but are not opened to. Arminian-Wesleyan readings emphasize the imperative *agōnizesthe* ("strive") as confirmation that the door is genuinely accessible and the failure to enter is the failure to strive. Universalist readings (Origen, Gregory of Nyssa in some readings, Robin Parry, David Bentley Hart) note that the saying's narrowness is *temporal* — the door is *now* shut, but the parable's frame does not declare the shutting permanent. The "people will come from east and west" gathering of 13:29 suggests an eschatological inclusion that exceeds ordinary expectations. Steelmanned: Calvin's reading honors the surface verdict and the historical realism of the saying — many *will* be excluded, by Jesus's own teaching. The Arminian reading honors the imperative and the moral seriousness of the saying — disciples are called to actually do something. The universalist reading honors the canonical-Lukan trajectory toward universal salvation (Acts 3:21's *apokatastasis*; the parable's framing of east-west-north-south gathering). **Where this leaves us:** Luke 13:22-30 does not finally settle the question. The saying's force is *kerygmatic* — it warns the present hearer to enter now — rather than systematic-theological. Reading it as a settled verdict on universal vs. particular salvation imposes a question the text itself does not pretend to answer. The Lukan emphasis falls on urgency.

**Tension 2: "Unless you repent, you will all likewise perish" (13:3, 5) — eschatological, historical, or both?** The classical Christian reading takes the warning as eschatological: unless the hearers repent, they will perish in the final judgment. A historicist reading (Conzelmann, more recently N.T. Wright in *Jesus and the Victory of God*) takes the warning as referring to the impending Roman destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE: unless the people repent of the path of armed resistance and adopt Jesus's way of peace, the city itself will fall. A pastoral-existentialist reading (Bovon, with some support from Green) takes the warning as the urgent call to repentance in *every* moment, with the *likewise* indicating the literal kind of perishing the disaster victims suffered — the warning's force is the unpredictability of death itself. Steelmanned: the eschatological reading has the broader New Testament context of judgment language. The historicist reading has the specificity of the Lukan placement (Jerusalem-bound, with the lament of 13:34-35 already in view) and the realism of 70 CE as the prophetic-historical referent. The existentialist reading has the saying's repetition and its urgent-present force. **Where this leaves us:** all three readings are present in the text. The Lukan Jesus is warning of a *historical* catastrophe coming for Jerusalem, an *eschatological* judgment that catastrophe foreshadows, and a *present-existential* call that no reader can postpone.

**Unresolved question:** The fig tree parable's gardener pleads for "one more year." Has that year already expired by the time Luke is writing (post-70 CE)? If so, what is the parable's force for Luke's post-destruction church? Does the parable read as encouragement (the deferred mercy held; gentle interventions worked) or as warning (the year ran out)? Commentators are divided. The parable's openness — it ends with the gardener's request, not the master's response — leaves the future deliberately undetermined.

## The Thread

The "daughter of Abraham" language (13:16) ties forward to Zacchaeus as "son of Abraham" (19:9), a Lukan inclusio bracketing the Travel Narrative's covenantal-inclusion theme. The fig tree parable (13:6-9) replaces the Markan fig-tree cursing in Lukan structure and rhymes forward to the Lukan Jerusalem lament (19:41-44). The Jerusalem lament (13:34-35) anticipates 19:41-44, 21:5-24, and 23:28-31 — four Lukan moments of grief over the city. The "I cast out demons today and tomorrow, and the third day I finish" (13:32) is the first of several Lukan three-day foreshadowings of the resurrection.
