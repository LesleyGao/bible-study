# Mark 1: The Beginning of the Gospel

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Mark opens with a single freighted word — *archē* (ἀρχή, "beginning") — and lands the reader in a Mediterranean world organized by Roman power, Herodian client kingship, and a Jewish religious order living under occupation. The Gospel is most plausibly composed in the 60s CE, during or just after the Neronian persecution of 64 CE that followed the great fire of Rome. Tacitus (*Annals* 15.44) describes Christians "covered with the skins of beasts… torn by dogs and perishing" — the audience that first heard "the beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ" likely heard it as people whose neighbors had been burned as living torches in Nero's gardens. The political timeline behind chapter 1 stretches back: Tiberius reigned (14–37 CE) when John the Baptist appeared (Luke 3:1 dates John's emergence to Tiberius's fifteenth year); Caligula (37–41) attempted to install his statue in the Jerusalem Temple in 40 CE — an event that scarred Jewish memory; Claudius (41–54) expelled Jews from Rome over disturbances "at the instigation of Chrestus" (Suetonius *Claudius* 25.4); Nero (54–68) became the first emperor to persecute Christians as Christians.

Galilee in chapter 1 is governed by Herod Antipas, tetrarch from 4 BCE to 39 CE — the same Antipas who will arrest John the Baptist (1:14) and behead him (6:14–29), and before whom Jesus will eventually stand (Luke 23). Antipas built Sepphoris and, around 19 CE, founded Tiberias on the Sea of Galilee in honor of Tiberius — a city Jesus conspicuously does not visit. Capernaum, where most of chapter 1's action occurs, sat on the northwestern shore of the Sea of Galilee at the border between Antipas's territory and Philip's tetrarchy — which is why Mark mentions a tax booth in chapter 2. The fishing economy of the lake supplied Magdala (*Migdal Nuniya*, "tower of fish"), where Magdala stone and first-century boat remains have been recovered, and Bethsaida — both critical to the gospel's geography. James Strange's excavations at Capernaum, alongside Stanislao Loffreda and Virgilio Corbo's Franciscan-led work since the 1960s, have exposed a black basalt residential quarter from the first century directly beneath the visible fourth-century white limestone synagogue. The "house of Simon and Andrew" (1:29) sits within that quarter; the so-called Insula Sacra under the octagonal Byzantine church preserves a courtyard house with graffiti naming Jesus and Peter, suggesting it was already venerated as Peter's house by the late first or second century.

The Jewish religious-political landscape that crowds 1:21–28 is dense. The synagogue at Capernaum is a meeting house, court, and study center; the *grammateis* ("scribes") are professional Torah-readers and legal experts whose authority Jesus's teaching is contrasted with (1:22). Pharisees do not yet appear in chapter 1 but will from 2:16; Sadducees, Essenes (the Dead Sea community at Qumran was active until 68 CE), and Zealot/sicarii movements form the ideological backdrop. Messianic expectation in this period was variegated: Davidic-royal (Psalms of Solomon 17), priestly (Qumran's two-messiah scheme — 1QS 9, CD 12–14), prophetic (a returning Moses or Elijah, cf. 1QS 9:11). John the Baptist's wilderness location, baptism of repentance, leather belt, and locust-and-wild-honey diet (1:6) consciously evokes 2 Kings 1:8 (Elijah) and Mal 4:5 ("Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet"). Josephus's independent account of John (*Antiquities* 18.116–119) confirms the Baptist as a historical figure whose moral preaching unsettled Antipas.

Material culture lines up tightly with the text. The "roof" through which a paralytic will be lowered in chapter 2 reflects the mud-thatch-over-palm-and-log construction documented at Capernaum and Korazim. Fishing technology — the *amphiblēstron* (cast-net) Simon and Andrew use (implied at 1:16) and the *diktyon* (drag-net) James and John mend (1:19) — is preserved in mosaics at Migdal and in the first-century "Jesus Boat" (Kinneret Boat, GS-1) recovered from the lakebed in 1986. The leather belt and the camel's-hair garment of John (1:6) match prophet-uniform imagery.

Textual evidence: P45 (Chester Beatty I, c. 250 CE) is the earliest substantial papyrus of Mark, though Mark was likely the least-copied of the four gospels in antiquity. Codex Vaticanus (B, 4th c.) and Codex Sinaiticus (ℵ, 4th c.) are the two foundational uncials; both end Mark at 16:8 (a major textual problem we revisit in chapter 16). Chapter 1 itself contains a famous variant — "Son of God" (*huiou theou*) at 1:1 is missing from Sinaiticus and Codex Koridethi (Θ); Bart Ehrman in *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* argues the longer reading is a scribal expansion serving anti-adoptionist polemic, while Bruce Metzger (*Textual Commentary*) and Joel Marcus (Anchor Bible) defend it as original on the basis of P45, Vaticanus, and the inclusio with 15:39. Greek terms worth carrying: ***archē*** (ἀρχή, "beginning"), ***euangelion*** (εὐαγγέλιον, "good news/gospel" — a politically loaded word; the Priene Calendar Inscription of 9 BCE describes the birthday of Augustus as "the beginning [archē] of good news [euangeliōn] for the world"), ***euthys*** (εὐθύς, "immediately"), ***schizō*** (σχίζω, "tear, split"), and ***metanoeite*** (μετανοεῖτε, "repent" — change of mind/turning).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

What kind of book is Mark? Richard Burridge's *What Are the Gospels? A Comparison with Graeco-Roman Biography* (1992; 2nd ed. 2004) decisively re-anchored the gospels in the genre of ancient *bios* — biographies organized around a subject's character revealed through speech and action. Mark is the shortest and most kinetic of the four; Adela Yarbro Collins (*Hermeneia*) prefers to call it a "Hellenistic-Jewish history" with apocalyptic features; Mary Ann Tolbert (*Sowing the Gospel*) reads it as a popular novelistic narrative. All three categories overlap, and chapter 1 announces the form.

The opening verse, "*Archē tou euangeliou Iēsou Christou*" (1:1), is a title — like Hesiod's *Theogony* or a Roman *vita*. Whether *huiou theou* belongs in the original or not, the inclusio with 15:39 (the centurion: "*alēthōs houtos ho anthrōpos huios theou ēn*") demands the divine-sonship key. Frank Kermode in *The Genesis of Secrecy* observes that Mark's narrative is structured by withheld and disclosed identity; chapter 1 begins the withholding the moment it announces the disclosure. Demons recognize Jesus (1:24, "I know who you are — the Holy One of God") but are silenced; the heavenly voice addresses Jesus in the second person (1:11) — the disciples do not hear it.

***Euthys*** — that signature Markan adverb "immediately" (εὐθύς) — appears 41 times across Mark and 11 times in chapter 1 alone (at 1:10, 12, 18, 20, 21, 23, 28, 29, 30, 42, 43). The drumbeat creates the urgent, breathless tempo for which Mark is famous. Add to it the historical present tense, employed roughly 150 times in the gospel — verbs like "he says" or "he comes" in present tense within past-tense narration, vivid in Greek and translated awkwardly. Mark is short — about 11,300 words against Matthew's 18,300 — because Mark is fast. Bonhoeffer caught the texture in *Discipleship*: when Jesus calls Simon and Andrew, "they immediately left their nets and followed him." There is no dialogue, no negotiation, no inner deliberation. The narrative force is the call.

Mark's literary signatures already visible in chapter 1: the **triadic structure** (three controversies will appear, three passion predictions, three boat scenes); the **Markan sandwich** or intercalation, where narrative B is inserted inside A so that each illuminates the other (the chapter's day-in-Capernaum, 1:21–34, is itself an A-B-A structure — synagogue exorcism, house healing, evening crowd); and the **Messianic Secret** (1:25, 34, 44 — silence the demons, silence the leper). William Wrede's 1901 *Das Messiasgeheimnis in den Evangelien* argued the secret was a redactional theme imposed by Mark to reconcile a non-messianic Jesus tradition with later messianic claims; James Dunn (*Jesus and the Spirit*), Heikki Räisänen (*The Messianic Secret in Mark*), and Christopher Tuckett have variously qualified, defended, or modified Wrede; most contemporary scholars read the secret as a literary/theological device structuring the gospel rather than a historical strategy.

The composite citation at 1:2–3 is its own literary event. Mark attributes "Behold, I send my messenger before your face… the voice of one crying in the wilderness" to "Isaiah the prophet" — but the first half is Mal 3:1 (with echoes of Exod 23:20), and only the second half is Isa 40:3. Richard Hays in *Echoes of Scripture in the Gospels* notes that this catena-style attribution to a single major prophet was acceptable rabbinic practice; Mark's choice of Isaiah signals that the gospel is deliberately framed as the long-promised return of YHWH to Zion (Isa 40), not just the announcement of a new teacher.

Two textual variants in chapter 1 deserve flagging: (1) 1:1 *huiou theou* (above); (2) 1:41 *splanchnistheis* "moved with compassion" — read by most Greek manuscripts and modern translations — versus *orgistheis* "moved with anger," found in Codex Bezae (D) and several Old Latin witnesses. The harder reading principle (*lectio difficilior potior*) suggests *orgistheis* is original; Marcus (Anchor Bible) and Ehrman favor *orgistheis*; the translators of the NIV 2011 adopted "indignant"; ESV retains "moved with pity." If Jesus was angry — at the disease, at the priestly system that quarantined this man, at the pollution of the world — chapter 1's leper episode reads very differently.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc into which Mark 1 inserts itself is a long arc: it begins, by Mark's own framing, where Isaiah 40 begins — with the announcement that the exile is ending and YHWH is returning. The wilderness setting is not a neutral geography. It is where Israel was constituted (Exodus), where Israel sinned and was tested (Numbers), and where the prophets repeatedly placed renewal (Hosea 2; Isa 35, 40; Ezek 20). John the Baptist's location at the Jordan invokes Joshua's crossing into the land — and the gospel's opening movement is, in effect, a re-conquest narrative in reverse: instead of armies crossing into Canaan, the Spirit drives Jesus back into the wilderness (1:12) for forty days, recapitulating Israel's forty years.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** Rabbinic tradition's most direct engagements with Jesus appear in Babylonian Talmud Sanhedrin 43a (Jesus as a Sabbath-eve hanged sorcerer-deceiver) and 107b (Jesus as a student of Joshua ben Perachiah turned heretic), bSotah 47a (the same), and the medieval polemical *Toledot Yeshu*. These are late, polemical, and refracted through centuries of Christian-Jewish conflict. The more important Jewish engagement with the Markan Jesus has come in the modern period. Geza Vermes in *Jesus the Jew* (1973) anchored Jesus in the *hasid* tradition of charismatic Galilean holy men (Honi the Circle-Drawer, Hanina ben Dosa) — for Vermes, the exorcisms and healings of Mark 1 belong to a recognizable Jewish charismatic type, not a unique Hellenistic divine man. E. P. Sanders in *The Historical Figure of Jesus* (1993) reads Mark 1's announcement of the kingdom as eschatological-restorationist Judaism, not as the founding of a new religion. Daniel Boyarin in *The Jewish Gospels* (2012) argues that the high Christology of "Son of God" and "Son of Man" was already a Jewish theological possibility — Mark 1 is not breaking from Judaism so much as making a Jewish argument. Amy-Jill Levine in *The Misunderstood Jew* and the *Jewish Annotated New Testament* (with Marc Brettler) consistently challenges Christian readers to recover Jesus's first-century Jewishness — including in chapter 1, where John's baptism is a Jewish purity ritual, the synagogue is a Jewish institution, and the priestly inspection of the cleansed leper (1:44) presupposes Lev 13–14 as living Torah. David Flusser's *Jesus* (1968; ET 1997) reads Jesus through the Pharisaic-pietist lens.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** The patristic engagement is fragmentary on Mark specifically because Augustine (*De Consensu Evangelistarum*) regarded Mark as Matthew's epitomator — a view that suppressed independent Markan commentary for centuries. Origen's *Commentary on Mark* survives only in fragments. Chrysostom preached on Matthew, integrating Mark by reference. The most extensive surviving early commentary on Mark is **Bede the Venerable's** *In Marci Evangelium Expositio* (early 8th c.), which set the medieval Latin reading of chapter 1 — John as Elijah, baptism as the inauguration of the new covenant, the wilderness as monastic precursor. Theophylact of Ohrid's *Explanation of Mark* carried the Byzantine tradition. Thomas Aquinas's *Catena Aurea* chained patristic commentary on Mark. Jerome's brief *Commentary on Mark* (its authenticity debated) circulated widely.

Reformation-era Mark commentary came largely through harmony: **Calvin's *Harmony of the Gospels*** (1555) treated Matthew, Mark, and Luke synoptically; Calvin reads 1:14–15 as the inaugural proclamation of the kingdom by which the gospel itself is defined. Luther's lectures touch Mark only glancingly. Modern critical-historical commentary recovered Mark as independent: Vincent Taylor's 1952 *The Gospel According to St. Mark* (still useful), C. E. B. Cranfield (*CGTC*, 1959), Robert Guelich and Craig Evans (*WBC*, 1989/2001), Morna Hooker (*BNTC*, 1991), R. T. France (*NIGTC*, 2002), Adela Yarbro Collins (*Hermeneia*, 2007), Joel Marcus (*Anchor Bible*, 2 vols, 2000/2009), James R. Edwards (*PNTC*, 2002).

In the twentieth century, Mark became the site for major theological projects. Bultmann's *History of the Synoptic Tradition* (1921) decomposed Mark into pre-Markan units to reconstruct the *Sitz im Leben* of the early church. Karl Barth scattered intense Markan engagement through *Church Dogmatics* — most strikingly on baptism (CD IV/4) where Barth reads 1:9–11 as the foundational revelation of the Trinity in Jesus's own person. **Bonhoeffer's *Discipleship*** (1937), more than any other twentieth-century book, fixed Mark 1:16–20 ("Follow me") as the paradigmatic call: "the disciple simply burns his boats and goes ahead." Werner Kelber's *The Oral and the Written Gospel* repositioned Mark as a deliberate textualization that destabilized the earlier oral kerygma.

Literary-narrative readings have been particularly fruitful. **David Rhoads, Joanna Dewey, and Donald Michie's *Mark as Story*** (1982; 3rd ed. 2012) treats Mark as a unified narrative with characters, plot, and rhetoric. **Mary Ann Tolbert's *Sowing the Gospel*** (1989) reads the parable of the sower as Mark's interpretive key — chapter 1 plants the seed. Frank Kermode (*The Genesis of Secrecy*, 1979) argues Mark's hermeneutic is essentially secretive — the narrative withholds even as it reveals. Robert Fowler's *Let the Reader Understand* and Stephen Moore's postmodern readings extend the literary turn.

Anti-imperial readings — Ched Myers's *Binding the Strong Man* (1988), Richard Horsley's *Hearing the Whole Story* — read the kingdom proclamation of 1:14–15 as a frontal challenge to the *Pax Romana*: Mark's "*euangelion*" deliberately commandeers a word the empire used for the emperor's accession (the Priene inscription), and announces a different king.

**N. T. Wright** (*Mark for Everyone*; *Jesus and the Victory of God*) reads Mark 1 as the opening of the story he takes to be Jesus's controlling narrative: Israel's long exile is ending and Israel's God is returning to be king. The Isaiah 40 catena at 1:2–3 is, for Wright, the interpretive key the evangelist hands the reader — "prepare the way of the LORD" means YHWH himself is coming back to Zion, and Mark is claiming that this return is happening in and as Jesus of Nazareth. The kingdom-proclamation of 1:14–15 is therefore not a timeless invitation to private piety but the announcement that the decisive moment of Israel's history has arrived: God is becoming king, the powers (Rome included, but also the spiritual forces dramatized in the Capernaum exorcism) are being confronted, and the appropriate response is *metanoia* — turning, reorientation around this new reality. Jesus's baptism, on Wright's reading, is the moment the Messiah is anointed and commissioned for that task; the wilderness testing recapitulates Israel's wilderness and Adam's garden, with Jesus succeeding where both failed.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What does it mean that Jesus is baptized by John?** John baptizes "for the forgiveness of sins" (1:4); if Jesus is the sinless Son of God, why submit to such a rite? The patristic answers (Ignatius, Justin, Tertullian, Origen): Jesus is baptized "to fulfill all righteousness" (Matthew's gloss), to sanctify the waters, to identify with sinners, and to inaugurate Christian baptism. The Catholic position (Aquinas, *ST* III q. 39) develops this: Jesus's baptism instituted the sacrament and revealed the Trinity. The Reformed position (Calvin, *Institutes* IV.15) reads it federally — Jesus stands in the place of his people, and the heavens open over him as the head of a new humanity. The Orthodox position (the feast of *Theophany*) emphasizes the cosmic sanctification of the waters and the descent of the Spirit as the inauguration of the new creation. The Wesleyan reading sees baptism as the inauguration of Jesus's prophetic-priestly ministry by the Spirit. The Jewish-historical reading (Sanders, Vermes) takes the baptism at face value: Jesus joined an apocalyptic Jewish renewal movement and accepted its rite. The anti-imperial reading (Myers, Horsley): Jesus identifies with the unwashed peasant crowds being baptized by an outlaw prophet whom the tetrarch will soon kill. **Where this leaves us:** Mark refuses to soften the scandal. Jesus comes from Nazareth (an obscure Galilean village) and gets baptized in a Jewish prophetic-renewal movement — and at that moment of solidarity-with-sinners, the heavens are *torn* (1:10, *schizō*) and the Father speaks. The descent into solidarity is the moment of revelation. Steelman the most uncomfortable reading — Sanders's — and the Christian reading sharpens: incarnation means going all the way down.

**Tension 2: Why does Jesus silence the demons?** The "messianic secret" begins in chapter 1 (1:25, 34, 44). Wrede's classic theory: the silencing is Markan redaction covering a non-messianic historical Jesus. The Catholic and patristic reading: Jesus silences the demons to refuse demonic testimony — truth from a defiled mouth is still defilement (cf. Acts 16:18 — Paul silencing the divining slave girl). The Reformed reading (Calvin): Jesus refuses premature acclamation that would short-circuit his cross-shaped revelation; the cross is where the divine identity becomes legible (Mark's *theologia crucis*). The Orthodox reading: Jesus's authority over the demonic is itself the disclosure; their words are irrelevant. The apocalyptic reading (Marcus, Yarbro Collins): the silencing is part of a cosmic war — the demons are conquered combatants, not credible witnesses. The literary reading (Kermode, Tolbert): the secret is a narrative engine — the reader knows what the characters do not, and the gap drives the plot. The anti-imperial reading (Myers): Jesus refuses spectacle and refuses to be co-opted by recognition before the kingdom's content (justice, exorcism, healing) is enacted. **Where this leaves us:** Mark's secrecy is not embarrassment — it is pedagogy. Saying "Son of God" before seeing the cross produces a wrong picture of God. The 15:39 confession by a Roman centurion at the foot of a corpse is the only legitimate end-point of the question Mark 1 plants.

**Unresolved question:** If Mark begins his gospel with a verb that is missing — there is no main verb in 1:1–3, only a heading and a citation — and the sentence finally lands at "John appeared" (1:4), is "the beginning of the gospel" John's appearance, Jesus's baptism, the resurrection, or the reading of this very book? Mark may be encoding into his syntax the question that drives the whole narrative: where, exactly, does the gospel begin?

## The Thread

The chapter sets up Mark's deepest brackets. **1:10 — the heavens are *torn open* (*schizomenous*, σχιζομένους, from *schizō*)** — pairs with **15:38 — the temple veil is *torn* (*eschisthē*, ἐσχίσθη)**. These are the only two uses of *schizō* in Mark, and they bracket the gospel: the tearing at the Jordan opens divine space *to* humanity; the tearing of the veil opens divine space *through* the cross. Watch for the bracket to close at 15:38. **1:11 — "you are my beloved Son"** — recurs at **9:7 (transfiguration)** and is finally confessed by a human being at **15:39 (the centurion: "truly this man was the Son of God")**. Mark is mapping a path from heavenly declaration to human confession, and the path runs through Caesarea Philippi (8:29) and Calvary. **1:14–15 — "the kingdom of God is at hand"** — is the programmatic statement; every subsequent parable in chapter 4, every healing, every controversy is an exposition. Watch chapter 4 (the parables of the kingdom) and chapter 13 (the apocalypse) for the kingdom's full Markan range. **1:16–20 — "follow me"** — initiates the discipleship arc that will collapse at 14:50 ("they all left him and fled") and only be restored by the messenger at the empty tomb (16:7). The chapter that begins with disciples leaving their nets to follow ends, in the gospel's larger shape, with disciples leaving everything to flee — and a young man in white announcing that he goes before them, still, into Galilee.
