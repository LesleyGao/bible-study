# Mark 5: Legion, the Bleeding Woman, and a Dead Girl Who Is Only Sleeping

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Mark 5 opens on the eastern shore of the Sea of Galilee in territory Jewish readers would have recognized as deeply Gentile. The text says the boat lands in "the country of the Gerasenes" (5:1), and here we meet our first historical-geographical problem. Gerasa — modern Jerash in Jordan — sits more than thirty miles inland, far too distant for a herd of pigs to plausibly stampede into the sea. The textual tradition splits: some manuscripts read *Gergesēnōn*, others *Gadarēnōn* (Matthew's preferred form, since Gadara was closer, about six miles from the lake), still others *Gerasēnōn*. Origen, working in the third century, already noted the difficulty in his *Commentary on John* and proposed Gergesa as the correct reading, identifying it with a small village he knew on the eastern shore. Modern archaeology has tended to vindicate his instinct. Excavations at Tel Khersa / Kursi, conducted by Vassilios Tzaferis in the 1970s and discussed in John Wilkinson's geographical surveys, uncovered a fifth-century Byzantine monastery built precisely to commemorate the exorcism, sitting beside a steep slope that descends abruptly into the lake. Whatever the original toponym, the early Christian community localized the event at Kursi, and the topography there fits Mark's description of a steep bank in a way Gerasa proper cannot.

The cultural geography is decisively Gentile. The Decapolis — ten Hellenistic cities including Gerasa, Gadara, Hippos, Pella, and Scythopolis — was a federation of Greek-speaking *poleis* on the eastern margin of Roman Syria, founded or refounded under Pompey after 63 BCE. Pigs were farmed openly here precisely because the population was largely non-Jewish; the herd of two thousand swine in 5:13 is a marker of foreign territory. For Mark's Roman audience, the geography matters less than the civilizational signal: Jesus has crossed into the unclean world, where tombs sit unkept on hillsides and a naked man cuts himself with stones.

The political resonance of the Greek term ***legiōn*** (λεγιών, 5:9) cannot be overstated. This is a Latin loan-word — *legio* — and Mark, writing for a Roman audience, would have known exactly what it conjured. A Roman legion in the first century numbered between 5,400 and 6,000 heavily armed soldiers. The Tenth Legion *Fretensis*, garrisoned in Syria and later reassigned to Judea after the First Jewish War, carried a boar (or in some periods a wild boar) among its standards. Ched Myers (*Binding the Strong Man*, 1988) and Richard Horsley (*Hidden Transcripts*, 2004) have argued — persuasively, if maximally — that the demoniac is a figure for the colonial subject, his very identity fragmented by occupation, and that the herd of pigs being driven into the sea echoes the Egyptians at the Red Sea: imperial power drowned. Whether or not Mark intended this reading at every level, the choice of *legiōn* over the available Aramaic and Greek synonyms for "many" or "horde" was deliberate, and a Roman reader would have heard it.

The Jairus-and-bleeding-woman pericope returns us to Jewish Galilee. Synagogue rulers (***archisynagōgos***, ἀρχισυνάγωγος) were lay leaders, not priests, charged with administering Sabbath worship and the building's affairs; epigraphic evidence from Theodotos's first-century synagogue inscription in Jerusalem and from later inscriptions at Sardis and Aphrodisias shows the office was real and prestigious. A man like Jairus throwing himself at the feet of an itinerant Galilean exorcist would have been a public event with social cost.

Three more Greek terms repay attention. The verb ***haptō*** (ἅπτω, "to touch," 5:27, 28, 30, 31) is repeated five times in the bleeding-woman scene, drawing attention to the forbidden nature of her contact. Under Leviticus 15:25–30, a woman with chronic uterine bleeding rendered ritually impure anyone she touched; her reaching for Jesus's *himation* (ἱμάτιον, the outer cloak with its tassels — *kraspeda*, *tzitzit*) was a transgression of a real social code. The Aramaic ***Talitha koum*** (5:41) — Mark gives a slightly Hellenized form *koumi* with feminine ending added — is preserved in the original tongue and translated for Mark's Greek audience, one of his five Aramaic preservations and a strong argument (with Papias's note in Eusebius *Hist. Eccl.* 3.39.15) that Mark draws on Petrine reminiscence. Finally, the term ***thygatēr*** (θυγάτηρ, "daughter," 5:34) is the only place in any Gospel where Jesus addresses a woman this way; the bleeding woman, anonymous, is given a kinship name even as Jairus's named daughter is being raised.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Mark 5 contains some of the densest literary patterning in the Gospel. The chapter is a triptych — exorcism, healing, raising — moving from the most extreme case of demonic possession Mark records, through chronic illness, to death itself. Each panel escalates the others.

The Jairus-and-bleeding-woman sequence (5:21–43) is the textbook example of ***Markan sandwich*** or ***intercalation***: A (Jairus's daughter is dying) → B (the bleeding woman is healed) → A' (Jairus's daughter is raised). The middle story is not a digression. It is the interpretive key. The two "daughters" mirror each other: a woman bleeding twelve years, a girl twelve years old; one anonymous in a crowd, one named and inside a synagogue ruler's house; both in states the Mosaic law marked as ritually defiling (Leviticus 15:25–30 for menstruation, Numbers 19 for corpse-impurity). The number twelve is unlikely to be accidental — Joel Marcus (*Mark*, Anchor Bible) and Adela Yarbro Collins (*Mark*, Hermeneia) both note the resonance with the twelve tribes of Israel and Mark's running concern with the restoration of the people. The sandwich's structural lesson, as James R. Edwards has argued (*JSNT*, 1989), is that the inserted story unlocks the framing one: faith reaches across purity boundaries, and the daughter raised is interpretable only through the daughter healed.

The chapter's literary signature includes Mark's beloved adverb ***euthys*** (εὐθύς, "immediately"), which fires three times in 5:29, 5:30, and 5:42 alone, creating a sense of compressed urgency that becomes almost comic when Jesus pauses inside that compression to ask, "Who touched my garments?" — a moment of narrative deceleration inside the breathless rush. Mark also runs his historical-present tense through the chapter ("they come" *erchontai*, "he says" *legei*), giving the prose the feel of an oral-performance text, the kind of thing Werner Kelber (*The Oral and Written Gospel*, 1983) argued was retained from the Gospel's pre-literary substrate.

Two literary moments deserve particular attention. The first is the demoniac's recognition of Jesus before any human in Mark's narrative has yet identified him. "Son of the Most High God" (*huios tou theou tou hypsistou*, 5:7) — a Gentile title for the supreme deity, used by Hellenistic Jews of YHWH but also intelligible in pagan religious vocabulary — is spoken by an unclean spirit on Gentile soil. The pattern is consistent with the ***Messianic Secret*** (Wrede, 1901): demons know who Jesus is, humans do not, and Jesus silences both. The second is the strange final command in 5:43 — "he strictly charged them that no one should know this, and told them to give her something to eat." A child raised from the dead must be told to eat. The detail is so utterly unliterary, so fragmentary, that Vincent Taylor (1952) treated it as evidence of eyewitness memory, and even Bultmann's form-critical skepticism (*History of the Synoptic Tradition*) had trouble fitting it into a clean genre slot.

Textual variants in this chapter are mostly minor — variations on "Gerasenes / Gadarenes / Gergesenes" in 5:1, the spelling of *Talitha koum* / *koumi* in 5:41, and the inclusion or omission of "of the Jews" qualifier on certain phrases — but the geographic variant has theological consequences, since each toponym yields a different geography for the miracle.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Read along the canonical arc, Mark 5 sits at the seam where Jesus's ministry first crosses into Gentile territory. The exorcism is not isolated — the Decapolis returns in chapter 7 (the Syrophoenician woman, the deaf-mute) and in chapter 8 (the feeding of the four thousand), forming a "Gentile cycle" that mirrors and extends the earlier Galilean ministry. The Gerasene demoniac is, in a quiet but unmistakable way, the first Gentile evangelist in the Gospel. Jesus refuses his request to come along (5:18) and instead sends him back to "tell them how much the Lord has done for you" (5:19). The verb in 5:20, ***kēryssein*** (κηρύσσειν, "to proclaim") is the same verb Mark uses of John the Baptist's preaching (1:4) and of Jesus's own (1:14). The man left for dead among the tombs becomes a herald.

The interpretive trajectory of this chapter shows striking divergence between Jewish and Christian readings.

Within the Jewish reception of Jesus, scholars have approached the chapter through the categories of charismatic Galilean piety and intra-Jewish purity reform. Geza Vermes (*Jesus the Jew*, 1973) placed Jesus's exorcisms inside the wider phenomenon of Galilean *hasidim* — Honi the Circle-Drawer, Hanina ben Dosa — for whom direct miraculous power and disregard for some priestly purity strictures were already attested. The bleeding-woman story, on this reading, is not a polemic against Torah but a story about a Jewish woman seeking healing from a Jewish healer in a Jewish way. E. P. Sanders (*The Historical Figure of Jesus*, 1993) emphasized that Jesus does not cancel the law of Leviticus 15 here; the woman is healed and presumably goes home and observes the standard purification. Daniel Boyarin (*The Jewish Gospels*, 2012) has argued more daringly that the "Son of the Most High God" / "Son of Man" Christology of Mark draws on Second Temple Jewish apocalyptic streams — a divine human figure (Daniel 7, the Parables of Enoch) — that were Jewish before they were Christian. Amy-Jill Levine (*The Misunderstood Jew*, 2006; with Marc Brettler in *The Jewish Annotated New Testament*, 2017) has consistently warned against reading the bleeding-woman story as a story about Jewish patriarchy versus Christian liberation: the Mosaic purity laws are about ritual status, not moral worth, and Mark's story works perfectly well as an internal Jewish narrative about a woman whose faith brings her into contact with God's healing presence. David Flusser (*Jesus*, 1968/1997) emphasized Jesus's continuity with Pharisaic and apocalyptic Judaism and read his exorcisms as eschatological signs of the Kingdom rather than as a break with Israel's tradition.

Within the Christian interpretive trajectory, the readings have been thicker but pull in several directions. Origen, in his *Commentary on Matthew* and surviving fragments on Mark, read the Gerasene demoniac allegorically: Legion is the demonic horde of vices, the tombs are the dead state of the soul before Christ. Augustine, in *De Consensu Evangelistarum* (Book 2), tackled the synoptic discrepancy — Matthew has two demoniacs, Mark and Luke have one — and argued for harmonization rather than picking one Evangelist over another. Bede's eighth-century *Commentary on Mark* read the chapter ecclesially: the demoniac in the tombs is the Gentile world before evangelization; the swine are demonic powers driven into the abyss; the bleeding woman is the church healed of sin by a touch of Christ's garment. Calvin's *Harmony of the Gospels* (1555) was characteristically restrained — he warned against allegorizing too freely and emphasized that the woman's faith, however irregular, was met by Christ's mercy rather than rebuked. Luther read the bleeding woman as the paradigm of *sola fide*: she does not even speak to Jesus, only touches his garment, and the touch of faith — however hidden in a crowd — receives what the most decorous and properly observant cannot.

Twentieth-century scholarship turned increasingly literary and political. Bonhoeffer's *Discipleship* read the chapter under the rubric of costly grace: the demoniac sent home rather than allowed to follow is the disciple sent into the world rather than allowed the comfort of pious proximity. Bultmann's form-critical work classed the exorcism among "novelistic miracle stories" with parallels in Hellenistic *aretalogies*, but his demythologizing program left him uncertain about what to do with the residual scandal of the swine. The towering modern commentaries — Joel Marcus (*Mark*, Anchor Bible, 2000), Adela Yarbro Collins (*Mark*, Hermeneia, 2007), R. T. France (*Mark*, NIGTC, 2002), Morna Hooker (*Mark*, BNTC, 1991), Robert Guelich and Craig Evans (*Mark*, WBC) — converge on a high-Christology reading of the chapter (Jesus exercises divine prerogative over chaos, demons, sickness, and death) while disagreeing on whether Mark's Christology is essentially apocalyptic-eschatological (Marcus, Yarbro Collins) or covenantal-fulfillment (France, Hooker).

The literary critics have made distinctive contributions. Frank Kermode's *Genesis of Secrecy* (1979) returned again and again to Mark 5 as a test case for the Gospel's strange refusal of explanation. Why does Jesus permit the demons to enter the swine? Why does he silence the demoniac who would have spread his fame and command the family who must explain a living child? Kermode argued the chapter is built on a structural reticence that is the Gospel's deepest signature. Rhoads, Dewey, and Michie (*Mark as Story*, 1982; 3rd ed. 2012) traced the chapter's narrative point of view, noting how Mark grants the bleeding woman an interior voice — "*if I touch even his garments*" — that he denies even Jesus's mother. Mary Ann Tolbert (*Sowing the Gospel*, 1989) read the chapter through the parable of the sower, with each healing as a different kind of soil. Ched Myers's political reading (already cited) and Richard Horsley's work on hidden transcripts of resistance have made the anti-imperial reading of Legion impossible to ignore. Wendy Cotter (*Miracles in Greco-Roman Antiquity*, 1999) and Robert Guelich's WBC treatment have set the chapter against the comparative background of Mediterranean touch-healing, underscoring the Markan distinctiveness of a Jesus who registers the bleeding woman's contact bodily — power *exelthousan*, "going out from him" (5:30) — but commends rather than rebukes her.

N. T. Wright, in *Mark for Everyone* (2001) and at greater length in *Jesus and the Victory of God* (1996), folds Mark 5 into his larger argument that Jesus's exorcisms are not free-floating displays of power but the in-breaking of the kingdom of God — the moment when YHWH's reign begins to push back the forces of chaos, idolatry, and (in the Legion narrative) pagan empire. For Wright, the demoniac among the tombs is a picture of Israel and the nations under bondage, and the drowning of the swine deliberately echoes the Exodus: the new exodus has begun, and the powers are being undone. The bleeding woman and Jairus's daughter then become signs of the same thing on the level of the body — the return from "death," ritual exclusion, and despair that the prophets had promised would mark the day of restoration. Wright resists both a purely spiritualized reading (the exorcism as a metaphor for inner peace) and a purely political one (the exorcism as coded anti-Roman polemic and nothing more); for him the chapter holds both, because the kingdom Jesus announces is YHWH becoming king over the whole created order.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions run through Mark 5, and each deserves a careful steelman before it is resolved (or, as is often the case in Mark, left unresolved).

**Tension 1: Why does Jesus permit the destruction of the swine?** The economic and ethical scandal of the chapter is that two thousand pigs die so that one man may be restored, and the Decapolis townspeople respond by asking Jesus to leave (5:17). Three positions:

The *high-allegorical* position (Origen, Bede, Calvin in moments) argues that the swine are themselves figures — for the unclean Gentile world, for the demonic horde, or for the consequences of unrestrained legion-evil — and that their destruction is theologically rather than economically read. The animals are vehicles of the parable, not its victims. The position is strongest when the chapter is read inside the canonical pattern of the Exodus (chariots into the sea, Pharaoh's army drowned).

The *political-anti-imperial* position (Myers, Horsley, more cautiously Yarbro Collins) reads the swine through the Tenth Legion *Fretensis* boar-standard. The colonial subject's psyche is fragmented by occupation; the demons request not to be sent out of the country (5:10) — they belong to the local power structure; the pigs into the sea is the Roman military thrown back on itself. The townspeople ask Jesus to leave because his kind of liberation costs them their accommodation with the empire. Steelmanned, this reading takes seriously the choice of *legiōn*, the geographic specificity, and the political stakes Jewish hearers would have heard.

The *historical-literal* position (Sanders, France, Hooker) takes the herd as a herd. Pigs were Gentile property; the loss is real and economic; the townspeople's fear and request for Jesus to leave are intelligible as the response of a community that has just witnessed massive economic destruction and an unsettling display of power. The theological cost of Jesus's healing is not erased — it is registered, and the chapter does not resolve it.

*Where this leaves us:* The text refuses any one of these readings. The swine die. The man is healed. The townspeople ask Jesus to leave. Mark does not explain. The most honest reading holds the political reverberation (Legion is not innocent vocabulary) alongside the economic-historical fact (a real herd, real property, real fear) and lets the moral residue stand.

**Tension 2: What is the relationship between faith and healing power?** The bleeding woman's story complicates everything. She is healed before she speaks to Jesus. The power leaves him without his consent. He turns to find her. Three positions:

The *transactional-faith* position (some Reformation readings, much popular preaching) argues that her faith is the cause of her healing — Jesus says so explicitly: "Daughter, your faith has made you well" (5:34). Faith, on this reading, is the active principle; Jesus is its proper object.

The *Christic-power* position (much patristic and Catholic reading, modernized in some sacramental theology) emphasizes the *dynamis* (power) that flows from Jesus's body. The power is real, ontological, located in him. Faith does not generate the power; it apprehends it. This reading is helped by the close link between the woman's touch of his cloak and later sacramental theology (cf. the early Christian use of relics and the typological reading of the woman's bronze statue at Caesarea Philippi reported by Eusebius).

The *encounter-relational* position (Marcus, Yarbro Collins, Hooker, with Wright in *Mark for Everyone*) sees neither pure faith nor pure power, but a relational event in which the healing is brought to completion only in the meeting. Jesus notices the loss of power; the woman comes "in fear and trembling" and tells the whole truth (5:33); the healing is finished in the dialogue, not in the touch alone. The woman who reached anonymously is named "daughter" and given the dignity of explicit declaration — and for Wright this is the point: the kingdom does not merely repair bodies, it restores people to the covenant family.

*Where this leaves us:* The chapter binds the three readings together rather than choosing among them. The woman's faith is real; Jesus's power is real; the encounter and word — *thygatēr*, "daughter" — is what completes the healing into shalom. The bleeding-woman pericope is, in this sense, the most relationally textured healing in Mark, and it is the interpretive key for what comes next: Jairus has just heard "your daughter is dead" (5:35), and Jesus has just demonstrated, in the sandwich's middle slice, what kind of God he serves.

**One unresolved question:** Why does Mark insist that the Gerasene demoniac *not* follow Jesus, while Jairus's family is told to *say nothing* about the resurrection of the daughter (5:43)? The two commands run in opposite directions — one sent out to proclaim, one sworn to silence. The Messianic Secret cannot easily account for both. Is the difference geographic (Gentile Decapolis can hear; Jewish Galilee cannot yet)? Christological (a Gentile witness to mercy is not yet a witness to messianic identity)? Or is Mark himself working with traditions he has not fully harmonized? The question remains open, and its sharpness is part of why the chapter has held interpreters' attention for two thousand years.

## The Thread

Mark 5 reaches forward and backward across the canon. The phrase "**the country of the Gerasenes**" inaugurates a Gentile cycle that returns at 7:24 (Tyre), 7:31 (Decapolis), and 8:1–10 (the four thousand) — Mark is building a structure in which the Gentile mission begins not at the resurrection, but already in Galilee, in the disciples' confused presence. The number twelve in the Jairus-bleeding-woman sandwich looks ahead to the twelve baskets of the feeding (6:43) and back to the twelve disciples (3:14); the daughter who is "only sleeping" anticipates the Son of Man who will rise (8:31). The Aramaic *Talitha koum* will be paired by *Ephphatha* in 7:34 and by *Abba* in 14:36 — Mark's three preserved Aramaic moments of intimate divine speech, each at a hinge of the Gospel's structure.

The chapter also points forward to two later passages worth flagging. The first is 9:14–29, the disciples' failed exorcism of the boy with a deaf-mute spirit: there, the disciples cannot cast out what Jesus dispatches, and the father's "I believe; help my unbelief" (9:24) is the explicit echo of Jairus's "Do not fear, only believe" (5:36). The second is 14:3–9, the anonymous woman who anoints Jesus's head at Bethany. Like the bleeding woman she is unnamed, like her she breaks a social code by touching, and like her she is granted Jesus's defense against the crowd that would dismiss her. Mark places anonymous women at the chapter's healing center and at the passion's threshold. The reader is meant to notice.
