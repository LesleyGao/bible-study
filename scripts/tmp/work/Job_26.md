# Job 26: The Outskirts of His Ways

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Job 26 sits inside one of the most disputed stretches of the Hebrew Bible. The third cycle of dialogues (chapters 22–27) appears textually disturbed: Bildad's third speech (25) is unusually short, Zophar disappears entirely, and Job's answer here opens with sarcasm aimed at a singular "you" ("How you have helped him who has no power!") before swelling into a cosmological hymn that many scholars judge out of character with Job's complaining voice. Marvin Pope, in the Anchor Bible *Job*, and David Clines, in his three-volume Word Biblical Commentary, both argue that 26:5–14 belongs to Bildad (or to a lost Zophar speech) and has been dislodged in transmission. Norman Habel, in the Old Testament Library *The Book of Job*, resists this and reads the hymn as Job's deliberate appropriation of friend-speech — Job seizing the very theology of divine majesty used against him and turning it toward his defense.

The question of authorship and dating remains genuinely contested. Marvin Pope, Clines, and Carol Newsom (in her New Interpreter's Bible commentary and later *The Book of Job: A Contest of Moral Imaginations*) argue for a post-exilic date, seeing Job as a sustained theological response to the catastrophe of 587 BC and the collapse of Deuteronomic retributive assumptions. Against them, Robert Gordis (*The Book of God and Man*) and Francis Andersen (Tyndale) argue for a pre-exilic setting — perhaps seventh century or earlier — citing the archaic Hebrew, the absence of Mosaic and covenantal vocabulary, and the patriarchal world of the narrative frame. Ezekiel 14:14 and 14:20, where Job appears beside Noah and Danel as a paradigmatic righteous sufferer, establishes only that the figure of Job was legendary by the sixth century; it does not settle when the book in its present form was composed.

Material and textual evidence anchors the chapter in a much older cosmological vocabulary. The *Ludlul Bel Nemeqi* ("I Will Praise the Lord of Wisdom"), a Middle Babylonian poem often called "The Babylonian Job," shares the pattern of a righteous sufferer abandoned by his gods and finally vindicated. The *Babylonian Theodicy*, an acrostic dialogue, closely parallels the Joban friends' defense of divine justice. More directly relevant to chapter 26 are the Ugaritic texts from Ras Shamra, especially the Baal Cycle, which preserve the West Semitic chaos-combat myth: Baal's defeat of Yam (Sea), of Litan/Lotan (the fleeing serpent), and of Tannin (sea-dragon). Job 26:12–13 deploys precisely this inherited imagery, demythologized into monotheistic creation theology. The Qumran *Targum of Job* (11QTargum Job, 11Q10), one of the oldest Aramaic translations of any biblical book, preserves fragments covering chapters 17–42; the Septuagint Job, notoriously about one-sixth shorter than the Masoretic Text, reflects an early translator's discomfort with the book's difficulty.

Several Hebrew terms carry the historical weight of the chapter. רְפָאִים (*rephaim*, v. 5) — "the dead" or "shades" — names the ghost-inhabitants of the underworld, cognate with Ugaritic *rpum*, the dead kings of Ugarit invoked in royal rites. אֲבַדּוֹן (*ʾavaddon*, v. 6) — "Abaddon," "destruction," "place of ruin" — pairs with Sheol as the lowest reach of the realm of the dead. רַהַב (*rahav*, v. 12) — "Rahab" — is a mythological chaos-monster, the same figure invoked in Isaiah 51:9, Psalm 89:10, and Job 9:13, cognate in function if not etymology to Ugaritic Yam. נָחָשׁ בָּרִחַ (*naḥash bariaḥ*, v. 13) — "fleeing serpent" — matches *bṯn brḥ* in the Ugaritic Baal Cycle's description of Litan/Lotan, the seven-headed dragon. צָפוֹן (*tsaphon*, v. 7) — "the north," but also "Zaphon," the mountain dwelling of Baal in Ugaritic mythology — collapses cosmic geography and theology in a single word. These are not decorative allusions. They are the structural vocabulary of second-millennium Levantine cosmology, preserved and transformed.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is built in two uneven halves. Verses 1–4 form a tight ironic prologue: four mocking questions, each opening with מָה (*mah*, "how"), addressed to a singular "you" who has "helped him who has no power." Verses 5–14 then shift register entirely into a cosmological hymn — ten verses of creation poetry moving from the depths (Sheol, v. 6) upward through earth (v. 7), clouds (vv. 8–9), the horizon-circle between light and dark (v. 10), and the pillars of heaven (v. 11), to a climactic combat-creation stanza (vv. 12–13) and a closing confession of God's unsearchable grandeur (v. 14).

This structural asymmetry is precisely what drives the textual-critical problem. Robert Alter in *The Wisdom Books* notes that the sarcastic opening fits Job's voice elsewhere in the book but the hymn's tone of awed submission fits it badly; Clines goes further and reassigns vv. 5–14 to Bildad, pairing them with the short fragment in chapter 25. Habel defends the unity: Job, he argues, is mastering the friends' rhetoric so completely that he can outdo them at their own cosmic praise. Janzen (Interpretation commentary) occupies a middle position, reading the hymn as Job's concession that God is indeed inscrutable — while refusing to concede that inscrutability licenses the friends' retributive theology.

Hebrew parallelism governs nearly every line. Verse 6 is a tight synonymous couplet (Sheol // Abaddon; "naked before him" // "has no covering"). Verse 7's famous cosmography — נֹטֶה צָפוֹן עַל־תֹּהוּ תֹּלֶה אֶרֶץ עַל־בְּלִי־מָה (*noteh tsaphon ʿal-tohu, toleh ʾerets ʿal-beli-mah*) — stretches north "over *tohu*" (the primordial void of Genesis 1:2) and hangs earth "on *beli-mah*" ("not-anything," a rare negative construction often translated "on nothing"). The terms echo Genesis's creation vocabulary while adding a quasi-architectural image (stretching, hanging) that reappears in Isaiah 40:22. Verse 10's "circle on the face of the waters" uses חֹק־חָג (*ḥoq-ḥag*), "an inscribed circle," the same root that gives Proverbs 8:27 its famous *ḥug* at creation. Verse 12's verbs — רָגַע (*ragaʿ*, "stilled") and מָחַץ (*maḥats*, "shattered") — are combat verbs, and the pairing mirrors Ugaritic Baal-Yam duels almost structurally.

Inclusio binds the hymn: it opens with Sheol beneath and closes with thunder above (v. 14), a vertical sweep from the dead to the storm-voice of God. The closing line — וְרַעַם גְּבוּרֹתָיו מִי יִתְבּוֹנָן (*weraʿam gevurotav mi yitbonan*), "but the thunder of his power who can understand?" — sets up the whirlwind speeches of chapters 38–41, where God finally speaks from precisely such a storm. The chapter is, among other things, an anticipatory epigraph for the book's climax.

Textual variants compound the puzzle. The LXX of Job 26 is substantially shorter, dropping or compressing several verses, a pattern characteristic of the Old Greek translator's tendency to smooth the book's difficult theology. 11QTargum Job 26 preserves most of the chapter in Aramaic paraphrase, generally confirming the Masoretic shape but occasionally softening the mythological vocabulary (Rahab, for instance, becomes a more generic term for the sea). These variants suggest that ancient readers, like modern ones, found the hymn both theologically crucial and uncomfortably close to the chaos mythologies it was demythologizing.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Canonically, Job stands in the third division of the Hebrew Bible (the Ketuvim) alongside Proverbs and Ecclesiastes as one of the three wisdom books. In most Christian orderings, it opens the poetic-wisdom section between Esther and the Psalms. Its placement matters: the retributive theology of Proverbs ("the wise prosper, the fools perish") and the cynical theology of Ecclesiastes ("all is vapor") frame Job as the middle term — the book that refuses both easy moralism and easy despair.

The Jewish reception trajectory begins early. The *Testament of Job* (first century BCE–first century CE) rewrites the narrative as pious martyrdom, making Job a proto-patriarch who destroys idols and trains his children in virtue. Bava Batra 15a–16b in the Babylonian Talmud contains the famous rabbinic debate about whether Job existed at all — Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani memorably says Job was "neither created nor existed, but was a parable" (*mashal hayah*) — and about when he lived (the range offered stretches from Abraham to Ahasuerus). Rashi reads Job 26 as Job conceding God's power while maintaining his innocence, not as capitulation. Ibn Ezra attends to the Hebrew precision of v. 7 and defends a philosophical reading of the hanging earth. Maimonides, in *Guide of the Perplexed* III.22–23, treats the book as a philosophical allegory of providence, arguing that each friend (and Elihu) represents a different doctrine of providence — Job himself representing the Aristotelian view before being corrected by revelation. Nachmanides reads Job through the lens of gilgul (transmigration) and divine mystery. In modern Jewish thought, Martin Buber (*At the Turning*) sees Job as a paradigm of dialogical faith confronting God directly; Jon Levenson (*Creation and the Persistence of Evil*) argues that Job 26's combat-imagery preserves the Israelite memory that creation is not the defeat of chaos once and for all but an ongoing divine labor against threat; James Kugel (*The Great Shift*) traces how Job's God becomes progressively more remote from speaking subject to inscrutable whirlwind; Avivah Zornberg reads Job as a working through of traumatic silence.

The Christian reception is even longer. Origen's fragmentary commentary treats Job as a type of Christ — the blameless sufferer. John Chrysostom's homilies emphasize patience. Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job*, written in thirty-five books across a decade, is the single most influential medieval reading, producing the allegorical interpretation that dominated Western Christianity for nine hundred years: Job is the Church, his wife is carnal temptation, his friends are heretics, his restoration is the resurrection. Augustine cites Job repeatedly in *City of God* on innocent suffering and providence. Aquinas's *Expositio super Iob* reads the book philosophically as an argument for providence after death — since the righteous clearly do not always prosper in this life, their reward must lie beyond it. John Calvin preached 159 sermons on Job in 1554–55, an extraordinary pastoral-doctrinal effort in which he stressed divine sovereignty, the depth of human corruption, and the limits of human understanding — reading Job 26 especially as a corrective to anthropocentric theology. Luther treated Job primarily in *Tischreden* and brief lectures, emphasizing the hiddenness of God (*Deus absconditus*).

The critical tradition begins with Julius Wellhausen and Hermann Gunkel, who identified the prose frame as older narrative and the poetic dialogues as later, and who traced the combat-creation imagery of Job 26:12–13 to ancient Near Eastern mythology. Gerhard von Rad, in *Old Testament Theology* and *Wisdom in Israel*, situated Job within Israel's wisdom tradition's growing awareness of the limits of human knowledge. The modern commentary tradition — Clines (WBC, three volumes), Newsom (NIB and monograph), Habel (OTL), Hartley (NICOT), Andersen (Tyndale), Pope (Anchor), Gordis, Janzen — produces the most detailed philological and literary analysis yet attempted, with Gordis in particular treating Job's protest as a prophetic act the book endorses rather than rebukes. Ellen Davis (*Getting Involved with God*) reads Job as a work of moral imagination. Susannah Ticciati (*Job and the Disruption of Identity*) reads Job's trajectory as the dismantling and reconstruction of selfhood before God. Robert Fyall's *Now My Eyes Have Seen You* gives particular attention to the mythological imagery of Rahab and Leviathan, arguing that the book treats cosmic evil with theological seriousness.

A significant convergence runs across Jewish and Christian readings on Job 26 specifically: both traditions read the hymn as an affirmation of divine transcendence that neither solves nor dismisses Job's protest. A sharp divergence appears over the status of Rahab: premodern Christian readers frequently allegorized Rahab as Satan or as the demonic, whereas the Jewish tradition — and modern critical scholarship across both faiths — treats the figure mythologically, as a chaos-monster within Israel's inherited cosmological vocabulary.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Does Job 26 belong to Job, or has it migrated from a friend's mouth?**

- *It belongs to Job, ironically appropriated.* Habel and (with qualifications) Janzen argue that Job is deliberately out-praising his friends, deploying their own cosmological vocabulary against them. The sarcastic opening is the hermeneutical key: Job frames the hymn as a mocking demonstration that he knows divine majesty at least as well as Bildad does, and that merely knowing it does nothing to answer his actual question about justice. On this reading, the chapter is rhetorically brilliant rather than textually disturbed.

- *It belongs to Bildad (or the missing Zophar speech).* Clines, Pope, and a broad stream of twentieth-century critical scholarship reassign vv. 5–14. The hymn's tone — unqualified submission before divine power — is what Job's friends urge and what Job consistently resists elsewhere. The sarcastic opening (vv. 1–4) would be Job's, the hymn (vv. 5–14) a friend's. On this reading, the third-cycle disruption is a genuine textual dislocation masked by later editorial smoothing.

- *Steelman — the hymn is editorial, a kind of cosmological chorus.* Newsom's subtler position (*Contest of Moral Imaginations*) is that Job is polyphonic by design: multiple voices, each genuinely articulated, are staged without the book forcing a resolution. Verses 5–14 may function something like a Greek chorus — a cosmological commentary spoken in Job's mouth but not strictly his, inviting the reader to hold the hymn's grandeur alongside the protest that frames it. This reading preserves the literary integrity of the received text while taking the voicing problem seriously.

**Tension 2: What does creation-combat imagery (Rahab, the fleeing serpent) actually do in Israelite monotheism?**

- *Demythologization.* The classic critical reading (Gunkel, von Rad, Clines) holds that Israel inherited West Semitic chaos-combat myths (Baal-Yam, Marduk-Tiamat) and systematically drained them of their mythological content: the gods are no longer rivals, the chaos is no longer divine, and YHWH's "shattering of Rahab" is a poetic memory of a combat that is no longer ontologically real. Creation is complete; the dragon is dead; the language is residual.

- *Live combat cosmology.* Levenson (*Creation and the Persistence of Evil*) and Fyall argue the opposite. The imagery is not residual but active: Israel's creation theology preserves the sense that creation is a *holding-back* of chaos rather than a finished masterpiece. Rahab is shattered, but chaos persists at the edges of the world; evil is not metaphysically abolished but continually restrained. On this reading, Job 26's combat-creation hymn is precisely the right theological vocabulary for a book about inexplicable suffering.

- *Steelman — apophatic cosmology.* Susannah Ticciati and readers in conversation with patristic theology suggest a third possibility. The imagery does not tell us what God is like; it tells us what God is *unlike*. "These are but the outskirts of his ways" (v. 14) is the epistemological claim around which everything else orbits. Rahab, Sheol, the pillars of heaven, the circle on the waters — these are the verbal limits of creaturely speech about God. The hymn is not finally a cosmology but a rhetorical performance of the unknowability of God.

Where this leaves us: Job 26 wants to do two incompatible things at once. It wants to confess God's majesty in inherited mythological vocabulary, and it wants to refuse that very confession as an answer to Job's specific question. The chapter's power is its refusal to decide. The thunder of his power who can understand? — *who can understand?* is not rhetorical. It is the question the book will not finally answer.

One unresolved question: If even Job's grandest cosmological confession leaves him still sitting on the ash-heap, demanding a hearing, what does that say about the relationship between doctrinal knowledge of God and lived encounter with God?

## The Thread

- **Proverbs 8:27** — *beḥuqo ḥug ʿal-pene tehom*, "when he inscribed a circle on the face of the deep" — the same vocabulary of inscribed circle and waters as Job 26:10, but now in the voice of personified Wisdom rather than suffering Job. The two chapters share a cosmology and divide on who gets to speak it.

- **Isaiah 51:9–10** — "Was it not you who cut Rahab in pieces, who pierced the dragon?" — the prophetic invocation of the same combat-creation myth at the heart of an exilic plea for God to act again in history. Job's past-tense hymn becomes Isaiah's prayer for repetition.

- **Psalm 89:10** — "You crushed Rahab like a carcass" — embedded in a lament over the failure of Davidic covenant. Rahab imagery is not confined to creation; it surfaces wherever Israel's theology is under pressure.

- **Colossians 1:17** — "in him all things hold together" (*ta panta en autō synestēken*) — the New Testament's Christological answer to "hangs the earth on nothing." Whatever the cosmic nothing is on which the earth hangs, Paul locates it in the Son.
