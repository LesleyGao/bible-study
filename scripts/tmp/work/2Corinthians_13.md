# 2 Corinthians 13 — Going Deeper

## Historical World

Chapter 13 closes both 2 Corinthians and what most modern critics regard as the most polemical letter in the Pauline corpus. Paul founded the church in Roman Corinth around AD 50–51, wrote 1 Corinthians from Ephesus around 53–54, made a "painful visit" that went badly, sent a "tearful letter" by Titus, and from Macedonia around 55–56 wrote what we now read as 2 Corinthians. The partition theory associated with Hausrath, Kennedy, F.F. Bruce, Victor Furnish (Anchor Yale 1984), and Ralph Martin (Word Biblical Commentary 1986) treats chapters 10–13 as the lost tearful letter, joined editorially to a separate, more reconciliatory body in chapters 1–9. Margaret Thrall (ICC 2000) and Frank Matera (NTL 2003) regard the unity question as undecidable but the rhetorical break at 10:1 as real. Paul Barnett (NICNT 1997), Murray Harris (NIGTC 2005), and Ben Witherington (Conflict and Community 1995) argue for unity. P46, the Chester Beatty papyrus from around AD 200, already preserves 2 Corinthians as a single document; whether the editorial joining happened before or after that point is one of the field's most durable cold cases.

The setting Paul addresses in chapter 13 is concrete. He is preparing his third visit to Corinth (13:1–2). Some Corinthians are still aligned with the rival "super-apostles" (11:5; 12:11) and continue to question Paul's apostolic authenticity. Others have repented but live under the same opaque cloud. Paul's letter is meant to do the disciplinary work in advance, so that on arrival he will not need to "be severe in [his] use of the authority that the Lord has given" (13:10). The threat is real — he cites Deuteronomy 19:15's two-or-three-witness rule (13:1) as the procedural framework for the discipline he intends. C.K. Barrett (BNTC 1973) read this as Paul invoking Torah court procedure for the church, the earliest concrete glimpse of how Pauline communities handled internal discipline.

The closing benediction in 13:14 is the most studied verse in the chapter. The triadic formula — "the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ and the love of God and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit be with you all" — appears nowhere else in the Pauline corpus in this exact form. Larry Hurtado, in Lord Jesus Christ (2003), placed it among the foundational texts for early high Christology: Paul names Jesus Christ coordinately with God and the Spirit, in a liturgical blessing, within twenty-five years of the crucifixion. Richard Bauckham's Jesus and the God of Israel (2008) made the same case from a different angle, arguing that Paul includes Jesus within the unique divine identity of the Shema-confessing God of Israel. Wesley Hill's Paul and the Trinity (2015) traced the relational logic — grace, love, fellowship — as a triadic mutuality that became, only later, the structural grammar of the Nicene-Constantinopolitan creed.

The Corinthian church Paul is addressing was a small house-church network in a wealthy Roman colonial city refounded by Julius Caesar in 44 BC. Acts 18 names Crispus the synagogue ruler, Gaius the host, Stephanas the householder, Erastus the city treasurer (Romans 16:23). The factions inside the church (1 Corinthians 1:12) and the patron-client tensions over Paul's refusal of pay (2 Corinthians 11:7–11) are part of why the discipline language in 13:1–10 lands so hard. Paul is writing into a community where status, money, eloquence, and pneumatic display all carry social weight, and his entire letter has been an effort to invert the value scale.

## Literature

The chapter divides into three units. Verses 1–4 announce the imminent third visit and ground Paul's authority in the cross-shaped pattern of weakness-and-power. Verses 5–10 invert the test: instead of the Corinthians testing Paul, Paul commands them to test themselves. Verses 11–14 close the letter with a five-fold final exhortation, the holy kiss, the saints' greeting, and the triadic benediction.

The opening (13:1–4) is dense with the chapter's central paradox. Paul cites Deuteronomy 19:15 ("every charge must be established by the evidence of two or three witnesses"), then frames his own three visits as the implicit witness-count: his coming, his warnings, his return. The argument shifts in verse 3: the Corinthians have demanded "proof that Christ is speaking in me." Paul will give them proof — but the proof is christologically structured. Christ "was crucified in weakness, but lives by the power of God" (13:4). Paul's apostolic life mirrors that pattern: weak in Christ, but powerful in dealing with the Corinthians by the power of God. The chiasm is exact. The cross is not a bypassed embarrassment but the template — for Christ, for Paul, for the church.

The middle unit (13:5–10) executes the chapter's most striking rhetorical move. The Corinthians have been examining Paul. Paul commands them, with force, to examine themselves: heautous peirazete, "test yourselves." The verb dokimazō and its negation adokimos ("disqualified," "failing the test") run through verses 5–7 like a refrain. Paul is willing to seem disqualified if it means the Corinthians will be vindicated (13:7). The pastoral generosity here is striking, given the polemic of the previous three chapters. Verse 8 — "we cannot do anything against the truth, but only for the truth" — is one of Paul's plainest theological statements about apostolic limits: the apostle is bound to the truth and has no authority against it. Verse 10 closes the unit with the binary of authority that has structured the whole letter: oikodomē (building up) versus kathairesis (tearing down). Paul wants to use authority for construction, not demolition.

The closing (13:11–14) shifts to the conventional letter-ending genre but loads it with theological weight. The five-fold imperative chain in verse 11 — "rejoice, aim for restoration, comfort one another, agree with one another, live in peace" — is shaped to address the Corinthian factions specifically. Katartizesthe ("aim for restoration," from katartisis, putting in order) is the same root as the noun used of mending nets in Mark 1:19 and Matthew 4:21. Paul wants the Corinthian community mended, not torn. The "holy kiss" (philēma hagion) is the standard Pauline closing greeting (Romans 16:16; 1 Corinthians 16:20; 1 Thessalonians 5:26), eventually becoming the kiss of peace in early Christian liturgy.

Verse 14 is the literary climax. The triadic benediction — charis tou Kyriou Iēsou Christou, agapē tou theou, koinōnia tou Hagiou Pneumatos — is metrically balanced, liturgically weighted, and theologically extraordinary. Each element pairs a quality with a divine person: grace with Christ, love with God, fellowship with the Spirit. The genitives are typically read as subjective: the grace that Christ gives, the love that God gives, the fellowship that the Spirit gives, or alternatively the fellowship that the Spirit creates among believers. The structure became, by the third and fourth centuries, the seedbed for explicit Trinitarian doctrine.

## Larger Story

### Jewish Trajectory

Paul's invocation of Deuteronomy 19:15 in 13:1 is not ornamental. The two-or-three-witness rule was the foundational procedural standard of Jewish legal life. The Mishnah, codified around AD 200 but preserving earlier oral tradition, devotes large tracts of tractates Sanhedrin and Makkot to witness procedure. The beit din (court) operated with strict standards on testimony: collusion was disqualifying, hearsay was excluded, the witnesses had to lay hands at the execution they had testified to. Paul, addressing a mostly Gentile church, nevertheless reaches for Jewish procedural law as the appropriate framework for ecclesial discipline. Daniel Boyarin's reading of Paul (A Radical Jew, 1994) emphasizes that Paul is operating within Jewish legal-religious idiom even as he addresses non-Jews; Mark Nanos and Pamela Eisenbaum within the Paul Within Judaism school have made the broader case that Paul's letters are best read as Jewish documents addressing Gentiles, not as documents announcing a break from Judaism.

The triadic structure of 13:14 is the more delicate Jewish-Christian question. First-century Judaism was strictly monotheistic: the Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4) — "Hear, O Israel, the LORD our God, the LORD is one" — was recited daily and structured every theological move. Paul affirms this Shema in 1 Corinthians 8:6 ("for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things") but immediately extends it ("and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things"). Bauckham's argument is that this is not abandonment of monotheism but reconfiguration: the unique divine identity of YHWH is enlarged to include Jesus. Hurtado's parallel argument is sociological: Pauline communities offered Jesus the kind of devotion (prayer, hymnody, baptism in his name, the Lord's Supper) that Jewish monotheism reserved for God alone — and they did this within a generation of the crucifixion.

The Jewish counter-position is sharp. Maimonides, in the Mishneh Torah (Yesodei haTorah 1) and in his Thirteen Principles of Faith (especially principle 2 on God's strict, incorporeal unity), articulated what became the standard medieval Jewish position: God is one in a sense that absolutely excludes any plurality of persons or any divine incarnation. The Trinitarian reading of 13:14, on this view, is precisely where Christianity ceases to be intelligibly continuous with Israel's faith. The disagreement is not a misunderstanding; it is the central theological divide between Judaism and Christianity, and 2 Corinthians 13:14 is one of the verses on which the divide has been worked out.

The Hebrew Bible itself contains triadic and binitarian phenomena that Christian exegetes have read as anticipations: the three figures who visit Abraham at Mamre (Genesis 18), the threefold "holy, holy, holy" of Isaiah 6:3, the Word and Spirit of Genesis 1, the Angel of the LORD in whom God's Name dwells (Exodus 23:21). Jewish exegesis reads these differently — the visitors as three angels, the threefold holy as emphatic intensification, the Word and Spirit as God's modes of action without ontological distinction. The disagreement is exegetical at the surface and ontological underneath.

### Christian Trajectory

The Trinitarian benediction generated centuries of doctrinal development. Tertullian, around AD 200 in Adversus Praxean, was the first Latin theologian to use trinitas as a technical term and to articulate the relations: una substantia, tres personae, one substance, three persons. Origen in De Principiis 1 worked out the eternal generation of the Son. The Council of Nicaea (325), pressed by the Arian controversy, defined the Son as homoousios — of the same substance — with the Father; Athanasius's lifelong defense of that formula in Contra Arianos and the Letters to Serapion extended the same logic to the Spirit. The Council of Constantinople (381) added the explicit formulation about the Holy Spirit ("the Lord, the Giver of life, who proceeds from the Father"). The Cappadocian theologians — Basil, Gregory of Nazianzus, Gregory of Nyssa — articulated the now-classical grammar: one ousia (essence), three hypostases (persons), with the persons distinguished by their relations of origin. Augustine's De Trinitate (begun around 399, finished around 419) explored psychological analogies — memory, understanding, will — and shaped Western Trinitarian theology for the next thousand years.

The Filioque controversy split the church on the question of the Spirit's procession. The original Constantinopolitan creed of 381 confessed the Spirit "proceeds from the Father." The Western church, beginning with the Third Council of Toledo (589) and working its way into general use, added "and the Son" — Filioque. Photius of Constantinople in the ninth century and the formal break of 1054 made this an east-west dividing line. The recent ecumenical conversations between Catholic and Orthodox bodies — Vatican-Orthodox dialogues since the 1980s, the 1995 Vatican Clarification on the Filioque — have not closed the gap, but they have produced more careful reading of what each tradition is actually claiming.

Modern Trinitarian theology has been shaped by a wave of fresh work. Karl Barth's Church Dogmatics (especially the doctrine of the Word of God in CD I/1) re-centered Trinitarian thinking on revelation. Karl Rahner's The Trinity (1967) argued the famous axiom that the immanent Trinity is the economic Trinity and vice versa, refusing the bifurcation between God in himself and God toward us. John Zizioulas's Being as Communion (1985) drew on the Cappadocians to make personhood-as-communion the structural shape of theology. Catherine Mowry LaCugna's God for Us (1991) pressed the doxological-relational dimension — the Trinity as the structure of the church's life with God, not a metaphysical puzzle. Wesley Hill's Paul and the Trinity (2015) returned to the New Testament and argued that the relational triadic logic in Paul (including 13:14) is itself a kind of incipient Trinitarian theology, not merely raw material later organized into doctrine.

The Reformed continuation is sober. Calvin's Institutes 1.13 affirms the Nicene-Constantinopolitan settlement and rejects both modalism and tritheism, making the Trinity foundational to his soteriology. The Westminster Confession (1646) chapter 2 codifies the doctrine for English-speaking Reformed churches. Liturgical use crosses the traditions: 13:14 is recited as a benediction in Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran, Reformed, and most Protestant services to this day. Few verses in the New Testament have so direct a path from text to ongoing liturgical life.

N.T. Wright (*Paul for Everyone: 2 Corinthians*; *Paul and the Faithfulness of God*) reads the closing benediction as the natural end-point of the whole letter rather than a tacked-on liturgical flourish: the "grace of the Lord Jesus, the love of God, the fellowship of the Holy Spirit" is the lived shape of the new-covenant community Paul has been fighting for, a people constituted by the self-giving Messiah, held in the creator's covenant love, and bound together in the Spirit's *koinōnia*. For Wright the discipline of 13:1–10 and the benediction of 13:14 belong together because the cross-shaped pattern — "crucified in weakness, alive by the power of God" (13:4) — is the only ground both for Paul's authority and for the community's hope; "test yourselves" (13:5) is the question whether the Messiah's own life is genuinely at work among them, and the triadic blessing names the God who makes that life possible. Wright treats the verse as part of the data the later creeds were written to interpret, not as Nicene metaphysics avant la lettre, but he insists the worship of Jesus alongside the Father and the Spirit is there in Paul from the start.

The self-examination passage in 13:5 generated its own reception trajectory. Catholic sacramental theology developed examination of conscience as the preparation for confession; the Catechism §1454–1458 lays out the practice in detail. Reformed theology built self-examination into the preparation for the Lord's Supper: the Westminster Larger Catechism 171–172 specifies the questions a believer should ask before approaching the table. Anabaptist traditions emphasized communal mutual accountability — the early Mennonite practice of footwashing, the regulated mutual admonition of the gathered congregation. Wesleyan theology built it into the class meeting: small groups in which members answered the question "How is it with your soul?" Quaker tradition made silent self-attendance to the inner Light its central practice. All trace, in different ways, back to 2 Corinthians 13:5.

## Theological Intent

Two tensions structure the chapter's theological field.

The first is the question of how to read 13:14. Three positions can be distinguished, with substantial scholarship behind each.

- The functional or economic-only reading argues that Paul invokes a triad of divine activity without making any ontological claim. Grace, love, and fellowship are modes by which the one God acts; "the Lord Jesus Christ" and "the Holy Spirit" name God's activity, not metaphysically distinct persons. James Dunn, in Christology in the Making (1980), articulated an evolutionary view in which full Trinitarian metaphysics emerged only in the centuries after Paul; the New Testament texts, including 13:14, present functional triads that later theology systematized. Maurice Casey and certain modern Unitarian-Universalist readings press this further, arguing that 13:14 cannot bear the weight of later Trinitarian doctrine.
- The proto-Trinitarian or early high Christology reading argues that Paul's triad is already, in its own first-century terms, a recognition of Christ and the Spirit within the unique divine identity. Hurtado and Bauckham represent this position with the most careful evidence: the patterns of devotion, the application of YHWH-texts to Jesus, the liturgical placement of Christ alongside God in benediction. On this reading, 13:14 does not yet contain the Cappadocian grammar of one ousia and three hypostases, but it contains the data that grammar was later developed to interpret.
- The classical Trinitarian reading takes 13:14 as straightforwardly Trinitarian in the developed sense — three persons coequal in one divine essence, the structure of which is read backward through Paul. Patristic exegesis from Tertullian through Augustine, the medieval scholastics, and the Reformed and Catholic confessional traditions all stand here. The reading is anachronistic in the technical sense (Paul did not have the conciliar vocabulary), but the claim is not that Paul had Nicene Greek; the claim is that Nicene Greek correctly articulates what Paul was already worshipping.

To steelman the position least immediately intuitive — for most contemporary Christian readers, that will be the unitarian or functional reading — note what it actually claims at its strongest. Paul writes within Jewish monotheism. He never abandons it. The triadic structure of 13:14 is liturgical and relational, not metaphysical. To read it as a compressed dogmatic statement is to import sixth-century Greek onto a first-century Greek text. The honest historical reading, on this view, is that Paul names a triad of divine activity and that the metaphysical question of how Christ and the Spirit relate to God in essence is one Paul does not directly answer. Hurtado and Bauckham have moved many critical scholars off the older evolutionary view, but the strict-monotheism reading remains a coherent option that takes Paul's Jewish context seriously.

The second tension is the question of self-examination and what 13:5 mandates.

- The Catholic sacramental tradition develops examination of conscience as the disciplined preparation for confession. The believer reviews specific acts, evaluates them by the moral catechism, names them in the sacrament of reconciliation. The system is rigorous, regular, and individual.
- The Reformed tradition centers self-examination on the Lord's Supper. Westminster Larger Catechism 171–172 specifies the questions: am I in the faith, am I trusting Christ, am I at peace with my neighbor? The frequency varies by tradition, but the structure is communal — the church examines together before approaching the table.
- The Anabaptist tradition makes the examination mutual and communal. The Mennonite practice of footwashing reenacts the Last Supper, and the gathered church holds members accountable. Self-examination is real, but it is inseparable from the community's eyes.
- The Wesleyan tradition built it into the class meeting: small groups, weekly, with the explicit question of one's spiritual state. The Anglican-Methodist hybrid produced a structured self-disclosure that Catholic confession and Reformed Supper-preparation did not provide.
- The Quaker tradition strips it back to silence. The believer waits in unprogrammed worship, attends to the inner Light, and lets the examination occur without ritual scaffolding.

What does Paul actually mandate? The verb peirazō and the noun adokimos point to genuine testing — not psychological self-doubt, but the question of whether faith is real, whether Christ is in fact present. The pastoral generosity of verse 7 ("we pray to God that you may not do wrong … though we may seem to have failed") cuts against any reading that uses 13:5 as a club for spiritual perfectionism. The minimal Pauline mandate may be: the church and its members are responsible to ask whether their faith is real, and the goal is not to pass the test but to be true.

Where this leaves us: the chapter that began with a citation from Deuteronomy on legal witnesses ends with the most explicit Trinitarian benediction in the undisputed Pauline corpus. The disciplinary frame and the doxological frame are part of the same paragraph in Paul's mind. The Corinthians are to test themselves; the test is whether Christ is in them; the chapter closes with the grace of that Christ, the love of God, and the fellowship of the Spirit. The structure of the test and the structure of the blessing are both triadic, both relational, both built on the cross-shaped pattern of weakness-and-power that has run through the entire letter.

One unresolved question: when Paul writes that the Lord's authority is given "for building up and not for tearing down" (13:10), and yet threatens severe discipline if his coming third visit finds unrepented sin (13:2), is the apparent tension an actual contradiction or a higher-order coherence? Paul seems to claim that genuine discipline is itself a mode of building up. The Corinthian rivals would have read that as bullying dressed in pastoral language. Two thousand years of church practice have not settled the question of when correction is construction and when it is demolition.

## The Thread

Paul's last word to the Corinthian church places the threat of discipline and the gift of the Trinity in the same letter. The cross-shaped pattern that the whole letter has worked out — weakness that bears divine power, suffering that becomes apostolic credential, authority bound to truth — receives its closing summary in the benediction. Grace, love, and fellowship are not three separate things. They are the same one God being present to a fractured community in three relations. The fool's speech of chapters 10–13 ends in worship.
