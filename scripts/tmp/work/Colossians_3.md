# Colossians 3: The Hidden Life and the Visible House

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The third chapter of Colossians arrives in a world structured around the *oikos* — the household — as the basic unit of Roman political and economic order. To understand why a first-century pastoral letter spends its concluding ethical paragraphs telling wives, children, and slaves how to behave, one has to feel the weight of a social form whose authority pervaded everything from imperial taxation to philosophical paraenesis. The *paterfamilias* held legal authority (*patria potestas*) over wife, biological children, adopted children, and slaves; he was the household's priest, judge, and economic agent. Aristotle's *Politics* (I.3–13) had already laid down the canonical analysis: the household is composed of three relations — master and slave, husband and wife, parent and child — and the well-ordered city is built upward from well-ordered houses. This triadic schema, the *oikonomia*, became the template that Stoic, Hellenistic-Jewish, and eventually Christian moralists inherited. Philo's *Hypothetica* (preserved in Eusebius) and Josephus's *Against Apion* 2.199–208 reproduce the same threefold order, and pseudo-Aristotle's *Oeconomica*, Hierocles, and Musonius Rufus (*Lectures* 13–14, on marriage) work the form into Stoic ethical literature.

Roman slavery, in the period of this letter, was not race-based. A slave (*servus*) might be *oikogenes* — born in the house — or captured in war, or sold for unpaid debt. Manumission was common; freedmen (*liberti*) could become wealthy patrons in their own right. Gaius's *Institutes* preserves the legal architecture: a slave was a *res*, a thing, but a thing endowed with *peculium* (working capital) and frequently with skills that gave him real social leverage. Yet the period of Colossians (early 60s, on the standard Pauline dating) was also a moment of acute Augustan-era anxiety about slave loyalty. Tacitus's *Annals* 14.42–45 records the case of Pedanius Secundus, the urban prefect who in 61 AD was murdered by one of his slaves; under the *senatus consultum Silanianum*, all four hundred household slaves were executed. The senate debated whether to relax the law; the conservative jurist Cassius Longinus argued that fear was what kept the *oikos* intact, and the executions proceeded. This is the world into which the Colossian *Haustafel* speaks. Telling slaves to "obey in everything" (3:22) was not abstract advice; it was a directive in a setting where the alternative was widely understood to be the kind of violence that produced collective punishment.

The wifely role had its own evolving legal frame. Augustus's marriage legislation (*lex Iulia de maritandis ordinibus*, *lex Papia Poppaea*) had penalized celibacy, rewarded fertility, and reasserted the *materfamilias* as a public-facing dignity. By the mid-first century, women of the senatorial and equestrian orders held significant property, sometimes managed estates, and patronized cults; the surviving Pompeian house frescoes (from the eruption of 79 AD, only fifteen years after this letter) show the social patterns of dining, household production, and religious observance in fine grain.

The authorship and dating of Colossians remain contested. Those who argue for Pauline authenticity (Dunn, Wright, Moo) place the letter in Paul's Roman imprisonment (early 60s); those who argue for pseudonymity (Lohse, Lincoln, MacDonald) date it slightly later and treat the household code as one piece of evidence — codes appear in Ephesians 5–6, 1 Peter 2–3, and the Pastorals, suggesting late-first-century development. David Balch's *Let Wives Be Submissive* (1981) and David Aune's work on the *Haustafel* genre frame this debate in social-historical terms.

Several Greek terms anchor the chapter:

- *Haustafel* (German, "household table") — Luther's term, now the standard scholarly designation for the genre running from 3:18 to 4:1
- *kekruptai* (κέκρυπται, 3:3) — perfect passive of *kryptō*, "has been hidden and remains hidden"; the perfect tense locks the past act and its present effect together
- *apekdyomai / endyomai* (ἀπεκδύομαι / ἐνδύομαι, 3:9–10, 12) — "strip off / put on"; the verbs evoke the baptismal liturgy in which the catechumen literally removed the old garment and put on a white robe
- *anakainoumenon* (ἀνακαινούμενον, 3:10) — present passive participle, "being renewed"; the tense matters: renewal is ongoing, not complete
- *hypotassesthe* (ὑποτάσσεσθε, 3:18) — "be submissive"; the voice is debated, middle ("submit yourselves") versus passive ("be subjected"), and the choice carries enormous interpretive weight

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter belongs to the genre of ethical paraenesis but braids two distinct sub-genres. The first, running through 3:5–9 and 3:12–17, is the virtue-and-vice catalog: a Hellenistic and Hellenistic-Jewish moral form found in Stobaeus's *Anthology*, in Philo's catalogues of passions, and across the Stoic literature. The second, 3:18–4:1, is the *Haustafel* — the household code — drawn from the Greco-Roman *oikonomia* tradition. The architecture of the chapter is held together by an "if-then" frame in 3:1–4: *if* you have been raised with Christ — and the conditional in Greek is realis, presupposing the truth of the protasis — *then* seek what is above. Eschatological participation grounds the practical conduct that follows. Ethics, in this chapter, is not free-standing; it is the visible shape of an invisible resurrection.

The baptismal undertone in 3:9–12 is one of the chapter's most discussed features. The "put off / put on" imagery, paired with the catholicity formula in 3:11 — "neither Greek nor Jew, circumcised nor uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave, free; but Christ is all, and in all" — clearly echoes Galatians 3:28 and 1 Corinthians 12:13. Wayne Meeks's classic 1974 essay "The Image of the Androgyne" argued that these formulae preserve a pre-Pauline baptismal liturgy: the new Christian, emerging from the water, declared that the old social categories had been transcended. The mention of "Scythian" is distinctive to Colossians. Scythians were the proverbial outer-rim barbarian; Josephus (*Against Apion* 2.269) names them as the abjected limit case of human civility. The author's choice to add "barbarian, Scythian" past the standard pairings of Galatians is rhetorically deliberate — a pushing of the category beyond its usual bounds.

What makes the Colossian *Haustafel* unusual within its genre is the symmetry of address. Greco-Roman household-management treatises — Xenophon's *Oeconomicus*, pseudo-Aristotle's *Oeconomica*, the Stoic handbooks — typically address only the *paterfamilias*. He is the moral agent; the wife, child, and slave are objects of his governance. Colossians 3:18–4:1 addresses both parties of each pair: wives *and* husbands, children *and* fathers, slaves *and* masters. Whether one reads this as a faint subversion of the genre or as a reinforcement of it depends on which interpretive tradition one stands in. The bare fact of bilateral address is the textual datum that both readings have to account for.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the New Testament canon, 3:11 functions as one of the load-bearing texts of the Pauline gospel's social inclusivity claim. And 3:18–4:1 functions as the founding text of the household codes that shaped Christian gender and labor ethics for two millennia. The collision between these two passages, within a single chapter, is the chapter's defining feature — and the engine of nearly every interpretive controversy attached to it.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

In the patristic period, John Chrysostom's homilies on Colossians read 3:18–4:1 as transforming pagan household relations from the inside. Slaves, in his exposition, now possess moral status as image-bearers. Wives are owed love, not merely obedience. Husbands are warned against harshness. But the structures themselves are preserved; Chrysostom does not advocate the abolition of slavery or the reordering of marital authority. The medieval period inherited this pattern and codified it. The household code became the basis for Christian *oeconomica* — Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* II–II.57 treats slavery as a natural-law accommodation to fallen conditions rather than as part of God's original ordering, and Bernardino of Siena's fifteenth-century preaching extended the *Haustafel* into a comprehensive theology of the Christian house.

The Reformation intensified the codes' authority. Luther's *Estates* doctrine (*Ständelehre*) read three God-ordained orders — church, civil, household — into Scripture, and Colossians 3 with its parallel in Ephesians 5–6 was the textual anchor for the household estate. Luther's application was rigorously patriarchal. Calvin's *Institutes* III.10 on the Christian household and his commentary on Colossians read the code as a permanent ethical pattern, modulated by mutual love but not relativized.

The most painful chapter of the text's reception is the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century slavery debate. The American pro-slavery apologists — Thornton Stringfellow's *A Brief Examination of Scripture Testimony on the Institution of Slavery* (1841) and James Henley Thornwell's writings — cited 3:22 ("bondservants, obey in everything those who are your earthly masters") as biblical warrant for the institution. The abolitionists — Frederick Douglass, Theodore Weld in *The Bible against Slavery* (1837), Henry Ward Beecher — cited 3:11 ("neither slave nor free"), 4:1 ("masters, treat your bondservants justly"), and the canonical trajectory toward the eschatological inclusion of all peoples. The hermeneutical method debate from this era — whether one reads individual proof-texts or whole-canon trajectories, whether the surface command or the deeper grammar carries the day — still shapes the egalitarian-complementarian argument over the household codes today; and to read 3:22 with that history of misuse in view is itself part of an honest interpretation.

The critical scholarship of the late twentieth century turned the codes into objects of historical analysis. David Balch (*Let Wives Be Submissive*, 1981) read them as apologetic accommodation: the early church, suspected of being a socially disruptive cult, adopted the standard *Haustafel* form to demonstrate respectability to the surrounding Greco-Roman culture. James D. G. Dunn's commentary takes the more sympathetic line — Christianizing transformation from within, not capitulation; Douglas Moo (PNTC), F. F. Bruce, and Scot McKnight (NICNT) develop variations of this transformation reading, stressing the bilateral address and the new motive ("in the Lord," "fearing the Lord," 3:18–24) as the leaven inside the inherited form. Andrew Lincoln, working primarily on Ephesians, treats the codes as creative reuse of a known genre, with significant tension between the old form and the new content. Peter O'Brien's WBC volume holds the household code together with the realized-eschatology language of 3:1–4: the new self "renewed in knowledge after the image of its creator" (3:10) is the agent who now inhabits the old structures differently.

Within Protestant theological ethics the codes have been the principal proof-text battleground between the egalitarian and complementarian readings. Egalitarian writers (William Webb's redemptive-movement hermeneutic, Scot McKnight, Ben Witherington, Lucy Peppiatt, the Council for Biblical Equality) read 3:11 as the deeper truth and the *Haustafel* as a time-bound concession the church made under social pressure, with the canonical trajectory pointing beyond it. Complementarian writers (Wayne Grudem, John Piper, Andreas Köstenberger, Thomas Schreiner, the Council on Biblical Manhood and Womanhood) distinguish equal status (3:11) from ordered roles (3:18–4:1) and read the codes as cross-culturally normative. This is a mainstream intra-Christian theological debate, not a marginal one, and the chapter gives both sides genuine purchase.

### Jewish Scholarly Engagement

Hellenistic-Jewish moral instruction generated *Haustafel*-like material independently of any Christian source. Philo's *Hypothetica* and *Decalogue* on family duties, Josephus's *Against Apion* 2.199–208, and the gnomic poem Pseudo-Phocylides all show the same triadic family structure being moralized from a Jewish standpoint. The genre, in other words, is not specifically Christian or pagan; it is the moral common-stock of the Hellenistic Mediterranean. Modern Jewish ethical writing on the family — Eliezer Berkovits on the conditions of halakhic flexibility, David Hartman on the egalitarian trajectory of interpretation — continues a long internal Jewish argument about how received family structures may legitimately develop.

Where contemporary biblical scholarship has shifted, broadly, is in reading the *Haustafel* as a moment in the social history of an early-Christian movement under pressure to appear respectable, rather than as a transcendent prescription. But this is contested: conservative complementarian writers such as Wayne Grudem, John Piper, Thomas Schreiner, and the Council on Biblical Manhood and Womanhood have held the codes as normative for all time, and they cannot be lightly dismissed as fringe — they remain influential in large sectors of evangelical Protestantism.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What God is doing in this chapter, on the most natural reading: God has hidden the believer's true life "with Christ in God" (3:3); the resurrection life shows up *now* in transformed character and renewed relationships, however ordered by the social forms of the present age. The eschaton has reordered the believer's identity (3:11) without yet having dismantled the world's structures (3:18–4:1). That is the theological description; what it means in practice has been the church's longest argument.

### Tension 1: 3:11 versus 3:22 — Does the Gospel Relativize or Sanctify the Social Order?

The egalitarian reading takes 3:11 as the deeper truth and the household code as a concession to the present age, historically time-bound. William Webb's redemptive-movement hermeneutic argues that the canonical trajectory pushes beyond the codes toward a fuller realization of the inclusion announced at baptism; Scot McKnight, Ben Witherington, and Lucy Peppiatt make versions of the same argument — the gospel relativizes the social order, and the *Haustafel* preserves the form of the surrounding culture while subverting its motive and reciprocity.

The complementarian reading, articulated by Grudem, Piper, Köstenberger, Schreiner, and the Council on Biblical Manhood and Womanhood, distinguishes status from role. Colossians 3:11 establishes equal *status* before God; 3:18 establishes ordered *roles* in the household. Status and role are presented as distinct categories, neither collapsing into the other. The codes remain normative for Christian practice across cultures and ages; cultural variation may modify their expression but not their substance.

The Catholic teaching, articulated in *Mulieris Dignitatem* (John Paul II, 1988), *Familiaris Consortio* (1981), and the *Catechism* (§§369–372 and §§1601–1666), affirms the equal dignity of men and women, the complementarity of marriage, and a doctrine of mutual subjection — citing Ephesians 5:21 ("submit to one another out of reverence for Christ") as the governing frame for the household codes. The Orthodox tradition operates similarly: the codes are read through the lens of mutual subjection, and marriage carries an iconic significance, imaging the relation of Christ and the Church.

N. T. Wright (*Colossians and Philemon*, Tyndale; *Paul for Everyone: The Prison Letters*) reads 3:18–4:1 as the gospel beginning to work on the most basic social structure from inside: the very fact that wives, children, and slaves are addressed *as moral agents* — and that husbands, fathers, and masters are placed under reciprocal obligation, with masters told they too "have a Master in heaven" (4:1) — was unheard of in the Greco-Roman *oikonomia*, and Wright treats it as the leaven of the new creation rather than its ceiling. He pairs this with the realized-eschatology language of 3:1–4: because "you have died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God," the believer's identity is no longer determined by household rank, and the structures are now inhabited by people whose true status lies elsewhere.

The honest steelman, on each side: if you came in egalitarian, take the complementarian's strongest case — that the canonical witness consistently distinguishes role from status, and that egalitarian readings have to declare large swaths of Scripture culturally bound. If you came in complementarian, take the egalitarian's strongest case — that 3:11 *is* the gospel, that the bilateral address and "in the Lord" motive are already cracking the form open, and that the redemptive-movement trajectory points beyond the codes' first-century shape. This is the live and unsettled question of contemporary church practice; the text gives both sides genuine purchase, and ducking that purchase from either direction is the surest way to miss what the chapter is asking.

### Tension 2: "Hidden with Christ in God" (3:3) — Realized versus Inaugurated Eschatology

Colossians has the most "realized" eschatology of the Pauline corpus. "You have died" (3:3); "you have been raised" (3:1); "you have put off the old self... put on the new" (3:9–10) — the verbs are completed action. In 1 Corinthians 15 and Romans 6, by contrast, resurrection is largely future for the believer; the present is only baptismal participation in Christ's death. By Colossians, resurrection is past.

Eduard Lohse and Andrew Lincoln read this shift as evidence of theological evolution and pseudonymity: a post-Pauline development in which the church's experience of its own life-with-Christ has been pushed back from future to present. N. T. Wright, James Dunn, and J. Christiaan Beker hold a different line — Paul (or his close Pauline coworker) holds both poles together, already and not yet. Colossians 3:4, "when Christ who is your life appears, then you also will appear with him in glory," explicitly preserves the future moment. The chapter's "hiddenness" is the not-yet; the "raised with Christ" is the already. The two are not in tension internal to the text; they are the two halves of an inaugurated eschatology in which the resurrection has begun in Christ but not yet shown its full effects in the world.

The pastoral implication is substantial. Too much "realized" produces triumphalism — the early gnostic communities, Christian perfectionism, and the prosperity-gospel deformations all pull in this direction. Too much "future" produces escapism, a spirituality detached from present transformation. The pastoral genius of Colossians is the *hidden* life: already real, not yet visible. The chapter forces a sustained meditation on an identity that is real now but invisible now — a difficult posture either to occupy or to articulate, but one that has shaped the Christian moral imagination across two thousand years of monastic, mystical, and ordinary lay practice.

**Closing question**: If your real life is "hidden with Christ in God" (3:3) — neither what you can see when you look in the mirror nor what others see when they look at you — what would it mean to act today as if that hidden self were the more real one?

## The Thread

- Galatians 3:28 — the parallel inclusion formula, an independent attestation of what looks like a pre-Pauline baptismal saying
- Ephesians 5:21–6:9 — the longer parallel household code, same author or circle, expanded with a christological reading of marriage
- Philemon — the same letter cycle's actual case study in a Christian master and his runaway slave, where the abstract code meets a concrete name
- 1 Peter 2:18–3:7 — a third early-Christian household code; the comparison shows the genre's plasticity and the variations across the early church
