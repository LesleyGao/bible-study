# 2 Chronicles 27 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

After the architectural sprawl of the Uzziah chapter, the Chronicler's account of Jotham (Hebrew *Yotam*, יוֹתָם, "Yahweh is perfect" or "Yahweh is upright") is strikingly compact — nine verses, half the length of most royal notices in the second half of the book. The parallel in 2 Kings 15:32-38 is comparable in length, and unlike most other Chronicles-Kings overlaps, here the Chronicler has not significantly expanded his source. He preserves the basics from Kings — twenty-five at accession, sixteen-year reign, mother named Jerusha, building of the upper gate of the temple, eventual reign of Ahaz — and adds only three pieces of original material: the explanatory clause *except he did not enter the temple of the LORD* (27:2), the building of cities and forts in the hill country (27:4), and the war with the Ammonites and the heavy tribute they paid (27:5). The compactness is itself meaningful. After the dramatic ascent and collapse of Uzziah, the Chronicler offers a quiet, faithful, undistinguished reign — and lets it stand uncluttered.

The Chronicler — composing in Persian-period Yehud, a community that had returned from exile but lived under imperial overlordship and felt itself diminished compared to the davidic golden age — has a particular interest in *short, faithful reigns* as theological exempla. Sara Japhet has noted that the Chronicler tends to reserve his most elaborate prose for kings whose reigns are dramatic in either direction (David, Solomon, Hezekiah, Josiah on the faithful side; Ahaz, Manasseh, Zedekiah on the unfaithful side). The merely-faithful king — Jotham, the unspectacular middle term between his father's fall and his son's apostasy — gets the kind of brief, dignified notice that one might call the Chronicler's *plain-style* register. Mark Boda reads this restraint as a deliberate authorial choice: not every faithful life requires elaboration; some lives are simply *ordered* (the verb in 27:6 is *hekhin*, הֵכִין, "established / ordered / arranged") before God, and the order itself is the testimony.

The verse the Chronicler adds that does *not* appear in Kings — *except he did not enter the temple of the LORD* (27:2, *raq lo'-ba el-heikhal YHWH*) — is doing precise theological work. Hugh Williamson and Ralph Klein both note the verse's deliberate echo of the previous chapter. Uzziah's defining transgression was *entering the temple* to burn incense; Jotham, the son, is praised — by negative definition — for *not* entering the temple. The clause is not a generic comment on temple attendance; it is a tightly specific reference to the priestly boundary Uzziah violated. The Chronicler is showing that Jotham learned the lesson his father did not. The very thing that destroyed Uzziah is the thing Jotham conspicuously avoids.

The qualifying second clause of the same verse — *but the people still followed corrupt practices* (*ve'od ha'am mashchitim*, "and still the people were acting corruptly") — is also unique to Chronicles. Steven McKenzie reads this as the Chronicler's honest acknowledgment that royal righteousness does not, in itself, transform popular religion. The grass-roots syncretism that Uzziah's father Amaziah opened (the Edomite gods, 2 Chr 25:14) and that Uzziah did not extirpate continues under Jotham. The king's personal fidelity is real but partial in its national effect. The note also functions, in Boda's reading, as a *foreshadowing*: the popular corruption that Jotham did not address will erupt under his son Ahaz into full-scale state apostasy. The seed is already in the soil.

The summary verse the chapter pivots on — *vayitchazzeq Yotam ki hekhin derakhav lifnei YHWH elohav* (וַיִּתְחַזֵּק יוֹתָם כִּי הֵכִין דְּרָכָיו לִפְנֵי יְהוָה אֱלֹהָיו), "So Jotham became mighty, because he ordered his ways before the LORD his God" (27:6) — is one of the Chronicler's most crystalline theological aphorisms. Three things deserve attention. First, the verb *chazaq* (חזק, "be strong") is the same root that became the danger in Uzziah's reign (*ukhechezqato gavah libbo*, "when he was strong, his heart was lifted up," 26:16). The Chronicler is not against strength; he is against the *pride* that often follows strength. Jotham's strength is reframed by the explanatory clause: he became strong *because* he ordered his ways. The causal direction is theological. Second, the verb *hekhin* ("ordered, established, set in order") is the same verb the Chronicler uses for Solomon's preparation of the temple (1 Chr 22:5, 14) and for David's preparation of materials. *Setting in order* is, for the Chronicler, the spiritual disposition par excellence. Third, the phrase *derakhav lifnei YHWH* ("his ways before the LORD") is the Chronicler's compressed restatement of the Deuteronomic *halakh lifnei YHWH* tradition (Gen 17:1, 1 Kgs 9:4). Jotham is the rare Chronicles king whose verdict-line is wholly positive without major qualification.

Archaeologically, Jotham's reign (probably circa 750-735 BCE, with significant overlap as regent during Uzziah's leprosy) coincides with the early stages of Tiglath-Pileser III's western campaigns — the moment Assyrian imperial pressure begins to crush the Levantine kingdoms. The *Ophel* fortifications (27:3) and the hill-country forts (27:4) are best understood in this context: Jotham is hardening Jerusalem against an empire that will, within thirty years, break the northern kingdom and besiege Jerusalem itself. The Ammonite tribute of 100 talents of silver and 20,000 cors of grain (27:5) is enormous — roughly 3,400 kg of silver and millions of kilograms of grain at a conservative estimate — and Knoppers reads it as evidence of a brief regional ascendancy in the early-to-mid 730s BCE before Aramean and Assyrian pressure inverted Judah's position.

## Lens 2: The Literature Itself

The chapter is structurally a single seamless paragraph, undivided by major narrative pivots. There is no central scene, no confrontation, no prophetic speech, no crisis. The literary effect is striking precisely *because* of its absence. Jotham's chapter reads like a building inventory: he built the upper gate; he did much on the Ophel wall; he built cities; he built forts and towers. The verb *banah* (בנה, "to build") appears five times in three verses (27:3-4). After the Chronicler's portrait of Uzziah's siege machines (26:15) — which were, in a sense, a king's *idea-engineering* — Jotham's portrait is a king's *quiet stonework*. The contrast is felt without being stated.

The chapter's one moment of military narrative (27:5) — the Ammonite war — is told in two sentences and dispatched. The Chronicler is uninterested in dramatizing the conflict. What interests him is the tribute: 100 talents of silver, 10,000 cors of wheat, 10,000 cors of barley, *paid in the same amount in the second and the third years*. The repetition of "second and third years" gives the verse a pulse. Jotham's success is measured not in the moment of victory but in the *durability* of its consequences. The contrast with Amaziah's Edomite war — instant tribute, instant apostasy — is implicit but legible.

The Hebrew of v. 6 — *vayitchazzeq Yotam ki hekhin derakhav lifnei YHWH elohav* — is a perfectly balanced bicolon with a causal hinge (*ki*). The *cause-and-effect* construction (became mighty / *because* / ordered his ways) inverts the usual modern intuition that strength comes from will, planning, or strategy. The Chronicler's grammar makes spiritual ordering the precondition of strength. Japhet observes that this verse may be the single clearest statement of the Chronicler's positive theology of kingship. The ascent is real, the strength is real, but the cause is liturgical — the king's ways set in order before the LORD.

The closing verse (27:9) — *Ahaz his son reigned in his place* — is the chapter's quiet thunder. After eight verses of order and faithfulness, the single name *Ahaz* falls. Williamson points out that Ahaz is the only davidic king the Chronicler refuses to bury *with the kings* in 2 Chr 28:27, parallel to the Uzziah-leper-burial notice in 26:23. The Chronicler is doing dynastic accounting: father and son, faithful and apostate, ordered ways and burning sons in the Valley of Hinnom. The juxtaposition is the chapter's last word.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Within the canon, Jotham occupies a quieter slot than his father or son but is a recurring referent in the prophetic literature. Isaiah's superscription (Isa 1:1) names *Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah*; Hosea (Hos 1:1) and Micah (Mic 1:1) similarly date their prophetic ministries by Jotham's reign alongside others. The literary effect is that Jotham's reign is the *first* of the three kings during which the great eighth-century prophets spoke. The popular corruption the Chronicler notes (*the people still followed corrupt practices*, 27:2) is the social background against which Isaiah, Micah, and Hosea preached.

In Jewish reception, Jotham has a quieter footprint than the dramatic kings. The midrashic tradition (Pirqe d'Rabbi Eliezer, Seder Olam) treats Jotham as the model of *yashar* (the upright) — the kind of king who does the work without seeking the glory. The Talmud (b. Bava Batra 14b) and later Kimhi note that Jotham's restraint in not entering the temple is the inverse of his father's transgression and is itself a spiritual achievement: avoiding a particular sin one's father committed is a form of *teshuvah* (repentance) extended generationally. The medieval Jewish ethical tradition (Bahya ibn Paquda, *Duties of the Heart*; Maimonides on character) cites Jotham as a paradigm of *order* — *seder* in Hebrew, the same root as the verb *hekhin* in 27:6 — as a spiritual virtue.

In Christian reception, Jotham has minimal direct citation but appears in the Matthean genealogy (Matt 1:9, *Ozias begat Joatham; and Joatham begat Achaz*) as a link in the messianic line. The genealogy's structure — naming faithful and unfaithful kings indiscriminately — incorporates Jotham not for his exemplarity but for his place in the davidic chain. Augustine, in *City of God* 17.20, briefly notes Jotham's faithfulness as a counterpoint to Ahaz's apostasy and reads the father-son sequence as a theological warning that righteous parentage does not guarantee righteous children. Calvin's commentary on 2 Chronicles 27 develops a lengthier reflection on Jotham as the type of the *quietly faithful magistrate* — a ruler who does not reform the people but does not corrupt them either, and whose limited achievement is nonetheless real before God.

The verse *Jotham became mighty because he ordered his ways before the LORD his God* (27:6) became a touchstone in Puritan and Reformed devotional literature on *the ordering of life*. Richard Baxter cites it in *The Christian Directory* as a scriptural anchor for the discipline of *holy order* — daily, hourly, weekly arrangement of one's affairs around the worship of God. The phrase *ordered his ways* (*hekhin derakhav*) maps onto the Latin *ordo vitae* and the Catholic monastic tradition of the *regula* (rule of life). The Chronicler's compressed aphorism became a structuring concept far beyond its original context.

The New Testament does not cite 2 Chronicles 27 directly, but the Pauline language of doing all things *euschemonos kai kata taxin* ("decently and in order," 1 Cor 14:40) and the broader New Testament theology of *taxis* (order, arrangement) — the angelic hierarchies of Colossians, the household codes, the church-order materials in the Pastorals — operate inside a similar spiritual instinct. Order before God is the precondition of fruitfulness in the world. Jotham's strength was not his strength.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The Chronicler's agenda in this chapter is to give the *short faithful reign* its proper theological weight. After the spectacle of Uzziah's collapse and before the catastrophe of Ahaz, Jotham's sixteen quiet years are easy to overlook. The Chronicler refuses to overlook them. He gives Jotham nine verses, the verb *banah* five times, the explanatory aphorism in v. 6, and a contrast with both his father's transgression (*he did not enter the temple*) and his son's apostasy (silently — by the very fact that the chapter ends and the next chapter begins). The compositional choice teaches a theology of the unspectacular faithful life.

**Tension 1: The short-and-faithful pattern and the question of whether quiet faithfulness counts.** Jotham's reign produces no temple, no national reform, no prophetic confrontation, no covenant renewal. He builds gates and forts, fights one war, takes one tribute, and dies. By the standards of dramatic biblical narrative, almost nothing happens. There are at least three honest positions on what to make of this. The first — the *pietistic-quiet* reading, articulated in Puritan devotional literature, in the Catholic *Little Way* of Thérèse of Lisieux, and in much contemporary contemplative spirituality — argues that the Chronicler's compact praise of Jotham is the central gospel of the chapter: most faithful lives are quiet, most are unspectacular, most never produce a national reformation, and the *ordering* of one's ways before God is itself the achievement. The second — the *prophetic-critical* reading, in the line of Walter Brueggemann's work on the prophetic imagination — argues that Jotham's quietness is precisely the chapter's diagnosis: he was personally faithful but did not address the popular corruption (27:2b), and the apostasy that follows under Ahaz is the bill coming due for failures of public reform that quiet personal piety cannot substitute for. The third — the *generational-honesty* reading, articulated in Old Testament theology by Ellen Davis and in contemporary parenting and pastoral literature — argues that Jotham's reign is the Chronicler's case study in *what one generation can and cannot do*: he could refuse his father's transgression, he could not transform his people, and his son's apostasy was not his fault but it was not unrelated. Each reading has steel. The pietistic reading takes the Chronicler's praise seriously and refuses to belittle the unspectacular life. The prophetic-critical reading takes the qualifying clause (27:2b) seriously and notices that personal righteousness has limits. The generational reading takes the dynastic structure seriously and resists both inflating and deflating Jotham. Where this leaves us: probably all three are partially right. The Chronicler's praise is real (v. 6), his diagnosis of unaddressed popular corruption is real (v. 2b), and his juxtaposition with Ahaz is real (v. 9). The text refuses to give us only one of these.

**Tension 2: The pride-of-Uzziah / order-of-Jotham contrast and what it costs to learn from a parent's fall.** The clause *except he did not enter the temple of the LORD* (27:2) is one of the most psychologically interesting half-sentences in Chronicles. Jotham watched his father be carried out of the temple with leprosy on his forehead. He served as regent during Uzziah's quarantine years. He buried his father in the burial field that belonged to the kings *for they said, he is a leper* (26:23). What did he carry from that experience? The Chronicler answers in three Hebrew words: *raq lo'-ba* — "only, he did not enter." Three positions deserve consideration. The first — the *generational-correction* reading — argues that Jotham's life is a paradigm case of a child learning the specific lesson his parent's failure taught and refusing to repeat it. The second — the *trauma-and-restraint* reading, prominent in contemporary trauma-informed pastoral theology — argues that Jotham's avoidance is not pure virtue but partly a wound: he stays out of the temple in part because he watched what happened to his father there. The two are not mutually exclusive but they read the same behavior differently — one as victory, one as scar. The third — the *covenantal-sobriety* reading, more in line with Japhet's commentary — argues that the Chronicler is uninterested in the psychological inner life and is simply recording covenantal compliance: Jotham respected the priestly boundary, full stop. Where this leaves us: the text supports the covenantal reading most directly, but the psychological and trauma-aware readings are not anachronistic projections — they ask the kind of questions any honest reader of family-dynastic narratives must ask. The most pastorally honest answer is probably that learning from a parent's fall is real spiritual work, that the work involves both growth and grief, and that the unentered temple is not less of an achievement for being entangled with a father's loss.

The closing question, then: what specific transgression in your inheritance — the one your parent or your last self committed and that broke something — would your three-word epitaph identify, in the form *raq lo'-ba* ("only, he did not enter")? And what would it cost you to keep that small, specific, life-defining "only"?
