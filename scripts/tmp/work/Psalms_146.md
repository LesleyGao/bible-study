# Psalm 146 — Scholarly Analysis

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 146 opens the **Final Hallel** of the Psalter — the closing doxology that runs from Psalm 146 through Psalm 150. Each of these five psalms begins and ends with the imperative *halleluyah* (הַלְלוּיָהּ), "Praise Yah!" — *halelu* (the plural imperative of *halal*, "to praise") + *yah* (the short form of the divine name *Yahweh*). The Final Hallel is distinct from the Egyptian Hallel (Pss 113-118) recited at Passover and from the Great Hallel (Ps 136). The Final Hallel is the climactic praise-cluster of the entire Psalter, the structural crown of Wilson's canonical-shape thesis: the Psalter has moved from torah-meditation in Psalm 1, through Davidic lament and royal-failure in Books I-III, through Yahweh-malak response in Book IV, through restoration and Davidic reframing in Book V, to total, all-creation praise in Pss 146-150.

Psalm 146 carries no Davidic superscription in the MT. It is **anonymous**, like all five psalms of the Final Hallel. The LXX assigns it (and 147) to "Haggai and Zechariah" — a tradition reflecting the post-exilic dating of the cluster. Hermann Gunkel classified it as a **hymn** of individual praise that opens with self-summons ("praise the Lord, O my soul") and broadens to communal-cosmic theology. Westermann placed it among the *descriptive psalms of praise*. Mowinckel saw it as a temple hymn. The mainstream-critical consensus (Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Kraus, deClaisse-Walford) dates Psalm 146 to the **post-exilic period** — Persian or early Hellenistic — based on its language, theology, and placement.

The historical theology of the psalm centers on the contrast between **trust in princes** and **trust in Yahweh**. Verses 3-4: *al-tivtechu vi-nedivim be-ven adam she-ein lo teshu'ah* (אַל־תִּבְטְחוּ בִנְדִיבִים בְּבֶן־אָדָם שֶׁאֵין לוֹ תְשׁוּעָה) — "do not put your trust in princes, in a son of man, in whom there is no salvation." Verse 4: *tetze rucho yashuv le-admato ba-yom ha-hu avdu eshtonotav* (תֵּצֵא רוּחוֹ יָשֻׁב לְאַדְמָתוֹ בַּיּוֹם הַהוּא אָבְדוּ עֶשְׁתֹּנוֹתָיו) — "his breath departs, he returns to his earth; on that day his plans perish." This is anti-monarchic theology in a post-monarchic register: princes die, plans perish, only Yahweh reigns. The wordplay between *adam* (man) and *adamah* (earth) in v. 4 echoes Genesis 3:19. The wordplay between *eshtonotav* (his plans) — a *hapax legomenon* in Hebrew — and the verb *avad* (perish) is poetic intensification.

The theological turn in v. 5 — *ashrei she-El Ya'akov be-ezro sivro al-Yahweh Elohav* (אַשְׁרֵי שֶׁאֵל יַעֲקֹב בְּעֶזְרוֹ שִׂבְרוֹ עַל־יְהוָה אֱלֹהָיו) — "blessed is he whose help is the God of Jacob, whose hope is in Yahweh his God" — uses the *ashrei* formula that opens the Psalter (Ps 1:1) and saturates Psalm 145. The Hebrew *sever* (שֵׂבֶר), "hope/expectation," is rare and suggests trust grounded in patient waiting.

The catalogue of vv. 7-9 — Yahweh executes justice for the oppressed (*mishpat la-ashuqim*), gives food to the hungry (*lechem la-re'evim*), sets prisoners free (*matir asurim*), opens the eyes of the blind (*pokeach ivrim*), lifts the bowed-down (*zoqef kefufim*), loves the righteous (*ohev tzaddiqim*), watches over the sojourner (*shomer et-gerim*), upholds the widow and orphan (*almanah ve-yatom ye'oded*) — is a structured **theology of divine concern for the vulnerable**. The vocabulary echoes Hannah's prayer (1 Sam 2:1-10), Isaiah 35:5 ("eyes of the blind opened"), Isaiah 61:1 ("good news to the oppressed, freedom for prisoners"), Deuteronomy 10:18 ("upholding the widow, orphan, sojourner"). This is the sustained ethical-prophetic tradition compressed into liturgy.

The DSS evidence: 4QPs-b contains fragments of Ps 146; the order in 11Q5 is different from MT in this section. The LXX numbers Ps 146 as the second part of LXX Ps 145 in some manuscripts and as Ps 145 in others, with associated numbering shifts. Psalm 146 is part of the daily *Pesukei DeZimra* in synagogue morning service.

## Lens 2: The Literature Itself

The psalm is bracketed by *halleluyah* (vv. 1a, 10c) — an inclusio that becomes the signature device of all five Final Hallel psalms. The opening self-summons — "praise the Lord, O my soul; I will praise the Lord as long as I live" — echoes Psalms 103 and 104, which similarly open with *barekhi nafshi* (bless, my soul). The first-person voice opens but quickly broadens: the second-person plural *al-tivtechu* (do not trust, plural imperative, v. 3) addresses the community.

Lowth's parallelism shapes the catalogue of vv. 7-9. The eight participial phrases describing Yahweh's action — *oseh mishpat*, *noten lechem*, *matir asurim*, *pokeach ivrim*, *zoqef kefufim*, *ohev tzaddiqim*, *shomer et-gerim*, *ye'oded almanah ve-yatom* — form a tightly structured list. Each begins with a Hebrew participle (or jussive); each names Yahweh as the subject; each names a vulnerable group as the object. The participial form is the standard hymnic device for naming God's character through repeated action: God is the one who *does this and this and this*.

The structural divisions, following Hossfeld-Zenger:
1. **Vv. 1-2:** Opening self-summons (*halleluyah*; "praise the Lord, O my soul").
2. **Vv. 3-4:** Negative lesson — do not trust princes; their breath departs.
3. **V. 5:** *Ashrei* formula — pivot to positive teaching.
4. **Vv. 6-9:** Yahweh as creator, faithful keeper, deliverer of the vulnerable.
5. **V. 10:** Closing — Yahweh reigns forever; *halleluyah*.

The contrast structure is the rhetorical engine: vv. 3-4 describe what fails (princes, breath, plans); vv. 5-9 describe what holds (the God of Jacob, creator, faithful, deliverer). The pivot at v. 5 — *ashrei* — is the literary hinge. The closing at v. 10 — *yimlokh Yahweh le-olam* (the Lord will reign forever) — answers v. 4 — *avdu eshtonotav* (his plans perish). Princes' plans perish; Yahweh's reign endures.

The theological catalogue of vv. 7-9 is one of the most concentrated lists of divine ethical action in the Hebrew Bible. The verbs are all participles, naming continuous action; the objects are all categories of the vulnerable; the rhetorical effect is cumulative — God is the one who, again and again and again, acts on behalf of the powerless.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Psalm 146 stands at the head of the Final Hallel. Wilson's canonical-shape thesis: after Psalm 145 closes the Davidic voice, Psalms 146-150 open the all-creation praise that is the Psalter's destination. McCann calls Pss 146-150 "the Psalter's grand finale." Mitchell reads them as eschatological — the songs of the messianic age. Brueggemann treats them as the doxology that "answers idolatry" by naming the only true sovereign.

**Jewish trajectory.** Psalm 146 is part of *Pesukei DeZimra* — the verses of song recited daily at the start of morning service. Together with Psalms 145, 147, 148, 149, and 150, it forms the daily-praise core of the synagogue liturgy. The Talmud (*Berakhot* 4b) connects Psalms 145-150 with the rabbinic claim about reciting *Tehillah le-David* (Ps 145) thrice daily; the broader Hallel-cluster is treated as the proper opening of any prayer service.

**Midrash Tehillim** on Ps 146 expounds verse 3 — *do not trust in princes* — with reference to historical examples: those who trusted in Pharaoh, in Sennacherib, in human rulers, all perished. **Rashi** glosses *nedivim* (princes, lit. "noble ones") as referring to Israel's kings as well as foreign rulers and reads the catalogue of vv. 7-9 as God's ongoing care for Israel. **Ibn Ezra** notes the unique word *eshtonotav* (his plans, v. 4) and the contrast with God's eternal reign. **Radak** reads the psalm as a teaching for the exile-and-return generation, when trust in Persian or Hellenistic rulers might have tempted the community.

The post-Holocaust Jewish reading is sharp. **Heschel** read v. 3 — *do not trust in princes* — as the Hebrew Bible's permanent caveat against political idolatry. **Levenson** treats the catalogue of vv. 7-9 as a substantive theology of God's covenantal commitment to the vulnerable that grounds Jewish ethics. **Sarna** notes the wordplay between *adam* and *adamah* in v. 4. **Buber** read v. 5 — *whose help is the God of Jacob* — as the I-Thou dependence that opens up real human freedom.

**Christian trajectory.** **Augustine**'s *Enarratio* on Ps 146 reads "do not trust in princes" christologically: the only Son of Man in whom there *is* salvation is Christ, who alone never returns to dust because he rose. The wordplay is theological: *be-ven adam she-ein lo teshu'ah* — "in a son of man in whom there is no salvation" — Augustine reads against Christ as the one Son of Man in whom salvation is. **Cassiodorus** follows Augustine. **Aquinas** treats v. 5 — *blessed is he whose help is the God of Jacob* — as the formal definition of beatitude. **Luther** reads the psalm as a teaching against trust in human powers and as the gospel of God's care for the wretched. **Calvin**'s 1557 commentary emphasizes the providence-doctrine and the warning against political idolatry, with sharp reference to the contemporary German princes.

Modern critical scholars: **Gunkel** (hymn-form), **Mowinckel** (temple cult), **Westermann** (descriptive praise), **Kraus** (post-exilic Yahwehist hymn), **Hossfeld-Zenger** (post-exilic teaching-poem), **Goldingay** (post-exilic hymn linking Yahweh-creator and Yahweh-deliverer-of-the-vulnerable), **Brueggemann** (doxology against idolatry), **Patrick D. Miller** (the Psalter's substantive theology of God's care for the marginal), **William P. Brown** (creation-and-justice link), **Beth Tanner** (the catalogue as ethical formation).

**NT use — and this is where Psalm 146 becomes especially important.** **The Magnificat** (Luke 1:46-55) — Mary's song — is permeated with the theology of Psalm 146 (and Hannah's prayer in 1 Sam 2). Compare:
- Ps 146:5, "blessed is he whose help is the God of Jacob" / Magnificat 1:54, "he has helped Israel his servant."
- Ps 146:7, "executes justice for the oppressed, gives food to the hungry" / Magnificat 1:53, "he has filled the hungry with good things."
- Ps 146:7-9, the catalogue of the vulnerable lifted up / Magnificat 1:52, "he has brought down the powerful from their thrones, lifted up the lowly."
- Ps 146:3-4, the perishing of princes' plans / Magnificat 1:51-52, the scattering of the proud.

Mary's song is the New Testament's announcement that the theology of Psalm 146 is being fulfilled. Luke 4:18-19 — Jesus' Nazareth sermon, citing Isaiah 61 — also resonates with Ps 146:7-8 (good news to the oppressed, sight to the blind, freedom for prisoners). Acts 14:15 ("the living God who made heaven and earth, the sea, and all that is in them") quotes Ps 146:6. James 1:27 (religion as caring for widows and orphans) echoes Ps 146:9. **N.T. Wright** (in *Luke for Everyone* and at length in *How God Became King* and *Surprised by Hope*) reads the Magnificat — and the Ps 146 / 1 Sam 2 theology it carries — not as a private spiritual sentiment but as the announcement that Israel's God is now becoming king on earth as in heaven: the toppling of the proud and the lifting of the lowly are the shape of the inaugurated kingdom, the same reversal Jesus enacts in his Galilean ministry, and the Psalter's "Yahweh reigns forever" (v. 10) is being made good in real history rather than postponed to a disembodied afterlife.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Anti-trust-in-princes theology and political faith.** Verse 3 — *do not trust in princes* — has been read across the centuries as a permanent caveat against political idolatry. But every age has its rulers, and every age must decide what kind of trust is appropriate. Three positions.

*Position A: Strict anti-political reading.* The psalm forbids placing ultimate trust in any human ruler — emperor, king, president, party. Augustine, Luther, Calvin, Heschel, Brueggemann all read v. 3 as the permanent biblical caveat against political messianism. *Princes* includes one's own princes, not only the foreign ones. Jewish post-Holocaust theology and Christian theology after the twentieth century's totalitarianisms have both drawn on this reading.

*Position B: Calibrated political-trust reading.* Calvin distinguishes: trust in princes for *salvation* is forbidden; trust in them for *ordinary governance* is permitted (and required by Romans 13). Aquinas similarly distinguishes between ultimate and proximate trust. On this reading the psalm rules out only ultimate-political-messianism, not ordinary political engagement.

*Position C: The substantive-justice reading.* The catalogue of vv. 7-9 is the substantive content of God's reign. Trust in Yahweh means siding with what Yahweh sides with — the oppressed, the hungry, the prisoners, the blind, the bowed-down, the sojourner, the widow, the orphan. The psalm is not anti-political but anti-the-wrong-politics. The prophetic-tradition readings of Heschel, Brueggemann, and Patrick Miller converge here: sovereignty in this psalm is not bare power but power defined by ethical action on behalf of the powerless.

**Steelman of Position B.** The psalm itself does not forbid all reliance on rulers — Israel had kings and prophets who served Yahweh, and the post-exilic community had to navigate Persian and Hellenistic powers. The anti-trust language is about *ultimate* hope, not *proximate* governance. Calvin's distinction is sound: God's providence works through human rulers when they are restrained by their right limits, and trusting them in their proper sphere is not idolatry.

**Tension 2: The Magnificat-precursor and Christian-Jewish theology.** Mary's song sings the theology of Psalm 146 (and Hannah's prayer). The Magnificat became the daily evening prayer of the Western church (Vespers in Catholic tradition, Evening Prayer in Anglican). But the Magnificat — and Psalm 146 — make claims about the lifting of the lowly and the bringing down of the powerful that have political and economic edge. Three positions.

*Position A: Spiritualizing reading.* The lifting of the lowly is spiritual — the humble of heart, the poor in spirit, the sinner who repents. Read this way, Psalm 146 and the Magnificat are about *interior* transformation, not political-economic reordering. This has been the dominant reading in much Christian piety.

*Position B: Materialist reading.* The hungry, the prisoners, the widows, the sojourners are literal categories. The psalm's theology is materially redistributive. Prophetic-tradition readings (Heschel, Walter Wink) read both Ps 146 and the Magnificat materially. Mary's song *means* the rich being sent away empty.

*Position C: Both-and canonical reading.* The poor, the hungry, the prisoner are both literal and spiritual; God's care reaches both materially and interiorly; the canonical scope is wider than either-or. **Patrick D. Miller**, **William P. Brown**, and **Beth Tanner** converge here. The psalm names a God whose justice is real-world but whose reach extends into the interior life as well.

**Steelman of Position A.** The interior reading has the weight of Christian mystical-pietistic tradition behind it (Augustine on the Confessions, the medieval mystics, Pietism, much evangelicalism). It correctly notes that mere material redistribution without interior conversion is not the kingdom. The danger of Position B is that it can collapse God's work into human political program.

**Where this leaves us.** Psalm 146 opens the Final Hallel with a teaching: the right object of trust is Yahweh, the creator, the faithful keeper, the deliverer of the vulnerable, the king who reigns forever. Princes die; their plans perish on the day their breath departs. The catalogue of vv. 7-9 names what God's reign substantively *is* — justice for the oppressed, food for the hungry, freedom for prisoners, sight for the blind, lifting for the bowed-down, love for the righteous, watching over the sojourner, upholding for the widow and orphan. This is not abstract sovereignty; it is sovereignty defined by ethical action. Mary's *Magnificat* takes this theology and announces it being fulfilled in the incarnation. The synagogue prays Ps 146 every morning; the Western church prays the Magnificat every evening.

**Closing question.** If the catalogue of vv. 7-9 names what God's reign substantively *is*, then how do we read our own political life? Not by withdrawing from politics — the psalm is in the prayer-book, not in a monastery — but by measuring every claim of human rulership against the catalogue of those whom Yahweh upholds. *Whose plans perish, and whose hope endures?* The psalm tells us where to look.

## The Thread

Psalm 146 opens the Final Hallel with anti-trust-in-princes theology and a catalogue of God's care for the vulnerable. The contrast between perishing princes and reigning Yahweh structures the psalm. The catalogue of vv. 7-9 — justice, food, freedom, sight, lifting, love, watching, upholding — is the substantive content of God's reign. Mary's *Magnificat* sings this theology. The synagogue prays it every morning. The Final Hallel begins by naming the right object of trust.
