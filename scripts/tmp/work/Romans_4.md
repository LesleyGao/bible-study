# Romans 4: Abraham, the Uncircumcised Father

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Romans 4 takes us back through fifteen centuries to Abraham of Ur and the Negev, but the historical horizons that matter most for reading the chapter are layered. There is the biblical-historical horizon of Genesis 12–22, the Second Temple Jewish reception of Abraham (1 Maccabees, Jubilees, 4 Maccabees, Philo, Josephus, the Genesis Apocryphon, the rabbis), and the Pauline-historical horizon of Corinth winter 56–57 CE, where Paul is dictating to Tertius (16:22) for an audience of Roman house-churches still working out how Gentile believers belong to the covenant family of Abraham.

Abraham was not just a patriarch in Second Temple Jewish thought. He had become *the* paradigmatic Jew. **1 Maccabees 2:52**, written in the late 2nd century BCE, asks: "Was not Abraham found faithful in temptation, and it was credited to him for righteousness?" The verse explicitly cites Genesis 15:6 but reads the *temptation* through the lens of Genesis 22, the Akedah (binding of Isaac). For 1 Maccabees and the Hasmonean tradition that produced it, Abraham's righteousness was earned through his obedient willingness to sacrifice Isaac—a profoundly different reading from the one Paul will offer. **Jubilees 17–18**, dated to the mid-2nd century BCE, expands the Akedah narrative dramatically; **4 Maccabees 13–18**, probably 1st century CE, makes Abraham the model of *philosophical* virtue under temptation. **Philo**'s *De Abrahamo* and his other Abraham-related treatises read the patriarch through Hellenistic-philosophical categories—Abraham as the *sage* who progresses from sense-perception to wisdom. **Josephus**'s *Antiquities* 1.7–8 retells Abraham as a model of pious philosophical reasoning who deduced monotheism from observation of the heavens. **Genesis Apocryphon** (1QapGen, Cave 1 at Qumran), the rabbinic *targumim*, and *Sirach* 44:19–21 all reflect a thriving Second Temple Abraham tradition.

Across this body of literature, Abraham's *faith*—when it is named—is regularly understood as a *meritorious work*: faithful endurance through testing, especially the Akedah. **Sirach 44:20**: "When he was tested, he was found faithful." **1 Maccabees 2:52**: "found faithful in temptation." **Philo**, *De Migratione Abrahami*: Abraham's faith is the climax of his philosophical-spiritual ascent. When Paul writes in 4:2 that "if Abraham was justified by works, he has something to boast about," he is engaging directly with this Second Temple reception of Abraham, in which Abraham's faith *was* his work—the great work of obedient endurance.

The chronological argument of 4:9–12—that Abraham was reckoned righteous in Genesis 15 *before* circumcision in Genesis 17—is not just clever interpretation. Paul is exploiting a feature of the Genesis text that Second Temple Jewish exegesis had largely passed over. **Jubilees** harmonizes the chronology by making circumcision part of the original covenant; the rabbinic tradition (*Genesis Rabbah*) elaborates Abraham's pre-circumcision righteousness in various ways. Paul is the first ancient interpreter we possess who turns the Gen 15 / Gen 17 *gap* into a structural-theological argument: justification chronologically precedes the covenant boundary marker, therefore the covenant family is broader than the boundary marker community.

The Roman audience situates itself perfectly for this argument. Mixed Jewish-Gentile Roman house-churches, post-Claudius (i.e., post-54 CE), arguing about whether Gentile believers must accept circumcision and Torah-observance to be full covenant members. Chapter 4's whole argumentative purpose is to show that the answer is no—Abraham himself stands as the founding example of a Gentile-positioned (uncircumcised) believer reckoned righteous before God.

The textual situation is stable. **𝔓⁴⁶**, **Codex Vaticanus**, and **Codex Sinaiticus** preserve a uniform text. The most-discussed variant is at 4:1, where the syntax is contested—"What then shall we say was gained [or *found*] by Abraham, our forefather according to the flesh?" Some manuscripts read *heurēkenai* ("to have found"), others place the verb differently; **Richard Hays**, *Echoes of Scripture in the Letters of Paul*, has argued that the question should be read "Have we found Abraham to be our forefather according to the flesh?" with the implication that the answer is *no*—Abraham is forefather not according to the flesh but according to faith. The reading is grammatically defensible and theologically illuminating but has not displaced the standard reading.

Five Greek terms reward attention. **Logizomai** (λογίζομαι, 4:3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 22, 23, 24)—"reckon" or "count"—appears eleven times in the chapter. The repetition is hammered. **Misthos** (μισθός, 4:4)—"wage" or "reward," the term for what is owed by contractual obligation. **Charis** (χάρις, 4:4, 16)—"grace" or "gift," contrasted with *misthos*. **Sphragis** (σφραγίς, 4:11)—"seal," circumcision as the seal of a previously-given righteousness. **Epaggelia** (ἐπαγγελία, 4:13, 14, 16, 20)—"promise," the central covenantal-theological term Paul deploys to reframe Abraham's relationship to Torah.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Romans 4 is structured as an extended midrashic argument on Genesis 15:6, "Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness." Paul cites the verse three times (4:3, 4:9, 4:22) and builds the chapter as a sustained exposition. **Richard Hays** has called Romans 4 "Paul's most extended midrash"; **Ross Wagner**, in *Heralds of the Good News*, develops the intertextual analysis at book length. The chapter is not just a proof-text deployment but a controlled exegetical argument: Paul takes the Genesis verse, asks specific exegetical questions of it, and develops the implications.

The chapter divides into four units. **4:1–8** establishes the basic thesis: Abraham was reckoned righteous on the basis of faith, not works. Paul supports this with a *gezerah shavah* argument—the rabbinic exegetical method of linking two scriptural texts that share a common term. The shared term is *logizomai* / *chashav*, which appears in both Genesis 15:6 (Abraham believed and it was *reckoned* to him) and Psalm 32:1–2 (David's blessing on the man whose sin the Lord does not *reckon*). The Psalm citation extends Genesis 15:6 to forgiveness language and demonstrates that the *non-reckoning* of sin is the same divine action as the *reckoning* of righteousness. The two texts together make the argument: God's reckoning is gracious gift, not contractual wage.

**4:9–12** poses the chronological question: Was Abraham's reckoning before or after his circumcision? The argument is exegetical detective work. Genesis 15 (Abraham's reckoning of righteousness) precedes Genesis 17 (Abraham's circumcision) by some thirteen years in the narrative timeline (since Ishmael is born to Hagar in Genesis 16, and Ishmael is thirteen years old at the institution of circumcision per Gen 17:25). Paul's conclusion: circumcision is the *seal* (*sphragis*) of a righteousness Abraham had already received by faith while uncircumcised. Therefore Abraham can be father of *both* the uncircumcised who believe and the circumcised who walk in his pre-circumcision faith.

**4:13–17a** broadens the argument from circumcision to law in general. The *promise* to Abraham (Gen 12:7; 13:14–17; 15:18; 17:8; 22:17–18) was given through faith, not through Torah—since Torah was given at Sinai four hundred years after Abraham (a chronological argument Paul develops at greater length in Galatians 3:17). Paul concludes: "*the promise depends on faith, in order that it may rest on grace and be guaranteed to all his offspring*" (4:16). The argument's purpose is to widen Abraham's covenant family to include Gentile believers without requiring Torah-observance.

**4:17b–25** climaxes with Abraham's faith *under impossibility*. Abraham believed in "the God who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist" (4:17). His own body was "as good as dead" and Sarah's womb was barren; the promise was humanly impossible (4:19). Yet "in hope he believed against hope" (*par' elpida ep' elpidi episteusen*, 4:18) and "did not waver" (4:20). The *creatio ex nihilo* and *resurrection* themes Paul has compressed into 4:17 ("gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist") connect Abraham's faith to Christian faith in the resurrection of Jesus (4:24)—the chapter ends with the *paradidōmi* echo of 1:24, 26, 28: Christ "was *delivered up* (*paredothē*) for our trespasses and raised for our justification" (4:25).

The literary architecture is precise. The same divine handing-over verb that ran as a refrain through chapter 1's indictment now appears as the verb of God's own redemptive action. The chapter that began with Abraham's *reckoning* ends with Christ's *delivering-up*; the *logizomai* of 4:3 is fulfilled in the *paredothē* of 4:25. **Beverly Gaventa** and **J. Louis Martyn** have both noted how 4:25's *paredothē* is structurally an apocalyptic claim: God's action *toward us* climaxes in the handing-over of the Son, which inverts the divine handing-over of humanity to the powers in chapter 1.

The intertextual layering of Romans 4 is extensive. The chapter quotes or alludes to Genesis 15:5–6, Genesis 17:5, Genesis 17:11, Psalm 32:1–2, and possibly Habakkuk 2:4 and Genesis 22 (the Akedah—4:25's *paredothē* may echo Gen 22's *epilabou*, Abraham's offering up of Isaac). **Richard Hays**, **Ross Wagner**, and **Francis Watson** have all documented the intertextual density. Reading the chapter at the level of its scriptural echoes is reading Paul as a Jewish exegete in the Second Temple tradition.

A textual variant question worth noting: at 4:19, some manuscripts (including 𝔓⁴⁶) read *katenoēsen to heautou sōma* with various qualifiers—the question is whether Abraham "did not consider his own body" or "considered his own body." The negative reading (without considering) softens Abraham's faith; the positive reading (considered) makes Abraham face the impossibility honestly. Most modern critical editions follow the positive reading: Abraham looked his impossibility in the eye and still believed.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Romans 4 is the chapter where the four major modern interpretive schools clash most directly, because Abraham is the test case for the entire argument about Jew-Gentile relations and the meaning of justification.

The **Old Perspective** (Augustine, Luther, Calvin, Cranfield, Moo, **John Piper**) reads Romans 4 as the chapter that decisively grounds the Reformation doctrine of justification by faith alone. Abraham is the paradigm of how an *individual sinner* is reckoned righteous before God: not by his works (or his circumcision, or his Torah-observance), but by his faith in God's gracious promise. The *logizomai* of 4:3, 4, 5 is *imputation*—the legal-judicial reckoning of a righteousness not one's own. Luther's *Lectures on Romans* (1515–16) and Calvin's *Commentary on Romans* (1540) read the chapter as the foundation of the Reformation. **John Piper**'s *Counted Righteous in Christ* (2002) and *The Future of Justification: A Response to N. T. Wright* (2007) defend the imputational reading at length against the New Perspective.

The **New Perspective on Paul** (Sanders, Dunn, **N. T. Wright**) reads Romans 4 as the climactic argument about *who counts as Abraham's family*—the eschatological covenant family that God promised to constitute through Abraham. Wright's reading is the most ambitious: Romans 4 is *not* primarily about how individual sinners get saved but about how God is fulfilling his Abrahamic promise to bless all nations by gathering a single multi-ethnic covenant family on the basis of faith, not on the basis of ethnic boundary markers. *Logizomai* is covenant-membership reckoning rather than individual righteousness imputation. Abraham is paradigmatic precisely because in Genesis 15 he was *uncircumcised, lawless, Gentile-positioned*—and yet reckoned righteous by faith. This is the founding charter of Gentile inclusion. **Wright**'s *Justification: God's Plan and Paul's Vision* (2009), responding to Piper, makes Romans 4 the central exegetical battleground of the New Perspective debate.

The **Apocalyptic Paul** school (Käsemann, Martyn, **Douglas Campbell**, Beverly Gaventa) reads Romans 4 with attention to the divine action that constitutes Abraham's faith. The God Abraham believed in is "the God who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist" (4:17)—a *creatio ex nihilo* and *resurrection* God whose saving action is invasive and creative, not merely contractual. Abraham's faith is *responsive* to a divine action that creates the possibility of his being a father of nations. The chapter ends with *paredothē* / *ēgerthē*—Christ delivered up and raised—which is the apocalyptic event that constitutes Christian faith parallel to the apocalyptic call that constituted Abraham's faith. **Käsemann**'s *Commentary on Romans* (1973) reads chapter 4 in this register.

The **Paul Within Judaism** reading (Nanos, Eisenbaum, **Matthew Thiessen**, Fredriksen) reads Romans 4 most distinctively. **Thiessen**'s *Paul and the Gentile Problem* (2016) and *A Jewish Paul* (2023) argue that Paul reads Abraham *as already a Gentile* in Genesis 15—uncircumcised, without Torah, without Jewish identity in the formal sense. Therefore Abraham is the paradigm specifically for *Gentile* inclusion in God's people. Paul is not arguing that Jewish believers should abandon Torah; he is arguing that Gentiles can be incorporated into the family of Abraham *as Gentiles*—on the same uncircumcised, pre-Torah, faith-based footing as Abraham himself in Genesis 15. Israel's Torah remains for Israel; the Abrahamic faith-paradigm is for the *ethnē*. **Pamela Eisenbaum**'s *Paul Was Not a Christian* (2009) and **Paula Fredriksen**'s *Paul: The Pagan's Apostle* (2017) develop the case at length.

The four readings disagree on every consequential question. Is the chapter primarily about *individual justification* (Old Perspective), about *covenant family membership* (New Perspective), about *apocalyptic divine action* (Apocalyptic), or about *Gentile-specific inclusion* (Paul Within Judaism)? Each reading has substantial textual support; each is doing real exegetical work; the chapter contains all four dimensions and resists being reduced to any one.

The story of how Romans 4 has been read traces the history of Christian engagement with Abraham. **Origen**'s *Commentary on Romans* offers the earliest extensive treatment. Origen reads Abraham as paradigm of *progressive* faith—from elementary trust to mature obedience, climaxing in the Akedah. He emphasizes the harmony of Genesis 15 and Genesis 22 and resists any reading that would set "faith" against "obedience." His reading is moderate, ethically substantive, and resistant to the later Augustinian-Pelagian polarization.

**Augustine**'s engagement with Romans 4, especially in *De Spiritu et Littera* (412 CE) and the anti-Pelagian writings, makes the chapter the foundational text for the doctrine of grace. Faith itself is God's gift; Abraham's reckoning is wholly the divine initiative. **Pelagius**'s *Commentary on Romans* (recovered by Souter 1922–31) reads the chapter as compatible with genuine human moral capacity—Abraham's faith is genuinely Abraham's, and the reckoning is God's gracious response to it. The Augustinian-Pelagian dispute shapes the entire subsequent Western reading.

**Chrysostom**'s 32 *Homilies on Romans* read the chapter with rhetorical brilliance—Abraham as the eloquent demonstration that Gentiles need not become Jews. Chrysostom's homiletic emphasis is on the *democratization* of righteousness: Abraham's faith is available to anyone who believes, of any ethnic origin. **Theodoret of Cyrus** and **Ambrosiaster** (the late-4th-century anonymous Roman commentator) provide complementary patristic readings.

**Aquinas**'s *Lectura super Epistolam ad Romanos* offers the most careful pre-Reformation engagement. Aquinas reads the chapter within his broader doctrine of justification as *both* gift and transformation: Abraham's faith is genuinely faith *and* it has the character of a *fides formata caritate* (faith formed by love), without which it would not be saving faith. The Catholic tradition through Trent reads Romans 4 in this register.

**Luther**'s engagement with Romans 4 is one of the most consequential acts of Reformation exegesis. The chapter is central to his 1515–16 *Lectures on Romans* and to his Galatians lectures (especially the 1535 commentary). Luther reads *logizomai* as forensic imputation; Abraham's faith is alien-righteousness received as gift, not earned by works of any kind. **Calvin**'s *Commentary on Romans* (1540) follows Luther closely. **Erasmus**'s *Annotationes* on Romans 4 engaged the philological details; **Melanchthon**'s *Loci Communes* (1521) organized Reformation soteriology around Romans 4 alongside Romans 3.

**Wesley**'s *Notes on the New Testament* and his sermon "The Righteousness of Faith" (Sermon 6 in the *Standard Sermons*) read Romans 4 within an Arminian framework: Abraham's faith is genuinely his, enabled by prevenient grace, and the reckoning of righteousness is real divine acceptance. Adam Clarke develops the Wesleyan reading in his commentary.

The 20th and 21st centuries have produced an explosion of Romans 4 scholarship. **Karl Barth**'s *Romans Commentary* (1922 2nd ed.) read the chapter in apocalyptic mode; **C. K. Barrett**'s *The Epistle to the Romans* (1957) provided a careful mid-century engagement; **Ernst Käsemann**'s *Commentary on Romans* (1973) read Abraham within the apocalyptic *dikaiosynē theou* framework. **E. P. Sanders**'s *Paul, the Law, and the Jewish People* (1983) and the New Perspective movement that followed have made Romans 4 the central exegetical battleground. **N. T. Wright**'s *Climax of the Covenant* (1991), *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* (2013), and *Justification* (2009) develop the New Perspective reading at length. **John Piper**'s *Counted Righteous in Christ* (2002) and *The Future of Justification* (2007) defend the Old Perspective imputational reading. **Matthew Thiessen**'s *Paul and the Gentile Problem* (2016) and *A Jewish Paul* (2023) develop the Paul Within Judaism reading. **Beverly Gaventa**'s essays in *When in Romans* engage the apocalyptic reading. The major commentaries—**Cranfield** (ICC 1975/79), **Fitzmyer** (AB 1993), **Moo** (NICNT 1996), **Dunn** (WBC 1988), **Jewett** (Hermeneia 2007)—each offer extensive Romans 4 treatment without converging.

We will not pick a winner. The chapter is doing all four things at once. Abraham *is* the paradigm of the individual sinner reckoned righteous by faith (Old Perspective is right); he *is* the founding figure of the multi-ethnic covenant family (New Perspective is right); he *is* the witness to God's apocalyptic life-giving action (Apocalyptic is right); he *is* the specifically Gentile-paradigmatic ancestor (Paul Within Judaism is right). The chapter integrates more than any single school allows.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What does *logizomai* mean (4:3 etc.)?**

The **Reformed / Old Perspective** position reads *logizomai* as **forensic imputation**: God credits Abraham (and the believer) with a righteousness that is not their own. The reckoning is a *legal-judicial transaction* by which Christ's righteousness is reckoned to the believer's account. **Charles Hodge**, **John Murray**, **Cranfield**, **Moo**, and **Thomas Schreiner** defend this reading. The doctrine of imputation is the heart of the Reformation soteriology and the central point of disagreement with Catholic and Wesleyan-Arminian traditions.

The **Catholic / Tridentine** position reads *logizomai* as describing God's gracious *acceptance* and *transformative making-righteous* of the believer. The Council of Trent's *Decree on Justification* (Session VI, 1547, especially canon 11) rejected the Protestant reading of imputation as a *mere* legal fiction and affirmed that justification is both gift and real interior renewal. *Logizomai* is divine *acceptance* that includes transformation, not bare crediting.

The **Wesleyan-Arminian** position (Wesley, Adam Clarke, Witherington) holds together divine imputation and human responsive faith enabled by prevenient grace. *Logizomai* is real divine reckoning, but it is responsive to genuine human faith, not coercive or unilateral.

The **New Perspective** (Wright, Dunn) reads *logizomai* as **covenant-membership reckoning**: God reckons Abraham (and the believer) as a member of the covenant family. The "righteousness" reckoned is *covenant standing*, not transferred moral merit. Wright distinguishes "righteousness" (*tsedaqah*) as a relational-covenantal concept from the medieval Catholic-Reformation forensic-substantialist concept and argues that the Reformation reading imported a Latin juridical framework foreign to Paul's Hebraic-covenantal context.

The **Apocalyptic** position reads *logizomai* as God's eschatological declaration of the believer's inclusion in the new creation rescue.

**Steelmanning**: The Reformed reading rightly hears Paul's emphatic distinction in 4:4–5 between the *worker who receives wages as due* and the *one who does not work but believes*—the contrast is starkly forensic-juridical. The Catholic reading rightly hears that justification cannot be a mere legal fiction without transformative reality if it is to be the Bible's salvation. The Wesleyan reading rightly hears that Paul's Abraham *did* believe—the faith is genuinely his, not a divine puppeteering. The New Perspective reading rightly hears that *tsedaqah* in the Hebrew Bible is overwhelmingly relational-covenantal, not bare moral attribution.

**Where this leaves us**: The forensic and the covenantal readings are not incompatible. Paul's *logizomai* in 4:3 is taken from Genesis 15:6, where the Hebrew *chashav* operates in a covenantal context but with judicial overtones. The Reformation reading is right that the reckoning is *not based on Abraham's prior righteousness*; the New Perspective is right that the *content* of what is reckoned is covenant standing, not abstract moral merit. We can hold both: God reckons the believer as *in covenant*, and this reckoning is wholly gracious gift, not earned. Whether the Catholic or Reformation rendering of the *transformative consequences* of justification is correct is a separate question that Paul does not fully resolve in chapter 4—chapters 6 and 8 will develop the transformation theme.

**Tension 2: Was Abraham a Jew or a Gentile in Genesis 15?**

The **traditional** reading (Augustine through Luther through Cranfield) treats Abraham as the *progenitor of Israel*, the founding figure of the Jewish covenant family, who happens to have been uncircumcised at the moment of Genesis 15 because circumcision had not yet been instituted. Abraham is a Jew avant la lettre; the chronological gap between Genesis 15 and Genesis 17 is significant for Paul's argument but does not make Abraham *Gentile* in any positive sense.

The **New Perspective** reading (Wright, Dunn) treats Abraham as the *eschatological father of a renewed multi-ethnic covenant family*. Abraham is the figure who anticipates and founds the inclusion of all nations in God's people—not because he was Gentile, but because the promise to him explicitly included "all the families of the earth" (Gen 12:3). His pre-circumcision status in Genesis 15 prefigures the inclusion of uncircumcised Gentiles in the covenant family that God always intended Abraham to inaugurate.

The **Paul Within Judaism** reading (Thiessen, Eisenbaum, Fredriksen) treats Abraham as *already Gentile* in the formally relevant sense at Genesis 15—uncircumcised, without Torah, called out from the nations, not yet incorporated into a Jewish people that did not yet exist. On this reading, Abraham's reckoning of righteousness in Genesis 15 is the *Gentile pattern* of relating to God by faith, and Paul deploys him in Romans 4 specifically to authorize the Gentile mode of inclusion in the family of God. Israel's covenant (instituted at Sinai, with Torah) remains for Israel; Abraham's uncircumcised faith is the paradigm for the *ethnē*. **Thiessen**'s argument is that Paul reads Abraham not as the universal father of all believers (collapsing the Jew/Gentile distinction) but as the specifically Gentile-paradigmatic father, alongside continued Jewish covenant standing.

The **Reformed-covenantal** reading (Geerhardus Vos, Meredith Kline, in different ways the redemptive-historical tradition) reads Abraham as the *covenantal head of the people of faith across both Testaments*—the one Abraham, the one covenant of grace, with Israel and the Gentile church as successive expressions of the same covenant family.

**Steelmanning**: The traditional reading rightly hears that Genesis itself treats Abraham as the founding figure of Israel, and that any reading that makes him simply Gentile distorts the canonical narrative. The New Perspective reading rightly hears that Paul's argument requires Abraham's pre-circumcision status to do real theological work and that the "all nations" dimension is in Genesis itself. The Paul Within Judaism reading rightly hears that Paul's specific argument in Romans 4 emphasizes Abraham's *uncircumcised* status as the basis for *Gentile* inclusion, and that the chapter does not require abandoning Jewish Torah-observance for Jewish believers. The Reformed-covenantal reading rightly hears that Abraham anchors a unified covenant of grace across both Testaments.

**Where this leaves us**: Paul's argument is sufficient to establish (a) that Gentile believers are full members of the family of Abraham without being circumcised, (b) that Abraham himself was reckoned righteous before circumcision was instituted, and (c) that the covenantal family God always intended to constitute through Abraham included all nations. Whether this entails the Paul Within Judaism conclusion that Jewish believers should *continue* in Torah-observance is a question Romans 4 does not directly answer. (Romans 14, Galatians, and 1 Corinthians 7:18–20 are the relevant passages, and they admit different readings.) The chapter's clearest theological achievement is the demonstration that *boundary-marker observance* is not the condition of covenant membership; what *is* the condition for any believer (circumcised or uncircumcised) is the faith of Abraham in the God who gives life to the dead.

**Unresolved question**: Does Paul's invocation of Genesis 22 (the Akedah) lurk implicitly in 4:25's *paredothē*? The verb *paradidōmi* is prominent in Genesis 22 LXX (Abraham's "offering up" of Isaac is *prosenenkē*, but the broader narrative of God's giving-up and provision-of-substitute uses delivering-language). If Paul is reading Genesis 22 alongside Genesis 15, then Abraham's faith in 4:18–22 includes the trust that God can raise the dead (Heb 11:19 reads Genesis 22 this way), and Christ's own delivering-up at 4:25 is the divine fulfillment of what Abraham foreshadowed. The Akedah-Christ typology was developed by Origen and elaborated in patristic tradition; it has been recovered in modern scholarship by **Jon Levenson** (*The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*, 1993) and others. Whether Paul has it explicitly in view is debated, but the verbal echo is real.

## The Thread

Romans 4 sets multiple threads that the rest of the letter weaves:

**Justification of the ungodly** (4:5)—"to the one who does not work but believes in him who justifies the *ungodly*"—is the foundational announcement that 5:6–10 will develop ("while we were still weak . . . while we were still sinners . . . while we were enemies"). The ungodly-justified pattern is the heart of Pauline soteriology.

**The God who gives life to the dead** (4:17, 24) is the resurrection God who appears at 6:4–11 (baptism into Christ's death and resurrection), 8:11 (the Spirit who raised Christ will give life to your mortal bodies), and 11:15 (Israel's restoration as life from the dead). Abraham's faith and Christian faith share the same object: the God of resurrection.

**The Abrahamic family widened to include the *ethnē*** (4:11–12, 16–17) is taken up at 9:6–8 (not all who are descended from Israel are Israel; the children of the promise are reckoned as offspring), 11:17–24 (Gentiles grafted into the cultivated olive tree), and 15:8–9 (Christ became a servant to the circumcised to confirm the promises to the patriarchs, in order that the Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy).

**The handing-over of Christ for our justification** (4:25, *paredothē*) inverts the divine handing-over of humanity to the powers in chapter 1 (1:24, 26, 28) and anticipates the climactic affirmation of 8:32 ("he who did not spare his own Son but *gave him up* for us all"). The same divine verb runs through the letter, marking the structural-theological architecture of Paul's argument.
