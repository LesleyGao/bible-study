# Acts 15: The Jerusalem Council — On What Terms Do the Gentiles Enter?

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Acts 15 narrates what is conventionally dated to **49 CE** — the year the Jerusalem leadership gathered to settle the most consequential theological question the early Jesus movement would face. The chapter places the event in Jerusalem, after the return of Paul and Barnabas from the First Missionary Journey, and before the Second Missionary Journey that begins at 15:36–41 and runs through chapter 18. The political backdrop is the reign of **Claudius** (41–54 CE), and the chapter falls just on either side of Claudius's expulsion of Jews from Rome (also 49 CE per Suetonius *Claudius* 25.4) — a fact that gives the larger Mediterranean context unusual sensitivity to the boundaries between Jewish and Christian communal identity. The procurator of Judea was Cuspius Fadus or Tiberius Alexander, and Jerusalem was governed under the Roman provincial system with the high priest serving as the local Jewish authority.

Geographically the chapter moves from **Antioch on the Orontes** (where the controversy ignites in vv. 1–2) to **Jerusalem** (where the Council meets in vv. 4–29) and back to **Antioch** (where the letter is delivered in vv. 30–35), with the chapter closing on the Pauline departure for the Second Missionary Journey through **Syria and Cilicia** (v. 41). The travel between Antioch and Jerusalem follows the well-attested coastal road through **Phoenicia** (Tyre, Sidon, Ptolemais) and inland through **Samaria**, a journey of roughly 250 miles that took approximately ten days under normal conditions.

The **Jerusalem church** of 49 CE is depicted as a structured community with two distinct authority groups — "the apostles and the elders" (*hoi apostoloi kai hoi presbyteroi*, vv. 4, 6, 22, 23) — under the practical leadership of **James the brother of the Lord**. The historical reconstruction is reasonably secure: Peter is present (his last appearance in Acts apart from a brief notice), James presides, the elders and apostles deliberate, and the broader congregation participates in the final decision (v. 22, "the apostles and the elders, with the whole church"). **John Painter**'s *Just James: The Brother of Jesus in History and Tradition* (Fortress, 2nd ed. 2004) is the standard treatment of James's leadership; **Richard Bauckham**'s *James: Wisdom of James, Disciple of Jesus the Sage* (Routledge, 1999) and *Jude and the Relatives of Jesus in the Early Church* (T&T Clark, 1990) develop the broader portrait.

The single most important historical-critical question for the chapter is the **Acts 15 / Galatians 2 relationship**. Galatians 2:1–10 narrates a Pauline visit to Jerusalem "after fourteen years" with Barnabas and Titus, in which Paul presents his Gentile gospel privately to the pillars (James, Cephas, John), Titus's circumcision is not required, and the pillars extend the right hand of fellowship while assigning Paul to the Gentile mission. Paul also describes a subsequent **Antioch incident** (Gal 2:11–14) in which he confronts Peter for withdrawing from table fellowship with Gentiles. The Acts 15 / Galatians 2 question has been worked over by every major commentator. Three positions: (1) **Acts 15 = Galatians 2:1–10**, with the Acts 15 visit being Paul's third visit to Jerusalem and the Acts 11:30 famine visit corresponding to no Galatians visit (so **Acts 15 = Galatians 2** mainstream view, **Schnabel**, **Marshall**, **Bock**); (2) **Acts 11:30 = Galatians 2:1–10**, with Galatians written before the Acts 15 Council and the famine relief constituting the second Pauline visit (so **F. F. Bruce** in *Paul: Apostle of the Heart Set Free*, supported by the South-Galatian theory of the Galatian addressees); (3) **Acts is Lukan compression** with chronology reorganized for theological purposes (so **Knox**, **Marguerat**). **Stanley Porter**'s *When Paul Met Jesus* defends a more historically-confident reading of Acts.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter. First, **τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον καὶ ἡμεῖς** (*to pneuma to hagion kai hēmeis*, v. 28) — "the Holy Spirit and us" — Luke's programmatic conciliar formula in the verb construction *edoxen tō pneumati tō hagiō kai hēmin*, "it seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us." Second, **ἀπόστολοι καὶ πρεσβύτεροι** (*apostoloi kai presbyteroi*, vv. 4, 6, 22, 23) — "apostles and elders" — Luke's designation of the Council's authority structure. Third, **τοῖς ἀλισγήμασι τῶν εἰδώλων** (*tois alisgēmasi tōn eidōlōn*, v. 20) — "things polluted by idols," the unusual word *alisgēma* (related to *alisgēma* in Daniel 1:8 LXX of Daniel's defilement-by-king's-food). Fourth, **πορνεία** (*porneia*, vv. 20, 29) — "sexual immorality" — the broadest of the Decree's prohibitions, ranging in semantic field from incest and adultery to ritual prostitution and forbidden marital unions (especially as Lev 18). Fifth, **πνικτοῦ καὶ αἵματος** (*pniktou kai haimatos*, vv. 20, 29) — "what is strangled, and blood" — the dietary core of the Decree, drawn from the Mosaic prohibition on consuming blood (Lev 17:10–14; Deut 12:23) and addressing methods of slaughter that retain blood in the meat.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Acts 15 stands at the structural and theological center of Luke's second volume. **Robert Tannehill**'s *Narrative Unity of Luke-Acts* describes it as "the hinge of Acts." The chapter resolves the question that has been pressing forward since chapter 10 (Cornelius), restated in chapter 11 (Peter's defense), expanded in chapters 13–14 (the First Missionary Journey), and now formally adjudicated. Everything before chapter 15 prepares the question; everything after chapter 15 proceeds from the answer.

The chapter's structure is precisely tripartite: (1) the controversy and convening (vv. 1–5, 6–7a); (2) the deliberation in three speeches — Peter (vv. 7–11), Barnabas and Paul (v. 12), James (vv. 13–21); (3) the decision and its communication — the letter to Antioch (vv. 22–29), the delivery and reception (vv. 30–35), and the Pauline-Barnabas separation (vv. 36–41). **Marion Soards**'s *The Speeches in Acts* (Westminster John Knox, 1994) treats the three speeches as the most carefully orchestrated speech-cluster in Acts: Peter's appeal to past divine action (the Cornelius episode), Paul and Barnabas's appeal to present divine action (the First Missionary Journey signs and wonders), and James's appeal to Scripture (Amos 9:11–12 LXX) — a tripartite epistemology of past witness, present demonstration, and prophetic-textual confirmation.

The **Amos 9:11–12 citation** is one of the most studied citations in Acts. The Hebrew Masoretic Text reads the verse as a promise to rebuild the fallen tent of David "that they may possess the **remnant of Edom** [*she'ērīt 'ĕdōm*] and all the nations called by my name." The Septuagint reads "that the **remnant of mankind** [*hoi kataloipoi tōn anthrōpōn*] and all the nations called by my name may seek out [the Lord]." The differences are: (1) MT *Edom* / LXX *adam* (mankind), reflecting an unpointed Hebrew *'dm* that can be read either way; (2) MT *yiyrāšū* (possess) / LXX *ekzētēsōsin* (seek), which assumes a different Hebrew *yidrešū* (seek). The LXX reading is theologically richer for the Council's purposes — it reads the eschatological gathering as a Gentile-seeking movement rather than an Israelite-conquering movement — and James (or rather the Lukan-James) uses precisely this LXX reading. **F. F. Bruce**, **C. K. Barrett**, **Markus Bockmuehl**, and **Daniel Marguerat** have all worked over the textual question; the consensus is that the Lukan-James citation requires the LXX reading and that the citation is doing theologically distinct work from what the MT would permit. **Joseph Fitzmyer**'s Anchor Bible commentary treats the citation as one of the most important pieces of evidence for early Christian use of the LXX in distinctively Christian theological argumentation.

The **Apostolic Decree** of vv. 19–21 and 28–29 is the Council's actual ruling. The four prohibitions — things polluted by idols, sexual immorality, what is strangled, and blood — have been variously interpreted (see Lens 3). The **Western Text** introduces a major variant: it omits "what is strangled" (probably treating it as redundant with "blood") and adds a Golden Rule clause ("and whatever you do not wish to be done to yourselves, do not do to another"), transforming the Decree from a primarily ritual-dietary list into an ethical-Decalogue summary. **Bruce Metzger**'s *Textual Commentary* and **Eldon Epp**'s *Theological Tendency of Codex Bezae* both treat the Western variant as secondary, but its existence attests to the difficulty later scribes had with the Decree's apparent ritual focus.

The chapter closes with the **Paul-Barnabas separation** over John Mark (vv. 36–41). The narrative is laconic — Luke uses the strong word *paroxysmos* ("sharp disagreement") — but does not assign blame. The split has been read variously: as evidence of authentic apostolic frailty, as Lukan honesty about the cost of disagreement, or as a Lukan editorial pivot moving Barnabas off the stage so the Pauline narrative can dominate. Mark is later reconciled to Paul (Col 4:10; Phlm 24; 2 Tim 4:11), and the breach is healed.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The **Christian patristic tradition** read the Council as the model for ecclesial decision-making. **Chrysostom**'s *Homilies on Acts* (Homilies 32–34 cover the chapter) treats the Council as the paradigm of how the church discerns truth: through assembly, through speech, through Scripture, and through the Spirit's guidance. The Greek Fathers identified Acts 15 as the first **synod**, the foundational instance of the conciliar tradition that would mature in the Ecumenical Councils of Nicaea (325), Constantinople (381), Ephesus (431), and Chalcedon (451). **Bede**'s *Expositio* read the Council as the definitive resolution of the Jew-Gentile question, with the Decree functioning as a temporary pastoral accommodation that fell away once the Jewish-Christian community itself faded.

The **Catholic tradition** developed the Council as the basis for **conciliar ecclesiology**. **Yves Congar**'s *Tradition and Traditions* (Burns & Oates, 1966) and *Lay People in the Church* (1957) treat the *edoxen tō pneumati tō hagiō kai hēmin* of v. 28 as the programmatic formula of synodal authority — every legitimate council from Nicaea forward has invoked this language. **Vatican II**'s *Lumen Gentium* (1964) draws explicitly on the Acts 15 model in articulating the relationship between papal authority and the college of bishops, and **Karl Rahner** in his post-Vatican-II writings developed the implications for collegiality. The contemporary **Synod on Synodality** (under Pope Francis, 2021–2024) returned repeatedly to Acts 15 as the foundational text. The **Eastern Orthodox tradition**, in figures like **John Zizioulas** (*Being as Communion*, 1985), reads Acts 15 as the foundational instance of conciliar authority that operates without monarchical primacy — the assembly speaks, the Spirit ratifies, and no single voice (not even Peter's) determines the outcome.

The **Reformation tradition** read the chapter through the lens of justification. **John Calvin**'s *Commentary on Acts* devotes substantial attention to Peter's speech (vv. 7–11), drawing the conclusion that the gospel saves through grace alone, that circumcision is a "yoke" that Israel itself could not bear, and that the Decree must be read as a temporary pastoral accommodation rather than a binding new law. **Luther**'s *Lectures on Galatians* invokes the Council in defending Paul's gospel against the Judaizers, though Luther worked primarily from the Galatians text rather than Acts 15. The Anabaptist tradition (Menno Simons, Hubmaier) read the Council as the model of a congregational discernment in which the whole community participates — the "whole church" of v. 22.

The **Pauline scholarship** of recent decades has reshaped the reading of the Council. **Krister Stendahl**'s *Paul Among Jews and Gentiles* (Fortress, 1976) reframed the Council not as the church deciding to abandon Judaism but as Jewish leadership working out the terms of Gentile inclusion in a continuing Jewish-messianic movement. **E. P. Sanders**'s *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (Fortress, 1977) provided the Second Temple Jewish context against which the Council's ruling must be read. **N. T. Wright**'s *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* (Fortress, 2013) and *Paul: A Biography* (HarperOne, 2018) treat the Council as the formal articulation of a covenant-renewal theology in which Gentiles enter the people of God as Gentiles, not as quasi-proselytes. **Pamela Eisenbaum**'s *Paul Was Not a Christian* (HarperOne, 2009), **Matthew Thiessen**'s *A Jewish Paul* (Baker Academic, 2023), and **Paula Fredriksen**'s *Paul: The Pagan's Apostle* (Yale, 2017) develop the Paul-Within-Judaism reading: the Council is settling the eschatological-Jewish question of how the Gentile pilgrimage to the God of Israel proceeds in the messianic age, with the Decree corresponding to the Noahide laws (or the Levitical *ger* / sojourner laws) that Jewish tradition had developed for Gentiles.

The **Apostolic Decree** has had four major interpretive traditions. (1) The **Noahide-law theory**, developed at length in **Markus Bockmuehl**'s *Jewish Law in Gentile Churches: Halakhah and the Beginning of Christian Public Ethics* (Baker Academic, 2003) and supported by **Paula Fredriksen**, reads the four prohibitions as parallel to the rabbinic seven Noahide laws (Tosefta Avodah Zarah 8:4; b. Sanhedrin 56a) — laws traditionally understood as binding on Gentiles in the eschatological age. The four Acts prohibitions overlap closely with the rabbinic list (idolatry, blood, sexual immorality), and the Lukan reading of the Decree aligns with this Jewish-eschatological framework. (2) The **Leviticus 17–18 *ger* theory**, developed by **Joseph Fitzmyer** in his Anchor Bible commentary and **Richard Bauckham** in *The Restoration of Israel* (in *History, Literature, and Society in the Book of Acts*, ed. Witherington, Cambridge, 1996), reads the four prohibitions as drawn from Leviticus 17–18, which explicitly applies certain laws to "the sojourner who lives among you" — the Gentile resident in Israel. The four Acts prohibitions correspond closely: idol-meat (Lev 17:8–9), blood (Lev 17:10–14), strangled (Lev 17:13, no draining), sexual immorality (Lev 18). (3) The **pragmatic table-fellowship theory**, defended by **F. F. Bruce**, **C. K. Barrett**, and the older mainstream commentary tradition, reads the Decree as a minimum requirement to make Jewish-Gentile shared meals possible — the Decree solves the practical Antioch-incident problem by giving Gentile believers a basic ritual-dietary observance that allows Jewish believers to eat with them. (4) The **ethical-summary theory**, supported by the Western Text variant and developed in some patristic readings (especially Tertullian), reads the Decree as a summary of basic moral law applicable to all peoples — but this requires the omission of "strangled" and the addition of the Golden Rule, both Western innovations. **Daniel Marguerat** in *The First Christian Historian* leans toward the Bockmuehl-Noahide reading, noting that this best explains both the four prohibitions and the v. 21 reference to Moses being preached in synagogues (the Gentile turning-to-Israel's-God context).

The **Catholic-Protestant ecumenical engagement** on the Council as model for synodal decision-making has intensified in recent decades. **Yves Congar**'s mid-century work, the **World Council of Churches** Faith and Order documents, the **Lutheran-Roman Catholic Joint Declaration on Justification** (1999), and the contemporary **Anglican-Roman Catholic International Commission** (ARCIC) reports all draw on Acts 15 as the foundational text for understanding how the church discerns truth across different traditions in genuine assembly.

The **Jewish interpretive trajectory**, developed in **Adele Reinhartz**, **Daniel Boyarin** (*Border Lines*, 2004), **Amy-Jill Levine**, **Lawrence Schiffman**, **Pamela Eisenbaum**, **Matthew Thiessen**, and **Paula Fredriksen**, treats the Council not as the moment Christianity broke from Judaism but as the moment a Jewish-messianic movement formalized its understanding of Gentile inclusion in the eschatological age. The Council is fully intelligible as a Jewish event: Jewish leaders deliberating, citing Scripture (Amos), invoking the Spirit, and applying recognizable Jewish-eschatological categories to the question of how Gentiles enter the people of God.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: How is the Council's authority structured?** The chapter narrates a deliberation in which Peter speaks first, Barnabas and Paul testify, James proposes the resolution, and the apostles, elders, and whole church together formalize the decision. The question of where final authority lay has divided Christian traditions for two millennia. Three positions.

The **Roman Catholic position** (developed at the First Vatican Council 1869–70, refined at Vatican II in *Lumen Gentium* and *Pastor Aeternus*, articulated in **Yves Congar** and **Karl Rahner**) reads Peter's speech as primary — Peter speaks first, his speech establishes the doctrinal framework (salvation by grace, vv. 9–11), and James's resolution is the practical-pastoral application of Peter's doctrinal ruling. The Petrine primacy is real but operates within conciliar form. The **Eastern Orthodox position** (developed in **John Zizioulas**'s *Being as Communion* and **Vladimir Lossky**'s *The Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church*) reads the Council as fully conciliar, with no individual voice — not Peter's, not James's — exercising determinative authority. The Spirit speaks through the whole assembly, and the *edoxen tō pneumati tō hagiō kai hēmin* formula attests this conciliar pneumatology. The **Reformed-Presbyterian position** (developed in Calvin's *Institutes* IV.9, in the *Westminster Confession* 31, and in **Edward Schillebeeckx**'s ecumenical writings) reads the Council as a model of representative-delegate assembly: the apostles, elders, and whole church together discern, with no individual having final authority but with structured representation ensuring orderly process.

Steelmanned: the Catholic reading takes seriously the prominence of Peter's speech and its doctrinal weight. The Orthodox reading takes seriously the chapter's repeated emphasis on the "whole church" (v. 22) and the conciliar form. The Reformed reading takes seriously the structured-representative character of the Council (apostles AND elders AND whole church). **Where this leaves us**: the chapter holds Petrine, Jacobean, and assembly authority together in a single act, and any ecclesiology that elevates one element over the others in absolute terms is reading against the chapter.

**Tension 2: What are the Gentile believers obligated to do?** The Apostolic Decree imposes four prohibitions, but the theological framework behind them is contested. Three positions.

The **Bockmuehl-Fredriksen Noahide reading** treats the Decree as the Jewish-eschatological framework of Gentile-inclusion: Gentiles enter the people of God as Gentiles, observing the basic Gentile-binding laws of the Noahide covenant tradition while not being required to take on full Torah observance. The **Fitzmyer-Bauckham Leviticus 17–18 reading** treats the Decree as the application of the *ger* / sojourner laws — the Gentile believer is a resident-alien in the people of God, observing the laws Leviticus specifically applied to such residents. The **Bruce-Barrett pragmatic reading** treats the Decree as a minimum-standard for table fellowship, allowing mixed Jewish-Gentile communities to share meals without violating Jewish conscience.

Steelmanned: the Noahide reading captures the Jewish-eschatological framework that explains both the four prohibitions and James's "Moses preached in every city" (v. 21) reference. The Leviticus reading captures the precise textual overlap between the Decree's four items and Leviticus 17–18. The pragmatic reading captures the immediate Antioch-incident problem the Decree was solving. **Where this leaves us**: the Decree functions on multiple levels — eschatological, scriptural, and practical — and the chapter's theological depth resists reduction to any single category. *Unresolved question*: When the chapter says "it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us" (v. 28), is the conciliar formula claiming that the Spirit infallibly guarantees the Council's decision, or is it claiming a more modest joint-discernment in which the church takes its best Spirit-led judgment without claiming infallibility — and does the answer commit any particular tradition to a specific doctrine of conciliar authority?

## The Thread

Acts 15 reaches across the canon in multiple directions. The **Council's resolution** of the Gentile question rests on the **Cornelius episode of Acts 10–11** — Peter's speech of vv. 7–11 explicitly invokes that prior divine action — and prefigures the broader Pauline argument of **Romans 9–11**, in which the same theological logic (gospel to the Jew first, then Gentile inclusion as God's mystery, then the eventual restoration of Israel) is developed at length. The **Amos 9:11–12 LXX citation** in James's speech connects to **Romans 15:8–13**, where Paul cites a chain of Old Testament texts (Deuteronomy, Psalms, Isaiah) testifying to Gentile inclusion. The **Apostolic Decree** corresponds to the **Pauline letters' discussion of food sacrificed to idols** in **1 Corinthians 8 and 10** and **Romans 14**, with Paul developing a more nuanced "weak and strong" framework that may extend the Decree's pastoral logic. The **Paul-Barnabas separation** of vv. 36–41 anticipates the Pauline-Markan reconciliation attested in **Colossians 4:10** ("Mark, the cousin of Barnabas... receive him"), **Philemon 24** ("Mark... my fellow worker"), and **2 Timothy 4:11** ("get Mark and bring him with you, for he is very useful to me for ministry"). The **conciliar formula** of v. 28 ("it seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us") will echo through every subsequent church council and remain the foundational formula of conciliar pneumatology. And the larger question the Council settled — on what terms do the Gentiles enter the people of God? — remains the deepest theological question of Christian-Jewish relations into the present, with the post-Holocaust Pauline scholarship returning to Acts 15 as the chapter that must be reread if the church is to recover the sense in which the gospel is the fulfillment rather than the replacement of Israel's covenant.
