# Proverbs 5 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Proverbs 5 is the first of three extended adultery-warnings in chapters 1-9 — the *isha zarah* (forbidden woman) discourses of chapter 5, 6:20-35, and chapter 7, joined together by chapter 9's banquet-of-Folly counterpart to the banquet-of-Wisdom. The traditional Jewish and Christian view attributes these chapters to Solomon directly (1:1); the mainstream-critical view, articulated in Bruce Waltke's NICOT, Michael V. Fox's Anchor Bible commentary, Roland Murphy's Hermeneia volume, and the work of Tremper Longman, William P. Brown, and Mark Boda, treats chapters 1-9 as a Persian-period editorial frame composed to give wisdom-pedagogical orientation to the older sentence-proverbs of chapters 10-29.

The *isha zarah* of Proverbs 5 has a complex social-historical referent. The Hebrew terms — **זָרָה** (*zarah*, "strange," "outsider") and **נָכְרִיָּה** (*nokhriyyah*, "foreign") — together identify a woman who is *not the addressee's wife* and who stands *outside* the kinship-and-covenant boundary in some way. The historical question is *how* she stands outside. Three readings have been advanced:

A **literal-foreigner reading** (older critical scholarship, some Persian-period dating arguments) treats the *zarah* as a non-Israelite woman, with the chapter participating in the post-exilic anxiety about intermarriage that fuels Ezra-Nehemiah. The Persian-period dating of chapters 1-9 fits this reading well: chapters 1-9 may be mobilizing the figure of the foreign-and-adulterous woman precisely against the social setting Ezra and Nehemiah confront.

A **social-status reading** (Michael V. Fox and much recent commentary) treats the *zarah* as *another man's wife* — a woman who is *strange* because she belongs to another household, whose adultery would dishonor her own husband and disorder the kinship structure. The legal context here is Deuteronomy 22:22-29 and the broader ancient Near Eastern family-honor framework; the wisdom-tradition's warnings sit on top of the legal prohibition.

A **personification reading** (Bruce Waltke, much patristic and medieval reading) treats the *isha zarah* as the literary-theological counterpart to the personified *chokhmah* of chapters 1, 8, and 9 — Folly personified as a woman, the *anti-Wisdom*. On this reading the figure is not a particular historical woman but a symbolic counter-figure to Wisdom-as-woman.

The strongest contemporary readings (Fox, Waltke, Longman in different ways) hold all three together: the *isha zarah* is at once a real social danger (the wife-of-another-man, especially the foreigner in the post-exilic setting), a legal-ethical warning, and a literary-symbolic counterpart to personified Wisdom. The chapter's force comes from this triple resonance.

The **ancient Near Eastern parallels** are dense. Egyptian *Instructions* warn the young man against the *foreign woman* in language strikingly similar to Proverbs 5 — the *Instruction of Ani* (c. 1300 BC) warns: *Beware of a woman from abroad, who is not known in her town. Do not stare at her when she goes by; do not know her carnally. She is a deep water whose windings one knows not.* The *Instruction of Ptahhotep* warns against affairs with women in households where one is a guest. The Mesopotamian *Counsels of Wisdom* and the Aramaic *Ahiqar* contain similar warnings. Michael V. Fox's commentary catalogs these parallels carefully; what is distinctive in Proverbs 5 is not the warning but the warning's positive counterpart in vv. 15-19 — the celebration of marital love.

A handful of Hebrew terms anchor the chapter:

- **זָרָה** (*zarah*, "strange woman," "outsider") — the foreign-and-adulterous figure of v. 3, 20.
- **נָכְרִיָּה** (*nokhriyyah*, "foreigner") — the parallel term, often used together with *zarah*.
- **שְׁאוֹל** (*Sheol*) — the realm of the dead toward which her path descends (v. 5).
- **לַעֲנָה** (*la'anah*, "wormwood") — the bitter plant whose name marks the chapter's key reversal in v. 4.
- **בּוֹר** (*bor*, "cistern") — the cistern of v. 15, the household water-source as a metaphor for marital fidelity.
- **אֵשֶׁת נְעוּרֶיךָ** (*eshet ne'urekha*, "wife of your youth") — the marital partner of v. 18.

The *drink water from your own cistern* in transliterated Hebrew runs: *shte mayim mi-borekha ve-nozelim mi-tokh be'erekha* (שְׁתֵה־מַיִם מִבּוֹרֶךָ וְנֹזְלִים מִתּוֹךְ בְּאֵרֶךָ) — *drink water from your cistern and flowing-waters from inside your well*.

## Lens 2 — Literature

Chapter 5 is built as **a single sustained discourse with three movements**, framed by the *my son* address at v. 1 and v. 20. The chapter has the literary precision of a courtroom argument: warning, evidence, alternative, conclusion.

**Movement One (vv. 1-6)** opens with the standard wisdom-instruction *attend* address and immediately sketches the *isha zarah* in vivid sensory imagery. *The lips of a forbidden woman drip honey, and her speech is smoother than oil* (v. 3) — the seduction is in *language*, which is significant: the wisdom-tradition's female personifications (Wisdom and Folly) both *speak*, and the contest between them is a contest of speech. *But in the end she is bitter as wormwood, sharp as a two-edged sword* (v. 4) — the literary turn from sweetness-to-bitterness, honey-to-wormwood, smooth oil to two-edged sword, is the chapter's first and most-cited reversal.

**Movement Two (vv. 7-14)** turns to the consequences of falling for the *zarah*. The literary form is what Bruce Waltke calls a *prospective lament* — the speaker imagines the addressee at the end of his life, looking back: *and at the end of your life you groan, when your flesh and body are consumed, and you say, "How I hated discipline"* (vv. 11-12). This rhetorical move — voicing the addressee's future regret — is one of the wisdom-tradition's most powerful pedagogical devices.

**Movement Three (vv. 15-19)** is the chapter's positive counterpart, the wisdom-tradition's only sustained celebration of marital sexual love outside the Song of Songs. The metaphor of the cistern, the well, the springs, the fountain (vv. 15-18) develops a sustained water-and-fertility image; *let her breasts fill you at all times with delight; be intoxicated always in her love* (v. 19) is shockingly direct in its praise of marital eros. Bruce Waltke's commentary notes that the same Hebrew verb for *intoxicate* (*shagah*) is then used in v. 20 for the dangerous intoxication of the *zarah* — the wisdom-tradition is not anti-eros but channel-eros, asking *which intoxication?* The Hebrew verb does double duty in vv. 19-20, deliberately.

**Movement Four (vv. 20-23)** closes with a theological ground (*for a man's ways are before the eyes of the LORD*, v. 21) and a sober verdict on the foolish man's death.

Hebrew **parallelism** is dense throughout. *The lips of a forbidden woman drip honey, / and her speech is smoother than oil* (v. 3) is a synonymous parallelism whose force comes from the doubled sweetness-imagery. *But in the end she is bitter as wormwood, / sharp as a two-edged sword* (v. 4) is the antithetic-reversal parallelism, sweetness flipping to bitterness in the same breath.

The chapter's most striking literary feature is the **water-as-marital-eros metaphor** of vv. 15-19. The cistern, well, springs, fountain — household water-sources in the ancient Near East were precious, defended, named, inherited — become a sustained figure for the wife. *Should your springs be scattered abroad, streams of water in the streets?* (v. 16) — the rhetorical question warns against squandering the household water. The Hebrew rhetoric of *let them be for yourself alone, and not for strangers with you* (v. 17) embeds the marital ethic inside an honor-and-property structure that needs to be read with attention to its ancient context.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The **Jewish trajectory** of Proverbs 5 has read the *isha zarah* in two complementary ways. The literal-ethical reading treats the chapter as a moral warning against adultery — *Mishlei Rabbah*, the medieval commentators (Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Saadia Gaon), and the broader rabbinic moral tradition. The allegorical reading — particularly developed in medieval Jewish thought and in the kabbalistic tradition — read the *zarah* as a figure for *idolatry*, the *strange* worship that the prophets repeatedly characterize as adultery (Hosea 1-3; Ezekiel 16, 23; Jeremiah 3). The marital eros of vv. 15-19, on the corresponding allegorical reading, becomes a figure for the covenant-faithful relation between Israel and the LORD. This double reading — literal-marital and figural-covenantal — runs through the medieval Jewish commentary tradition and survives into modern Jewish reception (Robert Alter's *The Wisdom Books*).

The **Christian trajectory** has likewise read the chapter in literal and allegorical registers. The patristic tradition — Origen, Augustine, the medieval *Glossa Ordinaria* — read the *zarah* simultaneously as adulteress (the moral warning) and as heresy or false teaching or worldly wisdom (the allegorical warning). Augustine's *City of God* uses the *isha zarah* as a figure for the seductive city of man over against the city of God; Bernard of Clairvaux's sermons on the Song of Songs read marital eros (Pr 5:15-19 read alongside Cant 4:12, *a garden locked is my sister, my bride; a spring locked, a fountain sealed*) as a figure for the soul's union with Christ. The Reformation tradition (Calvin's commentary, the Puritan tradition) read the chapter as a serious ethical text and as an allegory of fidelity to covenant truth. The Catholic moral-theological tradition has used Proverbs 5 in catechesis on the sixth commandment.

One feature of the chapter that modern commentary across the spectrum (Fox, Waltke, Longman, Brown) insists must be noticed is its **rhetorical frame**: the addressee throughout chapters 1-9 is *beni*, "my son" — the young Israelite male approaching adulthood — so the *zarah*-vs-*chokhmah* contrast presents *both* female personifications to a male reader: Wisdom is the woman to embrace, Folly is the woman to flee, and the implied audience is the man choosing between them. The honest contemporary reception cannot ignore this: the chapter is written for a male reader, and the *zarah* trope deserves careful theological and pastoral handling when read in mixed-gender contemporary contexts. This does not invalidate the chapter — adultery is a real moral issue, and the *isha zarah* trope has a real function in the wisdom-pedagogy — but it requires the reader to notice the frame rather than preach it unawares.

**New Testament use of Proverbs 5** is allusive. The marital-fidelity ethic of Pr 5:15-19 sits behind the New Testament's high view of marriage (Mt 19:3-9; Eph 5:21-33; 1 Cor 7). The water-and-spring imagery of Pr 5:15 is taken up in different theological registers in Jesus's *living water* discourse (John 4:10-14, *the water that I will give him will become in him a spring of water welling up to eternal life*) and in Revelation 22:1's *river of the water of life*. The *isha zarah* trope itself becomes the Apocalypse's *Babylon the great, mother of prostitutes* (Rev 17), the climactic biblical-theological figure of the *anti-Bride* over against the New Jerusalem as Bride.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

Two tensions are loaded into chapter 5.

**Tension One: the *isha zarah* trope and the gendered audience of Proverbs.** Chapter 5 — and chapters 6:20-35, 7, and 9's banquet-of-Folly counterpart — repeatedly stages a young man choosing between Wisdom (a woman to embrace) and Folly (a woman to flee). The female personifications in this book are presented to a male addressee, and that frame is something every contemporary reader of Proverbs has to reckon with. Two positions:

A **traditional pre-critical reading** simply takes the chapter at face value: a father warning his son against adultery, with the symbolic counterpart of personified Wisdom. The chapter's male-addressed framing is treated as a contingent feature of the original setting, not a problem for contemporary appropriation.

A **translational-care reading** (Bruce Waltke, Tremper Longman, William P. Brown, and increasingly the mainstream of commentary) acknowledges the gendered framing as a feature of the original setting that requires contemporary translational care. The pastoral application of the *zarah* trope cannot simply be *young women are dangerous to young men*; it has to be reframed for both genders (adultery is a real moral danger to anyone in a covenanted relationship, regardless of which gender is the addressee) and read inside the chapter's larger theological framework (the *zarah* is a figure for the seductive disorder that calls every person away from the wise life, not a figure for women specifically). Reproducing the chapter's frame uncritically in mixed-gender teaching can do real harm — to women who hear themselves being typed as either Wisdom or Folly, and to men whose moral imagination is shaped by a female-as-temptation pedagogy.

The steelman of both: the chapter has a real moral content (the destructive power of adultery, the goodness of marital fidelity) that does not depend on the gendered frame, but the gendered frame is not invisible and should be named in honest preaching and teaching. The reading that ignores the frame is not more pious; it is just less self-aware.

**Tension Two: the cistern-of-your-own-marriage and Christian / Jewish marriage theology.** Verses 15-19 are the wisdom-tradition's most sustained celebration of marital sexual love outside the Song of Songs. *Drink water from your own cistern... rejoice in the wife of your youth... let her breasts fill you at all times with delight; be intoxicated always in her love.* Three readings have shaped reception:

A **classical Jewish reading** (Mishnah, Talmud, the medieval commentators) treats Proverbs 5:15-19 as a charter for the seriousness with which the rabbinic tradition approaches marital sexuality — the *mitzvah* of *onah* (the husband's obligation of conjugal love), the wedding-blessing *birkat erusin* and *birkat nissuin*, the high view of marital intimacy that runs through the tradition from the rabbis through Maimonides into modern Jewish reflection. Marital eros is not a concession to weakness but a positive good ordained by the LORD.

A **Catholic-sacramental reading** treats the chapter as compatible with the sacramental theology of marriage — marital sexuality as a sign of Christ's love for the church (Eph 5:32) and as a participation in the goodness of creation. The chapter's enthusiastic affirmation of marital eros has been a corrective to ascetic strands within the Christian tradition that have undervalued marital sexuality.

A **Reformation reading** (Luther's *Estate of Marriage*, Calvin, the Puritans) treats marriage as a high vocation and uses Proverbs 5:15-19 as a corrective to medieval celibacy-elevation traditions. The Puritan literature on marriage (Richard Baxter, William Gouge) draws repeatedly on this chapter.

The contemporary contested question is how the chapter speaks into a culture whose sexual ethic has migrated far from the chapter's frame — into normalization of cohabitation, into pornography saturation, into late or no marriage as common patterns. The chapter's positive vision (covenantal eros as a source of lifelong delight) and its negative warning (the *zarah*'s sweetness becoming wormwood) both speak to this cultural moment, but the chapter cannot simply be applied without translation. The pastoral work is to recover the chapter's positive vision — that there is a good intoxication, the intoxication of covenanted love — without weaponizing the negative warning in ways that produce the purity-culture damage that the church is still recovering from.

**Where this leaves us:** Proverbs 5 is a serious and beautiful chapter that requires careful contemporary handling. Its core moral claim — that there is a sweetness that ends in wormwood and a sweetness that ends in lifelong delight, and the difference is covenantal — is true and durable. Its rhetorical frame — the male addressee, the female-as-trap trope — is contingent and requires translation. Its positive counterpart — the celebration of marital eros — is one of the wisdom-tradition's most distinctive contributions and a corrective to both ascetic-suspicious and libertine readings. The chapter rewards the kind of patient, theologically honest, gender-aware, pastorally serious reading that contemporary churches have not always managed.

**Closing question:** If the chapter's positive vision is *covenantal eros as a source of lifelong delight*, and if the cultural moment has largely lost both the covenantal frame and the patience for *lifelong*, what would it mean to actually preach this chapter — not as a purity-culture relic, not as a text to be quietly skipped, but as the wisdom-tradition's serious word about how human longing is meant to be channeled?
