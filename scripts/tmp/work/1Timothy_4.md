# 1 Timothy 4: The Goodness of Created Things and the Hands of the Elders

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The political situation behind 1 Timothy 4 depends on which dating one accepts. If the letter is genuinely Pauline, it sits in the mid-60s AD, with Timothy operating in Ephesus during the volatile final years before Nero's death and the Jewish War. Ephesus in this period was the chief city of Roman Asia, a port of approximately 200,000 inhabitants, dominated by the Temple of Artemis (one of the Seven Wonders) and increasingly saturated with the imperial cult — the Sebastoi temple to the Flavians would be erected by AD 89-90, but cultic veneration of Augustus and his successors was already woven into civic life. If the letter is pseudonymous, the chapter is read against a different sky entirely: a late first- or early second-century world in which Christian assemblies had begun to attract official suspicion (Pliny's letter to Trajan, c. 112 AD, describes the interrogation of two female *ministrae* under torture in nearby Bithynia, a passage that has become indispensable for situating the Pastoral Epistles' anxiety about respectability and order).

Material culture in Ephesus matters acutely for this chapter because 4:3 attacks teachers "who forbid marriage and require abstinence from foods" (κωλυόντων γαμεῖν, ἀπέχεσθαι βρωμάτων). Urban Ephesus was a city of slave-run households, of *triclinia* where meat from temple sacrifices entered the market, of mystery cult initiations involving ritual fasts, and of a Greco-Roman religious imagination in which sexual abstinence carried prestige (Vestal virgins; the *galli* of Cybele; later Cynic ascetics). Asceticism was already a coherent religious discourse before any Christian heresy attached itself to it. Abraham Malherbe's work on Cynic philosophy in the New Testament world has shown how thoroughly this language of self-mastery (ἐγκράτεια) saturated the moral imagination of cities like Ephesus.

The authorship debate must be named openly here. Most critical scholars — represented by Hans Conzelmann and Martin Dibelius's Hermeneia commentary, Norbert Brox, Lewis Donelson, Raymond Collins, and Margaret Mitchell — argue that 1-2 Timothy and Titus are pseudonymous, written between c. 90 and 130 AD. Their case rests on four pillars: (1) approximately 30 percent of the vocabulary is absent from the undisputed Paulines; (2) the church order described (overseers, elders, deacons, formal widow rolls) is too developed for the 60s; (3) the situation does not fit the framework of Acts; (4) the polemic against false teachers reads like reaction to second-century proto-Gnostic movements. Conservative scholars push back. William Mounce (Word Biblical Commentary), I. Howard Marshall (ICC, who proposes "allonymity" — written by a faithful disciple after Paul's death but in continuity with him), Philip Towner (NICNT), George Knight (NIGTC), and Luke Timothy Johnson (Anchor) defend Pauline authorship. Johnson's distinctive contribution is to read the letter through the genre of *mandata principis* — the Roman administrative letter from a superior to a delegate — which loosens the demand for thick autobiographical situational fit. A middle position championed by C.F.D. Moule, Stephen Wilson, and Jerome Quinn posits Pauline content mediated through an amanuensis, often Luke. This chapter will not resolve the debate, but its mention of the πρεσβυτέριον "council of elders" in 4:14 functions as a key data point on each side.

The textual record is itself partisan. The Pastorals are absent from Codex Vaticanus B (which breaks off in Hebrews) — a fact frequently weaponized by both sides of the authorship debate. They are present in Codex Sinaiticus א and Codex Alexandrinus A. The papyrus P32 (c. 200 AD) preserves only a fragment of Titus, but its early date secures the Pastorals' presence in second-century Christian use. Inscriptions from Asia Minor record female benefactors and *presbyterai* on civic and religious bodies, providing the institutional analogue against which the chapter's elder council should be read.

Greek key terms to fix: ψευδολόγων *pseudologōn* "liars" (4:2); κεκαυστηριασμένων *kekaustēriasmenōn* "seared" or "branded" (a slaveholder's verb — the conscience marked as if with a hot iron); σωμάτων ἄσκησις *somatōn askēsis* "bodily training" (4:8 — the very word from which "asceticism" enters Christian theological vocabulary); πρεσβυτέριον *presbyterion* "council of elders" (4:14); παραθήκη / λόγος θεοῦ καὶ ἐντεύξεως *logos theou kai enteuxeōs* "word of God and prayer" (4:5 — the verbs that "make holy" the food).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre identification is contested but converging. Luke Timothy Johnson's reading of the Pastorals as *mandata principis* — the formal letter of instruction from a Hellenistic ruler to a subordinate official — has gained traction because it explains the mixed register: paraenesis (moral exhortation), church-order regulation, and personal address all coexist because that is precisely what *mandata* contained. Benjamin Fiore's earlier monograph on the function of personal example in the Pastorals reinforces this: Greco-Roman exhortation literature regularly held up an exemplar, and Timothy himself functions as such in 4:12-16. Pseudo-Isocrates' *To Demonicus* and Plutarch's *Moralia* (especially *How a Young Man Should Listen to Poetry* and *On Listening*) supply the closest pagan analogues. Pliny's *Letters* are administrative analogues for the institutional dimensions.

The chapter contains a recognizable "faithful saying" formula at 4:9 (πιστὸς ὁ λόγος καὶ πάσης ἀποδοχῆς ἄξιος). Five such formulae appear across the Pastorals (1 Tim 1:15; 3:1; 4:9; 2 Tim 2:11; Titus 3:8), and they probably mark embedded catechetical or hymnic material that the author quotes from established tradition. Whether 4:10 ("we have our hope set on the living God, who is the Savior of all people") is the saying being introduced or whether 4:8 is, has been disputed since the patristic era; Mounce and Marshall argue for 4:10 as the saying, citing its rhythmic balance.

The vice list of 4:1-3 (deceitful spirits, demonic teachings, lying hypocrites with cauterized consciences, marriage forbidders, food restrictors) follows the standard Greco-Roman *peristasis*-style polemic, but the specifics — ascetic prohibitions of marriage and food — push toward a particular target. The literary device of "I-you" personal exhortation in 4:6-16 fits the *mandata principis* pattern and parallels the household-code-adjacent sections of Colossians 3, Ephesians 5-6, and 1 Peter 2-3 in their use of behavioral imperatives indexed to social roles.

Located in the book's argument, chapter 4 functions as the hinge between the church-order material of chapters 2-3 (prayer, women, overseers, deacons) and the practical pastoral instructions of 5-6 (widows, elders, slaves, the rich). The pivot from institutional structure to personal address is signaled by the demonic prophecy at 4:1 — eschatological framing, then immediate present concern.

Textual variants worth flagging: at 4:10 some witnesses (including א and most majuscules) read ἀγωνιζόμεθα "we strive/struggle," while others (particularly the Western tradition) read ὀνειδιζόμεθα "we are reproached." The latter intensifies the persecution motif and was favored by Tischendorf; modern critical editions (NA28, UBS5) prefer ἀγωνιζόμεθα. At 4:12 the Majority Text adds "in spirit" (ἐν πνεύματι) to the list of virtues; the earliest witnesses omit it.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical New Testament arc, 1 Timothy 4 does two distinct things. The first half locks in a doctrine of creation against ascetic dualism — "everything created by God is good, and nothing is to be rejected if it is received with thanksgiving" — which echoes Genesis 1's repeated "and God saw that it was good," Romans 14:14's "nothing is unclean in itself," and Acts 10's vision of the cleansed sheet. The second half (4:6-16) becomes the New Testament's most concentrated text on ministerial formation and ordination, and 4:14 specifically (the laying on of hands by the πρεσβυτέριον) becomes the linchpin of every ecclesiology that retains a sacramental theology of orders.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** John Chrysostom's homilies on 1 Timothy treated 4:1-5 as a refutation of the Manichaeans and a charter for the legitimate goodness of marriage and food (an emphasis that is striking because Chrysostom himself was personally ascetic). Ambrosiaster, Theodore of Mopsuestia, and Augustine all read the passage as anti-dualist; Augustine in *De Bono Coniugali* draws on it explicitly. Tertullian's *On the Veiling of Virgins* and *On Monogamy* sit awkwardly with the chapter — his rigorism in the Montanist phase pushed against marriage in ways the chapter would seem to forbid, and patristic readers noted the tension. Aquinas (Commentary on 1 Timothy and *Summa* II-II q.152) used the chapter to balance the goods of marriage and consecrated virginity, denying that the latter requires denigration of the former.

Medieval reading developed the ordination side. The Glossa Ordinaria treats 4:14 as evidence for ordination by elders' hands, and the rise of monastic communities and Beguine widow houses generated a body of practical theology around what it meant to live the life of consecrated training (askēsis) without falling into the dualism the chapter forbids.

The Reformation re-litigated everything. Luther's preface to 1 Timothy treated 4:1-3 as an explicit prophecy against Roman Catholic monasticism, clerical celibacy, and Friday fasts — the "doctrines of demons." His polemic was so heated that subsequent Protestant interpretation has had to work to recover the passage's actual targets. Calvin's commentary on 1 Timothy 4 is more measured: he reads the passage as a warning against any ascetic system that locates holiness in the abstention rather than in the disposition. Anabaptist communalism (the Hutterites in particular) drew from 4:4-5's "received with thanksgiving" a sacramental sense of common meals without affirming a Roman sacramental system. Wesley's reading in his *Notes on the New Testament* split the difference, retaining a doctrine of bodily discipline (4:8) while rejecting any required asceticism.

Critical scholarship turned a sharp corner in 1835 when F.C. Baur of the Tübingen school argued that the Pastorals' anti-ascetic polemic was specifically anti-Marcionite and therefore could not be Pauline — Marcion's mid-second-century rejection of the Old Testament god, his anti-creation theology, and his rejection of marriage all aligned too neatly with the chapter's targets. Heinrich Holtzmann developed this into a fuller pseudonymity argument. Adolf von Harnack famously called Marcion "the only Gentile who really understood Paul" — and the Pastorals' anti-Marcion tone became, on this reading, a sign of post-Pauline authorship. Dibelius and Conzelmann's Hermeneia commentary cemented this critical consensus for a generation. Mounce, Marshall, and Towner have responded that the asceticism described in 4:1-5 fits a broader Hellenistic and proto-Jewish-Christian ascetic milieu well before Marcion crystallized it — Cynic philosophy, Therapeutae, Essene tendencies, and what Karen King in *What is Gnosticism?* has cautioned us not to call "Gnosticism" too quickly.

Contemporary scholarship has complicated the categories further. Karen King and Michael Williams have argued that "Gnosticism" as a single category is largely a heresiological construction; the Nag Hammadi corpus reveals more diversity than coherence. Elaine Pagels has located the rise of orthodoxy partly in reaction to ascetic and dualist movements that the proto-orthodox (including the Pastorals' author) deemed dangerous. The contemporary reader must therefore avoid back-reading "Gnostic" as if it named a single coherent movement in the 60s or even the early 100s.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** The chapter's polemic against "irreverent, silly myths" (4:7) and the broader Pastoral concern with "myths and endless genealogies" (1:4) was historically read by patristic and medieval interpreters as anti-Jewish — a rejection of rabbinic midrash and genealogical speculation. Modern Jewish scholars and the Paul-within-Judaism movement have sharply contested this. Krister Stendahl's "introspective conscience" essay, E.P. Sanders' *Paul and Palestinian Judaism*, Daniel Boyarin's *A Radical Jew*, Mark Nanos's work on Romans and Galatians, and Paula Fredriksen's *Paul: The Pagans' Apostle* have together argued that the historical Paul did not oppose Torah or Jewish identity for Jewish believers. The Pastorals, however, sit awkwardly with this recovery: their anti-asceticism is anti-Jewish-Christian in some readings (food regulations evoking kashrut adjacent disputes), and the absence of any positive engagement with Israel's covenants in the Pastorals is striking. Whether this is evidence of post-Pauline authorship by a Gentile-only church (Brox's argument) or of Paul addressing a different audience than in Romans (Marshall's response) is unresolved. Contemporary Jewish scholarship — Amy-Jill Levine in particular — has continued to push back where Christian readings flatten the Jewish texture of New Testament documents and where the Pastorals' polemic has been used historically as a template for anti-Jewish supersessionism.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter's anti-ascetic polemic is real and consequential, but its target is plural and historically contested. The same five verses have been read against Manichaeans, monks, Marcionites, Jewish-Christian ascetics, and Gnostics; the burden on the contemporary interpreter is to resist collapsing them into a single foe.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: The Goodness of Matter and the Question of Asceticism.** The chapter's claim that "everything created by God is good, and nothing is to be rejected if it is received with thanksgiving... for it is made holy by the word of God and prayer" is one of the strongest charters for a sacramental view of creation in the New Testament. The Catholic and Orthodox traditions have read this as anticipating eucharistic theology — note that the verb behind "received with thanksgiving" is εὐχαριστία *eucharistia*. Maximus the Confessor's cosmic theology of the *logoi* of created things reaching their telos in Christ, and Alexander Schmemann's *For the Life of the World*, both depend on a theological move that 1 Timothy 4:4-5 makes possible: matter is not the enemy of spirit but the medium of communion. The CCC at paragraphs 299, 339, and 358 echoes this. Pope Francis's *Laudato Si'* (2015) extends the logic into environmental theology: a creation declared good cannot be treated as raw material.

The Reformed tradition affirms creation's goodness but with reservations about the sacramental extension. Calvin's commentary on 4:5 reads the "word of God and prayer" as referring to scriptural permission and personal prayer of thanksgiving, not as a quasi-sacramental consecration. Anabaptist simplicity reads the passage as commending common meals without elaborate ceremony — the Hutterites' communal table is its own theological statement. Wendell Berry's agrarian theology, drawing on Anabaptist roots, reads 4:4 as a prohibition on every form of contempt for embodied creation, including industrial agriculture.

The position to steelman is the ascetic one the passage rejects. Why would early Christians forbid marriage and food? Because they read the resurrection seriously: if the new creation has dawned, the ordering of the old (marriage, procreation, kinship, food chains) is provisional. Origen's voluntary celibacy, the Desert Fathers' fasting, and the Encratite movement were not obvious distortions of Christian theology but plausible inferences from a high eschatology. The Pastoral author rejects this inference, but his rejection requires a positive theology of created order that neither dismisses asceticism wholesale (4:8 affirms σωμάτων ἄσκησις has "some value") nor permits its absolutization. The threading of the needle is delicate, and the chapter does not give us a complete theory.

N.T. Wright, in *Paul for Everyone: The Pastoral Letters*, reads 4:1-5 within his larger account of new creation: the gospel does not abolish the created order but redeems it, so the ascetic who forbids marriage and food has misread the resurrection — far from rendering the old creation obsolete, the resurrection vindicates it as the raw material God intends to renew. For Wright, "received with thanksgiving" is the characteristic posture of the renewed humanity: not grasping, not despising, but blessing. He stresses that the antidote the chapter offers is not a programme but a person — Timothy himself, set as an example (4:12), the gospel "deposited" and to be guarded and passed on.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter affirms creation's goodness and the legitimacy of marriage and food, but it does not provide a developed theology of how rightly to order the body and the appetites — a gap the Christian tradition has filled with very different answers, from monastic rules to Reformed temperance to modern theologies of embodiment.

**Tension Two: Ordination and the Laying on of Hands.** 1 Timothy 4:14 — "Do not neglect the gift you have, which was given you by prophecy when the council of elders laid their hands on you" (μετὰ ἐπιθέσεως τῶν χειρῶν τοῦ πρεσβυτερίου) — is the New Testament's most concentrated ordination text, and its interpretation has divided traditions for two millennia.

The Catholic position reads the passage in continuity with 2 Timothy 1:6 ("the gift of God which is in you through the laying on of my hands") and with Acts 6 and 13 to construct a sacramental theology of Holy Orders. The CCC at 1536-1600 articulates orders as one of the seven sacraments, with an indelible sacramental character (*character indelebilis*) conferred at ordination. The Council of Trent's twenty-third session (1563) defined this against Protestant reformers. *Lumen Gentium* 21 reaffirmed apostolic succession through episcopal consecration.

The Orthodox position is closely parallel — *cheirotonia* (the laying on of hands) is the sacramental act, and the bishop's hands convey the apostolic gift in unbroken succession from the apostles. Where Orthodox and Catholic differ is in the precise mediation of authority (collegial vs. papal) and in the role of the gathered community in confirming the ordination through the *axios* acclamation.

The Reformed tradition, following Calvin (*Institutes* IV.3.16, IV.4-5), reads 4:14 as establishing presbyterial ordination — a council of elders, not a singular bishop in apostolic succession, conveys the ministerial gift. This is the explicit textual datum: τοῦ πρεσβυτερίου is plural and collective. The Westminster Form of Presbyterial Church-Government (1645) made this central. Lutheran ordination, while typically performed by a bishop or superintendent, theologically grounds itself in the priesthood of all believers and in the church's call rather than in sacramental succession. Wesleyan ordination occupies a middle position — Wesley himself ordained Coke for America in 1784 because he had become convinced that "presbyter" and "bishop" were synonymous in the New Testament.

The Anabaptist tradition reads the chapter through congregational calling: the gift is recognized rather than conferred, and 4:14 describes the community's confirmation of what God has already given. Pentecostal and charismatic appointment foregrounds 4:14's "by prophecy" (διὰ προφητείας) as the operative element — the Spirit's direct designation, ratified by hands.

The position to steelman is the strongest sacramental case. The New Testament's silence on a non-sacramental ordination ritual, combined with the consistent pattern of hand-laying for the conveyance of office and gift (Acts 6:6, 13:3, 1 Tim 4:14, 5:22, 2 Tim 1:6), suggests that the very earliest Christian communities understood the act as more than acknowledgment. Whether this rises to a sacrament in the technical sense, and whether sacramental character is indelible, are theological developments the New Testament does not adjudicate — but the developments build on something genuinely there.

**Where this leaves us:** 4:14 establishes that early Christian leadership was conferred through a ritual act involving prophetic discernment and the hands of a council of elders, but the precise theological weight of that act — sacramental, declarative, charismatic, or mixed — has been read in mutually exclusive ways by traditions that all claim continuity with the practice it describes.

**The unresolved question that lands:** if "everything created by God is good," and if marriage and food and the body are not to be rejected — then what exactly was Timothy training his body for in 4:8, and why did the same Christian tradition that produced this charter of created goodness also produce, within two centuries, the desert fathers, the stylites, and the long career of Christian world-renunciation?

## The Thread

**Genesis 1:31** — "And God saw everything that he had made, and behold, it was very good." 1 Timothy 4:4's καλόν "good" deliberately echoes the Septuagint's repeated καλά of Genesis 1. The creation theology is not new; it is asserted against forgetting.

**Acts 10:9-16** — Peter's vision of the sheet with all kinds of animals, and the voice saying "What God has made clean, do not call common." This is the closest narrative parallel to 1 Timothy 4:3-5's anti-ascetic polemic, and it suggests that the question of food restrictions inside the Christian community was older and more contested than the Pastorals' brief polemic indicates.

**Acts 13:1-3** — The Antiochene laying on of hands on Barnabas and Saul, with prayer and fasting, "after the Holy Spirit said." This is the closest narrative parallel to the πρεσβυτέριον ordination of 1 Timothy 4:14, and reading them together suggests that "by prophecy with the laying on of hands" was a recognizable pattern, not a novel institution.

**Colossians 2:20-23** — "If with Christ you died to the elemental spirits of the world, why, as if you were still alive in the world, do you submit to regulations — 'Do not handle, Do not taste, Do not touch'... These have indeed an appearance of wisdom in promoting self-made religion and asceticism and severity to the body, but they are of no value in stopping the indulgence of the flesh." This passage, written earlier in the canonical reading order and likely earlier in composition (whether or not one accepts Colossians as Pauline), supplies the doctrinal background for the Pastorals' anti-ascetic stance. The verbal overlap with 1 Timothy 4 is significant enough that some critical scholars (Brox, Donelson) have argued the Pastoral author is consciously developing the Colossian polemic.
