# Philippians 4: Two Women, Stoic Virtue, and the Fragrant Sacrifice

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Philippi was a Roman colony on the Via Egnatia, refounded as *Colonia Iulia Augusta Philippensis* in 42 BC after the Battle of Philippi by Antony and Octavian, populated with veterans whose status was inscribed in stone and whose civic culture revolved around the *cursus honorum* of magistracies, ancestry, and public benefaction. Joseph Hellerman's *Reconstructing Honor in Roman Philippi* describes a population habituated to ranked social vision; Peter Oakes's *Philippians: From People to Letter* models the urban demography Paul actually addresses — a small Roman elite, a wider artisan-tradesman stratum, slaves and freedmen, and a notable absence of any sizable Jewish community. Acts 16 narrates Paul's founding visit: the riverside *proseuchē*, the conversion of Lydia the purple-cloth seller, the exorcism of the slave girl, the imprisonment with Silas, and the Philippian jailer — a founding constellation in which two of the four named figures are women, and a third is a slave.

The place from which Paul writes Philippians is contested. The traditional view (O'Brien, Bockmuehl, Bruce) reads "those of Caesar's household" in 4:22 as imperial slaves and freedmen at Rome and dates the letter to c. 60–62 AD during Paul's Roman house-arrest. Hawthorne argues Caesarea. Many critical scholars (G.S. Duncan, Bonnie Thurston) prefer Ephesus in the mid-50s because the multiple journeys assumed at 2:25–30 and 4:18 fit poorly with the distance from Rome. Chapter 4 supplies one of the key data points for this debate: *Kaisaros oikia* in 4:22 ("Caesar's household") could plausibly designate imperial slaves and freedmen anywhere in the empire, since the *familia Caesaris* maintained branches in every provincial capital, but the most natural reading sits more comfortably in Rome.

For chapter 4, the philosophical environment is what most needs reconstructing. Stoic and Cynic moral exhortation was the lingua franca of Hellenistic ethics in the urban Mediterranean of the first century. Diogenes Laertius preserves the Cynic tradition, Epictetus's *Discourses* and *Enchiridion* model the Stoic diatribe, Seneca's *Epistulae Morales* address contentment and the wise man's relation to externals, and Cicero's *De Officiis* canonized the Stoic-Roman virtue tradition for the Latin elite. Abraham Malherbe's *Paul and the Popular Philosophers* documents the saturation of urban Hellenistic life with this philosophical-pastoral vocabulary; Troels Engberg-Pedersen's *Paul and the Stoics* makes the more aggressive case that Paul's ethical reasoning is structurally Stoic. The chapter's virtue list at 4:8 and the *autarkeia* of 4:11 cannot be heard as anything but conscious engagement with that environment.

Material culture supplies more. Philippi's archaeological record — its forum, basilicas, Latin inscriptions catalogued by Pilhofer — confirms a Romanized civic life. Carolyn Osiek and Margaret MacDonald's *A Woman's Place: House Churches in Earliest Christianity* documents, on the material-culture evidence, that Greco-Roman urban house-churches typically depended on women patrons as hosts and informal leaders. The textual evidence is solid: P46 (c. 200), Sinaiticus א, Vaticanus B, Alexandrinus A, with P16 supplementary. The chapter's Greek key terms include συναθλέω *synathleō* ("struggle alongside," 4:3), γνήσιε σύζυγε *gnēsie syzyge* ("true companion," vocative, 4:3), ἐπιεικές *epieikes* ("reasonableness," 4:5), ἀρετή *aretē* ("excellence/virtue," 4:8), αὐτάρκεια *autarkeia* ("self-sufficiency/contentment," 4:11), and the cult-language of 4:18: ὀσμή εὐωδίας *osmē euōdias* and θυσίαν δεκτήν *thysian dektēn*.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Philippians is best read as a friendship letter (*philophronesis*) braided with thanksgiving and paraenesis. Loveday Alexander, Stanley Stowers, and Abraham Malherbe each chart how the conventions of Hellenistic letter-writing — the topos of friends absent yet present, the exchange of gifts and reports, the moral exhortation — are bent to Christian use. Chapter 4 is where every one of those generic strands resurfaces: the appeal to mediate a quarrel between named partners (4:2–3), the philosophical paraenesis (4:4–9), the thanksgiving for the gift coupled with refusal-to-need (4:10–20), and the closing greetings (4:21–23) that have anchored two millennia of provenance debates.

The chapter folds in two distinctly philosophical inserts. The 4:8 virtue list — *alēthē, semna, dikaia, hagna, prosphilē, euphēma, aretē, epainos* — is unmistakable Stoic-Hellenistic virtue vocabulary, with *aretē* the classical Greek term that runs from Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* through the Stoa to Cicero. *Aretē* is a hapax legomenon in Paul: this is the only place he uses it. The 4:5 *epieikes* echoes Aristotle's *epieikeia* — the equity that corrects strict justice (*Nicomachean Ethics* V.10). Then 4:11's *autarkēs einai* is the Stoic-Cynic virtue par excellence: Diogenes the Cynic was the archetype of the *autarkēs* sage; Epictetus *Discourses* I.4 and II.1, Seneca *De Vita Beata*, and the wider Stoic tradition build their doctrine of the wise man around it.

Three literary devices hold the chapter together. First, the *koinōnia* leitmotif that has run from 1:5 ("partnership in the gospel") returns at 4:14–15 (4:14: "share my trouble"; 4:15: "no church entered into partnership with me in giving and receiving"). The book begins and ends in koinōnia. Second, the gift-acknowledgment formula of 4:10–20 follows ancient conventions for thanking a benefactor while refusing the patronage relationship those gifts could otherwise create — Paul receives but transmutes the exchange into a sacrifice offered to God rather than a debt owed to him. Third, the philosophical virtue list and *autarkeia* topos at 4:8–13 read as diatribe-style moral exhortation that has been Christianized at 4:13 — strength comes not from inner reason but "from him who strengthens me." The literary movement of the chapter is from the named, particular conflict of Euodia and Syntyche through the universal philosophical exhortation of 4:8–13 back to the particular gift from Philippi at 4:14–20, with the *Kaisaros oikia* greeting at 4:22 sealing the imperial frame the whole letter has presupposed.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical NT arc, Phil 4 sits at the intersection of three streams: the women's leadership data points scattered across the Pauline corpus (Romans 16, 1 Corinthians 16, Phil 4, with the pastoral epistles introducing tensions); the Christian engagement with Hellenistic philosophy that runs from the apologists through Aquinas; and the later devotional life built around verses like 4:6–7, 4:13, and 4:19.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Chrysostom's homilies on Philippians, preached at Antioch, dwell on 4:11–13 as evidence of Paul's moral training and on 4:8 as a license for engaging pagan virtue tradition wherever it accords with the gospel. Theodore of Mopsuestia in the Antiochene mode reads the chapter historically. Augustine cites 4:6–7 in pastoral contexts and 4:13 in defense of operative grace; the patristic engagement with *autarkeia* tracks Christian appropriation and transformation of Stoic ideals — from independence-from-externals to dependence-on-Christ. Aquinas's commentary on Philippians integrates 4:8's virtue list with the Aristotelian-Thomistic synthesis of natural and infused virtues in *Summa Theologiae* II–II; for Aquinas, pagan philosophical virtue is real but incomplete without the theological virtues.

The medieval *imitatio Christi* tradition more often took its texts from Phil 2 and 3, but 4:11–13 supplied the *Devotio Moderna* and Thomas à Kempis with material for contentment and detachment. The Reformation read 4:13 as testimony to grace's enabling power and 4:6–7 as warrant for prayer against anxiety. Calvin's commentary handles the chapter with characteristic care for the relationship between human effort and divine empowerment. The Anabaptist traditions read 4:8–9 alongside the Sermon on the Mount for the practical shape of the Christian life.

The critical era brought new questions. F.C. Baur's Tübingen school read Pauline letters polemically; the Religionsgeschichtliche Schule located 4:8's virtue list within Hellenistic moral catalogues. Krister Stendahl, E.P. Sanders, and James Dunn's New Perspective on Paul (more directly engaged with Phil 3 than 4) reframed Paul's relationship to Judaism and indirectly to philosophy. Engberg-Pedersen's *Paul and the Stoics* (2000) made the most aggressive recent case that Paul's ethical reasoning is structurally Stoic, with Christ functioning analogously to the Stoic *logos*. N.T. Wright (*Paul for Everyone: The Prison Letters*), Larry Hurtado, and Richard Bauckham push back: Paul borrows the vocabulary but transforms its content — Wright in particular reads 4:11–13 as Paul "learning a secret" that is the inversion of Stoic self-sufficiency, contentment grounded not in detachment-from-externals but in the Messiah who supplies what is needed. On women's leadership, Carolyn Osiek and Margaret MacDonald reconstruct house-church leadership from material evidence; Bonnie Thurston's Sacra Pagina commentary treats Euodia and Syntyche as authoritative co-workers; Eldon Epp's *Junia: The First Woman Apostle* makes the case for Romans 16:7 that aligns with the Phil 4 evidence. On 4:13, Kate Bowler's *Blessed: A History of the American Prosperity Gospel* documents how the verse has been weaponized in modern American Christianity for personal achievement and prosperity claims wholly foreign to Paul's contentment-in-scarcity context.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** Phil 4 is less polemically Jewish-Christian than chapter 3, but Jewish scholarship has contributed to its reading. The Paul-within-Judaism school — Mark Nanos, Pamela Eisenbaum's *Paul Was Not a Christian*, Paula Fredriksen's *Paul: The Pagans' Apostle* — argues that even Paul's most philosophically Hellenized moments remain inside a Jewish apocalyptic framework: the *autarkeia* of 4:11 is undergirded by Israel's God, not Stoic *logos*, and 4:18's sacrificial idiom presupposes the Jerusalem cult's metaphorical extension. Daniel Boyarin's *Border Lines* warns against a Pauline-Hellenization narrative that severs Paul from Judaism; the apparently philosophical 4:8 list, on Boyarin's reading, sits comfortably within the Hellenistic Judaism of Philo and Wisdom literature, both of which catalogued virtues drawn from the Greek philosophical tradition. Where contemporary Jewish scholarship still pushes back is on Christian readings that treat Paul's engagement with Stoicism as evidence that the Torah-Christ contrast in earlier chapters has been left behind: Phil 4's philosophical idiom does not entail a flight from Israel into universal Greek wisdom.

**Where this leaves us:** Phil 4's place in the canon is paradoxical. It is the most apparently universal-philosophical chapter Paul writes, yet its central concrete acts — naming Euodia and Syntyche, receiving the Philippian gift, greeting Caesar's household — are stubbornly particular. The interpretive history has oscillated between the universal and the particular, with neither pole stable.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — Women in ministry: Euodia and Syntyche as test case.** Phil 4:2–3 is one of the canon's hardest data points for any complementarian framework. Paul names two women, says they "struggled alongside me in the gospel" — *synathleō*, the same athletic-warfare verb used at 1:27 for the whole community's gospel-contending — and lists them with Clement and "the rest of my fellow workers, whose names are in the book of life." The vocative *gnēsie syzyge* ("true companion") that Paul addresses to mediate their dispute has been read variously: the church as a whole, a named co-worker (Lightfoot), Lydia (some moderns), even Paul's wife (an ancient speculation Clement of Alexandria entertained). Whoever the *syzygos* is, the women themselves are unambiguously placed inside Paul's apostolic working network.

The Catholic tradition holds the priesthood reserved to men in *Inter Insigniores* (1976), Pope John Paul II's *Mulieris Dignitatem* (1988), and *Ordinatio Sacerdotalis* (1994), while affirming women's full equality in baptismal dignity and a wide range of ministries. The Eastern Orthodox tradition similarly reserves sacramental priesthood. Among Protestants, complementarian readings (Wayne Grudem, John Piper, Andreas Köstenberger) treat Euodia and Syntyche as "co-laborers" rather than authoritative teachers or leaders, distinguishing partnership in evangelism from authoritative church office, and reading 1 Tim 2:11–12 as the structuring text. Egalitarian readings (Linda Belleville, Philip Payne, Ben Witherington, Cynthia Long Westfall, Lucy Peppiatt) read Euodia, Syntyche, Phoebe (Rom 16:1–2), Junia (Rom 16:7), Prisca (Rom 16:3), and the prophesying women of 1 Cor 11 as a coherent Pauline pattern of women in apostolic leadership networks, with 1 Tim 2:11–12 a contextual restraint rather than a transhistorical office-bar.

The least-intuitive position in current Anglophone evangelical discourse is the egalitarian one for those raised complementarian, and vice versa. Steelman the complementarian: 4:2–3 calls them "co-workers" but does not specify *teaching* or *governing* roles. The dispute Paul mediates is significant enough to address by name, but the same naming applies to Clement and "the rest" — Paul's working network was wider than his ordained leadership. *Synathleō* describes evangelistic struggle, not ecclesial office. The complementarian reading does not deny these women's significance; it denies that "co-worker" entails the specific authoritative office that 1 Tim 2:11–12 restricts. Steelman the egalitarian: Paul names women in his working network at every available opportunity, uses for them the same vocabulary he uses for himself and his male colleagues, and treats their gospel-work as constitutive of the apostolic mission. To partition that work into "evangelism but not authoritative teaching" requires a category Paul nowhere supplies. The 1 Tim 2:11–12 prohibition addresses a specific Ephesian situation; Phil 4:2–3 addresses a different specific situation in which two named women are exercising leadership influence consequential enough to require apostolic mediation.

Where this leaves us: the chapter does not resolve the contemporary office debate, but it makes any reading harder that excludes women from substantive Christian leadership. The 1 Tim 2 restriction must be read in light of Phil 4, not the reverse.

**Tension 2 — Christianized Stoicism versus distinctive Christian virtue.** The 4:8 virtue list and 4:11 *autarkeia* are explicitly Hellenistic philosophical vocabulary. *Aretē* is a Pauline hapax. The question is what to make of it.

Engberg-Pedersen's *Paul and the Stoics* argues that Paul's ethical reasoning is structurally Stoic — that the move from "old self" to "new self" parallels the Stoic move from passions to reason, with Christ functioning analogously to the Stoic *logos*. On this reading the difference between Paul and Epictetus is more idiomatic than substantive; Christianity is Stoicism in another idiom. The conservative pushback from N.T. Wright, Richard Bauckham, and Larry Hurtado argues that Paul Christianizes the vocabulary but transforms its content: 4:13's "him who strengthens me" makes the source of *autarkeia* not inner reason but external Christ-power, which is precisely *not* Stoic self-sufficiency.

The Catholic natural-law tradition, going back to Aquinas's adoption of Aristotelian-Stoic virtue ethics in *Summa Theologiae* II–II, sees pagan virtue as genuine but incomplete; the theological virtues of faith, hope, and love perfect the cardinal virtues without abolishing them, and 4:8's catalog is a license for that integration. Reformed theology accepts pagan moral wisdom under the rubric of "common grace" (Calvin) but denies that natural virtue contributes to saving righteousness. Anabaptist tradition, especially Stanley Hauerwas and John Howard Yoder, is suspicious of philosophical resources imported from outside the church's own narrative formation: virtue is shaped by the church's distinctive practices, not by translating Stoic categories. Eastern Orthodox theology, following Gregory of Nazianzus, Maximus the Confessor, and the broader patristic synthesis, is openly hospitable to philosophical wisdom as preparatory for theological understanding, with *theosis* the goal that philosophical virtue alone cannot reach.

Steelman Engberg-Pedersen's structurally-Stoic reading: Paul writes 4:8 in an unfiltered Hellenistic philosophical vocabulary; he calls *autarkeia* something he has *learned*, the same word Stoic teachers used; the virtue list is pagan, not biblical; the natural reading is that Paul has adopted Hellenistic ethics with Christ as his teacher. The strength of the argument is the linguistic data; the weakness is 4:13, where the source of strength is not the inner reason but Christ's external empowerment, which is structurally non-Stoic. Steelman the Wright-Bauckham reading: vocabulary borrowing is not the same as structural adoption; the move at 4:13 is decisive; Paul converts *autarkeia* from self-sufficiency to Christ-sufficiency, which is the inversion, not the embrace, of the Stoic ideal. Where this leaves us: Phil 4 cannot be read as if Paul invented his ethical language *ex nihilo*, but neither can it be read as Stoicism with a Christological gloss. The relationship between Christian and pagan virtue remains the live question, and 4:8 and 4:13 stand in productive tension.

**The unresolved question:** If 4:13's "I can do all things through him who strengthens me" is the Christological correction of *autarkeia*, what exactly is corrected — the *self*-sufficiency, leaving sufficiency intact, or the very ideal of needing-nothing-from-others, which Paul's whole gift-acknowledgment in 4:14–18 seems to qualify rather than endorse?

## The Thread

- **Romans 16:1–7** — Phoebe the *diakonos* and *prostatis*, Prisca and Aquila ("my fellow workers"), Junia ("outstanding among the apostles"). The single closest companion text to 4:2–3, naming an analogous network of women in Pauline leadership in a different city.
- **1 Corinthians 7:32–35** — "the unmarried man is anxious about the things of the Lord" — the same anxiety vocabulary Paul tells the Philippians to lay aside in 4:6, but here turned positive. The *merimnaō* word group ties the two passages together, and the contrast is illuminating.
- **Hebrews 13:15–16** — "let us continually offer up a sacrifice of praise to God … to do good and to share what you have, for such sacrifices are pleasing to God." The closest non-Pauline parallel to 4:18's transfer of cult-language to ethical-economic exchange, written after Paul and giving the move a more developed theological articulation.
- **1 Timothy 2:11–12** — the locus classicus of the women-in-ministry debate, written later in the canon (whether by Paul or in his name) and bearing the brunt of any reading of Phil 4:2–3. The two passages must be read together, and Phil 4 — being the undisputed Pauline witness — sets the interpretive baseline.
