# 2 Thessalonians 2: The Restrainer, the Lawless One, and the Mystery Already at Work

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 2 is the most cryptic eschatological passage in the Pauline corpus, and its cryptic-ness is itself a historical datum. The author refuses to name the figures involved — *to katechon* (neuter, "that which restrains"), *ho katechōn* (masculine, "he who restrains"), *ho anthrōpos tēs anomias* (the man of lawlessness), *to mystērion tēs anomias* (the mystery of lawlessness) — and Verse 5 acknowledges that the original audience already had the key: "Do you not remember that when I was still with you I told you these things?" Whatever was said in person was withheld from the letter, and the most economical historical explanation is political risk. Naming a Roman emperor as the eschatological *anthrōpos tēs anomias*, or naming Roman imperial authority itself as the *katechon*, in a city whose politarchs had already taken bond-money against Paul's gospel as treason against Caesar's decrees (Acts 17:7), would be a written confession of capital sedition.

The political situation is the same as in chapter 1. Thessalonica is a Greek free city under politarchs (Vardar Gate inscription, IG X 2,1 no. 126), governed in close alignment with Roman imperial interests. The imperial cult is well-attested at Thessalonica through coins, inscriptions, and the work of Holland Hendrix, Christoph vom Brocke, and Karl Donfried. The most acute first-century analogue to the figure of 2:4 — "who opposes and exalts himself against every so-called god... takes his seat in the temple of God, proclaiming himself to be God" — is the AD 40 incident in which Caligula attempted to install his statue in the Jerusalem Temple. Josephus narrates the incident in *Antiquities* 18.261–309 and in *Jewish War* 2.184–203, and Philo treats it at length in *Legatio ad Gaium* 184–348. The crisis was averted only by Caligula's assassination in January AD 41. If the historical Paul is writing about a decade later, the Caligula incident is living memory in the Jewish-Christian network, and 2:4 is structured as the kind of escalation that incident anticipated. Tacitus's *Annals* 12 and Suetonius's *Caligula* 22 corroborate the political theology of imperial self-deification that 2 Thessalonians 2:4 critiques.

The authorship debate intensifies in chapter 2. Verse 2 ("either by a spirit or a spoken word, or a letter seeming to be from us, to the effect that the day of the Lord has come") and 2:15 ("hold to the traditions that you were taught by us, either by our spoken word or by our letter") are read by pseudonymity-defenders (Wrede 1903, Trilling 1972 and 1980, Holland 1988, Menken 1994, Boring) as a fictional letter's protest against rival fictional letters — a forgery that names other forgeries to authenticate itself. Authenticity-defenders (Wanamaker 1990, Marshall, Bruce, Malherbe 2000, Green 2002, Fee 2009) read the same verses as Paul addressing real misinformation in the Thessalonian community, possibly an over-realized eschatological reading of 1 Thessalonians 4–5. Frank Hughes (*Early Christian Rhetoric and 2 Thessalonians*, 1989) reads the chapter as the *probatio* of a deliberative speech, with Paul (or "Paul") adducing apocalyptic evidence to dissuade the community from a present-day-of-the-Lord position.

Eschatological tension between the two letters is the second main pseudonymity argument, and chapter 2 is its center. 1 Thessalonians 4–5 describes the *parousia* as a thief in the night, a sudden surprise; 2 Thessalonians 2 describes a sequenced apocalypse with a checklist (rebellion, revelation of the lawless one, defeat by Christ's *epiphaneia*). The two schemes are not strictly contradictory — apocalyptic literature regularly combines suddenness-from-below with sequenced-from-above (Mark 13, 4 Ezra) — but the rhetorical posture is markedly different. Authenticity-defenders read the difference as Paul correcting a misreading of 1 Thessalonians; pseudonymity-defenders read it as a later author trying to hold the *parousia* expectation together as the first generation died out without its arrival.

The textual evidence for chapter 2 is reasonably stable but contains one famously contested variant. P46 (c. AD 200) and the major fourth-century uncials (Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus, Vaticanus) preserve the chapter substantially as printed in modern critical editions. In 2:3 the noun *apostasia* is uncontested in the Greek manuscripts, but in popular dispensational literature (E. Schuyler English, Kenneth Wuest, Tim LaHaye, Chuck Missler, more recently Andy Woods) the case has been pressed that the word means "departure" — i.e., the rapture — making 2:3 prove pretribulation removal of the church. BDAG, Louw-Nida, every major lexicon, and every major critical commentary reject this. *Apostasia* in Hellenistic Greek usage and in the LXX (Joshua 22:22, 2 Chronicles 29:19, Jeremiah 2:19, 1 Maccabees 2:15) means religious or political rebellion, never spatial departure. The dispensational reading is etymological speculation against established usage. In 2:13 there is a genuinely contested variant — *aparchēn* ("firstfruits," read by B, F, G, P, Vulgate, Syriac) versus *ap' archēs* ("from the beginning," read by Sinaiticus, D, Y, Byzantine majority) — which carries real theological weight: are the Thessalonians the *firstfruits* of Macedonia (a Pauline self-quotation from Romans 16:5, 1 Corinthians 16:15) or chosen *from the beginning* (a strong predestinarian reading echoing Ephesians 1:4)? Most modern editions favor *aparchēn*; the NA28 prints it.

Five Greek terms structure the chapter. *Apostasia* (ἀποστασία, 2:3) — religious/political rebellion, not spatial departure. *Ho anthrōpos tēs anomias* (ὁ ἄνθρωπος τῆς ἀνομίας, 2:3) — the man of lawlessness, with a parallel reading in some manuscripts as *ho anthrōpos tēs hamartias* (the man of sin); *anomia* is the LXX rendering of the prophetic critique of Torah-violating rulers (Daniel 11:36, Psalm 89:23 LXX). *To katechon / ho katechōn* (τὸ κατέχον / ὁ κατέχων, 2:6, 7) — neuter then masculine participles of *katechō* (to hold down, restrain, take possession of), the most contested two words in the Pauline corpus after *pistis Christou*. *Mystērion tēs anomias* (μυστήριον τῆς ἀνομίας, 2:7) — apocalyptic term for the hidden working of evil before its eschatological revelation, paralleled in 1 Corinthians 2:7, Ephesians 3:9, and the *raz* (mystery) language at Qumran (1QpHab, 4QInstruction). *Parousia* (παρουσία, 2:1, 8, 9) — used both for Christ's coming and for the lawless one's coming; the term in first-century Greek is the technical word for an emperor's official arrival in a city, with attendant acclamation, sacrifice, and statue installation.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre shifts within chapter 2. Verses 1–2 are an epistolary discourse-marker ("now concerning," *peri de* — Paul's standard formula for taking up a topic, as in 1 Thessalonians 4:9, 5:1, 1 Corinthians 7:1, 8:1). Verses 3–12 are apocalyptic instruction in epistolary form — densely allusive, sequenced, drawing on Daniel and the prophetic tradition without quoting them outright. Verses 13–15 return to thanksgiving and exhortation. Verses 16–17 are a liturgical-style benediction. The chapter compresses three distinct registers — epistolary, apocalyptic, liturgical — into thirty-eight verses.

Robert Jewett (1986) and Frank Hughes (1989) classify the chapter as the *probatio* of a deliberative speech. Charles Wanamaker (NIGTC 1990) reads it as an "eschatological correction" — Paul addressing a specific over-realized eschatology and dissuading the community from a particular conclusion. Beverly Gaventa (Interpretation, 1998) reads the apocalyptic core as a midrashic exposition of Daniel, comparable to the methods of contemporary Jewish apocalyptic writers (the author of 4 Ezra, the author of 2 Baruch).

The literary devices are dense. The chapter is a chain-quotation of prophetic texts without explicit citation: Daniel 11:36 ("the king shall do as he wills; he shall exalt himself and magnify himself above every god, and shall speak astonishing things against the God of gods") sits behind 2:4, with Daniel 8:11–12 and Daniel 7:25 contributing additional language. Isaiah 14:13–14 ("I will ascend to heaven... I will make myself like the Most High") and Ezekiel 28:2 ("you have said, 'I am a god, I sit in the seat of the gods'") provide the figure of the self-exalting ruler. Job 4:9 ("by the breath of God they perish, and by the blast of his anger they are consumed"), Isaiah 11:4 ("with the breath of his lips he shall kill the wicked"), and Psalm 33:6 echo in 2:8 (the lawless one destroyed by the *pneuma* of Christ's mouth). The *mystērion* language of 2:7 sits within the apocalyptic mystery-tradition of Daniel 2 and the Qumran *pesharim*. The reference in 2:9–11 to *energeia planēs* ("activity of error / strong delusion") evokes Deuteronomy 13:1–5 and 1 Kings 22:19–23 (the lying spirit sent to deceive Ahab) — God's permitted use of deception in judgment.

The chapter's structural location: the *probatio* of the letter, the explanation of why the day of the Lord is *not yet* present. Chapter 1 has set up the frame (persecution as evidence of coming vindication); chapter 2 deploys the frame to deny a misreading; chapter 3 will return to ethics (work, idleness, discipline). Chapter 2 does the heavy theological lifting.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** No passage in Paul has a longer or more contentious interpretive history. Bernard McGinn's *Antichrist: Two Thousand Years of Human Fascination with Evil* (1994) and *Visions of the End: Apocalyptic Traditions in the Middle Ages* (1979) are the standard surveys; Stephen Hultgren and L.J. Lietaert Peerbolte (*The Antecedents of Antichrist*, 1996) trace the Jewish background.

Patristic readings cluster around three poles. (1) The lawless one is a specific future ruler — Irenaeus, *Adversus Haereses* 5.25–30, develops the antichrist as a future Danite king in a rebuilt Jerusalem temple; Hippolytus, *On Christ and Antichrist* (c. 200), gives the most detailed patristic exegesis, building on Daniel 7. (2) The *katechon* is the Roman Empire — Tertullian (*De Resurrectione* 24, *Apology* 32): "the great force... is, in fact, the Roman state, whose removal... shall introduce Antichrist," a reading followed by Lactantius, Chrysostom (*Hom. on 2 Thess* 4), and Theodoret. The political theology is striking: the very empire that persecutes the church also restrains the eschatological tyrant. (3) Augustine, *City of God* 20.19, reviews the options — Rome, elect Christians, the Jerusalem temple — and refuses to fix one: "I frankly confess I do not know what he means." That agnosticism shapes Western readings for centuries.

The medieval and Reformation periods. Joachim of Fiore (twelfth century) reads the antichrist as imminent and identifies him with corrupted ecclesiastical power, planting a seed that the Reformation will detonate. Aquinas's commentary on 2 Thessalonians inherits Augustine's caution. The decisive Reformation move is Luther's identification of the papacy as the man of lawlessness (*Smalcald Articles* II.4; *On the Papacy in Rome*, 1520; *On the Babylonian Captivity*, 1520). Calvin's commentary on 2 Thessalonians (1551) endorses the same reading more guardedly, treating the antichrist as a *system* of corruption centered in but not limited to the papacy. The Westminster Confession 25.6 (1646) codifies the historicist reading: "The Pope of Rome... is that Antichrist, that man of sin, and son of perdition, that exalts himself, in the Church, against Christ and all that is called God." This is the classical Protestant historicist reading, and it shaped Anglo-American Protestant identity for two and a half centuries.

The Counter-Reformation responded with two new exegetical strategies. Francisco Ribera (1591) developed the futurist reading — the antichrist is a single end-time figure yet to appear, not the contemporary papacy — and Luis de Alcázar (1614) the preterist reading — the antichrist already came in Nero or Titus. Ironically, both Catholic moves became the basis of later Protestant traditions: futurism through Darby and dispensationalism, preterism through R.C. Sproul and the Reformed historic-preterist movement (Kenneth Gentry).

Modern critical scholarship splits on authenticity (Baur, Wrede 1903, Trilling 1972/1980, Menken 1994 for pseudonymity; Wanamaker 1990, Malherbe 2000, Beale 2003 for authenticity), but both wings read the chapter as a coherent midrash on Daniel; Beale's "already-not-yet" framework treats the *mystery of lawlessness* as the present hidden working of the same evil that will be eschatologically revealed, and the apocalyptic-Paul school (Beker, Gaventa, Campbell) reads it as a window onto Paul's cosmic-conflict theology. The dispensational tradition, widely held in American evangelicalism, derives from John Nelson Darby (1830s–1860s) — *katechōn* as the Holy Spirit in the church, church "taken out of the way" at the rapture, then antichrist revealed for a seven-year tribulation — popularized through Scofield (1909), Walvoord (1957), Hal Lindsey (1970), and Tim LaHaye's *Left Behind* (1995–2007), with the chapter-specific moves of reading *apostasia* as departure and identifying the lawless one with a future European or Middle Eastern ruler. The historic preterist reading (Sproul, Gentry) identifies the man of lawlessness with Nero (whose Hebrew-transliterated name equals 666, who presided over the first imperial persecution in AD 64, and whose *redivivus* legend persisted for decades). The idealist reading (Hendriksen, Hoekema, Riddlebarger, Beale) takes him as a recurring archetype with a final intensification before the *parousia*; the classical historicist reading (Westminster, classical Reformed and Lutheran) sees the figure progressively manifest in the papal system.

The imperial-critical school (Donfried, Koester, Horsley, Harrison, Neil Elliott) reads the chapter as a deliberate inversion of Roman imperial *parousia* language. The lawless one's *parousia* "with all power and false signs and wonders" (2:9) is the inverse of Christ's *parousia* and is structured as a critique of imperial divinization more generally — Caligula, Nero, Domitian as types.

**Jewish scholarly critique and its evolution.** The chapter sits squarely within second-temple Jewish apocalyptic: Daniel 7, 8, and 11 (the little horn / self-exalting king who blasphemes, takes away the *tamid*, and tramples the sanctuary), 4 Ezra 5 and 12, 2 Baruch 39–40, Sibylline Oracles 3–5 (Beliar as the eschatological adversary), Qumran 1QM, and the *Belial* / *Beliar* figure of the *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs* — the typological background for the *anomos*. David Flusser's *Judaism and the Origins of Christianity* (1988) and John J. Collins's *The Apocalyptic Imagination* (3rd ed., 2016) treat 2 Thessalonians 2 as a Christianized variant of the Jewish eschatological-tyrant tradition; Geza Vermes places 1QM 1 (the war against the *kittim*) in the same line; Maimonides' *Hilkhot Melakhim* 11–12 gives the medieval Jewish messianic standard, refusing apocalyptic specificity while affirming the messianic age as restoration.

Where the Jewish and Christian traditions converge: the eschatological tyrant is a real figure, his self-exaltation is the structural inverse of Israel's God, his temple-defiling is the climactic offense, his defeat is the messianic event. Where they diverge: the antichrist tradition's Christianization (the messianic figure is Jesus the *kyrios* personally returning), the temple specifics (Jewish texts assume the Jerusalem Temple; Christian readings vary on "the temple of God"), and the post-AD 70 trajectory (Jewish apocalyptic increasingly relocates the messianic event to a rebuilt Temple; Christian apocalyptic increasingly spiritualizes the *naos*).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What does the author believe God is doing? God is restraining. The most striking theological claim of the chapter is that the eschaton is delayed — not because God is absent, but because God is *holding back* the full revelation of evil so that the harvest of the elect can be gathered (cf. 2:13–14). The mystery of lawlessness is *already at work* (2:7) but is not yet permitted to unfurl into the figure of the lawless one. The Pauline (or pseudo-Pauline) author reads history as God's deliberate management of the time before the end — a deferred apocalypse, with the deferral itself being theologically meaningful.

**Tension 1: Identifying the man of lawlessness.** Three serious positions, each with internal coherence.

*Futurist (dispensational and some non-dispensational).* The lawless one is a future political ruler, not yet identified, who will arise in the eschatological tribulation. Lindsey, LaHaye, Walvoord; in non-dispensational futurist form, Joseph Ratzinger / Benedict XVI carefully gestures toward the "final trial" of CCC 675–677 in his eschatological writings. The strength of the futurist reading: it takes 2:8 ("destroyed by the appearance of his coming") with full seriousness — the lawless one is destroyed *at* the *parousia*, which has not happened, so the lawless one has not appeared in his full revealed form.

*Preterist (historic).* The lawless one is Nero or another first-century figure. Sproul, Gentry, Hank Hanegraaff. The strength: this reading respects the chapter's clear first-century horizon ("you know what is restraining him *now*") and the literary-historical context of the imperial cult. The weakness: Christ did not visibly arrive at the destruction of Nero or of Jerusalem; the preterist must spiritualize the *parousia* of 2:8.

*Historicist (Westminster and classical Reformed).* The lawless one is the papacy as a system progressively unveiled. Westminster Confession 25.6, Calvin (more guardedly), Luther (most pungently). Steelman this reading honestly: the historicist position is not anti-Catholic spite but a sustained reading of the chapter's criteria — a figure who *takes his seat in the temple of God, proclaiming himself to be God*. For the sixteenth-century Reformers, the papacy was a system that had taken its seat *in the church* (the *naos* of God on a New Covenant reading), claimed prerogatives of binding and loosing that they read as divinization (the Petrine extra-canonical claims), and was protected by imperial Christian power. The Reformers were not naïvely demonizing; they were deploying a reading of *temple* (the church), *exalts himself against every god* (the medieval ecclesial claims of supremacy), and *signs and wonders* (the late-medieval miracle traditions they considered fraudulent) that fit the chapter's grammar within their context. The historicist reading became implausible to most modern Protestants once the papacy lost temporal power and once the Reformed churches encountered comparable institutional pathologies of their own — but its internal coherence as exegesis is real.

*Idealist (recurring archetype).* William Hendriksen, Anthony Hoekema (*The Bible and the Future*, 1979), Kim Riddlebarger, G.K. Beale. The man of lawlessness is a typological figure manifesting in many historical instantiations, with a final eschatological intensification. The strength: this reading absorbs the historical pluralism of the antichrist tradition and resists single-figure identification. The weakness: it can become so abstract that no specific historical event would falsify it.

*Where this leaves us:* The chapter's grammar requires *some* eschatological figure who is genuinely defeated at Christ's coming; the chapter's first-century context strongly implies a Roman imperial referent in the original horizon; the chapter's two-thousand-year reception requires acknowledging that the figure has been read as papal, imperial, futurist, archetypal, and Neronian, often at great cost. No single identification is exegetically inevitable; all four traditions have caught something the chapter genuinely says.

**Tension 2: Who or what is the *katechōn*?** The most-contested two words in the Pauline corpus.

*The Roman Empire / Roman authority.* Tertullian, Chrysostom, most patristic readers, N.T. Wright (*Paul and the Faithfulness of God*), Bruce, Ben Witherington III. The argument: the *katechōn* is named with deliberate political caution because identifying Rome openly would be capital sedition. The Roman state and its officials restrain civic chaos and thereby delay the full eschatological tyrant. This reading sits well with Romans 13:1–7. The weakness: it makes the Roman Empire God's positive instrument while elsewhere in apocalyptic Paul the *exousiai* are at best ambiguous.

*The gospel mission to the Gentiles.* John Calvin's commentary, Oscar Cullmann (*Christ and Time*, 1946), I. Howard Marshall, Charles Wanamaker. The argument: the *katechōn* is the work of the gospel itself, which "must be preached to all nations" before the end (Mark 13:10). The masculine *ho katechōn* would be the apostle or the apostolic preaching personified. The strength: it integrates the eschatological scheme with the mission. The weakness: it requires reading *katechō* in a sense (positive holding-in-place) that is unusual for the verb.

*The Holy Spirit and/or the Church.* The dispensational reading. Scofield, Walvoord, Lindsey, LaHaye. The argument: *to katechon* is the indwelling Holy Spirit in the church, and *ho katechōn* is the Spirit personally; at the rapture the church is taken out, the Spirit's restraining presence is removed, and the lawless one is revealed. The strength: it provides a theological mechanism for the church's removal before tribulation. The weakness: there is no other passage where the Holy Spirit is described as being "taken out of the way," and the gender shift between neuter and masculine has a more natural reading in Greek (the abstract noun and the personal agent of the same restraining function).

*An angelic figure (Michael or another).* Lars Hartman, Charles Giblin (1967): *ho katechōn* is an angelic restrainer in the line of Daniel 10:13, 12:1. *Paul himself or the apostolic preaching.* Johannes Munck (1959): the apostle whose mission must be completed before the end, which would integrate 2:7's "until he is out of the way" with Paul's death. Both readings are at home in some stream of Jewish apocalyptic but speculative against the text.

The political theology stakes are substantial. If the *katechōn* is the Roman state, the Augustinian-Lutheran two-kingdoms tradition has direct biblical warrant for the state's positive role in restraining evil (Augustine's *City of God* 19, Luther's *Temporal Authority*, the Augsburg Confession Article 16). If the *katechōn* is the gospel mission, the missiological reading aligns with Reformed and post-Vatican II Catholic theologies of evangelization. If the *katechōn* is the Holy Spirit, the dispensational ecclesiology of pretribulation removal stands. If the *katechōn* is angelic, apocalyptic spiritual-warfare readings are reinforced.

The Anabaptist tradition (Schleitheim Confession, John Howard Yoder, William Cavanaugh) reads the chapter against the grain of the imperial-restrainer reading: the state is not the *katechōn* of evil but is itself implicated in the *mystērion tēs anomias*. The lawless one is *already* the imperial system, and the "restraint" is whatever in history holds back the worst version of imperial idolatry — including the witness of nonviolent communities.

*Where this leaves us:* The author refused to specify the *katechōn*, and two thousand years of attempted specification has not produced consensus. The most honest reading takes the chapter's own restraint at face value: there is *something* holding back the eschatological tyrant, that something is at work *now*, and the church's ethical task is to be on the side of restraint rather than the side of the *mystērion tēs anomias*.

**Unresolved question:** If the mystery of lawlessness is *already at work* in the present, and if the *katechōn* — whatever it is — is what holds it back, then what is the church's responsibility *toward* the *katechōn*? Augustine's silence may be wiser than any of the named identifications, but silence is not a politics. A community that believes the eschatological tyrant is being restrained must form some practical posture toward whatever does the restraining; refusing to name the agent does not exempt the church from cooperating with or resisting it.

## The Thread

- **Daniel 11:36** — "the king shall do as he wills; he shall exalt himself and magnify himself above every god, and shall speak astonishing things against the God of gods." The structural source of 2:4. Whether 2 Thessalonians 2 is read futurist, preterist, or historicist, the figure inherits Daniel 11's grammar.
- **Mark 13:14** — "when you see the abomination of desolation standing where it ought not to be" (echoing Daniel 9:27, 11:31, 12:11). The Synoptic apocalypse and 2 Thessalonians 2 share the sanctuary-defilement template, raising the synoptic question of how Paul's eschatology relates to Jesus's.
- **Revelation 13:1–18** — the beast from the sea, the second beast, the false prophet performing signs, the mark, the worship demanded. John's apocalypse explicitly fuses imperial political power with the eschatological tyrant figure of 2 Thessalonians 2; the two passages are read together in nearly every major historical reception.
- **1 John 2:18, 22; 4:3** — "many antichrists have come... by this we know it is the last hour... every spirit that does not confess Jesus is from God is the spirit of the antichrist, of which you heard that it was coming." The Johannine literature pluralizes the antichrist, offering an early canonical pivot from a single eschatological figure to a recurring spirit, anticipating the idealist reading by nineteen hundred years.
