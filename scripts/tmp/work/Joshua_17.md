# Joshua 17: Iron Chariots, Five Daughters, and the Forest You Must Clear

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Joshua 17 occupies a geographical and political crossroads. The territory described for the half-tribe of Manasseh stretches across the central hill country and into the Jezreel Valley, a corridor that in the Late Bronze Age (c.1550–1200 BCE) was among the most militarily contested real estate in the Levant. The chapter names a string of fortified cities — Beth-shean, Ibleam, Dor, En-dor, Taanach, Megiddo — and then admits, in v.12, that the Manassites could not take them. For the historian, this list is the single most important datum in the chapter. Every site named has been excavated. Beth-shean (Tell el-Husn) shows continuous Egyptian occupation through the reign of Ramesses III, with Egyptian garrison architecture and stelae of Seti I and Ramesses II recovered from the mound. Megiddo (Tell el-Mutesellim), excavated by the Chicago Oriental Institute in the 1920s–30s, then by Yigael Yadin in the 1960s, and in recent decades by the Tel Aviv expedition under Israel Finkelstein and David Ussishkin, shows a massive Canaanite city destroyed around 1130 BCE but rebuilt as a Canaanite-material-culture site well into the 10th century. Taanach and Dor show similar profiles. The archaeological picture accords strikingly with the biblical admission: the highland settlement pattern Finkelstein documented — small four-room-house villages proliferating in the hill country from c.1200 BCE — sits alongside lowland Canaanite cities that Israel did not conquer but eventually, as v.13 says, subjected to forced labor (*mas*).

The Canaanite "chariots of iron" (*rekhev barzel*, רֶכֶב בַּרְזֶל) in 17:16 reflect the technological reality of the Late Bronze–Iron transition. Robert Drews, in *The End of the Bronze Age* (1993), argued that chariot warfare was the dominant military system of the Late Bronze palace states, and its collapse — not replacement by iron-weapon infantry, but by swarms of skirmishers wielding javelins — produced the systems collapse around 1177 BCE. Whether the chariots were literally iron-plated or simply iron-fitted (wheel rims, axle caps, weapon scythes) remains debated; what is clear is that the lowland plain favored chariot armies and the hill country did not. The text's geography is militarily exact.

Five Hebrew terms organize the chapter:

- *naḥălâ* (נַחֲלָה) — inheritance, portion, heritable estate. The word appears constantly in the allotment chapters and signals that the land is not conquered plunder but intergenerational patrimony.
- *goral* (גּוֹרָל) — lot. The mechanism of distribution. In the cultic setting at Shiloh (implied here and stated in Josh 18), the lot is not chance but divine decision.
- *bat Zelophehad* (בַּת צְלָפְחָד) — "daughter of Zelophehad." The legal innovation of female inheritance when a man dies sonless.
- *mas* (מַס) — forced labor, corvée. V.13's "they put the Canaanites to forced labor" uses the same term later used of Solomon's levies on Israelites (1 Kgs 5:13–14; 9:21), a continuity that Deuteronomistic historians likely noticed.
- *bereʾ* (בָּרֵא) — the rare verb in 17:15, 18, "clear" (literally "cut down"). Used only a handful of times in the Hebrew Bible, once of God creating (Gen 1:1, different root but same form), here of humans hacking forest into field.

On dating and authorship, the Joshua allotment lists belong to what Martin Noth called the Deuteronomistic History (DtrH), a single historiographic work composed during or just after the Babylonian exile (Noth 1943). Frank Moore Cross's dual redaction model (*Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*, 1973) posited a Josianic edition (Dtr1) optimistically celebrating Davidic restoration, overwritten by an exilic edition (Dtr2) sobered by the collapse. The allotment material, with its administrative specificity, has been variously dated: Albrecht Alt and Noth treated it as an ideal pre-monarchic document (a tribal list projected backward); Cross and G. Ernest Wright argued the Judah list reflects late-monarchic Judahite districts; Zecharia Kallai's *Historical Geography of the Bible* (1986) and Anson Rainey's *The Sacred Bridge* (2006) leaned toward a Davidic-Solomonic administrative origin; Nadav Na'aman's *Borders and Districts in Biblical Historiography* (1986) disaggregated the lists, assigning some to different centuries. For Joshua 17 specifically, the unfulfilled-conquest note in v.12 and the chariot-of-iron anxiety in v.16 both read as pre-Davidic memory, whatever editorial framework they sit inside.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is administrative geography wrapped in narrative — a hybrid that modern readers find dry but that ancient Near Eastern scribes used with some sophistication. Comparable texts include the Egyptian Onomasticon of Amenope and Hittite treaty boundary lists. The narrator interrupts the territorial description three times with story: the daughters of Zelophehad (vv.3–6), the unfulfilled-conquest note (vv.12–13), and the Joseph tribe's complaint (vv.14–18). These narrative interruptions are not accidents. They sit at the seams of the genre, and they do argumentative work.

The chapter's literary center is the Joseph dialogue in vv.14–18. It is structured as a classic three-move disputation: the tribes complain (v.14), Joshua responds (v.15), the tribes counter-complain (v.16), Joshua answers with a final ruling (vv.17–18). Each of Joshua's replies escalates. In v.15, he essentially says: go do the work. In v.17–18, he adds: you are numerous and strong, you shall have more than one allotment, but the forest is yours to clear and the chariots are yours to drive out. The rhetorical movement is from deflating complaint to commissioning labor.

The daughters-of-Zelophehad pericope (vv.3–6) is the second time Scripture tells their story — the first is Numbers 27:1–11, with a follow-up at Numbers 36. The Joshua version is compressed, citing the earlier legal precedent ("the LORD commanded Moses"). This is a legal *recapitulation*: the narrative loops back to confirm that the Numbers ruling has been executed. Source-critically, Julius Wellhausen and later scholars identified the triple treatment as classical Priestly (P) material, though Eryl Davies (*Numbers: Based on the Revised Standard Version*, 1995) and Tamara Cohn Eskenazi have argued it is an independent legal tradition P absorbed rather than composed.

The device of *refusal-to-give* is rare in Joshua. Through chapters 13–22 Joshua distributes, approves, confirms. Here alone he declines the requested gift — more territory in developed form — and instead gives a harder version: forest to clear and chariots to fight. It mirrors a pattern in ancient Near Eastern royal grants where a vassal asks for more and the overlord responds with a challenge rather than a bigger estate.

Textually, the LXX Joshua of chapter 17 is somewhat shorter than the MT. Emanuel Tov (*The Text-Critical Use of the Septuagint in Biblical Research*) has long argued that LXX Joshua preserves an earlier Hebrew Vorlage, shorter in the allotment material specifically. For Joshua 17 the differences are minor but real: the Greek omits some of the city-list specificity in vv.7–10 and smooths the Ephraim-Manasseh boundary description. No variant alters theology, but the expansion pattern in MT suggests later scribal clarification.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Manasseh's inheritance sits inside the canonical arc of Joseph's double portion. Jacob's blessing in Genesis 48 grants Joseph a double share by adopting Ephraim and Manasseh as full tribes — a narrative device that preserves the twelve-tribe system after Levi is separated out for priestly service. Joshua 16–17 cashes this check. The chapter's closing line — Joseph will receive more than one allotment — is Genesis 48 vindicated.

**Jewish trajectory.** Second Temple period texts (Josephus, *Antiquities* 5.1.22) summarize the Manasseh allotment without special commentary but emphasize the legal continuity from Moses through Joshua. The rabbis treated Joshua 17 as a major halakhic deposit. The daughters of Zelophehad — *Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah, Tirzah* (מַחְלָה נֹעָה חׇגְלָה מִלְכָּה תִּרְצָה) — generate extensive treatment in Baba Batra 116b–119b of the Babylonian Talmud. The rabbis examine the order of the names (it differs between Numbers 26, 27, and 36), derive from it the principle that wisdom and righteousness merit name-prominence, and debate whether the daughters were married to clansmen or cousins. Sifre Bamidbar 133 praises them as *ḥakhamot* (wise) and *ṣadkaniyot* (righteous). The Talmudic ruling grounds the entire Jewish law of daughter-inheritance in cases of sonless fathers — still operative in contemporary rabbinic jurisprudence.

Medieval commentary focuses on two cruxes. Rashi (11th c.) on 17:15 reads Joshua's "go clear the forest" as rebuke: you are many, but you are complaining; go work. Ibn Ezra (12th c.) treats the chariots as a test of faith rather than a real military obstacle — the Canaanites' strength being secondary to Israel's resolve. Ramban (Nachmanides, 13th c.) connects v.13's "did not drive them out" to the entire theological problem of Judges, arguing that Israel's failure was not lack of power but lack of will. Radak (David Kimhi, 13th c.) explains the geographic complexities of the Ephraim-Manasseh boundary with characteristic lexical precision. Abarbanel (15th c.), writing after the expulsion from Spain, reads the Joseph complaint as a pattern of Jewish dispossession: the tribe that should have the most gets less, and has to struggle to make a home.

Modern Jewish scholarship treats the chapter as a window into premonarchic tribal geography. Martin Buber's tribal-league reading emphasized covenant over conquest. Jon Levenson (*Sinai and Zion*, 1985) situates the allotment in the logic of land-gift theology. Jacob Milgrom's priestly-law work illuminated the Zelophehad precedent. James Kugel (*How to Read the Bible*, 2007) notes the doubled narrative of Joshua's refusal as a scribal craft. Moshe Weinfeld (*The Promise of the Land*, 1993) read the partial-conquest notices as deliberate historiographic honesty by the Deuteronomist. Nahum Sarna, in his JPS Torah Commentaries, traced the legal development across Numbers and Joshua. Avivah Zornberg has written on the daughters as embodying *davar*, a word that seeks its legal home. Baruch Halpern (*The Emergence of Israel in Canaan*, 1983) read the partial-conquest material as proof that Israel's origin was largely indigenous, a highland differentiation from Canaanite society rather than invasion. Rachel Havrelock's *River Jordan* (2011) treats the boundary rhetoric as ideological geography — who gets the river, who gets the valley — and reads Joshua 17's partial conquest as a concession that boundaries are never as clean as texts pretend.

**Christian trajectory.** The Patristic reading of Joshua 17 — and indeed of the whole book — was dominated by Origen's *Homilies on Joshua* (early third century). Origen treats the forest Joseph must clear as the soul's own terrain: the timber is passion, the clearing is ascetic labor, the iron chariots are the demonic powers that resist the spirit's advance into the full inheritance Christ has promised. "If the hill country is too narrow for you, go up and clear the forest" becomes a command to monastic struggle. Jerome, in his Letter 108 to Eustochium, refers to the passage for a similar pastoral point. Augustine treats the partial conquest typologically as the church in the *saeculum* — possessing the land by title but not yet in full fact, awaiting consummation. Chrysostom preaches the daughters of Zelophehad as figures of Gentile inclusion — those traditionally outside inheritance now given it by divine decree. Theodoret of Cyrus offers the most literal-historical reading of the patristic group, commenting on the geographic specifics with unusual restraint.

Medieval Christian commentary is thin on Joshua overall. Aquinas cites the chapter mostly in discussions of just war and property rights (*Summa* II-II, q.57). The *Glossa Ordinaria* follows Origen's allegory without innovating. Reformation readers revived literal-historical reading. Luther preached Joshua 17 with the emphasis on vocation — the forest one must clear is the work before one, not a mystical stage. Calvin, in his *Commentaries on Joshua* (1564), reads the chariots of iron as a test of the tribes' faith and their failure to drive out the Canaanites as rebuke, not excuse. Menno Simons and the Anabaptist tradition largely avoided Joshua's conquest theology, finding in it the shape of what they rejected: state-sanctioned violence.

Modern critical scholarship is thick on this chapter. Noth's *Das Buch Josua* (1938) treated the allotment lists as archival deposits overlaid with Deuteronomistic frame. Gerhard von Rad (*Old Testament Theology*, 1957) read the entire Hexateuch as a theology of promise and land. Frank Moore Cross located Joshua 17 inside the Dtr1/Dtr2 schema. Brevard Childs (*Introduction to the Old Testament as Scripture*, 1979) emphasized the canonical coherence of the partial-conquest material with the theology of ongoing obligation. Richard Nelson (*Joshua: A Commentary*, 1997) reads vv.14–18 as a story of lazy complaint rebuked. Thomas Dozeman (*Joshua 13–21*, 2013) gives the chapter careful historical-geographic treatment. Trent Butler (*Joshua*, WBC, 1983) focuses on rhetorical structure. Kallai, Aharoni (*The Land of the Bible*, 1979), Rainey, and Na'aman form the core geographical-historical school, with detailed arguments about which lists reflect which administrative periods.

Contemporary readings include Walter Brueggemann's theology of the land (*The Land*, 1977 and 2002), which reads Joshua's partial conquest as a theology of incompleteness — promise held in the tension between gift and claim. Christopher Wright (*The Mission of God*, 2006) frames the whole allotment as a paradigm of socio-economic ethics. John Goldingay's *Old Testament Theology* treats the iron chariots as the chapter's quiet subversion of conquest rhetoric. Richard Hess (*Joshua*, TOTC, 1996), Gordon Wenham, and David Howard offer evangelical-critical readings that retain historical trust. Terence Fretheim emphasizes divine vulnerability to human failure. Ellen Davis (*Scripture, Culture, and Agriculture*, 2009) notes the ecological command in v.15 — clear the forest — and wonders whether Joshua's answer encodes a deep ambivalence about deforestation as colonization. L. Daniel Hawk (*Joshua in 3-D*, 2010) reads the chapter with attention to its ideological grammar: the tribes are negotiating incomplete sovereignty within an inherited imperial idiom. Lori Rowlett's earlier *Joshua and the Rhetoric of Violence* (1996) reads the ideology of conquest as a tool for internal Israelite discipline. The chapter's partial-conquest material has particular acuity: v.13's forced labor is not triumph but the older violence the text quietly legitimates — a point sharpened by Brueggemann's and Christopher Wright's flagging of the conquest paradigm's troubling modern political afterlife.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension one: Is inheritance gift or work?** Verses 14–18 cut to this directly. The tribes ask for a larger gift. Joshua answers with a commission to labor. Three positions.

*Position A: Pure gift.* The land is given; human work merely receives. Origen, Luther in certain sermons, and a strand of Reformed theology following Calvin on grace read the *goral* at Shiloh as decisive. The forest still stands at v.15, but it stands as a gift whose receiving will look like axe-work.

*Position B: Pure work.* Ideology-critical readings (Rowlett, Hawk) find in v.15 the honest theological fact: you want it, go take it. Joshua's refusal to give a pre-cleared homeland exposes the conquest rhetoric as labor masquerading as grace.

*Position C (the least intuitive; steelman).* Inheritance is constitutively gift-as-vocation. This view — visible in Brueggemann, developed in Wright, and carried in the rabbinic reading of *naḥălâ* — holds that to inherit the land is to become responsible for it. A pre-cleared homeland would not be inheritance; it would be spoil. The forest is the gift precisely because it is uncleared: the gift and the vocation are one act. This is harder to hold than A or B because it refuses the conventional separation between receiving and doing. But the text behaves as though this is what it means: the tribes receive their inheritance in v.17 ("you shall not have one allotment only") and are commanded in v.18 to clear and possess it. The two sentences are the same event.

Where this leaves us: the chapter dismantles a dualism modern readers find natural. You either earn or you are given. Joshua 17 will not let that stand. You are given what you must make. You make what you are given.

**Tension two: Why does partial conquest follow total promise?** V.12 is one of the most honest verses in Joshua: they could not take the cities. Three positions.

*Position A: Theological failure.* The tribes lacked faith. The Canaanites stayed because Israel would not trust the promise that had already defeated Jericho. Ramban, Calvin, and most evangelical commentators read this way. It places moral responsibility squarely on Israel.

*Position B: Narrative realism.* The Deuteronomistic historian is reporting what actually happened, and the theology is built around the facts. Israel did not conquer the plain, so the text says so. Noth, Cross, Halpern, and most critical scholarship read this way. The partial conquest is not failure but the real history the theology has to reckon with.

*Position C (least intuitive; steelman).* Partial conquest is theologically correct. God wills the incompleteness. The land is given as promise held in tension with fact, and a total conquest would foreclose the covenantal relation that depends on ongoing obligation. Brueggemann, Fretheim, and Childs edge toward this reading — though none commits fully. The logic: a completely possessed land would make the covenant redundant. The unpossessed cities keep Israel in need of God. The iron chariots in the plain are not Israel's failure; they are the theological condition under which inheritance remains live. This is the hardest position to hold because it seems to sanctify failure. But the canonical shape — Joshua's incomplete conquest leading into Judges' deep incompleteness leading to monarchy's partial fulfillment — suggests the text is modeling something structural about how God's gifts come: always already incomplete, always still being possessed.

Where this leaves us: the chapter refuses to tell a clean conquest story and refuses to call the incompleteness sin alone. Both readings have textual warrant; the text may want both held at once. The partial conquest is both indictment and condition.

Where does this leave us finally: Joshua's command to the Joseph tribes — clear the forest and drive out the chariots — reads either as a discouraging demotion ("you wanted a bigger gift, you got a harder job") or as the chapter's deepest grace ("you wanted a smaller life, you were given a larger vocation"). The text does not say which. Perhaps the question the chapter most wants to ask us is this: would you recognize the gift you have been given if it came to you as forest and chariots rather than as cleared plain?

## The Thread

Numbers 27:1–11 / Numbers 36 — The Zelophehad precedent. Joshua 17 is the executing legal act for the Torah's most radical inheritance innovation.

Judges 1:27–28 — The doublet of Josh 17:11–13. Judges names the same cities Manasseh could not take and repeats the forced-labor note. The reader is meant to see the partial conquest across two books, two genres, same fact.

1 Kings 9:20–21 — Solomon's forced labor over the remaining Canaanite populations. The *mas* of Joshua 17 becomes the *mas* of the Solomonic state — the chapter's small admission scaled into imperial policy.

Ephesians 6:12 — Origen's allegorical chariots: "we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities and powers." Whether or not one follows the allegorical reading, the Pauline text reads like the New Testament's version of 17:16 — the enemy is armored, the ground is the spirit, the call is to possess.
