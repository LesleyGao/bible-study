# Revelation 4: The Counter-Court Behind the World

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

After seven letters addressed to specific Asian congregations, the text breaks open. "After this I looked, and behold, a door standing open in heaven" (4:1). The literary pivot is sharp; the historical pivot may be sharper. Chapters 4-5 reframe everything that came before by showing readers the actual cosmic court whose judgments ground the diagnostic letters of chapters 2-3. To grasp what John believed he was doing, one has to understand what the available alternative throne rooms looked like in late-first-century Asia Minor.

The Flavian dynasty had inherited and intensified the imperial cult apparatus that the Julio-Claudians built. Domitian (81-96 AD) demanded the form of address *dominus et deus noster* — "our lord and god" — according to Suetonius (*Domitian* 13.2). The *Acta Senatus* and inscriptional evidence from Pompeii and elsewhere corroborate this title's circulation, though scholars now debate whether the address was as systematically demanded as Suetonius suggests; Brian Jones's *The Emperor Domitian* (1992) and Pat Southern's *Domitian: Tragic Tyrant* (1997) both argue the demand was real but episodic, while Steven Friesen's *Imperial Cults and the Apocalypse of John* (2001) documents the institutional thickness of imperial cult practice in Asia regardless of any individual emperor's policy. By 95 AD, when the late-date consensus (Mounce, Beale, Koester, Aune) places Revelation, the cities of the seven churches all participated in a vast network of imperial cult temples, festivals, processions, sacrifices, and oaths. Every local civic event, every guild banquet, every magistrate's inauguration involved acknowledgment of the emperor's divinity. To live in Asia was to live constantly in proximity to imperial worship.

The imperial throne room was the model. The emperor sat enthroned, ringed by senators in white togas, attended by lictors and bodyguards, presented with libations and acclamations. Provincial governors held their own miniature versions; the proconsul of Asia, headquartered in Pergamum and later in Ephesus, presided over a court that mimicked Rome's. Public ceremonies of imperial worship featured choral acclamations addressed to the seated emperor — "worthy are you" was an attested formula in imperial acclamations. Bauckham's *The Climax of Prophecy* (1993) develops this point at length: chapter 4 is structured as a deliberate counter-image to imperial court ceremony. The acclamation of 4:11 — "Worthy are you, our Lord and God, to receive glory and honor and power" — directly inverts the imperial cult acclamation. The emperor calls himself *dominus et deus*; the heavenly liturgy answers, no, only the One on the actual throne is. Bauckham, Friesen, Adela Yarbro Collins (*Crisis and Catharsis*, 1984), and Craig Koester (*Anchor Yale Bible*, 2014) all read chapter 4 as political theology pressed into liturgical form.

The material culture of imperial throne rooms is preserved in surviving sculptural and architectural evidence. The Sebasteion at Aphrodisias, excavated by Kenan Erim, preserves dozens of relief panels of Julio-Claudian emperors enthroned, attended by personifications of conquered peoples. The Domitianic temple of the Sebastoi at Ephesus housed a colossal cult statue (fragments survive in the Ephesus Archaeological Museum); the temple of Augustus and Roma at Pergamum, established 29 BC as the first provincial imperial cult temple in Asia, set the architectural template. The famous Prima Porta statue of Augustus, now in the Vatican, and the sculptural program of the Ara Pacis Augustae in Rome, both establish the iconographic vocabulary of imperial enthronement that Revelation contests.

John identifies the location of his vision as a heavenly throne room reached through "a door standing open in heaven" (4:1). The image is not novel. The throne-vision tradition stretches back through 1 Enoch 14, the *Apocalypse of Abraham* 17-18, and ultimately to Isaiah 6 and Ezekiel 1. Christopher Rowland's *The Open Heaven* (1982) traces the genealogy of the heavenly-throne-room genre across Second Temple Jewish apocalyptic literature. Loren Stuckenbruck's work on 1 Enoch's heavenly throne visions (especially the Book of the Watchers, chapters 14-16) demonstrates how the imagery had become a stable feature of apocalyptic vocabulary by John's time. What John adds is the specifically Christological framing — though chapter 4 reserves the entrance of the Lamb for chapter 5, the theological architecture of the entire vision is built around the slain-and-victorious figure.

**Manuscript evidence.** P47 (3rd century, Chester Beatty papyri) preserves portions of chapters 9-17, not chapter 4; the primary witnesses for chapter 4 are Sinaiticus (4th century, complete), Alexandrinus (5th century, generally preferred for Revelation per Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary*), and Codex Ephraemi Rescriptus (5th century, partial). The text of chapter 4 is relatively stable; significant variants are minor. The doxology of 4:11 is preserved in essentially the same form across all major witnesses.

**Greek terms.** *Thronos* (θρόνος, "throne," 4:2 et passim) — the word appears 47 times in Revelation, more than in all other New Testament books combined; thrones are the dominant political-theological image of the book. *Pantokratōr* (παντοκράτωρ, "Almighty," 4:8) — the Septuagint's translation of *YHWH Sabaoth* and *El Shaddai*, importing Hebrew Bible divine titles into a Greek-speaking imperial context. *Hagios hagios hagios* (ἅγιος ἅγιος ἅγιος, "holy, holy, holy," 4:8) — the Trisagion, drawn directly from Isaiah 6:3 and entering Christian liturgy from this verse onward. *Axios* (ἄξιος, "worthy," 4:11) — the formal acclamation of imperial cult and Hellenistic court ceremony, deliberately redirected here. *Zōa* (ζῷα, "living creatures," 4:6) — the same Greek word translates Ezekiel's *hayyot* in the Septuagint, marking the deliberate Ezekielian intertext.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 4 inaugurates the second major movement of Revelation, after the seven letters. Structurally, the throne-room of chapters 4-5 functions as the cosmic-liturgical foundation from which the seals (chapters 6-8), trumpets (chapters 8-11), and bowls (chapters 15-16) will be unleashed. Adela Yarbro Collins's *Crisis and Catharsis* and Bauckham's *Climax of Prophecy* both stress that chapters 4-5 must be read together as a single visionary unit; chapter 4 establishes the throne, chapter 5 introduces the slain Lamb who alone can open the sealed scroll. The chapter division (introduced in the 13th century by Stephen Langton) artificially separates what John composed as a continuous diptych.

The literary debt to Ezekiel 1 and Isaiah 6 is total. From Ezekiel 1: the throne, the four living creatures (Ezekiel's *hayyot*, with four faces each rather than four creatures with one face each — a meaningful adjustment), the wings, the eyes covering the wings (Ezekiel 10:12), the rainbow (Ezekiel 1:28), the crystal/firmament beneath the throne (Ezekiel 1:22). From Isaiah 6: the seraphim with six wings (Isaiah 6:2), the *hagios hagios hagios* acclamation (Isaiah 6:3), the smoke and the foundations shaking (Isaiah 6:4). G. K. Beale's *The Use of the Old Testament in the Book of Revelation* (1998) provides the most thorough mapping. Bauckham counts roughly 500 Hebrew Bible allusions across Revelation; chapter 4 alone contains the densest concentration in the book.

The four living creatures (4:6-8) merge Ezekiel's four-faced creatures with Isaiah's six-winged seraphim. Ezekiel's creatures had four faces each; John's have one face each — lion, ox, human, eagle — distributed among four creatures. The reorganization preserves the symbolism while making the imagery more visually tractable. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.11.8) was the first to associate the four creatures with the four evangelists: the lion with Mark (or sometimes Matthew, in different patristic schemes), the ox with Luke, the human with Matthew (or Mark), the eagle with John. The association is a creative second-century reading, not the seer's original intent. Yarbro Collins, Aune, and Beale all treat the four creatures as representing the totality of created life under God's throne — wild beasts (lion), domesticated animals (ox), humanity (human), and birds (eagle) — a literary census of creation rendered as living worship.

The twenty-four elders (4:4) are the chapter's most contested feature, and the contestation is ancient. Several main lines:
- Angelic beings of high rank, possibly drawn from Jewish apocalyptic traditions about the angelic priesthood (Aune's preferred reading, citing parallels in 1 Enoch and Qumran's *Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice*);
- Representatives of redeemed humanity — twelve patriarchs of Israel plus twelve apostles, totaling twenty-four (a patristic and Reformed tradition; Victorinus, Bede, and many medieval commentators);
- The twenty-four priestly courses of 1 Chronicles 24, who served by rotation in the temple — making the heavenly elders the eternal priestly order (a reading favored by Beale, Mounce, and Bauckham);
- Astrological — the twenty-four hours of day and night, or the twenty-four star-gods of Babylonian-Egyptian astronomical tradition (a minority reading developed by some history-of-religions scholars);
- A combination of multiple streams (Koester, who favors the priestly-courses reading while acknowledging multiple resonances).

The strongest case is for the priestly-courses reading: 1 Chronicles 24 establishes twenty-four priestly courses for temple service, and the elders' "white garments" (4:4) match priestly vestments while their "golden crowns" suggest royal-priestly office (cf. Exodus 19:6, "a kingdom of priests"). The conqueror-promises of chapters 2-3 — particularly the throne-sharing promise of 3:21 — connect the elders to redeemed humanity as well, but the priestly-courses background gives the imagery its primary weight.

The Trisagion (4:8) is the chapter's liturgical centerpiece. Drawn from Isaiah 6:3 (the seraphim's *qadosh qadosh qadosh*), it entered Christian liturgical practice from Revelation 4 onward — appearing in the Sanctus of the Western Mass, the Trisagion hymn of the Eastern Divine Liturgy, and innumerable hymnic compositions. Bauckham notes that John's version expands Isaiah 6:3's "the whole earth is full of his glory" into "Lord God Almighty, who was and is and is to come" — replacing earthly extent with temporal-eternal reach.

Genre-wise, chapter 4 displays the classical heavenly-throne-room *Gattung* of Jewish apocalyptic. John J. Collins's *The Apocalyptic Imagination* identifies the heavenly journey + throne vision as a stable subgenre with attested examples in 1 Enoch (the Book of the Watchers, the Similitudes), the *Apocalypse of Abraham*, the *Testament of Levi*, and 4Q400-407 (*Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice* from Qumran). Loren Stuckenbruck's work on 1 Enoch and Stuckenbruck's broader Second Temple apocalyptic studies illuminate how this genre had become formulaic by the late first century.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Chapter 4 has been the site of more agreement than most chapters of Revelation, in part because its liturgical-doxological character is harder to weaponize for chronological-prophetic schemes than later chapters.

The patristic period read chapter 4 primarily as throne-vision and as eschatological-liturgical prefiguration. Justin Martyr's *Dialogue with Trypho* invokes throne-room imagery without extensive exegesis of Revelation 4 specifically. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.11.8) introduces the four-evangelists association with the four creatures — a reading that became canonical in Christian iconography and persists today in the *tetramorph* of Romanesque and Byzantine apse-mosaics. Hippolytus, Tertullian, and Origen engaged the chapter through allegorical and typological frames. Victorinus of Pettau's commentary preserves an early reading of the twenty-four elders as the patriarchs-plus-apostles. Augustine (*City of God* 20) read the chapter through his recapitulation theory, placing the throne-vision in the eternal present rather than in any particular eschatological sequence.

The Eastern Orthodox tradition read chapter 4 as the heavenly liturgy that grounds and prefigures every earthly Eucharist. Andrew of Caesarea's 6th-century commentary established this reading definitively for the Greek East. The Divine Liturgy of John Chrysostom and the Liturgy of St. Basil both incorporate liturgical formulas drawn from chapter 4 — the *axios*-acclamation, the Trisagion, the doxology to the One who is and was and is to come. Liturgically, chapter 4 functions in Orthodox worship as the heavenly pattern of which the earthly Eucharistic assembly is a participating image.

The medieval Western tradition continued the patristic-Augustinian reading. Bernard of Clairvaux read chapter 4 through Christological-mystical lenses; Aquinas treated it in *Summa Theologiae* as a foundation for the doctrine of the beatific vision. Joachim of Fiore's three-ages historiography integrated chapter 4 into his tripartite scheme without disturbing its essentially eschatological-liturgical character.

The Reformation engaged chapter 4 through the doctrine of God's sovereignty. Luther, despite his ambivalence about Revelation as a whole, found the chapter's high view of God's throne congenial; he never preached at length on it but referenced it in his catechetical writings. Calvin, who never wrote a Revelation commentary, treated the throne-room imagery in his sermons on Isaiah 6 and Ezekiel 1. The Reformed tradition's emphasis on God's transcendent sovereignty drew on chapter 4's imagery extensively. Charles Hodge, B. B. Warfield, and the Princeton tradition treated the chapter as Scripture's clearest single statement of divine sovereignty.

The 19th-century rise of dispensationalism integrated chapter 4 into its eschatological scheme in a specific way: the "come up here" command of 4:1 was read as the rapture of the church, with chapters 4-19 then describing the post-rapture tribulation period (Darby, Scofield, Walvoord, Pentecost). On this reading, the absence of the word "church" between chapters 4 and 19 is taken as evidence that the church has been raptured. Steelmanning the dispensational reading: it preserves a strong futurist orientation and refuses to flatten the apocalyptic genre into mere allegory; it takes seriously the temporal markers of the text. The weakness is that "come up here" is addressed specifically to John, not to the church; the reading imports a 19th-century theological framework into the text.

The 20th-century critical tradition — R. H. Charles's ICC commentary (1920), G. B. Caird (1966), G. R. Beasley-Murray (NCB), John Sweet, Richard Bauckham (*The Theology of the Book of Revelation*, *The Climax of Prophecy*), David Aune (Word Biblical Commentary), Craig Koester (Anchor Yale 2014), G. K. Beale (NIGTC), Robert Mounce (NICNT), Grant Osborne (BECNT), Eugene Boring (Interpretation), Christopher Rowland (*The Open Heaven*), Adela Yarbro Collins (*Crisis and Catharsis*) — has converged on reading chapter 4 as both heavenly-liturgical vision and political-theological counter-statement to imperial throne-room ideology. Bauckham's reading of chapter 4 as the alternative court behind the Caesar's court has become near-consensus.

The mainstream scholarly reading of Revelation as anti-imperial witness — worked out at length by Bauckham (*The Theology of the Book of Revelation*, *The Climax of Prophecy*), Caird, Beale, Koester, Osborne, Ian Paul, and Aune — reads chapter 4 as direct political theology pressed into liturgical form. The acclamation of 4:11, addressed to the One on the heavenly throne, deliberately inverts the *dominus et deus* acclamations of the imperial cult; Caesar's claim has always been a counterfeit of the actual cosmic court. Within this broad consensus, Allan Boesak's *Comfort and Protest* (1987) and Brian Blount (*Revelation*, NTL, 2009) have pressed the chapter's contemporary political edge — reading 4:11 against apartheid-era acclamations of the South African state and against every empire that demands worship — without, on Bauckham's, Beale's, and Koester's account, severing that political force from the chapter's liturgical and doxological center.

N. T. Wright's *Revelation for Everyone* (2011) and *Surprised by Hope* (2008) read chapter 4 in keeping with his larger argument that Revelation is not a coded timetable of the end but a pastoral and political tract written to churches under Roman pressure. For Wright, the open door in heaven does not whisk the reader out of the world; it shows what is *already* true behind the world — that the Creator God, not Caesar, sits on the throne, and that the church's worship is the place where that truth becomes audible. The vision's purpose, on Wright's reading, is to retrain the imagination of believers tempted to think Rome has the last word: it gives them, in advance, the song they will need in chapter 5 and the courage they will need in chapters 6–18.

**Jewish Scholarly Engagement.** The dependence of chapter 4 on Ezekiel 1, Isaiah 6, Daniel 7, 1 Enoch 14, and the *Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice* makes this chapter the densest concentration in the New Testament of Second Temple Jewish apocalyptic-mystical material. Adela Yarbro Collins's social-historical work has illuminated how the chapter participates in a pre-rabbinic mystical tradition (often called *Merkavah* mysticism, though that term technically belongs to a slightly later phase) of throne-vision contemplation. Loren Stuckenbruck has extensively mapped the 1 Enoch parallels, particularly the Book of the Watchers' throne vision in chapter 14. John J. Collins's *The Apocalyptic Imagination* established the genre framework. Daniel Boyarin (*The Jewish Gospels*, 2012; *Border Lines*, 2004) has argued that Revelation's throne-room imagery is best understood as continuous with first-century Jewish apocalyptic-mystical tradition rather than as a Christian innovation; the Seer of Patmos is a Jewish mystical-prophetic figure whose vision happens to feature the slain Messiah. The connection between Revelation 4 and the *Hekhalot* literature of later Jewish mysticism, while not direct, illuminates how throne-vision contemplation persisted as a Jewish religious mode well into the rabbinic period.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** The sharpest divergence: dispensational futurism reads 4:1 as the rapture of the church; nearly every other reading rejects this. The sharpest convergence: across Catholic, Orthodox, Reformed, Lutheran, dispensational, critical, and politically engaged readings, chapter 4 is recognized as a foundational Christian articulation of God's sovereignty over history. The third convergence: the chapter's deep Jewish apocalyptic-mystical roots are now uncontested.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the seer believe God was doing? In chapter 4, the answer is structural: behind every imperial throne, behind every provincial governor's court, behind every guild banquet's libation to the emperor's *genius*, there is an actual cosmic court whose throne is occupied. The vision does not predict the future; it reveals the present. The argument is not that Caesar will eventually fall but that Caesar's claims have always been false. The acclamations of 4:11 are not aspirational. They are the actual liturgy of the universe.

**Tension One: The relation of heavenly liturgy to earthly worship.** Is chapter 4 prefigurative (earthly worship anticipates the heavenly), participatory (earthly worship enters the heavenly), or simply representational (earthly worship images the heavenly)?

- **Catholic**: The CCC (paragraphs 1090, 1136-1139) reads the heavenly liturgy as the eternal pattern of which earthly liturgy is a participating image. The Mass is understood as participation in the one heavenly liturgy. *Sacrosanctum Concilium* (Vatican II) emphasizes this participatory understanding. Bossuet and the partial-preterist tradition fit comfortably with this reading; the throne is occupied now and forever, regardless of historical contingencies.

- **Reformed**: The Reformed tradition has historically emphasized the prefigurative reading — earthly worship anticipates the heavenly. Calvin's eucharistic theology (real spiritual presence through the lifting up of the heart) drew on chapter 4 imagery. Beale and Vern Poythress read the chapter idealistically; the heavenly throne-room is the eternal-present reality that grounds every act of true worship.

- **Lutheran**: Combines elements of both. Luther's eucharistic theology (the *real presence in, with, and under* the bread and wine) drew on the cosmic-Christological scope of the heavenly throne, while the Lutheran emphasis on faith over works fit the doxological character of chapter 4.

- **Eastern Orthodox**: The participatory reading is foundational. Every Divine Liturgy is participation in the one heavenly liturgy that chapter 4 describes. The cherubic hymn, the Trisagion, the *axios*-acclamations are all liturgical realities the earthly church enters through worship. Andrew of Caesarea's commentary establishes this reading; the entire Orthodox liturgical tradition develops it.

- **Dispensationalist**: The heavenly throne-room is a real future location to which the church will be raptured. The participation is eschatological-future rather than eternal-present.

- **Critical/academic**: The heavenly throne-room is a literary-rhetorical construct designed to relativize earthly imperial claims. Aune treats it as anti-imperial protest; Bauckham treats it as cosmic political theology; Koester treats it as both.

- **Political reading**: The heavenly throne-room is solidarity literature. Read from under apartheid (Boesak) or any imperial order, the point stands: there is a court above the state's court, and that court is on the side of the persecuted.

The least intuitive position to steelman is the dispensational futurist reading. Its strength: it preserves a strong sense that the heavenly throne-room is not merely metaphorical but ontologically real, and it refuses to allegorize cosmic-political imagery into purely interior pieties. Its weakness: it treats 4:1's "come up here" (addressed to John as a singular vision-recipient) as if it were addressed to the whole church, importing a complex eschatological scheme that the chapter itself does not develop.

**Where this leaves us:** Chapter 4 cannot be reduced to either past, present, or future. The throne is occupied now; it has always been occupied; it will continue to be occupied through whatever crises lie ahead. The pastoral function of the vision is to relocate the reader's sense of where reality is located — not in Caesar's palace, not in Domitian's *dominus et deus* acclamations, but in the throne behind the door that opens in heaven.

**Tension Two: The political-theological force of "Worthy are you."** The acclamation of 4:11 is the cosmic answer to imperial cult. But how directly should that polemic be read, and what does it imply for Christian engagement with political authority?

- **Catholic**: The CCC (paragraphs 1897-1917) acknowledges legitimate political authority while subordinating it to divine sovereignty. Augustine's *City of God* provides the classical framework — earthly authority is real but penultimate. Bossuet's defense of divine right of kings drew on chapter 4 imagery; later Catholic political theology (Jacques Maritain, John Courtney Murray) developed more democratic readings. The CCC affirms that "no human authority can claim to be ultimate" (cf. paragraphs 1903, 1930).

- **Reformed**: The Reformed tradition has historically been the most politically activist on this point. The "lesser magistrates" doctrine (Calvin, John Knox, Theodore Beza, the Huguenot political theorists) drew on chapter 4 to authorize resistance to tyranny. Abraham Kuyper's "every square inch" vision read chapter 4's cosmic kingship as the warrant for comprehensive Christian engagement with culture and politics.

- **Lutheran**: The two-kingdoms framework distinguishes spiritual from temporal authority while subordinating both to God's sovereignty. Luther's reading of Romans 13 paired with chapter 4 produced a complex political theology that has had divergent histories — German Lutheran quietism in some periods, the Confessing Church's resistance to Nazism in the 1930s.

- **Dispensationalist**: Treats present political authority as an interim arrangement; the consummated kingship of Christ awaits the future. The political implication is often quietist or culture-warrior depending on the school.

- **Eastern Orthodox**: The Byzantine tradition of *symphonia* — emperor and patriarch as complementary authorities under God — drew on chapter 4 imagery for centuries; the post-Byzantine Orthodox traditions have engaged the question variously. The Orthodox emphasis on liturgical doxology over political activism has often muted direct political readings.

- **Critical/academic**: Chapter 4 is read as anti-imperial protest literature; the implications for contemporary politics are treated as a separate hermeneutical question. Bauckham's *The Theology of the Book of Revelation* draws political-theological implications carefully.

- **Political reading**: Within the mainstream anti-imperial scholarship (Bauckham, Caird, Beale, Koester, Aune), commentators such as Boesak and Brian Blount have read chapter 4 as a direct mandate for resistance to imperial powers — the acclamation of 4:11 incompatible with acclamations to apartheid, to military regimes, to economic empires.

The least intuitive position to steelman is the Eastern Orthodox quietist tradition. Its strength: it preserves the doxological-liturgical center of chapter 4 against politicization in either conservative or radical-activist directions; it insists that worship is itself the most radical political act, more radical than activism. Its weakness: it can underwrite passive accommodation to oppressive political orders.

**Where this leaves us:** The acclamation of 4:11 cannot be neutral toward political authority. It claims that *only* God is worthy of the acclamation Caesar demanded. The implications for any given political moment require discernment, but the text itself refuses any reading in which Caesar and God can be jointly acclaimed.

**One unresolved question.** If the heavenly liturgy never ceases — "day and night they never cease to say" (4:8) — and if earthly worship is participation in that eternal liturgy, what happens to the eternal liturgy when the earthly church falls silent, fragments, or compromises? Or does the heavenly liturgy continue regardless, and is that continuance a comfort or an indictment?

## The Thread

**Ezekiel 1 and 10**: The throne-chariot vision, the four living creatures, the wheels full of eyes, the rainbow around the throne — Ezekiel's vision is the deep structural source of chapter 4's imagery. John reframes the prophet's exilic-Babylonian throne vision as the eternal-cosmic throne behind Roman imperial Asia.

**Isaiah 6:1-4**: The seraphim's Trisagion, the throne, the smoke, the foundations shaking — Isaiah's commission vision supplies the Trisagion that becomes Christian liturgy through chapter 4. John extends Isaiah's "the whole earth is full of his glory" into "Lord God Almighty, who was and is and is to come" — replacing earthly extent with temporal-eternal reach.

**Daniel 7:9-10**: The Ancient of Days enthroned, the thousand thousands serving him, the books opened — Daniel's throne vision contributes the eschatological-judicial dimension to chapter 4's liturgical-doxological scene. The conjunction prepares for chapter 5's slain Lamb.

**Revelation 21:3-5** (later in reading order): "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man" — the throne that opens in heaven in chapter 4 finally descends to earth in chapter 21. The diptych of chapters 4-5 is the cosmic prelude to which chapters 21-22 are the final consummation; the same throne, now relocated.
