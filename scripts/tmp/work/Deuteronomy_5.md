# Deuteronomy 5: Not with Our Fathers But with Us

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Deuteronomy 5 opens Moses's second address (4:44–28:68) with the repetition — with significant variations — of the Decalogue originally given in Exodus 20. The setting is Moab, forty years after the original Horeb theophany, with the entire exodus generation dead and the children now standing at the threshold of the land. The chapter's rhetorical move (v. 3) — "Not with our fathers did the LORD make this covenant, but with us, who are all of us here alive today" — is one of the most theologically audacious claims in the Torah: a covenantal compression of generations.

The chapter's authorship and dating problems are the same ones that dominate the book. W.M.L. de Wette (1805) identified Deuteronomy with Josiah's reform scroll (622 BC). Wellhausen treated chapter 5 as part of the seventh-century Deuteronomic composition. Moshe Weinfeld (*Deuteronomy 1–11* AB, 1991; *Deuteronomic School*) demonstrated close parallels between Deuteronomy's treaty structure and neo-Assyrian vassal treaties, particularly Esarhaddon's VTE (672 BC). Against this, Kenneth Kitchen, Peter Craigie (NICOT 1976), and Daniel Block (NIVAC 2012) emphasize the Hittite second-millennium treaty form as a better structural fit for Deuteronomy as a whole. Jeffrey Tigay (JPS 1996) and Richard Nelson (OTL 2002) take middle positions.

The Decalogue itself — attested in both Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5 — almost certainly predates whatever composition of either book produced the forms we have. Frank Moore Cross, David Noel Freedman, and more recently Benjamin Sommer have argued that the "short" Decalogue stands apart as the oldest legal material in the Pentateuch. The two versions differ in the Sabbath rationale (Ex 20:11 cites creation; Deut 5:15 cites Exodus liberation), in the Sabbath's explicit inclusion of slaves (Deut 5:14 expanded), in the covet sequence (Deut 5:21 lists "wife" first, then "house"; Ex 20:17 reverses), and in the opening verb (Deut: "observe" *šāmôr*; Ex: "remember" *zākôr*). These variations are not textual corruptions; they are two received editions of the foundational law.

The Nash Papyrus — a single leaf of papyrus acquired by W. L. Nash in 1902 and now housed in the Cambridge University Library — is the most important extra-biblical witness to the Decalogue. Dated by Albright and others to approximately 150–100 BC, it contains a Hebrew text of the Ten Commandments followed by the Shema. The Decalogue on Nash is a harmonization of Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5, suggesting that by the late Second Temple period both versions were being used liturgically together. This supports rabbinic traditions (*Mekhilta*; *Shavuot* 20b) that at Sinai God spoke *zākôr* and *šāmôr* simultaneously — a theological way of reading the two texts as one revelation.

Qumran witnesses extend the picture. 4QDeut-n (4Q41) is one of the most famous biblical manuscripts from the Dead Sea: a small collection of passages including an abbreviated Deuteronomy 5 Decalogue, ending at v. 6:1 — suggesting it was used as a liturgical excerpt, perhaps a tefillin or mezuzah-like text. 4QDeut-j and others preserve additional Decalogue fragments with some variants, showing that both major editions were copied and studied. The Tefillin and Mezuzah scrolls from Qumran include the Decalogue along with the Shema and other core texts; later rabbinic halakha excluded the Decalogue from tefillin, but the practice at Qumran shows it was once included.

Key Hebrew terms: *šāmôr* (שָׁמוֹר, v. 12), "observe/keep" — the Deuteronomy Sabbath verb, vs. Exodus's *zākôr*, "remember." *Pānîm bĕpānîm* (פָּנִים בְּפָנִים, v. 4), "face to face" — describing YHWH's speaking at Horeb, a striking anthropomorphism given chapter 4's "no form." *Dĕbārîm* (דְּבָרִים, v. 22), "words" — the ten *dĕbārîm*, "Ten Words," literally; the Hebrew for "Ten Commandments" is *ʿăśeret hadĕbārîm* (not *miṣvōt*), "the ten words" — which is also the Hebrew title of the book itself. *Ḥāmad* (חָמַד, v. 21), "covet/desire" — stronger in Deuteronomy than in Exodus, where both *ḥāmad* and *hitʾavvāh* are used. *ʾAmad* (עָמַד, v. 5, 31), "stood" — Moses as mediator standing between YHWH and the people, a priestly-prophetic position.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter's structure is straightforward: Moses's introductory framing (vv. 1–5); the Decalogue itself (vv. 6–21); the people's fear and the request for mediation (vv. 22–27); YHWH's response and the summary exhortation (vv. 28–33). The Decalogue is centrally placed and formally marked — "These words the LORD spoke... and he added no more" (v. 22) — establishing the Ten Words as a canonical unit.

The Decalogue itself belongs to a recognized ANE legal genre: apodictic law. Albrecht Alt's classic distinction (*Die Ursprünge des israelitischen Rechts*, 1934) between apodictic ("you shall not...") and casuistic ("if a man does X, then...") law has been nuanced by later scholars (Weinfeld, Westbrook) but remains broadly useful. Apodictic law is comparatively rare in ANE legal codes; most of Hammurabi, the Middle Assyrian Laws, and the Hittite laws are casuistic. The Decalogue's apodictic form — categorical, unconditional prohibitions — marks it as distinctive.

Deuteronomy's variations from Exodus 20 have generated enormous scholarly literature. Patrick Miller (Interpretation 1990), Jeffrey Tigay (JPS 1996), and Moshe Weinfeld treat these as theologically significant editorial choices. The Sabbath rationale shift — from creation to Exodus liberation — aligns the Sabbath with Deuteronomy's broader emancipation theology. The explicit inclusion of male and female servants (v. 14) and the "that your male servant and your female servant may rest as well as you" (v. 14) clause extend Sabbath rest humanely. The coveting sequence — wife first, then house — may reflect a theological prioritization of person over property, or (per Weinfeld) simply a different textual tradition.

The theological *šāmôr* vs. *zākôr* distinction became the subject of rich midrashic creativity. The Mekhilta, Sifre, and later Talmudic texts explain that "observe" and "remember" were spoken simultaneously by God in a single utterance impossible for human speech but recorded as two words. This is preserved in the liturgical hymn *Lekha Dodi* (sixteenth century, Solomon Alkabetz): *shamor ve-zakhor be-dibbur eḥad* — "Keep and Remember in a single word." The interpretive move honors both texts as revealed.

The people's request for mediation (vv. 23–27) is theologically weighty. They fear the direct voice of God ("if we hear the voice of the LORD our God any more, we shall die," v. 25) and request that Moses serve as mediator. YHWH approves the request — indeed, commends their fear ("they are right in all that they have spoken," v. 28). Dennis Olson (*Deuteronomy and the Death of Moses*, 1994) analyzes this as the chapter's hinge: the Decalogue is given directly by God, but everything that follows in the book is mediated by Moses at the people's request. The book's entire legal structure is grounded in this moment of requested mediation.

The literary architecture uses a classic Deuteronomic move: doubling back on a past event to make present meaning. "Not with our fathers" (v. 3) performs covenant across generations. The same device appears in 29:14–15 ("not with you alone do I make this covenant") and governs the book's catechetical vision. Miller, McConville, and Block all note how the chapter blurs temporal lines deliberately: past Horeb becomes present Moab becomes future covenant renewal.

Textual variants: beyond the Nash Papyrus and 4QDeut-n, significant variants appear in the LXX (which preserves elements from both Exodus and Deuteronomy traditions in different places) and in the Samaritan Pentateuch (which adds a tenth commandment about worship at Mount Gerizim, replacing what Jews and Christians count as the prohibition of coveting with a Samaritan-specific Gerizim command — the "Samaritan Tenth Commandment"). This is one of the sharpest textual divisions in the ancient biblical witnesses.

The Catholic/Lutheran and Reformed/Jewish enumerations of the Decalogue differ. Catholics and Lutherans (following Augustine) combine the prohibition of other gods and the image prohibition into a single First Commandment and split coveting into two commandments (wife; house-field-etc.). Reformed, Orthodox, and Jewish traditions (following Philo, Josephus, and early rabbinic practice) treat the image prohibition as a separate Second Commandment and combine the covet prohibitions. The numbering affects catechetical order, but both are faithful countings of ten.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

The Decalogue is foundational to Jewish life. The Nash Papyrus (second–first c. BC) shows it paired liturgically with the Shema in late Second Temple Judaism. Tefillin from Qumran included the Decalogue; Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.5.5) describes its recitation. The rabbinic tradition (*Mishnah Tamid* 5:1) records that the Decalogue was recited daily in the Temple alongside the Shema. However, the rabbis later removed the Decalogue from daily liturgy — *Yerushalmi Berakhot* 1:5 explains that Christian-Jewish controversies (the *minim*, likely early Christians, claimed only the Decalogue was divinely given) led to this liturgical decision. The Decalogue remains liturgically central only on Shavuot, the festival of revelation.

*Sifre Deuteronomy* on chapter 5 and *Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael* on Exodus 20 provide detailed tannaitic exegesis. *Devarim Rabbah* develops the covenantal-generational claim of v. 3 extensively. Rashi (eleventh c.) interprets *šāmôr* and *zākôr* as "one utterance" — the classic rabbinic harmonization of the two Decalogue versions. Ibn Ezra (twelfth c.) offers a more philological approach, noting the variations as genuine textual differences and developing his "secret" of the editorial hand in the Torah. Nachmanides (Ramban, thirteenth c.) harmonizes at a theological rather than textual level, arguing that both the creation rationale (Exodus) and the liberation rationale (Deuteronomy) are true and complementary reasons for Sabbath.

Maimonides in the *Mishneh Torah* (*Hilkhot Yesodei HaTorah*, *Hilkhot Avodat Kokhavim*) structures his legal code around the Decalogue's commands. His *Sefer HaMitzvot* counts the 613 commandments with the Decalogue as their heart. Philosophical treatments — Halevi's *Kuzari*, Maimonides's *Guide* 3.32–49 — build extensively on the Decalogue.

Modern Jewish thought: Heschel (*The Sabbath*, 1951) draws deeply on Deuteronomy 5's liberation-rationale for Sabbath; Heschel's argument that Sabbath is "a palace in time" is grounded in Deuteronomy's humane inclusion of servants and sojourners in Sabbath rest. Jonathan Sacks (*Covenant and Conversation: Devarim*) treats v. 3's "not with our fathers" as the Jewish theology of collective memory across generations. Nehama Leibowitz's *Studies in Devarim* works through every verse. Jon Levenson (*Sinai and Zion*) treats the Sinai theology of Deuteronomy 5 as the counterweight to the Zion theology that develops later.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

Patristic reception of Deuteronomy 5 focused on the Decalogue as the universal moral law. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 4.15) distinguished the Decalogue from the ceremonial and judicial laws — a distinction taken up by Tertullian, Augustine, and the entire medieval tradition. Origen's *Homilies on Exodus* handle the Decalogue in depth, with Deuteronomy 5 providing supplementary material. Augustine (*Questions on the Heptateuch*; *Sermons on the Decalogue*) established the Catholic-Lutheran enumeration that would dominate Western Christendom — combining the image prohibition with "no other gods" and splitting the coveting.

Medieval Catholic tradition placed the Decalogue at the center of moral theology. Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* I-II qq. 99–105 treats the Decalogue as moral law (binding always) distinct from ceremonial law (abrogated) and judicial law (abrogated except as reflective of moral principle). The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* (#2052–2550) devotes an entire major section to the Decalogue — approximately 500 paragraphs. Catholic catechetical tradition, from the Baltimore Catechism to contemporary programs, has organized moral formation around the Ten Commandments.

Reformation theology took the Decalogue up with fresh intensity. Luther's *Small Catechism* (1529) and *Large Catechism* organize Christian teaching around the Decalogue, the Apostles' Creed, and the Lord's Prayer. Luther's reading of the Decalogue as both "mirror of sin" (accusing) and "guide for the Christian life" (instructing) became Lutheran orthodoxy. Calvin's *Institutes* 2.8 is an extended treatment of the Decalogue; Calvin treats both Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5 as one law and organizes his ethics comprehensively around the Ten Commandments. The Westminster Larger Catechism (1648) includes a commandment-by-commandment exposition running to many pages. Reformed theology developed the "third use of the law" — the Decalogue as positive guide for regenerate Christians — as a distinctive theological move.

Critical scholarship from de Wette and Wellhausen forward has treated the Decalogue as compositionally layered. The "short" Decalogue (an underlying shorter version perhaps as simple as "no other gods / no images / no misusing the name / Sabbath / honor parents / no murder / no adultery / no stealing / no false witness / no coveting") has been sought by many scholars. The prose expansions (Sabbath rationale, curses for image-making, etc.) are treated as later theological explanation. Weinfeld's AB commentary provides exhaustive discussion.

Contemporary Christian commentary remains extensive. Walter Brueggemann (AOTC 2001) reads the Decalogue as a charter for an alternative political community. Patrick Miller's *The Ten Commandments* (2009) is a comprehensive theological treatment of both versions. Christopher Wright (NIBC 1996; *Old Testament Ethics for the People of God*, 2004) works out the Decalogue's ongoing social ethics. Daniel Block (NIVAC 2012) provides careful pastoral exegesis. Richard Hays (*The Moral Vision of the New Testament*, 1996) traces NT reception. Ellen Davis (*Scripture, Culture, and Agriculture*) develops the Sabbath's economic implications.

### Convergence and Divergence

Both traditions treat the Decalogue as foundational and authoritative. Both have extensive catechetical traditions built on it. Both wrestle with the two versions and their variations. Both have used it as moral bedrock for civilization-building — Mosaic law in synagogue and church has shaped Western legal and ethical consciousness profoundly.

They diverge on several points. Jewish tradition has kept the Decalogue's liturgical use limited (Shavuot only) to avoid implying its elevation above the rest of Torah; Christian tradition has made it catechetically central. Jewish tradition treats the Decalogue as one part of 613 commandments (*taryag mitzvot*); Christian tradition has often implicitly elevated the Ten above other OT commands, with the ceremonial/judicial/moral distinction doing the work of separating "still binding" from "not binding."

The Sabbath question has been the sharpest divergence. Jewish observance has maintained Saturday as the seventh-day Sabbath with elaborate halakhic specification. Christian practice, from at least the second century, shifted the day of assembly to Sunday (the Lord's Day), with variable theologies of whether this is the new "Sabbath" or a distinct observance. Deuteronomy 5:15's liberation rationale has been recovered in modern Christian theology — especially in Catholic social teaching on labor (*Dies Domini*) and in ecological readings of Sabbath — as grounding Sabbath in justice, not only in creation ordinance.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

### Tension 1: "Not with Our Fathers But with Us" — Covenantal Generational Compression

Verse 3 makes a startling claim: the covenant YHWH made at Horeb was not made with the dead exodus generation but with the present generation standing in Moab — who were not there. This is either a rhetorical exaggeration, a theological claim about covenantal presence, or a factual error. Each tradition has wrestled with it.

**Reformed**: The Reformed tradition has read v. 3 as a warrant for the covenantal solidarity of generations. The Westminster Confession (7.2–6) treats God's covenants as binding successive generations. The children of believing parents are covenantally included. Deuteronomy 5:3 is cited as foundational for infant baptism in Reformed sacramental theology — the covenant embraces children.

**Catholic**: Catholic tradition reads v. 3 as affirming the continuity of the people of God across generations, a continuity realized in the Church. The *Catechism* on sacraments and tradition (#75–83) develops this.

**Jewish**: The rabbinic tradition treats v. 3 with enormous seriousness. *Devarim Rabbah* 3:11 explains that every Jew stands at Sinai; the midrashic tradition of the unborn souls' presence at the giving of the Torah (Shavuot liturgy) grounds itself here. Modern Jewish thought (Heschel, Sacks, Borowitz) treats v. 3 as the Torah's philosophical claim that revelation is eternally present, not locked in past time.

**Lutheran / Wesleyan**: Luther's *Large Catechism* on the First Commandment cites v. 3 as the Decalogue's personalizing claim — the covenant is addressed to you, not merely recorded in a book. Wesley develops this pastorally in his sermons on the covenant.

**Critical**: Critical scholarship (Weinfeld, Nelson, Olson) treats v. 3 as a Deuteronomic rhetorical device to actualize past event for later audiences. This is not evasion but insight: the Deuteronomic school understood revelation as needing reactivation in each generation. The text itself thematizes its own ongoing relevance.

**Steelman (Jewish mystical reading, least intuitive for literal-historical readers)**: Every Jew was present at Sinai. The souls of all future generations stood with Moses. Covenant is not made with the dead but with the living, and the living include the unborn. This is not metaphor; it is the metaphysics of covenantal time. Modern Jewish philosophy (Rosenzweig's *Star of Redemption*) and mysticism (Lurianic kabbalah) have developed this into rich ontological claims about the nature of revelation. On this reading, v. 3 is literally true: this generation was there.

**Where this leaves us**: The text insists on a claim that cannot be reduced to chronological reporting. Whether one takes the mystical, covenantal, critical, or rhetorical path, v. 3 forces readers to rethink what it means for a covenant to be made "with us." The Decalogue is not the law of a dead generation; it is present claim.

### Tension 2: The Sabbath — Creation, Exodus, or Both?

Exodus 20:11 grounds Sabbath in creation: God made the world in six days and rested on the seventh. Deuteronomy 5:15 grounds Sabbath in Exodus: "you shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt." Two rationales for one commandment.

**Reformed**: Calvin (Institutes 2.8.28–34) treats the Sabbath as primarily a ceremonial commandment with moral substance, now fulfilled in Christ's rest. The Puritan Sabbatarian tradition (Westminster Confession 21.7–8) treats the Lord's Day as the Christian Sabbath, binding by creation ordinance. Deuteronomy's liberation-rationale tends to be subordinated to Exodus's creation-rationale in this tradition.

**Lutheran**: Lutheran theology treats the Sabbath ceremonially as abrogated, morally as binding (setting apart time for worship). The Augsburg Confession (Article 28) distinguishes sharply between Sabbath and Sunday.

**Catholic**: The *Catechism* (#2168–2195) affirms Sunday as the Christian Sabbath by apostolic tradition, grounds it in resurrection and creation, and reads Deuteronomy's liberation-rationale as enriching rather than replacing Exodus's creation-rationale. Vatican II and later Catholic social teaching (*Dies Domini*, 1998) give Deuteronomy 5:15 new weight in a labor-rights frame.

**Wesleyan**: Wesley treats the Sabbath as moral (creation-grounded) but not ritually restrictive. The Wesleyan social-holiness tradition has increasingly emphasized Deuteronomy 5:15's slave-and-servant inclusion as grounding labor ethics.

**Jewish**: Jewish tradition holds both rationales as complementary. *Mekhilta* and later halakhic literature treat creation and liberation as two sides of Sabbath's meaning. Heschel (*The Sabbath*) builds Jewish Sabbath theology explicitly on both rationales but accents Deuteronomy's humane inclusion.

**Social-ethical / Critical**: Contemporary scholars (Norman Gottwald, Walter Brueggemann, Ellen Davis) have argued that Deuteronomy's liberation-rationale is not secondary but primary — Sabbath as weekly enactment of Exodus freedom, incorporating servants, sojourners, even animals in a weekly justice-practice. On this reading, Deuteronomy 5:15 recovers Sabbath's original social-ethical force from what became in later tradition a purely cultic commandment.

**Steelman (the social-ethical reading, least intuitive for creation-ordinance traditionalists)**: Sabbath is fundamentally about freedom, not rest per se. Creation's seventh day frames labor's dignity, but only Deuteronomy's Exodus-rationale captures why Sabbath must include slaves, servants, sojourners, and even animals — because Israel was itself a slave, and the memory of slavery creates the ethical demand that no one else be kept at constant labor. The Sabbath is Israel's weekly re-enactment of the Exodus. To treat Sabbath merely as private devotion and rest is to lose its political-economic force. Modern labor laws, worker protections, and ecological sabbaticals all draw — often unknowingly — on Deuteronomy 5:15's theological logic.

**Where this leaves us**: The two rationales are not contradictory but complementary. Creation grounds Sabbath in the order of being; liberation grounds it in the ethics of solidarity. Jewish tradition has held both. Christian tradition, when it privileges one over the other, tends to lose what the other contributes. The full weight of the commandment requires both.

**One unresolved question**: The Samaritan Pentateuch ends the Decalogue with a tenth commandment specifying Mount Gerizim as the place of sacrifice, replacing the coveting prohibition. The Samaritan version is at least as old as the Masoretic tradition and was the Decalogue of a biblical-era community. Who decides which is the correct Decalogue, and on what grounds? The question exposes how much of "the" Bible is a tradition-specific canon.

## The Thread

Three cross-references that open the chapter outward:

First, v. 3's "not with our fathers but with us" is picked up in Jeremiah 31:31–34, the classic "new covenant" passage, which explicitly contrasts "the covenant I made with their fathers" with the new covenant written on hearts. Jeremiah is in deep dialogue with Deuteronomy 5; the "new covenant" claim is less a break from Deuteronomy than an intensification of Deuteronomy's own covenantal theology of generational compression. Hebrews 8:8–12 quotes Jeremiah 31 and thus, indirectly, Deuteronomy 5.

Second, the people's request for mediation (vv. 23–27) — "Go near and hear all that the LORD our God will say, and speak to us" — is picked up in Hebrews 12:18–24, where the author contrasts the terrifying Sinai theophany with the approachable Zion. Hebrews reads Deuteronomy 5's mediation request as the right response to the unapproachable holiness of God at Horeb, resolved only in the new-covenant access opened in Christ.

Third, "oh that they had such a heart as this always" (v. 29) is one of the most poignant lines in the Torah — YHWH's wistful wish that the people might fear and obey forever. This line resonates with Ezekiel 36:26–27 ("I will give you a new heart... and cause you to walk in my statutes"), with Jeremiah 31:33 ("I will write it on their hearts"), and with Romans 2:29 ("circumcision is of the heart"). The trajectory from Deuteronomy 5:29's divine longing to the new covenant's promise runs through the whole prophetic tradition.

Finally — a fourth thread — "he added no more" (v. 22, *lōʾ yāsāp̄*) is echoed in Deuteronomy 4:2 and 12:32, the prohibition against adding to or taking from the commandments. The Decalogue is the only revelation in the Torah explicitly given "directly" (without Moses as intermediary per v. 4), and the "he added no more" clause marks it as a uniquely complete revelation. Revelation 22:18–19's closing warning against adding to or taking from the prophecy of the book echoes Deuteronomy 4:2 and 12:32. The canonical instinct of "do not add" runs from Sinai through the last book of the Christian Bible.
