# Romans 5: Adam, Christ, and the Reign of Grace

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Paul dictated Romans to Tertius (16:22) from Corinth in the winter of 56–57 CE, on the eve of his ill-fated journey to Jerusalem with the collection (15:25–28). Phoebe, deacon of the church at Cenchreae (the eastern port of Corinth), carried the letter to Rome (16:1–2). The Roman house churches she addressed were a mixed and politically scarred community. Suetonius records that Claudius "expelled the Jews from Rome because they were constantly making disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus" (*Life of Claudius* 25.4) — almost certainly a garbled reference to disputes about Christos within Rome's synagogues. The expulsion of 49 CE displaced Jewish believers (including Priscilla and Aquila, Acts 18:2), and the house churches that grew during their absence were Gentile-led. When Claudius died in 54 CE and his edict lapsed, returning Jewish Christians came back to find a community whose center of gravity had shifted. Romans is written into that fault line.

By 56–57 CE Nero was three years into his reign, still in the *quinquennium Neronis* — the early "good years" under Seneca and Burrus. The Great Fire and the Neronian persecution were eight years away. Paul's audience had not yet been killed for the faith, but they were already navigating an empire whose ideological center was the Pax Augusta and the deification of Caesar. When Paul writes that "we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (5:1), the word *eirēnē* (εἰρήνη) is doing imperial work: in Rome, *pax* was the gift of Caesar, secured by the legions, advertised on the *Ara Pacis Augustae* dedicated by the Senate in 9 BCE. Paul redirects the vocabulary. The peace that matters is not Caesar's; it is reconciliation with God secured by the cross.

Three Greek terms repay close attention. ***Eirēnēn echomen*** (εἰρήνην ἔχομεν, 5:1) is the famous textual crux — the indicative "we have peace" or the subjunctive *echōmen* (ἔχωμεν), "let us have peace." The earliest and best manuscripts (P46, Sinaiticus*, Vaticanus*, Alexandrinus, D, K, L) actually read the subjunctive; the indicative is supported by correctors of Sinaiticus and Vaticanus and a smaller body of witnesses. Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary* gives the indicative a "B" rating on theological rather than purely text-critical grounds: editors judged that scribes were more likely to confuse omicron and omega aurally than that Paul was issuing a hortatory subjunctive in the middle of a justification argument. Cranfield (ICC) and Fitzmyer (AB) follow the indicative; Jewett (Hermeneia) leans subjunctive. This is one of the most instructive case studies in textual criticism in the New Testament: the manuscripts say one thing, the theology says another, and the editions split the difference. ***Katallagē*** (καταλλαγή, "reconciliation," 5:10–11) carries the everyday Greek sense of changing money or settling a quarrel; Paul is the first NT writer to make it a major theological term. ***Typos tou mellontos*** (τύπος τοῦ μέλλοντος, 5:14) — Adam as "type of the one who was to come" — gives us the noun from which the entire enterprise of *typology* takes its name.

Material culture matters here in a way that often goes unnoticed. The chapter's metaphor of one man's act condemning many would have landed differently in Rome than anywhere else. Roman law knew the principle of *paterfamilias*: the head of a household had legal personhood for everyone under his *manus*. The Senate could declare *damnatio memoriae* against an emperor and condemn his entire memory. Adam-Christ headship is theologically distinct from Roman federal headship, but the social grammar in which one person legally represents many was already in the air the audience breathed.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Romans is a letter, but it is also the most carefully argued document in the Pauline corpus, and chapter 5 is its hinge. After the indictment of 1:18–3:20 and the doctrinal exposition of 3:21–4:25, chapter 5 turns from "how are we justified?" to "what does justification do?" Calvin and most Reformed commentators read 5:1 as the beginning of the *application* of justification (the section running through chapter 8). Cranfield argues 5:1–11 is the *capstone* of the justification argument and 5:12 begins a new movement on *participation in Christ*. Moo (NICNT) splits the difference, taking 5:1–8:39 as a coherent unit on assurance.

The genre Paul reaches for here is *diatribe*, the argumentative form perfected by Stoic teachers like Epictetus and Musonius Rufus. Stanley Stowers (*The Diatribe and Paul's Letter to the Romans*) showed in detail how Paul uses the diatribal moves: the imagined interlocutor, the rhetorical question, the *mē genoito!* exclamation, the *a fortiori* "much more" argument. Chapter 5 is built around the *qal vāḥōmer* / *a fortiori* structure: "*pollō mallon*" (πολλῷ μᾶλλον, "much more") repeats five times in 5:9, 10, 15, 17, 20. The argument is from the lesser to the greater: if while we were enemies we were reconciled by his death, *much more* shall we be saved by his life. The rhetorical machinery is the engine of the theology.

The Adam-Christ section (5:12–21) is one of the most compressed pieces of typological theology in the canon. The grammar of 5:12 famously breaks off mid-sentence: "Therefore, as through one man sin entered into the world, and death through sin, and so death spread to all men because (*eph hō* ἐφ' ᾧ) all sinned —" The thought is interrupted, and the apodosis only resumes in 5:18. This is anacoluthon, a deliberate rhetorical fracture, and Paul fills the gap (5:13–17) with a series of disclaimers about how the analogy doesn't quite work — the free gift is *not* like the trespass. The structure is point-counterpoint-point: assertion (12), qualification (13–17), restatement (18–19), expansion (20–21).

The textual tradition is generally clean for chapter 5, with the great exception of 5:1 already discussed. Codex Vaticanus (B), Sinaiticus (א), and the early papyrus P46 (c. 200 CE, currently held at the University of Michigan and the Chester Beatty Library) preserve the chapter. P46 is particularly important because it gives us an Egyptian text-form within roughly 150 years of composition.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical story Paul is telling here begins in Genesis 3 and ends in the eschaton. Adam's act introduced sin and death; Christ's act introduces grace and life. The question "what kind of act, by what kind of person, with what kind of consequences for everyone else?" has been the dominant question of Western Christian anthropology for sixteen hundred years.

The **Jewish interpretive trajectory** on Adam differs sharply from what Christianity made of Romans 5. Second Temple Judaism knew the Adam story but did not generally read it as installing a heritable condition of guilt. *4 Ezra* 7:118 famously laments, "O Adam, what have you done? For though it was you who sinned, the fall was not yours alone, but ours also who are your descendants" — a clear sense of inherited consequence, but not of inherited culpability. *2 Baruch* 54:19 cuts the other direction: "Adam is, therefore, not the cause, except only for himself, but each of us has become our own Adam." Rabbinic Judaism kept the tension. The *yetzer hara* (the evil inclination) is universal but not because of imputation; it is part of how God made human beings, and Torah is the antidote. There is no doctrine of original sin in any rabbinic source. Daniel Boyarin (*A Radical Jew*) argues Paul's Adam-theology is a creative midrash on Genesis 2–3 read through the new conviction that Christ has happened.

The **Christian interpretive trajectory** runs through three great fault lines. The first is **Origen** (*Commentary on Romans*, c. 246 CE, preserved primarily in Rufinus's Latin translation), who read Adam allegorically — Adam is every soul's primordial fall — and who understood Romans 5 as teaching universal consequence with the possibility of universal restoration (*apokatastasis*). The second is **Augustine** versus **Pelagius**. The Pelagian controversy (411–418 CE) turned in significant part on Romans 5:12. Pelagius's *Commentary on Romans* (c. 405–410) reads "death spread to all because all sinned" as describing *imitation*: each person sins as Adam did and dies as Adam did. Augustine (*De Peccatorum Meritis et Remissione*, 412; *Contra Julianum*, 421) reads the Greek *eph hō* (ἐφ' ᾧ) through the Vulgate's *in quo* — "in whom all sinned" — and grounds the doctrine of inherited guilt: all of humanity sinned *in Adam* as a kind of seminal participant. Joseph Fitzmyer's commentary (Anchor Bible) is the modern locus classicus on the Greek: *eph hō* is almost certainly a causal conjunction ("because") or a result clause ("with the result that"), not a relative pronoun. The Vulgate's *in quo* is, on the best Greek reading, a mistranslation. Augustine built a doctrine on a translator's choice. Yet his theological reading of Adam-as-corporate-head is not without textual basis in 5:18–19, where Paul does say that "the many were *made sinners*" through Adam's disobedience — a claim Pelagius's imitation-only reading struggles to absorb.

The third great fault line is the **East/West split**. Eastern Orthodox theology never accepted Augustinian inherited guilt. John Romanides (*The Ancestral Sin*, 1957) argues that the Greek fathers — Athanasius, the Cappadocians, Cyril of Alexandria, John of Damascus — read Romans 5 as teaching inherited *mortality* and *corruption*, not inherited *culpability*. We die because Adam died; we are born into a death-shadowed world; but we are not born guilty. The Orthodox doctrine of *ancestral sin* (*propatorikon hamartēma*) is a real doctrine of human fallenness, but it does not require *peccatum originale* in Augustine's sense. Chrysostom's 32 *Homilies on Romans* show this clearly: Adam's sin opened the door for death, and we all walk through it, but each of us also adds our own sins.

The **Reformation** doubled down on Augustine. **Luther's *Lectures on Romans* (1515–16)** make Adam-Christ headship the engine of the doctrine of imputation: Adam's guilt is reckoned to us, Christ's righteousness is reckoned to us, and the parallel runs through 5:12–21. **Calvin's *Commentary* (1540)** systematizes this into the doctrine of original sin as *both* corruption *and* imputed guilt. **Melanchthon** in the *Loci Communes* makes Romans 5 the locus classicus for the doctrine.

All four modern interpretive schools claim Romans 5, and they read it differently.

The **Old Perspective** (Cranfield, Moo, Schreiner, in continuity with Augustine, Luther, Calvin) reads 5:12–21 as teaching forensic imputation: Adam's guilt and Christ's righteousness are both reckoned, and this is the heart of justification. Moo's NICNT is the most thorough recent statement.

The **New Perspective** (Sanders, Dunn WBC, **N.T. Wright**) reads 5:12–21 through the lens of covenant and Israel. Wright (*Climax of the Covenant*; *Paul and the Faithfulness of God*) argues Adam is a stand-in for Israel: Israel was supposed to be God's faithful image-bearer and undo Adam's failure, but Israel recapitulated Adam's disobedience, and Christ — Israel's Messiah — finally fulfills the Adamic vocation on Israel's and humanity's behalf. The chapter is less about how guilt transmits and more about how vocations succeed or fail.

The **Apocalyptic Paul** school (J.L. Martyn, **Beverly Gaventa** *Our Mother Saint Paul*, **Douglas Campbell** *The Deliverance of God*, Susan Eastman, Martinus de Boer) reads 5:12–21 as the entry of Sin and Death as cosmic *Powers*. Hamartia (ἁμαρτία) in this chapter is capitalized: it is not a record of misdeeds but an enslaving force that "entered" (*eisēlthen*, εἰσῆλθεν) the world like an invading army and "reigned" (*ebasileusen*, ἐβασίλευσεν, 5:14, 17, 21). Christ's act is not the cancellation of a debt but the invasion-in-reverse: the breaking of the cosmic regime of Sin and Death by the apocalypse of grace. Käsemann's commentary (1973, ET 1980) is the bridge text for this reading.

The **Paul Within Judaism** school (Mark Nanos, Magnus Zetterholm, **Pamela Eisenbaum** *Paul Was Not a Christian*, **Matthew Thiessen** *A Jewish Paul*, **Paula Fredriksen** *Paul: The Pagan's Apostle*) reads Romans 5 as Paul addressing the Gentile situation specifically. Adam matters because Gentiles are descendants of Adam and have no Torah-covenant; Christ matters because Christ provides what Torah was never going to provide for Gentiles — incorporation into the covenant family. On this reading Paul is not building a universal anthropology of inherited guilt; he is explaining to Gentile believers in Rome how they have been brought into the eschatological people of God through Christ's faithful death without becoming Jewish proselytes. Thiessen in particular argues the chapter's logic only works if "all sinned" refers to the unique Adamic universal that includes Gentiles — Israel had Torah and atonement; Gentiles had only Adam.

Each school has the texts it loves: Old Perspective loves 5:18–19 (the imputation language); New Perspective loves 5:20 (the law's role); Apocalyptic loves 5:14, 17, 21 (the reign of Sin and Death); Paul Within Judaism loves 5:13 ("sin is not counted where there is no law"). A responsible reading hears all four.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions thread through this chapter, and on each there are at least three serious historical positions.

**Tension One: How does Adam's sin become my sin?**

*Position One: Realist / Seminal headship* (Augustine, classical Reformed). All humanity was *seminally present* in Adam and so really sinned in his act. Texts: 5:12 (read with Augustine's *in quo*), 5:18–19 ("the many were made sinners"). Strength: takes seriously the corporate language of "made sinners." Weakness: relies on a metaphysics (seminal presence) that the text does not articulate, and on a Latin translation choice that does not match the Greek.

*Position Two: Federal / Covenantal headship* (developed by Cocceius and Reformed orthodoxy in the 17th century; Hodge, Murray, Schreiner). Adam is the legal representative of humanity by divine appointment, and his act counts forensically as ours. Strength: keeps the corporate logic without requiring strange metaphysics; analogous to how Christ's righteousness is imputed. Weakness: the analogue to how Christ is our representative is only as strong as one's antecedent commitment to imputed righteousness; if you read justification participationally rather than forensically, federal headship loses its main support.

*Position Three: Mediate imputation / inherited corruption only* (Eastern Orthodox; Wesleyan with modifications; Placaeus). We inherit a corrupted nature and a death-shadowed world, and we sin because we are corrupted; we are not directly imputed Adam's guilt. Strength: matches the Greek of 5:12 and avoids the moral problem of holding infants guilty for an act they did not commit. Weakness: must explain "the many were made sinners" (5:19) without a strong forensic component.

*Position Four: Pelagian / Imitation* (Pelagius; modern liberal Protestantism in some forms). Adam is the bad example, Christ is the good example, and "all sinned" means each person individually replicates Adam's sin. Strength: keeps moral responsibility individual. Weakness: cannot account for 5:14 (death reigned even over those whose sin was *not* like Adam's transgression — i.e., over those who had not received an explicit commandment).

*Steelman the Pelagian position*: The strongest case for Position Four is not the historical Pelagius but the modern observation that any account of inherited guilt creates serious moral problems (the condemnation of infants, the apparent injustice of being held responsible for someone else's act) and that 5:12's "*because* all sinned" is grammatically the most natural reading of *eph hō*. Pelagius's exegesis was better than his theology of grace. Augustine won the political battle; Pelagius may have won the philological one.

*Where this leaves us:* The Greek of 5:12 most naturally reads as causal — "death spread to all because all sinned." But 5:18–19 unmistakably says the *many* were *made* sinners by *one* man's act and the *many* will be made righteous by *one* man's act. The corporate-representative logic is in the text. Some account of solidarity is required. Whether that account is *seminal*, *federal*, or merely *consequential* is partly a question of which metaphysic one is willing to import. The Eastern reading (corruption inherited, guilt not) is exegetically defensible and pastorally more livable than the Augustinian-Reformed reading; the Augustinian-Reformed reading captures the corporate weight of "the many were made sinners" more directly.

**Tension Two: What did Christ's death actually accomplish? (Atonement theory)**

*Position One: Substitutionary atonement* (Anselm *Cur Deus Homo*; Reformed orthodoxy; the dominant evangelical reading). Christ died *in our place*, bearing the wrath we deserved. Texts: 5:6, 5:8 ("Christ died for the ungodly… for us"); 5:9 ("saved from the wrath of God").

*Position Two: Christus Victor* (the patristic mainstream; **Gustaf Aulén** *Christus Victor*, 1931, recovered this). Christ's death is the cosmic defeat of the Powers — Sin, Death, the Devil. Texts: 5:14, 17, 21 (death's reign broken); 5:10 ("reconciled" as the end of cosmic war).

*Position Three: Moral influence* (Abelard; modern liberal theology). Christ's death is the supreme demonstration of God's love that moves us to love in response. Text: 5:8 ("God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us") read as the central note.

*Position Four: Representative / participationist* (Eastern Orthodox; some contemporary Reformed like Michael Gorman; Apocalyptic). Christ's death is the death of the old humanity in which we participate, so that we might participate in his resurrection life. Texts: 5:10 (saved by *his life*); 5:17 ("reign in life through the one man Jesus Christ").

*Steelman Christus Victor*: The chapter's vocabulary — Sin and Death *reigning* (5:14, 17, 21), enemies *reconciled* (5:10) — is the vocabulary of cosmic conflict, not law-court transaction. If Romans 5 were the only chapter we had, the Christus Victor reading would be the most natural fit. The substitutionary reading depends on Romans 3 and 4 having already done the forensic work.

*Where this leaves us:* The chapter holds at least three motifs together: "for us" (substitutionary), "reign of death broken" (Christus Victor), and "God shows his love" (moral demonstration). Reducing the chapter to any one of these flattens it. The fourth-position participationist reading (we are reconciled by his death and saved by his life, 5:10) integrates the others: substitution is real, the Powers are defeated, love is shown — and all of it works because we are *in him*.

**One unresolved question:** If "the free gift led to justification *for all men*" (5:18) and "by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous" (5:19) match the universalism of Adam's reach ("death spread to *all*"), why is universalism not the obvious conclusion? Origen drew it. Karl Barth flirted with it. Most of the tradition has read 5:18 as potential universal scope realized through faith, but the Greek does not obviously support that move. The text says what it says. That tension is a feature of Paul, not a bug, and it has not been resolved in two thousand years.

## The Thread

5:12–21 should be read alongside 1 Corinthians 15:21–22 and 15:45–49, where the Adam-Christ typology is developed eschatologically (the last Adam as life-giving spirit). The chapter looks back to Genesis 2–3 (the only direct OT reference Paul gives is the Adam story itself) and forward to Romans 8, where the redemption inaugurated here is finally completed (no condemnation; creation set free; nothing can separate). The cry of 5:8 ("while we were still sinners, Christ died for us") echoes in 8:32 ("he who did not spare his own Son… how will he not also with him graciously give us all things?"). Flag forward: 7:7–25 returns to the Adamic anthropology of this chapter and asks what the Law has to do with the reign of Sin; 8:18–25 returns to "much more" and asks what the reconciled cosmos finally looks like. The chapter is the doorway into the heart of Romans, and every reading of 6, 7, and 8 begins with what one decided here about Adam.
