# Joshua 16: The Canaanite in Ephraim's House

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Joshua 16 describes the overall Joseph allotment (16:1–4) and then narrows to Ephraim specifically (16:5–10). The chapter is short — only ten verses — but its geography is extraordinarily dense. The Joseph territory runs from the Jordan at Jericho, up into the central highlands through Bethel and Luz, westward through Ataroth and the Beth-horon pass, down to Gezer, and out to the Mediterranean. This is the central spine of Palestine, the archaeological core of any Israel-emergence discussion, and the territory that Egyptian and Canaanite powers most obsessively contested in the Late Bronze Age.

The political context of the region. The Amarna Letters (fourteenth century BCE) include correspondence from Shechem's ruler Labayu (EA 244–250) complaining of ʿApiru incursions. Shechem itself (Tell Balatah), excavated by G. Ernest Wright and later by Lawrence Stager, shows continuous occupation through the Late Bronze period with no dramatic destruction at the conquest horizon. Shiloh (Tell Seilun), excavated by Hans Kjaer (1926–1932) and Israel Finkelstein (1980s), shows a significant Iron I settlement that Finkelstein dates to the twelfth–eleventh century BCE and identifies as the first major Israelite cultic center. Bethel (Beitin or Khirbet el-Maqatir — the identification is debated, with Bryant Wood arguing for the latter) shows a Late Bronze destruction. Gezer (Tell Jezer), excavated by R. A. S. Macalister (1902–1909) and William Dever (1964–1974), is perhaps the chapter's most crucial site archaeologically: it features a Late Bronze destruction, continued Canaanite occupation through Iron I, and a monumental six-chambered gate attributed by Yigael Yadin to Solomon's reign (tenth century BCE, cf. 1 Kgs 9:16). The Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BCE, Cairo Egyptian Museum) names Gezer alongside Ashkelon, Yanoam, and "Israel" as defeated by the pharaoh — the first extrabiblical reference to Israel.

The Beth-horon pass, controlling the east-west route between the coastal plain and the central hill country, was the strategic artery of the Joseph territory. Control of Beth-horon meant control of highland-coastal movement. Lower Beth-horon (Beit ʿUr et-Tahta) and Upper Beth-horon (Beit ʿUr el-Foqa) form a stepped ascent that 16:3 traces westward.

Key Hebrew terms. First, *gôrāl* (גּוֹרָל), "allotment" (16:1), again the divinely-determined lot. Second, *gəbûl* (גְּבוּל), "boundary," the chapter's controlling term, used six times in the brief Ephraim section. Third, *bənê-yôsēf* (בְּנֵי יוֹסֵף), "sons of Joseph" / "children of Joseph" (16:1, 4) — a collective designation for Ephraim + Manasseh, reflecting Jacob's adoption of Joseph's two sons as full tribal equals (Gen 48:5) and their combined double-portion inheritance. Fourth, *mas-ʿōvēd* (מַס־עֹבֵד), "forced labor," literally "serving tribute" (16:10) — the technical term for corvée, unfree state-imposed labor; the same term is later used for Solomon's own conscription of Israelites (1 Kgs 5:13; 9:21, ironically paralleling the Canaanite labor of Josh 16:10). Fifth, the phrase *ʿad ha-yôm ha-zeh* (עַד הַיּוֹם הַזֶּה), "to this day" (16:10), which Brevard Childs analyzed in his 1963 JBL article "A Study of the Formula 'Until This Day'" as the Deuteronomistic redactor's formulaic anchoring of traditions in a particular monarchic present. Sixth, the verb *yāraš* (יָרַשׁ) in the hif'il *hôrîš*, "to drive out / dispossess" (16:10) — the conquest-theological verb; its negation here is programmatically significant.

Textual witnesses. LXX Joshua is again shorter and occasionally rearranges material; 16:10 is stable across witnesses. 4QJoshuaᵃ does not preserve chapter 16 substantially. The Samaritan tradition, based at Mount Gerizim, has a special interest in the Joseph region but does not preserve a distinct Joshua text.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: Joshua 16 is a boundary document embedded with a redactional coda. Verses 1–4 describe the overall Joseph territory as a single unit — a literary acknowledgment that Ephraim and Manasseh, though distinct tribes, share a common ancestry and a common geographic core. Verses 5–9 narrow to Ephraim. Verse 10 is the chapter's theologically weighted tailpiece: "however, they did not drive out the Canaanites who lived in Gezer."

Literary devices. The boundary description uses the same formulaic motion verbs as chapter 15: *yāṣāʾ* (goes out), *ʿāvar* (passes along), *yārad* (goes down), *ʿālâ* (goes up), *nāsav* (turns around). Four geographic compass points structure the Ephraim section: east (16:5b), west (16:6a), north (16:6b), and the return down to Jericho (16:7). The tribe is textually inscribed through cardinal motion. The phrase "ending at the sea" (16:3, 6, 8) — three times — establishes the Mediterranean as the natural boundary, the text's cartographic horizon.

The redactional structure of the chapter is notable. Verse 4, "the people of Joseph, Manasseh and Ephraim, received their inheritance," is a formal summary — a redactional seam. It treats Joseph's two sons as a narrative unit before chapter 17 breaks out Manasseh separately. The effect is that chapter 16 is "Joseph generally + Ephraim specifically" and chapter 17 is "Manasseh specifically + complications" — a diptych held together by the Joseph frame.

The real literary move of the chapter is the tailpiece in 16:10. After nine verses of confident boundary description, the text makes an admission that resonates through the rest of the book: "they did not drive out the Canaanites who lived in Gezer." This is the second in a series of three such admissions in this section: 15:63 (Jebusites in Jerusalem for Judah), 16:10 (Canaanites in Gezer for Ephraim), 17:12–13 (Canaanites in several cities for Manasseh). The pattern is deliberate. The redactor, having given each of the three major tribal blocks their allotments, also gives each its admitted failure. Richard Nelson (*Joshua*, OTL, 1997) and Trent Butler (*Joshua*, WBC) both read this pattern as a literary structure that counterbalances the total-conquest claims of chapters 10–12. The book's left hand undoes what its right hand has asserted.

The "forced labor" specification in 16:10 adds a further literary layer. The Canaanites are not merely unexpelled; they are conscripted. This compromised conquest echoes forward to 1 Kings 9:20–21, where Solomon's continued corvée over non-Israelite populations is explicitly traced back to this unfinished Joshua-era business. The redactor is making a canonical link across the Deuteronomistic History: the corvée institution that would fracture Israel under Rehoboam (1 Kgs 12) has its roots here, in Ephraim's willingness to tolerate Canaanite neighbors it could exploit rather than expel.

Textual variants. The LXX of 16:10 adds a gloss about Pharaoh giving Gezer to his daughter as Solomon's bride-gift — harmonizing with 1 Kgs 9:16. This gloss is absent from the MT, and most scholars regard it as a secondary LXX expansion. The presence or absence of this gloss affects how one reads the chronology of Gezer's eventual Israelite possession.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish trajectory.** Rabbinic tradition on Joseph's double-portion is extensive. Bereshit Rabbah (Genesis Rabbah) 97 treats Ephraim's and Manasseh's tribal status as a direct outworking of Jacob's adoption blessing (Gen 48). The reversal of order — Jacob blessing the younger Ephraim above the older Manasseh — becomes a recurring motif in midrash. Tanhuma on Vayechi elaborates this reversal as part of a biblical pattern: the younger is preferred over the older (Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau, David over his brothers).

The Gezer forced-labor notice generates a distinct tradition. Talmud Sotah 36a treats the corvée of Canaanites as a legal precedent within the boundaries of *milchemet mitzvah* (obligatory war) theology, though later rabbinic voices question whether the failure to expel was a concession or a sin. Rashi (d. 1105) on 16:10 notes that the Canaanites were subjected to tribute rather than expelled, and he connects this to the broader pattern of incomplete conquest. Ibn Ezra (d. 1167) attends to the geography and the historical plausibility of the borders. Ramban (Nachmanides, d. 1270), writing from Acre, reads the "could not drive out" verses as indicators of Israel's failure of faith and of the consequent long work of repossession across the monarchic period. Radak (David Kimchi) treats the forced labor as a half-measure with moral consequences. Abarbanel (d. 1508), ever politically astute, reads the Gezer exception as explaining Solomon's later need to build up Gezer (1 Kgs 9) — Solomon had to finish what Ephraim had declined to. Maimonides does not treat the chapter directly but his *Hilkhot Melachim* 6 on the laws of war frames the broader theology of conquest and tribute.

Modern Jewish scholarship. Moshe Weinfeld (*The Promise of the Land*, 1993) analyzes the "to this day" formula across the Joseph allotment as Deuteronomistic. Jon Levenson treats the Joseph tribe's double portion as a key moment in the covenant-through-election pattern. James Kugel (*How to Read the Bible*, 2007) surveys the aggadic Joseph traditions. Baruch Halpern (*The Emergence of Israel in Canaan*, 1983) argues that the archaeological record supports gradual Israelite settlement in the Ephraim hill country with continuing Canaanite presence — the biblical text's "did not drive out" is historically credible. Rachel Havrelock (*River Jordan*, 2011) reads the Joseph allotment as the geographic center around which Israel's eventual fracture (north-south, Ephraim-Judah) would pivot. Yohanan Aharoni (*The Land of the Bible*, 1979) and Anson Rainey (*The Sacred Bridge*, 2006) provide detailed site-by-site identifications for Ephraim's boundary.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic readers were fascinated by Ephraim and Manasseh because of the Genesis 48 blessing's reversal of birthrights. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 5.21) and Origen (in his *Homilies on Genesis*) both interpret the elder-younger reversal as a typology of the Gentile church (younger Ephraim) supplanting old Israel (elder Manasseh). Origen's *Homilies on Joshua* treats the Joseph allotment allegorically: the central highlands represent the spiritual heartland of the soul's possession; Gezer's unexpelled Canaanites represent the unmortified passions that remain within the believer's territory. Homily 21 develops this at length. Jerome's *Onomasticon* identifies the sites.

Augustine's *City of God* (17.5) invokes the Ephraim-Manasseh reversal as part of the two-cities typology. John Chrysostom's homilies develop the Joseph traditions through a moral-theological lens. Theodoret of Cyrrhus treats the chapter with sober grammatical-historical commentary in his *Quaestiones in Josue*.

Medieval Western exegesis. Aquinas treats the tribal allotments structurally in *Summa* I-II.105 on the old law. The *Glossa Ordinaria* preserves the Origenian allegory of Gezer as unmortified vice. Luther's remarks on Joshua are sparse; he reads 16:10 as honest testimony to human weakness and the incompleteness of works-based conquest. Calvin's *Commentary on Joshua* (1563) reads 16:10 as a reproof of Ephraimite slackness — they could have driven the Canaanites out but preferred tribute; the forced-labor compromise would become the seedbed of later idolatry. Menno Simons is not a substantial commentator on this material.

Critical scholarship. Martin Noth (*Das Buch Josua*, 1938/1953; *Überlieferungsgeschichtliche Studien*, 1943) placed Joshua within the Deuteronomistic History and read 16:10's "to this day" formula as a redactional seam. Frank Moore Cross's dual-redaction thesis (*Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*, 1973) located the admission-of-failure notices in the Josianic edition. Brevard Childs's 1963 JBL article on the "until this day" formula remains foundational — Childs counted over eighty occurrences across the Hebrew Bible and argued they mark the redactor's contemporary vantage point. Richard Nelson (*Joshua*, OTL, 1997), Thomas Dozeman (*Joshua 1–12*, AB, 2015), and Trent Butler (*Joshua*, WBC) all treat 16:10 as a key instance of the Deuteronomist's structural self-correction. Zecharia Kallai (*Historical Geography of the Bible*, 1986) treats the Ephraim boundary as reliably ancient. Volkmar Fritz's *The City in Ancient Israel* engages Gezer's archaeology. Yigael Yadin (*Hazor*, 1972) and William Dever (*Who Were the Early Israelites?*, 2003) provide the archaeological framework for Gezer. Israel Finkelstein's work on Shiloh and the Ephraimite highlands is crucial for the settlement pattern.

Contemporary voices. Walter Brueggemann (*The Land*, 1977) reads 16:10 as the book's acknowledgment that covenant land is never fully possessed in history. Christopher Wright treats the corvée motif as a significant moral-theological category. John Goldingay attends to the honesty of the admission. Richard Hess (*Joshua*, TOTC, 1996) defends the historical substratum. Gordon Wenham reads the chapter as a case study in the Deuteronomic pattern of gift-demand-partial fulfillment. David Howard (*Joshua*, NAC, 1998) attends to the theological tension. Terence Fretheim treats the unexpelled Canaanites as a theology of risk and human agency. Ellen Davis's agrarian theology illuminates the ecology of the Ephraimite hill country as a specific human-land relationship. L. Daniel Hawk's *Joshua* (Berit Olam, 2000) treats 16:10 as ideologically illuminating — the Canaanites' reduction to corvée is the text's honest admission that the conquest was economic as well as territorial. Lori Rowlett attends to the violence the corvée implies. The chapter is in this respect especially damning: not only were the Canaanites dispossessed, those who remained were subjugated to forced labor. The chapter does not resolve the violence of conquest; it institutionalizes it — a point Brueggemann and Christopher Wright both register when they flag how dangerously the conquest paradigm has traveled in modern political history (from Puritan New England forward).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The admission within the claim.** The chapter's overall structure is confident allotment, and yet it ends with a confession of failure. Three positions.

*Position A: Honest redaction.* The "did not drive out" notices (15:63, 16:10, 17:12–13) are the Deuteronomist's honest self-correction — the redactor preserves traditions of failure even when they complicate the book's larger conquest rhetoric. The text is trustworthy precisely because it does not whitewash. (Nelson, Brueggemann, Goldingay.)

*Position B: Theological deferral.* The admissions of failure are not testimony against the conquest theology but narrative setups for later generations — Gezer is eventually taken by pharaonic gift through Solomon (1 Kgs 9:16), Jerusalem by David. The book is operating on a longer timeline than one generation. (Some canonical-theological readers.)

*Position C* (steelman of the least intuitive): *Moral critique from within the text.* The "forced labor" clause is damning. Israel did not merely fail to expel the Canaanites; it discovered that unexpelled Canaanites were economically useful. The chapter is, read carefully, a self-indictment of Ephraimite opportunism. The Canaanites are not simply persistent; they are conscripted. This is the point at which the conquest theology begins to corrupt into the very Egypt-style labor-extraction pattern that the Exodus was supposed to have ended. Hawk and Rowlett sit here. This is the uncomfortable reading: the chapter reveals that Israel inherits not only the land but the economic structures of the Canaanites it claimed to displace — a pattern that comes to full fruition when Solomon, with his own Israelite corvée (1 Kgs 5), becomes a pharaoh to his own people.

*Where this leaves us:* the chapter's confession is not a pious aside. It is a structural admission that the conquest project, even in its most confident book, did not do what it claimed to do, and that the compromise it settled for was not morally neutral.

**Tension 2: Joseph as beloved and Joseph as fracture point.** The chapter's Joseph framing (16:1–4) honors Jacob's blessing of Joseph's two sons. But Ephraim's centrality in the central highlands is precisely what will make it the northern kingdom's core after the division of the monarchy — Ephraim becomes, in prophetic literature, a shorthand for the rebellious north (Hos 4:17; 5:3; 11:8). Three positions.

*Position A: Unqualified honor.* Joseph's double-portion is the patriarchal blessing carried forward; Ephraim's later failures are not the allotment's fault but the tribe's. The chapter simply delivers the patriarchal promise. (Traditional readings.)

*Position B: Historical irony.* The chapter's honor of Ephraim prefigures, in its very generosity, the territorial foundation of the later schism. Giving Joseph the central hills was demographically correct and historically fractious. (Cross, Nelson on the DtrH's awareness of later history.)

*Position C* (steelman of the least intuitive): *Theological warning embedded.* The placement of the Gezer failure at the end of the Ephraim allotment is not incidental. The chapter honors Ephraim with land and warns Ephraim in the same breath: you did not drive them out, you made them serve you instead, and this compromise will shape who you become. The prophetic Ephraim — syncretistic, economically stratified, proud — is already visible in Joshua 16:10. The chapter is not just history; it is prefiguration.

*Where this leaves us:* the Joseph tribe is both the book's most blessed recipient and the place where the book's internal warning signs concentrate.

One unresolved question: if the "forced labor" settlement of 16:10 is the seed of Solomon's own conscription and therefore of the monarchy's eventual fracture, can a conquest theology ever produce a politics that does not eventually reproduce the Egypt it tried to escape?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 48:5, 17–20** — Jacob's adoption of Ephraim and Manasseh and his reversed blessing, the patriarchal basis for the Joseph double portion.
- **Judges 1:29** — a near-doublet of 16:10 in Judges, preserving the same tradition of Gezer's uncompleted conquest.
- **1 Kings 9:16–17** — Pharaoh's conquest of Gezer and its gift to Solomon, finishing what Joshua 16 left undone.
- **Hosea 11:1–9** — the prophetic Ephraim, beloved and broken, whose wound is visible already in Joshua 16's unfinished work.
