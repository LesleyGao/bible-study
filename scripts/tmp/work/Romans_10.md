# Romans 10: The Word Near You

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Romans 10 sits in the middle of the chapter sequence Paul composes from Corinth in winter 56-57 CE, addressing a Roman *ekklēsia* of mixed Jewish and Gentile composition still adjusting to the post-Claudian return of Jewish believers. The chapter's argument turns on the universal availability of the gospel — "no distinction between Jew and Greek" (10:12) — and on the failure of most of Paul's kinsmen to embrace it. Behind this stands the world of the synagogue, the marketplace where the *kerygma* was proclaimed, and the network of Roman roads along which messengers (*apostoloi* in the broader Greek sense) traveled. Paul's quotation of Isaiah 52:7 ("how beautiful are the feet of those who preach the good news," 10:15) was, in its original setting, a reference to the herald running ahead of returning exiles to announce salvation; in Paul's appropriation it becomes a description of Christian missionary activity, mediated through the Greco-Roman institution of the herald (*kēryx*) who carried imperial proclamations through provincial cities.

Greek terms central to the chapter include ***telos*** (τέλος, 10:4) — the most contested single word in the chapter, ranging across "end," "termination," "goal," "fulfillment," and "purpose"; ***dikaiosynē*** (δικαιοσύνη, "righteousness," 10:3-6) — the Pauline term whose contested semantic field defines the entire Old Perspective / New Perspective debate; ***akoē*** (ἀκοή, "hearing/report," 10:16-17) — Paul's term for the proclaimed word, which carries both the act of hearing and the content heard; ***rhēma*** (ῥῆμα, "word," 10:8, 17-18) — the spoken word, distinguished by some commentators from *logos* but in Paul largely interchangeable; and ***homologēsē*** (ὁμολογήσῃ, "confess," 10:9) — a term that in classical Greek meant "to agree" or "to acknowledge publicly," and which here designates the public confession that distinguished believers from synagogue Judaism.

The phrase "Jesus is Lord" (***Kyrios Iēsous***, Κύριος Ἰησοῦς, 10:9) carries political weight in the Roman world. *Kyrios* was used of the emperor in inscriptions across Asia Minor (especially after Nero), and Larry Hurtado (*Lord Jesus Christ*, 2003) has documented how the early *Kyrios* confession functioned as the central Christological acclamation in the earliest Pauline communities. The acclamation likely originated in Jewish-Christian Aramaic-speaking communities (cf. ***Marana tha***, "our Lord, come," 1 Cor 16:22) and was translated into Greek for missionary contexts.

The "stone of stumbling" image carried over from 9:33, plus the conflated Isaiah/Joel citations, confirms the existence of an early Christian *testimonia* tradition — collections of Old Testament texts grouped by theme (stone-texts, suffering-servant texts, Davidic texts) that circulated in early communities and that surface in Romans, 1 Peter, Hebrews, and Justin Martyr. C. H. Dodd's *According to the Scriptures* (1952) remains the foundational study; the Dead Sea Scrolls (4QFlorilegium, 4QTestimonia) demonstrate that such collections existed in Second Temple Judaism prior to and contemporaneously with Christianity.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Romans 10 is structurally bracketed by Paul's concern for Israel: the chapter opens with prayer for Israel's salvation (10:1) and closes with Isaiah's lament over Israel's disobedience (10:21). Within this frame, Paul develops a sustained argument that pivots on the universal availability of righteousness through faith and the universal proclamation of the gospel. Robert Jewett (*Romans*, Hermeneia, 612-657) divides the chapter into three movements: 10:1-4 (Israel's failed righteousness), 10:5-13 (the righteousness of faith), and 10:14-21 (the question of whether Israel has heard).

The chapter contains one of Paul's most striking pieces of midrashic exegesis: the rereading of Deuteronomy 30:11-14 in 10:6-8. The original Deuteronomic passage promises that the commandment is not too far away, not in heaven or beyond the sea, but "near you, in your mouth and in your heart, that you may do it" — Moses's assurance that obedience to Torah is achievable. Paul takes this Torah-affirming text and applies it to Christ: "Do not say in your heart, 'Who will ascend into heaven?' (that is, to bring Christ down) or 'Who will descend into the abyss?' (that is, to bring Christ up from the dead)." Hays's *Echoes of Scripture* (73-83) treats this as a paradigmatic case of Pauline intertextuality: the Torah-text is recontextualized but not dismissed; it speaks to Christ because Torah was always pointing toward him. Wright (*Climax of the Covenant*, 240-250) calls this Paul's "Christian Targum on Deuteronomy 30."

The early creedal formula at 10:9-10 has long been recognized as **pre-Pauline kerygma**. Bultmann (*Theology of the New Testament* 1.82) and Cullmann (*The Earliest Christian Confessions*, 1949) identified the parallel structure ("confess with your mouth... believe in your heart") as catechetical formula already in use before Paul wrote. The Christological content (lordship of Jesus + resurrection by God) tracks with the formulas at 1 Corinthians 15:3-5 and Philippians 2:6-11 in pointing to a primitive Aramaic-speaking confession that Paul has translated and integrated.

The rhetorical sequence of 10:14-15 — four cascading questions ("how then will they call... how are they to believe... how are they to hear... how are they to preach") — is a classical *sorites* (chained syllogism) that builds inevitability. Each question presupposes the answer to the next; the final question is answered by the Isaiah citation about beautiful feet. This is virtuoso Greek rhetoric, and it forms the basis of much subsequent missiological thinking (William Carey's *Enquiry* of 1792 quotes Romans 10:14-15 as the foundation of Protestant missions).

The string of quotations in 10:16-21 weaves Isaiah 53:1, Psalm 19:4, Deuteronomy 32:21, and Isaiah 65:1-2 into a single mosaic — Israel has heard, has been provoked to jealousy, has been outpaced by the nations, and yet remains "disobedient and contrary." The Deut 32 citation will return in 11:11, 14, where the "jealousy" theme becomes structural to Paul's eschatological argument.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc into Romans 10 runs through the Deuteronomic call to "love the Lord your God" (Deut 6:4-5) and the prophetic indictment that Israel had "a zeal for God but not according to knowledge" (Hos 4:6, Isa 1:3). Paul's diagnosis of Israel in 10:2 is not novel hostility; it is prophetic critique from within the tradition. The image of Israel called and not responding runs through Isaiah 65:2 (which Paul quotes at 10:21) and feeds forward into the Synoptic parables of the rejected invitation (Matthew 22:1-14, Luke 14:15-24).

**Jewish interpretive trajectories**, especially rabbinic readings of Deuteronomy 30:11-14, took the passage as proof that the Torah is universally accessible — *Bava Metzia* 59b uses the verse in the famous "oven of Achnai" debate to argue that Torah interpretation belongs to the rabbis once it has been given. The medieval philosophical tradition (Saadia, Maimonides) read it as a charter for rational accessibility of revelation. Paul's appropriation of the verse for Christ would have struck rabbinic interpreters as a gross category error; this is one of the texts where Christian and Jewish exegesis diverge most sharply.

**Christian interpretive trajectories** through the **Patristic** period read Romans 10:4 as confirming Christ's status as the fulfillment and goal of the law. Origen (*Commentary on Romans* 8.2-3) interprets *telos* primarily as "goal" or "completion"; Chrysostom (*Homilies on Romans* 17) similarly. Augustine reads 10:3 ("being ignorant of the righteousness of God, and seeking to establish their own") as the paradigmatic description of human pride and self-justification — a reading that becomes definitive for the Western tradition.

The **Reformation** seizes on Romans 10:9-10 ("if you confess with your mouth Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart") as the most distilled gospel formulation in the New Testament. Luther's 1546 Bible has 10:9-10 marked as a *Hauptstück* (chief text); the *sola fide* of Reformation theology takes 10:4 ("Christ is the *telos* of the law") as decisive — *telos* must mean "termination" because if the law continues to bind for righteousness, salvation is by works. Calvin (*Commentary on Romans*) argues similarly, treating 10:5-6 as a sharp antithesis between the righteousness "of the law" (impossible to achieve) and the righteousness "of faith."

The **20th century** brings two transformative readings. Karl Barth's *The Epistle to the Romans* (2nd ed., 1922) treats 10:4 as a crisis text: Christ is the *telos* not just of the Mosaic law but of every religious system that humans construct to climb to God; Barth's "religion as unbelief" follows directly. Bultmann (*Theology of the New Testament* 1.260-285) reads the chapter as the transition from Israel's *Heilsgeschichte* (salvation history) to the existential moment of decision; the *kerygma* of 10:9 confronts the hearer with the possibility of authentic existence.

The **four major modern interpretive schools** read this chapter sharply differently.

**Old Perspective (Augustine/Luther/Calvin/Reformed):** Israel's failure in 10:1-4 is **legalistic works-righteousness** — they sought to establish their own righteousness through Torah-keeping rather than receiving God's righteousness as gift. Schreiner (*Romans*, BECNT, 542-555), Moo (NICNT, 632-655), and Stuhlmacher (*Paul's Letter to the Romans*) treat *telos* as "termination": Christ ends the law as the way of righteousness. The chapter is the crystallization of *sola fide*. The steelman of this reading: it preserves the doctrine of grace; it takes seriously Paul's contrast between "their own" righteousness (10:3) and "God's righteousness" (10:3); it explains why Paul, a Jew, could be so urgent about Israel's salvation if Torah-keeping were sufficient.

**New Perspective (Sanders, Dunn, N.T. Wright):** Israel's failure was not legalism but **boundary-marker exclusivism** — clinging to ethnic-covenantal markers (circumcision, food laws, Sabbath) that fenced Israel off from Gentiles, when God's plan all along was for the world. Cranfield (ICC, 2.515-520), Wright (*Romans*, NIB; *Climax of the Covenant*; *PFG*), and Dunn (*Romans*, WBC) all argue that *telos* in 10:4 means "goal" or "fulfillment": Christ is the goal toward which the law was always aimed, not its termination. "Their own righteousness" (10:3) is corporate-ethnic righteousness based on Israel's covenant privilege, not individual works-righteousness in the Reformation sense. The steelman: the reading integrates better with Paul's argument throughout 9-11, which is corporate-historical; it makes sense of Paul's continued affirmation of Torah (Rom 7:12, "the law is holy"); and it recovers a Paul whose critique of his contemporaries is theological-covenantal rather than psychological-legalistic.

**Apocalyptic Paul (J.L. Martyn, Beverly Gaventa, Douglas Campbell):** Israel's failure is **captivity to the cosmic Powers of Sin and Death**, which the apocalyptic intervention of Christ has now broken. Campbell's *Deliverance of God* (840-851) offers the most comprehensive apocalyptic reading: the chapter is not about humans climbing to God by the wrong method (whether legalistic or ethnic) but about God invading a world enslaved to Sin. The "righteousness of God" (10:3) is God's redemptive action, not a status humans attain. Gaventa (*When in Romans*, 79-95) emphasizes that the gospel in 10:14-17 is the *kerygma* of God's apocalyptic deliverance, not a transaction-formula. The steelman: this reading takes seriously the cosmic stakes throughout Romans (cf. ch 8); it locates Paul firmly in apocalyptic Judaism (Daniel, 1 Enoch) rather than Greco-Roman moralism; it makes sense of Paul's intensity.

**Paul Within Judaism (Mark Nanos, Pamela Eisenbaum, Matthew Thiessen, Paula Fredriksen):** Paul is **not critiquing Torah or Judaism per se** — he is critiquing one stream of his contemporaries' reading of Torah, specifically those who failed to recognize the Messiah while still holding to Torah. Nanos (*Mystery of Romans*) argues that Paul writes as a Jew within Jewish covenantal categories; the "righteousness of God" (10:3) is God's covenantal faithfulness to Israel, which is being expressed through the Messiah's inclusion of the Gentiles. Eisenbaum (*Paul Was Not a Christian*) and Thiessen (*A Jewish Paul*) read 10:4 with *telos* as "goal": Christ is the goal of Torah for *Gentiles* — i.e., Gentiles are now included in God's people through faith in Christ, without needing to convert to Judaism, while Israel remains in its covenant. Fredriksen (*Paul: The Pagan's Apostle*) emphasizes that Paul's mission is to the *Gentiles*; his concern in Romans 10 is whether Israel's lack of faith in the Messiah is final (chapter 11 says no) or terminal. The steelman: this reading honors Paul's grief from chapter 9, integrates with the olive tree image of chapter 11, and takes seriously the "no distinction" of 10:12 as inclusion-language rather than supersessionism.

The four readings are mutually exclusive at key points. Old Perspective and Paul Within Judaism read Israel's failure entirely differently. Apocalyptic Paul transforms what counts as "salvation" from individual transaction to cosmic event. Each reading shapes a different Paul.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: The meaning of *telos* in 10:4.** This is the most contested single word in Pauline studies after *dikaiosynē* itself. "Christ is the *telos* of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes." Does *telos* mean "termination" or "goal"?

*Position 1 — Termination (Reformation, Old Perspective):* Christ ends the law as a way of righteousness. This is the classical Lutheran-Reformed reading; *telos* in 10:4 is the boundary at which the old age of the law gives way to the new age of grace. Steelman: this reading preserves *sola fide* against any synergism, makes sense of the "either/or" antithesis Paul sets up between Moses (10:5) and the righteousness of faith (10:6-8), and reflects the predominant lexical sense of *telos* in Paul (cf. Rom 6:21-22 "the *telos* of those things is death," 1 Cor 1:8 "to the *telos*").

*Position 2 — Goal/Fulfillment (Cranfield, Wright, Dunn, New Perspective):* Christ is the goal toward which the law was always aimed; the law is not abolished but fulfilled in him. Steelman: *telos* can mean "goal/aim" in classical Greek (the *telē* of a thing is its purpose); it preserves the continuity of God's plan from creation through covenant to Christ; it avoids the implication that Paul thought God had given a defective revelation in the Torah; and it integrates with Romans 3:31 ("Do we then overthrow the law by this faith? By no means; on the contrary, we uphold the law").

*Position 3 — Both/and (Cranfield's nuance, also Vanhoozer):* *Telos* carries both senses — termination *and* completion — because Christ both fulfills and transcends. The law has reached its goal in Christ, and therefore its function as the marker of the people of God has come to an end with the inclusion of the Gentiles. This dual reading honors the lexical range of *telos* and the complexity of Paul's argument.

**Where this leaves us:** The lexical evidence cannot adjudicate the question alone; the larger Pauline frame must. If Romans 10 is the climax of an argument about how *individuals* are saved, *telos* tends toward "termination." If it is the climax of an argument about how *Israel and the nations* are integrated into God's people through the Messiah, *telos* tends toward "goal." Most modern commentators recognize that the dichotomy is too sharp; Christ is both the end and the goal, and the question is which sense predominates in this verse.

**Tension Two: The relation between hearing and believing.** "Faith comes from hearing, and hearing through the word of Christ" (10:17). What kind of "hearing" produces faith? Is faith a human response to a heard message, or is it itself a divine gift evoked by the Spirit-empowered word?

*Position 1 — Reformed monergism (Calvin, Edwards, Berkouwer):* The "word of Christ" carries with it the Spirit, who creates faith in the hearer; the hearing itself is graced. The chapter does not contradict the predestinarian language of chapter 9 because the proclamation is the means by which God elects.

*Position 2 — Synergistic Arminian (Wesley, Marshall):* The "word of Christ" addresses every hearer with a real call; prevenient grace enables the hearer to respond, but the response is genuinely the human's own. The chapter's emphasis on universal proclamation (10:12-13, "everyone who calls") underwrites a universal saving will.

*Position 3 — Apocalyptic gift (Käsemann, Martyn, Gaventa):* "Faith" here is not primarily human response but God's apocalyptic event of creating a faithing-people; the *akoē* (10:17) is the eruption of the new creation into the old. Faith is gift, not achievement, but it is also not predestined-individual; it is the form of the new humanity God is bringing into being.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter's syntax cannot decide the question, but it does lean: Paul's emphasis falls on the *act of proclamation* and the *responsiveness it requires*, not on a hidden decree. The chapter is profoundly missionary in its rhetorical force; whatever theology of grace one holds, it must underwrite rather than undercut the urgency of preaching.

**One unresolved question:** When Paul ends with Isaiah 65:2 — God holding out his hands "all day long" to a "disobedient and contrary people" — is the disobedience *Israel's responsibility* (in which case 11's hardening is judicial), or is it *Israel's predicament* (in which case 11's mercy comes despite, not because of, anything Israel does)? The relationship between human responsibility and divine sovereignty in Israel's case is set up here and resolved (or not) in chapter 11.

## The Thread

- **Deuteronomy 30:11-14** — the Torah-affirming "word is near you" passage, which Paul rereads Christologically at 10:6-8. The intertextual move is the chapter's hermeneutical fulcrum.
- **Joel 2:32** — "everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved" (10:13), the universal-availability text Paul redeploys to Christ. The same verse is quoted in Acts 2:21 at Pentecost.
- **Isaiah 52:7** — "how beautiful are the feet of those who preach the good news" (10:15), the herald-text that funds Paul's missiology and, later, Protestant missions theology.
- **Romans 11:11-14** — flag forward: the "jealousy" of Deuteronomy 32:21, cited at 10:19, becomes the structural mechanism by which Paul explains how Gentile inclusion will provoke Israel back to faith. Chapter 10 sets up the puzzle; chapter 11 resolves it.
