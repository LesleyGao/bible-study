# 1 Corinthians 7 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

By the time Paul reaches chapter 7 of the letter he sent from Ephesus around AD 53–54, he has stopped responding to oral reports from "Chloe's people" (1:11) and started answering written questions from the Corinthians themselves. The formula *peri de* (περὶ δέ), "now concerning," at 7:1 is a discourse marker scholars have long recognized as Paul's way of announcing he is taking up a topic the Corinthians raised in their own letter to him. Margaret Mitchell catalogued the formula's use across the letter; it recurs at 7:25, 8:1, 12:1, 16:1, 16:12. The implication is consequential. The line "it is good for a man not to have sexual relations with a woman" is not Paul's opening thesis but a quotation of the Corinthian position he is responding to. That single grammatical observation, mainstreamed by Gordon Fee in NICNT (1987), restructures the entire chapter.

The Corinth Paul addresses contained at least three sexual subcultures. First, a Roman colonial elite shaped by the *lex Iulia de adulteriis* (18 BC) and the *lex Papia Poppaea* (AD 9), Augustan legislation that pressured the propertied classes to marry, produce children, and avoid divorce. Second, an ascetic-philosophical strand—Cynic, Stoic, and proto-Gnostic Jewish—that prized continence as spiritual achievement. Will Deming's *Paul on Marriage and Celibacy* (1995, second edition 2004) reconstructs the Stoic-Cynic background and reads chapter 7 as Paul navigating between Stoic accommodation of marriage and Cynic rejection of it. Third, a Jewish background where marriage was a *mitzvah*, the first commandment ("be fruitful and multiply"), and rabbinic literature would later codify (Mishnah Yevamot 6:6) that a man who reaches twenty without marrying violates a positive commandment. The Qumran community at Khirbet Qumran practiced celibacy according to Pliny (*Natural History* 5.73) and Josephus (*BJ* 2.120–121), and Philo's Therapeutae of Egypt practiced it in their own way (*De Vita Contemplativa*). Paul writes inside all three streams.

The "present distress," *enestōsa anankē* (ἐνεστῶσα ἀνάγκη) at 7:26, has been variously located. Bruce Winter argued for the literal grain shortages and famine documented in Achaia in the 50s. Others read it eschatologically—the messianic woes Paul expects before the parousia. The two are not mutually exclusive; Paul probably intends both. The textual transmission (P46, P15, Sinaiticus, Vaticanus) is stable for the chapter, with the only significant variant clustered around 7:36–38, where the question of *who* the *parthenos* is—daughter, betrothed, or spiritual partner—affects translation but not the underlying Greek.

The Erastus pavement and the Gallio inscription continue to anchor the dating. The presence in the Corinthian assembly of slaves, freedpersons, and married couples of mixed religious commitment (the situation Paul addresses in 7:12–16) is exactly what Theissen's social-historical work would predict for a Roman colonial port assembly.

## Lens 2: How the Literature Works

Chapter 7 is structured as a series of pastoral cases responding to the Corinthian letter, each marked by an addressee:

- **7:1–7** — Married couples, with Paul correcting an ascetic Corinthian slogan.
- **7:8–9** — Unmarried and widows.
- **7:10–11** — Married believers (Jesus tradition cited).
- **7:12–16** — Mixed marriages (Pauline ruling, no Jesus tradition).
- **7:17–24** — General principle: remain in the calling.
- **7:25–35** — The betrothed, set against the "present distress."
- **7:36–38** — A specific betrothal case.
- **7:39–40** — Widows.

The first crux is 7:1. *Kalon anthrōpō gynaikos mē haptesthai* (καλὸν ἀνθρώπῳ γυναικὸς μὴ ἅπτεσθαι), literally "good for a man not to touch a woman," uses *haptesthai* in the sexual sense it commonly carried in Greek (Plutarch, Septuagint, Josephus). Calvin and most patristic commentators treated this as Paul's view; Origen and Jerome built ascetic theology on it. The modern majority (Fee, Thiselton, Garland, Hays, Witherington, Collins) reads it as a Corinthian slogan Paul corrects—principally because the *peri de* formula introduces a topic from the Corinthian letter, and because Paul's immediate response in 7:2–5 systematically reframes sexual abstinence as a temporary exception rather than a default. Antoinette Wire's reconstruction (*The Corinthian Women Prophets*, 1990) proposes that a pneumatic theology of celibacy was already circulating among members of the Corinthian assembly and that Paul's response is a partial pull-back from a position they had pressed further than he wanted; the reconstruction is contested, and most commentators treat the slogan simply as the ascetic Corinthian position without specifying its bearers.

The mutuality of 7:3–4 is Paul at his most striking. *Opheilē* (ὀφειλή), conjugal duty, is named in both directions; *exousiazō* (ἐξουσιάζω), the verb of authority, governs both spouses' bodies symmetrically. In a Roman patriarchal culture where the *paterfamilias* held *patria potestas* over wife, children, and slaves, Paul's reciprocity is genuinely countercultural. It does not abolish gender hierarchy elsewhere in his letters, but inside the marriage bed it inserts a mutuality the surrounding culture had no syntax for.

The second major crux is the *parthenos* (παρθένος) of 7:25–38. The term means "virgin," but the question is *whose* virgin and *what relation*. Three readings: (a) a young man's betrothed (Conzelmann, Fee, Garland, NIV, ESV); (b) a father's daughter being given in marriage (older patristic tradition, KJV "his virgin daughter"); (c) a spiritual partner with whom a celibate man lives in non-sexual cohabitation, the *syneisaktos* arrangement that emerges visibly in second-century Christian sources (so Hans Achelis, *Virgines Subintroductae*, 1902, and a minority modern stream). The third reading is the most exotic; the first is the modern consensus.

The third crux is 7:21. *Mallon chrēsai* (μᾶλλον χρῆσαι), "rather use [it]," can mean either "use the freedom you have been offered" or "make use of your slavery" by remaining in it. The ambiguity is genuine. Most modern translations (NIV, ESV, NRSV) take it the first way; older patristic readings (Chrysostom) often took it the second. The chapter's overall logic—remain in the calling—pushes toward the second reading, but the parenthetical "if you can gain your freedom" pushes toward the first.

The eschatological hinge is 7:29–31. *Ho kairos synestalmenos estin* (ὁ καιρὸς συνεσταλμένος ἐστίν), "the appointed time has grown very short." The "as though" clauses (*hōs mē*, ὡς μή) describe a believer's posture toward worldly attachments under conditions of imminent eschatological crisis. This is one of the most difficult passages for any reader trying to construct a settled Christian social ethic from Paul; whatever stable rules he gives for marriage, business, and social position are framed by a sense that the present form of the world is passing away.

## Lens 3: Where This Sits in the Larger Story

The **Jewish trajectory** before Paul tilts strongly toward marriage as norm. Genesis 1:28 ("be fruitful and multiply") was read as the first positive commandment; rabbinic literature would later debate whether the obligation fell on men only or on both spouses, but the assumption that marriage was a religious duty was uncontested in mainstream Judaism. The exceptions—Qumran's celibate community, the Therapeutae, occasional rabbinic figures (Ben Azzai)—were exactly that, exceptions. Paul writes from inside this world. His preference for celibacy is not Jewish in its mainstream; his reasoning for it (the present distress, undivided devotion) is more legible in the apocalyptic stream of Second Temple Judaism than in the rabbinic. Daniel Boyarin reads Paul's celibacy here as in part an attempt to relativize the patriarchal household, with the parties most drawn to celibacy in Corinth experiencing it as a release from those constraints. Pamela Eisenbaum and Mark Nanos, from the Paul-within-Judaism school, frame Paul's instructions as ad hoc directives to a Gentile-inclusive community in a peculiar eschatological moment, not a Christian replacement of Jewish marital theology.

The **Christian trajectory** runs in the opposite direction. The patristic ascetic tradition built an entire theology of celibacy on chapter 7. Origen (*Commentary on 1 Corinthians*), Tertullian (*Ad Uxorem*), Methodius (*Symposium*), and especially Jerome (*Adversus Jovinianum*, 393) read the chapter as establishing celibacy as a higher state. Jovinianus, the late fourth-century monk who argued that married and celibate Christians stood equally before God, was condemned at synods in Rome and Milan, and Jerome's polemic against him became normative. Augustine's *De Bono Coniugali* (401) attempted a more balanced position—marriage is genuinely good, with the threefold goods of *proles* (offspring), *fides* (fidelity), and *sacramentum* (sacramental indissolubility)—but Augustine still ranked celibacy higher. Aquinas (*ST* II-II q. 152, *Suppl.* qq. 41–68) systematized the medieval position: marriage as one of the seven sacraments, celibacy as a more excellent state for those called to it. Trent (Session 24, canon 10, 1563) anathematized those who said marriage was equal to virginity. Vatican II's *Lumen Gentium* 35–36 maintained the traditional hierarchy while affirming the lay vocation more strongly.

The Reformation rejected the celibacy hierarchy as scripturally unfounded. Luther's 1525 marriage to Katharina von Bora was a doctrinal statement; *On Monastic Vows* (1521) and *The Estate of Marriage* (1522) argued that mandated celibacy was a Roman corruption and that marriage was a divinely ordained estate without inferiority. Calvin (*Institutes* IV.12.23–28) treated celibacy as a particular gift, not a higher state, and read 7:7 to mean exactly that: each has his own gift. Anabaptist and later Pietist traditions followed the Reformers; Wesleyan and Pentecostal traditions inherited the Protestant flattening of the celibate/married hierarchy.

Eastern Orthodoxy has held a third position. Marriage is a sacrament, crowned in the *stephanoma* rite, and lay marriage is fully honored; but monastic celibacy, including episcopal celibacy, holds a particular weight. Orthodox practice on divorce (the *economia* tradition, allowing remarriage with penance after a first divorce) developed differently from both the Catholic and Protestant streams.

The contemporary fault line on chapter 7 runs through divorce and remarriage. Catholic teaching treats sacramental marriage as indissoluble; the only opening is the Pauline privilege of 7:15 (a non-baptized partner abandoning a baptized one) and the related Petrine privilege, codified in Code of Canon Law 1141–1150 and CCC 2382–2386. Eastern Orthodox practice permits remarriage with penance under *economia*. Protestant traditions generally allow divorce on biblical grounds—adultery (Matthew 19:9) and abandonment (1 Cor 7:15)—with Wesleyan and mainline traditions extending the grounds further. The conservative evangelical position (John Murray, John Stott) holds the two scriptural grounds; the Erasmian position widens them; some Reformed and Anabaptist traditions have held tightly to no-divorce-no-remarriage.

## Lens 4: What Is Theologically at Stake

**Tension 1: The relative valuation of marriage and celibacy.**

The *Catholic* position, formalized at Trent, holds that celibacy "for the sake of the kingdom" (Matthew 19:12 read alongside 1 Corinthians 7:7–9, 32–35) is a higher state than marriage, though both are honorable. The reasoning rests on undivided devotion to Christ, on the eschatological signal value of the celibate life, and on the patristic-medieval reading of 7:38 ("he who marries does well, but he who refrains will do even better"). Catholic priests of the Latin rite, members of religious orders, and consecrated virgins live this teaching institutionally.

The *Eastern Orthodox* position honors marriage and monastic celibacy as parallel sacramental paths without ranking them, though the bishop must be celibate and the monastic vocation has particular spiritual weight. The Orthodox crowning rite makes marriage a martyrdom of mutual self-giving, theologically dense in its own right.

The *Reformation* position rejects the hierarchy. Luther read 7:7 as Paul saying explicitly that each has his own gift—neither state is intrinsically higher. Calvin treated celibacy as a charism for some and held that mandatory celibacy was a violation of conscience. The Protestant tradition has generally followed.

The *Wesleyan and Anabaptist* traditions inherit the Protestant flattening, sometimes adding their own emphases (Anabaptist communities have generally honored both married and single states without hierarchy; Wesleyan holiness traditions have at times produced their own forms of consecrated singleness).

The exegetical question: what does 7:7 actually teach? Paul says he wishes all were as he is, but immediately adds "each has his own gift from God, one of one kind and one of another." The Catholic reading takes "I wish all were as I am" as a real preference; the Protestant reading takes "each has his own gift" as a real diversity. Both are in the text. The theological question is which clause governs.

**Steelman the Catholic position**, since most contemporary readers (especially Protestant-formed readers) find it counterintuitive. The argument is not that married Christians are second-class. It is that the New Testament foregrounds an eschatological community whose ultimate horizon is not the family but the resurrection, where marrying and being given in marriage cease (Matthew 22:30). Celibacy makes that horizon visible *now*, in a body, in a life. A church that valued only marriage would have no embodied witness to the not-yet of the kingdom; the celibate vocation is the visible word that the world is passing away. Add to that the practical undivided availability for ministry that Paul names in 7:32–35, and the patristic-medieval reading that celibacy is the higher state begins to look less like a Romanizing distortion and more like a serious response to the eschatological grammar of the New Testament. Protestant traditions have, on this reading, lost something they should not have lost.

**Tension 2: Divorce, remarriage, and the Pauline privilege.**

The *Catholic* position holds sacramental marriage indissoluble, dissolved only by death. The Pauline privilege (7:12–16) applies only to non-sacramental marriages (where one spouse was unbaptized) that are then dissolved by the unbeliever's departure; the Petrine privilege extends similar logic to other non-sacramental cases. Annulment investigates whether a sacramental bond ever existed. The discipline is rigorous; pastoral care wrestles with it constantly.

The *Eastern Orthodox* position permits divorce and remarriage under *economia*, the church's pastoral discretion in applying the law, with penitential rites for second and rare third marriages. The first marriage retains its theological priority; subsequent marriages are gestures of mercy.

The *Protestant* position generally permits divorce on biblical grounds: adultery (Matthew 5:32; 19:9) and abandonment (1 Corinthians 7:15). The mainstream Reformed and confessional Lutheran traditions hold these two; the Wesleyan and broader mainline traditions have extended the grounds (abuse, irreconcilable spousal hostility) on the reasoning that the categorical concern of 7:15 is the believer's protection from coercion or destructive bondage.

The *Anabaptist* and *Wesleyan-holiness* traditions have at times held a stricter no-divorce, no-remarriage line; some Reformed traditions (the Westminster Assembly debated this) ultimately permitted remarriage on biblical grounds.

Where this leaves us: chapter 7 contains stable rules (no separation, no divorce, conjugal mutuality, marriage of betrothed if needed) framed by an eschatological qualification (the time is short, the form of this world is passing away). Every tradition has had to decide how much weight to put on the rules and how much on the qualification. The Catholic tradition put more weight on the rules; the Protestant tradition put more weight on the called-to-peace qualification; the Orthodox tradition tried to honor both through the *economia* of pastoral discretion.

**One unresolved question:** what does Paul mean in 7:14 when he says the unbelieving spouse and children are "made holy" through the believing spouse? Federal-headship readings (Reformed) and prevenient-grace readings (Wesleyan) and ecclesiological-cleanness readings (some patristic) all have purchase. What it cannot mean, given 7:16 ("how do you know whether you will save your husband?"), is that the unbelieving spouse is automatically saved. The intermediate space between "made holy" and "saved" is one of the more theologically interesting under-explored territories in Pauline soteriology.

## The Thread

- **Mark 10:2–12 / Matthew 19:3–12** — Jesus' teaching on divorce is the source Paul cites in 7:10 ("not I, but the Lord"). Matthew's Jesus adds the eunuch saying about those who renounce marriage "for the sake of the kingdom"—the proof text for the Catholic celibacy hierarchy.
- **Genesis 2:24** — "the two shall become one flesh," already cited in 1 Corinthians 6:16. The creation grammar of marriage runs underneath chapter 7's pastoral cases.
- **Ephesians 5:21–33** — the marriage-as-Christ-and-church analogy. Whatever the authorship debates, the canonical pairing of 1 Corinthians 7 with Ephesians 5 has shaped how every Christian tradition holds marriage's mundane and sacramental dimensions together.
- **Galatians 3:28** — "neither male nor female" in Christ. The baptismal formula sits behind Paul's mutuality grammar in 7:3–4 and may underwrite the ascetic enthusiasm in the Corinthian assembly that Antoinette Wire's reconstruction tries to recover.

The chapter ends in 7:39–40 with a tone that has surprised readers for centuries: a widow is free to marry whom she wishes, "only in the Lord," and Paul thinks she will be happier if she does not. The freedom and the counsel sit together without being collapsed. Whatever else 1 Corinthians 7 is doing, it refuses to turn the eschatological qualification into a law and refuses to turn the marriage rules into the whole of the Christian life. The chapter holds both, awkwardly, and then turns the page.
