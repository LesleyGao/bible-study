# Joshua 3: The River That Stood Up

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The Jordan crossing is staged at a specific place and season. The text tells us the waters were cut off "at Adam, the city that is beside Zarethan" (v. 16), most plausibly Tell ed-Damiyeh, roughly sixteen miles upstream from the traditional fording point near Jericho. The crossing is placed "in the time of harvest" (v. 15) — i.e., the spring barley harvest in late March or April, when snowmelt from Mount Hermon combined with the latter rains (*malqôš*) swells the river. This is not a dry-season crossing being dressed up as a miracle. The Jordan in April is in genuine flood, and ancient travelers routinely had to wait for it to subside.

The geological detail is striking. The Jordan Valley is a rift zone, and the marly cliffs at the Damiyeh bend are unstable. Medieval chronicler Nuwairi records a 1267 CE earthquake-landslide that dammed the Jordan for ten hours; similar events were documented in 1906 and 1927 (the latter for about twenty-one hours). Conservative scholars (John Bright, Richard Hess) cite these as the mechanism of a divine act; critical scholars (Nelson, Dozeman) note them but insist the narrative's interest is a re-Exodus, not a mechanism.

The political moment, again, is the Late Bronze/Iron I transition. The Jordan River is not merely a geographic barrier — it is an ethno-cultural one. The Transjordan (the territory of Reuben, Gad, and the half-tribe of Manasseh in biblical memory) was often treated as outside the "real" land of Canaan, a distinction reinforced in Numbers 32 and Joshua 22. *ʿivrî* ("Hebrew") derives from the same root as *ʿāvar* ("to cross over," the chapter's thematic verb) — a folk etymology older than the text, which treats Israel as the people defined by crossing boundaries: Abraham across the Euphrates (Josh 24:2–3), Jacob across the Jabbok (Gen 32), Moses across the Sea of Reeds (Exod 14), Joshua across the Jordan.

The Ark (*ʾărôn bĕrît YHWH*, אֲרוֹן בְּרִית יְהוָה, "ark of the covenant of YHWH") is the chapter's actual protagonist. It is named fifteen times in seventeen verses. In the ANE, processional portable shrines housing cult objects are well attested — the Egyptian solar barques that carried divine images in the Opet festival, the Hittite *huwaši* processional stones, and the Mesopotamian *makkūru* cultic chests. What is unusual about the Israelite ark is what it does not contain: no image, only the tablets (Deut 10:1–5) and, in later texts, manna and Aaron's staff (Heb 9:4). In Joshua 3 the ark is functioning as a military-cultic standard: the divine Presence going before the army. The "2,000 cubits" distance (roughly 900 meters) the people are to keep from it (v. 4) enforces the sanctity zone Leviticus established around holy space.

Five Hebrew terms to keep close. *ʿāvar* (עָבַר, "cross over") appears twenty-two times in chapter 3 — the text's drumbeat verb, building Israel's identity one syllable at a time. *Nēd* (נֵד, "heap," v. 13, 16) is the rare word used of the Sea of Reeds in Exodus 15:8 ("the floods stood up in a heap") — a deliberate echo almost certainly intended to trigger the memory of the earlier crossing. Outside of these two Exodus/Joshua passages and their psalmic echoes (Ps 33:7, 78:13), *nēd* is vanishingly rare. *Qāḏaš* (קָדַשׁ, "consecrate," v. 5) is a cultic term demanding ritual preparation — the people are about to enter a sacred zone. *Pelaʾôt* (פְּלָאוֹת, "wonders," v. 5) is almost exclusively a word for God's saving acts at the Sea and in the plagues (Ps 77:14; Exod 15:11). And *ʾēl ḥay* (אֵל חַי, "living God," v. 10) — an unusual divine epithet that contrasts YHWH with the dead deities of the Canaanite pantheon whose altars the Israelites will smash.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is DtrH narrative with distinct liturgical-cultic coloring. Gerhard von Rad, in his 1938 essay "The Problem of the Hexateuch," argued that chapters 3–5 preserve the ritual outline of a regular cultic festival celebrated at Gilgal in premonarchic Israel — a "reenactment" of the crossing in which the community annually re-performed its origins. This thesis has been modified (Hans-Joachim Kraus, *Worship in Israel*, sees it as a tribal league festival; Frank Moore Cross in *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* sees it as part of a broader "ritual conquest" cycle) but its basic insight remains: the text reads like a liturgy.

The structure of the chapter bears this out. There are anticipatory announcements (vv. 5, 7–8, 9–13) followed by the event (vv. 14–17), with the event itself only four verses — the rest is preparation and interpretation. The same scene is narrated multiple times from slightly different angles, a liturgical rather than journalistic technique. Robert Polzin (*Moses and the Deuteronomist*) identifies a fourfold recursive structure: Joshua instructs, the narrator reports, Joshua interprets, the narrator reports again. The reader learns the crossing by hearing it described four times.

Intertextually, the chapter is saturated with Exodus 14–15. The *nēd* of Exodus 15:8 reappears in 3:13, 16. The priests "standing" in the midst of the water (*yāʿămdû*, v. 17) echoes the pillar of cloud "standing" between Israel and Egypt (Exod 14:19–20). The phrase "on dry ground" (*beḥārāvâ*, v. 17) matches Exodus 14:21–22, 29. Dozeman and Hawk conclude chapter 3 was composed to read as Exodus Part Two. Moses crossed out; Joshua crosses in; the two crossings frame the wilderness generation.

There is one striking structural difference from Exodus. At the Red Sea, Moses raises his staff and God acts. At the Jordan, Joshua never stretches out a hand. The agency is entirely with YHWH and the ark. The chapter's most prominent human act is not a miracle-working gesture but a priestly step into the water. Michael Fishbane (*Biblical Interpretation in Ancient Israel*) reads this as a deliberate "demilitarization" of the crossing motif: the crossing is an act of worship before it is an act of war.

Location in the book's argument: Joshua 1:1–9 is commission, chapter 2 is reconnaissance, chapters 3–4 are the liturgical crossing, chapter 5 is covenant renewal at Gilgal (circumcision, Passover, the manna ending), and chapter 6 is Jericho. The crossing is not isolated; it is embedded in a seven-chapter induction that positions the conquest itself as a cultic rather than military act.

Textual variants are instructive. 4QJosh^a from Cave 4 preserves a version that may have placed the altar-on-Mt.-Ebal episode (MT Josh 8:30–35) *before* Jericho, immediately after the crossing. Eugene Ulrich argues this represents an earlier ordering in which Ebal, not the crossing alone, was the true liturgical conclusion to entering the land. The LXX is slightly shorter than MT here (omitting some of Joshua's repeated speeches in vv. 9–13). Emanuel Tov uses Joshua 3–4 to show the book circulated in meaningfully different editions well into the Hellenistic period.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, the Jordan crossing completes the first major arc of Israel's redemptive history: Egypt to land. From Abraham's first "cross over" in Genesis 12 to Israel's final one in Joshua 3, the Pentateuch is a single unfinished sentence whose verb is *ʿāvar*. But the crossing also opens a new arc: covenant-fidelity-or-exile, in which Israel will be tested by the same God who brought them in.

**Jewish trajectories.** In Second Temple literature the crossing is remembered as paradigmatic miracle. Ben Sira 46:1–6 places Joshua just below Moses in Israel's hall of fame — "how great was his glory when he lifted up his hands and stretched out his sword against the cities." Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.1.3–4) recounts the crossing with expansions — he adds a wind stopping the water and emphasizes the orderly military procession, downplaying the cultic elements for his Greco-Roman audience. The Apocryphon of Joshua (4Q378–379) is fragmentary but preserves a blessing of the tribes that echoes Joshua's priestly-covenantal tone.

Rabbinic reception is rich. The Babylonian Talmud, Sotah 33b–34a, debates the exact timing and choreography of the crossing: did the priests step in, the waters part, and then the people cross, or did the people begin crossing first? The rabbis conclude that the priests stood in the dry riverbed while every last Israelite crossed, and only then stepped out — a scene of astonishing duration, with the miracle sustained by priestly obedience. Sotah 34a also takes up the question of the ark: did the ark carry the priests across, or did the priests carry the ark? The tradition preserves both readings. Midrash Tanhuma on the crossing emphasizes the tribal unity — for the first time since the Red Sea, the whole nation acts together. Bereshit Rabbah and Pirkei d'Rabbi Eliezer both highlight the typological pairing of the two crossings.

Medieval Jewish commentary works the details. Rashi reads *ʾēl ḥay* ("living God," v. 10) in pointed contrast to Canaanite idols — the God who acts now in history, not mythology. Ibn Ezra is sober about the miracle: the text says what it says, but we should note the natural features (the flood stage, the distant location of the damming). Ramban/Nachmanides emphasizes the priestly role and the ark's primacy: the crossing is a revelation of divine Presence more than a military maneuver. Radak (Kimchi) works the geography carefully and identifies Adam with Tell ed-Damiyeh before archaeology caught up with tradition. Abarbanel reads the "three days" as a deliberate Sabbath-reset: the people rest and prepare before the new creation of the land.

Modern Jewish readers: Abraham Joshua Heschel (*The Prophets*) reads the crossing as the paradigm of God's "pathos" made visible in the riverbed. Yeḥezkel Kaufmann argued it is the last great miracle narrative before Israelite religion moves from "age of magic" to "age of prophecy." Rachel Havrelock's *River Jordan: The Mythology of a Dividing Line* is the definitive modern treatment: the Jordan is a theological boundary, and Israel's identity is permanently inflected by being the nation that crossed over. Moshe Weinfeld reads chapter 3 as the distilled expression of deuteronomic "land theology"; Avivah Zornberg traces the chapter's linguistic echoes of Exodus.

**Christian trajectories.** The patristic typology is strong. Origen's *Homily on Joshua* 4 reads the crossing as baptism: "when you have passed through the Jordan by the ministry of priests, you will enter the land of promise, in which Jesus receives you after Moses." Jesus (= Joshua, same name in Greek) leads where Moses could not. Cyril of Jerusalem (*Catechetical Lectures* 3.3) cites the crossing in his baptismal catecheses: new Christians are to understand themselves as crossing the Jordan into the kingdom. Tertullian (*On Baptism* 9) and Ambrose (*On the Sacraments*) develop the same typology. The triple immersion of baptism is sometimes traced to the "three days" of Joshua 3:2. Jerome comments on the chapter in his letters and connects it to the baptism of Christ in the Jordan (Matthew 3), closing the typological circle.

Medieval: the *Glossa Ordinaria* reproduces Origen; Aquinas cites Joshua 3 as a figure of the sacraments (ark = Christ, crossing = baptism, land = heaven). Reformers pressured such readings. Luther accepts the typology but subordinates it to the plain historical sense. Calvin's Joshua commentary is a model of sober exegesis: he treats the miracle as real and rebukes both rationalizing and over-allegorizing — the crossing is a "pledge of God's continuing presence," not a code. Menno Simons makes it a sign of the gathered community entering a new land separated from the world.

Critical scholarship: Wellhausen located most of chapter 3 in a late Deuteronomistic redaction. Noth's DtrH framework made it part of a single theological project. Von Rad's cultic-festival hypothesis dominated mid-century discussion. Frank Moore Cross situated the chapter in broader Iron Age cultic-royal ideology. Richard Nelson (OTL) and Thomas Dozeman (Eerdmans) read it as carefully composed Dtr theology; Trent Butler (Word) works the form-critical seams. Contemporary: Walter Brueggemann (*The Land*) reads the crossing as the moment Israel ceases to be landless — with all the promise and peril that entails. L. Daniel Hawk reads chapter 3 as the ideological high point before the cracks show. The honest-critical strand (Brueggemann, Jerome Creach) hears in the liturgical choreography the ominous opening of a territorial invasion dressed in cultic clothing.

Convergence and divergence: Jewish and Christian traditions both see the crossing as a constitutive foundational event, and both draw thick typological lines — back to the Red Sea, and in Christian tradition forward to Christian baptism. They diverge on what the event authorizes. For Jewish tradition, the crossing authorizes Israel's continuing existence in relation to the land and Torah. For Christian tradition, it authorizes the spiritual migration of the Church out of the world into the Kingdom. The Jewish reading keeps Jordan as a geographical fact with theological depth; the Christian reading transmutes it into a sacramental symbol whose geographical referent becomes optional. The fundamental question is whether biblical geography is incidental to its theology or constitutive of it — a question on which the two traditions have diverged sharply since Origen.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What is God up to? Two tensions.

**Tension 1: The miraculous and the natural.** The chapter tells us water dammed upstream at Adam (v. 16) — a detail consistent with the documented Jordan landslides of 1267 and 1927. Is this a concession to naturalism or a precise theological claim about how God acts in the world?

- *Catholic (Benedict XVI; CCC 301–308)*: God's action in nature is ordinarily mediated through secondary causes. A landslide at Adam and a divine act are not competing explanations; the same event is both at once. Miracles are not violations of nature but intensifications of providence at theologically significant moments. The crossing is a miracle whether or not the mechanism is describable.
- *Reformed (Calvin, B. B. Warfield, contemporary Vern Poythress)*: Calvin insists the crossing is real and historical; God is not obligated to act "supernaturally" in the sense of ignoring his own creation. Warfield in *Counterfeit Miracles* later distinguishes between biblical miracles (true interventions at redemptive-historical turning points) and ongoing claims of the miraculous. Joshua 3 qualifies as redemptive-historical.
- *Lutheran (Luther; Forde)*: Luther is characteristically concrete — God does what he says he will do, and the manner is God's business. The crossing is a *pro nobis* event: God parted the river *for us*. Modern Lutheran theologians like Gerhard Forde emphasize that the point is not the mechanics but the address.
- *Anabaptist (John Howard Yoder, Ched Myers)*: The crossing is real and politically significant — a displaced people receives a land from God — but must be read alongside the *herem*; whether God's giving of land can be separated from the displacement of others is unavoidable.
- *Wesleyan (Wesley; Thomas Oden)*: Wesley's *Explanatory Notes* defend the miracle and read it as prevenient grace: God goes first into the water, and we follow.
- *Jewish rabbinic*: As noted above, Sotah 33b–34a take the miracle as real without speculating on mechanism. The rabbis are more interested in the moral and ritual details — who stood where, in what order.
- *Critical/academic*: Most critical scholars read the narrative as theological composition shaped by liturgical reenactment, not as a straightforward historical report. Von Rad's cultic festival, Nelson's Dtr theology, Dozeman's literary analysis all read the chapter as Israel's memory working through symbol rather than recording what a camera would have seen.

*Steelman of the most critical position (von Rad's cultic reenactment)*: If chapter 3 originated as a liturgical script for an annual Gilgal festival, the text's recursive structure, its liturgical formulae ("consecrate yourselves," "the living God among you"), its cultic terminology, and its ark-centric choreography all make sense as stage directions rather than journalism. Israel knew how to tell the difference between liturgy and chronicle; we sometimes do not. The text is doing what the Eucharist does: making a past event present through ritual word and action. To flatten it into "did water really pile up?" is to misread the genre.

*Where this leaves us:* The miraculous and the liturgical are not opposed categories — they are the native form in which the text preserves its claim that YHWH brought Israel across. How much of the crossing is Gilgal remembering itself into its origin is a question the text does not answer.

**Tension 2: The ark of the covenant as mediator of divine presence.** The ark leads, the people follow at 2,000 cubits, the priests stand in the water while the people cross. What does it mean that God's presence is mediated through a portable chest carried by priests — and what happens when the mediator becomes the object?

- *Catholic (CCC 1114–1134; Ratzinger, *The Spirit of the Liturgy*)*: The ark is a prototype of sacramental mediation — God's presence carried in and through a material object handled by priests. This is not idolatry because the ark points beyond itself to the God whose covenant it carries. The Eucharist and the ark are theologically continuous: divine presence made available through authorized material mediation.
- *Reformed (Calvin, Bavinck, Kevin Vanhoozer)*: Calvin is cautious. The ark is a divine accommodation, a sign of God's presence for a people who needed a sign. But Reformed theology has always warned against confusing sign with thing signified. The ark is not God. When Israel later takes the ark into battle assuming its presence guarantees victory (1 Samuel 4), the result is disaster. The ark's proper place is as instrument, not guarantor.
- *Lutheran*: Luther accepts sacramental mediation and defends the real presence of God in word and sacrament — the ark is a forerunner of this. But the ark is never divinized.
- *Anabaptist*: Sacramental mediation is precisely the question Anabaptists have historically pressed. The ark serves its moment; it does not translate into an ongoing priestly monopoly on divine presence. Menno Simons's reading of Joshua 3 emphasizes the people — all Israel crossed, not just the priests. The priestly function is instrumental and temporary.
- *Orthodox (Schmemann, Zizioulas)*: The ark prefigures the Theotokos and the Eucharistic chalice — created matter bearing uncreated Presence. Orthodox theology has no embarrassment about mediated presence because the Incarnation has already made the question moot.
- *Jewish rabbinic*: The ark is sacred but not worshiped. Talmud Sotah 35a addresses the fate of the ark (disappeared during the Babylonian destruction; see 2 Macc 2:4–8 for traditions of Jeremiah hiding it). The ark's disappearance is not a theological crisis because Torah itself has replaced the ark as the locus of Presence.
- *Critical (Cross, Mettinger)*: Tryggve Mettinger's *The Dethronement of Sabaoth* tracks a shift in Israelite theology from "presence" theology (God physically dwells in the ark/temple) to "name" theology (God's name dwells there while he himself is enthroned in heaven). Joshua 3 is a high point of the older presence theology.

*Steelman of the Anabaptist position*: Joshua 3 is an unusual moment because the priests carry the ark but then stand still while the people cross. The narrative actually disempowers the priestly guild — they become the still point around which the whole nation moves. If the point were priestly monopoly on divine presence, the priests would cross first and usher the people in. They do not. They become the servants of the crossing, not its gatekeepers. Anabaptist ecclesiology reads this carefully: ministry exists to enable the whole body to pass through, not to represent it.

*Where this leaves us:* The chapter's ark theology cannot be domesticated into any single ecclesiology. It authorizes mediated presence and it demotes the mediators. Every tradition that has read the chapter has had to decide which side to lean into.

*One unresolved question:* If the crossing required the priests to stand still in water while the whole people moved, what does faithful ministry look like when the vocation is to be the still point inside a current — and what does that cost?

## The Thread

- **Exodus 14–15** — the Sea crossing that Joshua 3 deliberately re-performs. The rare word *nēd* ("heap") appears in both and essentially nowhere else. When you read Exodus 15 again, listen for the verb Rahab's confession uses — *nāmōg* — and hear the song reaching forward to the Canaanites.
- **2 Kings 2:8, 14** — Elijah and Elisha cross the Jordan on dry ground, explicitly repeating Joshua's crossing. Elisha's request is for a double portion of Elijah's spirit; the chapter is asking whether the Moses-to-Joshua succession has become the template for every prophet-to-disciple transition.
- **Psalm 114:3, 5** — "the Jordan turned back... what ails you, O Jordan, that you turn back?" A liturgical rereading of the crossing for generations of worshipers who never saw it. Notice how the psalm personifies the river as a frightened witness.
- **Matthew 3:13–17** — Jesus's baptism in the Jordan. When you reach this moment in the Gospels, remember chapter 3: the *new* Joshua (same name in Greek) stands in the same water, but this time the voice from heaven speaks over one person, and the identification of Israel shifts from the people to the one who is baptized for them.
