# Psalm 123 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 123 is the fourth in the **Songs of Ascents** (*Shir HaMa'alot*, שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת) — the fifteen-psalm pilgrim collection (Pss 120–134) within Book V (Pss 107–150) of the Psalter. The superscription is the bare *shir ha-ma'alot*, with no Davidic or Solomonic attribution. The collection's setting is most plausibly the **post-exilic** period, when the Songs of Ascents were used by pilgrims ascending to Jerusalem for the three Deuteronomic festivals (Passover, Pentecost/Shavuot, and Tabernacles/Sukkot, per Deut 16:16). *Mishnah Sukkah* 5:4 preserves the tradition that the Levites sang these fifteen psalms on the **fifteen semicircular steps** that led from the women's court to the men's court (Court of Israel) in the Second Temple — one psalm per step. Sigmund Mowinckel's cult-functional reading places these in the actual liturgy of the autumn New Year and Tabernacles festival; Hermann Gunkel grouped them with festival hymnody more broadly.

After Ps 120's exile, Ps 121's confidence on the road, and Ps 122's arrival at Jerusalem's gates, **Ps 123 turns the eyes upward** — from the gates and the hills to the heavens. The opening Hebrew is striking: *eilekha nasati et-einai ha-yoshvi ba-shamayim* (אֵלֶיךָ נָשָׂאתִי אֶת־עֵינַי הַיֹּשְׁבִי בַּשָּׁמָיִם, "to you I lift up my eyes, O you who are enthroned in the heavens," 123:1). The verb *nasa* (נָשָׂא, to lift, raise) with *einayim* (eyes) is the same idiom as Ps 121:1 (*essa einai*) — but the direction is altered. Ps 121 looked to the hills and asked; Ps 123 looks past the hills to the One enthroned above them.

The Hebrew *ha-yoshvi ba-shamayim* uses an unusual archaic suffix, the *yod* on the participle *yoshvi* — a poetic form of the participle *yoshev* (sitting, dwelling, enthroned) that appears scattered through Hebrew poetry (cf. Pss 113:5, 114:8). The participle *yashav* used of God means "to sit enthroned" — the divine king on his throne. The same root underlies *moshav* (a seat, a settlement) and *yeshivah* (a sitting, a session, hence the rabbinic academy). The image is of God enthroned in the heavenly court, a vision familiar from 1 Kings 22:19 (Micaiah's vision), Isaiah 6, and later apocalyptic literature.

Gunkel's form-critical genre is **communal lament** (*Klagelied des Volkes*) with strong elements of **trust psalm** (*Vertrauenspsalm*). The opening is individual ("*to you* **I** lift up my eyes," v. 1), but the psalm shifts to first-person plural in the middle of v. 2 and remains plural through v. 4 — *eineinu* (our eyes), *naphshenu* (our soul), *chanenu* (have mercy on us). The shift mimics Ps 121's similar move from individual to corporate. The lament's trigger is named in vv. 3–4: the people have endured *buz* (בּוּז, contempt) and *la'ag* (לַעַג, scorn / mocking) at the hands of *ha-sha'ananim* (הַשַּׁאֲנַנִּים, "those at ease, the complacent") and *ge'ei yonim* (גְּאֵי יוֹנִים, "the proud" — a textually disputed phrase, see below).

The dating fits a **post-exilic Sitz-im-Leben** of Persian-period Yehud, when the returned community lived under foreign administration, surrounded by hostile neighbors (Samaritans, Edomites/Idumeans, Arabs, Phoenicians) who looked down on the small struggling province (cf. Neh 2:19; 4:1–4 — Sanballat and Tobiah's mockery of the wall-builders). The "contempt" and "scorn" of vv. 3–4 are not metaphorical hostility but the lived experience of a small remnant community surrounded by larger, more prosperous, more confident peoples. **Hossfeld–Zenger** (Hermeneia commentary) reads this as the post-exilic community's prayer; **Hans-Joachim Kraus** (BKAT) and **Goldingay** concur.

The phrase *ge'ei yonim* (גְּאֵי יוֹנִים) in v. 4 is one of the textual cruxes of the Songs of Ascents. The Masoretic Text reads *li-ge'ei yonim*, traditionally rendered "(scorn) of the proud-of-oppressors" (the Hebrew Qere — what is read aloud — divides the words to read *li-ge'ei yonim*, "of the proud oppressors"). The Ketiv (what is written) is *ligeyonim* — possibly "to the proud," possibly a corrupted text. Some scholars (including Mitchell Dahood in the Anchor Bible Psalms) propose emendations. The ESV translation "of the proud" follows the standard reading. The substantive meaning is uncontested: the speakers are the targeted of the powerful and at-ease.

The keyword of the psalm's central image is *yad* (יָד, hand). The image of v. 2 — eyes looking to the *hand* — is precise. *El-yad adoneihem* (אֶל־יַד אֲדוֹנֵיהֶם, "to the hand of their masters") and *el-yad gevirtah* (אֶל־יַד גְּבִרְתָּהּ, "to the hand of her mistress") name the place from which a servant's instruction, sustenance, and mercy come. In the ANE household, the master's hand gave food, gave wages, gave correction, gave release. The servant who cannot read the master's mind reads the master's hand.

The Septuagint numbers Ps 123 as Ps 122 (LXX one behind MT) and translates the psalm without major textual disagreement. The **Dead Sea Scrolls** preserve Ps 123 in 11Q5 (the Great Psalms Scroll), with the text substantially aligned to MT.

## Lens 2: The Literature Itself

The psalm divides into **two strophes of two verses each**: the trust-image (vv. 1–2) and the petition (vv. 3–4). The first strophe builds outward and upward — *I* lift my eyes to *you* enthroned, then *as servants' eyes / as a maidservant's eyes / so our eyes*. The second strophe presses inward and downward — the contempt that has filled the soul to the brim.

Robert Lowth's parallelism is intricate here. **Synonymous parallelism** with **gendered pairing** structures v. 2: "as the eyes of *servants* (masculine plural, *avadim*) look to the hand of their *master* (masculine singular, *adoneihem*) / as the eyes of a *maidservant* (feminine singular, *shifchah*) to the hand of her *mistress* (feminine singular, *gevirtah*)." The four-fold parallelism — male slave / male master / female slave / female mistress — is a Hebrew device for totality. Both genders, both roles, both forms of dependency are named; nothing is left out. This is the same totalizing rhetoric as Genesis 1:27 ("male and female he created them"). The psalm asserts that *every* form of dependent looking-to-the-master is the right image for the soul's posture before God.

**Climactic / staircase parallelism** structures v. 3, with the immediate doubling of *chanenu Yahweh chanenu* (חָנֵּנוּ יְהוָה חָנֵּנוּ, "have mercy on us, O LORD, have mercy on us"). The repetition without variation is rare in Hebrew poetry and signals **urgency**. The petition pushes through twice before naming why — *ki-rav sav'anu vuz* (כִּי־רַב שָׂבַעְנוּ בוּז, "for we have had our fill, more than enough, of contempt"). The Hebrew verb *sava* (שָׂבַע) means to be sated, full to the point of cannot-take-any-more — the verb used for satisfied appetite at a meal. To be *sated with contempt* is a striking phrase: the people have eaten so much scorn that they cannot swallow another bite.

**Parallelism with intensification** structures v. 4: "our soul has had more than enough of *the scorn of those at ease* (la'ag ha-sha'ananim) / *the contempt of the proud* (buz li-ge'ei yonim)." The pairing of *la'ag* (mocking laughter) and *buz* (contempt) covers the spectrum from active ridicule to passive disdain. The pairing of *ha-sha'ananim* (the at-ease, the complacently comfortable) and *ge'ei yonim* (the proud) names two postures of dominance — the casual contempt of the comfortable and the active arrogance of the powerful.

The psalm's **inclusio** is built on the *eyes* motif: v. 1's "*to you* I lift up my eyes" opens, and v. 2's "so *our eyes* look to the LORD our God" closes the trust strophe; the eyes are the load-bearing image of the whole psalm. By v. 3 the eyes have spoken (the petition rises out of the gaze), and by v. 4 the eyes are still on the master's hand even as the soul is sated with contempt.

Note the temporal framing of v. 2's final clause: *ad she-yechonenu* (עַד שֶׁיְּחָנֵּנוּ, "until he has mercy on us"). The *ad* (until) marks the eyes' position as **patient endurance** — the gaze is not a momentary glance but a sustained looking-and-waiting. The servant does not look away.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story (Jewish and Christian Trajectories)

Within Book V's editorial sequence, Ps 123 occupies a turn in the pilgrim journey. Pss 120–122 moved from exile to road to arrival at the gates; Ps 123 turns the gaze upward from inside the city. The pilgrim has arrived but has not been delivered from the conditions of dispersion. The community in Yehud is at the Temple but still surrounded by contempt. Ps 123 names this paradox: arrival at Zion is not yet rescue from the world.

In **Jewish reception**, Ps 123 is recited at significant penitential moments. Some traditions include it in the *Selichot* prayers leading up to Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur; the doubled *chanenu* of v. 3 makes it an obvious selection for petitionary services. **Rashi** reads the servant-eye image as the posture of Israel before God across exile: the diaspora condition is precisely the condition of a servant whose master's hand has not yet given mercy, but whose eyes do not look away. **Ibn Ezra** notes the gendered pairing of v. 2 as a literary device for completeness. **Radak** (David Kimchi) develops the psalm as a communal lament against Gentile mockery of post-exilic Israel. **Midrash Tehillim** on Ps 123 multiplies the imagery — the servant-master parable becomes a meditation on the long patience of the diaspora soul. Modern Jewish commentators — **Buber**, **Heschel** (whose theology of *kavanah*, intentional gazing, resonates strongly with this psalm), **Levenson**, **Sarna** — locate Ps 123 within the Jewish theology of *kavod ha-shem* (the honor of the divine name). The contempt the people endure is, in some Jewish readings, contempt of the God they serve; the petition for mercy is also the petition for the vindication of the divine name. The Yom Kippur liturgy's repeated *Avinu Malkenu* ("our Father, our King") prayers carry forward Ps 123's master-and-mercy structure.

In **Christian reception**, Ps 123 has been a constant resource for the church's prayer in conditions of marginalization, persecution, and waiting. **Augustine**'s *Enarrationes* on Ps 122 (Latin numbering) reads the upward gaze as the contemplative life — the *visio Dei* anticipated in this life by the upward-turned eye. **Cassiodorus** systematizes the contemplative reading. **Aquinas** treats the master-servant image as a pattern of the creature-Creator relationship that exceeds even the most intimate human dependencies. **Luther** preached on Ps 123 in conditions of Reformation persecution — the church under Roman contempt and political pressure — and read the *sha'ananim* (those at ease) as the worldly powers who scorn the gospel. **Calvin**'s 1557 commentary develops the theme of the church-as-servant: the world's contempt is the church's lot, and the only proper response is to keep looking upward. Among moderns, **Hermann Gunkel** classified the psalm as communal lament; **Mowinckel** placed it in festival liturgy; **Westermann** highlighted its lament-to-trust movement; **Kraus** (BKAT) developed the post-exilic community context; **Hossfeld–Zenger** (Hermeneia) provided the most rigorous form-critical treatment; **Goldingay** (Baker) emphasizes the linkage to Ps 121; **Wenham** (on the Psalter's shape) places it in the editorial logic of the pilgrim collection; **Brueggemann** treats it as the lament without which trust becomes triumphalism; **Miller**, **Brown**, and **Tanner** trace its hermeneutical legacy. **Eugene Peterson**'s *A Long Obedience in the Same Direction* devotes a chapter to Ps 123 under the title "Service" — taking the master-servant image as a positive paradigm of the freedom that is found in dependence on the right master.

The **New Testament** does not directly cite Ps 123, but its theology of patient endurance under contempt informs Hebrews 12:1–3 (looking *unto* Jesus), the Beatitudes' blessing on those who are reviled (Matt 5:11–12), 1 Peter's letter to the Christian *paroikoi* enduring slander (1 Pet 2:12; 4:14), and the book of Revelation's vision of the saints under the altar crying *how long?* (Rev 6:10) — the exact temporal posture of Ps 123:2's *until he has mercy*.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — Is the master-servant image a sustainable contemporary devotional model?** Ps 123:2's central metaphor — eyes of servants looking to the hand of their master, eyes of a maidservant to the hand of her mistress — has been a beloved image across Christian and Jewish piety for two and a half millennia, and it is also an image that **modern class-and-power-aware readers** find difficult. The image presupposes household slavery (the Hebrew *eved* and *shifchah* are the standard terms for the slave-class in ancient Israelite society), and it draws devotional inspiration from a relationship that, in its earthly form, was at best paternalistic and at worst violent and dehumanizing. Several positions have emerged. **(1) The traditional devotional reading** (the *eyes-on-the-master* tradition from Augustine through Luther through Peterson): the human servant-master relationship is a creaturely analogue, imperfect but real, that points to the qualitatively different and ultimately liberating relationship of the soul to God. The image is not endorsing slavery; it is borrowing slavery's most attentive feature (the watchful gaze) and transposing it. **(2) The ethically-cautious reading** (much contemporary commentary): the image cannot be received uncritically; it bears the marks of the household slave economy that produced it, and contemporary readers must do hermeneutical work to retrieve the watchful-gaze without reinscribing the master-slave power relation onto God. **(3) The covenantal-Hebrew reading** (Levenson, others): in biblical Hebrew, *eved* (servant) is also the term used of Moses, the prophets, and the king as *eved Yahweh* (servant of the LORD) — an honorific. The servant of the right master is in a position of dignity, not degradation, because the master is just (cf. Lev 25:42 — Israel's slaves are God's slaves first, and therefore not absolutely the property of any human master). *Steelman for each:* The traditional reading honors the long devotional fruit of the image; the ethically-cautious reading honors the seriousness modern Christians have learned from confronting their own histories of slavery; the covenantal-Hebrew reading honors the term's full biblical range. *Where this leaves us:* Ps 123 invites the soul to a posture of patient, dependent, watchful gaze toward the hand from which mercy comes. The posture is real — most life's deepest mercies are given by hands we cannot command. The image's ancient setting requires honest reckoning, not nervous abandonment; the *kind* of master makes the servanthood good or evil, and Ps 123's master is the one enthroned in the heavens whose dominant attribute is *chen* (mercy, grace).

**Tension 2 — How long is "until he has mercy on us"?** The temporal *ad* (until) in v. 2 is open-ended. The psalm gives no schedule, no countdown, no promised arrival of relief. The contempt of vv. 3–4 is *now* and the mercy of v. 2 is *whenever-it-comes*. This is not a comfortable structure for either ancient or modern readers. **Three positions** stand in the literature. **(1) The eschatological reading** (Augustine, much patristic and Reformed tradition): the *until* points to the eschatological consummation, the final mercy that resolves all contempts. Present suffering is bracketed by future vindication. **(2) The proximate-historical reading** (Mowinckel, Kraus, Hossfeld–Zenger): the *until* refers to the specific historical relief the psalmist's community awaits — the lifting of foreign domination, the restoration of Yehud's standing. The promise is generationally specific. **(3) The continuing-prayer reading** (Brueggemann, Peterson): the *until* is liturgically open — every generation's praying community sings this psalm into its own *until*, and the promise is fulfilled in the LORD's repeated mercies that arrive partially and provisionally rather than in one final stroke. *Steelman for each:* The eschatological reading honors the full biblical narrative arc and the cross's pattern of vindication-after-suffering. The proximate-historical reading honors the realism of post-exilic Yehud's actual circumstances. The continuing-prayer reading honors the psalm's liturgical Sitz-im-Leben — this is a song people *keep singing* across centuries. *Where this leaves us:* The psalm refuses both despair (which would stop looking upward) and triumphalism (which would claim immediate mercy). It teaches the soul to keep its eyes on the hand from which mercy comes, while acknowledging that the timing is not the servant's to set. The dignity of the servant is in the constancy of the gaze, not in the fulfillment of the petition.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 123 names a particular, often-underappreciated posture of faith — the patient watchful gaze toward a hand whose timing is not the servant's to set. The Songs of Ascents pilgrim has arrived at the gates (Ps 122), but arrival has not abolished the contempt of the world; the post-exilic community is in the city but not delivered from the surrounding peoples' scorn. Ps 123 does not pretend otherwise. Its prayer is doubled — *chanenu Yahweh chanenu* — and its image is precise: eyes on the hand, until. The psalm refuses to look down at the contempt, refuses to look away into distraction, and refuses to look ahead with false certainty about when the hand will move. It looks up, and waits, and asks again.

The psalm leaves us with a question shaped by the tension between the now of contempt and the then of mercy: *what does it look like to keep your eyes on a hand whose timing is not yours to set?*

What in your life requires a watchful patience with no scheduled end?
