# Numbers 33: These Are the Stages of the People of Israel

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Numbers 33 is a travel log — a *massĕʿê*, an itinerary of forty-two stages from Rameses in Egypt to the Plains of Moab opposite Jericho. It is nearly the last word of the book of Numbers, and it is unusual in the Pentateuch in claiming explicit Mosaic authorship: "Moses wrote down their starting places, stage by stage, by command of the LORD" (v. 2). This is one of only three or four places in the Torah where Moses's own writing activity is explicitly attested (cf. Exod 17:14, 24:4, 34:27-28, Deut 31:9, 24). The chapter thus positions itself as a deliberate, divinely commanded archival act — the formal record of the wilderness journey.

The historical-geographical frame is complex. The forty-two stations are structured in roughly three arcs: Egypt to Sinai (vv. 3-15, about fourteen stations), Sinai to Kadesh (vv. 16-36, about twenty stations — most of which appear only in this list), and Kadesh to the Plains of Moab (vv. 37-49, about eight stations). Some of the stations are well attested from elsewhere in the Torah (Rameses, Succoth, Marah, Elim, Sinai, Kadesh, Mount Hor); others appear only here, generating a scholarly puzzle about sources and reliability. Sites like Rithmah, Libnah (distinct from the Canaanite Libnah), Rissah, Kehelathah, Makheloth, Mithkah, Hashmonah have no known archaeological identification and appear nowhere else.

The geography of the wilderness itinerary has been debated since antiquity. Three main routes have been proposed: (1) the traditional southern Sinai route, ending at Jebel Musa (traditional Mount Sinai), developed by Byzantine monasticism and defended by scholars like G. I. Davies (*The Way of the Wilderness*); (2) a northern route through the central Sinai, arguing for a different Mount Sinai; (3) the Mount Karkom hypothesis of Israeli archaeologist Emmanuel Anati, who identified a substantial Middle Bronze Age cultic complex in the Negev that he argued was the real Sinai. None of these proposals has achieved consensus. Kenneth Kitchen (*On the Reliability of the Old Testament*) surveys the problem and argues for substantial authenticity of the tradition while acknowledging specific site-identifications are uncertain.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels for the itinerary genre are striking. Egyptian military campaign reports from the New Kingdom routinely list the stages of march — Papyrus Anastasi V, the Annals of Thutmose III at Karnak, and the Megiddo campaign reports all follow a similar format: departure point, daily stages with camp locations, notable features or incidents. The *Journey of Wenamun* (c. 1100 BCE) documents an Egyptian envoy's travel itinerary to Phoenicia. Hittite military annals use similar structures. Mesopotamian *iter* records from Mari to the Neo-Assyrian period log royal travel. The genre is well-established across the ancient Near East. Numbers 33 adapts a standard Near Eastern bureaucratic form for theological purpose.

The chapter's culmination is in vv. 50-56, a shift from itinerary to legislation: the divine command to dispossess the Canaanite inhabitants, destroy their idols, pillars, and high places, and take possession by lot. The warning — "if you do not drive out the inhabitants of the land from before you, then those of them whom you let remain shall be as barbs in your eyes and thorns in your sides" (v. 55) — is one of the harshest statements of the *ḥērem*-adjacent conquest theology in the Pentateuch. The chapter's final words (v. 56) echo the threat: "I will do to you as I thought to do to them."

Authorship and dating. Wellhausen assigned the itinerary to P; Noth treated it as a late priestly compilation derived from earlier source materials. George W. Coats and others have analyzed the list-form as potentially reflecting multiple traditions crystallized together. Milgrom and Levine argue for substantive authenticity of an ancient travel-record, with priestly shaping. Kitchen defends the essential reliability. Martin Noth in *Numbers* (OTL) treats the chapter as a secondary summary derived from the narrative itineraries of Exodus and Numbers, potentially with supplementary station-names from other sources; he attends especially to the stations that appear only here (vv. 18-36) and proposes they may reflect a pilgrimage route of later periods retrojected into the wilderness narrative.

Five Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. *Massĕʿê* (מַסְעֵי, v. 1) — "stages, journeys, departures" — from the root meaning "to pull up (tent stakes), to break camp." The word appears as the title of the whole passage and gives its name to the parashah (Parashat Masei). *Môṣāʾôtām* (מוֹצָאֵיהֶם, vv. 1, 2) — "their starting points, departures" — emphasizes the *leaving*, not the arriving. *Yirāshtem* (יְרַשְׁתֶּם, vv. 53, 55) — "you shall dispossess, take possession" — from *yāresh*, the covenant-theological verb for possessing the land. *Ha-ṣalmê maṣekōtām* (צַלְמֵי מַסֵּכֹתָם, v. 52) — "their molten images" — paired with *bāmôt* (בָּמוֹת, "high places") in the iconoclastic command. *Bĕgôrāl* (בְּגוֹרָל, v. 54) — "by lot" — the technical term for divinely-directed random allotment, the mechanism for tribal land distribution that will be used in Joshua 13-19.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Numbers 33 is a hybrid genre: itinerary (vv. 1-49) followed by legislation (vv. 50-56). The itinerary itself is a highly stylized list, using a repeated formulaic structure: "they set out from X and camped at Y" (*way-yisʿû mi-X way-yaḥănû bĕ-Y*). The formula repeats forty-two times with minimal variation. The effect is liturgical and mnemonic — a text designed to be recited, to be memorized, to enact the journey in the telling.

Forty-two is not an arbitrary number. It is a multiple of six times seven, or the number of stations organized around seven arcs of six stops each (some commentators) or six arcs of seven stops each (others). The numerological reading has generated massive commentary. Matthew's genealogy of Jesus (Matt 1:1-17) is deliberately structured around forty-two generations divided into three sets of fourteen — a literary move some scholars (Richard Bauckham, W. D. Davies) have connected to Numbers 33. The Catholic Stations of the Cross (fourteen stations) and the traditional Lenten meditation on the journey of Christ's passion draw, indirectly, on the Numbers 33 itinerary tradition. Dante's *Divine Comedy*, in one hundred cantos structured across *Inferno*, *Purgatorio*, and *Paradiso* (each 33 + the proem), has been read by Giuseppe Mazzotta and others as reflecting numerological sensitivity to the Numbers 33 itinerary pattern.

The list-form itself serves literary and theological functions. It creates the experience of duration — reading through the forty-two stations, one feels the weight of the journey. It makes the wilderness corporeal; each stop has a name, and therefore a history, even if the history is lost. It democratizes the narrative: the named stations include places of apostasy (Rephidim, where there was no water; Kibroth-hattaavah, where the quails brought plague; Hazeroth, where Miriam was struck with leprosy) alongside places of encounter (Elim with its twelve springs and seventy palms) without distinguishing them in the list. The list treats each equally, as if to say: the journey is the journey, the whole of it, failure and provision alike.

The embedded narrative in vv. 3-4 — recalling the exodus from Rameses, the death of the Egyptian firstborn, the judgments on the Egyptian gods — disrupts the itinerary form with a brief theological commentary that sets the whole journey in the frame of divine action. The phrase "on their gods also the LORD executed judgments" (v. 4) recalls Exod 12:12 and anchors the journey in a cosmic conflict frame.

The mention of Aaron's death at Mount Hor (vv. 38-39) is another narrative interpolation: the date is specified ("in the fortieth year after the people of Israel had come out of the land of Egypt, on the first day of the fifth month"), and Aaron's age at death (123) is noted. This detail — unique to Numbers 33 — has been used by commentators to try to synchronize the wilderness chronology with other biblical dates.

The concluding legislation (vv. 50-56) shifts register sharply. The itinerary formula stops; direct divine address begins. The legislation has three elements: dispossession of the inhabitants, destruction of Canaanite cult objects (idols, molten images, high places), and allotment of the land by lot. The warning in v. 55 uses vivid metaphor — *śikîm bĕʿênêkem wĕlî-ṣinînîm bĕṣiddêkem* — "barbs in your eyes and thorns in your sides." The image is physical and visceral: leftover enemies as embedded splinters.

Textual variants. The Samaritan Pentateuch has minor variations in city-name spelling. The LXX preserves the itinerary but varies in transliteration of place names. 4QNum fragments cover parts of the chapter, confirming the Masoretic text substantively. The LXX adds a clarifying phrase at v. 54. None of the variants materially alters the chapter's content.

Literary devices include the numerology (42 stations), the anaphoric formula (*way-yisʿû... way-yaḥănû*), the envelope (start in Egypt, end on Plains of Moab), the narrative interpolations at strategic points (Red Sea, Aaron's death, embedded exodus recollection), and the tonal shift from bureaucratic recitation to prophetic warning at vv. 50-56.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish reception.** Rabbinic treatment of Numbers 33 centers on Rashi's foundational question: why does the Torah list each station? Rashi's answer, drawing on *Bamidbar Rabbah* 23, has shaped virtually all subsequent interpretation: "The Holy One, blessed be He, wanted to make known to them the kindnesses of the Omnipresent. For although He decreed upon them that they should be moved about and caused to wander in the wilderness, do not think that they were continually being moved about and wandering from station to station throughout the forty years without rest. For behold, there are only forty-two stations here. Subtract fourteen that were before the decree... and fourteen after the death of Aaron... you find that over the thirty-eight years they made only twenty journeys." The midrash reframes the itinerary as testimony to divine mercy: not perpetual motion but intermittent rest with relatively few relocations across almost four decades.

*Bamidbar Rabbah* 23 expands: "These are the journeys — [meaning,] these are all the places where we were stubborn and He was patient with us." The rabbinic tradition reads the itinerary as a prompt for memory and gratitude, each station a location of both human failure and divine mercy. The Mishnaic-Talmudic tradition does not develop extensive halakha from the chapter (unlike Num 30 or 35), but treats it liturgically and homiletically.

Medieval Jewish commentary. Rashi on v. 1 provides the fundamental framing. Ibn Ezra attends to the geography, attempting to identify stations and noting the difficulty of specific locations. Ramban (Nachmanides) reads the chapter as a theological memorial: Moses writes at divine command precisely so that the journey will be remembered, not abstracted. Ramban also comments at length on vv. 50-56, treating the iconoclastic commands as the precondition for covenant possession of the land. Maimonides, in *Guide of the Perplexed* III:50, explicitly addresses the question of why the Torah includes such lists: his answer is that the apparent tedium of the itinerary serves as historical anchor. If the Torah had simply said "they wandered in the wilderness forty years and entered the land," later readers might disbelieve. The precise stations anchor the narrative in verifiable historical claim.

Sforno reads the chapter as theological summary: the whole wilderness experience is compressed into the itinerary form so that Israel may see the journey whole. The Kabbalistic tradition, particularly the Zohar on Parashat Masei, develops the forty-two stations into a mystical pattern corresponding to the divine name of forty-two letters (a rabbinic tradition of one of the names of God) and to forty-two aspects of the divine presence. This tradition, while speculative, has been extensively developed in Hasidic commentary (especially the Berditchever Rebbe, the Degel Machaneh Ephraim, and the Shem MiShmuel), who treat each station as a spiritual state.

Modern Jewish readings. Nehama Leibowitz treats the chapter's pedagogical function — the itinerary as memory-discipline that prevents the amnesia of arrival. Jonathan Sacks emphasizes the covenantal importance of memory: a people without a documented journey is a people without a self. Milgrom's JPS commentary provides geographic analysis and treats the iconoclastic legislation in vv. 50-56 as continuous with Deut 7 and Exod 23. Avivah Zornberg reads the itinerary psychologically: each station as a psychological stage in the formation of a people from slavery to covenant. Walter Benjamin's brief but influential notes on the "angel of history" have been connected by some Jewish commentators to the itinerary's retrospective form: the journey read backward from the Plains of Moab.

**Christian reception.** Origen's *Homilies on Numbers* 27 and 28 are the foundational Christian treatment of the chapter and have shaped Christian spirituality for seventeen hundred years. Origen reads each station allegorically as a stage in the soul's ascent from Egypt (the bondage of sin and materiality) to the Promised Land (union with God). He treats the forty-two stations as forty-two spiritual states or graces through which the soul must pass. The homily is one of his most sustained allegorical readings, influencing the entire monastic tradition of *itinerarium mentis* (journey of the mind) literature. Origen's interpretation of Mara ("bitterness," the place where the water was bitter, Exod 15:23 / Num 33:8) as the bitter encounter with the law that must be sweetened by the cross became a patristic commonplace.

Augustine develops itinerary spirituality in his *Confessions* and *De Doctrina Christiana*, where the Christian life is framed as a journey home (*patria*) from exile (*peregrinatio*). While Augustine does not treat Num 33 at length, his entire theology of pilgrimage draws on the itinerary tradition Origen developed. Gregory the Great in his *Moralia on Job* further develops stage-of-ascent spirituality.

Medieval Christian tradition. The *itinerarium* genre becomes an established form: Bonaventure's *Itinerarium Mentis in Deum* (The Journey of the Mind into God, 1259) is perhaps the most famous, structuring the soul's ascent to God through six stages plus a seventh of mystical rest. Bonaventure does not cite Num 33 directly, but the genre he works in descends directly from Origen's reading. The Stations of the Cross, developed in the medieval period (stabilized at fourteen by the seventeenth century), are the itinerary-form applied to Christ's passion; the spiritual logic is continuous with Numbers 33.

Dante's *Divine Comedy* (c. 1308-1320) is a grand itinerary in 100 cantos (33 × 3 + 1 proem): from Hell through Purgatory to Paradise. Giuseppe Mazzotta, John Freccero, and Peter Hawkins have explored the numerological and typological resonances with Numbers 33 and the itinerary tradition. Dante's pilgrim Dante is, structurally, an Israelite wandering from Egypt to the Promised Land, with Virgil and Beatrice as guides. The Divine Comedy is, among other things, a Christian reception of Numbers 33.

Reformation readings. Luther engages Numbers 33 in sermons and lectures, though not systematically. His reading generally takes the itinerary as historical record and as prompt for memory and gratitude. Calvin in the *Commentary on the Last Four Books of Moses* treats the chapter more substantively. He emphasizes Moses's writing at divine command (v. 2) as evidence of biblical authenticity; he reads the itinerary theologically as record of God's fidelity; he discusses the iconoclastic legislation of vv. 50-56 in relation to his developed theology of idolatry. For Calvin, the chapter's dispossession commands are historically particular, tied to the specific Canaanite religious situation, and not templates for later Christian practice.

Critical and contemporary. Martin Noth's *Numbers* (OTL) treats the itinerary as late compilation. Gordon Wenham takes a more conservative position. Dennis Olson (*Numbers*, Interpretation) emphasizes the chapter's canonical function as summary and preparation for Deuteronomy. Walter Brueggemann has written on the theological importance of itinerary memory, arguing that modern communities that forget their journeys lose their capacity for covenant. Terence Fretheim treats the chapter as theology of God's presence along the way. Ephraim Radner's figural reading treats the forty-two stations as the church's own journey through time. Christopher Wright reads the iconoclastic legislation of vv. 50-56 through the framework of *Old Testament Ethics for the People of God*, arguing the commands must be contextualized within ANE idolatry practices including child sacrifice.

The critical literature on the iconoclastic commands and the "barbs in your eyes" warning (v. 55) overlaps with the literature on Num 31: Regina Schwartz, Paul Copan, Chris Wright, John Yoder, Richard Hays, Walter Brueggemann all address the conquest commands, with the same spectrum of readings (hyperbolic ANE rhetoric, covenantal particularity, canonical trajectory toward Christ, critical ethical rejection).

**Where they converge and diverge.** Jewish tradition treats the itinerary as memorial discipline — Rashi's reading — and generally has not allegorized the stations. Christian tradition, starting with Origen, transformed the itinerary into a template for spiritual ascent, creating an entire genre of Christian spirituality. Both converge on the theological significance of memory and journey; they diverge on literal versus allegorical. Modern readings on both sides have tended to reintegrate: Jewish readings attending to psychological-spiritual stages (Zornberg), Christian readings attending more to historical ground (Wenham, Olson). On the iconoclastic legislation, the traditions converge on historical particularity: the commands are not templates for contemporary practice.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Itinerary as memory discipline versus itinerary as spiritual template.**

*Position 1: Historical memorial (Rashi, *Bamidbar Rabbah*, most Jewish reading, Calvin).* The itinerary's purpose is memorial: to keep before Israel the specific, concrete path of their journey so that they may remember God's mercy and their own weakness. The stations are stations; they are not allegories. The chapter disciplines memory. Steelmanned, this reading resists the dissolution of the specific into the universal; the journey's particularity is the point.

*Position 2: Spiritual template (Origen, Bonaventure, Dante, monastic tradition, Kabbalistic reading).* Each station is more than a geographic marker; it is a stage in the soul's journey that repeats across generations. The chapter provides a framework for contemplating sanctification. The forty-two stations correspond to spiritual states discoverable in the inner life. Steelmanned (less intuitive for modern historical-critical readers), this reading finds in the specific the universal: the journey Israel made is the journey every soul makes. The spiritual itinerary is not imposed on the text; it is released by the text.

*Position 3: Canonical-figural (Radner, Hays, Olson).* The itinerary is both historical and figural without collapsing either. The journey that Israel made is the journey the church makes through time; the stations are real geographic locations and theological stages. The forty-two stations find their resolution in the one who made the journey for all — Christ, who is tempted in the wilderness, who crosses his own Jordan at baptism, who arrives at his own Moab in Gethsemane. Steelmanned, this reading holds the historical and the spiritual together.

*Position 4: Critical-archival (Noth, Wellhausen, some modern historical-critical).* The itinerary is a post-exilic construction serving contemporaneous identity claims; its forty-two stations reflect later liturgical or political purposes retrojected into the wilderness. The theological task is to read the chapter critically, asking whose memory this was designed to shape and for what purpose. Steelmanned, this position asks hard questions about who controls memory and how itineraries produce identities.

**Where this leaves us:** All four positions contribute. The memorial reading (Rashi) is indispensable as the chapter's primary function. The spiritual-template reading (Origen) is the most generative for Christian devotional life and generated immense literary and contemplative tradition. The figural reading (Radner) holds the two together. The critical reading keeps the tradition honest about who remembers and why. A healthy theological treatment draws on all four: read the itinerary as specific memory, as pattern of the soul's ascent, as figure of the church's journey, and with critical awareness of how memory is constructed.

**Tension 2: The iconoclastic legislation and the "barbs in your eyes" warning (vv. 50-56).**

*Position 1: Covenant-necessity reading (Calvin, Ramban, traditional reading).* The iconoclastic commands are the precondition of covenant occupation of the land. Canaanite religion, as attested archaeologically (child sacrifice at Carthage and Tophet, cultic prostitution, idolatrous ritual), was incompatible with YHWH's covenant; the commands are specific to this historical moment and this religious confrontation. The warning in v. 55 is prudential: partial iconoclasm produces ongoing compromise. Steelmanned, this reading treats the commands as historically-bound, covenantally serious, and not applicable beyond their moment.

*Position 2: Hyperbolic ANE reading (Copan, Wright, Kitchen).* The language of total dispossession is conventional ANE rhetoric; the practical target was the destruction of religious objects and the weakening of hostile political structures, not the annihilation of populations. The literary form serves a theological purpose (total allegiance to YHWH) that the specific language overstates. Steelmanned, this reading does careful work on ANE parallels and offers a defense of the text as read within its genre.

*Position 3: Canonical-critical reading (Yoder, Hays, Brueggemann).* The chapter's commands must be read in canonical trajectory; the prophets progressively critique the conquest ideology (Isaiah's vision of the nations streaming to Zion; Jonah's mission to Nineveh); the New Testament's mission to the Gentiles explicitly reverses the exclusion logic. The iconoclastic commands are real features of the text that the canon itself critiques and transcends. Steelmanned, this reading honors the text as Scripture while refusing to treat it as timelessly normative.

*Position 4: Ethically critical reading (Schwartz, Seibert).* The commands encode an exclusionary religious logic that must be named and rejected. The theological task is to read the text with eyes open to what it does and to decline to ratify its specific commands. Steelmanned, this reading forces attention to what the text says and to what it has authorized historically (Crusades, conquest rhetoric, settler-colonial theology).

**Where this leaves us:** The iconoclastic legislation of Num 33 is theologically serious and morally difficult. The covenant-necessity reading captures the chapter's internal logic but does not address the wider canon or later receptions. The hyperbolic-ANE reading does important exegetical work but strains at some specifics. The canonical-critical reading is probably the most productive for contemporary theological use: read the text, honor its presence in Scripture, trace the canonical movement, and attend to what the prophets and Christ do with the conquest trajectory. The ethically critical reading keeps honest accountability for what the text has authorized.

**Unresolved question:** What is the proper form of memory for a people whose sacred text records both their journey and their commands to dispossess? The chapter poses the question in itself — by juxtaposing forty-two stations of divine mercy with the final command to drive out the inhabitants. Christian and Jewish traditions have answered the question in different ways; no answer is fully stable. The chapter leaves us inside the tension.

## The Thread

Four non-obvious cross-references:

1. **Matthew 1:1-17 — the genealogy of Jesus in forty-two generations.** Matthew organizes Jesus's genealogy into three sets of fourteen, totaling forty-two generations from Abraham to Christ. Richard Bauckham (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, *Gospel Women*) and W. D. Davies have argued this deliberately echoes Numbers 33's forty-two stations. The claim: Jesus is the culmination of the itinerary, the one to whom all the stations lead. This reading, if accepted, makes Num 33 a quiet but decisive structure behind the New Testament's opening.

2. **Psalm 68:7-10 — "O God, when you went out before your people, when you marched through the wilderness, the earth quaked." The psalm rehearses the itinerary form in lyric mode. It is one of several biblical psalms (also Ps 78, 105, 106, 136) that retell the wilderness journey, making the itinerary-as-liturgy an ongoing form of Israelite worship. Num 33 provides the archival prose; the psalms provide the liturgical recitation.

3. **Hebrews 4:1-11 — "there remains a sabbath rest for the people of God."** The epistle's interpretation of the wilderness journey and entry into rest is the New Testament's most sustained theological engagement with the itinerary theme. Hebrews argues that the Promised Land-rest was only provisional; the true rest is still promised. Read against Num 33, the epistle extends the itinerary canonically: the forty-two stations were not the end; the journey continues in the new covenant's eschatological horizon.

4. **Bonaventure, *Itinerarium Mentis in Deum* (1259).** The medieval Franciscan's structural dependence on Origen's reading of Numbers 33 is often overlooked. Bonaventure organizes the soul's ascent into six stages plus a seventh of mystical rest — a structure that maps onto Origen's allegorization of the wilderness stations. The work is a primary source for how the chapter shaped Christian spirituality beyond strictly biblical interpretation; the chapter's forty-two stations silently underwrite one of the most influential spiritual works of the Middle Ages.
