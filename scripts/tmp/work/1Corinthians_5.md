# 1 Corinthians 5 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Paul writes from Ephesus around AD 53–54 to a community he had founded perhaps three years earlier (Acts 18:1–18). The Gallio inscription discovered at Delphi (now dated AD 51–52) anchors that founding visit and gives 1 Corinthians one of the firmest chronological pegs in the Pauline corpus. Roman Corinth itself was not the classical Greek city Strabo described; it was a Caesarean refoundation of 44 BC, populated initially with freedmen, veterans, and Greek-speaking economic migrants, and by Paul's day it was the administrative capital of the senatorial province of Achaia. The Erastus pavement inscription—still visible northeast of the theater—records a city official who paid for street paving "at his own expense," and most scholars (following Henry Cadbury and more recently Andrew Clarke) think it points to the Erastus of Romans 16:23, an oikonomos of the city. The community Paul addresses thus included people of real social standing alongside slaves and dependents, exactly the patron-client mix Gerd Theissen mapped in his classic essays on the social setting of Pauline Christianity, and that Wayne Meeks developed in *The First Urban Christians* (1983).

The older homiletic image of Corinth as a uniquely depraved port with a thousand sacred prostitutes on the Acrocorinth has been substantially deflated. Jerome Murphy-O'Connor's *St. Paul's Corinth* showed that Strabo's hieroduloi notice was already archaic when he wrote it, referring to fifth-century BC practice that had not survived the Roman destruction of 146 BC. Bruce Winter's *After Paul Left Corinth* (2001) reframes the moral environment as Roman provincial elite culture—patronage, public banqueting, status display, civic competition—rather than as exotic Eastern licentiousness. That matters for chapter 5 because the case Paul confronts is not "another day of Corinthian decadence" but a specific scandal that even pagan moralists would have found shocking. Roman law (the *lex Iulia de adulteriis* of 18 BC) treated marriage with a stepmother (*noverca*) as *incestum*, punishable by relegation. Cicero (*Pro Cluentio* 6.15) calls a comparable case "an unheard-of crime." Paul's "not even tolerated among the pagans" is sociologically accurate, not rhetorical exaggeration.

The textual pedigree is unusually strong. P46 (c. AD 200), the Chester Beatty papyrus, preserves much of 1 Corinthians; P11 and P15 add early witnesses; Sinaiticus and Vaticanus supply the great fourth-century uncials. There are no significant textual variants in chapter 5 that change the sense, though the precise wording of the curse formula in 5:5 has produced exegetical fights since antiquity.

The "previous letter" Paul mentions in 5:9—what scholars label "Letter A"—shows that 1 Corinthians is the second extant document in a longer correspondence. Murphy-O'Connor and others have suggested that 2 Corinthians 6:14–7:1 might be a fragment of that lost letter, on grounds of vocabulary and theme; the majority view is more cautious and treats Letter A as simply lost. Either way, 5:9–13 preserves a fascinating self-correction: Paul realizes the Corinthians have misread his earlier instruction as a counsel to withdraw from pagan society altogether, and he writes back to clarify that the boundary line he is drawing runs through the church, not around it.

## Lens 2: How the Literature Works

Margaret Mitchell's *Paul and the Rhetoric of Reconciliation* (1991) reads 1 Corinthians as a single deliberative argument urging *homonoia*—civic concord—on a fractured assembly, and chapter 5 is the first place that argument turns to practical case-handling. The structure of the chapter is tightly chiastic in feel even if not formally so: a case (5:1–2), a verdict (5:3–5), a theological warrant (5:6–8), and a clarification of scope (5:9–13). Paul moves from indignation ("It is actually reported…") through judicial pronouncement, through Passover typology, to a quotation of Deuteronomy that closes the unit.

The Greek deserves attention. *Porneia* (πορνεία) in 5:1 is the broad term for sexual irregularity; Paul specifies it as "a kind not even named among the Gentiles," using *onomazetai* in some textual streams to mean "tolerated, named approvingly." *Paradidōmi tō Satana* (παραδίδωμι τῷ Σατανᾷ) in 5:5 is the sentence itself—"to hand over to Satan." Adolf Deissmann famously found parallels in Greek magical papyri where curse formulas hand named individuals over to underworld powers, which raised the question whether Paul is invoking something like a religious curse. Most modern interpreters (Fee, Thiselton, Garland) read it as a ritualized act of community discipline whose effect is exclusion from the protected sphere of the assembly—putting the offender back into the realm where Satan operates—rather than a magical malediction. *Olethros tēs sarkos* (ὄλεθρος τῆς σαρκός), "destruction of the flesh," carries the ambiguity. Is "flesh" the offender's physical body (so Conzelmann, *Hermeneia*, who allowed the possibility of expected physical death)? Or is *sarx* the Pauline category of the old self in its rebellion (so Fee in NICNT, Thiselton in NIGTC)? The latter reading aligns the verse with Paul's broader anthropology in Romans 7–8 and Galatians 5 and gives the discipline a redemptive aim: "that the spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord."

Then comes the sudden pivot to Passover. *Zymē* (ζύμη), leaven, was a stock Jewish image for moral corruption—rabbinic literature later codifies the *yetzer ha-ra*'s relation to leaven, but the trope is already old by Paul's time. Paul tells the assembly to "purge out" the old leaven, *exkatharate* (ἐκκαθάρατε), because "Christ our Passover has been sacrificed." The link between the disciplinary purge and the *bedikat chametz*—the Jewish search for leaven before Passover—is exact and almost certainly intentional. The festival language ("let us keep the feast") may also reflect that Paul was writing in proximity to Pascha; David Garland (BECNT) and others note the natural seasonal echo. The closing quotation in 5:13—"Purge the evil person from among you"—is drawn from Deuteronomy (the formula appears in 13:5; 17:7; 19:19; 21:21; 22:21, 24; 24:7), the same Mosaic refrain Israel used to mark its boundaries against internal corruption. Paul is mapping ekklesia onto qahal.

Bullets only here, since this is structural:

- **5:1–2** — The case stated, the community's response indicted ("you are arrogant").
- **5:3–5** — Apostolic verdict, "in the name of the Lord Jesus."
- **5:6–8** — Passover/leaven warrant, christological grounding.
- **5:9–13** — Scope clarification: judge inside the church, not outside.

## Lens 3: Where This Sits in the Larger Story

Both Jewish and Christian traditions have read this chapter inside long arcs about how a covenant community polices its own boundaries.

The **Jewish trajectory** runs from Leviticus 18:8 ("you shall not uncover the nakedness of your father's wife") and Deuteronomy 27:20, through Second Temple developments. The Qumran *Community Rule* (1QS 6–7) codifies a graduated discipline: rebuke, exclusion from the pure meal, expulsion. Mishnaic *niddui* (rabbinic minor excommunication) and *cherem* (full ban) follow a similar logic of community separation aimed at repentance. Daniel Boyarin's *A Radical Jew* (1994) reads Paul as appropriating, not abandoning, Jewish disciplinary forms—though Boyarin sees Paul as universalizing them in a way that ultimately corrodes Jewish particularity. Mark Nanos and the broader "Paul within Judaism" movement (Eisenbaum, Fredriksen) press the alternative reading: Paul is a Diaspora Jew applying Jewish purity logic to a Gentile-inclusive ekklesia, not breaking with it. Either way, the Passover allegory in 5:6–8 is unmistakably Jewish in grammar, and the Deuteronomic citation in 5:13 makes clear that Paul thinks of his community as standing in continuity with Israel's vocation to be holy.

The **Christian trajectory** is the long history of church discipline. Tertullian's *De Pudicitia* (early third century) protests against bishops who readmitted adulterers too quickly, citing 1 Corinthians 5 as the touchstone for a stricter line. Cyprian's *De Lapsis*, written after the Decian persecution of AD 249–251, wrestled with what to do about the *lapsi*, those who had sacrificed under duress; here 1 Corinthians 5 was paired with Matthew 18 to argue for staged readmission through penance. Medieval canon law systematized this into the penitential system and, eventually, the formal mechanism of excommunication. Aquinas (*ST* II-II q. 39) treats excommunication as a medicinal penalty oriented to reconciliation. The Reformers retained discipline but reorganized it: Calvin's Geneva Consistory used 1 Corinthians 5 weekly, and Reformed confessional documents (Belgic Confession 29, Westminster 30) made discipline one of the marks of the true church. The Anabaptist tradition went further. The Schleitheim Confession (1527) made the *Bann* central, and later Mennonite and Amish communities developed *Meidung*, the social shunning of an excommunicated member that extends into family and economic life. Modern mainline Protestantism has largely abandoned formal discipline, though there are episodic recoveries; conservative evangelicals and confessional Reformed bodies still practice it. Catholic excommunication continues under the 1983 Code of Canon Law (cc. 1331–1335); Archbishop Lefebvre's 1988 case is the most prominent recent example.

The interesting thing is what happened to the redemptive aim. The patristic and medieval tradition almost universally read 5:5—"so that his spirit may be saved"—as the purpose of the discipline. The Anabaptist *Meidung*, properly practiced, also aims at restoration. When discipline collapsed in mainline Protestantism in the twentieth century, what was lost was not first the punishment but the restorative pathway it presupposed.

## Lens 4: What Is Theologically at Stake

Two tensions sit on the surface of this chapter, and both have produced serious internal Christian disagreement.

**Tension 1: How and whether the church should practice discipline.**

The *Catholic* position holds that excommunication is a medicinal penalty under the church's binding-and-loosing authority (Matthew 16:19; 18:18), oriented to repentance and reconciliation through sacramental penance. The 1983 Code distinguishes *latae sententiae* (automatic) from *ferendae sententiae* (declared) excommunication, with reconciliation accomplished through confession and absolution.

The *Reformed* position, articulated by Calvin (*Institutes* IV.12) and codified in Westminster 30, treats discipline as one of the marks of the true church alongside word and sacrament. It is graduated (admonition, suspension from the Lord's Supper, excommunication) and combines Matthew 18 with 1 Corinthians 5; the goal is the offender's repentance and the assembly's holiness.

The *Anabaptist* position pushes hardest on social separation. The Schleitheim Confession (1527) and later Dordrecht Confession (1632) extended the *Bann* into ordinary life. *Meidung*, in conservative Mennonite and Amish practice, means not eating with, not doing business with, sometimes not even speaking to the excluded member outside of efforts toward repentance.

The *contemporary mainline* position—where formal discipline has largely lapsed—treats the chapter as either historically conditioned, oriented to a small house-church setting that does not transfer to large institutional churches, or as overridden by a higher-order ethic of welcome.

**Steelman the Anabaptist case**, since it is the one modern readers find hardest. The argument is not that shunning is fun or cruel; it is that genuine spiritual care for an unrepentant member who refuses ordinary admonition requires social honesty. If the church says, with its mouth, that a pattern of life is incompatible with discipleship, but says with its dinner invitations and business handshakes that nothing has actually changed, the words are emptied. Paul's "not even to eat with such a one" (5:11) is precisely a refusal to participate in the social fiction that all is well. The Amish, who have maintained this practice longest, also have the lowest rates of attrition under social pressure of any Christian community in North America; the practice forms a community capable of making the words of the gospel tell against the formative power of ambient culture. One does not have to adopt *Meidung* to feel the weight of why a serious community might.

**Tension 2: What "destruction of the flesh, that the spirit may be saved" means soteriologically.**

The *Catholic* reading frames the discipline as restorative penance: the flesh's *concupiscentia* is mortified through external chastisement so that the soul may be reconciled to God in the sacrament of penance. The *Reformed* reading treats it as sanctifying chastisement: God uses the church's verdict as an instrument of the Spirit's putting-to-death of the old self in the elect (Romans 8:13), so that perseverance is preserved through suffering. The *Lutheran* reading inflects this through *simul iustus et peccator*: the believer is always both sinner and justified, and the discipline targets the *vetus homo*, drowning the old Adam daily, until the new emerges. The *Eastern Orthodox* reading frames it as *metanoia*, a turning whose external form is ascetic in shape—exclusion from the eucharistic mystery is itself the fasting that produces compunction.

Where this leaves us: every serious Christian tradition has read 1 Corinthians 5 as describing something the church does *to* one of its members *for* that member's salvation. The differences concern the mechanism, the social form, and the ecclesiology that authorizes it. The collapse of discipline in much of contemporary Western Christianity is, on this reading, not progress but the loss of a tool the New Testament assumes.

**One unresolved question:** Did Paul expect the offender to die? Conzelmann thought so; most modern commentators do not. If the answer is yes, the chapter is harder than the redemptive readings make it. If the answer is no, what exactly does "destruction of the flesh" name as a process the assembly initiates? The text resists collapsing the ambiguity in either direction.

## The Thread

This chapter sits inside a set of cross-references that mostly read it.

- **Matthew 18:15–20** — Jesus' three-stage procedure (private, witnesses, church) is the New Testament's other fundamental discipline text. The Reformed tradition reads them together; the Anabaptist tradition foregrounds Matthew 18; Paul's case is more compressed because the offense is public and the Corinthians already know about it.
- **Deuteronomy 13:5; 17:7; 19:19** — the source of the closing quotation. Paul is borrowing Israel's covenantal self-purification language and applying it to a Gentile-inclusive ekklesia, exactly the move that animates his whole ministry.
- **2 Corinthians 2:5–11** — most likely the resolution of this same case (or a similar one). Paul writes that the offender, having been disciplined, should now be forgiven and comforted, "lest he be overwhelmed by excessive sorrow." The redemptive frame holds.
- **Galatians 6:1** — "you who are spiritual" should restore "in a spirit of gentleness." Paul's discipline is never mob justice; it is community surgery, and the pastoral disposition matters.
- **1 Timothy 1:20** — Paul names Hymenaeus and Alexander as "handed over to Satan that they may learn not to blaspheme," using the same *paradidōmi tō Satana* formula. The Pastorals' application widens the procedure beyond sexual offense to doctrinal-rhetorical breach, which suggests Paul (or the Pauline tradition) understood the Corinthian act as a generalizable disciplinary form, not a one-off curse for one scandal.

What this opens onto, and what the chapter refuses to resolve, is the question of how a community sustains both holiness and mercy at once. Paul gives the Corinthians no template for readmission—that comes later, in the second extant letter, where the same offender (or a comparable one) is to be restored "lest he be overwhelmed by excessive sorrow." The chapter as it stands shows the surgery; it does not show the bandage. Maybe that is part of the point. The community that learns to act on what it confesses must also learn the harder discipline of welcoming back the ones it has had to send away. The chapter is, in the end, less about a single scandal than about whether a community can name what it actually believes and let the naming have consequences—including, eventually, the consequence of forgiveness.
