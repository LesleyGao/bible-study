# 1 Samuel 23: The Ephod, the Wilderness, and the Hand That Strengthens

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter is a topographical document as much as a theological one, and its geography is unusually exact. Keilah lies in the western Shephelah on the route between the Philistine plain and the Judean hills, identified with Khirbet Qila about seven miles south of Adullam. Iron Age remains at the site (surveyed by Yehuda Dagan and others) include defensive walls and threshing-floor installations consistent with the chapter's opening detail — Philistines raid Keilah "robbing the threshing floors" (v. 1) at harvest, the most economically vulnerable moment in the agricultural year. The verse is almost a snapshot of late 11th-century Philistine-Israelite border economics: the Philistine pentapolis cities extracted grain from Shephelah villages by seasonal raid, a pattern attested also at Beth-Shemesh and the Sorek Valley sites.

The wilderness of Ziph and Maon, where the chapter's pursuit unfolds, is the limestone karst terrain east of Hebron descending toward the Dead Sea. Ziph (Tell Zif, four miles southeast of Hebron) and Maon (Tell Main, eight miles south of Hebron) are paired in the Joshua 15 city list, and the topography between them — networks of caves, dry watercourses (*nachalim*), and the bare ridges Saul and David circle in vv. 25–26 — is exactly as described. Anson Rainey and Yohanan Aharoni mapped this zone in detail; the "rock" (*sela*) of v. 25 is plausibly one of the prominent limestone outcrops near Khirbet Main, and the "Rock of Escape" (*Sela ha-Mahleqot*, v. 28) is a topographic naming that reflects an actual landscape feature local memory preserved.

Engedi (v. 29), where the chapter ends and ch 24 opens, is the spring-fed oasis on the western shore of the Dead Sea — Tell el-Jurn and the surrounding terraces, where Iron Age remains include a substantial Iron II settlement and date-palm cultivation. The Engedi plateau and its caves are some of the most distinctive landscape features in the southern Levant; the descent from Maon to Engedi is roughly 2,000 feet over a few miles of brutal terrain. **Baruch Halpern**, in *David's Secret Demons*, has emphasized how precisely the wilderness narratives map onto the actual topography of the Hebron-Dead Sea zone, and treats this as evidence that the David-as-fugitive material rests on early, place-anchored tradition rather than late literary invention.

The Ziphites' role (vv. 19–24) is sociologically intelligible: they are Calebite/Judahite hill-country residents whose loyalty to Saul over a fellow Judahite outlaw signals that Saul's monarchy still commanded local cooperation in the Hebron region, which complicates the easy narrative of Saul as universally rejected. McCarter and Halpern both note this as a check on the dtr framing — Saul's authority was real on the ground, and David's outlaw band was genuinely outlaw to many Judahites.

Hebrew terms:

- **אֵפוֹד** (*ephod*, vv. 6, 9). The priestly garment Abiathar brought from Nob; the apparatus through which David inquires of YHWH. The chapter is the clearest narrative depiction in the Hebrew Bible of how the ephod oracle worked.
- **אוּרִים וְתֻמִּים** (*Urim and Thummim*, not named in this chapter but operative). The lots within the ephod's breastpiece (Exod 28:30); the binary mode of the question-and-answer in vv. 9–12 has long suggested to scholars (Cornelis Van Dam, *The Urim and Thummim*; Edouard Lipinski) that two stones were drawn — light and dark, yes and no.
- **חֹרְשָׁה** (*Horesh*, "wood/forest/thicket," v. 15). A specific place name in the Ziph wilderness; Jonathan finds David here, where the noun-as-place may also retain its common meaning ("at the thicket"), giving the encounter a sheltered, hidden quality.
- **חִזֵּק יָדוֹ בֵּאלֹהִים** (*chizzeq yado be-Elohim*, "strengthened his hand in God," v. 16). The exact phrase appears nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible. *Chizzeq yad* (to strengthen the hand) is idiomatic for encouragement (Judges 9:24, Jer 23:14); the addition of *be-Elohim* — "in God" — is unique here.
- **סֶלַע הַמַּחְלְקוֹת** (*Sela ha-Mahleqot*, "Rock of Divisions/Escape," v. 28). The name's etymology is debated. *Mahleqot* could mean "divisions" (Saul's and David's forces divided around the rock) or "smooth/slippery places" (the topography); the LXX's "rock that was divided" suggests the former. Place-name etiologies of this kind are characteristic of early narrative tradition (cf. Beersheba in Gen 21).
- **מְצָדוֹת** (*metsadot*, "strongholds," vv. 14, 19, 29). Plural of the same noun used at Adullam in 22:4–5; David moves from one stronghold to the next — a fugitive vocabulary that, by 2 Sam 5:7, will become royal vocabulary at Zion.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is structured as a triptych: the Keilah deliverance and abandonment (vv. 1–13), the Horesh covenant with Jonathan (vv. 14–18), and the Ziph-Maon pursuit narrowly broken (vv. 19–29). Each panel turns on the same question — will David be handed over? — and answers it differently: by oracle (Keilah), by covenant (Horesh), by topographic providence (Maon). The triptych is a meditation on the modes by which the fugitive king is preserved.

Devices: the Keilah scene is built on the most rigorously formal use of the priestly oracle in any narrative book of the Hebrew Bible. David asks four questions across vv. 2, 4, 11, 12; each gets a binary answer. The doubled inquiry in vv. 2 and 4 (David asks once, his men object, David asks again and gets the same answer with added reassurance) is striking — the oracle does not adjust for human reluctance but reiterates with a strengthening rider ("for I will give the Philistines into your hand"). The Keilah question in vv. 11–12 is even more disciplined: David asks two distinct questions — *will Saul come down?* and *will the men of Keilah surrender me?* — and the narrator preserves both as separate oracular acts. This is the procedural texture of the Urim-Thummim system; the binary lots can answer "yes" or "no" to a precise question but cannot deliver compound information, so each datum is asked separately.

The Saul-side commentary in v. 7 — "*God has shut him in*" — is dramatic irony of the highest order. Saul reads David's entry into Keilah theologically: surely God has handed David over by giving him a city with gates. The reader, equipped with the oracle of vv. 11–12, knows what Saul does not: God will hand David over only if David stays. Saul's confident theology is exactly inverted by the narrative.

The Horesh covenant (vv. 16–18) is the literary heart of the chapter. The phrase "*va-yechazzeq et yado be-Elohim*" — strengthened his hand in God — is a hapax in the form, and Polzin treats it as the narrator's most lyric line about Jonathan. Jonathan does the work of priest and prophet here: he comes from the Saulide court to the wilderness, finds David in Horesh, and renders a theological consolation that the ephod oracle cannot give. The oracle answers questions; Jonathan answers despair. He delivers a remarkable speech — "you shall be king over Israel, and I shall be next to you. Saul my father also knows this" — which is at once an abdication of his own claim, a prophecy of David's kingship, and a sociological revelation: Saul knows. The whole pursuit is conducted in the knowledge that David will succeed.

This is also the third covenant Jonathan and David make (cf. 18:3, 20:16–17). The repetition is structural: Jonathan keeps re-binding himself because the political reality keeps shifting. Robert Alter calls it "the most generous gesture in the David narrative."

The Ziph-Maon pursuit (vv. 19–29) is staged as cinema. The Ziphites' speech to Saul (vv. 19–20) is detailed reconnaissance — they specify hill, region, and intent. Saul's response (vv. 21–23) is a portrait of a king grown careful: "make yet more sure," "see and take note," "come back to me with sure information." Saul is no longer the reckless spear-thrower; he is becoming a hunter. The pivot in vv. 25–28 is breathtaking — Saul and David are on opposite sides of the same mountain, the noose closing, and a Philistine raid pulls Saul away. The narrator does not invoke YHWH in v. 27; the deliverance comes through political contingency, and the contingency is the theology. Brueggemann reads this as the dtr historian's nuance: providence is woven through historical accident, not despite it.

Source-critically, the chapter is generally assigned to the History of David's Rise (Rost), with vv. 14b ("God did not give him into his hand") as a likely dtr summary insertion. The Ziphites reappear in 26:1 and in the superscription of Psalm 54, suggesting a deep tradition. The 4QSamᵇ fragments preserve portions of the chapter in MT-aligned form; LXX shows minor variants (the doubled oracle in vv. 2, 4 is condensed slightly in some Greek manuscripts, but the structure is intact).

The chapter's place in the book's argument: this is the moment David's fugitive court achieves operational maturity. He has 600 men (up from 400 at Adullam, 22:2); he has a priest with an ephod; he has the prophetic blessing of the heir-apparent; he has a working oracle; he has tactical command of the Judean wilderness. All that remains is the throne, and the throne requires Saul's death. The chapter sets up the long inevitability that 2 Samuel 1 will resolve.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish trajectory.** Josephus (*Ant.* 6.270–286) handles the chapter with characteristic interest in geography and process. He explains the Urim-Thummim oracle in some detail (*Ant.* 3.215–218) and treats David's consultation as a model of pious leadership — David never moves militarily without divine consultation. Josephus also rehabilitates the Keilahites slightly (they would have surrendered David out of fear, not malice) and dramatizes the Maon pursuit with the rock-around-the-mountain detail.

Rabbinic readings of the chapter focus heavily on the ephod oracle (b. Yoma 73a–b; Sanhedrin 16a) and on Jonathan's covenant. The Yoma passage is the locus classicus for medieval discussion of how Urim-Thummim worked: the rabbis debate whether the letters of the high priest's breastplate (engraved with tribal names) lit up to spell answers, or whether the oracle worked by lots. Both Rashi and Radak follow the lighting-letters tradition (Rashi on Exod 28:30 is the key text). Modern scholarship, going back to Wellhausen and developed by Lipinski and Van Dam, has rejected the lighting-letters view as midrashic and treats the oracle as a binary lot system. The narrative in 1 Sam 23 supports the lot-system reading.

Rashi on v. 16 ("strengthened his hand in God") glosses Jonathan's encouragement as "spoke words of consolation that strengthened his trust in the Holy One" — the unique phrase becomes, in Rashi, the paradigm of pastoral care. Radak observes that David's escape from Maon was providential but the Philistine raid was historical — the providence works through ordinary historical events. Ralbag systematizes the chapter's theology of inquiry: David inquires, receives, acts; this is the proper relationship between human agency and divine direction.

**Abarbanel** offers a political reading of Jonathan's speech. The line "Saul my father also knows this" (v. 17) is, for Abarbanel, the decisive evidence that the Saulide regime continued the persecution against its own theological knowledge — a reading that intensifies the moral indictment of Saul without exonerating the institutional structure. Abarbanel treats the Ziphites as a study in how political clientage can corrupt tribal loyalty: fellow Judahites betray a fellow Judahite to a Benjaminite king, because patronage trumps kinship.

Modern Jewish readers (Zakovitch, Garsiel, Zornberg) attend to the Jonathan-David relationship with literary care. Zakovitch reads the three Jonathan-David covenants as a deliberate ascending scale; the Horesh covenant is the most theological because it is made "before YHWH" (v. 18) without other witness. Zornberg, in essays on Samuel, dwells on the Horesh moment as a study in the theology of friendship: Jonathan brings what the oracle cannot — companionship, recognition, the human voice that names the future without commanding it.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic readers found the ephod oracle theologically generative. Origen (in fragments on Samuel) reads the binary inquiry as a figure of the disciplined life of prayer: the Christian asks specific questions and receives specific answers. Augustine, in the *Quaestiones in Heptateuchum* and scattered sermons, treats the oracle as a feature of the old covenant superseded by the indwelling Spirit; Christians no longer cast lots because they have been given the mind of Christ.

Chrysostom and Gregory the Great read Jonathan as a type of John the Baptist — the friend of the Bridegroom who declares "he must increase but I must decrease" (John 3:30). The verbal echo with Jonathan's "you shall be king and I shall be next to you" was noticed already by Origen. Calvin's sermons on 1 Samuel develop this typology in his sermon on 1 Sam 23: Jonathan is the model of the believer who recognizes the true king and renounces his own claim. The political application — clearly relevant to Calvin's Geneva audience — is that office-holders must yield to those whom God has anointed, even at cost.

Aquinas (*ST* II-II q. 95) discusses divination and lots; he treats the Urim-Thummim as a permitted mode of inquiry under the old covenant because it was divinely instituted, while general use of lots is forbidden. The casuistic distinction matters in subsequent discussion of practices like *sortes biblicae*. Luther's lectures on Samuel emphasize Jonathan as the friend who points away from himself — typologically pre-figuring the Christian who points to Christ.

Critical scholarship: Wellhausen treated the chapter as relatively unified narrative, with vv. 14–18 (the Horesh interlude) sometimes regarded as a separate strand because Jonathan disappears from the rest of the David narrative until 1 Sam 31 only by report. McCarter (AB) defends the chapter's integrity. Klein (WBC) emphasizes the legal-procedural texture of the oracle. Brueggemann reads vv. 14b ("but God did not give him into his hand") as the dtr theological summary that frames the chapter's events as providence under apparent contingency. Polzin treats the doubled oracle (vv. 11–12) as a deuteronomistic teaching device: this is how a king should inquire — precisely, repeatedly, ready to act on the answer.

**David Gunn's *The Story of King David*** treats the chapter as a turning point in his reading: David acquires the apparatus of legitimate leadership (oracle, prophetic blessing through Jonathan, military command) without yet acquiring the throne, and this asymmetry is the chapter's structural tension. **Halpern's *David's Secret Demons*** reads the chapter politically — David is consolidating a parallel administration whose legitimacy will eventually overwhelm Saul's. Eugene Peterson (*Leap over a Wall*) reads the wilderness as the school where David learns dependence; he dwells especially on the Horesh moment and on the strengthening of the hand in God as a paradigm of pastoral encouragement.

The chapter's cast is almost exclusively male — the women of Keilah are referenced only as the city's "inhabitants," David's wives Ahinoam and Abigail (whom we meet in chs 25–27) are not yet present, and the wilderness narratives as a whole keep women offstage until ch 25 introduces Abigail. That pattern is itself part of the dtr presentation of David's rise as a story of warriors, priests, and kings.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The morality of conditional promises.** David is told the Keilahites will hand him over (v. 12), and so he leaves; the prediction is then never tested. Was the oracle's word a genuine future-fact (the Keilahites would indeed have betrayed him) or a conditional (they would have betrayed him *if* he stayed)?

*Position A (compatibilist, classical):* The oracle revealed a true future contingent — God knows what would happen under all possible scenarios (medieval *scientia media*; Molina, Aquinas in some readings). The Keilahites would have betrayed; David's flight prevented the betrayal; the oracle is descriptively true and operationally protective. This is the smoothest reading and is the one Aquinas implies in his treatment of divine knowledge.

*Position B (open-theist or providentialist):* The oracle revealed God's intention to allow betrayal if David stayed, not a fixed future. The future was genuinely open and the oracle was a warning, not a fact. The text's silence about what would have happened is then deliberate — the oracle's purpose was guidance, not metaphysics.

*Position C (literary-narrative):* The text does not commit to either metaphysics. The oracle says, "they will surrender you," and David acts. The narrator records what David is told and what David does; the metaphysics of counterfactuals is not the text's interest. Brueggemann would press this: the dtr historian is interested in obedience, not in the modal logic of divine foreknowledge.

*Where this leaves us:* The compatibilist reading is theologically tidy but reads later philosophical categories into a narrative that does not invite them. The open-theist reading honors the ambiguity but introduces a metaphysical commitment the text does not make. The literary reading is most disciplined but least pastorally helpful. Methodologically the literary reading is best for exegesis; for theology, the compatibilist reading remains the most coherent system, though it must acknowledge it is doing work the text leaves undone.

**Tension 2: The role of friendship in election.** Jonathan strengthens David's hand in God (v. 16). The oracle gives information; Jonathan gives *consolation*. What is the theological function of friendship in the divine economy?

*Position A (instrumental):* Friendship is one of God's ordinary means by which divine purposes are accomplished. Jonathan is an instrument; the strengthening is God's work mediated through human relationship. This is Calvin's reading and the dominant Reformed position — God uses people as means without thereby making the means autonomous.

*Position B (incarnational/relational):* Friendship is not merely instrumental but is itself a mode of divine presence. Jonathan does what no oracle could; the human voice carries something that the institutional apparatus does not. The text's unique phrasing ("strengthened his hand in God") suggests the divine work happens *in* the friendship, not merely *through* it. Eugene Peterson and pastoral-theological traditions develop this reading.

*Position C (covenantal-political):* Jonathan's act is a public covenant before YHWH (v. 18), not a private consolation. The function is institutional — Jonathan publicly transfers the legitimate succession to David, and this transfer is a divinely-witnessed act of state. Halpern and the political readers emphasize this; the Horesh covenant is the constitutional foundation of David's later kingship.

*Where this leaves us:* All three readings can stand, and the chapter rewards holding them together. The instrumental reading honors divine sovereignty; the incarnational reading honors human depth; the covenantal reading honors political reality. Methodologically I am drawn to a layered reading: the moment is institutionally a covenant, relationally a consolation, and theologically a means of providence — the layers are the same act.

**Unresolved question:** Why does the oracle never tell David where to go next? David asks whether to go to Keilah (yes), whether Saul will come (yes), whether the Keilahites will surrender him (yes). He does not ask, "where should I go?" — he simply leaves and "goes wherever he can go" (v. 13). Either the oracle does not answer such open questions (consistent with the binary lot-system reading), or David does not ask. The chapter does not resolve which.

## The Thread

Cross-references: **Exodus 28:30** (the Urim and Thummim in the high priest's breastpiece — the apparatus operative in this chapter); **1 Samuel 30:7–8** (David inquires of the ephod at Ziklag, the procedural twin of this chapter); **Psalm 54** (superscribed "when the Ziphites came and said to Saul, 'Is not David hidden among us?'" — the chapter's events become the psalmic occasion); **2 Samuel 9** (David's later care for Mephibosheth, fulfilling the Jonathan covenant of Horesh and the earlier covenants).

Flag forward: chapter 24's Engedi cave encounter follows directly from v. 29's note that David moves to the strongholds of Engedi; chapter 26's second Ziphite betrayal is a deliberate doublet to vv. 19–24 here, which raises the source-critical question that ch 24 will sharpen — are chs 24 and 26 two reports of one event or two genuinely distinct episodes? Chapter 30's ephod inquiry at Ziklag completes the tradition this chapter inaugurates: the ephod oracle is operational, and David's kingship will be built on disciplined inquiry. The chapter's deepest irony — Saul's confident "God has shut him in" (v. 7) inverted by the same God who opens the gate — runs forward through the rest of the David narrative; the king who reads providence wrong loses, and the king who asks the right binary questions wins.
