# Joshua 8: The Ruin and the Stones

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Joshua 8 occupies the central Benjaminite plateau, a limestone ridge of narrow wadis and terraced slopes running north from Jerusalem. The narrative names three loci — Ai, Bethel, and the distant peaks of Ebal and Gerizim in the Samarian heartland — and the geography is not incidental. Control of the Benjamin saddle was control of the north-south road and the lateral descents to the Jordan Valley and the coastal plain; every later Israelite and Judahite kingdom would fight for this ridge. If the book describes Late Bronze II or early Iron I (roughly 1400–1150 BCE depending on which conquest chronology one adopts), the political situation is one of decaying Egyptian hegemony, small Canaanite city-states, and pressure from population movements the Egyptians called Shasu and Apiru. The Amarna letters (EA 287, 289) show the king of Jerusalem, Abdi-Heba, already complaining about Apiru raiders and the loss of fortified towns in precisely this region in the fourteenth century.

Two dating models frame everything. The high chronology (c. 1406 BCE) reads the 480 years of 1 Kings 6:1 literally back from Solomon; the low chronology (c. 1200–1150 BCE) aligns the conquest with the collapse of Late Bronze Age urbanism and the appearance of the highland village cluster documented by Finkelstein's *The Archaeology of the Israelite Settlement* (1988). The Merneptah Stele (1208 BCE) mentions "Israel" as a people group already in Canaan — the terminus ante quem everyone agrees on.

Ai is the chapter's archaeological scandal. The Hebrew name is *hāʿay* (הָעַי), literally "the ruin" — a name containing its own theological joke, since the city is destined to become what it is called. Traditional identification with et-Tell (about 2 km east of Bethel/Beitin) runs into Joseph Callaway's 1960s excavations, which showed et-Tell unoccupied between roughly 2400 and 1200 BCE, spanning every date available for Joshua. The interpretive options are sharply drawn: Albright proposed Ai is a literary transposition of the fall of Bethel; Noth read the narrative as etiology for an existing heap of stones; Bryant Wood has argued since the 1990s that the real Ai is Khirbet el-Maqatir, a smaller fortified site with Late Bronze destruction. Most academic consensus remains with Callaway's negative result; Hawk and other literary readers take the text as symbolic regardless.

Three Hebrew terms reward attention. *ʾōrēv* (אֹרֵב), "ambush," appears repeatedly in verses 2, 4, 7, 9, 12, 14, 19 — the root denotes lying in wait, and the density is unusual for Joshua, where most battles are miraculous. The chapter reads like an ancient military treatise: feigned retreat, flanking force, signal by raised weapon. *kîdôn* (כִּידוֹן) in verse 18, the "javelin" Joshua stretches out, is the same word the War Scroll (1QM) uses for the eschatological army's weapon; the gesture parallels Moses' raised staff at the Red Sea and at Rephidim, binding Joshua to Moses by image. *ḥērem* (חֵרֶם), "devoted to destruction" (v. 26), carries a density English cannot hold — to cut off, to consecrate irreversibly (Arabic *ḥaram* = sanctuary); simultaneously annihilation and sacralization.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is composite in form. Verses 1–29 are a battle report in the style of an ancient Near Eastern royal campaign inscription; verses 30–35 are a covenant-renewal liturgy. The seam is palpable, and it is part of why textual-critical questions cluster here. The battle narrative itself divides into a commission oracle (1–2), deployment (3–13), feigned retreat and ambush (14–23), total destruction (24–29). The Ebal pericope follows its own cultic logic: altar, copy of the law, blessing and curse, assembly.

Literary devices thicken. The *inclusio* of Joshua's outstretched hand — "do not draw back" (v. 26) — mirrors Moses' hands held up by Aaron and Hur in Exodus 17:11–12, where Israel prevails only while the hands stay raised. The city-as-smoke motif (v. 20–21) becomes a conventional prophetic image (Isa 30:27; Joel 2:30). The contrast with Joshua 7 is deliberate: the first Ai campaign failed because of Achan's secret theft of *ḥērem*; the second succeeds because the camp is purged. Read together, chapters 7–8 are a diptych on covenant loyalty — internal sin before external threat.

The chapter sits at a literary hinge in the book's argument. Joshua 1–5 moves Israel across the Jordan; 6–8 secures the central hill country (Jericho, Achan, Ai); 9–12 spreads outward into southern and northern coalitions; 13–21 allocates the land; 22–24 addresses the covenant's future. Joshua 8 is the first battle Israel fights as a full army using ordinary tactics rather than miracle (priests, horns, and walls at Jericho); it is the chapter where Joshua becomes a general rather than only a cult leader. Then, without warning, the narrative stops and turns the army into a congregation standing on two mountains for a liturgical reading of Torah. The placement is a claim: the conquest and the covenant are one action, and warfare without covenant is simply war.

Textual variants are unusually significant. The LXX places the Ebal altar pericope (8:30–35 in MT) after 9:2 — a different ordering of the same material. More striking, 4QJoshua-a (4Q47), one of the oldest extant Joshua manuscripts, dating to the late second century BCE, places the Ebal altar construction *immediately after the Jordan crossing, before Jericho*. Eugene Ulrich (the manuscript's first editor) and Emanuel Tov have argued that 4QJoshua-a may preserve an earlier literary edition of Joshua in which the covenant ceremony was Israel's inaugural act in the land, not an interruption mid-conquest. If Ulrich is right, MT represents a later editorial move that subordinates liturgy to campaign. The Samaritan Pentateuch — which knows a version of Deuteronomy 27 — places the altar on Gerizim (their sacred mountain) rather than Ebal, a polemic that survives into John 4 when the Samaritan woman tells Jesus "our fathers worshiped on this mountain."

Genre-wise, Richard Nelson's *Joshua* (OTL 1997) classifies the narrative as "holy war narrative with etiological coloring," while Trent Butler (WBC) reads it as "conquest story shaped for covenant catechesis." Thomas Dozeman's more recent commentary (AYB 2015) foregrounds the text as Deuteronomistic propaganda for a centralized cult. All three agree that the final form is crafted, not stenographic.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc moves through Joshua 8 on two tracks. Forward, the chapter anticipates every later Deuteronomistic judgment on Israel's kings — success follows covenant fidelity, failure follows its breach. Backward, Ebal fulfills Deut 11:29 and 27:2–8 word for word; the altar of uncut stones obeys Exod 20:25; Joshua's raised hand re-performs Moses' raised staff; even the hanging of the king of Ai and the pile of stones at the gate (v. 29) fulfill Deut 21:22–23. The chapter is written in the grammar of earlier texts.

**Jewish trajectory.** In Second Temple reception, Qumran's 4Q378–379 (*Psalms of Joshua*) recast Joshua as a prophetic figure whose prayers and exhortations rival Moses; fragments paraphrase the covenant-curse liturgy of Joshua 8:30–35. Philo of Alexandria reads the entire Joshua narrative allegorically as the soul's progress against its passions (*Migr. Abr.*). Josephus (*Ant.* 5.1.17) smooths the Ai battle into a straightforward military tale but preserves the Ebal ceremony as a public covenant oath. Ben Sira 46:1–6 praises Joshua but says little of the altar. Rabbinic tradition shows fascinating discomfort: *Mishnah Sotah* 7:5 and *b. Sotah* 32a–36a give a detailed choreography of the Ebal/Gerizim ceremony — the Levites turning toward each mountain to pronounce blessing and curse — and solve a chronological problem (how did Israel march from the Jordan to Shechem, perform the ceremony, and return?) by saying they did it all miraculously on a single day. Rashi (eleventh century) takes the text literally and defends the expedition. Ibn Ezra (twelfth century) notes the geographical difficulty more soberly. Ramban (Nahmanides, thirteenth century) reads the altar and the inscribed Torah as a prophetic sign-act for the nations, written in seventy languages (*y. Sotah* 7:5). Radak (David Kimhi) attends carefully to the verb forms; Abarbanel, writing after the Spanish expulsion, reads the covenant ceremony as a pattern for Jewish survival through ritual memory rather than political power. Maimonides (*Guide* 3.41) uses Joshua's execution of the king of Ai to defend public punishment as deterrent rather than vengeance.

Modern Jewish readings diverge. Martin Buber in *On the Bible* reads the Ebal ceremony as a paradigmatic moment of the "dialogical covenant" in which the people become a "you" addressed by God. Jon Levenson (*Sinai and Zion*) places Joshua 8 in a tradition that refuses to separate land from covenant: the mountains make the liturgy possible, and the liturgy makes the land inhabitable. Moshe Weinfeld (*Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School*) treats the Ebal ceremony as the literary template for all later Deuteronomistic covenant renewals. Baruch Halpern (*The First Historians*) reads the Ai narrative as a literary construction by a historian working from etiological sources. Nahum Sarna stresses the altar of uncut stones as a theological polemic against Canaanite worked-stone altars. Avivah Zornberg and Rachel Havrelock bring fresh attention to the chapter's violence and to the curious silence of women until they appear at the end as hearers of Torah (v. 35).

**Christian trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Joshua* (c. 240 CE) are the foundational Christian reading: every battle is allegorized. Ai becomes the vices that survive the initial victory of baptism (Jericho); the ambush is the hidden work of grace; the two mountains are the two covenants. Jerome, translating the Vulgate, worries over the numbers (30,000 troops becomes a troubled footnote) and preserves *insidiae* for *ʾōrēv*. Augustine, in *Quaestiones in Heptateuchum* 6.10, famously defends the ambush as a lawful act of God-commanded war — it is not the lie of a private person but a divinely authorized ruse — a reading that later informs just-war theory. Chrysostom preaches the chapter as a moral allegory of vigilance. Theodoret of Cyrus, commenting on the altar of uncut stones, says the prohibition of iron prefigures grace without human additions.

Medieval reception continued the allegorical grammar. The *Glossa Ordinaria* harmonizes Origen and Augustine. Aquinas (*Summa* II-II q. 40 a. 3) cites Augustine on Joshua's ambush to distinguish *stratagema* (lawful deception of an enemy) from lying. Reformation readings pivot to covenant and the written word. Luther, in his lectures, reads the Ebal inscription of the law as Scripture's public authority over the gathered people. Calvin (*Commentary on Joshua*, 1564 — his last commentary, completed weeks before his death) treats the altar and the law-reading as emblem of the whole Reformed program: *Verbum Dei audiatur*, the Word of God heard by the whole congregation. Menno Simons and the Anabaptist tradition took a different road — unable to assimilate the slaughter of Ai to the ethic of Jesus, they read the chapter figuratively only, and the Schleitheim Confession (1527) quietly placed Joshua's violence outside Christian imitation.

Critical Christian scholarship re-framed the terrain. Noth's *Das System der zwölf Stämme Israels* (1930) and *Überlieferungsgeschichtliche Studien* (1943) developed the Deuteronomistic History hypothesis: Joshua through 2 Kings are a single redactional work reflecting the Judahite crisis of 587 BCE. Cross (1973) refined this into a dual redaction (Josianic and exilic). Von Rad treated the holy-war language of Joshua as a preserved tradition from the Israelite tribal league. Brevard Childs (*Introduction to the Old Testament as Scripture*, 1979) recovered canonical coherence. Nelson, Dozeman, and Butler carry the redactional conversation forward.

Contemporary theological and postcolonial readings press the text's ethical difficulty. Walter Brueggemann reads Joshua 8 as a text about "the sanctioned violence" that the canon itself requires the reader to question. N.T. Wright and John Goldingay argue for Joshua's narrative as historical-but-rhetorical; Richard Hess and Gordon Wenham defend a modified historicity; David Howard reads Joshua with the grain of conservative evangelical scholarship. Terence Fretheim foregrounds divine vulnerability and the costs of partnership with Israel. Ellen Davis places the Ebal ceremony as a scriptural performance of "reading together" — Torah as public, communal, embodied. Daniel Hawk (*Every Promise Fulfilled*) reads the Ai narrative as a theological meditation on Israel's *own* identity as the ruin — a people almost destroyed, standing on the ashes of their failures. Lori Rowlett (*Joshua and the Rhetoric of Violence*, 1996) analyzes the text's ideology of enemy construction. And the history-of-effects critique — Regina Schwartz's *The Curse of Cain*, John Collins, and the broader scholarship on the reception of the conquest texts — reads Joshua *from the side of Ai*, the ruin, and traces how the narrative has been wielded as warrant for later conquest and dispossession.

Where the traditions converge is on the inseparability of covenant and conquest: the Jewish reader, the patristic Christian, and the critical scholar all see that Joshua 8 refuses to tell the battle without the altar. Where they diverge is on what that inseparability *means*. For the rabbis, covenant sanctifies land. For Origen and Luther, the battle becomes a figure for a spiritual struggle, and the literal violence recedes. For the honest-critical reading (Brueggemann, Schwartz, Collins), the intertwining is precisely the problem: any reading that sanctifies conquest by covenant has, in its history of effects, been turned against real peoples.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What is God doing? In the surface grammar of the text, God is doing three things at once: delivering the land, validating Joshua as Moses' successor, and binding Israel to Torah by public oath on two mountains. All three happen inside the same chapter, and the narrative refuses to separate them.

**Tension 1: Divinely commanded ambush — ethics of sacred war.** The text is startling because it explicitly has God *commanding a deception* (v. 2, "Lay an ambush against the city"). This is not a human stratagem; it is divine strategy. The positions on what this means differ sharply.

The Catholic reading (CCC §2313–2317 on the ethics of war, and Aquinas *Summa* II-II q. 40) distinguishes ambush from lying: deception of a combatant in a just war is not forbidden, and Joshua 8 is a leading proof-text. Modern Catholic just-war theorists (Finnis, Rhonheimer) extend this carefully. The Reformed tradition (Calvin, Turretin) agrees: the divine command makes the act righteous, though Calvin is uneasy about the extension to women and children (v. 25). Lutherans (following the *Formula of Concord* and Luther's own "Temporal Authority") argue that God uses the sword of magistrate and army, and Joshua's ambush belongs to God's left-hand kingdom, which operates by justice rather than gospel; the cross does not yet constrain it. Wesleyans affirm the command but emphasize progressive revelation — what was lawful under the old covenant is now constrained by the ethic of Jesus. The Anabaptist response (Menno Simons, and twentieth-century figures like John Howard Yoder and Stanley Hauerwas) is that the Christian simply cannot imitate Joshua; these texts are descriptive of pre-Christic Israel and cannot be applied.

Orthodox readings (John of Damascus, and modern theologians like David Bentley Hart) press the typological: Joshua is a type of Christ only in what *prefigures* salvation, not in what the atonement abolishes. Jewish rabbinic tradition (*b. Sanhedrin* 72a) generalizes from Joshua's tactic to a principle that applies in life-and-death threat: deception to save life is not only permitted but required. Critical scholarship (Niditch, *War in the Hebrew Bible*) reads the chapter as reflecting the normal ethics of Late Bronze Age warfare and warns against either sanitizing it or absolutizing it. Paul Copan and Matthew Flannagan (*Did God Really Command Genocide?*, 2014) argue the herem language is hyperbolic ANE war rhetoric. Eric Seibert (*Disturbing Divine Behavior*, 2009), Jerome Creach (*Violence in Scripture*), and John J. Collins (*Does the Bible Justify Violence?*) push back: the texts should not be softened, and Christian readers must own the moral weight. Douglas Earl (*The Joshua Delusion*) argues the entire book is a figurative meditation on fidelity, never a divine command to imitate literal conquest. Steelman the least intuitive position — the Anabaptist: if the crucified Jesus is the full self-disclosure of God, then any reading of Joshua 8 that lets us pick up a sword has mistaken what God was finally doing all along. The conquest narratives are preserved in the canon precisely so the cross can judge them.

Where this leaves us: the text will not let us either sanctify violence or exile it from the Bible. Whatever reading we adopt will be tested in how we handle the real Ais our own traditions have made.

**Tension 2: Covenant on two mountains — blessing and curse.** In verses 30–35, Israel stands on Ebal (curse) and Gerizim (blessing) and hears Torah read. This is the text's positive vision, and the theological positions cluster here too.

The Catholic reading (Ratzinger, *God and the World*, and the CCC on the covenant) treats the Ebal ceremony as anticipation of the Church's liturgical reading of Scripture: Word, sign, people, place. Reformed theology (Calvin, Kline, Horton) reads the two-mountain ceremony as covenant-treaty in classic ANE form, with the whole people pledged as suzerain-vassal to YHWH — and the curses of Ebal fall on Christ at Golgotha, a typology Paul draws in Gal 3:10–13 ("cursed is everyone who hangs on a tree" — the same phrase that governs Josh 8:29). Lutheran theology holds to law-gospel distinction: Ebal is law (curse), Gerizim is gospel (blessing), and the two must be held together without conflation; Joshua 8 is where this structure becomes visible liturgy. Wesleyans stress the possibility of real human obedience to covenant, not only forensic imputation. Anabaptists read the Ebal altar — uncut stones, no iron — as a sign that God's worship refuses human technological pride, and they extend the principle to churches without political power. Orthodox tradition reads the two mountains as the double grace of choice: God honors human freedom by making blessing and curse genuine alternatives. Jewish rabbinic tradition (*b. Sotah* 37b–38a) treats the ceremony as the constitutive moment of Israel's national covenant — each Israelite has personally sworn it, and the sages debate whether the oath is transferable to later generations (Ramban says yes; others say each generation renews). Critical scholarship (Weinfeld, Levenson) sees the ceremony as the literary ur-form of Deuteronomistic covenant theology. Hess and Nelson agree the form is treaty-shaped, borrowing Hittite suzerainty-treaty structure.

The least intuitive position here is the honest-critical one (Brueggemann, Schwartz): the two-mountain ceremony is constitutive of Israel precisely by *excluding* the land's prior inhabitants, and the blessing/curse structure is always also a structure of who counts and who does not. Steelmanning this: every covenant-community forms itself by drawing a boundary, and the honest reader of Joshua 8 has to face what the text does not say — the Canaanites do not stand on either mountain. To read the ceremony as only about Israel's piety is to miss what the geography itself is doing. (The text complicates this itself: v. 33 and v. 35 place "the sojourner as well as the native-born" among those who hear the Torah.)

Where this leaves us: the chapter offers blessing and curse as real, and it offers them in a particular place under a particular sky, inscribed on real stones. The theological question it sets is whether any human community can be formed without exclusion, or whether exclusion is so fused with formation that only a crucified king (or a Torah heard by sojourner and native alike, v. 33 and v. 35) can loosen the knot.

One unresolved question: if 4QJoshua-a preserves an earlier literary shape in which the altar and the Torah-reading precede Jericho — that is, if the original sequence is *covenant before conquest* rather than *conquest interrupted by covenant* — what does it mean that the canonical editors chose the latter, and does their choice make the conquest narrative more defensible or less?

## The Thread

**Deuteronomy 27:1–8** — The direct source for Joshua 8:30–35. Moses commands the altar on Ebal with uncut stones and the inscription of the Torah; Joshua 8 executes it to the letter. Reading them together shows how the book of Joshua functions as Deuteronomy's obedient afterlife.

**Exodus 17:8–13** — Moses' raised hands defeat Amalek while Aaron and Hur hold them up. Joshua's stretched-out javelin until the destruction is complete (Josh 8:26) rewrites this scene with Joshua in Moses' posture — a literary device that installs Joshua as Moses' full successor.

**Galatians 3:10–13** — Paul quotes Deut 21:23 ("cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree"), the same statute that governs the body of the king of Ai (Josh 8:29). Paul reads the curse typologically onto Christ, and the reader who follows the thread finds that Joshua's Ebal/curse gives Paul his grammar for Golgotha.

**John 4:19–24** — The Samaritan woman at the well raises the two-mountain question: "Our fathers worshiped on this mountain" (Gerizim). The ancient rivalry over whether altar and Torah belong on Ebal or Gerizim surfaces a thousand years later in a conversation Jesus uses to say the hour is coming when true worshipers will worship in spirit and in truth — a Johannine relativization of both mountains that depends on Joshua 8 for its force.
