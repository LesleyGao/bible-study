# John 3: Born from Above — Nicodemus, the World, and the Lifting Up

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Nicodemus comes by night, and the night is doing work in the text. In the Mediterranean honor-shame culture of first-century Judea — on which Bruce Malina, Jerome Neyrey, and Kenneth Bailey have written extensively — a public figure visiting a controversial teacher under cover of darkness is signaling caution about reputation. But the narrator is also doing his Johannine work: night versus day, light versus darkness, hidden versus revealed are the binaries the prologue established (1:5, 1:9) and that Culpepper's *Anatomy of the Fourth Gospel* identifies as the Gospel's structuring symbolism.

Nicodemus is identified as "a man of the Pharisees" and "a ruler (*archōn*, ἄρχων) of the Jews" (3:1) — most plausibly a member of the Sanhedrin, the seventy-one-member ruling council under the high priest. His name is Greek (Nikodēmos = "victory of the people"), but Greek names were common among first-century Judean elite (Andrew, Philip, and Bartholomew himself in the apostolic band). Rabbinic literature mentions a Naqdimon ben Gurion, a wealthy Jerusalem aristocrat from the late Second Temple period (b. Ta'anit 19b-20a; b. Gittin 56a), and some scholars (Brown, Bauckham) have wondered whether the historical figures might overlap; Bauckham's *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* takes the connection as plausible if uncertain.

**Aenon near Salim** (3:23), where John is still baptizing, is a contested site. The two main candidates are: (1) a location in northern Samaria near Tell Shalem in the Beit She'an valley (Eusebius's *Onomasticon* placed it eight Roman miles south of Scythopolis); (2) an Ainun spring near Salim in the Wadi Far'a east of Shechem. The detail "because water was plentiful there" (*hydata polla*, ὕδατα πολλά) is telling — both candidates have abundant springs, distinguishing them from sites along the Jordan River where John had baptized earlier. Rainer Riesner's *Bethanien jenseits des Jordans* and the broader recent archaeology of Baptist sites have not resolved the question.

The chapter's most consequential historical-theological background is the **Numbers 21:4-9 bronze serpent** to which Jesus refers in 3:14. Hezekiah's reform (2 Kings 18:4) destroyed the bronze serpent (*Nehushtan*) because Israel had been burning incense to it; later Jewish reflection (Wisdom of Solomon 16:5-7; Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Numbers 21:8-9) read the serpent not as a magical object but as a sign that turned the looking heart toward God. Jesus's typology — "as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up" — picks up this trajectory.

Greek terms in the chapter:
- ***Anōthen*** (ἄνωθεν, 3:3, 3:7) — "from above" or "again" — the central pun of the chapter. The same word means both. Nicodemus hears "again"; Jesus means both.
- ***Pneuma*** (πνεῦμα, 3:5-8) — "spirit" and "wind" — another Johannine pun: the Spirit blows like wind.
- ***Hypsoō*** (ὑψόω, "lift up") in 3:14 — the Johannine technical term for crucifixion-as-glorification (recurring in 8:28, 12:32-34).
- ***Monogenēs*** (μονογενής) in 3:16, 3:18 — "only-begotten" or "one and only" — central to Nicene Christology.
- ***Kosmos*** (κόσμος, "world") — used six times in 3:16-19 — Johannine ambivalent term: God loves the *kosmos* yet the *kosmos* loves darkness.
- ***Krisis*** (κρίσις, "judgment") in 3:19 — both verdict and crisis-of-decision.
- ***Pisteuō*** (πιστεύω, "believe / trust") — used eight times in chapter 3 — the verb that defines Johannine soteriology, which (notably) lacks the noun *pistis* (faith) that Paul makes central.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Where does Jesus's speech end and the narrator's commentary begin? The Greek of John has no quotation marks, and the boundary in chapter 3 is genuinely ambiguous. Most contemporary editors (NA28, UBS5, ESV) end Jesus's discourse at 3:15 and treat 3:16-21 as the narrator's theological reflection. Brown's *Anchor Bible* commentary, Schnackenburg, and Beasley-Murray defend this reading; Carson's *PNTC* and Köstenberger's *BECNT* defend continuing Jesus's speech through 3:21. The same question recurs at 3:31-36: are these John the Baptist's words or the narrator's? The ambiguity is itself characteristic — the Fourth Gospel speaks with a single integrated voice, and the boundary between Jesus's voice, the Baptist's voice, and the narrator's voice is porous by design. R. Alan Culpepper, Mark Stibbe, and Gail O'Day's *NIB* commentary all treat this as a Johannine literary feature, not a textual problem.

The **misunderstanding** device — programmatic in John, foundational to Brown's reading — appears here in its most famous form. Nicodemus hears *anōthen* as "again" (3:4: "Can he enter a second time into his mother's womb?"); Jesus means primarily "from above" but lets the double-meaning stand. Brown, Bultmann, and Schnackenburg all treat 3:3-7 as the paradigm case of the Johannine misunderstanding-and-clarification structure: a saying with double-meaning, an interlocutor's literal misreading, Jesus's expansive clarification. The structure recurs in chapter 4 (living water), chapter 6 (bread from heaven), chapter 8 (going where you cannot come), chapter 11 (Lazarus is asleep), chapter 13 (where I go you cannot follow), chapter 14 (we do not know the way).

The **double-amēn** (*amēn amēn*, ἀμὴν ἀμήν, "truly, truly") appears three times in chapter 3 (3:3, 3:5, 3:11) — a Johannine signature unattested in the synoptics. The synoptics use single *amēn* to introduce solemn sayings; John doubles it, twenty-five times across the Gospel. The likely background is Jewish liturgical practice (the *amēn* response in synagogue worship) intensified for solemn pronouncement.

The chapter contains **John 3:16** — "the most-cited verse in Christian history," as the chapter notes call it. Brown's *Anchor Bible* commentary observes that the verse's grammar — "*houtōs ēgapēsen*" (οὕτως ἠγάπησεν, "so loved") — is more accurately rendered "in this way" than as an intensifier ("so much"). The construction is *houtōs ... hōste* ("in such a way that..."), specifying the manner of God's love (the giving of the Son) rather than the magnitude. Most contemporary translations (NRSV, ESV, NIV) preserve the traditional "so loved" rendering for canonical reasons, but commentators consistently note the manner-not-magnitude grammar.

Textual variants in the chapter are notable. **3:13** has a major variant: many later witnesses add "who is in heaven" after "the Son of Man," a Christological heightening that asserts the Son's continuous heavenly presence even during incarnation. NA28 and UBS5 omit this addition. **3:15** has variants between *en autō* and *eis auton* ("in him" vs. "into him") for the object of belief. **3:31** has a variant in the second occurrence of "is above all" — some witnesses omit it, possibly as redundant.

The **structure of Brown's seven-signs schema** is built around discourse-and-sign pairs. Cana sign (chapter 2) is paired with the Nicodemus discourse on rebirth (chapter 3) — sign and discourse together unfold the meaning of the new creation. The chapter is also part of Brown's "Cana to Cana" narrative arc (2:1-4:54), framed by two Galilee signs at Cana, with the central material (chapters 3-4) showing the response to Jesus's revelation across three representative figures: Nicodemus (Pharisaic Judaism), the Samaritan woman (Samaritanism), and the royal official (a probably-Gentile imperial official) — a programmatic sequence widening the circle of belief.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish trajectory.** The "born again / from above" language has a deeper Jewish background than is often recognized. **Daniel Boyarin's** *Jewish Gospels* and **Adele Reinhartz's** *Cast Out of the Covenant* both warn against reading 3:3-10 supersessionistically — as if Nicodemus represents a "Jewish" inability to grasp "Christian" rebirth. Rabbinic tradition uses rebirth language for proselyte conversion: "a proselyte who has just converted is like a newborn child" (b. Yevamot 22a, 48b, 62a; b. Bekhorot 47a). The image of a Pharisaic teacher being told *he* must undergo rebirth is sharp not because rebirth is alien to Jewish thought but because it is normally for Gentile converts; Jesus is saying the established Pharisaic teacher must enter as if he were entering Israel for the first time. **Jacob Neusner's** rabbinic-tradition scholarship, **Marianne Meye Thompson's** *NTL*, and **Levine's** *Misunderstood Jew* all treat this background.

The bronze serpent typology of 3:14-15 has rich Jewish-interpretive history. **Wisdom of Solomon 16:6-7** — "they were troubled for a small moment, that they might have a sign of salvation, to put them in remembrance of the commandment of thy law. For he that turned himself toward it was not saved by the thing that he saw, but by thee, that art the Saviour of all" — already moves the meaning from the object to the act of turning. Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Numbers 21:8-9 reads: "whoever lifted up his face in prayer to his Father in heaven was healed." Jesus's typology assumes this interpretive trajectory. **Charlesworth** and **Mary Coloe** on the Qumran-Johannine background note that the **light/darkness dualism** of 3:19-21 echoes 1QS 3:13-4:26 ("two spirits / two ways") — sons of light walk in light; sons of darkness in darkness.

**John Ashton's** *Understanding the Fourth Gospel* (2nd ed. 2007) reads chapter 3's revelation discourse through Jewish apocalyptic — the *Son of Man* of 3:13-14, the ascent-and-descent of 3:13, the heavenly things vs. earthly things of 3:12 are all framed by Jewish apocalyptic texts (1 Enoch's Son of Man; Daniel 7's "one like a son of man"; the heavenly-ascent traditions in 2 Enoch and the Apocalypse of Abraham).

**Christian patristic trajectory.** **Origen's** *Commentary on John* (book 13 treats parts of chapter 3) reads the rebirth as the soul's transformation through the indwelling Word, with characteristic Origenist universalism — "the world" of 3:16 is universally embraced by divine love. **Augustine's** *Tractates on the Gospel of John* (tractates 11-14) treats the Nicodemus discourse foundationally; Augustine's reading of "born of water and the Spirit" (3:5) as baptismal regeneration becomes the standard Western reading and is canonized at the Council of Trent. **Cyril of Alexandria's** *Commentary on John* and **John Chrysostom's** *Homilies* (24-29 on John) develop the same baptismal reading. **Aquinas's** *Lectura super Joannem* synthesizes this tradition in scholastic form.

The **bronze-serpent typology** (3:14-15) becomes a centerpiece of patristic Christology. **Justin Martyr's** *Dialogue with Trypho* uses the typology against Trypho. **Irenaeus's** *Against Heresies* invokes it. **Athanasius's** *On the Incarnation* uses it as a primary type of crucified salvation. **Augustine** preached on it repeatedly. In medieval art and devotion, the lifting up of the serpent becomes one of the standard typological pairings with the crucifixion.

**Reformation.** **Calvin's *Commentary on John*** reads the Nicodemus discourse as the foundation of Reformed soteriology: regeneration is monergistic, sovereign divine work; "you must be born again" cannot mean self-effort but divine birth. Calvin's *Institutes* 3.3 develops the full doctrine. **Luther's** sermons on John 3:16 — "the gospel in miniature" — establish the verse as the core Reformation declaration: divine love, divine gift, faith alone, eternal life. The "born again" language becomes, especially in subsequent Anglo-American evangelicalism, a brand identifier (George Whitefield, the First Great Awakening, the modern evangelical "born-again Christian"). **Westminster Larger Catechism** Q. 67-68 develops the Reformed doctrine of effectual calling and regeneration with chapter 3 as locus.

**Mystical and Eastern Orthodox.** **Eastern Orthodox** soteriology reads 3:5 within the triple sacramental initiation (baptism, chrismation, Eucharist), with regeneration as the beginning of *theōsis* — the believer's incorporation into the Trinitarian life. **Bernard of Clairvaux** preached on John 3:16 as the inaugural text of divine love. **Eckhart's** mysticism reads "born from above" as the eternal birth of the Word in the soul. **John of the Cross** treats the *anōthen* birth as the dark night's transformation.

**Twentieth century commentaries** all engage chapter 3 substantially: **Brown** *Anchor Bible*, **Bultmann's** *Gospel of John* (1941, foundational existentialist reading: rebirth is decision-faith), **Dodd's** *Interpretation* (chapter 3 as foundational for "realized eschatology"), **Barrett**, **Schnackenburg**, **Haenchen**, **Beasley-Murray**, **Köstenberger**, **Carson**, **Keener**, **Thompson**, **Moloney**, **Brant**, **O'Day**, **Newbigin's** *The Light Has Come* (chapter 3 as the missional foundation: cosmic divine love sending Son into world). **Karl Barth's** *Church Dogmatics* IV/4 on baptism engages 3:5 substantially; **von Balthasar's** *Theo-Drama* treats the lifting-up Christology as central to Johannine *theologia crucis*.

**N.T. Wright** (*John for Everyone*; *How God Became King*) reads the Nicodemus dialogue as Jesus telling Israel's teacher that the renewal Israel longed for is not a tweak to the existing system but a new creation — "born from above" means being remade by God's Spirit, the Ezekiel-36 "new heart" promise enacted — and the "lifting up" of 3:14 ties that new birth directly to the cross: it is the crucified-and-exalted Son through whom heaven's life is given. Wright also stresses, against an other-worldly misreading, that "eternal life" in John is the life of God's coming age breaking into the present, not merely post-mortem survival. **Brown** and **Köstenberger** likewise track Nicodemus as a recurring figure — sympathetic but uncomprehending — who reappears in 7:50-51 defending Jesus procedurally and in 19:39 bringing burial spices.

**Adele Reinhartz** in *Cast Out of the Covenant* engages 3:16-21 with care: the universalism of "God so loved the world" is in tension with the dualism of 3:18-19, where "whoever does not believe is condemned already" and "people loved the darkness rather than the light." This light/darkness binary, she argues, has been weaponized in Christian polemic against Jews. The text is not anti-Jewish in its plain meaning — Jesus and Nicodemus are both Jews — but its reception history has been deeply anti-Jewish.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension one: what is regeneration, and how does it happen?** "Unless one is born of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God" (3:5).

The **Catholic** tradition reads this as foundational for baptismal regeneration: water is the sacramental sign, the Spirit the inner reality, and baptism objectively causes the new birth (Council of Trent, Session 7, Canons on Baptism; *CCC* 1213-1284). Infant baptism is the normative practice; regeneration is initiatory and graced. The **Eastern Orthodox** tradition holds the same baptismal-regeneration reading but integrates it into the triple sacramental initiation (baptism, chrismation, Eucharist) as a single liturgical act. The **Reformed** tradition (Calvin, Westminster) holds that regeneration is monergistic divine work that God may use baptism as a means of conveying — but the inner reality is sovereign Spirit-rebirth, not the water itself; infant baptism is a sign of covenant inclusion. The **Lutheran** tradition holds a strong baptismal-regeneration reading (Luther's *Small Catechism* on baptism as that which "works forgiveness of sins, rescues from death and the devil, and gives eternal salvation to all who believe this"). The **Wesleyan/Methodist** tradition holds a baptismal-regenerative reading but integrates it with the necessity of personal conversion and ongoing sanctification. The **Baptist/Anabaptist/Pentecostal** traditions read 3:5 as the new birth of the believer, sealed by *believer's* baptism — water and Spirit as inseparable in the believer's response, but causally located in the Spirit's work, not the water. The **Quaker** tradition reads the water and Spirit as a hendiadys ("water-and-Spirit" = Spirit-as-water) and rejects sacramental water-baptism altogether.

Steelmanned, the Catholic baptismal-regeneration reading takes seriously that John 3:5 names water and Spirit together and that the early church practiced baptism as the initiatory rite. Steelmanned, the Reformed reading takes seriously the agency of the Spirit ("the wind blows where it wishes," 3:8) and refuses to reduce regeneration to a ritual mechanism. Steelmanned, the believer's-baptism reading takes seriously the consistent New Testament pattern of personal faith preceding baptism and Jesus's address to a presumably-already-circumcised Pharisee who must still undergo this birth.

*Where this leaves us:* the 3:5 language is irreducibly both/and — water and Spirit, sign and reality, rite and rebirth. What divides Christian traditions is not whether John 3 mandates regeneration but how the Spirit's sovereign work and the Church's sacramental practice are related. The text grounds the question without dictating the answer.

**Tension two: is John 3:16's universal love compatible with 3:18's universal condemnation?** "God so loved the world... whoever believes in him is not condemned, but whoever does not believe is condemned already."

The **Calvinist** tradition (TULIP, the canons of Dort 1618-19) reads "world" in 3:16 as the elect from every nation — particular atonement for a definite world. The **Arminian / Wesleyan** tradition reads "world" as humanity universally — universal love, universal provision, particular response (prevenient grace makes faith possible for all). **Lutheran** orthodoxy reads similarly to the Wesleyan but with stronger emphasis on the sufficiency of Christ's atonement for all. The **Eastern Orthodox** tradition reads with characteristic restraint — divine love is universal, the response is genuine human freedom, and judgment is the working out of self-chosen separation from the light. **Hopeful universalism** (David Bentley Hart's *That All Shall Be Saved*; Origen's apokatastasis revival) reads 3:16-17 ("not to condemn the world, but in order that the world might be saved") as eschatologically promising universal salvation. **Karl Barth's** *Church Dogmatics* II/2 reads Christ as both elect and reprobate on humanity's behalf, opening a Christological-universalist horizon while declining to assert universal salvation as doctrine. **N.T. Wright** stresses the asymmetry of 3:17 — God's purpose in sending the Son is to *save* the world, not condemn it; "condemnation" is what people bring on themselves by preferring darkness (3:19), not a parallel divine intention.

Steelmanned, the Calvinist reading takes seriously the sustained Johannine binary (sons of light vs. sons of darkness) and the realism of unbelief. Steelmanned, the Arminian reading takes seriously the universal scope of *kosmos* in 3:16. Steelmanned, the Barthian-universalist reading takes seriously the asymmetry of 3:17 (the divine purpose is salvation, not condemnation; condemnation is the contradiction of that purpose, not its parallel).

*Where this leaves us:* the chapter holds together divine love and human responsibility without resolving them into either monergism or autonomous decision. The Light has come; the response is human; the wrath of God on unbelief is real; the sending purpose is salvation. Christian traditions have ordered these elements differently and will continue to.

**Unresolved question:** Nicodemus's fate. He appears again in 7:50-51 (defending Jesus on procedural grounds) and 19:39 (bringing seventy-five pounds of myrrh and aloes to Jesus's burial). Has he been "born from above" by chapter 19? The Gospel does not say. Brown reads him sympathetically as a slow-but-genuine seeker; Bultmann reads him as an emblem of crypto-discipleship that does not fully come to the light; Reinhartz reads him as a Pharisaic figure the narrator handles with more grace than the Gospel's broader rhetoric of "the Jews" would suggest. The text leaves him in twilight.

## The Thread

The "lifting up" Christology (3:14) reaches forward to 8:28 ("when you have lifted up the Son of Man, then you will know that I am he") and 12:32 ("when I am lifted up from the earth, I will draw all people to myself"). The "kingdom of God" language (3:3, 3:5) is otherwise rare in John (only here in the Gospel) — John has translated the synoptic kingdom-vocabulary into Johannine *zōē aiōnios* (eternal life), as Dodd and Brown both note. The *kosmos* of 3:16-19 is the same *kosmos* the Logos entered in 1:10 ("he was in the world, and the world was made through him"), and the same *kosmos* whose ruler will be cast out in 12:31. The light/darkness imagery of 3:19-21 closes the loop with the prologue (1:5, 1:9). Watch for John's ambivalent *kosmos*: God loves it (3:16), it does not know him (1:10), it hates the disciples (15:18-19), the believers are not to love it (1 John 2:15) — the Gospel's most contested noun.
