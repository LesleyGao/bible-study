# Luke 5: The Calling on the Lake and the Power That Forgives

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Luke 5 begins not in a synagogue or a city gate, but on a working shoreline. The "lake of Gennesaret" (Luke's preferred Hellenistic name; Mark and Matthew use *thalassa*, "sea") was the freshwater hub of a thriving fishing economy. By the first century, Galilean fishing had been industrialized to a degree that surprises modern readers: Mendel Nun, the kibbutznik scholar from Ein Gev whose decades of survey-work along the lake shore reconstructed sixteen ancient harbors, demonstrated that fishing was not subsistence but commercial enterprise, integrated into Herodian and Roman imperial markets through the salting-and-export industry centered at Magdala (Greek *Tarichaea*, "the place of salting"). The fishermen Jesus calls — Simon, James, John, and their unnamed partners — were not destitute peasants but small-business operators with capital tied up in boats, nets, and labor. The 1986 discovery of the **"Galilee Boat" or "Jesus Boat"** at Migdal, exposed during a severe drought that lowered the lake level, gave physical confirmation: a 27-foot cedar-and-oak fishing vessel from the first century, carbon-dated 40 BCE to 50 CE, with mortise-and-tenon construction and patches showing decades of repair-and-reuse. It seated four rowers, a helmsman, and could carry a substantial catch — exactly the kind of vessel Simon would have owned.

The Magdala excavations under **Dina Avshalom-Gor**, **Marcela Zapata-Meza**, and especially the work synthesized by **Stefano De Luca** and **Mordechai Aviam** have transformed our picture of first-century Galilee. The first-century synagogue uncovered there in 2009, with its limestone "Magdala Stone" carved with a seven-branched menorah and what appears to be a representation of the Jerusalem Temple, sits in a town that was processing fish on an industrial scale at the very moment Jesus was teaching from one of its boats. This was not the backwater Galilee of older liberal scholarship; it was a Hellenized, monetized, networked region with deep ties to Jerusalem and the Mediterranean.

Three scenes follow on land, each loaded with first-century texture. The leper of 5:12 inhabits a social-religious world structured by Leviticus 13–14: skin disease (Hebrew *tsara'at*) was less a medical category than a ritual one, marking the sufferer as outside the camp. Jesus's touch (***hēpsato***, ἥψατο) is doubly transgressive — defilement should flow from leper to Jesus, but Luke shows the reverse current. The instruction to "show yourself to the priest" preserves Torah observance even while subverting the social geometry of contagion. The paralytic-through-the-roof scene (5:17-26) presupposes the architecture of a typical first-century Galilean village house: dried-mud-and-thatch roof with branches over wooden beams, accessible by an outside staircase, easily dismantled and patched. The detail "**through the tiles**" (***dia tōn keramōn***, διὰ τῶν κεράμων, 5:19) is Luke's Hellenistic redaction for a Greco-Roman audience that pictured tile roofs; Mark's underlying scene is more authentically Galilean. Luke alone foregrounds the national-scope of opposition: Pharisees and teachers of the law gathered "from every village of Galilee and Judea and from Jerusalem." This is no local skirmish; Luke is staging a juridical inquiry.

The call of Levi (5:27-32) lands on the most despised social role of the Galilean economy. Tax-farmers (***telōnai***, τελῶναι) collected indirect taxes — tolls, customs, market-fees — for Herod Antipas, who sub-contracted from Rome. Capernaum sat on the road from Damascus to the Mediterranean and likely had a customs station; archaeological work by **Stanislao Loffreda** and the Franciscan team there has identified Roman-period structures consistent with such a checkpoint. To eat with *telōnai* was to break ritual table-fellowship with the *am ha'aretz* and to consort with collaborators. Levi's "great feast" is, in honor-shame terms, a public scandal — and Luke alone has Jesus add "**to repentance**" (***eis metanoian***, εἰς μετάνοιαν, 5:32), embedding the calling within his characteristic theology of conversion.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Luke 5 is the chapter where Luke's compositional independence from Mark becomes unmistakable. Mark places the call of Peter, Andrew, James, and John in his second chapter, briefly, with no miraculous catch (Mark 1:16-20). Luke delays the call, attaches it to a numinous fishing-miracle, and reorders the material. **Joseph Fitzmyer** (Anchor Bible) classifies the miraculous catch as a "Lukan reworking" of L material that may share a tradition-stream with John 21's post-resurrection catch — both feature Peter, miraculous abundance, and a vocational charge. **Joel Green** (NICNT) reads the doubling as deliberate: Luke is constructing Peter as the disciple who must be called twice, before and after failure. **Howard Marshall** (NIGTC) is more cautious, holding that Luke and John reflect independent traditions of a single event Luke moves to its theologically appropriate location.

The chapter is structured as a rising series of three controversies (5:17-26, 27-32, 33-39) preceded by two miracle-stories (5:1-11, 12-16), with each unit escalating the conflict-stakes: a private numinous encounter on a boat, then a public healing with a private prayer-withdrawal, then juridical confrontation over forgiveness, then table-fellowship scandal, then a hermeneutic clash about old and new. **Robert Tannehill** (*Narrative Unity of Luke-Acts*) reads this as a programmatic chapter: Luke is laying down the conflict-arc that will run through the entire Gospel.

Three Lukan compositional fingerprints appear in this chapter:

First, the **prayer motif**: 5:16 ("but he would withdraw to desolate places and pray") is uniquely Lukan. Luke alone among the Synoptics gives Jesus's prayer-life this systematic emphasis — at his baptism, before choosing the Twelve, before Peter's confession, on the mountain, in Gethsemane. **Conzelmann** in *Theology of St. Luke* identified this as a marker of Luke's Christology of the Spirit-led prophet.

Second, **economic-vocational radicalism**: "they left everything (***panta***) and followed him" (5:11, 28). Luke alone uses this totalizing language for Peter and Levi. Mark and Matthew say merely they "left their nets" or "left the tax booth." Luke is theologizing renunciation in a way that anticipates 14:33 ("any one of you who does not renounce all that he has cannot be my disciple") and the Acts community-of-goods passages.

Third, the uniquely Lukan saying about old wine: "**no one after drinking old wine desires new, for he says, 'The old is good'**" (5:39). This single sentence has split commentators for two millennia. Is it ironic — Luke skewering Pharisaic resistance to the new wine of the gospel? Is it conciliatory — acknowledging that the old (Torah, Israel) retains genuine goodness? **Joel Green** argues for Lukan irony with a dialectical edge: the saying captures the human-conservative reflex that the gospel must overcome. **François Bovon** (Hermeneia, with his unmatched reception-history work) catalogues both readings across the Fathers and the Reformers, noting that **Marcion** unsurprisingly excised the verse from his Luke. **Bock** (BECNT) splits the difference: the verse is a sober acknowledgment that the gospel will be slow to be received, not an endorsement.

Textually, 5:39 is absent from Codex Bezae (D) and several Old Latin witnesses, which has fueled speculation that it is a later gloss. The majority of modern critical editions retain it as authentically Lukan, with **Bruce Metzger** noting that its omission is more easily explained as an Anti-Marcionite reaction than as an interpolation.

The Q-source question hovers over the chapter mainly in the fasting-and-wineskins controversy (5:33-39), which is in triple-tradition material drawn from Mark. **Mark Goodacre's** *Case Against Q* and the broader Farrer-Goulder-Goodacre hypothesis would read Luke here as redacting Mark directly, with the old-wine saying as a Lukan composition rather than a Q fragment. The classical Two-Source view holds the same passage as substantially Markan with Lukan additions; the differences here are minor, but Goodacre's hypothesis becomes much more consequential when we reach the Sermon on the Plain in chapter 6.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The **Jewish interpretive trajectory** finds in Luke 5 both points of recognition and points of rupture. **Amy-Jill Levine** (*The Misunderstood Jew*; *Jewish Annotated New Testament*) reads the leper-cleansing as a Jewish-ritually-observant act: Jesus does not abolish the priestly system but works within it, sending the man to the priest and to the offering "as Moses commanded." The friction is not Jesus-vs-Judaism but a halakhic dispute within Judaism. **Geza Vermes** (*Jesus the Jew*) places the entire chapter within his charismatic-Hasid paradigm: Jesus stands in the line of **Honi the Circle-Drawer** and **Hanina ben Dosa**, holy men whose authority bypassed normal channels. The forgiveness-of-sins claim in 5:20-24 is the harder edge: **E.P. Sanders** (*Jesus and Judaism*) argues that pronouncing forgiveness apart from the Temple cult was the genuinely scandalous claim — not because no one could announce God's forgiveness, but because doing so without the sacrificial system implicitly relocated the locus of atonement. **Daniel Boyarin** (*The Jewish Gospels*) pushes further: the "Son of Man with authority" language is not foreign to Second Temple Judaism but draws on **Daniel 7** and the **Similitudes of Enoch**, where a heavenly Son-of-Man figure already wields divine prerogatives. For Boyarin, Luke 5:24 is a Jewish theological move, not a violation of monotheism. **Pinchas Lapide** the Orthodox dialogue partner read the call of Levi as a remarkable instance of Jewish *teshuvah* ("repentance/return"), recognizing in Luke's distinctive "to repentance" addition a deeply Jewish theological category. **David Flusser** noted that the parable of physician-and-sick has rabbinic parallels and that Jesus's table-fellowship with sinners, while controversial, was not unprecedented — fragments of similar practice appear in Hillelite traditions.

The **Christian interpretive trajectory** runs deep. **Origen's** *Homilies on Luke* read the miraculous catch allegorically: the two boats are Israel and the Gentiles, the breaking nets the universal mission's strain on the church, Peter's "depart from me" the proper humility before divine power. **Ambrose's** *Expositio in Lucam* developed the typology further: the deep waters (***to bathos***, τὸ βάθος, 5:4) are the depths of Scripture and the world's unconverted. **Cyril of Alexandria** in his Lukan commentary fragments emphasized Christ's divinity as the explanation of the catch — only the Creator commands the fish. **Bede** picked up Ambrose's allegory and passed it to medieval Latin tradition; **Aquinas's** *Catena Aurea* synthesized the patristic reading into a layered exposition where every detail (boats, nets, partners) carries spiritual significance.

**Calvin** in his *Harmony of the Synoptic Gospels* characteristically pushed against the allegorical drift, insisting on the historical-miraculous reading: Christ demonstrates his divinity by the catch, summons Peter to humble vocation, and the call is paradigmatic of all gospel ministry. **Luther's** lectures on Luke emphasized the paralytic-and-roof scene as a paradigm of justification: faith bears the sinner before Christ; Christ pronounces forgiveness; the physical healing is the visible sign of the invisible reality.

Twentieth-century scholarship reframed the chapter. **Conzelmann's** *Theology of St. Luke* situated the prayer-withdrawal and the controversies within his three-stage salvation-history scheme (Israel — Jesus's ministry — Church). **Tannehill's** narrative-critical reading saw 5:1-11 as the pivot from John-the-Baptist's prologue (chapters 1–4) to the gathering of disciples that drives the rest of the Gospel. **Fitzmyer's** Anchor Bible volumes remain the foundational reference work, and his commentary on 5:39 ("the old is good") is a model of measured judgment. **Joel Green's** NICNT volume reads the chapter through the lens of Lukan reversal: the "sick" are healed, the "sinners" called, the boundary-keepers thrown into confusion. **John Nolland's** Word Biblical Commentary (3 vols) and **Darrell Bock's** BECNT (2 vols) provide thorough exegetical detail on the Greek; **Luke Timothy Johnson's** Sacra Pagina volume reads the chapter through its Hellenistic-Jewish literary conventions.

**N.T. Wright**, in *Luke for Everyone* and at greater depth in *Jesus and the Victory of God*, reads the call of the fishermen and the forgiveness-of-sins controversy as two sides of one announcement: the kingdom of God is breaking in, which for Wright means that Israel's God is returning to act, to forgive, and to remake his people. "From now on you will be catching people" is not a generic call to evangelism but the summons to a renewed Israel — the nucleus of the people God is reconstituting around Jesus. And the paralytic scene is, for Wright, the sharp point: when Jesus pronounces forgiveness apart from the Temple, he is enacting the end of exile. Israel's long story of sin-and-banishment is being closed not in the sacrificial cult but in the person and word of Jesus, who does on the spot what the whole Temple system pointed toward. The scribes are right that "God alone" forgives — Wright's point is that Jesus is precisely the place where that divine forgiveness is now happening, the locus of YHWH's return to Zion relocated onto a Galilean street.

**Mediterranean and culturally-attentive readings** take the chapter in still other directions. **Kenneth Bailey's** *Through Peasant Eyes* and *Jesus Through Middle Eastern Eyes* read Peter's "depart from me" as an honor-shame response to numinous power, not as Western introspective guilt. **Justo González** *Luke (Belief)* foregrounds the calling of Levi as a paradigm of Jesus's reaching toward the despised classes, and finds in 5:11 ("they left everything") the radical economic reorientation that the gospel asks of its hearers.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Does the miraculous catch reveal Jesus's divinity, or his prophetic authority, or his identification-with-Peter's-failure?** Three positions. The **divine-revelation reading**, dominant in the patristic tradition (Cyril, Ambrose) and inherited by Calvin, holds that the catch is an epiphany: Peter's "depart from me, for I am a sinful man" is the proper Isaiah-6 response to the Holy One. Steelman: the chapter's escalating Christology (Son of Man with authority to forgive sins, lord of new wine) supports a high-Christological reading from the start. The **prophetic-Christology reading** (Conzelmann, Vermes in part) holds that Luke is presenting Jesus as the Spirit-anointed prophet whose word commands creation, in the line of Elijah and Elisha. Steelman: Luke 4:18-21 has just programmed Jesus's identity in prophetic-Isaianic terms; the catch is a prophetic sign-act, not a metaphysical disclosure. The **vocational-mercy reading** (Joel Green, Bailey) holds that the catch is primarily about Peter, not Christology: Jesus meets a failed fisherman in his failure, transforms scarcity into abundance, and reframes vocation. Steelman: the narrative weight falls on Peter's response and on the line "from now on you will be catching men" — the miracle's purpose is the calling, not the disclosure. **Where this leaves us:** these three need not exclude one another. Luke's narrative artistry layers them. The catch is an epiphany *that produces* a calling *in the mode of* mercy-meeting-failure. The reading that flattens any of the three impoverishes the text.

**Tension Two: What does it mean that "the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins"?** Three positions. The **divine-prerogative reading** (the scribes' framing in 5:21, accepted by patristic and Reformation traditions) holds that forgiveness of sins is a divine act that no human can perform; Jesus's claim therefore implies divine identity. Steelman: the scribes' theology is correct ("Who can forgive sins but God alone?"); Jesus does not contest their premise but acts in a way that, granted the premise, makes a Christological claim. The **delegated-authority reading** (Boyarin, some patristic strands) holds that "Son of Man" is a Danielic figure who shares God's prerogatives without simply being God; Jesus claims a heavenly-but-not-simply-divine authority already known in Second Temple Judaism. Steelman: Daniel 7 is genuinely the background; the synagogue-tradition of an exalted Son-of-Man is documented in 1 Enoch's Similitudes. The **prophetic-announcement reading** (E.P. Sanders, **Marcus Borg**) holds that Jesus is announcing God's forgiveness, not bestowing it ex officio; the scandal is the by-passing of Temple cult, not the claim to divine identity per se. Steelman: priests and prophets in the Hebrew Bible regularly mediate God's forgiveness; the title "Son of Man" can mean simply "this human being" in Aramaic. **N.T. Wright** offers a fourth angle that cuts across these: the scandal is neither bare announcement nor a metaphysics-claim but a *jurisdictional* one — Jesus is doing here, in his own person, what the Temple was built to do, and thereby signaling that Israel's exile is ending and her God is back in charge. The Christology follows from the act rather than preceding it. **Where this leaves us:** the text seems to push beyond mere announcement (Jesus's "but that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth" is a deliberate counter-claim to "God alone"), but it stops short of the explicit metaphysical claim later patristic theology will make. Luke is operating at the productive boundary where Jewish messianic-prophetic categories are being stretched to accommodate something the early church will eventually call "incarnation."

**Unresolved question:** When Jesus says "the old is good" in 5:39 — is he diagnosing a problem (people refusing the new) or affirming a truth (the old has its own goodness)? The chapter ends without resolution, and Luke has structured it that way deliberately. If the saying is irony, the gospel is the new wine that makes the old obsolete. If the saying is sincere, the gospel is continuous with Israel's story and the new does not annul the old. The whole subsequent question of Luke's view of Israel — and of Jew-and-Gentile in Acts — turns on which way we read this single ambiguous line.

## The Thread

Three cross-references mark this chapter as a node in the larger Lukan and canonical web. **Isaiah 6:1-8** stands behind Peter's "depart from me, for I am a sinful man" — the prophet's "Woe is me, for I am a man of unclean lips" before the holiness of God. Luke is staging Peter's call in prophetic-vocational terms. **2 Kings 5** (Naaman the Syrian leper) is in the air for the leper-cleansing; Jesus had just invoked Naaman in 4:27 as a programmatic Gentile-precedent. The leper of 5:12 is the first beneficiary of the program Luke 4 announced. **Luke 19:1-10** (Zacchaeus the chief tax-collector) is the chapter's destination: Levi's call in 5:27-32 is the seed of which Zacchaeus is the bloom, and the hospitality-of-sinners motif will reappear at every major Lukan turning-point. Watch also for **Acts 10** (Peter and Cornelius) — the "from now on you will be catching men" of 5:10 finds its full ecclesial meaning when Peter is called to fish for Gentiles; Luke 5 is already programming Acts.
