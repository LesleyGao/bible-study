# Mark 7: Pure Hands, a Gentile Mother's Wit, and Ephphatha

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Mark 7 stages a confrontation between Pharisaic ritual practice and Jesus's halakhic stance, then crosses out of Jewish territory entirely into Phoenicia and the Decapolis. The geographical and cultural distance the chapter covers in its short span is enormous: from the courtyard of a Galilean house to the coastal pagan polis of Tyre, from there to Sidon, and back along an arc through the largely Gentile Decapolis. The route Mark sketches in 7:31 ("returned from the region of Tyre and went through Sidon to the Sea of Galilee, in the region of the Decapolis") has long puzzled commentators because it is geographically perplexing — Sidon is north of Tyre, the wrong direction for a return to Galilee. R. T. France (NIGTC) treats the route as a deliberate Markan flourish marking the breadth of Gentile territory traversed; Joel Marcus (Anchor Bible) and Adela Yarbro Collins (Hermeneia) read it as Mark's emphasis on a sustained Gentile mission rather than a lapse in geography.

The hand-washing controversy in 7:1–13 reflects a genuine first-century debate. Mark's parenthetical aside in 7:3–4, explaining the practice for his Roman audience, is one of the strongest internal indicators that the Gospel was composed for non-Jewish readers (cf. Papias's note in Eusebius *Hist. Eccl.* 3.39.15 about Mark as Peter's interpreter writing in Rome). The practice Mark describes — washing the hands "properly" (Greek *pygmē*, an obscure term variously rendered "with the fist," "carefully," or "up to the elbow") and the immersion of cups, pots, and copper vessels — corresponds to the developing system of *halakhot* the rabbinic sources would later codify in tractate *Yadayim* of the Mishnah and in Mishnah *Kelim*. The Qumran Community Rule (1QS) and the Damascus Document also attest to elaborate purification practices, suggesting Mark's portrait of widespread Pharisaic concern with ritual purity is not a polemical caricature but a description of a real religious culture.

The exception Jesus highlights — ***Korban*** (κορβᾶν, 7:11) — is one of Mark's preserved Aramaic terms, transliterated rather than translated, then glossed for his Greek readers ("that is, given to God"). The practice is attested in Jewish legal texts: Mishnah *Nedarim* 3.2, 8.7, 9.7 discusses the *konam* and *qonam* vow formulas, which were lesser variants of the *qorban* dedication, and the rabbinic discussion explicitly addresses the very abuse Jesus names — using a vow to dedicate property "to God" in order to evade an obligation to support parents. The Mishnah's later position is closer to Jesus's: Rabbi Eliezer in *Nedarim* 9.1 holds that if a man sees his vow now causes harm to parents, the vow can be released. Mark places Jesus inside an intra-Jewish legal debate about which the rabbis themselves were uneasy. An ossuary found in 1956 by archaeologist J. T. Milik near Jerusalem bears the Aramaic inscription *Korban*, providing material confirmation of the term's living use in first-century Judea.

The Phoenician encounter in 7:24–30 brings Jesus into a different cultural world. **Tyre** (Tyros, modern Sour) and **Sidon** (Saida) were ancient Phoenician maritime city-states whose history stretched back to the Bronze Age and whose identity as Gentile centers ran deep in the Hebrew Bible's prophetic imagination (Isaiah 23, Ezekiel 26–28, Amos 1:9–10). By the first century, both were Hellenized Roman *poleis* with extensive Greek-speaking populations and continued mercantile prominence; excavations at Tyre and Sidon have revealed extensive Roman-period civic architecture, including hippodromes, baths, and necropoleis. Mark identifies the woman as ***Hellēnis Syrophoinikissa tō genei*** (Ἑλληνὶς Συροφοινίκισσα τῷ γένει, 7:26), "a Greek, a Syrophoenician by birth." The double designation matters: she is culturally Greek-speaking and ethnically Phoenician, a member of the Hellenized urban class, perhaps with means (she has a house, a daughter at home in a bed). Matthew's parallel calls her a "Canaanite" (Matthew 15:22), reaching back to the older biblical category for the same ethnic group — an interesting Matthean choice that Mark does not make.

The Decapolis healing in 7:31–37 returns Jesus to the region of the Gerasene exorcism. The man's condition — ***mogilalos*** (μογιλάλος, 7:32), a rare Greek term meaning "speaking with difficulty" or "having an impediment" — appears in the Septuagint of Isaiah 35:6, where the prophet promises that in the eschatological restoration "the tongue of the dumb [*mogilalōn*] shall sing for joy." Mark's choice of this exact, unusual word is almost certainly deliberate: he is signaling that what is happening in the Decapolis fulfills the eschatological pattern Isaiah named. The healing itself uses bodily-touch techniques common in Greco-Roman medical and magical practice — fingers in ears, spittle on the tongue, a sigh, a word in the local sacred tongue. Wendy Cotter (*Miracles in Greco-Roman Antiquity*, 1999) has documented analogous procedures in Asclepian healing accounts and in the magical papyri. The Aramaic ***Ephphatha*** (ἐφφαθά, 7:34) is preserved untranslated, then glossed — Mark's third major Aramaic preservation after *Talitha koum* (5:41) and *Korban* (7:11), with two more (*Abba*, 14:36; *Eloi Eloi lema sabachthani*, 15:34) yet to come.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Mark 7 has a sharp two-part structure. The first half (7:1–23) is a controversy dialogue concluded by a private explanation to the disciples — a typical Markan two-stage teaching pattern (cf. 4:1–20 on the parables). The second half (7:24–37) is two healing pericopae set in Gentile territory. The structural logic is unmistakable: Jesus has redefined purity as a matter of the heart, and the next two scenes show what this means in practice — the inclusion of a Gentile woman and the healing of a deaf-mute Gentile man.

The first half deploys Mark's signature parenthetical glosses. The aside in 7:3–4 explaining Pharisaic hand-washing customs is the longest such ethnographic note in the Gospel and a strong marker of authorial distance from Jewish practice. The interpretive gloss in 7:11 ("that is, given to God") translates *Korban* for the audience. And the most consequential gloss, in 7:19, is also the most contested: ***katharizōn panta ta brōmata*** (καθαρίζων πάντα τὰ βρώματα), "thus he declared all foods clean," or more grammatically defensibly, "[the stomach's expulsion] cleansing all foods." The grammar is awkward: the participle *katharizōn* is masculine nominative, agreeing with the participial subject earlier in the verse, but its theological import is enormous if Mark intends it as editorial commentary. Joel Marcus argues forcefully that this is Mark's editorial interpretation, an interpretive move on the part of the Evangelist or the early tradition rather than a verbatim Jesus saying. Adela Yarbro Collins concurs. James D. G. Dunn (*Jesus, Paul and the Law*, 1990) argues that the historical Jesus did not abrogate the food laws — Acts 10 (Peter's vision) treats the kosher question as still open in the early church — and that Mark 7:19 is a developed interpretation by Mark or his community.

The second half deploys Markan literary signatures. The Syrophoenician encounter is the only place in the Gospel where a character wins an argument with Jesus. The Greek of her reply — ***Nai, kyrie, kai ta kynaria hypokatō tēs trapezēs esthiousin apo tōn psichiōn tōn paidiōn*** (7:28), "Yes, Lord, even the dogs under the table eat from the children's crumbs" — is a careful inversion. Jesus has used the diminutive ***kynaria*** (κυνάρια, "little dogs," "household dogs") rather than the harsher ***kynes*** (dogs); she takes up the diminutive and turns it into household-dependent imagery, reframing his statement from exclusion to delayed inclusion. R. T. France (NIGTC) and Joel Marcus (Anchor Bible) both note that Mark gives her the rhetorical floor — she is granted a wit and a theological argument that none of the male disciples display in the same chapter.

The deaf-mute healing concludes with one of Mark's most distinctive ***Messianic Secret*** moments. Jesus charges the witnesses to tell no one (7:36), and yet "the more he charged them, the more zealously they proclaimed it." The phrasing creates a closed loop: secrecy commanded, secrecy violated, the gospel spreading despite (or because of) the prohibition. Wrede's *Messianic Secret* (1901) treated such moments as evidence of an early Christian editorial layer covering the historical absence of Jesus's messianic claims; Werner Kelber and most contemporary scholars (Marcus, Yarbro Collins, France, Hooker) read them more functionally — secrecy as a literary device for managing Christological revelation across the narrative.

Textual variants in the chapter are mostly minor. The most significant is the absence of verse 16 in many early manuscripts (including Sinaiticus and Vaticanus), where some manuscripts add "If anyone has ears to hear, let him hear." The verse is widely regarded as a later assimilation to Mark 4:9 and is omitted in critical texts.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Mark 7 sits at the structural pivot of the Gospel's geographic-Christological argument. Read backward, it completes the trajectory of opposition that has built since 2:1 (Pharisees and scribes opposing Jesus's authority) and the pattern of Gentile encounter that began at 5:1 (Gerasene demoniac). Read forward, it sets up the Gentile feeding (8:1–10) and the Caesarea Philippi confession (8:27–30).

The Jewish reception of Mark 7 has been thoughtful and increasingly important. Geza Vermes (*Jesus the Jew*) read the controversy as intra-Jewish — Jesus is taking the position of the *am ha-aretz* (the common people) against Pharisaic stringency, and his halakhic move is one option within Jewish purity debate, not a rejection of Judaism. E. P. Sanders (*Jesus and Judaism*, 1985; *The Historical Figure of Jesus*) argues that Jesus did not in fact abrogate Mosaic food laws — the early church's struggle over kashrut (Galatians 2; Acts 10–11, 15) only makes sense if Jesus had not settled the question. Sanders reads Mark 7:19 as a Markan editorial gloss, not a Jesus saying, with significant historical-critical implications. Daniel Boyarin (*The Jewish Gospels*) reads the chapter inside a Jewish purity-renewal trajectory: Jesus is making moral interiority primary while leaving the Mosaic categories formally in place. Amy-Jill Levine (*The Misunderstood Jew*; *The Jewish Annotated New Testament*) has been particularly forceful in warning against the supersessionist reading of Mark 7 — Jesus is not "freeing the church from Jewish ritualism" but engaging a real Jewish argument about how Torah and tradition relate. James Crossley (*The Date of Mark's Gospel*, 2004) has used Mark 7 to argue for a relatively early date for the Gospel: only an early Mark, written before the Gentile-mission Halakhic question was fully worked out, would phrase the Korban argument the way Mark does.

The Christian interpretive trajectory has read the chapter as foundational for the Gentile mission. Origen, in surviving fragments and in his *Commentary on Matthew* (extending to the parallel pericope), read the Syrophoenician woman as a type of the church gathered from the Gentiles; the children's crumbs are the eucharistic gifts available to all who approach with faith. Augustine, in *De Consensu Evangelistarum* and in sermons on the Syrophoenician woman, read her as an exemplar of humility — she accepts the diminutive "dog" and finds grace in that acceptance. Bede's *Commentary on Mark* read *Ephphatha* as the church's awakening to the gospel; the deaf and mute man is humanity in need of the gospel's opening word.

Calvin's *Harmony of the Gospels* engaged the Korban argument with characteristic precision, treating it as a paradigmatic case of human tradition undermining divine command — a foundational text for the Reformation's *sola scriptura*. Luther read the Syrophoenician woman as the great Gospel example of faith holding on through testing; her reply ("yet even the dogs eat the crumbs") is, on Luther's reading, the articulation of *sola fide* itself: faith that takes God at his word even when his word seems to refuse her.

Twentieth-century scholarship has been more diverse. Bonhoeffer (*Discipleship*) read the chapter on heart-purity as foundational to costly grace: the externals matter only as they express interiority, and interiority is what God commands. Bultmann's form-critical work (*History of the Synoptic Tradition*) classed the controversy dialogues as developed *Streitgespräche*, with the Syrophoenician story and the Ephphatha healing as miracle stories of Hellenistic-aretalogical character. Karl Barth treated the Syrophoenician's reply as a paradigm of the right relation of faith to Christ's freedom — she does not demand, she pleads, and her plea is heard. N. T. Wright (*Mark for Everyone*; *Jesus and the Victory of God*) reads the chapter inside his eschatological-fulfillment frame: the Gentile mission begins already in Galilee because the Kingdom is breaking in, and the heart-purity teaching is the *new covenant* circumcision Deuteronomy 30 and Jeremiah 31 promised.

The major modern commentaries triangulate. Joel Marcus's Anchor Bible volume reads the chapter as Christologically and ecclesiologically programmatic: Jesus is enacting the inclusion of the Gentiles even before the Caesarea Philippi confession. Adela Yarbro Collins's Hermeneia volume emphasizes the Hellenistic-religious context of the healing miracles while reading the controversy dialogue as a serious engagement with first-century halakha. R. T. France's NIGTC volume engages the editorial gloss "thus he declared all foods clean" carefully, treating it as Mark's interpretation but a defensible one in light of Jesus's teaching. Morna Hooker's BNTC volume reads the chapter as a literary unit with the heart-purity teaching as its center, the Gentile encounters as its embodiment. Vincent Taylor's earlier commentary noted the strikingly bodily and tactile character of the deaf-mute healing as evidence of vivid eyewitness tradition.

The literary critics have produced distinctive readings. Frank Kermode (*Genesis of Secrecy*) read the disciples' incomprehension in 7:18 ("Then are you also without understanding?") as a Markan signature of the secret that grows the more it is told. Mary Ann Tolbert (*Sowing the Gospel*) read the chapter in light of her sower-parable framework: the controversy with the Pharisees is rocky soil; the Syrophoenician is soil that bears fruit. Werner Kelber (*The Oral and Written Gospel*) emphasized the rhythm of the controversy dialogue as evidence of preserved oral tradition. Rhoads, Dewey, and Michie (*Mark as Story*) tracked the chapter's narrative voice, noting how Mark gives the Syrophoenician woman a sharper interior agency than any of the male disciples in the same chapter. Ben Witherington (*Women in the Ministry of Jesus*, 1984) observes that she is both the only character to win an argument with Jesus and the only Gentile woman to receive a healing-by-proxy in Mark.

Political readings have been important. Ched Myers (*Binding the Strong Man*) reads the controversy with the Pharisees as a confrontation between Jesus's prophetic Galilean populism and the centralizing religious authority of Jerusalem; the heart-purity teaching is a critique of an elite religious system. Richard Horsley's work on Galilee underscores that Tyre and Sidon were not just Gentile but specifically Hellenized urban centers exerting economic pressure on the Galilean countryside; the Syrophoenician's request inverts a familiar exploitation.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions run through Mark 7, each requiring careful steelmanning.

**Tension 1: Did Jesus actually declare all foods clean (7:19)?** This is one of the most consequential interpretive questions in the Gospel, with implications for early Christian halakhic development, Pauline-Petrine relations in Galatians 2 and Acts 10–11, and the ongoing question of how Jesus stood in relation to Mosaic Torah. Three positions:

The *Markan editorial gloss* position (Sanders, Dunn, Crossley, Marcus, Yarbro Collins) holds that the parenthetical "thus he declared all foods clean" is Mark's interpretation, not a verbatim Jesus saying. The historical Jesus, on this reading, redirected emphasis from external to internal purity without abrogating the Mosaic food laws. The early church then worked out the Gentile-inclusion question only gradually, with Peter's Joppa vision (Acts 10) and the Jerusalem Council (Acts 15) as the decisive moments. Mark, writing later, reads the trajectory backward into a programmatic editorial gloss. The position is strongest when one notices that Acts 10 portrays Peter as utterly surprised that unclean foods might be permissible — a surprise that would be inexplicable if Jesus had clearly settled the question.

The *Christological-fulfillment* position (some patristic readings; classical Christian exegesis; modernized in France, Edwards, and elements of Wright) holds that Jesus's teaching genuinely reshapes the food laws, and Mark's gloss is faithful interpretation rather than novel imposition. The food laws in this reading were always serving a deeper purpose (separating Israel for holiness; teaching internal purity); when the deeper purpose comes to fulfillment in Christ, the externals lose their pedagogical function. The reading is strongest when paired with Romans 14, Galatians 2, and the subsequent Christian tradition.

The *intra-Jewish-renewal* position (Boyarin, Levine in part, Vermes) holds that Jesus is contesting the *Pharisaic extension* of the food laws, not the Torah itself. The hand-washing and vessel-purification practices Mark describes were the *halakhot* of the Pharisees, not Mosaic command. Jesus's heart-purity teaching is consistent with Jewish prophetic tradition (Isaiah 1, Jeremiah 7, Hosea 6) and could be received as inner-Jewish renewal. The Markan gloss "thus he declared all foods clean" then represents a development of the tradition by Mark's Gentile-inclusive community rather than a decisive Jesus-saying.

*Where this leaves us:* The historical-critical case for the gloss being Markan editorial rather than verbatim Jesus is strong, but Mark's authority as interpreter of Jesus's teaching is itself part of the canonical witness. The question of whether Mark 7:19 represents Jesus or Mark cannot be settled by the verse alone; it is decided in conjunction with one's reading of how the gospel narrative as a whole relates to early Christian practice. What Mark gives us is a teaching that points beyond itself, and a community that received the teaching as having implications its original Jewish context did not need to spell out.

**Tension 2: How should we read Jesus's response to the Syrophoenician woman?** Three positions:

The *testing* position (some patristic readings, dominant in Catholic medieval exegesis) holds that Jesus knew his answer was true (the priority of Israel) and was testing the woman's faith. Her humble persistence drew out a deeper truth about God's mercy, and the testing functioned to reveal her faith. Steelmanned, this reading takes seriously that Jesus addresses her not with rejection but with a theological proposition; the diminutive *kynaria* (household dogs, not street dogs) softens the harshness; her quick reply is invited rather than provoked.

The *real-reluctance* position (some Reformation readings; Luther; some contemporary evangelical exegesis) holds that Jesus stated his actual mission priority — first to Israel — and the woman's reply genuinely opened a possibility his stated priority had not foreseen. The reading takes the rhetorical surface of the dialogue more literally and gives the woman more transformative agency.

The *Christological-development* position (Hooker, Marcus in part, and Wright in *Mark for Everyone*) holds that Jesus's exchange with the woman is a moment of Christological extension or refinement: Jesus's mission to Israel is real and ordered "to the children first," but the inclusion of the Gentiles, latent in the prophetic promise that through Israel the nations would be blessed, becomes explicit through this encounter. For Wright the scene is of a piece with the whole chapter — the Kingdom Jesus is inaugurating is the renewal of Israel *for the sake of the world*, and the Phoenician mother has, in effect, read that script better than the Twelve have. The reading does not resolve whether Jesus's priorities "changed" in any psychological sense but takes seriously that Mark presents the woman's reply as the catalyst for the healing.

*Where this leaves us:* The text refuses to flatten into any one of these readings. The diminutive *kynaria* matters, the woman's wit matters, the theological seriousness of the priority "to the children first" matters. What Mark records is a dialogue in which a Gentile woman participates in shaping the contours of the gospel's reach — and in which Jesus, however we understand his foreknowledge or his real reluctance, gives her his last word ("for this statement you may go your way") and acts on her plea. The encounter is one of the most remarkable scenes in any of the Gospels and resists tidy resolution.

**One unresolved question:** Why does Mark frame the Decapolis healing of 7:31–37 with the unusual word *mogilalos*, an Isaianic term for the eschatological deaf-mute restored, and then conclude with the crowd's spontaneous response, "He has done all things well" (7:37), which echoes Genesis 1:31 ("God saw everything that he had made, and behold, it was very good")? Mark is gesturing simultaneously toward the original creation and the eschatological new creation, mediated by an Aramaic-speaking healer in pagan territory. The signal is enormous, but Mark does not draw it out. The reader is left to perceive that what Jesus is doing in the Decapolis is what God did at the beginning and what the prophets promised at the end — and to ask, then, who Jesus is.

## The Thread

Mark 7 reaches forward and backward across the canon. The heart-purity teaching of 7:14–23 echoes Jeremiah 31:33 ("I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts") and Deuteronomy 30:6 ("the Lord your God will circumcise your heart"); the reader who hears Mark 7 with the prophets in mind is being trained to recognize the new covenant. The Syrophoenician's encounter prefigures the four-thousand feeding (8:1–10), in which Jesus will feed Gentile crowds as he fed the Jewish five thousand — the children's bread will, indeed, be shared. The Aramaic *Ephphatha* of 7:34 anticipates the disciples' partial-then-full sight at the two-stage healing of the blind man at Bethsaida (8:22–26) and the eventual eye-opening at the cross.

Two later passages worth flagging: 8:14–21, the disciples' incomprehension about the loaves, where Jesus explicitly asks "having ears do you not hear?" — the disciples are now in the position of the deaf-mute man, needing their own *Ephphatha*. And 11:15–17, the Temple cleansing, where Jesus quotes Isaiah 56:7 ("my house shall be a house of prayer for all the nations"); the Gentile inclusion enacted in Mark 7's Phoenician and Decapolis scenes will return as the explicit Temple critique. Mark is building a structure across the Gospel, and chapter 7 is one of its load-bearing walls.
