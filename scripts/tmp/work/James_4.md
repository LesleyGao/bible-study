# James 4: Friendship with the World, the Mist of Life, and the One Lawgiver

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens with a question that places the letter in a recognizably conflicted Christian community: "**What causes quarrels and what causes fights among you?**" (4:1). The Greek terms — *polemoi* (πόλεμοι, "wars") and *machai* (μάχαι, "battles") — are heightened military language used metaphorically for community conflict. The escalation includes accusation of murder ("**you murder**" / *phoneuete*, 4:2), which has been variously interpreted: literal bloodshed within the community (highly unlikely in 1st-c. Jewish-Christian house-churches); metaphorical destruction by hatred (echoing **Matthew 5:21-22** Sermon on the Mount tradition that anger is moral murder; this is the dominant patristic and modern reading); textual corruption of *phthoneite* "you envy" (the **Calvin emendation**, accepted by some critical editors but not most modern textual critics — engage **Bruce Metzger** *Textual Commentary*, **Davids** NIGTC).

The chapter's social-economic context emerges in the second half (4:13-17) — the merchant traveler scene. **First-century Mediterranean trade** was a major economic engine. The audience James envisions includes **traveling traders** ("**we will go into such and such a town and spend a year there and trade and make a profit**") — the *emporoi* class, mobile commercial agents moving between cities. **Bruce Longenecker** *Remember the Poor* documents the small-merchant class in 1st-c. Christianity (engage Lydia in Acts 16, the Corinthian merchants 1 Cor 7:21-24, the Pauline self-supporting tentmaking tradition). The Jacobean polemic against **boastful planning** challenges this class's confidence in commercial autonomy.

The phrase "**adulterous people**" (*moichalides*, 4:4 — feminine plural) is theologically loaded. Engage **OT prophetic-marriage tradition** — Hosea, Jeremiah, Ezekiel deploy adultery imagery for Israel's covenant unfaithfulness; the bride-of-YHWH is faithless when she pursues other gods. James's application of this prophetic tradition to Christian-pagan-world syncretism is striking. The feminine grammatical form deliberately evokes the prophetic tradition of Israel-as-faithless-bride. **Bauckham** *James* and **Davids** NIGTC develop this thoroughly.

The chapter's quotation at 4:5 — "**He yearns jealously over the spirit that he has made to dwell in us**" — is one of the chapter's hardest interpretive cruxes. The phrase appears in no extant OT or Second Temple text. Engage **Bruce Metzger** *Textual Commentary*; **Davids** NIGTC; **Bauckham** *James*. Possibilities:
- Loose paraphrase of OT theological tradition (Exod 20:5 jealous God + Gen 6:3 Spirit-dwelling)
- Lost apocryphal source (cf. similar phrasing in some Pseudepigrapha; engage Bauckham's reconstruction)
- Lukan-style summary citation rather than direct quote
The interpretive options are genuinely uncertain; engage carefully.

The chapter's closing conviction — "**you are a mist** (*atmis*, ἀτμίς) **that appears for a little time and then vanishes**" (4:14) — engages the **Hellenistic-Jewish vapor/breath/shadow tradition** of human transience. **Psalm 39:5-6** ("**every man at his best is but a breath / Surely a man goes about as a shadow**"); **Psalm 90:5-6** (humans like grass that withers); **Job 7:7, 9:25-26** (life is breath / a runner / a swift ship); **Wisdom of Solomon 2:1-5** (life is short and sorrowful, "**our breath will become smoke**"); **Sirach 14:18, 18:9-10** (mortal generations like leaves on a tree). The Jacobean image stands in this deep tradition.

A few Greek terms transform the chapter when you can hear them. ***Hēdonai*** (ἡδοναί) — "**passions**" or "**pleasures**" (4:1, 4:3) — Greek philosophical term central in **Stoic + Cynic + Platonic moral psychology**; the chapter's use connects to broader Greco-Roman moral tradition (engage **Stowers** *Diatribe*; **Margaret Mitchell**). ***Philia tou kosmou*** (φιλία τοῦ κόσμου) — "**friendship with the world**" (4:4) — programmatic Jacobean ethics. ***Echthra*** (ἔχθρα) — "**enmity**" (4:4) — the binary opposite. ***Hyperēphanous*** vs. ***tapeinous*** (ὑπερηφάνους / ταπεινούς) — "**the proud**" vs. "**the humble**" (4:6) — the **Septuagint of Proverbs 3:34** (LXX) directly cited. ***Atmis*** (ἀτμίς) — "**vapor / mist**" (4:14) — engages Wisdom-tradition transience-imagery. ***Ean ho kyrios thelēsē*** (Ἐὰν ὁ κύριος θελήσῃ) — "**if the Lord wills**" (4:15) — programmatic phrase, later canonized as *Deo volente* / "**D.V.**" in Latin Christian writing tradition (engage Augustine, Aquinas, the medieval and later Christian epistolary tradition).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

**Genre**: Hellenistic-Jewish moral exhortation continues; the chapter's diatribe-style escalation (questions, sharp accusations, prophetic vocative "**adulterous people!**") intensifies the rhetoric. **Stowers** *The Diatribe and Paul's Letter to the Romans* identifies the genre's signature features — direct address, hypothetical scenarios, OT citations, climactic exhortation. James 4 displays the diatribe's full repertoire.

The chapter's literary structure is bipartite:

**Unit 1: Quarrels and Friendship with the World** (4:1-12). Opens with diagnosis of community conflict (4:1-3); pivots to prophetic accusation (4:4); cites Scripture (4:5-6); issues ten imperatives in rapid succession (4:7-10): submit, resist, draw near, cleanse, purify, be wretched, mourn, weep, let your laughter be turned to mourning, humble yourselves. The imperatives reach a climax at 4:10: "**Humble yourselves before the Lord, and he will exalt you**." Then closes with the speech-and-judgment paragraph (4:11-12).

**Unit 2: Boastful Planning** (4:13-17). Direct address to merchant travelers ("**Come now, you who say...**"); diagnostic of presumption; transience reminder; correct formula ("**if the Lord wills**"); closing maxim (4:17 — "**whoever knows the right thing to do and fails to do it, for him it is sin**" — programmatic on **sins of omission**).

The chapter's literary craft is most concentrated at three points:

**The ten-imperative cluster** (4:7-10): rapid succession of ethical-spiritual commands; programmatic for **Christian repentance theology**. **Patrick Hartin** *A Spirituality of Perfection* and **Dietrich Bonhoeffer** *Cost of Discipleship* both engage this cluster. The structure parallels **Joel 2:12-17** (call to communal repentance) and the **rabbinic *teshuvah* tradition** (return to God).

**The Proverbs 3:34 LXX citation** (4:6): "**God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble**" — the same verse cited at 1 Peter 5:5, the closest cross-NT inter-textual link. Engage **Bauckham** *James and the Q Sayings of Jesus*; **Davids** *NIGTC*. The shared citation suggests both letters drew from a common Jewish-Christian liturgical or catechetical tradition.

**The mist-of-life image** (4:14): "**you are a mist that appears for a little time and then vanishes**." Engages **Wisdom-tradition transience-poetry** (Psalms, Job, Wisdom of Solomon). The image's force depends on the audience's experience — Mediterranean morning fog burns off quickly; the metaphor is concrete.

The chapter's **echoes of Jesus-tradition** continue James 1-3's pattern. Compare:
- 4:6 (proud/humble) / Matt 23:12 (humble/exalted), Luke 14:11, 18:14 (parable of Pharisee and tax collector)
- 4:7 (resist the devil) / Matt 4:1-11 (Jesus's wilderness temptation)
- 4:8 (cleanse hands / purify hearts) / Matt 5:8 (pure in heart)
- 4:9 (laughter to mourning) / Luke 6:25 (Lukan beatitudes-and-woes)
- 4:10 (humble / exalt) / Matt 23:12, Luke 14:11
- 4:11-12 (do not judge) / Matt 7:1-5 (Sermon on the Mount judging)
- 4:13-15 (planning / Lord willing) / Luke 12:13-21 (parable of the rich fool — the closest synoptic parallel)
- 4:14 (mist of life) / Luke 12:20 ("**this night your soul is required of you**")

The pattern continues James 1-3's deep engagement with Jesus-tradition. **Bauckham** argues James preserves Jesus-tradition close to its earliest form; the Lukan parallels at 4:13-15 are particularly striking — both letters are wrestling with what Luke would later codify as the Rich Fool.

There are no major textual variants of significance in James 4 except the disputed quotation at 4:5 (no clear OT source) and the *phoneuete*/*phthoneite* question at 4:2 (most modern critical editions retain the harder reading "you murder").

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, James 4 belongs to the broader Christian tradition of **anti-worldliness** texts — joining 1 John 2:15-17 ("do not love the world"), Romans 12:1-2 ("do not be conformed to this world"), and Galatians 6:14 ("the world has been crucified to me, and I to the world"). The chapter's vocabulary of **friendship with the world / enmity with God** is foundational for Christian anti-worldliness ethics across all major traditions.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** The chapter's prophetic-marriage tradition (4:4 "**adulterous people**") inherits the **Hosea-Jeremiah-Ezekiel covenant-adultery tradition**. **Pinchas Lapide** in his appreciative engagement of NT documents notes that James 4 stands squarely in this prophetic tradition — Christian-Jewish faithfulness vs. cultural-religious syncretism. The Mishnah Avot 4:21 ("Rabbi Eliezer ha-Kappar said: jealousy, lust, and the pursuit of glory drive a person from the world") is structurally similar to James 4:1-3. The Jewish wisdom tradition of *yetzer hara* (the evil inclination) provides cultural background for the Jacobean diagnosis of internal passions producing external conflict.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** The patristic and medieval traditions read James 4 substantially. **Origen** *Homilies on Numbers* + *Commentary on Romans* engaged the friendship-with-world theme. **John Chrysostom** preached extensively on the chapter's repentance-imperatives. **Augustine** *Confessions* (especially Books 8-9) treats the conversion experience in vocabulary that closely echoes James 4:7-10 (submit to God, resist temptation, draw near, humble oneself). **Bede** *Super Epistolam Iacobi* developed the medieval Latin tradition.

The medieval **monastic tradition** read James 4 as foundational for the **renunciation-of-the-world** practice. **Benedict's Rule** (chapter 4 on instruments of good works) cites James 4:6-10 substantially. **Bernard of Clairvaux** developed **fuga mundi** (flight from the world) as a Cistercian distinctive. **Thomas à Kempis** *Imitation of Christ* book 1 engages James 4's anti-worldliness.

The Reformation engagement was substantial. **Calvin's *Commentary on James*** treats the chapter at length, particularly the **friendship-with-world / enmity-with-God** binary. Calvin reads "the world" as **fallen humanity organized in opposition to God** (engage *Institutes* 1.5.11-12; 3.10.1-3). The Reformed tradition's **two-kingdoms theology** draws on James 4 + 1 John 2:15-17 + Augustine *City of God*. **Luther's** engagement is more limited (given his ambivalence about James generally) but he uses James 4:7 ("resist the devil") in his catechetical writings.

The **Anabaptist tradition** read James 4 as foundational for **separation from the world** practice — the **two-kingdoms** vs. **separated-community** distinction. **Schleitheim Confession 1527** + **Menno Simons** + the **Hutterite tradition** all engage James 4:4 as warrant for community-distinct life. The **Mennonite tradition** continues this engagement.

The **Wesleyan tradition** read James 4 within the framework of **entire sanctification** — **Wesley's *A Plain Account of Christian Perfection*** engages 4:8 ("cleanse your hands... purify your hearts") as describing the work of sanctifying grace. **Holiness movement** traditions develop this further.

The **Puritan tradition** developed substantial engagement: **Richard Baxter** *A Christian Directory*, **John Owen** *Mortification of Sin* (foundational engagement with 4:7 resistance imagery); **Jonathan Edwards** *Religious Affections*. The Puritan reading emphasizes the **internal dimension** — the war within (4:1) is the primary battleground.

The **20th-century theological recovery** is rich. **Dietrich Bonhoeffer's *Cost of Discipleship*** engages the chapter's repentance-imperatives extensively. **Bonhoeffer's *Life Together*** engages 4:11-12 on community-speech-discipline. **Eugene Peterson** + **Henri Nouwen** + **Dallas Willard** + **Eugene H. Peterson** *A Long Obedience in the Same Direction* all engage the chapter pastorally. **Jacques Ellul** *The Presence of the Kingdom* + *The Subversion of Christianity* engage James 4:4 as foundational for Christian critical engagement with secular culture and political power.

The contemporary critical scholarship has substantially developed the chapter. **Bauckham** *James: Wisdom of James, Disciple of Jesus the Sage* (foundational); **Patrick Hartin** *A Spirituality of Perfection*; **Luke Timothy Johnson** *Anchor Bible*; **Peter Davids** *NIGTC*; **Douglas Moo** *Pillar*; **Scot McKnight** *NICNT*; **Sophie Laws** *BNTC*; **Dan McCartney** *BECNT*; **Mariam Kamell + Craig Blomberg** *Zondervan ECNT*. **John Painter** *Just James* on the Jacobean voice. **Jacques Ellul** + **Vinoth Ramachandra** on Christian engagement with worldly powers.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** All major Christian traditions converge on the chapter's basic ethics: friendship with the world is incompatible with relationship to God; humility is the entry-point to grace; presumptuous planning is moral failure. They differ on the specific interpretation of "the world" (Catholic: fallen humanity; Reformed: kingdom-of-the-world; Anabaptist: separated-community-required; Wesleyan: sanctifying-grace-cleanses) and on the specific institutional applications (monastic renunciation vs. world-engaged Christianity vs. political-resistance vs. internal-spiritual-discipline). The contemporary discussion has revived attention to the chapter's anti-consumerism implications and its critique of presumptuous economic planning.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author of James believe God was doing? The chapter's theology is operative throughout. God is the giver of grace to the humble (4:6); the one who hears prayer when offered with right purpose (4:3); the one who jealously yearns over the spirit he has placed within his people (4:5); the one near whom believers can draw (4:8); the one who exalts the humble (4:10); the **one Lawgiver and judge** (4:12); the one whose will alone determines life and plans (4:15). The chapter's signature theological move is the **binary of friendship**: friendship with the world is enmity with God; humility is the path to divine drawing-near; presumption is the structural failure of forgetting God's sovereignty.

**Tension One: "Friendship with the world is enmity with God" — what is the world?** The chapter's most-cited verse (4:4) raises the question of what "**the world**" (*ho kosmos*, ὁ κόσμος) means and how Christians should relate to it.

The Catholic tradition reads "the world" as **fallen-creation-in-opposition-to-God** while affirming creation's basic goodness (engage Vatican II *Gaudium et Spes* — "The Church in the Modern World"; **Gaudium et Spes 22** Christ as the meaning of human history). The tradition holds that Christians engage the world as transformative agents of grace; renunciation is one Christian vocation alongside engagement.

The Reformed tradition reads "the world" through **two-kingdoms theology** (Calvin *Institutes* 4.20; Luther's *Two Kingdoms*). Christians inhabit both kingdoms simultaneously; James 4:4 names the spiritual loyalty question while Christians continue to live and work in worldly structures.

The Anabaptist tradition reads "the world" as **the realm of violence-and-coercion** that Christian community must be separate from (engage **John Howard Yoder** *The Politics of Jesus*; **Stanley Hauerwas** *The Peaceable Kingdom*). The chapter's binary requires an alternative-community formation.

The Wesleyan-Holiness tradition reads "the world" as the **sphere of sin and worldly desire** that the Spirit's sanctifying work substantially overcomes (engage Wesley *A Plain Account of Christian Perfection*).

The Eastern Orthodox tradition reads "the world" through the broader theological-anthropological framework of human persons in relation to creation; engagement vs. renunciation are differently calibrated within monastic vs. lay vocations.

The contemporary anti-consumerist reading (Wendell Berry, Vinoth Ramachandra, Jacques Ellul) reads "the world" as **the consumer-capitalist civilization that constitutes the dominant secular cultural order**. James 4:4 challenges Christian assimilation to consumerism's values.

The position to steelman is the **Anabaptist separated-community** reading. It is sometimes treated as withdrawn or sectarian, but it takes seriously the chapter's binary in its starkest form. The chapter does not say "friendship with the world is in tension with God" — it says it is **enmity** (*echthra*). To soften this is to lose the chapter's force. The Anabaptist tradition has insisted on this starkness for five centuries; the contemporary recovery of Christian counter-cultural identity (Hauerwas, Yoder, Wendell Berry) draws on this tradition.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter's binary forces every Christian tradition to articulate where the line between friendship-with-world and faithfulness-to-God falls. The various traditions answer differently, but all are answering the same question James poses.

**Tension Two: "If the Lord wills" — providence, planning, and human agency.** The chapter's closing case (4:13-17) challenges the merchant's confidence in commercial planning by relativizing all human plans to divine sovereignty.

The classical Christian reading (Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin) develops the chapter into the **doctrine of providence**: God's eternal will encompasses all events; human plans operate under divine providence; the proper Christian disposition is to plan with humility before God's sovereignty. **Aquinas** *Summa Theologiae* I q. 22 + I-II q. 14 develops this; **Calvin** *Institutes* 1.16-18 likewise.

The Reformed tradition reads 4:15 as warrant for ongoing acknowledgment of divine sovereignty in Christian life — the **Deo volente** / "**D.V.**" tradition in Christian correspondence and conversation. Letters from the 17th-19th centuries routinely include "D.V." after future-plans-statements.

The Wesleyan-Arminian tradition reads the chapter as compatible with substantial human agency under divine permission and providence; the chapter's vocabulary does not require meticulous determinism.

The Open Theist reading (Greg Boyd, John Sanders) reads 4:15 as evidence that the future is genuinely contingent — God's will allows space for human choice; the merchant's error is not taking the contingent future seriously, not denying meticulous determinism.

The contemporary spiritual-formation reading (Dallas Willard, Eugene Peterson, Henri Nouwen) reads 4:15 as the foundation for a non-anxious life — Christians live within divine providence and therefore plan without the existential weight of needing to control outcomes.

The position to steelman is the Open Theist reading. It is sometimes resisted as theologically deficient by classical theists, but it takes seriously what the chapter says: the future is genuinely uncertain ("**you do not know what tomorrow will bring**" 4:14). The classical reading has often used divine sovereignty to qualify the chapter's emphasis on contingency to the point where the chapter's force is lost. The Open Theist reading honors the text's emphasis on the genuinely-unknown future while wrestling with broader providence-tradition.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter's "**if the Lord wills**" formula has shaped Christian planning-discipline for two millennia. Whether understood within meticulous-providence, permissive-providence, or open-future frameworks, the chapter's basic call is to plan-without-presumption, knowing that human agency always operates within a sovereignty larger than itself.

The unresolved question this chapter leaves us with: when the practices of presumptuous planning that James condemns are now built into the very structure of contemporary life — quarterly forecasts, retirement projections, ten-year strategic plans, AI-driven prediction systems — what does it mean to inhabit those structures while also remembering that we are mist?

## The Thread

**Proverbs 3:34 LXX.** "**God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble**" — the chapter cites at 4:6, and 1 Peter 5:5 cites the same verse. The double NT citation of the same Hebrew Bible verse is one of the strongest cross-NT inter-textual links. When you read the LXX of Proverbs 3, the Christian community's shared use of the verse indicates a common catechetical or liturgical tradition.

**Hosea 1-3, Jeremiah 2-3, Ezekiel 16, 23.** The OT prophetic-marriage tradition that James 4:4 inherits. The bride-of-YHWH is faithless when she pursues other gods; James applies this prophetic tradition to Christian-pagan-world syncretism. When you read the prophetic engagement with covenant-adultery, James 4:4 is the apostolic-letter formulation of the same theological move.

**Luke 12:13-21 (Parable of the Rich Fool).** The closest synoptic parallel to James 4:13-17. The rich fool says, "**Soul, you have ample goods laid up for many years; relax, eat, drink, be merry**" (Luke 12:19); God says, "**Fool! This night your soul is required of you**" (12:20). James warns the planning merchant in similar terms: "**you are a mist...**" When you reread the Lukan parable, James 4:13-17 is its apostolic-letter sibling.

**1 John 2:15-17.** "**Do not love the world or the things in the world. If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him.**" The Johannine epistolary parallel to James 4:4. The two NT writings articulate the same anti-worldliness ethics from different theological vocabularies. When you read 1 John 2, James 4:4 is its sibling text in the Catholic Epistles tradition.
