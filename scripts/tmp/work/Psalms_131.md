# Psalm 131 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 131 carries the heading *shir hamma'alot le-David* (שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת לְדָוִד, "A Song of Ascents. Of David"), making it one of four Davidic Songs of Ascents (122, 124, 131, 133). It is the shortest psalm in the Songs of Ascents collection — only three verses, fewer than thirty Hebrew words — and one of the shortest psalms in the Psalter as a whole, exceeded in brevity only by Psalm 117. Its compactness is itself thematically significant: a psalm of quiet trust speaks little.

Hermann Gunkel's form-critical taxonomy (*Einleitung in die Psalmen*, 1933) classified Psalm 131 as a psalm of trust (*Vertrauenslied*) of the individual, related to but distinct from individual lament. Sigmund Mowinckel agreed, treating it as a personal confession of trust possibly used in a temple-entrance liturgy where worshippers attested their humble approach before being admitted to the sanctuary (compare Pss 15 and 24's gate-questions). Erhard Gerstenberger (*Psalms Part 2*, FOTL 2001) reads it as a wisdom-trust hybrid; Hossfeld and Zenger (*Psalms 3*, Hermeneia 2011) treat it as an individual psalm of trust deliberately positioned after the *de profundis* of Psalm 130 as the answering posture — the calm soul that follows the cry from the depths.

The historical setting is debated. Conservative readings, following the Davidic superscription, locate the psalm in David's own life — perhaps after the rebellion of Absalom, when the chastened king learns the limits of royal ambition (so Spurgeon and many traditional readings). Critical readings discount the superscription as later attribution and place the psalm in the post-exilic period; Hossfeld and Zenger argue for a setting in late Persian or early Hellenistic Yehud, with the psalm's quiet trust as the answering posture to a community that had learned through exile the futility of *gedolot u-niflaot* (great and marvelous things) pursued by human grasping. The Davidic superscription, on this reading, is honorific — placing the psalm in the Davidic spiritual lineage without claiming his historical authorship.

A particularly important interpretive question concerns whether the speaker is male or female. The two principal images in the psalm — humility before something too great, and the weaned child with its mother — work in either direction. The maternal language of verse 2 has led some commentators (e.g., Adele Berlin, Marc Brettler) to suggest the original speaker may have been a woman, perhaps a temple petitioner whose voice survived in this rare form. The grammatical evidence is inconclusive — the verbs and adjectives could fit either gender — but the question has remained a live one in scholarship on the psalm.

The Hebrew opens with *Yahweh lo-gavah libbi ve-lo-ramu einai ve-lo-hillakhti bi-gdolot u-vniflaot mimmenni* (יְהוָה לֹא־גָבַהּ לִבִּי וְלֹא־רָמוּ עֵינַי וְלֹא־הִלַּכְתִּי בִּגְדֹלוֹת וּבְנִפְלָאוֹת מִמֶּנִּי, "O Lord, my heart is not lifted up, my eyes are not raised too high, I do not occupy myself with things too great and too marvelous for me"). The opening triple negation *lo... ve-lo... ve-lo* is rhetorically striking — three "not"s establish the quiet posture as a deliberate refusal of three temptations. The verb *gavah* (to be high, lifted up) is precisely the word used in Isaiah 2:11 and 5:15 for the haughtiness God will bring low; the speaker explicitly disclaims the very disposition that scripture treats as the essence of pride. The phrase *ramu einai* (my eyes are not raised) draws on the wisdom commonplace that proud eyes are an abomination (Prov 6:17, 21:4, 30:13).

The phrase *bi-gdolot u-vniflaot mimmenni* is theologically dense. *Gedolot* (great things) and *niflaot* (marvelous, wondrous things) are normally Israel's vocabulary for praise — God's *gedolot* are God's mighty acts, God's *niflaot* are God's wonders (Pss 71:19, 86:10, 96:3, 106:21–22). The psalm uses these positive divine vocabulary terms negatively as the proper object of God's action that the human speaker must not arrogate. The Hebrew literally reads "I do not walk in great-things and in wondrous-things [too great] from-me" — "from me" being a comparative meaning "beyond me" or "above my station." The speaker does not pursue divine territory.

Verse 2's image is among the most striking and tender in the Psalter. *Im lo shivviti ve-domamti nafshi ke-gamul alei immo ka-gamul alai nafshi* (אִם־לֹא שִׁוִּיתִי וְדוֹמַמְתִּי נַפְשִׁי כְּגָמֻל עֲלֵי אִמּוֹ כַּגָּמֻל עָלַי נַפְשִׁי, "but I have calmed and quieted my soul, like a weaned child with its mother; like a weaned child is my soul within me"). The verb *shivviti* (from *shavah* — to set, place, level, smooth) describes the deliberate composing of the inner self, while *domamti* (from *damam* — to be still, silent) intensifies it. The double simile *ke-gamul alei immo ka-gamul alai nafshi* is one of the Psalter's most carefully constructed parallels: the weaned child with-his-mother / the weaned child upon-me-my-soul. The repetition of *gamul* in both halves of the simile drives home the central image.

The word *gamul* (גָּמֻל) is decisive and has been intensely debated. The verb *gamal* in Hebrew has a primary meaning of "to deal fully with, to ripen, to wean" — when applied to children, it specifically means "to wean," to bring through the painful transition from breastfeeding to independent eating, a process that in the ancient Near East could last to age three or even later (see Hannah's promise about Samuel in 1 Sam 1:22–24, which describes this exact weaning process). The image is therefore not of a nursing infant clinging to the breast — that would be *yoneq* — but of a child who has been weaned, has gone through the ordeal of separation from the breast, and has emerged into a new and quieter relationship with the mother. The *gamul* still seeks the mother but no longer for nourishment from her body; the relationship has matured past urgent dependence into peaceful proximity.

Mitchell Dahood's *Psalms* commentary (1970) registered the importance of this distinction: the weaned child is not the symbol of helpless need but of trained, contented presence. The maternal imagery of verse 2 is one of the Hebrew Bible's clearest cases of God implicitly likened to a mother — not in a passing simile but in the central image of one of the most beloved psalms — and it sits alongside Isaiah 49:15, 66:13, and Hosea 11:3 in the canon's stock of maternal-divine figures. Catholic theology has historically read the verse as a model of the soul's quiet trust; the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* (§239) appeals to this and other maternal-divine images to support its teaching that God transcends human gender categories.

Verse 3's *yachel Yisrael el-Yahweh me-attah ve-ad olam* (יַחֵל יִשְׂרָאֵל אֶל־יְהוָה מֵעַתָּה וְעַד־עוֹלָם, "let Israel hope in the Lord from this time forth and forevermore") closes the psalm with the same corporate exhortation that closed Psalm 130 — *yachel Yisrael el-Yahweh*. The repetition is structural: 130 and 131 share this closing phrase as deliberate liturgical pairing, with 131's added *me-attah ve-ad olam* extending the temporal scope.

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve Psalm 131 in 11QPs(a) and 4QPs(e). The Septuagint numbers it Psalm 130 and renders the *gamul* with *apogegalaktismenon* (weaned), preserving the precise sense. The Vulgate's *sicut ablactatus est super matre sua* (literally, "as the weaned-one upon his mother") follows the same reading.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Psalm 131 falls into three short movements: verse 1 (the disclaimer of pride), verse 2 (the image of the weaned child), and verse 3 (the corporate exhortation). The compactness is the message — a psalm of quiet trust does not sprawl.

Lowth's parallelism types are deployed across the small space. Verse 1's *Yahweh lo-gavah libbi / ve-lo-ramu einai / ve-lo-hillakhti bi-gdolot u-vniflaot mimmenni* is synonymous parallelism in three cola, the triplet structure marking the verse as a complete inventory of refused temptations. The parallel members are body-parts (*libbi*/heart, *einai*/eyes) followed by activity (*hillakhti*/I do not walk), creating a movement from inner to outer that traces the full landscape of pride.

Verse 2 is one of the Psalter's most tightly constructed similes. *Im lo shivviti ve-domamti nafshi / ke-gamul alei immo / ka-gamul alai nafshi*. The first colon describes the inner act ("I have calmed and quieted my soul"); the second introduces the simile ("like a weaned child upon his mother"); the third doubles back with *gamul* applied to the soul itself ("as the weaned child, so my soul upon me"). The chiastic repetition *ke-gamul... ka-gamul* is one of the Psalter's clearest examples of staircase or repetitive parallelism in compressed form. Wilfred Watson (*Classical Hebrew Poetry*, 1984) classifies this construction as *envelope simile*, where the same figure brackets the verse from both sides. Robert Alter (*The Book of Psalms*, 2007) emphasizes the artistic perfection of the verse: the most concentrated maternal imagery in the Hebrew Bible, expressed in two parallel cola of identical structure.

Verse 3 stands alone as the closing exhortation, identical in opening to Psalm 130:7 (*yachel Yisrael el-Yahweh*). The deliberate echo creates an intentional pairing of the two psalms: 130 cries from the depths and ends with corporate hope; 131 settles into the calm of the weaned child and ends with the same corporate hope, extended by *me-attah ve-ad olam* (from now and forever).

The phonology of the psalm is striking. Verses 1–2 are dominated by softer, quieter consonants — *libbi*, *einai*, *shivviti*, *domamti*, *immo* — appropriate to the calm soul. The triple liquid consonants of *gamul alei immo* (g-m-l / l-y / m-m-o) are smooth and rounded, almost musical. The contrast with the harder consonants of pride-language (*gavah*, *ramu*, *gedolot*) sets up an audible polarity between the two states.

Erich Zenger emphasizes the deliberate placement of Psalm 131 between 130 and 132 in the Songs of Ascents. The cry from the depths (130) becomes the calm of the weaned child (131) becomes the great Davidic-temple psalm (132). The brevity of 131 is emphasized by the length of its neighbors; it functions as a pause, a still point in the pilgrim journey.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Within Book V (Pss 107–150) and the Songs of Ascents, Psalm 131 occupies a deliberate position. The third sub-group of the Songs of Ascents (130–134) traces a movement from individual penitence (130) through individual trust (131) through covenantal memory (132) through corporate unity (133) to corporate praise (134). Psalm 131 is the personal heart of this movement — the psalm where the individual soul learns to be still, after the depths of 130 and before the corporate movements of 132–134.

In Jewish trajectory the psalm has had quieter liturgical use than its more dramatic neighbors but a deep spiritual presence. *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 131 reads David as the speaker, articulating the humility he learned through the various humbling events of his life — the rebellion of Absalom, the Bathsheba scandal, the rejection of his temple-building. Rashi reads verse 1 as David's disclaimer that he attempted royal feats beyond his measure, and verse 2 as the model of every Israelite's proper posture before God. Ibn Ezra emphasizes the universality — the speaker's calm soul is the model for every soul. Radak (David Kimhi) develops the *gamul* image as describing the spiritual life beyond infantile demand: the weaned child is the soul that has matured past clamoring for divine gifts and learned to rest in divine presence. Nachmanides (Ramban) reads the verse as a model for prophetic and contemplative life — the silent soul as the precondition for receiving prophecy.

In modern Jewish thought Martin Buber treated the psalm as paradigmatic of the I-Thou relation in its quietest mode. Abraham Joshua Heschel cited verse 2 in *Man Is Not Alone* (1951) as one of the deepest scriptural statements of contemplative trust. Jon Levenson (*Sinai and Zion*, 1985) places the psalm within his broader argument about the Temple as a place where ambitious imperial theology gives way to the quieter confession of dependence. Nahum Sarna (*Songs of the Heart*, 1993) emphasizes the psalm's countercultural force — in a world built on ambition, the psalm celebrates its renunciation. Avivah Zornberg has written movingly on the *gamul* image as a psychological achievement: the weaned soul has done the inner work of separating from urgent need, and the maternal proximity that remains is the deeper and more durable bond.

In Christian trajectory Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 130 (LXX numbering) develops the weaned-child image as the soul's posture before God — the soul that has been weaned from the milk of worldly comforts and now rests in the mother of contemplation. Augustine's *Confessions*, especially in its treatment of the weaning of Augustine's own attachments to lesser goods, draws implicitly on Psalm 131. Cassiodorus develops the verse 1 disclaimer as the proper monastic posture — the monk does not pursue *gedolot u-niflaot* but accepts the small allotment of his rule. Aquinas's *Postilla* connects verse 2 to his theology of contemplation: the calmed soul is the soul prepared for the *visio beatifica*.

The medieval mystical tradition made Psalm 131 a central text. *The Cloud of Unknowing* (anonymous, late 14th century) cites the weaned-child image as a model of the apophatic soul — the soul that has been weaned from positive images of God and rests in unknowing presence. The Hesychast tradition of Eastern Orthodox monasticism — the *prayer of the heart* developed by figures like Gregory Palamas — treats Psalm 131 as articulating the quiet of *hesychia* itself.

The Reformers received the psalm with appreciative care. Luther's commentary treats verse 2's *gamul* with the central Reformation theological move: the weaned soul is the soul that has stopped attempting to manipulate God through religious works and rests in pure faith. Calvin's 1557 commentary develops verse 1 as the renunciation of speculative theology — the believer does not pursue divine mysteries beyond his capacity but accepts the gifts of revelation as given. Calvin's *Institutes* I.13.21 cites Psalm 131:1 as the basis for theological humility. The Puritan tradition, especially Charles Spurgeon's *Treasury of David*, made Psalm 131 a frequent text for sermons on contentment and child-like trust; Spurgeon's chapter on Psalm 131 is among the most beloved in the *Treasury*.

The Catholic mystical tradition continued to develop the maternal-divine imagery. Julian of Norwich (*Revelations of Divine Love*, late 14th century) had developed an extensive theology of Christ as Mother that drew on Psalm 131 and similar texts; her famous "as truly as God is our Father, so truly God is our Mother" finds liturgical anchoring in the weaned-child image. Modern Catholic theology, especially through *Catechism* §239 and the writings of Hans Urs von Balthasar, has continued to develop the maternal imagery of God without compromising the predominant paternal imagery — the *Catechism* is explicit that God transcends all human gender categories while choosing to be addressed primarily as Father.

Modern critical readers have placed the psalm within wider studies of trust, contemplation, and humility. Westermann's *Praise and Lament* treats it as the paradigmatic psalm of quiet trust. Kraus's *Psalmen* commentary (1960) develops the post-exilic setting. Hossfeld and Zenger's three-volume Hermeneia treats the psalm as the central trust-confession of the third sub-group of the Songs of Ascents. John Goldingay's *Psalms 90–150* (2008) emphasizes the psychological depth of the *gamul* image. Walter Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*, 1984) treats Psalm 131 as a paradigmatic psalm of new orientation — the trust that emerges on the far side of disorientation. Patrick Miller (*They Cried to the Lord*, 1994), William Brown (*Seeing the Psalms*, 2002), and Beth Tanner (*NICOT Psalms*, 2014) all develop attentive readings of the maternal imagery and the calmed soul.

Eugene Peterson's chapter on Psalm 131 in *A Long Obedience in the Same Direction* (1980), titled "Humility," is one of the most-cited modern devotional treatments of the psalm. Peterson reads the psalm against the grain of American religious ambition: humility is not the failure of ambition but its mature outgrowing. His reception has shaped a generation of evangelical and mainline pastoral practice. Charles Spurgeon's *Treasury of David* on Psalm 131 likewise remains widely read.

The New Testament does not directly quote Psalm 131, but Jesus' teaching that "unless you turn and become like children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven" (Matt 18:3, with parallels Mark 10:15, Luke 18:17) inhabits the same imaginative world. Paul's "I have learned, in whatever situation, to be content" (Phil 4:11) echoes the psalm's posture. The maternal imagery is taken up by Jesus' lament over Jerusalem ("how often I would have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings," Matt 23:37) and by Paul's "we were gentle among you, like a nursing mother taking care of her own children" (1 Thess 2:7). The corporate exhortation *yachel Yisrael el-Yahweh* of verse 3 is the Old Testament template for Hebrews 6:18–19's "we who have fled for refuge to hold fast to the hope set before us. We have this as a sure and steadfast anchor of the soul."

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

Two tensions run through the reception of Psalm 131.

The first concerns the relationship between humility and ambition implied in verse 1. The speaker disclaims involvement with *gedolot u-niflaot* — great and marvelous things — and refuses to lift up the heart or raise the eyes high. Is this a permanent prohibition on ambition, an instruction to all believers to remain in their place? Or is it the speech of one who has done great things and is reflecting back, learning from chastening?

The traditional reading, articulated by Augustine, Aquinas, and the monastic tradition, treats verse 1 as a model for all believers: the speaker disclaims *gedolot* not because he never pursued them but because he has learned that the soul's rest is found by accepting one's measure rather than reaching beyond it. The strength of this reading is its reception of the verse's plain force. Its weakness, on which Reformation and modern readers have insisted, is that it can collapse into a quietism that discourages legitimate vocation and faithful enterprise.

The Reformation reading, articulated by Luther and Calvin, treats verse 1 as a renunciation of religious-spiritual ambition specifically — the attempt to manipulate God or to penetrate divine mysteries through one's own striving — rather than a disclaimer of all human enterprise. The believer pursues honest vocation, but does not pretend to pursue divine *gedolot* in his own strength. The strength is its preservation of vocational seriousness; its weakness is the difficulty of distinguishing religious from secular ambition cleanly.

A third reading, developed in modern psychology of religion (Henri Nouwen, *The Inner Voice of Love*; Eugene Peterson, *A Long Obedience*; Richard Rohr's writings on the second half of life), treats verse 1 as describing the spiritual achievement of mid-life and beyond — the psalm of one who has done much, has been chastened by both success and failure, and has emerged into the calm of accepted limitation. The young soul properly pursues *gedolot*; the mature soul has learned to release them. Its strength is its developmental sophistication; its weakness is that it can patronize the young soul that has not yet earned the calm.

Where this leaves us is something close to a synthesis. The psalm models a permanent posture for prayer regardless of vocational scale — the worshipper does not approach God with grasping or speculation. But the psalm also describes a particular life-stage, the soul that has been weaned and emerged into the calmer relationship beyond urgent demand. Both readings are scriptural. To pray Psalm 131 honestly is to pray in the calm posture even when one is still living in the world of *gedolot*, and to honor the developmental truth that the calm comes more easily to the weaned than the nursing.

The second tension concerns the maternal divine imagery of verse 2 and how it should function in Christian and Jewish theology. The image of the weaned child with its mother is one of the Hebrew Bible's most explicit maternal images for divine comfort, and its reception has been shaped by ongoing debates about feminine and masculine language for God.

The traditionalist position, articulated in classical Christian (Catholic, Orthodox, Reformed) theology, holds that God is properly addressed in the masculine — Father, Son, Lord — as God has revealed himself, and that maternal images like Psalm 131:2 are similes (God is *like* a mother in this respect) rather than ontological identifications. The strength is its respect for the dominant biblical-revelational pattern; its weakness, on which critics have insisted, is that it can subordinate the genuine biblical witness to feminine divine imagery into mere rhetorical decoration.

A second position holds that the biblical witness to feminine divine imagery (Ps 131:2; Isa 49:15; 66:13; Hos 11:3; Matt 23:37) deserves real theological weight alongside the masculine, and that an exclusively masculine address of God risks treating those texts as ornamental rather than as genuine revelation of the divine nature. The strength is its recovery of underused biblical resources; its weakness, on which traditionalists insist, is that the Bible's own masculine address is so dominant that any rebalancing must be bounded by the actual biblical pattern.

A third position, articulated in *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §239 and developed by Hans Urs von Balthasar, John Paul II's *Theology of the Body*, and recent magisterial Catholic theology, holds that God genuinely transcends human gender categories — God is neither male nor female but the source of both — while choosing to reveal himself primarily under the paternal name. The maternal imagery of Psalm 131:2 is therefore real revelation of the divine nature, not mere decoration, but it is properly integrated with rather than substituted for the predominant paternal imagery. The strength is its careful preservation of both biblical patterns; its weakness is the difficulty of articulating the synthesis without privileging one term.

Where this leaves us is a recognition that Psalm 131:2 is genuine biblical witness to maternal divine imagery, that it functions theologically rather than rhetorically, that it transcends but does not dissolve the predominant paternal address, and that it offers a particular consolation that the paternal images do not always carry. The mother who allows the weaned child to rest beside her without nursing him is a particular biblical icon of God whose grace is presence rather than transaction. To pray Psalm 131 is to allow oneself to be that weaned child, with all the specific intimacy that image requires.

The closing question is one the psalm itself implies. What *gedolot u-niflaot* — what great and marvelous things, religious or secular — are you currently striving after that exceed your measure, and what would it mean to come, today, with calmed and quieted soul, like a weaned child upon its mother, to the God whose grace is finally not what you can reach for but the presence beside whom you can rest?
