# Psalm 5: Morning Sacrifice and the Open Grave

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Psalm 5 carries the superscription *lamenatzeach el-hannechilot mizmor le-david* — "to the choirmaster: for the flutes [or: inheritances]. A Psalm of David." The term *el-hannechilot* (אֶל־הַנְּחִילוֹת) appears **only here in the Psalter** and is one of the more contested superscription terms. Three principal proposals: (1) **wind instruments / flutes** — from the root *chalal* (חָלַל), "to bore through, to pierce" — the same root behind *chalil*, the pierced reed-flute used in temple and mourning music (cf. 1 Kgs 1:40, Isa 30:29). The LXX renders *hyper tes kleronomouses* ("for the inheriting one"), suggesting the translator read *nachalah* (נַחֲלָה, inheritance). (2) **Inheritances** — from *nachalah*, suggesting a tune-name or a thematic association with Israel's heritage. (3) **A specific tune name** — "the Inheritances" or "the Flutes" as the opening words of a now-lost melody to which the psalm was set. Mowinckel and Kraus argue for a wind-instrument reference; Hossfeld-Zenger admit the term's obscurity; Goldingay translates "for the wind instruments" while noting the uncertainty. The most defensible position: probable wind-instrument reference, with genuine philological uncertainty.

Form-critically, Psalm 5 is an **individual lament with cult-orientation** in Hermann Gunkel's classification, sharing the form-elements Westermann identifies (invocation, complaint, petition, motivation, vow of praise). Mowinckel placed it specifically in the **morning-temple cult** — the *boker* (morning) language in v. 3 ("in the morning you hear my voice; in the morning I prepare a sacrifice for you and watch") and the explicit reference to entering the temple house and bowing toward the holy temple in v. 7 fix the *Sitz im Leben* in the cultic morning sacrifice (*tamid shel shachar*; cf. Exod 29:38-42). The speaker is approaching the temple at dawn for the daily morning offering, and the psalm is the prayer offered at the altar, in the temple court, while the offering is prepared.

Date and setting: classical pre-exilic Hebrew, no late linguistic features. The temple references (vv. 7, *heikhal qodshekha*, "your holy temple") fix the setting in either the first temple (pre-exilic) or the second (post-exilic); the cultic specificity suggests pre-exilic origin with continued use throughout the second temple period.

The material culture is the temple complex at dawn. The verb *arak* (עָרַךְ) in v. 3 — "I prepare" — is the technical verb for *arranging* the morning sacrifice on the altar, the same verb used in Lev 1:7-8 for arranging the wood and the dismembered offering. The Hebrew text is brief: *baboker e'erokh-lekha*, "in the morning I arrange [it] for you" — and the implied object is the *olah* (whole burnt offering) or the *minchah* (grain offering) that constituted the daily *tamid*. The verb *atzappeh* (אֲצַפֶּה) — "I watch / I keep watch" — refers to the watchman's posture: standing on the temple steps, watching for the dawn or for the divine response, the same verb used in Hab 2:1 ("I will take my stand at my watchpost"). The "spread your protection over them" of v. 11 (*tasekh*, from *sakkak*, "to cover, to overshadow") evokes the cherubim's wings over the ark — Yahweh's protective covering. ANE parallels: Mesopotamian *šigû* lament-prayers offered at temple gates, Egyptian morning hymns at the Karnak sun-temple, and Ugaritic ritual texts in which the king or priest watches for the dawn-god from the temple platform.

Hebrew terms controlling the psalm:

- **חֶסֶד** (*chesed*, 5:7) — "steadfast love / covenant loyalty / lovingkindness." The single most theologically loaded word in the Hebrew Bible — covenant-fidelity that goes beyond what is owed. The speaker enters the temple *be-rov chasdekha*, "through the abundance of your *chesed*." The grammar is explicit: the speaker is not earning entry by his righteousness; he is being admitted by Yahweh's covenant-loyalty.
- **קֶבֶר־פָּתוּחַ** (*qever-patuach*, 5:9) — "open grave." The metaphor of v. 9 — "their throat is an open grave" — depicts the enemies' speech as a yawning tomb, exhaling decay, swallowing victims. **Romans 3:13** quotes this verse (LXX) directly in Paul's catena of Old Testament citations on universal sin.
- **חוֹסֵי בָךְ** (*chosei vakh*, 5:11) — "those who take refuge in you." Same root (*chasah*, חָסָה) as the closing of Psalm 2 (*ashrei kol chosei vo*) — the *inclusio* between Pss 2 and 5 marks the refuge-language as a recurring framing motif of the opening of the Psalter.
- **הֵיכָל** (*heikhal*, 5:7) — "temple / palace." The word denotes the temple proper (the *naos*, the holy place) as Yahweh's royal palace; the speaker bows toward it, the gesture of approach.
- **אַשְׁרֵי** is *not* present in Ps 5, but **בָּרַךְ** (*barak*, 5:12) — "bless" — closes the psalm: *attah tevarekh tzaddiq*, "you bless the righteous." The closing benediction-pattern echoes the *ashrei*-benediction of Pss 1 and 2.

Textual evidence: Psalm 5 is preserved at Qumran in **4QPsa** in MT-aligned form. The LXX renders the superscription's *el-hannechilot* as *hyper tes kleronomouses* ("for the inheriting one"), preserving the alternate "inheritances" reading. Romans 3:13's citation of v. 9 follows the LXX wording.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Psalm 5 is twelve verses, a classical individual lament structured according to Westermann's form-elements:

- **vv. 1-3** — invocation of Yahweh, framed in the morning/cultic moment
- **vv. 4-6** — declaration of Yahweh's character: he does not delight in wickedness
- **v. 7** — pivot to confession of trust, the speaker entering the temple
- **v. 8** — petition for guidance
- **vv. 9-10** — complaint against enemies and imprecation
- **vv. 11-12** — closing benediction over the righteous

The structure has a chiastic shape: vv. 1-3 (speaker addresses Yahweh) / vv. 4-6 (Yahweh's character) / v. 7 (speaker enters Yahweh's house) / v. 8 (petition) / vv. 9-10 (enemies' character and Yahweh's judgment) / vv. 11-12 (refuge-takers' blessing). The center of the chiasm is the temple-entry of v. 7, marked grammatically by the emphatic *va-ani* — "but I."

Robert Lowth's parallelism: synonymous in v. 1 ("give ear to my words / consider my groaning"); antithetic in vv. 4-6 against vv. 11-12 (the wicked Yahweh hates / the righteous Yahweh blesses); climactic-staircase in v. 9 ("there is no truth in their mouth / their inmost self is destruction / their throat is an open grave / they flatter with their tongue"). James Kugel and Robert Alter both note Ps 5's tightly controlled imagery — every metaphor is anatomical (mouth, inmost self, throat, tongue) and the cumulative effect is of a body whose every speaking part is corrupted.

The morning emphasis is poetic and theological. *Boker* (morning) appears twice in v. 3 — *baboker tishma kolei / baboker e'erokh-lekha* — and the doubling marks the time-stamp emphatically. The morning is the temple's dawn sacrifice, the psalm's situation, and the metaphor for new mercy. Pss 3, 5, 88, 130 all share the morning-prayer pattern; Pss 4, 134 the evening counterpart.

The imprecation in v. 10 (*ha'ashimem Elohim, yipplu mi-mo'atzoteihem* — "make them bear their guilt, O God; let them fall by their own counsels") is concrete. The speaker prays not for personal vengeance but for divine retribution that mirrors the enemies' own designs back upon them — *yipplu mi-mo'atzoteihem*, "let them fall by their own counsels." The justice imagined is poetic: the wicked are caught in their own snare (cf. Ps 7:15-16, 9:15-16, 35:7-8). This pattern — Yahweh letting the wicked's own schemes destroy them — is one of the Psalter's most-repeated theological structures.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Placement

Pss 3-7 form a tight cluster of Davidic individual laments establishing the lament-shape of Book I. Psalm 5 is the third entry — morning prayer following Ps 3 (also morning) and Ps 4 (evening). The editorial pattern: orientation (Pss 1-2), followed by a series of laments (Pss 3-7) in which the anointed king prays through hostility, slander, and threat. Brueggemann's grid: Pss 1-2 are orientation, Pss 3-7 are disorientation, and the pattern of lament-into-trust will recur throughout the Psalter.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

**4QPsa** at Qumran preserves Ps 5 in stable MT-aligned form. **Midrash Tehillim** on Psalm 5 reads the morning sacrifice as the reader's daily Torah study and prayer — a typical rabbinic spiritualization of the cult after the temple's destruction. **Rashi** (eleventh century) reads the psalm as David's prayer against Doeg the Edomite and the slanderers around Saul, and treats v. 9's "open grave" as the slander that destroys reputations. **Ibn Ezra** treats *el-hannechilot* as a musical-instrument designation. **Radak (David Kimhi)** reads v. 7's "abundance of your steadfast love" as the gift that admits the unworthy speaker into the temple — Kimhi explicitly notes that the speaker enters not by his own merit but by Yahweh's *chesed*. **Nachmanides** treats the psalm as the soul's daily approach to God in prayer.

In modern Jewish scholarship, **Nahum Sarna** (*Songs of the Heart*) emphasizes the cultic specificity of v. 3's morning sacrifice and reads the psalm as the personal-prayer counterpart to the daily *tamid*. **Jon Levenson** treats the psalm as a paradigm of the temple-entry liturgy. **Avivah Zornberg** reads the morning-psalm tradition through the lens of Jewish dawn piety.

Liturgically, Psalm 5 is **not** part of the *Shir Shel Yom* daily cycle (Sun=24, Mon=48, Tue=82, Wed=94, Thu=81, Fri=93, Sat=92), is **not** in *Hallel* or *Pesukei deZimra*, and does not appear in the standard Jewish daily liturgy. Its function is canonical-Davidic rather than liturgical-recurrent.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

**Augustine's** *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 5 reads it as the church's morning prayer, with *baboker* (the morning) doubled in v. 3 referring to two mornings — the morning of this life and the morning of resurrection. The "open grave" of v. 9 is read as the corrupt speech of the wicked from which Christ delivers his people. **Athanasius's** *Letter to Marcellinus* recommends Ps 5 for the soul under slander. **Cassiodorus** systematizes. **Aquinas** in his *Postilla* reads the psalm fourfold.

**Calvin's** 1557 commentary makes Ps 5 paradigmatic of morning prayer: the believer rises at dawn, "arranges" his prayer to God as the priest arranges the sacrifice, and watches for the divine response. **Luther's** *Operationes in Psalmos* reads v. 7 — "through the abundance of your steadfast love I will enter your house" — as the sola-gratia center of the psalm: the speaker is admitted by Yahweh's *chesed*, not by his own merit. **Bonhoeffer** in *Psalms: The Prayer Book of the Bible* uses Pss 3, 4, 5 as paradigmatic morning/evening framing.

The historical-critical tradition (**Gunkel, Mowinckel, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate, Allen, Wilson**) has placed the psalm in the lament-with-cultic-orientation category. **Walter Brueggemann's** treatment in *The Message of the Psalms* and especially in *The Costly Loss of Lament* and *Praying the Psalms* takes Ps 5 as a paradigmatic case of how lament gives shape to grievance without authorizing violence. **Erich Zenger's** *A God of Vengeance? Understanding the Psalms of Divine Wrath* (1994) treats v. 10's imprecation in detail and argues that the imprecatory Psalms are protests against violence rather than authorizations of it. **Beth Tanner**, **Patrick Miller**, and **William P. Brown** all build on the canonical-shaping reading.

Liturgically, Psalm 5 has been used in Christian morning prayer (Lauds) since at least the patristic era. The Roman Breviary, the Benedictine Rule (chapter 13 prescribes Ps 5 for Lauds on Monday), and the Anglican Book of Common Prayer all preserve the morning-prayer use. **Patrick Henry Reardon** (*Christ in the Psalms*, Eastern Orthodox) treats the psalm as the church's dawn approach to the heavenly temple.

### NT Use

**Romans 3:13** quotes v. 9 (LXX) — *taphos aneogmenos ho larynx auton, tais glossais auton edoliousan* — "their throat is an open grave; with their tongues they have practiced deceit" — as part of Paul's catena of Old Testament citations (Pss 14:1-3, 5:9, 140:3, 10:7, Isa 59:7-8, Ps 36:1) demonstrating universal human sinfulness. The Pauline citation reads v. 9's accusation against the wicked enemies as applying to *all* humanity ("there is no one righteous, not even one"). This is one of the most theologically consequential NT citations of a Psalter verse — Paul universalizes the psalm's particular accusation into an anthropological diagnosis. The honest critical question — whether Paul's universalization fairly handles the psalm's specific complaint — has been debated by Jewish-Christian dialogue scholars (Levenson, Boyarin) and by Pauline scholars (Dunn, Wright). **N. T. Wright** (in his Romans work and *The Day the Revolution Began*) reads the Romans 3 catena, including the citation of Ps 5:9, not as a flat proof of "total depravity" abstracted from Israel's story but as Paul's indictment of the whole human race — Jew and Gentile alike — within the covenant-historical argument that leads to the revealing of God's righteousness in the Messiah; the open-grave throat of the wicked is, on this reading, the symptom of an exile that the gospel addresses.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author's vision is that the speaker, slandered by enemies whose mouths are open graves, approaches the temple at dawn for morning sacrifice and prayer. He cannot enter on his own merit; he enters *be-rov chasdekha*, through the abundance of Yahweh's covenant-loyalty. The structural center of the psalm is the temple-entry of v. 7 — admittance by *chesed*, not by self-righteousness. The speaker's enemies are *destroyed* by Yahweh (v. 6) and *fall by their own counsels* (v. 10); the speaker is *blessed* and *covered with favor as with a shield* (v. 12). The psalm is a complete cultic-prayer cycle compressed into twelve verses — the morning's drama from the watchman's first cry to the dawn benediction.

### Tension One: The Imprecation in v. 10 vs. Christian Ethics

"Make them bear their guilt, O God; let them fall by their own counsels; because of the abundance of their transgressions cast them out, for they have rebelled against you" (5:10). This is one of the more direct imprecatory verses in the Psalter. How should it be read in light of Christian ethics — particularly Jesus' teaching to love enemies and bless those who curse you (Matt 5:43-48; Luke 6:27-36)?

- **Augustinian / patristic spiritualizing reading**: the enemies are spiritual — sin, the flesh, the devil. The imprecation is real, but its objects are metaphysical-moral. This was the dominant reading from Augustine through the Middle Ages and is still defended by Eastern Orthodox readers (Reardon). It avoids the ethical conflict at the cost of treating the psalm's concrete enemies as code for abstractions.
- **Davidic-historical / Reformed reading (Calvin, contemporary Reformed)**: the enemies are real human enemies, and the imprecation is a real prayer for their judicial overthrow. The Christian who reads the psalm should distinguish between *praying* the imprecation (handing the case to God) and *enacting* it (vengeance with one's own hand). The Sermon on the Mount forbids the second; the Psalter teaches the first. This is the classic Calvinist position.
- **Walter Brueggemann's reading** (*The Message of the Psalms*, *Praying the Psalms*, *The Costly Loss of Lament*): the imprecatory Psalms give the believer a *language for rage* that is otherwise repressed and acted out in violence. To pray the imprecation is to *not* act it. The psalm is therapeutic and pastoral — it acknowledges the speaker's murderous anger and gives it form-as-prayer rather than form-as-deed. Brueggemann argues that suppressing the imprecatory Psalms (as much modern liturgy does) has cost the church its capacity to handle rage and grief honestly.
- **Erich Zenger's reading** (*A God of Vengeance? Understanding the Psalms of Divine Wrath*, 1994): the imprecatory Psalms are *protests against violence*, not authorizations of it. The victim cries out to God for justice precisely because justice is not available in the human courts of the powerful. To silence the imprecatory voice is to silence the victim. Zenger's reading has been formative for contemporary post-Holocaust theology — the imprecation is the cry of the oppressed handed to God for justice, not a license for the powerful to enact violence in God's name.
- **Pacifist / Anabaptist reading (Yoder, Hauerwas)**: the imprecation is real prayer, but its only legitimate ethical implication is *to refuse to enact violence oneself*. The believer prays "make them bear their guilt" precisely so that he does not have to make them bear it. The cross is the answer: Christ absorbed the imprecation rather than enacting it.
- **Jewish reading (Sarna, Levenson, Heschel)**: the imprecation is at home in the Hebrew Bible's frank acknowledgment of injustice and the speaker's right to lament. Christian readers who find the verse uncomfortable should reckon with the fact that the Hebrew Bible — which Jesus prayed — contains this language and was not embarrassed by it.
- **Critical-honest reading**: the imprecation is real, it cannot be sanitized, and it reflects an ancient theological framework in which enemies of the speaker are presumed to be enemies of God. Modern readers should pray it carefully, with self-examination — am I the slandered psalmist or the open-graved enemy? — and should not assume that the enemies of one's own party are *therefore* the enemies of God.

**Steelmanning the least-intuitive position** — the Brueggemann/Zenger reading that imprecation is *protest against violence*, not authorization of it. To readers who treat the imprecation as morally embarrassing and prefer to skip these verses, the protest reading sounds counterintuitive: how can a prayer for enemies' destruction be a protest *against* destruction? But the structural argument is serious. In an ancient society without judicial recourse for the slandered, the only place to take grievance was God. The imprecation is the speaker handing the case to the only court available, and *because* the case is handed to God, the speaker is freed from having to act on the rage himself. Brueggemann documents the cost of suppressing this voice: when the church loses the imprecatory tradition, its members do not stop being angry; they act the anger out, often violently, often in religion's name. Zenger documents the cost of having the imprecation read by the powerful: it becomes a license. The protest-reading is therefore conditional — it functions as protest only when prayed by the slandered, not when wielded by the powerful. The honest reading must include this conditionality.

**Where this leaves us**: Psalm 5:10's imprecation cannot be allegorized away. It is real prayer for the divine destruction of the wicked. Christian readers must pray it honestly — recognizing that to pray it is to hand the matter to God rather than enact it, that to pray it well requires self-examination about whether one is the slandered or the slanderer, and that the church's history of weaponizing imprecatory psalms against its own enemies has been a catastrophe that the protest-reading is meant to correct. The verse is the cry of the slandered seeking justice; it is not the cudgel of the powerful seeking dominion.

### Closing Question

If Psalm 5 admits the speaker into the temple by the abundance of Yahweh's *chesed* (v. 7) — and if the same Yahweh hates evildoers (v. 5) — then on which side of that line does the speaker stand, and on which side does the reader stand, and how do we know we have not mistaken our position?

## The Thread

- **Romans 3:9-18** — Paul's catena of OT citations on universal sinfulness. Psalm 5:9's "their throat is an open grave" is quoted directly in Rom 3:13. The Pauline universalization of the psalm's accusation against the wicked into an anthropological diagnosis of all humanity is one of the most consequential theological moves in the New Testament.
- **Psalm 3** and **Psalm 4** — the morning and evening psalms that bracket Pss 5's morning. The cluster Pss 3-5 establishes the day-and-night-and-day rhythm of Davidic prayer at the opening of Book I.
- **Matthew 5:43-48** — Jesus' teaching to love enemies and pray for those who persecute. The Sermon on the Mount stands in real tension with Ps 5:10's imprecation, and the Christian tradition has worked out that tension by distinguishing prayer (handing the case to God) from action (refusing to enact vengeance). Both texts must be held together; neither erases the other.
- **Habakkuk 2:1** — the prophet who climbs his watchtower to *atzappeh* (watch) for the divine response. Ps 5:3's morning watchman shares the same posture and the same verb. Watching for God in the morning, before the answer arrives, is the paradigmatic stance of biblical faith.
