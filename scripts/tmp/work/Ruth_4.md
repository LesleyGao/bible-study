# Ruth 4: At the Gate, Where Names Are Made

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens at the **city gate** (*sha'ar*, שַׁעַר), and the location names the chapter's social meaning before any action takes place. In Iron Age Israelite cities, the gate was the legal and political center of civic life. Excavations at Tel Dan, Megiddo, Beersheba, Tel Lachish, and Tel Gezer have uncovered substantial four- and six-chambered gates from the 10th-8th centuries BCE, with stone benches built into the gate chambers — the literal seats of the elders. Avraham Biran's excavation of the Tel Dan gate complex (1980s-90s) is the clearest archaeological reconstruction of the institution: a paved plaza inside the gate, benches along the chamber walls, a raised platform that may have been a judicial seat. The gate was where contracts were witnessed (Genesis 23:10-18, Abraham buying the cave at Machpelah), where civic disputes were settled (Deuteronomy 21:19, 22:15, 25:7), and where the city's elders adjudicated levirate refusal (Deuteronomy 25:7-9). The Mesopotamian parallels are extensive — the Code of Hammurabi presupposes a similar gate-court institution, and the Mari archives document gate proceedings in detail. When Boaz "goes up to the gate and sits down" at 4:1, every element of his action — the location, the posture, the convening of ten elders — is the formal staging of legal proceedings.

The **ten elders** (*ziqney ha'ir*, זִקְנֵי הָעִיר) Boaz seats at 4:2 form a quorum. The number ten as the minimum for a legal/cultic gathering is well-attested in later Jewish tradition (the *minyan* of ten adult males required for public prayer, the ten-witness threshold in various rabbinic procedures), but its origin in Israelite civic practice is harder to date. Frederic Bush in his WBC commentary notes that ten elders for a transaction of this scope is precisely the size we would expect for a small-town legal proceeding in the Iron Age. The elders are not judges in our sense; they are witnesses whose presence makes the transaction legally and socially binding.

The **legal procedure** Boaz orchestrates is precise but also strategically managed. He approaches the unnamed nearer redeemer (the *go'el qarov* — Hebrew at 3:12, here just "*ha-go'el*") with a sequenced offer. First: there is a parcel of land that Naomi is selling. Will you redeem it? Yes. Then: the day you buy the land you also acquire Ruth the Moabite (4:5). The legal hinge is the second clause — the land comes with the levirate obligation to "perpetuate the name of the dead." The nearer redeemer can buy the land, but doing so commits him to fathering an heir for Mahlon whose existence would dilute his own line and whose claim on the land would supersede his. The Pentateuchal codes do not quite specify this fusion of *go'el* (Lev. 25:25-28) and *yibbum* (Deut. 25:5-10), and the legal innovation here has been the subject of substantial scholarly debate. Edward Campbell (Anchor Bible), Frederic Bush (WBC), Robert Hubbard (NICOT), Jack Sasson, and Adele Berlin have all written on the legal mechanics. The most plausible reading: Boaz is presenting the land transaction and the levirate obligation as a single integrated package, possibly extending the legal categories beyond their strict letter, and the nearer redeemer recognizes the package and declines because it would damage his own patrimonial inheritance (*nahalah*, נַחֲלָה).

The **sandal exchange** (4:7-8) is a ritual marker the narrator has to gloss for his own audience — "this was the custom in former times in Israel" — which is one of the textual signals that the book's composition postdates the practice. The sandal-pulling at the gate may be related to but is distinct from the *halizah* ritual of Deuteronomy 25:7-10, in which a brother-in-law who refuses to perform levirate marriage has his sandal removed and is spat upon by the widow as a public shaming. Here in Ruth 4, the sandal exchange is not a shaming — it is a contract attestation. The relationship between the two rituals is debated. Some scholars (Sasson, Hubbard) read the Ruth 4 ritual as the older, more general ceremony from which the Deuteronomy 25 *halizah* is a specialized derivative; others (Campbell, Bush) read it as a separate practice. In either case, the narrator's own gloss confirms that by the time of writing, the practice was no longer current and required explanation.

The transaction structures itself around the **Israelite institution of *nahalah*** — the patrimonial land inheritance that ideally remained within the clan in perpetuity. Leviticus 25 lays out the theological logic: the land belongs to YHWH; Israelites are tenants on it; alienated land must be redeemed back to the original family; the Jubilee (every 50 years) returns all alienated land to its ancestral holders. The *go'el* mechanism is the family-level enforcement. Naomi's situation triggers all of these provisions: Elimelech's family has effectively lost the land (whether by sale during the famine or by abandonment is left ambiguous), Naomi as a widow cannot reclaim it on her own, and the male relatives must act. The chapter's drama is the question of which male relative will act, on what terms, and with what consequences.

The **blessing of the elders** (4:11-12) is dense with covenantal memory. The blessing names Rachel and Leah, "who together built the house of Israel" — the matriarchs whose competing fertility produced the twelve tribes. It then names Tamar, who bore Perez to Judah by the deception of Genesis 38. The elders are explicitly placing Ruth in the matriarchal line of foreign and morally complicated women whose initiative shaped the people of Israel. The blessing's reference to Tamar is striking because it surfaces the embarrassment of Genesis 38 (Tamar disguised herself as a prostitute to extract from Judah the levirate obligation he had withheld) without flinching. The elders are saying, in effect: this is the kind of marriage from which the great houses come.

The **birth and naming scene** (4:13-17) is the chapter's emotional climax. Naomi takes the newborn Obed and lays him on her lap (*chayq*, חֵיק, "bosom" or "lap") — the gesture of formal adoption or maternal claiming (cf. Genesis 30:3 of Bilhah's children, Genesis 50:23 of Joseph's grandchildren on his lap). The neighborhood women rather than Ruth name the child, and they name him "for Naomi" — "a son has been born to Naomi" (4:17). The legal fiction of the levirate (the child belongs to Mahlon, the dead husband) is here socially expressed as the child belonging to Naomi, the widow whose emptiness has been reversed. Ruth, the biological mother, is not named in this scene — a narrative choice commentators have read variously as the appropriate completion of the levirate institution's logic (the legal child of the dead husband, the social child of the surviving matriarch) or as a partial effacement that the rest of the book's attention to Ruth makes the reader feel. The name **Obed** (עוֹבֵד) means "servant" or "worshipper" — a theophoric name with the divine element implied, the short form of names like Obadiah ("servant of Yah"). The women's blessing at 4:14-15 calls the child a *go'el* — extending the redeemer term to the infant as the one who will redeem Naomi's line.

The **closing genealogy** (4:18-22) traces ten generations from Perez to David. The genealogy is structurally inverted: it begins with Perez (genealogically the start of Judah's main line, after the Tamar episode of Genesis 38) and ends with David, the climax. Boaz appears in the seventh generation and Ruth's son Obed in the eighth — placing Ruth herself outside the formal patrilineal genealogy even as the entire narrative depends on her. The genealogy uses the formula *toledot* (תּוֹלְדוֹת, "generations of"), the same formula that structures Genesis (Genesis 5:1, 6:9, 10:1, 11:10, 25:12, 25:19, 36:1, 37:2). Most modern commentators (Bush, Campbell, Sasson, Hubbard) treat the genealogy as a later editorial addition that ties the Ruth narrative explicitly into the Davidic line, though others (LaCocque, Berlin) defend its originality. The ten-generation structure parallels the genealogies of Genesis 5 and 11; the symmetry suggests deliberate compositional design. David at the tenth generation occupies the structural climax that Noah occupies in Genesis 5. The genealogy makes a theological claim by its placement: the foreign widow's child completes the line that produces the king.

A few Hebrew terms transform the chapter when you can hear them. ***Go'el*** (גֹּאֵל), already encountered in chapters 2 and 3, here reaches its institutional center. The chapter uses the term and its verbal form (*ga'al*, גָּאַל) over twenty times. The intensity of repetition is the narrator's way of making the institution itself the chapter's subject. ***Nahalah*** (נַחֲלָה), "inheritance," "patrimonial holding," is the theological term that gives the *go'el* institution its weight. The land is not just property; it is the family's portion in YHWH's land. ***Peloni almoni*** (פְּלֹנִי אַלְמֹנִי), the phrase the narrator uses at 4:1 to address the nearer redeemer — translated "friend" or "such-and-such" — is a Hebrew idiom for a deliberately unnamed party (cf. 1 Samuel 21:3, 2 Kings 6:8). The narrator pointedly refuses to name the man who refuses redemption; his anonymity is the narrator's judgment on him. ***Toledot*** (תּוֹלְדוֹת), "generations" or "descendants," the formula that structures Genesis, returns at 4:18 to mark the formal genealogical conclusion. The book ends with a Genesis-style formula. ***Bayit*** (בַּיִת), "house" — the elders pray that YHWH will make Ruth like Rachel and Leah who "built the *bayit* of Israel" (4:11). *Bayit* in Hebrew can mean both physical house and family/dynasty (cf. 2 Samuel 7's "house of David"). The elders' blessing is invoking the dynastic sense.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Ruth 4 is the chapter where the book's two major plot questions resolve, and the literary craft of the chapter is to braid them together so that the legal, the personal, and the theological resolve simultaneously. The chapter is structured in five movements:

- **Gate scene (4:1-12):** the legal proceeding, the nearer redeemer's refusal, Boaz's acquisition, the elders' blessing.
- **Marriage and birth (4:13):** condensed in a single verse — "Boaz took Ruth, and she became his wife. And he went in to her, and the LORD gave her conception, and she bore a son."
- **Women's blessing (4:14-15):** the only direct address to Naomi in the chapter, the only place where Ruth's *hesed* is named explicitly.
- **Naming scene (4:16-17):** Naomi takes the child, the women name him, the genealogical claim is announced.
- **Closing genealogy (4:18-22):** the ten-generation toledot from Perez to David.

The chapter contains the **only** verse in the entire book where the narrator says YHWH did something: "the LORD gave her conception" (4:13). Throughout chapters 1-3, YHWH has been invoked, blessed, blamed, and prayed to, but never described as the agent of any action. At the climactic moment of birth, the narrator finally names YHWH as agent — and then the divine action immediately fades back into the human drama of the women's blessing and the naming. The placement is theologically deliberate: the one explicit divine action is the conception that brings new life into the line. Every other resolution — the legal proceeding, the marriage itself, the naming, the genealogy — is human action that the book presents as the medium of providence rather than the action of providence itself. This is the book's distinctive theological method, and 4:13 is its most condensed expression.

The chapter is rich with **echoes and reversals** of chapter 1. Naomi went out "full" and came back "empty" (1:21); now she is filled by a grandchild whose conception is divinely given. Naomi was renamed Mara/Bitter (1:20); the women now reverse the renaming by calling the child "born to Naomi" (4:17). Ruth was the foreigner (*nokhriyah*, 2:10); now she is the woman whose offspring will produce the king of Israel. The famine of 1:1 has been answered by the barley harvest of 1:22, the gleaning of chapter 2, the threshing-floor abundance of chapter 3, and now the birth of an heir. The book's plot architecture is a slow, four-chapter movement from emptiness to fullness, traced through the agricultural calendar from famine to harvest to threshing to birth.

The narrator's craft in the **gate scene** is particularly admired. Boaz's framing of the offer is strategically managed: he presents the land question first, gets the nearer redeemer's "I will redeem" commitment, and only then introduces the levirate complication. The narrator does not editorialize — we are told what Boaz says, not what he intends — but the structure of the dialogue makes clear that Boaz has anticipated the nearer redeemer's reasoning and arranged the offer to produce the desired refusal. The reading is supported by Robert Alter, Jack Sasson, and Frederic Bush. The unnamed redeemer's calculation — "I cannot redeem it for myself, lest I impair my own inheritance" — is also revealing: he is willing to take on the land but not the full kinship obligation, because the levirate child would inherit Mahlon's portion and thereby reduce the share his own children would inherit. The legal procedure exposes a moral hierarchy: the nearer redeemer is willing to discharge the lighter obligation (land) but not the heavier one (lineage). Boaz takes the heavier one. The narrator's silence on motive — Boaz's affection for Ruth, his calculation, his sense of duty — leaves all three on the table.

The **women's blessing** at 4:14-15 is the chapter's quiet center. The neighborhood women, who have been a chorus throughout (1:19, 4:14, 4:17), here speak the chapter's most theologically loaded line: Ruth, the daughter-in-law, is "more to you than seven sons." Seven sons in Hebrew Bible numerology is the maximum imaginable male blessing (cf. 1 Samuel 2:5, Job 1:2 and 42:13). To say a Moabite daughter-in-law is worth more than the patrilineal maximum is a startling theological statement, and the narrator places it in the mouth of the women rather than in any male speaker. Brueggemann, LaCocque, and Alter all read 4:15 as the chapter's quiet theological climax — the place where the book names what its own four-chapter argument has been doing.

The **closing genealogy** has been read literarily in two main ways. The traditional reading takes it as the chapter's climax — the entire narrative has been driving toward David, and the genealogy makes the Davidic claim explicit. The alternative reading (LaCocque, Brueggemann) treats it as a partial undoing of what the rest of the book has accomplished: the narrative has insisted on Ruth's agency and Naomi's centrality; the genealogy reinscribes a patrilineal frame that names neither. Both readings can be held simultaneously. The book's final form contains both the women's blessing that calls Ruth worth more than seven sons and the patrilineal genealogy that does not name her — and the tension between them is the book's parting word.

The textual evidence for chapter 4 is generally stable. The DSS Ruth fragments (4QRuth-a, 4QRuth-b) preserve some of the chapter, including parts of the genealogy, with no significant variants. The LXX is close to the Masoretic text, with minor smoothing in the legal procedural language. The Targum on Ruth substantially expands the gate scene with rabbinic-style legal argumentation and adds explicit reference to the *halizah* connection. The Targum also substantially expands the women's blessing of Naomi.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, Ruth 4 is the chapter that completes the book's slow movement from famine to fullness and that ties the entire narrative to David and, in the New Testament, to Jesus. The genealogy makes the canonical claim: the Davidic line that the rest of the Bible will trace runs through a Moabite woman. The book's quiet polemic — that the boundaries of Israel are more capacious than the surrounding canon sometimes allows — is delivered by genealogical fact rather than by argument.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** *Ruth Rabbah* 7-8 expands the chapter substantially. The midrash reads Boaz as a model legal innovator, working within the law's spirit to extend its reach; the unnamed redeemer (whom the midrash names "Tov" or "Peloni") is treated as the failed Israelite who could not see what the law required. The midrash also develops the women's blessing extensively, treating "more than seven sons" as a halakhic category that establishes the priority of righteous love over biological progeny. The Talmudic tradition (Bava Batra 91a-b, Yevamot 76b-77a) takes the chapter as the textual basis for harmonizing the Deut. 23:3 prohibition on Moabites with David's descent: the prohibition applies only to males, *Ammoni v'lo Ammonit, Moavi v'lo Moavit*. The Talmud also treats the genealogy as central to messianic theology — the line of David must run through this particular sequence, and the Moabite link is therefore providential. Rashi (11th c., Troyes) follows the rabbinic reading closely, with attention to the legal precision of Boaz's procedure. Ibn Ezra (12th c.) is more textually focused. Maimonides treats the chapter primarily for its legal content. The Zohar develops the threshing-floor and gate scenes mystically, reading the levirate marriage as the unification of divine attributes that makes the Davidic line possible. Avivah Zornberg in modern Jewish scholarship attends to the chapter as the slow recovery of Naomi's capacity to receive blessing and notes that the resolution is delivered through the initiative of Naomi and Ruth and the chorus of Bethlehem women, but is socially encoded as a male achievement at the gate and in the closing genealogy. Yair Zakovitch's commentary develops the intertextual reading of Genesis 38 (Tamar) as the chapter's central interpretive frame.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic Christianity read the chapter typologically and genealogically. Origen's homilies, Jerome's letters, and Augustine's *Quaestiones in Heptateuchum* treat Boaz as a type of Christ (the redeemer who takes the bride the law could not redeem), the unnamed nearer redeemer as a type of the law that could not redeem, and the genealogy as the tracing of the line that would lead to Jesus. Ambrose in *De Officiis* extolled the chapter's vision of marriage and lineage as governed by *pietas* and *fides*. The medieval *Glossa Ordinaria* compiled the typological readings; Bernard of Clairvaux's sermons developed Boaz as the model of the ardent bridegroom. Aquinas treated the chapter mainly in legal and genealogical context. The Reformation shifted the emphasis. Luther's prefaces praised the chapter as evidence that God's promises run through ordinary marriage and ordinary law, not through religious extraordinariness. Calvin's sermons on Ruth (1554-55) gave the chapter sustained attention as a study in providence working through the orderly procedures of civil society. The post-Reformation Pietist and Puritan tradition (Matthew Henry, John Owen) developed the typological reading at length; Henry's commentary on Ruth 4 is unusually extensive. The critical period (Wellhausen, Gunkel) treated the chapter mainly for its legal and genealogical evidence, with substantial debate about the date of the genealogy. Hermann Gunkel's form-critical analysis treated the gate scene as preserving Iron Age legal procedure. The contemporary turn has been twofold. Literary (Alter, Sasson, Berlin) attends to the craft of the gate scene and the genealogy's structural symmetry, and to the way the women's blessing at 4:14-15 functions as the theological climax that the patrilineal genealogy then formally seals. Theological (Brueggemann, LaCocque, Ellen Davis) reads the chapter as the book's central argument about how providence works through ordinary kinship and law. Brueggemann and LaCocque both note the unresolved tension within Ruth 4 itself: Ruth is named as Moabite throughout the chapter (4:5, 4:10, "Ruth the Moabite"), the women's blessing makes her the theological center, but her son is named "for Naomi" and the genealogy does not name her at all — a tension the canonical form preserves rather than resolves.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Both traditions read the chapter as the climax of the book's plot and as the establishment of the Davidic genealogy. They diverge on the legal question: Jewish reading takes Boaz's legal innovation seriously and works through the halakhic harmonization; Christian reading typically treats the legal procedure as scenic background and moves quickly to the typology. They diverge on the theological weight of the women's blessing: contemporary literary scholarship across both traditions has elevated 4:14-15 to a central position that older readings, in both traditions, often passed over quickly. They converge on the genealogy's significance: both read it as a deliberate theological claim about the Davidic line and, in Christian reading, the Messianic line. The contemporary turn has been to hold the women's blessing and the genealogy together as the book's parting tension — a tension that the earlier traditions in both faiths often resolved by privileging one or the other.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author of Ruth believe God was doing in this chapter? The theological method finally surfaces. Throughout the book, YHWH has been invoked in blessings, prayers, oaths, and accusations, but never described as agent. Here at 4:13 the narrator finally says it explicitly: "the LORD gave her conception." That single act is the only direct divine action in the entire book. Everything else — the legal procedure, the marriage, the naming, the genealogy — is human action, presented as the medium of providence rather than its substance. The book's theology is that this *is* providence: the long, patient, slow working of *hesed* through ordinary law and ordinary kinship, with one decisive divine act at the moment when human action alone would not be sufficient. The conception is the theological hinge, and its placement at the climax of the chapter is the narrator's most explicit theological move.

**Tension One: How should we hold the women's blessing and the patrilineal genealogy together?** The chapter ends with two endings. The first ending (4:14-17) is the women's blessing of Naomi, the women's naming of the child, and the women's declaration that Ruth is "more than seven sons." The second ending (4:18-22) is the patrilineal genealogy that begins with Perez and ends with David and that does not name a single woman. Both are in the canonical text. Both are part of the book's final form.

The traditional Jewish reading has typically subordinated the women's ending to the genealogical ending, treating the genealogy as the chapter's climax and the women's blessing as the affective preparation for it. *Ruth Rabbah* and the Talmudic discussion focus heavily on the genealogy and the legal procedure that produces it. The chapter, in this reading, is fundamentally about the line of David, and the women's blessing is the appropriate emotional register for what is essentially a male-genealogical achievement.

The traditional Christian reading has typically followed the same pattern, with even greater emphasis on the genealogy's typological significance. The women's blessing is read devotionally; the genealogy is read theologically. Patristic, medieval, and Reformation readings all generally privilege the genealogy.

A contemporary literary-theological reading (Brueggemann, LaCocque, Alter, Berlin) reverses the priority. The women's blessing is the theological center: Ruth's *hesed* is named, Naomi's restoration is announced, the daughter-in-law is declared worth more than seven sons. The genealogy, on this reading, is a partial truth that the rest of the book corrects — because the book has spent four chapters arguing that the line of David runs through the agency of Naomi and Ruth and the women of Bethlehem, the genealogy that names only the men is the formal record, not the whole story.

A mediating position (Brueggemann, LaCocque, Ellen Davis) holds both endings as theologically necessary. The women's blessing names what the book has accomplished theologically; the genealogy names what the book has accomplished historically. Neither alone is the book's argument; the argument is the holding-together. The book is saying both that the line of David runs through ordinary kinship law (the patrilineal genealogy) and that the line of David runs through the *hesed* of women (the women's blessing). The two endings are doing different theological work that the canonical form holds together rather than resolves.

The position to steelman is the traditional reading that privileges the genealogy. It is sometimes dismissed by contemporary readers as patriarchal nostalgia, but it takes seriously the book's own canonical placement and the unmistakable signal that the genealogy is meant as climax (the formal *toledot* opening, the ten-generation structure that mirrors Genesis 5 and 11, the canonical placement just before 1 Samuel where David's story begins). The traditional reading honors the narrator's apparent intent. It also honors the larger biblical canon, which traces the messianic line through Davidic descent and which uses Ruth's genealogy as an important link.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter's two endings are doing different work, and the canonical form keeps them in tension. To privilege either at the expense of the other is to miss what the book's final form has accomplished. The chapter is asking us to hold both: that the line of David is a patrilineal achievement, and that the patrilineal achievement is made possible only by the *hesed* of women whom the genealogy does not name.

**Tension Two: What is the theological status of the legal innovation?** Boaz's fusion of *go'el* and *yibbum* extends the Pentateuchal codes in a way they do not strictly authorize. The book treats the extension as wisdom; the codes themselves do not.

The Catholic and Jewish legal traditions both develop sophisticated frameworks for this kind of legal extension. The Catholic tradition speaks of *epieikeia* (Aristotelian equity, Aquinas *ST* II-II q. 120), the principle that justice sometimes requires departure from the letter of the law in order to fulfill its spirit. The Jewish tradition speaks of *takkanah* (rabbinic ordinance) and *gezerah shavah* (analogical reasoning), formal mechanisms by which law can be extended. Both traditions read Boaz's procedure as an early model of how this kind of equitable extension can work.

The Reformation reading, especially Calvin, is more cautious about legal innovation but reads the chapter as evidence that the law itself, properly understood, is more capacious than its strict letter. Calvin's reading is somewhere between the Catholic *epieikeia* and a strictly literalist reading: the law's spirit authorizes Boaz's extension, but the extension is bounded by what the law's spirit can clearly support.

The strictly literalist reading would treat Boaz's procedure as an extension that the codes do not authorize and therefore as a problem the book must somehow address. This reading is rare in the major commentary tradition but is taken seriously by some critical scholars (Sasson, in his more cautious moments) who note that the chapter is doing legal work the Pentateuch does not quite sanction.

The contemporary critical reading (LaCocque, Berlin, Brueggemann) generally treats the legal innovation as part of the book's polemical project. If the book is composed in a post-exilic setting against the backdrop of Ezra-Nehemiah's strict ethnic boundary policy, then Boaz's willingness to extend the law in a direction that incorporates a Moabite woman is a deliberate counter-statement: the law can be applied in ways that include rather than exclude.

The position to steelman is the strict literalist reading. It is rare in the commentary tradition but it is honest about what is happening in the text. The Pentateuchal codes do not quite authorize the fusion of *go'el* and *yibbum* that Boaz performs. Either the book is preserving a legal practice that the codes do not capture in full, or the book is doing something the codes do not authorize. Both possibilities have to be taken seriously, and the strict literalist reading at least raises the question honestly. The contemporary critical reading then has to defend why the extension is faithful rather than unfaithful — and the defense is plausible, but it has to be made.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter is doing legal innovation in service of *hesed*, and the question of whether that is faithful or unfaithful to the law's intent depends on whether one reads the law as a fixed letter or as a spirit that can be extended. The book itself clearly takes the latter view, and the canonical placement of the book between Judges and 1 Samuel is one signal that the canon does too.

The unresolved question this chapter leaves us with: When the canonical form of a story preserves both an ending that names the *hesed* of women and an ending that traces only the names of men, what does it ask of a reader who cannot privilege one without losing the other?

## The Thread

**Genesis 38.** Tamar's deception of Judah produces Perez, the ancestor named at the start of Ruth 4's closing genealogy. The elders' blessing at 4:12 explicitly invokes the Tamar story: "may your house be like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah." The Davidic line is acknowledged at the city gate to run through the morally complicated initiative of foreign or marginal women. When you reread Genesis 38, remember that Perez is the ancestor whose line the elders are blessing.

**Deuteronomy 25:5-10.** The levirate marriage law and the *halizah* sandal-removal ceremony stand behind the Ruth 4 procedure, even though the ritual at the gate is a different ceremony (a contract attestation, not a public shaming). The narrator's gloss at 4:7 ("this was the custom in former times in Israel") signals that the practice was old. When you read Deuteronomy 25, notice how the law assumes a brother as the levirate party; Ruth 4 extends the obligation to a more distant kinsman, which is the legal innovation the book is performing.

**1 Samuel 16:1-13.** Samuel anoints David at Bethlehem, Jesse's youngest son. The Davidic kingship that 1 Samuel will inaugurate is set up by the genealogy at the end of Ruth 4. When you read the anointing scene at Bethlehem, remember that David's great-grandmother is the Moabite woman whose name the elders did not include in the city-gate witness list but whose *hesed* the book has spent four chapters making the medium of providence.

**Matthew 1:5-6.** "Salmon the father of Boaz by Rahab, Boaz the father of Obed by Ruth, Obed the father of Jesse, and Jesse the father of David the king." Matthew's genealogy of Jesus traces the same ten-generation line that Ruth 4:18-22 traces, and explicitly names Ruth (alongside Tamar, Rahab, and Bathsheba). Matthew is reading Ruth back into the genealogy that Ruth 4 named patrilineally, restoring the women whose *hesed* made the line possible. When you read Matthew 1, the women's names are doing the theological work the book of Ruth has been doing all along.
