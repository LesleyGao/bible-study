# John 21: The Sea of Tiberias, Peter Restored, the Beloved Disciple's Witness

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

John 21 takes place at the Sea of Tiberias (*hē thalassa tēs Tiberiados*, ἡ θάλασσα τῆς Τιβεριάδος) — the same body of water variously called the Sea of Galilee (Mark, Matthew), Lake Gennesaret (Luke), and the Sea of Chinneroth (Old Testament). The name "Tiberias" is the Roman name, derived from the city Herod Antipas built on the western shore in 18–20 CE in honor of the emperor Tiberius. By the late first century, when John was being written or finalized, the Roman name had become the standard one — a small index of how late this Gospel sits in the chronology.

The Galilean fishing economy is one of the best-documented features of first-century Palestinian life. Mendel Nun's lifetime of underwater archaeology around the lake — *The Sea of Galilee and Its Fishermen in the New Testament* (1989) — reconstructed harbor installations at Magdala, Tabgha, Capernaum, Bethsaida, and Kursi, and identified the distinctive ancient fishing nets: the *amphiblēstron* (cast net), the *sagēnē* (drag net or seine), and the *diktyon* (general term for net, used at 21:6, 21:8, 21:11). The 1986 discovery of the so-called "Galilee Boat" (the Kinneret Boat) at Migdal — a 27-foot fishing vessel of cedar planks and oak frames, dated by carbon-14 and pottery to roughly 100 BCE–70 CE — gave us the only physical example of a first-century fishing boat from the lake. The boat could hold a crew of about five with room for a substantial catch; the Johannine seven (21:2) is at the upper end but plausible for a working night.

Fishing at night with torches and lamps to attract fish is well-attested; pre-dawn returns with the catch unloaded at the harbor are the working rhythm of the lake. The "charcoal fire" (*anthrakia*, ἀνθρακιά) on the shore at 21:9 is a Johannine detail with a deliberate verbal echo: it is the same word used at 18:18 of the courtyard fire where Peter denied Jesus three times. Two charcoal fires bracket Peter's restoration arc.

The number 153 fish (21:11) has occupied interpreters since Jerome. The net not torn (21:11, *ouk eschisthē*, an aorist passive of *schizō* — "was not split") is striking against a lake-fishing background, where heavy catches routinely tore nets.

Greek terms: *paidia* (παιδία, "children," 21:5 — affectionate diminutive of address); *epenedutēs* (ἐπενδύτης, "outer garment" or "fisherman's smock," 21:7 — a hapax in the New Testament); *anthrakia* (ἀνθρακιά, "charcoal fire"); *agapaō* vs. *phileō* (ἀγαπάω vs. φιλέω, the two verbs alternated in 21:15–17); *boske ta arnia / poimaine ta probata / boske ta probata* (βόσκε τὰ ἀρνία / ποίμαινε τὰ πρόβατα / βόσκε τὰ πρόβατα, "feed my lambs / tend my sheep / feed my sheep").

## Lens 2 — The Literature

John 21 raises the most acute source-critical question in the Fourth Gospel: did this chapter belong to the original Gospel, or was it added later? The question matters because 20:30–31 reads as a clear ending — "Jesus did many other signs... but these are written so that you may believe" — and 21:25 reads as a second, alternative ending — "Jesus did many other things... that the world itself could not contain the books." Two endings in a single document is unusual.

Bultmann's *The Gospel of John* and Brown's Anchor Bible both treat the chapter as a later epilogue, possibly from the same author at a later date or from a member of the Johannine community shortly after the death of the Beloved Disciple. The reasons for treating it as a later addition: the apparent finality of 20:30–31; the slightly different vocabulary and style (more frequent definite articles, certain non-Johannine constructions); the third-person plural "we know that his testimony is true" at 21:24, which sounds like a community voice attesting the work of an individual; the rumor about the Beloved Disciple's death (21:23), which seems to address a specific pastoral problem caused by his actual death. Schnackenburg, Beasley-Murray, and Köstenberger broadly agree on later-epilogue status.

Bauckham's *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* and *The Testimony of the Beloved Disciple* defend a different position: the chapter is by the same author as the rest of the Gospel and is integral to its design. Bauckham's argument turns on careful literary analysis showing that John 21 is structurally bound to the rest of the Gospel by deliberate verbal links — the *anthrakia* of 21:9 echoing 18:18; the threefold restoration of Peter mirroring the threefold denial; the catch of fish recalling Luke 5 (and possibly an unrecorded Johannine tradition); the Beloved Disciple/Peter pairing of John 20 carried through. Bauckham reads 21:24 not as evidence of community editorship but as the Beloved Disciple's own self-authentication, with the "we" being a literary device of communal endorsement.

Whichever way one comes down, the chapter's *function* in the canonical Gospel is the same: it ties off open questions about Peter, the Beloved Disciple, and the future of the community.

**The catch of 153 fish** (21:11) is one of the most-discussed numerical puzzles in the New Testament. Three interpretive traditions have weight. First, Jerome (*Commentary on Ezekiel* 47.6–12, late fourth century) reports that ancient zoologists identified 153 species of fish; the number signals the universal scope of the gospel mission — every kind of fish is gathered. Whether Jerome's zoological claim is accurate is disputed, but the universal-scope reading has held. Second, Augustine (*Tractates* 122.8) develops the triangular-number argument: 153 is the sum of integers from 1 to 17; 17 is 10 (commandments) plus 7 (gifts of the Spirit); the number is therefore the sum of the law and the Spirit, the entire economy of grace. Cyril of Alexandria works similar territory. Third, modern critical scholarship (Brown, Carson, Köstenberger, Keener) treats 153 as likely an actual count preserved in eyewitness tradition, with later allegorical readings layered on. The net not torn (21:11) has been read since Augustine as the unity of the church under stress.

**The threefold "do you love me?"** (21:15–17) is structurally bound to the threefold denial of 18:17, 18:25, 18:27. Two charcoal fires, three denials, three confessions. The pastoral arc is unmistakable.

**The *agapaō* / *phileō* alternation** has been one of the most-debated features of the chapter. The classic Trench-Westcott reading (Richard Trench, *Synonyms of the New Testament*, 1854; B.F. Westcott's commentary) drew a sharp distinction: *agapaō* is the higher, divine love; *phileō* is the friendly, affectionate love. On this reading, Jesus asks Peter twice for *agapaō* love and gets only *phileō* love in return; on the third question, Jesus drops to *phileō* — meeting Peter where he is — and Peter is "grieved" (21:17) precisely because Jesus has had to come down to his level. This reading has had enormous homiletical afterlife.

Modern critical scholarship (Brown, Carson, Köstenberger, Keener, Beasley-Murray, Moloney, Marianne Meye Thompson) is broadly skeptical. Their objections: in Johannine usage *agapaō* and *phileō* are used interchangeably in many passages (compare 11:3, 11:5, 11:36; compare 13:23 with 20:2 — the Beloved Disciple is *agapētos* in one place and *philēos* in another); the lambs/sheep alternation (*arnia*/*probata*) and the feed/tend alternation (*boske*/*poimaine*) suggest the author is consciously varying vocabulary for stylistic reasons, not theological ones; the Septuagint regularly translates the same Hebrew verb with both Greek verbs without distinction. Carson's PNTC commentary calls the Trench distinction "a homiletical curiosity rather than a sustainable exegetical position." Robert Gundry has argued for a subtler version of the distinction; he is a minority voice.

**The pastoral commission** *Boske ta arnia / Poimaine ta probata / Boske ta probata* (21:15, 16, 17) places Peter in the role of under-shepherd, with Jesus as the Good Shepherd of John 10. The verbal echo of 10:11–18 is unmistakable. The Catholic Petrine-succession reading takes 21:15–17 alongside Matthew 16:18–19 as the foundation for papal primacy. The Reformed counter-reading places Peter as one shepherd among many, with the distinctive feature being the personal restoration after denial rather than the conferral of an office not given to others.

**The prophecy of Peter's death** (21:18–19) — "you will stretch out your hands, and another will gird you and carry you where you do not want to go" — is read as a martyrdom prophecy. The "stretch out your hands" language has been read since Tertullian (*Scorpiace* 15) as a reference to crucifixion. Eusebius (*Hist. Eccl.* 2.25.5, 3.1.2) preserves the tradition of Peter's crucifixion in Rome under Nero (mid-60s CE); the *Acts of Peter* (second century) adds the upside-down detail. Bauckham, Bockmuehl (*Simon Peter in Scripture and Memory*), and Brown all treat the basic martyrdom tradition as historically credible.

**The Beloved Disciple's future** (21:20–23) addresses what was clearly a pastoral problem: a rumor in the Johannine community that the Beloved Disciple would not die before Christ's return. The narrative carefully clarifies what Jesus did and did not say. Bauckham reads this as confirmation that the Beloved Disciple's death precipitated the final editing of the Gospel — the community had to address why the rumor was wrong.

**The closing testimony** (21:24–25) — "this is the disciple who is bearing witness about these things, and who has written these things, and we know that his testimony is true" — is the explicit identification of the Beloved Disciple as the source-witness of the Gospel. The plural "we know" is the central piece of evidence for community editorship.

Textual variants in John 21 are minor; the chapter is well-attested in *P*66 (which preserves much of John) and in the great fourth-century uncials.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

John 21 closes a Gospel that had previously seemed closed at 20:30–31, and in doing so it pulls in strands from across the Old Testament shepherd-king tradition (Ezekiel 34, Psalm 23, Jeremiah 23) and the Galilean call narratives (Luke 5; cf. Mark 1:16–20; Matthew 4:18–22).

Jewish interpretive trajectories: Boyarin's *Jewish Gospels* and Reinhartz's *Cast Out of the Covenant* are less directly engaged with chapter 21 than with the Passion chapters; the chapter is among the least anti-Jewish in the Gospel. The Galilean setting and the fishing context place the post-resurrection community firmly within the Jewish-Galilean world.

Christian interpretive trajectories: Origen's *Commentary on John* engaged with the 153 fish allegorically. Augustine's *Tractates* 122–124 are the major patristic treatment — Augustine reads the lake-fishing as the church's mission in the present age and contrasts it with the Lukan miraculous catch of 5 in which the nets *did* tear (Augustine: in the present age, mixed church; at the eschaton, perfect church). Chrysostom's Homilies 87–88 develop Peter's restoration as a model for pastoral care of fallen disciples. Cyril of Alexandria works the chapter Christologically. Aquinas's *Lectura* on John 21 is one of the great medieval treatments — careful, reverent, and theologically generous.

Calvin's *Commentary on John* on the Peter-restoration passage is, characteristically, more interested in the moral psychology of the scene than in any putative office-conferral. Calvin reads the *agapaō*/*phileō* alternation as stylistic variation. Luther's reception is similar.

Catholic Petrine theology has read 21:15–17 as a foundational text. *Lumen Gentium* 22, *Pastor Aeternus* (Vatican I), and the post-Vatican II ecumenical dialogues — including the 1973 Lutheran-Catholic statement on papal primacy and the Anglican-Roman Catholic International Commission's *The Gift of Authority* (1999) — all engage 21:15–17. Reformed and Eastern Orthodox traditions read the passage differently, with Peter's restoration as primarily personal and pastoral, his shepherding role as paradigmatic for all bishops without conferring monarchical primacy.

Bauckham's *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* and *The Testimony of the Beloved Disciple* make John 21 foundational for the eyewitness case for the Gospel. Bockmuehl's *Simon Peter in Scripture and Memory* traces the reception of the chapter in the Petrine tradition.

Twentieth-century theology: Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* IV/3 §72 ("The Holy Spirit and the Sending of the Christian Community") engages the missional commission of the chapter. Bonhoeffer's *Discipleship* uses the "follow me" of 21:19, 21:22 as its closing call. Hans Küng's *The Church* and Joseph Ratzinger's *Called to Communion* both engage the Petrine commission, from very different ecclesiological starting points.

N.T. Wright, in *John for Everyone* and across *Surprised by Hope* and *Simply Christian*, reads John 21 as the Gospel's deliberate hinge from resurrection to mission. The dawn breakfast on the shore — bread and fish given by the risen Lord beside a charcoal fire that deliberately recalls the fire of Peter's denial — is for Wright a new-creation meal, the risen Jesus quietly resuming table fellowship and, in doing so, healing what was broken. The threefold "do you love me? — feed my sheep" is the re-commissioning of Peter for the work of the kingdom: the denier is not merely forgiven but put back to work as under-shepherd of the people Jesus is gathering. The great catch of fish (153, the net unbroken) Wright takes as a sign of the church's mission to all the nations — every kind of fish, the whole world drawn in — and "follow me," running straight through the prophecy of Peter's martyrdom, makes plain that this mission is cruciform: the way of the shepherd is the way of the cross. And the Beloved Disciple's witness (21:24), for Wright as for Bauckham, anchors the whole Gospel in eyewitness testimony rather than in detached theological reflection — the resurrection is something someone saw.

Major commentaries: Brown (Anchor Bible), Barrett (CGTC), Bultmann, Köstenberger (BECNT), Carson (PNTC), Keener, Beasley-Murray (WBC), Schnackenburg, Marianne Meye Thompson (NTL), Moloney (Sacra Pagina), Brant (Paideia), O'Day (NIB), Newbigin. For the Petrine question: Bockmuehl, *Simon Peter in Scripture and Memory*. For the eyewitness question: Bauckham. Mendel Nun's *The Sea of Galilee and Its Fishermen* is the foundational archaeological work for the setting.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Was John 21 part of the original Gospel?** Position one: later epilogue from a redactor or community (Bultmann, Brown, Schnackenburg, Beasley-Murray). The double ending, the apparent stylistic shifts, the third-person "we" of 21:24, and the pastoral problem of the Beloved Disciple's death all point to a chapter added after the death of the Beloved Disciple, perhaps by the Johannine community to authenticate the Gospel and address the rumor. Position two: integral to the original Gospel by the same author (Bauckham, Carson, Köstenberger). The verbal links (*anthrakia*, the threefold pattern, the Peter–Beloved Disciple pairing) are too tight to be later interpolation; the apparent double ending is a literary feature, not evidence of redaction; 21:24's "we know" is the Beloved Disciple's own self-attesting framing. Position three: same author at a later date (a position Brown himself sometimes leaned toward). The Beloved Disciple wrote chapters 1–20, lived for some time, then added chapter 21 to address ongoing pastoral concerns and authenticate the work; the work was finalized for circulation after his death, with 21:24–25 as the final editorial seal. Steelman position one: the third-person plural "we know that his testimony is true" at 21:24 is genuinely difficult to read as anything but a community endorsement of the work; the "we" cannot easily be the Beloved Disciple speaking of himself, and a later editorial hand fits the data better than alternatives. **Where this leaves us:** position three is the most economical, holding the literary unity argument together with the editorial-seal evidence. The chapter is part of the canonical Gospel — that is settled — but the source-critical question shapes how we read 21:24's claim about authorship and 21:23's address to a community grieving the Beloved Disciple's death.

**Tension 2: What does the threefold "feed my sheep" do for ecclesiology?** Position one: Catholic Petrine primacy. *Pastor Aeternus*, *Lumen Gentium* 22, *Ut Unum Sint*: Peter's restoration includes a unique pastoral commission that grounds the office of the Bishop of Rome. Position two: Reformed parity-of-pastors. Calvin and the Reformed tradition: Peter's restoration is personal (a fallen disciple restored to fellowship and to ministry) and the pastoral vocabulary applies analogically to all under-shepherds; no monarchical office is conferred. Position three: Orthodox first-among-equals. The Eastern tradition reads Peter's commission as paradigmatic for the episcopate as a whole and recognizes the Bishop of Rome as a primacy of honor without supreme jurisdiction. Steelman the Catholic reading: the chapter's threefold structure is unmistakable, the verbal echo of John 10's Good Shepherd is direct, and Peter's role across the New Testament (Acts, the Petrine epistles, Galatians 2) does suggest a distinctive (not necessarily monarchical) role at the foundation. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter does *something* to Peter that it does not do to the other disciples; how strong that "something" is — institutional monarchy, primacy of honor, paradigmatic-but-not-unique — is the point at which the three confessional traditions divide. The text alone does not settle the question; it has to be read alongside the rest of the New Testament, the early conciliar tradition, and the historical development of the church's ministry. The chapter does not authorize sectarian polemic; it *does* authorize sustained ecumenical conversation.

One unresolved question: who was the Beloved Disciple? John of Zebedee (the traditional view, defended by Carson, Köstenberger, Keener); John the Elder, distinct from the apostle (Papias's two-Johns tradition, defended by Bauckham); a non-apostolic Judean disciple, possibly Lazarus or a wealthy Jerusalem follower (Brown, who explored several proposals over his career). The chapter that gives us the most explicit eyewitness claim is also the chapter most resistant to settling whose eyes those were.

## The Thread

John 21 picks up the call narrative of the Galilean disciples — the original "follow me" of Mark 1 and the miraculous catch of Luke 5 — and replays it on the far side of the resurrection. It picks up the Good Shepherd of John 10 and shows the under-shepherd being commissioned. It picks up the Peter-Beloved Disciple pairing of John 13 (the Beloved Disciple leaning on Jesus's chest at supper, Peter signaling him to ask the betrayer's identity), John 18 (the Beloved Disciple at the high priest's courtyard, getting Peter inside), John 19 (the Beloved Disciple at the cross, given Mary; Peter absent), and John 20 (the Beloved Disciple outrunning Peter to the tomb) — and resolves the tension at 21:22 with Jesus's "What is that to you? You follow me." Flag forward: the entire history of the church will be a working out of those final two words. The Johannine Jesus does not give Peter the answer to the Beloved Disciple's future; he gives Peter only a vocation. The Gospel that began *en archē* (1:1) ends with *akolouthei moi* (21:22) — from cosmic origin to personal call.
