# Luke 10: The Seventy-Two, the Samaritan, and the Sister Who Sat

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Luke 10 sits inside the Travel Narrative (9:51–19:27), the long Lukan section in which Jesus is set toward Jerusalem. Geographically, the narrative is restless and imprecise. Luke does not march his Jesus along a clean itinerary the way a Roman travelogue would. The "every town and place" of 10:1 is a smear across the Galilean–Samaritan–Perean borderlands rather than a map. Hans Conzelmann argued in *The Theology of St. Luke* that this geography is theological as much as cartographic: Galilee is the place of revelation, Jerusalem the place of suffering, and the long road between them is the place of teaching. The reader who keeps reaching for a map is missing what Luke is doing with space.

The sending of seventy-two (or seventy) presupposes a populous, literate, agriculturally organized landscape. The instructions to carry no moneybag (*ballantion*), no knapsack (*pēra*), no sandals — and to greet no one on the road — recall both the prophetic urgency of 2 Kings 4:29 (Elisha sending Gehazi) and the discipline of Cynic itinerants known across the Hellenistic Mediterranean. F. Gerald Downing's work on Jesus and the Cynics has been overstated by some and dismissed by others; the better historical position, defended by Joel Green and John Nolland, is that Jesus's wandering preachers would have *looked* like Cynic philosophers to a passing Greek observer while being driven by a quite different theology. The "peace be to this house" greeting (*eirēnē tō oikō toutō*) is not Cynic; it is the Hebrew *shalom* formula transposed into Greek, and it implies a household-by-household economy of hospitality that the archaeology of Galilean villages — Capernaum's *insulae*, Bethsaida's basalt courtyards excavated by Rami Arav — confirms. A village had perhaps a dozen extended-family compounds; a "son of peace" was a householder who would absorb the visitor and his colleague for the duration.

The Jericho road of the Good Samaritan parable is one of the most archaeologically concrete settings in the Gospels. The descent from Jerusalem (roughly 2,500 feet) to Jericho (about 800 feet below sea level) over seventeen miles passes through the Wadi Qelt — a serpentine canyon whose limestone shelves and bandit caves were notorious in antiquity. Jerome, writing four centuries later, called a stretch of it the "Bloody Way" (*Maledomim*). The 1st-century Roman road, partly traceable in masonry segments along the Wadi, made the route faster but no safer. Josephus mentions banditry along this corridor more than once. The parable's "fell among robbers" is not metaphor; it is reportage. The inn (*pandocheion*) at which the Samaritan deposits the wounded man would have been a roadside caravanserai of the kind excavated near Khan al-Hathrura, the so-called "Inn of the Good Samaritan" site, midway between Jerusalem and Jericho. The two denarii left with the innkeeper represent roughly two days of an unskilled laborer's wages — enough to cover several days of board, with the open promise to repay anything more.

First-century Jewish-Samaritan relations were poisonous. The Samaritan temple on Mount Gerizim had been destroyed by John Hyrcanus in 128 BCE; Josephus (*Antiquities* 18.29-30) records a Samaritan defilement of the Jerusalem Temple by scattering bones around Passover sometime in the procuratorship of Coponius (6–9 CE), well within living memory at Luke's composition. The rabbinic tractate *Mishnah Shebiit* later debated whether Samaritan produce could be eaten. To call a Samaritan one's neighbor was, for a Jerusalem lawyer, not a tender ecumenism but a category violation.

Mary and Martha live in Bethany (named explicitly in John 11–12, implied in Luke 10:38). Bethany sits on the eastern slope of the Mount of Olives, less than two miles from Jerusalem along the same Roman road that descends to Jericho. Excavations in the village (still partially extant beneath modern al-Eizariya) reveal first-century rock-cut tombs, ritual baths, and modest courtyard houses. The detail that "Mary sat at the Lord's feet and listened to his teaching" (10:39) places her in the physical posture of a rabbinic disciple — a posture later used by Paul to describe his own training under Gamaliel (Acts 22:3, *para tous podas Gamaliēl*).

Greek terms worth flagging:
- ***plēsion*** (πλησίον), "neighbor," the lawyer's word in 10:27, 29, 36. In LXX Leviticus 19:18 it translates the Hebrew *rēaʿ*, whose semantic field in Second Temple Judaism was actively debated.
- ***splanchnizomai*** (σπλαγχνίζομαι), "had compassion," 10:33 — literally to be moved in the splanchna (the inward parts, viscera). Luke uses this verb at three pivotal moments (here, the widow of Nain, the prodigal's father).
- ***diakonia*** (διακονία), 10:40, "serving" — the word that will bear enormous theological weight in Luke-Acts (Acts 6:1-4) and in the early church's vocabulary of office.
- ***hē agathē meris*** (ἡ ἀγαθὴ μερίς), 10:42, "the good portion" — *meris* meaning a share or portion, as of land, food, or inheritance.
- ***pandocheion*** (πανδοχεῖον), 10:34, "inn" — literally "the all-receiver," a commercial roadside lodging distinct from the *katalyma* (private guest room) of Luke 2:7.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 10 is one of the densest pieces of L material (Lukan special tradition) in the Gospel. The sending of the Seventy-Two has no Markan or Q parallel as a discrete second mission; the Good Samaritan is uniquely Lukan; the Mary and Martha pericope is uniquely Lukan. Only the woes against Chorazin and Bethsaida (10:13-15) and the "I thank you, Father" prayer (10:21-22) come from Q (cf. Matthew 11:21-27). The chapter is, in other words, *substantially* Luke's own contribution to the Synoptic tradition.

C.F. Evans's 1955 essay "The Central Section of St Luke's Gospel" proposed that the Travel Narrative as a whole is structured as a midrashic walk through Deuteronomy 1–26, with chapters and pericopes echoing the Mosaic sequence. On this hypothesis Luke 10 corresponds loosely to the Deuteronomic concern with the appointment of judges and elders (Deut 1:9-18) and the great commandment of Deut 6 (Shema), which the lawyer cites in 10:27. Evans's thesis has been refined and disputed — David Moessner's *Lord of the Banquet* extends it; James Resseguie and others have shown that the correspondences are not always tight — but it remains the most ambitious literary reading of the section's architecture, and it explains why the lawyer's question and answer are framed in distinctly Deuteronomic vocabulary.

The textual variant at 10:1 and 10:17 is one of the most evenly balanced in the New Testament. "Seventy-two" appears in P75, Vaticanus, the Old Latin, and the Coptic; "seventy" appears in Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus, the Byzantine majority, and most Syriac. Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary* gives the variant a {C} rating, and the NA28 prints "seventy-two" with brackets. Beyond text criticism, the symbolic numerology matters. Seventy is the count of the nations in the Masoretic Genesis 10 and the elders appointed in Numbers 11; seventy-two is the count in the Septuagint Genesis 10 and is the traditional number of LXX translators. Either way, the symbolism is the same: the mission of the Seventy(-two) is a programmatic foreshadowing of the Gentile mission Luke will narrate in Acts. Joel Green argues in his NICNT commentary that the variant should not be over-resolved; both numbers point to the same theological gesture.

Robert Tannehill's *Narrative Unity of Luke-Acts* showed how Luke 10 functions as a hinge between the Galilean ministry and the Travel Narrative proper. The Seventy-Two go ahead "into every town and place where he himself was about to go" (10:1) — they prepare the ground. Their return, with its report of demonic submission and Jesus's startling vision of Satan falling from heaven, transitions into the lawyer's test, then into the parable of the Samaritan, then into the Bethany scene. The flow is not random. Beverly Roberts Gaventa, in her work on Luke's apocalyptic dimension, reads the Satan-falling vision (10:18) not as a private mystical disclosure but as a cosmic-political marker: the mission of the Seventy-Two is itself the unraveling of the strong man's house. N.T. Wright's *Luke for Everyone* makes a similar move, locating the verse inside Second Temple expectations of Belial's defeat.

The Good Samaritan parable is the most-cited parable of Jesus in Western culture, which is itself a hermeneutical problem. The parables-genre debate from Adolf Jülicher (1899) onward has worked out a basic discipline: parables are not allegories to be decoded element-by-element; they make a single point through extended comparison. Joachim Jeremias's *The Parables of Jesus* recovered the Sitz im Leben of Jesus from later ecclesial overlay. C.H. Dodd's *Parables of the Kingdom* read them eschatologically. Klyne Snodgrass's massive *Stories with Intent* (2008) is now the standard reference, cataloging every Jesus parable's interpretive history. Amy-Jill Levine's *Short Stories by Jesus* (2014) is the most consequential recent reading; she reopens questions Jülicher closed. More on her in Lens 3.

The Mary/Martha pericope is twelve verses but has spawned a library. Luke's care with vocabulary is striking: Mary "listened" (*ēkouen*, imperfect — durative) "to his word" (*ton logon autou*); Martha was "distracted" (*periespato*) by "much serving" (*pollēn diakonian*). The verb *perispaō* means literally "to be drawn around in different directions," and *diakonia* is the same word the early church will use for office. Joseph Fitzmyer in the Anchor Bible commentary observes that the contrast is not between activity and inactivity but between *fragmenting* activity and *focused* attention. Commentators across the spectrum (Fitzmyer, Green, Bovon) note that the pericope belongs to Luke's recurring habit of pairing a male and a female recipient of the gospel — and that the Mary/Martha scene sits deliberately next to the Good Samaritan, the two together answering the lawyer's double citation of Deuteronomy 6 (love of God: Mary listening) and Leviticus 19 (love of neighbor: the Samaritan acting).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc Luke is constructing runs from Genesis through the prophets into the present mission. The Shema citation in 10:27 (Deut 6:5 + Lev 19:18) is the lawyer's, not Jesus's; Jesus's contribution is to *frame the question* about the neighbor as a parable. The combination of love-of-God and love-of-neighbor was not unprecedented in Second Temple Judaism — the *Testament of Issachar* 5:2 and 7:6 and the *Testament of Dan* 5:3 pair them — but the narrative dramatization of *plēsion* through a Samaritan was original.

**Jewish interpretive trajectories.** Amy-Jill Levine's reading of the Good Samaritan in *Short Stories by Jesus* is the most important recent shift in Christian engagement with the parable. Levine argues that the original force of the parable is missed when readers identify with the Samaritan ("be like him"). The lawyer asks who his neighbor is, and Jesus tells a story in which the listener is positioned, by every literary cue, with the man in the ditch — a Jew, anonymous, naked, and unable to identify any of his potential rescuers by ethnicity, profession, or class. The first-century Jewish hearer expects priest-Levite-Israelite (the standard rabbinic triad). Jesus breaks the triad with a Samaritan. The parable's force is then: *your neighbor is the one you didn't think could be your neighbor — perhaps the one whose help you don't want to need*. Levine argues this reading restores the parable's offense; centuries of Christian use have softened it into a generic exhortation to kindness. Levine's reading is contested but it has reframed the conversation.

David Flusser and Géza Vermes both noted that the Samaritan's actions — pouring on oil and wine, transporting the wounded, paying for ongoing care — fulfill the rabbinic concept of *gemilut chasadim* (deeds of loving-kindness) more rigorously than the priest's or Levite's concern with ritual purity. The parable, they argued, is intra-Jewish ethical critique rather than anti-Jewish polemic. Daniel Boyarin's broader work on Jewish-Christian boundaries pushes back on any reading that treats Samaritans as the parable's "Christian" surrogate; the Samaritan in the story is religiously, not yet christologically, marked.

E.P. Sanders's work on Jesus and Judaism would frame the parable as one of Jesus's restorationist gestures — including those at the margins of Israel — rather than as a critique of Judaism per se. Pinchas Lapide's *Sermon on the Mount* and related work emphasizes the parable's continuity with Jewish ethical tradition.

**Christian interpretive trajectories.** Origen's *Homilies on Luke* (Homily 34, on 10:25-37) preserves the most influential allegorical reading: the wounded man is Adam fallen from Jerusalem (paradise) to Jericho (the world); the robbers are demonic powers; the priest is the Law; the Levite is the Prophets; the Samaritan is Christ; the inn is the Church; the two denarii are the two Testaments (or, in Augustine's variant in *Quaestiones Evangeliorum* 2.19, the two commandments or two sacraments). Ambrose, Cyril of Alexandria, and Bede inherit and elaborate this allegory; it dominates Latin and Byzantine readings until Aquinas (*Catena Aurea*) consolidates it. Jülicher's 19th-century rejection of allegorizing was *aimed at* this tradition; modern parables scholarship is largely a reaction to fifteen centuries of Origenist reading. Kenneth Bailey's *Through Peasant Eyes* recovers the Mediterranean cultural matrix — the priest's and Levite's calculations of corpse-impurity (Lev 21:1-3, Num 19:11-13), the social shame of being half-naked on a road, the honor exchange between innkeeper and patron — and reads the parable inside that village logic without falling back into allegory. Calvin's *Harmony of the Evangelists* sides with the literal reading and chastises Origen's allegory for deflecting the moral demand. Justo González's *Luke* (Belief Commentary) presses the parable's social edge: the Samaritan is the ethnic outsider who turns out to be the rescuer, and the parable indicts every reader who is comfortable and on the road and going somewhere important.

On Mary and Martha, the medieval *vita activa / vita contemplativa* schema is the dominant Latin tradition. Augustine (*Sermon 104*) reads Martha as the active life of the present age and Mary as the contemplative life of the world to come; Aquinas in *Summa Theologiae* II-II q.182 a.1 follows him in ranking contemplative life higher because Mary's portion "will not be taken away." Meister Eckhart's Sermon 86 (sometimes Sermon 9 in older numberings) inverts the tradition: Martha is the *more* mature disciple because she has integrated contemplation with action; Mary still has to learn to do what Martha already does. Eckhart's reading is theologically daring and not widely reproduced before the twentieth century.

A point now widely accepted across the mainstream commentaries (Fitzmyer, Green, Nolland, Bock, Bovon) is that Mary "sat at the Lord's feet" is the technical posture of a disciple — the same idiom Paul uses for his training under Gamaliel (Acts 22:3) — and that Jesus's defense of her "good portion" is therefore a defense of her place as a learner of the word, not a verdict that domestic service is unspiritual. Eastern Orthodox tradition reads the sisters as complementary patterns of a single Christian life rather than as opposed types — a reading congenial to Eckhart's.

**N.T. Wright**, in *Luke for Everyone* and *Jesus and the Victory of God*, threads the chapter to his larger thesis. The mission of the Seventy(-two) is, for Wright, the kingdom of God going public — Jesus enlarging the circle of those who announce that Israel's God is becoming king, with the symbolic number pointing past Israel to the nations. The Satan-falling vision (10:18) is the cosmic correlate: the announcement of the kingdom is itself the binding of the strong man, the unraveling of the powers that have held Israel and the world captive. And the Good Samaritan, in Wright's reading, is part of Jesus's running redefinition of the people of God: the lawyer wants the boundary of "neighbor" drawn tightly around the covenant people, and Jesus blows the boundary open — the kingdom Jesus brings will not run along the old ethnic lines, which is exactly the offense the Travel Narrative keeps pressing. Bovon's *Hermeneia* commentary places Mary and Martha next to the Good Samaritan as twin instructions: love of neighbor (the Samaritan) and love of God (Mary listening) bracket each other; Green argues this is the Lukan two-fold answer to the lawyer's compound citation of Deut 6 + Lev 19. The architecture is deliberate.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Who is the parable's protagonist for the hearer?** The dominant Christian reading has been: identify with the Samaritan, go and do likewise (10:37). Levine's reading: identify with the man in the ditch; receive help from those you have despised. A third reading, defended by Bailey: the parable refuses to assign a fixed identification and instead exposes the lawyer's category error — *plēsion* is not a status to be assigned but an action to be done. Steelmanned: Position 1 honors Jesus's explicit closing imperative ("you go, and do likewise"); the parable does end as ethical instruction. Position 2 honors the parable's narrative cues (the wounded man is the one with whom the audience shares vulnerability) and recovers the Samaritan-as-other's offense. Position 3 honors the Socratic shape of the dialogue — the lawyer asked a definitional question and got a relational answer. **Where this leaves us:** all three readings are present in the text and probably operative for Luke. The most honest reading admits that Jesus has done what good rabbis do: refused the original question, broken its frame, and sent the questioner home with more to chew on than he came with.

**Tension 2: Mary's "good portion" — is contemplation really better than service?** The Augustinian-Thomistic reading says yes: Mary chose what will not be taken away. The Eckhartian-Orthodox reading says the dichotomy is false: Martha's serving *is* contemplative when properly oriented. A third reading holds that the contemplative-vs-active framing is itself imported onto the text — twelve verses cannot bear a whole typology of vocations — and that what Jesus commends is simply Mary's *attention*. Steelmanned: Augustine's reading has the textual data — Jesus does say one thing is necessary and Mary chose it. Eckhart's reading saves the value of work and refuses to make the gospel anti-domestic. The third reading notes how heavily the *vita activa / vita contemplativa* schema has been loaded onto a brief domestic scene. **Where this leaves us:** the pericope is about *attention*, not about a typology of vocations. Martha's problem is not that she serves but that she is *periespato* — fragmented. Mary has not chosen a higher caste of Christian; she has chosen, in this hour, to receive what Jesus is giving. The "good portion" is the word being spoken at this table, which Martha could also have had.

**Unresolved question:** What does it mean that Jesus "saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven" (10:18) at the return of the Seventy-Two? Was this a vision granted to Jesus during the mission, an apocalyptic disclosure of an event accomplished, or a cosmological commentary on the missionaries' exorcisms as participation in a larger cosmic battle? Wright reads it as Jesus announcing the kingdom's eschatological arrival; Gaventa reads it as Lukan apocalyptic; older commentators (down to Marshall) hesitate to overdetermine it. The verse remains, as it should, slightly luminous and slightly opaque.

## The Thread

The "I saw Satan fall like lightning" vision links forward to Luke 11:14-23 (the Beelzebul controversy, the strong man bound) and backward to the wilderness temptation (Luke 4:1-13). The Seventy-Two's mission anticipates Acts 1:8 and the missionary expansion of Acts 13–28. The lawyer's Shema citation (10:27) recurs as a structural device in Luke 18:18-23 (the rich ruler, who also asks about inheriting eternal life). And Mary "at the Lord's feet" (10:39) is rhymed forward to Mary of Bethany anointing Jesus's feet (John 12:3) — a different Mary in a different Gospel, but a Lukan reader trained by 10:39 will hear the echo.
