# Titus 2: When the Grace Appeared

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The household into which Titus 2 speaks was a Greco-Roman *oikos* situated on a Roman senatorial province with a substantial Jewish diaspora. Crete had been organized as a senatorial province jointly with Cyrenaica since 27 BC, governed by a proconsul resident at Gortyn. The imperial cult had penetrated the major Cretan cities — Caesarea, Augusta, and Sebaste shrines and altars are epigraphically attested at Gortyn, Knossos, Cnossus, Polyrrhenia, and Hierapytna. Honors to Augustus, Tiberius, Claudius, Nero, and the Flavians appear on inscriptions identifying these emperors with σωτήρ *sōtēr* "savior," εὐεργέτης *euergetēs* "benefactor," and θεός *theos* "god." The *epiphaneia* of an emperor — his ritualized arrival in a provincial city — was an event of social and religious magnitude, with civic delegations, sacrifices, and panegyric rhetoric. Adolf Deissmann's *Light from the Ancient East* documented how thoroughly the imperial-cult vocabulary saturated everyday provincial life. The Christian use of σωτήρ, ἐπιφάνεια, and θεός in Titus 2:11-14 cannot be heard apart from this background; the writer is conducting a deliberate vocabulary contest.

The household structure that Titus 2:2-10 addresses was the legal and economic unit on which Roman society rested. Slaves — οἰκέται and δοῦλοι — composed a substantial portion of urban populations, with estimates running from 15 to 30 percent in the eastern Mediterranean. Roman law fixed slaves as *res*, things, even as customary practice often treated them as members of the *familia*. Manumission was common but conditional. The *paterfamilias* held legal authority over wife, children, and slaves; the *materfamilias* exercised informal but real authority within the household economy. The pedagogical structure of 2:3-5 — older women training younger women — reflects the actual pattern by which Greco-Roman households passed domestic skill across generations. Cretan inscriptions and tomb reliefs document women's economic roles in textile production, marketing, and small commerce.

The authorship and dating debate that frames every Pastoral Epistle frames this chapter as well. Critical scholars (Hans Conzelmann and Martin Dibelius in the Hermeneia commentary; Lewis Donelson; Norbert Brox; Margaret MacDonald) read the *Haustafel* of 2:1-10 as evidence of post-Pauline accommodation to Greco-Roman household norms in service of social respectability — an argument first systematized by David Balch on Colossians and 1 Peter. The vocabulary count cuts the same direction: σωφροσύνη and its cognates, εὐσέβεια, and the noun-adjective register of "sober-minded, dignified, self-controlled" reflect a moral-philosophical lexicon closer to Stoic-Cynic paraenesis than to undisputed Pauline ethics. Defenders of Pauline authorship — William Mounce, I. Howard Marshall, Philip Towner, George Knight, Luke Timothy Johnson, Donald Guthrie, Jerome Quinn — argue that Paul, like any first-century moral writer, drew on the available Hellenistic vocabulary and that the *Haustafel* is broadly continuous with the household instruction of Colossians 3 and Ephesians 5. The interpreter of Titus 2 must hold the question open: whether Paul or a Pauline tradition has fitted Christian content into Hellenistic moral form, or has done so a generation later.

The textual evidence for Titus 2 is unusually rich at one point. Papyrus 32 (P32), dated c. 200 and the oldest surviving witness to any Pastoral Epistle, contains Titus 2:3-8 — a fragment of precisely the household instruction. Codex Sinaiticus (א) and Codex Alexandrinus (A) preserve the chapter intact. Codex Vaticanus (B) lacks the Pastorals altogether — its text breaks off at Hebrews 9:14. The textual tradition for 2:13 ("our great God and Savior Jesus Christ") is firm; no significant variant divides the witnesses on the article-noun-καί-noun structure that triggers the Granville Sharp Rule.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter. ἐπιφάνεια *epiphaneia* (2:11 verbal *epephanē*, 2:13 *epiphaneian*) — "appearing/manifestation," an imperial-cult term Christianized into a dual reference to incarnation and parousia. σωτήριος *sōtērios* (2:11) — "saving" or "salvation-bringing." μέγας θεὸς καὶ σωτήρ *megas theos kai sōtēr* (2:13) — the Granville Sharp construction. λυτρώσηται *lytrōsētai* (2:14) — "redeem," with sacrificial overtones. λαὸς περιούσιος *laos periousios* (2:14) — "a people for his own possession," a Septuagintal phrase from Exodus 19:5 and Deuteronomy 14:2.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre of Titus 2 is mixed. Verses 1-10 form a *Haustafel* — the household-code paraenesis familiar from Colossians 3:18-4:1, Ephesians 5:21-6:9, and 1 Peter 2:13-3:7. Greco-Roman parallels include Aristotle's *Politics* I.12 on the management of wife, children, and slaves, and the household instructions of Hierocles the Stoic. Luke Timothy Johnson, reading Titus as a *mandata principis* commission letter, understands the *Haustafel* as instruction to Titus on what to teach in his absence rather than direct address to households. The list moves through six categories — older men, older women, young women (mediated through older women), young men, Titus himself, and slaves — covering the *oikos* in roughly the order of social hierarchy. Notable is the asymmetry of the slave instruction (2:9-10): Titus 2 addresses only slaves, not masters, while Ephesians 6 and Colossians 3 address both. The asymmetry has been read as evidence of a pseudonymous accommodation to civic respectability (Donelson) and, contrariwise, as a function of Crete's particular social composition (Mounce).

Verses 11-14 shift register sharply. Most contemporary commentators (Marshall, Towner, Mounce, Knight, Quinn, Johnson) recognize this passage as a credal or hymnic fragment embedded in the paraenesis, providing the theological grounding for the household ethics that precede it. The structure is bracketed by two parallel *epiphaneia* statements: "the grace of God has appeared" (ἐπεφάνη ἡ χάρις, past) and "waiting for the appearing" (προσδεχόμενοι τὴν ἐπιφάνειαν, future). Between them sits the ethical formation clause (2:12 — "training us to renounce ungodliness") and a concluding redemption formula (2:14) that draws on Septuagintal vocabulary: "redeem" (λυτρώσηται, echoing Psalm 130:8), "purify" (καθαρίσῃ, echoing Ezekiel 37:23), and "a people for his own possession" (λαὸν περιούσιον, quoting Exodus 19:5 and Deuteronomy 14:2 LXX). The redemption logic is dense; it presents the Christ-event as an exodus, with believers constituted as a covenantal people through purification.

Verse 15 returns to the *mandata* frame: "Declare these things; exhort and rebuke with all authority. Let no one disregard you." The rhetorical envelope — instruction to the delegated officer — closes around the embedded credal core.

Within the letter's argument, Titus 2 supplies the positive content of the "sound doctrine" diagnosed in chapter 1. Where chapter 1 named the disease (insubordination, deception, false purity), chapter 2 offers the cure: a household restored to ordered virtue, grounded not in civic respectability for its own sake but in the redemptive *epiphaneia* of grace. Chapter 3 will then extend the frame outward to civil society and to the baptismal community.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical New Testament arc, Titus 2 is the densest Christological-soteriological summary in the Pastoral Epistles, and 2:13 is one of a handful of New Testament texts that may directly call Jesus θεός. The *epiphaneia* framework links Titus 2 backward to Jesus' incarnation (matched by 2 Tim 1:10's "appearing of our Savior Christ Jesus, who abolished death") and forward to the parousia (matched by 2 Tim 4:1, 4:8 and 1 Tim 6:14), giving the Pastorals a coherent two-epiphany Christology distinctive within the New Testament corpus.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** John Chrysostom, in his homilies on Titus, reads 2:11-14 as the structural heart of the letter and treats 2:13 as a direct confession of Christ's deity. Cyril of Alexandria, in his Christological controversy with Nestorius, repeatedly cites Titus 2:13 as a New Testament proof-text for the unity of Christ's person and the legitimacy of attributing both *theos* and *sōtēr* to one subject. Jerome's commentary on Titus likewise reads 2:13 in a mono-personal sense. Augustine cites the verse in *De Trinitate* and in his anti-Arian polemic. The patristic reading is overwhelmingly that "our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" names one person, Jesus, by both titles.

Medieval reception consolidated this reading through Aquinas's commentary on the Pauline epistles, where Titus 2:13 is treated as a robust proof-text for the deity of Christ. The Glossa Ordinaria preserves the patristic readings.

Reformation reading sharpened the soteriological logic. Luther's preface to Titus calls 2:11-14 "the very heart of Christian doctrine" and reads it as a compressed gospel: grace appears, trains, redeems. Calvin's commentary on Titus treats 2:13 as a clear statement of Christ's deity and uses it against contemporary anti-Trinitarian dissenters (Servetus, the Socinians). The Reformed tradition has generally read 2:11's "salvation for all people" through the lens of either the well-meant offer (e.g., John Murray) or, in stricter Calvinist circles, of "all kinds of people" rather than each individual without exception.

The critical-grammatical history of 2:13 is its own story. Granville Sharp, an English abolitionist and amateur Greek grammarian, formulated in 1798 the rule that bears his name: when two singular, personal, non-proper nouns of the same case are joined by καί and a single article precedes the first noun only, both nouns refer to the same person. Applied to τοῦ μεγάλου θεοῦ καὶ σωτῆρος ἡμῶν Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ, the rule yields "our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ" — both titles referring to Jesus. Westcott and Hort in the late nineteenth century, and a number of liberal-Protestant grammarians of the twentieth, preferred the older reading "the great God and our Savior Jesus Christ" — taking *theos* as a reference to the Father and *sōtēr* to Jesus. The recent grammatical scholarship has decisively reaffirmed Sharp's rule. Murray Harris's *Jesus as God: The New Testament Use of Theos in Reference to Jesus* (1992) provides the most exhaustive defense, examining every relevant New Testament construction. Daniel Wallace's *Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics* (1996) treats the rule rigorously and confirms its application to 2:13. Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* records the textual stability of the verse. Bart Ehrman, in *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture*, considers whether scribal Christological pressure may have stabilized texts of this kind, but does not contest the grammatical reading on Titus 2:13. The scholarly consensus across confessional and critical lines now reads 2:13 as a direct ascription of θεός to Jesus.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** Jewish scholarly engagement with Titus 2 has focused mainly on two issues. The first is the embedded Septuagintal vocabulary of 2:14 — *laos periousios*, "a people for his own possession" — which lifts the formative phrase of Israel's covenant identity (Exod 19:5, Deut 14:2) and re-applies it to the Christian community. Modern Jewish scholarship (Daniel Boyarin in *Border Lines*, Mark Nanos's Paul-within-Judaism work, Paula Fredriksen in *When Christians Were Jews*) has read this kind of supersessionist re-application carefully — as a marker of how the Pastoral Epistles' more developed ecclesiology has begun to absorb Israel's identity into the church. Whether this reflects Paul's own thought or a later development depends, again, on the authorship question.

The second is the *epiphaneia* / *theos sōtēr* framework. Jewish monotheism traditionally resisted any application of *theos* to a human figure, and the question of how Titus 2:13's high Christology can be squared with Jewish monotheism has been a recurring point of dialogue. Larry Hurtado's *Lord Jesus Christ* and Richard Bauckham's *God Crucified* have argued that very early Christian confession identified Jesus within rather than alongside the divine identity, drawing on Second Temple Jewish patterns of divine agency. The argument complicates older claims that high Christology is necessarily late and Hellenistic.

**Where this leaves us:** The dialogue is genuinely open. Christian readers can hold 2:13 as a direct confession of Christ's deity grounded in early Christian worship; Jewish interlocutors can press the question of how that confession relates to monotheism without simply repeating the older charge of Hellenization.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The Granville Sharp Rule and the direct deity of Christ.** Titus 2:13 is, on the Sharp-rule grammatical reading, one of the strongest direct ascriptions of θεός to Jesus in the New Testament — alongside John 1:1, John 20:28, Romans 9:5, Hebrews 1:8, and 2 Peter 1:1. Catholic dogmatic theology (Catechism of the Catholic Church 449, citing Titus 2:13 directly under the heading "Jesus Is Lord") reads the verse as a confessional support for Nicene-Constantinopolitan orthodoxy. Reformed and Lutheran orthodoxy reads the same way; the *Westminster Confession* 8 and the *Formula of Concord* both presume this Christology. Eastern Orthodoxy reads 2:13 in close connection with the Theotokos title and the Cyrillean Christology of one person in two natures. Critical-grammatical scholarship (Murray Harris, Daniel Wallace, Bruce Metzger) defends the Sharp reading. Larry Hurtado's *Lord Jesus Christ* and Richard Bauckham's *God Crucified* argue that the earliest Christian worship already identified Jesus within the divine identity, making 2:13 a textual surface of an underlying devotional pattern that goes back to the first generation.

The older two-person reading deserves to be steelmanned. James Dunn, in *Christology in the Making* and elsewhere, has long argued that explicit ascription of θεός to Jesus in the New Testament is rarer and more carefully bounded than later orthodoxy suggests, and that even the Sharp construction in 2:13 might bear a looser reading in which "our great God" refers to the Father and "our Savior Jesus Christ" to the Son, with a coordinate appositive. The position concedes the grammatical pressure of Sharp but argues that grammar is not destiny — that the coordinated rhetorical pattern in 2:13 may foreground the joint work of Father and Son rather than the predication of *theos* to the Son. The position has lost ground but is not vacuous.

N.T. Wright, in *Paul for Everyone: The Pastoral Letters*, reads 2:11-14 as the engine of the chapter's ethics: God's grace has "appeared" — Wright keeps the imperial-cult resonance, the *epiphaneia* of a sovereign — first in the coming of Jesus and now to be completed in "the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ" (2:13, which he takes, with the grammar, as a confession of Jesus' divinity). Between the two appearings the church lives the in-between life: grace is not only pardon but a teacher (2:12), training a redeemed people "eager to do good" (2:14), a *laos periousios* — the covenant people of Exodus now reconstituted around the Messiah. For Wright the household instructions of 2:1-10 are not free-floating respectability but the visible shape of a community that has been bought out of "all lawlessness" and is waiting, with its life already reordered, for its King to appear.

**Where this leaves us:** the grammatical case for the Sharp reading is, by present scholarly standards, very strong. The theological consequence — that one of the latest documents in the New Testament canon directly calls Jesus θεός — is significant for any account of how high Christology develops.

**Tension 2: Universal salvation language in 2:11.** "The grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation for all people" — σωτήριος πᾶσιν ἀνθρώποις. The verse stands at the head of a long history of soteriological dispute. Catholic teaching since Vatican II's *Lumen Gentium* 16 has affirmed that those who through no fault of their own are ignorant of Christ may yet attain eternal salvation; *Dominus Iesus* (2000) reaffirmed Christ as the unique mediator while preserving the inclusivist framework. Karl Rahner's "anonymous Christians" thesis represents the most developed twentieth-century Catholic articulation. Reformed limited-atonement theology — articulated by John Owen in *The Death of Death in the Death of Christ* (1647) and codified by the Westminster divines — reads "all people" as "all kinds of people," not each individual, and confines the actual saving efficacy of the cross to the elect. Wesleyan-Arminian theology reads the same verse as supporting unlimited atonement: Christ died sufficiently and intentionally for every person, with effectual application contingent on faith. David Bentley Hart's *That All Shall Be Saved* (2019) and Robin Parry's *The Evangelical Universalist* press the verse toward genuine universal salvation, arguing that the New Testament's universal-salvation language must be allowed its full weight.

The maximal Reformed reading deserves to be steelmanned. Owen's argument is not that the cross's worth is limited but that its design and efficacy are particular: a sacrifice cannot be intended to save the same persons whom it does not in fact save. The grammatical move from πᾶσιν ἀνθρώποις to "all kinds" is defended by appealing to similar phrases elsewhere in the New Testament (e.g., 1 Tim 2:1's "all people" in the context of distinct social categories — kings, those in high position) and by the prior dogmatic commitment that God's salvific purposes are not finally frustrated. **Where this leaves us:** σωτήριος πᾶσιν ἀνθρώποις names a real universality of address and offer. Whether it adjudicates between particular redemption, universal atonement, and eventual universal salvation — or simply asserts the universal *appearance* of grace whose particular application remains to be worked out — depends on prior theological commitments the verse itself does not settle.

**One unresolved question:** If Christ is "our great God and Savior" who "gave himself for us to redeem us from all lawlessness and to purify for himself a people for his own possession" — is the people *periousios* a transformed Israel, a replacement of Israel, or a reconfigured human community in which the historical Israel and the Gentile churches are both still subjects with distinct vocations?

## The Thread

**Exodus 19:5 / Deuteronomy 14:2 LXX** — the source of λαὸς περιούσιος in 2:14. Reading 2:14 against its Septuagintal source brings the supersessionist question into sharper relief: is the church here being constituted as a *new* covenant people, an *enlarged* covenant people, or a *parallel* covenant people?

**1 Timothy 6:14-16** — the only other New Testament passage that combines an *epiphaneia* of Christ with a direct ascription of divine titles (μακάριος καὶ μόνος δυνάστης, ὁ βασιλεὺς τῶν βασιλευόντων). The two passages together form the New Testament's clearest set of *epiphaneia*-Christology texts.

**2 Peter 1:1** — "the righteousness of our God and Savior Jesus Christ," another Granville Sharp construction, and the closest grammatical parallel to Titus 2:13 in the entire New Testament. The pair is the textual heart of the case for direct ascription of θεός to Jesus.

**Ezekiel 37:23 LXX** — "I will purify them … and they shall be my people" — the Septuagintal phrase whose vocabulary stands behind 2:14's "purify for himself a people." The Ezekiel new-covenant text gives the verse its eschatological texture: the redemption envisaged is not merely individual rescue but the constitution of a covenantally renewed humanity.
