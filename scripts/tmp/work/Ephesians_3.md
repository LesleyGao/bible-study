# Ephesians 3: The Manifold Wisdom and the Bowed Knee

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The first words of the chapter — "I, Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus" — anchor it in the carceral reality of the Roman world. If the letter is Pauline, the most plausible setting is the Roman house arrest of Acts 28:30-31, c. 60-62, where Paul was kept under military custody (*custodia militaris*), chained to a rotating Praetorian guard, but with sufficient liberty to receive visitors and dictate correspondence. The Prison Epistles (Philemon, Colossians, Philippians, Ephesians) plausibly date from this window. If the letter is post-Pauline (Mitton, Lincoln, Schnackenburg), the imprisonment language is a literary device honoring the apostolic memory after his death (c. 64-68 under Nero). Either way, "prisoner" was not metaphor for the original audience: Roman imprisonment carried the daily threat of capital sentence, the guard-shaped restriction of movement, and the social shame of being publicly bound. Paul's repeated framing of his chains as honor (Eph 3:1, 13; 4:1; 6:20; cf. Phil 1:12-14) inverts that shame.

The geography of the encyclical's audience matters for chapter 3's vocabulary about "rulers and authorities in the heavenly places." Asian cities — Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamon, Sardis, Laodicea — sat under interlocking systems of power: Roman provincial administration through the proconsul; the *koinon Asias*, the provincial assembly that coordinated imperial-cult worship at neokorate temples (Pergamon held the first, granted under Augustus, c. 29 BC; Smyrna a second, under Tiberius, c. 26 AD; Ephesus eventually a third, under Domitian, c. 89-90); local civic magistracies; powerful priesthoods at the Artemision and the temple of Roma and Augustus; commercial associations (*collegia*, *thiasoi*) with their own patron deities. Layered over these visible powers were the unseen ones: Asian magic, the *Ephesia grammata*, the Greek Magical Papyri (PGM XIII, the *Eighth Book of Moses*, with its catalog of cosmic intermediaries), the astrological doctrine of planetary archons, and the angelology of Second Temple Judaism (1 Enoch, Jubilees, the Qumran *Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice*). When 3:10 says "the manifold wisdom of God" is being made known to the *archais kai exousiais* in the heavenly places through the Church, it speaks into a cosmology that took both political and angelic powers as continuous realities.

Authorship and dating sit where chapters 1-2 left them. The critical position (Mitton, Lincoln, Schnackenburg, Pokorný, MacDonald, Käsemann) reads 3:5 — "his holy apostles and prophets" venerated as a prior generation — as a tell of post-Pauline composition by a disciple in the 70s-90s. Conservative defenders (Hoehner, O'Brien, Bruce, Thielman, M. Barth, Arnold) argue the language fits Pauline self-awareness as part of a foundational generation (cf. 1 Cor 12:28, undisputed Paul) and that the *autobiographical* tone of 3:1-13 is hard to reconcile with pseudonymity. The textual witnesses — P46 (c. 200, with the entire chapter intact), Sinaiticus א, Vaticanus B, Alexandrinus A — give us a stable Greek text; the variants in chapter 3 are minor (the genitive "of Christ Jesus" in 3:1, a prepositional shift in 3:9, the doxology's word order in 3:21).

Seven Greek key terms govern the chapter: μυστήριον (*mystērion*, "mystery") — the chapter's keyword, used four times (3:3, 4, 9, plus the conceptually equivalent 3:5); οἰκονομία (*oikonomia*, "stewardship, plan, dispensation") in 3:2, 9; ἐκκλησία (*ekklēsia*, "church") in 3:10, 21; παρρησία (*parrhēsia*, "boldness, free speech"), originally a political term for the citizen's right of frank speech in the Athenian *ekklēsia*, transferred here to the believer's standing before God in 3:12; συγκληρονόμα (*synklēronoma*, "fellow heirs"), σύσσωμα (*syssōma*, "fellow members of the body"), and συμμέτοχα (*symmetocha*, "fellow partakers"), the three syn- compounds of 3:6 that the author appears to coin; and πολυποίκιλος (*polypoikilos*, "manifold, many-colored, variegated") in 3:10, used in classical Greek for embroidered tapestry.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 3 has an unusual structure. It opens at 3:1 with "for this reason I, Paul…" but then breaks off into a long parenthetical (3:2-13) about Paul's stewardship of the *mystērion*, before resuming the original prayer at 3:14 with the same formula: "for this reason I bow my knees…" Some grammarians (Hoehner, Lincoln) treat 3:2-13 as an extended digression; others (O'Brien) read it as integral, prepared by the chapter break. Either way, the resumed prayer of 3:14-21 is the climax of the indicative half of the letter — chapters 1-3 — before the imperatives of chapters 4-6.

The genre, again, is epideictic and liturgical. The doxology at 3:20-21 ("now to him who is able to do far more abundantly than all that we ask or think… to him be glory in the church and in Christ Jesus throughout all generations, forever and ever, Amen") is a pure liturgical conclusion of a kind that scholars have argued may be an actual doxology used in early Christian assemblies. Markus Barth notes the doxology's combination of ecclesial and Christological referents — "in the church and in Christ Jesus" — as theologically dense and unusual.

Literary devices include the threefold *syn-* compounds of 3:6 (*synklēronoma*, *syssōma*, *symmetocha*), heaping the prefix to perform Gentile equality grammatically; the inclusio between 3:1 ("I, Paul, a prisoner") and 3:13 ("not to lose heart over what I am suffering"); the prayer's spatial metaphor of 3:18 — *to platos kai mēkos kai hypsos kai bathos* (the breadth and length and height and depth) — which patristic readers immediately heard as a hint of cosmic dimensions or the dimensions of the cross. The doxology's *hyperekperissou* (3:20, "far more abundantly") is a double-compound superlative, possibly coined by the writer.

Locating chapter 3 in the book's argument: it completes the indicative half. Chapter 1 announced what God has done in Christ; chapter 2 narrated the death-to-life and Jew-to-Gentile reconciliation; chapter 3 explains how Paul fits as steward of the *mystērion* and prays the readers into the comprehending love that holds the whole vision together. Chapters 4-6 will then work out the imperatives. Variant-wise, 3:9 has manuscript witnesses split between *oikonomia tou mystēriou* and *koinōnia tou mystēriou* ("stewardship" vs. "fellowship of the mystery"); the better-attested reading is *oikonomia*. The doxology's "in the church and in Christ Jesus" of 3:21 is sometimes read as "in the church in Christ Jesus" (single phrase) — a meaningful ecclesiological difference.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Canonically, the *mystērion* of Ephesians 3 — Gentile inclusion as fellow heirs without ceasing to be Gentiles — is the inner content of the eschatological hope already announced in Isa 49:6 ("a light to the nations"), Isa 56:6-8 (the foreigners gathered to the holy mountain), and the Servant Songs more broadly. It is the resolution of the Abrahamic promise of Gen 12:3 ("in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed") and the question Romans 9-11 wrestles with at length. The *polypoikilos sophia* of 3:10 echoes the wisdom traditions of Proverbs 8 and Sirach 24 — wisdom as an attribute of God deployed in creation and history, now made visible *through the Church* to the cosmic powers.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** The patristic reading of 3:18's "breadth and length and height and depth" was almost universally cosmic-cruciform. Origen's *Commentary on Ephesians* (extant in fragments and Latin translation through Jerome) read the four dimensions as the dimensions of the cross extending through all creation. Gregory of Nyssa in his *Treatise on the Inscriptions of the Psalms* and *Catechetical Oration* developed this most beautifully: the four dimensions are how the crucified Christ holds together the whole cosmos. Augustine's *On Christian Doctrine* and *Tractates on John* 118 treat the four dimensions as the cross-shape of love itself — breadth in good works, length in perseverance, height in heavenly hope, depth in mysterious grace. Chrysostom's homilies on Ephesians took 3:14-21 as a model of intercessory prayer and stressed the impossibility of comprehending the love that surpasses knowledge except in concrete love. Theodore of Mopsuestia kept the Antiochene historical-grammatical tone. Marius Victorinus's Latin commentary preserved a neoplatonic reading of the *plērōma* of God.

Aquinas's commentary (*Super Epistolam ad Ephesios* III) treats the *mystērion* as the eternal divine plan now revealed, and the four dimensions as the love of Christ measured against the four cardinal aspects of charity. Bernard of Clairvaux's *On Loving God* draws on 3:18-19. The Reformers — Luther, Calvin, Bullinger — focused on 3:14-21 as a prayer model and a doxological climax; Calvin's commentary stresses that the comprehension of love "with all the saints" is a corporate, not individual, act. The Puritan tradition (John Owen's *On Communion with God*; Jonathan Edwards's *Religious Affections*) made 3:14-21 a primary text for affectional theology.

Critical scholarship from F.C. Baur and Holtzmann onward read the developed apostles-and-prophets-as-foundation language (3:5) as evidence of late composition. Mitton's 1951 monograph consolidated the post-Pauline reading. Lincoln's commentary, Schnackenburg, Pokorný, MacDonald, and Käsemann maintain it. Contemporary readings split. Hoehner, O'Brien, Bruce, Thielman, M. Barth, and Arnold defend Pauline authorship and read 3:1-13 as autobiographical. Markus Bockmuehl's *Revelation and Mystery in Ancient Judaism and Pauline Christianity* (1990) is essential for understanding *mystērion*: it is not Hellenistic mystery-cult vocabulary (Eleusinian, Mithraic, Dionysian) but apocalyptic-Jewish, drawing on the *raz* of Daniel 2:18-19, 28-30 and the "mysteries of God" in Qumran (1QpHab 7; 1QS 11; the *Mysteries* scroll 1Q27). The Pauline mystery is eschatological revelation — what was hidden in God's plan and is now disclosed in the appointed time.

J.L. Martyn's apocalyptic Paul, Beverly Gaventa's *Our Mother Saint Paul*, Susan Eastman's *Paul and the Person*, and Douglas Campbell's *Deliverance of God* press the disclosure-of-the-hidden as God's invasive act. Walter Wink's trilogy — *Naming the Powers* (1984), *Unmasking the Powers* (1986), *Engaging the Powers* (1992) — reads the *archai kai exousiai* of 3:10 as both spiritual realities and the inward dimensions of social-structural domination systems (the spirit of empire, the spirit of corporations, the spirit of states). Hendrik Berkhof's *Christ and the Powers* anticipated this reading; N.T. Wright's *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* offers a both-and: the Powers are real spiritual realities AND they manifest socially. Stephen Fowl's theological commentary engages this tradition.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** Jewish reception of Ephesians 3:6's *synklēronoma / syssōma / symmetocha* — Gentiles as fellow heirs, fellow body, fellow partakers — has historically read it as a Christian universalization that risks erasing Israel's distinct election. Daniel Boyarin (*A Radical Jew*, 1994) framed Paul's universalism as a Jewish move that nevertheless threatened Jewish particularity. Krister Stendahl, E.P. Sanders, James Dunn, and N.T. Wright reframed Paul as addressing the inclusion of Gentiles into the people of Israel's God without erasing Israel. Mark Nanos (*The Mystery of Romans*; *Reading Paul within Judaism*) reads Paul as remaining within Judaism. Pamela Eisenbaum's *Paul Was Not a Christian* (2009) insists Paul's mission target was Gentiles, with Israel's continuing covenant assumed. Paula Fredriksen's *Paul: The Pagans' Apostle* (2017) sets the *mystērion* of Gentile inclusion in Paul's apocalyptic-Jewish horizon: God is gathering the nations to worship Israel's God in the last days, exactly as the prophets foretold.

Post-Holocaust dialogue — *Nostra Aetate* §4 (1965); the 1985 Vatican *Notes on the Correct Way to Present the Jews and Judaism in Preaching and Catechesis*; the 2015 *The Gifts and the Calling of God Are Irrevocable* — has shifted Catholic teaching firmly against supersessionism. Mark Kinzer's *Postmissionary Messianic Judaism* (2005) and Michael Wyschogrod's *The Body of Faith* defend ongoing Jewish particularity within the *ekklēsia*. R. Kendall Soulen's *The God of Israel and Christian Theology* (1996) names the structural problem that Pauline universalism, when read carelessly, makes Israel a placeholder. Where contemporary Jewish scholarship still pushes back is on the *mystērion* language itself: if the mystery was "hidden for ages" (3:9) and only now revealed, does that imply Israel's centuries of covenantal life were a kind of waiting room? Or, more carefully, that Israel's prophets always anticipated this disclosure (cf. Rom 16:25-26), and the "hiddenness" was the *Christological form* of Gentile inclusion, not the inclusion itself?

**Where this leaves us.** The dialogue is no longer a dispute between caricatures. Christian readers who take the New Perspective and post-Holocaust theology seriously read the *mystērion* as Israel-fulfilled-in-Messiah Gentile inclusion that does not displace Israel.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What does it mean that the *mystērion* was "hidden for ages and now revealed"?** Roman Catholic teaching in *Dei Verbum* 2-4 frames revelation as the divine economy of self-disclosure climaxing in Christ; the *mystērion* is the eternal plan now made visible, and Scripture and Tradition together transmit it (DV 9-10). Reformed sola scriptura agrees on the climactic revelation in Christ but locates the *norma normans* of revelation in the canonical Scriptures alone (Westminster Confession 1.6, 1.10). Apocalyptic Paul (Martyn, Campbell, Gaventa) reads the disclosure as God's invasive act that disrupts and recreates — the hidden mystery breaks in from outside, not as the natural unfolding of an immanent process. Eastern Orthodox theology, in Pseudo-Dionysius and Vladimir Lossky, holds that revelation is always also concealment: God reveals himself precisely as the hidden one, and apophatic theology preserves the *mystērion* as ongoing mystery rather than dissolved puzzle. The New Perspective reads the *mystērion* corporately: Gentile inclusion in Israel's covenantal blessing is what was hidden and is now revealed — a redemptive-historical, not metaphysical, mystery.

The position I find least intuitive is the Eastern apophatic, so let me steelman it. Pseudo-Dionysius in the *Mystical Theology* and Lossky in *The Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church* argue that any revelation that fully resolves into propositional knowledge is not yet revelation of the *true* God, who infinitely exceeds all categories. The *mystērion* of Eph 3 must therefore be understood as a *participatory* mystery — known by being entered, not by being explained — and 3:18-19's "love that surpasses knowledge" makes this explicit: the comprehension is real but never exhaustive, an ongoing dwelling rather than a closed dossier. The strength of this reading is that it takes seriously the *gnōnai tēn hyperballousan tēs gnōseōs agapēn tou Christou* ("to know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge") of 3:19 as a deliberate paradox, not a rhetorical flourish. **Where this leaves us:** the *mystērion* is genuinely revealed (otherwise the doxology and the prayer make no sense) and yet the revelation is participatory, not exhaustive (otherwise 3:19 is incoherent); both poles must be held together, which is where every major tradition actually arrives if it reads the chapter carefully.

**Tension 2: What does 3:10 mean — that "through the Church the manifold wisdom of God might now be made known to the rulers and authorities in the heavenly places"?** The traditional ontological reading (Origen, Augustine, Aquinas, the medieval angelological tradition) takes the *archai kai exousiai* as real spiritual beings — angels of various ranks, including fallen ones — to whom God's wisdom is being displayed in the formation of the multi-ethnic Church. Bultmann's demythologization treats the powers as mythological projections of human existential predicaments, to be reread in terms of authentic existence; few contemporary scholars hold this position straight, but its hermeneutical move (the powers as symbolic) lingers. Walter Wink's trilogy reframes the powers as both spiritual realities *and* the inner dimensions of social-structural domination — empires, corporations, ideologies, institutions all have a spiritual interiority that is fallen and can be redeemed. Hendrik Berkhof's *Christ and the Powers* and John Howard Yoder's *Politics of Jesus* (chapter on the powers) developed this further. N.T. Wright's *The Day the Revolution Began* and *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* offer a both-and: the powers are genuine spiritual realities, *and* they manifest as social-political structures, and Christ's victory addresses both. Apocalyptic Paul (Martyn, Campbell, Gaventa) reads the powers as the cosmic enemies (Sin, Death, Flesh, the Elemental Principles) that hold humanity captive and that God invades and defeats in Christ. Catholic angelology (CCC §§328-336) affirms the existence of angelic powers, with the *Catechism* citing Eph 3:10 implicitly in its account of the angels' service in salvation history.

The position I find least intuitive is Wink's social-structural reading, so let me steelman it. Wink argued (and his exegesis in *Naming the Powers* chapter 3 is careful) that the New Testament's powers vocabulary is consistently used in ways that resist a clean spirit/material distinction: *archai*, *exousiai*, *kosmokratores*, *thronoi*, *kyriotetes*, *dynameis* are deployed for emperors, priestly hierarchies, demonic forces, and angelic ones interchangeably. To split these into "real spiritual beings" versus "social structures" is to impose a modern Cartesian dualism on a pre-Cartesian text. Wink's proposal is that every social institution has an inner spirituality (its "angel," cf. Rev 2-3's "angels" of the seven churches) that can be fallen or redeemed, and the Church's existence as a multi-ethnic body — *that* is the *polypoikilos sophia* — is what announces to those powers that God's reign relativizes them. The strength is exegetical (it explains the slipperiness of the vocabulary) and pastoral (it makes the chapter applicable to systemic injustice without dissolving its spiritual dimension). The weakness is that it can be flattened in either direction by lazy readers — into pure sociology or pure demonology. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter requires us to take both spiritual realities and social-structural domination as real, and to see the multi-ethnic, reconciled Church as itself a public proclamation to both kinds of "rulers."

**Unresolved question:** If the very existence of a Church that holds Jew and Gentile together "in one body" is what announces God's wisdom to the cosmic powers, what does the manifest disunity of the actual Church across two thousand years say to those same powers — and is the announcement diminished, or is it that the powers are simply not paying attention to a Church that has stopped being what 3:6 said it was?

## The Thread

- **Daniel 2:18-19, 28-30** — the *raz* of Daniel ("the mystery / God in heaven who reveals mysteries") is the apocalyptic-Jewish background for *mystērion* in Eph 3; without Daniel, the chapter sounds Hellenistic (mystery cults) rather than Jewish-apocalyptic. Bockmuehl's monograph documents this thoroughly.
- **Romans 16:25-27** — the doxology that closes Romans is the closest parallel to Eph 3:20-21 in the Pauline corpus, and it explicitly says the mystery "kept secret for long ages but has now been disclosed and through the prophetic writings has been made known to all nations." This binds the Eph 3 *mystērion* tightly to Israel's prophetic tradition.
- **1 Corinthians 2:6-10** — "we speak the wisdom of God in a mystery, even the hidden wisdom which God ordained before the world… which none of the rulers of this age understood" — undisputed Pauline use of the same constellation of terms (*sophia*, *mystērion*, *archontes tou aiōnos toutou*) that Eph 3 deploys.
- **Revelation 5:11-14** (later in reading order) — myriads of myriads of angels around the throne worship the Lamb. If Eph 3:10 says God's wisdom is being made known to the heavenly powers through the Church, Revelation 5 is the eschatological scene in which those powers respond — the cosmic doxology that the Church's earthly doxology of Eph 3:20-21 anticipates.
