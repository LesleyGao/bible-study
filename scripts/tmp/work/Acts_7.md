# Acts 7: Stephen's Speech, the Standing Son of Man, and the Birth of Christian Martyrology

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Stephen's trial and execution occur in Jerusalem in roughly 33–35 CE — Hengel and Bruce both place it within two to four years of the resurrection — at a moment when the Jerusalem Sanhedrin still functioned as a working judicial body but operated under a procuratorial regime (Pontius Pilate, 26–36 CE) that ordinarily reserved capital sentencing to Roman authority. The legal status of Stephen's stoning is therefore historically vexed. Bruce, Barrett, Fitzmyer, and Schnabel read the execution as essentially a mob action under judicial cover — a council scene that escalates into lynching when the crowd "rushed together at him" (7:57) — which is consistent with the procuratorial gap that may have existed during the transition from Pilate to Marcellus, or with the Sanhedrin acting at the limit of its competence in cases of perceived blasphemy against the temple. Witherington and Pervo more cautiously read it as extra-legal violence the procurator chose not to prosecute. The detail that "the witnesses laid down their garments at the feet of a young man named Saul" (7:58) reflects Deuteronomy 17:7, which requires the witnesses to throw the first stones — Luke is signaling that the form of the execution followed Mosaic procedure even as the legal authorization was uncertain.

The temple Stephen indicts in 7:48 is the **Herodian Temple** as expanded under Herod the Great's program begun in 20–19 BCE and still under construction in the 30s CE (the works were not completed until 63 CE under Herod Agrippa II, only seven years before destruction). Excavations of the Temple Mount platform — Benjamin Mazar's southern wall excavations of the 1960s and 1970s, Ronny Reich and Eli Shukron's recent work on the Robinson's Arch staircase and the Herodian street — confirm the colossal scale and ritual centrality of the institution Stephen is claiming "the Most High does not dwell" in. The synagogue of the Freedmen and the diaspora congregations of 6:9 (Cyrenian, Alexandrian, Cilician, Asian) gathered around Greek-language Torah-reading communities in Jerusalem; the **Theodotos Inscription** (Weill, 1913–1914) attests the institutional pattern. Caesarea Maritima, where the Pilate Inscription was discovered in 1961 by Antonio Frova in the theater's reused stones, would become the procuratorial seat from which subsequent persecutions of the Hellenist circle would be authorized.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter. ***Skēnē tou martyriou*** (σκηνὴ τοῦ μαρτυρίου, 7:44) — "tent of witness," the LXX rendering of *ohel mo'ed* — is the load-bearing term for Stephen's anti-temple argument: the tabernacle was the divinely commanded mode of presence, the temple was a Davidic-Solomonic *innovation*. ***Ho hupsistos*** (ὁ ὕψιστος, 7:48) — "the Most High" — is a deliberately archaizing divine title (frequent in 1 Enoch, the Sibylline Oracles, and Hellenistic-Jewish prayers) that puts distance between God and the localized temple cult. ***Sklērotrachēloi*** (σκληροτράχηλοι, 7:51) — "stiff-necked" — quotes Exodus 33:3, 33:5, 34:9 directly, embedding Stephen's indictment in Mosaic prophetic-polemic genre. ***Ton huion tou anthrōpou hestōta*** (τὸν υἱὸν τοῦ ἀνθρώπου ἑστῶτα, 7:56) — "the Son of Man **standing**" — is the most theologically loaded participle in the New Testament, which we will return to. ***Kyrie Iēsou, dexai to pneuma mou*** (κύριε Ἰησοῦ, δέξαι τὸ πνεῦμα μου, 7:59) — "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit" — is direct prayer addressed to Jesus, programmatic for high Christology in the earliest Christian piety.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

At fifty-two verses, Stephen's speech is by some margin the longest in Acts and one of the longest pieces of continuous monologue in the New Testament. Its compositional history has been argued for over a century. **Earl Richard's** *Acts 6:1–8:4: The Author's Method of Composition* (1978) remains the foundational source-critical treatment: Richard demonstrates that Stephen's speech contains theological content (the anti-temple polemic, the Moses-rejected-prophet typology, the Most-High-language) that sits in tension with Luke's own temple-positive framing in Luke 1–2 (Zechariah, the presentation, Anna the prophetess) and Acts 1–5 (the apostles teaching daily in the temple), suggesting Luke is incorporating preserved Hellenist tradition rather than freely composing. **Marcel Simon's** *Saint Stephen and the Jewish Wisdom Movement* anticipated Richard by arguing for a wisdom-tradition substrate beneath the speech. **Hengel** and **Räisänen** subsequently read the speech as the closest thing we have to a Hellenist-Christian theological manifesto from the early 30s. **Pervo** (Hermeneia) and **Marguerat** push back with a more Lukan-compositional reading: the speech is a Lukan rhetorical set-piece in the conventions of Hellenistic historiography (Thucydides 1.22.1 on speeches), with Luke supplying what *would have been said* on the occasion. The current consensus is a synthesis — preserved Hellenist tradition reworked by Luke into Lukan prose, much as the speeches in Acts 13 and 17 show Pauline themes in Lukan style.

The speech's **structure** is a programmatic Old Testament survey: Abraham (7:2–8), Joseph (7:9–16), Moses (7:17–43), Tabernacle (7:44–46), Temple (7:47–50), Indictment (7:51–53). The proportion is diagnostic — Moses receives twenty-seven verses, more than half of the entire historical retrospect, and within the Moses section Stephen develops the theme that Israel's fathers *rejected* Moses (7:25, 7:27, 7:35, 7:39) before Moses was vindicated by God. This is Moses-as-rejected-prophet typology in service of a Christological argument: as Israel rejected Moses but God vindicated him as "ruler and redeemer" (7:35), so Israel rejected the "prophet like Moses" (Deut 18:15, quoted at 7:37) and God has now vindicated him at God's right hand. Tannehill's *Narrative Unity* notes that Luke is also constructing parallels between Stephen's trial and Jesus's: false witnesses (7:58 / Mark 14:57), a charge of speaking against the temple (6:14 / Mark 14:58), a Son of Man saying at the climactic moment (7:56 / Mark 14:62), a death-prayer of forgiveness for the killers (7:60 / Luke 23:34), a commendation of the spirit (7:59 / Luke 23:46). Stephen is the first imitator of Christ in Acts, and Luke is teaching his readers to read martyrdom Christologically.

The **Molech and Rephan citation** (7:42–43) is one of the most interesting textual moments in Acts. Stephen quotes Amos 5:25–27, but in the **LXX form**, which differs from the Masoretic Hebrew at three key points: the LXX has "tent of Moloch" where the MT has "Sikkuth your king," and "star of your god Rephan" where the MT has "Kiyyun your star-god"; and the LXX has "beyond Babylon" where the MT has "beyond Damascus." The discrepancy was already noted by patristic exegetes (Jerome flags it in his commentary on Amos) and has been used in modern scholarship — Bruce, Barrett, Fitzmyer, and Keener all discuss it — to demonstrate that Stephen (or Luke representing Stephen) is working from a Greek Bible, which fits the Hellenist circle's bilingual character. The substantive theological move is unaffected: Stephen reads Israel's wilderness sacrifices as already idolatrous, which sharpens the temple critique by claiming the cultic infidelity is older than Solomon's building.

Textual variants in chapter 7 are mostly minor, but at 7:55–56 the Western text (Codex Bezae) preserves "*standing* at the right hand of God" stably while some Old Latin witnesses smooth to "seated," reflecting later harmonization to Psalm 110:1 (cited at Mark 14:62, Acts 2:33, Hebrews 1:3). The textual stability of the *standing* participle in the best manuscripts is itself an argument that Luke is preserving an unusual Hellenist Christology rather than smoothing.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Jewish interpretive trajectory on Acts 7 is dominated by reception-history concerns. **Stephen's indictment in 7:51–53 has been used in Christian anti-Jewish polemic for two millennia** — read as a blanket condemnation of "the Jews" rather than as an intra-Jewish prophetic-polemic in the genre of Amos, Jeremiah, the Qumran *Hodayot* (1QHa), the *War Scroll* (1QM) self-critique, and the *Wisdom of Solomon's* indictment of Israel's idolatries. **Adele Reinhartz's** *Cast Out of the Covenant* and **Amy-Jill Levine's** *The Misunderstood Jew* are foundational post-Holocaust correctives: both insist that 7:51's "stiff-necked, uncircumcised in heart and ears" is *Stephen quoting Moses against Moses's own people* (Exod 33:3, Deut 10:16, Jer 9:26), a recognized prophetic move within the Hebrew Bible's own self-critical tradition, not a Christian condemnation of Judaism as such. **Anthony Saldarini** insisted on the methodological discipline that intra-Jewish polemic of the first century must not be read through the post-Constantinian lens of Christianity-versus-Judaism. The **Vatican II declaration *Nostra Aetate*** (1965) and the **Pontifical Biblical Commission's *The Jewish People and Their Sacred Scriptures in the Christian Bible*** (PCBC, 2001) formalized this hermeneutic shift in Roman Catholic teaching: Stephen's words must be read in their first-century intra-Jewish setting, and modern Jews are not the targets of his speech. **Daniel Boyarin's** *Border Lines* and **Lawrence Schiffman's** *Who Was a Jew?* underscore that the boundary between Stephen's Jewish-Christian community and his Jewish-non-Christian opponents was porous and contested, not the settled boundary later Christian and Jewish institutions would construct.

The Christian interpretive trajectory begins with Patristic absorption of Stephen as proto-martyr. **Eusebius's** *Ecclesiastical History* 2.1 makes Stephen the first martyr — *prōtomartyr* — and the cornerstone of the developing martyrology tradition. **John Chrysostom's** *55 Homilies on Acts* (Homilies 15–17 cover chapter 7) reads Stephen's death-prayer of forgiveness as the highest imitation of Christ available to the Christian, and the homilies became a major influence on Eastern liturgical reception. **Bede's** *Expositio Actuum Apostolorum* preserved the Patristic readings for the medieval West. **Augustine** preached repeatedly on Stephen — his Sermons 314–324 — and developed the theme that the church grows through martyrdom, treating Saul's later conversion as the *fruit* of Stephen's prayer in 7:60. The discovery of Stephen's relics by the priest Lucian in 415 CE near Jerusalem, and their translation to Constantinople and Rome, made Stephen a major cultic figure across Christendom. **Calvin's** *Commentary on Acts* reads the speech as a model of how to use scripture under pressure — Calvin admires Stephen's polemical use of Israel's own history against his accusers, and uses the chapter to defend Reformed prophetic preaching. **Luther** preached on Stephen as a model of *fides sub cruce* (faith under the cross). The medieval iconography of Stephen — vested as a deacon, holding stones, with a palm of martyrdom — preserved Chrysostom's diaconal reading of chapter 6 alongside the martyrological reading of chapter 7.

Twentieth-century critical scholarship reframed the speech. **Conzelmann's** *Theology of St. Luke* read it as the seam between salvation-history stages — the moment Israel's rejection of the Messiah becomes definitive and the Gospel begins moving outward. **Haenchen's** Acts commentary read the anti-temple polemic as a Hellenist-circle theology Luke is preserving without fully endorsing. **Hengel's** *Between Jesus and Paul* gave the field its now-canonical reconstruction: Stephen's circle held a temple-critical, law-relativizing theology that prepared the ground for Paul. **Richard Bauckham's** *Jesus and the God of Israel* reads 7:55–56 — the **Son of Man standing at the right hand of God** — as one of the highest Christological claims in the New Testament, since the unique *standing* (against the Psalm 110:1 *seated* tradition cited at Mark 14:62 and elsewhere) suggests Jesus is positioned as advocate, witness, or eschatological judge in the heavenly throne-room. Bauckham argues this is a Hellenist circle Christology that Luke is preserving from an early stratum. **N.T. Wright**, in *Acts for Everyone* and across *How God Became King*, *Jesus and the Victory of God*, and *Surprised by Hope*, reads Stephen's speech as the early church thinking out loud about the Temple in light of Easter: the long survey of Israel's story is built to make two points — that God's presence was never finally tied to a building (the patriarchs met him in Mesopotamia and Egypt and Sinai, long before Solomon laid a stone), and that Israel had a recurring habit of rejecting the deliverers God sent (Joseph sold by his brothers, Moses spurned, and now "the Righteous One" handed over and killed). For Wright the speech's climax — "I see the heavens opened, and the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God" — is the decisive thing: Daniel 7's vindicated son of man is on his feet, which means Jesus has been enthroned and is now both Stephen's advocate and the world's true judge, and it means the place where God's glory now resides is the risen Christ and the Spirit-filled people, not the Herodian sanctuary. Stephen himself, dying with a prayer of forgiveness on his lips, is for Wright the first Christian to walk the road Jesus walked — the pattern of cross-shaped witness that the rest of Acts will trace. Among the major contemporary Acts commentaries, **Bruce** (NICNT), **Barrett** (ICC), **Fitzmyer** (Anchor Bible), **Gaventa** (Abingdon), **Johnson** (Sacra Pagina), **Keener** (4 vols), **Witherington III**, **Schnabel** (ZECNT), **Conzelmann** (Hermeneia), **Pervo** (Hermeneia), **Peterson** (Pillar), **Marguerat**, and **Marshall** (TNTC) all give significant treatment to the speech; Keener's volume on chapter 7 alone runs to several hundred pages and is the most encyclopedic reference. Pentecostal scholarship (**Stronstad**, **Menzies**) reads Stephen's prophetic-charismatic speech and his death-vision as paradigmatic for Spirit-filled prophetic witness. **Justo González's** *Acts (Belief)* reads the speech as a model of how the church speaks truth to entrenched religious power; commentators across the spectrum (Gaventa, Johnson, Marguerat) note that Stephen's stoning is the first martyrdom-for-the-faith in Acts — the deaths of Ananias and Sapphira in chapter 5 are judgment, not witness — and that with it the word *martys*, "witness," begins its long slide toward "martyr."

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions structure the chapter. **First**, what is the relationship between the temple/tabernacle and God's presence? The **anti-temple reading** (Hengel, Richard, the Hellenist-source position) takes 7:48 — "the Most High does not dwell in houses made by human hands" — at maximum strength: Stephen is denying that Solomon's Temple was a legitimate divinely authorized site of presence in the way the wilderness tabernacle was. This reading sees the speech as a fundamental Hellenist-Christian critique of the temple cult, anticipating Hebrews's argument that the temple's services are a shadow of the heavenly reality. The **traditional Catholic and Reformed reading** treats 7:48 more moderately: God is *not localized* in any building (an Old Testament principle going back to 1 Kings 8:27, Solomon's own dedication prayer), but the temple was nevertheless legitimately commanded — Stephen's critique is of presumption *about* the temple, not the temple itself. The **Eastern Orthodox reading** preserved by Chrysostom takes a third position: the speech is about the *fulfillment* of temple worship in Christ, not its abolition; the church becomes the new temple in which the Spirit dwells. The steel-man for the anti-temple position is that 7:48 quotes Isaiah 66:1–2 in exactly the way Stephen's circle would have used it to relativize the cult, and that the Hellenist-circle's subsequent missionary trajectory (Antioch, Gentiles) makes most sense if their theology had already loosened the cultic-territorial frame. The steel-man for the traditional reading is that Luke himself depicts the apostles in chapters 2–5 as continuing to teach in the temple, and Paul in chapter 21 as participating in temple rites; Luke would not preserve a speech he understood as flatly anti-temple. **Where this leaves us:** the speech is a programmatic relativization of temple cult — God's presence is not localized, the temple is a Davidic-Solomonic development of an older tabernacle-mode of presence, and the cult cannot be the guarantor of fidelity — but it stops short of abolition. N.T. Wright would add that the speech is best read not as anti-temple but as *post-temple*: the sanctuary's vocation, to be the place where heaven and earth meet, has now passed to the risen Jesus and to the Spirit-filled community, so the building is not so much condemned as superseded. The Hellenist circle held a temple-critical theology that the Twelve did not fully share, and Luke is honest enough to preserve it.

**Second**, what does the *standing* Son of Man at God's right hand mean? The **forensic-advocate reading** (Bauckham, Hengel, Marshall) treats the standing as Jesus rising to receive Stephen as witness — Stephen has confessed Christ before the council, and Christ now confesses Stephen before the Father (Luke 12:8). The **eschatological-judge reading** (Pervo, Marguerat) treats the standing as Jesus assuming the posture of judgment — the Son of Man rising to render the eschatological verdict against the council that has rejected him. The **enthronement-reading** (Conzelmann) treats the *standing* as roughly equivalent to *seated* and merely a stylistic variant. The steel-man for the forensic reading is the Lukan parallel at Luke 12:8 ("the Son of Man also will acknowledge before the angels of God"), and the Lukan habit of programmatic Christological echoes. The steel-man for the eschatological-judge reading is the broader Son-of-Man tradition in Daniel 7:13–14 and Mark 14:62 where the figure is judge. **Where this leaves us:** the *standing* is theologically richer than either reading alone. Jesus stands as advocate *and* as judge — confessing Stephen, indicting the council, and receiving the martyr's spirit. The participle is doing too much work to collapse into either pole.

**Unresolved question**: did the Hellenist-circle Christology preserved at 7:55–56 — Son of Man, standing, prayer addressed directly to Jesus, commendation of spirit to Jesus — reflect a high Christology already operative in the early 30s, or is Luke retrojecting later-developed Christological language onto an earlier scene? The answer determines the rate of Christological development in the first decade of the Jesus movement and bears directly on debates between Bauckham (early high Christology) and earlier liberal-Protestant developmentalist accounts (Bousset, late Hellenistic emergence).

## The Thread

Stephen's prayer of forgiveness in 7:60 — "Lord, do not hold this sin against them" — programs the next chapter's narrative: Saul of Tarsus, present at the stoning (7:58), will become Paul through what Augustine reads as the answered prayer of his victim. The "young man named Saul" of 7:58 is the same Saul whose ravaging of the church in 8:3, conversion in 9:1–19, and missionary career in chapters 13–28 will dominate the rest of Acts. Stephen's anti-temple polemic at 7:48 is taken up directly by Paul at Athens in 17:24 ("the God who made the world... does not live in temples made by human hands") — Luke is signaling that Paul inherits Stephen's theology. The Moses-rejected-then-vindicated typology of 7:35 anticipates Peter's Christological claim at 3:13–15 retrospectively and structures the apostolic preaching pattern across the rest of the book. Finally, the *skēnē tou martyriou* of 7:44 — the tent of *witness* — quietly puns on *martys* (witness/martyr): Stephen has just made his life into a tent of witness, and the word "martyr" begins its semantic shift from witness-in-court to witness-unto-death in the chapters that follow.
