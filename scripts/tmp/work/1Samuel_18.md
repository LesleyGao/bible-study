# 1 Samuel 18: The Knit Soul, the Women's Song, and the Bride-Price of Foreskins

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter takes place entirely within the apparatus of Saul's emerging royal court at Gibeah—the only Israelite court of the period for which we have any literary depiction. Archaeological identification of Saul's Gibeah is debated; W. F. Albright's 1922–1933 excavations at Tell el-Ful identified an Iron I fortified residence as Saul's "rustic palace," and although later scholars (Lawrence Stager, Israel Finkelstein) have questioned the chronology, the site remains the leading candidate. The structure was modest: a fortified rectangular residence approximately fifty meters by thirty-five, hardly comparable to the palatial complexes of contemporary Egyptian or Hittite kingship. Saul's court is a tribal warlord's headquarters, not a Bronze Age imperial palace—and the chapter's depictions of personal access (David plays the lyre in the king's room, Saul throws his own spear) reflect that scale of operation.

The political mechanics displayed in the chapter are characteristic of early state formation. Saul's "thousand-man commands" (v. 13) reflect the *eleph* (אֶלֶף) social unit, originally a clan-based military levy of perhaps fifty to a hundred men rather than literally one thousand—George Mendenhall and Norman Gottwald both argued that the *eleph* terminology preserves an older tribal-military structure even after the term took on the round-numerical sense. The chapter's casual interweaving of "thousand" (military command) and "ten thousand" (the women's song, vv. 7–8) plays on the elasticity of these terms; Saul's outrage in v. 8 reflects his correct perception that the proportional disparity is socially significant, even if the literal numbers should not be taken arithmetically.

Marriage politics in the chapter illustrate dynastic alliance practice across the ancient Near East. The royal bride functioned as a mechanism of political incorporation; royal sons-in-law were drawn into the king's faction by the marriage and posed a threat by it (a son-in-law had a claim on the throne through his wife). The Mari archives, the Amarna correspondence, and Hittite vassal treaties all show this dynamic at work in the second millennium BCE. Saul's exploitation of the convention—offering Merab and then Michal explicitly as a "snare" (*moqesh*, מוֹקֵשׁ, v. 21)—is realistic. The bride-price (*mohar*, מֹהַר) Saul demands, "a hundred foreskins of the Philistines" (v. 25), is unusual but conceptually transparent: a *mohar* could be paid in any form of valuable object, and military trophies were a recognized form. Egyptian battlefield reports under Merneptah and Ramesses III famously include trophy counts—severed hands or phalli—as proof of slain enemies. The 1995 publication of the Medinet Habu reliefs depicting heaps of foreskins from Libyan and Sea Peoples enemies under Ramesses III is the closest cultural parallel; the Egyptian convention was specifically that uncircumcised enemies were marked by genital trophy, while circumcised enemies were marked by hand-trophies. Saul's demand mobilizes the same trophy logic, and the demand specifically that the foreskins be Philistine reinforces the same boundary marker David had invoked in 17:26—the Philistines as the conspicuously uncircumcised population.

The musical instruments in the women's processional (v. 6) are precisely identifiable. The **toph** (תֹּף, "tambourine" or "frame drum") is the small hand-held membrane drum attested across the eastern Mediterranean, depicted in Egyptian and Levantine reliefs and recovered as ceramic models in archaeological contexts (notably from Beit Shean and Megiddo). The phrase translated "musical instruments" in v. 6 is *shalishim* (שָׁלִשִׁים), an obscure term variously rendered "three-stringed instruments," "triangles," or "sistra"; its etymology is from the root *shalosh* ("three") and may indicate a three-cornered instrument. The **shir** of v. 6—the women's victory song—belongs to a known Israelite genre, the women's military celebration song, whose other examples are Miriam's song (Exod 15:20–21), Deborah's song (Judg 5), and Jephthah's daughter (Judg 11:34). The convention was for women to greet returning warriors with antiphonal song and dance.

Three Hebrew terms repay attention. First, *niqsherah nephesh-Yehonatan be-nephesh David* (נִקְשְׁרָה נֶפֶשׁ יְהוֹנָתָן בְּנֶפֶשׁ דָּוִד, "the soul of Jonathan was knit to the soul of David," v. 1)—the verb *qashar* (קָשַׁר) means literally "to bind, tie, conspire"; it is used elsewhere of military leagues (1 Sam 22:8) and treasonous conspiracies (2 Kings 11:14). The "knitting" is not a soft-focus metaphor but a strong covenantal binding. Second, *ahav* (אָהֵב, "love") appears no fewer than four times in this chapter—Jonathan loves David (vv. 1, 3), Michal loves David (vv. 20, 28), and "all Israel and Judah" love David (v. 16). Susan Ackerman's *When Heroes Love* (2005) catalogues the political-covenantal valences of *ahav* in this period: ANE vassal treaties routinely command the vassal to "love" the suzerain, and the term carries treaty-loyalty connotations alongside affective ones. Third, *moqesh* (מוֹקֵשׁ, "snare," v. 21) is the trapper's term for a hunting snare; Saul's frame for the marriage is explicitly predatory.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is one of the most densely worked pieces of narrative artistry in the Hebrew Bible. Robert Alter, in *The David Story*, treats it as a masterclass in indirect characterization: the chapter never tells us what David thinks or feels, but it tells us repeatedly what others think and feel about David, and it shows us David's actions but not his interior. The narrator is constructing David as a man whose interior is opaque—a feature that will define the entire narrative through 2 Samuel.

The chapter's structure can be analyzed as a triad of love-relations (Jonathan, the women, Michal) followed by a triad of Saul's attempts to destroy David (the spear, the Merab promise, the Michal/foreskins trap). Jan Fokkelman's literary commentary maps the parallel architecture: each of David's lovers is matched by a Saulide attempt to weaponize the love into David's destruction. Jonathan's covenant fails to provide cover (Saul will pursue David anyway); the women's song provokes Saul's paranoia; Michal's love becomes the marriage that Saul tries to make a death-trap. Love is consistently the medium through which Saul's hostility passes.

The textual situation here is again significant. The MT preserves verses that the LXX-B (Vaticanus) omits: vv. 1–5, 9–11, 17–19, 29b–30. The omitted MT material includes (1) Jonathan's covenant and stripping; (2) the spear-throwing; (3) the Merab episode. The LXX is shorter by approximately 25%. The same critical division applies as in chapter 17: Tov, McCarter, and others treat the LXX as preserving an earlier shorter text; Pisano and others treat it as abridgment. The Merab episode is particularly interesting—2 Samuel 21:8 has Merab's five sons handed over to the Gibeonites, and the MT of 1 Sam 18:19 says she was married to Adriel the Meholathite. The LXX omission may be an attempt to harmonize with 2 Sam 21:8 (which in some manuscripts reads "Michal" rather than "Merab"). The textual layers are thick.

Several literary devices anchor the chapter. The **antiphonal couplet of v. 7** ("Saul has struck down his thousands, / and David his ten thousands") is a parallel ascending couplet in classic Hebrew poetic form; the second colon intensifies the first. The intensification is conventional for Hebrew poetry, which typically escalates within parallel structures (cf. "the heavens declare the glory of God, / the firmament shows his handiwork"). Saul's outrage misreads the convention: he treats the intensification as a literal denigration. The narrator may be showing Saul's hermeneutical incompetence—he cannot read parallelism properly. Polzin's literary commentary makes this point sharply.

The **spear-throwing** is the chapter's signature device. The MT presents Saul throwing the spear twice on the same day (v. 11: "But David evaded him twice"); the LXX has only one throw. Saul's spear is a recurring narrative object across 1 Samuel: it identifies him at his first appearance (the *chanit* he holds) and accompanies him through the messengers-to-Naioth scene in chapter 19, the cave at Hachilah in chapter 26 (where David steals it), and his death on Gilboa in chapter 31 (where he falls on his own sword, but the spear is the symbol). David Gunn's *The Fate of King Saul* treats the spear as the externalized correlate of Saul's psychic disintegration: when Saul holds his weapon, his madness is gripping him.

The **two-stage Michal proposal** (vv. 20–27) is structurally elaborate. Saul approaches David indirectly through servants (a face-saving move for the rejected first proposal of Merab); David protests his unworthiness; the bride-price is communicated; David exceeds the demand (two hundred for one hundred). The doubling of the bride-price is significant—David is both rejecting the implied corruption (the deal exists for him to be killed) and converting the test into proof of his loyalty. Alter notes that David's overperformance is also dynastic positioning: by exceeding the demand, he places Saul in his moral debt.

The **chapter's ending** (vv. 28–30) compresses the resolution: Saul realizes the LORD is with David and Michal loves David, and Saul is "even more afraid"—Saul's fear escalates rather than abates with each demonstration of David's favor. David's success becomes Saul's wound. The chapter sets up the open hostility of chapters 19–20.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical thread initiated here is the **Jonathan-David covenant**, which will return in chapters 19 (Jonathan's intercession), 20 (the field covenant), 23 (the Horesh meeting), and 2 Samuel 1 (David's lament) and 2 Samuel 9 (David's kindness to Mephibosheth). The chapter is the friendship's founding moment, and Jonathan's act of stripping his royal robe and giving it to David is the chapter's most theologically loaded gesture. Jonathan, as Saul's heir, is symbolically abdicating his royal claim—the robe (*me'il*, מְעִיל) is the same garment that figures iconically in the Saul-Samuel exchange of 15:27 (where Saul tears it) and in David's later stealth-cutting of Saul's robe (24:4–5). The robe is the dynastic mantle, and Jonathan transfers it to David here.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory**: **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 6.193–217) emphasizes the political dimensions, treating Jonathan as a noble rival who recognizes David's kingship and yields gracefully. Josephus is uncomfortable with the strong "love" language and softens it toward "esteemed" or "admired." The rabbinic tradition takes a more nuanced position. **Pirke Avot 5:16** preserves the rabbinic reading that the love between Jonathan and David is exemplary because it was *ahavah she-einah teluyah be-davar*—"love that does not depend on a thing"—as opposed to the love of Amnon for Tamar (2 Sam 13), which was eroticized and conditional and therefore extinguished. The rabbinic distinction is precise: Jonathan's love is exemplary covenant-loyalty, not erotic attachment. **Rashi** glosses v. 1 by emphasizing the parity of souls: Jonathan recognized in David one whose merits matched his own. **Radak** treats the covenant as primarily political: Jonathan was acting prophetically, recognizing that David was the true future king. **Ralbag**, philosophically inclined, reads the love as Aristotelian *philia* between virtuous men—"perfect friendship" in the sense of *Nicomachean Ethics* book 8. **Abarbanel** treats the chapter as a study in Saul's deteriorating political judgment: a competent king would have either embraced David fully or eliminated him cleanly; Saul does neither, and the wavering paralysis between embrace and execution is the political pathology that destroys him. Abarbanel reads Saul's spear-throwing as moments of true clarity in the king's pathology—when the harmful spirit grips him, he sees the political situation correctly (David is his rival) and acts on it; in his "calmer" moments he is mired in equivocation. **Zornberg** reads the chapter through the lens of Saul's narcissistic injury at the women's song. **Zakovitch** maps the narrative doublings. **Garsiel** catalogues the verbal echoes.

**Christian interpretive trajectory**: The patristic tradition tends to allegorize. **Origen**, in his *Homilies*, reads Jonathan as a figure of John the Baptist who recognizes the Christ and yields his preeminence. **Augustine**, in *Sermons* 8 on the friendship, treats Jonathan-David as the type of perfect Christian friendship subordinated to a higher loyalty. **Ambrose**, in *De Officiis* book 3, develops Jonathan as the model friend whose loyalty to friendship contends with loyalty to father, and whose courage in defending his friend before his father is a model for Christian courage. **Aelred of Rievaulx**, in his twelfth-century *Spiritual Friendship* (*De spiritali amicitia*), takes Jonathan and David as the paradigm of *amicitia spiritualis*, friendship that participates in divine love. Aelred's reading is the high-medieval Christian tradition's most influential treatment of biblical friendship. **Aquinas** in the *Summa* treats Jonathan-David as an instance of *amicitia honesti*, friendship grounded in the good. **Calvin**, in his *Sermons*, returns to political reading: Jonathan's covenant with David demonstrates that the providential transfer of kingship is being recognized and ratified at the human level, even within Saul's own house.

**Reformation through Modern Critical**: Calvin reads Saul's progressive descent with theological precision—the harmful spirit's recurrence (v. 10) corresponds to Saul's progressive moral collapse, and Calvin warns against treating Saul as merely afflicted: the affliction is the divine ratification of an interior choice. Wellhausen identified the chapter's source layers, attributing the Jonathan covenant material to a different stratum than the spear-throwing material. McCarter (AB) provides the standard parallel-column comparison of MT and LXX. Halpern in *David's Secret Demons* reads the entire chapter as apologetic construction: every detail is engineered to demonstrate that David's rise was the consequence of love (Jonathan's, the people's, Michal's, the LORD's) rather than ambition.

**Contemporary**: A major contemporary debate concerns the **Jonathan-David relationship**. The dominant position is the **covenantal-political reading**, championed by **J. A. Thompson** ("The Significance of the Verb Love in the David-Jonathan Narratives," *VT* 1974), **Saul Olyan**, **McCarter**, **Halpern**, and **Alter**. On this reading, the *ahav* language is treaty terminology with deep ANE precedent (William Moran's foundational 1963 article on the love of God in Deuteronomy showed *ahav* as a treaty-loyalty term), the stripping (v. 4) is a royal-investiture symbol, and the covenant is a political alliance with succession implications. **Susan Ackerman**, in *When Heroes Love* (2005), reviews the same evidence — the "love" language, the stripping, the covenant-binding, the kissing in chapter 20, and David's lament in 2 Samuel 1:26 ("your love to me was extraordinary, surpassing the love of women") — and argues that the affective and the covenantal registers were not, in the ancient world, as separable as modern categories assume; the political reading and the affective surface need not be played off against each other. **Brueggemann** (Interpretation) reads the chapter as the working-out of the consequences of David's anointing in chapter 16—the chosen one accumulates love and provokes rejection in equal measure. **Polzin** reads the women's song as the people's spontaneous theological insight (they correctly perceive the providential transfer) which Saul cannot accept. **Eugene Peterson** treats the chapter pastorally as a meditation on how being loved exposes us. **Davis** offers expository reading. Several commentators (Alter, Brueggemann) note the silencing of Michal's voice—Michal loves David, but David's response goes unrecorded—and the troubling way the marriage politics treats women as instruments: Michal as bride-price currency, Merab handed off to Adriel, the women of Israel a chorus heard for one verse and never again. The same line of observation reads Michal's love here as the inception of a tragic arc that will be completed in 2 Samuel 6 when David humiliates her.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**First tension: What is the nature of the Jonathan-David relationship?** The text's language is deliberately overdetermined.

*Position 1 (covenantal-political, McCarter, Halpern, Olyan):* The vocabulary of the chapter is treaty-loyalty vocabulary, attested across the ANE political world (Moran on *ahav* as a covenant term). Jonathan's stripping is royal investiture; the covenant is a political alliance with succession implications. To read modern sexual-identity categories into the chapter is anachronistic; the registers in play are alliance and dynastic transfer.

*Position 2 (the affective surface, Ackerman):* The accumulation of "love," "soul knit to soul," stripping, covenant, kissing, and "surpassing the love of women" presses harder than a purely instrumental political reading allows. The narrative does not announce the relationship's affective intensity in modern terms, but the surface of the text does not resist it either; reading it away requires suppressing what is on the page.

*Position 3 (synthetic, Ackerman):* The covenantal and the affective registers are not mutually exclusive. Elite-male friendship in the ANE could carry affective, embodied, and contractual elements simultaneously; the modern habit of separating these is a recent cultural product. The text is doing covenantal-political work, and the affective register is genuinely present, and neither reading exhausts the other.

Steelman of 3: The strict either/or framing reflects the categorial assumptions of modern Western readers, for whom affection and covenant loyalty must be sorted into separate boxes. The text's authorial culture made no such separation. The strongest reading treats the layered surface as itself the point.

*Where this leaves us:* All three positions illuminate; no single reading is plainly correct. Position 1 is the most rigorous about ANE backgrounds; Position 2 is the most honest about the text's surface; Position 3 is the most generous to the text's complexity. A reader cannot make this disappear without either suppressing evidence (the affective surface) or imposing categories (the modern affective/platonic binary) the text does not honor.

**Second tension: Saul's responsibility for his trajectory.** The chapter shows Saul throwing spears under the influence of the harmful spirit (v. 10), and yet planning the foreskin-bride-price as a calculated trap (v. 25). Is Saul's antagonism the product of pathology or of agency?

*Position 1 (medical-pathological, modernist):* Saul's spear-throwing reflects an actual psychiatric condition (variously diagnosed as bipolar disorder, post-traumatic stress, or schizophrenia by modern commentators); his moments of malicious planning are the product of personality changes secondary to the disease process. The chapter shows mental illness, and Saul is more victim than agent.

*Position 2 (compatibilist-theological, classical):* Saul is genuinely responsible for the calculations he performs (the bride-price plot, the use of Merab as a ploy) and yet the harmful spirit is real and divinely commissioned. The spear-throwing and the plotting are not at different ontological levels—both are Saul's, the second sober and the first not, but both expressing the pathology of his interior rejection of YHWH.

*Position 3 (tragic, David Gunn):* The chapter is the unfolding of Saul's tragedy, in which divine and human action are fused. Saul cannot be cleanly absolved (the plotting is real) and cannot be cleanly condemned (the affliction is real); his trajectory is the proper subject of tragic, not moralistic, evaluation.

Steelman of 3: The classical Christian framework wants moral coherence (Saul is responsible) and the modernist framework wants pathological coherence (Saul is afflicted), but the chapter sustains both without resolution. The text's resistance to both flattenings is the specifically tragic feature.

*Where this leaves us:* The pastoral implication is that Saul's case stands as the canonical witness against both reductive moralism (Saul's failure was simple sin) and reductive pathologization (Saul was simply ill). The integrity of the tragic reading is theologically generative: it makes space for the realities of psychic affliction within a framework of moral seriousness.

*Unresolved question:* The narrator never tells us what David feels for Jonathan. Jonathan's love is named four times; David's response is silence. Is David's interior affection real but unspoken (the convention of biblical reticence), or is David politically receiving a love he does not return, or is the narrator deliberately holding open David's interior to keep us reading? The chapter refuses to disclose.

## The Thread

The Jonathan-David covenant of 18:3 is referenced again at 19:1 (Jonathan's intercession), 20:8, 16, 42 (the field covenant), 23:18 (the Horesh meeting), and is the foundation for David's later kindness to Mephibosheth in 2 Samuel 9 (where David explicitly cites the covenant). The women's song of 18:7 will be quoted back to David by the Philistine Achish in 21:11 and by the Philistine commanders in 29:5—the song circulates outside Israel as the marker of David's reputation. The "Saul's spear" object will recur as a continuous narrative symbol through chapters 19 (Naioth), 20 (the new moon feast), 22 (Saul holds his spear under the tamarisk), 26 (David steals it), and ultimately 2 Samuel 1 (the spear leans on Saul's body at his death). The "two hundred foreskins" episode will be remembered in 2 Samuel 3:14 when David demands Michal back from Paltiel ("whom I betrothed to myself for a hundred foreskins of the Philistines"). Cross-references: Genesis 24 (the bride from kin, the structurally prior marriage type, against which Saul's bride-as-snare is the inversion); Judges 14 (Samson's Philistine marriage and its political traps, the prior antitype); 2 Samuel 1:26 (David's lament for Jonathan, "your love to me was extraordinary"); Song of Songs 8:6 ("love is strong as death," the canonical commentary on covenantal love-language). Flag forward: chapter 19's escalation to direct kill-orders, where Jonathan's mediation will succeed temporarily, and Michal's covenant-breaking ruse with the teraphim will introduce a new Saulide kin-against-Saul pattern.
