# Job 25: The Shrinking Speech

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Bildad the Shuhite rises for the third time, and what comes out of his mouth is six verses. The first cycle gave him twenty-two verses (chapter 8); the second gave him twenty-one (chapter 18); the third gives him six. The silence surrounding these six verses — Zophar has no third speech at all, and fragments that sound like friends' speeches appear scattered across chapters 26-27 in what the MT attributes to Job — is itself a historical datum. Something happened to the text of Job in its transmission, or something happened to the friends in the poet's imagination, or both.

Shuah was a settlement in the middle Euphrates region, attested in the Mari archives (early second millennium BCE) as the land of the *Šuḫum*, a pastoralist district between Mari and Babylon. The patriarchal genealogies place Shuah as a son of Abraham and Keturah (Genesis 25:2), which connects Bildad to the eastern desert cousinship of Israel. Like Eliphaz of Teman and Zophar of Na'amah, Bildad carries a setting in the northern-Arabian and Mesopotamian frontier — the recognized home of ancient Near Eastern wisdom. The friends' speeches collectively index a pan-ANE wisdom tradition whose ethical vocabulary appears in the *Instructions of Shuruppak* (Sumerian, c. 2500 BCE), *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi*, the *Babylonian Theodicy*, and Egyptian wisdom texts.

The authorship and dating of Job remain contested. Marvin Pope (Anchor Bible), David Clines (Word Biblical Commentary), and Carol Newsom (New Interpreter's Bible) argue for post-exilic composition on the basis of Aramaisms, the figure of *ha-satan*, and theological trajectory. Robert Gordis and Francis Andersen argue for pre-exilic dating, citing archaic phonology and the patriarchal setting. Ezekiel 14:14 treats Job as legendary by the sixth century BCE, so the underlying tradition is ancient even if the poetic dialogue is late. For chapter 25 specifically, the dating question intersects with the textual question in a crucial way. The brevity of Bildad's speech, combined with the apparent Joban-voiced material in 26-27 that sounds more like friends' speeches, has led Pope, Clines, Habel, and many others to argue the third cycle was textually disturbed in transmission. The LXX of Job is approximately four hundred lines shorter than the Masoretic Text, and while the LXX does preserve chapter 25, it does so with minor variations that suggest the Greek translator was already working from a Hebrew *Vorlage* in which the third cycle was disordered.

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve 11QTargum Job, an Aramaic translation from Qumran whose column layout and content suggest the third cycle was present in some form at Qumran by the late Herodian period. The fragmentary state of 11QtgJob precisely at this portion of the text prevents definitive conclusions, but what survives is consistent with the MT's ordering.

Five Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. הַמְשֵׁל (*hamšēl*, "dominion, rule," הַמְשֵׁל) in 25:2 asserts divine sovereignty. פַּחַד (*paḥad*, "fear, dread," פַּחַד) in the same verse names the reciprocal creaturely response. רִמָּה (*rimmāh*, "maggot," רִמָּה) and תּוֹלֵעָה (*tôlēʿāh*, "worm," תּוֹלֵעָה) in 25:6 are the two terms for corpse-consumers; *tôlēʿāh* is also the crimson-dye insect and thus ironically a source of royal color — a pun Isaiah 1:18 and Psalm 22:6 both exploit. צָדַק (*ṣādaq*, "to be righteous, to be in the right," צָדַק) in 25:4 governs the chapter's central question: how can humanity stand righteous before God?

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 25 is notoriously short. Six verses. The six verses themselves are well-formed Hebrew poetry — tight bicola, synonymous parallelism, the classic Joban elevation of diction. Verses 2-3 assert divine dominion in a cosmological register: rule, fear, heavenly peace, innumerable armies, universal light. Verse 4 pivots to the anthropological question: how can the human be righteous or pure before such a God? Verses 5-6 answer with a descending triad: the moon is not bright enough, the stars not pure enough, and the human is less than a maggot, less than a worm. The rhetorical structure is *qal wāḥômer* (argument from lesser to greater): if the celestial lights are not pure, how much less the human.

The poetic architecture is polished. Pope and Clines both note that these six verses are among the tightest Hebrew poetry in Job — too tight, in fact, to be dismissed as fragmentary. The theological weight is disproportionate to the length. The chapter compresses into half a dozen lines the friends' accumulated doctrine of radical creaturely insufficiency before the holiness of God.

But the brevity is nonetheless the central literary fact of the chapter. The first cycle gives each friend a speech of comparable length. The second cycle maintains the pattern though with rising intensity. The third cycle collapses: Eliphaz in 22 delivers his most severe speech; Job responds in 23-24 at length; Bildad in 25 delivers six verses; Zophar never speaks at all. What happens instead is that chapters 26 and 27 are attributed by the MT to Job, but verses within them sound orthodox in the friends' register. Job 26:5-14 is a hymn on divine majesty that could fit perfectly in a friend's mouth. Job 27:13-23 catalogs the fate of the wicked in a way that sounds more like the retribution theology Job has been rejecting than Job's own position. Marvin Pope's Anchor Bible commentary reassigns these verses: Pope gives 26:5-14 to Bildad (extending his short speech) and 27:13-23 to Zophar (giving him a third speech). David Clines in WBC offers a detailed reconstruction along similar lines though with different line divisions. Norman Habel in OTL argues for a more modest reordering. Newsom in NIB declines to emend, arguing the disturbance itself is meaningful.

Two major theories of the textual disturbance compete. The corruption theory (Pope, Clines, much of the critical tradition) holds that the third cycle suffered scribal damage or reorganization in transmission; the received text is the result of later attempts to patch the damage by assigning orphaned speeches to Job. The redactional theory, held by Andersen, Hartley, and many conservative readers, maintains that the disintegration is deliberate — the poet is signaling that the friends have run out of arguments, that orthodoxy has exhausted its resources, and that Job now speaks into a silence the friends can no longer fill. A third mediating position, offered by Newsom, treats the textual state as both disturbed and meaningful: whatever happened to the text, the current shape of the third cycle performs a rhetorical disintegration that must be read as the book's theological move regardless of whether it was originally designed.

The legal and cosmological vocabulary of Bildad's six verses deserves notice. *Hamšēl* and *paḥad* belong to royal-theological language — the God who rules and is feared. The "armies" (*gĕdûdāyw*, 25:3) are the heavenly host, a divine-council image recurrent in Ugaritic and Canaanite literature. The light that rises on all belongs to solar theology, adapted from Egyptian and Mesopotamian cognates. The moon-and-stars imagery of 25:5 alludes to the created lights of Genesis 1:14-18 and the divine council of Psalm 89:5-7 and Psalm 148. The maggot-and-worm imagery of 25:6 resonates with Psalm 22:6 ("I am a worm and not a man") and Isaiah 41:14 ("Fear not, you worm Jacob") and Isaiah 66:24 ("their worm shall not die"). The Hebrew of 25:6 is startlingly harsh. Where Psalm 22:6 uses *tôlaʿat* (worm) in a speaker's self-abasement, Bildad uses both *rimmāh* and *tôlēʿāh* in a categorical anthropological claim about *'ĕnôš* (mortal humanity) and *ben-'ādām* (son of man). This is not self-humiliation; this is diagnostic contempt.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Jewish trajectory of reading chapter 25 has always noticed the brevity. The Talmud in *Bava Batra* 15-16 does not dwell on Bildad's third speech, but the medieval commentators do. Rashi reads the shortness as signifying that Bildad has run out of arguments. Ibn Ezra flags the textual oddity and cross-references the fragments of orthodox-sounding speech in chapter 26. Maimonides in *Guide of the Perplexed* III.22-23 reads Bildad as representing the Mu'tazilite position on providence; his third speech shrinks because the position has been philosophically cornered by Job's catalog in chapter 24. Nachmanides reads the chapter mystically, treating the maggot-and-worm imagery as pointing to the soul's ascent through bodily dissolution. Martin Buber in *The Prophetic Faith* treats the brevity as the death-rattle of the friend-theology. Jon Levenson reads the chapter as evidence that Israelite religion acknowledges the disintegration of its theological frameworks under pressure. James Kugel emphasizes how each generation of Jewish readers has noticed and interpreted the brevity differently. Avivah Zornberg's literary-psychoanalytic reading attends to the corpse-imagery of 25:6: Bildad has reduced the human to what consumes him, a defensive contempt that masks the friends' own incipient collapse.

The Christian trajectory is equally developed. Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job* devotes careful attention to chapter 25, reading the six verses as a statement of creaturely contingency that is true in itself but deployed in the wrong context. Gregory's threefold reading — literal, allegorical, moral — treats 25:4 as a true statement of human sinfulness (literal), a prefiguration of the universal need for Christ's atonement (allegorical), and a warning against spiritual pride (moral). Origen reads the chapter eschatologically. Chrysostom emphasizes the chapter's critique of human arrogance while noting Bildad's misapplication. Augustine reads 25:5-6 as material for his doctrine of original sin; the Pelagian controversy would later turn in part on how texts like this were to be understood. Thomas Aquinas in *Expositio super Iob* reads the chapter as true theology misapplied — the creaturely-Creator distinction Bildad asserts is correct but cannot be used to refute the specific complaint Job has raised. John Calvin in his 1554-55 Job sermons preaches chapter 25 with characteristic double move: he affirms the theology and rejects the application. Luther reads the chapter as a useful statement of the first-use of the law (revealing sin) but misused by Bildad as a closing argument. Critical scholarship — Wellhausen, Gunkel, von Rad — reads the chapter as the textual and theological disintegration of the friends' discourse. Clines's WBC offers the standard critical reconstruction of the third cycle. Newsom's *Book of Job: A Contest of Moral Imaginations* reads the chapter as a performative collapse: Bildad's speech shrinks because orthodoxy has run out of moves. Ellen Davis treats the chapter as a cautionary text for preachers, noting that Bildad's contempt for human dignity in 25:6 is what the God of Job 42 will ultimately rebuke. Susannah Ticciati reads the chapter as the climax of the disruption of the friends' identity-forming discourse. Norman Habel and John Hartley defend more modest reconstructions. Francis Andersen reads the received text straight and treats the brevity as meaningful in itself.

The convergences across traditions are striking. Nearly every reading — Jewish, Catholic, Protestant, Orthodox, critical — recognizes that the chapter's theology is correct in the abstract and wrong in the application. The divergences cluster around the textual question: whether to emend or to preserve. Jewish interpretive traditions have historically been more willing to let the received text stand and interpret its oddities theologically. Christian critical scholarship has been more willing to emend. Both find in chapter 25 a warning about how correct theology functions when wielded as weapon.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

### Tension 1: Is the doctrine of radical creaturely insufficiency true, and if true, does it close any particular argument?

Bildad asserts that no human can be righteous before God, that even the moon and stars are not pure in God's eyes, and that the human is a maggot and a worm. The doctrine he asserts is not obviously false. The question is what work it does in the argument.

- **Catholic (Aquinas, Augustine, Trent)**: The doctrine is true regarding justification apart from grace. Apart from Christ no creature can stand righteous before God. Within grace the situation changes. Bildad's theology is valid as natural-theology diagnosis and insufficient as pastoral response. Trent's *Decree on Justification* depends on something like Bildad's premise, but carefully distinguishes original from actual righteousness.
- **Reformed (Calvin, Westminster)**: Total depravity. The doctrine is flatly true. Human righteousness before God apart from imputed righteousness in Christ is impossible. Calvin in his Job sermons preaches 25:4 as a core text for justification by faith alone. But Calvin also insists Bildad has misused the doctrine by failing to apply it to himself. Total depravity includes the friends' smugness.
- **Lutheran (Luther, Book of Concord)**: The doctrine is the first-use of the law. It is designed to drive the hearer to Christ. Bildad uses it instead as a club against Job, which is a perversion of its purpose.
- **Orthodox (Lossky, Maximus)**: Creaturely insufficiency is real but framed not as forensic inadequacy but as ontological distance from the divine essence. Theosis bridges the gap. Bildad's forensic framing misses the point.
- **Wesleyan (Wesley, later holiness tradition)**: The doctrine states a true starting point but not a permanent condition. Prevenient grace makes response possible, and sanctification enables real creaturely righteousness.
- **Open Theist (Pinnock, Boyd)**: The doctrine in its Bildadic form is exaggerated. Humans created in God's image are not essentially maggots and worms. Bildad has over-read the gap between Creator and creature.
- **Jewish (Maimonides, Soloveitchik, Heschel)**: The doctrine of creaturely contingency is true, but the Jewish tradition generally rejects the maggot-worm anthropology as un-biblical. Image-of-God theology holds. Heschel's divine pathos requires a human dignity Bildad denies.

The least-intuitive position to steelman is Luther's — that Bildad's theology is correct in itself but functioning as destructive law in the absence of gospel. Luther's claim that the law without grace kills is the exact interpretive key for chapter 25. Bildad is not wrong about human insufficiency; he is wrong that this insufficiency is the closing argument. Luther would say: the closing argument is Christ. But within the book of Job, no such closing argument has yet appeared, which is why Bildad's six verses land as cruelty — they are law without gospel, diagnosis without cure.

### Tension 2: What does the textual disturbance of the third cycle theologically mean?

The brevity of chapter 25, the absence of a third Zophar speech, and the apparently misplaced orthodox-sounding material in chapters 26-27 constitute a textual problem that cannot be separated from the theological message of the book.

- **Corruption (Pope, Clines, much of critical scholarship)**: The text was damaged in transmission. Modern editions should reconstruct what was likely original. The theological meaning resides in the reconstructed text, not the disturbed one.
- **Redactional-intentional (Andersen, Hartley, some conservative readers)**: The disturbance is the poet's design. The friends are running out of arguments and the text reflects this. The theological meaning resides in the disturbance itself.
- **Received-text-canonical (Childs, canonical criticism)**: The church and synagogue received the text as we have it, and the theological meaning resides in the canonical form whatever its origins. The disturbance must be read theologically.
- **Both/and (Newsom)**: The text is probably disturbed in transmission, and the disturbance has become theologically meaningful. The two truths coexist.

The least-intuitive position to steelman is the conservative redactional-intentional reading — that the poet deliberately designed the third cycle to disintegrate. Its steelman strength is that the collapse of the friends' discourse is precisely what the book needs at this point. The friends' theology has been refuted by the catalog of chapter 24; they now can only repeat diminishing pieties. For the poet to give Bildad six verses of cosmic contempt and then silence is rhetorically brilliant regardless of whether the text was originally longer. The received shape of the third cycle performs the exhaustion of orthodoxy whether or not it was designed to.

Where this leaves us: chapter 25 is the shrinking speech that leaves a silence. Bildad cannot sustain his discourse in the face of what Job has said. He reaches for the highest possible theology — dominion, fear, heavenly peace, armies of light, human as worm — and it takes him six verses. Then nothing. The silence that follows chapter 25 is the silence of a theology that has run out of answers. Whatever the textual history, the canonical effect is this: the friends have been given their full opportunity and have used it up. What follows must come from somewhere else.

One unresolved question: if the six verses of Bildad's final speech are theologically correct but pastorally lethal, and if the book's silence after 25:6 is the silence of orthodoxy exhausted, what kind of speech is called for in the space the friends have vacated? The divine speeches of chapters 38-41 will fill that space, but they will not sound like anything the friends have said. What sort of theology begins only after orthodoxy has finished?

## The Thread

- **Psalm 8:3-4** — "When I look at your heavens, the work of your fingers, the moon and the stars... what is man that you are mindful of him?" The same cosmological ratio Bildad invokes, but resolved in the opposite direction. Psalm 8 concludes with human dignity; Bildad concludes with human contempt.
- **Isaiah 40:6-8** — "All flesh is grass... the grass withers, the flower fades." Second Isaiah uses similar creaturely-contingency imagery, but as prelude to consolation ("but the word of our God will stand forever"), not as closing indictment.
- **Romans 3:9-20** — Paul's catena of Old Testament texts on universal sin culminates Bildad-like in "there is none righteous, no not one." But Paul then pivots to 3:21-26 and the righteousness of God revealed through faith in Christ. The move Bildad could not make, Paul makes.
- **Philippians 2:6-11** — The Christ who "did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant." The Incarnation is the answer to Bildad's question of 25:4 — how can man be in the right before God — which proceeds not by humans ascending but by God descending to become the maggot-man Bildad despised.
