# Esther 2: Hadassah, the Concealed Myrtle

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 2 unfolds across roughly four years of Xerxes' reign — from Vashti's deposition in his third year (1:3, 483/482 BC) to Esther's coronation "in the tenth month, which is the month of Tebeth, in the seventh year of his reign" (2:16; January 478 BC, by the standard Babylonian-Persian calendar synchronization in Parker and Dubberstein's *Babylonian Chronology*). The four-year gap is precisely the window of Xerxes' Greek campaign — the spring 480 BC mobilization, the Hellespont crossing, Thermopylae and Artemisium in August, the burning of Athens, the catastrophe at Salamis (September 480), the retreat to Sardis, and the final defeat at Plataea and Mycale (August 479). Herodotus 9.108–113 places the king's return to Susa in 479/478 with the lurid story of Xerxes' adultery with his brother Masistes' wife and then their daughter Artaynte, climaxing in Queen Amestris's mutilation of Masistes's wife — a parallel-universe court story that Carey A. Moore (Anchor) and Adele Berlin (JPS) treat as the historical "noise" against which the Esther story is composed. The text's "after these things" (*aḥar ha-devarim ha-elleh*, 2:1) is doing real chronological work: it papers over the Greek war, much as Herodotus's account papers over the harem politics.

Susa under Xerxes — by then the empire's primary winter capital — contained the **Apadana** finished by Darius and Xerxes, the **palace of Darius**, and a separate **harem complex** archaeologically attested in Roland de Mecquenem's 1908–46 French excavations (the *résidence des dames*) on the southwest sector of the tell. The harem (Old Persian *xshapā*-, Akkadian *bīt sinnišāti* "house of women") was administered by eunuchs; Hegai (2:3, 8, 15) and Shaashgaz (2:14) bear plausibly Persian or Iranian names, though the etymology of "Hegai" is debated — Bush (WBC) connects it tentatively to Old Persian *hagaya*. The institution is well-documented: Herodotus 3.69 names Persian royal eunuchs; Ctesias (*Persica* fragments preserved in Photius) gives lurid harem detail; the **Persepolis Fortification Tablets** (PFT, ca. 509–494 BC, published primarily by Richard T. Hallock in 1969) record rations issued to royal women, named eunuchs, and harem personnel, confirming the administrative apparatus the chapter assumes.

The most-discussed archaeological datum for chapter 2 is the **PFT 81 / "Marduka" attestation**. Several Persepolis tablets name a court official *Marduka* (theophoric from Marduk) active under Darius and early Xerxes. A. Ungnad (1940–41) and Edwin Yamauchi (*Persia and the Bible*, 1990) argued for identification with the biblical Mordecai; Levenson (OTL) is openly skeptical, noting *Marduka* is a common Persian-period name. The tablets do confirm that Jews bearing Babylonian names served in the Persian administration.

The deportation note in 2:6 is famously difficult: Mordecai is said to have been "carried away from Jerusalem among the captives carried away with Jeconiah" (Jehoiachin, 597 BC). If literally so, Mordecai would be roughly 120 years old at chapter 2's events — impossible. The standard solutions: (a) the antecedent of "who had been carried away" is **Kish** (Mordecai's great-grandfather) rather than Mordecai (Berlin, Bush, Levenson); (b) the verse is a deliberate genealogical compression, signaling exilic *identity* rather than personal biography (Fox); (c) the Hebrew is loose and the chronology is novelistic rather than reportorial (Moore). Almost no contemporary commentator takes the verse to mean Mordecai personally was deported in 597.

Esther's age and the **12-month beauty regimen** of 2:12 (six months oil of myrrh, six months spices and cosmetics) are also literary excess. The Hebrew *tamruq* (תַּמְרוּק) — "beauty treatment, scrubbing" — appears five times in Esther and only here in the Hebrew Bible, a hapax-cluster signaling a specialized vocabulary borrowed from Persian harem terminology. Herodotus 3.69 references royal-women cosmetics; Strabo *Geography* 11.13.11 describes Median beauty practice; the actual cosmetics — myrrh imported from south Arabia, frankincense from Hadhramaut — are attested in Achaemenid trade records.

**Authorship and dating** continue from chapter 1: anonymous, late-Persian to early-Hellenistic (4th–3rd c. BC), with the strongest case for the early Hellenistic period made by Bickerman (*Four Strange Books of the Bible*, 1967). The textual situation is complicated. The MT, the LXX (with Additions A–F), and the **Alpha-text** (AT, preserved in Göttingen MSS 19, 93, 108, 319) diverge throughout chapter 2 in ways that matter: the AT compresses 2:1–18 into a much shorter coronation account and lacks the elaborate twelve-month preparation; this has led Charles Dorothy and Karen Jobes (whose 1996 dissertation revolutionized AT studies) to argue that AT preserves an independent Hebrew *Vorlage*. Josephus (*Antiquities* 11.198–208) substantially expands the chapter, naming Vashti's replacement as a process and giving Esther a more conspicuous piety.

**Key terms.** *Hadassah* (הֲדַסָּה, "myrtle"), 2:7 — Esther's Hebrew name, from *hadas*, the myrtle (Isa 41:19, 55:13; Zech 1:8); the Persian name *Esther* may derive from Old Persian *stara* "star" or, more provocatively, from the Babylonian goddess *Ishtar* (the dual-name diaspora trope: Daniel/Belteshazzar, Hananiah/Shadrach, Joseph/Zaphenath-paneah). *Tamruq* (תַּמְרוּק, "beauty treatment"). *Bet ha-nashim* (בֵּית הַנָּשִׁים, "house of women," 2:3) — the harem proper. *Sha'ar ha-melekh* (שַׁעַר הַמֶּלֶךְ, "the king's gate," 2:19, 21) — both an architectural feature (the great gate of the Apadana, excavated and partially reconstructed) and an administrative term denoting a posting, attested in Persepolis tablets as a place where royal officials sat in judgment.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: the **diaspora court tale** continues, with chapter 2 working as the queen-search interlude that doubles as the introduction of the protagonists. Niditch's *Underdogs and Tricksters* and Lawrence Wills's *Jewish Novel* both place this material in the broader Hellenistic-Jewish prose-fiction tradition. The carnivalesque register that governs the book is here softened — chapter 2 is not as overtly farcical as chapter 1 — but the comic excess of the twelve-month beauty regimen, the imperial talent search for one woman, and the king's "great feast for Esther" (2:18) belong to the same tonal register. Levenson calls 2:1–4 the "Cinderella" frame: a kingdom-wide search for the woman whose body fits the throne.

In the **chiastic structure** of the book, chapter 2 sits one ring inside chapter 1, paired with chapter 9: the elevation of Esther to queen (2:17) is structurally answered by the elevation of the Jews over their enemies (9:1ff.); the institution of "Esther's feast" (2:18) is answered by the institution of Purim (9:17–22); the seven young women given to Esther (2:9) match the seven days of the Purim celebration. Levenson and Radday both press the parallel.

The chapter introduces the book's two protagonists with deliberate economy. **Mordecai's genealogy** (2:5) is one of the most loaded sentences in the book: "a Jew in Susa the citadel whose name was Mordecai, the son of Jair, son of Shimei, son of Kish, a Benjaminite." Every name signals. *Kish* is Saul's father (1 Sam 9:1). *Shimei* is the Benjaminite who cursed David (2 Sam 16:5–13) — Yair Zakovitch's intertextual readings press this hard, arguing that Mordecai is a *redeemed* Shimei. The single word "Benjaminite" sets up the Saul–Agag rematch that will activate when Haman the Agagite enters in 3:1. Berlin: "The pedigree is the plot."

**Esther's introduction** (2:7) is arranged with similar care. She is *Hadassah, hi Ester* — "Hadassah, that is Esther." The dual name marks the diaspora condition: a Hebrew name held in private, a Persian name held in public, with concealment as the structural mode. *Yefat to'ar v'tovat mar'eh* — "beautiful in form and lovely to look at" — is the same phrase used of Joseph in Genesis 39:6 (with the inverted gender), and Berg's *Motifs, Themes, and Structure* makes the case that Esther 2 is deliberately patterning Esther on Joseph. The orphan note ("she had neither father nor mother… Mordecai took her as his own daughter") is doing literary work: it removes Esther from any other claim and makes her wholly Mordecai's project, which sets up the obedience motif of 2:10, 20.

**Literary devices.** The chapter is built on **doublings**: two queens (Vashti deposed, Esther crowned), two harems (the virgins' under Hegai, the concubines' under Shaashgaz), two gatherings (2:8, 2:19), two namings (Hadassah, Esther). The verb *l-kabesh / l-kafaf* "to keep, to guard" is repeated through the harem scenes, marking custody as the chapter's mode. The leitwort *mishteh* "banquet" recurs at 2:18 — "Esther's feast" — the third great banquet of the book, structurally answering Vashti's feast in 1:9. The legal leitwort *kedat* recurs at 2:8, 12 — "according to the law / regular period."

Two pieces of structural irony deserve flagging. First, the king "loved Esther more than all the women" (2:17) but does not know she is a Jew (2:10) — the central plot device of the book is the king's ignorance of his own queen's identity, which the narrator marks at 2:10 and again at 2:20 with deliberate redundancy (Berlin: "The narrator never lets us forget"). Second, **Mordecai uncovering the eunuchs' assassination plot** (2:21–23) reads as a minor episode but is the **Chekhov's gun** of the whole book — Bigthan and Teresh are the explosive charge laid in chapter 2 that detonates in 6:1, the chiastic pivot where the king cannot sleep, has the chronicles read aloud, and discovers that Mordecai's loyalty has gone unrewarded. Levenson and Bush both flag the placement: nothing in Esther is wasted.

**The absent God** continues to govern the chapter. The narrator never says *God* gave Esther favor with Hegai (2:9, "the young woman pleased him and won his favor"); the verb is purely human. Compare the explicit divine attributions of Joseph in Egypt ("the Lord was with Joseph," Gen 39:21) or Daniel's preferences in Babylon ("God gave Daniel favor," Dan 1:9). Esther does the work that Joseph and Daniel narratives credit to God. Beal's *The Book of Hiding* reads this as deliberate theological provocation: the diaspora reader is left to decide whether human favor *is* divine favor disguised, or whether the book is staging the world without explicit providence as a thought experiment.

**Textual variants.** The LXX **Addition A** (before 1:1) gives Mordecai a prophetic dream; **Addition C** supplies Mordecai's and Esther's prayers — both normalize the chapter's theological silence. The AT compresses 2:7–18 to about a third of the MT length, suggesting a different *Vorlage*.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Chapter 2 is where the book's deepest canonical link finally surfaces: the **Saul–Agag intertext** from 1 Samuel 15. Mordecai is "the son of Kish, a Benjaminite" (2:5); Saul was "the son of Kish, a Benjaminite" (1 Sam 9:1, identical phrase). Saul was commanded to put the Agagites under *ḥerem* (1 Sam 15:3) and failed — sparing Agag and the best livestock. Samuel had to finish the killing (1 Sam 15:33). Now, four centuries later in a diaspora court, a Benjaminite kinsman of Saul will face an "Agagite" (3:1), and this time the destruction will be carried through. Levenson treats this as the structural backbone of the entire book; Yair Zakovitch and Sandra Berg both read Esther as a Saulide redemption narrative; Yoram Hazony (*God and Politics in Esther*) reads it politically as a meditation on what it means for a people to finish a job their ancestors botched. The intertext is also why Mordecai will refuse to bow to Haman in 3:2 — bowing to an Agagite would be Saul's sin compounded.

Esther's diaspora identity strategy stands in revealing contrast to Daniel's. **Daniel** (Dan 1:8) "resolved that he would not defile himself with the king's food" — open Judaism, public refusal, costly piety. **Esther** is told by Mordecai (2:10) *not* to make her people known, and she enters the harem and the king's bed without ritual protest. Both books are canonical; both characters are honored. Erich Gruen (*Diaspora: Jews amidst Greeks and Romans*) treats this as the canonical witness to a *plurality* of legitimate diaspora strategies — open and concealed — and resists the urge to harmonize. The book of Esther refuses to make Daniel's path the only path.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** The Greek Additions (LXX) supply prayers and explicit piety the MT withholds; Addition C makes Esther insist that "I have not eaten at Haman's table, nor honored the king's feast, nor drunk the wine of libations" — a Hellenistic-Jewish theological "fix" for a chapter the translator found too compromised. Josephus 11.198–208 expands the chapter, gives Esther explicit kosher diet, and makes Mordecai a more conspicuous figure. Rabbinic literature (b. Megillah 13a–b, Esther Rabbah) goes further: Esther is read as a passive victim, *kishtelin nikhneset* "she was passively brought," she did not eat treif (the rabbis invent a maidservant-cycle so Esther could keep Sabbath without revealing her identity), and most strikingly the rabbis of Megillah 13b debate whether Esther was Mordecai's wife or daughter — opting for *both* in a midrashic compression. The rabbinic project is to keep Esther observant inside the harem.

Medieval commentators (Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Ralbag, Malbim) carry the apologetic line forward, with Ibn Ezra more philological and less defensive than the others. The rabbinic effort is sustained: Esther must be made observant, because if she is not, the diaspora reading collapses.

Modern Jewish scholarship — Berg, Fox, Berlin, Levenson, Hazony, Koller, Stern, Niditch — has largely rejected the rabbinic apologetic in favor of literary-ideological reading. Aaron Koller (*Esther in Ancient Jewish Thought*): the rabbis canonized the book despite the harem problem because the rabbis themselves were a diaspora community working out concealment.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** The patristic record on chapter 2 is sparse. *1 Clement* 55 reads Esther typologically as a self-sacrificial intercessor; the Alexandrian and Antiochene traditions do not produce a sustained chapter-by-chapter commentary. Origen's bilingual edition treated MT and LXX side by side. Jerome's Vulgate appended the Additions at the end of the book.

Medieval Christian reading is dominated by **Rabanus Maurus**, *Expositio in librum Esther* (early 9th c.), the Carolingian commentary that allegorizes Esther as Mary or the Church and Mordecai as the apostles preparing her with cosmetics-as-virtues. The 12-month beauty regimen becomes the Christian's ascetic preparation; Hegai is the Holy Spirit; the harem is the Church-in-the-world. The reading is devotionally rich and historically inattentive — Rabanus has no interest in the harem as a problem.

The Reformation was less generous. **Luther** (*Table Talk* §475): "I am so great an enemy to the second book of the Maccabees, and to Esther, that I wish they had not come to us at all, for they have too many heathen unnaturalities." The "heathen unnaturalities" almost certainly include chapter 2's harem participation. **Calvin** never lectured or wrote on Esther — Greidanus and Williamson treat the silence as significant, and chapter 2 is plausibly the place where Calvin's discomfort would have peaked: a Jewish woman entering the bed of a pagan king without protest, by the design of her foster-father, is hard to fit into Reformed sanctification frameworks.

Critical scholarship from Wellhausen through **Lewis B. Paton** (ICC, 1908) read the chapter as evidence of post-exilic moral compromise; Paton explicitly calls Esther's behavior "immoral." Bickerman's 1967 essays recovered the book as Hellenistic literary art. Contemporary Christian commentary (Moore, Bush, Jobes, Duguid) varies: Jobes reads Esther's submission as providentially used; Duguid emphasizes that the text neither commends nor condemns; Greidanus (*Preaching Christ from the Book of Esther*) acknowledges the difficulty.

Recent literary criticism has read chapter 2 as the empire's machinery of display turned on a single body: Beal (*The Book of Hiding*) reads Esther's submission as both compromise and survival, and Sidnie White Crawford's textual work on the Esther recensions stresses how differently the MT, LXX, and AT frame her degree of agency in the harem scenes. The chapter is a literary-critical crux, not a devotional one.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Jewish reading has spent two millennia making Esther observant; Christian reading has spent the same time making her allegorical or skipping her; modern literary criticism has spent fifty years asking whether the harem scenes show a victim or a strategist. All three are responses to the text's deliberate moral silence. Levenson reads the silence as load-bearing: Esther is the diaspora text precisely because it refuses to resolve.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Esther's harem participation — compromise, providence, or literary indifference?** The text is deliberately silent about Esther's interior life and ritual practice during the year of preparation and the night with the king. Three positions, and a fourth:

The **rabbinic** reading (b. Megillah 13b, Esther Rabbah, Rashi): Esther was passive throughout (*kishtelin*), kept Sabbath via a maidservant rotation, ate only kosher food, and remained ritually observant. This reading preserves halakhic coherence and makes Esther a model. Steelmanned: the rabbis were themselves a diaspora community; their Esther is the working-out of how a Jew can survive in a hostile court without forfeiting identity. The maidservant-cycle is fanciful, but the underlying instinct — that survival required deeply hidden practice — is empirically the Jewish diasporic experience.

The **medieval Christian allegorical** reading (Rabanus Maurus, Glossa Ordinaria): the harem becomes the Church, the cosmetics become virtues, Esther becomes Mary or the soul. Steelmanned: medieval allegory was the technique by which the OT was read as continuous with NT theology; chapter 2 was hard to read straight, so it was read upward. Largely abandoned post-*Nostra Aetate*.

The **Reformed providential** reading (Karen Jobes, Iain Duguid): God works through Esther's compromised situation without authoring it; the text's silence is not divine indifference but readerly invitation to recognize hidden providence. Steelmanned: this is the most defensible Christian reading available, and it preserves the seriousness of the moral problem. Difficulty: it imports a theology the text itself does not articulate.

The **critical-literary** reading (Fox, Beal, Berlin, Levenson): the text is morally indifferent — neither commending nor condemning Esther's harem participation — and the silence is the book's argument. Esther is a diaspora story for a diaspora people; the question of compromise is left open because diaspora life *is* an open question. Steelmanned: this is the reading most faithful to the book's plain shape, and Levenson presses it persuasively. Difficulty: it leaves the reader without normative guidance.

*Where this leaves us:* the four readings are not equally weighted. The rabbinic and Christian-allegorical readings are working *against* the text; the Reformed and critical-literary readings are working *with* it. The text refuses to resolve, and that refusal is the diaspora witness.

**Tension 2: Concealment as identity strategy — *pikuach nefesh* or assimilation?** Mordecai commands Esther twice not to disclose her people (2:10, 20). The Talmudic principle of *pikuach nefesh* (preservation of life overrides almost all commandments) is straightforward justification; but the concealment in chapter 2 is not yet under direct threat — it is *prophylactic*, anticipating threats not yet visible. Three positions:

**Augustinian** Christian ethics (and a strand of Catholic moral theology): concealment that approaches deception is morally costly; Augustine in *De mendacio* would not approve. **Reformed** Christian ethics (Calvin's heirs, even in his absence on this book): concealment to preserve life is permissible but troubling. **Rabbinic** ethics (Megillah 13a–b): concealment under threat is not only permitted but required (*chayyav* in some readings); the Esther–Daniel pluralism is canonized.

The critical position (Erich Gruen, Levenson, Hazony): the chapter is not adjudicating concealment; it is showing how diaspora identity *works*. Concealment and disclosure exist on a continuum, and the question is when each is required. The book's structural argument — that Esther will eventually have to disclose (7:3–4) — implies that concealment is provisional, never total. *Where this leaves us:* the chapter's silence on whether Mordecai's command is virtuous, prudent, or compromised reflects the book's broader refusal to resolve diaspora ethics into a rule. The reader must decide when concealment becomes assimilation, and the book offers Esther's eventual disclosure as one path without making it the only path.

**One unresolved question.** If Esther's eventual salvific act in chapter 7 depends on years of concealment in chapter 2, and Daniel's salvific witness depends on years of *non*-concealment, what does it mean that the canon holds both as faithful — and how does that pluralism rebuke any reader who is too sure she knows which path is the faithful one for her own diaspora?

## The Thread

**1 Samuel 9:1–2; 15:1–35.** The Saulide genealogy in 2:5 (*ben Kish, ish yemini*) is verbatim the Saul-introduction of 1 Sam 9:1. The book is announcing, with one verse, that what failed under Saul will be tried again. The reader who hears this allusion knows the Agagite is coming.

**Genesis 39:6; 41:14–45.** The Joseph parallels are dense: "beautiful in form and lovely to look at" (Gen 39:6, of Joseph; Esth 2:7, of Esther) is a direct verbal echo. Both are diaspora figures who hide their identity, are given foreign names by foreign rulers, and rise to second-in-command. Berg's monograph traces the parallel through the entire chapter.

**Daniel 1:1–21.** The chapter's diaspora-identity strategy stands in canonical *contrast* to Daniel's. Daniel refuses the king's food; Esther accepts the king's food (and bed). Both are honored in the canon. The pluralism is the canon's witness, not its embarrassment.

**Esther 6:1–3 (forward link).** The Bigthan-and-Teresh episode (2:21–23) is the structural pivot's loaded gun: when the king cannot sleep on the chiastic-center night, this is the chronicles entry he hears. Levenson: nothing in Esther is wasted.
