# 2 Peter 3: The Delay and the Fire

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

2 Peter as a whole is the most contested book in the New Testament on the question of authorship, and chapter 3 contains some of the most decisive evidence on either side of that debate. The chapter opens by referring to "the second letter that I am writing to you, beloved" (3:1) — usually taken as a back-reference to 1 Peter — and closes by noting that "our beloved brother Paul also wrote to you according to the wisdom given him… as he does in all his letters when he speaks in them of these matters. There are some things in them that are hard to understand, which the ignorant and unstable twist to their own destruction, as they do the other Scriptures" (3:15-16). It is this last passage, more than perhaps any other in the New Testament outside the Gospels themselves, that has shaped modern critical judgment about the date and authorship of the document.

Most modern critical scholars — Richard Bauckham (whose *Word Biblical Commentary* on 2 Peter and Jude, 1983, remains the standard scholarly reference), Ernst Käsemann, Anton Vögtle, Jerome Neyrey, Bultmann, Peter Davids — read 2 Peter as a *pseudonymous testament*, a Jewish-Christian farewell discourse in the genre of the *Testament of Moses*, the *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs*, and the closing speeches of Moses (Deut 31-33), Joshua (Josh 23-24), David (1 Kgs 2), and Paul (Acts 20). The arguments converge in chapter 3. First, the description of "scoffers" asking "where is the promise of his coming?" because "ever since the fathers fell asleep, all things are continuing as they were from the beginning of creation" (3:3-4) presupposes a temporal horizon long enough for "the fathers" — the first generation of believers — to have died and the delay of the Lord's coming to feel theologically urgent. This is not a problem one expects in the early 60s, when the apostolic generation was still alive; it fits a setting toward the end of the first century or into the early second. Second, the reference in 3:15-16 to "all" of Paul's letters being grouped with "the other Scriptures" (*tas loipas graphas*) is the earliest evidence in Christian literature of the Pauline corpus circulating as Scripture — a development scholars like David Trobisch (*Paul's Letter Collection*, 1994) and Harry Gamble (*Books and Readers in the Early Church*, 1995) and Lee Martin McDonald locate after Peter's death and into the late first or early second century. Marcion (c. 140 AD) had the first known formal Pauline corpus; 2 Peter 3:15-16 may witness an earlier proto-canonical awareness, but the awareness itself is hard to date before the 80s. On these grounds the critical consensus places 2 Peter somewhere between the 80s and the 130s, written by an admirer in the Petrine tradition.

Evangelical defenders of Petrine authenticity — Michael Green, Tom Schreiner (*New American Commentary*, the most thorough recent defense), Doug Moo, Charles Bigg — answer point by point. The "fathers" of 3:4 may refer not to the first Christian generation but to the OT patriarchs whose hopes for the day of the Lord had already long been deferred; the parousia-delay topos is older than Christianity (cf. Hab 2:3, Sirach 35:18-19). The reference to Paul's letters need not imply a closed canon; "scriptures" (*graphai*) had a flexible early-Christian usage, and Paul's letters were already circulating by the late 60s when Peter would have written. The vocabulary differences between 1 and 2 Peter follow from a different amanuensis, since 1 Peter 5:12 itself acknowledges Silvanus's hand. Witherington's mediating position holds that a Petrine voice was authorized through pseudonymous testamentary form acceptable in Jewish-Christian milieux. The early church's hesitation — Origen knew of doubts (Eusebius, *Hist. Eccl.* 6.25.8), Eusebius classed it among the *antilegomena* (*Hist. Eccl.* 3.25), and full canonical acceptance came only with Athanasius's 39th Festal Letter (367) and the Council of Carthage (397) — is consistent with either reading.

The chapter's intellectual world is rich. The "scoffers" of 3:3 (*empaiktai*, ἐμπαῖκται) reflect a recognizable Hellenistic-philosophical objection: Stoic philosophy taught the eternal cycles of the cosmos, and Epicureanism denied divine providence altogether. Both schools could supply the objection 2 Peter answers — that "all things are continuing as they were from the beginning of creation." Cicero's *De Natura Deorum* records similar objections in Roman philosophical conversation. The writer's reply rests on Genesis: the heavens and the earth were formed by the word of God (3:5), and by that same word the ancient world was deluged (3:6), and the present heavens and earth are "stored up for fire" by the same word (3:7). The cosmology is *creatio per verbum*, and the eschatology is conflagration.

That conflagration vocabulary is itself remarkable. Verbs and adjectives in 3:7, 3:10, and 3:12 — *kausoumena* (καυσούμενα, "burning"), *lythēsetai* (λυθήσεται, "will be dissolved"), *tēkesthai* (τήκεσθαι, "to melt") — together describe a cosmic conflagration that sounds remarkably like the Stoic doctrine of *ekpyrōsis*: the periodic burning-up of the cosmos in which all things are consumed by primordial fire. The Stoics, especially Chrysippus and Posidonius, taught that the cosmos went through cycles of *ekpyrōsis* and *palingenesia* — conflagration and rebirth. 2 Peter 3 takes that vocabulary and Christianizes it: the world is destroyed once, not cyclically, and what follows is a *new* heavens and a new earth (3:13) in which righteousness dwells, drawn from Isaiah 65:17 and 66:22. The writer is in active dialogue with Hellenistic cosmology, deploying its terms in service of a biblical eschatology.

The "thousand years" of 3:8 — "with the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day" — picks up Psalm 90:4 and a much-developed Jewish tradition. *2 Baruch* 14, *4 Ezra*, *Jubilees* 4:30, and especially the rabbinic tradition (Mishnah *Sanhedrin* 97a) developed from Psalm 90:4 the schema of a "world of six thousand years": six millennia for this present age, then a sabbatical seventh of messianic rest. The same parallel runs through Christian apocalyptic in *Epistle of Barnabas* 15. 2 Peter does not quite endorse the schema, but it draws on the same temporal imagination: God's clock is not human clock, and the apparent delay can be reframed as patience.

Other key terms: *makrothumei* (μακροθυμεῖ, 3:9, "is patient, long-suffering") names the divine virtue most under pressure in the chapter; *graphas* (γραφάς, 3:16, "Scriptures") applied to Paul's letters is an extraordinary claim, especially when paired with "the other Scriptures" (*tas loipas graphas*); and the use of *parousia* (παρουσία, 3:4, 3:12 — "coming," "presence") names the Christian eschatological event with the same word that Hellenistic vocabulary used for the formal arrival of a king or god. The chapter's vocabulary, like the rest of the letter, is more elevated and rhetorical than 1 Peter's.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The whole of 2 Peter has the structure of a *testament*: announcement of imminent death (1:13-15), recall of ministry (1:16-18), warning of false teachers (chapter 2), and apocalyptic prediction (chapter 3). Chapter 3 occupies the final section conventional for the genre — the vision of the future and ethical exhortation flowing from it. The form is well attested in the Jewish farewell discourse tradition (the Testaments, Moses in Deuteronomy, Joshua, David), and chapter 3 fulfills its conventional functions: it frames the present crisis in cosmic and eschatological perspective, it predicts the consummation, and it delivers the ethical demand the speaker most wants his hearers to keep.

Within the chapter, three literary moves stand out. First, the *delay-tradition apocalyptic* move (3:3-9) — the scoffers' objection, the writer's reply by appeal to creation and flood, the temporal recalibration of Psalm 90:4, the reframing of delay as patience. The form has biblical precedents: Habakkuk 2:3 ("the vision is yet for an appointed time… if it seems slow, wait for it; it will surely come"), Sirach 35:18-19, the rabbinic discussions of the Messiah's "tarrying" (*Sanhedrin* 97b-98a). Mark 13:32-37 in the Olivet Discourse anticipates the same problem and gives the watchman parable as response. 2 Peter 3 takes its place in this older tradition.

Second, the *cosmic-conflagration* move (3:10-13) — the day of the Lord arriving "like a thief," the heavens passing with a roar, the elements melting in fire, the new heavens and new earth. The thief-image is shared with 1 Thessalonians 5:2 and Revelation 16:15 (and goes back to Jesus's own teaching in Matt 24:43, Luke 12:39). The conflagration vocabulary, as noted, takes Stoic *ekpyrōsis* and Christianizes it. The new-creation vision draws on Isaiah 65:17 and 66:22, and runs parallel to Revelation 21:1.

Third, the *Pauline-corpus reference* (3:14-16) places Paul within the writer's circle of reference. The writer calls Paul "our beloved brother," cites the wisdom Paul wrote to "you" (so the recipients of 2 Peter are also recipients of Paul's letters), groups Paul's letters with "the other Scriptures," and observes that some things in Paul are hard to understand and are twisted by the ignorant and unstable. This last is one of the earliest Christian polemics about *misreading* — a remark that itself bears on questions of canonical authority and interpretation.

The Greek of chapter 3, like the rest of the letter, is more elevated and more self-consciously rhetorical than 1 Peter. The vocabulary is dense with terms drawn from Hellenistic cosmology, philosophy, and apocalyptic.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The patristic and medieval reception of chapter 3 was complicated by the slow canonical reception of 2 Peter as a whole. Once accepted, however, certain passages of chapter 3 became theologically central. The conflagration vocabulary of 3:10-12 underwrote a strand of Christian eschatology that read the end as cosmic destruction-and-renewal. Augustine engages 3:10's "elements" (*stoicheia*) in *City of God* 20, distinguishing destruction-by-removal from destruction-by-annihilation.

N. T. Wright, in *Surprised by Hope* (2007) and in *Early Christian Letters for Everyone* (2011), has made 2 Peter 3 a load-bearing text for his sustained argument that Christian hope is not for the soul's escape to a disembodied heaven but for the renewal of the whole creation. Wright reads the "dissolution by fire" of 3:10-12 not as the annihilation of the physical universe but as a purging judgment — fire that consumes corruption and injustice while the creation itself, like gold in a furnace, comes through transformed. He notes that the best-attested reading of 3:10 ends not with the earth and its works being "burned up" but "found" or "laid bare" (the *heurethēsetai* reading favored by most modern critical editors), which fits the refining sense better than the annihilating one. The goal, on Wright's account, is exactly what verse 13 says: "new heavens and a new earth, in which righteousness dwells" — a renewed cosmos, continuous with this one, in which God's justice is finally at home. Wright connects "the day of the Lord" here to his larger picture of God's future arriving to set the world right, and he reads the chapter's call to "lives of holiness and godliness" (3:11) as the appropriate response of people who know what kind of future they are made for.

The "thousand years" of 3:8 was put to dramatic theological use. Combined with Psalm 90:4 and the rabbinic six-millennia schema, Christian writers (the *Epistle of Barnabas* 15, Lactantius's *Divine Institutes* 7, Hippolytus) developed a "world-week" theology: six thousand years of history, followed by a thousand-year sabbath. The schema fed both premillennial and amillennial readings across the centuries. Modern scholars now recognize the rabbinic parallel (Mishnah *Sanhedrin* 97a) as part of a shared Jewish-Christian apocalyptic time-imagination.

The Reformation-era debate over 2 Peter touched chapter 3 sharply. Luther was famously skeptical of 2 Peter (placing it in his sub-canonical group with Hebrews, James, Jude, and Revelation in the 1522 NT preface), but he read 3:9 — "the Lord is not slow… but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance" — with care. Calvin defended the letter and the verse, generally reading "any" and "all" as referring to "the elect" rather than to humanity at large. The Calvinist tradition (Schreiner, Carson, John Piper) has continued this restriction. The Wesleyan and Arminian tradition (Wesley's *Notes Upon the New Testament*, Roger Olson) reads the verse as it stands — God genuinely does not wish any to perish — and takes it as a key text for universal salvific will. The Catholic tradition (CCC §1037: "God predestines no one to go to hell; for this, a willful turning away from God… is necessary") sits closer to the Wesleyan reading. The contemporary universalist position, advanced by Robin Parry (writing as Gregory MacDonald, *The Evangelical Universalist*) and David Bentley Hart (*That All Shall Be Saved*), reads 3:9 as one of the cluster of New Testament texts that warrants universal final salvation: if God truly does not wish any to perish, and God's will is finally accomplished, the conclusion is hard to escape.

The most consequential modern reading of chapter 3 is Käsemann's. His 1952 essay "Eine Apologie der urchristlichen Eschatologie" called 2 Peter "the worst writing in the New Testament," judging the chapter's theology of parousia-delay to be a retreat from primitive apocalyptic urgency into "early Catholic" institutional consolation. Käsemann's verdict provoked a generation of debate. Bauckham's *WBC* (1983) is the major rehabilitation: 2 Peter as a sophisticated testament whose theology of patience is not a retreat but a development consonant with biblical precedents (Hab 2:3). Schweitzer had argued for thoroughgoing eschatology that read the parousia delay as a fundamental crisis; C. H. Dodd's realized eschatology relocated the kingdom to the present; Oscar Cullmann's "already / not yet" charted a middle course. The contemporary apocalyptic Paul school (J. C. Beker, J. Louis Martyn) has revived a more apocalyptic reading.

3:15-16 has its own long history. Trobisch's argument that the Pauline corpus existed in a recognizable form by the late first century rests in part on this verse; Gamble's work on the early Christian book trade locates the moment Paul's letters circulate as a collection; Lee Martin McDonald's work on canon-formation treats 2 Peter 3:15-16 as a critical witness. The "twisting" of Paul (*streblousin*, στρεβλοῦσιν, 3:16) is among the earliest Christian polemics about misreading.

### Jewish Scholarly Engagement

Chapter 3 is dense with Jewish apocalyptic background. Psalm 90:4 (the source of 3:8) is developed in 2 Baruch 14, 4 Ezra, and *Jubilees* 4:30; the rabbinic six-thousand-years schema in Mishnah *Sanhedrin* 97a is the most direct parallel to the Christian world-week. The parousia-delay topos parallels rabbinic discussions of the Messiah's "tarrying" (*Sanhedrin* 97b-98a). Daniel Boyarin and David Stern have argued for shared Jewish-Christian apocalyptic time-imagination — both traditions produced similar reframings: God's clock is not human clock; delay is patience; "hastening" is possible through faithfulness. The new-creation vision of 3:13 draws on Isaiah 65:17 and 66:22; Annette Yoshiko Reed's work on shared apocalyptic forms applies here.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

In chapter 3 God is patient. The chapter centers on this divine attribute (*makrothumia*) and rebuilds the entire problem of delay around it. The scoffers see the absence of the parousia as evidence that the promise is empty; the writer reframes the absence as evidence that God is mercifully extending the time of repentance. The same God who created by his word, who deluged the world by his word, will also burn the world by his word — but the present interval is not cosmic indifference but divine patience. Two tensions warrant unpacking.

### Tension 1: The parousia delay — what does the deferred coming mean for Christian theology?

Käsemann's "early Catholic" critique reads the delay-theology of 2 Peter 3 as a retreat from the apocalyptic urgency of Jesus and Paul into an institutionally consolidated, this-worldly Christianity. On this view the chapter represents a theological loss — the nervy expectation of the imminent end has been domesticated into a doctrine of patience that lets the church settle into the world.

Bauckham's defense reads the same chapter as a sophisticated theological response to a real pastoral crisis. The delay was inevitable once the apostolic generation died; the question was how to absorb it without losing eschatological orientation. 2 Peter 3 absorbs it by appealing to creation, to the flood, to Psalm 90:4, and to divine patience as a positive content rather than mere consolation.

Schweitzer's thoroughgoing eschatology reads the delay as a fundamental crisis that the New Testament writers genuinely faced and that requires modern theology to acknowledge. C. H. Dodd's realized eschatology resolves the tension by relocating the kingdom to the present — the parousia in some sense has already happened in Jesus's resurrection, and the future event is the consummation of what is already real. Oscar Cullmann's "already / not yet" — the D-Day has happened, V-Day is awaited — is the dominant evangelical compromise. The contemporary apocalyptic Paul school (Beker, Martyn) reasserts the not-yet against what they see as Dodd's flattening, insisting that the future apocalyptic event remains constitutive.

Postmillennial and premillennial readings differ on temporal frame: postmillennials see a long historical preparation culminating in the day of the Lord; premillennials read 3:10-13 as the destruction following Revelation 20's millennium.

The least intuitive position to a contemporary evangelical is Käsemann's. Steelmanned: there is something genuinely lost when the apocalyptic urgency of "the time is short" (1 Cor 7:29) becomes "with the Lord one day is as a thousand years." A church that has internalized the long view is a different church from the one that expected the Lord any day. Käsemann is not wrong that something has changed; he is contestable on whether the change is loss.

**Where this leaves us**: The chapter is not the death of apocalyptic urgency but its refraction. The promise stands; the timing has been theologized. Whether that theologization is faithful or domesticating is a question the chapter forces and does not finally settle.

### Tension 2: 3:9 — "not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance"

The universalist reading, advanced by Robin Parry and David Bentley Hart, takes the verse at face value as declaring God's salvific will to be genuinely universal — and combined with the conviction that God's will is accomplished, draws the conclusion that all will finally be saved.

The Arminian reading, advanced by Wesley and Roger Olson, takes the verse as declaring God's universal salvific will but combines it with libertarian human freedom: God genuinely wills the salvation of all, but human refusal can finally resist God's will. Hell is real and eternal because freedom is real.

The Reformed reading, advanced by Tom Schreiner and D. A. Carson, restricts the "any" and "all" of 3:9 to the elect — God is patient toward "you" (3:9, *eis hymas*) and not wishing that any [of you, the elect] should perish. The natural-language read is constrained by larger covenantal commitments about election.

The Catholic position (CCC §1037) sits closer to the Arminian: God predestines no one to hell, but a free turning away from God to the end is necessary for damnation. The Catholic tradition holds out hope — though not certainty — for universal salvation (the position associated with Hans Urs von Balthasar's *Dare We Hope?*).

The least intuitive position to most evangelicals is the universalist one. Steelmanned: the verse genuinely says God does not wish any to perish, and the New Testament cluster of related verses (1 Tim 2:4, "God desires all to be saved"; 1 Cor 15:22, "in Christ shall all be made alive"; Rom 11:32, "God has consigned all to disobedience that he may have mercy on all") strains in the same direction. If divine will is finally efficacious, the conclusion follows. The traditional readings have to bend somewhere.

**Where this leaves us**: 3:9 is a verse that every tradition reads, but they read it differently because they bend it differently. The verse will not let any tradition keep both its plain sense and its full systematic apparatus without compromise.

### Closing question

The chapter ends with a remarkable instruction — "count the patience of our Lord as salvation" (3:15). What does it mean to *count* delay as grace? The chapter does not say what we should be doing with the time except to wait, to hasten, and to grow. That triple injunction — wait, hasten, grow — is a genuine theological grammar, but its practical content is left to the reader.

## The Thread

- Psalm 90:4 — "a thousand years in your sight are but as yesterday when it is past"; the source of 3:8 and the verse on which the rabbinic six-thousand-years schema (Mishnah *Sanhedrin* 97a) and the Christian world-week (*Epistle of Barnabas* 15) both rest.
- Isaiah 65:17, 66:22 — the new-heavens-and-new-earth source for 3:13; the prophetic vision the writer's apocalyptic appropriates.
- Habakkuk 2:3 — "the vision is yet for an appointed time… wait for it; it will surely come"; the OT delay-of-prophecy precedent that legitimates the New Testament's reframing of parousia delay as faithfulness.
- Revelation 21:1 — "I saw a new heaven and a new earth, for the first heaven and the first earth had passed away"; the parallel new-creation vision, drawing on the same Isaianic source and developing the apocalyptic into liturgical climax.
