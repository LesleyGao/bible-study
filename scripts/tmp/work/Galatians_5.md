# Galatians 5: Freedom That Cannot Be Bitten and Devoured

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Galatians 5 lands in a Roman provincial world where "freedom" was a word everyone used and almost no one possessed in Paul's sense. Roman *libertas* was a civic-juridical status — the condition of one who was not a slave, who held citizenship rights, and who could appear in court without an intermediary. *Eleutheria* in Greek civic discourse named the same thing: the autonomy of the *polis* and the standing of the free male citizen. The Stoics and Cynics had already philosophically internalized the term — Epictetus, writing a generation after Paul, defined freedom as the inner sovereignty of the wise man over his own assent (*Discourses* 4.1) — and Hellenistic-Jewish writers like Philo had begun to translate the language into a soteriological key (*Quod Omnis Probus Liber Sit*, "Every Good Man Is Free"). Paul's "for freedom Christ has set us free" lands inside this thick semantic field but turns it sideways: the freedom is not earned by virtue, claimed by citizenship, or achieved by ascesis; it is given by Christ.

The chapter's polemical edge — the wish in 5:12 that the agitators would emasculate themselves — operates against a specific cultural backdrop. Pessinus in Galatia was the historic center of the cult of Cybele, the Magna Mater, whose self-castrated priests, the *galli*, performed their dedication in a violent springtime rite. Pliny, Catullus (poem 63 on Attis), and Lucian (*De Dea Syria*) all describe the practice. Roman law, while tolerating *galli* in the imperial cult, prohibited castration of citizens, and the rite carried a heavy social stigma. When Paul, writing to Galatians who lived in proximity to this cult, wishes that those preaching circumcision would go all the way and emasculate themselves, the rhetorical force is not merely vulgar; it is a calculated cultural insult, mapping the agitators onto a religious type the Galatians knew to despise.

Greco-Roman moral philosophy in the first century had developed an extensive literature of vice-and-virtue catalogs — Stoic, Cynic, Hellenistic-Jewish, Pythagorean. Epictetus's *Discourses* 2.10 lists the social passions that tear the polity apart; the Stoic Hierocles articulated concentric circles of moral concern; Wisdom of Solomon 14:25–26 catalogs idolatry's downstream vices in a list that closely parallels Paul's *erga tēs sarkos*; 4 Maccabees 1:26–27 catalogs both passions and reasoned virtues; the Qumran Community Rule (1QS 4) maps two spirits — of light and darkness — each with its own list of attendant dispositions. Paul's vice-catalog (5:19–21) and virtue-catalog (5:22–23) are recognizably within this genre, but his framing is distinctive: the virtues are *karpos*, fruit, of a single source (the Spirit), not achievements of disciplined reason; and the entire opposition is dramatized as an eschatological conflict between *sarx* and *pneuma*.

The Jewish background is equally close at hand. Leviticus 19:18 — "you shall love your neighbor as yourself" — was already in the first century recognized as a summary of Torah. Hillel's famous answer to the Gentile inquirer ("What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor; this is the whole Torah, the rest is commentary," *b. Shabbat* 31a) reflects a contemporary tendency to seek a single integrative principle. Rabbi Akiva would later call Lev 19:18 "a great principle in the Torah" (*Sifra* on Leviticus, *Genesis Rabbah* 24:7). When Paul says "the whole law is fulfilled in one word: you shall love your neighbor as yourself" (5:14), he is not inventing a Christian abridgment of Torah; he is claiming the Jesus tradition (Mark 12:28–34, Matt 22:34–40, Rom 13:8–10) within a discernibly Jewish hermeneutic move.

The textual base is again strong: P46 (Chester Beatty II, ca. AD 200), Codex Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, and Alexandrinus give the chapter in continuous form. Two textual notes matter for meaning. At 5:6, the participle *energoumenē* has been read both as middle voice ("active through love") and as passive ("activated by love"), with consequences for the Catholic-Protestant debate at Trent. At 5:21, several manuscripts add *phonoi* ("murders") to the vice list after *phthonoi* ("envies"), apparently a Western expansion harmonizing with similar lists in Romans 1.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter. *Eleutheria* (ἐλευθερία, 5:1, 5:13) is "freedom" — civic, philosophical, theological. *Sarx* (σάρξ, 5:13, 5:16, 5:17, 5:19, 5:24) is "flesh" — not the body as such but the embodied human existence considered apart from the Spirit, oriented toward self-curving. *Pneuma* (πνεῦμα, 5:16–25) is "Spirit" — both the divine Spirit who indwells and the new mode of existence the Spirit constitutes. *Karpos tou pneumatos* (καρπὸς τοῦ πνεύματος, 5:22) is "fruit of the Spirit," a horticultural image that marks the virtues as organic outgrowths of divine indwelling rather than achievements of will. *Enkrateia* (ἐγκράτεια, 5:23) is "self-control" — a Stoic and Cynic philosophical term that Paul places at the end of his list, as if to say: the virtue ancient ethics most prized as the foundation of the moral life is, for Paul, the last fruit on the branch.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

If Hans Dieter Betz's rhetorical scheme holds, Galatians 5 sits squarely in the *exhortatio* (5:1–6:10), the moral-paraenetic section that follows the *probatio* and precedes the *peroratio*. Betz argued, controversially, that the *exhortatio* is the weakest link in his rhetorical analysis because forensic rhetoric does not standardly include extended ethical exhortation. Subsequent scholars (Longenecker, Witherington) have used this very awkwardness to argue for deliberative rather than forensic classification: deliberative rhetoric, oriented toward future action, naturally houses extended moral counsel. Either way, chapter 5 is where Paul turns from "what God has done" to "how then must you walk" — though, characteristically, his "walk" remains thoroughly grounded in the prior indicative.

Diatribe runs through the chapter. Verses 7–12 deploy the rhetorical question, the apostrophe, the proverb ("a little leaven leavens the whole lump"), the irony ("you were running well"), and the brutal aside ("I wish those who unsettle you would emasculate themselves"), in a sequence that classical rhetoricians would recognize as *sermocinatio* — staged conversational engagement with imagined or real opponents. The proverb at 5:9 reappears verbatim in 1 Corinthians 5:6, suggesting Paul drew from a stock of pithy rhetorical units; the leaven imagery would have resonated with Galatian Gentiles familiar with Passover practice through synagogue contact.

The vice and virtue catalogs of 5:19–23 deserve close literary attention. The vice list is structured loosely: sexual sins (porneia, akatharsia, aselgeia), then religious-supernatural (eidōlolatria, pharmakeia), then a long sequence of social-relational vices (echthrai, eris, zēlos, thumoi, eritheiai, dichostasiai, haireseis, phthonoi), then bodily-celebratory vices (methai, kōmoi). The structure foregrounds social-relational sins — a fact that is exegetically important: the catalog targets community-destroying behaviors as much as personal moral failures. The virtue list, by contrast, is a single grammatical unit — the singular *karpos* governing nine substantives — beginning with love and ending with self-control, with joy and peace as its first triad. The asymmetry between the lists matters: vices are plural *erga*, works performed by individuals; virtues are a singular *karpos*, fruit borne by the Spirit's indwelling.

The autograph subscription convention is preserved at 6:11 but anticipated structurally here: chapter 5 reads as the rhetorical climax of the moral argument, with chapter 6 functioning as the closing exhortation and signature. The literary device of the *peroratio* — the closing summation that drives home the case — begins to bleed backward into chapter 5's ending: 5:24, "those who belong to Christ Jesus have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires," reads as a near-summative declaration that the *exhortatio* will refine in 5:25–26 and that 6:1–10 will work out in concrete community practice.

Two textual variants in the chapter affect interpretation. The 5:6 question — middle versus passive voice on *energoumenē* — has been thoroughly worked over since the Reformation. The grammar permits both, and the theological stakes are real: Catholic interpreters at Trent emphasized that faith without love is dead (citing James 2:17 alongside Gal 5:6), while Lutheran interpreters defended the middle voice and read love as the visible *expression* of justifying faith, not its activator. Modern grammarians (Burton, Longenecker, Moo) tend to favor the middle voice but acknowledge that the difference may be smaller than the Reformation polemics suggested. The 5:21 variant adding *phonoi* is generally read as a secondary harmonization.

The chapter sits at a literary hinge. Chapters 1–4 have argued that Gentile believers are fully Abraham's heirs through faith and the Spirit, without circumcision; chapter 5 turns to the question that any pagan ethicist would press hardest — if you remove the disciplinary structure of Torah, what prevents moral collapse? Paul's answer is structural: the Spirit's indwelling produces a different shape of life, and the freedom Christ gives is not freedom *from* moral seriousness but freedom *for* love. The literary architecture mirrors the theological argument. The works/fruit asymmetry, the singular love at the head of the virtue list, the climactic crucifixion of the flesh in 5:24 — all of these are crafted to refute the antinomian charge Paul knew his Gospel would invite.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic engagement with Galatians 5 is dense. Marius Victorinus read the freedom of 5:1 as freedom from the servitude of the Old Law's ceremonies; Jerome took Paul's "I wish those who unsettle you would emasculate themselves" seriously in its ferocity, unembarrassed by the polemic. Augustine's commentary and his later *De Spiritu et Littera* and *Contra Faustum* developed the flesh/Spirit dialectic into the foundation of his mature theology of grace, with 5:17 ("the desires of the flesh are against the Spirit") becoming a key text for the Augustinian doctrine of indwelling sin. Chrysostom preached the chapter pastorally to an Antiochene audience for whom the *galli* of 5:12 were obvious, and did not soften the insult.

Aquinas, in his *Lectura* on Galatians and in the *Summa Theologiae* I-II.106–108 on the New Law, integrates Galatians 5 into his account of grace and virtue: the New Law is principally the grace of the Holy Spirit given inwardly, with external precepts secondary. The fruit of the Spirit (5:22–23) becomes, in Aquinas, the visible effects of the indwelling Spirit's grace. The medieval mystical tradition (Bernard, Bonaventure, the Rhineland mystics) read 5:22–23 as the marks of a sanctified soul.

The Reformation transformed how the chapter was read. Luther's 1535 *Lectures on Galatians* treats 5:1 ("for freedom Christ has set us free") as one of the fundamental texts of the Christian liberty motif and as a hammer against papal works-righteousness. Luther's treatment of 5:6 — "faith working through love" — was tactically delicate. He insisted that faith alone justifies but argued that genuine faith always works through love; the works are evidence, not cause. Calvin's commentary (1548) read the same verse with similar care: love is the inseparable companion of justifying faith, not its source. The Council of Trent (1547, Decree on Justification, sixth session) cited Galatians 5:6 explicitly: faith without love is "imperfect" and does not justify, and the Catholic position became that justifying faith is intrinsically a faith formed by love (*fides caritate formata*). The 1999 Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification between the Lutheran World Federation and the Roman Catholic Church found a "differentiated consensus" that significantly narrowed but did not eliminate the gap.

The Anabaptist tradition (Menno Simons, the Schleitheim Confession) read Galatians 5 as licensing — even mandating — a sharply distinct ethical community marked by nonviolence, mutual aid, and freedom from coerced religion. Wesleyan readings (John Wesley's *Notes on the New Testament*, his sermons on Christian perfection) treat the fruit of the Spirit as the substance of sanctification: the believer can, by grace, grow into the actual practice of love, joy, peace, patience, and the rest. Wesley's doctrine of entire sanctification — the possibility of being filled with love such that no rival affection remains — leans heavily on 5:22–23 as descriptive, not aspirational.

Modern critical commentary on Galatians 5 has been shaped by the same scholars who shaped chapter 4. Lightfoot (1865) gave the classical philological reading. Burton's 1921 ICC remains the gold standard for the Greek. Hans Dieter Betz's Hermeneia (1979) traced the rhetorical structure of the *exhortatio* and argued — controversially — that the *exhortatio* sits awkwardly within the rhetorical scheme. Longenecker's WBC (1990), Bruce's NIGTC (1982), Schreiner's ZECNT (2010), and Moo's BECNT (2013) provide thorough evangelical-historical treatments. J. Louis Martyn's Anchor Yale Bible (1997) reads the *sarx*/*pneuma* dialectic as the moral expression of the cosmic apocalyptic conflict that began in chapter 4: the Spirit's incursion into the present age constitutes a battlefield within the believer.

The New Perspective school has approached Galatians 5 with characteristic emphasis on the social-boundary functions of Torah. Dunn argues that "works of the flesh" should not be reduced to private moral failures; they are community-destroying behaviors that the Spirit's fruit reverses. N.T. Wright (Paul and the Faithfulness of God, 2013; Paul for Everyone: Galatians and Thessalonians, 2002) reads the chapter as the description of new-covenant life: the Spirit poured into the hearts of Jew and Gentile alike produces the character that Torah always pointed toward, so that "the fruit of the Spirit" is what the renewed-heart promise of Jeremiah 31 and Ezekiel 36 looks like in practice. On Wright's reading, 5:14 ("the whole law is fulfilled in one word") is not a Christian abridgment of Torah but the claim that the Messiah's people, walking by the Spirit, actually do what Israel was called to do; love-of-neighbor is Torah arriving at its goal, not Torah replaced.

Paul-Within-Judaism scholars (Nanos, Eisenbaum, Fredriksen) emphasize that 5:14 explicitly says the Law is *fulfilled* (peplērōtai), not abolished. Paul's argument is not against Torah but against Gentile proselyte conversion as a condition of inclusion. For Gentile believers, Spirit-led love fulfills Torah; for Jewish believers, life in the Messiah continues to be Torah-shaped, just no longer Torah-marked-as-boundary.

The ethical tradition (Richard Hays, *The Moral Vision of the New Testament*; Stanley Hauerwas, *A Community of Character* and *The Peaceable Kingdom*; Oliver O'Donovan, *Resurrection and Moral Order*) has read Galatians 5 as a foundational text for Christian ethics. Hays makes the chapter central to his thesis that NT ethics is fundamentally communal and Spirit-formed rather than individualistic and rule-based. Hauerwas reads the fruit of the Spirit as virtues constituted by participation in the church-as-polis, not as private dispositions.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** Abraham Joshua Heschel argued, against a long Christian caricature, that genuine Jewish freedom is not freedom from Torah but freedom in Torah — Torah as the structure within which a free life becomes possible. Daniel Boyarin's *A Radical Jew* reads Galatians 5:14 as a Hellenistic-Jewish move that flattens Torah's particular commandments into a single universal principle, and notes the cost: love-of-neighbor as universal abstraction can lose its grip on the concrete obligations Torah specifies. Boyarin admires the move and registers its dangers. Mark Nanos, reading 5:14 within a Paul-Within-Judaism frame, argues that Paul is recapitulating a contemporary Jewish summary tradition (the Hillel-Akiva line) and not abrogating Torah for Jews. Pamela Eisenbaum reads the entire ethical section as Gentile-directed: the Galatians have access to Spirit-led life apart from proselyte conversion, but Paul's argument does not require Jewish believers to abandon Torah.

Where the traditions converge: there is broad agreement that Paul stands within a recognizably Jewish-apocalyptic moral framework, that his vice and virtue catalogs have Hellenistic-Jewish parallels (Wisdom of Solomon, 4 Maccabees, Qumran), and that his *eleutheria* is not Stoic *autarkeia*. They diverge on the relation of Torah to the Spirit-led life. Lutheran tradition reads the Law as fundamentally accusing and the Spirit's fruit as the gospel's fruit beyond Law; Reformed tradition reads the Law's third use as continuing to guide the sanctified life; Wesleyan tradition reads Law and Spirit as pedagogically distinct but ethically convergent; New Perspective and Paul-Within-Judaism readings preserve Torah's integrity for Israel while affirming Gentile inclusion through the Spirit.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did Paul believe God was doing in Galatians 5? Paul thought God had constituted a new mode of human life, in which the Spirit of the Son indwells believers and produces in them the moral shape of Christ — not by external constraint but by internal animation. The freedom Christ gives is not freedom *from* moral seriousness; it is the only kind of freedom that can sustain moral seriousness, because it is freedom under the Spirit's own life-giving power. The chapter's deepest claim is that the entire Law is fulfilled in love (5:14), and that the Spirit's fruit is the actual material of that fulfillment. Paul thought he was watching the Galatians threatened with reverting from Spirit-given life to a Torah-as-boundary-marker that, however dignified in itself, would re-enslave them to a system the Messiah's coming has surpassed.

**Tension One: Law and freedom — what is the relation between Christ's freedom and moral structure?** The Lutheran two-uses-of-the-Law tradition reads the Law's primary functions as civil restraint (the *usus politicus*) and accusation that drives sinners to Christ (the *usus paedagogicus*). The Law cannot produce the love it commands; only the Spirit can. The Reformed tradition (Calvin, Westminster, Bavinck) adds a third use (*usus tertius*, the *usus didacticus*): for the regenerate, the Law remains a guide for sanctified life — not as a means of justification but as a structure for the love the Spirit produces. The Anabaptist tradition (Menno, Yoder, Hauerwas) reads Christ's law of love as supplanting Torah, with the Sermon on the Mount and the cross as the new norm; the Spirit forms a community whose practices embody this law. The Wesleyan tradition treats sanctification as a real, growing capacity for love, with the fruit of the Spirit (5:22–23) describing what the Spirit actually produces in disciplined believers. Catholic teaching (Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I-II.106–108; Vatican II's *Lumen Gentium*; CCC §§1965–1986 on the New Law) integrates these strands: the New Law is principally the grace of the Spirit given inwardly, with the moral teaching of Christ and the sacramental life as its visible structure. The New Perspective reads Torah as still standing for Israel — what changes is that Gentiles, through the Spirit, inherit Abrahamic promise without passing through Torah's boundary-marking ceremonies.

To steelman the Reformed third-use position — which I find harder to hold than the Anabaptist or Wesleyan readings, because it seems to risk reintroducing the very legalism Paul opposes — the strongest case is exegetical: Paul does not say "the Law is abolished"; he says "the whole Law is *fulfilled* in one word" (5:14, *peplērōtai*). The verb is plenitudinal, not abolitionary. The fruit of the Spirit corresponds to the substance of Torah's moral commands, not to their negation. If the Spirit produces love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control, then the Spirit produces the heart of Torah's intention. The Reformed third use names this: the Law, no longer a tutor that condemns, becomes a guide that the Spirit-formed heart follows from within. Where this leaves us: the relation between freedom and moral structure is best understood not as opposition but as transposition — what Torah specified externally, the Spirit produces internally, and the resulting life is recognizably Torah-fulfilling without being Torah-burdened.

**Tension Two: Faith working through love (5:6) — the Catholic-Protestant divide and its modern reconciliation.** The Tridentine Catholic position taught that justifying faith is *fides caritate formata* — faith formed by love — and that justification involves a real, infused righteousness, not merely an imputed legal status; Trent's appeal to Galatians 5:6 was central. The Lutheran position, formalized in the Formula of Concord (1577), distinguished sharply between the *imputed* righteousness that justifies and the *imparted* righteousness that follows: faith alone justifies; love is the inevitable fruit but not the cause. Calvin's Reformed position was similar but emphasized that faith and love are inseparable in the believer's actual life. The Anabaptist tradition fused the two more tightly: faith without obedient love is no faith. The Orthodox tradition (Lossky, Florovsky, Schmemann) approaches the question through theosis: "faith working through love" describes the synergistic life in which divine grace enables and human freedom responds. The 1999 Joint Declaration cited Galatians 5:6 and read it as compatible with both Lutheran and Catholic core claims — faith alone justifies, and that faith is never alone but always working through love.

To steelman the Catholic infused-righteousness reading — which Protestant traditions often hear as Pelagian — the strongest case is anthropological: human beings are not legal abstractions whose status can be changed by imputation alone; they are agents whose actual character is at stake. If justification is real reconciliation with God, it must change the agent, not merely her legal record. The Lutheran response — that imputation does not exclude transformation, but distinguishes its ground from its fruit — preserves the same insight by different means. Where this leaves us: the 1999 Joint Declaration suggests that the Reformation rupture on 5:6, while doctrinally serious, may have been more a question of emphasis and grammar than of substantive disagreement about what Paul means. Faith justifies, faith works through love, and the working is not optional.

One unresolved question: if the fruit of the Spirit (5:22–23) is genuinely produced by the indwelling Spirit and not by the believer's effort, then what is the moral status of believers who exhibit obvious failures of patience, kindness, or self-control — and what does it mean to "walk by the Spirit" when, biographically, the walk fails on Tuesday? The Wesleyan tradition reaches for entire sanctification; the Reformed for progressive sanctification; the Lutheran for *simul iustus et peccator*; the Catholic for the sacramental economy of confession and Eucharist. None of these answers fully closes the gap between 5:22–23's confidence and the believer's actual life. The chapter's deepest pastoral and theological tension lives in this gap.

## The Thread

**Romans 8:1–17** — The same flesh/Spirit dialectic structures Romans 8, where "those who live according to the flesh" cannot please God and "those who are led by the Spirit are sons of God." The Romans development is more cosmological (creation groans, awaiting redemption) than the Galatians focus on community formation, but the underlying anthropology is identical: a Spirit-indwelled humanity whose moral life is the visible shape of the Spirit's work.

**Leviticus 19:18** — Paul's claim that the whole Law is fulfilled in "love your neighbor as yourself" cites this Levitical command, which Jewish tradition (Akiva, *Sifra*; Hillel, *b. Shabbat* 31a) had already identified as Torah's central principle. Reading Galatians 5:14 alongside Leviticus 19's full holiness code reminds us that the love-command in its original setting carries a long catalog of concrete obligations — care for the poor, just weights, honest courts, sexual fidelity. Paul's "fulfilled" gathers these into the Spirit's animating principle without erasing them.

**1 Corinthians 13** — Paul's hymn to love in 1 Corinthians articulates what 5:22–23 lists. The Galatians fruit-of-the-Spirit catalog and the Corinthians love-hymn are mutually illuminating: love is the first fruit (Gal 5:22), and love is what abides when prophecy and tongues pass (1 Cor 13:8–13). The combination is the substance of Pauline Christian ethics.

**Mark 12:28–34 / Matt 22:34–40** — Jesus's identification of the love-of-God and love-of-neighbor commands as the two greatest is the tradition Paul stands inside when he abridges the Law to Leviticus 19:18. The Galatians passage compresses Jesus's two-command summary by foregrounding the horizontal command, perhaps because the vertical command (love of God) is implicit in Spirit-led life. The compression is rhetorically efficient and theologically loaded.
