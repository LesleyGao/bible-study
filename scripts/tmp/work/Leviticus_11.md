# Leviticus 11: The Boundaries of the Table

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

After the narrative shock of Leviticus 10, the book turns abruptly to the categories its opening verses had taught the priesthood to teach: *qōdeš* and *ḥōl*, *ṭāmēʾ* and *ṭāhôr*. Chapter 11 is the first of the purity chapters (Lev 11-15), and it is the broadest in reach. The sacrificial manual of chapters 1-7 and the ordination cycle of 8-10 concerned priests; chapter 11 concerns everyone who eats. It is, in functional terms, the place where Israelite priestly theology comes home for the laity.

The Hebrew title *Wayyiqrāʾ* (וַיִּקְרָא) frames the whole book as divine call; chapter 11 is one of its most culturally consequential calls. On composition, the Wellhausen consensus dated the dietary rules to exilic or post-exilic P, reading them as Second Temple identity-marker legislation. Haran and Kaufmann argued for a substantially earlier core. Milgrom (AB 3, on Leviticus 11) argues that the list of forbidden animals is unusually archaic — the four named quadrupeds (camel, rock badger, hare, pig) would not have been the animals a later redactor chose if legislating from Jerusalem, and the bird taxonomy reflects a pre-monarchic avifauna. Knohl's P/H distinction cuts through the chapter: the core list (vv. 1-23, 41-42) belongs to P, and the hortatory frame ("be holy, for I am holy," vv. 43-45) is H editorial expansion. This explains the chapter's odd tonal shift in its final verses.

The political situation of the composition debate matters: dietary laws are the kind of legislation that could function as identity marker for any period, and so the question of origin cannot be settled from the rules themselves. What can be settled is *effect*. Brian Hesse and Paula Wapnish's archaeozoological studies of Israelite highland sites (from the 1990s through the 2000s) found a sharp, consistent pattern: from roughly the 12th century BC onward, settlements in the central hill country that would become Israel and Judah show a near-total absence of pig bones, while contemporary Philistine coastal sites (Ashkelon, Ekron, Ashdod) show pig bones as 8-20% of faunal assemblages. Israel Finkelstein has used this as one of the strongest ethnic markers in Iron Age archaeology: the absence of pig becomes an Israelite signature before the Torah's legal traditions are fully codified. Whatever else Leviticus 11 is, its prohibitions reflect a practice older than the composed text.

Material culture clarifies the chapter's odd taxonomies. The ancient Near East possessed no Linnaean biology. Animal categories were folk-taxonomic — organized by habitat and locomotion — and Leviticus 11 works within that framework. Land animals are classified by hoof and cud; water animals by fin and scale; winged creatures by species name; swarming creatures by locomotion (belly, paws, many feet). Hittite priestly taboo lists (CTH 264, CTH 275) are similarly organized, restricting priests from specific animals deemed to contaminate the sanctuary. Egyptian priestly diets, recorded in Plutarch's *De Iside et Osiride* 8 and confirmed by Papyri from Oxyrhynchus and earlier temple inventories, prohibited pork, fish, and certain birds for ritual staff. Herodotus (*Histories* 2.37) records the Egyptian priests as abstaining from pork and fish and from "unclean" animals in terms strikingly close to Leviticus. The Ugaritic texts lack a comparable dietary code, which is itself revealing: the Canaanite cult does not seem to have extended purity concerns to lay diet, while P extends them across the whole community.

Five Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. *Ṭāhôr* (טָהוֹר, "clean") and *ṭāmēʾ* (טָמֵא, "unclean") — the two general categories of ritual state. *Šeqeṣ* (שֶׁקֶץ, "detestable") — a stronger category reserved in chapter 11 for aquatic, insect, and swarming creatures; the verb form *šiqqēṣ* means "to regard as abomination." *Šereṣ* (שֶׁרֶץ, "swarming thing") — the category of small creatures whose locomotion is ambiguous (snake-like, multi-legged). And *haḇdîl* (הִבְדִּיל, "to separate, distinguish") — the verb in verse 47, which echoes Genesis 1's creation-separations (*wayyaḇdēl*). Chapter 11 presents dietary distinction as a participation in the divine work of ordering creation.

Textual evidence is strong. 4QLev-a preserves fragments of chapter 11 with minor orthographic variation; 11QpaleoLev preserves the chapter almost entirely in paleo-Hebrew script. The LXX makes a few small harmonizations — the bird list diverges in several species identifications, which is itself a window into how uncertain the Hebrew ornithological vocabulary had become by the 3rd century BC. The Peshitta follows MT. The Samaritan Pentateuch adds minor clarifications, including an expansion at verse 42. For the Egyptian comparanda, the Papyrus Anastasi IV and the medical Papyrus Ebers (c. 1550 BC) record pork as associated with the skin diseases Leviticus 13 will address, a thread of ancient Near Eastern anxiety about pig that goes back at least to the Middle Kingdom.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Leviticus 11 is a taxonomy with a hortatory coda. The taxonomy proceeds by habitat: land (vv. 1-8), water (vv. 9-12), air (vv. 13-23), and the residual category of surface-swarmers (vv. 29-30, 41-43). Within each habitat, the text gives either an abstract criterion (hoof-and-cud, fins-and-scales, jointed-legs-for-hopping) or a species list. The asymmetry is significant: quadrupeds and fish get rules; birds and insects get lists. Milgrom proposes this is because the permitted species in each habitat were few enough to list individually in the case of birds and insects but not in the case of fish and land animals. Douglas, by contrast, argues that the list format preserves an older, pre-taxonomic layer.

The chapter's literary backbone is the fourfold creation-echo. Genesis 1 organizes creation into birds of the air (day 5), fish of the sea (day 5), creeping things and beasts of the earth (day 6). Leviticus 11 walks the same inventory in roughly reverse order (land, sea, sky, surface-swarmers) and legislates for each domain. Mary Douglas's foundational reading (*Purity and Danger*, 1966) identifies this echo and argues that holiness in P means *wholeness of type*: a land animal ought to chew cud and have split hooves (paradigmatic ruminant), a water animal ought to have fins and scales (paradigmatic swimmer), a sky animal ought to fly. Anomalies — camel-with-cud-but-not-split-hoof, pig-with-split-hoof-but-no-cud, bat-that-flies-but-is-mammalian, locust-that-has-legs-but-no-normal-hopping-mechanism — cross category lines and are therefore unclean. Unclean is not "dirty" but "ambiguous." Douglas updated this in *Leviticus as Literature* (1999), emphasizing that the category violations the text targets are structural: what the unclean animals share is that they confound the reader's expectation of what a creature of their habitat should be.

Douglas's reading has been challenged — Walter Houston, *Purity and Monotheism* (1993), argues that the taxonomy is less symmetric than Douglas claims and that economic and practical considerations (ease of herding, calorie availability) better explain much of the list — but it remains the single most influential literary reading of the chapter. Milgrom refines rather than rejects it: the Douglas category-violation logic explains the *selection*, and an overlaid ethical logic (animals that predate, animals that scavenge, animals whose consumption implicates the Israelite in death) explains the *weight*.

The hortatory coda (vv. 43-45) is literarily distinct. Its diction and rhythm belong to H, the Holiness editorial layer: *kî qādôš ʾānî, wihyîtem qĕdōšîm* ("for I am holy; you shall be holy"). This is the first appearance in the book of the refrain that will organize chapters 17-26. Verse 45's self-identification formula — "I am the LORD who brought you up out of the land of Egypt" — grounds dietary holiness in exodus memory. The taxonomy ends with an explicit purpose statement (v. 47): "to make a distinction (*haḇdîl*) between the unclean and the clean." The verb closes the chapter by returning it to Genesis 1.

Textual variants are minor. The LXX's bird list differs in perhaps half a dozen identifications; the uncertainty is already ancient. The SP's expansion at verse 42 clarifies that "whatever has many feet" includes centipedes and millipedes. 4QLev-a at verse 32 has a minor orthographic variant.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** The Letter of Aristeas (§128-171, 2nd c. BC) provides the earliest extended Jewish defense of the dietary laws: they are pedagogical, training the Jew in moral distinction by daily distinction of food, and the clean animals' traits symbolize virtues. This reading is picked up and intensified by Philo in *Special Laws* 4.100-131: the split hoof signifies moral discrimination, the cud signifies meditation, fins and scales signify self-restraint and shielding. Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.259-272) harmonizes and compresses.

The rabbinic engagement is immense. The Mishnah devotes the entire tractate *Hullin* (ten chapters) to the practical halakhah of permitted and forbidden animals and slaughter; the Tosefta and the Bavli *Hullin* 3b-5a and following extend it. Sifra's running commentary on Leviticus 11 is more exegetical than halakhic, probing verse by verse. The rabbinic tradition settled three key principles: that the four exceptional animals named in verses 4-7 exhaust the class of creatures with one-but-not-both of the defining traits (Mishnah Hullin 3:6); that the abstract criterion (hoof-and-cud) overrides the need for species lists where the criterion suffices; and that dietary laws, unlike purity laws more broadly, retained full force after the Temple's destruction.

Rashi (11th c.) treats the chapter verse by verse with a pedagogical emphasis on the priest as teacher. Ibn Ezra notes the zoological difficulties candidly — he is not sure what all the bird names refer to. Ramban (13th c.) offers a quasi-medical reading: the unclean animals produce unclean humors in the body and cloud the soul. Maimonides, in *Guide of the Perplexed* 3.48, argues that the dietary laws are primarily hygienic — the forbidden animals are "unwholesome" — and secondarily pedagogical. His *Hilchot Maachalot Asurot* ("Laws of Forbidden Foods") codifies the practical halakhah in eighty chapters. Sforno (16th c.) returns to the pedagogical line and argues that eating from the creation with discrimination is itself a form of Sabbath. Rabbi Samson Raphael Hirsch (19th c.) rejects the hygienic reading entirely and makes the dietary laws a symbolic pedagogy of self-mastery: the Jew who eats with discrimination learns to choose with discrimination in other domains.

Modern Jewish readers continue the split. Nehama Leibowitz sides with Hirsch; Rabbi Jonathan Sacks (*Covenant and Conversation: Leviticus*) combines Hirsch and Milgrom, arguing that the laws are a daily reminder that life comes at the cost of life. Jacob Neusner's form-critical work on the Mishnah identified *Hullin* as the tractate that most thoroughly constructs Israel as an "eating people." Nahum Sarna and Baruch Levine, in their JPS commentaries, incline toward Douglas. Historians of Jewish domestic practice (Schwartz, Goldman) have also noted that kashrut has been one of the religious domains in which the household, and the labour of those keeping it, has carried the bulk of practical responsibility.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** The New Testament's three crucial passages set the terms. Mark 7:19 reports Jesus's teaching on defilement and adds the parenthetical *katharizōn panta ta brōmata* ("thus he declared all foods clean") — a phrase that is almost certainly a Marcan editorial comment rather than Jesus's direct statement, but which shapes the entire Christian reception. Acts 10:9-16 records Peter's rooftop vision at Joppa, where a sheet of unclean animals is lowered and the voice says "what God has made clean, do not call common"; Peter reads this immediately as authorization to enter the house of the Gentile Cornelius. Acts 15, the Jerusalem Council, retains four Levitical prohibitions for Gentile Christians (blood, things strangled, meat sacrificed to idols, *porneia*) but drops the dietary taxonomy. Romans 14 treats food as *adiaphora*, a matter of conscience. Colossians 2:16 brushes past the question: "let no one pass judgment on you in questions of food and drink."

The Epistle of Barnabas 10 (early 2nd c.) allegorizes the dietary list on the Philonic model, but inverts Philo's intent: for Barnabas, the Jews misread their own text by literalizing it, and the forbidden animals are moral types (pig = the inconstant man, hare = the lustful, etc.). Origen's *Homilies on Leviticus* 7 follows the allegorical line but more charitably. Augustine, in *Contra Faustum* 6 and 32, distinguishes ceremonial from moral law: the dietary code is *umbra futurorum*, shadow of things to come, fulfilled and abrogated in Christ. Chrysostom, preaching on Acts 10, dwells on Peter's reluctance — the dietary laws were so deeply internalized that even apostolic vision required three repetitions.

Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I-II q. 102 a. 6, treats the dietary laws with considerable sophistication: they have three simultaneous functions — literal (some animals genuinely were unsuitable in context), figurative (moral typology), and disciplinary (separating Israel from the nations). The ceremonial law is abrogated for Christians but not foolish; Aquinas defends Maimonides's hygienic reading as one true but not exclusive account. Hugh of St. Victor had earlier made similar moves. Calvin (*Institutes* 4.14, *Commentaries on Leviticus*) denies the hygienic reading and emphasizes the pedagogical and separatist function. Luther, in *Against the Sabbatarians* (1538) and in his Genesis lectures, argues that Christians who revive dietary law rebuild the wall of partition Christ tore down.

Modern critical scholarship begins with Wellhausen's dating and turns a corner with Douglas. Jonathan Klawans's *Impurity and Sin in Ancient Judaism* (2000) distinguishes ritual impurity (contagious, contractible, remediable by ritual) from moral impurity (sin-generated, non-contagious in the same sense) and argues that Leviticus 11's concern is ritual rather than moral — an important correction to certain Christian readings. Gordon Wenham's NICOT commentary, Jay Sklar's Tyndale commentary, John Hartley's WBC, Ephraim Radner's *Leviticus* (Brazos Theological Commentary), and L. Michael Morales's *Who Shall Ascend the Mountain of the LORD?* all engage Douglas seriously. Christopher Wright's *Old Testament Ethics for the People of God* argues that the abrogation of ceremonial law does not abrogate its ethical shape: Christian engagement with the creation can still be marked by discrimination, restraint, and table-fellowship as identity. Amy-Jill Levine (*The Misunderstood Jew*) has been sharply critical of Christian readings that treat kashrut as mere legalism; she insists the laws have always been a form of daily sanctification, not a burden.

**Where they converge and diverge.** The point of deepest divergence is Acts 10 and Mark 7:19. Jewish tradition does not read kashrut as abrogated; Christian tradition, with rare exceptions (Seventh-Day Adventists, Messianic Jewish communities), does. The convergence is on the moral pedagogy of the text: Douglas, Milgrom, Sacks, Hirsch, Aquinas, Wright, and Radner all agree that the dietary laws trained Israel in distinction, in daily categorical discipline, in the recognition that the creation is not morally flat. They disagree about whether that training is ongoing or completed. A second point of convergence is the rejection of the purely hygienic reading: few modern scholars in either tradition accept Maimonides's reading as sufficient, though most admit it has a partial truth. Klawans's ritual/moral distinction has been accepted across traditions and has cleaned up a lot of muddy exegesis.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Do the dietary laws reveal the character of God, or are they pedagogical accommodation to human weakness?** Three positions.

The *revelatory* reading: the dietary distinctions encode how God has ordered creation, so eating with discrimination is participation in divine ordering. Douglas, Hirsch, Sforno, and Radner converge here — though from different directions. The creation-Leviticus link, sealed by the *haḇdîl* verb in verses 44 and 47, grounds this reading in the text itself: God who separated light from darkness separates clean from unclean, and Israel's eating recapitulates that separation.

The *pedagogical* reading: the dietary laws were instituted to train Israel in habits of restraint and discrimination, with no necessary revelation of the divine character. Maimonides (*Guide* 3.48), Calvin, Aquinas (in one of his modes), and Milgrom converge here. On this view the particular list is accommodated to the cultural context — the forbidden animals are not metaphysically dirty but contextually pedagogical — and a different cultural context might have seen a different list.

The *accommodationist-separatist* reading: the dietary laws were given to mark Israel off from the surrounding nations, and their content is arbitrary relative to that purpose. This is Aristeas's reading, revived by Michael Wyschogrod and in modified form by Hartley. Israel's distinctiveness requires tangible daily markers; the specific content matters less than the identity-forming function.

The least intuitive position to steelman is the revelatory one: that the particular animals declared unclean are not random, not merely pedagogical, but genuine windows onto how God orders creation. Douglas's structural case — that the unclean animals are systematically category-violators — means that avoiding them is not a matter of obeying an opaque rule but of honoring the shape of the creation. The rule is not arbitrary; the world really is organized so that a cud-chewing, split-hoofed quadruped is the paradigm land animal, and what departs from that paradigm departs from creation's ordering.

*Where this leaves us:* the three readings may be less exclusive than they look. A law can reveal divine character (revelatory), train human habit (pedagogical), and mark covenant identity (separatist) simultaneously, and the tradition's best readers have always held all three in tension. The Christian abrogation argument does not settle the meaning of the laws for the period in which they applied; it only terminates their binding force on Gentiles within the new covenant.

**Tension 2: Are dietary laws primarily symbolic, ethical-compassionate, or hygienic?**

Milgrom's most distinctive contribution to the chapter is his argument — in AB 3 and in *Leviticus: A Continental Commentary* — that the dietary laws embody a compassionate restraint on human dominion over animal life. Permitted animals are herbivores; forbidden ones tend to be predators, scavengers, or category-violators associated with death. The limit on consumption trains Israel in the recognition that life is costly and not all life is available for eating. This moral reading sits alongside but distinct from Douglas's symbolic reading and from Maimonides's hygienic reading.

The *symbolic* (Douglas) position: the laws enact the wholeness-of-type that holiness requires; the taxonomy is its own theology.

The *ethical-compassionate* (Milgrom) position: the laws limit Israel's participation in death; eating becomes reverent rather than predatory.

The *hygienic* (Maimonides, partial) position: the forbidden animals are those whose consumption is medically or environmentally problematic in ancient Levantine context. Pork, given ancient pastoralist conditions in the highlands, is genuinely problematic; shellfish in a non-refrigerated society is genuinely risky.

Steelmanning the least intuitive: the Milgrom ethical reading is the most counterintuitive against the surface of the text, because the text does not speak of compassion at all. But Milgrom's case rests on the *shape* of what is forbidden (predators, scavengers, carnivores) and what is permitted (domesticated herbivores, fish that move by fins rather than swarm), and that shape really does track a hierarchy of moral complicity in death. Blood, forbidden in Leviticus 17 and already hinted at in chapter 11's carcass rules, is the linking concept: eating animals draws the eater into the economy of death, and Leviticus circumscribes that participation.

*Where this leaves us:* the three readings are partially compatible. The symbolic reading gives the taxonomy its logic; the ethical reading gives the selection its weight; the hygienic reading gives the context its plausibility. The text does not force a choice, and the best commentators (Wenham, Sklar, Sacks) hold all three.

**One unresolved question:** If the dietary laws abrogated for Gentile Christians in Acts 10-15 were revelatory of God's character rather than merely pedagogical, in what sense has that revelation been abrogated, and if they were merely pedagogical, why did God reveal them in the first place? The New Testament does not directly answer. Chapter 11 leaves the question hanging over every subsequent debate about the continuing force of Torah for Christians.

## The Thread

Four non-obvious cross-references worth tracking.

First: Genesis 1's *haḇdîl* is reactivated in Leviticus 11:47. Eating with distinction is, in the priestly theology, a participation in the creational act of separation. When Acts 10:15 has the voice tell Peter "what God has made clean, do not call common," the verb deployed is the same semantic field; Peter is told to participate in a *new* act of divine separation-and-reordering.

Second: Isaiah 65:4 and 66:17 describe end-time transgressors as those "who eat pig's flesh and the broth of unclean things." The prophetic reception of Leviticus 11 is eschatological: pig-eating becomes the marker of a rebellious Israel, and 2 Maccabees 6-7 will make the martyrdom of those who refuse pork the paradigmatic act of covenant fidelity.

Third: Deuteronomy 14:3-21 presents a parallel dietary list with verbal overlap. The relationship between Lev 11 and Deut 14 is one of the cruxes of Pentateuchal source criticism; Milgrom argues Deut 14 abbreviates Lev 11, while Driver argued the reverse. Whichever direction, the doubling of the dietary code in two of the Pentateuch's five books marks it as foundational.

Fourth: Daniel 1:8's refusal of the royal food is the classic narrative enactment of Leviticus 11 in exile, and the story is structured to show that covenant fidelity under foreign power is possible without the Temple, precisely through diet. Daniel's dietary faithfulness becomes the template for 1 Maccabees, for the Essenes at Qumran (1QS 6:4-5), and later for the rabbinic construction of the home as *miqdāš meʿaṭ*, "a small sanctuary."
