# Exodus 4: The Mystery at the Lodging Place

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Exodus 4 moves across three physical settings — the mountain of God in Midian, the desert road back toward Egypt, and a night-stop somewhere between. Each has its own texture. The mountain of God (Horeb/Sinai) carries the residue of chapter 3's theophany. The road back threads through the Sinai peninsula's wadi systems, the watercourses that funnel trade and migration between the copper-rich Timna zone and the eastern Nile Delta. The "lodging place" (*mālôn*, מָלוֹן) of verse 24 is a way-station — not an inn in the later Greco-Roman sense, but a known watering stop along a regular caravan route. Egyptian administrative texts (Papyrus Anastasi I, for instance) describe such stops along the Ways of Horus — the military road from Sile in the Delta across northern Sinai to Gaza. Papyrus Anastasi III even describes the fortress at Tjaru (modern Tell Hebua) as the primary eastern border checkpoint.

The political horizon is a Ramesside Egypt, still dominant, still administering its eastern frontier by a network of forts and wells. When God tells Moses that "all the men who were seeking your life are dead" (v. 19), the narrative marks a generational transition in the Egyptian royal house — the Pharaoh of chapter 2 has died, and a successor reigns. This fits Ramesside chronology if Ramses II is the Pharaoh of the oppression and Merneptah the Pharaoh of the exodus (Kitchen's preferred reconstruction), though Hoffmeier has defended variations. The Merneptah Stele of c. 1208 BC, mentioning Israel as a defeated people in Canaan, remains the chronological anchor — by then Israel is in the land.

The staff-to-serpent sign demands attention against the Egyptian magical background. The Westcar Papyrus (Papyrus Berlin 3033), dating to the Hyksos period but preserving earlier traditions, tells of the lector-priest Djadjaemankh transforming a wax crocodile into a living one. The Harris Magical Papyrus and the Leiden Magical Papyrus I 348 describe serpent-magic rituals. Egyptian royal iconography places the cobra (*uraeus*) on the pharaoh's crown — the *ʾiret*, the protective serpent-goddess Wadjet. The serpent was a symbol of royal and divine power. When Moses' staff becomes a serpent, the text is deploying a sign within Egyptian semiotic space — a contest, not a foreign intrusion. The Hebrew word for the magicians in later chapters, *ḥarṭummîm* (חַרְטֻמִּים), is itself an Egyptian loanword, from *ḥry-tp*, "chief [of the priesthood]" — a technical term for lector-priests who specialized in ritual and magic. The text knows its Egyptian institutional vocabulary.

The leprosy-sign draws on another dense vocabulary. The Hebrew *ṣāraʿat* (צָרַעַת) is the skin-affliction group addressed at length in Leviticus 13–14. It is not Hansen's disease in the modern medical sense — it is a category covering various scaling and discoloring skin conditions, each carrying ritual-impurity implications. The phrase "leprous like snow" (*mĕṣōraʿat kaššāleg*) uses the same descriptor that will later mark Miriam's punishment in Numbers 12 and Gehazi's punishment in 2 Kings 5. The hand that reveals this sign is the same hand that will hold the staff — body as theological witness.

Authorship and source layers: the chapter's material has traditionally been assigned primarily to J (the Yahwist) by classical source critics, with verses 21–23 sometimes seen as later Deuteronomistic or Priestly redactional work that introduces the hardening-of-heart motif and the firstborn-son Christology in advance of chapter 12. Rolf Rendtorff and Erhard Blum would describe the composition more as multiple redactional layers shaping a coherent tradition. Konrad Schmid and Thomas Römer argue the chapter knits together originally independent traditions: a call-completion scene, a circumcision remnant from a much older layer, a firstborn-son theology from a redactional frame. The bridegroom-of-blood passage at verses 24–26 is widely regarded as one of the oldest fragments in the Pentateuch — so archaic and compressed that even the pronouns are ambiguous.

Five terms deserve their original script. *Maṭṭeh* (מַטֶּה), staff, the ordinary shepherd's tool that becomes the instrument of plague and deliverance. *Nāḥāš* (נָחָשׁ), serpent — the word of Genesis 3. *Ḥātan-dāmîm* (חֲתַן־דָּמִים), "bridegroom of blood," or "blood-relative by marriage" — the phrase in verses 25–26 whose interpretation has been disputed for two thousand years. *Bĕkōrî* (בְּכֹרִי), "my firstborn," the theological designation of Israel in verse 22 that Paul will pick up in Romans 9:4 (*huiothesia*, adoption). *ʿOrlâ* (עָרְלָה), foreskin, a term of ritual specificity in Genesis 17 and here — the covenantal sign cut into flesh.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre-wise, Exodus 4 continues the call narrative begun in chapter 3 and completes the five-part structure: encounter (ch. 3), commission, objection, reassurance, sign. Moses' objections in 4:1, 4:10, and 4:13 are the third, fourth, and fifth of his resistances to the call (counting "Who am I?" at 3:11 and "What is his name?" at 3:13 as the first two). The structure is extended beyond the typical call-narrative pattern precisely because Moses resists longer than any other biblical prophet. Norman Habel's analysis of call narratives notes that most prophets register one objection; Moses registers five. The accumulation is theologically load-bearing.

The chapter's major literary puzzle is the bridegroom-of-blood passage at verses 24–26. William Propp, in the Anchor Bible *Exodus 1–18*, catalogues more than twenty different interpretations. The Hebrew pronouns are ambiguous: "The LORD met *him*" — Moses or Gershom? "Sought to put *him* to death" — again ambiguous. "Touched *his feet* with it" — whose feet? "Feet" (*raglayim*, רַגְלַיִם) can be a euphemism for genitals (as in Isaiah 6:2 of the seraphim, and in the incident of Saul's relieving himself in the cave, 1 Samuel 24:3). The circumcision is either of Gershom (Moses' son) or Moses himself — the text is irresolvably unclear, and commentators have argued both sides for centuries. The narrative compression is itself the literary effect: the reader is stopped mid-journey by a sudden, near-catastrophic divine attack that Zipporah resolves through a ritual act. The episode is then left unexplained.

Literary devices throughout the chapter include the patterning of the hand. Moses stretches out his hand to catch the serpent, puts his hand inside his cloak, takes it out leprous, puts it back, takes it out restored. The staff becomes a serpent, becomes a staff. These are not just miracles but semiotic reversals — the world becomes briefly uncanny to demonstrate who its author is. The repetition of "serve" (*ʿāvad*, עָבַד) begins here: Pharaoh is making Israel serve him, but YHWH says "let my son go that he may serve me" (v. 23). Serving (*ʿăvōdâ*, worship/labor) will be the book's keyword — Israel will move from serving Pharaoh to serving God, and the Hebrew verb refuses the distinction between worship and work.

Within the book's argument, Exodus 4 completes the preparation. Chapter 3 called Moses; chapter 4 equips him (signs), commissions his co-worker (Aaron), and brings him back to Egypt. Chapter 5 will execute the first failed audience. The bridegroom-of-blood scene is the chapter's strangest moment and also its theological linchpin: before Moses can deliver Israel, something in his own household must be put right. Brevard Childs argues the episode is a ritually dense narrative insisting that Israel's deliverer must himself be inside the covenantal mark (circumcision). The narrative refuses to let the liberator operate outside the covenantal body.

Textual variants. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments. 4QExod-Levf contains bits of Exodus 4. The Samaritan Pentateuch has some expansions at 4:27 harmonizing with the earlier narrative. The Septuagint's rendering of the bridegroom-of-blood passage diverges significantly — it reads "the blood of the circumcision of my son is stopped," an interpretive rather than literal translation, suggesting the Alexandrian translators found the Hebrew as obscure as we do. The Targum Onkelos expands and clarifies, identifying the angel as the destroyer and clarifying that it is Moses whom God sought.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical arc, Exodus 4 is the hinge between call and confrontation. The firstborn-son declaration at verse 22 — "Israel is my firstborn son" — establishes a theological identity that will echo through the rest of Scripture. Jeremiah 31:9 will call Ephraim God's firstborn. Hosea 11:1 will say "out of Egypt I called my son." Paul will speak of *huiothesia*, adoption, as Israel's and the church's identity in Romans 9:4 and Galatians 4. The verse is a theological keystone for sonship across the canon. The bridegroom-of-blood episode connects to Genesis 17 (the Abrahamic circumcision) and Joshua 5 (the circumcision at Gilgal before entering the land) — circumcision as the embodied sign without which the deliverer cannot deliver.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Second Temple readings intensified the drama. Jubilees 48 retells the bridegroom-of-blood scene making the attacker Mastema, the satan-figure, trying to prevent Israel's deliverance. The Aramaic Targums (Onkelos, Pseudo-Jonathan) identify the attacker as an angel and clarify that Moses had delayed circumcising Gershom. Josephus (*Antiquities* 2.13) omits the episode — he couldn't figure out what to do with it.

Rabbinic readings in *Exodus Rabbah* and Mekhilta develop the theme. In *Nedarim* 31b–32a, the Talmud explains that Moses delayed circumcision because he calculated it was dangerous to circumcise an infant just before a long journey, and God held him to account for the delay. The rabbis thus made sense of the attack by finding a specific negligence: the covenantal mark took priority over prudential calculation. R. Shimon ben Gamaliel argues the attack was because Moses had not circumcised Eliezer (his second son); R. Yose says it was because he had not circumcised Gershom; the dispute is technical but the underlying reading is consistent — covenantal obedience precedes vocation.

Rashi follows the rabbinic tradition: Moses had delayed circumcision, and God held him responsible. Rashi adds a domestic dimension: Jethro had demanded that Moses commit to raising his sons in Midianite custom, which Zipporah resolved by the immediate circumcision. Ibn Ezra, more philologically cautious, notes the ambiguity of the pronouns and entertains multiple readings. Ramban (Nachmanides) synthesizes: the episode teaches that the deliverer must himself be fully within the covenant before he can deliver those covenanted.

Modern Jewish readings reopen. Nahum Sarna's JPS commentary on Exodus treats verses 24–26 with appropriate caution — he catalogues interpretations and concludes that the scene preserves an ancient tradition whose original sense is likely lost. Jon Levenson, in *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*, reads the scene as a near-filicide that anticipates the tenth plague: the firstborn son is threatened, and blood applied to the body averts the threat — exactly the Passover pattern, in miniature, performed first on the deliverer's own household. Avivah Zornberg, in *Particulars of Rapture*, reads the scene as a rupture in Moses' identity — the man who has spent forty years not-being-Israelite is violently reclaimed for the covenant. The attack at the lodging place is the price of coming back.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** The patristic tradition struggled with the episode. Origen (*Homilies on Exodus* 5) allegorizes: the attack represents the law's wrath against Moses for his own unbelief, and the circumcision represents Christ's flesh. Augustine, in *Contra Faustum* 19, reads the scene as a demonstration that the old covenant's signs are bodily and bloody, requiring what the new covenant fulfills spiritually. Gregory of Nyssa, in *Life of Moses*, reads the whole chapter allegorically: the signs are the soul's powers, the objections are the soul's weaknesses, the bridegroom-of-blood is the soul's purification.

Aquinas, in treating the sacraments of the old covenant (*ST* I-II q. 102 a. 5; III q. 70 on circumcision), reads circumcision as the precursor to baptism — a sign of covenantal incorporation. The bridegroom-of-blood scene reinforces that circumcision is salvifically ordered. Aquinas does not linger on the obscurity of the passage; his framework integrates it into sacramental theology.

Luther and Calvin handle the chapter differently. Luther reads Moses' objections as a reminder that God chooses the unlikely; the signs are for the strengthening of faith, not proof. Calvin, in the *Harmony*, reads Moses' four objections as gradations of sinful unbelief — each further than the last from faith. Calvin's reading is harder on Moses than the rabbinic tradition, which reads the objections as humility. On the bridegroom-of-blood, Calvin treats it soberly: God, having sent Moses, will not tolerate covenantal negligence in the deliverer's own house. Calvin resists allegory.

Critical scholarship from Wellhausen forward parsed the chapter. Gerhard von Rad's *Old Testament Theology* treated the bridegroom-of-blood as an archaic fragment preserving pre-Mosaic ritual. Martin Noth's *Exodus* commentary is cautious. Hugo Gressmann and others argued the episode originally involved a night-demon attack, later theologized. Brevard Childs's *The Book of Exodus* marked the canonical-theological turn — he read the scene as a deliberately compressed narrative whose theological function is clear even if philological resolution is impossible: the deliverer must himself be inside the covenant. Terence Fretheim's *Exodus* reads the episode as divine seriousness about covenant. William Propp's Anchor Bible commentary is the fullest philological treatment — he leans toward reading Moses as the near-victim and circumcision as substitute death (Propp's "apotropaic" reading). Cornelis Houtman's *Historical Commentary on the Old Testament: Exodus* catalogues interpretations in extraordinary depth. Walter Brueggemann reads the chapter as theological realism: deliverers are themselves resistant, even the chosen must be reclaimed, and the deliverer is formed rather than prepackaged.

**Where the trajectories converge and diverge.** Both traditions read Moses' objections as significant theologically, and both find the bridegroom-of-blood passage genuinely difficult. Both find in verse 22 a foundational sonship theology. The divergence concerns circumcision's status. Jewish readings hold circumcision as the enduring covenantal mark — the deliverer must himself be circumcised and must circumcise his son. Christian readings, following Paul, see circumcision as fulfilled in Christ and now superseded by baptism — the bridegroom-of-blood scene becomes typological rather than normative. A second divergence concerns the hardening-of-heart theology first announced at verse 21. Jewish readings (Maimonides) preserve Pharaoh's freedom even within the divine declaration. Reformed Christian readings (Calvin) hold to divine sovereignty more robustly. Both agree the passage is doing theological work rather than offering mechanical prediction.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author believe God was doing in Exodus 4? The chapter's claim is that the deliverer is not self-made, that the signs are God's and not Moses', that the people will believe not because Moses speaks well but because God speaks through him, and that before the deliverance of a people comes the covenantal ordering of the deliverer's own body. Moses is not a hero whose biography was always leading here; he is a reluctant, resistant man whose vocation God has to insist on, and whose household God has to correct before he can bear the commission.

**Tension 1: Moses' four objections — sinful unbelief or proper humility?** In verses 1, 10, and 13 Moses continues to resist. Is this faith's modesty or faithlessness?

- **Catholic teaching** (Aquinas *ST* II-II q. 161 on humility) holds that true humility is a virtue, but false humility — the refusal of a commission one is capable of — is a form of pride disguised. Aquinas would distinguish between Moses' legitimate self-assessment and his avoidance of the charge.
- **Reformed Protestant readings** (Calvin *Harmony*) are harder on Moses. Calvin reads each objection as a further withdrawal from faith, culminating in verse 13's "send someone else" as frank unbelief. The kindling of divine anger in verse 14 is proof of Moses' culpability.
- **Lutheran readings** (Luther's lectures on Exodus) are gentler. Moses resists because he understands the weight of the task, and God accommodates by giving Aaron. The resistance is not damning; it is formative.
- **Wesleyan readings** (Wesley's Notes on the Old Testament) read the objections as gradations requiring divine patience and response; God meets each objection with a specific provision (signs, eloquence, Aaron), showing that divine vocation works with human limitation rather than against it.
- **Jewish readings** (Rashi, Ibn Ezra) typically treat the objections as legitimate until verse 13, at which point Rashi notes God's anger and infers that Moses had crossed a line. The tradition is more sympathetic than Calvin, noting Moses' humility and limitations as genuine before they become evasive.
- **Orthodox readings** (Gregory of Nyssa) allegorize: the objections are the soul's stages of self-knowledge on the way to theosis. Each resistance is a deepening encounter with the divine.
- **Critical readings** (Fretheim, Brueggemann) read the objections as theologically realistic — God works with resistant prophets because that is the kind of creature God made.

Steelman the least intuitive: the Calvinist reading. It seems unduly harsh to someone shaped by modern therapeutic sensibilities. But Calvin is doing real theological work. He refuses to romanticize Moses as a humble hero and insists that any resistance to clear divine commission is, theologically, a form of unbelief. The text itself supports Calvin by recording that "the anger of the LORD was kindled against Moses" — a phrase the narrator does not use lightly. Calvin's reading takes the text's own verdict seriously.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter seems to hold both: Moses' objections are in part legitimate limitation and in part evasion, and God's response — anger, then provision — treats both dimensions.

**Tension 2: The bridegroom-of-blood episode and divine violence against the mediator.** In verses 24–26, the LORD seeks to kill the very man he has just commissioned. How is this theologically intelligible?

- **Catholic teaching** (Aquinas on divine providence; Catechism §§302–314) holds that God does not contradict himself, so the attack must be read as pedagogical rather than genuine threat. Aquinas would say the commission is secure, but Moses' unreadiness (uncircumcised household) must be resolved.
- **Reformed Protestant readings** (Calvin on the passage) take the threat as real: God is holy, and holiness will not tolerate covenantal negligence even in the chosen. Calvin reads the scene as a lesson that God's commission does not exempt from God's commands.
- **Lutheran readings** (Luther) emphasize the law/gospel dialectic: Moses is confronted with the law's demand in his own body before he can preach the gospel of deliverance.
- **Jewish readings** (Rabbinic, Rashi, Ramban) read the attack as consequential: Moses had delayed circumcision, and the consequence materialized. Zipporah's quick action is both practical and theologically decisive — a Midianite woman performs the covenantal rite.
- **Orthodox readings** (patristic) tend toward typology: the blood applied to the body prefigures Christ's blood, and the near-death-and-deliverance prefigures Passover.
- **Critical readings** (Childs, Propp) read the episode as an archaic ritual fragment preserved because it carried the theology of apotropaic blood — blood that wards off divine wrath, which will become central to Passover.

Steelman the least intuitive: the critical reading that the passage preserves an archaic, pre-theological ritual whose original meaning has been lost. This refuses the harmonizing move of both Catholic and Reformed readings. It acknowledges that the Hebrew Bible preserves older strata whose original coherence has not survived, and reads the passage as a testimony to Israel's layered religious history. The theological effect is still serious: the deliverer passes through near-death and blood application, setting the narrative pattern for Passover.

**Where this leaves us:** The text insists on the covenantal seriousness of circumcision, on blood as the sign that averts divine wrath, and on Zipporah as unexpected covenantal actor. The passage resists full resolution precisely because it is old.

One unresolved question: If the deliverer had to be nearly killed and then saved by his wife's ritual initiative, what does that mean for every later deliverance? Does Israel's every rescuer pass first through a lodging place in the dark?

## The Thread

**Genesis 17:9–14 — The Abrahamic circumcision.** The covenantal sign whose omission nearly costs Moses his life. When you reach Joshua 5 and the circumcision at Gilgal before entering the land, remember this lodging place — Israel cannot enter the land uncircumcised any more than Moses could enter Egypt uncircumcised.

**Exodus 12:7, 13 — The blood on the doorposts.** Zipporah touches Moses' (or Gershom's) feet/genitals with blood, and the destroyer passes. At Passover, blood on the doorposts causes the destroyer to pass over. The pattern is established at the lodging place and completed on the eve of deliverance.

**Romans 9:4 — "Theirs is the adoption as sons."** Paul picks up verse 22's "Israel is my firstborn son" and calls it *huiothesia* — adoption. The sonship language runs through Paul and is expanded in Galatians 4 and Romans 8.

**Matthew 2:15 — "Out of Egypt I called my son."** Matthew quotes Hosea 11:1, which reads Exodus 4:22 as template. Jesus recapitulates Israel's sonship. When you reach the infancy narratives, remember that "firstborn son" language begins at Moses' lodging place.
