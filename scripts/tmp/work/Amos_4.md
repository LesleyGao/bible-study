# Amos 4: Cows of Bashan and the Refrain of Refusal

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Amos 4 is structured around three of the prophet's most rhetorically arresting moves: the address to the "cows of Bashan" (vv. 1-3), the bitter parody of cultic invitation at Bethel and Gilgal (vv. 4-5), and the fivefold refrain "yet you did not return to me" (vv. 6-12). The chapter combines social critique, cultic satire, and a theology of failed pedagogy in a way that became foundational for subsequent prophetic-theological literature.

The "cows of Bashan" address (v. 1) is one of the most discussed openings in the prophetic corpus. *Bashan* (בָּשָׁן) was the fertile region east of the Sea of Galilee and Jordan, the area corresponding roughly to modern Golan Heights and southern Syria. The region was famous for its rich pastures and the size and quality of its cattle (Deuteronomy 32:14, Psalm 22:12, Ezekiel 39:18). Amos's metaphor — naming the elite women of Samaria as "cows of Bashan" — exploits the region's reputation: the elite Samarian women are well-fed, sleek, indulged, and demanding. The verse continues: *"who oppress the poor, who crush the needy, who say to your husbands, 'Bring, that we may drink!'"* The accusation is not that the women themselves directly oppressed the poor (eighth-century elite women's economic and political agency was limited), but that they participated in and demanded the fruits of their husbands' oppression. The wine they drank was paid for by the violence the chapter has documented in 2:6-8 and 3:9-10.

The metaphor's gendered force has occasioned substantial scholarly comment. Mays (OTL), Shalom Paul (Hermeneia), Andersen and Freedman (Anchor), and Jörg Jeremias have all noted the question of whether the metaphor singles out women for an injustice equally produced by men or names a specific class of elite Samarian women whose participation in oppression was real — and they observe that Amos elsewhere addresses the male elite in equally withering terms (especially chapter 6's "those who lie on beds of ivory"). The cows-of-Bashan verse is one panel of a broader prophetic indictment of the entire elite class, and the metaphor's bite — sleek, well-fed cattle who demand the wine their husbands' oppression buys — works because Bashan's pasture-fed herds were a byword for indulgence.

Verse 2's "they shall take you away with hooks, even the last of you with fishhooks" describes a specific Assyrian deportation practice. The Assyrian royal reliefs — particularly Sennacherib's Lachish reliefs from his palace at Nineveh, recovered by Henry Layard in the 1840s and now in the British Museum — depict captives being led away on hooks through their lips or noses, sometimes with rope or chain attached. The image was a standard Assyrian humiliation of conquered populations. Amos's verse, written before the Assyrian destruction of Samaria in 722 BCE, predicts the practice with grim precision.

Verses 4-5's parodic invitation — *"Come to Bethel, and transgress; to Gilgal, and multiply transgression"* — uses the standard liturgical-pilgrimage vocabulary of cultic invitation. Pilgrims came to the sanctuaries to offer sacrifices, tithes, and freewill offerings. Amos parodies the priestly invitation, substituting *peshe'u* (transgress / rebel) for the expected *zibchu* (sacrifice). The audience would have heard the parody immediately: the prophet has inverted the cultic call. The further specifics — bringing sacrifices "every morning" and tithes "every three days" rather than the standard schedules (annual or seasonal) — extend the parody. Israel was multiplying cultic activity to a degree the law did not require, and Amos says they were multiplying transgression. The chapter's verse 5 names the underlying problem: *"for so you love to do, O people of Israel"* — the cultic activity was not failure of compliance but excess of self-pleasing performance.

Verses 6-11's fivefold catalog of disasters that did not produce *teshuvah* lists: famine ("cleanness of teeth... lack of bread"), drought (selective rain), agricultural blight (locusts and mildew), plague and military defeat ("pestilence after the manner of Egypt"), and Sodom-like overthrow. Each disaster ends with the refrain: *"yet you did not return to me, declares the LORD."* The structure performs the chapter's theology: divine pedagogy operating through historical disaster, repeatedly attempted, repeatedly refused.

Verse 11's "as when God overthrew Sodom and Gomorrah" invokes Genesis 19's cataclysmic destruction. The Hebrew *kemahpekhat* (כְּמַהְפֵּכַת, "as the overthrow") is the technical term for the Sodom-Gomorrah destruction (Deuteronomy 29:23, Isaiah 13:19, Jeremiah 49:18, 50:40). Amos says some of Israel had already experienced Sodom-like destruction — perhaps a reference to a specific eighth-century Levantine catastrophe, perhaps the earthquake of 1:1, perhaps a generic prophetic memory. The verse names Israel as "a brand plucked out of the burning" — barely saved from the fire. Yet even this near-miss did not produce return.

Verse 12's *"prepare to meet your God, O Israel"* is the chapter's terrifying climax. The Hebrew *hikkon liqra't elohekha* (הִכּוֹן לִקְרַאת אֱלֹהֶיךָ) uses vocabulary of preparation for an encounter — the same verb used of Moses preparing the people to meet God at Sinai (Exodus 19:11, 15). The eighth-century audience that had multiplied cultic activity at Bethel and Gilgal is now to prepare for the encounter their cultic activity had not produced.

Five Hebrew terms govern the chapter. *Parot habashan* (פָּרוֹת הַבָּשָׁן, v. 1), "cows of Bashan." *Pesha'* (פֶּשַׁע, v. 4), "transgression" — recurring from chapter 1. *Niqyon shinayim* (נִקְיוֹן שִׁנָּיִם, v. 6), "cleanness of teeth" — Amos's striking metaphor for famine (no food residue between the teeth). *Shuv* (שׁוּב, vv. 6, 8, 9, 10, 11), "return" — the fivefold refrain's verb. *Hikkon liqra't elohekha* (הִכּוֹן לִקְרַאת אֱלֹהֶיךָ, v. 12), "prepare to meet your God."

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter's structure has three distinct movements joined by thematic continuity. Verses 1-3: the address to the cows of Bashan, with its prediction of deportation. Verses 4-5: the parodic cultic invitation. Verses 6-12: the disaster-pedagogy refrain culminating in the call to prepare for divine encounter. Verse 13 stands as a doxological coda, the first of three doxologies in the book (also at 5:8-9 and 9:5-6) that some critical commentators read as later editorial additions structuring the book.

The fivefold "yet you did not return to me" refrain is one of the most carefully constructed structural features in the prophetic corpus. The refrain appears in identical form at vv. 6, 8, 9, 10, 11 — five times following five different disasters. The cumulative effect is overwhelming: each disaster was a divine call; each call went unanswered. The chapter's theology of failed pedagogy is built on this repetition.

The chapter's parody of cultic invitation in vv. 4-5 is a remarkable rhetorical achievement. The Hebrew imperatives *"Come... transgress... multiply transgression... bring... offer... proclaim... publish"* mimic the standard priestly call to worship while substituting transgression-vocabulary at the central verb. The parody only works if the audience knows the standard liturgical formulas; the more religiously knowledgeable the listener, the more bitter the satire. The verse names a specific theological pathology: the audience that loves cultic activity precisely because it loves to perform — the worship is self-pleasing, not LORD-pleasing.

Verse 13's doxology shifts the chapter into hymnic register. *"For behold, he who forms the mountains and creates the wind, and declares to man what is his thought, who makes the morning darkness, and treads on the heights of the earth — the LORD, the God of hosts, is his name!"* The verse uses creation-vocabulary that resonates with Genesis 1 and the broader Hebrew creation poetry (Psalm 104, Isaiah 40). The placement is theologically pointed: the Israel that has multiplied cultic activity at Bethel and Gilgal is reminded that the LORD they claim to worship is the cosmic creator. Their cult has reduced the LORD to a manageable sanctuary-deity; the doxology reasserts the LORD's vastness.

The "declares to man what is his thought" of v. 13 (*umaggid le'adam mah-secho*, וּמַגִּיד לְאָדָם מַה־שֵּׂחוֹ) has been variously translated. Some readings: "declares to man what is his thought" (the LORD reveals to man God's own thought); "declares to man what his (the man's) thought is" (the LORD discloses to man the secret of man's own heart). The Hebrew *secho* can refer to either party's thought; the ambiguity is theologically generative. Either reading works in context: the LORD whose voice has been ignored is the LORD who can disclose either his own counsel or the human heart.

The chapter's voice is divine first-person throughout (with the exception of v. 13's third-person doxology). The grammatical persistence of "I gave... I withheld... I struck... I sent... I overthrew... I will do this to you" presents the LORD's first-person testimony of his pedagogical attempts. The cumulative effect is one of weary divine frustration: the LORD has tried multiple pedagogical strategies; none has produced the response the divine teacher sought.

The Hebrew text of Amos 4 is generally stable. Verse 3's "Harmon" (or "Hermon" in some readings) has occasioned debate; the place-name is not securely identified. Verse 13's doxology has been the subject of compositional discussion (whether original or editorial), but the text itself is preserved consistently across witnesses.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Amos 4's reception history has clustered around the cows-of-Bashan address, the parodic cultic invitation, and the fivefold refrain.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

Targum Jonathan paraphrases the cows-of-Bashan address with explicit historical specificity, identifying the women as the wealthy of Samaria. The Aramaic paraphrase has historicized the metaphor while preserving its critical force.

Rashi reads vv. 1-3 with characteristic precision: the elite women of Samaria are addressed for their participation in the broader injustice. Ibn Ezra develops the philological precision of the Bashan-cattle metaphor. Radak emphasizes the chapter's structure: the social critique, the cultic parody, the disaster-pedagogy, the call to encounter form a coherent theological argument.

The medieval Jewish reading of the parodic cultic invitation in vv. 4-5 became foundational for the rabbinic critique of *kavanah*-less worship (worship without proper intentionality). The tradition that worship requires both halakhic correctness and inner intentionality drew partly on Amos's parody of Israelite worship at Bethel and Gilgal.

Maimonides (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 5-7) integrates the fivefold refrain into his theology of *teshuvah*: the LORD provides multiple opportunities for return; failure to use these opportunities heightens accountability. The chapter's pedagogical structure shapes the rabbinic understanding of how divine discipline operates over time.

The Hasidic tradition (Levi Yitzhak of Berditchev, the Sefat Emet) developed verse 12's "prepare to meet your God" as a daily-spiritual discipline. The verse became part of the language of *avodah* (service) — the soul's daily preparation to encounter the LORD.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

Patristic readings developed Amos 4 in moral-ascetic mode. Origen, Chrysostom, and Ambrose all cite the cows-of-Bashan address in homilies on the spiritual dangers of luxury. The verse became part of the patristic vocabulary of asceticism.

Calvin's *Commentary on the Twelve* gives chapter 4 a sustained Reformed reading. Calvin emphasizes the parodic cultic invitation as evidence that orthodox liturgical practice does not exempt a community from prophetic critique. Calvin reads the chapter as a model for Reformed self-examination: a community that loves its worship may be loving the wrong thing.

The Wesleyan-evangelical tradition drew on verse 12's "prepare to meet your God" as a foundational call-to-conversion text. Charles Wesley's hymns include direct echoes of the verse. The phrase has become proverbial in evangelical preaching as the urgent summons to readiness for divine encounter.

The fivefold refrain's pedagogical theology has been particularly influential in Reformed and Puritan literature on providence and divine discipline. Cotton Mather's *Magnalia Christi Americana* (1702) and the broader Puritan reading of providence drew on the chapter's structure. The conviction that disasters can be theological pedagogy — and that the failure to learn from them deepens guilt — descends partly from this lineage.

The contemporary critical commentary (Mays OTL, Shalom Paul Hermeneia, Jörg Jeremias, John Barton, Walter Brueggemann, J. Andrew Dearman, John Goldingay) has engaged the chapter's social, cultic, and historical-pedagogical integration. Brueggemann's *The Prophetic Imagination* and Abraham Heschel's *The Prophets* both read the chapter as a model of prophetic critique that does not separate the elite's social ethics from their religious practice — the well-fed women of Samaria, the multiplied sacrifices at Bethel, and the unheeded disasters are a single indictment.

### Where the Traditions Converge and Diverge

Both traditions take Amos 4 as foundational for prophetic critique of orthodox worship-without-justice. Both develop the fivefold refrain as a theology of divine pedagogy through disaster. Both engage verse 12 as a foundational summons to readiness for encounter.

They diverge less on chapter 4 than on more theologically loaded chapters of Amos. The chapter's integrated critique of social, cultic, and historical patterns is shared territory; the differences emerge on broader theological frameworks.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author's vision is the disconnection between cultic vigor and covenant faithfulness. The chapter's deepest theological move is the assertion that Israel could be multiplying religious activity *and* failing the covenant simultaneously. The chapter does not call for cessation of cultic activity; it calls for *teshuvah* that the cultic activity has substituted for.

Two genuine tensions emerge.

**Tension 1: Worship and ethics.**

- *Catholic* (CCC 2099-2100, on the offering of sacrifice; the social-encyclical tradition): The Eucharist forms communities into the *hesed* the prophet demanded. The integration of liturgy and ethics is the Catholic theological achievement; either alone is insufficient.
- *Reformed*: The Reformed regulative principle of worship — that worship must be matched by covenant practice — descends partly from this lineage. Calvin's *Institutes* on prayer and worship (book 3) integrates these.
- *Lutheran*: The chapter operates in law-mode against false security in cultic practice. The law's word is preserved; the gospel does not abolish it.
- *Anabaptist / Mennonite (Bonhoeffer's *Cost of Discipleship*)*: The chapter warrants the radical-discipleship critique of religiosity-without-discipleship. Cheap grace is the love of religious activity that does not transform life.
- *Prophetic-ethics tradition (Mays OTL, Shalom Paul Hermeneia, Brueggemann, Heschel)*: The chapter is foundational for prophetic critique of liturgical communities that have lost contact with the marginalized. Worship that does not address economic injustice is condemned; the multiplied sacrifices at Bethel are not piety but self-pleasing performance.
- *Jewish (rabbinic-mussar tradition)*: The integration of *avodah* (service / worship) with *gemilut chasadim* (acts of *hesed*) is the rabbinic pattern. The verse's parody of empty cult is foundational for the *mussar* tradition's emphasis on inward formation.

**Steelmanning the position I find least intuitive**: the strict liturgical-traditionalist reading that the chapter does not condemn liturgical practice as such but only its abuse. The temptation in critical-prophetic readings is to dismiss the suggestion that the chapter has anything positive to say about Bethel and Gilgal worship. But the chapter's grammar is precise: the LORD does not abolish the practice of pilgrimage and sacrifice; the LORD says Israel "loves to do" them — meaning loves them as performance. The careful reading does not require abandoning liturgical-traditional Christianity or Judaism; it requires asking whether the love of the practice has displaced what the practice was meant to embody. The reading preserves both the tradition and its prophetic critique.

**Where this leaves us**: The chapter does not abolish liturgy; it tests it. Communities can remain liturgically active while failing what liturgy was meant to form.

**Tension 2: Disaster as pedagogy.**

- *Catholic*: Divine providence sometimes operates through difficult historical-natural events; the chapter warrants reading history theologically while preserving humility about specific interpretations.
- *Reformed* (Calvin's doctrine of providence): God ordains all things including disasters; the disasters are real divine pedagogy; the believer reads them with sober attention.
- *Lutheran*: Disasters are God's "alien work" (*opus alienum*) — what God does that is not God's "proper work" (*opus proprium*) of grace, but is preserved as preparation for grace.
- *Anabaptist / Mennonite*: The chapter warrants careful reading of history while resisting confident specific claims about divine intent in any particular disaster.
- *Prophetic-ethics tradition (Mays, Heschel, Brueggemann)*: Reading history theologically must include attention to who suffers in the disasters and who profits from them. The chapter's pedagogical theology is not a justification for suffering but a call to read it with prophetic care.
- *Jewish (Maimonides, contemporary)*: The chapter's pedagogical theology is part of the tradition's resources for reading Jewish history; the post-Holocaust theological tradition has wrestled with whether the framework can be sustained in light of twentieth-century catastrophe.
- *Critical*: The chapter's pedagogical theology reflects ancient Israelite assumptions about divine action through history; the contemporary reader must read with critical awareness of how such frameworks operate.

**Where this leaves us**: The chapter's pedagogical theology is real; its contemporary application requires discernment.

The unresolved question the author leaves: *What does it mean to "prepare to meet your God"?* The chapter does not specify the preparation. It names the necessity. The audience that has multiplied cultic activity is told their cultic activity has not been preparation. Something else is required. The chapter does not name what that something else is. The reader is left with the urgent demand to prepare and without a manual for the preparation.

## The Thread

- **Genesis 19** — The destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah. Amos 4:11's "as when God overthrew Sodom and Gomorrah" presupposes the narrative. Read together to feel the weight of the comparison.
- **Exodus 19:10-15** — Moses preparing the people to meet God at Sinai. The verb in v. 12 (*hikkon*) is the same verb used in Exodus 19. The chapter's call to "prepare to meet your God" invokes the Sinai pattern.
- **Hosea 6:4-6** — The LORD's complaint that Israel's *hesed* is morning-cloud thin and that the LORD desires *hesed* more than sacrifice. The eighth-century prophetic critique of cult-without-covenant is shared between Hosea and Amos.
- **Hebrews 12:25-29** — "Therefore let us be grateful for receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken, and thus let us offer to God acceptable worship, with reverence and awe, for our God is a consuming fire." The Hebrew letter's integration of worship and the consuming-fire God descends partly from the prophetic-Sinai tradition Amos represents. When you reach Hebrews 12, remember Amos 4's "prepare to meet your God."
