# Revelation 11: Two Witnesses and the Kingdom That Has Come

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 11 forces the dating debate to its sharpest point. The dominant scholarly consensus — Bauckham, Aune, Koester, Mounce, Beale, Osborne — places Revelation in the closing years of Domitian's reign (c. 95–96 AD). The minority early-date hypothesis (J. A. T. Robinson, Kenneth Gentry, in different forms) places it before AD 70, sometimes specifically during the Jewish War of 66–70. Chapter 11 is where the choice carries the most exegetical weight, because the chapter centers on the *temple of God* (11:1) and *the great city, where their Lord was crucified* (11:8). On the early-date reading, John is measuring the still-standing Jerusalem temple and prophesying its imminent destruction. On the late-date reading, John is writing twenty-five years after the temple's destruction in 70 AD, and the temple of 11:1 must therefore be a heavenly or symbolic temple, with the "great city" of 11:8 functioning as a coded reference whose identity has provoked centuries of debate.

The material culture is layered. Roman siege warfare against Jerusalem in 70 AD, executed by Titus and detailed by Josephus in *The Jewish War*, provides the most plausible historical referent for the trampling of the holy city — whether the chapter describes it prospectively (early date) or retrospectively (late date). The temple itself, before its destruction, was the largest sacred precinct in the Mediterranean, with its outer Court of the Gentiles open to non-Jews and its inner courts progressively restricted. The detail in 11:2 — "the court outside the temple, leave that out, for it is given over to the nations" — reproduces this architectural distinction precisely. *Hagia polis* (ἁγία πόλις, "holy city," 11:2) is a standard Septuagintal designation for Jerusalem (Isaiah 48:2, 52:1; Daniel 9:24; Nehemiah 11:1, 18). The "trampling" vocabulary echoes Daniel 8:13 (*synpatēsthēsetai*) and the broader prophetic-apocalyptic tradition of profanation.

The two witnesses are introduced with deliberate intertextual density. They prophesy "for 1,260 days, clothed in sackcloth" (11:3) — the numerical formula equivalent to 42 months (11:2) and to "a time, times, and half a time" (12:14), all denoting half of seven, the broken time of suffering. They are "the two olive trees and the two lampstands that stand before the Lord of the earth" (11:4), a direct citation of Zechariah 4:1–14, where Joshua the high priest and Zerubbabel the governor are figured as the two olive trees beside the lampstand. Their signs match Moses (turning waters to blood, striking with plagues — Exodus 7:14–25) and Elijah (shutting the sky to prevent rain — 1 Kings 17:1; calling fire from heaven — 2 Kings 1:10–12). The figure of two witnesses also has Mosaic legal resonance: Deuteronomy 17:6 and 19:15 require two witnesses for capital cases, a principle Jesus himself reproduces (Matthew 18:16) and Paul cites (2 Corinthians 13:1).

The "great city" of 11:8 is "symbolically called Sodom and Egypt, where their Lord was crucified." The most natural reading is Jerusalem, since Jesus was crucified there. But the symbolic naming as Sodom (Isaiah 1:9–10's address to Jerusalem) and Egypt (the archetypal place of bondage) creates a layered identification. Some interpreters (Aune, Beale partially) extend the reference to include Rome, on the basis that the "great city" elsewhere in Revelation (chapters 17–18) clearly designates Babylon/Rome. Bauckham develops a both-and reading: the great city is Jerusalem-historically and Babylon-typologically. For the late-date hypothesis, the question of whether John could have called destroyed Jerusalem "the great city" in 95 AD has been answered by Bauckham, Beale, and Koester through the recognition that the chapter mixes historical and typological registers.

Three Greek terms anchor the chapter. *Naos* (ναός, 11:1, 2, 19), the inner sanctuary of the temple, distinguished from *hieron* (the broader temple complex) — John's specific use of *naos* for what is measured signals the inner shrine, not the outer courts. *Marturōn* (μαρτύρων, 11:3, "witnesses") carries both legal-juridical weight (testimony in court) and the developing Christian sense of "martyr" (witness unto death) that becomes formalized in the second century. *Pneuma zōēs* (πνεῦμα ζωῆς, "breath of life," 11:11), echoing Genesis 2:7's *neshamat ḥayyim* and Ezekiel 37:5's vision of the dry bones — the witnesses' resurrection is patterned on Ezekiel's valley.

The manuscript witness for chapter 11 is again strong: P47 covers the chapter (P47 contains Revelation 9:10–17:2). Variants in the chapter are mostly minor. One meaning-affecting variant: at 11:1 some witnesses include the verb *kai histēkei ho angelos legōn* ("and the angel stood saying"), introducing an angelic figure as the speaker; other witnesses have only *legōn* ("saying") with no specified speaker. The shorter reading is supported by P47, Sinaiticus, and Alexandrinus and is printed in NA28; the longer reading appears in some Byzantine manuscripts and is reflected in the KJV. The variant shifts whether the chapter has a continuous angelic frame or whether the speaker remains unspecified.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre considerations remain the same: *apokalypsis*, *prophēteia*, *epistolē*. Chapter 11 is the most literarily concentrated chapter in the trumpet septet and arguably in the whole book. It contains the second part of the second interlude (the first part was chapter 10), the third woe announcement (11:14), the seventh trumpet (11:15–19), and the heavenly response that climaxes the cycle.

The literary structure is precise. Verses 1–13 form the unit on the two witnesses and the great city; verses 14–19 form the unit on the seventh trumpet. The chapter therefore *closes* the trumpet septet (which began in 8:6) and simultaneously sets up the central section of the book (chapters 12–14 on the cosmic conflict, chapters 15–18 on the bowls and Babylon, chapters 19–22 on the consummation).

The two-witnesses passage (11:3–13) is constructed by intertextual fusion at exceptional density. Five sources operate simultaneously. (1) Zechariah 4:1–14 is the explicit source for the olive trees and lampstands. (2) Exodus 7:14–25 and Moses-typology supply the plague-power. (3) 1 Kings 17 and 2 Kings 1 and Elijah-typology supply the rain-and-fire power. (4) Daniel 7:25 and 12:7 supply the half-of-seven temporal formulas (1260 days = 42 months = 3.5 years). (5) Ezekiel 37 supplies the breath-of-life resurrection pattern. The witnesses are therefore *typological* figures — they incarnate the prophetic vocation in continuity with Moses, Elijah, Joshua, Zerubbabel, and Ezekiel. This is the key interpretive move: the chapter does not flatten its witnesses into one source but compounds them, producing a typology that exceeds any individual identification.

The seventh trumpet (11:15–19) is a hymn-and-doxology unit, structured by the chorus-of-elders pattern that Bauckham analyzed extensively in *The Climax of Prophecy*. The hymn announces "The kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ, and he shall reign forever and ever" (11:15), which Handel set as the climax of *Messiah*. Critically, this is *not* the end of the visions: 11:19 opens the heavenly temple and shows the ark of the covenant, but chapters 12–22 follow. Bauckham, Beale, and Boring all argue that the seventh trumpet inaugurates rather than concludes the eschatological consummation — the rest of the book is the unfolding of what 11:15 announces.

The structural placement is decisive. Chapter 11 is the *midpoint* of the book by content: chapters 1–11 are the inaugural visions through the seven trumpets, chapters 12–22 are the cosmic-political battle and consummation. Many structural analyses (Bauckham, Beale, Koester, Boring) treat 11:19's opened heavenly temple as the structural pivot — the inner sanctuary visible from below at the moment when the kingdom has come.

The literary device of the *symbolic city* is foregrounded in 11:8. John tells us explicitly that "the great city … is symbolically [*pneumatikōs*] called Sodom and Egypt, where their Lord was crucified." The adverb *pneumatikōs* (πνευματικῶς) is rare and important: it marks the city as available for spiritual-typological reading. This is the chapter's most explicit acknowledgment that its symbolic register is multi-layered.

Comparison to non-canonical apocalypses sharpens the literary identification. 4 Ezra 11–12 contains a vision of an eagle (Rome) trampling the earth and being destroyed. 2 Baruch's later chapters describe the heavenly Jerusalem and the temple of God. The Sibylline Oracles 4–5 contain similar two-witness or prophetic-confrontation motifs. The Lives of the Prophets — a Second Temple Jewish text — develops Elijah's expected return as part of an eschatological prophetic mission. The combat-myth resonance, while less prominent than in chapter 12, is present in the rising of the beast from the abyss (11:7) — the first explicit appearance of the beast that will dominate chapters 13 onward.

A meaning-affecting textual variant at 11:17: some witnesses add *kai ho erchomenos* ("and who is to come") to the formula "Lord God Almighty, who is and who was." Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus, and P47 omit the addition, which is the harder reading and is printed in NA28. The omission is theologically significant: at the moment the kingdom has come, "who is to come" drops out — the future has become present.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Chapter 11's interpretive history is among the most contested in Christian theology, primarily because of the two witnesses.

The Patristic tradition was diverse. Justin Martyr, Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 5.30), Tertullian, and Hippolytus all read the two witnesses as Enoch and Elijah, returning at the end to confront the antichrist. The Enoch-Elijah identification has support in Jewish traditions about the eschatological return of Enoch (1 Enoch 90:31) and Elijah (Malachi 4:5). Origen reads the chapter allegorically — the witnesses are figures of the Old Testament and the New Testament prophetic word. Victorinus of Pettau identifies the witnesses as Elijah and Jeremiah, drawing on a Jewish tradition that Jeremiah would also return. Tyconius the Donatist reads the witnesses ecclesially: they represent the church bearing witness across history. Augustine in *City of God* 20 absorbs Tyconius's recapitulative reading.

The Eastern Orthodox tradition followed the Enoch-Elijah identification through Andrew of Caesarea's commentary, which became the standard Greek reading. The chapter has not been read in Byzantine liturgy, but the Enoch-Elijah eschatology is preserved in Orthodox iconography and patristic florilegia.

The medieval West engaged the chapter through Joachim of Fiore. Joachim and his followers read the 1260 days and 42 months as periodizations marking historical eras. Some Spiritual Franciscans identified the two witnesses with Francis and Dominic; some later medievals identified them with Pope Sylvester and Constantine, or with various reform figures. Nicholas of Lyra read the chapter historicistically, identifying the witnesses with major medieval prophetic figures. Aquinas treated the chapter only briefly.

The Reformation produced sharp identifications. Luther read the witnesses as figures of the gospel preached against the papacy and identified the "great city" with Rome (which Luther polemically equated with Babylon). Calvin did not comment on Revelation. Reformed historicism (Joseph Mede, Jonathan Edwards) developed the two witnesses as figures of faithful gospel preaching across church history. Anabaptist apocalypticism, especially in Münster (1534–35), read the witnesses as the radical reformers themselves.

The 19th century produced dispensationalism's distinctive contribution. John Nelson Darby and the Scofield Reference Bible read the two witnesses as *literal future figures* during a literal seven-year tribulation. Some dispensationalists (Walvoord, LaHaye in *Revelation Unveiled*) identify them as Moses and Elijah literally returned; others identify them as Enoch and Elijah; still others leave them as two future literal figures whose identity is undetermined. The reading must be steelmanned: it has the virtue of taking the text's plain force seriously — the witnesses prophesy, perform signs, are killed by the beast, and are physically resurrected after 3.5 days. The dispensationalist account at least faces these as events to be accounted for.

The 20th-century critical mainstream — Charles, Caird, Beasley-Murray, Sweet, Boring, Bauckham, Beale, Aune, Mounce, Osborne, Koester — has converged on a typological-ecclesial reading. The two witnesses are not Moses and Elijah literally; they are *Moses-and-Elijah-typed* figures who incarnate the church's prophetic witness. Bauckham's *The Climax of Prophecy* develops this with particular force: the witnesses represent the entire prophetic community of the church, and the chapter narrates the church's mission, martyrdom, and vindication during the period of its earthly witness.

The anti-imperial reading in the mainstream commentary tradition (Bauckham, Caird, Koester, Beale, Osborne, Ian Paul, Aune) takes the witnesses as figures of the prophetic-political community resisting empire. The 1260 days of sackcloth-clothed prophecy is the time of the church's testimony against the Beast; the killing in the great city is the political martyrdom that empire metes out; the resurrection is the vindication of God against the powers — and the chapter, on this reading, constitutes the witnessing community's very identity through its willingness to bear testimony unto death. The witnesses are the church *as* witnessing community, and the chapter is the seer's compressed account of what that community does in every age — testify, suffer, and be raised. Within that consensus, Boesak's *Comfort and Protest* and Brian Blount's *Revelation* press the chapter's contemporary political application.

N. T. Wright reads chapter 11 in *Revelation for Everyone* (2011) and *How God Became King* (2012) as one of the hinges of the whole book. The two witnesses, for Wright, are the church-shaped-by-Moses-and-Elijah — the prophetic people of God whose testimony, like Jesus' own, leads through suffering and apparent defeat ("their dead bodies will lie in the street of the great city … where their Lord was crucified") to vindication and resurrection. And the seventh trumpet's declaration — "the kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ" — is, on Wright's reading, the announcement that Jesus' bodily resurrection and enthronement have *already* changed the world's true government; the chapters that follow (12–22) do not delay that kingdom but narrate how it works its way out against the dragon and the beasts. This is Wright's standing argument that the cross and resurrection inaugurated God's reign on earth, "heaven coming to earth," rather than postponing it to a future timetable.

Jewish scholarly engagement focuses on the chapter's saturation in Hebrew Bible source-texts. Adela Yarbro Collins's *Combat Myth* attends to the rising of the beast from the abyss as a combat-myth element. Loren Stuckenbruck's work on Second Temple apocalyptic recovers the Watchers-and-resurrection backdrop of the witnesses' three-and-a-half-day death-and-resurrection. John J. Collins's *The Apocalyptic Imagination* compares Revelation 11 with 4 Ezra and 2 Baruch's eschatologies. Daniel Boyarin's work on Christian-Jewish boundaries reads chapter 11's temple imagery as continuous with Jewish post-70 reflection on the destroyed sanctuary.

Where the traditions converge: chapter 11 is about prophetic witness under conditions of persecution, with vindication coming through resurrection. Where they diverge sharply: whether the chapter describes literal future figures (dispensationalist), the church's historical witness (idealist-ecclesial mainstream), specific medieval or reformation figures (historicist), or a typological figure of prophetic vocation (Bauckham, Beale, Koester).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the seer believe God was doing? Two tensions deserve sustained attention.

**Tension 1: The identity of the two witnesses.** This is the chapter's most contested theological-exegetical question and it forces every interpretive school into a definite answer.

The Catholic tradition through most of its history has read the witnesses as Enoch and Elijah literally, following Irenaeus and the patristic mainstream. Modern Catholic biblical scholarship (Raymond Brown, the Pontifical Biblical Commission) has shifted toward an ecclesial-typological reading: the witnesses are figures of the church's prophetic witness, in continuity with Moses-and-Elijah typology. CCC §675 affirms a final trial for the church but does not commit to specific identifications.

Reformed readings have been varied. The historicist mainstream (Joseph Mede, Jonathan Edwards) read the witnesses as figures of gospel preaching across church history. Modern Reformed scholarship (Beale's NIGTC, Vern Poythress) has shifted toward an ecclesial-idealist reading: the witnesses are the prophetic church bearing witness in every age.

Lutheran readings, in the inheritance of Luther's polemic against Rome, often took the witnesses as figures of evangelical preaching against papal corruption. Modern Lutheran scholarship has moved toward more measured readings.

Dispensationalist readings remain committed to the literal-future identification. The witnesses are two literal individuals — usually identified as Moses and Elijah, sometimes Enoch and Elijah, sometimes left undetermined — who will appear during a future seven-year tribulation. This is the position to steelman. Its strength is that it preserves the chapter's narrative concreteness: the witnesses prophesy specific signs, are killed by a specific beast in a specific city, and are physically resurrected after a specific period. The non-dispensationalist alternatives — the witnesses are typological, ecclesial, or symbolic — must explain why John would describe them with such narrative specificity if no specific events are intended. The dispensationalist answer (specific events are intended, just future) is internally coherent.

Eastern Orthodox readings preserve the patristic Enoch-Elijah identification but treat it within a liturgical-eschatological frame, not a calendrical one. The witnesses are figures of the eschatological prophetic confrontation with antichrist, but the timetable is not predicted.

Critical readings (Bauckham, Beale, Koester, Aune, Boring, Caird, Mounce, Osborne) converge on the typological-ecclesial reading. The witnesses are not two literal individuals but figures of the prophetic church, with Moses-and-Elijah typology drawn from Zechariah 4 and Old Testament prophetic tradition. Bauckham's reading is the most influential: the witnesses are the church-as-prophetic-community, doing during the 1260 days exactly what the church does in every age — bearing witness, suffering martyrdom, and being vindicated by resurrection.

Where this leaves us: the chapter's specificity resists collapse into pure symbolism, but its dense typological saturation resists collapse into pure literalism. The dispensationalist and the idealist readings both have something the text demands and something the text resists. The witness imagery does specific work — it incarnates the church's vocation in concrete prophetic-typological figures — and any reading that flattens this specificity loses the chapter's force.

**Tension 2: The kingdom that has come (11:15) and the kingdom that has not yet come.** The seventh trumpet announces "The kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ" (11:15). And yet chapters 12–22 follow, narrating cosmic conflict, the rise of the beast, the persecution of the church, and the consummation. How can the kingdom have come and yet still be coming?

The Catholic tradition has held this tension through the partial-realized eschatology articulated by figures from Augustine through Joseph Ratzinger. The kingdom has come in Christ; it is being realized in the church; it will be consummated at the *parousia*. CCC §671 affirms that the kingdom is "already accomplished in Christ's Paschal Mystery" and yet "remains to be perfected in glory." Chapter 11:15 is therefore the proleptic announcement of what is already real and not yet consummated.

Reformed readings, especially those rooted in Calvin's amillennialism, have held the same tension. Beale's NIGTC reads 11:15 as a proleptic announcement that summarizes the consummation and is then narratively unpacked across chapters 12–22. The kingdom has come at the cross; it is being realized in the church; it will be revealed at the end.

Lutheran readings emphasize the "already" stronger than the "not yet" in many cases — Luther's *theologia crucis* read the kingdom as fundamentally hidden in the cross, with the consummation as the unveiling rather than the inauguration. Dispensationalist readings read 11:15 as future-predictive: the kingdom will come at a future seventh trumpet during the tribulation, and the chapters 12–22 narratives are the events surrounding that coming. This produces the well-known dispensationalist insistence on a future literal millennium.

Eastern Orthodox readings emphasize the liturgical-mystical realization of the kingdom: the eucharistic liturgy is the kingdom present, the *parousia* is the kingdom unveiled. Chapter 11:15 is a present-eschatological reality manifested in worship.

Critical readings (Bauckham, Caird, Boring) read the seventh trumpet as a proleptic vision: the seer is shown the end at the midpoint of the book and then narrates the path to it. The kingdom has come *in vision*; it is being realized in history; it will be consummated at the end of the visions.

Where this leaves us: chapter 11:15 forces every theology of the kingdom to articulate the relationship between the inaugurated and the consummated. The chapter does not allow either pole to be collapsed.

**The unresolved question:** If the kingdom has come at 11:15, and yet the dragon and the beasts and Babylon and the final battle still await across chapters 12–22, then what is the *temporal status* of the kingdom that has come? Is it real-but-hidden, prospective-and-anticipatory, or both? The chapter offers no metaphysical answer. It announces the kingdom and then narrates the conflict that follows the announcement.

## The Thread

**Zechariah 4:1–14 → Revelation 11:4.** The two olive trees and the two lampstands are quoted directly from Zechariah, where Joshua and Zerubbabel are figured as the priestly and royal anointed ones. John's witnesses are typological successors to this priest-king pairing.

**Daniel 7:25, 12:7 → Revelation 11:2–3.** The half-of-seven temporal formulas (1260 days, 42 months, 3.5 years) are inherited from Daniel's vision of the persecution under Antiochus IV and the eschatological tribulation. John adopts the formula to mark the time of the witnesses' prophecy and the trampling of the holy city.

**Ezekiel 37:1–14 → Revelation 11:11.** The "breath of life from God" entering the witnesses' dead bodies reproduces Ezekiel's vision of the dry bones. The chapter places the resurrection of the witnesses inside Ezekiel's prophetic horizon of national restoration.

**Psalm 2 → Revelation 11:15, 18.** The seventh trumpet's hymn echoes Psalm 2's "Why do the nations rage?" — explicitly cited at 11:18. The chapter places the kingdom's announcement inside the messianic-coronation horizon of the royal psalms.
