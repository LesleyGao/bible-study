# Isaiah 65 — Go Deeper

## The Thread

Isaiah 65 is the chapter where the Hebrew Bible imagines a *new creation*. After the long lament of chapters 63 and 64 — the cry for the heavens to be torn open, the question whether God will keep silent — Yahweh answers. But the answer is not what the lamenters expected. He says: *I was ready to be sought by those who did not ask for me*. The first half of the chapter is judgment: a people who have provoked Yahweh to his face will be repaid. The second half is the breathtaking vision: *behold, I create new heavens and a new earth, and the former things shall not be remembered or come into mind*.

The chapter contains the language Christianity has used for two thousand years to describe the eschaton — *new heavens and a new earth* — language that Revelation 21 turns into the climactic vision of the New Testament. It contains promises that have shaped utopian and apocalyptic imagination: no more weeping, no more infant mortality, no more building houses for others to inhabit, the wolf grazing with the lamb. It also contains the surprising line Paul will quote in Romans 10:20-21 to describe the inclusion of the Gentiles.

This is one of the great chapters of the Bible — and it is the chapter that finally tells the post-exilic community: the answer to your lament is not a return to what was, but a creation of what has never been.

## Lens 1 — Historical World

Isaiah 65 belongs to Third Isaiah (chs. 56-66). The mainstream-critical position since Bernhard Duhm's 1892 *Das Buch Jesaia* dates Trito-Isaiah to the late sixth or fifth century BC, post-exilic Yehud — a community wrestling with the disappointments of the rebuilt Jerusalem.

The mainstream-critical reading (Claus Westermann's *Isaiah 40-66*, Joseph Blenkinsopp's Anchor Yale *Isaiah 56-66*, Paul Hanson's *The Dawn of Apocalyptic*, John Goldingay, Jacob Stromberg, Patricia Tull) treats Isaiah 65 as Yahweh's answer to the lament of 63:7-64:12. Hanson's reading is sociological: the chapter reflects an internal Yehudite conflict between the visionary-prophetic faction (Yahweh's *servants*, the inheritors of the Trito-Isaiah hope) and a hierocratic-priestly establishment whose syncretistic-cultic practices the prophet condemns. The chapter draws a sharp line: *my servants will eat, but you will be hungry*.

The composite-authorship debate continues. The unified-authorship defense (Motyer, Oswalt) maintains that the eighth-century Isaiah ben Amoz could prophesy across centuries. Brevard Childs's canonical approach honors the layered composition while reading the canonical-final-form as authoritative. The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa-a) preserves all 66 chapters as one document by 125 BC.

The historical-cultural background is dense. The cultic-syncretism Yahweh condemns in 65:3-5 is concrete: *sacrificing in gardens and making offerings on bricks; who sit in tombs, and spend the night in secret places; who eat pig's flesh, and broth of tainted meat is in their vessels*. These are violations of Levitical purity and Deuteronomic worship-centralization. *Sacrificing in gardens* refers to the open-air shrines associated with fertility-cults (Asherah, Tammuz). *Sitting in tombs* points to necromancy or ancestor-cult practices. *Pig's flesh* and *tainted meat* are direct violations of Lev 11. The post-exilic community apparently continued some of the religious-practices that Deuteronomistic reformers had tried to eliminate.

The reference in 65:11 — *who set a table for Fortune (Hebrew Gad, גַּד) and fill cups of mixed wine for Destiny (Hebrew Meni, מְנִי)* — names two Aramaean and Arabian astral-deities of luck and fate. Gad (גַּד) was a god of fortune attested in northwest Semitic inscriptions; Meni (מְנִי) was a deity of destiny. The Yehudite community apparently included worshippers of these astral-fortune-gods, and Yahweh's response is sharp: he will *destine* (Hebrew *u-maniti*, וּמָנִיתִי — wordplay on *Meni*) them to the sword.

The new-creation language of 65:17 uses Hebrew *ki-hineni vore shamayim chadashim va-aretz chadashah* (כִּי־הִנְנִי בוֹרֵא שָׁמַיִם חֲדָשִׁים וָאָרֶץ חֲדָשָׁה). The verb *bore* (בּוֹרֵא, *creating*) is the same verb used in Genesis 1:1 — and used almost exclusively of God in the Hebrew Bible. Yahweh is doing what only Yahweh can do: creating ex nihilo, or at least creating with the kind of comprehensive power that belongs to deity alone.

## Lens 2 — Literature

Isaiah 65 has a clear two-part structure with a sharp pivot at 65:17.

**Part 1 (65:1-16): Judgment and the servant-remnant.**

*65:1-7 — The provoking people.* Yahweh has stretched out his hands all day to a rebellious people who practice cultic-syncretism. He will repay both their iniquities and their fathers' iniquities.

*65:8-10 — The cluster-of-grapes mercy.* The image: *as the new wine is found in the cluster, and they say, "Do not destroy it, for there is a blessing in it"*. Even in a corrupt vintage, some grapes produce blessing — and for the sake of the *servants* (the faithful remnant), Yahweh will not destroy all. Sharon and the Valley of Achor (places associated with judgment in earlier history — Achor was where Achan was stoned in Joshua 7) become pasture-lands again.

*65:11-12 — The astral-fortune indictment.* The Gad-Meni worshipers will be destined for the sword. The wordplay between *Meni* (Destiny) and *u-maniti* (I will destine) is sharp: the people who chased fate-gods will meet Yahweh's destiny.

*65:13-16 — The contrast-oracle.* Six *behold* statements: *my servants shall eat / you shall be hungry; my servants shall drink / you shall be thirsty; my servants shall rejoice / you shall be put to shame; my servants shall sing / you shall cry out; you shall leave your name as a curse / my chosen shall be called by another name*. The structure is rigorously contrastive — two destinies pulled apart. The closing line, *the former troubles are forgotten*, prepares the pivot.

**Part 2 (65:17-25): New heavens and new earth.**

*65:17 — The new-creation announcement.* *For behold, I create new heavens and a new earth*.

*65:18-19 — Jerusalem as joy.* The city named *Forsaken* and *Desolate* in 62:4 is now *Joy* and *Gladness*. Yahweh himself rejoices in her.

*65:20 — The infant-mortality line.* *No more shall there be in it an infant who lives but a few days, or an old man who does not fill out his days, for the young man shall die a hundred years old, and the sinner a hundred years old shall be accursed*. This is famously the verse that troubles literal-eternal-state readings: there is still death (the young dying at a hundred), but premature death is gone. Critical scholarship (Westermann, Blenkinsopp) reads this as an idealized-historical vision of long-life-blessing rather than a final-state-eschatology — closer to a renewed Eden than a fully new cosmos.

*65:21-23 — Labor not in vain.* *They shall not build and another inhabit; they shall not plant and another eat*. The Deuteronomic curse-language of Deut 28:30 is reversed. *Like the days of a tree shall the days of my people be*. The cedar and oak live for centuries; so will Yahweh's people.

*65:24 — Before they call I will answer.* The hidden-face complaint of 64:7 is reversed. The God who seemed silent now answers before being called.

*65:25 — The peaceable kingdom.* *The wolf and the lamb shall graze together; the lion shall eat straw like the ox, and dust shall be the serpent's food. They shall not hurt or destroy in all my holy mountain.* This is direct echo of Isaiah 11:6-9 (the messianic peace of the First Isaiah's vision) — the new-creation includes the reversal of predatory violence in nature itself.

The literary architecture moves from judgment (provoking people repaid) through preservation (servants saved as in a cluster) to absolute new creation (heavens-and-earth recreated). The text combines social, political, ecological, and cosmic-eschatological dimensions in a single sweeping vision.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Within the prophetic canon.** The new-creation language of Isaiah 65:17 stands at the head of a major eschatological tradition. It echoes back to the protological creation of Genesis 1, and forward through Isaiah 66:22, the apocalyptic literature (1 Enoch, Jubilees), and into Christian eschatology. The peaceable-kingdom of 65:25 reaches back to Isaiah 11 and forward to apocalyptic visions of cosmic restoration.

**Jewish trajectory.** Medieval Jewish commentators (Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Radak) read the new-creation as the messianic age (*olam ha-ba*, the world to come). Maimonides's *Mishneh Torah* (*Hilkhot Melakhim u-Milhamoteihem* 11-12) treats the messianic age as a real-historical era of cosmic-renewal — the Torah will still be observed, but evil will be removed, knowledge of God will fill the earth. The 65:20 verse (still some death) is read by some Jewish commentators as the messianic-age (where death persists but is dramatically reduced) leading toward a fuller resurrection-state.

The peaceable-kingdom vision (65:25) feeds into Jewish prayer-tradition and the imagery of the Sabbath as foretaste of the world-to-come. The new-creation is not annihilation of this world but its transformation — a strong continuity-emphasis in Jewish eschatology that shapes Christian theological-options as well.

The contrastive-oracle of 65:13-16 (my servants shall eat, you shall be hungry) entered Jewish thought as the language of the just-and-unjust at the end of days, and informs the Pirkei Avot tradition of *the world to come*.

**Christian trajectory.** The new-creation language is the foundation of Christian eschatology. 2 Peter 3:13 quotes Isaiah 65:17 directly: *but according to his promise we are waiting for new heavens and a new earth in which righteousness dwells*. Revelation 21:1 makes it the climactic vision: *then I saw a new heaven and a new earth, for the first heaven and the first earth had passed away*. Revelation 21:4 echoes Isaiah 65:19: *he will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more*.

The major Christian-theological question has been whether the new-creation is *replacement* (annihilation of the present cosmos and creation of a wholly new one) or *renewal* (transformation of the present cosmos into its glorified form).

The replacement view (some Lutheran scholastic theologians; Walvoord and dispensational-premillennial readings; some readings of 2 Pet 3:10 — *the heavens will be set on fire and dissolved*) treats the new-creation as an absolute new beginning. The present cosmos passes away; a new one is created.

The renewal view (mainstream Catholic — *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §1047, *the visible universe... is itself destined to be transformed*; Eastern Orthodox — the *theosis* theology applied cosmically; mainstream Reformed — the *Heidelberg Catechism* Q57-58 on the resurrection of the body and life everlasting; Anglican; N.T. Wright's *Surprised by Hope* and *Heaven*) reads the new-creation as the glorification and transformation of *this* cosmos. The Greek of 2 Peter 3:10 is text-critically uncertain (whether the earth will be *burned up* or *found out / disclosed*); the better-attested reading supports the renewal interpretation. Romans 8:19-23 — the creation itself groaning for liberation — supports renewal. The bodily resurrection of Christ is treated as the firstfruits of the new-creation: a transformation of *this* body, not its replacement with a different body.

N.T. Wright's *Surprised by Hope* has been particularly influential in popularizing the renewal-view among Protestant evangelicals — arguing that the Christian hope is not *escape from earth to heaven* but the redemption of this creation. The eschatological direction is *heaven coming to earth* (Rev 21:2 — the new Jerusalem coming *down* from heaven).

The peaceable-kingdom of 65:25 has shaped Christian art (Edward Hicks's nineteenth-century Quaker paintings of *The Peaceable Kingdom* depict this vision), Christian environmental theology, and Christian peace-witness. Pope Francis's encyclical *Laudato Si'* (2015) draws on the cosmic-renewal vision of Isaiah 65 to ground a theology of creation-care.

**New Testament use.** Beyond 2 Peter 3:13 and Revelation 21, Paul's quotation of Isaiah 65:1-2 in Romans 10:20-21 is theologically pivotal. Paul writes: *Then Isaiah is so bold as to say, "I have been found by those who did not seek me; I have shown myself to those who did not ask for me." But of Israel he says, "All day long I have held out my hands to a disobedient and contrary people."* Paul reads 65:1 as a prophecy of Gentile inclusion (Yahweh found by those who did not seek — the Gentiles) and 65:2 as describing Israel's resistance to the gospel. The reading is creative — Isaiah 65:1 is most naturally read in context as Yahweh's appeal to Israel itself — but Paul's typological-application has deeply shaped Christian-Jewish-mission theology and the Pauline understanding of the *mystery* of Israel and the nations (Rom 9-11).

The infant-mortality reversal (65:20) and the labor-not-in-vain promises (65:21-23) have shaped Christian social-ethical reflection. If the eschatological vision includes adequate housing, vineyards eaten by their planters, and children who reach adulthood, then the church's *now-but-not-yet* anticipation of that future is meant to bend present social-arrangements toward those realities. This is one of the theological-foundations of Christian engagement with public-health, housing-policy, and labor-ethics.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The 65:20 problem and the structure of eschatology.** *No more shall there be in it an infant who lives but a few days, or an old man who does not fill out his days, for the young man shall die a hundred years old.* The verse troubles purely-eternal-state readings. There is still death — premature death is gone, but death persists. Several theological-options:

(a) The vision is *millennial* — an extended messianic-age preceding the final eternal-state, in which death-rates drop dramatically but death is not yet fully abolished. This reading suits classic premillennialism.

(b) The vision is *idealized-historical* — Westermann, Blenkinsopp, and most critical commentators read 65:17-25 as a vision of greatly-blessed earthly life rather than a final-state cosmology. The new-creation is restoration of Eden-like conditions to *this* world.

(c) The vision is *progressive-eschatology* — partial fulfillment in this age, fuller fulfillment in a millennial age, full consummation in the eternal-state. This is the broad amillennial-postmillennial reading.

(d) The vision is *poetic-symbolic* — Isaiah is using the highest available human-imagery (long life, fruitful labor, predator-and-prey peace) to gesture toward an eschatological reality that exceeds the imagery itself. The numbers and details should not be pressed.

The Catholic tradition (CCC §1047) leans toward renewal-of-cosmos with eternal-state language; the Reformed-mainstream tradition treats the imagery as poetic-symbolic. Dispensational-premillennialism treats the imagery as describing a literal millennium. None of these readings escapes some interpretive choice; the text is rich enough to support multiple frameworks.

**Tension 2: The infant-mortality eschatology and present theodicy.** The promise that no infant will live but a few days raises the present-tense question: why do infants die now? Christian theodicy has wrestled with this. The honest answer: scripture does not explain *why* infant death is permitted now. It promises that it will not be permitted then. The new-creation language is, among other things, an indictment of present injustice — death-of-infants is not the way things are supposed to be, and the eschaton confirms it.

Modern theodicy-discussion (Marilyn McCord Adams's *Horrendous Evils and the Goodness of God*, David Bentley Hart's *The Doors of the Sea*) draws on this eschatological-vision to argue that the Christian answer to suffering is not philosophical-explanation but eschatological-promise. The new-creation does not justify present evil; it overturns it.

**Tension 3: Ecological-theology.** Pope Francis's *Laudato Si'* (2015), William P. Brown's *The Seven Pillars of Creation*, and a growing Protestant evangelical environmental theology (Calvin DeWitt, Steven Bouma-Prediger) all draw on Isaiah 65:17-25 (and 11:6-9, 66:22) to ground a Christian theology of creation-care. If the eschatological direction is renewal-of-this-cosmos, then ecological destruction in the present age is a contradiction of the eschatological-purpose. The peaceable-kingdom of 65:25, with predator-and-prey at peace, is a vision of the cosmos as it ought to be.

The criticism: ecological-theology can over-read the text in directions Isaiah's original-audience would not have recognized. The careful response: the canonical-trajectory from Isaiah 65 through Romans 8 through Revelation 21 sets up a robust theological-ecology that does not require imposing modern environmental concerns onto the text — it follows the text's own direction.

**Tension 4: Replacement vs. renewal.** The two-readings of new-creation generate significant pastoral and ethical differences. The replacement-reading tends toward escape-theology — *this world is not my home; we're just passing through*; Christian engagement with social-ecological-political realities can suffer. The renewal-reading tends toward incarnational-engagement — *the kingdom of God on earth as it is in heaven*. N.T. Wright has argued that the renewal-reading is essential to recovering the *full* Christian hope.

But the renewal-reading has its own dangers — over-realized eschatology, identification of the kingdom with particular political-projects, loss of the genuine *not-yet*. The mainstream Catholic-Reformed position holds the *already and not-yet* in tension: the new-creation is genuinely begun in Christ and the church, but its full consummation is future-eschatological.

**Tension 5: Paul's reading of 65:1-2.** Paul's appropriation of 65:1 as a Gentile-inclusion prophecy in Romans 10:20 is an example of Christian typological-reading that goes beyond the original Isaian context. Mainstream Jewish-Christian dialogue acknowledges this without resolving it. The Christian reading sees Paul's interpretation as Spirit-inspired discernment of the deeper meaning. The Jewish reading preserves the original-context meaning (Yahweh's appeal to Israel itself). Both readings are honest engagements with the same text.

**Where this leaves us.** Isaiah 65 is the chapter where the lament of 63-64 receives its answer. The answer is not what was asked for. The lament asked for the heavens to be torn so that Yahweh would *come down* and the *mountains quake*. The answer is bigger: not a divine intervention into the present-cosmos but the creation of new-heavens and a new-earth. The lament wanted Yahweh to act in history; Yahweh announces that he will act *beyond* history.

The chapter holds together what later Christian theology has often pulled apart: judgment of the unfaithful and creation of the new. The same chapter that condemns Gad-Meni worship promises that the wolf will graze with the lamb. The continuity between judgment-of-evil and renewal-of-creation is theologically important: the new-creation does not bypass moral seriousness, and moral seriousness does not stop short of cosmic renewal.

The chapter is the seedbed of Christian eschatology. Without Isaiah 65, there is no Revelation 21. Without the new-heavens-and-new-earth promise, there is no Christian hope of cosmic redemption. Without the peaceable-kingdom vision, there is no Christian theology of creation-care.

**Closing question.** If the eschatological promise is that no one shall labor in vain or build a house for another to inhabit, and if Christ is the firstfruits of that new-creation — what does it mean that we live now in a world where exactly that injustice continues? Are we waiting for a divine act that will fix it, or are we being formed into people whose lives anticipate the new-creation by refusing to participate in the old? Isaiah 65 does not require us to choose; perhaps it requires us to do both.
