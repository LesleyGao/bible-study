# Genesis 7 — The Waters Prevail

---

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political and cultural setting (of the text's composition).** Genesis 7 in its received form was almost certainly compiled during or after the Babylonian exile (6th century BC), though its source materials are far older. This matters: Israel's scribes were sitting in the heartland of Mesopotamia, where flood stories were the foundational literature of the culture that had just destroyed Jerusalem. The Babylonian *Enuma Elish* and the flood narrative embedded in *Gilgamesh* Tablet XI were temple-sponsored texts. Israelite priests preserving and shaping their own flood account were doing something polemical: writing a counter-myth in the very shadow of Marduk's ziggurat.

**Mesopotamian parallels.** Three flood texts dominate the comparison:
- The **Sumerian Flood Story** (Eridu Genesis, c. 1600 BC) — Ziusudra survives.
- The **Atrahasis Epic** (Old Babylonian, c. 1700 BC) — gods send the flood because humans are *too noisy* and disturb divine sleep.
- The **Gilgamesh Epic, Tablet XI** (standardized c. 1200 BC, discovered by George Smith in 1872 at Nineveh) — Utnapishtim's flood, with stunning verbal parallels: a divine warning, a giant boat sealed with bitumen, animals taken aboard, a flood lasting days, a mountain landing, birds released.

The differences are theologically decisive. In *Atrahasis*, the gods are capricious and hungry; when sacrifice resumes after the flood, they "gathered like flies over the offering" because they had been starving. In Genesis, the flood is a moral judgment on a creation that has become "filled with violence" (*ḥamas*, 6:11), executed by a single sovereign God who grieves (6:6).

**Source-critical analysis.** Genesis 7 is the textbook case for the Documentary Hypothesis. The chapter braids together two sources, and the seams are visible:

- **J (Yahwist)**: uses *YHWH* ("the LORD"), specifies **seven pairs** of clean animals (vv. 1–5), counts **forty days and forty nights** of rain, says YHWH "shut him in" (v. 16b).
- **P (Priestly)**: uses *Elohim* ("God"), specifies **two of every kind** (vv. 8–9, 15–16a), gives precise dating ("six hundredth year… second month… seventeenth day," v. 11), counts **150 days** of prevailing waters, describes cosmic mechanics ("fountains of the great deep… windows of the heavens").

The contradictions on the number of animals (two of each vs. seven pairs of clean) and the duration (40 days vs. 150 days) sit side by side in the same chapter. Wellhausen built his career on this. Conservative scholars like Kenneth Kitchen and Duane Garrett push back, arguing the "doublets" are a Near Eastern literary technique of resumptive repetition. But even Gordon Wenham, who defends the text's literary unity, concedes the source-critical evidence here is unusually strong.

**Material culture: the cubit and the calendar.** "Fifteen cubits deep" (v. 20) — about 22.5 feet — is the draft of the ark, just enough to clear the highest mountain. The dating ("second month, seventeenth day") follows what scholars call the **Priestly calendar**, likely reflecting post-exilic Babylonian-influenced reckoning where the year began in the spring (Nisan), making the second month roughly mid-April to mid-May.

**Key terms:**

- **תְּהוֹם** (*tehom*, "the deep," v. 11) — cognate with Akkadian *Tiamat*, the chaos-monster goddess Marduk slays in *Enuma Elish*. When "the fountains of the great *tehom* burst forth," Genesis is using freighted vocabulary: the chaos waters held back at creation (1:2, 1:6–7) are being unleashed. This is **uncreation**.
- **אֲרֻבֹּת הַשָּׁמַיִם** (*arubbot ha-shamayim*, "the windows of heaven," v. 11) — the *raqia* (firmament) of Genesis 1:6–8 has shutters. Ancient Near Eastern cosmology imagined a solid dome holding back the upper waters; rain came through opened sluices. This isn't metaphor for the author — it's cosmology.
- **מָחָה** (*maḥah*, "blot out," vv. 4, 23) — to wipe clean, as one wipes a dish (cf. 2 Kgs 21:13). The verb implies erasure, not just death. God is wiping the slate.
- **כָּל־בָּשָׂר** (*kol basar*, "all flesh," vv. 15, 16, 21) — a Priestly favorite. Includes humans and animals together; the judgment is on creaturely existence as such, not just human guilt.
- **וַיִּסְגֹּר יְהוָה בַּעֲדוֹ** (*va-yisgor YHWH ba'ado*, "and the LORD shut him in," v. 16) — the only verse where YHWH physically *acts on* a person in the flood account. The Hebrew is intimate, almost tender. Rashi will make much of this.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

**Genre.** This is theological prose narrative within a primeval history (Genesis 1–11), employing the conventions of ancient Near Eastern flood mythology but reshaped into covenant theology. It is *not* journalism, *not* parable, *not* allegory — it is mythopoeic narrative theology, using the grammar of cosmology to make claims about God's relationship to creation.

**Chiastic structure.** Gordon Wenham's classic 1978 analysis (*Vetus Testamentum* 28) demonstrated that Genesis 6:10–9:19 forms one massive chiasm, with the central pivot at **8:1**: "But God remembered Noah." Genesis 7 occupies the rising side of the chiasm — the building wave of catastrophe:

- A. Noah and sons enter ark (7:13)
- B. Wild beasts enter (7:14)
- C. Waters come upon earth (7:17)
- D. Waters increase (7:18)
- E. Mountains covered (7:19–20)
- F. **150 days** (7:24)
- F'. **150 days** recede (8:3)
- E'. Mountains visible (8:5)
- … etc.

The chapter is engineered. Verse 24's "150 days" is the apex of destruction — the silence at the top of the wave before God remembers.

**Repetition as theology.** The threefold "as God commanded" / "as the LORD commanded" (vv. 5, 9, 16) hammers obedience. The triple naming of "Shem and Ham and Japheth" (v. 13) sets up Genesis 10's Table of Nations. The fourfold "every… every… every… every" cataloguing of dying creatures (v. 21) is liturgical, almost a funeral chant for creation itself. The verbs of water *prevailing* — *gavar* used four times in vv. 18–20, 24 — personify the flood as a conquering army.

**Reverse creation.** Read against Genesis 1, chapter 7 is creation running backwards. Day 2's separation of waters above and below is undone (v. 11). Day 3's emergence of dry land is undone (v. 19). Days 5–6's swarms of birds, beasts, and humans are undone (v. 21). What remains floating is essentially Genesis 1:2 — a watery chaos with the *ruach* (here, the ark carrying the breath of life) hovering over its face. Brueggemann calls this "the de-creation."

**Textual variants.** The Septuagint (LXX) and the Samaritan Pentateuch differ from the Masoretic Text on the chronology in interesting ways — the LXX harmonizes some of the dating tensions, suggesting later scribes felt the source-seams. The Dead Sea Scrolls fragments of Genesis (4QGen-Exod^a, 4QpaleoGen-Exod^l) generally support the MT here.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**The canonical arc.** Genesis 7 is the great hinge of the primeval history. Creation (Gen 1–2) → Fall and spreading violence (Gen 3–6) → De-creation (Gen 7) → Re-creation (Gen 8–9). The flood is the OT's first paradigm of judgment-and-remnant, a pattern that will define Israel's self-understanding through exile and return. When Isaiah promises that God's covenant of peace will not depart "as I swore that the waters of Noah should no more go over the earth" (Isa 54:9), he is invoking this chapter as the floor beneath all subsequent grace.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

**Second Temple.** *1 Enoch* (3rd c. BC) reads Genesis 6–7 through the Watchers myth: the flood cleanses creation of the genetic and spiritual contamination of the fallen angels' offspring (the Nephilim). *Jubilees* expands the chronology and turns Noah into a Torah-keeper *avant la lettre*. **Philo** allegorizes: the ark is the body, Noah the rational soul, the flood the passions overwhelming the unwise. **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 1.3) cites Berossus and other "barbarian" historians to argue the flood is verifiable history, even pointing to Armenian relics of the ark — early apologetics.

**Rabbinic.** *Bereshit Rabbah* 32 wrestles with v. 16 ("the LORD shut him in") — why does God personally close the door? Rabbi Levi says: to protect Noah from the desperate generation outside who would try to break in. Rabbi Joshua of Siknin says: God shielded Noah from seeing the destruction. **Sanhedrin 108a–b** debates whether Noah was righteous *absolutely* or only *relative to his generation* — the famous reading of "in his generation" (6:9, echoed in 7:1) as a backhanded compliment. Compared to Abraham, the Talmud says, Noah would be nothing.

**Medieval.** **Rashi** (11th c.) on 7:7: Noah enters the ark "from the waters of the flood" — meaning he was a man of *little faith*, half-believing and half-doubting, who only entered when the water actually rose around his ankles. This is brutal and brilliant. **Ibn Ezra** disagrees, defending Noah's faith. **Nachmanides** (Ramban) reads the flood as a literal undoing of the second day of creation, with cosmic-cosmological seriousness. **Maimonides** (*Guide* 2.29) treats the flood as a real historical event but cautions against literalizing every detail.

**Modern Jewish.** **Nehama Leibowitz** highlights the moral horror Noah's silence represents — unlike Abraham, who argues for Sodom, Noah accepts the destruction without protest. **Jon Levenson** (*Creation and the Persistence of Evil*, 1988) argues the flood reveals that creation in Hebrew thought is not a one-time act but God's ongoing restraint of chaos — a restraint that can be withdrawn. **Avivah Zornberg** (*The Beginning of Desire*) reads Noah as a man inside a sealed coffin, mute and traumatized, and the chapter as an exploration of survival's psychic cost.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

**Patristic.** **1 Peter 3:20–21** already, within the NT, makes the ark a type of baptism — eight souls "saved through water." **Tertullian** (*On Baptism* 8) develops this: the dove returning to Noah prefigures the Spirit descending on Christ. **Origen** (*Homilies on Genesis* 2) allegorizes: the ark is the Church, its three decks the threefold structure of the faithful, Noah a type of Christ. **Augustine** (*City of God* 15.26): "without doubt this is a figure of the City of God on pilgrimage in this world, that is, of the Church, which is saved through the wood on which hung the Mediator." The ark's wood becomes the cross.

**Medieval.** **Hugh of St. Victor** writes an entire treatise (*De arca Noe morali*) using the ark as a contemplative diagram of the soul's ascent. **Aquinas** (*Summa* III, q. 49) treats Noah's ark as a sacrament of the Church, prefiguring Christ's saving work.

**Reformation.** **Luther's** *Lectures on Genesis* (1535–45) spends extraordinary time on Noah, identifying with him as a preacher to a hardened generation. **Calvin** (*Commentary on Genesis*) emphasizes the flood as proof of God's justice and Noah's faith, against any reading that softens the judgment.

**Critical era.** **Wellhausen** (*Prolegomena*, 1883) used Genesis 7 as Exhibit A for the JEDP hypothesis. **Gunkel** (*Genesis*, 1901) located the flood story in folkloric form-critical context, emphasizing Babylonian parallels. **George Smith's 1872 reading** of Tablet XI to the Society of Biblical Archaeology in London was a watershed — suddenly the Bible had a documented Mesopotamian cousin, and conservative scholarship has been reckoning with it ever since.

**Contemporary.** **Brueggemann** reads the flood as God's grief overwhelming God's wrath — and the turning point is divine self-restraint. **John Walton** argues the flood is a real but local/regional event narrated in cosmic terms because the author's "world" was the known ANE world. **Tremper Longman III** notes the flood is Yahweh's reassertion of cosmic kingship against the chaos that human violence has unleashed.

### Where They Converge and Diverge

**Convergence.** Both traditions read the flood as moral judgment, both wrestle with Noah's character, both see it as a paradigm event for understanding divine justice and mercy. Modern critical scholarship — Jewish and Christian — largely agrees on source-critical analysis and ANE comparative work.

**Divergence.** The decisive split is **typology**. Christianity from the NT onward reads Noah, the ark, and the flood as figures pointing to Christ, baptism, and the Church. Jewish tradition reads Noah as the father of a *universal* covenant (Gen 9), the basis of the **Noahide laws** that bind all humanity — a theology of righteous Gentiles that requires *no* Christological fulfillment. Where Christians see the ark as the Church (extra ecclesiam nulla salus), Jews see Noah as proof that God's covenant operates beyond the bounds of any particular religious community. **Boyarin** has shown how this divergence shapes everything downstream: Christian universalism is achieved through the particular (Christ); Jewish universalism is preserved through the covenant with all flesh (Noah).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author(s) of Genesis 7 believe God has done something terrifying and necessary: God has reversed creation to start it over, preserving a remnant through whom the divine project continues. The theological vision is that creation is *contingent* — held in being by divine will — and that the moral order is woven into the cosmic order, such that human violence (*ḥamas*) actually corrupts the world to the point of requiring its dissolution. The flood is not arbitrary divine temper; it is, in the author's vision, the natural consequence of moral collapse, executed by a God who both judges and preserves.

### Tension 1: Divine Sovereignty vs. Divine Grief

How can the God who "saw it was good" (Gen 1) become the God who "blots out" (Gen 7)? Is the flood the act of a sovereign judge or a heartbroken parent?

- **Catholic** (CCC §56–58, §1219): The flood is divine judgment but already economy of salvation — the ark is a figure of baptism and the Church. God's sovereignty and mercy are not in tension because both serve the covenantal plan culminating in Christ.
- **Reformed** (Calvin, Bavinck): God's sovereignty is primary; the flood demonstrates God's absolute right over creation. Divine grief (6:6) is anthropopathic — true of God's relation to us but not implying changeability in God.
- **Lutheran** (Luther, and modern Lutheran theology like Forde): God's grief is real and definitive. The flood reveals the *deus absconditus* — the hidden God whose wrath is real but who acts ultimately to save.
- **Wesleyan/Arminian**: God's foreknowledge of human freedom means the flood is response to genuine human choice; divine grief is not metaphor but the cost of love.
- **Jewish** (Levenson): God's sovereignty is *ongoing combat* against chaos. The flood shows that God's creative restraint of *tehom* is conditional on a moral cosmos. When humans fill the earth with violence, the waters return.
- **Process theology** (Whitehead, Cobb): God does not unilaterally cause the flood; the cosmos responds to moral disorder, and God grieves with creation.
- **Critical** (Brueggemann): The narrative *itself* discovers something new about God — that God is willing to limit divine judgment for the sake of relationship (which becomes explicit in 8:21). Genesis 7 is the dark night before the rainbow.

**Steelman the position I find least intuitive — the strict Reformed view.** The argument: any softening of divine sovereignty in the face of the flood's horror is a failure of theological nerve. If God is not free to undo creation, God is not God; if God's grief implies God could be otherwise persuaded, then prayer and providence collapse. The flood is *terrible* precisely because it is the act of the One whose holiness is incompatible with violence. To make God reactive or grieving in a literal sense is to project our limitations onto God and lose the very transcendence that makes salvation possible. The flood is a mercy *because* a God who tolerated *ḥamas* indefinitely would be a God who didn't care — and *that* would be the truly horrifying deity.

### Tension 2: Universal Judgment vs. the Innocent

The flood kills children, infants, animals. How does the author handle this?

- **The author seems not to**. There is a stunning silence in the text. No appeal, no Abraham bargaining as in Genesis 18, no lament. Only "all flesh died."
- **Patristic** response (Augustine, *City of God* 1.8): God knows the secret destinies of all souls; physical death is not the worst evil; infant death may be mercy.
- **Rabbinic** response: The animals are included because the moral corruption had spread to all flesh (the Talmud notes that even animals were committing cross-species mating in the antediluvian generation, *Sanhedrin* 108a).
- **Modern Catholic** (Joseph Ratzinger, *In the Beginning*): The flood story is true on the level of theological anthropology, not necessarily of biological history; its truth is about the human capacity to corrupt creation.
- **Jewish moral protest** (Elie Wiesel, Levinas): The flood is precisely the kind of divine action that the post-Holocaust theologian must refuse to celebrate. Noah's silence is the moral failure; we must be Abrahams, not Noahs.
- **Critical** (Brueggemann, Fretheim): the narrative must be read with the silenced victims — children, animals, the unnamed — in view, not just the saved remnant; the flood's moral cost is part of what the text means to make us feel.
- **Evangelical** (Wright, Goldingay): The flood reveals that God takes evil with ultimate seriousness — the alternative (a God who shrugs at *ḥamas*) is worse. But the rainbow promise (Gen 9) shows God will never again resolve evil this way; the cross is the new flood, where God absorbs the judgment in God's own self.

**Where this leaves us.** Genesis 7 will not let any reader off the hook. It refuses both the sentimentalism that makes God always-nice and the fundamentalism that makes God's actions self-evidently just. It sits there, ancient and unflinching, asking whether we believe creation is held by a moral will or merely by physics — and what we are willing to make of the cost.

**The unresolved question:** If the LORD shut Noah in (v. 16) — sealing him inside while the screams of the drowning rose outside the ark's walls — what does it do to your theology that *salvation* and *the inability to look away from suffering you cannot stop* might be the same door?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 1:2** — "darkness was over the face of the deep (*tehom*), and the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters." Genesis 7:11 is Genesis 1:2 returning. The *tehom* held back at creation breaks loose; only the ark with its breath of life floats where the *ruach* once hovered. Read together, they make creation look fragile by design.

- **Exodus 14:21–29** — The Red Sea: waters that destroy the wicked and save the remnant. The Exodus is a flood story in miniature; Pharaoh's army drowns where Israel walks dry. When you reach Exodus, notice how Israel's own founding is patterned on Noah's.

- **Matthew 24:37–39** — Jesus invokes "the days of Noah" to describe the suddenness of the Son of Man's coming. He reads Genesis 7 not as past judgment but as prototype of eschatological reckoning. **When you reach the Olivet Discourse, remember this chapter** — Jesus expects you to.

- **1 Peter 3:18–22** — "eight persons were brought safely through water. Baptism, which corresponds to this, now saves you." The earliest Christian reading turns the ark into the font. Watch for it; it shapes every patristic baptismal liturgy.

- **Isaiah 54:9–10** — "This is like the days of Noah to me: as I swore that the waters of Noah should no more go over the earth, so I have sworn that I will not be angry with you." The prophet anchors all post-exilic hope in the rainbow promise that Genesis 7 makes necessary.
