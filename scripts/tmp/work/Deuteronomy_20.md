# Deuteronomy 20: When You Go Out to War

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Deuteronomy 20 — a chapter in the book Hebrew tradition calls *Dĕbārîm*, "Words" — sits within the Deuteronomic Code (chapters 12–26) and presents the most detailed body of warfare legislation in the Hebrew Bible. Moses speaks on the plains of Moab, on the eve of the Jordan crossing and the conquest of Canaan. The laws address a people preparing not for abstract future battles but for specific imminent campaigns against the peoples of the land. Chapter 20 divides into three discernible units: pre-battle exhortation and exemptions (vv. 1–9); the law of distant cities (vv. 10–15); the law of Canaanite *ḥerem* and the siege-tree provision (vv. 16–20). The juxtaposition is morally vertiginous. Verses 10–15 prescribe offers of peace and restraint against foreign cities; verses 16–18 prescribe total destruction of Canaanite peoples. No chapter in the Pentateuch has generated more sustained ethical and theological controversy.

The political context of the chapter's composition is debated along established lines. Critical scholars since W. M. L. de Wette and Julius Wellhausen have located its final form in the seventh-century Judean context, often specifically in Josianic or early Deuteronomistic circles. Moshe Weinfeld (*Anchor Bible Deuteronomy*) argues the *ḥerem* laws reflect a Josianic reform rhetoric aimed at purging syncretistic cult from the land — a "theological archaism" written back into the conquest narrative. The exemption clauses (vv. 5–8), Weinfeld notes, echo Neo-Assyrian muster texts that likewise exempt new householders and bridegrooms, though in secular rather than theological idiom. Against this, Kenneth Kitchen (*On the Reliability of the Old Testament*) argues the treaty frame and warfare rhetoric fit second-millennium Hittite and Egyptian models more closely than first-millennium Assyrian ones, supporting a substantially Mosaic core. Peter Craigie (*NICOT*), J. G. McConville (*Apollos*), Daniel Block (*NIVAC*), and Christopher Wright (*Old Testament Ethics*) defend an early compositional horizon while accepting editorial updating.

The Ancient Near Eastern environment is everywhere behind the chapter. Neo-Assyrian royal inscriptions — the annals of Sennacherib, Tiglath-pileser III, and Ashurbanipal — describe siege warfare in gruesome detail, with language of total destruction ("I made their blood flow down the squares of their city") that reads as rhetorical topos more than operational fact. Mario Liverani and K. Lawson Younger Jr. (*Ancient Conquest Accounts*) have argued that the biblical conquest language participates in a recognizable ANE literary genre of hyperbolic warfare rhetoric — formulae not intended as quantitative reports. The "ban" or devotion-to-destruction, *ḥerem*, has close parallels: the Moabite Mesha Stele (ninth century BCE) boasts that King Mesha "put to the ban" (*ḥrm*) the Israelite town of Nebo for the god Ashtar-Chemosh. The concept was regionally intelligible. On exemptions, the Middle Assyrian Laws and Hittite military regulations include release provisions for newlyweds and new property-owners. On the siege-tree law, the *Assyrian Laws on Captives* and the treaty of Esarhaddon with Baʿal of Tyre include stipulations about agricultural destruction, though often in the opposite direction (commanding rather than forbidding it).

Archaeological evidence complicates the conquest picture. Kathleen Kenyon's excavations at Jericho in the 1950s showed no Late Bronze Age destruction layer matching Joshua's dating; Bryant Wood's reanalysis of the pottery has argued the contrary, though disputed. William Dever (*Beyond the Texts*), Israel Finkelstein, and Amihai Mazar debate whether the archaeological record supports invasion, infiltration, or indigenous emergence models for Israelite origins. The implications for reading Deuteronomy 20 are significant: the *ḥerem* laws may describe an idealized or retrospective vision rather than an executed military campaign, which is what Chris Wright and many evangelical commentators now propose.

Manuscript evidence is good. Qumran fragments 4QDeut-k and 4QDeut-d preserve portions of Deuteronomy 20 with substantial Masoretic agreement. The Samaritan Pentateuch contains the chapter with its standard minor variants. The Septuagint is relatively close.

Five Hebrew terms structure the chapter's vocabulary. First, *ḥerem* (חֵרֶם), "devotion to destruction" or "the ban" — from a root meaning "to set apart," often for sacred use; in warfare, to set apart for total destruction. Second, *qārôb* and *rāḥôq* (קָרוֹב / רָחוֹק), "near" and "far" — the spatial distinction that divides verses 10–15 from 16–18, foreign cities from Canaanite cities. Third, *lĕkôl-ʿārê haggôyim hārĕḥōqôt* (לְכֹל־עָרֵי הַגּוֹיִם הָרְחֹקֹת), "all the cities that are far off" — the category given diplomatic priority. Fourth, *yārēʾ wĕrak hallēbāb* (יָרֵא וְרַךְ הַלֵּבָב), "fearful and soft of heart" — the category exempted in verse 8, with the distinctive concern "lest his brothers' heart melt like his own." Fifth, *bal taššîḥît* / *bal taschît* (בַּל תַּשְׁחִית), "do not destroy" — rabbinic shorthand derived from the siege-tree law of verses 19–20, which becomes the halakhic foundation for a whole Jewish environmental ethic.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is military code framed as address. The chapter opens with a battle-speech (vv. 2–4) delivered by the priest, not by a commander — a distinctive Deuteronomic move. The officers then deliver four dismissals in structured parallel (vv. 5–8), each with the formula "Is there any man who…? Let him go back to his house, lest he die in the battle…" The fourth dismissal breaks the pattern: "Is there any man who is fearful and fainthearted? Let him go back… lest he make his brothers' heart melt like his own." The shift from "lest he die" to "lest he make others faint" is rhetorically central — individual self-interest gives way to concern for communal morale, and fear itself is treated as contagious and dangerous.

Literary devices are densely packed. The rhetorical question "Are the trees of the field men?" (v. 19) is a moral pivot — siege warfare must not treat creation as combatant. The chapter moves by deliberate contrast: "cities far away" versus "cities of these peoples" (vv. 15–16), peace terms versus total destruction, trees that feed versus trees that can be cut. The priest's speech in verses 3–4 uses the distinctive Deuteronomic rhetoric of divine accompaniment (*ʾănî hôlēk ʿimmākem*, "I go with you"), echoing the sermonic tone of the whole book. As Dennis Olson observes, the warfare laws are preached — they are exhortation more than operational manual.

Textual variants cluster around the list of nations in verse 17. The Masoretic Text gives six nations; some manuscripts and the Septuagint add the Girgashites to make seven, aligning with the longer list of Deuteronomy 7:1. The Samaritan Pentateuch preserves six. 4QDeut fragments show mixed traditions. Verse 19's rhetorical question "Are the trees of the field human?" has been translated variously — "Is the tree of the field a man?" (KJV), "Are the trees of the field human, that they should be besieged by you?" (ESV), "Are the trees in the field people?" (NJPS) — with some commentators reading it as "For the tree of the field is man's life" (Rashi), which inverts the syntax entirely. The Hebrew is ambiguous and the reception history reflects it.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish reception.** The Mishnah devotes tractate *Soṭah* 8 to the warfare laws of Deuteronomy 20, distinguishing carefully between *milḥemet miṣvah* (obligatory war) and *milḥemet rĕšût* (optional war). The exemption clauses apply only to optional wars, according to the Mishnah; in obligatory war — defensive war or war against Amalek and the seven nations — no exemptions are granted. Maimonides codifies this in *Hilchot Melachim* (Laws of Kings and their Wars) 5–7. *Hilchot Melachim* 6:1 requires offering peace terms even to Canaanite cities — an interpretive move going back to *Sifre Deuteronomy* — directly softening the plain sense of verses 16–17 by importing the peace-offer of verse 10 into the *ḥerem* context. The rabbinic tradition consistently nudges the warfare laws toward greater restraint.

The siege-tree provision of verse 19 generates the entire rabbinic principle of *bal taschît*, "do not destroy." Maimonides in *Hilchot Melachim* 6:8–10 extends it from fruit trees to any wasteful destruction: "Not only trees, but one who smashes vessels, tears clothes, destroys buildings, stops up springs, or wastes food — all transgress *bal taschît*." This becomes the halakhic foundation of Jewish environmental ethics, developed by modern thinkers including Jonathan Sacks, Jeremy Benstein, and the Heschel Center. The Samaritan tradition preserves the *ḥerem* laws but reads them through its distinctive theology of the Gerizim cult-place.

Medieval Jewish commentators grapple with verses 16–18 honestly. Rashi cites the Sifre's peace-offer principle. Ibn Ezra stresses the specific historical moment and the idolatrous danger. Ramban acknowledges the difficulty and appeals to the uniqueness of the Canaanite situation. Maimonides, in *Hilchot Melachim* 5:4–5, explicitly limits the *ḥerem* to the original seven nations whose identity has been lost to history — rendering the law halakhically inapplicable going forward. Modern Jewish thinkers — Yeshayahu Leibowitz, Michael Walzer (*Just and Unjust Wars*, *In God's Shadow*), Jonathan Sacks (*Not in God's Name*) — explicitly address the ethical horror. Walzer argues that the biblical warfare texts are "historical documents" requiring interpretive distance, not models for contemporary action. Sacks argues that the Canaanite conquest is a closed chapter — morally specific to a unique historical moment — and that applying it to any contemporary conflict is a "tragic misreading."

**Christian reception.** The chapter has shaped Christian ethical traditions in multiple directions, sometimes contradictorily. The just-war tradition rests partly on Deuteronomy 20. Augustine (*City of God* 1.21; *Contra Faustum* 22.74–79; *Questions on the Heptateuch* 6.10) argued that the warfare laws distinguish legitimate from illegitimate violence — legitimate violence is commanded by divine authority, pursues justice, and restrains escalation. Augustine is the architect of a Christian theology of just war, and Deuteronomy 20 supplies much of his biblical grammar. Aquinas (*ST* II-II q. 40) systematizes the just-war criteria — legitimate authority, just cause, right intention — and cites Deuteronomy 20 alongside Augustine. Calvin's *Commentary on the Four Last Books of Moses* (on Deut 20) defends the conquest as unique and unrepeatable while reading the general principles of restraint as perpetually binding. The magisterial Reformation tradition generally follows Augustine and Aquinas.

The pacifist and Anabaptist traditions read Deuteronomy 20 very differently. John Howard Yoder's *The Politics of Jesus* (1972) argues that the warfare texts represent a covenantal moment superseded by Christ's self-giving; the canonical reading runs from *ḥerem* through Isaiah's swords-into-plowshares to the cross. Millard Lind, in *Yahweh is a Warrior* (1980), argues that Israel's warfare was supposed to be miraculous — Yahweh fights, Israel watches — and that the shift from Yahweh-war to state-war under the monarchy was itself a departure from the ideal. Richard Hays (*The Moral Vision of the New Testament*) argues that the New Testament's witness requires Christians to read the conquest narratives typologically or eschatologically rather than as models for military action. Stanley Hauerwas and Preston Sprinkle continue this line.

The honest-critical tradition refuses both apologetic rescue and pious evasion. Walter Brueggemann (*Deuteronomy* AOTC) calls these laws "theologically raw" and insists readers not smooth them. Regina Schwartz (*The Curse of Cain*) argues that monotheism itself carries an exclusionary violence encoded in texts like Deuteronomy 20. Paul Copan (*Is God a Moral Monster?*) and Chris Wright (*The God I Don't Understand*) offer the most developed evangelical-apologetic responses, arguing that the *ḥerem* language is ANE hyperbolic rhetoric, that Israel is specifically acting as divine judgment against centuries of Canaanite moral atrocity (Gen 15:16), and that the laws are non-paradigmatic — a one-time act of judgment, not a template for war. Critical scholars — Weinfeld, Nelson (*OTL*), Dennis Olson, Nathan MacDonald — tend to see the *ḥerem* laws as literary constructs reflecting later Judean theology rather than historical campaigns.

Origen had already, in *Homilies on Joshua*, read the conquest allegorically — the "nations" as the passions and vices the Christian must destroy within. Chrysostom, in his *Homilies*, handles the warfare texts with both reserve and confidence in divine prerogative. Medieval commentators often followed Origen's spiritualizing reading alongside the literal-historical one.

Modern secular political theory has drawn on the chapter too. Michael Walzer (*Just and Unjust Wars*, 1977) uses Deuteronomy 20 as one of the foundational ancient texts for the Western ethics of war, alongside Thucydides and Cicero. His fourfold categorization — proportionality, discrimination, non-combatant immunity, proper authority — finds partial but genuine roots in the chapter.

**Where they converge and diverge.** All serious interpretive traditions converge on the exemption clauses (vv. 5–8) as morally remarkable — the ancient world produced little comparable concern for the emotional and relational fabric of the soldier. All traditions converge on the siege-tree law as a pioneering restraint on total warfare. They diverge sharply on the *ḥerem*. Just-war Christians (Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin) and rabbinic halakhists (Maimonides) tend to historicize and restrict it. Pacifists (Yoder, Lind, Hauerwas) treat it as superseded. Critical honesty readings (Brueggemann, Schwartz) refuse to soften it. Evangelical apologists (Copan, Chris Wright) argue for hyperbolic-rhetorical readings. Jewish and Christian traditions have both — in their mainstream expressions — declined to treat the *ḥerem* as a model for any contemporary campaign, even if for different reasons.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What is the moral-theological status of the *ḥerem* laws in verses 16–18?**

*Position A — Historicized and inapplicable.* Maimonides in *Hilchot Melachim* 5:4 declares the seven nations lost to history; the *ḥerem* is halakhically dead. Augustine and Calvin similarly treat the conquest as uniquely authorized by divine command, non-repeatable. Chris Wright and Paul Copan continue this with additional arguments for ANE hyperbolic rhetoric. The law is real, was once operative in a particular moment, but cannot be a template for any contemporary action.

*Position B — Theologically normative, pacifist-transcended.* Yoder, Lind, Hays: the *ḥerem* belongs to a covenantal moment that Christ's cross transforms. The narrative arc of canon moves from *ḥerem* to cross; disciples do not return to the first.

*Position C — Morally problematic, interpretively honest.* Brueggemann, Schwartz, Walzer: the *ḥerem* texts pose real theological difficulty. The responsible reader does not explain them away but lives with the tension, recognizing biblical religion as a tradition in argument with itself (see Deut 20:10's peace-offer juxtaposed with 20:16's extermination).

*Position D — Divine judgment.* Paul Copan, Block, traditional apologetics: the Canaanite culture had reached a level of moral atrocity (child sacrifice, ritual prostitution, extreme cruelty) that warranted judgment, and Israel was the temporal instrument. The *ḥerem* is analogous to divine judgment elsewhere in Scripture (the flood, Sodom), temporally executed through Israel.

Steelman the least intuitive: Position C, the honesty-reading, is probably the hardest for traditional theological readers who want the text to vindicate a God whose goodness is morally intelligible. Its strongest case is that the text itself preserves tension — the peace-offer of verse 10 sits in the same chapter as the *ḥerem* of verse 17. Rabbinic tradition nudges toward verse 10's principle; later Israel's practice never followed the *ḥerem* as described (compare 1 Kings 9:20–21, where Canaanites remain in the land and become forced laborers rather than being exterminated). The Bible's own reception of the *ḥerem* is evasive and partial. An honest reader may have to say: the text issued a command that was largely never executed even in Israel's own history, and the later tradition preferred to soften it than to enforce it — suggesting the canonical community itself found the command hard to hold.

**Where this leaves us:** Most responsible contemporary readers operate with some combination of Positions A and C. The laws are historicized and no longer applicable; the tension they raise for theology is real and must be acknowledged. Position D's "divine judgment" frame requires supplementation by A or C to function today, since no contemporary community can claim the authorization the text gives ancient Israel.

**Tension 2: What does the chapter teach about the ethics of war in general?**

*Position A — Just-war paradigm.* Augustine and Aquinas developed a tradition — proper authority, just cause, right intention, discrimination between combatants and non-combatants, proportionality, restraint in siege — that draws real substance from Deuteronomy 20. The chapter's exemption clauses, peace-offer requirement, and siege-tree law all point toward restrained, limited, morally-governed war.

*Position B — Pacifism.* Yoder, Lind, Hays: warfare as such is incompatible with Christ's way. The chapter's exemptions and restraints point toward a trajectory the gospel completes by rejecting war altogether.

*Position C — Realpolitik with limits.* Walzer's *Just and Unjust Wars*: war is sometimes necessary, must always be morally constrained, and the ancient texts supply some — not all — of the grammar. Walzer treats Deuteronomy 20 as an inherited starting point, not a finished ethic.

*Position D — The Brueggemann honest-reading.* Neither endorsing nor transcending, but naming: war in the chapter is both commanded and morally troubled. The faithful reader holds the tension.

Steelman the least intuitive: Position B, pacifism, is probably the hardest for readers inside the Augustinian-Calvinist mainstream. Its strongest case is not Deuteronomy 20 alone but the canonical arc: from Yahweh-war where Israel watches (Exod 14), through the prophetic critique of reliance on horses and chariots (Isa 31:1), to the Isaiah vision of swords into plowshares (Isa 2:4), to Jesus' refusal of the sword (Matt 26:52). The whole-canon reading pressures the reader toward a trajectory in which Deuteronomy 20's warfare is transcended, not emulated. Whether Christians should therefore renounce all participation in state violence is further contested, but the hermeneutical pressure is real.

**Where this leaves us:** Deuteronomy 20 alone does not settle the Christian ethic of war. Its material — exemptions, peace-offer, siege restraint — supplies grammar for just-war traditions; its *ḥerem* material pressures toward a pacifist trajectory or historicized reading; its honest reception names the tension. Most contemporary readers will combine elements, treating the exemption and siege-tree laws as foundational restraints and the *ḥerem* as historicized.

**One unresolved question:** If verse 18's rationale for the *ḥerem* is that the Canaanites "will teach you to do all their abominations," what does that imply about cultural contact in general? The text presupposes that moral influence runs strongly from the pagan to the faithful — that the only way to protect covenant identity is total separation. Is this a realistic anthropology of cultural influence, or does it underestimate the covenant community's capacity for counter-cultural witness? The question echoes across Christian history — in the monastic movements, the Anabaptist withdrawals, the missionary theologies — and remains unresolved.

## The Thread

First, the exemption clauses (vv. 5–7) echo and expand the earliest Israelite muster practice, but they also anticipate Jesus' parable of the great banquet (Luke 14:18–20), where the excluded guests offer precisely the same three excuses: a newly bought field, newly purchased oxen, a new wife. The parable reverses the exemptions — what excused a man from YHWH's war becomes a self-exclusion from YHWH's feast. The intertextual echo transforms Deuteronomy's tender concern for the householder into a warning about complacency.

Second, the siege-tree law (v. 19) is echoed in a striking way in Habakkuk 2:17 ("the violence done to Lebanon will overwhelm you for the destruction of the beasts terrified them, because of the blood of men and violence to the earth") and in the prophetic reading of creation under oath (Hos 4:1–3, Isa 24:4–6). The prophetic tradition takes the seed of *bal taschît* and expands it into a theology of creation under covenant — one that Jonathan Sacks, Wendell Berry, and Chris Wright all trace through their work.

Third, the peace-offer of verses 10–11 — "offer terms of peace" — is inverted as a warning in Psalm 120:7 ("I am for peace, but when I speak, they are for war"), and it reappears as ethical gold in Paul's "So far as it depends on you, live peaceably with all" (Rom 12:18) and Hebrews 12:14's "strive for peace with everyone." The priority of the peace-offer in Deuteronomy 20:10 seeds a canonical commitment to negotiated resolution.

Fourth, the "fearful and fainthearted" exemption (v. 8) is echoed almost verbatim in Judges 7:3, where Gideon's army is winnowed from 32,000 to 10,000 by the same formula: "Whoever is fearful and trembling, let him return home." Deuteronomy's law becomes narrative in Judges — and the text's theological interest is in how YHWH works through radical reduction. The exemption that looks humane in Deuteronomy 20 becomes divine strategy in Judges 7, and the faithful remnant reduces further still. The thread connects compassion for the individual soldier to a theology of reliance on YHWH rather than numerical might.
