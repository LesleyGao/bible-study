# Job 19: My Redeemer Lives, and My Skin Is Gone

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Job 19 is the hinge of the second cycle of speeches. It sits at the statistical center of the dialogue and, more importantly, it contains one of the most famous and most textually disputed passages in all of Hebrew wisdom literature — the *go'eli chay* declaration of verses 25 through 27. To read it historically is to reconstruct a world in which kinship, land tenure, legal advocacy, and the horizon of death were all woven together, and in which the vocabulary of that world could be pressed into religious language of almost unbearable intensity.

The institution of the *go'el* (גֹּאֵל) is central to the chapter's meaning. In pre-exilic Israelite society, the *go'el* was the nearest male kinsman who had defined legal obligations: to redeem family land sold under duress (Leviticus 25:25), to redeem an impoverished family member sold into slavery (Leviticus 25:47–49), to avenge the blood of a murdered relative (Numbers 35:19 — *go'el ha-dam*), and to raise up seed for a dead brother (Ruth 4). Field archaeology at Iron Age II sites like Tell Beit Mirsim and Beersheba, together with the ostraca from Arad and Lachish, reveals a society organized around the *beth av* (household of the father) in which land and lineage were inseparable. The *go'el* was the institutional mechanism for their preservation. For Job, stripped of land, children, and social standing, to cry out for a *go'el* is to reach for the one legal structure in his world that might still make a claim on his behalf.

The dating of the book remains disputed. Marvin Pope in the Anchor Bible commentary, David Clines in the Word Biblical Commentary, and Carol Newsom in her New Interpreter's study argue for a post-exilic composition (fifth century BCE), pointing to Aramaisms, thematic resonance with Second Isaiah, and philosophical distance from pre-exilic wisdom. Robert Gordis and Francis Andersen argue for pre-exilic composition in the seventh or sixth century, citing archaic morphology and the patriarchal setting untouched by Israel's covenantal specificity. Ezekiel 14:14 groups Job with Noah and Daniel as a paradigmatic righteous man by the sixth century BCE, which suggests that at minimum the Job legend was circulating that early. The textual witnesses are rich and complex: the Septuagint's Job is roughly four hundred lines shorter than the Masoretic Text (Origen marked the missing material with asterisks in the Hexapla), the Qumran Targum 11QTargum Job (dated paleographically to around 50 CE but reflecting older translation traditions) supplies an Aramaic version, and rabbinic tradition (Bava Batra 15a) records ancient disagreement about dating.

The material culture of chapter 19 repays attention. Job's wish in verses 23–24 that his words be "inscribed in a book" (*seifer*) and "engraved in the rock" with "an iron pen and lead" reflects two specific technologies of durable writing. Monumental rock inscriptions survive across the ancient Near East — the Behistun inscription of Darius, the Zakkur stele, the Mesha stele from Dibon (c. 840 BCE) discovered in 1868, the Siloam inscription from Jerusalem (c. 701 BCE). The phrase about "iron pen and lead" likely describes the practice of cutting letters into stone and then filling them with molten lead to make them legible — a technique attested at the Deir Alla inscriptions and described by Pliny the Elder (*Natural History* 34.17). Job's desire is for the permanence that ancient Near Eastern royalty sought: a monument that would outlast his destroyed body.

The social and familial isolation depicted in verses 13 through 19 is historically pointed. In a kinship society, the failure of "my brothers" (*aḥai*), "my relatives" (*qerovai*), "my maidservants" (*amhothai*), "my servant" (*avdi*), "my wife" (*ishti*), and even "the children of my own mother" (*benei vithni*) is a total social catastrophe. The reference to being a "stranger" and "foreigner" (*nokhri*) in one's own house evokes the legal category of *ger* in Israelite law — the resident alien without land rights. Job has become an internal exile in his own home. Archaeology of pillared four-room houses confirms the spatial tightness of the *beth av*; to be estranged in such a space was to be physically surrounded by people who refused to recognize you.

Parallel ancient Near Eastern texts sharpen the picture. *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi* (second-millennium Babylonian) describes a righteous sufferer whose family and city turn against him in strikingly similar terms. The *Babylonian Theodicy*, the Sumerian composition "Man and His God," and Ugaritic mourning texts from Ras Shamra all share the trope of the sufferer abandoned by kin. What distinguishes Job is the verbal density of the abandonment sequence and the intensity of the final hope — a kinsman-redeemer who is not among his human kin at all.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Structurally the chapter divides into four movements. Verses 2–6 are a rebuke to the friends for their verbal violence; verses 7–12 are an accusation against God as the attacker; verses 13–22 catalogue the social estrangement; and verses 23–29 are the culminating declaration of a *go'el* and a warning. The chapter's arc moves from defensive anger, through a portrait of God as enemy, through the human desolation, to a transcendent hope that ruptures the expected logic.

Hebrew parallelism works at high pressure throughout. Verse 2, *ad anah togyun nafshi u-tedakkunani ve-millim* ("how long will you torment my soul and crush me with words"), uses the pairing of torment and crushing to align verbal abuse with physical violence. The repeated *heshiv* constructions — "he has put me in the wrong," "he has walled up my way," "he has stripped from me my glory" — establish a legal-military frame in which God is the adversary. Verse 12's image of troops "casting up their siege ramp against me and encamping around my tent" (*solelot . . . va-yaḥanu saviv le-oholi*) is drawn directly from siege warfare vocabulary attested in the Lachish reliefs and in Assyrian annals of Sennacherib.

A key literary feature of chapter 19 is its invocation of legal and familial language for theological use. The word *go'el* is deliberately domestic and juridical. In choosing that word Job is not reaching for a generic deity but for a specific institutional function — the kinsman who has a claim-right to intervene. The word *aḥaron* in verse 25 ("at the last he will stand upon the earth" — *ve-aḥaron al afar yaqum*) has been translated variously as "at the last," "as the last one," or "afterward"; the same lexeme can mean "one who stands in the rear" (as in guarding the back of a column). Robert Alter's translation renders it "and last in the dust He will stand." The ambiguity is deliberate.

The famous verses 25–27 are among the most textually disputed in the Hebrew Bible. The Masoretic text reads *ve-ani yada'ti go'ali ḥai / ve-aḥaron al afar yaqum / ve-aḥar ori niqqefu zot / u-mi-besari eḥezeh eloah* — "And I know my redeemer lives / and at the last upon the dust he will stand / and after my skin has been struck down thus / and from my flesh I shall behold God." The particle *mi-* in *mi-besari* can mean either "from" (in the sense of "from within my flesh," supporting resurrection) or "from" (in the sense of "away from my flesh," supporting a disembodied vision after death) or "because of my flesh" (a third reading). David Clines argues for "apart from my flesh"; Francis Andersen and John Hartley argue for "in my flesh"; Marvin Pope reads it as "from the vantage of my flesh" in a present-life sense. The Septuagint's translation diverges significantly and removes some of the ambiguity by rendering the passage in a way that reads more like vindication in this life. 11QTargum Job's Aramaic rendering of these verses is partially preserved and supports a forensic-legal reading rather than a straightforward resurrection reading.

The chiastic structure of the whole chapter is notable. The opening complaint against the friends' words (vv. 2–3) mirrors the closing warning that the friends themselves will face judgment (vv. 28–29). The accusation that God has stripped Job's glory and broken him (vv. 9–10) sets up the counter-hope that the *go'el* will stand on the dust (v. 25). The social desolation of verses 13–19 finds its inverse in the declaration that Job will see God "for myself" (v. 27, *ani li*) — a line whose first-person intensity ("I, for myself / for me") is almost grammatically redundant and therefore emphatic.

Job's wish that his words be inscribed on rock (vv. 23–24) is a meta-literary gesture that the book itself fulfills. Newsom reads this as the book's self-reflexive moment: the words Job wants preserved are the words we are reading. The chapter is thereby the book's own monument, a stele erected against the erasure that Bildad threatened in the previous chapter.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Canonically, Job 19 sits at the heart of the book's theological drama and has had an outsized influence on both Jewish and Christian traditions. The chapter's vocabulary of *go'el* is drawn from the Pentateuch's redemption laws; its imagery of siege is drawn from prophetic and historical books; its hope for a vision of God in or beyond the flesh anticipates the apocalyptic developments of Second Temple Judaism.

The Jewish trajectory is remarkably diverse. The Testament of Job, a Jewish pseudepigraph composed between the first century BCE and the first century CE, narrates Job's eschatological vindication and includes visions of a heavenly throne and a restored glorified body, which many scholars see as drawing on Job 19. 11QTargum Job, recovered from Qumran Cave 11, gives an Aramaic rendering that reads *go'eli* as an advocate or witness rather than a resurrection-promising redeemer. The Talmud in Bava Batra 15a–16b treats Job as either historical or parabolic and debates the identity and meaning of the *go'el*; the Talmudic tradition generally resists a resurrection reading. Rashi, in his medieval commentary, reads *go'eli* as God himself who will redeem Job in this life, with *miv'sari* rendered as "from my flesh" in the sense of "with my own eyes while I still live." Ibn Ezra hears a forensic language: the *go'el* is the advocate who will vindicate Job's name. Maimonides in the *Guide for the Perplexed* III.22–23 treats the Job dialogue as exploring theories of providence and reads verses 25–27 as a moment of philosophical insight rather than eschatological prophecy. Nachmanides in his *Commentary on Job* is more willing than Maimonides to entertain a vindicatory afterlife reading but still resists a straightforward resurrection gloss. Martin Buber, in *The Prophetic Faith*, reads Job 19 as the moment when Job breaks through the theology of the friends to a direct I-Thou relation with God. Jon Levenson in *Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel* sees Job 19 as a boundary text standing between an older anthropology of death as ultimate and the emerging resurrection hope of Daniel and Second Temple apocalyptic. James Kugel in *The Great Shift* and *The God of Old* reads Job 19 as part of the transition toward religious interiority. Avivah Zornberg, with her characteristic literary sensitivity, reads the chapter as the moment when Job's isolated voice generates the very monument he requests. Nahum Sarna and the JPS tradition translate cautiously: "my witness" rather than "my redeemer."

The Christian trajectory has read Job 19:25 very differently. Origen in his fragments on Job, preserved in the catenae, reads *go'ali ḥai* as a prophetic voice of the resurrected Christ. Gregory the Great in the *Moralia in Iob* (composed in Constantinople in the late 580s and completed in Rome) reads the passage as one of the clearest pre-Christian witnesses to bodily resurrection and to Christ as Redeemer; his reading profoundly influenced medieval Latin exegesis. John Chrysostom in his *Homilies on Job* hears the passage as both a moral example of faith and a prophetic anticipation of the resurrection. Augustine in the *City of God* (especially Book XXII) cites Job 19:25–27 as scriptural evidence for bodily resurrection. Thomas Aquinas in the *Expositio super Iob ad litteram* (c. 1261) devotes sustained attention to this passage, arguing with careful philosophical precision that the text speaks literally of bodily resurrection. Aquinas's reading, which is grammatically sensitive to the *mi-besari* phrase, defends the translation "in my flesh I will see God" as a legitimate rendering of the Hebrew.

John Calvin's 159 sermons on Job, preached in Geneva in 1554–1555, treat chapter 19 with pastoral intensity. Calvin reads *go'ali ḥai* as a confession of faith that Job reaches in the depths of his trial, and he interprets the passage as testifying to Christ as Redeemer without claiming that Job had explicit knowledge of the incarnation. Luther's commentary is less systematic but similarly reads the verse christologically. The most famous reception of the passage is of course musical: Charles Jennens's libretto for George Frideric Handel's *Messiah* (1741) sets "I know that my Redeemer liveth" as the great soprano aria of Part III, binding the text forever in Anglophone consciousness to Easter. The engraving "I KNOW THAT MY REDEEMER LIVETH" on Jennens's own monument in Gopsall Hall and on countless Victorian gravestones attests to the chapter's domestication as funeral text.

Modern critical scholarship has generally pulled back from the strong christological reading. Julius Wellhausen's source-critical separation of dialogue from frame did not fundamentally change the exegesis. Hermann Gunkel's form-criticism treated the *go'el* language as drawn from family law. Gerhard von Rad in *Wisdom in Israel* read chapter 19 as the moment at which the act-consequence nexus is first definitively broken through by an appeal that transcends it. David Clines in the WBC argues that *go'el* here is most plausibly read as a heavenly witness or advocate figure parallel to the "witness in heaven" of Job 16:19, not as God himself, and that the passage stops short of resurrection hope. Carol Newsom in *The Book of Job: A Contest of Moral Imaginations* reads the *go'el* declaration as a performative act of hope rather than a propositional claim about eschatology. Robert Gordis and J. Gerald Janzen both hear in the passage Job's wager that some vindicator must exist even when every human channel of advocacy has failed. Ellen Davis in *Getting Involved with God* emphasizes the chapter's language of embodiment. Susannah Ticciati's *Job and the Disruption of Identity* reads the *go'el* passage as the rupture through which a new Job emerges. Norman Habel's OTL commentary organizes the chapter around the inversion of the normal patron-client relations. John Hartley (NICOT), Francis Andersen (TOTC), and Mike Fyall (*Now My Eyes Have Seen You*) all defend a more traditional Christian reading while acknowledging the textual difficulty.

Convergences across Jewish and Christian readings: both traditions agree that Job is reaching for a vindicator who transcends his human kin; both take the passage as one of the climactic moments of the book; both acknowledge the textual fragility of verses 25–27. Divergences: Jewish readings overwhelmingly decline to make the passage about a personal resurrection and locate the vindication within a forensic-advocacy frame; older Christian readings hear a christological and resurrection promise; modern critical scholarship of both traditions has narrowed the gap by restraining the christological reading toward a forensic one.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

### Tension 1: Who is the *go'el*?

- **Catholic**: The *go'el* is ultimately Christ, prophetically glimpsed. Aquinas defends this as the literal sense — not merely a spiritual allegory but the grammatical meaning of a text that only the incarnation fully discloses.
- **Lutheran**: Job's confession is a moment of *fides ex auditu* — faith apart from sight. The identity of the redeemer is not specified; what matters is that Job breaks the retribution framework by reaching outside it.
- **Reformed Protestant**: Calvin's position: the *go'el* is God himself, acting as kinsman-redeemer. The passage anticipates Christ but is not reducible to prophecy. Faith precedes full understanding.
- **Eastern Orthodox**: The *go'el* is the Logos who will be incarnate, read through the lens of the *theoria* tradition. The passage is a patristic locus for the doctrine that the saints before Christ knew him by the Spirit.
- **Jewish (majority rabbinic)**: The *go'el* is God himself acting as kinsman, or an angelic advocate, with vindication occurring in this life or in the corporate memory of Israel. A personal bodily resurrection reading is alien to the passage's native idiom.
- **Critical (Clines, Newsom)**: The *go'el* is a heavenly witness or umpire figure akin to the *mokhiaḥ* of Job 16:19–21 — a third party in the lawsuit between Job and God, whose identity is deliberately underspecified. The passage is an extraordinary act of faith precisely because its object is indeterminate.

**Steelman the least intuitive (Critical)**: If the *go'el* is deliberately underspecified, then what Job is doing is more religiously radical than either the Christian or Jewish traditional readings allow. He is positing a vindicator whose existence is unconfirmed, whose identity is unknown, and whose intervention is future — and he is staking his integrity on the bare structure of that hope. This is a kind of faith that precedes and exceeds any creed, because it is not predicated on knowing who the redeemer is. It is the faith of someone who trusts that the structure of justice must somewhere hold, even if he cannot name the one who holds it. The rhetorical force of the passage is strongest when the *go'el* is least identified.

### Tension 2: Flesh and resurrection — does Job envision bodily vindication?

- **Catholic (Aquinas)**: Yes, *in carne mea* — "in my flesh I shall see God" is the correct translation, and the hope is for bodily resurrection, even if Job's knowledge is only partial.
- **Reformed (Calvin)**: Yes in substance, though Job's own understanding is shadowed. The doctrine of resurrection is implicit.
- **Lutheran**: The passage testifies to a hope that the cross and resurrection of Christ will fully clarify. Job sees through a glass darkly.
- **Orthodox**: The bodily dimension is essential; the flesh is not to be stripped away but transfigured. The passage supports the tradition of deification in the flesh.
- **Jewish (majority)**: The *mi-besari* phrase most plausibly means "apart from my flesh" or "because of my flesh" in a present-life sense. The passage is not about bodily resurrection but about vindication now or in memory.
- **Critical**: The grammar genuinely allows either reading. Most modern scholarship (Clines, Newsom, Pope) leans toward "apart from my flesh" or a non-resurrection reading because bodily resurrection is not attested as a settled belief in the book's likely milieu. The passage stands at the edge of a hope that was not yet fully articulated.

**Steelman the least intuitive (Jewish)**: Read *mi-besari* as "because of my flesh" — the flesh that is being destroyed, the flesh that becomes the ground from which Job cries out, the flesh that makes the cry possible. Job is not asking for the flesh to be replaced or resurrected; he is asking that the very suffering flesh be the locus of the vision. This is a theology in which embodiment is not an obstacle to seeing God but the condition for it, and in which the language of resurrection becomes not a metaphysical claim about post-mortem existence but a phenomenological claim about how the sufferer experiences divine presence. This is harder to accept than either the Christian or the critical reading because it refuses both the comfort of afterlife and the cold rigor of reductionism.

### Where this leaves us

Job 19 is a text whose force comes from its textual fragility. The most famous lines in the book are the most disputed; the most hopeful are spoken from the most desolated body. The chapter invites us to inscribe Job's words in the rock — to make them durable precisely because they were voiced from ruin. What the chapter does not resolve is this: *if the identity of the go'el is genuinely underspecified, does the power of Job's declaration depend on that underspecification, or does it require the later identification to be complete? Does Christian exegesis complete the text, or does it domesticate it?*

## The Thread

- **Ruth 4:1–10**: The enactment of *go'el* law in the case of Ruth and Boaz. Reading Job 19 against Ruth 4 reveals the juridical machinery Job is pressing into theological service — a widow's redemption through a kinsman becomes a sufferer's cosmic hope.
- **Isaiah 44:6**: God identifies himself as "the first and the last" (*rishon ve-aḥaron*). The *aḥaron* of Job 19:25 resonates with this divine self-designation, which is why some Christian readings hear the *go'el* as God himself.
- **Daniel 12:2**: "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake." The phrase *afar* (dust) in Job 19:25 links to Daniel's later, explicit resurrection hope. The Joban moment is precisely where this hope is glimpsed but not yet articulated.
- **1 Corinthians 15:20**: Paul's "Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep." Reading Paul against Job 19 reveals how Christian tradition took Job's fragile, contested hope and built upon it a settled doctrine — an act of reception that illuminates both texts.
