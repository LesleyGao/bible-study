# Job 27: The Oath and the Missing Voice

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Job 27 opens with an unusual editorial notation — וַיֹּסֶף אִיּוֹב שְׂאֵת מְשָׁלוֹ (*wayyosef ʾiyyov seʾet meshalo*, "And Job again took up his discourse") — a formula the book elsewhere uses (29:1) to mark a shift in speech-act, typically indicating that a new unit has begun or that an interruption has preceded. The placement here is suggestive: no speech of Zophar precedes it, and Bildad's third speech (chapter 25) is conspicuously truncated to six verses. The third cycle of the dialogue (22–27) is, in the critical consensus stretching from S. R. Driver and G. B. Gray (ICC, 1921) through Marvin Pope (Anchor Bible), David Clines (WBC), and Norman Habel (OTL), the most disturbed stretch of text in the book. The chapter seems to contain two incompatible voices: vv. 1–12, which sound unmistakably like Job's defiant oath of integrity, and vv. 13–23, which articulate precisely the retributive theology of the wicked-cut-off-by-God that the friends have been pressing and Job has been resisting.

This voicing problem is not modern squeamishness; it is old. The medieval commentators noticed it. Rashi wrestles with why Job would suddenly endorse his friends' retributive framework. Ibn Ezra offers a subtle reading whereby Job describes the wicked's portion without conceding that he himself falls under it. But the simplest modern solution is the text-critical one: vv. 13–23 (or some portion of them) are a misplaced speech belonging to Zophar, whose expected third speech is otherwise absent from the text. Pope reassigns 27:13–23 to Zophar. Clines assigns 27:7–10 and 13–23 to Zophar. Habel assigns 27:13–23 to the missing Zophar speech. Westermann and Fohrer largely concur. A minority — Robert Gordis (*The Book of God and Man*) and Francis Andersen (Tyndale) — defend the chapter's unity, arguing that Job is either quoting the friends to mock them or is articulating a retributive theology that he believes applies to his *enemies* but not to himself (picking up on v. 7, "let my enemy be as the wicked").

On dating, the usual divisions hold. Pope, Clines, and Newsom argue post-exilic composition — the book as a reckoning with the theodic failure exposed by 587 BC. Gordis and Andersen argue pre-exilic, perhaps seventh-century, citing archaic Hebrew and the patriarchal world of the frame. Ezekiel 14:14 and 14:20 ("Noah, Danel, and Job") indicate that Job was a legendary figure by the sixth century BC, but the figure and the book are not the same. Kugel (*The Great Shift*) emphasizes that Job's characterization of God in chapter 27 — a God who has "taken away my right" — represents a mode of religious speech nearly unimaginable in pre-exilic Israelite piety, suggesting the book's final form belongs to a later, more theologically strained period.

The ancient Near Eastern background remains essential. The *Babylonian Theodicy* contains a long speech from the sufferer's friend enumerating the fates of the wicked — pestilence, loss of heirs, ruined wealth — in language remarkably close to Job 27:13–23. The *Ludlul Bel Nemeqi*'s righteous sufferer protests his innocence with an oath-language comparable to Job's here. The Ugaritic tradition supplies vocabulary: the "east wind" (רוּחַ קָדִים, *ruaḥ qadim*, v. 21) that carries off the wicked echoes the destructive Mediterranean sirocco invoked in West Semitic curse texts. 11QTargum Job preserves most of chapter 27 in Aramaic translation; the LXX compresses several verses, characteristic of the Old Greek translator's tendency to shorten Joban speeches whose theology he found difficult.

Hebrew terms carry the chapter's weight. תֻּמָּה (*tummah*, v. 5) — "integrity," "blamelessness" — is the same root (תָּם, *tam*) used to describe Job in the prologue (1:1). When Job says "I will not put away my *tummah*," he is clinging to the very quality the narrator ascribed to him before the disasters. חָנֵף (*ḥanef*, v. 8) — "godless," "hypocrite," one whose piety is corrupt — is the standard wisdom-literature term for the morally compromised. שָׁדַי (*Shaddai*, vv. 2, 10, 11, 13) — "the Almighty," the divine name most concentrated in Job, occurring thirty-one times — has disputed etymology (from *shadad*, "to destroy"; from *shad*, "breast"; from Akkadian *shadu*, "mountain") but consistently marks a God of overwhelming power. נַחֲלָה (*naḥalah*, v. 13) — "heritage," "inheritance" — locates the wicked's fate within covenantal land-vocabulary, ironic for a book set outside the covenant proper. חַי־אֵל (*ḥay-ʾel*, v. 2) — "as God lives" — is the standard Hebrew oath formula, invoking God's life as the guarantor of truthful speech. The irony is pointed: Job swears by the very God he accuses of denying him justice.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter divides structurally into three units. Verses 1–6 form Job's great oath of integrity, built on the formula *ḥay-ʾel* and culminating in the climactic "till I die I will not put away my integrity." Verses 7–12 form a transitional middle, moving from oath to the rhetorical question of what becomes of the godless and ending with Job's sharp rebuke to his friends: "why then have you become altogether vain?" Verses 13–23 then present a sustained description of the wicked man's portion (חֵלֶק רָשָׁע, *ḥeleq rashaʿ*) in ten verses of vivid destructive imagery — children killed by sword, pestilence burying survivors, silver heaped only to be worn by the righteous, the house built like a moth's web, the east wind hurling the wicked away.

The seam between vv. 12 and 13 is the textual crux. Clines observes that Job would have to undergo a complete theological reversal to deliver vv. 13–23 in his own voice after the oath of vv. 1–6. Habel catalogues the disruption in terms of formal features: the voice-shift, the sudden switch to the third-person wicked man, the disappearance of Job's personal complaint. Alter, however, in *The Wisdom Books*, argues for a possible literary function even if the voicing is disturbed: the chapter may be deliberately juxtaposing Job's oath with the friends' expected retributive response precisely in order to stage the collapse of the dialogue. On this reading, the "intrusion" is the point — the third cycle breaks down because the debate has exhausted itself.

Hebrew poetic craftsmanship is dense. Verses 2–4 use a classic tricolon oath structure: invocation (*ḥay-ʾel*), condition (breath in me, spirit in nostrils), assertion (lips will not speak falsehood). The parallelism is climactic rather than synonymous — each line intensifies the last. Verse 3's נִשְׁמַת אֱלוֹהַּ בְּאַפִּי (*nishmat ʾeloah beʾappi*, "the spirit of God in my nostrils") echoes Genesis 2:7 (the breath God breathes into Adam) and binds Job's integrity to his very creaturehood. Verse 6's חָזַקְתִּי בְצִדְקָתִי (*ḥazaqti betsiqdati*) — "I have held fast to my righteousness" — uses a covenantal verb of holding fast (*ḥazaq*) that reappears in Deuteronomy for Israel's grip on torah.

The second half's imagery is equally crafted. Verse 18's "house like a moth's" uses the Hebrew עָשׁ (*ʿash*, "moth") — the creature whose habitation is its own consumed cloth, proverbial for fragility. The LXX translator, troubled by the image, rendered it differently, reading "spider" (comparable to Bildad's spider-web in 8:14). Verse 20's "terrors overtake him like a flood" uses בַּלָּהוֹת (*ballahot*), an intensive plural noun for dread, rare and almost exclusively Joban. Verse 21's *ruaḥ qadim* ("east wind") picks up the imagery of Exodus 14:21, where the east wind drives back the sea — here it carries off the wicked as Pharaoh was carried off. Verses 22–23 end the chapter with personification of the wind itself: it claps its hands, it hisses (יִשְׁרֹק, *yishroq*) — the onomatopoeic verb that imitates the hiss of wind and ridicule at once.

Chiasm binds vv. 13–23 around the figure of the wicked man's possessions: children, wealth, house, wealth, death — a ring structure that encloses and destroys. Inclusio ties vv. 2 and 13 together through the phrase "Almighty" (*Shaddai*), creating a frame that makes it formally plausible the whole chapter is meant to be read as one voice, whatever the historical genesis of the text.

Textual variants compound the problem. The LXX of Job 27 is slightly shorter, collapsing several verses, though 27:13–23 appears substantially intact. 11QTargum Job 27 mostly follows the Masoretic shape. The Peshitta occasionally softens the oath-language. The Targum of Job expands the opening formula theologically, naming Job as a prophet. No single ancient version resolves the voicing question.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Job sits in the Ketuvim (Writings) of the Hebrew Bible, typically grouped with Proverbs and Ecclesiastes as the three wisdom books. In Christian orderings, it opens the poetic section. Its placement frames chapter 27's tension: Proverbs promises that the wicked fall and the righteous prosper; Ecclesiastes doubts whether the distinction holds. Job 27 is the collision of both claims — an oath of righteousness by a man whose life has fallen like the wicked's, followed by a description of the wicked's doom that, read in Job's mouth, either contradicts his experience or condemns him by his own words.

The Jewish reception tradition wrestles deeply with this. *Bava Batra* 15a–16b records the Talmudic debate about Job's existence and era, and on this chapter specifically some rabbinic traditions read Job as *quoting* the friends' retributive theology rather than endorsing it. Rashi follows this interpretive move: Job in vv. 13–23 is reporting what *they* say about the wicked, not what he himself now believes. Ibn Ezra is more philological — he parses the pronouns and reads the wicked man's portion as descriptive of others, not Job. Maimonides, in *Guide of the Perplexed* III.22–23, treats the whole book as a philosophical parable; the speeches of each character, including apparent contradictions like Job 27, represent stages in the refinement of the doctrine of providence. Nachmanides reads with greater attention to esoteric meaning and divine mystery. In modernity, Martin Buber treats the chapter's oath as the paradigmatic moment of dialogical faith — Job addressing God in protest while still swearing by him. Jon Levenson reads the oath of integrity as the Hebrew Bible's strongest assertion of human moral standing before God. James Kugel traces in this chapter the development of a strand of Israelite piety that can hold both covenant faithfulness and protest in one breath. Avivah Zornberg reads Job's oath as the speech-act that constitutes selfhood in the face of divine silence.

Christian reception begins with Origen, who read Job's oath typologically — Job's insistence on integrity prefigures Christ's sinlessness. Chrysostom's homilies emphasized patience under trial. Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job*, the most influential medieval treatment, allegorized vv. 13–23 as the fate of reprobate sinners and the spiritual danger of worldly wealth; Gregory took the voicing problem as non-existent and read Job as prophetically warning against wickedness. Aquinas's *Expositio super Iob* handled the chapter's tension philosophically, arguing that Job's description of the wicked (vv. 13–23) describes their fate in the next life, compatible with Job's own experience of suffering as a righteous man in this life. Calvin preached on Job 27 in his 1554–55 sermon cycle, reading the oath as exemplary Christian perseverance and the retributive verses as a theological affirmation of divine justice ultimately if not immediately realized. Luther emphasized the oath as a creaturely act of faith under temptation. Wellhausen and Gunkel's nineteenth-century critical scholarship first posed the voicing question as a text-critical problem, and the twentieth-century commentary tradition — Pope, Clines, Habel, Hartley (NICOT), Newsom — has mostly accepted that vv. 13–23 are displaced, while disagreeing on whose speech they originally belonged to. Robert Gordis reads Job's oath as a prophetic speech-act in which the innocent sufferer asserts his standing rather than concede a false verdict. Ellen Davis reads it as a paradigm of moral imagination under duress. Susannah Ticciati reads it as a reconstruction of self before God. Robert Fyall attends to the cosmological imagery of the east wind and its mythological background.

One significant convergence runs across Jewish and Christian readings: both traditions, even when they resolve the voicing problem differently, treat Job 27:1–6 as among the most powerful oaths of integrity in all of scripture. A sharp divergence appears on the question of vv. 13–23: premodern Christian readers typically read them as Job's own words, confirming retributive justice; modern critical Christian and Jewish readers overwhelmingly read them as displaced or as quoted speech. The medieval Jewish tradition occupies a subtle middle — closer to the critical reading than to the Christian medieval one, but on theological rather than text-critical grounds.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Whose voice is Job 27:13–23?**

- *Zophar's missing third speech.* The broad critical consensus (Pope, Clines, Habel, Westermann, Fohrer, Newsom in qualified form) holds that vv. 13–23 (or a larger block including vv. 7–10) belongs to Zophar's absent third speech, dislocated in transmission. The argument is primarily formal: the third cycle is visibly disturbed, Bildad's speech is truncated, Zophar is silent, and the theology of vv. 13–23 matches Zophar's pattern closely. This is the most economical textual solution.

- *Job's own quoted speech.* Rashi, Gordis, Andersen, and some traditional readers argue Job is quoting the friends' theology — perhaps mockingly — either to throw it back at them (note v. 7, "let my enemy be as the wicked") or to grant the abstract principle while denying its application to himself. On this reading, the voicing is unified though complex, and no text-critical intervention is needed. The difficulty is that no quotation marker appears in the Hebrew, and the speech is too long to read comfortably as mockery alone.

- *Steelman — deliberate literary rupture.* Alter and readers attentive to literary form suggest a third possibility. The third cycle is *supposed* to break down. The dialogue has exhausted itself; no new arguments are coming; the text itself begins to tremble. Reading vv. 13–23 as a deliberate disruption — Job speaking in a borrowed voice because his own voice has been outrun by his suffering — preserves the integrity of the received text while taking the formal disturbance seriously. This reading treats the textual-critical problem as a feature of the literary design rather than a transmission accident.

**Tension 2: Can Job's oath ("I hold fast my righteousness and will not let it go") stand theologically?**

- *Yes — Job is right, as the frame will confirm.* The prologue declares Job blameless (1:1, 8; 2:3), and the divine speech in 42:7–8 endorses Job's words over the friends'. The oath of 27:1–6 is accordingly the book's central act of truthful speech. Job's integrity is not the pious denial of sin (he does not claim sinlessness, cf. 7:20–21) but the assertion that his suffering does not correspond to moral desert. On this reading, the oath is theologically vindicated by the book itself.

- *No — it is creaturely overreach.* A traditional reading, strong in Calvin and in Reformed theology generally, holds that no human being can claim righteousness before God. Job's oath, however exemplary as a protest against the friends' false accusations, becomes problematic when read as a claim over against God himself. The whirlwind speeches (38–41) accordingly humble Job not for specific sins but for the presumption of the oath — "I have uttered what I did not understand" (42:3). On this reading, Job's oath must be theologically qualified.

- *Steelman — the oath is covenantal rather than metaphysical.* Levenson's reading, and Janzen's, offer a subtler alternative. Job's oath is not an abstract metaphysical claim about sinlessness but a covenantal assertion of fidelity within relationship. He is not saying "I am without sin"; he is saying "I have not broken faith with God." This reading takes Job's oath as the Hebrew Bible's template for how an innocent sufferer can legitimately address God — not by denying all wrong but by refusing to accept a false verdict. The oath is prayer, not philosophy.

Where this leaves us: Job 27 stages, within its own textual confusion, the book's deepest theological problem. The oath of integrity and the retributive theology of the wicked's fate cannot both be true of Job's life. The book refuses to settle the tension within the chapter. Either Zophar has intruded, or Job has contradicted himself, or the text itself has broken under the strain. Any of these possibilities is a theological claim.

One unresolved question: When a speech of retribution can be spoken by Job and by Zophar interchangeably, what does it mean that the *same words* can be true in one mouth and false in another?

## The Thread

- **Psalm 17:3–5** — "If you try my heart, if you visit me by night, if you test me, you will find nothing... I have held fast to your paths; my feet have not slipped" — a Davidic oath of integrity in precisely Job's register. The psalm shows the oath's liturgical afterlife in Israel's prayer.

- **Isaiah 40:24** — "Scarcely are they planted, scarcely sown, scarcely has their stem taken root in the earth, when he blows on them, and they wither, and the tempest carries them off like stubble" — the same wind-of-judgment imagery as Job 27:21, deployed against the nations. Job 27 and Isaiah 40 share a vocabulary of divine sweeping-away, used in one case for the wicked individual and in the other for empires.

- **Proverbs 10:25** — "When the whirlwind passes, the wicked is no more, but the righteous is established forever" — the retributive principle of Job 27:13–23 distilled into a single proverb. The question is whether Job 27 confirms it, displaces it, or ironizes it.

- **James 5:11** — "You have heard of the steadfastness of Job, and you have seen the purpose of the Lord" — the New Testament's remembrance of Job through the lens of *hypomonē* (endurance), picking up precisely the oath-of-integrity Job of chapter 27 rather than the complaining Job of other chapters. James reads the book selectively, and the oath is what survives.
