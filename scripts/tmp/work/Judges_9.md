# Judges 9: A Millstone from a Tower

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The political world of Judges 9 is unusually well-documented archaeologically because the chapter is set in Shechem, one of the most thoroughly excavated cities in the Levant. Ernst Sellin began work at Tell Balata in 1913–14 and again 1926–34; G. Ernest Wright resumed excavations from 1956 to 1973 (the Drew-McCormick-ASOR campaign); Edward Campbell continued through the 1980s; and Lawrence Stager's later analyses have refined the chronology. The site has yielded the architectural object that the narrator names in v. 46 — the *Migdal Shechem*, the "Tower of Shechem," a massive Middle Bronze IIB fortified temple of the *migdal* type, with three-meter-thick walls and a sanctuary cella oriented toward Mount Gerizim. This temple, identified by Wright as the temple of Baal-berith / El-berith, was destroyed and rebuilt multiple times across the second millennium and remained in use into early Iron I. It is the building Abimelech burns. The narrator's casual references — "the oak of the pillar at Shechem" (v. 6), "the house of Baal-berith" (v. 4), "the stronghold of the house of El-berith" (v. 46) — all refer to a complex that still stands as a stratigraphic reality on the tell.

The Amarna correspondence (mid-fourteenth century BCE) gives the political backdrop. Tablet EA 252 is from Lab'ayu, ruler of Shechem, defending himself before Pharaoh against accusations of brigandage and disloyalty; EA 254 is from his sons. The Amarna Shechem under Lab'ayu was a chronic political problem for Egyptian-allied city-states, conducting raids, forming opportunistic alliances with the *ʿapiru*, and pursuing autonomous regional ambitions. The political pattern Lab'ayu represents — a Shechem-based strongman drawing his power from the city's leading men, pursuing regional dominance, vulnerable to internal coups — is the same pattern Abimelech reproduces several centuries later. Judges 9 reads like an Amarna political vignette transposed into Israelite tribal history; the continuity of Shechem's political character across the Late Bronze / Iron I transition is one of the chapter's strongest historical anchors.

The material details are precise. The "seventy pieces of silver" Abimelech receives from the temple of Baal-berith (v. 4) is a small but realistic temple loan; ANE temple economies routinely held precious-metal reserves and disbursed them for political and military purposes. The "worthless and reckless fellows" (*ʾanashim reqim u-foḥazim*) hired with this money are the Iron I equivalent of mercenary skirmishers — perhaps Habiru-style displaced men, drawn into Abimelech's service for pay and plunder. The "one stone" on which the seventy sons are killed (v. 5) is a sacrificial slaughter site; the deliberate ritualization of the killing — all on one stone — frames the act as a political-cultic offering rather than a battlefield casualty. The "salt" sown over the razed Shechem (v. 45) is a known ANE curse-ritual; the Hittite text of the treaty of Šuppiluliuma cursing rebellious Hapalla involves sowing the city with weeds, and the Aramaic Sefire treaty (KAI 222) curses violators with the parallel imagery. Salt-sowing is a symbolic act of permanent ritual decommissioning, not (as is sometimes claimed) a literal agricultural sterilization.

Mount Gerizim, where Jotham delivers his fable (v. 7), is the southern of the two mountains that flank Shechem; the natural acoustic of the site means that a speaker on Gerizim could be heard at the city gate below. Joshua 8:33–35 records the covenant ceremony at Shechem with the tribes arrayed between Gerizim (blessing) and Ebal (curse); Jotham positions himself at the place of blessing to deliver a curse, and the irony is the narrator's. The "Diviners' Oak" (*ʾElon Meʿonenim*, v. 37) is one of the named sacred trees of the Shechem landscape; the city's religious topography is dense.

The "upper millstone" (*pelaḥ rekheb*, v. 53) that kills Abimelech is the rotating top stone of a household saddle quern, weighing perhaps 4–8 kg in domestic use, larger for communal grinding. Throwing it from a tower roof is a defensive technique consistent with the layout of an Iron I migdal: the roof would be the highest accessible point, household objects would be the available ammunition, and women would be the noncombatants pressed into emergency defense. The detail is realistic and historically representative of urban siege conduct in the period.

A handful of Hebrew terms repay attention. **בַּעַל בְּרִית (Baʿal Berit)** — "Lord of the covenant," the patron deity of Shechem, called both *Baal Berit* (v. 4) and *El Berit* (v. 46); the dual naming suggests an old syncretic title (El, the high god, with Baal, the storm god, both associated with covenant-making). The Sychar/Shechem covenant cult had ancient roots in oath-making rituals; the patriarchal narratives' association of Shechem with covenant ceremonies (Genesis 33–34, Joshua 24) belongs to the same pattern. **מִגְדָּל (migdal)** — fortified tower; the architectural type is documented at Shechem itself, at Tel Balata, and across the Bronze Age Levant. **אָטָד (ʾatad)** — the bramble of Jotham's fable, a low-growing thorny shrub (probably *Lycium europaeum* or *Rhamnus*); useless for shade, a fire hazard, the agricultural antithesis of the noble fruit trees. **עוֹלֵל (ʿolel)** — to glean, the secondary harvest left for the poor, the basis of Gideon's quip in 8:2. **חֵרֶם (ḥerem)** is implied but not used; the destruction of Shechem with salt is functionally a *ḥerem* act.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Judges 9 is a literary tour de force and the longest narrative chapter in the book. Its structural backbone is a triadic curse-and-fulfillment scheme that Yairah Amit, Daniel Block, and Barry Webb have all mapped: Jotham's curse in vv. 16–20 specifies that fire should come from Abimelech and devour Shechem, and from Shechem and devour Abimelech; the chapter then shows the fulfillment in painstaking detail — Abimelech burns the men of Shechem in the El-berith temple (vv. 46–49), and a woman of Thebez crushes his skull with a millstone (vv. 53–54). The narrator's editorial coda in vv. 56–57 closes the loop explicitly: "Thus God returned the evil of Abimelech... and upon them came the curse of Jotham." It is one of the cleanest curse-fulfillment narratives in the Hebrew Bible.

Jotham's fable (vv. 7–15) is the central literary set-piece of the chapter and the only extended political fable in the Hebrew Bible. The genre is well-attested in the wider ancient Near East: the Akkadian *Tamarisk and Date Palm* and *Ox and Horse* disputation poems, the Sumerian disputations between *Bird and Fish*, *Hoe and Plow*, and *Summer and Winter*, are all related forms in which non-human entities (plants, animals, abstractions) debate or arrange themselves into hierarchies as a vehicle for political or ethical commentary. Aesop's beast fables share the genre. Niditch and Brettler both place Jotham's fable squarely in the disputation tradition; the closest parallel may be the Aesopic fable of the trees choosing a king, which suggests a shared Mediterranean folkloric stock. The fable's structure is elegant: three noble fruit trees (olive, fig, vine) are asked to reign and refuse, each citing the productive work that kingship would interrupt; the bramble alone accepts, and his acceptance is conditional on the trees' good faith and includes a threat ("let fire come out of the bramble and devour the cedars of Lebanon"). The political logic is sharp. The productive members of a society do not want to rule because rule means abandoning their productive work; the worthless members (the bramble) seek rule and threaten destruction if their authority is questioned. The fable is anti-monarchic in a particular way: it does not say all kingship is bad; it says that the people who *want* to be king are precisely the people who should not be, and that the cost of bad leadership is mutual destruction.

The fable's interpretation in vv. 16–20 is the chapter's editorial signature. Jotham translates the fable into political accusation with a conditional structure: *if* you acted in good faith in making Abimelech king, then rejoice; *if not*, then the mutual destruction will follow. The conditional is rhetorical — the audience already knows it has not acted in good faith — but the formal structure preserves the fable's ethical neutrality. Jotham does not pronounce; he proposes the test, and the chapter shows the test passing.

The chapter's narrative pacing is unusually sophisticated. Yairah Amit's structural analysis identifies five movements: (1) Abimelech's coup at Shechem (vv. 1–6); (2) Jotham's fable and curse on Gerizim (vv. 7–21); (3) the Gaal episode and the fragmentation of Shechemite loyalty (vv. 22–41); (4) Abimelech's destruction of Shechem (vv. 42–49); (5) Abimelech's death at Thebez (vv. 50–55), with editorial summary (vv. 56–57). Each movement contains its own internal escalation; the cumulative effect is the steady tightening of the curse-and-fulfillment loop. Robert Alter notes the deliberate verbal echoes that hold the chapter together: *fire* (the bramble's threat, the Migdal burning, Abimelech's torches), *brothers* (the seventy on a stone, "is he not our brother?", the sister-cities of Shechem and Thebez), *one stone* (the seventy slaughtered, the millstone that kills the killer).

The Gaal son of Ebed episode (vv. 26–41) is a small political comedy embedded in the larger tragedy. Gaal arrives at Shechem, drinks at the festival, denounces Abimelech in his cups, and promises that if he were in charge he would handle things differently; Zebul, Abimelech's mole inside the city, sends to Abimelech for the ambush; Gaal at the morning gate sees the men coming and is told by Zebul "you mistake the shadow of the mountains for men" — until it is too late. The episode is sharply observed: a drunk pretender, a cynical insider, a fatal delay before recognition, a one-day rebellion. Soggin reads the Gaal pericope as preserving an older oral tradition with a different geographical orientation (Gaal speaks of "the men of Hamor the father of Shechem" — a memory of the Genesis 34 dynasty) that the deuteronomistic editor has incorporated without fully integrating. The seam is visible.

Textually, Judges 9 is more variant-rich than chapters 7 and 8. LXX A and LXX B differ substantially in vv. 28–29 (Gaal's speech is shorter in B), and in the place names of vv. 41 and 50. The reference to "the oak of the pillar" (*ʾelon mutsav*, v. 6) is rendered variously and may preserve an older textual form alluding to a standing-stone cult site, perhaps the same as Joshua 24:26's stone "under the oak in the sanctuary of the LORD." The Hebrew of v. 31 ("secretly," *be-tarmah*) is a hapax that LXX renders interpretively. There is no DSS fragment of Judges 9 of textual consequence.

In the larger argument of Judges, chapter 9 is the chapter where the deuteronomistic editor's anti-monarchic argument reaches its fiercest and clearest expression. It is also the only chapter in which YHWH is essentially absent as an actor: the editor reports that "God sent an evil spirit between Abimelech and the leaders of Shechem" (v. 23) and pronounces the divine retribution (vv. 56–57), but the active agency in the chapter is human throughout. Brueggemann reads this as deliberate: the absence of YHWH from the surface of the narrative is the editor's point — when human beings make a king the way Shechem made Abimelech, they have already excluded YHWH, and the political logic plays out without divine intervention until the closing summary collects the wreckage.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc that Judges 9 contributes to is the long argument about kingship, but at a more concrete level it is the Hebrew Bible's most fully developed case study of usurpation and tyrannicide. The chapter is in conversation with two other dynastic-bloodbath narratives: Jehu's destruction of the house of Ahab in 2 Kings 10 (where seventy sons of Ahab are killed and their heads piled at the city gate of Jezreel — a deliberate echo of Abimelech's seventy brothers on one stone), and Athaliah's massacre of the Davidic line in 2 Kings 11. The pattern of the chapter — usurper kills the legitimate sons, occupies the throne, is destroyed by a counter-coup — is the generic political tragedy that the deuteronomistic historian rehearses in multiple registers.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Pseudo-Philo's *LAB* (chs. 37–38) gives the Abimelech story significant retelling, with additions and ethical commentary; the *LAB* expands Jotham's fable, presents Abimelech's death as exemplary divine justice, and treats the woman of Thebez as a heroic instrument of providence. Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.7.1–5) sanitizes nothing in the Abimelech story; he presents the chapter straightforwardly as a tragedy and frames the moral lesson as a warning about ambition unrestrained by piety. The Talmud cites Abimelech in *Sotah* 9b as one of the figures whose punishment fit his crime — the man who killed his brothers on a stone is killed by a stone — and uses him as a paradigm of *middah ke-neged middah* (measure for measure) divine justice. Rashi on 9:5 emphasizes the ritual character of the seventy-brother killing and treats it as the sin from which all subsequent disasters in the chapter flow. Radak on 9:53 highlights the woman's act as divine providence working through a humble instrument. Ralbag, characteristically philosophical, reads the chapter as a study in political contingency and the natural-causal mechanisms by which evil regimes destroy themselves, with God orchestrating the natural process. Jon Levenson's broader work on covenant theology reads Jotham's fable as one of the Hebrew Bible's clearest articulations of the social contract: legitimate authority requires good faith reciprocity, and authority gained without it cannot endure. Yair Zakovitch and Yairah Amit treat the chapter as the editorial high point of the book's anti-monarchic argument; for Zakovitch the curse-fulfillment structure is too clean to be anything but composed.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Origen, in *Homilies on Judges* and in his *Commentary on the Song of Songs*, treats Jotham's fable allegorically: the olive, fig, and vine are the patriarchs (or, in a different reading, the prophets), the bramble is Antichrist, and the fire is eschatological judgment. Augustine, in *City of God* 17.15 and in his *Sermons*, reads the Abimelech story as a paradigm of the *civitas terrena* — the worldly city built on fratricide (echoing Cain and Romulus), destined to collapse into the violence on which it was founded. Augustine's reading is one of the earliest sustained theological interpretations of political violence in the Christian tradition, and Jotham's fable becomes for him a prooftext of the inherent instability of regimes built on the destruction of legitimate authority. Theodoret of Cyrus on Judges 9 treats Abimelech as a type of the antichrist figure of 2 Thessalonians 2; the typology is loose but generative for medieval reception. Aquinas, in *De Regimine Principum* and the *Summa* (II-II, q. 42 on sedition), uses Jotham's fable to develop a doctrine of legitimate resistance: the bramble's claim to authority is forfeit because of how it was acquired, and resistance to such authority is licit. The Calvinist political tradition seizes on this Aquinas reading and develops it into a full theory of resistance to tyranny: Theodore Beza's *Du Droit des Magistrats* and the *Vindiciae contra Tyrannos* both cite Jotham's fable as scriptural warrant for the proposition that an illegitimately constituted authority may be opposed by legitimate magistrates. Calvin's own sermons on Judges (1561) develop the same doctrine more cautiously. Luther, in his *Lectures on Judges* and in scattered references, treats the chapter as a mirror of God's hidden hand in political affairs and uses Abimelech as an example of a king "anointed" by the people rather than by God — a category Luther applied to several political opponents. Critical scholarship (Boling, Soggin, Block, Webb, Younger, Niditch) reads the chapter as the editorial expression of the deuteronomistic anti-monarchic strand; Brueggemann reads it as Hebrew Bible's most developed political theology of accountability. Block, Webb, and Niditch all attend to the unnamed woman of Thebez whose millstone closes the curse and to Abimelech's deathbed concern that the story not record a woman as his killer — the narrative's quiet insistence that the chapter's curse-fulfillment runs through the very figure the king tried to write out.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Both Jewish and Christian traditions read the chapter as a paradigm of divine retribution and as a warning about illegitimate authority. The Jewish tradition (Talmud, Rashi, Radak) emphasizes the *middah ke-neged middah* logic — the man who killed by stone is killed by stone, the man who killed brothers is destroyed by a brother-city. The Christian tradition (Augustine, Aquinas, the Reformed political theorists) develops a more elaborated political theology — the chapter becomes scriptural warrant for theories of legitimate resistance and for the doctrine of the *civitas terrena*. The deepest convergence is on Jotham's fable: both traditions read it as a critique of self-seeking authority and as evidence that the productive members of a society are reluctant to rule because rule is incompatible with productive work. The deepest divergence is on the woman of Thebez: the Jewish tradition (Pseudo-Philo, Radak) makes her a heroic instrument of providence; the patristic Christian tradition is largely silent about her; modern commentary (Block, Niditch, Webb) reads her action as the chapter's hinge, the figure through whom the curse runs and whom Abimelech's deathbed line cannot quite expunge from the record.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What the deuteronomistic editor believed God was doing in chapter 9 is given in the editorial coda (vv. 56–57): God returned the evil of Abimelech onto his head and the evil of the men of Shechem onto theirs, and the curse of Jotham was fulfilled. The chapter is structured as a theodicy in the form of a curse-and-fulfillment narrative. But the structure generates two genuine tensions that the editor does not finally resolve.

**Tension One: The moral status of Jotham's curse and the political theology of resistance.** Jotham pronounces a conditional curse on Mount Gerizim and the chapter shows the curse's fulfillment with notarial precision. Was the curse a prophetic word from God, a political prediction, or a private imprecation that God chose to honor? The Catholic tradition (Aquinas, the medieval canon lawyers) developed a doctrine of legitimate resistance to tyranny that treats Jotham's fable as a theological foundation for the proposition that a regime constituted by fratricide is illegitimate from its inception and that resistance to it is not rebellion but justice. The Reformed tradition (Beza, the *Vindiciae*, the later Puritan resistance theorists) extended the doctrine into a full theology of magisterial resistance: lesser magistrates may oppose unjust kings, citing Jotham as scriptural warrant. The Lutheran tradition is more cautious; Luther's "two kingdoms" doctrine generally counsels obedience to civil authority even when corrupt, and Luther's reading of Judges 9 emphasizes God's hidden working through political collapse rather than human active resistance. The Orthodox tradition has historically been more comfortable with imperial authority and reads the chapter as a warning against ambition rather than as a charter for resistance. The Jewish tradition is divided: the rabbis broadly accept the *dina de-malkhuta dina* principle (the law of the kingdom is law) but preserve a strong tradition that illegitimate authority forfeits its claim. The critical reading (Brueggemann, Brettler) treats the chapter as deuteronomistic political theology in its purest form: a regime built on the destruction of legitimate authority destroys itself by its own internal logic, with God ratifying the natural process. The position least intuitive to a modern liberal reader is probably the Lutheran one — that even a manifestly illegitimate regime should not be actively resisted by private citizens, and that God's hidden working is the answer to political evil rather than human counter-violence. Steelmanned: a chapter in which the destruction is universal (Shechem destroyed, Abimelech destroyed, Israel left to start over) is a chapter that does not finally vindicate any human party; the divine vindication is the curse's fulfillment, not the success of any earthly resistance, and Jotham himself flees and never returns. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter authorizes the language of curse and consequence without authorizing any party's claim to be the agent of that consequence; the only winner in the chapter is the deuteronomistic editor's theology, and the only survivor in the narrative is the man who walked away.

**Tension Two: Divine retribution and the moral economy of mass violence.** The chapter's editorial coda celebrates the destruction of "the men of Shechem" as just retribution for their complicity in the murder of the seventy. But the chapter has shown the destruction at first hand: Abimelech burns one thousand men and women in the temple of El-berith (v. 49), razes the city, sows it with salt, and then attacks Thebez and is killed there. The "men of Shechem" against whom the curse is supposedly directed are not only the political leaders who voted for Abimelech; they include the ordinary inhabitants who burn in the temple. The Augustinian and Reformed traditions tend to accept the editor's theodicy at face value: the city collectively chose Abimelech, and collective punishment is just. The Catholic just-war tradition is more uncomfortable: the killing of noncombatants, even in a context of collective civic guilt, requires moral analysis the chapter does not provide. The Orthodox tradition, with its strong sense of the cosmic and corporate dimensions of sin, accepts the corporate-guilt frame more readily but reads the destruction as tragic rather than triumphant. The Jewish tradition (especially Levinas in his Talmudic readings, drawing on the Talmudic principle that the righteous die for the sins of their generation) registers the asymmetry: the destruction is just at the level of cosmic accounting and tragic at the level of individual lives. The historical-critical reading (Brettler, Niditch) presses the further question: the noncombatants of Shechem are killed under the rubric of "the men of Shechem," and their actual participation in the chapter's politics (which made Abimelech king) was nonexistent; the destruction is a moral problem that the editor's theology smooths over too quickly. The position least intuitive to most modern readers is the Augustinian-Reformed corporate-guilt one — that an entire city can be morally complicit in the act of its leaders. Steelmanned: a society is constituted by relations of solidarity, and solidarity is morally serious; the citizens of Shechem who were not at the meeting that crowned Abimelech were nevertheless living in a city whose temple disbursed seventy pieces of silver to fund the assassination, and they did not leave. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter's theology of consequence is more austere than most modern moral frameworks can accept, and the editor does not stop to soften it; the curse falls on the city, not only on the council, and the woman with the millstone is the only character whose moral standing the chapter is willing to underwrite without reservation.

The chapter ends with a man begging to be killed by his own sword so that history will not record him as having been killed by a woman, and he gets his wish — and then 2 Samuel 11:21 records him as having been killed by a woman anyway. What sort of God arranges that the very thing the killer feared most should become the way his story is told?

## The Thread

The "killed by a woman" theme is one of the chapter's deepest threads. Joab, in 2 Samuel 11:21, cites the death of "Abimelech the son of Jerubbesheth" (a deliberate substitution of *bosheth*, "shame," for *baal*, in the deuteronomistic editor's signature theophoric scrubbing) and asks "did not a woman cast an upper millstone on him from the wall, so that he died at Thebez?" The line is Joab's case-law citation that besieging armies should not approach city walls too closely. The narrator of 2 Samuel preserves the very memory Abimelech tried to expunge.

The seventy sons of Ahab killed and their heads piled at Jezreel's gate in 2 Kings 10 is a deliberate echo of the seventy sons of Jerubbaal killed on one stone. When you reach the Jehu narrative, hold Judges 9 next to it; the deuteronomistic historian is rhyming his political tragedies on purpose.

When you get to 1 Kings 12:25 and Jeroboam establishing his kingdom at Shechem after the division of the kingdom, remember Abimelech. The Shechem-as-political-capital pattern is older than Jeroboam and the editor wants you to feel the rhyme: Shechem is the city that elevates kings YHWH did not authorize, and the pattern has consequences.

When you get to Matthew 13:55 and Jesus' brothers being named — "James and Joseph and Simon and Judas" — and to the recurring Gospel motif of "is he not our brother?" (the very logic Abimelech used to claim Shechem in 9:2–3), the pattern of kinship-as-political-claim has a long Hebrew Bible genealogy. The Gospels are working with material the deuteronomistic editor knew very well.
