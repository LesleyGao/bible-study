# Jeremiah 22: The Four Kings and the Cedar Palace

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jeremiah 22 is a compressed history of the last decades of the Davidic monarchy, told as a series of oracles against four kings. The chapter spans roughly twenty-two years — from the death of Josiah at Megiddo in 609 BC to the second deportation of 597 — and yet it is composed as a unity, almost certainly assembled by Baruch and the prophet's circle as a single literary indictment of the dynasty.

The four kings appear in this order: **Shallum / Jehoahaz** (22:11–12), the son of Josiah, who reigned three months in 609 BC before Pharaoh Necho II deported him to Egypt (2 Kings 23:31–34); **Jehoiakim** (22:13–19), Josiah's elder son installed by Necho as a puppet, who reigned 609–598 and is the most savagely denounced king in the entire Hebrew Bible; **Coniah / Jehoiachin / Jeconiah** (22:24–30), Jehoiakim's son who reigned three months in 597 before surrendering to Nebuchadnezzar; and **Zedekiah** (Mattaniah), implicit throughout but addressed in chapter 21 — the last Davidic king, blinded and deported in 586. Lundbom (Anchor) treats chapters 21–23 as a deliberately constructed "kings collection" framed by the Branch oracle as its messianic resolution.

The material world of this chapter is very specific. Verses 13–17 describe Jehoiakim's cedar palace — *I will build myself a great house with spacious upper rooms… paneling it with cedar and painting it with vermilion* (22:14). The archaeological correlate is almost certainly **Ramat Rachel**, a royal estate on a hill three kilometers south of the City of David, excavated by Yohanan Aharoni in 1959–1962 and re-excavated by Oded Lipschits and Manfred Oeming from 2005–2010. The site yielded an Iron II palace with proto-Aeolic capitals, a sophisticated hydraulic system, and one of the largest collections of *lmlk* royal storage jar handles in Judah. The current excavators identify it as the *Beth-haccerem* of Jeremiah 6:1 and a likely candidate for the very palace condemned here. The cedar comes from Lebanon — the Phoenician trade route that ran through Tyre and Sidon and required either coercive extraction (the corvée labor of v. 13's *makes his neighbor serve him for nothing*) or expensive royal silver. The vermilion (*shashar*, שָׁשַׁר) is a red pigment imported from Egypt, mentioned only here and in Ezekiel 23:14 in the entire Hebrew Bible, and it signals luxury at the level of imperial decadence.

The historicity of Coniah / Jehoiachin's exile is one of the most spectacularly confirmed events in the Hebrew Bible. The **Weidner Tablets** — four cuneiform ration lists discovered in the southern citadel near the Ishtar Gate of Babylon by Robert Koldewey's German expedition between 1899 and 1917, published by Ernst Weidner in 1939 — record monthly rations of oil and barley issued from Nebuchadnezzar's royal stores between 595 and 570 BC. Among the recipients are *Yaukin shar mat Yahudu* — "Jehoiachin king of the land of Judah" — along with his five sons and a Judean court entourage. The ration tablets confirm what 2 Kings 25:27–30 reports: that Jehoiachin was kept alive in Babylon, even with royal status, and that Evil-Merodach (Amel-Marduk) elevated him to a place of honor in 561 BC. The tablets are now housed in the Vorderasiatisches Museum in Berlin. Jeremiah 22:24–30, written before this elevation, predicts only Jehoiachin's exile and dynastic disinheritance — not his survival. The historical irony is that **Zerubbabel**, the leader of the post-exilic return in 538, is explicitly Jehoiachin's grandson (1 Chr 3:17–19; Hag 1:1), and Haggai 2:23 deliberately reverses Jeremiah's image: *I will take you, O Zerubbabel… and make you like a signet ring*. The curse of 22:24 is undone by the prophecy of Haggai 2:23 — a seam in the canon that has occupied interpreters from Sanhedrin 38a to Calvin to Raymond Brown.

Bullae from late-monarchic Jerusalem confirm the official cast of this period: the **Pashhur bulla** (City of David excavations, 2008, by Eilat Mazar), the **Gemariah ben Shaphan bulla** (Yigal Shiloh's excavations, 1982), the **Jehucal ben Shelemiah bulla** (Mazar, 2005), the **Gedaliah ben Pashhur bulla** (Mazar, 2008). The cumulative effect is that the men around Jeremiah are not literary inventions; they left their seal impressions in the burned debris of Stratum 10.

Key terms in this chapter operate as theological compressions. *Mishpat u-tzedaqah* (מִשְׁפָּט וּצְדָקָה, "justice and righteousness," 22:3, 15) is the irreducible double-formula of prophetic ethics — Amos 5:24, Isaiah 1:17, Micah 6:8 all converge here. *Da'at oti* (דַּעַת אֹתִי, "knowing me," 22:16) is the verse that compresses prophetic theology into eight Hebrew words: *Is not this to know me? declares the LORD*. *Chotam* (חוֹתָם, "signet ring," 22:24) is the royal authority-seal worn on the right hand, the very symbol Haggai 2:23 reuses. *Coniah* (כָּנְיָהוּ, 22:24, 28) is a deliberately diminished form of *Jeconiah / Jehoiachin* (יְהֹויָכִין), a kind of throne-name demotion built into the prophet's vocabulary itself.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is a tightly engineered literary unit. Verses 1–9 are prose introduction, presenting the unconditional indictment in conditional form (*if you will indeed obey… but if you will not obey*); the rhetorical effect is a contract that has already been broken. Verses 10–12 are an oracle on Shallum / Jehoahaz, framed as a counter-lament: *Weep not for him who is dead* (Josiah, who died at Megiddo) *but weep bitterly for him who goes away* (Jehoahaz, deported to Egypt). The poetic device is *paranomasia* on Shallum — a name meaning "peaceful one" used here for a king dying in foreign exile.

Verses 13–19 are the **woe-oracle** on Jehoiakim. The form is the *hoy*-oracle (הוֹי), with characteristic three-beat lines and the prophet-against-tyrant register that runs from Isaiah 5:8–24 to Habakkuk 2:6–20. The literary climax is verses 15–16: a rhetorical question that compares Jehoiakim unfavorably with his father Josiah. The Hebrew of v. 15 is austere and pointed — *ha-meleq ata ki ata mit'achareh ba-arez* (הֲמֶלֶךְ אַתָּה כִּי אַתָּה מִתְחָרֶה בָאָרֶז), literally "Are you a king because you compete in cedar?" The verb *mit'achareh* is denominative from *erez*, "cedar"; the prophet has coined a verb to mock the king's architectural self-aggrandizement. Verse 19 — *with the burial of a donkey he shall be buried, dragged and dumped beyond the gates of Jerusalem* — is one of the most violent royal indictments in any ancient Near Eastern text, more savage than anything in Akkadian royal-curse literature.

Verses 20–23 are a **lament-oracle** addressed to personified Jerusalem ("inhabitant of Lebanon, nested among the cedars" — the cedars are now the city's identification, having been built into her palaces). Verses 24–30 are the **signet-ring oracle** on Coniah, structured around a triple address: the divine oath (vv. 24–27), the broken-pot lament (v. 28), the threefold land-address (*eretz, eretz, eretz*, "O land, land, land," v. 29), and the formal disinheritance (*write this man down as childless*, v. 30). The threefold address to the land is a literary signal: it is the rhetorical climax that names the audience that outlasts the kings. Holladay (Hermeneia) read this triple-call as a deliberate echo of the *Shema*'s threefold structure, turned from confession into lament.

Genre-wise the chapter weaves together prose oracle (1–9), counter-lament (10–12), woe-oracle (13–19), lament-apostrophe (20–23), and disinheritance-oracle (24–30). McKane (ICC) read this layering as evidence of a long redactional process; Lundbom defended its compositional unity. The MT and LXX of this chapter are close, with minor variants; the Qumran fragment 4QJer-a preserves portions of vv. 4–16 that match the MT.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical trajectory of this chapter is shaped above all by verse 16 — *Is not this to know me? declares the LORD* — and by the Coniah-Zerubbabel arc that crosses from this chapter to Haggai 2:23.

In the Jewish trajectory, Sirach 49:4–5 reads the kings of Judah after Josiah as those who "abandoned the law of the Most High." The Bavli at **Sanhedrin 38a** preserves a remarkable rabbinic move: the rabbis read Jeremiah 22:30's *write this man down as childless* alongside 1 Chronicles 3:17 (which lists Jeconiah's sons) and conclude that Jeconiah was forgiven on Yom Kippur in his Babylonian exile, and his sons were granted to him *after* his repentance. **Yoma 67b** reinforces this: the curse is conditional, the repentance unbinds it. **Rashi** on Jeremiah 22:30 explicitly cites the Sanhedrin reading: the curse means "childless during his days" — i.e., no son of his will inherit the throne while he lives — but the line continues. **Radak** harmonizes with Haggai. **Abrabanel** on this chapter develops one of his most sustained meditations: he reads Jehoiakim as the type of every tyrant who confuses architectural grandeur with kingship, and he reads Coniah as the type of repentance under judgment. Maimonides (*Hilkhot Melakhim* 11–12) treats the Davidic line through Coniah as theologically unbroken.

In the Christian trajectory, Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah* (Hom. XII–XIV) read the four-kings sequence christologically: each failed king is a foil for the true king of David's line. Jerome's commentary stresses verse 16 as the OT root of the Pauline insistence that *those who claim to know God but deny him by their works* (Titus 1:16) miss the prophetic measure. **Aquinas** (*ST* II-II.10.1) cites 22:16 as evidence that knowledge of God is inseparable from charity and justice. **Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah*** (1563) treats verses 13–17 with characteristic political bite — Calvin had no patience for kings who built palaces on labor they did not pay for, and he reads the verse as a permanent rebuke to Christian princes. The Coniah problem occupied Calvin extensively: he reads 22:30 as a prophecy of the ending of *political* Davidic kingship, not of the *messianic* line, which proceeds through Coniah to Christ via Matthew 1:11–12. **Raymond Brown's *Birth of the Messiah*** (1977, rev. 1993) takes Matthew's deliberate inclusion of Jeconiah in the genealogy as a theological signal: Matthew is asserting that the curse is broken in Christ, and the broken-pot of 22:28 becomes the cornerstone of 1 Peter 2:7. **Brueggemann's *Theology of the Old Testament*** (1997) treats v. 16 as the high-water mark of prophetic theology: knowing God *is* doing justice, full stop, and any theology that detaches knowledge from justice is a rejection of this verse. **Kathleen O'Connor**, reading Jeremiah as exilic disaster literature, treats the Coniah oracle as a moment of communal humiliation that becomes, paradoxically, the condition for return; **Leslie Allen** (NICOT) gives the steady historical reading of the kings collection. The critical tradition (Duhm, Mowinckel, Carroll OTL) has tended to treat the four-kings collection as a Deuteronomistic indictment with a Jeremianic core; recent scholarship (Stulman, Lundbom, O'Connor) has moved toward a more unified Jeremianic origin.

Where Jewish and Christian readings most sharply diverge is on the Coniah curse. Jewish tradition reads it as conditional and unbound by repentance; Christian tradition reads it as fulfilled in the political failure of the Davidic dynasty and dissolved in the messianic line that runs through Coniah to Christ. Both traditions preserve the Davidic line through Coniah; they differ on how the curse and the line interact.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: To know God is to do justice (22:16).** The verse is one of the most epigrammatic in the prophetic corpus, and every major theological tradition has had to take a position on it. The Catholic *fides quae / fides qua* distinction (the faith *which* is believed vs. the faith *by which* one believes) has been used to preserve a logical priority of doctrinal knowledge over ethical action; but Aquinas himself, citing this verse, refuses any clean separation — *fides caritate formata*, faith formed by love, is the only living faith (*ST* II-II.4.3). The Reformed tradition, especially Calvin (*Institutes* 1.2.1), insists that *true knowledge of God is bound up with knowing his will*; the verse is foundational for the Reformed identification of theology and ethics. Patrick Miller, in his NIB exposition, treats v. 16 as the chapter's hinge — the king who "judged the cause of the poor and needy" simply *did* what knowing God consists in, and the verse leaves no room for a piety detached from the courts and the marketplace. The Jewish reading, in line with Maimonides's treatment of the *mitzvot* as the embodied form of love of God (*Mishneh Torah*, *Hilkhot Yesodei ha-Torah*), holds that knowledge of God is inseparable from observance and from justice; *da'at* and *mishpat* are two faces of one thing. The most uncomfortable reading is the radical one (Brueggemann, Heschel): if *knowing God is doing justice*, then the abundance of orthodox creed without justice is theologically empty — and any tradition that has confessed the right things while exploiting the poor has by this verse committed apostasy. Steelmanning the most cautious reading: Christian tradition has sometimes emphasized credal orthodoxy because the alternative is moralism, the collapse of grace into ethics; the verse cannot mean that ethics *is* the whole of theology, or grace becomes works. Where this leaves us: verse 16 cannot be domesticated into either orthodoxy without ethics or ethics without orthodoxy; it requires that the two be inseparable in practice, and any tradition that splits them is being measured here.

**Tension 2: The signet-ring curse and its reversal.** Jeremiah 22:24 says of Coniah: *though you were the signet ring on my right hand, yet I would tear you off*. Haggai 2:23 says of Zerubbabel, Coniah's grandson: *I will take you, O Zerubbabel… and make you like a signet ring; for I have chosen you*. The Christian reading (Calvin, Aquinas, Ambrose; consolidated by Raymond Brown) treats Matthew's deliberate inclusion of Jeconiah in the genealogy of Christ (Matt 1:11–12) as the theological resolution: the political throne is forfeit, but the messianic line is preserved, and the Branch oracle of Jeremiah 23:5–6 finds its referent precisely in the descendant of cursed Coniah. The Jewish reading (Sanhedrin 38a, Yoma 67b, Rashi, Radak) treats the curse as conditional on repentance, with Coniah's Yom Kippur repentance in Babylon unbinding it. The critical-historical reading (Carroll OTL, Duhm) treats the Jeremiah-Haggai contradiction as a redactional seam, evidence that the canonical editors did not always smooth their materials. Steelmanning the critical reading: the canon is itself a record of theological argument, and the Coniah-Zerubbabel seam preserves a debate rather than a resolution. The most theologically consequential reading is patristic: the curse and its undoing together describe the structure of grace — judgment is real, repentance is real, and the line that survives is the line that has been through both. Where this leaves us: the signet-ring oracle and its reversal in Haggai work together only if we accept that the prophet's word is conditional in a way that the prophet himself does not always disclose; the canon teaches us to read prophetic curse with one eye on prophetic mercy.

**One unresolved question:** If verse 16 is correct that *knowing God is doing justice*, then how should we read the long history of theological orthodoxy that has coexisted with grave injustice — including in the very traditions that confess this verse?

## The Thread

The justice-and-righteousness double formula of 22:3 and 22:15–16 is foundational for the Branch oracle of 23:5–6, where the coming king *executes justice and righteousness in the land* — Jeremiah is preparing the contrast that the next chapter will resolve. The cedar-palace indictment of 22:13–17 is recapitulated in James 5:1–6 (the rich who withhold the wages of the laborers) and finds its New Testament image-counterpart in the rich fool of Luke 12:16–21 who builds bigger barns. The signet-ring curse and its reversal threads through Haggai 2:23 and into Matthew 1:11–12, where Jeconiah's name is preserved in the genealogy of Christ — Matthew's editorial decision is itself a theological argument. N.T. Wright reads that genealogy, with its hinge at "the deportation to Babylon," as the structural confirmation of his larger thesis: the Babylonian exile is the unfinished chapter of Israel's story, and Matthew presents Jesus, descendant of cursed Coniah, as the one in whom the exile finally ends and the true Davidic king of Jeremiah 23:5–6 arrives. The donkey-burial of Jehoiakim in 22:19 finds its grim echo in 36:30, where the curse is repeated, and stands in dark counterpoint to the Palm Sunday donkey of Zechariah 9:9 / Matthew 21:5 — where the true king, unlike Jehoiakim, comes humbly, riding the very animal whose burial would have been Jehoiakim's shame.
