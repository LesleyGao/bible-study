# Joshua 23: The Old Man's First Goodbye

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Joshua 23 opens "a long time afterward" (*miyyāmîm rabbîm ʾaḥărê*) — a deliberately fuzzy chronological marker that places the speech somewhere in the lifetime of the conquest generation after the allotments have been made and the coalition wars have ended. Joshua, according to 24:29, dies at 110, and the chapter presents him as *zāqēn bāʾ bayyāmîm*, "old and advanced in years" (23:1-2). The setting is almost certainly Shiloh, the cultic center of the Ephraimite hill country (Josh 18:1), though the chapter does not name the location. The audience is strictly the leadership class — "all Israel, its elders and heads, its judges and officers" (23:2). Chapter 24's audience will be different and broader: *kol-šibṭê yiśrāʾēl*, "all the tribes of Israel," at Shechem. Joshua 23 is the *leadership briefing*; Joshua 24 is the *national convocation*. This distinction matters for reading both.

The material world assumed is the late Iron I Canaanite plateau. The central hill country in the twelfth century BCE shows a surge of small unwalled settlements — Israel Finkelstein's surveys identified over 250 new hill-country sites between c. 1200 and 1000 BCE, small farmsteads typically of four-room houses with collared-rim storage jars and terraced agriculture. The valleys and coastal plain, by contrast, remained under Philistine and Canaanite control — exactly the "nations that remain" (23:4, 7, 12) whose continuing presence troubles Joshua's speech. The archaeological record thus corroborates the chapter's frank admission: the conquest was incomplete. Tell el-Farʿah North, Megiddo, Beth-Shean, Gezer, and the Philistine pentapolis all continued as Canaanite or Philistine centers into the early Iron Age. Joshua 23 is not pretending otherwise.

Joshua himself is a figure to disambiguate. Yəhôšūaʿ bin-Nûn, "Joshua son of Nun," of the tribe of Ephraim (Num 13:8, 16), is the Mosaic aide who became conquest commander. He is not Joshua son of Jehozadak, the post-exilic high priest of Zechariah 3 and Haggai. He is not the Yehoshua of 2 Kings 23:8. The late Second Temple and early Christian tradition also began to read the Hebrew name *Yəhôšūaʿ* as "YHWH saves" — the same name that in Hellenistic Greek transliteration becomes *Iēsous*, Jesus, which sets up much of the patristic typology.

Dating the composition depends on the Deuteronomistic History theory. On Noth's 1943 single-exilic-author model, Joshua 23 is the work of the late seventh- or early sixth-century Deuteronomist using Joshua as a vehicle for the book's theological conclusions. On Cross's 1973 dual-redaction model, Joshua 23 bears marks of Dtr2 — the exilic editor who adapted Dtr1's Josianic work to post-586 BCE conditions, adding material that anticipates exile ("you shall perish quickly from off the good land," 23:16). Richard Nelson and Thomas Römer have argued that Joshua 23 is specifically a late Deuteronomistic supplement inserted before the older chapter 24, creating a two-speech structure that lets Joshua deliver a Deuteronomic sermon (ch 23) and then lead a covenant renewal ceremony (ch 24). The linguistic evidence — the accumulation of Deuteronomic idioms is dense — supports late composition.

Five key terms anchor the speech. First, *dāvaq* (דָּבַק), "cling, cleave" (23:8, 12) — the same covenantal intimacy verb that appeared in Joshua 22:5. The chapter uses it twice, once positively ("you shall cling to the LORD") and once negatively ("if you turn back and cling to the remnant of these nations"), making *dāvaq* the hinge verb. Whatever or whoever you cling to is the thing you have married. Second, *nilḥām* (נִלְחָם) in the Niphal, "fought for" (23:3, 10) — as in *yhwh hûʾ ha-nilḥām lākem*, "the LORD is the one who fights for you." This is classic holy-war formulation, the subject of Gerhard von Rad's *Der heilige Krieg im alten Israel* (1951, ET *Holy War in Ancient Israel*, 1991). Von Rad argued that holy war in Israel's pre-monarchic period was a sacral institution in which the human warrior was essentially passive and YHWH did the actual fighting; Joshua 23:10 ("one man of you puts to flight a thousand") is a locus classicus of the motif. Third, *šāmar* (שָׁמַר), "keep, guard" (23:6, 11), the verb of Torah-fidelity, coupled with the Deuteronomic idiom *lĕbiltî sûr yāmîn ûśĕmōʾl*, "not turning to the right or left." Fourth, *môqēš* (מוֹקֵשׁ), "snare, trap" (23:13) — a metaphor repeatedly applied to foreign gods and foreign women (Exod 23:33; 34:12; Deut 7:16). Fifth, *dābār ʾeḥād lōʾ nāpal* (דָּבָר אֶחָד לֹא נָפַל), "not one word has failed" (23:14) — the Deuteronomist's formulaic promise-fulfillment declaration, echoing Joshua 21:45 and anticipating 1 Kings 8:56. It is the DH's own editorial signature.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is *farewell discourse* (sometimes called *testamentary speech*), a type well-attested in ancient Near Eastern and biblical literature. Moses's triple farewell in Deuteronomy 31-33 (prose speech, song, blessing) is the direct model. Jacob's blessing of his sons in Genesis 49, David's charge to Solomon in 1 Kings 2, Samuel's departure speech in 1 Samuel 12, Paul's Miletus address to the Ephesian elders in Acts 20, and Jesus's farewell discourse in John 14-17 all participate in the genre. Standard features include: (1) announcement of imminent death; (2) rehearsal of past faithfulness — God's or the leader's; (3) commands for future conduct; (4) warnings of consequences; (5) sometimes a blessing. Joshua 23 checks boxes 1-4; the blessing proper appears in the next chapter.

The speech divides cleanly into two movements separated by the *wĕʿattâ* ("and now") in 23:14. Movement one (23:2-13) looks back: God has fought, God has allotted, God will continue to drive out — so cling to God, keep Torah, do not intermarry. Movement two (23:14-16) looks forward: I am dying; not one word has failed; the same God who kept every good promise will keep every threatened curse if you turn. The structure is that of a two-panel diptych: gospel first, law first, promise first — then warning. Von Rad read the inversion as deliberate: the warning lands harder because it comes after the unbroken record of divine fidelity.

Literary devices are Deuteronomic by design. The chapter is saturated with what Weinfeld catalogued as the Deuteronomic idiom list: "the LORD your God" (15 times in 16 verses), "book of the Law of Moses," "turn aside neither to the right nor to the left," "love the LORD your God," "cling to the LORD your God," "with all your heart and with all your soul," "the good land which the LORD your God has given you." Weinfeld's *Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School* (1972) argued that the density of these idioms in Joshua 23 is diagnostic of Deuteronomic authorship, not merely theme. The chapter is virtually a Deuteronomic sermon.

The textual variants are modest. The LXX lacks "and the LORD your God will push them back before you" in 23:5 in some manuscripts; the phrase appears to be a doublet in the Masoretic tradition. 4QJosh^a, the Qumran Joshua fragment, is damaged in this section but shows some textual fluidity. More theologically interesting: the shift between singular and plural "you" in the Hebrew is more pronounced than English translations preserve, and the alternation is one of the Deuteronomic style markers.

Within Joshua's macro-structure, chapter 23 serves as the reflective companion to chapter 24. Noth famously argued that chapter 24 (the Shechem covenant) was the older core and that chapter 23 was a later Deuteronomistic addition reframing the covenant material as a farewell sermon. The rhetorical effect of the doubling is that the book ends not with one climactic speech but with two: a charge to leaders and a covenant with the people. Gordon McConville and Stephen Williams in *Joshua* (Two Horizons, 2010) read the doubling as deliberate literary pacing — the leaders hear the warning first and are expected to internalize it; only then does Joshua take the people through the ritual of self-binding at Shechem.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish trajectory.** Second Temple literature absorbs Joshua 23 into larger reflections on covenant conditionality. Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.1.28) paraphrases the speech with Greek rhetorical flourishes, casting Joshua as a philosopher-leader warning his people against luxury and foreign customs. Philo, more interested in allegory, treats Joshua's farewell as the soul's address to its faculties on the eve of departure from the body. The Qumran community — especially the *Rule of the Community* (1QS) and the *Damascus Document* — echoes Joshua 23's separationist logic: the covenanters must not mingle with "the men of injustice." The anxiety is the same anxiety of Joshua 23.

Rabbinic tradition reads Joshua's two farewells through the Mishnah-derived ethos of succession. Talmud Bavli *Yoma* 22b and especially *Megillah* 14a-15b discuss Joshua among the seven prophets who prophesied to Israel, analyzing his leadership style in contrast to Moses. *Sotah* 11a-13b, though focused on Moses's death and burial, adjacent tractates treat the formal handoff from Moses to Joshua and the question of whether Joshua's leadership was fully equivalent. The midrash *Bereshit Rabbah* 100.9 compares Joshua's farewell with Jacob's deathbed speech in Genesis 49 and concludes that both patriarchs sought to reveal the "end of days" but were prevented by the Shekhinah's withdrawal. *Pirkei deRabbi Eliezer* adds that Joshua's farewell speech was delivered seven times to ensure the leaders could repeat it.

Rashi on 23:7 glosses the prohibition against "making mention of the names" of foreign gods as forbidding even the passing invocation of their names in oaths or curses — a strict reading that shaped medieval Jewish ethical practice. Ibn Ezra reads *lōʾ-tābōʾû bannôǵîm* ("you shall not mix with these nations") as primarily about marriage but with broader cultural implication. Ramban notes the theological precision of 23:14 — "not one word has failed" — as one of the Tanakh's strongest affirmations of divine fidelity. Radak addresses the apparent contradiction between 23:9 ("no man has been able to stand before you") and the actual record of military setbacks (the Ai disaster of Josh 7) by arguing that the statement refers to the overall trajectory, not individual episodes. Abarbanel, writing after 1492, reads Joshua's warning about "perishing quickly from off the good land" with special urgency — his generation had seen it happen. Maimonides in the *Mishneh Torah*'s laws of idolatry invokes Joshua 23:7 as proof of the severity of even verbal association with other gods.

Modern Jewish readers have engaged the chapter in distinctive ways. Jon Levenson's work on covenant emphasizes that the conditionality of Joshua 23 is not a demotion of the Abrahamic unconditional promises but their proper application — unconditional *to the people*, conditional *to any generation*. Moshe Weinfeld's decades of work on Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School placed Joshua 23 at the center of his reconstruction of the late pre-exilic and exilic theological project. Jacob Milgrom's work on the priestly tradition, though focused on Leviticus, helps illuminate the cultic anxieties underlying Joshua 23's intermarriage warnings. Nahum Sarna's commentaries on the Pentateuch provide the background for the intermarriage prohibition (Deut 7:3-4), which Joshua 23 reactivates. Avivah Zornberg's psychological-midrashic readings, while mostly focused on Torah, offer a methodological model for reading Joshua as tragic narrative — the leader who successfully led the conquest warning that the hardest war is the one about to begin.

**Christian trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Joshua* reads Joshua 23 allegorically: the "nations that remain" are the residual passions of the baptized Christian, and Joshua's warning that they will become "a snare and a trap" is a pastoral description of spiritual backsliding. Jerome's Latin translation made the Deuteronomic idioms the property of Western spiritual theology. Augustine in *City of God* reads the chapter's promise-and-threat structure as archetypal of the two cities — the earthly city always on the edge of perishing from its good land, the heavenly city whose city is promise-kept. Chrysostom's homilies emphasize 23:11, "Be very careful, therefore, to love the LORD your God" — love as the central command, echoed by Jesus in Matthew 22.

Medieval interpreters used Joshua 23 in treatises on the last things. Aquinas cites the chapter in the *Summa* on the theological virtues, particularly on the relation between love of God and obedience. The *Glossa Ordinaria* reads the remnant nations as a figure of residual sin. Hugh of St. Victor treats the chapter as teaching the insufficiency of external conquest without internal fidelity.

Luther's Joshua lectures press the "old and advanced in years" motif hard — the aging leader's duty is to *preach law clearly* so the next generation cannot plead ignorance. Luther is especially moved by 23:14, "not one word has failed," and uses it as a proof-text for the trustworthiness of divine promise. Calvin's sermons on Joshua 23 (the 1563 series, preserved in the *Opera*) treat the chapter as a model of pastoral succession: the faithful minister's final duty is to warn, not flatter. Calvin's reading of the aging-leader-as-testamentary-charge has influenced every subsequent Reformed tradition of "farewell sermons." Dietrich Bonhoeffer, in his fragmentary late writings and letters, develops what could be called an "ethics of leaving well" — the question of how a leader or institution hands off without either clinging to power or abandoning responsibility. Joshua 23 is an Old Testament exemplar for that ethic.

Critical scholarship on Joshua 23 is largely a history of locating the chapter within the Deuteronomistic History. Noth assigned it to DtrH as a whole; Cross's dual-redaction model placed it in Dtr2. Richard Nelson's OTL commentary treats the chapter as a sophisticated Deuteronomistic composition marking the pre-monarchic climax. Thomas Dozeman reads it as integrated with chapter 24 through careful editorial linking. Trent Butler (WBC) emphasizes the covenant-formulary analysis. McConville (Apollos) reads the chapter as integrating Deuteronomic theology with the successor-speech genre. The "not one word has failed" formula (23:14, cf. 21:45; 1 Kgs 8:56) is the DH's own refrain identifying its editorial presence.

Contemporary theological readers are divided on the chapter's ethics. Walter Brueggemann (*Theology of the Old Testament*, 1997) reads Joshua 23 as the moment when the Deuteronomistic theologian stakes the entire later history on a conditional covenant — and thus sets up the tragedy of 2 Kings 25. Daniel Hawk's *Every Promise Fulfilled* takes the chapter's title from 23:14 and treats it as the DH's victory speech before its long defeat. Ellen Davis reads Joshua 23 as agrarian theology — the "good land" is a gift that can be lost through unfaithful stewardship. Rowlett's *Joshua and the Rhetoric of Violence* reads the chapter as aging-leader rhetoric designed to maintain central authority. The reception history of the chapter (which Brueggemann and Christopher Wright both flag as a misappropriation the text's specificity should preclude) presses an ideology-critical question the chapter cannot dodge: the "nations that remain" are still people, and Joshua 23's program of non-association — including the intermarriage prohibition — has been weaponized in communities where racial and religious endogamy became tools of exclusion.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension one: fidelity versus assimilation.** *Position one (strict separation)*: Joshua's warning is unambiguous — no intermarriage, no invocation of other gods' names, no mixing. The prohibition is protective, not xenophobic. A small community surrounded by dominant cultures survives by guarding its distinctives. The modern secular reader's discomfort with 23:12-13 is itself evidence that assimilation is the default drift. *Position two (pragmatic engagement)*: the prohibition cannot mean absolute non-contact; Rahab is already in the genealogy, and the Gibeonites are serving the sanctuary. The chapter's target is *religious* syncretism, not cultural engagement as such. Israel's calling is to be a blessing to the nations (Gen 12:3), which requires being among them. *Position three (the steelman — least intuitive)*: Joshua 23 is not about preserving ethnic purity or even cultic purity as ends in themselves. It is about the *prior question of allegiance*. The chapter's theology is that whatever you cling to (*dāvaq*) is the thing you worship, and the choice is binary. Mixing is not a cultural phenomenon but a theological one — you cannot be married to YHWH and to the other gods at the same time. On this reading, the non-intermarriage command is less a law of racial separation than an analogical argument: marriages involve clinging, clinging involves allegiance, allegiance is exclusive. The steelman refuses to rescue the chapter from the hard reality that its logic does license ethnic-religious separation; but it notices that the *reason* is theological, not biological. *Where this leaves us*: the chapter does not offer a middle path. It asks: what are you clinging to? and it is willing to name specific behaviors (intermarriage, oaths, worship) that signal competing allegiance. It leaves each generation to decide which behaviors count in its own time.

**Tension two: unconquered territories and the theology of success.** *Position one (still-future conquest)*: 23:5 says the LORD "will push them back" — the work is not done but will be done; Israel's task is fidelity while God finishes. *Position two (admission of failure)*: 23:12-13 sets out what happens if Israel does not drive them out — they become a snare — and by the time of Judges, this is exactly the condition. The chapter is preparing the reader for the failure narrative to come. *Position three (the steelman — least intuitive)*: the unconquered territories are not a problem to be solved but a theological ecology. The chapter's frank admission that "nations remain" (23:4, 7, 12) and its warning that they might always remain is a theological acknowledgment that *completion is not promised in this age*. Israel's covenantal task is not to complete the conquest but to remain faithful amid the incompletion. On this reading, Joshua 23 is a surprising correction to any triumphalist reading of the book: the conquest was never supposed to be total, and the ongoing work of the covenant community happens in the space between promise and fulfillment. *Where this leaves us*: the chapter refuses the triumphalist and the defeatist reading. It names the incompletion, promises that God will complete it, and warns that unfaithfulness can forfeit the promise. It lives in the tension that later prophets and apostles will call "already / not yet."

The ethical difficulty the chapter cannot evade is its logic of corporate collective destruction. The threat of 23:15-16 — "he will bring upon you all the evil things, until he has destroyed you from off this good land" — is covenant-curse rhetoric drawn directly from Deuteronomy 28. It is the same logic that, by 2 Kings 25, will produce the exile, and the Deuteronomist is writing with that exile in view. Modern readers who find the curse-theology uncomfortable should notice that the chapter is not predicting a capricious divine act but describing the structural consequences of unfaithfulness in a covenantal relationship. Still, the chapter's readiness to speak of divine destruction of the covenant people is not softened by its own literary context, and it is part of the Bible's theology that the Deuteronomic threat and the prophetic promise both belong to the one God.

**One unresolved question**: If Joshua's speech to the leaders already contains the full Deuteronomic warning, what is the covenant ceremony at Shechem in chapter 24 for — does the people need something the leaders did not, or do the leaders need something the people's ceremony will do to them?

## The Thread

1 Kings 8:56 — Solomon's dedication speech — quotes Joshua 23:14 almost verbatim: "not one word has failed of all his good promise." The Deuteronomist is linking the temple dedication to the pre-monarchic fulfillment. Acts 20:17-38, Paul's Miletus farewell to the Ephesian elders, is modeled on Joshua 23 and Moses's Deuteronomy farewell: the aging leader, the summoned leaders, the warning about "fierce wolves" (literal Greek: sheep-devourers) who will enter from among them. John 15:1-11, Jesus's farewell about abiding in the vine, picks up the *dāvaq/abide* theology of Joshua 23 and makes it explicitly about union with the person, not the nation. And 1 John 2:15-17 — "do not love the world or the things in the world" — is Joshua 23's intermarriage warning translated into first-century Mediterranean Christian ethics.
