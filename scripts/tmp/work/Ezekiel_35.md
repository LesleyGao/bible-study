# Ezekiel 35: Mount Seir, the Brother's Enmity, and the Theology of Edom

## Lens 1 — Historical World

Chapter 35 is the second of Ezekiel's two oracles against Edom — the first being the brief 25:12–14 in the seven-nation oracle block — and stands now within the *restoration block* (chs. 33–48) at a strategic position: the chapter pivots from the indictment of Israel's failed shepherds (ch. 34) to the announcement of the mountains of Israel's restoration (ch. 36), and Edom's oracle is the dark mirror against which Israel's restoration is announced. The structural logic, as Block develops it (NICOT, 1998), is that Mount Seir's *desolation* (35:3, 4, 7, 9, 14, 15) is the deliberate counterpoint to the mountains of Israel's promised re-tilling (36:8–11). The chapter has no date formula; its setting is post-fall, late 587 or 586 BC by the standard chronology, with the news of Jerusalem's fall now received by the Tel-abib community and the political reality of Edom's opportunism on Judah's borders likely fresh in the prophet's awareness.

Edom's role in the catastrophe is historically attested across multiple OT and extra-biblical sources. Obadiah's compact prophecy denounces Edom for *standing aloof* on the day of Jerusalem's calamity and for *cutting off the fugitives* who fled the burning city (Obad 11–14). Lamentations 4:21–22 and Psalm 137:7 attest the same: *Remember, O LORD, against the Edomites the day of Jerusalem, how they said, "Lay it bare, lay it bare, down to its foundations"*. The Babylonian Chronicle does not name Edom directly, but the archaeological record at Buseirah (Iron Age Bozrah, the Edomite capital), Tawilan, and Umm el-Biyara (excavations published by Bennett 1973, 1975, 1977 and reanalyzed by Bienkowski 1992 and 2002) shows continued Edomite settlement and even expansion in the early sixth century BC, while Judahite settlements east of the Dead Sea — Hebron, Arad, the Negev forts — were destroyed in the Babylonian campaign. Edom appears to have benefited politically and territorially from Judah's collapse, occupying the southern Negev and the Hebron hill country in the post-587 period. Itzhak Beit-Arieh's excavations at Horvat Qitmit (1995) recovered an Edomite cult center on what had been Judahite territory before the fall, attesting the political reality.

The *Mount Seir* of 35:2 is the central highland of Edom, the territory east and south of the Dead Sea bounded by the Wadi Zered to the north and the Gulf of Aqaba to the south. The name *Seir* (*śēʿîr*) is associated in Genesis 36 with Esau's settlement in *the hill country of Seir*, and the Edomite identification is built on the Esau-Jacob narrative complex. The patriarchal stories in Gen 25–36 establish the Edom-Israel relationship as constitutively *fraternal* — Esau and Jacob are brothers — and constitutively *antagonistic*: the womb-struggle (Gen 25:22–23), the sold birthright (25:29–34), the stolen blessing (27:1–40), and the Esau-meets-Jacob reconciliation that is also a separation (33:1–17, with Jacob declining to follow Esau to Seir). The *Esau-Jacob* archetype is paradigmatic; Bert Dicou's *Edom, Israel's Brother and Antagonist* (JSOTSup 169, 1994) develops the case that the OT's repeated return to Edom is not redundant condemnation but a structural meditation on the brother-as-enemy pattern.

The Hebrew vocabulary repays close attention. *Har Seʿîr* (הַר שֵׂעִיר, "Mount Seir") at 35:2, 3, 7, 15 is the territorial designation. *ʾÊbat ʿôlām* (אֵיבַת עוֹלָם, "perpetual enmity") at 35:5 names the relationship between Edom and Israel in covenantal-juridical terms — *ʾêbâ* is the same noun used in Gen 3:15 for the enmity between the woman's seed and the serpent's seed. *Šĕmāmâ* (שְׁמָמָה, "desolation") at 35:3, 4, 7, 9, 14, 15 is the chapter's repeated keyword, paralleling its negative use in Israel-restoration contexts where the same root will *cease* to apply (cf. 36:34, where the *šemāmâ* is explicitly reversed). The recognition formula *and you shall know that I am the LORD* appears at 35:4, 9, 12, 15 — four times in fifteen verses, the highest density of the formula in any single chapter of Ezekiel. Block notes that this density signals the chapter's juridical emphasis: the Edom oracle is structured as a divine forensic establishment of YHWH's lordship over the *nations*, not just Israel.

The political-economic stakes named at 35:10 are explicit: *Because you said, "These two nations and these two countries shall be mine, and we will take possession of them" — although the LORD was there*. The *two nations* are Israel and Judah (the northern and southern kingdoms, both now collapsed by Ezekiel's day), and the territorial appropriation Edom pursued in the wake of Judah's fall is named as the precipitating offense. The phrase *although the LORD was there* (Hebrew *wayhwh šām hāyâ*) is theologically loaded: even in the desolated land, YHWH's presence was not withdrawn; Edom's appropriation was therefore not merely political seizure but theological transgression. Greenberg (Anchor 22B, 1997) reads the phrase as the chapter's structural pivot — the offense is not the territorial claim *per se* but the territorial claim made *in disregard of YHWH's continuing presence* in the desolated land.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter is structured in two oracles with parallel form. Verses 1–9 are the first oracle, opening with the messenger formula and the *because-therefore* logic (*because you cherished perpetual enmity… therefore I will prepare you for blood*, vv. 5–6). Verses 10–15 are the second oracle, opening with the same formula and the same logic (*because you said… therefore I will deal with you*, vv. 10–11), then expanding into the *I have heard* speech-act (v. 12) that adds an evidentiary moment, and closing with the recognition formula and the territorial expansion of the judgment to *all Edom, all of it* (v. 15). The form is *Gerichtswort* (judgment word) — the standard prophetic forensic-juridical pattern of indictment and sentence — and Block notes its formal precision: the two oracles are nearly identical in structure, suggesting deliberate parallel composition.

The *leitwort* of the chapter is *šĕmāmâ* — desolation. Six occurrences in fifteen verses, distributed across both oracles. The repetition is the punishment: Mount Seir's punishment will *be* desolation, and the chapter's repeated naming of *šĕmāmâ* enacts the desolation it announces. Block compares this with Ezek 6's earlier oracle against the mountains of Israel, which deploys the same *šĕmāmâ* vocabulary — the Edom oracle of ch. 35 inverts the earlier Israel oracle, transferring the *šĕmāmâ* from the mountains of Israel to the mountains of Edom and clearing the way for ch. 36's reversal of the earlier judgment.

The recognition formula's four occurrences in 35 are notable for their uniformly outward-directed object. *And you* (Edom) *shall know that I am the LORD*. In the earlier nation oracles (chs. 25–32), the formula's object varies: sometimes the nation, sometimes Israel, sometimes a third-party witness. In ch. 35, the object is consistently Edom itself: the Edomite must come to recognize YHWH's lordship as the meaning of its own desolation. This is theologically distinctive — the Edom oracle is not merely an announcement of judgment but a structural *call to recognition*, even for Edom.

The literary device of v. 12 deserves attention: *I have heard all the revilings that you uttered against the mountains of Israel, saying, "They are laid desolate; they are given us to devour"*. The phrase deploys *quoted enemy speech* as evidence in the divine court — Edom's own words, recorded by YHWH and cited back. Greenberg notes that the device is used elsewhere in Ezekiel (cf. 35:10 above; 36:2) and is characteristic of the prophet's juridical style. The effect is that the indictment is grounded not in YHWH's *imputation* of motive but in Edom's own *attested utterance*; the chapter is structured as a courtroom in which the defendant's own words are the prosecution's evidence.

Form-critically, the chapter is *Fremdvölkerorakel* (oracle against a foreign nation) and stands formally outside the *Heilsorakel* of chs. 33–48 — but its placement *within* the restoration block (rather than within the chs. 25–32 nation-oracles block) is a deliberate redactional choice. Zimmerli (Hermeneia, 1983) and Block both argue that the redactor positioned ch. 35 as the *necessary preliminary* to ch. 36's restoration: the mountains of Israel cannot be restored while the mountains of Edom claim them, and the prophetic announcement of Edom's desolation clears the territorial space for Israel's promised return.

Textual variants are minor. The MT is well preserved. The LXX renders v. 6's *I will prepare you for blood, and blood shall pursue you* with consistent vocabulary; the Targum Jonathan paraphrases v. 10's *although the LORD was there* with the explanatory expansion *though the Memra of the LORD was there*, preserving the theological force. The Peshitta and Vulgate read with the MT.

## Lens 3 — Larger Story

**Jewish reception** of the Edom oracles develops in two stages. The first stage — biblical and Second Temple — treats Edom as the historical adversary of Genesis 25 to the post-exilic period, with Edomite political enmity an ongoing theological problem (cf. the deuterocanonical 1 Maccabees 5:3, 65; Josephus *Antiquities* 13.257 on the Hasmonean conquest of Idumea). Edom's identification with Idumea in the Hellenistic period and the eventual incorporation of Idumea into Hasmonean Judea (with Herod the Great as an Idumean king of the Jews) keeps the Edom-question politically live for the rabbinic tradition.

The second stage — rabbinic and medieval — is the *Edom-as-Rome* identification. The Bavli at *Avodah Zarah* 2b reads the prophetic Edom oracles as referring to Rome: *Edom* becomes the rabbinic code for the imperial power that destroyed the Second Temple. Gerson Cohen's classic essay *Esau as Symbol in Early Medieval Thought* (in *Jewish Medieval and Renaissance Studies*, 1967) traces the Edom-Rome midrashic identification from the second century AD through medieval European Jewish thought, where Edom comes to encode first pagan Rome, then Christian Rome, then European Christendom generally. The midrash *Genesis Rabbah* 65 makes the identification explicit; the *Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael* on Exodus 17 develops it; Rashi's Torah commentary deploys it routinely. The identification is not arbitrary etymology — it is a midrashic technique by which the prophetic Edom oracles are *re-applied* to subsequent imperial enemies of Israel, preserving the prophetic word's address to ongoing imperial reality.

Daniel Boyarin's *Border Lines* (2004) and Israel Yuval's *Two Nations in Your Womb* (2006) develop the rabbinic Edom-tradition further: the *brother-as-enemy* archetype of Genesis 25 becomes, in late-antique and medieval Jewish thought, the structural figure for Christianity itself — the daughter-religion that grew from the same patriarchal stock and now claims the inheritance. The Edom-Rome midrashic tradition thus encodes a complex theological-political stance: Christianity is read as *Esau*, the brother who is also the rival, the kin who is also the persecutor.

**Christian reception** of the Edom oracles is more diffuse. The patristic tradition reads them historically — Edom is a real ancient nation, judged for real ancient crimes — but with little structural theological development. Origen's *Homilies on Ezekiel* (only fragments survive on chs. 35–36) and Jerome's *Commentary on Ezekiel* read the chapter morally — the *brother's enmity* is the diagnostic for what later writers will call the *peccatum invidiae* (the sin of envy) — and pastorally, with Edom serving as exemplum of the soul that resents the brother's blessing. Augustine's *De Civitate Dei* 16 treats the Esau-Jacob narrative typologically: Esau is the *civitas terrena* (earthly city), Jacob the *civitas Dei* (city of God), and the prophetic Edom oracles articulate the eschatological judgment of the earthly city. The medieval typological tradition (Bede, Rabanus Maurus, the *Glossa Ordinaria*) deploys Edom uniformly as the figure of the carnal-against-the-spiritual.

The Reformation and post-Reformation Protestant tradition reads the Edom oracles with renewed historical attention. Calvin's *Lectures on Ezekiel* on chapter 35 (1564) treats Edom as the historical-political Edom, judged for the historical-political offenses of Obadiah and Lamentations 4:21–22, and resists the medieval typological collapse. The Puritan tradition (Matthew Poole's *Synopsis Criticorum*, John Owen's exegetical works) likewise reads historically. The contemporary critical tradition (Bert Dicou *Edom, Israel's Brother and Antagonist*, 1994; Elie Assis *Identity in Conflict*, 2016) returns to the historical reading while developing the *brother-archetype* analysis.

Where the readings converge is in identifying chapter 35 as a meditation on the structural problem of *the kindred enemy*. Edom is not Babylon — Babylon is a foreign empire, alien in language and lineage. Edom is *brother* — same ancestor, same patriarchal narrative, same God in his ultimate origin (cf. Deut 2:4–5, where YHWH explicitly forbids Israel to dispossess Edom). The chapter's theological problem is that the worst enemy in the prophetic literature is the kin who turns against kin in the day of catastrophe.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The persistent Edom oracle.** Why does the OT return to Edom *repeatedly*? Obadiah is dedicated entirely to Edom; Joel 3:19, Amos 1:11–12, Isaiah 34, Isaiah 63:1–6, Malachi 1:2–5, Lamentations 4:21–22, Psalm 137:7, Jeremiah 49:7–22, Ezekiel 25:12–14, and Ezekiel 35 all return to Edom. No other foreign nation receives this density of prophetic attention; even Babylon, which actually destroyed Jerusalem, receives less sustained prophetic indictment than Edom, which merely exploited Babylon's victory.

The five positions:
1. *Brother-archetype theological*: Edom's persistence in the prophetic literature reflects the theological-anthropological intuition that the kindred enemy is the most theologically fraught form of enmity; the Esau-Jacob womb-struggle of Gen 25 is paradigmatic for the moral-theological problem of fraternal betrayal. Bert Dicou *Edom, Israel's Brother and Antagonist*; Elie Assis *Identity in Conflict*. The position locates the persistence in the structural significance of the kindred-enemy figure.
2. *Historical-political proximate*: Edom's persistence reflects ongoing political conflict in the Iron Age and Persian period; the prophetic oracles are responses to specific political offenses (the post-587 territorial appropriation, the post-exilic Idumean expansion); the typological reading is post-prophetic midrash. John Bartlett *Edom and the Edomites*; Itzhak Beit-Arieh's archaeological work. The position locates the persistence in proximate political reality.
3. *Eschatological-typological*: Edom in the prophetic literature increasingly functions as the type of *all* hostile nations and ultimately of the eschatological enemy of God's people; the persistence reflects the typological elasticity of Edom as the figure of opposition. Augustine *De Civitate Dei* 16; Bede; the medieval typological tradition. The position locates the persistence in typological development.
4. *Edom-as-Rome rabbinic*: the persistence reflects the prophetic word's continuing applicability to subsequent imperial enemies; *Edom* in the rabbinic tradition is the figure for whatever empire currently oppresses Israel; the typological elasticity is the prophetic word's adaptability. *Avodah Zarah* 2b; Gerson Cohen *Esau as Symbol*; Israel Yuval *Two Nations in Your Womb*. The position locates the persistence in midrashic re-application.
5. *Trauma-theological*: the persistence reflects unresolved exilic trauma; the prophetic literature returns to Edom because the betrayal in the day of catastrophe was particularly searing and required repeated articulation across the centuries of exile and return; Daniel Smith-Christopher *A Biblical Theology of Exile*. The position locates the persistence in collective traumatic memory.

Steelman the position you find least congenial. If you tend toward historical-political readings, steelman the brother-archetype position: a reading that treats the multiple Edom oracles as merely responses to multiple proximate offenses misses the structural significance of *which* nation receives the persistent attention; the prophetic-canonical concentration on Edom over Babylon is theologically meaningful, not merely historically incidental. If you tend toward brother-archetype readings, steelman the historical-political position: the structural-theological reading risks importing later allegorical patterns into texts whose proximate sense was simply political, and the prophets' own diction is largely historical-juridical rather than archetypal-typological.

**Tension 2: The Edom-as-Rome typology.** What does it mean that the prophetic Edom oracles are *re-applied* in later Jewish tradition to subsequent imperial enemies — first pagan Rome, then Christian Rome, then European Christendom? The midrashic move is not naive; it is a hermeneutical strategy by which the prophetic word remains addressed to ongoing imperial reality. Is the typological re-application faithful exegesis or a category error?

The four positions:
1. *Faithful midrashic re-application*: the prophetic word's authority extends to subsequent imperial situations that share the structural pattern of the original; *Edom* is rabbinic shorthand for *the imperial enemy*, and the typological elasticity is part of the prophetic word's normal mode of application. Cohen *Esau as Symbol*; Daniel Boyarin *Border Lines*. The position affirms the legitimacy of typological re-application.
2. *Hermeneutical overreach*: the Edom oracles are addressed to a specific historical Edom; their re-application to subsequent empires risks importing sixth-century-BC condemnation onto later situations whose moral-political structure may differ; the typological move is a pious abuse rather than faithful exegesis. John Bartlett's historical-critical work. The position resists the typological re-application as anachronism.
3. *Christological reading*: the prophetic Edom oracles are fulfilled in the eschatological judgment of all opposed-to-God power, with Christ as the eschatological judge; the rabbinic Edom-Rome identification is partially right (in identifying Rome as an opposed-to-God power) but misidentifies the eschatological structure; G.K. Beale *Revelation* commentary on Edom-imagery in apocalyptic eschatology. The position offers a Christological-eschatological alternative to the rabbinic typology.
4. *Two-narrative coexistence*: the rabbinic Edom-Rome typology and the Christian Christological-typological reading represent two valid and incompatible developments of the same prophetic substrate; the texts permit both readings; the differing applications reflect differing theological-political situations. Boyarin *Border Lines*; Daniel Boyarin and Yuval's argument that Christianity and rabbinic Judaism developed in mutual definition. The position affirms the legitimacy of multiple receptions.

ONE unresolved question: When a prophetic text addresses a specific historical enemy (sixth-century-BC Edom) but is re-applied across millennia to successive imperial enemies, is the re-application a faithful extension of the prophetic word or a theologically loaded political act? The answer matters because the same hermeneutical question applies to Christian re-application of the OT enemy oracles: when *Babylon* in Revelation 17–18 reads Rome (or, in subsequent Christian reception, Catholic Rome, or modern empire), is the reading faithful prophetic application or politically motivated exegesis?

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references. First, **Genesis 27:39–41** — Isaac's blessing of Esau (*by your sword you shall live, and you shall serve your brother; but when you grow restless, you shall break his yoke from your neck*) and Esau's resulting hatred of Jacob — establishes the *ʾêbat ʿôlām* (perpetual enmity) of Ezek 35:5 as a *patriarchal-prophetic* echo. The phrase used in Ezekiel is heard in Genesis: Esau's hatred of Jacob "for the blessing his father had given him" (Gen 27:41) is the proximate scriptural background for the *perpetual enmity* the prophet now condemns. Ezekiel reads Edom's post-587 opportunism as the historical realization of the patriarchal narrative's foreshadowed enmity.

Second, **Malachi 1:2–5** — *Yet I have loved Jacob but Esau I have hated, and I have laid waste his hill country and left his heritage to jackals of the desert. If Edom says, "We are shattered but we will rebuild the ruins," the LORD of hosts says, "They may build, but I will tear down, and they will be called 'the wicked country,' and 'the people with whom the LORD is angry forever'"* — is the post-exilic prophetic confirmation that Ezek 35's promised desolation has been realized historically. Malachi writes c. 450 BC, after the Edomite kingdom has collapsed and the Idumeans are increasingly displaced from their original Seir territory.

Third, **Romans 9:13** — *as it is written, "Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated"* — Paul's deployment of Mal 1:2–3 in the argument for divine election. The chain runs: Genesis 25 (the womb-struggle) → Genesis 27 (the stolen blessing) → Obadiah and Ezek 35 (the prophetic Edom oracles) → Malachi 1 (the historical realization) → Romans 9 (the Pauline theological deployment). The Edom-Esau material in Romans 9 is one of the OT-NT bridges most closely tied to the Edom oracle complex of which Ezek 35 is a structural piece. Paul's argument for the freedom of divine election is grounded in a typological chain that runs through Ezekiel.
