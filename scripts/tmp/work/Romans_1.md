# Romans 1: The Gospel and the Wrath

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The opening of Romans confronts us with a letter dictated from Corinth in the winter of 56–57 CE, near the end of Paul's third missionary journey, as he prepared to carry the collection from the Aegean churches to the Jerusalem poor (Rom 15:25–28). Tertius, who appears at 16:22, was Paul's amanuensis; Phoebe of Cenchreae (16:1), patron of the Corinthian harbor church, almost certainly carried the scroll to Rome. The letter therefore enters a city Paul had never visited, governed in its early years by **Nero** (reigned 54–68 CE), whose first quinquennium under the tutelage of Seneca and Burrus is often described by ancient historians (Tacitus, *Annals* 13) as comparatively moderate—Romans was written before the great fire of 64 CE and the subsequent Neronian persecution.

The Roman Christian community Paul addresses is itself a historical artifact of imperial policy. **Suetonius**, *Life of Claudius* 25.4, records that Claudius "expelled the Jews from Rome because of their continual disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus" (*impulsore Chresto*)—a notice almost certainly garbled in transmission, with *Chrestus* a common variant of *Christus*. The expulsion of 49 CE, attested independently in Acts 18:2 (where Aquila and Priscilla, recently arrived from Italy, meet Paul in Corinth), drove Jewish believers out of Rome and left the house-churches in Gentile hands. When Claudius died in 54 CE and the edict lapsed, returning Jewish Christians found a Gentile-majority movement reorganized along different cultural lines—a tension Paul addresses systematically through chapters 14–15 and which informs the entire theological argument about Jew and Gentile alike standing under sin and grace.

Material culture matters here. Roman Christianity in the 50s CE was urban, tenement-based, and unmistakably plebeian. The probable house-church locations—identified by **Peter Lampe** in *From Paul to Valentinus: Christians at Rome in the First Two Centuries*—cluster in Trastevere, the Aventine, and along the Appian Way, immigrant districts where Jewish *synagōgai* and Gentile *collegia* mingled. Paul's greeting list in chapter 16 names twenty-six individuals; prosopographic analysis suggests several were freedmen and slaves of imperial households. When Paul writes about *douloi* (slaves) of Christ (1:1), the metaphor was social as well as theological for many of his hearers.

The textual witnesses to Romans are unusually rich. **𝔓⁴⁶** (Chester Beatty Papyrus II, c. 200 CE) preserves substantial portions and is our earliest substantial Romans witness; **Codex Vaticanus** (B, 4th c.) and **Codex Sinaiticus** (א, 4th c.) provide complete texts. The famous **doxology problem** at 16:25–27 already shows itself in the manuscript tradition: 𝔓⁴⁶ places the doxology at the end of chapter 15; the Byzantine majority places it at the end of chapter 14; the great uncials place it at chapter 16. This will be addressed in subsequent chapters' Lens 2, but the textual evidence already suggests that Romans circulated in multiple recensions—**Harry Gamble**'s *The Textual History of the Letter to the Romans* remains foundational on the question.

Five Greek terms reward attention. **Doulos Christou Iēsou** (δοῦλος Χριστοῦ Ἰησοῦ, 1:1)—"slave of Christ Jesus." English "servant" softens what Paul writes; *doulos* meant chattel slavery in Roman society, and Paul opens by describing himself with the lowest legal status in his hearers' world. **Euangelion theou** (εὐαγγέλιον θεοῦ, 1:1)—"good news of God." Imperial inscriptions used *euangelion* for the proclamation of an emperor's accession, victory, or birthday (the Priene calendar inscription of 9 BCE calls Augustus's birthday "the beginning of the good news"). Paul's vocabulary is provocatively counter-imperial. **Dikaiosynē theou** (δικαιοσύνη θεοῦ, 1:17)—"the righteousness of God"—is the most disputed phrase in the letter; we will engage all four interpretive schools below. **Pistis** (πίστις, 1:5, 17)—"faith" or "faithfulness," the same word covering both human trust and divine fidelity. **Paradidōmi** (παραδίδωμι, 1:24, 26, 28)—"hand over" or "deliver up," the divine handing-over verb that returns at 4:25 and 8:32 and forms a backbone for the apocalyptic reading of Romans.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Romans is a Pauline letter cast in the conventions of the Hellenistic epistle—superscription (sender), adscription (recipient), salutation, thanksgiving, body, paraenesis, closing—but stretched and modified to bear the longest sustained argument in the New Testament. Paul's salutation (1:1–7) is itself extraordinary: a six-verse, syntactically continuous sentence in Greek, packed with what most scholars recognize as **pre-Pauline creedal material** at 1:3–4. Oscar Cullmann (*The Christology of the New Testament*) and Martin Hengel (*The Son of God*) argue persuasively that the parallel formulae—"descended from David according to the flesh / declared Son of God in power according to the Spirit of holiness"—reflect a primitive Aramaic-speaking Christology that Paul has received and is here rehearsing for his unfamiliar Roman audience. Romans 1:3–4 may be the oldest Christological confession we possess.

The body proper opens at 1:16–17 with the **propositio**—the rhetorical thesis statement—announcing the letter's argument: "I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation . . . the righteousness of God is revealed from faith for faith." **Robert Jewett**'s monumental Hermeneia commentary analyzes Romans according to classical rhetorical canons (*exordium*, *narratio*, *propositio*, *probatio*, *peroratio*) and finds the structure unusually disciplined for a Pauline letter—Romans is the closest thing Paul wrote to a treatise, though it remains an occasional letter addressed to a specific community.

The most consequential literary observation is the **diatribe** style that surfaces beginning at 1:18 and dominates much of chapters 2–11. **Stanley Stowers**, *The Diatribe and Paul's Letter to the Romans* (1981), drew on the work of Rudolf Bultmann (his 1910 dissertation, *Der Stil der paulinischen Predigt und die kynisch-stoische Diatribe*) to argue that Paul writes in the style of Hellenistic philosophical popular discourse: an imagined interlocutor, rhetorical questions, *mē genoito* exclamations ("by no means!"), apostrophe, and *prosōpopoiia* (speech-in-character). The diatribe was the rhetorical idiom of Epictetus, Musonius Rufus, and Dio Chrysostom; it was the way moral instruction sounded in the urban Mediterranean. Recognizing it changes how we read the second-person address that erupts at 2:1 and the imagined Jewish interlocutor of 3:1ff.

Chapter 1 itself is structured as a tripartite indictment of Gentile humanity (Stowers and others see chapter 2 as continuing this rhetoric, but with a turn against the moralizing judge). The threefold "**God gave them up**" (*paredōken autous*, 1:24, 26, 28) is one of Paul's most striking literary patterns—a hammered refrain of divine handing-over that mirrors the threefold human "exchange" (1:23 "exchanged the glory"; 1:25 "exchanged the truth"; 1:26 "exchanged natural relations"). The structure is symmetrical and deliberate: human exchange, divine handing-over, human exchange, divine handing-over, human refusal, divine handing-over. The literary form is itself the theological argument.

Romans 1:23—"exchanged the glory of the immortal God for images resembling mortal man and birds and animals and creeping things"—deliberately echoes both Psalm 106:20 ("they exchanged their glory for the image of an ox that eats grass") and **Wisdom of Solomon 11–15**, the most extensive Hellenistic-Jewish polemic against Gentile idolatry. Wisdom 13–14, written probably in 1st-century BCE Alexandria, lays out exactly the argument Paul will make: the Gentiles can perceive God from creation but exchange the Creator for created things, leading to idolatry and sexual disorder. The literary debt is substantial; Paul's first-century Jewish readers would have heard the Wisdom echoes immediately.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Romans 1 has been read in radically different ways across two thousand years, and the four major modern interpretive schools each offer different readings even of the chapter's apparently clearest verses.

The **Old Perspective**—Augustine, Luther, Calvin, the Reformed tradition through Charles Hodge, John Murray, **Cranfield** (ICC), and **Douglas Moo** (NICNT)—reads Romans 1 as the opening salvo in a forensic argument about how individual sinners are justified before a holy God. *Dikaiosynē theou* (1:17) means a righteousness from God imputed to the believer; *pistis* is the human act of trust that receives this gift. The wrath revealed (1:18) is God's judicial response to sin; the indictment of Gentile humanity prepares for the universal verdict of 3:23. Augustine read 1:18–32 as the foundational text for original sin and for the bondage of the will under the *massa damnata*; Luther's autobiographical breakthrough at the Black Tower in Wittenberg (recorded in his 1545 *Preface to Latin Writings*) turned on understanding 1:17's "righteousness of God" not as God's punitive justice but as the gracious righteousness God *gives*—and from that recognition, Luther reports, "I felt myself altogether reborn and to have entered through open gates into paradise itself."

The **New Perspective on Paul**—launched by **E. P. Sanders**'s *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977), developed by **James Dunn** (Romans WBC), and given its most ambitious systematic form by **N. T. Wright** (*Paul and the Faithfulness of God*, *Justification*, *The New Testament and the People of God*)—reads Romans 1 within the larger story of Israel's vocation. *Dikaiosynē theou* is **God's covenant faithfulness**, his commitment to fulfill the promises to Abraham by rescuing the world through Israel. The "righteousness from faith for faith" of 1:17 refers not to a transactional imputation but to God's faithful action and the answering faithful response. Wright reads 1:3–4 as announcing that Jesus has been enthroned as Israel's true Messiah and the world's true Lord, and the indictment of 1:18–32 as the diagnosis of why the Abrahamic project of blessing the nations has stalled—because Gentile humanity is held under the same idolatry-driven distortion that Wisdom of Solomon described. The covenant must therefore be renewed in a way that does not require Gentiles to become ethnic Jews.

The **Apocalyptic Paul** school—**J. Louis Martyn** (*Theological Issues in the Letters of Paul*; *Galatians* AB), **Beverly Gaventa** (*Our Mother Saint Paul*; *When in Romans*), **Douglas Campbell** (*The Deliverance of God*), **Susan Eastman** (*Paul and the Person*), **Martinus de Boer**—reads Romans 1 in a third register entirely. The wrath revealed from heaven (1:18) is not God's juridical response to individual sins but God's apocalyptic invasion against the cosmic powers (Sin, Death, Flesh) that have enslaved humanity. The threefold *paredōken* (1:24, 26, 28) is divine handing-over to *the Power of Sin*—God permits humanity to fall into the bondage they have chosen, so that the rescue operation through Christ can be all the more transparently a deliverance, not a contractual exchange. Campbell reads 1:18–32 controversially as Paul's *speech-in-character* on behalf of a Jewish-Christian "Teacher" whose theology Paul will repudiate beginning at 2:1—a thesis hotly contested but worth engaging seriously. Gaventa's reading of the *paredōken* refrain is gentler and more textually anchored: God's giving-over is a maternal act of permitting the runaway child to come to the end of their own resources, not a forensic abandonment.

The **Paul Within Judaism / Radical New Perspective**—**Mark Nanos** (*The Mystery of Romans*), **Magnus Zetterholm**, **Pamela Eisenbaum** (*Paul Was Not a Christian*), **Matthew Thiessen** (*Paul and the Gentile Problem*; *A Jewish Paul*), **Paula Fredriksen** (*Paul: The Pagan's Apostle*)—reads Romans 1 as a Gentile-only indictment. Paul, in this school, never left Judaism; his apostolic commission is specifically to the *ethnē* (Gentiles, 1:5, 13–14), and the entirety of 1:18–32 describes the Gentile situation in pagan terms drawn from Hellenistic-Jewish polemic (especially Wisdom of Solomon). Thiessen argues that Romans is structurally a Gentile-problem letter: how do pagans, enslaved to idols and the disordered passions that follow, become children of Abraham without becoming proselytes? The wrath of 1:18 is not against humanity-in-general but against pagan humanity in its idolatry. Israel's covenant remains operative; Torah remains the proper way of life for Jews who follow Jesus as Messiah.

The story of how Romans 1 has been read is itself the story of Western Christian self-understanding. **Origen**'s *Commentary on Romans*, preserved in Rufinus's Latin translation and now available in the critical edition by Theresia Heither and C. P. Hammond Bammel, is the most extensive patristic commentary—Origen reads the chapter through a moderately optimistic anthropology, distinguishing the natural knowledge of God available to all (1:19–20) from the saving knowledge of Christ; he resists any reading that would deny human freedom. **Augustine**'s anti-Pelagian Romans (*Ad Romanos*, *De Spiritu et Littera*) hardens into a doctrine of original sin and bound will read out of 1:18–32 in conjunction with 5:12. **Pelagius**'s *Commentary on Romans*, recovered in critical edition by **Alexander Souter** between 1922 and 1931, reads the chapter without Augustine's bondage-of-the-will framework: the indictment is real but does not exclude Gentile capacity for moral response. **John Chrysostom**'s 32 *Homilies on Romans* offer the richest Greek patristic engagement, reading 1:26–27 with characteristic moralism but reading 1:18–20 with characteristic confidence in natural revelation. **Ambrosiaster** (the anonymous late-4th-century Roman commentator), **Theodoret of Cyrus**, and **Aquinas**'s *Lectura super Epistolam ad Romanos* round out the pre-modern story.

The Reformation produced the most consequential rereading. **Luther's *Lectures on Romans*** (1515–16), recovered in 1908 from the Berlin manuscript, document the moment when the medieval *iustitia distributiva* framework collapsed under exegetical pressure on 1:17. Luther's 1522 *Preface to Romans* called the letter "the truly purest gospel" and shaped Protestant piety for four centuries. **Melanchthon**'s *Loci Communes* (1521) organized Protestant theology around Romans. **Calvin**'s *Commentary on Romans* (1540), his first New Testament commentary, sealed the Reformed reading. Erasmus's *Annotationes* on Romans, with which Luther engaged closely, kept the philological pressure on the text.

The 20th century broke the Reformation consensus open. **Karl Barth**'s *Romans Commentary* (2nd ed., 1922) read the letter in apocalyptic mode—"the strange new world of the Bible"—as the announcement of God's senkrechte von oben (vertical from above) crisis irrupting into human religion and culture. Barth's Romans changed continental theology. **Krister Stendahl**'s essay "The Apostle Paul and the Introspective Conscience of the West" (1963; reprinted in *Paul Among Jews and Gentiles*, 1976) argued that Luther had read Romans through a late-medieval Augustinian lens that Paul did not share—that Paul's "I" in Romans 7 was not the introspective conscience of Western anxiety but the apostle thinking historically about Israel under Torah. Stendahl set the stage for Sanders's New Perspective. **Daniel Boyarin**'s *A Radical Jew* (1994), reading Romans from within Jewish studies, argued that Paul radicalized rather than abandoned his Judaism. **Stowers**'s *A Rereading of Romans* (1994), and the explosion of Romans monographs and commentaries in the past three decades—**Käsemann** (Tübingen 1973), **Cranfield** (ICC 1975/79), **Fitzmyer** (AB 1993), **Moo** (NICNT 1996), **Dunn** (WBC 1988), **Jewett** (Hermeneia 2007)—have made Romans 1 the most contested chapter in modern New Testament scholarship.

We will not pick a winner among the four schools. Each illuminates real features of the text that the others miss. Old Perspective is right that Paul is talking about how sinners stand before a holy God; New Perspective is right that the question is *who counts as Abraham's family*; Apocalyptic is right that the gospel is cosmic rescue, not transactional; Paul Within Judaism is right that the chapter targets specifically Gentile idolatry. The chapter is doing all of these things at once.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What is "the righteousness of God" (*dikaiosynē theou*) at 1:17?**

The **Old Perspective / Reformed** position (Cranfield, Moo, Schreiner) reads *dikaiosynē theou* as a *genitivus auctoris*—the righteousness from God, the gift God imputes to the believer through faith. Luther's breakthrough hangs here. The verse promises that God credits the believer with a righteousness she does not possess in herself, on the basis of Christ's perfect righteousness, received through faith alone. The righteous shall live by faith.

The **New Perspective** position (Wright, Dunn, Sanders) reads *dikaiosynē theou* as **God's own covenant faithfulness**—God's saving justice, God's commitment to keep his promises to Abraham. Wright argues at length that *tsedaqah* in the Hebrew Bible (especially Isaiah 40–55) refers to God's relational fidelity rather than to a moral attribute or imputed gift. The verse therefore announces that in the gospel, God's covenant faithfulness has been revealed—God has done what he promised: provided a way for the nations to share in Abraham's blessing.

The **Apocalyptic** position (Käsemann's classic 1961 essay "*The Righteousness of God in Paul*"; Martyn; Campbell) reads *dikaiosynē theou* as God's saving power, God's invasive rescuing act—neither a forensic gift nor a covenantal attribute but God's eschatological power-in-action breaking into the cosmos. Käsemann's reading foregrounds the dynamic, performative dimension: the righteousness *of God* is God's deliverance happening.

The **Catholic** tradition, in Joseph Fitzmyer (Anchor Bible Romans) and the broader Catholic exegetical mainstream, reads *dikaiosynē theou* as God's salvific activity that justifies (makes righteous, not merely declares righteous) the sinner—a position that mediates between the Reformed forensic reading and Wright's covenantal reading by emphasizing both gift and transformation.

**Steelmanning each**: The Reformed reading rightly hears that Paul is talking about how the unrighteous come to be in the right; the New Perspective rightly hears that *dikaiosynē theou* in the LXX and in Second Temple texts overwhelmingly refers to God's own fidelity; the Apocalyptic reading rightly hears that the verse is announcing an event, not describing a doctrine; the Catholic reading rightly resists collapsing justification into a juridical fiction. **Where this leaves us**: the phrase is doing more than any single school allows. Paul is announcing that in the gospel, *God's covenant faithfulness* (Wright) has *invasively appeared* (Käsemann) in such a way that *the unrighteous receive a righteousness they could not produce* (Luther) and are *actually transformed* (Catholic). The four readings are complementary, not exclusive.

**Tension 2: How do we read 1:26–27, the "natural / unnatural" passage?**

The **traditional reading** (Augustine through Aquinas through the Reformers; in modern form **Robert Gagnon**, *The Bible and Homosexual Practice*; Richard Hays, *The Moral Vision of the New Testament*) takes 1:26–27 as a categorical condemnation of all same-sex sexual activity, rooted in a creation-order argument: Paul's *para physin* ("contrary to nature") refers to the created complementarity of male and female, and the verses describe a paradigm of human disorder traceable to the idolatry of 1:23.

The **revisionist / contextual readings** (**Dale Martin**, *Sex and the Single Savior*; **Sarah Ruden**, *Paul Among the People*; **James Brownson**, *Bible, Gender, Sexuality*; **William Loader**, *The New Testament on Sexuality*) argue that *para physin* in Greco-Roman moralist literature refers to violations of the active/passive social hierarchy rather than to sexual orientation as moderns understand it; that Paul's vocabulary is indebted to Stoic and Hellenistic-Jewish polemic against pederasty and exploitative same-sex relations specifically; and that contemporary committed same-sex partnerships are not what Paul has in view. **Sarah Ruden** in particular argues that Greco-Roman same-sex practice was overwhelmingly hierarchical and exploitative (older free male / younger slave or freedman); Paul's critique is closer to a critique of sexual abuse than of orientation.

The **Paul Within Judaism** reading (Thiessen, Fredriksen) treats 1:26–27 as part of the **Gentile-only indictment**—Paul is describing the disordered sexuality of pagan culture as a symptom of idolatry, a standard topos of Hellenistic-Jewish ethics. The verses are not addressed to Christians but describe the situation Christ rescues Gentiles *from*. Whether this changes the verses' normative force for Christian ethics is a separate question.

A **fourth position**, which we might call the **trajectory reading** (**Luke Timothy Johnson**'s *Scripture and Discernment*; William Webb's *Slaves, Women, and Homosexuals*), holds that the church's reading of Scripture has historically moved on slavery and on women's roles in ways that complicate any flat appeal to Paul's first-century categories—and asks whether the same hermeneutical movement is appropriate here.

**Steelmanning**: The traditional reading rightly hears that Paul is making a moral claim, not merely describing a sociological fact, and that his *para physin* echoes a creation-order theology developed across Romans (cf. ch. 5 on Adam). The revisionist readings rightly hear that Paul's first-century cultural location is genuinely different from ours and that his vocabulary is doing specific contextual work. The Paul-Within-Judaism reading rightly hears that this passage is rhetorically positioned in a Gentile-targeted indictment and is doing diagnostic work. The trajectory reading rightly hears that Christians have changed their minds before about how Paul's words apply across cultural distance.

**Where this leaves us**: This is the locus classicus of contemporary interpretive controversy in the church and the academy, and any honest reading must hold the exegetical evidence with care. Paul's language is real, his diagnosis of pagan disorder is sharp, and his moral seriousness is unambiguous; at the same time, the cultural distance between first-century Roman sexual practice and modern partnerships is also real, and the long history of Christian rereading on slavery, on women in ministry, and on multiple other questions is also real. We do not resolve the tension here. We name what each position genuinely sees.

**Unresolved question**: Is Romans 1:18–32 describing the pre-Christian Gentile world specifically, the pre-Christian *human* world generally, or *every* human heart including the Christian's? The chapter's rhetorical position—Paul about to spring the trap of 2:1 against the moralizing judge—suggests that the indictment is functioning as a setup, not as Paul's own settled diagnosis of how things are. But the *paredōken* refrain has been read by Augustine, Luther, and Calvin as describing the universal human condition under sin. Where you stand on this question shapes much of how you read Romans 1–3.

## The Thread

Romans 1 sets four threads that run through the whole letter:

**The gospel as power for salvation, to the Jew first and also to the Greek** (1:16) returns at 2:9–10, 3:9, 9–11, and the great climactic affirmation of 11:32 ("God has consigned all to disobedience, that he may have mercy on all").

**The righteousness of God revealed from faith for faith** (1:17, citing Hab 2:4) is taken up at 3:21–26, the central kerygmatic passage of the letter, and at 4:1–25 (Abraham as paradigm), and 10:3–4 (Israel's failure to recognize the righteousness of God).

**The wrath of God revealed against ungodliness** (1:18) is answered at 5:9 ("we shall be saved by him from the wrath of God") and 8:1 ("there is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus").

**God gave them up** (1:24, 26, 28) finds its inversion at 4:25 ("delivered up [*paredothē*] for our trespasses and raised for our justification") and 8:32 ("he who did not spare his own Son but gave him up [*paredōken*] for us all"). The same divine handing-over verb—first against humanity, then for humanity—is one of Paul's most powerful structural devices.

[Flag: Romans 1:3–4 (Davidic Son / Son of God by resurrection) anticipates 8:3 (sending the Son), 8:11 (Spirit of him who raised Jesus), and 8:32 (sparing not his own Son)—Paul's Christology is built into the letter's frame from the first sentence.]
