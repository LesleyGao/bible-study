# Mark 14: The Long Night — Anointing, Supper, Garden, Trial, Denial

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens at a calendrical hinge: "two days before the Passover and the Feast of Unleavened Bread" (14:1). In first-century Jerusalem this meant the city was filling. Josephus claims (with characteristic exaggeration) that 2.7 million pilgrims gathered for Passover (*Jewish War* 6.422–425); modern estimates by E.P. Sanders (*Judaism: Practice and Belief 63 BCE–66 CE*) and Joachim Jeremias (*Jerusalem in the Time of Jesus*) bring this down to perhaps 100,000–300,000 in a city whose normal population was 30,000–80,000. The chief priests' anxiety in 14:2 — "not during the feast, lest there be an uproar from the people" — is the anxiety of a Sanhedrin facing a city swollen with Galilean pilgrims who had cheered Jesus's entry six days earlier. Roman procurator Pontius Pilate was in town for crowd control, having moved up from his Caesarea Maritima residence to the Antonia Fortress for the festival.

**Bethany** (14:3) sits about 2.4 km southeast of Jerusalem on the eastern slope of the Mount of Olives. The traditional site, *el-Eizariya* ("the place of Lazarus"), preserves the Aramaic name. Excavations by the Franciscans (Sylvester Saller, 1949–53) found 1st-century tombs and a domestic dwelling that became a 4th-century pilgrimage site. The host's name — *Simon the leper* — is meaningful: a leper would have been ritually unclean, the very kind of person Jesus has been associating with throughout Mark. The diners are "reclining" (*katakeimenou autou*, 14:3) on Greco-Roman *triclinia* couches, a Hellenized Jewish custom for festal meals.

The ***nard*** (νάρδος *pistikē*, 14:3) is *Nardostachys jatamansi*, a Himalayan-foothill plant whose root yields an aromatic oil known across the ancient pharmacopoeia. Pliny (*Natural History* 12.42–46) ranks Indian nard among the most expensive imports. Three hundred denarii (14:5) is roughly a year's day-laborer wages (cf. Matt 20:2). The ***alabastron*** is a long-necked stone flask sealed at the top — it had to be broken to release the contents, signaling that the entire flask is committed to a single use.

The Last Supper sits within the chronology problem that has dogged the New Testament since Patristic times: the Synoptics make 14:12 ("on the first day of Unleavened Bread, when they sacrificed the Passover lamb") an unambiguous Passover meal, while John 18:28 and 19:14 place the meal the day before, with Jesus crucified at the hour the Passover lambs were slaughtered. **Annie Jaubert** (*The Date of the Last Supper*, 1957) proposed that Jesus followed the older solar calendar attested at Qumran, with Passover on Tuesday evening, while the Sadducean lunar calendar pushed the official feast to Friday — this would harmonize the Synoptic Passover with John's pre-Passover crucifixion. Most modern scholars do not accept Jaubert's reconstruction, but **Joachim Jeremias** (*The Eucharistic Words of Jesus*) defends the Synoptic chronology on linguistic and ritual grounds: the bread blessed and broken, the four cups, the singing of the *Hallel* (14:26 — Psalms 113–118 traditionally sung at Passover), the *afikoman* gesture all reflect Passover liturgy. **Raymond Brown** (*The Death of the Messiah*, 2 vols.) judges the chronological problem irreconcilable on historical grounds and reads the Synoptics and John as theologically motivated narratives that cannot be harmonized at the day-level.

The "**man carrying a jar of water**" (14:13) is conspicuous because water-carrying was women's work. Bargil Pixner and Jerome Murphy-O'Connor (*The Holy Land*) have proposed the upper room was in the Essene Quarter on the southwest hill — the Essenes being a male-only community — and identified the traditional *Cenacle* on Mount Zion as a plausible site.

**Gethsemane** (14:32) — *Gat-shemanim*, "oil press" — sits at the foot of the Mount of Olives on the western slope above the Kidron. The traditional site adjacent to the Church of All Nations contains olive trees that have been radiocarbon-dated to the 11th–12th centuries CE, but the *roots* and the place itself are continuous; the Bordeaux Pilgrim (333 CE) and Egeria (380s CE) both knew the location. Gethsemane was an oil-press grove — not pristine wilderness but a working agricultural property, likely owned by someone Jesus knew.

The high priest in 14:53 is Caiaphas (Joseph son of Caiaphas), who served 18–36 CE. The **ossuary of Caiaphas** discovered in 1990 in the Peace Forest south of Jerusalem (excavated by Zvi Greenhut) bears the Aramaic inscription *Yehosef bar Qayafa* and contained the remains of a 60-year-old man — the most direct archaeological connection to a named person in the passion narrative.

Five Greek terms drive the chapter. ***paradidōmi*** (παραδίδωμι, "hand over / betray") is the programmatic Markan passion verb, occurring at 14:10, 11, 18, 21, 41, 42, 44 — saturating the chapter; the verb echoes the Septuagint of Isaiah 53:6, 12 (*paredothē*, "he was handed over"). ***Abba ho patēr*** (Ἀββα ὁ Πατήρ, 14:36) preserves the Aramaic vocative — "*Abba*" is the intimate paternal address Joachim Jeremias famously argued was distinctive of Jesus's prayer; Paul preserves the same form in Romans 8:15 and Galatians 4:6, evidence the early church kept the Aramaic. ***egō eimi*** (ἐγώ εἰμι, "I am," 14:62) — Jesus's most explicit self-disclosure in Mark, layered with both ordinary affirmation and the divine name of LXX Exodus 3:14 and Isaiah 43:10. ***lēstēs*** (λῃστής, 14:48, "robber/insurgent") is the term Josephus uses for the Zealot revolutionaries (*Jewish War* 2.253–254); not "thief" but armed political insurgent. ***blasphēmia*** (14:64) is the formal Sanhedrin charge, regulated in *m. Sanhedrin* 7:5 — Jesus's specific words and the high priest's tearing of garments fit the procedural pattern.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Mark 14 is the longest chapter in the Gospel and contains its most virtuosic literary architecture. Two devices dominate.

The **Markan sandwich (intercalation)** of 14:53–72 is the chapter's structural masterpiece. Mark begins Peter's approach to the courtyard at 14:54 ("And Peter had followed him at a distance"), then suspends Peter to narrate the Sanhedrin trial (14:55–65), then returns to Peter for the three denials (14:66–72). The two stories interpret each other. Inside the high priest's house, *Jesus speaks the truth and is condemned*; outside in the courtyard, *Peter speaks the lie and is spared*. Inside, Jesus is asked "Are you the Christ?" and answers *egō eimi*, "I am"; outside, Peter is asked "Were you with the Nazarene?" and answers *ouk oida* ("I do not know"). The two confessions are mirrored, and Mark's reader cannot read either without the other. James R. Edwards (*The Markan Sandwich*) and Tom Shepherd (*Markan Sandwich Stories*) have catalogued this device across Mark; chapter 14 is its theological climax.

The second device is the **anointing inclusio**. Mark opens his passion narrative with a woman anointing Jesus's body for burial (14:3–9) and ends it with women coming to anoint his body for burial (16:1–8) and finding the tomb empty. The first anointing succeeds; the second is rendered unnecessary by the resurrection. Joel Marcus (AB) reads the structure as Mark's framing of the entire passion under the sign of *anointing* — the messianic act (Hebrew *māshîaḥ*, the anointed one) is performed not by a king-maker prophet but by an unnamed woman in a leper's house. Frank Matera (*The Kingship of Jesus*) makes the case that this is *royal* anointing: the head, not the feet (contrast John 12:3, where Mary anoints Jesus's feet — engage which-anointing-is-which scholarship; Mark's account, John's, and Luke 7:36–50's Galilean sinful-woman story have been variously identified, conflated, and distinguished since Augustine, who in *De Consensu Evangelistarum* 2.79 identified two anointings).

The pericope carries its own remarkable commission: "wherever the gospel is preached…what she has done will be told in memory of her" (14:9). The contrast Mark builds is pointed — the disciples in the same chapter cannot stay awake, Judas is even now arranging the betrayal, and yet the unnamed woman has perceived what they have not, that Jesus is going to die, and has acted on it. Joel Marcus (AB) and Morna Hooker (BNTC) both note the irony that the Gospel preserves the betrayer's name and not hers, while granting her act a permanence attached to the gospel-proclamation itself.

The discourse at the supper (14:22–25) is the most carefully formed liturgical material in Mark. The bread word — *touto estin to sōma mou* ("this is my body") — and the cup word — *touto estin to haima mou tēs diathēkēs* ("this is my blood of the covenant") — have parallels in 1 Corinthians 11:23–26 (Paul, ca. 54 CE, attesting an even earlier tradition), Matthew 26:26–29, and Luke 22:19–20. **Joachim Jeremias** (*The Eucharistic Words of Jesus*) reconstructs the Aramaic substrate and argues for the antiquity and historical reliability of these words; he reads the *hyper pollōn* ("for many") at 14:24 as a Semitic inclusive ("for the many" = "for all") drawing directly on Isaiah 53:11–12 (*la-rabbim*).

Markan signature features: ***euthys*** ("immediately") returns at 14:43 and 14:45 (the betrayal sequence); the triadic structure governs Gethsemane (Jesus prays three times, finds the disciples sleeping three times) and Peter's denials (three times); the failure-of-the-disciples motif reaches its climax (the inner three sleep in Gethsemane, all flee at the arrest, Peter denies). Mark's distinctive realism in the Gethsemane scene — *ekthambeisthai kai adēmonein* (14:33, "to be greatly distressed and troubled") — is a level of emotional candor about Jesus that Matthew and Luke soften and that some early Christian copyists found theologically uncomfortable.

A textual variant worth flagging: 14:68's "and the rooster crowed" is omitted in some important manuscripts (including Sinaiticus and Vaticanus). If original, it allows the *two* crowings (14:30, 72) to bracket the three denials cleanly; if a later harmonization, the single crowing of 14:72 stands alone. Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary* gives the longer reading a {C} rating — genuinely uncertain.

The **young man fleeing naked** (14:51–52, *neaniskos*… *gymnos ephygen*) is unique to Mark. Three readings dominate. (1) **Eyewitness self-signature**: this is Mark himself (John Mark of Acts 12:12, whose mother's house may have been the upper-room location), inserting himself anonymously as the Gospel's only eyewitness signature. (2) **Theological symbol**: the flight echoes Genesis 39:12 (Joseph fleeing Potiphar's wife, leaving his garment) and inverts Amos 2:16 ("the stout-hearted shall flee away naked in that day"). Robin Scroggs and Werner Kelber suggested baptismal symbolism — the *neaniskos* leaves his linen burial cloth (*sindōn*, the same word for Joseph of Arimathea's burial cloth in 15:46) and the resurrected body recovers what the disciple lost. (3) **Coincidence with the resurrection scene**: the young man (*neaniskos*) in white at the empty tomb (16:5) deliberately echoes the young man fleeing naked here — a paired structure of failure and vindication. The 1958 **Morton Smith** *Secret Mark* / *Mar Saba letter* controversy proposed an expanded Markan version with homoerotic overtones at this verse; the letter is now widely (though not universally) judged a modern forgery (engage Stephen Carlson *The Gospel Hoax*; Peter Jeffery *The Secret Gospel of Mark Unveiled*).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc behind chapter 14 is Israel's Passover. The ***exodus*** is the structuring story: a lamb slain, blood smeared on doorposts, deliverance through the night. Mark places Jesus's words of institution against this background — "this is my blood of the covenant" (14:24) directly cites Exodus 24:8, where Moses sprinkles the people with sacrificial blood and says, "behold, the blood of the covenant which the Lord has made with you." The supper is not a generic last meal; it is a covenant meal that retrospectively interprets the cross as exodus, sacrifice, and covenant inauguration in one. *Hyper pollōn* draws Isaiah 53's Suffering Servant into the explanation. **N.T. Wright** (*Jesus and the Victory of God*; *The Day the Revolution Began*) builds his entire atonement theology from this moment: Jesus is performing a prophetic-symbolic act that interprets his own death as the inauguration of the new exodus.

Within Second Temple Judaism, *Abba ho patēr* (14:36) sits in interpretive tension. **Joachim Jeremias** famously argued that *Abba* was distinctively intimate, even childlike, and unique to Jesus among first-century Jewish prayer practice — a claim that James Barr (*Abba Isn't Daddy*, 1988) and James Dunn have substantially qualified. *Abba* is the ordinary Aramaic word for father; it is not toddler-speak. But the *vocative direct address to God as Father* in personal prayer is, on the available Second Temple evidence, less common than later Christian piety has assumed, and Jeremias's substantive point survives the qualification.

**Geza Vermes** (*Jesus the Jew*) places Gethsemane in the tradition of the *hasidim* — Galilean charismatic-prophetic figures whose intimacy with God in prayer was distinctive. **Amy-Jill Levine** (*The Misunderstood Jew*) cautions that the Sanhedrin trial scene has been used as a template for Christian anti-Judaism for nineteen centuries; her work and that of **Daniel Boyarin** (*The Jewish Gospels*) press for reading the trial within Jewish messianic discourse rather than as a Jews-vs-Christians confrontation. Boyarin in particular argues that Jesus's "Son of Man coming with the clouds" claim (14:62) was *not* obviously blasphemous in Second Temple Jewish thought — there was a robust binitarian tradition in Daniel-7 reception (1 Enoch's *Parables*; the *Two Powers* tradition documented by Alan Segal) — and the high priest's outrage may reflect political threat as much as theological scandal.

The Christian interpretive trajectory has poured itself into this chapter. **Origen** (*Commentary on Matthew*; *On Prayer*) reads Gethsemane allegorically and as a model of the soul's submission. **Augustine** (*Tractates on John* 119; *Sermon* 232) makes the Eucharistic words foundational for sacramental theology — *crede et manducasti* ("believe and you have eaten"). **Bede** (*Commentary on Mark*) and **Theophylact** (*Explanation of the Gospel of Mark*) carry the patristic line into the medieval West and East. **Aquinas** (*ST* III q. 73–83 — the entire Eucharistic *quaestio*; *Catena Aurea* on Mark 14) develops transubstantiation from Mark 14:22's *touto estin*. **Calvin** (*Institutes* 4.17) and **Luther** (*Babylonian Captivity*; *Small Catechism*) construct opposed Reformation Eucharistic theologies from the same words: Calvin's *spiritual real presence*, Luther's *sacramental union* (in, with, and under the elements), Zwingli's *memorial*. Anglican via media (Cranmer, Hooker) and Anabaptist memorialism diverge from there.

**Joachim Jeremias** (*The Eucharistic Words*) is the modern foundation; **Raymond Brown** (*The Death of the Messiah*) is the most exhaustive modern passion-narrative commentary, treating chapter 14 across hundreds of pages. **Joel Marcus** (AB), **Adela Yarbro Collins** (Hermeneia), **R.T. France** (NIGTC), **Morna Hooker** (BNTC) carry the modern critical reading. **Ched Myers** (*Binding the Strong Man*) reads chapter 14 politically — the supper as anti-imperial table-fellowship, the *lēstēs* charge as evidence Jesus was crucified as a political insurgent.

**Bonhoeffer** (*Discipleship*; *Letters and Papers from Prison* — the so-called "Outline for a Book") makes Mark 14 a load-bearing chapter for his Christology. The Christ of Gethsemane is the *suffering* Christ — "Christ helps us not by virtue of his omnipotence but by virtue of his weakness and his suffering"; "only the suffering God can help." **Hans Urs von Balthasar** (*Mysterium Paschale*) reads Gethsemane and the cup as the eternal Son's reception, in time, of the Father's bitter chalice — the Trinitarian structure of the passion. **Jürgen Moltmann** (*The Crucified God*) frames the Gethsemane prayer as the boundary of Christology: any Christology that flinches at *adēmonein* has not yet seen the Son.

**Bailey** (*Jesus Through Middle Eastern Eyes*) reads the narrative through Mediterranean honor-shame conventions — the woman's anointing in the leper's house, the disciples' shame at sleeping, Peter's curses, the kiss of betrayal — drawing out the cultural texture of dishonor that Western readers regularly miss. **Klassen** (*Judas: Betrayer or Friend of Jesus?*) and **Susan Gubar** (*Judas: A Biography*) trace the textual and interpretive history of Judas; the Coptic *Gospel of Judas* (manuscript discovered in the 1970s, published 2006) presents Judas as the disciple who alone understood Jesus's mission — a Sethian Gnostic revaluation that has been received with both fascination and skepticism (engage April DeConick *The Thirteenth Apostle*).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: How is Christ present in the supper?**

The ***Roman Catholic*** position, dogmatically defined at Trent (1545–63, Session 13) and articulated in the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §1373–77, holds *transubstantiation*: by consecration the substance of the bread and wine is converted into the substance of Christ's body and blood; the accidents (appearance, taste) remain. The strength: it takes *touto estin* with maximal seriousness and preserves a genuinely sacrificial and adoration-worthy presence. The weakness: it requires Aristotelian metaphysics of substance and accidents that not all theological traditions accept.

The ***Lutheran*** position (Luther's *Babylonian Captivity*; Formula of Concord VII; Lutheran scholasticism's *sacramental union* doctrine, formerly mislabeled "consubstantiation") holds that Christ is truly present "in, with, and under" the bread and wine — the elements and the body coexist sacramentally without conversion. The strength: it preserves real presence without metaphysical conversion. The weakness: it shares the metaphysical difficulty of locating the body of Christ in Christological space.

The ***Reformed*** position has two streams. **Zwingli's** memorialism reads *touto estin* as figural: the bread *signifies* the body. The strength: it aligns with the ordinary-language sense of figurative predication. The weakness: it strains *poured out for many* and the covenant-blood language, and it severs supper from sacrifice. **Calvin's** *spiritual real presence* (Institutes 4.17) holds that the believer is lifted by the Spirit to feed on the ascended Christ; the presence is real but pneumatic. This is, for many modern Reformed and ecumenically minded interpreters, the most elegant formulation.

The ***Anglican via media*** (Cranmer, Richard Hooker *Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity* 5.67) holds the question as a deliberate ambiguity — the presence is real, the mode is mystery; do not overdefine. The ***Anabaptist*** memorial tradition reads the supper primarily as church covenant-renewal and Christ-remembrance.

**Where this leaves us**: every tradition reads *touto estin* honestly in some sense. The question is not whether Christ is given in the supper but how that giving relates to the bread. Mark's text, by itself, will not adjudicate; Mark gives us the *act* and lets the church think about it for two thousand years.

**Tension Two: Is Gethsemane an exemplary submission, an unrepeatable atoning act, or a real wrestling?**

The ***exemplarist*** reading (Abelard's *Commentary on Romans*; modern liberal Protestantism, e.g., Hastings Rashdall *The Idea of Atonement*) takes Gethsemane as the pattern for human submission to God's will — Jesus models the prayer of *not what I will but what you will*, and the disciple is to imitate. The strength: it preserves the ethical force and avoids reducing Christ to a transactional offering. The weakness: it tends to evacuate the cup of objective content.

The ***penal-substitutionary / objective atonement*** reading (Anselm *Cur Deus Homo*; Calvin *Institutes* 2.16; Charles Hodge; modern Reformed) reads the cup as the cup of God's wrath against sin (Isa 51:17, 22; Jer 25:15–16) — Jesus's anguish is the anguish of a sinless one who is about to bear the curse. The strength: it takes the cup imagery's Old Testament background with full seriousness. The weakness: it can collapse into a transactional metaphysics that makes the Father an opponent of the Son.

The ***Trinitarian-relational*** reading (Balthasar *Mysterium Paschale*; Moltmann *The Crucified God*; T.F. Torrance) holds that the cup is real and bitter, that the Father's will and the Son's will remain perfectly united in love but tragically opposed in the moment of the cup's drinking; the obedient Son receives the chalice from the Father not as judicial penalty but as the cost of incarnation entering the depths of human alienation. The strength: it preserves the genuine drama of Gethsemane without falling into either transactionalism or sentimentality. The weakness: it requires careful Trinitarian formulation to avoid implying conflict within the divine life.

**Where this leaves us**: Mark refuses to *explain* Gethsemane. He shows us the prostration, the sweat, the silence of heaven, the threefold sleep of friends. The theological frame must come from elsewhere — from Isaiah 53, from Hebrews 5:7, from Paul on the obedience of the Son. But Mark has set the limit: any atonement theology that does not feel the *adēmonein* — the genuine distress — has not yet read this chapter.

**An unresolved question**: if Peter, who has eaten the bread and drunk the cup with Jesus, denies him within hours, what does the supper *do*? The chapter sets the sacrament and the failure side by side, refusing to make the supper a magic shield against denial. The same question that haunts every Christian after every communion haunts the chapter itself.

## The Thread

14:24's "blood of the covenant…poured out for many" returns at 15:38 in the temple veil torn from top to bottom — the new covenant inaugurated by the supper is sealed by the death the supper anticipated. 14:36's *Abba* prayer carries forward into Romans 8:15 and Galatians 4:6, where Paul makes the disciple's *Abba* prayer the evidence of the Spirit's adoption — Gethsemane is not a private agony but the door through which the church learns to pray. 14:50's "they all left him and fled" reaches to 16:7's "go, tell his disciples and Peter" — the resurrection is a regathering of the very disciples who failed at the arrest. And 14:62's "Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power" is the same parousia formula from 13:26 — what the high priest hears as blasphemy, the reader has been taught in chapter 13 to hear as the Gospel's eschatological horizon.
