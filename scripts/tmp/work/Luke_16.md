# Luke 16: Mammon, Stewardship, and the Great Chasm

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Luke 16 is the chapter where the social pressure of money lies heaviest in the entire Gospel. To read it without the first-century economic substrate is to read it twice removed from its bite. Three contexts matter.

First, the *agrarian patron-debtor economy*. The "manager" (***oikonomos***, οἰκονόμος) of 16:1 is a steward in a large estate — typically a freedman or slave with delegated authority over a wealthy landowner's affairs. K.C. Hanson and Douglas Oakman's *Palestine in the Time of Jesus*, drawing on the social-economic anthropology pioneered by Gerhard Lenski, describe a deeply stratified rural economy in which absentee landowners (perhaps 1–2 percent of the population) controlled the bulk of arable land while subsistence farmers, tenants, and day laborers struggled at the bottom. A hundred *batous* of oil (ESV "measures," 16:6) is roughly 800–900 gallons — the yield of about 150 olive trees, an enormous quantity reflecting a substantial commercial debt rather than peasant arrears. A hundred *korous* of wheat (16:7) is roughly 1,000 bushels, the yield of perhaps 100 acres. These are merchant-scale obligations. The reductions the steward authorizes (50 percent on oil, 20 percent on wheat) are correspondingly large. J.D.M. Derrett, in his much-cited 1961 essay "Fresh Light on the Parable of the Unjust Steward," argued that the steward removed his own commission — a reading Bailey extended in *Poet and Peasant* by proposing the reductions removed the *interest* on these debts (interest concealed as "in-kind" surcharges to skirt the Torah's prohibition of usury, Deut 23:19–20), which is why the master could not openly punish the steward without exposing his own evasion of the Law.

Second, the *Mediterranean honor-shame culture* in which the steward operates. His monologue in 16:3 — "I am not strong enough to dig, and I am ashamed to beg" — names the two outlets unavailable to him. Manual labor and begging are below the dignity of an *oikonomos*; what he does instead is buy patron-style obligation from the master's debtors, ensuring "they may receive me into their houses" (16:4). Bruce Malina and Jerome Neyrey have shown that this is a recognized strategy of "limited good" maneuvering — converting one form of capital (institutional position) into another (a network of obligated patrons) before the position is lost.

Third, the *Lazarus-and-the-rich-man* scene draws on a network of well-attested cultural materials. The "purple and fine linen" (***byssos***, βύσσος) of 16:19 is Tyrian purple and Egyptian linen — the most expensive fabrics in the Mediterranean economy, signifying senatorial-class luxury. The rich man "feasted sumptuously every day" (***euphrainomenos lamprōs***, εὐφραινόμενος λαμπρῶς) — daily feasting was an aristocratic practice contrasted in Lukan rhetoric with the daily bread of the poor. Lazarus (***Lazaros***, Λάζαρος, from Hebrew *El'azar*, "God has helped") is the only character given a name in any Jesus parable — a rhetorical reversal, since in real first-century Mediterranean society it was the rich whose names were known and the poor who were anonymous. Dogs licking his sores (16:21) compounds the indignity: dogs in Jewish reckoning were unclean scavengers, and the image is of an outcast unable even to drive them off. K.C. Hanson notes that the description of Lazarus at the *pylōn* (πυλών, "gate") of the rich man's compound matches the architecture of elite urban *domus* — a gated street entrance with a vestibule.

Three Greek terms anchor the chapter. ***Mamōnas tēs adikias*** (μαμωνᾶς τῆς ἀδικίας, "mammon of unrighteousness," 16:9) — *mamōnas* is a Semitic loanword, possibly derived from a root meaning "that which is entrusted," which Jesus renders sinister by personifying it. ***Oikonomos*** (16:1) — manager, steward, the same root as our "economy"; the parable is literally about how to manage the household. ***Hadēs*** (ᾅδης, 16:23) — the Greek term Jesus uses, mapped onto the Jewish *Sheol*; the choice of terminology matters for the eschatology debate below.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The Dishonest Manager (16:1–13) is the single most disputed Jesus parable in interpretive history. Joel Green calls the chapter's first thirteen verses "perhaps the most difficult passage of Luke's Gospel," and Klyne Snodgrass, in *Stories with Intent*, devotes more than fifty pages to surveying proposed solutions. The interpretive crux is 16:8: "the master commended the dishonest manager for his shrewdness." Who is "the master"? — the rich man in the parable, or Jesus himself ("the Lord")? And on what grounds is the steward commended?

A taxonomy of proposed solutions: (1) the *allegorical* reading (Augustine, much of the patristic tradition) — the master is God, the steward is humanity, the debtors are sinners whose debts are reduced by mercy; (2) the *self-commission removal* reading (Derrett) — the steward gave up his own cut, which the master could only commend; (3) the *interest removal* reading (Bailey) — the steward exposed the master's illegal interest by removing it, putting the master in a position where he could only publicly applaud; (4) the *shrewdness without endorsement* reading (Fitzmyer, Bock) — the steward's actions are not endorsed but his crisis-decisiveness is held up as an example, much as Jesus elsewhere uses morally compromised characters (the unjust judge, 18:1–8) to make a *qal va-homer* argument; (5) the *parable as parable of the kingdom* reading (Joachim Jeremias) — the kingdom irrupting calls for crisis-response, and only the worldly know how to act under crisis. Snodgrass himself favors a version of (4) and (5) combined.

The literary structure of Luke 16 is bracketed by two parables about a "rich man" (***anthrōpos tis plousios***, 16:1 and 16:19) — Bovon, Tannehill, and Joel Green all note this inclusio. Between them sits a cluster of teachings that are at once thematically tight (money, Law, kingdom) and structurally jarring — Joel Green calls 16:14–18 "the most enigmatic short passage in Luke." The verses on the Law and the Prophets (16:16–17), divorce (16:18), and the Pharisaic mockery (16:14–15) sit between the two parables not as a non-sequitur but as a hinge: the Pharisees are "lovers of money" (***philarguroi***, φιλάργυροι, 16:14), the periodization of the Law marks Jesus' ministry as the eschatological turning, and the divorce saying functions — as Tannehill argues — as a concrete instance of the "not one dot of the Law" claim. Luke's Lukan periodization here ("the Law and the Prophets were until John") is the textual foundation for Hans Conzelmann's three-epoch scheme in *Die Mitte der Zeit* (*The Theology of St. Luke*) — Israel, Jesus, Church.

The Rich Man and Lazarus (16:19–31) is uniquely Lukan. It draws on a recognizable folktale-pattern about reversal in the afterlife — the Egyptian "Si-Osiris" tale, the rabbinic story of the rich publican Bar Ma'jan and the poor scholar (b. Sanhedrin 44b, j. Sanhedrin 6.6, j. Hagigah 2.2), and other Mediterranean iterations. Joachim Jeremias, in *The Parables of Jesus*, traced the parallels in detail. Levine, in *Short Stories by Jesus*, cautions that Jesus' version differs from the rabbinic ones in important respects: there is no moral foothold given to the rich man (no last-minute mitzvah, no hidden virtue), and the parable resolves not in afterlife geography but in a charge to "Moses and the Prophets" — making it programmatically Lukan in its insistence on Torah-shaped ethics as the test of faithfulness.

Textually, the chapter is largely stable; the most-discussed variant is at 16:9 ("when it fails" / "when you fail"), and at 16:16 the verb ***biazetai*** (βιάζεται, "forces his way" or "is forcefully entered") is famously ambiguous in voice (middle or passive) — a classic crux for Lukan eschatology.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish readings.** First-century Jewish afterlife views were not monolithic. E.P. Sanders in *Judaism: Practice and Belief, 63 BCE–66 CE*, N.T. Wright in *The Resurrection of the Son of God*, and Daniel Boyarin in his work on early Jewish-Christian eschatology have all stressed the diversity. The Pharisees affirmed bodily resurrection and an intermediate state; the Sadducees denied resurrection; apocalyptic streams (1 Enoch, 4 Ezra) developed elaborate geographies of post-mortem reward and punishment. The image of "Abraham's bosom" (***eis ton kolpon Abraam***, 16:22) appears in 4 Maccabees 13:17 and rabbinic literature as a euphemism for the resting place of the righteous dead. Pinchas Lapide and Geza Vermes both note that the parable's afterlife framework would have been entirely intelligible to a Pharisaic audience without requiring novelty. Levine reads the parable's punchline — "they have Moses and the Prophets" — as a strongly Jewish theological claim: revelation is sufficient; resurrection appearance is not the necessary precondition for faithful response. She is sharp, in *Short Stories by Jesus*, on the supersessionist Christian readings that have made the rich man a figure for "the Jews" who reject Jesus; the parable's logic is internal to Israel, not against it.

**Christian readings.** The patristic tradition treated the Rich Man and Lazarus as a window into the geography of the afterlife. Tertullian's *De Anima* used the parable to defend an intermediate state. Augustine, in his *City of God*, distinguished between the "bosom of Abraham" (the temporary rest of the righteous before Christ's harrowing of hell) and the heavenly state. Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* Supplement (qq. 69–70) and the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §§1030–1032 build the doctrine of purgatory partly from the parable's intermediate-state imagery, though the parable does not itself describe purgatory. The Eastern Orthodox tradition reads the parable in the framework of the *toll-houses* tradition (more popular than dogmatic) and the broader teaching on the *aerial particular judgment*.

The Reformation pivoted. Calvin in his *Harmony* read the parable as a warning against the abuse of wealth, not a topographical map of the afterlife. Luther preached on the parable repeatedly, focusing on faith and vocation — the rich man's sin is unbelief expressed as indifference. The Reformed and Lutheran traditions have generally affirmed *Eternal Conscious Torment* (the rich man's torment is everlasting and conscious), with the parable as one of its key supports. John Wesley, in his *Notes on the New Testament*, read it the same way.

In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the parable has become the central New Testament text in three competing eschatological reconstructions. *Annihilationism* (Conditional Immortality) — represented by Edward Fudge in *The Fire That Consumes*, John Stott (in dialogue with David Edwards), and Clark Pinnock — argues that the parable is a parable, not a doctrinal map, and that the consistent biblical witness is to ultimate destruction of the wicked, not eternal torment. *Universalism* — represented in patristic form by Origen's *apokatastasis*, in modern Reformed form by Karl Barth (cautiously), and in contemporary form by David Bentley Hart in *That All Shall Be Saved* — argues that the chasm of 16:26 is in this life, not the next, and that the parable does not foreclose ultimate restoration. *Eternal Conscious Torment*, defended in contemporary terms by D.A. Carson, Robert Peterson, and Larry Dixon, holds the parable's plain reading. The debate is alive.

Lazarus has long been read as a programmatic figure for the chapter's economic indictment. Justo González in *Luke* (Belief commentary) makes the point sharply: the rich man's sin is never specified — he simply ate while Lazarus starved at his gate; the chasm between them in the afterlife dramatizes the chasm they had already created in this one. The same emphasis runs through patristic preaching on the parable (Basil, Chrysostom, Ambrose) and through the Lukan Beatitudes-and-Woes that the parable enacts.

N.T. Wright, in *Luke for Everyone*, *Jesus and the Victory of God*, and (on the cross-as-revolution theme) *The Day the Revolution Began*, reads Luke 16 as a chapter aimed squarely at Israel's establishment in the moment the kingdom is breaking in. The "lovers of money" who scoff at Jesus in 16:14 are the rich man of the second parable; the great reversal the Magnificat sang — the hungry filled, the rich sent empty away — is what the Lazarus story dramatizes, and it is dramatized as a warning, not a curiosity about afterlife geography. The pivot verse for Wright is 16:16: "the law and the prophets were until John; since then the good news of the kingdom of God is preached." This is one of his load-bearing periodization texts — the old era of waiting has given way to the era of fulfillment; God's kingdom is now arriving, which is precisely why how one handles money, debt, and the poor at one's gate has become the test of whether one has heard the announcement. And the parable's closing line — "neither will they be convinced if someone should rise from the dead" — Wright takes as Luke's pointed glance at those who, having Moses and the Prophets, will not be moved even by Jesus's own resurrection.

Among contemporary commentators: Joseph Fitzmyer (Anchor Bible) provides exhaustive philological analysis and remains the standard reference for the steward parable; Joel Green (NICNT) emphasizes the chapter's Lukan economic theology; John Nolland (WBC) is the most thorough on redaction; Darrell Bock (BECNT) defends a conservative evangelical reading; I. Howard Marshall (NIGTC) is the standard critical-grammatical reference; Luke Timothy Johnson (Sacra Pagina) reads the parables as Lukan literary-theological artistry; François Bovon (Hermeneia) integrates the European tradition. Richard Bauckham has written extensively on the parable's apocalyptic background. Klyne Snodgrass's *Stories with Intent* surveys both parables exhaustively. N.T. Wright's *Luke for Everyone* and his *Jesus and the Victory of God* place the parable within Jesus' kingdom announcement.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: How does the Dishonest Manager parable function ethically?** Three positions:

*Position A — Endorsed shrewdness.* Bailey, Derrett, and a stream of commentators argue that the steward is genuinely commended because his actions, on a closer cultural reading, were not dishonest — he removed his commission or the master's illegal interest. The parable then teaches *active, decisive use of resources* in light of the kingdom's irruption. Money is to be deployed, not hoarded.

*Position B — Qal va-homer (a fortiori) shrewdness.* Fitzmyer, Bock, Joel Green, and Snodgrass argue the parable does not endorse the steward's morality but his crisis-decisiveness. Jesus argues from the lesser to the greater: if a worldly man can be this clear-eyed about temporal stakes, how much more should the disciples of the kingdom be clear-eyed about eternal ones. The parable is rhetorical, not ethical-prescriptive.

*Position C — Allegorical / vocational.* Augustine and large stretches of the patristic tradition read the steward as the disciple, the debts as sins, the reductions as mercy. This reading underwrites a robust Christian theology of *almsgiving as eschatological investment* — "make friends for yourselves by means of unrighteous mammon, so that when it fails they may receive you into the eternal dwellings" (16:9) — directly informing the patristic and medieval almsgiving tradition.

*Steelmanning all three:* The endorsed-shrewdness reading rightly takes seriously the cultural specifics that make the parable cohere as a story. The qal-va-homer reading rightly recognizes Jesus' rhetorical pattern and avoids ethical contortion. The allegorical reading rightly receives 16:9 as a teaching with bite — money is in fact to be deployed for the kingdom, and almsgiving is in fact a Lukan summum bonum. *Where this leaves us:* the parable will not yield a single ethic, but the chapter's larger argument is unmistakable — money is a master that must be served against, not for, and disciples are called to a clear-eyed, decisive disposition toward wealth that mirrors but inverts the steward's worldly shrewdness.

**Tension 2: What does the Rich Man and Lazarus teach about the afterlife?** Three positions:

*Position A — Topographical reading.* The parable describes — in parable form, but with theological commitment — a real intermediate state with conscious post-mortem experience, a fixed division between the righteous and the wicked, and the impossibility of crossing the chasm. The Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and traditional Protestant tradition all read 16:23–26 as substantively informative about post-mortem reality.

*Position B — Parabolic / non-topographical.* Calvin, much of mainstream contemporary scholarship (Fitzmyer, Joel Green, Bovon), and Levine read the afterlife scene as parable furniture, not afterlife doctrine. The parable's *point* is in 16:31 — "if they do not hear Moses and the Prophets, neither will they be convinced if someone should rise from the dead" — a programmatic claim that revelation in Torah and Prophets is sufficient and that resurrection appearance does not bypass the moral demand. The afterlife geography is the story's stage, not its lesson.

*Position C — Annihilationist / universalist re-reading.* Edward Fudge, David Bentley Hart, and others re-read the parable in light of broader biblical data, arguing that the parable's *function* — to indict indifference to the suffering poor in this life — is preserved on any of several afterlife schemas, and that the parable's specific imagery cannot bear the doctrinal weight that ECT places on it.

*Steelmanning:* The topographical reading rightly registers that Jesus chose this imagery and presumably did so deliberately. The parabolic reading rightly registers that the parable's punchline is about Moses and the Prophets, not afterlife real estate. The annihilationist/universalist reading rightly registers that no parable should bear sole doctrinal weight on a question this large. *Where this leaves us:* the parable's theological intent is not primarily to inform afterlife doctrine but to indict a present-tense indifference to the Lazarus at the gate. It does, however, refuse the consoling fiction that wealth and indifference are without consequence.

**Unresolved question:** Is 16:31 — "neither will they be convinced if someone should rise from the dead" — Luke's veiled commentary on the rejection of Jesus' own resurrection by those who already had Moses and the Prophets, or is it a more general theological claim about the priority of Scripture over miracle?

## The Thread

Luke 16 looks back to 6:20–26 (the Lukan Beatitudes and Woes — "blessed are you who are poor … woe to you who are rich, for you have received your consolation"; the rich man in the parable lives the woe), back to 12:13–34 (the rich fool — another parable about wealth as security that fails), and forward to 18:18–25 (the rich ruler — wealth as the obstacle to discipleship). The chapter also reaches outward to Deuteronomy 15 and the Jubilee tradition (Leviticus 25), where the periodic remission of debts is the Torah's economic theology — the steward's reductions can be read as a parodic enactment of Torah debt-forgiveness. Forward: 19:1–10 (Zacchaeus) is the chapter's positive answer — a rich man who *does* notice the poor, whose response to Jesus is restitution and almsgiving, and whose house is told that "today salvation has come." The contrast between the unnamed rich man of Luke 16 and the named Zacchaeus of Luke 19 is one of the Travel Narrative's most pointed structural rhymes.
