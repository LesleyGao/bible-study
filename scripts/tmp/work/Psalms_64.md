# Psalm 64 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

The superscription is sparse: *lamnatzeach mizmor le-David* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד), "to the choirmaster, a Psalm of David." Unlike its Davidic neighbors (Pss 51, 52, 54, 56, 57, 59, 60, 63), Psalm 64 has no biographical heading attaching it to a Davidic episode. The Septuagint preserves the same minimal heading. The Targum, characteristically, fills in: it places the psalm in David's flight from Saul, with the conspirators of v. 5-6 read as Doeg the Edomite or the Ziphites who betrayed David's hiding place to Saul (1 Sam 22-23). Modern critical scholarship (Gunkel, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger) treats the absence of a biographical anchor as evidence that the psalm was composed for general use against unjust accusers — a courtroom-and-conspiracy template, not a fixed historical occasion.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 64 as a *Klagelied des Einzelnen* (individual lament) with strong wisdom and imprecatory features. The classification is straightforward: the psalm opens with an invocation and complaint (v. 1), develops the description of enemies (vv. 2-6), and pivots to the announcement of divine intervention (vv. 7-9) and a closing call to the righteous (v. 10). What distinguishes the psalm from a pure lament is the absence of explicit petition for the speaker's vindication — the imprecation is implicit in the description of God's reversing arrows, not voiced as a wish. Sigmund Mowinckel placed psalms of this type in the cultic context of one falsely accused, perhaps as part of a rite at the sanctuary where the accused sought divine adjudication (cf. 1 Kgs 8:31-32; the *ordeal* hypothesis). Claus Westermann's narrower category of "lament-of-the-falsely-accused" (Pss 5, 7, 17, 26, 27, 35, 64) fits the psalm well.

The dominant rhetorical figure is *reverse-warfare metaphor*: speech is weaponized. *Sword* (*cherev*, חֶרֶב), *arrow* (*chetz*, חֵץ), *ambush* (*mistarim*, מִסְתָּרִים), *snare* (*moqshim*, מוֹקְשִׁים) — the vocabulary of military violence is mapped onto verbal aggression. The reversal of v. 7 — *God shoots his arrow* (*va-yorem Elohim chetz*, וַיֹּרֵם אֱלֹהִים חֵץ) — turns the metaphor against its users. This is the *retributive boomerang* that runs throughout the Psalter (cf. Pss 7:14-16; 9:15-16; 35:7-8; 57:6): the wicked fall into the pit they dug.

Hebrew vocabulary worth flagging. *Sodh* (סוֹד, "secret counsel / inner circle / private plot") in v. 2 names the conspirators' hidden assembly — the same word used in Genesis 49:6 (Jacob's curse on Simeon and Levi: "let my soul not enter their *sodh*") and in Job 15:8 ("Have you listened in the *sodh* of God?"). The word holds a polarity between the wicked council and the divine counsel. *Tam* (תָּם, "blameless / whole / complete") in v. 4 names the righteous victim — the same word that opens the book of Job. *Yorah* (יָרָה, "to shoot / aim") is used three times: the wicked aim words (v. 3), shoot from ambush (v. 4), and God shoots his arrow back (v. 7). The lexical mirroring is the psalm's structural backbone.

Psalm 64 stands in Book II of the Psalter (Pss 42-72), the *Elohistic Psalter*, and uses *Elohim* (vv. 1, 7, 9) for most divine references. The closing v. 10 breaks through to *YHWH* (rendered "the LORD"), as Psalm 62:12 had done — a small but consistent feature of the Elohistic redaction's closing verses. The Dead Sea Scrolls (4QPsa, 11QPsa fragments) preserve Psalm 64 with no significant variants from the Masoretic Text. The Septuagint translates the difficult vv. 6-7 with its own interpretive choices — the LXX reads v. 7 as God shooting his arrow ἐξάπινα, "suddenly," a temporal reversal of the wicked's *suddenly* in v. 4.

The Ancient Near Eastern parallels are striking. Akkadian curse texts and the Egyptian Execration Texts (Middle Kingdom, 19th-18th c. BCE) deploy similar verbal-weapon metaphors: hostile speech as arrow, slander as snare. The Mesopotamian *šuilla* prayers and the Egyptian funerary "Negative Confession" both contain protests of innocence against verbal accusation. What is distinctive in Psalm 64 is the explicit theology of divine reversal — not just protection from the slanderer's words, but the slanderer's words turning against him in a divinely ordered moral physics.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Lowth's parallelism categories all surface, but the dominant pattern is *climactic intensification*. Verses 2-6 build the description of the enemies in escalating layers: the *sodh* (secret plot, v. 2), the *cherev* (sword, v. 3), the *chetz* (arrow, v. 3), the *mistarim* (ambush, v. 4), the *pit'om* (suddenly, v. 4), the *moqshim* (snares, v. 5). Each line adds a new piece of weaponry or stealth. This is parallelism as accumulation — the psalm wants the reader to feel the enemies' machinery before God's reversal arrives.

The strophic structure is bipartite, hinging on v. 7. Verses 1-6 are the *enemies' speech-violence* strophe; vv. 7-10 are the *divine reversal* strophe. The hinge is grammatical and stunning: v. 6 ends on the wicked's confidence (*amru tamnu chefes mechupas*, "they say, 'we have accomplished a diligent search'") and the gnomic line *ve-qerev ish ve-lev amoq* ("the inward mind and heart of a man are deep"). Then v. 7 opens with a single waw-consecutive: *va-yorem Elohim chetz* — "*and* God shot an arrow." The structural turn is one Hebrew letter. The wicked were just congratulating themselves on the depth of their concealment; before they finish the sentence, God's arrow is in the air.

The chiasmic structure binds the two halves with reversal. The wicked *whet their tongues like swords* and *aim bitter words like arrows* (v. 3); God *shoots his arrow* and the wicked *are wounded suddenly* (v. 7). The wicked shoot from *ambush at the blameless... suddenly* (v. 4); God strikes them *suddenly* (v. 7). Their *tongues* attack (v. 3); their own *tongues* are *turned against them* (v. 8). The reversal is exact and lexical — the same vocabulary, redirected.

Inclusio binds the psalm's frame. Verse 1's *yir'at oyev* (יִרְאַת אוֹיֵב, "dread of the enemy") gives way to v. 9's *va-yir'u kol-adam* (וַיִּירְאוּ כָּל־אָדָם, "all mankind fears"). The same root *yare* (יָרֵא, "to fear") begins as the speaker's dread of human enemies and ends as the universal fear of God's act. The displacement of fear from the human plot to the divine response is the psalm's theological motion.

The *selah* is absent, like Psalm 63 — these two adjacent psalms break with the more common Davidic pattern of mid-psalm musical breaks. Some commentators (Tate, Hossfeld-Zenger) read the absence as signaling that the psalm was meant to be heard or read as a single unbroken arc, the divine reversal arriving with no liturgical pause to soften it.

The closing v. 10 is the standard *imperative-of-praise* ending of many laments (cf. Pss 7:17, 22:25-31, 31:23-24, 35:27-28). The threefold call — *yismach... ve-chasah... ve-yithallelu* (יִשְׂמַח... וְחָסָה... וְיִתְהַלְלוּ, "let the righteous rejoice... and take refuge... and let all the upright glory") — turns the speaker's individual deliverance into a community lesson. The lament has done what laments are supposed to do: it has moved the speaker (and the assembly) from terror to praise.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

In Book II of the Psalter, Psalm 64 sits in the trust-and-lament cluster of Psalms 61-64, attributed to David, just before the great hymns of Psalms 65-68. The placement matters: the four psalms move from confident trust (62-63) into renewed lament (64) before opening into communal praise. The arc reflects the Psalter's overall theology: trust does not insulate from new attack, and lament is the appropriate response when speech-violence resumes.

**The Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 64 reads the conspirators as Doeg, Ahithophel, and the slanderers across the Davidic narrative — the long line of betrayers who used their tongues against the king. Rashi (11th c.) identifies the enemies with Saul's courtiers and follows the standard Davidic-historical reading. Ibn Ezra (12th c.) is more concerned with the difficult Hebrew of v. 6, where the wicked's self-congratulation is read variously. Radak (David Kimchi, 13th c.) develops the *retributive boomerang* theology: the wicked's words become the means of their own undoing — God does not need to manufacture punishment; he lets the slanderer's own speech recoil. The medieval *piyyut* tradition draws on the psalm's verbal-arrow imagery in penitential contexts. Rabbinic ethics around *lashon hara* (לָשׁוֹן הָרַע, "evil tongue / slander") — codified in the *Chofetz Chaim* (Rabbi Israel Meir Kagan, late 19th c.) — treat Psalm 64 as a key biblical witness to the gravity of speech-violence and to its divine countermeasure. Martin Buber's reading in *Right and Wrong* (1952) treats the psalm as one of the great biblical statements of moral physics: the structure of reality itself rebounds against the slanderer. Abraham Joshua Heschel and Jon Levenson both note the psalm's importance for understanding biblical justice as immanent in the moral order, not externally imposed.

**The Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 63 (his numbering, our 64) reads the wicked's tongues as both the literal slanderers of the church and the figurative tongues of heretics whose words wound the body of Christ. He develops the *retributive boomerang* into a theology of providence: heresy undoes itself, and the church endures by doing nothing but speaking truth. Cassiodorus (6th c.) divides the psalm into its two halves and reads the divine arrow of v. 7 as the Word of God, which both wounds the wicked and heals the righteous. Aquinas's lectures on the Psalms treat the psalm as a meditation on divine providence and the natural recoil of evil. Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* (1519-21) reads Psalm 64 as a psalm for the Reformation: the secret plots of v. 2 are the conspiracies of the papal curia, and God's arrow in v. 7 is the Gospel's piercing of the false church. Calvin's 1557 commentary cautions against the temptation to read the psalm too directly against personal enemies and instead frames it as a theology of divine government: God allows the wicked to seem to flourish in their plotting, then reverses everything in a moment.

The 19th-20th-century critical tradition — Gunkel, Mowinckel, Kraus, Westermann, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate, Wenham — has read Psalm 64 within the broader category of laments-of-the-falsely-accused and emphasized its *moral physics*: the psalm's theology is that evil contains the seed of its own undoing. Walter Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*) places it among "psalms of disorientation" that move toward "new orientation" through divine reversal. Patrick Miller's work on the imprecatory psalms treats the absence of explicit curse-language in Psalm 64 as significant: the psalm describes God's reversal rather than asking for it. Erich Zenger's *A God of Vengeance?* (1996) places Psalm 64 in his sympathetic reading of the imprecatory tradition: the psalmist refuses revenge precisely because he hands the matter to God. Beth Tanner's *NICOT* contribution (2014) underscores the psalm's social dimension — the *qahal* (assembly) of v. 9 is the witness whose ponder transforms the deliverance from private vindication to public theology. Ellen Davis's reading in *Wondrous Depth* attends to the psalm's fierce honesty about how words wound.

The New Testament does not directly cite Psalm 64, but its theology of speech-violence echoes through James 3:5-12 ("the tongue is a small member, yet it boasts of great things... a fire") and through 1 Peter 3:10 (which quotes Ps 34 but stands in the same tradition). Jesus's warnings against careless words (Mt 12:36-37), Paul's commands about corrupt speech (Eph 4:29; Col 3:8-9), and the slander-vocabulary scattered through the New Testament catalogues of vice all stand within the world Psalm 64 names. The retributive-boomerang theology surfaces in Galatians 6:7 ("whatever a man sows, that will he also reap").

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

Two tensions run through Psalm 64.

**Tension 1: speech-violence and contemporary applications.** The psalm's central image — slander as arrow, gossip as ambush, false witness as pit — has uncomfortable contemporary resonance. The social-media-era applications are obvious: anonymous accusation, viral defamation, character assassination at scale. Christian and Jewish ethicists have read Psalm 64 in three main ways. The first (the *individual ethics* reading) treats the psalm as a warning to the worshipper not to participate in *lashon hara* and as comfort to those who have been the targets of slander. The second (the *political ethics* reading) treats the psalm as a critique of unjust judicial systems, false accusation, and conspiratorial politics — a tradition that runs from Calvin's reading through modern commentators (Kraus, Zenger) who hear the *sodh* of v. 2 as a structurally political category. The third (the *moral physics* reading, developed by Buber, Brueggemann, and Zenger) treats the psalm not primarily as ethics but as descriptive theology: this is how the moral universe actually works, and the psalm is not commanding us to wait for God's arrow but reporting that the arrow flies.

The steelman of the individual-ethics reading: the psalm gives victims of slander a language of trust without revenge, and it gives potential slanderers a sobering picture of their own end. The steelman of the political-ethics reading: the *sodh* (secret plot) of v. 2 is structurally a political category — councils, conspiracies, networks of power — and to read it merely individually is to defang the psalm. The steelman of the moral-physics reading: the psalm's most striking feature is that the wicked's *own tongues are turned against them* (v. 8) — the punishment is internal to the sin, not externally imposed.

**Tension 2: imprecation and the divine warrior.** Psalm 64's imprecation is unusually restrained — there is no explicit curse, no asked-for harm, only the description of God's reversal. But the divine-warrior imagery is unmistakable: God shoots an arrow, the wicked are wounded suddenly, they are brought to ruin (vv. 7-8). The Christian tradition has wrestled with this in three positions. The *literal-historical* reading (much patristic and medieval interpretation, and contemporary divine-warrior theology — Tremper Longman III, Daniel Reid, *God Is a Warrior*, 1995) treats God's military action as a real category of biblical theology that must be honored, not allegorized. The *typological-Christological* reading (Augustine, Calvin, much Reformation interpretation) treats God's arrow as the Word that pierces the heart of the wicked unto either repentance or judgment — the divine arrow as ultimately the Cross. The *ethical-hermeneutical* reading (Westermann, Brueggemann, Zenger, Miller) treats the imprecatory imagery as the Bible's honest naming of moral outrage and the psalmist's refusal to take revenge into his own hands — the imprecation as sublimation of vengeance, not its glorification.

A fourth position — the *honest-with-violence* reading associated with Bonhoeffer (*Psalms: Prayer Book of the Bible*, 1940) and developed by Stanley Hauerwas and Ellen Davis — argues that the church's discomfort with imprecation is itself the problem: refusing to pray these psalms is refusing to face the reality of evil and the depth of victims' anger. To pray Psalm 64 is to refuse both vengeance (taking the matter into our own hands) and quietism (pretending we are not angry). The honest naming of the violence is the precondition of handing it over.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 64 is not primarily about the wicked. It is about how the speaker, terrified by speech-violence, learns to entrust the matter to God and ends in praise. The psalm refuses both the lie that the wicked will not be reckoned with and the temptation to do the reckoning ourselves. The retributive-boomerang theology is not bloodthirsty — it is, if anything, restrained. The wicked are not cursed by the psalmist; their own words become their undoing. In a culture saturated with verbal aggression, the psalm offers neither escape nor revenge but a third option: trust that the moral physics is real, hand the matter over, and rejoin the assembly.

**Closing question:** When the wicked's tongues are *turned against them* (v. 8) and the psalm makes no distinction between God's arrow and the recoil of their own speech, is the psalm telling us that justice is something God *does* to the wicked, or something that the wicked, by their words, do to themselves — and does the answer change how we should pray when we are slandered?

## The Thread

- Psalm 7:14-16 — the parallel retributive-boomerang ("he makes a pit, digging it out, and falls into the hole that he has made")
- Psalm 35 — the longer false-accusation lament, with explicit imprecation that Psalm 64 echoes more restrainedly
- James 3:5-12 — the New Testament theology of the tongue that stands in Psalm 64's tradition
- Galatians 6:7 — Paul's gnomic restatement of the moral physics ("whatever a man sows, that will he also reap")
