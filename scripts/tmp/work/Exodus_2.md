# Exodus 2: The Name That Means Drawn Out

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Exodus 2 unfolds in the delta country of the Eastern Nile, the region the Egyptians called Kemet's frontier and the Hebrew Bible calls Goshen. The political situation presumes a late Bronze Age Egypt at the apex of its imperial reach, a bureaucratic state organized around corvée labor, a ruling house that saw foreign resident populations as both a security risk and a valuable workforce. The unnamed "king of Egypt" of the opening verses is deliberately anonymous — the text refuses him the dignity of a throne name, an inversion in a culture whose pharaohs had their cartouches inscribed on everything they touched.

The dating of the events (or the composition of the narrative) remains contested. Kenneth Kitchen, working from Egyptian chronology and the construction references in Exodus 1:11 (the store-cities of Pithom and Rameses), has argued forcefully for a Ramesside context — Ramses II in the thirteenth century BC, roughly 1275–1250 BC as the likely horizon for any historical kernel. James Hoffmeier, in *Israel in Egypt* and *Ancient Israel in Sinai*, has defended the plausibility of thirteenth-century backgrounds by pointing to attestations of Semitic slaves in Egyptian household archives and to Papyrus Anastasi VI, which records Edomite shepherds crossing into the eastern Delta for water — precisely the sort of bidirectional movement Exodus presumes. Bryant Wood and a minority of evangelical scholars have defended a fifteenth-century date, reading 1 Kings 6:1 chronologically (480 years before Solomon's fourth year). The Merneptah Stele of c. 1208 BC, naming "Israel" as a defeated people in Canaan, sets a terminus ante quem for Israel's existence as an ethnic entity in the land, which makes a thirteenth-century exodus tight but possible.

The material culture of the chapter is densely Egyptian. A Hebrew mother seals a basket with bitumen and pitch — *chēmār* (חֵמָר) and *zāphet* (זָפֶת) — the same waterproofing compounds used in Egyptian boat-building and attested in the bitumen trade along the Dead Sea rift. The Rekhmire tomb at Thebes (Tomb TT100, eighteenth dynasty) depicts Semitic laborers making mudbricks under Egyptian supervision, and in its register paintings one can still see the reeds and marsh-craft of the Nile fringe. The basket itself, *tēbâ* (תֵּבָה), is a loanword — likely from Egyptian *ḏbꜢt*, a chest or coffin — and appears in the Hebrew Bible only here and in the Noah narrative. This is philologically striking and theologically charged: the only two objects called *tēbâ* in all of Hebrew scripture are vessels carrying the seed of a new humanity through water. Pharaoh's daughter bathing in the river fits the New Kingdom court practice of royal women bathing in garden pools fed by canals — Nefertari's tomb paintings show this. Egyptian princesses of the eighteenth and nineteenth dynasties did adopt foreign children; the historian Donald Redford and others have noted that Semitic names appear in court registers and that "son of the king's daughter" was a real status designation.

Authorship and source layers matter here. The Documentary Hypothesis in its classical Wellhausenian form assigned the birth narrative largely to E (the Elohist), with redactional seams visible in the shift between divine names — but the chapter is primarily pre-Priestly, the covenantal capstone at verses 23–25 bearing the marks of later P framing. Rolf Rendtorff and Erhard Blum's redactional models have eroded confidence that J and E can be cleanly disentangled here, and Konrad Schmid and Thomas Römer have argued that the Moses story and the patriarchal stories were originally independent traditions stitched together by exilic and post-exilic editors to create a continuous salvation-history. What is undisputed is that verses 23–25, with their piling of verbs — *vayyēʾānḥû*, *vayyizʿāqû*, *vattaʿal šawʿātām*, *vayyišmaʿ*, *vayyizkōr*, *vayyarʾ*, *vayyēdaʿ* — read like a theological coda reaching backward to the Abrahamic covenant in Genesis 15 and forward to the call at the bush.

Five terms carry freight the English hides. *Tēbâ* (תֵּבָה), the ark/basket, I have already noted. *Mashah* (מָשָׁה), "to draw out," provides the folk-etymology for Moses' name in verse 10 — but the name *Mōšeh* is almost certainly Egyptian in origin, the same *mes/mose* element found in Thutmose ("Thoth is born"), Ramses ("Ra is born"), and Ahmose ("the moon is born"). The Egyptian verb *msy* means "to bear, give birth." Pharaoh's daughter names the Hebrew child with an Egyptian formula stripped of its theophoric element, and the narrator rebaptizes it with a Hebrew pun. *Gēršōm* (גֵּרְשֹׁם), Moses' firstborn, etymologized "sojourner there" — *gēr šām* — encoding exile into the next generation. *Zākhar* (זָכַר), "to remember," in verse 24 is covenant vocabulary, not cognitive recall; when God "remembers" the covenant, God acts. And *nāgas* (נָגַשׂ), the word for the taskmasters' driving of the Hebrews, is the same verb used of debt collectors in Isaiah 58 — economic violence, not simply forced labor.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre-wise, Exodus 2 is a composite: birth narrative with exposed-infant motif, flight narrative, and betrothal type-scene, capped by a covenantal coda. The birth narrative (vv. 1–10) bears the closest structural resemblance to the Sargon of Akkad birth legend (cuneiform text, Neo-Assyrian library copies, but preserving a tradition at least as old as the third millennium) in which Sargon's mother places him in a reed basket sealed with bitumen, sets him on the river, and he is drawn out by a gardener and raised to kingship. Brian Lewis's *The Sargon Legend* catalogued the parallels — the reed vessel, the bitumen, the river, the adoption, the rise to rule — and the question whether the Exodus account is dependent, parallel, or subversive is genuinely open. Nahum Sarna's JPS commentary argues that the biblical writer knows the motif and inverts it: Sargon is a foundling who becomes a king; Moses is a prince who becomes a shepherd. The typical rise-narrative is undone.

The betrothal type-scene at the well (vv. 15–21) is one of Robert Alter's signature literary identifications. In *The Art of Biblical Narrative*, Alter maps the convention: a hero travels to a foreign land, meets a young woman at a well, water is drawn, she runs home to tell the family, a meal and a marriage follow. Isaac's servant meets Rebekah (Genesis 24), Jacob meets Rachel (Genesis 29), and Moses meets Zipporah. Alter argues the genre expectations illuminate the variations. Unlike Jacob, Moses is alone and in flight; unlike Isaac's servant, he is neither sent nor prayerful — he simply stands up when shepherds harass the daughters. The hero is a fugitive murderer, the well-scene is an act of solitary defense, and the marriage is a consolation rather than a patriarchal strategy. The type-scene is being bent toward something new.

Literary devices punctuate the chapter. There is inverted hierarchy throughout: Pharaoh's genocide is undone by five women — two Hebrew midwives (chapter 1), Jochebed, Miriam, and Pharaoh's daughter. In chapter 2 specifically, the mother who "sees" (*tēreʾ*) that the child is good echoes Genesis 1's refrain; Miriam stationed at a distance to "know" (*lādaʿat*) prefigures God's "knowing" in verse 25. Wordplay runs through the chapter: the reeds where Moses is hidden (*sûph*) foreshadow the *yam sûph*, the Sea of Reeds through which he will lead Israel. The verb *rāʾâ* (see) patterns obsessively: the mother sees, Miriam watches, Pharaoh's daughter sees the basket, Moses looks this way and that, God sees. To see is to act.

The coda at verses 23–25 forms an inclusio with Exodus 1:8, in which the Pharaoh who "did not know Joseph" opens the oppression. Now a different Pharaoh dies, and the God who *knows* closes the first act. Within the book's larger architecture, chapter 2 sets up the call narrative of chapter 3 — Moses must have biography before he can have vocation — and it bridges the patriarchs to the nation by naming the Abrahamic covenant as the hinge (v. 24). It closes the prelude and opens the exodus proper.

Textual variants are worth flagging. The Dead Sea Scrolls fragment 4QExodᵇ preserves portions of Exodus 1–2 and generally aligns with the Masoretic text, with minor orthographic variations. The Septuagint renders *tēbâ* with *thibē*, transliterating rather than translating — the Alexandrian translators preserved the rarity. In verse 10, the LXX adds that Pharaoh's daughter "named him Mōysēs" with slightly different etymological reasoning. The Samaritan Pentateuch and the LXX occasionally harmonize chronological details, but the substance is stable.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical arc — creation, covenant, kingdom, exile, return, hope — Exodus 2 sits at the hinge between the patriarchal promises and their national fulfillment. God's remembering in verse 24 reaches backward to Genesis 15's covenant of the pieces, in which YHWH himself walked between the severed animals and promised Abraham that his descendants would be enslaved four hundred years and come out with great possessions. Exodus 2:24–25 is that promise activating. Forward, the rescued infant prefigures every rescued remnant — the preserved seed that allows the story to continue.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Second Temple interpreters expanded Moses' birth into full hagiography. Philo's *Life of Moses* makes the infant visibly divine — his face radiated such beauty that Pharaoh's daughter knew at once he was no common child. Josephus (*Antiquities* 2.9) tells that Amram, the father, received a prophetic dream that his son would deliver Israel, and that Pharaoh's own scribes had foretold a Hebrew deliverer's birth, which prompted the infanticide decree. The *Wisdom of Solomon* 10–11 personifies Wisdom as the one who guided Moses and brought Israel through the deep. Jubilees harmonizes chronology and fixes dates.

Rabbinic readings in *Exodus Rabbah* and the Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael amplify every gap. Miriam becomes a prophetess who predicts Moses' birth while her mother was still pregnant. The water of Pharaoh's daughter's bathing becomes a cure for leprosy, by which she was drawn into the river that day. *Sotah* 11a–12b expands the midwives' heroism and the mother's resourcefulness; it has Moses refusing the breast of Egyptian wet-nurses so that his own mother could be hired back. Rashi, the eleventh-century French exegete, focuses on verbal minutiae — why *vattereʾ otô ki ṭov*, "and she saw that he was good," echoes Genesis 1 — and concludes that light filled the house at Moses' birth. Ibn Ezra applies philological rigor: *tēbâ* is Egyptian, the basket's dimensions are practical, the etymology of Moses is both Egyptian and Hebrew, and the narrator simply preserves both layers. Ramban (Nachmanides) stresses the providence of small things — that Miriam stood at a distance was itself God's doing.

Maimonides treats Moses more philosophically than narratively; in the *Guide of the Perplexed* (especially 2.35) Moses is the uniquely prophetic figure, and his biography recedes behind his function. But in the *Mishneh Torah*, *Hilchot Yesodei HaTorah*, Maimonides insists Moses' authority rested on public events, not private prodigies — so the birth narrative is acknowledged without being decorated.

Modern Jewish readings reopen the text. Martin Buber's *Moses* (1946) refuses to moralize Moses' killing of the Egyptian; for Buber, it is the first eruption of a man who cannot bear injustice, and it is precisely that character that God calls. Jon Levenson, in *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*, reads Moses' basket as one in a series of Israelite firstborn figures handed over to death and returned — a pattern that culminates in Isaac, continues in Joseph, and is remembered at every Passover. Nahum Sarna's JPS Exodus commentary is the best synthesis of philology and history. Avivah Zornberg's *Particulars of Rapture* reads Exodus 2 psychoanalytically: the child who is drawn from the water carries, she writes, a wordless memory of being let go, and this will mark every later decision Moses makes about whom to rescue.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** The patristic tradition found Christ everywhere in the chapter. Origen's *Homilies on Exodus* treat the basket as a type of the church, and Moses' being drawn from the water as a type of baptism. Augustine (*Contra Faustum* 22) defended Moses' killing of the Egyptian as a prefiguration of Christ's striking down sin — a reading that strains the text but shows the pressure Christians felt to redeem a morally ambiguous act. Ambrose was uncomfortable and softened it. Gregory of Nyssa's *Life of Moses* reads the whole of Moses' biography allegorically as the soul's ascent to God, and the Nile waters are the turbulent passions through which the soul must be borne.

Aquinas engages Moses less in allegory than in law: in the *Summa Theologica* I-II qq. 98–105 he treats the Mosaic Law as the pedagogue that brought humanity to Christ. Luther, in his lectures on Exodus (1524–27), reads the chapter as a demonstration that God raises deliverers in obscurity — a reformational theme. Calvin, in the *Harmony of the Four Last Books of Moses*, is characteristically sober: Moses' killing of the Egyptian is not simple heroism but "rash zeal" tempered by God's providence. Calvin resists hagiography; Moses is forty years old and still learning.

Critical scholarship from Wellhausen forward fragmented the text into J and E strands and treated the birth story as folkloric. Hermann Gunkel's form-critical readings identified the exposed-infant motif across cultures. Gerhard von Rad and Martin Noth placed the chapter within the Moses tradition complex and Noth in particular in his *Exodus* commentary was skeptical of historical recovery. Brevard Childs, in *The Book of Exodus* (1974), marked the decisive canonical turn — he argued the final form is what matters theologically, and he read verses 23–25 as the theological bridge between patriarchal promise and national deliverance. Terence Fretheim's *Exodus* foregrounded the maternal agency and the theology of God's "seeing and knowing." Walter Brueggemann read the chapter as subversion: a deliverance narrative built out of the small defiances of women, in which God moves toward the oppressed before anyone has uttered a theological word about God. Göran Larsson drew out its Jewish reception.

**Where the trajectories converge and diverge.** Both traditions agree the chapter is about providence in hiddenness, the preservation of the deliverer, and the activation of covenant memory. Both treat Moses' killing of the Egyptian as theologically fraught. The fundamental divergence is typological pressure: Christian readings press hard toward Christ (the basket as baptism, Moses as Christ-type, the water as gospel sacrament), while Jewish readings resist allegorical displacement and elaborate instead the halakhic and narrative particulars. The second divergence concerns the status of Moses himself: in rabbinic tradition he is *Rabbenu*, our teacher, the one through whom Torah was given; in Christian tradition he is the forerunner who must diminish before the prophet greater than Moses. Both traditions take the Abrahamic covenant memory (v. 24) as hinge, but Christians read it forward into Pauline adoption theology while Jews read it as the ongoing ground of national existence.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author (or final redactor) believe God was doing in Exodus 2? The chapter is a theology of hidden providence. God is not named as actor until verse 24 — for twenty-two verses the narrative is carried by women, by coincidences, by Moses' own rash choices and flight. Then the verbs accumulate: God heard, God remembered, God saw, God knew. The author's claim is that the seemingly human story is already the theological story. The deliverer is being formed in Pharaoh's own house by Pharaoh's own daughter, while a Hebrew mother is paid Egyptian wages to nurse her own son. The God who has been absent from the page has been working the loom.

**Tension 1: Divine agency and human initiative.** Is God the one who preserves Moses, or is it the courage of women? Is Moses' flight to Midian a failure or a formation?

- **Catholic teaching** (Catechism §§302–314 on divine providence; Aquinas, *ST* I q. 22) holds that divine providence operates through secondary causes without diminishing them. The women's courage is fully their own and fully God's instrument. Moses' killing is a free act whose consequences God weaves into providence without endorsing the act.
- **Reformed Protestant readings** (Calvin, Sproul) push harder on sovereignty: every detail is ordained, including the killing, which God uses despite its sinfulness. Calvin's language on Moses' "rash zeal" is a characteristic attempt to hold human culpability and divine use together.
- **Wesleyan/Arminian readings** stress synergism: God waits for the cry and responds. The people's groaning in verse 23 is not decorative but causal — God hears, remembers, acts. The human cry is integral to the divine economy.
- **Anabaptist readings** (John Howard Yoder's early work on the politics of Jesus, tracing back to Exodus) foreground the nonviolent resistance of the women against the violent resistance of Moses. The chapter's theological weight lies with the midwives and mothers, not with the man who kills.
- **Jewish readings** (Buber, Heschel, Zornberg) refuse the sovereignty/freedom dichotomy. Buber's "I-Thou" God encounters human agents as partners. Heschel's *pathos of God* has God suffering with Israel's suffering and moving when Israel cries.
- **Critical readings** (Fretheim) read divine agency as relational: God's power is expressed through, not against, human initiative. Moses' flight is formation, not failure.

Steelman the least intuitive: the Anabaptist reading insists that the chapter's theological center is not Moses at all, but the women whose quiet insubordination defeats the state. If the narrative is read as the redactor arranged it — chapter 1's midwives leading to chapter 2's mother and sister and princess — then Moses is a consequence of women's disobedience. This is not a devotional flourish; it is a structural claim about where the text locates agency.

**Where this leaves us:** The author seems to hold divine and human agency not as rivals but as layered — providence works precisely through the initiative of those with the least power.

**Tension 2: The morality of Moses' killing and the question of justice without authorization.** In verses 11–14, Moses sees, strikes, hides, and is exposed. Is this proto-justice or murder?

- **Catholic reading** (Aquinas *ST* II-II q. 64 on killing; Ambrose) holds that private citizens lack the authority to execute, even in defense of the oppressed. Moses' act is sinful because unauthorized, though his motive was just.
- **Reformed reading** (Calvin) allows that zeal for justice can be right even when the particular act is rash. Calvin won't endorse vigilantism, but he reads Moses' flight as formation: God removes him from premature leadership.
- **Rabbinic reading** (Exodus Rabbah, *Sotah*) often justifies Moses by noting that the Egyptian was himself about to kill the Hebrew, or had already committed adultery with the Hebrew's wife; some midrashim have Moses using the Divine Name to kill. Philo defends Moses as executing justice where the state refused.
- **Critical reading** (Brueggemann) foregrounds the class analysis: the second day's exposure comes from the Hebrew who asks "who made you a prince?" The oppressed police their own potential deliverers. Moses flees not only Pharaoh but Israel.

Steelman the least intuitive: the Catholic-Ambrosian reading that refuses to celebrate Moses' violence. The text, read on its own terms, does not praise Moses. He looks this way and that (sign of bad conscience), hides the body (sign of known wrongdoing), and flees. Scripture's own verdict is Moses' flight and God's long detour through Midian — forty years of sheep before he is ready.

**Where this leaves us:** The author does not romanticize the violence. The chapter's theology is that justice requires formation, and formation requires the wilderness.

One unresolved question: if the God of Exodus heard only when the people cried out, what happens to the suffering that is too voiceless, too numbed, too institutionally managed to cry? The text says Israel groaned and God heard. But what of the groaning that never reaches speech?

## The Thread

**Genesis 6–9 — Noah's ark.** The only other object called *tēbâ* in Hebrew scripture. Both vessels carry humanity's future through water, sealed with pitch, through divine judgment. When you get to the Sea of Reeds (Exodus 14), remember: salvation has always come through water, and the same word structures both stories.

**Genesis 15:13–16 — The covenant of the pieces.** God's promise to Abraham that his descendants would be enslaved four hundred years and come out with possessions. Exodus 2:24 is that covenant activating. The chapter is not origin; it is fulfillment in process.

**Hosea 11:1 — "Out of Egypt I called my son."** This later reading retroactively reframes Moses' biography as Israel's biography: hidden, drawn from the water, formed in exile. When you reach Matthew 2:15 and the flight into Egypt, remember that Matthew is reading Exodus 2 through Hosea.

**Acts 7:17–29 — Stephen's sermon.** Stephen's retelling of Moses' killing of the Egyptian as the tragic first attempt at deliverance, rejected by his own people, prefiguring the rejection of Christ. Luke reads Exodus 2 as the template for the rejected deliverer — the figure who must withdraw before he can return.
