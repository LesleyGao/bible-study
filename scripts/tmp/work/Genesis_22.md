# Genesis 22 — The Akedah ("The Binding")

There is no chapter in the Hebrew Bible that has been more continuously read, reread, and contested than this one. Jewish tradition calls it simply *ha-Akedah* — "the Binding." Kierkegaard called it the site of the teleological suspension of the ethical. Erich Auerbach used it to define what biblical narrative *is*. We approach it with appropriate trembling.

---

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political and cultural situation.** The narrative is set in the Middle Bronze Age (c. 2000–1800 BC), in the semi-nomadic pastoral world of the patriarchs moving between the Negev (Beersheba) and the central hill country. Abraham owns livestock, has household retainers ("young men," *ne'arim*), travels with donkeys, and relates to God at altars and high places. The scene is plausibly MB I–II.

But the text as we have it was shaped far later. Source critics traditionally assign the core of Gen 22 to **E** (the Elohist), noting that God is called *Elohim* in vv. 1–10 and only *YHWH* appears in vv. 11–18 (via the angel). Many scholars (von Rad, Westermann) see vv. 15–18 — the second angelic speech — as a later redactional addition, because the oath-blessing repeats what was already accomplished in v. 12 and uses language closer to Deuteronomistic covenant theology. The identification with "Moriah" (v. 2) and the etiological note "on the mount of the LORD it shall be provided" (v. 14) appears tendentiously linked to Jerusalem by the Chronicler (2 Chron 3:1), suggesting post-exilic editorial shaping for Temple theology.

**Child sacrifice in the ancient Near East.** This is the indispensable context. Child sacrifice, particularly of firstborn sons, is attested in:
- **Phoenician-Punic Carthage**: the *tophet* precinct has yielded urns with cremated infant remains (Lawrence Stager's excavations, 1970s–80s), matching Classical reports of *mulk* sacrifices.
- **Moabite practice**: Mesha king of Moab sacrifices his firstborn on the wall (2 Kings 3:27).
- **Canaanite precedent**: the Ugaritic texts, Philo of Byblos citing Sanchuniathon, and later Israelite polemic against passing children "through fire to Molech" (Lev 18:21; Jer 7:31).
- **Biblical ambivalence**: Exodus 22:29 ("the firstborn of your sons you shall give to me") sits uneasily beside Exodus 13:13 and Numbers 18:15, which command *redemption* of firstborn sons. Ezekiel 20:25–26 shockingly claims God gave Israel "statutes that were not good" including child sacrifice — perhaps a retrospective theological wrestling with a practice once thought legitimate.

Jon Levenson's *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son* (1993) argues that Genesis 22 operates within, not outside, a world where the firstborn son genuinely *belongs* to the deity — and the story narrates the decisive *substitution* that reframes the practice.

**Key terms:**

- **נִסָּה** *nissah* (v. 1) — "tested." The root suggests proving, trying, putting to the test of experience. Not entrapment; the verb is used of refining metal. The narrator tells us this at the outset; *Abraham does not know*.
- **הִנֵּנִי** *hinneni* (vv. 1, 7, 11) — "Here I am." Spoken three times: to God, to Isaac, to the angel. This is the prophetic word of availability (Moses at the bush, Samuel, Isaiah 6). Its threefold deployment is the narrative's structural spine.
- **יְחִידְךָ** *yeḥidekha* (v. 2) — "your only one." Not *bekhorkha* (firstborn — Ishmael exists). *Yaḥid* means unique, irreplaceable, beloved. LXX translates *agapētos* — "beloved" — the same word the Father speaks over Jesus at his baptism (Mark 1:11).
- **הָעֹלָה** *ha-olah* (v. 2) — "burnt offering." Literally "that which goes up." Totally consumed; no portion returned to offerer. Leaves no ambiguity about what is asked.
- **עָקַד** *aqad* (v. 9) — "bound." A rare verb (hapax in this form in the Hebrew Bible) that gives the whole episode its Jewish name. Its specificity — binding an animal for slaughter — marks the horror.
- **יִרְאֶה** *yir'eh* (vv. 8, 14) — "will see / will provide." The verb *ra'ah* ("to see") puns throughout: God sees, God is seen, God provides. The place-name *YHWH yir'eh* plays on vision and provision as one act.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

**Genre.** This is Hebrew narrative at its most compressed — what Auerbach in *Mimesis* (1946) famously described as "fraught with background." No interior monologue. No description of Abraham's face. No detail of Isaac's weight or the knife's gleam. Everything material, nothing psychological. The silences are the story.

**Structural features:**

- **Threefold *hinneni*** — Abraham to God (v. 1), to Isaac (v. 7), to the angel (v. 11). This is the architectonic device.
- **Repetition of "together"** (*yaḥdav*, vv. 6, 8) — "so they went both of them together." The repeated phrase, bracketing Isaac's only spoken words, is unbearable. On the return journey (v. 19) Abraham goes back alone to the young men; the word *yaḥdav* is conspicuously absent from the descent.
- **Chiastic echo with Genesis 12.** Abraham's call ("Go... to the land *that I will show you*," 12:1) and Genesis 22 ("Go... on one of the mountains *of which I shall tell you*," 22:2) are bookends. Both use the same rare construction *lekh-lekha* — "go you" (only here in Torah). The faith journey opens and closes symmetrically: leave your father's house / offer your son.
- **Threefold delayed identification** in v. 2 — "your son / your only son / whom you love / Isaac." Each phrase tightens the screw. The rabbis saw this as a *dialogue* in which Abraham attempts to narrow the demand (Bereshit Rabbah 56:7): "Which son?" "Your only son." "Each is the only one of his mother." "Whom you love." "I love them both." "Isaac."
- **The ram "caught in a thicket by his horns"** — the visual image that answers Isaac's question. *Ne'eḥaz ba-sevakh* rhymes aurally, trapping the animal as Isaac was almost trapped.

**Placement.** This is the tenth and climactic trial of Abraham (Pirkei Avot 5:3), positioned as the culmination of his narrative. Chapters 12–22 form an arc: the promise is given, obstructed, nearly lost, renewed, tested. After Genesis 22, Abraham speaks to neither God nor Isaac again in the narrative. Sarah dies in chapter 23 — and rabbinic midrash (Lev. Rabbah 20:2) reads her death as caused by hearing what happened on Moriah. The genealogy at vv. 20–24 introduces Rebekah, quietly pivoting the story toward Isaac's future.

**Textual variants.** The Samaritan Pentateuch reads "Moreh" instead of "Moriah" — aligning the mountain with Shechem rather than Jerusalem, reflecting the Samaritan-Jewish dispute over the legitimate cult site. The LXX preserves "Moriah." Also notable: the Targum Pseudo-Jonathan and 4Q225 (a Dead Sea Scroll fragment called "Pseudo-Jubilees") dramatically expand the scene, with Isaac asking to be bound tightly and Mastema (Satan) appearing as accuser — showing how the Second Temple period was already elaborating this story.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Place in the OT arc: this is the hinge of the patriarchal covenant. The promise of offspring (Gen 12:2, 15:5, 17:2) is placed in lethal jeopardy and then reaffirmed by divine oath (22:16 — *"by myself I have sworn,"* the only self-oath by God in Genesis, cited climactically in Hebrews 6:13). The covenant now rests not on promise alone but on tested fidelity.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

**Second Temple period.** The Akedah is *already* being theologized by the 2nd century BC. Jubilees 17–18 attributes the test to Prince Mastema (a Satan-figure) rather than God directly. 4 Maccabees 7, 13, 16 uses Isaac as the paradigm of willing martyrdom in the face of Antiochene persecution. Josephus (*Antiquities* 1.13.4) has Isaac, aged 25, eagerly consent. **Isaac's active participation**, virtually absent in the Hebrew text, is grafted in.

**Rabbinic period.** The Akedah becomes the theological engine of Jewish liturgy. It is read on **Rosh Hashanah**; the ram's horn (*shofar*) that inaugurates the new year is traditionally linked to the ram of Moriah. The doctrine of *zekhut avot* — the "merit of the fathers" — holds that Isaac's willingness provides ongoing atonement for Israel. Bereshit Rabbah (56) imagines ashes of Isaac on the altar (though Isaac did not die), language remarkably close to atonement theology. Some rabbinic texts (*Pirke de-Rabbi Eliezer* 31) even entertain that Isaac *did* die and was resurrected — Shalom Spiegel's classic study *The Last Trial* (1950, trans. 1967) documents how medieval Jewish poetry under Crusader massacre (the *piyyutim*) imagined fathers actually sacrificing their sons in Isaac's image, binding the horror of Rhineland 1096 to Moriah.

**Medieval.** **Rashi** (11th c.) emphasizes Abraham's love-driven obedience without psychological penetration. **Maimonides** (*Guide* III.24) reads the story as a prophetic proof: the test reveals the outer limit of what love and fear of God can achieve, vindicating prophetic experience against skeptics. **Nachmanides** (Ramban) wrestles with God's foreknowledge — "now I know" (v. 12) cannot mean new information to the omniscient; it means "I have *actualized* what was in potential."

**Modern.** **Martin Buber** reads the test as the crucible of authentic I–Thou relation. **Abraham Joshua Heschel** treats it as the high point of prophetic pathos. **Jon Levenson** (1993) powerfully recontextualizes: the story is a polemic not against all child sacrifice but an affirmation that God has *claim* to the firstborn — and the claim is satisfied by substitution. **Avivah Gottlieb Zornberg** (*The Beginning of Desire*, 1995) reads the silences psychoanalytically: Abraham and Isaac never speak again; something is sundered that language cannot repair. **Omri Boehm** (*The Binding of Isaac*, 2007) proposes a minority reading: Abraham disobeyed — he *refused*, and the angel's intervention confirmed his righteous refusal. The text, Boehm argues, has been read against itself.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

**Patristic.** Typology is immediate and total. **Melito of Sardis** (2nd c.) calls Isaac a "type of Christ." **Origen** (*Homily on Genesis* VIII) reads every detail christologically: the wood Isaac carries prefigures the cross; the three-day journey prefigures burial and resurrection; Isaac's silence prefigures Christ's. **Augustine** (*City of God* XVI.32): the ram caught in the thicket is Christ crowned with thorns. **Chrysostom** emphasizes Abraham's faith as exemplary.

**Medieval.** **Aquinas** (*ST* II-II.104.4) addresses the ethical scandal: God, as author of life, has sovereign right over life; Abraham is not a murderer because the command comes from the rightful Lord. The act is not intrinsically evil when divinely commanded — a position Kant would later violently reject.

**Reformation.** **Luther** (*Lectures on Genesis*) reads it as the deepest trial of faith — Abraham holds the promise (Isaac as seed of nations) and the command (kill Isaac) together in irresolvable contradiction, and trusts God against God. **Calvin** emphasizes that Abraham *believed in resurrection* (appealing to Heb 11:19).

**Critical/modern.** **Gerhard von Rad** (1961 commentary): the text is the severest trial imaginable, aimed at a post-exilic community wrestling with whether God can be trusted when God's own promises seem revoked. **Walter Brueggemann**: the story holds together two theological impulses — obedience to command and trust in promise — that elsewhere threaten to fly apart. **Richard Hays** and **N. T. Wright** read it within the "Abrahamic" substructure of Paul's theology (Rom 4, 8:32 — "he who did not spare his own son" deliberately echoes Gen 22:12, 16 LXX).

**Contemporary.** Readers since Robert Alter have noted Sarah's complete absence from the chapter — she has no voice here, and dies at the opening of the next; the narrator's silence about the mother is part of the story's terror. **R. W. L. Moberly** (*The Bible, Theology, and Faith*, 2000) argues the text is fundamentally about Abraham's discovery that the God who demands is the God who provides.

### Convergence and Divergence

Both traditions agree: this is a story about costly covenant fidelity, divine provision, and the *rejection* of child sacrifice through substitution.

They diverge fundamentally:
- **Who is the type?** Jewish tradition: Isaac as atoning martyr whose merit avails for Israel. Christian tradition: Isaac as shadow, Christ as substance — and crucially, *where Isaac was spared, the Son was not*.
- **What is the mountain?** Jewish: Moriah = Temple Mount, site of ongoing sacrificial system. Christian (Hebrews 10): Calvary, which renders sacrifice obsolete.
- **Role of substitution.** Jewish: the ram stands in for Isaac, and Isaac's merit stands in for Israel. Christian: the ram prefigures Christ, and Christ stands in for all.

Post-Holocaust, many Jewish thinkers (Elie Wiesel, Yeshayahu Leibowitz, Richard Rubenstein) have read the Akedah with new bitterness — where was the angel for the children of Auschwitz? The story's power now includes the scandal of its unrepeatability.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What the author(s) believed God was doing: distinguishing the true God from Canaanite-Phoenician deities who genuinely demand children; establishing that God has total covenantal claim on human life *and* that God provides the substitute; sealing the Abrahamic promise with an unbreakable divine oath; demonstrating that the covenant requires a fidelity deeper than possession of its benefits. Abraham must be willing to lose the promise in order to truly hold it.

### Tension 1 — The Ethics of the Command

Can God command an intrinsically evil act, and if so, what does this say about the relation of divine command to morality?

- **Catholic (Thomistic)**: God is Lord of life; what would be murder if done on human authority is not murder when God rightfully wills a life's return. The act's moral species is changed by its author. The CCC affirms (2570) that Abraham "conformed to the divine image." Steelmanned: Aquinas is not saying ethics is arbitrary; he is saying God's sovereignty over created life is analogically like a sovereign's rightful claim, not like a tyrant's caprice.
- **Reformed (Calvin, Barth)**: The command tests whether Abraham will trust God as God — the One who gives and can rightly reclaim. **Karl Barth** (CD II/2): this is the obedience of faith that corresponds to God's election. God's command *is* the standard, not subject to a prior ethical rule.
- **Lutheran (Luther, and more radically Kierkegaard)**: **Kierkegaard** (*Fear and Trembling*, 1843) — Abraham is the "knight of faith" who performs a "teleological suspension of the ethical." The ethical (do not kill your son) is *real* and *valid*; Abraham is genuinely a potential murderer from the standpoint of ethics. Only faith — absurd, non-communicable — makes him something else. Kierkegaard refuses to domesticate the horror.
- **Jewish (multiple)**: Maimonides — pedagogical demonstration of prophetic certainty. Levenson — the story operates *within* a real cultural expectation of firstborn sacrifice and dramatizes God's merciful substitution. Omri Boehm's minority reading — Abraham's *refusal* was the righteous act; the angel represents Abraham's own moral conscience.
- **Kantian/critical**: **Immanuel Kant** (*Conflict of the Faculties*) infamously said Abraham should have replied: "That I ought not to kill my good son is quite certain. But that you, this apparition, are God — of that I am not certain." For Kant, no voice from heaven can override the categorical moral law. This is the modern ethical objection in its sharpest form.

**Where this leaves us**: the story is genuinely morally scandalous and refuses to be smoothed. It forces the question: is there any authority that supersedes the immediate ethical? Every reader must answer — and the answers divide cultures.

### Tension 2 — Did God "Learn" Something?

"For now I know that you fear God" (v. 12). Does the omniscient God learn?

- **Classical theism (Augustine, Aquinas, Maimonides)**: God's "knowing" is anthropomorphic — the test actualizes what was already known eternally. The knowing is *ours* (and Abraham's); the revealing is temporal. Maimonides: "now I know" means "now I have made known."
- **Open theism (Pinnock, Sanders, Boyd)**: Take the text at its word. God genuinely awaited the outcome. The future of free creatures is not exhaustively known even to God. This radical view accepts the plain sense at the cost of classical omniscience.
- **Process theology (Whitehead, Hartshorne)**: God and world develop together; the test is a genuine event in God's own experience.
- **Jewish (midrashic)**: Many rabbis are comfortable with a God who, in relationship, experiences surprise without threat to divine sovereignty. The covenantal God is not the Unmoved Mover.
- **Barth**: God freely enters time to *be* the God who responds. "Now I know" is God's freedom to make the human act genuinely count before God.

Steelmanning open theism: the relentless domestication of divine language — every surprise, grief, or change of mind reread as anthropomorphism — eventually empties the biblical portrayal of God. At some point, you are reading Aristotle, not Moses.

**Where this leaves us**: the text presses a question classical theology prefers to answer philosophically and biblical narrative prefers to answer relationally. The gap between these answers is older than the Middle Ages and shows no sign of closing.

### The unresolved question

Abraham is praised because he *did not withhold* his son. The narrator tells us it was a test — but Abraham never knew that. If the voice in your head that sounds like God ever asks you for your Isaac — the thing promised, the thing beloved, the thing that makes the future possible — how do you know whether you are hearing God, or yourself, or something worse? And how do you know which answer — raising the knife, or refusing — is faith?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 12:1** — *lekh-lekha* opens Abraham's story ("Go... to the land that I will show you"); *lekh-lekha* closes it here ("Go... to one of the mountains of which I will tell you"). The call to leave his father and the call to sacrifice his son are the grammatical bookends of his life.
- **Exodus 13:13** — "Every firstborn of man among your sons you shall redeem." The Akedah inaugurates the principle: firstborn sons belong to God but are redeemed by substitute. Keep this in mind at Passover: the Exodus works by the same logic.
- **2 Chronicles 3:1** — Solomon builds the Temple on Mount Moriah. The Chronicler explicitly fuses the site of Abraham's trial with the site of Israel's altar. Every lamb sacrificed there answers Isaac's question: "Where is the lamb?"
- **Romans 8:32** — "He who did not spare his own son but gave him up for us all." Paul is deliberately echoing Gen 22:16 (LXX *ouk epheisō*). **When you get to the Gospel of John** and hear "Behold, the lamb of God" (John 1:29), remember Isaac asking "Where is the lamb?" — the Christian canon has received that question as unanswered for two thousand years until that moment.
- **Hebrews 11:17–19** — the earliest extant Christian interpretation: Abraham "considered that God was able even to raise him from the dead, from which, figuratively speaking, he did receive him back." Already in the first century, resurrection is the frame.
