# Luke 9: The Hinge — From Galilee Toward Jerusalem

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Luke 9 is the most structurally pivotal chapter in the gospel. It contains the feeding of the five thousand, Peter's confession, the first passion prediction, the Transfiguration, the boy with the unclean spirit, and — at 9:51 — the verse that swings the whole gospel onto its long road: "**when the days drew near for him to be taken up, he set his face to go to Jerusalem**." Geographically and theologically, the chapter pivots from Galilee to the Travel Narrative that will run to 19:27.

The chapter opens with the sending of the Twelve (9:1-6) and then plants us, in 9:10, at **Bethsaida** — Lukan distinctive against Mark's "desolate place" by the lake. Bethsaida ("house of fish") was the village where Andrew and Peter originated (John 1:44), elevated by **Philip the tetrarch** (Antipas's brother who governed the territories east and north of the lake) to the status of *polis* and renamed **Bethsaida-Julias** in honor of Augustus's daughter Julia, around 30 CE. The site has been the subject of archaeological dispute: the **et-Tell** excavations under **Rami Arav** (since 1987) identified what Arav argues is the Hellenistic-Roman Bethsaida, while a competing identification at **el-Araj** by **Mordechai Aviam** and **R. Steven Notley** has more recently produced first-century evidence (a Byzantine basilica plausibly built over the apostles' house). The two-site debate continues, but the basic geography — a town on the northeastern lake-shore, near the inflow of the Jordan River — is secure.

The feeding of the five thousand (9:10-17) takes place in remote country near Bethsaida. The crowd "sat down in groups of about fifty each" (9:14) — Lukan distinctive against Mark's "groups of hundreds and fifties." The numbering recalls Israel's wilderness organization (Exodus 18:21-25, where Moses appoints leaders over thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens) and Qumran's military-eschatological organization in the **War Scroll (1QM)**, which structures the eschatological community in similar tens-and-fifties. Luke is staging the feeding as a wilderness-Israel typology and an eschatological-banquet anticipation simultaneously.

Caesarea Philippi appears not by name in Luke (Mark 8:27 names it; Luke 9:18 says only that Jesus was praying) but is presupposed as the geography. **Caesarea Philippi** (modern **Banias**) was Philip the tetrarch's capital, built at the source of the Jordan at the foot of Mount Hermon, where a massive Greco-Roman shrine to **Pan** (Greek *Paneion*) had stood since the Hellenistic period. **Vassilios Tzaferis** and the Israel Antiquities Authority excavations have documented the cult-niches carved into the cliff face, an Augusteum (temple to Augustus) built by Herod the Great, and Philip's later expansion. To ask "who do you say that I am?" at this site, surrounded by pagan deity-shrines and Roman imperial monumental architecture, is to stage the question against the available Greco-Roman religious-political alternatives. Luke removes the geographical specificity, perhaps because his audience would not recognize the resonance, but the underlying scene retains its rhetorical force.

The Transfiguration (9:28-36) takes place "**on the mountain**" (***eis to oros***, εἰς τὸ ὄρος) "about eight days after these sayings" (Matthew and Mark say "after six days"). The traditional identification with **Mount Tabor** rises about 1,800 feet above the Jezreel Valley plain, with sweeping views of Galilee. The competing identification with **Mount Hermon** (9,232 feet, the dominant peak above Caesarea Philippi) better fits the geographical sequence and the high-mountain-experience imagery. Luke does not specify; the theological geography matters more than the topographic one. The two-figures-with-Jesus, Moses and Elijah, embody the **Law and the Prophets** — the canonical structure of Hebrew scripture — speaking with the one in whom both find their fulfillment. The Lukan distinctive: they speak of his "***exodos***" (ἔξοδος, 9:31), his "departure" or "exodus" he was about to accomplish at Jerusalem. This single Greek word, uniquely Lukan in this context, programs the entire Travel Narrative as a Moses-typological journey toward a new exodus through the cross.

Three Greek terms anchor the chapter. ***Exodos*** (ἔξοδος, 9:31) — already noted, the uniquely Lukan term for Jesus's departure-as-exodus. ***Analēmpsis*** (ἀνάλημψις, 9:51) — "reception, taking up," the noun for Jesus's being-taken-up; this is the only occurrence in the New Testament, and its semantic range encompasses both the resurrection-ascension and the cross-as-being-taken-up. ***Esterixen to prosōpon*** (ἐστήρισεν τὸ πρόσωπον, 9:51) — "he set / fixed his face," a Septuagintism drawn from Ezekiel 21:2 and Isaiah 50:7 (where the prophet "sets his face like flint"). Luke is composing in deliberately Septuagintal Greek for theological resonance.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Luke 9 is structured as a hinge with three registers. The first half (9:1-50) completes the Galilean ministry: sending the Twelve, Herod's perplexity, the feeding, the confession, the first passion prediction, the Transfiguration, the boy, the second passion prediction, the disputes about greatness. The second half (9:51-62) opens the Travel Narrative with the programmatic "set his face" verse, the Samaritan rejection, and the three would-be followers.

Three Lukan compositional fingerprints saturate the chapter:

First, the **prayer motif** reaches its concentrated peak. Jesus is praying when he asks the disciples about his identity (9:18, Lukan distinctive); he goes up the mountain "to pray" (9:28) and the Transfiguration occurs "as he was praying" (9:29, Lukan distinctive). Mark and Matthew place the question and the Transfiguration without prayer-frames; Luke alone makes the prayer the occasion of revelation. This is consistent with the Lukan pattern at 3:21 (baptism while praying), 6:12 (all-night prayer before choosing the Twelve), 11:1 (the disciples ask him to teach them to pray after watching him pray), 22:41-46 (Gethsemane), and 23:34, 46 (the cross-prayers).

Second, the **Lukan addition of "daily"** to the cross-bearing saying (9:23). Mark 8:34 and Matthew 16:24 have "let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me." Luke alone adds ***kath' hēmeran*** (καθ' ἡμέραν, "daily"). The addition transforms a one-time martyrological pronouncement into an everyday discipleship-ethic. **Joel Green** (NICNT) reads this as Luke's pastoral move: most disciples will not face literal martyrdom, but all face daily occasions of self-denial. **John Nolland** (WBC) and **Bock** (BECNT) treat it as Lukan redaction. **Conzelmann** in *Theology of St. Luke* connected the addition to the prolonged-time-of-the-church period in his three-stage salvation-historical scheme.

Third, the **9:51 turning point**. **Conzelmann** and **C.F. Evans** in his classic essay *The Central Section of St. Luke's Gospel* both identified 9:51-19:27 as a coherent literary unit, the Travel Narrative or Central Section. Conzelmann read it as Luke's geographical-theological structuring: Galilee (the time of revelation) → Jerusalem journey (the time of decision) → Jerusalem (the time of passion). Evans proposed a more specific Deuteronomy-typological structure: the Travel Narrative as a Lukan recapitulation of Deuteronomy, with sections in 9:51-18:14 corresponding to Deuteronomy's sequence. **David Moessner's** *Lord of the Banquet* developed the Mosaic-typology further: Jesus as the prophet-like-Moses (Deut 18:15-18) leading the new exodus. **Joel Green** treats the Evans-Moessner thesis as suggestive but not strictly demonstrated. **Fitzmyer** AB is more cautious. The basic structural fact — that 9:51 opens a long, geographically-loose journey toward Jerusalem during which Luke gathers his distinctive L material (most of the parables, much of the teaching, including the Good Samaritan, Mary and Martha, the Prodigal Son, Lazarus and the rich man, the Pharisee and the tax collector) — is uncontested.

The **uniquely Lukan exodos** at 9:31 has generated significant scholarly attention. Moses and Elijah speak of Jesus's "***exodos*** which he was about to accomplish at Jerusalem." The word's range encompasses death (used for death in 2 Peter 1:15, Wisdom 3:2, 7:6), departure-from-life, and exodus-from-Egypt-pattern. **Joel Green** reads the term comprehensively: Jesus's death-resurrection-ascension is a new exodus, with the cross as the Red Sea crossing and the resurrection-ascension as the entry into the promised land. **Fitzmyer** AB stresses the Mosaic typology: Moses, who could not enter the promised land, speaks now with the Moses-like-prophet who will accomplish the new exodus. **Bovon** (Hermeneia) catalogues the patristic developments of the exodus-typology, particularly in **Origen** and **Gregory of Nyssa**.

The **Samaritan rejection** scene (9:51-56) is uniquely Lukan and has a textual variant of theological significance. After Jesus rebukes James and John for wanting to call down fire, several manuscripts add "**you do not know what manner of spirit you are of; for the Son of Man came not to destroy people's lives but to save them**" (some witnesses). The longer reading is omitted by the major early uncials and most modern critical editions; Metzger's *Textual Commentary* judges it a later harmonizing expansion. The shorter reading retains the bare rebuke without explanation. The James-and-John request explicitly echoes **Elijah** in 2 Kings 1:9-12, who calls down fire on the soldiers of King Ahaziah; the disciples are imitating their Elijah-typological master, but Jesus rejects the Elijah-fire mode in favor of the prophet-suffering mode.

The **three would-be followers** (9:57-62) are uniquely Lukan in this constellation; Matthew has the first two with significant overlap (Matt 8:18-22). The "let me first go and bury my father" saying has been the subject of intense scholarly debate. **Kenneth Bailey** in *Through Peasant Eyes* points out that the man's father is not necessarily already dead; the phrase may mean "let me wait until my father is deceased and the inheritance is settled before I disrupt my life." Jesus's response — "let the dead bury their own dead" — works as a paradox on either reading: spiritually-dead family burying physically-dead family, contrasted with the kingdom-summoned proclamation. The "no one who puts his hand to the plow and looks back" saying (9:62) draws on the agricultural realism of straight-furrow plowing in Mediterranean fields and possibly echoes Elisha's plowing in 1 Kings 19:19-21, where Elisha is permitted to say farewell before following Elijah but Jesus does not extend the same permission.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The **Jewish interpretive trajectory** for Luke 9 is rich. **Geza Vermes** read Peter's confession ("the Christ of God") within the Jewish messianic-expectation matrix; the title is one of several available messianic categories alongside "Son of David," "Son of Man," "Prophet-like-Moses," and "Suffering Servant." **E.P. Sanders** in *Jesus and Judaism* read the Transfiguration's Moses-and-Elijah pairing as a thoroughly Jewish theological move: the convergence of Law and Prophets on a single eschatological figure was a recognizable Jewish messianic-expectation, attested in Qumran's *4QTestimonia* (4Q175) and elsewhere. **Daniel Boyarin** in *The Jewish Gospels* argues that the heavenly figures conversing with a transfigured human were a familiar Second Temple Jewish category; the Transfiguration narrative is Jewish theological-narrative, not a violation of Jewish monotheism.

The **exodus-typology** of 9:31 has deep Jewish roots. The Passover-haggadic tradition reads the exodus as the paradigmatic redemption-event, recapitulated in successive deliverances. **Pinchas Lapide** noted the rabbinic concept of *ge'ulah* (redemption) as patterned on the exodus; the Lukan claim that Jesus accomplishes a new *exodos* at Jerusalem is intelligible within this framework, however much later Christian theology develops it Christologically.

The **Samaritan rejection** scene engages the long Jewish-Samaritan rivalry. **Jonathan Bourgel's** work on the Samaritans documents the deep tensions; Samaritan rejection of Galilean Jewish pilgrims passing through Samaritan territory en route to Jerusalem was historically attested (Josephus, *Antiquities* 20.118-136, narrates the murder of Galilean pilgrims by Samaritans c. 50 CE). Luke's narrative has Jesus refusing to retaliate, choosing instead the patient way around the village — a programmatic Lukan position on inter-ethnic conflict that will be developed in the Good Samaritan parable (10:25-37) and Acts 8 (Philip's Samaritan mission).

**Levine** in *The Misunderstood Jew* and *Short Stories by Jesus* engages the would-be-followers passages with characteristic precision: the "let the dead bury their dead" saying is shocking within Jewish piety, where burial of parents was a foundational filial obligation under the Fifth Commandment. Jesus is not abolishing the obligation but radically subordinating it to the kingdom-summons; the saying works as paradox precisely because the filial obligation is so weighty.

The **Christian interpretive trajectory** is dense. **Origen's** *Homilies on Luke* read the Transfiguration as a glimpse of the divine glory revealed to those whose souls have ascended the mountain of contemplation. **Ambrose** *Expositio in Lucam* developed the cloud as the Holy Spirit overshadowing, paralleling the cloud at the Annunciation. **Cyril of Alexandria** read the Transfiguration as a definitive Christological-glory revelation that grounds his anti-Nestorian theology of the single divine-human person. **Bede** synthesized the patristic readings.

**Calvin** in his *Harmony* read the Transfiguration with characteristic Christological focus: the manifestation of glory authenticates Jesus's divine identity; the Father's voice authorizes his teaching authority. On the cross-bearing saying with Lukan "daily" addition: discipleship is a continual daily commitment, not an episodic heroic moment. On 9:51: the determination that fixes Jesus's face toward Jerusalem is the obedience-of-love that anchors the entire passion-narrative theology of Reformed atonement.

**Luther's** lectures on Mark and Matthew (with Lukan parallels) developed the Transfiguration as the cross's reverse-side: the same one who is transfigured in glory will be transfigured in suffering, and the resurrection will reveal the transfiguration's true reality.

**Bonhoeffer's** *The Cost of Discipleship* engaged the cross-bearing saying with unforgettable force: "when Christ calls a man, he bids him come and die." The Lukan "daily" addition gives this its everyday reach.

Twentieth-century: **Conzelmann** *Theology of St. Luke* placed the chapter at the structural center of his salvation-historical scheme, with 9:51 as the hinge between the time of Galilee and the time of journey-toward-Jerusalem. **C.F. Evans** *The Central Section of St. Luke's Gospel* (essay, 1955) proposed the Deuteronomy-typological reading of the Travel Narrative. **David Moessner's** *Lord of the Banquet* developed the Mosaic-typology. **Joel Green** NICNT, **Fitzmyer** AB, **Nolland** WBC, **Bock** BECNT, **Marshall** NIGTC, **Johnson** Sacra Pagina, **Bovon** Hermeneia all treat 9:51 as the structural turning point of the gospel.

**Tannehill** *Narrative Unity of Luke-Acts* reads the chapter as the gathering and recommissioning of the Twelve before the journey: the sending in 9:1-6 prepares for the fuller missionary structure of the Seventy(-two) in 10:1-12; Peter's confession authorizes the journey; the Transfiguration provides theophanic confirmation; the failure of the disciples with the boy, the dispute about greatness, and the strange exorcist together set up the disciples' continuing need for instruction that the Travel Narrative will provide.

The chapter is densely male-disciple-focused: the Twelve, Peter-James-John as inner circle, the disciples disputing about greatness — the women patrons of 8:1-3 are presumptively present but narratively offstage. **Beverly Gaventa** has worked on the Lukan use of "child" (***paidion***, παιδίον, 9:47-48) as theological category — humility and reception-of-the-other — the still-point against which the disciples' jockeying for greatness is measured.

**N.T. Wright**, in *Jesus and the Victory of God* and *Luke for Everyone*, reads Luke 9 as the chapter where Jesus's whole purpose comes into focus: he is going to Jerusalem to do for Israel — and through Israel for the world — what only Israel's God could do. The Transfiguration's word *exodos* is, for Wright, the key: Jesus is leading a new exodus, and the cross is its Red Sea. Peter's confession ("the Christ of God") is right but radically incomplete, which is why Jesus immediately reframes messiahship around suffering — the Messiah will not defeat Rome with the sword but will go to Jerusalem and absorb the worst that evil can do, taking Israel's exile (and the world's) onto himself so that it can finally end. "Setting his face" toward Jerusalem (9:51), echoing Isaiah's suffering servant who sets his face like flint, is for Wright the moment Jesus consciously takes up that vocation. The disciples' running incomprehension in this chapter — failing with the boy, arguing about greatness, wanting to call down Elijah's fire — is precisely the point: they are still imagining a kingdom of conventional power, and the long road to Jerusalem will be Jesus's attempt to re-educate them about what God's victory actually looks like.

**Mediterranean and politically-attentive readings** find Luke 9 fertile. **Justo González** *Luke (Belief)* foregrounds the Samaritan rejection and Jesus's non-retaliatory response as paradigmatic for inter-ethnic encounter. **Kenneth Bailey** *Jesus Through Middle Eastern Eyes* offers extensive cultural-context readings of the would-be-followers passages and the Samaritan rejection.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: What does the Lukan addition of "daily" to the cross-bearing saying do to the theology of discipleship (9:23)?** Three positions. The **routinizing-domesticating reading** (some critical scholarship, e.g., **Gerhard Schneider**) holds that Luke's "daily" softens the original martyrological intensity of Jesus's saying; the cross becomes a metaphor for everyday self-denial rather than literal life-loss. The Lukan church-pastoral context required a sustainable discipleship-ethic for ordinary believers, and "daily" is the redactional accommodation. Steelman: the original saying is plausibly addressed to disciples facing real martyrdom; Luke's audience, decades later in mixed-ecclesial conditions, needed a more sustainable application. The **intensifying-internalizing reading** (Bonhoeffer, much modern theological commentary) holds that "daily" radicalizes rather than softens: the cross is not a one-time heroic moment but a daily continuous reality of dying-with-Christ; the disciple's whole life is cruciform. Steelman: Bonhoeffer's "when Christ calls a man, he bids him come and die" is fully Lukan if read with "daily"; the daily reach makes the cross-summons more searching, not less. The **integrative reading** (Joel Green, Fitzmyer) holds that "daily" extends rather than replaces the original: martyrdom remains the limit-case, daily self-denial is the constant texture, and these are not alternatives but the same cruciform reality at different intensities. Steelman: the saying's structure with "deny himself / take up his cross / follow me" suggests a comprehensive discipleship-pattern, not a single moment; "daily" makes explicit what was always implicit. **Where this leaves us:** the integrative reading is most adequate to the Lukan text. Luke is not domesticating the cross but extending its reach to encompass the whole life of the disciple. The pastoral truth is that "daily" is more demanding than the heroic moment, not less, because most people can muster heroism for an hour and not for a lifetime.

**Tension Two: What does it mean that Jesus "set his face to go to Jerusalem" (9:51)?** Three positions. The **fated-necessity reading** (Conzelmann, much critical scholarship) holds that the verse expresses divine necessity (***dei***, δεῖ, the Lukan key-word for "must") in salvation-historical terms: Jesus's journey is the predestined path of the Christ. Steelman: the Lukan use of *dei* throughout the gospel (especially 9:22 — "the Son of Man *must* suffer") supports a salvation-historical-deterministic reading; Jerusalem is where prophets are killed (13:33), and Jesus's path to Jerusalem is the path to fulfillment of prophetic destiny. The **freely-chosen-obedience reading** (Calvin, much Reformed-evangelical commentary) holds that the verse expresses Jesus's free obedience to the Father's will: "he set his face" is the human-volitional decision, the active obedience that grounds the atonement. Steelman: the Septuagintal "set his face like flint" of Isaiah 50:7 is the language of the Suffering Servant's free embrace of suffering; the verse is portraying Jesus's volitional commitment, not deterministic compulsion. The **prophetic-vocational reading** (Joel Green, N.T. Wright) holds that the verse expresses Jesus's prophetic-vocational consciousness: he is the prophet-like-Moses leading the new exodus to its accomplishment, and his "setting his face" enacts the prophetic vocation of suffering-servanthood that climaxes Israel's story. Steelman: the chapter's saturation with Mosaic-typology (Transfiguration, *exodos*, fifty-and-hundred organization) supports the prophetic-vocational reading; Jesus is consciously inhabiting the Israel-prophet role at its cost. **Where this leaves us:** the prophetic-vocational reading integrates the others. Jesus's journey is divinely-necessary (the salvation-historical *dei*), freely-chosen (the volitional embrace), and prophetic-vocational (the conscious inhabiting of Israel's role) simultaneously. The reading that flattens any of the three impoverishes the verse's theological richness.

**Unresolved question:** The disciples three-times-failure in this chapter — failing to cast out the demon (9:40), arguing about who is greatest (9:46), wanting to stop the strange exorcist (9:49) — followed by James and John wanting to call down fire (9:54) — is concentrated and unsoftened. Why does Luke keep them in such persistent failure mode at the very moment Jesus is turning toward Jerusalem? The narrative-theological function is not entirely settled. Is Luke preparing us for the disciples' Passion-Week failure (the desertion, Peter's denial)? Is he showing that the Travel Narrative's teaching is necessary because the disciples have not yet understood? Is he making a theological point about the gap between the Twelve's vocation and their formation? The chapter resists tidy resolution and leaves the disciples' incomprehension as a thematic shadow over the Travel Narrative's opening.

## The Thread

Three cross-references frame this chapter's web. **Deuteronomy 18:15-18** — "the Lord your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among you, from your brothers — it is to him you shall listen" — stands behind the Father's voice at the Transfiguration ("This is my Son, my Chosen One; listen to him!"). The Mosaic-typology integrates Luke 9 with Deuteronomy's prophet-promise; **C.F. Evans** and **David Moessner** read the entire Travel Narrative as Deuteronomy-recapitulation. **2 Kings 2:1-12** — Elijah's being-taken-up to heaven in a whirlwind, with Elisha refusing to leave him — stands behind the *analēmpsis* language of 9:51 and the would-be-followers passages of 9:57-62. Jesus's journey toward being-taken-up is patterned on Elijah's; Luke is consciously evoking the Elijah-Elisha cycle that has run through the gospel since chapter 4. **Acts 1:9-11** — Jesus's ascension — completes the *analēmpsis* trajectory that 9:51 opens. The journey that begins with "the days drew near for him to be taken up" reaches its formal completion only in Acts 1, with the cross and resurrection as its decisive intermediate stages. Watch also for **Luke 19:28-44** (the entry into Jerusalem) — the journey's destination and the city's failure to recognize "the time of your visitation"; and for **Luke 24:13-35** (Emmaus) — the post-resurrection scene where the journey-pattern reappears and the disciples finally recognize the one who has accomplished his exodus at Jerusalem.
