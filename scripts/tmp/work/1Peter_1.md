# 1 Peter 1: Born Again to a Living Hope in the Diaspora

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The letter addresses Christians scattered across five Roman provinces in northern and central Anatolia: Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia (1:1). These were not arbitrary names. By the late first century, these provinces formed an arc of Roman administration in Asia Minor that had been progressively absorbed into the empire over a century — Asia in 133 BC, Bithynia-Pontus in 63 BC, Galatia in 25 BC, Cappadocia in AD 17. The region's cities were nodes of imperial cult, civic religion, and Greco-Roman household structure. Christianity here was a small, dispersed minority embedded in a robust pagan civic life and exposed to social pressure: slander, ostracism, occasional local persecution.

The classic external witness to provincial Christianity in this exact region is the correspondence between Pliny the Younger, governor of Bithynia-Pontus, and the emperor Trajan (Pliny, *Letters* 10.96-97), dated c. AD 112. Pliny describes Christians being denounced anonymously, examined under torture, executed for *contumacia* (obstinate refusal to recant), and a temple economy disrupted because so many had abandoned the sacrifices. Whether or not 1 Peter precedes Pliny by decades, the letter's pastoral logic — exile identity, slander, embodied witness — fits this Anatolian texture with uncanny precision. Mark Wilson and Stephen Mitchell, working on Anatolian provincial inscriptions and the social texture of Roman Asia Minor, have shown how the geography Peter names was knitted together by Roman roads, conventus circuits, and overlapping civic loyalties — a real postal route, not a literary flourish.

The material culture matters. The recipients live as *paroikoi* — resident aliens. The term is sociological as much as theological. John H. Elliott's *A Home for the Homeless* (1981) argued that the recipients were already legally and socially marginal — non-citizens, transient laborers, slaves, freedpersons — and the letter inscribes that status into a theological identity rather than promising escape from it. David Balch, in *Let Wives Be Submissive* (1981), pushed in the other direction: the household codes embedded in 1 Peter (especially 2:18-3:7) read as apologetic accommodation, a strategy to *reduce* social friction by demonstrating that Christians were good householders, slaves, and wives by Greco-Roman civic standards. The Balch-Elliott debate has shaped a generation of interpretation: was 1 Peter's posture defensive accommodation or resident-alien resistance? Most scholars now accept that the letter does both at once.

On authorship and dating, the field divides into three camps and the choice has interpretive consequences:

- **Authentic Petrine** (Richard Bauckham, I. Howard Marshall, Thomas Schreiner, Karen Jobes, Donald Guthrie): Simon Peter the apostle is the author, possibly through Silvanus (Silas) as amanuensis (5:12), which explains the polished Greek. The persecution language fits a pre-AD 65 Neronian setting, with 5:13's "Babylon" already functioning as code for Rome. Date: late 50s to mid-60s, before Peter's martyrdom under Nero.
- **Pseudonymous** (Paul Achtemeier in the *Hermeneia* commentary, John H. Elliott in the *Anchor Bible*, Leonhard Goppelt, Ernest Best): the polished Greek is too refined for a Galilean fisherman; the ecclesiology and theological vocabulary are developed; "Babylon" as code for Rome is most natural after AD 70 in the wake of the second temple's destruction (the analogy with the Babylonian destruction of the first temple); the social setting matches the Pliny-era diffuse pressure rather than a single Neronian episode. Date: AD 80s-90s.
- **Mediating** (Peter Davids): Petrine authority is preserved through a Petrine circle or disciples after Peter's death; the letter genuinely transmits Peter's voice but in a later stylistic dress.

Five Greek terms anchor chapter 1:

- **παρεπιδήμοις** (*parepidēmois*, "sojourners," 1:1) — those temporarily residing in a place not their own; the LXX uses it of Abraham in Genesis 23:4. The recipients are diaspora not because they have left a homeland but because their citizenship is now elsewhere.
- **διασπορά** (*diaspora*, 1:1) — the technical term for the Jewish dispersion (LXX Deuteronomy 28:25; James 1:1). Peter applies a Jewish self-understanding to a largely Gentile readership. The grammar of exile is borrowed wholesale.
- **ἀναγεννήσας** (*anagennēsas*, 1:3, "having begotten anew") — a rare verb. New birth here is by God's act through the resurrection, not initiated by the believer. The cognate appears again in 1:23.
- **ἄφθαρτον** (*aphtharton*, "imperishable," 1:4) — paired in 1:4 with *amianton* (undefiled) and *amaranton* (unfading). The triad describes an inheritance unlike anything in the Greco-Roman patrimonial economy: not subject to decay, ritual pollution, or fading glory.
- **ἀγαπήσατε** (*agapēsate*, 1:22, with the cognate noun in 1:8) — the imperatival aorist of *agapaō*; the love command is corporate and active, the response of a community already begotten anew.

For external attestation: Pliny *Ep.* 10.96-97 on Bithynian Christians; Tacitus *Annals* 15.44 on Neronian persecution; Suetonius *Nero* 16. None of these mention 1 Peter directly, but together they sketch the ambient social pressure the letter addresses.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre matters. 1 Peter is a Christian diaspora letter — formally an epistle but inflected as paraenesis (moral exhortation) for a community under pressure. Older twentieth-century scholarship (Richard Perdelwitz, Herbert Preisker, Frank Cross) read 1 Peter as a baptismal liturgy or homily, with 1:3-4:11 as a baptismal sermon and only 4:12 onward as the actual letter. Most contemporary scholars have rejected the strong liturgical hypothesis (Achtemeier, Elliott, Jobes), though the baptismal-paraenetic *tone* remains widely acknowledged: chapter 1's language of new birth, sprinkling with blood (1:2), purification (1:22), and being born again of imperishable seed (1:23) sits comfortably in catechetical terrain.

The chapter is rhetorically dense. The opening blessing (1:3-12) is one extended Greek sentence, a single piece of eulogistic syntax that piles up the salvific benefits — mercy, new birth, living hope, imperishable inheritance, guarded faith, tested genuineness, inexpressible joy, salvation of souls — before pausing for breath. This is not the Greek of a fisherman writing on a wax tablet between casts. Whether one reads that as evidence for Silvanus (Bauckham) or for pseudonymous authorship (Achtemeier), the literary register is unmistakably elevated.

The chapter's intertextuality with Israel's scriptures is dense. The triad of foreknowledge, sanctification, and obedience-with-blood-sprinkling in 1:1-2 echoes Exodus 24:3-8, where Moses sprinkles the people with blood to seal the covenant. The holiness command of 1:15-16 quotes Leviticus 11:44 and 19:2 directly: "You shall be holy, for I am holy." The grass-and-flower passage in 1:24-25 is an extended quotation from Isaiah 40:6-8 — the same text Mark and Luke use to introduce John the Baptist. Peter's catena technique — clustering scriptural quotations to drive a single argument — appears already here in nascent form and will reach full expression in chapter 2.

The Christological structure of 1:10-12 is striking. The prophets searched and inquired carefully into the sufferings and subsequent glories of Christ; the Spirit of Christ was already at work in them. Markus Bockmuehl and Richard Bauckham have argued that this constitutes one of the New Testament's earliest articulations of preexistent Christology — the same Christ who suffered in time was active in the prophets before incarnation. Peter is doing serious theological work, not merely citing proof texts.

Within the book's argument, chapter 1 establishes identity. The recipients are elect (1:1-2), reborn (1:3), guarded (1:5), tested (1:6-7), loved-without-seeing (1:8), heirs of an imperishable inheritance (1:4), purchased not with silver or gold but with the blood of Christ (1:18-19), and called to holiness (1:15-16). Chapter 2 will then articulate the *corporate* form of this identity — chosen race, royal priesthood, holy nation — and the *social posture* that follows in pagan civic order. Chapter 1 is the foundation; chapter 2 is the structure built on it.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

**Patristic.** Polycarp's *To the Philippians* (early second century) shows extensive verbal dependence on 1 Peter — quoting or alluding to it more than any other New Testament text outside the Gospels. This is the earliest external attestation that 1 Peter circulated, was authoritative, and was read in the very region the letter addresses. Origen and Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39) accept Petrine authorship without hesitation. Augustine, writing on suffering and Christian identity, returned repeatedly to the letter's pattern of suffering Christology as moral exemplar — though Augustine's treatment of 2:21 (Christ as *hypogrammon*, model) belongs more properly to chapter 2.

**Medieval.** The medieval church read 1 Peter primarily for its ethical paraenesis and for its Christology of suffering. Thomas Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* III, q. 22) integrated the letter's priestly themes into his account of Christ's priesthood; the corporate priesthood of all believers, however, received minimal medieval emphasis. Sacramental priesthood was the dominant Catholic frame.

**Reformation.** Martin Luther's *Address to the Christian Nobility* (1520) and John Calvin's *Institutes* IV.19.28 transformed the chapter's reception by way of 2:9. But chapter 1 itself entered the Reformation imagination through 1:18-19 (ransom not by silver or gold but by Christ's blood) — a passage Luther preached and exposited repeatedly in his polemics against indulgences. The *new birth* language of 1:3 and 1:23 became central to evangelical revival traditions in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries — George Whitefield, John Wesley, and Jonathan Edwards all worked the soil of 1 Peter 1:3 in their soteriology.

**Critical.** Paul Achtemeier's *Hermeneia* commentary (1996) remains the most thorough social-historical treatment. John H. Elliott's *Anchor Bible* commentary and *A Home for the Homeless* (1981) argued that the *paroikoi* language is sociological reality first, theological metaphor second. Reinhard Feldmeier (*Die Christen als Fremde*, 1992) read the sojourner identity as the letter's master metaphor, organizing every other theme — a faith that lives faithfully under empire by inhabiting its alien status rather than by seizing power, a reading that has made 1 Peter a frequent touchstone for theologies of diaspora Christianity.

### Jewish Scholarly Engagement

The diaspora identity language of 1:1 is borrowed directly from Jewish self-understanding. LXX Deuteronomy 28:25 uses *diaspora* as the curse-language for Israel's scattering; James 1:1 likewise addresses "the twelve tribes in the diaspora." 1 Peter's strategy is to invite predominantly Gentile believers to inhabit a Jewish identity grammar — to *become* diaspora, to take on the categories Israel had already lived in for centuries.

This is theologically explosive. Daniel Boyarin, Pamela Eisenbaum, and Magnus Zetterholm — major voices on the so-called parting of the ways — have argued that the late first century did not yet know a clean separation of Christianity from Judaism. 1 Peter is one of the texts that complicates any simple separation narrative. The application of Israel's titles to Gentile believers can be read as supersessionism (the church *replaces* Israel as the elect people) or as participational (Gentiles are grafted into Israel's covenant grammar without displacing Israel). Most contemporary Christian scholars — and many Jewish ones working in dialogue — favor the participational reading, though tension remains.

Rabbinic diaspora ethics offer a parallel tradition with which to think. The principle *dina d'malkhuta dina* — "the law of the kingdom is law" (Babylonian Talmud *Bava Kamma* 113a, *Nedarim* 28a) — articulates a Jewish theological accommodation to Gentile rule that has its own long, sophisticated history. Peter's paraenesis sits adjacent to this tradition and shares its core problem: how does a covenant minority live faithfully under a sovereignty not its own?

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What God is doing in chapter 1: God has, by great mercy, begotten the believers anew through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead (1:3). This new birth produces a living hope oriented toward an imperishable inheritance kept in heaven (1:4) and a tested faith more precious than gold (1:7). The believers, scattered as elect exiles across Anatolian provinces, are simultaneously guarded by God's power for a salvation ready to be revealed (1:5) and called to holiness in all conduct (1:15) — a holiness modeled on God's own. They have been ransomed not with perishable currency but with the precious blood of Christ, the unblemished lamb foreknown before the foundation of the world (1:18-20). The chapter's theological posture is thus simultaneously realized (you have been born again, you have been ransomed) and eschatological (a salvation ready to be revealed in the last time).

### Tension 1 — The "elect exiles" identity: does election ground social withdrawal or social engagement?

The opening salutation calls the recipients *eklektois parepidēmois diasporas* — elect sojourners of the diaspora (1:1). This juxtaposition has driven Christian political theology for two thousand years. If the believers are elect, they have a positive identity that distinguishes them from the surrounding pagan order. If they are sojourners, that distinction does not authorize departure from the civic order — it authorizes a particular mode of presence within it.

- **Augustinian "two cities."** Augustine's *City of God* reads the church as pilgrim community within the earthly city — engaged but never identified with imperial structures. The Catholic mainstream, especially after Vatican II's *Gaudium et Spes*, has held this engaged-but-distinct posture, with later modulations toward *aggiornamento* (engagement with the modern world) and *ressourcement* (return to patristic resources).
- **Anabaptist withdrawal.** The radical Reformation read the elect-exile identity as warrant for withdrawal from coercive political structures — Menno Simons, the Schleitheim Confession, and modern Hauerwasian "resident aliens" (Stanley Hauerwas and William Willimon's 1989 book takes its title and frame from this passage). The church is the polis; the world's politics are not the church's politics.
- **Reformed cultural engagement.** Calvinist and neo-Calvinist traditions (Abraham Kuyper, the Dutch neo-Calvinist tradition, the contemporary Reformed engagement of Tim Keller) read election as a mandate for transformation of cultural and civic life — every square inch of creation belongs to Christ.
- **Steelmanned: the Hauerwasian withdrawal.** It is easy to dismiss the withdrawal position as quietism. It deserves a fairer hearing. Hauerwas's argument is that the church's primary political act is to *be the church* — a body whose virtues, practices, and worship constitute an alternative polity that the surrounding order cannot generate. The temptation of engagement is that the church's distinctive identity gets absorbed into the values of whichever civic order it engages. The Anabaptist tradition's refusal to swear oaths, take up arms, or enter magistracies was not principally about avoiding contamination; it was about preserving a witness the world could not otherwise see. Read through Hauerwas, the elect-exile identity says: do not lose the alterity that makes the witness possible.

**Where this leaves us:** The text does not resolve the tension, and the church's history shows that overcorrection in either direction — total withdrawal or full assimilation — produces communities that fail to be either elect or exile. The wisdom of the letter may be that the two terms are meant to hold each other in tension rather than be resolved into a program.

### Tension 2 — Inherited corruptible vs. imperishable inheritance: what is the relationship between Christian hope and the goods of this world?

Chapter 1 sets up a sharp contrast: the inheritance is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading (1:4); the believers were ransomed from the futile ways inherited from their forefathers, *not* with perishable things such as silver or gold (1:18). The temptation is to read this as a flat depreciation of material reality — eternal goods are real, temporal goods are not. But the chapter resists that flattening.

- **Realized eschatology / spiritualizing reading.** A long Christian tradition reads the imperishable inheritance as a purely heavenly reality contrasted with the corruption of earthly life. Some patristic readings (especially those influenced by Platonic categories), some popular evangelical eschatology, and the older Catholic *contemptus mundi* tradition all gesture in this direction. The body, the earth, and the goods of creation are temporary scaffolding.
- **Inaugurated eschatology / new creation.** N. T. Wright's *Surprised by Hope* (2008) and the broader new-creation movement read the imperishable inheritance as the *transformed* material order — resurrection of the body, renewal of the earth, not escape from creation. The contrast is not spirit-vs-matter but corrupt-creation-vs-redeemed-creation.
- **Catholic sacramental realism.** The Catholic tradition (especially as articulated in *Lumen Gentium* and the sacramental theology of Joseph Ratzinger / Benedict XVI) holds that material reality already participates in the eternal through the sacramental economy. The inheritance is not future-only; it is partially present in the church's sacraments.
- **Jewish reading.** Jewish eschatology, especially in its rabbinic and post-rabbinic forms, has generally resisted the spirit-matter dualism. The age to come (*olam ha-ba*) is bodily, communal, and earthly — not an evacuation of creation but its rectification. Pamela Eisenbaum and other Jewish scholars working in Christian-Jewish dialogue have noted that the resurrection-of-the-body strand in 1 Peter aligns more closely with Jewish than with later Christian Platonized eschatology.
- **Steelmanned: the spiritualizing reading.** The contemporary push to read every inheritance text as new-creation can flatten what the chapter actually says. 1 Peter does insist on a contrast — the inheritance is *kept in heaven for you* (1:4), and the believers' present circumstances are explicitly grievous (1:6). The hope is not yet visible. The spiritualizing tradition may have erred in *direction* but it was responding to something genuinely in the text: the inheritance cannot be reduced to a renewed earthly life that the believers can already see and hold. There is a real eschatological reserve here that the new-creation reading sometimes underplays.

**Where this leaves us:** The most defensible reading holds the imperishable inheritance as both genuinely future (kept in heaven, ready to be revealed in the last time) and genuinely material (resurrection of the body, not gnostic escape). The chapter's hope is neither a flight from creation nor a politics of the already-now. It sits at the joint where present suffering and future glory meet — a joint the letter refuses to dissolve.

### Closing question

If you have been begotten anew to a living hope through the resurrection — and if the inheritance is genuinely already kept for you, and you are genuinely already guarded for it — then what name does the chapter give to the part of you that is *grieved by various trials* (1:6) and waits, sometimes badly, for a salvation it cannot yet see?

## The Thread

- **James 1:1** — the parallel diaspora opening; James addresses "the twelve tribes in the diaspora," using the same Jewish identity grammar Peter applies to a wider audience. The two letters together establish diaspora as a New Testament ecclesial category, not merely a Jewish geographic one.
- **Hebrews 11** — the cloud of witnesses living by faith for an unseen inheritance. The faith-not-seeing pattern of 1 Peter 1:8 ("though you have not seen him, you love him") is structurally identical to Hebrews 11's roll call. Both texts theologize the gap between present and future.
- **Leviticus 11:44 and 19:2** — the holiness command Peter cites directly in 1:16 ("be holy, for I am holy"). Peter's move is to apply Israel's cultic holiness command to the moral conduct of Gentile believers in the diaspora, transposing temple categories into ethical ones.
- **Isaiah 40:6-8** — the grass that withers, the flower that fades, the word that abides forever. Peter quotes this in 1:24-25 and identifies the abiding word with the gospel preached to the recipients. The Isaianic comfort to Israel in Babylonian exile is reread as the comfort to Christians in the Anatolian diaspora.
