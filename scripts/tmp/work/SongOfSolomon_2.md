# Song of Solomon 2 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 2 plunges directly into the most lyrical landscape in the Hebrew Bible. The opening line, *ani chavatzelet ha-Sharon, shoshanat ha-amaqim* (אֲנִי חֲבַצֶּלֶת הַשָּׁרוֹן, שׁוֹשַׁנַּת הָעֲמָקִים) — "I am a rose of Sharon, a lily of the valleys" — has been embroidered, hymn-set, and tattooed for centuries. The botany itself is uncertain. *Chavatzelet* is rendered "rose" by the King James tradition but is more likely a crocus, a meadow saffron, or an asphodel — there were no true roses in Iron Age Palestine. *Shoshannah* is the lily, possibly the white Madonna lily or, on Othmar Keel's reading, the lotus (a cross-cultural Egyptian-influenced reading). Sharon is the coastal plain north of Jaffa, famously fertile. The two flowers are not exotic prizes but ordinary wildflowers — the woman is comparing herself to common, abundant beauty, not rarefied luxury.

The Hebrew construction also raises a long-debated question: who speaks 2:1? The verse is in the first person without a clear introduction, and English translations have long assigned it to the woman — but a strong line of interpreters from the rabbis through the Church Fathers has read it (or read part of it) as the man's praise of her. The ESV and most modern translations assign it to her, especially since 2:2 is clearly the man's response: *like a lily among brambles, so is my love among the young women.* The man picks up her self-description and turns the comparison back on her — *if you are a lily, then every other young woman is a thornbush by comparison.*

The chapter's setting is overwhelmingly **springtime**. *Ki hineh ha-stav avar, ha-geshem chalaf halach lo* (כִּי הִנֵּה הַסְּתָו עָבָר, הַגֶּשֶׁם חָלַף הָלַךְ לוֹ) — "for behold, the winter is past; the rain is over and gone" (2:11). In the Levantine climate, the rains run from October through March; April brings flowers, fig trees putting forth their early fruit (the *paggim*), the return of migratory birds. *The voice of the turtledove* (2:12) — the *tor*, a migratory species — is the soundtrack of Mediterranean spring. *Et ha-zamir higi'a* (עֵת הַזָּמִיר הִגִּיעַ) — "the time of singing has come," or, on a competing translation, "the time of pruning has come" (the Hebrew root *zmr* covers both). Ariel and Chana Bloch argue for "pruning" on agricultural grounds; most commentators retain "singing" because of the parallelism with the turtledove.

The animals of chapter 2 — the **gazelle** (*tzvi*) and the **young stag** (*ofer ha-ayalim*) — are the recurring totems of the male beloved (also at 2:9, 2:17, 8:14). In the iconography of the ancient Near East, particularly on Levantine seals and ivories studied by Keel, the gazelle is associated with male beauty, swiftness, and fertility. The same animals appear in 2:7 in the **first adjuration refrain**, sworn "by the gazelles or the does of the field" — pointed scholars have noted that the Hebrew *tzva'ot u-vy-aylot ha-sadeh* (צְבָאוֹת אוֹ בְּאַיְלוֹת הַשָּׂדֶה) sounds remarkably close to *YHWH tzva'ot* (the LORD of hosts) and *El Shaddai*, possibly a euphemistic substitute that allows the woman to swear a near-sacred oath without invoking the divine name in a love poem.

The "little foxes that spoil the vineyards" (2:15) reference a real agricultural pest. The "banqueting house" of 2:4 is literally *beit ha-yayin* (בֵּית הַיַּיִן), "house of wine," and the man's "banner" (*diglo*) over her is a military image — the lover plants his standard over her like a victorious king claiming territory. Pope and Murphy note the martial register of the image; the Bloch translation retains it; some readings soften *diglo* etymologically to "his look" or "his glance."

## Lens 2 — Literature

Chapter 2 contains the **first of the three formal *adjuration* refrains** that structure the whole book (the others fall at 3:5 and 8:4). *Hishba'ti etchem benot Yerushalayim... im ta'iru ve-im te'oreru et ha-ahavah ad shetechpatz* (הִשְׁבַּעְתִּי אֶתְכֶם בְּנוֹת יְרוּשָׁלָיִם... אַל־תָּעִירוּ וְאַל־תְּעוֹרְרוּ אֶת הָאַהֲבָה עַד שֶׁתֶּחְפָּץ) — "I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem... that you not stir up or awaken love until it pleases." The Hebrew uses the verb *'ur* (to wake, to rouse) doubled (*ta'iru, te'oreru*) for emphasis: do not wake, do not arouse. The phrase *ad shetechpatz* — "until it pleases" or "until it is willing" — uses a feminine verb whose subject is grammatically the *ahavah* (love) itself. Love has its own pleasure, its own readiness; it cannot be forced.

Roland Murphy and most recent commentators read the three refrains as the structural spine of the Song, dividing the book into three roughly equal sections. The refrain functions like a chorus, returning the reader from each ecstatic episode back to a single sober counsel: love has its own time. Whether the addressees — *benot Yerushalayim*, the daughters of Jerusalem — are real young women, a literary chorus, or a stand-in for the reader is debated. They function rhetorically as confidantes, witnesses, and audience; the woman speaks both *to* them and *through* them to anyone listening.

The chapter's central scene (2:8–14) is one of the Song's most narratively coherent passages — what Robert Alter calls a "small dramatic vignette." The woman hears her beloved coming, sees him bound across the hills, watches him peer through the lattice, and quotes his speech. The poetic perspective is hers throughout; he never speaks except through her quotation. This is *her* voice telling the story of his arrival.

The man's invitation to "arise, my love, my beautiful one, and come away" (2:10, repeated 2:13 with the formal Hebrew *qumi lach ra'yati yafati u-l-chi-lach*) is one of the Song's lyrical pinnacles. The Hebrew *lach* and *l-chi* (feminine imperative + ethical dative) cannot be reproduced in English: "arise *for yourself*, my love, and come *for yourself*." It is an invitation that returns her agency to her even as he calls her out.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Chapter 2 has been one of the most allegorically generative passages in the Song's two-thousand-year reception. The Targum *Shir HaShirim* reads 2:8–14 as the moment of the Exodus: the beloved is Yahweh, leaping over the mountains of Egypt, gazing through the lattice (the "lattice" of the angelic protectors), calling Israel out of slavery into the springtime of Sinai. The "winter is past" becomes the end of the Egyptian bondage; the "voice of the turtledove" becomes the voice of Moses. *Midrash Shir HaShirim Rabbah* extends this throughout the chapter. Rashi codifies the reading: the chapter is the love-call of Yahweh to Israel at the Exodus, a call still echoing through every Passover.

This is one of the reasons the Song became one of the **Five Megillot** assigned to **Passover** in the Jewish liturgical calendar. The springtime imagery, the call to "come away," the bridegroom's leap across the mountains — all read forward as the great covenantal romance born at the Exodus and renewed each year at the seder. The Passover assignment is not incidental ornamentation; it is the canonical and liturgical home of the Song in Jewish life.

In Christian reception, chapter 2 became a central text for the *Christ-soul* and *Christ-Church* mystical readings. Origen treats the beloved who comes leaping over the mountains as the incarnate Christ overcoming the obstacles between heaven and the human soul. Bernard of Clairvaux returns to chapter 2 across multiple sermons (the famous Sermon 35 onward), reading 2:1 (*flos campi et lilium convallium*, the Vulgate's "flower of the field and lily of the valley") as Christ's self-description and the soul's response. The Vulgate's translation choice — placing 2:1 partly in Christ's mouth — shaped the medieval Christian reception, and the phrase "rose of Sharon" became a common Christological title in medieval and early modern English (the Anglican hymnody is full of it). Bernard reads the "winter is past" as the death and resurrection — the long winter of sin ended, the springtime of redemption arrived. The chapter has been a Easter/Eastertide text in Christian devotional practice for over a millennium.

The Catholic mystical tradition — Teresa of Avila, John of the Cross — read 2:5 (*sustain me with raisins, refresh me with apples, for I am sick with love*) and 2:6 (*his left hand is under my head, and his right hand embraces me*) as the most precise scriptural images of contemplative union. John of the Cross's *Spiritual Canticle* has stanzas that closely shadow the chapter. The phrase "wounded by love" (the Vulgate reading of *amore langueo* in 2:5) became a technical term in mystical theology — the soul wounded by the love of God.

The modern Christian reception of chapter 2 has been more troubled. The **adjuration refrain** of 2:7 has been domesticated, sometimes severely, into a piece of dating-and-courtship advice: *do not awaken love before its time.* In purity-culture circles since the 1990s, the verse became a slogan against premarital sexual contact, often pulled from its context in a frankly erotic poem and used in ways the text does not authorize. Murphy, Longman, and Hess all observe that the verse, in context, is the woman's own counsel — it is the *woman* who speaks the refrain, addressed to the daughters of Jerusalem, so it reads more naturally as her self-aware wisdom about the nature of love than as a restraint imposed on her from outside.

The refrain has also been received in more measured ways. Ellen Davis reads it as a wisdom-text about the *ethics of desire* — love is real, dangerous, and not to be aroused frivolously. Pope John Paul II's *Theology of the Body* lectures dwell on the refrain as an articulation of the *integrity* of love, the recognition that desire is not a switch to be flipped but a movement of the whole person. Christopher West has popularized this reading.

The "little foxes that spoil the vineyards" (2:15) became, in monastic reception, an image of the small temptations and habits that destroy the soul's love for God. Bernard preaches at length on the verse; the medieval Cistercian tradition turned it into a standard topic.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

The chapter's central theological tension is the **adjuration refrain**. It is the Song's own internal commentary, its own moral compass — and it has been read in radically different directions.

**Position 1 (wisdom about desire):** The refrain is the woman's recognition that love has its own time, its own readiness. To force it is to spoil it. This is wisdom-literature in the love-poem mode: love is good, but it is not a possession to be seized — it is a gift to be received in its season. Ellen Davis, Robert Alter, and Roland Murphy all converge here. On this reading, the refrain is the Song's most adult moment.

**Position 2 (ethical restraint):** The refrain is a counsel of premarital chastity, a brake on the eroticism of the surrounding poetry. This is the dominant reading in conservative Christian reception, especially in evangelical purity culture. The reading takes the refrain as Solomon's (or the wise woman's) authoritative voice over and against the more impulsive moments. Tremper Longman gives a chastened version of this reading; popular evangelical works often give an unchastened one.

**Position 3 (allegorical patience):** The refrain is the soul's counsel against grasping at God prematurely, against forcing the spiritual marriage. The Christ-soul mystical tradition — Bernard, John of the Cross, Teresa — reads the refrain as the contemplative's wisdom: do not stir up the union before God himself wills it. The grace of mystical love cannot be coerced.

**Position 4 (covenant-historical):** In the rabbinic Targum/Rashi tradition, the refrain is Israel's counsel to the nations or to itself: do not force the messianic age, do not stir the covenantal love before its appointed time. Read this way, the refrain is eschatological — patience for the coming day of redemption.

The honest reckoning is that the same refrain has been used to liberate and to constrain, to celebrate desire and to police it. The mainstream commentary tradition — Pope, Murphy, Longman, Hess — insists that any reading must begin with whose voice is speaking, and it is the *woman's* voice, addressed to other women. The refrain is her own wisdom about her own desire, not a restraint imposed on her from outside. Once that grammatical fact is honored, the various theological extensions become more credible.

The chapter's other theological tension is the **springtime/creation imagery** and its interpretive valence. The literal reading: the natural world's renewal is the proper backdrop for love. The Jewish reading: the Exodus springtime, the deliverance from winter-bondage, read each Passover. The Christian reading: the resurrection springtime, the death of winter, the rising of life from the cleft of the rock — Bernard's Easter reading, retained in Christian liturgical practice. The both/and reading insists these are not in competition: the Song's literal celebration of springtime love opens, by the Bible's own typological logic, onto Exodus and resurrection. The natural and the redemptive springtimes are layered, not alternate.

A subtler tension emerges in 2:16, the famous *dodi li va-ani lo* (דּוֹדִי לִי וַאֲנִי לוֹ) — "my beloved is mine, and I am his." This formula of mutual possession has been read as the Song's most concentrated theological statement. Mark Boda calls it the chapter's covenant-formula echo: it parallels the Hebrew Bible's covenantal statement *I will be your God and you will be my people* (Lev. 26:12; Jer. 7:23; Ezek. 36:28). Whether deliberately or not, the woman's love-formula in 2:16 mirrors the covenant-formula of the Torah and the Prophets — and this is one of the textual hooks that allowed the Yahweh-Israel allegory to take root.

**Where this leaves us:** Chapter 2 is the Song at its most lyrical and at its most ethically pointed. The springtime, the gazelle, the lover at the lattice — these are images that can be read as natural beauty, as Exodus, as resurrection, as mystical union. The adjuration refrain insists that love, however celebrated, is not a thing to be hurried. The chapter's gift is the joining of unrestrained celebration with unhurried wisdom — desire and patience held together in a single voice.

**Closing question:** If the woman's wisdom is *do not awaken love until it is willing*, what does our culture (or our religious community) tend to wake too early — and what do we tend to leave sleeping past its time?
