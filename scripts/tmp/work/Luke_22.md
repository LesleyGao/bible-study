# Luke 22: The Last Supper, Gethsemane, and the Hour of Darkness

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens at the convergence of two festivals that the first-century world had already begun to fuse: the Feast of Unleavened Bread (*Hag HaMatzot*) and Passover (*Pesach*). Strictly speaking, Passover was a single day (14 Nisan), with the lamb slaughtered in the temple courts in the late afternoon and eaten that evening; Unleavened Bread followed for seven days (15–21 Nisan). By the late Second Temple period, popular usage collapsed the two into a single eight-day complex (Josephus, *Antiquities* 14.21; *War* 5.99). The passover lambs were slaughtered at the temple between roughly 3 and 5 p.m. on 14 Nisan; **Josephus** estimated 256,500 lambs sacrificed in 65 CE (*War* 6.423–425) — almost certainly an exaggeration, but suggesting the scale of the festival in pilgrim Jerusalem, which swelled from a normal population of perhaps 30,000–50,000 to several hundred thousand.

The "large upper room furnished" (22:12) — the *anagaion* — was almost certainly on the second floor of a Jerusalem house. The traditional site, the *Cenaculum* on Mount Zion, has been venerated since at least the fourth century; the current Crusader-era structure (above what may be the much-disputed "Tomb of David") sits on Byzantine remains. Bargil Pixner's controversial proposal that an "Essene Quarter" lay on Mount Zion's southwestern slope and that Jesus arranged the meal there has not won broad scholarly acceptance, though the man-with-a-water-jar (typically women's work) has been read as a covert recognition signal among an Essene-adjacent community.

The high priest's house (22:54) is most plausibly identified with the so-called **Palace of Caiaphas** on the southwestern hill, just south of today's Zion Gate. The current Church of St. Peter in Gallicantu (built 1931) sits over Byzantine and earlier remains including a courtyard, dungeon-like cisterns, and stairs leading down from the upper city — possibly the very Roman-period steps Jesus descended after the night interrogation. The 1990 discovery of the **Caiaphas ossuary** in Peace Forest (the limestone bone-box inscribed "Yehosef bar Qayafa," widely accepted as belonging to the high priest of the Gospels) gives a haunting concreteness to the figure who presided over the night assembly.

The Mount of Olives — *Har HaZeitim* in Hebrew, *Tour Zaita* in Aramaic — rises directly east of the temple platform across the Kidron Valley. The traditional Gethsemane site (from Aramaic *gat-shmanim*, "oil press") at the foot of the mount preserves ancient olive trees: radiocarbon and dendrochronological work published by the National Research Council of Italy in 2012 found three of the eight major trees datable to 1092, 1166, and 1198 CE — Crusader-era plantings, but with shared genetic material suggesting they were propagated from a single ancestral root system that may stretch back much further. An olive tree's root system can persist for millennia even when the trunk is replaced. The Church of All Nations stands on Byzantine and Crusader remains.

Greek terms central to the chapter: ***paradidōmi*** (παραδίδωμι, "hand over / betray") appears nine times in this chapter alone — Lukan programmatic. ***Diathēkē*** (διαθήκη, "covenant") in 22:20, the technical term for "testament" in Greco-Roman law and for "covenant" in the LXX. ***Anamnēsis*** (ἀνάμνησις, "remembrance") in 22:19 — a thicker term than English "remembrance," carrying overtones of the actualizing memorial that brings past event into present efficacy (as the Passover Haggadah says, "in every generation a person must regard himself as if he came forth out of Egypt"). ***Diakonōn*** (διακονῶν, "the one who serves") in 22:27. ***Genomenos en agōnia*** (γενόμενος ἐν ἀγωνίᾳ, "being in agony") in the disputed 22:44. ***Hē hōra hymōn kai hē exousia tou skotous*** (ἡ ὥρα ὑμῶν καὶ ἡ ἐξουσία τοῦ σκότους, "your hour and the power of darkness") in 22:53.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Luke 22 contains some of the densest concentration of Lukan distinctive material in the entire Gospel, including two of the most consequential textual variants in the New Testament.

**The Last Supper words (22:14–20).** Luke's account is unique among the Synoptics in having the cup come *before* the bread (22:17–18), then bread (22:19), then a *second* cup (22:20). This double-cup arrangement has generated the most famous textual problem in the Lukan passion: the so-called **shorter and longer readings**. The "longer reading" (the text of NA28 and most modern translations) includes 22:19b–20 — "which is given for you. Do this in remembrance of me. And likewise the cup after they had eaten, saying, 'This cup that is poured out for you is the new covenant in my blood.'" The "shorter reading," found in **Codex Bezae (D)**, Old Latin, and a handful of other Western witnesses, omits these words, leaving only the first cup-then-bread sequence. **Bart Ehrman** (*The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture*, 1993) argued the shorter reading is original and the longer is a scribal harmonization toward 1 Corinthians 11:23–25. **Bruce Metzger** (*Textual Commentary*, 2nd ed.) defended the longer reading and explained the shorter as a Western non-interpolation produced by scribal confusion over the two cups. **Joel Green** (NICNT), **Joseph Fitzmyer** (Anchor Bible), and the broad weight of modern textual criticism affirm the longer reading. The theological stakes are real: the longer reading explicitly grounds the eucharistic words in Pauline and proto-Christian sacrificial-covenantal theology; the shorter reading would leave Luke with a more eschatological, less explicitly atoning Last Supper. The ESV and NA28 print the longer reading.

The phrase "**Do this in remembrance of me**" (***touto poieite eis tēn emēn anamnēsin***, 22:19) is a Lukan distinctive (also Pauline at 1 Cor 11:24–25, but absent from Mark and Matthew). It has carried enormous theological weight: Catholic memorial-sacrifice theology (the Mass as ***anamnēsis*** that re-presents Calvary, developed by **Aquinas** in *Summa Theologiae* III q. 73–83), Lutheran Real Presence (Christ's body and blood truly given "in, with, and under" the elements), Reformed memorialism (Zwingli's symbolic reading vs. **Calvin**'s pneumatic-spiritual real presence in *Institutes* IV.17). The Greek ***anamnēsis*** is closer to the Catholic-Lutheran end of this spectrum than to a bare-symbolic reading: it denotes liturgical actualization, not mere mental recall.

**The dispute about greatness at the supper (22:24–30)** is uniquely Lukan in its placement. Mark 10:35–45 and Matthew 20:20–28 place a similar dispute earlier, on the journey to Jerusalem. Luke's placement — at the supper itself, immediately after the words about the betrayer — is jarring. The disciples argue about thrones precisely as the body is being given for them. The Lukan composition is theologically deliberate: the kingdom is here being inaugurated through service, not enthronement.

**The prayer for Peter (22:31–34)** is uniquely Lukan: "Simon, Simon, behold, Satan demanded to have you, that he might sift you like wheat, but I have prayed for you that your faith may not fail; and when you have turned again, strengthen your brothers." This Petrine commissioning — distinct from the Matthean confession at Caesarea Philippi (Matt 16:17–19) and the Johannine restoration (John 21:15–19) — is programmatic for Luke-Acts: Peter's failure is foreseen, his restoration is prayed for, and his future ministry of strengthening is anticipated (cf. Acts 1–12). **Joel Green** and **Robert Tannehill** treat this passage as the hinge of Lukan Petrine theology.

**The two swords (22:35–38)** is uniquely Lukan and has resisted easy interpretation. "Let the one who has no sword sell his cloak and buy one... 'Look, Lord, here are two swords.' And he said to them, 'It is enough'" (***hikanon estin***). **John Howard Yoder** (*The Politics of Jesus*) reads ***hikanon estin*** as Jesus's exasperated dismissal — "enough of this!" — given that two swords would be obviously insufficient for any actual armed resistance. **Joel Green** and **Bock** broadly concur that the saying is ironic; the disciples have misunderstood. **Bonhoeffer** in *Discipleship* read it as Jesus indicating the eschatological hour in which the disciples would be treated as transgressors (citing Isa 53:12). The just-war tradition has occasionally tried to read it as legitimating armed Christian self-defense, but this reading struggles with 22:51 ("No more of this!") just hours later.

**Gethsemane and the disputed 22:43–44.** The verses describing the angel strengthening Jesus and the sweat-like-blood are textually disputed. They are **omitted from P75 (early third century), Vaticanus, the original hand of Sinaiticus, Codex Alexandrinus**, and important minuscules; they are **included in Codex Bezae, the corrector of Sinaiticus, the majority of later manuscripts, and most patristic citations** (notably **Justin Martyr**, *Dialogue with Trypho* 103). **Metzger** in his *Textual Commentary* gave the omission a "C" rating but bracketed the verses as showing strong external attestation in early patristic sources. **Bart Ehrman** has argued the verses were a second-century anti-docetic addition to underscore Jesus's full humanity. **Claire Clivaz** has more recently argued for their originality on stylistic and theological grounds. The medical reality of *hematidrosis* — the rare condition of bloody sweat under extreme stress, well-documented in modern clinical literature — has been raised as evidence that the description has experiential plausibility, but this argues neither for nor against textual originality. The ESV prints the verses with brackets noting the textual question.

**Peter's denials (22:54–62)** include the haunting Lukan distinctive: "**the Lord turned and looked at Peter**" (22:61). Mark 14:72 and Matthew 26:75 lack this gaze; Luke alone records it. The detail has been engaged in Christian devotional tradition from the patristic period (Ambrose's *Exposition of Luke* 10.88) through Bach's St. Matthew Passion (which imports Luke's gaze into Matthean material) to contemporary visual art.

**The healing of the servant's ear (22:51)** is uniquely Lukan among the Synoptics (John 18:10 names the servant Malchus but lacks the healing). It is Luke's last reported miracle, performed in the act of arrest.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Last Supper participates in the deep typology of the Passover. The lamb sacrificed at the temple in the afternoon (Exodus 12) becomes the body broken at the table that evening; the blood smeared on the doorposts to spare Israel from the death of the firstborn becomes the cup of the new covenant "poured out for you." **Joachim Jeremias**'s *The Eucharistic Words of Jesus* (1949, 4th ed. 1967) remains the foundational study of the Aramaic substratum and Passover background. **Brant Pitre**'s more recent *Jesus and the Last Supper* (2015) develops the Mosaic and Wisdom typologies. The **new covenant** language (22:20) cites Jeremiah 31:31–34 — the prophetic promise of an internalized covenant with sin forgiven — and the Lukan Jesus's invocation of this oracle at the table is the most explicit canonical-covenantal claim in the passion narrative.

The Suffering Servant typology runs through the chapter. "**He was numbered with the transgressors**" (22:37) cites Isaiah 53:12 directly. **Morna Hooker** in *Jesus and the Servant* (1959) classically argued against an over-reading of Isaiah 53 in Jesus's self-understanding; **Otfried Hofius** and **N.T. Wright** have pressed back, arguing the Isaian background is foundational at least for Luke and Paul. The Lukan citation here is unmistakable.

Patristic readings worked the chapter at every point. **Origen**'s *Commentary on Matthew* and his fragments on Luke read Gethsemane as the *real* battle — the cross is its consummation, but the will-of-the-Father is wrestled with at the olive press. **Ambrose** on Luke 22 sees the angel's strengthening as proof of Christ's full humanity (against incipient docetism). **Cyril of Alexandria**'s *Commentary on Luke* (Homilies 141–146) reads the Petrine prayer (22:31–32) as the foundation of papal stability and apostolic continuity. **John Chrysostom** preached extensively on Peter's denial as a comfort to penitents. **Augustine** (*Tractates on John* 116; *Sermon* 91) wrestled with the relationship between Jesus's prayer ("remove this cup") and his obedience ("nevertheless not my will"). **Aquinas** (*Catena Aurea* on Luke 22; *Summa Theologiae* III q. 18) systematized the medieval consensus on the two wills of Christ — the human will recoiling from death, the divine will and the deeper human will assenting to the Father.

**Hans Urs von Balthasar**'s *Mysterium Paschale* (1969) is the foundational twentieth-century theological reading of the passion as a single dramatic arc from the Last Supper through Holy Saturday to the resurrection. Balthasar develops the kenotic Christology in which the Son's self-handing-over (***paradidōmi***, **παραδίδωμι**) at the supper is the iconic act that the cross consummates. **Jürgen Moltmann**'s *The Crucified God* (1972) reads Gethsemane as the moment in which the trinitarian God enters the abyss of human suffering — the Son's cry to the Father is the constitutive event of salvation. **Karl Barth** (*Church Dogmatics* IV/1 §59) treats Gethsemane and the trial as "the judge judged in our place," the Son of God taking on the verdict that should have fallen on humanity. **Bonhoeffer** in his *Letters and Papers from Prison* drew on Gethsemane as he faced his own death — "Christians stand by God in his hour of grieving" — and on the Last Supper as the foundational act of Christian community.

Among major commentators: **Joseph Fitzmyer** (Anchor Bible) is the gold standard on the redactional and Greek questions. **Joel Green** (NICNT) is theologically rich, especially on the Petrine material. **John Nolland** (Word) is patient on the textual variants. **Darrell Bock** (BECNT) defends a more conservative reading. **I. Howard Marshall** (NIGTC) is philologically careful. **Luke Timothy Johnson** (Sacra Pagina) reads the chapter as Lukan literary architecture. **François Bovon** (Hermeneia) is invaluable on patristic and medieval reception. **Raymond Brown**'s monumental *The Death of the Messiah* (1994) is the foundational study of all four passion narratives in synoptic comparison.

Jewish readings: **Geza Vermes**'s *The Passion* and **Amy-Jill Levine**'s contributions to the *Jewish Annotated New Testament* press for caution about the night-trial scene's historical plausibility (the Mishnaic prohibition on capital trials at night, *m. Sanhedrin* 4:1, may not have been operative in the Second Temple period, but the question is real). **E.P. Sanders** (*Jesus and Judaism*) defends the basic historical core: the temple authorities saw Jesus as a serious threat after the temple action and moved to neutralize him quickly. **David Flusser** read the Last Supper as a *kiddush* meal with eschatological orientation, broadly continuous with Pharisaic table piety. **Daniel Boyarin** has explored the way the Lukan passion participates in broader Second Temple traditions of righteous-sufferer narratives (Isaiah 53; Wisdom 2; 4 Maccabees).

Mediterranean and cultural readings: **Kenneth Bailey**'s *Jesus Through Middle Eastern Eyes* and *The Cross and the Prodigal* recover the honor-shame dynamics of the Lukan denial scene — Peter's failure is a public shame whose burden the Lord's gaze both intensifies and absorbs. It is worth noting, with **Joel Green** and **Robert Tannehill**, that the women who funded Jesus's ministry (Luke 8:1–3) are absent from the supper-and-arrest scene but reappear at the cross (23:49) and tomb (24:1–10), with no failure recorded against them — a contrast Luke draws without comment.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What does Luke's eucharistic ***anamnēsis*** institute?** A first position, the dominant Catholic reading codified at Trent (Session 22, 1562) and developed in the Eucharistic theology of **Aquinas** and **Joseph Ratzinger / Benedict XVI** (*Sacramentum Caritatis*), takes the supper as instituting a memorial-sacrifice that re-presents Calvary on every altar — not a new immolation but the same single sacrifice made present through ***anamnēsis***. A second position, the Lutheran Real Presence affirmed in the Augsburg Confession Article 10 and developed by **Luther** in the Marburg Colloquy and *Babylonian Captivity*, holds that Christ is truly present "in, with, and under" the elements but rejects the sacrificial-memorial reading. A third position, the Reformed pneumatic real presence of **Calvin** (*Institutes* IV.17), holds that the believer is lifted by the Spirit to feed on the heavenly Christ; a fourth, Zwinglian-memorialist reading, takes the supper as a symbolic remembrance without ontological presence. Steelmanning each: Catholic theology takes seriously the cultic-liturgical density of ***anamnēsis*** in the LXX and Second Temple Judaism; Lutheran theology takes seriously the unmistakable "is" of "this is my body"; Reformed pneumatic theology takes seriously the presence of the ascended Christ and the role of the Spirit; Zwinglian memorialism takes seriously the simplicity of the Synoptic accounts and the danger of magical misreading. *Where this leaves us:* the Lukan ***anamnēsis*** is more than memory and less than Catholic transubstantiation as classically formulated; it is the actualizing remembrance by which the gathered church participates in the once-for-all event of the cross, and in the risen Christ who hosts the meal. The Lukan text is more spacious than any single confessional reading allows.

**Tension 2: What did Jesus mean by "buy a sword" (22:36)?** A first position, the just-war reading occasionally pressed in medieval and early-modern Christian political theology, takes the saying as authorizing legitimate armed defense. A second position, the pacifist reading of **John Howard Yoder**, **Stanley Hauerwas**, and the historic peace churches, takes the saying as ironic or pedagogical — Jesus is preparing the disciples for the eschatological hour in which they will be treated as outlaws (22:37, citing Isa 53:12), not authorizing them to take up arms. The "it is enough" (***hikanon estin***) is Jesus's exasperated dismissal of their literal misunderstanding, confirmed minutes later by his "no more of this!" at the arrest (22:51). A third position, the apocalyptic-symbolic reading developed by **Joel Green** and **N.T. Wright**, takes the saying as part of Jesus's ironic preparation of the disciples to share his fate as a transgressor — the swords are real but their purpose is misread. Steelmanning each: the just-war reading takes seriously the literal sense of the imperative; the pacifist reading takes seriously Jesus's immediate "no more of this!" and the broader Lukan emphasis on enemy-love; the apocalyptic reading takes seriously the Isaiah 53:12 citation. *Where this leaves us:* the saying is not a charter for Christian armed force. The most careful reading takes Jesus's "it is enough" as ironic-dismissive, the Isaian citation as the operative interpretive frame, and the immediate sequel (the disciples brandish a sword, Jesus rebukes them, heals the casualty) as the practical exegesis Jesus himself provides.

One unresolved question: how should we read the disputed status of 22:43–44 (the angel and the bloody sweat)? The textual evidence is genuinely divided. If the verses are original, they show the most physically intense Lukan portrayal of Jesus's human anguish. If they are a second-century addition, they reflect the early church's anti-docetic emphasis on Christ's full humanity. The verses are theologically valuable in either case, but their textual status remains unresolved, and that unresolvedness is itself part of how the church reads its own passion tradition.

## The Thread

The verb ***paradidōmi*** runs through the chapter like a chain (22:4, 6, 21, 22, 48), tying the betrayer's act, the disciples' coming sufferings (already forecast at 21:12, 16), and the Father's giving-up of the Son (Romans 8:32) into a single typology. Peter's prayed-for restoration (22:32) sets up the Acts narrative directly: it is Peter who preaches at Pentecost (Acts 2), heals the lame man (Acts 3), opens the gospel to the Gentiles in Cornelius's house (Acts 10–11) — the strengthening of the brothers begun. The Gethsemane prayer "Father, if you are willing" anticipates the dying word "Father, into your hands" (23:46). And the bread-broken-and-recognized of 22:19 will return at Emmaus (24:30–31), where the disciples' eyes are opened in the breaking of bread.
