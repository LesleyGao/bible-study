# Psalm 83 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 83 carries a brief but freighted superscription: *shir mizmor le-Asaph* (שִׁיר מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף) — "A Song. A Psalm of Asaph." It is the last of the Asaphite psalms (a cluster running from Psalm 73 to Psalm 83), associated with the Levitical guild traced to Asaph, one of David's three chief musicians (1 Chr 16:4-7; 25:1-2). The Asaphite collection has its own theological signature: prophetic urgency, national crisis, the question of theodicy under empire. Psalm 83 closes that cluster on a note of acute geopolitical alarm.

It also closes something larger. Psalms 42–83 form the so-called **Elohistic Psalter**, a stretch in which the divine name *Elohim* (אֱלֹהִים) appears far more frequently than the covenant name *YHWH*. Beginning with Psalm 84, the tetragrammaton re-emerges as the dominant divine title. Psalm 83 thus sits at a textual seam: the last word of the Elohistic Psalter is a plea that the nations would "know that you alone, whose name is the LORD (יהוה), are the Most High over all the earth" (83:18). The very name suppressed for forty psalms breaks back into the climax.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 83 as a **community lament** (*Volksklagelied*), and more specifically a national lament with an extended **imprecation** (*Verwünschung*). The form follows Gunkel's typical lament shape: invocation (v. 1), description of the crisis (vv. 2-8), historical recital (vv. 9-12), petition for divine action (vv. 13-15), and a final theological purpose (vv. 16-18). Sigmund Mowinckel placed psalms of this kind in cultic settings of national emergency — the day of fasting called when "an army comes against us" (cf. 2 Chr 20:3-4; Joel 1-2). Hans-Joachim Kraus suggested a setting in temple liturgies of communal supplication; Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger (in their Hermeneia commentary) read it as a late composite, drawing on the long memory of Israel's enemies rather than a single attested moment.

That last point is the crux of the historical question. The psalm names a coalition of ten enemies in vv. 6-8: Edom, the Ishmaelites, Moab, the Hagrites, Gebal, Ammon, Amalek, Philistia, Tyre, and Asshur. **No single attested moment in Israelite history features all ten of these peoples attacking together.** Some commentators have proposed the 9th-century BCE crisis under Jehoshaphat (2 Chr 20), others the Assyrian threat of the 8th century, others a late-Persian or Maccabean setting (Asshur understood as a cipher for Seleucid Syria — a reading favored by the medieval Jewish commentator Radak and revived by some 20th-century critics). The most defensible scholarly judgment is that the catalogue is **composite-historical**: a stylized list of perennial adversaries, drawing memory of Midian and Sisera (vv. 9-12 — the deliverances under Gideon in Judg 7-8 and under Deborah and Barak in Judg 4-5) into a generalized prayer against any coalition that would erase Israel as a nation. The psalm's genius is that its enemies are particular enough to feel real and general enough to outlast their own century.

Hebrew terms worth noting: *yachad lev* in v. 5 — "they conspire with one accord" — uses *lev echad* (לֵב אֶחָד), literally "one heart," ironic against the prayer of Ps 86:11 to "unite my heart" (*yachid lebavi*, יַחֵד לְבָבִי). The verb *kichdu* in v. 4 ("let us wipe them out as a nation") is *naḵḥidem mi-goy* (נַכְחִידֵם מִגּוֹי) — *kachad* in the hiphil meaning to *blot out, annihilate*. The phrase *yishvu va-yibahalu* in v. 17 — *yevoshu ve-yibbahalu* (יֵבֹשׁוּ וְיִבָּהֲלוּ) — couples shame (*bosh*) with terror (*bahal*); the doubling intensifies. The petition *shitemo galgal ke-qash lifne ruach* in v. 13 ("make them like whirling dust, like chaff before the wind") draws on standard ANE dust-and-chaff imagery for routed armies.

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragmentary witnesses to the Asaphite collection in 4QPs<sup>a</sup> and 11QPs<sup>b</sup>, broadly confirming the Masoretic text. The Septuagint (Psalm 82 LXX) renders Asshur as Ἀσσούρ and preserves the geographic coalition essentially intact. ANE parallels for the form include Assyrian and Babylonian *šuilla* prayers in which a king petitions his god against a coalition of named enemies, and Egyptian execration texts in which the names of foreign rulers were inscribed on bowls and broken to enact ritual cursing. The Israelite version is distinctive in that the petitioner is *the people*, not the king, and the appeal is grounded in covenant memory, not magical efficacy.

## Lens 2: The Literature

The psalm's literary architecture is tighter than its martial vocabulary suggests. It moves through three strophes marked by the single *selah* at v. 8: vv. 1-8 (the crisis), vv. 9-12 (historical recital), vv. 13-18 (petition and purpose). The strophic balance is roughly equal — eight verses, four verses, six verses — with the *selah* hinging the description of the coalition into the appeal to past deliverances.

Bishop Robert Lowth's three categories of Hebrew parallelism are all present. **Synonymous parallelism** opens the psalm: "do not keep silence; / do not hold your peace or be still" (v. 1) — three near-synonyms for divine inactivity stacked in tricolon. The same pattern governs v. 14: "as fire consumes the forest, / as the flame sets the mountains ablaze." **Antithetic parallelism** is rarer in this psalm, but the turn from v. 4 ("let the name of Israel be remembered no more") to v. 18 ("that they may know that you alone, whose name is the LORD") sets up an antithesis at the level of the whole composition: their plot to erase the name, defeated by his disclosure of his name. **Synthetic parallelism** drives the historical recital in vv. 9-11, where each new line adds a fresh historical reference (Midian, Sisera and Jabin, Oreb and Zeeb, Zebah and Zalmunna), accumulating witnesses rather than restating.

The psalm uses a near-**inclusio** at the level of names: it begins with the silence of God (v. 1) and ends with the disclosure of his name as YHWH and Elyon (v. 18) — silence transposed into self-revelation. A subtler **chiastic** structure organizes the central petition: the four images of vv. 13-15 (whirling dust, chaff, fire, tempest) move from horizontal scattering (dust, chaff) to vertical consumption (fire, tempest), then return to horizontal pursuit ("pursue them with your tempest").

The most memorable line is the imprecation of v. 13 — *Elohai shitemo ka-galgal ke-qash lifne ruach* (אֱלֹהַי שִׁיתֵמוֹ כַגַּלְגַּל כְּקַשׁ לִפְנֵי רוּחַ) — "O my God, make them like whirling dust, like chaff before the wind." *Galgal* is a tumbleweed or thistle-wheel; the image is of something rootless and weightless, blown without resistance. It is not, in the original, a wish for annihilation so much as a wish that the coalition's *weight* be removed — that the conspiracy lose the gravity it has assumed.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

In the canonical shape of the Psalter, Psalm 83 sits in **Book III** (Psalms 73-89), which Gerald Wilson read as the book of crisis: the failure of the Davidic monarchy, the questioning of covenant, the cry for divine action when the throne has fallen. Wilson's seam-theory of Psalter composition places Psalms 89 (the lament over the broken Davidic covenant) and 90 (the turn to Moses) at the structural center of the whole collection. Psalm 83, near the end of Book III, voices the geopolitical face of that same crisis: not just an internal failure of Davidic kingship but an external coalition of nations who would erase Israel altogether.

In **Jewish reception**, the psalm has had a complex life. *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 83 dwells on the list of nations as a typology of perennial adversaries, reading the coalition as a pattern more than a date. **Rashi** (11th c.) reads the psalm as a prayer for the days of Jehoshaphat. **Ibn Ezra** (12th c.) is more cautious about the date, noting the difficulty of any single historical referent. **Radak** (David Kimhi, 13th c.) reads it eschatologically — as a prayer about the final coalition against Israel in messianic days. In the modern period, **Martin Buber** treats the imprecatory psalms as honest theological speech rather than embarrassment to be explained away; **Abraham Joshua Heschel** (in *The Prophets*) reads the lament tradition as continuous with prophetic protest; **Jon Levenson** (*Sinai and Zion*; *Creation and the Persistence of Evil*) emphasizes that the psalm's theology is not Israelite-exceptionalist but cosmic — the sovereignty of YHWH "over all the earth" (v. 18) is the eschatological horizon, not a tribal warrant. **Nahum Sarna** observed that the psalm's catalogue of enemies is structured by geographic encirclement, naming peoples to the south, east, north, and west — a literary 360-degree threat. In Jewish liturgy, Psalm 83 is not part of the daily or Sabbath rotation, though selections appear in petitions during national crises.

In **Christian reception**, the trajectory is longer. **Athanasius** in his *Letter to Marcellinus* lists Psalm 83 among the psalms to be prayed when one is besieged by enemies. **Augustine's** *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 82 (LXX numbering) reads the coalition allegorically: the ten nations are figures for the appetites and ideologies arrayed against the City of God; the prayer that they be made "like whirling dust" is a prayer that worldly power lose its weight in the soul of the believer. **Cassiodorus** follows a similar allegorical line in his *Expositio Psalmorum*. **Aquinas** (in his commentary on the Psalms) reads the psalm as fitting both Israel literally and the church spiritually, noting the doctrinal force of v. 18 — that even the enemies are made to know God's name. **Luther's** *Operationes in Psalmos* (1519-1521) treats the psalm with characteristic intensity: the coalition is the world's hatred of the gospel; the prayer for shame in v. 16 is read in light of v. 16b ("that they may seek your name") — shame as the path to repentance, not the end of repentance. **Calvin** (1557 commentary) is more historically minded, locating the psalm in Jehoshaphat's crisis but expanding it to "all ages of the church" who pray against ungodly conspiracies.

Among modern critical commentators: **Gunkel** classified the form, **Mowinckel** the cultic setting, **Westermann** the lament structure, **Kraus** the temple liturgy, **Hossfeld-Zenger** the composite redactional history, **John Goldingay** the theological-canonical reading, **Marvin Tate** (the WBC volume) the philological detail, **Gordon Wenham** (in *Psalms as Torah*) the moral instruction the psalms train. **Walter Brueggemann** in *The Message of the Psalms* and *Praying the Psalms* reads imprecation as legitimate speech of the powerless: the alternative to imprecatory prayer is not piety but suppression, and suppression breeds violence rather than preventing it. **Patrick Miller**, in *They Cried to the Lord*, traces the catalogue of enemies as a theological act of *naming* — bringing the threat into liturgical speech rather than letting it remain anonymous and overwhelming. **William Brown** (*Seeing the Psalms*) and **Beth Tanner** (in the NICOT replacement volume) note the psalm's image-density as a feature, not a flaw. **David Mitchell** (*The Message of the Psalter*) reads Psalm 83 as part of the Psalter's eschatological storyline.

In the **New Testament**, Psalm 83 is not directly quoted. But its theological grammar — the prayer that the nations would "know that you alone are the LORD" (v. 18) — surfaces in Paul's vision of every knee bowing and every tongue confessing (Phil 2:10-11) and in Revelation's nations bringing their glory into the New Jerusalem (Rev 21:24-26). The plot to "wipe Israel out as a nation" (v. 4) finds canonical resonance in Esther 3:13 (Haman's edict) and in Revelation 12 (the dragon's pursuit of the woman).

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

Two tensions deserve careful attention.

**Tension 1: Imprecation — protest or authorization?** The psalm asks God to make Israel's enemies "like whirling dust," to "pursue them with your tempest," to "fill their faces with shame." Christian readers, especially since the Enlightenment, have struggled with imprecatory speech. C. S. Lewis in *Reflections on the Psalms* called the imprecations "devilish"; Bonhoeffer, in *Life Together* and *The Prayerbook of the Bible*, defended them as the necessary speech of those who refuse to take vengeance into their own hands. **Position A (the protest reading — Brueggemann, Zenger, Miller):** Imprecation is legitimate prayer of the wounded. Its very form — addressed to God — is the act of *not* killing; it surrenders judgment to the only one entitled to render it. To suppress imprecation is not to be more loving but to send the violence underground. **Position B (the authorization reading, popular in some Christian Zionist circles):** the psalm authorizes its prayers as prescriptions for foreign policy; it gives believers a script for naming present-day enemies and asking God to destroy them. **Position C (the typological reading — Augustine, Cassiodorus, much of the patristic tradition):** the enemies are spiritual; the prayer is for the destruction of sin in the soul, not the body of any actual nation.

The steelman of Position A: imprecation is not the same as violence. It is the *renunciation* of violence in favor of leaving judgment to God. The petitioner who prays "make them like whirling dust" is precisely the one who is *not* taking the sword. To remove imprecation from the prayerbook is to leave the wounded with no liturgical home for their rage; in clinical and pastoral experience, suppressed rage becomes either depression or violence. The honest Christian engagement with imprecation is not to apologize for it but to receive it as the speech of those who have suffered more than we have.

The steelman of Position C: the New Testament's teaching to love enemies (Matt 5:44) and Christ's own prayer of forgiveness (Luke 23:34) reorients imprecation. The whirling dust is now the vanishing of all that opposes God in *me* and in *us*; the enemies are interior. Augustine and Cassiodorus do not abolish the psalm's force; they relocate it.

**Tension 2: The modern weaponization — Christian-Zionist political-prophecy.** Psalm 83 has had a strange career in 20th- and 21st-century American evangelicalism. Hal Lindsey's *The Late Great Planet Earth* (1970) and his successors built dispensational schemes in which the psalm's coalition predicts a specific imminent Arab-Israeli war (sometimes called "the Psalm 83 War"). John Hagee, Bill Salus, and others have treated the ten-nation list as a contemporary geopolitical prediction: Edom = southern Jordan, Moab = central Jordan, Ammon = northern Jordan, Hagrites = Egypt or Saudi Arabia, Tyre = Lebanon/Hezbollah, Asshur = Syria/Iraq, and so on. **Position A (the dispensational-prophetic reading):** Psalm 83 is a yet-future prophecy of an Arab-Israeli war that will precede the rapture or the Ezekiel 38 war. **Position B (the mainstream-evangelical and Catholic rejection):** the dispensational hermeneutic misreads the genre (the psalm is a lament/prayer, not a prophecy in the predictive sense), the geography (the equations between ancient and modern peoples are tendentious), and the canonical context (the psalm's resolution is not military victory but the nations *knowing* YHWH). N. T. Wright, Gary Burge (*Whose Land? Whose Promise?*), and the Catholic *Pontifical Biblical Commission* have all rejected the dispensational appropriation; Wright in particular argues that the New Testament reads Israel's story as reaching its goal in the Messiah and the worldwide people gathered around him, not in a programme of modern geopolitical conquest. **Position C (the on-the-ground critique — Gary Burge, Mitri Raheb):** the dispensational use of the psalm in Western Christian Zionism functions as theological cover for the dispossession of actual Middle Eastern Christians and Muslims, including descendants of the very peoples the psalm names; the hermeneutical question is no longer abstract — the misreading has political consequences in living human lives.

The steelman of Position A: the psalm does name nations geographically arrayed around modern Israel; coincidence is hard to dismiss; if Scripture is predictive, why not here. The steelman of Position C: the psalm's own resolution in v. 18 — that the nations would *know* God, not be annihilated — undercuts the militarized reading; the canonical horizon is conversion, not conquest. The mainstream scholarly position — held in some form by Wright, Burge, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay — is that the dispensational reading violates basic principles of genre, context, and canonical trajectory.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 83 is the prayer of a small people facing a coalition larger than itself. Its imprecation is honest speech; its catalogue of enemies is the act of naming what threatens to erase. But its closing petition — that the nations would *know* God's name — pulls the prayer back from annihilation toward disclosure. The psalm wants the coalition stopped, but its final ambition is theological, not military. The imprecation is the floor; the doxology of v. 18 is the ceiling. To pray Psalm 83 well is to hold both.

**Closing question:** When you face a coalition arrayed against what you love — a project, a people, a calling — can you bring the rage to God in liturgical speech without letting the rage become the script for what you do with your own hands?
