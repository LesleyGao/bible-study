# Matthew 2: Magi, Massacre, and the Geography of Fulfillment

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Matthew 2 is set in the last months of **Herod the Great's reign**, which Josephus dates to a death in the spring of 4 BCE following a lunar eclipse and just before Passover (*Antiquities* 17.167; *War* 1.665). The chapter's geography traces a precise late-Herodian map: Jerusalem (the royal seat), Bethlehem of Judea (six miles south), Egypt (Roman province since 30 BCE under Augustus's direct control), Ramah (in Benjamin, on the road north out of Jerusalem), and finally Galilee — the territory Augustus assigned in 4 BCE to **Herod Antipas**, while Judea, Samaria, and Idumea went to **Archelaus**, and the northeastern tetrarchies (Iturea, Trachonitis, Auranitis) to **Philip**. Archelaus's brutality (he massacred 3,000 pilgrims at Passover 4 BCE, per Josephus *Ant.* 17.213–218) led to his deposition in 6 CE and Judea's conversion to direct Roman procuratorial rule — the political condition Joseph's fear in 2:22 anticipates.

**Herod the Great** had ruled as Rome's client king since 37 BCE. His paranoid violence is documented extensively. He executed his Hasmonean wife **Mariamne I** (29 BCE), her brother **Aristobulus III** (the high priest, drowned at age 17 in 35 BCE), her grandfather **Hyrcanus II** (30 BCE), and three of his own sons — **Alexander** and **Aristobulus IV** strangled at Sebaste (7 BCE), **Antipater** executed five days before Herod's own death (4 BCE). Augustus's reported quip — preserved by Macrobius (*Saturnalia* 2.4.11) — that "it is better to be Herod's pig (*hys*) than Herod's son (*huios*)" — depends on a Greek pun and probably reflects later tradition, but it captures the dynastic atmosphere. The **Bethlehem massacre** (2:16–18) is unattested outside Matthew, but **Raymond Brown** (*Birth of the Messiah*, 1977/1993) calculates that Bethlehem c. 6–4 BCE had perhaps 300–1,000 inhabitants, yielding maybe 6–20 male children under two — a number small enough to escape Josephus's notice yet entirely consistent with Herod's documented character. **R.T. France** (NICNT, 2007) and **Davies/Allison** (*ICC Matthew* vol. 1, 1988) treat the historicity question as open but plausible.

**Archaeology** anchors the chapter at multiple points. **Herodium** — Herod's fortress-tomb 12 km south of Jerusalem and visible from Bethlehem — was excavated by **Ehud Netzer** from 1972 until his discovery of Herod's actual tomb on the slope of the artificial mountain in **2007**. The mausoleum's sarcophagus had been deliberately smashed (by Jewish rebels during the First Revolt, c. 66–70 CE), but its scale, location, and decorative program confirm Josephus's descriptions and concretize the kind of king who appears in Matthew 2. **Bethlehem** itself yielded a First Temple-period bulla reading "Bethlehem" found in 2012 in the City of David excavations (Eli Shukron) — the earliest extra-biblical attestation of the town's name. The **Church of the Nativity** sits over a cave-complex venerated as Jesus's birthplace already by the mid-second century (**Justin Martyr**, *Dial.* 78, c. 155 CE: "in a certain cave very close to the village"; **Origen**, *Contra Celsum* 1.51, c. 248 CE), with Constantinian basilica construction begun under **Helena** c. 327 CE. **Nazareth** in the late Hellenistic / early Roman period was an unremarkable village of perhaps 200–500 people — a fact significant for its absence from the Hebrew Bible, Josephus, and the Talmud, and consonant with Matthew 2:23's etymological play. **James Strange**, **Bellarmino Bagatti** (Franciscan excavations 1955–1969), and more recently **Ken Dark** (the Sisters of Nazareth excavations) have uncovered first-century domestic structures, hewn-rock cisterns, and a rock-cut tomb consistent with a small Jewish village.

The ***magoi*** of 2:1 are not "kings." The Greek term ***magoi*** (μάγοι) denotes the Persian Zoroastrian priestly-astrological class, attested in **Herodotus** 1.101 and 1.131–132 as the priestly tribe of the Medes, in **Strabo** 15.3.13–15 as the *magousaioi* of Persian religion, and in Daniel LXX (where it translates Aramaic *ḥarṭummîm*) as Babylonian court astrologers. Their gifts — **gold, frankincense, and myrrh** — are luxuries of South Arabian and East African trade (Sheba, Hadramaut, Punt), priced and used in 1st-century Mediterranean medicine, perfumery, and royal protocol. The tradition of "three kings" with names Caspar / Melchior / Balthasar is a 6th-century Western development (the names first appear in the *Excerpta Latina Barbari*); the tradition of a precise number derives from the three gifts.

The **star** has occasioned a 2,000-year astronomical-theological literature. **Kepler** (1606) proposed the 7 BCE triple conjunction of Jupiter and Saturn in Pisces (a real astronomical event of unusual beauty); **Halley's comet** (12 BCE) is too early; modern proposals include the 6 BCE Jupiter-Mars-Saturn massing in Pisces, a hypothetical supernova, and (perhaps most likely) a non-naturalistic theological motif drawing on Numbers 24:17 ("a star shall come out of Jacob") rather than a discrete astronomical event. **Brown** treats the question with appropriate caution: the narrative's interest is theological, not astronomical.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter: ***magoi*** (μάγοι, 2:1) — Persian astrologer-priests; ***proskyneō*** (προσκυνέω, 2:2, 8, 11) — "to fall down and worship," used in LXX for worship of YHWH and in Greco-Roman context for prostration before kings; ***Christos*** (Χριστός, 2:4) — "anointed one"; ***Nazōraios*** (Ναζωραῖος, 2:23) — "Nazarene," a contested term we will return to; and ***hina plērōthē*** (ἵνα πληρωθῇ, 2:15, 17, 23) — the formula citation that recurs three times in this chapter alone.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Matthew 2 belongs structurally to the **infancy narrative** (chapters 1–2), which functions as the Gospel's *prologue* — analogous to the prologue of John 1 and the genealogies of Luke 3, but distinctively constructed around five geographical movements (Bethlehem → Jerusalem → Bethlehem → Egypt → Nazareth) each anchored by a fulfillment-formula citation. **B.W. Bacon**'s 1918 thesis on the **five-discourse structure** of Matthew (the body of the Gospel built around five teaching blocks ending with "when Jesus had finished") finds a structural prefiguration here in the five geographical-fulfillment movements of the prologue — a five-fold echo of the Pentateuch that **Dale Allison** develops at length in *The New Moses: A Matthean Typology* (1993).

The chapter's literature is dominated by the **fulfillment formula citations**: 2:6 (Mic 5:2 with 2 Sam 5:2), 2:15 (Hos 11:1), 2:17 (Jer 31:15), and 2:23 ("he shall be called a Nazarene"). These are five (counting 1:22) of Matthew's ten formula citations — the densest concentration in the Gospel. **Krister Stendahl**'s *The School of St. Matthew* (1954) argues these citations exhibit a **pesher-like** exegetical method (akin to the Qumran *pesharim*) using mixed text-forms (LXX, Hebrew, possibly Aramaic targums) selected to fit the narrative; **Davies/Allison** moderate Stendahl's "school" hypothesis but accept the philological observation. **Richard Hays**'s *Echoes of Scripture in the Gospels* (2016) reads the citations as functioning **figurally** — the Hosea 11:1 quote in 2:15 is the paradigmatic case, since in its Hebrew Bible context it refers retrospectively to *Israel's* exodus, not prospectively to a Messiah's; Matthew is reading Jesus as the *embodied* Israel whose life recapitulates Israel's history.

The **Moses-typology** structuring the chapter is unmistakable. **Pharaoh** kills Hebrew baby boys to forestall the rise of a deliverer; **Herod** kills baby boys for the same reason. **Moses** is born under the death-decree, hidden, escapes; **Jesus** is born under the death-decree, hidden, escapes. **Moses** flees Egypt, returns when "those who sought your life are dead" (Exod 4:19); the angel tells Joseph "those who sought the child's life are dead" (Matt 2:20) — a near-verbatim citation. The differences are also instructive: Moses *flees* Egypt to escape death, Jesus *flees to* Egypt; Moses returns to Egypt to rescue Israel, Jesus returns from Egypt to *be* Israel. **Allison**'s *New Moses* gathers the parallels exhaustively. **R.T. France** notes that the typology is layered: Jesus is at once a new Moses (deliverer), a new Israel (exiled and restored), and a new David (born in Bethlehem, of Judah).

The **chapter's structure** is five panels each ending in fulfillment:
- 2:1–6: Magi arrive; Bethlehem named; Mic 5:2 / 2 Sam 5:2 cited
- 2:7–12: Magi worship; depart by another way (no explicit formula but Numbers 24:17 echo)
- 2:13–15: Flight to Egypt; Hos 11:1 cited
- 2:16–18: Massacre; Jer 31:15 cited
- 2:19–23: Return; settlement in Nazareth; "he shall be called a Nazarene" cited

Textual variants are minor. The 2:23 citation — "**he shall be called a Nazarene**" — has no direct OT source and remains one of Matthew's most contested citations: **Davies/Allison** survey four main proposals: (1) Isa 11:1 *netzer* (the Davidic "branch"), (2) Judg 13:5 / 16:17 *nazir* (Samson's nazirite consecration), (3) Isa 49:6 (a generic "despised one" reading), or (4) a generic prophetic theme ("he shall be called X," with Nazareth's obscurity producing the messianic-rejection echo). The plural "prophets" (τῶν προφητῶν) in 2:23 — distinctive among the formula citations — suggests Matthew himself recognized the citation as a *summary* rather than a single text-quote.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish interpretive trajectories.** Matthew 2 is engaged sharply by Jewish counter-traditions. The **Toledot Yeshu** (medieval, drawing on traditions from late antiquity) attacks the birth narrative directly, presenting Jesus's flight to Egypt as a learning-magic episode (a polemical inversion of Matthew 2:13–15). **Peter Schäfer**'s *Jesus in the Talmud* (2007) traces b. Sanhedrin 107b and b. Sotah 47a's reference to "Yeshu's" sojourn in Egypt as polemical material drawing on Matthew 2 for inversion. Modern Jewish scholarship — **Vermes**, **Sanders**, **Levine** in the *Jewish Annotated NT* (2nd ed. 2017) — generally treats the magi narrative as Christian-Jewish midrash on Numbers 24:17 (Balaam's star-prophecy) and 1 Kings 10 (queen of Sheba bringing gifts to Solomon), with the historicity of the magi visit bracketed and the historicity of the massacre treated skeptically (no extrabiblical attestation, dependence on Mosaic typology). **Daniel Boyarin**'s *The Jewish Gospels* (2012) reads the magi episode as legible within Second Temple Jewish messianic-Gentile-pilgrimage expectation (cf. Isa 60:1–6, "nations shall come to your light… they shall bring gold and frankincense").

**Christian interpretive trajectories.** Patristic engagement was extensive. **Justin Martyr** (*Dial.* 78, 88, 102–103, c. 155 CE) is the earliest extra-canonical witness to the cave-tradition and to the magi's reception; **Origen** (*Contra Celsum* 1.34–60) defends the historicity against Celsus's objections and reads the gifts allegorically (gold = kingship, frankincense = divinity, myrrh = death); **Tertullian** (*Adv. Marc.* 3.13) emphasizes the prophetic-fulfillment logic; **Chrysostom**'s Homilies 6–9 on Matthew (c. 390 CE) treat the chapter at length, reading Herod's troubled state in 2:3 as the unveiling of his demonic alignment and the magi's worship as the firstfruits of Gentile faith. **Jerome**'s *Commentary on Matthew* (398 CE) defends the historicity of the massacre against pagan objections; **Augustine** (*De Consensu Evangelistarum* 2.5–11) handles the harmonization with Luke (which has no flight to Egypt) by proposing a chronology in which the flight occurs after the Lukan Temple presentation. **Hilary of Poitiers** reads the chapter typologically; **Aquinas**'s *Catena Aurea* on Matthew 2 gathers the tradition; his *Lectura super Matthaeum* gives Thomistic systematic treatment.

The **Massacre of the Innocents** acquired its own liturgical and theological tradition: commemorated as a feast (Holy Innocents) from at least the 5th century in the West (Dec 28); read as the first martyrs (martyred *for* Christ though not yet *of* Christ); engaged philosophically by **Augustine** (*De Civ. Dei* 16.32) and **Aquinas** (*ST* III.27.5) on the question of how unbaptized infants participate in salvation. **Reformation** engagement: **Calvin**'s *Harmony* (1555) reads the chapter primarily as evidence of Christ's accordance with prophecy and the providence of God in protecting the Christ-child; **Luther**'s sermons on Epiphany emphasize the magi as Gentile firstfruits.

20th-century engagement: **Karl Barth** (*CD* IV/2 §64.3) reads the flight to Egypt as the early hiddenness of the incarnate Lord; **Bonhoeffer** in his Christology lectures emphasizes the political vulnerability of the incarnate Christ. **Bultmann** treats the chapter as legendary in the technical sense and theologically secondary. **Raymond Brown**'s *Birth of the Messiah* (1977/1993) is the touchstone critical Catholic engagement. Contemporary commentary: **Davies/Allison** *ICC* vol. 1 (1988); **Ulrich Luz** *Hermeneia* vol. 1 (2007 ET) — outstanding on reception history including art-historical traditions of magi imagery; **R.T. France** *NICNT* (2007); **Craig Keener** *Matthew* (1999); **Daniel Harrington** *Sacra Pagina*; **Donald Hagner** *WBC*; **John Nolland** *NIGTC*; **Wilkins** *NIVAC*; **D.A. Carson**; **Richard Bauckham** on the geography and the magi-tradition; **Allison** *The New Moses* (1993) is foundational on the Moses typology.

**N.T. Wright** (*Matthew for Everyone* vol. 1; *Jesus and the Victory of God*; *How God Became King*) reads Matthew 2 as a study in two kingships colliding. Herod is the model of the kingdom of the world — frightened, murderous, clinging to power; the child in Bethlehem is the true king of the Jews, and the magi — pagan astrologers, of all people — are the first to do him homage, a deliberate signal that this kingdom will burst Israel's borders. The flight to Egypt and the return is, for Wright, Matthew telling the story of a new exodus: Jesus is recapitulating Israel's own journey — down into Egypt, out again, into the land — so that the Hosea 11:1 citation in 2:15 ("out of Egypt I called my son") is not a misfired prediction but the controlling clue that Jesus *is* Israel-in-person, come to do what Israel was called to do. Wright also notes that the Numbers 24:17 echo (Balaam's "star out of Jacob") and the Micah 5:2 quotation together place Jesus in the line of the long-awaited Davidic king whose coming the prophets tied to the end of exile; the chapter is, in his reading, the opening salvo in Matthew's argument that God is now becoming king on earth, in person, and that the powers-that-be — Herod first, Caesar by implication — are on notice.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The historicity of the massacre and the moral problem of providential rescue.** Matthew 2 narrates that God warned Joseph in a dream, sparing Jesus, while the angel did not warn the parents of Bethlehem's other male infants under two — who were slaughtered. The asymmetry is morally vivid and theologically pointed.

The **historical-skeptical position** (Vermes, Lüdemann, much critical scholarship) reads the massacre as a Mosaic-typological construction without historical foundation, citing Josephus's silence and the parallel to Pharaoh's massacre. On this reading, the moral problem evaporates because the event did not occur, but the literary problem of why Matthew constructed the narrative this way remains, and the theological reception of the narrative as describing a real event remains untouched.

The **historical-defensive position** (France, Carson, traditional Catholic and Protestant interpretation) holds that a small-village massacre of 6–20 male children in 4 BCE is plausibly within Josephus's noise floor — Herod committed many atrocities Josephus did not record — and that the silence of other sources is not probative. This preserves the historicity at the cost of leaving the moral asymmetry unresolved.

The **theological-confessional position** (Brown, Davies/Allison, much modern critical Catholic interpretation) treats the historicity question as not fully settleable but theologically secondary — the narrative *function* is to place Jesus in solidarity with the suffering of innocents under tyrannical power, while marking him out as the singular bearer of an irreplaceable salvific mission. **The steelman of all three positions converges**: the chapter is *not* a theodicy. It is not arguing that God-rescued-Jesus-and-not-others proves either God's justice or Jesus's privilege. It is asserting that the Messiah's life unfolds *within* a world of unspeakable violence and grief, that "Rachel weeps for her children and refuses to be comforted" (2:18), and that this grief is part of the narrative shape of the gospel itself.

**Where this leaves us.** Matthew is not offering a justification for the massacre. He is naming it. The Jeremiah 31:15 quotation places Bethlehem's mothers in the long line of Israelite mothers who weep — and Jeremiah 31 *continues* (31:16–17) with a promise of return that Matthew leaves unquoted but available to the attentive reader. The unquoted continuation is the chapter's silent theological register: the lament is real and unanswered within the chapter; the answer is deferred to the larger Gospel and indeed the whole canonical arc.

**Tension 2: Hosea 11:1 — "Out of Egypt I called my son" — and the limits of fulfillment language.** Matthew 2:15 quotes Hosea 11:1, which in its original context is unambiguously *retrospective*: "When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son" — referring to the historical exodus. The verse is not a prediction. It is a memory. Matthew's claim that Jesus's return from Egypt **fulfills** (*hina plērōthē*) Hosea 11:1 has been called Matthew's most exegetically aggressive citation.

The **predictive-prophecy position** (older patristic tradition; some modern conservative interpretation) reads Hosea 11:1 as containing a *sensus plenior* — a fuller meaning that, while not evident in the original context, was always present in the divine intention and is uncovered by the New Testament event. The position preserves a strong correspondence between OT text and NT fulfillment but at the cost of straining the original sense of Hosea 11.

The **typological-figural position** (Hays, Davies/Allison, France, much contemporary critical Christian interpretation) reads the citation as **figural**: Matthew is identifying Jesus as the embodied Israel — the one whose life recapitulates and consummates Israel's story. Hosea 11:1 was about Israel; Jesus *is* Israel-in-person. The "fulfillment" is not the satisfaction of a prediction but the embodied reenactment and consummation of a pattern. **Hays**'s *Echoes of Scripture in the Gospels* makes this case at length.

The **Jewish-critical position** (Vermes, Levine, Brettler, *Jewish Annotated NT*) reads the citation as a misappropriation of Hosea 11:1, exegetically forced and theologically tendentious. From a strict Jewish interpretive frame, the verse cannot mean what Matthew says it means. **The steelman of all three positions** is not a synthesis but a recognition that the three positions are operating with different definitions of *fulfillment* — predictive, figural, or strictly grammatical-historical. Matthew is not doing strict grammatical-historical exegesis; he is doing figural reading. Whether that figural reading is theologically warranted depends on whether Jesus is in fact the embodied Israel — a Christological claim that the chapter is making rather than establishing.

**Where this leaves us.** Matthew 2 is not arguing from prophecy that Jesus is the Messiah. Matthew 2 is *reading Israel's story through Jesus* — finding in Hosea 11:1, Jeremiah 31:15, and Micah 5:2 the patterns that Jesus's life makes legible. The hermeneutical movement is from the Christ-event back to the Scriptures, not from the Scriptures forward to the Christ-event. This is *figural* reading, in Hays's strict sense, and it stands or falls with the prior Christological claim, not the other way around.

**Unresolved question.** What does Matthew mean by "**he shall be called a Nazarene**" (2:23), and which prophetic source (or sources) does he have in view? The plural "prophets" suggests no single text. **Davies/Allison**'s preferred solution — Isa 11:1 *netzer* (the Davidic branch) layered with the general theme of the despised Messiah — is plausible but not certain. The verse remains the most exegetically opaque of the formula citations.

## The Thread

Matthew 2 reaches back to **Exodus 1–4** (Pharaoh's massacre, Moses's flight, the death of those who sought Moses's life), to **Numbers 24:17** (Balaam's star), to **Hosea 11:1** (Israel's exodus), and to **Jeremiah 31:15** (Rachel's lament — with its unquoted continuation in 31:16–17 as restoration promise). The **flight to Egypt and return** anticipates **Matthew 4:1–11** (the wilderness testing, where Jesus replays Israel's wilderness as the obedient Son), and the geographical climax at **Nazareth** (2:23) sets up **Matthew 4:13–16** (the move to Capernaum, "Galilee of the Gentiles"). Flag forward: **Matthew 27:25** ("his blood be on us and on our children") will revisit the chapter's theme of corporate guilt and innocent blood, and **Matthew 28:16–20** (the Galilean commission) will close the geographical arc that began in Matthew 2:22 with the move to Galilee.
