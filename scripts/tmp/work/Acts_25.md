# Acts 25: Festus, Bernice, and the Appeal to Caesar

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

When **Porcius Festus** arrived in Caesarea in roughly 60 CE, he inherited a province on the verge of revolt. Josephus (*Antiquities* 20.8.9-10; *War* 2.14.1) gives a brief but pointed account of his procuratorship: he died in office after only two years (60-62 CE), but in that short tenure he was generally remembered as more competent and more honest than either his predecessor Felix or his successor Albinus. Tacitus (*Annals*) is silent on Festus's death—a notable omission given his interest in the Eastern provinces—but Josephus says Festus pursued the *sicarii* assassins energetically and broke up several brigand bands that Felix had tolerated. The chronological pivot here is genuinely contested. **F. F. Bruce**'s NICNT and **Schnabel**'s ZECNT both place Festus's accession in 59 or 60 CE, with the most influential argument coming from Eusebius (*Chronicon*) placing the transition in Nero's second year. **Pervo** in Hermeneia, more skeptical of Eusebian chronography, prefers a slightly later date.

Festus's first move (25:1) is the standard *adventus provincialis*: a new governor, three days after assuming office, traveled to the senatorial city in his province to receive the local elite and signal accessibility. Lee Levine's *Caesarea Under Roman Rule* notes that Caesarea was the administrative capital but Jerusalem remained the religious center, and every Roman procurator made some version of this Jerusalem visit early in his tenure. The Sanhedrin's request to have Paul transferred (25:2-3) is the same maneuver they had attempted under Felix two years earlier—an ambush en route. Festus, though new to the province, is alert enough to refuse: the legal point he articulates in 25:16, that Roman custom does not surrender an accused man before he has met his accusers face-to-face, is a precise statement of the Roman legal principle of *cognitio extra ordinem* as it operated in the provinces. **A. N. Sherwin-White**, in *Roman Society and Roman Law in the New Testament* (the foundational study of Acts' legal accuracy), treats this passage at length: Festus is not citing a written statute but the customary procedural framework that governed provincial *cognitio*, and Luke's portrayal is technically exact.

Paul's response in 25:11—"***Kaisara epikaloumai***" (Καίσαρα ἐπικαλοῦμαι)—is one of the most consequential single sentences in the New Testament. The phrase invokes the Roman right of *provocatio*, originally the citizen's appeal against a magistrate's *imperium* in the Republic, by the late Julio-Claudian period transformed into something closer to an appeal *ad Caesarem* available to citizens facing capital charges in provincial *cognitio*. Sherwin-White's chapter on the appeal in *Roman Society and Roman Law* documents how the institution had evolved: by Paul's time, a citizen could shift his case to the imperial tribunal at Rome more or less at will, though the magistrate retained discretion in non-capital matters. Festus's consultation with his *consilium* (25:12) is also procedurally exact—the *consilium principis* in miniature, the advisory council a Roman magistrate was expected to consult before granting an appeal. The verdict "*Kaisara epikeklēsai, epi Kaisara poreusē*"—"to Caesar you have appealed, to Caesar you shall go"—is a formula, not a colloquialism; the construction in Greek mirrors Latin legal phrasing.

The arrival of **Agrippa II and Bernice** (25:13) carries enormous historical weight. **Marcus Julius Agrippa II** (born ca. 27 CE; died ca. 100 CE) was the great-grandson of Herod the Great, son of Agrippa I (whose grim death at Caesarea Luke narrates in 12:21-23), and the last Herodian dynast to hold significant Roman trust. **Daniel R. Schwartz**'s *Agrippa I* and his subsequent work on Agrippa II reconstruct the dynasty's long arc with care: Agrippa II was raised at the imperial court in Rome under Claudius, eventually granted the small kingdom of Chalcis (in Lebanon) and later territory in Galilee and Bashan, including the right to appoint the Jerusalem high priest. He retained the title *basileus*—"king"—which Luke uses at 25:13 and throughout the narrative, even though his actual realm was modest. He would later side with Rome during the revolt of 66-70, attempt unsuccessfully to dissuade the rebels (Josephus reproduces a long speech in *War* 2.16.4), and live out his final decades in Roman favor.

**Bernice** (Greek *Berenikē*; Hebrew likely *Berenice*, from the Macedonian *Pherenikē*) is described by Luke simply as Agrippa's companion. Josephus is more revealing: she was Agrippa II's older sister, married twice (to Marcus Julius Alexander, who died young, and to her uncle Herod of Chalcis), and after Herod's death lived in her brother's household. **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 20.7.3) reports that "*pollos kateichen logous hōs adelphō synousē*"—she "fell under much rumor of being intimate with her brother." She would later become the famous lover of **Titus**, the future emperor, during his command in Judea (Tacitus *Histories* 2.81; Suetonius *Titus* 7); their affair scandalized Rome, and Titus eventually dismissed her—"*Berenicen statim ab urbe dimisit invitus invitam*" (Suetonius)—when he became emperor. Helen Bond's work on Roman women in the New Testament and Ruth Boatwright's archaeological treatment of Berenice's coin portraits both engage the question of how to read Luke's portrayal: he says nothing of the rumors, but the appearance of an unmarried sister in royal pomp at her brother's side, in a Greco-Roman audience that knew the rumors well, is itself a Lukan signal.

The "great pomp" (*pollēs phantasias*) of 25:23 is a small Lukan masterstroke. Joseph Patrich's reconstruction of the praetorium suggests the audience hall (*akroatērion*) was likely in the upper level of the seaside palace complex, opening onto a colonnaded court overlooking the Mediterranean. *Phantasia* is a deliberately weighted Greek term—a pretentious display, a theatrical effect; the same root that gives us "fantasy."

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Acts 25 is the middle panel of the **defense-trilogy** (Felix, Festus, Agrippa) that Tannehill identifies as the Lukan trial-narrative architecture. Within this trilogy, chapter 25 has a peculiar literary function: it does not climax. Both the prosecution and the defense are summarized rather than reproduced; Paul's actual speech is not given (only paraphrased at 25:8). The chapter is structurally a *bridge*, a hinge between the Felix hearing of 24 and the Agrippa speech of 26. The literary energy of 25 is reserved instead for two genre-shifting moments: the **appeal to Caesar** (25:11-12), which redirects the entire remaining narrative trajectory of Acts; and the **Festus-Agrippa private consultation** (25:13-22), which serves as a narrative *retrospective* in which a Roman governor reframes the whole Pauline case for a Jewish king.

This second feature is rare in ancient narrative. **Pervo** in Hermeneia notes that the Festus-to-Agrippa speech (25:14-21) is essentially a doublet—Luke has Festus rehearse for Agrippa what the reader already knows. This kind of internal recapitulation is a Greco-Roman historiographical convention (Polybius and Thucydides do it routinely), but Luke's deployment is sharper: it allows him to offer a Roman characterization of the dispute that is comically hollow—Festus speaks of "*deisidaimonia*" (a delicately ambiguous Greek word that can mean either piety or superstition; *NIV* renders "religion," *NRSV* "religion," but the term carries an edge in Roman ears) and reduces the resurrection of Christ to a procedural footnote: "*peri tinos Iēsou tethnēkotos hon ephasken ho Paulos zēn*"—"about a certain Jesus, dead, whom Paul asserted to be alive" (25:19). Luke is performing dramatic irony on a literary scale: the most consequential proposition in human history is rendered, in Festus's mouth, as an idiom of provincial bafflement.

The chapter also contains one of Luke's most important pieces of **chronological architecture**. Festus tells Agrippa that the prosecutors brought "*aitiamata*" against Paul that "they could not prove" (25:7), and Festus's later admission—"I have nothing definite to write to my lord about him" (25:26)—is a Roman governor's blunt confession that there are no charges to forward. This is a key piece of Luke's apologetic argument throughout Acts: at every Roman judicial encounter, the verdict is non-conviction. **Marguerat** in *The First Christian Historian* reads this consistently across all of Acts: the Lukan-narrative thesis is that Christianity is *not* seditious, and that Roman jurisprudence, when it functions properly, recognizes this. The thesis culminates here, in a chapter where the governor declares to a king that he has nothing to write.

There is also the structural pivot of *Kaisara epikaloumai*. **Tannehill** observes that this single utterance retroactively reframes everything Paul has done since 19:21 ("after I have been there [Jerusalem], I must also see Rome"). The reader has known since the middle of Acts that Rome is the destination; the appeal-to-Caesar transforms the destination from a missionary aspiration into a juridical certainty. Luke's careful shaping of the trial sequence is thereby revealed as a long ramp toward this single sentence. Once it is spoken, every subsequent character (Festus, Agrippa, Julius the centurion, the ship's captain) becomes a means to the destination already decreed. The narrative of Acts becomes, from 25:11 forward, a transit narrative.

A subtler literary detail: **Bernice's silence**. She is named four times in Acts 25-26 (25:13, 23; 26:30) but never speaks. **Susan Marie Praeder** has noted Luke's tendency to give voice to women elsewhere in his narrative (Mary, Elizabeth, Anna, Tabitha, Lydia, Priscilla, the daughters of Philip). Bernice's silence—standing beside her brother in royal pomp, scandal-laden—is conspicuous. Whether the silence is reverential, prudent, or deliberately ironic is debated, but Johnson (Sacra Pagina) reads it as a Lukan refusal to give a Herodian woman the dignity of speech in the trial of an apostle. The Herodians appear and disappear from Luke-Acts as silent royal pomp; Mary's *Magnificat* belongs to a different family altogether.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Read against the Pauline letters, Acts 25 confronts us with a question of historical recovery. Paul's own writings are silent on the appeal to Caesar—he never refers to it in any extant letter, even though some scholars place 2 Timothy and the Pastoral Epistles after this point in his life. **N. T. Wright** in *Paul: A Biography* takes this silence as compatible with the Acts narrative: Paul's letters are pastoral, not autobiographical, and the absence of legal detail is not the absence of legal fact. **F. F. Bruce** in *Paul: Apostle of the Heart Set Free* treats the appeal as historically secure, supported by the Roman legal framework Sherwin-White reconstructs, the consistency of Lukan procedural detail, and the absence of any plausible motive for Lukan invention. Pervo and Conzelmann are more skeptical, treating the appeal as a Lukan literary device that produces the Roman ending, but neither offers a fully developed alternative reconstruction.

The Jewish-interpretive trajectory is curious here. Most contemporary Jewish scholars (Reinhartz, Boyarin, Levine, Schiffman) have less to say about Acts 25 specifically than about Acts 26 or 28, since chapter 25 is procedural rather than theological. But the appearance of Agrippa II and Bernice does occasion important historical reflection. **Daniel Schwartz** in his work on the Herodian dynasty notes that Agrippa II was the last Jewish ruler to hold the kind of Roman trust that allowed appointment of the Jerusalem high priest—after his death, that prerogative would not return to Jewish hands. The Acts 25-26 portrait of Agrippa II is therefore an unusual primary source: a contemporaneous depiction (assuming Lukan composition by 80-90 CE) of a Herodian whose political position is liminal between Jewish self-understanding and Roman service. Pamela Eisenbaum's *Paul Was Not a Christian* engages this point obliquely: the Pauline self-understanding presented in chapter 26—still to come in the narrative—presupposes that an audience including a Jewish king and his sister can hear Paul's gospel as inner-Jewish discourse. Acts 25, with its careful staging of "the king" attending the hearing, sets up that presupposition.

Patristic interpretation focused most closely on **Bernice**. **Chrysostom** in his *Homilies on Acts* devotes parts of two homilies (Hom. 51-52) to her presence, treating her as a moral counterweight: where Drusilla (chapter 24) is the absent wife of a Roman freedman, Bernice is the present sister of a Jewish king, and Chrysostom finds in her an emblem of royal vanity. Bede follows Chrysostom in this typological reading. **Calvin** in his *Commentary on Acts* is harsher: Bernice is "a woman of infamous and incestuous life," and her "great pomp" is the visible marker of corrupt royalty contrasted with the true royal authority of the imprisoned apostle. Luther's fragmentary lecture notes on Acts continue this contrast.

Modern commentary has converged on several focal points. **Bruce NICNT** treats the chapter primarily as a chronological fix-point (the Felix-to-Festus transition is one of the key dates in NT chronology). **Barrett ICC** focuses on the legal procedure and on Luke's apparent familiarity with Roman provincial *cognitio*. **Fitzmyer AB** and **Johnson Sacra Pagina** both note the ironic juxtaposition of "great pomp" and the imprisoned apostle, and both see in the chapter a Lukan theology of *power inversion*—the kingdom of God appearing in chains while the kings of the earth appear in fanfare. **Gaventa Abingdon** is particularly attentive to how Luke uses the chapter to demonstrate *Roman-Jewish* relational complexity: a Roman governor consults a Jewish king on a Jewish theological dispute, the king (despite Roman tutelage) responds with curiosity rather than dismissal, and the prisoner—a Roman citizen and a Jewish Pharisee—is the only competent theologian in the room. **Keener**, with his customary thoroughness, devotes substantial space to the Roman appellate procedure and its historical attestation across Cicero, the Digest, and the imperial-period legal sources.

**Brian Rapske** in *Paul in Roman Custody* addresses the most thorny historical question raised by chapter 25: what *exactly* did appeal to Caesar trigger? Did Paul receive the imperial tribunal's full *cognitio*? Did his case ever come before Nero personally? Rapske notes that by the early Neronian period, the bulk of imperial appeals were handled by the *praefectus praetorio* or by an imperial *delegatus*—not by Caesar in person—so the colloquial "Paul appealed to Caesar" should be understood as "Paul shifted his case from provincial cognitio to the imperial system." The implications for Acts 28's "two whole years" become clearer: Paul is not awaiting a personal audience with Nero; he is awaiting his place in a queue of provincial appeals, with no clear timeline.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Why does Paul appeal to Caesar (25:11) when the appeal will likely cost him his life?** Three positions. (1) The *providential-divine-plan* reading (Bruce, Marshall, Fitzmyer): Paul appeals because he has long known he must "see Rome" (19:21), confirmed by the dominical word in Acts 23:11 ("as you have testified to the facts about me in Jerusalem, so you must testify also in Rome"); the appeal is the means God has provided to fulfill the prophetic word; Paul is acting in conscious obedience to a charted destiny. (2) The *strategic-evangelical* reading (Witherington, Schnabel): the appeal is Paul's calculated decision to use his Roman citizenship as a vehicle for the gospel; faced with a likely return to Jerusalem and almost certain assassination, the only path open to him for reaching Rome with the gospel is through the Roman appellate system; the appeal is missiological pragmatism. (3) The *rights-based-juridical* reading (Sherwin-White, Conzelmann): the appeal is fundamentally a citizen's exercise of legal right against a procedurally compromised provincial proceeding; Paul, recognizing that Festus is "wishing to do the Jews a favor" (25:9) and is therefore unable to render impartial verdict, exercises his juridical privilege and shifts his case to a higher tribunal; the move is legal, not theological.

Steelman: the providential reading has the most direct textual support (the prior Lukan signaling at 19:21 and 23:11), but the rights-based reading has the most precise procedural fit—Paul names his juridical reasoning in 25:10-11 itself ("if there is nothing to their charges against me, no one can give me up to them").

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter resists the choice between divine sovereignty and human agency that later Christian tradition tends to enforce. Paul exercises a Roman right because he has been told he must see Rome. Both clauses are simultaneously true. The Lukan theology is one in which providence operates *through* legal-political means, not *around* them; the citizenship Paul received from a Roman administrative office becomes the conduit for a kingdom announcement.

**Tension 2: What is the theological function of "great pomp" (25:23)?** Three positions. (1) The *power-inversion* reading (most patristic, much modern devotional commentary): the chapter contrasts Roman-Jewish royal pageantry with the genuine royal authority of the imprisoned apostle; the visible kingdom is empty, the invisible kingdom is full; the literary detail of "*phantasia*" is a Lukan judgment on imperial display. (2) The *missiological-staging* reading (Tannehill, Marguerat): the gospel is being brought before "kings and rulers" precisely as Jesus prophesied (Luke 21:12-13); the pomp is the platform; Luke wants the reader to see that the audience for the Pauline gospel includes the highest political authorities the late Second Temple period could produce. (3) The *historical-realism* reading (Pervo, Haenchen): the pomp is incidental Lukan color, period detail rather than theological commentary; Luke is showing what a provincial royal *adventus* looked like.

Steelman: the missiological-staging reading does the most work, because the actual content of chapter 26—Paul's longest speech in Acts—follows immediately, and Luke clearly wants the pomp to set up the speech rather than to function as standalone irony.

**Where this leaves us:** Luke is interested in the sociological reach of the gospel. The same gospel that began with shepherds at Bethlehem now stands before the last Herodian king and his Roman-tied sister. The pomp is the platform; the speech that follows is the point.

**One unresolved question:** Did Paul ever appear before Nero in person? The Acts narrative ends (28:30-31) without recording an imperial hearing, and the Pauline letter corpus does not address it. Tradition—1 Clement 5:7, the *Acts of Paul* and second-century apocrypha, Eusebius—presumes a hearing and a release, followed by further travel and a second imprisonment ending in execution. The evidence is thin. The text leaves the question open.

## The Thread

The "you must see Rome" arc begun at 19:21 ("after I have been there, I must also see Rome") and confirmed at 23:11 ("you must testify also in Rome") reaches procedural confirmation at 25:12 ("to Caesar you shall go"). Cross-reference Romans 1:11-15, where Paul writes from Corinth several years earlier of his long-standing intention to come to Rome. Cross-reference also Luke 21:12-13 ("you will be brought before kings and governors for my name's sake"), which Acts 25-26 enacts with unusual literalness. Flag the dual chronological frame: the "two whole years" of 24:27 (Caesarea) is about to be paralleled by the "two whole years" of 28:30 (Rome).
