# 1 Samuel 15: The Ban, the Regret, and the Torn Robe

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

1 Samuel 15 is the chapter in which the institution of *cherem* — the war-ban, total devotion of an enemy population and its property to YHWH — collides with the political realities of Saul's kingship and the moral conscience of every later reader. The historical setting is the southern frontier of Israelite settlement, on the edge of the Negev. Saul musters at **Telaim**, probably in the southern Judean hill country (the location is uncertain; some identify it with Telem in Joshua 15:24). He moves against **Amalek** in the territory the text describes as "from Havilah to Shur, east of Egypt" — that is, the arid steppe and northern Sinai zone where the Amalekites lived as a confederation of nomadic and semi-nomadic groups.

The **Amalekites** are presented in the Hebrew Bible as Israel's archetypal external enemy, the nation that attacked Israel at **Rephidim** during the wilderness journey (Exod 17:8–16) and that is therefore the object of a perpetual divine war (Deut 25:17–19). Historically, the Amalekites are difficult to fix in extrabiblical evidence. **Nadav Na'aman**, **Anson Rainey**, and others have surveyed the very limited evidence: an Egyptian text from the 19th Dynasty mentions a region called *‘Amu* in the eastern Delta, and various Iron Age toponyms in the Negev have been associated with Amalekite territory, but no single inscription names "Amalek" outside the biblical corpus until much later. The Amalekites appear to have been a tribal confederation centered in the northeastern Sinai and southern Negev, raiding the agricultural margins of Judah and the trade routes between Egypt and the Levant.

The chapter mentions the **Kenites** (15:6), a related tribal group whose ancestor (Hobab/Jethro, Moses's father-in-law in Judges 4:11 and elsewhere) had shown kindness to Israel during the wilderness wanderings. Saul's warning to the Kenites to depart before the strike is one of the few moments in the *cherem* literature where humanitarian concession to non-target populations is actually narrated. The Kenites in this period were associated with **metallurgy** (the etymology of *qayin*, קַיִן, "smith") and had settled in the southern Judean wilderness near Arad.

The figure of **Agag** is, in some readings, a dynastic title rather than a personal name, comparable to "Pharaoh" in Egypt or "Caesar" in Rome. **Numbers 24:7** mentions a future king of Israel exalted "above Agag," suggesting "Agag" was a recognized title of Amalekite kings. The ANE practice of preserving captured kings alive for ritual humiliation, public display, or trade in royal prisoners is well-attested in **Assyrian** royal annals (Tiglath-pileser III, Sargon II, Ashurbanipal all describe leading captured kings in chains). Saul's preservation of Agag is thus consistent with normal ANE military practice; the problem is that it is *not* consistent with what YHWH commanded.

The institution of **cherem** (חֵרֶם, "ban, devotion-to-destruction") is itself a recognizable ANE phenomenon. The **Mesha Stele** (Moabite Stone, ca. 840 BCE), discovered at Dhiban in 1868 by F.A. Klein, contains the only extra-biblical attestation of *cherem* in West Semitic languages: King Mesha of Moab boasts that he "devoted (*hrm*) to destruction" the Israelite town of Nebo for his god **Chemosh**. The institution exists as a real cultic-military category across the southern Levant; it is not a uniquely Israelite invention.

Six Hebrew terms are essential. **Cherem** (חֵרֶם, 15:3, 8, 9, 15, 18, 20, 21) — total devotion to destruction; cognate with the noun for the consecrated/forbidden zone (cf. Arabic *haram*). **Niham** (נִחַם, 15:11, 29, 35) — "to relent, regret, repent, change one's mind." This verb appears three times in this chapter in deeply contradictory ways and is the theological center of gravity. **Mered** (מֶרֶד) and **mara'** (מָרָה) — the family of "rebellion" terms in 15:23. **Qesem** (קֶסֶם, "divination") and **terapim** (תְּרָפִים, household idols) — the comparison set in 15:23. **Padah** does not appear here (no ransom for Saul). **Niham** carries the chapter; everything turns on what it means.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: this is the **second and final rejection-narrative** of Saul. **Polzin** in *Samuel and the Deuteronomist* and **McCarter** (Anchor Bible) both read chapters 13 and 15 as a deliberate doublet: the first rejection (announced) and the second (enacted). The narrative form is **prophetic-juridical confrontation** — Samuel pronounces YHWH's verdict and Saul attempts to defend himself, with the verdict ratified at the end.

The chapter has a clear structure: divine command (15:1–3); Saul's mission and partial obedience (15:4–9); divine regret (15:10–11); Samuel confronts Saul (15:12–23); Saul's confessions and Samuel's refusal (15:24–31); Samuel executes Agag (15:32–33); Samuel and Saul part forever (15:34–35). The **chiasm** of confessions is striking — Saul confesses twice (15:24, 30), and each confession Samuel meets with a different response. The first confession, Samuel rejects ("the LORD has rejected you"). The second, Samuel grants the political dignity of a public bow. The chapter is examining what *real* repentance looks like and what it does not.

The poetry of 15:22–23, the **"to obey is better than sacrifice"** oracle, is one of the high points of prophetic literature in 1 Samuel. The form is characteristic Hebrew poetic parallelism, with chiastic structure and an ascending intensity from "burnt offerings and sacrifices" through "obeying the voice" to "fat of rams" — a deliberate climbing of the sacrificial system from animals to organs. Then in 15:23 the comparison is flipped: rebellion (a sin of action) is compared to divination (a sin of religion); presumption (a sin of attitude) is compared to *terapim* (idolatry). The argument is that disobedience is *itself* a form of false worship — Saul has been doing the religion of sacrifice (15:21) while practicing the religion of his own will (15:9). This is one of the great prophetic insights, repeated in different idiom by Hosea (6:6), Amos (5:21–24), Micah (6:6–8), Isaiah (1:11–17), and the New Testament (Mark 12:33).

The **textual issue** at 15:27–28 is significant. The Hebrew is ambiguous about who tears whose robe. The MT can be read as Saul tearing Samuel's robe ("Saul seized the skirt of his robe, and it tore"), but **4QSam-a** and the LXX preserve readings that some scholars (McCarter, Tov) interpret as describing Samuel tearing his own robe in a sign-act. The ambiguity may be deliberate: the *kingdom is torn* (15:28), and it does not matter which hand did the literal tearing because the symbolic meaning is the same. Robert Alter notes that the same root *qara'* (קָרַע, "to tear") will reappear when **Ahijah the Shilonite** tears his garment into twelve pieces to symbolize the division of the kingdom under Rehoboam (1 Kgs 11:30). The torn robe at Gilgal in 1 Sam 15 is the prophetic origin of an entire theology of fractured kingship.

Place in the book argument: chapter 15 is the **definitive end of the Saul story** in narrative-theological terms. The next chapter introduces David. From this point forward, Saul is a king without a future, a man who continues to rule but whose kingship has been canceled in heaven. **Brueggemann** has argued that 15:35 — "Samuel did not see Saul again until the day of his death" — is one of the saddest verses in the Old Testament, marking the permanent rupture of the prophet-king relationship that the institution of monarchy was supposed to embody.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical arc, 1 Samuel 15 stands at the intersection of three trajectories: the **Amalekite war** (Exod 17:14–16, Deut 25:17–19, eventually Esther's Haman the "Agagite"); the **theology of niham** (Gen 6:6, Exod 32:14, Num 23:19, 1 Sam 15:11/29/35, Jer 18:8, Joel 2:13, Jonah 3:10, Mal 3:6); and the **prophetic critique of sacrifice without obedience** (Hosea 6:6, Amos 5, Micah 6, Isaiah 1, Mark 12:33). Each of these threads pulls the chapter into long canonical conversations.

The **Jewish interpretive tradition** has read this chapter with extraordinary intensity, partly because Amalek became a paradigmatic theological category in Jewish thought (the Talmud's treatment of the *amalek* question in **b. Sanhedrin 96b**, **b. Megillah 13a**, and elsewhere). **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 6.131–155) presents Saul's failure as the corruption of royal magnanimity — he kept Agag out of misplaced courtly courtesy. The **rabbis** debated extensively whether the *cherem* command was meant to be perpetually applicable or was historically conditioned; the Maimonidean tradition (*Mishneh Torah*, Hilkhot Melakhim 5:4–5) treats the genocidal command as historically specific, not as a model for any subsequent action. **Rashi** glosses 15:11 ("I regret") with the careful note that *niham* with respect to YHWH is anthropomorphic — God speaks in human idiom but is not subject to human change. **Radak** offers a sustained discussion of 15:11 versus 15:29: the same Hebrew root *niham* is used to describe YHWH's regret over making Saul king (15:11, 35) and to insist that "the Glory of Israel will not lie or have regret" (15:29). Radak's solution: the verb in 15:29 means "lie/recant" in the sense of taking back a stated decision; the verb in 15:11 means "regret" in the sense of grieving an outcome. Two senses, same root, both intentionally placed in the same chapter.

**Ralbag** elaborates the philosophical question further: divine immutability requires that God not change knowledge or will, but it does not require that the relational quality of God's response to a free creature remain unchanged. **Abarbanel** offers a politically attuned reading: Saul's failure is not random; it shows the structural problem that kingship cannot subordinate itself to prophecy when its political incentives are pulling the other direction. Saul says "I feared the people" (15:24) — exactly the structural pathology that Samuel had warned about in chapter 8. **Avivah Zornberg**, **Yair Zakovitch**, and **Moshe Garsiel** all discuss the chapter's literary and theological intricacies in modern Jewish scholarship.

The **Christian tradition** has been preoccupied with two questions in this chapter: the *niham* problem and the moral problem of the *cherem*. **Origen** and **Augustine** both addressed *niham* extensively. Origen's homilies on 1 Samuel treat YHWH's "regret" as a divine accommodation to human capacity — God speaks in human registers without being subject to human limits. Augustine in *City of God* and elsewhere develops what becomes the classical Christian doctrine: God's knowledge and will are eternally one, but the *outcomes* in time include genuine creaturely action that grieves God in the relational mode without altering God's eternal knowledge. **Chrysostom** focuses on the moral example of Saul's deflection ("the people made me do it"). **Gregory the Great** treats Saul's partial obedience as the type of every Christian's compromised devotion. **Aquinas** (*Summa* I, q.19, a.7 on the will of God) cites 1 Sam 15:29 as central to the doctrine of divine immutability; the apparent regret in 15:11 is interpreted, on the Thomistic model, as the eternal disposition of God toward sin manifested in time.

The **Reformation** intensified the *niham* debate. **Calvin's sermons on 1 Samuel** address the apparent contradiction directly: God's eternal counsel is unchangeable, but God's covenantal relations include genuine response to human action; the regret of 15:11 is real to God in the way that human regret is real to humans, without compromising divine perfection. Calvin's resolution is, broadly, the classical Reformed position. **Luther** preached on this chapter as a meditation on the pretensions of the legal-righteous Saul — the man who thinks he has obeyed because he has done some of what was commanded.

The **modern theological debate** over *niham* is sharper. **Bruce Ware** in *God's Greater Glory* defends classical immutability against the openness of God movement, arguing that 15:29 must control 15:11 (the relenting language is anthropopathic). **Greg Boyd**, **Clark Pinnock**, and other **open theists** read 15:11 as showing that God's foreknowledge is not exhaustive of free creaturely choice — God genuinely responds to Saul's failure, and the "regret" is not metaphorical. **Process theologians** push further. The ESV's translation choices (15:29: "will not lie or have regret"; 15:11, 35: "I regret … the LORD regretted") show the difficulty: the same verb has to be translated differently depending on which theological reading is adopted. The chapter does not settle the question, and refusing to settle it may be the chapter's point.

The **moral problem of the *cherem*** is the other massive Christian conversation. **Eric Seibert** in *Disturbing Divine Behavior*, **Susan Niditch** in *War in the Hebrew Bible*, and **Ellen Davis** in *Opening Israel's Scriptures* all engage directly. Seibert and Boyd take the most critical line: the genocidal command cannot be reconciled with the character of God revealed in Christ; it represents the human authors' projection of war ideology onto God. Niditch traces the *cherem* tradition historically and culturally without dismissing its moral weight. Davis, more cautiously, reads the *cherem* as a real theological category that the canon itself critiques (the Jonah story; the inclusion of Rahab the Canaanite in the line of David; the subversive presence of Ruth the Moabite in the same line). **C.S. Lewis** in *Reflections on the Psalms* and **Chris Wright** in *The God I Don't Understand* both attempt theological-pastoral resolutions; neither pretends the problem is easy.

**Critical scholarship**. **Wellhausen** assigned chapter 15 to a Dtr stratum reflecting the Northern prophetic tradition's anti-Saul polemic. **Cross** confirmed the textual situation with 4QSam-a. **McCarter** and **Klein** treat the chapter as a coherent rejection-narrative possibly composed in stages but reflecting a strong prophetic-Northern theological commitment.

**Contemporary readers**. **Brueggemann** centers the chapter as the moral tragedy of Saul: the man whose only way to keep his kingdom is by total obedience, and whose own political instincts make total obedience impossible. **Polzin** reads the chapter structurally as the second member of the rejection-doublet. **David Gunn** in *The Fate of King Saul* offers his most sympathetic reading: Saul's preservation of the spoils for sacrifice is not unreasonable; the chapter shows YHWH demanding obedience that human moral and political reasoning cannot fully understand. **Eugene Peterson** in *Leap Over a Wall* draws pastoral lessons. **Ellen Davis** insists on the moral seriousness of the *cherem* problem. **John Goldingay** stresses both the literary and the theological complexities, and like Davis refuses to allegorize away the explicit inclusion of "woman, child and infant" in the destruction command — the moral burden of these texts, on this reading, must be borne rather than dissolved.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Divine *Niham* and Divine Immutability.** The chapter says, in the same set of words, that YHWH "regretted that he had made Saul king" (15:11, 35) and that YHWH "is not a man, that he should have regret" (15:29). Three positions: (a) **Classical immutability**, the Augustinian/Thomistic/Calvinist consensus: 15:29 controls; the *niham* of 15:11 and 15:35 is anthropopathic, a creaturely-affective register that describes God's eternal disposition as it is experienced in time. (b) **Open theism / relational view**, defended by Boyd, Pinnock, and others: 15:11 and 15:35 are theologically substantive, not merely figurative; God genuinely responds to Saul's failure with grief and a changed plan; 15:29 means only that God will not retract the specific judgment just announced (Saul's rejection is final), not that God is incapable of any kind of change. (c) **Dialectical view**, defended by Karl Barth and developed by Brueggemann: God's faithfulness is unchangeable but God's relational engagement is real; the apparent contradiction is the surface of a deeper truth that classical immutability cannot fully express and open theism risks underweighting. Steelman: position (a) preserves the doctrine of God across the canon; position (b) takes the textual surface most seriously; position (c) holds the tension without resolving it. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter does not adjudicate. It places the two statements side by side and demands that the reader hold both. This is the Bible's habitual mode on the deepest questions about God.

**Tension Two: The Moral Status of the *Cherem*.** Three positions: (a) **Divine command sufficient**, the position of the chapter's surface and of much classical interpretation: YHWH commanded, the command was just because YHWH's commands are constitutive of justice, and the moral problem dissolves into trust in divine wisdom. (b) **Historically conditioned**, the Maimonidean and most modern moderate position: the *cherem* command was specific to a particular ANE situation in which Israel's religious-cultural identity was vulnerable to absorption by surrounding populations; it is not transferable to any modern context, and even within the canon it is repeatedly subverted (Rahab, Ruth, Jonah). (c) **Morally problematic, theologically critiqued by the canon itself**, the Davis/Wright/Seibert/Boyd line: the *cherem* texts pose a real moral problem that requires honest engagement; Christ's command to love enemies is the canonical critique of the *cherem* logic; the New Testament does not extend the *cherem* but ends it. Steelman: each position has serious advocates and serious problems. Position (a) protects divine sovereignty but at heavy moral cost; position (b) is intellectually elegant but risks domesticating the text; position (c) takes moral seriousness most seriously but must explain why YHWH commanded it in the first place. **Where this leaves us:** Christians cannot read 1 Samuel 15 as simple prescriptive moral instruction. We must hold it as part of the long canonical conversation in which the New Testament's command to love enemies is itself in conversation with the *cherem* that defined Saul's failure.

**One unresolved question:** Saul confesses, twice. He bows, eventually. He grieves the rest of his life. But "the LORD regretted that he had made Saul king" (15:35) and Samuel does not see him again. What is the relationship between repentance and the irreversibility of consequences? The chapter does not say. It only says that some doors, once closed, do not open again — and that even YHWH grieves the closing.

## The Thread

- **Exodus 17:8–16** — The original Amalekite attack at Rephidim and the perpetual war declared. The *cherem* of 1 Sam 15 is the deferred enactment of that ancient command.
- **Deuteronomy 25:17–19** — The repeated charge to "blot out the memory of Amalek." Saul's failure to do so transfers the obligation forward; the line continues to **Esther 3:1**, where Haman the **Agagite** appears — a literary descendant of the king Saul spared.
- **Genesis 6:6 / Exodus 32:14 / Jonah 3:10** — The other major *niham* texts. 1 Sam 15 must be read in their company.
- **Hosea 6:6 / Mark 12:33** — The prophetic-evangelical line continued from Samuel's "to obey is better than sacrifice." The verse is one of the most consequential in the whole prophetic tradition.
- **1 Kings 11:29–31** — Ahijah the Shilonite tears his robe to symbolize the division of the kingdom. The torn robe of 1 Sam 15:27–28 is the prophetic precedent. **Flag forward**: kingdom-tearing is the Bible's grammar for political judgment.
- **1 Samuel 28** — Saul, in his final hour, will seek YHWH and find no answer; will go to Endor; will hear from the dead Samuel the same verdict pronounced here. The end of Saul is already written in this chapter.
