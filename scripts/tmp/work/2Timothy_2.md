# 2 Timothy 2: Soldier, Athlete, Farmer — and the Resurrection That Already Happened

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The second chapter of 2 Timothy moves from the testamentary opening into vocational training. If the letter is genuinely Pauline, written from a Roman cell in the mid-60s during or after the Neronian persecution Tacitus describes in *Annals* 15.44, then the soldier-athlete-farmer triad of 2:3-7 lands with grim irony: Paul writes "bound with chains as a criminal" (2:9) about the costs of fidelity to a movement Rome was actively crucifying and burning. Suetonius, *Nero* 16, calls the Christians "a class of men given to a new and mischievous superstition." The chains in 2:9 are likely literal Roman *catenae*, of the kind referenced in Acts 28:16 and 28:20. By comparison, if the letter is pseudonymous and dates to the 90s-130s — the era of Domitian's late-reign tensions or Pliny's letter to Trajan (*Epistles* 10.96, c. 112) about Christians who refused to sacrifice to the imperial cult — the chapter functions as a generalized theology of post-apostolic suffering rather than eyewitness reportage.

The three vocational images Paul deploys map directly onto first-century Roman material culture. The Roman *miles*, the legionary, was on oath (the *sacramentum*) to obey his commander absolutely; the regulation against being "entangled in civilian pursuits" (2:4) reflects the Roman legal principle that soldiers could not engage in trade or hold office during their term of service (the lex Iulia de re militari and earlier Republican statutes preserved in Justinian's *Digest* 49.16). Athletic crowning (2:5) maps onto the Greek games — Olympia, Isthmia, Nemea, Pythia — where the *stephanos* was a wreath of olive, pine, celery, or laurel, and disqualification for rule-breaking was a public ritual; Pausanias's *Description of Greece* documents the elaborate oath-taking and policing of contestants. The hard-working farmer (2:6) reflects the agrarian baseline of Roman provincial life; Cato the Elder's *De Agri Cultura* and Columella's *De Re Rustica* describe the labor and the deserved priority of the laboring farmer in dividing the harvest.

The over-realized eschatology of Hymenaeus and Philetus (2:17-18) — "saying that the resurrection has already happened" — has clear historical resonance. Among the Nag Hammadi codices recovered in 1945, the *Treatise on the Resurrection* (NHC I,4, also called the *Letter to Rheginos*, late 2nd c.) makes precisely this claim: "Already you have the resurrection… Why not consider yourself as risen?" The Valentinian and broader proto-Gnostic movements interpreted resurrection as a present spiritual ascent rather than a future bodily event. Hymenaeus is named again at 1 Timothy 1:20, where Paul says he has "handed him over to Satan that he may learn not to blaspheme" — an early instance of the disciplinary excommunication Paul also describes in 1 Corinthians 5:5.

Textual witnesses for the chapter are stable. Codex Sinaiticus א (4th c.) and Codex Alexandrinus A (5th c.) preserve it in full; Codex Vaticanus B notoriously breaks off in Hebrews and lacks the Pastorals (Bart Ehrman flags this in *Forged*; Bruce Metzger and Daniel Wallace argue physical loss is the better explanation than deliberate exclusion). The faithful saying in 2:11-13 has unusual formatting in some early manuscripts that strongly suggests it was a pre-existing hymnic or liturgical fragment.

Five Greek key terms anchor the chapter: στρατιώτης *stratiōtēs* (soldier, 2:3); ἀθλέω *athleō* (to compete athletically, 2:5); γεωργός *geōrgos* (farmer, 2:6); ὀρθοτομέω *orthotomeō* (rightly handle, "cut straight," 2:15 — etymological root of the word *orthodoxy*); ἀνάστασις ἤδη γέγονεν *anastasis ēdē gegonen* (the resurrection has already happened, 2:18).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

As testamentary letter, 2 Timothy 2 is the chapter where the dying mentor delivers vocational metaphor and creedal anchor — both standard elements of farewell literature. Margaret Mitchell, Loveday Alexander, Benjamin Fiore, and Abraham Malherbe have all noted that Greco-Roman moral exhortation regularly used triplets of professional analogues. Plutarch's *Moralia* (e.g. *How to Profit from One's Enemies*) and Epictetus's *Discourses* (especially 3.15, where Epictetus tells students to count the cost like an athlete or soldier) provide direct parallels. Cynic-Stoic diatribe deployed the *peristasis* catalog and the athletic metaphor heavily; Seneca's *Epistulae* and Musonius Rufus's surviving fragments do likewise. Abraham Malherbe's *Paul and the Popular Philosophers* documents the overlap. The *stratiōtēs–athlētēs–geōrgos* triad is therefore not an idiosyncratic Pauline invention but a recognizable rhetorical signature of the period's moral instruction. What is distinctive is the way the Pastoral author yokes these images to a specifically apostolic and ecclesial vocation: Timothy is not a generic philosophical disciple but a custodian of the *parathēkē* introduced in chapter 1.

The chapter's literary center of gravity is the faithful saying in 2:11-13, marked by the formula πιστὸς ὁ λόγος *pistos ho logos* — one of five such markers in the Pastorals (1 Tim 1:15, 3:1, 4:9; 2 Tim 2:11; Tit 3:8). The structure is chiastic and tightly poetic in the Greek:

— if we have died with him, we will also live with him;
— if we endure, we will also reign with him;
— if we deny him, he also will deny us;
— if we are faithless, he remains faithful — for he cannot deny himself.

Mounce, Marshall, Towner, Knight, and Luke Timothy Johnson all read this as a pre-existing hymnic or baptismal fragment incorporated by the author. The first two clauses are positive parallel ("died with"/"live with"; "endure"/"reign with"); the third turns negative with strict reciprocity ("deny"/"deny"); and the fourth breaks the parallelism by refusing the symmetry — God's faithfulness does not match our faithlessness. The last clause ("for he cannot deny himself") supplies a metaphysical ground rather than a behavioral mirror, and that asymmetry is the theological hinge of the entire saying.

Other literary features: the medical metaphor of "gangrene" *gangraina* (2:17) for false teaching is a Hellenistic medical commonplace (Galen, Hippocratic corpus); the "great house with vessels of gold and silver, wood and clay" (2:20) is a deliberate echo of Jeremiah 18 and Romans 9:21 — the Pauline image of God as potter; the "snare of the devil" (παγίς τοῦ διαβόλου *pagis tou diabolou*, 2:26) is Septuagintal language drawn from Psalm 91 (LXX 90):3 and Proverbs 22:25 LXX. The quotation in 2:19 is layered: "The Lord knows those who are his" comes from Numbers 16:5 LXX (the Korah rebellion narrative — God identifies his elect against rebels), with possible secondary echoes of Sirach 17:26 and Isaiah 26:13 LXX. The Korah subtext is striking: the author treats Hymenaeus and Philetus as a new Korah, an internal rebellion against God-appointed leadership.

Textual variants in the chapter are minor. Some witnesses at 2:14 read "before the Lord" (κυρίου) rather than "before God" (θεοῦ), and at 2:19 a few late witnesses harmonize the second seal-quotation toward Isaiah 52:11; neither variant is doctrinally significant.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical NT arc — Israel's covenant, Jesus' messianic enactment, the Spirit's outpouring, the Church's mission — 2 Timothy 2 sits at the moment when the church begins to have to police its own boundaries. The faithful saying in 2:11-13 is one of the earliest creedal compressions of cross-and-resurrection-and-eschaton theology; the Hymenaeus-Philetus polemic is one of the earliest documented battles within a Christian community over what counts as right teaching.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic readers seized the chapter for ecclesiology and moral theology. John Chrysostom's *Homilies on 2 Timothy* (Homilies 4-6) preached the soldier-athlete-farmer triad as a manual for clergy and laity alike. Tertullian's *De Corona* and *Ad Martyras* deploy the *miles Christi* image extensively, and the language of "soldier of Christ" becomes a foundational metaphor for monastic life by the time of the *Rule of St. Benedict* (Prologue: *militaturus Domino Christo vero Regi*, "about to do battle for the Lord Christ the true King"). Origen's *Against Celsus* uses 2:25 ("correcting opponents with gentleness") to defend the Christian preference for argument over coercion. Augustine in *Confessions* and *City of God* repeatedly cites 2:19 — "the Lord knows those who are his" — as the seal of the *ecclesia invisibilis*, the mixed church whose true members God alone discerns.

Medieval readers extended the soldiering image into knightly piety (the *Imitation of Christ*; Bernard of Clairvaux's *In Praise of the New Knighthood* on the Templars). Aquinas's *Lectura* on 2 Timothy treats the over-realized eschatology of Hymenaeus and Philetus as a heresy against the *resurrectio carnis* — the bodily resurrection — and ties 2:19 to the theology of the elect.

Reformation readers found 2:19 magnetic. Luther preached it for assurance of election; Calvin in his *Institutes* III.21-24 cited it on the doctrines of grace; the Westminster Confession 3.5-6 echoes its sealing language. The Magdeburg Centuries (1559-1574) used 2:14-18 to indict Roman teaching as the spread of "gangrene." Roman Catholic responses, including Bellarmine's *De Controversiis*, returned the polemic by reading 2:15's *orthotomeō* as requiring Magisterial interpretation.

Critical scholarship from F.C. Baur (1835) and H.J. Holtzmann (1880) onward read 2:17-18 as evidence of late-first or second-century anti-Gnostic polemic, citing the resemblance to the *Treatise on the Resurrection* and to passages in Irenaeus's *Against Heresies* (1.23-24) describing Menander, Saturninus, and other figures who taught that resurrection is a present spiritual reality. Martin Dibelius and Hans Conzelmann's Hermeneia commentary canonized this dating. Bart Ehrman in *Lost Christianities* and *Forged* extends the reading. The middle position — that proto-Gnostic over-realized eschatology has roots reaching into the 50s and 60s and is already detectable in 1 Corinthians 4:8 ("already you have come to reign without us") and in the Corinthian denial of bodily resurrection (1 Cor 15:12) — is held by Mounce, Marshall, Towner, Johnson, and Larry Hurtado. On this reading, Hymenaeus and Philetus are not an anachronism but a continuation of the Corinthian dispute. James Dunn and Richard Bauckham have noted that the bodily-resurrection question was already live in the apostolic generation.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** The chapter sits inside a larger Jewish-Christian conversation about how a covenant community handles internal heresy. The "delivered to Satan" language at 1 Tim 1:20 (echoed by the Hymenaeus reference here) parallels the rabbinic *cherem* — the formal ban — and the Qumran *yachad*'s discipline (*1QS* 6-9). The Korah-rebellion citation in 2:19 is striking from a Jewish-reception standpoint: the author treats Numbers 16 not merely as backstory but as a typological lens for present ecclesial discipline. *Pirkei Avot* 5:17 contrasts the dispute of Hillel and Shammai (which was "for the sake of heaven") with the dispute of Korah and his company (which was not), and treats the Korah dispute as the type of all destructive controversy. The Pastoral author and the rabbinic compilers read Numbers 16 in remarkably similar typological terms.

On the resurrection question, the Jewish landscape was internally divided in the first century: the Pharisaic tradition preserved by the rabbis affirmed bodily resurrection (m. Sanhedrin 10:1, "All Israel has a share in the world to come… and these are the ones who have no share: he who says there is no resurrection from the Torah"), the Sadducees denied it (Mark 12:18; Acts 23:8; Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.16), and apocalyptic texts like 2 Maccabees 7 and 1 Enoch 22-25 imagined varied scenarios. Modern Paul-within-Judaism scholars — Krister Stendahl, E.P. Sanders, Daniel Boyarin, Mark Nanos, Paula Fredriksen — read Pauline resurrection theology as recognizably Pharisaic, future-oriented, and bodily, against any Hellenizing spiritualization. The Hymenaeus heresy on this account is not a disagreement between Jewish and Greek paradigms but a deviation from the Pharisaic-Pauline mainstream into something more Platonist.

**Where this leaves us.** The chapter encodes two features that Jewish and Christian traditions handle in parallel: the disciplinary management of internal heresy, and the preservation of bodily-resurrection hope against spiritualizing reductions. Jewish scholarship continues to push back where Christian readings of 2 Timothy turn the *parathēkē* into supersessionism, but on the resurrection-as-future question, Pharisaic Judaism and Pauline Christianity stand together against Hymenaeus.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Realized vs. inaugurated vs. future eschatology.** Hymenaeus and Philetus said "the resurrection has already happened" (2:18). The author treats this as heresy *upsetting the faith of some*. The question is what exactly the right doctrine should look like. Twentieth-century theology has staked four main answers. Rudolf Bultmann's existentialist demythologization (in *New Testament and Mythology* and *Theology of the New Testament*) treated bodily resurrection as a mythological symbol whose true content is the existential transformation of the believer in the present moment — effectively a theological version of what the Pastoral author calls heresy, though Bultmann did not see himself as endorsing Hymenaeus. C.H. Dodd's *realized eschatology* (in *The Parables of the Kingdom* and *The Apostolic Preaching*) argued that the kingdom of God arrived fully in Jesus' ministry and that future-oriented language is largely metaphor for the unfolding consequences of that arrival. Oscar Cullmann's *Christ and Time* and George Eldon Ladd's *A Theology of the New Testament*, followed by N.T. Wright in *The Resurrection of the Son of God* and *Surprised by Hope*, articulated the *already-not-yet* or inaugurated framework — resurrection has begun in Christ, is partly present in the Spirit, and awaits bodily completion at the *parousia*. Dispensationalist futurism (Darby, Scofield, Walvoord, the Dallas Theological Seminary tradition) reads resurrection as wholly future, divided into stages tied to a tribulation-millennium scheme.

The Pastoral author's polemic in 2:18 demands at minimum that resurrection cannot be wholly past; it does not by itself adjudicate among the future-leaning options. Steelman the realized position: 2:11 ("if we have died with him, we will also live with him") draws Romans 6 and Ephesians 2 logic into the same orbit as Hymenaeus's claim — the believer has, in some genuine sense, already been raised. The line between realized and over-realized is therefore narrower than the polemic admits. Reformed theology (Calvin, Bavinck) has historically pressed the present efficacy of union with Christ, and modern Reformed writers like Richard Gaffin (*Resurrection and Redemption*) have shown that Paul holds together a present participation in Christ's resurrection life with a future bodily resurrection without contradiction. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter forbids reducing Christian hope to a present spiritual experience, but it does not authorize collapsing it into a wholly future one either; the inaugurated-eschatology framework best honors both 2:11 and 2:18.

**Tension Two: Divine fidelity and human apostasy.** The faithful saying in 2:11-13 holds two clauses in tension that have generated centuries of soteriological dispute. Verse 12: "if we deny him, he also will deny us." Verse 13: "if we are faithless, he remains faithful — for he cannot deny himself." The Reformed Calvinist tradition reads the saying through the lens of perseverance of the saints: the faithlessness of 2:13 is the wavering of the truly elect, whom God upholds because he cannot contradict his eternal decree, while the denial of 2:12 describes the apostate who was never truly elect (Westminster Confession 17; G.C. Berkouwer, *Faith and Perseverance*; John Murray, *Redemption Accomplished and Applied*). The Lutheran tradition, in the Formula of Concord and in modern interpreters like Gerhard Forde and Oswald Bayer, holds the *simul iustus et peccator* dialectic — the believer is always simultaneously justified and sinner, and God's faithfulness in 2:13 is the constancy of the gospel's verdict against all evidence to the contrary. The Wesleyan-Arminian tradition (Wesley's *Sermons*, Thomas Oden's *Systematic Theology*) reads 2:12 as a real warning of conditional perseverance — believers can fall away — and 2:13 as God's structural integrity: even if all humanity becomes faithless, God's character remains. Catholic theology (Trent on justification, Aquinas's *Summa* I-II.109-114 on grace) integrates the two clauses through the *concursus* of grace and free cooperation: the persevering believer is upheld by efficacious grace, the falling-away believer freely rejects sufficient grace, and God's faithfulness is the ground of every offering of grace. Eastern Orthodox synergism (Lossky, *The Mystical Theology*; Kallistos Ware, *The Orthodox Way*) holds that salvation is co-worked by divine grace and human freedom, with no contradiction between warning and assurance — both are real because the human person is genuinely free.

The steelman of the Wesleyan-Arminian reading: the parallelism of 2:11-12 strongly suggests that the *if* clauses describe genuine alternatives — dying with him, enduring with him, denying him, being faithless — and the four conditions cannot be sorted into "this happens to the truly elect, that to the merely professing." On the Wesleyan reading, 2:13 is a doxological affirmation of God's character, not a guarantee that any individual believer will not finally fall away. **Where this leaves us:** the saying refuses both the determinist consolation that no warning can finally fall on me and the moralist anxiety that no promise can finally hold me; it sets the warning and the assurance side by side and trusts the believer's heart to be reformed by both.

**Unresolved question.** If God's faithfulness in 2:13 truly cannot be unwound by my faithlessness, why does the author still have to write 2:12 — and which clause ought to be loud in my ear when I am tempted to deny him?

## The Thread

- **1 Corinthians 15:12-19** — The Corinthian denial of bodily resurrection ("How can some of you say that there is no resurrection of the dead?") is the proximate Pauline antecedent of the Hymenaeus heresy. Read against 2 Tim 2:18, it shows the over-realized or denial-of-bodily-resurrection problem was not new to the Pastorals but a recurring battle in Pauline communities from the early 50s onward.

- **Numbers 16:5 LXX** — The seal-quotation in 2:19 ("the Lord knows those who are his") is drawn from Moses' response to Korah's rebellion. The author treats Hymenaeus and Philetus typologically as a new Korah and the Christian community as the new wilderness assembly. The non-obvious move is reading church discipline through the lens of Numbers' rebellion narratives.

- **2 Peter 1:16-21 / 3:3-7** (later in canonical reading order) — The same testamentary anxiety about over-realized or distorted eschatology, framed as Peter's farewell. Both letters come from the apostolic generation's late-stage recognition that the most dangerous false teaching may emerge from inside the community, not outside. The literary parallel with 2 Timothy is unmistakable.

- **Revelation 2:13 / 3:5** — The reward for the one who endures, "I will not blot his name out of the book of life," and the warning to the unfaithful at Pergamum and Sardis. The eschatological logic of 2 Tim 2:11-13 — endurance, denial, faithfulness — is the same logic that runs through the seven letters in Revelation. The image of *enduring soldiers* and *crowned conquerors* is the bridge.
