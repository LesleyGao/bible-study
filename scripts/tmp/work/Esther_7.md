# Esther 7: The Second Banquet and the Gallows that Turned

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter unfolds entirely within the king's private banquet quarters at Susa — the *bitan ha-melekh* (בִּיתָן הַמֶּלֶךְ, 1:5; cf. 7:7–8), a Persian loanword denoting the royal pavilion or pleasure-palace, distinct from the formal Apadana audience hall and from the residential quarters proper. The archaeological record at Susa, excavated by Loftus in the 1850s and by the French Délégation under Dieulafoy and de Mecquenem in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, has uncovered exactly this layered palace complex: a public columned hall, a residential apartment, and an enclosed garden (the *paradeisu*, παράδεισος in Greek transliteration, the source of the English "paradise"), into which the king "arose in his wrath" and withdrew (7:7). Persian royal banquets are well attested by Herodotus 1.133, 9.110 and by Athenaeus 4.145, who cites Heracleides of Cumae on the lavish protocols of Achaemenid drinking — the *symposion* phase after the meal proper, where business and intrigue happened.

The reign is Xerxes I (Heb. *Achashverosh*, אֲחַשְׁוֵרוֹשׁ; the Old Persian *Khshayārshā* yields the Hebrew form precisely, settling the long-disputed identification with Xerxes I rather than Artaxerxes I or the Ahasuerus of Daniel 9:1, who is a different figure of obscure historicity). The chapter's chronology places it on the thirteenth of Nisan or shortly after — the second of two consecutive banquets Esther arranges — somewhere in the spring of what the book counts as Xerxes' twelfth year (3:7), nominally 474 BC. This sits four to five years after the failed Greek campaign and about a decade before Xerxes' assassination by his courtier Artabanus in 465 BC (Ctesias, *Persica* fr. 13; Justin 3.1). The historical Xerxes whom Greek sources record is precisely the petulant, easily inflamed, easily redirected figure Esther portrays — Herodotus 7.39 records his savage punishment of the merchant Pythius, whose son was sawn in half because Pythius had asked that one son be exempted from the army; Herodotus 9.108–113 describes Xerxes' affair with his niece Artaynte and the resulting domestic violence in which Queen Amestris (the historical queen of Xerxes, traditionally identified with or distinguished from Vashti and from Esther in different reconstructions) had Artaynte's mother mutilated. The figure of the Esther narrator's Ahasuerus — angry, suggestible, dangerous, easy for a courtier like Harbona to redirect with a single sentence — is consonant with the Greek historiographical Xerxes.

Material details of execution merit attention. The text says they "hung" (Heb. *talu*, תָּלוּ) Haman on the gallows (Heb. *etz*, עֵץ, lit. "tree" or "wood") of fifty cubits, roughly seventy-five feet, that he had prepared for Mordecai. Both Berlin (JPS) and Bush (WBC) note that Persian capital practice was overwhelmingly impalement, not gallows-hanging in the modern sense; Herodotus 3.125 records Polycrates of Samos crucified or impaled by Oroetes; 7.238, Xerxes' impalement of Leonidas's body; the Behistun inscription brags of impalements as a routine punishment for rebellion. The Hebrew *talah*, "to hang," is therefore better rendered "impale on a stake," and the fifty-cubit height is likely hyperbolic — though Bickerman noted that high impaling stakes were a Persian status marker, the higher the stake, the more visible the disgrace. The eunuch Harbona (Heb. *Charvona*, חַרְבוֹנָה, from Old Persian *Kharvanā*) had appeared earlier in 1:10 as a passive functionary; here at 7:9 he becomes a narrative agent, the quintessential courtier who reads the room and offers the convenient solution. Authorship and dating remain anonymous and post-Persian, with Bickerman, Moore, Bush, Fox, and Levenson again converging on the late Persian to early Hellenistic period; the Late Biblical Hebrew with Persian loanwords (*partemim*, *patshegen*, *bitan*, *karpas*) anchors the linguistic dating per Polzin.

Key terms: *bitan* (בִּיתָן, royal pavilion), *etz* (עֵץ, gallows/stake), *charon* (חָרוֹן, royal wrath, 7:7, 10), *tzar oyev* (צַר אוֹיֵב, "foe and enemy," Esther's compact denunciation in 7:6), and the triple verb of 7:4 — *le-hashmid la-harog u-le-abbed* (לְהַשְׁמִיד לַהֲרוֹג וּלְאַבֵּד), "to destroy, to kill, and to annihilate," which echoes the wording of the original decree in 3:13 in inverted reception.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

If chapter 6 was the structural pivot, chapter 7 is the *peripeteia* itself — the reversal completed. The book's chiastic architecture (Radday, Levenson, Berlin) makes 7:1–10 the formal mirror of 3:7–15: the decree of doom is matched, banquet for banquet, by the decree of doom's author falling on the queen's couch. The leitwort *mishteh* (מִשְׁתֶּה, "banquet"), which appears twenty times in the book, peaks in chapters 5 and 7 — the doubled banquets are not redundancy but a comic-narrative device, the technique that Levenson identifies as Esther's signature "delay-and-double." Esther had her chance at 5:6 to make her request and chose instead to invite the king to a second banquet, an authorial choice Berlin reads as deliberate dramaturgy. The reader must wait an entire chapter (chapter 6, the king's sleepless night) before the request is made, and by the time it comes, the table has been set: Mordecai elevated, Haman humiliated, Zeresh's prophecy uttered, the eunuchs already arriving to fetch Haman to his fall.

The literary devices are dense and almost theatrical. Esther's speech in 7:3–4 is a masterpiece of rhetorical compression — three clauses, building from the personal ("my life") to the corporate ("my people") to the legal-economic ("we have been sold"), with the deliberate triple verb *le-hashmid la-harog u-le-abbed* echoing Haman's own decree language verbatim and inverting it as accusation. The pun on "sold" (*nimkarnu*, נִמְכַּרְנוּ, 7:4) recalls Haman's payment of ten thousand talents of silver to the king's treasuries (3:9): the Jews have been *sold*, but it is Haman who has done the selling. Esther names Haman without naming herself or Mordecai as Jewish — she has not yet outed her ethnicity in those exact terms; the reader knows but the king does not, and the dramatic irony tightens.

The peripety in 7:7–10 is staged with comic precision. The king storms out into the garden — the *gan bitan ha-melekh* (גַּן בִּיתַן הַמֶּלֶךְ) — and Haman, recognizing immediately that "harm was determined against him" (Heb. *kalta elav ha-ra'ah*, 7:7, an idiom Bush notes is technical for irrevocable royal sentence), throws himself on the couch where Esther reclines to beg. The king returns at exactly the wrong moment, sees Haman in physical proximity to the queen, and reads it as sexual assault. The reader knows it is supplication; the king sees rape. The literary device here is what Northrop Frye called the comic *anagnorisis* — recognition deliberately misfired, the wrong reading turned into the right outcome. Berlin's JPS and Beal's *The Book of Hiding* both note the theatrical staging: characters enter and exit a single room with farcical precision; the eunuchs cover Haman's face the instant the king pronounces the implicit sentence ("As the word left the mouth of the king, they covered Haman's face," 7:8 — Persian protocol, attested by Curtius Rufus 6.8.22, that condemned men's faces were covered before execution). Harbona's intervention in 7:9, naming the gallows already built, is the comic-tragic chorus: the courtier who knew, who said nothing earlier, but who knows when the wind has shifted. The closing inclusio in 7:10 — "Then the wrath of the king abated" (*va-chamat ha-melekh shakhakhah*, וַחֲמַת הַמֶּלֶךְ שָׁכָכָה) — echoes 1:12 ("the king became enraged," *va-yiktzof ha-melekh me'od*) at the book's opening, framing the entire arc as a single royal fury aroused and finally settled, with one queen displaced and another vindicated.

The genre register is comic-carnivalesque (Beal, Berlin, Levenson, applying Bakhtin's category): the man who built the seventy-five-foot stake is impaled upon it; the official who covered for the king is now covered as a corpse; the queen who could not protect her own husband from a courtier protects an entire people. Textual variants are notable: the LXX intensifies the scene at 7:8, having Haman explicitly fall *upon* Esther rather than *on* the couch where she lay, sharpening the king's misreading; Addition E (the LXX) inserts an extended royal counter-decree denouncing Haman in Hellenistic rhetorical style. The Alpha-text shortens the scene considerably. The MT's terseness is its art.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical placement of Esther 7 sits inside the Saul-Agag arc that the book sustains as its deepest intertext. Mordecai of "the tribe of Benjamin… son of Kish" (2:5 — the very genealogy of Saul, 1 Sam 9:1) faces Haman "the Agagite" (3:1 — descendant of Agag the Amalekite king, 1 Sam 15:8), and Haman's death by impalement is the death Saul should have given Agag. Levenson's reading: chapter 7 is the moment Saul's failure is undone, the *cherem* completed three thousand years after the fact, and the careful staging of Haman's death — by his own gallows, in the king's house, in the queen's presence — is the Hebrew Bible's most elaborate revenge fantasy turned theological argument. Deuteronomy 25:17–19 commands Israel to "blot out the memory of Amalek"; Numbers 24:7 prophesies a king "higher than Agag"; 1 Samuel 15 stages the failure; Esther 7–9 stages the completion. The fact that the completion happens in the diaspora, by a Jewish queen and her cousin operating inside a Persian palace, is the book's argument about how covenantal narrative now runs.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** In the Second Temple period, 1 Maccabees 7:49 institutes "Mordecai's Day" alongside the Hasmonean festivals; 2 Maccabees 15:36 names Purim explicitly. Josephus *Antiquities* 11.265–268 dramatizes the banquet scene with theatrical flourishes — Haman literally throwing himself on the queen's couch, the king's misreading, the eunuch's intervention — and the LXX's Addition E gives the king a long anti-Haman speech absent from the MT. Rabbinic readings (b. Megillah 16a) celebrate Haman's downfall without qualification: the Talmud delights in detail, asking why the gallows were fifty cubits, naming Haman's children, calculating the exact timing. Esther Rabbah 10:9 reads Esther's "foe and enemy" speech as prophetic accusation. Targum Sheni elaborates the scene with extensive midrashic additions. Maimonides (*Hilkhot Megillah*) preserves the carnivalesque ruling that the festival reading must be performed annually. Medieval commentators (Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Ralbag, Malbim) all read the scene as straightforward poetic justice — Rashi notes pointedly on 7:10 that Haman was hanged on his own gallows as a sign of God's hidden justice. Modern Jewish scholarship: Sandra Berg on the structural reversal, Fox on the moral ambiguity, Berlin on the literary staging, Levenson on the Saul/Agag completion, Hazony on the political ethics of poetic justice, Koller on the rabbinic centrality, Niditch on the trickster pattern. Elie Wiesel's post-Holocaust readings are particularly clear-eyed: Haman's death is not celebrated for its own sake but as the avoidance of a different death, the people's.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** Patristic sources are sparse. The chapter is rarely cited in early Christian literature — its direct vindication of Jewish ethnic survival has no obvious Christological hook. Rabanus Maurus's Carolingian *Expositio* allegorizes Haman as the devil whose plot rebounds upon him; the gallows become the cross by which Christ's enemy is undone. Bede follows. The Glossa Ordinaria preserves the allegorical reading. Reformation engagement is conspicuous in its absence — Luther, in *Table Talk* §475, dismisses Esther wholesale ("I wish they had not come to us at all"), and Calvin produces no commentary on the book. The Council of Trent (1546) reaffirmed the Greek-with-Additions Esther for Catholic use, which means most Catholic exposition of chapter 7 also incorporates Addition E's elaborate counter-decree. Lewis B. Paton's ICC (1908) is corrosive about the chapter — Paton reads Esther's denunciation as melodramatic, the king as ridiculous, the moral world of the book as Jewish nationalism at its worst; this is the high-water mark of Christian critical condescension toward Esther. Contemporary Christian readings have substantially recovered the chapter: Carey Moore's Anchor commentary takes the literary art seriously; Frederic Bush's WBC reads chapter 7 as the climax of a tightly engineered narrative; Karen Jobes's NIVAC and Iain Duguid offer Reformed providential readings; Greidanus's Christological preaching tradition treats Haman's fall as a type of evil's self-defeat at the cross. Timothy Beal's *The Book of Hiding* and Berlin's JPS commentary have brought sustained attention to the staging of the scene — the queen's denunciation, the king's misreading of a sexual threat, the way the king's jealousy over his own household becomes, ironically, the vehicle of an entire people's deliverance.

**Convergence and divergence.** Jewish tradition has always read chapter 7 as a vindication; Christian tradition has historically been embarrassed by its violence and ethnic specificity. The post-Holocaust era has shifted the Christian reading substantially — reading Esther as a Jewish text in a Christian Bible, rather than a Christian allegory in Hebrew dress. Where the traditions still diverge is on the *celebratory* register: rabbinic Judaism celebrates Haman's death liturgically every Purim, with noisemakers and drinking until *arur Haman* and *barukh Mordekhai* become indistinguishable (b. Megillah 7b); Christian readings have rarely found a way to celebrate the scene that does not feel triumphalist. Levenson reads this difference as load-bearing: the carnival is the point, and Christians who cannot sit with it are missing the diaspora theology the book is teaching.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: poetic justice and the ethics of vengeance.** The chapter's structural elegance — Haman dying on his own gallows — is morally satisfying in a way that ought to be theologically interrogated. The wisdom-tradition Catholic reading takes Proverbs 26:27 ("Whoever digs a pit will fall into it") as the operative principle; the moral order of creation is self-executing, and Esther 7 stages it. The Reformed providential reading (Jobes, Duguid) treats God's hidden hand as the engine driving the reversal — Haman's gallows are not coincidence but covenantal justice working through gentile politics. The Lutheran reading, were one to construct it past Luther's dismissal, would distinguish between God's left-hand and right-hand work: Haman's death is the temporal sword wielded by the Persian state, not the gospel. The Anabaptist reading (Yoder's brief treatment of Esther in *The Politics of Jesus*) is most cautious: poetic justice in the text is a literary phenomenon, not a license for Christians to celebrate enemies' deaths or to weaponize Romans 12:19's "vengeance is mine" the wrong way. The Jewish rabbinic tradition (Megillah 16a) celebrates without qualification — Haman is the descendant of Amalek, his death is the *cherem* completed, and to mourn him would be to repeat Saul's error. The position least native to most Christian readers is the post-Holocaust Jewish one — Wiesel, Hazony, Koller — that reads the scene with absolute clarity: the question is not whether Haman deserves to die, but whether *not killing him* would be moral. To steelman this position fairly: a community that survives genocidal intent by killing its would-be killer is not engaging in vengeance; it is exercising the most basic right of self-preservation, and Christian discomfort with the spectacle may say more about Christian distance from existential threat than about the chapter's moral universe. Where this leaves us: the chapter resists both naive celebration and squeamish condemnation; sitting honestly inside its discomfort is the first step.

**Tension 2: the king's dispassion — what does it mean that Ahasuerus reacts to Esther's danger but not to genocide?** The chapter's most disturbing detail, easily missed, is what moves the king to wrath. He has signed a decree of annihilation against an entire ethnic group within his empire (3:10–11) without serious investigation. He does not become enraged when Esther tells him "we have been sold to be destroyed, to be killed, to be annihilated" (7:4); he asks calmly "Who is he, and where is he?" (7:5). What enrages him is the perceived sexual threat to his queen (7:8). The genocide produced a shrug; the touch on the couch produced a death sentence. Fox's *Character and Ideology* names this directly: "Esther's narrative gives us a deeply unflattering portrait of imperial power that nevertheless God uses." The Catholic reading often emphasizes the *praeter-intentional* operation of divine providence — God works through the king's misreading because God works through whatever materials are available. The Reformed reading treats the king's dispassion as evidence of the noetic effects of sin — even pagan rulers can be moved, but not always for the right reasons. The Anabaptist reading reads the chapter as a cautionary tale about imperial power: deliverance achieved through a king whose moral compass tracks his sexual property rights is deliverance you should not be sentimental about. The Jewish reading (Hazony, Berlin) is the most philosophically interesting: politics is the art of the possible, and the diaspora Jew in Esther 7 has to use the king's own pathologies (his anger, his vanity, his sexual jealousy) as the levers of survival, because no other levers are available. Critical readings (Fox, Beal) push this further: the book may be quietly indicting *all* gentile imperial authority, even when it serves Jewish ends. Where this leaves us: deliverance in Esther 7 comes through a king who is not good, who does not become good, and whose anger at the right thing is for the wrong reason — a theological provocation Christian readings tend to soften and Jewish readings tend to face squarely.

**The unresolved question:** if the deliverance of God's people in Esther 7 depends on a gentile king's failure to distinguish between the murder of an entire people and an apparent affront to his own sexual honor, what does the chapter quietly say about the materials God will use, and is a deliverance that runs through such materials something to celebrate without remainder?

## The Thread

- **1 Samuel 15:8–9; Numbers 24:7; Deuteronomy 25:17–19** — the Saul/Agag/Amalek thread completes here. Saul kept Agag alive; Mordecai and Esther do not keep Haman alive. The *cherem* the first Benjamite king failed is fulfilled by the last Benjamite courtier, and the unspoken halakhic implication is that the obligation to "blot out the memory of Amalek" is finally discharged.
- **Proverbs 26:27 / Psalm 7:15** — the digger of the pit falls into it. The wisdom tradition's literary anthropology of justice meets the diaspora court tale's narrative engineering, and chapter 7 is where they shake hands.
- **Daniel 6:24** — Daniel's accusers and their families thrown into the lions' den they prepared for him. The diaspora court tale's stock device of the enemy destroyed by his own machine appears in both books; Esther 7 secularizes it (no divine voice, no explicit miracle) where Daniel 6 sacralizes it (angelic intervention).
- **Genesis 50:20** — Joseph to his brothers: "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good." The forward-and-backward symmetry with Esther 7 is striking; Joseph and Esther are the two great diaspora-deliverance stories, and one names God explicitly while the other does not. The pairing is a deliberate canonical comment on how the same theology can be told two ways.
