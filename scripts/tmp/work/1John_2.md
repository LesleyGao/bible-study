# 1 John 2: Advocate, Anointing, and the Departure

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The same authorial situation that anchors chapter 1 anchors chapter 2. The text is anonymous; the implicit voice is "the elder" of 2-3 John; three reconstructions remain in play. The traditional view, going back to Irenaeus's *Against Heresies* 3.1.1 and supported by Polycarp's *To the Philippians* 7 and Eusebius's *Historia Ecclesiastica* 3.39, identifies the elder with John the Apostle in old age in Ephesus — though Eusebius preserves Papias's possible distinction between **John the Apostle** and **John the Elder** (πρεσβύτερος Ἰωάννης), which has fueled centuries of disagreement about whether these are two figures or one. The critical view, dominant in modern scholarship, treats the author as an anonymous member of the Johannine community: Raymond Brown's *The Community of the Beloved Disciple* (1979) and his Anchor Bible commentary, Rudolf Bultmann's earlier source-critical work, Judith Lieu's *I, II, & III John* and *The Theology of the Johannine Epistles*. Date in the 90s AD; Asia Minor, likely Ephesus; the Johannine community hypothesis (Brown, Lieu, J. Louis Martyn) supplies the institutional backdrop.

Chapter 2 is the chapter where the historical situation becomes visible at the surface of the text. The schism announced in 2:19 — "they went out from us, but they were not of us" — is the single most important historical datum in the entire letter. The mainstream historical-critical reading (Brown, Lieu) is that this is a real community split, not a hypothetical caution. A group of teachers, formed inside the Johannine community, has departed; their teaching survives and continues to circulate, and the letter is in part a pastoral response. Their teaching, on the standard reconstruction, was a proto-Docetic Christology that minimized or denied the genuine humanity of Jesus and the saving significance of his blood — hence the chapter's tight binding of confession and Christology in 2:22-23 and the strong note on the "blood of Jesus" already in 1:7. Whether these *secessionists* are best identified with Cerinthian followers, with a Cerinthus-adjacent stream, or with an emergent proto-gnostic teaching is debated; Irenaeus's account of Cerinthus in *Against Heresies* 1.26.1 (and the bathhouse story preserved at *Hist. Eccl.* 3.28) anchors the Cerinthian reading.

The chapter's Christology turns on two dense terms in 2:1-2. *Paraklētos* (παράκλητος, "Advocate") is one of the great Johannine words: in the Fourth Gospel (14:16, 14:26, 15:26, 16:7) it names the Spirit; here in 1 John 2:1 it names the risen Christ in his ongoing work. The shift is theologically significant — the elder's community knows the term in both registers, and the dual reference (Christ as Advocate before the Father, Spirit as Advocate within the believer) is the seedbed of later Trinitarian articulation. *Hilasmos* (ἱλασμός, "propitiation" or "expiation," 2:2) is one of the most contested cultic terms in the New Testament. The translation debate — propitiation (turning away wrath) versus expiation (covering sin) — is the famous C. H. Dodd / Leon Morris exchange. Dodd's 1935 *The Bible and the Greeks* argued for expiation as the dominant LXX usage; Morris's *The Apostolic Preaching of the Cross* (1955) argued, against Dodd, that the wrath-of-God dimension is preserved in the term. Catholic and Orthodox readings tend to emphasize objective transformation; evangelical-Reformed readings tend to retain the propitiatory dimension. The "for the sins of the whole world" of 2:2 is the hinge for the limited-atonement debate within Reformed scholasticism.

The "old/new commandment" of 2:7-11 is a direct echo of John 13:34: "A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another." The elder is invoking known community tradition. The "antichrists" (ἀντίχριστοι, 2:18, 22) are the most theologically charged term in the chapter and the first New Testament occurrence of the word. The plural form is decisive: for the elder, *antichrist* is a present-multiple reality, not solely a future singular figure. The four New Testament occurrences of *antichristos* (2:18, 2:22, 4:3, 2 John 7) all sit in the Johannine epistles. The "anointing" (χρίσμα *chrisma*, 2:20, 27) is pneumatological — the Spirit's anointing teaches all believers — and has fueled Catholic sacramental confirmation imagery and Pentecostal-charismatic *chrisma* recovery alike. *Parousia* (παρουσία, 2:28) is the only Johannine epistolary use of the term. The cluster of terms to hold across the chapter: *paraklētos* (παράκλητος), *hilasmos* (ἱλασμός), *antichristoi* (ἀντίχριστοι), *chrisma* (χρίσμα), *parousia* (παρουσία).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter's structure is more clearly articulated than chapter 1's. Five movements work in sequence: the *paraklētos*-*hilasmos* Christology (2:1-2); the knowledge-and-commandment-keeping pericope (2:3-6); the old/new commandment with its love/light dyad (2:7-11); the famous staircase address to children, fathers, young men (2:12-14); the world-renunciation passage (2:15-17); the antichrist warning (2:18-27); and the closing exhortation to abide and the *parousia* note (2:28-29). The staircase structure of 2:12-14 is one of the most lyrical passages in the New Testament — six addresses, three pairs (children, fathers, young men, and again children, fathers, young men), nearly identical openings ("I am writing… I am writing… I write… I write…"), ascending Christological content. Judith Lieu reads it as deliberately liturgical or hymnic; Stott observed the rhetorical patterning is meant to be heard, not analyzed.

The genre questions raised in chapter 1 apply here too. There is no formal letter form. Bultmann's "Revelation Source" hypothesis sits underneath the antithetical sayings of 2:4 ("whoever says 'I know him' but does not keep his commandments is a liar") and 2:9-11 ("whoever says he is in the light and hates his brother is still in darkness"). The repetition of *abide* (μένω *menō*) — six occurrences in the chapter (2:6, 2:10, 2:14, 2:17, 2:24, 2:27, 2:28, with multiple substantive uses) — is the lexical thread that ties the schism passage (2:19, "they went out") to the closing exhortation (2:28, "abide in him"). The contrast is structural: those who departed did not abide; those who remain are urged to do so.

Robert Yarbrough's *BECNT* commentary observes that the chapter's argument moves by negative-positive pairs throughout: the one who keeps and the one who does not keep (2:3-4); the one who loves and the one who hates (2:9-11); the one who loves the world and the one who does the will of God (2:15-17); the one who confesses and the one who denies (2:22-23); the one who is taught by the anointing and those who try to deceive (2:26-27). The pedagogic logic is clarification by contrast.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the New Testament canon, 1 John 2 is the chapter most often invoked in patristic anti-heretical writing. Polycarp's citation of 1 John 4:2-3 in *To the Philippians* 7 sits in the same theological register as 2:22-23 and was the reading most readily applied against early Docetism. Irenaeus's extended anti-Cerinthian polemic in *Against Heresies* 3.16 deploys the "denies the Son" language of 2:23 as a weapon. Origen and Cyril of Alexandria preserve continued patristic exegesis. Augustine's *Tractates on the First Epistle of John* (the *In Epistolam Ioannis ad Parthos* series) — though the famous *dilige et quod vis fac* line is on chapter 4 — work through chapter 2's *abide* theology and the antichrist warning in detail.

The medieval tradition pulled chapter 2 in two directions. Aquinas's *Lectura super Epistolam I Johannis* developed the *paraklētos*-*hilasmos* Christology in conversation with the doctrine of satisfaction; Bonaventure's commentary tied the *chrisma* to confirmation. Catherine of Siena read 2:6 ("walk in the same way in which he walked") in her mystical-imitative key.

The Reformation read the chapter for its assurance and its anti-papal polemic. Luther identified the papacy as a manifestation of the *antichristos* of 2:18-22; the identification was widespread in early Protestant exegesis and reached its sharpest formulation in the Westminster Confession (25.6, original 1647 form, modified later). Calvin's *Commentaries* on the chapter are more restrained and ecclesiological — he reads the antichrists as a class of false teachers in every age. The propitiation-vs-expiation question of 2:2 became a wedge: the Reformed-evangelical tradition (later Owen, Turretin) read 2:2's "for the sins of the whole world" as cosmic-phenomenological language compatible with particular redemption; the Wesleyan-Arminian tradition read it as straightforward universal-offer.

The critical period reread the chapter through the Johannine community lens. Brown's *Community of the Beloved Disciple* and his Anchor Bible commentary on the epistles read 2:19 as the social-historical pivot of the entire letter. Lieu has been more cautious about the specifics of Brown's reconstruction but accepts the schism frame. J. Louis Martyn's work on the Johannine community's relation to the synagogue provides background. The contemporary Catholic conversation continues to draw on Brown and on *Dei Verbum*. Eastern Orthodox readers hear the *chrisma* sacramentally — the same word names the chrismation that follows baptism in Orthodox practice. Protestant evangelical commentary — Stott (*The Letters of John*), Marshall (*NICNT*), Yarbrough (*BECNT*), Colin Kruse (*PNTC*), Stephen Smalley (*WBC*), Karen Jobes — reads the assurance theme as central, with the "abide" cluster anchoring the doctrine of perseverance.

N. T. Wright, in *Early Christian Letters for Everyone* (2011), reads chapter 2 as a string of practical tests by which the readers can know whether their fellowship with God is the real thing. He takes the "advocate with the Father" of 2:1 as the answer to the worry that confessing sin (1:9) leaves the believer perpetually anxious: Jesus stands on their side, and his death "for the sins of the whole world" (2:2) means there is no shortfall in the provision. The "new commandment that is also old" (2:7-8) Wright reads as the love-command of John 13 now built into the bloodstream of the community — the surest sign of walking in the light. On the "do not love the world" of 2:15-17, Wright is careful to say the writer does not mean the created order or human society as such, but the whole system of self-serving desire and status-craving that runs counter to God's will and is, in any case, passing away. The "antichrists" of 2:18-23 are, for Wright, exactly those former members who have detached Jesus from the flesh-and-blood reality the prologue insisted on — denying the Son in the very act of claiming a higher spirituality — and the "anointing" of 2:20, 27 is the Spirit's gift that lets the whole community recognize the truth and stay put.

### Jewish Scholarly Engagement

The *antichristos* tradition behind 2:18 has Jewish apocalyptic roots. Belial in the Dead Sea Scrolls (especially in 1QM, the *War Scroll*, and 1QS), Beliar in the Sibylline Oracles, Gog of Magog in Ezekiel 38-39 and later apocalyptic, the "abomination of desolation" of Daniel 11-12 — these constitute the Jewish substrate from which the Johannine *anti-* prefix Christianizes a prior tradition. The chapter assumes the reader knows that "antichrist is coming" (2:18) — language that would have been familiar to Jewish-Christian audiences from Daniel and from the apocalyptic tradition the Pauline letters also draw on (cf. 2 Thess 2:1-12). James Charlesworth and John Collins have mapped these parallels in detail.

The "anointing" (*chrisma*, 2:20, 27) draws from the Hebrew prophetic and royal anointing tradition. The verb *māšaḥ* (to anoint) underlies *māšîaḥ* (Messiah), and Joel 2:28-32's "I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh" supplies the prophetic anticipation of the universal-anointing motif the elder applies to the believing community. The Christianization is the elder's claim that this prophetic universal-anointing is now in effect for those who hold to the apostolic confession.

The *koinōnia*-of-light/love language of 2:7-11 has roots in the Levitical "love your neighbor as yourself" (Lev 19:18), already cited as the second great commandment in the Synoptic tradition (Mark 12:31). The "old commandment" of 2:7 is, on the most natural reading, the Torah's love command refracted through Jesus's Johannine new-commandment formulation in John 13:34.

The post-*Nostra Aetate* awareness raised in chapter 1 applies again. The Johannine corpus's anti-Jewish polemic (especially in the Gospel of John 8 and 9) does not directly drive 1 John 2 — the conflict here is intra-Christian — but the responsible reader holds in view Adele Reinhartz's *Befriending the Beloved Disciple* and Daniel Boyarin's *Border Lines* on the late-first-century intra-Jewish boundary-making that produced the Johannine literature's polemical idiom. Magnus Zetterholm's work on Antiochene Jewish-Christian relations supplies the institutional context.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What God is doing through this chapter, on a charitable reading: through the Advocate-and-propitiation Christology of 2:1-2, God grounds the assurance of forgiveness while not loosening the call to keep the commandments; through the antichrist warning of 2:18-22, God preserves the church from the dissolution of incarnational confession; through the Spirit's anointing of 2:20, 27, God places the teaching of truth not only in apostolic memory but in every believer's interior life. What is at stake is the relation between Christology, ethics, and ecclesial boundaries — and the question of how a community knows it has not departed.

### Tension One: *Hilasmos* — Propitiation vs. Expiation (2:2)

The propitiation reading, anchored in Leon Morris's *The Apostolic Preaching of the Cross* (1955), holds that *hilasmos* preserves the Old Testament *kpr* (atonement) language's wrath-bearing dimension. Christ's death turns aside the holy wrath of God against sin. The Reformed tradition reads this as the centerpiece of penal-substitutionary atonement; Calvin's *Institutes* 2.16-17 developed the position; Owen's *The Death of Death* anchored the limited-atonement variant. The "whole world" of 2:2 is, on the strict-Reformed reading, phenomenological-cosmic language for the universal scope of the *offer* without committing to universal application.

The expiation reading, anchored in C. H. Dodd's *The Bible and the Greeks* (1935), holds that *hilasmos* in LXX usage is dominantly cult-objective rather than wrath-directed: it is sin that is covered, not God who is appeased. Catholic and Orthodox readings have generally been more comfortable with this reading; the objective-transformation language fits the sacramental imagination. Gustaf Aulen's *Christus Victor* (1931) added a third dimension — the cross as cosmic victory over the powers — that complements rather than replaces the expiation reading.

A third position: cosmic-scope universalism, of the kind David Bentley Hart defends in *That All Shall Be Saved* (2019) and which Origen anticipated in his *apokatastasis* doctrine. The "whole world" of 2:2 is read in its plain sense, the propitiation reaches all, and the soteriological narrative ends in universal restoration. This reading has been minoritarian in Western Christianity but has had patristic and Orthodox defenders.

A fourth position: Wesleyan-Arminian unlimited atonement with conditional application. The propitiation is genuinely for the whole world; the application requires the responsive faith the Spirit enables but does not coerce. Wesley's *Predestination Calmly Considered* and *The Question, "What is an Arminian?" Answered* are the loci. This reading takes the cosmic-scope language seriously without requiring universalism.

The least-intuitive position to steelman is the strict-Reformed limited-atonement reading. It looks at first like a refusal to read 2:2 in its plain sense. But Owen's argument, carefully made, is that the *hilasmos* is *for* whomever it is *applied to*; if it were applied to all, all would be saved (since the propitiation is by definition effective). The "whole world" language, on this reading, is necessary to combat ethnic particularism (the gospel is not just for Jews) without committing to numerical universality. The reading preserves the efficacy of the propitiation at the cost of restricting its scope.

**Where this leaves us**: the chapter binds Christology to ethics so tightly that any reading of *hilasmos* that detaches the cross from the call to walk as Jesus walked (2:6) misses what the elder is doing. The reader is left to judge among the four positions on grounds the chapter itself does not arbitrate.

### Tension Two: Antichrist (2:18-22)

The dispensational-popular-evangelical reading takes *antichristos* as a singular future figure, the world ruler of the last days, whom Daniel 7-12 and 2 Thessalonians 2 and Revelation 13 jointly describe. The plural *antichristoi* of 2:18 are precursors or types; the singular awaits. Hal Lindsey and Tim LaHaye's popularizations are the late-twentieth-century vector; behind them sit the dispensational schematizations of J. N. Darby and the Scofield Reference Bible.

The mainstream-critical reading takes the elder's plural at its plain force. *Antichristos* is, in 1 John, a class of false teacher present in the church now — specifically those who have departed in the schism of 2:19 and who deny incarnational Christology in 2:22. There is no future singular Antichrist in the Johannine epistles; the chapter announces "the last hour" (2:18) precisely because the antichrists are already present. Brown, Lieu, Marshall, Yarbrough, Stott — the commentary tradition is largely unified on this reading.

The Reformation-papal-identification reading, dominant in early Protestant exegesis, identified the papacy as the *antichristos* of 2:18 and the corresponding Pauline "man of lawlessness" of 2 Thessalonians 2. Luther's polemical writings, Calvin's careful but identifiable formulations, and the original Westminster Confession 25.6 made the identification confessional. Most contemporary Reformed bodies have softened or removed the identification; some confessional Presbyterian and Reformed denominations retain the original language.

A fourth position: identification with specific historical figures across history. Hippolytus's *Treatise on Christ and Antichrist* (early 3rd c.) read Daniel and the Johannine corpus together to identify a future imperial figure; modern dispensational identifications have ranged from Roman emperors to popes to twentieth-century dictators to anticipated future world leaders.

The least-intuitive position to steelman is the mainstream-critical reading, since for many evangelical readers it removes a piece of furniture from the eschatological landscape. But the steelman is straightforward: the elder uses the plural, the elder names the antichrists' theological content (denial of the incarnation, 2:22), the elder ties them to a specific historical event (the schism, 2:19), and the elder declares "the last hour" already present. The reading honors the chapter's plain sense and refuses to import a singular figure the chapter does not name.

**Where this leaves us**: the chapter's *antichristos* language is doing pastoral-ecclesial work in an immediate community crisis. Whatever further eschatological deployment the term has elsewhere, it cannot eclipse what the elder is using it for here.

### Closing Question

If the *chrisma* of 2:27 means that the believers "have no need that anyone should teach you," and yet the elder is in the act of teaching them, what is the relation between the Spirit's interior anointing and the apostolic-elder's exterior instruction? The chapter places both in the same paragraph and does not reconcile them.

## The Thread

- **John 13:34-35** — the new commandment in its source-form, the explicit echo behind 2:7-11
- **Daniel 11-12** — the apocalyptic-adversary tradition behind the *antichristos* concept of 2:18, with the "abomination of desolation" supplying the Jewish substrate
- **Joel 2:28-32** — the Spirit-anointing tradition Peter cites in Acts 2 and the elder applies in 2:20, 27
- **2 Thessalonians 2:1-12** — the parallel Pauline "man of lawlessness," whose singular-figure form contrasts instructively with the Johannine plural *antichristoi*
