# 2 Timothy 3: Last-Days Vices and the Breath of God

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 3 opens by relocating the present moment into eschatological time: ἐν ἐσχάταις ἡμέραις *en eschatais hēmerais*, "in the last days." If the letter is genuinely Pauline, written from a Roman cell in the mid-60s during or after the Neronian persecution Tacitus describes in *Annals* 15.44, then "the last days" names the period the author and his readers are already in — the time between Christ's resurrection and the *parousia*, intensified by present persecution. If the letter is pseudonymous, dating to the 90s-130s, "last days" still names the present era of the implied recipients but takes on the additional weight of decades of Christian experience under intermittent imperial pressure (Domitian, Trajan-era policies as documented in Pliny the Younger's *Epistles* 10.96 to Trajan, c. 112 AD).

The vice catalog in 3:2-5 is one of the longest in the New Testament — eighteen or nineteen items depending on the parsing — and it follows a recognizable Greco-Roman literary form. Hellenistic moral philosophy from the Stoics through Philo of Alexandria (*De Sacrificiis*, *De Virtutibus*) routinely produced such lists, and the Qumran *Community Rule* (*1QS* 4) preserves a Jewish two-spirits vice catalog that shares vocabulary and structure with the Pauline tradition. The catalog's social diagnosis — "lovers of self," "lovers of money," "lovers of pleasure rather than lovers of God" — uses the φιλο- compound to brand a particular kind of moral disorder; Plutarch and Epictetus, ascetic in their own modes, deploy the same compounds to mark misordered desire. The depiction of false teachers who "creep into households and capture weak women" (3:6) reflects the realities of early Christian house-church mission, where itinerant teachers gained access through household introductions; Carolyn Osiek and Margaret MacDonald's work on the Greco-Roman household, and Ramsay MacMullen's *Christianizing the Roman Empire*, document how the household was the principal arena of Christian growth and therefore the principal arena of doctrinal infiltration.

The reference to Jannes and Jambres in 3:8 anchors the chapter in Jewish extra-canonical tradition. The names appear nowhere in the Hebrew Bible or the Septuagint Exodus narrative, but they appear in the Damascus Document (*CD* 5:18-19) from the Qumran caves, where they are called "Yohane and his brother." Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Exodus 1:15 and 7:11-12 names them; the *Book of Jannes and Jambres*, a Jewish pseudepigraphon extant in Greek and Coptic fragments published by Albert Pietersma (*The Apocryphon of Jannes and Jambres the Magicians*, 1994), recounts their opposition to Moses, their magical contest, and their final judgment. Origen in *Commentary on Matthew* 27:9 mentions the names, and Pliny the Elder's *Natural History* 30.11 attests pagan knowledge of "Iannes" as a magical figure. The Pastoral author's casual citation indicates that the names were familiar to his audience, evidence of immersive Second-Temple Jewish literacy in the early Christian movement.

Verse 3:15 — "from childhood you have been acquainted with the sacred writings" — refers almost certainly to the Tanakh in Septuagint form, the Bible of diaspora Jewish-Christian households like Timothy's. Verse 3:16's *theopneustos* θεόπνευστος is a hapax legomenon in the New Testament and a near-hapax in surviving Greek literature; it appears in Plutarch (*De Placitis Philosophorum* 5.2) and Pseudo-Phocylides 121, but is otherwise rare. Among textual witnesses, Codex Sinaiticus א and Codex Alexandrinus A preserve the chapter in full; Codex Vaticanus B's lacuna in the Pastorals continues; Codex Bezae D, where extant, supports the reading of 3:16 as πᾶσα γραφὴ θεόπνευστος καὶ ὠφέλιμος *pasa graphē theopneustos kai ōphelimos* — though there is a long-running interpretive debate (see Lens 4) about whether to translate "all Scripture is God-breathed and profitable" or "every God-breathed Scripture is also profitable."

Greek key terms: θεόπνευστος *theopneustos* (God-breathed, 3:16); γραφή *graphē* (writing/Scripture, 3:16); ἱερὰ γράμματα *hiera grammata* (sacred letters, 3:15); ὠφέλιμος *ōphelimos* (useful, profitable, 3:16); παιδεία *paideia* (training, instruction, 3:16).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 3 carries the testamentary letter into its prophetic register. Greco-Roman and Jewish farewell discourses regularly include predictions of impending trouble — Plato's *Phaedo*, Moses' Deuteronomic curses, Jacob's blessings (Gen 49), the *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs*, Paul's Miletus address (Acts 20:29-30, "fierce wolves will come in among you, not sparing the flock"). Margaret Mitchell, Loveday Alexander, and Benjamin Fiore have all noted that the *peristasis* — predicted hardship — is a structural element of the genre. The Pastoral author follows the form precisely: prediction of moral collapse (3:1-9), personal anamnesis of past suffering (3:10-11), generalized prediction of persecution for the godly (3:12-13), and exhortation to remain in what has been learned (3:14-17).

Three literary devices anchor the chapter. First, the vice catalog (3:2-5) deploys the Hellenistic *peristasis* form with a distinctive theological signature — the φιλο- compounds set up the climactic antithesis "lovers of pleasure rather than lovers of God" (φιλήδονοι μᾶλλον ἢ φιλόθεοι), making misordered love the diagnostic of the last days. Second, the typological deployment of Jannes and Jambres turns Old Testament-shaped Jewish tradition into a paradigm for present false teachers — *just as* (ὃν τρόπον *hon tropon*) they opposed Moses, *so also* (οὕτως *houtōs*) these oppose the truth. The grammar is explicitly typological. Third, the climactic statement of 3:16-17 functions as an inclusio with the *parathēkē* language of chapter 1: the deposit guarded in 1:14 is now identified, at least in part, with the *graphē* breathed-out by God. The arc from chapter 1 to chapter 3 rises to a doctrine of inspired Scripture as the resource for the man of God's completeness.

Textual variants in 3:16 are minor but interpretively consequential. The structure of πᾶσα γραφὴ θεόπνευστος καὶ ὠφέλιμος leaves open whether *theopneustos* is a predicate adjective ("all Scripture is God-breathed and profitable") or an attributive ("every God-breathed Scripture is also profitable"). Mounce, Towner, Knight, and Marshall favor the predicate reading on grammatical grounds — the *kai* "and" between the two adjectives most naturally coordinates two predicates of the subject — and that reading is preserved by virtually all early Greek manuscripts and by the Vulgate (*omnis Scriptura divinitus inspirata*). B.B. Warfield mounted the classical Reformed defense of the predicate reading in *The Inspiration and Authority of the Bible*. The attributive reading was preferred by Jerome in some of his glosses and gained traction in some 19th-century critical commentary, but it is not text-critically grounded; it is interpretively motivated.

Verse 3:8's mention of Jannes and Jambres supports the literary observation that the Pastoral author writes with a Jewish-pseudepigraphic library at hand. Jude similarly draws on 1 Enoch, and 2 Peter draws on Jude. The Pastoral author is not a pure Septuagint reader; he reads in the broader Second-Temple Jewish corpus.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical NT arc — Israel's covenant, Jesus' messianic enactment, Spirit-poured Pentecost, the church sent into the world — 2 Timothy 3 sits at the moment when the apostolic generation defines the resource by which post-apostolic communities will navigate. The deposit of chapter 1, the right handling of the word in chapter 2, and the inspired writings of chapter 3 form a sequence: tradition, custodianship, text. The chapter ends with the man of God "complete, equipped for every good work" (3:17) — a rare hint of confidence amid an otherwise dark assessment of the last days.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic readers found 3:16 the foundational text for the doctrine of inspiration. Origen's *De Principiis* 4.1.6 quotes it as proof that all Scripture has divine origin. John Chrysostom's *Homilies on 2 Timothy* (Homily 9) treats the verse as authorizing the church's continual use of Scripture for discipline and formation. Athanasius cites 3:16 in his *Festal Letter 39* (367 AD), where he produces the first surviving New Testament canon list explicitly in Greek; Jerome quotes it in defense of the inspiration of even the deuterocanonicals he was reluctantly translating from the Septuagint. Augustine in *De Doctrina Christiana* 2.8 lists the canonical books and grounds their authority in apostolic-and-prophetic inspiration parallel to 3:16.

The medieval period built the verse into the architecture of the *quadriga* — the fourfold sense of Scripture (literal, allegorical, moral, anagogical) attested by Cassian, Augustine, and systematized by Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* I.1.10. Aquinas's *Lectura* on 2 Timothy treats *theopneustos* as the metaphysical ground of Scripture's authority and 3:17's "equipped for every good work" as the basis for understanding sacred doctrine as both speculative and practical.

The Reformation made 3:16 a battleground. Luther and Calvin both read the verse as authorizing *sola scriptura* — Scripture alone is *theopneustos* and therefore alone has the breath of God; tradition does not share that breath. The Westminster Confession 1.4 cites the verse as authorizing Scripture's "authority… because it is the word of God." Roman Catholic responses, codified at Trent's Fourth Session (1546) and developed at Vatican I's *Dei Filius* (1870), accepted the inspiration but expanded the canon to include the deuterocanonicals (Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1-2 Maccabees, additions to Esther and Daniel) and insisted that interpretation belongs to the Magisterium.

Modern critical scholarship has turned 3:16 into a meta-question. F.C. Baur and the Tübingen School treated the inspiration claim as itself second-century, evidence of an emerging Catholicism. B.B. Warfield's collected *Inspiration and Authority of the Bible* (1948) defended verbal-plenary inerrancy on the basis of 3:16 against German liberal rejections of supernatural inspiration. Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* I/2 §19-21 reframed the doctrine: Scripture is human witness that becomes the Word of God by the act of God in present hearing, not a static deposit identical with revelation. G.C. Berkouwer's *Holy Scripture* offered an "organic" inspiration model — God works through, not despite, the human authors' historical particularity. John Webster's *Holy Scripture: A Dogmatic Sketch* (2003) rehabilitated a high doctrine of Scripture in dogmatic, Trinitarian terms. N.T. Wright's *The Last Word* (US: *Scripture and the Authority of God*) argues that the authority of Scripture is the authority of God exercised through Scripture in the church's life. Bart Ehrman's *Misquoting Jesus* and *Forged* press the historical-critical case that Scripture's transmission and authorship complicate any straightforward inerrancy claim. Daniel Wallace's *Reinventing Jesus* and Larry Hurtado's textual work respond.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** When the Pastoral author writes that "from childhood you have been acquainted with the sacred writings" (3:15), he means the Tanakh in Septuagint form. The "sacred letters" *hiera grammata* is a Jewish phrase — Philo and Josephus both use it for the Hebrew Scriptures. The Tanakh's threefold authority (Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim) is the framework into which 3:16 places the Christian text. *Theopneustos* — God-breathed — has a strong parallel in Jewish doctrine of *ruach ha-kodesh*, the holy spirit that inspired the prophets (b. Sanhedrin 11a; Tosefta Sotah 13:2-4). The rabbinic tradition holds that prophetic inspiration ceased with the post-exilic prophets Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi; everything after is *bat kol*, a heavenly echo, not direct prophecy. The Pastoral author's claim that all Scripture (in whatever inventory he has in mind) is breathed by God therefore stands inside the Jewish doctrinal vocabulary while pushing it in a new direction.

The canon question divides Christian and Jewish receptions sharply. The Jewish canon, fixed by the second century at twenty-four books matching the thirty-nine of the Protestant Old Testament, excludes the Septuagint deuterocanonicals; the Catholic and Orthodox canons, drawing on the Septuagint, include them. Bruce Metzger's *The Canon of the New Testament*, Lee McDonald's *The Biblical Canon*, and Larry Hurtado's *The Earliest Christian Artifacts* trace the divergence. Modern Jewish scholars including Jon Levenson (*The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*) and Adele Berlin have engaged Christian interpretive readings of the Tanakh substantively, often pushing back where Christian appropriation has obscured Jewish self-understanding.

Modern Paul-within-Judaism scholars — Krister Stendahl, E.P. Sanders, Daniel Boyarin, Mark Nanos, Paula Fredriksen — have argued that Paul's high view of Scripture in Romans 9-11 and elsewhere remains within a Jewish framework of covenantal Scripture-use. The Pastoral author's claim that *graphē* makes one "wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus" (3:15) is the Christological hinge that distinguishes the Christian reception from the rabbinic — but the underlying doctrine of inspiration is shared. Where contemporary Jewish scholarship continues to push back is on Christian readings that treat the Tanakh as a closed Old Testament whose meaning is exhausted in pointing to Christ; Levenson, Boyarin, and others have insisted on the Tanakh's irreducible self-standing meaning.

**Where this leaves us.** Christian and Jewish readings of 3:15-16 share a conviction that God speaks through inspired written texts, and they part decisively on the canon's scope and on whether the texts terminate in or transcend Christology. The dialogue continues to be productive at exactly the points where each tradition is willing to take the other's textual self-understanding seriously.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Inspiration mechanics.** What does *theopneustos* actually claim? The strict verbal-plenary inerrancy position, articulated by B.B. Warfield, Charles Hodge in *Systematic Theology* I.6, Norman Geisler, R. Albert Mohler, and codified in the Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy (1978), holds that God so superintended the human authors that every word of the original autographs is true, including in matters of history and science. The dynamic or organic inspiration position of Herman Bavinck (*Reformed Dogmatics* I.10-15), G.C. Berkouwer (*Holy Scripture*), and John Webster preserves a high doctrine of Scripture but locates inspiration in God's working through, not against, the historical-cultural particularity of the human authors; the result is reliable for what God intends to teach without claiming inerrancy on every incidental detail. Karl Barth's witness model (*Church Dogmatics* I/2) goes further: Scripture is fallible human witness that bears the Word of God by the Spirit's act in present reading. Catholic teaching, articulated in Vatican II's *Dei Verbum* 11 ("the books of Scripture must be acknowledged as teaching solidly, faithfully and without error that truth which God wanted put into sacred writings for the sake of salvation"), affirms inspiration without requiring inerrancy on incidental matters not pertaining to salvation. N.T. Wright's critical-realist account in *The Last Word* refuses the inerrancy-vs-fallibility binary by reframing Scripture's authority as the authority of God exercised through Scripture in the unfolding five-act drama of salvation history.

The steelman of the strict-inerrancy position: if Scripture is breathed by God, and God does not lie or err, then Scripture's truthfulness must extend to whatever it actually asserts — and the burden of proof is on those who would carve up Scripture's reliability into "salvific" and "incidental" categories the text itself does not draw. The steelman of the Barthian witness model: the Scripture we possess is manifestly a human document with all the marks of historical contingency, and only an account that lets the Word be God's free act through that human document does justice both to the divine sovereignty and to the textual evidence. **Where this leaves us:** *theopneustos* asserts divine origination of Scripture; it does not by itself adjudicate the precise mechanics of how that origination interacts with human authorship, and the major Christian traditions are arguing about how to honor the verse, not whether to.

**Tension Two: Canon and authority.** When the Pastoral author wrote "all Scripture is breathed out by God," what was its scope? Almost certainly some form of the Tanakh in Septuagint version, since the New Testament writings were in process of being written. But which Tanakh — the proto-Masoretic Hebrew, the longer Greek Septuagint with deuterocanonicals? The Catholic 73-book canon, ratified at Trent's Fourth Session (1546) against Protestant trimming, preserves the Septuagintal scope including Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1-2 Maccabees, and the Greek additions to Esther and Daniel. The Protestant 66-book canon, articulated in the Westminster Confession 1.2-3 (1647), and earlier in Luther's German Bible's relegation of the deuterocanonicals to an appendix labeled *Apocrypha*, follows the Jewish canon as supposedly fixed at Jamnia (a meeting whose existence and decisions Jack Lewis and others have shown are largely a modern scholarly construct). The Eastern Orthodox 78-book canon adds 1-2 Esdras, 3 Maccabees, Psalm 151, and the Prayer of Manasseh; the Ethiopian Tewahedo Orthodox canon goes further, including 1 Enoch and Jubilees.

The historical-critical complications are sharp. The author of Jude clearly cites 1 Enoch 1:9 as prophecy (Jude 14-15); the Pastoral author cites Jannes and Jambres from extra-canonical tradition (3:8); the Septuagint shows variant text-forms of Hebrew books and includes the deuterocanonicals as part of its received corpus in the Christian East and West for the first four centuries. Bruce Metzger's *The Canon of the New Testament* and Lee McDonald's *The Biblical Canon* document the gradual emergence of canon lists, the regional variation, and the hardening of boundaries. The steelman of the Catholic position: the canon recognized by the church for over a millennium before the Reformation included the deuterocanonicals; the Protestant trimming was a sixteenth-century novelty justified by appeal to a Jewish canonical norm that Christians had never historically followed. The steelman of the Protestant position: the apostolic generation's "sacred writings" almost certainly meant the proto-Masoretic Hebrew canon, and the deuterocanonicals were never quoted by Jesus or the apostles as Scripture; Trent's affirmation was itself a polemical innovation.

**Where this leaves us.** The verse asserts that "all Scripture" is God-breathed; the church has never agreed on what counts as Scripture, and the boundaries are different across the major Christian communions today. The doctrine of inspiration as 3:16 articulates it cannot, by itself, settle the canon question — and how communities settle that question is itself a tradition-laden act of judgment.

**Unresolved question.** If the breath of God is what makes a text Scripture, and if we have no neutral standpoint from which to read off the list of God-breathed texts independently of communal judgment, who is the breath of God authoritatively recognized through — the bishop, the council, the congregation, the conscience, the consensus of the centuries?

## The Thread

- **2 Peter 1:19-21** — "knowing this first of all, that no prophecy of Scripture comes from someone's own interpretation. For no prophecy was ever produced by the will of man, but men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit." The closest NT parallel to 3:16's inspiration claim. Read together, the two letters form the New Testament's strongest internal articulation of how Scripture comes to be Scripture.

- **Exodus 7:11-12 with Targum Pseudo-Jonathan** — The Egyptian magicians who oppose Moses in Exodus 7 are identified by Targum Pseudo-Jonathan as Jannes and Jambres (in some manuscripts also at Exodus 1:15). The Pastoral author's casual citation in 3:8 is a window into how a first-century Christian read his Old Testament — through layered Jewish midrashic and pseudepigraphic tradition, not through bare Hebrew text alone.

- **Psalm 33:6 / Genesis 2:7** (earlier in canonical reading order) — "By the word of the LORD the heavens were made, and by the breath of his mouth all their host"; "and the LORD God formed the man of dust from the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life." The same divine breath that creates the cosmos and animates Adam is, in 3:16, the breath that produces Scripture. Inspiration is a creation-grade act.

- **John 20:22** (later in canonical reading order if one reads chronologically by composition) — "He breathed on them and said to them, 'Receive the Holy Spirit.'" The risen Jesus' Pentecostal breathing on the disciples uses the same Greek verb root (ἐμφυσάω *emphysaō*) that the Septuagint uses for God's creating breath in Genesis 2:7. The inspiration of Scripture (3:16), the inspiration of the apostolic mission (John 20:22), and the creation of humanity (Genesis 2:7) are linked by a single biblical theology of divine breath.
