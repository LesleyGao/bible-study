# 1 Timothy 6: Slaves, Money, and the Deposit

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The political situation behind 1 Timothy 6 sharpens around two facts of the ancient Mediterranean: nearly every household economy in urban Asia Minor depended on enslaved labor, and the wealth gap between the Greco-Roman elite and the commercial middle was widening through the first century. If the chapter is genuinely Pauline (mid-60s AD), it sits in the closing years of Nero's reign, when imperial finances were strained, a tax revolt in Judea was brewing, and the urban Christian assemblies in Ephesus were a small minority within a city of roughly 200,000. If pseudonymous (c. 90-130 AD), the chapter belongs to a moment when Christian congregations had begun to attract members of higher social standing, including wealthy benefactors whose patronage threatened to reproduce within the assembly the same hierarchies the gospel claimed to relativize — a tension Margaret MacDonald and Ramsay MacMullen have documented for early Christian communities of the late first and early second centuries.

Material culture is the bedrock for reading 6:1-2. The slave economy in first-century Asia Minor was vast: estimates place the enslaved population of urban centers at 15-30 percent. Slavery was a legal status, not an ethnic category; freedmen could become wealthy and even powerful (Trimalchio in Petronius's *Satyricon* is the literary type). Chattel slavery in Roman law was nevertheless brutal in its core principle — the *dominus* held *vitae necisque potestas* (power of life and death) over the slave, and the *Lex Aquilia* protected slaves only as property of their owners, not as persons. Manumission was common but not universal, and the manumitted *libertus* still owed *operae* and reverence to his former master. The chapter's instruction to slaves with believing masters (6:2) addresses an actual social reality: Christian masters and Christian slaves shared the eucharistic table on Sunday and the master-slave hierarchy on Monday.

Ancient economic anxiety was distinct from modern. The chapter's denunciation of φιλαργυρία *philarguria* "love of money" (6:10) and ὀρεγόμενοι *oregomenoi* "those who reach for [riches]" (6:10) operates in a moral register that the Cynic-Stoic philosophical tradition had already populated. Diogenes' contempt for wealth, Crates' famous renunciation, Seneca's *Epistulae Morales* (especially Letter 17 on poverty), and the *diatribe* tradition of street-corner moral argument all supplied vocabulary the Pastoral author draws on. Abraham Malherbe's work on the Cynic background of the Pastorals is essential here. The phrase "the love of money is a root of all kinds of evils" (6:10) has parallels in Stoic moralists; Plutarch's *De Cupiditate Divitiarum* makes the same argument at length.

The authorship and dating debate must be named for this chapter as for the others. Critical scholars (Brox, Donelson, Conzelmann-Dibelius, Collins) read 6:20's warning against "what is falsely called knowledge" (ψευδωνύμου γνώσεως) as a polemic against second-century Gnosticism, which fits a pseudonymous post-Pauline date. Conservative scholars (Mounce, Marshall, Towner, Knight, L.T. Johnson) push back: γνῶσις was a contested term across first-century Hellenistic Judaism and proto-Christianity, and the "knowledge" attacked here may be Jewish-Christian speculation, Cynic philosophical pretension, or proto-Gnostic incipient teaching — not necessarily the developed second-century systems. Karen King's *What is Gnosticism?* and Michael Williams's *Rethinking "Gnosticism"* have substantially destabilized the category itself, making the dating argument from 6:20 weaker than it once was.

Archaeological and textual evidence: the chapter is fully present in Sinaiticus א and Alexandrinus A; absent from Vaticanus B (which lacks the Pastorals); the early second-century papyrus P32 covers Titus only. Pliny the Younger's letter to Trajan (Epistle 10.96, c. 112 AD) describing the interrogation of two female *ministrae* under torture, and the Lyon martyrs of AD 177, provide the second-century context against which a "good confession before many witnesses" (6:12) and "Christ Jesus, who in his testimony before Pontius Pilate made the good confession" (6:13) were read by the early audience. Inscriptions from Asia Minor record substantial benefaction by wealthy converts — the chapter's instructions to "the rich in this present age" (6:17-19) addressed a real and growing population in the assemblies.

Greek key terms: φιλαργυρία *philarguria* "love of money" (6:10); αὐτάρκεια *autarkeia* "contentment, self-sufficiency" (6:6, a load-bearing Stoic-Cynic technical term); παραθήκη *paratheke* "deposit, that which has been entrusted" (6:20, a financial-banking term that becomes one of the central theological concepts of the early church); ψευδώνυμος γνῶσις *pseudōnymos gnōsis* "falsely-named knowledge" (6:20); ὁ μακάριος καὶ μόνος δυνάστης *ho makarios kai monos dynastēs* "the blessed and only Sovereign" (6:15); βασιλεὺς τῶν βασιλευόντων καὶ κύριος τῶν κυριευόντων *basileus tōn basileuontōn kai kyrios tōn kyrieuontōn* "King of kings and Lord of lords" (6:15).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre identification: chapter 6 contains the densest concentration of distinct literary forms in the Pastoral Epistles. The opening (6:1-2) is a household-code fragment of the master-slave variety familiar from Colossians 3:22-4:1, Ephesians 6:5-9, 1 Peter 2:18-25, and Titus 2:9-10. Greco-Roman parallels include Aristotle's *Politics* I.2 (the famous treatment of the household as the state's foundation), Stoic Hierocles' *On Duties* (with its concentric circles of obligation), and the *Oeconomica* attributed to Pseudo-Aristotle. David Balch's *Let Wives Be Submissive* (1981) and David deSilva's work on Greco-Roman household codes situate the form historically.

The attack on the false teachers (6:3-5) is a vice list in the *peristasis* tradition, and the contrast to "godliness with contentment" (6:6) is a virtue formula. The economic exhortation in 6:6-10 is a moral aphorism set, with 6:7 ("we brought nothing into the world, and we cannot take anything out of the world") possibly drawing on Job 1:21 and Ecclesiastes 5:15, and 6:10 functioning as a recognizable proverbial formula.

The personal charge to Timothy in 6:11-16 is the chapter's most striking literary unit. It carries the form of a Greco-Roman *fight the good fight* athletic-military exhortation (cf. Stoic and Cynic *agōn* imagery), with virtue list (6:11), athletic verb (6:12 ἀγωνίζου τὸν καλὸν ἀγῶνα), credal-confessional reference (6:12-13), and culminating in a doxology (6:15-16) of probable liturgical origin. The doxology's distinctive features — "King of kings and Lord of lords" parallels 2 Maccabees 13:4 and 3 Maccabees 5:35; "who alone has immortality" parallels Hellenistic Jewish prayer language; "who dwells in unapproachable light" echoes Psalm 104:2 and Hellenistic Jewish theology of divine glory — strongly suggest the author is quoting an established hymnic or liturgical fragment. Mounce, Marshall, and L.T. Johnson all treat 6:15-16 as an embedded hymn. Larry Hurtado has cited the doxology in his work on early high Christology and Jewish-Christian liturgical practice.

Literary devices include vice/virtue lists (6:4-5 vs. 6:11), the *agōn* athletic-military image (6:12), an embedded confessional reference to Christ before Pilate (6:13 — possibly the earliest non-gospel allusion to the passion narrative), and a closing benediction structure ending with "Grace be with you" (6:21). The chapter's structure runs: slaves (6:1-2a) → false teachers and money (6:2b-10) → personal charge with doxology (6:11-16) → the rich (6:17-19) → final charge about the deposit and grace (6:20-21).

Located in the book's argument: chapter 6 closes the letter and gathers up the dominant themes — false teaching, communal order, personal example, and the goodness of created things rightly used. The deposit-language in 6:20 (παραθήκη) anticipates 2 Timothy 1:14's developed use of the same metaphor. The closing has the formal features of a *mandata principis* peroration.

Textual variants worth flagging: at 6:5 some Western witnesses add the gloss "from such withdraw thyself" (ἀφίστασο ἀπὸ τῶν τοιούτων), absorbed into the Majority Text and KJV; the earliest witnesses (א, A) omit it. At 6:7 the precise reading varies in some witnesses, with some adding "and it is true that" or smoothing the syntax; NA28 follows the harder reading with א*. At 6:13 some witnesses read "the gives life to all things" while others read "the one who preserves all things" — manuscript evidence favors ζῳογονοῦντος. At 6:19 the variant "eternal life" vs. "true life" (αἰωνίου vs. ὄντως ζωῆς) is meaning-affecting; modern critical editions read ὄντως.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical New Testament arc, chapter 6 contributes three legacies of consequence: the slavery instructions that would define Christian engagement with the institution for nearly two millennia; the wealth ethic that sits in productive tension with Jesus' more radical sayings in Luke; and the deposit (παραθήκη) language that becomes the early church's primary metaphor for the integrity of apostolic teaching across generations.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic reading on slavery is troubling and fascinating. John Chrysostom's homilies on 1 Timothy 6 spiritualize the master-slave relationship and exhort masters to remember they are themselves "slaves of God" — but he does not call for the abolition of slavery. Augustine in *City of God* XIX.15 develops a theology of slavery as a *consequence* of the Fall — God did not create slavery, but in a fallen world it serves as both punishment for sin and pedagogy. He affirms the institution while denying it is natural. Ambrose, Jerome, and the Cappadocians follow similar lines.

The exception that proves the rule: Gregory of Nyssa's Homily 4 on Ecclesiastes (c. 379 AD) contains what is widely recognized as the earliest unambiguous Christian abolitionist argument. Gregory pillories slavery as incompatible with the *imago Dei*: "You condemn man to slavery, when his nature is free and possessed of free will, and you legislate in competition with God." Chris L. de Wet's *Preaching Bondage* and David Bentley Hart have re-emphasized Gregory's argument, which was in its own time a minority position and did not carry the day. The mainstream patristic accommodation continued through Aquinas (*Summa* II-II q.57 a.3), who treated slavery as a legitimate consequence of natural law mediated through human convention.

Medieval Christianity continued the pattern. The Catholic Church's institutional position on slavery was complex: papal bulls condemned the enslavement of Christians (often) and indigenous peoples (sometimes — *Sublimis Deus* 1537 condemned the enslavement of American Indians; *In Plurimis* 1888 condemned slavery generally), but the institution was never categorically rejected by magisterial teaching until the modern era. Reformation theology continued the accommodationist line. Luther's *Wider die räuberischen und mörderischen Rotten* (1525) attacked the peasants' uprising and explicitly defended the social hierarchy that included slavery. Calvin permitted slavery within his Geneva framework. American defenders of slavery in the antebellum South drew on 1 Timothy 6:1-2 and Ephesians 6:5 with relentless frequency; Eugene Genovese's *Roll, Jordan, Roll* documents the systematic deployment.

The Christian abolitionist arc emerged from the margins: Quaker testimony beginning in 1688 (Germantown Quaker Petition); John Wesley's *Thoughts on Slavery* (1774); William Wilberforce and the Clapham Sect (Slave Trade Act 1807, Slavery Abolition Act 1833); Frederick Douglass and Sojourner Truth, who argued from Scripture against the proslavery readings; Theodore Dwight Weld's *The Bible Against Slavery* (1837). The enslaved themselves, in the spirituals and in the preaching that the slaveholders tried to suppress, consistently read 6:1-2 against rather than with the masters who quoted it, holding instead to Exodus, the prophets, Galatians 3:28, and Philemon. Walter Wink's *Engaging the Powers* has framed the question of structural sin and Christian witness more broadly. The contemporary scholarly consensus across most traditions reads 6:1-2 as a contextual accommodation that the church took 1700-1800 years to recognize required transformation rather than perpetuation.

The wealth-ethic side of the chapter has its own trajectory. Patristic reading of 6:6-10 and 6:17-19 is severe. Clement of Alexandria's *Quis Dives Salvetur* ("Who is the Rich Man That Shall Be Saved?") works out an early sophisticated reading: wealth is morally neutral, but attachment to wealth is deadly. Basil the Great's homilies on social ethics (especially *I Will Tear Down My Barns*) are scorching against the rich. John Chrysostom preached extensively on wealth as deposit, almsgiving as repayment of what was always God's. The Cappadocian and Antiochene traditions consistently read 1 Timothy 6 as a rebuke to Christian acquisitiveness, not as a baptism of moderate wealth.

Medieval reception developed monastic poverty as a theological vocation (Benedict's *Rule*, the mendicant orders — Francis and Dominic), with 1 Timothy 6 as one of the controlling texts. Aquinas's *Summa* II-II q.118 on covetousness draws on 6:10. The Reformation introduced new tensions. Luther's reading of 6:6-10 is rigorous but accommodated to the magistracy's right to wealth; Calvin's reading is famously moderate, opening the theological space within which Max Weber's *Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1905) argued the work-ethic and wealth-blessing reading developed. Anabaptist and Quaker traditions held to rigorous simplicity. Wesleyan tradition produced Wesley's "Earn all you can, save all you can, give all you can" — a careful threading of 6:9-10 against 6:17-19.

Modern Catholic social teaching has read 1 Timothy 6 as a foundational text for the *option for the poor*. *Rerum Novarum* (Leo XIII, 1891) cites the chapter; *Centesimus Annus* (John Paul II, 1991) and *Caritas in Veritate* (Benedict XVI, 2009) develop the wealth ethic against unfettered market capitalism. Pope Francis's *Laudato Si'* (2015) and *Fratelli Tutti* (2020) extend the argument, reading 6:9-10 in continuity with the prophets' and Jesus' anti-wealth teaching. The contemporary Pentecostal and prosperity-gospel movements occupy the polemical opposite pole; Kate Bowler's *Blessed: A History of the American Prosperity Gospel* documents the rise. David Bentley Hart's recent work has argued that Christianity is structurally incompatible with capitalism as a final loyalty, drawing on 1 Timothy 6 alongside the Sermon on the Mount.

The deposit (παραθήκη) language has a separate trajectory. Irenaeus's *Against Heresies* draws on 6:20 to defend the apostolic *regula fidei* against gnosticizing innovation. Tertullian's *De Praescriptione Haereticorum* makes the deposit argument central to his polemic against the heretics. Vincent of Lérins's famous formula "what has been believed everywhere, always, by all" stands in this tradition. The Catholic doctrine of the *depositum fidei* — explicitly cited in *Dei Verbum* and CCC 84 — runs directly from this verse. Protestant tradition has also drawn on the deposit metaphor, though usually in a Scripture-centered rather than tradition-centered framing.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** Jewish engagement with 1 Timothy 6 has focused on several points. First, the chapter's address to slaves and masters, while disturbing in its accommodation, does have parallels in rabbinic legal and ethical literature concerning the *eved kena'ani* (Canaanite slave) and the *eved ivri* (Hebrew slave) — both regulated by Torah and Talmud, both never categorically abolished within historic rabbinic Judaism, and the parallel uneases the historic Christian self-distinction on this point. Second, the wealth ethic of 6:6-10 stands in deep continuity with Jewish wisdom literature (Proverbs, Sirach, Qoheleth, the Mishnaic *Pirkei Avot*'s "who is rich? he who is content with his portion"). Mark Nanos and Paula Fredriksen have argued that reading the chapter against Hellenistic Jewish moral discourse rather than against Cynic philosophy alone produces a more historically accurate picture. Third, the doxology of 6:15-16 draws extensively on Hellenistic Jewish liturgical language; Larry Hurtado has cited it in his work on the Jewish-Christian devotional matrix.

The Paul-within-Judaism movement (Stendahl, Sanders, Boyarin, Nanos, Fredriksen) has not focused intensely on the Pastorals because their Pauline authorship is contested, but the broader claim of the movement bears on the chapter: the historical Paul did not oppose Torah for Jewish believers, and the Pastoral chapters' polemic against "myths and genealogies" should not be reflexively read as anti-Jewish. Where contemporary Jewish scholarship still pushes back is on the chapter's pseudonymous-or-not anti-asceticism (insofar as it bears on Jewish-Christian observance), and on the historical use of 6:1-2 as a text deployed to enslave African and indigenous peoples — a use that, whatever its original intent, shaped Christian-Jewish relations in modernity through the institutions of slavery the church helped sustain.

**Where this leaves us:** chapter 6's twin legacies on slavery and wealth have worked themselves out in the Christian tradition with very different velocities — the slavery question took the church 1700+ years to formulate clearly, while the wealth-ethic question has been argued continuously since the patristic era and remains live.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Christian Witness Within Unjust Structures.** 1 Timothy 6:1-2 instructs slaves to honor their masters, especially believing ones. The instruction is paralleled in Colossians 3:22-4:1, Ephesians 6:5-9, 1 Peter 2:18-25, and Titus 2:9-10. Together these texts constitute the New Testament's accommodationist stance on slavery — the institution is regulated, humanized, internally subverted by the eucharistic fellowship of master and slave, but not formally abolished.

The historic Catholic position has been complex. The CCC at 2414 unambiguously condemns slavery: "The seventh commandment forbids acts or enterprises that for any reason — selfish or ideological, commercial or totalitarian — lead to the enslavement of human beings, to their being bought, sold and exchanged like merchandise, in disregard for their personal dignity." But this clarity is recent; *Sublimis Deus* (1537) condemned the enslavement of indigenous peoples but did not categorically reject the institution; *In Plurimis* (1888) finally condemned slavery globally. The historic Catholic accommodation has been re-read by the modern Magisterium as a failure of the church to apply the gospel's full implications. A line of interpreters running from Gregory of Nyssa through the abolitionists and into much contemporary New Testament scholarship has argued that the chapter's accommodationist surface conceals a deeper subversion — the master-slave fellowship at the eucharistic table relativizes the social hierarchy and sets in motion forces that, given enough time, undo it.

The Reformed and Lutheran traditions have an equally mixed record. Luther's defense of the social hierarchy in *Wider die räuberischen und mörderischen Rotten* against the peasants is well known; Calvin's framework permitted slavery in Geneva for legal cause; the Westminster divines did not categorically reject slavery. American Presbyterians split in 1837 in part over slavery; Old School Presbyterian theologians like Robert Lewis Dabney developed elaborate biblical defenses of slavery citing 1 Timothy 6:1-2. The Reformed confession had to be revised, often painfully, by the post-abolition tradition (the Confession of 1967 of the PCUSA explicitly addresses racial reconciliation). Lutheran traditions show parallel revisions.

The Anabaptist withdrawal tradition reads 6:1-2 as accommodationist counsel for those who live within the world's structures, while the church itself constitutes an alternative society where slavery has no place. The Hutterite community-of-goods, Mennonite peace witness, and Quaker abolitionist activism all stand in this lineage. Reinhold Niebuhr's *Christ and Culture* typology categorizes these as "Christ against culture" approaches; H. Richard Niebuhr's analysis frames the spectrum from quietist accommodation to transformative engagement.

The Wesleyan tradition's record is honorably mixed. John Wesley's *Thoughts on Slavery* (1774) stands as one of the strongest 18th-century Christian abolitionist arguments. The Wesleyan revival's social-holiness emphasis fed directly into the Clapham Sect, the British Slave Trade abolition, and the American abolitionist movement. But American Methodism split in 1844 over slavery, with Southern Methodism defending the institution.

The historical record of the Black church in America bears this out: the enslaved and their preachers read 1 Timothy 6:1-2 against rather than with the slaveholders' use, drawing on the same texts (Exodus, the Hebrew prophets, Galatians 3:28, Philemon) that the slaveholders deliberately excluded from their preaching to the enslaved. In that reading practice the chapter's surface accommodationism is understood as not the canonical center but the canonical periphery — a verdict much of the wider church has since come to share.

The position to steelman is the accommodationist reading. The early Christian assemblies were small, politically powerless, dispersed, and surrounded by an institution they could not abolish. To call for slave revolt would have invited massacre, and to refuse to address the master-slave reality at all would have abandoned slaves to spiritual neglect. The chapter does what it can: it asserts the spiritual equality of master and slave (6:2's "they are brothers"), it constrains master behavior elsewhere (Eph 6:9, Col 4:1), and it sets in motion a logic the chapter itself does not fully draw out. This reading does not absolve the chapter or its later interpreters, but it acknowledges the actual historical situation against which the alternative — silent acquiescence — was the only realistic choice in the first century.

N.T. Wright, in *Paul for Everyone: The Pastoral Letters*, reads 6:1-2 the way he reads Philemon and the slavery material in Colossians and Ephesians: the early church could not abolish the institution by decree, but it planted inside it a charge — master and slave as brothers at the same table — that would, given time, blow it apart. For Wright the danger the chapter actually worries about is not subversion but discredit: a Christian slave who despises a Christian master because they are "now brothers" hands Rome a reason to call the gospel anti-social, and the gospel's slow revolution depends on not being caricatured before it can do its work.

**Where this leaves us:** the church took 1700-1800 years to articulate clear abolition, and the question of why — whether because the gospel's implications had not yet been worked out, or because the church had been captured by its own complicity — is the question every contemporary engagement with 6:1-2 must face. The chapter's accommodationism is not mere weakness; it is also evidence of how slowly social transformation can be willed even by movements that contain its theological seeds.

**Tension Two: Mammon and Asceticism.** The chapter's wealth ethic — "godliness with contentment is great gain" (6:6), "the love of money is a root of all kinds of evils" (6:10), and the rich "in this present age" charged to be generous (6:17-19) — has produced one of the most contested theological conversations in Christianity.

The Catholic social teaching tradition reads 6:6-10 against unfettered acquisitiveness and 6:17-19 as a charter for generosity — *Rerum Novarum* through *Fratelli Tutti*, treating *autarkeia* (6:6) as a positive virtue compatible with the proper enjoyment of created goods and the chapter as a stewardship rather than a renunciation framework. The Reformed tradition, especially in its post-Weber readings, has tended toward a more positive theology of work and wealth; Max Weber's *Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1905) argued — controversially, and later complicated by Tawney, Brad Gregory, and Eugene McCarraher — that Calvinist theology produced the cultural conditions for modern capitalism, and the strain of treating prosperity as evidence of blessing has sometimes softened the chapter's edge. The Anabaptist tradition has held to rigorous simplicity and community-of-goods (Hutterite communities, Mennonite Mutual Aid, the Bruderhof), and the Eastern Orthodox kenotic-poverty tradition — the Holy Fools, the New Martyrs, Mother Maria Skobtsova who died at Ravensbrück — embodies an economic asceticism rooted in identification with the poor. The prosperity-gospel movement, documented by Kate Bowler in *Blessed*, reads 6:6 as a promise that godliness produces material wealth — a reading the chapter itself appears to deny in 6:5b ("imagining that godliness is a means of gain") — while David Bentley Hart has argued it is not a marginal heresy but the natural theological expression of late capitalism. Against it, a long line of socially-engaged reading from Basil and Chrysostom through modern Catholic social teaching takes 6:9-10 in continuity with the Hebrew prophets and Jesus' Lukan emphases on the poor, reading the chapter alongside Luke 6:20-26 as a single hermeneutical complex.

The position to steelman is the accommodationist Reformed-Wesleyan synthesis: "earn all you can, save all you can, give all you can." This reading neither demands voluntary poverty nor permits acquisitiveness; it threads the needle of 6:6-19 with practical pastoral wisdom that has actually shaped generations of believers' financial behavior. It insists on the goodness of work, the legitimacy of property, the obligation of generosity, and the danger of attachment — and it does so without requiring the radical voluntary poverty that the chapter does not unambiguously demand.

Wright, in the same commentary, makes *autarkeia* (6:6) the hinge: "contentment" is not stoic resignation but the freedom of those who know they brought nothing into the world and will take nothing out, so that money loses its grip and generosity (6:18) becomes the natural overflow rather than a strain. He ties this directly to the letter's last word, "guard the deposit" (6:20): the deposit is the gospel itself, and a church gripped by the love of money has, in effect, already lost it.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter's wealth ethic operates with categorical seriousness about the dangers of acquisitiveness while leaving substantial room for legitimate use of created goods, and every Christian tradition's reading of the chapter is also that tradition's working theology of late-modern economic life — which is why the conversation will not finish.

**The unresolved question that lands:** if the chapter explicitly tells slaves to honor their masters and the rich to be generous, but does not tell masters to free their slaves or warn that wealth held under conditions of structural injustice may be tainted at the root, then what does it mean that the same chapter ends with "guard the deposit" — and what exactly is the deposit, if not also the unfinished work the chapter itself leaves to those who come after?

## The Thread

**Philemon** — Paul's letter to Philemon about the runaway slave Onesimus is the only New Testament document that engages a specific case of Christian master-slave relationship. The contrast with 1 Timothy 6:1-2 is instructive: Philemon does not categorically demand manumission, but it pressures Philemon toward it through the language of brotherhood and "more than a slave." Reading the two together complicates any simple accommodationist or abolitionist verdict.

**Luke 12:13-21 / Luke 16:19-31** — The parable of the Rich Fool and the parable of Lazarus and the Rich Man. Both parables are direct theological background for 1 Timothy 6:6-10 and 6:17-19. Luke's economic theology is more pointed than 1 Timothy's, and the question of how 1 Timothy's measured ethic relates to Jesus' own more radical sayings is a live theological question, not a settled exegetical conclusion.

**2 Timothy 1:14** — "By the Holy Spirit who dwells within us, guard the good deposit (παραθήκη) entrusted to you." The deposit-language of 1 Timothy 6:20 is taken up and developed here, and reading the two passages together gives the metaphor's full theological weight: the deposit is not just doctrine but a Spirit-mediated trust transmitted across generations. Whether the same hand wrote both letters, or whether 2 Timothy is an even later development of the metaphor, the canonical placement makes the connection unavoidable.

**Revelation 19:16** — "On his robe and on his thigh he has a name written, King of kings and Lord of lords." The same title as 1 Timothy 6:15. Whether 1 Timothy is drawing on common Hellenistic Jewish liturgical language, or whether Revelation is drawing on the same hymnic tradition the Pastoral author quotes, the cross-reference makes the doxology of 6:15-16 more than a private literary flourish — it is one node in a wider Christian-Jewish liturgical theology of divine sovereignty against imperial pretension.
