# Philippians 2: The Mind That Came Down

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter sits inside a letter from a Roman colony's most famous Christian convert to the colony itself. Philippi had been refounded as *Colonia Iulia Augusta Philippensis* in 42 BC by Mark Antony and Octavian after the Battle of Philippi, where the assassins of Julius Caesar — Brutus and Cassius — were defeated, and was settled with veterans of the praetorian cohorts. Augustus reinforced the settlement after Actium in 31 BC. Latin dominated the city's epigraphic record, the imperial cult was cultivated through the provincial assembly of Macedonia (the *koinon* with its priesthoods of Roma and Augustus), and Roman citizenship was widely held among the urban elite. The honor culture of the *cursus honorum* — the public ladder of civic and military offices by which a Roman man rose toward *dignitas* — saturated public imagination. Joseph Hellerman's *Reconstructing Honor in Roman Philippi* (2005) reads the entire letter, and chapter 2 above all, as a counter-honor inversion: a poem about a divine figure who descended the *cursus*, all the way to the most degrading Roman punishment, and was then exalted by God rather than by acclamation. Peter Oakes, in *Philippians: From People to Letter*, models the city's social pyramid demographically and shows that suffering of the kind Paul presupposes — economic loss, public shaming, occasional violence — was a normal experience for Christians of the artisan and slave classes who likely composed most of the church.

The place-of-writing question is unresolved and bears directly on the chapter. Three candidates compete. The traditional view — Rome, c. 60–62 AD, during Paul's house arrest at the end of Acts 28 — appeals to 1:13's *praitōrion* and 4:22's "Caesar's household," and is defended by Peter O'Brien (NIGTC), Markus Bockmuehl (BNTC), F.F. Bruce, and Moisés Silva (BECNT). A second view places Paul in Caesarea (Acts 23–26, c. 58–60 AD), pointing to Herod's *praetorium* in Acts 23:35; Gerald Hawthorne (WBC) makes that case. A third view, Ephesus during Paul's Acts 19 ministry in the mid-50s, was advanced by G.S. Duncan in *St. Paul's Ephesian Ministry*, and is treated seriously by Bonnie Thurston (Sacra Pagina) and John Reumann (Anchor Yale Bible). The Ephesian hypothesis has the logistical advantage of compressing the multiple back-and-forth journeys the letter assumes (notably the news cycle around Epaphroditus's illness in 2:25–30). For chapter 2 the stakes are real: if the letter is Ephesian and pre-Romans, the theology of 2:6–11 sits earlier in Paul's career than is often supposed, with implications for how to read the development of his Christology.

The textual base is secure. P46, the Pauline papyrus codex from c. 200, contains Philippians; the great fourth-century uncials Sinaiticus (א) and Vaticanus (B) preserve it, and Alexandrinus (A) adds a fifth-century witness. The Christ passage in 2:6–11 is among the most stable texts in the Pauline corpus. Variants are minor — there is, for instance, a question whether 2:1 should read *tis* or *ti splanchna*, and whether *epigeiōn* in 2:10 is original — none of which alter the theological architecture. Excavations at Philippi have uncovered the forum, two large basilicas, and the Octagon church traditionally identified with Paul's prison; what mattered for the original recipients, though, was not pious geography but the inverse-imperial cadence of the chapter's central poem.

Greek key terms anchor the chapter: μορφή *morphē* (form — 2:6, 2:7); ἁρπαγμός *harpagmos* (something to be grasped or exploited, 2:6); ἐκένωσεν *ekenōsen* (he emptied, 2:7); ἐταπείνωσεν *etapeinōsen* (he humbled, 2:8); ὑπερύψωσεν *hyperupsōsen* (highly exalted, 2:9); κύριος *kyrios* (Lord, 2:11); κατεργάζεσθε *katergazesthe* (work out, 2:12); ἐνεργῶν *energōn* (the one working, 2:13); φρονεῖν *phronein* (to think, to be of mind, threaded throughout).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter's center of gravity is the passage at 2:5–11 that scholarship has called the *Carmen Christi* since Ralph Martin's 1967 monograph of that name. Whether it is a pre-Pauline hymn quoted by Paul, a Pauline composition with hymnic elements, or rhetorical prose shaped by parallelism is the most debated question in the chapter's literary history. Ernst Lohmeyer's 1928 *Kyrios Jesus* first proposed a pre-Pauline Aramaic hymn behind the Greek text. Ernst Käsemann's 1950 essay "A Critical Analysis of Phil 2:5–11" refined and partly contested Lohmeyer, fixing the Christ-poem at the center of mid-twentieth-century Christology. Stanza divisions vary widely — three couplets, six strophes, two strophes — and no scheme commands consensus. Modern commentators (Bockmuehl, Reumann, Stephen Fowl in *The Story of Christ in the Ethics of Paul* and his *Two Horizons* commentary, Matthew Gordley in *Teaching Through Song*) have largely concluded that the passage is hymnic in feel but probably Pauline in composition or substantial reworking, not a quoted independent fragment. Even Martin's title "Carmen Christi" stuck though many of his specific conclusions — Adamic Christology, Aramaic *Vorlage* — no longer command assent.

The chapter's wider genre is friendship-letter paraenesis. Stanley Stowers's *Letter Writing in Greco-Roman Antiquity* classifies Philippians as *philophronesis*, the genre of intimate friend-to-friend exchange; Loveday Alexander has shown how Pauline letters blend the friendship form with diatribe and testimonial; Abraham Malherbe's *Paul and the Popular Philosophers* situates Paul's moral idiom in Stoic and Cynic letter culture. The chapter opens with paraenesis (2:1–4), stages the Christ-poem as the warrant for that paraenesis (2:5–11), draws practical conclusions (2:12–18), and closes with travel news that simultaneously commends two model figures, Timothy and Epaphroditus, who embody the very *phronein* the poem demands (2:19–30). Read this way, 2:19–30 is not appendix but argument: Timothy and Epaphroditus do in miniature what Christ did supremely.

The chapter's literary architecture is held together by *phronein* — to think, to be of one mind, to set the mind on. The verb opens the unit (2:2 *to auto phronēte*), bridges into the poem (2:5 *touto phroneite en hymin*), and recurs as the letter's signature term (1:7, 3:15, 3:19, 4:2, 4:10). Paul is asking the Philippians to share a mind, and the mind he asks them to share is the mind that came down. The composition-unity question — whether Philippians is one letter or a stitched composite of two or three (W. Schmithals, B.D. Rahtjen, F.W. Beare argued the latter; Bockmuehl, O'Brien, Reumann, Fowl, Witherington, Garland defend unity) — does not seriously affect chapter 2, whose internal coherence is striking and which is part of the same letter-fragment in every reconstruction.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The chapter's reception history is denser than that of any other Pauline passage outside Romans. It has carried the weight of every major Christological controversy from Nicaea forward.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic readings center on 2:6–11 as proof that the eternal Son took on humanity without ceasing to be divine. Athanasius, in *On the Incarnation* and *Orations Against the Arians*, reads *en morphē theou* as full divinity and *ekenōsen* as the assumption of humanity, not the surrender of deity — the Word does not subtract; he adds. Cyril of Alexandria, in his *Christological Letters* and the *Commentary on John*, develops this into the formula that became Chalcedonian: one person, two natures, the divine nature unchanged in the incarnation. The Council of Chalcedon (451) — *vere Deus, vere homo* — codified the patristic non-kenotic reading. Chrysostom's homilies on Philippians moralize the passage paraenetically while preserving its high Christology. Augustine reads 2:6–11 in Trinitarian register, especially in *De Trinitate*, working out how the Son's obedience to the Father in the incarnation does not imply ontological subordination.

The medieval reception comes through Aquinas's *Lectura* on Philippians and his treatment of the incarnation in *Summa Theologiae* III qq.1–26, where 2:7's *ekenōsen* is glossed as the assumption of human nature without loss of divine attributes. Bernard of Clairvaux's sermons on humility lean on 2:8 — *etapeinōsen heauton* — and the medieval mystical tradition reads *kenōsis* as the pattern of the soul's descent into self-naughting before its ascent to God.

The Reformation produced two divergent confessional readings of *kenōsis*. The Lutheran *communicatio idiomatum*, codified in the Formula of Concord (1577) and especially the Solid Declaration Article VIII, holds that in the incarnation the divine and human natures share their attributes through the personal union — the *genus majestaticum* — so that the human Christ truly possesses divine majesty. The Reformed *extra Calvinisticum*, stated in Calvin's *Institutes* II.13.4, holds the opposite emphasis: the divine Logos is not exhausted by the incarnation; even while incarnate, the Son sustains the cosmos *etiam extra carnem*, "also outside the flesh." The two traditions agree on Chalcedon and divide on what *ekenōsen* implies for divine attributes during the incarnation. Luther's commentary on Philippians and his Christmas sermons make 2:5–11 the heart of the *theologia crucis*: God revealed precisely under the contrary, the divine glory hidden in the cross.

The nineteenth century produced the most theologically radical reading of the chapter — kenotic Christology proper. Gottfried Thomasius (*Christi Person und Werk*, 1853–61) argued that the Son, in the incarnation, literally surrendered his "relative" attributes (omnipresence, omniscience, omnipotence) while retaining his "essential" attributes (love, holiness). P.T. Forsyth's *The Person and Place of Jesus Christ* (1909) and Charles Gore's *The Incarnation of the Son of God* (1891) developed Anglican versions of the move; the position was attractive to theologians wrestling with the historical Jesus's apparently limited human consciousness. Catholic teaching has consistently judged radical kenoticism heretical (the CDF declaration *Mysterium Filii Dei*, 1972, is the most explicit modern statement). Karl Rahner, in *Grundkurs des Glaubens*, retrieves a non-radical kenosis: God can become other than himself precisely because he is who he is. Hans Urs von Balthasar's *Theo-Drama* IV and *Mysterium Paschale* offer the most ambitious twentieth-century kenotic theology — the cross as the inner-trinitarian event, the Son's self-surrender mirroring the Father's eternal self-giving, *kenōsis* not as alien to deity but as expression of it.

The contemporary scholarly debate centers on three related questions. First, is *en morphē theou* (2:6) a pre-existence claim or an Adamic claim? James Dunn's *Christology in the Making* (1980, rev. 1989) famously argued for Adamic-only Christology in Phil 2:6: Christ is the second Adam who, unlike the first, refused to grasp at equality with God; *morphē* parallels *eikōn* in Gen 1:26–27 LXX, and pre-existence is not in view. Dunn's reading has been heavily contested by Larry Hurtado (*Lord Jesus Christ*, 2003), Richard Bauckham (*God Crucified*, 1998; *Jesus and the God of Israel*, 2008), Gordon Fee (*Pauline Christology*, 2007), and N.T. Wright (*Paul and the Faithfulness of God*) — all of whom argue for pre-existent divine Christology with the Adamic motif present but secondary. Second, what does *harpagmos* mean? Roy Hoover's 1971 *Harvard Theological Review* article on the idiom *harpagmon hēgēsato* — followed and extended by N.T. Wright's 1986 *Journal of Theological Studies* article "ἁρπαγμός and the Meaning of Philippians 2:5–11" — argued that *harpagmos* refers to "something to be exploited / used to one's advantage": Christ already had divine equality and chose not to exploit it. This Hoover-Wright reading has shifted the consensus and is now followed by Bockmuehl, O'Brien, Fee, and most major commentators. Third, do the Isa 45:23 echoes in 2:10–11 — "every knee shall bow… every tongue confess" — apply to Jesus a YHWH-text in such a way as to include him within the divine identity? Bauckham's *God Crucified* coined "divine identity Christology" for this move; Hurtado documents the early "binitarian devotional pattern" in *Lord Jesus Christ*. Steelman Dunn: the second-Adam pattern can be made to do real work, the *morphē theou* / *eikōn theou* parallel is verbally close, and a non-pre-existent Christology has the merit of locating earliest Christology firmly within Jewish monotheistic resources without appealing to a "high" leap. Steelman Bauckham/Hurtado/Wright/Fee: the Isa 45:23 quotation, the *harpagmos* idiom, and the explicit *kyrios* acclamation are very hard to reduce to Adamic categories without remainder.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** The "Paul within Judaism" school — Krister Stendahl, E.P. Sanders, Daniel Boyarin, Mark Nanos, Paula Fredriksen, Pamela Eisenbaum — has reframed Pauline studies but engages chapter 2 less than chapter 3, since the autobiographical Jewish material concentrates there. Where Jewish scholarship most often pushes back is on 2:9–11. The application to Jesus of Isa 45:23 — a text in which YHWH alone declares his exclusive sovereignty — raises hard questions about the relation of nascent Christology to Jewish monotheism. Daniel Boyarin's *Border Lines* argues that the boundary between "Judaism" and "Christianity" is constructed later than Paul; Christ-devotion of the *Carmen Christi* type, on Boyarin's reading, lies along a continuum of Second Temple Jewish reflection on God's hypostases (Wisdom, Word, Logos) and only retroactively becomes the line of demarcation. Other Jewish scholars (Alan Segal in *Two Powers in Heaven*, Daniel Boyarin in *The Jewish Gospels*) have argued that there were already Jewish traditions of a second divine figure — though the consensus position in contemporary Jewish thought continues to find the inclusion of Jesus within YHWH's identity theologically incompatible with rabbinic monotheism as it later developed. **Where this leaves us:** the Christological intensity of 2:6–11 is precisely where Jewish-Christian dialogue is hardest, and the contemporary scholarly conversation is more honest about that than older harmonizing readings allowed.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — Pre-existence and *harpagmos*.** What does Phil 2:6–11 actually claim about Christ's identity? The Dunn position (*Christology in the Making*, 1980) reads the passage as Adamic Christology: the man Christ Jesus, made in the image of God like every human, refused (unlike Adam) to grasp at the divine equality the Genesis narrative portrays Adam as reaching for; he chose instead the path of obedience and was exalted. Pre-existence is not in view; *morphē theou* is anthropological, not metaphysical. The Bauckham/Hurtado/Fee/Wright position, dominant in current scholarship, reads the passage as an unambiguous claim about the pre-existent divine Christ who, possessing divine equality from eternity, chose not to exploit it but instead emptied himself by taking the form of a slave. The Hoover-Wright lexical work on *harpagmos* — that the idiom *harpagmon hēgēsato* refers to using something one already possesses to one's advantage — settles the philological question in favor of pre-existence: one does not refuse to exploit what one does not have. Catholic Christology (Chalcedon, *Mysterium Filii Dei*), Eastern Orthodox theology, and confessional Protestantism (Lutheran, Reformed, Wesleyan) all converge on the pre-existent divine reading; the Adamic-only reading has remained a distinctly minority academic position. Steelman Dunn fairly: the *eikōn theou* / *morphē theou* parallel is verbally striking, the second-Adam motif is undeniably present in Paul (Rom 5, 1 Cor 15), and an Adamic reading avoids the embarrassment of having Paul construct a high pre-existent Christology in what may be one of his earliest letters (if Ephesian provenance is granted). The reading also has the ecumenical advantage of staying inside Jewish monotheistic resources without invoking later trinitarian categories. **Where this leaves us:** the Hoover-Wright reading of *harpagmos*, combined with the Isa 45:23 quotation in 2:10–11, has shifted the burden of proof onto the Adamic-only reading, but the Adamic motif remains genuinely present as a secondary structure within a pre-existence framework.

**Tension 2 — Kenosis and the divine nature.** What does it mean for God to "empty himself"? Five major positions divide the field. (1) Patristic non-kenotic Chalcedonianism (Athanasius, Cyril, the Council of Chalcedon): the Son does not surrender any divine attribute; *ekenōsen* refers to the assumption of humanity, an addition rather than a subtraction; the divine and human natures coexist unmixed and undivided in the one person. (2) Lutheran *communicatio idiomatum / genus majestaticum* (Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VIII): the human nature receives divine attributes through the personal union, so that the incarnate Christ's flesh truly possesses divine majesty even while veiled. (3) Reformed *extra Calvinisticum* (Calvin, *Institutes* II.13.4): the divine Logos is not exhausted by the incarnation; the Son sustains the cosmos *etiam extra carnem* even while in the womb of Mary, on the cross, in the tomb. (4) Nineteenth-century kenoticism (Thomasius, Forsyth, Gore): the Son literally surrenders certain "relative" divine attributes — omnipresence, omniscience, omnipotence — for the duration of the incarnation, retaining only "essential" attributes such as love and holiness. (5) Balthasarian Trinitarian kenosis (*Theo-Drama* IV, *Mysterium Paschale*): *kenōsis* expresses rather than contradicts deity, because the Father's eternal self-giving to the Son is itself kenotic; the cross is the temporal projection of an eternal Trinitarian kenotic structure. The Catholic magisterium has rejected radical nineteenth-century kenoticism (CDF, *Mysterium Filii Dei*, 1972) while preserving Balthasarian and Rahnerian non-radical readings. Reformed and Lutheran traditions divide between themselves over the *genus majestaticum* / *extra Calvinisticum* question and largely reject radical kenoticism. The Wesleyan tradition, less committed to the Lutheran-Reformed scholastic categories, has been more open to moderate kenoticism. Steelman radical kenoticism: it takes seriously the apparent limits of Jesus's earthly knowledge (Mark 13:32, Luke 2:52), the genuine human suffering of the cross, and the prima facie sense of *ekenōsen* as actual emptying rather than addition; it answers the modern question of how a fully omniscient mind could be authentically human; and it preserves the cross as cost rather than performance. **Where this leaves us:** the Chalcedonian non-kenotic reading remains the ecumenical baseline, but the chapter's text genuinely supports stronger kenotic readings than Chalcedon allows, which is why Balthasar's retrieval has been so attractive across confessional lines.

**The unresolved question:** If 2:13's "God works in you both to will and to work" describes salvation as fully divine action and 2:12's "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling" demands it as fully human action, what kind of agent are we — and is the answer the same for the cosmic Christ of 2:6–11 as for the saints of 2:12–18, or is the *kenōsis* the very bridge that makes the parallel theologically coherent?

## The Thread

**Isaiah 45:22–23 LXX.** "To me every knee shall bow, every tongue swear allegiance" — a YHWH self-oath of exclusive sovereignty. Phil 2:10–11 quotes the line and applies it to Jesus, performing the inclusion of Christ within the divine identity that Bauckham's *God Crucified* analyzes. The Isaiah text is the single most important scriptural background to the chapter.

**Genesis 1:26–27 / Genesis 3:5.** The image-of-God language (*kat' eikona theou* LXX) underlies *en morphē theou*, and Adam's reaching to "be like God" supplies the contrast Dunn presses for Adamic Christology. Whatever one decides about pre-existence, the second-Adam pattern is part of the chapter's resonance.

**Daniel 7:13–14.** The "one like a son of man" who is brought before the Ancient of Days and given universal dominion supplies an exaltation pattern that Phil 2:9–11 echoes — sovereignty conferred by God to a human-shaped figure to whom every nation will render service.

**Romans 14:11.** Paul quotes the same Isa 45:23 oracle in Romans, but applies it to "God" rather than to Christ, with no apparent sense of contradiction. Read alongside Phil 2:10–11, the Romans usage shows how naturally Paul moves between God-language and Christ-language at exactly the point where Jewish monotheism is most at stake.
