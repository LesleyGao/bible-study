# Judges 1: The Inventory of Failure

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Judges 1 opens at a hinge moment in the southern Levant. Joshua is dead, the centralized leadership of the wilderness generation has dissolved, and the loose tribal coalition that crossed the Jordan now faces the unfinished work of dispossessing the Canaanite city-states that controlled the agricultural valleys. The political situation on the ground — independent of the biblical text — is illuminated by the Amarna Letters (mid-14th century BCE), a corpus of roughly 380 clay tablets recovered from Akhenaten's archive at Tell el-Amarna. In them, Canaanite mayors of cities like Jerusalem (the famous letters of Abdi-Heba), Megiddo, and Shechem write desperately to the Egyptian pharaoh begging for archers against rival mayors and roving bands called *'apiru* (often translated "Habiru"). Whether or not the *'apiru* are linguistically or sociologically related to "Hebrew," the picture they sketch — fragmented Canaanite polities, no central authority, predatory marginal groups exploiting weakness — is precisely the political vacuum into which the Iron Age I highland settlements (and the Israelite tribes) emerged. The Merneptah Stele (c. 1207 BCE) gives us the earliest extra-biblical mention of "Israel" as a people-group (not a territory) already established in Canaan, which sets a *terminus ante quem* for the events Judges 1 narrates and confirms that "Israel" by then was a recognizable ethnic-political entity.

Materially, the world of Judges 1 is the Iron Age I highlands — small, unfortified four-room houses on terraced ridges, plastered cisterns and silos hewn into bedrock (the cistern technology that allowed permanent year-round settlement away from springs), no pig bones in the faunal record (a marker that distinguishes highland sites from coastal Philistine sites at this stratum), and a striking absence of monumental architecture. The economy was subsistence: wheat, barley, olives, grapes, sheep, goats. The chapter's repeated note that tribe after tribe "could not drive out the inhabitants of the plain because they had chariots of iron" (v. 19) is materially accurate. Iron was not yet truly common, but the Canaanite city-states of the Jezreel and the coastal Shephelah had inherited Bronze Age chariotry and, increasingly, iron-fitted weaponry. Israelite highlanders had donkeys, slings, javelins, and the topographic advantage of broken hill country. They could not contest open valleys.

The chapter is part of the Deuteronomistic History (Deut–2 Kings), a unified theological work first articulated by Martin Noth in his 1943 *Überlieferungsgeschichtliche Studien*. Noth argued the whole sweep was composed in the exile (6th c. BCE) by a single editor working with older sources. Frank Moore Cross's 1973 modification (*Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*) introduced the now-dominant double-redaction model: a primary Josianic edition (late 7th century, written to support Josiah's reform) followed by an Exilic update that reframed the whole as theodicy for the Babylonian catastrophe. Richard Nelson and others have refined this. Judges 1 itself, however, has long been recognized as a *non-deuteronomistic* layer — Robert Boling (*Judges*, Anchor Bible, 1975) and J. Alberto Soggin both argue ch. 1 preserves an older, more realistic conquest tradition, redactionally bolted onto the front of the book to set up the failure motif. Notice how it contradicts Joshua 21:43-45's totalizing claim that "not one of all their enemies had withstood them." The two accounts cannot be harmonized at the historical level; they can only be read as two different theological perspectives juxtaposed.

A few Hebrew terms repay attention. The verb *yaraš* (יָרַשׁ), conventionally "drive out" or "dispossess," appears as a leitmotif throughout the chapter — and crucially in its negated form, *lo' horiš* (לֹא הוֹרִישׁ), "did not drive out," which functions as a refrain (vv. 19, 21, 27, 29, 30, 31, 33). The hiphil stem implies causative action: not just defeating but expelling, taking the land into permanent possession. English flattens this into a generic "did not drive out"; the Hebrew makes it a structural verdict against tribe after tribe. Second, *mas* (מַס), "forced labor" or "corvée" (vv. 28, 30, 33, 35), is the same word used for Solomon's later labor levies in 1 Kings 9 — and the same word the Egyptians used for Israel's labor in Exodus. The narrator is quietly noting that Israel becomes the new Egypt to those who remain. Third, *ḥerem* (חֵרֶם), "devoted to destruction" (v. 17, the etymology of *Hormah*), is the technical term for the war-ban — total annihilation as religious offering. Susan Niditch's *War in the Hebrew Bible* unpacks this concept as a deeply ANE category; the Mesha Stele (c. 840 BCE) records King Mesha of Moab "devoting" Nebo to his god Chemosh, using a cognate of *ḥerem* (Moabite *ḥrm*) — direct linguistic confirmation that this practice was not uniquely Israelite. Fourth, the place-name *Bochim* (בֹּכִים), "weepers," in 2:1, is etiological — the chapter ahead names the place by what happened there. And fifth, *ʾadoni-bezeq* (אֲדֹנִי־בֶזֶק), "Lord of Bezek," is a throne-name not a personal name, paralleling Adoni-zedek of Jerusalem in Joshua 10 — a clue that some scholars (Boling) think these may be variant traditions of a single figure.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre-wise, Judges 1 is a non-deuteronomistic conquest summary functioning as the prologue's first half (1:1–2:5). It reads almost as an inventory or military gazetteer: tribe by tribe, city by city, success and failure tabulated. The structure is deliberate. The chapter moves from Judah's qualified victory (with the Adoni-bezek episode and the Achsah negotiation as set pieces) through Joseph/Benjamin in the center to a steady cascade of northern failures — Manasseh, Ephraim, Zebulun, Asher, Naphtali, Dan. Barry Webb (NICOT, 2012) and Daniel Block (NICOT, 1999) both note that the geography descends roughly from south to north, and the *theological success rate* descends in lockstep. Judah, archetype of the eventual Davidic monarchy, succeeds substantially. By the end of the chapter we reach Dan, the tribe that will collapse most spectacularly later (chs. 17–18), and Dan can't even hold the plain — the Amorites push them *back into the hills*, a reverse conquest.

The literary devices are quiet but precise. The repeated formula *lo' horiš* — "did not drive out" — appears at least seven times (the count varies by how you parse the verses) and functions as an inclusio of failure. Robert Alter (*The Art of Biblical Narrative*, 1981) calls this kind of repetition "the technique of repetition with significant variation": each instance adds a new tribe, a new geography, a new excuse, until the pattern itself becomes the indictment. Marc Brettler (*The Book of Judges*, 2002) reads the whole chapter as deliberately structured around this antithesis between Judah-success and northern-failure — a southern, pro-Davidic redaction that sets up Judges' implicit argument that the north was rotten from the start.

There are at least four small embedded narratives that interrupt the gazetteer rhythm. The Adoni-bezek vignette (vv. 4–7) is a *measure-for-measure* tale, the kind of moral anecdote ANE wisdom literature loves: the king who mutilated seventy royal captives is mutilated himself, and the narrator allows him the line "as I have done, so God has repaid me" — the only theological reflection by a foreigner in the chapter. The Achsah scene (vv. 11–15) is more subtle. A type-scene: a young woman dismounts from her donkey and asks a gift of her father, paralleling Rebekah's dismount in Genesis 24. But here the gift is *land and water* — the means of survival in the Negev — and Achsah is the only female agent in the entire prologue. She negotiates. She says, "Give me a blessing." The verb she uses is the same verb of patriarchal blessing. Robert Alter and Daniel Block have both noted how this brief episode functions as a quiet counterpoint: the one woman who *takes initiative* in the prologue stands as a contrast to the women later in the book to whom violence is done (Achsah is paired across the book with Jephthah's daughter, who also has to ask her father for something). Third, the Bethel infiltration (vv. 22–26) is a dark mirror of the Rahab story in Joshua 2: an informant betrays his city in exchange for safe passage. But where Rahab confessed YHWH, this man simply gets safe passage and *founds a new Luz* in Hittite territory — the Canaanite religious-cultural pattern is preserved and propagated. The narrator is making a quiet point.

Textually, Judges 1 is reasonably stable. The Dead Sea Scrolls 4QJudg-a (which famously omits the deuteronomistic insertion at 6:7-10) and 4QJudg-b cover other portions of the book. LXX Judges has two major recensions, A (Codex Alexandrinus) and B (Codex Vaticanus), but the variants in ch. 1 are minor compared to chs. 6, 16, and 19–21. The most notable LXX expansion in this chapter is at v. 18, where the LXX *negates* Judah's capture of Gaza, Ashkelon, and Ekron — reading "Judah did *not* take" — apparently to harmonize with the later assertion that the Philistine cities remained un-conquered. The MT's positive reading is the more difficult and almost certainly original.

Located in the book's argument, ch. 1 is the indictment that makes everything that follows intelligible. The deuteronomistic cycle in ch. 2 (sin-oppression-cry-deliverance-relapse) needs a *cause*. Ch. 1 is the cause: incomplete dispossession means the Canaanites remain, and they remain as a religious-cultural temptation, not just a military problem. Read as a whole, 1:1–2:5 is the geography of compromise; 2:6 onward is the theology of consequence.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, Judges 1 sits exactly between conquest and kingship. Genesis–Deuteronomy is promise and exodus. Joshua is initial fulfillment. Judges is the slow unraveling that creates the demand for a king. 1 Samuel 8 — when Israel asks for a king "like all the nations" — is incomprehensible without the failures Judges 1 inaugurates. The chapter also reaches *back*: the Achsah-Caleb episode echoes Joshua 15:13–19 almost verbatim (a literary doublet, probably from a shared source rather than direct copying), and the Bethel scene rhymes with Genesis 28's Bethel and Joshua 2's Jericho.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Pseudo-Philo's *Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum* (1st century CE) — perhaps the single most important Second Temple retelling of Judges — treats the period as a sequence of moral exempla, and significantly *expands* the role of female figures (foreshadowing his treatment of Deborah and Jephthah's daughter). Josephus, in *Antiquities* 5, smooths out the conquest failures and presents Israel's setbacks as straightforward divine discipline for Canaanite intermarriage — already a midrashic move. The rabbis in the Talmud (*Yoma* 22b, *Zevachim* 118b) take Adoni-bezek's "as I have done, so God has repaid me" as a foundational text for *middah ke-neged middah* (measure for measure) — the principle that divine justice mirrors human action, which becomes a cornerstone of rabbinic theodicy. Rashi (11th c.) reads the chapter strictly, treating each failure as a specific covenantal breach; he is particularly hard on the tribes that *enslaved rather than expelled* the Canaanites, since this preserved the religious threat. Radak (David Kimhi, 12th–13th c.) and Ralbag (Gersonides, 14th c.) introduce more rationalist explanations — Ralbag in particular notes the *military realism* of the iron chariots verse, refusing to read it purely as a theological failure when it might be read as a constraint. Modern Jewish scholars: Yair Zakovitch has written extensively on Judges' literary patterning and the Achsah scene as a buried positive type-scene. Jon Levenson's broader work on the Deuteronomistic History informs how Jewish biblical scholarship now reads ch. 1 as a deliberately ironic counter-text to the optimism of Joshua.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Origen's Homilies on Judges (3rd c.) reads everything allegorically — Adoni-bezek's seventy mutilated kings become the seventy nations of Genesis 10 brought low, Caleb is faith, Achsah is the soul desiring spiritual waters from the Father. This allegorical mode dominated patristic reading of Judges until the Reformation. Augustine, in *City of God* 17, treats the conquest failures as a typological lesson about the church's incomplete warfare against sin in this age — a powerful reading that still echoes in evangelical preaching. Theodoret of Cyrus (5th c.) and Jerome both grapple with the ethics of *ḥerem* warfare; Theodoret tends toward allegory, Jerome toward historical concession (these were a different age's customs). Aquinas (*Summa* II-II Q.40, on war) defends the conquests as divinely commanded and therefore just, but the Glossa Ordinaria layers patristic readings without resolving the ethical tension. Calvin's *Sermons on the Book of Judges* (preached 1561, published posthumously) reads ch. 1 as a sober warning about "the corruption of our nature" — even after divine deliverance, we tolerate what we should expel. Calvin is unusually frank about the chapter's pessimism. Luther, by contrast, gave Judges relatively little attention.

The critical era reframed everything. Wellhausen's documentary hypothesis didn't bear directly on Judges, but his historical skepticism set the tone. Martin Noth's 1943 DH thesis — Deut through 2 Kings as a single exilic theological work — became foundational. Noth treated Judges 1 as a non-deuteronomistic source preserved for theological reasons by the redactor. Gerhard von Rad worked out the theology of "holy war" as a recoverable institution. Boling's Anchor Bible commentary (1975) pushed back against the maximalism of older conquest readings, embracing the realistic, fragmentary picture of Judges 1 as historically more credible than Joshua's totalizing claims. Soggin and Lawson Younger continued this. The current standard commentaries (Block NICOT, Webb NICOT, Butler WBC) read ch. 1 as deliberately programmatic — the editor wants you to feel the failure.

Recent literary scholarship has also pressed on the chapter's smaller scenes. Daniel Block (NICOT, 1999) and Barry Webb (NICOT, 2012) treat the Achsah pericope as a deliberately placed bright moment of agency the rest of the book will not sustain. The reception history of the conquest framework — its modern political afterlife from Puritan New England forward — has been flagged by Brueggemann and Christopher Wright as a misuse the text's own historical specificity should preclude.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Both Jewish and Christian traditions agree the chapter is about *covenantal failure*. Both treat the Canaanite remnant as a spiritual threat. They diverge sharply, however, on (a) the *typology*: Christian readers consistently read the chapter as a figure of ongoing sanctification (incomplete victory over sin); Jewish readers tend to keep it historical and ethical, focused on the actual covenantal community. (b) Adoni-bezek: Jewish tradition makes him a foundational example of measure-for-measure justice; Christian tradition either allegorizes the seventy kings or quickly moves on. (c) Achsah: largely ignored in Christian premodern reading; given more weight in Jewish midrash and in modern literary scholarship (Alter, Block, Webb) across both traditions.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author believe God was doing? The deuteronomistic editor believed YHWH had given the land — the verb is past tense, *natan*, "I have given" (v. 2) — and that the failure to take possession was *Israel's*, not God's. The land is gift; dispossession is response. The author believed that incomplete obedience is itself disobedience, and that the religious presence of the Canaanites in the land would corrupt Israel from the inside. He believed in covenant as a real, juridical relationship with material consequences. Whether the modern reader finds this theology compelling is a separate question; what the chapter *means* in its own terms is unambiguous on this point.

But two genuine theological tensions deserve attention.

**Tension One: Did God Command Genocide, and What Do We Do With That?**

The chapter assumes the *ḥerem*, the war-ban that authorizes total destruction. Hormah (v. 17) is named for it. The cities of Judah are devoted; the Adoni-bezek episode shows ancient Near Eastern brutality on both sides. Modern readers cannot evade the question: did God command this, and if so, what does that mean about God?

*Catholic position* (and most patristic): The conquest was a unique, divinely authorized event, dispensational and not normative. Aquinas defends its justice on grounds of divine command (*Summa* II-II Q.64), but Catholic teaching since the *Catechism* §2261 unambiguously affirms the prohibition on intentional killing of innocents; the conquest is an exception located in salvation history, not a model.

*Protestant Reformed position*: Calvin is unusually direct — God as Lord of life had every right to command Canaanite destruction, and human moral squeamishness is itself a sign of fallenness. This position survives in some contemporary Reformed scholarship.

*Protestant evangelical mainstream (Webb, Block)*: The Canaanites were under judgment for specific, named practices (child sacrifice, ritual bestiality per Lev 18) and the conquest was judicial, not ethnic. Block in particular argues the *ḥerem* language is rhetorical hyperbole common in ANE military reports — the Mesha Stele uses similar language about Israelite cities — and the actual practice was more like dispossession than annihilation, supported by the very fact that Judges 1 shows survivors everywhere.

*Jewish position* (rabbinic to modern): The Talmud (*Berakhot* 28a) reports a tradition that Sennacherib's deportations had so mixed the nations that the *ḥerem* obligation was no longer applicable to any identifiable group — a practical termination of the law. Maimonides and Rashi treat the conquest as historical and not normative. Modern Jewish ethics (Yeshayahu Leibowitz, David Hartman) confront the texts directly without softening them, treating them as morally difficult passages requiring honest reckoning.

*Critical position*: The conquest never happened as described. Israel emerged largely from indigenous Canaanite populations (Israel Finkelstein's "highland settlement" model, supported by the lack of an LB-IA destruction layer at most claimed conquest sites). The *ḥerem* texts are post-exilic ideological projections defending later identity boundaries. The ethical problem then becomes: why was such a violent self-narrative composed at all?

*Steelmanning the position I find least intuitive*: I find the Calvinist position the hardest to sit with. But take it seriously: if God is the genuine source of all life and the moral order is grounded in divine will, then divine commands to take life are categorically different from human acts of taking life. The Canaanites were not innocent in the abstract — they were inhabitants of a culture whose ritual practices Leviticus 18 names with grim specificity. To assume our moral intuitions outrank revelation is, on Calvin's view, to make ourselves the standard. The position is internally coherent, even if it remains for many of us morally vertiginous.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter assumes a divine-command framework most modern readers cannot simply inhabit. Honest engagement requires neither special pleading nor airy dismissal — it requires sitting with the genuine difficulty that this is part of the canon, and that no interpretive tradition has fully resolved it.

**Tension Two: Whose Failure Is the Failure?**

The chapter blames Israel — *lo' horiš*, they did not drive out. But notice v. 19: "the LORD was with Judah, and he took possession of the hill country, but he could not drive out the inhabitants of the plain because they had chariots of iron." The chariots of iron defeat the LORD's presence with Judah? The text seems internally contradictory.

*Traditional Jewish reading* (Rashi, Radak): The "could not" reflects Israel's lack of faith; God could have given the chariot-plain into their hand if they had asked. The fault is theirs.

*Block / evangelical reading*: The text is anthropocentric here — describing what was militarily possible from the human side, with the theological "the LORD was with Judah" referring to the hill country campaign. No contradiction, just narrative shift.

*Critical reading* (Boling, Soggin): The verse preserves an older tradition where YHWH was a regional storm-god whose effective power was bounded — the chariots-of-iron disclaimer reflects an older theology of localized divine sovereignty, before the deuteronomistic editor elevated YHWH to universal Lord. This is theologically uncomfortable but historically illuminating.

*Contemporary process-theological reading*: God's power is genuinely persuasive, not coercive. Iron chariots were a real material constraint that even divine accompaniment did not magically override. Israel's responsibility was to do what was possible, not to expect supernatural shortcut.

**Where this leaves us:** The verse is a small theological earthquake in an otherwise neat indictment. It admits, accidentally or deliberately, that material constraints matter — that "the LORD was with Judah" did not erase iron technology. The deuteronomistic editor probably let it stand because it was already in his source; later traditions had to either contort it (Rashi) or absorb the discomfort.

**One unresolved question:** If incomplete obedience is itself disobedience, is there *any* faithful tribe in this chapter — even Judah, who could not take the plain — and if not, what does that mean for a covenant whose terms no human community can actually keep?

## The Thread

**Joshua 21:43–45.** "Not one of all their enemies had withstood them; the LORD had given all their enemies into their hands. Not one word of all the good promises that the LORD had made to the house of Israel had failed; all came to pass." Read this against Judges 1. The two cannot be harmonized historically; they can only be understood as two different theological frames laid side by side. Joshua tells the story of promise fulfilled. Judges tells the story of promise unraveling. Both are canonized.

**1 Samuel 8.** When Israel demands a king, remember Judges 1. The king is the answer to the failure that begins here. The northern tribes that "could not drive out" the Canaanites in ch. 1 will be the first to demand a centralized military monarchy — and will eventually be the first carried into exile. When you get to 1 Samuel 8, this chapter is what makes the demand intelligible.

**1 Kings 9:20–22.** Solomon conscripts the descendants of the very Canaanites Judges 1 says Israel "could not drive out" into corvée labor — *mas*, the same word — to build the temple. The unfinished business of Judges 1 becomes the labor force of the monarchy. The compromise persists for centuries.

**Matthew 1:5.** When Matthew traces Jesus' genealogy, he names Rahab and Ruth — Canaanite and Moabite women whose presence in the line of David is the *positive* mirror of Judges 1's failure. The covenant that could not expel the Canaanites instead absorbs them, redeems through them. Whether you read this as theological resolution or ironic reversal, Matthew is reading Judges 1 closely.
