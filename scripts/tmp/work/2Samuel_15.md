# 2 Samuel 15: The King Goes Out Weeping

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens with Absalom assembling the public theater of monarchy: a chariot (*merkavah*, מֶרְכָּבָה), horses, and fifty *ratsim* (רָצִים, "runners") to clear his path. This is not idle pageantry. Iron Age Levantine kingship encoded itself in mobility — chariotry was a Late Bronze prestige technology that, by the tenth century, marked aristocratic identity (cf. Yadin, *The Art of Warfare in Biblical Lands*; Mazar on Megiddo's chariot stables). Adonijah will mimic exactly this gesture in 1 Kings 1:5, suggesting an established usurper's repertoire. The fifty runners — possibly inflated for narrative effect — recall ANE royal courier corps attested in Mari and Neo-Assyrian texts (Postgate).

Absalom's daily station at the *sha'ar* (שַׁעַר, "gate") is materially specific. Iron Age IIA gate complexes at Tel Dan, Beersheba, Gezer, and (controversially) Megiddo include benched chambers explicitly used for adjudication, market exchange, and the receiving of grievances (Herzog; Faust). The text's setting is therefore not metaphorical: Absalom occupies the actual judicial node of the city, intercepting petitioners before they can reach the king's *mishpat* (מִשְׁפָּט, "judgment"). His complaint that "there is no man designated by the king to hear you" (15:3) speaks to a real administrative gap — David's bureaucracy, as McCarter notes, was thinly developed compared to Solomon's later cabinet (cf. 2 Sam 8:15-18; 20:23-26). The crown was outgrowing the king's personal capacity to dispense justice; Absalom exploited the bottleneck.

The Hebron pivot (15:7-10) is loaded. Hebron was David's original capital for seven and a half years (2 Sam 2:11; 5:5), the seat of his Judahite kingship before Jerusalem. Choosing Hebron for the coronation is geopolitical theater: it claims the older, deeper Judahite legitimacy and signals to the southern tribes that the Jerusalemite consolidation under David has betrayed local interests. Halpern, in *David's Secret Demons*, reads this as a Judahite revolt as much as a personal one — Absalom (whose mother Maacah was an Aramean princess from Geshur, 3:3) becomes the unlikely vehicle for southern grievance. The "four years" of 15:7 has textual issues: LXX and Syriac read "forty," manifestly impossible; 4QSama and most modern critics (McCarter, Anderson WBC) accept "four."

Ahithophel of Giloh (15:12) — Giloh likely Khirbet Jala in the Judahite hill country — is the chapter's silent earthquake. 2 Samuel 23:34 lists Eliam son of Ahithophel among David's Thirty; 11:3 names Bathsheba as "daughter of Eliam." If the identifications hold (McCarter, Halpern argue yes; Anderson is cautious), Ahithophel is Bathsheba's grandfather, and his defection is the slow detonation of David's Uriah crime. The narrative architecture of 2 Samuel makes the genealogy theologically necessary even if historically uncertain.

The military procession out of Jerusalem includes the *Kerethi* and *Pelethi* (כְּרֵתִי וּפְלֵתִי, possibly Cretans and Philistines — David's foreign mercenary bodyguard; cf. 8:18), and the six hundred *Gittim* (Gittites) under Ittai, who had attached themselves to David since his Philistine exile. The Wadi Kidron (*Nachal Qidron*, נַחַל קִדְרוֹן) and the *Har ha-Zeitim* (Mount of Olives, הַר הַזֵּיתִים) appear here for the first time as a topographic pair. Both are extant — the Kidron remains a seasonal wadi separating the City of David from the western slope of Olives; archaeology of the eastern slope has yielded Iron Age burials and agricultural terracing (Reich and Shukron). The route described — across Kidron, up the ascent of Olives, eastward toward the Jordan fords — is geographically coherent and would have been the natural escape vector toward Mahanaim.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

This chapter inaugurates what von Rad called the literary high point of the Hebrew Bible: the dramatic core of the Court History (2 Sam 9-20 + 1 Kings 1-2), the unit isolated by Leonhard Rost in 1926 and elaborated by Gunn (*The Story of King David*), Fokkelman (*King David*, vol. 1 of his four-volume Samuel), and Alter (*The David Story*). What von Rad meant by "origin of history-writing" is on display from the first verse: psychologically motivated characters, narrative time that compresses years into clauses, indirect characterization through reported action, and a refusal to moralize.

The genre is best classified as **historiographic prose narrative with apologetic substrate**. Halpern and McKenzie read the larger Court History as political apologetic — its function is to manage accusations against David: he did not engineer Absalom's death, he wept genuinely, he restrained vengeance against Saulides. The narrator's job is to make David's hands clean while showing his flaws. Chapter 15 begins this architecture by depicting David as **passive recipient of betrayal** — never plotting, never striking first, only fleeing.

Devices accumulate. The repeated *vayehi* + temporal clause ("and it came to pass after this," "at the end of four years") manages narrative pace; Bar-Efrat's work on biblical narrative time is the standard reference. **Dialogue dominates over description** — Sternberg's "scenic preference." Absalom's gate routine (15:2-6) is rendered in iterative-frequentative form ("would call... would say... would do") — the Hebrew imperfects build a habit-pattern that compresses four years of conspiracy into five verses. Then time abruptly shifts to punctual aspect at 15:7: the conspiracy is done, Hebron beckons.

The phrase "**stole the hearts of the men of Israel**" (15:6, *vay'ganev Avshalom et-lev anshe Yisrael*) is one of the chapter's most discussed lines. *Ganav* (גָּנַב, "to steal") is morally loaded — Absalom is a thief of affection. Alter notes the irony: David, who took (*laqach*) Bathsheba, has a son who steals (*ganav*) Israel. Sternberg observes the compound effect of *lev* (heart) + *ganav*: not merely persuaded but covertly extracted. Polzin reads the verb as the hinge between Davidic seduction and Absalomic seduction — both kings as charmers.

The exodus from Jerusalem (15:13-30) is structured as a **reverse processional**: the king who entered Jerusalem dancing before the ark (2 Sam 6) now leaves it weeping, barefoot, head covered. Fokkelman's analysis of the chapter's chiastic geometry centers on this contrast. The procession is composed of a series of dialogue-encounters — Ittai, Zadok, Hushai — each functioning as a **loyalty test** that David, paradoxically, tries to fail. He sends them all back. The movement is one of **deliberate self-stripping**: army, priesthood, ark, counselor — all returned to the city. David walks out reduced to bare kingship.

Textual variants: 4QSama, the most important Qumran witness for Samuel (Tov; Ulrich), preserves a slightly different procession sequence and resolves several MT difficulties in chs 15-19. Cross argued for an Old Palestinian text-type behind 4QSama and the LXX, distinct from the proto-MT. McCarter's AB commentary uses 4QSama extensively in this chapter.

The ascent of Olives (15:30) is the literary apex of the chapter — six verbs of motion and grief (going up, weeping, barefoot, head covered, weeping) layered into one verse. Alter calls this "the most affecting piece of prose in the Court History." It is the visual emblem the chapter has been building toward: the king without his city.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish trajectory.** Josephus (*Antiquities* 7.196-210) renders the chapter with embellishment, naming Absalom's chariot as "splendid" and emphasizing his comeliness as a tool of seduction; he makes Ahithophel's defection a matter of personal ambition. The rabbis treat David's flight with extraordinary sympathy. **Sanhedrin 107a** preserves a remarkable tradition: David, fleeing barefoot, prays Psalm 3 ("*A psalm of David, when he fled from Absalom his son*") and the Sages debate whether his suffering was punishment for the Bathsheba affair or a freely accepted purification. The same passage records David's request that he be allowed to suffer humiliation rather than the destruction of his lineage — a moment Tractate Sanhedrin treats as a kind of voluntary atonement.

Rashi on 15:7 accepts the "four years" reading and dates them from Absalom's return from Geshur (14:23). Radak (Kimhi) reads the Hebron choice as Absalom exploiting Judahite memory of David's earlier reign. **Abarbanel**'s treatment is the standout. His commentary on Samuel — written in late-fifteenth-century Iberia and Italy after his own experience advising and then fleeing kings (Portugal, Castile, Naples) — reads Absalom's gate-station as a textbook lesson in demagoguery. Abarbanel sees four sequential techniques: (1) populist accessibility (Absalom comes to the people, not vice versa); (2) institutional delegitimation ("there is no one to hear you"); (3) personal flattery (the kiss); (4) symbolic relocation (Hebron over Jerusalem). For Abarbanel, the chapter is a manual on how legitimate kingship is dissolved from within. Ralbag (Gersonides) and Ralbag's later expositors emphasize Absalom's failure to consult — a king who does not seek counsel before usurping is destined to be defeated by counsel (foreshadowing Hushai/Ahithophel).

Modern Israeli scholarship: Yair Zakovitch reads the chapter as deliberate ironic mirror to David's own rise (Absalom imitates the David-of-1-Samuel — charismatic, accessible, gathering followers). Avivah Zornberg, in her psychological-midrashic mode, dwells on David's barefoot ascent as a moment of intentional *yeridah* (descent) that parallels Sinai-imagery in reverse. Moshe Garsiel's rhetorical analysis of name-puns notes that *Avshalom* ("father of peace") is the great chapter-irony — he is the ungiver of peace.

**Christian trajectory.** Origen, in his (fragmentary) homilies on Samuel, reads Absalom typologically as Antichrist. The substantial patristic move, however, is **Augustine's** *City of God* 17.20, which treats David's barefoot ascent of Olives, weeping, with covered head, as a christological figure: the king betrayed by his counselor, fleeing the city across Kidron, ascending the very mountain Christ would ascend the night before his Passion. Augustine reads Ahithophel's coming betrayal-and-suicide (ch 17) as figuring Judas. **Ambrose** (*Apologia David*) develops the same typology with greater restraint, focusing on David's silence under Shimei's curse (next chapter) as a figure of Christ before Caiaphas. Jerome echoes the figure in his letters. **Chrysostom**'s homilies on 2 Samuel emphasize David's submission to providence — he sends back the ark not from despair but from non-manipulation of the sacred. **Gregory the Great** (*Moralia* on Job) uses David's flight as exemplum of humility under chastisement.

Aquinas (*Summa* II-II) cites David's restraint and prayer in 15:31 as evidence that the prudent ruler trusts secondary causes (Hushai) without renouncing prayer (the "turn the counsel of Ahithophel into foolishness" line is Thomas's locus classicus on cooperation between divine and human agency in political life). Bernard's sermons take David's covered head as monastic figure of confession.

**Calvin's** sermons on 2 Samuel — preached in 1562-63 and recently translated — devote substantial attention to chapters 15-19. On chapter 15, Calvin emphasizes three things: (1) Absalom's slow conspiracy is God's just chastisement of David's adultery — Nathan's prophecy unfolding in real time; (2) David's flight is exemplary not because it is heroic but because it accepts the verdict; (3) the sending back of the ark is the chapter's high point — David refuses to weaponize cult against his own son. Luther's *Lectures* are less developed here but follow similar lines.

Critical-historical: Wellhausen (*Composition*) located the chapter in the J source's expansion; Cross's Harvard school placed it in the older Palestinian text-type. McCarter's Anchor Bible commentary is the standard critical edition; A.A. Anderson's Word Biblical Commentary is the conservative-critical complement; Hertzberg (OTL) the older ecumenical reference. Tsumura (NICOT) is the recent evangelical synthesis.

Contemporary: Brueggemann reads the chapter as the unmasking of "establishment" David — the king must lose Jerusalem to recover what kingship was supposed to be. Polzin's Deuteronomistic reading sees the flight as a reversal of the conquest itinerary — David un-conquers Jerusalem. Eugene Peterson, in *Leap over a Wall*, dwells on the barefoot ascent as a posture of accepted mortality. Davis and Goldingay both note the integrity of David's "if I find favor" prayer — a refusal of religious manipulation. Brueggemann and Alter both flag the ten concubines left "to keep the house" (15:16): the line is dropped in without comment, but David's casual abandonment of them is the structural precondition for their public violation in chapter 16 — Ahithophel's brutal counsel only works because David walked out and left them there.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is David's flight cowardice or theological maturity?**

Position A (older heroic reading; some patristic): David flees because a king must preserve himself for the kingdom — his exit is strategic, not weak. He preserves Jerusalem from sack and reorganizes from Mahanaim.

Position B (Calvin, Brueggemann, Davis): David's flight is **acceptance of Nathan's prophecy** (12:10-11). He does not contend with the verdict. The barefoot, head-covered posture is the sign of one who knows he is being chastised. This is theological maturity, not weakness.

Position C (Halpern, McKenzie, suspicious-political reading): David's flight is **politically calculated apologetic**. He sends back the ark, leaves the concubines, restrains himself from cursing Shimei — every action is exactly what a future restored king would want on the record. The text protects David from Saulide and pro-Absalom accusation.

Steelman of C: The Court History does demonstrably function as apologetic; the patterns are too clean. David never strikes first, never gives an order he cannot publicly justify. Halpern's reading takes the text seriously as political document.

**Where this leaves us:** Positions B and C are not mutually exclusive. The text can be both genuine theological surrender *and* apologetic shaping; the narrator's purpose and David's piety can coexist. The question is whether we trust the narrator's representation of David's interiority. The "if I find favor" prayer (15:25-26) is the strongest evidence for B — it is not a line a pure apologist would invent, because it makes David's restoration contingent on YHWH's mercy rather than David's righteousness.

**Tension 2: Does sending the ark back constitute faith or fatalism?**

Position A (traditional Christian): It is faith — David refuses the 1 Samuel 4 mistake (using the ark as battle talisman). He acknowledges that YHWH cannot be coerced.

Position B (Brueggemann): It is **post-Davidic theology speaking** — the priestly hand is shaping David into a model of properly desacralized kingship for an exilic audience. The "if he says I have no pleasure in you" line is exilic theology of acceptance.

Position C (Polzin): It is fatalism with theological vocabulary — David is exhausted, not faithful. The Deuteronomist redactor inserts the pious frame; the underlying narrative is of a broken king passing the buck to the priests.

Steelman of C: David has lost three children (the unnamed infant, Amnon, now Absalom's affection), watched his court fracture, and is fleeing barefoot. Fatalism is psychologically plausible. The "what seems good to him" formula is found elsewhere in the mouths of beaten men (e.g., Eli in 1 Sam 3:18).

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter resists the question by refusing access to David's inner life. The narrator gives speech, action, and posture — never thought. We are forced to read the prayer through the action: David sends the ark back *and* sends Hushai forward. He does not manipulate God, but he does deploy intelligence. Faith and shrewdness coexist. This is the Court History's signature theology: providence works through human action, not against it (the explicit aside will come in 17:14).

**One unresolved question:** What is the moral weight of leaving the ten concubines "to keep the house"? The text does not editorialize. They become collateral in 16:21-22 and are sequestered the rest of their lives in 20:3 — "living as widows." Was this oversight, calculation, or callousness? The text's silence is itself the discomfort.

## The Thread

The chapter sets up cross-references that will run through the rest of the unit:
- **15:12 (Ahithophel)** → 16:23 (counsel as oracle) → 17:14 (counsel defeated by YHWH) → 17:23 (suicide).
- **15:16 (ten concubines)** → 16:21-22 (Absalom's appropriation, fulfilling Nathan 12:11) → 20:3 (lifelong widowhood).
- **15:30 (ascent of Olives, weeping)** → 19:8 (return through the gate, weeping for Absalom in 18:33).
- **15:25-26 (ark sent back)** → 1 Kings 8 (Solomon's temple as the ark's permanent home; David's deferred return becomes architectural permanence).

**Christological typology.** The patristic tradition reads David's flight as a deliberate prefigurement: David crosses the Wadi Kidron (15:23) and ascends the Mount of Olives (15:30) weeping, betrayed by his own counselor. The gospels choreograph Jesus's Passion onto the same map. **John 18:1**: "When Jesus had spoken these words, he went out with his disciples across the brook Kidron, where there was a garden, which he and his disciples entered." **Luke 22:39**: "And he came out and went, as was his custom, to the Mount of Olives." The geographic precision is striking. Augustine, Ambrose, and Jerome all read the Davidic flight as figura of the Passion, with Ahithophel-the-counselor-who-betrays-and-hangs-himself prefiguring Judas (Mt 27:5). The figure should be engaged seriously: the gospel writers, deeply read in 2 Samuel, almost certainly chose to highlight the topographic coincidence. But it should not be overread. David weeps for himself; Jesus weeps for the city. David flees a son's revolt; Jesus walks toward voluntary death. The figure is real but asymmetric — typology that holds the difference, not just the resemblance.

What 2 Samuel 15 leaves us with is a king walking out of his own city, deliberately reduced. Absalom rides in chariots; David walks barefoot. The narrator does not yet tell us which posture YHWH prefers. Chapter 17 will tell us. For now, the chapter ends with two men entering Jerusalem — Hushai and Absalom — at the same moment, from opposite directions, with opposite intent. The plot has been planted.
