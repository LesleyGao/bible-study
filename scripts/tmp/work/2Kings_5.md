# 2 Kings 5: Naaman, the Slave Girl, and Two Mules of Earth

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens with one of the most striking theological statements in the Deuteronomistic History, embedded in the most casual diplomatic prose: "Naaman, commander of the army of the king of Syria, was a great man with his master and in high favor, because **by him the LORD had given victory to Syria**." The narrator explicitly attributes Aramean military success to YHWH — over against Israel, the covenant people the prophet serves. This is the kind of theological claim that the chapter will spend the rest of its 27 verses unpacking: YHWH is not a tribal asset.

**Aram-Damascus** in the mid-ninth century BCE was Israel's chief regional rival and one of the major Iron Age polities of the southern Levant. The royal house in this period included **Hadadezer** (called Ben-Hadad II in some reconstructions; Hadadezer son of Rehob according to Assyrian sources) and his successor **Hazael** (the same Hazael whom Elisha will anoint in chapter 8 and who appears as a major military force in 2 Kings 8-13). The Aramean army's effectiveness against Israel is well-attested both biblically and archaeologically. The **Tel Dan Stele** (discovered by Avraham Biran in 1993-94 in two stages of fragments at Tel Dan in northern Israel; in basalt, dated paleographically to the late 9th century BCE) is an Aramaic royal inscription, almost universally attributed to Hazael, that boasts of killing "Joram son of Ahab king of Israel" and "Ahaziah son of Joram of the House of David" (*bytdwd*). The inscription is the only extra-biblical reference to the House of David and a major synchronism with the events of 2 Kings 8-9. The "victory the LORD had given to Syria" through Naaman is exactly the kind of military success the Tel Dan Stele commemorates.

**Damascus** itself, called *Dimashqa* in Akkadian, *Dimashq* in Aramaic, was a major commercial and military hub on the trade routes from Mesopotamia to the Mediterranean. The **Abana** (אֲמָנָה, modern Barada) and **Pharpar** (פַּרְפַּר, modern Awaj) rivers flow through and around Damascus. Naaman's protest in v. 12 is geographically pointed: Damascus's rivers are clear, abundant, fast-flowing mountain water descending from the Anti-Lebanon range. The Jordan in its lower stretches is muddy, slow, and silt-laden. The ritual humiliation of having to wash in inferior water is part of the chapter's theology of obedience.

**Naaman's leprosy** (*tzara'at*, צָרַעַת) is almost certainly not Hansen's disease (modern leprosy). The Hebrew term covers a range of skin conditions including psoriasis, vitiligo, eczema, and various fungal infections — codified in detail in Leviticus 13-14. Hansen's disease as we know it is not unambiguously attested in the southern Levant before the Hellenistic period. The condition is socially stigmatizing under the Levitical purity code (which is what makes Naaman's social standing in Aram so striking — his condition is ritualistically excluding only in Israelite cultic terms, not in Aramean) but does not prevent his command of the army. The detail "his flesh was restored like the flesh of a little child" (v. 14) suggests a complete remission of a chronic skin disorder.

**Rimmon** (רִמּוֹן) is **Hadad-Ramman**, the storm-god worshipped throughout Aramean territory. *Rimmon* may be a deliberate Hebrew parodic spelling (the word means "pomegranate") of the Aramean *Ramman* ("the thunderer"). Hadad is the same storm-deity worshipped in Phoenicia (where his title is Baal); the Aramean and Phoenician traditions differ in particulars but converge on the storm-god type. Mark S. Smith's *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* and Patrick Miller's *The Divine Warrior in Early Israel* lay out the broader Levantine storm-god ecology. The "house of Rimmon" in v. 18 is the temple of Hadad in Damascus — well-attested in the Aramean and Assyrian records. Excavations at Damascus are limited because of continuous occupation, but Aramean Hadad temples are known archaeologically at sites like Aleppo (the recently re-excavated Hadad temple of Aleppo is one of the most important Late Bronze and Iron Age sanctuaries in northern Syria) and Tell Halaf (Guzana).

The currencies in vv. 5 and 22 are real and impressive. **Ten talents of silver** (perhaps 340 kg) and 6,000 shekels of gold (perhaps 68 kg) is royal-treasury-scale wealth — the kind of gift a king sends to a king, not the kind of fee a patient pays a doctor. **Ten changes of clothing** signals high-status dress: not workaday tunics but the embroidered, dyed, fringed garments of the Iron Age elite, attested in Mari and Assyrian textile lists. Gehazi's request for one talent and two changes of clothing for "two young men of the prophets" is plausibly modest by comparison — exactly the deception that would seem credible.

**Two mule loads of earth** (v. 17) is the chapter's most archaeologically resonant detail. The ancient understanding that deities had territorial association meant that worship outside a god's territory required either a sanctuary in the new territory or earth from the home territory. Naaman's request reflects exactly this theology — he wants Israelite earth to construct (or symbolically establish) a YHWH-sanctuary in Damascus. The Mesha Stele's vocabulary of *bamot* (high places) built for Chemosh in conquered territory illustrates the parallel logic in Moabite religion. Naaman is reasoning his way into worship using Aramean cultic categories.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is the **most artfully constructed** of the Elisha narratives. It is a tightly plotted reversal-cycle in which every major character's status is inverted. The high gentile commander becomes a humble Jordan-bather; the unnamed Israelite slave girl drives the action; the trembling Israelite king plays no role in the cure; the prophet's servant ends in the leprosy from which the gentile has been delivered. Robert Alter, Adele Berlin, and Shimon Bar-Efrat all treat the chapter as a paradigm of biblical narrative compression and irony.

The chapter opens with a **rhetorical accumulation** — Naaman is great, in favor, valorous, victorious — and closes the introductory sentence with the single Hebrew word *metzora* (מְצֹרָע), "leprous." The five-word build-up collapses on the sixth. This is what Sternberg calls the "summative reversal" — the narrator front-loads a character's status precisely to make the deflating final clause hit harder. The same technique structures the chapter's larger arc.

The **slave girl's role** (v. 2-3) is theologically extraordinary. She is anonymous, captured, female, young, foreign in her workplace — five separate marginal categories — yet she is the first character in the chapter to speak the prophet's existence into the Aramean royal house. The chapter is consciously inverting the power-status pyramid: gentile general at the top, slave girl at the bottom, but the slave girl is the one with the saving knowledge. Walsh and Brueggemann both note that, as with the Shunammite in chapter 4, the narrative turns on a brief speech act by an unnamed character at the social margin — the captured girl's two sentences (vv. 2-3) set the entire chapter in motion.

The **threefold servant scene** is a structural backbone. (1) The slave girl speaks to Naaman's wife (vv. 2-3); (2) Naaman's road-companions reason him into obedience (v. 13: "My father, it is a great word the prophet has spoken to you; will you not do it?"); (3) Gehazi runs after Naaman (vv. 20-27). Three servants, three speeches, three different outcomes: the first opens salvation; the second persuades into salvation; the third corrupts after salvation. The chapter is a meditation on the role of subordinates in salvation-history.

The **Jordan-seven-times** dipping (v. 14) — *va-yitbol ba-yarden sheva pe'amim* (וַיִּטְבֹּל בַּיַּרְדֵּן שֶׁבַע פְּעָמִים) — uses the verb *taval* (טבל), the same root that will become the standard term for ritual immersion in second-temple Judaism (and in the Greek transliteration *baptismos*). The seven repetitions echo the seven sneezes of the Shunammite's son in chapter 4 and the seven days of the Levitical purification ritual (Lev 14). The narrative is conscious of its cultic resonance.

Naaman's confession in v. 15 — *ki ein elohim be-khol ha-aretz ki im be-yisrael* (כִּי אֵין אֱלֹהִים בְּכָל־הָאָרֶץ כִּי אִם בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל), "there is no God in all the earth except in Israel" — is the chapter's theological climax. The structure of the confession deliberately echoes Elijah's question in 1 Kings 18:39 and 2 Kings 1:3: the gentile is being given the answer the Israelite kings have been refusing. **Cogan & Tadmor** in the Anchor Bible note that Naaman's confession is the strongest single monotheistic statement on the lips of a foreigner in the Hebrew Bible, exceeding even Rahab's at Joshua 2:11.

The **two-mules-of-earth** scene (vv. 17-19) and the **Rimmon dispensation** are the chapter's most theologically complex moment. Naaman asks for (1) earth to enable home worship, and (2) permission to bow when his master leans on his arm in the Rimmon temple. Elisha's reply is a single phrase: *lekh le-shalom* (לֵךְ לְשָׁלוֹם), "Go in peace" — exactly the same phrase the Shunammite uses to her servant in 4:23 and that David and Joab use in their negotiations. The phrase is genuinely ambiguous; it neither approves nor disapproves. The text is leaving the resolution of the syncretism question to the reader.

The **Gehazi scene** (vv. 19-27) is constructed as a **deliberate Achan-parallel** (Joshua 7) — the servant who covertly takes what the prophet has refused, hides it, lies about it, and is judged. The Achan-parallel is structurally exact: forbidden plunder, hiding, denial under prophetic interrogation, public judgment. Walsh and Fokkelman note the deliberate pattern. The "leprosy of Naaman shall cling to you and to your descendants forever" (v. 27) reverses the chapter's central healing — what was lifted from a foreigner is laid on an Israelite.

Textually, the chapter is well-preserved. The LXX (4 Kingdoms 5) follows MT closely. Some Lucianic manuscripts have slight expansions of Naaman's confession. The 4QKgs fragments do not preserve this material. The closing phrase about leprosy *clinging* (*davaq*, דָּבַק) "to your descendants forever" is the strong covenantal verb of clinging, used elsewhere of Israel cleaving to YHWH (Deut 10:20, 11:22) — it is being inverted, the curse-equivalent of covenant adhesion.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc places this chapter as the **theological climax of the Elisha cycle's miracle stories** and as one of the most consequential Old Testament passages for the New Testament's theology of Gentile inclusion. Jesus's explicit citation at Luke 4:27 makes this chapter foundational for the Lukan-Pauline mission to the nations.

**Jewish interpretive tradition** has read the chapter with care for its theological complexity. Josephus (*Antiquities* 9.51-60) gives an extended retelling that emphasizes Naaman's rationality (the servants' argument is given great weight), the king of Israel's panic, and Gehazi's avarice. Rashi reads the slave girl as a model — she exemplifies fearing God in captivity, and her discreet helpful speech is what every diaspora Jew should imitate. Radak (Kimhi) emphasizes the philological argument for *Ramman* over *Rimmon* and treats the entire scene as polemic against Aramean storm-god worship. Ralbag (Gersonides) reads Naaman's confession as authentic but limited monotheism — Naaman recognizes YHWH as universal sovereign but still operates within an ANE territorial-religion frame. **Abarbanel** is sharp on the Gehazi judgment: he reads the leprosy not as personal punishment alone but as a sign that prophetic inheritance can be forfeited. Gehazi was Elisha's likely successor (the firstborn-portion logic of chapter 2 implied a continuing line); the leprosy disinherits him. **Yair Zakovitch** in his work on biblical narrative cycles places Naaman alongside Rahab, Ruth, and the Queen of Sheba as the great gentile-confessor figures of the Hebrew Bible. The Talmud (Sotah 47a; Sanhedrin 107b) discusses Gehazi as one of the four commoners who forfeit the world to come, alongside Doeg, Ahithophel, and Balaam — a formidable judgment that registers the rabbinic seriousness about the chapter's moral weight.

**Christian patristic tradition** read the chapter christologically and ecclesiologically with great vigor. Origen's Homilies on Kings treats Naaman as type of the gentile soul washing in the waters of baptism — the seven dippings as the seven gifts of the Spirit, the Jordan as the typological waters that John will later employ for his baptism. Tertullian in *On Baptism* uses Naaman explicitly as the prefigurement of Christian baptism — water, command, submission, healing. Cyril of Jerusalem's *Catechetical Lectures* on baptism cite Naaman as paradigm of the convert. **Theodoret of Cyrus** in his *Quaestiones in 1-2 Reg* engages the Rimmon dispensation directly: Theodoret argues Elisha's "go in peace" was a temporary accommodation for a new convert and not a general license, distinguishing pastoral care of weak conscience from approval of syncretism. Augustine in *City of God* treats Naaman as evidence that God's saving knowledge has always included gentiles. Chrysostom's homiletical treatment makes Naaman a model for the catechumen who must submit to humble means despite expectations of grand ritual. Gregory the Great in *Pastoral Rule* and *Moralia in Job* uses Gehazi as the type of the cleric who exploits ministry for material gain — an exegetical move with profound consequences for medieval ecclesiastical reform discourse.

**Calvin's sermons on 2 Kings** treat the chapter as a sustained meditation on the pedagogy of grace: Naaman has to learn that salvation does not come on his preferred terms (no waving of hands, no Damascus rivers, no proper ceremony), and the obedience the prophet demands is precisely the humbling that the grace requires. Calvin reads the Rimmon dispensation cautiously — he treats Elisha's "go in peace" as a real but bounded accommodation, refusing to extend it into a general license for divided loyalty in worship. Luther preached the slave girl as a pattern of confessional witness — small voices in pagan households can carry saving knowledge.

In modern critical scholarship, **Cogan & Tadmor's Anchor Bible 2 Kings** is the indispensable reference for the chapter's diplomatic and military realism, the philology of *tzara'at*, and the theology of the Naaman confession. T.R. Hobbs (WBC) treats the chapter as the Elisha cycle's theological summit. Marvin Sweeney (OTL) emphasizes the Deuteronomistic argument that gentile recognition of YHWH's universal sovereignty is a recurring feature of the prophetic narratives (Hiram of Tyre, the Queen of Sheba, Naaman, the captives in Babylon). **Iain Provan** reads the chapter pastorally as a study in how grace meets entrenched assumption. Eugene Peterson's writings on Kings emphasize the chapter's ironies of status and the unexpected agents of salvation. Walter Brueggemann reads the chapter as the cycle's clearest demonstration that YHWH's economy operates outside Israelite borders. **Robert Coote** treats the Naaman story as one of the more historically grounded Elisha narratives, possibly drawing on diplomatic memory of Aramean-Israelite relations under Hadadezer. **Mark S. Smith**'s work on the YHWH/Hadad polemic illuminates the Rimmon scene: the storm-god of Damascus is being implicitly displaced by YHWH's recognition in Damascus. **Jon Levenson**'s wider work on Israel and the nations frames Naaman as one of the canonical signposts toward universalist theology. Brueggemann and Provan both note the chapter's persistent interest in the unexpected agent — the slave girl, the road-companions, the muddy Jordan — as the medium through which a great man is humbled and healed.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two theological tensions structure the chapter.

**First: the Rimmon dispensation — what does Elisha permit?** Position one — the **strict-monotheism reading** (Calvin, much of the magisterial Reformed tradition, parts of patristic interpretation) — argues Elisha's "go in peace" is a pastoral accommodation to a new convert who cannot yet renegotiate his court duties, not a general license for syncretism. The text is showing grace toward a man whose situation cannot immediately resolve, but the underlying theology of exclusive worship is unchanged. Naaman bowing physically while leaning on his master's arm is a physical posture, not a confession of Rimmon's deity. Position two — the **conscience-protection reading** (Theodoret, much of patristic and pastoral commentary) — emphasizes the *interior* dimension. Naaman declares he will "no longer offer burnt offering or sacrifice to any god but the LORD"; the bowing-when-leaning is a public political act that does not engage his interior worship. The dispensation protects his conscience while permitting him to keep his role. Position three — the **canonical-tension reading** (Brueggemann, much of contemporary commentary) — refuses to soften the difficulty. The chapter is letting a brand-new monotheist participate in storm-god ritual on the strength of his sincerity, and the text records this without comment because it knows the question is not closed. The reader is given the data, not the verdict. Steelmanned, the conscience-protection reading is strongest in patristic and pastoral terms, but the canonical-tension reading is most honest about what the text actually does — it records an "in peace" without resolving the question.

Where this leaves us: the chapter teaches that conversion sometimes precedes the full reorganization of public life, and that the prophet sends a new monotheist back into a polytheistic court without giving him a complete protocol. Christian readers in courts that demanded ritual conformity (Roman emperor cult, medieval state religion, modern Communist regimes) have read this verse with their own pastoral need.

**Second: the universal scope of salvation — does the chapter teach that gentiles can be saved without becoming Israelites?** Position one — the **Israel-centered reading** (much of premodern Jewish commentary, parts of patristic exegesis) — treats Naaman's confession as recognition that YHWH is the universal God *whose chosen worship is in Israel*. Naaman returns to Damascus equipped with Israelite earth, vowing exclusive YHWH-worship, but he does not become Israelite. The chapter teaches that YHWH's reach is universal but YHWH's chosen people remain singular. Position two — the **inclusive-monotheism reading** (Brueggemann, Levenson, much of contemporary commentary) — argues the chapter is one of the canonical seeds of universalist theology. Naaman's confession is theologically complete without his ethnic conversion; salvation is not reducible to Israelite ethnic incorporation. The chapter prepares the canonical ground for the Servant Songs of Isaiah, the universalism of Jonah, and the New Testament's mission to the nations. Position three — the **canonical-trajectory reading** (Wright, Hays, much of Christian commentary) — sees the chapter as one node in a larger trajectory that culminates in Christ's incorporation of Naaman's logic into the Lukan kerygma. Jesus's Luke 4:27 citation activates the chapter as foundation for Gentile inclusion in the new covenant. Steelmanned, the canonical-trajectory reading is most fruitful for Christian theology — the chapter is generative for what comes later — while the inclusive-monotheism reading is most direct as a reading of the chapter on its own terms.

Where this leaves us: this is one of the Old Testament's clearest texts that gentile faith is real, sufficient, and recognized, and the Lukan reception (Luke 4:27) is not a Christian imposition but the activation of a theological possibility the chapter itself opens.

**The unresolved question**: What happens to Naaman in Damascus after he leaves the prophet? The text does not tell us. Did the dispensation hold? Did the syncretism corrode? Did he build a sanctuary on the Israelite earth? The chapter ends his story before the harder long-term questions arise.

## The Thread

Three cross-references anchor the chapter forward. **Luke 4:25-27** is the chapter's most famous New Testament uptake: Jesus invokes "many lepers in Israel in the time of Elisha but only Naaman the Syrian was cleansed" as indictment of Nazareth's unbelief. The synagogue tries to throw him off a cliff. Jesus is using this chapter as a hermeneutic key for his entire ministry: the gentile who comes is healed, the Israelite who refuses is bypassed. **Luke 17:11-19** (the ten lepers, only the Samaritan returns) is the practical re-enactment: Jesus heals ten, only one — the foreigner — returns to give thanks, in deliberate echo of Naaman's return in 5:15. **Joshua 7** (Achan) is the structural parallel for the Gehazi scene: forbidden plunder, hidden, judged. The chapter is using Achan-typology to mark Gehazi's act as covenant-breaking on the Achan model. **2 Kings 7:3-10** will return to four lepers at the gate of Samaria as a thematic counterweight: in chapter 5 a foreign leper receives Israelite cure; in chapter 7 four Israelite lepers carry news of foreign retreat to Israel. The leprosy theme threads through the cycle.

The chapter ends with Gehazi white as snow, leaving the prophet's presence. Naaman is on the road home with two mules of earth. The slave girl is unnamed and unmentioned again. The king of Israel has done nothing. The prophet has stood firm. And the chapter has done what no other Old Testament narrative has quite done in this concentration: it has demonstrated, through one foreigner's seven dips in the Jordan, that there is a God in all the earth — and he is found, of all places, in Israel.
