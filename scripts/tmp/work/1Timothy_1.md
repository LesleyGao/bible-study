# 1 Timothy 1: Mercy for the Foremost

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The opening of 1 Timothy drops the reader into Ephesus — but precisely *which* Ephesus is the first historical question, because the answer depends on when the letter was written. If the letter is genuinely Pauline and dates to the mid-60s AD (after a hypothetical release from the Acts 28 imprisonment in Rome and before Paul's martyrdom under Nero), the city is the Julio-Claudian Ephesus of Acts 19: a Greek city under Roman rule, dominated economically and religiously by the Artemision, the Temple of Artemis, one of the seven wonders of the ancient world; a node of trade with a substantial Jewish diaspora attested in Josephus and inscriptions. If the letter is pseudonymous and dates from c. 90–130 AD, the Ephesus is the Flavian and Trajanic city — the same city Ignatius writes to c. 110 AD assuming a single bishop with a presbyterate beneath him, the same provincial milieu in which Pliny the Younger as legate of Bithynia was interrogating Christians and finding two female *ministrae* (deacons) under torture.

The imperial cult was unavoidable in either era. Ephesus had received a temple to the Sebastoi (the Flavian emperors) under Domitian and was a *neokoros* "temple-warden" city. The phrase "God our Savior" (θεοῦ σωτῆρος ἡμῶν, *theou sōtēros hēmōn*) in 1:1, applied to the Father, and the parallel application of "Lord" to Christ Jesus, sit deliberately against an imperial-cult vocabulary in which σωτήρ and κύριος were standard predicates of Caesar. Paul Trebilco's *The Early Christians in Ephesus from Paul to Ignatius* (2004) is the standard work mapping the social world the chapter assumes. Ephesus's wealth flowed through patron-client networks with civic benefactors — including women like the Phoebe of Cenchreae attested at Rom 16:1, the Junia of Rom 16:7, and women priestesses of Artemis attested in inscriptions — funding public works and cultic offices. This matters for the polemic in 1 Tim 1 against teachers who promote "myths and endless genealogies" (μύθοις καὶ γενεαλογίαις ἀπεράντοις, *mythois kai genealogiais aperantois*), since one influential reading (Richard and Catherine Kroeger, Sandra Glahn) places the false teaching in conversation with Artemis-myth speculation that elevated female primacy.

Authorship and dating must be named openly. F.C. Baur's 1835 *Die sogenannten Pastoralbriefe* declared the Pastorals second-century catholicizing forgeries; Heinrich Holtzmann formalized the linguistic case in 1880; Martin Dibelius and Hans Conzelmann's Hermeneia commentary (German 1955; English 1972) became the standard pseudonymous reading. The arguments are fourfold: roughly 30 percent of the vocabulary is absent from the undisputed Paulines; the church order (overseers, elders, deacons, widow rolls) is more developed than the charismatic communities of 1 Corinthians; the situation does not fit Acts; and the polemic against false teachers reads to many like second-century anti-Gnosticism. Conservative defenders include William Mounce (Word Biblical Commentary), I. Howard Marshall (who proposes "allonymity" — a faithful Pauline disciple writing in Paul's name shortly after his death), Philip Towner (NICNT), George Knight (NIGTC), and Luke Timothy Johnson, whose Anchor Bible commentary defends Pauline authorship by appeal to the *mandata principis* genre: royal-commission letters from a superior to a delegated officer differ generically from congregational letters, so vocabulary divergence is a function of genre, not authorship. A middle position, advanced by C.F.D. Moule, Stephen Wilson, and Jerome Quinn, posits Pauline content delivered through an amanuensis (Luke favored). Raymond Collins (Sacra Pagina), Lewis Donelson, and Norbert Brox press the pseudonymous case literarily, with Donelson arguing the Pastorals fit the conventions of philosophical pseudepigraphy.

Textually, the witnesses for 1 Timothy are sober. Codex Vaticanus (B) famously breaks off in Hebrews and lacks the Pastorals altogether; the chapter is preserved in Sinaiticus (א, fourth century), Alexandrinus (A, fifth century), and Bezae's later supplement, with the Greek minuscule tradition full and uniform. P32 (Rylands, c. 200) preserves only Titus 1–2, but its existence demonstrates that the Pastorals were circulating as a corpus by the late second century. Important Greek terms for this chapter include πιστὸς ὁ λόγος (*pistos ho logos*, "the saying is trustworthy"), the formula introducing 1:15 and recurring four more times in the Pastorals; νομοδιδάσκαλοι (*nomodidaskaloi*, "law-teachers") in 1:7, an unusual word also found in Luke 5:17 and Acts 5:34; παραθήκη (*parathēkē*, "deposit"), the trust-image that will dominate 1 Tim 6 and 2 Tim 1; οἰκονομία (*oikonomia*, "stewardship") in 1:4, with its household-management background; and the contested vice-list term ἀρσενοκοῖται (*arsenokoitai*) in 1:10, a word almost certainly coined from the LXX Septuagint translation of Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13, which renders the prohibition with the cognate phrase ἄρσενος κοίτην (*arsenos koitēn*, "lying with a male").

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Generically, 1 Timothy is paraenesis — sustained moral exhortation in the tradition of Hellenistic philosophical letters — fused with the *mandata principis*, the royal commission a sovereign delegated to a viceroy or officer. Benjamin Fiore's *The Function of Personal Example in the Socratic and Pastoral Epistles* (1986) and Abraham Malherbe's work on Hellenistic moralists are the standard reference points. Pliny the Younger's letters to Trajan as legate of Bithynia (Books 10) supply extant *mandata*-style correspondence; Pseudo-Isocrates *To Demonicus* and *To Nicocles* are the closest paraenetic parallels, with their characteristic blend of personal address, virtue catalogues, and exemplary models. Margaret Mitchell's work on Pauline rhetorical conventions and Luke Timothy Johnson's literary defense both point to this generic frame: the letter is not a congregational treatise like Romans or 1 Corinthians but an officer's manual.

The literary devices in chapter 1 are dense. The vice list of 1:9–10 is a Hellenistic catalogue of the sort beloved by Stoic moralists — Epictetus, Seneca — and adapted by Paul in Romans 1, 1 Corinthians 5–6, and Galatians 5. The structure here is paired (the lawless and disobedient, the ungodly and sinners, the unholy and profane), gestures toward the second table of the Decalogue (those who strike their fathers and mothers correspond to commandment five; murderers to six; the sexually immoral and ἀρσενοκοῖται to seven; ἀνδραποδισταῖς, "enslavers" or "kidnappers," to eight; liars and perjurers to nine), and is capped by the catch-all "whatever else is contrary to sound doctrine." This structural alignment with the Decalogue's second table is noted by Mounce, Towner, and Johnson and is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that the author is reading Torah christologically as moral law for the unregenerate.

The "faithful saying" formula πιστὸς ὁ λόγος (1:15) is a literary signature of the Pastorals (also 1 Tim 3:1; 4:9; 2 Tim 2:11; Titus 3:8). It marks what most commentators, including Knight in *The Faithful Sayings in the Pastoral Letters* (1968), regard as pre-formed traditional material — possibly catechetical, possibly hymnic. The doxology of 1:17 ("To the King of the ages, immortal, invisible, the only God, be honor and glory forever and ever. Amen") has the rhythm and predicates of synagogue prayer (the *aiōnion*, the negation theology of "immortal, invisible," the *monos theos* declaration) and shows the Pastorals' debt to Hellenistic-Jewish liturgical idiom.

Within the letter's argument, chapter 1 sets the agenda. The crisis (false teachers in Ephesus) and the solution (Timothy's charge, anchored in Paul's own apostolic mercy-narrative) are introduced together. Paul's autobiographical excursus in 1:12–17 is not digression but warrant: the gospel that saved the foremost of sinners is the same gospel that the false teachers, with their speculation and their misuse of law, have abandoned. The chapter ends with Hymenaeus and Alexander "handed over to Satan" (1:20), a phrase that echoes 1 Corinthians 5:5 and signals the disciplinary teeth of the apostolic charge. There are no major meaning-affecting variants in the chapter, though the doxology in 1:17 has a minor variant ("the only wise God" in the Byzantine tradition vs. "the only God" in Sinaiticus and Alexandrinus); the modern critical text reads μόνῳ θεῷ.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc — Israel called, Christ come, church sent — 1 Timothy 1 sits at the moment when the gospel has turned outward into the cities of the empire and is now confronting the ordinary problem of any movement past its first generation: how to keep the message uncorrupted when its founder is no longer present and when the second-generation teachers begin to drift. The chapter's law-gospel argument folds Paul's whole career into a single autobiographical paragraph (1:12–16): the persecutor became the preacher because the law that condemned him gave way to the mercy that converted him. This arc — from Pharisee to apostle, from blasphemer to herald — is the apostolic version of the larger biblical pattern by which the wrath of God in covenant law gives way to the mercy of God in covenant fulfillment.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** The Patristic reception is dominated by John Chrysostom, whose *Homilies on First Timothy* (delivered at Antioch in the 390s) became the East's standard reading: Chrysostom takes Paul's "I am the foremost of sinners" as literal humility born of grace and uses 1:8 to argue against Marcionite rejection of the Old Testament. Ambrosiaster and Theodore of Mopsuestia produce the other significant fourth-century commentaries; Augustine returns repeatedly to 1:13 ("I acted ignorantly in unbelief") in his anti-Pelagian writings. Thomas Aquinas's lecture-commentary on 1 Timothy reads the chapter through his theology of law: the Mosaic law functions as a *paedagogus* (Gal 3:24) to drive sinners to Christ. The *Glossa Ordinaria* fixed Chrysostom and Augustine as the standard medieval frame.

Reformation readings sharpen the law-gospel structure. Luther's preface to the Pastoral Epistles (1522) uses 1:8–11 as a key prooftext for the *usus theologicus* of the law — the second use, in which the law accuses the conscience and drives the sinner to Christ. Calvin's commentary on 1 Timothy (1556) accepts this but supplements it with the *tertius usus*, the third use, in which the law guides the believer's sanctification — Calvin's distinctive Reformed addition. Anabaptist readers (Menno Simons, the Hutterite tradition) emphasize 1:5 ("love that issues from a pure heart and a good conscience and a sincere faith") as the inward sign of regeneration that distinguishes the true church from the territorial state-church. The critical reception begins with F.C. Baur's 1835 *Die sogenannten Pastoralbriefe*, which read the Pastorals as second-century anti-Marcionite, anti-Gnostic catholicizing forgeries; Holtzmann's 1880 monograph systematized the linguistic argument; Adolf von Harnack treated the Pastorals as evidence of the Frühkatholizismus, "early Catholicism," that on his Lutheran reading marked the church's fall from Pauline gospel into institutional religion.

The contemporary scene is genuinely divided. Mounce, Marshall, Towner, Knight, and Johnson defend Pauline or Pauline-circle authorship with detailed lexical and rhetorical argument. Raymond Collins (Sacra Pagina), Jerome Quinn and William Wacker (Eerdmans Critical Commentary), Norbert Brox, and the older Dibelius-Conzelmann line maintain the pseudonymous reading. On 1:9–10, the egalitarian reading (Linda Belleville, Philip Payne, Craig Keener, Ben Witherington) emphasizes the structural decalogue alignment and the social-historical context of the vice list; the complementarian / traditionalist reading (Andreas Köstenberger, Wayne Grudem, Robert Gagnon in his *The Bible and Homosexual Practice*) treats the list as a stable moral catalogue with continuing universal force. Kyle Harper's *From Shame to Sin: The Christian Transformation of Sexual Morality in Late Antiquity* (2013) supplies the indispensable historical background: the sexual world of the early imperial cities was structured by status (free male citizen vs. slave, child, foreigner) rather than orientation, which is why ἀρσενοκοῖται's etymology (from LXX Lev 18:22 / 20:13: ἄρσενος κοίτην) and the company it keeps (πόρνοις, ἀνδραποδισταῖς) matter so much. Robin Scroggs's *The New Testament and Homosexuality* (1983) argued the term referred specifically to pederastic exploitation; Dale Martin in *Sex and the Single Savior* (2006) pushed the lexical-uncertainty case further; Robert Gagnon (2001) defended the broad sense. The question of what the term denotes is a live exegetical and ethical battleground and one that the chapter's structural ties to the Decalogue's second table do not by themselves settle.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** The Pastorals' polemic against "law-teachers" who do not understand what they say (1:7) and against "myths and endless genealogies" (1:4) has historically been read by Christian commentators as anti-Judaizing, but the picture is more complex. The opponents seem to be Christians of Jewish background promoting an esoteric reading of Torah genealogies, possibly proto-Gnostic or proto-Kabbalistic in temper. Modern Jewish scholarship — beginning with Krister Stendahl's epoch-shifting essay "The Apostle Paul and the Introspective Conscience of the West" (1963) — has pressed the question whether the Lutheran reading of Paul's law-gospel binary is in fact a sixteenth-century projection onto a first-century Jew. E.P. Sanders's *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977) recast Second Temple Judaism as a religion of "covenantal nomism" rather than works-righteousness, dismantling the caricature on which the Lutheran reading depended; James Dunn coined the phrase "the new perspective on Paul"; N.T. Wright's *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* (2013) extended the rereading covenantally. Daniel Boyarin's *A Radical Jew* (1994) and Mark Nanos's *The Mystery of Romans* (1996) read Paul as a thoroughgoing Jew working out the place of Gentiles in the eschatological people of God. Paula Fredriksen's *Paul: The Pagan's Apostle* (2017) carries the within-Judaism reading further. On 1 Tim 1:8–11, this scholarship pushes back against any reading that opposes Christian gospel to Jewish law as such; it reads the chapter's affirmation that "the law is good, if one uses it lawfully" as continuous with Paul's covenantal Judaism in Romans 7:12 and not as a step toward Marcionism.

**Where this leaves us.** The dialogue is alive. The Lutheran reading retains pastoral force (the gospel really does meet sinners at the law's accusation), but the historical Paul almost certainly did not think in Reformation categories, and 1 Tim 1:8 will not bear an anti-Jewish reading. The vice-list debate over ἀρσενοκοῖται is unsettled, with significant scholars on both sides arguing in good faith from genuine evidence.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author of 1 Timothy 1 believed God was doing something specific in the Ephesian church's life: protecting the gospel deposit from speculation that would dilute it into philosophy, and protecting the law from teachers who would weaponize it against the gospel of mercy. Two theological tensions inhabit the chapter.

**Tension 1: The relationship of law and gospel.** The chapter's claim — that "the law is good, if one uses it lawfully" (1:8), and that it is laid down "for the lawless and disobedient" (1:9) — admits of multiple legitimate readings.

- *Lutheran (two-uses).* Luther and the Lutheran confessions read the law as having a *usus civilis* (restraining evil in society) and a *usus theologicus* (accusing the conscience and driving to Christ). On this reading, 1:8–11 articulates the second use: the law is "for sinners" because it shows them their sin and brings them to the gospel.
- *Reformed (three-uses).* Calvin and the Reformed tradition add the *tertius usus*, the third use, in which the law instructs the regenerate believer in the path of sanctification. Reformed readers cite 1:5's "love from a pure heart" as the law's positive moral aim for those in Christ.
- *Wright / covenantal-narrative.* N.T. Wright and the broader new-perspective school read Torah as Israel's covenantal vocation, fulfilled in Christ. On this reading, the law's "lawful use" is its function as a witness to the messianic fulfillment, not as an abstract moral code; Paul's vice list aligns with the Decalogue's second table because Christ is the goal of the law's covenantal teaching.
- *Sanders / within-Judaism.* E.P. Sanders, Mark Nanos, and Paula Fredriksen would emphasize that "law is good if used lawfully" is a thoroughly Jewish claim and that the polemic in 1:7 is against bad teachers, not against Torah. The chapter is ecclesial discipline, not theological supersessionism.
- *Catholic.* The Catechism (CCC 1961–1986) integrates these readings within a teleology of natural law, divine law, and the law of the Gospel as the new law written by the Spirit on the heart (Jeremiah 31). The Council of Trent's *Decree on Justification* (1547) holds law and grace together against what it took to be Lutheran antinomianism.

The position that is hardest to steelman from a Protestant default is the within-Judaism reading, but it is in fact the most historically credible. Paul is a first-century Jew writing to a second-generation Gentile-majority church in Ephesus. He has never repudiated Torah; he has reframed it around the Messiah. "The law is good, if one uses it lawfully" is not a concession to Judaizers but a positive claim about Torah's continuing role for those outside Christ. The Lutheran reading captures something true existentially but flattens the historical texture.

**Where this leaves us.** No one reading exhausts the chapter, but readings that pit gospel against Torah misread Paul's Jewishness. The law in 1 Timothy 1 is good, has a function, and is not abolished; what is rejected is its weaponization by teachers who do not understand it.

**Tension 2: Conversion and the depth of human depravity.** The chapter's other theological knot is Paul's self-description as πρῶτος (*prōtos*, "foremost," "chief") of sinners (1:15). This raises the question of what model of conversion the chapter assumes.

- *Augustinian-Calvinist.* The Augustinian tradition reads Paul's self-accusation as authentic, not rhetorical. Total depravity means the saved sinner remains aware of his prior condition; "I am the foremost" is the regenerate believer looking back at the unregenerate self with theological clarity. Conversion is monergistic, the work of grace alone on a will incapable of cooperation.
- *Wesleyan.* Wesley's reading allows for prevenient grace operating in Paul before conversion (the "ignorance" of 1:13 is itself a grace-conditioned state) and for a synergistic response. Paul is "foremost" not as an essentialized identity but as a paradigm of the universal possibility of conversion through grace cooperated with by faith.
- *Eastern Orthodox.* The Orthodox tradition reads through the lens of theosis: conversion is participation in the divine life, beginning in baptism and continuing as ascetic transformation. Paul's "foremost" is not forensic confession but ascetic recognition that humility is the precondition of deification. Chrysostom's homilies on 1 Timothy take this line.
- *Critical / rhetorical.* A long line of critical scholarship (Dibelius-Conzelmann, Brox, more recently Donelson) reads "foremost of sinners" as paraenetic hyperbole — a literary trope of the philosophical letter, in which the teacher exemplifies the reform he commends. On this reading, the autobiographical claim is functioning rhetorically, not psychologically.
- *Wright / paradigm-of-mercy.* N.T. Wright (*Paul for Everyone: The Pastoral Letters*) takes 1:15–16 as the hinge: "Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners" is the trustworthy saying, and Paul presents himself as "exhibit A" — *prōtos*, the foremost — precisely so that he becomes the *hypotypōsis*, the prototype or pattern, for everyone who will afterward believe. The deposit Timothy must guard (1:11, 18) is this gospel of mercy entrusted to a former blasphemer; the false teachers' speculation has lost exactly that, the news that the law's accusation gives way to the King's clemency.
- *Catholic.* The Catholic synthesis (CCC 1987–2029, 2006–2011) integrates merit and grace: justification is utterly gratuitous but engages the will it heals. Paul is the foremost not by metaphysical fact but by the office of apostle to the Gentiles, which makes his prior persecution of the church a paradigmatic case of mercy.

The hardest position to steelman is the critical-rhetorical reading, but it has real force. The verse stands inside a *pistos ho logos* formula and a doxology, both of which are public, formulaic, paraenetic devices. Reading 1:15 as functioning literarily — as the apostle exemplifying the gospel he commends — does not deny psychological reality but it does refuse to read the verse as raw confession.

**Where this leaves us.** The chapter is theologically generative precisely because it will not be reduced. Paul is making a claim about himself, about the gospel, and about the relationship between law and mercy, and the four traditions all touch real aspects of what he is doing.

The unresolved question that lands: if the law is good and the gospel is mercy and the apostle calling himself foremost of sinners is the bridge between them, what does it cost to be a teacher in this church — to handle the deposit you did not write, the law you did not give, and the mercy you did not earn, without becoming Hymenaeus?

## The Thread

- **Galatians 1:13–17** — Paul's other autobiographical conversion narrative. The Galatians version is polemical (defending apostolic authority); 1 Timothy 1 is paraenetic (offering the conversion as paradigm). The two together show Paul's life functioning differently in different rhetorical situations.
- **Romans 7:12** — "The law is holy, and the commandment is holy and righteous and good." The same affirmation, in the undisputed Pauline corpus, that the Pastorals make in 1 Tim 1:8. The lexical and conceptual continuity is one of the strongest in-house arguments for Pauline authorship of the Pastorals.
- **2 Timothy 1:13–14** — "Follow the pattern of the sound words" and "guard the good deposit (παραθήκη) entrusted to you." The deposit-language of 1 Timothy 1's charge becomes explicit in the second letter, structuring the Pastoral concern with traditioning and succession.
- **1 Corinthians 5:5** — "Hand this man over to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, that his spirit may be saved." The disciplinary formula in 1 Tim 1:20 (Hymenaeus and Alexander handed over to Satan) echoes a procedure already attested in the undisputed Paulines, suggesting that whatever the developed church order of the Pastorals, this particular practice predates them.
