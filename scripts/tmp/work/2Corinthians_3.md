# 2 Corinthians 3: Letter and Spirit, the Veil, and the Glory That Transfigures

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

- **Political/social situation**: Roman Corinth—colonial capital of Achaia, refounded by Caesar 44 BC, anchored chronologically by the Gallio inscription at Delphi (*Fouilles de Delphes* III.4 286, AD 51-52)—was Paul's pastoral home base and the audience here. Paul writes 2 Corinthians from Macedonia c. AD 55-56 after the Asian crisis (1:8-11) and Titus's restorative report (7:5-16). The chapter's pivotal social fact is the practice of *epistolai systatikai* (letters of recommendation) in Greco-Roman patronage networks. Theissen (*Social Setting of Pauline Christianity*) and Witherington (*Conflict and Community in Corinth*) both document how letters of recommendation circulated as the standard mechanism by which itinerant teachers—philosophers, rhetoricians, religious functionaries—established credentials in new cities. Cicero's *Letters to Friends* preserves dozens; the Oxyrhynchus papyri include hundreds. Paul's rivals at Corinth had apparently arrived with such letters and were challenging Paul's lack thereof. The chapter opens (3:1-3) by inverting the genre: the Corinthians themselves *are* Paul's letter.
- **Material culture**: Paul's metaphors are concrete and bivalent. *Letters written on stone tablets* (3:3, 7) point directly at the Decalogue tradition (Exod 31:18; 32:15-16; 34:1, 28-29; Deut 9:10), but they would have evoked, for Greco-Roman readers, public stelae as well—the inscriptional medium of Roman law and civic decree. The Roman colony of Corinth was thick with stele inscriptions. *Letters written on hearts* (3:3) draws on the Jeremianic new-covenant promise (Jer 31:31-34) and the Ezekielian heart-of-flesh promise (Ezek 11:19; 36:26-27). The *veil* of 3:13-18 takes its proximate background from Exod 34:29-35, where Moses' shining face is veiled so that the Israelites can bear it. The visual culture of Roman Corinth would have made veils familiar—women's *flammeum*, ceremonial veils in cult practice, the distinct ritual veiling of the *Vestales* and certain initiates of the mystery religions. Dale Martin (*The Corinthian Body*) and Bruce Winter (*After Paul Left Corinth*) document how veiling was a public language of status and ritual.
- **Authorship & dating**: Authentic Pauline. Within the unity-versus-partition debate, 2:14-7:4 is the unit Bornkamm identified as a separate apologetic letter inserted into a 1:1-2:13 + 7:5-16 thanksgiving frame. Chapter 3 sits at the heart of that proposed insertion. Margaret Thrall (ICC, 1994), Murray Harris (NIGTC, 2005), and Linda Belleville defend literary unity; Bornkamm, Furnish (Anchor Yale, 1984), and Welborn defend partition. P46, Sinaiticus, and Vaticanus all preserve the chapter whole—no manuscript witness reflects partition.
- **Archaeological & textual evidence**: P46 (c. AD 200), Sinaiticus, and Vaticanus carry the chapter cleanly. The Gallio inscription anchors the chronology. The Erastus pavement at Corinth provides the material context for stele-based public inscription. Qumran's *Damascus Document* (CD), *Community Rule* (1QS), and the *Hodayot* (1QH) preserve Second Temple Jewish meditation on covenant, hardness of heart, and divine illumination—essential parallels for Paul's exegesis here.
- **Key terms**: *epistolē* (ἐπιστολή, "letter"), *gramma* and *pneuma* (γράμμα, "letter [of writing]" / πνεῦμα, "Spirit"), *diakonia* (διακονία, "ministry, service"—of death, of condemnation, of righteousness, of the Spirit), *doxa* (δόξα, "glory"), *katargoumenēn* (καταργουμένην, "being abolished/brought to an end"—a present passive participle whose temporal force is one of the chapter's exegetical cruxes), *kalymma* (κάλυμμα, "veil"), *epistrephō* (ἐπιστρέφω, "to turn"—the verb of conversion in 3:16), *metamorphoumetha* (μεταμορφούμεθα, "we are being transformed"—the verb behind "metamorphosis"), *eikōn* (εἰκών, "image"), *eleutheria* (ἐλευθερία, "freedom").

## Lens 2 — The Literature

- **Genre**: Apostolic apologia in midrashic-exegetical mode. Paul defends his ministry by performing a sustained Christian midrash on Exod 34:29-35—reading the Mosaic veil-narrative as a typological-eschatological key to the relation between old and new covenants. Carol Stockhausen's *Moses' Veil and the Glory of the New Covenant* (1989) and Linda Belleville's *Reflections of Glory* (1991) are the foundational monographs on Paul's exegetical procedure here.
- **Devices**: The chapter is built on chains of *qal-vahomer* (lesser-to-greater) argument, the standard rabbinic argumentative form: if the ministry-of-death came with glory (3:7), how much more (*pollō mallon*) the ministry of the Spirit (3:8); if the ministry of condemnation had glory (3:9), the ministry of righteousness must far exceed it (3:9-10); if what was passing came with glory (3:11), how much more what is permanent. The midrashic re-reading of Exod 34 in 3:13-16 is technically virtuosic—Paul exploits ambiguities in the Hebrew (whose face the veil obscured, why it was veiled, what was being hidden) to construct a typological reading of Israel's hardened heart. 3:17-18 climaxes in a doxological-pneumatological-soteriological compression: the Lord *is* the Spirit, freedom is the Spirit's effect, and unveiled-face glory-beholding is transformative. The rhetorical questions of 3:1 (Are we beginning to commend ourselves? Do we need letters?) reach back to the open question of 2:16 ("Who is sufficient?") and are answered in 3:5-6.
- **Locate in argument**: Chapter 3 is the theological heart of the apostolic apologia (chapters 3-7). 2:16's question ("Who is sufficient?") receives its answer in 3:5-6 (sufficiency from God for new-covenant ministry). 3:6's contrast (letter kills / Spirit gives life) generates the entire glory-comparison of 3:7-11. 3:12-18's veil-meditation prepares the open-faced apostolic boldness of 4:1-6 and the cruciform shape of 4:7-12.
- **Textual variants**: 3:1 is firm, with very minor variation in the construction of the rhetorical question. 3:3 has variation between *kardiais sarkinais* ("hearts of flesh"—Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, the great uncials) and *kardiais sarkikais* ("fleshly/sinful hearts"—a few Western and Byzantine witnesses); the first reading is original, with the Ezekielian "heart of flesh" promise as backing. 3:7's *katargoumenēn* (present passive participle) is firm but exegetically charged: was the Mosaic glory *fading*, or *being abolished*, or *being brought to an end* in some other temporal mode? The lexical question is unresolved—Hafemann (1995) argues against the "fading" reading on Exodus-Rabbah grounds. 3:14's *katargeitai* is similarly central—is it the veil, the old covenant, or both that is being abolished in Christ? 3:17's "the Lord is the Spirit" (*ho de kyrios to pneuma estin*) is textually firm but theologically explosive—the trinitarian-pneumatological identification raises questions the patristic tradition wrestled with for centuries. 3:18's *katoptrizomenoi* ("beholding as in a mirror" or "reflecting"—the middle voice is genuinely ambiguous) is one of the great exegetical cruxes; Belleville and Hafemann take it as "beholding," Hays as both, others as "reflecting."

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory**

- Patristic: Origen's *Homilies on Exodus* and *De Principiis* IV use 2 Cor 3 as the foundational text for allegorical hermeneutics—the *gramma* / *pneuma* distinction maps directly onto Origen's literal/spiritual senses of Scripture. The Alexandrian tradition (Clement, Origen, Cyril) reads the chapter as a hermeneutical key. Augustine's *De Spiritu et Littera* (AD 412) is the great Western patristic monograph; Augustine reads the *gramma kills* against Pelagius—the Law as bare moral demand without grace cannot give life, and Paul's *gramma* / *pneuma* contrast becomes the Augustinian-Pauline ground for the doctrine of grace. John Chrysostom's homilies on 2 Cor 3 read the chapter against Marcion (rejecting any contempt for the Old Covenant) while affirming the surpassing glory of the new.
- Medieval: Aquinas's *Super Secundam Epistolam ad Corinthios* and the systematic treatment in *ST* I-II qq. 106-108 (the New Law) build directly on 2 Cor 3:6. The New Law, Aquinas argues, is "principally the grace of the Holy Spirit, given through faith in Christ" (q. 106 a. 1)—a formulation directly indebted to *gramma* / *pneuma*. The *Glossa Ordinaria* preserves the patristic harmonization. The Eastern medieval tradition (Symeon the New Theologian, Gregory Palamas) reads 3:18's transformation-by-glory as foundational for *theōsis* and the hesychast doctrine of the divine energies.
- Reformation: Luther's Law/Gospel hermeneutic is forged in 2 Cor 3:6. The *gramma* that kills is the Law as *Gesetz*—commanding without enabling—and the *pneuma* that gives life is the Gospel-mediated Spirit. Luther's preface to Romans, his Galatians lectures (1531/1535), and the Lutheran confessions (Apology IV; Formula of Concord V-VI) all run on this distinction. Calvin moderates—the *third use of the Law* (Inst. II.7.12-14) reads 3:6 as condemning the Law-without-Spirit, not the Law itself; the Law remains useful as guide for the sanctified believer when read in the Spirit. The Lutheran-Reformed Law/Gospel disagreement has 2 Cor 3 at its center.
- Modern critical: Käsemann ("Geist und Buchstabe," 1962/1969) and Bultmann read the *gramma* / *pneuma* contrast existentially—the Law as letter is the law as a structure of human self-justification; the Spirit is divine grace dissolving that structure. Sanders's *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977) and Dunn's *New Perspective on Paul* (Manson Lecture 1982) push back: Paul is not opposing Law per se but legalistic appropriation of Law without Spirit. Wright (*Climax of the Covenant*, 1991) reads 2 Cor 3 as Paul's covenantal-narrative argument: the new covenant fulfills the prophetic promise (Jer 31, Ezek 36), and the old covenant's glory was always meant to give way to the greater glory of fulfillment, not to be repudiated. Hays (*Echoes of Scripture in the Letters of Paul*, 1989) reads the chapter as a sophisticated intertextual midrash. Hafemann's *Paul, Moses, and the History of Israel: The Letter/Spirit Contrast and the Argument from Scripture in 2 Cor 3* (1995) is the most sustained recent monograph; Hafemann argues for a non-supersessionist Paul who is reading Israel's own *Heilsgeschichte* in light of fulfillment, not replacement. Belleville's *Reflections of Glory* and Stockhausen's *Moses' Veil* round out the field.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory**

- Exodus 34:29-35 is the source text. Moses descends Sinai with shining face after speaking with YHWH; the people are afraid; he veils his face after speaking with them and unveils it when speaking with YHWH. The Hebrew leaves several questions open, and Jewish midrashic tradition explores them. *Exodus Rabbah* 47 reads the shining face as recompense for Moses' refusing to eat or drink during the forty days on Sinai. Pseudo-Philo (*Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum* 12) emphasizes Moses' superhuman radiance. Targum Onqelos and Targum Pseudo-Jonathan to Exod 34 expand the narrative theologically. There is no Jewish parallel to Paul's claim that the *radiance was fading*—a claim Paul appears to develop from the present-passive participle *katargoumenēn* but which Jewish tradition does not read in the source text.
- The supersessionism question is the fault line. Christian readers from Marcion (rejecting the Old Testament) through the medieval *adversus Iudaeos* literature through Luther's late polemics have read 3:14-15 ("their minds were hardened… a veil lies over their hearts") as warrant for theological anti-Judaism—Israel as veiled, hardened, in need of conversion. The post-Holocaust correction has been seismic. Vatican II's *Nostra Aetate* (1965) §4 explicitly repudiated collective-guilt readings; the Vatican's 1985 "Notes on the Correct Way to Present Jews and Judaism in Preaching and Catechesis" addresses 2 Cor 3:14 by name, urging careful exegesis that does not warrant supersessionist contempt.
- Daniel Boyarin (*A Radical Jew*), Mark Nanos (*The Mystery of Romans*; *Reading Paul Within Judaism*), Pamela Eisenbaum (*Paul Was Not a Christian*, 2009), and Paula Fredriksen (*Paul, the Pagans' Apostle*, 2017) develop the Paul-Within-Judaism reading. On their account, Paul is not arguing that the old covenant is replaced. He is arguing, in the prophetic mode, that *some* of his Jewish contemporaries cannot yet read Moses with the messianic key Paul has been given. This is an *intra-Jewish* prophetic critique, not a Christian repudiation of Judaism. Paul addresses Gentile churches, but his argument with Israel is one Jewish voice among several. Hays and Wright stop short of the full Paul-Within-Judaism reading but explicitly reject hard supersessionism in their reading of 2 Cor 3.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge**: They converge on the scriptural fact: Paul is reading Exod 34 with messianic eyes, and the result is a transformation-theology of unveiled glory. They diverge sharply on whether this reading necessarily implies supersession of the Mosaic covenant or whether it is a fulfillment-within-covenant reading that does not displace Israel. The painful Christian history of using 3:14-15 to ground theological anti-Judaism is the elephant in the room. Post-Holocaust Christian theology cannot read this chapter without that history in view.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What Paul believed God was doing in this chapter is best captured as the *vindication of new-covenant ministry as the unveiling of God's glory in transforming Spirit*. The Corinthians had been impressed by rivals carrying letters of recommendation; Paul's answer is to relocate the entire question of credentials from external paper to interior transformation, and to ground that transformation in the fulfillment of the prophetic new-covenant promise. Two genuine theological tensions emerge.

**Tension 1: Letter and Spirit—what does Paul mean by *gramma* and *pneuma*?** 3:6 is the most controverted single verse in the chapter, and its reading determines the theology of the entire Christian relation to Torah.

- *Lutheran* (Luther's Galatians lectures; Apology IV; Käsemann; Bultmann): the Law as letter kills. The Old Covenant Law, read in itself, demands without enabling, and so condemns. The Spirit gives life because Christ has fulfilled the Law and the Spirit applies that fulfillment. The Lutheran instinct is sharp Law/Gospel disjunction. 3:6's *gramma* is Torah-as-such considered apart from the Spirit's gift in Christ.
- *Reformed* (Calvin Inst. II.7.12-14; Bavinck *Reformed Dogmatics* IV): third use of the Law. Calvin distinguishes *Lex* in three uses—mirror (showing sin), restraint (in society), and guide (for the sanctified). 3:6's *gramma* is the Law abused as a structure of self-justification (the *opus operatum* without the Spirit), not Torah itself. Read with the Spirit, Torah is the believer's joy and guide. Calvin's pneumatological reading of Torah is the great Reformed hermeneutical contribution.
- *Catholic* (CCC 121-130 on the Old and New Testaments; Aquinas ST I-II qq. 98-108): continuity-and-fulfillment. The Old Law was good and given by God but propaedeutic; the New Law is the grace of the Holy Spirit given through faith. Aquinas's *Lex Nova* directly invokes 2 Cor 3:6. *Dei Verbum* §16 (Vatican II) affirms continuity: the Old Testament books have permanent value, attain their full meaning in the New. There is no Marcionite repudiation of Torah.
- *Eastern Orthodox*: typological-economic reading. The Old Covenant is the *paideia* of the new; the *gramma* / *pneuma* contrast is read as the contrast between the shadow (*skia*) and the reality (*sōma*), drawing on Heb 10:1. The Mosaic glory is genuine glory but partial; the new covenant glory unveils what was always already in the divine economy.
- *New Perspective / Paul Within Judaism* (Sanders, Dunn, Wright, Hafemann, Hays, Nanos, Fredriksen): Paul is not opposing Torah-in-itself. He is opposing Torah read without the Spirit's messianic illumination. *Gramma* is the bare letter that, without the Spirit's interpretive illumination, cannot deliver the covenantal life it promises. Hafemann's *Paul, Moses, and the History of Israel* argues at length that 2 Cor 3 is a covenantal-narrative argument about Israel's history, not a supersessionist repudiation of Israel.
- *Critical/academic*: Käsemann, Bultmann, Schoeps tilt toward sharper discontinuity; Sanders, Wright, Dunn, Hays, Hafemann tilt toward continuity-with-fulfillment. The center of gravity in current Pauline scholarship has shifted away from the older Lutheran reading.

The position least intuitive to many evangelical Protestants is the New Perspective / Paul-Within-Judaism reading, because it appears to undercut the Reformation's foundational hermeneutical move. *Steelmanned*: the New Perspective is not denying that the Law condemns sin or that grace is necessary for salvation. It is denying that Paul's quarrel was with Torah-as-such, and arguing that Paul's quarrel was with appropriations of Torah that severed it from the Spirit's enabling work. The exegetical case is strong—Paul calls the Law "holy, righteous, and good" (Rom 7:12), affirms its abiding validity in summary form (Rom 13:8-10), and in 2 Cor 3 presents the Mosaic glory as *real glory* (3:7), only superseded in degree by the greater glory of the Spirit's ministry. The historical case is strong—Second Temple Judaism was never a religion of bare legalism, and reading Paul as opposing Torah-as-such retrojects later Christian polemics onto a Jewish thinker who never repudiated Torah. The pastoral case is strong—the older Lutheran reading has been used to ground Christian contempt for Judaism with catastrophic consequences. *Where this leaves us*: 3:6 will not yield to a flattened "Law bad, grace good" reading. What Paul has insisted on is that any covenantal life that does not flow from the Spirit's fulfilling work is dead, whether the covenantal text in question is Mosaic or otherwise.

**Tension 2: Supersessionism in 3:14-18—is the old covenant veiled and Israel hardened until conversion to Christ?** This is the post-Holocaust question that no Christian commentator can ignore.

- *Traditional Christian replacement / hard supersessionist*: the old covenant is replaced; Israel as a covenantal community has lapsed; only individual Jewish conversions to Christ restore the veil-removal. Justin's *Dialogue with Trypho*, Chrysostom's *Adversus Judaeos*, the medieval *adversus Iudaeos* tradition, and Luther's late *On the Jews and Their Lies* (1543) embody the hard form. Most Christian commentators today repudiate this position by name.
- *Catholic post-Nostra Aetate non-supersessionist* (Vatican II *Nostra Aetate*; CCC 839-840; the Pontifical Biblical Commission's 2001 *The Jewish People and Their Sacred Scriptures*): the old covenant is "never revoked" (John Paul II Mainz 1980; Benedict XVI confirmed 2010, with careful nuance). The Jewish people remain in covenantal relationship with God. 2 Cor 3:14-15 is read carefully as Paul's pastoral grief over those of his contemporaries who have not yet seen the messianic fulfillment, not as a doctrinal indictment of Judaism as such. Romans 9-11 is the controlling Pauline text; 2 Cor 3 is read in its light.
- *Reformed dispensational* (Darby; Scofield; Ryrie *Dispensationalism*; some American evangelicalism): a sharp distinction between Israel and the church. The old covenant pertains to Israel and will be restored at the eschaton (Rom 11:26); the new covenant pertains to the church. 2 Cor 3 then describes the church's experience of new-covenant glory while preserving Israel's distinct future.
- *Reformed covenantal* (Westminster; Hodge; Bavinck): one covenant of grace administered differently across redemptive epochs. The new covenant fulfills the old; Israel-and-church are one people of God. 2 Cor 3:14-18 describes the situation of those (Jew or Gentile) who have not yet turned to Christ, with the conviction that the Christian church is the continuing covenant community.
- *Paul Within Judaism* (Nanos, Eisenbaum, Fredriksen): 2 Cor 3:14-18 is an intra-Jewish prophetic critique. Paul is mourning that some of his Jewish contemporaries do not yet read Moses with the messianic key he has received. The "veil" is not a doctrine of Jewish theological inferiority; it is a phenomenological description of unrecognized fulfillment. Paul addresses Gentile churches, and his argument with Israel is one Jewish voice among the messianic-Jewish voices of the first century. Mark Nanos's *Reading Paul Within Judaism* (2017) develops the argument systematically.
- *Eastern Orthodox*: typological reading; the old covenant is fulfilled, not abolished, in Christ. Less culturally entangled with the Western *adversus Iudaeos* tradition, the Orthodox reading tends to keep the typological grammar (shadow/reality) without the polemical edge.

The position that requires the most careful steelmanning is the traditional supersessionist reading, *because it has been so thoroughly disowned that contemporary readers risk caricaturing it without engaging it*. *Steelmanned*: the supersessionist reader is not (in the careful patristic and medieval forms) claiming Jewish theological inferiority. The claim is that the Christ-event has *fulfilled* the covenantal promises in such a way that the old covenant's dispensational role has terminated; what remains for Israel is the same path open to all—turning to Christ (3:16, *epistrephō*). The exegetical case rests on Paul's actual language: "their minds were hardened" (3:14, *epōrōthē*); "to this day" (3:14-15) the veil remains until "one turns to the Lord" (3:16). The text does say what it says. The post-Holocaust correction is right that this reading has been used to ground monstrous evil; the supersessionist's defense is that the abuse of a reading is not the same as its falsity, and Paul's own categories deserve to be read as his own. *Where this leaves us*: 3:14-18 demands a reading that takes Paul's own language seriously *and* refuses any extraction of the text from its catastrophic Christian afterlife. The most defensible contemporary readings (Hafemann, Hays, Wright, Catholic post-*Nostra Aetate*) hold both demands together: Paul's claim that messianic illumination unveils the covenantal text *and* the conviction that this is an intra-covenantal claim, not a warrant for theological contempt. Paul has not given Christians a license for anti-Judaism. He has given Christians a way of reading Israel's own scriptures as fulfilled—and he has insisted, in Romans 9-11, that the gifts and calling of God to Israel are irrevocable.

**One unresolved question**: 3:18 makes the audacious claim that the apostolic community, with unveiled face, beholds the Lord's glory and is being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another, and 3:17 grounds this in the identification "the Lord is the Spirit." If transformation into Christ's image is the present effect of the Spirit's unveiling work, what does it mean for Christian discipleship that the metric of authentic ministry is not what we say or do but what the looking *makes of us*—and how would a community recognize when its supposed glory-beholding has degenerated into religious self-regard, given that self-regard has its own way of feeling like glory?

## The Thread

- **3:3 ("written on tablets of human hearts") → Jeremiah 31:31-34 / Ezekiel 36:26-27**: Paul's claim that the Corinthians are a letter from Christ written by the Spirit on hearts of flesh is a direct invocation of the new-covenant promise. Reading 3:3 without Jeremiah and Ezekiel collapses Paul's argument; reading it with them makes the chapter an extended exegesis of prophetic fulfillment.
- **3:6 (*gramma kills, pneuma gives life*) → Romans 7:6 / Romans 2:29**: Paul uses the *gramma* / *pneuma* contrast in three places, all addressing the relation of Torah to Spirit-mediated covenantal life. Romans 7:6 ("we serve in the new way of the Spirit and not in the old way of the letter") and Romans 2:29 ("circumcision is a matter of the heart, by the Spirit, not by the letter") are the parallels. The triad must be read together to avoid one-sided readings.
- **3:7-11 (Mosaic glory and surpassing glory) → Exodus 34:29-35 / John 1:14-18**: The Mosaic veil narrative is the source text. John 1:14-18 ("the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory… for the law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ") is the parallel NT meditation, with closer affinities to 2 Cor 3 than is often noticed.
- **3:17 ("the Lord is the Spirit") → Romans 8:9-11 / John 14:16-26**: The Trinitarian-pneumatological identification raises questions the patristic tradition wrestled with for centuries. Whether Paul collapses Christ and Spirit (modal Christology) or speaks of an economic identification (the risen Christ acts in and through the Spirit) determines the verse's Trinitarian implications. Most patristic and modern Trinitarian readings take the economic option—the Lord (Christ) is encountered in this age as the Spirit, without ontological collapse of the persons.
- **3:18 (*metamorphoumetha*) → Romans 12:2 / Philippians 3:21 / 1 John 3:2**: The transformation language threads through the NT. Romans 12:2's "be transformed by the renewal of your mind" uses the same root for ethical-noetic transformation; Phil 3:21 for eschatological-bodily transformation; 1 John 3:2 ("when he appears we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is") for the eschatological vision. 3:18 is the present-tense claim that the eschatological metamorphosis has already begun under the Spirit's gaze-mediated work.
