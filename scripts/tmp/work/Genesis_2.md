# Genesis 2 — A Scholarly Reading

Genesis 2 opens with the completion of creation (vv. 1–3, which properly close the Genesis 1 account) and then pivots at v. 4 into what scholars almost universally regard as a *second*, independent creation narrative — one with a different vocabulary, theology, order of events, and divine name. Understanding this chapter requires understanding that pivot.

---

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political and cultural matrix.** Genesis 2:4b–25 is, in the classical Documentary Hypothesis (Wellhausen, refined by von Rad and Noth), the Yahwist (J) source. J is traditionally dated to the united monarchy or early divided monarchy (10th–9th c. BC, Judah), though a growing minority (Van Seters, Schmid, the "neo-documentarians" at Hebrew University) argue for an exilic or post-exilic date. Genesis 2:1–3 belongs to the Priestly (P) source, which most scholars date to the exilic or early post-exilic period (6th–5th c. BC), when Sabbath observance became a central identity marker for Judeans without a temple or land.

The *setting* of the narrative — a garden irrigated by four rivers including the Tigris and Euphrates — reflects a Mesopotamian imaginative geography. The author knows of Cush (Nubia/Ethiopia), Assyria, and Havilah (likely Arabian peninsula, where gold, bdellium resin, and onyx were traded). This is not a geography of Canaan; it is a cosmopolitan map of the ancient Near Eastern world, with Eden as its ungraspable center.

**Material culture.** The man is formed from *ʿadamah* (arable soil, not desert sand) and placed in a *gan* — a walled, irrigated pleasure-garden. Royal paradise-gardens are well-attested: Assyrian kings (Ashurnasirpal II, Sennacherib) boasted of planting gardens with exotic trees watered by diverted rivers. Cyrus's Persian *pairidaēza* (whence "paradise") is the archetype. The reader is meant to see Eden as a *royal* garden — and the man as placed there by the divine king to "work it and keep it" (*le-ʿovdah u-leshomrah*, v. 15), verbs later used of priestly Levitical service in the tabernacle (Num 3:7–8). Gordon Wenham has argued extensively that Eden is depicted as the archetypal sanctuary.

**Authorship and dating debate.** The seams are visible:
- Gen 1: God is *Elohim*; creation is by word, cosmic, ordered, humans (male and female together) created last as the climax.
- Gen 2: God is *YHWH Elohim* (the LORD God); creation is hands-on, earthy, anthropocentric. Man is created *first*, then plants, animals, woman.
- Gen 1 begins with watery chaos requiring separation; Gen 2 begins with arid land requiring irrigation.

These are not contradictions to be harmonized but two theological accounts preserved side-by-side by a redactor (R) who valued both.

**Archaeological/textual parallels.**
- **Enuma Elish** (Babylonian, copies from 7th c. BC but older): humans formed from clay mixed with the blood of the slain god Kingu, to serve the gods as slaves. Genesis inverts this: humans are formed not to relieve divine labor but to enjoy a garden.
- **Atrahasis Epic** (Old Babylonian, c. 1700 BC): the god Ea mixes clay with the flesh and blood of a slain god; the goddess Nintu/Mami pinches off fourteen pieces to form seven couples. The material substrate (clay/dust) matches Genesis; the theology diverges sharply.
- **Adapa Myth**: a primal man offered food by the high god An that would have granted immortality but refuses it on bad advice — a clear thematic cousin to the tree of life.
- **Gilgamesh** XI: Utnapishtim speaks of a plant of rejuvenation stolen by a serpent. The motif-pool is shared; Israel's theological use is distinctive.

**Key terms.**

- **אָדָם / ʾadam** — not a personal name here but "humankind" or "the earthling," punning on *ʾadamah* (ground/soil). The ESV's "Adam" in v. 20 is a translation choice; the Hebrew has the definite article *ha-ʾadam* ("the human") through most of the chapter. Only at 4:25 does *ʾadam* clearly become a proper name.
- **נֶפֶשׁ חַיָּה / nephesh ḥayyah** — "living creature" (v. 7). Crucially, this phrase is also used of animals in 1:20, 24. The human does not "have" a soul distinct from the body; the human *becomes* a living being when breath meets dust. Hebrew anthropology is holistic, not dualistic — a point Bultmann, Wolff (*Anthropology of the Old Testament*), and N. T. Wright hammer repeatedly against later Platonized readings.
- **עֵזֶר כְּנֶגְדּוֹ / ʿezer kenegdo** — "helper fit for him" (v. 18). *ʿEzer* nowhere in the Hebrew Bible connotes subordination; it is used sixteen of twenty-one times of God as Israel's helper (e.g., Ps 121:2). *Kenegdo* means "corresponding to/opposite him" — a counterpart, an equal-and-facing. The lexical data tells against the older translation-tradition that read subordination into this phrase.
- **צֵלָע / tsela** — traditionally "rib" (v. 21), but elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible (over forty occurrences) it means "side" — of the tabernacle, of a hill. Ziony Zevit (*What Really Happened in the Garden of Eden?*, 2013) argues this should be "side," suggesting the primal human was split rather than that a bone was extracted. The rabbinic tradition of the androgynous first human (b. Ber. 61a; Gen. Rab. 8:1) reads this way.
- **תַּרְדֵּמָה / tardemah** — "deep sleep" (v. 21), a rare word used for prophetic trance states (Gen 15:12 at Abram's covenant; Job 4:13). This is not anesthesia; it is a liminal, visionary state.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

**Genre.** Ancient Near Eastern cosmogonic myth, functioning as etiology — explaining why things *are* as they are: why humans work the ground, why men leave their parents for wives, why snakes crawl (ch. 3), why childbirth hurts. "Myth" in the technical sense: a narrative that grounds present reality in primordial events. Not "false"; rather, a mode of truth-telling distinct from historiography.

**Placement.** 2:1–3 completes the seven-day structure of 1:1–2:3, the whole forming a chiastic, liturgical hymn. Then 2:4 — "These are the generations (*toledot*) of the heavens and the earth" — begins the ten-part *toledot* formula that structures all of Genesis (2:4; 5:1; 6:9; 10:1; 11:10, 27; 25:12, 19; 36:1; 37:2). This editorial seam is the strongest internal evidence for composite authorship: the P narrative of 1:1–2:3 is bracketed off, and the J material begins fresh.

**Literary devices.**
- **Wordplay**: *ʾadam* from *ʾadamah* (v. 7); *ʾish*/*ʾishshah* (man/woman) in v. 23 — the woman named by a sound-echo, not by taxonomic dominion as the animals are named. Robert Alter notes the man's first recorded speech is poetry — ecstatic verse at the sight of the woman.
- **Chiasm**: Wenham maps the whole pericope as a concentric structure centered on the divine command of vv. 16–17.
- **Inclusio**: The chapter opens with "heavens and earth" finished; closes with man and woman united "one flesh." Cosmic completion mirrors human completion.
- **Typology-in-seed**: Eden as temple (cherubim will guard it in ch. 3; the tabernacle will be guarded by cherubim; the menorah evokes the tree of life). Wenham, Beale (*The Temple and the Church's Mission*), and Alexander develop this extensively.

**Textual variants.** The LXX at 2:2 reads "on the *sixth* day God finished" — likely a harmonizing change to avoid the awkwardness of God finishing *on* the seventh. The Samaritan Pentateuch and Peshitta agree with LXX against the Masoretic. Jubilees 2:16 preserves the MT reading. Most critical editions favor MT as *lectio difficilior*.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**In the canonical arc.** Genesis 2 establishes the baseline from which the rest of Scripture measures loss and hope: humans in unashamed communion with God, each other, and the land; vocation (work) that is not yet cursed; marriage as covenant union; sacred space where heaven and earth interpenetrate. Every subsequent biblical vision of restoration — prophetic, apocalyptic, Pauline — reaches back toward Eden. Revelation 22 deliberately closes the canon with the tree of life, the river, no shame.

### Jewish interpretive trajectory

**Second Temple.** *Jubilees* (2nd c. BC) legislates Sabbath back into creation itself, making Gen 2:1–3 the charter of Jewish identity. *Sirach* 17 retells creation with the human as glorified steward. Philo of Alexandria reads Gen 1 and Gen 2 as two creations: first the ideal, incorporeal human (Platonic Form); then the material human (*Leg. All.* 1.31). This two-Adams reading will prove fateful for Paul. Qumran's *4QInstruction* treats Eden as a sapiential paradigm.

**Rabbinic.** *Bereshit Rabbah* (5th c. AD) is extraordinary in its imaginative range. R. Yirmiyah ben Elazar: the first *ʾadam* was created androgynous, with two faces, then split (8:1) — likely reading *tsela* as "side." The Talmud (b. Sanh. 38b) debates whether the man was created from dust gathered worldwide. Crucially, rabbinic tradition does *not* develop a doctrine of original sin from Genesis 2–3; the *yetzer hara* (evil inclination) is present in humans from birth but balanced by *yetzer hatov*. The fall is real, but not ontologically corrupting in the Augustinian sense.

**Medieval.** Rashi (11th c.) reads closely and often plainly: on *ʿezer kenegdo*, "if he is worthy, she is a help; if unworthy, she is against him" — wordplay on *kenegdo*. Ibn Ezra emphasizes peshat (plain sense) and is skeptical of allegory. Nachmanides (Ramban, 13th c.) sees deep kabbalistic meaning: the tree of knowledge confers *da'at* in the sexual/volitional sense; pre-fall humans acted by instinct like the heavenly bodies. Maimonides (*Guide* I.2) reads the tree of knowledge as the fall from intellectual apprehension of truth/falsehood into the lesser realm of social convention (good/evil) — a strikingly philosophical reading.

**Modern Jewish.** Martin Buber reads Eden as the archetype of I-Thou immediacy, lost in shame and objectification. Abraham Joshua Heschel, in *The Sabbath*, takes Gen 2:1–3 as the key to Jewish theology: Judaism is a religion of *time*, not space; the Sabbath is a "cathedral in time." Jon Levenson (*Creation and the Persistence of Evil*) emphasizes that Genesis creation is not *creatio ex nihilo* but the ordering of chaos, with Sabbath as the liturgical completion. Avivah Zornberg (*The Beginning of Desire*) offers a psychoanalytic reading: the garden is a state of pre-desire, which the tree introduces — the human condition begins with the eruption of longing.

### Christian interpretive trajectory

**Patristic.** Irenaeus (*Against Heresies*) reads Adam as a *child*, immature, destined to grow into the image of God realized fully in Christ — recapitulation theology. The fall is tragic but not catastrophic in the Augustinian sense. Origen allegorizes: the garden is the soul, the trees are virtues, the rivers are the cardinal disciplines. Augustine — decisively for the West — reads Genesis 2 in conjunction with Romans 5 to produce the doctrine of *original sin* transmitted through sexual generation (*De Genesi ad litteram*; *City of God* XIV). Pre-fall sexuality, Augustine speculates painfully, would have been rational and passionless. Chrysostom, representing the Antiochene literal tradition, is more restrained, emphasizing the goodness of marriage in 2:24.

**Medieval.** Aquinas (*ST* I.92) treats the woman's creation philosophically: she is from the man's side ("not from the head, lest she rule over him; not from the feet, lest he despise her; but from the side, to be his companion" — a line sometimes attributed to Peter Lombard or earlier). The *Glossa Ordinaria* standardized typological readings: Adam's sleep = Christ's death; woman from his side = church from Christ's pierced side (a reading patristic in origin, found in Tertullian and Augustine).

**Reformation.** Luther's Genesis lectures are extensive; he reads 2:24 as instituting marriage as a creation ordinance, against monasticism. Calvin emphasizes human vocation (the mandate to "work and keep") as the dignity of labor, grounding the Protestant work ethic. Both reject allegory in favor of *sensus literalis*, though both retain typological readings of Adam-Christ via Romans 5.

**Historical-critical.** Wellhausen (*Prolegomena*, 1878) crystallized the JEDP source theory. Gunkel brought form criticism and comparative Near Eastern material. Von Rad's commentary (1956) stands as a classic theological reading informed by criticism. Brevard Childs redirected attention to the *canonical* form: whatever the sources, the received text juxtaposes two accounts for a purpose.

**Contemporary.** Walter Brueggemann emphasizes the *vocational* reading: humans are covenant partners entrusted with the earth. N. T. Wright reads Eden as prototypical temple-and-vocation, with humans as royal priests — Israel's calling, and then Christ's and the church's, is to be the true Adam who succeeds where the first one failed; for Wright the Genesis 2 vocation ("work it and keep it") is exactly the commission Jesus takes up and the church inherits. John Walton (*The Lost World of Genesis One*) argues Genesis is a *functional* cosmology, not a material one — concerned with assigning roles, not describing mechanism. Terence Fretheim emphasizes divine vulnerability: YHWH experiments ("what will he name them?"), and the first thing God calls "not good" is human loneliness.

### Convergences and divergences

Jewish and Christian readings **converge** on: Eden as sacred space; humans as dignified creatures of dust-and-breath; marriage as covenantal; Sabbath as theologically weighty; creation as good.

They **diverge** sharply on:
1. **Original sin.** Christian (especially Western) tradition reads Gen 2–3 through Rom 5 as universal hereditary corruption. Jewish tradition reads the *yetzer hara* as present from creation, with fall-as-maturation rather than fall-as-ontological-ruin. Eastern Orthodox tradition is closer to the Jewish reading than to Augustine.
2. **Typology.** Christian readings take Adam's sleep + wound-in-side as prefiguring Christ-and-church. Jewish readings reject this as eisegesis.
3. **Sabbath.** For Jewish tradition, 2:1–3 is the foundational law. For most Christian traditions, Sabbath is fulfilled/transformed in Christ (Heb 4) or transferred to the Lord's Day — a move Jewish interpreters see as severing the text from its plain meaning.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What is the author (or the redactor preserving both accounts) doing theologically?

**The P author of 2:1–3** is making a polemical claim against Babylonian theology. In *Enuma Elish*, creation climaxes in Marduk's temple and the gods' banquet. In Genesis, creation climaxes not in a temple but in *time* — a consecrated day. Heschel's reading is exactly right: this is creation's first act of holiness, and it is attached to time, not space or matter. The exilic community without temple or land is told: your God sanctified time itself before anything else was sanctified.

**The J author of 2:4b–25** is making a different theological move. Humans are *ʿaphar* (dust) animated by divine breath — radically humble, radically exalted. They are placed in a garden not as slaves (contra Atrahasis) but as priest-kings who "work and keep." The first thing declared "not good" is solitude. The creation of woman is narrated with tenderness; her arrival is met with the Bible's first poem. Sexuality precedes shame.

### Theological tension 1: What is the image of God — and is it fully present in Gen 2?

- **Catholic** (CCC 355–361, *Gaudium et Spes* 12): image consists in rationality, freedom, and the capacity for communion; realized in the communion of persons, male and female. Gen 2 is the vocational dimension of what Gen 1 declares ontologically.
- **Reformed** (Calvin, Bavinck, Hoekema): image is *relational and ethical*, consisting originally in righteousness, knowledge, holiness — damaged in the fall and restored in Christ. Gen 2 shows the image in its original functional deployment.
- **Lutheran** (classical): image is primarily the original righteousness, *lost* at the fall; what remains post-fall is only a remnant. This puts enormous weight on redemption.
- **Wesleyan / Eastern Orthodox**: image is capacity for growth into likeness (*theosis*). Gen 2 depicts humans at the beginning of a journey, not at a lost height. Irenaeus's child-Adam flourishes here.
- **Jewish** (Maimonides, *Guide* I.1): image is the intellect capable of apprehending God. Levenson and Sarna: image is royal, a democratization of ancient Near Eastern royal ideology where only the king is "image of the god."
- **Critical/comparative** (Middleton, *The Liberating Image*): image is *functional-royal*, consistent with Egyptian and Mesopotamian royal statuary ideology, democratized to all humans.

**Steelmanning the Lutheran view** (least intuitive to many modern readers): if the image is essentially the unbroken relationship of righteousness with God, then what is "lost" at the fall is not a faculty we can introspect or recover by effort, but a relational reality that can only be restored from God's side. This protects against every form of self-salvation — including the modern progressive confidence that human dignity is self-evident and self-sustaining. The 20th century, Luther would say, vindicated the doctrine: humans who are "in the image" produced Auschwitz. Only a rescued image holds.

### Theological tension 2: What is the relationship between the sexes in Genesis 2?

- **Complementarian** (Grudem, Piper, Ortlund): man's prior creation, his naming of the woman, and her role as "helper" establish a created order of male headship, affirmed by Paul in 1 Cor 11 and 1 Tim 2. Equal in dignity, differentiated in role.
- **Egalitarian** (Bilezikian, Fee, Bauckham, Webb): *ʿezer kenegdo* means strong counterpart; the woman is not named by the man until after the fall (3:20); "one flesh" implies mutuality not hierarchy. Hierarchy is a *consequence* of the fall (3:16), not a creational good.
- **Catholic new-feminism** (John Paul II, *Theology of the Body*): the sexes are ontologically distinct yet equal; the "one flesh" of 2:24 is nuptial iconography of Trinitarian self-gift. Gender is theologically weighty but not hierarchical in the complementarian sense.
- **Jewish** (rabbinic androgyne tradition; Zornberg): the original human was unified-then-differentiated; desire and recognition are the point; halakhic patriarchy coexists with deep valuation of the woman.

**Steelmanning the complementarian view** (least intuitive to many contemporary readers): the text does show an asymmetry — the man is formed first, receives the command, names the creatures, is the one from whom the woman is taken. Paul reads it this way (1 Cor 11:8–9). To read this purely as literary order rather than theological order requires arguing against both the narrative's obvious shape and its canonical reception. One need not conclude patriarchal subordination to acknowledge that the text stages an asymmetrical relationship that egalitarian readings sometimes too quickly flatten.

### The ethical difficulty

The chapter's shadow is the prohibition: "in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die." This divine threat has troubled readers from Philo onward. Why forbid knowledge of good and evil? Why attach death to disobedience? Why plant the tree at all? The tradition's answers vary: Augustine says the prohibition was easy, testing love; Maimonides says the "knowledge" in question is a degradation, not an ascent; Bonhoeffer (*Creation and Fall*) says the tree is the limit that makes freedom real — without a "no," the "yes" is meaningless. The text itself offers no theodicy; it simply stages the condition.

**Where this leaves us:** Genesis 2 refuses the modern choice between scientific cosmology and religious myth. It is doing something neither: it is staging the *meaning* of being human — dust-and-breath, vocation-and-limit, solitude-and-communion, unashamed-and-vulnerable — in a form dense enough to bear three millennia of reading without exhausting.

---

**The question that lands:** You were formed — so this text insists — from dust and divine breath, placed in a world to "work and keep," given company because aloneness is the first thing God called "not good," and set beneath a limit that makes your freedom real. Which of those four — the dust, the breath, the vocation, or the limit — are you currently pretending doesn't apply to you?

## The Thread

- **Exodus 20:8–11** — The Sabbath command grounds itself explicitly in Gen 2:2–3. When you get there, notice that Deut 5 grounds Sabbath in the Exodus instead. Two theological anchors for the same practice.
- **Ezekiel 28:13–14** — The king of Tyre is indicted as having been "in Eden, the garden of God... on the holy mountain of God." Eden-as-cosmic-mountain-sanctuary is assumed by the prophet; watch for the temple imagery.
- **Song of Songs** — read by the rabbis (Akiva: "the Holy of Holies") as Eden restored: a garden, two lovers, no shame, no mention of God because God is ambient. The canonical counterweight to Gen 3.
- **Romans 5:12–21 and 1 Corinthians 15:45–49** — When you get to Paul, you'll meet the "last Adam" reading that will transform how Christians henceforth read Genesis 2. Philo's two-Adams reading stands behind Paul's move.
- **Revelation 22:1–5** — The canon closes with the river, the tree of life, the servants who reign, and no shame. The Bible is an inclusio: the garden you're reading about now is the garden you end in — but a city this time.
