# Ezekiel 27: The Lament for Tyre and the Trade-Network of the Ancient World

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political situation**: Chapter 27 is the second oracle in the Tyre cycle (chs. 26–28), undated in its superscription but presupposing the same 587/586 BC horizon as chapter 26. Where chapter 26 *announces* Tyre's destruction, chapter 27 *laments* it in a fully developed *qina* (lament-song). The genre shift is significant: the chapter is the longest sustained piece of *trade-and-economic poetry* in the Hebrew Bible, structured around the conceit of **Tyre as a magnificent ship** that sinks in the heart of the seas. Embedded within the ship-metaphor is a remarkable **trade-itinerary** (27:12–24) listing the peoples and goods of the Phoenician commercial network: from Spain (Tarshish) in the west to the upper Tigris (Asshur, Eden, Canneh) in the east, from Cilicia (Tubal, Meshech) in the north to Yemen (Sheba, Raamah) in the south. **Greenberg (Anchor)**, **Block (NICOT)**, and **Zimmerli (Hermeneia)** all treat the chapter as essentially Ezekielian, though Zimmerli identifies the prose insertion of 27:9b–25a (the trade-list itself) as possibly secondary — a redactional interpolation expanding the original poetic core. The chapter is unique in the Hebrew Bible for the *concentration* of named foreign peoples and exotic luxury goods.

**Material culture**: The chapter is **a primary source document for late-7th to early-6th century BC Phoenician commerce**. The named trade partners in 27:12–24 are nearly all attested archaeologically: **Tarshish** (almost certainly **Tartessos** in southwestern Spain near modern Huelva, attested by Phoenician inscriptions and the **Cancho Roano sanctuary** site; alternatively the **Sardinian Nora Stele**'s *TRSS* reference); **Javan** (Greek *Iōn-* / Ionian Greek world, attested in the **Persepolis Fortification tablets** as *Yauna*); **Tubal and Meshech** (the **Tabal** and **Mushki** of Neo-Assyrian inscriptions, southeastern Anatolia and central Cappadocia); **Beth-togarmah** (Tegarama / Til-garimmu of the Neo-Assyrian sources, in eastern Anatolia near Malatya); **Dedan** (the oasis-city in northwestern Arabia, attested by the **Dedanite inscriptions** in the al-Ula valley); **Sheba** (Saba in Yemen, attested by hundreds of **Sabaic inscriptions** and the **Marib dam complex**); **Raamah** (probably **Raghmat** in southern Arabia); **Aram** (Damascene Syria); **Helbon** (modern **Halbun** in the Damascus region, archaeologically known for fine wines into the medieval period); **Haran** (the major caravan city in upper Mesopotamia, modern **Harran** in southern Turkey, with the famous Sin-temple); **Asshur** (the Assyrian heartland); **Chilmad** (variously identified, possibly **Calneh** or near Asshur). The goods are equally well-attested: **silver, iron, tin, lead** from Tarshish (the western Mediterranean was the ancient world's main source of all four); **bronze vessels and human beings** from Javan-Tubal-Meshech (the eastern-Mediterranean and Black-Sea slave trade is well-documented, the Greek *andrapodon* trade running through the Aegean and Black Sea ports); **horses, war horses, mules** from Beth-togarmah (Anatolia was the great horse-region of the ancient Near East — the **Mitanni horse-training tablets of Kikkuli** are the most famous textual evidence); **ivory tusks and ebony** from Dedan (East African and Indian Ocean trade routed through Arabia); **emeralds, purple, embroidered work, fine linen, coral, ruby** from Syria; **spices, precious stones, gold** from Sheba (the famous *frankincense road*, archaeologically traced through the Hejaz to the **Arabia Felix** Yemeni source-region).

**Phoenician shipbuilding**: 27:5–9 gives a **detailed inventory of materials** used in Phoenician ship construction: planks of *fir from Senir* (the Hermon-Antilebanon region), masts of *cedar from Lebanon*, oars of *oaks from Bashan*, decks of *pines from Cyprus inlaid with ivory*, sails of *fine embroidered linen from Egypt*, awnings of *blue and purple from Elishah* (probably **Cyprus** again, possibly **Alashiya** of the late-Bronze-Age cuneiform record). The two great late-Bronze-Age shipwrecks excavated by **George Bass and the Institute of Nautical Archaeology** — the **Uluburun wreck** (c. 1300 BC, off southwestern Turkey) and the **Cape Gelidonya wreck** (c. 1200 BC, off the same coast) — both contain *exactly* this material profile: copper, tin, glass, ivory, amber, hippopotamus tusks, Mycenaean and Cypriot pottery, Canaanite and Egyptian goods. The Uluburun wreck has been definitively identified as a **Phoenician (or proto-Phoenician) merchant ship**. **Aubet (*The Phoenicians and the West*, 3rd ed. 2001)** is the comprehensive synthesis. **Lipiński (*Itineraria Phoenicia*, 2004)** maps the trade-network from the eastern Mediterranean to the Atlantic.

**Authorship & dating**: Ezekiel ben Buzi, exilic priest. The chapter's literary unity is debated. **Greenberg** treats the chapter as essentially Ezekielian with a coherent compositional structure. **Zimmerli** identifies the poetic core (27:1–9a, 25b–36) and treats the prose trade-list (27:9b–25a) as secondary insertion — possibly Ezekielian itself, possibly slightly later. **Block** notes that the trade-list reads like a **commercial-administrative document** repurposed for poetic effect, and the precision of its geographic and economic detail is unusual for prophetic literature. The chapter is dated to the same 587/586 BC horizon as chapter 26, with the redactional layer possibly later within Ezekiel's own lifetime.

**Archaeological & textual evidence**: **The Phoenician trade-network archaeology** is exceptionally well-developed. Beyond the Uluburun and Cape Gelidonya wrecks, the **Tartessian sanctuaries** in southwestern Spain (Cancho Roano, El Carambolo, Cerro Salomón), the **Nora Stele** in Sardinia (the oldest Phoenician inscription in the western Mediterranean, c. 9th c. BC), the **Pyrgi tablets** in Etruria, the **Cádiz Melqart-temple complex**, and the **Carthage tophet** all anchor the western network. The **Phoenician inscriptions of Cyprus** (Kition, Idalion, Lapethos), the **Eshmun temple at Sidon**, and the **Tyre-area excavations** (Bikai 1978; Aubet 2001) anchor the eastern center. **The LXX of chapter 27** preserves a slightly different text in places — notably **27:32**, where the LXX has *who is like Tyre, brought to silence in the midst of the sea*, while the MT has *who is like Tyre, like one destroyed in the midst of the sea*. The variants are stylistic rather than substantive. The **Targum Jonathan** preserves the trade-list largely intact, identifying *Tarshish* as *the African coast* and *Javan* as *Greece* (anachronistic in Ezekiel's horizon but accurate in Targum's later Roman-era horizon).

**Key terms**: *qina* (קִינָה, "lament," 27:2, 32 — the chapter's genre marker); *kelilat-yofi* (כְּלִילַת יֹפִי, "perfect in beauty," 27:3, 4 — Tyre's self-claim that becomes the lament's central irony); *Tarshish* (תַּרְשִׁישׁ, 27:12, 25 — the western terminus of the trade network); *Senir* (שְׂנִיר, 27:5 — the Antilebanon-Hermon range, supplying ship-timber); *Elishah* (אֱלִישָׁה, 27:7 — the source of *blue and purple* dyes, probably Alashiya/Cyprus); *ruaḥ qadim* (רוּחַ קָדִים, "east wind," 27:26 — the wind that wrecks the Tyre-ship); *bele' yam* (בְּלֶב יַמִּים, "in the heart of the seas," 27:25, 26, 27 — the chapter's central spatial location).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: ***qina* (lament-song) with embedded trade-itinerary**, a sub-genre unique to this chapter. The *qina* meter (Hebrew 3:2 or 3:2 stress pattern) is identifiable in the poetic frame (27:1–9a, 25b–36); the embedded trade-list (27:9b–25a) is in elevated prose. The chapter's **central conceit** is the **personification of Tyre as a magnificent ship**, built of the finest materials from across the Mediterranean, manned by the most skilled mariners, laden with the wealth of the world's nations — and then *wrecked by the east wind in the heart of the seas*.

The literary architecture:

- A. Lament announcement and ship-construction (27:1–9)
  - A1. Tyre at the entrances of the sea (27:3)
  - A2. Materials: fir, cedar, oak, pine, linen, blue and purple (27:4–7)
  - A3. Crews: Sidon and Arvad rowers, Gebal caulkers (27:8–9)
- B. Mercenaries and trade-list (27:10–25a)
  - B1. Persia, Lud, Put as soldiers (27:10–11)
  - B2. Trade-itinerary: Tarshish, Javan, Tubal-Meshech, Beth-togarmah, Dedan, Aram, Israel-Judah, Damascus, Helbon, Uzal, Dedan, Arabia-Kedar, Sheba-Raamah, Haran-Canneh-Eden-Asshur-Chilmad (27:12–24)
  - B3. Tarshish ships fill the heart of the seas (27:25)
- C. The wreck (27:25b–36)
  - C1. East wind wrecks the ship (27:26)
  - C2. All cargo and crew sink (27:27)
  - C3. Pilots' cry, mariners' lament (27:28–32a)
  - C4. Lament-song embedded (27:32b–36)

The **ship-of-state metaphor** is the chapter's organizing image. Tyre is *both* a commercial empire *and* a literal vessel; the metaphor is so sustained that the distinction collapses. The east wind (*ruaḥ qadim*, 27:26) — the same scirocco that withers crops in Hosea 13:15 and breaks the ships of Tarshish in Psalm 48:7 — is the *agent of destruction*. The east wind is *Mesopotamian-direction*: the Babylonian armies come from the east, and the prophetic image of the east wind as instrument of divine judgment is consistent (cf. Ex 14:21, Jer 18:17, Hab 1:9). The metaphor and the geopolitics align: Babylon, the eastern power, is the east wind that wrecks Tyre.

The **trade-itinerary** (27:12–24) is the longest single trade-document in the Old Testament. It catalogues **eighteen** trading partners by name, **fifteen distinct goods or commodity-categories**, and the implied geography spans roughly **5,000 km east-west** (Spain to Persia) and **3,000 km north-south** (Cappadocia to Yemen). The list is not random — it follows a rough **counterclockwise spiral** starting in the far west (Tarshish), moving north (Javan, Tubal, Meshech, Beth-togarmah), then south through Arabia (Dedan, Sheba, Raamah), then east (Haran, Canneh, Eden, Asshur, Chilmad). The geographic logic mirrors the **trade-route logic**: the western Mediterranean networks first, then the Anatolian-Black Sea networks, then the Arabian-Indian Ocean networks, then the upper Mesopotamian land-routes.

The **leitwort** *bele' yam* (in the heart of the seas, 27:25, 26, 27, 32; cf. 28:2, 8) anchors the chapter spatially. Tyre's pride is being *in the heart of the seas* — and that location, which made her unassailable by land siege (the actual problem Nebuchadnezzar faced), becomes the location of her drowning. The *qina* trades on the irony.

**MT/LXX variants**: 27:6 — MT has *deck of pines from the coasts of Cyprus, inlaid with ivory*. The LXX reads *deck of pines from the coasts of Cyprus*, omitting the ivory-inlay phrase. **Greenberg (Anchor)** retains MT. 27:16 — MT has *Aram* (Syria); some Hebrew manuscripts and the LXX have *Edom*. The variant matters because *Edom* fits geographically with *emeralds and purple* trade routes (Red Sea), while *Aram* fits with the Damascene fine-textile trade. Greenberg, Block, and Zimmerli all retain MT *Aram*. 27:17 — MT has *wheat of Minnith, meal, honey, oil, and balm*. The LXX reads slightly differently with no major theological consequence.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Place in the canonical arc**: Chapter 27's *qina* completes the announcement-and-lament structure begun in chapter 26 (announcement) and feeds directly into chapter 28's two oracles to the prince and king of Tyre. The *ship-of-state* metaphor is taken up canonically in the New Testament's most extended echo: **Revelation 18:11–19** (the merchants of the earth weep over Babylon-the-great), which is a direct redeployment of Ezekiel 27's lament-genre, trade-list-content, and mariners-mourning structure. **Bauckham's *Climax of Prophecy*** (1993) demonstrates that Revelation 18 cannot be read except as a sustained allusion to Ezekiel 26–27.

**Jewish trajectory**: **Targum Jonathan** preserves the trade-list and identifies the geographic referents in 1st-millennium-AD terms — *Tarshish* as the African coast, *Javan* as Greece. **Rashi** treats the chapter philologically, identifying ship-construction terms and trade-goods with characteristic care. **Radak** elaborates on the geographic referents and is one of the earliest medieval commentators to recognize the **trade-list as an actual commercial document**, not merely poetic invention. **Maimonides** (*Guide* 3.50) refers to chapter 27 in connection with his discussion of *the natural philosophy of human commerce*, treating the chapter's trade-list as evidence that ancient Israel knew the wider commercial world. **Pesiqta de-Rab Kahana** preserves an aggadic tradition that the trade-goods of Tyre eventually flow to *Jerusalem in the messianic age* — the **redirection-of-trade** motif that runs through Isa 60:5–17.

**Christian trajectory**: Patristic — **Origen's *Homilies on Ezekiel*** allegorizes Tyre's ship as *the soul* and the trade-goods as *the soul's accumulated worldly attachments*; the wreck is the soul's eschatological accountability. **Jerome's Commentary on Ezekiel** treats the trade-list with characteristic learning, identifying the geographic referents from his own travels and from classical sources. **Gregory the Great's *Homilies on Ezekiel*** focuses on the moral allegory: Tyre's beauty and wealth are spiritual gifts misused. **Calvin's *Lectures on Ezekiel*** (1565) reads the chapter as a *prophetic critique of commercial luxury* and develops a substantive doctrine: *commerce is not in itself evil, but commerce divorced from the recognition of God's sovereignty is the seedbed of pride and idolatry*. Calvin's reading became the foundation for the **Reformed common-grace doctrine of commerce**: trade is morally legitimate, but its pretensions to autonomy are corrupting. Critical — **Cooke (ICC)**, **Eichrodt (OTL)**, **Zimmerli (Hermeneia)**. Contemporary — **Greenberg (Anchor)**, **Block (NICOT)**, **Allen (WBC)**, **Aubet** on Phoenician commerce, **Bauckham** on Revelation 18's appropriation, **Brueggemann** on the chapter's economic critique.

**Where they diverge**: the **patristic-allegorical** reading (Origen, Gregory) treats Tyre as the *symbol of the worldly soul*. The **Reformed common-grace** reading (Calvin, modern Reformed economic ethics — Wayne Grudem, Michael Novak in *The Spirit of Democratic Capitalism*) treats commerce as morally neutral but commerce-without-God as corrupting. **Catholic Social Teaching** (Leo XIII *Rerum Novarum*, John Paul II *Centesimus Annus*, Benedict XVI *Caritas in Veritate*) reads Ezekiel 27 as part of the prophetic tradition that grounds critique of *unjust* economic structures while affirming the dignity of commerce in service of the common good. A **prophetic-critical** reading (Brueggemann, and Block in his comments on 27:13) presses Tyre's lament as a critique of imperial-commercial exploitation — the trade-list's mention of *human beings* (27:13) among the commodities exchanged by Javan-Tubal-Meshech is the chapter's most damning detail, and this slave-trade reference is, on this view, the moral key to the whole. The **critical-historical** reading takes the chapter as documentary evidence for 6th-c. BC commerce.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Economic globalization and prophetic critique — does Ezekiel's *lament* over Tyre's destruction simultaneously *celebrate* the wealth Tyre concentrated?** The chapter's tonal complexity is its hardest theological feature. The *qina* is a *lament*: Tyre is mourned, the ship's beauty is praised, the trade-list reads with evident admiration. Yet the chapter is embedded in a judgment-cycle: Tyre is being destroyed *by YHWH*, the *east wind* of 27:26 is the divine instrument, and the chapter follows the announcement of chapter 26.

**Catholic Social Teaching** (especially Benedict XVI's *Caritas in Veritate*, 2009) reads economic activity as **morally evaluable in two distinct dimensions**: the *immediate* productive activity (which is morally good when oriented toward the common good) and the *systemic* configuration (which can be unjust even when individual transactions are blameless). **CST** treats Ezekiel 27 as a prophetic critique of *systemic* injustice — Tyre's concentration of wealth, slavery (27:13), and arrogance (27:3, *I am perfect in beauty*) — while affirming the *legitimate* dimensions of commerce. **Reformed common-grace** (Calvin, Kuyper's *Lectures on Calvinism*, modern Reformed economic ethics) reads the same: commerce is *good*, but pride-in-commerce is sin. A **prophetic-critical** reading (Brueggemann, and Block in his comments on the chapter) presses further: Tyre is the *paradigm* of the imperial-commercial city whose wealth is built on the periphery's extraction, and the **slave-trade reference** in 27:13 (*they exchanged human beings and vessels of bronze for your merchandise*) is the moral-theological key to the entire chapter — every reader who admires Tyre's beauty is admiring a system that traded in enslaved people.

The least intuitive position to a free-market reader is **the reading that the chapter's *lament* is itself ironic — that the admiration of Tyre's commercial achievement is the prophet's *trap*, drawing the reader into complicity before the foundation in slavery and arrogance comes into view**. The steelman: the canonical text mentions *human beings* (Hebrew *nephesh adam*, נֶפֶשׁ אָדָם, 27:13) as a *commodity* in the same parallel-construction as *vessels of bronze* — the Aegean slave-trade is an explicit element of the Tyrian commercial system, and the prophet does not separate it from the rest. If one is moved by the beauty of the ship in 27:5–9 but disturbed only in passing by 27:13, one has read the chapter the way Tyre read herself. The New Testament's own redeployment in Revelation 18:13 — which closes its trade-list with *slaves, that is, human souls* — confirms that the canon hears 27:13 as the sting in the catalogue.

Where this leaves us: Ezekiel 27 forces a confrontation with the *moral structure* of commercial achievement. The unresolved question: where is the line between admiring the *craftsmanship* of Tyrian shipbuilding (the legitimate dimension) and being seduced by the *system* it represents (the slavery, the imperial concentration of wealth, the *I am perfect in beauty* arrogance)? The chapter does not draw the line; it puts the question on the table.

**Tension 2: The *ship of state* metaphor — the *foundering* (27:26–27) as eschatological-political symbol.** The chapter's foundational metaphor — *Tyre as ship* — has had an extraordinary afterlife in political theology. The *ship of state* image runs from Plato's *Republic* (the ship's pilot, 488a–489d) through Cicero (*De Officiis* 2.21) and Horace (*Odes* 1.14, *O navis, referent in mare te novi fluctus*) into medieval and modern political thought. Ezekiel 27 is not the source of this tradition (Plato's *Republic* and the older Greek nautical-political imagery are independent) but it is its **most theologically saturated biblical instance**.

**Revelation 18** redeploys Ezekiel 27 explicitly: the merchants of the earth weep over fallen Babylon-the-great, the shipmasters and seafarers stand far off and cry out (Rev 18:17–19) in language directly drawing on Ezek 27:28–32. **Bauckham's *Climax of Prophecy*** (1993) traces the appropriation in detail: Revelation's *Babylon* is *Rome*, and the prophetic critique of the Roman commercial empire is built on the Tyrian template. **Augustine in *City of God*** uses Tyre and Carthage (the Tyrian colony) as the dual-paradigm of the *earthly city* whose wealth is impressive but whose foundation is self-exaltation — and Augustine's *two cities* dichotomy depends on this Ezekiel-derived imagery. **Modern political theology** (Carl Schmitt, *Political Theology*; Reinhold Niebuhr, *The Irony of American History*) has redeployed the *ship of state* and the *fall of Babylon* imagery for modern empire and for late-modern global capitalism.

The least intuitive position to a contemporary American Christian reader is **the political-theological tradition that reads Ezekiel 27 / Revelation 18 as a typological critique of the present commercial-imperial order**. The steelman: the New Testament's own appropriation of Ezekiel 27 in Revelation 18 is *not* allegorical-spiritual but *political-typological* — the Tyre of Ezekiel is the *type* of every commercial-imperial city, including Rome, including Babylon-the-great, including any successor in the same office. If the canonical NT itself reads Ezekiel 27 typologically-politically, then to read it only allegorically-spiritually is to read it less than the NT does.

Where this leaves us: Ezekiel 27 establishes the *ship of state / fall of the great commercial city* as a recurring theological-political type. The unresolved question: which contemporary political-economic configurations *are* the type's referent, and how does the church discern its time? The chapter does not answer; it puts the question on the table.

## The Thread

The trade-list of Ezekiel 27:12–24 is taken up almost item-for-item in **Revelation 18:11–13** (*the merchants of the earth weep ... cargoes of gold, silver, jewels, pearls, fine linen, purple, silk, scarlet, all kinds of citron wood, articles of every kind made of ivory, costly wood, bronze, iron, marble, cinnamon, spice, incense, myrrh, frankincense, wine, oil, fine flour, wheat, cattle, sheep, horses, chariots, slaves, that is, human souls*). The Greek phrase *sōmatōn kai psychas anthrōpōn* (*bodies and souls of human beings*, Rev 18:13) is a direct echo of Ezekiel 27:13's *nephesh adam*. The *east wind* that wrecks Tyre (27:26) is the same wind of judgment that scatters Israel in Hosea 13:15 and breaks the ships of Tarshish in Psalm 48:7 — and the canonical thread runs forward into Acts 27:14 (the *euraquilo*, the northeast wind, that wrecks Paul's ship en route to Rome). The *I am perfect in beauty* claim of 27:3 sets up the *I am a god* claim of 28:2 — the chapter's commercial pride preparing the next chapter's theological pride. **Lamentations 2:15** (*is this the city that was called the perfection of beauty?*) uses identical language — *kelilat-yofi* — for fallen Jerusalem, suggesting the *qina* genre of Ezekiel 27 deliberately echoes the *qina* of Lamentations. The Tyre-Tyrian-trade chapter and the Babylon-Babylonian-luxury chapter (Rev 18) thus form the canonical **bookends of the prophetic critique of the commercial-imperial city**, with Ezekiel 27 supplying the language and the trajectory and Revelation 18 closing the loop.
