# Judges 2: The Cycle Named

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Judges 2 splits cleanly into two scenes set in radically different theological registers, both anchored in concrete geography. The opening (vv. 1–5) takes place at Bochim — a place we cannot precisely locate but which the text deliberately names "weepers" (etiology in motion). The angel of YHWH "went up from Gilgal to Bochim." Gilgal is identifiable: a stone circle near Jericho where, according to Joshua 4–5, the twelve memorial stones were set up after the Jordan crossing. By the time of Judges 2, Gilgal functioned as a covenantal sanctuary — possibly the site some scholars (Hans-Joachim Kraus, Frank Cross) think hosted a regular covenant-renewal ceremony in the early tribal period. The angel's movement *from* Gilgal — the place of covenant memory — *to* Bochim — the place of covenant lament — is itself a theological geography.

The second scene (vv. 6–23) is the famous deuteronomistic theological summary, set against the backdrop of Joshua's death and burial at Timnath-heres in the hill country of Ephraim. (The same site is called Timnath-serah in Joshua 19:50 and 24:30 — the consonants are reversed, ḥeres vs. seraḥ. The Septuagint of Joshua 24:30 actually preserves the form Timnath-heres, suggesting a deliberate scribal change in MT Joshua away from a name that contained the word "sun" — *ḥeres*, an old west-Semitic sun-deity term — to avoid pagan resonance.) Joshua's death at 110 is the same age recorded for Joseph in Genesis 50:26, an Egyptian ideal lifespan attested in the Egyptian *Instruction of Ptahhotep* and other Middle Kingdom wisdom texts. Both Joseph and Joshua, the two great Israelite leaders most embedded in Egyptian context, share this number — likely deliberate.

Politically, the chapter sketches the period roughly between c. 1200 and 1050 BCE — Iron Age I — when Egyptian imperial control over Canaan, attested in detail by the Amarna Letters (c. 1350 BCE) and the campaigns of Merneptah (c. 1207 BCE) and Ramesses III, was collapsing. The Sea Peoples migrations (Philistines among them) were destabilizing the coastal Levant. Hittite hegemony in the north had ended with the bronze-age collapse around 1180 BCE. Into this vacuum, the highland Israelite settlements grew. The "surrounding enemies" of v. 14 — Moab, Ammon, Edom, Aramean polities, Philistia, Midianite raiders, and the local Canaanite city-states — are not a fictional pastiche but a documentable Iron Age I landscape. Each of these peoples is now archaeologically attested independent of the Bible: Moab through the Mesha Stele (c. 840 BCE) and the Iron I site of Khirbet Balua; Ammon through the Amman Citadel inscription; Edom through the recent excavations at Khirbat en-Nahas (Thomas Levy's work demonstrating substantial Iron Age I copper-mining infrastructure, against earlier minimalist assumptions); Philistia through the Pentapolis cities of Ashkelon, Ashdod, Ekron, Gath, and Gaza, with their distinctive bichrome pottery and pig-bone faunal record.

The "Baals and Ashtaroth" of vv. 11–13 are not generic pagan figures. Baal (*baʿal*, "lord") and Ashtoreth/Astarte are now richly known from the Ugaritic texts discovered at Ras Shamra in 1929. The Baal Cycle (KTU 1.1–1.6) presents Baal-Hadad as a young storm-god who battles Yam (Sea) and Mot (Death) for kingship. Astarte/Ashtoreth is his consort or sometimes Anat is. The polemical Hebrew vocalization *Ashtoreth* (with the vowels of *bošet*, "shame") was a deliberate Masoretic distortion. The agricultural rhythms of Canaanite religion — fertility, rain, the dying-and-rising god — were powerfully attractive to Israelite farmers whose livelihood depended on autumn rains. The deuteronomistic editor names this religious threat as the central problem.

The literary form of the chapter, especially vv. 11–19, is the deuteronomistic *cycle* itself: sin → divine anger → "sold into the hand" → cry/groaning → judge raised up → deliverance → death of judge → relapse. This cycle is the interpretive key to the entire book and one of the most studied theological frameworks in the Hebrew Bible. Yairah Amit (*The Book of Judges: The Art of Editing*, 1999) has worked out the redactional layering in painstaking detail: vv. 11–19 are a tightly composed deuteronomistic introduction; vv. 20–23 add a different theological note (testing) likely from a slightly later layer; vv. 1–5 (Bochim) and vv. 6–10 (Joshua summary) come from yet other source layers, possibly bridging older traditions.

A few Hebrew terms repay close attention. *Ḥemed* / *bōkīm* (חֶמֶד / בֹּכִים): Bochim, "weepers," from the verb *bākāh*, "to weep." Etiological place-naming is a deeply ancient narrative form across the ANE — Mari texts, Ugaritic narratives, and Akkadian lexical lists all do this. Second, *zānāh* (זָנָה), "to whore," used in v. 17: "they whored after other gods." This is the standard prophetic metaphor for covenant-breaking idolatry (Hosea will weaponize it), making the covenant relationship marital and idolatry adulterous. The English "whored" preserves the harshness; some translations soften it ("prostituted themselves," "played the harlot"), losing the ferocity. Third, *šōfeṭ* (שֹׁפֵט), "judge," is the Hebrew title that gives the book its name. It does *not* primarily mean a black-robed jurist. It means a "deliverer," "ruler," or "executor of justice" — a charismatic military-political leader empowered by YHWH for a specific crisis. Mari texts use a cognate (*šāpiṭum*) for tribal leaders. Carthage had *suffetes* (the Punic cognate) as chief magistrates. The judges are closer to Roman dictators or Greek tyrants in the technical sense — emergency office-holders. Fourth, *māḵar* (מָכַר), "to sell," in v. 14 ("he sold them into the hand of their enemies") is a striking covenant-breach metaphor: God transfers ownership of his own people, treating them as commodities forfeited by their breach. Fifth, *nāḥam* (נָחַם) in the niphal, "was moved to pity" in v. 18 — but the verb's range includes "relented," "changed his mind," "regretted." It is the same verb of divine pathos used in Genesis 6:6 and Exodus 32:14. The deuteronomistic God is not the unmoved mover of later philosophical theology.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre-wise, Judges 2:1–3:6 is the *deuteronomistic prologue proper* — the theological key by which the editor wants us to read everything that follows. Where ch. 1 was a non-deuteronomistic source (older, more realistic conquest summary) repurposed as evidence of failure, ch. 2 is the editor's own voice. Robert Polzin (*Moses and the Deuteronomist*, 1980) reads the chapter as the moment the implied narrator steps forward to *interpret* the events ch. 1 has just laid out. Daniel Block (NICOT) calls this the "preamble to the cycles." Barry Webb names it the "frame of the book."

The literary structure is a study in deliberate redactional sequencing. Yairah Amit identifies at least three distinct units glued together: the Bochim oracle (vv. 1–5) is an older source preserving a pre-monarchic tradition of divine-messenger judgment; the Joshua-summary (vv. 6–10) is a deuteronomistic transition that overlaps significantly with Joshua 24:28–31 (the same material reused, what scholars call a "resumptive" or *Wiederaufnahme* technique to bridge editorial seams); and the cycle-formula (vv. 11–23) is the editor's own theological key. The seams are visible. The chapter does not flow chronologically — vv. 6 retroactively resumes Joshua's life *after* the Bochim scene already implied his death — and the editor is comfortable letting the seams show. Marc Brettler argues this is deliberate: the editor wants the reader to experience the layered, multi-source nature of the tradition.

The cycle pattern itself — sin / anger / oppression / cry / judge / peace / death / relapse — is the most famous *literary device* in the book. Once you see it, you see it everywhere: Othniel (3:7–11) hits every beat in textbook compression; Ehud, Deborah, Gideon, Jephthah, Samson all variant on it; Samson breaks the pattern (he never delivers Israel during his lifetime, doesn't lead an army, and Israel doesn't cry out before he is raised up — Block notes the broken cycle as deliberate diagnostic). Robert Alter calls this kind of pattern-with-variation the engine of biblical narrative. The reader learns the rule and then watches the rule be broken; meaning emerges in the gap.

The chapter is also rich in *intertextual allusion*. Vv. 1–3 are an oracle of *covenantal lawsuit* that echoes Deuteronomy 7 almost line by line — "make no covenant with them," "break down their altars," "their gods shall be a snare to you." This is the deuteronomistic editor citing his own source-tradition. The "snare" (*môqēš*) imagery returns in Psalm 106:36 — "they served their idols, which became a snare to them" — a psalm that essentially summarizes Judges in a few stanzas, and which most critical scholars date to the post-exilic period as a meditation on Judges. The "thorns in your sides" of v. 3 is varied in Joshua 23:13 ("thorns in your eyes"). The deuteronomistic editor was a careful weaver.

Textually, ch. 2 is reasonably stable across MT, LXX A, and LXX B. The Dead Sea Scrolls fragment 4QJudg-a does not extend to ch. 2 (its main interest is the absence of the deuteronomistic insertion at 6:7–10, suggesting that some pre-deuteronomistic editions of the book existed without certain framework material). LXX-A and LXX-B diverge on minor lexical choices in the cycle vocabulary but do not shift the meaning.

Located in the book's argument, ch. 2 is the hermeneutical key the editor hands you before you read the rest. Everything that follows — Othniel's textbook deliverance, Ehud's bathroom assassination, Deborah's victory hymn, Gideon's fleece and ephod, Jephthah's vow, Samson's hair — is meant to be read against this framework. When the framework breaks (as it does increasingly in chs. 13–21), the breakage itself is the meaning.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, Judges 2 is the moment the Deuteronomistic History first explicitly names its theology. From this point through the end of 2 Kings, the same logic governs the narrative: covenant breach → divine judgment → cry → deliverance → relapse, scaled from individual judges to whole dynasties to the destruction of Jerusalem. The chapter is the seed from which the entire 6th-century theological account of Israel's history grew.

It also reaches back to Deuteronomy 28's covenant blessings and curses. The chapter assumes the reader knows Deuteronomy 28: the curses of military defeat, the "you shall be a horror to all the kingdoms of the earth," the "iron yoke." Judges 2 is Deuteronomy 28 cashed in. And it reaches forward to 1 Samuel 12, where Samuel summarizes the Judges era in language nearly identical to Judges 2 — same cycle, same vocabulary — before installing Saul.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Pseudo-Philo (*Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum* 30) expands the Bochim scene dramatically — adding speeches, dramatic details, naming the angel as Phinehas (a tradition also found in some midrashim that identifies the angel with the priest who acted zealously in Numbers 25). Josephus (*Ant.* 5.2) downplays the deuteronomistic framework and rationalizes the cycle as the natural result of factional infighting. The rabbinic tradition focuses heavily on the Bochim oracle. *Eruvin* 19a takes "What is this you have done?" as the divine rebuke that defines repentance theology. Rashi reads v. 10 — "another generation arose that did not know the LORD" — as the foundational text for *generational transmission*: religious knowledge is not inherited biologically; it must be taught, and a single failure of transmission is enough to lose it. This becomes a major theme in medieval Jewish education theory. Ralbag (Gersonides) and Radak both expand the testing-language of vv. 22–23 into a sophisticated theodicy: the Canaanites remained as a *pedagogical* device, an ongoing covenant test, not merely as punishment. Modern Jewish scholarship: Yair Zakovitch on the chapter's redactional layering; Jon Levenson on the cycle as a theological pattern that anticipates exile-and-return.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Origen's Homilies on Judges read the cycle allegorically as the soul's struggle: each "judge" is a virtue that delivers the soul from a vice (the Baals are different forms of spiritual disordering). This allegorical mode dominated for a millennium. Augustine in *City of God* 17 takes Judges 2 as a typological account of the Two Cities: Israel's repeated apostasy is a figure for the church's perpetual struggle within itself. Theodoret of Cyrus is more historical, reading the cycle as a real account but extracting moral lessons. The Glossa Ordinaria layered patristic readings onto the verse-by-verse text and made them standard for medieval preaching.

The Reformation broke the allegorical hegemony. Calvin's *Sermons on Judges* (1561) reads the chapter as devastating doctrine: vv. 10–19 prove the *total depravity* of human nature even after divine deliverance. Each generation must be evangelized; nothing can be assumed. Calvin draws a direct line from "another generation arose that did not know the LORD" to the urgency of Reformation preaching. Luther treats the chapter more sparingly but in the same register. Puritan preachers in 17th-century England and New England used Judges 2 constantly — Cotton Mather and Jonathan Edwards both have sermons on "the rising generation" that lean directly on this text.

The critical era reframed the chapter as the editorial keystone of the Deuteronomistic History. Wellhausen treated the cycle as a late ideological imposition. Martin Noth's 1943 thesis made the chapter the literal seam between the older source traditions (chs. 1, 3–16 in various forms) and the editor's interpretive overlay. Frank Cross's *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* (1973) refined this with the double-redaction model: the cycle as composed for Josiah's reform, then reframed in exile. Gerhard von Rad's *Old Testament Theology* read the cycle as one of the great theological achievements of ancient Israel — a way of making sense of repeated political catastrophe. Boling, Soggin, and Lawson Younger all treat the chapter as the editor's manifesto.

Contemporary readings have been generative. Walter Brueggemann reads the cycle as a deeply *honest* theological framework that refuses both naive optimism and despair — a "drama of fidelity and infidelity" that takes both seriously. Daniel Block's NICOT reads the chapter as the editor's gift to the reader: the framework is given upfront so we can read the stories with theological clarity. Barry Webb (NICOT) treats the cycle as a *spiral* rather than a circle — each iteration descends further. Block and Webb both observe how the cycle frame, by collapsing every actor into an undifferentiated "Israel," tends to obscure the particularity of the individual stories that follow — including the named women whose fates carry much of the book's argument. Robert Alter's literary work elsewhere on Judges presses a similar point: the framework is editorial scaffolding, and the stories repeatedly exceed it.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Both Jewish and Christian traditions take the cycle seriously as a theology of history. Both treat the Bochim oracle as a paradigmatic divine lament. They diverge on (a) the *typology*: Christians have used the cycle to allegorize the soul or the church, frequently flattening the historical specificity; Jews have generally kept the cycle historical and ethical. (b) The "another generation that did not know the LORD" verse: a foundational text for Jewish religious education; for Reformed Christians, a foundational text for the necessity of conversion in each generation. (c) Universalism: Jewish readings tend to focus on Israel's specific covenant; Christian readings have generalized the cycle into a universal anthropology of fallen humanity, sometimes losing the chapter's covenantal specificity.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the deuteronomistic author believe God was doing? He believed YHWH was the genuine sovereign of history, that covenant was a real juridical-relational bond with material consequences, and that Israel's recurrent infidelity required a theological account that did not collapse into either fatalism or arbitrariness. The cycle of sin-judgment-cry-deliverance-relapse was his solution: God is just (judgment follows breach), God is merciful (deliverance follows cry), and human freedom is real (the cycle keeps turning because Israel keeps choosing). The "testing" language of vv. 22–23 adds another layer: even the Canaanite remnant is part of YHWH's pedagogical economy. None of this is incidental color; it is the editor's load-bearing theology.

But two genuine theological tensions deserve attention.

**Tension One: Is the Cycle a Theology of Hope or of Despair?**

The cycle keeps turning. Each iteration ends with relapse "more corrupt than their fathers" (v. 19). Is this a hopeful framework — God always raises a deliverer — or a despairing one — Israel never actually changes?

*Catholic / sacramental position*: The cycle is hopeful because it shows God's perpetual willingness to enter again into deliverance. Each judge is a type of Christ; the rhythm anticipates the Eucharistic re-presentation of redemption. The downward spiral is real but bounded by divine fidelity.

*Reformed / Calvinist position*: The cycle is honest about depravity. Israel does not get better. The cycle reveals that human beings, even the elect, will repeatedly choose other gods. Hope is located not in human moral improvement but in God's unilateral covenantal commitment, which culminates not in another judge but in a different kind of king — David's son. Calvin takes vv. 10–19 as proof that all moral optimism about religious community is naive.

*Eastern Orthodox position*: The cycle reveals the *theosis* trajectory in negative — Israel is being shaped through suffering and deliverance toward eventual transformation. The cycle is purgative, not despairing.

*Jewish position*: The cycle is realistic and pedagogical. Rabbinic tradition has typically resisted reading it as either total depravity (which Jewish anthropology rejects) or naive optimism. Generation matters; teaching matters; choice matters. Maimonides treats the cycle as evidence of free will: Israel could have broken the pattern.

*Critical / Brueggemann position*: The cycle is the deuteronomistic editor's profound theological honesty about the political experience of a small nation between empires. It refuses both the imperial myth ("we always win") and the despairing myth ("history is meaningless"). It locates meaning in the relentless return of divine fidelity.

*Steelmanning the position I find least intuitive*: I find the Calvinist "no real moral improvement" reading the most uncomfortable — it seems to evacuate human agency. But take it seriously. The text genuinely says vv. 19, "they were more corrupt than their fathers." Each cycle descends. If you read the book straight, the trajectory is downward, not improving. Calvin's reading is the *plain sense*. To soften it is to soften the text.

**Where this leaves us:** The cycle is more honestly read as both hopeful and despairing simultaneously — God always returns; Israel never permanently learns. Christian and Jewish traditions have stretched the dialectic in different directions, but the text resists collapse to either pole.

**Tension Two: Why Did God Leave the Canaanites — Punishment or Pedagogy?**

The chapter gives two *different* explanations for why the Canaanites remained. V. 3 (the Bochim oracle): "I will not drive them out *before you*" — punishment for breaking covenant. Vv. 22–23: "in order to test Israel" — pedagogy. These are not the same theology.

*Traditional rabbinic position* (Rashi, Radak): The two are not contradictory but sequential. Vv. 1–5 give the *immediate* reason (punishment); vv. 22–23 give the *long-term* divine purpose (testing). Both are true.

*Boling / critical position*: The two represent different redactional layers with different theologies. The Bochim oracle is older and more retributive; the testing language is later, possibly even post-deuteronomistic, attempting to soften the harsh judgment with a more pedagogical frame.

*Block / evangelical position*: There is no contradiction because punishment and pedagogy are aspects of the same divine action. God does both at once. The Canaanites are both consequence and curriculum.

*Process-theological position*: Neither punishment nor pedagogy is centrally about divine intention; both are reframings by Israel of the *fact* of Canaanite presence. The text is a community's attempt to make theological sense of an inherited reality (we share the land) by retroactively projecting divine purpose.

*Steelmanning the position I find least intuitive*: I find the process-theological reading the hardest to inhabit — it dissolves divine agency in a way the text resists. But take it seriously: human beings do, in fact, retroactively narrate their circumstances theologically. The Canaanites *were* still in the land. The deuteronomistic editor *did* face the question why. Multiple answers were available; the text preserves more than one. That preservation is itself evidence that early Israel was working out its theology in real time, not handing down a pre-completed system.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter holds two explanations in tension because the editor inherited multiple traditions and refused to flatten them. The reader who insists on a single answer is doing what the text declines to do.

**One unresolved question:** If God *raises up* judges and is *with* them (v. 18), why do those very judges fail to halt the downward spiral — and what does that imply about the limits of charismatic deliverance, and about the trajectory toward kingship and eventually Messiah?

## The Thread

**Deuteronomy 7:1–6.** The covenant injunction not to make covenants with Canaanites or to leave their altars standing. Judges 2:1–3 is the divine prosecutor reading the indictment from this very text. Read Deuteronomy 7 first, then return to Bochim — the weeping makes more sense.

**1 Samuel 12.** Samuel's farewell speech reuses the cycle of Judges 2 almost verbatim — sin, oppression, cry, deliverance, relapse — to argue that monarchy itself is part of the next iteration. The cycle does not end with the institution of kings; it absorbs them. When you reach 1 Samuel 12, recognize the deuteronomistic hand still at work.

**2 Kings 17:7–23.** The deuteronomistic editor's account of the fall of Samaria is essentially Judges 2 rewritten at scale. Same vocabulary (whoring after other gods, abandoning the LORD), same logic (covenant breach → handed over → exile). Judges 2 is the small drama; 2 Kings 17 is the same drama as the destruction of a kingdom. The cycle was always pointed here.

**Hebrews 11:32.** The New Testament's only positive collective treatment of the judges names Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, David, Samuel — placing them in the catalogue of faith. Hebrews refuses the deuteronomistic frame's bleakness and reframes the judges as exemplars. Whether this is faithful to the text or a deliberate reinterpretation is a question worth holding.
