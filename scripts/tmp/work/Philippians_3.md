# Philippians 3: Rubbish, Résumé, and the Reach Forward

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Philippi sits on the Via Egnatia in eastern Macedonia, the great Roman trunk road linking the Adriatic to the Aegean. After Octavian and Antony's defeat of Brutus and Cassius at the Battle of Philippi in 42 BC, the city was refounded as *Colonia Iulia Augusta Philippensis*, settled with Roman veterans whose loyalty to the Caesarian house was rewarded with land grants and the *ius Italicum*. Latin epigraphy dominates the surviving inscriptions; the imperial cult was prominent; and the *cursus honorum* — the ladder of magistracies on which Roman elite lives were oriented — produced an honor-saturated public culture. Joseph Hellerman's *Reconstructing Honor in Roman Philippi* shows how the colony's veterans and their descendants prized status acquired through ancestry, military service, and civic office, with public benefaction inscribed on stone for posterity. Peter Oakes's demographic reconstruction in *Philippians: From People to Letter* models the urban population the letter would actually have reached: a small Roman elite, a larger artisan-tradesman stratum, slaves and freedmen.

Where Paul writes from, no one knows for certain. The traditional view, defended by Peter O'Brien (NIGTC) and Markus Bockmuehl (BNTC) and given an older defense by F.F. Bruce, places the letter in Rome around 60–62 AD, drawing on the references to the *praitōrion* (1:13) and "those of Caesar's household" (4:22). Gerald Hawthorne (WBC) argues for Caesarea. A wide stream of critical scholarship — G.S. Duncan a century ago, more recently Bonnie Thurston (Sacra Pagina) — favors Ephesus in the mid-50s, on the grounds that the multiple journeys assumed in 2:25–30 and 4:18 between Paul's prison and Philippi fit Ephesus far better than Rome's vast distance. Each provenance reshapes the chapter: a Roman setting makes 3:20's *politeuma* polemic ricochet directly off the imperial city; an Ephesian setting embeds the autobiographical résumé inside Paul's bruising controversy with rival missionaries on the Asian-Macedonian circuit.

For chapter 3 in particular, the relevant background is Paul himself. The autobiographical résumé presupposes Pharisaic Judaism in the Diaspora and Jerusalem schools — circumcision on the eighth day, Hebrew-of-Hebrews lineage, training in Torah-observance, and the zealous persecution of the early Jesus movement. Pharisaic schools transmitted halakhic interpretation; Diaspora synagogue life embedded Greek-speaking Jews in the wider Hellenistic urban world. Behind 3:5's "tribe of Benjamin" stands a tribal genealogy that, in a Roman colony obsessed with ancestry, would have read as the Jewish counterpart to the elite Roman *imagines maiorum*. The polemic of 3:2 also points to Jewish or Jewish-Christian circumcision-advocates active somewhere in the early Pauline mission field, whether or not they were physically present at Philippi when Paul wrote.

The textual evidence for Philippians is solid. P46 (c. 200), Sinaiticus א, Vaticanus B, and Alexandrinus A all preserve the chapter; P16 and P61 add fragmentary witnesses. Excavations at Philippi — the forum, the basilicas, the inscriptions catalogued by Pilhofer — anchor the social setting. The Greek key terms drive the chapter: κατατομή *katatomē* (the polemical "mutilation," 3:2), περιτομή *peritomē* (the true "circumcision," 3:3), ἄμεμπτος *amemptos* ("blameless," 3:6), σκύβαλα *skybala* ("rubbish/dung," 3:8), and πολίτευμα *politeuma* ("citizenship/commonwealth," 3:20).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Philippians is best read as a friendship letter (*philophronesis*) infused with thanksgiving and paraenesis — a generic blend that Loveday Alexander, Stanley Stowers, and Abraham Malherbe each chart in different ways. Chapter 3 sits inside the broader argument as a sharp pivot. After two chapters of partnership-language and the Christ-hymn, 3:1's "finally, my brothers, rejoice in the Lord" looks like a closing — and then, abruptly, 3:2 unloads: "look out for the dogs, look out for the evildoers, look out for those who mutilate the flesh."

This 3:1→3:2 fracture is the single strongest data point for the multi-letter theory. Walter Schmithals and B.D. Rahtjen, with F.W. Beare's commentary, argue that Philippians as we have it stitches together two or three originally separate notes — a thanksgiving for the gift, a warning letter, and possibly a final commendation — assembled by an early editor. Defenders of compositional unity (Bockmuehl, O'Brien, John Reumann's Anchor Yale volume, Stephen Fowl, David Garland) respond that ancient letter-writing tolerated emotional swerves, that the *koinōnia* leitmotif holds the whole together, and that the polemic of chapter 3 has been quietly anticipated by 1:15–18 and 1:28. The reader does not have to settle the debate, but the seam is real, and 3:2 is where it shows.

Within the chapter the dominant device is the autobiographical résumé. Paul builds a Pharisaic CV in 3:5–6 — birth, lineage, party, zeal, observance — only to overturn it with the bookkeeping metaphor of "gain" and "loss" in 3:7–8. The polemical pun *katatomē*/*peritomē* in 3:2–3 is rhetorical jiu-jitsu: those who push circumcision are not the *circumcised* but the *mutilated*. The athletic metaphor of 3:13–14 (forgetting what lies behind, straining forward, pressing on toward the goal, the prize of the upward call) imports the gymnasium and the foot-race. And 3:20's *politeuma* metaphor weaponizes the most loaded word in a Roman colony: a heavenly *commonwealth* that relativizes earthly citizenship without denying it. The chapter functions inside the book as the negative pole to chapter 2's positive Christ-hymn: where Christ "did not count" equality with God a thing to be grasped (2:6), Paul "counts" his Pharisaic gains as loss (3:7–8). The verbal echo binds Christ's self-emptying to the apostle's own.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical NT arc, Philippians 3 is one of the densest concentrations of Pauline self-disclosure outside Galatians 1–2 and 2 Corinthians 11–12. It supplies Pauline biographers with hard data and Pauline theologians with the locus classicus for the relationship between Torah-observance, Christ-faith, and righteousness.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** John Chrysostom's homilies on Philippians, delivered at Antioch in the 390s, treat 3:8's *skybala* as evidence of Paul's wholehearted reorientation toward Christ. Theodore of Mopsuestia, in the Antiochene tradition, gives a more sober historical-grammatical reading. Augustine's reading of Phil 3 is decisive for Western Christianity: in the *Confessions* and especially in the anti-Pelagian writings, 3:6's *amemptos* is read against the grain — as Paul's pre-conversion *self-perception*, exposed as illusion by encounter with Christ — and 3:9's "righteousness from God" becomes a key text for the gratuitous, unmerited character of grace. The Augustinian reading installs a tortured-conscience Paul at the heart of Western anthropology.

The medieval *imitatio Christi* tradition seizes on 3:10's *koinōnia pathēmatōn autou*, "the fellowship of his sufferings." Bernard of Clairvaux's mystical Christology, the affective devotion of the Franciscans, Thomas à Kempis's *De Imitatione Christi* (with explicit Philippians 3 echoes), and the late-medieval *Devotio Moderna* all read 3:10 as prescribing conformity to the crucified Christ. Aquinas's commentary on Philippians integrates the chapter into the *Summa*'s account of grace and merit.

The Reformation pivots on 3:8–9. Luther's discovery of *iustitia Dei* as the righteousness God *gives* rather than the righteousness God *demands* — narrated in his 1545 preface to the Latin works — leans heavily on Philippians 3 alongside Romans 1:17. The very word *skybala* fueled Luther's anti-monastic polemic against works-righteousness; Calvin's commentary follows the same trajectory but with characteristic precision about union with Christ. Anabaptist *Nachfolge* ("following-after") readings of 3:10's "share his sufferings" pulled the verse toward discipleship, suffering, and martyrdom rather than forensic righteousness.

The critical turn begins with F.C. Baur's Tübingen school in the nineteenth century, which read Pauline texts as polemical interventions in a Petrine-Pauline conflict — for Phil 3 that meant the "dogs" and "mutilators" were Judaizing rivals. The decisive twentieth-century rupture is Krister Stendahl's 1963 lecture, "The Apostle Paul and the Introspective Conscience of the West" (reprinted in *Paul Among Jews and Gentiles*, 1976). Stendahl argued that 3:6's *amemptos* — Paul calling himself "blameless" under Torah — proves Paul did *not* have a tortured Augustinian-Lutheran conscience prior to his Damascus encounter. Paul experienced not "conversion from guilt to forgiveness" but "calling from one mission to another." Stendahl's lecture is the seed of the New Perspective on Paul.

E.P. Sanders's *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977) extended Stendahl by arguing that Second Temple Judaism was structured by "covenantal nomism" — entry by grace, maintenance by obedience — and was not the legalistic works-religion the Lutheran tradition had imagined. James Dunn's 1983 Manson Memorial Lecture, "The New Perspective on Paul," coined the label and argued that Paul's *erga nomou* ("works of the law") referred specifically to the Jew/Gentile boundary markers — circumcision, food laws, Sabbath — rather than to all works of obedience. N.T. Wright (*What Saint Paul Really Said*, *Paul and the Faithfulness of God*, *Paul for Everyone: The Prison Letters*) reads Phil 3 as Paul re-narrating his own life around the Messiah's covenantal climax: "the righteousness from God" in 3:9 is not a moral quality infused into Paul but the verdict of covenant membership pronounced over those who belong to the Messiah, set against "a righteousness of my own" which for Wright means the badge of ethnic Israel rather than a Pelagian ladder of merit. Counter-voices — Stephen Westerholm's *Perspectives Old and New on Paul*, the two-volume *Justification and Variegated Nomism* edited by D.A. Carson, Peter O'Brien, and Mark Seifrid — defended the older Lutheran-Reformed reading and pressed the diversity of Second Temple Judaisms against Sanders's covenantal-nomist synthesis.

A third stream, the Apocalyptic Paul school — J. Louis Martyn's *Galatians* commentary, Beverly Gaventa's essays, Douglas Campbell's *The Deliverance of God* — reads Phil 3 as neither old-perspective forensic nor new-perspective covenantal but as the apocalyptic invasion of God's deliverance into a cosmos enslaved to powers. A fourth, Paul-within-Judaism, takes the radical step of saying Paul never *left* Judaism at all (see below).

In contemporary commentary the chapter is parsed by Bockmuehl, O'Brien, Hawthorne, Reumann, Gordon Fee (NICNT), Moisés Silva (BECNT), and Stephen Fowl. The 3:9 *pistis Christou* phrase is itself a fault line: Richard Hays's *The Faith of Jesus Christ* (1983/2002) argues for a subjective genitive (Christ's own faithfulness as ground of righteousness), with Wright in agreement; R. Barry Matlock and others defend the traditional objective genitive (our faith in Christ).

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** Stendahl was a Lutheran but the conversation he opened drew Jewish scholars into the Pauline questions in unprecedented ways. Sanders's covenantal nomism rehabilitated rabbinic Judaism for Christian readers. Daniel Boyarin's *A Radical Jew: Paul and the Politics of Identity* (1994) reads Paul as a Jewish critic of Jewish particularism on universalist grounds and, in *Border Lines* (2004), tracks the slow mutual self-construction of "Judaism" and "Christianity" as separate religions long after Paul. Mark Nanos (*The Mystery of Romans*, *The Irony of Galatians*) and Pamela Eisenbaum (*Paul Was Not a Christian*, 2009) push further: Paul, on this reading, never abandoned Judaism, his mission was to Gentiles only, and Phil 3 does not repudiate Jewish identity but re-prioritizes it within the new messianic situation. Paula Fredriksen's *Paul: The Pagans' Apostle* (2017) places Paul firmly within first-century Jewish apocalyptic. Where Jewish scholarship still pushes back on Christian readings of Phil 3 is on supersessionism: a chapter that treats Pharisaic Torah-observance as *skybala* has been weaponized for two millennia against Jews as such, and the Paul-within-Judaism school is in part an attempt to return the chapter to its first-century pluralism without reducing it.

**Where this leaves us:** Phil 3:4–11 has carried more theological weight than perhaps any other autobiographical passage in the canon. The dialogue between Stendahl's *amemptos* Paul, Sanders's covenantal-nomist Judaism, and traditional readings has not been resolved but has become genuinely conversational; the supersessionist temptation remains live, and how one reads "rubbish" matters far beyond exegesis. One more thread belongs in Lens 3: 3:20's *politeuma en ouranois*. N.T. Wright (*Surprised by Hope*, *Paul for Everyone: The Prison Letters*) presses against the popular reading that "our citizenship is in heaven" means Christians are headed *to* heaven. Philippi was a Roman colony — a outpost of Rome in Macedonia, and its colonists' job was not to sail back to Italy but to bring Roman order to where they were planted. So heaven's colonists, Wright argues, await a Saviour who will come *from* heaven (3:20b) to transform the body of our humiliation and, by extension, the world — the colony of heaven is the bridgehead of new creation on earth, not an evacuation plan.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — Justification and the perspectives on Paul.** Reading 3:4–11 forces a choice. The traditional Lutheran-Reformed reading hears Paul confessing the futility of his pre-Christian Torah-observance: legal righteousness was a dead end, and Christ-righteousness is forensic acquittal received by faith. The Catholic tradition, while affirming grace, integrates 3:9's "righteousness through faith in Christ" with the wider scriptural witness to faith working through love (Trent, *Decretum de Iustificatione*; CCC §§1987–2029); the Council of Trent insisted that justification is genuinely transformative as well as imputative. The New Perspective (Sanders, Dunn, Wright) hears Paul rejecting not works as such but the boundary markers that walled the covenant off from Gentiles; Phil 3:6's *amemptos* shows that Paul's pre-Christian conscience was unbroken, and the issue is Christological reorganization, not psychological collapse. The Apocalyptic Paul school (Martyn, Gaventa, Campbell) hears something different again: an apocalyptic disclosure that has invaded and disqualified the entire prior framework of human evaluation. The Paul-within-Judaism radicals (Nanos, Eisenbaum, Fredriksen) hear Paul saying that Jewish identity-markers, valuable in themselves, are now relativized only because the messianic age has arrived for Gentiles too — and Paul never gave up being a Pharisee.

The least-intuitive position to most Christian readers is the Paul-within-Judaism radical reading, so steelmanning is in order. The argument: Paul addresses Gentiles ("you" in 3:3 likely Gentile believers being pressured toward circumcision); the *katatomē*/*peritomē* polemic is targeted at Gentile circumcision, not Jewish identity per se; Paul calls his Pharisaic credentials "loss" *for the sake of Christ* and *in comparison to* knowing Christ — comparative, not absolute repudiation; nowhere in Phil 3 does Paul tell Jews to stop being Jews, only Gentiles to stop adopting Jewish identity-markers as conditions of salvation. *Skybala* is a comparative judgment within an eschatological reordering, not a metaphysical one. The strength of this reading is its first-century plausibility and its refusal to sanction the supersessionism that has scarred Jewish-Christian history. Its weakness is that "righteousness of my own that comes from the law" in 3:9 sounds, to most readers, like a deeper repudiation than mere comparative reordering.

Where this leaves us: Phil 3 does not require the Augustinian-Lutheran "introspective conscience," but it does claim that Christ-righteousness is now the determinative category. Whether that displaces, fulfills, or relativizes Torah-observance is the live question, and the answer one gives reshapes the whole of Christian theology.

**Tension 2 — Knowing Christ in suffering.** Phil 3:10 — "to know him and the power of his resurrection and may share his sufferings, becoming like him in his death" — is the New Testament's most concentrated articulation of participatory soteriology. The Catholic mystical-suffering tradition reads it through Bernard of Clairvaux, John of the Cross's dark night, Teresa of Ávila's interior castle, Thérèse of Lisieux's "little way," Mother Teresa's letters, and John Paul II's *Salvifici Doloris* (1984), which articulates redemptive suffering as a sharing in Christ's salvific work. Reformed union-with-Christ theology — Calvin's *duplex gratia* in *Institutes* III.1–2, Sinclair Ferguson, J. Todd Billings's *Union with Christ* — reads 3:10 as the participatory dimension of the same justifying union, holding suffering and resurrection together without making suffering itself meritorious. Lutheran *theologia crucis* — Luther's Heidelberg Disputation (1518), Gerhard Forde — sees 3:10 as exposing the inversion of all theologies of glory: God is found *sub contrario*, hidden in suffering. Eastern Orthodox *theosis* (Vladimir Lossky, Kallistos Ware, Maximus the Confessor) reads 3:10 as the believer's participatory ascent through Christ's passion into divine life.

A caution cuts across all four streams, and the Lutheran-Reformed wing voices it most sharply against the others. Gerhard Forde and the *theologia crucis* tradition warn that "redemptive suffering" rhetoric can slide from Paul's *koinōnia tōn pathēmatōn* — fellowship in the Messiah's sufferings, suffering taken on for the gospel's sake — into a generalized sacralization of pain as such, which is precisely the "theology of glory" inverted. The grammar of 3:10 is the constraint: Paul writes of *sharing* (κοινωνία) Christ's sufferings as one already united to him, not of pain as a meritorious work or a thing to be sought for its own sake. Calvin's reading in the *Institutes* (III.8, "On Bearing the Cross") makes the same move from the other direction — the cross the believer bears is conformity to Christ within union, not a transaction that adds to Christ's finished work.

Where this leaves us: 3:10 will not let the Christian off the hook of participation, but the participation is the cruciform fellowship Paul names — suffering borne *with* and *for* the crucified Messiah — not suffering in the abstract dignified by theological description.

**The unresolved question:** If Paul's pre-Christian conscience was *amemptos* and his Torah-observance was no failure, then what exactly did he *lose* on the Damascus road that he is now calling *skybala* — and is the loss universalizable to every reader of the chapter, or particular to Paul's own apostolic vocation?

## The Thread

- **Galatians 6:14–15** ("far be it from me to boast except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ … neither circumcision counts for anything, nor uncircumcision") — the Galatian controversy in compressed form, with the same circumcision/no-circumcision polarity as Phil 3:2–3. The resemblance is one reason scholars debate whether the same opponents stand behind both letters.
- **Romans 7:7–25** — the *other* Pauline autobiographical passage. The Lutheran-Reformed tradition reads Romans 7's wretched "I" as autobiographical and combines it with Phil 3 to produce the tortured-conscience Paul. Stendahl read Romans 7 as a rhetorical *prosopopoeia* and combined it with Phil 3:6's *amemptos* to argue the opposite. The two passages must be read together.
- **2 Corinthians 4:7–12** ("always carrying in the body the death of Jesus, so that the life of Jesus may also be manifested in our bodies") — the closest parallel to 3:10's *koinōnia pathēmatōn autou*, written probably in the same Macedonian-Asian period as Philippians on the Ephesian hypothesis.
- **Hebrews 11:13–16** ("they were strangers and exiles on the earth … they desire a better country, that is, a heavenly one") — the canonical resonance of 3:20's *politeuma en ouranois* in a non-Pauline voice, written later in the canon and giving the citizenship metaphor an Abrahamic-pilgrim cast that complements Paul's Roman-colonial framing.
