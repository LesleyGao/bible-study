# Ephesians 6: Slaves, Sons, and the Armor of the Divine Warrior

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The political horizon of Ephesians 6, like the rest of the letter, depends on dating. If Pauline (c. 60-62, the standard conservative position of Hoehner, O'Brien, Bruce, Thielman, Markus Barth, and Arnold), the letter is composed during a Roman imprisonment under Nero, before the persecutions of 64; the "ambassador in chains" of 6:20 fits this. If post-Pauline (the position of Mitton, Lincoln, Schnackenburg, MacDonald, Pokorný, Käsemann), the date moves to c. 70-90, after the Jewish War, with the imperial cult in Asia tightening under the Flavians and Domitian. Either way, the chapter's two distinct halves — the slave/master code (6:5-9) and the armor peroratio (6:10-20) — sit inside a Roman provincial world saturated with the symbols of imperial military power.

The slave economy of Roman Asia is indispensable context for 6:5-9. Estimates from Walter Scheidel and Keith Hopkins put the slave population of the Empire at 10-15 million; in urban centers like Ephesus, slaves were domestic workers, dock laborers, skilled craftsmen, accountants, and prostitutes. Roman law treated slaves as property — *res*, not *personae* — though manumission was widespread enough by the first century that a substantial freedman class existed. The slave's body was the master's: sexual access, corporal punishment, and *ius vitae necisque* were legally available. The classical Greek *Haustafel* tradition going back to Aristotle's *Politics* I.4-7 argued the slave was a "living tool," and even sympathetic Stoic voices like Seneca (*Epistle* 47) did not question the institution.

The armor peroratio (6:10-20) draws on a different material world: the equipment of the Roman legionary. Archaeology and literary sources (Polybius, Josephus, Vegetius, Trajan's Column reliefs) give a clear picture of the *miles*. The *cingulum militare* held the *gladius* and *pugio*; the *thorax/lorica* could be *segmentata* (plate), *hamata* (mail), or *squamata* (scale); the *galea* was the helmet; the *scutum* the large shield, often soaked to extinguish enemy fire-arrows (the *malleoli*); the *caligae* were hobnailed military sandals. Thomas Neufeld's *Put on the Armour of God* (1997) traces the divine-warrior intertexts in detail.

The third local context is the magical-supernatural world of Ephesus. Clinton Arnold's *Ephesians: Power and Magic* (1989) documents that Ephesus was perhaps the magical capital of the eastern Mediterranean; the *Ephesia grammata* (six apotropaic syllables) were widely deployed, and the Greek Magical Papyri preserve countless invocations against *daimones*, *archai*, and *exousiai* — the vocabulary Eph 6:12 deploys. Acts 19:19 reports the burning of magical books worth fifty thousand silver pieces after Pauline preaching. To readers in this milieu, Eph 6:10-20 does not introduce powers theology — it reframes it.

The authorship debate matters here too. The chapter's powers theology is adduced by both sides: critics like Käsemann and Schnackenburg note its developed cosmic dualism as evidence of post-Pauline reflection; defenders like Hoehner argue Pauline thought already shows this trajectory in 1 Cor 15:24-26 and Col 1:16. The textual destination remains unstable: P46, Sinaiticus א*, and Vaticanus B* lack "in Ephesus" at 1:1. The principal Greek terms for chapter 6 are: ὑπακούετε (*hypakouete*, "obey," 6:1, 5); δοῦλοι (*douloi*, "slaves/bondservants," 6:5); πανοπλία (*panoplia*, "full armor," 6:11, 13 — Greek term covering the entire suit of armor); ἀρχάς, ἐξουσίας, κοσμοκράτορας (*archas, exousias, kosmokratoras*, 6:12); παλή (*palē*, "wrestling," 6:12, the only NT use of this term, evoking athletic-not-military combat); ῥῆμα θεοῦ (*rhēma theou*, "word/utterance of God," 6:17, distinct from *logos*).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Ephesians 6 contains the conclusion of the household code (6:1-9) and the letter's peroratio (6:10-20), followed by personal closing (6:21-24). The Haustafel completes the Aristotelian triad: husband-wife (5:22-33), parent-child (6:1-4), master-slave (6:5-9). Each address-pair follows a recognizable rhetorical pattern: subordinate addressed first, exhortation grounded christologically, dominant addressed second, exhortation also grounded christologically. The chapter's striking innovation, noted especially by David Balch (*Let Wives Be Submissive*, 1981) and James Dunn, is the address to slaves *as moral agents* — in classical and Stoic Haustafeln, slaves are not addressed because they are property, not persons; here they are addressed first and given more verses than masters. Verse 9 is theologically explosive within the form: masters and slaves share a single *kyrios* in heaven, "and there is no partiality with him." The phrase ἀπροσωπολημψία (*aprosōpolēmpsia*, "no respect of persons / impartiality") is used in the LXX of God's judgment (Deut 10:17, 2 Chr 19:7) and is here transferred to the master-slave relationship, embedding an eschatological leveling within an apparent accommodation.

The peroratio of 6:10-20 is one of the most rhetorically charged passages in the Pauline corpus. The genre is military-rhetorical exhortation, with closest parallels in (1) Greco-Roman generals' battle speeches preserved in Thucydides, Livy, and Josephus, (2) Hellenistic-Jewish apocalyptic warfare imagery (1QM, the Qumran War Scroll), and (3) the divine-warrior tradition of the Hebrew Bible — most importantly Isaiah 11:5 ("righteousness shall be the belt of his waist, and faithfulness the belt of his loins"), Isaiah 59:17 ("he put on righteousness as a breastplate and a helmet of salvation on his head"), and Wisdom of Solomon 5:17-20, which expands these images into a fully kitted-out divine warrior. The astonishing rhetorical move of Eph 6:10-20 is the *transfer*: Yahweh's own armor in Isaiah 59 is now placed on the church. Tremper Longman III (*God Is a Warrior*, 1995) and Susan Niditch (*War in the Hebrew Bible*, 1993) trace the holy-war background; Thomas Neufeld puts the transfer at the center of the passage's logic.

The list of armor pieces is structured chiastically and pneumatologically: belt (truth) — breastplate (righteousness) — feet (gospel of peace) — shield (faith) — helmet (salvation) — sword (Spirit / word of God) — prayer. The rhythm builds from defensive (belt, breastplate, footwear) through receptive (shield, helmet) to active (sword, prayer). The sword is the only offensive weapon, and it is identified as *τὸ ῥῆμα θεοῦ* (*to rhēma theou*) — the *spoken* word of God, the utterance, distinct from *logos*. That this is the only offensive equipment is theologically weighted in Anabaptist and pacifist traditions (Yoder, Hauerwas).

Verse 12's enemy list — *archai, exousiai, kosmokratoras tou skotous toutou, pneumatika tēs ponērias en tois epouraniois* — is unparalleled in length and specificity within the Pauline corpus. The four terms are not simply synonyms; commentators from Lincoln through Arnold to Thielman read them as a graduated catalogue of supernatural-political opposition. The verb παλή (*palē*, "wrestling") is unexpected: it is the language of Greek athletic combat (the *palaistra*), not military battle, suggesting close-quarters intimate struggle rather than impersonal warfare.

Within the letter's argument, chapter 6 brings to a climax a theme introduced at 1:21 (Christ enthroned above all *archē* and *exousia*) and developed at 2:2 (the "prince of the power of the air"), 3:10 (the *archais* and *exousiais* in the heavenlies who learn the wisdom of God through the church), and 4:27 (give no opportunity to the devil). The peroratio is not a sudden military turn; it is the eschatological-cosmic climax to which the letter has been building.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the New Testament canon, Eph 6 sits in dialogue with the Pauline armor language of 1 Thess 5:8 and Rom 13:12, the cosmic-Christ passages of Col 1:15-20 and Phil 2:9-11, the slave-master parallels of Col 3:22-4:1, 1 Pet 2:18-25, and Phlm 16, and the apocalyptic warfare of Revelation. The Onesimus-Philemon connection is especially dense: Phlm and Eph likely circulated together among Asian churches; Phlm 16 ("no longer as a bondservant but more than a bondservant, as a beloved brother") is the narrative enactment of Eph 6:9's "no partiality."

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Origen's *On First Principles* III.2 and his commentary fragments treat 6:12 as a serious doctrine of demonic *archai* and *exousiai* organized hierarchically; this becomes the seedbed for the patristic angelology that culminates in Pseudo-Dionysius' *Celestial Hierarchy* (c. 500). Chrysostom's homilies on Ephesians (Hom. 22-24) read the armor passage as concrete spiritual discipline — fasting, prayer, almsgiving as the practical equipping. Tertullian (*De Idololatria*) used the armor passage's spiritual character to argue Christians could not bear the sword of Rome. Jerome and Augustine read 6:12 as referring to fallen angels organized in opposition to God; Augustine in *City of God* developed the two-cities frame in which the Powers' opposition is the cosmic backdrop of human history.

The medieval West reorganized the chapter around moral allegory. The armor became a list of virtues; the *Psychomachia* of Prudentius (early 5th c.) had already personified the virtues and vices in armed combat, and medieval moralists worked the Ephesians armor list into virtue catalogues. Aquinas (ST II-II.10-25 on faith, hope, and love; II-II.123 on fortitude) develops the armor-as-virtue tradition systematically. Bernard of Clairvaux's *De Laude Novae Militiae* (1135) is a striking countertext — written for the Templars, it baptizes literal warfare, with significant violence to Eph 6:12's "not against flesh and blood." The Crusades-era reading of the armor as authorizing literal Christian violence is the great patristic-medieval failure.

The Reformation reread the chapter through the doctrine of bondage and liberation. Luther's *On the Bondage of the Will* (1525) drew on Eph 6:12 to argue that human beings are not neutral agents but enslaved either to Satan or to Christ; the cosmic dualism of the chapter is real, and grace must be a divine intervention. Calvin's commentary (1548) read the armor as the means by which the elect persevere; he insisted strongly on the spiritual-not-carnal character of the warfare. The Anabaptist tradition — Schleitheim Confession (1527), Menno Simons, the Hutterite Chronicle — treated 6:12 as the decisive text against Christian use of the sword: if our struggle is not against flesh and blood, then carnal weapons cannot be the means. This nonresistance reading is preserved in modern Anabaptist theology, especially John Howard Yoder's *The Politics of Jesus* (1972) and Stanley Hauerwas' work; Mennonite, Brethren, and Quaker traditions retain it.

Critical and twentieth-century scholarship transformed the chapter. F.C. Baur and the Tübingen school read 6:12 as evidence of post-Pauline cosmological speculation. Rudolf Bultmann's demythologization program (*Kerygma and Myth*, 1948) reinterpreted the Powers existentially, as the structures of the human existential predicament. The decisive turn came with Hendrik Berkhof's *Christ and the Powers* (1953, ET 1962), which reread the *archai* and *exousiai* as both spiritual realities and the structural-institutional forces of history (states, ideologies, economic systems); the cross "disarmed" the Powers by exposing them. Walter Wink's trilogy — *Naming the Powers* (1984), *Unmasking the Powers* (1986), *Engaging the Powers* (1992) — developed Berkhof's insight into a full social-political theology: the Powers are the inner spiritual reality of outer institutional forms, simultaneously good (created), fallen, and to be redeemed. Greg Boyd's *God at War* (1997) and *Satan and the Problem of Evil* (2001) argued for a more traditional warfare worldview against demythologization. N.T. Wright (*The Day the Revolution Began*, 2016, and earlier work) takes a both-and position: the Powers are real spiritual realities and they manifest in social-structural ways. Gustaf Aulén's *Christus Victor* (1931) had already reframed atonement theory around the chapter's victory-over-Powers logic, recovering a dimension of patristic theology lost in Anselmian-Reformed substitutionary models.

The slavery passage (6:5-9) generated its own enormous reception history. Christian abolitionism is older than often acknowledged: Gregory of Nyssa's *Homily 4 on Ecclesiastes* (c. 380) declared that slavery cannot be reconciled with the *imago Dei* — the earliest unambiguous Christian abolition argument. Quaker testimony emerged in the 1650s-1680s (Fox, Edmundson); Wesley's *Thoughts on Slavery* (1774), Wilberforce, and the Clapham Sect drove the 1833 British Abolition Act on explicitly biblical grounds. In America, the Grimké sisters, the Tappan brothers, Garrison, and Frederick Douglass deployed Scripture against pro-slavery readings; Douglass' *Narrative* (1845) is partly a theological text, as is Sojourner Truth's prophetic Methodism.

But the same texts sanctified slavery. Confederate Bible commentary (Thornwell, Dabney) read 6:5 as divine sanction; antebellum proslavery theology was a coherent biblical project. African-American biblical scholarship has documented this contradiction with unparalleled depth: Cain Hope Felder's *Stony the Road We Trod* (1991), Vincent Wimbush's *African Americans and the Bible* (2000), Allen Dwight Callahan's *The Talking Book* (2006), and earlier, Howard Thurman's *Jesus and the Disinherited* (1949). Callahan in particular shows the Bible functioning simultaneously as oppressive instrument and source of hope — the same Bible.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** Modern Paul-within-Judaism scholarship reads Eph 6 in dialogue with Second Temple holy-war traditions. The 1QM War Scroll from Qumran, the Sons of Light vs. Sons of Darkness apocalyptic, is the closest Jewish parallel to 6:10-20; Wisdom of Solomon 5:17-20 is the closest Hellenistic-Jewish parallel; Philo's allegorical readings of armor in *On the Confusion of Tongues* and elsewhere give a parallel exegetical method. Jewish scholarship (Daniel Boyarin, Mark Nanos, Pamela Eisenbaum) has noted that the transfer of Yahweh's armor (Isa 59:17) to the church (Eph 6) carries a quietly supersessionist edge: what was said of Israel's God being put on by the people of God is now spoken of the church. Where modern Jewish scholarship still pushes back: against the Christian abolitionist self-narrative that treats Christianity as uniquely anti-slavery — Jewish abolitionists (David Einhorn, Sabato Morais), the Talmudic restrictions on Hebrew slavery (Mishnah *Kiddushin* 1:2-3, the seventh-year manumission of Exod 21 / Deut 15), and the absence of any major Jewish proslavery theology complicate the picture. David Daube's *The New Testament and Rabbinic Judaism* (1956) noted that NT Haustafel ethics import more from Hellenistic philosophy than from rabbinic legal tradition; the rabbinic ethics of Hebrew slavery (debt-bondage with strict legal limits) is structurally different from the Roman *servitus* into which 6:5 speaks.

**Where this leaves us.** The chapter is the New Testament's most explicit theological-political text. Its slavery passage forces the question of how a text can carry both the seeds of abolition (6:9's "no partiality") and the historical use as proslavery proof-text. Its armor passage forces the question of what kind of warfare Christians are actually in. Both questions remain unsettled.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The Powers — what are they?** Take 6:12: *archai*, *exousiai*, *kosmokratoras tou skotous toutou*, *pneumatika tēs ponērias en tois epouraniois*. Four major positions divide the field. **Traditional Catholic-Orthodox-evangelical:** the Powers are real spiritual beings, fallen angels organized hierarchically against God, with demonic agents operating in human affairs. The CCC §§391-395 on the fall of angels, Eastern Orthodox demonology, Pentecostal-charismatic deliverance ministry, and Greg Boyd's warfare-worldview theology all share this. **Wink-Berkhof structural reading:** the Powers are the inner spiritual reality of outer institutional forms — states, corporations, ideologies, racial-economic systems; they are simultaneously created good, fallen, and to be redeemed; they "exist" both spiritually and socially. **Bultmannian existential demythologization:** the Powers are mythological language for the human existential situation under the sway of inauthenticity, sin, and death; properly understood, they name an anthropological-existential reality. **N.T. Wright's both-and:** real spiritual realities that manifest in concrete political-social structures, with the cross's "disarming" of them being both a cosmic and a historical event.

Steelmanning the Wink-Berkhof reading I find more contested than its popularity suggests: Wink's account is not a dilution of demonology but an enrichment. The traditional "demons in the air" reading struggles to explain *how* spiritual beings act in history if not through the institutions, ideologies, and corporate behaviors humans construct; Wink answers that they act precisely *as* the inner reality of those structures. The cross "disarmed" the Powers (Col 2:15) not by chaining demons in a cosmic prison but by exposing the spiritual bankruptcy of imperial-religious-economic violence at Calvary. This makes 6:12 directly applicable to systems analysis without abandoning supernatural reality. The traditional reading worries this collapses into mere sociological reductionism; Wink answers that *both* the spiritual and the structural are real, and the contemporary church's failure is to treat them as alternatives. **Where this leaves us:** the fight over the Powers is partly a fight over what counts as "real" — and the longer one reads Wink and Berkhof, the harder it becomes to defend a clean separation between spiritual and structural evil.

**Tension 2: Slavery accommodation and Christian moral progress.** Eph 6:5-9 addresses slaves to obey masters; it does not prescribe abolition; and yet it embeds 6:9's "no partiality" within the Haustafel form. Two readings contend. **The "implicit abolition" reading** (held in various forms by John Stott, N.T. Wright, Tom Holland in *Dominion*, and the older abolitionist tradition): the chapter plants the seed of human equality before God that takes 1700+ years to articulate as legal abolition, but the implicit logic is unmistakable; Christianity is the slow moral revolution that finally abolished an institution other ancient cultures took for granted. Gregory of Nyssa's homily 4 on Ecclesiastes (c. 380), the medieval canonist developments, the Quaker testimony, Wesley, Wilberforce, the abolition movements — these are the working-out of what 6:9 implies.

**The reception-history critique** (Cain Hope Felder, Vincent Wimbush, Allen Dwight Callahan): the text *and its dominant reception* coexisted with the Atlantic slave trade, the antebellum American slave system, and the spiritualizing readings that displaced material liberation. To celebrate Christian moral progress is to grade the tradition on a curve; to read 6:5-9 honestly is to face that the church endorsed slavery far more often, and far longer, than it opposed it. The "implicit abolition" reading risks a triumphalism that ignores how Scripture was used. Callahan's *The Talking Book* shows the Bible functioning simultaneously as the slave master's instrument and the enslaved community's source of hope — the same Bible.

Steelmanning this critique against the more reassuring abolitionist narrative: the data is not on the abolitionist side until very late. From the Council of Gangra (c. 340) anathematizing slaves who fled their masters under religious pretext, through medieval ecclesiastical slave-holding, through the papal slave-trade bulls *Dum Diversas* (1452) and *Romanus Pontifex* (1455) which authorized perpetual enslavement of "Saracens, pagans, and other unbelievers," through the Southern Baptist Convention's founding in 1845 explicitly to defend slaveholding missionaries — the institutional church's record on slavery is overwhelmingly accommodationist. Gregory of Nyssa is a striking exception, not the rule. The "Christianity slowly abolished slavery" narrative is a story the church likes to tell itself but cannot fully document. The seeds were genuinely there in 6:9, but it took centuries of *reading against* the dominant interpretive tradition for those seeds to germinate, and the people who did the reading-against were often the enslaved themselves — the spiritual songs, the hush-harbor preaching, Frederick Douglass, Sojourner Truth — not the institutional church. **Where this leaves us:** the answer is probably both. The seeds were really there (Greg of Nyssa, Wesley, Wilberforce demonstrate this); the institutional church suppressed them for most of its history; the suppressed reading was preserved most vividly by the enslaved themselves. Christian witness in unjust structures today should be sober about which of these patterns it is replicating.

**One unresolved question:** If the dominant Christian reading of Eph 6:5-9 endorsed slavery for most of church history, and the minority reading that produced abolition was preserved largely by the enslaved — then who, today, are the enslaved-readers preserving the minority readings of the texts the institutional church is using to legitimize structural violence?

## The Thread

- **Isaiah 59:17** — the source for the breastplate and helmet imagery; reading Yahweh's armor in Isaiah and the church's armor in Ephesians side by side reveals the audacious transfer at the heart of the peroratio.
- **Philemon 16** — Paul's request that Onesimus be received "no longer as a bondservant but more than a bondservant, as a beloved brother"; the narrative working-out of Eph 6:9's "no partiality" within a single concrete relationship.
- **1QM (the Qumran War Scroll)** — the Second Temple Jewish apocalyptic backdrop for the cosmic warfare of 6:10-20; Sons of Light vs. Sons of Darkness as the conceptual matrix Paul (or his disciple) is drawing on.
- **Colossians 2:15** ("he disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him") — the Pauline christological logic that grounds the Eph 6 armor: the Powers have already been defeated at the cross, which is why the church's posture is *standing* (4x in Eph 6:11-14), not advancing.
