# Joshua 4: The Doubled Stones and the Question That Will Come

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter deposits Israel at Gilgal (*Gilgāl*, גִּלְגָּל, from *gālal* "to roll," denoting a "circle" — possibly a ring of standing stones) on the eastern edge of Jericho's territory. Traditional identifications point to Khirbet el-Mefjer near modern Jericho; Adam Zertal's surveys identified some fifteen "foot-shaped" Iron I enclosures he interpreted as *gilgālîm*, suggesting a wider phenomenon of cult-sites on the margins of emergent Israel. The text dates the crossing precisely: "the tenth day of the first month" (v. 19) — the same day Israel chose the Passover lamb in Exodus 12:3, four days before Passover. The liturgical calendar is being layered onto the conquest chronology.

The material culture is stones. Standing stones (*maṣṣēvôt*, מַצֵּבוֹת, sing. *maṣṣēvâ*) are found across the Levant — the Gezer row excavated by Macalister and re-dated by Dever to Middle Bronze, Yadin's "High Place" at Hazor with its basalt *maṣṣēvôt* and anthropomorphic figure, the Arad sanctuary. Canaanite religion was full of them. Israelite religion is ambivalent: Jacob sets them up (Gen 28:18; 31:45), Moses erects twelve at Sinai (Exod 24:4), but Deuteronomy 16:22 prohibits them and reforming kings tear them down. Joshua 4 uses the generic *ʾăvānîm* (stones, vv. 3, 8, 9, 20), not *maṣṣēvôt* — but the cultic resonance is unmistakable. Israel is adapting a pan-Levantine practice by theological redefinition.

Four Hebrew terms merit close attention. *Zikkārôn* (זִכָּרוֹן, "memorial," v. 7) is the same word used in Exodus 12:14 for the Passover observance and in Exodus 17:14 for the written memorial of Amalek's defeat. It names an object or practice whose function is to make the past present. *Môpēṯ* is not used here but the concept hovers — the stones are a "sign" (*ʾôṯ*, אוֹת, v. 6) in the same sense as the rainbow (Gen 9:12) and circumcision (Gen 17:11): a physical marker that binds memory to body. *Yāraš* (יָרַשׁ, "take possession," implicit in the approach to "the plains of Jericho," v. 13) is the conquest verb par excellence; its absence here is striking — chapter 4 is about stones, not seizure. And the repeated *kî yišʾālûn* (כִּי יִשְׁאָלוּן, "when they will ask," vv. 6, 21) is a Deuteronomistic formula (Deut 6:20 is the primary parallel — "when your son asks you in time to come...") that assumes an intergenerational catechetical setting.

The archaeological situation at Gilgal is frustrating — no Late Bronze or Iron I remains at the traditional site have been convincingly matched to the biblical Gilgal; Zertal's "sandal sites" are suggestive but contested. What we do have is rich ANE context for commemorative stones — Hittite treaty-stelae, the Mesha Stele, the Merneptah Stele, the Dan Inscription — in which stones function as public announcement and claim on memory. Israel's stones operate in this shared cultural economy while being theologically repurposed.

The forty thousand armed men from Reuben, Gad, and half-Manasseh (v. 13) honor the Numbers 32 agreement — Transjordanian tribes owe military service in the western campaigns as a condition of their eastern inheritance. The chapter names the load-bearing fact: the land is conquered by a federation, not a single Israel.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre remains DtrH narrative with a catechetical-memorial structure. But chapter 4 is also, and notoriously, a textual crux. The chapter describes *two* sets of twelve stones: one set taken from the riverbed and carried to the encampment (vv. 3, 8, 20), and another set set up *in* the Jordan itself at the place where the priests stood (v. 9). Martin Noth's 1953 commentary argued these are two rival etiologies harmonized in the final text — one explaining the stones at Gilgal, one explaining stones visible in the Jordan (either actual boulders, or a local memory of such). Wellhausen had already proposed source division here. Richard Nelson (OTL) works the double etiology carefully and concludes the chapter is a deliberate literary doubling, not a redactional accident: one set of stones is *out in the land* (visible, instructive, catechetical), the other set is *hidden in the water* (invisible, eternal, a permanent witness under the surface). Robert Polzin and L. Daniel Hawk read the doublet as intentional — memory is always both public and submerged.

The chapter's main literary device is the repeated catechetical formula in vv. 6–7 and 21–24. The verses at the beginning and end of the chapter are near-identical in form ("when your children ask in time to come, 'What do these stones mean?' then you shall tell them...") — an inclusio that frames the entire narrative as a pedagogical tool. The second iteration expands on the first: the first version names the Jordan, the second connects the Jordan to the Red Sea (v. 23), then expands beyond Israel ("so that all the peoples of the earth may know," v. 24). The rhetoric widens in concentric rings: stone → family → Israel → the nations.

Chiastic structure: the chapter begins and ends with the LORD speaking to Joshua and Joshua speaking to the people (A–B–Bʹ–Aʹ), with the physical crossing and the stones at the center. The central section (vv. 10–18) is framed by the priests' feet — they "stood firm" in the middle of the Jordan (v. 10) and they "were lifted up" onto dry ground (v. 18), at which point the waters return. The chapter's physical action is framed by priestly feet.

Intertextually, the chapter is dense with Exodus and Deuteronomy. V. 23 explicitly compares the Jordan crossing to the Red Sea (*yam sûp*) — the first time in Joshua the comparison is made out loud. V. 24's "that all the peoples of the earth may know" echoes the purpose-formula of the Exodus plagues (Exod 9:16; 14:18). The catechetical formula of vv. 6, 21 is the structural twin of Deuteronomy 6:20–25, Exodus 12:26–27, and Exodus 13:14 — a Deuteronomic didactic device that makes ritual a question-and-answer across generations. The "forty thousand armed men" (v. 13) echoes the census figures of Numbers.

The chapter contains one of Joshua's signature narrator-present intrusions: "they are there to this day" (v. 9). This phrase (*ʿad hayyôm hazzeh*) appears more than forty times in the DtrH — locating composition in a moment when the stones were still visible, or memorable enough to claim they were. Brevard Childs's 1963 essay "A Study of the Formula 'Until This Day'" remains the classic analysis.

Placement in the book's argument: chapter 4 closes the crossing sequence begun in chapter 3, but it also opens the Gilgal cycle of chapters 5–9, where the tribes circumcise, celebrate Passover, encounter the commander of the LORD's army, and launch the Jericho campaign. The stones at Gilgal are the physical marker that the people are now *here* — the landless nation has become a people with a place and a history.

Textual variants: LXX Joshua 4 is substantially similar to MT but shorter, omitting a few of the repeated details (the LXX version of v. 9 is compressed, and the repetition in vv. 10b, 11 is less redundant). The famous 4QJosh^a fragment, studied by Eugene Ulrich and Emanuel Tov, preserves portions of chapters 3–8 in what appears to be a rearranged order, with the Mt. Ebal altar (MT Josh 8:30–35) placed earlier. The implication: the stones-memorial/Jordan-crossing/Ebal-altar/Jericho sequence was still being reordered in the late Second Temple period. The text is a text about memory, which was itself still being formed as a memory.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, chapter 4 is the memorial-creation moment. If Genesis gives you creation, Exodus gives you redemption, and Joshua 3 gives you arrival, chapter 4 gives you the first monument — the first physical object in the land whose purpose is to remember. This is the beginning of Israel's public memory as a land-holding people, and every subsequent prophetic call to "remember" presupposes the stones at Gilgal. When the prophets later accuse Israel of forgetting, the stones are what Israel forgot.

**Jewish trajectories.** Second Temple literature: Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.1.4) follows and harmonizes the two-sets reading. Ben Sira 46:1–6 treats Joshua but not the stones. 4Q378–379 (Apocryphon of Joshua) preserves fragmentary liturgical material that may reflect Gilgal traditions. Pseudo-Philo's *LAB* 21 has Joshua *bury* the stones rather than display them — a striking alternative memorial theology.

Rabbinic reception: Babylonian Talmud, Sotah 35b–36a, wrestles with the doubled stones. R. Judah argues there were *three* sets — one in the Jordan, one at Gilgal, and one that Joshua inscribed with the Torah per Deuteronomy 27:3. The tradition reads chapter 4 with Deuteronomy 27 and Joshua 8 as a single monumental program. Targum Jonathan and Midrash Tannaim read the stones as *witnesses* at a lower register than heaven and earth (Deut 4:26). The Mekhilta connects the question-and-answer formula to the "Four Sons" of the Passover Haggadah.

Medieval Jewish commentary: Rashi assigns one set as memorial (Gilgal), one as witness under the water. Radak emphasizes pedagogy — the stones are *mesorah*, tradition-in-object-form. Ramban reads v. 23's linking of Jordan and Red Sea as theologically decisive: Israel's history is patterned, every crossing reiterating Exodus. Abarbanel weights the Transjordanian tribes' obligation (v. 12) as evidence of covenantal interconnection. Maimonides draws on the Joshua 4 paradigm in his discussion of memorial commandments (*Mishneh Torah*, Hilkhot Shevitat Yom Tov).

Modern Jewish readers: Yosef Hayim Yerushalmi's *Zakhor* (1982) is indispensable — biblical Israel was uniquely commanded to remember (*zakhor*), and the stones are a foundational institution of that memory-work. They ritualize, not record. Halbwachs's *On Collective Memory* and Pierre Nora's *lieux de mémoire* apply almost verbatim to Gilgal. Avivah Zornberg reads the doubled stones as a theology of presence-and-hiddenness — visible at Gilgal, invisible in the riverbed, together figuring Israel's relationship to its own past.

**Christian trajectories.** Patristic: Origen's *Homily on Joshua* 4–5 allegorizes the stones as the twelve apostles, planted by Christ in the Jordan of baptism so that the Church can cross. "The twelve stones are the twelve apostles; the twelve stones are the twelve foundations of the heavenly Jerusalem" (cf. Rev 21:14) — this became the dominant Christian mode. Augustine (*Quaestiones in Heptateuchum* VI.13) follows Origen and connects the stones to the building of the Church. Ambrose extends the typology in baptismal catechesis. Jerome's letters reference the stones in connection with Holy Land pilgrimage — by the fourth century Christians were venerating stones claimed to be the originals (Eusebius's *Onomasticon*). Theodoret, Chrysostom, and Ephrem each offer variations on the twelve-apostles reading.

Medieval: the *Glossa Ordinaria* reproduces Origen; Aquinas (*Summa* III q. 66, a. 11) uses Joshua 4 for apostolic-foundation ecclesiology. Hugh of St. Victor and Bede extend it. The Byzantine liturgical tradition reads parts of Joshua 3–4 at Theophany.

Reformation: Luther reads the stones as admonitions, not relics. Calvin's Joshua commentary is exacting: the stones are pedagogy for children, not cultic shrine; he rebukes Catholic relic-veneration through Joshua 4 — the instinct to see presence in objects is what the chapter warns against. Menno Simons reads the chapter's pedagogy as a model for free-church catechesis — the point is intergenerational teaching, not monument.

Critical scholarship: Wellhausen assigned the doubled stones to two sources. Noth's *Das Buch Josua* (1953) is the classic source-critical treatment. Von Rad read the chapter within his Gilgal festival hypothesis. Frank Cross located the pedagogical formulae in the Dtr1 Josianic layer, where royal ideology grounded national identity in covenant memory. Richard Nelson (OTL) reads the final form as deliberate theological composition. Thomas Dozeman (Eerdmans) treats the chapter as the pivot from wilderness to land. L. Daniel Hawk reads it as ideology production — monuments are always political. The honest-critical strand (Brueggemann, Creach, the broader collective-memory literature of Halbwachs/Nora/Assmann) notes that memory-making is always partly memory-erasing; Israel's stones install on Canaanite ground and overwrite what was previously remembered there.

Convergence and divergence: Jewish and Christian traditions converge in reading the chapter as fundamentally catechetical — the stones exist to provoke the children's question. They diverge on the referent of the stones. For Jewish tradition the stones are markers of a historical crossing and an ongoing covenantal memory belonging to Israel. For Christian tradition the stones become the apostles, and the crossing becomes baptism, and the memory is expropriated into a new referent. The divergence maps onto the larger hermeneutical question: does the Hebrew Bible generate its own reception within Israel's ongoing life, or does it disclose its true meaning only in Christian typology? Both readings leave marks on the chapter, and both are answerable for what they do to it.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What is God up to in this moment? The chapter is doing memory-work. It is inventing the category of national-religious monument for Israel, and handing it to the next generation as the primary instrument of covenantal transmission. Two tensions.

**Tension 1: Memory as gift versus memory as political construction.** The chapter explicitly produces memory. The stones are not found — they are chosen, carried, and set up. The question-and-answer formula is a script. The narrator both preserves and produces the past. Is this a divine gift or an ideological apparatus — or both?

- *Catholic (Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith; CCC 1066–1075)*: Sacred tradition (*traditio*) is the Spirit-guided transmission of revelation through the life of the Church, including liturgical memory. The stones are the Old Testament prototype of *anamnesis* — the liturgical remembering that does not merely recall but makes-present. This is not ideology; it is the Spirit's ongoing work in the community. The construction of memory is authorized by the One who is himself Memory.
- *Reformed (Calvin, Bavinck, Richard Lints)*: Memory is a gift, but its mediation is always human and therefore always liable to corruption. Calvin's warning against relics and Catholic veneration arises from Joshua 4: the stones are permitted as admonition but forbidden as cult. The Reformed tradition has always been suspicious of memorial objects becoming objects of worship. The catechism replaces the stone.
- *Lutheran (Luther; Oswald Bayer)*: Memory is the gift of God's word addressed to us across generations. The stones are *viva vox* in stone form — living voice. Bayer's work on promise and memory develops this: memory is not passive recall but active reception of promise.
- *Anabaptist*: The chapter's pedagogy is the model for the believing community. Memory is transmitted through the confession and practice of the gathered church, not through state-supported monuments. The Anabaptist suspicion of national-religious monuments (which became violent persecution under Christendom) is partly formed by a reading of Joshua 4 that refuses to let the stones become imperial.
- *Jewish rabbinic*: Yerushalmi's *Zakhor* is the classical modern formulation: memory is the command (*zakhor*), and the stones are one of its material forms. The rabbinic tradition trusts memory because it is covenantal — it is the work of a people under command. This is not ideology-production in the cynical sense; it is covenantal fidelity over time.
- *Critical/academic*: Halbwachs, Nora, Jan Assmann, Paul Connerton — collective memory is always partly construction, partly present-serving, partly erasure. Gilgal is a *lieu de mémoire* in Nora's sense: a site crystallized because continuous memory has become precarious. Hawk and the honest-critical commentators add that Israel's memory overwrites the memory of prior inhabitants.

*Steelman of the critical position*: The chapter openly admits that memory is being produced — the stones are set up precisely because the coming generations will not have the experience. "When your children ask in time to come" is not a description of spontaneous inquiry but a script for authorized transmission. This is what every national memorial does: the Arlington cemetery, the Lincoln Memorial, the Yad Vashem pavilion. They are designed to produce a specific kind of memory in a specific kind of subject. Critical theory is not saying the memory is false; it is saying memory is always made. Joshua 4 does not dispute this — it performs it. Theological readings that treat the stones as *merely* divine gift without noticing the construction are reading less carefully than the text.

*Where this leaves us:* Memory in the biblical tradition is simultaneously divine gift and human construction, and the chapter does not collapse one into the other. The honest reader has to hold both.

**Tension 2: The *herem* frame of the memorial.** The stones at Gilgal commemorate the Jordan crossing, but the crossing is the entry into the land that will be taken by *herem* in the next two chapters. Every time the stones do their work — every time a child asks and a parent answers — the answer implicates the child in the conquest.

- *Traditional apologetic (Copan, Hess, Goldingay)*: The memorial celebrates God's faithfulness to bring Israel in. The moral problems of the conquest are a separate question. The stones commemorate deliverance, not extermination. Goldingay adds pastoral nuance: the violence should be acknowledged, but the memorial is about what God did, not what Israel did.
- *Divine Command critical (Creach, Seibert, Collins)*: There is no way to separate the Gilgal memorial from what Gilgal became — the base camp for the destruction of Jericho. The question a child asks is answered with the Red Sea, but the Red Sea was the destruction of Pharaoh's army. The memorial celebrates deliverance-through-enemy-destruction. Seibert says the text is disturbing, and the memorial extends the disturbance into every future generation.
- *Reformed*: Calvin takes the violence seriously and insists it is God's sovereign action, but also that the memorial function is primarily pedagogical, not celebratory of destruction. The stones teach fear-of-YHWH, not triumphalism.
- *Anabaptist/pacifist*: The memorial, to the extent it celebrates conquest, is exactly the kind of religious nationalism that Jesus's refusal of the sword refutes. But the memorial function itself — teaching the next generation — is preserved in the Anabaptist tradition. The content is transformed.
- *Jewish rabbinic*: The memorial commemorates God's action *on behalf of* Israel, which in rabbinic framing is a distinct category from celebrating Israel's own violence. The rabbis have spent centuries disentangling these, and Maimonides's argument that the Canaanite nations are untraceable (and therefore the *herem* inapplicable) preserves the memorial function while retiring the conquest template. The stones remain; the sword does not.
- *Honest-critical (Brueggemann, Creach, Hawk)*: The memorial is literally installed on someone else's land. The child who asks is learning to inhabit a land whose prior inhabitants have been erased. The honest-critical question is whether a memorial can be just when its very installation erases a prior memory — and the chapter, on its own terms, does not pose that question.

*Steelman of the traditional apologetic position*: The memorial is about God, not about Israel. When the child asks what the stones mean, the answer is "the waters of the Jordan were cut off before the ark of the covenant of the LORD" — the agent is YHWH, the miracle is the subject, the crossing is the content. The chapter does not commemorate Jericho or the *herem* or Canaanite displacement. It commemorates a river parting. To read the memorial as fundamentally about conquest is to read the later chapters back into this one. The chapter's own theological content is narrower and more specific than the honest-critical reading allows.

*Where this leaves us:* The chapter's pedagogy works because it is specific — the answer is about God's action, not Israel's. But the pedagogy is embedded in a book whose subsequent chapters cannot be un-embedded. Readers inherit the stones and everything else.

*One unresolved question:* If the stones' pedagogy depends on a child's question — and the child will ask whatever they ask — what do we do when our children's actual questions ("Why did God command the killing?") are not the questions the text prepared an answer for?

## The Thread

- **Deuteronomy 6:20–25** — the primary parallel for the "when your children ask" formula. Read these chapters together and notice that the Joshua chapter *performs* the catechism Deuteronomy merely *commanded*. The stones give the Shema a material anchor.
- **1 Samuel 7:12** — Samuel sets up the stone Ebenezer ("thus far the LORD has helped us"). When you get there, remember that Israel's memorial practice is plural and ongoing — Gilgal is the first but not the last; every generation makes its own stone.
- **Psalm 78** — a poetic performance of the very catechesis Joshua 4 establishes. The psalm is what the parents eventually sang to the children the stones taught them to teach. Notice how the psalm is both memory and indictment; memorial never guarantees fidelity.
- **1 Peter 2:5** — "you yourselves, like living stones, are being built up as a spiritual house." When you reach this verse in the New Testament, remember the twelve stones at Gilgal: the metaphor is old, the apostolic writer is rereading it, and the question of what memorial stones *are* — people or monuments — has been contested the whole way through.
