# Ezekiel 5: The Razor, the Balance, the Hair

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Ezekiel ben Buzi, a deported Jerusalem priest (1:3), is preaching beside an irrigation canal in the alluvial plain south of Babylon — Tel-abib by the Chebar (3:15), almost certainly the *naru kabari* of the Murashu archive in the Nippur region. The first wave of Judean deportation came in 597 BC under Nebuchadnezzar II, sweeping up King Jehoiachin, the royal household, the artisan class, and Ezekiel himself. The book's superscriptions (1:2; 8:1; 20:1; etc.) date from "the fifth year of the exile of King Jehoiachin," which places his prophetic ministry from roughly 593 to 571 BC. Chapter 5 belongs to the inaugural cycle of judgment oracles (chs. 1–24) preached *before* the final 588–587 BC siege under Zedekiah's revolt — Ezekiel is warning the exiles that Jerusalem's fall is imminent and inevitable, against the optimistic Hananiah-style prophets in Babylon (cf. Jer 29) who promised a quick return.

The exilic community itself is no longer a hypothetical from cuneiform shadows. The **Al-Yahudu tablets** published by Pearce and Wunsch (*Documents of Judean Exiles and West Semitics in Babylonia*, 2014) record Judean deportees living in a settlement explicitly called *al-Yahudu* ("Judah-town" / "city of Judah") in the Nippur countryside, transacting in Akkadian, paying rents and taxes, holding land in the *ilku* obligation system, naming children with Yahwistic theophorics. The **Murashu archive** from a generation later confirms ongoing Judean integration into Babylonian commerce. This is the milieu in which Ezekiel performs his sign-acts — not an isolated prophet on a riverbank but a priest among priests with a community of elders that visit his house to consult him (8:1; 14:1; 20:1).

For Ezekiel 5 the material-culture frame is military rather than mystical. Babylonian siege warfare followed a brutal protocol: famine inside the walls, plague from the resulting filth and corpse-density, breach by ramp and battering ram, slaughter of survivors at sword-point in the breach, the rest deported. Ezekiel's threefold division of hair (5:2, 5:12) — fire, sword, scattering — is a precise typological inventory of a Mesopotamian siege's casualty distribution. The Babylonian Chronicle (BM 21946) and Nebuchadnezzar's own cylinder inscriptions document exactly this protocol applied to Tyre, Ashkelon, and Jerusalem.

Authorship and dating questions have largely settled. C.C. **Torrey**'s 1930 hypothesis (*Pseudo-Ezekiel and the Original Prophecy*) that the book was a third-century BC pseudonymous Palestinian composition is now broadly rejected. The book displays remarkable structural unity, consistent priestly idiom, dated superscriptions in chronological order, and a Babylonian Akkadian-loanword stratum that fits the sixth century. **Moshe Greenberg** (Anchor Bible, 1983/1997), **Daniel Block** (NICOT, 1997/1998), and **Walther Zimmerli** (Hermeneia, 1979/1983) all argue for substantial sixth-century authorship by the prophet himself with limited disciple-circle redaction. **G.A. Cooke**'s 1936 ICC commentary, while older, anchored the conservative dating; **Walther Eichrodt** (OTL, 1970) and **Margaret Odell** (Smyth & Helwys, 2005) extend that consensus. The Qumran witnesses (4QEzek-a, 4QEzek-b, 4QEzek-c, plus the small Masada fragment) confirm a stable text type by the second century BC.

Key vocabulary already establishing itself in chapter 5: *kavod YHWH* (כְּבוֹד יְהוָה, the "weighty glory" — though it appears in chs. 1, 8–11, 43 rather than 5, the theology underlies the whole), *ben adam* (בֶּן אָדָם, "son of man," 93× across the book and beginning here at 5:1 in a chain that started at 2:1), *to'ebah* (תּוֹעֵבָה, "abomination," 5:9, 11), and *gilulim* (גִּלּוּלִים, "dung-pellet idols," soon to flood the next chapter).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 5 is a sign-act oracle (genre: *symbolische Handlung* in Zimmerli's classification) — the prophet's body and its props become the message. The structure is tight: vv. 1–4 the act, vv. 5–17 the interpretation, with two oracle-formulas ("thus says the Lord GOD") cycling through the explanation. Greenberg notes the text uses *hineni 'al* ("Behold, I am against you," v. 8) — a formula reserved in Ezekiel for the most aggressive judgment, applied here for the first time to Jerusalem itself rather than to a foreign nation.

The chiastic logic of the chapter pivots on v. 5: *This is Jerusalem. I have set her in the center of the nations.* The opening sign (vv. 1–4) and the closing curse (vv. 14–17) frame a center that re-reads the city's *centrality* not as honor but as exposure — Jerusalem is in the middle so the nations can see her humiliation.

The leitwort is the threefold division — *šelîšît* (שְׁלִשִׁית, "third part") — appearing in vv. 2 and 12 as a structural binding of sign to interpretation. Greenberg points out the additional fold in vv. 3–4: a *small portion* bound in the skirts of the prophet's robe, then *some of those* thrown back into fire. This is not redundancy. It is the *remnant theology in miniature*: even the rescued bundle is not safe; even the small number bound up will see further winnowing. Block reads this as Ezekiel's deliberate corrosion of the Isaianic remnant promise — the survivors are not the saved, only the not-yet-judged.

For text criticism: 5:1 has a small MT-LXX divergence on the verb for the razor's action; LXX softens the priestly self-defilement (cf. Lev 21:5). 5:13 has a particularly aggressive MT in *hitnaḥamtî* ("I will be comforted / satisfy myself") — divine satiation through wrath — that LXX renders more passively. The Qumran 4QEzek-a fragments do not preserve chapter 5 directly but support MT readings in adjacent chapters.

The rabbinic restriction matters here too. Mishnah **Hagigah 2:1** forbids exposition of the *Merkavah* to anyone other than a sage who already understands it on his own; **Bavli Hagigah 11b–14a** elaborates with the famous *Pardes* narrative (four sages enter the orchard; only Akiva exits whole). The general anxiety extended in some rabbinic circles to all of Ezekiel — Rabbi Hananiah ben Hezekiah is credited with burning 300 jars of oil to harmonize Ezekiel with the Torah and avoid suppression (Bavli Shabbat 13b). Chapter 5 was never the censored part — chs. 1, 8, 40–48 were — but the chapter's vocabulary of priestly self-defilement (a priest taking a razor to his head) sits uncomfortably with Levitical purity law and underlines why later tradition treated Ezekiel as a book best read with supervision.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish trajectory.** Sirach 49:8 already canonizes Ezekiel by the early second century BC ("It was Ezekiel who saw the vision of glory which God showed him above the chariot of the cherubim"). The Mishnah/Talmud restrictions (Hagigah 2:1; Bavli Hagigah 11b–14a) shape rabbinic reading. *Targum Jonathan* renders chapter 5 with its characteristic moralizing expansions, glossing the sign-act for diaspora readers. *Pesikta Rabbati* takes the threefold judgment into haggadic elaboration on divine *middat ha-din* (attribute of justice) tempered by *middat ha-rachamim* (attribute of mercy) — the bound remnant of v. 3 becomes the seed-image for the haggadic principle that judgment never exhausts mercy. **Rashi** reads the hair literally and the fire of v. 4 typologically — the post-exilic period's renewed apostasies (the Hellenistic crisis) burning what the first exile spared. **Radak** focuses on the priestly dimension: Ezekiel's act is permitted because of *prophetic command* overriding ritual law (*hora'at sha'ah*). **Ibn Ezra** more cautiously suggests the act may have been *visionary* rather than physical. **Maimonides** in *Guide of the Perplexed* 3.1–7 places the entire visionary apparatus — though especially chs. 1 and 10 — within his scheme of figurative prophetic communication; the literalist reading is a concession to the imagination, not to the intellect.

In modern Jewish scholarship: **Greenberg**'s Anchor commentary refuses to over-historicize, arguing the sign-act *was* performed (against Ibn Ezra) but in a contained, interpretable space. **Jon Levenson** (*Theology of the Program of Restoration of Ezekiel 40–48*, 1976; *Sinai and Zion*, 1985) reads the chapter against the temple-theology backdrop: Jerusalem's *centrality* (5:5) is Zion-theology under inversion. **Benjamin Sommer** (*The Bodies of God*, 2009) engages Ezekiel's anthropomorphic theology in the visionary chapters; chapter 5 is collateral but the body-theology question (the prophet's body as instrument of YHWH's body of action) is live. **Jacqueline Lapsley** (*Can These Bones Live?*, 2000) reads Ezekiel's moral anthropology — the chapter's *more wicked than the nations* indictment turns on a reconstituted moral self that the book later gives in chs. 36–37.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic reception is dominated by three figures. **Origen**'s *Homilies on Ezekiel* (14 surviving in Jerome's Latin translation, c. 240) reads chapter 5 allegorically: the threefold division as the threefold judgment of body, soul, and spirit; the bound remnant as the church preserved through tribulation; the secondary fire of v. 4 as final purgation. **Jerome**'s *Commentary on Ezekiel* (414, 14 books, completed under the shadow of Alaric's sack of Rome) reads more historically, applying chapter 5 to the literal 587 BC fall and then typologically to Rome's chastisement — Jerome's own sense of *Roma cadens* is palpable. **Gregory the Great**'s *Homilies on Ezekiel* (delivered 593 during the Lombard siege of Rome, the most influential medieval treatment) reads chapter 5 morally and ecclesially: the razor is the preacher's discriminating word, the threefold division is the threefold pastoral rebuke. Gregory's homilies fix Ezekiel's reception in the Latin West for a millennium.

Medieval: **Hugh of St. Victor** and **Richard of St. Victor** (*De Visione Ezechielis*) extend the allegorical-mystical line. **Aquinas** treats Ezekiel sparingly in the *Summa* but uses the chapter's *judgment-on-the-elect* logic in his treatment of divine providence (*ST* I.22). Reformation: **Calvin**'s *Lectures on Ezekiel* (1565, two volumes, incomplete at his death — covering only chapters 1–20, so chapter 5 is included and substantial) reads the chapter against accommodation theory and covenant-fidelity. Calvin's exegesis is severe: Jerusalem's privilege is the ground of her greater judgment; the sign-act is *real* and *priestly* and *binding*. The Reformed tradition's reading of covenant accountability descends from Calvin's chapter 5 here as much as from Deuteronomy 28.

Critical: Cooke's ICC (1936) marked the consolidation of the historical-critical reading. **Zimmerli** (Hermeneia, two volumes 1979/1983) systematized form-critical analysis of the sign-acts. **Greenberg**'s holistic-rhetorical reading and **Block**'s evangelical-NICOT synthesis represent the late-twentieth-century consolidation. **Daniel Smith-Christopher**'s work on exile as collective trauma (*A Biblical Theology of Exile*, 2002) and **Margaret Odell**'s commentary read the chapter's extreme violence as the language of a community processing catastrophe — and several of these commentators register that the imagery is morally hard to read, a difficulty the book itself does not soften.

**Where Jewish and Christian readings diverge.** Christian interpreters from Origen forward read the chapter into a typological future — Jerusalem's first fall foreshadowing the second (70 AD) and the eschatological judgment. Jewish interpreters tend to read the chapter backward into covenant-history and forward into the not-yet-resolved exile of the present. The *bound remnant* of v. 3 is in Christian reading the church through history; in Jewish reading it is the historical remnant whose preservation is itself the divine mystery.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — Priestly self-defilement as prophetic vocation.** A priest of the line of Buzi, bound by Lev 21:5 ("They shall not make bald patches on their heads, nor shave off the edges of their beards"), is commanded by YHWH to take a sword as razor and shave both (5:1). The act is not just cosmetic; it is *priestly disqualification*. The body that was set apart for sanctuary service is now set apart for sign. Catholic tradition reads this within the larger frame of *vocational suffering* — the priest's body offered as sacrificial sign (Aquinas, the Tridentine theology of priestly character marked by suffering, more recently *Pastores Dabo Vobis* 1992). Reformed tradition reads it as cross-bearing — Calvin's "private will gives way to public office" gloss on the chapter and Bonhoeffer's later cost-of-discipleship reading. Anabaptist tradition reads it as witness through the body (Yoder, Hauerwas) — the prophet's defilement is the church's testimony in a defiled world. Steelman the least intuitive: the Anabaptist reading insists the *defilement* is itself the gospel — that purity is not preserved but *given over* to the sign. The priest's holiness was never the point; God's holiness expressed through the priest's defacement is. *Where this leaves us:* the prophet's body is not exempt from the message; the body that bears witness bears scars, and Lev 21 does not get the last word over Ezekiel 5.

**Tension 2 — Jerusalem more wicked than the nations (5:6–7).** The verse that punctures the chapter: "she has rebelled against my rules by doing wickedness more than the nations… and have not even acted according to the rules of the nations that are all around you." The covenant-elect underperform the covenant-stranger. Calvin's *Institutes* (esp. 4.20 on the magistrate; 2.8 on the moral law as accessible to natural reason) reads this through the pedagogy of privilege: the more given, the more required (cf. Lk 12:48). Brueggemann's *Theology of the Old Testament* (1997) reads it as the prophetic destabilization of all chosen-people triumphalism — the prophets weaponize the categories of election against elect complacency. Catholic social-teaching reads similar logic into responsibility-to-protect frameworks (more privilege = more accountability before God and history). Steelman the least intuitive: a hard *natural-law* reading would say the verse implies a baseline of common moral knowledge accessible without revelation, against which Israel is judged for *not even reaching the floor*. Calvin's Geneva edition of the Institutes engages exactly this point — natural law functions as the lower bound, covenant as the upper, and Israel has fallen below the lower. *Where this leaves us:* election is a heightened standard, not a guarantee. The chapter refuses the comfortable distinction between covenant insider and pagan outsider; the insider is judged by both standards and fails both.

The unresolved question that lands hard: if the bound remnant (v. 3) is itself partly thrown into the fire (v. 4), what kind of remnant theology are we left with? Not the Isaianic survivors, not yet the Pauline elect according to grace — something more austere. Survivors are not innocent; they are not even safe. Ezekiel does not yet name the resolution. Chapters 36 and 37 will. Chapter 5 ends with no remedy on the table — only the conviction that judgment is *spent* on the city, that anger has limits because it consumes itself, and that "they shall know that I am the LORD" (5:13) is the only outcome that matters. Whether knowing-the-LORD-by-judgment is good news depends entirely on which side of the razor you stand.

## The Thread

- **Lev 21:5** — the priestly prohibition Ezekiel breaks under prophetic command; the chapter is an inverted Leviticus 21, the priest defiled to make Israel see her own defilement.
- **Lam 4:10** — "the hands of compassionate women have boiled their own children." The cannibalism oracle of 5:10 is fulfilled per Lamentations; Ezekiel speaks before, Lamentations remembers after.
- **Deut 28:53–57** — the covenant-curse foundation for the cannibalism threat; Ezekiel is reading the Mosaic curse forward into the present.
- **Rev 6:8** — sword, famine, pestilence, wild beasts; the four horsemen of John's Apocalypse use Ezekiel's exact fourfold inventory (5:17 plus the wild beasts brought in at 14:21), the chapter's vocabulary inherited intact across six centuries into Christian apocalyptic.
