# Job 37: A Voice in the Thunder

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 37 closes Elihu's fourth and final speech, and it is constructed as a theophanic hymn — a praise-poem describing the approach of a storm. The ancient Near Eastern material here is specific and technical. The storm god was the chief high god of Syro-Palestinian and Anatolian polytheism: Baal-Hadad in Ugarit, Adad in Mesopotamia, Teshub in Hurrian-Hittite religion. Each carried a distinctive storm iconography — lightning as spear, thunder as voice, the whirlwind (*seʿarah* סְעָרָה) as chariot. The Ugaritic Baal Cycle (14th c BC, tablets from Ras Shamra) preserves exactly the vocabulary Elihu deploys: Baal's voice thunders, his lightning is his weapon, he rides the clouds. Hadad stelae from Aleppo and Tell Ahmar show the storm god wielding lightning bolts. The Kilamuwa Stele (9th c BC, Zincirli) and the Zakkur Stele (8th c BC) preserve the storm-god vocabulary in West Semitic royal rhetoric.

The Hebrew Bible inherits this vocabulary and reappropriates it for YHWH. Exodus 19's Sinai theophany, Psalm 29's "voice of the LORD" hymn, Psalm 18's descent-on-the-cherub, and 1 Kings 19's wind-earthquake-fire-still-small-voice are all working with storm-god imagery, sometimes polemically (YHWH is not *in* the storm in 1 Kings 19), sometimes appropriatively (YHWH speaks *as* the thunder in Psalm 29). Frank Moore Cross's *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* (1973) documents the storm-god substrate in the Hebrew theophany texts. Chapter 37 of Job sits inside that tradition and sets up the most sustained Hebrew storm-theophany outside Exodus: the YHWH speeches of chapters 38-41, which begin "Then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind (*min ha-seʿarah*)." Elihu's chapter 37 *seʿarah* in verse 9 is the same word.

The dating debate for Job as a whole remains unsettled: Pope, Clines, and Newsom place composition in the sixth century BC or later; Gordis and Andersen argue earlier. For the Elihu speeches specifically (chapters 32-37), most critical scholars (Pope, Clines, Westermann, Newsom) argue for secondary insertion: Elihu is absent from the prologue and epilogue, his vocabulary contains more Aramaisms than the surrounding material, and he directly quotes earlier Joban speeches in ways that suggest familiarity with the written book. Defenders of Elihu's originality (Gordis, Francis Andersen, David Fyall, John Hartley) argue the stylistic differences are consistent with characterization and that Elihu's speeches serve a definite structural function. The debate is not settled; Habel in the OTL commentary leans toward secondary insertion but acknowledges the evidence is not decisive.

The cosmology Elihu invokes is ancient Near Eastern in a quite specific way. Verse 9 — "from its chamber comes the whirlwind" — assumes the "chambers" (*chadarim* חֲדָרִים) of the winds, storehouses from which the storm god releases his weapons. The same imagery appears in Ugaritic texts, Jeremiah 10:13, and Psalm 135:7 ("who makes lightnings for the rain, who brings forth the wind from his storehouses"). Verse 18's "can you, like him, spread out the skies, hard as a cast metal mirror?" assumes the ancient Near Eastern cosmography of a metallic firmament (Genesis 1:6's *raqia*), a fixed dome that archaeological iconography from Sumerian boundary stones onward depicts as a solid vault. The archaeological and textual evidence for this cosmography is summarized in Othmar Keel's *The Symbolism of the Biblical World* (1978/ET 1997).

Five Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. First, *seʿarah* (סְעָרָה, "whirlwind," 37:9), the same word that will introduce YHWH's speech in 38:1 — Elihu's chapter predicts, or prepares for, the theophany that follows. Second, *raʿam* (רַעַם, "thunder," 37:2, 4, 5), the storm-god's characteristic voice. Third, *sheleg* (שֶׁלֶג, "snow," 37:6) and *qerach* (קֶרַח, "ice," 37:10), rare meteorological phenomena in the Levant that signal divine control of extraordinary weather. Fourth, *shaddai* (שַׁדַּי, "Almighty," 37:23), the archaic divine name used throughout Job that predates the Mosaic *YHWH* disclosure and fits the patriarchal setting. Fifth, *mishpat* (מִשְׁפָּט, "justice," 37:23) paired with *tzedaqah* (צְדָקָה, "righteousness") — the classical prophetic word-pair now deployed by Elihu in the book's most formally orthodox theological claim: "justice and abundant righteousness he will not violate."

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 37 is the final section of Elihu's fourth speech (the whole unit runs from 36:1 through 37:24). The genre is theophanic hymn, a specifically Hebrew subgenre of praise-poetry in which the approach or presence of the divine is narrated through storm imagery. Psalm 29 is the purest biblical example; Habakkuk 3 is another. The Ugaritic Baal hymns provide the closest Northwest Semitic comparanda.

The chapter's structure falls into three movements: the storm as thunder and voice (vv. 1-13), the challenge to Job to comprehend it (vv. 14-20), and the concluding statement on divine transcendence and justice (vv. 21-24). Verses 1-13 use descriptive participial clauses, parallel in form to Amos's doxologies and to Job 9's bitter hymn (Job's turning the vocabulary of praise back against God). Verses 14-20 pivot to second-person address directed at Job, with rhetorical questions — "Do you know how God lays his command upon them?" (v. 15), "Do you know the balancings of the clouds?" (v. 16), "Can you, like him, spread out the skies?" (v. 18). These questions anticipate the YHWH speeches by shape: chapters 38-41 will be one long rhetorical question, and Elihu in chapter 37 teaches Job the genre of the coming divine interrogation.

The literary devices are dense. Verse 5's "God thunders wondrously with his voice" is a synthetic parallel that repeats rather than balances — the second clause amplifies rather than counters the first. Verse 7's "he seals up the hand of every man" uses the image of a wax seal to describe winter's immobilization of human labor. Verse 13's triad — "whether for correction or for his land or for love" — names three possible purposes of weather with studied ambiguity; the storm could be *shebet* (rod of discipline), *eretz* (for the land's fertility), or *chesed* (for steadfast love). Elihu does not commit to any one. Verse 18's metal-mirror image is the chapter's most concrete piece of ancient material culture — bronze mirrors polished to reflection were a known luxury object in Iron Age Levantine archaeology (examples from Megiddo, Lachish, and Kuntillet Ajrud). Elihu assumes the sky is that kind of solid polished surface.

The chapter's rhetorical function in the book's argument is complex. On one reading, Elihu fails — his discourse is interrupted by the actual theophany he is rehearsing, and God's speech supersedes his. On another reading, Elihu succeeds — he has prepared Job rhetorically for the register of divine address that follows, teaching him to hear divine speech in storm form. On a third reading, the juxtaposition is ironic: Elihu lectures about a God he has not met; chapter 38 brings the God he has been theologizing about into direct contact. Carol Newsom (*A Contest of Moral Imaginations*) argues that Elihu's chapter 37 is the book's great comic-ironic moment: a young man expounding divine transcendence at length just as the transcendent God is audibly approaching in the storm he is describing.

The textual situation is generally secure for chapter 37; the main difficulties are lexical (verse 11's *bri* is uncertain, verse 13's triad is compressed) rather than structural. The Septuagint of Elihu's speeches is considerably shorter than the MT in places, consistent with the broader LXX abridgment of Job (approximately 400 lines shorter than MT); the Origen Hexapla supplemented the LXX from Theodotion to produce the received Christian form.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Chapter 37 sits in the wisdom corpus but its closest canonical cousins are the storm-theophany texts: Exodus 19 (Sinai), Judges 5:4-5 (the Song of Deborah), Habakkuk 3, Psalm 18, Psalm 29, Psalm 77:16-20, Nahum 1, and ultimately Ezekiel 1 (the chariot vision). The chapter's rhetorical questions also prepare the reader for the YHWH speeches of chapters 38-41.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

**Second Temple.** The Qumran 11QTargum Job preserves chapter 37 in Aramaic with some paraphrastic expansions. The Testament of Job barely engages the Elihu material, consistent with the Hellenistic-Jewish tradition's general discomfort with Elihu's speeches. Ben Sira does not directly cite Job 37 but his own meditation on the cosmic weather (Sirach 43:1-26) draws on similar Hebrew vocabulary.

**Rabbinic.** *Bava Batra* 15b-16b treats the Elihu speeches without isolating chapter 37. The rabbis are notoriously divided on Elihu: some regard him as a prophet (Rabbi Elijah, cited in the Zohar); others treat him as a pretentious young man whose speeches add nothing. Rashi (d. 1105) follows the more favorable line, reading Elihu as sincere and his chapter 37 storm-hymn as authentic praise. Ibn Ezra is more reserved. The midrash *Tanchuma* reads Elihu as representative of the unvalidated wisdom of youth.

**Medieval.** Maimonides in *Guide for the Perplexed* III.22-23 is famously positive about Elihu. Maimonides reads Elihu's theory of suffering as pedagogical warning (articulated in 33:14-30 and elaborated throughout 36-37) as the philosophical position closest to his own refined-providence view. For Maimonides, Elihu's chapter 37 storm-hymn is not a failure but the propaedeutic to the divine speeches — it teaches the intellect to approach the cosmos with awe that will not reduce God to the friends' retributive categories. Nachmanides (Ramban) reads the chapter's *mishpat u-rov tzedaqah lo ye'aneh* ("justice and abundant righteousness he will not violate," 37:23) as the book's closest approach to orthodox theodicy from within the dialogues, and treats it with respect.

**Modern.** Avivah Zornberg in *The Murmuring Deep* reads chapter 37 as the rhetorical moment where language reaches the limit of what it can say and then keeps talking anyway — Elihu's eloquence is beautiful but futile, and the chapter's function is to show that even the best human discourse about God is outrun by God's arrival. Jon Levenson does not treat chapter 37 extensively in *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* but the storm imagery maps onto his broader framework of divine kingship expressed through combat-cosmology. James Kugel in *The Great Shift* treats the chapter's storm theology as a late articulation of the cosmic deity that is replacing the earlier "small god" of personal patronage.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

**Patristic.** Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job* (c. 595) reads Elihu as a figure of arrogant youth whose eloquence exceeds his understanding. For Gregory, chapter 37's storm-hymn is praise that is formally correct but spiritually misplaced — Elihu speaks as if he has seen what he has not. Origen fragments read Elihu more positively, and John Chrysostom's homilies on Job tend to harmonize Elihu with the friends' orthodoxy without significant critique. Jerome's Vulgate preserves Elihu faithfully but his letters occasionally echo Gregory's suspicion.

**Medieval.** Aquinas's *Expositio super Iob* (c. 1261-65) treats Elihu respectfully as articulating a provisional theological position that the divine speeches will then complete. For Thomas, Elihu's chapter 37 is a legitimate natural-theology argument — cosmic wonder as evidence of divine majesty — that Thomas himself would develop in the Five Ways. The chapter prepares for but does not substitute for the revelation that follows.

**Reformation.** Calvin's sermons on Job 37 (sermons 141-143 of the 1554-55 series) read the chapter with a mix of admiration and reserve. Calvin is pulled by the text's theological content — absolute divine sovereignty expressed through cosmic majesty — but suspicious of Elihu's authority to deliver it. Calvin's reading is: Elihu speaks truth, but he has not earned the right to speak it. Luther's treatment is sparser and generally positive: Elihu's storm-hymn is an authentic doxology.

**Critical.** Julius Wellhausen treats the Elihu speeches as a later insertion by an editor dissatisfied with the divine speeches' apparent moral ambiguity. Hermann Gunkel applies form-criticism and isolates chapter 37 as a theophanic hymn fragment. Gerhard von Rad in *Wisdom in Israel* (1970) treats Elihu as the book's editorial attempt to tame its radical theology, restoring retributive coherence that the dialogues had dismantled. Brevard Childs in *Introduction to the Old Testament as Scripture* (1979) treats the chapter canonically — whatever its origin, it now serves a specific function in the completed book as the rhetorical transition between human and divine speech.

**Contemporary.** Robert Gordis reads Elihu as the voice of systematic theological orthodoxy confronting the experience of suffering, and chapter 37 as the last attempt of that orthodoxy before the theophany displaces it. David Clines in the Word Biblical Commentary is the definitive philological treatment. Carol Newsom in *A Contest of Moral Imaginations* reads chapter 37 as the most formally brilliant speech in the book and precisely because of that brilliance, the book's most ironic moment. Susannah Ticciati treats Elihu's eloquence as one of the book's forms of linguistic failure; the chapter succeeds as poetry and fails as theology. Norman Habel's OTL commentary is structurally attentive. J. Gerald Janzen reads chapter 37 as a real, if penultimate, preparation of Job for the divine voice. David Fyall, in *Now My Eyes Have Seen You* (2002), argues for Elihu's theological adequacy against the critical consensus, reading chapter 37 as the book's own preferred theodicy.

### Where the Traditions Converge and Diverge

Both traditions recognize chapter 37 as theologically rich and rhetorically brilliant. Both acknowledge that Elihu is an unusual figure — introduced late, not mentioned in the epilogue's verdict, yet not explicitly rebuked either. The traditions diverge on whether to read Elihu favorably or skeptically. The medieval Jewish tradition, especially Maimonides, reads Elihu positively: his chapter 37 is the philosophically most adequate position the human speakers reach. The Christian tradition is more divided: Gregory suspects Elihu's arrogance, Aquinas reads him respectfully, Calvin is ambivalent, and modern critical Christian scholars (Pope, Clines) lean toward secondary-insertion skepticism. A structural divergence: Jewish tradition tends to read chapter 37 as genuinely preparing the soul for the theophany; Christian critical tradition tends to read it as the orthodoxy the theophany will displace. The evidence is genuinely ambiguous, and both readings can be defended from the text.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author of Job 37 — whether original Joban or secondary Elihu-interpolator — is building a specific rhetorical effect: a brilliant, formally complete theological hymn in the mouth of a human speaker, positioned immediately before the divine speech that will render it either superfluous or foundational. The theological vision of the chapter is that cosmic order, expressed through the storm's majesty, is the strongest evidence of divine transcendence, and that this transcendence is compatible with justice (verse 23: "justice and abundant righteousness he will not violate"). Elihu's answer to the book's theodicy problem is: God is so much greater than human categories that the apparent injustice is a problem of our measurement, not of divine character.

### Tension 1 — Is Elihu Vindicated or Superseded by Chapter 38?

This is the deepest tension in the chapter's reception, and it determines the book's overall theological shape.

**Maimonides.** Elihu is vindicated. The chapter's storm-hymn is the correct preparation for the theophany; God's speech in chapter 38 continues and elevates Elihu's theology rather than replacing it. On this reading, chapter 37 is the book's own best answer to the theodicy question, qualified only by the direct theophany that follows.

**Gregory the Great.** Elihu is superseded. The chapter is beautiful but misplaced; the theophany of chapter 38 corrects its arrogance by displacing human storm-theology with divine storm-speech. The fact that God's speech opens with *min ha-seʿarah* ("from the whirlwind") and Elihu's hymn has just concluded with storm imagery is, for Gregory, ironic — God is showing up in the register Elihu has been imagining, and the imagination was not equal to the reality.

**Calvin.** Elihu is partially vindicated. His theological content is orthodox; his authority to deliver it is provisional. Chapter 37's declarations about divine justice are true, but the believer should receive them from God's mouth in chapter 38, not from Elihu's mouth in chapter 37.

**Critical.** Newsom and Habel treat chapter 37 as rhetorically preparatory without committing to theological endorsement. The chapter teaches the reader how to hear the coming theophany but does not pre-empt it. The theophany may confirm or contest Elihu's theology; the text does not say. The epilogue's silence about Elihu — unlike the explicit rebuke of the three friends — has been read both ways.

**Steelmanning Gregory.** The reading most contemporary readers resist is the patristic suspicion of Elihu. The steelman: the book is structurally designed so that every human theological position is inadequate to the problem, and Elihu's fate is to be the most formally adequate human theology that is still human theology. The fact that God does not directly rebuke Elihu in the epilogue is not endorsement but dismissal — Elihu is beneath notice. The three friends merited rebuke because they had the status of friendship with Job; Elihu was a bystander whose speeches did not rise to the level of needing correction. On this reading, chapter 37's brilliance is precisely what makes it insufficient: the best human theology is still human.

**Where this leaves us:** The book does not settle whether Elihu is vindicated or superseded. The epilogue's silence on Elihu is as eloquent as its naming of the three friends, and both Jewish and Christian traditions have defensibly read that silence either way.

### Tension 2 — Can Divine Justice Be Proven from Cosmic Majesty?

The chapter's closing claim — "the Almighty — we cannot find him; he is great in power; justice and abundant righteousness he will not violate" (37:23) — is the tightest articulation in the book of a specific theodicy: that divine transcendence guarantees divine justice. The tension is whether this move succeeds.

**Catholic.** Aquinas in the *Summa Theologiae* I.22.2 and I-II.19.10 argues that divine justice is not contingent on human verification; the cosmos itself is evidence of an ordered divine intelligence, and ordered intelligence implies justice. Elihu's storm-hymn is natural-theology argument in the best sense: creation's grandeur witnesses to the Creator's character. The CCC (269, 302-308) follows this line.

**Reformed.** Calvin affirms the Elihu principle but conditions it. Cosmic majesty cannot be relied upon as an independent demonstration of divine justice; the majesty establishes divine sovereignty, and divine sovereignty must be accepted by faith as just. Calvin's reading of chapter 37 is that the storm does not prove God is just; the storm reveals God is powerful, and the believer trusts that the powerful one is also the just one because Scripture says so.

**Lutheran.** The Lutheran distinction between *deus absconditus* (hidden God) and *deus revelatus* (revealed God) complicates chapter 37. Luther would say the storm-hymn shows the hidden God whose power is terrifying and inscrutable; the revealed God who is certainly just is shown only in Christ. Chapter 37 on its own cannot yield assurance of divine justice.

**Jewish.** Maimonides treats the Elihu line of argument as the culmination of the book's natural-theology argument — cosmic grandeur is genuine evidence of refined providence, even if not accessible to ordinary intellectual perception. Levenson is more skeptical: cosmic power does not yield moral assurance, and the theophany of chapters 38-41 will make this clear by failing to explicitly vindicate Job's moral standing.

**Critical.** Clines and Newsom treat the chapter's theodicy as formally complete but rhetorically undermined by the theophany that follows. God in chapters 38-41 does not argue from cosmic majesty to divine justice in the way Elihu does; God argues from cosmic majesty to human ignorance, which is not the same thing.

**Steelmanning the Reformed reading.** The reading most modern readers resist is Calvin's: that cosmic majesty yields divine sovereignty but not divine justice, and the latter must be received by faith. The steelman: the theophany of chapters 38-41 refuses to argue for God's justice. God speaks at length about God's power and at no point claims to be just. If Elihu's chapter 37 move — inferring justice from majesty — were endorsed, we would expect God to repeat it in chapter 38. God does not. Chapter 37's move, therefore, is the human attempt to derive an answer the divine speech declines to give. Faith, not cosmology, must bridge the gap.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter articulates a theodicy that every tradition takes seriously, but that the book itself does not clearly endorse. The book's divine speeches decline to make the Elihu move, which is either a silent endorsement by continuation or a silent supersession.

### One Unresolved Question

If the voice in the thunder is God's, and if Elihu has spent a whole chapter telling Job to listen for it — then when the voice actually comes, will it say what Elihu predicted? Or is there something about God's speech that cannot be rehearsed before it arrives?

## The Thread

- **Psalm 29** — "the voice of the LORD is over the waters; the God of glory thunders." Psalm 29 is the pure Hebrew storm-hymn, probably the closest poetic cousin to Job 37. Scholars (Frank Moore Cross, Mitchell Dahood) have argued Psalm 29 was originally a Baal hymn repurposed for YHWH. When you read Psalm 29, remember that Job 37 is working with the same inherited material.
- **1 Kings 19:11-13** — Elijah at Horeb. "The LORD was not in the wind... not in the earthquake... not in the fire... and after the fire the sound of a low whisper." 1 Kings 19 is the Hebrew Bible's most pointed critique of storm-god theology. Where Job 37 assumes God is in the thunder, 1 Kings 19 says he is not. When you reach 1 Kings, remember that Job 37's theology is one Hebrew option; Elijah's vision is another.
- **Exodus 19:16-19** — Sinai. "There were thunders and lightnings and a thick cloud on the mountain and a very loud trumpet blast... the whole mountain trembled greatly." The archetypal Hebrew theophany. Job 37 is preparing the reader for a divine-speech-from-storm experience that the book's chapter 38 will deliver in explicitly Sinai-type terms (though for one person, not a nation). When you read Exodus 19, remember that the same storm imagery will return in Job.
- **Revelation 4:5** — "From the throne came flashes of lightning, and rumblings and peals of thunder." The Apocalypse draws on the same storm-theophany vocabulary that Job 37 uses. When you reach Revelation, notice how the throne-vision of chapter 4 is deploying language that Elihu has already prepared in Job — and how the Apocalypse, unlike Job, will explicitly identify the thunders with divine speech (Revelation 10:3-4).
