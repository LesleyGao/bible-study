# Genesis 13: The Parting of Abram and Lot

---

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political landscape.** If we locate Abram in Middle Bronze I–IIA (c. 2000–1800 BC, following Albright's reconstruction, refined by Kitchen and Dever), Canaan in this period was a patchwork of small city-states — Hebron, Shechem, Jerusalem (Urusalim in the later Execration Texts), Hazor — with substantial pastoral populations in the hill country and Negev. Egypt under the late Middle Kingdom (12th Dynasty, Amenemhet and Senusret pharaohs) exerted cultural and trade influence but not direct rule. The Execration Texts (c. 1900–1800 BC), which curse Canaanite chieftains by name, give us actual Semitic rulers in the very regions Abram traverses. The "Canaanites and Perizzites" (v. 7) is a standard merism; the *Prizzî* (פְּרִזִּי) likely refers to rural, unwalled village-dwellers (from *perāzôt*, "open settlements") as opposed to the walled Canaanite cities — exactly the demographic mix archaeology confirms for MB I.

**Material culture.** Abram is a *tent-dwelling transhumant pastoralist* with substantial wealth in flocks — a familiar type attested in the Mari letters (18th c. BC), which document tribal groups like the Binu-Yamina ("sons of the right/south") moving between pasturage and settled zones, often in tension with city-based herdsmen. The "strife" of v. 7 is not petty bickering but the central socioeconomic problem of pastoral life: carrying capacity. The Negev and Judean hills can support only so many animals per dunam; when two large herds try to share wells and grazing, violence is the default outcome. Nuzi tablets (15th c. BC, but reflecting older customs) document elaborate legal arrangements for exactly this situation.

Abram's wealth in "silver and gold" (v. 2) — portable, weighable metal before coinage (which arrives only in the 7th c. BC Lydia) — reflects the Egyptian sojourn of chapter 12. Pharaoh's gifts have made him a man of substance.

**Authorship & dating of the text.** Classical documentary criticism (Wellhausen, refined by von Rad and Noth) assigns most of Genesis 13 to **J (Yahwist)** — note the consistent use of YHWH (יהוה), the anthropomorphic deity who "said to Abram," the vivid narrative style, the southern (Judahite) interest in Hebron and Mamre. Verses 6 and 11b–12a are often assigned to **P (Priestly)** for their formulaic land-and-property language (*rĕkûš*, "possessions," is a P word). More recent scholarship (Van Seters, Rendtorff, Schmid) has challenged the neat J source, arguing for exilic or later composition of the patriarchal narratives — stories told by a people without land, projecting promise backward. Whatever the date of final composition (likely 9th–6th c. BC), the material culture embedded in the story — tent pastoralism, the prominence of Bethel and Hebron, the Perizzite designation — preserves older tradition.

**Archaeological touchpoints.** Bethel (Beitin) shows MB occupation. Ai (et-Tell) is the famous problem: excavated by Judith Marquet-Krause and Joseph Callaway, it shows a gap in occupation from c. 2400 to 1200 BC — there was nothing there in Abram's time or Joshua's. This has fueled debate about the historicity of the patriarchal itineraries; one response (Livingston, Bimson) is that biblical Ai is a different site. Hebron/Mamre: the terebinth sanctuary at Ramat el-Khalil has Iron Age and later remains, but the tradition of patriarchal worship there is ancient.

**Key terms.**

- **רִיב (*rîb*)**, "strife" (v. 7). More than squabble — this is the technical Hebrew term for a legal dispute, a covenant lawsuit. When prophets later indict Israel, they use *rîb*. The word plants the theme: kin are meant to adjudicate, not litigate.
- **כִּכַּר הַיַּרְדֵּן (*kikkar ha-yardēn*)**, "the Jordan Valley/Plain" (v. 10). *Kikkar* means "round" or "disk" — a round loaf of bread, a talent-weight, and here the circular basin of the lower Jordan. Lot chooses the round, fertile disk; the geography is made to feel like a coin being flipped.
- **כְּגַן־יְהוָה (*kĕgan-YHWH*)**, "like the garden of the LORD" (v. 10). The narrator explicitly evokes Eden. But the next clause — *"before YHWH destroyed Sodom"* — turns Eden into a site awaiting catastrophe. The verbal echo is doing theological work.
- **נָשָׂא עֵינָיו (*nāśāʾ ʿênāyw*)**, "lifted up his eyes" (vv. 10, 14). Lot does it, then YHWH commands Abram to do it. Same verb, radically different outcomes. Hebrew narrative often works through these twin verbs.
- **לְעֹלָם (*lĕʿôlām*)**, "forever" (v. 15). Not necessarily "eternal" in a metaphysical sense; closer to "for the indefinite future, for all generations to come." But the word carries enormous weight in later covenantal and Zionist discourse.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

**Genre.** This is **patriarchal saga** — an ancestral narrative with etiological and theological functions, structured around movement, altar-building, and divine speech. Robert Alter identifies it as a **type-scene** of *separation*: the larger family splits, one line continues the covenant, the other drifts toward danger. (We'll see it again with Isaac/Ishmael, Jacob/Esau.)

**Structure.** The chapter is a tight chiasm around Lot's choice:

- A. Abram returns to the altar at Bethel, calls on YHWH (vv. 1–4)
  - B. The problem: too much wealth, strife (vv. 5–7)
    - C. Abram's offer: "Separate yourself" (vv. 8–9)
      - D. **Lot lifts his eyes** and chooses (vv. 10–11)
    - C'. The separation occurs; Lot toward Sodom (vv. 12–13)
  - B'. YHWH's speech: wealth of *offspring*, not livestock (vv. 14–16)
- A'. Abram moves to Hebron, builds an altar (vv. 17–18)

**Literary devices.**

- **Inclusio by altar** (vv. 4, 18): the chapter begins and ends at an altar, framing the human drama with worship.
- **Ironic juxtaposition**: v. 10's "like the garden of YHWH, like the land of Egypt" is brilliantly double-edged. Eden and Egypt — one is paradise lost, the other is the house of slavery. Lot sees a *garden*; the narrator has already told us (the reader) it's Sodom. The narrator's parenthesis "(This was before the LORD destroyed Sodom…)" is a dramatic aside straight to the audience.
- **Eye-lifting parallelism**: Lot lifts his eyes toward fertile land and moves east (v. 10–11); Abram is *told* by YHWH to lift his eyes in all four directions (v. 14). Going "east" (*qēdĕmāh*) in Genesis is almost always regressive — Adam east of Eden, Cain east, Babel east. Abram, by contrast, is given the compass rose.
- **Dust motif** (v. 16): counters 12:2 ("I will make of you a great nation") with a new image. Dust = innumerability but also = the curse on the serpent and the "dust thou art" of 3:19. The blessing subverts the curse.
- **Intertextual echo** (v. 17, "walk through the length and the breadth"): legal symbolic possession. In Mesopotamian and later rabbinic law, walking the boundaries constitutes a claim (*ḥazaqah*). Abram is deeded the land by ritual perambulation.

**Location in argument.** Chapter 12 gave the promise; 12:10–20 immediately threatened it (Sarai in Pharaoh's harem, the mother of the nation endangered). Chapter 13 is the *recovery*: Abram returns to the altar he first built (v. 4), recommits, and receives an expanded promise. Chapter 14 will then test Abram's relationship with the Lot who just walked away. The chapter sets up Sodom (chs. 18–19) and the covenant ceremony (ch. 15). In literary terms, Genesis 13 is the hinge.

**Textual variants.** Relatively minor here. The Samaritan Pentateuch harmonizes some geographical descriptors. LXX reads smoothly. 4QGen fragments confirm the MT in substance. The most interesting variant is v. 18, where some LXX traditions read "the oak [singular] of Mamre" vs. MT's plural "oaks" (*ʾēlōnê*) — affecting whether we picture one sacred tree or a grove.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**The canonical arc.** Genesis 13 is a foundation stone of the land promise — arguably *the* foundation, since 12:7 was brief and 15 is the ratifying covenant. The land of Canaan, given "forever," becomes the hinge of the entire Hebrew Bible: entering it (Joshua), keeping it (Kings), losing it (2 Kings 25), longing for it (Lamentations, Ezekiel, Isaiah 40–66), returning to it (Ezra-Nehemiah). The New Testament then *universalizes* the promise: Paul in Romans 4:13 says the promise was that Abraham would be "heir of the *world* [*kosmos*]" — notably expanding beyond Canaan.

### Jewish interpretive trajectory

**Second Temple.** *Jubilees* 13 retells the chapter with added piety: Abram tithes (anticipating Melchizedek), and Lot's choice is framed as a moral failing. Philo (*De Abrahamo* 208–216) allegorizes: Lot represents the soul attached to the visible and sensual; Abram represents the soul that chooses the invisible and virtuous. The separation is *psychagogic* — the mind departing from the passions. Josephus (*Antiquities* 1.169–170) rationalizes: Abram's generosity in letting Lot choose first shows his noble character.

**Rabbinic.** *Bereshit Rabbah* 41 reads v. 11 ("Lot journeyed east," *mi-qedem*) hyper-literally: Lot "journeyed from the Ancient One" (*qadmôn*, a divine epithet) — he took leave of God. The same midrash draws a sharp contrast: Lot saw with physical eyes (v. 10), Abram was shown by God (v. 14); human vision is myopic, divine vision is panoramic. The Talmud (*Bava Batra* 100a) derives the halakhah of *ḥazaqah* — acquisition by walking — from v. 17.

**Medieval.** **Rashi** (11th c.) glosses "well watered everywhere" as a hint of Lot's libertinism: the region was "well-watered" in licentiousness, like the land of Egypt in its depravity. **Ibn Ezra** is more sober, focused on the geography and syntax. **Nachmanides (Ramban)** reads the chapter as foundational: Abram's walking the land (v. 17) is a *performative act* that secures the inheritance for his descendants — the patriarchs' deeds sign and seal the promise (*maʿaśeh avot siman la-banim*).

**Modern.** **Martin Buber** (*On the Bible*) reads the "lift up your eyes" sequence as the difference between grasping and receiving; Lot grasps, Abram receives. **Jon Levenson** (*The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*, and his work on the land promise) stresses that the land grant here is *unconditional* — a pure gift, contrasted with the Sinai covenant's conditionality. **Avivah Zornberg** (*The Beginning of Desire*) reads the chapter psychoanalytically: Abram's capacity to relinquish (offering Lot first choice) is what makes him capable of receiving the expanded promise; Lot's grasping forecloses him from blessing.

### Christian interpretive trajectory

**Patristic.** **Origen** (*Homilies on Genesis* 4) allegorizes aggressively: Abram and Lot are two dispositions of the soul; Lot's descent toward Sodom is the soul's descent into carnality. **Augustine** (*City of God* 16.20) reads it as the separation of the two cities — Abram's city-of-God choosing pilgrimage, Lot's city-of-man choosing the fertile plain that becomes fire. **Chrysostom** (*Homilies on Genesis* 33) moralizes on Abram's magnanimity: the uncle yields to the nephew, modeling Christian humility.

**Medieval.** **Aquinas** (*ST* I-II.102.6) treats the altar-building as a natural-law act of worship prior to ceremonial Torah; Abram's religion is the paradigm of the *via naturae*. The *Glossa Ordinaria* layers Augustine's two-cities reading with typology: Abram's altar at Mamre prefigures the Church, Sodom prefigures the world awaiting judgment.

**Reformation.** **Luther** (*Lectures on Genesis*) is extraordinary here — 40+ pages on ch. 13. He reads Abram as the paradigm of faith enduring *through* domestic conflict: the true saint lives not in ecstatic vision but in the grubby work of herdsmen-quarrels. **Calvin** (*Commentary on Genesis*) emphasizes providence: Lot's choice seems prudent but leads to ruin; Abram's deference seems imprudent but inherits the land. God reverses human calculations.

**Historical-critical.** Wellhausen separated the sources (above). **Gerhard von Rad** (*Genesis*, OTL) reads the chapter as J's theology of promise under threat: the land seems inadequate (not enough pasture), but God's word enlarges it. **Claus Westermann**'s massive *Genesis* commentary emphasizes the *itinerary* structure — Abram's life is a series of stations, each marked by altar and word. **Brevard Childs** reads it canonically: the chapter must be heard alongside the final form's trajectory toward exile and return.

**Contemporary.** **Walter Brueggemann** (*Genesis*, Interpretation) reads the land promise as scandalously particular — God binds God's self to a specific territory, and this particularity resists every attempt to spiritualize it away. **John Goldingay** treats Abram's generosity as *diplomatic* more than pious: kin-conflict in the ancient Near East could escalate to blood feud; Abram prevents it. **Robert Alter** and **Meir Sternberg** note Sarai's silence across a chapter where enormous decisions about her future are made by men — a narrator's reticence that the next several chapters will let press on the reader. And the chapter itself raises the uncomfortable question that v. 7 will not let go of: what of the Canaanites and Perizzites already dwelling in the land — to whom the land is promised without their consent?

### Convergences and divergences

Both traditions converge on Abram as exemplar of faith and Lot as cautionary figure. Both find the "lift up your eyes" contrast theologically fertile.

They diverge on three fundamental points:

1. **Scope of the land promise.** Jewish tradition reads the *lĕʿôlām* ("forever") of v. 15 as an enduring, non-abrogated claim on the physical land — foundational for later Zionist and halakhic thought. Christian tradition, especially after Paul (Romans 4:13, Hebrews 11:9–16 — "he looked forward to a city whose designer and builder is God"), tends to spiritualize and universalize: the promise is of the *kosmos*, or of the heavenly country, and the physical land is typological.

2. **Status of Abram's works.** Medieval Christianity (and then Reformation) debates whether Abram's altar-building, generosity, and obedience are merits or fruits of faith. Rabbinic tradition has no such anxiety: Abram's deeds *are* his faithfulness, and they bind blessing to his descendants (*zekhut avot*, the merit of the fathers).

3. **Lot's trajectory.** Christian typology tends to flatten Lot into "worldly soul" or, conversely, rehabilitate him via 2 Peter 2:7 ("righteous Lot"). Rabbinic tradition is more ambivalent — Lot is flawed but genealogically vital (Ruth the Moabite descends from him, and thus David, and thus — for Christians — Jesus). The rabbis can hold the complexity.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**What is God up to?** The author(s) portray God as the *sovereign land-giver* and *promise-enlarger*. The theological grammar of the chapter is: (1) human restraint and worship (Abram at the altar, Abram ceding choice); (2) human grasping and drift (Lot's eye-lifting, eastward movement toward Sodom); (3) divine response in which the yielded land is re-given, expanded, and secured by ritual walking. The vision is that God's promises *survive and grow through* loss — Abram loses his nephew-heir and gains innumerable offspring; he cedes the lush plain and gains the whole land. The logic is cruciform in shape, though the author would not have used that word.

The altar at Hebron is not incidental. Mamre becomes Abram's home base (14:13, 18:1), and Hebron becomes the burial site of the patriarchs (ch. 23), the first capital of David (2 Sam 2), and a theologically charged piece of ground ever after. The chapter is establishing *holy geography*.

### Theological tension 1: Is the land promise unconditional and perpetual?

- **Jewish (Rashi, Nachmanides, Levenson).** Yes. The Abrahamic covenant is a *bĕrît ʿôlām*, a perpetual grant. Exile is punishment within the covenant, not abrogation of it. Levenson argues this is a "royal grant" covenant (like Hittite parallels), given without conditions on the recipient's behavior — distinct from the Sinai covenant.

- **Catholic (CCC 762, *Nostra Aetate*, Pope John Paul II).** The Church increasingly affirms that "God's covenant with the Jewish people is irrevocable" (quoting Romans 11:29). The CCC holds a both/and: the promise is fulfilled spiritually in the Church while Israel's election is not revoked. The territorial dimension is largely left bracketed.

- **Protestant — Reformed (covenant theology, classical).** The land promise is typological and fulfilled in Christ and the new creation; physical territory has no ongoing theological claim. **Dispensationalist Protestant** (Scofield, Ryrie, Chafer). The land promise remains literally in force for ethnic Israel; contemporary Zionism is its partial fulfillment. These two Protestant camps are 180 degrees apart.

- **Critical/academic.** The promise functions rhetorically to legitimate ancient Israel's claim against rival populations; it is a theological construct of later (often post-exilic) communities seeking to ground identity.

- **Steelman for dispensationalism** (the view I find least intuitive): If words like *lĕʿôlām* and "to your offspring" mean anything stable, and if the biblical narrative portrays God as keeping promises across catastrophes, then allegorizing the land out of the promise is an interpretive evasion. The text *insists* on specific territory. A consistent reading honors that specificity. The weakness: the same hermeneutic applied elsewhere produces incoherence, and the New Testament itself reads Abrahamic promise universally (Rom 4:13, Gal 3).

### Theological tension 2: Is Abram's yielding a virtue or a gamble?

- **Classical Christian (Chrysostom, Calvin).** Virtue — magnanimity, trust in providence. Abram models discipleship: lose your life to find it.

- **Rabbinic (mixed).** Pragmatism more than piety. Kin-strife threatened both men; Abram acts as elder to defuse it. The "virtue" language maps awkwardly onto rabbinic ethics, which prize *wise action* over abstract humility.

- **Modern critical (Brueggemann, Goldingay).** Political realism. Abram is the senior figure and could have commanded; instead he offers parity. This is statesmanship, and it is also theologically significant: the bearer of promise refuses coercion.

- **Narratological (Alter, Sternberg).** Whatever it is, it is a decision made by men over a household that includes women and servants whose lives are completely rearranged without voice — the narrator does not soften that, and the reader is meant to feel it.

- **Steelman for the pragmatic reading**: The romantic reading of Abram-as-self-emptying-saint obscures the hard truth that faith usually looks like competent, prudent action under pressure. Calling it "virtue" risks turning the narrative into moralism; calling it "shrewdness" keeps it honest and imitable.

**The ethical difficulty.** Verse 7 names the Canaanites and Perizzites as *already there*. Verse 15 promises their land to Abram's offspring *forever*. This is the text that colonial and settler movements have cited, and it is the text that has been cited against Palestinians in the 20th and 21st centuries. Any serious reader must sit with this. Responses across the spectrum:

- Some (Brueggemann, later work) argue the Bible itself relativizes its own land claims (Leviticus 25:23 — "the land is mine; you are sojourners"), so that the gift is always held under conditions of justice and never as absolute possession.
- Some (Levenson) insist the promise is irreducibly particular and that universalizing readings are their own form of violence against Jewish identity.
- Some (critical scholars) argue the whole construct is a literary artifact projected backward to justify what ancient Israel had already done.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter gives us a God who works through yielding and whose promises expand in the space left by human restraint — and, in the same breath, a promise of land over people already living there. The text refuses to resolve that tension, and neither should we pretend to.

**One question that lands:** Lot lifted his eyes and chose what looked like paradise; Abram was told to lift his eyes only after he had let go. When in your life have you confused what you *saw* with what you were being *given* — and would you know the difference before the fire fell?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 18:1** — "The LORD appeared to Abraham by the oaks of Mamre…" The altar Abram builds in 13:18 becomes the site of the great theophany and the announcement of Isaac. Where Abram built in response to promise, God comes to meet him. The Mamre ground matters.

- **Genesis 19:24–28** — Abraham looks toward Sodom and sees smoke rising "like the smoke of a furnace." The *kikkar* that was "like the garden of YHWH" (13:10) has become its inverse. The narrator's parenthesis in 13:10 ("before YHWH destroyed Sodom") is paid off here.

- **Deuteronomy 34:1–4** — Moses on Mount Nebo, told by YHWH to "lift up your eyes" and see the land he will not enter. The verbal echo of Gen 13:14 is exact. Moses sees what Abram was promised; neither man possesses it in his lifetime. The promise runs on ahead of every bearer.

- **Romans 4:13 (flag for later reading)** — Paul says the promise to Abraham and his offspring was that he would be "heir of the *world*." When you get there, remember that the chapter you just read specified Canaan from Dan to the Negev — and ask what it meant for Paul to read *that* as *kosmos*. The move is audacious and contested, and it is where Jewish and Christian readings of Genesis 13 finally part ways.
