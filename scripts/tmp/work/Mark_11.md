# Mark 11: The King Enters, the Temple Falls

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Mark 11 stages a calculated provocation. Jesus enters Jerusalem from the east — descending the Mount of Olives via the road from Bethany and Bethphage — at precisely the moment when, on the western side of the city, Pontius Pilate would have been making his Passover-week entry from Caesarea Maritima with auxiliary troops to maintain order during the festival. **Marcus Borg** and **John Dominic Crossan** (*The Last Week*, 2006), drawing on Josephus and the social-historical reconstruction of festival-week Jerusalem, argue that Jesus's eastern entry was a deliberate counter-procession to the Roman cavalry's western entry: peasant prophet on a donkey colt versus imperial governor on warhorse. Whether Mark intends this parallel as sharply as Borg/Crossan claim is debated (Joel Marcus, AB, treats it as plausible context but not Mark's explicit theme), but the geographical staging — a king arriving at his city — is unmistakable.

The **Mount of Olives** carried specific eschatological weight. Zechariah 14:4 declared that "**on that day his feet shall stand on the Mount of Olives**," when the LORD comes to fight against the nations. Pilgrims approaching Jerusalem from Jericho would crest the Olivet ridge and see, for the first time, the Temple Mount across the Kidron Valley — a sight Josephus (*War* 5.5.6) describes as one of the most stunning architectural views in the ancient world. **Bethany** and **Bethphage** were small villages on the eastern slope; archaeology has identified Bethany with modern al-Eizariya (Arabic for "Lazarus"), where 4th-century pilgrim accounts (Egeria, c. 384 CE) record a tomb-veneration tradition. Bethphage's exact location remains contested.

The ***pōlon*** (πῶλον, "colt," 11:2) is significant. Mark says only "a colt"; Matthew 21:2 specifies "a donkey... and a colt with her" to fit Zechariah 9:9's parallelism. Mark assumes readers will catch the Zechariah echo without flagging it (Mark is generally less explicit than Matthew about Old Testament fulfillment). The detail "**on which no one has ever sat**" (11:2) carries cultic significance: Numbers 19:2 specifies an unyoked heifer for the red-heifer purification rite, 1 Samuel 6:7 specifies an unused cart for the ark's return, Deuteronomy 21:3 specifies an unworked heifer for the unsolved-murder rite. Mark is signaling sacred consecration.

The crowd's shout — ***Hōsanna*** (ὡσαννά, 11:9-10) — is the Greek transliteration of the Hebrew-Aramaic ***hoshia-na*** (הושיעה־נא, "**save now!**" or "**save, please!**"). The cry comes directly from **Psalm 118:25**, part of the **Hallel** (Psalms 113-118), the festival liturgy chanted at Passover, Pentecost, Tabernacles, Hanukkah, and the new moon. Originally a desperate prayer for divine deliverance ("save now!"), by the 1st century *Hosanna* had partially evolved into an exclamation of praise (similar to "Hallelujah"). The Mishnah (m. Sukkah 4.5) records that worshipers at Tabernacles waved palm branches (***lulav***) while chanting Hallel and shouting "Hosanna." Mark's combination — "Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord! Blessed is the coming kingdom of our father David!" — fuses Psalm 118 with explicit Davidic-messianic acclamation that Psalm 118 itself does not contain.

The **Temple** Jesus enters was Herod's massive expansion (begun 20-19 BCE, still under construction in Jesus's day per John 2:20, completed only c. 63 CE — just before its destruction in 70 CE). Excavations under **Benjamin Mazar** (1968-78) and the **Temple Mount Sifting Project** (Gabriel Barkay, 2004-present) have recovered massive Herodian ashlars (some weighing over 500 tons), the **Trumpeting Place** inscription, the **Soreg** warning inscription forbidding Gentiles on pain of death, and tens of thousands of small finds including coins, jewelry, and worship implements. The **Court of the Gentiles** — the outermost court, where the money-changers operated — was a vast paved plaza 35 acres in extent.

The **money-changers** exchanged Roman, Greek, and Tyrian coinage for the **Tyrian shekel**, the only coinage accepted for the Temple tax (Exodus 30:13-15) because of its high silver content (about 94% pure). The irony — that the Temple required pagan Tyrian coinage bearing the image of **Melqart** (the Phoenician Heracles) — was widely noted. **Pigeons** (or doves) were the offering of the poor (Leviticus 5:7, 12:8 — Joseph and Mary's offering at Jesus's presentation, Luke 2:24); their sale in the Temple courts targeted the most economically vulnerable worshipers. Jesus's overturning the **tables** (***trapezas***, τραπέζας — the term gives us the modern Greek word for bank) and the **seats** of the dove-sellers is a small-scale prophetic enactment, not an attempted coup; the Temple police and Roman garrison at the Antonia Fortress would have crushed any actual takeover instantly. Yarbro Collins (Hermeneia) treats it as a symbolic prophetic act in the tradition of Jeremiah's broken jar (Jeremiah 19) and Ezekiel's siege models (Ezekiel 4).

Three Greek terms anchor the chapter. ***Kataluma*** does not appear here, but ***hierou*** (ἱεροῦ, the Temple complex) does — the broader sanctuary precinct including the courts, distinct from ***naos*** (the inner sanctuary). Jesus operates in the ***hieron***; only priests entered the ***naos***. ***Spēlaion lēstōn*** (σπήλαιον λῃστῶν, 11:17) — "**den of robbers**" — quotes Jeremiah 7:11 directly. ***Lēstēs*** is the term Josephus uses for armed insurgents and bandits, also the term used for the men crucified with Jesus (Mark 15:27). The vocabulary of ***lēstai*** is politically loaded: a "den of robbers" is a hideout for armed insurrectionists, and Jesus is implying that the Temple authorities have made the sanctuary a base for the kind of nationalist-violent messianism that will indeed bring Roman destruction in 70 CE. ***Sykēs*** (συκῆς, fig tree, 11:13) — the cursed fig tree — connects to a thick prophetic tradition (Hosea 9:10, Micah 7:1, Jeremiah 8:13, Joel 1:7) of the fig tree as Israel.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Mark 11 contains the gospel's most carefully constructed **intercalation** (Markan sandwich): the cursing of the fig tree (11:12-14), the cleansing of the temple (11:15-19), and the withered fig tree (11:20-25). The structural logic is interpretively decisive — the fig tree action **interprets** the temple action. **Joel Marcus (AB)**, **Adela Yarbro Collins (Hermeneia)**, **Edwin Broadhead** (*Prophet, Son, Messiah*), and **Ched Myers** (*Binding the Strong Man*) all converge: Mark is reading the temple-cleansing not as **reform** but as **prophetic judgment**. The fig tree, found leafy but fruitless and cursed permanently, is the temple — externally impressive, religiously sterile, and now under prophetic sentence. The temple will not be reformed; it will fall. Mark is writing in the 60s CE, on the eve of or in the wake of the temple's destruction, and he reads Jesus's prophetic action as the foundation of his theology of the temple's end.

The detail that "**it was not the season for figs**" (***ou gar ēn kairos sykōn***, 11:13) has generated centuries of puzzlement. C.S. Lewis famously called it "the most disturbing miracle" in the gospels; Bertrand Russell used it as evidence of Jesus's bad temper. The interpretive tradition divides:

- **Literalist apologetic reading** (Augustine, partly): early figs (***paggim***, the small green precursor figs) appeared on fig trees with leaves; their absence indicated genuine fruitlessness, and Jesus's curse was instrumental, not capricious.
- **Allegorical-symbolic reading** (most patristic and modern critical): the action is a prophetic sign-act; the question of "season" is irrelevant because the meaning is symbolic — Israel/the temple has leaves but no fruit.
- **Eschatological reading** (Marcus, Yarbro Collins, Wright): "it was not the season" signals that the time is **out of joint** — the messianic season has arrived, and yet there is no fruit.

The **fig tree withering "to its roots"** (11:20) — Mark, alone among the gospels, gives the curse a 24-hour delayed effect, framing the temple action between the curse and its fulfillment. Matthew (21:18-22) collapses the timing into a single immediate withering and loses Mark's intercalation.

The "**moves mountains**" saying (11:23) is structurally weighted. Joel Marcus argues that "**this mountain**" likely refers to **the Temple Mount** itself, viewed from the Mount of Olives across the Kidron Valley. Faith that moves "this mountain" is faith that bears witness to the temple's removal. The saying is hyperbole, but its situated geography matters.

**Mary Ann Tolbert** (*Sowing the Gospel*) reads the **authority question** (11:27-33) as Mark's narrative climax of the chief-priestly opposition. Jesus's counter-question about John's baptism is not evasion but pedagogy: the chief priests' refusal to render judgment on John reveals that they will not exercise the very authority they claim. The pericope ends in a stalemate that is itself a verdict: the Temple authorities have forfeited the moral right to interrogate Jesus.

A textual issue: many manuscripts include verse 26 ("**But if you do not forgive, neither will your Father in heaven forgive your trespasses**"), a clear harmonization to Matthew 6:15. NA28 omits it; modern translations (ESV, NIV, NRSV) skip from 25 to 27. The shorter reading is supported by the earliest witnesses (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus).

***Euthys*** (εὐθύς, "immediately") appears at 11:2 and 11:3 — a Markan signature even in this carefully staged royal-entry sequence; Jesus is *urgent* even in calmness. The historical present surfaces at 11:1, 11:7, 11:15. Mark's narrative tempo is preserved.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The **triumphal entry** has been read along two divergent canonical-theological trajectories.

The **politically deflationary Christian reading** (Augustine, much medieval tradition): the donkey signals **humility** rather than royalty; Jesus is the meek king, not the conquering one; the crowd's expectations are misplaced and Jesus does not endorse them. **Bonhoeffer** (*Discipleship*) reads the entry as the king of suffering deliberately refusing the crown of triumph.

The **politically substantive reading** (N.T. Wright, *Jesus and the Victory of God*; Yoder, *Politics of Jesus*; Borg/Crossan, *The Last Week*; Ched Myers, *Binding the Strong Man*): Jesus is making a real political claim — he is the messianic king, fulfilling Zechariah 9:9 — but the kingship he embodies is a different **kind** of power than Roman or Davidic-restorationist messianism. The donkey is not deflation; it is **redefinition**. The procession is genuinely royal but reorders the meaning of royalty. **Wright** in particular insists that the deflationary reading evacuates Jesus's politics; the king really did enter the city and the city really did kill him for it.

The **Jewish reception** of Hosanna and Psalm 118 is rich: the **Hallel** continues to be chanted at Passover and other festivals. **Geza Vermes** (*Jesus the Jew*) treats the entry as evidence that Jesus accepted, at minimum, eschatological-prophetic acclamation; whether he accepted explicit Davidic-messianic claims is harder to determine. **Daniel Boyarin** (*Jewish Gospels*) argues that the Markan staging is intelligible only against a fully Jewish messianic-eschatological framework; this is not yet a Christianized scene.

The **temple action** has generated centuries of confessional reading.

The patristic and medieval Christian tradition read it as the founding act of Jesus's authority over religious institutions. **Origen** (*Commentary on Matthew*) treats it allegorically: the temple is the soul, and Jesus drives out the polluting passions. **Augustine** (*Tractates on John* 10) reads it both literally (as historical event) and allegorically (as ecclesial purification). **Aquinas** treats it as evidence of Jesus's **zeal** and his authority as priest-king. **Calvin** (*Harmony*) and the Reformers read it as the founding warrant for Reformation: the church is to be cleansed of its commercial accretions; Tetzel and indulgence-sellers are the modern money-changers.

The **20th-century critical-historical trajectory** redirected the reading. **E.P. Sanders** (*Jesus and Judaism*, 1985) argued influentially that the temple action was not a "cleansing" but a **prophetic destruction-prophecy**: Jesus was not reforming the temple's commercial practices (which were necessary for sacrificial worship) but enacting its end. The animals had to be sold for sacrifice; the money had to be changed for the temple tax; these were not corruptions but practical necessities. Sanders argued that the action is Jesus's most historically certain provocation and the proximate cause of his execution. **Marcus** (AB), **Yarbro Collins** (Hermeneia), and **Wright** (*Jesus and the Victory of God*) largely follow Sanders's redirection: Mark is reading the action as **judgment-prophecy**, not reform.

**Ched Myers** (*Binding the Strong Man*) extends Sanders politically: the temple was not just a religious institution but the economic and political center of 1st-century Judean society; Jesus's action is a direct attack on the **temple-state** that was extracting tithes, taxes, and offerings from the rural peasantry. The intercalation with the cursed fig tree marks the entire economic-religious system as fruitless and under prophetic sentence.

The **Jewish counter-reading** — best articulated by **Amy-Jill Levine** (*Misunderstood Jew*) — pushes back against Christian supersessionist readings that treat the temple-action as God's rejection of Judaism. Levine notes that Jesus quotes Isaiah 56:7 ("**a house of prayer for all nations**") and Jeremiah 7:11 ("**den of robbers**") — both **internal** prophetic critiques of Israel's worship by Israelite prophets. The action is intra-Jewish reform-or-judgment discourse, not anti-Jewish polemic. Reading it otherwise has produced a long history of Christian anti-Judaism.

**The cursed fig tree** has its own reception arc. **Origen** allegorized it as the synagogue. **Bede** (*Commentary on Mark*) read it eschatologically: the fig tree is Israel before its restoration; the cursed-then-restored arc points to Romans 11. **Calvin** read it as a sign of Jesus's prophetic authority over creation. **Bonhoeffer** treated it as a parable of religious form without substance.

**Mary Douglas** (*Purity and Danger*, 1966) and the social-anthropological school read the temple action through the lens of **purity systems**: Jesus's action targets the boundary-maintaining function of the temple, not its sacrificial function — money-changing and dove-selling were boundary-mediating activities. **N.T. Wright** in *The New Testament and the People of God* and *Jesus and the Victory of God* synthesizes this with the Sanders-prophetic reading: Jesus is acting as the true temple, replacing the system that has failed.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions sit at the heart of Mark 11.

**Tension 1: What does the temple action mean — reform or replacement?** The interpretive stakes are large: the answer shapes Christian theology of Israel, of religious institutions, and of supersessionism.

**Position A (Traditional reform reading)** — Augustine, Calvin, much pre-critical exegesis, Reformation polemic — argues that Jesus is purifying the temple of its commercial corruptions; the temple is a legitimate institution that has been defiled and must be restored. The steelman: this fits the immediate narrative (Jesus quotes "**house of prayer**" approvingly), preserves continuity between Old and New Covenant worship, and avoids supersessionism.

**Position B (Sanders-Wright destruction-prophecy reading)** — Sanders, Marcus, Yarbro Collins, Wright, Myers — argues that Jesus is enacting the temple's end; the intercalation with the fig tree, the citation of Jeremiah 7 (which prophesies the temple's destruction), and the historical outcome (the temple's destruction in 70 CE) all indicate that Mark reads the action as judgment-prophecy. The steelman: this reading takes the intercalation seriously, fits the Jeremiah citation precisely (Jeremiah 7 prophesies destruction), and explains the proximate cause of Jesus's execution.

**Position C (Levine-Jewish-internal-critique reading)** — Levine, Boyarin, modern post-Holocaust biblical scholarship — argues that the action must be read as **intra-Jewish prophetic critique**, not anti-Jewish polemic; Christian readings that treat the action as God's rejection of Judaism are theologically false and historically harmful. The steelman: this reading is morally and historically necessary in the wake of Christian anti-Judaism, and exegetically defensible (Jesus's citations are from Hebrew prophets).

Where this leaves us: positions B and C are not mutually exclusive — the action can be both a destruction-prophecy of **this particular temple** and an intra-Jewish prophetic critique. Position A is exegetically thin given the intercalation. The harder question: if Mark reads the temple as under judgment, does that authorize or prohibit Christian use of analogous prophetic critique against Christian institutions? The answer shapes ecclesiology.

**Tension 2: What does "**moves mountains**" prayer mean?** The saying (11:23-24) has generated enormous pastoral confusion.

**Position A (Prosperity reading)** — Word-of-Faith movement, Kenneth Hagin, contemporary televangelism — argues that the saying is a quasi-mechanical promise: faith without doubt produces what it asks. The steelman: takes Jesus's words at face value, refuses to soften them, generates real expectant prayer.

**Position B (Conditioned-by-God's-will reading)** — Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin, mainstream Reformed/Catholic — argues that the saying must be read in the context of Jesus's broader teaching (Gethsemane: "not my will but yours"); prayer is effective in conformity with the divine will, not as mechanical acquisition. The steelman: takes the canon as a unity, refuses prosperity distortions, preserves the sovereignty of God.

**Position C (Marcus situated reading)** — argues that "**this mountain**" is the Temple Mount, and the saying is specifically about the eschatological reality that the temple's removal (which would have seemed impossible) is now genuinely available to the community of faith. The steelman: takes the geographical situation seriously, fits the chapter's overall theme, and explains the saying's hyperbole.

Where this leaves us: Position A is pastorally toxic and exegetically thin (it ignores Gethsemane). Position B is theologically sound but can collapse into a generic "your will be done" that evacuates the saying's specific force. Position C explains the saying's situated meaning but requires acknowledging that the temple is now gone — what does the saying mean for the post-temple church? Possibly: the radical possibility of removing what looks immovable in the religious-political order, by faith.

The unresolved question: what is the relationship between Jesus's "**no one ever sat**" colt (11:2 — the cultic detail of consecration) and his deliberate refusal to be enthroned in any conventional sense? Mark stages a coronation that is also an unthrone-ing, and the grammar of that paradox is left unresolved.

## The Thread

Mark 11:9-10's "**Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord!**" (Psalm 118:25-26) chains forward to Mark 12:10-11, where Jesus quotes the immediately preceding verses of the same psalm ("**The stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone**"). The crowd shouts the praise psalm; Jesus quotes its rejection psalm.

Mark 11:17's "**house of prayer for all nations**" (Isaiah 56:7) — by quoting "for all nations" specifically, Jesus signals the Gentile inclusion that will be the gospel's centrifugal trajectory. The torn temple veil at 15:38 fulfills the prophetic action.

Mark 11:23's "**this mountain**" reverberates with Mark 13:1-2 (Jesus's prophecy of the temple's destruction) and 13:14's "abomination of desolation."

Mark 11:32's "**they were afraid of the people**" anticipates Mark 12:12 and 14:1-2 — the Temple authorities' fear of the crowd structures the entire passion plot until Judas resolves it.
