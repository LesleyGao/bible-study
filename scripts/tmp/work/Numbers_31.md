# Numbers 31: You Have Let All the Women Live?

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Numbers 31 stands as one of the most ethically troubling chapters in the Hebrew Bible. Situated in the Plains of Moab section (Num 22-36), it narrates a divinely commanded war of vengeance (*nĕqāmāh*, נְקָמָה) against Midian as reprisal for the Baal-Peor apostasy narrated in Num 25, where Midianite and Moabite women led Israelite men into idolatrous sexual rites. The chapter ends Moses's active leadership: "afterward you shall be gathered to your people" (v. 2). The war is presented as a final act of the wilderness generation before the transition to Joshua.

The historical and institutional framing is dense. Phinehas ben Eleazar — not Joshua, not any tribal chief — leads the campaign, carrying "the vessels of the sanctuary and the trumpets for the alarm" (v. 6). This is a holy war (*milḥemet ḥōbāh*) with cultic apparatus at its center. The force is small and symbolic: a thousand men per tribe, twelve thousand in total, against a confederation of Midianite "cities" (v. 10) that the text presents as substantial. The five kings — Evi, Rekem, Zur, Hur, and Reba (v. 8) — appear also in Josh 13:21 as allies of Sihon of Heshbon, which has led scholars (Milgrom, Levine) to suspect an older tradition of a coalition war underlying the present priestly presentation. Balaam ben Beor is killed (v. 8), and a retrospective explanation is given in v. 16: Balaam had counseled Midian on how to corrupt Israel at Peor. This explanation sits uneasily with Num 22-24, which ends with Balaam blessing Israel and "returning to his place" (Num 24:25). The tension has generated commentary for two millennia.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels clarify the genre. The Mesha Stele (c. 840 BCE), a Moabite royal inscription, records King Mesha's campaign against Israel under a divine mandate from Chemosh, using the precise root *ḥērem* (𐤇𐤓𐤌 in Moabite) for the total devotion of conquered populations to the god: "I killed all the people of the town as a *ḥērem* to Ashtar-Chemosh." This is the closest direct linguistic parallel to biblical *ḥērem* warfare and establishes that the practice was not unique to Israel but part of a regional West Semitic ideology of sacred war. Hittite campaign annals of Suppiluliuma and Mursili show similar theology — gods command, kings execute, populations are devoted. Neo-Assyrian royal inscriptions of Tiglath-Pileser III, Sargon II, and Sennacherib use hyperbolic total-destruction language for cities that were, archaeologically, often not totally destroyed; scholars like K. Lawson Younger Jr. (*Ancient Conquest Accounts*, 1990) and Paul Copan argue the biblical idiom shares this conventional hyperbole. Egyptian campaign reports (the Merneptah Stele, Papyrus Anastasi) likewise use annihilation rhetoric.

Authorship and dating. Wellhausen and Noth assigned the chapter to P and dated it late, seeing its concern with booty division, Levite portions, and ritual purification as reflecting Second Temple priestly interests. Milgrom and Levine acknowledge priestly composition but argue the war tradition is pre-exilic, with archaic features (the Phinehas-Balaam connection, the king-list) preserved through priestly redaction. Kitchen and Hoffmeier defend a substantively early tradition. Dennis Olson notes that whatever the compositional date, the chapter functions in the canonical shape as the closing of the wilderness generation's account.

Five Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. *Nĕqāmāh* (נְקָמָה, "vengeance," vv. 2-3) — a juridical term for requital by a legitimate authority, not private revenge. *Ḥērem* (חֵרֶם) — though the word itself does not appear, the chapter's logic is clearly *ḥērem*-style total devotion, as the parallel Mesha Stele confirms. *Shālāl* (שָׁלָל, vv. 11-12, 26-27) — "plunder, spoil," the booty division a major part of the chapter. *Mekhes* (מֶכֶס, v. 28) — "tribute, tax," a rare technical term (Akkadian *miksu*) for a levy from the spoils. *Kipper* (כִּפֵּר, v. 50) — the verb of atonement; the officers bring gold "to make atonement for ourselves before the LORD," treating the survival of all twelve thousand as itself requiring ritual mediation.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Numbers 31 is a composite genre: war narrative framed by priestly legislation on post-combat purification (vv. 19-24) and booty division (vv. 25-47), with a concluding census-and-offering scene (vv. 48-54). The narrative portions (vv. 1-18) are sparse and almost stylized; the legal-statistical portions are elaborate, with precise numbers that in Hebrew narrative function symbolically as much as reportorially.

The literary structure is concentric. (1) Divine command and muster (vv. 1-6); (2) battle report — the killing of men, kings, and Balaam (vv. 7-12); (3) Moses's anger and revised orders (vv. 13-18); (4) purification rituals (vv. 19-24); (5) booty division law (vv. 25-31); (6) booty census (vv. 32-47); (7) officers' atonement offering (vv. 48-54). The center is the purification section, which reframes the war as a cultic event requiring ritual cleansing of warriors, captives, and objects. The narrative purpose is not military glory; it is the transition of a generation through a rite.

The ratio of destruction to discourse is striking. The battle itself gets two verses (7-8). The legal and purificatory aftermath gets more than forty. The text is not interested in describing combat; it is interested in what happens to combatants and spoil *after* combat. This is a characteristic priestly literary move: focus on the cultic processing of events rather than the events themselves. Compare the minimal description of the Red Sea crossing versus the elaborate legislation of Passover.

The moral centerpiece of the chapter is Moses's anger (vv. 14-15). "Have you let all the women live?" The verse is a narrative cliffhook. The commanders had followed what they took to be standard practice — kill the men, take the women and children as captives — but Moses's fury makes plain that this war is not a standard campaign of conquest. It is tied specifically to the Peor apostasy, and the women themselves were the instruments of that apostasy. Moses's orders in vv. 17-18 are, for modern readers, the chapter's horror: kill male children, kill non-virgin women, keep virgin women. The literary framing is without comment; the narrative withholds evaluation. This narrative reticence is part of what makes the chapter so difficult.

Textual variants. 4QNum preserves fragments consistent with the Masoretic text. The Samaritan Pentateuch has minor orthographic differences. The LXX follows the Hebrew closely but renders the numeric details with slight variation in two places (the exact count of captives). The Peshitta and Targum Pseudo-Jonathan add interpretive expansions: Targum Ps-Jon expands the Balaam-death narrative with folkloric details (Balaam flying, Phinehas bringing him down). The Temple Scroll (11QTᵃ) reworks the purification rules with expansions. None of the witnesses substantially mitigates the moral content.

Literary devices include the numeric structure (12,000 warriors, 675,000 sheep, 72,000 cattle, 61,000 donkeys, 32,000 captive women — all divisible by the priestly arithmetic) and the envelope of Moses and Eleazar that opens and closes the chapter. The repeated phrase "as the LORD commanded Moses" (vv. 7, 31, 41, 47, 54) insists on divine authorization — the literary move that makes the chapter's content theologically weighted and exegetically inescapable.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish reception.** Jewish tradition has wrestled with Num 31 for two millennia, never simply celebrating it. The Mishnah and Talmud treat the chapter as the locus classicus for post-battle purification (Mishnah *Oholot* 1, Bavli *Yevamot* 60b on the virgin-captive category). Bavli *Sanhedrin* 105-106 discusses Balaam extensively — his motives, his death, his place in the eschatological future (denied a share in the world to come).

Maimonides, in *Hilchot Melachim u-Milchamoteihem* (Laws of Kings and Their Wars, chapters 5-7), systematizes the halakha of war. Chapter 5 distinguishes *milḥemet mitzvah* (obligatory war) from *milḥemet reshut* (discretionary war). The Seven Nations and Amalek wars are obligatory; other wars require sanhedrin authorization and a prior offer of peace. Crucially, Maimonides (Chapter 6:1) rules: "No war, whether obligatory or discretionary, is waged until peace is offered first." If peace is accepted under specific terms (Noahide laws, taxation, service), no war is fought. Maimonides treats the Midianite war as a special case tied to Peor, not a template. The rabbinic tradition largely treats these wars as historical-specific rather than normative for later practice. Reuven Kimelman, in "War" (*Contemporary Jewish Religious Thought*), argues that the rabbinic corpus progressively narrows the applicability of biblical war-law until, in the post-Temple era, it becomes almost entirely theoretical.

Rashi on Num 31:8 notes the tradition that Balaam returned to solicit reward from Midian and was killed while plotting further harm. Rashi on 31:16 harmonizes the Num 22-24 / Num 31 tension by inserting a narrative episode between the two: after his blessings, Balaam advised Midian privately before returning home. Ibn Ezra is more reticent on the moral question, treating the chapter as historical-specific. Ramban discusses the war ethics at length: the Midianites, not Moabites, are targeted because Midian initiated the seduction at Peor; Moab receives a separate, less severe legal treatment (Deut 23:3-6). Ramban also defends Moses's order in vv. 17-18 as requital specifically of the guilty — the married women were the agents of Peor. This defense, whether accepted or not, shows the rabbinic tradition's discomfort requiring justification.

Medieval commentary. Sforno reads the chapter as a final test of Israel's fidelity; the war was not about territorial conquest but about severing the spiritual corruption of Peor. The Kabbalistic tradition, especially the Zohar, develops Balaam as an anti-Moses, a prophet of the *sitra achra* (the other side). Maimonides' *Guide for the Perplexed* treats many biblical war commands as accommodated to the moral formation of a people in a particular historical moment.

Modern Jewish readings. Milgrom in his JPS commentary acknowledges the moral difficulty directly. Nehama Leibowitz, in *Studies in Bamidbar*, reads Moses's anger as righteous indignation at commanders who had not internalized the spiritual logic of the war; but she also quietly marks the passage as difficult. Yeshayahu Leibowitz (distinct from Nehama) argues that religious Jews today cannot read this chapter as a blueprint; it belongs to a historical moment and has no direct application. Jonathan Sacks in his Numbers commentary ("Lessons in Leadership") reads the chapter primarily through Moses's grief — this is his last act, and it is not celebratory. Avivah Zornberg reads the text psychoanalytically, as the return of the repressed from Peor. Michael Walzer, in *Just and Unjust Wars* and in *In God's Shadow: Politics in the Hebrew Bible*, argues the biblical war tradition is narrowly confined, frequently criticized within the Bible itself, and does not underwrite any contemporary ideology of holy war. Reuven Kimelman treats the text as documenting an ancient institution that Judaism has progressively abandoned in practice, even while preserving in text.

**Christian reception.** Origen's *Homily on Numbers* 25 reads the chapter allegorically and thoroughly — the war is the soul's war against sinful thoughts; the women are spiritually dangerous impulses; the purification is sanctification. Origen's allegorization, while theologically generative, has the effect of dissolving the literal-historical moral problem.

Augustine addresses the question directly in *Contra Faustum* 22 and in *City of God* 1.21: commands to kill in Scripture are licit when given by God, because God is the lord of life; the prohibition "do not kill" does not apply when the one killing acts as God's agent. Augustine uses this principle to underwrite both OT war narratives and, reluctantly, the just war tradition. This argument has been the historical Christian pivot for accepting divine war commands while holding Christians individually to non-retaliation.

Medieval reception. Aquinas in *Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 40 develops the classical just war criteria (legitimate authority, just cause, right intention) but treats the biblical wars as *commanded by God* and therefore a distinct category. Aquinas is careful: Christians cannot invoke Num 31 to authorize war; they must meet the natural-law criteria of just war. Bernard of Clairvaux preaching the Second Crusade (1146) controversially drew on OT war imagery, including the language of sacred vengeance; Bernard's sermons helped launch what became a disastrous campaign. Subsequent Christian tradition has largely distanced itself from Bernard's rhetorical borrowings.

Reformation. Luther, in works like *Whether Soldiers, Too, Can Be Saved* (1526), preserves the Augustinian distinction: OT commands are not templates for Christian war, but individual Christians may serve in lawful wars. Calvin, in his *Commentary on the Last Four Books of Moses*, handles Num 31 with unusual restraint. He acknowledges the severity, defends divine authorization, but cautions against application. He writes: "We must not judge the piety of Phinehas and the army by the severity of the command; the Lord who gave life has the right to require it back. But let no private person nor prince imagine that such acts are models for their own arms."

The Anabaptist and pacifist traditions — Menno Simons, later John Howard Yoder — rejected the entire Christian embrace of OT war and formulated a theology of non-resistance. Yoder in *The Politics of Jesus* (1972) argues that the definitive Christian posture is Jesus's rejection of the sword, and the OT war traditions, including Num 31, must be read typologically as pointing toward Christ's non-violent victory. Millard Lind in *Yahweh Is a Warrior* argues the OT wars are consistently framed as *YHWH's* fighting, not human military power, which undermines any nationalist or militarist appropriation.

Critical modern readings. Regina Schwartz in *The Curse of Cain* (1997) treats Num 31 and similar texts as the genealogical root of a monotheistic violence that has shaped Western civilization. Walter Brueggemann in *Divine Presence amid Violence* (2009) engages Num 31 and Joshua, arguing the text must be heard in its unsettling form — read against, not for, its rhetoric of divine sanction — neither explained away nor accepted as normative. Richard Hays in *The Moral Vision of the New Testament* argues the OT violence texts must be read Christologically: the cross is the hermeneutical center, and texts in tension with cruciform ethics cannot be taken as direct moral templates.

Apologetic responses. Paul Copan, *Is God a Moral Monster?* (2011), argues the extermination language is ANE hyperbole; the target was combatants, not a literal population; the Canaanite context was extreme Bronze Age religious practice including child sacrifice. Christopher Wright, *The God I Don't Understand* (2008), acknowledges the moral difficulty without dissolving it: the wars are historically particular, not moral templates, and Christians must hold the tension. William Lane Craig argues divine command can suspend ordinary moral constraint. Critics including Wes Morriston, Randall Rauser, and Eric Seibert have responded that the hyperbole argument does not address Num 31:17-18 specifically, where the command distinguishes categories of persons and cannot be dismissed as rhetorical excess.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Jewish tradition preserves the text, theorizes the halakha (Maimonides), and progressively narrows its applicability. It does not apologize for the text, but it does not take it as a blueprint either. Christian tradition is more divided. Augustine and Aquinas legitimized divine command readings that influenced the Crusades; Reformation readings became more cautious; Anabaptist and modern pacifist readings reject application; liberal and postmodern critical readings often reject the text itself. Both traditions largely agree on the present: no contemporary Jewish or Christian ethics authorizes a Num 31-style campaign. They divide on how to read the text itself — as divinely authorized historical particular (majority traditional), as canonical witness to God's presence that must now be interpreted through Christ (Hays, Radner), as ANE hyperbole (Copan, Wright), as narrative the canon itself critiques (Yoder, Brueggemann), or as text whose moral authority is voided (Schwartz, some of Seibert).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The ethics of the *ḥērem* war and the killing commanded in vv. 17-18.**

*Position 1: Divine command authorization (Augustine, Calvin, much traditional reading).* God, as lord of life, can command what would otherwise be murder; the commanders' obligation was obedience. This position is not flippant. It holds that the moral order is grounded in the divine will, and that if God genuinely speaks, even the hardest commands bind. Calvin's caution — "let no prince imagine such acts are models" — matters. The position denies that Num 31 authorizes present violence but affirms the text's divine origin.

*Position 2: ANE hyperbole and covenantal particularity (Copan, Wright, Kitchen).* The extermination language is conventional ANE war rhetoric; the target was a specific confederation that had conducted a specific act of spiritual sabotage; the historical frame is unique and unrepeatable. Steelmanned, this reading does heavy exegetical work: the Mesha Stele, Merneptah Stele, and Assyrian annals all use such hyperbole, and interpretive honesty requires reading biblical war texts within their generic conventions. The position, however, strains against vv. 17-18's specificity.

*Position 3: Narrative-critical and canonically-critical reading (Brueggemann, Yoder, Hays, Radner).* The text must be read as the canon's witness to a particular moment, interpreted through the full arc of Scripture. The prophetic tradition (Jonah, Isaiah's servant, Hosea's love of the wayward wife) progressively relativizes the war tradition. The New Testament's Christology culminates this trajectory: the Messiah takes violence upon himself rather than inflicting it. Steelmanned (this is the least intuitive position for many traditional readers), the view insists that Scripture is a *conversation*, not a uniform voice; that Num 31 is real, divinely given, and historically particular; that it points beyond itself to a resolution that only the cross provides. The text is not explained away; it is heard as part of the journey. Radner's reading is key: the text is the body of Scripture's suffering witness that only Christ transfigures.

*Position 4: Critical rejection (Schwartz, some of Seibert).* The text, whatever its provenance, encodes an ideology of religiously-sanctioned mass killing that has done catastrophic historical harm. The theological task is to name this and to refuse the text's moral authority on the specific question of war. Steelmanned, this position asks: what would it mean to read our sacred texts with eyes open to their capacity for violence? It refuses both the hyperbole-deflection and the "trust and hold the tension" approach, calling for ethical critique of Scripture itself.

*Position 5: Jewish halakhic narrowing (Maimonides, Kimelman, Walzer).* The text is preserved as historical particular, categorized halakhically (*milḥemet mitzvah*), and rendered practically inapplicable by the combination of: requirement of offered peace (Hilchot Melachim 6), requirement of a functioning Sanhedrin, specific identification of the Seven Nations as no longer identifiable, and the general principle that "we do not learn halakha from the specific cases of biblical war." The position is simultaneously conservative (text is preserved) and pacifying (text has no contemporary bite).

**Where this leaves us:** No responsible reading of Num 31 endorses it as a template for present action. The disagreement is how to read it as Scripture. The apologetic and canonical-critical readings do important exegetical work but cannot fully dissolve the moral difficulty of vv. 17-18. The critical reading does important moral work but risks losing the text as Scripture rather than as document. The Jewish halakhic tradition offers a model of preservation-without-application that Christian readers sometimes underutilize. Most fruitful is probably a hybrid: hold the historical-particular framing, attend seriously to ANE parallels, read the text canonically toward Christ, and refuse to sanitize the horror. The text interrogates us as much as we interrogate it.

**Tension 2: The figure of Balaam and the Num 22-24 / Num 31 inconsistency.**

*Position 1: Rabbinic harmonization (Rashi, most rabbinic readings).* After blessing Israel in Num 24, Balaam privately counseled Midian and Moab on the Peor seduction strategy, then returned to collect his reward, then was killed. The inconsistency between "Balaam returned to his place" (Num 24:25) and his death in Num 31:8 is resolved by inserting the Peor advice narratively between the two chapters. This becomes the standard reading and generates elaborate traditions about Balaam's character.

*Position 2: Source-critical reading (Wellhausen, Noth, Milgrom).* Numbers 22-24 preserves an older Balaam tradition in which he is a genuinely authorized pagan prophet who blesses Israel; Num 25 introduces the Peor tradition without Balaam's involvement; Num 31:16 retrospectively connects Balaam to Peor as part of the priestly theological framework. The inconsistency is compositional. The reading does not dissolve the moral content of Num 31 but relocates Balaam's villainy to later redactional layers.

*Position 3: Literary-theological reading (Alter, Olson, Radner).* Balaam is a deliberately ambiguous figure whom the canonical text holds in tension — prophet of YHWH and yet not of Israel, speaker of blessings and yet agent of Peor. The two portraits are not meant to harmonize neatly; they produce a complex picture of a gifted but ultimately corrupt religious professional. Steelmanned, this reading accepts the canonical form and extracts theological meaning from the tension rather than resolving it.

**Where this leaves us:** The Balaam figure matters theologically because his case raises the question of religious gift without covenantal fidelity. 2 Peter 2:15, Jude 11, and Rev 2:14 all treat Balaam as the archetype of the false teacher who combines genuine religious facility with moral corruption. The rabbinic tradition (particularly Bavli Sanhedrin 106a, 105b) develops this into the figure of the spiritual adversary who uses sacred language for profane purposes. Whether one harmonizes the accounts or reads them as a canonical polyphony, Balaam becomes the Bible's standing warning that religious gift does not equal holiness.

**Unresolved question:** If we grant that Num 31 reflects a real encounter between Israel and the divine in historical time, what did that divine presence actually require of the commanders in the field, and what is the relationship between what Scripture records and what God commanded? The question is not answered by any reading surveyed here. It presses us toward the cross and toward silence.

## The Thread

Four non-obvious cross-references:

1. **Revelation 2:14 on "the teaching of Balaam."** The Balaam-as-corruptor reading of Num 31:16 becomes the New Testament's template for false teaching that uses religious position to lead people into idolatry and sexual immorality. The reception history of Num 31 shapes the NT ecclesial imagination directly.

2. **2 Chronicles 28:9-15 — the prophet Oded.** When northern Israel captures Judahite men, women, and children, the prophet Oded orders them released, fed, and returned home. The incident is a pointed narrative counter to Num 31: a prophet intervenes to *prevent* the mistreatment of captives. The canon supplies its own critical voice.

3. **Ezra 9-10 — the post-exilic foreign-wife crisis.** The Ezra narrative enacts a peaceable version of the Num 31 moral concern — foreign women associated with religious corruption — but resolves it through divorce and separation, not war. The comparison illuminates how post-exilic Judaism domesticated the ancient *ḥērem* logic.

4. **The cross as the end of *ḥērem*.** Gal 3:13 — "Christ became a curse (*katara*, the LXX equivalent of *ḥērem* in some passages) for us" — is not a standard cross-reference for Num 31, but it is structurally decisive. The logic of total devotion to God, whether of spoil or of persons, is in Paul's reading absorbed into Christ himself. The one *ḥērem* that completes and ends all *ḥērem* is the death of the Messiah. This is the canonical-Christological trajectory that readers like Hays, Yoder, and Radner trace.
