# Jeremiah 24: The Two Baskets of Figs and the Birth of Diaspora Theology

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jeremiah 24 is a short chapter with disproportionately large theological consequences. The sign-vision is dated precisely: *after Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon had taken into exile from Jerusalem Jeconiah the son of Jehoiakim* — that is, **after the deportation of 597 BC**. This is the same Coniah whose disinheritance was pronounced in chapter 22, the same Jeconiah whose ration-tablets in Babylon are preserved in the Vorderasiatisches Museum in Berlin. The vision belongs to the period 597–588, after the elite were gone but before the second siege began. The reigning king in Jerusalem is Zedekiah, his court hopeful that Babylon's hold on Judah will weaken, that the deported elite will be restored, that the political center of gravity will return.

Jeremiah's vision contradicts every part of that hope. The historical setting is critical: 2 Kings 24:14–16 records that Nebuchadnezzar deported approximately 10,000 of the elite in 597 — *all the officials, all the mighty men of valor, ten thousand captives, and all the craftsmen and the smiths; none remained, except the poorest people of the land*. The text emphasizes that the *elite* — the officials, the craftsmen (*charash*, חָרָשׁ), the metalworkers (*masger*, מַסְגֵּר, literally "lock-smiths" or "smiths") — were taken; the *am ha-aretz* (the people of the land, the non-elite, the rural poor) remained. The Babylonian Chronicle (BM 21946) confirms the campaign on its own terms: *the king of Akkad… besieged the city of Yehud and on the second day of the month of Adar he took the city and captured the king. He installed there a king of his own choice, took heavy tribute, and brought it to Babylon*. The deportation is the most fully documented event in the late history of Judah, attested in Hebrew narrative (2 Kings, 2 Chronicles, Jeremiah, Ezekiel), in cuneiform chronicle (BM 21946), and in cuneiform administrative records (the Weidner ration tablets, listing "Yaukin king of Judah" and his sons).

The remarkable feature of Jeremiah's vision is its theological reversal of Judean common sense. The Jerusalem-remaining party — the king, the court, the priesthood, the *am ha-aretz* who had been promoted into the gap left by the deportees — is the **bad figs**. The Babylon-deported party, including Jeconiah, the officials, the craftsmen, and the smiths, is the **good figs**. The redemptive future of God's people lies *in exile*. The Jerusalem-remnant is doomed.

Among the deportees of 597 — that is, among the "good figs" — were two figures whose later writings would become canonical: **Ezekiel ben Buzi**, a priest deported in 597 according to Ezek 1:1–3 and 33:21; and **Daniel** (according to the chronology of Daniel 1:1–6, though the dating of Daniel is contested in critical scholarship; the traditional reading puts him among the 605 BC first-wave exiles, while the book's literary frame places him squarely in the same Babylonian community as Ezekiel). The historical irony is exquisite: the most theologically radical literature of the exilic period — Ezekiel's chariot vision, Daniel's apocalyptic, the Deuteronomistic History's compositional layer of exile — comes from the community Jeremiah identified as God's redemptive future.

The temple location of the vision (*two baskets of figs placed before the temple of the LORD*, 24:1) is significant. The basket of first-fruits was a covenantal offering (Deut 26:1–11); placing baskets before the temple invokes the Sukkot / harvest setting. *Te'enim tovot ki-te'enei ha-bakkurot* (תְּאֵנִים טֹבוֹת כִּתְאֵנֵי הַבַּכֻּרוֹת, "good figs like the first-ripe figs," 24:2) refers specifically to the early summer figs that ripened in late June, before the main harvest — the most prized of the season's produce. The bad figs are the late-summer ones that have rotted before harvest could be made. The agricultural metaphor is precise: timing matters; what looks like the wrong moment is the right moment, and what looks like the right moment is too late.

Key Hebrew terms: *te'enim tovot* (תְּאֵנִים טֹבוֹת, "good figs," 24:2, 5), *bakkurot* (בַּכֻּרוֹת, "first-ripe," 24:2), *te'enim ra'ot* (תְּאֵנִים רָעוֹת, "bad figs," 24:2, 8), *charash u-masger* (חָרָשׁ וּמַסְגֵּר, "craftsmen and metalworkers," 24:1 — the technological elite of Judah), *lev la-da'at oti* (לֵב לָדַעַת אֹתִי, "a heart to know me," 24:7 — the precursor to the new-covenant theology of Jer 31:33–34), *gola* (גּוֹלָה, "exile," 24:5).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre-wise the chapter is a **sign-vision** (*chazon ot*), a sub-genre of prophetic vision-report in which the prophet sees a concrete object, the LORD asks *what do you see*, the prophet describes the object, and the LORD interprets it. The closest formal parallels are **Amos 7:7–9 (the plumb line) and 8:1–3 (the basket of summer fruit)**, where the LORD asks Amos *what do you see*, Amos answers, and the LORD reads the object as a sign of judgment. Amos 8:1–3 is particularly close: *qayitz* (summer fruit) and *qetz* (end) make a paronomastic pair, and the basket of summer fruit becomes the sign that the *qetz* has come for Israel. **Jeremiah 1:11–14** (the almond branch / *shaqed*, punning on *shoqed*, "watching") and **Zechariah 5:1–11** (the flying scroll, the woman in the ephah) extend the same form. The form-critical signature is unmistakable.

Jeremiah 24 inverts the Amos paradigm. In Amos 8, the basket of fruit is the sign of judgment; in Jeremiah 24, *one* basket is the sign of judgment and the other is the sign of redemption. The chapter is structured as a binary: good figs / bad figs, exile / Jerusalem, future / no-future. The structural symmetry is broken only by the theological asymmetry — the redemptive future lies with the population that human eyes had written off.

The vision is in prose, not poetry, but its prose is taut. Verses 5–7 are the redemptive oracle, with verbs piling up: *I will regard as good… I will set my eyes on them for good… I will bring them back… I will build them up… I will plant them… I will give them a heart to know me*. Verses 8–10 are the corresponding judgment oracle, with verbs piling up in the opposite direction: *I will make them a horror… I will send sword, famine, and pestilence… until they shall be utterly destroyed*. The structural mirroring is rhetorical: the LORD's hand is doing two opposite things to two populations at the same moment.

The redemptive oracle of v. 7 — *I will give them a heart to know me, that I am the LORD, and they shall be my people and I will be their God, for they shall return to me with their whole heart* — is one of the earliest articulations in the prophet of what will become the **new-covenant theology** of Jeremiah 31:31–34: *I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts… I will be their God, and they shall be my people… they shall all know me*. Lundbom (Anchor) treats 24:7 as the seed of 31:33–34. The covenant formula *I will be their God and they shall be my people* runs from Lev 26:12 through Jer 7:23, 11:4, 24:7, 30:22, 31:33, 32:38, Ezek 36:28, and culminates in Rev 21:3.

The MT and LXX of chapter 24 are virtually identical; there are no significant textual variants in the Qumran fragments. This is one of the textually stable chapters of the book, which has implications for its compositional priority — Carroll (OTL) and Stulman both treat the figs-vision as belonging to the earliest layers of the Jeremianic tradition.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The figs-vision is one of the most consequential texts in the OT for the development of **diaspora theology** — the theological recognition that life outside the land is not merely punishment but can be the locus of God's redemptive future.

In the Jewish trajectory, the figs-vision is the foundational text for what would become the *galut* tradition. The **Babylonian Talmud at Berakhot 17a** and **Megillah 14a–b** treat the exiled community as theologically privileged in particular ways. The **Targum Jonathan** to Jeremiah 24:7 paraphrases *a heart to know me* as *a heart to fear before me* — a softening that nonetheless preserves the radical claim that the redemptive transformation happens *in Babylon*, not in Jerusalem. **Rashi** reads the chapter as a vindication of the prophets' counsel to the exiles in Jeremiah 29 (*build houses and live in them; plant gardens and eat their produce*) — the figs-vision and the letter to the exiles are theologically continuous. **Radak** stresses the conditional nature of the redemption: it depends on the exiles' return *to me with their whole heart* (24:7), making the geography of redemption secondary to the disposition of the heart. **Abrabanel**, writing in the shadow of his own expulsion from Spain in 1492 and his subsequent diaspora wanderings (Naples, Corfu, Venice), develops the most sustained Jewish reading: he treats the figs-vision as the paradigm for understanding all subsequent Jewish exiles, and he reads the *am ha-aretz* of Jerusalem as the type of every Jew who chooses cultural assimilation in the false security of the homeland over the harder fidelity of the dispersed community. **Maimonides** (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 7) cites Jer 24:7 alongside Jer 31:33 as the textual ground for the rabbinic theology of teshuvah-as-heart-transformation.

The contemporary Jewish reception of the figs-vision is complicated by the existence of the State of Israel. **Yeshayahu Leibowitz** (*Judaism, Human Values, and the Jewish State*, 1992), in his anti-Zionist religious critique, cited Jeremiah 24 as evidence that the locus of Jewish faith cannot be identified with political sovereignty over the land. **Daniel Boyarin** (*A Radical Jew*, 1994; *Border Lines*, 2004) extends the Leibowitz reading: diaspora is theologically primary, not derivative. The opposing Jewish position — religious Zionism, especially in the Kookian tradition — reads the prophetic restoration promises as having been initiated with the founding of the modern state, with the figs-vision's "good figs" as the Zionist pioneers and the "bad figs" as the diaspora that refused to return.

In the Christian trajectory, the figs-vision was read typologically from the patristic period. **Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah*** (Hom. XX) treats the good figs as the church under persecution, the bad figs as the institutional religion of the unconverted Jerusalem. **Jerome's commentary** treats the chapter as a prophecy of the dispersion of 70 CE. The medieval Christian tradition tended to spiritualize the geography: the church is the new diaspora, *peregrinatio* is the Christian condition, and the earthly Jerusalem is a figure of the heavenly. **Augustine** in *City of God* draws extensively on the diaspora-as-redemptive theology to articulate the Christian's situation in the world. The Reformation tradition — **Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah*** (1563) — reads 24:7 as a foundational text for the Reformed doctrine of regeneration: God *gives* the heart to know him; the human cannot manufacture the heart-knowledge. The verse is part of the Reformed exegetical case for monergistic regeneration.

The contemporary Christian critical scholarship has converged on the figs-vision as a foundational text for **diaspora theology**. **Daniel Smith-Christopher's *A Biblical Theology of Exile*** (2002) treats the chapter as the textual warrant for understanding exile not as a deviation from God's plan but as a privileged locus of theological work. **Erich Gruen's *Diaspora: Jews Amidst Greeks and Romans*** (2002) traces the second-temple Jewish diaspora theology back to the Jeremianic vision. **Jacob Wright's *Rebuilding Identity*** (2004) extends the analysis to the post-exilic returnee community. **Jon Levenson** (*The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*, 1993) treats the figs-vision as part of the broader prophetic logic that life often emerges through apparent death. **Kathleen O'Connor's *Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*** (2011) reads the chapter as a moment of theological reframing under conditions of communal trauma — the prophet helps the deportee community see itself not as cursed but as chosen.

The Christian post-supersessionist scholarship — N.T. Wright, R. Kendall Soulen, Brueggemann's later work — has had to reckon seriously with Jeremiah 24's claim that exile is the locus of God's future *for Israel*, not for the church-as-replacement. Romans 11's *all Israel will be saved* and the Pauline insistence that *the gifts and calling of God are irrevocable* (Rom 11:29) read more clearly when Jeremiah 24 is understood as a prophecy specifically about Israel's diaspora-future, not a generalizable Christian topos.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Diaspora as the locus of God's redemptive future.** Jeremiah 24's central claim is that the population God's prophets and people had written off — the deportees, the dispersed — is in fact the bearer of the future, while the population that retained political sovereignty in Jerusalem is on the path to destruction. Smith-Christopher, Gruen, Levenson, and Boyarin all treat this as the foundational text of diaspora-as-redemptive theology. The Christian patristic tradition (Origen, Augustine) extended the principle: the church is permanently in *peregrinatio*, exile is the Christian condition, and political sovereignty is a temptation rather than a goal. The Anabaptist tradition (Yoder, Hauerwas) makes the figs-vision foundational for a theology of the church as exile community. The Jewish *galut* tradition makes it foundational for a theology of diaspora Judaism. The contemporary Christian / Jewish argument over the State of Israel is, at one level, an argument over how to read Jeremiah 24. **Christian Zionism** treats the prophetic restoration promises as fulfilled in the modern state and reads the figs-vision's promise of return as having come to pass. **Christian non-Zionism** (Yoder, Hauerwas, Wright with qualifications) treats the figs-vision's redemptive vision as more theologically subversive than the Zionist reading allows: God's people remain a diaspora community whose faithfulness is not dependent on political sovereignty. **Jewish religious Zionism** (Kook, Soloveitchik with qualifications) treats the State of Israel as the beginning of redemption (*atchalta de-geula*), with the figs-vision as one warrant among many. **Jewish anti-Zionism** (Leibowitz, Boyarin) treats the figs-vision as a permanent reminder that political sovereignty is theologically secondary. Steelmanning the most uncomfortable readings: the Zionist reading at its strongest insists that the prophetic promises must have *some* concrete historical referent or they collapse into spiritualized abstraction; the diaspora reading at its strongest insists that the figs-vision was always about the *exiles* as the redemptive locus, not about the return as the redemptive event, and that turning return into political sovereignty inverts the prophet's own theology. Where this leaves us: Jeremiah 24 will not arbitrate between Christian and Jewish Zionisms and their opposites, but it will not allow any of them to claim it as straightforward warrant. The figs-vision is more theologically subversive than any of its institutional uses.

**Tension 2: Heart-knowledge of God as foretaste of new covenant.** Verse 7 — *I will give them a heart to know me… they shall return to me with their whole heart* — is one of the earliest articulations of what becomes the new-covenant theology of Jer 31:31–34, which Hebrews 8:8–12 quotes in full as fulfilled in Christ. Does Jeremiah's *I will give them a heart to know me* replace the Sinai covenant or fulfill it? The Christian supersessionist reading (long Christian tradition, Augustine in some moods, Luther's *On the Jews and Their Lies*, Protestant scholasticism in some forms) treats the new covenant as replacing the old, with Israel as a defunct dispensation. The Christian fulfillment-without-supersessionism reading (Reformed orthodoxy in its better moods, Karl Barth in CD IV/1, N.T. Wright, R. Kendall Soulen) treats the new covenant as the deepening and extension of the Sinai covenant, not its abrogation. The Jewish reading treats Jer 24:7 and 31:33 as descriptions of the covenant *internalized* — the same covenant, now written on the heart rather than on stone, but still the covenant of Sinai. The Pauline reading in Romans 11 — *the gifts and calling of God are irrevocable* — refuses supersessionism explicitly: the new-covenant heart-transformation is *for Israel* in the first instance and only thereby for the Gentiles. **N.T. Wright** (*Paul and the Faithfulness of God*, 2013) develops the strongest contemporary case for reading Jer 24:7 / 31:33 / Rom 11 as a coherent covenant theology in which the new covenant fulfills rather than replaces the Sinaitic one. Steelmanning the supersessionist reading at its most cautious (not its most polemical): if the new covenant is *new*, then something about the old has been completed and surpassed; the question is whether "completed and surpassed" must mean "abolished" or can mean "fulfilled and continued at deeper register." Where this leaves us: the figs-vision is the theological seed of a covenant promise that the entire NT inherits, but the inheritance can be read either as supersession or as continuation, and the Pauline insistence that *the gifts and calling of God are irrevocable* (Rom 11:29) makes supersessionism difficult to maintain on biblical grounds.

**One unresolved question:** If exile is the redemptive locus in Jeremiah 24, what does that mean for any community — Jewish, Christian, or otherwise — that has come to identify covenant fidelity with political sovereignty over a particular land?

## The Thread

The heart-knowledge promise of 24:7 is the seed of Jeremiah 31:31–34, which Hebrews 8:8–12 quotes in full as the warrant for the new covenant in Christ — the figs-vision is the rooting of new-covenant theology, not its New Testament invention. The fig metaphor itself runs across the canon: Hosea 9:10 (*like the first fruit on the fig tree*), Micah 4:4 (each man under his own fig tree as the sign of messianic peace), and the cursing of the fig tree in **Mark 11:12–25 / Matthew 21:18–22** — Jesus's fig-tree action enacts a Jeremiah-24 judgment on the temple establishment of his own day, with the cursed fig tree as the bad figs and the cleansing of the temple between the two halves of the cursing as the sign that the redemptive future has moved elsewhere. The covenant formula *I will be their God and they shall be my people* (24:7) runs from Leviticus 26:12 through Jeremiah's chapters of consolation (30:22, 31:33, 32:38) and Ezekiel 36:28, climaxing at Revelation 21:3 — *Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. He will dwell with them, and they will be his people*. The prophet's vision in the temple courtyard ends, in the canon's last book, in a city without a temple, where the formula has finally been completed.
