# Genesis 37 — Four Lenses

The Joseph novella begins. What has been episodic patriarchal narrative (Abraham in chapter fragments, Isaac in scattered glimpses, Jacob in cycles) now shifts gears into what Gerhard von Rad called "the most sustained piece of narrative art in the Old Testament." Chapter 37 is the overture — every theme introduced here will play out across fourteen chapters.

---

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Setting and political backdrop.** The narrative places Jacob's family in the southern Canaanite hill country — Hebron in the Valley of Eshcol — with seasonal grazing pushing the brothers 50 miles north to Shechem and then another 15 miles further to Dothan. This is transhumant pastoralism: flocks move with the pasture, family stays at the home base. The Middle Bronze Age IIB context (c. 1800–1650 BC, if we accept a patriarchal-period setting for the traditions) places this in a Canaan of fortified city-states loosely under Egyptian influence, with the Semitic Hyksos about to rise to power in the Egyptian Delta — which makes the Egypt-bound caravan at v. 25 historically plausible rather than anachronistic.

**The caravan economy.** The Ishmaelite/Midianite traders carry *nəkōʾt* (gum tragacanth), *ṣŏrî* (balm, likely from the Jericho balsam tree), and *lōṭ* (laudanum/myrrh resin) — luxury goods for Egyptian embalming and cosmetics. This is the King's Highway trade corridor from Gilead through the Jezreel Valley down the coast to Egypt. Dothan sits exactly on this route; the brothers have not chosen a random pit. **Twenty shekels of silver** (v. 28) is the going price for a male slave aged 5-20 in Middle Bronze Age texts — the Laws of Hammurabi §116 and Old Babylonian contracts from Mari confirm this. By the Neo-Assyrian period slave prices had risen to 50-60 shekels, and Leviticus 27:5 sets the vow-valuation of a male aged 5-20 at twenty shekels. The number fits the second-millennium context with striking precision — K. A. Kitchen has made this a cornerstone of his argument for early tradition.

**Authorship and source criticism.** Genesis 37 is the classic test case for the Documentary Hypothesis. Classical source critics (Wellhausen, then refined by Noth and von Rad) identify two intertwined strands:
- **J strand**: Judah proposes selling Joseph (v. 26-27), **Ishmaelites** buy him (v. 25, 27, 28b), Jacob = "Israel."
- **E strand**: Reuben tries to save him (v. 21-22, 29-30), **Midianites** draw him up and sell him (v. 28a, 36), Jacob = "Jacob."

The doublet of rescuer (Reuben/Judah) and trader (Ishmaelite/Midianite) and the oscillation of the patriarch's name is the textbook case for redactional layering. Recent literary critics (Robert Alter, Gary Rendsburg) push back: the "contradictions" may be intentional narrative compression — a deliberately chaotic scene in which the brothers' murderous intent unravels in confusion. Alter reads the Ishmaelite/Midianite alternation as showing that Joseph is handed off multiple times, increasing the sense of helpless transit.

**Material culture — the robe.** The Hebrew is *kəṯōneṯ passîm* (כְּתֹנֶת פַּסִּים). The LXX rendered *chitōna poikilon* ("many-colored tunic"), which Jerome gave us as *tunicam polymitam* and the KJV as "coat of many colours." But *passîm* probably does not mean "colors." The same phrase appears in 2 Samuel 13:18 describing Tamar's garment as what royal virgin daughters wore. *Passîm* likely means "reaching to the palms and soles" — a long-sleeved, ankle-length tunic. This is not work clothing. This is aristocratic, non-laboring garb. Egyptian tomb paintings from Beni Hasan (c. 1890 BC) show Semitic traders in multicolored garments, so the visual tradition isn't baseless — but the point is status, not chromatics. Joseph wears what says "I don't herd sheep."

**Key terms:**
- *kəṯōneṯ passîm* כְּתֹנֶת פַּסִּים — the ornate/long-sleeved tunic; marker of elevated status, not just favor.
- *ben zəqunîm* בֶּן־זְקֻנִים — "son of old age" (v. 3). A formulaic expression (cf. Gen 44:20) for the youngest cherished child.
- *dibbâ rāʿâ* דִּבָּה רָעָה — "bad report" (v. 2). Same root as "slander" in Num 14:36-37 of the spies. Joseph's first act is ambiguous — informant or truth-teller?
- *hinnēh* הִנֵּה — "Behold" (v. 7, appears three times in one verse). The dream-speech piles this particle up, mimicking breathless wonder and — to his brothers — insufferable self-regard.
- *Šəʾōl* שְׁאוֹל — "Sheol" (v. 35). The shadowy underworld; earliest use in Torah. Jacob's grief invents the theology of descent.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

**Genre.** This is the opening of a novella — what Hermann Gunkel called a *Novelle* and von Rad treated as a distinct genre within Genesis. Unlike the Abraham and Jacob cycles, which are chains of independent episodes, Joseph is a single sustained plot with psychological interiority, dramatic irony, and a structured arc. Many critics have noted its affinities with Egyptian wisdom literature — von Rad famously argued Joseph is a wisdom hero embodying *Ma'at*. The "Tale of Two Brothers" (Papyrus D'Orbiney, 13th c. BC) shares striking motifs with Genesis 39 (false accusation by a woman), though direct literary dependence is contested.

**The toledot formula.** Verse 2: "These are the generations (*tôlədôt*) of Jacob" — the tenth and final toledot-marker in Genesis. Readers expect a genealogy. Instead we get a story. The text is signaling that Jacob's "generations" — his legacy — will be worked out through this narrative, not a list.

**Chiastic and structural architecture.** The chapter itself is a self-contained unit with inclusio:
- A: Jacob loves Joseph, gives robe (vv. 3-4)
- B: Two dreams of exaltation (vv. 5-11)
- C: Sent to brothers (vv. 12-17)
- D: Plot and pit (vv. 18-24)
- C': Sold and taken away (vv. 25-28)
- B': The robe returns bloodied, anti-dream (vv. 29-33)
- A': Jacob mourns, refuses comfort (vv. 34-35)

Note how the **robe** frames the narrative — bestowed in love, stripped in hate, returned in deception. And in Hebrew there is a brutal wordplay: they "recognize" (*hakker-nāʾ*, v. 32) the robe. In chapter 38 (which interrupts the Joseph story), Tamar will confront Judah with his own pledge using the identical phrase *hakker-nāʾ* (38:25). The redactor placed Genesis 38 exactly where it is to let Judah experience being deceived by a recognition-scene he just orchestrated. Alter calls this "the measure-for-measure justice of biblical narrative."

**Doubling and patterns.** Dreams come in pairs (two here, two in ch. 40 for butler/baker, two for Pharaoh in ch. 41) — a pattern Joseph himself explains in 41:32: doubled dreams signal divine fixity. Joseph is stripped of clothing three times in the novella (robe here, garment by Potiphar's wife, prison clothes before Pharaoh), and each stripping precedes a descent that precedes an elevation.

**The wandering man.** Verses 15-17 are strange. An unnamed "man" (*ʾîš*) finds Joseph wandering and redirects him to Dothan — sending him, unknowingly, into the ambush. Rabbinic tradition (Rashi, citing earlier midrash) identifies him as the angel Gabriel. Literary critics call it Hitchcockian — a moment of narrative stillness that pivots the whole story. Without this man, Joseph returns home. The text refuses to explain, and that refusal is the point.

**Textual variants.** The Septuagint at v. 3 reads *chitōna poikilon* (varied-colored robe), reflecting its interpretation of *passîm*. The Samaritan Pentateuch harmonizes some of the Ishmaelite/Midianite tensions. 4Q364-365 (Reworked Pentateuch from Qumran) preserves portions of Gen 37 with minor variants, none meaning-affecting.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Canonical placement.** This chapter pivots Genesis from patriarchal promise to the mechanism by which Israel ends up in Egypt — setting up Exodus. The promise to Abraham that his descendants would be sojourners in a land not theirs (15:13) begins here, through fraternal betrayal. The theme of younger-over-older (Abel, Isaac, Jacob, Ephraim) reaches its most elaborate iteration in Joseph. The motif of a garment as site of deception connects backward (Jacob deceives Isaac with Esau's garment, 27:15; Laban deceives Jacob by substituting Leah, 29:23) and forward (Tamar and Judah, 38:14-25). Jacob, who deceived his father with a goat's skin and a garment, is now deceived by his sons with a goat's blood and a garment. The narrator is merciless.

### Jewish interpretive trajectory

**Second Temple.** *Jubilees* 34 softens the brothers' guilt and emphasizes providence. Philo (*De Iosepho*) reads Joseph allegorically as the *politikos* — the statesman-soul who governs passion. Josephus (*Ant.* 2.9-39) expands the psychology — the brothers' hatred is rationalized, Reuben's rescue attempt dignified.

**Rabbinic.** *Bereshit Rabbah* 84 turns the chapter over many times. The rabbis are troubled by Joseph: the *dibbâ rāʿâ* (bad report) is bad — R. Meir says Joseph reported the brothers were eating from live animals (violating the Noahide proto-kashrut), R. Judah says he accused them of sexual impropriety with Canaanite women. Either way, the tradition holds Joseph partly culpable. The robe is punished: because Jacob favored Joseph with "two sela's worth of wool," the brothers went down to Egypt (*Shabbat* 10b). A single act of parental favoritism causes the exile. Midrash on v. 15 names the man as Gabriel (*Bereshit Rabbah* 84:14). On "he refused to be comforted" (v. 35), the rabbis note that one can only refuse comfort for the dead — for the living, comfort eventually takes hold. Jacob's heart *knew* Joseph was alive, but consciously he could not access it (*Bereshit Rabbah* 84:21).

**Medieval.** Rashi follows midrash closely — Joseph's "bad report" was slander, and he was punished with slavery measure-for-measure. Ibn Ezra is more psychological and less midrashic; he notes the political realism of the dreams — Joseph as seventeen-year-old tactlessly broadcasts what a wiser man would keep silent. Nachmanides (Ramban) emphasizes divine orchestration: even Joseph's wandering in the field (vv. 15-17) is the hand of God sweeping him toward Egypt to fulfill the prediction of 15:13. Maimonides, with his theory of prophecy, treats Joseph's dreams as genuine lower-order prophetic revelations.

**Modern Jewish.** Avivah Zornberg (*The Beginning of Desire*) reads the chapter as a drama of *language failing* — the brothers "could not speak peacefully (*dabbēr lə-šālōm*) to him," and the whole novella is about restoring speech between them. Joseph talks too much (dreams, reports); silence becomes his apprenticeship. Jon Levenson (*The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*) reads Joseph as the beloved son who undergoes symbolic death (the pit, the bloodied robe) and restoration — part of a pattern including Isaac and culminating in the Jewish theological imagination of exile and return.

### Christian interpretive trajectory

**Patristic.** From Tertullian and Melito of Sardis onward, Joseph is *the* Old Testament type of Christ. The template hardens: beloved son, sent by the father to brothers, rejected, stripped of his garment, cast into a pit (= grave), sold for pieces of silver, handed over to Gentiles, rises to glory, feeds the world, is finally recognized by his brothers. Ambrose wrote an entire treatise *De Joseph* elaborating the typology. Origen (*Hom. in Gen.* 15) allegorizes further — the pit is Hades, the robe stripped is the incarnation. Caesarius of Arles reads Reuben as the Law (unable to save) and Judah as the Gospel (bringing redemption, though here ironically).

**Medieval.** The *Glossa Ordinaria* compiles the patristic typology into standard form. Aquinas treats Joseph as a *figura* of Christ while maintaining the literal-historical sense (*Summa* I.1.10). Typology by this point is virtually automatic.

**Reformation.** Luther's *Lectures on Genesis* (late 1530s) are remarkably extensive — he spent years preaching through Joseph. Luther pulls back from allegorical excess but retains Christological reading, emphasizing Joseph's faith under suffering as the paradigm of the Christian life. Calvin (*Commentaries on Genesis*) is harder-headed — he's more interested in the moral psychology of the brothers and in divine providence working through human evil, which becomes his central theme for the whole novella.

**Critical and modern.** Wellhausen dissected the sources; von Rad reintegrated them theologically in his *Genesis* commentary, arguing the novella's theme is a "hidden God" who works through the freedom of human action (contrasting with the theophanies of earlier patriarchal stories — God never speaks in Genesis 37-50 except in dreams). Brueggemann (*Genesis*, Interpretation series) emphasizes the chapter's portrayal of family violence as the arena of covenant. Terence Fretheim focuses on human responsibility — God does not cause the brothers' evil, God weaves through it.

### Convergence and divergence

Both traditions converge on: the theme of fraternal jealousy as primal sin (echoing Cain and Abel), the theological weight of providence working through human evil, the moral ambiguity of Joseph himself. Both traditions find parental favoritism terrifying in its consequences.

They diverge sharply on typology. For Jewish readers, Joseph is Joseph — a patriarch whose story anchors the exile/redemption pattern that defines Israelite identity. Any "type" he becomes is a type of Israel, not of an individual future redeemer. For traditional Christian readers, every detail (the silver, the robe, the pit, the brothers' recognition in ch. 45) points to Christ. Post-Holocaust Christian scholarship (e.g., Walter Moberly) has grown more cautious about supersessionist typology that empties the Joseph story of its Jewish-historical weight. The Jewish scholar Amy-Jill Levine and Christian scholar Richard Hays have both argued that typology can be honored as one mode of reading without being the only mode.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What is the author doing theologically? Genesis 37 is the beginning of an extended meditation on **divine providence through human evil** — the theology that will explode in Joseph's climactic declaration at 50:20: "You meant evil against me, but God meant it for good." This chapter plants every seed of that paradox without showing God's hand at all. Unlike Abraham, who hears God speak in almost every chapter, Joseph in chapter 37 receives only dreams. The narrator never tells us God is at work. We must believe it from the outcome. The theological vision is: **God's faithfulness operates beneath the surface of human freedom, even when that freedom is murderous.**

### Tension 1 — Divine providence and human evil

Is God the author of the brothers' crime, or does God redeem what evildoers freely do?

- **Augustine and the Reformed tradition** (following Calvin on Gen 50:20): God positively ordains the evil for a good end. Calvin writes that nothing happens "except what God has deliberately willed." The brothers' sin is their own; the orchestration is God's. This preserves God's sovereignty but strains the moral intuition — did God *want* Joseph sold?
- **Arminian / Wesleyan**: God permits and works through, but does not author, the evil. The brothers genuinely could have not sinned. Providence is God's responsive wisdom, not God's exhaustive script.
- **Catholic** (CCC 312, which cites this very story): distinguishes God's permissive will from God's positive will. "From the greatest moral evil ever committed… God… brought the greatest of goods." The story of Joseph is explicitly invoked.
- **Orthodox** (following Maximus the Confessor's logoi): evil has no ontological substance; God's providence consists in weaving the *logoi* of events toward theosis. Less concerned with legal causation than with teleological beauty.
- **Jewish** (Nachmanides, later Levenson): providence is narrative — *gilgul*, unfolding — not mechanistic. The brothers are fully guilty; God writes with crooked lines. The *hester panim* (hiding of face) through Genesis 37-50 is itself theologically meaningful. Zornberg frames it as God's pedagogy of absence.
- **Critical**: von Rad calls this "wisdom theology" — the chapter teaches that life's meaning is visible only retrospectively. Brueggemann reads it as Israel's confession that their whole national existence emerged from a family disaster.

**Steelmanning the Calvinist position** (which I find least intuitive): if God does not in some strong sense ordain the evil, then Israel's entire existence hangs on a contingency God merely hoped would turn out. The covenant becomes fragile. Calvin's instinct — that God's faithfulness requires sovereignty all the way down — is not authoritarianism but covenantal protection. A God who only reacts to free human choice may be moral, but is such a God the one who promised Abraham?

### Tension 2 — The ethics of Joseph

Is Joseph victim, or brat, or both?

- **Rabbinic**: partly culpable. The dreams and the slander are not innocent. The trauma of slavery forges him into someone who can save his family. The text requires Joseph's flaws.
- **Christian typological**: Joseph is righteous, foreshadowing Christ's innocent suffering. The brothers' hatred is unjustified. Any moral ambiguity in Joseph is minimized.
- **Modern literary** (Alter, Fokkelman): Joseph is a spoiled, unwise seventeen-year-old who broadcasts dreams that any wise man would conceal. The narrator's portrayal is deliberately complex — we are meant to feel both that he deserves something less than slavery and more than adoration.
- **Family-systems and narrative readings** (Brueggemann, Fretheim, Zornberg): the real protagonist is the dysfunctional family. Jacob's favoritism — itself the product of his own favoritism-scarred childhood — transmits trauma. Joseph, Reuben, Judah are all responding to a father who has never learned to love evenly.

**Ethical difficulty we cannot dodge.** This chapter contains attempted fratricide, human trafficking, and sustained deception of a grieving father. The text does not condemn the brothers explicitly in the narrative voice. It simply shows. The refusal to moralize is itself a theological stance — the narrator trusts the reader to feel the horror. But the narrator also refuses to give Jacob justice in this chapter. He is left in sackcloth for years. Any theology that rushes to "it all worked out" skips over the fact that between verse 35 and Genesis 45, Jacob lives more than two decades believing his son was eaten by an animal.

**Where this leaves us:** Genesis 37 refuses cheap providence. It presents evil as evil, grief as unconsoled, and God as silent. The theological claim — that this same silent God is faithfully authoring a rescue — can only be made retrospectively, and the text forces the reader to wait for it.

**The unresolved question:** If Joseph's story is true — that betrayal, exile, and the silence of God were exactly how the promise moved forward — then what are you refusing to be comforted about, that may turn out to be the place God is most at work?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 4:1-16** — Cain and Abel. The first brothers-in-jealousy; the first time clothing/blood speaks to a father. Joseph's brothers are Cain with a plan to make Jacob (not God) ask "Where is your brother?" Reuben's question in v. 30 — "where shall I go?" — rhymes with Cain's exile.

- **Genesis 38** — Read immediately and ask why the redactor put it there. Judah, who in 37:26-27 sold his brother using a *hakker-nāʾ* ("identify, please"), will in 38:25-26 be on the receiving end of the same phrase from Tamar. The narrator forces Judah to taste deception.

- **Genesis 27:15-16** — Jacob deceives his father Isaac using Esau's garment and a slaughtered goat. In 37:31-33 his sons deceive him using Joseph's garment and a slaughtered goat. The same Hebrew verbs appear. Ancient audiences heard the echo immediately.

- **When you reach Matthew 27** — the stripping of Jesus' garment, the silver of Judas (same root name as Judah; thirty pieces vs. twenty), the handing over to Gentiles. Whatever you decide about typology, the Gospel writers are reading Joseph when they shape the Passion. Hold this chapter in memory; it will return.

- **Acts 7:9-16** — Stephen's speech compresses the entire Joseph narrative to make a single polemical point: the pattern of Israel rejecting its God-sent deliverer starts here. Stephen uses Joseph as the template for Moses and then Jesus. Whether his hearers accepted that reading or killed him for it was the test.
