# Job 5: Sparks Flying Upward

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The fifth chapter completes Eliphaz's first speech. He moves from the abstract theological claim of chapter 4 to practical pastoral counsel, and in doing so he deploys the full inventory of ancient Near Eastern sapiential commonplaces — the fate of the fool, the inviolability of divine justice, the numerical saying about sixfold and sevenfold deliverance, and the blessing-list of long life, peace, and many descendants. These are the stock formulas of the wisdom tradition across the Fertile Crescent. The Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemope* (c. 1200 BCE), the *Instruction of Ptahhotep* (c. 2400 BCE), and the Akkadian *Counsels of Wisdom* all contain lists of blessings promised to the wise and pious. Eliphaz is speaking within a shared international idiom, and his counsel would have been recognized as orthodox wisdom in Thebes, Babylon, or Teman.

The specific cultural references repay attention. "Crushed in the gate" (5:4) refers to the city gate as the site of judicial proceedings; children without a father to defend them were legally defenseless there. Ruth 4 and Deuteronomy 21:19 describe the gate as the court of justice. "The hungry eat his harvest" (5:5) images the collapse of agricultural property rights — bands of hungry gleaners who would normally be excluded come and strip a dead fool's fields. The "league with the stones of the field" (5:23) is an agricultural idiom; stones in a Palestinian or Edomite field were obstacles to plowing, and to be at peace with them meant to have cleared and cultivated land. Robert Alter notes the pun: *ki im-avnei ha-sadeh beriteka* plays on *even* (stone) and *ben* (son), suggesting also a covenant with one's own household. The "sheaf gathered up in its season" (5:26) is harvest vocabulary and implies a natural, timely death — the counter-image to Job's losses.

On dating, Pope (Anchor Bible) and Clines (Word Biblical Commentary) again read the Hebrew as late poetic composition, noting archaic-sounding lexemes that may nonetheless be learned archaism. Gordis and Andersen defend pre-exilic dating on the basis of the traditional wisdom content. Carol Newsom in *Contest of Moral Imaginations* reads the chapter as a literary reconstruction of orthodox wisdom — the author of Job is ventriloquizing what orthodox wisdom would say, in order to subject it to critique. Ezekiel 14:14 again establishes the sixth-century terminus ante quem for the Job tradition.

Archaeological and textual parallels: the numerical saying "six . . . seven" (5:19) matches the *graduated numerical proverb* form (Proverbs 6:16-19, Proverbs 30:15-31, Amos 1-2, and Ugaritic parallels; see W. M. W. Roth's *Numerical Sayings*, 1965). The *Ludlul Bel Nemeqi* sufferer is eventually restored through the pattern Eliphaz commends — submission to discipline followed by deliverance. The Ugaritic Kirta epic contains a blessing-list for the righteous king with close formal parallels to 5:19-26. 11QTargum Job preserves portions of the chapter. The Septuagint of Job 5 is slightly shorter than the MT in verses 14-16.

Key Hebrew terms: *qadoshim* (קְדֹשִׁים, "holy ones") in 5:1 — the angelic beings, the *bene ha-elohim* of chapter 1, referenced as potential but inadequate intercessors. *Evil* (אֱוִיל, "fool"), the moral-religious fool rather than the intellectually incompetent. *Amal* (עָמָל, "trouble/toil") in 5:6-7, a key wisdom term describing the burden of human existence. *Yasar* (יָסַר, "discipline") in 5:17, the same verb Eliphaz used of Job's former teaching in 4:3 — Eliphaz turns the word back on Job, making him now the pupil of divine *musar* rather than its dispenser. *Shaddai* (שַׁדַּי, "the Almighty") in 5:17, the divine name that dominates the Job dialogues and whose etymology remains contested (breast/mountain/destroyer are the three major proposals). *Resheph* (רֶשֶׁף, "flame/spark/plague") in 5:7 — the same word as the Canaanite deity Resheph, god of plague and fire; the sparks fly upward because they are Resheph's children.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Job 5 extends the formal rhetorical structure of chapter 4 into four movements: the opening challenge (5:1-7), the doxology of divine action (5:8-16), the disciplinary counsel (5:17-26), and the peroration (5:27). The whole speech from 4:2 through 5:27 is a carefully constructed rhetorical unity. Norman Habel (Old Testament Library) argues that Eliphaz's first speech is the most formally polished of any in the book — a deliberate authorial choice to give the first friend the strongest articulation of orthodoxy before the dialogues dismantle it.

The opening (5:1-7) deploys a pointed rhetorical question: "Call now; is there anyone who will answer you?" The challenge is sardonic. Eliphaz knows Job has been calling; the silence is the answer. The "holy ones" — *qedoshim* — are angelic beings who function in other Near Eastern texts as intercessors (compare the *kalu* priests of Mesopotamia). Eliphaz's implication: no heavenly mediator will respond to a fool. Clines notes this is a subtle accusation that Job has been behaving like the *evil* (fool) whose fate is described in 5:2-5.

The fool's fate in 5:3-5 is a cameo portrait drawn in three verses: the fool takes root (prospers briefly), is suddenly cursed, his children are crushed in the gate, his harvest is eaten by strangers. The specifics map with uncomfortable precision onto Job's recent experience — Job's children have died, Job's wealth has been taken by raiders. The rhetorical move is passive-aggressive: Eliphaz describes the fool's fate without explicitly accusing Job, leaving the inference to the sufferer.

Verse 7 — *ki adam l'amal yulad uvenei-reshef yagbihu uf* — "man is born to trouble as the sparks fly upward" — is one of the most quoted lines in the book. The Hebrew is denser than the English: literally "man to toil is born, and sons of Resheph lift up flight." *Bene reshef* probably means "sons of flame" or "sparks," but the cultic echo of the Canaanite deity Resheph is almost certainly present. Pope argues the phrase is a proverb quoted by Eliphaz, not his own composition. The image is strikingly ambiguous — it could mean that trouble is inevitable (a cosmic principle) or that trouble is the human vocation (a moral principle) or both. Robert Alter treats the line as a miniature wisdom-saying Eliphaz has appropriated.

The doxology (5:8-16) is built on the hymnic form used in the Psalms — a recitation of divine deeds, each line beginning with a participle or finite verb describing God's action. David Clines observes that this hymn is formally similar to Psalm 107 and Psalm 147; Eliphaz deploys the vocabulary of Israelite worship to reinforce his theology. The central claim — God "frustrates the devices of the crafty" (5:12) — is the positive form of the retributive argument. The wicked are caught in their own craftiness; the needy are saved. Paul quotes 5:13 in 1 Corinthians 3:19 — the only direct quotation of Job in the New Testament — treating Eliphaz's line as scripture.

The disciplinary counsel (5:17-26) is a beatitude: "Blessed is the one whom God reproves." The Hebrew *ashrei enosh yokhichenu Eloah umusar Shaddai al-tim'as* — "happy is the mortal whom God argues with, and the discipline of Shaddai do not despise" — is formally a wisdom *ashrei* saying (compare Psalm 1, Psalm 32, Proverbs 3:13). The numerical saying in 5:19 ("six . . . seven") launches a list of deliverances. The blessing list that follows (5:20-26) enumerates famine, war, slander, destruction, wild beasts, barrenness, premature death — each reversed. Newsom observes that this list is the canonical wisdom promise in its most concentrated form: long life, many children, peace with the land, timely death. It is the Deuteronomic blessing compressed into seven couplets.

The closing peroration (5:27) — *hinneh zot chaqarnuha ken hi; sh'ma'ennah v'atah da'-lakh* — "behold, this we have searched out, it is so; hear it and know it for yourself" — is the classic wisdom-teacher's closing. *Chaqarnu* ("we have searched out") uses the plural, invoking the authority of the tradition. Newsom calls this "the rhetoric of tradition-as-research": the friend claims that the orthodox position has been tested and confirmed.

Literary devices: the chapter's structure is tightly chiastic. The opening challenge (5:1) is balanced by the closing peroration (5:27); the fool's children crushed in the gate (5:4) is balanced by the righteous man's many descendants (5:25); the hungry eating the fool's harvest (5:5) is balanced by the righteous man's sheaf gathered in its season (5:26). Habel maps the chiasm in detail. The inverted creation echoes from chapter 3 give way to a counter-inversion: where Job wished the stars dark and the day un-made, Eliphaz promises a world in which everything returns to its proper order.

Textual variants: 5:5's *zamim* (thirsty) is sometimes emended to *tsammim* (noose/snare); the LXX takes the latter reading. 5:7's *bene reshef* is rendered variously — "sparks," "birds of prey," "sons of the Plague-god."

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Job 5 is the classic distillation of retributive wisdom in Hebrew scripture. Its intellectual lineage runs through Proverbs 3, Psalm 91, and Deuteronomy 28. The chapter's promises are the orthodox wisdom tradition's inheritance, and the rest of Job will subject them to critique.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

Second Temple Jewish readings of Job 5 tended to accept its theology as correct and apply its promises to the faithful. Ben Sira (Sirach, second century BCE) echoes the counsel of 5:17 in Sirach 2:1-6, "My son, when you come to serve the Lord, prepare yourself for temptation" — a passage that reads like an apologia for the disciplinary theology of Job 5. Testament of Job preserves the prologue and end but minimizes the dialogues. Philo of Alexandria cites 5:13 and the disciplinary teaching approvingly in *De Iosepho*. Josephus does not quote Job 5 directly but his treatment of divine providence is consistent with Eliphaz's theology.

Rabbinic readings distinguished between Eliphaz's true statements and his false application. Bava Batra 15b-16a treats the friends' speech as a legitimate theological position that is nonetheless inapplicable to Job's particular case. Rashi glosses 5:7 as a general statement about human life without reading it as an accusation of Job. Ibn Ezra treats the blessing list of 5:19-26 as true of the righteous in general. Maimonides in *Guide* III.23 again places Eliphaz in a particular school — the Torah-based retributive view, which Maimonides considers partially true. Gersonides gives extended attention to 5:17 ("Blessed is the man whom God reproves"), reading it as a correct principle of divine pedagogy even when not applicable to Job. Nachmanides in *Sha'ar ha-Gemul* treats the disciplinary theology of 5:17-18 as central to his account of *yisurim shel ahavah* ("chastisements of love") — the rabbinic concept that God disciplines those he loves.

Modern Jewish readings have been sharper. Jon Levenson treats Job 5 as the most articulate defense of retributive theology in the Bible and therefore as the clearest target of the book's critique. Avivah Zornberg reads Eliphaz's hymnic doxology psychologically, as the friend retreating into safe liturgical language when faced with the disorienting reality of Job's suffering. James Kugel places Job 5 at the threshold between the older intuitive religion and the newer interior experience of faith. Edward Greenstein treats 5:17-26 as a pious parody — the author of Job is showing what pious counsel sounds like, not endorsing it.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

The Christian tradition's use of Job 5 is complicated by Paul's quotation of 5:13 in 1 Corinthians 3:19, which is the only explicit New Testament quotation of Job. Paul treats Eliphaz's line ("he catches the wise in their craftiness") as authoritative scripture. This single quotation has enormous weight in subsequent Christian interpretation: if the New Testament treats Eliphaz's line as scripture, the whole speech gains authority.

Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job* devotes several books to chapter 5. Gregory reads the disciplinary theology of 5:17 as a cornerstone of Christian ascetic theology — the *musar Shaddai* becomes the paradigm of divine pedagogy through suffering. Chrysostom preaches on 5:17 as a founding text of the monastic conception of affliction as spiritual formation. Augustine cites 5:7 ("man is born to trouble") in *City of God* as evidence of the consequences of the Fall. Jerome quotes the blessing list of 5:19-26 in his letters to indicate the blessings of the faithful.

Aquinas's *Expositio super Iob* treats 5:17 as a generally true principle: God does chasten those he loves, and the Christian reception of affliction as discipline is correct. Aquinas's discussion of suffering in *Summa Theologiae* I-II q.87 on the penal consequences of sin draws on Job 5. Calvin's sermons on Job 5 (sermons 18-24 in the 1554-55 cycle) are characteristically dense. Calvin reads 5:17 as foundational to the Reformed theology of sanctification through affliction; he simultaneously warns that Eliphaz's error is pastoral, not doctrinal. Luther cites 5:17 in his lectures on Genesis as evidence for the theology of the cross. The Puritan tradition (Thomas Goodwin, John Owen) made 5:17-18 a key text for the doctrine of sanctification.

Critical Protestant scholarship has largely repudiated Eliphaz's theology while preserving his most useful lines. David Clines's commentary (Word Biblical Commentary, 3 vols.) notes the tension between Paul's use of 5:13 and the book's ultimate condemnation of the friends; Clines argues Paul took the line in isolation and the book's own verdict in 42:7 does not repudiate every individual claim but the overall framework. Carol Newsom reads Eliphaz's hymn as "the voice of tradition" and the rest of the book as the challenge to that voice. Robert Gordis observes that the disciplinary theology is tolerable in the abstract but becomes pastoral cruelty the moment it is used to diagnose a specific sufferer who has lost his children. Ellen Davis's treatment is more sympathetic: she reads the blessing list of 5:19-26 as genuinely descriptive of God's typical care for creation, though misapplied to Job. Norman Habel (Old Testament Library), John Hartley (NICOT), Francis Andersen (TOTC), and J. Gerald Janzen offer similarly mixed verdicts.

The key Jewish-Christian divergence on this chapter: the Christian tradition, following Paul's quotation in 1 Corinthians, has generally treated individual lines of Eliphaz's speech as canonical wisdom while rejecting his overall pastoral application. Jewish tradition has been more willing to treat the whole speech as one position within a dialogue, with no single line having authority independent of its dialectical placement. Hebrews 12:5-11 quotes Proverbs 3:11-12, which is formally similar to Job 5:17, and the New Testament thus absorbs the disciplinary theology into Christian formation — even as the book of Job itself, in 42:7, withholds endorsement from the framework as a whole.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension one: is suffering divine discipline?** Verse 17 — "Blessed is the one whom God reproves; therefore despise not the discipline of the Almighty" — is the single most quoted line from the friends' speeches, and it has shaped Christian and Jewish theology of affliction from Sirach to the present. Does the book endorse this framework, or does it subvert it?

- Catholic (Aquinas *Expositio* on 5:17; CCC 1500-1510 on the pastoral meaning of illness; *Salvifici Doloris* by John Paul II): suffering can be pedagogical and even redemptive; the disciplinary framework is true but must not be used to diagnose the sufferer. Aquinas carefully distinguishes between *poena* (punishment) and *medicina* (medicinal discipline), and reads 5:17 in the latter sense.
- Reformed Protestant (Calvin, sermons on Job 5; Westminster Larger Catechism Q.28): affliction is a means of sanctification for the elect. Hebrews 12 is the canonical interpretation of Job 5:17. The Reformed tradition has been committed to the disciplinary reading for centuries.
- Lutheran (Luther's theology of the cross): affliction is the instrument by which God kills the old Adam and raises the new. 5:17 is a gospel text, not a law text, when properly read.
- Wesleyan / Holiness (Wesley's sermons on Christian Perfection): affliction is a means of grace, ordered toward entire sanctification. The Holiness tradition has preserved 5:17-18 as pastoral counsel.
- Anabaptist: the community accompanies the sufferer without claiming to diagnose divine purpose. Eliphaz's error is precisely in claiming to see what affliction is for.
- Orthodox (Chrysostom, later ascetic tradition): the monastic tradition made 5:17 a founding text of the life of askesis.
- Jewish (Nachmanides on *yisurim shel ahavah*; Rashi; modern Levenson, Zornberg): the rabbinic "chastisements of love" is a serious theological position, though Levenson argues Job itself subverts it. Maimonides accepts the principle but limits its scope.
- Critical (Newsom, Clines, Greenstein, Gordis): the disciplinary framework is the book's target. Eliphaz's 5:17 is what the book means to refute, not to endorse — the framework, once applied to a concrete innocent sufferer rather than left as a general maxim, turns into accusation.

Steelmanning the critical position: the book's final verdict in 42:7 — "you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" — is a blanket condemnation of the friends' overall theology. If Eliphaz's 5:17 is one pillar of that theology, it falls with the others. Paul's quotation of 5:13 in 1 Corinthians 3:19 may reflect a hermeneutic that treats Job as a quotation-mine for isolated sayings rather than a literary whole; the book's own architecture arguably resists that method. Where this leaves us: the disciplinary framework is neither wholly true nor wholly false; it applies in some cases and becomes cruelty in others, and the task of pastoral wisdom is to know when to invoke it and when to refuse it. The book of Job itself seems to counsel the refusal.

**Tension two: the problem of Paul's quotation.** 1 Corinthians 3:19 quotes Job 5:13 as authoritative, but God in 42:7 condemns the friends' theology. How do these data points relate?

- Catholic (Aquinas on 1 Corinthians 3:19): Paul cites a valid proposition from Eliphaz without endorsing the speaker's overall framework.
- Reformed (Calvin's commentary on 1 Corinthians): Paul's quotation confirms that 5:13 is authoritative; 42:7 condemns the application, not every proposition.
- Lutheran: the law/gospel distinction allows Eliphaz's statements to function as law while his pastoral application is the false counsel of a theologian of glory.
- Jewish: from a Jewish perspective the whole book is one integrated argument; isolated lines should not be treated as independent axioms.
- Critical (Clines, Newsom): Paul's broader argument in 1 Corinthians 1-3 is in fact aligned with Job's critique of human wisdom, not Eliphaz's confidence in it.

Steelmanning the critical reading: Paul in 1 Corinthians 1-3 argues that the world's wisdom is folly in God's sight — closer to Job's position than to Eliphaz's. The quotation of 5:13 may therefore be a *counter-use* — Paul deploying an Old Testament line against the kind of confidence Eliphaz himself represents. Where this leaves us: even Paul's quotation is compatible with the book's verdict against the friends; the appearance of scriptural endorsement may be a trick of surface reading.

One unresolved question that lands: if the most quoted verses of Job (5:13, "he catches the wise in their craftiness"; 5:17, "blessed is the one whom God reproves") come from a man the book ultimately condemns, what does that do to the practice of proof-texting? Is it possible that the quotability of a verse is inversely related to its reliability — that the most memorable lines in the wisdom tradition are precisely the ones the tradition itself is trying to outgrow?

## The Thread

- **Proverbs 3:11-12** — "My son, do not despise the LORD's discipline or be weary of his reproof, for the LORD reproves him whom he loves." This is the proverb that Eliphaz is echoing in Job 5:17. When you read Proverbs 3, notice that the disciplinary framework is canonical wisdom's own formula — and that Job's whole dialectic is built on testing this framework against the case of the genuinely innocent sufferer.

- **1 Corinthians 3:19** — "For the wisdom of this world is folly with God. For it is written, 'He catches the wise in their craftiness.'" Paul quotes Job 5:13 as scripture. When you reach 1 Corinthians 3, hold it against Job 42:7 where God condemns the friends. The New Testament's use of Job is more selective than proof-texting allows.

- **Hebrews 12:5-11** — "Have you forgotten the exhortation that addresses you as sons? 'My son, do not regard lightly the discipline of the Lord, nor be weary when reproved by him.'" Hebrews quotes Proverbs 3, not Job 5, but the whole argument draws on the disciplinary theology that both texts share. When you reach Hebrews 12, ask whether the New Testament has endorsed Eliphaz or simply recovered a parallel wisdom-saying from Proverbs.

- **James 5:10-11** — "As an example of suffering and patience, brothers, take the prophets who spoke in the name of the Lord. Behold, we consider those blessed who remained steadfast. You have heard of the steadfastness of Job, and you have seen the purpose of the Lord, how the Lord is compassionate and merciful." James reaches back past the friends' theology to Job's endurance as the example. When you read James 5, note what he does *not* say: he does not cite the friends, he does not cite the disciplinary framework, he cites only Job's endurance and God's mercy. The New Testament's most sustained engagement with Job refuses the friends' counsel.
