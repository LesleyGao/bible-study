# 1 Thessalonians 1: A Letter from a Hunted Man to a Model City

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Thessalonica in AD 50 was not a backwater. It was the capital of the Roman province of Macedonia, the largest city in the region, and a *civitas libera* — a "free city" granted by Rome the privilege of governing itself through its own magistrates rather than a Roman prefect. Those magistrates carried the unusual Macedonian title *politarchēs*, a word that for centuries existed in Greek literature only in Acts 17:6 and 8 — until nineteenth-century epigraphers began pulling Macedonian inscriptions out of the ground confirming it was a real, technical civic title (the Vardar Gate inscription, IG X 2,1 no. 126, now in the British Museum, alongside additional politarch inscriptions catalogued in IG X 2,1 nos. 127 and following). Luke's apparent oddity turned out to be local administrative precision. The city sat on the Via Egnatia, the Roman military and commercial highway running from the Adriatic across the Balkans toward Byzantium, which made it a natural relay point for trade, troops, and itinerant teachers alike.

Religiously, Thessalonica was saturated with civic and imperial cult. There was a temple to Roma and a long-standing association with the Caesars; coins from the city in this period bear divine titulature for Augustus and his successors. The imperial cult was not theology in the modern sense; it was civic loyalty rendered as worship, the sinews of urban life. To stand outside it — to refuse the libation, the festival, the public sacrifice — was to mark oneself as antisocial in a culture where social cohesion was a primary virtue. Acts 17:1–9 records a brief Pauline mission ending in a riot in which the accusation lodged before the politarchs is precisely the political one: Paul and his companions "are all acting against the decrees of Caesar, saying that there is another king, Jesus." That charge would have been intelligible to anyone in the forum.

The material culture of the converts is shaped by this context. Most were Gentiles (1:9, "you turned to God from idols"), embedded in Greco-Roman household structures — extended families, clients, slaves, dependents — where conversion was rarely solitary. Macedonian honor culture prized public reputation, benefaction, and visible loyalty; refusing imperial cult cost honor. Manual labor was the social bedrock of the artisanal class to which Paul, a tentmaker, belonged (cf. 2:9), and the ambivalence of Greco-Roman elites toward such labor sharpens Paul's later defense of his working hands.

Authorship and dating are unusually secure. The letter is universally accepted as authentically Pauline (Wanamaker, Malherbe, Fee, and Green all treat the question as settled), co-authored with Silvanus and Timothy. The Gallio inscription at Delphi (Fouilles de Delphes III.4 286), an imperial rescript naming Gallio as proconsul of Achaia, anchors Paul's Corinth ministry to roughly AD 51–52, which in turn places the writing of 1 Thessalonians during that Corinthian stay, c. AD 50–51. That makes 1 Thessalonians the earliest extant Pauline letter and almost certainly the earliest surviving New Testament document — older than Mark, decades older than the Gospels of Matthew, Luke, and John.

Textual transmission is robust. Papyrus 46 (c. AD 200) preserves substantial portions of Paul's letters including 1 Thessalonians; Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, and Alexandrinus give us complete fourth- and fifth-century witnesses. The text is exceptionally stable, with no major variant disrupting Chapter 1.

**Key Greek terms in Chapter 1.** *Ekklēsia* (ἐκκλησία, 1:1) — "assembly," the standard Greek civic word for a town meeting of voting citizens, repurposed by Paul for the gathered community in Christ. *Eklogē* (ἐκλογή, 1:4) — "choosing, election," an Old Testament covenant term applied to a Gentile congregation. *Eidōla* (εἴδωλα, 1:9) — "idols," the polemical Hellenistic-Jewish word for the gods of the nations. *Parousia* (παρουσία, anticipated by 1:10) — the technical Greek for an emperor's official "arrival" or *adventus* into a city, a word loaded with imperial pageantry that Paul will appropriate for the return of Christ. *Orgē* (ὀργή, 1:10) — "wrath," the eschatological judgment from which Jesus delivers his people.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre of 1 Thessalonians is a pastoral letter saturated with what Hellenistic rhetoricians called *philophronēsis* — the warm friendship register of the Greek epistolary tradition. Abraham Malherbe (*Paul and the Thessalonians: The Philosophic Tradition of Pastoral Care*, 1987; *Anchor Yale Bible*, 2000) made the case at book length that Paul writes within the conventions of philosophical paraenesis — the moral exhortation perfected by Cynic and Stoic moralists like Epictetus, Dio Chrysostom, and Musonius Rufus. The vocabulary of mild teaching, the nurse and father metaphors of Chapter 2, the tone of remembered affection, the invocation of personal example — these are paraenetic tropes Paul knew and reshaped. Robert Jewett (*The Thessalonian Correspondence*, 1986) reads the letter as "demonstrative" rhetoric, a praising letter rather than a deliberative or judicial one. The two readings are compatible: Paul is praising his converts even as he is forming them.

The structure of 1 Thessalonians is unusual among Paul's letters. It contains *three* thanksgivings — 1:2–10, 2:13–16, and 3:9–13 — where most Pauline letters open with one. Walter Schmithals influentially argued in the 1960s that the letter is composite, two letters spliced together. Most current scholars (Malherbe, Wanamaker, Fee, Green) reject the partition and read the triple thanksgiving as evidence of the letter's distinctive emotional register: Paul cannot stop being grateful for these people. The thanksgiving keeps recurring because the affection keeps recurring.

Chapter 1 functions as the opening movement: the salutation (1:1) yields directly into a sustained thanksgiving (1:2–10) that doubles as an indirect characterization of the Thessalonians. Paul is doing two things at once. He is praising his converts, and he is showing them — and any other readers — what the gospel looks like when it lands in a city. The famous triad of 1:3 — "your work of faith and labor of love and steadfastness of hope" — establishes the faith-love-hope pattern that recurs throughout the letter (5:8) and that Paul develops at length in 1 Corinthians 13. The chapter ends in 1:9–10 with what reads almost like a creedal summary: turning from idols to a living God, serving him, waiting for his risen Son from heaven, who delivers from the wrath to come.

Many scholars have recognized 1:9–10 as containing pre-Pauline material. Charles Wanamaker (NIGTC, 1990), F.F. Bruce (WBC, 1982), and Larry Hurtado have argued that the language here is a distillation of an earlier missionary preaching summary used in Hellenistic-Jewish-Christian outreach to Gentiles. The structure is unusually compact and confessional: monotheism (the one true God), conversion (turning from idols), eschatology (waiting for the Son from heaven), Christology (the resurrection), and salvation (deliverance from wrath). If Wanamaker and Bruce are right, this is among the earliest preserved Christian creeds — a snapshot of how the apostolic message was condensed for the synagogue and the agora before Paul wrote it down.

Literary devices in Chapter 1 include epistolary thanksgiving, paraenetic recall ("you know what kind of men we proved to be," 1:5), exemplum (the converts as imitators of Paul and the Lord, 1:6, and as model for Macedonia and Achaia, 1:7), and parousia language echoing imperial *adventus* (1:10). Chapter 1 has no major meaning-affecting textual variants; the textual difficulties of 1 Thessalonians cluster in 2:7 (*nēpioi/ēpioi*) and 2:14–16. Chapter 1 is, in textual terms, a clear field.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** The patristic tradition treated 1 Thessalonians 1 as a portrait of model conversion. John Chrysostom devoted eleven homilies to the letter (c. AD 400) and read 1:5–7 as a pastoral standard: the gospel arrives "in word," yes, but not only in word — it arrives in *power* and in the *Holy Spirit*, and it produces visible imitation. Theodoret of Cyrus and Theodore of Mopsuestia echoed this emphasis. Augustine drew on the chapter sparingly but recognized in 1:4 ("knowing your election") a foothold for his developing doctrine of predestination. Aquinas, in the *Super Epistolas* (his *Commentary on the Letters of Saint Paul*), framed the chapter's election language within the broader Catholic doctrine of grace, distinguishing between divine foreknowledge and human cooperation.

Calvin's commentary (1551) treated 1:4 as one of his clearest proof-texts for unconditional election: Paul *knows* their election precisely because the gospel came to them with power and the Holy Spirit; the manifestation of effectual calling is the evidence of God's prior choice. Luther had no full commentary on the letter but referenced it frequently; later Lutheran orthodoxy resisted Calvin's reading and preferred an emphasis on the means of grace (the word and sacraments) by which election is made known.

In modern critical scholarship, F.C. Baur and the Tübingen school briefly questioned the letter's authenticity in the nineteenth century — a position long since abandoned. Twentieth-century commentaries — Ernest Best (Black's, 1972), I. Howard Marshall (NCB), Charles Wanamaker (NIGTC, 1990), Abraham Malherbe (Anchor Yale, 2000), Earl Richard (Sacra Pagina, 1995), Beverly Gaventa (Interpretation, 1998), Gene Green (Pillar, 2002), and Gordon Fee (NICNT, 2009) — establish a scholarly consensus around Pauline authorship, the c. AD 50–51 date, and the literary unity of the letter. The "apocalyptic Paul" school (J. Christiaan Beker, J. Louis Martyn, Beverly Gaventa, Douglas Campbell) reads 1:10 — the waiting for the Son from heaven — as the cornerstone of Paul's apocalyptic gospel: God's invasion of a captive cosmos through the resurrection of Jesus, with the *parousia* as its still-pending second movement.

The imperial-critical reading, advanced by Karl Donfried (*Paul, Thessalonica, and Early Christianity*, 2002), Helmut Koester, Richard Horsley (*Paul and Empire*), and James Harrison (*Paul and the Imperial Authorities at Thessalonica and Rome*), insists that *parousia* in 1:10 is a deliberate counter-imperial claim. Caesar's *parousia* in a city was a moment of orchestrated public worship; Paul redirects the word to Jesus. To "wait for his Son from heaven" in the shadow of the imperial cult is to refuse the Caesar's adventus as the ultimate one.

**Jewish scholarly engagement.** Chapter 1 is less contested in Jewish-Christian dialogue than Chapter 2's 2:14–16, but it is not invisible to Jewish readers. Mark Nanos and the Paul Within Judaism school (Pamela Eisenbaum, Magnus Zetterholm) read 1:9–10's "turn from idols to a living God" as classic Hellenistic-Jewish missionary language, the kind of monotheistic appeal also visible in *Joseph and Aseneth*, the *Sibylline Oracles*, and parts of Philo. On this reading, Paul is not inventing a new religion but offering Gentiles a Jewish-style monotheistic conversion through Christ. Daniel Boyarin (*A Radical Jew*) and Paula Fredriksen (*Paul: The Pagans' Apostle*) emphasize that for Paul, Christ is the eschatological vindication of *Israel's* God; Gentile turning to "the living and true God" is the prophets' end-time pilgrimage, finally arriving.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Christian and Jewish scholars agree, broadly, that 1:9–10 sits inside Jewish apocalyptic monotheism — the one God of Israel acting in eschatological time. They diverge on the Christological move: whether the resurrection of Jesus is the hinge on which that eschatology turns (the Christian reading) or one possible Jewish reading among others that ultimately did not commend itself to most Jews (the Nanos / Fredriksen framing). They also diverge sharply on 1:4's election language. For Christian Reformed readers, *eklogē* applied to a Gentile congregation entails a redefinition of covenant Israel; for Jewish readers and Paul Within Judaism scholars, it more naturally means Gentile *engrafting* — these Gentiles share in Israel's election without replacing Israel.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What is God up to in 1 Thessalonians 1? On Paul's account, God has done something staggering and *recent*: a small congregation in a Macedonian capital has, within months, abandoned the religious infrastructure of the empire to wait for a crucified Galilean Paul says God raised. Paul's theological claim is that this is not sociology but election — God's own act, accompanied by power and the Holy Spirit and full conviction (1:5), now visible as faith, love, and steadfast hope (1:3) in a city that takes its civic identity seriously. Paul reads the conversion as the leading edge of an eschatological reality already in motion.

**Tension One: Election (1:4).** Paul says he *knows* their election. How?

The Augustinian-Reformed reading (Augustine, Calvin, the Westminster tradition, Herman Bavinck) hears this as unconditional election: God chose this people in eternity past; the manifestation of that choice in time is the gospel's power and the Spirit's effectual presence. Paul is not inferring from their good behavior; he is recognizing the visible outworking of a prior divine decree. Calvin, on this verse: the gospel does not merely sound, it *seizes*; where it seizes, election is exposed. The 1559 *Institutes* uses 1 Thessalonians 1:4–5 as one of its supporting texts for the doctrine of effectual calling.

The Wesleyan-Arminian and Eastern-broadly-shared reading (John Wesley, F.F. Bruce, I. Howard Marshall) hears election here as *corporate* and *responsive*. God elects a people in Christ; individuals enter that election by faith. "Knowing your election" means: the marks are present — power, Spirit, conviction, fruit — therefore you are part of the elect community. F.F. Bruce in particular argues that Paul's election language in 1 Thessalonians is more about Israel's covenant pattern (a chosen people) than about a hidden eternal decree applied to individuals.

The Eastern Orthodox synergistic reading (Vladimir Lossky, Georges Florovsky, John Zizioulas) refuses the binary. God's grace is sovereign and prior, and it always invites human cooperation; election is real but never coercive. Lossky in *The Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church* would say: election is not a decree but a relation, the relation that grace establishes when love freely receives love.

**Steelmanning the position I find least intuitive — the Augustinian-Reformed reading.** It is easy for moderns to bristle at unconditional election. But the Reformed reading takes seriously something the text plainly does — Paul's confidence. He does not say "I hope you are elect." He says "I *know* your election." That confidence has to come from somewhere. The Reformed answer is ultimately the most economical: Paul knows because the gospel's effective working is itself the visible side of God's prior choice. Without that prior act, there is no explanation for why this particular city, this particular household, these particular Gentiles received the word in power. The Reformed reading honors Paul's own confidence rather than softening it into pastoral encouragement.

**Where this leaves us:** Paul's election language is real and load-bearing; the question of what *kind* of election he means cannot be settled from this verse alone, and probably not from the letter alone. What is clear is that Paul is not anxious about it.

**Tension Two: Imminent eschatology (1:10).** Paul ends Chapter 1 with the Thessalonians "waiting for his Son from heaven." How imminent did Paul expect this return to be?

Albert Schweitzer's *consequent eschatology* (1906, 1930) argued that early Christianity was driven by the imminent expectation of the end and that the failure of that expectation was the great unresolved trauma of the New Testament. C.H. Dodd's *realized eschatology* moved the other direction: the kingdom has *already* come in Jesus' ministry; what looks like future expectation is mostly residual or symbolic. Oscar Cullmann (*Christ and Time*, 1946) split the difference with the famous "already / not yet" formulation: D-Day has happened, V-E Day has not. N.T. Wright's *inaugurated eschatology* (e.g., *Paul and the Faithfulness of God*, 2013) sharpens Cullmann: the resurrection of Jesus is the genuine inauguration of the new creation, and the *parousia* is its public completion.

Catholic teaching (CCC §§668–682) holds the *parousia* as a real future event whose *time* is not given; the Church lives in vigilant expectation without datable urgency. Lutheran orthodoxy similarly stresses the *certainty* and *non-imminence-by-prediction* of the return. Pentecostal and charismatic readings (Gordon Fee, J. Rodman Williams) emphasize the experiential continuity between Spirit-filled present (1:5) and Spirit-completed future (1:10): the Spirit's power *now* is the down payment on the parousia *then*. Eastern Orthodox liturgical practice locates this tension inside the Eucharist itself, where the *parousia* is anamnetically present.

**Steelmanning Schweitzer.** The most uncomfortable reading is Schweitzer's: Paul really did expect Jesus to come back soon, in a relatively narrow window, and he was wrong. Schweitzer is not trying to embarrass Paul; he is trying to honor the texture of Paul's urgency. 1 Thessalonians, written within two decades of the crucifixion, does carry a register of expectation that is hard to render fully comfortable on either Cullmann's or Wright's schemes. The "already / not yet" framework is doing genuine theological work, but it is also a way of managing a tension Schweitzer thought we should sit with.

**Where this leaves us:** Paul's expectation of the *parousia* is real, undatable, and oriented toward present formation rather than calendar prediction. The historical question of whether he expected it sooner than it came is real and unresolved; the theological question of whether that affects the gospel's truthfulness is answered differently by every tradition above.

**The unresolved question.** If "election" in 1:4 means corporate inclusion through the gospel's power, what does Paul's confidence in *knowing* it look like in a congregation where some, on his own later admission (4:13–18; 5:14), are wavering?

## The Thread

**Acts 17:1–9.** Luke's account of Paul's Thessalonica mission supplies the political backdrop the letter assumes — the riot, the politarchs, the charge of preaching "another king." 1 Thessalonians 1:6 ("you received the word in much affliction") reads as Paul's own glance back at that scene from the Corinthian months that followed.

**Isaiah 2:2–4 and the prophets' Gentile pilgrimage.** Paul's claim in 1:9 that the Thessalonians "turned to God from idols" reaches into the prophetic vision of the nations streaming to the God of Israel at the end of days. This is not a generic theistic conversion; it is a specific eschatological shape, and Paul reads it as fulfilled in real time in a Macedonian capital.

**1 Corinthians 15:1–8.** The compact creedal formula in 1:9–10 — turn, serve, wait, raised, delivers — anticipates the longer creedal summary in 1 Corinthians 15. The two passages together suggest that Paul inherited and circulated short, structured proclamations of the gospel and adapted them by setting.

**Philippians 3:20.** "Our citizenship is in heaven, and from it we await a Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ." Written to another Macedonian congregation a decade or so later under Roman pressure, Philippians 3:20 redeploys the same imperial counter-language — *politeuma*, awaiting, savior — that 1 Thessalonians 1:10 introduces. The thread is Paul's consistent theological habit of taking imperial vocabulary and repointing it at Jesus.
