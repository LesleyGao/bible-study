# Romans 3: The Mercy Seat and the Justified

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Romans 3 sits at the rhetorical and theological center of the letter's first half. Composed in Corinth in the winter of 56–57 CE, dictated to the scribe Tertius (16:22) and carried to Rome by Phoebe of Cenchreae (16:1), the chapter contains the densest concentration of Jewish-Hellenistic religious vocabulary in the Pauline corpus. To read 3:21–26 historically is to read it inside a Second Temple Jewish thought-world undergoing rapid pressure from Roman imperial reality.

Two specific historical contexts illuminate the chapter. First, the **Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur)** ritual described in Leviticus 16 and elaborated in Mishnah Yoma stands directly behind the *hilastērion* of 3:25. The high priest's annual entrance into the Holy of Holies, the sprinkling of bull's and goat's blood upon the **kapporet** (the gold cover of the Ark of the Covenant), and the cleansing of the sanctuary from accumulated impurity together constitute the most sacred ritual moment of Jewish worship. The Septuagint translates *kapporet* as *hilastērion* in Exodus 25:17–22 and Leviticus 16. When Paul writes that God "put forward [Christ] as a *hilastērion*" (3:25), Jewish ears would have heard the Yom Kippur referent before the more abstract Greco-Roman sense of "propitiation" or "expiation." **Daniel Bailey**'s 1999 Cambridge dissertation "Jesus as the Mercy Seat" remains the most thorough treatment; **Jarvis Williams**, **N. T. Wright**, and **Hans-Josef Klauck** have all advanced the reading.

Second, the **Qumran library** and especially **4QMMT** (*Miqsat Ma'asei ha-Torah*, "Some Works of the Law") provide a Second Temple Jewish parallel for the phrase *erga nomou* / *ma'asei ha-torah*. 4QMMT, discovered in Cave 4 at Qumran and published in critical edition by Elisha Qimron and John Strugnell in 1994, is a halakhic letter in which the sectarian community lays out specific Torah practices that distinguish them from competing Jewish groups. The phrase *miqsat ma'asei ha-torah*—"some of the works of the Torah"—refers to specific covenantal obligations whose observance marks one as inside or outside the covenant community. **James Dunn**, *The New Perspective on Paul* (essays collected 2005, expanded 2008), seized on 4QMMT as decisive evidence that Paul's *erga nomou* in 3:20, 28 refers not to "good works" or "works-righteousness" in general but to specific covenantal boundary markers. The reading remains contested—**Mark Seifrid** in *Justification and Variegated Nomism* vol. 1 challenges it—but the historical evidence is substantial.

The Roman audience itself is again worth holding in mind. Mixed Jewish-Gentile congregations, freshly reorganized after the lapse of Claudius's expulsion edict in 54 CE, are arguing about how Gentiles enter God's covenant family. Paul's question at 3:29—"Or is God the God of Jews only? Is he not the God of Gentiles also?"—is not abstract systematic theology but a live pastoral question for the Roman house-churches.

The textual situation is largely stable but not uniformly so. **𝔓⁴⁶** (c. 200 CE), **Codex Vaticanus**, **Codex Sinaiticus**, and the major witnesses give substantially the same Greek text. The most discussed variant is at 3:22, where some witnesses read *eis pantas kai epi pantas* ("for all and upon all") and others *eis pantas* ("for all") simply; the shorter reading is preferred and has been adopted by NA28. A more theologically consequential question concerns *pistis Iēsou Christou* (3:22, 26)—not a textual variant but a translation question, addressed below.

Five Greek terms reward attention. **Hilastērion** (ἱλαστήριον, 3:25) is the most contested word in the chapter. **Dikaiosynē theou** (δικαιοσύνη θεοῦ, 3:21, 22) is "the righteousness of God"—reprised from 1:17. **Pistis Iēsou Christou** (πίστις Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ, 3:22, 26)—objective genitive ("faith *in* Jesus Christ") or subjective genitive ("the faithfulness *of* Jesus Christ")? **Erga nomou** (ἔργα νόμου, 3:20, 28)—"works of the law." **Apolytrōsis** (ἀπολύτρωσις, 3:24)—"redemption," with a slave-market metaphor of ransom-buying. We engage each in Lens 4.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Romans 3 contains some of the most carefully constructed prose in the Pauline corpus. The chapter divides cleanly into three units: a continuation of the diatribe with the Jewish interlocutor (3:1–8); a catena of Old Testament quotations sealing the universal indictment (3:9–20); and the central kerygmatic announcement of God's saving righteousness (3:21–31).

The diatribe of 3:1–8 is the most virtuosic stretch of *prosōpopoiia* in the letter. Paul stages a rapid-fire exchange of objections and replies: "What advantage has the Jew? . . . What if some were unfaithful? . . . But if our unrighteousness serves to show the righteousness of God, is God unrighteous to inflict wrath? . . . And why not do evil that good may come?" Each objection is voiced and dismissed, often with the trademark *mē genoito* ("by no means!"—3:4, 6). The technique is the Hellenistic philosophical diatribe at its most disciplined; **Stowers**, **Jewett**, and **Käsemann** all read these verses as a stylized rhetorical exchange, not as Paul's settled doctrinal exposition. The function is to clear the rhetorical ground for the catena that follows.

The catena of 3:10–18 is one of the most concentrated Old Testament quotation-clusters in the Pauline corpus. Paul stitches together passages from at least six different Hebrew Bible texts (LXX numbering): Pss 14:1–3 / 53:1–3 (10–12), Ps 5:9 (13a), Ps 140:3 (13b), Ps 10:7 (14), Isa 59:7–8 (15–17), Ps 36:1 (18). Several of these are textually adjacent in the LXX manuscript tradition, and the catena may reflect a pre-existing Jewish testimonia-collection rather than Paul's independent compilation; **Leander Keck**'s essay "The Function of Romans 3:10–18" (1977) and **Christopher Stanley**'s *Paul and the Language of Scripture* document the issue. The literary effect is overwhelming: a cumulative scriptural verdict against humanity, sealed by the universal *ouk estin dikaios oude heis* ("none is righteous, no, not one") that opens the catena.

The transition at 3:19–20 closes the indictment ("every mouth may be stopped, and the whole world may be held accountable to God") and prepares for the dramatic *nuni de* of 3:21—"**but now**." This *nuni de* is one of the most theologically loaded conjunctions in the Pauline corpus, marking the apocalyptic shift from the time of universal sin and law-incapacity to the time of God's manifest saving righteousness. **J. Louis Martyn**'s *Galatians* (Anchor Bible) and **Beverly Gaventa**'s essays on Pauline apocalyptic identify the *nuni de* of 3:21 as one of the structural pillars of Paul's apocalyptic theology: a *kairos*-shift of cosmic proportions.

Romans 3:21–26 is structurally the central paragraph of the letter's first eight chapters. **Käsemann**, in his 1961 essay "*The Righteousness of God in Paul*" (later in *New Testament Questions of Today*), argued that 3:24–26 contains a **pre-Pauline traditional formula** which Paul has incorporated and reframed. The formal markers Käsemann identified—the participial construction *dikaioumenoi* in 3:24, the relative pronoun *hon proetheto* introducing 3:25, and the unusual collocation of *hilastērion* with *en tō autou haimati* and *dia [tēs] pisteōs*—suggest a piece of received liturgical or catechetical tradition. The thesis has been refined by **Eduard Lohse**, **Wilhelm Wrede**, and others; not all scholars accept it (**Cranfield** is skeptical), but the literary observation is widely acknowledged: 3:24–26 carries an unusual density of theological vocabulary that may not be original to Paul but is here put to Paul's specific argumentative purpose.

The chapter's literary climax at 3:27–31—"What then becomes of our boasting? It is excluded"—is rhetorically a return to diatribe, with another series of imagined objections and replies, but theologically a summary of the whole argument: justification by faith, *not* by works of the law, available to circumcised and uncircumcised alike, and therefore upholding rather than overthrowing the law.

The textual variant question that bears engaging here concerns the famous **doxology problem** at 16:25–27, mentioned in our framing of the letter as a whole. **Harry Gamble**'s *The Textual History of the Letter to the Romans* (1977) argued that the variable placement of the doxology—at the end of chapter 14 in the Byzantine majority, at the end of chapter 15 in 𝔓⁴⁶, at the end of chapter 16 in the great uncials—reflects different recensions of the letter circulating in the early church. **T. W. Manson**, **Cranfield**, and **Jewett** offer differing reconstructions, but the consensus is that Romans circulated in at least two forms: a fuller form including chapter 16 (the Roman version) and a shorter form ending at chapter 14 or 15 (perhaps an Ephesian recension or a generalizing copy made for wider church use). The textual evidence already in our chapter—including subtle stylistic shifts and the presence of pre-Pauline traditional material at 3:24–26—reflects the same dynamic of inherited and incorporated material that characterizes the letter as a whole.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Romans 3:21–26 is, by general scholarly consensus, the theological center of gravity for the entire Pauline corpus. The four major modern interpretive schools each read the passage with characteristic emphasis.

The **Old Perspective** (Augustine, Luther, Calvin, Cranfield, Moo, Schreiner) reads 3:21–26 as the crystalline declaration of *forensic justification*: God declares the unrighteous sinner to be righteous on the basis of Christ's atoning death received by faith. The *dikaiosynē theou* is the gracious righteousness God imputes; *hilastērion* is *propitiation* (the appeasement of God's just wrath against sin by means of Christ's substitutionary blood); *pistis Iēsou Christou* is the believer's *faith in Christ*; *erga nomou* is *works-righteousness in general*. The passage reveals how the unrighteous can stand before a holy God: through Christ's penal substitution received by faith. Luther's *Lectures on Romans* (1515–16) and his 1522 *Preface to Romans* identified this passage as the heart of the gospel; Calvin's *Commentary on Romans* (1540) and the Heidelberg Catechism Q&A 60–61 make the passage the foundation of Reformed soteriology.

The **New Perspective on Paul** (Sanders, Dunn, **N. T. Wright**) reads 3:21–26 as the climactic announcement that *God has acted on his covenant promises*. *Dikaiosynē theou* is **God's covenant faithfulness**, his commitment to fulfill the promises to Abraham by rescuing the world through Israel. *Pistis Iēsou Christou* is best read as the **subjective genitive**—**the faithfulness of Jesus Christ**, his obedient covenant-fulfilling life and death—a reading developed at length by **Richard Hays** in *The Faith of Jesus Christ* (1983, 2nd ed. 2002). *Erga nomou* refers specifically to the **boundary markers** (circumcision, food laws, Sabbath) that excluded Gentiles; the question Paul is answering is *not* "how do individual sinners get saved?" but "how do Gentiles get included in the covenant family without becoming Jews?" *Hilastērion* is the **mercy seat** of Leviticus 16—the place where God's covenantal cleansing happens. Justification is the eschatological declaration that one is a member of the covenant family; the passage announces that this membership is now opened to all who share the faithfulness of Jesus, irrespective of ethnic boundary marker observance.

The **Apocalyptic Paul** school (Käsemann, Martyn, Gaventa, **Douglas Campbell**) reads 3:21–26 as the *announcement of God's invasive saving action*. *Dikaiosynē theou* is God's saving power, God's eschatological deliverance breaking into the cosmos; *hilastērion* is the *means of God's invasive cleansing*; *pistis Iēsou Christou* is again the **faithfulness of Jesus**, the Son's obedient submission to the Father in accomplishing the rescue. The *nuni de* of 3:21 marks the apocalyptic turn from the old age (under Sin, Death, Flesh) to the new age (under God's saving righteousness in Christ). Käsemann's classic essay foregrounds the dynamic, performative dimension: 3:21–26 is the announcement of God's *act*, not the explanation of a *doctrine*. **Beverly Gaventa**'s essays (collected in *When in Romans*) develop the apocalyptic reading with particular care for how God's action *toward us* is the syntactic subject across the passage.

The **Paul Within Judaism** reading (Nanos, Eisenbaum, **Matthew Thiessen**, Fredriksen) reads 3:21–26 as the announcement of how *Gentiles* are included in God's renewed people without becoming Jewish proselytes. The argument is internally consistent with this school's reading of 1:18–32 (Gentile-only indictment) and 2:17–29 (the inward-Jew redefinition for Gentile inclusion). Israel's covenant remains operative. Justification "apart from works of the law" (3:28) means apart from the boundary-marker observance that would otherwise be required of Gentile converts; it does not mean Jewish believers must abandon Torah. **Thiessen**'s *Paul and the Gentile Problem* and **Fredriksen**'s *Paul: The Pagan's Apostle* develop the case at length.

These four readings differ on every consequential question. They disagree about *what* the *dikaiosynē theou* is, *whose* faith *pistis Iēsou Christou* names, *what* the *erga nomou* are, and *how* the *hilastērion* functions. Each school reads the same Greek text and produces a substantially different theological account.

The story of how Romans 3:21–26 has been read maps the central history of Western Christian soteriology. **Origen**'s *Commentary on Romans* offers the earliest extensive treatment: he reads *hilastērion* with the LXX Lev 16 mercy-seat referent foregrounded, sees the passage as both gift and transformation, and resists any reading that would deny the moral progress of the Christian. **Augustine**'s *De Spiritu et Littera*, written against the Pelagian Caelestius around 412 CE, reads 3:24 ("justified by his grace as a gift") as the most decisive scriptural ground for the doctrine of grace as the unmerited divine initiative. **Pelagius**'s *Commentary on Romans* (recovered by **Alexander Souter**, 1922–31) reads the passage as describing God's gracious provision *enabling* human moral response, with justification both gift and ethical transformation. **Chrysostom**'s 32 *Homilies on Romans* read *hilastērion* with full Yom Kippur resonance and emphasize the participatory dimension of being justified.

**Aquinas**'s *Lectura super Epistolam ad Romanos* offers the most careful pre-Reformation treatment, distinguishing the formal cause of justification (God's righteousness) from the meritorious cause (Christ's passion) from the instrumental cause (faith and the sacraments). The Catholic tradition through Trent reads 3:21–26 as describing both the imputation and the infusion of righteousness, holding the forensic and the transformative dimensions together.

**Luther** was emphatic that 3:28—"one is justified by faith *apart from* works of the law"—required the addition of *allein* ("alone") in his German translation, prompting a furious Catholic-Reformation polemic that runs through Cardinal Cajetan, Robert Bellarmine, and the Council of Trent's *Decree on Justification* (Session VI, 1547). The Lutheran *Formula of Concord* (1577), the Reformed *Westminster Confession* (1647), and the Catholic catechetical tradition all treat 3:21–26 as foundational. **Karl Barth**'s *Romans Commentary* (1922 2nd ed.) read the passage in apocalyptic mode—the *nuni de* as the irruption of God's senkrechte von oben—and decisively shaped 20th-century Protestant theology.

The 1977 publication of **E. P. Sanders**'s *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* changed the conversation. Sanders argued that Second Temple Judaism was not a religion of works-righteousness but of "**covenantal nomism**"—God's gracious covenant election was the soteriological foundation, with Torah obedience the response of those already inside the covenant. If Sanders is right, then the Reformation reading of *erga nomou* as "works-righteousness in general" misses the historical target. **James Dunn** developed the argument in his 1982 Manson Memorial Lecture and his subsequent Romans commentary; **N. T. Wright** built it into his comprehensive Pauline theology, climaxing in *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* (2013) and *Justification: God's Plan and Paul's Vision* (2009, his response to John Piper). The New Perspective debate has dominated Pauline studies for forty years.

**Krister Stendahl**'s essays in *Paul Among Jews and Gentiles* (1976), especially "The Apostle Paul and the Introspective Conscience of the West," prepared the ground. **Daniel Boyarin**'s *A Radical Jew* (1994) read Romans 3 from within Jewish studies. **Stowers**'s *A Rereading of Romans* (1994) emphasized the diatribe form. **Douglas Campbell**'s *The Deliverance of God* (2009) launched the apocalyptic reading in its most ambitious systematic form. **Pamela Eisenbaum**'s *Paul Was Not a Christian* (2009), **Mark Nanos**'s *The Mystery of Romans* (1996), and **Matthew Thiessen**'s *Paul and the Gentile Problem* (2016) and *A Jewish Paul* (2023) have launched the Paul Within Judaism school. The major commentaries of recent decades—**Fitzmyer** (Anchor Bible 1993), **Moo** (NICNT 1996), **Jewett** (Hermeneia 2007)—each engage the schools at length without converging.

We will not pick a winner. The chapter is unmistakably *also* about how unrighteous sinners stand before a holy God (Old Perspective is right that this is in the text); it is unmistakably *also* about how Gentiles are included in the covenant family without ethnic conversion (New Perspective and Paul Within Judaism are right that this is in the text); it is unmistakably *also* the announcement of God's apocalyptic deliverance (Apocalyptic is right that this is in the text). The four schools each illuminate something the others miss.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: How should we translate *hilastērion* (3:25)?**

The **Reformed / Old Perspective** position translates *hilastērion* as **propitiation**: Christ's death turns aside God's just wrath against sin. **Leon Morris**'s *The Apostolic Preaching of the Cross* (1955) and *The Atonement* (1983) argue extensively that the LXX usage and the Hellenistic Jewish background support a wrath-appeasement reading. Cranfield, Moo, and the broader Reformed tradition follow. The verse becomes the central New Testament foundation for penal substitutionary atonement.

The **Catholic / RSV** position, building on **C. H. Dodd**'s 1935 essay "*Hilaskesthai*, Its Cognates, Derivatives, and Synonyms in the Septuagint," translates *hilastērion* as **expiation**: Christ's death cleanses sin rather than appeasing wrath. Dodd argued that the LXX usage of the *hilask-* word group consistently meant cleansing from sin (with sin as the object) rather than appeasement of God (with God as the object). The verse becomes the foundation for an expiatory rather than propitiatory atonement.

The **Yom Kippur / Mercy Seat** position (Käsemann, **Daniel Bailey**, N. T. Wright, **Jarvis Williams**) translates *hilastērion* as **mercy seat**, with direct reference to LXX Lev 16:14 *kapporet*. The Greek term in 3:25 has the definite article in some manuscripts and is preceded by *proetheto* ("set forth publicly"), suggesting a specific cultic referent. On this reading, Christ is the place where God's covenantal cleansing happens—the antitype of the gold cover of the Ark in the Holy of Holies. The atonement is *both* propitiatory and expiatory, but the framework is the Yom Kippur ritual, not a Greco-Roman appeasement model.

The **Apocalyptic** position (Campbell, Martyn, Gaventa) tends toward expiation while emphasizing the dynamic, invasive dimension: Christ as God's *means of saving deliverance*, with the cosmic powers as the ultimate targets of the cleansing rather than divine wrath as the ultimate object of appeasement.

**Steelmanning**: The propitiation reading rightly hears the strong wrath-vocabulary of Romans 1:18 and 2:5 setting up 3:25; if God's wrath has been the rhetorical drumbeat through chapters 1–2, the announcement of *hilastērion* in chapter 3 most naturally picks up that thread. The expiation reading rightly hears the LXX usage and the consistent biblical concern that God's holiness sets the framework, not Greco-Roman appeasement-of-an-angry-deity. The mercy-seat reading rightly hears the cultic specificity of *hilastērion* in LXX Leviticus and the parallel of Christ's blood publicly displayed (3:25, *en tō autou haimati*, *eis endeixin*) with the high priest's sprinkling of blood on the *kapporet*.

**Where this leaves us**: The mercy-seat reading is exegetically and historically the strongest, because it accounts for both the wrath-language of chapters 1–2 (Yom Kippur cleanses *from* sin in a context that takes God's holiness seriously) *and* for the Septuagintal cultic specificity of *hilastērion*. It also accounts for Paul's "publicly displayed" language at 3:25, since the mercy seat was the most hidden place in Israelite worship—now publicly displayed in Christ. The propitiation/expiation debate of 20th-century Protestantism, while real, may be a false binary if we let the Yom Kippur framework do its work: God's covenantal cleansing of his people *both* turns aside the proper consequences of unholiness *and* removes sin's pollution.

**Tension 2: *Pistis Iēsou Christou*—objective or subjective genitive (3:22, 26)?**

The **objective genitive** (traditional) reads *pistis Iēsou Christou* as "**faith *in* Jesus Christ**"—the believer's act of trust as the means of receiving justification. This is the dominant Reformation reading and remains the position of most major translations (NIV, ESV, NRSV in main text). Cranfield, Moo, and Schreiner defend it.

The **subjective genitive** reads *pistis Iēsou Christou* as "**the faithfulness *of* Jesus Christ**"—Christ's own obedient covenant-fulfilling life and death as the basis of justification. **Richard Hays**, *The Faith of Jesus Christ* (1983, 2nd ed. 2002), made the case at book length; **N. T. Wright**, **Douglas Campbell**, and **Leander Keck** have developed it. The argument runs: (1) elsewhere in Paul, *pistis* + genitive of person is normally subjective (cf. Rom 4:12, "the faith *of* Abraham"); (2) the redundancy problem—on the objective reading, "the righteousness of God *through faith in Jesus Christ*, *for all who believe*" is awkwardly redundant; on the subjective reading, the two clauses distinguish Christ's faithfulness and the believer's responsive faith; (3) the theological coherence—the subjective reading gives Paul a Christological foundation for justification (Christ's obedience) parallel to the Adam-Christ contrast of 5:12–21.

A **double-meaning / both** position (Morna Hooker; some recent commentators) reads the genitive as deliberately ambiguous, holding both Christ's faithfulness and the believer's responding faith together.

**Steelmanning**: The objective reading rightly hears that Paul elsewhere uses *pistis* for the believer's trust (Rom 1:5, 17; 4:5; 5:1), and the parallel construction in 4:1–25 weighs heavily on this side. The subjective reading rightly hears that *pistis Christou* is grammatically more naturally subjective and that the redundancy on the objective reading is real. The double-meaning reading rightly hears that the ambiguity may be deliberate.

**Where this leaves us**: The grammatical and intertextual case for the subjective genitive is strong, but the case is not decisive—Paul's actual usage admits both readings. Hays's argument has shifted scholarly opinion meaningfully but not unanimously. The theological stakes are real: the subjective reading gives Paul a more Christologically anchored justification and softens the danger of treating "faith" as the new "work" the believer must produce. The objective reading preserves the relational moment of personal trust as essential to justification. We can hold both, with the recognition that Christ's faithfulness is the *ground* of justification and the believer's responding faith is the *means* of receiving it.

**Unresolved question**: What exactly does Paul mean by saying that justification "*upholds* the law" (3:31)? Does he mean (a) the law is upheld in the sense of being shown to bear genuine prophetic witness to the gospel (Cranfield's reading), (b) the law is fulfilled in Christ's obedience and in Spirit-empowered Christian obedience (Wright's reading), (c) the law is upheld in the sense that its proper function—revealing sin and restraining evil—remains operative (Calvin's *tertius usus legis* reading), or (d) the law is upheld in the sense that the Spirit's work in believers fulfills its righteous requirement (Augustinian-Reformed reading building toward 8:4)? The verse is a hinge that opens to chapter 4's Abrahamic argument and to chapters 7–8's law-and-Spirit dialectic.

## The Thread

Romans 3 sets multiple threads that the rest of the letter will weave:

**The catena of OT condemnation** (3:10–18) is answered by the catena of OT promise that runs through chapters 9–11 (Isa 28:16; Joel 2:32; Isa 52:7; Isa 53:1; Deut 30:12–14; Isa 65:1; etc.). Paul builds the indictment from Scripture and builds the rescue from Scripture, demonstrating throughout that "the Law and the Prophets bear witness" (3:21) to what God is doing in Christ.

**The *nuni de* of 3:21**—"but now the righteousness of God has been manifested apart from the law"—is the apocalyptic hinge of the letter, picked up at 6:22 ("now that you have been set free from sin"), 7:6 ("but now we are released from the law"), 8:1 ("there is therefore now no condemnation"), and 11:30–32 (Israel's *now* of mercy after disobedience).

**Justification by faith apart from works of the law** (3:28) is developed at length in chapter 4 (Abraham as paradigm), echoed at 5:1 ("therefore, having been justified by faith"), and brought to its eschatological climax at 8:33–34 ("Who shall bring any charge against God's elect? It is God who justifies").

**The single Lord of Jews and Gentiles** (3:29–30, "since God is one") is the foundation of the Jew-Gentile argument that runs through chapters 9–11 and reaches its pastoral expression in chapters 14–15.

[Flag: The pre-Pauline traditional formula at 3:24–26, identified by Käsemann and developed by Lohse and others, is one of several places (1:3–4; 4:24–25; 1 Cor 15:3–5) where Paul incorporates received early Christian material. The chapter therefore preserves not only Paul's theology but a window into pre-Pauline Christian confession.]
