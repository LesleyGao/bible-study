# Leviticus 25: Proclaim Liberty Throughout the Land

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Leviticus 25 sits at the programmatic center of the Holiness Code (Lev 17–26), the literary stratum that Israel Knohl famously distinguished from the priestly base (P). Where P concerns itself with sanctuary, sacrifice, and the distinction between pure and impure, H extends the logic of holiness outward — into the fields, the debt contracts, the kin obligations, the marketplace. The world presupposed by Leviticus 25 is agrarian, clan-based, and saturated with the assumption that land is the inalienable patrimony of a family. The chapter addresses a people not yet in the land (v. 2: "when you come into the land"), but it legislates for a settled peasant society with seasonal agriculture, field sales, urban housing, hired laborers, debt-slavery, and chattel slavery of foreigners.

The political-economic backdrop comes sharply into focus when one reads Leviticus 25 against the Mesopotamian *mīšarum* and *andurārum* edicts. The most celebrated is the *ṣimdat šarrim* issued by Ammi-ṣaduqa of Babylon (c. 1646 BCE), a ruler of the late Old Babylonian period who, upon accession, proclaimed a general remission of consumptive debts, the return of forfeited fields, and the release of debt-slaves. Similar acts are attested for Hammurabi and several of his successors; the fragmentary Edict of Ammi-ṣaduqa preserved on multiple tablets lays out the categories of remitted obligation in painstaking detail. Jacob Finkelstein's landmark study and Raymond Westbrook's work on ancient Near Eastern law showed that such edicts were not utopian gestures but irregular royal prerogatives designed to arrest the creeping insolvency that threatened the tax base of the crown. John Bergsma's *The Jubilee from Leviticus to Qumran* (2007) — the definitive treatment — argues that Leviticus 25's innovation is not the concept of *dĕrôr* (release) but its detachment from royal discretion: the *yôbēl* is no longer a one-time accession favor but a fixed calendrical event, announced not by king but by trumpet on the Day of Atonement, administered not from the palace but from the sanctuary. The sovereign who proclaims liberty is YHWH.

Material culture fills in the picture. The *šemiṭṭâ* ("release") of the seventh year (Exod 23:10–11; Deut 15; Lev 25:1–7) presupposes a dry-farming system where fallow was already a rational agricultural practice, extended here into a theological discipline. The *yôbēl* itself takes its name from the ram's-horn trumpet (*šôpar hayyôbēl*) whose blast on the tenth of Tishri — Yom Kippur — inaugurates the fiftieth year. Ancestral land, *ʾăḥuzzâ*, is held in perpetuity by a household unit, subdivided across generations; the kinsman-redeemer, *gōʾēl*, functions as the mechanism by which land alienated under economic pressure returns to the family line. Baruch Levine's JPS commentary reconstructs the institution by triangulation with Ruth 4 (Boaz's redemption of Elimelech's field and levirate claim on Ruth) and Jeremiah 32 (Jeremiah purchasing the Anathoth field from his cousin Hanamel during the Babylonian siege — a literal act of *gĕʾullâ* performed as prophetic sign). The valuation system called *ʿerkĕḵā* appears here in nascent form — fields are priced by their remaining productive years until the next Jubilee (v. 15–16).

The ethically freighted distinction between Hebrew debt-slaves (vv. 39–46) and foreign chattel slaves (vv. 44–46) reflects the clan-preserving logic of the chapter. The Hebrew who sells himself because of poverty is not *ʿeḇed* but a hired worker (*śāḵîr*) and sojourner (*tôšāḇ*); he is released at Jubilee. Foreigners bought from surrounding nations are property, bequeathable — an asymmetry whose moral weight commentators from Milgrom to Brueggemann have refused to soften. Nuzi tablets (15th c. BCE) — discovered in the 1920s in northeastern Iraq — document similar kin-based redemption practices and adoption-for-inheritance strategies among Hurrian families, illuminating the social world in which Israel's *gōʾēl* institution made sense.

Archaeologically, iron-age Judahite four-room houses and Iron II family tombs corroborate the picture of a kin-grounded land system where the household (*bêt-ʾāḇ*) inherited specific plots. The Sefire Aramaic treaties (8th c. BCE) and Esarhaddon's succession treaty (672 BCE) supply the treaty-curse vocabulary that shapes Leviticus 26, but Leviticus 25's land-reversion theology has its closest analogues in the earlier Old Babylonian remission acts.

Five Hebrew terms anchor the chapter:

- **yôbēl** (יוֹבֵל) — jubilee, named for the ram's horn
- **dĕrôr** (דְּרוֹר) — liberty, release; Isaiah 61:1 and Jeremiah 34 reuse this word pointedly
- **ʾăḥuzzâ** (אֲחֻזָּה) — ancestral holding, inalienable family land
- **gōʾēl** (גֹּאֵל) — kinsman-redeemer, one with both right and obligation
- **ʿerkĕḵā** (עֶרְכְּךָ) — "your valuation," the priestly valuation system that governs vows, houses, fields, and (in ch. 27) persons

Authorship: the chapter is H. Knohl in *The Sanctuary of Silence* (1995) argues H is a seventh- or early-sixth-century redactional layer that retools P's sanctuary-centered holiness into a land-extensive ethic. Milgrom, revising his own earlier Wellhausen-indebted chronology, ultimately placed H before Deuteronomy and read Deut 15 as Deuteronomy's democratized reworking of Lev 25's priestly jubilee. Wellhausen's post-exilic dating of all priestly material, once the consensus, has been decisively challenged by the Hebrew linguistic work of Hurvitz and by the archaeological-textual arguments of Haran.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Leviticus 25 is a legal text with homiletic inflections. It opens on Sinai (v. 1) — the same Sinai that closed Exodus — binding its legislation to the founding theophany rather than to Moab (as Deuteronomy will). The chapter's architecture is doubled: a short sabbatical-year unit (vv. 1–7) yields to the larger jubilee block (vv. 8–55), which itself subdivides into the proclamation (vv. 8–13), regulation of land sales (vv. 14–17, 23–28), the objection-and-reassurance exchange (vv. 18–22), urban-vs-rural housing (vv. 29–34), the ladder of poverty (vv. 35–55), and three parallel "if your brother becomes poor" cases of escalating severity.

The programmatic theological center is v. 23: *kî-lî hāʾāreṣ kî-gērîm wĕtôšāḇîm ʾattem ʿimmādî* — "the land is mine, for you are strangers and sojourners with me." The verse is a claim about ownership that subverts the economics of the chapter from within. All the valuations, redemptions, and sales that follow in chs. 25 and 27 are pricing conventions internal to a tenancy in which the ultimate freeholder never changes. Walter Brueggemann (*The Land*, rev. 2002, and *Sabbath as Resistance*, 2014) builds his entire theology of land around this verse; Ched Myers's *Sabbath Economics* treats it as the structural axiom of biblical economics.

Literary devices are subtle but present. The fivefold "if your brother becomes poor" formula (*kî-yāmûḵ ʾāḥîḵā*) in vv. 25, 35, 39, 47 forms a rhetorical ladder — from loss of land, to inability to maintain subsistence, to self-sale to a Hebrew, to self-sale to a resident alien — with progressively more intrusive interventions required by the community. The chiastic inclusio of Egypt-exodus formulae appears three times (vv. 38, 42, 55): "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of Egypt" — each time grounding a provision in the economic theology that redeemed slaves cannot be re-enslaved by other redeemed slaves. The phrase "they are my servants" (*ʿăḇāḏay hēm*, v. 55) inverts the chattel slavery of v. 46 with a pun: Israelites cannot be *ʿăḇāḏîm* of men because they already are *ʿăḇāḏîm* of YHWH.

The objection-reply of vv. 20–22 ("if you say, what shall we eat?") is unusual in priestly legal material; such second-person rhetorical interjections are more typical of Deuteronomy. Knohl reads this as evidence that H borrows hortatory techniques from Deuteronomic circles while preserving its priestly grammar of holiness.

Textual variants are relatively modest. 4QLevd and 4QLevb preserve fragments of the chapter; LXX smooths some ambiguities, rendering *dĕrôr* as *aphesis* — the very word Luke 4:18 will use for Jesus's Nazareth declaration. 11QpaleoLeva uses paleo-Hebrew script for the Tetragrammaton, marking the text's sanctity. The Samaritan Pentateuch preserves the consonantal text substantially as in MT.

Genre-wise, the chapter is casuistic law (the *kî* / *ʾim* formulas) wrapped in apodictic theology (v. 23, v. 38, v. 55). Comparison with the Covenant Code (Exod 21–23) and Deuteronomy 15 shows Leviticus 25 occupying a median position — more institutional than the Covenant Code, more cult-anchored than Deuteronomy, which knows only a seven-year cycle and no land reversion.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** In Second Temple literature, the jubilee functions as both halakhic legislation and cosmological grid. The book of Jubilees (2nd c. BCE) periodizes all of history from creation to Sinai in forty-nine jubilee units — fifty weeks of years times seven, a staggering chronological ambition. The Qumran document 11QMelchizedek weaves Isaiah 61:1–2 ("proclaim liberty to the captives") together with Leviticus 25 to construct an eschatological jubilee at which the heavenly Melchizedek will liberate captives and atone for the sons of light; this text is pivotal for understanding how first-century Jewish readers had already fused jubilee with eschatology. Philo's *On the Special Laws* 2 allegorizes the jubilee as the soul's release from the bondage of passions while insisting on its literal observance. Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.280–286) describes the institution as historical.

Rabbinic treatment is extensive. Mishnah Arakhin and Sheviit codify the operative halakhah; Sifra Behar-Bechukotai parses verse by verse. Bavli Arakhin 32a–33a debates whether jubilee functions today; Maimonides (*Hilchot Shemitah ve-Yovel*) rules that observance requires all twelve tribes in the land, a condition unmet since 722 BCE. The sabbatical year remains rabbinically binding in Israel (Hillel's *prosbul*, Mishnah Sheviit 10:3, functions as a debt-remission workaround analogous to later *heter mechira*). Rashi reads v. 23 as a check on pride; Ramban emphasizes the cosmological parallelism of six-days / seventh and reads the fiftieth year as a type of the messianic age.

Modern Jewish thought recovers jubilee as economic ethics. Jonathan Sacks presents it as Judaism's master-paradigm against hereditary concentration of wealth. Nehama Leibowitz's *Studies in Leviticus* works through the exegetical choices verse by verse; Zornberg brings psychoanalytic sensibilities to the land theology.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Leviticus* 15–16 allegorize jubilee as liberation from sin through Christ the true redeemer, while insisting against Marcion that the literal command bound Israel. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 5) reads the seven-millennium world-week eschatologically, with Christ inaugurating the final jubilee. Augustine slots jubilee into the typological chronology of *City of God* 18. Aquinas (*ST* I-II q. 105) classifies jubilee among the *praecepta iudicialia* — judicial precepts abrogated as positive law with Christ but persisting as natural-law principles of equity and ancestral restoration.

The medieval Franciscan poverty movement drew on jubilee imagery in its polemics against property; Joachim of Fiore's speculative theology of three ages wove jubilee into his eschatology of the Spirit. Luther's *Open Letter to the Christian Nobility* (1520) invokes Leviticus 25's prohibition of usury in arguing for reform of German economic practice; Calvin, in his *Commentary on the Four Last Books of Moses* (published 1563, which harmonizes Exodus–Deuteronomy), treats jubilee at length as a law "abrogated in the letter" but "whose spirit remains" — the permanent principle of charity toward the poor and the impermanence of fortune. The Westminster divines generally adopted Calvin's threefold division of law (moral / ceremonial / judicial) and placed jubilee in the judicial category.

In critical scholarship, Wellhausen's *Prolegomena* treated jubilee as a post-exilic priestly utopian retrojection, a scheme that could never have functioned. Martin Noth's *Das dritte Buch Mose* kept Wellhausen's dating. The revisionist wave — Milgrom's *Anchor Bible* Leviticus (3 vols., 1991, 2000, 2001), Knohl's *Sanctuary of Silence*, Sarna's work on pentateuchal chronology — has largely dismantled the post-exilic dating. Bergsma's *The Jubilee from Leviticus to Qumran* (2007) is now the definitive monograph; he argues methodically that the institution's literary evidence is pre-exilic and that its practical observance was, nevertheless, rare to non-existent in historical Israel (2 Chron 36:21 implicitly acknowledges this by rationalizing the exile as the land making up its unkept sabbaths).

Contemporary theological use is vigorous. N.T. Wright, in *Jesus and the Victory of God* and elsewhere, reads Jesus's Nazareth sermon (Luke 4:16–21, citing Isa 61:1–2 including *aphesis* / *dĕrôr*) as the deliberate announcement of eschatological jubilee. John Howard Yoder's *Politics of Jesus* (ch. 3) developed this reading in the 1970s and made jubilee central to Anabaptist social ethics. Ched Myers's *Sabbath Economics* popularized it; Christopher J. H. Wright's *Old Testament Ethics for the People of God* offers the most exegetically disciplined evangelical treatment.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Both traditions affirm the text's force as theology of the land and economy; both have worried about its practicability. Maimonides's suspension of jubilee on the grounds that twelve-tribe occupancy has not obtained since 722 BCE is matched on the Christian side by Aquinas's classification of jubilee as an abrogated judicial precept. Both preserve an eschatological afterlife: for rabbinic tradition, the messianic age will reinstate full jubilee; for the church, Jesus has already proclaimed it and inaugurated its fulfillment. Where they diverge: Christian reception has sometimes spiritualized the economic content into a metaphor of forgiveness of sin (Origen's tendency), stripping the agrarian reform of its teeth; the Jewish trajectory, especially in Sacks and in Israeli-era halakhic debates over shemitta, preserves the economic literalness.

Modern jubilee revivals have moved between the two. John Paul II's *Tertio Millennio Adveniente* (1994) called for the Great Jubilee of 2000 to include international debt relief; Bono and Jubilee 2000 pressed G8 governments toward cancellation of roughly $130 billion in developing-country debt. Bergsma's skepticism about whether any jubilee was ever actually observed casts a long shadow over such appeals.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**First tension: inalienable property vs. marketplace exchange.** Leviticus 25 simultaneously presupposes the legitimacy of land sales and denies that sales alienate the underlying title. How are these held together?

- **Catholic:** Leo XIII's *Rerum Novarum* (1891) defends private property as a natural right while insisting that its use is subject to stewardship under the universal destination of goods; John Paul II's *Centesimus Annus* (1991) and the USCCB *Economic Justice for All* (1986) treat Leviticus 25 as a biblical anchor for the principle that property rights are not absolute. *CCC* 2402–2406 systematizes this: private property serves the common good; hoarding violates its purpose.
- **Reformed:** Calvin interpreted jubilee as ceremonial-typological in its specific form but permanent in its principle of charity; Abraham Kuyper's *Stone Lectures* and subsequent Reformed social thought develop "sphere sovereignty," where economic life is subject to divine norms but not collapsed into state regulation. Contemporary Reformed ethicists (Nicholas Wolterstorff, *Justice in Love*) read Leviticus 25 as constitutive of biblical justice.
- **Lutheran:** Luther's two-kingdom ethic treats Leviticus 25's specifics as Mosaic civil law, not binding, but its moral substance — the prohibition of usury, the obligation to the poor — falls under the order of creation. Luther's early pamphlets on usury were harsh; his later works compromised toward commercial practice.
- **Anabaptist:** Yoder and Myers push the hardest. Jubilee is not abrogated; it is fulfilled in Jesus's announcement (Luke 4) and must be practiced by the church as a visible economic community. Intentional communities from the Hutterites to Reba Place Fellowship have attempted it.
- **Orthodox:** Schmemann's liturgical theology treats sabbath and jubilee as ingress of eschatological time; Ecumenical Patriarch Bartholomew I has extended jubilee logic ecologically — the land's right to rest as a creation-care imperative.
- **Jewish:** Maimonides (*Hilchot Shemitah ve-Yovel*) codifies the halakhic mechanisms; Sacks presents jubilee as Judaism's structural antidote to hereditary inequality.
- **Critical:** Bergsma, Milgrom, and Knohl read the tension historically: the law is a theological ideal, probably never implemented, but its literary function is to subordinate the marketplace to a higher Sovereign. The steelman for the "never observed" position: the silence of Kings, Chronicles, and the prophets about any jubilee enactment, combined with explicit complaint about debt-slavery (Neh 5), suggests the system remained programmatic.

The least intuitive position to steelman is the strict Anabaptist one. Yoder reads Jesus's jubilee announcement as binding on the church, not merely suggestive; the church is to be the visible society in which Leviticus 25 becomes flesh. This sounds utopian, but it has produced real communities that function economically for generations. The standard objections — scale, incentive, property — are met by Yoder's counter-point that the text was always meant to produce an alternative society, not regulate a secular state.

**Where this leaves us:** All major traditions affirm Leviticus 25's abiding principle that property is penultimate and the poor have legitimate claims; they diverge sharply on whether that principle expresses itself through the state's regulation of markets (Catholic social teaching), through the church's countercultural practice (Anabaptist), through individual charity under the order of creation (Lutheran), or through eschatological hope that awaits messianic fulfillment (rabbinic and much Reformed).

**Second tension: Hebrew vs. foreign slaves (vv. 39–46).** The chapter that proclaims *dĕrôr* also entrenches chattel slavery of foreigners. How is this read?

- **Catholic (modern):** The Second Vatican Council's *Gaudium et Spes* 27 names slavery among intrinsic evils; the Church has formally repudiated earlier theological tolerations. The text is historicized as accommodation to ANE economic reality.
- **Reformed:** Older Reformed thought (especially pro-slavery antebellum exegesis) cited Lev 25:44–46 as divine sanction; the abolitionist Reformed response (Charles Finney, Jonathan Blanchard) reframed the passage as descriptive regulation of a fallen institution, not prescriptive endorsement.
- **Jewish:** Maimonides (*Hilchot Avadim*) classifies Canaanite slavery as a legal institution that ideally should be softened by conversion; Sacks treats the passage as the Torah's accommodation to a non-ideal world while planting the seeds (the exodus memory, the universal imago Dei) that eventually dismantle the institution.
- **Anabaptist:** Yoder and Myers insist the asymmetry cannot be explained away. The chapter's liberating logic, applied to Israelites, was built on the backs of a non-Israelite underclass; the church that claims jubilee must read this against itself.
- **Critical:** Milgrom argues the distinction tracks the logic of covenant membership, not ethnic superiority; Sklar similarly reads it within the chapter's grammar of divine ownership. The steelman: foreigners in Israel had legal protections unparalleled elsewhere in the ANE (the *gēr* laws), and perpetual chattel slavery of foreigners was the surrounding world's universal default. The text both participates in this default and begins to subvert it via the exodus paradigm.

**Where this leaves us:** No tradition now defends the differential as normative. The serious exegetical questions are whether the text offers internal resources for its own critique (the exodus memory; the universal image; the gēr laws) and whether those resources are strong enough to bear the moral weight readers place on them. The asymmetry should not be papered over; Milgrom's historical-legal contextualization illuminates without neutralizing it.

**One unresolved question:** If Bergsma is right that the jubilee was never actually observed in ancient Israel, then what does it mean that Scripture preserves at its theological center a law that was a perpetual rebuke rather than a functioning institution — a utopian horizon whose power was precisely that it was not realized?

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references.

First: Jeremiah 34. In the late Judean monarchy, facing Babylonian siege, King Zedekiah proclaims a *dĕrôr* — a jubilee-style release of Hebrew slaves (34:8–10). The slaveholders comply under threat of siege, then re-enslave the released when the Babylonians briefly withdraw (34:11). Jeremiah pronounces judgment: *dĕrôr* for your slaves, YHWH says — so I proclaim a *dĕrôr* for you, to sword and plague and famine (34:17). The bitter pun weaponizes Leviticus 25's own vocabulary; liberty denied becomes liberty-to-be-destroyed. This is Leviticus 25 operating as active moral rebuke inside the Deuteronomistic History.

Second: Isaiah 61:1–2 and Luke 4:16–21. "The Spirit of the Lord God is upon me, because the LORD has anointed me to bring good news to the poor . . . to proclaim liberty (*dĕrôr*) to the captives . . . to proclaim the year of the LORD's favor." Isaiah weaves Leviticus 25's jubilee vocabulary into the servant-herald's announcement; Jesus at Nazareth reads this scroll, closes it, and declares, "Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing." Luke frames the whole of Jesus's ministry — healings, table-fellowship, forgiveness of debts, release of captives — as the eschatological jubilee. 11QMelchizedek had already fused Lev 25 with Isa 61; Jesus stands in a specific Second Temple interpretive tradition.

Third: Ruth 4 and Jeremiah 32. Boaz at the Bethlehem gate executes a *gĕʾullâ* — the kinsman-redemption of Elimelech's land for Naomi — and simultaneously assumes levirate responsibility for Ruth; the genealogy that closes the book (4:18–22) produces David. Jeremiah, imprisoned during the final siege, buys the field of his cousin Hanamel at Anathoth (Jer 32:6–15), performing the *gĕʾullâ* as prophetic sign that "houses and fields and vineyards shall again be bought in this land" — jubilee's hope in the teeth of exile. The Hebrew institution of redemption, obscure on first reading, turns out to anchor the two most consequential lineages in the Hebrew Bible: the house of David and the prophetic hope of return.
