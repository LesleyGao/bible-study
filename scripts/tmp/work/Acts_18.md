# Acts 18: Corinth, Gallio, and the Apollos Episode

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Acts 18 is one of the most chronologically anchored chapters in the New Testament. It contains two of the most secure synchronisms between Pauline mission and Roman imperial history: the **Claudian expulsion of Jews from Rome** (18:2) and the **proconsulship of Gallio in Achaia** (18:12). These external dating points form the spine on which most reconstructions of Pauline chronology hang.

**Claudius's edict** is attested by **Suetonius** in his *Life of Claudius* 25.4: "Iudaeos impulsore Chresto assidue tumultuantes Roma expulit"—"because the Jews were continuously rioting at the instigation of Chrestus, he expelled them from Rome." The most plausible interpretation, defended by F.F. Bruce, **Helmut Koester**, and **Peter Lampe** (*From Paul to Valentinus: Christians at Rome in the First Two Centuries*), is that *Chrestus* is a common Latin misspelling of *Christus*, and the disturbances reflect early intra-Jewish controversies over messianic preaching about Jesus in the Roman synagogues. The dating is contested—Orosius places it in 49 CE, but **H. Dixon Slingerland's** *Claudian Policymaking and the Early Imperial Repression of Judaism at Rome* argues for a different reading—but a 49 CE date is the broad consensus and aligns with Luke's "recently come from Italy" (18:2). **Aquila and Priscilla** are caught up in this expulsion. Aquila is from **Pontus** (a Black Sea province with a long-established Jewish community attested by Philo *Legatio* 281); their migration to Corinth, then to Ephesus (18:18-19), and eventually back to Rome (Romans 16:3) is a remarkable trajectory of mobile, urban-Jewish Christianity.

The shared trade is ***skēnopoioi*** (σκηνοποιοί, 18:3)—literally "tent-makers," but the term is debated. **Ronald Hock's** *The Social Context of Paul's Ministry* (1980) argues for leather-working more broadly, since first-century Cilician tents were typically made of *cilicium*, a goat-hair cloth, but the leather and canvas trades were closely related. The detail matters because Paul's manual labor (cf. 1 Thess 2:9, 1 Cor 4:12) was a deliberate strategy of self-support that gave him ethical leverage and freed his preaching from patron-client obligations.

**Corinth** in the mid-first century was a freshly Roman city. Old Corinth had been destroyed by the Roman general Lucius Mummius in 146 BCE; Julius Caesar refounded it as **Colonia Laus Iulia Corinthiensis** in 44 BCE. By Paul's day it was a bustling commercial hub at the isthmus, with two ports (Lechaion to the west, Cenchreae to the east), a population of perhaps 80,000-100,000, and a reputation for sexual license (the verb *korinthiazomai* meant "to fornicate"). **Jerome Murphy-O'Connor's** *St. Paul's Corinth: Texts and Archaeology* (4th ed. 2002) is the foundational synthesis. The chapter's geography is concrete: the **synagogue** at Corinth has yielded a fragmentary Greek inscription reading "*Synagogē Hebraiōn*" (Synagogue of the Hebrews), now in the Corinth Museum; while it dates to the third or fourth century CE, it likely marks a continuous Jewish synagogue site from the apostolic period. The ***bēma*** (βῆμα, "tribunal," 18:12, 16-17) where Paul stood before Gallio has been excavated in the central forum—a raised stone platform from which Roman officials addressed assemblies and adjudicated cases. The site is one of the few places in the world where the visitor can stand on the actual stones where a New Testament event occurred.

The **Erastus inscription** at Corinth—a paving stone inscribed *Erastus pro aedilitate sua pecunia stravit* ("Erastus laid this pavement at his own expense in return for his aedileship")—was discovered in 1929 next to the theater. **Henry Cadbury** in 1931 proposed that this Erastus is the same man Paul names in **Romans 16:23** as the *oikonomos tēs poleōs* ("city treasurer") of Corinth. The identification is plausible but not certain (Erastus was a common name; *aedile* and *oikonomos* are not exact equivalents); **Steven Friesen** in *Reading the Corinthians Letters* has critiqued the identification, while **Andrew Clarke's** *Secular and Christian Leadership in Corinth* defends it. Either way, the inscription anchors a wealthy, civically engaged stratum of early Corinthian Christianity.

The **Gallio Inscription** is the chapter's—and arguably Acts's—most important external chronological anchor. Discovered in fragments at **Delphi** beginning in 1905 and published by Émile Bourguet in 1923 with subsequent reconstructions by **Adolf Deissmann**, the inscription is a copy of a letter from the Emperor Claudius to Delphi referring to "Lucius Junius Gallio my friend and proconsul of Achaia" (*Loukios Iounios Gallion ho philos mou kai anthypatos*). The letter dates to the period of Claudius's 26th imperial acclamation, which can be fixed to the first half of 52 CE. Since proconsuls served annual terms (typically beginning in early summer and running about a year), Gallio's term most plausibly ran from approximately July 51 to summer 52 CE. Paul's appearance before Gallio at 18:12 thus falls within this window. Working backward from this anchor and using Luke's "stayed a year and six months" (18:11), Paul's Corinthian mission can be dated approximately 50-52 CE. **Bruce's** NICNT, **Schnabel**, **Marguerat**, **Witherington**, and **Keener** all treat the Gallio Inscription as the keystone of Pauline chronology; **Jerome Murphy-O'Connor's** *Paul: A Critical Life* is the most thorough single-volume reconstruction.

**Gallio** himself is well-attested. He was the elder brother of the Stoic philosopher **Seneca the Younger** (born Marcus Annaeus Novatus; adopted into the Junius Gallio family), and Seneca dedicated his *De Ira* and *De Vita Beata* to him. Pliny the Elder, Tacitus, and Cassius Dio all mention him. He was apparently of mild disposition; after his Achaian proconsulship he held the suffect consulship in Rome, but eventually he was forced to commit suicide under Nero in 65 CE during the same purge that took Seneca.

Greek terms worth noting: ***bēma*** (βῆμα, 18:12) carries judicial weight—it is the official Roman tribunal, distinct from informal civic platforms. ***archisynagōgos*** (ἀρχισυνάγωγος, "ruler of the synagogue," 18:8, 17) is a common honorific in Diaspora-Jewish inscriptions. **Crispus** is named in 1 Cor 1:14 as one of the few Paul personally baptized; **Sosthenes** (18:17) is plausibly the same man named in 1 Cor 1:1 as Paul's co-author—a trajectory from synagogue ruler beaten before Gallio to Christian co-worker.

Finally, the **Cenchreae vow** (18:18). Paul cuts his hair "for he was under a vow"—almost certainly a Nazirite vow per Numbers 6, taken voluntarily and concluded by hair-cutting. The detail is striking because it shows Paul, the apostle to the Gentiles, observing a distinctively Jewish ritual practice. **Matthew Thiessen** in *A Jewish Paul* and **Paula Fredriksen** in *Paul: The Pagan's Apostle* read this as evidence that Paul's mission did not abolish his own Jewish observance—he remained a Torah-observant Jew while preaching a gospel that did not impose Torah on Gentiles.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 18 is structurally transitional. It closes the Second Missionary Journey (18:22, "went down to Antioch") and opens the Third (18:23). Luke compresses what must have been months of travel into a few verses, signaling that his interest is not exhaustive itinerary but selective theology—the long Corinthian stay (a year and six months, 18:11) gets disproportionate space because Corinth becomes a major Christian center.

The **Gallio scene** (18:12-17) is one of Luke's most carefully constructed legal narratives. The charge is religious-legal: "this man is persuading people to worship God contrary to the law" (18:13). The ambiguity in *nomos* ("law")—does it mean Jewish law or Roman law?—is rhetorically deliberate. Gallio cuts through it: "questions about words and names and your own law" (18:15). The proconsul's ruling has been read as **the first major Roman judicial precedent regarding Christianity**: Christianity is treated as an intra-Jewish dispute and therefore as part of the legally-protected *religio licita* of Judaism. **Adolf Deissmann** and **F.F. Bruce** read this as Luke's deliberate apologetic—Luke shows that imperial authority itself recognized Christianity's legitimacy. **Conzelmann** and **Pervo** treat the Lukan apologetic motive as so pronounced that the historicity is doubtful, but **Witherington** and **Keener** defend the scene as historically plausible: Gallio's mild, philosophical temperament (consistent with what Seneca tells us about his brother) and the well-attested principle of Roman non-interference in religious disputes both fit.

The narrative beat at 18:17—"all seized Sosthenes, the ruler of the synagogue, and beat him in front of the tribunal. But Gallio paid no attention"—is structurally ironic. The plaintiffs become the victims; the proconsul's indifference protects the church indirectly. **Tannehill** reads this as Luke's typical reversal pattern.

The **Apollos episode** (18:24-28) is one of the chapter's most discussed literary units. Luke introduces Apollos with unusual detail: native of **Alexandria** (the great center of Hellenistic-Jewish scholarship, home of Philo and the Septuagint), eloquent (*logios*—a term that can mean "learned" or "skilled in rhetoric"), competent in the Scriptures, instructed in the way of the Lord, fervent in spirit. But he "knew only the baptism of John." **Priscilla and Aquila** privately instruct him "the way of God more accurately." The order in 18:26—"Priscilla and Aquila"—names the wife first, an unusual ordering that **Beverly Gaventa**, **C.K. Barrett**, and many commentators take as a deliberate signal of Priscilla's prominence in the instruction of Apollos. Codex Bezae and some Western witnesses reverse the order to "Aquila and Priscilla," apparently uncomfortable with Priscilla's precedence; this is one of the clearer cases of theologically-motivated textual variation.

Apollos's later prominence at Corinth (1 Cor 1:12, 3:4-6) is foreshadowed at 18:27-28. Paul's relationship with Apollos in 1 Corinthians is famously generous—"I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth"—suggesting the catechetical correction at Ephesus produced a fellow worker rather than a rival. The whole episode is a quiet study in the dynamics of theological transmission: an eloquent, learned, partially-informed teacher; private correction by a married pair; commendation by letter to another church; expanded fruitfulness.

Luke's "**WE**" passages drop out in chapter 18 (they will not return until Acts 20:5). The narrator returns to third person, and Paul travels alone or with named companions. This narrative discipline supports the reading that the "we" passages reflect actual presence rather than literary convention—they appear and disappear with the apparent comings and goings of the narrator.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Chapter 18 has been a battleground in Pauline studies, particularly around two questions: the **shape of Paul's mission strategy** and the **role of women in early Christianity**.

On **mission strategy**, the chapter shows a pattern that Christian interpreters have read variously. Paul enters a city, seeks the synagogue, faces Jewish opposition, "shakes out his garments" (18:6—a prophetic gesture parallel to Nehemiah 5:13), turns to the Gentiles, gathers a mixed congregation including some Jews (e.g., the synagogue ruler Crispus), and stays as long as the work requires. **F.F. Bruce's** *Paul: Apostle of the Heart Set Free* presents this as Paul's standard methodology; **N.T. Wright's** *Paul: A Biography* emphasizes that Paul's "to the Gentiles" turn at 18:6 is not a permanent abandonment of Jewish mission but a specific local response—he returns to synagogue preaching at Ephesus (18:19) and elsewhere. **E.P. Sanders'** *Paul, the Law, and the Jewish People* and **Krister Stendahl's** *Paul Among Jews and Gentiles* both insist that Paul's mission is fundamentally apostolic to the nations, not anti-Jewish; the synagogue-then-Gentiles pattern reflects the pragmatic reality that Diaspora synagogues were the most efficient access point to both Jews and God-fearing Gentiles.

The **Paul-within-Judaism school** (Eisenbaum, Thiessen, Fredriksen) reads the Cenchreae Nazirite vow (18:18) as decisive evidence: the Paul of Acts is a Torah-observant Jew whose mission to Gentiles does not abolish his own Jewish identity. **Reinhartz**, **Levine**, and **Boyarin** all agree at least on this minimal point: Acts 18 does not portray a "former Jew" but a Jewish missionary.

On **women in the apostolic mission**, **Priscilla** is the chapter's most significant figure. She and her husband Aquila are named six times in the New Testament (Acts 18:2, 18, 26; Rom 16:3; 1 Cor 16:19; 2 Tim 4:19), and in four of those six the woman is named first. The plain textual fact—that Priscilla is named alongside (and often before) Aquila, and that the pair together "explained to Apollos the way of God more accurately"—has long been taken by commentators as evidence that early Christian house churches included women in substantial instructional roles; this datum figures prominently on the egalitarian side of the ongoing **complementarian–egalitarian** debate over women in ministry (Linda Belleville's *Women Leaders and the Church*, Philip Payne, Craig Keener, and Ben Witherington III on the one side; Köstenberger, Schreiner, and Grudem on the other). **Adolf Harnack** in 1900 famously proposed that Priscilla wrote Hebrews; the proposal has not gained scholarly consensus but reflects the high estimate of her capacity.

**Patristic readers** treated the Apollos episode as a model of catechetical correction. **Chrysostom's** 40th homily on Acts emphasizes Apollos's humility in receiving correction from a married pair without status credentials. **Calvin's** commentary draws the lesson that even the most gifted teachers need ongoing instruction.

**Gallio's ruling** has cast a long shadow on church-state theology. The patristic tradition—**Tertullian**, **Eusebius**—appealed to Acts 18:14-15 as evidence that Roman law historically distinguished religion from sedition, a precedent the church would invoke during persecutions. **Calvin** and the Reformed tradition treated Gallio's indifference more cautiously: a magistrate who refuses to judge religious truth is at best neutral, not an ally. **Modern church-state writings** (e.g., Robert Wilken's *The First Thousand Years*) read the scene as one of the founding moments of Christian-Roman jurisprudence: the precedent that Christianity is internal-Jewish protected the early church for several decades before the Neronian persecution and again before Domitian.

**Contemporary commentators** treat the chapter densely. **Bruce's** NICNT integrates the Gallio Inscription thoroughly; **Barrett's** ICC analyzes the legal language with characteristic precision; **Fitzmyer**, **Gaventa**, **Johnson**, **Keener**, **Witherington**, **Schnabel**, **Conzelmann**, **Haenchen**, **Pervo**, **Peterson**, **Marguerat** all give it sustained attention. **Murphy-O'Connor's** Corinth-focused work is essential.

**Justo González's** Acts commentary notes that the riot at 18:17 is borne by Sosthenes, a Jewish leader, while the Gentile Roman proconsul looks on indifferently—the scene exposing the precarity of religious minorities under Roman rule and the way imperial "neutrality" can function as tacit permission for violence. The Priscilla material continues to anchor the broader theological question of how the church's later instructions on women teaching (1 Tim 2:11–15) relate to the instructional role she plainly exercises here—a question debated across the egalitarian and complementarian commentary literature.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: divine encouragement and human persistence.** At 18:9-10, the Lord appears to Paul in a night vision: "Do not be afraid, but go on speaking and do not be silent, for I am with you, and no one will attack you to harm you, for I have many in this city who are my people." **Three positions** on what this means theologically: (1) **Reformed providence** (Calvin, the Westminster tradition): the vision discloses divine election—God already has his people in Corinth, and Paul's preaching is the means by which they are gathered, not the cause of their salvation. (2) **Arminian-evangelical**: the vision is encouragement to a fearful apostle, and "many in this city who are my people" describes the foreknown rather than the predestined; Paul's persistence is the contingent means of their conversion. (3) **Open theism** / relational theism (Pinnock, Boyd): the vision is a divine guarantee of present and future protection, not a metaphysical claim about pre-temporal election; God's "having" people is anticipatory, not strictly foreknown. The steelman: (1) the language is striking and underscores divine initiative; (2) Paul's freedom and fear are real; (3) the vision is genuinely promissory and addresses Paul's fear, not just his metaphysics. **Where this leaves us**: the vision does not resolve the predestination-foreknowledge debate, but it does insist that mission rests on divine guarantee rather than missionary heroics. Paul's eighteen-month stay is *given*, not earned.

**Tension 2: the church and Roman law.** Gallio's refusal to adjudicate (18:14-16) sets a precedent that Christians have evaluated variously. **Three positions**: (1) **Constantinian / establishment**: Roman law's recognition of Christianity (even by indifference) is providential; the church should welcome legal protection and seek it. (2) **Anabaptist / sectarian** (Yoder, Hauerwas): the church should not seek imperial legal recognition because it inevitably compromises gospel witness; Gallio's protection is incidental, not normative. (3) **Two Kingdoms** (Lutheran, neo-Reformed): the magistrate has a divinely ordained role to maintain civil order, and the church should expect and accept such protection without confusing it with the gospel itself. The steelman: (1) the church benefited concretely from Gallio's ruling and continues to benefit from rule of law; (2) Roman protection was always conditional and would later become persecution under Nero; (3) civil and ecclesiastical authority are distinct and should not collapse into each other. **Where this leaves us**: the chapter neither sanctifies imperial protection nor rejects it. Paul does not seek Gallio's ruling; he receives it. The gospel can flourish under indifferent magistrates, hostile magistrates, or favorable ones, but it should not depend on any of these.

**Unresolved question**: what becomes of Sosthenes? He is beaten before the tribunal in 18:17. If he is the Sosthenes who appears as Paul's co-author in 1 Corinthians 1:1, then the man beaten as the Jewish synagogue ruler (likely the new ruler after Crispus's conversion) became a Christian co-worker—a remarkable trajectory that Luke leaves untold. The text does not specify whether this is the same Sosthenes; the canonical silence preserves the genuine uncertainty.

## The Thread

The Gallio Inscription's c. 51-52 CE dating (18:12) anchors **1 Thessalonians**—plausibly written from Corinth during this stay—as one of the earliest New Testament documents. Priscilla and Aquila's trajectory across **Romans 16:3, 1 Corinthians 16:19, and 2 Timothy 4:19** maps the geographic mobility of apostolic Christianity. The Cenchreae Nazirite vow (18:18) anticipates Paul's later vow-completion in **Acts 21:23-26** and stands in tension—generative tension—with the Pauline insistence in **Galatians** that Gentiles need not Judaize. Apollos's emergence at Corinth (18:27-28) prepares the divisive partisan loyalties that Paul will rebuke in **1 Corinthians 1:10-17 and 3:4-9**, where the resolution—"neither he who plants nor he who waters is anything, but only God who gives the growth"—is the apostle's mature reflection on what should never have been a rivalry.
