# Psalm 78 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1 — The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 78 is the second-longest psalm in the Psalter — seventy-two verses, surpassed only by Psalm 119. Its superscription names it a *maskil of Asaph* (מַשְׂכִּיל לְאָסָף, *maskîl le-Asaph*), and that double tag locates it socially and pedagogically. *Maskil* (from the root *s-k-l*, "to be prudent, to instruct") signals a teaching-poem; thirteen psalms carry the label, and Psalm 78 fits the description more visibly than any of them. Asaph, the named author or guild, is the Levitical chief musician David appointed at the ark in 1 Chronicles 16. Twelve psalms (50, 73-83) belong to the Asaphite cluster — eleven of them gathered here in Book III, the heart of the so-called **Elohistic Psalter** (Pss 42-83), where the divine name *YHWH* gives way disproportionately to *Elohim*. Psalm 78 is therefore Levitical sanctuary poetry: written for, and likely chanted in, the Jerusalem cult by guild musicians whose vocation was to shape Israel's collective memory through song.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 78 as a **historical psalm** (*Geschichtspsalm*) — a small subgenre of didactic poetry that recites Israel's national past as moral instruction. It belongs with Psalms 105, 106, and 136, but stands apart from all of them. Where Psalm 105 narrates triumph and Psalm 106 narrates failure, Psalm 78 holds both in tension across two parallel cycles, each tracing the same arc — divine wonder, human rebellion, divine wrath, divine mercy. Erhard Gerstenberger refines the form-critical label to "didactic-historical recital," and Sigmund Mowinckel locates the psalm in the cult's covenant-renewal ceremonies, where the priestly-Levitical guild rehearsed Israel's past so that each new generation would hear it as their own story.

Dating is contested but converges on a window. The psalm climaxes with Yahweh's rejection of "the tent of Joseph" and the tribe of Ephraim and his choice instead of "the tribe of Judah, Mount Zion, which he loves" (78:67-68). The psalm also memorializes the destruction of the Shiloh sanctuary (78:60), a northern shrine whose fall is implied in 1 Samuel 4 and confirmed archaeologically by the eleventh-century BCE destruction layer Israel Finkelstein excavated at Khirbet Seilun. Most critical scholars therefore place Psalm 78 either in the late tenth century (Mitchell Dahood, propagating Davidic-era court theology) or after the fall of the northern kingdom in 722 BCE, when a Judean poet looking northward could read Samaria's collapse as the long-deferred ratification of Yahweh's southward turn (Hossfeld and Zenger, Marvin Tate). Richard Clifford and J. Clinton McCann lean toward the post-722 date; Goldingay holds open both possibilities. The text's pro-Davidic, anti-Ephraimite political theology is unmistakable on either reading.

Hebrew terms anchor the historical world. The psalmist opens by promising to speak in *mashal* (מָשָׁל, "parable, proverb, riddling instruction") and *chidot mini-qedem* (חִידוֹת מִנִּי־קֶדֶם, "dark sayings from of old," 78:2). *Mashal* is the wisdom-tradition's proper noun for instructive comparison — Solomon's *mishlei* in Proverbs 1:1 use the same root — and pairing it with *chidah* (the riddle Samson poses in Judges 14) signals that the recital that follows is not bare chronicle but interpretive disclosure, history-as-pedagogy. The verb *bachar* (בָּחַר, "he chose") rings four times in the closing strophe — *bachar Yehuda* ("he chose Judah," 78:68), *bachar David* ("he chose David," 78:70) — the key verb of Israel's election theology, transposed from the nation as a whole onto a single tribe and a single king. *Shilo* (שִׁלוֹ, 78:60) names the lost northern sanctuary; *Tzion* (צִיּוֹן, 78:68) names what replaces it. The geography of the psalm is also the geography of its argument.

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of Psalm 78 in 4QPsa, 4QPse, and Mas1e, with text largely matching the Masoretic Tradition. The Septuagint renders the psalm faithfully as *psalmos* 77, with the noteworthy choice of *parabole* for *mashal* in v. 2 — a rendering that will matter enormously for Matthew's Gospel. Ancient Near Eastern parallels are looser here than in lament psalms: the genre of recited national history is more characteristically Israelite than Mesopotamian, but the Hittite "Plague Prayers of Mursili II" share the form's underlying logic — that present catastrophe must be narrated against a backdrop of broken covenant.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Bishop Robert Lowth's parallelism is the basic engine of the psalm's seventy-two verses, and Psalm 78 deploys all three of his types in close succession. *Synonymous parallelism* opens the psalm: "Give ear, O my people, to my teaching; / incline your ears to the words of my mouth" (78:1) — the second line restates the first with intensified verbs. *Antithetic parallelism* surfaces in the final strophe's contrasts — "He rejected the tent of Joseph; / he did not choose the tribe of Ephraim, / but he chose the tribe of Judah" (78:67-68). *Synthetic parallelism* — where the second line completes rather than mirrors the first — drives the long narrative middle, allowing the psalm to advance across decades of remembered history without breaking poetic line.

The macro-structure is a **double recital**, two parallel cycles arranged as a literary diptych. Cycle one (78:12-39) covers Egypt, sea-crossing, wilderness wandering, manna, and quail; cycle two (78:40-72) re-narrates Egypt with the plague-list, conquest, the apostasy of the high places, the destruction of Shiloh, and finally the choice of David and Zion. Each cycle moves through the same emotional grammar — divine deliverance, Israelite rebellion, divine wrath, surprising mercy — and the repetition is deliberate. The psalm is teaching a pattern, not a chronicle. Strophic divisions follow the cycles' internal turns: 78:1-8 (prologue, the pedagogical contract), 78:9-16 (Ephraim's failure, Egypt-to-wilderness deliverance), 78:17-31 (wilderness rebellion and quail), 78:32-39 (rebellion-mercy summary), 78:40-55 (Egypt re-narrated through the plagues, conquest), 78:56-64 (post-conquest apostasy, Shiloh's fall), 78:65-72 (the divine awakening and the choice of David). The doublet structure is itself a *mashal* — a parable in which the same moral plays twice and the second performance reveals what the first only implied.

Inclusio binds the psalm. The opening verse promises the *mashal* of "things that we have heard and known, that our fathers have told us" (78:3); the closing verses deliver that *mashal* in the figure of David, whom God "took from the sheepfolds... to shepherd Jacob his people" (78:70-71). The frame moves from speaking to shepherding, from the teacher's mouth (78:1-2) to the king's hand (78:72) — *kaf-pav* (כַפָּיו, "his palms, his skillful hand"). Pedagogy and pastoral rule are the psalm's bookends.

Chiastic structure organizes the cycles' internal logic. The wilderness cycle (78:17-39) pivots on the verbs of forgetting and remembering: Israel "forgets" (*shakhechu*, שָׁכְחוּ, 78:11), God "remembers" (*va-yizkor*, וַיִּזְכֹּר, 78:39) that they are flesh — the human memory fails, the divine memory holds. The conquest cycle (78:40-72) pivots on the verbs of choosing: God "rejects" Joseph (*va-yim'as*, וַיִּמְאַס, 78:67) and "chooses" Judah and David (*va-yivchar*, וַיִּבְחַר, 78:68, 70). The two cycles are also linked by chiastic inversion: cycle one ends with God remembering Israel's frailty; cycle two ends with God choosing one specific Israelite. The arc moves from compassion-for-flesh to election-of-flesh.

The psalm has no *selah* markers — unusual for a long psalm in the Asaphite collection. Hossfeld and Zenger argue this absence reflects the psalm's didactic genre: a *mashal* is to be heard through, not punctuated. The omission underscores the psalm's pedagogical urgency. The teacher speaks until the lesson lands.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story (Jewish and Christian Trajectories)

Psalm 78 sits in **Book III** of the Psalter (Pss 73-89), the book Gerald Wilson reads as the canonical bookkeeping of Davidic kingship in crisis. Book III opens with Psalm 73's question of theodicy and closes with Psalm 89's anguished cry that the Davidic covenant has failed. Psalm 78, near the start of the Asaphite cluster (73-83), supplies the historical justification for what Book III mourns. If Psalm 89 grieves a broken covenant, Psalm 78 explains how God came to make it in the first place — through a long arc of rebellion that climaxes in Yahweh's choice of David and Zion. Wilson reads the placement as deliberate: Book III asks whether Israel's God still keeps faith, and Psalm 78 answers by showing that he kept it once, decisively, when no human party deserved it.

The **Jewish trajectory** weighs the psalm's pedagogy heavily. The Dead Sea sectarians at Qumran included the psalm in their psalters but did not, as far as we can tell, give it special prominence — perhaps because their sectarian identity sat uneasily with a psalm that vindicated the Jerusalem cult and the Judean line. *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 78 dwells on the verbs of teaching: "Give ear, O my people, to my teaching" — the rabbis read *torati* (תּוֹרָתִי, "my teaching") as Torah itself, so that Asaph speaks in God's voice and the psalm becomes a model of how Torah is to be transmitted across generations. Rashi reads 78:2 — "I will open my mouth in a parable" — as Asaph's claim to prophetic authority; Ibn Ezra emphasizes the *peshat* (plain-sense) reading, that the parable simply means the teaching itself; Radak treats the psalm's anti-Ephraim climax as a historical warning to all later northern apostates. Nachmanides reads the choice of David in 78:70-72 as foundational for Jewish messianic expectation — the psalm not only commemorates an election but anticipates a final shepherd. Levenson reads the doublet structure theologically: the psalm refuses to let Israel forget that mercy followed rebellion, twice, and that a third turn — return from exile, restoration of David — must follow the same pattern. Sarna emphasizes the psalm's pedagogy of memory; Buber, the dialogical "you" the speaker addresses to Israel; Heschel, the psalm's prophetic indignation directed inward at the people. Liturgically the psalm is not a fixed daily reading in synagogue use, but verses (especially 78:38, *ve-hu rachum yekhapper avon*, "but he, being compassionate, atoned for their iniquity") feature in penitential prayers and find a permanent place in *Tachanun*.

The **Christian trajectory** runs through the psalm's verse 2 with extraordinary weight. Matthew 13:35 quotes the verse — "I will open my mouth in parables; I will utter what has been hidden since the foundation of the world" — and applies it directly to Jesus's parable-teaching, identifying Jesus as the *mashal*-speaker the psalm names. The Septuagint's choice of *parabole* for *mashal* makes the move possible: Matthew reads Jesus as the long-promised teacher of *paraboles* whose dark sayings disclose what was hidden from of old. Augustine, in his *Enarrationes in Psalmos*, reads the whole of Psalm 78 Christologically — the rebellion of the wilderness generation prefigures the unbelief of the Jews who reject Jesus, and the choice of David from the sheepfolds prefigures the choice of Christ from the obscurity of Nazareth. Athanasius and Cassiodorus follow similar paths. Aquinas, in his commentary on the Psalms, reads David's pastoral office (78:71-72) as a type of Christ's good shepherding — the link to John 10 is explicit in the medieval reading. Luther in the *Operationes in Psalmos* uses Psalm 78 to preach simul iustus et peccator: Israel is forgiven and rebellious at once, and the psalm's God is the God who atones (*kipper*, 78:38) for the very people who provoke him. Calvin's 1557 commentary reads the psalm as a primer in covenant pedagogy: parents are to instruct children, and the church is to instruct itself, by remembering both grace and failure.

Modern critical readings deepen the picture. Gunkel set the form-critical category; Mowinckel located the psalm in the cult; Westermann emphasized its didactic function; Kraus and Hossfeld-Zenger gave the most thorough treatments of the psalm's editorial placement and its relation to Book III's crisis-of-Davidic-kingship architecture. Goldingay's three-volume *Psalms* commentary handles 78 with care, refusing to reduce its anti-Ephraim climax to mere propaganda. Tate, Wenham, Brueggemann (especially in *David's Truth* and *The Message of the Psalms*), Patrick Miller, William Brown, and Beth Tanner all engage the psalm seriously — Brueggemann reads it as Davidic-cult ideology that nonetheless tells truth about human rebellion, and Miller emphasizes the psalm's claim that history itself becomes scripture for the worshipping community. Christopher Mitchell and the messianic-trajectory school (Robert Cole, David Mitchell) read the closing election of David as part of the Psalter's larger eschatological design.

N. T. Wright treats psalms like 78 — alongside the long historical recitals of Nehemiah 9 and Ezra's prayers — as evidence for one of his central claims (*The New Testament and the People of God*, *The Climax of the Covenant*): Second-Temple Jews understood themselves as living inside a continuing story, not merely remembering a closed past, and that story had the shape of rebellion, judgment, exile, and a still-awaited deliverance through a Davidic shepherd-king. On Wright's reading the Gospels' claim is that Jesus is the climax of exactly that story; Matthew's quotation of 78:2 is not a proof-text mined at random but a signal that the *mashal*-speaker who would disclose "what has been hidden since the foundation of the world" has arrived. The psalm's NT use beyond Matthew 13:35 is real but quieter. The image of Christ as the shepherd from the sheepfolds (John 10:11; Heb 13:20; 1 Pet 5:4) draws on the same Davidic shepherd typology Psalm 78 climaxes with. Hebrews 3-4 echoes the wilderness-rebellion theme in its warning against unbelief, drawing in part on Psalm 78's pattern (though more directly on Ps 95).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions are unavoidable, and the honest reading holds both rather than dissolving either.

**Tension one: the pro-Davidic, anti-Ephraim political theology.** Psalm 78's climactic strophe rejects Joseph, refuses Ephraim, and chooses Judah and Zion. There is no way to read this neutrally. Three positions on the field. (1) **Cult-propaganda reading** (Mowinckel, in part Brueggemann's *David's Truth*): the psalm is Davidic-Zionic court theology, composed to justify the southern monarchy's claim against northern rivals — read as such, it is a partisan document and its God-language must be interpreted as ideological. (2) **Historical-vindication reading** (Tate, Hossfeld-Zenger, the broadly post-722 dating school): after Samaria's fall the psalm reads northern collapse as theological judgment, but does so within a tradition that included the northern prophets (Amos, Hosea) and is therefore not pure southern partisanship — the psalm's theology is Israelite-wide even when its political conclusions are Judean. (3) **Selective-recital reading** (Brueggemann in *David's Truth*; Goldingay): the psalm's recital is openly selective — it privileges one tribe, one sanctuary, one king, and passes over a great deal — and a careful reader names that selectivity rather than naturalizing it, reading Psalm 78 against the Joseph traditions and the northern prophets that also belong to the canon. The steelman of all three: the psalm itself acknowledges that the choice of Judah is *bachar* — election, gracious and undeserved — not merit-based reward; the partisan conclusion sits on a non-partisan foundation, that grace, not desert, is what does the choosing. **Where this leaves us:** the psalm is unembarrassed about its southern angle, and a faithful reading must be unembarrassed too — both about hearing the partisan voice and about pressing it against the prophets and the Joseph traditions that also belong to scripture. The God who chooses Judah in Psalm 78 is the same God who, according to Hosea, never stops loving Ephraim.

**Tension two: the parable-form and Matthew 13:35's Christological appropriation.** The psalm calls itself a *mashal*; Matthew's Gospel calls Jesus the *mashal*-speaker fulfilling 78:2; Christian tradition reads the whole psalm as typology pointing to Christ. Three positions. (1) **Direct prophetic-fulfillment reading** (older Christian tradition; Aquinas, Calvin, much evangelical commentary): Psalm 78:2 is a predictive prophecy that Jesus fulfills literally, and Matthew is reading it correctly as such. (2) **Typological reading** (Augustine, Luther, much patristic exegesis): the psalm establishes a pattern — God speaks in *meshalim*, and the supreme *mashal*-speaker is Jesus — without making 78:2 a one-to-one prediction. (3) **Christianizing-overreach critique** (Jewish scholars, some critical Christian readers like Walter Moberly): Matthew's appropriation is a creative midrashic move that the psalmist himself would not have recognized, and treating the psalm as primarily Christological risks erasing the Jewish pedagogy that 78 is actually doing. Steelman across all three: Matthew's quotation is neither a violent misreading nor a flat prediction-fulfillment; it is the kind of inner-biblical interpretation that the rabbis themselves performed when they read Asaph's *torah* as Torah, and recognizing it as such honors both the Christian and the Jewish reading practices. **Where this leaves us:** Christians can hold that Matthew's reading discloses something true about Jesus without claiming it exhausts what Psalm 78 was doing. Jews can hold that the psalm is fundamentally Israel's pedagogy without denying that Christians have heard a real echo of Christ in its parable-language. The psalm is generous enough to bear both readings.

A final closing question. The psalm ends with David shepherding Israel "with upright heart... and skillful hand" (78:72). Every reader of the books of Samuel and Kings knows that David's heart was not always upright and his hand not always skillful. The psalm tells the truth about Israel's rebellion but seems to tell only part of the truth about Israel's chosen king. Is the psalm therefore propaganda, or is it teaching something subtler — that the election of David, like the election of Israel, is grace, and that grace describes God's commitment more accurately than it describes the recipient's performance? The question is the psalm's, not ours, and Book III as a whole — culminating in Psalm 89's grief over the failed Davidic covenant — suggests that the Psalter itself is wrestling with exactly this tension. Psalm 78 names David as the chosen shepherd; Psalm 89 weeps that the chosen shepherd's house has fallen. To read 78 honestly is to read it as the opening half of a question the rest of the Psalter spends decades answering — and that the New Testament will claim has been answered, finally, in another shepherd from another sheepfold.

## The Thread

- **Matthew 13:35** quotes Psalm 78:2 — "I will open my mouth in parables; I will utter what has been hidden since the foundation of the world" — applying the *mashal*-speaker's identity to Jesus.
- **Hosea 11:1-9** stands in counter-tension with Psalm 78's anti-Ephraim climax: Hosea's God refuses to give Ephraim up, even after judgment.
- **John 10:11-16** echoes the Davidic-shepherd image of Psalm 78:70-72, transposing the shepherd-king typology onto Jesus as the good shepherd.
- **Hebrews 3:7-4:11** recapitulates the wilderness-rebellion pattern Psalm 78 narrates, warning the church not to repeat the unbelief of the generation that perished in the desert.
