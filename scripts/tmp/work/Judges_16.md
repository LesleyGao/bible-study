# Judges 16: The Strong Man Grinding at the Mill

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Judges 16 lands us in the heart of Philistia at the close of Iron Age I (roughly the 11th century BCE), the period when the Philistine pentapolis — Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Gath, and Ekron — had completed its consolidation along the southern coastal plain. The Philistines were one wave of the Sea Peoples whose movements convulsed the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE; the inscriptions of Ramesses III at Medinet Habu memorialize their defeat by Egypt and likely their resettlement as garrison populations on the Egyptian frontier. By the time of Samson's death, the Philistines had become not refugees but a settled urban civilization with their own iconography, ceramics (the famous bichrome ware), pork-heavy diet (anomalous in the region), and a politically tight five-city league governed by lords called *seranim* (סְרָנִים) — a non-Semitic word, possibly cognate to Greek *tyrannos*, almost certainly preserving the Aegean political vocabulary the Philistines brought with them. The Israelite hill-country tribes, meanwhile, had no king, no standing army, and no Iron Age technology comparable to what Philistine smiths controlled (cf. 1 Sam 13:19-22). Samson is not leading an army. He is one man on the western frontier, in the no-man's-land between the Sorek Valley and Philistine Gaza.

The material culture of the chapter is dense and specific. Gaza's city gate, which Samson rips up "bar and all" (v. 3), would have been a multi-chambered stone-and-mudbrick structure on the order of the Iron Age gates excavated at Ashkelon by Lawrence Stager and at Tel Miqne-Ekron by Trude Dothan and Seymour Gitin — heavy, fortified, hinged on stone sockets. Carrying its doors to "the top of the hill that is in front of Hebron" is folkloric exaggeration of the highest order; Hebron sits roughly forty miles inland in the Judean hill country. The mill (v. 21) is a rotary or saddle-stone grain mill whose operation in Iron Age Israel was, almost without exception, household women's labor (Carol Meyers, *Discovering Eve*, on the gendered division of food-processing labor). Block, Webb, and Niditch all note the humiliation registered in v. 21: the strong man assigned the slave's grinding task is being forced into the most domesticated and least martial form of forced labor a defeated warrior could be assigned. The Dagon temple at Gaza, where the chapter's catastrophe unfolds, has not been excavated, but the architectural reconstruction is plausible: Amihai Mazar's excavation of the Philistine temple at Tell Qasile (just north of Tel Aviv) revealed a roofed central hall whose ceiling rested on two wooden pillars set close together on stone bases. A strong man could conceivably push them apart. The Ekron temple complex shows the same general pattern. The narrator knew Philistine architecture.

Authorship and dating are contested in familiar ways. Martin Noth's 1943 thesis read Judges as part of the Deuteronomistic History, redacted in the exilic period to explain the catastrophe of 587 BCE through the lens of covenant infidelity. Frank Moore Cross modified this with a double-redaction model — a Josianic edition pushing for centralized worship, then an exilic update. Samson sits at the end of the savior-cycles proper. Robert Polzin and Marc Brettler have argued that the entire Samson cycle (chs. 13-16) was likely a folkloric tradition cluster — Susan Niditch and Gregory Mobley (in *The Empty Men*) compare it explicitly to ancient Near Eastern strongman tales like Gilgamesh and Enkidu — that the Deuteronomistic editor placed at the climax of the savior period to demonstrate the bankruptcy of the charismatic-judge institution before the appendix (chs. 17-21) drives home the case for monarchy.

Five Hebrew terms shape what English flattens. *Seranim* (סְרָנִים), the Philistine "lords," is a title found nowhere in cognate Semitic languages — a fossil of Aegean political vocabulary. *Nazir* (נָזִיר, "consecrated, separated one"), referenced when Samson finally tells Delilah his secret in v. 17, ties this story to the legislation in Numbers 6: hair uncut, no wine, no contact with the dead. Samson has violated every condition except the haircut, and that final breach is what undoes him. *Daggon* (דָּגוֹן), the Philistine deity, was once popularly etymologized as "fish" (from *dag*, fish), but the Ugaritic and Eblaite evidence makes clear that Dagon was a grain god, possibly a storm god — fitting that Samson, the man of the field who burned the Philistine harvest in ch. 15, dies in the temple of the grain god. *Tachan* (טָחַן, "to grind"), the verb in v. 21, has a sexual double-entendre well-attested in Job 31:10 and in rabbinic literature; some commentators (notably Yair Zakovitch and James Crenshaw) have suggested that the Hebrew is hinting that Samson is being used sexually in the prison as well. *Sachaq* (שָׂחַק, "to entertain, sport, amuse" in v. 25) is the same verb that describes Isaac "playing" with Rebekah in Gen 26:8 in a clearly sexual sense — what exactly the Philistines made Samson do "between the pillars" before he prayed is darker than the polite English suggests.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Judges 16 is, in genre terms, the closing act of a tragic novella. Niditch and Mobley have classed the broader Samson cycle as folkloric heroic narrative, structurally close to the Mesopotamian Gilgamesh tradition and the Greek Heracles cycle, but the closing chapter is something more concentrated: a tightly built tragedy of the strongman-as-fool, with three failed deceptions ramping toward the fatal fourth. The threefold-then-decisive-fourth pattern is a folkloric type-scene; it is also the same pattern that governs Balaam's donkey (Num 22) and Job's three rounds of friend-speeches before Elihu. Robert Alter has read the Samson narrative as one of the great Hebrew Bible examples of the type-scene deployed for ironic effect: where the standard hero of folklore would learn from his first betrayal, Samson learns nothing from three.

The literary devices are dense. The chapter is built on a vision/sight motif that has been running since Samson "saw" the Timnite woman in 14:1 and "saw" the Gaza prostitute in 16:1. Block and Crenshaw have both made this central: Samson's eyes drive his appetites, and the Philistines understand the symbolism precisely. They gouge his eyes (v. 21) — the part of him that has always been the engine of his downfall. There is a dark joke embedded in the Hebrew: the man who cannot stop seeing women is now, finally, blind. The dramatic irony culminates in v. 20: "But he did not know that the LORD had left him." This is one of the most theologically devastating lines in the Hebrew Bible, and it is also a perfect tragic recognition reversed — the hero does not know the very thing the audience knows. Alter has pointed out the structural parallelism between the four scenes of binding/escape/binding/loss: each scene is a miniature of the whole, and the whole is the dissolution of the man who cannot tell himself he is being killed.

Place in the book's argument: Judges 16 closes the savior-cycle proper. Yairah Amit has shown that the Deuteronomistic narrator has been gradually downgrading the judges in moral stature from Othniel through Samson — a deliberate degeneration. Othniel is faultless; Ehud is morally ambiguous; Gideon flirts with idolatry; Jephthah sacrifices his daughter; Samson is a Philistine-loving womanizer who breaks every Nazirite vow. By the end of ch. 16, the institution has collapsed. The narrator is then ready to launch the appendix (chs. 17-21) with its drumbeat refrain: "in those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (17:6, 18:1, 19:1, 21:25). The Samson story is the last beat of the dying judge-system.

Textual variants matter here. The Septuagint comes in two recensions for Judges, conventionally LXX A (Codex Alexandrinus) and LXX B (Codex Vaticanus). They diverge substantially in Judges 16, particularly in the prison scene: LXX A expands Samson's prayer with explicit theological language; LXX B follows the Masoretic text more closely. The Qumran fragments 4QJudg-a and 4QJudg-b do not preserve much of ch. 16, but where they do, they confirm the general MT contours. Josephus (*Ant.* 5.317-318) gives Samson's death speech in a more philosophical register, transforming the tragic hero into a Hellenistic figure. Pseudo-Philo (*LAB* 43) compresses and moralizes the chapter. The tradition is not unstable in its plot but in its theological key.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, Judges 16 sits at a hinge. The Samson cycle echoes back to the patriarchal annunciation type-scenes (Sarah, Rebekah, Manoah's wife in Judges 13 — whose anonymity Block, Webb, and Niditch all set against the chapter's pointedly named Delilah) and forward to the failure of Saul, the troubled rise of David, and the long shadow of charismatic kingship gone wrong. Samson's blinded suffering anticipates Zedekiah's blinding in 2 Kings 25 — the last king of Judah, eyes gouged after the fall of Jerusalem, dragged in chains to Babylon. Hosea will later remember Gibeah (9:9, 10:9) but no prophet directly invokes Samson; the Hebrew Bible largely lets Samson's story speak for itself. Hebrews 11:32 lists Samson with the heroes of faith, a startling rehabilitation given everything ch. 16 records about him.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory** — Pseudo-Philo (*LAB* 43) retells the Samson cycle with significant moralizing expansion, having Samson confess his sin before his death and frame the temple collapse as divine vindication. Josephus (*Ant.* 5.317) gives Samson a Hellenistic-philosophical send-off, presenting him as a tragic hero whose virtues outweighed his failings. The Talmud (Sotah 9b-10a) is unsparing: Samson "followed his eyes" and was punished through his eyes; the rabbis read his name *Shimshon* as a play on *shemesh* (sun) and connect him to the divine glory but warn that even a Samson can fall through lust. Ber. 31b discusses Samson's death prayer as an example of bold petition. The medieval commentators — Rashi, Radak (David Kimhi), Ralbag — read the chapter morally and prophetically: Samson is a flawed but legitimate deliverer whose final act restores his place. Rashi notes the wordplay on grinding. Yair Zakovitch and Yairah Amit have, in modern Israeli scholarship, drawn out the Hebrew's ironies and double-entendres that earlier traditions softened. Jon Levenson has read the Samson narrative as an example of Israelite engagement with surrounding heroic literatures, neither fully embracing nor fully rejecting the Mesopotamian and Aegean strongman traditions.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory** — The patristic tradition struggled hard with Judges 16, and the central battle was over Samson's death. Was it suicide? Origen's *Homily 9 on Judges* allegorizes Samson as a type of Christ — bound, mocked, killing more in his death than in his life — and reads the haircut as the loss of grace through carnal weakness. Ambrose treated Samson with caution. Augustine, in *City of God* 1.21, addresses the suicide question directly and argues that Samson was acting under divine command — that what would otherwise be sinful (taking one's own life) was justified because the Spirit moved him. This becomes the dominant Christian reading. Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 64 a. 5 ad 4) follows Augustine almost verbatim: Samson is exempted from the prohibition on suicide because of explicit divine inspiration. Bernard of Clairvaux uses Samson sermonically as a warning against the seductions of the world. Calvin's sermons on Judges (preached in 1561) read Samson as a real but compromised servant of God — Calvin is hard on Samson's appetites but maintains him as a true judge whose faith Hebrews 11 vindicates. Luther praised Samson as a type of Christ, picking up the Origen tradition. The Reformers all faced the same exegetical difficulty as the Fathers: how to make a man who slept with prostitutes and married Philistines into a hero of faith. Critical scholarship — Wellhausen, C. F. Burney, Robert Boling (Anchor Bible), J. Alberto Soggin — largely set the theological question aside and pursued source-critical and folkloric readings, treating the Samson cycle as an ancient hero-tale anchored in Danite tribal memory. Contemporary biblical scholars — Daniel Block (NICOT), Barry Webb (NICOT), Lawson Younger, Susan Niditch, Gregory Mobley (*The Empty Men*), James Crenshaw — have variously emphasized the folkloric dimensions, the Deuteronomistic theological agenda, the eye/sight motif, and the humiliation of the mill scene. John Milton's *Samson Agonistes* (1671) is the great Christian literary reception — Milton, himself blind, wrote his closet drama with full sympathy for the strong man brought low and made Samson's final prayer the climactic vision.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge** — Both traditions agree that Samson is a flawed hero whose final act gives him a place in the canon of faith. They diverge sharply on suicide. Augustine's defense is Christian-specific; the Jewish tradition, reading Samson's death as a martyr's prayer answered, never frames it as a moral problem in the same way. Both traditions share an awareness that Samson is morally compromised; the rabbis are arguably more direct about it than the Fathers. Modern commentary (Crenshaw, Block, Webb, Niditch) has given Delilah a more careful hearing — pointing out that she is acting under enormous economic pressure (1,100 silver pieces from each lord, which would be a fortune) and that the older coding of her as simple temptress has obscured the Philistine political logic of the scene; she is a paid asset of the *seranim*, not a private antagonist.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two genuine tensions structure this chapter, and neither admits a neat resolution.

**Tension 1: Divine power working through morally compromised vessels.** Samson is a serial fornicator, a violator of his Nazirite vow, a man whose appetites consistently override his calling. And yet "the LORD" repeatedly empowers him — and Hebrews 11:32 lists him among the faithful. What is the Deuteronomistic narrator saying about how God works? The Reformed reading (Calvin, contemporary Reformed theologians like Block and Webb) emphasizes God's sovereignty: God uses whomever God chooses, regardless of moral fitness, because God is committed to the deliverance of God's people. The Catholic reading (Aquinas, contemporary Catholic exegetes like Roland Murphy) tends to allow more space for natural-law concerns and treats Samson as exemplifying how grace can work through nature even when nature is severely damaged. The Eastern Orthodox tradition reads Samson typologically — his strength is a type of Christ's, his blinding a type of Christ's mockery, his temple collapse a type of harrowing hell — and is comfortable letting the moral problems sit in the typology's shadow. The Jewish reading (Rashi, Radak, Talmudic) takes Samson's character as instructive ethical material — God's empowering does not exempt one from accountability. The critical reading (Niditch, Mobley, Crenshaw) treats the narrative as a folkloric tradition theologized after the fact: the strongman is the hero, and the theology is a later overlay. The position least intuitive to me is the Reformed sovereignty reading at its strongest — that God's choice of Samson tells us nothing about Samson and everything about God's purposes. Steelman: this reading takes seriously the narrative's own emphasis ("the Spirit of the LORD rushed upon him" — 14:6, 14:19, 15:14), refuses to make divine action contingent on human worthiness in a way that would humanize God into a moral evaluator, and grounds the theological coherence of the cycle. The chapter does not portray Samson as deserving anything; it portrays God as choosing this man for these purposes. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter forces a hard choice between treating the narrator's theology as descriptive (God really does work through such people) or as ironic (the narrator is showing us how dysfunctional the savior-system has become). The Hebrew text supports both readings.

**Tension 2: Samson's death — suicide, martyrdom, or vengeance?** Augustine's defense (*City of God* 1.21) was prompted by genuine theological discomfort. Samson prays to be avenged "for my two eyes" (v. 28) — a deeply personal motive — and then says "let me die with the Philistines" (v. 30). Aquinas follows Augustine: divinely inspired exception. Rabbinic tradition reads the prayer as legitimate vengeance against Israel's enemies — no ethical problem, because the framework is corporate, not individualist. Modern Catholic ethical tradition has used Samson as a borderline case in discussions of double effect — Samson intends to kill the Philistines (good); his death is a foreseen but unintended consequence (permitted). Protestant pietist tradition tends to soften the vengeance into something more like sacrificial deliverance. Critical scholarship (Boling, Soggin) treats the suicide problem as anachronistic — the ancient narrator did not have post-Augustinian categories of moral psychology to apply. The position least intuitive to me is the rabbinic/critical reading that there is no moral problem here at all — that Samson's prayer for personal vengeance, combined with his deliberate death, is unproblematically heroic. Steelman: in the corporate theology of pre-monarchic Israel, the deliverer's life is bound to the people's deliverance; an act of self-sacrificing destruction of the enemy is not categorically different from a soldier's last stand; the introduction of suicide as a moral category is a Christian-philosophical overlay on a narrative whose worldview is pre-philosophical. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter resists clean ethical categorization. Samson's prayer is both selfish (vengeance for his eyes) and corporate (deliverance from the Philistine yoke). The narrator does not adjudicate.

**Unresolved question:** When Samson "did not know that the LORD had left him" (v. 20), at what moment had the LORD actually left? When the seventh lock fell? When he told Delilah his secret? When he loved her in the first place? Or — most disturbing of all — was the LORD ever really *with* him in the Augustinian sense, or was the Spirit only ever rushing upon him as a borrowed weapon, never inhabiting him?

## The Thread

When you reach 1 Samuel 13-14, watch how Saul's first kingship is built around Philistine pressure on the same Sorek-Shephelah frontier — and how Saul, like Samson, will be a charismatic leader undone by his own appetites and unfit for the institutional weight he carries. The Philistines are still the problem; the savior-figure is still inadequate; only the institution has changed.

When 2 Kings 25:7 records that the Babylonians "put out the eyes of Zedekiah" and brought him in chains to Babylon, remember Samson at the mill. The Deuteronomistic Historian is closing a long arc that began here. The blinded leader is the recurring image of covenant failure.

When you encounter Hebrews 11:32 listing Samson among the heroes of faith, hold ch. 16 in your hand and ask the question the writer of Hebrews seems content to leave unanswered: what kind of faith is this?

And when you read John Milton's *Samson Agonistes* — written in 1671 by a blind Puritan poet who had seen the English revolution fail — recognize that Milton found in this chapter the deepest image he could of a man who had betrayed his own gift, lost everything, and prayed his way to one final, devastating act. The Christian reception of this chapter has always been about more than the strongman's biceps.
