# Matthew 10: The Mission Discourse

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Matthew 10 plants its mission instructions in the geography of first-century Galilee but already projects them outward toward Roman governors, Gentile audiences, and family courts where Christians will be tried in the decades after Easter. The chapter knows two horizons at once.

The "twelve" (***dōdeka***, δώδεκα) of 10:1 is not an arbitrary number. It claims the symbolic weight of the twelve tribes of Israel — a claim made explicit at 19:28, where Jesus tells the Twelve they will sit on twelve thrones judging the tribes. **E. P. Sanders** (*Jesus and Judaism*) argued that this twelve-tribe symbolism is one of the most secure historical anchors of Jesus's ministry: a movement that constituted twelve representatives announces itself as the eschatological reconstitution of Israel. The names listed in 10:2-4 reflect the social geography of the lake: at least seven of the Twelve were Galileans, four (Peter, Andrew, James, John) were fishermen from the Bethsaida-Capernaum corridor, one (Matthew) was a tax collector, and one (Simon the *Kananaios* / Zealot) carried the political designation of resistance to Rome. The list is varied enough to refuse a clean ideological reading.

The "tax collector" tag attached to Matthew (10:3) is unique to Matthew's gospel — Mark and Luke list the apostle without the marker. **Bauckham** (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*) reads this as the evangelist's self-disclosing humility. *Thaddaeus* (Matt 10:3, Mark 3:18) is *Judas son of James* in Luke 6:16 and Acts 1:13, and the manuscript variant *Lebbaeus* (D and several Old Latin) further complicates the harmonization. **Bauckham's** preferred reconstruction: Thaddaeus / Lebbaeus and Judas son of James are nicknames or alternate names for the same individual — a not-uncommon practice in a multilingual Jewish milieu where men commonly carried both Aramaic and Greek-Hellenistic names.

The geographical restriction in 10:5-6 — "go nowhere among the Gentiles, and enter no town of the Samaritans, but go rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" — names a specific historical reality. Galilee was bounded by Gentile territory (Phoenicia to the north, the Decapolis to the east) and by Samaria to the south; the Samaritan town of Sebaste / Samaria and the major Greek city of Caesarea Maritima sat along clear ethnic-cultural lines. **Anthony Saldarini** (*Matthew's Christian-Jewish Community*) has shown how the Samaritan-Jewish hostility was concrete and ancient (cf. John 4:9, "Jews have no dealings with Samaritans"); the temple Mount Gerizim had been destroyed by John Hyrcanus in 128 BCE, and resentment ran in both directions. The mission Jesus gives in 10:5-6 is *to Israel first* — a salvation-historical staging that the Great Commission of 28:19 will eventually broaden to "all nations."

The travel instructions (10:9-10) tell us something about the Galilean economy. The forbidden items — gold, silver, copper, a bag, two tunics, sandals, a staff — describe the standard kit of an itinerant. ***Mē ktēsēsthe chryson*** (Μὴ κτήσησθε χρυσὸν) prohibits *acquisition* (the verb is causative) rather than possession; the disciples are not to provision themselves through fundraising. They are to be received in homes, fed at tables, and to depart when the welcome ends. Ancient hospitality codes (cf. Deut 23:3-4 on Moabites refused entry, 2 Kgs 4:8-10 on the Shunammite hosting Elisha) made this reciprocity legible. The ***xenia*** (ξενία, "guest-friendship") of the Greco-Roman world worked similarly. **Wayne Meeks** (*The First Urban Christians*) and **Bruce Malina** have shown how the early Christian mission rode atop a pre-existing infrastructure of household hospitality.

The "shake off the dust from your feet" gesture (10:14) draws on a specific Jewish practice. Pious Jews returning from Gentile territory shook the dust off their sandals to symbolize separation from impurity (cf. *m. Tohorot* 4:5, *m. Oholoth* 2:3). Acts 13:51 records Paul and Barnabas doing this at Pisidian Antioch. The disciples' performance of the gesture against an unwelcoming Jewish town reverses the polarity: this town is now under judgment more severe than Sodom (10:15).

The "Beelzebul" reference (10:25) — the householder's nickname applied to the household — alludes back to the controversy intimated at 9:34 and developed at 12:24. ***Beelzeboul*** (Βεελζεβούλ) is a Hellenized form of either *Baal-zebul* ("Lord Prince") or *Baal-zebub* ("Lord of the Flies," 2 Kgs 1:2-3). In contemporary intertestamental literature (1 Enoch, *Testament of Solomon*) it names a high demonic prince.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Matthew 10 is the **second of Matthew's five major discourses** in the **B. W. Bacon** (*Studies in Matthew*, 1918) structural scheme: Sermon on the Mount (5-7), Mission Discourse (10), Parables (13), Community / Ecclesial Discourse (18), Olivet (24-25). Each closes with a formulaic transition: "When Jesus had finished these sayings..." (cf. 7:28, 11:1, 13:53, 19:1, 26:1). The Mission Discourse closes at 11:1.

Bacon read the five discourses as a deliberate Matthean Pentateuch — Jesus as the new Moses giving five "books" of teaching. **Dale Allison** (*The New Moses*) developed this typology in detail: chapter 10's mission instructions parallel the commissioning of Israel's leaders in Numbers (cf. Num 11:16-30, the seventy elders), and the chapter's persecution warnings echo the Mosaic Deuteronomic curses on covenant unfaithfulness. **Davies and Allison** (ICC) are more cautious about the new-Moses typology as a *programmatic* structure but acknowledge the Mosaic resonances throughout.

The discourse is literarily complex. Verses 1-15 give the *initial sending* — instructions for the limited Galilean mission to Israel. Verses 16-23 shift to *persecution under courts and synagogues* — language that fits the post-Easter church better than the pre-Easter commissioning ("you will be dragged before governors and kings... to bear witness before them and the Gentiles," 10:18). Verses 24-25 transition through a "disciple not above teacher" saying. Verses 26-33 address fearless confession. Verses 34-39 give the radical-discipleship sayings (sword, family division, cross). Verses 40-42 close with reception sayings.

**Davies and Allison** read this as Matthew's compilation of multiple sayings traditions into a single discourse — material that in Mark and Luke is scattered. Mark's Mission instructions (Mark 6:7-13) parallel only Matt 10:1, 7-14. The persecution sayings (Matt 10:17-22) parallel material in Mark 13:9-13 (the Olivet Discourse) and Luke 21:12-19. The fearlessness and family-division sayings cluster around Luke 12. Matthew has *thematized* — gathering scattered mission-related material into a coherent ecclesial address.

The most disputed verse in the chapter is **10:23**: "you will not have gone through all the towns of Israel before the Son of Man comes." **Albert Schweitzer** (*Quest of the Historical Jesus*) made this verse the cornerstone of his "thoroughgoing eschatology": Jesus expected the parousia within the timeframe of the disciples' Galilean mission, the prediction failed, and the gospel tradition is the residue of that crisis. Most modern scholarship — **Davies/Allison**, **France**, **Hagner**, **Nolland** — rejects Schweitzer's reading. France argues the "coming of the Son of Man" refers not to the parousia but to the Danielic enthronement vindicated at the resurrection or at 70 CE. **Davies/Allison** survey roughly seven distinct interpretive options without committing definitively. **Bultmann** read the saying as authentic apocalyptic that the church re-interpreted when its prediction did not materialize.

Three Greek terms anchor the chapter. ***Apostolos*** (ἀπόστολος, "apostle / sent one") appears for the first and only time in Matthew at 10:2 — elsewhere Matthew calls them ***mathētai*** (μαθηταί) or ***hoi dōdeka*** (οἱ δώδεκα). The single use marks the sending. ***Exousia*** (ἐξουσία) returns at 10:1 — Jesus *gives* the Twelve the same authority over unclean spirits and disease that he himself has wielded throughout chapters 8-9. ***Marturēsate*** (μαρτυρήσατε, "testify / witness," 10:18) introduces the New Testament's developing martyrological vocabulary; the Greek root will become the technical term for those who die for the faith.

Textually the chapter is mostly stable. The most consequential variant is the name *Lebbaeus* / *Thaddaeus* / *Lebbaeus called Thaddaeus* in 10:3, where the textual tradition is genuinely divided. NA28 prints *Thaddaios*. The "Iscariot" of 10:4 is variously spelled (*Iskariōtēs*, *Iskariōth*); the etymology remains contested (from *ish-Kerioth*, "man of Kerioth" in Judah; or from *sicarius*, the Latin "dagger-man").

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Matthew 10 is the gospel's first sustained ecclesiology. It tells the church what it is for, what it will face, and how it will be received.

**Jewish interpretive trajectories** read this chapter with mixed responses. The opening restriction to "the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (10:6) is theologically clarifying for **Amy-Jill Levine** (*The Misunderstood Jew*; *Jewish Annotated NT*) and **Anthony Saldarini**: Matthew's Jesus prioritizes Israel both historically and salvifically; the later Gentile mission does not abolish but extends this priority. **Daniel Boyarin** (*The Jewish Gospels*) reads the chapter's high Christology — the disciples' fate bound to the master's, the Father's voice speaking through them, the Son of Man's coming — as continuous with Second Temple Jewish messianic categories rather than a Hellenistic departure. **David Flusser** stressed that the persecution warnings reflect early-Christian experience inside the synagogue — flogging "in their synagogues" (10:17) presupposes Jewish disciplinary jurisdiction over Jewish-Christians, the kind of context attested by Paul's "forty lashes minus one" five times (2 Cor 11:24). The chapter does not anticipate Jewish-Christian separation; it presupposes that the conflict runs *through* Jewish communal structures.

**Christian interpretive trajectories** form the deepest layer of reception. **Origen** read the mission instructions allegorically: gold and silver as worldly wisdom the apostles must lay aside; the staff and tunic as worldly support; the dust shaken from feet as the renunciation of evil counsel. **John Chrysostom**, in homilies 32-35 of his *Ninety Homilies on Matthew*, reads the chapter as the manual for clerical and missionary life — the bishops and priests of the Antiochene church standing in apostolic succession to those first sent. **Augustine** in his *Tractates* and *Sermons* worked the chapter's persecution warnings into his developing theology of the church under siege; his *De Civitate Dei* draws on Matt 10's two-cities logic in muted form.

**Aquinas** in the *Catena Aurea* assembles patristic comments verse by verse, and in his *Lectura* takes the chapter as the locus for treating apostolic authority, the church's mission, and the priestly state. The Mission Discourse becomes, in Aquinas, a key text for medieval ecclesiology.

**Calvin's** *Harmony of the Evangelists* reads 10:5-6 as historically situated — the Israel-only restriction belonging to the pre-Easter mission, abrogated by the Great Commission — but the persecution sayings (10:16-25) as enduring instruction for the church in every age. **Luther** preached the chapter often: "Beware of men" became a mainstay of his counsel to Reformation pastors; "fear not those who kill the body" the comfort he offered confessors facing imperial coercion.

**Bonhoeffer**, in *Discipleship* and *Letters and Papers from Prison*, took Matthew 10 as the textual heart of his theology of *Nachfolge*. The Mission Discourse is the chapter Bonhoeffer cites most often after the Sermon on the Mount; the saying "whoever does not take his cross and follow me is not worthy of me" (10:38) became, for him, the gospel's interior logic. *Discipleship* opens famously with the assertion: "When Christ calls a man, he bids him come and die." That formulation is a paraphrase of Matthew 10. Bonhoeffer's own death at Flossenbürg in April 1945 — hanged for participation in the conspiracy against Hitler — was widely read by his contemporaries as a Matthew-10 enactment.

**Bultmann's** form-critical analysis treated the discourse as a compilation of dominical sayings (some authentic, some early-church creations) that the evangelist has organized into a kerygmatic frame. The historical-Jesus core, for Bultmann, was minimal; the existential meaning lay in the demand for radical decision.

Contemporary commentary is dense. **Davies and Allison's** ICC volume 2 remains the most exhaustive philological treatment; **Ulrich Luz's** Hermeneia traces the reception of "fear not those who kill the body" through martyrological literature, the Reformation, and modern political-theological resistance. **R. T. France** (NICNT) is the clearest narrative-critical reader; **Craig Keener** (NICNT) the richest in social-historical context; **Daniel Harrington** (Sacra Pagina) economical and Catholic; **John Nolland** (NIGTC) careful on Greek; **Donald Hagner** (WBC) judicious; **D. A. Carson** the leading evangelical conservative voice.

**Jack Dean Kingsbury** (*Matthew as Story*) reads chapter 10 as the moment the gospel's *implied audience* shifts: the discourse is addressed to the historical Twelve in the narrative frame but speaks past them to the post-Easter church for whom every persecution warning has become urgently descriptive. The future tenses of 10:17-22 are not predictions; they are the church's present.

**Political readings** have been particularly intense. **Warren Carter** (*Matthew and Empire*) reads the chapter's "governors and kings" (10:18) as Matthew's coded reference to the Roman imperial machinery — the disciples' fate parallels Jesus's, who will himself stand before Pilate and Herod. The chapter's "sword" saying (10:34), so often softened in pious commentary, is read by Carter as the recognition that Jesus's mission *will* generate violent response from the imperial order it implicitly subverts. The chapter is not a celebration of conflict but a clear-eyed prediction.

**N. T. Wright** (*Matthew for Everyone* vol. 1; *Jesus and the Victory of God*; *How God Became King*) reads the Mission Discourse as Jesus extending his own kingdom-campaign through the Twelve, who are not random recruits but the symbolic re-founding of Israel — twelve men for twelve tribes, sent out to announce that God is at last becoming king. For Wright the urgency of the chapter (no money-bag, no spare tunic, shake the dust off, "you will not have gone through the towns of Israel before the Son of Man comes," 10:23) belongs to a *prophetic crisis*: Jesus, like Jeremiah before the Babylonian disaster, is summoning Israel to turn around now, because the road the nation is on leads to catastrophe, and the time is desperately short. The persecution warnings (10:16-23) and the cross-saying (10:38, "whoever does not take up his cross and follow me is not worthy of me") tell the disciples that following this Messiah means walking the same road of suffering love that he will walk to Calvary — a theme Wright develops at length in *The Day the Revolution Began*: the kingdom comes not by the sword but through the cross, and the church that carries the message will carry it the same way. "I have not come to bring peace but a sword" (10:34) is, on Wright's reading, not a license for Christian violence but the sober recognition that the announcement of God's reign cuts across every loyalty, even the family, and provokes the powers that would rather things stayed as they were.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions structure the theological reading.

**First tension: the Israel-first mission and the Gentile commission.** The chapter's restriction to "the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (10:5-6) sits in unresolved tension with the Great Commission's "make disciples of all nations" (28:19). Three positions. **(1) Salvation-historical staging reading**: the Israel-first mission is *temporally* prior; after Easter, the Gentile mission supersedes (or completes) it. This is the dominant patristic and Reformation reading and is preserved in **Davies/Allison**, **Saldarini**, and **N. T. Wright**. Steelmanned, it accounts for both poles of the gospel: the historical Jesus's mission is to Israel; the risen Jesus commissions the nations. **(2) Continuing-priority reading**: the Israel-first mission is *permanent*; the Gentile mission extends but does not displace it. This is **Levine's** position — Christianity's ongoing obligation to the Jewish people is rooted in Matthew's salvation-historical priority. Steelmanned, it preserves Romans 9-11 (Israel's irrevocable election) within the Matthean frame. **(3) Replacement reading**: the Israel-first mission failed; the Gentile mission replaces it. This is the supersessionist reading that haunts Christian history and against which Levine, Saldarini, Davies/Allison, and Wright all push back. Steelmanned, it points to the gospel's increasing tension with Pharisaic Judaism (chapters 12, 15, 23) and to "the kingdom of God will be taken away from you" of 21:43. *Where this leaves us:* (1) and (2) together. The Israel-first mission is historically prior and theologically permanent; the Gentile mission extends rather than replaces it. (3) misreads the gospel's internal logic and produces the anti-Jewish history we now have to repent of.

**Second tension: peace and the sword.** Jesus's "I have not come to bring peace, but a sword" (10:34) is one of the gospel's hardest sayings. Three positions. **(1) Apocalyptic-eschatological reading**: Jesus refers to the eschatological division his coming inaugurates — the messianic woes that pit family against family (cf. Mic 7:6, which the saying directly echoes). The sword is not violent agency but the cutting effect of fidelity. This is **Davies/Allison's** preferred reading. Steelmanned, it grounds the saying in Jewish apocalyptic categories and saves it from a Crusader misreading. **(2) Discipleship-cost reading**: the sword names the *concrete persecution* the disciples will face when fidelity to Jesus disrupts family loyalty; the saying is descriptive, not prescriptive. This is **Bonhoeffer's** reading. Steelmanned, it is faithful to the immediately following verses (10:35-39) which spell out family conflict in concrete detail. **(3) Pacifist-paradox reading**: the saying must be read against the gospel's broader nonviolence (5:39, 26:52); the "sword" Jesus brings is the paradoxical sword of a love that cuts through false peace. This is **John Howard Yoder's** kind of reading and is preserved by some Anabaptist traditions. Steelmanned, it preserves the gospel's overall pacific witness without softening 10:34's bite. *Where this leaves us:* (1) and (2) together; the saying is apocalyptic in frame and concretely descriptive of disciple experience. (3) is right that the sword is *not* literal violent agency but is too quick to absorb the saying into a programmatic pacifism. The saying does not authorize Christian violence; it predicts the violence Christians will *suffer*.

**Unresolved question.** What does 10:23 — "you will not have gone through all the towns of Israel before the Son of Man comes" — actually mean, and how does it relate to the parousia? Schweitzer's "delayed parousia" reading is unstable for any high view of Jesus's authority, but the alternatives (an inaugurated coming at the resurrection, a typological coming at 70 CE, a coming "in the disciples' mission" itself) all leave significant exegetical residue. Davies/Allison's seven options are not seven; they are evidence that the saying does not yield to a clean reading.

## The Thread

The "authority" Jesus gives the Twelve at 10:1 is the same ***exousia*** he himself wielded across chapters 8-9 — the chapter is the bridge between Jesus's authority and the church's. The "lost sheep of the house of Israel" of 10:6 returns at 15:24 (the Canaanite woman: "I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel"). The "shake off the dust" gesture of 10:14 is enacted in Acts 13:51. The "Beelzebul" reference of 10:25 develops at 12:22-32 in the full Beelzebul controversy. The "fear not those who kill the body" of 10:28 echoes through the New Testament's martyrological literature (cf. Rev 2:10, Heb 11:35-38). The cross-saying of 10:38 returns at 16:24 ("If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross"), and the "find / lose your life" saying of 10:39 is doubled at 16:25. The "whoever receives you receives me" of 10:40 anticipates the judgment scene of 25:31-46, where care for "the least of these my brothers" is reckoned as care for Christ himself.
