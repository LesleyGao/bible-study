# Revelation 17: The Woman, the Beast, and the City of Seven Hills

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Revelation 17 is the chapter where the seer's coded geography becomes nearly unmistakable. A woman sits on seven mountains (17:9); she is "the great city that has dominion over the kings of the earth" (17:18); she is dressed in purple and scarlet, adorned with gold and pearls, holding a cup; and her name, written on her forehead, is "Babylon the great." For first-century readers in the cities of Asia Minor — Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia, Laodicea — this combination of cues had a single referent: Rome. The seven hills (Capitoline, Palatine, Aventine, Caelian, Esquiline, Quirinal, Viminal) were a topographical commonplace; the *Dea Roma* cult, with its iconography of a goddess seated on the seven hills holding a cup, appeared on Vespasianic *sestertii* and was familiar across the empire. Steven Friesen's archaeological work on imperial cult installations in Asia Minor (especially *Imperial Cults and the Apocalypse of John*) documents how dense the architectural and ritual presence of Rome was in precisely the cities Revelation addresses.

The dating debate remains live but tilts heavily toward the late, Domitianic period (c. 95–96 AD). Irenaeus (*Adversus Haereses* 5.30.3) places the vision toward the end of Domitian's reign, and the assumption of an aggressive imperial cult — *dominus et deus noster*, the title Suetonius and Dio attribute to Domitian — fits the social pressure visible in the seven letters of chs. 2–3. Leonard Thompson's *The Book of Revelation: Apocalypse and Empire* has tempered the older "persecution under Domitian" narrative by showing that systematic state persecution is harder to document than once assumed; what is clear is that participation in the imperial cult was an entry condition for civic and economic life, and that John of Patmos (not to be confused with John the Apostle or the author of the Fourth Gospel — a distinction Eusebius preserves through his quotation of Dionysius of Alexandria, and one almost universally maintained in modern critical scholarship) is writing a sustained refusal of that participation. A minority — most prominently J. A. T. Robinson and, more recently, Kenneth Gentry from a preterist standpoint — argue for a Neronian dating (c. 65–69 AD), which would make the "five fallen, one is" calculation of 17:10 land naturally on Nero or his immediate successors. Aune (*Word Biblical Commentary*, vol. 3, the relevant volume for chs. 17–22) treats the Domitianic date as the working consensus while acknowledging the early-date arguments are not negligible.

The material culture saturating the chapter is Roman luxury and Roman power. The woman's purple (*porphyra*) and scarlet (*kokkinos*) recall the Tyrian and kermes dyes that Pliny the Elder catalogues in *Natural History* 9.124–141 as the most expensive textile pigments in the empire. The "gold and jewels and pearls" map onto the documented imperial fashion criticised by Pliny in *NH* 9.114, where pearls and emeralds had become the markers of senatorial and imperial-household status. The *cursus honorum* and the emperor list — Augustus, Tiberius, Caligula, Claudius, Nero, then the Year of the Four Emperors (Galba, Otho, Vitellius), then the Flavians (Vespasian, Titus, Domitian) — supply the raw material for the "seven kings" puzzle of 17:10–11. Aune surveys at least six counting schemes; Bauckham's chapter "Nero and the Beast" in *The Climax of Prophecy* argues persuasively for a count beginning with Augustus, treating the three short-lived emperors of 68–69 AD as not legitimate, yielding Vespasian as the sixth (the one who "is"), Titus as the seventh, and Domitian as the "eighth who is of the seven" — the Nero-redivivus figure in whom imperial cruelty returns. The textual tradition for ch. 17 is stable: P47 (3rd century) covers the chapter through 17:2, after which Codex Sinaiticus (4th c.), Codex Alexandrinus (5th c., generally regarded as the best textual witness for Revelation), and Codex Ephraemi Rescriptus (5th c.) carry the load, with no meaning-affecting variants in the seven-kings passage.

Several Greek terms anchor the chapter. *Pornē* (πόρνη, "prostitute," 17:1) is the lexical center — a noun whose Hebrew Bible echoes (Isaiah's Tyre as *zonah*, Hosea's faithless Israel) John presses into a critique of Rome. *Babylōn* (Βαβυλών, 17:5) is the cipher; the Roman-as-Babylon equation was already current in Jewish apocalyptic of the period (4 Ezra 3:1–2, 28; 2 Baruch 67; cf. 1 Peter 5:13). *Mystērion* (μυστήριον, 17:5, 7) marks the woman's name as a coded revelation requiring "a mind with wisdom" (17:9, *nous ho echōn sophian*). And *thērion* (θηρίον, "beast," 17:3) carries forward the Daniel 7 imagery — the beast with ten horns is now ridden by the woman, an image that fuses political-military power (the beast) with cultural-economic seduction (the woman) into a single composite of empire.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Revelation belongs to a hybrid genre — apocalypse, prophecy, and circular epistle — that John deploys with unusual self-consciousness. Chapter 17 is structured as a *deixis* scene: an angel offers to "show" (*deixō*, 17:1) the seer the judgment of the prostitute, then carries him "in the Spirit" into a wilderness (*erēmos*, 17:3), where the vision is given. This matches the earlier visionary transports in 1:10 and 4:2 and parallels the deixis-and-interpretation pattern of Daniel 7 and Zechariah 1–6, where a heavenly interpreter explicates symbolic content. The chapter is thus paired structurally with Revelation 21:9–10, where another of the seven bowl-angels carries the seer in the Spirit to a high mountain to "show" him the Bride, the New Jerusalem. The two cities — Babylon and the New Jerusalem — are framed as deliberate inversions of each other: each is a woman, each is a city, each is shown by a bowl-angel after a wilderness or mountain transport. Bauckham (*Theology of the Book of Revelation*) treats this paired structure as the chapter's most important literary signal.

The prophetic substrate is dense. Isaiah 23 (the oracle against Tyre, where the city is "forgotten as a prostitute" for seventy years and her merchandise becomes "holy to the Lord") and Nahum 3:1–4 (Nineveh as the "graceful prostitute, the mistress of sorceries, who betrays nations with her whorings") supply the metaphor of the imperial city as *pornē*. Isaiah 47, the taunt over Babylon — "you said, 'I shall be a mistress forever'... I am, and there is no one besides me" — is the source of John's Babylon vocabulary; the boast is reversed in Revelation 18:7. Jeremiah 50–51 supplies the destruction language. Daniel 7's beast-with-ten-horns is reused, but where in Daniel the four beasts are sequential empires, John collapses the imagery into a single composite beast (Rev 13) that re-emerges in 17:3 with the woman astride it. Caird (*A Commentary on the Revelation of St. John the Divine*) and Beale (*The Book of Revelation*, NIGTC, eclectic-idealist) both stress that John is doing more than allusion: he is performing a sustained rereading of the prophetic Babylon-corpus and applying it to Rome.

The literary device that has generated the most scholarly attention — and the most ethical discomfort — is the extended metaphor of Babylon-as-prostitute. The trope is gendered and graphic, and the violence imagined against the woman in 17:16 ("they will make her desolate and naked, and devour her flesh and burn her up with fire") borrows directly from Ezekiel 16 and 23, the prophetic indictments of Jerusalem and Samaria as adulteresses stripped and burned by their lovers. Mainstream commentators do not flinch from naming this as a difficulty: Bauckham, Beale (NIGTC), Aune (WBC), Koester (Anchor Yale), Osborne (BECNT), and Ian Paul (TNTC) all observe that the imagery is harsh and that readers across the centuries have found it troubling, and most resolve it on two grounds. First, the figure is a *city*, not a woman — the prophetic genre routinely personifies imperial capitals (Tyre, Nineveh, Babylon, Jerusalem itself) as female, and the destruction is Rome's, not a woman's. Second, the violence in 17:16 is the *empire eating itself*: the beast and its client-kings turn on the imperial center, as the Year of the Four Emperors actually witnessed — God's judgment enacted through the self-cannibalism of the system, not through divine assault on a body. The patristic and Byzantine commentators (Victorinus of Pettau, Tyconius, Oecumenius, Andrew of Caesarea) already read the woman straightforwardly as Rome / the world-empire, and the modern "Babylon = Rome / imperial economic-religious system" consensus stands in that line.

The key textual variant in the chapter is at 17:8, where some witnesses read *kai parestai* ("and shall be present") rather than *kai paragenetai* ("and shall come"); the variant does not significantly affect meaning. More interesting is the manuscript treatment of 17:18, where the identification of the woman with "the great city that has dominion over the kings of the earth" is unambiguous in every major witness — there is no textual escape from the Roman identification.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Revelation 17 sits at the structural climax of John's anti-imperial vision. The seven seals, seven trumpets, and seven bowls have built toward the unveiling (*apokalypsis*) of what stands behind the imperial order; ch. 17 names it. From here the book pivots: the lament-celebration of ch. 18, the heavenly "Hallelujah" of 19:1–8, the marriage supper, the rider on the white horse, and the New Jerusalem all flow from the judgment announced here. The chapter is also where the canonical thread to Daniel 7 is cinched tightest — the beast with ten horns, originally the fourth beast of Daniel's vision, becomes the political-military substrate that the seductive imperial culture rides.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.**

The four major schools — preterist, historicist, futurist, and idealist/eclectic — divide most sharply on chapter 17.

The patristic period favored a futurist reading without distinguishing it sharply from a historicist one. Justin Martyr (*Dialogue with Trypho* 80) treats the imagery as referring to a coming antichrist; Irenaeus (*Adv. Haer.* 5.25–30) reads the seven heads as Roman emperors and the eighth as a coming antichrist figure, marking the first explicit attempt to count the kings. Tertullian (*Apology* 35) and Hippolytus (*On Antichrist*, esp. §§36–42) extend the line; Hippolytus reads the woman as Rome explicitly. Victorinus of Pettau, in the earliest extant Latin commentary on Revelation (late 3rd c.), identifies the woman as Rome and reads the eighth king as a future Nero-redivivus. Origen's allegorizing tendency softened the political identification; Tyconius's lost commentary, transmitted via Bede and Beatus of Liébana, pushed toward an ecclesiological-allegorical reading that Augustine in *City of God* (esp. books 17–22) developed into the influential framework of two cities — *civitas terrena* and *civitas Dei* — in which Babylon is the human community organized around self-love rather than any specific empire.

Eastern Orthodox reception of Revelation was famously slow; the book was not formally canonized in the East until the late patristic period and is not read liturgically in the Byzantine rite. Andrew of Caesarea (early 7th c.) wrote the most important Greek commentary, treating ch. 17 with restraint — Babylon as Rome in some sense, but the eschatological reading dominates.

The Medieval period brought Joachim of Fiore's periodization (late 12th c.), in which Babylon was identified — cautiously but unmistakably — with the corruption of the institutional Church, particularly the papacy of his own age. Joachim's reading was decisive for later Spiritual Franciscans and for Wycliffe and Hus. Bernard of Clairvaux and Thomas Aquinas held more traditional patristic-Augustinian readings.

The Reformation crystallized the historicist Babylon-equals-Papacy reading. Luther's *On the Babylonian Captivity of the Church* (1520) and Calvin's polemical writings pressed the identification, and from the 16th to the 19th centuries this reading was the dominant Protestant historicist position — encoded in the Westminster Confession (25.6, on the Pope as antichrist) and the Lutheran Smalcald Articles. The Anabaptist apocalypticism that erupted at Münster in 1534–35 took the imagery further, with Jan van Leiden ruling a millennial New Jerusalem; the disaster left a long shadow over Anabaptist eschatology.

The 19th-century rise of dispensationalism, beginning with John Nelson Darby and codified in the *Scofield Reference Bible* (1909), shifted Babylon from the papal past into the eschatological future. In Darby's framework, ch. 17's woman is a future world-religious system (often "Mystery Babylon," distinguished from the political Babylon of ch. 18 in some dispensationalist schemes) that will rise during the tribulation period. This reading remains dominant in popular American evangelicalism through Hal Lindsey, Tim LaHaye, and the *Left Behind* franchise.

The 20th-century critical and academic stream is largely preterist on the historical referent (Babylon = Rome) while eclectic on the theological application. R. H. Charles's *International Critical Commentary* (1920) is the foundational modern critical work; G. B. Caird, G. R. Beasley-Murray, R. Mounce (NICNT), Eugene Boring (Interpretation), Grant Osborne (BECNT), Craig Koester (Anchor Yale Bible, 2014), and Aune all situate the chapter firmly in its first-century Roman context. Beale (NIGTC) is the leading idealist-eclectic voice: Babylon as Rome historically but as a transhistorical pattern — the world-system organized in opposition to the Lamb. Bauckham's *The Theology of the Book of Revelation* and *The Climax of Prophecy* are the most influential late-20th-century treatments, particularly his case that the seven-kings calculation supports a Domitianic date and that the chapter functions as the political-theological climax of the book.

N. T. Wright reads chapter 17 in *Revelation for Everyone* (2011) as the unveiling of the true nature of Rome — and, behind Rome, of every empire that styles itself eternal and demands the allegiance owed to God alone. For Wright the woman on the seven hills is unmistakably the imperial city in her wealth, her power over the kings of the earth, and her drunkenness on "the blood of the saints" — but precisely because the seer names her with the *ancient* code-word "Babylon," the picture is portable: it is the prophetic indictment of Tyre and Nineveh and the old Babylon brought forward, and it will fit every successor-Babylon, every economic-political-religious system that crushes the weak and intoxicates the powerful. The grim irony Wright underlines is in 17:16–17: the beast turns on the woman it carried — empire devours itself — and God's judgment comes "from inside the system it built." The chapter is not a coded forecast of a future world-religion; it is a summons to the churches of Asia (and after) to see what they are living in, and to refuse to ride the beast.

The political-reading tradition, largely from Latin American and South African contexts, takes Babylon as a transhistorical figure for global imperial-economic systems. Allan Boesak's *Comfort and Protest* (1987), written under apartheid, reads the chapter as a warning to all empires; Pablo Richard's *Apocalypse: A People's Commentary* makes the economic dimension central; Brian Blount's *Revelation* (NTL, 2009) and Wes Howard-Brook and Anthony Gwyther's *Unveiling Empire* (1999) read the book as a sustained critique of imperial structure. This line presses the social edge of what Bauckham, Koester, and Ian Paul also argue from within the mainstream commentary tradition: that Revelation 18's economic critique (and ch. 17 with it) is no marginal note but the heart of the indictment.

**Jewish Scholarly Engagement.**

The Roman-as-Babylon equation was already established in Jewish apocalyptic literature contemporary with Revelation. 4 Ezra (2 Esdras) 3:1–2, 28–31 and 2 Baruch 10–12, 67 — both responses to the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 AD — use Babylon as code for Rome. 1 Peter 5:13 ("she who is at Babylon... sends you greetings"), generally read as a Christian use of the same code, suggests the equation was widespread among the Jewish-Christian communities of the eastern Mediterranean. Adela Yarbro Collins's *Crisis and Catharsis: The Power of the Apocalypse* situates Revelation within this Jewish apocalyptic stream; Loren Stuckenbruck's work on the apocalyptic tradition, including the *1 Enoch* corpus, has further developed the lineage. Jewish scholarly engagement with the book's reuse of the Hebrew prophets — especially the way Isaiah 47, Jeremiah 50–51, and Ezekiel 27 are recombined in Rev 17–18 — has tended to read this not as supersessionist appropriation but as continuation of the Day-of-YHWH tradition into the Roman period. Daniel Boyarin and others have argued that the boundary between "Jewish" and "Christian" apocalyptic in this period is porous; Revelation belongs to a shared imaginative world.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Convergence: across nearly every school, the immediate referent of the woman in ch. 17 is Rome — including most modern dispensationalists, who read Rome as a type of the future system. The seven hills, the imperial luxury, the persecuting power, and the mention of the great city that rules the kings of the earth are too tightly fixed for serious dispute. Divergence: whether the Roman referent exhausts the meaning (preterist), is repeated through history (historicist), is fulfilled finally in the future (futurist/dispensationalist), or functions as a transhistorical type (idealist). A persistent secondary debate concerns the harshness of the imagery in 17:16 — most commentators (Bauckham, Caird, Aune, Koester, Beale) read the violence as the empire turning on itself, expressed in the prophetic idiom of Ezekiel 16 and 23, and caution against reading it as either a literal future event or a license for human violence.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the seer believe God was doing in ch. 17? At the most basic level, he believed God was unveiling — *apokaluptein* — the spiritual nature of an empire whose political and economic surface had become so taken-for-granted that its readers needed a vision to see what they were inhabiting. Two genuine theological tensions deserve mapping.

**Tension 1: Is Babylon Rome, or is Babylon a transhistorical type?**

The Catholic position, articulated in CCC §§677–678 on the Church's final trial, treats the chapter as eschatological without identifying Babylon with any particular institution; in modern Catholic exegesis the woman is generally read as the unbelieving world-system in opposition to the Church. The Reformed tradition inherits a historicist Babylon-equals-Papacy reading from the 16th–19th centuries, but contemporary Reformed scholars — Beale, Vern Poythress (*The Returning King*), Dennis Johnson — have largely abandoned that identification in favor of an idealist or eclectic reading: Babylon as the worldly system in any age. The Lutheran trajectory mirrors this. Eastern Orthodox interpretation tends to read the chapter eschatologically, with Andrew of Caesarea's commentary as the canonical guide. Critical academic readings — Aune, Yarbro Collins, Koester, Bauckham, Ian Paul — read Babylon as Rome, full stop, in the historical referent, while allowing that the imaginative pattern can be applied analogically. The political-reading tradition (Richard, Boesak, Blount, Howard-Brook & Gwyther) reads Babylon as imperial economic order; Bauckham's reading of ch. 18 (and by extension ch. 17) as economic critique gives this position significant academic backing. Dispensationalist readings (Walvoord, LaHaye) — which must be steelmanned here, because they are the dominant popular reading in the United States — argue that the chapter prophesies a future world-religious system distinct from but allied with the future political Babylon of ch. 18. Steelmanning the dispensationalist position: it takes seriously that ch. 17 has not yet, in any historical sense, been fulfilled in a way that matches the cosmic finality of chs. 19–22, and it preserves the chapter's eschatological force where idealist readings risk dissolving it into pattern. The strongest dispensationalist case is not "the Pope is Mystery Babylon" but rather that the apocalyptic timeline of the book demands a future referent that pure preterism cannot supply. *Where this leaves us:* the chapter's first referent is Rome, but Rome as the seer sees it — not as bricks and aqueducts but as a spiritual structure — and the structure is portable enough to recur. The question of whether recurrence is metaphorical, typological, or eschatological in the strict dispensationalist sense remains genuinely open.

**Tension 2: The violence of 17:16 — divine judgment, imperial self-cannibalism, or both?**

The text says the ten kings "will hate the prostitute. They will make her desolate and naked, and devour her flesh and burn her up with fire." The traditional reading (across Catholic, Reformed, Orthodox, dispensationalist lines) takes the violence as imagined divine judgment expressed in Hebrew Bible idiom — Ezekiel 16 and 23 supply the same imagery for the indictment of Jerusalem and Samaria, and the prophetic genre speaks of cities as women. The reading favored by most critical scholars (Bauckham, Caird, Aune, Koester, Beale) is imperial self-cannibalism: the empire's own client-kings turn on the imperial center, as the Year of the Four Emperors actually witnessed; the violence is the system eating itself, with God's role being to "put it into their hearts" (17:17) rather than to enact the violence directly. Both readings agree the imagery is harsh, and that the patristic reception (in which "the Whore" became a stable trope for heretical movements) shows it is not inert; the standard caution is that, whatever its referent, the passage is a vision of judgment, not a script for the saints, who in this book always "conquer" by faithful witness. *Where this leaves us:* the political referent of the violence is the empire turning on itself, expressed in the prophetic idiom of city-as-woman; honest reading holds the political substance and the harshness of the imagery together rather than letting the allegory dissolve either.

**One unresolved question to close:** if Babylon's destruction is the empire eating itself — *they will eat her flesh* — then who, in the seer's vision, is actually doing the judgment? Is it God, who put it into their hearts (17:17)? Is it the beast, who turns on his rider? Is it the kings, who finally see what they have served? The chapter does not let us choose, and the refusal may be the point: empire's judgment, in John's vision, comes from inside the system it built.

## The Thread

— Daniel 7:7–8, 19–25. The fourth beast with ten horns is the substrate that ch. 17 reuses, but where Daniel sees four sequential empires, John collapses the lineage into a single composite ridden by a woman — the political and the cultural fused.

— Ezekiel 16 and 23. The prophetic indictment of Jerusalem and Samaria as adulteresses stripped and burned by their lovers supplies the violence of 17:16 directly; reading ch. 17 without these chapters is reading a sequel without the original.

— 1 Peter 5:13. "She who is at Babylon... sends you greetings." The same Roman-as-Babylon code that John uses appears in a letter from a different community, suggesting the cipher was widely shared among the early Christian movement and not John's invention.

— Revelation 21:9–10. The structural twin: another bowl-angel, another transport, another woman, another city — but this woman is the Bride and this city is the New Jerusalem. The chapter is unintelligible without its mirror.
