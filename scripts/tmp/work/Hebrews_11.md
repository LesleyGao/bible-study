# Hebrews 11 — By Faith: The Great Catalog

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Hebrews is the most rhetorically polished Greek text in the New Testament, and chapter 11 is its rhetorical summit — a sustained anaphora on πίστει (*pistei*, "by faith") that runs roughly eighteen times across thirty-nine verses, building one of the most famous periodic structures in early Christian literature. Yet the document is anonymous. Origen's resigned verdict still stands: "But who wrote the epistle, in truth God knows" (Eusebius, *Historia Ecclesiastica* 6.25.14). Tertullian floated Barnabas; Luther proposed Apollos; Adolf von Harnack in 1900 made the case for Priscilla — one of the perennial guesses, alongside Apollos, Barnabas, Silas, Clement, and Luke, none of which the text confirms. The catalog's inclusion of Sarah and Rahab as positive paradigms of faith — and, on one reading of 11:11, the foregrounding of Sarah's own act of faith — is a notable feature of the chapter's rhetoric in its own right. William L. Lane (Word Biblical Commentary) and Barnabas Lindars locate the audience among Roman Jewish-Christians, the same community Paul addresses in Romans, sometime between 60 and 90 AD. Heb 12:4 ("you have not yet resisted to the point of shedding blood") combined with the prior persecution recalled in 10:32-34 places the homily after Nero's pogrom of the mid-60s but before the Domitianic intensification of the 90s.

Genre matters. The author calls his own work a λόγος παρακλήσεως (*logos paraklēseōs*, "word of exhortation") in 13:22, the same phrase Luke uses for synagogue homilies (Acts 13:15). Lawrence Wills and Hartwig Thyen have demonstrated that Hebrews is a Hellenistic-synagogue sermon with a brief epistolary postscript glued to its end. Chapter 11 is the homily's encomiastic crescendo. Albert Vanhoye, in his structural analysis (*La structure littéraire de l'Épître aux Hébreux*), identified the catalog as the second of three concentric panels in the central exhortation. Pamela Eisenbaum's landmark monograph *The Jewish Heroes of Christian History: Hebrews 11 in Literary Context* (1997) reads the chapter against parallel Hellenistic-Jewish faith-catalogs — Wisdom of Solomon 10, Sirach 44-50 ("Praise of the Fathers"), 1 Maccabees 2, Philo's *De Praemiis et Poenis* 11-21, and the speeches of 4 Maccabees — and argues that Hebrews uniquely detaches the heroes from Torah and covenant identity, reframing them as proto-Christian witnesses awaiting the better thing in Christ. This is a major Jewish-scholarly critique to which Christian interpreters owe a careful answer.

The text's manuscript history is unusually rich. P46 (c. 200 AD) places Hebrews directly after Romans, suggesting an early Pauline corpus that found Hebrews necessary near its head. Sinaiticus (א), Vaticanus (B), and Alexandrinus (A) preserve substantially the same text we read today. The famous textual problem at 11:11 — whether Sarah is the subject of the participle or merely its impediment — is genuinely a problem of grammar rather than transmission, and we will return to it.

## Lens 2: The Literary Architecture

The chapter opens with a definitional clause that has reverberated through every subsequent generation of Christian theology: Ἔστιν δὲ πίστις ἐλπιζομένων ὑπόστασις, πραγμάτων ἔλεγχος οὐ βλεπομένων (*estin de pistis elpizomenōn hypostasis, pragmatōn elenchos ou blepomenōn*) — "now faith is the *hypostasis* of things hoped for, the *elenchos* of things not seen" (11:1). The translation question is enormous. The KJV's "substance" and "evidence" carry the older Aristotelian-Patristic-Scholastic reading, in which faith is an *ontological* foundation — not merely a psychological state but a participation in the reality of what is hoped for. Aquinas cites this verse as the locus for his treatment of faith in *Summa Theologiae* II-II q.4 a.1, glossing *hypostasis* as substantia and *elenchos* as evidence such that faith functions as the inchoate possession of beatific vision. Calvin in *Institutes* III.2.41 oscillates between the objective and subjective senses, finally weighting toward *fiducia* ("trustful confidence") but without losing the objective ground. The modern subjective rendering (RSV/NIV "assurance" and "conviction") follows Helmut Köster's *TDNT* analysis (8:572-589), which traces *hypostasis* through Philo (*Quod Deus Immutabilis Sit* 172) and the LXX (Ezek 19:5; Ps 39:7) and concludes that both senses are linguistically available. Madison Pierce's *Divine Discourse in the Epistle to the Hebrews* (2020) argues for a participatory ontological recovery: faith is the standing-under-with the things hoped for. The translation choice has theological consequences: faith as inner certainty versus faith as participation in the real.

After the definition, the catalog unfolds in five discernible movements. The pre-deluge heroes (Abel, Enoch, Noah, 11:4-7) introduce faith as that which testifies, escapes death, and prepares an ark against the unseen. The patriarchs (11:8-22) introduce faith as exile and pilgrimage: Abraham obeys without knowing where he goes, lives in tents in the land of promise, looks for a city whose architect is God. The literary intrusion at 11:13-16 — "these all died in faith, not having received the things promised, but having seen them and greeted them from afar, and having acknowledged that they were strangers and exiles on the earth" — is one of the great pieces of New Testament theological prose. The author's word for the patriarchs' status, παρεπίδημοι (*parepidēmoi*, "sojourners"), would be picked up by 1 Peter 1:1 and would shape the entire Christian theology of pilgrimage from Augustine's *City of God* through Hauerwas's *Resident Aliens*.

Verse 11 introduces the famous grammatical crux: Πίστει καὶ αὐτὴ Σάρρα δύναμιν εἰς καταβολὴν σπέρματος ἔλαβεν (*Pistei kai autē Sarra dynamin eis katabolēn spermatos elaben*). The phrase εἰς καταβολὴν σπέρματος ("for the depositing of seed") is technically male reproductive language, which led some commentators to argue Abraham must be the implicit subject and Sarah a parenthetic note ("by faith [Abraham] received power for procreation, even though Sarah was barren"). Attridge in the Hermeneia volume rejects this reading on grammatical grounds and reaffirms Sarah as subject. Lane in the WBC argues similarly. P. Trummer surveys the textual history and concludes that the traditional reading is preferable but that the language genuinely strains. The reading matters: if Sarah is subject, chapter 11 includes her as paradigm of faith alongside Abel, Enoch, Noah, Abraham, and Moses; if Abraham is subject, the catalog reverts to a male typology punctuated by the later mention of Rahab as an outlier.

The Moses cycle (11:23-29) is the longest single treatment, occupying seven verses. The exodus motif intensifies the pilgrim theme. The conquest cluster (11:30-31) is briefer: Jericho and Rahab. Then the rhetorical accelerando: "and what more shall I say?" The author drops the anaphoric πίστει and begins listing without it: Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, David, Samuel, the prophets — a deliberate breaking of the pattern that signals the reader has been mapped onto the entire scriptural narrative.

Then comes the post-canonical extension that has unsettled exegetes for centuries. The phrase "women received back their dead by resurrection" (11:35a) refers to the widows of Zarephath (1 Kgs 17) and Shunem (2 Kgs 4). But "others were tortured (ἐτυμπανίσθησαν, *etympanisthēsan*), refusing to accept release, that they might rise to a better life" (11:35b) shifts registers entirely. The verb *etympanizō* refers to a torture device, the *tympanon* (a stretching-rack or drum-shaped instrument), and the construction "refusing to accept release that they might rise to a better life" almost verbatim quotes 2 Maccabees 6:18-7:42 — the martyrdom of Eleazar and the seven brothers and their mother under Antiochus IV Epiphanes — and is elaborated philosophically in 4 Maccabees, the Hellenistic-Jewish treatise that fuses Stoic *ataraxia* with covenantal fidelity. David deSilva has shown that 4 Maccabees was widely circulated in first-century Hellenistic Judaism and likely known to Hebrews' audience.

Then the references continue: "they were stoned, they were sawn in two (ἐπρίσθησαν, *epristhēsan*), they were killed with the sword" (11:37). The phrase "sawn in two" almost certainly references the *Martyrdom of Isaiah* — a pseudepigraphical Jewish text in which Isaiah is sawn in half on Manasseh's order, cited by Justin Martyr (*Dialogue with Trypho* 120.5), Tertullian (*De Patientia* 14), and Origen (*Commentary on Matthew* 13:57). The *Lives of the Prophets* §1 records the same tradition. Hebrews canonizes faith-narratives drawn from texts the Reformers later excluded from the canon. The implications for the canon question are not small.

The chapter closes with a theologically loaded coda: "all these, though commended through their faith, did not receive what was promised, since God had provided something better for us, that apart from us they should not be made perfect (τελειωθῶσιν, *teleiōthōsin*)" (11:39-40). The verb τελειόω is one of Hebrews' signature theological terms; it appears in the prologue (2:10), the high-priestly central panel (5:9; 7:28), and now at the catalog's close. Faith awaits its perfecting in the eschatological congregation that includes both the ancient witnesses and the present readers.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story — Jewish and Christian Trajectories

Hebrews 11 belongs to a recognizable genre. Wisdom of Solomon 10 reviews the patriarchs as exempla of personified Wisdom; Sirach 44-50 ("Praise of the Fathers, in their generation") catalogs Israel's heroes as honored ancestors; 1 Maccabees 2:51-60 (the deathbed speech of Mattathias) lists Abraham, Joseph, Phinehas, Joshua, Caleb, David, Elijah, the three young men, and Daniel as exemplars of zeal for Torah. Philo's *De Praemiis et Poenis* 11-21 catalogs Enosh, Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Moses — almost the same opening sequence as Hebrews 11. The genre is established Hellenistic-Jewish rhetoric.

What is *different* about Hebrews 11 is the controlling principle. In Sirach and 1 Maccabees, the heroes are exemplars of *zeal for Torah*. In Wisdom 10, they are exemplars of personified Wisdom's saving work. In Philo, they are exemplars of philosophical virtue. In Hebrews 11, they are exemplars of πίστις — and that πίστις is defined, at the catalog's close, as anticipation of "something better" that has now appeared in Christ (11:40; cf. 1:1-4). Eisenbaum's critique cuts here: the move detaches the heroes from their Israelite-covenantal context and re-narrates them as proto-Christian witnesses. From a post-Holocaust Jewish-Christian dialogue standpoint (R. Kendall Soulen, Mary Rose D'Angelo), this is the mechanism of supersessionist hermeneutics — the very form of the hero-list reframes the Hebrew Bible toward Christological fulfillment. The Christian response — articulated for instance by Richard Hays in *Echoes of Scripture* — is that Hebrews' move is figural rather than supersessionist: the heroes remain themselves and remain heroes of faith, but their faith reaches forward into a fulfillment that does not erase its origin. The disagreement is live and serious.

Within Christian reception, Hebrews 11 has shaped four major doctrinal architectures. (1) The doctrine of *fides ex auditu* — faith as response to the divine word — depends heavily on 11:3 (the worlds were prepared by the word of God) and the catalog's repeated structure of obedient response to revelation. (2) The doctrine of the "communion of saints" draws partly from the cloud-of-witnesses figure of 12:1 but is grounded in the catalog itself: the dead saints remain present to the living congregation. (3) The doctrine of *creatio ex nihilo* found one of its earliest proof-texts in 11:3 ("what is seen was made out of things not visible"), though the Greek phrase μὴ ἐκ φαινομένων is closer to "not out of things appearing" than strict ex nihilo; Erich Grässer and James W. Thompson trace the patristic appropriation. (4) The eschatological "city that has foundations" (11:10, 16) became one of the foundational images for Augustine's *Civitas Dei* and for the entire Christian theology of pilgrimage. Augustine cites Hebrews 11 throughout *City of God* 11-22, and the medieval pilgrim spirituality of Bernard of Clairvaux through Bonaventure draws on the παρεπίδημοι motif.

The Jewish trajectory after Hebrews is equally significant. The Maccabean martyrs of 2 Macc 6-7, which Hebrews canonizes by reference, became the foundational martyrology of rabbinic Judaism — the foundation for the medieval *Hannah and her Seven Sons* tradition, the *Yom Kippur* martyrology liturgy *Eleh Ezkerah*, and the Tisha B'Av kinot. The same texts that shaped Hebrews 11:35 shaped Jewish covenantal memory along an entirely different trajectory.

N.T. Wright (*Hebrews for Everyone*; *Surprised by Hope*, 2007; *The Resurrection of the Son of God*, 2003) reads chapter 11 — and its sequel image, the "great cloud of witnesses" of 12:1 — as a charter of forward-leaning, resurrection-shaped hope rather than of disembodied heaven. The heroes "all died in faith, not having received what was promised" (11:13, 39); they are not enjoying the consummation in some timeless realm but, like the living readers, still waiting for it, "so that apart from us they should not be made perfect" (11:40). For Wright that closing line is the point of the whole catalog: the witnesses and the present congregation belong to a single people on a single journey toward a single goal — the "city that has foundations, whose architect and builder is God" (11:10, 16), which is not an escape from creation but God's new creation, heaven and earth joined. The "cloud of witnesses," on his reading, is less a grandstand of spectators than the company of those who have run before us and whose own finishing line is the same as ours, in the resurrection at the Messiah's appearing.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — Two Tensions

### Tension 1: Faith as Ontological Participation versus Subjective Conviction (11:1)

The first tension is generated by the famous definition itself. Read with the Aristotelian-Patristic strain, faith is *hypostasis* in the strong sense: the standing-under, the substantial foundation, the inchoate possession of what is hoped for. Aquinas in *ST* II-II q.4 a.1 makes this the controlling sense and develops the entire theology of *fides quaerens intellectum* (Anselm's phrase) on this footing: faith is not merely the subjective state of the believer but the actual ontological foothold the believer has in the realities God promises. Calvin retains a strong ontological dimension when he glosses faith as "a firm and certain knowledge of God's benevolence toward us, founded upon the truth of the freely given promise in Christ" (*Institutes* III.2.7) — though Calvin's controlling word becomes *fiducia*, "trust." The Eastern Orthodox tradition, articulated by Gregory Palamas and Vladimir Lossky, reads faith as the entry-point of θεωρία, the participatory contemplation that ultimately opens onto θέωσις. The strong reading must be steelmanned: in this view, when Hebrews calls faith *hypostasis* of things hoped for, the author is saying that faith is not *about* the unseen — faith is the present mode of standing in the unseen.

The modern subjectivized reading replaces *hypostasis* with "assurance" and *elenchos* with "conviction." Bultmann's existentialist hermeneutic radicalizes this: faith is the existential decision through which authentic existence is constituted, with no claim to participation in trans-temporal reality. The pastoral utility of the subjective reading is real — it speaks directly to the experience of doubt, since faith here is precisely the inner posture that holds when sight fails. But it pays a price. If faith is only inner certainty, then 11:1 dissolves into a tautology: faith is the conviction by which one is convinced. The strong reading preserves what the weak reading loses — the claim that faith touches the real.

### Tension 2: The Maccabean-Pseudepigraphical Citation and the Boundaries of Canon

The second tension concerns what Hebrews does in 11:35-37 and what it implies. The author cites 2 Maccabees 7 and the *Martyrdom of Isaiah* as smoothly continuous with Abel, Noah, Abraham, and Moses. He draws no boundary. Yet by the sixteenth century, three different canonical settlements had emerged. The Catholic Church at the Council of Trent (1546, *Decretum de Canonicis Scripturis*) ratified a 73-book canon including 1-2 Maccabees, Tobit, Judith, Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, Baruch, and additions to Esther and Daniel as deuterocanonical scripture, on a par with the protocanonical books. Eastern Orthodoxy, in the 1672 Synod of Jerusalem and the standard Septuagint editions, settled on a still wider 78-book canon adding 3 Maccabees, 1 Esdras, and Psalm 151. The Protestant Reformation, finalized by the Westminster Confession (1647), settled on a 66-book canon excluding the apocrypha entirely, on the grounds that they were never part of the Hebrew Bible.

Bruce Metzger's *The Canon of the New Testament* and Lee Martin McDonald's *The Biblical Canon* have shown that the boundaries were not yet firm in the first century. Albert Sundberg's research demonstrated that there was no single closed Jewish canon at the time of the New Testament. Hebrews 11 is a vivid window onto that fluidity. The author can cite the *Martyrdom of Isaiah* alongside Genesis without explanation. What does this mean for contemporary Christian Bibles? The Protestant must explain why Hebrews casually crosses a boundary the Reformers later drew. The Catholic must explain why the *Martyrdom of Isaiah* — clearly cited in 11:37 — is not in the Catholic canon either. The Orthodox can reach further but still does not include the *Martyrdom of Isaiah*. The honest answer may be that the canon of the Old Testament reflects a settled boundary the author of Hebrews did not yet recognize, and that the discontinuity between his Bible and ours is real.

**Closing question:** If the same faith that walks Abraham across the desert also stretches Eleazar on the *tympanon* and saws Isaiah in two — and if Hebrews places these heroes in a single unbroken cloud — then what does it mean that the Bible the author cites is not, in any of its modern editions, the Bible we hold? Does faith bind us to a wider witness than the canon now records?

## The Thread

Faith, in Hebrews 11, is the standing-under by which the unseen becomes present to the one who waits. The chapter is a long exhalation of names — Abel, Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Sarah, Moses, Rahab, the prophets, Eleazar, the seven brothers, Isaiah — and each name is the body of a faith that did not see its promise and trusted anyway. The catalog ends with a theological scandal: the witnesses do not yet have what they were promised, because they were waiting for *us*. The cloud is not complete without the present congregation. To read this chapter is to be told that one has been waited for by the dead, that the saints of every generation are gathered around a single horizon, and that the faith they exercised was not theirs alone but ours with them — one πίστις, awaiting one τελείωσις, in the better thing now revealed.
