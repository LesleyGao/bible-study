# 2 Corinthians 5 — Scholarly Analysis

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Roman Corinth in the mid-50s of the first century was no longer the classical Greek polis Cicero's grandfather had visited. Lucius Mummius razed it in 146 BC, and Julius Caesar refounded it in 44 BC as Colonia Laus Iulia Corinthiensis, a Roman colony stocked initially with Italian freedmen. By the time Paul wrote 2 Corinthians from Macedonia, probably in AD 55 or 56, the city had become the capital of Achaia, the seat of a proconsul, and a commercial hinge between the Aegean and the Adriatic. Gerd Theissen, Wayne Meeks, Dale Martin, and Bruce Winter have shown that Paul's correspondents were a socially layered ekklesia containing the Erastus of Romans 16:23 (likely the city's aedile or treasurer), some merchants and artisans, slaves, freedmen, and almost certainly Romans, Greeks, and Diaspora Jews. Jerome Murphy-O'Connor's *St. Paul's Corinth* assembled the inscriptional and archaeological evidence for a culture saturated by patronage, status competition, and a particular Corinthian taste for rhetorical display.

Paul's relationship with this community by the time of 2 Corinthians 5 was bruised. The standard reconstruction runs as follows: founding visit c. AD 50–51 (Acts 18); 1 Corinthians from Ephesus c. AD 53–54; a "painful visit" implied by 2:1 and 13:2; a "tearful letter" carried by Titus and referenced in 2:3–4, 7:8; and finally 2 Corinthians from Macedonia after Titus's reassuring report. Murray Harris (NIGTC, 2005), Margaret Thrall (ICC, 1994/2000), Victor Furnish (Anchor Yale, 1984), Ralph Martin (WBC, 1986), and Frank Matera (NTL, 2003) all converge on this rough chronology even when they disagree about partition.

Chapter 5 sits in the long apologetic arc that runs from 2:14 through 7:4, what Hans Dieter Betz and others identify as Paul's defense of his apostolic ministry against rivals (the "super-apostles" of 11:5) who had insinuated themselves at Corinth. Five concrete background images frame the chapter. First, the *skēnos* metaphor of 5:1–4 trades on the language of military and pilgrim tents familiar in Greco-Roman everyday life and on the Jewish Sukkot festival, where the temporary booth (*sukkah*) symbolized both Israel's wilderness fragility and divine shelter. Second, the "guarantee" or *arrabōn* of 5:5 is a commercial term for earnest money documented in Greek papyri at Oxyrhynchus. Third, the *bēma* of 5:10 was a recognizable physical object in Corinth itself: the elevated stone tribunal in the agora where Gallio judged Paul (Acts 18:12–17), a fragment of which still stands. Fourth, the political-diplomatic vocabulary of 5:20 — *presbeuomen*, "we are ambassadors" — borrows directly from imperial diplomatic practice in which envoys spoke with the authority of the sovereign who sent them. Fifth, the language of *katallagē* (reconciliation) carries both household-finance overtones (the settling of accounts) and political ones (peace treaties between cities).

Paul's apostolic predicament shapes the rhetoric. He has been accused, on the evidence of 10–13, of being "weighty" in letters but "weak" in person, of inconsistency in his travel plans, of refusing patronage support, and of lacking the proper letters of recommendation. In chapter 5 he answers these charges by reframing the entire ministerial enterprise eschatologically: the body that looks weak is being prepared for a building not made with hands; the apostle who looks foolish stands before a higher tribunal; the message that looks scandalous is in fact the cosmic announcement of a new creation already inaugurated. The historical Corinthian setting — a city obsessed with status performance — clarifies why Paul keeps insisting that what is visible is not what counts.

## Lens 2: The Literature of the Text

The chapter divides cleanly into three movements. Verses 1–10 develop the body/dwelling/clothing imagery and the *bēma*. Verses 11–15 turn to the love of Christ as the controlling logic of apostolic ministry. Verses 16–21 unfold the new-creation, reconciliation, and atonement vocabulary, climaxing in the dense formulation of 5:21.

Paul's metaphor cluster in 5:1–10 is unusually thick. He moves from architecture (*oikodomē* — building, *oikia* — house) to fabric (the tent, *skēnos*) to clothing (the layered verbs *endysasthai*, *epi-endysasthai*, *ek-dysasthai*) and back to architecture again (the heavenly *oikētērion*). The remarkable hapax legomenon *epi-endysasthai* in 5:2, 4 ("to be further clothed") is unattested in earlier Greek literature and appears to be Pauline coinage. The sequence resists harmonization into a single picture, which suggests Paul is improvising — reaching for whatever image will hold the paradox of a self that is simultaneously dying and being prepared.

The *arrabōn* of 5:5 (also at 1:22 and Eph 1:14) is a Semitic loan-word into Greek (cognate to Hebrew *erabon*), used in commercial papyri for earnest money: a partial payment that legally guarantees the larger debt to come. The Spirit, in this reading, is not merely a sign but a legally binding installment.

The *bēma* of 5:10 is technical. Linda Belleville (IVP, 1996) and Paul Barnett (NICNT, 1997) note the deliberate parallel to Romans 14:10. The verb *phaneroō* ("to appear") is passive — believers do not present themselves; they are made manifest. The word *komisētai* ("receive") is forensic but commercial: it suggests recompense, not punishment in the strict sense.

In 5:11–15 Paul interweaves a classic forensic *captatio*: "we persuade" (*peithomen*), "we commend" (*synistanomen*), "we are not commending" (negation), "you may be able to answer" (*kauchēma* — "boast"). The chiastic patterning around *kardia* / *prosōpon* in 5:12 contrasts inward and outward, surface and depth — a Pauline theme that runs through 3:18 and 4:6.

Verse 14 introduces a participatory logic that shapes everything that follows: "one died for all, therefore all died" (*hoti heis hyper pantōn apethanen, ara hoi pantes apethanon*). The argument hinges on *hyper* (on behalf of, in the place of) and on the inferential *ara*. Morna Hooker's "interchange" thesis (especially in *From Adam to Christ*) reads this as the foundational Pauline soteriological grammar.

Verses 16–21 build a tight chiastic structure. The opening "from now on" (*apo tou nyn*) signals eschatological time. *Kainē ktisis* in 5:17 ("new creation") echoes Isaiah 43:18–19 and 65:17 LXX. Paul shifts from third-person observation ("if anyone is in Christ") to first-person inclusion ("us" in 18) to second-person pleading ("be reconciled" — 20) and finally to third-person formula again (21). The verbs of 5:18–20 — *katallaxantos*, *katallagēs*, *katallassōn*, *katallagēte* — pile up the reconciliation root five times in three verses. The cognate *logizomenos* ("not counting") in 19 lifts vocabulary from accounting; God's books toward the world have been zeroed out.

Verse 21 is one of the most compressed Christological-soteriological sentences in the New Testament: *ton mē gnonta hamartian hyper hēmōn hamartian epoiēsen, hina hēmeis genōmetha dikaiosynē theou en autō*. Three terms collide: *hamartia* (sin or sin-offering), *hyper hēmōn* (on our behalf), and *dikaiosynē theou* (the righteousness of God — the contested Pauline phrase from Romans 1:17 and 3:21–26). Whether *hamartia* in its second occurrence means moral sin or the technical Levitical sin-offering (*hattat*, rendered *peri hamartias* in Lev 4–5 LXX) is genuinely undecidable on lexical grounds alone, as Margaret Thrall and Murray Harris both concede.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Two larger trajectories meet in this chapter: a Jewish trajectory about death, the body, and the world to come, and a Christian trajectory about the cross, atonement, and new creation. Both must be set out fairly.

The Jewish background is dense. The *skēnos* / tent imagery is doubly resonant. It evokes Israel's wilderness *mishkan* (the tabernacle the LXX renders *skēnē*) and the *sukkah* of the Sukkot festival, when devout Jews still left their permanent houses for a week to live in temporary booths. The Wisdom of Solomon 9:15 had already used the tent metaphor for the body: "the perishable body burdens the soul, and the earthy tent (*to geōdes skēnos*) weighs down the thoughtful mind." Paul knows this text. The ascetic dualism of Wisdom is not quite Paul's, but the language is shared.

On the intermediate state, Second Temple Judaism was not unanimous. Sheol in the Hebrew Bible (Job, Psalms, Ecclesiastes) is a shadowy underworld where the distinction between righteous and wicked is muted. By the second century BC, however, texts like 1 Enoch 22 imagine compartmentalized chambers in which souls await judgment, with the righteous in light and the wicked in darkness. 4 Ezra 7 offers an even more developed scheme: seven days of soul-rest after death, then a return to dust until resurrection. Luke 16's parable of Lazarus and the rich man, with its chasm between Abraham's bosom and torment, presupposes such a developed schema. Pharisees affirmed bodily resurrection (Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.14; *War* 2.163); Sadducees denied it (Mark 12:18). Paul, who self-identifies as a Pharisee in Philippians 3:5 and Acts 23:6, stands in the resurrection tradition.

On atonement, the Jewish background of 5:21 is Yom Kippur and the Servant Song of Isaiah 53. Richard Hays, N. T. Wright, and Morna Hooker independently argue that 5:21 should be read against Leviticus 16, where one goat bears Israel's sin and is sent away while the other is offered as *hattat* (sin offering). The Servant of Isaiah 53:6, 10 LXX is the figure who is given over for sins. Daniel Boyarin, in *A Radical Jew* and elsewhere, has insisted that 5:21 is intelligible Jewish-apocalyptic language, not a Hellenistic invention; the framework is Jewish messianic suffering, not Greek philosophical substitution. Pamela Eisenbaum, Mark Nanos, and the broader "Paul within Judaism" school continue to press the case that Paul's atonement language never leaves the categories of Israel's scriptures and cult.

The Christian trajectory expands these seeds. On the intermediate state, three readings have organized Christian tradition. First, Tertullian (*De Anima* 55–58) imagined a *refrigerium* — a place of refreshment for the righteous awaiting bodily resurrection, with martyrs alone admitted directly to God's presence. Origen sketched a graduated post-mortem progress through purgative stages. Augustine in *City of God* 13 settled the matter for Latin Christianity: the disembodied soul of the just experiences something real but inferior to bodily resurrection, which awaits the eschaton. Pope Benedict XII's bull *Benedictus Deus* (1336) crystallized the Catholic dogma that souls of the just enjoy the beatific vision *before* the final resurrection — a position now in CCC 1023. Eastern Orthodoxy preserves a parallel but less juridical tradition; some Orthodox sources speak of "aerial toll houses" through which the soul passes after death, though this teaching is contested even within Orthodoxy.

The Reformation reopened the debate. Luther sometimes spoke as if the dead simply slept until resurrection (e.g., in his 1542 letter to Nicholas von Amsdorf), though he was inconsistent. Calvin's first theological treatise, *Psychopannychia* (1542), was a polemic against soul-sleep. The Westminster Confession 32 codified the Reformed position: the souls of the righteous "are received into the highest heavens" immediately at death. F. F. Bruce was unusual among modern evangelicals in being open to soul-sleep on exegetical grounds in *Paul: Apostle of the Heart Set Free*. Murray Harris in *Raised Immortal* pushed the opposite direction, arguing 5:1–10 supports an immediate post-mortem reception of the resurrection body. N. T. Wright in *Surprised by Hope* defends the more traditional reading: a conscious intermediate state ("paradise" or "with the Lord") followed by bodily resurrection at the parousia. Joachim Jeremias's earlier work on intertestamental Judaism informed all of these.

On atonement, the Christian streams diverge sharply at 5:21. Anselm in *Cur Deus Homo* developed a satisfaction theory that the Reformers (Luther, Calvin, Beza) sharpened into penal substitution: Christ takes the legal penalty of sin on himself so the elect are credited with God's righteousness. John Stott's *The Cross of Christ* and Mark Seifrid's exegesis stand in this line; Simon Gathercole's *Defending Substitution* presents the modern philological case. Gustaf Aulén's *Christus Victor* (1931) reasserted an older patristic emphasis on Christ's victory over the powers, with atonement as cosmic deliverance rather than juridical exchange. Joel Green and Mark Baker's *Recovering the Scandal of the Cross* argues that no single theory exhausts the New Testament's atonement metaphors. Hans Boersma in *Violence, Hospitality, and the Cross* tries to hold penal and participatory motifs together. N. T. Wright (especially in *The Day the Revolution Began* and *Justification*) has been emphatic that 5:21 does not teach a "bare-mechanical" penal substitution; the verse is, on his reading, about apostles becoming the embodiment of God's covenant faithfulness in their commissioning. Morna Hooker's "interchange" — Christ takes our condition so that we share his — has become an influential bridge category.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent and Tensions

Two questions matter here. What does Paul mean by the body and what comes after death? And what theory of atonement is presupposed in 5:21?

On the intermediate state, the most honest reading recognizes that 5:1–10 is improvisational and unsystematic. Paul oscillates between wanting to be "further clothed" without an interval of nakedness (5:4) and being willing to be "away from the body and at home with the Lord" (5:8) — which presupposes some interval. Most Catholic, Reformed, and mainline Protestant traditions hold to a conscious intermediate state followed by bodily resurrection, with internal differences over purgatory (Catholic) and the specific character of the post-mortem state (CCC 1021–1029 catalogues the Catholic position; Westminster 32 the Reformed). The soul-sleep position deserves to be taken seriously as a steelman: it preserves the holism of Hebrew anthropology, refuses to read Greek body-soul dualism into Paul, locates personal continuity in God's memory rather than a free-floating soul, and points to the consistent New Testament emphasis on resurrection rather than disembodied immortality. Bruce, the Adventists, and the Jehovah's Witnesses all draw real support from this exegetical instinct, even if most readers conclude that 5:8 weighs against them. Murray Harris's immediate-resurrection-body reading, conversely, takes 5:1–4 at face value but at the cost of complicating 1 Corinthians 15's apparently sequential eschatology and the parousia hope of 1 Thessalonians 4. Wright's mediating position — *intermediate* but *anticipatory*, a real but provisional state preceding the bodily resurrection that is the actual hope — captures the Pauline emphasis better than either alternative.

On atonement, the genuine tension at 5:21 is whether Paul means a substitution (Christ takes the penalty I deserve) or a participation (Christ enters my condition so I can share his). The Reformed mainstream insists on substitution. Wright, Hooker, and the participatory school insist that "in him" (*en autō*) at the verse's end is irreducibly relational: we become the righteousness of God *in* Christ, not in exchange for him. Catholic theology has historically spoken of both — the *commercium admirabile*, the "wonderful exchange" of the Latin liturgy, holds substitution and participation together. Eastern Orthodox theosis emphasizes the participatory pole almost exclusively. The most defensible reading is that Paul's grammar refuses the dichotomy. *Hamartian epoiēsen* — "he made him to be sin" — is too dense to be reduced either to a transactional crediting or to a purely existential identification. The Levitical *hattat* background suggests a cultic register in which the sin-offering does not "carry guilt" in a forensic sense but absorbs ritual impurity. Paul is doing apocalyptic theology in cultic vocabulary, and the result is that Christ's death is at once judicial, cultic, participatory, and victorious — not because Paul is incoherent but because the event he is naming exceeds any single category.

The pastoral payoff in this chapter is severe and consoling at once. Severe: the *bēma* of 5:10 is real; what is done in the body matters. Consoling: the verdict of that *bēma* has been anticipated in Christ's death, and the apostle's commission is not to broker forgiveness but to announce that God has already reconciled the world and is now appealing through human ambassadors. The new creation of 5:17 is not a private interior change but a cosmic claim about what kind of world it now is. To regard no one *kata sarka* is to refuse the evaluative framework — Roman, Corinthian, status-driven, surface-reading — that Paul's opponents at Corinth had brought into the church.
