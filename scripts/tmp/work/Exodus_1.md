# Exodus 1: The Empire That Forgets

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The book opens in Egypt, and the first thing a contemporary reader would have noticed is the careful bookkeeping of a bureaucratic empire. Pharaoh numbers his laborers, assigns taskmasters, orders infrastructure, and attempts to regulate reproduction. This is not a primitive tyranny. It is the administrative voice of one of the longest-lived, most literate civilizations of the Bronze Age, a state whose scribes counted grain, cattle, and corvée workers on papyrus and ostraca, and whose kings built their legacies out of mudbrick and stone.

The political situation depends on which Pharaoh the narrator has in mind, and the text deliberately refuses to name him. Two dominant scholarly positions have framed the debate for a century. The "early date" (fifteenth century BC, roughly the reign of Thutmose III or Amenhotep II) depends on a straightforward reading of 1 Kings 6:1's "four hundred and eightieth year." The "late date" (thirteenth century BC, the reign of Ramses II, c. 1279–1213 BC) is favored by most archaeologists because of the explicit mention of the store city "Raamses" in verse 11. Kenneth Kitchen and James Hoffmeier have argued the late date vigorously on the basis of Ramesside administrative texts (*Papyrus Anastasi* VI, for example, describes Semitic laborers tending flocks in the eastern Delta), while Bryant Wood has defended the early date on chronological grounds. The Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BC), discovered by Flinders Petrie in 1896, names Israel as a people already settled in Canaan, which sets an outside boundary: whatever the exodus was, it happened before then.

The material culture of bondage is described with uncomfortable specificity. The Israelites work in *ḥōmer* (חֹמֶר), mortar, and *lǝbēnîm* (לְבֵנִים), bricks — the lowest stratum of Egyptian construction labor. Egyptian tomb paintings, most famously the fifteenth-century tomb of the vizier Rekhmire at Thebes, show Semitic laborers making bricks with straw, water, and clay under the supervision of overseers with rods. Pithom (Egyptian *Per-Atum*, "House of Atum") and Raamses (*Pi-Ramesses*, "House of Ramses") have been excavated — Tell el-Retaba and Qantir/Tell el-Dab'a respectively — and Pi-Ramesses was indeed the capital Ramses II built in the eastern Delta with heavy reliance on Asiatic labor. These store cities were logistical hubs for military expeditions into Canaan, not temple complexes. The oppression has a strategic shape: the empire is militarizing its frontier.

Authorship and dating are inseparable from the documentary hypothesis. The critical tradition following Julius Wellhausen divided the Pentateuch into J (Yahwist, tenth century), E (Elohist, ninth), D (Deuteronomist, seventh), and P (Priestly, sixth–fifth). In Exodus 1, most source critics identify the genealogical list in vv. 1–5 and the summary in v. 7 as Priestly (with its characteristic concern for numbers and fruitfulness echoing Gen 1:28), while the midwife narrative in vv. 15–21 is typically assigned to E or J, and the brick-making oppression to a mixed redaction. More recent scholarship — Rolf Rendtorff, Erhard Blum, Konrad Schmid, and Thomas Römer — has largely abandoned a clean four-source theory for a model of successive exilic and post-exilic redactions, though the final form still visibly joins Genesis's patriarchal promises to Exodus's deliverance narrative.

Five Hebrew terms do work the English translations cannot. *Wayyiprû wayyišrǝṣû wayyirbû wayyaʿaṣmû* (וַיִּפְרוּ וַיִּשְׁרְצוּ וַיִּרְבּוּ וַיַּעַצְמוּ, "they were fruitful and swarmed and multiplied and grew mighty," v. 7) stacks four verbs from Genesis 1:20–28 — a deliberate creation-echo arguing that Pharaoh is fighting the blessing spoken over humanity itself. *Šǝraṣ* ("swarm") is the verb used of fish and creeping things; it is almost insulting applied to a people, and Pharaoh's dread in v. 12 turns on exactly this sense of proliferation beyond control. *ʿĀbad* (עָבַד) and its noun *ʿăbōdâ* (עֲבֹדָה) mean both "work" and "serve/worship" — the same root that will designate Israel's future cultic service to YHWH. The book is already asking: whom will Israel serve? *Wayyāqom melek ḥādāš ʿal-miṣrayim ʾăšer lōʾ-yādaʿ ʾet-yôsēp* (v. 8): "a new king arose over Egypt who did not know Joseph." The verb *yādaʿ* ("know") is covenantal vocabulary; this pharaoh's forgetting is not amnesia but a willed ignorance, a refusal to acknowledge obligation. And *yirʾû* (v. 17) — "the midwives feared God" — uses the core theological term *yirʾat ʾĕlōhîm* that wisdom literature will make the foundation of ethical life.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is narrative prologue, and its structural work is enormous. In fifty-two verses the author bridges Genesis and Exodus, establishes the political conflict of the entire book, introduces the theme of oppression, and sets up the birth of Moses. The prologue splits into three panels that escalate in brutality: verses 1–7 (multiplication), 8–14 (hard labor), 15–22 (infanticide). Each cycle shows Pharaoh's intensifying countermeasure failing against the blessing.

The opening genealogy in vv. 1–5 is not decorative. It is a resumption of Genesis 46:8–27, using the same formula and the same number — seventy persons — to link the two books and assert that the covenant people described here are the very family of Jacob described there. Robert Alter calls this technique "epic repetition with variation." The "seventy" has Near Eastern resonance: in the Canaanite *Baal Cycle* seventy are the sons of Athirat, and in the Ugaritic pantheon seventy represents the ideal complete number. By echoing it here, the author signals that Israel, though a family, is a nation-in-waiting.

Verse 7 is a carefully composed chiastic centerpiece for the book's opening. Its four verbs move from biology (*pārâ*, fruitful) through proliferation (*šāraṣ*) to simple multiplication (*rābâ*) to power (*ʿāṣam*). Scholars including Nahum Sarna, Terence Fretheim, and Brevard Childs have read this as an intentional echo of Genesis 1:28 and 9:1, making the argument that Pharaoh's campaign in vv. 8–22 is not merely political but cosmological: he is attempting to reverse creation.

The literary device most characteristic of this chapter is what James Ackerman calls "the inverted hierarchy." The greatest empire on earth, the most powerful king in the known world, is foiled by two women whose names the text preserves — Shiphrah and Puah — while Pharaoh himself remains anonymous throughout Exodus. This inversion is deliberate and sustained. The midwife scene in vv. 15–21 uses the rhetorical form of a disputation: Pharaoh's command, the midwives' disobedience, Pharaoh's interrogation, their sly reply, God's reward. Their answer in v. 19 — that Hebrew women are *ḥāyôt*, "lively/vigorous" (the word also means "wild animals") — may be a truth, a lie, or a sardonic flattery of Pharaoh's own bestial view of his slaves. Robert Alter notes that the women turn the oppressor's own contemptuous category for his slaves into the very thing that defeats him.

The chapter is also an inclusio. It opens with the sons of Jacob coming to Egypt (v. 1) and closes with the sons of Israel being thrown into the Nile (v. 22). The Nile, which in Egyptian mythology is the source of life, becomes the instrument of death. That inversion will be repaid exactly in chapter 7, when the Nile turns to blood, and in chapter 14, when the sea that was meant to swallow Israel swallows Pharaoh's army instead.

Textual variants matter here less than in later chapters, but one is worth noting: the Septuagint and the Samaritan Pentateuch both give seventy-five for the number of Jacob's descendants (v. 5), which is the number Stephen cites in Acts 7:14. The Masoretic Text reads seventy. Dead Sea Scroll fragment 4Q13 (4QExodb) confirms the seventy-five reading. This is not a contradiction so much as a live scribal tradition: the smaller number counts only direct descendants; the larger adds Joseph's grandsons.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Place Exodus 1 in the canonical arc and it becomes pivotal. It executes the transition from the patriarchal narratives — which are essentially family stories — to the national story of Israel. It also re-activates the oldest promise in Scripture: "be fruitful and multiply" (Gen 1:28, 9:1, 12:2, 17:2). God's word in Genesis is now working against the most powerful empire in the world, and it is winning without any explicit divine action yet. This sets up the pattern that will run through Scripture — from Ruth to Hannah to Elizabeth to Mary — of hidden, often feminine, agency advancing the redemptive story against imperial opposition.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory**

Second Temple writers preserved an intense memory of Egyptian bondage. The *Wisdom of Solomon* (first century BC), in chapters 17–19, reads the plagues as retributive justice fitted to the sin; the same chapter celebrates the midwives indirectly through praise of those who fear God under tyranny. Philo of Alexandria in *Life of Moses* 1.7–17 turned the midwives into paragons of philosophical courage, while Josephus (*Antiquities* 2.206) preserved a tradition that Pharaoh's fear was triggered by a prophecy from a scribe that a Hebrew deliverer would arise — a detail absent from the biblical text but present in the Targum Pseudo-Jonathan.

The rabbinic tradition, particularly *Exodus Rabbah* and *Sotah* 11a–12a, made Shiphrah and Puah central. The Talmud in *Sotah* 11b identifies them midrashically as Jochebed (Moses' mother) and Miriam (his sister), reading Shiphrah from *shafar* ("to make beautiful" — she beautified the newborn) and Puah from the soothing sound she made to calm crying infants. This identification was not universal; other rabbinic voices read them as Egyptian converts, making the chapter the Torah's first recorded story of righteous Gentiles. Rashi (eleventh century) follows the Jochebed-Miriam reading. Ibn Ezra (twelfth century) is more cautious, treating them as Hebrew women whose names are simply preserved. Maimonides in *Mishneh Torah* (*Hilchot Melakhim* 10:2) and the Ramban both cite the midwives as the Torah's foundational text for civil disobedience in the face of unjust law.

Modern Jewish readings have focused on the theology of the hidden God. Martin Buber, in *Moses* (1946), noted that God does not yet speak in Exodus 1; the chapter is the story of what humans do when God has not yet intervened. Jon Levenson in *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son* (1993) read the chapter as a reversal of the Akedah: where Abraham was asked to sacrifice his son and was stopped, Pharaoh demands that fathers sacrifice their sons and is not stopped by human law. Avivah Gottlieb Zornberg in *The Particulars of Rapture* (2001) developed the psychoanalytic reading: Pharaoh's fear of Israelite proliferation is the fear of the empire that cannot tolerate the existence of a people that is not fully accounted for in its ledgers.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory**

Patristic writers read Exodus 1 almost entirely typologically. Origen in *Homilies on Exodus* 1 treated Pharaoh as the devil, Egypt as the world, the hard labor as enslavement to sin, and the midwives as figures of the Church preserving spiritual life against the world's death-dealing. Augustine in *City of God* 16.43 used the multiplication of Israel under oppression as an emblem of the Church's flourishing under persecution. Jerome, more historically minded, worked out the chronology in his *Chronicon* and placed the Pharaoh of the oppression in the mid-fifteenth century.

Medieval Christian readings multiplied the allegorical layers. Bernard of Clairvaux preached on the midwives as types of spiritual directors who refuse to kill the nascent Christ-life in the soul. Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 104, a. 6) used the midwives' disobedience as the paradigm case for his treatment of when a subject may refuse a ruler's command — specifically, when the command violates divine law.

The Reformation brought the civil disobedience argument to political prominence. John Calvin's *Commentary on the Four Last Books of Moses* treats Shiphrah and Puah as examples of conscience binding above earthly authority, though he is uncomfortable with their apparent lie to Pharaoh and argues they told a partial truth. Luther in his lectures on Exodus was less troubled by the lie and more interested in the chapter's demonstration that God blesses the oppressed through apparently powerless instruments.

Critical scholarship from Julius Wellhausen through Martin Noth to Brevard Childs reorganized the discussion around sources and redaction, but Childs's *Book of Exodus* (1974) pulled the theological reading back toward the canonical form, arguing that the final editor wanted the midwife story to frame the whole book: human courage in the face of tyranny is a true form of God's action even when God does not speak. Contemporary readings by Walter Brueggemann (*The Prophetic Imagination*), Terence Fretheim (*Exodus*), and Ellen Davis have emphasized that Pharaoh's policy is the first biblical instance of state-sponsored genocide, and that the Church's political theology must reckon with a text that treats sustained civil disobedience as righteousness. N.T. Wright, who treats the exodus as the controlling paradigm of redemption across Scripture — the story Israel told to explain who its God was, and the story the New Testament writers say has been re-enacted and outstripped in Jesus — would read Exodus 1 as the necessary darkness before that paradigm begins: the empire's ledger-keeping and infanticide define the "Egypt" out of which every later rescue, climactically the cross and resurrection, is patterned.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge**

Jewish and Christian readings agree on the ethics: the midwives are heroes, civil disobedience to unjust law is a duty, God rewards those who fear God over kings. They diverge sharply on the identity of the midwives (rabbinic tradition often identifies them as Israelites, especially Jochebed and Miriam; patristic and medieval Christian tradition often types them to the Church), on the weight given to the lie (Reformation Protestants agonized over it, the rabbis mostly did not), and on whether the chapter is most fundamentally about the birth of a nation (Jewish) or a type of the Church's persecution (Christian). The critical tradition has pulled both away from allegory toward history, and recent narrative critics — Alter, Fretheim, Brueggemann — have insisted that the chapter's genuine theological center is in its smallest characters, the unnamed midwives, not in its anonymous king.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What is God doing in Exodus 1? The striking answer is that God does not speak, and yet everything that happens is the work of God. Israel multiplies because of the blessing in Genesis 1:28. The midwives fear God and refuse to kill. God "deals well" with them (v. 20) and gives them families. The action is visible only in its effects. The author seems to be arguing that divine faithfulness to the covenant does not wait for theophany; it operates through the fidelity of human actors who fear God more than they fear empire.

**Tension One: The hiddenness of God under oppression**

If God is sovereign, why does Israel suffer four hundred years of slavery before God intervenes? Exodus 1 does not answer. It simply shows us a God whose action is indirect, mediated through demographics and courage, until the burning bush.

- **Catholic teaching** (*CCC* 2414, *Gaudium et Spes* 27) treats slavery as a grave evil violating human dignity, but also frames divine permission of suffering through the doctrine of God's permissive will and the ultimate good brought out of evil (Augustine's *felix culpa* logic extended to history). John Paul II in *Sollicitudo Rei Socialis* argued that the long wait of Exodus is itself part of God's pedagogy of liberation.
- **Reformed** (Calvin, John Frame, Michael Horton) emphasize God's hidden decree and the mystery of providence — God has reasons inaccessible to creatures, and the appropriate response is not complaint but trust. Calvin on Exodus 1 is striking in his assertion that the time of oppression was itself God's instrument for forming Israel into a people.
- **Wesleyan/Arminian** (Thomas Oden, Randy Maddox) read the delay as accommodation to human freedom: God works within history without overriding Egyptian agency, and the long oppression is the price of genuine secondary causation.
- **Anabaptist/peace church** readings (John Howard Yoder, *The Politics of Jesus*) argue that the midwives' nonviolent resistance is already God's action — there is no gap to fill; the chapter's plain sense is that God is already present in the fidelity of the oppressed, so the theological problem is less God's hiddenness than any theology that uses it to justify quiescence.
- **Jewish** readings — Levenson, Zornberg, Arthur Green — often frame this as the most honest text in Scripture about divine concealment, and the Holocaust has intensified rather than resolved the question.

*The least intuitive position, steelmanned:* The Reformed emphasis on hidden decree is the position a modern reader is most likely to find offensive. Its best form is this: if the text is true that God is sovereign, then the four hundred years of slavery are not a theological embarrassment that requires explanation; they are the form in which Israel's identity is forged. The nation that emerges from Egypt is the nation that remembers Egypt. A shorter oppression might have produced a smaller people with a thinner memory. This is not a sufficient theodicy, but it is a serious claim about what it takes to make a covenant community.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter refuses to explain the wait. It simply shows that fidelity is possible within it. A reader of the text who demands an answer before continuing will stop at verse 22 forever.

**Tension Two: The ethics of the lie**

The midwives deceive Pharaoh (v. 19), and God blesses them (v. 20–21). This is a scandal for any ethical system that treats truthfulness as absolute.

- **Catholic** (Aquinas *ST* II-II q. 110, following Augustine's *On Lying*) held lying to be intrinsically evil, and therefore read the midwives' words as a partial truth or as God rewarding their *fear of God* despite a lesser sin.
- **Reformed** (Calvin, Charles Hodge, John Murray in *Principles of Conduct*) split: some followed Augustine, others argued that in a state of war (and Pharaoh's decree is an act of war against Israel), the normal duty of truthfulness does not obtain.
- **Lutheran** (Bonhoeffer in *Ethics*) distinguished between "the cynical truth-teller" who reveals everything to evil power and "the living word" which serves the truth by protecting the innocent. The midwives are his paradigm case.
- **Jewish ethics** (Rashi, Rambam, contemporary halakhic writers like Rabbi Joseph Telushkin) generally permit the *lie of preservation* — deception that saves innocent life from an unjust killer — and the midwives are often the proof text.
- **Anabaptist/pacifist** traditions have historically held the strictest line against lying even to preserve life, creating sustained internal discussion around this chapter.

*The least intuitive position, steelmanned:* Augustine's absolute prohibition of lying is the position contemporary readers find hardest to accept. Its best form is this: if truthfulness is a habit of the soul rather than a tactic, then permitting lies in extreme circumstances corrodes the soul's capacity for truthfulness in ordinary circumstances. The midwives, on this reading, are not justified by their lie but blessed despite it — their blessing is for the fear of God, not the deception. This preserves a higher view of truth, at the cost of making the chapter awkward.

**Where this leaves us:** The text rewards the midwives without moralizing. Readers who want a cleaner ethics must decide which they want more: a coherent theory of truthfulness, or the actual judgment of the text.

**One unresolved question:** The midwives were women whose names the text preserves when Pharaoh's is erased, and whose fear of God became the first crack in an empire that could not be toppled by armies. Which empire in your life depends on your silence, and whose names would be remembered if you broke it?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 1:28 / 9:1** — "Be fruitful and multiply." Exodus 1:7 is the fulfillment of the creation blessing under conditions that should prevent it. Hold this pairing: the blessing is not passive.
- **1 Samuel 2:1–10** — Hannah's song. When you get there, notice that another woman, another mother, announces the reversal of the mighty and the lifting up of the poor. Exodus 1 is the template; Hannah is the reprise; Mary's *Magnificat* (Luke 1:46–55) is the third panel.
- **Matthew 2:13–18** — Another tyrant, another decree to kill male children, another escape by night. Matthew is deliberately writing Jesus' infancy over Moses' infancy. Pharaoh's fear has a long shadow.
- **Revelation 12:1–6** — The dragon waits to devour the child of the woman. Exodus 1 is already apocalyptic; Revelation is already Exodus. When you arrive at Revelation, remember the Nile and the midwives: this pattern has a name.
