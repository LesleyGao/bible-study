# Job 35: The Heavens Higher Than You

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Job 35 is the shortest of Elihu's four discourses — sixteen verses in contrast to chapter 34's thirty-seven and chapter 36's thirty-three — but it introduces the most philosophically ambitious claim in the Elihu cycle: that human action does not truly reach God. The setting remains the land of Uz, traditionally placed in Edomite or northern Arabian territory. The addressees have shifted subtly: Elihu now includes "your friends" (v. 4, רֵעֶיךָ, *re'ekha*) alongside Job, suggesting a broadening of his critique beyond the single sufferer to the entire disputation. The social world presupposed is that of a wisdom tribunal — older men seated, younger men standing until invited — which Job 32's introduction had explicitly framed.

Dating the Elihu material remains contested. Marvin Pope placed the chapters firmly in the post-exilic period on linguistic grounds, noting Aramaized vocabulary and syntactic patterns alien to pre-exilic Hebrew. David Clines in the Word Biblical Commentary agrees with a late date, probably fifth or fourth century BCE, while reading the speeches as a literary expansion rather than an editorial misstep. Carol Newsom in *The Book of Job: A Contest of Moral Imaginations* situates the Elihu speeches in a Persian-period milieu where Babylonian wisdom traditions had been absorbed into a more schematic theology of divine pedagogy. Against these, Robert Gordis defended Elihu's originality as pre-exilic, arguing the linguistic peculiarities are characterization rather than chronology. Francis Andersen in the Tyndale commentary treats chapter 35 as integral to the book, noting that its argument is necessary to prepare the ground for chapters 36–37.

The argument of chapter 35 — that the gods are so transcendent that human deeds neither benefit nor harm them — has striking parallels in Mesopotamian wisdom literature. The *Babylonian Theodicy* (roughly 1000 BCE) contains a similar line of reasoning from the friend: the gods' ways are far removed, and their response to human action is neither predictable nor proportionate. The *Ludlul Bēl Nēmeqi* likewise acknowledges the distance between divine intention and human comprehension. The Akkadian *Dialogue of Pessimism* pushes the idea to satirical extremes. Elihu's version is more restrained and more systematic — closer in tone to the Akkadian *Counsels of Wisdom* — but the underlying metaphysical move is shared across the region. The Qumran 11QTargum Job (11Q10) contains fragmentary coverage of this section; what survives shows an Aramaic paraphrase that softens some of Elihu's sharper claims, perhaps out of theological discomfort. The Septuagint is notably shorter throughout Job but preserves chapter 35 with relatively few omissions.

Five Hebrew terms carry the weight of the chapter. First, גָּבַהּ (*gavah*), "to be high," underlies v. 5's visual argument ("look at the heavens, and see; and behold the clouds, which are higher than you") — the root names both physical elevation and ontological transcendence. Second, פָּעַל (*pa'al*), "to do" or "accomplish," appears in v. 6 ("what do you accomplish against him?") and governs the chapter's question of whether human agency reaches God at all. Third, שָׁוְא (*shav*), "empty" or "vain," appears in v. 13 ("God does not hear an empty cry") — the same root that anchors the commandment against taking God's name in vain (Exodus 20:7), and that Elihu now applies to Job's protest. Fourth, זְמִרוֹת (*zemirot*), "songs" (v. 10, "songs in the night"), is a rare plural with liturgical resonance, used elsewhere in Psalms for hymns of confidence. Fifth, גַּאֲוָה (*ga'avah*), "pride" (v. 12), names the posture Elihu believes blocks the cry from reaching God — the same noun used in Proverbs for the pride that precedes a fall.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 35 displays a tight three-part structure. Verses 1–8 argue from transcendence: the heavens are higher than humans, so human action cannot touch God; sin and righteousness affect fellow humans, not the deity. Verses 9–13 argue from pedagogical silence: the oppressed cry, but their cry is "empty" because they do not cry TO God, they cry merely because of circumstance. Verses 14–16 return to Job with a stinging application: Job's insistence on waiting for God's judgment is itself a species of empty speech.

The literary-critical questions surrounding the Elihu cycle bear with particular force on this chapter. Pope catalogued multiple Aramaisms in the sixteen verses, including the rare construction in v. 10 and several lexical items paralleled only in late biblical Aramaic. Clines (WBC) notes that the chapter's argument is philosophically more sophisticated than anything the three friends offered, which has been used both as evidence of later composition (a more developed theology reflects a later period) and as evidence of originality (the book needs this voice to reach its full argumentative scope). Claus Westermann in *The Structure of the Book of Job* saw chapter 35 as an interpolation that introduced Hellenistic-style divine apatheia into what had been a more covenantal dispute. Newsom reads the chapter as a deliberate dialogical contribution — the voice of a theology that treats suffering as pedagogy, inserted into the book precisely because the book wants to hold this voice up for examination.

Defenders of originality have focused on chapter 35's tight integration with the surrounding Elihu material. Gordis notes that the argument in v. 6–8 logically completes chapter 34's claim about divine perfection — if God is perfect and sovereign, then human action cannot augment or diminish God, and the question of retributive justice must be reframed. Robert Fyall in *Now My Eyes Have Seen You* argues that chapter 35 is essential to Elihu's literary function as a bridge to Yahweh's theophany: by radicalizing divine transcendence in this chapter, Elihu sets up the theological altitude from which Yahweh will speak in chapter 38. Andersen points out that v. 10 ("songs in the night") is one of the book's most lyrical lines and fits poorly with a clumsy interpolator.

The distinctive vocabulary of Elihu continues here. Verse 11's verb פַּלֵּף (*pallep*, "teaches us more than"), if that is the correct reading, is a hapax legomenon whose precise meaning has been debated since the medieval grammarians; the LXX translator clearly struggled with it, offering a reading that diverges from the MT. The syntactic construction in v. 15 is notoriously obscure — Clines translates it with bracketed conjectures, and the ancient versions diverge. These difficulties concentrate, as they did in chapter 34, precisely where Elihu's theology is most radical.

The chapter contains one of the most beautiful lines in the Elihu cycle: "Where is God my Maker, who gives songs in the night" (v. 10). Even readers most suspicious of Elihu have acknowledged the poetic quality here. The line may echo or be echoed by Psalm 42:8 ("his song is with me in the night") and Psalm 77:6. Whether Elihu composes this line ironically — to show what the oppressed fail to say — or sincerely, as a moment of theological insight that breaks through his own argument, is one of the chapter's interpretive cruxes.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Canonically, Job 35 takes its place within a wisdom meditation that includes Psalm 73, Ecclesiastes, Proverbs 30:1–9, and the theodicy passages of Jeremiah (12:1–4) and Habakkuk. Elihu's argument — that God's transcendence renders human action ontologically negligible — stands in productive tension with Deuteronomy's covenantal framework, where human action decisively matters to God, and with the prophetic tradition, where God hears the cry of the oppressed (Exodus 3:7, Isaiah 3:15). Chapter 35's claim in v. 9–13 that the oppressed are not heard because they do not cry TO God directly inverts the Exodus pattern, where God hears before being named.

The Jewish trajectory on this chapter is especially rich. The Babylonian Talmud's discussion of Job in Bava Batra 15a–16b — whether Job existed, when he lived, who wrote the book — does not address chapter 35 specifically but sets the frame within which medieval readings developed. Rashi treats Elihu's words in chapter 35 largely as correction of Job's excess, though with less unqualified endorsement than he gave earlier Elihu speeches. Ibn Ezra noted linguistic oddities and offered alternative construals for the obscure verses. The pivotal reception comes from Maimonides in *Guide of the Perplexed* III.22–23, where the argument of chapter 35 becomes central to his own account of providence. For Maimonides, Elihu's claim that human action does not reach God must be understood through the doctrine of negative attributes: one cannot predicate benefit or harm of God univocally. Maimonides reads Elihu as correcting the anthropomorphism latent in both Job's protest and the friends' retribution scheme — Job imagines God as one who can be wronged, the friends imagine God as one who can be appeased, and Elihu alone grasps that neither is metaphysically accurate. Nachmanides engages the chapter more cautiously, preserving a stronger sense of covenantal reciprocity. Martin Buber, in *The Prophetic Faith*, treats Elihu's transcendence argument as the dangerous end-point of theological abstraction — the moment when God becomes too removed to be Israel's God. Jon Levenson in *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* develops this critique: Elihu's God, for whom human action makes no difference, is not the God of the covenant but a Hellenistic Unmoved Mover. James Kugel reads Elihu's "songs in the night" as one of the book's genuine moments of religious insight, accidentally authentic within a larger misframing. Avivah Zornberg, characteristically, presses the psychological dimension — Elihu's speech in chapter 35 is what the young do when confronted with suffering they cannot help.

The Christian trajectory runs along similar fault lines. Gregory the Great devotes significant space in the *Moralia in Job* to chapter 35, reading Elihu's words as mixture of truth and pride: the claim that God is transcendent is true; the claim that the oppressed cry emptily is dangerously close to despising them. Gregory's interpretation — that Elihu grasps the doctrine but wields it poorly — has shaped Latin Christian reading for over a millennium. Thomas Aquinas in *Expositio super Iob* defends chapter 35 as a genuine theological contribution: God is not moved by human action in the way that creatures are moved, and this is part of the orthodox doctrine of divine impassibility. John Calvin, in his Job sermons (1554–1555), embraces chapter 35 as a sound meditation on divine sovereignty; Calvin's own theology of providence owes much to Elihu, filtered through Augustine. Martin Luther was more ambivalent, worried that Elihu's transcendence risked abstracting God from the covenant. Nineteenth-century critical scholars (Wellhausen, Gunkel) treated the chapter as inauthentic and therefore theologically non-normative. Gerhard von Rad in *Wisdom in Israel* saw chapter 35 as characteristic of a sapiential turn toward generalized divine sovereignty at the expense of covenantal specificity. Clines in WBC reads the chapter as literarily coherent but theologically dubious. Carol Newsom highlights the dialogic function — the chapter gives voice to a theology the book itself wants to interrogate. Robert Gordis presses hard on v. 9–13: Elihu's claim that the oppressed cry emptily reframes a wail of pain as a failure of piety, and the book — which everywhere else assumes God hears the afflicted — does not endorse it. Norman Habel (OTL), John Hartley (NICOT), Ellen Davis, Susannah Ticciati, and Andersen all offer more integrative readings, each trying to preserve the chapter's theological seriousness without endorsing every Elihu claim.

The divide is most visible here between Maimonides and Gregory. Maimonides treats chapter 35 as Elihu's sharpest insight — the place where the book achieves metaphysical precision. Gregory treats it as Elihu's most dangerous overreach — where a young man uses a true doctrine to silence a suffering one. Both readings are exegetically defensible from the text. What separates them is whether one gives hermeneutic priority to the theological content of Elihu's claims or to their rhetorical function within a story about a sufferer who is later vindicated.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

### Tension 1: Does God hear the cry of the oppressed?

Verses 9–13 present Elihu's most startling argument: the oppressed cry, but God does not answer, because they cry only out of pain, not in genuine appeal to God. This collides head-on with the Exodus narrative, where God hears Israel's groaning before they invoke the divine name (Exodus 2:23–25).

- **Elihu's position: God hears only the cry that addresses him.** The oppressed in v. 9 cry out of the "multitude of oppressions," but they do not say "Where is God my Maker." Elihu's point is pedagogical — suffering becomes revelatory only when it is directed Godward. The cry that treats God as mere relief-provider misses the theological point of suffering. Maimonides, Aquinas, Calvin, Andersen, and Gordis largely defend this reading as a sophisticated distinction between reflex pain and prayer.

- **The covenantal counter-position: God hears the cry regardless of its theological quality.** The Exodus pattern and the Psalms of lament suggest that God responds to suffering itself, not to the correctness of its articulation. Gordis presses this hard — the cry of the afflicted does not need to be liturgically proper to be heard. Levenson and Buber align with this critique: the God of the covenant is not a pedagogue requiring correctly framed appeals.

- **Newsom / Zornberg (steelman of the least-intuitive position): Elihu's claim is *psychologically accurate but theologically mistimed*.** The steelman of Elihu's view: there is something true in his distinction. A cry that assumes God is merely instrumental — the vending-machine deity who dispenses relief on command — does reduce God to a utility. Elihu's error is not the theological point but its application. Spoken to a sufferer, the point becomes cruel; spoken in catechesis, it might be useful. Newsom's dialogic reading suggests the book preserves the speech precisely to show that right doctrine at the wrong moment becomes its own distortion. Zornberg develops this: Elihu's speech reveals how theology can function as a defense against the disturbance of another's pain.

### Tension 2: Does human action make any difference to God?

Verses 6–8 state the stark claim: "If you have sinned, what do you accomplish against him? And if your transgressions are multiplied, what do you do to him? If you are righteous, what do you give to him?" This is the argument from divine transcendence to human ontological negligibility.

- **Classical transcendence reading (Maimonides, Aquinas, Calvin): Elihu is correct.** God is not augmented by our righteousness or diminished by our sin. Divine impassibility is a sound doctrine. Human action matters horizontally (v. 8: "your wickedness concerns a man like yourself") but not vertically in the sense of changing God.

- **Covenantal counter-position (Levenson, Buber, Gordis): Elihu is wrong in a way that matters.** The biblical God grieves over human sin (Genesis 6:6), delights in human faithfulness (Zephaniah 3:17), and binds himself covenantally to a people whose action genuinely affects the divine commitment. Elihu's Hellenistic-flavored impassibility strips God of covenantal specificity. The whole prophetic tradition assumes human action matters to God.

- **Clines / Ticciati (steelman of the least-intuitive position): *Elihu is saying something true in a way that functions as false*.** The steelman: Elihu is right that God is not wounded by sin in the way creatures are wounded, and not enriched by righteousness as creatures are enriched. But he states this truth in a form that implies human action is ontologically negligible, which is not what classical impassibility actually entails. Ticciati develops the point through a reading of Aquinas: God's relation to creatures is real from the creature's side and only "logical" from God's side, but this does not mean human action is irrelevant — it means the relevance is differently structured. Elihu collapses a sophisticated distinction into a blunt claim, and the book preserves his blunt form so the reader can see the difference between metaphysical precision and rhetorical weaponization.

Where this leaves us: Chapter 35 asks whether the God of the philosophers can also be the God of the sufferer. One unresolved question: if "songs in the night" are the sign that suffering has become prayer, what do we do with the sufferer who cannot yet sing?

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references. First, Psalm 42:8 ("by day the LORD commands his steadfast love, and at night his song is with me") — the phrase "song in the night" may be picked up from Elihu or behind him, and the Psalmist speaks the line from within precisely the kind of oppression Elihu claims cannot pray. Second, Exodus 2:23–25, where God hears Israel's groaning without any indication that the cry is properly addressed — the foundational Israelite pattern Elihu implicitly denies. Third, Romans 8:26, "the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words" — Paul's claim that the Spirit mediates a cry the sufferer cannot articulate is a structural inversion of Elihu's position: where Elihu says the empty cry is not heard, Paul says the cry that cannot be spoken is heard through the Spirit's own interceding. The canonical trajectory bends decisively away from Elihu.
