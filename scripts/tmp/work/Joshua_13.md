# Joshua 13: The Admission and the Allotment

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Joshua 13 opens with a sentence that undoes the totalizing claim of chapter 11 and the archival closure of chapter 12: "You are old and advanced in years, and there remains yet very much land to possess" (13:1). After a chapter that counted thirty-one defeated kings, the land-giver Himself concedes that the conquest is incomplete. The chapter then hinges from conquest to distribution, cataloguing first what remains unconquered (vv. 2–6), then recapping the Transjordanian allotments Moses had already made to Reuben, Gad, and half-Manasseh (vv. 8–33). It is the structural turn of the book.

The unconquered territory named in verses 2–6 is politically precise. The Philistine pentapolis — Gaza, Ashdod, Ashkelon, Gath, and Ekron (13:3) — is the first named item. Philistine material culture is among the best-attested in Levantine archaeology. Distinctive Philistine bichrome pottery (Aegean-derived, with red and black painted decoration on cream slip) is documented at Tel Miqne (Ekron), Ashkelon, Ashdod, and Tell es-Safi (Gath) from roughly 1180 BCE onward — precisely when Ramses III's reliefs at Medinet Habu (c. 1175 BCE) depict the Egyptian defeat of the Sea Peoples, including the *prst* (Peleset, = Philistines). Excavations at Ekron under Trude Dothan and Seymour Gitin, at Ashkelon under Lawrence Stager, and at Gath under Aren Maeir have established the chronology firmly. This creates a dating problem the chapter's readers must face: if the conquest occurred c. 1406 BCE (high/early date based on 1 Kgs 6:1's 480 years), there were no Philistines at Gaza, Ashdod, Ashkelon, Gath, or Ekron to be unconquered. If c. 1200–1150 BCE (low/late date based on archaeology of destruction layers), the pentapolis was just taking shape and the mention is contemporary. Most scholars now favor the late date; the high-date defenders (Bryant Wood, Eugene Merrill) typically argue the ethnonym "Philistines" in this text is anachronistic updating by a later editor, a point the text would permit if not require.

The "five rulers of the Philistines" in 13:3 uses the Hebrew *sarnê pĕlištîm* (סַרְנֵי פְלִשְׁתִּים) — a technical term. *Seren* (סֶרֶן) is not the normal Hebrew word for "ruler" (*śar*, *melek*, *nāśîʾ*). It appears only for Philistine leaders and likely preserves an Aegean loanword, possibly related to Greek *týrannos* (tyrant), with the Luwian/Anatolian *tarwanis* sometimes proposed as intermediary. The word itself registers that Philistines are not Canaanites but a foreign element with distinct political vocabulary — a lexical marker of the Sea Peoples' Aegean origin that modern archaeology confirms.

Other unconquered territories listed: the *Geshurites* (13:2, 11, 13), a small Aramean kingdom in the northern Transjordan (Bashan region), archaeologically associated with Et-Tell (Bethsaida) excavations under Rami Arav and the wider Golan region; later David marries Maacah of Geshur (2 Sam 3:3), the mother of Absalom. The *Maacathites* (13:11, 13) are similar. The *Sidonians* (13:4, 6) — Phoenician Sidon, active as a major trading power through the Late Bronze and Iron Ages, with archaeological work at Sidon itself (British Museum under Claude Doumet-Serhal) confirming continuous LB–Iron occupation. The *Gebalites* (13:5) from Byblos — one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world, with the Ahiram sarcophagus (c. 1000 BCE) preserving one of the earliest alphabetic inscriptions.

The Transjordanian allotments of 13:8–33 recapitulate Numbers 32 with archaeological and geographical detail. Aroer (13:9, 16) has been excavated at Khirbet Arair on the edge of the Arnon (Wadi Mujib) gorge — a spectacularly sited fortress with Iron Age occupation confirmed. Heshbon (Tell Hisban), Dibon (Dhiban, with the Mesha Stele), and Medeba (Madaba, with the famous 6th c. CE mosaic map) are all archaeologically attested though with the dating issues discussed in the Joshua 12 study. Mahanaim (Tell edh-Dhahab el-Gharbi) and Succoth (Tell Deir ʿAllā) anchor the Gadite allotment.

Several Hebrew terms organize the chapter. *Naḥălâ* (נַחֲלָה, "inheritance," appearing more than a dozen times in the chapter — 13:6, 7, 8, 14, 23, 28, 32, 33) is the organizing theological category. It is not ordinary property but covenantally granted patrimony; English "inheritance" captures the legal sense but flattens the theological — this is land-as-gift that cannot be alienated (cf. 1 Kgs 21, Naboth's vineyard). *Yāraš* (יָרַשׁ, "to possess, dispossess," 13:1, 6, 13) is the verb of taking-and-holding — the imperative "to possess" in 13:1 is striking given that the subject being addressed is told he is too old to do it. *ʾIššê YHWH* (אִשֵּׁי יְהוָה, "the offerings by fire of YHWH," 13:14) is Levitical sacrificial vocabulary — the Levites' "inheritance" is the sanctuary service itself. *ʿad hayyôm hazzeh* ("to this day," 13:13) is the Deuteronomistic historian's signature formula, naming the gap between the narrative moment and the composition moment and implicitly dating the text later than the events described.

Authorship is standard DtrH (Noth 1943; Cross 1973 dual redaction). The Transjordan allotment lists in particular look to Alt and Noth like older administrative documents absorbed into the Dtr framework. Cross and Wright argued that the Judahite district list (which surfaces most clearly in Josh 15) reflects Josianic reorganization; the Transjordan list here may be genuinely earlier.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre shifts midway through the book. Joshua 1–12 is conquest narrative; Joshua 13–21 is allotment catalogue; Joshua 22–24 is covenant renewal. Chapter 13 straddles the first seam. Verses 1–7 are direct divine speech that recalibrates the book's project; verses 8–33 open the distribution catalogue with the Transjordan recap.

The literary structure is deliberate. Verse 1 is the pivot sentence — "Joshua was old and advanced in years" repeats with "you are old and advanced in years," the doubling an emphatic narrative marker that the story has reached a new phase. The inventory of unconquered land (vv. 2–6) is formally the inverse of chapter 12's inventory of conquered kings: one counts what has been done, the other names what has not. Together they form a literary diptych. Hawk (*Every Promise Fulfilled*, 1991; *Joshua*, Berit Olam 2000) reads this as the book's most honest moment — the narrative will not allow the triumphalist closure of chapter 11 and the archival completion of chapter 12 to stand unqualified.

The Transjordan recap in verses 8–33 uses the list-form characteristic of administrative catalogues. Each tribe's allotment follows a pattern: "Moses gave an inheritance to [tribe] according to their clans" → territorial boundaries and cities → concluding formula ("this was the inheritance of [tribe] according to their clans with their cities and villages"). Reuben (13:15–23), Gad (13:24–28), and half-Manasseh (13:29–31) each receive the treatment in turn. The formulaic repetition reinforces that each tribe is the land's legitimate holder on identical legal grounds.

Two literary peculiarities reward attention. First, Balaam ben Beor appears in verse 22 — "Balaam also, the son of Beor, the one who practiced divination, was killed with the sword by the people of Israel among the rest of their slain." This brief mention gestures back to Numbers 22–24 (Balaam's oracles of blessing over Israel) and Numbers 31:8 (his death in the Midianite war) and forward to Deuteronomy 23:4–5, Joshua 24:9–10, Nehemiah 13:2, Micah 6:5, and 2 Peter 2:15 / Jude 11 / Revelation 2:14 (the New Testament's "Balaam" as paradigm of prophetic corruption). The Tell Deir ʿAllā plaster inscriptions (discovered 1967, dated c. 800 BCE) preserve an Aramaic text about "Balaam son of Beor, seer of the gods" — external evidence that Balaam was an actual ANE religious figure remembered outside Israel. The brief mention in Josh 13:22 packs enormous intertextual density into a single clause.

Second, the Levitical non-allotment appears twice — verses 14 and 33 — bracketing the Transjordan material. This inclusion is a literary signature marking the Levitical exception as a recurring theological point: YHWH is their inheritance. The doubled statement is not accidental redundancy but structural.

Textual variants: the LXX of Joshua 13 is close to MT in the main but has some different place-names and a slightly different ordering of the Gadite cities. The LXX's tendency toward brevity in Joshua is less pronounced here than elsewhere. No major Qumran manuscript preserves substantial portions of chapter 13 that would resolve the variants.

The chapter's location in the book's argument is explicit: it is the necessary bridge. Without verses 1–7's admission, the book's move from conquest to distribution would be jarring; without the Transjordan recap, the eastern tribes' status would remain uncertain. The chapter does the work the narrative needs.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, Joshua 13 is the book's honest middle. The land-promise trajectory that began with Abraham (Gen 12; 15) and was concretized in Moses' Transjordan allotments (Num 32; Deut 3) now becomes the program for distribution on the western side. But the unconquered-land list forecasts Judges, the failures of the judges' period, and ultimately the monarchy's and exile's land-losses. The book of Joshua is never triumphalist unambiguously; chapter 13 is the proof.

**Jewish reading trajectory.** Second Temple sources give the chapter limited sustained treatment, though the Balaam mention in 13:22 becomes a focal point. Philo (*Life of Moses* 1.48–55) handles Balaam as the type of the corrupt prophet who sells his gift. Josephus (*Antiquities* 4.102–158, 5.89) treats Balaam in detail. The Qumran *Apocryphon of Joshua* (4Q378–379) has no clear engagement with chapter 13 as such.

Rabbinic tradition treats the Levitical non-inheritance (13:14, 33) as foundational for priestly theology. *Mishnah Bikkurim* and *Mishnah Maʿaser Sheni* develop the halakhah of priestly portions — the *ʾiššê YHWH* language becomes the source-text for why the tribe of Levi eats from the altar. *Sanhedrin* 105a-106a treats Balaam extensively as the archetype of the evil prophet. The Talmud (*Baba Bathra* 14b–15a) discusses who wrote the book of Joshua (Joshua himself, with the last verses added by Eleazar and Phinehas), with Joshua 13's admission of incompleteness treated as authentic to the Joshuan-period composition. Rashi on 13:1 takes the "you are old" address as a gentle reminder that the allotment must not wait — a practical reading rather than a theological one. Ibn Ezra on 13:3 is philologically careful about *seren* as Philistine-specific vocabulary, noting its oddity. Ramban on 13:6 emphasizes divine promise — "I myself will drive them out" — as the continuing guarantee that human failure to complete does not void God's project. Radak provides geographical precision throughout. Abarbanel reads the chapter politically, noting that incomplete conquest became Israel's recurring strategic problem.

Maimonides, in *Hilkhot Shmita V'Yovel* and *Hilkhot Bikkurim*, codifies how Joshua's territorial allotments define the land's halakhic status — which zones bear sabbatical, jubilee, first-fruits, and tithing obligations. The chapter's boundaries remain legally load-bearing in rabbinic thought, which is one reason the precision of verses 8–33 matters halakhically and not only theologically.

Modern Jewish readings: Moshe Weinfeld (*Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School*; *The Promise of the Land*, 1993) treats the land-promise theology here in full detail. Jon Levenson on land and covenant. Nahum Sarna on the pentateuchal background to the Transjordan settlements. Rachel Havrelock's *River Jordan* (2011) foregrounds the Jordan boundary theology of verses 8–33 — that the Transjordanian tribes complicate any simple identification of Israel with the Cisjordan. Baruch Halpern on the list's source-critical layers.

**Christian reading trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Joshua* (Hom. 16 on chapter 13) reads the unconquered land allegorically as the remaining passions the soul has yet to mortify and reads the Levitical non-inheritance as the typological foreshadowing of the Christian clergy whose inheritance is Christ, not property — a reading that becomes foundational for later Catholic and Orthodox theologies of clerical poverty and the evangelical counsels. Jerome transmits and extends. Augustine (*Questions on Joshua* 17) treats the Levitical portion allegorically along similar lines; the Levites typify those who have no portion on earth because their portion is God.

Chrysostom preaches on the chapter's honesty — Joshua is not afraid to say the conquest is incomplete, which is itself a theological posture the Christian should imitate: not false triumphalism but acknowledgment of what remains. Theodoret of Cyrus offers more grammatical treatment.

Medieval Christian reading carried the allegorical tradition through the *Glossa Ordinaria*. Aquinas (*ST* II-II, Q. 185 on religious life) uses the Levitical landlessness as a scriptural foundation for the evangelical counsel of poverty. Luther and Calvin both move from allegory to more typological reading — Luther on ministry in a fallen world (the pastor "possesses" only the gospel); Calvin's commentary treats the chapter's admission of incompleteness as a pastorally useful reminder that God's work continues beyond any one generation's faithfulness. The Anabaptist tradition (Menno Simons) takes the Levitical non-inheritance as the template for the believing church — a people without a land, whose citizenship is in heaven (Phil 3:20).

Critical scholarship: Noth saw the Transjordan lists as older administrative material; Alt agreed. Cross and Wright argued the Cisjordan allotment lists (emerging in chapters 15 and following) reflect Josianic administrative reorganization. Richard Nelson's *Joshua* commentary treats chapter 13 as Dtr programmatic statement. Thomas Dozeman's *Joshua 1–12* (2015, followed by forthcoming volume covering 13–24) and Trent Butler's *Joshua* (Word, rev. 2014) both give substantial treatment. John Goldingay on land theology. Richard Hess, David Howard, Gordon Wenham as conservative-evangelical readers. Walter Brueggemann's *The Land* (1977, 2002) is the programmatic modern study — Joshua 13's tension between gift and unfulfilled task anticipates the entire prophetic and exilic struggle.

Christopher Wright's *The Mission of God* (2006) reads the unconquered territory as the continuing scope of divine mission rather than as military program. Terence Fretheim on the theological complexity of divine promise and human failure. Ellen Davis on the land as agrarian reality. Daniel Hawk's *Joshua* (Berit Olam 2000) and *Joshua in 3-D* (2010) treat the chapter as the narrative's most honest moment.

A reception-historical note belongs here as well: the unconquered territories of verses 2–6 are peoples who remain under their own sovereignty even within the conquest narrative, and the allotment language of verses 8–33 was later transposed onto colonial land-division schemes in ways the text's own honesty about incompleteness should chasten. Lori Rowlett (*Joshua and the Rhetoric of Violence*, 1996) treats the ideological functions of the allotment lists.

Convergence and divergence: Jewish and Christian traditions converge on reading the Levitical non-inheritance (13:14, 33) as theologically weighty — a priesthood whose "portion" is God. They converge on the chapter's honesty about incompleteness as theologically intentional. They diverge over whether the Balaam mention (13:22) is primarily halakhic/moral (rabbinic) or typological of false prophecy (Christian, especially New Testament reception). They diverge over the ongoing political claim the allotment lists support — modern Jewish readings range from maximalist (religious Zionist) to non-territorial (diaspora); modern Christian readings from dispensationalist mapping to a wholly typological non-territorial reading.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Promise and incompleteness (13:1, 6–7).** YHWH says both that much land remains unpossessed and that He Himself will drive out the inhabitants — and then commands Joshua to allot the land anyway, including what is not yet possessed.

The Reformed position (Calvin, Hess, Howard) reads this as divine pedagogy about the nature of promise. The allotment of unpossessed land is not empty gesture but a performative act that defines the scope of the promise — the tribes are assigned what God will give even before they have it. Faith holds title before possession. Where this leaves us: a theology in which the church likewise holds promised inheritance not yet possessed, and in which the distance between promise and possession is the space faith operates.

The Catholic position (CCC §121–123 on the Old Testament's permanent value; §706 on the promises) takes a similar line with more emphasis on the promise's continuing eschatological horizon. The unconquered land anticipates the already-not-yet of the kingdom. The allotment of what remains unpossessed is a prophetic act. Where this leaves us: land theology is typological of the wider promise, and neither dispensational mapping nor allegorical evaporation captures it.

The Wesleyan-holiness reading takes the "you are old" acknowledgment as honest pastoral realism. Joshua cannot finish the work; no one generation can. The tribes' partial possession is the normative condition of redeemed life — the called-out people always face territory still to be claimed. Where this leaves us: a theology of ongoing sanctification in which completion is eschatological, not biographical.

The critical-historicist reading (Nelson, Dozeman, Collins) steelmanned: the tension is not theological wisdom but compositional evidence. The book's Josianic or exilic editor needs to account for geographic realities — there really were Philistines and Phoenicians outside Israelite control — while also asserting divine faithfulness. The solution is theological: *God* will drive them out later. The tension is real because the composition is honest about its ideological needs, and the reader can see the seams. Where this leaves us: even on a critical reading the chapter's theological claim has integrity — the text does not pretend the conquest is complete, and the strategy of deferring completeness to continuing divine action is one the text itself authorizes.

The Jewish rabbinic reading (Maimonides, Ramban, Abarbanel) treats the promise as genuine and the completion as deferred by Israel's own unfaithfulness (Judges makes this explicit). The allotment is juridically valid; Israel's failure to take what has been given is the moral problem the subsequent narrative will address. Where this leaves us: promise is not voided by incomplete possession, and the remedy lies in teshuvah — returning to covenant obedience.

**Tension 2: Levitical inheritance (13:14, 33) — YHWH as inheritance means what, exactly?** The chapter twice stipulates that Levi has no land-portion because "the offerings by fire to the LORD God of Israel are their inheritance" and "the LORD God of Israel is their inheritance."

The Catholic position (Aquinas on the evangelical counsels; CCC §914–945 on consecrated life) reads the Levitical portion as scriptural foundation for a spiritual economics: some are called to hold their inheritance in God rather than in property, and the church's monastics and clergy live this vocation. The Levite is typological of the consecrated religious. Where this leaves us: a theology of vocation in which not every vocation is landed, and material portion is not the measure of covenant inclusion.

The Reformed and Lutheran position reads the Levitical non-inheritance christologically. Christ the true Priest receives no earthly portion (cf. Luke 9:58, "the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head"); the church as royal priesthood (1 Pet 2:9) holds its inheritance in Him. Where this leaves us: Christian theology of the church's pilgrim character, where the visible church may hold property but the deeper inheritance is not reducible to it.

The Anabaptist reading (Menno Simons, Yoder) takes the Levitical portion more directly. The Christian community is a landless Levitical people — a diasporic priesthood whose citizenship is elsewhere. The institutional landedness of Constantinian Christianity is a departure from this calling. Where this leaves us: a serious challenge to any Christianity that confuses spiritual and territorial inheritance, with implications for how the church relates to property, power, and nation.

The Jewish rabbinic reading grounds the Levitical economics in halakhic specifics. Levites serve, receive priestly portions, and are supported by the tithes and offerings the land produces. The non-inheritance is not spiritualized into metaphor but preserved as concrete — a genuine alternative economic structure that the tithing system makes workable. Where this leaves us: a theology in which priesthood implies specific material practices of giving and receiving, and the "inheritance of YHWH" is not metaphor but sacramental reality mediated through the cult.

The Eastern Orthodox reading follows the patristic tradition — the Levitical inheritance is eucharistic. The priest's portion is the altar; the altar is Christ; Christ is the inheritance. This is the most liturgically dense reading and the least easily reduced to ethics or politics. Where this leaves us: a theology that refuses to flatten the Levitical portion into spiritualization — the inheritance is God but God is tangibly present at the altar.

Steelmanning the least intuitive reading: the Anabaptist one. To claim that Christian identity is fundamentally landless can seem escapist, especially to those for whom dispossession is a present injustice rather than a spiritual figure. But the Anabaptist reading honors the specific canonical grammar — Levi's non-inheritance is not a deficit but a vocation — and challenges the confusion of covenant with territory that has marked much Christian political theology. The challenge is sharper, not softer, than it first appears: the church as Levitical people cannot measure its faithfulness by its real estate.

**Unresolved question:** If 13:1 concedes that much land remains to possess and 13:6 promises that YHWH Himself will drive out the remaining inhabitants, but the subsequent history (Judges through Kings) shows many of these peoples were never driven out and instead became the neighbors, enemies, intermarriage partners, and trading partners of Israel — what theology of divine promise survives the promise's visible non-completion, and is "I myself will drive them out" a claim the text means literally, eschatologically, or as a rhetorical authorization of the allotment that follows?

## The Thread

**Numbers 32** — The original Transjordan allotment that Joshua 13:8–33 recapitulates. The back-reference insists Joshua's distribution continues Moses' work; the book's earliest allotment was made before the Jordan was crossed.

**Numbers 22–24 + Numbers 31:8** — The Balaam narrative that Joshua 13:22's brief mention compresses. The New Testament's reception of Balaam (2 Pet 2:15; Jude 11; Rev 2:14) reads back through this node.

**Judges 1:27–36** — The Judges opening inventory of unconquered territory overlaps directly with Joshua 13:2–6, confirming that the land Joshua 13 names as "remaining" stayed remaining.

**Hebrews 11:13–16** — "These all died in faith, not having received the things promised, but having seen them and greeted them from afar." The New Testament reads the patriarchs' (and implicitly Joshua's) incomplete possession as typological of Christian hope — the promise is real, possession is partial, completion is eschatological.
