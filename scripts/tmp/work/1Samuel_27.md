# 1 Samuel 27: David Among the Philistines

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter relocates David from the Judean wilderness to the **Philistine pentapolis**, specifically to the city of **Gath** ruled by **Achish son of Maoch**. The political-geographic shift is enormous and the narrator handles it almost casually, leaving its moral and theological weight for the reader to absorb.

**Gath** is now confidently identified with **Tell es-Safi**, excavated since 1996 by **Aren M. Maeir** of Bar-Ilan University. The Iron I/IIA city Maeir has uncovered is, by considerable distance, the largest Philistine site of David's era — perhaps fifty hectares at its peak — with a massive mudbrick fortification destroyed (probably by Hazael) in the late ninth century. The discovery in 2005 of an ostracon bearing two Indo-European-looking names — *alwt* and *wlt*, plausibly Lydian or Anatolian linguistic relatives, and conceivably the etymological background of the name **Goliath** — has reinforced the picture of Gath as a culturally hybrid city with Aegean roots. **Aren Maeir, Louise Hitchcock, Brent Davis** and others have argued that the early Iron Age Philistine elite preserved Aegean cult practices (notched scapulae, hearth-room architecture, Aegean-style cooking pots) for several generations before assimilating Canaanite norms. David enters not a primitive coastal foe but the most powerful and cosmopolitan urban center in the south Levant.

**Ziklag** (צִקְלַג, *Tsiqlag*), the country town Achish grants David, is geographically harder to pin. The traditional candidate is **Tel Sera** (Tell esh-Sharia), excavated by Eliezer Oren; another strong contender is **Tel Halif** (Lahav). More recently, **Yosef Garfinkel** has proposed **Khirbet a-Ra'i**, where excavations since 2015 have revealed a fortified late-eleventh-century site with both Philistine and Judahite material culture in transition — a "border town" in exactly the sense the chapter implies. Whichever identification stands, the chapter's geographic logic is sound: Ziklag lies in the **northwestern Negev**, on the seam between Philistine, Amalekite, and Judahite zones — a frontier garrison far enough from Gath that David has operational autonomy and far enough from Saul's reach to be safe.

The chapter's key political institution is **vassal patronage**. David becomes Achish's *'eved* (עֶבֶד, "servant," v. 12) — the standard term in Late Bronze and Iron Age Levantine diplomacy for a subordinate ruler bound by oath to a suzerain. The Amarna letters (fourteenth century, three centuries earlier but institutionally continuous) preserve hundreds of letters from such *'abdu* vassals to the Egyptian pharaoh; the political grammar is the same. Achish receives a fortress town's worth of mercenaries who hold the southern frontier; David receives sanctuary, a base, and a livelihood from the spoils of his raids. **Baruch Halpern** (*David's Secret Demons*, 2001) and **Steven McKenzie** (*King David: A Biography*, 2000) have argued that David here functions exactly like an ANE warlord on the make — comparable to the **Hapiru/'apiru** war-bands of the Amarna age and to the chiefdoms of the Iron I highlands. The narrator's terseness on the moral dimension is itself a clue: this is the kind of episode an apologist must include but cannot dwell on.

The **Geshurites, Girzites, and Amalekites** of v. 8 are southern desert peoples — pastoralist-raider groups occupying the Negev, the northern Sinai, and the Shur corridor toward Egypt. (The Geshur of v. 8 is *not* the Aramean Geshur east of the Jordan from which Absalom's mother came — that toponym is reused.) These were peoples without urban centers, outside the Egyptian and Philistine spheres of administrative interest, and outside Israelite tribal coverage — perfect targets for a frontier vassal's raids in that the spoils could be pocketed and the victims would not be missed. The narrator is precise about why David leaves no survivors (vv. 9, 11): no witnesses can carry word to Gath that he has been raiding deserts other than those he claims.

Material details that repay attention: the **livestock list** — sheep, oxen, donkeys, camels, garments (v. 9) — is standard ANE booty inventory, with **camels** (*gemallim*, גְּמַלִּים) the giveaway that the targets are pastoralist-nomadic. Camels were rare in Israelite inventory of this period but standard among Negev/Sinai groups; their presence here is consistent with archaeological evidence (Saidel and Levy on early camel pastoralism). The phrase "as far as Shur, to the land of Egypt" places the raids on the major southern caravan corridor that ran from the Negev to the eastern Delta.

Five Hebrew terms warrant attention. **Ya'ash** (יָאַשׁ, "to despair," v. 1) — David's interior verb in the chapter's opening, a comparatively rare word that frames the chapter's theology of David's lapse in trust. **'Eved** (עֶבֶד, "servant" / "vassal," v. 12) — Achish's term for David, with the political-juridical weight described above. **Mishrash** is *not* in the chapter; the relevant cognate is **bav'ash** (בָּאַשׁ, "to stink," v. 12), Achish's verb for what David has made himself to Israel — literally "an utter stench" (*hav'esh hiv'ish*, infinitive absolute construction for emphasis). **Negev** (נֶגֶב), used three times in v. 10 with three different qualifiers, is the technical term for the "south country" of Judah and a regional toponym for the larger arid south. And **Tsiqlag** (צִקְלַג) itself is etymologically opaque — likely non-Semitic, a Philistine or pre-Israelite name, the kind of toponym that survives political turnover.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre remains heroic prose narrative within the **History of David's Rise** (Rost, 1926; McCarter, *I Samuel*, AB), but the chapter performs a striking generic shift toward something closer to political memoir. There is no battle scene, no prophetic encounter, no direct speech of YHWH; the action is economic, diplomatic, and ethically uncomfortable. The literary device of **narratorial reticence** is the chapter's defining feature.

**Robert Alter**, in *The David Story*, calls 1 Samuel 27 "the chapter of the omitted commentary." The narrator records David's lying to Achish (v. 10), the systematic massacre of witnesses (vv. 9, 11), and Achish's confident misjudgment (v. 12) without a single evaluative phrase. Compare the moral framing surrounding chapters 24 and 26 (where David's restraint is praised by Saul and self-glossed by David) and chapter 24 (where the narrator inserts an editorial sigh about Saul's recognition). Here: nothing. Alter argues this is the highest art of biblical narrative — meaning communicated by the deliberate refusal to comment.

**Meir Sternberg** (*The Poetics of Biblical Narrative*) reads the chapter as a master class in what he calls the "gap": the narrator stages a moral question and forces the reader to do the ethical work. **Shimon Bar-Efrat** (*Narrative Art in the Bible*) catalogs the chapter's stylistic understatement — short clauses, paratactic chains ("and David would strike… and would leave neither… but would take away…"), iterative imperfects suggesting routine.

The chapter opens with a soliloquy — **"David said in his heart"** (וַיֹּאמֶר דָּוִד אֶל-לִבּוֹ, v. 1). This is one of only a handful of biblical soliloquies, and the formula carries a charge: it usually marks a theologically dubious thought. The same construction frames the wicked man's "There is no God" in Psalm 14:1 and Esau's murderous resolve in Genesis 27:41. **Polzin** notes the resonance: the narrator signals David is reasoning without YHWH in view. There is no consultation of priest, prophet, or ephod — striking, given that David has consulted the ephod twice in chapter 23 (vv. 9–12) and will again in chapter 30 (v. 7). The omission is the meaning.

The structural division is clear. Verses 1–4 are the **decision and migration**. Verses 5–7 are the **establishment of Ziklag** with the editorial gloss ("Therefore Ziklag has belonged to the kings of Judah to this day," v. 6) — a Deuteronomistic etiological note placing the chapter in the larger argument that David's actions established institutions of the southern kingdom. Verses 8–12 are the **raid pattern**, with the iterative "would" verbs constructing a routine rather than a single event.

**Devices**. The infinitive absolute *hav'esh hiv'ish* in Achish's mouth (v. 12) is double-edged: Achish thinks David has made himself a stench to Israel, but the reader knows David has rather made Achish his dupe. Dramatic irony is the chapter's basic technique. **Bar-Efrat** also notes the **chiastic geography**: David moves from Israel (Saul's territory) into Gath (royal city) into Ziklag (frontier town) and out into the Negev (no-man's-land) — and brings the spoils back through the chain in reverse. The geography mirrors the moral descent.

**Textual variants** are minor in this chapter. The DSS preservation for ch. 27 in **4QSam-a** is fragmentary; **Eugene Ulrich** and **Emanuel Tov** note no meaning-shifting differences from MT. The LXX renders the soliloquy of v. 1 with a slightly different verb ("I shall be given over"), perhaps softening David's despair, but the divergence is stylistic.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The chapter's place in the canonical arc is structurally important. It marks David's deepest descent — outside the land, vassal to a Philistine, deceiver of his patron, slaughterer of women and children — and is also the platform from which his ascent will launch (Ziklag is the base from which David will receive news of Saul's death in 2 Samuel 1 and from which he will move to Hebron to be anointed king of Judah).

**Jewish interpretation.** **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 6.13.10) handles the chapter with characteristic Hellenistic moralizing reframing: David's flight is *prudence*, his raids are against the *barbaroi* who deserved punishment, his deception of Achish is *strategēma* — strategic ruse, a virtue in Greco-Roman military ethics. The discomfort of David serving a Philistine and lying systematically is dissolved into classical categories. **Pseudo-Philo** is silent on this chapter, which is itself revealing.

The classical rabbis say less here than at neighboring chapters. **Rashi** glosses the chapter primarily linguistically; on the moral question of the slaughter, he notes briefly that the Amalekites and Geshurites were among the seven nations dispossessable under Deuteronomic law. **Radak** (Kimhi) takes the same line and adds a political reading: David spared no one because he could not afford witnesses; this is *milḥemet mitzvah* (commanded war) by other means. **Ralbag** (Gersonides), with his characteristic ethical extraction, finds the chapter more difficult — he focuses on the lesson that righteous people sometimes fall into states where they must do uncomfortable things, and YHWH protects them through it. **Abarbanel** is the most penetrating premodern Jewish reader of this chapter. Writing as a former courtier of the Iberian and Italian crowns who knew vassal politics intimately, he reads David's Philistine sojourn as a deliberate political education — David learns court politics, military logistics, and the management of mixed populations from Achish, knowledge he will deploy as king. Abarbanel does not flinch from the moral cost: the chapter, he says, shows that political life involves compromises a private piety cannot anticipate.

**Avivah Zornberg** has written illuminatingly on the chapter as the dark mirror of David's wilderness years — the period in which he becomes a person who could be king, including its ugly necessities. **Yair Zakovitch** treats the chapter as evidence of an "underside" tradition the editor preserved despite its embarrassment; the unfiltered portrait paradoxically vouches for the historical kernel.

**Christian interpretation.** Patristic readings of this chapter are surprisingly rare and often allegorizing. **Ambrose** (*De Officiis*) praises David's flight as prudent self-preservation but does not engage the deception. **Augustine** (*Quaestiones in Heptateuchum* and scattered references) is more troubled: in *Contra mendacium* and elsewhere he debates whether the patriarchs and David lied, and although he cites primarily the wife-sister episodes and the Egyptian midwives, his framework applies — David lies, and this is one of the texts the church had to work around. **Gregory the Great** allegorizes Ziklag as the soul's exile from the heavenly city. **Aquinas** addresses the chapter glancingly in *Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 110 (on lying), citing it among the Old Testament difficulties on dissimulation; his answer is that the historical narrative records sin without endorsing it.

In the Reformation, **Calvin's sermons on 1 Samuel** (Geneva, late 1550s) read the chapter as a lesson in the failure of trust. David's "said in his heart" is, for Calvin, the entrance of unbelief: David allows fear to override the promises he had received. The flight to Gath, the deception, the slaughter — all are consequences of one earlier failure of faith. Calvin is not interested in exonerating David; he is interested in showing that even the chosen lapse, and that YHWH's covenant is not contingent on the chosen's perfection. **Luther** comments more briefly and characteristically: David is *simul iustus et peccator*, and the chapter shows the latter half.

**Critical scholarship**. **Wellhausen** noted the chapter's tension with the official portrait of David and treated it as evidence of pre-redactional source preserved despite the redactor's preferences. **Frank Moore Cross**'s Deuteronomistic schema places ch. 27 within the History of David's Rise but acknowledges its unusual narratorial restraint. **P. Kyle McCarter** treats the chapter as crucial to the apologetic argument: by including the embarrassment, the narrator preempts opposition charges that David collaborated with Philistines. **Halpern** (*David's Secret Demons*) goes further, treating the chapter as the smoking gun of an apologetic that imperfectly conceals a darker political reality — David really was, in some sense, a Philistine collaborator who turned the relationship to advantage. **Steven McKenzie** (*King David: A Biography*) develops similar themes. **Joel Baden** (*The Historical David*) is more cautious but accepts the basic framework.

**Contemporary** readings. **Walter Brueggemann** (*First and Second Samuel*, Interpretation) treats the chapter as the theological scandal it is and refuses easy resolution: David thrives by deceit, prospers by violence, and YHWH neither rebukes nor blesses. The narrator's silence is YHWH's silence. **Robert Polzin** (*Samuel and the Deuteronomist*) reads the chapter as evidence of the narrator's sophisticated moral irony — the reader is invited to see what Achish cannot. **Eugene Peterson** (*Leap Over a Wall*) struggles honestly with the chapter, framing it as the period of David's life when "the wilderness has gotten into him" and noting that David emerges chastened, not triumphal. **Ellen Davis** (Duke) reads David's Philistine years as an education in displacement that will surface later in his openness to non-Israelite figures (Ittai the Gittite, Uriah the Hittite). **David Gunn** (*The Fate of King Saul*) treats this chapter from a slightly different angle: while David disappears into Philistia, Saul is left without his rival in the land, and the absence is the precondition for the Endor catastrophe of ch. 28.

Ethically attentive readers have noted with concern the chapter's untroubled inclusion of women among those David "left neither man nor woman alive" (vv. 9, 11). The systematic obliteration of populations — including women and children — to preserve operational security is a war crime by any ancient or modern standard, and the narrator's refusal to comment on it is part of the chapter's ethical scandal.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions structure the chapter, both tied to the narrator's silence.

**First tension: faithlessness or pragmatism?** Did David's "said in his heart" reflect a theological lapse, or a realistic strategic calculation that YHWH did not condemn?

*Position 1 (Calvinist-faithlessness):* The opening verse marks David's failure of faith. He has just received a kingdom-grade benediction from Saul ("Blessed be you, my son David! You will do many things and will succeed in them," 26:25), and his immediate response is to despair of YHWH's protection and flee to the enemy. **Calvin** reads this as the entrance of unbelief and the seed of the chapter's ugliness. *Position 2 (apologetic-realist):* The chapter shows mature political prudence. David has now been spared three Ziphite betrayals (chs. 23, 26) and has no reason to think the cycle will not continue. Going to Achish is the only move on the board. **Halpern** and **McKenzie** read this way: David is not theologically failing, he is rationally calculating. The narrator's silence is approval. *Position 3 (Brueggemannian-tragic):* Both. The chapter holds together the necessity and the cost. David could not have reached the throne by purely righteous means within Saul's hunting; he had to enter the Philistine economy. But the price is real, and the narrator records the price by refusing to record approval. Steelman the Calvinist position: the narrator has gone out of his way to mark David's interior state with the unusual "said in his heart" formula, signaling that this is reasoning *without YHWH*. The omission of ephod, prophet, or priest from the chapter is not innocent.

*Where this leaves us:* The chapter does not let us choose between vocation and compromise. David is being shaped for the throne *through* the compromise. The theological discomfort is not a defect of the narrative but its substance.

**Second tension: the moral status of the raids.** The narrator places the slaughter of Geshurites, Girzites, and Amalekites without editorial comment alongside the deception of Achish.

*Position 1 (commanded-war):* The Amalekites at least are under the Deuteronomic *ḥerem* (Deut 25:17–19; cf. 1 Sam 15), and David's raids fulfill, in attenuated form, the very command Saul had failed (1 Sam 15). **Rashi** and **Radak** read this way; some Christian commentators echo it. *Position 2 (cover-up genocide):* The slaughter is functional cover-up — David kills women and children specifically so that no witness can report to Achish. This is not commanded war; it is operational secrecy with civilian victims. **Brueggemann** and other readers who refuse to sanitize the episode press this. *Position 3 (cultural-translation):* What the narrator describes is standard ANE raiding practice, ethically intolerable to modern readers but unremarkable within its world. The chapter neither commends nor condemns; it reports. The reader's moral revulsion is theologically valuable precisely because the canon does not pre-process it. Steelman the cultural-translation position: imposing modern just-war categories on Iron I raid economy is anachronistic; the chapter's task is to transmit, not to evaluate. Steelman the cover-up position: the narrator could have left the motive implicit; instead he twice spells out that David's reason for total slaughter was the prevention of testimony. That detail is not anachronistic — it is the narrator naming the moral structure plainly.

*Where this leaves us:* The chapter is the canon's most sustained refusal to moralize a story that demands moralizing. The reader is the conscience the narrator has declined to embody. The unresolved question is whether that silence is editorial discipline (let the reader judge), apologetic embarrassment (don't dwell on it), or theological wisdom (some things are recorded so we will know what kingship costs, not so we will admire it).

**One unresolved question:** YHWH does not appear in this chapter. No prophet speaks, no ephod is consulted, no divine voice intervenes. Is this absence a judgment on David, a permission, or simply the texture of providence in seasons when the called must walk in their own counsel? The chapter does not say.

## The Thread

Several threads run from this chapter through the canon. **Forward:** Ziklag becomes the location of the Amalekite raid in **1 Sam 30**, where David's failure to leave a garrison behind allows the very kind of raid he had been conducting to be conducted on his own people — a moral chiasmus the narrator does not gloss but invites the reader to feel. **Forward farther:** Achish reappears in **1 Sam 28:1–2** and **29:1–11**, where David's collaboration tightens until the Philistine commanders themselves refuse to allow him into battle against Israel — providence intervening through suspicion. **Forward into 2 Samuel:** David's frontier years shape his openness to non-Israelite servants like **Ittai the Gittite** (2 Sam 15:19–22) and his retention of Philistine bodyguards (the Cherethites and Pelethites). **Outward:** Achish (or his successor of the same name) appears again in **1 Kings 2:39–40** as a still-active Philistine king during Solomon's reign, suggesting the institutional memory of David's vassalage persisted. **Backward:** the chapter sits in dialectical tension with **1 Sam 15**, where Saul's *failure* to obliterate the Amalekites costs him the kingdom, and David's *success* in doing so (in irregular form) accumulates kingly capital — the irony bordering on indictment.
