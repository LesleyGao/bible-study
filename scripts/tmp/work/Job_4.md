# Job 4: The Spirit That Glided Past His Face

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Eliphaz the Temanite speaks first among the friends, and the order is not accidental. Teman was a district of Edom (Genesis 36:11, 15; 1 Kings 11), and Edomite wisdom had a reputation in the ancient Near East comparable to Egyptian or Babylonian wisdom. Jeremiah 49:7 asks "Is wisdom no more in Teman? Has counsel perished from the prudent?" and Obadiah 8-9 mocks the wise men of Teman. Baruch 3:22-23 speaks of the "sons of Hagar who seek for understanding on earth, the merchants of Merran and Teman, the storytellers and seekers for understanding." Eliphaz is the senior sage of the group, and his opening speech represents the mainstream of ancient Near Eastern retributive wisdom. The fact that the book gives the first oracle to a gentile wise man — and the fact that, in 42:7, God rebukes Eliphaz by name for having spoken wrongly — is one of the sharpest subversions in the Hebrew Bible's treatment of wisdom tradition.

The material world of chapter 4 is the world of the city gate and the teacher's chamber. Eliphaz opens with a reminiscence of Job's former teaching role (4:3-4): Job had "instructed many" and "strengthened the weak hands." The Hebrew *yissarta rabim* (יִסַּרְתָּ רַבִּים) uses the verb *yasar* — to discipline, instruct, correct — which becomes the chapter's key motif. Job had been one of the wisdom teachers, and Eliphaz's speech implicitly asks why the teacher cannot now teach himself. The reference to "weak hands" and "feeble knees" (4:3-4) echoes Isaiah 35:3 and will be picked up by Hebrews 12:12; the phrases belong to the stock vocabulary of pastoral encouragement in the wisdom tradition.

The night vision in 4:12-21 inserts a piece of Near Eastern incubation-oracle experience directly into the poem. The vocabulary — *tardemah* (תַּרְדֵּמָה, "deep sleep," the same word used of Adam in Genesis 2:21 and Abram in Genesis 15:12), *pachad* (פַּחַד, "dread"), *se'ara* (שְׂעָרָה, "hair standing on end") — is the language of theophany. Dream-oracles are attested throughout the ancient Near East: the Egyptian *Tale of Sinuhe*, the Mesopotamian *Atrahasis* and the dream episodes in *Gilgamesh*, and, more directly, the dream literature of the Ugaritic Kirta and Aqhat epics. The Babylonian Theodicy (c. 1000 BCE) includes the sufferer's friend citing received divine wisdom as authority; Eliphaz is doing the same, and Pope and Newsom both note the parallel.

Dating is continuous with the book as a whole. Clines (WBC) and Pope argue for a post-exilic redaction; Gordis and Andersen for pre-exilic; Newsom leaves the question open. The night-vision form itself is old and widely attested, but the theological content of the oracle — the transcendent purity of God versus the dust-bound creature — has its nearest parallels in later Israelite priestly and apocalyptic literature, which slightly favors a later date for this particular passage within the book.

Archaeological and textual parallels: the *Ludlul Bel Nemeqi* sufferer hears divine communication through a dream-messenger; Balaam in Numbers 22-24 receives oracles in the night (*va'yavo Elohim el-Bil'am lailah*, Numbers 22:20); Samuel's call in 1 Samuel 3 is a night-oracle. The LXX of Job 4 is closer to the MT length here than in some other chapters, though it softens some of the theological severity of the Hebrew. 11QTargum Job preserves a fragmentary Aramaic translation of parts of the chapter.

Key Hebrew terms: *yasar* (יָסַר, "to discipline/instruct"), the motif word running from 4:3 into chapter 5. *Tam* (תָּם, "blameless") and *yashar* (יָשָׁר, "upright") in 4:6-7 — the same pair used of Job in 1:1 — making Eliphaz's question biting: does Job's supposed *tom* really match God's justice? *Tardemah* (תַּרְדֵּמָה, "deep sleep") in 4:13. *Ruach* (רוּחַ, "spirit/wind/breath") in 4:15 — the same *ruach* that hovered over the waters in Genesis 1:2 and the *ruach* God will breathe into Job at the end. *Enosh* (אֱנוֹשׁ, "mortal/weak human") in 4:17, a term that emphasizes frailty, in contrast to *adam* or *ish*. *Gever* (גֶּבֶר, "man in his strength") also in 4:17, setting up the ironic question whether even the strong *gever* can be pure.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Job 4 is the first friend-speech, and it establishes the rhetorical pattern the dialogues will follow: courteous preface, appeal to observation (the *hinneh*/behold structure), appeal to inherited wisdom, and appeal to special revelation. The chapter is a masterclass in rhetorical tact combined with theological menace.

The opening (4:2-6) is a classic *captatio benevolentiae* — Eliphaz softens his challenge with the rhetorical question, "If one ventures a word with you, will you be impatient?" Then he immediately reminds Job of his former role as teacher. The Hebrew syntax is courteous but the subtext is sharp: Job taught others, now Job must be taught. Robert Alter notes the deft use of parallelism in 4:3-4, where four cola (instruct/strengthen/uphold/make firm) build a portrait of Job's former strength that Eliphaz then inverts in 4:5 — the one who strengthened others is now weak.

The retributive argument (4:7-11) is built on the *hinneh* of personal observation: "As I have seen" (*ka'asher ra'iti* כַּאֲשֶׁר רָאִיתִי). Eliphaz appeals to empirical induction — the innocent never perish. The rhetorical question *mi hu naki avad* ("who that was innocent ever perished?") is structured to compel assent. David Clines notes this is the foundational claim of orthodox retributive theology, and Eliphaz's rhetoric treats it as self-evident. The lion metaphors (4:10-11) deploy five different Hebrew words for lion — *aryeh*, *shachal*, *kefir*, *layish*, *lavi* — a lexical extravagance that signals a formal proverb-form. J. Gerald Janzen (Interpretation commentary) reads the piled-up lion vocabulary as a wisdom-tradition proverb quoted or composed by Eliphaz to illustrate the breaking of the strong.

The night vision (4:12-21) is the chapter's literary center and its most famous passage. The scene is built on seven elements: the stealthy word, the whisper, the thoughts of night, the dread, the spirit-form, the silence, and the voice. Marvin Pope argues the passage represents an older theophany tradition inserted into the dialogue; Clines treats it as Eliphaz's own composition designed to lend his argument the authority of revelation. Edwin Good calls this "the most uncanny passage in the book," noting the deliberate ambiguity of the spirit's form — Eliphaz cannot say what he saw, only that his hair stood on end. The Hebrew *demamah* (דְּמָמָה, "silence") in 4:16 is the same word used in 1 Kings 19:12 of Elijah's *qol demamah daqqah* — the "still small voice" or "sound of thin silence." The intertextual echo is striking.

The oracle itself (4:17-21) is a three-stage argument from the greater to the lesser: if God charges his servants (angels) with error, how much more creatures of dust; if those of heaven are imperfect, how much more those who live in houses of clay; and all die "without wisdom." The *qal vachomer* (light-and-heavy) argument form is a standard rabbinic and ancient Near Eastern logical move. The critical question in 4:17 — *ha'enosh me'Eloah yitsdaq? im me'osehu yithar gaver?* — can be translated two ways: "Can mortal man be more righteous than God? Can a strong man be purer than his Maker?" or (more defensibly from the Hebrew comparative particle *min*) "Can mortal man be righteous before God? Can a strong man be pure before his Maker?" The ambiguity is theological: the first translation makes Job's complaint absurd (no one is more righteous than God); the second makes it impossible (no creature is righteous at all before God). Clines, Newsom, and most modern translations take the second.

The moth image in 4:19 — *yedakkeum lifnei ash* ("they are crushed like the moth") — is brutal and precise: the moth is small, short-lived, easily destroyed. Norman Habel notes the poetic density of verses 19-21: houses of clay, foundation in dust, crushed like the moth, beaten between morning and evening, tent-cord plucked up. Five images of human fragility piled in four verses.

The speech ends abruptly in 4:21 without formal closure, which the Hebrew signals by leaving the oracle's voice hanging. This unfinished quality is part of the literary effect: Eliphaz has delivered the oracle but has not yet told Job what to do with it. Chapter 5 will continue the speech.

Textual notes: verse 18's *toholah* (תָּהֳלָה, "error") is a hapax legomenon — a word occurring only here in the Hebrew Bible — and its meaning is uncertain; the LXX reads "something crooked." Verse 20's *mibli mesim* ("without anyone regarding it") is also difficult; some read "no one puts it to heart."

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Job 4 introduces the theology that the book will spend thirty-eight chapters dismantling. Its intellectual placement in the canon is therefore peculiar — it articulates the retributive framework (Deuteronomy 28, Proverbs 10-14) that the rest of Job contests.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

Second Temple Jewish readings treated Eliphaz's night vision as a genuine revelation. Testament of Job preserves the prologue but elides much of the dialogue, including this vision; where it does reference Eliphaz, it treats him ambiguously. Philo of Alexandria in *De Somniis* uses Eliphaz's dream-oracle as an example of divine communication through sleep, treating the oracle as authentic. The 11QTargum Job preserves parts of the chapter in Aramaic.

Rabbinic readings are divided. Bava Batra 15b-16a does not settle whether Eliphaz's vision was real or feigned. Rashi treats Eliphaz as a genuine prophet but one whose application to Job is wrong — the oracle is true in general but misapplied in particular. Ibn Ezra notes the night-vision form and compares it to Balaam. Maimonides in *Guide* III.23 takes the most striking position: each friend represents a theological school, and Eliphaz represents the Mosaic / Torah-based retributive view, which Maimonides considers partially true but insufficient to explain Job's case. The vision itself Maimonides reads as genuine but reflecting the limits of prophetic experience without full understanding. Nachmanides in *Sha'ar ha-Gemul* treats Eliphaz's claim that no one is innocent before God as theologically correct but inappropriate as pastoral counsel for Job. Gersonides (Ralbag) gives a detailed philosophical analysis and concludes Eliphaz's theology is correct in general but inapplicable to Job's specific case.

Modern Jewish readings have been sharper. Jon Levenson in *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* treats Eliphaz's retributive theology as the target of the book's critique. Avivah Zornberg reads the night vision psychologically — Eliphaz is describing his own trauma in claiming to have received an oracle; the vision may be less revelation than projection. James Kugel in *The Great Shift* treats the oracle as an example of the older divine-intuition that the book itself is transcending. Edward Greenstein's Job commentary argues Eliphaz's vision is presented by the narrator with ironic distance — we are not meant to accept it as authoritative.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

The Christian tradition has read Eliphaz's vision more positively than the Jewish tradition. Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job* treats the night oracle as a genuine revelation of divine holiness, and Gregory extracts from it the doctrine that even the angels are imperfect by comparison with God — a doctrine he uses extensively in his angelology. Origen and Chrysostom both cite Eliphaz's vision approvingly, reading it as authentic theophany. Augustine uses 4:18 ("Even in his servants he puts no trust, and his angels he charges with error") as a key proof-text for his anti-Pelagian theology of grace — if angels cannot be righteous before God, much less fallen humanity. This is probably the single most theologically consequential verse in Job for Western Christianity, because Augustine's use of it shaped the subsequent Reformed doctrine of total depravity.

Aquinas in *Expositio super Iob* reads the vision as genuine but limited — Eliphaz has received a true word from God about creaturely insufficiency, but he has drawn the wrong conclusion about Job's particular guilt. Calvin's sermons on Job 4 (sermons 15-17 in the 1554-55 cycle) treat the vision as theologically accurate and cite 4:17-19 repeatedly in his Institutes (especially I.17.2 and II.1.5) as evidence for human depravity. Luther quotes the passage in his lectures on Romans. Wesley cites 4:17 in his sermons on Christian perfection to describe the condition from which grace rescues.

The critical twentieth century has reread Eliphaz's vision with suspicion. Westermann's form-critical work treats the vision as a rhetorical device, not a revelation. David Clines's WBC argues that if God in 42:7 says the friends "have not spoken of me what is right," this must include the night vision. Carol Newsom in *Contest of Moral Imaginations* treats the oracle as Eliphaz's claim to special revelation, which the book as a whole undermines. Ellen Davis, Susannah Ticciati, Norman Habel, and John Hartley all treat the vision as Eliphaz's own rhetorical construction rather than genuine divine communication, and read the chapter as the voice of pious counsel that adds insult to the suffering of the innocent.

The key Jewish-Christian divergence on this chapter: Augustine and the Reformed tradition treated 4:17-19 as a key proof-text for the fallenness and unrighteousness of humanity before God, a reading that feeds directly into the doctrines of original sin and total depravity. Jewish tradition has overwhelmingly refused this move: humans are not intrinsically unrighteous before God, and Eliphaz's absolutizing of that claim is part of what makes his counsel bad. The Reformation's dogmatic structure depends, in a surprisingly direct way, on taking Eliphaz's oracle as authoritative — which the book's own ending arguably refuses.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension one: the status of Eliphaz's night vision as revelation.** Is this a genuine oracle that speaks truth, even if misapplied, or is it false from the start?

- Catholic (Aquinas, *Expositio* on Job 4:17; CCC 304-305 on providence, 309-314 on theodicy): the vision is genuine revelation of a true principle (creaturely dependence on God) but misapplied pastorally. Catholic tradition generally preserves the content of the oracle while distancing it from Eliphaz's use.
- Reformed Protestant (Calvin, sermons on Job 4; Institutes I.17.2): the vision is true and theologically foundational for the doctrine of total depravity. Eliphaz's error is pastoral, not dogmatic. The Westminster Confession's anthropology leans on this reading.
- Lutheran (Luther, lectures on Romans): the oracle speaks the law's accusation against all flesh, and is therefore true. Luther reads 4:17-19 as anticipation of the Pauline theology of justification.
- Wesleyan / Arminian: the oracle accurately describes fallen humanity but not regenerate humanity; grace transforms what Eliphaz describes as the universal condition.
- Anabaptist: the oracle is Eliphaz's own speech, and its authority depends on God's judgment in 42:7, which condemns it.
- Orthodox (Chrysostom, later Lossky on apophatic theology): the vision touches on the divine mystery — even the angels do not fully know God — and is therefore theologically serviceable, though Eliphaz misuses it.
- Jewish (Rashi, Maimonides, Gersonides): the oracle is true but misapplied; humans are not intrinsically unrighteous, but relatively so before God.
- Critical (Clines, Newsom, Westermann): the oracle is Eliphaz's rhetorical invention or, at minimum, his misreading of an ambiguous experience; the book's ending repudiates it.

Steelmanning the Reformed reading: if God condemns the friends in 42:7 for not speaking what is right, Calvin's move is still defensible — the friends spoke some things rightly and some things wrongly, and 4:17-19 might belong to the right category even if 4:7-8 (no innocent person ever perished) does not. Calvin's reading is not obviously refuted by 42:7, and the Pauline echo in Romans 3:10-18 ("none is righteous, no, not one") suggests the New Testament takes something like Eliphaz's principle as canonical even while the book of Job rejects its application. Where this leaves us: the reader faces a genuine choice between canonical readings. To take 4:17-19 as canonical truth requires softening 42:7; to take 42:7 at full weight requires disowning a verse that shaped Western Christian anthropology. The book refuses to decide.

**Tension two: the pastoral violence of retributive theology.** Eliphaz's speech is well-intentioned. He believes he is helping. He reminds Job that Job has taught this doctrine to others. And yet the speech wounds the sufferer it means to console. Does the book indict the retributive framework itself, or only its misuse?

- Catholic (CCC 1500-1525 on the pastoral care of the suffering; *Salvifici Doloris* by John Paul II on the meaning of suffering): suffering has pedagogical and redemptive dimensions, but pastoral counsel must not presume diagnosis. Catholic pastoral theology has increasingly refused to read suffering as divine indictment.
- Reformed (Calvin, Westminster Larger Catechism Q.28 on sanctification through affliction): affliction is legitimately read as discipline, and Eliphaz is not wrong in principle; his error is in presuming to know which affliction is disciplinary.
- Lutheran: the *theologia crucis* refuses to read suffering as evidence of sin; Eliphaz is a theologian of glory, trying to explain suffering in terms of deserved outcomes.
- Wesleyan: affliction is one of the means of grace, and the community's role is to accompany, not diagnose.
- Anabaptist: the community of the church is called to sit with the sufferer in silence, as the friends originally did in 2:13, and to refuse the diagnostic move.
- Orthodox: suffering belongs within the mystery of the divine economy, and any attempt to render it transparent through principle is a form of pride.
- Jewish (Levenson, Zornberg, Zvi Grumet): the book critiques the retributive framework by showing what it does to real sufferers; the theology itself is not discarded but is forced to yield to a larger mystery.
- Critical (Clines, Newsom): Eliphaz's speech is pious cruelty dressed as counsel; the book's rejection of Eliphaz in 42:7 is the narrator's verdict on the diagnostic move itself.

Steelmanning the Anabaptist / pastoral-silence position: the most countercultural reading is that the friends failed the moment they spoke. Their seven days of silence in 2:13 were their best theology. The rest of the book is a tragedy of well-intentioned speech — even true words, spoken badly, wound. This reading asks whether theological knowledge itself is dangerous in the wrong mouth. Where this leaves us: if Eliphaz is right in content and wrong in application, the problem is pastoral; if he is wrong in content as well, the problem is theological. The book seems to indict him on both counts — 4:7 ("who that was innocent ever perished?") is flatly false given the prologue we have just read.

One unresolved question that lands: Eliphaz appeals to a personal night vision as the ground of his theology. Job will appeal to his own experience as the ground of his counter-theology. Whose experience is authoritative, and by what criterion can a hearer decide between two sincere appeals to revelation? The book of Job seems to think this question is settled only when God speaks directly — but what about in the long silence before that speaking?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 15:12** — "As the sun was going down, a deep sleep fell on Abram, and behold, dreadful and great darkness fell upon him." The Hebrew *tardemah* of Eliphaz's vision (4:13) is exactly Abram's *tardemah*. When you read Genesis 15, hold it alongside Job 4: both are night-dread theophanies, but Abram receives a covenant, and Eliphaz receives a pessimistic oracle he applies wrongly. The same divine idiom yields very different content.

- **1 Kings 19:11-13** — Elijah at Horeb hears the *qol demamah daqqah*, the "sound of thin silence," after the wind and fire and earthquake. Eliphaz's oracle opens with *demamah* (silence, 4:16) before the voice. The formal parallel is exact, which is either a deep vindication of Eliphaz's experience or a deliberate literary echo meant to press the question — whose silence issues in whose voice? When you read 1 Kings 19, remember this.

- **Romans 3:10-18** — Paul's chain of proof-texts that "none is righteous, no, not one" echoes Eliphaz's oracle in 4:17 almost exactly: "Can mortal man be righteous before God?" When you reach Romans 3, remember that the principle Paul treats as canonical Old Testament teaching is first articulated in the mouth of a man God will later condemn for speaking wrongly. The New Testament's use of Job is more complex than it appears.

- **James 1:5** — "If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask of God, who gives to all generously and without reproach." James's pastoral theology — asking, not diagnosing — stands as a deliberate counter to Eliphaz's method. When you read James 1, hear Job 4 in the background: the epistle of James is in many ways the post-Joban wisdom book of the New Testament.
