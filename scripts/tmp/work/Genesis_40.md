# Genesis 40: Two Dreams in an Egyptian Prison

---

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

### Political Situation

Joseph sits in an Egyptian prison belonging to the *sar ha-tabbachim* (שַׂר הַטַּבָּחִים), "captain of the guard" — the same Potiphar of Genesis 39, whose house doubles as a detention facility for royal officials. The narrative takes place at an unnamed Pharaoh's court, and pinning down which Pharaoh has vexed Egyptologists for over a century.

Two main windows contend:
- **Hyksos period (c. 1720–1550 BC, 15th–16th Dynasties)**: Favored by Kenneth Kitchen and many evangelical Egyptologists. A Semitic-background Pharaoh would explain Joseph's rapid elevation in Genesis 41. The Hyksos capital Avaris (Tell el-Dab'a) has yielded Semitic material culture, including a palace with a statue of a Semitic dignitary with a multicolored coat — tantalizing but not dispositive (Manfred Bietak's excavations).
- **Middle Kingdom 12th Dynasty (c. 1985–1773 BC)**: The Story of Sinuhe and the Beni Hasan tomb paintings (c. 1890 BC) depicting Semitic traders entering Egypt fit a patriarchal-era backdrop. Donald Redford prefers a much later, essentially literary reconstruction.

### Material Culture

The chapter is saturated with authentic Egyptian court detail:

- **Cupbearer** (*mashqeh*, מַשְׁקֶה) and **chief baker** (*sar ha-ofim*, שַׂר הָאֹפִים) were genuine high offices. Egyptian titles *wdpw* (cupbearer) and *rtḥty* (baker) appear in Old and Middle Kingdom records. The cupbearer tasted wine for poison and had constant access to Pharaoh — a position of intimate trust.
- **Pharaoh's birthday feast** (v. 20): Egyptian celebration of the *msyt* (birth-day, often the accession day understood as rebirth) is attested; the Rosetta Stone mentions Ptolemy V's birthday amnesty centuries later, showing a real royal custom of prisoner release on such days.
- **Hanging on a tree** (v. 19): Impalement, not gallows-hanging. Egyptian execution by impalement on a stake is documented in the Amherst Papyrus and tomb reliefs.
- **Bird-pecked corpses**: Denial of burial was a horror in Egyptian thought, which obsessed over bodily preservation for the afterlife. The baker's fate is theological as well as physical.
- **Dreams**: Egypt had an extensive dream-interpretation industry. The *Chester Beatty Dream Papyrus* (c. 1275 BC, 19th Dynasty) is essentially a manual with hundreds of symbolic dreams and their meanings — "If a man sees himself in a dream eating crocodile flesh: good; it means becoming an official among his people." Dream interpretation was a professional craft, and Joseph's claim that interpretation belongs to God (v. 8) is a direct polemical jab at this guild.

### Authorship & Dating

Critical scholarship (since Wellhausen) treats the Joseph cycle as unusually unified — so much so that Gerhard von Rad famously called it a freestanding "novella" (Genesis: A Commentary, 1961), closer to Egyptian wisdom literature than to the episodic patriarchal stories. The traditional J/E source division breaks down here; most scholars now see either a single author working with traditions or a very light editorial hand. Claus Westermann and Robert Alter treat chapters 37–50 as an artistic whole.

### Key Terms

- **pitrōn** (פִּתְרוֹן), "interpretation" — the root *p-t-r* occurs only in this Joseph cycle within the Hebrew Bible, echoing the Egyptian/Aramaic cognate *pšr*. Same root as Qumran's *pesher* commentaries.
- **nāśā' rō'š** (נָשָׂא רֹאשׁ), "lift up the head" — the narrator's devastating pun in v. 20. He lifts up the cupbearer's head in restoration and the baker's head in decapitation/impalement. The same idiom, opposite fates.
- **bôr** (בּוֹר), "pit" (v. 15) — Joseph's word choice for the prison links this dungeon to the *bôr* his brothers threw him into (37:24) and will reappear when the cupbearer finally "remembers" him (41:14). The life of Joseph is a descent into and ascent from pits.
- **ḥesed** (חֶסֶד, v. 14) — Joseph's plea to "do me *hesed*" invokes covenant-loyalty language; he is asking the cupbearer for the kind of faithfulness Yahweh shows Israel. The cupbearer fails.
- **zākar** (זָכַר), "remember," and **shākaḥ** (שָׁכַח), "forget" — paired at v. 23 with theological weight. These are covenant verbs (cf. Exodus 2:24).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

### Genre

This is Hebrew narrative prose of the highest craft — what Alter calls "the art of biblical narrative" at its most sophisticated. Specifically, it's a **dream-pair episode** within a larger novella. Dream-pairs are a structural signature of the Joseph cycle: Joseph's own two dreams (37:5–11), the cupbearer and baker's two dreams (here), and Pharaoh's two dreams (41). Three sets of two — a narrative triad of paired dreams, the middle set hinging the others.

### Literary Devices

- **Chiastic symmetry of the dreams**: Both have the number three, both involve food/drink for Pharaoh, both end with the interpreter "lifting up the head." The symmetry makes the asymmetric outcomes shocking.
- **Dramatic irony**: The reader knows from chapter 37 that Joseph himself is a dreamer whose dreams anticipated elevation. Now he interprets dreams of elevation and execution for others while himself awaiting vindication. The cupbearer's rising cup is Joseph's own trajectory in miniature.
- **Fulfillment formula**: "as Joseph had interpreted to them" (v. 22) authenticates Joseph's prophetic gift, setting up Pharaoh's confidence in chapter 41.
- **Narrative echoes**: The "pit" (*bôr*) language in v. 15 reactivates 37:24. Joseph's protest that he was "stolen" (*gunnōb gunnabtī*, intensive construction) from "the land of the Hebrews" is the first use of "Hebrews" as a geographic/ethnic identifier in Genesis — remarkable that Joseph, in Egypt, names his homeland ethnographically rather than by family.
- **Structural placement**: Chapter 40 is the pivot of Joseph's fall-and-rise. Chapter 39 ended with Joseph imprisoned but favored; chapter 41 will elevate him. Chapter 40 is the hinge — and its final verse ("but forgot him") twists the knife, forcing two years of further waiting (41:1).
- **The withheld name**: Joseph's God is referenced obliquely ("Do not interpretations belong to *Elohim*?") — he witnesses to pagans without using the covenant name YHWH.

### Textual Notes

The Masoretic text is stable here. The LXX renders "hang on a tree" with *kremasei epi xylou* (κρεμάσει ἐπὶ ξύλου) — the exact phrasing Paul cites in Galatians 3:13 from Deuteronomy 21:23. This Greek phrase becomes the NT lexicon for crucifixion, which is why Christian tradition later reads the baker's fate typologically (more in Lens 3).

### Location in the Book's Argument

Genesis 37–50 answers the question: how did Israel end up in Egypt? Chapter 40 is the narrow passage through which divine providence moves from Joseph's descent to his ascent. Without the baker's death, no empty throne-adjacent position opens. Without the cupbearer's restoration, no one to remember Joseph when Pharaoh dreams. Without Joseph's eventual elevation, no grain in famine, no family reunion, no 400 years in Egypt, no Exodus. Chapter 40 is the smallest hinge on which the largest door swings.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### The Canonical Arc

Genesis 40 sits on the long thread of Israelite identity formed in foreign courts. It inaugurates what will become a biblical genre: **the Jew who interprets the foreign king's inscrutable revelation**. Joseph → Daniel → Esther/Mordecai. Each figure rises in a pagan court by access to divine wisdom the court cannot access on its own. This is a diaspora-survival pattern, and some critical scholars (notably Lawrence Wills, *The Jew in the Court of the Foreign King*, 1990) argue the Joseph novella was shaped in or for exilic/Second Temple audiences reading themselves into Joseph's shoes.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

**Second Temple (c. 200 BC – 100 AD)**: Joseph becomes a paradigmatic righteous figure. *Jubilees* 39–40 expands his piety. Philo (*De Iosepho*) reads him allegorically as the statesman-philosopher whose virtue thrives in the chaotic "body-politic" of Egypt. Josephus (*Antiquities* 2.59–86) emphasizes Joseph's rhetorical skill. The *Testament of Joseph* (in the *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs*) makes him a model of chastity and endurance.

**Rabbinic (c. 200–600 AD)**: *Bereshit Rabbah* 88–89 mines chapter 40 for moral lessons. The rabbis are troubled by Joseph's plea to the cupbearer (v. 14). *Bereshit Rabbah* 89:3 famously reads the two extra years of imprisonment (41:1) as **punishment** for Joseph's lack of faith — "Because he said to the chief cupbearer, 'remember me' and 'mention me,' two extra years were added to him." Rashi preserves this: Joseph should have trusted God alone, not sought deliverance through a pagan's favor. This is a strikingly self-critical reading: even the righteous can hedge their faith.

**Medieval**: Rashi (11th c.) follows the midrash on Joseph's "sin" of petitioning the cupbearer. Ibn Ezra (12th c.), more rationalist, defends Joseph — using proper means is not faithlessness. Nachmanides/Ramban (13th c.) synthesizes: the petition was legitimate, but Joseph leaned on it too heavily. Maimonides doesn't directly treat the chapter but his theory of prophecy (*Guide* II.36–48) places dream-interpretation in a middle rank — higher than natural cognition, below true prophecy, which matters for how Joseph's gift is categorized.

**Modern**: Nehama Leibowitz (*Studies in Bereshit*) marshals both Rashi and Ramban and leaves the tension productive. Avivah Zornberg (*Genesis: The Beginning of Desire*, 1995) reads Joseph's "Remember me" as the cry of the forgotten, aligning him with all exiles. Robert Alter, in his translation commentary, highlights the artistry: "forgot him" (v. 23) is the narrative's deliberate cliff.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

**Patristic**: The baker hanged on a tree whose flesh the birds eat reads, to Christian allegorists, as a type of the crucified — or, more pointedly, as the antitype. Caesarius of Arles (6th c.) and the *Glossa Ordinaria* contrast the two prisoners: the cupbearer restored (resurrection/salvation) and the baker hanged (judgment). Ambrose (*On Joseph*) makes Joseph a Christ-figure: innocent, imprisoned between two condemned men (anticipating Luke 23:32–43 — the crucified Christ between two thieves, one saved, one lost). This parallel becomes standard: Joseph between cupbearer and baker prefigures Jesus between the two criminals.

**Medieval**: Aquinas uses the Joseph narrative sparingly but accepts the typological reading in *Summa* III and biblical commentary. The cupbearer's "forgetting" becomes a standard exemplum of worldly ingratitude.

**Reformation**: Luther's *Lectures on Genesis* (1535–45) devotes substantial space to chapter 40. Luther sees Joseph's patience as a model of faith under trial (*Anfechtung*) and reads the cupbearer's forgetfulness as God's providence — had the cupbearer remembered too soon, Joseph would have been released on merely human terms, not as God's instrument in famine. Calvin (*Commentary on Genesis*, 1554) strikes a characteristic note: Joseph's plea was legitimate, and we are not obliged to despise natural means, but God deliberately frustrated it to accomplish something greater. Calvin uses this chapter as his leading case for the doctrine of **meticulous providence**.

**Critical**: Wellhausen and Gunkel paid less attention to chapter 40 than to cosmogonic and legal material. Von Rad (1961) restored its prestige by naming the Joseph story a wisdom novella, its theological vision: "God's hidden governance" — precisely the kind of providence Calvin saw, but now literary rather than doctrinal.

**Contemporary**: Walter Brueggemann (*Genesis*, Interpretation, 1982) emphasizes the chapter's political-theological weight: God's wisdom operates *through* and *against* the Egyptian knowledge-system, subverting imperial epistemology. Terence Fretheim reads providence as relational rather than deterministic. Goldingay treats it as a study in vocation under constraint.

### Convergence and Divergence

Jews and Christians **converge** in reading Joseph as a righteous sufferer, model of providence, and exemplar of wisdom in foreign exile. Both traditions love this chapter's quiet faith.

They **diverge** on two fronts:
1. **Typology**: Christians read Joseph → Christ; Jews do not. The cupbearer/baker framing especially invites Christian reading of Luke 23, which is entirely absent from Jewish interpretation.
2. **Joseph's petition**: Rabbinic tradition finds fault (explaining the two years); Christian tradition almost universally exonerates him, reading the delay as pure providence. This reflects deeper differences in how each tradition handles righteous figures' flaws — Jewish midrash is comfortable finding subtle sins in patriarchs; Christian typological reading tends to preserve the type's integrity to preserve the antitype.

### Where This Leaves Us

The chapter has become a favorite of both traditions for preaching providence in the dark. Post-Holocaust, Jewish readers like Emil Fackenheim and Zornberg engage the "forgotten Joseph" with new weight — what does it mean to be the one the cupbearer forgets, not for years but for generations?

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

### What Is God Up To?

God does not appear in chapter 40. No vision, no voice, no theophany. The only mention is Joseph's question: "Do not interpretations belong to *Elohim*?" This is the theological heart. The author's vision is of a God who acts through **hidden governance** — dreams given, rightly interpreted, fulfilled exactly, without divine speech, without miracle, without even a name. The narrator is making a claim: God's sovereignty is most itself when most concealed. This chapter is the Genesis equivalent of Esther — a book where God is never named but everywhere operative.

The author also seems to argue: **revelation crosses ethnic lines**. Pagan Egyptians receive divinely-sent dreams. God is not tribal. But interpretation requires a covenant mediator — revelation and understanding are distributed unequally. The Egyptians dream; the Hebrew interprets.

### Two Theological Tensions

#### Tension 1: Providence and Means — how does God's hidden governance relate to human action?

**Catholic (CCC 302–314)**: God is primary cause; human acts are genuine secondary causes. Joseph's plea to the cupbearer is a legitimate secondary cause; its failure and the two-year delay are under primary causality without negating Joseph's agency. Thomist concurrence.

**Reformed (Calvin, Westminster Confession V)**: Meticulous providence. God "ordereth them to fall out according to the nature of second causes, either necessarily, freely, or contingently." Calvin's reading of Genesis 40 is his paradigm — the cupbearer's forgetting is not accident but decree.

**Lutheran**: God works through means (*Deus absconditus/revelatus*) but the hiddenness of God in suffering (Luther's theology of the cross) is central. Joseph's prison is a locus of the hidden God who works contrary to appearances.

**Wesleyan**: Resists determinism; prefers prevenient grace cooperating with free human response. The cupbearer's forgetfulness is his own moral failure, which God incorporates but does not ordain.

**Anabaptist**: Focuses less on metaphysics of causation, more on the community of the faithful waiting without coercion.

**Jewish (Rashi/Ramban tension)**: As above — Joseph's plea was either insufficient faith or legitimate means, with the tradition leaving both readings in play.

**Critical/academic**: The text's literary theology doesn't require metaphysical commitments; von Rad's "hidden guidance" is a narrative-theological category about how stories of divine action are *told*, not a doctrinal claim to be systematized.

**Steelman the least intuitive — Calvinist meticulous providence**: The critique is obvious: if God ordains the cupbearer's forgetting, is God responsible for Joseph's two extra years of injustice? The strongest Reformed response (Calvin, Bavinck, contemporary Paul Helm) is that our intuitions about causation are formed in a univocal metaphysics inadequate to Creator/creature distinction. God's ordaining is not the same kind of act as the cupbearer's forgetting — they operate on different ontological planes. The cupbearer is fully responsible for his ingratitude; God is fully responsible for weaving it into Joseph's salvation-trajectory and Israel's preservation through famine. Both claims hold without competing. The alternative — a God who merely reacts — leaves the famine, the formation of Israel, and arguably the Exodus hostage to human fickleness.

#### Tension 2: Revelation Among the Nations — what do pagan dreams mean?

**Catholic**: General revelation is real; all truth is God's truth (cf. *Lumen Gentium* 16, *Nostra Aetate* 2). Pagan dreams can bear genuine divine content. The Church's interpretive authority applies to scripture, not to all religious experience.

**Reformed**: More cautious. Dreams given to pagans are providential rather than revelatory in the covenantal sense. The Egyptians receive messages about their own fate, not saving knowledge of God.

**Orthodox (Lossky, Florovsky)**: The Logos is at work in all human knowing; *logoi spermatikoi* (seed-words) scatter divine truth among the nations. The cupbearer and baker's dreams are instances of the pre-incarnate Word addressing humanity.

**Jewish (Maimonides)**: Dreams are low-grade prophecy available to non-prophets and non-Jews. The *Guide* II.36 classifies dreams as a natural faculty that can be touched by divine influence — Egyptians can have it; only prophets get the full version.

**Critical**: The text reflects ancient Near Eastern dream culture, where dreams were universally regarded as divine messages requiring professional interpretation. The author is not making a systematic claim but narrating within inherited assumptions.

### Ethical Difficulty

The baker is executed. Joseph interprets the dream truthfully but offers no appeal, no mercy, no protest. The text seems untroubled. A serious contemporary reader notices: one prisoner walks free, one is impaled, and the difference appears to be — what? The narrative gives no moral backstory. Did the baker actually poison Pharaoh's bread? Was the cupbearer actually innocent of the wine offense? We don't know. Joseph doesn't ask. The asymmetry of divine disclosure — some receive grace, some receive judgment, and the text does not explain — is the same scandal that runs through the Bible from Cain to Esau to Pharaoh to Judas.

Responses:
- **Traditional**: Trust the justice of God in what is not revealed.
- **Critical-protest (Brueggemann, Westermann)**: Name the scandal rather than resolve it; refuse premature theodicy.
- **Jewish (Levenson, *Creation and the Persistence of Evil*)**: Biblical theology is comfortable with divine action that does not ethically satisfy modern sensibilities; the text resists systematization.

### Where This Leaves Us

Genesis 40 teaches that God works in the dark — through dreams not explained, through prisoners not rescued yet, through ingratitude incorporated into deliverance. The chapter refuses to moralize Joseph's petition or the cupbearer's forgetting. It simply ends: "but forgot him." The author trusts readers to sit with that silence for two chapter-years.

### The Unresolved Question

If the cupbearer had remembered Joseph immediately — if the prayer you keep praying had been answered on day three instead of year two — would the famine-bread you were placed in the world to bake for others ever have risen?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 41:9–13**: The cupbearer finally remembers. Note the verb *zākar*. Two full years later (41:1). The narrator's patience with Joseph's waiting matches Joseph's own.
- **Daniel 2 and 4**: Daniel interprets the Babylonian king's dreams when the court magicians cannot — a direct narrative descendant of Genesis 40. Same formula: "There is a God in heaven who reveals mysteries" (Dan 2:28) echoes Joseph's "Do not interpretations belong to God?" When you get to Daniel, this chapter is the template.
- **Luke 23:32–43**: Jesus crucified between two criminals, one saved, one not. The Christian typological tradition from Ambrose onward reads Joseph between cupbearer and baker as the template. Whether you accept the typology or not, note the structural echo.
- **Esther 6:1**: The king cannot sleep and "happens" to read the chronicle that records Mordecai's forgotten loyalty. Another story where a pagan court's memory (or insomnia) becomes the pivot of Jewish deliverance. The cupbearer who forgot has his inverse in the Persian king who remembered.
- **Acts 5:30, Galatians 3:13**: Paul's language of Christ "hanged on a tree" draws from the LXX idiom first used here (v. 19) and codified in Deuteronomy 21:22–23. A small verbal root that grows a vast theological trunk.
