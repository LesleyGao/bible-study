# Deuteronomy 24: A Certificate of Divorce

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Deuteronomy 24 sits in the latter third of the Deuteronomic Code and is presented, in the book's literary frame, as part of Moses's farewell discourse on the plains of Moab. The book's Hebrew title, *Dĕbārîm* ("words"), is taken from its opening clause. The chapter opens with the single most consequential piece of legislation on marriage in the Hebrew Bible — the divorce-certificate provision of vv. 1–4 — and proceeds through a cluster of social-justice laws on military exemption, collateral, kidnapping, wages, individual responsibility, and provision for the sojourner, orphan, and widow. Whether the underlying material reaches back to the late second millennium (Kitchen; Craigie) or reflects a Josianic reform document reworking older oral tradition (de Wette; Wellhausen; Weinfeld), the law here presupposes a settled agrarian-and-mercantile society with written instruments, flat-roof houses, vineyards, olive groves, grain fields, day-laborers, and cross-border travel.

The divorce certificate (*sēper kĕrîtût*, v. 1) assumes a literate instrument handed by a husband to a wife at the moment of separation. Archaeologically, the Elephantine papyri from the fifth-century BCE Jewish military colony in Upper Egypt preserve actual marriage and divorce documents — Kraeling and Porten published them — and show the legal machinery Deuteronomy 24 anticipates. Cuneiform tablets from Nuzi, Emar, and Mari preserve comparable contracts and sometimes divorce clauses stipulated within the marriage document itself. The Middle Assyrian Laws (MAL A §§37–38) permit divorce with varying compensation arrangements; Hammurabi §§137–143 distinguishes grounds and consequences. Eshnunna §59 preserves a particularly sharp divorce provision in which a husband who divorces the mother of his children must pay substantial compensation. Against this background, Deuteronomy 24's distinctive contribution is not the introduction of divorce (which the ANE world already permitted) but the insistence on a written instrument placed in the woman's hand — a piece of her own legal documentation — and the specific restriction on remarriage to a former husband.

The military-exemption clause (v. 5) fits the Iron Age conscription pattern documented in Assyrian annals and at Mari: citizen-soldiers called up for seasonal campaigns could request deferments for specific life circumstances. Deuteronomy broadens the exemption beyond the prior chapter's list (Deut 20:5–7) to include a full year for the newly married man "to be happy with his wife." The Hebrew *sameaḥ* here (piel infinitive, "to make happy") has been pressed in both conjugal and obligational directions: the husband is to spend the year bringing the wife joy.

The millstone-collateral law (v. 6) reflects the architecture of daily life. Domestic grain grinding was women's work done at the hand mill (cf. Mic 4:12; Matt 24:41); Iron Age basalt millstones have been excavated across the Shephelah and the hill country. The upper millstone (*rekeb*) was the lighter turning stone; the lower (*šekeb*) was embedded. Taking either as collateral effectively deprived a household of its daily bread — hence "taking a life in pledge."

The cloak-collateral rule (vv. 12–13) assumes the Iron Age outer garment that served as both day-clothing and night-blanket for the poor. Excavations at Masada, Cave of Letters, and various Judean sites preserve textile fragments that illuminate the garment's function. Exodus 22:26–27 treats the same provision; Amos 2:8 denounces its violation at the sanctuary. The hired-worker wage law (vv. 14–15) assumes day labor — *śākîr*, the hireling — paid at sunset. ANE parallels in Hammurabi §§257–258 fix laborer wages; the distinctively Deuteronomic move is the *moralized* urgency of paying before sundown "for he is poor and counts on it."

The individual-responsibility clause (v. 16) pushes against the collective-responsibility presumption of surrounding legal systems. Assyrian and Hittite law codes regularly punish families for an individual's offense (Hittite Laws §§1–4 on family compensation for homicide; Middle Assyrian Laws permitting wife-punishment for husband's offense in certain cases). Deuteronomy 24:16 is cited as decisive precedent in 2 Kings 14:6 (Amaziah sparing the children of his father's assassins) and forms the background for Ezekiel 18's extended theological development of the principle.

Key Hebrew terms: *sēper kĕrîtût* (certificate of divorce, v. 1, 3); *ʿerwat dābār* (literally "nakedness of a thing," the notorious "some indecency," v. 1); *šillēaḥ* (to send away, the standard verb for divorce, vv. 1, 3, 4); *yibbûm* — not here but shadowed by the absence of its reverse case; *ḥōbēl* (the one who takes pledge/collateral, vv. 6, 17); *śākîr* (hired laborer, v. 14); *gēr*, *yātôm*, *ʾalmānâ* (sojourner, orphan, widow — the triadic object of Deuteronomic protection, vv. 17, 19–21).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is a mosaic of casuistic and apodictic forms, punctuated by motive clauses that invoke historical memory ("you shall remember that you were a slave in Egypt," vv. 18, 22) and prophetic warning ("lest he cry against you to the LORD, and you be guilty of sin," v. 15). Patrick Miller's characterization of Deuteronomy as "preached law" is particularly apt here: the legal provisions are embedded in rhetorical exhortation.

The divorce law of vv. 1–4 is one continuous sentence in Hebrew — a single grammatical protasis running through verses 1–3 ("when a man … and if … and if …") with the apodosis delayed until verse 4 ("then her former husband … may not"). Every major English translation (including ESV) breaks the period into multiple sentences, obscuring that the divorce procedure of vv. 1–2 is *narrative description* of existing practice, not *legal prescription*. Deuteronomy 24:1 does not command or institute divorce; it assumes divorce occurs and legislates only the specific case of return-after-second-marriage. This grammatical reading, emphasized by Samuel Driver (ICC) and more recently by David Instone-Brewer (*Divorce and Remarriage in the Bible*), is crucial for interpreting Jesus's engagement with the text.

The chapter's arrangement has invited the usual structural debates. Calum Carmichael (*The Laws of Deuteronomy*; *Law and Narrative in the Bible*) argues that chapters 23–25 still belong under the seventh and eighth commandments in a Decalogue-keyed reading, with Deuteronomy 24 running through adultery-adjacent (divorce) and theft-adjacent (collateral, wages, kidnapping) concerns. Stephen Kaufman places the chapter under the ninth commandment as a cluster on witness and accurate judgment. Dennis Olson (*Deuteronomy and the Death of Moses*) reads the chapter thematically, centered on the protection of the vulnerable. Georg Braulik finds a chiastic structure around the individual-responsibility clause. The topical clustering is clear — marriage, life-sustaining goods, wages, justice for the vulnerable — even if the micro-ordering remains contested.

Literary devices include the remember-Egypt refrain (vv. 18, 22) as structural framing, the "that you be not guilty of sin" motive clauses, and the triadic formula *gēr-yātôm-ʾalmānâ* repeated three times in vv. 17, 19, 20–21. The gleaning laws (vv. 19–22) unfold in parallel triptych (sheaf, olive, grape) with nearly identical formulation, creating a liturgical cadence in the prose.

Textually, 4QDeut fragments preserve large portions of the chapter with minor orthographic variation. The Samaritan Pentateuch occasionally harmonizes motive clauses. The Septuagint's renderings have been important for early Christian interpretation — the LXX's *biblion apostasiou* for *sēper kĕrîtût* is the phrase Jesus uses in Matthew 19:7 and 5:31, ensuring the Deuteronomic text stands directly behind the Gospel debates.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish reception.** Philo (*Special Laws* 3) engages the divorce law briefly; Josephus (*Antiquities* 4) paraphrases it with apologetic reshaping. Qumran's *Damascus Document* (CD 4:20–5:2) cites divorce-and-remarriage material polemically against "builders of the wall" who take multiple wives; the *Temple Scroll* (11QT 56–57) extends stricter prohibitions on the king. The Qumran stance on divorce is debated — some read CD 4–5 as forbidding all remarriage even after a spouse's death; others as targeting polygamy specifically.

The entire Mishnah tractate *Gittin* works out the halakhah of the divorce certificate (the *get*) — format, witnesses, delivery, competencies, conditional provisions, and a remarkably detailed forensic apparatus. Tractate *Kiddushin* handles betrothal. Tractate *Yevamot* handles the levirate-release procedure. The famous Mishnah *Gittin* 9:10 preserves the House of Shammai / House of Hillel debate on the meaning of *ʿerwat dābār*: Shammai reads "nakedness of a thing" as restrictive ("except for sexual misconduct"); Hillel reads the two words distributively ("a matter of nakedness *or* any matter") and permits divorce for virtually any reason. Rabbi Akiva extends Hillel's position ("even if he finds a woman more beautiful"). Rashi follows the Shammai-Hillel dispute closely; Ibn Ezra attends to the philology of *ʿerwat dābār*; Ramban reads the prohibition on return (v. 4) as protecting the dignity of the woman and preventing wife-swapping arrangements. Maimonides (*Hilchot Gerushin* — the laws of divorce in the *Mishneh Torah*) produces the most influential medieval systematization, codifying *get* procedure in detail and treating the husband's coerced consent with characteristic analytic rigor.

Modern Jewish scholarship reads the law through multiple lenses. Tigay (*JPS Torah Commentary: Deuteronomy*) treats the text with ANE-comparative care, reading the *sēper kĕrîtût* as a legal instrument that gives the woman documented status — her evidence of changed status and her license to remarry — and protects her from informal repudiation in an otherwise patriarchal structure. Adele Berlin pays attention to the narrative grammar. David Weiss Halivni and the standard halakhic histories trace the rabbinic innovations that progressively made divorce harder for men to initiate unilaterally. Jonathan Sacks treats the chapter's humanitarian cluster (wages, collateral, gleaning) as the spine of Deuteronomy's social ethics. Amy-Jill Levine, writing for both Jewish and Christian audiences, has repeatedly cautioned Christian readers against caricaturing rabbinic divorce law as cavalier.

**Christian reception.** The interpretive history runs directly through the Gospels. Jesus's engagements with Deuteronomy 24:1 in Mark 10:2–12 and Matthew 19:3–9 are central to the Christian tradition's entire treatment of marriage. In Mark, Jesus responds to the question "is it lawful for a man to divorce his wife?" by pointing to the Genesis creation order ("from the beginning of creation") and declaring Moses's permission a concession to hardness of heart. In Matthew, Jesus adds the exception clause "except for *porneia*" (Matt 19:9; cf. 5:32) — the so-called Matthean exception that sides Jesus with the Shammaite reading against Hillel. Paul, in 1 Corinthians 7:10–16, adds what came to be called the Pauline privilege (permitting separation in the case of an unbelieving spouse who departs).

Origen reads the divorce law with allegorical flexibility. Augustine, in *De Bono Coniugali* and *On Adulterous Marriages*, constructs the classic Latin position: marriage is a sacrament (*sacramentum*) whose bond is indissoluble, such that even when separation is permitted for adultery, remarriage is not. Augustine's reading of Matthew 19 pushes against the plain sense of the exception clause in favor of the sacramental bond. Chrysostom's homily on divorce is more concessive than Augustine on some points. Aquinas (*ST* Supplement qq. 62–67) systematizes the Augustinian position: the Old Law permitted divorce by concession; the New Law restores the original intent. Medieval canon law worked out procedures for annulment (a determination that no valid marriage ever existed) as the Church's primary mechanism for marital dissolution.

The Reformation reopened the debate. Luther (*The Estate of Marriage*, 1522) reclassified marriage as a civil rather than sacramental matter and allowed divorce with remarriage for adultery and willful desertion. Calvin took a more restrictive position on remarriage than Luther but aligned with him against the sacramental Catholic view. The Anabaptists were often stricter, permitting separation but resisting remarriage in many circumstances. Milton's *The Doctrine and Discipline of Divorce* (1643–44) argued, on Deuteronomic grounds, for divorce on grounds of incompatibility — a position that scandalized contemporaries but anticipates modern Protestant practice. Reformed divorce law in early modern Europe (documented by John Witte Jr. in *From Sacrament to Contract*) progressively expanded permissible grounds.

Modern critical commentary is rich. David Instone-Brewer's *Divorce and Remarriage in the Bible* (2002) reconstructs the first-century halakhic context in which Jesus's teaching was received, arguing that the question in Mark 10 and Matthew 19 was not "is divorce ever permitted?" (the whole first-century Jewish world agreed it was, per Deut 24) but rather "is divorce permitted for any cause?" (the specifically Hillelite innovation). Instone-Brewer reads Jesus as siding with the Shammaite position but radicalizing it further by calling the Hillelite "any-cause" divorces invalid and thus rendering subsequent remarriages adulterous. Weinfeld, Nelson, McConville, Block, Wright, and Brueggemann all engage the text with care; Brueggemann emphasizes its place within Deuteronomy's humanitarian trajectory. Amy-Jill Levine writes against the tendency to caricature the Hillel position as laxity — the rabbinic system placed real procedural and financial barriers on divorce through the *ketubah* (marriage-contract) obligations. N. T. Wright locates Jesus's divorce teaching within a broader kingdom-ethic that prioritizes covenantal faithfulness.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Jewish and Christian traditions converge on reading Deuteronomy 24 as both ancient and contested. Both traditions acknowledge the Shammai-Hillel dispute as the interpretive background to Jesus's teaching. Both traditions have developed internal mechanisms that restrain arbitrary male divorce — the rabbinic *ketubah* financial obligations and *get* refusal procedures on the Jewish side, canon-law annulment processes and later civil-divorce debates on the Christian side. They diverge most sharply on remarriage after divorce: classical Jewish halakhah permits remarriage freely (excepting the v. 4 return-to-former-husband case); classical Catholic teaching forbids remarriage while the former spouse lives; Orthodox and Protestant positions fall along a spectrum. On the social-justice legislation of vv. 5–22 — wages, collateral, gleaning, widow-and-orphan protection — the two traditions converge almost completely, treating these laws as embodying the perennial moral demands of covenantal life. The story worth telling is that Deuteronomy 24 is the text that both Mishnah *Gittin* and Augustine's *De Bono Coniugali* argue from — the same chapter funding strikingly different regulatory worlds on marriage while funding a single moral tradition on economic compassion.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is divorce divinely permitted, divinely tolerated, or divinely regulated?** Three positions, each with serious standing:

*Position A — Concessionary.* Jesus's explicit characterization (Mark 10:5) — "because of your hardness of heart he wrote you this commandment" — supports the reading that Deuteronomy 24:1 is divine concession to human brokenness, not divine ideal. The Genesis 2 one-flesh vision stands as the norm; the Deuteronomic provision as accommodation. Augustine, Aquinas, and the Roman Catholic tradition build here.

*Position B — Protective regulation.* Driver, Instone-Brewer, Tigay, and much modern scholarship emphasize that the text's actual legal grammar regulates only the specific case of return-after-second-marriage (v. 4) while presupposing divorce as already practiced. The function of *sēper kĕrîtût* is to give the woman documented legal status — a protection against informal repudiation and a license to remarry. The text, on this reading, does not *permit* divorce (as if the hearers needed permission); it *disciplines* it by requiring writing, delivery, and finality.

*Position C — Trajectory toward reversal.* The canonical arc — from Deuteronomy 24 through Malachi 2:16 ("I hate divorce") to Jesus's appeal to Genesis 2 — reads the Deuteronomic provision as one moment in a movement whose terminus is the restoration of the creation ideal. N. T. Wright, McConville, and much evangelical scholarship operate here.

**Steelman for Position B (least intuitive to readers formed by the concessionary tradition).** The protective-regulation reading deserves a stronger hearing than it often gets. The text's single-sentence structure means the divorce *procedure* of vv. 1–2 is grammatically assumed, not legally enacted. What the law *does* is require a written instrument placed in the woman's hand and limit one pathological case of recycling. In an ANE world where men could effectively repudiate women by verbal dismissal leaving them without documented status, the *sēper kĕrîtût* is a woman's legal safeguard. Jesus's "hardness of heart" reading does not deny this function — he addresses the *cause* (hardness of heart necessitated legal accommodation) while the text addresses the *protection* (given that hardness of heart produces divorce, let the woman at least have a document). Both readings can stand.

**Where this leaves us.** The three positions are not mutually exclusive but layer upon one another. Deuteronomy 24 is a divine concession to the reality of marital breakdown (A); it functions as protective legal regulation for the vulnerable party (B); and it belongs within a canonical trajectory that points back to the creation ideal and forward to its recovery (C). To emphasize only one of these is to flatten the text's complexity.

**Tension 2: Individual or communal moral accountability?** The non-transferability clause (v. 16) — "fathers shall not be put to death for children, nor children for fathers" — sits in apparent tension with other biblical materials affirming intergenerational consequence (Exod 20:5's "visiting iniquity on the third and fourth generation"; Lam 5:7's "our fathers sinned, and are no more; and we bear their iniquities"). Three readings:

*Position A — Juridical distinction.* Block, Wright, and Nelson read v. 16 as specifically forbidding *judicial* punishment across generations while leaving intact the observed reality of intergenerational consequence. The distinction is between what courts may do (only individual accountability) and what social and providential systems inescapably produce (multi-generational effects of sin). Ezekiel 18 explicitly works out this distinction.

*Position B — Progressive individuation.* Critical scholars (Weinfeld; Nelson) read the Deuteronomic legislation as part of a developmental trajectory within Israelite law away from collective liability and toward individual responsibility — a trajectory that continues through Jeremiah 31 ("no more shall they say, 'The fathers have eaten sour grapes'") and Ezekiel 18, culminating in the eschatological New Covenant.

*Position C — Covenantal solidarity retained.* Brueggemann and other corporate-reading commentators push back against too quickly resolving the tension toward individualism. The text prohibits judicial vengeance across generations but does not dissolve covenantal solidarity. Israel stands as a people before YHWH; Achan's family perishes (Josh 7); Rahab's family is spared (Josh 6) — the text keeps both individual and collective frames in productive tension.

**Steelman for Position C (least intuitive to readers formed by Enlightenment individualism).** The covenantal-solidarity reading has this going for it: the chapter's triadic focus on *gēr*, *yātôm*, *ʾalmānâ* presupposes precisely the corporate liability that v. 16 restricts — the orphan suffers for the father's death, the widow for the husband's death, the sojourner for the homeland's catastrophe. The individual-responsibility clause protects the courts from inflicting what life itself already inflicts; it does not deny corporate solidarity but frames it. Modern Western readings eager to find proto-individualism in v. 16 often miss that the very next verses (17–22) are communal-ethics legislation for the corporate body.

**Where this leaves us.** The Deuteronomic text holds individual judicial accountability and covenantal solidarity together. The tension between v. 16 and Exodus 20:5 is not a contradiction but a calibrated duality: courts deal in individual guilt; life and history move in corporate arcs; the covenant relates to both simultaneously.

**Unresolved question.** The divorce-certificate law is placed near wage-protection and widow-protection and collateral-protection. What should we make of a chapter that puts the legal dissolution of marriage in the same rhetorical neighborhood as protecting the day-laborer from unpaid wages and the widow from having her garment taken? Is Deuteronomy 24 quietly insisting that marital breakdown, like unpaid wages, is a *social-justice* issue — not merely a private-ethics issue — because the wife is typically the economically vulnerable party whom the law must protect at the moment of her greatest exposure?

## The Thread

Four cross-references worth carrying with this chapter:

1. **Deut 24:1 and Matthew 19:3–9 / Mark 10:2–12.** Jesus's engagement with this verse is the most-cited Gospel interaction with a single Deuteronomic text. The Matthean version explicitly frames the question in Hillelite terms ("for any cause") and renders Jesus's answer in Shammaite form (except for *porneia*) — a reading that makes more sense when the grammatical observation of Driver and Instone-Brewer is held in mind: Deuteronomy 24:1 never commanded divorce, merely assumed it. Jesus addresses the assumption.

2. **Deut 24:7 and the Exodus-Genesis kidnapping narrative.** The death penalty for kidnapping "a brother" (v. 7) evokes Joseph's abduction by his brothers (Gen 37). The Deuteronomic law retrospectively condemns what the patriarchal narrative showed happening within Israel's own founding family — a legal intervention against the archetypal Israelite sin.

3. **Deut 24:14–15 and James 5:4.** The wage-on-the-same-day law is directly invoked by James: "the wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you kept back by fraud, are crying out against you, and the cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord of hosts." James reads the Deuteronomic provision as permanently binding ethics, not superseded ceremonial law.

4. **Deut 24:19–22 and Ruth 2.** The gleaning provisions undergird the Ruth narrative. Ruth, a Moabite widow — herself near the convergence of the excluded (Deut 23:3) and the protected (Deut 24:19–21) — gleans in Boaz's field under the explicit protection of these laws. Boaz's generosity toward her is not free-form charity but a deliberately maximal application of the Deuteronomic statute. The redemption narrative begins as a worked example of Torah-observance, and that Torah-observance widens precisely the membership boundary the previous chapter had drawn.
