# Hebrews 9 — The Earthly Sanctuary, the Heavenly Entry, the Once-for-All Sacrifice

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The anonymous author of Hebrews — Origen's "in truth, God knows" (Eusebius, *HE* 6.25.14) holds for chapter 9 as fully as anywhere — turns in this chapter from the *covenant* contrast of chapter 8 to the *cultic* contrast that the covenant comparison was always heading toward. The proposals of authorship (Apollos, Barnabas, Priscilla, Silas, Clement, Luke) all bow before what the chapter itself reveals: an author so saturated in tabernacle architecture, sacrificial procedure, and Yom Kippur liturgy that he can recite them in compressed Greek without footnoting his sources. He writes for a community Paul Ellingworth (NIGTC) and William L. Lane (WBC) place in Rome, Jewish-Christian, fluent enough in the Levitical system to recognize abbreviated descriptions. The genre remains *λόγος παρακλήσεως* (13:22) — synagogue homily.

Dating sharpens still further at chapter 9. The present-tense verbs of 9:6-7 — "the priests *go* (εἰσίασιν) regularly into the first section," "into the second only the high priest *goes* (εἰσέρχεται), and he but once a year, and not without taking blood, which he *offers* (προσφέρει)" — read with peculiar force if the second temple's priests are still going in as the author writes. Lane and Cockerill (NICNT) read this as decisive evidence for a pre-70 CE composition. Attridge (Hermeneia) and Koester (Anchor Yale) reply that the author argues from the *tabernacle* of Exodus and Leviticus, not the contemporary temple, and that present-tense reference to scriptural cult is a normal Hellenistic-Jewish convention (Philo and Josephus do the same after 70). Yet the absence of any reference to the temple's destruction — a fact that would have settled the author's argument like a thunderclap — weighs heavily for the earlier dating.

The architectural description of 9:2-5 contains a famous historical wrinkle. Hebrews places the *χρυσοῦν θυμιατήριον* — the "golden altar of incense" — in the *Holy of Holies*, behind the second curtain. Exodus 30:6 places the incense altar *in front of* the curtain, in the Holy Place. Several explanations have been offered: (i) *thymiatērion* could mean "censer" (a portable incense pan) rather than "altar," and the high priest carried such a censer into the inner sanctuary on Yom Kippur (Lev 16:12-13); (ii) the author may be following an alternative tradition like 1 Kings 6:22 LXX, which says the incense altar "belongs to" the inner sanctuary; (iii) the author follows the apocalyptic-temple traditions of the period (the Temple Scroll 11Q19 and Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice 4QShirShabb associate the incense altar liturgically with the inner sanctuary even when spatially distinct). Attridge favors the third reading. Lane the second. The point is not error but that Hebrews thinks of the inner sanctuary as the locus of the highest priestly act, which incense materially enables.

The Yom Kippur framework is unmistakable behind 9:6-14. The Mishnah's tractate Yoma — compiled c. 200 CE but preserving liturgy actively practiced in the temple — describes the high priest's solitary annual entry into the Holy of Holies (Yoma 5), the carrying of blood from the slaughtered bull and goat (Yoma 4), the sprinkling of the mercy seat (Yoma 5:3), and the confession over the scapegoat (Yoma 6). Hebrews 9 compresses all this into a single sentence ("but into the second only the high priest goes, and he but once a year, and not without taking blood") and then makes Christ's heavenly entry the *eschatological Yom Kippur*. The Sifra on Leviticus and the Mekilta de-Rabbi Ishmael are the corresponding rabbinic-exegetical streams the author is implicitly arguing within.

The Greek manuscript tradition for chapter 9 is unusually rich. P46 (c. 200 CE) preserves the chapter with only minor variants. Sinaiticus (א), Vaticanus (B), and Alexandrinus (A) are unanimous in the key Christological terms — *ἐφάπαξ* (9:12, "once for all"), *αἰωνίαν λύτρωσιν* ("eternal redemption"), and *πραγμάτων ἐλεγχος*-adjacent vocabulary. The most-discussed textual variant in the chapter is at 9:14, where some witnesses read "purify *our* conscience" and others "*your* conscience" — a difference of the Greek pronoun ἡμῶν vs. ὑμῶν, with negligible theological weight. The chapter's textual integrity is not in serious dispute.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is structured as a controlled descent and ascent. Verses 1-10 walk into the earthly tabernacle from outer to inner court, naming furnishings (lampstand, showbread table, incense altar, ark, mercy seat, cherubim) and procedures (the priests' daily service in the Holy Place, the high priest's annual entry into the Most Holy Place). Verses 11-14 then pivot upward and inward: Christ has entered the *true* sanctuary, *not made with hands*, by *his own* blood. Verses 15-22 explain *why* blood is needed at all, with a notorious word-play on *διαθήκη*. Verses 23-28 close with a triple "once" that organizes the entire eschatology: heavenly things purified by *better* sacrifices; Christ's *once-for-all* entry; man appointed to die *once*; Christ offered *once* to bear sins, returning *not* to deal with sin but to save those waiting.

The Greek registers are dense. *Καταπέτασμα* (curtain, veil) appears twice in the chapter (9:3 of the inner curtain, and implicitly behind the imagery of 10:20). *Ἐφάπαξ* and *ἅπαξ* — "once for all" and "once" — are the chapter's drumbeat (9:7 *hapax* once a year; 9:12 *ephapax* into the holy places; 9:26, 27, 28 *hapax* three times in succession). The lexical contrast is exact: the high priest enters *hapax* (once each year, repeatedly), Christ enters *ephapax* (once and for all, never to be repeated). *Συνείδησις* (conscience) at 9:9 and 9:14 names what the Levitical sacrifices *cannot* purify — the inner faculty of moral self-perception. *Διαθήκη* (covenant/will/testament) carries the chapter's most contested rhetoric in 9:15-17. *Ἄφεσις* (release, forgiveness) closes 9:22 with the chapter's strongest claim: "without blood-shedding there is no *aphesis*."

The **διαθήκη word-play of 9:15-17** is one of the most discussed grammatical-rhetorical passages in New Testament scholarship. Greek *διαθήκη* renders Hebrew *berit* (covenant) throughout the LXX; in standard Greco-Roman legal usage, however, *diathēkē* means "last will and testament." The author exploits both senses. "Therefore he is the *mediator of a new covenant* (διαθήκης καινῆς μεσίτης), so that those who are called may receive the promised eternal inheritance (κληρονομίας), since a death has occurred that redeems them from the transgressions committed under the first covenant. For where a *διαθήκη* is involved, the death of the *διαθεμένου* must be established. For a *διαθήκη* takes effect only at death (ἐπὶ νεκροῖς), since it is not in force as long as the one who made it is alive." Here *diathēkē* shifts unmistakably toward "testament": testaments take effect only when the testator dies. Attridge (Hermeneia) is emphatic that *diathēkē* must mean "testament" in 9:16-17, since covenants are not commonly said to require the death of their maker. Lane (WBC) and Cockerill (NICNT) reply that even within the OT, covenants involved sacrificial deaths (the cut animals of Gen 15:9-18, the blood of the covenant of Exod 24, on which Heb 9:18-22 immediately draws), so the death of the *covenant-maker* may be a metaphorical extension of the death of the covenant-victim. Most modern scholars (Ellingworth, O'Brien, Attridge, Johnson) accept that 9:16-17 leans testamentary while 9:18 immediately swings back to covenantal. The author plays the double meaning deliberately; the word-play is rhetoric, not confusion.

The **9:22 "without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness"** statement is the strongest formulation of cultic-blood necessity in the New Testament. *Χωρὶς αἱματεκχυσίας οὐ γίνεται ἄφεσις* — "without blood-shedding there comes no release." Attridge, Lane, and Vanhoye all note that Heb 9:22 is not formally a citation of any Old Testament verse; it is a summary inference from Lev 17:11 ("the life of the flesh is in the blood, and I have given it to you upon the altar to make atonement for your souls") and the practice of Lev 16. The verse will become — across two thousand years of atonement theology — the load-bearing pillar of every "blood is necessary for salvation" argument.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Chapter 9 is the New Testament chapter where atonement theology converges. Every major Christian theory of how the cross saves traces its language back to verses in this chapter, and the differences between those theories are not academic — they shape how Christians worship, what they believe sacraments do, and how they read suffering.

**Anselm's satisfaction theory** (*Cur Deus Homo*, 1098) reads 9:14, 9:22, and 9:26 as the New Testament's fullest exposition of why a divine-human victim is required: sin offends God's infinite honor; only an offering of infinite worth can satisfy that offense; only a God-man can offer it. Anselm's argument is pre-juridical (the language is honor and debt, not punishment), but the structure becomes the spine of medieval Catholic atonement, and Aquinas in *ST* III qq. 46-49 extends and refines it. Trent's Session 22 (1562) on the sacrifice of the Mass relies on Heb 9:11-14 to argue that Christ's once-for-all sacrifice is *re-presented* (not repeated) in every Eucharist — the priest offers the same victim by the same priest in an unbloody manner.

**Calvin's penal substitution** (*Institutes* 2.16-17) reads the same verses through legal rather than feudal categories: Christ as the substitute who bears the divine wrath legally due to sinners. The Westminster Confession 8.4-5, the Heidelberg Catechism Q. 37-44, Charles Hodge, and modern Reformed dogmatics (Wayne Grudem, John Murray) develop this reading, which has dominated Anglo-American evangelical Protestantism. On this reading 9:22 is not merely descriptive but normative: God's righteousness *requires* blood-shedding for the remission of sin, and Christ's blood-shedding fulfills that requirement once for all.

**Gustaf Aulén's Christus Victor** (*Christus Victor*, 1931) reread the New Testament and the Greek Fathers (Irenaeus, Athanasius, Gregory of Nyssa) and argued that the dominant patristic atonement theory was neither satisfaction nor substitution but cosmic victory: Christ's death-and-resurrection defeated sin, death, and the devil. Heb 2:14-15 is Aulén's most-cited verse, but Heb 9:14's "*through the eternal Spirit* offered himself without blemish" — and 9:26's "to put away sin" — fit Christus Victor as well as any substitutionary frame. Eastern Orthodoxy has always read the cross primarily through this lens (Athanasius *On the Incarnation*; the Paschal Anaphora).

**Abelard's moral influence** reading (*Commentary on Romans* 3) treats the cross as supreme manifestation of divine love that transforms us morally. **Irenaeus's recapitulation** (*Adversus Haereses* 3.18-22) treats Christ as retracing and reversing Adam's path. **René Girard's scapegoat-dismantling** reading (*I See Satan Fall Like Lightning*, 2001) treats the cross as exposing and ending the scapegoat mechanism — Christ is the scapegoat whose unjust death finally reveals the whole machinery of victim-making. **Kathryn Tanner's** *Christ the Key* (2010) reads atonement as union and transfer — Christ takes our humanity into himself and gives us his divine sonship, atonement as participation rather than transaction. **Karl Barth's CD IV/1 §59** ("The Judge Judged in Our Place") preserves penal-substitutionary structure while embedding it in a Trinitarian-covenantal frame. **Fleming Rutledge's** *The Crucifixion* (2015) holds these together as overlapping metaphors, refusing to reduce atonement to a single theory.

**N.T. Wright** (*Hebrews for Everyone*; *The Day the Revolution Began*, 2016; *Surprised by Hope*, 2007) reads chapter 9 as the New Testament's most architecturally precise account of the cross as the true Day of Atonement: Jesus enters "once for all" (*ephapax*, 9:12) into "the greater and more perfect tent... not of this creation" — heaven itself, God's own dwelling — carrying not the blood of bulls and goats but his own, and there obtains "eternal redemption." For Wright the point is that the temple and its sacrifices were always pointing forward to this; the new covenant Jeremiah promised is sealed in this blood, and 9:14's purification of the *conscience* shows the goal is not ritual cleanness but a renewed people freed to "serve the living God." And 9:28 is, on his reading, resurrection-shaped hope, not escapist heaven: Christ "will appear a second time" to "those who are eagerly waiting for him" — the consummation of the rescue already begun, when heaven and earth are joined. Wright is wary of squeezing the chapter into a single mechanical atonement formula; he reads it as the climax of Israel's story, the place where the creator's covenant purpose is carried through by the Messiah-priest.

The most consequential recent monograph is **David Moffitt's *Atonement and the Logic of Resurrection in the Epistle to the Hebrews*** (2011, revised 2022). Moffitt argues that the heavenly *entry* of Heb 9:11-12 is not simultaneous with the cross. The cross is the *slaughter* of the victim (the prerequisite); the *priestly act* — the sprinkling of blood in the Most Holy Place — is the resurrected, ascended Christ's appearance before God. Moffitt reads 9:24 ("Christ has entered... into heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God on our behalf") as describing a present heavenly liturgy, not a finished past event. This contradicts the standard Reformation atonement structure that locates the priestly act on the cross itself, and it pushes Hebrews closer to the patristic-Eastern emphasis that resurrection is *part of* atonement, not its sequel. Moffitt's reading has not won universal assent (O'Brien, Cockerill resist it), but it has reshaped Hebrews scholarship.

The **Eucharistic question** sits inside this same chapter. Heb 9:25-26 ("Nor was it to offer himself repeatedly... he has appeared once for all") is read by the Reformed tradition (Calvin *Institutes* 4.18; Heidelberg Catechism Q. 78-80, which calls the Roman Mass "an accursed idolatry") as decisively excluding any repetition of the sacrifice. The Catholic answer (Trent Session 22; CCC 1362-1372; *Mediator Dei* of Pius XII, 1947) is that the Mass *re-presents* (not *re-immolates*) the once-for-all sacrifice — the same victim, the same priest, the same offering, made present sacramentally. The Lutheran *Augsburg Confession* 10 and the *Formula of Concord* 7 affirm a real sacramental union ("in, with, and under" the bread and wine) without holding the Mass to be a propitiatory sacrifice. The Anglican *Articles of Religion* XXVIII-XXXI affirm a "real spiritual presence" while rejecting Tridentine eucharistic sacrifice. Eastern Orthodoxy's anaphoras describe the Eucharist as the heavenly liturgy made present — closer to the Catholic position but with different conceptual machinery. Anabaptist communion (Schleitheim Confession, 1527) is purely memorial. What does Heb 9's *ephapax* require? The honest answer is that it requires the cross to be unrepeatable, which all parties affirm; whether the Eucharist *is* a repetition (which all parties deny) or a *re-presentation* (Catholic), a *real participation* (Lutheran/Orthodox/Anglican), or a *memorial* (Reformed/Anabaptist) is a downstream theological question that 9:25-26 alone cannot settle.

The Jewish tradition that Hebrews 9 implicitly addresses has its own sustained life. The Mishnah Yoma — the very Yom Kippur liturgy 9:6-7 condenses — is not merely preserved as halakhah for a future temple; it is read aloud as part of the Yom Kippur Avodah service in every synagogue still today. Jacob Milgrom's *Leviticus* commentary (Anchor Yale, 1991) reads the Levitical blood theology not as transactional but as *purificatory*: blood is the ritual detergent that cleanses sancta from the contagion of sin. Susan Haber's *They Shall Purify Themselves* extends this purity-system reading and argues that Hebrews engages it within rather than against. Philo's *De Vita Mosis* 2.95-108 and Josephus's *Antiquities* 3.6-8 give the contemporary Jewish-Hellenistic tabernacle interpretations the author of Hebrews would have known.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

The author intends to demonstrate, by an *a fortiori* argument from the Yom Kippur liturgy itself, that Christ's once-for-all heavenly entry has accomplished what the annual entry could only point toward. Two tensions matter most.

**Tension 1: The atonement — where and how does Christ's sacrifice work?** Heb 9 is the chapter where every theory of atonement must do its work. The Anselmian-satisfaction reading hears 9:22 as the bedrock of why infinite-honor restitution requires a divine-human victim. The Calvinist-penal-substitutionary reading hears the same verse as the legal axiom that demands wrath-bearing. Aulén's Christus Victor hears 9:14's "through the eternal Spirit" as the cosmic-pneumatic frame that makes the cross-and-resurrection a single act of liberation. Abelard hears 9:14's exemplary self-offering. Irenaeus hears recapitulation. Girard hears the scapegoat mechanism named and ended. Tanner hears participation. Rutledge holds the metaphors together. Moffitt's recent argument disturbs the whole conversation by relocating the priestly act *after* the cross — at the heavenly entry of the resurrected Christ. The exegetical pressure is real: 9:11-12 says Christ "*entered* the holy places" (an event), and 9:24 says he is now "appearing in the presence of God" (an ongoing reality). What reading honors all of this? Probably none alone. The chapter resists being collapsed into a single theory; the author piles up sacrificial, juridical, cosmic, and participation language without choosing among them.

**Tension 2: Eucharist and the once-for-all sacrifice.** *Ephapax* is unconditional. Christ's offering was, is, and will be unrepeatable. The Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, Anglican, and Reformed traditions all agree on this. They divide on whether the Eucharist is a *re-presentation* of that sacrifice (Catholic Mass), a *real participation* in it (Orthodox synaxis, Lutheran sacramental union, Anglican real spiritual presence), or a *memorial* of it (Reformed, Anabaptist). The dividing question is not "is the cross repeated?" but "what *is* the eucharistic action?" Trent Session 22 said it most carefully: "the same Christ who offered himself once in a bloody manner on the altar of the cross is contained and offered in an unbloody manner" in the Mass. Heidelberg Q. 80 said it most polemically: the popish Mass is "a denial of the once-for-all sacrifice." A serious reading of 9:25-26 requires the *non-repetition* claim; a serious reading of 1 Cor 11 and the institution narratives requires *real* sacramental presence in *some* sense; the dispute is over what "in some sense" is doing.

**Closing question:** If Heb 9:22 means "without blood-shedding there is no release of sins," is this an *eternal axiom of divine justice* (the satisfaction-substitution stream), an *internal logic of the covenant cult* now consummated (the participation-recapitulation stream), or a *culturally located scriptural pattern* God enters and ends (the Girardian-apocalyptic stream)? The chapter does not tell us which; but how we answer determines what we believe the cross *is*.

## The Thread

Hebrews 9 has walked us into the earthly tabernacle, named its furnishings, watched the high priest enter the Holy of Holies "once a year, and not without blood," and then — in a single hinge — pivoted upward and inward to a Christ who has entered the *true* sanctuary not made with hands, by his own blood, *once for all*. The chapter has framed atonement (9:14, 22, 26), Eucharist (9:25-26), and second-coming hope (9:27-28) inside a single Yom Kippur typology. Chapter 10 will draw the consequences: a single offering has perfected forever those being sanctified; the curtain into the holy places is now Christ's flesh; therefore *let us draw near, hold fast, stir one another up* — and beware deliberately throwing this provision away, because there remains for that case no other sacrifice for sins.
