# Deuteronomy 15: There Shall Be No Poor Among You

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Deuteronomy 15 stands at the social-economic heart of the Deuteronomic Code (chs. 12–26), and it stands in a particular historical moment: Moses, on the plains of Moab, is addressing a generation about to organize its own land economy for the first time. The Hebrew title *Dĕbārîm* — "words" — names the book's form (Moses's speech), but this chapter is something more specific: a welfare statute. It legislates three interlocking institutions — debt release, liberal lending, and slave release — and closes with a firstborn-dedication rule that ties the whole economy to cultic centralization.

The debt-release law of vv. 1–11 introduces the sabbatical *šĕmiṭṭâ* — literally, a "dropping" or "letting fall." The seventh-year release of loans is the text's distinctive Deuteronomic move; Leviticus 25 emphasizes the jubilee and the land's sabbath; Exodus 21 gives the six-year slave law; Deuteronomy 15 consolidates all three into a single institutional architecture. The political-material stakes are high: debt slavery was the dominant mode of social stratification in the ancient Near East, and the sabbatical release cuts at its root.

The ANE parallels are dense and illuminating. Mesopotamian kings periodically declared *mīšarum* ("equity") edicts — debt cancellations and manumission of those enslaved for debt — especially at accessions or jubilee years. Ammi-saduqa's edict (c. 1646 BCE) is the best-preserved example: debts are cancelled, Akkadian debt-slaves are freed, real-estate forfeitures reversed. The cognate term *andurārum* ("release, freedom") appears throughout Akkadian administrative texts and is the semantic cousin of the Hebrew *dĕrôr* that Leviticus 25:10 proclaims at the jubilee. Moshe Weinfeld's *Social Justice in Ancient Israel* (1995) shows how deeply Deuteronomy draws on this *mīšarum* tradition. The Code of Hammurabi §117 caps debt-service at three years; Deuteronomy 15 matches it for slave release (six years of service, release in the seventh) in a way that was probably *stricter* in its social effect because it is statutory rather than royal discretion.

The slave-release law (vv. 12–18) operates with specific Hebrew terms. **עֶבֶד עִבְרִי** (*ʿebed ʿibrî*, "Hebrew slave") — whether *ʿibrî* is an ethnonym or the older ANE social category *ḫapiru* (landless, stateless laborers) is debated; Nahum Sarna argued for the latter, Tigay for the former. Deuteronomy 15 makes the term explicitly covenantal: the released person is your "brother." **תְּהָדְרֶנּוּ** ("furnish him liberally," v. 14, from a root meaning to decorate or honor) is unique here; the master does not merely free but endows. The verb's force is that the release must not be symbolic. Compare Exodus 21, which says only that the slave "goes out free for nothing"; Deuteronomy adds the endowment. Lev 25:42–43 frames the same institution as a prohibition against buying a fellow Israelite at all.

The structural tension between v. 4 ("there will be no poor among you") and v. 11 ("there will never cease to be poor in the land") has generated two millennia of commentary. Dennis Olson (*Deuteronomy and the Death of Moses*, 1994) calls this the chapter's "idealistic-realistic" duality. The rabbis read v. 4 as conditional ("if only you will strictly obey," v. 5) and v. 11 as descriptive of the covenant-breaking reality. Tigay sees the pair as programmatic and pastoral respectively — the goal is the elimination of poverty, the command is responsive mercy while the goal is unmet. The text does not resolve the tension; it lives in it.

The *prosbul* workaround (Mishnah *Sheviit* 10:3–4; Bavli *Gittin* 36a) is the most documented implementation history. Hillel the Elder, near the end of the Second Temple period, observed that v. 9's warning ("take care lest there be an unworthy thought in your heart, and you say, 'the seventh year, the year of release, is near'") had become self-fulfilling: lenders refused to lend in the sixth year. His solution was a legal fiction — a document transferring private debts to the court, which as a non-private entity was not subject to *šĕmiṭṭâ*. Maimonides codifies this in *Hilchot Shemitah ve-Yovel* 9. Ethically mixed: the *prosbul* preserves credit markets but guts the law's redistributive force. Saul Lieberman, Shmuel Safrai, and more recently David Kraemer have traced the rabbinic ambivalence.

Three Hebrew terms organize the chapter. **שְׁמִטָּה** (*šĕmiṭṭâ*, "release," vv. 1, 2, 9), the sabbatical release, with its cognate verb *šāmôṭ*. **אֶבְיוֹן** (*ʾebyôn*, "needy," vv. 4, 7, 9, 11), the deep-need poor (distinct from the milder *ʿānî*). **אֲחִיךָ** (*ʾāḥîkā*, "your brother," appearing repeatedly) — the chapter refuses any other word for the poor person. The whole welfare ethic hangs on kinship construction.

The firstborn law (vv. 19–23) ties the chapter back to Deut 12's centralization: the firstborn is eaten "before YHWH" at the chosen place, yearly. Blemished firstborns may be eaten in the towns, like wild game (gazelle or deer — the same wild animals of Deut 14:5). This is the bridge from social ethics to cultic practice: the same people who release debts also feast before YHWH.

Dating: the de Wette/Wellhausen line places the Deuteronomic Code at or near Josiah's reform, reading the sabbatical law as a late innovation; the Kitchen/Kline line, based on Hittite treaty structure, places the text earlier. The *mīšarum* parallels are at home in the second millennium, but the institutional regularity of the seventh year is distinctive. Manuscript evidence (4QDeut^j includes fragments near ch. 15; LXX, Nash Papyrus) shows a stable text.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is legal code in speech form. The chapter is rhetorically heated — more hortatory than procedural. Every law comes with a reason (the motive clause, a Deuteronomic signature), and the reasons escalate.

Literary structure. The chapter splits into four units: debt release (vv. 1–11), slave release (vv. 12–18), firstborn dedication (vv. 19–23), and inside vv. 1–11 a tight two-part structure — positive commandment (1–6) and pastoral expansion (7–11). The pastoral expansion is extraordinary: v. 9's "beware lest there be a *dābār bĕlîyaʿal* in your heart" ("unworthy/base thought") is one of the Torah's sharpest penetrations of inner life. The base thought is specifically a *calculation*: I will not lend because the release is near, and I will lose. The text names the temptation that follows from its own legislation.

The parallelism with Leviticus 25 is programmatic. Leviticus 25 centers on the *yôbēl* (jubilee, fiftieth year) and treats the seventh year as the land's sabbath (fields lie fallow); Deuteronomy 15 treats the seventh year as a debt release and emphasizes persons. Exodus 21 gives the slave release as a *mishpat* (case law); Deuteronomy 15 gives it as a covenant obligation with a remembered history ("you were a slave in Egypt," v. 15). The three texts are mutually illuminating: Exodus sets the case, Leviticus sets the land, Deuteronomy sets the heart.

The slave-law literary unit (vv. 12–18) advances step by step: release (v. 12) — endowment (vv. 13–14) — memory of Egypt (v. 15) — voluntary servitude (vv. 16–17) — reassurance to the master (v. 18). The door-and-awl ritual (v. 17) is the same ritual as in Exodus 21:6, but Deuteronomy relocates it: Exodus stages it at the sanctuary ("before God," *hāʾĕlōhîm*); Deuteronomy stages it at the master's door. The shift matches Deuteronomy's desacralization of ordinary life (Weinfeld's term) — cultic acts migrate from local shrines to the household, and what remains at the sanctuary is national feasting.

Key literary device: **the motivating memory**. "You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt" (v. 15) roots the ethics of release in the ethics of exodus. The same clause appears in Deut 5:15 (sabbath), 16:12 (festival), 24:18 and 24:22 (gleaning, wage justice). Deuteronomy gathers its ethics around one historical memory.

Textual variants. The LXX smooths some idiomatic turns and in v. 18 gives "for according to the annual wage of a hired servant" — close to the MT's "at half the cost of a hired worker," a phrase whose exact sense is debated (double the work? or half the cost, i.e., twice the value?). The Peshitta has "double." The rabbinic reading (and the modern consensus, Craigie, Tigay) is "double" — the slave has produced twice the labor of a hired worker in those six years, and therefore the master loses nothing by releasing him.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish tradition.** The Second Temple evidence is sparse but suggestive. Josephus's *Antiquities* 3.281 treats the sabbatical release as a law still observed in his day. Qumran community documents (the Rule of the Community, 1QS) treat debt and property in their own sectarian way; the Temple Scroll's sabbatical provisions are partial but echo the biblical text.

Rabbinic development is rich. Mishnah *Sheviit* (the entire tractate) treats the sabbatical year of the land and debt together. The *prosbul* in *Sheviit* 10:3–4 is the single most documented rabbinic legal innovation. Bavli *Gittin* 36a explains Hillel's reasoning: he saw that people were transgressing the positive command to lend (v. 8) because they feared the release, and he judged this worse than a workaround. Rashi on Deut 15:2 clarifies the mechanics: only loans, not wages or deposits, are cancelled. Ibn Ezra reads v. 11 to qualify v. 4: poverty is the norm to which generosity responds. Nachmanides sees in v. 4 an eschatological promise. Maimonides in *Hilchot Matnot Aniyim* 10 gives the famous eight levels of charity — the highest being not charity at all, but a loan or employment that prevents the person from becoming poor; this flows directly from Deut 15:8.

Sifre *Re'eh* §116–118 builds the classical midrashic reading: v. 11's "you shall open wide your hand" becomes the basis for the rabbinic duty of *tzedakah*. In the modern period, Nechama Leibowitz's *Studies in Devarim* reads the sabbatical as an education of the heart; Jonathan Sacks in *To Heal a Fractured World* makes Deut 15 the centerpiece of his social ethics; Jon Levenson in *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* treats the sabbatical rhythm as a cosmological claim — time itself is structured toward release. Abraham Joshua Heschel's *The Sabbath* extends this: the seventh year is the sabbath's great echo. Weinfeld's *Social Justice in Ancient Israel* sets Deut 15 within the *mīšarum* tradition and argues that the Hebrew innovation is to statutize what was royal prerogative — to take debt release out of the king's hands and give it to the calendar.

**Christian tradition.** Patristic reception reads Deuteronomy 15 through the lens of almsgiving. Origen's *Homilies* allegorize the seventh-year release as the soul's release from the debts of sin. Chrysostom's *Homilies on 2 Corinthians*, and his famous sermons on Lazarus and the rich man, quote the chapter's v. 7–11 as direct commandment. Augustine treats the law as fulfilled in Christ's release from sin's debt, but also as ongoing exhortation: the poor are always with you (a line Jesus quotes, in Mark 14:7, from Deut 15:11), and the church's obligation is perpetual.

Medieval Christianity systematizes. Aquinas in *Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 32 (on almsgiving) leans on Deut 15:7–11; in q. 66 on restitution and q. 77 on usury, the sabbatical release becomes the typological ground for condemning interest-taking on the poor. *ST* I-II q. 105 classifies the sabbatical release as a judicial precept, fulfilled but morally instructive. The medieval canon law of usury (Lateran III, 1179; Vienne, 1311) builds partly on the Deuteronomic debt-release framework.

Reformation readings. Luther's commentary on Deuteronomy reads ch. 15 as civil law for Israel, but also as a gospel illustration: Christ is the true seventh-year release. Calvin's *Commentary on the Harmony* treats the sabbatical as a civil law that does not bind Christian magistrates directly but carries a moral principle — the obligation of structural mercy. Calvin, unusually among his contemporaries, was willing to permit low-rate lending but condemned predatory lending by direct appeal to Deut 15.

The radical Reformation and later English dissent read it more directly. Samuel Rutherford's *Lex Rex* (1644) quotes Deuteronomic social laws against royal absolutism. Gerard Winstanley's Digger writings appeal to the sabbatical year as a warrant for commons. John Owen preaches the chapter as grace-in-law.

Modern and contemporary. Critical scholarship: Weinfeld, Tigay, Richard Nelson (OTL), Christopher Wright (*Old Testament Ethics for the People of God*, 2004 — whose "paradigm approach" reads Deut 15 as a model for contemporary economics), Walter Brueggemann (*Deuteronomy*, AOTC), Patrick Miller (IBC), Daniel Block (NIVAC). The most influential contemporary reception is Ched Myers's *The Biblical Vision of Sabbath Economics* (2001), which makes Deut 15 and Lev 25 the axis of a Christian economic ethic. The **Jubilee 2000** debt-cancellation movement, formally launched in the mid-1990s and endorsed by John Paul II's *Tertio Millennio Adveniente* (1994), appealed explicitly to Deut 15 and Lev 25 to call for the cancellation of impoverished-nation debt. Michael Walzer's *Exodus and Revolution* (1985) places the sabbatical institutions in a line of liberation traditions.

**Convergence and divergence.** Jewish and Christian traditions converge on Deut 15's central claim: poverty is a scandal to covenant, and the community has structural obligation, not merely charitable impulse. Maimonides' highest level of *tzedakah* — preventing poverty by loan or employment — and Aquinas's reading of v. 11 both protect the claim that the poor hold a right. The traditions diverge on the legal force of the sabbatical. Judaism retained it (modified by *prosbul*) as a live halakhic institution; Christianity, reading it as judicial/ceremonial law fulfilled in Christ, generally spiritualized it while extracting moral principles. In the last century, both traditions have converged again around the claim that the sabbatical speaks directly to contemporary debt — Jubilee 2000, the shemitah observance movement in modern Orthodoxy (Israeli chief rabbinate rulings 5768/2008 on *heter mechirah*), and the liberation-theological readings of Latin American and African scholars (Elsa Tamez, *Bible of the Oppressed*).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is release idealistic or realistic?**

Three positions. The **idealistic** reading (rabbinic, Augustine, much classical commentary): v. 4's "there will be no poor among you" is the eschatological telos of covenant faithfulness. If Israel obeys fully, poverty vanishes. V. 11 describes failure, not destiny. The **realistic** reading (Tigay, Weinfeld, modern critical): poverty is a feature of agrarian economies, v. 11 names that permanent feature, and v. 4 names the community's obligation to contend against it. The **dialectical** reading (Olson, Brueggemann): the text itself holds the two clauses together as irreducible. The ideal names the goal; the realism names the continuous labor; the contradiction is the chapter's theology. Steelmanned on the least intuitive (idealistic): if v. 4 is genuinely possible — not rhetorical overstatement but a real claim about what covenant society can be — then v. 11's "you shall open wide your hand" is not resignation but the instrument of v. 4's achievement. Generosity is the means to the no-poverty end. To concede that v. 4 is impossible is to concede that the covenant cannot deliver what it promises, which is a larger theological cost than most readers calculate.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter's pedagogy depends on holding both claims. The reader who gives up on v. 4 stops trying; the reader who denies v. 11 stops giving. The text insists on both.

**Tension 2: Whose property is it?**

Three positions. The **absolute-property** reading, common in modern liberal political theory (and present in some Calvinist covenant theology): property is genuinely held by the owner, and the seventh-year release is a gift from the owner, freely commanded by God but still the owner's gift. The **stewardship** reading (Brueggemann, Chris Wright, Ched Myers): property is held in trust; what YHWH commands is the return of what was always God's; the release is not gift but restoration. The **kinship** reading (Tigay, Weinfeld): the text consistently calls the poor "your brother," and the release is not a property transaction at all but a family obligation — brothers do not foreclose on brothers. Steelmanned on the stewardship reading: the firstborn dedication at the chapter's end (vv. 19–23) is the hermeneutical key. The firstborn of the herd is not yours to work or shear; it belongs to YHWH. The chapter places debt release and slave release *next to* firstborn release because the pattern is the same: what seems to be yours (your herd, your debtor, your slave) turns out, on covenantal examination, not to be. Therefore v. 1's "at the end of every seven years you shall grant a release" is not generosity but honesty.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter unsettles ownership. Debt, slavery, and livestock all belong finally to YHWH; the owner is a steward on a seven-year leash. This is the theological architecture that the *prosbul* and later capitalist readings have to work against.

**Unresolved question.** The *prosbul* poses the question the chapter does not answer: when a law designed to prevent a behavior (v. 9's calculating withholding) becomes the cause of that behavior because the wider economic system cannot absorb it, what does faithfulness look like? Hillel's solution preserved the letter of v. 8 (lending continues) at the cost of v. 1's release. A stricter solution would have preserved v. 1 at the cost of v. 8 (credit collapses). The text gives no guidance for this trade-off, and the tension is live every time a faith community confronts a structural economic question.

## The Thread

**Non-obvious cross-references.**

First, Jeremiah 34:8–22 is the chapter's implicit trial. King Zedekiah leads Jerusalem in a covenant to release Hebrew slaves — a direct Deut 15:12 invocation, probably occasioned by the siege — and then the city reneges and reclaims them. YHWH's response through Jeremiah: "You have not obeyed me by proclaiming liberty, every one to his brother and to his neighbor. Behold, I proclaim to you liberty — to the sword, to pestilence, and to famine." Failure to release generates its own *šĕmiṭṭâ* in exile. Deut 15 predicted how covenant breach would be judged.

Second, Luke 4:16–21 — Jesus reading Isaiah 61 at Nazareth and announcing "the year of the Lord's favor" — places his public ministry inside the sabbatical-jubilee tradition that begins in Deut 15 and Lev 25. The proclamation of release (*aphesis* in the LXX, the translation of *dĕrôr*/sabbatical release) becomes, in Luke's narration, Jesus's inaugural self-definition. The chapter's economic ethics become Christological.

Third, the Lord's Prayer's "forgive us our debts as we forgive our debtors" (Matt 6:12) is Deut 15 grammar in six words. *Aphes* — release. Christian prayer is the sabbatical release repeated daily.

Fourth, Nehemiah 5:1–13 is the chapter's post-exilic application. The restoration community has fallen into internal debt-slavery; Nehemiah convenes the nobles and forces a release of pledged fields and of debt-interest. He shakes out his garment and says, "So may God shake out every man from his house and from his labor who does not keep this promise." Deuteronomy 15's program, partially abandoned and partially enforced, drove the shape of the Second Temple community's self-correction.
