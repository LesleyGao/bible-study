# 1 Corinthians 4: Stewards, Spectacle, and the Father's Rod

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

- **Political/social situation**: Roman Corinth's social-political world supplies three vivid backgrounds for chapter 4. (1) The *oikonomos* (4:1-2)—the household steward—was a familiar figure in Roman elite households, often a slave or freedman entrusted with managing the master's property and accounts; the role carried real authority (over other slaves, over the household economy) but was always derivative, never proprietary. The steward's defining virtue was *fides* (faithfulness), since he answered to a master who could remove him at will. (2) The *theatron* (4:9)—the public spectacle—was specifically the kind of staged display in the amphitheater or arena where condemned criminals were paraded as a final humiliation before execution. The Roman *triumphus* (the victorious general's parade) ended with the public execution of war captives at the Capitoline; the Roman *munera* (gladiatorial games and damnatio ad bestias) made spectacle of dying men. Paul's image of apostles as "last of all, like men sentenced to death... a spectacle to the world, to angels, and to men" plays on this Roman public-execution drama. (3) Patron-client and household-paternal authority shaped Paul's claim "I became your father in Christ Jesus through the gospel" (4:15)—in the Roman *familia*, the *paterfamilias* held real authority over household members; the "rod" of 4:21 (*rhabdos*) is the disciplinary instrument used in domestic and pedagogical contexts.
- **Material culture**: The Corinthian theater and the smaller odeon are both visible archaeologically, and the city's *agora* hosted public assemblies and judicial proceedings. The bēma seat (Acts 18:12) where Paul stood before Gallio is the same kind of platform from which civic judgments were rendered. *Perikatharma* and *peripsēma* (4:13—"refuse," "scum," "scrapings")—the ESV translates as "the scum of the world, the refuse of all things"—were terms used in Greek and Roman culture for the ritual scapegoat or expiatory victim, sometimes a condemned criminal led around the city before being killed for the city's purification. The terms appear in this sense in Achilles Tatius and the Septuagint (Tobit 5:18 *peripsēma*; Prov 21:18 *perikatharma*).
- **Authorship & dating**: Authentic Pauline. Ephesus, c. AD 53-54. Timothy has been sent (4:17) and Paul plans his own coming visit (4:18-21).
- **Archaeological & textual evidence**: P46, Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Alexandrinus all carry the chapter cleanly. The Erastus pavement, the bēma seat, the theater and odeon, and the surviving Roman household structures (the so-called Anaploga Villa with its triclinium) all illuminate the chapter's social register. The Corinthian *agora*'s archaeological evidence of judicial proceedings makes 4:3 ("judged by you or by any human court," literally "human day," *hēmera*, an idiom for a court session) materially specific.
- **Key terms**: *oikonomos* (οἰκονόμος, "household steward, manager"), *hypēretēs* (ὑπηρέτης, "servant, assistant"—originally an under-rower in a galley, with connotations of subordination), *theatron* (θέατρον, "spectacle, public display"), *perikatharma* (περικάθαρμα, "scrapings, scapegoat-refuse"), *peripsēma* (περίψημα, "wiped-off filth, expiatory victim"), *rhabdos* (ῥάβδος, "rod, disciplinary staff").

## Lens 2 — The Literature

- **Genre**: The deliberative argument concludes its first major section. Paul has moved from diagnosis (1-3) to apostolic apologia (4) before turning in chapter 5 to the specific case of incest. Mitchell reads chapter 4 as the *peroratio* of the first major argumentative unit, with the rod-or-love conclusion (4:21) ending the section's appeal. Witherington reads it rhetorically as an *exempla* section in which Paul offers his own apostolic life as the model.
- **Devices**: Heavy and sustained *irony*. 4:8 ("Already you have become kings!") is among Paul's most cutting rhetorical inversions—he casts the Corinthians as triumphant rulers and himself as the condemned criminal at the bottom of the social ladder, then turns and asks them to imitate *him*. The structure is a series of antitheses (4:10): "We are fools / you are wise; We are weak / you are strong; You are honored / we are dishonored," followed by a vice/virtue performative list (4:11-13) that pulls from the *peristasis* (hardship list) tradition Paul develops more fully at 2 Cor 4:8-12, 6:4-10, 11:23-29. The chapter ends with the rhetorical question of 4:21 (rod or love), a deliberate pastoral ultimatum.
- **Locate in argument**: Chapter 4 closes the opening four-chapter unit on factionalism. Paul has unmasked the Corinthians' wisdom-evaluation (1-2), exposed their factional patron-loyalties (3), and now defends his own ministry as the cruciform pattern they should imitate (4). From chapter 5 onward, the letter turns to specific case-by-case ethical and liturgical issues. Chapter 4 is therefore the rhetorical hinge between the diagnostic opening and the prescriptive bulk of the letter.
- **Textual variants**: **4:6** is the most-emended sentence in modern critical scholarship: *to mē hyper ha gegraptai* ("the [maxim] 'do not [go] beyond what is written'"). The Greek is grammatically awkward—the article *to* with a negative *mē* introducing a clause is rare—and J. M. S. Baljon (1898) and more famously John Strugnell ("A Plea for Conjectural Emendation in the New Testament," 1974) proposed it as a marginal scribal gloss that crept into the text. The conjectural emendation has not been adopted by the major critical editions; NA28, UBS5, and the major modern translations including the ESV retain the text as transmitted. The exegetical question—what does "what is written" refer to (the Old Testament generally, the specific OT citations Paul has just made in chapters 1-3, or some pre-existing Christian rule of measure)—remains contested. **4:17** has minor variation in the divine name. **4:2** has a variant *zēteite* / *zēteitai* ("seek" / "is sought"), but the overall sense (faithfulness is what is required of stewards) is undisturbed.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory**

- Patristic: John Chrysostom (Homilies 11-13 on 1 Cor) reads 4:1-5 as the apostolic stewardship model and 4:9-13 as the apostolic suffering pattern; he draws from this chapter to construct his ascetic-pastoral ideal of bishop-as-suffering-servant. Augustine repeatedly cites 4:7 ("What do you have that you did not receive?") in his anti-Pelagian writings as the decisive scriptural rebuke to any human merit-claim—the verse becomes a centerpiece of the Augustinian theology of grace and is cited heavily in *On the Predestination of the Saints* and *On the Gift of Perseverance*. Origen's commentary (fragmentary) reads 4:5 ("the Lord, who will bring to light the things now hidden") in eschatological-apokatastatic terms. Ambrosiaster reads 4:1 (stewards of mysteries) sacramentally.
- Medieval: Aquinas (*Super 1 Cor*, lect. 4) systematizes 4:1's "stewards of the mysteries" into a theology of priestly office tied to the sacraments. The Glossa Ordinaria preserves the patristic instincts. Augustine's reading of 4:7 becomes the scriptural anchor of medieval Augustinianism on grace (Bradwardine, Gregory of Rimini).
- Reformation: Luther reads 4:6 ("not to go beyond what is written") as a foundational text for *sola scriptura*, despite the verse's textual difficulty. The reading was already controversial in his day and has been contested ever since. Calvin's commentary on 1 Cor 4 reads 4:7 with full Augustinian force (the verse is also at the heart of Calvin's anti-Pelagian polemic) and reads the apostolic suffering catalog of 4:9-13 as the cruciform pattern that marks true ministry against the prosperity of Roman ecclesial pretension.
- Anabaptist: 4:9-13 became a load-bearing text for the Anabaptist *Gelassenheit* (yieldedness) and the doctrine that suffering is the apostolic mark of the true church. The Schleitheim Confession and the *Martyrs Mirror* draw on this chapter's apostolic hardship pattern. The Anabaptist reading takes the chapter's irony at face value: the *theatron* of suffering is not an embarrassment but a sign of identity.
- Modern critical: Conzelmann (Hermeneia, 1969) reads 4:1-5 as Paul's apologia in the rhetorical sense, addressing implicit Corinthian charges. Gordon Fee (NICNT, 1987/2014) emphasizes the Pauline-cruciform pattern of 4:9-13 as paradigmatic for Christian ministry generally. Anthony Thiselton (NIGTC, 2000) reads 4:6's "what is written" as the Old Testament background Paul has cited (Isa 29:14, Jer 9:24, Job 5:13, Ps 94:11) and against which the Corinthians have measured wisdom. David Garland (BECNT, 2003) reads chapter 4 as the *peroratio* of the first major argument unit. Joseph Fitzmyer (Anchor Yale, 2008) gives a careful Catholic exegesis of 4:1-5 with attention to apostolic stewardship without overspecifying succession. Hays (Interpretation), Mitchell (rhetorical), Witherington (rhetorical) all treat the chapter as the rhetorical climax of the first major movement.
- Sociological: Theissen and Meeks read 4:9-13's hardship list against the social-status world of the urban Corinthian church—Paul deliberately occupies the lowest position (manual laborer, beaten, homeless) to invert the patron-client metric the Corinthians have been using. Dale Martin (*The Corinthian Body*, 1995) reads the *perikatharma*/*peripsēma* terms in their full ritual-scapegoat valence as Paul taking on the lowest body-position in the social hierarchy of bodies. Justin Meggitt's *Paul, Poverty, and Survival* (1998) reads the labor language of 4:12 against the actual destitution of urban manual laborers in the Roman Empire.
- On Paul's paternal language: the "father in Christ Jesus" claim of 4:15 and the rod-or-love of 4:21 have been read by some as Paul reasserting authority over Corinthian leaders (including the prophetically active members of Wire's reconstruction) who were claiming Spirit-given standing independently. Cynthia Long Westfall locates the paternal imagery within Paul's self-described cruciform suffering pattern (4:9-13), and most recent commentators (Fee, Thiselton, Garland) read the rod chiefly as the conventional pedagogical-domestic image it was, bound to the imitation-of-the-cross theme rather than to raw hierarchy.
- Apocalyptic Paul (Martyn, Gaventa, Campbell): 4:5's "the Lord comes, who will bring to light the things now hidden" is read against the broader apocalyptic structure of Paul's thought—the *parousia* as the cosmic disclosure that retroactively re-evaluates all human judgments. N.T. Wright (*Paul for Everyone: 1 Corinthians*; *Paul: A Biography*; *The Day the Revolution Began*) reads chapter 4 as Paul's portrait of cruciform leadership in the inaugurated kingdom: apostles are "stewards of God's mysteries" (4:1)—trusted with the secret-now-revealed plan that the crucified Messiah is Lord—and their authority is the upside-down authority of the cross, "a spectacle to the world" (4:9) in deliberate parody of the Roman triumph, where the world's metrics of power and honour are turned inside out. Wright stresses that 4:21's "rod or love" is paternal, not domineering: Paul's fatherhood is the self-giving, suffering kind modelled on the Messiah, and the Corinthians are summoned to imitate that pattern (4:16) rather than the rhetorical-status game of the surrounding culture.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory**

- The *beit din* (the Jewish court) and rabbinic discussions of judgment provide a parallel framework for 4:3-5. The *Mishnah*'s tractate Sanhedrin and the rabbinic principle that final judgment belongs to God (with human courts standing under divine review) parallel Paul's claim "It is the Lord who judges me" (4:4). Boyarin and Nanos read this as Paul operating recognizably within Jewish judicial theology while pushing it eschatologically.
- The wisdom-literature inversion of fool and sage (Qoheleth especially, with its repeated *hevel*—vapor, vanity—of human pretension) is the closest Jewish parallel to 4:10's irony. Sirach's wisdom of suffering and the apocalyptic *Wisdom of Solomon* 2-3 (the suffering righteous, vindicated by God) are also in the background. Wisdom of Solomon 5 is especially close to 4:5's "Lord will bring to light the things now hidden."
- The fool/sage inversion is particularly resonant with Qoheleth (Ecclesiastes), where the wisdom of the world is repeatedly named *hevel*. Paul's irony in 4:10 ("we are fools for Christ, you are wise") sits comfortably within this wisdom-tradition genre.
- Maimonides on the limits of human judgment (*Mishneh Torah*, Hilkhot Sanhedrin) and on the proper humility of the judge provides a parallel ethical-judicial frame for 4:3-5.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge**: They converge on the deferral of final judgment to God (Paul's "do not pronounce judgment before the time" at 4:5 and rabbinic-prophetic deferral of ultimate vindication to the Lord). They diverge on the apostolic-suffering Christology that grounds Paul's claim—Paul's *theatron* image (4:9) as the apostles' public crucifixion-shaped display has no Jewish parallel because the cross is the unique christological pivot. The *sola scriptura* reading of 4:6 has no Jewish counterpart, since Jewish hermeneutics holds the written Torah and the oral Torah together.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What Paul believed God was doing in chapter 4 was demonstrating, by his own life, the pattern of the cross applied to ministry. The deferral of judgment, the public spectacle of suffering, and the paternal call to imitation are not three separate themes but three faces of one claim: that the apostolic life is a re-enactment of the cross under the conditions of the present age, and the church grows when it joins that pattern rather than seeking to escape it.

**Tension 1: "Do not pronounce judgment before the time" (4:5) vs church discipline (chapters 5-6).** When do believers judge, and when do they refrain?

- The literary tension is internal to 1 Corinthians itself. Paul writes in chapter 4 that final judgment belongs to the Lord and the Corinthians should not pronounce judgment prematurely—and then in chapter 5 demands the church judge and expel the man living with his father's wife, and in chapter 6 rebukes them for taking lawsuits before pagan courts when "the saints will judge the world... judge angels." So Paul appears to forbid judgment in 4:5 and require it in 5-6. The resolution lies in distinguishing kinds of judgment.
- *Catholic* (CCC 1422-1498 on penance and reconciliation; *Code of Canon Law* on ecclesial discipline): the church judges in foro externo (external forum, with church courts, canonical penalties) and in foro interno (the confessional, where the priest judges sin to absolve it). 4:5 speaks against premature evaluation of motives and ultimate destinies; chapter 5 grounds proper sacramental and disciplinary judgment.
- *Reformed* (Westminster Confession on church government, ch. 30; Geneva consistory model): church courts (sessions, presbyteries, general assemblies) judge matters of doctrine and discipline. 4:5 prohibits judging hearts; chapter 5 requires judging external behavior. The Genevan consistory under Calvin took this seriously and rigorously.
- *Anabaptist* (Schleitheim Confession art. 2 on the ban): mutual judgment within the community, with the ban (excommunication) as the terminal disciplinary act. 4:5 is held alongside chapter 5 as twin guardrails: humility about hearts, seriousness about communal holiness.
- *Lutheran*: distinguishes the office of the keys (binding and loosing, exercised by the church with reference to specific sin) from final judgment (God's alone). Article 11 of the Augsburg Confession on confession.
- *Eastern Orthodox*: the bishop and the spiritual father (*pneumatikos*) exercise discernment and discipline, with the deeper sense of *epitimia* (penance as therapeutic) rather than juridical-punitive. 4:5 frames the deep humility appropriate to all such judgment.
- *Pentecostal/charismatic*: tends to emphasize Spirit-discernment and church discipline through congregational rather than hierarchical processes.
- *Critical/academic*: most modern commentators (Fee, Thiselton, Garland, Mitchell) note that 4:5 prohibits judgment of *hidden things* (motives, hearts) while chapters 5-6 require judgment of *manifest behavior*. The grammatical key is 4:5's *ta krypta tou skotous*—"the hidden things of darkness"—which is exactly what humans cannot rightly evaluate.

Steelmanned least-intuitive position: the Anabaptist mutual-judgment ecclesiology. To Catholic ears it sounds undisciplined; to Reformed ears insufficiently structured; to modern liberal ears uncomfortable with communal accountability. Properly understood, the Anabaptist position is that the local church under the Spirit is the proper site of discernment and discipline—not a hierarchy above the congregation, not a court of clergy, but the gathered community testing one another in love. 4:5's deferral of ultimate judgment to God and chapter 5's call to communal action together produce a discipline that is serious about behavior, humble about hearts, and rooted in shared discernment rather than external juridical structures. The Schleitheim Confession's discipline is the application of Matthew 18 read through 1 Cor 4-5 together. *Where this leaves us*: Paul does not prohibit ecclesial judgment; he prohibits judgment of hearts and hidden things, while requiring the community to judge manifest behavior. The institutional form of that judgment is exegetically underdetermined.

**Tension 2: Apostolic office and succession.** Does 4:1 ("servants of Christ, stewards of God's mysteries") underwrite Catholic apostolic succession, Protestant priesthood of all believers, or Orthodox Eucharistic-bishop ecclesiology?

- *Catholic* (CCC 880-896; Lumen Gentium 18-25; Aquinas ST IIIa qq. 64-65 on sacraments): apostolic succession is the unbroken line of episcopal consecration from the apostles, transmitting sacramental authority and stewardship of the mysteries. 4:1 names apostles as *oikonomoi* of the mysteries, and the *oikonomos* role passes through the bishop's office. The "mysteries" (sacraments) are entrusted to ordained stewards.
- *Eastern Orthodox*: the bishop is the iconic head of the local Eucharistic community; succession is *koinonia* in the apostolic faith, conciliar and Eucharistic, not primarily juridical. 4:1's stewardship of the mysteries is read sacramentally and pneumatologically. Zizioulas's *Being as Communion*.
- *Reformed* (Calvin Inst. IV.1-3; Westminster on the church): all Christians share in the priesthood of believers (1 Pet 2:9), but ministers are distinguished by call and ordination for the ministry of word and sacrament. 4:1 names a particular ministerial role, not a priestly caste; succession is in apostolic *teaching* (the gospel rightly preached), not in episcopal ordination as such.
- *Lutheran* (Augsburg Confession arts. 5, 14): ordained ministry is a divinely instituted office, but the church has authority to call and ordain its ministers. The "stewards of the mysteries" language is honored without underwriting Catholic-style succession.
- *Anabaptist/free church*: 4:1 is corporate, not individual. The community as a whole stewards the mysteries; specific ministers are called by the community for specific functions. No priestly caste.
- *Pentecostal/charismatic*: emphasizes the present pneumatic gifting of ministers; the steward language underwrites a functional rather than ontological account of office.

Steelmanned least-intuitive position to Protestant readers: Eastern Orthodox Eucharistic-bishop ecclesiology. The Orthodox claim is that 4:1's stewardship of mysteries is intrinsically sacramental—not juridical authority but iconic-pneumatic presence. The bishop celebrating the Eucharist makes Christ present to and in the community, and that presence is what *constitutes* the church catholic. Succession is real because the Eucharistic community is real and unbroken; it is not a chain of legal transmission but a continuity of pneumatic-sacramental life. To Protestant ears the position can sound like ontologizing office, but Orthodox theology holds that the office is iconic precisely because it transparent to Christ, not opaque. *Where this leaves us*: 4:1 names the apostles as servants and stewards. The institutional translation of that role into office is the work of subsequent church history; the text underdetermines the question. What 4:1-2 unambiguously requires is *faithfulness*—the steward's defining virtue—and that is a standard by which all ecclesial offices, however structured, can be measured.

**One unresolved question**: If Paul's apostolic life is the *theatron* of suffering—publicly exposed, materially poor, beaten, homeless—and the Corinthians' status-saturated triumphalism is the foil, then any ministry that has acquired wealth, status, security, and public regard has at minimum a problem of plausibility with its own scriptural warrant. What would it look like for the contemporary church's leadership patterns to take 4:9-13 as descriptive of normal apostolic existence rather than as exceptional and historically located?

## The Thread

- **4:7 ("What do you have that you did not receive?") → Romans 11:35-36; James 1:17**: Augustine's favored verse against Pelagianism. The Pauline grammar of received grace runs through Rom 11:35-36 ("Who has given a gift to him that he might be repaid? For from him and through him and to him are all things") and is echoed in James 1:17 ("Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights"). The whole biblical-theological doctrine of grace as prior gift hangs partly on this verse.
- **4:5 ("the Lord, who will bring to light the things now hidden in darkness and will disclose the purposes of the heart") → Wisdom of Solomon 5; Romans 2:16; 1 Cor 14:25**: The eschatological revelation of hidden things is a load-bearing theme in Wisdom of Solomon 5 (the apocalyptic vindication of the suffering righteous), Romans 2:16 (God will judge the secrets of men through Jesus Christ), and 1 Cor 14:25 (the unbeliever entering the assembly has the secrets of his heart disclosed). These four passages together form a Pauline-apocalyptic doctrine of eschatological self-disclosure.
- **4:9-13 → 2 Corinthians 4:8-12; 6:4-10; 11:23-29**: Paul's apostolic *peristasis* (hardship) catalogs are a recurring rhetorical and theological feature, with the fullest forms in 2 Corinthians. The cruciform pattern Paul claims here as the apostolic shape is developed at greater length and with sharper edges in the later Corinthian correspondence. Reading them together makes the claim of 4:9-13 stand out as paradigmatic rather than incidental.
- **4:15 ("I became your father in Christ Jesus through the gospel") → Galatians 4:19; 1 Thessalonians 2:7-12**: Paul's parental imagery (father in 4:15 and 1 Thess 2:11; mother in 1 Thess 2:7 and Gal 4:19, where he is in *labor pains* until Christ is formed) is binocular—both paternal and maternal—and is one of the most striking pieces of self-description in the Pauline corpus. Reading 4:15 alongside Gal 4:19 prevents a one-sidedly paternal-authoritarian reading and recovers the cruciform-formational shape of Paul's pastoral self-understanding.
