# 1 John 5: Three Witnesses, Two Sins, One Closing Warning

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The First Epistle of John is anonymous in the text itself, with the author of 2 and 3 John identifying himself only as "the elder" (*ho presbyteros*, ὁ πρεσβύτερος). The traditional attribution to John the Apostle in old age rests on Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1), Polycarp's verbal echoes in his *Letter to the Philippians*, and the famous passage in Eusebius (*Hist. Eccl.* 3.39.4) that preserves Papias's apparent distinction between *John the Apostle* and *John the Elder* — a distinction that has functioned as a Rorschach test ever since. The critical tradition, anchored by Raymond Brown in *The Community of the Beloved Disciple* and his Anchor Bible commentary on the Johannine Epistles, treats the letter as the product of an anonymous community member writing in the wake of a schism (the *secessionists* of 2:19). Rudolf Bultmann's source-critical reading and Judith Lieu's *I, II, & III John* and *The Theology of the Johannine Epistles* both place the letter in Asia Minor, most likely Ephesus, in the 90s AD, written into a Christological crisis in which the adversaries deny that Christ has come *en sarki* (ἐν σαρκί, "in the flesh," 4:2). Robert Yarbrough (*BECNT*) and Karen Jobes (*ZECNT*) defend traditional apostolic authorship while engaging the critical evidence; I. Howard Marshall's *NICNT*, John Stott's *Tyndale*, Leon Morris, and C. H. Dodd's *Moffatt* commentary fill out the spectrum.

Chapter 5 is the closing chapter, and it carries an unusually heavy load. The opening verses (5:1-5) round off the love-and-faith argument of chapter 4 and introduce a new image — *nikē* (νίκη, victory): "this is the victory that has overcome the world — our faith" (5:4). The middle of the chapter (5:6-12) contains the disputed *triadic witness* and the Christological argument that runs through the water and the blood; 5:13-17 turns to assurance and intercession, with the haunting distinction between sins that lead to death and sins that do not; and 5:18-21 closes the letter with three "we know" affirmations and the abrupt warning, "little children, keep yourselves from idols."

The triadic witness of 5:6-8 is the Christological hinge. "This is he who came by water and blood — Jesus Christ; not by the water only but by the water and the blood. And the Spirit is the one who testifies, because the Spirit is the truth. For there are three that testify: the Spirit and the water and the blood; and these three agree." The historical interpretation that has won the modern field reads the verses as direct anti-Cerinthian polemic. Cerinthus, on Irenaeus's account (*Against Heresies* 1.26.1), separated the man Jesus (who was baptized in the Jordan — the *water*) from the heavenly Christ (who descended on him at baptism and departed before the crucifixion — leaving only the man to die, the *blood*). The elder insists that the same Jesus came by *both* water and blood, "not by the water only" (5:6) — Christ was incarnate at the baptism *and* at the cross. The Spirit is the ongoing forensic witness to this fact. Against this Christological-historical reading stands an older sacramental reading (water = baptism, blood = eucharist), which has remained influential in some Catholic and Orthodox traditions but is harder to sustain exegetically given the explicit "not by the water only" of 5:6, which makes more sense as a polemical correction than as a sacramental gradation.

The most consequential textual problem in the chapter — perhaps in the entire New Testament — is the *Comma Johanneum*. In the Textus Receptus and KJV, 5:7-8 includes the words: "in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one. And there are three that bear witness in earth." This clause is absent from every Greek manuscript before the fourteenth century. It first appears in fourth-century Latin sources, possibly in Priscillian (d. 385) or in pseudo-Cyprian, and spreads through the Vulgate tradition. Erasmus, preparing the first published Greek New Testament in 1516, omitted the Comma. He was attacked, most prominently by Diego López de Zúñiga (Stunica), and reportedly challenged his critics to produce any Greek manuscript containing it. Codex Montfortianus appeared — almost certainly back-translated from the Latin Vulgate — and Erasmus, true to his word, included the Comma in his third edition (1522). From there it entered Stephanus, Beza, the Textus Receptus, and the KJV. Bruce Metzger, *The Text of the New Testament* (4th ed., 2005, with Bart Ehrman), and Daniel Wallace's "The Comma Johanneum and Cyprian" mount the case for inauthenticity, now virtually universal in modern scholarship. NA28 and UBS5 omit it. The Catholic position has shifted: the 1897 *Holy Office* affirmed authenticity; the 1927 *Holy Office* permitted scholarly questioning; Pius XII's *Divino Afflante Spiritu* (1943) opened textual-critical work to Catholic scholars. Trinitarian doctrine does not depend on the Comma; its removal is a textual correction, not a doctrinal crisis.

The sin-unto-death distinction of 5:16-17 — *hamartia pros thanaton* (ἁμαρτία πρὸς θάνατον) and *hamartia ou pros thanaton* (ἁμαρτία οὐ πρὸς θάνατον) — became the *locus classicus* for the Catholic mortal/venial distinction. The *Catechism* develops it at §§1854-1864, and the Council of Trent (Session 14) treats it dogmatically. The closing warning, "little children, keep yourselves from idols" (5:21), is exegetically surprising. *Eidōla* (εἴδωλα) in a Greek-speaking Asian context most naturally means literal pagan idols — the imperial cult, Artemis at Ephesus, civic religion. But the abruptness of the warning, in a letter that has been about intra-Christian schism, has led some interpreters (Brown, Lieu) to read *eidōla* metaphorically as the secessionists' false images of Christ — Christology gone wrong is idolatry. Both readings have textual support; the elder may have intended both. Key terms: *nikē* (νίκη, victory, 5:4), *martyria* (μαρτυρία, witness — used seven times in 5:6-11), *zōē aiōnios* (ζωὴ αἰώνιος, eternal life), *hamartia pros thanaton* (sin unto death), *eidōla* (idols).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter's structure is more visibly segmented than chapter 4. The opening (5:1-5) closes the love-faith argument of the preceding chapter with the *nikē* image. The middle (5:6-12) is a forensic disquisition on testimony, organized around the triadic witness and the noun *martyria*, which appears seven times in six verses. Verses 13-17 turn to assurance and intercession, with the famous "purpose statement" of 5:13 ("I write these things… that you may know that you have eternal life") functioning as a Johannine inclusio with John 20:31 ("these are written so that you may believe"). Verses 18-21 close with three "we know" affirmations (*oidamen*, οἴδαμεν), each of which crystallizes a theme of the letter — and then the abrupt idol-warning.

The literary register shifts noticeably across these segments. The triadic-witness passage uses forensic language drawn from Deuteronomy 19:15: the elder invokes the halakhic-rabbinic principle to argue that the Spirit, the water, and the blood together meet the legal threshold for established testimony. The assurance section uses pastoral language — *parrhēsia* (παρρησία, confidence, 5:14, also 4:17) — and the closing turns abruptly hortatory. The chapter as a whole is less spiraling than chapter 4 and more compressive, gathering the letter's threads into closing affirmations. Judith Lieu has argued that the closing's abrupt shift in register may indicate a layered composition or a final pastoral note appended to the homiletic body; some critical readings have proposed an editorial scribe's addition for 5:21, though no manuscript evidence supports such a partition. Most contemporary commentators read the closing as a deliberate, if startling, pastoral move by the elder himself.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the New Testament, 1 John 5 stands at a peculiar intersection. It contains the densest concentration of *martyria* language in the Johannine corpus outside the trial narrative of John 18-19; it preserves the most famous textual variant in the New Testament; and it is the source of the mortal/venial distinction that has shaped Catholic moral theology for fifteen centuries. The chapter's reception history is therefore unusually dense.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

The patristic reception of the triadic witness reads the water-and-blood almost universally in Christological-anti-Docetic terms. Tertullian, *Against Praxeas* 25, draws on the verses for Trinitarian and Christological argument; Cyprian, *On the Unity of the Catholic Church* 6, has a passage some have read as referring to the Comma but that more likely reflects an allegorical reading of 5:8. Augustine, *Tractates on the First Epistle of John*, takes the triadic witness Christologically. The Comma proper first appears clearly in late-fourth-century Latin and is well-attested in Spanish Vulgate manuscripts by the sixth.

The medieval Catholic tradition received the Comma as scripture, and Aquinas, Bonaventure, and Catherine of Siena drew on it as a Trinitarian proof-text. The mortal/venial distinction of 5:16-17 was developed in the same period: Aquinas in *Summa* I-II.72-89 treats the moral structure of sin systematically, with 1 John 5 the central biblical text. Bernard of Clairvaux develops the *peccatum mortale* / *peccatum veniale* distinction pastorally; Lateran IV (1215) presupposed the framework in requiring annual confession.

The Reformation reception is bifurcated. On the Comma, Erasmus's textual judgment was followed by careful Protestant scholars but resisted by confessional traditions tied to the Textus Receptus. Luther's 1522 German New Testament initially omitted it; later editions included it. Calvin acknowledged the textual difficulty but accepted the Comma. The KJV (1611) included it. The textual-critical revolution of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries — Mill, Bengel, Griesbach, Tischendorf, Westcott and Hort — built the case for inauthenticity that Metzger and Wallace inherit. On the sin-unto-death, the Reformers broke from the mortal/venial framework. Luther read 5:16 as final, unrepented apostasy — the parallel of Hebrews 6:4-6 and 10:26-31. Calvin in *Institutes* III.4 reads similarly. The Reformed tradition has held this reading: sin "unto death" is not a category of sin but a condition of the sinner. The Wesleyan tradition holds that any unrepented willful sin can become unto death because backsliding is genuinely possible. The Eastern Orthodox tradition resists the mortal/venial dichotomy, treating sin therapeutically and reading 5:16-17 as the difference between wounds that admit healing through repentance and those that have severed communion.

The Catholic position on the Comma has shifted dramatically. After *Divino Afflante Spiritu* the New American Bible, Jerusalem Bible, and NRSV (Catholic Edition) all omit the Comma. Modern Catholic biblical scholarship, with rare exceptions, accepts its inauthenticity. The KJV-only and Textus Receptus traditions in some Protestant communities continue to defend it — Edward Hills's *The King James Version Defended*, David Otis Fuller's edited volumes, and the Trinitarian Bible Society are the most rigorous statements. The steelman is providential-preservation: the Reformation-era textual tradition is not a collection of accidents but a providentially guided witness whose church-reception carries weight that purely text-critical methodology cannot easily dismiss.

N. T. Wright, in *Early Christian Letters for Everyone* (2011), reads chapter 5 as the letter gathering its threads into a closing assurance. The "victory that has overcome the world — our faith" (5:4-5) Wright takes as the heart of it: not a triumphalist boast but the confidence that, because Jesus is God's Son and the readers belong to him, the pressure of the surrounding culture cannot finally claim them. On the three witnesses (5:6-8) Wright follows the Christological-anti-Cerinthian reading — the Spirit, the water (baptism), and the blood (the cross) all testify to one and the same Jesus, against any teaching that would split the human Jesus from a heavenly Christ — and he treats the *Comma Johanneum* as a later well-meaning gloss that the church does not need. The purpose statement of 5:13 ("that you may know that you have eternal life") is, for Wright, the whole point of the letter: eternal life is not merely a future hope but a present possession of those who hold the true confession and love one another, and the appropriate posture toward God is therefore the bold, expectant prayer of 5:14-15. The closing "keep yourselves from idols" (5:21) Wright reads against the backdrop of pagan Asia Minor while allowing that, in this letter, the worst idol of all is a false picture of Jesus.

### Jewish Scholarly Engagement

The triadic witness is grounded in a Jewish forensic principle. Deuteronomy 19:15 — "by the mouth of two or three witnesses every word shall be established" — is the halakhic rule the elder invokes to argue that the testimony to Jesus as the Christ has met the legal threshold. Joseph Sievers and Lawrence Schiffman have written on the rabbinic-witness tradition; Adele Reinhartz addresses how Johannine literature handles "the world" and the Jewish tradition. The mortal-venial framework is harder to map onto Second Temple categories, though distinctions between intentional and unintentional sin, and the *karet* ("cutting off") tradition in Leviticus and Numbers, are present. Daniel Boyarin in *Border Lines* has argued that early Christian and rabbinic distinctions about sin developed in parallel rather than in linear succession.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What God is doing through this chapter, in the elder's framing, is closing the letter with three crystallizations: the testimony to Jesus as the Christ has met the legal threshold (5:6-12), the believer can have *parrhēsia* before God in prayer and assurance of eternal life (5:13-17), and the community must keep itself from the false images that dissolve the apostolic confession (5:21). The chapter's two great theological tensions — the Comma Johanneum and the sin-unto-death — are not peripheral; they are pressure points where the chapter's claims have shaped Christian doctrine and practice for two millennia.

### Tension 1: The Comma Johanneum — what does its inauthenticity mean?

The mainstream textual-critical position — Metzger, Wallace, Ehrman, NA28, UBS5 — is that the Comma is not part of the original text. Its absence from every pre-fourteenth-century Greek manuscript, from the early Latin tradition before the late fourth century, from the Greek and Syriac fathers, and from the Coptic, Ethiopic, Armenian, and Georgian versions, is decisive. Modern translations — ESV, NIV, NRSV, NAB — omit it or relegate it to a footnote.

The KJV-only and Textus Receptus position — Edward Hills's *The King James Version Defended*, David Otis Fuller, the Trinitarian Bible Society — defends the Comma on theological-historical grounds. The argument runs: if the church received the Textus Receptus as authoritative for centuries, and if the Reformation Bibles produced from it shaped Christian piety and doctrine for the modern era, then the providential preservation of scripture is more naturally located in the received text than in the eclectic critical text reconstructed in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries from earlier but disputed manuscripts. The steelman: the textual-critical method itself involves theological assumptions (about which manuscripts are most likely original, about the direction of corruption, about scribal habits) that are not religiously neutral; the providentialist case is consistent and internally coherent even if its conclusions are contested. The cost: it requires defending a passage that all available pre-fourteenth-century Greek evidence excludes, which strains the available data hard.

The Catholic post-1943 position — represented in modern Catholic biblical scholarship and in NAB, Jerusalem Bible, NRSV (Catholic Edition) — accepts the Comma's inauthenticity while insisting that this acceptance does not destabilize Trinitarian doctrine. *Divino Afflante Spiritu* opened textual-critical work to Catholic scholars; the dogmatic position on Trinitarian doctrine rests on multiple scriptural and conciliar foundations, not on the Comma alone. The strength is its honest reckoning with the textual evidence; the weakness, from a Catholic-traditionalist perspective, is the awkward shift from the 1897 *Holy Office* declaration to the present consensus.

**Where this leaves us:** the Comma's inauthenticity is the strongest textual-critical case in the New Testament and is virtually unanimous in modern scholarship. The KJV-only steelman is real but narrow. Trinitarian theology does not depend on the Comma; the loss is textual-pastoral, not dogmatic. The harder pastoral question is what Christians who grew up reading the KJV do with a beloved verse that turns out to be a sixteenth-century insertion — a question about the relationship between received tradition and recovered evidence that runs much deeper than this one passage.

### Tension 2: Sin unto death (5:16-17) — what is the elder distinguishing?

The Catholic mortal-venial reading takes 5:16-17 as the biblical foundation for a sacramentally classified moral framework. *Mortal* sins are grave matter committed with full knowledge and deliberate consent; they sever the soul from sanctifying grace and require sacramental confession for remission. *Venial* sins are less grave, do not sever the relationship, and are remitted through ordinary means of grace (the Eucharist, prayer, contrition). The *Catechism* §§1854-1864 develops this carefully, and the Council of Trent (Session 14) treats it dogmatically. The strength is its psychological realism — Catholic moral theology has long recognized that not all sins are equal — and its sacramental coherence. The weakness, from Reformed perspective, is that the elder does not appear to be distinguishing categories of sin so much as conditions of the sinner.

The Reformed reading — Calvin, the Westminster tradition, contemporary Reformed exegesis (Marshall, Stott, Yarbrough leans this way) — takes "sin unto death" as the irrevocable hardening of apostasy: the parallel of Hebrews 6:4-6 and Hebrews 10:26-31. The distinction is not between categories of sin (mortal vs. venial) but between the repentant sinner, for whom intercession avails, and the unrepentant apostate, for whom the elder does not say one should pray. This reading is consonant with the Johannine *menein* theology — the question is whether the sinner abides in the community or has departed (the parallel with 2:19 is suggestive). The strength is its grammatical and contextual fit; the weakness is the pastoral difficulty of identifying when, in any concrete case, a brother's sin has crossed into "unto death."

The Eastern Orthodox reading treats sin therapeutically rather than juridically. The distinction in 5:16-17 is between sins that admit healing through repentance and the sacraments and those whose acceptance has cut the sinner off from the divine energies. The framework is closer to medical diagnosis than to legal classification. The steelman: the Western juridical framework, both Catholic and Reformed, has tended to flatten the dynamic, processual character of sin and grace that the Greek fathers (Maximus the Confessor, Gregory of Nyssa) preserved. The cost is that the therapeutic framework, taken alone, can soften the genuine eschatological seriousness of the elder's warning.

The Wesleyan reading — Wesley's sermons on sin in believers, *A Plain Account of Christian Perfection*, the Methodist tradition — holds that any unrepented willful sin can become unto death because backsliding is genuinely possible. This is closer to the Reformed reading than the Catholic in rejecting a fixed mortal/venial classification, but closer to the Catholic in taking the eschatological stakes of present sin seriously. The strength is its pastoral urgency; the weakness is the difficulty of giving the believer any settled assurance, which 5:13 seems to want to provide.

**Where this leaves us:** the four readings converge more than their polemics suggest. All four agree that not all sins are alike in their effects, that final apostasy is a real possibility, and that the elder is gesturing toward something genuinely serious in 5:16-17. They diverge on whether the distinction is categorical (Catholic), conditional (Reformed), therapeutic (Orthodox), or volitional (Wesleyan). The text supports the conditional reading most naturally — the elder is talking about whether one should *pray* for a brother, which is a question about the brother's state, not about a classification of the sin itself. But the longer Christian tradition has rightly sensed that the verse warrants serious moral-theological reflection, and the four traditions' developments are not foreign to the text.

**Closing question for chapter 5:** the letter ends with "little children, keep yourselves from idols." After five chapters about love, abiding, confession, and assurance, why is this the final word? The elder either thinks idolatry is the primary danger left for his community — pagan civic religion in Asia Minor — or he is using *eidōla* to name the secessionists' false Christ as idolatrous. If the latter, then the closing reframes the entire letter: every false image of Christ is an idol, and the work of the love-and-truth community is iconoclastic.

## The Thread

Four threads run from 1 John 5 into the rest of the canon. John 19:34 — "one of the soldiers pierced his side with a spear, and at once there came out blood and water" — is the proximate gospel source for the water-and-blood of 5:6, and the parallel locks the Christological reading in place: Christ came by water *and* blood at his death, not just by water at his baptism. Deuteronomy 19:15 — "by the mouth of two or three witnesses every word shall be established" — is the halakhic forensic principle the elder invokes in 5:7-8, and reading the chapters in parallel makes clear the rabbinic-Jewish substrate of his argument. Hebrews 6:4-6 and Hebrews 10:26-31 — the parallel "no further sacrifice for sin" passages — are the New Testament's other treatment of the sin-unto-death problem, and any responsible reading of 5:16-17 must engage them. And Romans 8:37 — "in all these things we are more than conquerors (*hypernikōmen*, ὑπερνικῶμεν) through him who loved us" — is the Pauline parallel to the *nikē* image of 5:4-5: Paul's hyper-victory and John's victory of faith are the same theology in different keys.
