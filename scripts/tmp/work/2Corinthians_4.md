# 2 Corinthians 4: Treasure in Jars of Clay and the Eternal Weight of Glory

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

- **Political/social situation**: Roman Corinth—Caesar's colonial refoundation of 44 BC, capital of Achaia, anchored chronologically by the Gallio inscription at Delphi (*Fouilles de Delphes* III.4 286, AD 51-52)—was the audience. Paul writes from Macedonia c. AD 55-56 after the Asian crisis (1:8-11) and Titus's report (7:5-16). Chapter 4 unfolds inside the apostolic apologia of chapters 3-7, defending Pauline ministry against rivals who valued oratorical display, charismatic credentials, and patron-client clout. Dale Martin (*The Corinthian Body*, 1995) and Bruce Winter (*After Paul Left Corinth*, 2001) document the cultural premium on bodily presence and rhetorical self-display in the colonial city. Paul's "treasure in jars of clay" (4:7) is constructed to be intelligible in exactly that culture: Roman colonists understood the difference between the bronze and silver vessels of the elite *triclinium* and the cheap fired-clay storage pots of the common household. The metaphor is class-coded.
- **Material culture**: *Ostrakinos* (clay) jars in 4:7 are the cheap, mass-produced amphorae and ollae of everyday Roman commerce—the kind of pottery archaeologists at Corinth pull from kitchen middens by the ton. Bronze and silver vessels signaled status; clay jars were domestic infrastructure, often used to hide valuables (the famous Roman practice of hiding coin hoards in clay vessels buried under floors—well-attested archaeologically at Pompeii and elsewhere). Paul's metaphor of "treasure in jars of clay" maps onto a familiar visual practice: precious contents inside throwaway containers. The "light shine out of darkness" of 4:6 is a direct citation of Genesis 1:3, applying the *fiat lux* of creation to apostolic illumination. The "outer/inner self" of 4:16 (*ho exō / ho esō anthrōpos*) draws on a vocabulary deeply embedded in Greco-Roman moral psychology—Plato's *Republic* IX 589a-b distinguishes the inner human from the lion-and-many-headed-beast outer human; Philo of Alexandria (*Quis Heres* 263, *De Plantatione* 42) develops the Hellenistic-Jewish version. The "god of this world" (4:4, *ho theos tou aiōnos toutou*) sits in apocalyptic Jewish vocabulary about the present evil age governed by hostile spiritual powers (cf. *Testament of the Twelve Patriarchs*, *1 Enoch*, the Qumran *Treatise of the Two Spirits* in 1QS 3-4).
- **Authorship & dating**: Authentic Pauline. Within the unity-versus-partition debate, chapter 4 sits inside Bornkamm's proposed apology insertion (2:14-7:4). Margaret Thrall (ICC, 1994), Murray Harris (NIGTC, 2005), Linda Belleville, and Paul Barnett defend literary unity; Furnish (Anchor Yale, 1984), Welborn, and Bornkamm defend partition. P46, Sinaiticus, and Vaticanus all preserve the chapter whole.
- **Archaeological & textual evidence**: P46 (c. AD 200), Sinaiticus, and Vaticanus carry the chapter cleanly. The Gallio inscription anchors Pauline chronology. The Erastus pavement at Corinth provides material context. The Roman Asclepieion at Corinth (with its anatomical votives of healed body parts) and the gladiatorial arenas of the Roman east supply the cultural backdrop for the *peristasis* (hardship) catalog of 4:8-9—affliction-but-not-crushed, persecuted-but-not-forsaken, struck-down-but-not-destroyed.
- **Key terms**: *thēsauros en ostrakinois skeuesin* (θησαυρὸς ἐν ὀστρακίνοις σκεύεσιν, "treasure in clay jars"), *nekrōsis* (νέκρωσις, "death, dying, the carrying-around of dying"—4:10), *exō / esō anthrōpos* (ἔξω / ἔσω ἄνθρωπος, "outer / inner person"), *aiōnion baros doxēs* (αἰώνιον βάρος δόξης, "eternal weight of glory"—a Hebraism, since Heb *kabod* (כבוד) means both "glory" and "weight"), *prosōpon* (πρόσωπον, "face"—of Jesus Christ in 4:6), *kataleuomai* (καταλείπω vs ἐγκαταλείπω, the verbs of being "abandoned"—4:9 plays on Ps 22 vocabulary).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

- **Genre**: Apostolic apologia continuing chapter 3's defense, transitioning into existential-eschatological meditation. The chapter shifts from the doxological midrash of chapter 3 to a more autobiographical and confessional register, while preserving rabbinic argumentative form.
- **Devices**: The chapter is built on a series of rhythmic antitheses—the *peristasis* catalog of 4:8-9 (afflicted/not crushed, perplexed/not despairing, persecuted/not forsaken, struck down/not destroyed) is one of Paul's most polished rhetorical sequences, with paronomasia and asyndeton tightly controlled. The death/life pattern of 4:10-12 is sustained chiasm. 4:13's quotation from Ps 116:10 LXX ("I believed and so I spoke") is one of the few explicit OT quotations in 2 Corinthians; Paul links the apostolic *parrhesia* (speaking) to the Davidic-Psalmist's faith-grounded speech. 4:16-18 closes with extended antithesis (outer/inner, momentary/eternal, light/weighty, seen/unseen) that has shaped Christian devotional language for two millennia. The Hebraism of 4:17 (*aiōnion baros doxēs*—weight of glory) is a deliberate Semitic-Greek hybrid that punning rabbinic readers would have caught immediately.
- **Locate in argument**: Chapter 4 develops the implications of chapter 3's transformation theology in the concrete shape of apostolic suffering. If 3:18 says we are being transformed by glory, 4:7-12 says how—through the death-and-life pattern of *nekrōsis* and *zōē*, dying lived publicly so that life is mediated to others. The chapter prepares chapter 5's eschatology (the heavenly dwelling, the judgment seat) by establishing in 4:16-18 the orientation toward the unseen-eternal that will be the theological foundation of the eschatological argument.
- **Textual variants**: 4:4 has a textually firm but theologically charged construction. *Ho theos tou aiōnos toutou* ("the god of this age") is read by most witnesses as referring to Satan; a minority patristic tradition (Origen, Tertullian, Marcion's opponents) parsed the sentence differently to make God the subject who blinds the unbelievers' minds (motivated by anti-Marcionite concerns to defend monotheism). The standard reading is Satan / spiritual powers, but the syntactic ambiguity is real and the patristic anxiety reflects a genuine theological concern. 4:6 is firm. 4:10's *nekrōsis* is firm; the variant "the dying of the Lord Jesus" (some Western witnesses add "Lord") is secondary. 4:14's "with Jesus" vs "through Jesus" varies among major manuscripts; meaning is preserved either way. 4:16's *ho exō hēmōn anthrōpos* / *ho esō* is firm.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory**

- Patristic: John Chrysostom devoted homilies 8-10 to chapter 4. Homily 8 on 4:7 reads the *ostrakina skeuē* (clay jars) as the great patristic emblem of apostolic humility-as-credential—Paul's point is that the inadequacy of the messenger is the proof of the gospel's divine origin. Tertullian's *Apology* 50 ("the blood of the martyrs is the seed of the church") is the classic patristic appropriation of 4:8-12; Cyprian's *Ad Fortunatum* (de Exhortatione Martyrii, c. AD 257) builds on the same ground. Augustine's *De Trinitate* 14 develops the inner-person psychology of 4:16 as the theological-psychological frame for his Trinitarian anthropology—the *imago Dei* is in the *homo interior*, in mind, knowledge, love. *Confessions* 10 pursues the inner-person introspection that 4:16 makes paradigmatic. Origen reads 4:6 as the foundation of his theology of illumination—the same God who said *fiat lux* now shines in the believer's heart.
- Medieval: Aquinas's *Super Secundam Epistolam ad Corinthios* and *ST* I-II q. 4 (on beatitude as glory) build directly on 4:17. The "eternal weight of glory" becomes a classic medieval theological topos for the beatific vision. Bonaventure's *Itinerarium Mentis in Deum* develops the inner-person ascent on Augustinian-Pauline grounds. The mystical tradition (Bernard, the Victorines, the Rhineland mystics) uses 4:16-18 as an exegetical foundation for contemplative interiority.
- Reformation: Luther's *theologia crucis* finds in 4:7-12 its most concentrated apostolic instance. The Heidelberg Disputation (1518) theses 19-21—*Theologus crucis*—are written with this passage in view. Calvin's commentary on 2 Cor 4:16 develops the inner renewal theme as fundamental to Reformed pneumatology. The Reformation pastoral tradition reads 4:17 as a comfort against persecution, as the Marian persecutions and Huguenot tribulations made urgently practical.
- Modern critical: Ralph Martin's WBC (1986) and Frank Matera's NTL (2003) treat the chapter as the crystallization of Pauline cruciform theology. Carey Newman's *Paul's Glory-Christology* (1992) reads the *doxa* language of 4:6 as the apocalyptic-Jewish glory-tradition (Merkavah mysticism, Ezekiel's vision) appropriated christologically. Brad Eastman's *The Significance of Glory* (1999) develops the glory-suffering dialectic. On the inner-outer person, Robert Gundry's *Sōma in Biblical Theology* (1976) argues that Paul's anthropology is *not* Greek-philosophical body-soul dualism but a holistic Hebrew-Jewish anthropology in which the *exō / esō* contrast is functional rather than ontological. Dale Martin's *Corinthian Body* (1995) develops the anthropological argument further. The apocalyptic Paul school (Martyn, Gaventa, Campbell) reads 4:4's "god of this age" as a paradigmatic apocalyptic claim—the present evil age is genuinely under hostile sovereignty, and the gospel is cosmic invasion. N.T. Wright (*Paul for Everyone: 2 Corinthians*, 2003; *Paul and the Faithfulness of God*, 2013; *Surprised by Hope*, 2007) treats 4:7-12 as the centerpiece of his account of new-covenant ministry: the "treasure in clay jars" is precisely the point — the messenger's frailty is not an embarrassment to the gospel but its delivery system, since a power that came in obvious strength would not be the power of the crucified Messiah. On 4:16-18 Wright presses his characteristic resistance to an escapist eschatology: the "eternal weight of glory" is not a disembodied heaven the soul flees to but the renewal of all things — God's new creation — for which present suffering is, in the strict Pauline sense, the labor pain. Bonhoeffer's *Letters and Papers from Prison* takes 4:7-12 as the theological ground of his late "religionless Christianity"—the church only finds its true form in conformity to Christ's public dying.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory**

- Genesis 1:3 ("Let there be light") is explicitly cited in 4:6. Paul's claim is that the same divine speech-act that inaugurated the cosmos now inaugurates apostolic illumination. The Jewish meditation on Gen 1:3 (Targum Ps-Jonathan, Genesis Rabbah, Philo's *De Opificio Mundi*) provides the immediate background. Wisdom of Solomon 7:25-26 ("she is a breath of the power of God, and a pure emanation of the glory of the Almighty… a reflection of eternal light, a spotless mirror of the working of God, and an image of his goodness") is the Hellenistic-Jewish wisdom-glory tradition Paul appropriates christologically when he speaks of "the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (4:6). Carey Newman documents the Jewish glory-mysticism background.
- The "treasure in jars of clay" metaphor has Jewish precedent. The rabbinic apothegm preserved in *Pirqei Avot* 4:20 and *Ta'anit* 7a—Rabbi Joshua's exchange with the emperor's daughter, in which the daughter is taught that wisdom is preserved in plain vessels rather than precious ones—is a striking parallel, though probably later than Paul. The Jewish humility-and-mystery tradition (Sirach 3:17-20; the *anavim* of the Psalms) provides the broader background. Daniel Boyarin reads Paul's "treasure in jars of clay" as standing inside this Jewish humility tradition, not as Hellenistic-philosophical innovation.
- Jewish suffering-of-the-righteous traditions are essential. *4 Maccabees* (the Hellenistic-Jewish meditation on the martyrdom of Eleazar and the seven brothers) develops a theology in which the righteous sufferer's death is generative—a theology that 2 Cor 4:10-12 echoes structurally. The Suffering Servant of Isa 52-53 is the canonical fountainhead. The *Hodayot* (1QH) at Qumran preserve Second Temple meditation on the righteous afflicted-but-vindicated. The "afflicted but not crushed, persecuted but not forsaken" of 4:8-9 echoes Ps 22 and Ps 118 in the canonical Davidic-Psalmist mode of righteous suffering vindicated by God.
- Merkavah mysticism (Hekhalot literature, the *Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice* from Qumran) develops the glory-of-God-on-the-throne tradition. Paul's "glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (4:6) is an extraordinary christological compression of the Merkavah glory-tradition: where the visionary mystics ascend to behold the glory on the throne, Paul claims that the glory has descended into a human face that ordinary eyes have seen.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge**: They converge on the dignity of suffering taken up in obedience and on the participatory ontology of the afflicted-righteous. They diverge on the christological inflection: Paul's "glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" is a christologically intensified version of Merkavah glory-tradition that no Jewish reader accepts. They diverge on the *aiōn*-cosmology of 4:4: the apocalyptic-Jewish framework Paul shares with Qumran and *1 Enoch* identifies the present age as under hostile sovereignty, but the messianic-Jewish reading places the inaugurated solution in the Christ-event, which mainstream Jewish readings do not accept. The convergence is on the structural shape; the divergence is on the christological identification.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What Paul believed God was doing in this chapter is best captured as the *grounding of apostolic ministry in the cruciform pattern of Christ's death-and-life made visible in the apostle's body, oriented toward an eschatological glory that reframes all present affliction*. The chapter is autobiographical, theological, and existential at once. Two genuine theological tensions emerge.

**Tension 1: Theology of the cross versus theology of glory.** 4:7-12 and 4:16-18 are the most concentrated NT statement of glorification-through-suffering in the cruciform mode, and the traditions diverge sharply on what the suffering accomplishes.

- *Lutheran theologia crucis* (Heidelberg Disputation 1518; Bonhoeffer *Cost of Discipleship* and *Letters and Papers from Prison*; Jürgen Moltmann *The Crucified God*): God's chosen mode of self-revelation is hiddenness in suffering. The "afflicted but not crushed" pattern is not despite the gospel but is the very form the gospel takes in the world. Bonhoeffer radicalizes: the church only finds its true form when it is conformed to Christ's public dying; any "religion" that bypasses this is *theologia gloriae* in disguise. Moltmann pushes further: God identifies with suffering not strategically but ontologically; the Trinity's life is open to creaturely affliction.
- *Catholic redemptive suffering* (CCC 1502, 1521; John Paul II *Salvifici Doloris* 1984): suffering taken up in Christ becomes participation in the redemption already accomplished. Col 1:24's "filling up what is lacking in Christ's afflictions" supplies the controlling image; 2 Cor 4:10-12's "death at work in us, but life in you" reads naturally on this account. The Mystical Body extends Christ's redemption through the afflictions of its members. The Catholic tradition does not add to the sufficiency of Christ's atonement but distributes its application through the Body's participation.
- *Reformed sovereign suffering* (Calvin Inst. III.8; Berkhof; Murray): God's providence governs suffering; suffering sanctifies, drives the believer to faith, and witnesses to the world. 4:17's "this light momentary affliction is preparing for us an eternal weight of glory" is the canonical Reformed proof-text for the formative-not-meritorious function of affliction. Calvin's commentary emphasizes that the apostle's confidence is grounded in God's prior choice and ongoing sanctifying work, not in the apostle's performance under suffering.
- *Wesleyan progressive sanctification*: 4:16's "inner self is being renewed day by day" is the canonical Wesleyan proof-text for *gradus*-based sanctification. The believer is being progressively transformed in this life; perfection in love is possible by grace; suffering is one of the formative means. Wesley's *Plain Account of Christian Perfection* and the Holiness movement that followed deploy 4:16-18 as a foundational text.
- *Prosperity gospel* (Kenneth Hagin; Kenneth Copeland; modern variants): the prosperity gospel reads 4:7's "treasure in jars of clay" or skips it for 4:17's "eternal weight of glory" and effectively transmutes apostolic suffering into a doctrine of present material flourishing. Bonhoeffer's *cheap grace* critique applies *avant la lettre*—any reading that bypasses the *nekrōsis* of 4:10 is, on Bonhoefferian grounds, a *theologia gloriae* dressed up in apostolic vocabulary. Most magisterial Christian traditions (Catholic, Reformed, Lutheran, Eastern Orthodox, mainline Protestant, much evangelical Protestant) explicitly reject the prosperity gospel as a misreading of 2 Cor 4.
- *Apocalyptic-cosmic / structural reading* (Martyn, Gaventa, Campbell; in conversation with N.T. Wright on the "powers"): the apostolic *peristasis* catalog describes the lived collision between the gospel and "the god of this age" (4:4) — the present evil age genuinely under hostile sovereignty. On this reading 4:8-12 is not a generic spiritual-formative trial but the specific consequence of preaching a message that contradicts the political and economic powers of the present order; the church recognizes the apostolic *peristasis* most clearly when its gospel cuts against the grain of those powers, which is why the apostolic "death at work in us, but life in you" is public and structural, not merely interior.
- *Eastern Orthodox*: theosis through cruciform suffering. Maximus the Confessor and the Philokalia tradition read 4:7-12 as the apostolic instance of the Christ-pattern that the ascetic life pursues. The "treasure in jars of clay" is the *kenosis* of grace into creaturely vessels—a participation in the divine self-emptying.

The position least intuitive to most contemporary American evangelical readers is the apocalyptic-cosmic / structural reading. *Steelmanned*: it is not reducing the gospel to politics. It is asking *what kind of suffering* the apostolic *peristasis* catalog actually describes—and noting that "afflicted, perplexed, persecuted, struck down" describes the lived condition of those who are crushed by the unjust arrangements the church too often blesses or ignores. The hermeneutical claim is that the apostolic suffering Paul describes is not a generic spiritual-formative trial but a specific consequence of preaching a gospel that contradicts the powers of the present age (4:4's *theos tou aiōnos toutou*) — so that an apolitical reading of 4:8-12 has *already* been politicized, in favor of the existing arrangement. The American evangelical instinct that this over-politicizes the gospel is fair; the counter-instinct that a purely interior reading domesticates Paul's cosmic claim is also fair. *Where this leaves us*: 4:7-12 will not yield to readings that make apostolic suffering decorative or readings that make it merely individual-spiritual. The *nekrōsis* Paul carries in his body is public and structural, and the life it mediates is public and structural as well.

**Tension 2: Inner / outer person anthropology in 4:16—dualism, dichotomy, or holism?** *Ho exō hēmōn anthrōpos / ho esō* (4:16) is one of the most consequential anthropological verses in the NT, and the traditions read it very differently.

- *Greek-philosophical dualism appropriated* (older patristic and medieval reading): Plato's *Republic* IX, Philo's *Quis Heres*, and the Hellenistic-Jewish moral-philosophical tradition supply a body-soul dualism in which the inner person is the rational soul and the outer person is the bodily-passionate self. Augustine's *De Trinitate* 14 develops the Christian version: the *imago Dei* is in the *homo interior*, the rational soul in mind, knowledge, and love. The medieval mystical tradition (Bernard, the Victorines, the Rhineland mystics) deepens the inner-outer ascent grammar. Cartesian dualism is the secular descendant of this Christianized-Platonic appropriation.
- *Pauline dichotomous-but-holistic* (Robert Gundry *Sōma in Biblical Theology* 1976; James Dunn *Theology of Paul the Apostle* 1998): Paul's anthropology is not Platonic-philosophical body-soul dualism. The *sōma* in Pauline usage is the whole person under bodily/social conditions, not a "body" set against a "soul." The *exō / esō* contrast in 4:16 is functional and eschatological—the outer person under the conditions of the present age (subject to *phthora*, decay) and the inner person being renewed by the Spirit toward the resurrection life. This is Hebrew-Jewish anthropology with Hellenistic vocabulary, not Platonic dualism.
- *Catholic synthesis* (CCC 362-368; Aquinas *ST* I qq. 75-89): the human person is body and soul united; the soul is the form of the body (Aristotelian-Aquinas); body and soul together constitute the *imago Dei*. The inner-outer language of 4:16 is read within this hylomorphic anthropology rather than dualistic. Vatican II's *Gaudium et Spes* 14 affirms unity of body and soul.
- *Reformed dichotomous* (Hodge; Berkhof; most magisterial Protestant tradition): body and soul are real distinct realities created together, separated at death, reunited at resurrection. The *exō / esō* of 4:16 maps loosely onto this dichotomy without the full Platonic-dualist baggage.
- *Eastern Orthodox*: holistic anthropology rooted in the doctrine of theosis. The whole person—body, soul, spirit—is being transfigured in the divine energies. The hesychast tradition (Gregory Palamas) emphasizes the body's participation in prayer and theosis, against any spiritualizing dualism.
- *Post-Cartesian critique of dualism* (Charles Taylor *Sources of the Self*; Stanley Hauerwas; Nancey Murphy *Bodies and Souls, or Spirited Bodies?*): the modern Cartesian inheritance has produced a dualism that the NT does not actually teach. Pauline anthropology is more holistic than Christian readings have often allowed; the renewal of the inner self in 4:16 is the renewal of the *whole person* in its inward-Spirit-related dimension, not the renewal of an immaterial soul against a material body. Hauerwas and Murphy push the post-Cartesian recovery of holistic Christian anthropology in conversation with cognitive science and philosophy of mind.

The position least intuitive to most traditional Western Christians is the post-Cartesian holistic reading. *Steelmanned*: the post-Cartesian critique is not denying that humans have an interior dimension or that the mind is real. It is denying that the Christian tradition has read its own anthropology accurately when it has imported Cartesian (and through Cartesian, Platonic) dualism into Pauline categories. Gundry's lexicographical work is exacting: *sōma* in Paul does not mean what *body* means in Cartesian dualism; *psychē* in Paul does not mean what *soul* means in Cartesian dualism. The Pauline picture is closer to *spirited bodies* than to *embodied spirits*. The pastoral payoff is significant: a holistic anthropology refuses the spiritualizing of pain, the dismissal of the body in formation, and the privatization of inner life from public bodily action. The traditional Western dualist response is fair on its own terms—classical Christian metaphysics needs *some* mode of distinguishing the body that decays from the person who survives death—but the holistic critique is right that the way this distinction has been worked out in much Western Christianity is closer to Plato than to Paul. *Where this leaves us*: 4:16 will not yield to readings that flatten the inner-outer distinction or readings that hyper-spiritualize it. Paul has insisted that the *whole person* is being renewed by the Spirit even as the *whole person*, in its outward-bodily dimension under the present age, is wasting away. The renewal is real now; the wasting is real now; both will be resolved in the resurrection of the body that chapter 5 takes up.

**One unresolved question**: 4:17 calls present afflictions "light and momentary" and weighs them against an "eternal weight of glory beyond all comparison," and Paul has just catalogued afflictions that nearly killed him (1:8) and that he describes as carrying the dying of Jesus in his body (4:10). The arithmetic is jarring. What does it mean to call genuinely crushing affliction "light and momentary"—not by minimizing it but by weighing it against an eschatological glory whose weight is incomparably greater—and how would a community recognize when its language about future glory has degenerated into a denial of present pain rather than a faithful relativization of it?

## The Thread

- **4:6 ("Let light shine out of darkness") → Genesis 1:3 / John 1:4-9**: Paul's claim that the same divine speech-act that inaugurated cosmic light now inaugurates apostolic illumination is one of the boldest creation-redemption parallels in the NT. John's prologue ("the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it") is the closest parallel. Reading 4:6 without Gen 1:3 collapses the cosmological-soteriological scope of the claim.
- **4:7 ("treasure in jars of clay") → 2 Cor 12:7-10 / Phil 2:5-11 / Mark 14:3**: The treasure-in-cheap-vessel metaphor threads through Paul's apostolic self-presentation (the thorn in the flesh of 12:7-10; the *kenosis* of Phil 2). Mark 14:3's alabaster jar broken to release its precious contents is the same theological grammar in narrative form.
- **4:10 ("carrying in the body the death of Jesus") → Galatians 6:17 / Philippians 3:10 / Romans 6:3-11**: The cruciform-conformity language threads through Paul. Gal 6:17's "I bear on my body the marks of Jesus" is the Pauline parallel; Rom 6:3-11 is the baptismal-ontological foundation; Phil 3:10 ("becoming like him in his death") is the same theology in a different rhetorical register.
- **4:13 ("I believed and so I spoke") → Psalm 116:10 LXX / Acts 4:20**: Paul links apostolic *parrhesia* to the Davidic-Psalmist's faith-grounded speech. Peter and John's "we cannot but speak of what we have seen and heard" (Acts 4:20) is the apostolic parallel. The psalmic-prophetic mode of bold speech under affliction runs through both Testaments and into the apostolic age.
- **4:17 ("eternal weight of glory") → Romans 8:18 / 1 Peter 1:6-7**: The present-affliction-versus-future-glory axis runs through the NT. Rom 8:18's "the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory that is to be revealed" is the closest Pauline parallel; 1 Pet 1:6-7 ("various trials… so that the tested genuineness of your faith… may be found to result in praise and glory and honor at the revelation of Jesus Christ") is the Petrine parallel. The eschatological weighing of present and future is the canonical NT consolation against affliction.
