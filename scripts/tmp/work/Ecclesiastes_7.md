# Ecclesiastes 7: The House of Mourning, the Crooked Work, and the Misogyny Problem

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 7 marks a tonal shift. After the despair of chapter 6, Qohelet returns to the *tov-min* ("better than") proverb form that the wisdom tradition had inherited. The chapter contains fourteen *tov-min* proverbs in the opening cluster (7:1–14), more than any other chapter in the Hebrew Bible. But Qohelet's *tov-min* proverbs are not Proverbs' *tov-min* proverbs: they invert, qualify, and complicate the conventional comparatives, producing what Crenshaw calls "wisdom turned against itself." The day of death better than the day of birth (7:1), sorrow better than laughter (7:3), the house of mourning better than the house of feasting (7:2) — these are not Proverbs' priorities.

The Persian-period setting (5th–4th c. BC) is supported in chapter 7 by Seow's philological signals: *šallîṭ* (7:19, "rulers"), *yākōl* + *l-* in 7:13 (Late Biblical syntactic), and *zākar* in 7:14 in the future-oriented sense. Material culture in Persian-period Yehud included professional mourners and formal mourning houses — the *beit ʾēbel* (house of mourning, 7:2, 7:4) is a known social institution, evidenced in the *bēt marzeaḥ* references in earlier biblical texts (Jeremiah 16:5, Amos 6:7) and in later inscriptions from Marseilles and Palmyra. Qohelet's preference for the *beit ʾēbel* over the *beit mishteh* draws on a known social contrast, not a metaphor.

The "do not be overly righteous" passage (7:15–18) is the chapter's most contested locus. The Hebrew *ʾal tehî ṣaddîq harbēh* has been read as Aristotelian *via media* (Maimonides, Ralbag), as anti-works-righteousness (Luther), or as Qohelet's pragmatic recognition that overzealous righteousness is socially dangerous (Crenshaw). The "bitter woman" passage (7:26–28) is the other contested locus, and a notorious crux. Fox (*A Time to Tear Down*, 1999) reads the verses as Qohelet quoting a misogynistic tradition in order to subvert it in 7:29; Crenshaw (OTL) and Seow (Anchor) read them as Qohelet's own view, inherited from the wisdom tradition's anxiety about the "strange woman" (Proverbs 5–7); Longman (NICOT) treats the passage as part of Qohelet's deliberately overstated despairing voice that the epilogist later qualifies.

Five Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. *Tov-min* (טוֹב מִן) — the "better than" form, fourteen instances. *Šēm tov* (שֵׁם טוֹב, "good name," 7:1) — honorable reputation, the wisdom-tradition's durable good. *Yašār* (יָשָׁר, "upright," 7:29) — what God made man, set in contrast with *ḥiššĕbônôt* (חִשְּׁבוֹנוֹת, "schemes," 7:29). *ʿāqōm* (עָקֹם, "crooked," 7:13) — what God has made and what no human can straighten. *Mār mimmāwet* (מַר מִמָּוֶת, "more bitter than death," 7:26) — the phrase used of the dangerous woman, a stock wisdom-tradition formulation (Proverbs 5:3–6, 7:26–27) Qohelet either inherits or appropriates.

ANE parallels are dense. The Egyptian *Instruction of Ankhsheshonq* contains warnings against the dangerous woman that parallel 7:26. The Mesopotamian *Counsels of Wisdom* contains "do not marry a prostitute." Gilgamesh Tablet VI (Ishtar's seduction) provides the mythological background. *Ptahhotep* anticipates 7:9. Qohelet stands within an ANE wisdom stream he selectively inherits and contests.

Textually: 4QQoh-a preserves portions of chapter 7, including 7:1–10 with minor variations from MT. LXX is close to MT throughout. Targum Qohelet expands the misogyny passage with halakhic material about the *isha rasha* (wicked woman) of rabbinic tradition.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 7 falls into four units. The "better than" cluster (7:1–14), the "do not be overly righteous" passage (7:15–18), the wisdom and confession cluster (7:19–22), and the search for wisdom and the misogyny passage (7:23–29). The chapter is more diffuse than chapter 6; the units are linked by the leitwort *ḥākam* / *ḥokmâ* (wise / wisdom, used eight times) and by the search for *ḥešbôn* (scheme, accounting, 7:25, 7:27, 7:29).

The "better than" cluster (7:1–14) is the densest *tov-min* concentration in the book. Verse 1 inverts Proverbs' standard valuation of the day of birth as blessing: *the day of death better than the day of birth*. Crenshaw notes that the inversion turns Proverbs' priorities upside down. The proverb is sometimes read as Qohelet's death-positivity (the day of death is the day of consummation; the day of birth is the day of beginning a precarious life), sometimes as conventional sapiential wisdom about reputation (a man's reputation is fixed at death and not at birth). Seow reads it both ways. Verse 2 — *better to go to the house of mourning than to the house of feasting* — develops the same theme: mortality is the great teacher, and the wise heart attends to it. Verse 3 — *sorrow is better than laughter* — pairs with verse 4 — *the heart of the wise is in the house of mourning* — to construct what Murphy calls Qohelet's *seven-fold beatitude of mourning* (the four verses 7:1–4 each contain a different *tov-min* proverb on mourning).

Verses 5–7 turn from mourning to rebuke and laughter. The crackling of thorns under a pot (7:6) is a vivid image: thorns burn fast and hot but produce no lasting heat — the laughter of fools is similarly evanescent. Verse 7 — *oppression drives the wise into madness, and a bribe corrupts the heart* — is sometimes read as out of place but Seow argues it belongs to the chapter's pattern of warnings against social pressures that compromise wisdom.

Verses 8–10 add three proverbs on patience, anger, and nostalgia. Verse 10 — *say not, "why were the former days better than these?"* — is Crenshaw's *locus classicus* of Qohelet's rebuke of wisdom-tradition nostalgia. Verses 11–12 affirm wisdom *with* an inheritance — wisdom plus material security is the actual social good. Verses 13–14 are the chapter's most theologically loaded: *consider the work of God: who can make straight what he has made crooked?* / *In the day of prosperity be joyful, and in the day of adversity consider: God has made the one as well as the other*. Both are God's work; both are *crooked* in the sense that the human cannot rectify them. Seow reads this as Qohelet's most balanced theological statement; Fox reads it as recognition that even the wise have no leverage on the divine ordering.

The "do not be overly righteous" passage (7:15–18) opens with a direct contradiction of Proverbs' theology: *there is a righteous man who perishes in his righteousness, and there is a wicked man who prolongs his life in his evildoing* (7:15). Proverbs would not have written this sentence. Qohelet's response is the famously contested 7:16–18: do not be overly righteous, do not be overly wise, do not be overly wicked, do not be a fool, the one who fears God shall come out from both. The phrase *yēṣēʾ ʾet-kullām* ("come out from both," 7:18) is grammatically ambiguous — it can mean "escape both," "fulfill both," or "go beyond both." The translation choice carries enormous theological weight.

The wisdom and confession cluster (7:19–22) tightens the focus. *Wisdom gives strength to the wise more than ten rulers* (7:19) — wisdom as practical power. *Surely there is not a righteous man on earth who does good and never sins* (7:20) — Qohelet's clearest statement of universal sinfulness, a verse Paul will quote in Romans 3:10 (though through the LXX-derived Psalm 14:3). The servant-cursing-master proverb of 7:21–22 turns the universality of sin domestic: do not take to heart what others say, because *you yourself* have cursed others.

The search-for-wisdom and misogyny passage (7:23–29) is the chapter's most contested unit. Qohelet announces the search — *I said "I will be wise," but it was far from me* (7:23) — and reports the result: *I find something more bitter than death: the woman whose heart is snares and nets* (7:26). Then 7:27–28: *adding one thing to another to find the scheme of things — which my soul has sought repeatedly, but I have not found. One man among a thousand I found, but a woman among all these I have not found*. Then the chapter's closing summary (7:29): *God made man upright, but they have sought out many schemes*.

The literary architecture of 7:23–29 is debated. Fox argues that the "bitter woman" passage is Qohelet's quotation of a traditional misogyny that Qohelet then subverts in 7:29 (the "schemes" of men include that misogyny itself, which deviates from God's upright making of human beings). Crenshaw and Seow argue that the passage is straightforwardly Qohelet's view, attached to the wisdom-tradition complaint about the dangerous woman (Proverbs 5–7). The interpretive divergence is sharp, and either way the verses' plain sense is harsh enough that interpreters across the spectrum agree it cannot simply be domesticated.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical wisdom arc, chapter 7 is the book's most direct engagement with the proverbial tradition. The fourteen *tov-min* proverbs of 7:1–14 are formally Proverbs' form, but their content inverts Proverbs' values. Crenshaw, Murphy, and Brueggemann all note the dialectical relationship: Qohelet uses Proverbs' form to undo Proverbs' confidence. The chapter is also the book's clearest statement of the failure of retributive theology (7:15) and of universal human sinfulness (7:20).

In Jewish interpretive trajectory, Qohelet Rabbah expands 7:1 ("the day of death better than the day of birth") at length, drawing on the rabbinic tradition that one's eternal reward is determined at death, not at birth. The midrash redirects the proverb's potentially nihilistic reading toward eschatological reward. Targum Qohelet similarly inserts afterlife material. Among medieval commentators, Rashi reads 7:1 with the rabbinic tradition; Ibn Ezra is philological; Ralbag reads the chapter as Aristotelian wisdom (7:16–18 as Aristotelian *via media* — the mean between excess and defect in righteousness, wickedness, and wisdom). Sforno reads 7:18 as the *fear of God* providing the practical *via media*. Maimonides, in *Guide* III.51 and elsewhere, draws on Qohelet 7 for his theory of the philosophical *via media*: the wise man is neither extreme in his pursuit of righteousness (which can become spiritual pride or social conflict) nor extreme in laxity (which becomes folly). For Maimonides, 7:16–18 is the *locus classicus* of wisdom moderation, and the *yēṣēʾ ʾet-kullām* of 7:18 is the philosophical achievement of fulfilling both conditions.

Modern Jewish reception has read the "bitter woman" passage with increasing critical attention; Fox's reading — that the verses are quoted misogyny that Qohelet subverts in 7:29 — has been received cautiously by Crenshaw, Seow, and others as reading the intent into the text rather than out of it. The post-Holocaust Jewish reception has read 7:15 (the righteous who perish in their righteousness) with renewed gravity: the verse is not abstract but descriptive of the catastrophe.

In Christian interpretive trajectory, Gregory of Nyssa's extant *Homilies on Ecclesiastes* do not extend to chapter 7, but his treatment of the *house of mourning* motif in his other works (the *Life of Macrina*, the consolation literature) draws on 7:2 to argue that mourning teaches the soul what feasting cannot. Jerome's commentary reads 7:1 strongly through ascetic lenses: the day of death is the day of release from worldly attachment; the *tov-min* cluster is foundation for Christian *contemptus mundi*. The patristic and medieval reception of 7:1–4 was strongly ascetic, and the verses became central to monastic *memento mori* literature. Aquinas reads 7:16–18 alongside his treatment of the moral virtues as means: virtues stand between excess and defect, and the *fear of God* is the rectitude that maintains the mean. The Aristotelian reading of 7:16–18 found in Aquinas is continuous with Maimonides.

Luther's *Notes on Ecclesiastes* (1526/1532) reads 7:16–18 explosively as anti-works-righteousness: the prohibition of being "overly righteous" is the prohibition of attempting to establish one's own righteousness before God, which is the prohibition of works-righteousness. For Luther, 7:20 (no righteous man on earth never sins) is the chapter's load-bearing verse, anticipating Romans 3:10. The Lutheran tradition has read 7:16–18 in this gospel-precursor register ever since. The Reformed tradition (Westminster Larger Catechism Q. 149, citing 7:20 on universal sinfulness) has agreed about 7:20 but read 7:16–18 more cautiously, often as warning against pharisaic self-righteousness rather than as anti-works-righteousness *tout court*. Calvin's silence on Ecclesiastes leaves a Reformed gap; Bullinger's contemporary commentary reads 7:16–18 as warning against the appearance of righteousness without inward grace.

Modern critical commentary has been particularly attentive to 7:15–18 and 7:25–29. Crenshaw reads 7:16–18 as Qohelet's recognition that overzealous righteousness is socially dangerous, not as *via media*. Seow reads it as pragmatic counsel against destabilizing excess. Fox reads 7:25–29 as quoted-and-subverted misogyny. Longman reads the chapter as Qohelet's despairing voice awaiting epilogist-correction. Provan reads more positively.

Where the trajectories converge: virtually all interpreters across traditions agree that 7:20 is one of the OT's clearest statements of universal sinfulness. Where they diverge: whether 7:16–18 is Aristotelian *via media* (Maimonides, Ralbag, Aquinas), anti-works-righteousness (Luther), or pragmatic counsel (Crenshaw, Seow); whether 7:25–29 is straightforward misogyny (Crenshaw, Seow) or quoted-and-subverted misogyny (Fox); and whether the *crooked* of 7:13 is theodicy-laden (Calvinist providence, Augustinian permission) or simply opaque (Fox's reading).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two genuine tensions deserve sustained attention.

The first is **the "do not be overly righteous" passage** (7:16–18). The Maimonidean-Aristotelian reading treats the verses as the philosophical *via media*: the wise man's virtue is the mean between excess and defect. Maimonides develops this in *Hilchot Deot* and in *Guide* III.51, where the *fear of God* of 7:18 is the practical achievement of fulfilling both conditions — the virtuous mean. The reading has continuity with Aquinas, who locates the moral virtues at the rational mean between extremes. The Aristotelian reading has the merit of taking the chapter's practical orientation seriously: Qohelet is not denying that righteousness is good, but warning against forms of righteousness-pursuit that destroy the pursuer.

The Lutheran *simul iustus et peccator* reading rejects the *via media* on theological grounds. There are no degrees of righteousness before God; one is righteous by grace through faith or one is not. Luther reads 7:16 not as warning against excess in righteousness but as warning against the works-righteousness that imagines righteousness can be achieved through human effort at all. The "overly" of 7:16 is, for Luther, the "any at all" — any human attempt to establish righteousness is *too much*, because it presumes to do what only Christ has done. The reading is not philosophically continuous with the Aristotelian; it is a different theology of righteousness altogether.

The Wesleyan progressive sanctification reading, developed in the eighteenth century by John Wesley and the Methodist tradition, takes a third path. Sanctification is real progressive growth in holiness, but the human's pursuit of sanctification must be balanced — not because there are degrees of righteousness before God, but because the pursuit itself can become disordered (spiritual pride, scrupulosity, neglect of the neighbor). Wesley read 7:16–18 as warning against perfectionism that becomes self-righteousness, and he pastorally applied the verses to early Methodist holiness movements that had begun to develop perfectionist tendencies.

Steelmanning the Maimonidean reading: the chapter's broader argument supports it more naturally than the Lutheran or Wesleyan readings. Qohelet is concerned throughout with practical wisdom in a world where retributive theology has failed (7:15). The Maimonidean *via media* offers a framework for wise living that is responsive to that practical concern: avoid extremes, fear God, recognize the limits of righteousness-pursuit. The Lutheran reading imports a New Testament theological framework (justification by faith) that the chapter itself does not articulate; the Wesleyan reading imports a doctrine of sanctification that the chapter likewise does not articulate. The Maimonidean reading takes the chapter at face value as practical wisdom.

Where this leaves us: the Maimonidean reading has the strongest claim on the chapter's own register; the Lutheran and Wesleyan readings require importing theological frameworks the chapter does not develop. But the Lutheran reading's intuition — that 7:16 is warning against the *kind* of righteousness-pursuit that is itself self-defeating — is registered in the Maimonidean reading, just framed differently. The traditions are closer than their polemics suggest.

The second tension is **the "bitter woman" passage** (7:26–28). The verses are direct: *I find something more bitter than death: the woman whose heart is snares and nets, and whose hands are fetters. He who pleases God escapes her, but the sinner is taken by her* (7:26); and *one man among a thousand I found, but a woman among all these I have not found* (7:28).

Fox's reading argues that Qohelet is quoting traditional misogyny in order to subvert it: the closing summary of 7:29 (*God made man [adam, generic humanity] upright, but they have sought out many schemes*) functions as Qohelet's correction — the misogynistic tradition is one of the *schemes* that humanity has invented in deviation from God's upright making. The reading has the merit of reconciling the passage with the chapter's broader argument and with the LXX's reading. But Fox has been criticized — by Crenshaw, Seow, Murphy, and others — for reading the subversive intent into the text rather than out of it. The Hebrew of 7:26–28 does not mark itself as quotation; the *I found* (*māṣāʾtî*) verb runs through the unit and identifies the observation as Qohelet's own.

Crenshaw's straightforward reading takes the verses at face value: Qohelet shares the wisdom tradition's harsh register about the "dangerous woman" (Proverbs 5–7), and the verses are his own view, whatever apologetic readers wish for. Seow concurs. The implication is that the wisdom corpus includes this kind of material, and that Qohelet's voice — for all its skeptical sophistication — is not exempt from it.

A third strand — represented in Longman's commentary — reads the verses as one more piece of the deliberately overstated despairing perspective that the book's epilogist (12:9–14) frames at a distance: 7:28's "a woman among all these I have not found" is the bleak conclusion of a search ("which my soul has sought repeatedly, but I have not found") that 7:23 already pronounced a failure, and the canon presents that failure as a foil, not as a verdict to be endorsed.

Where this leaves us: the verses are harsh in their plain reading, regardless of whether Fox's subversive-quotation hypothesis is correct, and the wisdom tradition's anxiety about the "dangerous woman" — running from Proverbs 5–7 through Qohelet 7 to Sirach 25 — has to be acknowledged rather than apologetically explained away. The Christian tradition's reading of 7:26–28 — particularly in the patristic and medieval periods, but extending into modern evangelical apologetics — has too often perpetuated that register through allegorizations that always located the *bitter woman* as a real or symbolic threat to the male wisdom-seeker. The honest position is the one Crenshaw, Seow, and Longman share from different angles: don't pretend the verse says something other than it does, and don't treat Qohelet's failed search (7:23, 7:28) as Scripture's last word on the subject.

**Where this leaves us at the chapter's end**: the chapter closes with 7:29 — *God made man upright, but they have sought out many schemes*. The verse can be read as Qohelet's anthropology (humans were created upright but have invented evil) or as Qohelet's epistemology (humans were created to know simply but have invented complications) or as Qohelet's ethic (humans were given a clear path but have invented many byways). The chapter's lingering question: *if God made man upright but humans have sought out many schemes, then what is the place of the wisdom-tradition's own schemes — including its misogynistic schemes — in the catalog of human deviations?* Qohelet does not answer.

## The Thread

Cross-references for the thread, non-obvious. The "day of death better than the day of birth" (7:1) tracks against Job 3:1–10 (Job's curse of his birth-day) and against Jeremiah 20:14–18 (Jeremiah's parallel curse), but Qohelet generalizes what for Job and Jeremiah was particular grief into a *tov-min* proverb. The "no righteous man on earth who does good and never sins" of 7:20 is quoted by Paul in Romans 3:10 (though Paul reaches it through Psalm 14:3 in LXX form), and the chain Qohelet-Psalm-Paul is the canonical thread of universal sinfulness from wisdom skepticism through psalmic lament to apostolic theology. Sirach 25:13–26 develops the dangerous-woman trope of 7:26 at length, and Sirach 26:1–4 develops the *good wife* counter-trope; Sirach's expansion of the wisdom tradition's gender register is the inter-testamental staging-point for later Jewish and Christian misogyny. James 4:13–16 picks up on 7:13–14 (the crooked work of God, the day of prosperity and adversity) by warning the merchant-planner that he does not know what tomorrow will bring; James reads Qohelet's epistemological humility into the eschatological frame of patient endurance. The forward thread from Qohelet 7's "no righteous man" through Romans 3 to Reformation theology is the most powerful canonical inheritance the chapter has, and it has been the engine of Protestant reception of Ecclesiastes since Luther.
