# Acts 19: Ephesus, the Sons of Sceva, and the Riot of the Silversmiths

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Acts 19 places Paul in Ephesus for what becomes the longest documented stop in his missionary career—roughly three years (cf. 19:8 + 19:10 + 20:31). First-century **Ephesus** was the fourth-largest city of the Roman Empire (after Rome, Alexandria, and Antioch), the capital of the senatorial province of Asia, the seat of the proconsul, and home to the **Temple of Artemis**, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. The city's population is variously estimated at 200,000-250,000. Its harbor (already silting up by the first century, fully silted by late antiquity) connected it to the Aegean trade routes; the **Arcadian Way**, a 530-meter colonnaded street, ran from the harbor to the **theater**.

The **Temple of Artemis** (the Artemision) is a presence the chapter assumes throughout. Built and rebuilt several times since the Archaic period, the temple in Paul's day was the fourth temple, completed in the late 4th century BCE after the famous arsonist Herostratus burned the third in 356 BCE (the very night, ancient sources noted, that Alexander the Great was born). Pliny the Elder (*NH* 36.95) gives its dimensions: 425 by 220 feet, 127 columns each 60 feet high—roughly four times the footprint of the Parthenon. **Anton Bammer** and **Ulrike Muss** (Austrian Archaeological Institute excavations from 1965 onward) have produced the standard archaeological synthesis. Almost nothing remains of the temple today—a single reconstructed column stands on the marshy site—but the foundations and votive deposits have been thoroughly excavated.

The **theater** at Ephesus, into which the rioting crowd dragged Paul's companions (19:29), has been excavated and partially restored. **Friedmund Hueber's** publications document the seating capacity of approximately 25,000—a figure that aligns precisely with the kind of mob the chapter describes. The theater is built into the western slope of Mount Pion (Panayır Dağ) and faces west toward the harbor; visitors today can stand on the same stones where the silversmiths chanted "Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!" for two hours.

The cult of ***Artemis Ephesia*** was distinctive within the broader Greek Artemis tradition. The Ephesian goddess was depicted with a multi-breasted (or, on the more recent reading, multi-egg or multi-bull-testicle) torso and a rigid, mummiform body wrapped in zodiac and animal imagery. **Lynn LiDonnici's** *The Images of Artemis Ephesia and Greco-Roman Worship* (1999) is the leading study; she argues that the figure should be read not as a fertility goddess in the simple sense but as a cosmic mother whose body symbolizes universal nurturance. The cult was administered by the *neōkoroi* (temple wardens, the term Luke uses at 19:35 of the city itself) and the *megabyzoi* (eunuch priests). The "**sacred stone that fell from the sky**" (19:35) likely refers to a meteoric image (*diopetēs*), a common feature of ancient cult sites. Pliny mentions such stones at Pessinus and elsewhere.

The chapter's economic substructure rests on the **silversmith trade**. Demetrius and his guild manufactured **silver shrines** (*naous argyrous*, 19:24)—miniature shrines or aedicula housing small images of Artemis, sold to pilgrims. Excavation has not produced any silver examples (the metal would have been melted down), but terracotta votive shrines of similar form have been recovered, and a famous inscription from Salutaris (104 CE) records the donation of multiple gold and silver Artemis statuettes for procession. Pilgrim economics drove a substantial portion of the Ephesian economy. **Paul Trebilco's** *The Early Christians in Ephesus from Paul to Ignatius* (2004) is the foundational scholarly study; **Helmut Koester's** *Ephesos: Metropolis of Asia* documents the city's archaeology and religious diversity.

The **Library of Celsus**, often photographed as the iconic Ephesian ruin, postdates Paul (built c. 110-135 CE), but it occupies the same urban core where Paul taught. The **Hall of Tyrannus** (19:9)—a daily lecture hall where Paul taught for two years—has not been identified archaeologically, but it fits a known pattern of philosophical lecture halls in major Hellenistic cities. Codex Bezae adds a detail: Paul taught "from the fifth hour to the tenth"—11 a.m. to 4 p.m., the heat of the day when the hall would have been free. Whether or not the textual variant is original, the cultural pattern is correct: a teacher rented or borrowed space during the off-hours.

Three Greek terms anchor the chapter's religious texture. ***Artemis Ephesia*** (Ἄρτεμις Ἐφεσία) is the formal cult title. ***Megalē Artemis Ephesiōn*** (Μεγάλη Ἄρτεμις Ἐφεσίων, "Great is Artemis of the Ephesians," 19:28, 34) is a documented liturgical acclamation; the formula appears on inscriptions and coins. ***Asiarchs*** (Ἀσιάρχαι, 19:31) were senior provincial officials, members of the wealthy elite who funded games and imperial cult observances; that some were friends of Paul (19:31) is a striking detail and indicates the social reach of the Ephesian church into the upper civic strata.

The **Sons of Sceva** episode (19:13-17) plugs into the well-documented world of first-century Mediterranean magic. **Hans Dieter Betz's** *The Greek Magical Papyri in Translation* (1986) catalogues the magical formulary tradition: invocation of foreign divine names, layered powers, threats and binding-spells, and the specific use of "Jesus" as a name of power (PGM IV.3007-3019: "I adjure you by the Jesus, the God of the Hebrews"—a striking pagan parallel to Acts 19:13). Ephesus was particularly associated with magic; the so-called ***Ephesia grammata*** were six magical syllables (*askion, kataskion, lix, tetrax, damnameneus, aision*) inscribed on the cult statue of Artemis and circulated as protective amulets across the Greek world (Plutarch *Quaestiones convivales* 7.5; Athenaeus 12.548c). The **fifty thousand pieces of silver** worth of magic books burned at 19:19 (probably 50,000 silver drachmae, equivalent to many years of laborer's wages) is a concrete economic measure of the magical-text economy.

The chapter's ***neōkoros*** (νεωκόρος, "temple keeper," 19:35) for the city of Ephesus is technically attested. By the late first century the title was used of cities that hosted imperial temples; in Paul's day it more typically described individual temple wardens, but the term was already extending to civic boasting about Artemis. The town clerk's speech is full of properly Roman-civic vocabulary—**proconsuls** (*anthypatoi*, plural, possibly because the proconsulship was vacant or in transition; or perhaps a generalizing reference to Roman officials), the **regular assembly** (*ennomos ekklēsia*, the legally constituted civic gathering), the danger of being charged with **rioting** (*stasis*, the Roman crime of civil disturbance, punishable by removal of civic privileges).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Acts 19 is structurally pivotal. It marks the high point of the Pauline mission's success ("all the residents of Asia heard the word of the Lord," 19:10) and simultaneously plants the seed of its disruption (the riot, the resolution to go to Jerusalem, the Spirit-driven impulse toward Rome at 19:21). The chapter's narrative arc moves from spiritual transformation (rebaptism of John's disciples, exorcisms, magic-book burning) through Pauline strategic resolution (19:21) to civic crisis (the riot). Each scene escalates the chapter's claim: the gospel is reshaping not only individual conversions but the religious-economic substructure of the city.

The chapter's **opening rebaptism** (19:1-7) is one of the most theologically loaded passages in Acts. Twelve men, knowing only John's baptism, are rebaptized in the name of the Lord Jesus, receive the laying on of hands, and speak in tongues and prophesy. The structure parallels Pentecost (Acts 2), the Samaritan reception (Acts 8), and Cornelius's household (Acts 10), forming what Tannehill calls the **fourfold Lukan Spirit-reception pattern**. Each reception confirms the gospel's reach across a boundary: Jewish (Pentecost), Samaritan (Acts 8), Gentile (Acts 10), and now disciples-of-John (Acts 19). The narrative order is significant: the chapter begins with the Spirit descending on the marginal periphery of the Jesus movement and ends with the gospel's encounter with the city's most powerful religious-economic structure.

The **Sons of Sceva** episode (19:13-17) is one of the few moments of genuine humor in Acts. The seven sons—identified, oddly, as belonging to a Jewish high priest, though no Sceva appears in any extant high-priestly list (probably a self-promoting claim, perhaps a synagogal honorific)—attempt to deploy "Jesus" as a magical name. The demon's reply ("Jesus I know, and Paul I recognize, but who are you?") is a deliberate punchline; the sons flee naked and wounded. Luke's literary purpose is twofold: to differentiate apostolic miracle from magical technique, and to mark Christianity's incompatibility with the magical economy. The episode triggers the magic-book burning—the gospel's penetration into the religious imagination of the city.

The **riot scene** (19:23-41) is one of Luke's most accomplished pieces of dramatic narrative. He builds from one provocateur (Demetrius) to a craft guild meeting, to a city-wide rumor, to a confused mob filling the theater, to two hours of unison chanting, to the town clerk's deflating legal speech. The narrative has the texture of eyewitness reporting: the detail that "most of them did not know why they had come together" (19:32) is a touch of social-psychological realism that Luke does not need but supplies anyway. **Witherington's** socio-rhetorical commentary treats the chapter as Luke's most accomplished extended narrative scene.

**Western Text variants** continue. Codex Bezae expands several places, most notably 19:9 ("from the fifth hour to the tenth") and 19:14 (specifying "in which they wished to do this also"). The variants are interpretive rather than primary, supplying details the original leaves implicit.

The **resolution to go to Jerusalem and then Rome** (19:21) is the chapter's most important structural beat. Luke marks it carefully: "Paul resolved in the Spirit"—the verb (*etheto*, "set" or "purposed") echoes Old Testament prophetic vocations and signals that this is not Pauline strategic planning but Spirit-directed orientation toward the chapter's true endpoint. From this verse onward, every narrative arc in Acts moves toward Rome.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The chapter has been a battleground for several distinct theological traditions, and its interpretive history is correspondingly thick.

The **rebaptism episode** (19:1-7) has been the foundational text in the divergent baptismal-confirmation theologies of Western Christianity. **Catholic confirmation theology** (Aquinas *Summa* III q.72; Trent's *Decree on the Sacraments*) treats Paul's laying-on-of-hands as a distinct sacramental moment, separate from baptism, conferring the seven gifts of the Spirit. The Catechism cites Acts 19 alongside Acts 8:14-17 as biblical warrant for confirmation. **Anglican** theology preserves a similar structure (the Book of Common Prayer's Order of Confirmation), as do some **Lutheran** liturgies. **Eastern Orthodox** practice administers chrismation immediately after baptism, treating both as a single initiation; for Orthodox readers, Acts 19 is Pauline restoration of an incomplete initiation, not a precedent for separating baptism and chrismation.

**Pentecostal "subsequence" theology** (Charles Parham, William Seymour, the Azusa Street tradition) read Acts 19 as paradigmatic for a "second blessing"—a Spirit-baptism distinct from and subsequent to conversion, evidenced by speaking in tongues. **Roger Stronstad's** *The Charismatic Theology of St. Luke* and **James Dunn's** *Baptism in the Holy Spirit* (1970) represent two ends of the debate: Stronstad defending Lukan Spirit-reception as a distinct experience from initiation; Dunn arguing that Lukan and Pauline pneumatology together describe Spirit-reception as inseparable from conversion. **Reformed and Wesleyan** mediating positions read the Ephesian disciples as a transitional anomaly—genuine John-disciples who had not yet received the Pentecostal Spirit, whose situation cannot be normative for post-Pentecost Christianity.

**Patristic readings** of the chapter are rich. **Chrysostom's** 41st-42nd homilies on Acts dwell on the magic-book burning as the gospel's confrontation with idolatry, urging hearers that genuine conversion requires the costly destruction of incompatible practices. **Augustine** treats the laying on of hands at 19:6 in his pneumatological writings as evidence of the Spirit's free distribution. **Calvin's** commentary reads the sons of Sceva episode as a sober warning that the name of Jesus is not a magical formula but presupposes faith.

The **magic-book burning** has become a culturally weighty episode. Christian missionaries through the centuries have appealed to it as a model for cultural transformation; modern critics have noted its ambiguity—is the burning a free Christian act of repentance (as Luke clearly portrays it) or has it become, in Christian history, a precedent for coercive book-burning? **Justo González's** liberation reading distinguishes carefully: at 19:18-19 the converts themselves bring their own books; this is repentance, not censorship, and the distinction matters when later Christian cultures conflated the two.

The **Ephesian background** of John's Gospel and 1-3 John is relevant. Patristic tradition (Irenaeus, Eusebius) located the apostle John at Ephesus in his old age, and the **Johannine community's** Ephesian residence is a long-standing scholarly hypothesis. **Trebilco** is cautious but admits the Ephesian connection. The fact that **Revelation** 2:1-7 addresses the Ephesian church critically ("you have abandoned the love you had at first") is a poignant follow-up to Paul's three-year mission—the church survives but loses its early intensity.

**Pauline scholarship**'s engagement with Acts 19 has focused on the chronological and theological synchronism with the **Corinthian correspondence**. Most reconstructions place the writing of **1 Corinthians** during the Ephesian residence (cf. 1 Cor 16:8: "I will stay in Ephesus until Pentecost"). The "fight with wild beasts at Ephesus" of 1 Cor 15:32 may be metaphorical reference to opposition in the city, and 2 Cor 1:8-9 mentions affliction in Asia "so utterly burdened beyond our strength that we despaired of life itself"—possibly the riot or its aftermath.

The chapter's **commentary tradition** is dense. **Bruce's** NICNT integrates the Ephesian archaeology; **Barrett's** ICC analyzes the Greek with characteristic precision; **Fitzmyer**, **Gaventa**, **Johnson**, **Keener** (whose treatment of the magical background is encyclopedic), **Witherington**, **Schnabel**, **Conzelmann**, **Haenchen**, **Pervo**, **Peterson**, **Marguerat** all provide sustained treatment. **Robert Wall's** Acts commentary is theologically integrative. **Trebilco** is essential for Ephesus specifically.

Commentators note the relative absence of named women in Acts 19—a striking contrast to chapters 16 and 18. The silence is plausibly more a function of the chapter's chosen scenes (the male-dominated craft guild, the civic assembly) than a claim about the Ephesian church, which certainly included women: Priscilla had remained at Ephesus from 18:19, and Paul's later letters to and from the city presuppose women among the believers.

**N. T. Wright**, in *Acts for Everyone*, reads Ephesus as the place where the gospel's reach is shown to be total: it converts the disciples on the movement's margins (the John-baptized twelve), it strips the city of its magical apparatus (the costly book-burning), and finally it threatens the religious-economic engine itself—Artemis and the silver-shrine trade—so that the riot is, in Wright's terms, the predictable convulsion of a culture discovering that "Jesus is Lord" is not a private add-on but a rival claim on everything, including the marketplace. For Wright the resolve of 19:21 ("I must also see Rome") gives the chapter its shape: the Spirit is steering the whole story toward the imperial capital, where the same collision will play out at the center.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: spiritual power and economic disruption.** The riot at Ephesus is triggered not by theological dispute but by economic threat: Paul's preaching is bad for the silver-shrine business. **Three positions** on the relation of gospel and economic order: (1) **Liberation theology** (Justo González, Latin American base-community readings): the chapter is paradigmatic. The gospel is structurally disruptive of unjust economies, and the silversmiths' riot is the predictable response of an economic order built on idolatrous commerce. The church should expect such opposition wherever its preaching threatens unjust accumulation. (2) **Reformed common-grace** (Kuyper, Dooyeweerd, the neo-Calvinist tradition): the riot reveals the antithesis between the city of God and the city of man, but the church's mission is not primarily economic disruption; the silversmiths' loss is incidental, not the gospel's purpose. (3) **Two Kingdoms / Lutheran**: the gospel addresses individual souls and creates spiritual community; civic and economic orders are governed by natural law and civil authority, and the gospel's effects on those orders are indirect. The steelman: (1) the riot is real and the economic threat is real; (2) Paul did not preach against the silver trade specifically; (3) Christianity did historically transform Mediterranean economies, sometimes by gospel intent and sometimes as side effect. N. T. Wright's reading sits close to the first while keeping the second's caution: Paul did not campaign against the silversmiths, but a gospel that says "gods made with hands are not gods" cannot help unsettling an economy built on selling such gods—the disruption is real precisely because the lordship of Jesus is comprehensive. **Where this leaves us**: the chapter refuses both the privatized gospel (Christianity has economic effects) and the politicized gospel (Paul did not target the silver trade). The gospel's reshaping of religious imagination has economic consequences without being reducible to economic strategy.

**Tension 2: the church and miraculous signs.** The chapter pairs extraordinary miracles (handkerchiefs and aprons that healed the sick, 19:11-12) with the mocking failure of the sons of Sceva (19:13-17). **Three positions**: (1) **Cessationist** (Warfield, *Counterfeit Miracles*): apostolic miracles were unique to the foundational era and have ceased; the chapter shows extraordinary divine action that should not be expected as normative. (2) **Continuationist / charismatic**: the chapter shows Spirit-empowered miracle as ongoing apostolic ministry, normative for the church across history; the failure of Sceva's sons distinguishes genuine Spirit-empowerment from magical technique but does not restrict miracle to the apostolic age. (3) **Reformed mediating** (Lloyd-Jones, Piper, Grudem): miracles continue but are not normative; God grants them sovereignly, and the chapter neither requires nor forbids contemporary miracle. The steelman: (1) the chapter's miracles are extraordinary even by Acts's standards and may not be paradigmatic; (2) the gospel is itself supernatural and cannot be confined to natural means; (3) the Spirit's distribution of gifts is sovereign and cannot be predicted from one chapter. **Where this leaves us**: the chapter neither requires modern Christians to expect handkerchief-healing nor permits dismissing all miraculous claims as deception. Discernment is what distinguishes the apostolic Spirit-power from the sons of Sceva.

**Unresolved question**: what does the town clerk's speech (19:35-41) tell us about Lukan apologetics? The clerk explicitly defends Paul's companions ("neither sacrilegious nor blasphemers of our goddess," 19:37) and frames the riot as illegal civic disorder. Luke clearly approves the speech. But the clerk's defense rests on a misreading: Paul *did* preach that "gods made with hands are not gods" (19:26), and that preaching was, in any honest reading, blasphemy against Artemis. Did Luke craft the chapter to mute the gospel's confrontation with Artemis, or did Paul's actual practice avoid direct attack on the goddess (preferring positive proclamation of the resurrected Christ)? The text does not resolve this. The chapter's apologetic surface ("Christianity is not a civic threat") sits beside its theological depth ("the gospel is reshaping Asia").

## The Thread

The Ephesian rebaptism (19:1-7) closes the Lukan Spirit-reception pattern that began at **Pentecost (Acts 2:1-13)** and ran through **Samaria (Acts 8:14-17)** and **Cornelius (Acts 10:44-48)**. The resolution at 19:21 ("I must also see Rome") sets the trajectory that will not resolve until **Acts 28:14**, where Paul finally arrives. The "**fight with wild beasts at Ephesus**" of **1 Corinthians 15:32** and the deep affliction of **2 Corinthians 1:8-9** plausibly belong to this Ephesian period. Most pointedly, the Ephesian church's later reception of the warning in **Revelation 2:1-7**—"you have abandoned the love you had at first"—is a sobering reminder that even three years of apostolic mission and miraculous power do not guarantee the church's continued vitality. Mission gathers, but the gathered must keep on being addressed by the Lord who first gathered them.
