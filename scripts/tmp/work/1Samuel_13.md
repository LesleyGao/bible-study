# 1 Samuel 13: The Missing Numbers and the Iron Monopoly

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

1 Samuel 13 is among the densest chapters in the book for political-military and material-cultural history. The setting is the central Benjaminite hill country — **Michmash** (modern Mukhmas), **Geba** (Jaba'), **Gibeah** (most likely Tell el-Ful) — a corridor of villages and ridges separated by the dramatic **Wadi es-Suweinit**, a steep limestone gorge that controls movement between the Jordan Valley and the central Israelite highlands. Anyone who has stood on the heights at Mukhmas and looked across to Jaba' understands why this terrain became the theater of the war. The two ridges face each other across a chasm; whoever holds both controls the highland route to Jerusalem and the descent to Jericho. The chapter places **Saul at Gilgal** in the Jordan Valley first, then withdrawing to **Geba/Gibeah** as the Philistine pressure mounts; the Philistines, meanwhile, have advanced inland from the coastal plain and encamped at **Michmash**, deep inside what was nominally Israelite territory. The strategic situation, in modern military terms, is desperate: the Philistines hold the high ground, control the iron supply, and outnumber the Israelite muster perhaps fifty to one.

The **Philistines** in this period are by archaeological consensus a **Sea Peoples** group who arrived on the southern Levantine coast in the early twelfth century BCE, shortly after the collapse of the late Bronze Age palatial system. Excavations at **Tel Miqne (Ekron)** under Trude Dothan and Seymour Gitin, **Ashkelon** under Lawrence Stager, and **Tell es-Safi (Gath)** under **Aren Maeir** have produced a coherent picture of a culturally Aegean-derived people — Mycenaean-style pottery, hearths, pig consumption, megaron architecture — who progressively acculturated to local Canaanite norms over the eleventh and tenth centuries. **Ann Killebrew**'s work on Iron I material culture and Maeir's ongoing publication of the Tell es-Safi excavations confirm that by the late eleventh century — the period of 1 Samuel 13 — the Philistines controlled an unmatched metallurgical capacity in the southern Levant.

Which brings us to one of the chapter's most-cited verses: **13:19–22**, the report that "there was no blacksmith to be found throughout all the land of Israel" because the Philistines deliberately suppressed Israelite metalworking. The historicity of this is corroborated by metallurgical archaeology. **Iron** is significantly harder to smelt than bronze (it requires sustained temperatures around 1100–1200°C and careful management of carbon content), and early Iron IIA iron metallurgy in the southern Levant is concentrated overwhelmingly in coastal Philistia. The highland Israelite sites of the same period yield mostly bronze tools and only a trickle of iron, much of it likely imported. Whether the Philistine "monopoly" was as deliberately enforced as 1 Samuel 13 portrays — a kind of Iron Age technology embargo — or simply a function of where the smiths were trained, is debated; but the **outcome** the text describes is materially accurate. Israelites taking their plowshares to Philistine smiths for sharpening (13:20) and paying in **shekel weights** (13:21) is one of the few passages in the Hebrew Bible to mention specific iron-age commercial pricing. The Hebrew word the ESV translates "two-thirds of a shekel" is **pim** (פִּים), a word that puzzled translators until **Robert Macalister** at Gezer and others began excavating small inscribed weights labeled *pym* — confirming that a *pim* was about two-thirds of a shekel by mass. The chapter contains, hidden in a single word, a triumph of nineteenth-century archaeology over centuries of translational guesswork.

Five Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. **Netsib** (נְצִיב, "garrison" or "officer," 13:3) is ambiguous; the LXX understood it as "officer," and the question of whether Jonathan attacked a Philistine fort or a Philistine governor changes the political stakes. **Migdal** and the place-name **Migron** (cf. 14:2) name the small fortified towers that dot the Benjaminite ridge. **Cherev** (חֶרֶב, "sword") and **chanith** (חֲנִית, "spear") are the two weapons specifically denied to Israelite hands in 13:22. **Charash** (חָרָשׁ, "craftsman, smith," 13:19) is the technical term for the metalworker. And **machareshah** (מַחֲרֵשָׁה, plowshare, 13:20) and **'eth** (אֵת, mattock or coulter) name the agricultural iron tools that needed periodic resharpening. The chapter is, among other things, a window into the daily economy of a subject population.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter opens with the **most famous textual problem in the entire book**. **MT 13:1** literally reads: "A son of [—] year[s] was Saul when he became king, and two years he reigned over Israel" (*ben-shanah Sha'ul be-malko, u-shtei shanim malakh 'al-yisra'el*). The first number is missing entirely from the Hebrew — the formulaic regnal-year notice that every other king in 1–2 Kings receives ("X was Y years old when he began to reign, and reigned Z years in Jerusalem") is here truncated, leaving Saul "one year old when he became king" if read literally. The second number — "two years" — is also too short to be his actual reign. **The LXX manuscripts simply omit the verse**, evidently judging the textual corruption beyond repair. **4QSam-a** is fragmentary at this exact point, providing no help. **Origen's Hexapla** marked the verse with an asterisk to indicate it was missing from the Greek and supplied from the Hebrew. **The Old Latin** preserves a reading of "thirty years." **Acts 13:21**, in Paul's Antioch sermon, reports Saul's reign as "forty years," suggesting a tradition known in the first century but not preserved in the Hebrew text we now have.

Engaging this honestly: **Emanuel Tov** in *Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible* treats 13:1 as a clear case of mechanical transmission failure — the formulaic notice was once present, the numbers fell out, and the surviving text is a fossil of the loss. **McCarter** (Anchor Bible) reconstructs by reasoning from Acts 13:21 and from the chronological needs of the surrounding narrative. **Klein** (WBC) and **Hertzberg** (OTL) are similarly cautious. The ESV's "Saul lived for one year and then became king, and when he had reigned for two years over Israel" is a translational gloss that smooths the Hebrew but does not resolve the underlying problem. There is no responsible way to read 1 Samuel 13:1 without acknowledging that the text we have is broken.

Beyond the textual issue, the chapter is a tightly composed **first-rejection narrative**. The literary form is the **prophetic dynastic-doom oracle** — Samuel arrives, names Saul's specific failure, declares the kingdom will not continue, and announces (in 13:14) that "the LORD has sought out a man after his own heart." This phrase, *'ish kilbavo* (אִישׁ כִּלְבָבוֹ), is the first explicit narrative anticipation of David, and Robert Alter has shown in *The David Story* how the entire Saul narrative from 13:14 onward operates under the shadow of this announcement. The reader knows Saul is finished; Saul does not.

Literary devices include the **inverted muster** (Saul mustered an army of 3,000 in 13:2; by 13:15 he is down to 600 — the army shrinks as the chapter proceeds), the **Philistine "three companies" of raiders** in 13:17–18, which echoes (and inverts) Saul's three companies in 11:11 (his own tactical move is now used against him), and the **structural symmetry** between 13:8–14 (Saul offers an unauthorized sacrifice and is rejected) and the parallel scene in chapter 15 (Saul keeps the *herem* spoils and is finally rejected). **Polzin** in *Samuel and the Deuteronomist* reads chapters 13 and 15 as deliberate doublets: Saul receives two warnings, fails both, and only on the second is the rejection made final.

Place in the book argument: 1 Samuel 13 is the **beginning of Saul's downfall** in narrative terms, even though the formal end will not come until chapter 15. Brueggemann, McCarter, and Polzin all emphasize that the book's structure pivots here. Chapters 1–12 are the rise of Samuel, the request for a king, the granting of Saul. Chapters 13–15 are Saul's two failures and his rejection. Chapter 16 introduces David. The architecture of the book is built on this sequence, and 13:14 is its hinge.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc places this chapter in conversation with several earlier and later texts. The **failure to wait** echoes Israel's earlier impatience at Sinai (Exod 32, the golden calf, when Moses delays on the mountain and the people "make for themselves a god"). The **unauthorized sacrifice** anticipates both **Uzzah's death** (2 Sam 6) and the **Korah rebellion** (Num 16) as instances of cultic violation by those not authorized. The **iron monopoly** description finds echo in later prophetic critiques of military self-reliance (Isa 31:1, Ps 20:7). The chapter is doing canonical work in multiple directions.

The **Jewish interpretive tradition** has wrestled with the *justice* of Saul's rejection. **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 6.95–104) presents Saul's sacrifice as a clear violation of priestly office. The **rabbis** in **b. Yoma 22b** discuss whether Saul's failure was technical (offering without the high priest) or substantive (failing to trust YHWH in the seven-day waiting period); they tend toward the latter. **Rashi** glosses *na'asita ne'evel* ("you have done foolishly") as morally rather than intellectually foolish — Saul knew better. **Radak** elaborates: Saul's sin was not the sacrifice itself but the *forcing* of it (*va'et'appaq*, "I forced myself," 13:12) — a sacrifice offered against the heart's natural restraint is no sacrifice. **Ralbag** focuses on the chronological problem of when Samuel was supposed to arrive and whether Saul technically waited the full seven days; he concludes Saul waited the days but did not wait the *moment*, and the moment is what counted. **Abarbanel** offers a sharply political reading: Saul's sin was not just cultic but *constitutional* — he conflated the offices of king and priest in a way that the Mosaic constitution forbade, anticipating the same problem that would later condemn King Uzziah (2 Chr 26:16–21). Modern Jewish readers — **Avivah Zornberg** on the psychology of waiting, **Yair Zakovitch** on the literary patterning of Saul's failures, **Moshe Garsiel** on the comparative ANE evidence for prophetic-royal authority disputes — have generally read the chapter as a careful theological argument about the limits of royal power.

The **Christian tradition** has read this chapter in distinct registers. **Origen**'s homilies treat Saul's premature sacrifice as the type of the Christian who rushes ahead of God's timing in matters of grace. **Augustine** in *City of God* and in his correspondence with Jerome cites Saul as a paradigm of how *external* religious observance without obedience is rejected by God — a key building block of Augustinian sacramentology. **Chrysostom** focuses on the moral fault of impatience. **Gregory the Great**, in *Pastoral Rule* and *Moralia in Job*, returns to Saul repeatedly as the figure of the *praepositus* (leader) whose office becomes an occasion for usurping spiritual prerogatives. **Aquinas** (*Summa* II-II, q.99 on the sin of irreligio) cites Saul's sacrifice as an instance of the sin of sacrilege — undertaking a sacred act without proper authorization. The medieval Christian tradition consistently used 1 Samuel 13 to discipline kings who attempted to control bishops.

The **Reformation** intensified this reading. **Calvin's sermons on 1 Samuel** treat 13:8–14 as a double warning: against impatience in the face of divine delay, and against the assumption that political necessity justifies cultic shortcut. Calvin uses Saul to argue against the Anabaptists' claim that any Spirit-led individual could lead worship without ordained office, and equally against the Erastians' claim that civil rulers could direct the church's worship. **Luther**, who cited 13:14's "man after his own heart" repeatedly in his commentaries, made the contrast between Saul (formal religion without faith) and David (real faith despite real sin) one of the structural arguments of his theology of the cross.

**Critical scholarship** has been more interested in the textual and source-critical issues. **Wellhausen** assigned chapter 13 to a relatively early stratum and noted the textual corruption at 13:1. **Cross** confirmed that 4QSam-a does not preserve the verse intact. **McCarter** treats 13:7b–15a as a Dtr insertion that splits the older battle-narrative; the original story may have run from 13:7a directly to 13:15b, with the rejection-of-Saul scene inserted later. **Klein** and **Hertzberg** broadly agree, though they differ on details. **Halpern** in *David's Secret Demons* reads the chapter as part of a sustained polemic against Saulide kingship that took shape under David's court.

**Contemporary readers**. **Brueggemann** centers the chapter as the *moral argument* of the Saul narrative: kingship that does not trust YHWH is not viable kingship, regardless of military success. **Polzin** reads chapters 13 and 15 as doublet rejection-narratives that together establish the Dtr's theology of conditional kingship. **David Gunn** in *The Fate of King Saul* offers the most sympathetic reading: Saul faces a real military emergency, makes a decision under pressure that any reasonable commander would make, and is rejected on what looks (to Gunn) like a technicality; the chapter, on Gunn's reading, is a *tragic* text in the Greek sense — the protagonist's destruction is set in motion by his honest attempt to do his duty. **Eugene Peterson** in *Leap Over a Wall* draws pastoral lessons about waiting under pressure. **Ellen Davis** focuses on the iron-monopoly description as evidence of how thoroughly economic subordination structures the lives of YHWH's people. **John Goldingay** stresses the political-theological ambiguity. Literary readers (Polzin, Brueggemann, Alter) note the entirely male cast of the war narrative — and the irony that the chapter's most economically vivid detail, the plowshares and mattocks taken to Philistine smiths for sharpening (13:20–21), points to a household economy whose women, who did much of the field work those tools were used for, never appear in the scene.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Was Saul's Sin Cultic, Constitutional, or Existential?** The text is famously underdetermined on what exactly Saul did wrong. Three positions: (a) **Cultic violation**, the patristic and medieval reading: Saul, as king, was not a Levite or priest and had no authorization to offer burnt offerings; the act was technically illegal regardless of motivation. (b) **Constitutional usurpation**, Abarbanel's reading: Saul confused the offices of king and prophet/priest, threatening the Mosaic separation of powers; his sin was less about the specific sacrifice and more about reaching beyond royal prerogative. (c) **Existential failure of faith**, the Reformed and most Protestant reading: Saul's real sin was not waiting on YHWH; the sacrifice is the symptom, not the disease; what God rejects is the heart that would not trust enough to wait through the seventh day. Steelman: the text supports all three, and the ambiguity may be deliberate. Saul's "I forced myself" (13:12) admits the inner conflict that position (c) names; Samuel's "you have not kept the command of the LORD" (13:13) emphasizes the legal violation that position (a) names; the broader narrative concern with the king-prophet relationship supports position (b). **Where this leaves us:** Saul's failure is overdetermined. He sinned in multiple ways at once, which is why the text refuses to specify. The reader is meant to see that obedience is more demanding than any single rule it might break.

**Tension Two: The Severity of the Sentence.** The punishment — loss of the dynasty in 13:13–14 — appears, to many readers, disproportionate. Three positions: (a) **Just severity**, defended by Calvin and most Reformed commentators: Saul's role was not merely personal; the king's failure shapes the people's covenant standing, so the consequences scale with the office, not with the act. (b) **Tragic disproportion**, defended by Gunn and some contemporary literary readers: the sentence is harsh; the text wants us to feel the harshness; God's choice of Saul and rejection of Saul are both given and both grieved. (c) **Pedagogical narrative**, defended by Polzin and structural readers: the doublet structure (13 and 15) shows that the sentence is announced in chapter 13 but only enacted in chapter 15; Saul is given a second chance, which he also fails; the severity is not in the announcement but in the failure to amend. Steelman: position (b) takes the moral weight of the text most seriously; position (c) accounts best for the narrative architecture; position (a) preserves the theological principle that office magnifies sin. **Where this leaves us:** the rejection of Saul is the most morally costly act of YHWH in the books of Samuel. The text wants us to understand both that it is just and that it is hard.

**One unresolved question:** If the iron monopoly was real, then Saul went into the great battle of his career with an army that had only two iron weapons in it — his own and Jonathan's. What does it mean that YHWH chose to save Israel in chapter 14 not by upgrading the technology but by letting two men do what 600 could not?

## The Thread

- **Exodus 32** — Israel's failure to wait while Moses delays on Sinai. Saul's failure to wait for Samuel is the same failure transposed into the monarchic era.
- **Numbers 16** — Korah's challenge to the priestly office. Saul's unauthorized sacrifice is in the same theological family.
- **2 Chronicles 26:16–21** — King Uzziah enters the temple to burn incense and is struck with leprosy. Abarbanel's reading of 1 Sam 13 as a constitutional warning against royal usurpation of priestly office finds its later confirmation here.
- **1 Samuel 15** — Saul's second and final failure, and the formal enactment of the rejection announced in 13:14. **Flag forward**: the doublet structure means chapters 13 and 15 must be read together.
- **Acts 13:21** — Paul's reference to Saul's forty-year reign, which preserves a chronological tradition not found in the corrupted Hebrew of 13:1.
