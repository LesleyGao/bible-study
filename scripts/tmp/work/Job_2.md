# Job 2: Skin for Skin, Ash for Ash

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The second chapter of Job opens with a second session of the divine council and closes on an ash heap outside a town, and these two settings mark the boundaries of the chapter's world. The patriarchal setting — a wealthy pastoralist in the land of Uz, three friends from identifiably Edomite or north Arabian regions, wealth measured in livestock rather than coin — places the narrative's surface somewhere east of Israel, in the cultural zone where Edom, Teman, and the northern Arabian tribes overlapped. Eliphaz the Temanite comes from a region Jeremiah 49:7 and Obadiah 8-9 associate with proverbial wisdom; Bildad the Shuhite's tribe may be connected to Shuah son of Abraham by Keturah (Genesis 25:2); Zophar the Naamathite is harder to locate but probably northwest Arabian. The political situation implied is a world of tribal sheikhs and chieftains, city gates where elders render judgment, and raiding bands (the Sabeans and Chaldeans of chapter 1) operating across poorly defended frontiers. Job sits outside the settlement, not inside his house, because the *ash heap* (Hebrew *epher* אֵפֶר, but the Septuagint and the later tradition read also *mezbelah* — the dung heap outside the town where refuse was burned) was the liminal space for the ritually unclean, the leprous, and the bereaved.

On authorship and dating, scholarly opinion divides sharply. Marvin Pope (Anchor Bible) and David Clines (Word Biblical Commentary, 3 vols.) argue that the Hebrew of the poetic dialogues contains Aramaisms and rare lexemes best explained by a post-exilic composition, perhaps sixth or fifth century BCE. Carol Newsom (*The Book of Job: A Contest of Moral Imaginations*, 2003) leaves the date open but agrees the book reflects a crisis of wisdom thought characteristic of the exilic and post-exilic period. Robert Gordis and Francis Andersen (Tyndale) defend an earlier, perhaps Solomonic or early monarchic composition, reading the archaic features as genuinely old rather than learned archaism. What almost everyone grants is that by the time of Ezekiel 14:14, 20 — where Noah, Daniel, and Job are named as paradigmatic righteous men — Job is already a legendary figure in Israelite memory, which pushes the core traditions back well before the sixth century even if the final poetic form is later.

The archaeological and comparative context is unusually rich. The Mesopotamian *Ludlul Bel Nemeqi* ("I Will Praise the Lord of Wisdom," roughly 1300 BCE) describes a righteous sufferer covered in sores, abandoned by friends and god, before eventual restoration. The Babylonian Theodicy (c. 1000 BCE) is a dialogue between a sufferer and a friend over divine justice that parallels Job's form. The older Sumerian "Man and His God" (c. 1800 BCE) adds a third text to this tradition of protest. The Ugaritic texts from Ras Shamra (fourteenth century BCE) give us the Canaanite divine council vocabulary that Job 1-2 deploys: the *bene ha-elohim* (בְּנֵי הָאֱלֹהִים, "sons of God") presenting themselves before the high god. The 11QTargum Job fragment from Qumran Cave 11 is one of the oldest substantial targums and suggests the book was the object of intense scribal attention in the Second Temple period. The Septuagint version of Job is famously about four hundred lines shorter than the Masoretic Text, especially in the dialogues, suggesting either Greek translators abbreviated a difficult text or that Hebrew expansion occurred late — Pope argues the LXX represents an earlier, shorter Hebrew *Vorlage*.

Key Hebrew terms in this chapter repay attention. *Tam* (תָּם) — translated "blameless" in verse 3 — is the word also used of Jacob in Genesis 25:27 and carries the sense of wholeness, integrity, unblemishedness, not sinlessness; it is a sacrificial-grade descriptor. *Hinnam* (חִנָּם) in verse 3 — "without reason" or "for nothing" — is the key theological word of the prologue; the same word the Satan used in 1:9 ("Does Job fear God for nothing?"). God now throws the word back at the Satan. *Barakh* (בָּרַךְ) in 2:9 is the infamous euphemism: the word literally means "bless," but here and in 1:5, 11; 2:5 it is used antiphrastically for "curse." Scribes reluctant to write "curse God" in the same breath substituted its opposite. *Epher* (אֵפֶר), the ashes Job sits in, functions as both literal refuse heap and ritual marker of mourning and self-abasement. *Nefesh* (נֶפֶשׁ) in verse 6 — "only spare his life" — is the word for breath-soul, animating life, the part God reserves.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Job 2 is the second half of the folkloric prose frame that brackets the book. The frame itself (1:1-2:13 and 42:7-17) is written in a tightly formulaic Hebrew prose with repetitive structures, numerical patterns, and ritualized dialogue — the style of an oral tale, what Hermann Gunkel would classify as *Novelle* or didactic wisdom tale. The poetic dialogue core (3:1-42:6) that follows uses an entirely different register of Hebrew. This seam is one of the most-studied literary features of the Hebrew Bible. Norman Habel (Old Testament Library commentary) argues the prose and poetry were composed together with deliberate stylistic contrast; Claus Westermann and Pope tend to see an older prose tale into which a later poet inserted the dialogues; Newsom argues for "polyphony" in Bakhtin's sense — multiple genres held in unresolved dialogue within the book.

The chapter's architecture is a mirror of chapter 1. The first heavenly scene (1:6-12) produces the first wave of disasters (1:13-19) and Job's first response (1:20-22). The second heavenly scene (2:1-6) produces the second disaster (2:7-8) and Job's second response (2:9-10). This doubling, with its formulaic repetition — "Again there was a day," "from going to and fro on the earth, and from walking up and down on it" — is classic folktale numerical escalation, the same structure that gives us three little pigs and seven fat cows. Edwin Good (*In Turns of Tempest*) notes that the escalation is anatomical: first property, then children, then body.

The narrator uses the repeated *hinnam* (חִנָּם) as an *inclusio* binding the two council scenes: the Satan's question "for nothing?" in 1:9 becomes the Lord's accusation "without reason" in 2:3. This creates what Robert Alter calls a "type-scene with variation" — the structure repeats but the moral weight shifts. God accepts the Satan's framing of disinterested piety as the axis of the test, but he turns the word *hinnam* into an indictment. The Satan's proverb in 2:4, *or be'ad or* (עוֹר בְּעַד עוֹר), "skin for skin," is a commercial idiom; Pope reads it as a trader's saying about equivalent exchange, and it shows the Satan reducing Job to a utility-maximizing economic agent. Note also the Satan's grammatical shift: in 1:11 he demands that God "touch all that he has"; in 2:5, "touch his bone and his flesh." The Hebrew *nega* (נָגַע, "touch") in both cases is also the word for being afflicted with a skin disease — a pun the narrator exploits.

Job's wife's speech in 2:9 is grammatically stripped, almost telegraphic. The Hebrew is spare: *od-ka mahaziq b'tummateka? barek elohim vamut* — "still you hold fast integrity? bless-God-and-die." Augustine called her *adiutrix diaboli*, the devil's helper; the Septuagint's translator, apparently uncomfortable with her brevity, expanded her speech into a lengthy lament about the loss of their children and her own household labor. The Testament of Job (first century BCE to first century CE) gives her a name, Sitis, and a sympathetic back-story. The MT's refusal to soften her is probably original.

Job's answer in 2:10 introduces the legal-forensic language that will dominate the book: *gam et-hatov neqabel me'et ha'Elohim v'et-hara lo neqabel* — "shall we receive the good from God, and the evil shall we not receive?" The verb *qabal* (קָבַל) is Aramaic-flavored, a later legal term for receiving or accepting what is due. Job uses the vocabulary of contract, of reckoning, though not yet of lawsuit; that escalation is coming.

The final scene (2:11-13) deploys the technical vocabulary of mourning: tearing robes (*qara'* קָרַע), sprinkling dust toward heaven, sitting on the ground, seven days of silence. This is the formal shiva observance — the same number of days that Joseph mourned Jacob (Genesis 50:10) and that later rabbinic halakha would codify. The fact that the three friends perform this ritual correctly, in silence, is literarily important; when they speak, they ruin it. As Janzen observes in his Interpretation commentary, their silence is their last act of genuine comfort.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Job belongs to the canonical wisdom corpus alongside Proverbs and Ecclesiastes, and the three books are often read together as a trialogue — Proverbs presenting the retributive framework in which righteousness produces prosperity, Job contesting that framework from the position of the innocent sufferer, and Ecclesiastes (Qohelet) radicalizing the protest into the recognition that everything is *hevel* (הֶבֶל, vapor). Gerhard von Rad's *Wisdom in Israel* (1970) argues that Job marks the moment when Israelite wisdom discovered its own limits.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

Second Temple Jewish readings treated Job as a heroic figure of endurance. The Testament of Job (first century BCE to first century CE) expands the prologue into a martyr narrative in which Job knowingly challenges idolaters and accepts Satanic affliction. The 11QTargum Job fragment from Qumran renders the prose frame in Aramaic; the Septuagint adds an appendix identifying Job with Jobab king of Edom (Genesis 36:33), placing him firmly in the patriarchal period. Philo of Alexandria allegorized Job as the exemplar of patient endurance. Josephus does not retell Job but accepts him as a real Edomite king.

Rabbinic readings are remarkably divided. Bava Batra 15a-16b preserves a famous debate: did Job ever exist, or was he a parable? Rabbi Levi holds the latter — Job never was, but the book is scripture's exploration of the theodicy problem. The Talmud also debates whether Job served God from love or from fear, with Abba ben Kahana arguing fear and Rabbi Yohanan arguing love. Pesikta Rabbati and Genesis Rabbah treat Job as a gentile prophet and as a contemporary of the Patriarchs. Rashi (eleventh century) reads the prose frame historically but treats much of the dialogue as theological dispute. Ibn Ezra acknowledges the book's linguistic strangeness and suggests translation from another language. Maimonides in *Guide of the Perplexed* III.22-23 takes the most radical position: Job is a philosophical parable, and the characters — Job, Eliphaz, Bildad, Zophar, Elihu — represent the schools of Mosaic, Ashʿarite, Muʿtazilite, Aristotelian, and a fifth position he identifies with the Torah's true teaching. Nachmanides (Ramban) in his *Sha'ar ha-Gemul* uses Job as a site for developing his doctrine of *gilgul* — reincarnation — arguing Job's suffering is the working out of a prior soul's debt. Modern Jewish readers have been drawn back to Job repeatedly: Martin Buber (*The Eclipse of God*) reads Job as a witness to the hiddenness of the divine face; Jon Levenson (*Creation and the Persistence of Evil*) treats Job as evidence that Israelite monotheism preserves a strong doctrine of chaos and divine combat; Avivah Zornberg's close readings emphasize the psychological interior of the biblical characters; James Kugel's *The Great Shift* places Job at the hinge point where ancient and modern religious experience diverge.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

Patristic readings were shaped by Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job*, a thirty-five-book exposition written in the 580s that became the most influential medieval biblical commentary of any kind. Gregory reads Job christologically — Job is a type of Christ the innocent sufferer — and allegorically, with each detail coded for moral and spiritual meaning. Origen and Chrysostom both preached extensively on Job; Chrysostom's sermons treat Job's wife with surprising sympathy as a second-order victim. Augustine uses Job sparingly but treats him as exemplary faith. Jerome translated Job from Hebrew for the Vulgate and comments on its linguistic difficulty.

Thomas Aquinas's *Expositio super Iob* (c. 1261-65) is the first great philosophical-theological commentary on the book. Aquinas reads Job as a demonstration that divine providence governs the world, with Job's suffering teaching that temporal goods are not the measure of divine favor. Calvin preached 159 sermons on Job between February 1554 and March 1555 — one of the largest continuous sermon cycles of the Reformation — emphasizing providence, the secret will of God, and the insufficiency of human protest. Luther's engagement with Job was less systematic but he returned repeatedly to the theme of *Anfechtung*, the assault of God on the conscience, which he read partly through Job.

Critical Protestant scholarship in the nineteenth century (Wellhausen, Gunkel) questioned the unity of the book. Twentieth-century critical readings include Westermann's form-critical work arguing the book is fundamentally a lament, von Rad's reading of it as the crisis point of wisdom, and Brevard Childs's canonical approach. Contemporary commentary is dominated by David Clines's massive three-volume Word Biblical Commentary (1989-2011), which stresses the book's literary complexity; Carol Newsom's *Contest of Moral Imaginations* read through Bakhtinian polyphony. Ellen Davis reads Job as sapiential wisdom working out a non-retributive providence; Susannah Ticciati (*Job and the Disruption of Identity*) gives a Barthian christological reading; Norman Habel's literary-theological commentary; John Hartley's New International Commentary; Francis Andersen's Tyndale; and David Fyall's *Now My Eyes Have Seen You* for the mythological-combat reading.

The key Jewish-Christian divergence that this chapter already previews: Christian tradition has tended to read Job's suffering through a future-oriented theodicy — suffering has meaning because of the cross and resurrection, and 19:25-27 ("I know that my Redeemer lives") is read christologically from Jerome onward. Jewish tradition has overwhelmingly kept Job's question terrestrial and within this life; Maimonides and Gersonides explicitly refuse to solve Job by appeal to olam ha-ba — the question must be answered in history, not deferred to eternity.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The second chapter forces two theological tensions more sharply than the first.

**Tension one: the moral status of the heavenly wager.** Chapter 2 has God admit, *v'tesiteni bo l'val'o hinnam* — "you incited me against him to destroy him without reason" — and then immediately grant a second round of affliction. The text is forthright about the scandal: God has allowed the Satan to act without justified cause.

- Catholic (Aquinas, *Expositio* on this passage; CCC 309-314 on providence and evil): the apparent "incitement" is concessive language within the narrative; divine providence permits evil only for a greater good ordered to the manifestation of divine glory and the perfection of the sufferer. Aquinas reads God's grant as pedagogical rather than sadistic, with Job's own growth in virtue as the *finis operis*.
- Reformed Protestant (Calvin's sermons on Job; Westminster Confession III.1): the text's frankness about divine sovereignty is precisely the point; God ordains whatsoever comes to pass, including Job's affliction, yet is not the author of sin. Calvin explicitly refuses to soften the narrative — the wager stands as it is written, and our place is to recognize the *incomprehensibilis majestas*.
- Lutheran (Luther, lectures on Job fragments; Gerhard Forde's work on *Deus absconditus*): the prologue reveals the hidden God behind the revealed God; the wager is the kind of thing God does that cannot be reconciled with his revealed will but must be held in tension.
- Wesleyan / Arminian: less comfortable with the prologue; tends toward the reading that God's "permission" is not causation, and that Job's free response is itself the locus of theological interest.
- Anabaptist: the narrative is descriptive, not prescriptive; what matters is how the community of the faithful accompanies the sufferer, which is precisely what the friends fail to sustain.
- Jewish (Maimonides, *Guide* III.22; Saadia Gaon): Maimonides argues the prologue is parable, and the "Satan" is not a metaphysical agent but a narrative personification of the privation of being — a reading that preserves divine simplicity by denying the wager happens in the literal cosmos at all. Saadia treats the suffering as corrective discipline.
- Critical (Newsom, Clines): the prologue is a literary device — a thought experiment that sets up the dialogues — and the theological question is whether the book ultimately endorses or subverts the premise of the wager. Newsom argues the rest of the book dismantles the framework the prologue establishes.

Steelmanning the position I find least intuitive — Maimonides's — the argument is that taking the prologue literally makes God a moral monster, and the principle of charity demands reading Job as parable rather than history. The whole book then becomes a controlled philosophical experiment, not a report of an actual wager. Where this leaves us: the text refuses to relieve the scandal of a God who concedes "without reason." Any reading that smooths over *hinnam* has stopped reading the chapter.

**Tension two: the wife's counsel and the ethics of integrity.** *Barek elohim vamut* — bless (curse) God and die. The text presents her as an adversary to Job's piety, yet modern readings have pressed for a sympathetic rereading.

- Catholic (CCC 1808 on fortitude; traditional patristic reading): Job's wife represents the voice of despair against the virtue of fortitude. Augustine's *adiutrix diaboli* becomes standard.
- Protestant Reformed (Calvin's sermons, sermon 9): Calvin is uncharacteristically severe — the wife speaks from the flesh, and Job's refusal is the voice of faith against natural reason.
- Orthodox (Chrysostom, echoed in Lossky on the apophatic): more sympathy — she too is a victim, and her speech is a lament misspoken; the patristic Greek tradition often names her alongside Job's losses.
- Jewish (Rashi on 2:9; the Targum; Avivah Zornberg): the Targum identifies her with Dinah, daughter of Jacob, giving her an Israelite identity; Zornberg's close reading hears in her brevity the speech of one who has also lost the children and the household, and whose "die now" is at least honest in ways the friends' theodicy is not.
- Critical (Newsom, Ticciati): her speech is arguably the most theologically honest in the chapter — she sees that the prior piety has become untenable and counsels release. Newsom suggests the wife offers a truer reading of the situation than Job's formal piety does.

Where this leaves us: the text gives us too little of her to resolve whether she is the devil's helper or the first honest voice. The interpretive tradition's treatment of her is itself a test of the reader.

One unresolved question that lands: if God calls Job *tam* again in 2:3 after the first round of loss, and then concedes a second round of destruction *hinnam* — without reason — what exactly is the test meant to prove, and to whom? The Satan is already exposed as a cynic; Job has already passed; the reader already knows the setup. The test seems to be for no one in the story. Who, then, is the reader watching?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 3:19** — "till you return to the ground, for out of it you were taken; for you are dust, and to dust you shall return." Job scraping himself with a potsherd while sitting in ashes reenacts the primal sentence on Adam. The ash heap is Eden inverted, and Job's body is becoming the dust it came from while he is still alive.

- **Ezekiel 14:14, 20** — "even if these three men, Noah, Daniel, and Job, were in it, they would deliver but their own lives by their righteousness." Ezekiel already knows Job as a legendary figure paired with two other pre-Mosaic righteous men. Read this and remember Job 2: the chapter is constructing, in real time, the figure who will later be cited as the paradigmatic righteous sufferer.

- **Luke 22:31-32** — "Simon, Simon, behold, Satan demanded to have you, that he might sift you like wheat, but I have prayed for you that your faith may not fail." When you reach this passage in the Gospels, notice that the heavenly-council logic of Job 2 — the Satan requesting access to test the righteous — has been assumed rather than abolished. Jesus does not deny the structure; he answers it with intercession.

- **James 5:11** — "you have heard of the steadfastness of Job, and you have seen the purpose of the Lord, how the Lord is compassionate and merciful." James reads Job 2 as a paradigm of *hupomone*, steadfast endurance, and connects it to the "purpose" — *telos* — of the Lord. Read James 5 and return: the early Christian community read the ash heap as a site of revelation, not only of suffering.
