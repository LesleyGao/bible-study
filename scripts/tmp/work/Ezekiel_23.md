# Ezekiel 23: Oholah and Oholibah, and the Hardest Chapter to Read

## Lens 1 — Historical World

Ezekiel 23 is one of the **most controversial chapters in the Hebrew Bible**, and any honest scholarly engagement has to admit the controversy at the outset rather than at the end. Composed from the Babylonian *gōlâ* probably in the 590s–580s BC, against the backdrop of Zedekiah's failed vassal-rebellion and the imperial response documented in **Babylonian Chronicle BM 21946** (Wiseman 1956), the chapter narrates the international diplomatic history of the divided kingdoms — Israel and Judah — as the sexual biography of two sisters.

The historical referents are reasonably clear. *Oholah* ("her tent") = Samaria, the northern kingdom, who "played the whore" with **Assyria** — the political reality being the eighth-century Israelite kings (Jehu, Menahem, Hoshea) who paid tribute to Tiglath-Pileser III, Shalmaneser V, and Sargon II, attested in the **Calah / Nimrud annals** and the **Sargon prism**. The "Egyptian youth" of 23:3 references the centuries of Egyptian-Israelite political entanglement going back to Solomon's marriage alliance and visible in the diplomatic correspondence of the **Amarna letters** centuries earlier. *Oholibah* ("my tent is in her") = Jerusalem, the southern kingdom, whose dalliances with Assyria (Hezekiah's tribute, 2 Kgs 18:14–16; Manasseh's vassalage, attested in the **Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal annals**) and with Babylon (Hezekiah's reception of Merodach-Baladan's envoys in 2 Kgs 20:12–19; Zedekiah's rebellion against Nebuchadnezzar) form the documented historical arc.

The names *Oholah / Oholibah* themselves are wordplays on *ʾōhel* ("tent" — the wilderness tabernacle) and may carry deliberate cultic-architectural resonance: Samaria built her own *ʾōhel* (rival sanctuaries at Bethel and Dan, 1 Kgs 12), while in Jerusalem *YHWH's ʾōhel* (the Solomonic temple) was located. **Greenberg (Anchor Yale)**, **Block (NICOT)**, and **Allen (WBC)** all develop this etymology, and the city-as-sanctuary-as-wife metaphor cluster running through Ezekiel is one of the chapter's keys.

The chapter parallels Ezekiel 16, which also narrates Jerusalem as YHWH's unfaithful wife, but Ezek 23 is more explicit, more violent, and adds the doubling — two sisters rather than one bride. **Origen famously refused to publicly expound Ezekiel 16 and 23** as too sexually explicit, restricting the homilies to private, advanced students; **Jerome** preserved the censorship principle when translating his commentary. **Calvin's Lectures on Ezekiel** treats the chapter at length but with visible discomfort, repeatedly noting that the language is "harsh" and explaining it as accommodation to the depth of the people's hardened hearts.

The judgment in 23:25–26 — *they shall cut off your nose and your ears, and your survivors shall fall by the sword. They shall seize your sons and your daughters… they shall also strip you of your clothes and take away your beautiful jewels* — corresponds to **documented Mesopotamian and Egyptian penalties for adultery**. The **Middle Assyrian Laws (MAL)** §§ 5, 8, 15 prescribe nose-mutilation, ear-mutilation, and stripping for women convicted of adultery; the **Code of Hammurabi** prescribes drowning. Greenberg, Block, and Zimmerli (Hermeneia) all note that Ezekiel applies real ANE legal punishments — these were not literary inventions but the actual judicial repertoire. The historical horror is not merely metaphor; it describes punishments that were inflicted on actual women in the prophet's world.

Standard Hebrew vocabulary: *zānâ* (to fornicate / commit adultery), *zənûnîm* (whoredoms), *taznūt* (the abstract noun, used 22 times in chs. 16 and 23 alone), *gillûlîm* (Ezekiel's signature pejorative for idols, *dung-pellets* — Block notes the term's scatological force), *zimmâ* (lewdness, sexual outrage). The *qînâ*-rhythm intrudes in the cup-of-horror oracle of 23:32–34. Cooke (ICC), Eichrodt (OTL), and Odell all note the Hebrew's frank physicality: 23:20's reference to Egyptian lovers' members *like those of donkeys* and emissions *like that of horses* is among the most explicit physical description in the Tanakh.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The literary genre is the **prophetic marriage allegory** — covenant infidelity narrated as sexual infidelity — with deep roots in Hosea 1–3 and Jeremiah 2–3. Ezekiel inherits and intensifies this tradition. Block treats Ezek 16 and 23 as a paired literary diptych. The narrative structure of chapter 23 is biographical: childhood (Egypt, vv. 3, 8, 19–21), youthful entanglement (Assyria for Oholah, vv. 5–10; Assyria and Babylon for Oholibah, vv. 11–21), judgment (vv. 22–35), shared verdict (vv. 36–49). The doubling — two sisters — produces a **typological parallelism**: what happened to Samaria *will happen* to Jerusalem, and the audience knows the first half of the parallel from the historical record (Samaria fell in 722 BC).

The literary devices are dense. **Allegory** — the entire chapter is sustained extended metaphor. **Leitwort** — *zānâ* and its derivatives appear over fifty times in 49 verses. **Catalogues** — the Assyrian and Babylonian lovers are described in specific costume detail (purple, full armor, vermilion-painted murals on walls — 23:6, 12, 14–15) that appears to draw on actual Mesopotamian iconography. The **bed of love** (23:17) and the **stately couch with table spread** (23:41) furnish a domestic-erotic scene; the *bracelets and crowns* (23:42) are the trappings of a courtesan-queen. The **cup-of-horror** poem (23:32–34) is in *qînâ* meter, recalling Jer 25:15–28's similar oracle of nations drinking the cup of YHWH's wrath; it is poetically beautiful and ethically violent at once. The closing *pedagogical-warning* note — *that all women may take warning and not commit lewdness as you have done* (23:48) — explicitly frames the chapter as didactic.

Textual variants worth flagging. The names *Oholah / Oholibah* themselves vary in some Greek manuscript traditions. The graphic vocabulary of 23:20 was sometimes softened in early translations; the LXX is generally faithful, but later versions (Vulgate, Targum Jonathan) occasionally moderate the explicitness. Greenberg and Zimmerli treat the MT as substantially intact. The cup-of-horror poem's meter and the precise sense of *gnaw its shards, and tear your breasts* (23:34) are debated; Block reads it as climactic physicality, others as text-corruption.

## Lens 3 — Larger Story

Jewish reception engaged the chapter with care and at distance. Rashi reads the allegory as a covenant-history of the divided kingdoms; Radak develops the political-historical decoding (Assyria = the eighth-century alliances; Babylon = the seventh-century turn). Ibn Ezra is more cautious about specifics. The Talmud (b. *Megillah* 25b) records the principle that **Ezekiel 16 may be read in synagogue but not translated** for the congregation — a recognition that the language is uniquely difficult to render publicly. The Mishnah (*Megillah* 4:10) notes the boundary between text-as-text (allowed) and oral exposition (restricted). Maimonides in *Guide* III.8 treats the chapter as evidence of Israel's grossly material lapse from contemplative covenant.

Christian reception has always had to reckon with the chapter's difficulty, and the honest account begins with that fact rather than burying it.

The traditional Christian reading (Origen's restricted homilies, Jerome's commentary, Gregory the Great, and Calvin's *Lectures*) takes the chapter as covenant-allegory of unfaithful Israel and finds in it three theological lessons: (1) idolatry is *spiritual adultery*, a betrayal of the covenant marriage between YHWH and his people; (2) divine jealousy is righteous rather than petty, the love of the covenant partner who has been wronged; (3) the punishment fits the offense in a moral economy where Israel's "exposure" is the moral consequence of her self-exposure. Calvin treats the chapter throughout *Lectures on Ezekiel* with insistent emphasis on the metaphor's allegorical character — the prophet is not commending the violence to women but using a culturally-available image of violated marriage to communicate covenant-violation — and with repeated acknowledgment that the language is "harsh." The patristic tradition typologizes the two sisters as Synagogue and Church (Origen, Jerome) or as the two cities (Augustine, *City of God*).

But the moral difficulty of the chapter is itself part of its reception history, and not a modern discovery. The rabbis ruled that Ezekiel 16 could be read in synagogue but not translated aloud (Mishnah Megillah 4:10; Bavli Megillah 25b), and the wider rabbinic anxiety that nearly suppressed Ezekiel — the restriction on public exposition of the *merkavah* (Mishnah Hagigah 2:1), the tradition of Hananiah ben Hezekiah's three hundred jars of oil (Bavli Shabbat 13b) — reflects a sense that the book's imagery is dangerous unsupervised. Origen would not expound chapters 16 and 23 to ordinary hearers; Jerome preserved the principle. Modern commentary states the difficulty plainly. **Greenberg** (Anchor) notes that Ezekiel applies real ANE adultery-penalties — nose- and ear-mutilation, public stripping, the seizure of children — to Jerusalem-as-female, and that this "describes punishments that were inflicted on actual women in the prophet's world"; **Zimmerli** (Hermeneia), **Block** (NICOT), **Leslie Allen** (WBC), **Margaret Odell**, **Paul Joyce**, and **Walter Brueggemann** all flag the chapter's diction — including the deliberately crude physicality of 23:20 — as among the most morally disturbing in the prophetic corpus, and as something the contemporary reader is right to find hard. The chapter has also been wrenched from its allegorical frame and misused — as 23:30's *your lewdness and your whoring have brought this upon you* maps all too readily onto the rhetoric of an abuser blaming a victim — and these commentators agree that it cannot responsibly be preached without explicit framing against that misuse. **Christl Maier's *Daughter Zion, Mother Zion* (2008)** reads the Jerusalem-as-woman imagery across the prophets and points to canonical resources — Lamentations, Second Isaiah's *daughter-Zion* of Isa 49:14–26 — that recover dignity for the female personification.

There is also a canonical counter-voice. **Christ's own gentleness with women** — the woman with the discharge of blood (Mt 9:20–22), the woman at Simon's house (Lk 7:36–50), the Samaritan woman (Jn 4), the woman caught in adultery (Jn 8:1–11) — engages women caught in exactly the situations Ezek 23's rhetoric exploits, and his consistent move is to *de-shame* rather than to shame, to *cover* rather than to expose. This does not erase Ezek 23's violence; it gives the canon a counter-voice. And the wife-of-the-Lamb image of Revelation 19 and 21 is the eschatological inversion: the Bride is *clothed*, *radiant*, *unveiled in glory*, not exposed in shame. Bauckham notes the canonical arc.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — The shocking vehicle.** Ezek 16 and 23 communicate covenant infidelity through a sustained, graphic depiction of a woman's sexual humiliation and punishment. The question is whether the chapter *reveals* divine pathos (the heart of a wronged covenant-partner) or *performs* gendered violence in religiously-sanctioned form — and the most serious form of the objection, voiced by readers from the rabbis (who would not have chapter 16 translated aloud) through modern commentators (Greenberg, Zimmerli, Block, Allen, Brueggemann), is that the prophet writes from a male-priestly position to a male audience, applies real ANE adultery-penalties — nose and ear mutilation, public stripping, seizure of children — to Jerusalem-as-female, and frames the imperial siege as deserved sexual punishment. The pastoral implications for survivors of sexual and intimate-partner violence are real: the abuser-rhetoric of "you brought this on yourself" maps directly onto 23:30's *your lewdness and your whoring have brought this upon you*. The chapter cannot be safely read in a congregation that includes survivors without explicit naming of the rhetorical structure. The least intuitive form of the steelman is this: **the objection is not asking whether the chapter is canonical (it is) but whether the chapter's preferred reading is the one that allows its violence to function as theology rather than as accidental damage.** A canonical reading that treats the violence as a regrettable cultural-rhetorical wrapper around an essentially abstract point (covenant infidelity) lets the chapter off too easily; a canonical reading that lingers on the violence and asks what it costs to use a woman's body as the metaphor for national shame takes the text more seriously, not less. Greenberg's own commentary models this — naming the horror, refusing apologetics, and still reading the chapter as covenant-allegory.

**Tension 2 — The covenant-marriage metaphor's place in Christian theology.** Ephesians 5:22–33 inherits the marriage-metaphor explicitly: *husbands love your wives as Christ loved the church*. But Ephesians **inverts** the prophetic logic — Christ *gives himself up for* the church, not against her; the husband's pattern is sacrificial self-giving, not corrective punishment. Catholic and Reformed marriage-theology has built on Eph 5 (the Catholic sacramental theology of marriage; the Reformed covenantal theology of the household) without sufficient engagement with the difficulty that Eph 5 sits in canonical tension with Ezek 23. The contemporary question — after centuries in which the marriage-metaphor and especially "wives, submit to your husbands" (Eph 5:22) have been misused as a mandate for women to remain in violent marriages — is how the metaphor functions today. Pastoral theology has had to do this work in the open: the Catholic post-Vatican-II theology of marriage (John Paul II's *Theology of the Body*; *Familiaris Consortio*, 1981), the Reformed engagement with domestic violence (Catherine Clark Kroeger and Nancy Nason-Clark, *No Place for Abuse*, 2010), and the broader recovery of the Christ-as-bridegroom imagery in the self-giving register of Ephesians 5:25 rather than the punitive register of Ezekiel 23.

**Unresolved question.** Can Ezek 23 be *read* in liturgical or homiletical contexts in a way that honors both its canonical status and its gendered violence — or does honest reading require permanent restriction (Origen's solution, the Mishnaic principle for Ezek 16)? The text has no comfortable resolution. Most modern Christian lectionaries either skip the chapter or include only fragments; most synagogues do not read it as *haftarah*. The Bible's own canon contains the violence; the Bible's communities have generally chosen to read it carefully, restrictedly, or not at all. The chapter declines to give itself an easy place in worship.

## The Thread

Four non-obvious cross-references. (1) **John 8:1–11** — *let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone at her*. The woman caught in adultery is the canonical New Testament moment that most directly addresses the social practice Ezek 23 invokes; Jesus refuses to play the prosecutor-role the prophets themselves played. The contrast is not erasure of Ezek 23 but a counter-canonical voice that the Christian tradition must hold alongside it. (2) **Hosea 2:14–23** — *I will allure her, and bring her into the wilderness, and speak tenderly to her*. Hosea is the source-text of the prophetic-marriage tradition Ezekiel inherits, but Hosea ends in restoration and tender speech. Ezek 23 declines that ending; the divergence is theologically significant. (3) **Lamentations 1** — *daughter-Zion* weeping in the streets, raped and abandoned. Lamentations is canonically the *response* of the besieged, female-personified city to her own destruction; it gives voice to the Jerusalem-as-wife from the side of the suffering rather than the prosecuting prophet. Maier (*Daughter Zion, Mother Zion*) traces this canonical counterweight at length, and the contrast with Ezek 23's prosecutorial voice is theologically pointed. (4) **Revelation 21:2** — *the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband*. The eschatological inversion: the city-bride who in Ezek 23 was stripped, mutilated, and shamed is, at the end of the canon, clothed, prepared, and glorified. The trajectory is real, but it is *trajectory*; it does not retroactively heal Ezek 23, which retains its cost.
