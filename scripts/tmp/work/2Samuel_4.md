# 2 Samuel 4: The Last Saulide and the Lame Heir

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 4 is the shortest chapter in the unification narrative — twelve verses — but its political weight is enormous. With Abner dead, the Saulide cause has lost its only effective commander, and what remains is a politically demoralized king (Ish-bosheth) and a five-year-old crippled grandson (Mephibosheth). The chapter terminates Saul's adult heirs and clears David's path to all-Israel kingship.

The political geography is precise. Rechab and Baanah are *Beerothites* — from **Beeroth**, one of the four cities of the Gibeonite confederacy that tricked Joshua into a covenant (Josh 9). Beeroth is most plausibly identified with **Khirbet el-Burj** (proposed by Edward Robinson and refined by subsequent surveys), though some scholars have argued for el-Bireh. The narrator's parenthesis — "the Beerothites fled to Gittaim and have been sojourners (*gerim*) there to this day" (4:3) — is a buried piece of social history. Something happened to the Beerothites that made them flee from their original town. McCarter and Anderson speculate, drawing on the Gibeonite-massacre material in 2 Samuel 21:1–2, that this displacement may date to a later episode of Saulide aggression against the Gibeonite confederacy. If so, then Rechab and Baanah are men with a generational grudge against Saul's house, and their assassination of Ish-bosheth has motives beyond political opportunism. The narrator does not say this explicitly, but the parenthesis hints at a backstory the apologetic surface does not develop.

**Gittaim** itself is identified tentatively with **Ras Abu Hamid** near modern Ramla (Aharoni; Mazar's surveys), though the identification is not certain. The toponym means "two winepresses" and recurs in Nehemiah 11:33. The Beerothite displacement to Gittaim is the chapter's social-historical depth: an entire population displaced by some earlier political event, raising sons who would eventually return to settle a score.

Ish-bosheth is killed during his **midday rest** — *be-chom ha-yom* (4:5). The custom of the noonday siesta (still practiced in much of the Levant) was vulnerable to attackers; the assassins reach his bedroom by feigning a grain-collection errand. The Hebrew is interesting: the LXX preserves what may be the original reading where the assassins enter pretending to fetch wheat (perhaps a tax or rations collection), find the woman who guarded the door dozing, and slip past her. The MT compresses this into a tighter narrative. McCarter prefers the LXX as preserving the older form.

The mutilation of the assassins' bodies (4:12) — David's young men *kill them, cut off their hands and feet, and hang them beside the pool at Hebron* — reflects ancient Near Eastern punitive display practices. The cutting off of hands and feet is *talionic* in symbolic logic: the hands that struck and the feet that fled are removed and exposed. The hanging beside the pool of Hebron — in plain public view — performs justice as spectacle. Compare the Philistine display of Saul's body on the walls of Beth-Shean (1 Sam 31:10), the Davidic display of the heads of his enemies (Goliath in 1 Sam 17:54), and the eventual hanging of the Saulide descendants by the Gibeonites (2 Sam 21:9). Ancient Levantine political culture used the body as a billboard.

Mephibosheth's introduction (4:4) is a parenthetical interruption that does no narrative work in chapter 4 itself but seeds chapter 9. He is five years old at Saul's death; the nurse fleeing in panic drops him; he becomes lame in both feet (*pisseach shte raglav*). The detail that his nurse dropped him while fleeing is the kind of incidental human detail that suggests an underlying tradition rather than pure invention. McCarter notes that the narrator could simply have stated the lameness as a fact; instead, the cause is given. The character of Mephibosheth — Saul's only surviving direct male heir, except for Saul's grandson via Merab — is introduced here as a political non-threat. His lameness disqualifies him from kingship under ancient Near Eastern ideology of royal physical wholeness (compare the priestly disqualifications in Lev 21:18). David can leave Mephibosheth alive without political risk.

The name *Mephibosheth* is itself a *boshet*-substitution for the original *Merib-baal* (cf. 1 Chron 8:34, 9:40), the same scribal censorship that produced "Ish-bosheth" from "Eshbaal." The pattern is consistent across the Saulide household.

Five Hebrew terms drive the chapter:

1. **רָפוּ יָדָיו** (*rafu yadav*) — "his hands grew slack" or "his courage failed" (4:1). The idiom of slack hands is the Hebrew political vocabulary for collapse of resolve. Compare Jer 6:24, 38:4.

2. **גֵרִים** (*gerim*) — "sojourners" (4:3). The same term used of the Amalekite in chapter 1. The chapter is dense with displaced peoples.

3. **פִּסֵּחַ שְׁתֵּי רַגְלָיו** (*pisseach shte raglav*) — "lame in both feet" (4:4). The doubled lameness will become Mephibosheth's defining trait through chapter 9, the chapter where David takes him into the royal household.

4. **חוֹמֶשׁ** (*chomesh*) — "the belly" (4:6). The same anatomical term used of Joab's blow to Abner (3:27) and of Abner's blow to Asahel (2:23). The chapter belongs to a sequence united by the gut-strike.

5. **בְּרֵכַת חֶבְרוֹן** (*berekat Hevron*) — "the pool of Hebron" (4:12). The display location. The pool of Hebron itself is identifiable — a substantial Iron Age cistern survives in the old city — though precise correspondence to the chapter's pool is debated.

Archaeologically, the parenthesis about the Beerothite displacement and the Gittaim refuge connects to the broader question of the Gibeonite confederacy under Saul. The Saulide displacement of the Gibeonite-allied populations, which 2 Sam 21 presupposes, has been correlated with archaeological destruction layers at certain sites in the Benjaminite hill country (Tell el-Ful — Saul's capital at Gibeah — and surrounding sites). The picture is suggestive but not decisive.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 4 is structured as a tight three-movement narrative: the political collapse of the Saulide cause and the parenthetical introduction of the Beerothite assassins and Mephibosheth (1–4), the assassination itself (5–8), and David's judgment-and-execution speech (9–12). The chapter is unusually compact and moves quickly.

The opening verse (4:1) — Ish-bosheth's collapse of resolve — uses the Hebrew idiom of "slack hands" that has political-military weight. The narrator is signaling that the Saulide cause has structurally ended even before the assassination; what remains is bookkeeping. McCarter and Brueggemann both note that the narrator could have ended the Saulide narrative more dramatically; instead, the death is staged as anticlimactic, almost squalid — a midday assassination of a sleeping man.

The parenthetical about the Beerothite displacement (4:2–3) is a narrator's intrusion of unusual length for a single piece of background. The information is offered, then the narrative resumes. McCarter argues that the parenthesis is integrated apologetic: the assassins are already marked as displaced and disgruntled, which both motivates their action (they have an old grievance against Saul's family) and distances David from any commissioning role. The narrator wants the reader to know that these men were not Davidic agents but men with their own agenda.

The Mephibosheth interruption (4:4) is even more striking. It interrupts the narrative momentum to introduce a character who will not appear again until chapter 9. Polzin and Fokkelman have analyzed the placement. The most plausible reading is that the narrator inserts Mephibosheth here to establish that, after the assassination of Ish-bosheth, Saul's house is not literally extinct — there is one surviving direct male heir — but he is politically disqualified. The narrative needs to forestall the obvious objection: *if David wanted to clear the throne, what about Jonathan's son?* The parenthesis answers: *he is five years old and crippled, and David will eventually take him into the household.* The chapter's apologetic shape extends even to this incidental note.

The assassination scene itself (4:5–8) is staged with grim economy. The two brothers approach the house at the noon hour; they enter under pretext of fetching wheat; they reach the bedroom; they kill Ish-bosheth and behead him; they travel by night through the Arabah to Hebron. The Arabah route — south through the Jordan rift valley — is roughly 100 kilometers from Mahanaim to Hebron. The trip would take a determined party two to three nights of fast travel.

The presentation of the head (4:8) is the assassins' political miscalculation. Their speech — "Here is the head of Ish-bosheth, the son of Saul, your enemy, who sought your life. The LORD has avenged my lord the king this day on Saul and on his offspring" — is constructed to flatter David by invoking Saul-as-enemy and the LORD-as-avenger. They expect reward. They have miscalculated in three ways: they have misunderstood David's repeated public commitment to the inviolability of the LORD's anointed; they have misunderstood the apologetic logic that requires David never to benefit from a Saulide death he can be associated with; and they have invoked the LORD's vengeance in a way that insults David's piety. Brueggemann notes the parallelism with the Amalekite of chapter 1: the same flattering speech, the same misjudged hope of reward, the same outcome.

David's response speech (4:9–11) is a textual masterpiece of forensic apologia. He invokes the LORD as redeemer of his life from every adversity (positioning himself as receiver-of-grace, not seeker-of-power). He recalls the Amalekite case (4:10) explicitly — establishing that his prior pattern, applied to a man bringing news of the death of Saul, will all the more apply to men bringing news of the death of Saul's son. The argument is from the lesser to the greater: if I executed the messenger who *claimed* to have killed Saul, how much more will I execute men who *actually* killed Ish-bosheth in his bed? The rhetorical structure is the *qal va-chomer* of rabbinic argumentation, the standard ancient Jewish form of "all the more so."

The execution and display (4:12) is the chapter's closing image. The brothers are killed, mutilated, hung beside the pool. The head of Ish-bosheth is buried in Abner's tomb at Hebron — a striking gesture of dynastic respect. Abner had been the Saulide commander; Ish-bosheth had been the Saulide king; David buries them together in the same tomb. The act constructs a kind of Saulide memorial within Hebron itself, performed by the rival claimant. McCarter reads this as an authentic political gesture intended to assure the northern tribes that David honors Saul's memory; Halpern reads it as apologetic theater.

Within the book's argument, chapter 4 is the formal end of the Saulide-Davidic civil war. After this chapter, no Saulide claimant remains. Chapter 5 will record the elders of all-Israel coming to Hebron to anoint David. The Deuteronomist has structured the transition as a series of removals — Saul (1 Sam 31), Jonathan and his brothers (1 Sam 31), Abner (2 Sam 3), Ish-bosheth (2 Sam 4) — each followed by David's public mourning or judgment. The pattern is too consistent to be accidental.

Textually, the chapter has minor LXX-MT variants. The most significant is the LXX expansion of the entry-into-the-house scene (4:6), which preserves what appears to be an older form of the narrative. The MT's compression is plausibly secondary. 4QSam-a is fragmentary here. The *boshet/baal* substitution operates throughout, affecting both Ish-bosheth and Mephibosheth.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical arc, chapter 4 closes the Saulide-Davidic political conflict and clears the way for the unified kingdom of chapter 5. The chapter is the structural counterpart to chapter 1: that chapter dealt with the death of Saul; this chapter deals with the death of Saul's son. Both chapters are framed by David's execution of the killer-claimants.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** Josephus (*Ant.* 7.2) treats the chapter with restraint, emphasizing the assassins' miscalculation and David's piety. Rabbinic tradition is harsh on the assassins. *Sanhedrin* 49a treats the killing of Ish-bosheth as straightforward murder and David's execution of Rechab and Baanah as exemplary justice. Rashi reads the Mephibosheth interruption as the narrator's preemptive answer to the question of dynastic continuity. Radak develops the legal analysis: Ish-bosheth, although not the LORD's anointed in the technical sense (he had been installed by Abner without divine word), was nevertheless a legitimate political figure, and his murder in his bed by men under no military authorization was simple homicide. Ralbag emphasizes the *qal va-chomer* structure of David's response speech and treats it as a model of judicial reasoning. Abarbanel, characteristically, develops a sustained reading of the chapter as a study in the doctrine of providence: the Saulide line ends not by Davidic action but by Saulide internal failure (Beerothite captains, displaced and disgruntled, acting on their own initiative). The chapter, for Abarbanel, demonstrates that the LORD's promise to David is fulfilled without David himself having to act unjustly. Yair Zakovitch's literary reading attends to the parallel structure with chapter 1. Moshe Garsiel's typological work emphasizes the inversion: Saul had pursued David through 1 Samuel; now Saul's house is liquidated without David lifting a hand. Avivah Zornberg has read the Mephibosheth parenthesis as the chapter's most affecting moment — a child dropped by his fleeing nurse, a permanent disability inscribed at the moment of dynastic catastrophe.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** Patristic readings cluster around the chapter's apparent demonstration of providence. Origen's fragmentary homilies on Samuel treat the chapter typologically as the demonstration that the kingdom of God advances without the king of God doing harm. Augustine in *City of God* book 17 treats the chapter as a stage in the political drama of Davidic accession; he is particularly interested in the contrast between human violence (the Beerothites' assassination) and divine providence (David's reception of the news without complicity). Ambrose in *De Officiis* uses David's execution of the assassins as a model of judicial integrity: even when a death benefits the ruler, the ruler must punish illegitimate killers. Chrysostom dwells on the doubled apologetic — the Amalekite and the Beerothites — as evidence of David's settled character. Gregory the Great in the *Moralia* reads the Mephibosheth parenthesis as a meditation on the wounds inflicted by political turmoil on the innocent. Aquinas, in his treatment of capital punishment (*Summa* II-II q. 64), cites David's execution of the Beerothites as a textbook case of just punishment: their action was murder regardless of its political effect. The principle is foundational for Christian political theology.

Calvin's sermons on 2 Samuel — preserved in the Genevan manuscripts — give substantial attention to chapter 4. Calvin reads the chapter as a study in the doctrine of providence: the LORD removes obstacles to David's kingship without requiring David to act unjustly. He treats David's response speech as exemplary of how godly magistrates must distinguish between desirable outcomes and the means that produce them. Luther's marginal notes are brief but emphasize the chapter's depiction of the assassins' moral squalor.

Critical scholarship: Wellhausen identified the chapter as part of David's apologetic source. McCarter's Anchor Bible commentary treats the apologetic shape carefully and develops the LXX-MT comparison at 4:6. A. A. Anderson (WBC) gives the most detailed text-critical and source-critical analysis; he is particularly valuable on the Beerothite background and the Gibeonite-confederacy connection. Hertzberg's Old Testament Library volume remains the standard mid-twentieth-century treatment. Brueggemann reads the chapter as the moment when David's cause becomes irreversibly the LORD's cause; the apologetic shape is deliberate but not, in Brueggemann's reading, dishonest. Halpern (*David's Secret Demons*) is sharper: the chapter is the third in a sequence (chapters 1, 3, 4) where Saulide deaths produce political benefit for David and where David always punishes those who acted. Halpern reads the pattern as too consistent to be accidental and treats the chapter as part of the apologetic court source. McKenzie (*King David: A Biography*) follows a similar line; he is particularly interested in the question of whether David could have prevented the assassination if he had wanted to. Polzin (*David and the Deuteronomist* vol. 3) emphasizes the Deuteronomist's theological framing: the chapter's events are presented as providential, not contingent. Fokkelman's literary analysis is thorough on the Mephibosheth parenthesis; he and Alter both observe how the chapter depends on women's roles for the plot to turn — the unnamed nurse who drops Mephibosheth, the unnamed woman dozing at Ish-bosheth's door in the LXX form — while granting none of them speech or name, a narrative economy of its own.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is David's repeated pattern of innocence credible?** Chapters 1, 3, and 4 all stage Saulide deaths from which David benefits, all followed by David's public lament or judgment. By the third repetition, the apologetic structure is conspicuous.

*Position A: The pattern is credible because it is consistent with David's prior behavior.* This is the rabbinic and patristic reading. David refused to kill Saul in the cave (1 Sam 24) and in the camp (1 Sam 26), establishing his personal commitment to the inviolability of the LORD's anointed. The pattern in 2 Sam 1, 3, and 4 is consistent with that commitment, not engineered for political effect.

*Position B: The pattern is structural apologia and the question of David's actual involvement is unanswerable.* This is Halpern's and McKenzie's reading. The text protects David in every case; whether David protected himself is a question the apologetic surface forecloses. The repetition itself is evidence of authorial design.

*Position C: The pattern is providential rather than personal.* This is Abarbanel's reading and the dominant Christian patristic line. The LORD removes obstacles to David's kingship; David's role is to react with appropriate piety. The text presents the providential frame, not a forensic frame.

The steelman of A is strong: David's personal commitment to royal inviolability is established before the apologetic pattern starts. The steelman of B is also strong: the pattern is too consistent to be coincidence. The steelman of C is strongest theologically: the chapter is structured to emphasize divine providence, not Davidic agency.

*Where this leaves us:* The three positions are not mutually exclusive. The pattern is real (B); David's commitment is real (A); the providential frame is the chapter's primary theological move (C). The text is honest about the convergence of all three: David's character, the political situation, and the LORD's providence all point in the same direction. Whether this convergence is theologically generative or apologetically suspect is a question the reader must answer.

**Tension 2: What is the moral status of the Mephibosheth interruption?** A five-year-old crippled grandson of Saul is introduced parenthetically, set aside as politically non-threatening, and not engaged again until chapter 9.

*Position A: The interruption is narrative housekeeping.* The narrator needs to acknowledge that Saul's house is not literally extinct and to forestall the obvious dynastic-continuity question. The interruption serves its function and moves on.

*Position B: The interruption is theological commentary.* The chapter's apologetic shape requires a remaining Saulide whom David can later treat with magnanimity. Mephibosheth's introduction here sets up his eventual reception in chapter 9 as an exhibit of Davidic *chesed* toward Saul's house.

*Position C: The interruption is the chapter's most affecting human moment.* A child dropped by his nurse at the moment of Saul's defeat carries a permanent wound. The narrator includes the cause of the lameness as an act of historical compassion: the disability has a story, and the story is dropped-and-fled. Zornberg's reading.

*Where this leaves us:* All three positions are simultaneously true. The interruption serves multiple purposes — narrative, theological, and humane. The Deuteronomist refuses to choose among them. The chapter's most striking feature is that the assassination of Ish-bosheth and the disabling of Mephibosheth are placed side by side: one Saulide killed in his bed, one Saulide crippled at his nurse's flight. The chapter frames the Saulide tragedy as a sequence of human catastrophes, not just a political transition.

**Unresolved question:** What did David think when he received the head of Ish-bosheth? The narrator gives us the response speech but not the reaction. Did David grieve as he had for Saul and Abner? The chapter omits the grief that the previous chapters made central. Is the omission an apologetic refusal (showing Ish-bosheth as too marginal to mourn), a narrative restraint (the lament-pattern has been established and need not be repeated), or a moral refusal (David genuinely did not grieve for the king he was replacing)? The chapter does not answer.

## The Thread

The Mephibosheth introduction at 4:4 is one of the most consequential narrative plants in the David story. Mephibosheth will return at 9:1–13 (David's reception of him into the royal household), 16:1–4 (Ziba's accusation during Absalom's revolt), 19:24–30 (the post-revolt confrontation), and 21:7 (David's sparing him from the Gibeonite execution). The five verses devoted to Mephibosheth across 2 Samuel are nearly all set up by this single parenthetical verse in chapter 4.

The pattern of David's punishment of Saulide-killers (Amalekite in 1, Beerothites in 4) establishes a doctrine of dynastic non-aggression that will be tested and confirmed at chapter 21, when David must decide what to do about the Gibeonites' demand for Saulide blood. The chapter's principle — that those who kill the Saulides are themselves killed — is reversed at chapter 21, where David allows Gibeonite vengeance in expiation of Saul's earlier violation. The thread runs through both directions.

The Beerothite displacement to Gittaim (4:3) seeds the broader narrative of the Gibeonite confederacy's grievance against Saul's house. The full account of Saulide aggression against the Gibeonites is held back until 21:1–2; the chapter 4 parenthesis is the first hint.

Flag forward: the burial of Ish-bosheth's head in Abner's tomb at Hebron (4:12) creates a Saulide memorial within David's first capital. When David moves the capital to Jerusalem in chapter 5, the Hebron tomb-complex remains as a site of dynastic memory. The narrator does not develop this further, but the chapter establishes that the Davidic regime is willing to honor the Saulide dead even as it consolidates the Saulide territories. The political-theological move will echo through subsequent chapters.
