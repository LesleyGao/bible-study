# 1 Corinthians 10 — Going Deeper

## Historical World

Chapter 10 returns to the eidōlothyton question of chapter 8 with a sharper edge. Where chapter 8 ceded the strong's theological premise (idols have no existence) and bound them with the weak-brother principle, chapter 10 introduces a second register: the cultic eating in temple precincts is not merely a scandal to the weak; it is a real spiritual peril for the strong. The Corinthian "knowledge" had treated the temple meal as innocuous because the gods behind the meat are nothing. Paul agrees the gods are nothing — and then says behind those nothings stand demons, and you cannot share the cup of demons and the cup of the Lord both.

The cultic-meal context Paul presupposes is well attested archaeologically. Three small dining triclinia at the Corinthian Asclepieion, with stone benches for about eleven each, formed a setting where the strong ate sacrificial meat in a setting deliberately marked as the god's table. Inscriptions from across the Greek world confirm the standard formula of the dinner invitation: "X invites you to dine at the table of the lord Sarapis" (or Asclepius, or another deity). Murphy-O'Connor reproduced one such P. Oxy. invitation; the formula trapeza tou kyriou makes Paul's polemic at 10:21 ("the table of the Lord [trapeza Kyriou] / the table of demons [trapeza daimoniōn]") a deliberate counterposition. The meal in the Asclepieion really did claim a kyrios; the Christian Eucharist claims another.

The macellum (meat market) was a different setting altogether. Excavations at Roman Corinth have not produced the city's macellum with certainty, but Justin Meggitt (Paul, Poverty and Survival, 1998) and Bruce Winter (After Paul Left Corinth, 2001) reconstruct a typical macellum from comparative evidence: a colonnaded market building selling meat, fish, and produce, with butchers, fishmongers, and produce vendors operating individual stalls. Most meat in the Roman macellum was a byproduct of temple sacrifice; in non-festival weeks the supply was thin enough that the question of provenance often did not arise practically. Paul's permission at 10:25 ("eat whatever is sold in the meat market without raising any question") cuts the conscience problem at the source: do not investigate.

The wilderness narratives Paul invokes in 10:1-13 had been intensely worked over in Second Temple Jewish exegesis. The cloud and sea (Exod 13-14), the manna (Exod 16, Num 11), the water from the rock (Exod 17, Num 20), the golden calf (Exod 32), the Baal-Peor incident (Num 25, where 23,000 or 24,000 fell — Paul takes the lower figure), the serpents (Num 21), the destroyer in the Korah revolt (Num 16) — these formed a standard catechetical sequence of warning examples. Paul does not invent the typology; he steps into it.

## Literature

The chapter has three movements. (1) 10:1-13: midrashic exposition of the wilderness narratives as warning typology. (2) 10:14-22: the cultic-eating prohibition based on koinōnia. (3) 10:23-11:1: the macellum-and-dinner-invitation casuistry, with the two-cases-and-two-rules structure that resolves the chapter 8 tension.

Richard Hays (Echoes of Scripture in the Letters of Paul, 1989) reads 10:1-13 as Paul's most sustained piece of "ecclesial midrash" — the wilderness generation as a typological mirror in which the Corinthian church can see itself. The structure is built on an emphatic five-fold pantes ("all"): all under the cloud, all passed through the sea, all baptized into Moses, all ate the same spiritual food, all drank the same spiritual drink. The repetition is doing structural work: Paul wants to deny the Corinthians the move that says the wilderness generation lacked what we have. They had it. They had the analogues of baptism and Eucharist. And nevertheless God was not pleased with most of them. The five-fold pantes pivots on the all' ("nevertheless") of 10:5, which collapses sacramental possession into nothing without endurance.

The hē petra de ēn ho Christos at the end of 10:4 — "and the rock was Christ" — is one of the most theologically loaded sentences in Paul. The grammatical move is identification (de ēn, "and was"), not similitude. Joseph Fitzmyer (Anchor Yale 2008) and Anthony Thiselton (NIGTC) catalog the three main readings: typological (the OT rock prefigures Christ), allegorical (the OT rock signifies Christ), or pre-existence (the OT rock was, in some real sense, the pre-incarnate Christ). The verbal tense ēn (imperfect) does heavy work; combined with the imperfect akolouthousēs ("the rock that followed [them]") it suggests Paul is drawing on the Jewish midrashic tradition of Miriam's well — the rock that traveled with Israel through the wilderness, attested in the Tosefta (Sukkah 3:11), the Mekhilta of R. Ishmael, and Pseudo-Philo (Biblical Antiquities 10:7). Paul takes this Jewish midrash and gives it a Christological turn.

The exhortative center at 10:11-13 is built on three claims pressed in sequence: the wilderness narratives were "written down for our instruction (nouthesia), on whom the end of the ages has come" (eschatological framing); "let anyone who thinks he stands take heed lest he fall" (warning); "no temptation has overtaken you that is not common to man" (consolation). Paul sets warning and assurance in tension. The Corinthians are not exempt from the wilderness generation's fate; God is faithful and provides the way out.

The koinōnia argument at 10:14-22 is the rhetorical core. Paul deploys koinōnia three times in three sentences. The cup of blessing is a koinōnia of the blood of Christ; the bread is a koinōnia of the body; we who are many are one body because we all partake of the one bread. He extends the principle to Israel: those who eat the sacrifices are koinōnoi of the altar. He extends it to pagan sacrifice: what pagans sacrifice they sacrifice to demons; eating the demons' food is being koinōnoi with demons. The argument depends entirely on the claim that table fellowship constitutes a real participation in the cult to which the meal belongs.

The macellum casuistry at 10:23-11:1 is structured as two cases with two rules. Case 1: the macellum and the unbeliever's dinner invitation — eat without questioning, on the principle of Psalm 24:1 ("the earth is the Lord's"). Case 2: when an informer (perhaps a fellow Christian, perhaps a scrupulous host) flags the meat as eidōlothyton — do not eat, for the sake of his conscience. The qualifying sentences at 10:29-30 ("I do not mean your conscience, but his; for why should my liberty be determined by someone else's conscience?") are notoriously difficult. Garland (BECNT 2003) and Fee read them as Paul anticipating the "strong" Corinthian objection and conceding that yes, your liberty is real, but love trumps liberty; the "why should my liberty be determined" is the strong's question, not Paul's settled answer.

## The Larger Story

### The Jewish trajectory

The wilderness typology Paul deploys is itself a Jewish form. The midrash on the rock that "followed" Israel through the wilderness is a Jewish exegetical solution to the textual problem that water comes from the rock at the start of the wilderness (Exod 17, at Rephidim) and again at the end (Num 20, at Kadesh). The Tosefta Sukkah 3:11 reads: "And so the well that was with Israel in the wilderness was a rock, in shape like a sieve, and it would roll along with them on their journeys." Pseudo-Philo's Biblical Antiquities 10:7 names the same tradition. Philo (Legum Allegoriae 2.86) read the rock allegorically as the Wisdom of God. Paul stands within this Jewish midrashic stream, and his Christological identification is the kind of move a first-century Jewish midrashist could make: identifying the Wisdom-figure, the Logos-figure, the Memra, with a specific historical instantiation. Daniel Boyarin (A Radical Jew, 1994) sees Paul's midrash as Jewish exegesis with a Christ-shaped resolution.

The 23,000-figure in 10:8 is exegetically interesting. Numbers 25:9 reads 24,000. The discrepancy has been explained as: a Pauline lapse of memory (Conzelmann); a deliberate harmonization of two distinct judgments in the Baal-Peor narrative (Garland, Fitzmyer); or reliance on a textual variant. The point Paul is making is not arithmetic; the wilderness sexual-cultic transgression at Baal-Peor is the closest analogue to the Corinthian situation — eating sacrificial meat at a cult that combined religion and sexual license.

The cup of blessing (kos shel berakhah, kotērion tēs eulogias) is the Jewish cup of the third blessing in the Passover seder (Mishnah Pesachim 10) — the cup over which the blessing after meals (birkat ha-mazon) was said. By the late first century the Christian Eucharist had taken its language from the Jewish meal blessing. Paul's "cup of blessing that we bless" is a Jewish liturgical expression appropriated for the new meal.

The "law of jealousy" theme at 10:22 (parazēloumen ton Kyrion) cites Deut 32:21 — the song of Moses, in which Israel provokes YHWH to jealousy by going after no-gods. Paul uses the same Septuagintal verb (parazēloō). The Corinthians who eat at the demons' table are repeating the wilderness sin; they are the contemporary instance of the song of Moses' indictment.

### The Christian trajectory

Patristic interpretation of 10:1-13 turned the wilderness narratives into the standard catechetical typology of baptismal grace and post-baptismal danger. Origen (Homilies on Numbers) and Cyril of Alexandria read the cloud as the Holy Spirit, the sea as baptism, the manna as the Eucharist; the wilderness was the catechumenate and the Christian life. Augustine (Tractates on John, Sermon 71 on the Donatist controversy) deployed 10:1-13 against Donatist sectarianism: the wilderness generation possessed the sacraments and was nevertheless lost; therefore sacramental possession does not guarantee salvation; therefore the visible church contains both wheat and tares.

The eucharistic verses at 10:16-17 became the central proof text in the medieval and Reformation debates over the Real Presence. Trent (Session 13, 1551) declared that "by the consecration of the bread and wine there takes place a change of the whole substance of the bread into the substance of the body of Christ our Lord, and of the whole substance of the wine into the substance of his blood. This change the holy Catholic Church has fittingly and properly named transubstantiation." The text was Paul's koinōnia. Luther's reading (Marburg 1529) — "this is my body" combined with "is it not a participation in the body of Christ?" — defended a sacramental union, in which Christ's body is present "in, with, and under" the bread without transubstantiation. The Formula of Concord (1577) codified the Lutheran view. Calvin (Institutes 4.17) read koinōnia as the work of the Spirit lifting the believer to a real participation in the heavenly Christ — the "spiritual real presence." Zwingli (Marburg) read koinōnia as memorial and signifying participation, not substantial. The Anglican Articles (Article 28) charted a comprehensive middle: the body of Christ "is given, taken, and eaten in the Supper, only after an heavenly and spiritual manner. And the mean whereby the Body of Christ is received and eaten in the Supper, is Faith."

The pre-existence Christology of 10:4 was deployed in Nicene-era polemic. Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho 113-114) and Irenaeus read "the rock was Christ" as evidence that the pre-incarnate Logos was the agent of the OT theophanies. Augustine (Tractates on John 9:9; De Trinitate 2.10-13) made the same move standard. The reading became part of the broader patristic claim that the Son was active throughout salvation history, not merely from Bethlehem onward.

## Theological Intent

### Tension 1: Eucharistic presence

What is the koinōnia in 10:16?

**The transubstantiation reading (Trent, 1551).** Christ is substantially present under the appearances of bread and wine. The koinōnia is participation in the substantial body and blood, transubstantiated by consecration. Steelman: the realism of Paul's language is striking. "Cup of blessing" / "table of demons" — the pagan parallel cannot be merely memorial; the demons are really there, really being feasted with. By symmetry, the Lord's table must be a real participation in him, not a mental act of remembering. Aquinas's metaphysical articulation specifies how the substance can change while the accidents remain.

**The Lutheran sacramental union reading (Marburg 1529, Formula of Concord 1577).** Christ's body and blood are really present "in, with, and under" the bread and wine, by virtue of the words of institution. The koinōnia is real but the bread remains bread. Steelman: the reading takes both the realism of Paul's language and the realism of bread-as-bread seriously, and refuses to specify the metaphysics beyond what the text warrants. Luther's repeated "this is my body" hammer at Marburg was a refusal to abstract the question into philosophical categories.

**The Reformed pneumatic real presence (Calvin, Institutes 4.17).** The Holy Spirit makes the koinōnia real by lifting the believer's soul to participate in the ascended Christ. Christ's body remains in heaven (Calvin's argument from the ascension); the Spirit bridges the spatial gap. The koinōnia is genuinely a participation in the body and blood, mediated pneumatologically. Steelman: this reading takes seriously the eschatological-locative texts (Christ at the Father's right hand) without collapsing the meal into mere memorial. Calvin pressed hard on the Spirit's role precisely so that "real presence" would not require local presence.

**The Zwinglian memorial reading.** The Supper is a remembrance and confession; the koinōnia is the church's collective participation in the meaning of Christ's death, not an objective presence in the elements. Steelman: Paul's "is it not a koinōnia" is rhetorical — the participation is the church's, and the meal signifies and seals it without conveying it. Zwingli pressed John 6:63 ("the flesh profits nothing") against Luther.

The text's pressure runs toward realism. The pagan parallel — "the cup of demons" — Paul takes seriously enough to forbid; the demons are really at the demonic table. By the symmetry of the argument the Lord is really at the Lord's table. Whether the realism is best articulated by transubstantiation, sacramental union, pneumatic presence, or some other category, Paul forbids the deflationary memorial reading from taking the demonic-presence side seriously. The least intuitive reading — for many modern Protestants, the Catholic transubstantiation — should be steelmanned at exactly this point: the realism of "table of demons" is the realism of "table of the Lord."

### Tension 2: Christological pre-existence in 10:4

How much weight can "the rock was Christ" bear?

**The full pre-existence reading (Justin, Augustine, much patristic and modern Catholic-Reformed traditions).** The OT rock was, in a real sense, the pre-incarnate Christ. Combined with John 1:1-3 ("through whom all things were made"), 1 Cor 8:6 ("through whom are all things"), and Phil 2:6-11 (the kenosis hymn), Paul has a settled pre-existence Christology by AD 54. Steelman: the imperfect verbs (ēn, akolouthousēs) point to a continuous ontological identity, not a typological pattern of resemblance. Paul is locating Christ within the OT theophanies.

**The typological-Christological reading (Hays, Wright).** Paul's move is exegetical-Christological, not metaphysical-Christological. "The rock was Christ" means: the OT rock prefigures and typifies the Christ-event; the Jewish midrash of the following rock is given a Christological resolution. Pre-existence may be elsewhere in Paul (Phil 2, Col 1), but 10:4 alone is making a typological-exegetical claim. Steelman: this reading explains Paul's freedom with the Jewish midrashic tradition without requiring him to articulate a fourth-century metaphysics.

**The midrashic-allegorical reading (Boyarin, with patristic Origen-style allegorism).** Paul is doing the kind of figural reading Philo does with the rock-as-Wisdom; the move is not pre-existence in the strong sense but figural identification of an OT motif with the Christological figure. Steelman: this reading is exegetically plausible because the tradition Paul draws on (Philo's rock-as-Wisdom) is itself a figural-allegorical move, and Paul gives it a Christological turn within that genre.

The reading that does most justice to the text is probably the typological-Christological one (Hays, Wright). Paul is doing Jewish exegesis with a Christological terminus. Whether this implies pre-existence in the strong sense depends on what other Pauline texts (Phil 2, Col 1, 1 Cor 8:6) are doing, not on 10:4 alone.

### Where this leaves us

The chapter holds two warnings together. The first is the wilderness warning: sacramental possession does not save you. The Corinthians have baptism and Eucharist; so did the wilderness generation; God was not pleased with most of them. The second is the cultic warning: you cannot eat at the demons' table and the Lord's table both. The ground of both warnings is the same: tables really do bind their guests. The wilderness generation's manna and water bound them to the giver — and they broke the bond. The Corinthian Eucharist binds them to Christ. The cultic meal at the Asclepieion or the temple of Aphrodite binds them to demons. They cannot have both.

The casuistry that closes the chapter (10:23-33) is the resolution of the chapter 8 tension. Macellum meat: eat without asking. Dinner invitation: eat what is set before you. But if it is identified as eidōlothyton: do not eat, for the sake of the informer's conscience. The double rule — liberty in private, restraint in public scandal — is what the chapter has been working toward. The strong are theologically right; the weak are pastorally protected; the cultic line is drawn at the temple's door.

### One unresolved question

What does Paul mean by demons (daimonia) in 10:20-21? The line that "what pagans sacrifice they sacrifice to demons and not to God" (citing Deut 32:17 LXX) is doing real metaphysical work. The chapter 8 concession — "an idol has no real existence" — is qualified here by the claim that behind the no-existent idols stand real spiritual powers to whom worship is given and from whom contamination comes. How this fits Paul's wider demonology (the principalities and powers of Romans 8 and Ephesians 6) is contested; how it fits a contemporary Christian metaphysics is more contested still. Paul takes the demonic seriously enough to forbid the cultic meal. What that demands of Christians at any analogous contemporary table — civic religion, ideological cult, economic-symbolic feast — the chapter does not specify.

### The Thread

Tables form their guests. The wilderness generation ate spiritual food and drank spiritual drink and were nevertheless overthrown because they did not stay at God's table. The Corinthians have a better table, the table of the Lord, and they cannot have it and another. The cup is real on both sides. Macellum meat is just meat; the temple meal is something else. The chapter ends not with a rule but with a posture: do all to the glory of God; give no offense to Jews or Greeks or the church; seek the good of many that they may be saved. The cultic line is drawn for the sake of the people — both the strong who must not break with the Lord, and the weak whose conscience must not be broken, and the unbeliever for whom the church is supposed to be the table they may yet be invited to.
