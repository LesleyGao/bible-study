# Judges 13: The Woman Who Saw the Flame

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens on the geography of the Shephelah — the foothills between the Philistine coastal plain and the Judean highlands. Zorah and Eshtaol, the named villages of Manoah's family, sit on the northwestern edge of Judah's territory, roughly twenty miles inland from the Philistine pentapolis (Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Gath, Ekron). The tribe of Dan, originally allotted territory in this region (Joshua 19:40–48), was being squeezed out by Philistine pressure throughout the Iron I period and would eventually relocate north (Judges 18). Manoah's household is therefore on the front lines of an ongoing demographic and military contest, and the Spirit of the LORD that begins to stir Samson "in Mahaneh-dan, between Zorah and Eshtaol" (13:25) is being stirred in a contested borderland.

The Philistines who oppress Israel for forty years (13:1) are now far better understood archaeologically than they were a generation ago. Excavations at Tel Miqne (biblical Ekron) under Trude Dothan and Seymour Gitin from 1981 to 1996, at Ashkelon under Lawrence Stager from 1985 onward, at Ashdod under Moshe Dothan, and at Tell es-Safi (biblical Gath) under Aren Maeir from 1996 to the present, have established the Philistine arrival in Canaan as part of the broader Sea Peoples settlement around 1175 BCE. The bichrome pottery, locally produced but stylistically Aegean, the Mycenaean-derived architectural forms (notably the megaron-style hearth-room buildings at Ekron Stratum VI), the imported and locally adapted cultic figurines (the seated female "Ashdoda" type), and the consumption of pig in significant quantities (a sharp dietary marker distinguishing Philistine from Israelite sites in the Iron I) all confirm the biblical picture of a culturally distinct group with maritime origins. By the time of Samson's birth, the Philistines had moved from initial settlement to political consolidation; Iron IIA architecture at Ekron and Gath shows fortified urban centers with administrative complexity. Israel was being squeezed by a more technologically advanced and politically organized neighbor.

The chapter's central religious institution is the Nazirite vow, set out programmatically in Numbers 6:1–21. The Nazirite (Hebrew *nazir*, נָזִיר, from the root *nazar* — to separate, consecrate) abstains from three things: products of the vine (wine, grape juice, raisins), the razor (no haircut for the duration of the vow), and contact with corpses (including those of close family members). The vow in Numbers is voluntary and time-limited, ended by ritual shaving and offering at the sanctuary. The Samson Nazirite vow is unusual in two ways: it is lifelong (compare Samuel in 1 Samuel 1:11 and John the Baptist in Luke 1:15), and it is imposed by divine annunciation before the child's birth rather than chosen by the adult. The maternal restrictions in 13:4 — no wine, no strong drink, no unclean food — extend the Nazirite obligation backward into the womb, an unparalleled extension. Jacob Milgrom's *Numbers* commentary in the JPS Torah series treats the Samson Nazirite as a special category, "Nazirite from the womb," distinct from the standard Numbers 6 vow.

The annunciation type-scene that structures the chapter has deep ANE roots. Robert Alter's *The Art of Biblical Narrative* identified annunciation as one of the recurring biblical type-scenes, with key examples in Sarah (Genesis 18), Rebekah (Genesis 25), Rachel (Genesis 30), Hannah (1 Samuel 1), the Shunammite woman (2 Kings 4), Elizabeth (Luke 1), and Mary (Luke 1). The standard elements are barren wife, divine messenger, promise of a son, sometimes a name and a destiny. The Judges 13 version inverts the type in striking ways: the angel appears to the woman (not the man), the husband requires a second visitation to be persuaded, and the wife is the theological interpreter who tells her husband he will not die.

The material culture of the chapter is densely sketched. The "young goat" (*gedi ʿizzim*, גְּדִי עִזִּים) Manoah prepares for the visitor is the standard hospitality offering of the period, attested in the Ugaritic Keret and Aqhat texts and in the Mari diplomatic correspondence. The grain offering (*minḥah*, מִנְחָה) and the rock altar (*tzur*, צוּר) follow patriarchal-period practice (compare Gideon's offering in Judges 6:19–24 and Abraham's in Genesis 22:13). The flame ascending from the altar with the angel inside it is a theophanic motif also attested in Exodus 3, 1 Kings 18, and 2 Kings 2. The naming of the child by his mother (13:24) is the more common Hebrew Bible pattern; paternal naming (as in Abraham's naming of Isaac in Genesis 21:3) is the marked exception.

Five Hebrew terms repay attention. *Malʾakh YHWH* (מַלְאַךְ יהוה), "the angel of the LORD," is the chapter's central theological category — a figure who speaks as God, accepts sacrifice, and yet is grammatically distinct from God; the so-called *malʾakh-YHWH* problem in biblical theology turns on cases like this one. *Nazir* (נָזִיר) is the Nazirite vow itself. *Peliʾ* (פֶּלִיא), "wonderful," at 13:18, is one of only a few uses of this exact form; the cognate noun *pele* (פֶּלֶא) appears in Isaiah 9:6 ("his name shall be called Wonderful Counselor"), and the patristic and rabbinic tradition both noted the connection. *Shimshon* (שִׁמְשׁוֹן), "Samson," derives from *shemesh* (שֶׁמֶשׁ), "sun," and is widely read as a solar-hero name; the geographical proximity to Beth-Shemesh ("house of the sun") strengthens the etymology. *Mahaneh-dan* (מַחֲנֵה־דָן), "camp of Dan," is the location where the Spirit begins to stir him, named again in Judges 18:12 as a place of muster.

The unnamed status of Manoah's wife is one of the chapter's most-discussed features. Mieke Bal in *Death and Dissymmetry* made it a structural observation: across Judges, women who carry theological weight are systematically denied names. Pseudo-Philo (*LAB* 42) supplies a name — Eluma, the daughter of Remac — and gives her a sustained voice. Rabbinic tradition (*Bava Batra* 91a) names her Hazzelelponi or Zelelponi (cf. 1 Chronicles 4:3), a name preserved in some later Jewish exegesis.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Judges 13 is genre-specifically an annunciation type-scene with an embedded theophany. Robert Alter, Meir Sternberg, and Cheryl Exum have all analyzed it as exemplary of how the Hebrew narrator deploys conventional patterns and breaks them at significant points. The chapter's most striking literary device is the inversion of expected gender roles: the woman receives the revelation first, the woman correctly interprets it, the woman names the child, the woman corrects her husband's theological panic at the end. Mieke Bal calls this the chapter's "dissymmetry" — the man asks the questions, the woman has the answers, but only the man's questions are recorded with full conventional weight. Cheryl Exum, in her essays on the Samson cycle, has argued that the chapter's gender inversion is part of a sustained critique of Manoah's slowness — the husband cannot perceive what the wife sees.

The wordplay is dense. The angel's name is *peliʾ* — "wonderful" — and at 13:19 the narrator describes God as "the one who works wonders" (*maflʾi laʿasot*, מַפְלִיא לַעֲשׂוֹת). The chapter is thus framed by the same root *p-l-ʾ* used at the beginning and end. Robert Alter notes the alliterative pattern in the Hebrew. The repeated verb of perception — the wife's "I did not ask" (13:6), Manoah's "Now when your words come true" (13:12), the parenthetical "for Manoah did not know" (13:16), and finally "then Manoah knew" (13:21) — tracks the husband's slow movement toward the recognition the wife had already achieved.

The chapter's place in the book's argument is as the formal opening of the Samson cycle (chapters 13–16). Many scholars (Boling in his Anchor Bible commentary, Soggin, Niditch, Mobley) treat the Samson cycle as a relatively self-contained novella, possibly drawn from older oral folkloric traditions and incorporated into the deuteronomistic framework relatively late. The opening verse of the chapter (13:1) is in the standard deuteronomistic cycle formula ("the people of Israel again did what was evil in the sight of the LORD"), but the rest of the chapter has the literary feel of a folktale opening — barren wife, mysterious visitor, promise of a hero. Susan Niditch (*Folklore and the Hebrew Bible*) and Gregory Mobley (*The Empty Men: The Heroic Tradition of Ancient Israel*) have developed extensive arguments that the Samson cycle is the Hebrew Bible's most folkloric extended narrative, with parallels to Mediterranean and Near Eastern hero-traditions including Heracles, Enkidu, and the Aqhat epic of Ugarit.

Textual variants in Judges 13 are less dramatic than in the later Samson chapters but still significant. The LXX A and B recensions diverge in the spelling of Mahaneh-dan and in some details of the angel's instructions. The Vulgate's "Wonderful" at 13:18 (*mirabile*) became a major patristic prooftext for the angel's identification with the pre-incarnate Christ. The Old Greek of 13:25, "the Spirit began to stir him," uses a verb (*archomai*) that the LXX translator chose for its inceptive sense, emphasizing the beginning of Samson's career.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc of Judges 13 ties annunciation to vocation. Samson's birth narrative shares conventional shape with Isaac, Samuel, John the Baptist, and Jesus, placing him in a clear typological lineage of divinely promised deliverers. Yet the chapter's treatment of his future deliverance is significantly muted: the angel says only that "he shall begin to save Israel from the hand of the Philistines" (13:5), with the verb *yaḥel* (יָחֵל), "shall begin." Samson is not promised completion of the deliverance; he is promised inauguration. The reader is told from the start that this hero will not finish what he starts.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Pseudo-Philo (*LAB* 42–43) provides one of the most sustained early Jewish retellings, naming Manoah's wife Eluma and giving her a long monologue in which she debates with her husband about which of them is the cause of their barrenness. The retelling foregrounds the wife's spiritual perception in even sharper terms than the biblical text. Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.8.2–4) treats the annunciation more or less straightforwardly, but adds a remarkable detail: he records that Manoah was so jealous of the angelic visitations that he questioned his wife's chastity, a midrashic embellishment that survives in some rabbinic sources. The Talmud (*Sotah* 9b–10a) develops a sustained Samson aggadah, treating his birth as marking the beginning of the end of the period of judges and his failure to complete the deliverance as a typological pattern repeated in later Israelite history. Rashi reads the angel's identity as fully divine in function but creaturely in being, a position consistent with later Jewish theological reluctance to identify the *malʾakh YHWH* as God himself. Radak (Kimhi) develops the linguistic analysis of *peliʾ*. The medieval Jewish liturgical tradition does not commemorate Samson's birth in the way that Samuel's is commemorated, perhaps because of the eventual moral catastrophe of the Samson cycle; this is itself a striking absence. Maimonides' more general treatment of prophecy (*Guide* 2.32–48) provides the framework within which medieval Jewish readers understood the angelic appearances — as products of the prophetic imagination operating on an external reality.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** The patristic tradition embraced Judges 13 enthusiastically as a typological prefiguration of the Annunciation to Mary. Origen's homilies on Judges develop the parallel between Manoah's wife and Mary; the angel's name as "Wonderful" (Latin *mirabile*) was read against Isaiah 9:6 and treated as a Christological clue. Ambrose, Augustine, and Jerome all develop the typological reading. The medieval lectionary tradition placed Judges 13 in proximity to the Marian feasts, and the Glossa Ordinaria treats Manoah's wife as a type of Mary. The visual tradition (notably in medieval manuscript illumination and Renaissance painting — Rembrandt's "Manoah's Sacrifice" of 1641 is the most famous) consistently reads the chapter through Marian typology. The Reformation maintained the annunciation typology but read the chapter more cautiously, with Calvin in his *Sermons on Judges* emphasizing that Samson is no Christ and that the typological correspondence is partial. Critical scholarship has worked through the form-critical, source-critical, and tradition-historical questions: Wellhausen treated the chapter as a relatively late insertion, Boling and Soggin trace the underlying folkloric materials, and Block and Webb provide synchronic literary readings. Mieke Bal, Cheryl Exum, and Pamela Tamarkin Reis have developed the feminist analysis of the wife's role. Susan Niditch and Gregory Mobley situate the chapter in the broader Samson cycle as the formal opening of a folkloric novella. Hebrews 11:32 lists Samson among the heroes of faith, a verse that has shaped Christian reception decisively.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Both Jewish and Christian traditions treat the chapter as an annunciation narrative and recognize the wife's spiritual perception as the chapter's interpretive center. They diverge sharply on Christological typology: the Christian tradition reads "wonderful" against Isaiah 9 and treats the angel as a Christological figure, while the Jewish tradition reads the angel within the broader category of *malʾakh YHWH* without messianic content. They also diverge on the naming of Manoah's wife: the Pseudo-Philo tradition (Eluma) and the rabbinic tradition (Hazzelelponi) both supply names that the Christian tradition has largely ignored, perhaps because Christian Marian typology requires the wife's role to be representative rather than individuated.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The first tension is the identity of the angel. Is the *malʾakh YHWH* a created messenger distinct from God, or a manifestation of God himself, or — in the Christian reading — a pre-incarnate appearance of the Son? The chapter pushes hard in both directions. The angel speaks as God, accepts a burnt offering (13:19–20), bears a "wonderful" name that Manoah cannot grasp, and ascends in the flame. Manoah concludes "we have seen God" (13:22). Yet the narrator distinguishes "the angel of the LORD" from "the LORD" grammatically throughout. The patristic and medieval Christian traditions, developing the Christophany reading, treat figures like the Judges 13 angel as pre-incarnate appearances of the Son; this reading is sustained in conservative Reformed theology (Geerhardus Vos) and in some Catholic theology (the Christophany tradition), but rejected by other Catholic and Orthodox theologians who treat such figures as created angels speaking in God's authority. The Jewish tradition, particularly after the medieval period, consistently rejects identification of the *malʾakh YHWH* with God himself, treating the figure as a created messenger whose speech-acts represent God without ontological identity. Critical scholarship (von Rad, Westermann) treats the figure as a remnant of an earlier Israelite theophany tradition gradually abstracted into the more transcendent angelology of later Judaism. The Eastern Orthodox tradition, with its developed theology of theophany, treats such appearances as genuine but partial manifestations of the divine energies (in Palamas's vocabulary), distinguishing the divine essence (which remains hidden) from the divine energies (which are encountered). Where this leaves us: the chapter wants the reader to be uncertain about what exactly Manoah's wife saw — and that uncertainty is itself the theological content. The God of Israel can be encountered in the flame and named without being grasped.

Steelmanning the position I find least intuitive — the rabbinic position that the *malʾakh YHWH* is strictly a created messenger with no divine identity — requires recognizing that this position is doing important theological work. If the angel is God, then God can die, eat, be confused with men, and be reasoned with at the level of bargaining. The rabbinic move to abstract the angel as a representative figure preserves the otherness and singularity of God against the encroachments of polytheistic and incarnational thinking. Maimonides' theology of prophecy and his anti-anthropomorphic exegesis stand in this lineage. The Christian Christophany reading, by contrast, treats the very ambiguity that the rabbinic tradition wants to remove as the chapter's theological center. The two readings are not exegetically reconcilable; they are pursuing different theological projects. Where this leaves us: the chapter is a test case for the deepest theological question in both traditions — how can an absolutely transcendent God be encountered without ceasing to be transcendent — and the text refuses to answer the question for us.

The second tension is the moral status of Samson's lifelong vocation. The angel imposes a Nazirite vow on Samson before birth, and the rest of the cycle (chapters 14–16) records his serial violation of every element of that vow. Was the vow given to set up his failure, to provide a framework for his eventual recognition of failure, or as a gift he was free to accept or reject? Catholic moral theology has wrestled with this: Aquinas distinguishes between the divine permissive will and the divine antecedent will, arguing that God could foreknow Samson's failure without having caused it. Protestant Reformed theology, particularly in the Calvinist tradition, treats Samson as an example of the doctrine that God's purposes are accomplished even through the failures of his chosen instruments. Eastern Orthodoxy, with its more developed theology of human cooperation, would emphasize that the vow was a genuine offer and that Samson's failure was a real failure rather than a foreordained outcome. The Jewish tradition (Talmud *Sotah* 9b–10a) treats Samson's failure as a tragedy traceable to specific moral choices, particularly his fascination with Philistine women. Critical scholarship (Niditch, Mobley) emphasizes the folkloric inevitability — the hero must fail because the genre demands it — and treats the theological framework as secondary to the narrative pattern. Where this leaves us: the question of whether the divinely imposed vocation was a gift or a trap is the question the Samson cycle as a whole will pose, and the chapter's annunciation has already loaded the question into the narrative before Samson speaks a word.

The unresolved question that lands like a punch: what does it mean that the woman who saw the flame, who knew the angel was God, who corrected her husband's theological panic, and who named the child — is herself never named in the text that names everyone around her?

## The Thread

When you reach 1 Samuel 1, where Hannah prays at Shiloh and the priest Eli mistakes her devotion for drunkenness, remember Manoah's wife — the women who perceive what the men around them cannot. When you reach Luke 1, where the angel Gabriel appears first to Zechariah (who doubts) and then to Mary (who consents), remember the structural inversion of Judges 13 — the man's slowness, the woman's perception. When you reach Hebrews 11:32 listing Samson among the faithful, remember the chapter where the vow was placed on him before he could speak, and ask whether faith is a gift, a discipline, or a trap. And when you reach the silence at the end of Mark's gospel — "they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid" — remember Manoah saying "we shall surely die, for we have seen God," and his unnamed wife who answered him without flinching.
