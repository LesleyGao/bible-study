# Job 15: The East Wind in Your Belly

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Eliphaz the Temanite opens the second dialogue cycle in chapter 15, and his geographical identification anchors him in a specific historical imagination. Teman, mentioned thirty-two times in the Hebrew Bible, was a district or clan of Edom descended from Esau (Genesis 36:11, 15), located in the southern Transjordan. The region had a reputation for wisdom: Jeremiah 49:7 asks rhetorically, "Is wisdom no more in Teman?" and Obadiah 8–9 couples Temanites with Edomite wise men. When the book of Job introduces its three friends as coming from Teman (Eliphaz), Shuah (Bildad, a region associated with Abraham's concubine's sons, Genesis 25:2), and Naamah (Zophar, less certainly identified), the narrator is populating the dialogue with non-Israelite sages from the wider Semitic wisdom tradition. Eliphaz is not a fool. He speaks with the authority of a recognized tradition.

The question of when the book was written continues to shape how chapter 15 reads. If Pope (Anchor Bible, 1965) and Clines (WBC) are right that the Hebrew dialogue was composed in the post-exilic period, then Eliphaz's claims to traditional wisdom — "what wise men have told, without hiding it from their fathers, to whom alone the land was given, and no stranger passed among them" (15:18–19) — take on the added irony of a late author deploying ancient-sounding formulae to stage a conversation whose theological tensions were very much alive in his own fifth- or fourth-century context. Newsom reads the book as a literary staging of competing theological discourses in the exilic and post-exilic period, and chapter 15 represents the retributive orthodoxy that Deuteronomistic theology bequeathed to later Judaism — now stressed to breaking point. Gordis and Andersen, holding to an earlier dating, see Eliphaz as representing the older patriarchal or monarchic wisdom consensus that Job's author inherits and interrogates. Ezekiel 14:14 shows the Job tradition was already legendary by the sixth century BC.

The ancient Near Eastern analogues for chapter 15 are particularly instructive. The Babylonian Theodicy, a Kassite-era acrostic dialogue between a sufferer and his friend, stages exactly the kind of conversation Eliphaz conducts here: the friend defends the gods' justice with traditional pieties while the sufferer protests their opacity. *Ludlul Bel Nemeqi*, "I Will Praise the Lord of Wisdom," opens with the sufferer's bewilderment at being abandoned by Marduk despite his righteousness, and it is noteworthy how the speeches of Job's friends — including Eliphaz's in chapter 15 — rehearse arguments that Babylonian sages had already been arguing for centuries before. The Sumerian "Man and His God" text goes further back still. The point is that Eliphaz is not articulating specifically Israelite orthodoxy; he is speaking from a broader ancient Near Eastern consensus that the cosmos is morally ordered and that prosperity tracks virtue.

A particular archaeological note enriches chapter 15. The Ugaritic divine council texts from Ras Shamra, particularly in the Baal Cycle, feature assemblies of the gods (Hebrew sôd in 15:8) where decrees are issued. Eliphaz's sarcastic question to Job — "Have you listened in the council of God?" (ha-běsôd ʾělôah tišmāʿ) — directly invokes this Northwest Semitic conception of divine assembly. The irony is devastating: Job 1–2 has already shown the reader that Job is the *subject* of such a heavenly council, that the Satan and Yahweh have indeed been discussing him, but Job (and Eliphaz) do not know this. Eliphaz's rhetorical put-down is actually, from the reader's vantage, closer to the truth than he realizes. The text operates on dramatic irony of the highest order.

Key Hebrew terms shape the chapter. Rûaḥ qādîm (רוּחַ קָדִים, "east wind") in 15:2 is the desert sirocco, the hot dry wind that withers vegetation and, metaphorically, the speech of a bloviator who fills himself with what ought to be exhaled — an image of intellectual vanity turned self-destructive. Sôd (סוֹד, "council" or "secret counsel") in 15:8 names the heavenly deliberative body. Qědôšîm (קְדֹשִׁים, "holy ones") in 15:15 denotes the angelic beings of the divine council. Nitʿāb (נִתְעָב, "abominable") in 15:16 is strong cultic language, used elsewhere of idolatry and defilement. Reša`îm (רְשָׁעִים, "the wicked") recurs throughout 15:20–35 as the object-lesson, the anti-type of the pious sage.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 15 is the opening speech of the second round of the dialogues. The first round, chapters 4–14, followed a pattern: Eliphaz, Job, Bildad, Job, Zophar, Job. The second round (chapters 15–21) repeats the pattern but with marked escalation. Eliphaz in chapter 4 had been relatively gentle, even mystical, recalling his night vision. By chapter 15, the diplomatic veneer has cracked. Newsom and Alter both observe that the second cycle replaces wisdom's cautious comforts with direct accusation: Eliphaz no longer says "if you are wise" but moves straight to "your iniquity teaches your mouth" (15:5). Clines calls this the moment when classical retributive wisdom gets weaponized — the friends move from counselors to prosecutors.

The structure of chapter 15 unfolds in three main movements. Verses 1–16 form the rebuke: Job's speech is windy (verses 2–3), his theology is impious (verses 4–6), his claim to special knowledge is absurd (verses 7–10), his resistance to comfort betrays his real condition (verses 11–13), and humans simply are not pure (verses 14–16). Verses 17–19 transition via Eliphaz's appeal to ancient authority — "I will show you" — setting up an argument from tradition. Verses 20–35 comprise an extended anatomy of the wicked man's fate, a kind of wisdom-poem grafted into the dialogue, moving from the wicked's inner torment (verses 20–24) through his defiance (verses 25–28) to his loss of wealth and progeny (verses 29–35).

The chapter's Hebrew poetry displays standard parallelism throughout. Synonymous pairs dominate: "few of days" parallel to "full of trouble" in 14:1 is mirrored here by "windy knowledge" parallel to "east wind" in 15:2. Habel identifies a characteristic Joban technique in Eliphaz's speech — the rhetorical question cascade. Verses 7–9 deliver four consecutive questions, each tightening the screw: "Are you the first man born? Before the hills? Have you listened in the council of God? Do you limit wisdom to yourself? What do you know that we do not know?" The cascade functions as a kind of verbal assault, and the ironic effect — given the reader's knowledge of the heavenly council in chapter 1 — is that Eliphaz, sure that Job cannot have listened in the sôd, is himself deaf to it.

The chapter contains significant wordplay and intertextual echoes. The image of Job filling his belly with the east wind (15:2) inverts the proper direction of prophetic speech, in which the prophet receives wind/spirit (rûaḥ) from God and speaks it out; Job, on Eliphaz's telling, inhales empty wind and spews empty words. The phrase ʾādām yělûd ʾiššâ in 15:14 deliberately echoes Job's own phrase in 14:1, turning Job's lament about human frailty into Eliphaz's weapon against Job's pretense of righteousness: *you yourself said man is unclean, and now you claim purity?* Habel calls this Eliphaz's appropriation of Job's vocabulary, turning the sufferer's own words against him. The language of 15:15 ("God puts no trust in his holy ones, and the heavens are not pure in his sight") is recycled from Eliphaz's earlier night vision in 4:17–19, signaling that Eliphaz is returning to his own trusted material.

Legal and forensic vocabulary saturates the chapter. The rîb (רִיב, covenant lawsuit) form structures Eliphaz's attack: he functions as prosecutor, presenting Job's "own mouth" (15:6) as the chief witness against him. Verse 3, "Should he argue (yôkîaḥ) in unprofitable talk?" uses the hiphil of ykḥ, the standard verb for legal arraignment. The closing section on the wicked (15:20–35) reads almost as a jurisprudential brief: the catalog of fates is not random — it reads like a legal finding with evidence in each clause.

The imagery shifts from fire and darkness (verses 22–23, 30, 34) to botanical destruction in the final verses (29–33). The wicked's "branch" will not be green; he will shake off his "unripe grape" and "blossom" like a tree that miscarries its fruit. Alter notes the deliberate inversion of the tree-image from Job 14:7–9: there the tree had hope of revival; here the wicked man's botanical life is aborted and consumed. The structural chiasm across chapters 14–15 is deliberate — Job's hopeful tree answered by Eliphaz's blighted vine.

In textual terms, chapter 15 is relatively stable in the Masoretic tradition. The Septuagint abbreviates somewhat but preserves the main arguments. The 11QTargum Job fragment at Qumran contains pieces of the chapter in Aramaic and generally supports the MT. One notable variant: verse 23's "he wanders abroad for bread, saying, 'Where is it?'" has been read by some (following Dhorme) as a textual corruption with underlying meaning closer to "he is destined for the vultures' bread," though Clines defends the MT as it stands.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Chapter 15 has functioned in Jewish and Christian tradition as the classic statement of retributive orthodoxy — the theology that suffering follows sin — and also, increasingly, as the classic statement that such orthodoxy is inadequate to reality. Its reception history tracks the broader history of how the book of Job has been read.

In the Jewish tradition, the rabbis of Bava Batra 15a–16b recognized Eliphaz's speeches as articulating a view that the Tanakh itself qualifies. The Talmudic discussion never treats Eliphaz as simply wrong; rather, the rabbis weigh his words against Job's and against the divine speeches, and they acknowledge a genuine tension. Rashi on chapter 15 follows the traditional reading that Eliphaz has truth on his side regarding the general principle that the wicked suffer, but Rashi also notes that Eliphaz misapplies the principle to Job, who has not sinned in the way Eliphaz supposes. Ibn Ezra, with his philological sensitivity, focuses on the linguistic complexities of the chapter and is less invested in defending any particular theology. Maimonides in *Guide for the Perplexed* III.22–23 classifies Eliphaz as representing the traditional view of divine retribution — the view Maimonides associates with the masses and with the simple Deuteronomic schema. For Maimonides, none of the three friends articulates the highest philosophical truth, but Eliphaz's position is the most conventionally pious. Nachmanides treats Eliphaz more sympathetically, reading him as voicing a position that contains real truth, even if incomplete.

Modern Jewish readers intensify the critique. Martin Buber, in *The Prophetic Faith*, reads Eliphaz as the voice of impersonal theology — the kind of God-talk that fails to meet the other as other, that closes off the I-Thou relationship between God and sufferer. Jon Levenson treats Eliphaz's position as the theological default that Israel had inherited and that Job's author is deliberately dismantling. James Kugel emphasizes how Eliphaz's speeches expose the brittleness of retributive categories when pressed against real suffering. Avivah Zornberg reads chapter 15 as a moment of linguistic violence: Eliphaz tries to pin Job's mouth, to make his speech testify against him, and Job's counter-speeches in 16–17 are attempts to recover linguistic space. Nahum Sarna noted that Eliphaz's appeal to ancient tradition in 15:18–19 is itself a sign of inadequacy — Eliphaz must reach for the authority of the fathers because his argument cannot stand on its own.

The Christian trajectory has been mixed. Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job* devotes substantial treatment to chapter 15, reading Eliphaz's words at the literal level as containing partial truth but at the allegorical level as representing the incomplete wisdom of those who have not yet received the gospel. Gregory reads 15:14 ("What is man, that he can be pure?") as a genuine theological insight about creaturely sinfulness, even as he recognizes that Eliphaz's application to Job is wrong. Aquinas in his *Expositio super Iob* handles chapter 15 carefully: Eliphaz's general principles are largely sound in Aquinas's estimation, but he misapplies them because he lacks adequate knowledge of Job's case. Calvin's sermons on chapter 15 are particularly severe on Eliphaz: Calvin sees Eliphaz as speaking the truth about human sinfulness (especially in 15:14–16) while tragically failing to speak the gospel of grace that alone meets that condition. For Calvin, Eliphaz is orthodox without being merciful.

Chrysostom's homilies treat Eliphaz's speeches pastorally, using them to illustrate how friends can fail sufferers by substituting accurate doctrine for attentive love. Augustine draws on 15:14–16 in anti-Pelagian polemic alongside 14:4. Luther's reading of chapter 15 emphasizes the law-versus-gospel distinction: Eliphaz preaches law without gospel, accusation without promise. This Lutheran reading has shaped much subsequent Protestant preaching.

The critical scholarship of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries has tended to read chapter 15 sociologically and literarily. Wellhausen treated Eliphaz as representing the older wisdom tradition that the book of Job is interrogating. Gunkel saw the friends as representative types from ancient Near Eastern wisdom literature. Von Rad placed Eliphaz within the wisdom schools' confidence in a morally ordered cosmos. Clines's WBC reads chapter 15 as a marker of escalation: Eliphaz's tone hardens decisively, and the move from defense of tradition to accusation of the sufferer shows how retributive theology can turn cruel when confronted with counterexamples. Newsom's literary-theological reading identifies Eliphaz with a particular "moral imagination" — the worldview in which the cosmos is a stable system of reward and punishment — and reads the book as exposing the limits of that imagination. Habel notes how the chapter deploys classical wisdom forms (rhetorical questions, appeals to tradition, catalog of types) and thus makes Eliphaz speak in the most orthodox register possible, precisely so the reader can see the inadequacy of the orthodox register.

Liberation readings (Gutiérrez, *On Job: God-Talk and the Suffering of the Innocent*) treat Eliphaz as the voice of ideological self-protection: the privileged maintain the doctrine of retribution because it justifies their privilege and blames the poor for their poverty. Ellen Davis and Susannah Ticciati read chapter 15 within a theological framework that refuses to flatten the conflict between Eliphaz and Job — they hold that the book makes Eliphaz articulate a position that cannot be merely dismissed even as it is judged by the divine speeches. Hartley and Andersen, from more conservative positions, allow that Eliphaz says many true things while misapplying them to Job. Gordis treats Eliphaz as the sophisticated representative of traditional piety whose failure is pastoral, not doctrinal.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Is Eliphaz wrong in general or only in application?**

Job 42:7 has Yahweh rebuke Eliphaz with the words, "You have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has." This verdict hangs over the entire history of reading chapter 15.

- **Catholic**: The Thomistic reading allows that Eliphaz states genuine truths about divine justice and human sinfulness while drawing incorrect conclusions about Job's specific case. On this view, Eliphaz's doctrine is right in general terms; his diagnosis of Job is wrong. Aquinas would say Eliphaz's major premise is sound but his minor premise (that Job is wicked) is unjustified.
- **Protestant, Reformed**: Calvin reads Eliphaz as speaking law rightly but failing to speak gospel. The truth about human inability and creaturely sinfulness is real; the error is in thinking that Job's present suffering proves particular wickedness. Reformed readers often note that 15:14–16 articulates depravity accurately while 15:20–35 applies an inadequate theodicy.
- **Protestant, Evangelical**: Hartley and Andersen tend to read Eliphaz similarly — correct in his premises, wrong in his diagnosis.
- **Orthodox**: Reads Eliphaz as speaking partial truth that is completed only in Christ. The catalog of the wicked in 15:20–35 describes a real spiritual reality but is misapplied to Job.
- **Jewish**: Maimonides treats Eliphaz as representing one legitimate but incomplete theological position. Rashi allows Eliphaz's general truths while noting his misapplication. Buber and later Jewish thinkers read Eliphaz as articulating a position that the book systematically critiques.
- **Critical**: Newsom and Clines tend to read Eliphaz as representing a theological discourse (retributive orthodoxy) that the book as a whole calls into question. On this view, Yahweh's verdict in 42:7 is against the entire theological framework Eliphaz represents, not just his specific diagnosis of Job.

Steelman for the least intuitive reading: the critical position that Eliphaz is substantively wrong, not merely misapplied. Read carefully, Eliphaz's claim in 15:14–16 that *no human* can be righteous flattens the moral landscape in a way the book of Job itself will not. Job is called "blameless and upright" in 1:1 and 1:8 by the narrator and by Yahweh. The category of tām wĕyāšār (תָּם וְיָשָׁר) applies to Job in a way that Eliphaz's anthropology cannot accommodate. If Eliphaz is right that no one can be pure before God, then Yahweh's endorsement of Job's blamelessness is incoherent. The book thus suggests that Eliphaz's general principles, not only his application, are too totalizing. The retributive framework leaves no space for innocent suffering, but the frame narrative establishes innocent suffering as the starting premise. Eliphaz is wrong at the level of worldview.

**Tension Two: Is traditional wisdom trustworthy?**

Eliphaz appeals explicitly to inherited tradition in 15:17–19: "I will show you; hear me, and what I have seen I will declare (what wise men have told, without hiding it from their fathers, to whom alone the land was given, and no stranger passed among them)."

- **Catholic**: Tradition carries genuine authority, but tradition must be tested against revelation and against experience. Eliphaz's appeal is legitimate in form but inadequate in content because he has not integrated what revelation can show (which the reader has seen in chapters 1–2) and what experience shows (Job's actual innocence).
- **Protestant, Reformed**: *Sola Scriptura* radically relativizes tradition. Eliphaz's appeal to "what wise men have told" is precisely the kind of human tradition that must be tested against the divine word, and in Job's case it fails that test.
- **Protestant, Evangelical**: Tradition is valued as accumulated wisdom but subordinated to Scripture. Eliphaz's appeal is a cautionary tale about absolutizing inherited norms.
- **Orthodox**: Tradition (paradosis) is revelation's proper vehicle, but it must be the tradition of the Spirit-guided community, not merely the tradition of the ancestors. Eliphaz's appeal falls short because his tradition is generic Near Eastern wisdom, not the covenantal witness of Israel.
- **Jewish**: Traditional wisdom is honored but always contested within the ongoing midrashic conversation. The rabbis themselves would have recognized Eliphaz's appeal as a legitimate but not decisive move in a wisdom argument.
- **Critical**: Eliphaz's appeal to tradition is read as an ideological move — the conservative defense of inherited categories in the face of new evidence that calls them into question. Newsom treats it as Eliphaz's attempt to shore up a failing paradigm by invoking authority rather than engaging Job's specific case.

Steelman for the least intuitive reading: the conservative (Catholic/Orthodox/evangelical) position that tradition, properly used, is genuinely authoritative and that Eliphaz's error is not in appealing to tradition but in appealing to an inadequate tradition. Modern readers instinctively side with the individual voice (Job) against the traditional voice (Eliphaz), but the book is not simply on Job's side either — Yahweh's speeches in chapters 38–41 will humble Job too. The book's theology may be that tradition and experience both need to be held accountable to revelation, which in the book of Job comes from the whirlwind. Eliphaz's problem is not that he values tradition; it is that his tradition lacks the category of the divine freedom that the book ultimately insists on.

Where this leaves us: the cruelty of Eliphaz in chapter 15 is not the cruelty of a bad man but the cruelty of a good man with a theology that cannot accommodate his friend's reality. This may be the more disturbing finding. We are easily warned against villains. We are not easily warned against orthodox friends whose doctrine is correct in 80 percent of cases and who apply it with confidence to the 20 percent where it fails.

The punch-line question: if retributive wisdom is mostly right, when is it worth being catastrophically wrong to the friend in front of you?

## The Thread

- **Ezekiel 18** — The prophet's most sustained critique of inherited retribution ("the fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge"). Reading Ezekiel 18 against Eliphaz shows how the later prophetic tradition explicitly dismantles the generational-retributive framework Eliphaz still operates within.
- **John 9:2** — The disciples ask Jesus about the man born blind, "Who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" Jesus's response ("neither") is a direct rejection of the Eliphaz framework. The disciples of Jesus have inherited Eliphaz's categories and Jesus must teach them otherwise.
- **Luke 13:1–5** — Jesus's two examples (the Galileans whose blood Pilate mixed, the tower of Siloam) address the retributive logic head-on: "Do you think that these Galileans were worse sinners than all the other Galileans, because they suffered in this way? No, I tell you."
- **Job 42:7** — Yahweh's verdict on Eliphaz: "you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has." The most devastating judgment possible in the book's theology. The tradition that sounded orthodox turned out, on divine verdict, to be the falsehood.
