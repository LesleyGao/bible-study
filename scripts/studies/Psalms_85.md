# Psalm 85 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 85 is the second of the second Korahite cluster (Psalms 84-85, 87-88, with Psalm 86 inserted between as a Davidic interruption). The superscription reads *la-menatzeach li-vne-Qorach mizmor* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ לִבְנֵי־קֹרַח מִזְמוֹר) — "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of the Sons of Korah." Like Psalm 84, it is the work of the Levitical guild whose ancestor's rebellion (Num 16) was followed by a long history of liturgical service (1 Chr 9:19; 2 Chr 20:19). And like Psalm 84, it sits in **Book III** (Psalms 73-89), the book of crisis and restoration.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 85 as a **community lament** (*Volksklagelied*) with a striking shift in the second half toward a **prophetic-oracular response** — what some form-critics call a *Heilsorakel* or salvation oracle. Sigmund Mowinckel suggested a setting in temple liturgies of communal supplication: a national fast, a gathering after harvest failure or military defeat, a moment when the people called for divine mercy and a temple prophet (perhaps Levitical) responded with an oracle of peace. The psalm's three-part structure — past deliverance recalled (vv. 1-3), present petition (vv. 4-7), prophetic-oracular promise (vv. 8-13) — fits this liturgical drama.

The historical setting is contested. The opening verse, *ratzita YHWH artzekha shavta shevut Ya'akov* (רָצִיתָ יְהוָה אַרְצֶךָ שַׁבְתָּ שְׁבוּת יַעֲקֹב) — "LORD, you were favorable to your land; you restored the fortunes of Jacob" — uses the technical phrase *shuv shevut*, "restore the fortunes," which appears in Deuteronomy 30:3 and recurs in Jeremiah and Ezekiel as language for the return from exile. This makes a **post-exilic setting** (late 6th or 5th century BCE) the most defensible scholarly judgment: the psalm looks back on the return from Babylon as a real but partial restoration, and now prays for the deeper restoration the return did not yet bring. Hans-Joachim Kraus, John Goldingay, Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger all read it post-exilically. Some earlier commentators (and a strand of Jewish reception) place it in the time of Jehoshaphat or Hezekiah, but the *shuv shevut* phrase tilts toward exile-and-return as the framing memory.

Hebrew terms worth attending to. *Shavta shevut* (שַׁבְתָּ שְׁבוּת) in v. 1 is a paronomasia (root *shuv* doubled) — "you turned the turning" — language for total reversal of fortune. *Avon* (עָוֹן, "iniquity," v. 2) and *chatta'at* (חַטָּאת, "sin," v. 2) are paired with the verbs *nasata* (נָשָׂאתָ, "you bore/forgave") and *kissita* (כִּסִּיתָ, "you covered") — the standard Hebrew vocabulary of forgiveness as bearing-the-weight and covering-the-stain. Verse 4's petition *shuvenu Elohei yish'enu* (שׁוּבֵנוּ אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׁעֵנוּ) — "restore us again, O God of our salvation" — uses the same root *shuv* that v. 1 used for the past restoration; the psalm's argument is that the God who turned-toward must turn-toward again. The famous line of v. 10 — *chesed ve-emet nifgashu tzedeq ve-shalom nashaqu* (חֶסֶד־וֶאֱמֶת נִפְגָּשׁוּ צֶדֶק וְשָׁלוֹם נָשָׁקוּ) — "steadfast love and faithfulness meet; righteousness and peace kiss each other" — uses the verbs *paga'* (in the niphal *nifgashu*, "to meet, encounter") and *nashaq* ("to kiss"); it is the most extraordinary personification of divine attributes in the Psalter. The four nouns are *chesed* (steadfast covenant love), *emet* (truth/faithfulness), *tzedeq* (righteousness), and *shalom* (peace/wholeness).

The Septuagint (Psalm 84 LXX) preserves the personification with high fidelity: ἔλεος καὶ ἀλήθεια συνήντησαν, δικαιοσύνη καὶ εἰρήνη κατεφίλησαν — translating *chesed* with ἔλεος (mercy) and *nashaq* with the intensified κατεφίλησαν (kissed warmly). This LXX rendering — "mercy and truth meet, righteousness and peace kiss" — is the seedbed of the Latin tradition's **misericordia et veritas obviaverunt sibi, iustitia et pax osculatae sunt** (Vulgate Ps 84:11), which fed an entire iconographic tradition.

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of Psalm 85 in 4QPs<sup>e</sup> and possibly 11QPs<sup>d</sup>; the text is broadly stable. ANE parallels for the form include Mesopotamian *šigû* and *eršahungal* (lament) prayers and the Hittite plague prayers of Mursili II, in which a national disaster is met with a recital of past divine favor and a petition for renewed mercy. The Israelite version is distinctive in that the response in vv. 8-13 is not a private divine word but a **public oracle of peace** that the singer reports having heard — *eshme'ah mah yedabber ha-El YHWH* (אֶשְׁמְעָה מַה־יְדַבֵּר הָאֵל יְהוָה), "Let me hear what God the LORD will speak," v. 8.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Psalm 85's architecture is **tripartite**, with each section roughly equal: vv. 1-3 (past — three verses), vv. 4-7 (present — four verses), vv. 8-13 (future — six verses). The proportions tilt forward: the past is shorter than the present, and the present is shorter than the promise. The structural movement is itself an argument — the future is not just longer in verses but heavier in weight.

Lowth's three categories of parallelism shape the poetry. **Synonymous parallelism** opens each strophe: "you were favorable to your land; / you restored the fortunes of Jacob" (v. 1); "restore us again, O God of our salvation, / and put away your indignation toward us" (v. 4). Each pair states the same thing twice, intensifying. **Antithetic parallelism** structures the contrast across vv. 1-3 (you forgave, you covered, you withdrew) versus vv. 4-5 (will you be angry, will you prolong) — the past benediction set against the present complaint. **Synthetic parallelism** drives the climactic vv. 10-13, where each new line adds another image: meeting, kissing, springing up, looking down, yielding, going before.

The psalm uses a sophisticated **chiastic** organization in its final movement. Verse 10's two cola pair *chesed* with *emet* and *tzedeq* with *shalom*; verses 11-12 then redistribute the four attributes vertically — *emet* springs up from the ground (v. 11a), *tzedeq* looks down from the sky (v. 11b), *YHWH gives the good* (v. 12a), and *the land yields its increase* (v. 12b). The vertical axis (sky and ground) maps onto the divine and human poles, and the encounter at v. 10 is precisely the meeting of the vertical at the horizontal — heaven and earth touching, divine attributes embracing. Verse 13 closes with a procession: *tzedeq lefanav yehallekh ve-yasem la-derekh pe'amav* (צֶדֶק לְפָנָיו יְהַלֵּךְ וְיָשֵׂם לַדֶּרֶךְ פְּעָמָיו) — "righteousness will go before him and make his footsteps a way." Righteousness is now personified as a herald walking ahead of YHWH and clearing his path.

The most distinctive literary feature is the **personification** of v. 10 — the four divine attributes treated as agents who meet and kiss. The Hebrew is muscular: *paga'* in the niphal carries the connotation of meeting unexpectedly, even of meeting in struggle (cf. Gen 32:1, Jacob's meeting with the angels); *nashaq* is the verb of greeting, of family reunion, of covenant kiss. The two pairs — *chesed/emet* and *tzedeq/shalom* — are themselves classic Hebrew word-pairs (Exod 34:6; Ps 89:14), and bringing them together in a single image is a moment of theological compression unmatched elsewhere in the Psalter.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

In the canonical shape of the Psalter, Psalm 85 sits in the middle of Book III's crisis sequence. Gerald Wilson read Book III as the book that asks whether the covenant has failed; Psalm 85's answer is that the *shuv shevut* of the past warrants the *shuvenu* of the present — the God who restored once will restore again. The psalm reads as a measured response to the despair that surfaces in Psalm 88 (the darkest psalm) and the lament over the broken Davidic covenant in Psalm 89.

In **Jewish reception**, Psalm 85's afterlife is rich. *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 85 reads the four attributes of v. 10 as a debate: when God was about to create humanity, *chesed* and *tzedeq* counseled in favor (humans will do acts of love and justice), and *emet* and *shalom* counseled against (humans will lie and quarrel) — only when the four were reconciled could humanity be created. This midrash, with parallels in *Genesis Rabbah* 8:5 and the Midrash on Psalm 85, was later picked up by Christian exegetes as the seedbed of the **four daughters of God** allegorical tradition. **Rashi** reads the past restoration as the return from Babylonian exile and the prayer for renewed restoration as the still-unfulfilled hope of full redemption. **Ibn Ezra** is more philological. **Radak** reads vv. 8-13 messianically — the days of the Messiah are the time when *chesed* and *emet* truly meet. In modern Jewish thought, **Martin Buber** in *I and Thou* draws on the meeting-imagery of v. 10 for his theology of encounter; **Abraham Joshua Heschel** in *The Prophets* and *God in Search of Man* treats the *attribute formula* tradition as foundational; **Jon Levenson** in *Sinai and Zion* reads Psalm 85 as a programmatic statement of the Hebrew Bible's ethical theology — the divine attributes are not abstract but performative; **Nahum Sarna** notes the agricultural framing (the land yielding its increase, v. 12) as evidence of a setting around the time of the autumn festival. **Avivah Zornberg** has written movingly on the *kiss* of righteousness and peace as an image of reconciliation under strain.

In **Christian reception**, Psalm 85 — and especially v. 10 — became the source of one of the most enduring Christian allegorical traditions: the **four daughters of God** (Latin: *quattuor filiae Dei*). The tradition begins in Latin with Hugh of St Victor and Bernard of Clairvaux (Bernard's *Sermo in festo Annuntiationis* on the four daughters is the great patristic-medieval treatment) and flowers in **Bonaventure**, in the *Speculum Humanae Salvationis* (an enormously influential 14th-century typological compendium), in the *Ludus Coventriae* mystery plays, in Langland's *Piers Plowman*, and visually in countless medieval and Renaissance paintings. The four — *Misericordia*, *Veritas*, *Iustitia*, *Pax* — debate whether God should redeem humanity; their reconciliation in the Incarnation is the moment v. 10 is fulfilled. **Augustine's** *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 84 (LXX) reads the meeting and kiss as the meeting of divine and human in Christ. **Cassiodorus** follows. **Aquinas** on Psalm 85 reads v. 10 as a christological prophecy — the union of natures in Christ is the *meeting* of mercy and truth. **Luther** in his *Operationes in Psalmos* (1519-1521) and later lectures on Psalm 85 reads it as one of the great Advent texts: the prayer for restoration is the church's prayer for Christ's coming, and v. 10 is the gospel itself — *iustitia et pax osculatae sunt* in the cross. **Calvin** (1557 commentary) is more sober; he reads vv. 8-13 as the church's confidence in God's covenant promises rather than as direct christological prediction, but he too treats v. 10 with reverence as a statement of how the gospel reconciles the seemingly opposed attributes of God.

In the modern critical tradition: **Gunkel** classified the form, **Mowinckel** the cultic setting, **Westermann** the lament-and-praise structure, **Kraus** the temple liturgy, **Hossfeld-Zenger** the post-exilic redaction, **John Goldingay** the canonical-theological reading, **Marvin Tate** (WBC) the philological detail, **Gordon Wenham** (*Psalms as Torah*) the moral instruction. **Walter Brueggemann** treats Psalm 85 as a paradigmatic *psalm of new orientation* — the song of those who have been through disorientation and now articulate a renewed but transformed faith. **Patrick Miller** reads vv. 8-13 as one of the clearest articulations in the Hebrew Bible of *shalom* as comprehensive flourishing — economic, ecological, and theological. **William Brown** in *Seeing the Psalms* attends to the agricultural metaphors. **Beth Tanner** in the NICOT replacement volume notes the gendered grammar of the four attributes (all four nouns are feminine in Hebrew) — they are sisters meeting.

In the **New Testament**, Psalm 85 is not directly quoted, but its theological grammar runs deep. The *Magnificat* (Luke 1:46-55) and the *Benedictus* (Luke 1:68-79) both echo Psalm 85's structure of recalling God's past mercy and announcing its renewed enactment. Zechariah's prophecy in Luke 1:78-79 — "the sunrise from on high will visit us, to give light to those who sit in darkness… to guide our feet into the way of peace" — picks up Psalm 85:13's procession-imagery, with *the way of peace* directly echoing the *shalom* of Psalm 85. Paul's vision in Romans 5:1, "we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ," and his teaching in Ephesians 2:14-17 that Christ is "our peace" who reconciles by his cross, are read by the entire Christian tradition (Augustine, Aquinas, Luther, Calvin) as the New Testament unfolding of Psalm 85:10.

In **liturgical use**, Psalm 85 is one of the great **Advent psalms** — appointed in the Roman Catholic *Liturgy of the Hours* and *Lectionary* for the Second Sunday of Advent (Year B), in the Anglican *Book of Common Prayer*, and across Reformed psalters. The *Rorate caeli* tradition and the great Advent antiphons of the Western tradition draw on Psalm 85's longing-and-promise structure.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

Two tensions are worth holding.

**Tension 1: The four daughters of God — christological allegory or restoration poetry?** The Christian tradition's reading of v. 10 as a personification of the four divine attributes meeting in Christ is one of the great achievements of Western typology. But it is also clearly an *imposition* on the Hebrew text, which is doing something more modest and more concrete — celebrating the restoration of the land in agricultural and covenantal terms. **Position A (the patristic-medieval allegorical reading — Augustine, Bernard, Aquinas, Luther):** v. 10 is a christological prophecy; the meeting of *chesed* and *emet* and the kiss of *tzedeq* and *shalom* is fulfilled in the Incarnation, where divine mercy and divine truth are reconciled in the person of Christ. The reading is not arbitrary; it draws on the canonical trajectory and the rabbinic precedent (the midrash of the four attributes debating creation). **Position B (the original-restoration reading — most modern critical scholars):** v. 10 is a poem about Israel's restoration after exile. The four attributes are not yet personified in the medieval sense; they are paired classical Hebrew word-pairs whose *meeting* is the public flourishing of a forgiven people. The land yields its increase (v. 12) because the covenant has been restored. **Position C (the both-and reading — Brueggemann, Goldingay, much of contemporary theological exegesis):** the psalm's restoration-poetry is itself the seedbed of the christological reading. The four attributes meeting *as forgiveness becomes harvest* is a structure that Christ fulfills without violating; the medieval allegory is an *extension*, not a replacement, of the original.

The steelman of Position A is two thousand years of homiletic and pastoral fruit — including the reading of v. 10 in Advent, where the meeting of mercy and truth is *exactly* what Christmas is. The steelman of Position B is hermeneutical honesty — the psalm in its original context is not predicting the Incarnation; it is praying for harvest after exile. The most defensible synthesis is Position C: the reason v. 10 *can* be read christologically is that the original poem already understood divine attributes as performative agents whose meeting changes the world. Christmas does not replace the harvest; it discloses what the harvest was always about.

**Tension 2: The oracle of peace — promise or warning?** Verse 8 contains a striking aside: "Let me hear what God the LORD will speak, for he will speak peace to his people, to his saints; *but let them not turn back to folly*." The closing clause (Hebrew *ve-al yashuvu le-kislah*, וְאַל־יָשׁוּבוּ לְכִסְלָה) is a sober qualifier — the promise of peace is not unconditional. **Position A (the unconditional reading):** vv. 8-13 are a pure oracle of grace; the warning of v. 8b is a parenthetical aside that does not constrain the magnificent promise of vv. 9-13. **Position B (the conditional reading):** the promise is real but not automatic; the people's *return* to folly would forfeit the *return* of God's favor. The repeated *shuv* root in the psalm — six occurrences in various forms — sets up the symmetry: God turned (v. 1), put away wrath (v. 3), is asked to turn again (v. 4), and the people are warned not to turn back (v. 8). **Position C (the eschatological reading):** the warning is real for the present generation but the promise of vv. 9-13 carries an eschatological surplus that exceeds present conditions; the messianic harvest of v. 12 is not finally contingent on Israel's behavior because it depends on the divine attributes themselves.

The steelman of Position B: the Hebrew Bible repeatedly conditions covenant blessing on covenant faithfulness, and Psalm 85 is no exception; reading the warning as parenthetical risks cheap grace. The steelman of Position C: the prophetic tradition holds together both the warning and the surplus — Israel can fail and Israel will be redeemed; the *both* is the heart of the prophetic message.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 85 is the prayer of those who remember being forgiven and who need to be forgiven again. Its theological achievement is the line in v. 10 — the personification of mercy and truth meeting, of righteousness and peace kissing — but the line is not abstract metaphysics. It is the agricultural reality that comes when a forgiven people are restored to a land and the land yields its increase. The sky and the ground are reconciled because the people and God are reconciled. Christian readers have heard in v. 10 the prophecy of Christmas, and they have heard rightly — but they have heard rightly only because the original poem already understood that divine attributes are not abstract. Mercy is performed when the harvest comes in. Truth is performed when the wronged are vindicated. Righteousness and peace kiss when the wars stop and the fields ripen. The psalm prays for that meeting, and trusts that it has come and will come.

**Closing question:** In your own life, where would you most need *chesed* and *emet* to meet and *tzedeq* and *shalom* to kiss — what reconciliation, harvest, or restoration are you longing for that would be itself the gospel made local?
