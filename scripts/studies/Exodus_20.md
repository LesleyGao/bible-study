# Exodus 20: Ten Words

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Exodus 20 contains the Decalogue, the single most culturally influential text in the history of Western law. Its historical horizon is the late second millennium for those who accept a Ramesside dating of the exodus (Kitchen, Hoffmeier, Propp), and the exilic or post-exilic period for those who accept a late compositional model (Wellhausen, Dozeman, Kratz). No topic in biblical studies has a wider compositional date-range in contemporary scholarship, in part because the Decalogue's apodictic form lacks the precise markers that allow ANE legal collections to be dated narrowly.

The material-cultural context is ANE law. The Decalogue stands in a tradition of legal collections stretching from the third millennium to the first: the *Laws of Ur-Nammu* (c. 2100 BC), the *Laws of Lipit-Ishtar* (c. 1930 BC), the *Laws of Eshnunna* (c. 1770 BC), the *Code of Hammurabi* (c. 1750 BC, 282 laws on a diorite stela now in the Louvre), the *Hittite Laws* (14th–13th c. BC), the *Middle Assyrian Laws* (c. 1076 BC), and the *Neo-Babylonian Laws* (c. 700 BC). The *Code of Hammurabi* is the most famous and the nearest formal analogue, but its laws are almost exclusively casuistic — "if a man does X, then Y shall be done." The Decalogue is distinctive in its apodictic form: the unconditional prohibition ("you shall not"). Albrecht Alt's foundational 1934 essay *The Origins of Israelite Law* proposed the apodictic/casuistic distinction and argued that the apodictic form is peculiarly Israelite, reflecting the covenantal context of direct divine speech. Moshe Weinfeld's *Deuteronomy* and *The Place of the Law in the Religion of Ancient Israel* refined and partly challenged the thesis; apodictic forms do exist in Hittite instruction texts and in some loyalty oaths. But the concentration of apodictic commands without casuistic qualification remains distinctive of the Decalogue.

The treaty context (Hittite second-millennium, or Neo-Assyrian first-millennium) shapes the Decalogue's framing. The self-identification "I am YHWH your God who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery" (v. 2) is not a command but a preamble-and-historical-prologue in treaty form (Mendenhall, Kline, Kitchen). The commands that follow function as the stipulations of the covenant. This is why Jewish numbering counts v. 2 as the first commandment — it is the foundational declaration of relationship on which the rest depend.

Archaeological evidence for Decalogue-like material in ancient Israel is thin but not absent. The *Ketef Hinnom* silver amulets (late seventh century BC, discovered 1979, published by Gabriel Barkay) preserve the Aaronic blessing but imply a priestly-liturgical culture in which covenantal formulas circulated. The *Nash Papyrus* (c. 150–100 BC, held at Cambridge University Library), discovered in Egypt in 1898, preserves a Hebrew Decalogue combined with the Shema (Deut 6:4–5). Its Decalogue reading is a hybrid of Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5, with the order of the adultery, murder, and theft commandments matching LXX Codex Vaticanus (adultery-theft-murder) rather than MT. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve additional Decalogue variants: 4QDeut-n combines Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5; 4QDeut-j contains phylactery-type texts; *4QpaleoExod-m* is a Samaritan-like Exodus with expansive readings. The rabbinic phylacteries (*tefillin*) discovered at Qumran show that the Decalogue was included in tefillin until the Tannaitic period, when (according to Bavli Berakhot 12a) the sages removed it "because of the murmuring of the heretics" — probably Jewish-Christians who argued that only the Decalogue, not the full Torah, was binding.

On authorship and dating, the Decalogue is the hardest test of source-critical method. Wellhausen and the classical JEDP reconstruction placed the Exodus 20 Decalogue in an Elohistic or early J core, edited by a Deuteronomistic hand. Martin Noth in *Exodus* treated it as a pre-literary tradition later inserted. Erhard Gerstenberger's *Wesen und Herkunft des "apodiktischen Rechts"* argued for a clan-ethical setting of origin. Frank Crüsemann's *The Torah: Theology and Social History of Old Testament Law* treats it as a late-monarchic composition with much older building blocks. David Flusser and Yair Zakovitch have emphasized its liturgical role. Brevard Childs's *Exodus* resisted premature compositional judgment and focused on canonical form. William Propp's Anchor Bible provides the most sustained recent philological treatment. Benjamin Sommer's *Revelation and Authority* argues for a participatory theology of revelation that accommodates both divine origin and human editorial work.

Key Hebrew vocabulary:
- **עֲשֶׂרֶת הַדְּבָרִים** (*ʿăśeret haddĕbārîm*) — "ten words," the term used in Ex 34:28 and Deut 4:13; hence the Greek δεκάλογος (Decalogue).
- **לֹא תִרְצָח** (*lōʾ tirṣāḥ*) — "you shall not murder." *Rāṣaḥ*, not *hārag* or *qāṭal*; the verb excludes most categories of judicial, defensive, and wartime killing in Jewish and Christian exegesis.
- **לֹא תַחְמֹד** (*lōʾ taḥmōd*) — "you shall not covet." *Ḥāmad* often implies intent toward acquisition, not mere desire.
- **זָכוֹר אֶת־יוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת** (*zākôr ʾet-yôm haššabbāt*) — "remember the Sabbath day." Deut 5:12 has *šāmôr* ("observe"), a variant that rabbinic tradition (Bavli Shavuot 20b) resolved by saying both words were spoken simultaneously.
- **פֶּסֶל** (*pesel*) — a carved cultic image, specifically.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The Decalogue is apodictic, compressed, and structured. The ten commands (however they are counted) divide naturally into a God-ward table (vv. 2–11) and a neighbor-ward table (vv. 12–17), and most traditions have assumed this bipartite structure even while disagreeing about the internal enumeration.

Enumeration is the classical interpretive problem. There are ten "words" but the verses are ambiguous about where one word ends and the next begins. Three major schemes divide the text:

- **Jewish:** (1) v. 2 ("I am YHWH… who brought you out"); (2) vv. 3–6 (no other gods + no images, understood as one composite command against idolatry); (3) v. 7 (name); (4) vv. 8–11 (Sabbath); (5) v. 12 (parents); (6) v. 13 (murder); (7) v. 14 (adultery); (8) v. 15 (theft); (9) v. 16 (false witness); (10) v. 17 (coveting).
- **Catholic and Lutheran (following Augustine in *Questions on the Heptateuch*):** (1) vv. 2–6 (God and images as one); (2) v. 7 (name); (3) vv. 8–11 (Sabbath); (4) v. 12 (parents); (5) v. 13 (murder); (6) v. 14 (adultery); (7) v. 15 (theft); (8) v. 16 (false witness); (9) v. 17a (neighbor's wife); (10) v. 17b (neighbor's goods).
- **Reformed / Anglican (Philonic and early Greek Fathers):** (1) v. 3 (no other gods); (2) vv. 4–6 (no images as separate command); (3) v. 7 (name); (4) vv. 8–11 (Sabbath); (5) v. 12 (parents); (6) v. 13 (murder); (7) v. 14 (adultery); (8) v. 15 (theft); (9) v. 16 (false witness); (10) v. 17 (coveting, as one).

Each scheme is theologically motivated. The Jewish reading foregrounds v. 2 as the foundational declaration. The Catholic-Lutheran reading combines the image prohibition with the first commandment to avoid suggesting a separate iconoclasm, and splits coveting into two distinct commands (an exegetical move that treats "neighbor's wife" as distinct from property — relevant to later theology of marriage and chastity). The Reformed reading maintains the image prohibition as a separate second commandment, making iconoclasm a distinct covenantal obligation. These are not neutral tallies; they are sites where theological commitments shape the reading of the text.

Literary devices in the Decalogue include the shift from first-person divine speech to the people's third-person reference. The chapter opens with "God spoke all these words, saying, 'I am YHWH your God…'" — direct first-person speech through v. 6 — then gradually moves toward the third person ("for YHWH will not hold him guiltless," v. 7; "for in six days YHWH made," v. 11). Jewish tradition in Bavli Makkot 24a and Rashi on v. 1 argues that only the first two commandments were heard directly; the rest came through Moses. The shift from first to third person gives some textual support to this reading, though it is contested.

The Sabbath command is anomalously long (vv. 8–11), with both a positive form ("remember") and an extensive rationale ("for in six days YHWH made…"). Deuteronomy 5:12–15 gives the same command with a different rationale (Egyptian bondage remembered). The double rationale has generated substantial exegesis: why does the Exodus version ground Sabbath in creation and the Deuteronomy version ground it in redemption? Heschel's *The Sabbath* treats the two rationales as complementary: creation and liberation, cosmos and history. Rashi harmonizes by saying God spoke both rationales simultaneously. Critical scholarship (Weinfeld) sees evidence of two compositional strata.

Textual variants. The Samaritan Pentateuch adds a commandment after v. 17 requiring an altar on Mount Gerizim, making the tenth commandment a sectarian claim. The LXX order in some manuscripts (Codex Vaticanus) places adultery before murder and theft, matching the Nash Papyrus and cited by Jesus in Luke 18:20 and by Paul in Rom 13:9 (in some manuscripts). The order matters for doctrine: the traditional MT order foregrounds murder as the first social prohibition, while the LXX order foregrounds adultery. Paul's occasional citation of the LXX order is one of many pieces of evidence that the NT writers read a Greek OT.

The response of the people (vv. 18–21) is literary inclusio with Exodus 19's theophany. The people see and tremble; they request Moses's mediation. This is the formal establishment of prophetic mediation: the people choose to have the law communicated through Moses. The Decalogue is heard directly (on one rabbinic reading, only the first two commandments), and the rest of Sinai legislation comes through Moses.

The altar laws (vv. 22–26) follow the Decalogue as a first concretization. They are unusually archaic: an altar of earth or unhewn stone, no steps (to avoid exposure). This suggests a very old cultic text, predating the temple and the centralization of worship. Critical scholarship regards these verses as among the oldest cultic prescriptions in the Torah.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** The Decalogue is the center of Jewish covenantal memory. Second Temple treatments include Philo's *De Decalogo* — an entire treatise arguing that the Decalogue contains the general principles from which the specific laws derive — and his *De Specialibus Legibus* 1–4, which organizes the Mosaic legislation under each commandment. Josephus's *Antiquities* 3.89–101 narrates the Sinai event with philosophical gloss. *Pseudo-Philo's Biblical Antiquities* 11 expands imaginatively. The *Temple Scroll* (11Q19) and *4QReworked Pentateuch* rework the Decalogue material. Qumran tefillin (phylactery texts found in Caves 4 and 8) preserve Decalogue texts, showing the daily liturgical use of the commandments.

Rabbinic tradition devoted extraordinary attention to the Decalogue. The Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael *Bachodesh* is a sustained commentary on Exodus 20. The Mekhilta de-Rabbi Shimon bar Yohai offers parallel materials. Bavli Shabbat 86b–89a unfolds Sinai aggadot. Bavli Makkot 23b–24a derives the 613 commandments and treats the Decalogue as their summary. Bavli Kiddushin 31a treats the commandment to honor parents extensively. Philosophically, Saadia Gaon's *Beliefs and Opinions* treats the Decalogue as the ground of rational religion. Maimonides in *Sefer HaMitzvot* counts the commandments, and in *Hilchot Avodat Kokhavim* treats the prohibition of images exhaustively; in *Guide* 2.33 he argues that only the first two commandments were heard directly from God. Ramban contests Maimonides on the directness of the revelation. Rashi's commentary remains the standard Jewish entry point; Ibn Ezra offers grammatical and contextual readings; Ramban provides covenantal-theological synthesis. Judah Halevi's *Kuzari* makes the public Sinai theophany — and hence the Decalogue's public reception — the apologetic cornerstone of Jewish faith.

Modern Jewish treatments. Abraham Joshua Heschel's *The Sabbath* reads the fourth commandment as the fulcrum of Jewish spirituality: Sabbath is a "cathedral in time." Heschel's *God in Search of Man* places the Decalogue at the heart of prophetic consciousness. Martin Buber's *Moses* treats the commandments as "address," a personal call. Franz Rosenzweig's *The Star of Redemption* makes the Decalogue the textual voice of revelation. Nahum Sarna's JPS *Exodus* provides the standard contemporary scholarly Jewish treatment. Jacob Milgrom's essays on specific commandments, Moshe Greenberg's "Postulates of Biblical Criminal Law," and David Weiss Halivni's *Peshat and Derash* all contribute. Jonathan Sacks's *Covenant and Conversation* reads the Decalogue as a constitution of freedom — each command secures a dimension of human dignity.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** The Decalogue has been at the center of Christian theology and catechesis from the patristic period forward. Origen's *Homilies on Exodus* and *Contra Celsum* treat the commandments allegorically and morally. Augustine's *Sermons* and *On the Spirit and the Letter* develop the distinction between the Law's accusing function and the Spirit's enabling grace. Augustine's *Questions on the Heptateuch* proposed the enumeration that Catholic and Lutheran traditions follow. Gregory of Nyssa's *Life of Moses*, Chrysostom's homilies, and Ambrose's *On the Duties of Ministers* all engage the Decalogue as moral formation.

The medieval synthesis comes to rest in Aquinas. *Summa Theologiae* I-II q. 100 is an entire question on the Decalogue — its status as natural law, its promulgation, its binding force. Aquinas argues that the Decalogue's moral content is the revealed summary of natural law, known by all rational creatures, and binding always; the ceremonial and judicial precepts are given to Israel in a particular historical form and are fulfilled differently in Christ. This framework underwrites the Catholic Catechism's treatment (CCC 2052–2557), which organizes the third part of the catechism around the Decalogue, and Pope John Paul II's *Veritatis Splendor*, which reasserts the Decalogue as the natural law accessible to all peoples.

The Reformation produced the great catechetical Decalogue treatments. Martin Luther's *Large Catechism* (1529) devotes roughly a third of its content to the Decalogue — a pastoral masterpiece, still used liturgically in Lutheran churches. Luther's exposition reads each commandment in both its prohibitive and positive form ("You shall not kill" also requires that we help and befriend our neighbor in every bodily need). John Calvin's *Institutes of the Christian Religion* 2.8 is a sustained exposition that influenced every Reformed catechism afterward. Calvin formalized the three uses of the Law: civil (restraining evil in society), pedagogical (driving sinners to Christ), and normative (guiding the regenerate in godly life). Lutheran theology acknowledges the civil and pedagogical uses but debates the third use; the *Formula of Concord* Article VI (1577) affirmed it against a rigorist Lutheran minority. The Reformed Westminster Confession 19 canonizes the three uses. Anabaptist traditions, following the Sermon on the Mount's intensification, tend to limit the Decalogue's civil use and read the commandments through the lens of discipleship (Yoder, *The Politics of Jesus*; Hays, *The Moral Vision of the New Testament*).

Modern Christian treatments. Dietrich Bonhoeffer's *Ethics* and his lectures on the Decalogue in Finkenwalde recover the commandments as concrete ethical call. Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* II/2 and III/4 treat the Decalogue in his command-of-God framework. Brevard Childs's *Exodus* provides the standard canonical-critical reading. Walter Brueggemann, Terence Fretheim, and Christopher J. H. Wright all contribute missional and ethical readings. N. T. Wright's *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* and *The New Testament and the People of God* reread the Pauline relation to the Decalogue in the New Perspective frame, reducing the sharp Luther-to-Bultmann Law/gospel dichotomy.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Jewish tradition reads the Decalogue as the eternal constitution of Israel — each commandment unfolding into a web of rabbinic law that makes the covenant concrete. Catholic tradition reads it as the moral law, binding all humans as natural law, revealed definitively at Sinai, fulfilled in Christ, and reasserted in the church's moral teaching. Reformed tradition reads it as the moral law that abides for all Christians in its civil, pedagogical, and normative uses. Lutheran tradition reads it as primarily pedagogical, driving to Christ, with a third use debated and affirmed. Anabaptist tradition reads the Decalogue under the Sermon on the Mount, intensifying and relativizing it as appropriate. The convergence: virtually all major traditions treat the Decalogue's moral content as binding. The divergence: how the commandments relate to Christ, to the civil order, and to the ceremonial and judicial Torah.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The second commandment and religious imagery.** Does "you shall not make for yourself a carved image" prohibit all religious imagery, or only imagery intended as cultic objects of worship?

- **Catholic (Aquinas, ST II-II q. 94 a. 2; Council of Nicaea II, 787; CCC 2129–2132; *Veritatis Splendor*):** The commandment prohibits idolatry, not religious imagery as such. The incarnation of Christ changes the theological situation — God has made himself visible in human flesh, and images of Christ and the saints are therefore legitimate as aids to devotion, provided the worship (*latreia*) goes to God alone and images receive only veneration (*dulia*).
- **Reformed (Calvin, *Institutes* 1.11; Heidelberg Catechism Q. 96–98; Westminster Confession 21):** The commandment prohibits all religious images in worship, including images of Christ. The iconoclasm of the Reformed tradition is grounded in this reading. Calvin's argument is that images inevitably migrate from aids to objects of devotion and that God has communicated himself through Word, not image.
- **Lutheran (Luther, *Against the Heavenly Prophets*):** The commandment prohibits idolatrous use of images but not their possession or ancillary use. Luther argued forcefully against Karlstadt's iconoclasm and insisted that images could remain in churches as long as they were not worshipped.
- **Eastern Orthodox (John of Damascus, *On the Divine Images*; Theodore the Studite):** The incarnation authorizes icons and their veneration; the Seventh Ecumenical Council of 787 canonized the theology. The commandment prohibits idolatry, and icons are not idols because they point beyond themselves to their prototypes.
- **Anabaptist:** Generally iconoclastic, following the Reformed reading; the simplicity of worship reflects the commandment's full force.
- **Jewish (Maimonides, *Hilchot Avodat Kokhavim* ch. 1; Rashi):** The commandment prohibits all representations of God and by extension, for much of Jewish tradition, three-dimensional representations of any creature in contexts that might risk idolatry. The strictness varies across traditions.
- **Critical (Mettinger, *No Graven Image?*):** Israel's aniconism was a developing tradition, not uniform from Sinai. Archaeological evidence (the Taanach cult stand, the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions) suggests images were used in Israelite worship even during the monarchy.

*Steelman the Eastern Orthodox position.* The iconographic argument is stronger than Protestant readers often realize. John of Damascus's *On the Divine Images* and Theodore the Studite's *On the Holy Icons* argue from the incarnation: if God has taken on visible human flesh in Christ, then to forbid visible representation of Christ is tacitly to deny the incarnation. The pre-incarnational prohibition reflected the invisibility of God; the incarnation changes that fundamentally. This is not a workaround of the commandment but a theological reading of what the commandment prohibits (idolatry) vs. what it does not prohibit (truthful representation of what God has revealed).

**Where this leaves us:** the commandment prohibits idolatry; whether it also prohibits the use of images in worship depends on whether one reads the incarnation as modifying the pre-Christian prohibition. Catholic, Orthodox, and Lutheran traditions say yes; Reformed and Anabaptist traditions say no. The traditions have not converged, and probably cannot without one side revising core commitments.

**Tension 2: "You shall not murder" (v. 13) — how far does the prohibition extend?** Does *lōʾ tirṣāḥ* exclude capital punishment, just war, self-defense, and abortion, or only unjust private killing?

- **Catholic (Aquinas, ST II-II qq. 64–66; CCC 2258–2330; *Evangelium Vitae*):** The commandment prohibits unjust killing; capital punishment and just war are not categorically excluded, but the modern magisterium (John Paul II and Pope Francis) has narrowed the scope of permissible capital punishment almost to nothing. Direct abortion is always prohibited as the killing of innocent human life.
- **Reformed (Calvin, *Institutes* 4.20; Westminster Larger Catechism Q. 135–136):** The commandment prohibits unjust killing; the magistrate's use of the sword and the soldier's obedience in just war are legitimate. Westminster's exposition treats the commandment as requiring preservation of life wherever possible.
- **Lutheran (Luther, *Whether Soldiers Too Can Be Saved*):** Two-kingdoms framing: the commandment binds private individuals absolutely, but the magistrate's office can require the sword for the preservation of order.
- **Wesleyan (Wesley, sermons on Christian perfection):** The commandment extends into the attitudes of the heart — anger, hatred, contempt — following Matt 5:21–22.
- **Anabaptist (Yoder, *The Politics of Jesus*; Hays, *The Moral Vision of the New Testament*):** The Sermon on the Mount radicalizes the commandment into nonviolence; Christian discipleship excludes warfare and capital punishment. The state's sword is not the Christian's sword.
- **Jewish (Mekhilta on 20:13; Bavli Sanhedrin 74a on the three capital sins; Maimonides *Hilchot Rotzeach*):** The verb *rāṣaḥ* is technical; it excludes judicial execution, defensive killing, and war, but these are governed by their own extensive halakhic frameworks. The commandment is the root of a complex legal edifice, not a simple absolute.
- **Critical (Greenberg's "Postulates of Biblical Criminal Law"; Milgrom):** The commandment reflects a distinct ANE view — life is sacred because the human is made in the image of God (Gen 9:6) — and biblical law develops differentiated responses within that framework.

*Steelman the Anabaptist position.* Its argument is that the trajectory of the Decalogue is intensified by the Sermon on the Mount and the cross; Jesus's teaching on enemy-love and his refusal of violent defense at his arrest constitute a fulfillment that reorients the commandment away from any form of sanctioned killing. The steelman: traditions that permit capital punishment and war have to explain why Jesus's example and teaching should not be read as decisive for the Christian reading of the sixth commandment. Hays's argument in *Moral Vision* is that the pacifist reading is the honest reading of the NT data; the just-war and capital-punishment traditions are accommodations to political necessity, not direct readings of Christ.

**Where this leaves us:** the commandment certainly prohibits unjust killing. Whether it prohibits all killing depends on how the NT's intensification is read and how Christian ethics relates to the state. The traditions disagree, and the disagreement is irreducible absent agreement on prior questions about the relation of Christ's ethics to political order.

**Unresolved question:** If the Decalogue's ten commandments are the summary of moral law — as Aquinas and Calvin maintain — why is the Sabbath commandment (ceremonial in form, tied to specific day and specific rationale) included alongside commandments that are universally and obviously moral? The tradition has never settled whether the fourth commandment belongs to the moral law (with the first day of the week transposed to Sunday for Christians, per Reformed tradition) or to the ceremonial law (abrogated in Christ, per many Lutheran and most Anabaptist readings), and the unresolved status of Sabbath is a fault line in Christian practice.

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references anchor the Decalogue in the wider canon.

*First,* the altar law in v. 24 — "in every place where I cause my name to be remembered I will come to you and bless you" — is in tension with Deuteronomy's centralization of worship at "the place YHWH will choose" (Deut 12:5). The tension is foundational to the source-critical argument that Exodus's altar law is older than Deuteronomy's centralization and that the shift from local altars to a single sanctuary is a historical development reflected in the Pentateuch's compositional layers. Critical scholars (de Wette; Wellhausen) used this tension as a keystone of the documentary hypothesis. Theologically, the tension maps onto the church's later debate over the universal availability of worship vs. the liturgical concentration in appointed places.

*Second,* the Decalogue's prohibition of bearing false witness (v. 16) is the juridical foundation of the biblical concept of testimony. It underwrites the "two or three witnesses" requirement (Deut 19:15, cited in Matt 18:16; 2 Cor 13:1; 1 Tim 5:19; Heb 10:28), the narrative of Naboth's execution by false witnesses (1 Kings 21), and the Passion narrative's explicit references to false witnesses at Jesus's trial (Matt 26:59–60; Mark 14:56–57). The commandment thus becomes a hermeneutical key for reading the injustice of Jesus's condemnation: the court violated the ninth commandment to convict the true witness (John 18:37).

*Third,* the coveting prohibition (v. 17) is the commandment that explicitly regulates the inner life, not only outward action. Paul in Romans 7:7–13 identifies this commandment specifically as the one that awakened him to the depth of the law's demand: "I would not have known covetousness if the law had not said, 'You shall not covet.'" For Paul, the tenth commandment is the one that exposes that the Law reaches into the heart, and thereby drives the reader to recognize the inadequacy of external obedience. The commandment against coveting is the hinge between external and internal righteousness, a hinge that the Sermon on the Mount turns fully (Matt 5:21–30) and that will shape the Christian theology of indwelling sin.
