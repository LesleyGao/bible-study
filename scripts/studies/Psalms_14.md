# Psalm 14: The Practical Atheism of the Fool — and Its Doublet

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The superscription is the spare *lamenatzeach le-David* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ לְדָוִד), "to the choirmaster. Of David." The Davidic ascription places the psalm in Book I; the *menatzeach* signals professional choral performance. What makes Psalm 14 unusual in the Davidic collection is its existence as a near-doublet of Psalm 53. The two psalms preserve substantially the same poem with significant differences that have shaped scholarly understanding of how the Psalter was edited and compiled.

The most striking variation is the divine name. Psalm 14 uses *YHWH* (יְהוָה, "the LORD," the personal-covenantal divine name) four times (vv. 2, 4, 6, 7). Psalm 53, which sits in Book II within what scholars since the eighteenth century have called the "Elohistic Psalter" (Pss 42-83), uses *Elohim* (אֱלֹהִים, "God," the more generic divine name) throughout. Hossfeld and Zenger, building on a long line of editorial-criticism, argue that the Elohistic recension was a deliberate scribal substitution applied to a once-discrete collection of psalms, and the doublet with Ps 14 is a key piece of evidence: the same poem exists in two redactional environments, and the divine-name usage tracks the redactional context. Other variations include Ps 53:5's reading "for God has scattered the bones of him who encamps against you" — a more militarized vindication imagery absent from Ps 14:5-6's terse parallel.

Form-critically, Psalm 14 is a wisdom-lament, blending wisdom-tradition vocabulary (*naval*, the fool; the universal indictment of human corruption) with lament-tradition complaint about the wicked oppressing the poor and YHWH's people. Gunkel classified it under wisdom psalms with prophetic-lament admixture; Mowinckel located such hybrid psalms in cult-prophetic settings where wisdom teaching and intercessory lament converged. The poem's structure resists tidy form-critical categorization — it contains a divine-perspective scene (vv. 2-3, YHWH looking down from heaven), a complaint against evildoers (v. 4), an assurance of vindication (vv. 5-6), and a closing wish for Zion-restoration (v. 7). The closing v. 7 is widely thought to be a later editorial addition, perhaps post-exilic, since it presupposes a need for the "fortunes of Jacob" to be restored.

The Hebrew lexicon centers on *naval* (נָבָל) in v. 1 — the fool who says in his heart, *ein Elohim* (אֵין אֱלֹהִים, "there is no God"). The *naval* is a wisdom-tradition figure: not the theoretical atheist (the Hebrew Bible has no such figure; metaphysical denial of divine existence is not a category in Israelite thought) but the practical atheist — the person who lives as if God will not act, will not see, will not judge. The *naval*'s atheism is moral, not metaphysical: it is the conviction that the moral order is not enforced, that consequences do not follow actions, that the powerful can act with impunity. The *naval* root produces the proper name Nabal in 1 Sam 25, the foolish husband of Abigail; Nabal's name (which Abigail herself glosses with the proverb "as his name is, so is he") becomes the wisdom tradition's anchor for the broader figure.

Other key Hebrew terms: *hishchitu* (הִשְׁחִיתוּ, "they have become corrupt") in v. 1 echoes Gen 6:11-12's pre-flood corruption-language. *To'evah* (תּוֹעֵבָה, "abomination") in v. 1 is the cultic-ethical category for that which God finds repugnant. *Ein osei tov* (אֵין עֹשֵׂה טוֹב, "there is none who does good") is repeated for emphasis in vv. 1 and 3, with v. 3 adding *gam echad* (גַּם אֶחָד, "not even one"). *Sukal* / *maskil* (the verbal noun for "those who understand," from the root *sakal*) and *doresh* (דֹּרֵשׁ, "seeker") in v. 2 are wisdom-tradition vocabulary for the religious-ethical seeker. *Achlu ammi achlu lechem* (אָכְלוּ עַמִּי אָכְלוּ לֶחֶם, "they eat my people as they eat bread") in v. 4 deploys the cannibalism-trope familiar from prophetic indictments (Mic 3:3) for the rapacity of oppressive elites.

ANE parallels: the wisdom-tradition figure of the fool appears across the ancient Near East. Egyptian wisdom (Amenemope, the Instruction of Ptahhotep) features parallel "fool" types whose practical irreligion brings them to ruin. Mesopotamian wisdom (the *Counsels of Wisdom*, the *Babylonian Theodicy*) features comparable figures. The distinctive Israelite contribution is the binding of folly specifically to the denial of YHWH's active moral governance.

DSS evidence: Ps 14 is preserved in fragments without significant variants from MT. The LXX numbers it 13 (offset by one) and includes after v. 3 an extended catena of curses on the wicked drawn from Pss 5:9, 140:3, 10:7, Isa 59:7-8, and Ps 36:1 — the very catena Paul quotes in Rom 3:10-18. This LXX expansion is preserved in some Vulgate manuscripts and underlies the medieval Christian liturgical use of the psalm; the Hebrew tradition lacks the expansion.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The poem's structure is loose by the standards of the more tightly architected psalms in this cluster. Verses 1-3 articulate the universal indictment (the fool, the corruption of all). Verse 4 voices the complaint specifically against oppressors of YHWH's people. Verses 5-6 deliver the assurance of vindication. Verse 7 closes with the Zion-restoration wish. The breaks between sections are perceptible but not architecturally chiastic.

Hebrew parallelism throughout displays Berlin's repertoire of devices. Verse 1's parallelism is climactic-staircase: "the fool says... // they are corrupt, they do abominable deeds // there is none who does good." Each line escalates the diagnosis. Verse 2's parallelism is synonymous: "to see if there are any who understand // who seek after God" — with the second line specifying the first. Verse 3 combines synonymous and intensifying parallelism: "they have all turned aside // together they have become corrupt // there is none who does good // not even one." The fourfold escalation drives toward the absolute *gam echad*.

Verse 4's interrogative-indignation ("Have they no knowledge?") is a wisdom-tradition rhetorical question characteristic of prophetic indictment. Verse 5's *sham pachadu pachad* (שָׁם פָּחֲדוּ פָחַד, "there they are in great terror" — the cognate accusative intensifies: "they fear a great fear") deploys the wisdom-tradition trope of sudden divine reversal: the wicked who lived in apparent impunity are overtaken by terror in a moment.

The poem contains no acrostic, no *selah* in MT punctuation, no formal refrain. The literary signature is the doublet with Ps 53, which makes Ps 14 a privileged site for studying the redactional history of the Psalter.

Westermann's structural analysis: the wisdom-lament hybrid does not fully conform to the lament-form sequence, but the elements are recognizable. The complaint occupies vv. 1-4. The assurance (functioning analogously to the divine response in pure laments) appears in vv. 5-6. The closing v. 7 substitutes for the vow of praise with a wish for restoration.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Psalm 14 sits in Book I and closes the Pss 9-14 cluster. Hossfeld and Zenger see Ps 14 as the cluster's culminating reflection: after laments against personal enemies (Pss 9-10), trust amid threat (Ps 11), the corruption of speech (Ps 12), and the experience of divine silence (Ps 13), Ps 14 widens to a universal anthropological diagnosis. The cluster moves from individual to communal to universal, and Ps 14 is the universalizing capstone.

The Jewish trajectory of interpretation begins in DSS material that preserves the psalm consistent with MT. *Midrash Tehillim* on Ps 14 develops the *naval* figure with reference to Nabal in 1 Sam 25 and to other biblical fools, treating the psalm as a wisdom-tradition meditation on the moral pathology of those who deny God's active rule. Rashi reads the psalm with attention to the historical context of David's enemies and to the universal-anthropological dimension. Ibn Ezra emphasizes the literary structure and the rhetorical force of the universal indictment. Radak (Kimchi) reads the closing v. 7 as a messianic-eschatological wish: the salvation that will come out of Zion is the messianic restoration. Modern Jewish readings: Levenson treats the doublet phenomenon and the Elohistic Psalter as evidence for the editorial-redactional history of the Psalter; Sarna attends to the wisdom-prophetic hybrid character; Heschel's prophetic-protest framework illuminates v. 4's indictment of those who eat YHWH's people; Buber's distinction between religion and religiosity speaks to the *naval* as one whose religious form persists without religious substance.

In Jewish liturgy, Ps 14 (like its doublet Ps 53) is recited as part of the *tehillim* cycle and appears in some communities' weekly Psalter readings.

The Christian trajectory has been particularly shaped by the NT use of the psalm. Romans 3:10-12 quotes Ps 14:1-3 (in the LXX expanded form) at the climax of Paul's argument that "all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God." The Pauline reading universalizes the indictment: the *naval*'s practical atheism is not a special pathology of a few but the condition of fallen humanity. This Pauline-Augustinian reading has dominated Christian interpretation. Augustine's *Enarrationes* on Ps 13 (LXX = MT 14) develops the doctrine of original sin from this text; the universal corruption is the pre-baptismal condition of all humanity. Athanasius and Cassiodorus follow the Augustinian line. Theodore of Mopsuestia, more historically minded, reads the psalm in its Davidic-historical context and resists the universalizing-anthropological move.

Aquinas's *Postilla* integrates the Pauline-Augustinian reading into the Thomistic doctrine of grace and nature: the psalm describes humanity *in statu naturae lapsae* (in fallen nature), but grace restores the capacity for righteousness. Luther's *Operationes* deploys the psalm at the heart of his case for *simul iustus et peccator*: the universal indictment of vv. 1-3 names the believer's continuing condition apart from Christ. Calvin's 1557 commentary develops the total-depravity reading from this text as one of his anchor passages.

Critical scholarship has been preoccupied with the doublet phenomenon. Gunkel classified the psalm and its parallel as wisdom-lament hybrids. Mowinckel located the editorial activity behind the Elohistic Psalter in the cult. Westermann analyzed the form. Kraus's *Psalmen* commentary works through the parallels with Ps 53 in detail. Hossfeld and Zenger's *Hermeneia* commentary integrates the editorial-shaping argument: the doublet is evidence for the Elohistic recension and for the editorial layering of the Psalter. Goldingay reads the psalm with attention to both its wisdom diagnosis and its concrete prophetic indictment of oppression. Brueggemann places the psalm within his disorientation category and emphasizes the social-prophetic dimension of v. 4. Patrick Miller's work on the structural shape of the Psalter attends to Ps 14's role in the Pss 9-14 cluster. William Brown attends to the *naval* as a rhetorical-anthropological figure. Beth Tanner reads the psalm with attention to the political theology of v. 4's indictment of oppressors. Susan Gillingham traces the psalm's reception in Christian doctrine of sin.

NT use is foundational: Rom 3:10-12 quotes the psalm at a pivotal point in the argument; the LXX expansion underlies the broader catena of Rom 3:10-18.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The first tension is the relationship between the psalm's original this-worldly oppressor reading and the Pauline universal-sinfulness reading. Three positions map across the tradition.

The Pauline-Augustinian-Reformation reading (Augustine, Luther, Calvin, the Reformed and Lutheran confessional traditions) takes Rom 3:10-12 as the canonical key. Paul's reading is not a misappropriation but a Spirit-guided exegetical move that draws out the universal-anthropological implication latent in vv. 1-3. The "there is none who does good, not even one" of v. 3 names the universal condition of humanity apart from grace. This reading has dominated Western Christian interpretation for sixteen hundred years.

The historical-critical reading (Gunkel, Kraus, much modern academic Psalter scholarship) restores the original Davidic-context reading. The "fool" of v. 1 is not all humanity but a specific class of practical atheists — the corrupt elites of v. 4 who oppress YHWH's people. The hyperbolic universalism of vv. 1-3 is wisdom-tradition rhetorical exaggeration, not metaphysical anthropology. The psalm is a prophetic indictment of specific oppressors, not a doctrine of original sin.

The Jewish reading (medieval and modern) keeps the psalm closer to its concrete-historical sense while recognizing the wisdom-tradition's universalizing tendency. The *naval* is a real category — practical atheism is a real human possibility — but the indictment is not metaphysically determinative for all of humanity at all times. The closing v. 7's wish for restoration presupposes that there are still those who hope for and would receive the restoration, which sits uneasily with a strict total-depravity reading.

The steelman of the least-intuitive reading is the historical-critical: the Pauline reading, magnificent as it is, has obscured the psalm's specific prophetic edge. The psalm's most urgent claim is not that all humans are sinners but that the oppressors who eat up YHWH's people will be terrified by sudden divine reversal (v. 5). To universalize the indictment is to defang it; to pull "all have sinned" out of v. 3 is to lose the specific political-prophetic force of vv. 4-6.

Where this leaves us: the canonical Christian reader receives both — the Pauline universalization and the original this-worldly indictment. To read only the Pauline level is to lose the prophetic political edge; to read only the historical-critical level is to refuse the canonical depth Paul opened. The mature reading holds both.

The second tension is the doublet phenomenon and what it implies about the Psalter's compositional history. The bare fact that Ps 14 and Ps 53 preserve substantially the same poem with systematic name-substitution undercuts the simplest views of psalmic transmission. A poem can travel between collections, undergoing redactional adaptation in transit. The Elohistic Psalter (Pss 42-83) is a redactional unity that once existed independently and was integrated into the larger Psalter with substitutions left in place. This is uncomfortable for traditions that insist on a strict pre-existing canonical form; it is unproblematic for traditions that take canonical formation to be a historical process Spirit-guided through human editorial work.

Where this leaves us: the doublet is a window into how the Psalter was made. Editorial agency is not a threat to canonical authority but the medium through which canonical authority took historical shape.

Question to sit with: When you find yourself thinking "there is no God" not as a philosophical proposition but as a working assumption — that no one is watching, that consequences will not follow — where in your life is that practical atheism most operative?

## The Thread

- Gen 6:11-12 — "the earth was corrupt... all flesh had corrupted their way" — the pre-flood corruption-language that underlies Ps 14:1's *hishchitu*
- 1 Sam 25 — the Nabal narrative; the *naval* (fool) whose name encodes the psalm's diagnostic figure
- Rom 3:10-12 — Paul's quotation of Ps 14:1-3 in the catena of universal-sinfulness texts
- Ps 53 — the doublet of Ps 14 within the Elohistic Psalter, witness to editorial redaction
