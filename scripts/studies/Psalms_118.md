# Psalm 118 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 118 stands as the climactic close of the Egyptian Hallel (Psalms 113–118), the cluster of praise-psalms sung at the great pilgrimage festivals of ancient Israel — Passover, Weeks (Shavuot), and Tabernacles (Sukkot). The superscription is missing in the Hebrew text — the psalm carries no Davidic or Asaphite attribution, no notation of musical setting, no indication of historical occasion. This editorial silence is itself meaningful. The Hallel collection was assembled liturgically rather than biographically; what mattered was its placement in the festival cycle, not the identity of any individual author. By the late Second Temple period, Ps 118 functioned as the festal climax — the song the worshipers sang as the Passover lamb was eaten and again as the worshiper ascended to the temple gates.

Hermann Gunkel, the founder of form-critical psalm study, classified Ps 118 as a *Danklied des Einzelnen* — an individual thanksgiving song — but noted that the psalm has been adapted for corporate, processional use. The opening and closing refrain *hodu la-Yahweh ki-tov ki le-olam chasdo* (הוֹדוּ לַיהוָה כִּי־טוֹב כִּי לְעוֹלָם חַסְדּוֹ, "Give thanks to the LORD, for he is good; his steadfast love endures forever") is responsive — a leader cries the first line, the assembly answers with the second. This refrain appears in virtually identical form in 1 Chronicles 16:34, Psalm 106:1, Psalm 107:1, Psalm 136 (where it is the response of every verse), Jeremiah 33:11, and Ezra 3:11. It is the standard Israelite thanksgiving formula, the doxological sentence that gathered up Israel's covenant memory in a single rhythmic line. Sigmund Mowinckel, building on Gunkel, argued that Ps 118 was likely composed for the autumn festival enthronement procession — the king or representative figure entering the temple precincts after deliverance from enemies, voicing both individual rescue and corporate celebration. The hybrid genre — thanksgiving fused with royal-procession liturgy — accounts for the psalm's unusual movement between the singular voice ("Out of my distress I called on the LORD," v. 5) and the corporate antiphony of vv. 1–4.

The Hebrew vocabulary saturates the psalm with covenant theology. *Chesed* (חֶסֶד, "steadfast love" or covenant-loyalty) is the keyword of the refrain — Nelson Glueck's classic study established that *chesed* denotes faithful love bounded by covenant obligation, the love that holds because it has bound itself. The cry *hoshi'ah-na* (הוֹשִׁיעָה־נָּא, "save now," v. 25) — from the root *yasha* (ישע), the same root that yields the name *Yeshua* / Jesus — became, through Greek transliteration, the Christian acclamation *Hosanna*. The blessing *baruch ha-ba be-shem Yahweh* (בָּרוּךְ הַבָּא בְּשֵׁם יְהוָה, "blessed is he who comes in the name of the LORD," v. 26) was a priestly greeting offered to pilgrims as they entered the temple gates. The "stone the builders rejected" (v. 22) uses *eben* (אֶבֶן, stone) and *rosh pinnah* (רֹאשׁ פִּנָּה, "head of the corner") — the cornerstone or, in some scholarly readings, the keystone that locks an arch. The image is architectural and political: a stone deemed worthless by the master builders has been chosen by Yahweh for the place of greatest structural significance.

The dating is contested. Some scholars (Mowinckel, Kraus) place Ps 118 in the pre-exilic monarchy, reading it as a royal liturgy for the king's victorious return. Others (Hossfeld–Zenger, Goldingay) argue for a post-exilic composition, reading the rejected-stone imagery as the returning Judean community's self-understanding under Persian rule — a small, despised people now placed at the head of God's restoration project. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of Psalm 118 in 4QPs(b) and 11QPs(a); the Septuagint renders the psalm as Psalm 117 in its differently-numbered system, with *lithon hon apedokimasan hoi oikodomountes* for the rejected-stone clause — the exact phrasing the New Testament writers will quote. The psalm sits in **Book V** of the Psalter (Pss 107–150), the final book, which assembles the great thanksgiving and Hallel collections that bring the Psalter to its doxological conclusion.

## Lens 2: The Literature in the Text

The literary architecture of Ps 118 is masterful in its layering. The psalm opens (vv. 1–4) and closes (v. 29) with the same refrain — a textbook *inclusio* that frames the entire composition in the language of *hodu* (give thanks) and *chesed* (steadfast love). Within that frame, vv. 2–4 unfold a triadic call-and-response — Israel, the house of Aaron, and "those who fear the LORD" each summoned in turn to confess "his steadfast love endures forever." This three-fold liturgical summons mirrors the structure of Psalm 115:9–11 and Psalm 135:19–20, suggesting a fixed processional pattern in temple worship.

Robert Lowth's foundational analysis of Hebrew parallelism — synonymous, antithetic, and synthetic — illuminates almost every couplet. Vv. 8–9 form a perfect synonymous pair with rising intensification: "It is better to take refuge in the LORD than to trust in man / It is better to take refuge in the LORD than to trust in princes." The progression from *adam* (man, common humanity) to *nadiv* (princes, the powerful) tightens the contrast — even the political elite are no match for refuge in Yahweh. Vv. 10–12 stack a triple repetition with progressive imagery: "All nations surrounded me… they surrounded me, surrounded me on every side… they surrounded me like bees" — a rhetorical *climax* technique where the same verb is reiterated with intensifying simile. The bee-image is striking: bees sting and die, suggesting that the encircling enemies expended themselves in the assault.

The strophic structure breaks naturally into seven movements: (1) opening refrain and triadic summons, vv. 1–4; (2) personal testimony of rescue, vv. 5–7; (3) wisdom maxim on refuge, vv. 8–9; (4) battle narrative of encirclement and victory, vv. 10–14; (5) liturgy of the right hand of the LORD, vv. 15–18; (6) gate-procession with the rejected-stone oracle, vv. 19–24; (7) congregational *hoshi'ah-na* and closing inclusio, vv. 25–29. The strophic seam at v. 19 — "Open to me the gates of righteousness" — marks the dramatic climax: the worshiper has moved from distress (v. 5) through battle (vv. 10–13) to triumphant entry. There is no *selah* notation in this psalm, distinguishing it from many of the more obviously sectional psalms; the breaks are signaled instead by shifts in voice, imagery, and addressee.

Verses 22–24 form the rhetorical center of the psalm — what Adele Berlin and Robert Alter would call the "narrative pivot." The image of the rejected stone becoming the cornerstone is itself a chiastic reversal: what was *cast aside* is *raised up*, what was *despised* is *honored*. Verse 23 — *me-et Yahweh hayetah zot* (מֵאֵת יְהוָה הָיְתָה זֹּאת, "this is from the LORD") — names the reversal as divine action, not human accident. Verse 24, *zeh ha-yom asah Yahweh* (זֶה הַיּוֹם עָשָׂה יְהוָה, "this is the day the LORD has made"), turns the reversal into festal time: the day of unexpected vindication becomes the day to be celebrated forever. The festal-sacrifice clause of v. 27 — *isru chag ba-avotim ad qarnot ha-mizbeach* ("bind the festal sacrifice with cords, up to the horns of the altar") — depicts the actual moment of sacrifice in the temple courtyard, the cords binding the animal to the altar's horns being the technical Levitical procedure attested in Mishnah *Tamid*. The literary effect is to embed the psalm in real ritual space — the words are not free-floating poetry but liturgical script for an act being performed.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story Beyond the Text

The placement of Ps 118 in Book V of the Psalter is theologically deliberate. The Egyptian Hallel collection (Pss 113–118) is bracketed within Book V by the great thanksgiving Pss 107 and the long Torah-meditation Ps 119; Ps 118 is the climactic last movement of the Hallel before the alphabetic Torah-saturation of Ps 119. Gerald Wilson's structural analysis of the Psalter argued that Book V responds to the crisis of Books I–III (the failure of the Davidic covenant) by reorienting Israel's hope toward Yahweh's eternal kingship and the praise that surrounds his throne. Ps 118 sits at the apex of that reorientation — the rejected stone of v. 22 names Israel's own paradox under empire: a small, defeated nation, yet chosen by God for the place of greatest architectural weight.

In the Jewish trajectory, Ps 118 is the heart of the Hallel liturgy. The Mishnah (*Pesachim* 5:7) records that the Levites sang the Hallel — Pss 113–118 — while the Passover lambs were slaughtered in the temple. The Talmud (*Pesachim* 117a) preserves rabbinic discussions about which lines were sung antiphonally and which by leader and congregation; the structure of Ps 118:1–4 with its triadic summons matches the rabbinic description of the Hallel's responsive performance. The *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 118 reads the rejected-stone verse as a Davidic typology — David himself was the stone the prophets rejected when they assumed Saul's lineage would continue, but God chose the youngest son, the despised shepherd. Rashi reads v. 22 in messianic-Davidic terms; Ibn Ezra notes the architectural metaphor and connects it to the post-exilic restoration; Radak emphasizes the corporate reading — Israel as the rejected stone now exalted. Nachmanides treats the gate-procession of vv. 19–20 as the pattern of all true worship. Modern Jewish readers — Martin Buber, Abraham Joshua Heschel, Jon Levenson, Nahum Sarna, and Avivah Zornberg — have read Ps 118 as the great song of the people who survive history precisely because they refuse to forget what Yahweh has done. Levenson in particular has written eloquently on the *many* cornerstones in the Hebrew Bible — Davidic, royal, prophetic, covenantal — resisting any reading that flattens the image to a single referent.

The Christian trajectory of Ps 118 is among the deepest in the New Testament. The Synoptic Gospels record Jesus quoting v. 22 — "the stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone" — at the conclusion of the parable of the wicked tenants (Mark 12:10–11; Matthew 21:42; Luke 20:17). The placement is loaded: Jesus has just told a parable in which the vineyard's tenants kill the owner's son, and he closes by applying Ps 118:22 to himself. Acts 4:11 has Peter, before the Sanhedrin, applying the verse to Jesus directly — *houtos estin ho lithos ho exouthenetheis* ("this is the stone that was rejected"). 1 Peter 2:7 weaves Ps 118:22 into a Petrine collage that includes Isaiah 28:16 and Isaiah 8:14, building an entire Christology of the *living stone* and the church as *spiritual house*. Ephesians 2:20 names Christ the cornerstone of the new humanity. Romans 9:33 fuses Ps 118 with Isaiah 8:14 and 28:16 to articulate Israel's stumbling and the inclusion of Gentiles.

The Palm Sunday narratives are saturated with Ps 118:25–26. The crowd at Jerusalem's gates greets Jesus with *hoshi'ah-na* (Hosanna) and *baruch ha-ba be-shem Yahweh* — the very pilgrimage greeting offered to those entering the temple for festival. Mark 11:9, Matthew 21:9, Luke 19:38, and John 12:13 each preserve the citation. The evangelists are reading Ps 118 as the script of the king's processional entry — the gates of righteousness opened, the festal sacrifice bound to the altar, the rejected stone becoming the cornerstone. Joachim Jeremias, in *The Eucharistic Words of Jesus*, observed the haunting note in Mark 14:26 — Jesus and the disciples "sang the hymn" before going out to Gethsemane. The hymn was the Hallel; the climactic psalm of the Hallel was Ps 118. Jesus sang this very psalm — *I shall not die, but I shall live, and recount the deeds of the LORD* (v. 17); *the stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone* (v. 22) — within hours of his arrest. N. T. Wright in *Jesus and the Victory of God* draws out the implication: Jesus knew the Hallel as the soundtrack of his Passover, and he stepped into its imagery with deliberate self-understanding.

Patristic and medieval reception runs deep. Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Ps 118 reads the rejected stone Christologically, but with characteristic theological nuance — Christ is the cornerstone joining two walls, the Jews and the Gentiles, into one structure. Cassiodorus and Aquinas extend the architectural reading. Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* (1519–1521) lingered on Ps 118 with personal intensity — he called it "his own beloved psalm" and said v. 17 had sustained him through his darkest crises. Calvin's 1557 commentary reads the psalm as the archetype of the believer's deliverance through trial, with the rejected-stone verse pointing unambiguously to Christ. Modern Christian commentators — Hermann Gunkel, Mowinckel, Claus Westermann, Hans-Joachim Kraus, Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger, John Goldingay, Marvin Tate, Gordon Wenham, Walter Brueggemann, Patrick Miller, William P. Brown, Beth Tanner — have continued to mine the psalm for both its liturgical architecture and its Christological resonance. Brueggemann's reading is particularly arresting: Ps 118 is the song of the *small one* who is exalted, the song that refuses to let the empire's verdict be the final word.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The Christological cornerstone — exclusive or polyphonic?** The New Testament writers read Ps 118:22 as a prophecy fulfilled in Jesus. For nearly two millennia of Christian reading, this has been the controlling lens. But Jewish readers have always heard other cornerstones in the verse — David, the post-exilic restoration community, the suffering remnant, the people of Israel itself. The position taken by classical Christian exegesis (Augustine, Aquinas, Luther, Calvin, and the Reformed tradition through to Kraus and Hossfeld–Zenger) is that the psalmist's words *find their fullest meaning* in Christ — a *sensus plenior* reading where the human author's intent is real but partial, and the divine intent extends to the messianic fulfillment. The position taken by classical Jewish exegesis (Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Radak, the *Midrash Tehillim*) is that the rejected-stone image is a *typology of Israel itself* — the people who are despised by the nations and yet chosen by God, the David-figure of corporate deliverance that recurs in every generation. The position taken by modern dialogical readers (Levenson, Daniel Boyarin, Brueggemann, Tanner) is that the image is *polyphonic* — it has always carried multiple referents in Israelite memory, and the New Testament's Christological reading is one legitimate voicing within a larger choir, not the abolition of the others. Steelmanning the exclusive-Christological position: it takes seriously the New Testament's own claim that Jesus identified himself with this verse (Mark 12:10–11), and treats apostolic interpretation as authoritative for Christian readers. Steelmanning the Jewish position: it preserves the original Sitz im Leben of the psalm — written for a community, not a single messiah — and resists a supersessionism that erases the psalm's first hearers. Steelmanning the polyphonic position: it honors both the historical-critical integrity of the Hebrew text and the New Testament's lively, creative use of it, without forcing a winner. Where this leaves us: the cornerstone image is structurally generative — a single stone that holds together two walls. To insist that only one wall hears it rightly may be to mistake the very architecture the verse describes.

**Tension 2: The Hallel at the Last Supper — what does it mean that Jesus sang this psalm before crucifixion?** Mark 14:26 records that Jesus and his disciples "sang the hymn" — the Hallel — before walking to Gethsemane. The climactic psalm of the Hallel was Ps 118. Jesus sang the verses *I shall not die, but I shall live* (v. 17) and *the stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone* (v. 22) and *bind the festal sacrifice with cords, up to the horns of the altar* (v. 27) within hours of his arrest. The position taken by Jeremias, N. T. Wright, and most New Testament scholars is that Jesus read his own death and resurrection through Ps 118 — the psalm shaped his self-understanding as the rejected-then-vindicated one, and his Passover Hallel was an act of deliberate self-interpretation. The position taken by more skeptical historical-Jesus scholars (E. P. Sanders' more cautious moments, Géza Vermes) is that the Hallel was simply the standard Passover liturgy and we should not read Jesus' singing of it as messianic self-consciousness. The position taken by liturgical-theological readers (Bonhoeffer, William Cavanaugh, Hans Boersma) is that the question of historical self-consciousness is less interesting than the canonical fact: the Christian church has always heard Ps 118 as Jesus' own song, and the eucharistic liturgy now performs the Hallel as the song of the crucified-and-risen one. Steelmanning the Wright position: the synoptic placement of the Hallel notice is too pointed to be incidental, and Jesus' use of v. 22 in Mark 12 already shows him interpreting himself through this psalm. Steelmanning the skeptical position: we should not project later Christological reflection back onto the historical Jesus' interior life. Steelmanning the liturgical position: the church's reception is itself a theological datum — what the church has heard in this psalm is part of what the psalm now means. Where this leaves us: Christians who sing Ps 118 in Holy Week — *Hosanna* on Palm Sunday, *the stone the builders rejected* in Eastertide — are not reading the psalm out of context. They are reading it through the most ancient context Christians have ever had, the context of Jesus singing it on the night he was betrayed.

**Closing question:** The refrain — *his steadfast love endures forever* — is repeated four times in the opening four verses, and again at the close. The repetition is not decoration; it is the theological substance. If you had to confess the *chesed* of God after an experience of being the rejected stone, what would it sound like in your own voice — and would you sing it antiphonally with anyone, or alone?
