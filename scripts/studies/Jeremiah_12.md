# Jeremiah 12: The Theodicy Question, the Horses, and the Compassion for the Nations

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jeremiah 12 follows directly from the Anathoth plot of ch. 11 — the prophet has just been told that his hometown sought his life, and the second confession (vv. 1–6) opens with the question that follows from betrayal: why do the wicked prosper? The historical setting is most plausibly late in Josiah's reign (after 622, the Deuteronomic Reform) or early in Jehoiakim's (post-609), with the prophet's family treachery (v. 6, *aḥekha u-vet avikha gam-hemmah bagdu vakh*, "your brothers and your father's house — they too have dealt treacherously with you") as the immediate trigger. The wider horizon is the late Judean monarchy in its terminal phase: Jehoahaz deposed by Pharaoh Necho (609), Jehoiakim installed as Egyptian vassal, the Battle of Carchemish (605) flipping the regional power to Babylon, and the first deportation that follows.

The "destroyers" who have ravaged the vineyard (vv. 10–13) most likely point to one of the Babylonian or pre-Babylonian incursions. McKane and Holladay date vv. 7–13 to the period of Babylonian raids preceding the 597 siege; some critics push the dating later, into the post-587 desolation period itself. The "many shepherds" (*ro'im rabbim*) who "trampled my portion" (v. 10) are probably foreign rulers — the Hebrew *ro'eh* commonly designates kings (cf. 2 Sam 5:2, Mic 5:5, Ezek 34) and the parallel imagery of trampled vineyards is well-attested for foreign invasion (Isa 5, Ps 80).

The "thicket of the Jordan" (v. 5, *ge'on ha-yarden*) is a real geographical feature: the dense vegetation along the Jordan riverbanks, particularly south of the Sea of Galilee, where lions were attested into the medieval period (the last Asiatic lion in the southern Levant was killed in the 13th century, per the Crusader chronicles). The phrase carries connotations of mortal danger; Jeremiah 49:19 and 50:44 use the same image. Pre-modern Levantine fauna included Asiatic lions, Syrian brown bears, leopards, and wolves; the *ge'on ha-yarden* was the most dangerous wilderness in the southern Levant.

Material culture: the agricultural imagery of vv. 7–13 reflects late Iron II Judean farming. Vineyard terraces (*kerem*), wheat and barley fields (the "wheat" and "thorns" of v. 13), and the cycle of plowing-sowing-reaping-threshing dominate the late-monarchy economy. The Lachish ostraca and the Arad ostraca preserve the agricultural-administrative texture of the period — wine, oil, and grain rations distributed to military outposts. The Khirbet el-Qom and Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions for syncretistic Yahwism remain relevant for v. 16's reference to swearing by Baal.

The reference to "my evil neighbors who touch the heritage" (v. 14) almost certainly points to Edom, Moab, Ammon, the Philistine cities, and Aram-Damascus — Judah's small-state neighbors who alternately allied with Babylon, exploited Judah's weakness, and would themselves face Babylonian punishment. The oracles against the nations in Jer 46–51 fill out the historical content of v. 14's "neighbors."

Authorship and dating remain contested. **Jack R. Lundbom (Anchor Bible 1999–2004)** treats the second confession (vv. 1–6) as authentically Jeremianic and places the divine-lament oracle (vv. 7–13) in the same period. William Holladay (Hermeneia 1986/89) reads vv. 7–13 as one of the most powerful Jeremianic poems, dated to the late Jehoiakim or Zedekiah period. Robert P. Carroll (OTL 1986) reads vv. 7–17 as substantially redactional. William McKane treats the chapter as part of the rolling corpus. Walter Brueggemann reads the chapter theologically as the testimony of YHWH's own grief over a beloved heritage. Kathleen O'Connor (*Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*, 2011) reads it as trauma-theology — the prophet's pain and the divine pain are one voice.

Key terms: *tzaddiq attah YHWH ki ariv elekha* (צַדִּיק אַתָּה יְהוָה כִּי אָרִיב אֵלֶיךָ, v. 1, "righteous are you, O LORD, when I plead my case to you"), the *riv*-formula appropriated by the prophet *against* God; *bagad* (בָּגַד, "deal treacherously," vv. 1, 6), the verb that runs through chs. 9–12; *naḥalah* (נַחֲלָה, "heritage, inheritance," vv. 7–9, 14–15), Yahweh's word for Israel and now for the nations who will be "plucked up" and brought back; *ayit tzavua* (עַיִט צָבוּעַ, "speckled bird of prey," v. 9, contested translation); *ge'on ha-yarden* (גְּאוֹן הַיַּרְדֵּן, v. 5, "thicket / pride of the Jordan"); *shema* (שָׁמַע, "listen, learn," v. 16), the verb of covenant inclusion now offered to the nations.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is structured in three movements: the second confession (vv. 1–6), the divine lament over the abandoned heritage (vv. 7–13), and the oracle on the nations (vv. 14–17). The three movements are not loosely associated; they perform a tightly choreographed argument. The prophet asks why the wicked prosper (vv. 1–4); Yahweh refuses the question and intensifies the test (vv. 5–6); Yahweh then reveals that Yahweh, too, has lost the beloved heritage and grieves over it (vv. 7–13); and finally the oracle expands the heritage-language to include the nations themselves (vv. 14–17). The chapter moves from the prophet's grief to the divine grief to the universalizing of covenant.

The second confession (vv. 1–6) is the canonical-Hebrew form of the theodicy question. Verse 1's *tzaddiq attah YHWH* opens with the *riv*-formula — the lawsuit language Yahweh uses to indict Israel (Mic 6, Hos 4) — but turned inside out: the prophet brings the lawsuit *to* Yahweh. This is theologically audacious. It parallels Habakkuk 1:13 ("why do you idly look at traitors and remain silent when the wicked swallows up the man more righteous than he?"), Psalm 73 ("they are not in trouble as others are; they are not stricken like the rest of mankind"), and the entire Joban argument. But Jeremiah's form is more compressed and more personal. The treacherous brothers of v. 6 are not abstract wicked but the specific Anathoth conspirators of ch. 11.

Yahweh's answer (vv. 5–6) is one of the most extraordinary in the Hebrew Bible. The expected pattern (Job 38–41, Psalm 73's sanctuary-vision) is either overwhelming theophany or shifted perspective. Yahweh's answer here is neither. It is a question that *intensifies* the test: *ki et raglim ratzta va-yal'ukha ve-ekh tetacharreh et ha-susim*, "if you have raced with men on foot and they have wearied you, how will you compete with horses?" Brueggemann calls this "Yahweh's stunning refusal of cheap consolation." The prophet expected divine sympathy; he got a training metaphor. The structure is indebted to ANE wisdom literature (Job, Babylonian Theodicy) but the move is uniquely Jeremianic: Yahweh does not console, does not explain, does not reassure — Yahweh *raises* the difficulty. If the small betrayals of Anathoth have wearied Jeremiah, what will he do when Jerusalem falls? If a safe land has shaken him, what about the lion-thicket of the Jordan?

The divine lament (vv. 7–13) is the chapter's emotional center. The first-person voice belongs unambiguously to Yahweh: "I have forsaken my house, I have abandoned my heritage, I have given the beloved of my soul into the hands of her enemies." The Hebrew is intensely personal — *yedidut nafshi* ("the beloved of my soul") is unparalleled in covenant language for its tenderness. Yahweh has *given up* what Yahweh *loves*. The shepherds who destroyed the vineyard (v. 10) did so as Yahweh's own instruments, but the destruction is not less grievous to Yahweh for being Yahweh's own work. Heschel's pathos-theology finds its fullest exegetical home in this passage: God grieves the judgment God enacts.

The contested *ayit tzavua* (v. 9) is one of the textual cruxes. *Ayit* means "bird of prey." *Tzavua* could mean "speckled" (from *tzeva*, color), "striped, banded" (cognate with hyena terms), or — by emendation — refer to a hyena (the LXX reads *spelaion hyaines*, "hyena's cave," apparently reading *tzavu'a* as "hyena"). The ESV reflects the textual-critical preference: "Is my heritage to me like a hyena's lair? Are the birds of prey against her all around?" The image is of a wounded animal pursued by predators.

The oracle on the nations (vv. 14–17) is structurally surprising and theologically generous. The "evil neighbors who touch the heritage" (v. 14) will themselves be "plucked up" — but then the verb shifts: "after I have plucked them up, I will again have compassion on them" (*aḥarei nateshi otam ashuv ve-riḥamtim*, v. 15). The very verb of judgment (*natash*) becomes the verb of return (*shuv*, "turn"), and the oracle culminates in the conditional offer of v. 16: if the nations *learn the ways of my people, to swear by my name "as the LORD lives," even as they taught my people to swear by Baal, then they shall be built up in the midst of my people*. The conditional inclusion of the nations into the covenant community is one of the genuinely universalist moments in the prophetic corpus, paralleled most closely by Isa 19:23–25 (Egypt and Assyria as Yahweh's peoples).

Genre and devices: leitwort: *naḥalah* ("heritage," vv. 7, 8, 9, 14, 15) — the chapter's central word, applied first to Yahweh's relationship to Israel, then extended to the nations. Inclusio: vv. 1–4's question about the wicked prospering is answered by vv. 14–17's promise that the wicked nations will themselves be plucked up — and then perhaps gathered in. The horse-image of v. 5 reverses prophetic-call language: the prophet was promised divine accompaniment (Jer 1:8–10), and now Yahweh offers harder running, not easier.

Textual notes: the LXX of ch. 12 is generally close to the MT. The *ayit tzavua* crux of v. 9 is the main translational difficulty. The chapter is comparatively stable.

Canonical position: Jer 12 closes the temple-sermon block (chs. 7–10), the Anathoth-confession block (ch. 11), and the second confession. The chapter's three-part structure (confession, divine lament, nations oracle) anticipates the book's own three-part structure on the largest scale (oracles against Judah, oracles against the nations, narrative epilogue).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Backward in the canon: the theodicy question echoes Job 21 (the wicked prosper question) and Psalm 73 (the sanctuary-perspective answer). Habakkuk 1:13 voices the same question in the same period. Jeremiah's form is sharpest because it is most personal — the prosperity of the wicked includes his own brothers' treachery.

Yahweh's "horses" answer (v. 5) belongs to the ANE wisdom-tradition that frames hardship as training — Babylonian Theodicy, Egyptian Instruction texts, and within the Hebrew Bible, Proverbs 3:11–12 (which Hebrews 12 will quote). But the form is unique: the divine voice raising the stakes rather than lowering them.

Forward in the canon: Romans 11:26–32 — Paul's vision of universal salvation (or near-universal, depending on reading) takes Jer 12:14–17 as one of its substrate texts, alongside Isa 19:23–25 and Jonah 4:11. Hebrews 12:1–11's training-discipline metaphor reads Jer 12:5 typologically — if you have wearied with smaller trials, prepare for the race that is set before you. The early Christian missionary movement read v. 16's "if they will diligently learn the ways of my people" as the inclusion of the Gentiles into the covenant community; Paul's whole argument in Romans 11 about wild olive branches grafted in is intelligible in this frame. Lamentations 5's closing question — "why do you forget us forever?" — is Jer 12:1's grief in the next register, after the destruction has come.

**Jewish trajectory.** Sirach 49:6–7. The Bavli treats Jeremiah's confessions as evidence of authentic prophecy (Bavli Sanhedrin 89a, 105b on prophetic suffering). Targum Jonathan paraphrases the Hebrew with characteristic expansions — the *yedidut nafshi* ("beloved of my soul") becomes "my beloved people Israel" in the Targum. Rashi reads v. 5 (the horses) as Yahweh's instruction that Jeremiah will face worse and must prepare. Radak attends to the historical context of the Anathoth plot. Ibn Ezra parses the difficult Hebrew of v. 9 (*ayit tzavua*). Abrabanel reads the universalist oracle (vv. 14–17) through the lens of late-medieval Jewish-Christian-Muslim relations — the nations' inclusion is conditional on their renouncing idolatry and embracing Mosaic monotheism. The post-Holocaust Jewish theology of Emil Fackenheim, Elie Wiesel, and Emmanuel Levinas takes Jer 12:1's theodicy question as the irreducible Jewish voice — *why do the wicked prosper?* is not answered, and any theodicy that pretends to answer it is itself a betrayal.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic: **Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah*** reads the second confession Christologically — Christ is the truly innocent one whose enemies plot against him; the divine grief of vv. 7–13 prefigures the Father's grief over the Son's death; the universal oracle (vv. 14–17) prefigures the Gentile mission. **Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah*** is more philological. Augustine's *City of God* 22 treats Jer 12 alongside Job and Psalm 73 in his theodicy discussion. Aquinas in *Summa* I.22 (on providence) and II-II.2 (on faith and the problem of evil) cites the chapter. **Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah* (1563)** reads vv. 5–6 as Yahweh's training of his prophet, and vv. 14–17 as anticipating the gospel's universal extension. The Reformed reading takes the chapter's theodicy as illustrative of providence's depth: the wicked prosper because Yahweh has not yet completed the harvest.

**Where they diverge.** Christian universalist readings (Origen, some patristic readings, modern Christian universalists) take vv. 14–17 as anticipating universal salvation. The mainstream Christian tradition reads the verse more cautiously — the nations are conditionally included if they "learn the ways" of Yahweh's people. Pauline reading (Rom 11) takes the conditional inclusion seriously: the *grafting in* of the Gentiles is the conditional inclusion of v. 16. Jewish reading takes the conditional inclusion as the *ger toshav* tradition — the resident alien who renounces idolatry can join the covenant community. Modern critical reading often locates vv. 14–17 in the post-exilic period, when the Jewish community in diaspora was actively negotiating relations with the Gentile world.

The post-Holocaust Jewish refusal of theodicy (Fackenheim, Wiesel, Levinas) is its own school. *There is no theodicy that does not betray the dead.* The systematic justification of evil — Augustine's eschatological resolution, Calvin's secret providence, Aquinas's privatio boni — is, on this reading, an offense against the victims. Jer 12:1 asks the question and receives the horses-and-Jordan answer; the question is not answered, and the refusal to answer is more honest than the systems that claim to answer.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The theodicy question.** The chapter contains the sharpest Hebrew form of the theodicy question (v. 1) and one of the strangest answers (v. 5). The Christian theodicy tradition has built large structures: Augustine's *City of God* 22 with its eschatological resolution (the wicked prosper now but face eternal judgment; the righteous suffer now but face eternal joy); Aquinas's *Summa* I.22 on providence with the *privatio boni* that locates evil as absence of being rather than positive substance; Calvin's *Institutes* 1.16–18 on the secret providence that disposes all things; the Lutheran *Deus absconditus* tradition that distinguishes God's revealed will from God's hidden will; Leibniz's *best of all possible worlds* in the *Théodicée* (1710). Each of these answers Jer 12:1 in some way, and none of them is what Yahweh says in v. 5.

The Jewish post-Holocaust refusal of theodicy is the strongest counter-tradition. Emil Fackenheim's *To Mend the World* (1982) argues that systematic theodicy after Auschwitz is theologically impermissible — to justify the suffering of the six million is to spit on their graves. Elie Wiesel's *Night* and *The Trial of God* dramatize the impossibility. Emmanuel Levinas in "Useless Suffering" (1982) argues that the *only* legitimate theological response to suffering is *non-theodicy* — to refuse to integrate the other's suffering into a theodical scheme that consoles the observer. The Levinasian voice deserves serious steelmanning: any answer to v. 1 that makes the wicked's prosperity intelligible to me is a betrayal of those whose lives the wicked have ruined.

The Christian counter-steelman: there is a theological difference between the *trying* to make sense of suffering (Augustine, Aquinas) and the *justifying* of it (the worst of vulgar theodicy). The Joban tradition does not justify Job's suffering — Job's friends are condemned for their attempt — but the canonical book ends with theophany, not with the question's full closure. Jeremiah 12:5 sits in the same tradition: Yahweh does not justify the wicked's prosperity but *redirects* the prophet to the work that lies ahead. The genuine Christian theodicy is eschatological, not systematic — the resolution awaits the consummation, and in the meantime the prophet's task is to keep running.

The Jewish steelman that the Christian tradition has often missed: the Tisha B'Av liturgy holds the unanswered question in the *liturgical present*. Lamentations does not resolve into Joban theophany but ends with "why do you forget us forever, why do you forsake us so long?" (Lam 5:20). The Jewish tradition has learned to *keep liturgically present* the question that the Christian tradition is sometimes too eager to resolve. The post-Holocaust refusal of systematic theodicy is, in part, a recovery of this liturgical posture.

Where this leaves us: Jer 12:1–6 is a decisive text *against* easy theodicy. Yahweh's answer is not consolation; it is intensification of the test. Whether the Christian eschatological resolution honors this answer or evades it depends on whether one experiences the New Testament's resurrection-hope as cheap consolation or as the *only* possible answer to the cross.

**Tension 2: The inclusion of the nations.** Verses 14–17 are one of the most theologically generous moments in Jeremiah. The "evil neighbors who touch the heritage" — Edom, Moab, Ammon, the Philistines, Aram — will be *plucked up*, but then *brought back to their own land*. And then the conditional offer: if they "learn the ways of my people," they will be "built up in the midst of my people." This is theologically explosive.

Christian universalist reading takes v. 17's threat ("if any nation will not listen, then I will utterly pluck it up and destroy it") as the conditional pole of an offer that is, in principle, extended to all. The whole oracle becomes a forecast of the Gentile mission, where every nation is offered the conditional inclusion that some accept and some refuse. Origen reads it this way; modern Christian universalists from Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* II/2 onward have made similar moves.

Jewish *ger toshav* reading (the resident alien tradition, Lev 25, Deut 14, Maimonides Hilkhot Melakhim 8–10) takes v. 16 as foundational for the rabbinic doctrine of the seven Noahide commandments — the universal moral law that any non-Jew can keep and thereby be counted righteous in the world to come. Maimonides explicitly cites the prophetic universalist passages (Isa 56, Mic 4, Jer 12:14–17) in articulating this view. The Gentile is not required to become a Jew but is required to renounce idolatry and observe the Noahide commands.

Pauline reading (Rom 11) takes the *grafting in* metaphor as the redemptive-historical figure for the Gentiles' inclusion. Wright (*Justification* 2009) and Sanders (*Paul and Palestinian Judaism* 1977) read this as continuous with Second Temple Jewish universalism — Paul is articulating, in Christ-shaped form, what was already implicit in Isa 19:23–25 and Jer 12:14–17.

Steelman the universalist reading: the prophetic tradition repeatedly opens covenant identity to the nations on terms of repentance and renunciation of idolatry, and Jer 12:14–17 is one of the clearest cases. To read the verse merely as a threat against bad neighbors misses its own conditional structure — *if they will diligently learn, they shall be built up*. Steelman the conditional reading: the verse is genuinely conditional, and most nations *will not* learn. The threat of v. 17 is not rhetorical fluff. The oracle is a generous offer with serious teeth, not a forecast of universal salvation.

Where this leaves us: the chapter's universalist horizon is real and theologically weighty, but it is conditional. The conditional structure rules out cheap universalism (everyone is included regardless) and cheap exclusivism (only Israel is included). The conditional offer is the prophetic shape of grace.

The unresolved question that lands hard: if Yahweh grieves over the abandoned heritage in vv. 7–13, and if Yahweh extends compassion to the very neighbors who destroyed the heritage in vv. 14–17, then the *naḥalah* — Yahweh's heritage — is becoming larger and stranger than Israel could have anticipated. The compassion of v. 15 is the same word (*riḥam*) that the Pentateuch uses for Yahweh's tender love. Yahweh will *be tender with the nations who destroyed Yahweh's people*. What is the moral logic of a love that grieves the beloved's loss and yet has compassion on the destroyers? Either Yahweh is morally incoherent, or the prophets are showing us a love whose internal coherence we have not yet understood.

## The Thread

Job 21 and Job 38–41 — the canonical-Hebrew theodicy parallel, where the question is asked and answered with theophany rather than explanation. Psalm 73 — the sanctuary-perspective answer to the same question, complementary to Jer 12. Habakkuk 1:13 — the question in its sharpest contemporary form, from a prophet active during the same Babylonian rise. Romans 11:25–32 — Paul's vision of universal Israel-and-nations salvation, which takes Jer 12:14–17 as one of its substrate texts. Hebrews 12:1–11 — the discipline-as-training metaphor of Yahweh's "horses" answer (Jer 12:5), now redirected through the Son. Lamentations 5 — the closing-question form of theodicy that the rest of the canon does not finally close.
