# Ezekiel 2: The Call, the Rebellious House, and the Scroll

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political situation**: Continuing the **exilic period (593–571 BC)** scene of chapter 1. The vision of the chariot (1:1–28) collapses the prophet to the ground; chapter 2 picks up immediately with the commission. The audience is *a rebellious house* (*bet meri*) — the Judean exiles in Babylonia, but addressed as representatives of "the people of Israel" (2:3) in a deliberately pan-tribal idiom. Ezekiel is being sent not to the Jerusalem rump community (still standing in 593, six years from destruction) but to the deportees with whom he lives at Tel-abib by the Chebar canal (3:15). This is theologically pointed: in Jeremiah 24's famous figs vision, the *good figs* are the Babylonian exiles — the future of the covenant lies with them — but Ezekiel will not let them congratulate themselves on that. They are *as rebellious as their fathers* (2:3).

**Material culture**: The Babylonian exilic community in the Nippur region. The setting is the same as chapter 1 — a Mesopotamian agricultural and canal landscape. The prophetic-call genre, however, comes loaded with specifically Mesopotamian textual conventions. **Akkadian tablet-of-destinies traditions** stand behind the scroll-eating motif: in *Enuma Elish* and in the *Anzu* myth, possession of the *Tablet of Destinies* (*ṭuppi šīmāti*) confers oracular and sovereign power. **Babylonian scribal practice** is also present in the chapter's specific detail that the scroll has writing on both sides (2:10) — unusual for ANE leather scrolls (typically inscribed on the smoother flesh-side only), but *standard for cuneiform clay tablets* in the Babylonian scribal tradition, which were inscribed on obverse, reverse, and edges. Ezekiel's *opisthograph* scroll thus reads as a Mesopotamian-shaped Israelite text. The **Murashu archive** and **Al-Yahudu tablets** continue to anchor the social setting — a settled, literate, tablet-using exile.

**Authorship & dating**: Ezekiel ben Buzi the priest (1:3), in the same vision-cycle as chapter 1 (593 BC). The prophetic-call form (commission + objection + reassurance + sign) follows a pattern documented across the Hebrew Bible — Moses (Exodus 3–4), Isaiah 6, Jeremiah 1 — and this typological positioning is not accidental. **Greenberg (Anchor)** reads the call as carefully constructed against the Mosaic and Jeremianic precedents; **Block (NICOT)** stresses the priestly cast (Ezekiel does not object as Moses or Jeremiah do — a priest receives orders); **Zimmerli (Hermeneia)** reads the chapter through the form-critical lens of *Berufungsgeschichten* (call-narratives). **Odell** highlights the deliberate inversion: where Isaiah volunteers (*Here am I, send me*), Ezekiel is *raised* by the Spirit — a passive object of vocation. **Eichrodt (OTL)** places the chapter in the broader exilic crisis of prophetic legitimacy.

**Archaeological & textual evidence**: 4QEzek-a is fragmentary at chapter 2 but the surviving readings agree with MT. LXX has minor stylistic variations. The phrase *bet meri* ("rebellious house") becomes a leitmotif across Ezekiel; its concentration here marks chapter 2 as the inaugurating use. **Mishnah Hagigah 2:1** and **Bavli Hagigah 11b–14a** — the rabbinic prohibition on chariot-study under thirty and the *Pardes* warning — apply specifically to chapter 1 but bracket chapter 2 by association: the call narrative is the bridge from the unspeakable vision to ordinary prophetic speech.

**Key terms**: *ben adam* (בֶּן אָדָם, "son of man," 2:1, 3, 6, 8 — the first four occurrences of what becomes the prophet's vocational title, used 93× in the book; the address marks Ezekiel as *creature*, not *participant*, in the divine throne-room); *bet meri* (בֵּית מְרִי, "rebellious house," 2:5, 6, 7, 8 — the audience-name used 16× across the book); *ruach* (רוּחַ, "spirit/wind," 2:2 — *the Spirit entered into me and set me on my feet*; the same word, with deliberate ambiguity, that designates the wind out of the north in 1:4); *megillat sefer* (מְגִלַּת סֵפֶר, "scroll of a book," 2:9); *qinim va-hegeh va-hi* (קִנִים וָהֶגֶה וָהִי, "lamentation and mourning and woe," 2:10).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: **prophetic call narrative** with **commissioning vision**. The chapter follows the classical call-form: confrontation (2:1–2), commission (2:3–5), reassurance (2:6–7), sign (2:8–10). What is striking is what is *missing*: there is no objection-clause. Moses raises five objections (Exodus 3–4); Jeremiah pleads youth (Jer 1:6); Isaiah confesses uncleanness (Isa 6:5). Ezekiel says nothing. The priest receives orders. **Block** reads this as ecclesial — Ezekiel is on duty before the divine throne, and a priest does not object. **Lapsley** reads it as traumatic — Ezekiel is so flattened by the vision that speech is unavailable. Either reading attends to the silence.

The chapter's literary technique is dominated by **leitwort** — *meri* (rebellion) is hammered: *people of rebels*, *they have rebelled*, *rebellious house*, *rebellious house*, *rebellious house*, *rebellious house*. Six times in seven verses. The repetition is deliberate: the audience's defining quality is named and renamed before Ezekiel is sent. The contrast is then made explicit at 2:8 — *be not rebellious like that rebellious house; open your mouth and eat what I give you*. The prophet's fidelity is defined antithetically against the audience's rebellion. *Eat the scroll, do not be a rebel.*

The **scroll-eating motif** (2:8–10) is the chapter's literary climax. The image is a deliberately bizarre figure for *prophetic ingestion of the divine word*: not a scroll *given* to be read, recited, or carried, but a scroll *handed over to be consumed*. The action will continue into 3:1–3 (*and it was in my mouth as honey for sweetness*), and the *scroll-eating* echoes will reach Jeremiah 15:16, Psalm 119:103, and ultimately Revelation 10:9–10, where John eats a scroll given to him by an angel (sweet in mouth, bitter in stomach). The fact that the scroll is *opisthograph* — written on front and back — marks it as full to overflowing: the message exceeds the medium. The fact that its content is *lamentation, mourning, and woe* (2:10) precommits the prophet's vocation: Ezekiel will be a prophet of *qinah* (lament) before he is anything else.

Textual variants: minor. The LXX flattens *bet meri* in places to a more general "rebellious people," but the leitwort survives in Greek by repetition.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Place in canonical arc: the chapter belongs to the chain of **prophetic call narratives** — Moses (Exodus 3–4), Samuel (1 Samuel 3), Isaiah 6, Jeremiah 1, Amos 7:14–15 — and to the smaller chain of **scroll-eating texts** (Jer 15:16; Ps 119:103; Rev 10:9–10). It also opens the book's defining theme: the prophet's vocation as a *witness* (3:16–21) to a *rebellious* audience whose response is not the prophet's responsibility.

**Jewish trajectory**: Sirach 49:8 already groups Ezekiel with Jeremiah and Daniel as the three exilic-period theologians. The rabbis read the call narrative as Ezekiel's *qabbalat ol* (acceptance of the yoke) — and contrast his silent obedience with Moses's objections to make a homiletical point about priestly versus prophetic vocation. Targum Jonathan moderates the anthropomorphism (the *hand* of 2:9 becomes "an instrument of prophecy"). **Rashi** reads *son of man* as an emphasis on Ezekiel's *creaturely lowliness* in contrast with the divine throne; **Radak** notes the philological detail of *opisthograph*; **Maimonides *Guide* 2.41–48** treats the call narratives as paradigmatic for the philosophy of prophecy — the *imaginative faculty* is the medium through which the *Active Intellect* communicates with the prophet, and the scroll-eating is a vivid figure for this. The medieval Jewish mystical tradition (the *Hekhalot* literature) reads the *Spirit entered into me* (2:2) as the moment of mystical investiture. **Levenson** in *Theology of the Program of Restoration* (1976) places the call as the constitutive moment of Ezekiel's priestly-spatial theology: the priest is sent away from the temple to do priestly work in exile, and the scroll is the new portable sanctum. **Sommer** reads the *ruach entering* as a specifically *fluid-divine-self* moment.

**Christian trajectory**: Patristic — **Origen's *Homilies on Ezekiel* (Hom. 1–2)** treats the call as paradigmatic for the soul's vocation: every soul is called to be *raised from prostration* by the Spirit. **Jerome's *Commentary on Ezekiel*** (414) reads the rebellious-house motif typologically against Rome and the church's enemies. **Gregory the Great's *Homilies on Ezekiel*** (Book 1, Hom. 6–8) takes the call as the foundation of the *cura pastoralis* — every preacher is sent to a rebellious house and must speak whether they hear or refuse to hear. This text feeds directly into Gregory's *Liber Regulae Pastoralis* (the *Pastoral Rule*), which becomes the central handbook of medieval pastoral theology. Medieval — Hugh of St. Victor; **Richard of St. Victor**; Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 171–174 on prophecy draws heavily on Ezekiel's call). Reformation — **Calvin's *Lectures on Ezekiel* (1565)** on chapter 2 is the most pastorally acute of his Ezekiel work: the *whether they hear or refuse to hear* clause is treated as foundational for Reformed preaching theology. The preacher is responsible for *fidelity*, not *outcome*. Critical — Cooke (ICC); **Zimmerli (Hermeneia)** with the *Berufungsgeschichten* form-critical reading; **Greenberg (Anchor)** for literary unity; **Block (NICOT)**. Contemporary — **Lapsley (*Can These Bones Live?*)** reads the call traumatically; **Wilson** historically; **Wright** pastorally. **Friebel**'s *Jeremiah's and Ezekiel's Sign-Acts* (1999) treats the scroll-eating as the inaugural sign-act.

**Where they diverge**: the Jewish tradition has typically read the scroll-eating *cognitively* — internalization of *Torah* by study and recitation, *mille pi-cha* (Ps 119:103) — while the Christian tradition has read it *sacramentally*, with explicit Eucharistic resonance: *eat the word, the word becomes flesh in you* (cf. John 6, "the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh"). The Reformed tradition splits the difference: the Word is *eaten* by hearing and faith, but the eating is more than metaphor.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Prophetic vocation as *ingestion* — what does it mean that the word must be *eaten*?** The scroll-eating (2:8–3:3) is one of the most physical figures for revelation in Scripture. Word does not stand at a distance to be assented to; it enters the body.

**Catholic and Orthodox sacramental theology** runs a direct line from Ezekiel's scroll through John 6 (the bread of life discourse) to the Eucharist: the divine word, having become flesh, is now eaten and assimilated by the faithful, and the assimilation is not metaphor but mystery. The Eucharist is the *medicine of immortality* (Ignatius of Antioch, *Eph.* 20.2) precisely because the divine self is given to be ingested. **Reformed *inscripturation*** (Westminster Confession 1; Bavinck *Reformed Dogmatics* 1) reads the scroll-eating differently: the Word is internalized through faithful hearing, but the *Word* and the *recipient* remain distinct — the metaphor of eating preserves intimacy without collapsing the ontological difference. **Jewish *talmud Torah*** (the discipline of internalizing Torah) and *yireh shamayim* (the fear of heaven that comes from the Torah inscribed on the heart) read scroll-eating as habituation: by recitation and study and observance, the Torah becomes part of the self. The least intuitive position to a contemporary Protestant reader may be the Catholic-sacramental line. The steelman: the canonical text really does present revelation as something that *enters the body*, and the New Testament really does take this up in John 6 in a way that resists pure metaphorization. The early church (East and West) read this material univocally for a thousand years before the Reformation split. The sacramental reading is not innovation; it is the older interpretation. Where this leaves us: revelation in Ezekiel 2 is not information but ingestion, and *what we do with that figure* is the substance of one of the deepest divisions in Christian theology.

**Tension 2: *Whether they hear or refuse to hear* — is the prophet's task *successful preaching* or *faithful witness*?** The phrase repeats three times (2:5, 7; cf. 3:11). It is the chapter's pastoral spine.

**Calvinist effectual call** doctrine (Westminster Confession 10; Bavinck *RD* 4) holds that the elect *will* hear — not because the preacher is effective but because the Spirit is sovereign. The prophet's role is faithful witness; the *call* is God's. **Wesleyan / Arminian prevenient grace** (Wesley, *Sermon on Free Grace*) holds that all may genuinely respond — the prophet's preaching aims at *all* and rests on the Spirit's prior enabling work in *all*. **Catholic invincible ignorance** (CCC 847; *Lumen Gentium* 16) holds that those who refuse to hear because they have never genuinely heard are not condemned for refusing. **Anabaptist witness without expectation** (Yoder, *The Politics of Jesus*) takes the *whether they hear or refuse to hear* as the central political-theological orientation: the church witnesses regardless of result, and *result* is not the church's category. **Karl Barth** (*CD* I.1, IV.3) reads the verse as foundational for the doctrine of the Word: *the Word is what it is regardless of whether it is received*; the prophet's task is to *witness*, and witness is its own justification. The least intuitive position to the activist contemporary Christian may be the Anabaptist-Barthian one. The steelman: *whether they hear or refuse to hear* really does mean what it says. If the prophetic vocation depends on visible response, it is not the prophetic vocation Ezekiel is given. The exilic context matters: most of Ezekiel's audience does not turn. The book registers their refusal. The vocation does not collapse on that account. Where this leaves us: the chapter cuts the cord between *fidelity* and *outcome*. The prophet who measures fidelity by outcome will not last, and the prophet who measures outcome by fidelity is not measuring outcome at all.

**The unresolved question**: If the prophet's responsibility is fidelity rather than outcome, what is the responsibility of the *audience*? The chapter does not say — the watchman discourse of chapter 3 will sharpen the question to its breaking point, and *then* leave it.

## The Thread

The scroll-eating of Ezekiel 2:8–3:3 echoes Jeremiah 15:16 (*your words were found and I ate them*) and Psalm 119:103 (*how sweet are your words to my taste, sweeter than honey to my mouth*), and is taken up in **Revelation 10:9–10** where John eats the angel's scroll — sweet in his mouth and bitter in his stomach. The double-sided scroll of 2:10, written *front and back* with lamentation and woe, prefigures the seven-sealed scroll of Revelation 5: a document so dense with covenantal reckoning that no medium can be wide enough for it, and so dense with promise that only the Lamb can open it. The address *son of man* will be picked up in Daniel 7:13's *one like a son of man* and through it become Jesus's preferred self-designation in the Synoptics — Ezekiel's repeated *ben adam*, naming creaturely lowliness, is heard in reverse by Jesus and turned into the title of the one who comes with the clouds. The *whether they hear or refuse to hear* clause shapes Christian preaching theology from Augustine's *De Doctrina Christiana* through Gregory's *Pastoral Rule* through Calvin's *Lectures on Ezekiel* through Bonhoeffer's *Spiritual Care* — the long line of pastors who have stood in front of rebellious houses and learned that fidelity is the only metric the text offers them.
