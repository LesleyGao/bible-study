# Lamentations 2: The Lord Has Become Like an Enemy

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Where chapter 1 had personified Jerusalem and watched her weep, chapter 2 turns its eyes upward and says something almost unsayable: the destroyer is not Babylon. The destroyer is God. "He has bent his bow like an enemy" (v. 4); "the Lord has become like an enemy" (v. 5); "he has destroyed without pity" (v. 17). Verse 17 is the Deuteronomistic axis — "the LORD has done what he purposed; he has carried out his word, which he commanded long ago" — but verses 1-16 describe the doing in terms that read as theological accusation. The poet is staring directly at the wrecked Temple and naming the agent.

The historical referent is the same catastrophe as chapter 1: the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BCE under Nebuchadnezzar II. But chapter 2 lingers in the rubble of specific institutions. "His footstool" in v. 1 is the Temple — an idiom established in 1 Chronicles 28:2 and Psalm 132:7 ("Let us go to his dwelling place; let us worship at his footstool") and Psalm 99:5. The Temple was the place where the invisible God's presence touched earth; calling it "his footstool" expressed both intimacy and condescension. The horror of v. 1 is that God has not "remembered" his own footstool — the divine memory, which Israel had counted on through every cycle of exile and return (Genesis 8:1, Exodus 2:24, 1 Samuel 1:19), has failed at the moment of greatest need. Verses 6-7 catalog the institutional ruin: the booth (*sukkah* — same word as the festival of Tabernacles), the meeting place, festival, Sabbath, king, priest, altar, sanctuary. The poet is not inventorying property losses. He is inventorying the disappearance of a covenant infrastructure.

The siege famine is now in the foreground. Verses 11-12 describe infants fainting in the streets, dying at their mothers' breasts. Verse 20 asks the unspeakable: "Should women eat the fruit of their womb, the children of their tender care?" The horror is not rhetorical. Deuteronomy 28:53-57 had warned that siege starvation would drive Israelite mothers to cannibalism; 2 Kings 6:28-29 records the practice during the Aramean siege of Samaria; Jeremiah 19:9 had repeated the threat. The poet's question to God is whether the curse has now actualized in Jerusalem itself. Josephus, *Jewish War* 6.3.4, describes the same horror during the Roman siege of Jerusalem in 70 CE, in passages later Jewish tradition would cross-read with Lam 2:20.

Material culture saturates the verses. The "measuring line" (*qaw*) of v. 8 was the surveyor's tool — Isaiah 28:17, Zechariah 1:16 use it as a positive image of restoration; here it is inverted into the line by which God measures destruction. Sackcloth and dust on the head (v. 10) were the standard mourning conventions — Job 2:12, Esther 4:1, 2 Samuel 13:19. The gates "sunk into the ground" (v. 9) is not metaphor: archaeological excavation at the City of David shows Babylonian-era destruction collapsing the Iron Age gate complex into the slope below. The "false and deceptive visions" of v. 14 indict the court prophets Jeremiah had spent his career denouncing — Hananiah son of Azzur in Jeremiah 28, the prophets of Jeremiah 23 who said "peace, peace" when there was no peace. Jeremiah's vindication is bitter: he was right, and the city he loved is rubble.

Five Hebrew terms repay close attention. *Adonai* (אֲדֹנָי), "Lord," appears with striking frequency in chapter 2 — fourteen times — far more than the covenantal *YHWH* (יְהוָה). The choice is theologically loaded: *Adonai* names sovereign authority without invoking the covenant name, distancing the destroyer-figure from the covenant partner of the fathers. *Hadom raglaw* (הֲדֹם רַגְלָיו), "his footstool," is the Temple-as-throne-base imagery shared with Mesopotamian temple theology. *Bal'a* (בִּלַּע), "swallowed up," appears three times in vv. 2, 5 (twice), and again in v. 8 (verb form) — this is the verb of Sheol's hunger (Numbers 16:30, Proverbs 1:12), now used of the Lord himself. *Shaved* (שׁוֹד), "ruin," in v. 11 is the same root as *Shaddai* — "El Shaddai," "Almighty" — and Phyllis Trible has noted the bitter wordplay: the God of Power is the cause of *shaved*. *Mo'ed* (מוֹעֵד), "festival" or "appointed time," used in v. 7 of God's appointed feasts that the enemies have desecrated, is contrasted with v. 22's terrifying inversion: "you summoned as if to a festival day my terrors on every side" — the same word, *mo'ed*, now meaning the appointed day of slaughter.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The acrostic continues — twenty-two verses, *aleph* through *taw* — but with a famous textual peculiarity. In Lamentations 2, 3, and 4, the letters *pe* (פ) and *'ayin* (ע) appear in inverted order: *pe* before *'ayin*, rather than the standard alphabet's *'ayin* before *pe*. For centuries this was treated as a scribal error to be corrected. F. M. Cross and other epigraphers, examining inscribed alphabet exercises (*abecedaria*) from Kuntillet 'Ajrud (c. 800 BCE), Tel Zayit (c. 950 BCE), and Izbet Sartah (c. 1200 BCE), recognized that the *pe-'ayin* order was an alternative ancient Hebrew alphabetic convention — possibly older than the order that became standard in the post-exilic period. Lamentations 2, 3, and 4 may preserve a pre-exilic Judahite alphabet. (Chapter 1 follows the standard order, suggesting either different authorship or a scribal harmonization.) This is not pedantry: the alphabet itself in Lamentations is an archaeological artifact.

The poem is structured around two voices. Verses 1-10 are third-person description by the narrator, cataloging God's actions in a relentless drumbeat — every verse begins with a divine action (he cast down, he swallowed, he cut down, he bent, he became, he laid waste, he scorned, he determined, he ruined). Eleven of these twenty-two verses begin with "the Lord has..." or some equivalent. Adele Berlin's commentary calls this "theology by anaphora" — the repetition is the argument.

At verse 11 the poet's voice breaks into first person: "My eyes are spent with weeping." This first-person speaker is generally read as the narrator-poet, not Jerusalem (as in chapter 1). The poet has been describing — now he collapses. Verses 13-19 then turn outward to address the city directly: "What can I say for you?" — the poet attempting to console and finding no analogy. Verse 13's question — "your ruin is vast as the sea; who can heal you?" — has no answer in the chapter. It will be partially, and only partially, taken up at 3:22-24.

Verses 20-22 perform the chapter's most radical move: the poet turns to address God himself and demands he look. "Look, O LORD, and see! With whom have you dealt thus?" The question is not rhetorical — it is forensic. Tod Linafelt argues this is the chapter's theological climax: not theodicy, not confession, but the act of speaking back to God. The Hebrew imperative *re'eh* ("see!") forces God into the role of witness to his own work.

The chapter's literary technique is the inversion of festival and warfare. Verse 22 — "You summoned as if to a festival day my terrors on every side" — uses the verb *qara'* (to call, summon) which is the standard verb for proclaiming a feast. The day of festival has become the day of slaughter. The poet has read Amos 5:21 ("I hate, I despise your feasts") to its terrifying conclusion: the feasts have not just been refused, they have been replaced.

The chapter shares vocabulary with Psalm 22 ("they hiss and wag their heads," v. 15; cf. Ps 22:7) and with the divine warrior poetry of Exodus 15 — but with the polarities reversed. In Exodus 15 the LORD is the warrior who destroys the enemy for Israel's sake; here the LORD is the warrior who destroys Israel as if she were the enemy. This is one of the most theologically vertiginous moves in the Hebrew Bible.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Lamentations 2 stands at the brutal center of the canonical question: what does it mean that the same God who delivered Israel from Egypt has now turned his bow against her? The chapter forces every later reader of Scripture, Jewish and Christian, to grapple with divine wrath against the elect.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

Eikhah Rabbah (Lamentations Rabbah) treats v. 1's "footstool" with extraordinary tenderness. The midrash imagines God himself weeping, summoning the patriarchs and Moses to mourn with him. Abraham complains: "Where is the covenant?" Isaac complains: "Where is my binding?" Moses complains: "Was my Torah given for this?" Rachel weeps for her children, and only Rachel's tears move God. The midrash refuses to let the destruction be merely God's will — it stages the divine self-accusation. This is interpretation as confrontation.

Rashi (11th c.), writing after the Crusader massacres of the Rhineland Jewish communities (1096), reads v. 5 ("the Lord has become like an enemy") with the precision of someone who has buried his own. Rashi notes the simile — *like* an enemy, not an enemy. The Hebrew preposition *ke-* (כ) is the hinge of his reading: God resembles the destroyer in the moment of destruction, but resemblance is not identity. This is not theodicy; it is grammar pressed into pastoral service.

Maimonides (*Guide for the Perplexed* 3.51) integrated suffering into providence; the destruction of the Temple is not arbitrary but part of the divine pedagogy that calls Israel back to philosophical clarity. Nachmanides (Ramban, 13th c.) emphasized exile as a covenantal mechanism — Israel's sins activated, did not invent, the curse.

The post-Holocaust Jewish reading reframes everything. Eliezer Berkovits's *Faith After the Holocaust* (1973), Emil Fackenheim's *To Mend the World* (1982), and Irving "Yitz" Greenberg's "voluntary covenant" essay (1981) all wrestle directly with whether the language of Lam 2 can survive Auschwitz — whether v. 17's "the LORD has done what he purposed" can be said of a God who watched the gas chambers. David Roskies in *Against the Apocalypse* documents how Hebrew and Yiddish Holocaust poets (Yitzhak Katznelson's *Song of the Murdered Jewish People*, Itzik Manger, Abraham Sutzkever) wrote in conscious dialogue with Lamentations 2, sometimes adopting its acrostic, more often shattering it. Tod Linafelt argues that any reading of Lam 2 that resolves into v. 17 ("the LORD has done") suppresses the chapter's actual climax in vv. 20-22's accusation.

Avivah Zornberg, in her readings, attends to the silent space between v. 22 and chapter 3 — the canonical silence after the accusation, before any answer, where the reader must dwell.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

Origen's surviving fragments read v. 1's footstool typologically as the Incarnation: God's "footstool" is the human body of Christ, and the casting-down anticipates the cross. Patristic and medieval readings tended to allegorize the chapter onto the church under persecution or onto the soul under conviction.

Calvin's commentary on v. 5 is the great Reformation engagement. Calvin will not soften "the Lord has become like an enemy." He insists on its full force. But he reads it through Hebrews 12:6 ("the Lord disciplines the one he loves") — the appearance of enmity is the deepest mode of love. Calvin: "The faithful do not pretend that God is gentle when he is severe; they confess he has come as their enemy, and yet refuse to surrender their faith that he is their Father." Reformed orthodoxy after Calvin (Wollebius, Turretin) integrated this into the doctrine of *opus alienum* — God's "alien work" of judgment, distinguished from his *opus proprium* of grace.

Bonhoeffer in Tegel prison cited Lamentations 2 in correspondence with Eberhard Bethge: the cross of Christ is the place where God himself becomes the enemy of his own beloved Son, and so absorbs into himself the contradiction Lamentations 2 names but does not resolve. Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* II/2 develops a related move: God's "no" against sin is taken up into Christ, so that the wrath Lam 2 describes is now refracted through the cross.

Walter Brueggemann's *The Prophetic Imagination* (1978) and *Theology of the Old Testament* (1997) read Lam 2's accusation as the legitimate counter-testimony Israel preserves against its own core testimony of divine love — the canon's structural honesty about God's hiddenness. Kathleen O'Connor reads through trauma theory: the chapter performs the survivor's task of speaking the unspeakable, naming the perpetrator (here, terrifyingly, God) so that healing might begin. Soong-Chan Rah reads the chapter for communities under systemic violence: the accusation against God is, paradoxically, the beginning of theological survival.

### Where the Traditions Converge and Diverge

Both traditions recognize that v. 17 ("the Lord has done what he purposed") and vv. 20-22 (the demand that the Lord see what he has done) cannot be separated without losing the text. Both treat the chapter as a school of honest prayer.

They diverge on resolution. Christian readings tend to read v. 5's "the Lord has become like an enemy" through the cross — Christ becomes the enemy of God on Israel's behalf, exhausting the wrath. Jewish readings, especially after 1945, deliberately refuse this resolution. The chapter for them does not point forward to the cross; it points to Tisha B'Av's silence, where the wound is named and held without being closed. Daniel Boyarin and Marc Saperstein have argued that this interpretive difference is a difference about whether suffering is, in the end, *redemptive* — and that the Christian instinct to make it so risks instrumentalizing the very catastrophe Lamentations refuses to instrumentalize.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author's vision is not protest atheism. It is something more disquieting: faith in the destroyer. The poet does not deny God; he demands God see. The grammar of vv. 20-22 — imperative, second-person, addressed to YHWH — presupposes a covenant partner who can be summoned. The chapter is theologically extreme but not theologically nihilist.

Two genuine tensions emerge.

**Tension 1: Can God be the enemy of his own people?** The chapter says yes (vv. 4-5), and qualifies (the comparison is *like* an enemy), and confesses (v. 17), and accuses (vv. 20-22).

- *Catholic* (CCC 209-210, 304-305): Divine providence works through, but is not exhausted by, instances of judgment; God's apparent enmity is, in the deeper telos, his fidelity. Aquinas (*Summa* I.21) distinguishes God's antecedent and consequent will — the destruction is God's consequent will responding to human sin, not his antecedent will for creation.
- *Reformed* (Calvin, Edwards): Yes — and this is the most important thing about God to keep before us. God's wrath against sin is real and present; his love does not dilute his justice. Calvin's reading of v. 5 is the classical reformed text on divine *opus alienum*. Edwards's "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" reads in this lineage.
- *Lutheran*: God hides his love under the form of wrath (*Deus absconditus*). Luther argued from passages like Lam 2 that to know God truly is to know the God who slays before he makes alive (1 Sam 2:6). The cross is the final disclosure that the apparently enemy-God was always the Father.
- *Anabaptist / Mennonite* (Yoder, Stanley Hauerwas): More uncomfortable with the divine-warrior tradition; tends to read v. 5 against the grain — the chapter is preserved precisely so that we understand the violence as failure, not divine method.
- *Jewish — Eikhah Rabbah and the post-Holocaust voices*: Yes, *and the canon preserves this against any theology that would normalize it*. The chapter is permission to accuse, not invitation to resignation.
- *Critical (Westermann, Linafelt)*: The text holds the contradiction without resolving it. Its theological achievement is the holding.

**Steelmanning the position I find least intuitive**: the Lutheran reading of God's hiddenness under wrath. It is tempting to dismiss this as paradox-mongering. But Luther is responding to something real in the text — the chapter's refusal to choose between "the LORD is faithful" and "the LORD has destroyed." Luther sees that anything less than the paradox falsifies the witness. To soften v. 5 is to lose the chapter; to absolutize v. 5 is to leave the canon. The hiddenness doctrine names this without dissolving it.

**Where this leaves us**: The chapter forbids two opposite consolations: the consolation that minimizes the destruction (it wasn't really God; it was just history) and the consolation that absolutizes it (whatever God does is good by definition). The reader must dwell with a text that says God did this, and that the doing is not the last word.

**Tension 2: What does the demand "Look, O LORD, and see!" (v. 20) presuppose?**

- *Catholic / Orthodox*: Prayer itself is communion; even accusatory prayer is communion. The chapter is liturgically honest; the wound is held within the relationship.
- *Reformed*: The demand presupposes the covenant; an unbeliever does not demand God see, because she does not expect God to be present. The accusation is an act of faith.
- *Process theology* (John Cobb, David Ray Griffin): The demand makes sense because God is genuinely affected — God *can* see, God *can* be moved. The chapter implies a God who is responsive, not impassible.
- *Jewish (Heschel)*: The demand is the fulfillment of biblical faith — God's *pathos* requires Israel's responding *pathos*; the prophets and lamenters speak back because the relationship requires it.
- *Critical*: The demand presupposes nothing; it performs grief in the inherited liturgical idiom because that is what the genre requires.

**Where this leaves us**: To pray "Look, O LORD, and see!" is to confess the destroyer is also the addressee. This is the chapter's most demanding theological act.

The unresolved question the author leaves: *What kind of love is preserved in addressing God after God has done this?* Not love in the soft sense — not affection, not warmth. Some other kind of love that can survive its addressee being its destroyer. The chapter does not name what that love is. The next chapter will gesture toward it. But here, at the end of chapter 2, the question is left open.

## The Thread

- **Exodus 15:1-18** — The Song of the Sea celebrates the LORD as warrior who destroys the enemy. Lamentations 2 takes the same divine warrior imagery and inverts every polarity: the right hand that delivered (Ex 15:6) now destroys (Lam 2:3-4); the festival day of Passover is now the festival day of slaughter (Lam 2:22). Read together to feel the canonical reversal.
- **Deuteronomy 28:53-57** — The covenant curses describe siege famine and the unspeakable acts they produce. Lamentations 2:20 asks if the curse has now actualized in Jerusalem. The canon embeds the prediction and the lament back to back.
- **Psalm 22:6-8** — "All who see me mock me; they wag their heads." Lamentations 2:15 uses the same vocabulary; both texts will be quoted at the crucifixion (Mark 15:29). When the Gospels show the passers-by mocking Jesus on the cross, the language is doing double duty: Christ as the suffering psalmist and as the lamenting city.
- **Luke 19:41-44; Luke 23:28-31** — Jesus weeps over Jerusalem in language saturated with Lamentations, and at the cross he tells the daughters of Jerusalem to weep for themselves and their children. The Lukan Jesus carries Lam 2 in his speech. When you reach Luke 23, remember Lam 2:11-12 — children fainting in the streets — and the connection will rearrange the passion.
