# Jeremiah 45: A Personal Oracle to Baruch — The Scribe's Colophon

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political situation.** Jeremiah 45 is a personal oracle delivered to Baruch ben Neriah, Jeremiah's scribe, dated to the **fourth year of Jehoiakim son of Josiah, king of Judah** (45:1) — that is, **605 BC**, the same year as the dictation of the first scroll in chapter 36 and the same year as Nebuchadnezzar's victory over Egypt at Carchemish (described in the very next chapter, 46:2). The chapter is profoundly out of chronological sequence: the events around it in the canonical (Masoretic) order describe the post-587 BC chaos in Judah and Egypt (chs. 40–44), yet 45 reaches back two decades to the great year of imperial transition. The redactors chose to place it here deliberately, as a literary bridge between the biographical narrative of Jeremiah's life (chs. 26–44) and the Oracles Against the Nations (chs. 46–51) that follow. In the Masoretic text it serves as the **scribe's signature** before the OAN; in the LXX, where the OAN are inserted at 25:13, ch. 45 is positioned differently still, a fact that matters for any account of how the book was assembled.

**Material culture.** The chapter's setting is scribal. Baruch ben Neriah belongs to a known scribal family: a clay bulla (seal impression) reading *lbrkyhw bn nryhw hspr* — "belonging to Berekhyahu son of Neriyahu the scribe" — surfaced on the antiquities market in the 1970s, and a second purportedly bearing his fingerprint surfaced later. Their authenticity has been contested (Yuval Goren and Christopher Rollston have raised forgery concerns; others, including Hershel Shanks, have defended at least the first), but the figure himself is independently mentioned in 32:12, 36:4, 43:3, and 51:59 — the clearest extra-prophet biographical figure in Jeremiah. The literary culture surrounding him is well-attested: the **scribal training tradition** of late-Iron-Age Judah, the *bet sopher* milieu, the use of Egyptian-style papyrus scrolls (cf. Jer 36 itself, where Jehoiakim slices and burns the scroll column by column with a *taʿar ha-sopher*, a scribe's knife) attested by carbonized papyri at Wadi Murabbaʿat and Ketef Hinnom. Karel van der Toorn's *Scribal Culture and the Making of the Hebrew Bible* (Harvard, 2007) reads ch. 45 as Baruch's *colophon* — the scribal signature appended to the dictated work, marking authorship and authority.

**Authorship and dating.** The fourth-year-of-Jehoiakim dating is shared with chs. 25, 36, and 46:2, all of which cluster around 605 BC. Carroll (OTL) treats the chapter as a late editorial creation projecting back; Lundbom (Anchor) defends Jeremianic authenticity, reading the oracle as part of the Urrolle-stratum (the original scroll). Holladay (Hermeneia) sees a small Baruch-narrative source absorbed into the book by the deuteronomistic redactor. McKane (ICC) emphasizes the redactional placement question: why is 605 BC material placed here, after the Mizpah massacre and the flight to Egypt? The most persuasive answer is that the redactor needed Baruch's vocational oracle to function as an ending to the prose biographical material before opening the OAN with the same year's other great event, Carchemish.

**Archaeological and textual evidence.** The bullae of Baruch (whatever their authenticity status) are joined by undisputed evidence for the scribal-family network of late Iron II Judah: the **City of David bullae cache** including a seal of Gemaryahu son of Shaphan (cf. Jer 36:10), and the **Yehukal son of Shelemiah bulla** (cf. Jer 37:3). For the dating, the **Babylonian Chronicle (BM 21946)**, lines 1–8 of the obverse, places Nebuchadnezzar's accession and the Carchemish campaign in the same season referenced in 45:1 — 605 BC, *Tammuz* (June/July).

**Key terms.** *Sopher* (סוֹפֵר, "scribe"), here applied indirectly through Baruch's writing function in v.1; *yagaʿti be-anḥati* (יָגַעְתִּי בְּאַנְחָתִי, "I am weary in my groaning," 45:3); *gedolot* (גְּדֹלוֹת, "great things," 45:5) — the substantive that names what Baruch is told *not* to seek; *shalal* (שָׁלָל, "spoil, plunder"), the metaphor in v.5 — life as *prize of war*, what one carries off from a defeated city.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is a single, brief, exquisitely compact prophetic oracle, only five verses long, structured as a quoted complaint (Baruch's lament in v.3) followed by a divine reply (vv.4–5). Its genre is the **personal oracle** or *Heilsorakel* — comparable in scale and tone to the personal oracles to Ebed-melech (39:15–18) and to Jeremiah himself in his confessions (e.g., 15:19–21). The oracle's literary logic is question-and-answer: Baruch's "Woe is me!" is met with YHWH's stunning counter-question, *And do you seek great things for yourself?*

The chapter's placement is the great literary fact. Following the Egypt-flight narrative (chs. 42–44) and immediately preceding the OAN (chs. 46–51), it functions as what Robert Carroll calls a "redactional hinge." Karel van der Toorn's reading of it as a *colophon* — the scribe's authenticating sign-off — finds support in Mesopotamian and Egyptian parallels, where colophons name the scribe and date the work. The structural placement of the same date (605 BC, fourth year of Jehoiakim) at 25:1, 36:1, 45:1, and 46:2 forms an inclusio around the prose material. The MT-LXX divergence is severe: in the LXX the OAN sit at 25:13ff. and ch. 45 is in a different position, and the order of nations in the OAN is itself different. Tov (*Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible*) and J.G. Janzen (*Studies in the Text of Jeremiah*) treat this as the most important text-critical fact in the entire book — the LXX likely preserves an earlier, shorter Hebrew Vorlage; the MT represents an expanded, rearranged edition.

The verse "Cursed is he who does the work of the LORD with slackness" (48:10), positioned just three chapters later, plays against 45:5's "do not seek great things for yourself," producing a tension within the OAN section about ambition and faithfulness. The intra-canonical contrast is itself a literary device.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Place in the canonical arc.** Ch. 45 sits at the junction between the prophet's biographical narrative — the long, painful descent from Josiah's reform through the destruction of Jerusalem and the exile to Egypt — and the universal vision of the OAN, where YHWH's word goes out to the nations. In that sense, it is the bridge from particular to universal, from one prophet's life to the fate of empires. Its theme — the renunciation of ambition in the time of disaster — echoes through the wisdom tradition (Qoheleth on vanity), through the suffering-servant strand (Isa 53), and forward into the apostolic tradition (Phil 2:5–11, the kenosis of Christ).

**Jewish trajectory.** The rabbinic reception of Baruch is rich. *Bavli Megillah* 14b counts seven prophetesses but also discusses the prophets, and the Talmud's reception of Baruch as a near-prophetic figure intensifies in the apocalypses bearing his name (1 Baruch in the LXX, 2 Baruch / Syriac Apocalypse, 3 Baruch / Greek Apocalypse — all post-70 AD pseudepigrapha taking Baruch as the spokesman of Jewish lament after the Temple's destruction). Sirach 49:6–7 names Jeremiah among the great worthies but is silent on Baruch. *Targum Jonathan* on 45:5 paraphrases "do not seek great things for yourself" as a warning against priestly or prophetic office for one not called to it — a pastoral reading. **Rashi** reads "great things" as wealth and honor, glossing the verse against the backdrop of the imminent disaster. **Radak** reads it more existentially: in a time when YHWH is uprooting his own planting (v.4), no individual ambition is appropriate. **Abrabanel** treats the *life as plunder* phrase as a promise of bare survival — Baruch will live, but only that.

**Christian trajectory.** **Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah*** include a lost or fragmentary engagement with this chapter (Origen wrote 45 homilies on Jeremiah; only 20 survive in Greek and 14 in Jerome's Latin translation, and ch. 45 is not directly preserved). **Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah*** treats the oracle as a model of clerical humility, pressing the *seek not great things* against the ambition of bishops. **Aquinas** does not comment on Jeremiah at length but the principle is folded into his treatment of *vana gloria* (*ST* II-II.132) as an acquired vice. **Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah***, characteristically, reads v.5 as Baruch's lesson in providence: the man who knows what God is doing must measure his ambitions by it, and in a season of demolition the proper posture is renunciation and prayer for survival. **Duhm** treated the oracle as authentically Jeremianic, one of the few scraps of pre-redactional Baruch material; **Mowinckel** assigned it to source B (the Baruch biographical source). **Holladay** and **Lundbom** broadly agree with Mowinckel. **Brueggemann** (*Theology of the OT*; *Commentary on Jeremiah*) reads the oracle as Jeremiah's rejection of the success-narrative of the Davidic monarchy and an embrace of the exile-as-vocation.

The most famous modern reception comes from **Dietrich Bonhoeffer**. In his **letter to Eberhard Bethge from Tegel prison, dated 21 August 1944**, Bonhoeffer writes: "Some time ago you wrote that one should not seek great things for oneself, but should always seek the will of God for one's life. I'm not entirely sure that this is right, for greater things are sometimes asked of us than we ourselves want to receive. The point is to know God's will and to follow it." Bonhoeffer's reading is more dialectical than the standard "renounce ambition" gloss: he hears Jer 45 as forming a believer who *does not* seek personal advancement *yet may receive vocational greatness*. This is the kenotic-vocational reading.

**Where the trajectories diverge.** The Christian reception generally folds 45:5 into a doctrine of vocation: do not seek great things, *and* be ready for what God assigns. The Jewish reception (Rashi, Radak, Abrabanel) reads it more austerely as an oracle of disaster — Baruch is to scale his hopes to the catastrophe. The critical reception (Carroll, McKane) reads it as a redactor's literary flourish — a manufactured colophon. The post-Holocaust Jewish reception (Wiesel, Fackenheim's *Mitzvah 614*, "the survivor is the witness") returns to *life as prize of war* with new force: in the hour of the camps, mere survival was the testimony.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Ambition under disaster — is "Seek not great things" a permanent vocation or a season-specific instruction?**

The verse forms a small canon-within-the-canon for Christian reflection on ambition. **Catholic** moral theology folds it into the *evangelical counsel of humility* (CCC 2540 on envy and ambition; the religious counsel as a path of life that takes 45:5 as its rule). The Catholic reading allows that the apostolic and episcopal vocations are great, but that they are received, not sought; cf. *Lumen Gentium* 18 on episcopal succession. **Reformed** thought (Calvin, *Institutes* 3.10 on the use of the present life) reads ambition under providence: the believer seeks great things for the kingdom, not for the self, and the test of one's ambition is whether it survives the dismantling that v.4 describes. **Lutheran** vocational theology, especially in the *Two Kingdoms* tradition, reads "seek not great things" against the medieval double-tier ethic — every Christian is in a vocation, and the *Stand* (estate) is given, not chosen for advancement. **Wesleyan** spirituality has a strand of *holy ambition* — Wesley's *Plain Account of Christian Perfection* commends earnest pursuit of sanctification — that sits in tension with 45:5; the Wesleyan steelman is that sanctification *is* the greatness God wills, so to seek it is not selfish ambition. **Anabaptist** tradition (Yoder, Hauerwas) reads the verse as a warrant for kenotic simplicity: the church's vocation is the small, faithful witness, not imperial scale.

The least intuitive position to most contemporary readers is the Wesleyan one. *Steelman:* the verse forbids great things *for yourself*; it does not forbid great things *of God in you*. A faithful holy ambition is not the egoism rebuked here but the vocational drive that animates a Wilberforce or a Bonhoeffer himself, seeking abolition or martyrdom not for personal renown but as the form their obedience takes. **Where this leaves us:** the verse is regulative of motive (whose glory? whose advancement?) rather than scale (small or large). The renunciation 45:5 demands is of self-aggrandizement, not of vocational scope.

**Tension 2: Life as prize of war — minimal salvation, or the deepest soteriology?**

The promise *I will give you your life as a prize of war (shalal)* (45:5) is austere. It is not deliverance, restoration, or vindication. It is the soldier's sole recompense after the battle is lost: he carries off his life as the only loot. **Catholic** sacramental theology can incorporate this as the truth that *survival itself is grace*, anticipating the doctrine that being-in-existence is a continuing gift; cf. Aquinas on *creatio continua*. **Reformed** thought, especially in Kuyper's doctrine of *common grace*, reads bare preservation as a real divine gift even short of saving grace; the providential keeping of any life through disaster is theologically meaningful. **Jewish** halakhic tradition has an even sharper resource: *pikuach nefesh* (the preservation of life) overrides nearly every other commandment, on the principle that life-itself is the ground of all further obedience. **Post-Holocaust** Jewish theology — Wiesel, Fackenheim's *Mitzvah 614* ("the survivor is the witness"), Greenberg — has read *life as plunder* with awful contemporary force: the survivor is not vindicated, not restored, only kept; and that keeping is itself the testimony. **Process and liberation** theologies will sometimes find this minimalism inadequate — God owes the suffering more than mere survival — but the steelman of 45:5 is that the alternative is annihilation, and life-against-death is itself an asymmetric victory.

The least intuitive position is the post-Holocaust one — that mere survival, without restoration, is the deepest divine gift. *Steelman:* the camp survivor who lives is not a footnote to redemption but the form redemption took in that hour. To demand more from God than 45:5 offers — to demand vindication, restoration, a tidy theodicy — is to fail to honor what was actually given. **Where this leaves us:** the verse de-romanticizes salvation. Sometimes the gift is small, late, and bare. To receive *life as prize of war* is to receive everything and to know it is everything.

**Closing unresolved question.** If the disaster Baruch lived through was the demolition of the planting and building of YHWH's own work (45:4) — that is, if even God's own projects can be uprooted — what does that mean for any communal endeavor today that calls itself God's work? Is the proper posture toward our ecclesial, political, or cultural building always also one of held-loose, ready-to-let-go anticipation that what is built may be broken down by the same hand that built it?

## The Thread

The personal oracle to Baruch resonates outward along several lines. *Seek not great things for yourself* anticipates the kenotic logic of **Phil 2:5–11**, where Christ does not grasp at equality with God; James 4:13–17 on presumptuous planning; and Mt 6:33 on seeking first the kingdom rather than the things. *Life as prize of war* has its closest canonical companion in **Lam 3:22–24** ("his mercies never come to an end"), where the prophet in the ruins of Jerusalem still finds in mere preservation the ground of hope. The chapter's location, as a hinge between particular biography and universal OAN, prefigures the apostolic move from the particular Jewish people to the universal mission of the nations (**Acts 1:8**, **Rom 1–11**), and the scribal-vocational vignette of Baruch finds its NT echo in **Tertius's signature** at Rom 16:22 — the scribe of Paul's epistle, whose name is preserved precisely because the scribal vocation, faithfully exercised in obscurity, is itself a form of "life as prize of war."
