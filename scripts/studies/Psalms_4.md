# Psalm 4: Evening Trust and the Light of His Face

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Psalm 4 carries the first **musical superscription** in the Psalter: *lamenatzeach binginot mizmor le-david* — "to the choirmaster: with stringed instruments. A Psalm of David." Three of these four elements are first occurrences. *Lamenatzeach* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ) — "to the choirmaster" or "for the music director" or, on Mowinckel's reading, "for the cultic-prophetic enactment" — opens fifty-five psalms and is one of the most contested superscription terms. The traditional translation follows the LXX *eis to telos* ("unto the end") via the Latin *in finem*, but most modern critical scholars (following the Pi'el participle of *natzach*, "to lead, to direct, to be foreman") render it as a guild-instruction to the temple's chief musician (cf. 1 Chr 15:21). *Binginot* (בִּנְגִינוֹת) — "with stringed instruments" — appears in the superscriptions of Pss 4, 6, 54, 55, 67, 76 and in Hab 3:19. The Hebrew root *nagan* refers to plucking strings; the *neginot* are the lyre (*kinnor*) and the harp (*nevel*) of the Levitical singers, instruments whose archaeological reconstruction has been aided by Egyptian and Mesopotamian iconography and by the discovery of the Megiddo and Beth Shean lyre depictions. *Mizmor* (מִזְמוֹר) — "psalm," from *zamar*, "to make music" — is the most common Psalter genre-label, occurring in fifty-seven superscriptions. *Le-david* — "of/for/concerning David" — places the psalm in the Davidic collection.

Form-critically, Psalm 4 has been classified differently by different scholars. **Hermann Gunkel** in his *Einleitung* (1933) read it as an **individual lament**, paired with Pss 5, 6, 7. **Sigmund Mowinckel** treated it as an **individual prayer of trust** with cultic associations — perhaps a temple incubation ritual (sleeping in the sanctuary precincts to receive divine revelation, well-attested in ANE religion). **Claus Westermann** in *Praise and Lament in the Psalms* refined the category as a **psalm of trust** — laments often pivot to trust at a *Stimmungsumschwung* (mood-shift), but Ps 4 leads with the trust note (the lament elements are subordinated to the dominant key of confidence). Hossfeld and Zenger in the Hermeneia commentary classify it as a **psalm of confidence with lament elements**. Goldingay in his *Baker* commentary treats it as a **prayer in the evening** — the situational genre matters more than the formal classification.

Date and *Sitz im Leben*: The presence of the musical superscription places the psalm firmly in **temple-cult use** — it was sung in the second temple and almost certainly inherited from the first temple's Levitical liturgy. Linguistically the psalm is classical Hebrew without late features, suggesting pre-exilic composition that was preserved and transmitted in the cultic repertoire. The *Sitz im Leben* most defensible: an **evening sacrifice** (the *minchat erev*, the late afternoon grain-offering, paired with the morning *tamid*; cf. Exod 29:38-42; 1 Kgs 18:36) and the moment of personal preparation for sleep in the courts of the temple or at home. Psalm 3 is the morning prayer; Psalm 4 is its evening counterpart. The **morning/evening pair** structure is one of the editor's clearest signals — Pss 3-4 are the Psalter's first explicit liturgical-temporal frame.

The material culture: *neginot* are stringed instruments, plucked by the Levitical singers in the temple courts (1 Chr 25:1-7 lists their guilds). The "right sacrifices" (*zivchei tzedek*, 4:5) are the prescribed temple sacrifices offered with proper priestly procedure — the morning and evening *tamid*, the personal *zevach shelamim* (peace offering), the *minchah* grain offering. The "lift up the light of your face" (4:6) echoes the priestly blessing of Num 6:25 (*ya'er Yhwh panav elekha*) — the same Aaronic benediction whose silver scrolls were found in Ketef Hinnom in 1979, dated to the late seventh century BCE, the oldest extant biblical text. ANE parallels: Mesopotamian *šigû* lament-prayers and Egyptian morning/evening hymns to the sun-god are formally similar; the **Mesopotamian incubation rituals** in which a worshipper sleeps in the sanctuary to receive revelation provide a possible cultic backdrop for v. 8's trust-in-sleep.

Hebrew terms controlling the psalm:

- **בָּטַח** (*batach*, 4:5) — "trust." The controlling theological word of the psalm. Distinct from *aman* (faith-as-firmness) and *qavah* (hope-as-waiting), *batach* is the trust of leaning on a support — the verb of resting one's weight on something that holds. In v. 5 the call is to *u-vitchu* — and trust — paired with right sacrifice as the proper response to Yahweh's covenantal presence.
- **רִגְזוּ** (*rigzu*, 4:4) — "be angry / tremble / be agitated." The Hebrew root *ragaz* covers a range from righteous anger to physical trembling to fear-quaking. The LXX translates *orgizesthe* ("be angry"), which is what Paul cites in **Eph 4:26** ("be angry and do not sin"). Many modern critical translations render the Hebrew as "tremble" — i.e., be in awe, recognize the gravity of your standing before God. **James Kugel** and **John Goldingay** both note the divergence and treat both readings as defensible — the Hebrew is genuinely ambiguous, and the LXX/Pauline reading is one legitimate construal of the Hebrew, not a translation error.
- **חָסִיד** (*chasid*, 4:3) — "godly / faithful / loyal one." From *chesed* (covenant loyalty). The *chasid* is one bound to Yahweh by covenant faithfulness. Later Jewish tradition turned this term into a movement name (*Hasidim*, the medieval German Pietists; modern *Chasidism*).
- **אוֹר פָּנֶיךָ** (*or panekha*, 4:6) — "the light of your face." Direct echo of the priestly blessing in Num 6:25. To have Yahweh's face turned toward you, illuminated, was the supreme blessing of presence — its inverse, the *hester panim* (hiding of the face), the supreme catastrophe.
- **לְבָטַח** (*levetach*, 4:8) — "in safety / in trust." The closing word of the psalm, from the same root as *batach*. The poetic-structural inclusion: trust spoken in v. 5 becomes the dwelling-condition in v. 8.

Textual evidence: Psalm 4 is preserved in **4QPsa** at Qumran, in MT-aligned form. The LXX renders the psalm with *neginot* as *en hymnois* ("with hymns") rather than translating the specific stringed-instrument reference, and translates *rigzu* as *orgizesthe* (anger), preserving the reading Paul will quote.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Psalm 4 is eight verses divided by two *selah* markers into roughly three movements. The shape is debated; the most defensible division:

- **v. 1** — opening petition / invocation
- **vv. 2-3** — address to the *bnei ish* (the men of standing, the social adversaries) — closes with *selah*
- **vv. 4-5** — exhortation to the same audience: anger-without-sin, ponder in silence, offer right sacrifice, trust — closes with *selah*
- **vv. 6-8** — pivot to confession of joy and trust; closing benediction

The *bnei ish* of v. 2 (literally "sons of men" — but here the Hebrew likely refers to *men of high standing* rather than humanity generically; cf. Ps 49:2 where *bnei ish* are paired with *bnei adam* and likely mean the wealthy versus the common) suggests that the psalm's social setting is a conflict between the speaker and powerful adversaries who have turned his *kavod* (honor) into *kelimmah* (shame). The accusation is that they "love vain words" (*rik*, emptiness) and "seek after lies" (*kazav*) — slandering the speaker's reputation. The psalm therefore moves from petition to *rebuke-and-instruction* of these adversaries (vv. 2-5) before turning to the speaker's own confession of trust (vv. 6-8).

Robert Lowth's parallelism: synonymous in the doublet of v. 2 ("how long shall my honor be turned into shame / how long will you love vain words"); antithetic in vv. 6-7 (the *many* who ask "who will show us some good?" against the speaker who has joy more than abundant grain and wine); synthetic-staircase in vv. 4-5. James Kugel and Robert Alter both highlight the psalm's compactness: in eight verses Ps 4 contains a petition, a rebuke, an exhortation, a confession, and a benediction — a five-part structure compressed into thirty-five Hebrew lines.

The closing verse (4:8) — *be-shalom yachdav eshkevah ve'ishan ki attah Yhwh levadad la-vetach toshiveni* — is one of the most beloved verses in the entire Psalter for its prosodic balance: *eshkevah ve'ishan* ("I will lie down and sleep"), *be-shalom yachdav* ("in peace altogether"), *attah levadad* ("you alone"), *la-vetach toshiveni* ("you make me dwell in safety"). The Hebrew *levadad* — "alone" or "in solitude" — is theologically loaded: Yahweh *alone* makes the speaker dwell in safety, contrasted with the many adversaries of v. 2. The contrast is the whole point: many threaten, *one* protects.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Placement

Psalm 4 follows Ps 3 and pairs with it as morning/evening. The editorial placement extends Pss 1-2's programmatic frame: the anointed king of Pss 1-2 has become the persecuted king of Ps 3, and now in Ps 4 he closes the day in trust. Books I-III of the Psalter are structured around individual Davidic prayer; Ps 4 is the second voice in that sequence. Brueggemann's orientation/disorientation/reorientation grid: Ps 4 has lament elements but the dominant key is reorientation-trust — the speaker has come through enough to confess that Yahweh's joy outweighs the world's grain and wine.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve Ps 4 with the musical superscription intact in **4QPsa** — confirming the superscription's antiquity. **Midrash Tehillim** on Ps 4 expands v. 2's *bnei ish* into "the sons of Eli" who slandered David, and v. 4's "be angry, do not sin" is read as instruction to the body's evil inclination (*yetzer ha-ra*) — a paradigmatic rabbinic move that turns a psychological-ethical instruction into a piece of moral psychology. **Rashi** (eleventh century) reads vv. 2-5 as direct address to David's enemies during the Saulide pursuit. **Ibn Ezra** treats the psalm as a general instruction to those who slander the righteous. **Radak (David Kimhi)** reads v. 8 as a paradigmatic confession of trust. **Nachmanides** reads v. 4's "ponder in your own hearts on your beds" as the foundational verse for **kriat shema al ha-mitah**, the bedtime *Shema*. **Avivah Zornberg's** treatment of bedtime piety in Jewish tradition draws on Ps 4 as the textual ground of the practice.

Liturgically, Psalm 4 is the **central psalm of kriat shema al ha-mitah** — the bedtime *Shema* — recited in both Sephardic and Ashkenazi rites before sleep, alongside Ps 3 (the pairing inverted from the cosmic order — both are recited at bedtime, even though Ps 3 is the morning psalm in its own internal logic). The verse "in peace I will both lie down and sleep" (4:8) is one of the most frequently recited verses in Jewish daily liturgy. Psalm 4 is **not** part of the *Shir Shel Yom* daily cycle (Sun=24, Mon=48, Tue=82, Wed=94, Thu=81, Fri=93, Sat=92).

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

**Augustine's** *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 4 is one of his most pastorally tender readings — he treats v. 4 ("be angry and do not sin") as the foundation of Christian moral psychology, and v. 8 as the soul's evening confession to its Lord. **Athanasius's** *Letter to Marcellinus* recommends Ps 4 for the soul resting in faith. **Cassiodorus** systematizes. **Aquinas** in his *Postilla* reads it fourfold.

**Calvin's** 1557 commentary makes Psalm 4 — and especially v. 8 — paradigmatic of "rest in God." The *levadad* ("alone") of v. 8 became central to Calvin's doctrine of divine sufficiency: only Yahweh, alone, makes the believer dwell in safety. Calvin treats Ps 4:8 as the model verse for the Christian's preparation for sleep and for death. **Luther's** *Operationes in Psalmos* treats v. 4's "be angry and do not sin" as Pauline-Christian instruction (citing Eph 4:26 directly) and v. 8 as the believer's rest in justification. **Bonhoeffer** in *Psalms: The Prayer Book of the Bible* makes Pss 3-4 his paradigmatic morning-evening prayer pairing.

Liturgically, **Psalm 4 is one of the four psalms of Christian compline** in the Roman Breviary and the Divine Office (alongside Pss 31, 91, and 134). Compline — the last office of the day, prayed before sleep — has used Psalm 4 since at least the time of Benedict (whose *Rule*, chapter 18, prescribes it). Anglican Evensong and the Book of Common Prayer continue this use. For fifteen hundred years the Christian church has closed the day with Psalm 4. **Patrick Henry Reardon** (*Christ in the Psalms*, Eastern Orthodox) treats the psalm as the church's prayer at the close of life as well as the close of the day.

The historical-critical tradition (**Gunkel, Mowinckel, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate, Allen, Wilson**) has classified the psalm formally and traced its cultic use. **Brueggemann** treats it as a psalm of trust and emphasizes the *bnei ish* as the social context of distress. **Beth Tanner**, **Patrick Miller**, and **William P. Brown** all build on the canonical-shaping reading. **Susan Gillingham's** reception-historical work documents the deep liturgical embedding of Pss 3-4 in both Jewish and Christian bedtime traditions.

### NT Use

**Ephesians 4:26** quotes Ps 4:4 (LXX) directly: *orgizesthe kai me hamartanete* — "be angry and do not sin." The Pauline citation reads the verse as instruction in righteous anger that does not pass into sin. The Hebrew *rigzu* can mean "tremble," and many modern translations prefer that reading; but the LXX's *orgizesthe* (anger) is a defensible construal of the Hebrew, and Paul has canonized the anger-reading for Christian readers. The Ephesians passage therefore inflects how Christian readers approach the verse — even those who prefer the "tremble" reading must reckon with the canonical Pauline appropriation.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author's vision is that the speaker, surrounded by social adversaries who slander his honor, addresses both Yahweh (vv. 1, 6-8) and the adversaries themselves (vv. 2-5) — and the prayer's trajectory is from grievance to confidence to evening rest. The closing image (4:8) is one of the most powerful in the Psalter: the believer lying down to sleep, *in peace*, because Yahweh *alone* — without ally, without backup — is sufficient to keep him.

### Tension One: "Be Angry and Do Not Sin" — The Hebrew/Greek Reading Divergence

The verb in 4:4 is *rigzu*. The LXX reads it as *orgizesthe* (be angry). The Hebrew root *ragaz* can mean "be angry," "tremble," "quake," "be agitated." **Paul cites the LXX reading in Eph 4:26**. Most modern critical translations of the Hebrew prefer "tremble." Which reading is right?

- **LXX / Pauline / patristic / Reformation tradition**: "Be angry, and do not sin." Anger is a permitted emotion; the moral question is whether anger leads to sinful action. Augustine, Luther, and Calvin all read the verse this way and treat it as foundational instruction in righteous anger. Eph 4:26's canonical authority anchors this reading for Christian readers.
- **Hebrew-philological / modern critical reading**: "Tremble, and do not sin." The exhortation is to *awe-trembling* before Yahweh as the deterrent from sin. Robert Alter (*The Book of Psalms*, 2007), John Goldingay (*Baker* commentary), and many modern critical commentators favor this reading on the grounds that the broader context (vv. 4-5: "ponder in your hearts on your beds, and be silent / offer right sacrifices") fits awe-piety better than anger-management.
- **James Kugel's reading**: the Hebrew ambiguity is itself the point. The Hebrew reader hears both — agitation and anger — and the LXX translator made one of two defensible choices. Modern readers should hold both meanings as available.
- **Catholic-canonical reading**: both readings are in the canon — the Hebrew is in the Hebrew Bible, the Greek (and Pauline citation) is in the New Testament. The church reads both, holding them in tension.
- **Jewish reading (Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Radak)**: the Hebrew is "tremble" or "be agitated" — in the sense of recognizing one's standing before Yahweh and ceasing from sin out of awe. The Pauline anger-reading is a Christian appropriation but does not bind the Hebrew text.

**Steelmanning the least-intuitive position** — the Hebrew "tremble" reading. To Christian readers raised on Eph 4:26, the verse *means* "be angry and do not sin" because Paul says so. But the Pauline citation is itself an interpretation of the LXX, which is itself a translation choice. The Hebrew underlying both is *rigzu*. The "tremble" reading is exegetically more probable in the Hebrew context — the verse is followed by "ponder in your hearts on your beds, and be silent," which is the language of contemplation and awe, not of anger-management. The Pauline anger-reading is a legitimate construal of the Hebrew that the LXX preserved and Paul deployed for his own pastoral purposes (to instruct the Ephesian church about handling conflict), but it should not be retrojected onto the original Hebrew as if the Hebrew unambiguously means "anger." The honest reading admits that Eph 4:26 is canonical Christian instruction *that draws on a defensible-but-not-definitive reading of Ps 4:4*.

**Where this leaves us**: For Christian readers, both readings are in the canon. The Hebrew probably means "tremble"; the LXX/Pauline canonical citation means "be angry." Both are spiritually true: trembling-awe before God, and the proper handling of righteous anger that does not pass into sin. The two readings are pastorally complementary even when the philological question is technically disputed.

### Tension Two: Joy More Than Their Grain and Wine vs. The Visible Prosperity of the Wicked

In 4:7 the speaker says: "you have put more joy in my heart than they have when their grain and wine abound." This is a remarkable claim. The *they* are the *bnei ish* of v. 2 — the powerful, the prosperous, those who have visible material abundance. The speaker is socially diminished, slandered, surrounded by enemies. Yet he claims his joy outweighs their wealth. Is this true?

- **Stoic-philosophical reading**: the speaker's claim is the universal sage's claim — that interior peace is greater than external goods. The psalm is a piece of ancient wisdom-literature in this register.
- **Calvin / Reformed reading**: the *joy* is the joy of the Holy Spirit, given by election to those whom God has separated for himself (v. 3). It is real, supernatural, and outweighs material abundance. The believer who has experienced this joy testifies that it is so.
- **Lutheran / pietist reading**: the joy is justification-rooted. The believer who knows himself reconciled to God has a peace the world cannot give and cannot take away.
- **Eastern Orthodox reading**: the joy is the indwelling presence of God — *theosis* in seed-form. The believer participates in divine joy by the Spirit.
- **Jewish reading (Sarna, Levenson)**: the joy is the joy of being among the *chasidim* whom Yahweh has set apart (v. 3). The psalm is not a denial of material goods but a relativization of them — they are not the supreme good; covenantal presence is.
- **Wesleyan reading**: the joy is the inward witness of the Spirit, available to all who trust Yahweh; the psalm is a paradigm of experiential religion.
- **Critical-honest reading (Brueggemann)**: the verse is a *daring claim* made by a socially threatened speaker who has chosen to relativize material abundance in light of covenantal trust. Whether the claim is *true* depends on whether the speaker's experience is genuine, and the psalm offers the testimony as an invitation, not a proof. The honest reader notices both the claim and the social conditions under which it is made — a hunted person *needs* to believe his joy is greater than his enemies' wealth, and this need is part of what the psalm is doing.
- **Skeptical reading**: the verse is the consolation literature of the dispossessed — what people without grain and wine tell themselves to make the loss bearable. This is a real reading, and it must not be evaded.

**Where this leaves us**: The verse is a testimony, not an apologetic argument. The speaker claims his joy outweighs their abundance. The reader is invited to believe the testimony or to dismiss it. The skeptical reading is honest, but it is not the only possible reading: the long history of mystics, monks, and martyrs claiming the same thing — that interior joy in God outweighs material loss — provides corroborative testimony from many cultures and centuries. Brueggemann is right that the verse is daring; the daring is the point.

### Closing Question

If Psalm 4 has been the Christian church's evening psalm for fifteen hundred years and the Jewish bedtime *Shema*'s textual ground for two thousand, what does it mean that we still — in the age of insomnia and chemical sleep — find ourselves needing the same prayer the ancient Levitical singers offered at the close of the temple's day?

## The Thread

- **Numbers 6:24-26** — the priestly Aaronic blessing: "may Yahweh make his face shine upon you and be gracious to you." Ps 4:6 ("lift up the light of your face upon us, O LORD") is the speaker's prayer that the Aaronic blessing's promise be granted to him personally. The Ketef Hinnom silver scrolls (late seventh century BCE, the oldest extant biblical text) preserve the Aaronic blessing — the same blessing Ps 4:6 invokes.
- **Psalm 3** — the morning prayer companion. Pss 3-4 are the Psalter's first explicit morning/evening pair, opening and closing the day in Yahweh's keeping.
- **Ephesians 4:26** — Paul's citation of Ps 4:4 (LXX), inflecting the verse for Christian readers as the foundation of righteous-anger ethics. The seam between Hebrew and Greek readings of *rigzu* is one of the most discussed translation cruxes in the Psalter.
- **Mark 4:35-41** — Jesus asleep in the boat in the storm. The disciples' panic and Jesus' sleep enact Ps 4:8 in the Gospel narrative: "in peace I will both lie down and sleep." The chosen one sleeps because Yahweh alone makes him dwell in safety.
