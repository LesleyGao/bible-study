# Revelation 9: The Plagues That Do Not Convert

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The fifth and sixth trumpets erupt against a Mediterranean backdrop saturated with imperial anxiety. The dominant scholarly position, defended by David E. Aune in his three-volume Word Biblical Commentary, by Craig Koester in the 2014 Anchor Yale volume, and by Richard Bauckham in *The Climax of Prophecy*, places the composition of Revelation in the closing years of Domitian's reign (c. 95–96 AD). The minority early-date hypothesis, defended in different forms by John A. T. Robinson and Kenneth Gentry, locates it in the late 60s, just after Nero's persecution and either before or amid the Jewish War. For chapter 9 the dating debate matters less than it does for chapter 11, but it conditions how one reads the imperial subtext: a Domitianic seer addresses provincial congregations under intensifying pressure from the imperial cult in cities like Pergamum, Smyrna, Ephesus, Sardis, Thyatira, Laodicea, and Philadelphia, where temples to the *divi* and to the living *Sebastos* anchored the civic calendar. Steven Friesen's *Imperial Cults and the Apocalypse of John* documents the cultic infrastructure with archaeological precision; Leonard Thompson cautions that the persecution under Domitian was less a systematic juridical campaign than an atmosphere of social and economic pressure exerted through trade guilds, civic festivals, and the loyalty oath.

The material culture stitched into the chapter is dense. The locust army of 9:1–11 draws on agricultural memory — locust swarms across Asia Minor and the Levant were not literary exotica but an annual terror, and Joel 1–2 had already stylized them into a divine army. But the locusts of Revelation are no longer just insects; they have crowns, faces, hair, breastplates, and the noise of "many chariots with horses rushing into battle" (9:9). Roman military formations stand behind this image. The *lorica segmentata*, the segmented iron breastplate of the legionary, was the most recognizable kit of the imperial army; the cohort drill, the trampling sound of an advancing legion, the iron sound of armor and *gladius* — these were not metaphors for John's audience but ambient experience in a province crisscrossed by *cohortes* and *vexillationes*.

The 200 million cavalry from the Euphrates (9:13–19) gives the imagery a precise geopolitical address. The Euphrates was the eastern frontier of the empire, the only border Rome could not push beyond. Beyond it lay Parthia, the rival empire that had defeated Crassus at Carrhae in 53 BC, that had embarrassed Rome again under Antony, and that under the Arsacids remained the strategic obsession of every Roman emperor from Augustus to Trajan. The Parthians were synonymous with mounted archers and with the cataphract — armored cavalry whose horses bore scale armor. Aune notes that the colors John lists — fire-red, hyacinth-blue, sulfur-yellow — match the dyed leather and metallic finishes of cataphract gear as represented on Roman coins and triumphal reliefs. The fear of a Parthian invasion was already grafted to the *Nero redivivus* legend: stories that the dead emperor would return from the East with Parthian armies circulated through Asia Minor in the very decades surrounding Revelation's composition.

The chapter's most striking proper-name moment occurs in 9:11. The angel of the abyss is named twice: in Hebrew as *Abaddon* (אֲבַדּוֹן), and in Greek as *Apollyon* (Ἀπολλύων). *Abaddon* means "destruction" or "place of destruction" and appears in the Hebrew Bible in Job 26:6, Job 28:22, Psalm 88:11, Proverbs 15:11, and 27:20, where it functions as a poetic synonym for Sheol. *Apollyon* is the masculine participle of *apollymi*, "to destroy," meaning "the Destroyer." Aune, G. B. Caird, and Eugene Boring all note that this is almost certainly a deliberate punning attack on Apollo, whose name the Greeks themselves sometimes etymologized from this same verb (a folk-etymology Aeschylus exploits in *Agamemnon* 1080–82). The polemic is sharp: Augustus had claimed Apollo as patron and even as divine father; Nero had been celebrated as a new Apollo; Domitian patronized Apollo's cult with conspicuous zeal. To name the angel of the abyss "Apollyon" is to identify the imperial patron god as a destroyer rising from the pit.

Three other Greek terms anchor the chapter. *Abyssos* (ἄβυσσος, "bottomless," 9:1, 2, 11) is not classical Greek but Septuagintal — it translates Hebrew *tehom*, the primordial deep of Genesis 1:2, and in Second Temple literature (1 Enoch 10, 18, 21; Jubilees 5) it becomes the prison of fallen Watchers and rebellious spirits. *Basanizō* (βασανίζω, "to torment," 9:5) is the verb for judicial torture, particularly the interrogation of slaves under Roman law — a vocabulary that lands hard on hearers who knew the *quaestio per tormenta* as a procedure of the imperial court. *Pharmakeia* (φαρμακεία, 9:21, "sorceries") encompasses both magical practice and pharmaceutical poisoning, and it indexes a specific cluster of provincial vices that moralists from Pliny to Galen also named.

For the manuscript history, chapter 9 is a textual jewel: P47, the Chester Beatty papyrus dated to the third century, contains Revelation 9:10–17:2. This means our oldest substantial witness to the Apocalypse begins almost exactly at this chapter. Variants in the text of 9:10–21 can therefore be tracked across P47, Codex Sinaiticus (4th c.), Codex Alexandrinus (5th c., the strongest witness for Revelation as a whole), and Codex Ephraemi Rescriptus (5th c.). Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary* notes a small set of meaning-affecting variants in this chapter — the most discussed concerns the verbal forms in 9:5, where some manuscripts read *basanisthōsin* (passive subjunctive) and others *basanisei* (future active), shifting the agency of the torment. None of these alters the chapter's argument, but they confirm that the text was being copied with conservative care from an early stage.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Revelation announces itself simultaneously as *apokalypsis* (1:1), as *prophēteia* (1:3), and as *epistolē* (1:4). The book is therefore a hybrid of three genres: the Jewish apocalypse with its visionary cosmology, the Hebrew prophet's oracle against the nations, and the Pauline circular letter to specific congregations. Chapter 9 is dominated by the first two registers and is heavy with the apocalyptic vocabulary developed across Daniel 7–12, the Book of the Watchers (1 Enoch 1–36), the Animal Apocalypse (1 Enoch 85–90), 4 Ezra, and 2 Baruch.

The fifth trumpet's locust army is constructed by literary fusion. The base layer is Joel 1–2, where the prophet describes a real plague of locusts and then escalates the imagery into a divine army on the day of the LORD. John assumes Joel and intensifies him: the locusts are not just like an army; they are demonic warriors with iron breastplates and chariot-noise. The second layer is the Book of the Watchers. The descent of a "star fallen from heaven" who receives the key to the abyss (9:1) reproduces the Watcher mythos: 1 Enoch 10 narrates the binding of Asael in a pit beneath the desert until the great judgment, and 1 Enoch 18–21 describes a fiery pit reserved for rebellious astral beings. Loren Stuckenbruck's work on Second Temple apocalyptic has been particularly attentive to how Revelation builds on this Watcher tradition, and Bauckham in *The Climax of Prophecy* treats 1 Enoch as the indispensable interpretive grid for Revelation's demonology. The third layer is the Exodus pattern: the locusts of the eighth plague (Exodus 10), the smoke of the burning furnace (Exodus 19:18), the column of darkness (Exodus 10:21–22), all reactivated.

The sixth trumpet brings a different combat register. The four angels bound at the Euphrates and the 200 million horsemen draw less from Daniel and more from the Greco-Roman invasion-from-the-East topos that Adela Yarbro Collins examined in *The Combat Myth in the Book of Revelation* (1976). Yarbro Collins demonstrates that John repeatedly takes pagan combat-myth elements — the dragon, the army from the East, the cosmic battle — and reorients them inside a monotheistic frame. The horsemen of 9:17 with their fire-and-sulfur breath and their lion-headed mounts read like a deliberate inversion of the cataphract triumph: instead of Rome's enemy, they are God's instrument; instead of being kept at bay by imperial garrisons, they are released by an angel.

Structurally the chapter sits inside a tightly engineered septet. Revelation deploys septets recursively: seven letters (chs. 2–3), seven seals (chs. 5–8), seven trumpets (chs. 8–11), seven bowls (chs. 15–16). Each septet has a 4+3 division, with an interlude between the sixth and seventh members. Chapter 9 contains trumpets 5 and 6 (woes 1 and 2, by the announcement of 8:13 and the bookend of 11:14); the interlude follows in chapters 10–11; the seventh trumpet concludes in 11:15–19. This is not casual structure: Bauckham, Beale, and Boring all read the trumpet septet as an intensification of the seal septet — what was a fourth in the seals (6:8) becomes a third in the trumpets (9:15), but with the difference that the targets are no longer impersonal but human and explicitly impenitent.

Two literary devices deserve naming. First, the deliberate withholding of the locusts' actual lethality: they may *torment* but not *kill* (9:5), a restraint that contrasts sharply with the sixth trumpet, where a third of mankind dies. The fifth-and-sixth pairing therefore moves from torment to death. Second, the chapter's grim refrain in 9:20–21, where the survivors of the sixth trumpet "did not repent" — a five-fold catalogue of vices (idolatry, murder, *pharmakeia*, *porneia*, theft) that deliberately recalls the Decalogue inverted. Many scholars (Caird, Boring, Bauckham) read this refrain as the chapter's actual climax: the plagues are not the message, the impenitence is.

A meaning-affecting textual variant deserves a flag: in 9:13 some witnesses read "from the four horns" while others read simply "from the horns" of the golden altar. The longer reading is supported by Sinaiticus and Alexandrinus and is the printed text in NA28 and UBS5; the shorter reading is supported by P47 and a handful of minuscules. The choice subtly shifts whether John is invoking the Tabernacle altar (Exodus 27:2, four horns specified) or a more abstracted altar of prayers.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Chapter 9 lives at the heart of one of the longest interpretive arguments in the history of Christian theology. The four classical schools — preterist, historicist, futurist, and idealist — have read the locusts and the cavalry in radically incompatible ways.

The Patristic period is uneven. Justin Martyr cites Revelation but rarely interprets it in detail. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 5.30) takes the book's symbolism seriously and reads it eschatologically, but he does not produce a sustained chapter-by-chapter commentary. Tertullian quotes Revelation often, with a roughly futurist orientation. Hippolytus of Rome wrote *On Christ and Antichrist* and a now-fragmentary commentary on Daniel that treats Revelation's eschaton as still future. Origen reads Revelation allegorically, and his successor Methodius continues that tradition. The first surviving Latin commentary on Revelation is by Victorinus of Pettau (d. c. 304), who reads the trumpets recapitulatively — they cover the same ground as the seals and the bowls, not a strict sequence. Tyconius the Donatist (late 4th c.) developed a thoroughgoing ecclesial reading: the locusts represent heretics and persecutors of the church across history. Augustine, in *City of God* book 20, absorbed Tyconius's recapitulation and the broadly idealist reading became Western default for a millennium.

The Eastern Orthodox tradition received Revelation slowly and tentatively. The book was disputed in the East well into the fourth century — Eusebius brackets it among the *antilegomena*; the Council of Laodicea (c. 363) excluded it from the canon list. Athanasius's 39th Festal Letter (367) included it, but Revelation has never to this day been read in the Byzantine liturgical lectionary. Andrew of Caesarea's commentary (c. 600) became the standard Greek reading and is broadly recapitulative-symbolic, treating chapter 9 as a vision of demonic activity throughout the church age rather than a future timetable.

The medieval West complicated things. Joachim of Fiore (d. 1202) introduced periodization: the 1260 days, 42 months, and 3.5 years that recur across Revelation became markers of the transition between his three ages (Father, Son, Spirit). Joachim's followers — Spiritual Franciscans, later radical reformers — used these numbers to calculate dates. Chapter 9's "five months" (9:5, 10) and the "hour, day, month, year" of 9:15 fed the same calculative impulse. Bernard of Clairvaux read Revelation devotionally; Aquinas treated it briefly in his commentaries on Paul and the Gospels but produced no full Apocalypse commentary. Nicholas of Lyra in the 14th century gave the trumpets a historicist reading, mapping them to specific historical events from the apostolic period to his own day.

The Reformation produced ambivalence. Luther in the 1522 preface to Revelation famously called it "neither apostolic nor prophetic" and said his spirit could not accommodate the book; he revised the preface in 1530 to a more cautious position, allowing a historicist reading in which the locusts represent the Saracens and the Euphrates cavalry the Turks. Calvin commented on every New Testament book except Revelation. The Reformed mainstream that followed Calvin tended to historicism — Matthew Henry, Jonathan Edwards, and the early Princeton theologians read chapter 9 as a coded history of Islam and the Ottoman threat to Christendom.

Anabaptist apocalypticism produced a darker chapter. The Münster rebellion of 1534–35, under Jan Matthys and Jan van Leiden, read Revelation as a present-tense battle script. The chapter 9 imagery of plagues against the impenitent fed the Münsterite conviction that they were the eschatological remnant.

The 19th century gave us dispensationalism. John Nelson Darby's reading and the Scofield Reference Bible (1909) treated chapter 9 as future warfare during a literal seven-year tribulation. The locusts became literal demonic creatures or, in mid-20th-century popular dispensationalism (Hal Lindsey, Tim LaHaye, John Walvoord), helicopters, attack aircraft, or chemical-weapons platforms; the 200 million cavalry became China's army crossing the Euphrates. The interpretive moves are exegetically strained but the underlying logic is consistent: literal-where-possible, future-where-unfulfilled.

The 20th-century critical mainstream — R. H. Charles in his 1920 ICC, G. B. Caird in his 1966 Black's commentary, G. R. Beasley-Murray in NCB, John Sweet in TPI, Eugene Boring in Interpretation, and Bauckham across his books — read chapter 9 as a symbolic depiction of demonic activity in the present age, refracted through specific anxieties about Roman and Parthian power. Beale's NIGTC develops an eclectic-idealist reading in which the visions are present-and-eschatological at once. Koester's Anchor Yale is the most thorough recent socio-historical commentary, anchoring every image in the material culture of Asia Minor.

Liberation readings approach the chapter differently. Allan Boesak's *Comfort and Protest*, written under apartheid, takes the impenitent of 9:20–21 as a name for the powers that refuse to relinquish their idolatries despite the mounting cost; the locusts and cavalry become the inevitable judgment of structures of injustice. Pablo Richard's *Apocalypse: A People's Commentary* reads from Latin American base communities and identifies the impenitent with imperial powers in every age. Elisabeth Schüssler Fiorenza in *Revelation: Vision of a Just World* presses on the question of whose violence the chapter authorizes.

Feminist criticism is sharper still. Tina Pippin in *Death and Desire* and Catherine Keller in *Apocalypse Now and Then* both push back on a chapter that imagines apocalyptic torment as a theological norm, and both note that the book's gender symbolics — though concentrated in chapter 12 — already shape the violence of chapter 9.

Jewish scholarly engagement has been substantial despite Revelation's exclusion from the Jewish canon. Adela Yarbro Collins's *Crisis and Catharsis* and *The Combat Myth* are the indispensable studies. Daniel Boyarin's work on Christian-Jewish boundaries has shown how Revelation's apocalyptic vocabulary belongs squarely inside Second Temple Jewish discourse. John J. Collins's *The Apocalyptic Imagination* situates Revelation alongside Daniel, 1 Enoch, 4 Ezra, and 2 Baruch, treating it as a Jewish apocalypse written in Christian register. Loren Stuckenbruck's work on the Watchers tradition is essential for chapter 9's demonology.

The traditions converge on one point: chapter 9 is about plagues that fail to convert. They diverge sharply on the timetable. Preterists place the events in the Jewish War. Historicists distribute them across church history. Futurists localize them in a still-coming tribulation. Idealists collapse the timetable entirely.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the seer believe God was doing? Two tensions deserve sustained attention.

**Tension 1: The morality of torment without conversion.** The fifth trumpet authorizes five months of demonic torment of those who lack the seal of God (9:4–5). The sixth trumpet authorizes the killing of a third of humanity (9:15). And then the chapter ends with the haunting refrain that the survivors did not repent (9:20–21). Is the seer commending divine pedagogy that fails, or is he diagnosing human hardness so deep that even apocalyptic violence cannot crack it?

The Catholic tradition (CCC §§676–677, partial-preterist in tendency) reads the imagery as symbolic and treats the impenitence as a soteriological fact about human nature in rebellion: the visions reveal what is already true, that under the conditions of judgment some hearts grow harder. The Mariological emphases that center on chapter 12 do not bear directly on 9, but the broader Catholic frame of grace as both gift and offer-that-can-be-refused holds. Reformed readings, especially those rooted in Calvinist soteriology, read 9:20–21 as a textual demonstration of the bondage of the will: the impenitent do not repent because they cannot, absent regenerating grace. Lutheran readings, inheriting Luther's ambivalence, tend to soften the chapter's force by treating it as proximate-historical (Saracens, Turks) rather than ontological. Dispensationalist readings (Walvoord, LaHaye) take the torment as a literal future event and argue that those who survive have hardened past the possibility of repentance — they are the eschatologically reprobate. This is the position to steelman, because it has the virtue of taking the text's plain force seriously: the survivors do not repent, full stop, and the dispensationalist reading at least faces this with a clear theological account. Eastern Orthodox readings dehistoricize: the torment is the present condition of souls in rebellion, the impenitence the cumulative hardening of *passions*, the chapter a liturgical-contemplative warning rather than a predictive timetable. Critical readings (Caird, Boring, Bauckham) emphasize that John is making a theodicy claim against the Exodus pattern: where the Exodus plagues eventually produced release, the apocalyptic plagues produce only deepening idolatry, and this is the diagnosis of the imperial age.

The liberation reading (Boesak, Richard) presses hardest. The impenitent are not generic humanity but specifically those whose power and wealth are bound up with the structures of empire. They cannot repent because repentance would dismantle them. The plagues do not work because the plagues are not pedagogical at this stage; they are diagnostic.

Where this leaves us: the chapter resists every reading that wants the violence to be redemptive. It is not. The plagues reveal what is, they do not change what is. This is a deeply uncomfortable theological claim, and any reading that softens it has stopped reading the text.

**Tension 2: The status of demonic agency.** The chapter has an angel descending with the key to the abyss, demonic locusts emerging, four bound angels released, an angelic king named Apollyon. Is the seer affirming a literal demonology, deploying it as cultural shorthand, or constructing a theological fiction?

The Catholic magisterial tradition affirms the existence of demonic agents (CCC §§391–395) and reads chapter 9 as consonant with a real ontology of fallen angels, while declining to commit to the chapter's specific imagery as journalistic. Reformed readings vary; Beale's NIGTC affirms demonic reality and reads the chapter symbolically without flattening the agents into mere metaphors. Eastern Orthodox theology has a robust demonology, especially in the desert-fathers tradition (the *Philokalia*), and reads chapter 9 in continuity with that ascetic-monastic experience of demonic encounter. Dispensationalist readings affirm the literal demonology and add a literal future fulfillment. Lutheran readings tend toward the symbolic. Critical readings (Boring, Caird, Yarbro Collins) treat the demonology as cultural-cosmological furniture: the seer's age believed in fallen Watchers and bound spirits, and the seer uses that vocabulary to make a structural-theological point about the powers. Bauckham in *The Theology of the Book of Revelation* threads the needle: the imagery encodes real spiritual realities under cultural forms, and the question of literal-versus-symbolic is the wrong question to ask of an apocalypse.

Where this leaves us: chapter 9 is unintelligible without a robust account of evil as more than human, and unintelligible if read as a flat demonological catalogue. The seer is operating in a register where the cosmic and the political are the same dimension, where Apollo-the-imperial-patron and Apollyon-the-angel-of-the-abyss are not distinct entities to be disambiguated but a single reality named twice.

**The unresolved question:** If the plagues do not produce repentance, and if 9:20–21 closes the chapter with the impenitents still impenitent, then on what theological basis does the seer expect anyone to be saved? The chapter offers no answer. The answer comes only later, in the Lamb who was slain — but in chapter 9 itself the question stands without resolution, and the silence is the point.

## The Thread

**1 Enoch 18–21 → Revelation 9:1–2.** The descent of the star with the key to the abyss is unintelligible without the Watcher tradition. Stuckenbruck and Bauckham have made this connection central; the abyss is not a generic metaphor but the specific prison of bound rebellious astral beings.

**Joel 2:1–11 → Revelation 9:3–11.** Joel's locust army was John's source-text and his interpretive control. The differences are diagnostic: Joel's locusts come on the day of the LORD as judgment-and-restoration; John's come on the day of the LORD's wrath without restoration following.

**Exodus 10:1–20 → Revelation 9:1–11, 20–21.** The eighth plague produced eventual release; this plague produces only deepening idolatry. John is rewriting the Exodus pattern in a darker key and asking his audience to feel the difference.

**Wisdom of Solomon 11:15–16 → Revelation 9:20–21.** The deuterocanonical wisdom tradition had already developed the trope that idolaters are punished by the very objects of their idolatry. John extends it: the impenitent worship works of their hands that "cannot see or hear or walk," and the plagues that strike them are images of those very dead idols come to grotesque life.
