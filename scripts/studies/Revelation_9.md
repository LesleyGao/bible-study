# Revelation 9: The Abyss Opened and the Cavalry of the Euphrates

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Revelation reaches us, on the mainstream consensus of Richard Bauckham, G.K. Beale, David Aune, and Craig Koester, as a Domitianic composition c. AD 95, anchored in the testimony of Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 5.30.3) and located in the Aegean province of Asia. The minority Neronian dating (A.T. Robinson, Kenneth Gentry) places the book before AD 70 and reads the trumpet-cycle as the apocalyptic register of the Jewish War. Either way, the audience is the Christian communities of Asia Minor under the cumulative pressure of the imperial cult — meticulously documented by Steven Friesen in *Imperial Cults and the Apocalypse of John*. Leonard Thompson cautions that the persecution under Domitian was not the systematic juridical campaign of later imagination; Adela Yarbro Collins's *Crisis and Catharsis* names the dissonance between Christian confession and Roman civic religion as the felt crisis. Chapter 9, more than any other in the trumpet-cycle, presses against this imperial horizon — the Apollyon/Apollo wordplay of 9:11 and the Euphratean cavalry of 9:13-19 are direct engagements with the cultic and military landscape of late first-century Rome.

The chapter unfolds in two trumpet-blasts — the fifth and sixth — which together constitute the first two of the three "woes" announced by the eagle at the close of chapter 8.

**The fifth trumpet (9:1-12)** opens with a star fallen from heaven to earth, given the key to the shaft of the Abyss. The Greek *abyssos* (ἄβυσσος) is the Septuagint's rendering of *tehom* (תְּהוֹם), the primordial deep of Genesis 1:2; in Second Temple apocalyptic — particularly 1 Enoch 18-21 — the Abyss is the prison-pit of the rebellious Watchers. The fallen star opens the shaft, smoke rises like a great furnace, and from the smoke come locusts with scorpions' power, told to torment only those without the seal of God. The torment lasts five months — Bauckham, Aune, and Beale note that five months is the natural life-span of a locust swarm. The locusts' descriptions (9:7-10) blend Joel 2 with surreal detail: like horses for battle, crowns of gold, faces like men, hair like women, teeth like lions, iron breastplates, scorpion-tails. Their king is named in two languages: Hebrew *Abaddōn* (אֲבַדּוֹן), Greek *Apollyōn* (Ἀπολλύων), both "Destroyer." The bilingual pairing is itself a signal — John writes for an audience that hears both registers.

The Abaddon-traditions are deep. *Abaddōn* appears in Job 26:6, 28:22, 31:12, Psalm 88:11, and Proverbs 15:11 as a Sheol-related term — the place of destruction, paired with Sheol or death itself. Rabbinic developments in *Talmud Bavli Pesachim* 54a treat Sheol-Abaddon as the lowest layer of the underworld. The Greek *Apollyōn*, the destroyer, has a sharper edge: David Aune and Steven Friesen both argue that the name is a deliberate pun on Apollo. Domitian publicly cultivated Apollo as patron deity; coins minted under his reign feature Apollo prominently; the Delphic oracle was a centerpiece of Roman imperial religion. To name the king of the Abyss "Apollyon" is to reframe the imperial patron god as the destroyer — a pointed political-theological move that the original Asian Christian audience would have heard immediately.

**The sixth trumpet (9:13-21)** is summoned by a voice from the four horns of the golden altar — a callback to the censer-altar of 8:3-5, again positioning prayer as the trigger of judgment. Four angels bound at the Euphrates are released; they had been prepared for "the hour, the day, the month, and the year" — a Jewish-apocalyptic locution for divine pre-determination. The Euphrates was the eastern boundary of the Roman empire, and the cavalry-from-the-east imagery has a sharp historical edge. The Roman trauma at Carrhae in 53 BC — the catastrophic defeat of Crassus by the Parthians, in which seven legions were destroyed and the eagles lost — left a permanent scar on the Roman psyche. Parthian cavalry were the great fear of Rome's eastern frontier. The number of the mounted troops is *dis myriades myriadōn* (δὶς μυριάδες μυριάδων, "twice ten thousand times ten thousand") — 200 million, an apocalyptic-symbolic infinity rather than a literal census.

The horses themselves blend natural and supernatural: breastplates of fire, sapphire, and sulfur; heads like lions; fire, smoke, and sulfur from their mouths; tails like serpents with heads. By these three plagues — fire, smoke, sulfur — a third of mankind is killed. The fire-smoke-sulfur combination is straight Sodom-and-Gomorrah imagery (Gen 19:24) and Day-of-the-Lord imagery (Isa 30:33). The lion-headed horses with serpent-tailed strikes are surreal; Bauckham reads them as deliberately unrepresentable, breaking the visual register to mark the supernatural origin of the assault.

The chapter's central pastoral note arrives at 9:20-21: "The rest of mankind, who were not killed by these plagues, *did not repent* of the works of their hands nor give up worshiping demons and idols of gold and silver and bronze and stone and wood, which cannot see or hear or walk, nor did they repent of their murders or their sorceries or their sexual immorality or their thefts." The catalogue of unrepented sin echoes the prophetic indictments of Isaiah 2 and Jeremiah 10 against idolatry, and the Decalogue's second-table sins. The pastoral knife: judgment alone does not produce repentance.

Greek terms anchoring chapter 9: *abyssos* (ἄβυσσος, "the Abyss"), the LXX rendering of *tehom*; *Abaddōn* (אֲבַדּוֹן / Ἀβαδδών, "Destruction"); *Apollyōn* (Ἀπολλύων, "Destroyer," with the Apollo wordplay); *basanizō* (βασανίζω, "to torment"); *daimonion* (δαιμόνιον, "demon"); *pharmakeia* (φαρμακεία, "sorcery" — the etymological root of "pharmacy," covering drug-induced occult practice); *metanoeō* (μετανοέω, "to repent" — the verb that 9:20-21 negates twice, and the chapter's pastoral hinge).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Revelation is apocalyptic literature in the formal sense John Collins and Christopher Rowland have specified: vision-cycle disclosure mediated through angelic interpreter, generically related to 1 Enoch, 4 Ezra, and 2 Baruch. Chapter 9 continues the trumpet-cycle (chs 8-11) but executes a register-shift: the first four trumpets (8:7-12) targeted creation (earth, sea, fresh water, heavenly bodies); the fifth and sixth (9:1-21) target humanity directly, and the source of the assault shifts from natural disaster to demonological warfare. Bauckham notes the architecture: the first four trumpets recapitulate the Egyptian plagues; the fifth and sixth break out of that template into something stranger — the Abyss opened, the Euphratean cavalry, supernatural horses with lion-heads and serpent-tails. The literary effect is to escalate, not merely intensify.

Recapitulation theory (Hendriksen, Beale) holds that the trumpet-cycle covers the same period as the seal-cycle from a different angle. Linear-sequential readings (Walvoord, MacArthur) treat the trumpets as consecutive future events. Either way, chapter 9 is the chapter where the trumpet-cycle's distinctive demonological register comes fully into view — and where the Apocalypse's anti-imperial register reaches one of its sharpest points (the Apollyon/Apollo pun).

The chapter's literary style is deliberately fragmented and surreal. The locust-descriptions (9:7-10) and the cavalry-descriptions (9:17-19) refuse smooth visualization; the imagery breaks because the realities being figured exceed visual representation. Bauckham reads this as a feature, not a bug — the apocalyptic register requires the visual to break. Wilfrid Harrington (*Sacra Pagina*) makes the same point: the chapter's surrealism is theological, not aesthetic.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Chapter 9 sits at the convergence of three streams of OT and Second Temple apocalyptic tradition. Joel 2:1-11, the great locust-army oracle, supplies the immediate template: locusts as army, the cosmic darkening that accompanies their advance, the Day-of-the-Lord horizon. Genesis 1:2's *tehom* — the primordial deep — supplies the apocalyptic geography of the Abyss. 1 Enoch 6-10, the Watchers tradition, supplies the prison-pit theology: rebellious angels bound in the depths, awaiting eschatological release. The Pauline parallel is 2 Thessalonians 2:7-12 — the "lawlessness already at work but restrained" — which speaks of a present-yet-bounded mystery of evil.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

**Patristic**: Origen reads the locusts allegorically as demonic temptations attacking the soul (cf. *On First Principles* 3.2). Hippolytus, in *On the Antichrist*, takes the chapter as part of his eschatological scheme. Augustine's cautious treatment in *City of God* 20 reads the trumpet-cycle as the Church's tribulations through history, with the demonological imagery as figurative rather than ontologically literal.

**Medieval**: Joachim of Fiore reads the trumpet-cycle as church-historical periods. The medieval period produced a striking re-reading: the locust-army was widely identified with the Saracen armies of the seventh and eighth centuries — Bede's commentary, the *Glossa Ordinaria*, and later expositors mapped the fifth trumpet onto the rise of Islam, with the five months interpreted variously as a literal period or as 150 years.

**Reformation**: Bullinger and Luther extend the historicist reading. Luther identified the fallen star of 9:1 with rising papal corruption; Bullinger systematized the historicist mapping. Calvin declined a full Revelation commentary.

**Critical**: David Aune's three-volume Word Biblical Commentary catalogues Greco-Roman parallels — the Apollo cult under Domitian, the Parthian cavalry imagery, the Roman fear of the eastern frontier. Steven Friesen presses the Apollyon/Apollo wordplay as central to the chapter's anti-imperial theology. Adela Yarbro Collins reads the chapter through the Combat Myth backdrop. Bauckham's *Climax of Prophecy* anchors the recapitulation reading and the deliberate symbolism (200 million as apocalyptic infinity, not literal census).

**Contemporary**: G.K. Beale's NIGTC integrates the patristic, medieval, and modern readings into a sustained idealist framework. Brian Blount's Westminster commentary reads the chapter through the African-American liberationist tradition — the locust-army as the systemic powers that torment those without the seal of God. Allan Callahan and Stephen Moore extend postcolonial readings: the Euphratean cavalry is anti-imperial liturgy. Eugene Boring (*Interpretation*) and Wilfrid Harrington (*Sacra Pagina*) hold the moderate middle. Walter Wink, in his *Powers* trilogy, develops a reading of the chapter's demonology that resists both literal-personal and Bultmannian-demythologizing interpretations: the powers are real, "the inner aspect of material reality." John Christopher Thomas, in his Pentecostal commentary, presses a more straightforwardly demonological line.

### Four Interpretive Schools

- **Preterist**: the fifth and sixth trumpets are the apocalyptic register of the Jewish War. The locust-army is the Roman invasion under Vespasian and Titus; the cavalry from the Euphrates evokes the auxiliary forces drawn from the eastern provinces. Kenneth Gentry's *Before Jerusalem Fell* is the standard articulation.
- **Historicist**: the fifth trumpet is the rise of Islam (the medieval and Reformation reading), and the sixth trumpet is variously identified with the Ottoman Turks (the cavalry from the Euphrates) or other historical movements.
- **Futurist (dispensational)**: the trumpets are future tribulation judgments. Walvoord reads the locust-army as a literal demonic horde, and the 200-million cavalry as a literal future army (some popular dispensationalists once mapped this onto Communist China, a reading that has aged poorly).
- **Idealist**: the chapter depicts perennial features of demonological assault and judgment in history. The locust-army is the chronic demonic torment of the unsealed; the Euphratean cavalry is the recurring shape of imperial violence under God's permission.

### Jewish Scholarly Engagement

The chapter is saturated with Second Temple Jewish apocalyptic. The Abyss as the prison of rebellious angels comes directly from 1 Enoch 18-21, where Enoch is shown the place where the fallen Watchers are bound; James VanderKam's foundational work on 1 Enoch and the Qumran community traces the development of this geography. The Joel 2 locust-army is read by John alongside the Watchers tradition — a fusion that John Collins documents as characteristic of Second Temple apocalyptic. The Abaddon-traditions of Job, Psalms, Proverbs, and the rabbinic literature (especially *Pesachim* 54a) supply the Hebrew register; the bilingual Hebrew-Greek pairing of Abaddon-Apollyon signals an audience that hears both. Daniel Boyarin's work on the porous boundary between Judaism and early Christianity reframes the chapter not as a Christian text against Judaism but as deeply Jewish-apocalyptic, inscribing the messianic community into the prophetic-apocalyptic horizon. The locust-tradition itself runs through the Joseph and Aseneth pseudepigraphon and the Qumran *War Scroll*'s eschatological-warfare imagery.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What God is doing in chapter 9: the Abyss is opened *under God's permission* — the fallen star is *given* the key (passive voice, divine-passive throughout); the locusts are *told* whom to torment and whom to spare; the four angels at the Euphrates are *released*; the cavalry is *prepared* for the appointed hour. The chapter's theology is a theology of bounded permission. Evil is not autonomous; the Abyss does not open itself; the destroyer is named and limited. And yet the pastoral register turns sharp at 9:20-21: even after this dramatic, demonological judgment, "the rest of mankind... did not repent." The chapter refuses the easy theology that judgment will, in itself, produce repentance. Judgment is necessary but not sufficient.

### Tension 1: Demonology — what is the locust-army?

The chapter's demonology has provoked one of the longest-running interpretive debates in Christian theology. The mapped positions range across a wide spectrum.

The **patristic-medieval literal** reading (Hippolytus, much of the medieval tradition, John Christopher Thomas in his Pentecostal commentary on Revelation, the contemporary Roman Catholic tradition as articulated in CCC §391-395) holds that the locusts are real demonic beings, ontologically distinct, acting under God's permission. The strongest case: the chapter's grammar treats them as agents — they are "told," they are "allowed," they have a king named in two languages. They are not figures of speech.

The **Reformation moderate** reading (Calvin retained demons but cooled the speculation; Luther similarly) holds that demonology is real but is not to be elaborated beyond Scripture's reserve. The strongest case: Scripture's own demonology is reticent and sober, and Christian theology should match the register.

The **Bultmannian demythologizing** reading (Rudolf Bultmann's program, mid-twentieth-century liberal Protestantism) holds that the demonological imagery is mythological packaging for an existential-anthropological reality — the powers that torment the soul are psychological and social, not ontologically distinct beings. The strongest case: the chapter's surrealism (locusts with women's hair and lions' teeth) is literary, not ontological; to take it ontologically literally is a category mistake.

The **Walter Wink** reading (*Naming the Powers*, *Unmasking the Powers*, *Engaging the Powers*) holds that the powers are real but are "the inner aspect of material reality" — institutional and spiritual at once. Wink's position resists both the Bultmannian deflation and the literalist hypostatization. The locust-army is a real *power* — the spiritual interiority of the imperial-cultic-military complex — without being a literal swarm of supernatural insects.

The **contemporary evangelical and Catholic** retrieval (C.S. Lewis's *The Screwtape Letters*, Peter Kreeft's apologetic work, Richard Beck's *Reviving Old Scratch*) renews engagement with personal demonology after the mid-century deflation, arguing that the Christian tradition's reticence about demons is not the same as denial.

Steelman the least intuitive: the Bultmannian demythologizing reading is least intuitive to a contemporary evangelical hearer, but its strongest version names a real interpretive problem — namely that taking the locust-imagery as literal ontological description (women's hair, lions' teeth, scorpion-tails) misses what the apocalyptic register is doing. The imagery is *meant* to break visualization; to insist on its literal-ontological reference is to misread the genre. Bultmann's mistake was not in noticing the mythological register but in concluding that the register meant the underlying reality was unreal. Wink's correction — that the powers are real *as powers*, not as cartoon entities — preserves the genre while honoring the ontological claim.

Where this leaves us: the chapter's demonology is real, bounded, and grammatically active; the Apocalypse refuses both Bultmannian deflation and naive literalism, and the tradition's best reading (Augustine, Wink, Beale) holds the powers as real but always under God's permission.

### Tension 2: "They did not repent" (9:20-21)

The chapter's most theologically sharp verses. Why does judgment not produce repentance? The mapped positions divide along a soteriological axis.

The **Augustinian/Reformed irresistible-grace** reading holds that the verse confirms what Augustine and Aquinas argued and what the Reformed tradition systematized: grace must precede repentance. Judgment, however dramatic, does not produce repentance unless God grants the grace of conversion. The strongest case: Romans 2:4 ("God's kindness is meant to lead you to repentance") names *kindness* as what produces repentance, not judgment; Ezekiel 36:26 ("a new heart I will give you") names regeneration as the prerequisite of true repentance. The locust-army and the Euphratean cavalry torment and kill, but they do not regenerate.

The **Wesleyan-Arminian / Catholic free-rejection** reading holds that the verse establishes that God respects free human refusal. The Catholic Catechism §1730 names the human person as "endowed with a spiritual and immortal soul" possessing "freedom — *libertas* — by which one can determine oneself toward what is good." Judgment is invitation, not coercion; the unrepentant remain unrepentant by their own continued willing. The strongest case: the chapter does not say the unrepentant *cannot* repent; it says they *did not* repent. The grammar preserves agency.

A third position — the **Eastern Orthodox synergistic** reading (Maximus the Confessor's understanding of the cooperation of divine grace and human will, mediated through Vladimir Lossky and contemporary writers like David Bentley Hart in some moods) — holds that grace and human response are synergistic; judgment is part of God's pedagogical address to the human person, but the response remains the person's. The strongest case: the chapter's pastoral register is precisely pedagogical — God *is* addressing the unrepentant — and the failure of the address is the human refusal, not the divine intent.

A fourth position — the **Reformed-modulated Bauckham** reading — holds that the verse is the Apocalypse's diagnosis of the human condition under judgment, not a verdict on whether anyone *can* repent. The Apocalypse names what *is*: even after this judgment, repentance does not come. The chapter is naming the failure, not adjudicating its mechanism.

Steelman the least intuitive: the Wesleyan-Arminian / Catholic free-rejection reading is the least intuitive to a Reformed hearer, but its strongest version reads the chapter's grammar with care. The text says "did not repent," not "could not repent." The Apocalypse's pastoral register elsewhere (the calls to repent in chapters 2-3, the patient summoning of the trumpet-cycle's bounded one-third judgments) presupposes that repentance was a real possibility, not a counterfactual. To collapse the human refusal into divine non-election is to miss the chapter's lament.

Where this leaves us: chapter 9's pastoral knife is the failure of judgment to produce repentance; the chapter refuses the easy soteriology that drama will, in itself, save.

**Closing question**: If even the Abyss opened and the cavalry of the Euphrates do not produce repentance — if judgment is necessary but not sufficient — what *does* produce the turning?

## The Thread

Chapter 9 weaves four threads — three OT and one NT — into its vision. **Joel 2:1-11**, the great locust-army oracle, is the source pattern: locusts as army with horses' appearance, the cosmic darkening that accompanies them, the Day-of-the-Lord horizon. John reads Joel's oracle into the Apocalypse's apocalyptic register and intensifies it (Joel's locusts strip the land; John's locusts torment the unsealed). **Genesis 1:2** supplies the Abyss — *tehom*, the primordial deep — which the LXX translates as *abyssos*, and which becomes in apocalyptic Judaism the prison-pit of the rebellious. **1 Enoch 6-10**, the Watchers tradition, supplies the prison-pit theology directly: rebellious angels bound in the depths, awaiting eschatological release. The Apocalypse's fallen star with the key to the shaft of the Abyss is straight Watchers-tradition. **2 Thessalonians 2:7-12**, Paul's "lawlessness already at work but restrained," is the NT parallel: the present mystery of evil, bounded by a divine restraint that will be lifted at the appointed time, after which deception will run its full course among "those who are perishing... because they refused to love the truth and so be saved." Paul's "they refused to love the truth" sits beside John's "they did not repent" as the same diagnosis: the failure of repentance is human, not the failure of God's address. The chapter's four threads — Joel, Genesis, 1 Enoch, 2 Thessalonians — cohere into a single statement: evil is bounded, evil is permitted, and judgment alone will not save.
