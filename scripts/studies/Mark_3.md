# Mark 3: The Strong Man Bound

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Mark 3 climaxes the Galilean controversy cycle in a synagogue Sabbath confrontation, escalates into mass-movement geography, and then breaks into two of Mark's most charged scenes — the Beelzebul controversy and the redefinition of family. The Roman backdrop is the early-to-mid Tiberian period; the immediate political backdrop is Herod Antipas's Galilee. The unprecedented coalition formed in 3:6 — "the Pharisees went out and immediately held counsel with the Herodians" — is historically significant. Pharisees and Herodians were natural opponents: Pharisees were Torah-observant religious renewal advocates wary of Herodian-Roman accommodation; Herodians (mentioned by Josephus only obliquely; named in Mark 3:6 and 12:13, and Matt 22:16) were political loyalists of the Herodian dynasty. Their alliance against Jesus signals that Jesus's Galilean ministry has crossed a threshold both religious and political — he has become a problem for both establishment factions.

The geography of 3:7–8 is the gospel's first widening lens. The crowd is drawn from Galilee (Antipas's territory), Judea and Jerusalem (Roman direct rule under the prefect — by the early-to-mid 30s CE that prefect was Pontius Pilate, 26–36 CE), Idumea (the Herodian dynasty's ancestral Edomite homeland to the south, recently Judaized in the second century BCE under John Hyrcanus), Transjordan (Perea, also under Antipas; the Decapolis to the north of Perea), and the coastal cities of Tyre and Sidon (Phoenician, Roman provincial Syria). This is a crowd from across all the political-administrative jurisdictions of the southern Levant — a fact Mark notes deliberately. Jesus's healing program is producing a regional movement.

The Capernaum synagogue continues in 3:1, on the same site of the eventual fourth-century white limestone synagogue that overlies the first-century basalt foundations. The "withered hand" is *xēra* (ξηρά, "dried up") — not paralytic but desiccated, possibly post-stroke or congenital, rendering the man unable to perform the manual labor that defined Galilean peasant life. The Sabbath halakhah at issue is the principle of *pikuach nefesh* (saving of life) — the rabbinic tradition would later codify life-saving healing as Sabbath-permissible, but a chronic non-life-threatening condition was generally postponed to a weekday. Jesus's framing — "to do good or to do harm, to save life or to kill" (3:4) — pushes the *pikuach nefesh* logic into broader ethical territory. Schalom Ben-Chorin and Geza Vermes have noted that Jesus's argumentative style is recognizably rabbinic.

The "mountain" of 3:13 is unspecified but probably the elevated region above Capernaum — sometimes identified with the so-called Mount of Beatitudes (Karne Hittin or the hill above Tabgha). Mark's mountain motif is theological-typological more than topographical: mountains in Mark are always sites of revelation, calling, and transfiguration (3:13, 6:46, 9:2, 13:3). The choice of Twelve invokes the twelve tribes of Israel — 1QS at Qumran also organized leadership in twelves, evidencing that the Twelve-as-eschatological-Israel symbol was a current Second Temple Jewish trope (Sanders, *Jesus and Judaism*).

The Twelve themselves are a sociological mosaic. Simon Peter, Andrew, James, John, and Levi/Matthew are previously introduced. "Simon the Zealot" (or *Kananaios*, from Aramaic *qanāʾanā* — "zealous one") may be a reference to a pre-66 CE proto-Zealot tendency or simply to religious zeal; Josephus does not yet use "Zealot" as a party name before 66 CE, but the term existed (*qannaʾim*). Judas Iscariot's epithet *Iskariōth* is most plausibly *ish-Qeriyot* ("man of Kerioth," a Judean village), making him the only non-Galilean among the Twelve — a detail historically suggestive. James son of Alphaeus may be Levi's brother (since Levi is also "son of Alphaeus" at 2:14), opening the possibility that Levi/Matthew and James are siblings, though the texts do not explicitly say so.

Material culture: the boat held in readiness (3:9) reflects the lakeside fishing economy (the Migdal/Magdala mosaics and the Kinneret Boat); the "house" of 3:20 (where the family seeks Jesus and where the Beelzebul controversy unfolds) is again likely Peter's Capernaum house. Greek terms: ***exousia*** (ἐξουσία, "authority"); ***apostolous*** (ἀποστόλους, "apostles" — first Markan use, 3:14); ***Boanerges*** (Βοανηργές, transliterated Aramaic preserved); ***Beelzebul*** (Βεελζεβούλ, Aramaic / Hebrew *baʿal zĕbûl* — "lord of the high place" or by polemical pun *baʿal zĕbûb* "lord of flies," 2 Kgs 1:2); ***blasphēmia*** (βλασφημία, "blasphemy").

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 3 completes Joanna Dewey's chiasm (2:1 – 3:6) at its A′ panel — the withered-hand healing — and immediately opens a new structural unit. The verb *egeire* ("rise") of 2:11 echoes in 3:3 ("come here," literally "rise up into the middle," *egeire eis to meson*) — the resurrection vocabulary and the public framing of healing as confrontation. Jesus's emotion at 3:5 ("he looked around at them with anger, *met' orgēs*, grieved at their hardness of heart") is striking. Mark is the only synoptic to record Jesus's anger here; Matthew and Luke both omit it. The textual clue is a literary-theological one: Mark refuses to scrub Jesus's emotional life. *Orgē* (ὀργή) in Mark is precise — it is the divine indignation against systemic refusal of mercy. (See also Mark 10:14, where Jesus is "indignant," *ēganaktēsen*, at the disciples blocking children.)

The triadic-and-sandwich pattern that defines Mark structures 3:20–35 with surgical precision. This is a textbook **Markan intercalation**: A — Jesus's family hears and goes to seize him "for they said, 'he is out of his mind'" (3:20–21). B — The scribes from Jerusalem accuse him of being possessed by Beelzebul, prompting Jesus's strong-man parable and the saying about blaspheming the Spirit (3:22–30). A′ — The family arrives outside; Jesus redefines family as those who do God's will (3:31–35). The B-material illuminates the A-material: the family's "he is out of his mind" (*exestē*) is, in Mark's structural logic, in the same category as the scribes' "he has an unclean spirit." The redefinition of family in 3:33–35 thus has unexpected sharpness — Jesus's biological family is, in the narrative moment, on the wrong side of the diagnosis. This is the Markan sandwich at its most theologically piercing.

Three nicknames are conferred in 3:16–17 — Simon → Peter (Aramaic *Kepha*, "rock"); James and John → *Boanerges* (Mark's Aramaic transliteration glossed as "Sons of Thunder" — *bĕnê regesh* or *bĕnê rōges*; etymology debated; Marcus and France discuss). The naming-as-conferral pattern echoes the patriarchal renaming of Genesis (Abram→Abraham, Jacob→Israel) — Jesus is constituting a new people. The list itself is a Markan literary set-piece: the names form a four-three-three-two-or-thereabouts cadence, ending with the chilling "Judas Iscariot, who *betrayed* him" (*paredōken*, παρέδωκεν — the "handing-over" verb that recurs throughout Mark's passion arc).

The strong-man parable (3:27) is one of Mark's earliest and most concentrated parables: "no one can enter a strong man's house and plunder his goods, unless he first binds the strong man." It is a programmatic statement of Mark's exorcism theology — Jesus's exorcisms are not isolated wonders but installments of a cosmic re-conquest. **Ched Myers** in *Binding the Strong Man* makes this parable the structural key to the entire gospel: Jesus is binding the strong man (Satan, but in Mark's world also collusive earthly strong men — Antipas, the temple establishment, Rome) and plundering his house.

The blasphemy-against-the-Holy-Spirit saying (3:28–30) — placed at the structural center of the chapter — is Mark's contribution to one of the most pastorally fraught texts in the New Testament. The form is solemn (*amēn legō hymin* — "truly I say to you") and the framing (3:30 — "for they were saying, 'he has an unclean spirit'") is Mark's own editorial gloss specifying *what* the unforgivable blasphemy is: attributing the works of the Spirit to demonic power. Mark is unusually direct here — he tells the reader exactly what is meant.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc behind chapter 3 traces a long line from Genesis 1's Sabbath through Exodus 31:13–17 (Sabbath as covenant sign), 1 Sam 8 (Israel's demand for a king like the nations), the prophetic critique of hardness of heart (Isa 6:10; Jer 5:21; Ezek 12:2), Daniel 7's vision of the Son of Man given dominion over kingdoms, and the Maccabean crisis where the question "is it lawful on the Sabbath?" became life-or-death (1 Macc 2:32–41 — the Maccabees' decision to fight on Sabbath when attacked).

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** Rabbinic Judaism's elaboration of *pikuach nefesh* (Mishnah Yoma 8:6; bShabbat 132a; Sifra on Lev 18:5) developed the very principle Jesus invokes in 3:4 — that danger to life suspends the Sabbath. The deeper Jewish question that Mark 3 raises is whether the Pharisees of the narrative are caricatured. Geza Vermes (*Jesus the Jew*) reads Jesus's argument as recognizably Pharisaic in form. E. P. Sanders (*Jewish Law from Jesus to the Mishnah*) argues that pre-70 Pharisees were probably more flexible than the post-Yavneh rabbinic memory and would not necessarily have plotted Jesus's death over a non-life-threatening Sabbath healing — Sanders thus treats 3:6 as a Markan compression. Daniel Boyarin (*The Jewish Gospels*) reads the Beelzebul controversy as a recognizable Second Temple debate over the source of charismatic power (compare Qumran's polemics about false prophets). Amy-Jill Levine (*The Misunderstood Jew*; *Jewish Annotated NT*) cautions against reading 3:1–6 as a wholesale indictment of Pharisaism — Mark is critiquing a particular hardness, not an entire tradition. The Babylonian Talmud's polemical reference to Jesus (Sanhedrin 43a) imagines him as a sorcerer who "led Israel astray" — a polemical inversion of the Beelzebul accusation.

The Twelve as twelve-tribes-restoration is a major Sanders thesis. In *Jesus and Judaism* (1985), Sanders treats the choice of the Twelve as one of the most historically secure facts about Jesus — the symbolic gesture only makes sense if Jesus saw himself as inaugurating a renewed Israel.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** Origen (Commentary fragments) read the withered hand as the soul incapacitated by sin, healed by command. Augustine (*De Sermone Domini in Monte*; *Sermons*) and Aquinas (*ST* II-II q. 14) gave the most extensive patristic-medieval treatment of the unforgivable sin: Augustine read it as final impenitence — the closing-off of the heart against the Spirit's work — and Aquinas codified it as one of six sins against the Holy Spirit (despair, presumption, impugning known truth, envy of fraternal grace, obstinacy, final impenitence). Bede's *Commentary on Mark* read the strong-man parable as Christ's harrowing of hell — Christ binds Satan in the descent and plunders the house of death.

The Reformation made 3:28–30 a pastoral question. Calvin (*Institutes* III.3.22) reads the unforgivable sin not as any single act but as the obstinate, deliberate, willful rejection of clearly known truth — and notes that those troubled by whether they have committed it almost by definition have not. Luther's pastoral counsel was similar. John Wesley's *Sermon 86: A Call to Backsliders* explicitly addresses anxious souls fearing they have blasphemed the Spirit — Wesley's reading is that such fear itself is evidence of the Spirit's continuing work. James Dunn (*Jesus and the Spirit*; *Jesus Remembered*) argues 3:30 specifies the sin precisely: it is calling the Spirit's work demonic — not a careless word but a deliberate moral inversion. Heikki Räisänen and Christopher Tuckett have continued the modern discussion.

R. T. France (*NIGTC*) emphasizes the chapter's structural pivot: 3:6 closes the Galilean controversy chiasm; 3:13–19 inaugurates a new movement (the Twelve); 3:20–35 reframes the very question of who Jesus's people are. Joel Marcus (*Anchor Bible*) reads the Beelzebul controversy through Jewish demonology and Second Temple apocalyptic — Jesus as eschatological warrior. Adela Yarbro Collins (*Hermeneia*) reads the Twelve through Greco-Roman categories of philosophical school formation as well as Jewish twelve-tribe restoration. Morna Hooker (*BNTC*) emphasizes the unfolding rejection theme: family rejects, scribes reject, but a new family forms around Jesus.

**Literary and political readings.** **Ched Myers's *Binding the Strong Man*** makes 3:27 the gospel's interpretive key — Jesus's program is the systematic plundering of the house of "the strong man" (Satan-and-his-collaborators in religious and political establishments). David Rhoads, Joanna Dewey, and Donald Michie (*Mark as Story*) read the chapter as a major plot-rotation: the public conflict has reached the point of conspiracy; Jesus must now consolidate followers. Mary Ann Tolbert (*Sowing the Gospel*) reads the family scene of 3:31–35 as inaugurating the soils-of-the-heart theology that will fully bloom in chapter 4.

Feminist readings: Joanna Dewey notes that 3:35 ("brother and sister and mother") explicitly includes "sister" — Jesus's redefined family is gendered-inclusive. The omission of "father" is telling — only one Father is acknowledged in this kinship reordering. Sharon Ringe and Elaine Wainwright have read the new-family pericope as foundational for Markan ecclesiology that decenters patriarchal household structures.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What is the unforgivable sin, and how should Christians live with it?** The position of Augustine and Aquinas (final impenitence — the deathbed-closed heart): blasphemy of the Spirit is not a discrete utterance but a settled disposition of refusal. The Reformed position (Calvin, *Institutes* III.3.22): a deliberate, knowing, willful inversion that calls the Spirit's work demonic; the troubled conscience is evidence one has *not* committed it. The Wesleyan position (Wesley, *Sermon 86*): fear of having blasphemed the Spirit is itself the Spirit's drawing — the sin is structural rejection, not pastoral anxiety. The Orthodox position: the sin is a hardening of the *nous* (the spiritual mind) against the divine energies, repented through ascesis and the mysteries. The historical-critical position (Marcus, Hooker; Räisänen): Mark 3:30 specifies the meaning — attributing the Spirit's exorcising work to Beelzebul; the saying is contextual to the scribes' specific accusation, not a free-floating warning. The pastoral-Catholic position (the Catechism §1864): the sin is the deliberate refusal to accept the mercy God offers; this hardness "can lead to final impenitence and eternal loss." The Pentecostal-Charismatic position has wrestled with this text as a warning against attributing genuine charismatic phenomena to demonic sources. **Where this leaves us:** Mark 3:30 ("for they were saying, 'he has an unclean spirit'") fences the warning against careless homiletical generalization. The sin is specifically the willful inversion of Spirit-into-demon — calling good evil. Steelman the historical-critical narrowing (the saying is occasion-bound), and the pastoral force of the broader tradition still holds: a heart that systematically calls the Spirit's mercy demonic — over time, with knowledge — has closed itself to the only door of forgiveness available. The saying's pastoral function is not to torment anxious souls but to warn against a settled, knowing inversion.

**Tension 2: Who is family — biological kinship or covenantal community?** Mark's account (3:31–35) is the synoptic tradition's sharpest. The Catholic position on the *adelphoi* of 3:31 (the "brothers" — interpreted as cousins or step-siblings or distant relatives, on the basis of the perpetual virginity of Mary; cf. Helvidius's challenge in the 4th c. and Jerome's response *Adversus Helvidium*): biological family is real, but covenantal family is supreme; Mary stands as the supreme example of "doing the will of God" (Luke 1:38). The Reformed position: the *adelphoi* are most plausibly siblings (so most modern Protestant exegesis), and Jesus's redefinition of family is radical: doing God's will constitutes covenant kinship that supersedes blood. The Orthodox position: the *adelphoi* are children of Joseph by a previous marriage (Protoevangelium of James tradition); Mary is *Theotokos*; the church is the new family. The Wesleyan-evangelical position: the new family of those who do God's will is the visible church community, which has priority over but does not abolish biological family. The Jewish-historical position (Sanders, Vermes): Jesus is forming an apocalyptic-renewal community in which biological family ties are subordinated to eschatological commitment — comparable to the Qumran community's celibate-priestly sub-group. The liberation reading (Myers): Jesus's redefinition of family is a critique of the patriarchal honor-shame household economy that governed Mediterranean society. The feminist reading (Dewey, Wainwright): Jesus's including "sister" but omitting "father" reorganizes household authority around the will of God rather than around male headship. **Where this leaves us:** The Markan sandwich is the interpretive key. Jesus's biological family in 3:21 has come "to seize him" because they think he is unhinged. The redefinition of family in 3:33–35 is not a contempt of biological kinship in the abstract but a refusal to be re-domesticated when his blood relatives have aligned with the diagnostic framework of the Jerusalem scribes (3:22). Doing the will of God has produced a community that crosses biological lines — and any biological family unwilling to share that commitment finds itself, painfully, "outside" (3:31–32). Steelman the Catholic emphasis on Mary as the model of "doing the will of God" — and the Markan force still holds: kinship is reconstituted around obedience.

**Unresolved question:** If Jesus's biological family thought he was "out of his mind" (3:21), and if even Mary stood "outside" in the moment of 3:31, then how should the reader hold Mary's role between the Annunciation (Luke) and the empty tomb (Mark 16)? Mark's silence about Mary at the cross (15:40 lists Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James and Joses, and Salome — not Mary the mother of Jesus by name) is one of the gospel's haunting omissions. Is the redefinition of family in 3:35 ultimately reconciliatory or ultimately separating?

## The Thread

The chapter plants four thematic threads that run forward through Mark. **3:6 — the Pharisee-Herodian plot** — pairs with **12:13 (the same coalition's question about taxes to Caesar) and 14:1 (the chief priests and scribes seeking to put him to death)**. **3:11 — "you are the Son of God"** spoken by unclean spirits and silenced — pairs with **5:7 (the Gerasene demoniac), 9:7 (the transfiguration voice), and 15:39 (the centurion at the cross)**. The progression — demons recognize, the Father confirms, a Roman finally confesses — is Mark's Christological architecture. **3:14 — "that they might be with him and he might send them out"** — pairs with **6:7 (the actual sending of the Twelve) and 16:7 (the messenger sending the women back to "tell his disciples and Peter")**. The discipleship arc that begins here will fail catastrophically (14:50, "they all left him and fled") and only be reopened by resurrection. **3:27 — "binding the strong man"** — is enacted at **5:1–20 (the Gerasene exorcism, with Roman-imperial echoes), 11:15–17 (the temple cleansing as confrontation with the religious strong man), and 15:38 (the temple veil torn, the strong man's house finally laid open)**. By the gospel's end the strong man is bound through the most counter-intuitive means — not by overpowering force but by Jesus's death.
