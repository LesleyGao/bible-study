# Psalm 66

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The superscription is unusually spare: *lamnatzeach shir mizmor* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ שִׁיר מִזְמוֹר), "To the choirmaster. A Song. A Psalm." It lacks the *le-David* attribution that runs through most of Book II — a striking absence, and one of the few psalms in this section without it. Hossfeld and Zenger read the missing Davidic ascription as significant: Psalm 66 may have entered the psalter as part of an originally separate liturgical collection, perhaps tied to Levitical or pilgrimage use. The double genre marker (*shir* + *mizmor*, with the order inverted from Psalm 65's *mizmor shir*) signals festival context. Goldingay calls Pss 65–68 the "festival cluster" of Book II: each marked as *mizmor* and/or *shir*, each centered on temple worship and corporate praise.

Hermann Gunkel's form-critical classification of Psalm 66 was famously perplexed. The psalm divides cleanly into two halves with very different forms: vv. 1–12 are a *Volksdanklied* (community thanksgiving) with strong hymnic features, while vv. 13–20 are an *Individualdanklied* (individual thanksgiving) tied to a personal vow of burnt offering. Gunkel saw this as awkward; later form-critics (Westermann, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger) treated it as evidence of editorial layering — two psalms editorially fused, perhaps in early Second Temple use. Goldingay, working against the grain, argues for unified composition: a single voice that moves from corporate to personal because that movement reflects how Israelite worship actually worked at the temple — communal recitation, then individual fulfillment of vow.

Date is debated. The unambiguous reference to Exodus deliverance (v. 6, "he turned the sea into dry land; they passed through the river on foot") and the trial-and-deliverance motif of vv. 10–12 have led many critics — Kraus, Tate, Hossfeld-Zenger — to read Psalm 66 against the post-exilic horizon, where the Exodus is recited as paradigm for the return from Babylon. Others (Mowinckel, Goldingay) see no necessity for an exilic date: the Exodus is constitutive for Israelite cult worship from earliest times, and the psalm's confidence in the standing temple cult (vv. 13–15) reads more naturally pre-exilic. The compromise position — pre-exilic core with exilic editorial framing — is now standard.

The Hebrew anchors deserve close attention. Verse 5's *nora alilah al-bnei adam* (נוֹרָא עֲלִילָה עַל־בְּנֵי אָדָם), "his deeds are awesome for the children of man," uses *alilah* — a word that can mean "deed" but also carries connotations of theatrical, public action. The "children of man" (*bnei adam*) is the universalist register, paralleling Psalm 65's "all flesh." Verse 6's *hafakh yam le-yabashah* (הָפַךְ יָם לְיַבָּשָׁה), "he turned the sea into dry land," uses the Exodus-specific verb *hafakh* — to turn or overturn — the same verb used in Exodus 14:21. The psalm is making a precise scriptural citation, not a general allusion.

The crucible imagery in vv. 10–12 deserves a slow read. *Tzeraftanu ki-tzerof kasef* (צְרַפְתָּנוּ כִּצְרָף־כָּסֶף), "you have tried us as silver is tried" — the verb *tzaraf* is the metallurgical term for refining metal in a smelting furnace, separating dross from purity through fire. The image is technically precise: ancient silver-refining used a cupellation furnace where the silver-lead alloy was heated until the lead oxidized and could be skimmed off, leaving pure silver. The psalm assumes this is what God has done to the community — and the verbs of v. 11 (*meta'tanu va-metsudah*, "you brought us into the net"; *samta mu'aqah be-motneinu*, "you laid a crushing burden on our backs") are concrete, even brutal.

Then v. 12: *banu va-esh u-vamayim* (בָּאנוּ בָאֵשׁ וּבַמָּיִם), "we went through fire and through water." This is the psalm's signature line. The pairing of fire and water is a merism — extreme opposites taken together to mean *every form of trial*. The line is lapidary, often quoted, and in rabbinic and Christian tradition becomes a way of speaking of the totality of suffering survived by the people of God. The conclusion — *va-totsi'enu la-revayah* (וַתּוֹצִיאֵנוּ לָרְוָיָה), "yet you have brought us out to abundance" — uses *revayah*, a word that means "saturation" or "abundant moisture," and Hossfeld-Zenger note the deliberate echo of Psalm 65's agricultural abundance: the *revayah* of Psalm 66:12 is the *revivim* (showers) of Psalm 65:10.

The closing benediction, *barukh Elohim asher lo hesir hasdo me-itti* (בָּרוּךְ אֱלֹהִים אֲשֶׁר לֹא־הֵסִיר חַסְדּוֹ מֵאִתִּי), "Blessed be God, because he has not removed his steadfast love from me" (v. 20), uses *hesed* — the central covenantal term — to wrap the entire psalm. The community's deliverance and the individual's answered prayer are both, finally, manifestations of *hesed*.

The Qumran psalter fragments preserve Psalm 66 in agreement with the MT. The LXX (Psalm 65 in its numbering) is largely faithful, though it softens some of the more graphic trial-language. The Targum Tehillim adds explanatory glosses linking v. 6 explicitly to the Exodus and v. 12's fire-and-water to the trials of Daniel's three companions and Daniel himself, foreshadowing the rabbinic readings to come.

Crucially, Psalm 66 is part of the **Elohistic Psalter** (Pss 42–83), where divine names skew toward *Elohim*. Psalm 66 follows this pattern: *Elohim* dominates throughout, and the personal name YHWH appears only in the closing section's allusive verbs. The Elohistic redaction shapes Psalm 66's universal-horizon address to "all the earth" (v. 1) and "you peoples" (v. 8) — the deity addressed is recognizable to the nations as well as Israel.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Lowth's parallelism categories all appear, but Psalm 66's distinctive feature is the structural-rhetorical layering rather than the parallelism alone. *Synonymous* parallelism opens the hymn — "shout for joy to God, all the earth / sing the glory of his name" (vv. 1–2). *Synthetic* parallelism dominates the historical-recital strophe (vv. 5–7), where each line builds the picture of God's mighty acts. *Antithetic* parallelism shows up sharply in v. 18–19: "If I had cherished iniquity in my heart, the Lord would not have listened — but truly God has listened."

Strophic structure is the central interpretive question. The psalm divides into five strophes, each with internal coherence: vv. 1–4 (universal call to praise), vv. 5–7 (historical recital of God's deeds), vv. 8–12 (community testimony of trial and deliverance), vv. 13–15 (individual vow-fulfillment of burnt offerings), vv. 16–20 (individual testimony of answered prayer). The three *selah* markers — at v. 4, v. 7, and v. 15 — mark the major joints: between universal praise and historical recital, between historical recital and community testimony, and between vow-fulfillment and individual witness. The function of *selah* is debated (musical interlude? volume cue? meditative pause?), but its placement here clearly maps the psalm's rhetorical architecture.

The strophic shift from "we" (vv. 8–12) to "I" (vv. 13–20) is the structural crux. Hossfeld-Zenger argue this is a redactional seam — two originally separate psalms joined. Goldingay argues for unity: the move from "we" to "I" enacts the move from corporate temple worship (community thanksgiving) to individual vow-fulfillment at the same temple — the way actual Israelite festival worship worked. The double "Come and see" / "Come and hear" (v. 5 and v. 16) creates a deliberate structural rhyme: the first invites the nations to see God's public deeds in history; the second invites the faithful to hear what God has done in one soul. Patrick D. Miller calls this "the universal and the particular held in a single psalm."

Inclusio and chiasm operate. The psalm opens with "shout for joy to God, all the earth" (v. 1) and closes with "Blessed be God" (v. 20) — a praise-frame around the testimony in the middle. James L. Mays identified a chiastic structure across the whole: A (universal praise, vv. 1–4) → B (deeds toward all, vv. 5–7) → C (community trial, vv. 8–12) → B' (individual fulfillment, vv. 13–15) → A' (individual praise as universal witness, vv. 16–20). The center, C, is the trial and deliverance — the psalm's theological gravity sits in the middle, where God refines the community through fire and water before bringing them out into abundance.

The "Come and see" / "Come and hear" pair is also a deliberate sensory inclusio — visible deed and audible testimony together constituting the full witness. Brueggemann reads this as Israel's distinctive epistemology: God is known both by what is publicly seen (the Exodus, history) and by what is privately heard and then proclaimed (the answered prayer, the vow fulfilled).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Within Book II.** Psalm 66 is the second psalm of the Pss 65–68 festival cluster. Its lack of Davidic superscription (in a section dominated by them) and its hybrid form make it stand out. Hossfeld-Zenger see Pss 65–68 as a deliberate compositional sequence: Psalm 65 celebrates harvest at Zion, Psalm 66 recites the Exodus and the personal vow, Psalm 67 prays for the harvest blessing to spread to the nations, and Psalm 68 recapitulates God's victorious procession. Psalm 66 is the cluster's historical and personal witness — what the gathered community remembers (the Exodus) and what one worshiper testifies (answered prayer).

**Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 66 reads v. 12's fire and water typologically: through fire (Abraham at Ur of the Chaldeans, the three companions in Daniel's furnace), through water (the Red Sea, the Jordan crossing), and out into abundance (the messianic age). Rashi reads v. 6's "there did we rejoice in him" as a present-tense participation: every generation that recites the psalm at Passover joins the original Exodus rejoicing. Ibn Ezra works through the philological details, especially the difficult *be-shamenu* / *be-shimchah* variants in early manuscripts. Radak emphasizes the unity of the psalm against the early form-critical tendency to split it: for Radak, the move from "we" to "I" is a normal psalmic pattern, not a redactional break.

Modern Jewish scholars extend the conversation. Buber's reading emphasizes the dialogic structure — the psalm speaks to God in two voices (corporate, individual), and both voices receive answer. Heschel reads the trial imagery (vv. 10–12) as theological realism: the prophets and psalmists never sentimentalize suffering, but they also never let it have the last word. Levenson's *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son* draws on Psalm 66's deliverance language as part of his broader thesis that Israelite religion is not afraid to speak of God as the agent of trial and the agent of rescue in the same breath. Sarna ties the burnt-offering vocabulary of vv. 13–15 to Leviticus 1's *olah* prescription — the totally consumed offering that signifies undivided dedication.

Liturgically, Psalm 66 is associated in some Jewish traditions with the second day of Passover (commemorating the crossing of the sea) and with thanksgiving prayers tied to *Birkat Ha-Gomel* — the blessing recited after deliverance from danger. The psalm's "fire and water" line entered rabbinic and later liturgical vocabulary as a stock phrase for surviving extremity.

**Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarrationes* on Psalm 65 (his numbering) reads the psalm Christologically and ecclesiologically: the corporate "we" who pass through fire and water is the Church under persecution, and the individual "I" who fulfills vows is Christ himself, whose body is the perfect *olah*. Athanasius commends the psalm to those who have been delivered from grave danger. Cassiodorus organizes his commentary around the five-strophe structure modern critics independently recovered. Aquinas treats the psalm in his exposition as an account of grace operative in history and in the individual soul.

The Christian baptismal reading of Psalm 66 was established early. The fire-and-water line (v. 12) was read against the New Testament typology of baptism (1 Cor 10:1–2, where Paul says Israel was "baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea"). Tertullian, Cyril of Jerusalem, and Ambrose all use Psalm 66 in baptismal catechesis. The Catholic Easter Vigil, in its current form, draws on the Exodus typology that Psalm 66 makes explicit — passing through the water of baptism into the abundance of the new life.

Luther preached on Psalm 66 in connection with Romans 5 and the doctrine of *theologia crucis* — God refines the believer through trial, not despite it. Calvin's 1557 commentary takes the psalm seriously as both communal and individual; he is particularly attentive to v. 18 ("if I had cherished iniquity in my heart, the Lord would not have listened") as a key text on the relationship between repentance and prayer.

Modern critical scholarship — Gunkel, Mowinckel, Westermann, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate, Wenham — converges on the form-critical reading of the psalm's hybrid structure while debating unity vs. layered composition. Brueggemann reads Psalm 66 as a paradigm of "psalms of new orientation" — the disorientation of trial (vv. 10–12) is followed by the new orientation of testimony (vv. 13–20), and the whole community is invited into the witness. Patrick D. Miller emphasizes the universal address ("all the earth") as evidence of Israel's missional theology even within the temple cult. William P. Brown reads the trial imagery against the wisdom tradition's account of God's pedagogy. Beth Tanner emphasizes the integration of corporate memory and personal testimony.

**New Testament use.** Psalm 66 is not directly cited in the New Testament, but its themes resonate strongly. The fire-and-water imagery feeds into 1 Corinthians 10:1–2 (the typological baptism in the sea), 1 Peter 1:6–7 (faith tested by fire as gold is tested), and Revelation 7:14 (those who have come through the great tribulation). The "come and see" / "come and hear" structure echoes the discipleship language of John 1:39, 46, where Philip says to Nathanael, "come and see." The vow-of-burnt-offerings strophe (vv. 13–15), with its assumption that the temple cult is the proper venue of personal thanksgiving, is part of the cultic background that Hebrews 13:15–16 reframes ("through him let us continually offer up a sacrifice of praise to God").

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Composition — unified or layered?** This is the long-standing critical question. The mainstream form-critical position (Gunkel, Westermann, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger) reads the move from "we" (vv. 1–12) to "I" (vv. 13–20) as evidence of two psalms editorially joined — perhaps a community thanksgiving fused with an individual thanksgiving in early Second Temple liturgical practice. The unity position (Goldingay) reads the move as native to Israelite worship: corporate recital plus individual vow-fulfillment is what actually happened at the temple at festival time, and the psalm preserves this double structure by design. A third position (Brueggemann) treats the question as somewhat secondary to what the psalm does in its current canonical form: whether by composition or by editing, the psalm now holds corporate and individual together, and that is the theological gift. Steelman the layered reading: the awkward seam at v. 13 makes more sense if two voices were joined; the language and form of vv. 1–12 are hymnic and historical, while vv. 13–20 are individual and cultic. Steelman the unified reading: every Israelite festival worshiper made the move from corporate "we" to individual "I" each time they brought a vow-offering, and the psalm tracks that liturgical reality.

**Tension 2: The Exodus-recital and Christian-typological reading.** The psalm's central historical claim — God turned the sea into dry land, his people passed through on foot — is the Exodus, told in three lines (v. 6). For Christians, this verse has been baptismal at least since Tertullian: passage through water into new life. But the typological reading risks colonizing the original. Three positions live here.

*Position A: Patristic-typological (Augustine, Cyril, Ambrose).* The Exodus is fulfilled in Christ; the passage through the sea is fulfilled in baptism; the abundance to which God brings the community (v. 12) is fulfilled in the Eucharist and the new creation. Steelman: this reading takes the New Testament's own typological moves (1 Cor 10:1–2; Heb 4:8–11) seriously and reads the psalm in the canonical-Christian frame. To refuse the typology would be to read the psalm against the Christian canon's own grain.

*Position B: Jewish-historical (Buber, Heschel, Levenson, Sarna).* The Exodus is not a metaphor but the founding event of Israel's existence as a people. To absorb it into a baptismal typology is to spiritualize away its political-historical force. Steelman: Psalm 66 is making a historical claim about a real liberation, and the psalm's rhetoric — "all the earth" should know what God has done for Israel — depends on that historical specificity. Christians who read the psalm only typologically miss what it actually says.

*Position C: Goldingay's both-and.* The Exodus is both the founding historical event and the paradigmatic deliverance, and Israel itself read it both ways from early on (see Isa 43:16–21, where the prophet uses Exodus to describe the return from Babylon). Christian typology extends a move already native to Hebrew Scripture's own use. Steelman: the typological reading is not a Christian imposition on Jewish text but the further extension of the way the text has always functioned. Honest reading holds the historical and the typological together.

**Tension 3: The vow-of-burnt-offerings and prophetic critique.** Verses 13–15 are unusually material: "I will offer to you burnt offerings of fattened animals, with the smoke of the sacrifice of rams; I will make an offering of bulls and goats." This is the vocabulary of cult realism — actual animals, actual smoke, actual fat. But the prophetic tradition is sharply critical of exactly this language: Psalm 50:8–13, Psalm 51:16–17, Isaiah 1:11–17, Hosea 6:6, Amos 5:21–24, Micah 6:6–8 all push back against the assumption that burnt offerings are what God wants. How do we hold Psalm 66 alongside the prophetic critique? The honest answer: the psalter contains both voices, and the canonical shape refuses to resolve the tension. The vow-of-burnt-offerings is real; the prophetic critique is real; both belong to Israel's worship tradition. The Christian Eucharist, in part, reads itself into this dialectic — material elements offered in praise, but reframed by prophetic ethics ("if you bring your gift to the altar and there remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there" — Matt 5:23–24).

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 66 holds together what we are tempted to separate: corporate memory and personal testimony, public history and private prayer, fire-and-water trial and burnt-offering thanksgiving. It refuses to spiritualize the trials (the fire and water are real) and refuses to materialize the praise away from ethics (v. 18: "if I had cherished iniquity in my heart, the Lord would not have listened"). The psalm is honest about what suffering survived feels like and honest about the cost of the praise that follows.

**Closing question:** Have you been through your fire and water — and what would it look like to "come into your house with burnt offerings" today, in the cult-form available to you?

## The Thread

- **Exodus 14:21–22.** "He turned the sea into dry land; they passed through the river on foot" (v. 6) is a direct citation of the Reed Sea crossing — the founding act of Israel's deliverance and the historical kernel of the psalm's universal-praise frame.
- **1 Corinthians 10:1–2.** Paul's typological reading — that Israel was "baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea" — establishes the Christian liturgical reading of Psalm 66's fire-and-water as baptismal passage.
- **Isaiah 43:1–3.** "When you pass through the waters, I will be with you ... when you walk through fire you shall not be burned" — Isaiah's use of fire-and-water as covenant language draws on the same imaginative pool as Psalm 66:12.
- **1 Peter 1:6–7.** "Now for a little while, if necessary, you have been grieved by various trials, so that the tested genuineness of your faith — more precious than gold that perishes though it is tested by fire — may be found to result in praise and glory and honor at the revelation of Jesus Christ." Peter draws on the same metallurgical-refining theology that Psalm 66:10 articulates: trial as the medium of praise.
