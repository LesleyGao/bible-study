# 1 Kings 6: The House of Cedar and Gold

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens with one of the most discussed chronological notices in the Hebrew Bible: "In the four hundred and eightieth year after the people of Israel came out of the land of Egypt, in the fourth year of Solomon's reign over Israel, in the month of Ziv, which is the second month, he began to build the house of the LORD." On a conventional reading the fourth year of Solomon falls around 966 BCE, which would push the Exodus to roughly 1446 BCE — a date championed by some evangelical scholars but rejected by most archaeologists who place an Exodus event (if any) in the thirteenth century. Mordechai Cogan in the Anchor Bible commentary, following work by Gershon Galil, treats the 480 as a programmatic, schematized number: twelve generations of forty years, a Deuteronomistic measurement of sacred time rather than a chronicler's count. The figure tells the reader where Temple-building stands in the long arc from liberation to settlement to king to sanctuary. It is theology with the shape of arithmetic.

The material world the chapter conjures is Iron Age IIA Levantine sacred architecture. The dimensions — sixty cubits long, twenty wide, thirty high (roughly 27 × 9 × 13.5 m) — are modest by the standards of Egyptian or Mesopotamian temples but substantial for a hill-country kingdom and consistent with what we now know of regional sanctuaries. The single most significant comparative discovery is the **'Ain Dara temple** in northern Syria, excavated and published by Ali Abu Assaf in 1990. 'Ain Dara dates to the tenth-ninth centuries BCE, sits on a tripartite plan (porch, nave, inner shrine), features lions and cherubim in relief, and matches the Solomonic Temple's proportions to a degree that is genuinely uncanny. John Monson's comparative studies have treated 'Ain Dara as the closest known parallel and it has reshaped how scholars read 1 Kings 6. Tell Tayinat in the Amuq plain offers another Iron II tripartite temple with similar proportions. The cultural model is not Egyptian but **North Syrian-Phoenician**: the Phoenician temples at Tyre and Sarepta (the latter excavated by James Pritchard) provide the immediate idiom.

Three architectural Hebrew terms anchor the chapter and the wider Temple discourse. The **אוּלָם** (*ʾûlām*, "vestibule, porch") is the entry hall projecting eastward; the **הֵיכָל** (*hêkāl*, "nave, main hall") is the long central chamber where lampstands, table, and incense altar stand; the **דְּבִיר** (*dĕbîr*, "inner sanctuary, Most Holy Place") is the cube-shaped innermost room housing the ark. The word *dĕbîr* itself is etymologically debated — older lexicography linked it to *dbr* "to speak" (the place where God speaks), but most contemporary lexicographers prefer a derivation from a root meaning "back, hindmost." Two further terms shape the literary texture: **כְּרֻבִים** (*kĕrubîm*, "cherubim"), the composite winged creatures whose iconography (sphinx-like with human face, lion body, eagle wings, often in throne-flanking pairs) is densely attested in Phoenician and North Syrian ivories — see the Megiddo and Samaria ivory hoards — and **תִּמֹרִים** (*timmōrîm*, "palm-tree carvings"), a stylized vegetal motif also widespread in Iron Age Levantine art.

The Stepped Stone Structure on the eastern slope of the City of David and Eilat Mazar's so-called "Large Stone Structure" have been argued — by Mazar herself — to belong to a tenth-century palatial complex consistent with the biblical description, though Israel Finkelstein and Lily Singer-Avitz have vigorously redated these to later periods. The Solomonic Temple itself has left no archaeological trace, since the Temple Mount has not been excavated and the site has been overbuilt continuously since the Babylonian destruction. What we have, then, is a literary-historical reconstruction triangulating between text, comparable Levantine temples, and the few possibly tenth-century remains in the City of David. The text, on a sober reading, plausibly describes a real building of recognizable Iron IIA type, even if the chronological frame has been theologized.

The labor force is partly elided here and partly displaced to chapter 5: cedars from Lebanon supplied by Hiram of Tyre, Phoenician craftsmen, conscripted Israelite labor — an economy of timber, gold, and sweat. The note that "neither hammer nor axe nor any tool of iron was heard in the house while it was being built" (6:7) reflects a real ancient construction practice — fitting pre-cut ashlar blocks at the site — but is also clearly being given a sacred reading.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is best described as a **building-account** in the technical Near Eastern sense. Victor Hurowitz's monograph *I Have Built You an Exalted House: Temple Building in the Bible in Light of Mesopotamian and Northwest Semitic Writings* showed that 1 Kings 5–9 follows a recognizable five-part building-account structure documented across Mesopotamian royal inscriptions: (1) commission and decision, (2) preparations, (3) description of construction, (4) dedication, (5) divine response and blessing. Chapter 6 sits squarely in stage three, the description proper, and its details — quarry-cut stone, cedar paneling, gold overlay, cherubim — line up with topoi we find in Gudea of Lagash's Temple cylinders or Esarhaddon's Esagila account. The genre signals to ancient readers: this is a serious sanctuary, royally founded, divinely sanctioned.

Within that genre several literary devices stand out. The chapter is structured concentrically around verses 11–13, the divine word that interrupts the architectural description: "if you will walk in my statutes... I will dwell among the children of Israel." Robert Alter and Jerome Walsh have both noted how the architecture is literally framed by covenant: the building lists pause for the conditional, then resume. The Temple's permanence, the form suggests, is not granted by its grandeur but by Solomon's obedience. Polzin reads this as a Deuteronomistic narratorial fingerprint, the redactor's hand pressing into the source.

A second device is the **inclusio** between 6:1 (the four hundred and eightieth year, the fourth year, the month of Ziv, "he began to build") and 6:37–38 (the fourth year, the month of Ziv, the eleventh year, the month of Bul, "he was seven years in building it"). The chapter opens and closes with the same temporal markers, sealing the unit. The mention that the palace took thirteen years (7:1) is held back so that this chapter belongs entirely to the Temple. Fokkelman has argued that the seven-year frame, in a book obsessed with sevens, is itself programmatic.

A third feature is the **carving repertoire**: cherubim, palm trees, open flowers (גֻּדִּים וּפְטוּרֵי צִצִּים — gourds and open flowers in 6:18; cherubim, palm trees, and open flowers repeatedly in 6:29, 32, 35). Carol Meyers in her work on Israelite iconography has read this triad as a stylized Eden — guardian creatures, life-giving tree, blossom — making the *dĕbîr* a microcosm of the primeval garden. Levenson develops this in *Sinai and Zion*: the Temple is cosmos in miniature.

Textually, 1 Kings 6 has known LXX/OG variants, though they are less dramatic than those in surrounding chapters. The Old Greek of 1 Kings ("3 Reigns") reorders substantial blocks of chapters 4–11 and adds duplicate or alternative materials (the so-called Miscellanies of 1 Kings 2 and 1 Kings 12). Within chapter 6, the most discussed variant clusters concern the divine word at 6:11–13, which the Septuagint locates differently — Frank Cross, Julio Trebolle Barrera, and Adrian Schenker have argued that the OG sometimes preserves an earlier Hebrew Vorlage shorter than the MT, with the divine address possibly being a later MT insertion calibrated to dtr theology. If so, the inclusio I described above is the work of a later editor sealing the chapter with covenant. Whether one judges that addition theologically necessary or theologically intrusive shapes the whole reading.

The chapter sits at the structural pivot of 1 Kings 1–11, the Solomon source. After the political consolidation of chapters 1–2, the wisdom display of chapter 3, the administrative apparatus of chapters 4–5, the narrative now reaches its climax: the Temple. Walsh notes the careful pacing — Solomon's wealth and wisdom build through the early chapters and are now expended in stone and gold, all for YHWH. The shadow of chapter 11's apostasy already falls, however, when readers know what is coming.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish reading.** Josephus in *Antiquities* 8.61–98 narrates the construction with characteristic Hellenistic embellishment, multiplying figures and dwelling on the wonder of the work; he is writing for a Greco-Roman audience that will recognize the Temple as a Wonder. The rabbinic tradition treats 6:7 — the silence of iron tools — as a launching point for sustained meditation. The Mishnah (*Middot* 3.4) and the Tosefta develop a tradition that even the iron tools themselves were forbidden because iron is the metal of war and the altar is the maker of peace. The famous legend of the **Shamir worm** — a creature able to cleave stone without iron — appears in *Sotah* 48b and *Gittin* 68a; Solomon, the rabbis say, captured the worm to cut the Temple stones noiselessly. Rashi at 6:7 notes the practice of finishing stones at the quarry and reads the silence as showing that the Temple is a place not of human violence but of divine word.

Radak (Kimhi) and Ralbag both read the chapter philosophically. Ralbag, the Aristotelian, focuses on the proportions and the rationality of the design, treating the Temple as a sensible image of cosmic order. Abarbanel — writing in the wake of the 1492 expulsion from Spain — gives chapter 6 one of its richest medieval readings: he sees in the cherubim of the *dĕbîr* the doubled witness of Israel and humanity, and in the gold overlay covering all stone the truth that holiness ultimately covers and transforms the natural. Avivah Zornberg, in her aggadic mode, has written on the silence of 6:7 as the silence of a making that does not violate; Yair Zakovitch and Moshe Garsiel have both attended to the literary architecture of the building accounts, with Garsiel especially noting puns and name-play.

**Christian reading.** Patristic exegesis quickly typologized the Temple. **Origen** in his *Homilies on Joshua* and scattered fragments takes the Temple as a figure of the Church and of the soul, an interior architecture. **Augustine** in *City of God* 17.8 argues that the Solomonic Temple is a "shadow and prophecy" of the heavenly city, real in its time but pointing beyond. **Ambrose** treats the cedar of Lebanon as a figure of the saints, the gold as charity. **Chrysostom**, more historically grounded, dwells in his Old Testament homilies on the discipline of the labor and the seriousness of the worship. **Theodoret of Cyrus** in his *Questions on 3 Reigns* attends to the architectural details with characteristic precision, defending the historicity of the account against allegorizing excesses while acknowledging typological depth. **Gregory the Great** in *Moralia* and his Ezekiel homilies returns frequently to the cherubim as guardians of contemplation.

**Aquinas** in *Summa Theologiae* III, q. 102 (on the ceremonial law and its types) reads the Temple as instituted to figure Christ — the *hekhal* as Christ's humanity, the *dĕbîr* as his divinity, the cherubim as the angelic witnesses. The typology is not arbitrary but, on Aquinas's account, providentially ordered. **Calvin** in his sermons on 1 Kings (and in the *Institutes* II.7.1–2 on the ceremonial law) is more reserved: the Temple is real and historical, given by God, but Calvin will not allegorize every cubit; he focuses on Solomon's obedience and on YHWH's condescension to be present among an idolatry-prone people. **Luther** in his lectures hammers the point that the Temple is provisional — it points beyond itself to Christ, the true Temple, and Christians who treat church buildings as the new Solomonic Temple have missed the trajectory.

In the modern critical period, **Frank Moore Cross** and **Richard Nelson** have established the Deuteronomistic redactional layers; **Mordechai Cogan**'s Anchor Bible volume is the standard historical-critical commentary; **Marvin Sweeney**'s OTL volume and **Simon DeVries**'s WBC remain heavily used. **Jon Levenson** in *Sinai and Zion* and *Theology of the Program of Restoration of Ezekiel 40–48* reads the Temple as cosmos and Eden in microcosm — the most influential single piece of scholarship on Temple theology in a generation. **Tryggve Mettinger** and **Sandra Richter** have developed name-theology against glory-theology — material I will press hardest in chapter 8 but which begins here. **Walter Brueggemann** reads the Temple ambivalently: it is a real gift and a real danger, a means of presence that can become an idol of presence. **Eugene Peterson**, **Ellen Davis**, **Iain Provan** read pastorally and literarily, attending to Solomon's piety alongside the dtr warning. **Carol Meyers** has done foundational work on Temple iconography and gender. **Israel Finkelstein** raises the harder archaeological question: can a tenth-century Jerusalem have built this?

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — Divine presence: where does YHWH dwell?** The chapter culminates in 6:13: "I will dwell among the children of Israel." Three positions are live. (a) The maximalist incarnational reading: YHWH genuinely takes up residence in the *dĕbîr*, the Temple is his house, and chapter 8's protests that heaven cannot contain him are concessive — God *does* dwell in the Holy of Holies, even if not exhausted there. This is the older glory-theology, with the cloud filling the house, reflected in much patristic and rabbinic thought. (b) The transcendentalist name-theology reading: the Temple is where YHWH causes his *shem* (name) to dwell, which is a way of saying it is the address of prayer, not the residence of God. Mettinger and Richter develop this from chapter 8 backward into chapter 6, arguing that even the cherubim are guardians of an empty throne. (c) The dialectical reading: presence and absence are held together. Brueggemann, in *Theology of the Old Testament*, argues that the Temple stages a *real* presence in tension with a *real* freedom — God shows up and is also free. The strongest version of (a) honors the chapter's plain sense that YHWH means to *dwell* (יָשַׁכְתִּי) here. The strongest version of (b) honors the dtr instinct that YHWH is not domesticated. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter writes presence onto stone but inscribes condition into the writing — YHWH will dwell *if* Israel walks in the statutes. Presence is real and conditional at once.

**Tension 2 — Sacred construction and human labor.** The silence of iron tools (6:7) and the conditional word of 6:11–13 create a tension with what we know — and what chapters 5 and 9 admit — about the human cost: cedars felled, hill-country quarried, conscripted labor, foreign craftsmen paid in wheat and oil. Three positions. (a) The traditional reading: Solomon's piety is the chapter's center, the labor is sanctified by its purpose, and the silence of iron is a sign of the sanctity. Most patristic and medieval readings, much of Calvin, sit here. (b) The prophetic reading: the chapter's lavishness already plants the seed of Solomon's ruin. Brueggemann and Polzin argue that the gold-and-cedar excess sets up chapter 11 by making Solomon's house indistinguishable from any Phoenician monarch's — and the dtr narrator knows it. (c) The post-colonial reading: scholars like Richard Bohannon and, in different idiom, Carol Meyers, read the Temple as imperial architecture, an instrument of state consolidation that dignifies royal power as worship. The strongest version of (a) honors the text's straight-faced theological intent. The strongest version of (b) honors the dtr irony. The strongest version of (c) honors the human cost the text partly elides. **Where this leaves us:** sacred building is never innocent, and the chapter's silence about hammers may be both a piety and a screen.

**Unresolved question:** If 6:11–13 is, as Cross and others argue, a later dtr insertion, does the conditioning of YHWH's dwelling on Solomon's obedience belong to the original Temple theology or is it the exilic editor reading the destruction of 587 backward into the foundation? The pastoral and theological stakes are high either way.

## The Thread

The chapter looks back to **Exodus 25–31**: the Tabernacle is the prior dwelling and the obvious template — cherubim, gold, ark, the cloud that will fill in chapter 8. The Temple is a stone Tabernacle, mobile presence become founded presence. It looks back to **2 Samuel 7**: David is denied the right to build, the right is given to his son, and that son here begins. It looks forward to **1 Kings 8**: the dedication, where the conditions hinted at here become the explicit prayer-theology of exile. It looks forward — and one feels the dtr knowing — to **1 Kings 9:6–9** and finally to **2 Kings 25**, where the Babylonians strip the gold, break the bronze pillars, and burn the cedar. The chapter's most painful intertext is **Jeremiah 7**, where the prophet stands at the gates of this same Temple and announces that its stones will not save a faithless people. To read 1 Kings 6 honestly is to read it with Jeremiah 7 and with the smoke of 587 BCE in the background. The cedar will burn. Whether the *shem* survives the burning is the question 1 Kings 8 will press.
