# Psalm 38 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 38 carries the heading *mizmor le-David le-hazkir* (מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד לְהַזְכִּיר, "a psalm of David, for the memorial-offering"). The notation *le-hazkir* — found only here and on Psalm 70 — is a hiphil infinitive of *zakar* ("to remember") and most commentators connect it to the *azkarah* (אַזְכָּרָה), the "memorial" portion of the grain offering described in Leviticus 2:2, 9, 16 and 24:7. Whether Psalm 38 was actually recited at the offering of the *azkarah* or whether the title is later cultic ascription is debated; Mowinckel (*The Psalms in Israel's Worship*) treated it as cultic prescription, while Hossfeld-Zenger (*Hermeneia*) read it as later editorial context.

Form-critically, Psalm 38 is the third of the seven traditional **Penitential Psalms** of Christian liturgical tradition — Psalms 6, 32, 38, 51, 102, 130, 143 — a grouping first explicitly named by Cassiodorus (sixth century) and canonized by the Western church for use in Lent. Hermann Gunkel classified it as an **individual lament** (*Klagelied des Einzelnen*), with the additional features that make it specifically penitential: explicit confession of sin (vv. 3, 4, 18), connection of suffering to divine displeasure (vv. 1-2), and prayer for forgiveness rather than mere deliverance. Claus Westermann, in *Praise and Lament in the Psalms*, refined Gunkel's category by tracing the typical lament-structure: address, complaint, confession of trust, petition, vow of praise. Psalm 38 contains all but the closing vow of praise — its ending is bare petition, *make haste to help me, O Lord, my salvation*. The truncation is part of its theological texture.

Hebrew terms structure the suffering. *Ein metom bi-vsari* (אֵין־מְתֹם בִּבְשָׂרִי, "no soundness in my flesh," vv. 3, 7) — the noun *metom* denotes integrity, wholeness; its absence is bodily disintegration. *Hivishu nameqqu chaburotai* (הִבְאִישׁוּ נָמַקּוּ חַבּוּרֹתָי, "my wounds stink and fester," v. 5) — the verb *namaq* (to ooze, dissolve) is rare; it appears in Lev 26:39 of those who "rot away" in their iniquities and in Zech 14:12 of the eschatological plague. The collocation with *hivishu* ("stink") gives the verse a clinical-poetic intensity that no English translation fully captures. *Avonotai avru roshi ke-massa kaved yikhbedu mi-meni* (v. 4, "my iniquities have gone over my head; like a heavy burden, they are too heavy for me") — the doubling of the *k-b-d* root (*kaved* / *yikhbedu*) emphasizes the felt-weight.

Dating is contested. The Davidic ascription is traditional. Mitchell Dahood and others see a post-exilic composition; Ross and Goldingay accept the possibility of pre-exilic origin. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of Psalm 38 in 4QPsa, with text largely matching the Masoretic. The Septuagint (Ps 37 LXX) translates *le-hazkir* as *eis anamnēsin* — "for memorial / for remembrance" — preserving the cultic sense.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels are striking. The Babylonian *Ludlul Bēl Nēmeqi* ("I will praise the Lord of Wisdom"), the so-called "Babylonian Job," features a sufferer whose body has been struck by disease, who has been abandoned by friends, and who longs for divine reversal. Mesopotamian *šuilla* prayers ("hand-lifting" prayers) offer the same gestural-ritual context for petition under sickness. Psalm 38 stands in this broader ANE landscape but is distinctive in its tight integration of *bodily suffering* with *moral confession* — the body's ruin is read as the visible aftermath of sin, not merely as fate.

The medieval and early-modern reading often took Psalm 38 as a leprosy or plague psalm, suggesting that the speaker suffered some severe skin disease. The vocabulary fits: *makah* (plague/blow, v. 11), the festering wounds, the social isolation ("my friends and companions stand aloof from my plague"), all parallel the leprosy laws of Leviticus 13-14. Whether the psalmist literally had *tzara'at* or whether the language is a poetic figure for any catastrophic illness is disputed — Goldingay reads the imagery as deliberately ambiguous, available for any sufferer to enter.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Robert Lowth's parallelism patterns dominate. Verse 1 is classical synonymous parallelism with antithetical reinforcement: "rebuke me not in your anger / nor discipline me in your wrath" — *aph* (אַף) and *chemah* (חֵמָה) are the two nouns of divine displeasure, paired everywhere from Deut 9:19 to the prophetic literature. Verse 3 uses tightly synonymous parallelism: "no soundness in my flesh / no health in my bones."

The psalm is built on a triple structure of complaint, marked by three petitions to *Adonai* (vv. 1, 9, 21):

- **Movement 1 (vv. 1-8):** the body in ruin — divine arrows, no soundness, festering wounds, the burning sides.
- **Movement 2 (vv. 9-14):** the social and inner ruin — friends stand aloof, enemies plot, the speaker becomes deaf and mute.
- **Movement 3 (vv. 15-22):** the petition — confession of trust (*ki-lekha Yahweh hochalti*, v. 15), confession of sin (v. 18), and closing prayer for nearness.

The three address-formulas (*Yahweh* in v. 1, *Adonai* in v. 9, *Yahweh* / *Adonai* / *Adonai yeshu'ati* compounded in vv. 21-22) frame the movements. Patrick Miller and Goldingay note this as a deliberate liturgical scaffolding.

The middle of the psalm contains a **silence-strophe** (vv. 13-14) of unusual psychological depth: *va-ani ke-cheresh lo-eshma u-khe-illem lo-yiftach piv* (וַאֲנִי כְחֵרֵשׁ לֹא אֶשְׁמָע וּכְאִלֵּם לֹא יִפְתַּח־פִּיו, "but I am like a deaf man; I do not hear, like a mute man who does not open his mouth"). The speaker, surrounded by accusing voices, refuses speech. This silence-motif is structurally central — it is the hinge between the description of suffering and the prayer for help.

Inclusio binds the psalm. The opening *al-be-qetzpekha tochicheni* ("rebuke me not in your wrath") and the closing *chusha le-ezrati Adonai teshu'ati* ("make haste to help me, O Lord, my salvation") frame a movement from dread of divine anger to plea for divine help — the same God, addressed in two postures. There is no *selah* in this psalm; the lament is sustained, unbroken, with no liturgical pause.

The verse-count (22 verses) suggests for some scholars a *quasi-acrostic* structure — Psalm 38 has the same number of verses as the Hebrew alphabet, though the verses do not actually run alphabetically. Skehan and others have noted that several Davidic laments share this 22-verse architecture (Pss 25, 33, 34, 38, 103, 145), perhaps a structural memory of the alphabet's completeness. Whether intentional or not, the device gestures at totality — *all* the letters of suffering are spelled out.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Within Book I, Psalm 38 sits between the wisdom-confidence of Psalm 37 and the meditative brevity of Psalm 39. The juxtaposition is striking: the wisdom psalm has just declared *I have not seen the righteous forsaken* (37:25), and immediately we are confronted with a sufferer whose friends stand aloof and whose body is rotting. Patrick Miller and Gerald Wilson read this as a deliberate canonical move — the Psalter holds wisdom-confidence and lament side by side, refusing to let either silence the other.

**Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 38 connects the *azkarah* heading to the priestly memorial-offering and reads the psalm as Israel's plea that her suffering be "remembered" before God in the way the offering's incense ascends. Rashi reads the psalm as Davidic — the suffering is David's after the Bathsheba episode (2 Sam 12), the wounds and disease the bodily aftermath of sin. Ibn Ezra reads more universally, treating the psalm as available to any sufferer of grave illness. Radak sees a typological reading: the psalm fits both David and Israel-in-exile. Nachmanides develops the connection between sin and bodily suffering with characteristic moral seriousness.

In modern Jewish scholarship: Nahum Sarna treats the psalm as a sustained lament with no easy resolution. Jon Levenson's *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* and *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son* situate such laments within Israel's covenantal expectation that suffering is heard. Buber, in *Right and Wrong*, attends to the psychology of the silence-strophe (vv. 13-14) as the moment when the sufferer becomes the *eved* (servant) — refusing self-justification, leaving the case in YHWH's hands. Heschel emphasizes the psalm's prayer-quality: the sufferer continues to address YHWH even when the body and friends have collapsed. Avivah Zornberg's literary approach would read the silence-strophe as the deepest theological gesture — the *Schweigen* of one who knows speech is futile yet keeps facing God.

Liturgically, Psalm 38 is recited in some Sephardic traditions on fast days and in penitential contexts.

**Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* (Latin Ps 37) is among his most personally felt expositions. He reads the psalm Christologically — the wounds, the silence, the abandonment by friends become figurations of Christ's passion — and ecclesiologically — the church suffers in her members what Christ suffered in his body. The silence-strophe is for Augustine the moment of Christ before Pilate and Caiaphas (Mark 14:61, 15:5; Matt 26:63), the *agnus Dei* who "did not open his mouth" (Isa 53:7). This Christological reading of Psalm 38's silence becomes standard in the Western tradition.

Athanasius, in his *Letter to Marcellinus*, recommends Psalm 38 for those who recognize their sin and feel the weight of divine discipline. Cassiodorus formalized the psalm's place in the seven Penitential Psalms; for Cassiodorus, Psalm 38 is the cry of the soul aware that suffering is the medicine of mercy. Aquinas treats the psalm in the *Postilla* as an extended exposition of the relationship between sin, satisfaction, and divine compassion.

Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 38 develops the theology of *Anfechtung* (spiritual trial): the believer feels the weight of sin and the silence of God simultaneously, and the psalm teaches the soul to wait. Calvin's 1557 commentary cautions against a mechanical sin-suffering equation while affirming that, for the faithful, suffering can be the chastening of a Father's hand. The Penitential Psalms became a major Lutheran genre — Bach's *Tilge, Höchster, meine Sünden* (BWV 1083) is a setting of Psalm 51, but the broader Lutheran chorale tradition drew on all seven, including 38.

Modern critical interpreters — Gunkel, Mowinckel, Westermann, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate, Wenham, Brueggemann, Patrick Miller, Brown, Tanner, Gillingham — generally agree on the lament-with-confession genre. Brueggemann's *The Message of the Psalms* draws from Psalm 38 his category of "psalms of disorientation" — the worshipper has fallen out of the *ordered* world and articulates the chaos in prayer. Bonaventure (*Lignum Vitae*) reads the silence-strophe as figural of Christ's silent passion. In modern pastoral theology, Henri Nouwen (*The Wounded Healer*, *The Inner Voice of Love*) draws on Psalm 38 and the silence of suffering as a resource for Christian ministry of presence.

**NT use.** Psalm 38 is not directly cited in the New Testament, but its imagery permeates the Passion narratives. Mark 14:50 ("they all left him and fled") and Mark 14:61, 15:5 (Jesus' silence before Caiaphas and Pilate) read like enactments of Ps 38:11-14. Hebrews 5:7 and 12:5-11 develop the theology of suffering-as-discipline.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The connection between sin and bodily suffering.** Psalm 38 reads its body's ruin as the consequence of sin (vv. 3, 5, 18). This reading is contested across the canon. (a) **Direct retributive reading (some rabbinic tradition, Job's friends, much medieval Christian piety):** sin causes suffering; suffering reveals sin. (b) **Job's protest:** Job suffers without sin proportionate to his ruin; the friends' theology is rebuked. (c) **John 9:3:** "neither this man sinned, nor his parents, that he was born blind" — Jesus refuses the direct equation. (d) **Pauline reading (Rom 5, 1 Cor 11:30):** sin and suffering are related at the cosmic level (creation under futility) but not always at the personal level; specific suffering can sometimes be discipline (1 Cor 11:30), but not always. (e) **Modern theological reading (Moltmann, Wright, Hauerwas):** the sufferer's body is a site of fallen creation, not always of personal sin; the cross-event reframes suffering as divine solidarity rather than divine punishment.

The strongest steelman for the canonical reading is to hold Psalm 38 *with* Job and *with* John 9. Psalm 38 articulates one true voice: the worshipper who *does* know his sin and who feels his body broken under its weight. Job articulates another: the worshipper whose suffering exceeds any proportion to his sin. John 9 articulates a third: the suffering that is not personal-sin-related at all. The canon does not collapse these voices into a single theology. Psalm 38 is true *for the one who, like David, knows his sin*, not as a universal explanatory schema. The danger is reading it as the universal; the loss is reading it as merely sectional. The truth is: when sin and suffering converge, this is the prayer.

**Tension 2: The silence of the suffering and the Christological reading.** The silence-strophe (vv. 13-14) has carried enormous theological weight in Christian tradition. (a) **Original-Davidic reading (Goldingay, much modern critical reading):** the silence is the sufferer's strategic refusal to engage accusers — a wisdom of self-restraint, not theological mystery. (b) **Christological-typological reading (Augustine, Aquinas, Luther, much patristic and medieval tradition):** the silence is figural of Christ's silence before Caiaphas and Pilate; the psalm prefigures the Passion. (c) **Existential-spiritual reading (Bonaventure, Henri Nouwen, modern contemplative tradition):** the silence is a posture for any sufferer — the moment when speech fails and the only prayer is wordless presence before God. (d) **Liturgical reading (the seven Penitential Psalms tradition):** the silence is the church's silence in Lent, the body of Christ silent before its sin and its judge.

The strongest steelman for the Christological reading is canonical: Isaiah 53:7 explicitly portrays the Servant who "did not open his mouth," and the gospel writers consciously frame Jesus' Passion in the cadence of these lament-and-silence texts. Whether or not Psalm 38 was *originally* written with such a figure in view, the New Testament's reception of these silences as a theological pattern is itself a canonical fact. Augustine's reading is not arbitrary; it is reading-with-the-canon.

Where this leaves us: Psalm 38 sits at the most painful intersection of body, sin, and silence. It does not tell us how to disentangle the threads. It teaches us how to pray when they have already tangled. The closing prayer — *make haste to help me, O Lord, my salvation* — is the bare petition of one who has nothing left to say. The psalm grants permission to pray exactly that, when nothing more is possible.

**Closing question:** When the sufferer in v. 18 says "I confess my iniquity; I am sorry for my sin," is this an explanation of his suffering, a free confession alongside it, or a transformation of his suffering into something else — and how does the answer change the way we sit beside someone whose body is in ruin?

## The Thread

- **Psalm 6:** the first of the seven Penitential Psalms; *Yahweh, al-be-aphkha tochicheni* — Ps 38 quotes the same opening, marking its place in the penitential sequence.
- **Psalm 51:** the heart of the Penitential Psalms; David's confession after the Bathsheba episode that Ps 38's tradition reads as the same David, the same sin-and-suffering grammar.
- **Isaiah 53:7:** "he was oppressed and afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth" — the Servant Song's silence that Ps 38:13-14 anticipates.
- **Mark 14:61 / 15:5:** Jesus silent before Caiaphas and Pilate — the Christological enactment of the silence-strophe.
