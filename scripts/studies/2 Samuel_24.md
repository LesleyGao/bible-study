# 2 Samuel 24: The Census, the Plague, and an Altar on the Threshing Floor

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens with a theological declaration that has produced more interpretive debate than perhaps any other single verse in Samuel: "**Again the anger of YHWH was kindled against Israel, and he incited David against them, saying, 'Go, number Israel and Judah'**" (24:1). The Hebrew *vayyaset* (וַיָּסֶת) — "and he incited" — is a hiphil that means "to entice, to instigate." The subject is YHWH; the object is David; the action incited is what the text immediately treats as sinful. The theological problem is direct: YHWH incites David to a sin for which YHWH then punishes Israel. The parallel at **1 Chronicles 21:1** — written several centuries later — substantially revises the theology: "**Satan stood up against Israel and incited David to number Israel**." The Chronicler's substitution of *Satan* for YHWH is one of the clearest examples in the Hebrew Bible of evolving theological reflection on the question of evil's source. **Peggy Day** in *An Adversary in Heaven: Satan in the Hebrew Bible* (1988), **Henry Ansgar Kelly** in *Satan: A Biography* (2006), **Elaine Pagels** in *The Origin of Satan* (1995), and **Neil Forsyth** in *The Old Enemy: Satan and the Combat Myth* (1987) all develop the trajectory: in pre-exilic Israelite theology, evil was attributed directly to YHWH (compare Exodus 4:21, "I will harden Pharaoh's heart"; 1 Kings 22:19-23, the lying spirit in YHWH's council; Isaiah 45:7, "I form light and create darkness, I make peace and create evil — I YHWH do all these things"); in post-exilic Israelite theology, the function of inciting evil was increasingly attributed to a supernatural adversary (the *satan* of Job 1-2, of Zechariah 3, and finally of 1 Chronicles 21). The 2 Samuel 24 / 1 Chronicles 21 pair is the most studied case in this trajectory.

The **census** itself raises distinct theological questions. In the broader ANE world, census-taking was politically routine — Assyrian, Egyptian, and Hittite royal records preserve census documents — but it carried sacred risk in the Israelite tradition. **Exodus 30:11-16** prescribes that whenever Israel is numbered, each man must give a half-shekel "ransom for his life" (*kofer nafsho*) to YHWH, "lest there be no plague among them when you number them." The provision treats census as inherently dangerous: the act of counting persons exposes them to divine plague unless properly cultically managed. **Mari texts** preserve similar concerns about the cultic management of census-taking; the apprehension is not unique to Israel. The narrative assumes the reader knows the danger; Joab's resistance ("why does my lord the king delight in this thing?" — 24:3) is the political-religious instinct of a soldier who knows the cost.

The political function of the census is also clear. Counting "the men who drew the sword" (24:9) is military mobilization — David is preparing for some kind of administrative or military expansion that requires accurate manpower numbers. The total figures — **800,000 in Israel, 500,000 in Judah** — are typically inflated narrative figures (real Iron Age IIA Israelite populations were almost certainly smaller; Israel Finkelstein's demographic estimates put 10th-c. BCE Israelite population in the 100,000-200,000 range, with armed males a fraction of that). The 1 Chronicles 21:5 parallel gives different numbers (1,100,000 in Israel, 470,000 in Judah). The numerical disagreement is one of the standard textual-critical puzzles.

The **geographic itinerary** of Joab's census team (24:5-7) is the chapter's most specific historical-geographic content. The route runs from **Aroer** on the Arnon River (the southern Transjordanian border with Moab — well-attested in the Mesha Stele as a contested town between Israel and Moab in the 9th c.), across the Jordan, north through **Gilead** to **Kadesh** in the **land of the Hittites** (likely Kadesh on the Orontes, the famous Hittite-Egyptian battle site of 1274 BCE — but the geographical reach implied is debated; some scholars identify Kadesh more locally), then to **Dan** at the headwaters of the Jordan (Tel Dan, where the Tel Dan Stele was later set up), then to **Sidon** and **the fortress of Tyre** on the Phoenician coast, then down through Hivite and Canaanite cities to **Beersheba** in the Negev. The route describes the maximal reach of David's claimed influence — from Aroer to Beersheba is the southern axis; Dan to Beersheba (24:15) is the standard merism for the full extent of Israel; the inclusion of Tyre and Sidon implies Phoenician tributary status. The **nine months and twenty days** (24:8) for the census is plausible for an Iron Age administrative survey of this scale.

**Gad the prophet** (24:11) is one of David's three named prophets (alongside Nathan and possibly the unnamed prophet of 1 Samuel 22:5). Gad first appears in 1 Samuel 22:5, urging David to leave the cave at Adullam; he reappears here at the end of David's reign as the bearer of the divine choice-of-three. The Chronicler attributes a "Book of Gad the Seer" (1 Chronicles 29:29) as one of his sources for Davidic history; whether such a book ever existed is uncertain. Gad's role here as the divine emissary delivering the choice ("three things I offer you; choose one of them") is structurally similar to the prophetic-messenger role at later moments in Israelite history (e.g., Isaiah and Hezekiah at 2 Kings 20).

The **threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite** (24:16, 18) is the chapter's most theologically and historically loaded location. **Araunah** (אֲרַוְנָה) is named with what some scholars (P. Kyle McCarter, Mary Douglas) have analyzed as a Hurrian or Hittite-derived royal title — possibly related to the Hurrian *ewri* "lord" — suggesting Araunah may have been a remnant Jebusite ruling figure rather than a private landowner. The 1 Chronicles parallel names him **Ornan** (אָרְנָן), suggesting variant traditions of the name. The threshing floor is identified at **2 Chronicles 3:1** as the site of Solomon's Temple — "Then Solomon began to build the house of the LORD in Jerusalem on **Mount Moriah**, where the LORD had appeared to David his father, on the place that David had appointed, on the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite." The Chronicler links three sites in one verse: the chapter 24 threshing floor, Solomon's Temple Mount, and **Mount Moriah** of Genesis 22 (where Abraham bound Isaac). The identification gives Jerusalem's Temple Mount the deepest possible patriarchal-Davidic-Solomonic genealogy. The **Temple Mount** (Haram esh-Sharif) sits today on the rocky outcropping called the *eben shetiyyah* (foundation stone), traditionally identified as the threshing-floor rock; archaeology of the site has been severely limited by political-religious sensitivity, and the historical reconstruction depends primarily on textual evidence.

The **purchase price** is one of the chapter's discussed details. 2 Samuel 24:24 gives **fifty shekels of silver** for the threshing floor and the oxen; 1 Chronicles 21:25 gives **600 shekels of gold by weight** for "the place." The discrepancy is variously explained (the 50 shekels were for the immediate threshing floor and oxen; the 600 gold shekels were for the surrounding Temple-site land — Sara Japhet on the Chronicler's harmonization). The purchase is the foundational legal-cultic act of Israelite Temple ownership: the holiest site in Israelite religion is acquired by a price, not by conquest or inheritance.

The **angel of YHWH** (24:16, 17) standing at the threshing floor with sword drawn is one of the most vivid theophany images in the historical books. Compare Joshua 5:13-15 (the commander of YHWH's army with sword drawn at Jericho), Numbers 22:23 (the angel of YHWH with sword drawn before Balaam's ass). The visual vocabulary is distinct: divine intermediary, drawn sword, threshold position. The angel's halt at the threshing floor — between the destroying angel and Jerusalem — establishes the site's cultic significance.

A few Hebrew terms transform the chapter. ***Vayyaset*** (וַיָּסֶת), "and he incited," at 24:1 — the verb that creates the chapter's central theological problem. The hiphil of *sut* means to entice or induce, and its application to YHWH inducing David to sin is the chapter's hardest theological line. ***Niham*** (נִחָם), "relented, repented," at 24:16 — the same divine-regret vocabulary that appears at Genesis 6:6 (YHWH regretting the creation of humanity), Exodus 32:14 (YHWH relenting from destroying Israel after the golden calf), Jonah 3:10 (YHWH relenting from destroying Nineveh). Engages classical immutability vs. open-theist debates. ***Hoshia*** (הוֹשִׁיעַ), "save," and ***ya'akhel*** (יֹאכַל), "consume," form the chapter's vocabulary of plague-and-rescue. ***Goren*** (גֹּרֶן), "threshing floor," is the chapter's pivotal location term — the same Hebrew word used at Ruth 3 of Boaz's threshing floor and at numerous other texts of the threshing-floor as cultic-civic space (1 Kings 22:10, Hosea 9:1, etc.). ***Tappel*** (תַּפֵּל), "pleaded, accepted," at 24:25 of YHWH responding to David's intercession.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is structured in five movements: the divine incitement and census (24:1-9), David's repentance (24:10), Gad's choice-of-three (24:11-14), the plague and the angel (24:15-17), the altar-purchase and intercession (24:18-25). Each movement is short and dense; the chapter compresses a full theological-narrative arc into 25 verses.

The chapter functions as the **closing of the Samuel narrative** and as the canonical-narrative bridge to Solomon's Temple. The placement at the appendix's conclusion (and as the final chapter of the book) gives it weight as theological summation. **Brevard Childs** in his canonical-criticism work treats the chapter as the appendix's intentional terminus — the entire Samuel narrative concludes with David building an altar on the future Temple site. **Walter Brueggemann** treats the chapter as the "Davidic mortgage" payment — the king who built the kingdom dies setting the conditions for the Temple. **Robert Polzin's *David and the Deuteronomist*** reads the chapter literarily as the deuteronomist's theological closing of the Davidic narrative.

The chapter's **structural mirroring with chapter 21** (the appendix's opening Saulide-bloodguilt narrative) completes the chiastic frame. Both chapters: divine wrath against Israel revealed; consultation produces the cause (Saul's bloodguilt against the Gibeonites in 21; David's census in 24); resolution requires costly action; intercessory figures (Rizpah in 21; David in 24) step into the breach; the resolution is achieved at a specific cultic site. The chiastic A-A' position of chapters 21 and 24 within the appendix is widely recognized (Childs, Brueggemann, J. Cheryl Exum, Klaus Koch).

The chapter's literary craft is concentrated in three places. First, the **opening theological declaration** at 24:1 is one of the Hebrew Bible's most condensed difficult lines: divine wrath, divine incitement, command-to-sin in seventeen Hebrew words. The line forces the theological problem on the reader without softening. **Brueggemann** notes that the canonical narrator could have softened (the Chronicler did soften, by substituting Satan); the Samuel narrator chose not to. Second, the **choice-of-three** at 24:13 is one of the cleanest narrative dilemmas in the historical books: famine, military defeat, plague — three years, three months, three days. The numerical pattern (3-3-3) creates a clear formal symmetry; David's choice ("let us fall into the hand of YHWH for his mercy is great, but let me not fall into the hand of man") is the chapter's theological key. Third, the **altar-purchase scene** at 24:18-25 is structured as a classical Iron Age contract negotiation: Araunah offers the threshing floor and oxen as gift; David refuses, insisting on purchase; price agreed; transaction completed. The narrative pattern is similar to Genesis 23 (Abraham purchasing the cave at Machpelah from Ephron the Hittite) — both Davidic-patriarchal narratives of foundational land purchase by negotiated price. **Robert Alter** (*The David Story*) and **Brueggemann** both note the parallel.

David's intercessory cry at 24:17 — **"Behold, I have sinned, and I have done wickedly. But these sheep, what have they done? Please let your hand be against me and against my father's house"** — is one of the most striking moral statements in the David narrative. The king who has compassed his throne by political calculation here speaks pure intercession: the people are sheep, the responsibility is mine, let the hand fall on me. The vocabulary recalls Exodus 32:32 (Moses interceding for Israel after the golden calf — "but now, if you will forgive their sin — but if not, please blot me out of your book that you have written"). The Mosaic-Davidic parallel is part of the chapter's canonical placement.

Textual evidence: **4QSam-a** preserves substantial fragments of the chapter, with notable variants. The most significant variant cluster involves the angel's halt at the threshing floor (24:16-17), where 4QSam-a preserves a longer reading that some scholars treat as the original. The **1 Chronicles 21 parallel** is structurally very similar but theologically and textually different: Satan replaces YHWH as inciter (21:1), the numbers differ (21:5), the price is given as 600 gold shekels (21:25), and the Chronicler adds the explicit identification of the threshing floor as the Temple site (1 Chron 22:1). The Chronicler's editorial method — preserving the structure while revising key theological and detail-level elements — is a paradigm case for the relationship between Samuel-Kings and Chronicles. **Sara Japhet's *I and II Chronicles*** commentary develops the comparison.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, 2 Samuel 24 is the bridge that closes Samuel and opens the architectural arc to Solomon's Temple. The threshing floor David purchases here becomes the foundation site of the Temple in 1 Kings 5-7 and in 2 Chronicles 3:1; the Temple becomes the religious center of Israel for nearly a thousand years (built c. 950 BCE, destroyed 587/586 BCE, rebuilt c. 516 BCE, finally destroyed 70 CE); the Temple Mount becomes the contested holy site of three major world religions. The chapter's altar on the threshing floor is foundational geography in the most far-reaching sense.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Josephus in *Antiquities* 7.318-334 retells the chapter at length, smoothing some theological difficulty (Josephus implies the divine incitement was a test rather than command-to-sin) and developing the threshing-floor location identification. The Talmud (Berakhot 62b, Mo'ed Katan 27a, Yoma 22b) discusses the chapter at length: Berakhot 62b famously narrates that David asked the angel for the right to sacrifice a *me'il* (robe) instead of the censer of incense, reflecting on David's role as priest-king; Yoma 22b discusses the divine-incitement question with rabbinic theological seriousness. The rabbinic tradition treats the chapter as foundational for Temple theology and develops the link to Mount Moriah extensively. **Rashi** (11th c.) develops the rabbinic readings; he treats the divine incitement as YHWH's hidden providence working through David's pride. **Radak** (David Kimhi, 12th c.) attends to the textual cruxes (the discrepancy with 1 Chronicles, the variant numbers, the angel's location). **Ralbag** (Gersonides, 14th c.) extracts moral *to'aliyot* about pride, intercession, and the proper relationship between king and people. **Maimonides** in the *Guide for the Perplexed* (III.8-12) develops a sustained engagement with the divine-evil question that has 2 Samuel 24 as one of its key texts; Maimonides' position is that what appears as divine causation of evil is actually divine permission of evil, and the human will is the proximate cause. **Abarbanel** (15th c.) reads the chapter as the closing study in Davidic kingship — the king who concludes the book repenting of his sin and interceding for his people is the chapter's pastoral-political portrait. **Avivah Zornberg** in modern Jewish reading attends to David's intercessory cry as moment of pure ethical clarity. **Tikva Frymer-Kensky** notes the absence of women's voices in the chapter and the fact that the 70,000 dead were all "men who drew the sword" by the census's own definition — the women and children whose lives the plague affected are not counted.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic Christianity read the chapter through several lenses. **Origen's** homiletic fragments treat the divine-incitement problem theologically and develop the altar-purchase as figure of Christ's purchase of redemption. **Augustine** in *City of God* 17 and elsewhere develops the chapter's theological problem at length: the divine incitement is to be read as God's permission of David's pre-existing pride, not God's direct causation of sin. Augustine's reading shaped the medieval tradition. **Jerome's** commentary engages the textual variants. **Ambrose** treats the chapter as model of royal repentance. **Chrysostom**'s homilies on 2 Samuel develop David's intercessory cry ("these sheep, what have they done?") as model of pastoral leadership. **Gregory the Great** in the *Moralia* uses the chapter's three-fold choice as figure of three modes of divine discipline. The medieval *Glossa Ordinaria* compiles the patristic readings; **Bernard of Clairvaux** develops the threshing-floor as figure of the Christian altar. **Aquinas** in the *Summa* treats the chapter in his discussion of providence (I q. 22) and in his discussion of the relationship between divine and human causation in moral evil. The Christian tradition has generally preferred either Augustine's permission-not-causation reading or some version of the Chronicler's Satan-revision.

The Reformation shifted register. **Luther's** lectures attend to the chapter's theology of repentance and the typological reading of the altar. **Calvin's** sermons on 2 Samuel give the chapter substantial attention as a study in providence: Calvin defends a strong-providence reading (YHWH's incitement is real and direct) while preserving David's moral responsibility (David acted from his own pride, even though YHWH's secret providence was working through it). The Reformed scholastic tradition (Westminster Confession III.1 on providence) develops similar accounts. **Open Theist** readings (Greg Boyd, John Sanders, Clark Pinnock) read the chapter as evidence against meticulous divine sovereignty: divine incitement of evil is theologically impossible, so the text's surface meaning must be revised. The post-Reformation Pietist and Puritan tradition (Matthew Henry, Jonathan Edwards) develops both the theological problem and the devotional content of David's intercession.

The critical period (Wellhausen, Noth) treated the chapter as part of the Davidic narrative's theological closure. **McCarter's** Anchor Bible commentary develops the chapter at length, especially the textual issues. **A. A. Anderson's** WBC commentary is detailed. **Sara Japhet's** Chronicles commentary develops the Samuel-Chronicles comparison. **Peggy Day's *An Adversary in Heaven*** treats the chapter as foundational for the development of the Satan figure. **Baruch Halpern's *David's Secret Demons*** and **Steven McKenzie's *King David: A Biography*** read the chapter politically — the census was real military mobilization, the plague was real epidemiological event, the apologetic narrative manages the theological framing. **Walter Brueggemann's *First and Second Samuel*** holds the theological and political readings together. **Robert Polzin's *David and the Deuteronomist*** reads the chapter literarily as the deuteronomist's closure. The contemporary turn includes feminist readings (the chapter's silence about women among the 70,000 dead), epidemiological readings (Iron Age plague pathology — possibly bubonic plague, possibly cholera), and political-theology readings (the relationship between sovereign census-power and sovereign accountability).

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Both traditions read the chapter as foundational for the Temple-site etymology and develop the link to Mount Moriah. They diverge sharply on the divine-incitement question: classical Christian theology has generally preferred Augustine's permission-not-causation reading; classical rabbinic theology has generally preserved the surface meaning while developing midrashic context; Open Theism reads against both. They converge on David's intercessory cry as a moment of moral clarity. They diverge on whether the Chronicler's Satan-revision is a theological development (most contemporary critical scholarship) or a parallel preserved tradition (some traditional readings). The shift across centuries has been from interpretive harmonization to honest engagement with the theological tension the canonical text preserves.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author of 2 Samuel believe God was doing in this chapter? The theology operates on two registers. The **surface register** attributes the entire sequence to YHWH: YHWH's anger, YHWH's incitement, YHWH's plague, YHWH's halting of the plague, YHWH's response to David's intercession. The chapter does not soften the divine causation. The **canonical register** holds the chapter alongside the Chronicler's parallel and alongside the broader Hebrew Bible's evolving theological reflection on evil's source. The chapter is a load-bearing text in the canonical conversation about how to think about divine causation, divine permission, and human responsibility.

**Tension One: Divine incitement to sin — direct causation, permission, or theological development?** The chapter's opening verse is the clearest statement in the Hebrew Bible of YHWH directly inciting a human being to a sin for which YHWH then punishes the people. The Chronicler's parallel (1 Chron 21:1) substitutes Satan; the substitution itself is evidence that the theological question was felt acutely.

The classical Christian reading (Augustine, Aquinas) holds that YHWH does not directly cause sin but permits it; YHWH's incitement of David is a metaphor for YHWH's lifting of restraint, allowing David's pre-existing pride to bear its natural fruit. The Catholic tradition's distinction between God's primary causation (which is good and never evil) and God's permission of secondary causation (which can be evil) is the framework. Augustine in *City of God* and Aquinas in *Summa* I q. 22 develop this at length.

The classical Reformed reading (Calvin, Westminster Confession) holds a stronger view: divine providence governs all events directly, including events that involve sin, but the divine governance does not transfer moral responsibility from the sinful agent to God. The text's surface meaning (YHWH incited David) can be preserved without making God morally responsible for the sin. Calvin's *Institutes* I.16-18 develops this at length.

The classical Jewish tradition holds something between, with Maimonides (*Guide* III.8-12) developing a permission-not-causation account similar to Augustine's, while the Talmudic tradition (Berakhot 62b) preserves the surface meaning with various harmonizing midrashic supplements.

The Open Theist reading (Greg Boyd, John Sanders, Clark Pinnock) reads the chapter as evidence against meticulous divine sovereignty: God cannot incite sin, so the chapter's surface meaning must be revised — perhaps as poetic-rhetorical attribution, perhaps as later theological judgment.

The contemporary critical reading (Peggy Day, Henry Ansgar Kelly, James Kugel) reads the chapter alongside the Chronicler's parallel as evidence for the **historical evolution** of Israelite theology. In the pre-exilic period, evil was attributed directly to YHWH because the theological vocabulary for an alternate supernatural agent (Satan) did not yet exist. The Chronicler's substitution reflects the post-exilic development of the Satan figure. The 2 Samuel 24 / 1 Chronicles 21 pair is evidence that Israelite theology developed.

The position to steelman is the historical-evolution reading. It is sometimes resisted by traditional readers because it treats the canonical text as theologically developmental rather than internally consistent. But the reading takes seriously what the textual data shows: the same story is told with different theological framings in two canonical books. The development is not an argument against the truth of either; it is recognition that the canonical traditions held both framings as authoritative for their respective communities.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter's divine-incitement theology is harder to read straight than the harmonizing traditions sometimes acknowledge. The Chronicler's Satan-substitution is itself canonical evidence that the question was felt to be hard. To read the chapter honestly requires either a permission-not-causation framework, a strong-providence framework that holds moral responsibility separately, or a developmental account that treats the two canonical framings as the conversation's stages.

**Tension Two: David's intercessory cry — pure pastoral love or partial moral evasion?** David's cry at 24:17 — "Behold, I have sinned, and I have done wickedly. But these sheep, what have they done? Please let your hand be against me and against my father's house" — is the chapter's most morally clear moment. It echoes Moses's intercession for Israel at Exodus 32:32 and prefigures Jesus's pastoral identification with the sheep in John 10.

The traditional reading (rabbinic, patristic, Reformation) treats the cry as pure: the king who has just sinned takes responsibility, names the people as innocent sheep, and asks that the punishment fall on him. The intercessory cry is the chapter's moral redemption.

The contemporary critical reading (Brueggemann, Halpern, Polzin) holds the cry as both real and partial. David takes responsibility, which is real moral clarity; but the people are not actually innocent in the chapter's logic ("the anger of YHWH was kindled against Israel" at 24:1 — the people had done something, even if the text does not name it). The chapter's framing is that YHWH's anger against Israel was the prior condition; the census was the proximate occasion; David's responsibility is real but partial. The intercessory cry is morally clear but theologically incomplete.

A mediating position (some traditional readings, Eugene Peterson) holds that both can be true: David's intercession is genuinely pastoral, and the people's sin was real even if unspecified; the king takes the responsibility for the proximate occasion of the punishment without absolving the deeper theological condition.

The position to steelman is the contemporary reading that holds the cry as partial. It is sometimes treated as deflating, but it preserves something the traditional reading sometimes elides: the chapter's opening verse names YHWH's anger against Israel (not David alone) as the prior condition. The cry is real moral clarity, but it is also pastoral-political move that effectively absorbs the punishment into the king's family ("against me and against my father's house" — 24:17) without engaging the deeper theological question of why YHWH was angry with Israel in the first place.

**Where this leaves us:** David's cry is the chapter's moral pinnacle and also its incomplete theological reckoning. Both readings can be held without choosing — the cry is morally generous, the chapter's deeper theological condition remains unresolved.

The unresolved question this chapter leaves us with: When the divine incitement to a sin and the divine punishment for the sin are spoken in the same text by the same speaker, what does it mean to hold the canonical witness honestly — without softening the difficulty, without erasing the moral seriousness, and without losing the David who built an altar where there had been only plague?

## The Thread

**Genesis 22.** Abraham binds Isaac on **Mount Moriah** — the same mountain that 2 Chronicles 3:1 identifies as the Temple Mount, the same site as the threshing floor David purchases in 2 Samuel 24. The patriarchal-Davidic-Solomonic genealogy of the holiest site in Israelite religion runs from Abraham's near-sacrifice of his son to David's altar to Solomon's Temple. When you reread Genesis 22, the rock where Abraham raised the knife is the rock where David built the altar.

**Exodus 30:11-16.** The Mosaic legislation that requires every numbered Israelite to give a half-shekel "ransom for his life" so that "no plague may come upon them when you number them." The chapter's plague after the unmediated census is the legislation's narrative consequence. When you read the Exodus census-ransom provision, the 70,000 dead in 2 Samuel 24 are the cost of the unpaid ransom.

**1 Kings 5-8 and 2 Chronicles 3.** Solomon builds the Temple on the threshing floor David purchased, using the silver and gold David accumulated, as the architectural fulfillment of David's intent. The chapter that closes Samuel sets the foundation for the chapter that opens the Temple. When you read 1 Kings 5-8 or 2 Chronicles 3, the threshing floor of Araunah is the site already prepared by the closing chapter of Samuel.

**1 Chronicles 21:1.** The same story, with Satan substituted for YHWH as the inciter. The Chronicler's revision is one of the clearest cases in the Hebrew Bible of post-exilic theological reflection on the question of evil's source. When you read 1 Chronicles 21, hold it next to 2 Samuel 24 to see the canonical conversation about how to think about divine causation and human responsibility.
