# 2 Chronicles 33 — Manasseh's Repentance: The Chronicler's Most Distinctive Theological Move

## Lens 1 — The Historical World Behind the Text

Second Chronicles 33 is one of the most theologically charged chapters in the Chronicler's entire history, and it is so for a single reason: it tells a story about King Manasseh that 2 Kings 21 — the parallel account — does not tell. In 2 Kings, Manasseh is the worst king in Judah's history, the king whose sins (Kings explicitly says) cause the Babylonian exile (2 Kgs 21:11-15, 23:26, 24:3-4). In 2 Chronicles 33, Manasseh is still the worst king at the start — child sacrifice, the high places, the idol in the temple, the sorcery — but he is *deported to Babylon, repents, prays, is restored to Jerusalem, and reforms his kingdom* (33:11-16). This deportation-and-repentance episode is **unique to the Chronicler**. There is no parallel anywhere else in the Hebrew Bible.

The Chronicler is writing in the late fifth or early fourth century BC, working with Kings as a primary source but adding, subtracting, and reframing as his theology requires. He inherits the Manasseh-as-cause-of-exile tradition but cannot square it with his own immediate-retribution theology — the theology that says faithfulness produces blessing in this generation and faithlessness produces curse in this generation. If Manasseh's sins caused the exile a hundred years after his death, the Chronicler's theology breaks. If, however, Manasseh repented and was restored — and his *son* (Amon) was the one who failed to humble himself, and *that* failure rolls forward toward exile — then the immediate-retribution theology can be saved.

The historicity question is sharp. Did Manasseh actually go to Babylon? The mainstream-critical consensus (Sara Japhet, *I & II Chronicles*, OTL, 1993; H.G.M. Williamson, *1 and 2 Chronicles*, NCBC, 1982; Ralph Klein, *2 Chronicles*, Hermeneia, 2012; Gary Knoppers; Steven McKenzie) is that the deportation-and-repentance episode is the Chronicler's theological pattern projected onto a king who, in Kings, was the very figure of unrepentant evil. The evidence:

First, no extra-biblical source corroborates the deportation. We have substantial Assyrian-period documentation from the reigns of Esarhaddon (681-669 BC) and Ashurbanipal (669-631 BC), the two Assyrian kings whose reigns overlap Manasseh's (697-642 BC). Manasseh appears in two Assyrian texts: Esarhaddon's *Prism B* lists him among twenty-two western kings who supplied building materials for Esarhaddon's palace at Nineveh (cited in James Pritchard, *ANET*, p. 291), and Ashurbanipal's *Cylinder C* lists him among twenty-two kings who supplied troops for Ashurbanipal's Egyptian campaign. In both texts, Manasseh appears as a normal Assyrian vassal — not a deported rebel. There is no Assyrian record of Manasseh's revolt, deportation, or restoration.

Second, the chapter says Manasseh was taken "to Babylon" (33:11). This is geographically odd for an Assyrian deportation. Assyrian kings normally deported rebels to Nineveh (the imperial capital), not Babylon. However, Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal did make Babylon a secondary capital and held court there at times (Ashurbanipal's brother Shamash-shum-ukin ruled Babylon from 668-648 BC). It is *possible* — though not directly attested — that Manasseh was summoned to Babylon during one of these episodes. Some defenders of the historicity (Edwin Yamauchi; Kenneth Kitchen, *On the Reliability of the Old Testament*, 2003) note that the Babylon detail actually fits the late Assyrian context better than a free invention would have, since by the Chronicler's day Babylon was associated with the *exile* of Judah and would have been a natural projection.

Third, the literary shape of 33:11-16 fits the Chronicler's distinctive theological pattern almost too well. The Chronicler has a recurring sequence: sin → divine warning → divine punishment (deportation, defeat, illness) → humbling → prayer → restoration → reform. We see this with Rehoboam (2 Chr 12), with Asa (partially, 2 Chr 16), with Jehoshaphat (2 Chr 20), with Uzziah (partially, 2 Chr 26), with Hezekiah (briefly, 32:24-26). Manasseh's episode in 33:11-16 fits the pattern exactly. This could mean the Chronicler invented it; it could equally mean the pattern is real and Manasseh's life happened to fit it.

Fourth, the rabbinic and pseudepigraphical tradition around Manasseh's prayer suggests early reception of the Chronicler's account. The *Prayer of Manasseh* — fifteen verses preserved in some Septuagint manuscripts and in the *Apostolic Constitutions* — is an attempt (probably second century BC, possibly later) to reconstruct the prayer the Chronicler mentions. It is included in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles as part of the Apocrypha (in some traditions, in the Prayer of Manasseh as a stand-alone deuterocanonical book; in others as an appendix). Protestant Bibles exclude it from canonical status but Luther, Calvin, and Wesley all knew it. The very existence of the *Prayer of Manasseh* shows that ancient Jewish and Christian readers found the Chronicler's claim about Manasseh's repentance theologically compelling enough to want to fill in its content.

The honest historical-critical assessment, then, is: the deportation-and-repentance episode is *probably* the Chronicler's theological reframing rather than historical reportage. The base data — Manasseh was an Assyrian vassal, his reign included substantial cultic abuses, he reigned fifty-five years (the longest in Judahite history) — is historical. The deportation-repentance-restoration narrative is the Chronicler's distinctive contribution and serves his immediate-retribution theology.

Whether or not the episode is historical, the Chronicler's *theological* claim is what matters for the chapter's afterlife: *no one is too far gone for repentance to work*. Manasseh — who burned his sons as an offering, who set the carved idol in the temple, who shed innocent blood "until he had filled Jerusalem from one end to another" (Kings's framing, 2 Kgs 21:16, which Chronicles softens) — even *he* could repent. Even *he* could be restored. This is the Chronicler's gospel.

## Lens 2 — The Literature of the Text

The chapter divides into three sections, plus a brief Amon coda.

**Section 1 (33:1-9): Manasseh's apostasy.** This section closely parallels 2 Kings 21:1-9 with minor variations. Manasseh rebuilds the *bamot* his father Hezekiah destroyed, erects altars to the Baals, makes *asheroth*, worships *kol-tseva ha-shamayim* (כָּל־צְבָא הַשָּׁמַיִם, "all the host of heaven" — the astral cult), builds altars in the temple courts, "burns his sons as an offering in the Valley of the Son of Hinnom" (*Gei ben-Hinnom*, גֵּיא בֶן־הִנֹּם, the valley that becomes *Gehenna* in later usage), uses *qosem* (fortune-telling), *me'onen* (omen-reading), *kishshef* (sorcery), and consults *ovot* (mediums) and *yidde'onim* (necromancers). The Chronicler escalates the language slightly at 33:9: Manasseh "led Judah and the inhabitants of Jerusalem astray, to do more evil than the nations whom the LORD destroyed before the people of Israel."

**Section 2 (33:10-13): the deportation, prayer, and restoration — UNIQUE TO CHRONICLES.** The Chronicler narrates: YHWH spoke (presumably through prophets, though no name is given); the people did not listen; therefore YHWH brought "the commanders of the army of the king of Assyria" who "captured Manasseh with hooks (*ba-chochim*, בַּחֹחִים — possibly nose-rings or fishhook-style restraints) and bound him with bronze chains (*nechushtayim*, נְחֻשְׁתַּיִם) and brought him to Babylon." In his distress (*be-hatsar lo*, בְּהָצַר לוֹ), Manasseh "entreated the favor of the LORD his God and humbled himself greatly before the God of his fathers." The crucial verb is *vayithanchen* (וַיִּתְחַנֵּן), the Hithpael of *chanan*, "to make supplication, to plead for grace." This is the Chronicler's signature verb for the kind of prayer that triggers divine mercy.

YHWH's response: "God was moved by his entreaty (*vayye'ater lo*, וַיֵּעָתֶר לוֹ — note the same root as Isaac's "prayer" for Rebekah in Gen 25:21) and heard his plea and brought him again to Jerusalem into his kingdom." The chapter culminates in 33:13: *az yada Menashshe ki YHWH hu ha-elohim* (אָז יָדַע מְנַשֶּׁה כִּי יְהוָה הוּא הָאֱלֹהִים), "*then* Manasseh knew that YHWH is God." The word *az* — "then" — is loaded. *Then*, after deportation and repentance, knowledge came. The fifty-five-year reign of evil was not the final word; *az*, knowledge, came.

**Section 3 (33:14-17): reform.** Restored to Jerusalem, Manasseh fortifies the city (the outer wall, west of Gihon, around Ophel — engineering details that may reflect actual Manasseh-period archaeology; some Iron Age IIc fortifications around the Ophel have been dated to his reign), removes the foreign gods and the idol from the temple, throws them outside the city, restores the altar of YHWH, offers peace and thanksgiving sacrifices, and commands Judah to serve YHWH. The chapter notes one limitation: "nevertheless, the people still sacrificed at the high places, but only to the LORD their God" (33:17). The reform is incomplete; the *bamot* remain even though the worship there is monolatrous.

**Coda (33:18-25): Amon.** The Chronicler closes Manasseh's reign by referencing two source documents — the *Chronicles of the Kings of Israel* and the *Chronicles of the Seers* (*divrei chozai*, דִּבְרֵי חוֹזָי) — both of which are otherwise unknown. The mention of Manasseh's prayer being preserved in these sources is what gave rise to later attempts to reconstruct the actual *Prayer of Manasseh* (Apocrypha). Amon is then introduced briefly: he reigned two years, did evil like his father, but unlike his father did *not* humble himself, and was assassinated by his servants. The verb *hikhniya* (הִכְנִיעַ, to humble oneself) at 33:23 is highlighted as the decisive contrast: Manasseh humbled himself; Amon did not. This is the Chronicler's pivot, the entire theological work of the chapter compressed into a single verb-contrast.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Manasseh-of-Chronicles becomes one of the great repentance figures of the biblical and post-biblical tradition.

**Jewish trajectory.** The rabbis took the Chronicler's Manasseh seriously. The Mishnah, *Sanhedrin* 10:2, lists Manasseh among kings who do *not* have a share in the world to come — but the Gemara (*Sanhedrin* 102b-103a) reverses this, citing 2 Chr 33:13 explicitly: God heard his prayer; therefore the rabbis must include him among those who repented. There is a beautiful midrashic tradition (*Pesiqta de-Rab Kahana, Shuvah*) in which the angels try to block Manasseh's prayer from reaching God's throne; God responds by "making a tunnel under his throne" so that the prayer can come up. The image is of divine eagerness for repentance overruling angelic objection. Maimonides cites Manasseh's case in *Hilkhot Teshuvah* as evidence that no sin is beyond *teshuvah*. The Yom Kippur liturgy invokes the principle that all who turn are received, and Manasseh stands behind that principle.

**Christian trajectory.** The early church found Manasseh enormously useful. Origen, Augustine, Jerome all cite Manasseh's repentance as evidence of God's mercy to the worst sinners. The *Prayer of Manasseh* (Apocrypha) entered Christian liturgical use early. In Eastern Orthodoxy, the Prayer of Manasseh is part of the *Great Compline* service during Lent and is recited regularly. In Roman Catholicism, the Prayer of Manasseh is included as an appendix to the Vulgate (post-Trent, technically *not* canonical for Catholics, but liturgically in use). In Anglicanism, it appears in the Apocrypha and is sometimes used at Ash Wednesday. In Protestantism, the Prayer is excluded from canon but Calvin (*Institutes* III.4.4) and Luther both approved its content as theologically sound — they simply denied it canonical status.

The Reformation pattern of reading Manasseh emphasizes the *grace-to-the-worst-sinner* theme. Calvin's commentary on 2 Chr 33 makes the point sharply: if Manasseh — adulterer, murderer, idolater, child-sacrificer — could be received, then no Reformed believer should despair. Wesley's preaching takes the same line in his sermons on assurance.

The Catholic-Protestant **canon debate** around the *Prayer of Manasseh* is theologically interesting but practically minor. The Catholic position (codified at Trent, 1546) excluded the Prayer from the canon proper while including it in an appendix to the Vulgate. The Protestant position excluded it from canon entirely. The Eastern Orthodox position varies by jurisdiction but generally treats it as deuterocanonical and liturgically valuable. None of these positions actually disputes the *Chronicles* account — they only dispute whether the reconstructed prayer should be read as scripture.

**Christian-typological reading.** Augustine in *City of God* uses Manasseh as a type of every fallen humanity restored. Calvin reads Manasseh through the lens of Paul's "I was the chief of sinners" (1 Tim 1:15) — the worst case made into the demonstration case. Wesley reads Manasseh as a type of the prodigal son. The pattern is so attractive that some commentators find it suspicious; the Chronicler may have had it exactly right and the church found him so useful precisely because his theology of unconditional welcome to the repentant fits the gospel's grain.

**NT use.** Manasseh is mentioned in the Matthean genealogy (Matt 1:10) but otherwise the NT does not cite 2 Chr 33 directly. The pattern of the chapter — repentance as the breaking of generational sin — runs through Luke 15 (the prodigal son), through Acts (Saul of Tarsus's conversion, the persecutor turned apostle), through 1 Timothy 1:13-16. The theological grammar is consistent.

## Lens 4 — The Chronicler's Theological Intent

This chapter is the Chronicler's most distinctive theological move in the entire history. It deserves careful naming.

The agenda: *immediate retribution holds — but it operates through humbling-and-mercy as well as through punishment. No king is so far gone that repentance cannot restore him; no generation is so corrupt that humbling cannot save it. Manasseh, the worst king Judah ever had, repented and was received. So can you.*

This is the Chronicler's gospel, and it is bold. He is rewriting the Manasseh tradition that Kings gave him because Kings's theology — that Manasseh's sins, decades after Manasseh's death, finally caused the exile (2 Kgs 24:3-4) — does not fit the Chronicler's framework. The Chronicler's framework requires that each generation answer for itself. So the Chronicler retrieves a tradition (or constructs one) in which Manasseh repents and his repentance is real, his restoration is real, and the door to *teshuvah* is genuinely open even at the bottom.

**Tension 1: the historicity question.** Did Manasseh actually go to Babylon and repent?

The mainstream-critical position (Williamson, Japhet, Klein, McKenzie, Knoppers): probably not. The episode is the Chronicler's theological pattern projected onto Manasseh; the Assyrian sources don't attest it; the literary shape fits the Chronicler's signature too perfectly. *But* this does not make the episode false in a meaningful theological sense. The Chronicler is doing something legitimate within ancient historiography: telling the deeper truth he believes about Manasseh through narrative. The mainstream-critical reader can hold that the *event* is theological while the *theology* is real.

The conservative position (Kitchen, Yamauchi): the episode is plausibly historical. Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal both held court in Babylon at times; vassal kings could be summoned; the Babylon detail is unusual enough to suggest it isn't free invention. The silence of Assyrian sources is explicable — Assyrian royal inscriptions are not interested in vassal-king restorations. The Chronicler likely had access to genuine traditions that Kings did not use because Kings was not theologically interested in them.

The honest steelman of each position: the critical reader is right that the literary shape is too neat to be coincidence and the Assyrian silence is suggestive. The conservative reader is right that absence of evidence is not evidence of absence and that the Chronicler had source materials Kings did not use. The chapter probably contains a kernel of historical possibility (Manasseh as Assyrian vassal who at some point was disciplined and reset), wrapped in the Chronicler's theological pattern.

**Tension 2: the Chronicler vs. Kings on the cause of the exile.** This is the deeper tension.

Kings says explicitly (2 Kgs 24:3-4): "Surely this came upon Judah at the command of the LORD, to remove them out of his sight, *for the sins of Manasseh, according to all that he had done, and also for the innocent blood that he had shed*, for he filled Jerusalem with innocent blood, and the LORD would not pardon."

Chronicles says implicitly (33:13): Manasseh repented, was forgiven, reformed, and his *son* Amon failed to repent. The exile is therefore not Manasseh's fault but the cumulative failure of subsequent generations.

These are two different theologies of how sin and judgment work over time. Williamson is sharp on this: the Chronicler is *consciously revising* Kings's exile theology. He cannot accept that one generation's sin condemns later generations. His doctrine of personal-and-generational responsibility, drawn from Ezekiel 18 in part, requires that each generation answer for itself.

The honest Christian reader has to hold both. Kings's account is canonical scripture. Chronicles' account is canonical scripture. They are saying somewhat different things about how sin operates across generations. The Christian and Jewish traditions have generally read this not as contradiction but as complementarity: there *are* generational consequences (Kings is right) *and* there are real opportunities for repentance that break the chain (Chronicles is right). Neither voice cancels the other.

**Tension 3: the apocryphal Prayer of Manasseh.** Should the reconstructed prayer be considered scripture? Catholics and Orthodox say partially; Protestants say no. The actual *content* of the Prayer is theologically rich and uncontroversial — confession, plea, doxology — and Reformed and evangelical readers have generally been comfortable using it devotionally even when denying its canonical status. The canonical question is real but downstream of the more important question, which is: does the Chronicler's *theological* claim about Manasseh stand? On that, both Catholic and Protestant traditions agree: yes.

**Where this leaves us:** 2 Chronicles 33 gives us the Chronicler's deepest pastoral move. He looks at the worst king he knows of, the king Kings had condemned as the cause of the exile, and he says: *even him*. Even the child-sacrificer, even the temple-defiler, even the necromancer-consulter. *Az yada Menashshe ki YHWH hu ha-elohim* — *then Manasseh knew that YHWH is God*. The "then" arrives only after the deportation, after the chains, after the bottom. But it arrives. The Chronicler is telling his fragile post-exilic community — and any reader since — that the door is open as long as you are alive to walk through it.

**Closing question:** Is there a Manasseh in your story — yourself, someone you have given up on, a generational pattern you assume cannot be broken — for whom the door is more open than you have allowed yourself to imagine?

## The Thread

Chapter 33 is the Chronicler at his most distinctive. He has rewritten Manasseh; he has saved his immediate-retribution theology by pushing the cause of the exile forward to Amon and beyond; he has given the post-exilic community its great repentance precedent. The next chapter introduces Josiah at age eight — the king whose reform will be the last great cleansing before the descent to exile. The Chronicler is moving his readers toward the end now, but he is not yet at the end. Josiah is coming.
