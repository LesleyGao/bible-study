# Leviticus 15: When the Body Speaks

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Leviticus, the *Wayyiqrāʾ* (וַיִּקְרָא) of Hebrew tradition, continues the narrative suspended at Exodus 40 where the cloud of glory descends on the tabernacle. The book is static in space — Israel does not move within it — and densely liturgical in texture. Its dating sits at the center of the documentary debate: Wellhausen placed P (the Priestly source) in the post-exilic period as an idealized retrojection; Menahem Haran's *Temples and Temple-Service in Ancient Israel* (1978) and Milgrom's three-volume Anchor Bible commentary (1991–2001) argue for a pre-exilic core with substantial First Temple roots, citing terminological archaisms, cultic practices untethered to Second Temple realities, and ANE comparanda. Israel Knohl's *The Sanctuary of Silence* (1995) distinguishes P (cult-centered, terse, hierarchical) from H (Holiness School, ethically inflected, often redactional), with Leviticus 17 opening the H material and chapter 15 firmly within P's great purity block. Leviticus 11–15 forms a tight quartet — food, parturition, skin disease, bodily flux — cresting into Yom Kippur in chapter 16. Chapter 15 is the last of the bodily-impurity chapters before that theological pivot.

The material culture of chapter 15 is the human body itself: genital discharges, both pathological and ordinary. Milgrom identifies four categories: *zāb* (זָב, the man with abnormal discharge, probably gonorrhea, vv. 2–15), *šikbat-zeraʿ* (שִׁכְבַת־זֶרַע, ordinary seminal emission, vv. 16–18), *niddâ* (נִדָּה, the menstruating woman, vv. 19–24), and *zābâ* (זָבָה, the woman with abnormal prolonged bleeding, vv. 25–30). The Hebrew technical vocabulary is precise: *ṭāmēʾ* (טָמֵא, "unclean"), *ṭāhēr* (טָהֵר, "clean"), *midday* (מִדְוָה, "illness," v. 33), and *nepeš* (נֶפֶשׁ, "person/life") all carry load.

ANE parallels are extensive and important. Hittite texts from Hattusa include purification rites for men after intercourse and for women after childbirth; the "Instructions for Priests and Temple Officials" (CTH 264) prohibits priestly service under sexual impurity conditions analogous to Leviticus 15:16–18. Mesopotamian *šurpu* and *maqlû* incantations presuppose contagion by contact and require laving rites. The Ugaritic corpus is sparser on menstrual impurity but attests general purity ritual. Egyptian New Kingdom priestly purity regulations at Karnak include sexual abstinence periods. What distinguishes Leviticus 15 is not that bodily fluids defile — this is almost universal in ANE cultic regulation — but the asymmetric theological anchoring in verse 31: impurity threatens the tabernacle at Israel's center, and the issue is not hygiene but the risk of desecrating the sanctuary of a holy God who dwells in the camp.

On Israel's own archaeology, scattered *miqveh*-like installations from Hasmonean and Herodian periods (Masada, Qumran, Sepphoris) testify to the enduring centrality of these laws in Second Temple Jewish practice — they are the infrastructure without which this chapter becomes theoretical. Qumran texts (notably 4QD, 4Q274 *Tohorot A*, 4Q284) expand these laws with stringency beyond the MT, including extensions like the red heifer ash's application to menstrual impurity and the required separation of the *zāb*. Manuscript witnesses to chapter 15 include 4QLev-Num-a and LXX variants; the MT is well preserved.

Three to five Hebrew terms to register: *zāb* (זָב) for the discharging man, *niddâ* (נִדָּה) — the root *ndd* ("move apart, exclude") is morally loaded; the noun eventually becomes a Jewish technical term for menstruation and the entire halakhic system surrounding it. *zābâ* (זָבָה), the flowing woman. *šikbat-zeraʿ* (שִׁכְבַת־זֶרַע), literally "lying down of seed." And crucially *ṭimmēʾ* (טִמֵּא, in the hiphil "to defile, render unclean"), the verb governing the chapter's logic of contagion.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: ritual-legal, casuistic, with a recurring *if-then* structure (כִּי/when…). The chapter has a pronounced symmetrical architecture that most commentators since Milgrom have highlighted. It opens and closes with a chiasm: male discharge (vv. 2–15), male semen (vv. 16–18), female menstruation (vv. 19–24), female abnormal discharge (vv. 25–30), summary (vv. 31–33). The A-B-B'-A' chiasm — abnormal male | normal male | normal female | abnormal female — is striking for its parity. Mary Douglas, in her later revision of her position (*Leviticus as Literature*, 1999), foregrounds this symmetry as theologically significant: male and female bodies are treated with equivalent ritual gravity, neither privileged, both subject to the same structural logic of bodily boundary and sanctuary proximity.

Repetitive formulas drive the rhythm: *yiṭmāʾ ʿad hā-ʿāreb* ("shall be unclean until evening") appears sixteen times. The chapter is a textbook of contagion physics — what touches what, who sits on what, which vessel is broken (earthenware) and which merely rinsed (wood). Earthenware vessels are broken (v. 12) because porous ceramic absorbs impurity and cannot be purged — an insight consistent with Leviticus 11:33 and Numbers 19:15. Wooden vessels, nonporous at the surface, can be rinsed. This distinction, apparently technical, telegraphs Milgrom's entire reading: impurity is a dynamic, quasi-physical substance that behaves in predictable ways relative to the sanctuary's center.

The climactic verse 31 — "Thus you shall keep the people of Israel separate from their uncleanness, lest they die in their uncleanness by defiling my tabernacle that is in their midst" — is the chapter's thesis sentence. It is not a statement about hygiene or about women's bodies; it is a statement about sacred space and the catastrophic consequences of its contamination. The connecting syntax — *hizzartem* (you shall separate) — is the hiphil of *nzr*, the same root as *nazir* (Nazirite, one consecrated). Purity discourse bleeds into consecration vocabulary. The summary in verses 32–33 functions as a *tôrat* label, a rubric-colophon ("this is the law"), matching the recurring structure of chapters 11–15.

Textual variants: LXX is largely faithful with occasional clarifying expansions. The Samaritan Pentateuch aligns. 4Q274 and later Mishnaic sources preserve and expand the legal tradition rather than introducing new textual readings.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** The rabbinic engagement with chapter 15 is vast. Mishnah *Zabim* (five chapters) elaborates the male flux; *Niddah* (ten chapters, with extensive gemara in Bavli Niddah) elaborates the menstrual laws; *Mikvaot* (ten chapters) details the ritual bath infrastructure these laws presuppose. Mishnah Niddah 2:1 — "every woman who has a flow knows it of her own body" — encodes a striking rabbinic empirical approach, locating diagnostic authority in women's own knowledge. Sifra and later midrashim layer legal and homiletic commentary. Rashi hews close to the plain sense; Ibn Ezra tends rationalist. Ramban takes the laws as divine pedagogy with theological weight about life and death — blood and semen carry the potential for life, and their loss is an encounter with the edge of mortality. Maimonides (*Guide* 3.47; *Mishneh Torah*, Hilkhot Metame'ei Mishkav u-Moshav; Hilkhot Issurei Bi'ah 4–11) frames the whole purity system as pedagogy against pagan hypersexuality and as preparation for sanctuary approach, but also preserves the operative legal system in halakha. Contemporary modern Orthodox and Conservative Jewish communities retain *niddah* laws (called *taharat ha-mishpacha*, "family purity") as living practice, with mikvaot immersion marking the end of the menstrual separation period.

Feminist Jewish scholarship on this chapter is rich and contested. Rachel Biale's *Women and Jewish Law* (1984) gave an early systematic treatment. Rachel Adler's 1973 essay "Tumah and Taharah: Ends and Beginnings" (and its later revisions in *Engendering Judaism*, 1998) initially argued for reclaiming menstrual purity laws as women's spiritual experience, then revised her position critically, emphasizing how the system had functioned patriarchally in practice. Judith Plaskow's *Standing Again at Sinai* (1990) reads the chapter as part of the Torah's male-centered ritual universe that marginalizes women's experience; her critique is that the laws are not balanced in their social effects even if they are symmetrical in ritual text. Tirzah Meacham has written on menstrual impurity across Jewish literature. Tova Hartman and Naomi Marmon's 2004 study of contemporary *niddah*-observing women found complex testimonies — for many, a practice that could have been experienced as patriarchal constraint functioned as marital rhythm and embodied spirituality.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** Patristic readings are often allegorical. Origen (*Homilies on Leviticus* 8) reads the discharges as moral passions and the cleansing rites as spiritual purification. Gregory the Great's *Moralia* treats bodily impurity as the figure of sin's spread. Augustine does not treat chapter 15 extensively but applies its logic in *De civitate Dei* 14 on the body and sin. Aquinas (*ST* I–II q. 102 a. 5 ad 2) treats the purity laws as the old law's figurative preparation, not binding on Christians per Acts 10 and 15. Calvin's *Institutes* 4.19 follows the standard Reformation line: ceremonial law abrogated, moral core preserved. The Reformers did not find the chapter embarrassing but did find it obsolete as cultic law.

Contemporary Christian scholarship has been more productive. Gordon Wenham (NICOT, 1979) reads the chapter as teaching the sacred-space logic of Israel's worship. Jay Sklar (*Sin, Impurity, Sacrifice, Atonement*, 2005) works out the *kippēr* vocabulary. Jacob Milgrom, though Jewish, has set the scholarly terms. Hans Boersma's *Violence, Hospitality, and the Cross* (2004) and Fleming Rutledge's *The Crucifixion* (2015) engage purity categories in atonement theology. Ephraim Radner's *Leviticus* (Brazos, 2008) reads the chapter figurally as pointing to Christ's incarnation and the sanctification of the body.

Crucial to both traditions is Mark 5:25–34, the woman with a twelve-year discharge (*zābâ*-state). Amy-Jill Levine's *The Misunderstood Jew* (2006) argues against the common Christian preaching line that Jesus "broke the purity law" — the text presents no rebuke, and first-century Jewish practice likely did not involve public contagion concerns for a bleeding woman in a crowd. Jesus' response — "Daughter, your faith has made you well; go in peace" — addresses her suffering rather than adjudicating purity categories. Yet the narrative echoes Leviticus 15 with striking precision: the *zābâ*'s contagious touch, the garment, the purification by encounter. In Mark's theology, contagion runs in reverse: holiness flows out and cleans.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Both traditions read the chapter as a statement about embodied life before God. Judaism has preserved the operative legal system (in modernized form); Christianity has reassigned its force to the moral and Christological plane. The Jewish reading retains the body's ritual rhythm as a structure of holiness; the Christian reading (especially Patristic) has risked spiritualizing the body into mere metaphor. Feminist scholarship in both traditions has pressed hard: do the laws encode misogyny (Plaskow) or symmetry (Douglas)? The honest answer is both — the ritual grammar is remarkably symmetrical; the social history in which it was received and applied was patriarchally skewed. Christian readers cannot pretend the chapter is neutral about bodies; Jewish readers cannot pretend *niddah* practice has been politically innocent. The story these traditions tell together is that embodiment matters theologically, and that the costs and gifts of that mattering have been unevenly distributed.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is the impurity system ritual (cultic, sanctuary-oriented) or ethical (about respect for life)?** Milgrom's reading is emphatically ritual-cultic but theologically anchored: impurity is not sin, but loss of life force. Semen, menstrual blood, and abnormal genital flow all represent the egress of life-substance from the body; encounter with the "edge of death" is what requires separation from the sanctuary of the Living God. Mary Douglas's earlier *Purity and Danger* (1966) read the laws symbolically — boundary violations, fluid-boundary categories — and her later work refined this into a holistic cultural logic. Jonathan Klawans (*Impurity and Sin in Ancient Judaism*, 2000) distinguishes sharply between ritual and moral impurity in Jewish tradition; chapter 15 is ritual. Against this, Maimonides (*Guide* 3.47) moralizes the system as pedagogy in bodily restraint. Christian moral-theological readings (Calvin, the Reformed tradition) tend to interpret the laws as teaching reverence for the body and for sanctuary. Lutheran theology (Luther, then Bonhoeffer's *Ethics*) insists the categories are abolished in Christ but their moral intent survives in call to chastity and bodily reverence. Wesleyan sanctification readings (Oden) frame the laws as pedagogy in holiness-as-wholeness. Orthodox theology (Alexander Schmemann, *For the Life of the World*) integrates the laws into a cosmic sacramental vision: the body is not unclean, it is holy, and its flows mark it as participant in the divine economy. The Catholic position (CCC 364–365, 2297) affirms the body's sacredness and the cultic pedagogy of the old law without reviving its ritual details.

The least intuitive position is Milgrom's "life-force" reading, because it refuses both the moralizing and the symbolic reductions. Steelman: if blood and semen represent potentiated life — life that could have been — then their loss is, ritually speaking, a brush with death. The tabernacle is the space of the Living God; the dying body cannot enter it without mediation. On this reading, chapter 15 is not about disgust, not about hygiene, not about patriarchy primarily — it is about mortality's proximity to the sacred. This is theologically weightier than it first appears, because it frames the body as the site where life and death actually meet, not as a neutral biological fact but as a covenantal signal.

**Where this leaves us:** the laws are ritual, not ethical in the strict sense; but the ritual itself encodes a theology of embodiment that has serious ethical implications. The body matters because it is the place where life is received and given, and the God who meets Israel in the tabernacle does not meet a disembodied soul.

**Tension 2: Is the chapter's symmetrical treatment of male and female bodies a theology of parity or a camouflaged asymmetry?** Mary Douglas reads the A-B-B'-A' structure as an explicit ritual parity. Milgrom concurs: the ritual gravity of male and female genital flux is equivalent. Christian feminist scholarship (Phyllis Trible; Kathleen O'Connor) and Jewish feminist scholarship (Plaskow) have pressed the asymmetry argument: menstruation is periodic and structural to female life; male discharge conditions are episodic and often pathological. Therefore, even symmetrical treatment produces asymmetrical burdens. Tikva Frymer-Kensky's *In the Wake of the Goddesses* (1992) reads the priestly writer as genuinely aiming at ritual parity within an ancient context that was not parity-friendly. The Catholic tradition, Orthodox Church, and most Protestant traditions have inherited the asymmetry in their own gendered liturgical histories (menstruating women barred from communion in some Orthodox practice, churching of women rituals following childbirth, and so on), though these are increasingly softened or dropped. The Jewish tradition has preserved the operative system but also, in recent decades, reclaimed it in mikveh practice for diverse life-events (conversion, healing, post-trauma).

The least intuitive position is Douglas's ritual parity reading, because it runs against the observable history of patriarchal application. Steelman: the text itself is symmetrical. A generous reading of authorial intent, not of subsequent reception, must grant that the priestly writer distributed ritual weight equally across male and female bodies — and that any asymmetrical effect belongs to social history, not to the text. This is important because it refuses both apologetic and polemic: the text is better than its history, but the history is worse than the text.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter's ritual grammar is symmetrical; its social legacy has not been. Any honest theological appropriation must hold both.

**Unresolved question:** If the theology of chapter 15 is fundamentally about the body as the site where life-force is given and received and the sanctuary as the space of the Living God, what does it mean that modern Christian liturgy has almost entirely decoupled bodily cycles from worship — and has the cost of that decoupling been a theology of embodiment too thin to bear the questions now pressing on it (sexuality, gender, disability, mortality)?

## The Thread

Four non-obvious cross-references. First, Mark 5:25–34 (the woman with a hemorrhage) deliberately stages chapter 15 in narrative form: a twelve-year *zābâ* who has "spent all she had" on physicians, who touches Jesus' garment expecting to defile and is instead healed. Luke amplifies the priestly vocabulary (*hē rhysis tou haimatos*, "the flow of blood"). The narrative reverses chapter 15's directional flow of contagion: holiness leaves the holy one, and the one who should have defiled is made clean. Second, 2 Samuel 11:4 — the almost parenthetical note that Bathsheba was "purifying herself from her uncleanness" when David summoned her. The single clause locates her in Leviticus 15:19–24, confirms the pregnancy's paternity, and lets the priestly grammar expose David's sin. Third, Ezekiel 36:17 — "When the house of Israel lived in their own land, they defiled it by their ways and their deeds; their ways before me were like the uncleanness of a woman in her menstrual impurity [*niddâ*]." The prophet deploys chapter 15's ritual vocabulary as moral metaphor — a move that generates both theological depth and later homiletic trouble, since the comparative figure depends on menstruation as a salient image. Fourth, Hebrews 9:13–14 — "For if the blood of goats and bulls, and the sprinkling of defiled persons with the ashes of a heifer, sanctify for the purification of the flesh, how much more will the blood of Christ... purify our conscience from dead works?" Hebrews lifts chapter 15's logic of bodily purification and relocates its scale to the conscience, using the ritual grammar to articulate an interior soteriology. The priestly writer's bodily theology is not abolished there, but recapitulated.
