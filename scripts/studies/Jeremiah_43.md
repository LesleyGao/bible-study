# Jeremiah 43: The Forced Return to Egypt and the Stones at Tahpanhes

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political situation**: The chapter records the survivors' definitive rejection of Jeremiah's ten-day oracle (chapter 42) and their forced relocation, prophet and all, into 26th-Dynasty Saite Egypt. The likely date is late 587 or early 586 BC, immediately following the Mizpah assassination cycle. Pharaoh Apries (Hophra in Hebrew, Wahibre in Egyptian; reigned c. 589–570 BC) was the Saite king who had marched out in 588 BC to relieve besieged Jerusalem (cf. Jer 37:5–11) — the relief that briefly lifted the siege and emboldened Zedekiah's faction before the Babylonians turned on Egypt and pushed Apries back. Apries's recruitment of West-Semitic refugees as garrison troops for the eastern Delta is well-attested; Herodotus (*Histories* 2.30, 2.161–69) describes Apries's mercenary policies, and the Elephantine archive (Cowley collection, 5th c. BC) documents the longer trajectory of Jewish military settlement in Egypt that this chapter inaugurates. The flight to Tahpanhes represents an ironic political choice: the survivors flee Babylonian reprisal by entering the territory of the very Pharaoh whose failed relief had encouraged Zedekiah's catastrophe.

**Material culture**: Tahpanhes (תַּחְפַּנְחֵס) is identified with Tell Defenneh in the eastern Nile Delta, about 27 kilometers west of modern Qantara, on the old Pelusiac branch of the Nile. The site was excavated by Sir William Matthew Flinders Petrie in 1886, in what was effectively the third major British excavation of Delta sites; Petrie published the results as *Tanis II: Nebesheh and Defenneh* (Egypt Exploration Fund, 1888). Petrie identified the site as Daphnae, the Greek name for the same fortress mentioned by Herodotus (*Histories* 2.30) as a major garrison-station of Apries. Petrie excavated a fortified enclosure with a substantial brick platform he called the *Qasr Bint el-Yahudi* — "the palace of the Jew's daughter," the local Arabic toponym he found attached to the ruin — and identified a brick-paved court at the structure's entrance that he tentatively connected with Jeremiah 43:9's "pavement that is at the entrance to Pharaoh's palace." Petrie's identification of the pavement-stones sign-act site is, by his own admission, a *plausible* but not definitive correlation; later scholarship (Kitchen, *On the Reliability of the Old Testament*; Lipiński and Redford on the Saite Delta) has accepted Defenneh as Tahpanhes while remaining cautious about the specific pavement. The "obelisks of Heliopolis" (43:13) are the *matzevot beit-shemesh*, literally "pillars of the house of the sun" — the famous obelisks of Iunu (Heliopolis), the cult-center of Re-Atum, several of which still stand or stood into modern times (the obelisk of Senusret I at Heliopolis remains *in situ*; others were transported in late antiquity to Rome and Constantinople). Heliopolis is consistently treated in the prophetic corpus as the symbolic heart of Egyptian solar theology (cf. Isa 19:18's "city of the sun").

**Authorship & dating**: The chapter is biographical "B source" prose. Its sign-act in 43:8–13 is one of the most concrete and historically locatable of all Jeremiah's symbolic acts — an unusual feature, since most prophetic sign-acts (Isaiah's nakedness, Hosea's marriage, Ezekiel's tile and brick) are not tied to identifiable archaeological sites. The oracle in 43:10–13 predicts Nebuchadnezzar's invasion of Egypt, an event the Babylonian Chronicle records in fragmentary form. The relevant cuneiform fragment (BM 33041, edited by Donald Wiseman) records a campaign of Nebuchadnezzar against Egypt in his 37th year (568/567 BC), against Pharaoh Amasis (Ahmose II), who had by then succeeded Apries. The fragment's preservation is partial; it confirms the campaign's occurrence but not its scope. Josephus (*Ant.* 10.180–82) reports the same campaign and connects it explicitly to Jeremiah's prophecy. Lundbom (Anchor) treats the chapter as substantially historical and the sign-act as authentic. Holladay (Hermeneia) reads it through Deuteronomistic editorial framing. Carroll (OTL) is more skeptical. McKane (ICC), Bright, Stulman, and Brueggemann treat it as historically rooted. The MT and LXX of chapter 43 differ in some details — most notably the LXX renders the obelisks of Heliopolis as the *pillars of On*, preserving the Egyptian name *Iunu*.

**Archaeological & textual evidence**: Tell Defenneh (Petrie 1886; published 1888) for Tahpanhes. The Babylonian Chronicle fragment BM 33041 (Wiseman) for Nebuchadnezzar's 37th-year Egyptian campaign. Josephus *Ant.* 10.180–82 for the campaign tradition. Herodotus *Histories* 2.30, 2.161–69 for Apries's mercenary policies and the Daphnae garrison. The Elephantine papyri (Cowley, 5th c. BC) for the longer Jewish-military-colony trajectory in Egypt. The obelisk of Senusret I at Heliopolis as a surviving witness to the *matzevot* of 43:13. The Bani Hassan tomb paintings (Beni-Hasan, Middle Kingdom) for the long visual evidence of Semitic settlement in the eastern Delta.

**Key terms**: *zedim* (זֵדִים, "insolent men," 43:2) — the same root as Babel's *zadon* and the proverbial *zed* of Proverbs; the term marks the speakers as deliberately, knowingly defiant. *Tahpanhes* (תַּחְפַּנְחֵס) — the Egyptian *Ta-ḥut-pa-Nehesy*, "fortress of the Nubian." *malbeen* (מַלְבֵּן, "pavement" or "brick-court," 43:9) — a brick or paved platform; Petrie identified a structure of this type at Tell Defenneh. *avadi* (עַבְדִּי, "my servant," 43:10) — the title YHWH gives to Nebuchadnezzar, the same title used for Moses and David, here applied to a foreign emperor as instrument of divine action.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter has a sharp two-part architecture: the rejection-and-flight scene (43:1–7) and the Tahpanhes sign-act (43:8–13). The first is biographical narrative in third-person, structured around the inversion of chapter 42's oath. Where the survivors swore in 42:5 to obey "whether good or bad," here in 43:2 they accuse Jeremiah of *lying* — the prophet whose word has just been historically vindicated by Babylon's victory is dismissed as a fraud. The accusation in 43:3 is novelistic in its specificity: Baruch ben Neriah, named only briefly elsewhere as Jeremiah's scribe (cf. 36:4–32, 45:1–5), is here named as the supposed manipulator behind the prophet — a charge that tells us something about how the public perceived the relationship between prophet and scribe in the prose tradition. The forcible relocation in 43:5–7 is rendered with a comprehensive list (men, women, children, princesses, and Jeremiah and Baruch) that emphasizes the *involuntary* character of the prophet's exile — Jeremiah does not choose Egypt; he is *taken* there.

The sign-act in 43:8–13 is one of Jeremiah's most concretely staged. Hebrew prophetic sign-acts are generally interior, household-scale objects (the linen waistcloth of Jer 13, the wine jars of Jer 13:12, the broken flask of Jer 19, the yoke of Jer 27–28). The Tahpanhes act is exterior, public, performed at the very threshold of Pharaoh's palace, and uses materials of imperial scale — large stones hidden in mortar in a brick pavement. The act is framed *in the sight of the men of Judah* (43:9), making it a piece of refugee public theater rather than a secret deposit. The oracle that interprets it (43:10–13) is among the most cosmopolitan in the prophetic corpus — it names a foreign emperor as YHWH's *servant*, predicts the destruction of Egyptian temples, and uses the striking image of Nebuchadnezzar wrapping himself in Egypt as a shepherd cleans vermin from his cloak (43:12). The image is earthy and almost ridiculous, undermining Egyptian pretension.

Devices include the prophetic delay broken (the oracle comes immediately upon arrival at Tahpanhes, in contrast to chapter 42's ten-day wait), the public sign-act, the hostile name-check ("Baruch the son of Neriah has set you against us"), the comprehensive deportation-list, and the foreign-emperor-as-servant motif. MT and LXX differ in some details, most notably in the rendering of Heliopolis.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The chapter is the structural undoing of the exodus. The book of Genesis ends with Jacob's family entering Egypt for famine relief; Exodus 1 begins with the descendants of Jacob in Egypt, sons of Israel "fruitful and multiplying" but soon enslaved; Exodus 12–15 records the deliverance from Egypt as the founding event of Israel's existence as a covenant people. Jeremiah 43 reverses every term. The remnant enters Egypt not for famine relief but in flight from political reprisal; not at Joseph's invitation but on Johanan's coercion; not under Pharaonic favor but under refugee status; and the prophet who would lead them out is not Moses but a frail old man dragged in their train against his will. Deuteronomy 17:16's prohibition — "the king shall not cause the people to return to Egypt" — is here violated by the survivors' commanders, with the prophet himself as involuntary witness. Hosea 8:13 ("they shall return to Egypt") and 9:3 ("Ephraim shall return to Egypt") had already typologized the return-to-Egypt as covenantal regression; Jeremiah 43 makes the typology literal.

**Jewish trajectory**: The Bavli (Megillah 14b–15a) discusses Jeremiah's involuntary descent to Egypt and treats it as one of the sorrows that bookend the prophet's life. Targum Jonathan paraphrases the chapter with theological clarification. Rashi on 43:9 stresses the public, Judah-witnessed character of the sign-act — the stones are buried *so that* the men of Judah see, and so that the diaspora cannot later claim ignorance of YHWH's judgment on Egypt. Radak develops the *avadi* ("my servant") title for Nebuchadnezzar at length, noting that the title aligns with the divine sovereignty motif that runs through the prophetic books. Ibn Ezra notes the historical specificity of the obelisks of Heliopolis. Abrabanel reads the chapter through the lens of his own Iberian exile to Egypt-and-back: the forced flight to Egypt, for him, is the diaspora's perennial temptation to flee further, and the sign-act is YHWH's promise that even the apparent safe haven is under judgment.

**Christian trajectory**: Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah* (in the Greek and Jerome's Latin translations) treats the forced descent as typological — the prophet involuntarily entering the land of bondage prefigures Christ's *kenosis*, his self-emptying into the place of human alienation. Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah* (ad loc.) is historically focused; he discusses Tahpanhes and Heliopolis with characteristic geographic care. Aquinas (*Summa* II-II q.174) and the medieval scholastic tradition treat the foreign-emperor-as-servant motif (43:10) under the doctrine of providence. Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah* spends considerable time on the sign-act and on the question of whether Jeremiah and Baruch were morally complicit in the exodus-reversal — Calvin's answer is no: their involuntary presence sanctifies the prophet's office without endorsing the people's act. Critical scholarship since Duhm has read the chapter as Deuteronomistic shaping of remembered events. O'Connor's trauma-theology reading (*Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*) treats the prophet's involuntary descent as the trauma narrative's deepest moment — the prophet, like the people, ends in the place he most feared, and is required to keep prophesying there.

**Where they diverge**: Christian typological tradition reads the descent to Egypt as proto-Christological *kenosis* (the prophet emptied into bondage prefiguring the Word emptied into flesh, with the further reversal of Matthew 2:13–15's flight to Egypt with the infant Jesus). Jewish tradition reads it as *galut*, exile-from-the-land in its most painful form — exile to the very place from which YHWH first redeemed Israel. Critical readings see historical realism: refugees fleeing reprisal, prophet hostage to circumstance.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: the forced return to Egypt as un-exodus.** The chapter raises the sharpest possible question about whether the founding event of Israel — the exodus — can be reversed. The Christian tradition's typological reading turns on Matthew 2:13–15, where Joseph and Mary flee *to* Egypt with the infant Jesus to escape Herod, and Matthew quotes Hosea 11:1 ("out of Egypt I called my son") to read the family's return to Nazareth as a *new exodus* with the infant Christ as the true Israel re-enacting the founding pattern. On this reading, Jeremiah 43's catastrophic descent is the dark counter-image that makes Matthew 2's redemptive descent meaningful — the prophet's involuntary descent is undone by the messianic family's voluntary descent and return. The Jewish tradition resists this typology and reads the descent flatly as *galut* — exile that is endured, lamented, and endured again, without the typological closure Christian reading provides. The critical reading is more historical: the descent was the practical-political response of refugees, and the typological frames are later theological construction. Steelman the position one might dismiss: the *historical-political* reading. Even shorn of typology, the chapter is a meditation on what happens when survival demands exactly the act that founding identity forbids — and the prophet's involuntary presence inside the forbidden act is theologically irreducible. **Where this leaves us:** Egypt becomes, in this chapter, both the place of bondage and the place where YHWH's word still arrives — a tension that remains canonically unresolved until Matthew 2.

**Tension 2: Nebuchadnezzar as YHWH's servant.** The title *avadi* applied to a foreign emperor (43:10, also 25:9, 27:6) is one of the most theologically charged moves in the prophetic corpus. *Avadi* is the title of Moses (Num 12:7), Joshua (Josh 24:29), David (2 Sam 7:5), and the suffering servant of Isaiah 40–55. Its application here to a Babylonian who has destroyed YHWH's house is meant to shock. The Catholic tradition through Augustine's *De civitate Dei* and Aquinas's doctrine of providence reads this through the *causae secundae* — secondary causes (Nebuchadnezzar) instrumentally serve the primary cause (YHWH) without sharing in the primary cause's holiness. The Reformed tradition through Calvin's strong doctrine of providence accepts the title with full force: Nebuchadnezzar is YHWH's servant *as instrument*, not as covenant partner. The Orthodox tradition through the patristic *oikonomia* literature reads it as divine condescension to history. Jewish tradition through the Bavli and the medieval commentators (especially Radak) treats the title carefully, distinguishing the instrumental servanthood of pagan empires from the covenantal servanthood of Moses and David. The critical tradition reads it as theological reframing of imperial reality. Steelman the most uncomfortable position: that the title implies a degree of divine identification with imperial violence that Christian and Jewish ethics cannot easily absorb. The chapter does not soften the title, and theology must reckon with what it means that the destroyer of the Temple is named *my servant*. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter forces a hard doctrine of providence — YHWH's purposes work through agents who do not know they are agents and who may, by every other measure, be enemies.

**Unresolved question:** Why is the prophet *required to be present* at the people's act of disobedience? Jeremiah does not choose Tahpanhes; he is taken. Yet YHWH continues to speak through him in Tahpanhes (43:8). What does it mean that the prophet's office requires presence at the people's worst choice — that the word of YHWH can only address the diaspora *from inside* the disobedient diaspora? Is the prophet's involuntary descent itself a kind of divine accommodation?

## The Thread

The forbidden return to Egypt threads from Deut 17:16's prohibition through Hos 8:13 and 9:3 and Jer 42:14–18 into the literal violation of Jer 43:5–7, and forward into Matthew 2:13–15's redemptive reversal where the infant Jesus enters and exits Egypt as the new Israel. The foreign-emperor-as-YHWH's-servant motif (43:10) links to Jer 25:9, 27:6, Isaiah 45:1's naming of Cyrus as YHWH's anointed, and forward to Romans 13:1–7's doctrine of governing authorities. The pavement-stones sign-act prefigures Daniel 2:34–35's stone that strikes the image of the kingdoms — empire under judgment from a stone-of-divine-origin. The accusation against Baruch in 43:3 echoes the longer biblical pattern of attacking the prophet's circle (Jeremiah 36's burning of the scroll, Acts 7's stoning of Stephen) when the prophet's word becomes politically intolerable.
