# Romans 11: The Olive Tree and the Mystery

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Romans 11 brings to climax the three-chapter movement Paul has been building since 9:1, and it does so by reaching for the most concrete agricultural image available to a Mediterranean audience: the olive tree. Olive cultivation was the backbone of Mediterranean agriculture; in Italy alone, Cato's *De Agri Cultura* (mid-2nd c. BCE) and Columella's *De Re Rustica* (1st c. CE) provide detailed instructions on grafting (*insitio*). Paul's image of grafting a wild olive shoot into a cultivated tree (11:17, 24) was, as Origen and later commentators noted, "contrary to nature" (*para physin*, παρὰ φύσιν, 11:24) — the standard horticultural practice was the reverse: a cultivated scion grafted onto a hardy wild rootstock. Paul knows this. Sir William Ramsay's *Pauline and Other Studies* (1906) cited a passage from Columella (5.9.16) describing precisely the rare, restorative practice of grafting wild branches into a flagging cultivated tree to revive its productivity. A. G. Baxter and J. A. Ziesler (*JSNT* 24 [1985]) defended the agricultural realism of Paul's image. Either way — whether Paul is describing routine practice or a deliberately "contrary to nature" inversion — the metaphor lands with force.

The chapter is composed in Corinth, winter 56-57 CE, and addresses a Roman *ekklēsia* in which Gentile believers have, since the post-Claudian return of Jewish believers, begun to assume cultural dominance. Paul writes pastorally to head off Gentile arrogance: "do not be arrogant toward the branches" (11:18), "do not become proud, but fear" (11:20). The warning has a specific ecclesiological purpose. The historical fact behind the warning is that Roman Gentile believers had, in the absence of Jewish believers during the years 49-54 CE, developed habits of self-sufficiency that Paul knows will damage the community when Jewish believers reintegrate.

Greek terms central to the chapter include ***eklogē*** (ἐκλογή, "election," 11:5, 7, 28) — used here with corporate-historical force; ***pleroma*** (πλήρωμα, "fullness," 11:12, 25) — an eschatological term denoting completed number or full measure; ***sklerōsis*** / ***epōrōthēsan*** (σκλήρωσις / ἐπωρώθησαν, "hardening," 11:7, 25) — the latter from *pōroō*, literally "petrify, become callous"; ***charismata*** (χαρίσματα, "gifts," 11:29) — Paul's term for free gifts of grace; ***ametamelēta*** (ἀμεταμέλητα, 11:29, "irrevocable" — literally "not regretted," used elsewhere in Greek of decisions one will not take back); and ***mystērion*** (μυστήριον, "mystery," 11:25) — a Pauline term denoting eschatological revelation, drawn from apocalyptic Judaism (Daniel, 1 Enoch).

The doxology at 11:33-36 weaves together Job 11:7-9, Isaiah 40:13, Jeremiah 23:18 LXX, and possibly Wisdom of Solomon 9:13-17. The triadic prepositions of 11:36 (*ex autou kai di' autou kai eis auton*, "from him and through him and to him") echo Stoic formulas (cf. Marcus Aurelius *Meditations* 4.23, where the Stoic universe is described as "from you, in you, to you") but redirect them theistically toward the God of Israel.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Romans 11 is the rhetorical climax of the *probatio* that began at 9:1. Robert Jewett's *Romans* (Hermeneia, 651-735) reads it as a sustained argument that the failure of Israel is not final but functional within God's larger plan. The chapter has six movements: 11:1-10 (the remnant); 11:11-16 (the strategic purpose of the stumbling); 11:17-24 (the olive tree allegory); 11:25-27 (the mystery and "all Israel will be saved"); 11:28-32 (the irrevocable gifts); and 11:33-36 (the doxology).

The diatribe form continues from chapters 9 and 10, with the imagined Gentile interlocutor replacing the imagined Jewish interlocutor. Paul addresses "you Gentiles" (11:13) explicitly. The diatribe shifts from defensive theodicy in chapter 9 ("is there injustice on God's part?") to offensive ethical-pastoral correction in chapter 11 ("do not be arrogant").

Two textual cruxes deserve attention. First, 11:25-26: the phrase "*kai houtōs pas Israēl sōthēsetai*" ("and in this way all Israel will be saved"). The adverb *houtōs* ("thus, in this way, in this manner") has been read in three ways: temporally ("and then"), modally ("and in this way"), or comparatively ("and in the same way"). Most modern commentators (Cranfield, Moo, Wright, Schreiner, Jewett) take it modally — describing the manner in which all Israel will be saved (through the process of Gentile fullness provoking Jewish jealousy) rather than asserting a sequence (first Gentiles, then Jews). The interpretive consequences are significant: the modal reading does not require a future mass conversion of Israel at the parousia.

Second, 11:26: "all Israel" (*pas Israēl*). Five major options exist (treated below in Lens 3). The textual evidence is stable; the question is hermeneutic.

The doxology at 11:33-36 is one of the most highly crafted passages in the Pauline corpus. The triple "depth-riches-wisdom-knowledge" piles superlatives in a manner reminiscent of Hellenistic Jewish hymnody (cf. *4 Maccabees* 1:13-30). The two rhetorical questions of 11:34-35 invert standard Jewish wisdom topoi (Job 41:11, "who has first given to me, that I should repay him?"). The closing triad of prepositions in 11:36 functions as a creedal formula that echoes through Christian liturgy (cf. Colossians 1:16, 1 Corinthians 8:6).

Daniel Boyarin's *A Radical Jew* (1994) argues that Romans 11 is "the heart of Paul's spirit of Jewishness" — the chapter where Paul's wrestling with God's faithfulness to Israel produces his most profound theological achievement. Beverly Gaventa's *When in Romans* (2016) and *Our Mother Saint Paul* read the chapter through the apocalyptic lens, with particular attention to the cosmic Powers of Sin and Death whose grip on humanity (Jew and Gentile) Paul depicts in 11:32 ("God has consigned all to disobedience, that he may have mercy on all").

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc into Romans 11 runs through every major covenant text in the Hebrew Bible: the unconditional Abrahamic covenant (Gen 12, 15, 17), the Sinaitic covenant (Ex 19-24, Deut), the Davidic covenant (2 Sam 7, Ps 89), and the new covenant of Jeremiah 31 and Ezekiel 36. Paul's claim that Israel's "gifts and calling" are "irrevocable" (11:29) presupposes the unconditional covenants while wrestling with the apparent failure of Israel to recognize the Messiah. The seven thousand who had not bowed to Baal (1 Kings 19:18, cited at 11:4) becomes Paul's prototype of the remnant within Israel — Paul, his fellow Jewish believers, the early Jewish-Christian church.

**Jewish interpretive trajectories** through the medieval and modern periods rarely engage Romans 11 directly (it being a Christian text), but the underlying claim — that God's covenant with Israel is irrevocable — has resonance with rabbinic and later Jewish theology of *brit olam* (eternal covenant). The post-Holocaust Jewish theology of Emil Fackenheim (*To Mend the World*, 1982), Eliezer Berkovits (*Faith After the Holocaust*, 1973), and Irving Greenberg (*The Jewish Way*, 1988) has articulated covenantal theologies that, while developed independently, share with Paul the affirmation that the covenant cannot be revoked — an affirmation that, in Greenberg's case in particular, has been a basis for Jewish-Christian dialogue.

**Christian interpretive trajectories** through the **Patristic** period mostly read 11:25-26 as a future mass conversion of Israel at the parousia. Origen (*Commentary on Romans* 8.12) defends the literal future of Israel; Theodoret similarly. Augustine (*City of God* 20.29) reads the passage as a future pre-parousia conversion of Jews led by the figure of Elijah, a reading that becomes traditional in Western Christianity and in medieval eschatology (Joachim of Fiore, Aquinas).

The **Reformation** period brings sharply divergent readings. Calvin in his *Commentary on Romans* (1540) reads "all Israel" not as a future mass of ethnic Jews but as the **total elect of historical Israel** — every Jew who across history has belonged to the elect remnant. This reading becomes the standard Reformed position and is defended by Hofius and (with modifications) by Reformed federalists who read Israel here as the spiritual Israel = the Church (a position Calvin himself sometimes wavers on). Luther's view shifted across his career, ending in the bitter anti-Jewish polemic of *On the Jews and Their Lies* (1543) — a position later Lutheran tradition has had to reckon with painfully.

The **dispensationalist** tradition (Darby, Scofield, Charles Ryrie, John Walvoord, John MacArthur) reads 11:25-26 as the cornerstone of premillennial eschatology: the fullness of the Gentiles is the church age, ending with the rapture; God then returns to Israel and brings about a future ethnic-national conversion at the parousia, fulfilling the Davidic promises in a literal millennial kingdom.

The **20th century** brings the most consequential transformations. **Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* II/2** (1942) treats Romans 11 as the theological center of the doctrine of election. Israel and the Church are two forms of the one elect community of God; Israel's election is irrevocable because Christ — the elect of God — sums up Israel in himself. The "all Israel" of 11:26 is fulfilled christologically, not by a separate future conversion. Barth's reading transforms the question of Christian-Jewish relations and provides the theological substrate for much post-Holocaust Christian engagement.

The **post-Holocaust** rereading of Romans 11 is now central to Catholic and mainline Protestant theology. **Vatican II's *Nostra Aetate*** (1965) cites 11:28-29 directly: the Jews "remain most dear to God, for the sake of the fathers, for he does not repent of the gifts he makes nor of the calls he issues." The **Pontifical Biblical Commission's** *The Jewish People and Their Sacred Scriptures in the Christian Bible* (2002) develops this hermeneutically; the **PBC's *The Gifts and the Calling of God Are Irrevocable*** (2015) takes its title directly from Romans 11:29 and articulates a Catholic theology of Israel that explicitly rejects the supersessionism that read the Church as having replaced Israel. **Hans Joachim Schoeps's *Paul: The Theology of the Apostle in the Light of Jewish Religious History*** (1959), **Krister Stendahl's *Paul Among Jews and Gentiles*** (1976), **Lloyd Gaston's *Paul and the Torah*** (1987), **Franklin Littell's *The Crucifixion of the Jews*** (1975), **A. Roy Eckardt's *Elder and Younger Brothers*** (1967), and **Paul van Buren's *A Theology of the Jewish-Christian Reality*** (3 vols., 1980-88) form a tradition of Christian theology after Auschwitz for which Romans 11 is the canonical text.

The **four major modern interpretive schools** read this chapter sharply differently.

**Old Perspective (Augustine/Luther/Calvin/Reformed):** The chapter is about God's faithfulness to his elect, not to ethnic Israel as such. "All Israel" in 11:26 is either the total elect of historical Israel (Calvin) or the spiritual Israel = the Church (Augustine, Hofius). The hardening is judicial — Israel hardened itself first (cf. ch 10), and God confirms it. Schreiner (*Romans*, BECNT) modifies this somewhat — he allows for a future eschatological inclusion of ethnic Israel — but locates it within an electing-grace framework. Steelman: this reading preserves the systematic-theological consistency of Reformed doctrine and prevents any "second way" of salvation outside Christ.

**New Perspective (Sanders, Dunn, N.T. Wright):** Wright's reading in *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* (vol. 2, ch. 11) and his *Romans* commentary takes the chapter as the demonstration that God's covenantal faithfulness to Israel is fulfilled through the Messiah. "All Israel" in 11:26 refers to the eschatologically restored people of God, which includes Jews who come to recognize Jesus as Messiah at or before the parousia, alongside the Gentiles already incorporated. Wright is firm that this is *not* two-covenant theology; salvation is always through the Messiah. But the Messiah's salvation is for Israel and the nations together, restoring the Genesis-12 promise. Steelman: this reading honors the corporate-narrative shape of Romans 9-11, integrates with Pauline christology, and avoids both supersessionism and two-covenant pluralism.

**Apocalyptic Paul (J.L. Martyn, Beverly Gaventa, Douglas Campbell):** Romans 11 is the climax of the apocalyptic drama in which God's invasive grace breaks the cosmic Powers' grip on humanity — Jew and Gentile alike. Gaventa's *Our Mother Saint Paul* and *When in Romans* read 11:32 ("God has consigned all to disobedience, that he may have mercy on all") as the chapter's center: God has shut up everyone in disobedience as the precondition for showing mercy to all. The "all Israel" of 11:26 is not a separate national salvation but the eschatological completion of God's deliverance. Campbell's *Deliverance of God* takes the universalist edge most strongly. Steelman: this reading takes seriously the cosmic stakes, integrates Romans 11 with Romans 5 and 8, and recovers Paul's apocalyptic frame.

**Paul Within Judaism (Mark Nanos, Pamela Eisenbaum, Matthew Thiessen, Paula Fredriksen, Lloyd Gaston, John Gager):** The chapter teaches **two-covenant theology** or a *Sonderweg* ("special way"): Israel is saved through its own covenant with God, while Gentiles are included through faith in the Messiah. Gaston's *Paul and the Torah* and Gager's *Reinventing Paul* argue that 11:26 ("all Israel will be saved") is fulfilled apart from Jewish faith in Christ; Israel's salvation is by God's covenantal faithfulness, not by conversion. Eisenbaum (*Paul Was Not a Christian*) and Thiessen (*A Jewish Paul*) develop this further: Paul's mission is to Gentiles; he has no mission to Israel; Israel remains in its covenant. Fredriksen (*Paul: The Pagan's Apostle*) emphasizes that the parousia, in Paul's expectation, will bring the eschatological reconciliation of Israel and the nations under the Messiah, but does not require Israel's prior conversion. Steelman: this reading takes 11:28-29 with utmost seriousness, integrates with post-Holocaust Christian theology, and recovers a Paul who is genuinely Jewish.

The four schools yield genuinely different theologies of Christian-Jewish relations. Old Perspective tends toward soft supersessionism; New Perspective rejects supersessionism while retaining christological universalism; Apocalyptic Paul tends toward universalism; Paul Within Judaism approaches two-covenant pluralism. The choice has consequences not just for academic theology but for liturgy, dialogue, and mission.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: The meaning of "all Israel will be saved" (11:26).** Five interpretive options compete:

*Option 1 — Every individual Israelite who has ever lived* (universalist-millennial; some early millenarians). The plain sense maximally extended. Steelman: it honors the universal language of 11:32 ("mercy on all").

*Option 2 — The eschatological future Israel at or near the parousia* (most major modern commentators: Cranfield, Moo, Wright, Schreiner, Dunn, Stuhlmacher; dispensationalist premillennials more elaborately). A future mass turning of Jewish people to the Messiah. Steelman: this is the most natural reading of *pleroma* and *pas Israēl*, integrates with the climactic structure of the chapter, and accounts for Paul's lament in chapter 9.

*Option 3 — The full number of Jews who believe across history* (Calvin, some Reformed). The total elect of historical Israel without any future mass event. Steelman: it preserves Reformed soteriology and integrates with chapter 9's election-language.

*Option 4 — Israel = the spiritual Israel = the Church* (Augustine, Hofius, some Reformed federalists). The chapter dissolves into ecclesiology. Steelman: it integrates Romans 11 with Galatians 6:16 ("the Israel of God") and Philippians 3:3 ("we are the circumcision").

*Option 5 — Israel maintains its own covenant alongside the Christian community* (two-covenant: Gaston, Gager, Nanos, Stendahl, Eisenbaum, Fredriksen; consonant with *Nostra Aetate* and PCBC 2015). The chapter teaches that Israel's salvation is through God's irrevocable covenant, not through Christian conversion. Steelman: it takes 11:28-29 with full weight, addresses the post-Holocaust theological imperative, and accounts for Paul's own continued Jewish identity.

**Where this leaves us:** The text under-determines the choice. Option 4 has the weakest exegetical case and is now broadly rejected (the term "Israel" elsewhere in Romans 9-11 unambiguously means ethnic Israel). Option 5 has the strongest dialogical case but the weakest fit with Pauline christology elsewhere. Option 2 is the most widely held by modern commentators across the theological spectrum, but it leaves unresolved the relationship between this future event and Paul's general statements about salvation through Christ. The chapter resists tidy systematization. Schreiner once observed that "every interpretation of Romans 11 has problems"; the question is which problems one is willing to live with.

**Tension Two: Divine determination and human freedom revisited.** The chapter recapitulates the tension of chapter 9 in a new key. Israel was hardened (11:7, 25); the hardening is partial and temporary (11:25); when the fullness of Gentiles comes in, the hardening lifts. Is the hardening God's act or Israel's?

*Position 1 — Judicial hardening (Reformed, Augustinian):* God hardens those who have hardened themselves. Israel rejected the Messiah; God confirms the rejection by sending a "spirit of stupor" (11:8). The hardening is just because it follows guilt.

*Position 2 — Strategic hardening (Wright, New Perspective):* The hardening is functional within God's plan — Israel's stumbling opens space for Gentile inclusion (11:11), which then provokes Israel to jealousy (11:14), which then leads to Israel's restoration. The hardening is not punitive but instrumental.

*Position 3 — Apocalyptic captivity (Martyn, Gaventa):* The hardening is the captivity to the Powers from which Israel, like the Gentiles, must be delivered. God consigns *all* to disobedience precisely so that mercy becomes the only ground of salvation (11:32). The mystery is mercy, not judgment.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter holds together divine sovereignty (the hardening is God's act, 11:8, 25), human responsibility (Israel was hardened "because of unbelief," 11:20), and divine purpose (the hardening serves a strategic end, 11:11-12, 25). No single resolution dissolves the tension; the doxology of 11:33-36 acknowledges this with the cry "how unsearchable are his judgments." Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* II/2 read this not as resignation but as the theological climax: the God who acts in election cannot be domesticated by any of our schemes.

**One unresolved question:** When Paul writes that "the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" (11:29), does this commit a Christian theology to affirming God's covenantal commitment to *Jewish people as Jewish*, including those who do not confess Jesus as Messiah? The 2015 PCBC document *The Gifts and the Calling of God Are Irrevocable* takes this position formally for the Catholic Church. The unresolved question for Christian theology is whether this position is exegetically grounded or pastorally well-meant; whether Paul intended it or whether he intended only that ethnic Israel will be christologically restored at the eschaton. The text does not decide.

## The Thread

- **1 Kings 19:18** — the seven thousand who have not bowed to Baal (cited at 11:4) — Paul's prototype of the remnant within Israel, demonstrating that God's preservation of his people is invariably through a faithful minority.
- **Isaiah 59:20-21 / Jeremiah 31:33-34** — the deliverer from Zion who establishes the new covenant (cited at 11:26-27) — Paul's eschatological proof-text for Israel's future restoration, drawn from the prophetic promise of covenant renewal.
- **Romans 9:6** — flag back: "not all who are descended from Israel belong to Israel." Chapter 11's "all Israel will be saved" must be read against chapter 9's redefinition of Israel; the tension is the chapter's interpretive crux.
- **Romans 12-15** — flag forward: the doxology of 11:33-36 marks the structural pivot of Romans. The theological argument concludes; the practical exhortation begins. "I appeal to you therefore" (12:1) presupposes everything from 1:18-11:36.
