# Jeremiah 48: The Moab Oracle and the Shaming of Chemosh

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political situation.** Jeremiah 48 is the third of the Oracles Against the Nations (chs. 46–51) and is the *longest single OAN in the entire book* — 47 verses, exceeded only by the two-chapter Babylon oracle (chs. 50–51). Its target is **Moab**, Israel's eastern neighbor on the Transjordanian plateau, the kingdom occupying the territory between the Arnon River (modern Wadi Mujib) in the north and the Zered (Wadi al-Hasa) in the south. Moab's late history runs roughly thus: vassal of Assyria from Tiglath-pileser III through Ashurbanipal (c. 740–630 BC), with Moabite kings (Salamanu, Kammusunadbi, Mutsuri, Kammusilu, Kaashalta) appearing in Assyrian tribute lists; tributary in turn to Babylon after the Assyrian collapse; engaged with Judah in shifting alliances through Jehoiakim's reign (cf. 2 Kgs 24:2, where Moabite raiding bands accompany Babylonian forces against Judah in Jehoiakim's last years). The destruction described in ch. 48 is most plausibly tied to **Nebuchadnezzar's 582 BC campaign** through Transjordan (Josephus, *Antiquities* 10.181–2, records a Moab-and-Ammon campaign in Nebuchadnezzar's 23rd year, c. 582 BC), which depopulated the eastern kingdoms and ended Moab as an independent political entity. Moabite sites show extensive destruction layers in the early 6th century; by the Persian period the Moabite identity has dissolved into the broader Transjordanian Arab population.

**Material culture.** Iron Age Moab is among the best-attested non-Israelite cultures of the southern Levant. Its **language**, **religion**, and **political institutions** are independently documented. The **Mesha Stele** (also called the **Moabite Stone**) — a basalt stele c. 1.15 m tall, discovered at Dhiban (biblical **Dibon**, named in Jer 48:18, 22) in 1868 by F. A. Klein, now in the Louvre — preserves a 34-line royal inscription by **Mesha king of Moab**, dated to c. **840 BC**. Mesha celebrates his recovery of Moabite territory from Israel and explicitly attributes his victories to *Chemosh* (the Moabite *Kemosh*): *Chemosh was angry with his land… I built Qarchoh, with prison-walls and acropolis-walls… I have built Aroer and made the highway by the Arnon… I have built Bezer for it was in ruins, with fifty men of Dibon…* The stele is the longest extant text in the Moabite language (a close cognate of Hebrew, with distinctive features such as preserved final-vowel mimation in some forms, and the use of *waw-consecutive* much as in Hebrew). It corroborates the geography of Jer 48 — Dibon, Aroer, Heshbon, the Arnon, the Israel-Moab border — and confirms that **Chemosh** is the national deity in whose name Moabite political power is exercised. Trude and Moshe Dothan, P. Bordreuil, Ze'ev Herzog, and most recently the **Karak Resources Project** and **Dibon excavations** have continued to clarify Moabite material culture; **Larry Herr** and **Bruce Routledge** (*Moab in the Iron Age*, 2004) provide the standard archaeological synthesis. **Kenneth Kitchen** has worked on the Moabite epigraphic corpus. Moabite ceramics, the **King's Highway** (the Iron Age Transjordanian trade route running from Damascus to the Gulf of Aqaba, named in Num 20:17), the wine-producing terraces of Sibmah (Jer 48:32), and the cultic high places (Jer 48:35) are all archaeologically confirmed.

**Authorship and dating.** **Bright** (Anchor) and **Lundbom** (Anchor) defend Jeremianic authorship for the bulk of the chapter, while acknowledging extensive intertextual borrowing from Isaiah 15–16 and possibly Numbers 21:27–30. **Holladay** (Hermeneia) treats vv.29–38 as drawn directly from Isaiah 16:6–10, vv.43–44 from Isaiah 24:17–18, and v.45–46 from Numbers 21:28–29 — the chapter is, more than any other in the OAN, a literary mosaic incorporating prior oracular material. **Carroll** (OTL) treats it as substantially redactional. **McKane**'s ICC volume 2 is the most thorough commentary on the chapter, with sustained attention to the Isaiah-Jeremiah literary relationship. The MT-LXX divergence is acute: in the LXX the Moab oracle sits at chapter 31 (in the LXX numbering) and is *significantly shorter*, lacking several MT plus-verses; **J. G. Janzen** (*Studies in the Text of Jeremiah*) and **Tov** treat the LXX as preserving an earlier, shorter Hebrew Vorlage that was later expanded in the MT tradition.

**Archaeological and textual evidence.** **The Mesha Stele / Moabite Stone (Louvre AO 5066)** is the dominant epigraphic source — its theology (Chemosh angry / appeased; the *ḥerem* of Israelite captives offered to Ashtar-Chemosh) directly parallels biblical theology, with the polarity reversed. The **Dhiban excavations** (1950s–60s, 2000s) confirm the city's Moabite character. **Heshbon** (Tell Hesban, excavated by Lawrence Geraty and others) shows occupation through the Iron Age. Jer 48 names **Heshbon, Dibon, Aroer, Nebo, Sibmah, Bozrah, Madmen, Horonaim, Eglath-Shelishiyah, Luhith, Kerioth, Holon, Jahzah, Mephaath, Beth-diblathaim, Kiriathaim, Beth-gamul, Beth-meon, Kir-hareseth, Elealeh, Jahaz, Zoar** — an exceptionally dense topographical inventory, most of which is securely identified on the Madaba Plateau and the southern Transjordanian plateau. The chapter also references **Sihon** (v.45), the Amorite king whose defeat by Israel in Num 21 yielded the Moabite-Amorite border region to Israel.

**Key terms.** *Kemosh* (כְּמוֹשׁ, Moabite *kmš*, the Moabite national god, vv.7, 13, 46 — directly attested on the Mesha Stele); *miqdash* (מִקְדָּשׁ, "high-place sanctuary," v.35, the cultic installation where Moabite worship takes place); *zaʿaq* (זָעַק, "cry out," vv.3–4, the lament-cry that recurs); *tsiyyim* (צִיִּים, "wild creatures of the desert," frequent in OAN imagery though not directly here, the broader semantic field of desolation); the punning name *Madmen* (מַדְמֵן, v.2) — a Moabite town whose name plays on Hebrew *damam* "to be silent" (*also you, Madmen, shall be silenced*).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is a sustained dirge of extraordinary length, structured loosely as a series of overlapping panels rather than a single linear argument: announcement of disaster (vv.1–10), the metaphor of unmoved-wine (vv.11–13), the boast and downfall (vv.14–25), the drunkard image (vv.26–28), the proud Moab (vv.29–39), the final invasion (vv.40–47). The *qina* meter (3:2 stress) dominates, marking the genre as lament. **John Geyer's** *Mythology and Lament* (2004) treats this chapter as the showcase of OAN lament-form — the prophet *grieves* Moab even as he announces her destruction, and at vv.31–32 the divine voice itself takes up the lament: *Therefore I wail for Moab… more than for Jazer I weep for you, O vine of Sibmah*.

The most striking literary feature is the **inter-prophetic borrowing**. Jer 48:29–38 closely parallels **Isaiah 16:6–10** (the Isaianic Moab oracle); Jer 48:43–44 reproduces the *terror, pit, snare* triad from **Isaiah 24:17–18**; Jer 48:45–46 echoes the Heshbon-Sihon poem from **Numbers 21:27–30**. This is the densest case of intra-prophetic textual reuse in the entire OAN corpus. **Holladay** treats the direction of dependence as Jeremiah → Isaiah (i.e., Jeremiah quotes the older Isaianic text); **Lundbom** holds that both prophets may be drawing on a shared Moab-OAN tradition (an oral or written stock of anti-Moab oracles). **Childs**, in his *canonical* approach, brackets the source-critical question and reads the parallels as the Bible's own self-thickening — the canon develops by recombining and re-proclaiming earlier prophetic word.

The chapter's literary devices are dense:

- The **wine-vessel metaphor** (vv.11–13): Moab has been *at ease from his youth*, settled on his lees, never poured from vessel to vessel. The image is technical viticulture: undisturbed wine becomes thick and sour; the Lord will send "pourers" who will empty Moab from vessel to vessel and break the jars. This is one of the most memorable extended metaphors in Jeremiah.
- The **wagging head** (v.27): just as Moab has *wagged his head* at Israel, so now Moab will become an object of derision.
- The **drunkard / vomit** image (v.26): Moab made drunk on its own pride, wallowing in its vomit.
- The **eagle / wings** image (v.40): the Babylonian invader as predator descending.
- The **terror, pit, snare** sequence (vv.43–44): the unavoidable cascading judgment.
- The **dove in the gorge** (v.28): the only refuge is the cleft of a wadi-cliff.

Verse 10's curse — *Cursed is he who does the work of the LORD with slackness* — is striking and debated. Calvin reads it as a directive to those who execute divine judgment; Bonhoeffer found in it a chilling text. The verse is theologically uncomfortable in its endorsement of military zeal, yet the canonical placement marks it as the redactor's emphatic frame.

Verse 47's restoration promise — *Yet I will restore the fortunes of Moab in the latter days* — is the chapter's eschatological turn and one of the most theologically loaded sentences in the OAN. The same closing formula appears for Egypt (46:26), Ammon (49:6), and Elam (49:39); but it is *not* offered to Edom or to the Philistines.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Place in the canonical arc.** Moab is canonically Israel's *kindred enemy*: descended from Lot and his elder daughter (Gen 19:30–38), the Moabites are blood relatives of Abraham. The Moabite Ruth, a Davidic ancestress, complicates any simple anti-Moabite reading; the Mesha Stele's Israel-Moab border conflicts (parallel to 2 Kings 3) document the political tension; Deut 23:3–6 excludes Moabites from the assembly to the tenth generation; Isaiah 15–16 and Jeremiah 48 announce judgment; yet 48:47 promises restoration. The arc runs: kinship → enmity → judgment → restoration.

**Jewish trajectory.** The Talmudic and midrashic engagement with Moab is rich. **Bavli Yevamot 76b–77a** discusses the famous halakhic problem of how Ruth's status as a Moabite could allow her marriage to Boaz given Deut 23:3; the solution (a Moabite *male* is excluded, but a Moabite *female* is not) became the legal foundation for Davidic legitimacy. **Bavli Bava Batra 91a** treats Naomi and Elimelech's emigration to Moab. **Targum Jonathan** on 48:7 paraphrases the shaming of Chemosh as the futility of idol-trust. **Rashi** on 48:13 pairs Moab's shame at Chemosh with Israel's shame at Bethel (the Northern Kingdom's idolatrous sanctuary), reading both as warnings against false confidence in any cultic system not rooted in YHWH. **Radak** on 48:47 reads the restoration promise as an assurance that the converts to Judaism from Moab (cf. Ruth) form the spiritual fulfillment of *the latter days*. **Abrabanel** treats the chapter as evidence for divine universal sovereignty; the Mesha-style theology of national gods is decisively overturned in vv.7, 13.

**Christian trajectory.** **Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah*** treat Moab allegorically as *carnal pride*; the wine-vessel metaphor (vv.11–13) becomes a figure for the soul that has not been disturbed by spiritual discipline and so has soured. **Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah*** is detailed on Moabite geography (he had walked the Madaba Plateau himself and discusses the Roman-period names of the towns). **Aquinas** does not treat the chapter directly but uses ch. 48:10 in his discussion of zealous obedience (*ST* II-II.81–84). **Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah*** are particularly extensive on this chapter, treating the wine-vessel metaphor with sustained attention: the soul that has been at ease without trial does not develop the depths required for true faith. Calvin reads v.13 (Moab ashamed of Chemosh as Israel was ashamed of Bethel) as devastating — *no nation*, including the visible church, is exempt from idol-shaming. **Reformation** readings (Luther's commentary on the prophets is briefer) tend toward typological-spiritual readings.

**Modern critical reception.** **Duhm** assigned much of ch. 48 to a redactor and treated it as a derivative composition built from Isaiah 15–16. **Mowinckel** placed it in source C. **Holladay** (Hermeneia) provides the most detailed treatment of the Isaiah-Jeremiah literary parallels. **Lundbom** (Anchor) defends substantial Jeremianic authenticity while acknowledging the borrowings. **Carroll** (OTL) treats it as a redactional patchwork. **McKane** (ICC vol. 2) is the standard reference for philology and inter-prophetic borrowing. **Bright** (Anchor) provides the historical-critical framing. **John Geyer's** *Mythology and Lament* (2004) argues for the chapter as a major exhibit of the lament-genre in the OAN. **Christopher Wright** (*The Mission of God*) and **Brueggemann** read the closing restoration (v.47) as theologically programmatic.

**Where the trajectories diverge.** The Christian reception, especially in patristic allegory, reads Moab as a type of carnal pride — opening into a moral-allegorical reading that the Jewish tradition does not pursue. The Jewish tradition reads ch. 48 historically and halakhically (the Ruth precedent, the Davidic line, the Moabite-conversion possibility) — opening into a different kind of universalism, what the Talmud and later commentators call the **Noahide framework** (Avodah Zarah 2b — the seven Noahide commandments as the universal moral law of the nations). The critical reception (Carroll, McKane) reads the chapter as redactional theology. **Pauline** universalism (Rom 11:25–32 — *all Israel will be saved* and the inclusion of the Gentiles) and **patristic universal restoration** (Origen, *de Principiis*; Gregory of Nyssa's *apokatastasis*) read 48:47 as warrant for cosmic-restoration theology — even Moab is restored in the latter days. **Catholic** doctrine (*Lumen Gentium* 16 on possible salvation of those outside the visible church, with the strong qualifications of *Dominus Iesus*) and **Reformed inclusivism** (Karl Barth's universal-tendency, in the strand opened by *Church Dogmatics* II/2 on election) develop in different directions. The least intuitive reading is the **apokatastatic** one — that 48:47 commits the canon to a hope of universal restoration so wide it embraces even pagan kingdoms whose national god (Chemosh) demanded human sacrifice.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Inter-prophetic borrowing — what does it mean for the doctrine of inspiration that Jer 48 quotes Isaiah 15–16, Numbers 21, and possibly other earlier sources?**

The textual fact is hard to dispute: Jer 48:29–38 is closely parallel to Isa 16:6–10; Jer 48:43–44 reproduces Isa 24:17–18; Jer 48:45–46 echoes Num 21:28–29. **Holladay** reads this as Jeremiah using the older Isaianic text. **Lundbom** suggests both prophets draw on a shared Moab-OAN tradition. The doctrinal stakes are real. **Catholic** doctrine of inspiration (*Dei Verbum*) treats the human author as a true author whose individual style and use of sources is preserved within the divine authorship; the borrowing is theologically unproblematic. **Reformed** doctrines of inspiration (Westminster Confession 1.4, Warfield's *plenary verbal* inspiration) similarly accommodate human use of sources without compromising divine authority. **Childs's canonical approach** brackets the source-critical question: the canonical form of Jer 48, including its borrowings, is what scripture gives the church; the borrowing itself becomes part of the text's self-thickening, the canon's ongoing re-proclamation of earlier word. **Critical** readings often treat the borrowing as evidence that the OAN are a redactional construction rather than original Jeremianic prophecy. The least intuitive position is the canonical one — that the borrowing *is* the inspiration. **Steelman.** Childs's argument is that scripture's inspiration includes its own internal echoing: when Jeremiah quotes Isaiah, he is not plagiarizing but standing in continuity with the prophetic tradition, and the Spirit's authorship runs through both texts and their relationship. The doctrine of inspiration is not threatened by the recognition that the prophets read each other; it is strengthened. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter's literary mosaic is theologically meaningful, not theologically problematic. Scripture re-uses scripture as a sign of its own coherence under one Spirit.

**Tension 2: The restoration of Moab (48:47) — does the OAN end in universal hope, and how widely should that hope be read?**

The closing promise *Yet I will restore the fortunes of Moab in the latter days* is the canonical-theological hinge of the chapter. **Pauline universalism** (Rom 11:25–32 — *all Israel will be saved* and the *fullness of the Gentiles*) draws on this OAN-restoration tradition for its eschatological vision. **Patristic universal restoration** — Origen's *apokatastasis* in *de Principiis* (a doctrine condemned in its strict form at the Second Council of Constantinople in 553, though Origen himself remains a respected though qualified father), Gregory of Nyssa's more guarded universalism (in his *Catechetical Oration* and *de Anima et Resurrectione*) — reads such verses as scriptural warrant for cosmic restoration. **Catholic** doctrine (*Lumen Gentium* 16, on the salvation of those who through no fault of their own do not know Christ but seek God; *Dominus Iesus* on the necessity of Christ for salvation) negotiates between universalism and particularism; the catechism's hope for the salvation of all (CCC 1058) is qualified but real. **Reformed** tradition splits: classical Calvinism (Westminster, Synod of Dort) restricts saving grace to the elect; Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* II/2 develops a universal-tendency reading of election in Christ that has been read (rightly or wrongly) as quasi-universalist; modern Reformed inclusivists (Bloesch, Stackhouse) treat 48:47-style restoration verses as scriptural ground for hope. **Jewish Noahide universalism** (Avodah Zarah 2b — the seven Noahide commands as the law of the nations; **Maimonides** *Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Melachim* 8.11 on the righteous of the nations having a portion in the world to come) offers a parallel framework: salvation for the nations is real, on the basis of natural moral law plus monotheism, without requiring conversion. The least intuitive reading for most evangelical Protestants is the **apokatastatic** one — that 48:47 may commit the canon to a hope of restoration so wide that even pagan kingdoms whose national god demanded human sacrifice (cf. 2 Kgs 3:27, Mesha sacrificing his son to Chemosh) are eventually included. **Steelman.** The apokatastatic reading takes seriously that the verse offers no qualification: it does not promise restoration only to a remnant of Moab, only to converted Moabites, only on terms of Mosaic obedience. The restoration is unconditional. To read it more narrowly is to qualify what scripture itself does not qualify. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter ends with a hope wider than its judgment, and the canonical reader is left to decide how widely to extend that wider hope. There are responsible readings on both sides; the verse is the rock around which they break.

**Closing unresolved question.** If Moab — whose national god demanded child sacrifice, whose kings boasted on the Mesha Stele of *ḥerem* (devotion to destruction) of captured Israelite cities to Ashtar-Chemosh, whose pride is denounced in 47 verses of dirge — is nevertheless promised restoration in the latter days (48:47), what kind of restoration is it? Is it the inclusion of righteous individual Moabites under a Noahide framework? The conversion of the Moabite people *qua* people? The cosmic-restoration theology in which all things are reconciled in Christ (Col 1:20)? The text refuses to specify, and that refusal is itself the chapter's final theological question.

## The Thread

Ch. 48 is the longest member of the OAN tradition (**Isa 13–23, Ezek 25–32, Amos 1–2, Obadiah, Nahum, Zephaniah 2:4–15, Zech 9–10**) and is in direct literary dependence on **Isaiah 15–16** (the corresponding Isaianic Moab oracle), **Isaiah 24:17–18** (the *terror, pit, snare* triad), and **Numbers 21:27–30** (the Heshbon-Sihon poem). The wine-vessel metaphor (vv.11–13) finds its NT echo in **Matthew 9:17** (new wine in old wineskins). The shaming of Chemosh (vv.7, 13) prefigures the Pauline argument that *we know an idol has no real existence* (1 Cor 8:4) and that the gods of the nations are *no gods* (Gal 4:8–9). The restoration of Moab (v.47) flows forward into **Romans 11** (the inclusion of the Gentiles) and **Revelation 21:24** (the kings of the nations bringing their glory into the new Jerusalem). The Moabite Ruth in the Davidic genealogy (Mt 1:5) is the canon's quiet anticipation of 48:47: a Moabite already incorporated into the messianic line, the latter days already breaking into the early days.
