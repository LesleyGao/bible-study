# Leviticus 1: The Hand on the Head

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Leviticus opens where Exodus ended, at the doorway of the tent of meeting. The cloud has settled, the glory has filled the tabernacle, and Moses — who could not enter at the close of Exodus 40 — is now addressed *from* the tent. The book begins with a verb of invitation: *wayyiqrāʾ* (וַיִּקְרָא), "and he called." This is the Hebrew title of the book, taken from its first word. English readers miss the opening warmth of the summons because "Leviticus" (from the Greek Λευιτικόν, "matters concerning Levites") shifts the emphasis to the officials rather than to the God who calls.

The political situation of the narrative is a people at the foot of Sinai, not yet settled in the land. The critical scholarly consensus is that Leviticus in its final form was produced much later — during or after the Babylonian exile, when Israel had lost the temple and needed the portable, repeatable pattern of worship the book describes. Julius Wellhausen argued in *Prolegomena to the History of Israel* (1878) that the entire Priestly source, including Leviticus, was a post-exilic retrojection — a Second Temple blueprint dressed in Mosaic robes. That claim has been the field's central argument ever since. Menahem Haran in *Temples and Temple-Service in Ancient Israel* (1978) and Jacob Milgrom across his three-volume Anchor Bible *Leviticus* (1991–2000) pushed back hard, arguing that Leviticus 1–16 preserves pre-exilic Priestly (P) traditions, with Leviticus 17–26 representing a later but still pre-exilic "Holiness Code" (H) edited by a distinct school. Israel Knohl's *The Sanctuary of Silence* (1995) took the H/P distinction further, arguing that H was a later reform movement critiquing P from within the priestly tradition itself. The question of when matters because the late-dating school reads Leviticus as theology in the form of liturgy — a way to preserve cult in the absence of temple — while the early-dating school reads it as pre-exilic cultic practice that became scripture.

The material culture of the chapter is the cultural world of ancient Near Eastern sacrifice. Every major civilization in the region — Egyptian, Hittite, Mesopotamian, Canaanite, Greek — killed animals as food for their gods. The Ugaritic texts from Ras Shamra (fourteenth century BC, discovered 1928) describe *šlmm* offerings remarkably similar in vocabulary and procedure to Israel's *šĕlāmîm*. Punic sacrificial tariffs from Marseille and Carthage (c. fourth century BC) prescribe specific animals and cuts for specific occasions in language that echoes Leviticus word for word. The Hittite *Instructions for Temple Officials* (fourteenth–thirteenth century BC) preserves purity concerns, priest dressing, and sacrificial procedure nearly identical in form. Leviticus's originality is not the *fact* of sacrifice but the *meaning* it assigns: not feeding the gods (the standard ANE view — gods need food, humans provide it), but covering sin, establishing nearness, enacting a relationship the deity has already initiated. Milgrom made this point the pivot of his commentary: Israelite sacrifice is not *do ut des* ("I give so that you give") but a grammar of restoration.

Five Hebrew terms do work the English cannot. *Qorbān* (קָרְבָּן, "offering," v. 2) comes from the root *qārab*, to draw near — the word names what sacrifice is *for*, not what it is. Jesus cites this term in Mark 7:11 ("corban") when critiquing its misuse. *ʿŌlâ* (עֹלָה, "burnt offering," v. 3), from *ʿālâ* (to go up), is literally "that which ascends" — smoke rising, the offering and the offerer going up to God. The LXX renders it *holokautōma* ("wholly burned"), from which we get *holocaust*; English translators after the Shoah have moved to "burnt offering" to avoid the lexical collision. *Sāmak* (סָמַךְ, "to lay the hand," v. 4) — the worshipper *leans* his hand on the animal's head; the verb is the same one used when Jacob blessed Ephraim and Manasseh (Gen 48:14) and when Moses ordained Joshua (Num 27:18). The verb names transfer: something of the offerer passes into the animal, or something of the animal is designated *for* the offerer. *Kipper* (כִּפֵּר, "make atonement," v. 4) is the central theological verb of the book. Its core meaning is disputed: "cover" (Arabic cognate *kafara*, preferred by older Christian commentators), "wipe" (Akkadian *kuppuru*, preferred by Milgrom, reflecting purgation of the sanctuary rather than the sinner), or "ransom" (Hebrew *kōper*, the ransom price of Ex 30:12). Each translation drives a different atonement theology. *Rêaḥ nîḥōaḥ* (רֵיחַ נִיחוֹחַ, "pleasing aroma," v. 9) is a technical formula inherited from ANE sacrificial language — in the Mesopotamian flood epic *Gilgamesh* XI, the gods smell the sacrifice Utnapishtim offers after the flood and "gather like flies" around it; Noah's offering in Genesis 8:21 uses exactly this phrase, deliberately refashioned. The gods of ANE mythology needed the smell because they had gone hungry during the flood. Israel's God accepts the aroma but is not fed by it.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is priestly instruction (*tôrâ* in the technical sense — "teaching," not legal code). The form is case-based manual: "If his offering is X, he shall do Y." Milgrom called this literary form "ritual jurisprudence." It presumes a trained reader — probably a priest — who knows the sanctuary's default procedures and needs only the case-specific variations.

The book divides cleanly into two halves. Chapters 1–16 form the Priestly (P) core, moving from sacrificial system (1–7) to priestly ordination (8–10) to purity laws (11–15) to the Day of Atonement (16) — the ritual climax. Chapters 17–26 are the Holiness Code (H) with Chapter 27 as appendix. Lev 1 opens the first cycle. Within chapters 1–7, the manual is double-voiced: chapters 1–5 address the *worshipper*, chapters 6–7 the *priest*. Both need to know, but not the same things. Lev 1 belongs to the worshipper's manual.

The literary structure of chapter 1 is a tight threefold division by offering material: bull (vv. 3–9), sheep or goat (vv. 10–13), bird (vv. 14–17). Each unit follows the same sequence — bring, present, slaughter, blood, cut, burn — with variations graded to the animal's size and the offerer's wealth. Bird offerings (v. 14) were for the poor (cf. Lev 5:7, Lev 12:8, and Mary and Joseph's offering in Luke 2:24). The structure encodes a quiet egalitarianism: the same verbs of atonement (v. 4) and the same "pleasing aroma" (vv. 9, 13, 17) apply to the bull of the wealthy and the pigeon of the poor.

A key literary device is the laying on of hands (v. 4), which appears as the hinge between offerer and priest. The worshipper brings and slaughters; the priest handles blood and fire. This division is deliberate — the sanctuary's economy is collaborative, not sacerdotal in the full sense. The priest does not *do* the sacrifice *for* the offerer; the offerer participates bodily. Mary Douglas's *Leviticus as Literature* (1999) read the whole book as an architectural rhetoric, a prose-shaped sanctuary: chapter 1 is the outer courtyard, chapter 16 the inner sanctum.

Textual variants in Leviticus 1 are minor. The Dead Sea Scrolls yielded fragments from 4QLeviticus (4Q17, 4Q23, 4Q24, 4Q25, 4Q26), generally conforming to the Masoretic Text with only small variants. The Septuagint occasionally expands for clarity ("the priest" where MT has just "he"). The Samaritan Pentateuch offers a few harmonizations. None of these shifts the chapter's theology.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Place Leviticus 1 in the canonical arc and it sits at a hinge point: Israel has been liberated (Exodus) and now must learn how a holy God can live in the midst of an unholy people without consuming them. The sacrificial system is the protocol for that presence. Without it, the glory that filled the tabernacle in Exodus 40 becomes a danger (cf. the fire that later consumes Nadab and Abihu in Lev 10). With it, the presence becomes a home.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory**

Second Temple writers read Leviticus 1 with reverent detail. Philo of Alexandria in *On the Special Laws* 1.162–256 allegorized the burnt offering as the total self-offering of the soul to God — the bull's head as intellect, the entrails as appetite, all consumed together, because God wants the whole self. Josephus in *Antiquities* 3.224–236 summarized the sacrificial system for Roman readers with an air of cultural apologetic, arguing that Israelite sacrifice was more restrained and rational than Greek and Egyptian parallels. The Dead Sea Scrolls *Temple Scroll* (11Q19) elaborated and tightened the sacrificial rules for the Qumran community, which was already living without access to the Jerusalem temple.

The rabbinic tradition rebuilt the sacrificial system textually after the temple's destruction in 70 AD. The entire order of *Qodashim* in the Mishnah — six tractates including Zevachim (on animal sacrifice), Menachot (on grain), and Chullin (on slaughter outside the sanctuary) — reconstructed in astonishing detail what could no longer be performed. The Babylonian Talmud in Menachot 110a preserves Rabbi Isaac's saying: "Whoever engages in the study of the laws of the burnt offering is reckoned as if he had offered a burnt offering." Study *became* sacrifice. Rashi (eleventh-century northern France), whose commentary on Leviticus is often tighter and more halakhic than his Torah work, focused on the word *ʾādām* (v. 2) — "when *any person* brings an offering" — and noted the deliberate echo of Adam, the first offerer. Ibn Ezra was more philological; Nachmanides (Ramban, thirteenth century) broke with the rationalist tradition of Maimonides (who in *Guide for the Perplexed* 3.32 had argued sacrifices were a divine concession to idolatrous habit) and insisted in his commentary on Leviticus 1 that sacrifice mirrors the offerer's fate — "the man *should* have been the one slain" — a reading startlingly close to later Christian substitutionary theology.

Modern Jewish readings have moved in several directions. Nehama Leibowitz's *Studies in Vayikra* combed the classical rabbis into a teaching anthology; Jacob Milgrom's Anchor Bible commentary remains the touchstone, arguing Leviticus's theological originality is precisely its refusal of ANE sacrificial ontology (the gods do not eat). Jonathan Sacks in *Covenant and Conversation: Leviticus* emphasized that Leviticus makes holiness a moral and relational category, not merely a cultic one. Avivah Zornberg's readings push into the psychic terrain: the offerer's body drawing near is an act of courage against the instinctive distance one keeps from the holy.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory**

Patristic readings of Leviticus 1 were almost entirely typological. Origen in *Homilies on Leviticus* (c. 240 AD) found Christ in every sacrifice: the bull was Christ's divinity, the sheep his humility, the bird his resurrection ascent. Augustine treated the burnt offering as a figure of love that withholds nothing (*On the Trinity* 4.14). Gregory of Nazianzus and Gregory of Nyssa pursued the same pattern. Chrysostom, more historically minded, emphasized that the sacrifices foreshadowed what Christ fulfilled once for all — a reading the letter to the Hebrews had already established.

Medieval Christian reading followed the allegorical tradition with elaboration. Aquinas in *Summa Theologiae* I–II q. 102 a. 3 distinguished the *figurative* meaning of the sacrifices (pointing to Christ) from the *moral* meaning (the self-offering demanded of every believer) and the *literal* sense (the actual ritual law, now abrogated). This threefold distinction made the book readable without committing the Church to continuing its practice.

The Reformation hardened the typological reading. Luther in his *Lectures on Leviticus* (not widely preserved but reflected in his *Prefaces to the Old Testament*) saw the whole sacrificial system as Christ's shadow, useful only as it illuminated the cross. Calvin in *Institutes* 2.7 and in his *Commentary on the Last Four Books of Moses* pressed the same point — the sacrifices were real in their time but "ceased of their own accord" in Christ. Calvin was uncomfortable with Origen's allegorism and insisted on historical sense first. The Anabaptist tradition generally sidestepped Leviticus as a book about a dispensation now closed.

Critical scholarship from Wellhausen through Gerhard von Rad and Roland de Vaux treated Leviticus as priestly theology in narrative form — important historically, ethically relevant selectively, but not normative ritual law for Christians. Brevard Childs in *Old Testament Theology in a Canonical Context* argued that the Church cannot ignore Leviticus because it is Christian Scripture, even if the Church does not obey its ritual code.

Contemporary readings have recovered more. Walter Brueggemann has emphasized Leviticus's insistence that bodies matter to God. Ephraim Radner's *Leviticus* (Brazos Theological Commentary, 2008) argued for a typological return — the Church has lost something by treating Leviticus as a closed chapter. L. Michael Morales's *Who Shall Ascend the Mountain of the LORD?* (2015) reads the whole Pentateuch as a theological ascent toward dwelling with God, with Leviticus as its structural heart.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge**

Jewish and Christian interpreters agree that Leviticus 1 describes how a gap between human and divine is closed through a substitutionary animal and the worshipper's embodied participation. They diverge sharply on what happens next. Jewish tradition reads the system as a *real ongoing claim* — suspended by the temple's destruction but waiting, preserved in liturgy (the traditional *Aleinu* and *Avodah* still invoke the sacrificial cult), and destined for restoration in messianic time. Christian tradition reads the system as *fulfilled* in Christ's once-for-all sacrifice (Hebrews 9:11–14) — not continuing but completing. Nachmanides's "the man should have been the one slain" is the closest medieval Jewish touch to later Christian substitutionary readings, and the distance is instructive: for Ramban, the animal substitutes *within* a covenant the offerer already inhabits; for Anselm, the substitute is needed *to establish* a covenant the offender has broken.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author of Leviticus 1 believe God was doing? Three things, stacked: inviting (*wayyiqrāʾ*), receiving (the aroma), and making nearness possible (*qorbān*, *kipper*). The theological claim is remarkable: the holy God of Sinai, whose presence threatened to "consume" Israel on the way (Ex 33:3), has built a protocol by which the unholy can draw near *without* being consumed. Sacrifice is not appeasement; it is the terms of relationship.

**Tension One: What does atonement mean?**

The chapter says the burnt offering "shall be accepted for him to make atonement for him" (v. 4). But what does *kipper* do?

- **Catholic teaching** (CCC 613–618, 1366) reads all Levitical atonement as typological of Christ's sacrifice on the cross. The language of "oblation" and "propitiation" applies to Christ as the true burnt offering, and the Eucharist represents his sacrifice without repeating it. Aquinas ST III q. 48 systematized this under the language of *satisfactio*.
- **Reformed** (Calvin, John Owen in *The Death of Death in the Death of Christ*, 1648) read *kipper* as *propitiation* — God's wrath is really turned aside by a substitutionary offering. The cross is the only true propitiation; the Levitical sacrifices were foreshadows accepted provisionally.
- **Lutheran** (Luther on Galatians, Bonhoeffer in *Ethics*) also emphasize propitiation but with a different accent: the cross reveals God's love by absorbing wrath, so atonement is less a transaction and more a disclosure.
- **Wesleyan** and Eastern Orthodox traditions lean toward *expiation* (purging of defilement) and *theosis* (participation) rather than propitiation; Athanasius's *On the Incarnation* focuses on corruption healed rather than wrath appeased.
- **Milgrom's critical reading**, dominant in academic OT study, argues *kipper* means *purge the sanctuary* — the blood cleanses the altar, not the offerer. Sin is imagined as contamination that sticks to the sanctuary; the priest wipes it off. The offerer is forgiven as a consequence, not as the direct object of the verb.
- **Jewish liturgical reading** (the synagogue *Musaf* prayers, Maimonides's *Hilchot Maaseh HaKorbanot*) treats sacrifice as a school for the heart: the offerer witnesses the animal's death, realizes he himself should die, and is drawn toward repentance (*teshuvah*).

*The least intuitive position, steelmanned:* Milgrom's "purge the sanctuary" reading strikes Christian readers as oddly deflationary, even evasive. Its best form is this: the chapter's grammar supports it (the verb *kipper ʿālāyw* technically says "cover *for him*" — the atonement is done *on his behalf*, not directly *to* him). If sin primarily contaminates the sanctuary — the place of God's presence — then forgiveness is a second-order effect of cleaning the space. This reading preserves God's holiness as ontological fact rather than legal posture, and it explains why sin's consequences linger even after forgiveness (the sanctuary gets dirty again).

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter refuses to settle what atonement is. It simply shows that a pleasing aroma rises and the offerer is accepted. The Church's atonement debates are arguments over what *kipper* means at the root — and Leviticus 1 holds them all in tension.

**Tension Two: Is sacrifice obsolete?**

- **Catholic teaching** affirms both: the Levitical sacrifices are abrogated *as to their letter*, fulfilled in Christ, yet the Church offers the Eucharist as a re-presentation (*anamnesis*) of that one sacrifice. The altar at the front of a Catholic sanctuary is theologically continuous with Leviticus's altar.
- **Reformed** tradition insists the sacrifices are *finished*. Any talk of ongoing sacrificial action (including the Mass) is a reversion. The Lord's Supper is a memorial and a means of grace, not a sacrifice.
- **Lutheran** is nuanced: Luther rejected the Mass as sacrifice but retained a robust sense of Christ's bodily presence in the supper.
- **Orthodox** liturgy has never abandoned sacrificial language. The priest at the altar performs the *proskomidē* — the preparation of the offering — and the liturgy is called the Divine Liturgy, the offering.
- **Jewish** tradition treats sacrifice as suspended, not abrogated, with the traditional prayer that "may it be your will… that the Temple be rebuilt speedily in our days." Maimonides in *Mishneh Torah: Hilchot Beit HaBechira* catalogued the laws as binding law awaiting restoration.
- **Critical/academic** readings vary from "sacrifice was always problematic" (feminist critiques of blood and violence) to "sacrifice is anthropologically necessary and Christianity has just internalized it" (René Girard's mimetic theory applied to Leviticus).

*The least intuitive position, steelmanned:* The Orthodox/Catholic insistence that sacrifice has never ended — only transformed — is hardest for Protestant readers to accept. Its best form: if Leviticus teaches that holy presence requires a sustained protocol of self-offering, then "Christ has fulfilled it" cannot mean "you no longer need to offer yourself." The Eucharist, the saint's relics, the daily liturgy — these are not competitors to Calvary but *its continuation in time*. The Reformed account risks rendering the Church's ongoing worship merely expressive.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter ends with smoke rising and the aroma received. What the Church does with that image is a dogmatic question. The text does not resolve it.

**One unresolved question:** The worshipper laid his hand on the animal's head and watched something die *for* him. If you have never watched something die for you, can you read this chapter at all?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 22:13** — The ram caught in the thicket is offered *instead of* Isaac. The first substitutionary burnt offering in the canon. When you read Leviticus 1, remember Moriah; when you read Moriah, remember that every Israelite worshipper is standing where Abraham stood.
- **Isaiah 1:11–17** — "I have had enough of burnt offerings," says the LORD. The prophet attacks the sacrificial system not because it was always wrong but because it has been severed from justice. Hold Leviticus 1 and Isaiah 1 together or you will misread both.
- **Hebrews 10:1–14** — "The law has but a shadow of the good things to come… every priest stands daily at his service, offering repeatedly the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins." Hebrews argues from Leviticus to Christ. When you get there, notice that the author assumes the reader already feels the weight of the book you are now beginning.
- **Romans 12:1** — "Present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable to God, which is your spiritual worship." Paul takes the vocabulary of Leviticus 1 — *present*, *holy*, *acceptable* — and rewrites it as the daily life of the believer. The burnt offering was never about dead animals. It was about whole selves.
