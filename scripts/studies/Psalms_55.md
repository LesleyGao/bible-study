# Psalm 55 — A Scholarly Study

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 55 is among the most psychologically intense laments in the Psalter — a poem that gives sustained voice to the unique pain of betrayal by an intimate friend. The superscription is comparatively spare: "To the choirmaster: with stringed instruments. A *Maskil* of David." Unlike its neighbors Pss 52, 54, 56, the rubric does not anchor the psalm to a specific narrative episode. The Davidic *maskil* designation places it among the wisdom-laments of Book II (Pss 52, 54, 55, 74, 78, 88, 89, 142). The musical rubric *bi-neginot* (בִּנְגִינוֹת) calls for stringed instrument accompaniment in temple performance.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 55 as a *Klagepsalm des Einzelnen* — an individual lament — but recognized its hybrid character: lament, imprecation, and wisdom reflection are densely interwoven. Mowinckel located the poem in a cultic setting of a worshiper besieged by personal enemies. Westermann notes that Ps 55 is unusual in its sustained development of the betrayer's character — most laments name the enemy briefly, but Ps 55 returns repeatedly to the figure of the friend-turned-traitor.

The poem belongs to **Book II of the Psalter (Pss 42–72)** and to the **Elohistic Psalter (Pss 42–83)**. The divine-name pattern is striking: *Elohim* dominates (vv. 1, 14, 16, 19), but *YHWH* appears in vv. 16, 22 (and in v. 9, in some manuscript traditions, though the MT here reads *Adonai*). The mixed pattern suggests an older composition with *YHWH* throughout, partially redacted toward *Elohim* but with significant survival of the original divine name — characteristic of Elohistic Psalter editing, which was systematic but not absolute.

The historical backdrop most readers — premodern and modern alike — have associated with the psalm is the rebellion of Absalom (2 Sam 15–17), in which David's trusted counselor Ahithophel defected to Absalom and counseled the prince's military strategy against the king. The phrase "my companion, my equal, my familiar friend" (אֱנוֹשׁ כְּעֶרְכִּי אַלּוּפִי וּמְיֻדָּעִי, *enosh ke-erki allufi u-meyudda'i*, vv. 13) is most often read as targeting Ahithophel, whose suicide by hanging (2 Sam 17:23) is the only such death in the Hebrew Bible apart from Judas Iscariot. While the connection is editorial inference rather than textual evidence, the psalm's themes of urban violence, betrayed counsel, and the famous line "we used to take sweet counsel together" point unmistakably to a court setting in which a senior advisor has turned against the king.

The Hebrew *iti hamtikkenu sod* (אִתִּי הִמְתִּיקְנוּ סוֹד, "we used to take sweet counsel together," v. 14) is a remarkable construction. The verb *himtik* (to make sweet) governs *sod* (intimate counsel, secret, council). The image is of two friends sitting together and "sweetening counsel" — making the deliberation itself an act of intimacy. The phrase in v. 14b — *be-veit Elohim nehallekh be-ragesh* (בְּבֵית אֱלֹהִים נְהַלֵּךְ בְּרָגֶשׁ, "within God's house we walked in the throng") — places the friendship in a liturgical setting. The betrayed friendship was not secular; it was forged in worship.

The famous line *hashlekh al-Yahweh yahavkha ve-hu yekhalkelekha* (הַשְׁלֵךְ עַל־יְהוָה יְהָבְךָ וְהוּא יְכַלְכְּלֶךָ, "cast your burden on the LORD, and he will sustain you," v. 22) gives the psalm its place in the wider New Testament conversation. The Hebrew *yahav* (יְהָב) is a *hapax legomenon* in this exact form, meaning "burden, lot, what is given." The cognate Aramaic verb *yhb* means "to give." The image is of casting onto God whatever has been *given* to you — your portion, your fate, your appointed burden. The verb is imperative; the action is decisive.

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of Ps 55 in 4QPsa and 11QPsd, with no significant textual variants. The Septuagint (LXX 54) follows the MT closely. ANE parallels: Akkadian and Babylonian lamentations regularly invoke the friend-turned-betrayer, especially in royal court literature; the *Babylonian Theodicy* features the friend as both companion and source of distress. But the Psalter's distinctive note is the friend's location *in worship* — the betrayal happened in God's house.

Date: linguistic features point to pre-exilic composition with possible exilic redaction. Most critics (Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate) treat it as late monarchic, with the *Ahithophel* connection a plausible historical anchor preserved in editorial tradition.

## Lens 2: Literature

Psalm 55 is one of the longest individual laments in the Psalter (23 verses) and uses Robert Lowth's parallelism in dense, recursive patterns. The poem combines synonymous parallelism (v. 4: "my heart is in anguish within me / the terrors of death have fallen upon me") with synthetic / ascending parallelism (v. 6–8: the wings-of-a-dove fantasy unfolds across three verses) and antithetic parallelism (v. 12–13: "it is *not* an enemy / it is *you*").

The poem is strophically tripartite, with two *selah* markers organizing it:

- **Strophe I (vv. 1–8):** Invocation, complaint, escapist longing. The wings-of-a-dove fantasy (vv. 6–8) is the imaginative climax. Closes with *selah*.
- **Strophe II (vv. 9–19):** The city as scene of violence; the betrayer named; first imprecation; first confidence; closes with *selah*.
- **Strophe III (vv. 20–23):** The betrayer's two-faced speech; the famous "cast your burden" call to trust; final imprecation; closing affirmation: "But I will trust in you."

The chiastic structure of the poem pivots on v. 14 ("we used to take sweet counsel together"), the moment of remembered intimacy that gives the entire lament its weight. Hossfeld-Zenger trace an inclusio: the prayer to God to *give ear* (v. 1) and to *give ear* (v. 19) frames the psalm; the betrayer's *smooth speech* (v. 21) is set against the speaker's *prayer* (v. 17, three times daily). The whole architecture is a contrast between two kinds of speech — the betrayer's smooth deception and the speaker's persistent prayer.

The wings-of-a-dove image (*mi-yitten li-aver ka-yonah*, מִי־יִתֶּן־לִי אֵבֶר כַּיּוֹנָה, v. 6) is one of the most poignant escapist longings in scripture. The dove (*yonah*) was the bird of peace and offering (Gen 8; Lev 1:14); to long for dove's wings is to long for the gentleness of withdrawal. William Brown (*Seeing the Psalms*) treats this as the Psalter's signature meditation on the desire for retreat from human society. Importantly, the wish remains unfulfilled — the speaker does *not* fly away. He stays in the city of violence and prays.

The two-faced speech imagery in v. 21 — *chalku machma'ot piv u-qrav-libbo rakku devarav mi-shemen ve-hemmah petichot* (חָלְקוּ מַחְמָאֹת פִּיו וּקְרָב־לִבּוֹ רַכּוּ דְבָרָיו מִשֶּׁמֶן וְהֵמָּה פְתִחוֹת, "his speech was smooth as butter, yet war was in his heart; his words were softer than oil, yet they were drawn swords") — uses a double food/weapon contrast. *Machma'ot* (curdled cream, butter) and *shemen* (oil) are domestic, comforting substances; *qrav* (battle) and *petichot* (drawn swords) are martial. The friend's mouth offered hospitality while his heart drew weapons.

*Selah* at vv. 7 and 19 punctuates the psalm at moments of imaginative climax (the dove's wings) and theological affirmation (the eternal God will humble them) — possibly marking liturgical pauses for instrumental interlude or reflection.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Psalm 55 sits in the Davidic Elohistic cluster of Book II (Pss 51–72), placed after Pss 52–54's escalating reflection on betrayal (Doeg's violent informing, universal corruption, Ziphite betrayal) and before Ps 56's *miktam* on enemy assault. The editorial logic moves from external betrayers (Doeg, Ziphites) to the internal betrayer — the friend. The psalmic universe of Book II teaches that the worst threat is not the foreign enemy but the intimate companion who turns.

**Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 55 explicitly identifies the betraying friend as Ahithophel, citing 2 Samuel's narrative. The midrash develops Ahithophel as the prototype of the *kofer ba-tov* — the one who repays good with evil. Rashi follows this line, glossing v. 13's "my equal, my companion, my familiar friend" as a direct reference to Ahithophel. Ibn Ezra, more philological, parses *yahav* and notes the *hapax* status of v. 22's vocabulary. Radak emphasizes both the historical Ahithophel reading and the wider applicability to any betrayer. The Targum personalizes the betrayer as Ahithophel in v. 13.

In modern Jewish thought, Psalm 55 has received remarkable attention. Avivah Zornberg's *The Beginning of Desire* and *The Murmuring Deep* both reflect on the dove-wings fantasy as a paradigm of psychological flight from unbearable reality. Martin Buber treats the psalm in *Right and Wrong* as evidence that the Hebrew Bible knows the I-Thou relation can be ruptured precisely by those one was closest to. Levenson and Sarna both emphasize the Ahithophel association. Heschel's *The Prophets* alludes to v. 17's threefold daily prayer as a paradigm of liturgical discipline. Liturgically, Psalm 55 is recited in some Sephardic *bakkashot* traditions; in Ashkenazi practice, it does not have a fixed weekday placement but appears in some kabbalistic prayer cycles.

**Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 55 (LXX 54) is one of his most extensive psalm commentaries. He reads the betraying friend christologically as Judas Iscariot — "you, a man, my equal, my companion, my familiar friend" becomes the Lord's lament over Judas, who walked with Jesus in worship and then betrayed him. The line "we used to take sweet counsel together; within God's house we walked in the throng" Augustine reads as Judas in the company of the disciples in the temple. Cassiodorus follows Augustine's typology with elaboration. Aquinas's *Postilla* preserves the Judas reading and develops the *butter-and-oil* deception of v. 21 as a typology of false counsel.

The Reformers gave Ps 55 sustained attention. Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* extends the Judas reading and adds a polemical edge: the false brothers within the church are the most dangerous enemies. Calvin's 1557 commentary stays close to the Davidic/Ahithophel reading and warns Genevan readers about ecclesiastical betrayers — those who "ate at our table and now turn against us." Calvin reads v. 22 ("cast your burden on the LORD") as central to the Reformed doctrine of providence: the believer's burdens are not random but assigned, and to be cast on the Lord is to entrust them to providential ordering.

Modern critical readers extend the conversation. Gunkel and Mowinckel pioneered the form-critical reading. Westermann classifies it among the developed individual laments. Kraus and Hossfeld-Zenger develop the rigorous treatment of the Elohistic Psalter context. Goldingay's *Psalms 42–89* reads the psalm pastorally, asking how a worshiping community handles betrayal by intimate insiders. Tate's *Word Biblical Commentary* catalogs the dense lexical features. Wenham's *Psalms as Torah* reads it as moral formation: the psalm trains worshipers to bring betrayal grief to God rather than retaliating. Walter Brueggemann's *The Costly Loss of Lament* treats Psalm 55 as a paradigm of how lament protests rather than acquiesces — refusing to suppress the fact of betrayal. Patrick Miller's *They Cried to the Lord* and Beth Tanner's recent commentary both note the psalm's psychological depth.

**NT use.** The most significant New Testament use is 1 Peter 5:7: "casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you" (πᾶσαν τὴν μέριμναν ὑμῶν ἐπιρρίψαντες ἐπ' αὐτόν). The Greek participle *epirripsantes* directly translates the Hebrew *hashlekh* of Ps 55:22, and *merimna* (anxiety, care) extends *yahav* (burden, given lot). Petrine theology takes the Davidic burden-casting and universalizes it for the persecuted Christian community. The Augustinian reading of the betrayer-as-Judas extends the Judas typology that Acts 1 develops, where Ahithophel's suicide by hanging (2 Sam 17:23) and Judas's death by hanging (Matt 27:5) are linked through Ps 41:9 ("he who ate my bread has lifted his heel against me") and Ps 55:13–14.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

Two tensions structure the theological work of Psalm 55.

**Tension 1: Betrayal-of-a-friend and the Christian / Jewish ambivalence about flight from suffering.** The psalm contains both the urge to fly away ("if only I had wings like a dove") and the decision to stay and pray. How are believers to handle the desire to escape unbearable relational pain?

- **The traditional Christian position (Augustine, Calvin, modern evangelical):** The dove-wings fantasy is honest expression of the desire for escape, but the psalm models the right response — to *not* fly, to remain in the field of suffering, to entrust oneself to God in the city of violence. Faithful endurance is the lesson.
- **The Brueggemann lament-as-protest position:** The psalm's protest *includes* the desire for flight as a legitimate part of grief. To suppress the flight-desire in the name of "faithful endurance" is to make the church incapable of honest lament. The dove-wings fantasy must be voiced before the worshiper can faithfully stay.
- **The Zornberg / Jewish psychological position:** The wish for flight is not opposed to faith but constitutive of the full human response to betrayal. The psalm holds desire-to-flee and decision-to-stay together; one without the other is psychologically truncated. Healthy faith does not erase the urge to escape but contains it within a wider trust.

Steelman of the Brueggemann/Zornberg view: A spirituality that requires immediate "faithful endurance" without permission to feel the longing for escape produces brittle Christians who break under sustained relational pain. The psalm's wisdom is precisely that it gives voice to the longing in vv. 6–8 *before* the trust of v. 22. To skip the lament is to forfeit the trust. Pastoral ministry that skips Ps 55:6–8 and lands directly on 1 Peter 5:7 produces shallow comfort that does not hold.

**Tension 2: The imprecation against the betraying friend.** Verses 15 ("let death steal over them; let them go down to Sheol alive") and 23 ("you, O God, will cast them down into the pit of destruction; men of blood and treachery shall not live out half their days") are among the harshest imprecations in the Psalter. The betrayer is asked to die — and to die early.

- **The Lewis / Augustinian position:** The imprecation is not a model for our prayers but an honest expression of feeling that we must spiritualize. The "betrayer" becomes the spiritual betrayer in our own souls; the prayer becomes a prayer against sin itself.
- **The Brueggemann / Zenger position:** The imprecation is a prayer that renounces personal vengeance. By asking *God* to cast the betrayer into the pit, the worshiper refuses to do it himself. The act of praying the curse is the act of *not* enacting it.
- **The pacifist Christian critique:** The imprecation is not a prayer Christians who follow Jesus' "love your enemies" can authentically pray. It must be read as ancient feeling that the gospel transcends.
- **The historical-realist position:** The imprecation makes sense in a world where actual betrayers operate without legal accountability. The psalm gives liturgical voice to victims who have no other recourse. Removing it from the Christian Bible would silence the betrayed.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 55 is the prayer of a person whose closest companion has become the source of greatest pain. The psalm's gift is fourfold: it names the betrayal precisely (vv. 12–14, 20–21), it permits the desire to escape (vv. 6–8), it sustains the discipline of prayer at fixed hours (v. 17), and it commits the burden to God (v. 22). The famous "cast your burden on the LORD" arrives only at v. 22 — after twenty-one verses of complaint, lament, escape-fantasy, and imprecation. The Petrine appropriation in 1 Pet 5:7 is true, but the path to it runs through the rest of the psalm.

The closing question: When the friend who walked with you to God's house turns against you, does your faith have language for the rage, the grief, the desire to flee, and the entrusting of judgment to God — or does it only have language for the entrusting? The psalm refuses to let trust be cheap.

## The Thread

- **2 Samuel 15–17:** The Absalom rebellion, in which David's counselor Ahithophel betrays the king and dies by hanging — the historical narrative most often associated with Ps 55's betraying friend.
- **Psalm 41:9:** "He who ate my bread has lifted his heel against me" — the parallel betrayed-friend lament, cited in John 13:18 of Judas at the Last Supper.
- **1 Peter 5:7:** "Casting all your anxieties on him" — the New Testament's direct appropriation of Ps 55:22.
- **Matthew 26:47–50 and 27:3–5:** Judas's betrayal kiss and his death by hanging — the Christian typological pairing with Ahithophel that Augustine and the patristic tradition develop.
