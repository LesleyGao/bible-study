# Psalm 55 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

The superscription reads *lamnatzeach binginot maskil le-David* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ בִּנְגִינֹת מַשְׂכִּיל לְדָוִד), "to the choirmaster: with stringed instruments. A *Maskil* of David." The term *maskil* (מַשְׂכִּיל) appears thirteen times in the Psalter and probably designates a "contemplative" or didactic poem — Hossfeld and Zenger render it "instructive song," while older scholarship (Mowinckel) suggested a category of skilled liturgical performance. The musical direction *binginot* indicates accompaniment by stringed instruments, situating the psalm in temple liturgy. Psalm 55 sits in Book II of the Psalter (Pss 42–72), the so-called **Elohistic Psalter**, in which the divine name *YHWH* (יהוה) is systematically replaced by *Elohim* (אֱלֹהִים) — a redactional layer whose origins remain debated (a northern collection? a deliberate theological choice toward the universal "God" rather than the covenant name?). Psalm 55 partly resists this pattern: *YHWH* still appears in v. 16 and v. 22, suggesting either incomplete redaction or theological emphasis at climactic moments.

Form-critically, Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 55 as an **individual lament** (*Klagelied des Einzelnen*) with elements of the imprecatory tradition. The psalm contains nearly all the classic lament features Gunkel catalogued in his *Einleitung in die Psalmen* (1933): invocation (vv. 1–2), complaint (vv. 2–8, 9–11), description of enemies (vv. 3, 9–11, 20–21), petition for vengeance (vv. 9, 15, 23), confession of trust (vv. 16–19, 22), and vow of trust (v. 23b). Sigmund Mowinckel, in *The Psalms in Israel's Worship*, would have read it cultically — perhaps a temple petition during personal crisis, possibly tied to the institution of the "I-psalms" of the king. Westermann distinguishes between *Klage* and *Bitte* (complaint and petition), and Psalm 55 oscillates between the two with unusual psychological intensity.

Dating is contested. The traditional Davidic ascription points to the Absalom-Ahithophel betrayal of 2 Sam 15–17, where David's trusted counselor Ahithophel defects. This identification is ancient — the rabbis of *Midrash Tehillim* read the psalm through Ahithophel, and Ibn Ezra (12th c.) and Radak (David Kimhi, 13th c.) both connect vv. 12–14 to that episode. Modern critical scholarship (Kraus, Goldingay, Tate in the WBC commentary) generally places the psalm in the late monarchic or early post-exilic period, noting the urban "city" imagery of vv. 9–11 (violence in the marketplace, walls, oppression) which feels closer to a settled Jerusalem than nomadic warfare.

The Hebrew terminology repays attention. *Sod* (סוֹד), in v. 14 — *iti himtikenu sod* (אִתִּי הִמְתִּיקְנוּ סוֹד, "we used to take sweet counsel together") — denotes intimate, confidential council, the same word used for the divine council in Jer 23:18. The friend was not merely an ally but a participant in the speaker's inner circle. *Yonah* (יוֹנָה, "dove," v. 6) carries Ancient Near Eastern resonance: in the Gilgamesh Epic (Tablet XI) Utnapishtim releases a dove from the ark; doves were sacred to Ishtar and to the Israelite imagination signaled both vulnerability and longing for shelter. *Sheol* (שְׁאוֹל, v. 15) is the underworld of Hebrew imagination, comparable to Akkadian *erṣetu lā târi* ("the land of no return"). The famous v. 22 — *hashlekh al-YHWH yehavkha* (הַשְׁלֵךְ עַל־יְהוָה יְהָבְךָ, "cast your burden on the LORD") — uses the rare noun *yehav* (יְהָב), occurring only here in the Hebrew Bible, probably an Aramaism meaning "what is given" or "lot/portion." Targumic and rabbinic translations rendered it as "burden" (*massa*), and this reading enters Christian use through the LXX *merimna* ("anxiety") and 1 Pet 5:7.

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of Psalm 55 in 4QPsa and 11QPsa, and the textual differences are minor — a few orthographic variants, no significant theological divergence from the Masoretic text. The Septuagint (LXX, Psalm 54 in its numbering) translates *yehav* as *merimna* (μέριμνα, "care/anxiety"), the Greek that 1 Peter 5:7 will pick up.

## Lens 2: The Literary Architecture

Robert Lowth's eighteenth-century discovery of biblical parallelism — synonymous, antithetic, synthetic — finds rich material in Psalm 55. The lament's opening pleads in synonymous parallelism: "Give ear to my prayer, O God, / and hide not yourself from my plea for mercy" (v. 1). The terror sequence in vv. 4–5 stacks synonymous lines — anguish, terror, fear, trembling, horror — building emotional crescendo. The dove-flight passage (vv. 6–8) uses synthetic parallelism, each line advancing the wish: "I would fly away," "I would wander far," "I would lodge in the wilderness," "I would hurry to find shelter."

The strophic structure follows the *selah* markers (vv. 7, 19) and shifts in voice. Hossfeld-Zenger and Goldingay propose roughly three movements: vv. 1–8 (personal anguish and longing for escape), vv. 9–15 (city violence and the friend's betrayal), and vv. 16–23 (trust in God, with the imprecatory turn at v. 23). Within these, the central passage on the friend (vv. 12–14) functions as the emotional core — the "wound from a friend" passage that gives Psalm 55 its distinctive force.

There is a clear inclusio between v. 1's plea ("Give ear to my prayer") and v. 19's ("God will give ear and humble them") — the same Hebrew verb *azan* (אָזַן, "give ear") frames the petition section. The contrast between the friend's sweet speech and hostile heart in v. 21 — "His speech was smooth as butter, yet war was in his heart; / his words were softer than oil, yet they were drawn swords" — uses an antithetic-synonymous chiastic structure: smooth/butter // softer/oil contrasted with war // drawn swords.

The psalm's signature literary device is **emotional whiplash**. The speaker oscillates between confessions of trust and outbreaks of imprecation, between a wish for dove-wings (v. 6) and a wish that his enemies descend living into Sheol (v. 15). This is not poor composition; it is the actual texture of grief. Walter Brueggemann's category of "psalms of disorientation" is paradigmatic here — Psalm 55 refuses to resolve its tensions for the reader's comfort.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Within Book II of the Psalter, Psalm 55 stands just before the **Davidic *miktam* cluster** (Pss 56–60) and shares lament form with surrounding psalms (54, 56, 57). Hossfeld-Zenger note that Pss 52–55 form a "betrayal subgroup" — psalms preoccupied with deceitful speech, treacherous friends, and the violence of the tongue.

In Jewish reception, the *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 55 reads the betrayal as Ahithophel's defection to Absalom; the rabbis dwelled on the tragedy of intimate treachery and on the moral dimension of speech. Rashi connects vv. 12–14 explicitly to Ahithophel and notes that the verse "we used to take sweet counsel together" describes the Sanhedrin-style legal consultation. Ibn Ezra preserves a more philosophical reading, focusing on the universal human experience of broken trust. Radak interprets the dove-wings as longing for the Spirit. The Talmud (b. Berakhot 7b) cites Psalm 55:23 ("men of blood and treachery shall not live out half their days") as a teaching about divine retribution. Modern Jewish readers — Buber, Heschel, and especially Levenson and Sarna — have been particularly drawn to the psalm's portrait of inner anguish and to the Hebraic refusal to suppress rage in prayer. Heschel's *The Prophets* and Brueggemann (a Christian reader deeply formed by Jewish exegesis) both see in lament a "speech of dignity" that takes God seriously enough to protest. Liturgically, Ps 55:23 is recited in Jewish daily worship as part of the *tachanun* prayers of supplication, and v. 18 ("he redeems my soul in safety") is a common text for personal prayer in trouble.

Christian reception has been even more layered. **Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos*** reads Psalm 55 christologically: the betraying friend is **Judas**, whose intimate participation in the meal and kiss ("a man my equal, my companion, my familiar friend... we used to take sweet counsel together") prefigures the dominical betrayal. Augustine's reading became canonical for the Latin tradition. **Athanasius**, in his *Letter to Marcellinus*, lists Psalm 55 as appropriate for "when enemies surround you and your friend has betrayed you." **Cassiodorus** in the sixth century read the dove as the Holy Spirit and the wish for flight as the soul's longing for contemplation. **Aquinas** in his *Postilla super Psalmos* developed the Judas reading further, distinguishing the friend's external profession from his interior treachery — relevant to the medieval question of intention and betrayal. **Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos*** (1519–1521) read Psalm 55 through the experience of *Anfechtung* (spiritual struggle), and his lectures at Wittenberg connected v. 22 — "cast your burden on the Lord" — to the doctrine of justification by faith. **Calvin's 1557 Psalms commentary** is more cautious; he reads Psalm 55 through David's experience but resists pure typology, emphasizing the moral lessons of trust and the wisdom of bringing griefs to God.

Modern critical scholarship has refined the form-critical work. **Gunkel** classified the psalm; **Mowinckel** placed it in the cult; **Westermann** emphasized the structural movement from plea to praise; **Kraus** in his BKAT commentary stressed the psalm's juridical dimension (the imprecation as appeal to divine justice); **Hossfeld-Zenger** in *Psalms 2* (Hermeneia) provide the most thorough recent treatment, reading the psalm as a "literary composition" rather than a single occasional poem. **Goldingay** (BCOTWP) stresses the psalm's prayerful realism. **Tate** (WBC) develops the urban-violence reading. **Brueggemann's** *The Message of the Psalms* and *The Costly Loss of Lament* placed Psalm 55 at the center of his argument that the church has impoverished itself by suppressing lament. **Patrick Miller's** *They Cried to the Lord* and **William Brown's** *Seeing the Psalms* and **Beth Tanner's** work in the NICOT volume all reflect the post-Brueggemann recovery of lament as legitimate Christian speech. **Wenham's** *The Psalter Reclaimed* engages the imprecation problem honestly, refusing the older liberal move of simply excising vv. 9, 15, and 23.

In the New Testament, **1 Peter 5:7** — "casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you" — quotes Ps 55:22 directly, picking up the LXX *merimna* (anxiety/care). It is the chief NT use of Psalm 55, and it shapes Christian devotional life: from Spurgeon's sermons to contemporary pastoral counseling, "cast your burden on the Lord" has become shorthand for spiritual surrender. The Judas typology — though never explicit in the Gospels — has shaped passion-narrative imagination ever since Augustine.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent and Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: The imprecation problem.** Psalm 55:15 prays, "Let death steal over them; let them go down to Sheol alive." Verse 23 prays for the death of "men of blood and treachery." Christian readers have struggled with this for two millennia. **Position A** (the Augustinian-allegorical move): the enemies are sins, demons, or unbelief — to be destroyed spiritually, not literally. **Position B** (the dispensational move, or older liberal excision): these are pre-Christian sentiments superseded by "love your enemies" (Matt 5:44); Lewis in *Reflections on the Psalms* moved partly in this direction, calling the imprecations "devilish." **Position C** (Brueggemann, Wenham, Miller, Tanner): the imprecations are honest speech to God, not endorsements of violence. They hand the rage *to* God instead of acting it out, and they refuse the false piety that pretends not to feel rage. Steelmanning Position B: there is real continuity with the way Christ refuses retributive prayer at the cross ("Father, forgive them"). Steelmanning Position C: the imprecations refuse pious dissimulation; they take seriously that violence done to one person is violence God should care about.

**Tension 2: The "if only I had wings like a dove" wish.** The desire for retreat raises a perennial question: when is flight from suffering faithful, and when is it betrayal of vocation? **Position A** (Catholic monastic tradition, *fuga mundi*): withdrawal can be a form of pursuit — desert fathers, contemplative orders, the life of prayer is a legitimate and even higher form of engagement. **Position B** (Bonhoeffer's *Ethics* and *The Cost of Discipleship*): the Christian is called to remain in the world, to share its suffering; the wish for dove-wings can be cowardice dressed as piety. **Position C** (Anabaptist withdrawal-vs-engagement): the gathered community of disciples must separate from the violence of the world, but this is a corporate not individual flight. Steelmanning all three: each reads the same impulse — the longing for shelter from "raging wind and tempest" — through different ecclesial logics. The psalm itself does not resolve the question; it expresses the wish, then returns to "I call to God" (v. 16) and to costly, ongoing lament. The pattern matters: the wish is voiced and released, not enacted.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 55 is the prayer of someone who has been betrayed by an intimate, who feels the city is full of violence, who wishes to disappear, and who nevertheless brings all of this — including the rage — to God. It refuses both the pietism that sanitizes prayer and the secularism that sees no point in praying when betrayed. The psalm is a theological permission slip: God can be told everything. The Christian reading that hears Judas in v. 13 does not soften the psalm; it intensifies it. Christ himself prays this kind of psalm. The pattern Bonhoeffer found in the Psalter — that the prayers of Israel become the prayers of Christ become the prayers of the church — applies here with particular force.

**Closing question:** When the betrayal comes from someone with whom you "took sweet counsel" inside "God's house" — when the wound is from inside the body — is the proper response to flee, to fight, or to pray Psalm 55, where the speaker does neither and does both, and lets God hold the unresolved?

## The Thread

- Psalm 41:9 — "Even my close friend in whom I trusted, who ate my bread, has lifted his heel against me" (also applied to Judas in John 13:18). The "intimate-betrayer" theme runs through the Psalter and into the passion narrative.
- 2 Samuel 15:31 — Ahithophel's defection: "And it was told David, 'Ahithophel is among the conspirators with Absalom.' And David said, 'O LORD, please turn the counsel of Ahithophel into foolishness.'" The traditional historical setting of Psalm 55.
- 1 Peter 5:7 — "casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you." Direct quotation of Psalm 55:22 in the New Testament; the verse that has carried Psalm 55 into Christian devotional vocabulary.
- Matthew 26:49–50 — Judas's kiss: "And he came up to Jesus at once and said, 'Greetings, Rabbi!' And he kissed him. Jesus said to him, 'Friend, do what you came to do.'" The Augustinian reading hears Psalm 55 here.
