# Psalm 56 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

The superscription is unusually detailed: *lamnatzeach al-yonat elem rechoqim le-David miktam be-echoz oto Pelishtim be-Gat* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ עַל־יוֹנַת אֵלֶם רְחֹקִים לְדָוִד מִכְתָּם בֶּאֱחֹז אוֹתוֹ פְלִשְׁתִּים בְּגַת), "to the choirmaster: according to *The Dove on Far-off Terebinths*. A *Miktam* of David, when the Philistines seized him in Gath." Each element repays attention.

*Al-yonat elem rechoqim* (עַל־יוֹנַת אֵלֶם רְחֹקִים) — "according to *The Dove of the Distant Silence*" or "the Dove on Far-off Terebinths" — is almost certainly a melody-name, identifying the tune to which Psalm 56 was sung in the temple. The phrase has puzzled translators for two millennia. The LXX read *elem* (אֵלֶם) as a noun related to silence ("the silent dove"), the Vulgate as "the people far removed," while modern critical scholars (Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay) accept the melody-name reading. The Targum, characteristically, allegorized the whole phrase as a reference to Israel scattered among the nations.

*Miktam* (מִכְתָּם) is the genre marker for a tight cluster: Psalms 16, 56, 57, 58, 59, 60. The five psalms of Pss 56–60 form the **Davidic *miktam* cluster**, all assigned to specific narrative episodes in David's flight from Saul. The etymology of *miktam* is uncertain — possibly from *katam* (כָּתַם, "to inscribe"; hence "inscription" or "epigram"), or from an Akkadian cognate suggesting "covering" or "atonement." Some rabbinic traditions read it as a play on "gold" (*ketem*, כֶּתֶם) — a "golden psalm." Modern scholarship leaves the term obscure but observes that the *miktam* psalms share a tone of trust under threat.

The historical superscription anchors the psalm to **1 Samuel 21:10–15**, the Achish-of-Gath episode: David, fleeing Saul, takes refuge in the Philistine city of Gath, is recognized as the slayer of their giant, and feigns madness — "scratching on the doors of the gate and letting his spittle run down his beard" — to escape. Strikingly, **Psalm 34's superscription** assigns that same episode to a different psalm. This either suggests the two psalms were paired in some early collection or, more likely, that the superscriptions are editorial assignments — later interpretive headings that read narrative situations into existing poems. The historical-superscription convention is itself a kind of early canonical interpretation.

Psalm 56 sits in **Book II of the Psalter** (Pss 42–72), the **Elohistic Psalter**, where *YHWH* is largely replaced by *Elohim*. Psalm 56 mostly follows the pattern (*Elohim* dominates), but v. 10 strikingly preserves both names side by side: "in God I praise his word, in *YHWH* I praise his word" — a potentially deliberate liturgical effect.

Form-critically, **Hermann Gunkel** classified Psalm 56 as an **individual lament** (*Klagelied des Einzelnen*) with strong elements of the *Vertrauenspsalm* (psalm of trust). Mowinckel's cultic reading would have located the psalm in temple petition; Westermann's structural analysis identifies movement from plea (vv. 1–2) through petition (vv. 7) to confidence and praise (vv. 9–13). Gunkel's category is sound, but Hossfeld-Zenger and Goldingay note the psalm's distinctive feature: a **refrain** structure (vv. 4 and 10–11) that gives the trust-statement liturgical weight.

The Hebrew terminology is rich. *Be-Elohim batachti lo ira* (בֵּאלֹהִים בָּטַחְתִּי לֹא אִירָא, "in God I trust, I shall not be afraid," vv. 4, 11) — the verb *batach* (בָּטַח) is the foundational Hebrew word for trust, used both for the false trust of idolaters (Hab 2:18) and the true trust of the faithful. The negation *lo ira* (לֹא אִירָא, "I shall not fear") is the same construction used in the Aaronic priestly assurance and in the Psalter's most famous line (Ps 23:4).

The signature image of v. 8 is breathtaking: *no'di safarta atta simah dimati be-no'dekha ha-lo be-sifratekha* (נֹדִי סָפַרְתָּה אַתָּה שִׂימָה דִמְעָתִי בְנֹאדֶךָ הֲלֹא בְּסִפְרָתֶךָ), "you have kept count of my tossings; put my tears in your bottle. Are they not in your book?" The wordplay between *nod* (נוֹד, "wandering/tossing") and *no'd* (נֹאד, "leather wineskin/bottle") is exquisite Hebrew punning. The *no'd* was the standard ancient Near Eastern container for liquids — wine, milk, water — made from a whole goat or sheep skin sewn shut. Mesopotamian and Egyptian iconography preserve images of such bottles. The image is of God collecting human tears as one would collect precious wine. The parallel image — *sifratekha* (סִפְרָתֶךָ, "your book") — points to the heavenly ledger tradition (cf. Mal 3:16; Dan 7:10), where God keeps an accounting of the suffering of the righteous.

**Dead Sea Scrolls** preserve fragments of Psalm 56 in 4QPsa, with no significant variants. The **LXX** (Psalm 55 in Greek numbering) translates the dove-of-distant-silence as *huper tou laou tou apo tōn hagiōn memakrymmenou* — "concerning the people removed far from the holy ones" — an interpretive translation that already begins the allegorizing tradition.

## Lens 2: The Literary Architecture

Robert Lowth's parallelism categories illuminate Psalm 56 beautifully. The opening (vv. 1–2) uses synonymous parallelism with intensification: "Be gracious to me, O God, for man tramples on me; / all day long an attacker oppresses me; // my enemies trample on me all day long, / for many attack me proudly." The repetition of *kol-hayom* (כָּל־הַיּוֹם, "all day long") in vv. 1, 2, and 5 forms a triple insistence on the unrelenting nature of the threat.

The psalm's strophic structure is organized around its **refrain** (vv. 3–4 and vv. 10–11): "In God, whose word I praise, in God I trust; I shall not be afraid. / What can flesh/man do to me?" The refrain's slight variation — "what can *flesh* (*basar*, בָּשָׂר) do to me?" in v. 4 and "what can *man* (*adam*, אָדָם) do to me?" in v. 11 — is a deliberate poetic choice. *Basar* emphasizes physical frailty (mere meat); *adam* emphasizes creatureliness (mere mortal). Both are belittling terms over against *Elohim*.

The structure breaks naturally into three sections marked by refrain placement: vv. 1–4 (complaint and trust-affirmation), vv. 5–9 (renewed complaint and tear-bottle petition), vv. 10–13 (refrain returns and resolves into thank-offering). This gives the psalm an A-B-A' shape, with the central panel containing the most distinctive imagery (the tear-bottle, vv. 7–9).

The inclusio between the opening "be gracious to me" (*chonneni*, חָנֵּנִי, v. 1) and the closing "you have delivered my soul from death" (v. 13) frames the petition with its answer. Verse 13's phrase *bedor ha-chayim* (literally "in the light of the living") — here translated "in the light of life" — recalls Job 33:30 and looks forward to the Johannine "light of life" (John 8:12).

The chiasm at the heart of the trust-affirmation is striking:
- v. 4a: *be-Elohim* — in God I praise his word
- v. 4b: *be-Elohim batachti* — in God I trust; I will not fear
- v. 10a: *be-Elohim* — in God I praise his word
- v. 10b: *ba-YHWH* — in YHWH I praise his word
- v. 11a: *be-Elohim batachti* — in God I trust; I will not fear

The structure circles back, but with an addition: v. 10 introduces *YHWH* alongside *Elohim*, breaking the Elohistic-Psalter convention at the moment of deepest trust.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Psalm 56 stands as the first of the **Davidic *miktam* cluster** (Pss 56–60), all of which carry historical superscriptions tied to David's flight from Saul. The cluster forms a sub-collection within Book II, perhaps once an independent booklet of "trust-under-threat" psalms. Hossfeld-Zenger note the literary unity: shared *miktam* designation, shared narrative-superscription convention, shared theme of trust amid persecution.

In Jewish reception, the *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 56 develops the dove-image extensively, identifying Israel with the dove and the Philistines with predators surrounding her. **Rashi** glosses *yonat elem rechoqim* as referring to the community of Israel exiled and silent, anticipating restoration. **Ibn Ezra** is more philological, suggesting either a melody-name or a poetic self-designation by David. **Radak** (David Kimhi) connects the tear-bottle image to God's careful accounting of righteous suffering — a theme important in medieval Jewish theodicy after the First Crusade. The Talmud (b. Shabbat 105b) cites the tear-bottle image to teach that God values tears of repentance. **Buber** and **Heschel** read Psalm 56 as paradigmatic of the Hebraic God — a God who counts tears, not a remote philosophical deity. **Levenson** in *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* and **Sarna** in *Songs of the Heart* both stress the psalm's portrait of divine attentiveness to particular suffering. Liturgically, Ps 56:9 ("This I know, that God is for me") appears in personal-petition contexts in modern Jewish worship.

Christian reception has read Psalm 56 through multiple lenses. **Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos*** reads the psalm christologically — David in flight prefigures Christ in his passion, "trampled on" by his persecutors. The dove-of-distant-silence Augustine reads as the Holy Spirit, hovering over the suffering Christ. **Athanasius** in his *Letter to Marcellinus* assigns Psalm 56 to those "in great fear of enemies." **Cassiodorus** reads the tear-bottle image typologically as the church's preservation of the saints' suffering. **Aquinas** in his *Postilla super Psalmos* engages the question of how a creature can be "for God" and notes the verse "God is for me" (v. 9) as a theological statement of grace. **Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos*** treats Psalm 56 in the context of *Anfechtung* — the spiritual struggle of the believer who fears yet trusts. The famous line *be-Elohim batachti* — "in God I trust" — shaped Luther's theology of *fiducia* (trusting faith). **Calvin's 1557 Psalms commentary** reads Psalm 56 as a model for the believer's psychology: fear and faith coexist; the proper response to fear is not its absence but its placement under God's care. Calvin's reading has shaped the Reformed pastoral tradition ever since.

Modern critical scholarship — **Gunkel, Mowinckel, Westermann, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate** — has refined the form-critical and structural analysis. **Brueggemann's** *The Message of the Psalms* treats Psalm 56 as a "psalm of new orientation" — moving through trouble to a settled trust. **Patrick Miller's** *They Cried to the Lord* and **William Brown's** *Seeing the Psalms* note the visual power of the tear-bottle. **Beth Tanner's** NICOT volume on Pss 1–72 (with deClaisse-Walford and Jacobson) provides the most accessible recent treatment. **Wenham's** *The Psalter Reclaimed* engages the psalm pastorally.

In Christian devotional reception, **Charles Spurgeon's** *Treasury of David* devotes substantial attention to v. 8 — "put my tears in your bottle" — calling it "one of the tenderest verses in the Psalter." Spurgeon's sermons on Psalm 56 became a fixed point in evangelical devotional vocabulary. The image has parallels in **Revelation 6:9–11** — the souls under the altar crying "how long?" and waiting until the full number is gathered — suggesting an ongoing biblical theme of God's accounting of the suffering of his people. The verse has provided comfort for centuries to those who feel their pain is unseen: God's bottle, God's book.

In the New Testament, Psalm 56 is not directly quoted, but **Hebrews 13:6** picks up the theme — "The Lord is my helper; I will not fear; what can man do to me?" — likely drawing on the Psalter's broader trust tradition (Ps 118 most directly, but Ps 56's refrain is in the same register).

## Lens 4: Theological Intent and Honest Tensions

**Tension 1: The trust-amid-fear paradox.** The refrain says "When I am afraid, I put my trust in you" (v. 3) and immediately "in God I trust; I shall not be afraid" (v. 4). Is the psalmist afraid or not? **Position A** (Calvin, much of the Reformed tradition): trust does not eliminate fear; it places fear under God. The two co-exist; faith is what we do *with* fear, not its replacement. **Position B** (some Pentecostal and prosperity-tradition readings): genuine faith expels fear; v. 4's "I shall not fear" is the goal-state of mature trust, and v. 3's "when I am afraid" is a beginner's condition to be outgrown. **Position C** (Brueggemann, contemporary lament theology): the oscillation is the point. The psalm refuses to flatten the believer's psychology; it represents fear and trust as a single dynamic motion, not stages on a ladder. Steelmanning each: Position A respects the realism of the text; Position B respects the eschatological pull toward "perfect love casts out fear" (1 John 4:18); Position C respects the texture of how prayer actually works. The psalm itself supports Position A and C; Position B reads against the text's plain repetitive structure.

**Tension 2: The unity-of-the-psalm question.** Does the historical superscription (Achish in Gath) match the content of Psalm 56? **Position A** (traditional, including most pre-critical readers and contemporary canonical readers): yes — David in the Philistine city, surrounded by enemies who know him as a giant-killer, fearing for his life, prays this psalm; it fits the situation. **Position B** (much critical scholarship, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger): the superscription is a later editorial assignment; the psalm's "city of violence" and "marketplace fraud" imagery (which actually fits Psalm 55 more than 56) and the generic "all day long they trample on me" do not specifically describe Gath. The historical heading is interpretive midrash. **Position C** (canonical-criticism, Childs, more recent Wenham): even if the superscription is editorial, it has become canonical and shapes interpretation; the question "what does this psalm mean as the prayer of David in Gath?" is theologically valid even if not historically primary. Steelmanning: Position B is honest about the editorial layer; Position C is honest about how the canonical text actually functions for communities of faith.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 56 is the prayer of someone surrounded — "all day long" — by hostility, who names fear honestly and places trust over it. The tear-bottle image is not sentimental; it is a theological claim that no suffering is unseen, no tear unrecorded. For Christians reading the psalm in the line of Christ — who himself fled, hid, prayed, sweated blood, and trusted — Psalm 56 belongs to the family of psalms the Suffering Servant prays. Its refrain ("what can man do to me?") is not bravado but liturgical defiance — a deliberate diminishment of the threat in light of the One who counts tears.

**Closing question:** What does it mean to live as if your tears were collected in a bottle and recorded in a book — that your unseen sufferings are *seen* by the only One whose seeing matters?

## The Thread

- 1 Samuel 21:10–15 — the Achish-of-Gath narrative referenced by the superscription. David feigns madness in the city of his enemies, escapes by his wits and (the psalm claims) by God's hand.
- Psalm 34 — its superscription names the same Achish-Gath episode but tells the story of deliverance with confidence already settled. Psalm 56 is the prayer-during; Psalm 34 is the praise-after.
- Hebrews 13:6 — "The Lord is my helper; I will not fear; what can man do to me?" — the refrain of Psalm 56 transposed into Christian liturgical confidence.
- Revelation 6:9–11 — the souls under the altar crying out, awaiting God's accounting. The tear-bottle image expanded into apocalyptic vision: God remembers, God counts, God will act.
