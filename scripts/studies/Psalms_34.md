# Psalm 34 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

The superscription is unusually narrative: "Of David, when he changed his behavior before Abimelech, so that he drove him out, and he went away" (*le-David, beshannoto et-ta'mo lifnei Avimelech va-yegarsheihu va-yelach*, לְדָוִד בְּשַׁנּוֹתוֹ אֶת־טַעְמוֹ לִפְנֵי אֲבִימֶלֶךְ וַיְגָרְשֵׁהוּ וַיֵּלַךְ). This places the psalm in the narrative of 1 Samuel 21:10–15, where David, fleeing from Saul, takes refuge with the Philistine king of Gath, and when recognized as the slayer of Goliath, feigns madness — drooling, scratching the gates, behaving as a lunatic — to escape.

A famous **harmonization problem** sits in the superscription. In 1 Samuel 21, the king of Gath is named **Achish**, but the psalm's superscription names **Abimelech**. The mainstream scholarly answer (going back to medieval Jewish commentators and confirmed by ANE comparative work) is that *Abimelech* — meaning "my father is king" — was a **Philistine royal title**, like *Pharaoh* in Egypt or *Caesar* in Rome. The personal name was Achish; the royal title was Abimelech. The same usage appears in Genesis 20 and 26, where the kings of Gerar in different generations are both called "Abimelech." The psalm's superscription uses the formal title; the Samuel narrative uses the personal name. This is the consensus reading among Rashi, Radak, and most modern commentators (Goldingay, Tate, Hossfeld-Zenger).

Form-critically, Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 34 as a *Danklied des Einzelnen* — an **individual thanksgiving psalm** — with strong **wisdom** elements (vv. 11–22). Like Psalm 32, it is a thanksgiving that has crossed over into didactic teaching. Westermann's category of "declarative thanksgiving" (the speaker declares what God has done) fits the first half (vv. 1–10), while the second half (vv. 11–22) shifts into wisdom-instruction (*Lehrgedicht*). The poem moves: praise → testimony → invitation to taste → instruction to children → wisdom contrast.

The most famous Hebrew phrase in the psalm is at v. 8: *ta'amu u-r'u ki-tov Yahweh* (טַעֲמוּ וּרְאוּ כִּי־טוֹב יְהוָה, "taste and see that the LORD is good"). The verb *ta'am* (טָעַם) means literally to taste with the tongue — sensory experience, not abstract evaluation. The same root appears in the superscription's *ta'mo* ("his behavior/sense") — David **changed his sense**, the psalm calls others to **taste and see**. The wordplay is deliberate: the speaker who pretended his senses were broken now invites the hearers to use theirs.

Psalm 34 is an **alphabetic acrostic** in Hebrew. Each verse begins with a successive letter of the Hebrew alphabet — but with two well-known irregularities. First, the **vav line is missing**: there is no verse beginning with *vav*. Second, after the letter *taw* (which would normally end the acrostic), the psalm has an additional verse beginning with *pe* (v. 22), creating a deliberate **second pe-line**. This second *pe* (פ) at the close also appears in Psalms 25, the other major partial acrostic in Book I — strong evidence that this is a deliberate compositional pattern, not a copyist error. Adele Berlin, in her work on biblical poetics, has shown that the closing additional *pe*-line forms an acrostic spelling *aleph-lamed-pe* (אלף, the first letter, hidden across the structure), which can be read as alluding to *aluph* ("teacher/leader") or to the alphabet itself — a signature of completeness embedded in apparent incompleteness.

The DSS preserves Psalm 34 in 4QPs-a and 4QPs-q, with the acrostic intact and the missing *vav* line confirmed in the earliest manuscripts — meaning the gap is **original**, not a transmission error. The Septuagint numbers it Ps 33 and translates the famous v. 8 phrase as *geusasthe kai idete hoti chrēstos ho Kyrios*, the form Peter will quote in 1 Peter 2:3.

ANE parallels for the genre include Mesopotamian thanksgiving prayers and Hittite votive texts, but the alphabetic acrostic is a distinctly Hebrew formal device — used to signal that the praise covers the totality (A to Z) of what can be said.

## Lens 2: The Literature

The acrostic structure governs everything. The 22-line acrostic (with the *vav*-gap and the appended *pe*-line) is the formal architecture, and the meaning runs along it. The literary effect is **comprehensiveness with one gap**: the psalm covers the alphabet but leaves a hole.

Why is the *vav* missing? The scholarly proposals are several: (1) the original *vav* line was lost in transmission, but the early manuscript evidence makes this less likely; (2) the gap is theologically intentional — perhaps to signal that praise is never quite complete this side of the eschaton; (3) the gap is structural, dividing the psalm into two halves and allowing the additional *pe*-line at the close to balance it; (4) the gap is one of the standard variations within Hebrew acrostic practice, of which more than one example exists. Berlin and Wilfred Watson lean toward (3): the missing *vav* is a structural marker that divides praise (vv. 1–10) from instruction (vv. 11–22) and the appended *pe* at the close echoes the *pe* that opens v. 16 — creating a chiastic balance.

Lowth's parallelism is everywhere. Synonymous in v. 1 ("I will bless the LORD at all times // his praise shall continually be in my mouth"), antithetic in v. 10 ("the young lions suffer want and hunger // but those who seek the LORD lack no good thing"), synthetic in v. 18 ("the LORD is near to the brokenhearted // and saves the crushed in spirit").

The strophic structure tracks the form-critical analysis:

- **Strophe 1 (vv. 1–3):** Opening call to praise; the speaker invites the community to magnify with him.
- **Strophe 2 (vv. 4–7):** Personal testimony — "I sought, he answered."
- **Strophe 3 (vv. 8–10):** The invitation — taste and see; fear the LORD; lack nothing.
- **Strophe 4 (vv. 11–14):** Wisdom instruction — "Come, children, listen."
- **Strophe 5 (vv. 15–18):** The two ways — God's eyes toward the righteous, face against the wicked.
- **Strophe 6 (vv. 19–22):** Closing summary — affliction, deliverance, the unbroken bones, the redemption of servants.

A literary feature worth noting: v. 11 ("Come, O children, listen to me; I will teach you the fear of the LORD") is the formal **wisdom-teacher's call** — the same formula that opens many wisdom lectures in Proverbs (Prov 1:8, 4:1, 5:7, 7:24, 8:32). The psalm has shifted from praise to pedagogy in a single verse, and the rest of the psalm reads as wisdom lecture.

The phrase "not one of them is broken" in v. 20 (*echat me-hennah lo nishbarah*, אַחַת מֵהֵנָּה לֹא נִשְׁבָּרָה) refers in the Hebrew to the bones (*atzmotav*, "all his bones") — God preserves the bones of the righteous. The image is bodily and totalizing: of all the bones of the righteous person, not one will be broken. This is the verse John's Gospel will pick up at the crucifixion.

There is no *Selah* in Psalm 34 — the acrostic itself provides the structural pacing.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**In the canonical Psalter.** Psalm 34 closes the Pss 25–34 sub-collection identified by Hossfeld and Zenger, providing the alphabetic resolution to the refuge-theme that began in Ps 25 (also a partial acrostic). The pairing of Pss 25 and 34 — both partial acrostics with the appended *pe*-line — frames the sub-collection. Pss 32–34 form a tighter triad (Hossfeld-Zenger): personal confession (32), communal hymn (33), wisdom-thanksgiving (34) — three modes of post-deliverance speech.

**The Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Ps 34 takes the Achish-Abimelech harmonization for granted (Abimelech as title) and treats the psalm as David's testimony to the deliverance from Gath. The Talmud (*Berakhot* 61a) cites v. 13 ("keep your tongue from evil") in discussions of speech ethics. Rashi reads the psalm as Davidic and historical; he addresses the missing *vav* by suggesting that the *vav* line was about God's wonders and was suppressed for theological humility (a creative reading without strong textual support). Ibn Ezra notes the acrostic structure carefully and the *vav* gap without speculation. Radak takes the psalm as fundamentally about *bittachon* (trust). Nachmanides on the acrostic emphasizes its didactic completeness.

In modern Jewish thought, Buber on the I-Thou of testimony reads vv. 4–6 as the dialogical core: "I sought, he answered." Heschel on prophetic and wisdom speech treats the psalm as exemplary of how thanksgiving moves into ethical instruction. Levenson on covenantal blessing reads vv. 9–10 cautiously — God's people may yet experience hunger; the language of "no lack" is covenantal hope rather than empirical guarantee. Sarna on biblical poetics treats the acrostic-with-gap as a deliberate signal of imperfect completeness. Zornberg on instruction sees vv. 11–14 as the wisdom voice teaching from one's own deliverance.

In the Jewish liturgy, Psalm 34 is recited in full on **Shabbat morning** in the Ashkenazi *Pesukei DeZimra* and on festival days. The tradition treats it as one of the great teaching psalms.

**The Christian trajectory.** Augustine in his *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Ps 34 spends extensive time on v. 8 (*gustate et videte*) and reads it eucharistically — *taste and see* becomes one of the great patristic Eucharistic texts, applied to the bread and the cup. The bishop's invitation at communion in many ancient liturgies draws on this verse. Augustine also reads v. 20 (the unbroken bones) Christologically through John 19:36.

Athanasius's *Letter to Marcellinus* lists Ps 34 among the psalms of thanksgiving and instruction. Cassiodorus reads the psalm as the church's testimony of deliverance and divides it carefully along its acrostic. Aquinas treats v. 8 sacramentally and v. 20 Christologically in his Psalms commentary; he handles the wisdom-instruction in vv. 11–14 as Christian moral catechesis.

Luther on Ps 34 (in his lectures and sermons) emphasizes v. 18 ("the LORD is near to the brokenhearted") as a comfort psalm for sinners under conviction — the brokenhearted are not the proud righteous but those crushed by their sin. Calvin's commentary takes the psalm as Davidic and historical; he handles v. 8 metaphorically (taste = experience) without sacramental development; he treats vv. 11–14 as the heart of the psalm's pedagogy.

Among modern critical interpreters: Gunkel (thanksgiving with wisdom elements), Mowinckel (cultic thanksgiving with instruction), Westermann (declarative thanksgiving moving to teaching), Hans-Joachim Kraus (acrostic thanksgiving, post-exilic instructional), Hossfeld and Zenger (concluding the 25–34 sub-collection; deliberate acrostic-with-gap), John Goldingay (thanksgiving with strong wisdom shape), Marvin Tate, Gordon Wenham (canonical-ethical reading: the wisdom instruction in vv. 11–14 as the psalm's center). Walter Brueggemann puts Ps 34 in "psalms of new orientation" — testimony from the other side of distress. Patrick D. Miller on prayer and pedagogy. William P. Brown on the metaphor cluster of taste and sight. Beth Tanner reads the psalm pastorally for testimony in congregational settings.

**NT use — extensive.** Psalm 34 is one of the more frequently cited psalms in the New Testament:

- **1 Peter 2:3** quotes Ps 34:8: *if indeed you have tasted that the Lord is good* (*ei egeusasthe hoti chrēstos ho Kyrios*). Peter applies the psalm to the experience of those who have come to Christ.
- **1 Peter 3:10–12** quotes Ps 34:12–16 at length — the wisdom instruction about keeping the tongue and pursuing peace becomes the apostolic ethical teaching for suffering Christians.
- **John 19:36** applies Ps 34:20 ("not one of his bones is broken") to the crucifixion: when the Roman soldiers break the legs of the two crucified with Jesus to hasten their death but find Jesus already dead and do not break his legs, John reads the moment as fulfilling Ps 34:20 (alongside the Passover-lamb regulation of Exod 12:46).

The cumulative weight of these citations made Psalm 34 deeply formative in early Christian self-understanding — both for its sacramental and ethical reach (1 Peter) and for its Christological fulfillment (John).

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — The Acrostic, the Bones, and the Tasting

**Tension 1: The acrostic constraint and the urgency of thanksgiving.** Psalm 34 is a thanksgiving for deliverance — one would expect spontaneous, urgent speech. But it is also a 22-line alphabetic acrostic — a highly constrained literary form requiring careful composition. Does the formal constraint serve or hinder the testimony?

- **Position A (Constraint serves):** Adele Berlin, Wilfred Watson, much of modern poetics. The acrostic's completeness signals that the praise is total — A to Z, every letter, nothing left out. The form is theological: the comprehensive deliverance demands comprehensive praise. The discipline of the acrostic also prevents thanksgiving from collapsing into mere effusion; the speaker has thought carefully about what to say. Steelman: every great prayer in the Christian tradition has been formed (the Psalms, the Our Father, the great liturgies). Form is not opposed to sincerity; form is what enables sincerity to be passed on.
- **Position B (Constraint hinders):** Some modern Romantic readings would see the acrostic as artifice covering experience. Steelman: a real cry of deliverance is not 22 careful lines; it is broken speech. The acrostic risks turning testimony into performance.
- **Position C (The gap matters):** The missing *vav* line is the psalm's self-correction. The form is comprehensive but imperfect — there is still a hole. The acrostic invites totality but acknowledges incompleteness. Steelman: praise this side of the eschaton is necessarily structured but necessarily partial. The poem refuses both the Romantic sincerity-vs-form binary and the over-confidence of a completed acrostic.

**Tension 2: The Christological "broken bones" reading and the typological hermeneutic.** John 19:36 reads Psalm 34:20 ("he keeps all his bones; not one of them is broken") as fulfilled in the unbroken legs of Jesus on the cross. But the psalm in its original context is about the **righteous person in general** — not about a single Messianic figure.

- **Position A (Robust Christological / typological reading):** Augustine, Aquinas, classical Christian exegesis, much patristic and medieval tradition. The psalm in its original sense addresses the righteous, but the *true* righteous one is Christ; what is said in general of the righteous is fulfilled in particular in him. John's Gospel shows the Spirit's hermeneutic — the Old Testament is read forward through Christ. Steelman: the unity of the canon and the Christological center of Scripture demand that texts about the righteous find their fullest meaning in Jesus.
- **Position B (Cautious historical-grammatical):** Goldingay, much modern critical commentary. The psalm is a generalized thanksgiving, and "not one of them is broken" is hyperbolic language about God's protection of his people, applied to bones because broken bones in the ANE were often fatal. John's application to Jesus is a Christological reading that the psalm permits but does not predict. Steelman: forcing every righteous-figure text to be a direct Messianic prophecy flattens the psalm's pastoral usefulness for non-Christological readers and disrespects the original Israelite context.
- **Position C (Both/and, canonical):** Brevard Childs, Gordon Wenham, Beth Tanner. The psalm is genuinely about the righteous in general and genuinely fulfilled in Christ as the true Righteous One. John 19:36 is not mistaken exegesis but inspired hermeneutic — the Evangelist sees that the protection-of-bones promised generally has its decisive instance in the body of the crucified and risen Lord, whose unbroken bones at the cross are the down payment on the resurrection of all righteous bodies. Steelman: the psalm and John together teach that what God does in general for the righteous, he has done supremely in Christ, and what he has done in Christ secures the general promise.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 34 is the canonical model of how testimony becomes teaching. The speaker tasted, was delivered, and now invites others to taste and instructs the children in the fear of the LORD. The acrostic structure says: *I will tell you the whole story, A to Z, and even the gap I leave is honest about what I cannot yet say.* The unbroken bones of v. 20 became, in the New Testament, an image of God's protection that culminates at Calvary, where the bones of the Righteous One were not broken because the resurrection of the body had to be prepared in him. The psalm's invitation to *taste and see* became, in the Christian tradition, the bishop's call at the Eucharist — and the deepest meaning of "taste and see" is that experience of God is sensory before it is intellectual, embodied before it is abstract.

**Closing question:** What have you actually tasted of the goodness of the LORD that you could now use to teach the next person? And if you cannot answer, what does that mean for your testimony?

## The Thread

- Psalm 34:8 ("Oh, taste and see that the LORD is good") is quoted in 1 Peter 2:3 and became a central patristic Eucharistic text — Augustine's reading of it in his *Enarrationes* shaped the bishop's invitation at communion across the Western tradition.
- Psalm 34:12–16 is quoted at length in 1 Peter 3:10–12 — the wisdom-instruction about keeping the tongue and pursuing peace becomes apostolic ethical teaching for suffering Christians.
- Psalm 34:20 ("not one of his bones is broken") is applied by John 19:36 to the crucifixion of Jesus, where the Roman soldiers do not break his legs — connecting the psalm to Exod 12:46's Passover-lamb regulation and identifying Jesus as the true Passover.
- The acrostic-with-gap structure (Pss 25, 34, 145) embeds completeness symbolism in Hebrew poetry — total praise A to Z, with one missing letter that reminds the worshiper that this side of the eschaton, the alphabet of praise still has a hole.
