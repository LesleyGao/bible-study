# Psalm 43 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 43 is the shortest of the Korahite psalms — five verses — and the only one in the cluster Pss 42–49 without a superscription. That bare opening is itself the key historical-critical evidence for what most modern critical commentators (Goldingay, Hossfeld–Zenger, Kraus, Tate, Wenham) regard as the most likely scenario: **Pss 42 and 43 are originally a single composition**, divided in the transmission for liturgical reasons but never fully rejoined. The shared refrain (42:5, 42:11, 43:5), the parallel three-strophe architecture, the absence of a heading on Ps 43, and the testimony of multiple Hebrew manuscripts (Kennicott catalogs at least thirty-seven that join them) and several LXX witnesses all point in the same direction. The Septuagint itself counts our Pss 42 + 43 as Ps 42 in some recensions.

If Ps 43 is the third strophe of the larger Korahite poem, its *Sitz im Leben* shares Ps 42's: a temple-functionary or pilgrim displaced from Jerusalem, longing to return to the sanctuary cult. Mowinckel placed the original poem in pilgrimage-festival context. The geographical anchor in 42:6 ("from the land of Jordan and of Hermon, from Mount Mizar") points to the far north of Israel; some commentators (Dahood; cautiously, Goldingay) suggest a temple-singer caught in northern displacement, perhaps in the late monarchy, perhaps later. The psalm sits in the **Elohistic Psalter** (Pss 42–83), in which *YHWH* has been editorially replaced by *Elohim*; Ps 43 uses *Elohim* five times and YHWH not at all.

Form-critically, Ps 43 belongs to Gunkel's individual lament category, but it leans toward the petition end of the form — what Westermann calls the "imperative element" of lament. Where Ps 42 dwells on memory and self-address, Ps 43 makes the request the strophe never quite voiced before: *shophteni Elohim* (שָׁפְטֵנִי אֱלֹהִים), "judge me, O God" (43:1). The poem moves from grief-and-memory in Ps 42 to **petition-and-vow** here.

Hebrew terms worth marking:

- *shophteni Elohim ve-rivah rivi* (שָׁפְטֵנִי אֱלֹהִים וְרִיבָה רִיבִי), "judge me, O God, and plead my cause" (43:1). *Shaphat* here is forensic — "render judgment for" — and the cognate-noun-with-verb construction *rivah rivi* ("contend my contention") is technical legal language.
- *or-kha va'amittekha* (אוֹרְךָ וַאֲמִתֶּךָ), "your light and your truth" (43:3). The pairing of *or* (light) and *emet* (truth, faithfulness, reliability) is one of the most theologically generative phrases in the Psalter — and is the reason *Lux et Veritas* became Yale's motto and a Benedictine devotional pairing.
- *har-qodshekha* and *mishkenotekha* (הַר־קָדְשְׁךָ … מִשְׁכְּנוֹתֶיךָ), "your holy hill … your dwelling places" (43:3). Zion-temple language, anchoring the petition geographically.
- *El simchat gili* (אֵל שִׂמְחַת גִּילִי), "God my exceeding joy" (43:4). Two near-synonyms for joy stacked: *simchah* and *gil*, the second more exuberant than the first.

Textual evidence: Dead Sea Scroll fragments are sparse for Ps 43 specifically; the LXX joins Ps 43 to 42 in some traditions and counts Ps 43 separately in others. The Vulgate (Jerome from Hebrew) and most medieval Hebrew codices preserve the division, but the Peshitta in some manuscripts joins them. Augustine treated them as separate psalms but recognized their kinship.

## Lens 2: Literature

Ps 43 is built as a tight three-part movement:

- **Petition (43:1–2)**: judge, plead my cause, deliver — followed by the complaint "why have you rejected me?"
- **Vow with conditional plea (43:3–4)**: send light and truth, let them lead me — *then* I will go to the altar.
- **Refrain (43:5)**: the third occurrence of *mah-tishtochachi nafshi*.

Lowth's parallelism: 43:1 is a tight synonymous-with-staircase pattern — "judge me // plead my cause // deliver me," three forensic imperatives ascending in concreteness. 43:3 stacks two pairs of personified attributes ("send out your light and your truth // let them lead me; let them bring me…") in what Berlin would call **double-duty parallelism**: light-and-truth function grammatically as a single envoy, like a pair of angelic guides. Alter notes how the verse personifies abstract divine attributes — *or* and *emet* are sent like messengers — anticipating Wisdom-personification motifs that will flower in Proverbs 8 and Sirach 24.

The poem's most striking literary move is the **deferred climax** at v. 4: *ve-avo'ah el-mizbach Elohim el-El simchat gili* — "then I will go to the altar of God, to God my exceeding joy." The whole arc of Pss 42–43, beginning with "as a deer pants for streams of water," reaches the altar. The thirst is finally vocalized as a *destination*. The arrival is grammatically future ("then I *will* go"), but it is the first moment in the unified poem where the hoped-for place is named without ambiguity. Wenham points out that the place-words pile up — holy hill, dwellings, altar — like a series of concentric circles closing on the Holy of Holies.

The refrain at 43:5 is identical to 42:5 and 42:11 — the third stamp of *mah-tishtochachi nafshi*. The poem does not resolve the soul's downness; it returns to the same self-address. Westermann's lament-form analysis treats this as the closing "vow of praise," but it is a vow keyed to the future tense. Brueggemann reads the tripled refrain as **liturgical pedagogy** — the soul is being taught a phrase to say *every time* the down-pull returns.

There is no *selah* in Ps 43 (consistent with its likely original unity with Ps 42), and the lack of superscription is its own literary fact: the poem opens not with attribution but with imperative — *shophteni Elohim*. The reader is dropped mid-prayer.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Placement.** Ps 43 sits inside the Korahite collection (Pss 42–49) at the head of Book II. If Pss 42 + 43 are read as a unity, the larger poem performs the Korahite-Levitical voice's exile-and-return arc in miniature: thirst (42:1–5), drowning (42:6–11), petition-for-light (43:1–4), refrain. The placement at the threshold of Book II is editorial: after the Davidic individual voice of Book I, the Psalter introduces the Levitical-singer voice longing for the cult.

**Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* tends to treat Pss 42–43 together, dwelling on the petition for light-and-truth as the prayer of Israel in exile asking to be guided home. Rashi reads "send out your light and your truth" as a plea for messianic illumination. Ibn Ezra emphasizes the legal-forensic vocabulary of v. 1 — the psalmist asks to be judged by God *against* the unjust nation, not as a defendant but as a plaintiff with God as arbiter. Radak treats "your holy hill" as Jerusalem and "your dwelling" as the temple. Nachmanides reads light-and-truth as paired gifts: *or* the inward illumination, *emet* the outward steadfastness. In modern Jewish reception, Sarna highlights the poem's confidence that the altar is recoverable — exile is not final. Levenson reads the petition as covenantal: the psalmist appeals to God's prior commitments to Zion. Liturgically, Ps 43 is recited in some Sephardic rites at the entrance to the synagogue, the *or-kha va'amittekha* serving as a threshold prayer.

**Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarratio* on Ps 42 (his numbering) reads "send out your light and your truth" Christologically — *Christus est lux, Christus est veritas* — pointing to John 14:6 ("I am the way, the truth, and the life") and John 1:9 ("the true light"). This Augustinian reading shaped a thousand years of Latin devotion. Cassiodorus develops the light-and-truth as a Trinitarian sending. Aquinas in his *Postilla* treats *or* and *emet* as the gifts of intellect and will perfected. Luther preached on the petition for light-and-truth in the 1520s; the verse appears in his catechetical material on the Holy Spirit. Calvin (1557) emphasizes the verse's pedagogical function: the believer asks for guidance precisely because she does not yet have it. The pairing migrated into liturgical use early — *Lux et Veritas* became inscribed on the seals of universities (Yale, most famously, but also numerous European Benedictine houses) and on baptismal fonts. The Roman Missal incorporates a paraphrase of 43:3–4 as an entrance antiphon — *Iudica me, Deus* — which became one of the prayers at the foot of the altar in the Tridentine Mass: the priest, before ascending to the altar, prays Ps 43 as his own act of approach. Through the entire pre-Vatican II Latin Mass, every priest began the eucharistic action with the words of this psalm.

Modern critical commentary: Gunkel and Mowinckel established the form-critical and cult-functional readings; Westermann mapped the lament-arc; Kraus's *Psalmen* commentary remains a touchstone; Goldingay treats Pss 42–43 as one in his *Psalms* volume; Hossfeld–Zenger's Hermeneia volume is the most thorough recent treatment. Brueggemann reads the petition for light-and-truth as the cry of the disoriented for new orientation — "send something I can follow." Tate, Wenham, Anderson, Miller, Brown, Tanner, and Gillingham each contribute chapters on the unified Pss 42–43.

**NT use.** Ps 43 is not directly cited in the NT, but the light-and-truth pairing reverberates through Johannine vocabulary: "the true light, which gives light to everyone, was coming into the world" (John 1:9); "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6); "if we walk in the light, as he is in the light" (1 John 1:7). The petition-vocabulary of Ps 43 — *send out* (*shelach*) — is taken up in John's *Father-sent-the-Son* theology of mission.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

The psalm presses two tensions worth holding.

**Tension one: What does it mean to ask God to "judge me"?** *Shophteni Elohim* — the imperative of v. 1 — sounds, to modern ears, like asking for trouble. The Christian devotional ear hears "judge" as condemnation. But the Hebrew *shaphat* in this construction is a plea to be **vindicated**, to have one's cause heard against an unjust counter-party. Five mapped positions:

- *Catholic*: the petition is the plea of the just-but-afflicted soul for divine arbitration. The Tridentine *Iudica me* before the altar is the priest's own plea to be vindicated as he approaches the holy. The verse is ecclesial: the priest stands in for the whole church asking to be made worthy.
- *Protestant Reformed (Calvin, Edwards)*: the plea is forensic — the believer in Christ has already been vindicated *coram Deo* and prays for that verdict to be enacted in history against unjust accusers. This is not works-righteousness but the eschatological cry of the justified for visible vindication (cf. Rev 6:10, "how long?").
- *Orthodox*: the petition is the cry of the suffering body of Christ against the powers; the *Iudica me* in the entrance liturgy is the church saying she belongs to God's judgment, not the world's.
- *Jewish (Levenson, Sarna)*: the plea is covenantal-legal. The psalmist invokes the *rivah rivi* — covenant-lawsuit language — and asks God to act as the just judge between Israel and her oppressors. The plea presumes a relational standing, not a moral perfection.
- *Critical / liberation (Brueggemann, Soong-Chan Rah)*: the petition is the protest-prayer of the politically displaced, who refuse to be silent before unjust power and insist that the world has a final court. To pray "judge me" is to refuse the verdicts of the present.

The least intuitive position, for a modern Christian shaped by individualized soteriology, is the Jewish-covenantal reading. Steelman: the psalmist is not asking God to weigh him on a moral scale; he is invoking covenant law and asking God to act as the kinsman-redeemer-arbiter against an oppressor. To say "judge me" is to assert *that there is a court* and that God is bound by his own covenant to convene it. The forensic posture is a posture of *trust*, not anxiety. To privatize the verse — to read it as "search my heart, O God" — is to lose the legal-political work the prayer was doing.

**Tension two: What is the relation between guidance and arrival?** The poem's hinge is "send out your light and your truth; *let them lead me*; *let them bring me*… *then* I will go to the altar." The structure is conditional: the going-up to the altar is contingent on the sending-out of light-and-truth. The believer is not left to find the way unaided; but the believer is also not transported to the altar. Light-and-truth lead. Mapped positions:

- The Augustinian-Catholic tradition reads light-and-truth as Christ-and-Spirit, the divine agents who lead the soul from exile to home. The believer follows.
- The Reformed tradition reads them as Word and Spirit, the means of grace by which the believer is brought to assurance and worship.
- The Orthodox tradition reads them as the divine energies, the uncreated light of Tabor and the truth of the Logos, by which the soul ascends.
- The Jewish reading takes light-and-truth as God's guiding presence in exile — the pillar of cloud and fire, the Torah's lamp — that brings the people back to the place where God dwells.

**Where this leaves us:** Ps 43 makes the going-to-the-altar a future act conditioned on a divine sending. The believer's task is to *ask for the guides*, not to find the way. And the refrain at v. 5, identical to the two refrains in Ps 42, refuses to let the petition close the poem in triumph: even after the vow to come to the altar, the soul is still cast down, and the hope is still in the future tense — *od odennu*, "yet I shall praise him."

**Closing question.** If the longest movement of the unified Pss 42–43 ends not at the altar but at the third repetition of "why are you cast down, O my soul," what does the psalm teach a tradition that prefers its prayers to end with arrival rather than with the same question asked again?

## The Thread

- John 1:9 — "the true light, which gives light to everyone"; the Johannine extension of *or va'emet*.
- John 14:6 — "I am the way, the truth, and the life"; Christ as the personification of *or va'emet*.
- Hebrews 10:19–22 — "let us draw near with a true heart" to the holy place; the new-covenant analogue of "I will go to the altar."
- Revelation 22:5 — "the Lord God will be their light"; the eschatological terminus of the petition for light-and-truth.
