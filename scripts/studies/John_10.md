# John 10: The Good Shepherd, Hanukkah, and "I and the Father Are One"

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

John 10 is unusual in the Fourth Gospel for the precision of its temporal-spatial markers. Verse 22 — "At that time the Feast of Dedication (*ta enkainia*, τὰ ἐγκαίνια) took place at Jerusalem; it was winter" — is the only New Testament reference to **Hanukkah**, the eight-day festival commemorating the rededication of the Temple after Antiochus IV Epiphanes's desecration in 167 BCE and Judas Maccabeus's purification in 164 BCE (1 Maccabees 4:36–59; 2 Maccabees 10:1–8; Josephus, *Antiquities* 12.7.7 §§323–326). The festival fell on 25 Kislev (roughly mid-December), which fits the chapter's "it was winter." Jesus walks in **Solomon's Colonnade** (*hē stoa tou Solomōnos*, ἡ στοὰ τοῦ Σολομῶνος), the eastern portico of the Herodian Temple platform — Josephus describes it (*Antiquities* 20.9.7) and Acts 3:11 and 5:12 confirm it as a gathering place for the early Jerusalem church. Archaeology has located the eastern retaining wall of the platform; the colonnade itself is gone, but the Herodian foundations are visible. The choice of *enkainia* — the same Greek word the Septuagint uses for the dedication of Solomon's Temple (1 Kings 8:63) and Ezra's rededication (Ezra 6:16) — is loaded. Jesus is in the colonnade named for the first temple-builder during the festival celebrating the temple-rededicators, presenting himself as the one whom "the Father consecrated and sent into the world" (10:36, *hēgiasen*, ἡγίασεν, "consecrated/dedicated"). The verb *hagiazō* is the same root the LXX uses for the Maccabean rededication. C.K. Barrett, Brown (AB), and Köstenberger (BECNT) all converge on the point: the chapter weaponizes Hanukkah's theology — God consecrating a sanctuary against a foreign desecrator — and refers it to Jesus as the new consecrated *naos*.

The shepherd discourse (10:1–21) is rooted in **Ezekiel 34**, the foundational Old Testament shepherd-text where YHWH denounces Israel's faithless shepherds (the political and religious leadership), promises personally to shepherd his scattered flock, and pledges to "set up over them one shepherd, my servant David" (Ezekiel 34:23). The Johannine discourse simultaneously claims both halves of the Ezekiel promise — Jesus is *both* YHWH the shepherd *and* the Davidic shepherd. **Psalm 23**, **Genesis 48:15**, **Isaiah 40:11**, **Jeremiah 23:1–8**, and **Zechariah 11–13** stand behind the imagery. **Ancient Near-Eastern royal-shepherd ideology** is the wider frame: Hammurabi's prologue calls him "the shepherd called by Enlil"; Egyptian Pharaohs were depicted with the shepherd's crook (*heka*) as one of the two royal scepters; Mesopotamian kings from Sumer onward used the shepherd-king title routinely. The metaphor was already saturated.

**Qumran shepherd traditions** add a near-contemporary Jewish layer. The **Damascus Document (CD-A) 13.7–11** describes the *mevaqqer* (overseer) as a shepherd to his flock; **4Q521** (the Messianic Apocalypse) and the *War Scroll* envision an eschatological shepherd-figure. Charlesworth's *John and the Dead Sea Scrolls* (1972) and *John and Qumran* document the parallels closely. The Qumran community's strong dualism (1QS 3:13–4:26, the Two-Spirits passage) provides the closest extant Jewish parallel to the chapter's binary structure of true vs. false shepherds, sheep who hear and sheep who do not, light and darkness.

A few Greek terms anchor the chapter. *Egō eimi hē thura* (ἐγὼ εἰμὶ ἡ θύρα, "I am the door," 10:7, 9) — the **second I AM saying with predicate** in the Gospel — uses *thura* in the sense of the gate of a stone-walled sheepfold; archaeological surveys of first-century Judean pastoral architecture (e.g., Shimon Gibson) confirm the open-field stone enclosures with a single gate that match the imagery exactly. *Egō eimi ho poimēn ho kalos* (ἐγὼ εἰμὶ ὁ ποιμὴν ὁ καλός, "I am the good shepherd," 10:11, 14) — the **third I AM saying with predicate** — uses *kalos* (καλός) rather than *agathos* (ἀγαθός); *kalos* connotes "model, ideal, beautiful, noble," not merely "morally good." The good shepherd is the *paradigmatic* shepherd. *Tithēsi tēn psychēn* (τίθησι τὴν ψυχήν, "lays down his life," 10:11, 15, 17, 18) is repeated four times in seven verses — a Johannine signature that anticipates the Passion. *Hen esmen* (ἕν ἐσμεν, "we are one," 10:30) — neuter *hen*, "one thing/one reality," not masculine *heis*, "one person" — is grammatically precise and theologically load-bearing.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter contains **two of the seven I AM sayings with predicates**: "I am the door" (10:7, 9) and "I am the good shepherd" (10:11, 14). With the prior "I am the bread of life" (6:35) and "I am the light of the world" (8:12; 9:5), the Gospel has now staged four. Three remain: "I am the resurrection and the life" (11:25), "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (14:6), and "I am the true vine" (15:1) — and the chapter's "I and the Father are one" (10:30) belongs to the parallel pattern of absolute *egō eimi* sayings (without predicate, recalling Exodus 3:14).

Structurally, John 10 is bound to John 9 by 10:21 ("Can a demon open the eyes of the blind?") — the Pharisees' division reaching back to the prior healing. Most modern commentators (Brown, Köstenberger, Carson, Moloney) treat 9:1–10:21 as a single literary unit; Brown calls 10:1–21 the "shepherd discourse appended to the man-born-blind narrative," with the false shepherds explicitly identified with the Pharisees who expelled the healed man. The Hanukkah pericope (10:22–39) then forms a discrete second unit, separated by approximately two months in narrative time (the Feast of Tabernacles in chapter 7 occurred in Tishri / September–October, two months before Kislev), but linked thematically by the shepherd-imagery (10:26–28 reprises 10:14–16) and by the consecration motif (the Maccabean rededication of the Temple ↔ Jesus as the consecrated one).

Culpepper's *Anatomy* identifies the chapter's structural seams cleanly. The discourse moves through a *paroimia* (παροιμία, "figure of speech," 10:6) that the audience does not understand, then a clarifying interpretation (10:7–18), then audience response (10:19–21), then a temporal jump (10:22), then a confrontation (10:23–39), then a withdrawal (10:40–42). The withdrawal across the Jordan to the place of John the Baptist's first ministry (10:40) is a deliberate inclusio with John 1:28: the public ministry effectively begins and ends at the Jordan crossing-point. Beasley-Murray (WBC) reads this as the "great retreat" preceding the climactic Lazarus sign.

Textual variants: 10:29 has a notoriously difficult variant — was it the Father (*ho patēr mou ho dedōkōs moi pantōn meizōn estin*, "my Father, who has given them to me, is greater than all," P66, ℵ, B, K) or the gift (*ho dedōken moi pantōn meizon estin*, "what he has given me is greater than all," D, W, lat)? Modern critical editions split; Metzger gives the masculine reading a {C} rating with admitted difficulty. The theological force is similar but not identical.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish interpretive trajectories.** Daniel Boyarin's *Border Lines* (2004) and *The Jewish Gospels* (2012) frame "I and the Father are one" (10:30) not as a sudden Christological shock invented by John but as one option within a developed late-Second-Temple Jewish "binitarian" or "two powers in heaven" speculation — visible in the Enochic literature, the Similitudes (1 Enoch 37–71), Philo's Logos doctrine, and rabbinic discussions of *Metatron*. The mainstream rabbinic tradition (b. Hagigah 14b–15a; b. Sanhedrin 38b) eventually condemned "two powers" as heresy (the apostasy of Elisha ben Abuyah is the parade case), but Boyarin argues that this condemnation post-dates John and reflects an emergent rabbinic effort to draw boundaries against precisely the kind of Jewish messianism John represents. Adele Reinhartz's *Cast Out of the Covenant* presses the *aposynagōgos* problem here, and Amy-Jill Levine's *The Misunderstood Jew* warns against reading John 10:31–39 as confirmation of an essential Jewish hostility to Jesus's identity claim — the dispute is intra-Jewish, internal to the question of how the One God is to be understood. James Charlesworth's *John and Qumran* and *The Messiah* show the Davidic-shepherd messianism of the Dead Sea Scrolls as a precise contemporary parallel.

**Christian interpretive trajectories.** Origen's *Commentary on John* takes the seven I AM sayings as the centerpiece of the Gospel's Christology and reads "I and the Father are one" against early adoptionist and modalist readings. Athanasius's *Discourses Against the Arians* makes 10:30 a centerpiece of Nicene exegesis: the neuter *hen* asserts unity of substance (*homoousios*) without confusion of person. Cyril of Alexandria's *Commentary on John* extends the argument; the great Cappadocians use it for the developed pro-Nicene grammar. Augustine's *Tractates* (Tractates 45–49) reads the shepherd discourse pastorally and sacramentally — the door is the sacramental entry point, the shepherd is Christ feeding the flock through Word and Eucharist. Chrysostom's *Homilies on John* (Homilies 59–61) emphasizes the contrast between the hireling and the true shepherd, applying the text to fourth-century episcopal corruption with characteristic bluntness. Aquinas's *Lectura super Joannem* (cap. X) develops the substantive-unity reading scholastically and integrates it with his own treatment of *De Trinitate*.

The Reformation reread the chapter in two distinctive ways. Calvin's *Commentary on John* takes 10:28–29 ("no one shall snatch them out of my hand") as a foundational text for the perseverance of the saints, and the doctrine of election. Luther preached from John 10 as the locus classicus for the priesthood of believers and for distinguishing the true Word-bearing pastor from the hireling. The Calvinist tradition (Westminster Confession, Canons of Dordt) made 10:11 ("the good shepherd lays down his life *for the sheep*") a textual anchor for definite atonement; Arminian readers (e.g., John Wesley) read it more inclusively in light of "other sheep not of this fold" (10:16) and the universal gathering at 12:32.

Modern critical commentary: Bultmann reads the discourse in his usual demythologizing mode but recognizes its rhetorical power. Käsemann's *Testament* presses the chapter for its sectarian tone (the elect sheep / the world's wolves). Brown's Anchor Bible details the Ezekiel 34 dependence comprehensively. Schnackenburg's *HthKNT* develops the Jewish-Christological background. Barrett (CGTC), Beasley-Murray (WBC), Carson (PNTC), Köstenberger (BECNT), Keener, Moloney (Sacra Pagina), Marianne Meye Thompson (NTL), Gail O'Day (NIB), and Newbigin (*The Light Has Come*) all give the chapter sustained treatment. Newbigin, writing from the Indian missionary context, attends especially to "other sheep not of this fold" — a verse that demanded careful theology in a religiously plural setting.

**On 10:34's "I said, you are gods"** — a citation of **Psalm 82:6** — Carl Mosser's "The Earliest Patristic Interpretations of Psalm 82, Jewish Antecedents, and the Origin of Christian Deification" (*JTS* 56, 2005) and the wider study *Jesus and Theosis* document the patristic tradition of reading this verse alongside 2 Peter 1:4 ("partakers of the divine nature") to ground the doctrine of *theōsis* — deification, not in the sense of becoming God by nature but participation in the divine life by grace. Athanasius's famous formulation, "He became man so that we might become god" (*De Incarnatione* 54), draws this trajectory into systematic Christian theology. Eastern Orthodox theology has held this as a foundational doctrine; Western theology, especially since the Reformation, foregrounded it less but is recovering it (Reformed scholar Michael Horton, Catholic Henri de Lubac, Pope Benedict XVI in *Jesus of Nazareth*). The Jewish background of Psalm 82 — the divine council, the *bene elohim*, the surrounding gods judged by YHWH — is itself a contested exegetical field; Michael Heiser's *The Unseen Realm* gives the most accessible recent treatment, James Kugel's *The God of Old* the more historical.

**On "other sheep not of this fold" (10:16)** — this verse has been the locus classicus for theologies of religious pluralism and Christian inclusivity. The Catholic *extra ecclesiam nulla salus* tradition (Cyprian, Florence, Boniface VIII's *Unam Sanctam*) reads the verse strictly: the "other sheep" are Gentiles who must be brought into the one fold (the visible Church). Vatican II's *Lumen Gentium* §16 and *Nostra Aetate* §2 opened a more inclusive reading, recognizing rays of truth in non-Christian religions. Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* IV/3 develops a christocentric universalism that holds together the particularity of Christ and the universality of his shepherding work. Reformed exclusivist readings (e.g., the Westminster tradition) hold the verse to refer to the Gentile mission, not to a salvific work outside the Christian gospel. Lesslie Newbigin, again from the Indian context, navigates the question with characteristic care: Christ is Lord of all sheep, but the *means* of their belonging to the flock is the gospel proclaimed.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: How is Jesus's "I and the Father are one" (10:30) related to monotheism?**

Position A — Modalist / Sabellian reading (rejected by mainstream tradition): the Father and Son are the same divine person under different modes; *hen* in 10:30 is collapsed into *heis*. Position B — Arian / subordinationist reading: Jesus is one with the Father in *purpose* and *will* but not in *substance*; *hen* is moral, not metaphysical. Position C — Pro-Nicene reading (Athanasius, Cappadocians, Cyril, Aquinas, mainstream Catholic-Orthodox-Protestant Christianity): the neuter *hen* asserts unity of being (*homoousios*) while preserving the distinct personhood (*hypostasis*) of Father and Son; the verse together with 14:9 ("whoever has seen me has seen the Father") and 1:1 grounds the doctrine of the Trinity. Position D — Boyarin's intra-Jewish reading: the verse fits within a late-Second-Temple Jewish "two powers" tradition that Christianity later inherited and rabbinic Judaism later condemned; it is not a departure from Jewish monotheism but a contested form within it.

Where this leaves us: the pro-Nicene reading remains the structural backbone of Christian Trinitarian theology, and the Greek grammar (neuter *hen*, plural *esmen*) genuinely supports it against modalism and against a flat Arianism. But Boyarin's framing matters historically: the verse was not yet Nicene-Christian in 90 CE, and its eventual Trinitarian reception was a genuine intellectual achievement, not a self-evident reading. The Jewish monotheistic horizon within which it was first uttered must be preserved if we are to read it without supersessionism.

**Tension 2: What is the scope of "the good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" (10:11) — for whom does Christ die?**

Position A — Definite / particular atonement (Calvin's late position, Dordt, Owen's *Death of Death*): Christ dies *for* his sheep — those given to him by the Father (10:29) — and his atoning death is efficacious for the elect. The "for" (*hyper*, ὑπέρ) is substitutionary and particular. Position B — Universal atonement (Arminius, Wesley, Catholic tradition): Christ's death is sufficient for all, made effective by faith; the "other sheep not of this fold" (10:16) widens the scope toward all humanity. Position C — Christus Victor / cosmic reading (Aulén, some patristic): Christ's death-and-resurrection defeats the powers; the atonement's scope is the whole created order, with the gathering of the flock as one expression. Position D — Barthian universal-particular reading: in Christ all are objectively reconciled; the gathering "into one flock" (10:16) anticipates the ultimate eschatological unity.

Where this leaves us: 10:15–16 holds particularity and universality in deliberate tension — "I lay down my life *for the sheep* … *and* I have other sheep not of this fold." The text resists collapse into either a strict particularism or a flat universalism. The shepherd dies for *his* sheep, *and* his sheep are gathered from beyond the visible fold. Newbigin's framing — that Christ is the universal shepherd whose particular gospel must be proclaimed — captures the New Testament's own balance. The particular atonement tradition risks shrinking the gospel's scope; the universalist tradition risks evacuating its summons.

One unresolved question: what is the relationship between "the works that I do bear witness about me" (10:25) and "if I am not doing the works of my Father, then do not believe me" (10:37)? Jesus appeals to his works as sufficient evidence even for those who disbelieve his words. This implies an evidentialist Christology in tension with the chapter's other strand ("you do not believe because you are not among my sheep," 10:26) — which approaches an apophatic predestinarianism. The Gospel holds both, but it does not reconcile them.

## The Thread

The chapter looks back to Ezekiel 34 (false shepherds replaced by YHWH and the Davidic shepherd), Psalm 23 (the Lord as my shepherd), Psalm 82:6 (the contested divine-council text Jesus cites), and Genesis 48:15 (Jacob's confession of "the God who has been my shepherd all my life"). It looks forward to John 11 (the seventh sign — the resurrection that confirms the shepherd's claim to have authority "to take it up again," 10:18), to John 17 (the high-priestly prayer's "that they may be one, even as we are one," 17:11, picking up 10:30's *hen*), and to John 21 (Peter's threefold "feed my sheep," where the resurrected shepherd hands the pastoral office on). Cross-reference Hebrews 13:20 ("the great shepherd of the sheep, by the blood of the eternal covenant"), 1 Peter 2:25 ("the Shepherd and Overseer of your souls"), and 1 Peter 5:4 ("when the chief Shepherd appears") — Petrine appropriations of the same imagery. Acts 20:28–29 (Paul to the Ephesian elders: "fierce wolves will come in among you, not sparing the flock") shows the shepherd-discourse as the formative metaphor for early Christian pastoral office.
