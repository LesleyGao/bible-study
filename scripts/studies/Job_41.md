# Job 41: The King Over All the Sons of Pride

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 41 is the longest sustained portrait of a single creature in the Hebrew Bible — thirty-four verses devoted to Leviathan (לִוְיָתָן, *liwyāṯān*). The chapter closes the second divine speech and, with chapter 40's Behemoth, the entire Joban theophany. The verse enumeration in English translations (ESV, NIV, NRSV) differs from the Hebrew Masoretic Text, which begins chapter 41 three verses earlier, with what English translations designate as 40:25. The standard English numbering begins with the question "Can you draw out Leviathan with a fishhook?" — the question that has shaped every subsequent reading of the chapter.

The Leviathan of Job 41 is emphatically not a crocodile in any mundane zoological sense, though there are commentators (Aquinas in moments, Bochart, and a line of early modern naturalists) who attempt that identification. The overwhelming evidence — textual, comparative, and intertextual — points to a chaos monster drawn directly from the ancient Near Eastern mythological repertoire.

The Ugaritic Baal Cycle (Ras Shamra tablets, c. 1300 BCE) contains the crucial comparative material. In KTU 1.5.I.1–3, the goddess Anat boasts of having crushed *ltn*, "Lotan" — a seven-headed serpent also called "the twisting serpent" (*btn ʿqltn*) and "the fleeing serpent" (*btn brḥ*). The consonantal root *l-t-n* in Ugaritic corresponds to Hebrew *l-w-y-t-n*; Lotan is Leviathan. The same vocabulary (*bāriaḥ*, "fleeing"; *ʿăqallāṯôn*, "twisting") appears verbatim in Isaiah 27:1 applied to Leviathan. The Baal Cycle's Yamm (Sea) is the cosmic adversary whom Baal defeats; Leviathan is a figure of the chaos waters. The continuity between Ugaritic and biblical Leviathan is linguistically explicit.

The Babylonian *Enuma Elish* (c. 1100 BCE, though older) presents the parallel figure Tiamat, the salt-water chaos-goddess whom Marduk defeats by shooting arrows into her belly and splitting her carcass to make heaven and earth. The chaoskampf — divine warrior against sea-monster — is a shared Near Eastern narrative template. Levenson's *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* (1988) argues that Israelite creation theology is deeply embedded in this template, and that the creation accounts of Genesis 1 and Job 38–41 cannot be understood without it.

The iconographic record confirms. Cylinder seals from Akkad through Assyria depict the divine warrior battling a multi-headed sea serpent. The New Kingdom Egyptian *Book of the Dead* depicts the sun-god Ra nightly fighting the serpent Apophis. The seven-headed serpent reappears in Revelation 12:3 as the dragon who threatens the woman — a direct inheritance of the Lotan/Leviathan tradition mediated through Jewish apocalyptic (4 Ezra, 2 Baruch, Daniel 7).

Textually, Job 41 presents significant difficulties. The LXX is substantially shorter, omitting entire lines. 11QTargum Job preserves Aramaic fragments. The Hebrew itself contains multiple hapax legomena — words occurring only here — making lexical decisions hard (e.g., *ʾăpîqê māgınnîm*, "channels of shields," 41:15, for the scales). The description of fire from mouth and smoke from nostrils (41:19–21) has been read as hyperbolic poetry for crocodilian snorting, as mythological dragonology, or as ancient observation of bioluminescence in churned water — no consensus exists.

Five Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. First, לִוְיָתָן (*liwyāṯān*, 41:1), from the root *l-w-y*, "to twist, coil" — the twisting one. Second, תְּהוֹם (*təhôm*, implicit in 41:31–32), the primordial deep of Genesis 1:2, cognate with Akkadian Tiamat — the sea Leviathan inhabits is not the mundane Mediterranean but the cosmic deep. Third, בְנֵי שָׁחַץ (*bənê šāḥaṣ*, 41:34, "sons of pride"), a poetic term for the proudest of predators, the apex — Leviathan is king over them. Fourth, תִּקְוָה (*tiqwāh*, 41:9), "hope" — the hope of catching him is "false," a rare negative use of this otherwise salvific term. Fifth, the phrase אֵין עַל־עָפָר מָשְׁלוֹ (*ʾên ʿal-ʿāpār mošlô*, 41:33), "on earth there is not his like" — "dust" (ʿāpār) evokes Genesis 2:7's creation-dust, positioning Leviathan as outside ordinary creatureliness.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is a single, sustained, technically virtuosic poem. Newsom calls it "the grandest extended metaphor in biblical literature." The structure is clear: 41:1–11 asks questions testing whether Leviathan can be managed (captured, bargained, harpooned, touched, stirred up); 41:12–34 describes Leviathan's body and behavior in positive terms — limbs, back, face, sneezings, breath, neck, heart, underparts, wake. The chapter moves from Leviathan's ungovernability to Leviathan's magnificence, culminating in the verdict: "On earth there is not his like, a creature without fear. He sees everything that is high; he is king over all the sons of pride" (41:33–34).

Alter, in *The Art of Biblical Poetry*, calls this the climax of the whirlwind speeches and analyzes its characteristic heightening parallelism. The lines relentlessly intensify: sword, spear, dart, javelin (41:26), arrow and sling stones (41:28), clubs and javelins (41:29) — every weapon is enumerated and nullified. The poem's cumulative rhetoric is one of exhaustive inadequacy: human military-technological mastery is paraded and demolished.

The pivotal theological verses are 41:10–11 and 41:33–34. 41:10–11 turns the creature description into divine self-reference: "No one is so fierce that he dares to stir him up. Who then is he who can stand before me? Who has first given to me, that I should repay him? Whatever is under the whole heaven is mine." The creature who cannot be stirred up is paired with the God who cannot be obliged. Nobody has standing against Leviathan; nobody has standing against God. The logic is analogical. Paul cites 41:11 in Romans 11:35 — the only explicit New Testament citation of Job — precisely at the moment when Paul is arguing the inscrutability of God's purposes in election.

41:33–34 is the poem's final verdict. "He is king (*melekh*) over all the sons of pride." Leviathan is crowned, not defeated. This is the decisive moment for Levenson's interpretation. Unlike Marduk splitting Tiamat's carcass in *Enuma Elish*, unlike Baal dismembering Yamm in the Ugaritic cycle, YHWH does not kill Leviathan here. The chapter ends with Leviathan alive, enthroned, unassailable, and supremely present. Levenson's decisive thesis: the YHWH speeches withhold the triumphant combat-myth closure. God does not demonstrate sovereignty by slaying the chaos monster; God demonstrates sovereignty by showing Job a creature Job cannot slay and implicitly locating divine power as what alone could address such a being. Leviathan persists in the world Job inhabits, and the cosmos is the cosmos of a persisting Leviathan.

The literary genre is sometimes called "mock-hymn" or "paradoxical doxology" — Newsom's phrase is "perverse encomium." The chapter praises Leviathan in the formal language of royal or divine description (scales of the warrior's armor, breath of fire from the nostrils, fearlessness, kingship) while turning that language against Job's (and the reader's) expectation. What is being praised is not domesticated power but unconquered power.

Textually, LXX of Job 41 is significantly abbreviated — by some counts nearly a quarter shorter than MT — and renders some of the most difficult lines (like 41:18–21 on fire and smoke) in diminished form. The Theodotionic additions restored by Origen in the Hexapla align more closely with MT. Commentators have long debated whether the shorter Old Greek preserves an earlier textual form or reflects the translator's difficulty with a mythological register.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Canonically, Leviathan in Job 41 is the culmination of a tradition that runs from Psalm 74 and Psalm 89 through Isaiah 27 to the apocalyptic literature and finally to Revelation 12 and 20. Psalm 74:13–14 and Psalm 89:9–10 celebrate YHWH's *past* defeat of Leviathan at creation: "You divided the sea by your might; you broke the heads of the sea monsters on the waters. You crushed the heads of Leviathan; you gave him as food to the creatures of the wilderness." Psalm 104:26 takes a different tone: "there is Leviathan, which you formed to play in it" — Leviathan is God's pet, God's playmate in the sea. Isaiah 27:1 prophesies the *future* defeat: "In that day the LORD with his hard and great and strong sword will punish Leviathan the fleeing serpent, Leviathan the twisting serpent, and he will slay the dragon that is in the sea." Job 41 sits between these — neither recalling a past defeat nor prophesying a future one, but displaying the creature's present unsubdued magnificence.

In Jewish tradition, the Leviathan material ramified extensively. The Babylonian Talmud (Bava Batra 74b–75a) preserves rich aggadic traditions: Leviathan was created male and female but God killed the female to prevent reproduction; in the messianic age, Leviathan will be slaughtered and the righteous will feast on his flesh in a tent made of his hide. The tradition is preserved in the standard Jewish liturgy for Sukkot (the "*yehi ratson*" after the *Leshev ba-sukkah*), which asks that the worshippers be privileged to sit "in the sukkah of Leviathan's skin." Rashi and the medieval commentators read Leviathan as both a real (if superlative) sea creature and a type of eschatological power. Maimonides, as with Behemoth, demythologizes: Leviathan is an occasion for recognizing the limits of human cognition (*Guide* III.22–23). Nachmanides preserves mystical readings. Ibn Ezra attends to linguistic detail.

Levenson's reading is now widely influential. In *Creation and the Persistence of Evil*, he argues that Job 41 is the strongest biblical statement of the thesis that creation is "ordered but not unopposed." God does not annihilate Leviathan; God plays with him (the resonance with Psalm 104:26 is decisive — the "play" of God with Leviathan is not innocent animal affection but the play of a champion with a restrained adversary). The cosmos Job inhabits contains Leviathan, and the divine claim to sovereignty does not depend on Leviathan's abolition. James Kugel, Avivah Zornberg, and Michael Fishbane have all developed related readings. The Jewish tradition thus largely refuses the triumphalist resolution.

In Christian tradition, Leviathan was read christologically almost from the start. Origen, Chrysostom, and Augustine all identify Leviathan with Satan, the ancient serpent. Gregory the Great's *Moralia* devotes extensive treatment to Leviathan as devil, with each verse of chapter 41 allegorized accordingly — the scales are Satan's ministers, the fire from the mouth is heretical teaching, the "king over all the sons of pride" is explicit Satanological vocabulary. The identification was sealed by Revelation 12:9, which names "the great dragon, that ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan." On this reading, Job 41 is a pre-Christian glimpse of the adversary whom Christ defeats at the cross (Colossians 2:15, Hebrews 2:14) — the dragon of Revelation 12:7–9 and 20:1–3 who is finally cast into the lake of fire.

Aquinas in his Job commentary takes a more moderate position: Leviathan refers primarily to a great sea creature that symbolically represents the devil. Calvin's sermons on chapter 41 emphasize the humiliation of Job but make less of the Satanological identification than Gregory. Luther reads Leviathan straightforwardly as Satan. The Reformation and post-Reformation tradition inherited the Christological reading.

Modern Christian commentary has divided. Clines (WBC) and Hartley read Leviathan as a mythological figure redeployed within the specific argument of the Joban theophany — not strictly Satan, but an image of chaos ultimately governable only by God. Fyall (*Now My Eyes Have Seen You*) is unusual in strongly retrieving the Satanological reading; he argues that Leviathan and Behemoth are literary-theological ciphers for Death and Satan respectively, encoding the spiritual architecture of the book. Habel takes a chaoskampf reading without strong Satanological identification. Hartley stays closer to Gregory's allegorical reading. Gutiérrez reads Leviathan as a symbol of the powers of oppression ultimately contestable only by God — a political theology reading.

The decisive convergence across traditions: Leviathan is a figure of power beyond human mastery. The decisive divergence: Christian readings typically import a Satanological identification that retrojects Revelation 12 and 20 into Job 41, rendering Leviathan a defeated enemy in the cosmic drama; Jewish and much critical-modern reading (Levenson especially) resist this retrojection and preserve Leviathan as a figure of chaos God restrains but does not eliminate. The theological stakes are significant: on the Christian-triumphalist reading, Leviathan is already beaten; on the Levensonian reading, Leviathan persists as a cosmic fact, sustained in restrained existence by ongoing divine action. Which reading one takes determines what kind of world Job is being shown.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is Leviathan defeated, restrained, or cherished?**

- *Defeated reading* (Gregory, Luther, much Christian tradition): Leviathan is the chaos enemy whom God has conquered, finally identifiable with Satan, whose defeat is completed in Christ. The chapter displays the creature to Job to show the creature's ultimate nullity before God.
- *Restrained reading* (Levenson, Kugel, the chaoskampf tradition): Leviathan is a persistent cosmic reality whom God controls without eliminating. The cosmos is ordered-but-opposed. Divine sovereignty is demonstrated precisely by Leviathan's unabolished presence under God's governance.
- *Cherished reading* (Psalm 104:26, Newsom, Davis): Leviathan is God's creature, crowned by God as "king over the sons of pride," and God takes genuine aesthetic and relational pleasure in this creature. The poem is doxological; Leviathan participates in the creaturely goodness that God celebrates. This is shocking because it includes what looks like cosmic adversary within the category of "things God delights in."
- *Steelmanned least-intuitive reading* (the radical Levensonian reading, sharpened): Leviathan is not only restrained and not only cherished — Leviathan is *necessary* to the cosmos as God has made it. The world's goodness is not the goodness of pacified order but of ordered dynamism. Without Leviathan's resistance, there would be no cosmos of the kind God has made. Evil's persistence is not a bug in creation but a feature — not because evil is good, but because the finitude, danger, and resistance represented by Leviathan are constitutive of the specific cosmos God has chosen to make. This reading is hardest because it seems to concede too much to evil. But it takes with full seriousness that God does not slay Leviathan in the speech, describes Leviathan with what can only be called affection, and positions Leviathan as king rather than as slave.

**Tension 2: What does Leviathan's presence reveal about God?**

- *Omnipotence reading* (Calvin, Aquinas): God is the only being before whom Leviathan's power is nothing. The chapter exalts divine omnipotence by contrast.
- *Wisdom reading* (Maimonides, Newsom): God is the only being who can give coherent description of Leviathan — the speech itself is the demonstration. Job's silence in 42:1–6 is in response to cognitive reality beyond his grasp, not merely to power.
- *Relational reading* (Davis, Ticciati): God is the one who stands in relation to Leviathan — playing, ruling, describing. The chapter reveals not a detached sovereign but an engaged creator who has relational commitments to every creature, including the terrifying ones.
- *Steelmanned least-intuitive reading* (Buber-adjacent, Zornberg): Leviathan's presence reveals that God is the one who *answers* not by solving but by presencing. The cosmos contains Leviathan; God speaks. That is the relationship. Job's problem is not that Leviathan exists; Job's problem was that Job thought God's existence should preclude what Leviathan's existence represents. The chapter reveals a God who coexists with Leviathan — restraining, delighting in, ruling — and invites Job to inhabit that same coexistence. The revelation is not about God's power vis-à-vis Leviathan; it is about God's mode of being in a Leviathan-containing world.

*Where this leaves us:* Chapter 41 refuses to kill the monster. God's final word before Job's response is not triumph over chaos but the naming of chaos as king over the proud. This may be the most theologically radical moment in the Joban poem — a claim about the cosmos that canonical theology has rarely absorbed in its full weight.

The unresolved question: If Leviathan is king over the sons of pride — the apex predators, the fiercest of creatures — and God has made him so, does the cosmos contain a legitimate kingship of fearlessness that human beings are wrong to try to usurp? And if so, does the whole project of human attempts at moral cosmic administration (which chapter 40:11–14 mocked) stand rebuked not just by our incapacity but by our having misread the cosmos itself?

## The Thread

**Psalm 74:13–14** recounts: "You divided the sea by your might; you broke the heads of the sea monsters on the waters. You crushed the heads of Leviathan; you gave him as food to the creatures of the wilderness." The past-tense combat-myth language of Psalm 74 stands in sharp contrast to Job 41's present-tense, undefeated, enthroned Leviathan. The juxtaposition is itself theologically significant: the same tradition holds both that God has defeated Leviathan and that Leviathan remains. The two readings are held together canonically as complementary rather than contradictory — Leviathan is both "crushed in the past" and "present now," a paradox the Joban author refuses to resolve in favor of triumph.

**Isaiah 27:1** prophesies: "In that day the LORD with his hard and great and strong sword will punish Leviathan the fleeing serpent, Leviathan the twisting serpent, and he will slay the dragon that is in the sea." The language (*liwyāṯān nāḥāš bāriaḥ* and *liwyāṯān nāḥāš ʿăqallāṯôn*) is lifted nearly verbatim from the Ugaritic Lotan tradition. Isaiah 27:1 is the prophetic counterpart to Job 41: the creature Job is shown as undefeated is the creature Isaiah promises will be defeated. Between them lies the entire eschatological horizon.

**Revelation 12:3–9** depicts a "great red dragon with seven heads and ten horns" — the seven-headed Lotan of Ugaritic tradition, mediated through Jewish apocalyptic — identified explicitly as "the devil and Satan." Revelation 12:7–9's war in heaven and the dragon's defeat, and Revelation 20:1–3 and 10's eschatological casting of the dragon into the lake of fire, complete the trajectory. Job 41 withholds what Isaiah promises and Revelation enacts: the final defeat of the sea-serpent. Reading Job 41 canonically within this trajectory requires holding together the creature's present unconquered status (Job) and the eschatological certainty of its defeat (Isaiah, Revelation).

**Romans 11:35**: "Or who has given a gift to him that he might be repaid?" This is Paul's only explicit New Testament citation of Job, lifted verbatim from Job 41:11. Paul uses it at the climax of his argument about Israel's unbelief and Gentile inclusion — precisely the hardest theodicy problem Paul addresses. Paul cites Job 41 not for its monster but for its theological logic: no one is God's creditor, no one has prior claim on God, and the inscrutability that Job was shown through the Leviathan poem is the same inscrutability that governs Paul's account of divine election. The unanswered question of Job 41 becomes, for Paul, the structural ground of Christian eschatological patience.
