# Deuteronomy 25: Forty Stripes, Less One

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Deuteronomy 25 is the penultimate chapter of the Deuteronomic Code (chs. 12–26) and one of the most miscellaneous in the book. It opens with a judicial flogging provision, turns to an agricultural maxim about the threshing ox, walks through the levirate-marriage institution and its release ceremony (*ḥăliṣâ*), legislates a uniquely severe penalty for a fight-intervening wife, regulates honest weights and measures, and closes with the command to remember — and to blot out the memory of — Amalek. The Hebrew title of the book, *Dĕbārîm* ("words"), is taken from its opening. The dramatic frame places Moses on the plains of Moab, rehearsing the covenant law for the Jordan-crossing generation.

Critical scholarship from de Wette (1805) through Wellhausen reads the book as reflecting Josianic reform (2 Kings 22–23); Weinfeld treats it as Neo-Assyrian-era work drawing on both inherited tradition and ANE treaty collections. Kitchen, Kline, and Craigie defend substantively Mosaic origin on the basis of Late Bronze Age Hittite treaty parallels.

The flogging legislation (vv. 1–3) assumes a judicial setting with elders in the gate, a formal determination of innocence and guilt, and a proportional corporal punishment capped at forty stripes. ANE parallels — MAL A §§18–20, 40; the Code of Hammurabi §202 (sixty blows); the Hittite Laws — all legislate corporal punishment, but Deuteronomy is alone in the ANE corpus for its explicit humanitarian cap and its motive clause: "lest your brother be degraded in your sight." The Hebrew verb *niqlâ* (degraded) is the theological center of the provision — punishment stops short of dehumanization.

The threshing-ox law (v. 4) presupposes the actual agricultural practice excavated across Iron Age Levantine threshing floors, where oxen were driven in circles over laid grain, sometimes pulling a threshing sledge (*môrag*) studded with flint or iron teeth. Muzzles (Hebrew *ḥāsōm*) were indeed used by some ANE agriculturalists to prevent eating; the Deuteronomic law is precisely an argument against that labor-optimizing practice.

The levirate law (*yibbûm*, vv. 5–10) reflects an institution widely attested across the ANE. The Middle Assyrian Laws (MAL A §§30–33) legislate a levirate-like institution; Hittite Laws §§192–193 do so even more explicitly. Nuzi tablets preserve actual adoption and levirate-like contracts. The Hebrew *yābām* (husband's brother, v. 5) and its verbal form *yibbēm* (v. 5) give the institution its name in rabbinic Hebrew. The purpose clause — "that his name may not be blotted out of Israel" — reflects the ANE theology of name-preservation, in which a lineage extinguished was a form of social death. The refusal ceremony uses a specialized verb *ḥālaṣ* (to draw off, vv. 9–10), giving the rabbinic term *ḥăliṣâ* for the release procedure. The symbolic gestures — sandal removal, spitting — have parallels in ANE legal gestures of transfer and renunciation; the Nuzi and Mari texts document sandal-transfer as legal symbol for property cession. Ruth 4:7–8 treats the sandal-gesture as an older custom already needing explanation in the narrator's day.

The fight-intervention law (vv. 11–12) is one of the most shocking provisions in the Torah — the only case in the Hebrew Bible where bodily mutilation is prescribed. The Hebrew *weqaṣṣōtâ ʾet-kappāh* literally means "you shall cut off her hand." Rabbinic tradition from Sifre onward, and consistently in the Bavli (*Bava Kamma* 28a), softened the penalty to monetary compensation, reading the text as specifying a penalty *measure* rather than a literal act — a trajectory Jeffrey Tigay (JPS) argues reflects already-ancient Jewish interpretive discomfort with the literal reading.

The weights-and-measures legislation (vv. 13–16) assumes the basket of stone weights carried in a merchant's bag (*ʾeben wāʾeben*, "stone and stone") and the double set of measuring vessels in the household (*ʾēpâ wĕʾēpâ*, "ephah and ephah"). Excavations at Lachish, Gezer, and elsewhere have recovered Iron Age Judean weight stones inscribed with denominations (beka, pim, shekel); fraud by doubled weights was a well-documented ancient problem, denounced also in Amos 8:5, Micah 6:10–11, and Proverbs 11:1, 20:10. The Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemope* (late second millennium BCE) contains strikingly similar maxims about weights, and most scholars now accept Egyptian influence on Proverbs 22:17–24:22.

The Amalek memory provision (vv. 17–19) presupposes the narrative of Exodus 17:8–16, in which Amalek attacks Israel at Rephidim, and the chronicle continues through 1 Samuel 15 (Saul's failed extermination), the books of Samuel (David's struggles with Amalekite raiders), and the Esther narrative (Haman the Agagite). "Cut off your tail" (v. 18) is a technical military expression for attacking the stragglers in the rear of a marching column — the weak, the wounded, the exhausted.

Key Hebrew terms: *malqîyyût* (flogging, inferred from rabbinic tradition); *niqlâ* (degraded, v. 3); *yibbûm* and *yābām* (levirate institution and brother-in-law, v. 5); *ḥăliṣâ* (release through sandal removal, v. 9); *ʾeben wāʾeben* (stone and stone = double weights, v. 13); *zēker ʿămālēq* (memory of Amalek, v. 19).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter alternates casuistic case law with apodictic injunction. Patrick Miller's category of "preached law" fits here: the motive clauses ("lest your brother be degraded," "that his name may not be blotted out," "that your days may be long," "you shall not forget") consistently embed the rules in moral-theological explanation.

The chapter's miscellaneous surface has driven structural-compositional analysis. Calum Carmichael (*The Laws of Deuteronomy*; *Law and Narrative in the Bible*) argues that Deuteronomy 25 completes the Decalogue-keyed structure of chs. 12–26, with these provisions corresponding to the eighth commandment (theft, via the weights legislation), the ninth (false witness, via the judicial-flogging procedure), and reaching back into narrative-triggered cases derived from Genesis episodes. Carmichael's characteristic move is to argue, for instance, that the levirate legislation here is narratively triggered by the Judah/Tamar episode in Genesis 38 and the Jacob/Esau sandal-gesture traditions. Stephen Kaufman's Decalogue correlation agrees broadly; Dennis Olson (*Deuteronomy and the Death of Moses*) is more cautious about the grid and reads the chapter as thematic clusters on proportionality, continuity, honesty, and memory. Georg Braulik finds chiastic patterning. Duane Christensen (WBC) offers his characteristic verse-by-verse logoprosodic analysis.

Literary devices are notable. The flogging law's cap of forty stripes rhymes phonetically and conceptually with the Amalek command to "blot out the memory" — both deploy proportional reckoning against unchecked retaliation. The ox-muzzle maxim (v. 4) is set aphoristically in the midst of human-law legislation, a minor provision that Paul will seize as a major Christian ministry text. The ceremonial speech of the widow ("So shall it be done to the man who does not build up his brother's house," v. 9) is rhetorically climactic — a formal legal performance preserved in direct address. The house-of-him-who-had-his-sandal-pulled-off appellation (v. 10) is a preserved shaming-nickname, a form also attested in Judges 6:32 (Jerubbaal) and elsewhere.

Textually, 4QDeut fragments preserve portions of the chapter with only minor orthographic variants. The Samaritan Pentateuch occasionally harmonizes. The Septuagint renders the key legal terms faithfully; the LXX's rendering of Deuteronomy 25:4 (*ou phimōseis boun aloōnta*) is verbatim what Paul quotes in 1 Corinthians 9:9 and 1 Timothy 5:18 — a direct witness to early Christian interpretive practice. The Amalek provision is preserved in the liturgically important *Parashat Zakhor* reading, which Orthodox Jewish tradition requires to be heard annually on the Sabbath before Purim; textual precision in this passage has been treated with particular care.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish reception.** Philo (*Special Laws* 2, 3) treats the flogging cap as evidence of the Torah's humanitarian moderation and engages the levirate institution with apologetic purpose. Josephus (*Antiquities* 4, 5) paraphrases the provisions and narrates their operation. Qumran materials engage the weights law in their broader halakhic concerns; the Temple Scroll rewrites related material.

The Mishnah devotes the entire tractate *Yevamot* to the levirate institution — its applicability, its procedures, its dissolution through *ḥăliṣâ*, the status of rivals, and the complex case law that develops when a brother is already married or when multiple brothers are involved. Tractate *Makkot* handles the flogging legislation, working out the number and force of the stripes. The famous Mishnah and Bavli *Makkot* 22a tradition fixes the maximum at thirty-nine stripes — "forty less one" — on the interpretive principle that the Torah's cap must not be exceeded, and therefore the punishment stops one short to preserve a margin of error. This is precisely the number Paul reports having received five times (2 Cor 11:24), making the apostle's testimony a direct witness to Second Temple synagogue judicial practice.

Rashi glosses the levirate and *ḥăliṣâ* provisions in close conversation with the Bavli. Ibn Ezra attends to the philology of *yibbûm*. Ramban develops a distinctive reading of the levirate as soul-continuation — the dead brother's "name" transmitted through the son born to the widow — a reading influential in later kabbalistic tradition. Maimonides (*Hilchot Yibbum wa-Ḥăliṣâ*) systematizes the halakhah; he prefers *yibbûm* in principle, though later poskim reverse the preference and *ḥăliṣâ* becomes the universal practice in contemporary Orthodox Judaism.

On Amalek, rabbinic tradition is extensive. The Bavli identifies Haman the Agagite as Amalekite (1 Sam 15), making Purim a delayed completion of Saul's failed extermination. Maimonides (*Hilchot Melachim* 5–6) codifies the command but notes that ancient Amalekite genealogy is unrecoverable — effectively rendering the command inoperative while preserving its liturgical memory in *Zakhor*. Sforno reads Amalek as the archetype of gratuitous cruelty. Leibowitz warned against identifying contemporary enemies as "Amalek"; Sacks treats the command as instructing Israel to oppose evil's structural form rather than to inflict violence on descendants.

Tikva Frymer-Kensky gives the fight-intervention law (vv. 11–12) close critical attention, reading its literal harshness against the rabbinic softening. Adele Berlin, Judith Hauptman, and Nehama Leibowitz all engage the levirate institution with attention to the widow's agency.

**Christian reception.** The threshing-ox verse (v. 4) takes on outsized importance in Pauline theology. Paul quotes it twice: in 1 Corinthians 9:9 to argue that apostolic ministers deserve material support, and in 1 Timothy 5:18 pairing it with Luke 10:7 ("the laborer deserves his wages") as dual scriptural warrants for paying elders. Paul's exegesis — "Is it for oxen that God is concerned? Does he not certainly speak for our sake?" — has generated centuries of interpretive debate. Augustine reads it as a straightforward Pauline pesher. Origen's allegorical method takes it as typological teaching on the support of Christian teachers.

Aquinas (*ST* I-II q. 104; Supplement on matrimony) engages both the levirate legislation (as abrogated judicial precept) and the weights legislation (as perennial natural-law principle). The levirate institution itself is treated by Christian tradition largely as a ceremonial-judicial precept of the Old Law not binding on Christians, though its memory underwrites the Sadducees' question to Jesus in Mark 12:18–27 and the Lukan parallel.

The Sadducean question ("there were seven brothers …," Mark 12:18–27) constructs a levirate-law absurdity to embarrass resurrection belief. Jesus redirects to the character of resurrection life ("they neither marry nor are given in marriage") and grounds resurrection in the God-of-Abraham formula. Jesus neither abrogates nor defends the levirate institution but subsumes the question into a larger frame.

On Amalek, the Christian tradition developed a strong typological reading. Origen and Augustine treat Amalek as a figure for sin and the fleshly disposition to be eradicated in the Christian life. Modern critical commentators — Weinfeld, Tigay, Nelson, Block, McConville, Wright — have resisted the typological displacement and treated the command as a historically concrete, ethically difficult problem. Brueggemann reads the text against the grain of triumphalism.

On the flogging and weights provisions, Christian tradition is largely convergent with Jewish tradition, reading both as perennial moral principles — proportional punishment, honest commerce — normative under the New Covenant. N. T. Wright has written on the broader shape of Deuteronomy's ethical imagination as a resource for Christian moral reflection.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Jewish and Christian traditions converge almost completely on the proportional-punishment principle (vv. 1–3), the agricultural-maxim reading (v. 4, especially as extended to human workers), and the honest-weights legislation (vv. 13–16). They diverge on the continuing force of the levirate institution: Jewish halakhah preserves it as living law (with *ḥăliṣâ* as the standard practice today); Christian tradition treats it as a ceremonial-judicial precept superseded by the resurrection-era transformation of marriage. On the fight-intervention provision (vv. 11–12), both traditions engage the literal reading with discomfort and privilege alternatives: rabbinic tradition through the Bavli's monetary-compensation reading, Christian tradition through typological readings and historical contextualization. On Amalek, both traditions face the hardest ethical problem in the chapter and have developed parallel strategies of typological or historical distancing while preserving the liturgical memory of the command. The Purim story in the Jewish calendar and the general Christian category of "spiritual warfare" testify to the command's afterlife in displaced form. The story worth telling is that Deuteronomy 25 supplies one of the few Torah texts where Paul's exegesis (threshing ox) and rabbinic halakhah (forty less one; the entire Yevamot tractate) stand on equal exegetical footing — Paul's Corinthian letter was written by a Pharisee-trained scholar working the Torah the way his Pharisaic contemporaries did.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: How should memory serve ethics in the Amalek command?** The "remember — blot out the memory — do not forget" pattern (vv. 17–19) has produced three distinct traditions of reading.

*Position A — Literal-historical extermination.* The text's plain sense commands the eradication of a specific people. Saul's failure (1 Sam 15) and Samuel's rebuke show the command's seriousness. This reading is preserved in Maimonides's codification (*Hilchot Melachim* 5–6) but disabled in practice by Maimonides's own ruling that ancient Amalekite genealogy is unrecoverable. The literal-historical reading has few modern defenders; its rhetorical force remains central to understanding the text's original trajectory.

*Position B — Liturgical-typological memory.* Jewish tradition preserves the command in the annual *Parashat Zakhor* reading before Purim. Christian tradition typologizes Amalek as sin and the flesh. Both traditions perform the command in displaced form — neither literal genocide nor simple discard, but ritualized remembrance that disciplines ethical imagination. Sacks, Fackenheim, and Wright work in this register.

*Position C — Ethics of memory and structural evil.* Leibowitz, Brueggemann, and many contemporary ethicists read the text as instructing Israel to remember *unprovoked attack on the vulnerable* as the specific form of evil to be opposed. Amalek attacked stragglers — the weak, the weary. The command's moral content, on this reading, is not the annihilation of a people but the permanent refusal to let the memory of gratuitous cruelty fade, coupled with the active opposition to its structural reproduction.

**Steelman for Position C (least intuitive for readers uncomfortable with historicizing the command).** The structural-evil reading is often dismissed as a modern domestication of an ancient bloody text. But it has a textual foundation: the motive clause of v. 18 specifies *exactly* what Amalek did — attacked the tail, the stragglers, those lagging behind, those who were faint. The text itself defines Amalek by its method, not by its bloodline. Maimonides's ruling on unrecoverable ancestry, combined with the text's own emphasis on the *method* of Amalek's offense, yields a reading in which "to blot out the memory of Amalek" means "to ensure that the world no longer remembers a people as having been permitted to attack the weak with impunity" — an ethical-historical program rather than a genealogical one.

**Where this leaves us.** The three positions layer rather than exclude. The text's original horizon included a literal-historical command (A); Jewish and Christian traditions have for two millennia preserved the command in liturgical and typological forms (B); the most defensible contemporary reading centers the command's moral content in opposition to gratuitous cruelty against the vulnerable (C). The canonical instruction "you shall not forget" seems to instruct both the memory and the trajectory toward C.

**Tension 2: Is the levirate institution protective of the widow, or instrumentalizing of her?** The *yibbûm* legislation (vv. 5–10) has been read in at least three ways.

*Position A — Widow-protection.* In an ANE economy where a widow without sons faced destitution — no inheritance share, no social protection, no path to remarriage within her husband's lineage — the levirate institution provided continuity of economic support and genealogical place. Block, Wright, and Tigay read the law in this protective key.

*Position B — Dynastic preservation.* The stated motive — "that his name may not be blotted out of Israel" (v. 6) — centers the dead brother's lineage, not the living widow's welfare. The law functions to preserve patrilineal continuity; the widow is the instrument. Ramban's metempsychotic reading pushes in this direction.

*Position C — Woman's agency embedded in patriarchal form.* The *ḥăliṣâ* ceremony (vv. 7–10) places the widow in the position of initiating legal action against her brother-in-law, speaking the formal accusation, performing the shaming ritual. This is a rare case in Torah legislation where a woman is scripted as the legal actor. Frymer-Kensky, Hauptman, and Leibowitz read the institution as giving the widow real if constrained agency — she can compel either the marriage-or-release, and the release ritual publicly shames the refusing brother.

**Steelman for Position C (least intuitive in either protective or instrumentalizing framings).** The widow-agency reading is often overlooked because readers focus on the marriage-obligation side rather than the release side. But the text gives most of its verses (vv. 7–10) to the release ceremony, in which the widow is the sole active agent. She "goes up to the gate," "says to the elders," "goes up to him in the presence of the elders," "pulls his sandal off his foot," "spits in his face," "answers and says." The brother-in-law is the object of her action, not the subject. For a woman in an Iron Age legal environment to be scripted as the speaking, acting, publicly shaming agent is not nothing. Ruth's narrative exploits precisely this opening: Ruth and Naomi use the levirate-adjacent institutions to secure their own future. The institution is patriarchal in structure and genuinely empowering in the narrow window it opens.

**Where this leaves us.** The institution is protective of the widow's material welfare (A), instrumental to patrilineal continuity (B), and — in its release-ceremony half — a scripted opening for the widow's public legal agency (C). All three dimensions are textually grounded; reading only one is a flattening.

**Unresolved question.** The chapter opens with a humanitarian cap on judicial flogging (vv. 1–3) and closes with a command to blot out the memory of Amalek (vv. 17–19). The same text both refuses to degrade a guilty brother with excessive stripes and commands the eradication of an enemy people's memory. Is the chapter's internal logic that *proportional* justice is the norm and *unlimited* memory the exception — or that *some* enemies stand outside the economy of proportionality altogether? If the former, what conditions produce such an exception; if the latter, how is the category "Amalek" not infinitely expandable?

## The Thread

Four cross-references worth carrying with this chapter:

1. **Deut 25:3 and 2 Corinthians 11:24.** Paul's "five times I received forty lashes less one" is the direct testimony to synagogue judicial practice under Mishnah *Makkot* — the rabbinic refinement of the Deuteronomic cap from forty to thirty-nine to preserve a margin. Paul's autobiographical detail is a window into how Deuteronomy 25:3 was operationalized in the first-century Diaspora synagogue.

2. **Deut 25:4 and 1 Corinthians 9:9 / 1 Timothy 5:18.** The threshing-ox verse becomes Paul's scriptural warrant for apostolic-minister support. The interpretive move — "is it for oxen that God is concerned?" — is often read as Paul allegorizing, but it may better be read as Paul applying the standard rabbinic *qal waḥomer* (light and heavy) inference: if Torah extends humanitarian consideration even to a threshing animal, how much more to the human laborer. The verse's Pauline afterlife is one of the most concrete examples of Torah-grounded Christian ethics in the New Testament.

3. **Deut 25:5–10 and Ruth 4 / Matthew 22:23–33.** The levirate legislation undergirds the Ruth narrative's kinsman-redeemer arc (Boaz as *gōʾēl*, the sandal-gesture of Ruth 4:7 explicitly invoking this chapter) and funds the Sadducees' trick question to Jesus about the seven brothers. Both receptions — narrative exploitation of the institution's openings (Ruth) and philosophical absurdity constructed from its provisions (the Sadducees) — demonstrate how deeply the institution shaped the imagination of Second Temple and rabbinic Judaism.

4. **Deut 25:17–19 and 1 Samuel 15 / Esther / Exodus 17:8–16.** The Amalek command forms a canonical arc: the wilderness attack (Exod 17), the Mosaic memorialization (Deut 25), Saul's partial obedience and prophetic rebuke (1 Sam 15), the Agagite Haman of the Esther narrative, and the Purim festival's annual *Parashat Zakhor* reading. The command does not rest in a single moment but generates a structure of remembered injustice that runs through the Jewish liturgical year and shadows the political history of the canon. The ethical weight of the command is carried not by its original-horizon literalism but by its canonical afterlife as a discipline of moral memory.
