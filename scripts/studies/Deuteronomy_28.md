# Deuteronomy 28: The Longest Chapter of Blessings and Curses

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Deuteronomy 28 is the longest chapter in the book and one of the longest in the Torah. It operates as the liturgical and theological completion of the covenant ratification ceremony begun in chapter 27. Moses, still speaking from the plains of Moab, delivers an extended poetic oration that reads as a prospective history of the nation: if Israel obeys, these blessings; if not, these curses. The formal imbalance is striking — fourteen verses of blessing, fifty-four verses of curse. The chapter is longer than most prophetic books.

The setting remains pre-Jordan; the audience is the generation Moses has catechized since chapter 5, now about to cross into the land. The book's Hebrew name *Dĕbārîm* (דְּבָרִים, "words") is especially apt here: what Moses pronounces in chapter 28 is, in the deepest sense, words that the coming centuries will inhabit.

The most important comparative-historical datum is the close formal and verbal parallel between Deuteronomy 28 and Neo-Assyrian treaty-curse literature, especially the *Vassal Treaty of Esarhaddon* (VTE, or *Esarhaddon Succession Treaty*), dated 672 BCE. D. J. Wiseman published the tablets in 1958; Simo Parpola's *State Archives of Assyria II* (1988) produced the authoritative critical edition. The treaty bound Esarhaddon's vassals (including Manasseh of Judah, per 2 Chronicles 33:11) to loyalty to Ashurbanipal. Its curse section runs for hundreds of lines and moves through themes of fertility loss, pestilence, siege cannibalism, military defeat, and expulsion. Moshe Weinfeld's *Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School* (1972) and *Deuteronomy 1-11* (AB) catalogued the parallels in detail.

Among the most cited pairings: Deuteronomy 28:23 ("the heavens over your head shall be bronze, the earth under you iron") matches VTE §§63-64 closely. Deuteronomy 28:26 (corpses as food for birds and beasts) parallels VTE §41. Deuteronomy 28:27 (boils, tumors, scabs) echoes the Assyrian plague-god curses. Deuteronomy 28:29 ("grope at noonday") = VTE §40. Deuteronomy 28:30 (betrothed raped, house built not inhabited, vineyard not enjoyed) = VTE §§41-42. Deuteronomy 28:32 (sons and daughters given to another people) = VTE §45. Deuteronomy 28:53-57 (siege cannibalism of own children) parallels Assyrian treaty language of siege horror.

Weinfeld's thesis, developed with Hans Ulrich Steymans and Bernard Levinson, is that Deuteronomy 28 was redacted under Manasseh or early Josiah, deliberately recasting the Assyrian vassal oath so that Israel's loyalty is owed to YHWH rather than to the Assyrian king. The curses Assyria threatened against disloyalty to empire are turned against disloyalty to covenant. The political subversion is radical: the form of imperial coercion is appropriated and redirected against empire itself. This reading has become near-consensus in critical scholarship.

The conservative response does not deny the parallels but disputes the implied dating. Kenneth Kitchen, in *On the Reliability of the Old Testament*, argues that treaty-curse formulae are attested across the second millennium BCE — Hittite treaties, Ugaritic curses, Middle Assyrian texts — and that the similarity to VTE reflects a broad ANE genre rather than a specific literary dependence. Delbert Hillers's *Treaty-Curses and the Old Testament Prophets* (1964) documented the wider curse tradition and served as foundational reference. J. Gordon McConville (*Apollos Deuteronomy*) and Daniel Block (*NIVAC*) argue that second-millennium parallels are genuine and the chapter need not be seventh-century. The debate turns not on whether parallels exist but on whether the specific density of parallels with VTE requires a seventh-century redaction.

Archaeologically, the horror of the siege cannibalism passage (vv. 53-57) has referents in Israel's own history. The siege of Samaria in 2 Kings 6:24-31 records mothers eating their children; Lamentations 2:20 and 4:10 lament the same horror in the fall of Jerusalem 586 BCE. Josephus's *Jewish War* 6.201-213 recounts the same horror during Titus's siege of Jerusalem in 70 CE (the story of Mary of Bethezuba eating her infant son). The curse was not hypothetical in Israel's memory.

Three or four Hebrew terms anchor the chapter: *bārûk* (בָּרוּךְ, "blessed," the opening participle of the blessing catalog); *ʾārûr* (אָרוּר, "cursed," opening each curse); *šāmaʿ bĕqôl* (שָׁמַע בְּקוֹל, "obey the voice," literally "hear in the voice of," v. 1), the distinctive Deuteronomic formula for covenant fidelity; and *haddĕbārîm hāʾēlleh* (הַדְּבָרִים הָאֵלֶּה, "these words," v. 14), marking the torah-word as the object of obedience.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter falls into two main sections: the blessings (vv. 1-14) and the curses (vv. 15-68), with an internal structure within the curses that moves from short formal curses mirroring the blessings (vv. 15-19) through escalating disasters (vv. 20-57) to a final climactic reversal of the Exodus (vv. 58-68).

The blessings are compact and architectural. Verses 3-6 form a six-fold chiasm: in the city, in the field; fruit of womb and ground and cattle; basket and kneading bowl; coming in and going out. Verse 7 promises military victory in the iconic formula "flee from you seven ways." Verses 8-14 elaborate into covenant privilege: firstborn status among the nations, rain in season, head-not-tail.

The curses reverse the blessings in precise mirror (vv. 16-19), then break the mirror. From verse 20 onward, the curses escalate in length, intensity, and horror. The rhetorical design is calibrated to overwhelm. Moshe Weinfeld observed that the curses cluster into five thematic waves: disease and drought (vv. 20-24); military defeat and shame (vv. 25-37); agricultural ruin and invasion (vv. 38-48); foreign siege with siege cannibalism (vv. 49-57); and the final reversal — plagues like Egypt, scattering among the nations, return to Egypt "by the way I said you should not see again" (vv. 58-68).

The final reversal is the chapter's theological climax. The Exodus, the foundational act of Israelite identity, is *undone*. Egypt, the house of bondage, becomes the destination of exile: "And the LORD will bring you back in ships to Egypt, a journey that I promised that you should never make again; and there you shall offer yourselves for sale to your enemies as male and female slaves, but there will be no buyer" (v. 68). The horror is not only that the people will be enslaved but that no one will even want to buy them. The Exodus run in reverse, without even the dignity of being worth enslaving.

Literary devices include catalog (the six-fold blessing, the mirroring curse), anaphora ("cursed shall you be"), climactic intensification (each wave of curse more devastating than the last), and inverse parallel to the Exodus. The siege-cannibalism passage (vv. 53-57) is the chapter's most extraordinary literary moment: it describes "the most refined and delicate man" and "the most refined and delicate woman" — the citizens least capable of violence — driven by starvation to hide their own children's flesh from their nearest relatives. The passage does what prophecy at its most potent does: it imagines a specific human scene of moral degradation so vivid that argument becomes unnecessary.

Textual variants are generally minor. 4QDeut^c and related Qumran fragments preserve substantial sections of chapter 28 and largely confirm MT. The LXX makes occasional small expansions. One notable variant: verse 22 in some manuscripts of the LXX adds an extra curse category, reflecting ongoing textual fluidity in the catalog.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Jewish Reception

**Second Temple.** Jewish communities read Deuteronomy 28 not as hypothetical but as descriptive of their experience. The Deuteronomistic History (Joshua-Kings) narrates Israel's decline as a fulfillment of 28's curses; 2 Kings 17's account of the Northern Kingdom's fall and 2 Kings 25's account of Jerusalem's destruction both implicitly operate under the chapter's schema. Josephus in *Jewish War* 5-6 explicitly reads Titus's siege of Jerusalem through Deuteronomy 28:49-57. The *Temple Scroll* (11Q19) reworks the blessings and curses within its own covenantal program. The *Damascus Document* and related sectarian texts read themselves as the faithful remnant within a broader nation under curse.

**Rabbinic.** *Mishnah Sotah 7:8* records the reading of the blessings and curses as part of the *Hakhel* ceremony (the septennial reading of the Torah at Sukkot). The *Talmud Bavli Megillah* and *Berakhot* treat the chapter as liturgically heavy — many communities read the *tokhachah* (תּוֹכָחָה, "rebuke," the rabbinic term for the curses passage) in a lower voice, or at a faster pace, or by the Torah reader himself (not by an *oleh* called to it) out of superstition about taking the curses on oneself. *Sifre Deuteronomy* works line by line. *Devarim Rabbah* devotes several sections to the curses. Rashi treats each curse with both plain-sense gloss and midrashic expansion. Ibn Ezra is more sober and philological. Ramban reads the curses as prophetic — descriptive of the two exiles (Babylonian and Roman) rather than merely threatened. Maimonides in *Hilchot Melachim* works the blessings into his account of the messianic future; in *Guide* 3.36-39 he treats the curses as pedagogy against idolatry. Sforno follows Ramban in reading the chapter prophetically. The *tokhachah* holds a unique position in rabbinic liturgy: read but feared, proclaimed but lowered.

**Modern.** Jeffrey Tigay's *JPS Torah Commentary* and Weinfeld's *AB Deuteronomy* give the most detailed contemporary Jewish treatments, both with extensive comparative material. Nechama Leibowitz's *Studies in Devarim* surveys the classical commentators. Yeshayahu Leibowitz reads the blessings and curses as categorical rather than conditional — covenant fidelity is its own content, not a means to material outcomes. Jon Levenson's *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* treats the curses within a theology of covenant contingency. Michael Fishbane's *Biblical Interpretation in Ancient Israel* examines how later prophetic texts reread chapter 28.

**Post-Holocaust.** Here the chapter becomes agonizing. Can 28:49-68 — "a nation from far, whose language you do not understand... scatter you among all peoples" — be read as predicting the Shoah? Richard Rubenstein's *After Auschwitz* refuses covenantal readings: the curses framework cannot be extended to the industrial murder of six million without making God the author of the death camps. Emil Fackenheim's "614th commandment" ("not to give Hitler posthumous victories") reframes Jewish post-Holocaust obligation without covenantal causation. Irving Greenberg's dictum — "no statement should be made that would not be credible in the presence of the burning children" — sets the standard. Eliezer Berkovits, Jon Levenson, Heschel's later work, and David Hartman each negotiate the tension differently. The chapter cannot be read innocently by any contemporary Jewish reader.

### Christian Reception

**Patristic.** Origen's *Homilies on Deuteronomy* treats the curses allegorically, reading them as the fate of the unrepentant soul. Augustine's *City of God* 16-17 takes the curses as historically fulfilled in Israel's fall and as warnings to the church. Chrysostom's *Adversus Judaeos* (a troubling text by contemporary standards) invokes Deuteronomy 28 as evidence of Israel's displacement — this supersessionist reading became deeply embedded in Christian Jewish-polemic and has consequences that Christian theology is still working to repent of. Cyril of Alexandria's *Glaphyra* reads the curses typologically.

**Medieval.** Aquinas, *ST* I-II q. 102-105, treats the blessings and curses within his account of the divine pedagogy of the ceremonial and judicial precepts. Bernard of Clairvaux preaches occasionally on the curses as moral pedagogy. Nicholas of Lyra, following his Jewish sources, treats the chapter with more historical rigor than most medievals.

**Reformation.** Luther's 1525 *Lectures on Deuteronomy* reads the blessings and curses as law's two voices — prosperity and judgment — and locates the gospel outside the chapter's framework. His later *Table Talk* uses the curses as a picture of what life apart from Christ looks like. Luther's troubling late treatises on the Jews appeal to Deuteronomy 28 in ways Christian theology has now decisively repudiated. Calvin's *Commentary on Deuteronomy* is one of his most extensive; he works through the curses with sustained theological reflection on covenant contingency, divine providence, and the use of the law. Calvin insists that the curses are not mere threats but warnings through which God calls the covenant people to faithfulness. Reformed federal theology (Westminster Confession, Johannes Cocceius) reads Deuteronomy 27-28 as the paradigmatic statement of the "covenant of works" — a reading that frames the covenant of grace in Christ.

**Critical.** De Wette, Wellhausen, Weinfeld, Hillers, and Wiseman are the comparative-historical reference points. Richard Nelson (*OTL*) treats the chapter as redacted over multiple stages. Thomas Römer and Konrad Schmid situate it within the Deuteronomistic History. Bernard Levinson's work on Deuteronomy's "legal revision" applies to the treaty-form arguments.

**Contemporary.** Daniel Block (*NIVAC*), J. Gordon McConville (Apollos), Christopher Wright, Peter Craigie (*NICOT*), Duane Christensen (*WBC*), Walter Brueggemann (*AOTC*), Dennis Olson (*Death of Moses*), and Patrick Miller all give extended treatment. Brueggemann reads the chapter as Israel's honest self-reckoning: covenant is real, and the costs of breach are catastrophic. Olson frames the curses pedagogically in his generational reading. N. T. Wright's *The Climax of the Covenant* and *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* treat Deuteronomy 27-30 as the sustained storyline within which Paul works: Israel is in curse-as-exile, Messiah bears the curse, the blessing of Abraham flows to the nations.

### Where They Converge and Diverge

Both traditions read Deuteronomy 28 as covenant realism. Both see the curses as describing historical catastrophe rather than abstract threat. Both struggle with the chapter's severity. Christianity's historical appropriation of the curses as supersessionist polemic — as evidence that Israel has been displaced — is among the darkest chapters of Christian exegesis; that Christian theology is learning to repent of this reading is one of the most important developments of the late twentieth and twenty-first centuries. The Jewish reading, rabbinically attentive to the chapter as *tokhachah* to be read with trembling, preserves what Christianity often obscured: the curses fall within the covenant, not outside it. Return is always possible (chapter 30). The Pauline reading, especially as N. T. Wright has recovered it, moves in the same direction: Christ does not supersede Israel; he bears Israel's curse so that the blessing may flow. The contemporary convergence, after much Christian repentance, is closer to the rabbinic reading: covenant and curse belong to the same covenant, and any theology that severs them distorts both.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

### Tension 1: Does Deuteronomy 28 Predict or Describe?

The chapter is formally conditional ("if you obey... if you do not obey"), but its reception has consistently treated it as predictive. Does the chapter foretell what will happen to Israel, or does it articulate the covenantal logic within which Israel's history will be legible?

**Position A: Predictive.** Ramban, Sforno, much rabbinic tradition, and a strand of contemporary dispensational Christian reading: the chapter is prophecy. The curses describe the Babylonian exile, the Roman destruction, the medieval pogroms, and, for some, modern catastrophes. The conditional form is God's way of announcing a future; the conditions will be broken, and the curses will fall.

**Position B: Descriptive of covenant logic, not events.** Most contemporary critical scholarship, much modern Jewish thought (Leibowitz, Levinas in another register), and a strand of Protestant reading: the chapter articulates what covenant contingency means. It does not predict specific events but explains the grammar by which later events become intelligible. 586 BCE and 70 CE become Deuteronomy 28 events retrospectively; the chapter is a hermeneutical frame, not a script.

**Position C (steelman for the least intuitive):** The chapter is pedagogy of the possible, not prediction and not description. Walter Brueggemann, Patrick Miller, Dennis Olson: Deuteronomy 28 opens imagination for what covenant fidelity and infidelity *could* mean — in material, political, bodily, communal terms. Its function is neither foretelling nor merely interpretive; it is formative. By hearing the blessings and curses, Israel learns what stakes the covenant has. The chapter is pastoral theology before it is historical theology.

**Where this leaves us:** The Shoah has made position A almost impossible to hold without theological violence. Rubenstein, Fackenheim, and Greenberg have rightly closed the door on reading the death camps as curse-fulfillment. Positions B and C preserve the chapter's seriousness while relinquishing the claim that the particular horrors of Jewish history are divine enactments of Deuteronomy 28. The chapter remains witness to the covenant's contingency without becoming warrant for any particular theological verdict on any particular catastrophe.

### Tension 2: The Moral Asymmetry of Blessing and Curse

Fourteen verses of blessing, fifty-four verses of curse. Why the imbalance?

**Position A: The asymmetry reflects covenant realism.** Weinfeld, Brueggemann, McConville: ancient treaties typically had imbalanced curse-to-blessing ratios because the rhetorical weight of the vassal contract fell on compliance enforcement. Deuteronomy 28 inherits the form; the imbalance is generic, not theological.

**Position B: The asymmetry reflects the human condition.** Luther, Calvin, and much Reformation reading: the extended curses reflect the covenant's honest assessment of human incapacity. Blessing is what faithful obedience yields; the reality is that obedience will not be sustained, so the chapter gives more space to what will actually happen. The curses are longer because they are more likely.

**Position C (steelman):** The asymmetry is pedagogical and is calibrated to a particular audience. Daniel Block, Dennis Olson: the chapter is addressed to people about to enter the land; they have just experienced forty years of wilderness-curse and one generation of destruction. They need to know both the gravity of breach and the possibility of blessing. The curses are longer because the audience needs to be warned; the blessings are shorter because they are obvious. In a different audience — say, the exiles in Babylon after 586 BCE — the chapter's proportions would be reversed. The proportions are rhetorical, not metaphysical.

**Where this leaves us:** The asymmetry is real and has been variously accounted for; each account captures something. What the chapter resists is a symmetric reading in which blessing and curse are equal alternatives. The curse is the shadow that makes the blessing legible; the blessing is the possibility the curse warns against foreclosing. The proportions say something about human life under covenant: the possibility of breach is always larger than the possibility of fidelity, but fidelity remains genuinely possible.

### Unresolved Question

What is the theological status of the chapter's descriptive force after Auschwitz? If Deuteronomy 28:49-68 describes being scattered among the nations, hunted, starving, children killed — and if the Shoah enacted these realities with industrial precision against a people the covenant declared beloved — then the chapter cannot be read the same way again. Rubenstein refuses covenantal reading; Fackenheim rebuilds obligation without covenantal causation; Greenberg insists on the presence of the burning children as the hermeneutical filter. Christianity, with its long history of supersessionist appropriation of these curses, faces the additional task of repenting the use to which it put this chapter. No stable theological consensus has emerged. What stands is the need for any reader to hold the chapter's words in the presence of the actual suffering those words seem to describe, and to refuse any reading that trades on that suffering for theological advantage.

## The Thread

- **Leviticus 26** — the other great covenant-blessing-and-curse passage, structured similarly but more compact and ending with explicit promise of return after repentance ("I will remember my covenant with Jacob"). Deuteronomy 28 escalates Leviticus 26's structure but leaves the return explicit only in chapter 30. The relationship between the two chapters is one of the key questions of Pentateuch source-criticism: Leviticus 26 is usually attributed to H (the Holiness Code) and Deuteronomy 28 to D, and their interdependence is debated. Read together, they form the Pentateuch's sustained covenantal warning.
- **Lamentations 2:20 and 4:10** — "Should women eat the fruit of their womb, the children of their tender care?" ... "The hands of compassionate women have boiled their own children." The book of Lamentations is, in significant measure, Deuteronomy 28:53-57 sung as dirge. The liturgical function of Lamentations is to hold together the curse-description and the people's voice — to lament without explaining. The book does not say the curse was unjust; neither does it say the suffering was proportionate. It stays in the space where Deuteronomy 28 leaves the question open.
- **Galatians 3:10-14** — Paul's argument that "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law, having become a curse for us" (citing Deuteronomy 21:23) draws Deuteronomy 28 into its frame through 27:26. For N. T. Wright, Paul reads all of Deuteronomy 27-30 as a sustained storyline: Israel is in curse-as-exile (Deuteronomy 28:64-65), the Messiah bears the curse, the blessing of Abraham flows to the nations. The cross becomes a Deuteronomy 28 event interpreted through the Servant Songs.
- **Luke 19:41-44 and 21:20-24** — Jesus's lament over Jerusalem and prophecy of the siege draw directly on Deuteronomy 28:49-57's imagery: a nation from far, encircled, children dashed, not one stone left on another. The destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE becomes, in the Gospels' framing, a Deuteronomy 28 event — and, significantly, the lament is in Jesus's mouth: "Would that you, even you, had known on this day the things that make for peace!" The weeping of the prophet over the curse he describes is already present in the text of the Gospels.
