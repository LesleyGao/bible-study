# Matthew 21: The King Enters, the Temple Trembles

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Matthew 21 places Jesus on a topographically and politically charged ascent. Bethphage ("house of unripe figs") and Bethany sat on the eastern slope of the Mount of Olives, roughly two miles east of Jerusalem along the pilgrim road from Jericho. Pilgrims approaching for Passover would have crested the Mount of Olives — the same ridge from which Zechariah 14:4 prophesied that YHWH would one day stand to judge the nations — and seen the Herodian Temple complex blaze white and gold across the Kidron Valley. Josephus (*Wars* 5.5.6) describes the Temple Mount, Herod the Great's massive expansion completed only decades before Jesus's entry, as the largest sacred precinct in the Greco-Roman world: a thirty-five-acre platform built atop retaining walls of dressed limestone ashlars, some weighing over five hundred tons.

Archaeology grounds the scene with unusual precision. **Robinson's Arch**, the southwestern monumental staircase identified by Edward Robinson in 1838 and excavated by Benjamin Mazar in the 1960s and 70s, marks the principal pilgrim entrance to the Royal Stoa — exactly the southern Temple precinct where commercial activity (money-changing, sale of approved sacrificial animals) is most plausibly located. The **Western Wall** tunnels reveal the herringbone Herodian masonry that the disciples will admire two chapters later (24:1). Excavations along the southern wall have recovered piles of fallen stones from the 70 CE destruction lying as Roman legionaries cast them down — the visual confirmation of Jesus's prediction. Iron Age **rolling-stone tombs** along the Mount of Olives, including the so-called "Tomb of the Prophets" complex, evoke the burial landscape Jesus will invoke in 23:29.

The **money-changers** (κολλυβισταί, *kollybistai*) served a real ritual need: Tyrian shekels — required for the half-shekel Temple tax (Exod 30:13) because of their stable silver content — had to be exchanged from Roman, Greek, and provincial currencies, with a commission. The **pigeon-sellers** (πωλοῦντες τὰς περιστεράς) supplied the offering of the poor (Lev 12:8). Jesus's protest is not against commerce *per se* but against where and how it functioned within sacred geography — and, on the most plausible historical reading, against a system in which the high-priestly families (Annas and Caiaphas) drew revenue from concessions inside YHWH's house.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter. ***Hosanna*** (ὡσαννά, transliterating Hebrew-Aramaic *hôšîʿâ-nāʾ*, "save now!") from Psalm 118:25 was a Hallel acclamation sung at Sukkot and Passover. ***Praÿs*** (πραΰς, "humble/meek") in the Zechariah citation echoes Numbers 12:3's description of Moses. ***Hierón*** (ἱερόν), the broader Temple precinct including the Court of the Gentiles, must be distinguished from ***naós*** (ναός), the inner sanctuary where only priests entered — Jesus's protest occurs in the *hierón*, not the *naós*. ***Lēstēs*** (λῃστής, "robber/insurgent"), the word translated "robber" in Jeremiah 7:11's "den of robbers," is the same term Josephus uses for the violent revolutionaries who would seize the Temple in 66 CE. ***Akrogōniaios*** (ἀκρογωνιαῖος), the LXX rendering of Psalm 118:22's "cornerstone," signals the Christological capstone of the Wicked Tenants parable.

The political-religious factions are all present or implied. **Sadducees**, the priestly aristocracy, controlled Temple operations. **Pharisees** (perhaps two thousand strong in Jesus's day, per Josephus *Antiquities* 17.2.4) operated more in synagogues and lay piety; their direct confrontation with Jesus intensifies in chapters 22-23. **Herodians** appear in chapter 22. Behind it all is **Pontius Pilate**, prefect 26-36 CE, who would normally come up from Caesarea Maritima for Passover and station auxiliary cohorts at the Antonia Fortress on the Temple's northwest corner — meaning Roman troops were watching this triumphal entry from the parapet.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Matthew 21 opens the third major narrative block of the gospel — the **Jerusalem Passion narrative** — and stands between the fourth Matthean discourse (the Community/Church discourse of ch. 18) and the fifth (the Olivet Discourse of chs. 24-25). On B. W. Bacon's classic five-discourse hypothesis (*Studies in Matthew*, 1930), Matthew structures Jesus's ministry as five teaching blocks each closed by the formula "when Jesus had finished" — deliberately echoing the Pentateuch and presenting Jesus as a new Moses. By chapter 21 the fourth discourse has closed (19:1), the journey to Jerusalem is complete, and Matthew is positioning the reader for the climactic fifth discourse on the Mount of Olives.

The chapter is structured as a **triptych of confrontations** framed by two prophetic-symbolic acts. The triumphal entry (vv. 1-11) and the temple cleansing (vv. 12-17) are paired enacted prophecies; the cursed fig tree (vv. 18-22) functions as the bridge. Then come three controversies: the authority question (vv. 23-27), the parable of the two sons (vv. 28-32), and the parable of the wicked tenants (vv. 33-46). The unifying motif is **fruit** — what the king demands, what the fig tree fails to produce, what the tax collectors and prostitutes do produce, what the wicked tenants withhold, what the new tenants (or "people," ἔθνος, v. 43) will yield. Davies and Allison's ICC volume 3 traces this *karpos* motif as the chapter's organizing key.

Matthew's redactional choices repay attention. Most famously, where Mark 11:7 has Jesus mounted on a single colt, Matthew supplies **two animals** — donkey and colt — and reports that "he sat on them" (ἐπεκάθισεν ἐπάνω αὐτῶν, v. 7). Critics from Strauss onward have charged Matthew with naïvely literalizing the Hebrew parallelism of Zechariah 9:9 ("on a donkey, even on a colt, the foal of a donkey") into two animals. France (NICNT) and Davies/Allison defend a more sophisticated reading: Matthew's "on them" plausibly refers to the cloaks, not both animals, and the doubling preserves a colt unbroken-to-the-saddle (cf. Mark 11:2's "on which no one has ever sat") accompanied by its mother. Either way, Matthew is reading Zechariah with rabbinic precision.

The fig tree pericope shows another distinctive Matthean technique. Mark **intercalates** the cursing (11:12-14) and the discovery of withering (11:20-21) around the temple cleansing, a sandwich structure that interprets the cleansing as judgment on the fruitless temple. Matthew **collapses** the intercalation and reports immediate withering (v. 19) — sacrificing Mark's narrative subtlety for visual impact and pedagogical clarity. Hagner (WBC) reads this as Matthew's catechetical orientation: the lesson on faith and prayer that follows lands harder when the miracle is instantaneous.

The parable of the **two sons** (vv. 28-32) is uniquely Matthean and exhibits a notoriously vexed textual tradition — manuscripts disagree on which son represents the obedient one, suggesting early scribes felt the polemical force and tried to soften it. The Nestle-Aland critical text follows Sinaiticus and Vaticanus: the first son refuses but goes; the second agrees but doesn't. Western witnesses (Bezae, Old Latin) reverse the order. The point is identical in either direction: religious profession without action loses to apparent rebellion that ends in obedience.

The parable of the **wicked tenants** (vv. 33-46) draws explicitly on **Isaiah 5:1-7**'s vineyard song — the audience would have recognized the trope immediately — but with crucial alterations. Where Isaiah's vineyard *itself* is judged for yielding wild grapes, Matthew's vineyard is intact; only the **tenants** are condemned. Verse 43's "the kingdom of God will be taken away from you and given to a people [ἔθνος] producing its fruits" launches one of the most contested verses in Matthew, to which the third lens returns. The chapter ends with the chief priests and Pharisees perceiving that Jesus speaks against them but unable to act because of the crowd — a narrative tension that Matthew sustains through chapter 26.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Matthew 21 sits at the literary hinge of the gospel and at the historical hinge of the Jewish-Christian story. It demands a side-by-side reading of how Jewish and Christian interpretive traditions have handled Matthew's increasingly polemical portrait of Jewish leadership.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** **Geza Vermes** (*Jesus the Jew*, 1973) reads the triumphal entry as a recognizably Jewish prophetic-symbolic act of the kind performed by figures like Theudas or the Egyptian prophet Josephus describes — a messianic claim staged in the language of Zechariah, perfectly intelligible within first-century Jewish messianism. **E. P. Sanders** (*Jesus and Judaism*, 1985) famously argued that Jesus's **temple action** was the historical trigger for his execution: not a "cleansing" implying the Temple was salvageable but a prophetic enactment of its destruction. Sanders rejects the older Christian reading that Jesus opposed Temple Judaism *in principle*; rather, Jesus's action prophesied judgment in the manner of Jeremiah, whose own Temple sermon (Jer 7) supplies Matthew's "den of robbers" citation.

**Daniel Boyarin** (*Border Lines*, 2004; *The Jewish Gospels*, 2012) argues that the messianic categories Matthew applies to Jesus — the suffering Son of Man, the Davidic king arriving in Zechariah's image — were *already* fully Jewish theological categories before Christianity diverged from rabbinic Judaism. The triumphal entry is not Christianity over against Judaism; it is one Jewish messianic claim among others.

**Amy-Jill Levine** (*The Misunderstood Jew*, 2006; *Short Stories by Jesus*, 2014; co-editor *Jewish Annotated New Testament*) is foundational for reading Matthew 21 responsibly. Levine warns that the wicked tenants parable, especially v. 43, has been weaponized in Christian preaching as a wholesale supersessionist text — God taking the kingdom from "the Jews" and giving it to "the Church." She insists this reading distorts Matthew's intra-Jewish polemic. The targets in v. 45 are specifically "the chief priests and the Pharisees," not Israel as such; the ἔθνος of v. 43 is grammatically singular and may refer to a renewed Israel, not an ethnically Gentile church.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** The **patristic** reading of v. 43 trends supersessionist almost from the start. **Origen**'s *Commentary on Matthew* and **Chrysostom**'s *90 Homilies on Matthew* (Hom. 68) read the parable as judgment on Jewish unbelief and the transfer of election to the Gentile church; Chrysostom's homilies, brilliant as exegesis, also contain some of the patristic era's most rhetorically violent anti-Jewish passages. **Jerome** and **Augustine** continue the trajectory, though Augustine's *Tractates* are more nuanced about the ongoing role of Israel. **Aquinas**'s *Catena Aurea* compiles the patristic chorus.

The **Reformers** sharpen the polemic. **Calvin**'s *Harmony of the Evangelists* reads v. 43 as a definitive transfer of the covenant; **Luther**'s late writings, especially *On the Jews and Their Lies* (1543), draw on Matthew 21-23 in ways that the Lutheran World Federation has officially repudiated.

The **twentieth-century turn** comes after the Holocaust. **Rosemary Radford Ruether**'s *Faith and Fratricide* (1974) traced how the patristic and medieval reading of texts like Matthew 21:43 helped construct the theological infrastructure of Christian anti-Judaism. The Catholic response — **Vatican II's *Nostra Aetate*** (1965) and the **Pontifical Biblical Commission's 2001 *The Jewish People and Their Sacred Scriptures in the Christian Bible*** — explicitly rejected the supersessionist reading of Romans 9-11 and, by extension, the wholesale anti-Jewish use of Matthew. **Mary C. Boys** (*Has God Only One Blessing?*, 2000) and **John Pawlikowski** continue the constructive work.

Among major modern commentators, **Davies and Allison** (ICC vol. 3) carefully document the supersessionist reading's textual basis while resisting its broader anti-Jewish application. **Ulrich Luz** (*Hermeneia*, vol. 3) — uniquely among major commentaries — devotes extensive attention to *Wirkungsgeschichte* (reception history), tracing how Matthew 21 has been preached, painted, and politicized across two millennia. **R. T. France** (NICNT) and **Craig Keener** (NICNT) argue for a more limited reading: the kingdom is taken from the *current leadership* and given to those (Jewish and Gentile) who produce its fruits. **Donald Hagner** (WBC), **John Nolland** (NIGTC), and **Daniel Harrington** (Sacra Pagina) round out the contemporary scholarly consensus that the parable functions as intra-Jewish prophetic critique, not Christian replacement theology.

**N. T. Wright** (*Jesus and the Victory of God*, 1996; *Matthew for Everyone*) reads the entire Jerusalem sequence — entry, temple action, fig tree, parables — as Jesus enacting the role of YHWH returning to Zion and the Temple after the long exile, and finding the house unprepared. The fig tree, on Wright's reading, is an enacted parable of the Temple's failure to bear fruit; the wicked tenants parable announces the same judgment in narrative form. Wright's preterist-leaning reading of the Olivet Discourse two chapters later flows directly from the framework set up in chapter 21.

**Anthony Saldarini** (*Matthew's Christian-Jewish Community*, 1994) provides the indispensable historical sociology: Matthew's community was a Jewish-Christian sect in painful competition with the emerging rabbinic movement at Yavneh after 70 CE. Read in that context, Matthew 21's polemic is not Christianity attacking Judaism from outside but one Jewish faction making a theological case against another for the future shape of post-Temple Israel.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Did Jesus mean to abolish the Temple system or reform it?** The temple action is the most contested symbolic act in the gospels. Three positions deserve steelmanning. **(1) Reform reading** — held by E. P. Sanders's earlier critics and many evangelical commentators including Carson and Keener — argues Jesus's quotation of Isaiah 56:7 ("a house of prayer for all nations") implies the Temple's true purpose, against which the present commercial activity is contrasted. The Temple itself remains valid; its corruption requires correction. **(2) Prophetic-destruction reading** — Sanders, Wright, France — argues the action *enacts* the Temple's coming destruction in the manner of Jeremiah's smashed flask (Jer 19); Jesus is not cleaning the Temple but condemning it. **(3) Replacement reading** — older Reformed and dispensational readings — argues Jesus signals the Temple's permanent obsolescence, replaced by his own body (cf. John 2:19-21) as the locus of divine presence. Each position can cite the text. The Isaiah quotation supports reform; the parallel cursing of the fig tree supports prophetic destruction; Matthew's later "your house is left to you desolate" (23:38) and the Olivet prediction support replacement. **Where this leaves us:** the action operates on multiple registers simultaneously — protest, prophecy, and inauguration — and any single reading flattens what Matthew presents as theologically dense symbolic action. The Isaiah-Jeremiah pairing in v. 13 itself signals layered meaning: the Temple is what it ought to be (prayer for nations) and what it has become (a robbers' den), and judgment falls on the gap.

**Tension 2: Does v. 43 teach supersessionism — that God has rejected Israel and replaced it with the Church?** Three positions. **(1) Hard supersessionist reading** — patristic, Reformation, much pre-twentieth-century Christianity — takes "the kingdom of God will be taken from you and given to a people producing its fruits" as announcing a definitive transfer of covenant from Jewish people to Christian church. The "you" is Israel; the "people/nation" (ἔθνος) is the Gentile church. **(2) Limited-leadership reading** — Davies/Allison, France, Keener, Hagner — restricts the "you" to the chief priests and Pharisees explicitly named in v. 45, not Israel as a whole; the "people producing fruits" is a renewed Israel that includes Jewish disciples like the Twelve. The parable critiques leadership, not ethnic Israel. **(3) Post-Holocaust corrective reading** — Levine, Boys, Vatican II *Nostra Aetate*, PCBC 2001 — accepts that Matthew's text contains polemic against specific Jewish leadership but rejects the broader supersessionist reception as theologically unsustainable in light of Romans 9-11 ("the gifts and call of God are irrevocable") and historically unsustainable in light of the Shoah. **Where this leaves us:** the limited-leadership reading is most defensible exegetically — Matthew himself supplies the limiting clause in v. 45 — but the supersessionist reading has dominated Christian preaching for nineteen centuries, with consequences. Reading Matthew 21:43 responsibly today requires both honest exegesis (the verse is polemical, against named leaders) and honest reception history (this verse helped underwrite Christian Jew-hatred and must be read with that history in view, not against it).

**Unresolved question:** Is the singular ἔθνος of v. 43 — "a people/nation producing its fruits" — meant to evoke 1 Peter 2:9's description of the church as "a holy nation," or is it meant to evoke a renewed Israel along the lines of Matthew's twelve disciples? The grammar is genuinely ambiguous, and the answer determines whether Matthew 21 is read primarily as ecclesiology or as intra-Jewish prophecy.

## The Thread

Matthew 21 picks up Zechariah 9:9 (the humble king on a donkey), Psalm 118:22-26 (the rejected stone, the Hosanna), Isaiah 5:1-7 (the vineyard song), Isaiah 56:7 (the house of prayer for all nations), and Jeremiah 7:11 (the den of robbers) — five Hebrew Bible texts woven into a single chapter. The cornerstone citation will return at Acts 4:11 (Peter before the Sanhedrin), Romans 9:33, Ephesians 2:20, and 1 Peter 2:6-7, becoming foundational for early Christian self-understanding as God's eschatological building. The fig tree image returns in Luke 13:6-9 (the parable of the fruitless fig tree given another year) and behind James 3:12. Forward in Matthew, the temple action sets up the false-witness charge at 26:61 ("I am able to destroy the temple of God"), and the wicked tenants parable foreshadows 27:20-25 — flag this for the troubling "his blood be on us and on our children" trajectory that Levine and Boys have taught the church to read with care.
