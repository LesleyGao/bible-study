# Isaiah 43 — Go Deeper

If Isaiah 42 ends with the empirical servant Israel blind, deaf, plundered, and burning under Yahweh's judgment, Isaiah 43 begins with one of the most tender reversals in the Hebrew Bible. *But now thus says the Lord… fear not, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by name, you are mine.* The chapter is among the most beloved in Second Isaiah, anchoring centuries of Jewish and Christian liturgy with its imagery of being called by name, of passing through waters and fire, of a *new thing* springing forth, of a *new exodus* through a wilderness made watered by Yahweh's hand. It is also one of the great monotheistic declarations of the Hebrew Bible: *I, I am the Lord, and besides me there is no savior* (43:11).

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Second Isaiah's setting remains Babylonian exile around 545–540 BC. The mainstream-critical consensus (Westermann's *OTL*, Blenkinsopp's *AB*, Goldingay's *ICC*, Roy Melugin, Patricia Tull) reads chapters 40–55 as the work of an anonymous exilic prophet writing in Babylon as Cyrus rises; conservative-evangelical scholars (Motyer, Oswalt, E.J. Young) defend eighth-century Isaianic authorship; Childs's canonical-criticism brackets the question. Isaiah 43 itself contains the explicit reference to Babylon as the place from which Yahweh will bring his people home (*for your sake I send to Babylon and bring them all down as fugitives*, 43:14), which on the critical reading is contemporary commentary and on the conservative reading is predictive prophecy.

The chapter's central historical-theological move is the **New Exodus** motif. The prophet repeatedly draws on the language of the Exodus from Egypt — passing through waters (43:2, echoing the Red Sea), Yahweh making *a way in the sea, a path in the mighty waters* (43:16), the destruction of *chariot and horse, army and warrior* (43:17, echoing Exodus 14–15), the wilderness made watered (43:19–20). But the prophet then says, paradoxically: *remember not the former things, nor consider the things of old; behold, I am doing a new thing* (43:18–19). The point is not that the original Exodus is forgotten — Second Isaiah's whole rhetorical strategy depends on the audience remembering it — but that the new act of deliverance from Babylon will exceed the original. The prophet is writing *typologically*, treating the original Exodus as the pattern that the present return will fulfill and surpass.

The ancient Near Eastern context that bears most on this chapter is the **Cyrus Cylinder** (539–538 BC, British Museum), which records Cyrus's policy of returning displaced peoples and restoring their gods. The historical fact corroborates the prophet's announcement: when Cyrus took Babylon in 539 BC, he did issue the decree (recorded in 2 Chronicles 36:22–23 and Ezra 1:1–4) allowing the Judeans to return. Second Isaiah's claim that Yahweh is moving Cyrus is the theological commentary on that historical reality.

The Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. *ʾAl-tîrāʾ kî gəʾaltîḵā qārāʾtî bəšimḵā lî-ʾāttâ* (אַל־תִּירָא כִּי גְאַלְתִּיךָ קָרָאתִי בְשִׁמְךָ לִי־אָתָּה), *fear not, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by name, you are mine* (43:1). The verb *gāʾal* (redeem) carries family-law weight: the *gōʾēl* in Israelite custom was the kinsman-redeemer who bought back land or family members sold into slavery (Leviticus 25:25–55, Ruth 4); Yahweh becomes Israel's *gōʾēl*, treating the people as kin to be ransomed. *Qārāʾtî bəšimḵā* (called by name) signals personal knowledge and ownership, a phrase used elsewhere of Yahweh's calling of Cyrus by name (45:3–4) and of the stars (40:26). *Lî-ʾāttâ* (you are mine) is the covenantal formula in its most intimate form. *Ḥădāšâ ʾănî ʿōśeh* (חֲדָשָׁה אֲנִי עֹשֶׂה), *I am doing a new thing* (43:19), uses the feminine form of *ḥādāš* (new) substantively; the same verbal idea will recur in Jeremiah 31:31's *new covenant* and in Revelation 21:5's *behold, I make all things new*. *ʾAnōḵî ʾanōḵî hûʾ* (אָנֹכִי אָנֹכִי הוּא), *I, I am he* (43:25), uses the doubled first-person pronoun for emphatic divine self-identification; the construction recurs at 51:12 and is one of the most concentrated *I-am* declarations in Second Isaiah.

## Lens 2 — Literature

Isaiah 43 mixes several of Second Isaiah's signature literary forms. Verses 1–7 are a **salvation-oracle** (Begrich's *Heilsorakel*) addressed to Jacob/Israel, with its characteristic *fear not* assurance, divine self-identification (*for I am the Lord your God*), and promise of help. Verses 8–13 return to the **trial-speech** form against the gods of the nations, with the courtroom summons (*all the nations gather together*) and the demand for prediction. Verses 14–21 contain the **New Exodus oracle**, with its historical-typological shape: remember the former Exodus, and now behold the new one. Verses 22–28 are a **disputation** against Israel itself, charging the people not with idolatry but with weariness — having tired of Yahweh and burdened him with their sins.

The chapter's poetic structure uses repetition for rhetorical force. The three *for* clauses in v. 3 (*for I am the Lord your God, the Holy One of Israel, your Savior; I give Egypt as your ransom*) cumulate Yahweh's titles. The *I, I am* (*ʾanōḵî ʾanōḵî*) of vv. 11 and 25 emphasizes divine self-assertion. The fourfold gathering imagery of vv. 5–6 (east, west, north, south) gives the people-gathering a cosmic scope.

The **water and fire** image of v. 2 — *when you pass through the waters, I will be with you; and through the rivers, they shall not overwhelm you; when you walk through fire you shall not be burned* — is one of the most quoted verses in Christian devotional and pastoral practice. The image draws on the Red Sea crossing (water) and the Sinai theophany or Daniel 3 fiery furnace (fire), but functions in the chapter as a general assurance for any exilic crisis.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

For Jewish tradition, Isaiah 43 is one of the cardinal texts of God's covenantal love for Israel. The Targum Jonathan paraphrases v. 1 to emphasize Yahweh's redemption from Egypt and from exile in parallel. Rashi reads *I have called you by name* as an expression of God's particular knowledge of and relationship with Israel as a chosen people. Ibn Ezra connects v. 1 directly to the New Exodus motif of vv. 14–21. The Targum's reading of v. 10's *you are my witnesses* — *you, the house of Israel, are my witnesses, says the Lord* — reinforces the corporate-covenantal reading of *witness*. In modern Jewish thought, the chapter is often read alongside other Second Isaiah passages as the high point of biblical monotheism (Levenson, Boyarin, Sarna). The verse 43:25 — *I, I am he who blots out your transgressions for my own sake, and I will not remember your sins* — has been central in rabbinic debates about repentance and divine forgiveness, especially in the High Holy Day liturgy. The phrase *blot out* (*māḥâ*) is the verb used in the High Holy Day prayer asking God to *blot out* sins.

For Christian tradition, the chapter has had several major appropriations. The New Exodus theology of vv. 14–21 becomes foundational for Pauline Christology: 1 Corinthians 10:1–2 treats the Exodus as a type fulfilled in baptism, and Mark's Gospel structures Jesus' ministry as a New Exodus from the wilderness through the cross. The *new thing* of 43:19 is taken up in 2 Corinthians 5:17 (*if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation*) and Revelation 21:5. The water-and-fire image of 43:2 has become one of the most quoted Christian devotional verses; it appears in countless hymns (*How Firm a Foundation*, the third stanza of which paraphrases the verse directly), in pastoral counsel, in funerals, in moments of crisis. Justin Martyr, Origen, Jerome, Aquinas, Calvin all develop the chapter's monotheistic declarations Christologically; modern critical Christian commentary (Westermann, Blenkinsopp, Goldingay, Brueggemann) reads the chapter first in its sixth-century BC setting and treats Christological readings as theological appropriation.

The **monotheism** declared in 43:10–11 — *before me no god was formed, nor shall there be any after me. I, I am the Lord, and besides me there is no savior* — has been central to the development of biblical monotheism and to interfaith dialogue. Modern Catholic teaching, especially the Second Vatican Council's *Nostra Aetate* (1965), affirms a shared monotheism with Judaism and Islam while preserving the distinctive Christian claim about Christ; this declaration in Isaiah 43 is one of the texts that grounds the shared monotheism and that requires careful handling in interfaith conversation, since the *no savior besides me* line could be read narrowly as exclusivist or broadly as the assertion that all true salvation comes from the one God whom Jews, Christians, and Muslims worship under different names. Both readings have textual warrant.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

Two tensions run through Isaiah 43 and must be named.

The first is the **adoption-and-belonging theology** of v. 1 — *I have called you by name, you are mine* — read alongside the chapter's later declaration that *your first father sinned, and your mediators transgressed against me; therefore I will profane the princes of the sanctuary, and deliver Jacob to utter destruction and Israel to reviling* (vv. 27–28). The two declarations sit side by side in the same chapter without obvious resolution. Three readings deserve hearing. The covenant-fidelity reading holds that Yahweh's *you are mine* is irrevocable; the judgment of vv. 27–28 has already occurred (the exile is the *delivering Jacob to destruction*), and the chapter as a whole declares that Yahweh's claim on his people outlasts even his judgment of them. The dialectical reading holds that the chapter performs the prophetic tension itself: the same God who called Israel by name is the same God who judged them, and the consolation of v. 1 is meaningful only because the judgment of v. 28 is real. The historical-redemptive reading holds that vv. 22–28 reach back to Israel's history of unfaithfulness as the ground for the surprise of v. 25's blotting-out: Yahweh forgives *for his own sake*, not because Israel has earned it. All three readings have textual warrant; the chapter does not foreclose any of them.

The second tension is the **monotheistic exclusivism** of vv. 10–11 — *besides me there is no savior* — read in the context of modern interfaith dialogue. Three positions deserve hearing. The exclusivist reading holds that the verse means what it says: Yahweh alone is God, and worship of any other deity (Marduk, Bel, Nebo, the gods of the nations) is idolatry. The universal-monotheist reading holds that the verse asserts the oneness of God in a way that grounds the shared monotheism of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, since all three traditions confess the same one God under different names. The contextual reading holds that Second Isaiah's polemic is specifically against the gods of Babylon as competitors with Yahweh, and that its application to interfaith dialogue requires nuance: the prophet was not addressing Buddhism or Hinduism, and pressing his polemic into modern interfaith contexts requires care. *Nostra Aetate* and modern Jewish-Christian-Muslim dialogue typically take the universal-monotheist reading, while preserving each tradition's distinctive confession.

Steelmanned: a thoughtful exclusivist can grant that *Nostra Aetate*-style dialogue is possible without surrendering the prophet's polemic against idolatry; a thoughtful interfaith dialogue partner can grant the prophet's polemical specificity without weakening the shared monotheistic confession; a thoughtful contextual reader can grant both that the prophet addressed Babylon's gods and that the verse has continued to ground monotheistic confession across millennia.

Where this leaves us: a chapter whose central declaration is that the people who are blind, deaf, plundered, and burning are nevertheless called by name and are nevertheless his — and whose theology of *I am doing a new thing* refuses to let memory of past judgment imprison hope for present deliverance.

Closing question: If the same God who blots out transgressions *for my own sake* also delivers Jacob to utter destruction, what does it mean to be *called by name* by such a God — to belong to a love that does not flinch from judgment but does not let judgment have the last word?
