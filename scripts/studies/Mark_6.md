# Mark 6: Rejected at Home, Beheaded at the Banquet, Walking on the Sea

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Mark 6 stitches together four locales — Nazareth, the Galilean villages, Herod Antipas's court at Tiberias or Machaerus, and the desolate northern shore of the Sea of Galilee — and each carries dense first-century freight. Nazareth in 6:1–6 was a tiny agricultural village, perhaps 200 to 400 souls in Jesus's day, perched on a hillside in lower Galilee about four miles from the larger Greek-speaking city of Sepphoris. Excavations directed by Bellarmino Bagatti in the 1950s and 1960s, and more recently by Yardenna Alexandre under the Israel Antiquities Authority, have uncovered first-century domestic structures including rock-cut storage pits, terraces, and a partial residential structure, confirming Nazareth's character as a poor, kin-network village whose economy was tied to grain, olives, and small-scale stoneworking. The Greek term Mark uses for Jesus's trade in 6:3, ***tektōn*** (τέκτων), is broader than the English "carpenter": it covered builders working in wood and stone, the kind of artisan likely employed at Sepphoris's Hellenistic construction sites four miles away. R. T. France (NIGTC) and Joel Marcus (Anchor Bible) both note that *tektōn* implies a working-class craftsman, not a peasant, but not a man of means either.

The naming of Jesus's brothers in 6:3 — James (Iakōbos), Joses (Iōsēs), Judas, and Simon, with unnamed sisters — is one of the chapter's most historically loaded sentences. James "the brother of the Lord" appears in Galatians 1:19 and Acts 15 as the leader of the Jerusalem church; Josephus (*Antiquities* 20.9.1) reports his execution by the high priest Ananus in 62 CE. The interpretive divergence on what "brother" (*adelphos*) means here is itself a marker of three later Christian traditions. The Helvidian view (held by the second-century writer Helvidius and most Protestant exegetes) takes the term in its plain sense: blood brothers, sons of Mary and Joseph born after Jesus. The Epiphanian view (named for Epiphanius of Salamis, foundational in Eastern Orthodoxy and held by some Catholics) holds that they were Joseph's children from a prior marriage, preserving Mary's perpetual virginity without strain. The Hieronymian view (Jerome's, dominant in Roman Catholic exegesis since the late fourth century) reads *adelphoi* as cousins, drawing on the Septuagint's flexible use of the term. Each reading has different implications for ecclesiology and Mariology, and Mark 6:3 is the principal proof text for all of them.

Herod Antipas's court (6:14–29) is the chapter's other historically rich setting. Antipas, son of Herod the Great by his Samaritan wife Malthace, ruled Galilee and Perea from 4 BCE to 39 CE as tetrarch under Roman oversight. Josephus (*Antiquities* 18.5.1–4) gives a parallel account of John's execution but locates it differently — at the desert fortress of **Machaerus**, on a high cliff east of the Dead Sea — and gives a political rather than personal motive: Antipas feared John's popular following could spark insurrection. Excavations at Machaerus by the Franciscan archaeologist Virgilio Corbo in the 1970s and, more extensively, by the Hungarian archaeologist Győző Vörös from 2009 onward have uncovered the Herodian palatial complex Josephus describes, including a courtyard plausibly suitable for the kind of banquet Mark narrates and a dungeon below the throne hall consistent with John's imprisonment. The two sources do not contradict each other — Mark gives the public court intrigue, Josephus the political reading — but read together they triangulate a real event with real geography.

The unnamed dancing girl in 6:22 is identified by Josephus (*Antiquities* 18.5.4) as **Salome**, daughter of Herodias by her first husband Herod II (whom Mark calls "Philip"; Josephus distinguishes between Herod II and Philip the Tetrarch). Mark's "Philip" naming has long been recognized as a possible confusion or abbreviation. Herodias's marriage to Antipas was a flagrant violation of Leviticus 18:16 and 20:21, which forbade marrying a brother's wife while the brother lived; this is precisely the basis for John's public denunciation in 6:18.

Three Greek terms repay attention. ***Splanchnizomai*** (σπλαγχνίζομαι, 6:34), the verb translated "had compassion," is rooted in the noun *splanchna* (entrails, viscera) and conveys a gut-level pity, not a polite sentiment. Mark uses the verb at the threshold of the feeding miracle, marking a Christological turn: Jesus sees the crowd as "sheep without a shepherd" (a phrase echoing Numbers 27:17 and Ezekiel 34) and *splanchnizetai*. The verb returns at 8:2 (the four thousand) and at 9:22 (the boy with the unclean spirit), each at moments when divine compassion meets human inadequacy. ***Egō eimi*** (ἐγώ εἰμι, "I am" or "it is I," 6:50) is the phrase Jesus speaks to the terrified disciples on the sea. The two-word formula is unremarkable Greek for self-identification but echoes the Septuagint's translation of God's self-disclosure in Exodus 3:14 and the *anî hû'* declarations of Deutero-Isaiah (Isaiah 41:4, 43:10, 43:13, 46:4, 51:12). Whether Mark intends a divine-name claim is contested (more on this below), but the resonance is undeniable. ***Sklērokardia*** (σκληροκαρδία, "hardness of heart," 6:52, related verb *pepōrōmenē* used here) is the technical Septuagint vocabulary for Israel's covenantal failure under Moses (Deuteronomy 10:16; Jeremiah 4:4). Mark applies it not to opponents but to the Twelve themselves, an extraordinary diagnostic claim about the disciples' condition.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Mark 6 is built around a major **Markan sandwich**. The mission of the Twelve (6:7–13) is the A panel; the death of John the Baptist (6:14–29) is the B insert; the return of the Twelve (6:30–32) is the A' panel. The sandwich performs theological work that neither panel could perform alone. The Twelve go out to preach repentance and cast out demons just as their forerunner-prophet John dies because he too preached repentance and confronted royal sin. Mark is telling the disciples (and his readers) what apostolic preaching costs.

The chapter is also one of the most narratively dense in Mark, packing six pericopae into fifty-six verses: Nazareth rejection, mission of the Twelve, Herod's confusion about Jesus, John's beheading, feeding of the five thousand, walking on the sea (with the Gennesaret summary as a coda). The pace is unrelenting; Mark's signature ***euthys*** (immediately) fires repeatedly (6:25, 6:27, 6:45, 6:50, 6:54), and the historical present pulses through ("they go," "he says"). Rhoads, Dewey, and Michie (*Mark as Story*) note that the chapter functions as a hinge between the Galilean ministry's ascendant phase (chapters 1–5) and its complication (chapters 7–8): home rejects him, the disciples show power but then incomprehension, the political authority that killed John already foreshadows the political authority that will kill Jesus.

The John the Baptist story (6:17–29) is itself a literary marvel — a self-contained novella with its own prologue, complication, climax, and tomb-burial denouement. Bultmann, in *History of the Synoptic Tradition*, classified it as a Hellenistic court tale, drawing parallels with Esther, Daniel, and the courtly novellas of the Greco-Roman world. Adela Yarbro Collins (Hermeneia) noted echoes of Persian-Greek queen-and-king narratives. The structure is careful: Antipas's mixed conscience (he "feared John, knowing that he was a righteous and holy man, and he kept him safe... he heard him gladly," 6:20), Herodias's grudge, the birthday banquet, the rash oath, the daughter's compliance, the king's regret, the swift execution. The narrative's irony is that Antipas thinks Jesus is John raised (6:14, 6:16) — and the reader knows that resurrection is a category Mark is reserving for the Son.

The feeding of the five thousand (6:30–44) deploys Eucharistic vocabulary with deliberate force. The four-fold action verbs — *labōn* (took), *eulogēsen* (blessed), *kateklasen* (broke), *edidou* (gave) — appear in the same sequence at 14:22, the Last Supper. The "green grass" detail in 6:39 (*chlōrō chortō*) is unusual enough that it has been read as eyewitness memory (Vincent Taylor) and as a deliberate echo of Psalm 23 ("he makes me lie down in green pastures"). Twelve baskets full (6:43) — twelve, the number of the tribes of Israel and of the disciples themselves — sets up the symbolic counterweight to the seven baskets of 8:8.

The walking-on-water pericope (6:45–52) carries some of Mark's most distinctive theological prose. The phrase ***ēthelen parelthein autous*** (ἤθελεν παρελθεῖν αὐτούς, 6:48), "he meant to pass by them," is among the most contested lines in the Gospel. The verb *parerchomai* with its theophanic resonance — God "passing by" Moses on Sinai (Exodus 33:19, 22, 34:6); "passing by" Elijah on Horeb (1 Kings 19:11) — has led commentators from Adela Yarbro Collins to Joel Marcus to read this as a theophanic episode in which Jesus is shown doing what God does. The connection to Job 9:8 ("he alone... trampled the waves of the sea") is widely noted; the Septuagint translates Job 9:8 with vocabulary close to Mark's. The disciples' explicit "hardness of heart" (6:52) — applied not to outsiders but to those closest to Jesus — is one of Mark's bluntest interpretive tags.

Textual variants are mostly minor. The most notable is at 6:22, where some manuscripts read "his daughter Herodias" (suggesting the dancer was Antipas's own daughter, not Herodias's) — a reading that some scholars (Adela Yarbro Collins among them) take as the harder and possibly older text, though Josephus's testimony favors the traditional reading.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Mark 6 sits at a structural junction in the canonical arc. Read backward, the Nazareth rejection completes the trajectory of opposition that has built since 3:6 (Pharisees and Herodians plot to destroy him) through 3:21 (his family thinks he is "out of his mind"). Read forward, the chapter's twin failures — the disciples' incomprehension of the loaves, Antipas's deluded hope that John is back — set up the great Caesarea Philippi confession in chapter 8 and the passion predictions that follow.

The interpretive trajectories diverge in interesting ways.

Within Jewish reception of Jesus, the rejection at Nazareth has often been read as a window onto Galilean village dynamics rather than as a polemic against unbelief in general. Geza Vermes (*Jesus the Jew*) noted that the Galilean villages were tightly kin-bound; an itinerant preacher from a known family faced specific suspicion not because he claimed too much but because his neighbors knew his trade and his siblings. E. P. Sanders (*The Historical Figure of Jesus*) emphasized that Jesus's mission to "the lost sheep of Israel" begins with concrete villages and families, not with abstract Israel; the Twelve sent two by two are dispatched into precisely the social fabric Jesus himself navigates. Daniel Boyarin (*The Jewish Gospels*) has argued that the figure of Jesus as Elijah-redivivus (6:15) and the Son of Man Christology Mark develops are drawn from a Second Temple Jewish stream that already contained these categories. Amy-Jill Levine (*The Misunderstood Jew*; *The Jewish Annotated New Testament*) has insisted that the John-and-Herodias episode be read as Jewish-on-Jewish moral conflict — a Jewish prophet calling out a Jewish ruler under a Jewish law (Leviticus 18:16, 20:21) — not as a generic morality tale. David Flusser (*Jesus*) read John's death as a paradigm of the prophetic-martyr tradition the Maccabean and post-exilic literature had already established (cf. *Lives of the Prophets*, the assumed-martyrdom of Isaiah).

Within the Christian interpretive trajectory the chapter has carried weight. The patristic readings of the feeding miracle — Origen's allegorical interpretation in his *Commentary on Matthew* (extending to the parallel feedings), Augustine's sacramental reading in his *Tractates on the Gospel of John* (on John 6, the parallel feeding pericope) — placed the loaves and fish inside a Eucharistic frame that became dominant. Bede's *Commentary on Mark* read the twelve baskets as the apostles' authority to teach the twelve tribes; the green grass as the church's pastoral renewal; the disciples' failure to understand as a typological foreshadowing of all the church's slow learning. Calvin's *Harmony of the Gospels* engaged 6:5 — "he could do no mighty work there" — with theological care: Calvin warned against any reading that compromised divine omnipotence, arguing that the limitation was not in Christ's power but in his self-restraint, choosing not to display power to those who would not receive it. Luther read the Nazareth rejection as a paradigm of the *theologia crucis*: the very neighbors who knew Jesus best could not see God in him because they expected God to come in glory rather than in the cloak of an artisan's son.

Twentieth-century readings have been varied. Bonhoeffer's *Discipleship* engaged the mission of the Twelve (6:7–13) at length: the staff but no bread, the sandals but no extra tunic, the dependence on hospitality — these are the marks of cross-shaped discipleship. Bultmann's form-critical work classed the John-the-Baptist novella as a developed legend of historical kernel, while leaving the feeding miracle in a more ambivalent place between aretalogy and Eucharistic etiology. Karl Barth read the walking on the sea as a Christological sign — the *egō eimi* as a divine self-disclosure — and the disciples' fear as the proper human response before the Lord who walks on chaos. N. T. Wright (*Mark for Everyone*; *Jesus and the Victory of God*) frames the chapter's miracles as enacted parables of the Kingdom: Jesus feeding Israel in the wilderness is the second Moses doing what the first Moses did, and the people are gathered in groups of fifty and a hundred precisely as Moses arranged Israel in Exodus 18 and Numbers 31.

The major modern commentaries triangulate. Joel Marcus (Anchor Bible) reads the chapter as Christologically ascendant — Jesus is shown to be Israel's eschatological shepherd, the Son who walks on the sea as God walks on the sea. Adela Yarbro Collins (Hermeneia) presses the Hellenistic-religious context, noting parallels in Greco-Roman miracle traditions, while preserving the chapter's distinctively Jewish messianic vocabulary. R. T. France (NIGTC) and Morna Hooker (BNTC) both emphasize the chapter's structural role in Mark's Christological argument: by chapter 8 the question "who do you say that I am?" will be unavoidable, and the loaves and the sea-walking are evidence the disciples ought to be reading. Vincent Taylor's earlier commentary (1952) was particularly attentive to the Nazareth rejection as eyewitness-tinged historical memory; Robert Guelich's WBC volume (extended by Craig Evans for chapters 9–16) reads the feeding miracle within a tightly Jewish typological frame.

The literary critics have made distinctive contributions to chapter 6. Frank Kermode (*Genesis of Secrecy*) returned to the disciples' "hardness of heart" (6:52) as a Markan signature of incomprehension that resists reduction. Rhoads, Dewey, and Michie (*Mark as Story*) traced the chapter's narrative point of view: Mark grants the reader access to Antipas's interior conflict (6:20, 6:26) in a way he rarely grants for any other character, marking the Baptist-novella as a literary set piece. Mary Ann Tolbert (*Sowing the Gospel*) read the feeding miracle through her sower-parable framework: the crowd as soil, the disciples as planters who do not yet understand the seed they are dispensing. Werner Kelber (*The Oral and Written Gospel*) emphasized the rhythm of the chapter as evidence of oral-narrative composition: the Aramaicisms, the shifts in tense, the doublets.

The political readings have been increasingly important. Ched Myers (*Binding the Strong Man*) reads the John-and-Herodias episode as an exposé of imperial-client decadence: a banquet for *megistanes* (great ones), military commanders, and the leading men of Galilee, in which a prophet's head is served on a platter as entertainment. Richard Horsley has read Antipas's court as a specific site of Roman-Jewish collaboration, with John's death as the cost of speaking truth to colonial power. Brigitte Kahl and other postcolonial readers have emphasized the contrast between Antipas's banquet (decadent, indoor, ending in death) and Jesus's banquet (in a desolate place, on green grass, ending in twelve baskets of leftovers) as a deliberate Markan diptych: two kinds of kingdom, two kinds of bread.

The feminist readings have been pointed. Joanna Dewey and Sharon Ringe (*Searching the Scriptures*) have noted how Mark's presentation of Herodias and her daughter has carried disproportionate weight in later misogynist traditions — the dancing girl became Salome the *femme fatale*, a figure constructed by Christian art and homiletics far beyond what the text supports. The text itself names Herodias but not the dancer; the medieval and modern construction of "Salome" is drawn largely from Josephus and from imagination.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions run through Mark 6, and each repays steelmanning.

**Tension 1: What does it mean that Jesus "could do no mighty work there" (6:5)?** The verse is among the most Christologically sensitive in the Gospel, since it appears to limit Jesus's power. Three positions:

The *high-Christological-restraint* reading (Origen in his way; Augustine more carefully; Aquinas in *Summa* III, Q. 43; Calvin in *Harmony*) holds that the verb *edynato* (he could) describes a moral or fittingness limitation rather than an ontological one. Jesus *could not* in the sense that he *would not* perform mighty works among those whose unbelief made such works pointless or even counterproductive. The reading preserves divine omnipotence and reads Mark's apparent limitation as a theological-pastoral choice. The reading is strongest when paired with 6:5b — "except that he laid his hands on a few sick people and healed them," which already qualifies the apparent absolute.

The *covenantal-relational* reading (Marcus, Yarbro Collins, Hooker, with feminist resonance in Ringe) takes the limitation more seriously. Mighty works in Mark are not isolated displays of power; they are encounters with persons in faith. Without faith, the encounter cannot complete, and what would have been a mighty work remains, at most, a healed individual. This reading takes seriously the bleeding-woman pericope of chapter 5: the woman's faith and Jesus's power were both involved; both must be present for the encounter to fully take place. Nazareth's unbelief is not Jesus's incapacity but the relational truncation of what mighty works are.

The *kenotic-incarnational* reading (some Lutheran tradition; explicitly developed in 19th-century kenotic Christology; modernized in Hooker and in some patristic-influenced contemporary work) holds that the incarnate Son operates within real human limitations — not just chosen restraints but actual conditions of his incarnate state. Jesus's astonishment at unbelief (*ethaumazen dia tēn apistian*, 6:6) is genuine; he is not playing a role. The reading preserves the integrity of Mark's portrait of a Jesus who marvels, hungers, sleeps, prays, and weeps.

*Where this leaves us:* The text does not let any one reading dominate. Mark wrote *edynato* and not *ouk ēthelen* (he did not wish), and the verb's plain force should not be muted. But Mark also wrote 6:5b — Jesus did, in fact, heal some sick people, and the limitation is precisely on "mighty works" that would have functioned as signs to a willing community. The reading that holds together Jesus's astonishment, his real limitation, and his unimpeded compassion, without resolving the Christological strain, is closest to what Mark gives us.

**Tension 2: Is the *egō eimi* of 6:50 a divine-name claim?** The phrase "Take heart; it is I [*egō eimi*]; do not be afraid" sits at the climax of the walking-on-water episode, and its theological weight is contested. Three positions:

The *idiomatic-recognition* reading (R. T. France; some classical Catholic exegesis) holds that *egō eimi* is straightforward Greek for "it's me" — the disciples thought they saw a ghost; Jesus identifies himself. The phrase carries no metaphysical freight on its own; only context can give it theological weight, and on this reading the context here is reassurance, not theophany.

The *theophanic* reading (Marcus, Yarbro Collins, N. T. Wright, Edwards) presses the cumulative resonances. The *egō eimi* echoes the Septuagint of Exodus 3:14 and the Deutero-Isaianic *anî hû'* formula; the verb *parelthein* in 6:48 ("meant to pass by them") echoes God's "passing by" Moses on Sinai (Exodus 33:19, 33:22) and Elijah on Horeb (1 Kings 19:11); the walking on the sea echoes Job 9:8 ("he... trampled the waves of the sea"). All three resonances are in the same passage; Mark is not idly placing them. The disciples' "hardness of heart" (6:52) is precisely the failure to read these signs.

The *Christologically-progressive* reading (Hooker, in part Marcus) holds that *egō eimi* is genuinely ambiguous — Mark is laying down theophanic resonances that the reader will only fully recognize in retrospect, especially after the Caesarea Philippi confession (8:29) and the centurion's confession (15:39). The phrase is not yet a divine-name claim in the sense that the high-priest scene (14:62, where Jesus's *egō eimi* is unmistakable) will be, but it is preparing the reader to recognize that Christology when it comes.

*Where this leaves us:* The cumulative case for theophanic resonance is strong. *Parelthein*, *egō eimi*, and walking on the sea — together — are not accidental. Mark is showing that Jesus does what God does in Israel's sacred memory. Whether 6:50 is a "claim" that crosses some metaphysical threshold is the wrong question; Mark's narrative theology works by accumulation, and the reader is being trained to recognize divine action in the carpenter's son before the Christological vocabulary becomes explicit.

**One unresolved question:** Why does Mark add the diagnostic comment about the disciples' "hardness of heart" specifically here, and specifically tied to "the loaves" (6:52)? *Sklērokardia* in the Septuagint is the language of covenantal failure under Moses; it is what required the prophets and what awaits the eschatological circumcision of the heart (Deuteronomy 30:6; Jeremiah 31:33). Mark is applying it to the Twelve. The theological scandal — that the people closest to Jesus, who saw the loaves with their own eyes, are still in covenantal failure — sets the trajectory for everything that follows. But Mark does not tell us what would *cure* the hardness; the loaves should have been enough, and they were not. The chapter ends without explanation of the cure, and the Gospel as a whole only resolves the question through the cross and, perhaps, the abrupt empty tomb.

## The Thread

Mark 6 reaches forward and backward across the canon. The mission of the Twelve in 6:7–13 is the seed of the larger Christian mission inaugurated at 13:10 ("the gospel must first be proclaimed to all nations") and at the (textually disputed) longer ending. The four-fold Eucharistic action verbs at the feeding (6:41) are the same as at 14:22 — Mark is asking us to read the Last Supper through the lens of the green-grass feeding, and vice versa. The "sheep without a shepherd" of 6:34 reaches back to Numbers 27:17 (Moses asking for a successor) and Ezekiel 34 (the failed shepherds of Israel) and forward to 14:27 ("strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered"). The disciples' "hardness of heart" (6:52) returns explicitly at 8:17 — Jesus will repeat the exact charge — and finds its diagnostic completion only at the empty tomb (16:8), where the women flee in terror.

Two later passages worth flagging: 8:1–10, the feeding of the four thousand, which the disciples' failure to "understand about the loaves" (6:52) already anticipates — and where Jesus will ask, in 8:18, "do you not remember? When I broke the five loaves for the five thousand, how many baskets full of broken pieces did you take up?" The 6:52 charge is being explicitly set up to land at 8:17–21. The second is 14:1–11, where another anonymous-but-named woman (Mary at Bethany) will anoint Jesus at a meal during which betrayal is plotted; Mark deliberately rhymes her with Herodias — both at meals, both involving heads (Mary anoints, Herodias decapitates), both with male hosts whose moral compass fails. The chapter is not a sequence of episodes but a structure of echoes, and Mark expects the reader to be listening.
