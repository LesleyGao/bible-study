# Mark 10: The Road to Jerusalem and the Cost of Following

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Mark 10 traces the Galilean prophet's geographical descent toward execution: from "**the region of Judea and beyond the Jordan**" (10:1, the Perean territory east of the Jordan ruled by Herod Antipas), down through Jericho (a Hasmonean-Herodian winter capital, lying 853 feet below sea level — the steep ascent from Jericho to Jerusalem rises some 3,500 feet over fifteen miles, and Roman milestones from this *Wadi Qelt* route survive in the archaeological record). Bartimaeus's begging post outside Jericho (10:46) places him along this pilgrimage road in the festival season; Joel Marcus (Anchor Bible) notes that Jericho was a tax-farming town, and a blind beggar at its gate would have lived off the alms of pilgrims ascending for Passover.

The **divorce question** (10:1-12) was the most contested halakhic dispute of 1st-century Galilean Judaism. The Mishnah (m. Gittin 9.10) preserves the dispute between **Hillel and Shammai**: Shammai held that Deuteronomy 24:1's "**something indecent**" (Hebrew ***ervat davar***) meant unchastity alone; Hillel held it could mean any cause of displeasure ("even if she burned his food"). Akiba later took an even more permissive line ("even if he found another more beautiful"). The Pharisees' question to Jesus ("**Is it lawful for a man to divorce his wife?**") presupposes this exact dispute. Jesus's answer cuts beneath the disputed clause to Genesis 1:27 and 2:24, refusing the Deuteronomic concession altogether. **David Instone-Brewer** (*Divorce and Remarriage in the Bible*, 2002) reconstructs the rabbinic context in detail and argues that Jesus is taking the strict-Shammaite line and pushing it further toward indissolubility. The clause "**and if she divorces her husband**" (10:12) addresses a Greco-Roman, not strictly Jewish, situation — Jewish women in the 1st century could not formally initiate divorce (though they could pressure a court to compel a husband's bill of divorce); Roman women could. Marcus reads 10:12 as Mark's redactional adaptation for a Roman/Gentile audience.

The **Decalogue list** Jesus gives the rich man (10:19) is striking: he names commandments 5-9 (the second table) but substitutes "**do not defraud**" (***mē aposterēsēs***, μὴ ἀποστερήσῃς) for the tenth commandment against coveting. The verb ***apostereō*** appears in James 5:4 (defrauding wages from harvesters) and 1 Corinthians 6:7-8 (defrauding fellow believers); it carries connotations of withholding wages from a day laborer, a sin condemned in Leviticus 19:13 and Deuteronomy 24:14-15. France (NIGTC) and Yarbro Collins (Hermeneia) both treat the substitution as deliberate: Jesus is targeting a particular form of wealth-derived sin to a man whose wealth (10:22, "**great possessions**") was likely landholding in an agrarian economy where defrauding tenant farmers was a structural reality.

Three Greek terms repay attention. ***Lytron anti pollōn*** (λύτρον ἀντὶ πολλῶν, 10:45) — "ransom for many." ***Lytron*** in Hellenistic Greek meant the price paid to free a slave or prisoner of war; the LXX uses it for ransom-redemption (Leviticus 25:51 for redeeming a kinsman; Numbers 18:15 for redeeming a firstborn). The preposition ***anti*** (in place of, in exchange for) carries unmistakably substitutionary force. ***Polloi*** (many) is widely read as a Semitic idiom (Hebrew ***rabbim***) drawn from Isaiah 53:11-12, where the Servant "**bears the sin of many**" — though Morna Hooker (*Jesus and the Servant*) famously disputes the directness of the Isaiah 53 echo, arguing that "many" was a standard inclusive Jewish term and need not signal Servant Songs. ***Huios Dauid*** (υἱὸς Δαυίδ, 10:47-48) — "Son of David," the title Bartimaeus shouts twice. This is the **first messianic title in Mark spoken by a non-disciple**, and it is shouted by a blind man, framed by Mark as an irony: the blind see what the sighted do not. ***Hodos*** (ὁδός, 10:32, 46, 52) — "the way" / "the road" — Mark's structurally weighted noun. The journey to Jerusalem is "the way," and Bartimaeus, having been healed, "**followed him on the way**" (10:52), closing the second-half-of-Mark journey section.

The **camel through the eye of a needle** (10:25) generated a popular but unfounded medieval allegory — "the Needle Gate" of Jerusalem was supposedly a small low gate through which a camel could pass only by being unloaded and made to crawl. Marcus (AB), France (NIGTC), and the standard commentaries demolish this reading: there is no archaeological evidence for any such gate, the earliest reference appears in 11th- or 15th-century commentators, and the literal hyperbolic absurdity of a camel passing through a sewing needle is the rhetorical point. Some manuscripts substitute ***kamilon*** (καμίλον, "rope" or "cable") for ***kamēlon*** (κάμηλον, camel) — a clearly secondary scribal softening.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Mark 10 completes the central journey section (8:22-10:52) with characteristic Markan symmetry. The section opens with the two-stage healing of a blind man at Bethsaida (8:22-26) and closes with the healing of blind Bartimaeus (10:46-52). The two healings frame the three passion predictions and the three discipleship-failure cycles that follow each. **Rhoads, Dewey, and Michie** (*Mark as Story*) treat this *inclusio* as the single most deliberately constructed unit in the gospel: the disciples, like the man at Bethsaida, see partially and need a second touch; Bartimaeus, by contrast, sees fully on the first encounter and follows on the way.

The **third passion prediction** (10:33-34) is the most detailed of the three, naming five specific elements (delivery to chief priests and scribes, condemnation, delivery to Gentiles, mocking and spitting and flogging, killing, rising after three days). Vincent Taylor (1952) read this density as evidence of post-Passion editorial elaboration; Joel Marcus reads it as Mark's narrative escalation, building the reader's tension as the entry into Jerusalem approaches. Each prediction is followed by disciple failure: 8:31 → Peter rebukes Jesus; 9:31 → disciples argue who is greatest; 10:33-34 → James and John request the seats of glory. The pattern is a deliberate triadic structure, and Mary Ann Tolbert (*Sowing the Gospel*) reads it as Mark's parable of the church's chronic inability to grasp servant-leadership even after explicit teaching.

The "**Jesus, looking at him, loved him**" (10:21) is one of Mark's rare interior emotional notes about Jesus. Mark uses ***ēgapēsen*** (ἠγάπησεν) — the same verb used in 1:11 of the Father's love for the Son. France notes that this is the only place in the synoptics where Jesus is said to **love** an individual interlocutor, and the love does not soften the demand. The literary structure is a deliberate refusal of pastoral easing: love **is** the call to sell everything.

The "**rich man** / **rich young ruler**" terminology blends three gospels — Mark calls him simply "**a man**" (10:17), Matthew adds "young" (Matt 19:20), Luke adds "ruler" (Luke 18:18). This conflation has shaped the Western pastoral imagination but obscures Mark's actual portrayal: an adult of indeterminate age and status, marked only by his wealth and his earnest spiritual seeking. Bultmann (*History of the Synoptic Tradition*) treated the dialogue as a form-critical "controversy story" (apophthegm) compressed around a pronouncement saying.

A major textual crux sits at 10:24: Jesus says "**how difficult it is to enter the kingdom of God!**" without qualifier. Many later manuscripts (Byzantine tradition, Vulgate) add "**for those who trust in riches**" — clearly a softening gloss. The shorter, harder reading (no qualifier) is supported by the earliest witnesses (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus) and is the reading of NA28 and modern critical translations. The harder reading sharpens the disciples' astonishment in 10:26: if entering is just hard for everyone, no one can be saved.

Frank Kermode (*Genesis of Secrecy*) noted that Bartimaeus is one of only a few minor characters in Mark named (others: Jairus, Simon of Cyrene, Joseph of Arimathea, Mary Magdalene), and the naming itself ("son of Timaeus") signals a person known to Mark's community — a probable early Jerusalem-church convert whose memory Mark preserves.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The **divorce sayings** have generated the most divergent confessional trajectories of any teaching of Jesus.

The **Roman Catholic** tradition reads 10:9 as establishing strict indissolubility. Augustine (*De Bono Coniugali*) developed the theology of marriage as a **sacramental bond** that creates an ontological reality not dissoluble by human action. The **Council of Trent** (1545-63, Session 24, *Decree on the Sacrament of Matrimony*) anathematized any teaching that the marriage bond can be dissolved on grounds of adultery and that either party could remarry. **Pope John Paul II's** *Familiaris Consortio* (1981) and **Pope Francis's** *Amoris Laetitia* (2016) maintain indissolubility while allowing pastoral discernment for divorced-and-remarried Catholics. The annulment process recognizes that what looked like a marriage was never sacramentally valid — but does not dissolve a true marriage.

The **Eastern Orthodox** tradition follows the principle of ***oikonomia*** (pastoral economy): the marriage bond is sacramental and ideally indissoluble, but in cases of moral death (adultery, abandonment, prolonged absence, abuse), the church may permit a second marriage with a penitential rite. The Council of Trullo (692) developed canonical norms; modern Orthodox jurisdictions allow up to three marriages with progressive penitential structures.

The **Reformed** tradition (Calvin, *Institutes* 4.20.14; Westminster Confession of Faith 24) allowed divorce on grounds of adultery and willful desertion, drawing on Matthew 5:32, 19:9, and 1 Corinthians 7:15 (the "**Pauline privilege**"). The **Erasmian exception** (named for Erasmus's exegesis) extended the desertion ground to allow remarriage after abandonment by an unbelieving spouse. **Anabaptist** traditions tended toward stricter indissolubility within community discipline. **David Instone-Brewer** has argued that all 1st-century Jews assumed at least the Shammaite ground (adultery) was valid, that Jesus's "no exception clause" in Mark presumes that ground without specifying it, and that Matthew's ***porneia*** clause (5:32, 19:9) makes explicit what was implicit. The **Mark-Matthew comparison** is structurally significant: Matthew has "except for ***porneia***" (sexual immorality), Mark has no exception. Joel Marcus and Yarbro Collins read Mark as the more stringent original; Matthew adapts for a community navigating actual case-by-case discipline.

The **rich man** scene generated the second-most divisive reception history.

The **literal-radical reading** runs from the early church (Acts 4:32-35; Didache 1; Justin Martyr 1 Apology 14) through the desert fathers (Antony of Egypt sold his land after hearing this passage in church and went to the desert) through Francis of Assisi (whose conversion was sealed by hearing Matthew 19:21) to contemporary movements (Catholic Worker, **Shane Claiborne**, **John Howard Yoder**). Yoder (*Politics of Jesus*) treats the call as a paradigmatic political-economic ethics for the Christian community — not a counsel of perfection for the elite few but the basic shape of discipleship.

The **counsels-of-perfection reading** developed in patristic and medieval Catholicism: the call to sell all is **for those called to monastic vocation**; ordinary Christians are called only to right relation to wealth, not its renunciation. Aquinas (*Summa Theologica* II-II, Q. 184-186) systematized this as the distinction between **precepts** (binding on all) and **counsels** (binding only on those called to higher perfection). The Reformers largely rejected this two-tier ethic; Luther denounced monastic vows as a denial of the priesthood of all believers.

The **idiomatic-spiritual reading** (Augustine, Calvin, much modern evangelicalism) treats "**sell all**" as diagnostic rather than universally prescriptive: Jesus identified **this man's** specific idolatry (wealth) and called for its renunciation; the call to discipleship is the same for all but addresses each person's particular bondage. **N.T. Wright** (*Mark for Everyone*) reads it through the lens of Israel's vocation: Jesus is calling for a Jubilee restructuring (Leviticus 25) and the man cannot enter the kingdom because he cannot release his hold on accumulated wealth.

The **camel-through-needle** generated patristic ingenuity. **Origen**, **Cyril of Alexandria**, and others treated it as a deliberate impossibility intended to drive the disciples to verse 27 ("**with God all things are possible**"). The medieval **Needle Gate** allegory, debunked above, attempted to soften the impossibility but at the cost of the saying's force.

The ***lytron anti pollōn*** (10:45) is among the most theologically loaded sentences in the New Testament. **Origen** (*Commentary on Matthew* 16.8) developed the **ransom-paid-to-Satan** theory: humanity was held captive by the devil, who held a legal claim on sinners; God paid Christ as ransom but the devil, unable to hold him, was tricked. **Gustaf Aulén** (*Christus Victor*, 1931) argued this was the dominant patristic atonement model and the genuinely biblical one. **Anselm** (*Cur Deus Homo*, c. 1098) replaced the ransom-to-Satan model with the **satisfaction theory**: the offense of sin against God's honor required infinite satisfaction, which only the God-man could pay. **Abelard**, contemporary with Anselm, articulated a **moral influence** theory: the cross moves us by displaying God's love, transforming us morally. The **Reformers** developed Anselm's framework into **penal substitution**: Christ bore the **penalty** demanded by divine justice. **J.I. Packer** (*What Did the Cross Achieve?*, 1973) defended penal substitution as essential to the gospel; **Joel Green** and **Mark Baker** (*Recovering the Scandal of the Cross*) challenge its centrality. **Morna Hooker** (*Jesus and the Servant*) disputed the direct Isaiah 53 background; **N.T. Wright** affirms it. **Joel Marcus** (AB) treats 10:45 as a substitutionary saying without endorsing any particular atonement model.

**Bartimaeus**'s "**Son of David**" cry has generated rich Jewish-Christian comparative reading. **Geza Vermes** (*Jesus the Jew*) noted that Davidic-messianic expectation was concretely political in 1st-century Judaism: a son of David would restore the kingdom and expel Rome. Bartimaeus's cry is itself politically loaded; the rebukes from "many" who tell him to be silent (10:48) may reflect a fear of Roman attention as much as social disapproval. **Boyarin** reads the title as Mark's careful staging of Jewish-messianic vocabulary that the gospel will then reshape through the cross.

**Ched Myers** (*Binding the Strong Man*) reads the Bartimaeus pericope as Mark's structural climax of the journey section: the marginalized blind beggar becomes the model disciple, the wealthy seeker walks away grieving, and the inversion is the thesis statement of the whole gospel.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions sit at the heart of Mark 10.

**Tension 1: The relation of marriage to creation, fall, and discipleship.** Jesus appeals to creation (10:6-9) over against Mosaic concession (10:5). What does this entail for actual Christian practice in a fallen world?

**Position A (Catholic indissolubility)** — Trent, Familiaris Consortio, contemporary defenders like **Cardinal Robert Sarah** — argues that 10:9 establishes an ontological reality: God has joined; humans cannot un-join. Pastoral exceptions are made through annulment (recognition that no true sacramental bond was formed) but never dissolution. The steelman: this position takes Jesus's words with maximal seriousness and refuses cultural accommodation.

**Position B (Eastern Orthodox oikonomia)** — argues that the church's pastoral mission requires recognizing that marriages can die, and that requiring lifelong celibacy after marital death is itself a form of cruelty. The penitential rite for second marriages preserves the ideal while engaging the real. The steelman: this reflects the actual Pauline acknowledgment of marital reality (1 Cor 7:15) and the patristic-conciliar tradition of pastoral discernment.

**Position C (Reformed exception clauses)** — argues that the Matthean ***porneia*** exception and the Pauline desertion clause are normative; Mark's lack of exception is a function of context, not a teaching that no exceptions exist. The steelman: takes the canonical witness as a unity, allows for hard cases, refuses both legalistic indissolubility and casual divorce.

Where this leaves us: the modern American church operates de facto on Reformed exception logic, often without theological reflection. Mark 10:9 sits as a perpetual indictment of the casualness with which Christian marriages dissolve, and as a perpetual question to indissolubility traditions about cases of abuse, desertion, and adultery. The text resists settled resolution.

**Tension 2: Wealth, the kingdom, and the call to discipleship.** Jesus tells one man to sell all and tells the disciples that wealth makes entering the kingdom virtually impossible.

**Position A (Counsels of perfection)** — Aquinas, Catholic moral theology — distinguishes precepts (binding on all) from counsels (binding only on those called to perfection); ordinary Christians are called to detachment from wealth, not its renunciation. The steelman: maintains the radical call for those called while preserving a viable ethic for the rest.

**Position B (Universal radical call)** — Yoder, Claiborne, Catholic Worker — argues that the kingdom requires substantive economic reorganization; the early Jerusalem community's holding all in common (Acts 4:32-35) is normative, not aspirational. The steelman: refuses to neutralize Jesus's clearest teaching with class-protective hermeneutics.

**Position C (Diagnostic-spiritual reading)** — Augustine, Calvin, mainstream Reformed/evangelical — argues that "sell all" addresses **this man's** idolatry; the universal call is to lordship of Christ over wealth, expressed differently in different vocations. The steelman: acknowledges the variety of biblical teaching on wealth (Abraham, David, Lydia, Joseph of Arimathea — wealthy figures of faith) and refuses a flat reading.

Where this leaves us: the Western middle-class church's relationship to Jesus's wealth sayings is deeply uncomfortable. Mark 10:25 sits as a permanent disturbance of any spiritualizing reading. **Position C** is exegetically defensible but pastorally too easily evacuated of its force; **Position B** is uncomfortably correct in a globalized economy where Western Christian wealth coexists with global poverty; **Position A** preserves the radical witness in a structurally bounded form but at the cost of two-tier discipleship.

The unresolved question: if "**no one is good except God alone**" (10:18), and Jesus is good, what is Mark's Christology? The Arian controversy turned in part on this verse — Arius cited it against Christ's deity; Athanasius and Augustine argued Jesus was teaching the man to follow the implication of his own address. Mark himself does not resolve the question.

## The Thread

Mark 10:38-39's "**cup**" and "**baptism**" point forward to Mark 14:36 (Gethsemane: "**remove this cup from me**") and back to Mark 1:9-11 (Jesus's baptism, where the heavens are torn — the same verb that recurs at 15:38 with the Temple curtain).

Mark 10:45's ***lytron*** chains forward to Mark 14:24 ("**this is my blood of the covenant, poured out for many**" — the same ***polloi***), making the Last Supper the ritual enactment of the ransom saying.

Mark 10:34's prediction of mocking, spitting, flogging, and killing is precisely fulfilled in Mark 14:65, 15:15, 15:19, 15:24 — Mark expects readers to track the prediction-fulfillment correspondence in narrative real time.

Mark 10:31's "**many who are first will be last, and the last first**" reverberates through the passion narrative: the disciples flee, the centurion confesses, the women remain at the cross — the last become first.
