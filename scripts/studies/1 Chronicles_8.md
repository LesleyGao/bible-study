# 1 Chronicles 8 — Benjamin Expanded and the Saulide Line

## Lens 1 — Historical World

The Chronicler returns to Benjamin in chapter 8 even though he has already covered the tribe in 7:6-12. The doubling is purposeful: Benjamin gets a full chapter because Saul belongs to Benjamin, and Saul is the necessary preface to the David-narrative that begins in chapter 10. The chapter's terminus is the Saulide genealogy in verses 33-40, traced from Ner through Kish to Saul, Jonathan, Eshbaal, Merib-baal, and a long descendant-line down to Azel and his six sons. Everything before is preparation; everything after — beginning with chapter 9's repetition of the same Saulide line — is the bridge into chapter 10's death-narrative.

The book is anonymous; rabbinic tradition (b. Bava Batra 15a) attributes it to Ezra, but modern scholarship dates composition to Persian-period Yehud somewhere between roughly 400 and 350 BC. Sara Japhet's *I & II Chronicles* (OTL, 1993), Gary Knoppers's *I Chronicles 1-9* (Anchor Bible, 2003), Hugh Williamson's *1 and 2 Chronicles* (NCBC, 1982), Ralph Klein's *1 Chronicles* (Hermeneia, 2006), Roddy Braun (WBC, 1986), Mark Boda, and Steven McKenzie all agree on the broad outlines: an anonymous Levite, possibly a temple singer, working in a small Persian-imperial province.

The historical issue this chapter raises most acutely is the *baal*-name preservation. In verse 33, Saul's son is named *Eshbaal* (אֶשְׁבַּעַל) — "man of Baal" or "Baal exists" — where 2 Samuel 2:8 calls him *Ish-bosheth* (אִישׁ־בֹּשֶׁת), "man of shame." In verse 34, Jonathan's son is *Merib-baal* (מְרִיב־בַּעַל) — "Baal contends" or "warrior of Baal" — where 2 Samuel 4:4 calls him *Mephibosheth* (מְפִיבֹשֶׁת), "from the mouth of shame." The Samuel form replaces *baal* with *bosheth*; the Chronicler retains *baal*. P. Kyle McCarter's two-volume *I-II Samuel* (Anchor Bible, 1980, 1984), Mark Smith's *The Early History of God* (1990, 2nd ed. 2002), and Raz Kletter's archaeological surveys all converge on the explanation: *baal* in pre-monarchic and early monarchic Israel was a generic divine epithet meaning "lord," and Israelite parents gave their children *baal*-theophoric names as a matter of course. Saul's grandson Merib-baal would have been named for YHWH-as-lord, not for the Canaanite storm god.

The shift from *baal* to *bosheth* is a Deuteronomistic editorial move. Once *baal* had become exclusively associated with the Canaanite deity (after Hosea, Jeremiah, the late Deuteronomistic editors), the original *baal*-names became theologically embarrassing, and the Samuel text was systematically altered to read *bosheth* — literally "shame" — as a polemical replacement. The Chronicler's text, working from older Benjaminite genealogical sources, preserves the unaltered form. This is one of the clearest examples of textual archaeology in the Hebrew Bible: Chronicles, often dismissed as a late and theologically tendentious work, here preserves an older textual tradition than its Samuel parallel.

The Hebrew terms worth marking in this chapter: *avot* (אָבוֹת), "fathers' houses," the basic Persian-period administrative unit; *ra'shei avot* (רָאשֵׁי אָבוֹת), "heads of fathers' houses," the technical term for clan elders in vv. 6, 10, 13, 28; and *gibborei chayil* (גִּבּוֹרֵי חַיִל), "mighty warriors," the military-census formula carried over from chapter 7. The geographic terms — Gibeon, Geba, Anathoth (implied via Anthothijah), Aijalon, Ono, Lod, Manahath — map onto a real Benjamite territory that the Persian-period community would have recognized. Lod and Ono in particular (v. 12) were Benjamite settlements just east of the coastal plain that show up in Ezra 2:33 and Nehemiah 7:37 among the returnees, suggesting the Chronicler is anchoring his Benjamite genealogy in lineages that returned from exile.

Archaeologically, Gibeon (modern el-Jib) has been excavated by James Pritchard, who recovered the famous wine-jar handles inscribed *gb'n* — confirming Gibeon's identity and its Iron Age importance. Saul's connection to Gibeon (his ancestor Jeiel "the father of Gibeon," v. 29) is plausibly ancient. The Saulide cult-center may originally have been at Gibeon before consolidation around Gibeah of Saul (Tell el-Ful).

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter is built in two large blocks. The first (vv. 1-28) is a segmented Benjamite genealogy moving through Benjamin's five sons (Bela, Ashbel, Aharah, Nohah, Rapha), then through Bela's sons, then through the more obscure Shaharaim and Elpaal lines, and finally to a list of clan-heads who "lived in Jerusalem" (v. 28). The second block (vv. 29-40) is the Saulide branch: Jeiel of Gibeon, his sons including Kish and Ner, Saul's line through Jonathan, Merib-baal, Micah, and a long descendant chain that ends with Azel and his six sons plus Eshek's three sons.

The literary structure is a classic Chronicler move: a wider segmented genealogy whose rhetorical purpose is to deliver a focused linear genealogy. The wider Benjamite lines establish the tribe's depth and breadth; the Saulide line establishes the legitimate house from which Saul came. The fact that the Saulide line continues for thirteen generations beyond Saul (down to Azel's sons in v. 38) is theologically loaded — it tells us that even after Saul's catastrophic failure, his house continued, and the Benjamite tribal identity survived not just the loss of the kingship but the trauma of the king's death.

Two Hebrew literary features deserve attention. First, the recurring formula *eleh ra'shei avot l'toldotam* (אֵלֶּה רָאשֵׁי אָבוֹת לְתֹלְדוֹתָם), "these were heads of fathers' houses according to their generations," in verse 28, brackets the first block. The formula is administrative-cultic: it situates these lines within the kind of Persian-period roster a temple scribe would maintain.

Second, the geographical-historical interjections give the genealogy texture. Verse 6 mentions an exile of Benjaminites *to Manahath* — an unexplained reference, likely to a forced migration within the land. Verse 8 has Shaharaim fathering sons "in the country of Moab" after sending away two wives — a divorce-and-remarriage story compressed into a single phrase. Verse 12 credits Elpaal's sons with building Ono and Lod. Verse 13 credits Beriah and Shema with causing "the inhabitants of Gath to flee" — a Benjamite military exploit otherwise unattested. These narrative fragments embedded in the genealogy suggest the Chronicler is working with a rich body of tribal lore, much of it not preserved elsewhere.

The Saulide section's literary peak is the catalog of names in verses 33-40. *Ner holid et Kish, v'Kish holid et Sha'ul, v'Sha'ul holid et Y'honatan v'et Malki-shua v'et Avinadav v'et Eshbaal* — "Ner fathered Kish, Kish fathered Saul, Saul fathered Jonathan, Malchi-shua, Abinadab, and Eshbaal." The verb *holid* (הוֹלִיד), "fathered," repeated six times in two verses, drives the genealogy forward like a metronome. The list ends with Azel's six named sons and Eshek's three — and then a coda: "the sons of Ulam were men who were mighty warriors, bowmen, having many sons and grandsons, 150." A specific number, a specific weapon, a specific clan trait. Benjamin's archers were proverbial (Judges 20, 1 Chronicles 12:2); the chapter closes with a glimpse of that tribal vocation surviving long after the Saulide kingship has fallen.

## Lens 3 — Larger Story

In the Hebrew canonical order Chronicles closes the Tanakh, and the Saulide genealogy of chapter 8 functions as a memorial: Saul's house continues, named and remembered, even as the narrative is about to record his catastrophic death. The rabbis took the Saulide afterlife seriously. The Talmud (b. Yoma 22b) discusses Saul's reign sympathetically, and rabbinic tradition broadly preserved a complicated portrait — Saul as a tragic king whose moral failings did not erase his anointed status. Rashi, commenting on the Eshbaal/Ish-bosheth question, harmonizes the two names without flagging the theological-editorial issue, treating them as two names for one person. Radak (David Kimhi), more attentive to philological detail, notes the difference but does not pursue its religious-historical implications. Don Isaac Abarbanel's commentary on the Saulide line treats the long descendant chain (down to Azel's sons) as evidence that God preserves even the deposed house, a theme he develops in his messianic theology.

Modern Jewish scholarship engages the *baal*-name evidence directly. Sara Japhet, in her OTL commentary, recognizes the Chronicler's older textual tradition. Isaac Kalimi (*The Reshaping of Ancient Israelite History*, 2005) treats the Saulide genealogy as an example of the Chronicler's deliberate harmonization-and-preservation method: he keeps older material even when it cuts against later theological norms. Gary Knoppers's Anchor Bible volume gives perhaps the most thorough treatment, working through the Persian-period Benjamite resurgence (the post-exilic Benjaminites of Ezra-Nehemiah) and showing how the Chronicler's expanded Benjamin genealogy serves the political-cultic claims of returning Benjamite families.

The Christian trajectory has typically passed over chapter 8 quickly. Jerome translated the Saulide names without comment. Cassiodorus and the medieval glossators barely noted the chapter. Aquinas occasionally cited the Saulide line in arguments about the legitimacy of deposed houses. Calvin's 1561 commentary on Chronicles is brief here, treating the chapter as a record of God's faithfulness in remembering even the failed king's family. Modern Christian scholarship — Williamson, Klein, Knoppers, Boda, Louis Jonker — has begun to take the *baal*-name evidence as a window into the textual history and the religious history of pre-monarchic Israel.

The most significant scholarly reception of this chapter in the last fifty years has been textual-critical and religious-historical rather than theological. P. Kyle McCarter's Anchor Bible Samuel volumes (1980, 1984) used the Chronicles parallel to argue that the Samuel text had been deliberately altered. Mark Smith's *The Early History of God* (1990) drew on this and similar evidence to argue for an early Israelite religious culture in which YHWH was *one* god among many, with *baal* as a generic epithet that only later became polemicized. Raz Kletter's archaeological surveys of Iron Age Israelite religion document the slow theological monotheizing that Chronicles 8 here partially preserves a record of. The chapter has become a primary text for the study of Israelite religious history rather than primarily a theological text.

The New Testament's use of this material is essentially zero. Saul of Tarsus identifies himself as "of the tribe of Benjamin" (Phil 3:5, Rom 11:1), invoking Benjamite identity in a way that presupposes the kind of genealogical memory the Chronicler preserves, but he does not cite the Saulide line specifically. Ehud, Shimei, the bowmen of Benjamin — none receive direct New Testament attention. The chapter's gravitational pull is entirely toward chapter 10's narrative of Saul's death, and the New Testament reads that material primarily through Acts 13:21-22, which paraphrases the Chronicler's negative judgment without engaging the genealogical material.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

The Chronicler's distinctive theological agenda in chapter 8 is twofold. First, the all-Israel theology requires that Benjamin be remembered with the same care as Judah and Levi — and remembered with extra weight because Benjamite returnees were a major component of Persian-period Yehud, and because Saul's house, however failed, is a piece of Israel's own constitutional memory. Second, the chapter sets up the theological move of chapter 10: by establishing the Saulide line in detail, the Chronicler makes the loss of the kingship in chapter 10 a *family* loss, not just a political one. The genealogy is the moral weight that gives the death-narrative its tragic specificity.

The theological tension that this chapter forces is the tension between *preserving* and *editing* sacred memory. The Chronicler preserves *baal*-names that the Deuteronomist had polemically replaced with *bosheth*. He preserves Saul's family in detail thirteen generations after the king's catastrophic failure. He preserves Benjamite military traditions and city-foundings without theological moralizing. The Deuteronomist's move was to clean up the record; the Chronicler's move is to keep the record rough. Both moves are theological choices.

Three positions on this material are alive in the tradition. The first, the harmonizing-tradition position, treats Eshbaal and Ish-bosheth as equivalent names without religious-historical depth, and reads the Chronicler as a faithful scribe preserving alternate spellings. Rashi, Calvin, and much of pre-critical Christian and Jewish exegesis sit here. The strength of this position is its piety and its attention to canonical coherence; the weakness is that it cannot account for the systematic Deuteronomistic editorial pattern.

The second position, the religious-historical, treats the *baal*-names as evidence for an early Israelite religion in which Yahwism was not yet differentiated from broader West Semitic religion, and the Chronicler's preservation as accidental — he is working with old sources and has not noticed the theological problem. McCarter, Smith, and the broader history-of-religion school sit here. The strength of this view is its explanatory power; the weakness is that it can collapse the Chronicler into a passive transmitter and miss the theological intent of his preservation.

The third position, attempted by Williamson, Knoppers, Klein, and Japhet, is a both-and: the Chronicler preserves the older forms because his theology of all-Israel and continuous remembrance does not let him sanitize the sources. The unaltered *baal*-names are not a mistake; they are an act of theological integrity — the Chronicler will not edit Saul's grandson's name to make Saul look better, and he will not edit Israel's pre-monarchic religious vocabulary to make Israel look more theologically tidy. The strongest case for this reading is that it matches what the Chronicler does elsewhere: preserve, structure, name, even when the named realities are uncomfortable.

To steelman the editorial-piety reading: there is a real theological cost to preserving *baal*-names without comment, especially in a Persian-period community whose identity was being defined against Canaanite-Phoenician-Aramaean religious environments. The Deuteronomist's edit was pastoral. The Chronicler's preservation might be antiquarian rather than theological — he kept the names because they were in his sources, not because he had a deliberate program.

Where this leaves us: chapter 8 raises the question of how communities of faith handle the unfinished textual and religious history embedded in their sacred memory. The Chronicler's answer is that you keep the rough edges. Eshbaal stays Eshbaal. Saul's line stays in the record. Sheerah of chapter 7 keeps her city. The all-Israel theology requires not a sanitized memory but a thick one. The closing question is whether modern communities of faith — facing their own pressures to clean up sacred memory for present-day comfort — can imitate the Chronicler's tolerance for theologically untidy preservation.

## The Thread

From Eshbaal to Merib-baal to the 150 Benjamite bowmen of Ulam's line — the Chronicler keeps the older names, the failed king's family, and the obscure clan traditions, all without sanitizing them. To remember Benjamin in this much detail is to confess that even the failed house belongs to Israel, and that sacred memory is sturdier than the editor's reflex to tidy it.
