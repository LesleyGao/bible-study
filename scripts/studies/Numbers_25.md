# Numbers 25: Zimri and Cozbi

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Numbers 25 opens at Shittim (*haššiṭṭîm*, הַשִּׁטִּים), the acacia-grove encampment on the plains of Moab where Israel has been stationed through the Balaam narrative. It will appear again in Joshua 3:1 as the departure point for the crossing of the Jordan. Archaeologically, Shittim is widely identified with Tell el-Hammam or nearby Tell Kefrein in the southern Jordan Valley, though scholarly consensus is not settled (Randall Younker, Steven Collins have offered differing proposals). The site is within sight of Mount Peor (Khirbet ʿAyun Musa or nearby) where Balaam delivered his oracles. The spatial compression is narratively deliberate: directly below the mountain from which Balaam blessed Israel, Israel engages the local cult and is blessed with a plague.

The deity at the center of the apostasy is **Baal-Peor** (*baʿal pĕʿôr*, בַּעַל פְּעוֹר, vv. 3, 5). This is a local manifestation of Baal — the Canaanite storm and fertility god known from the Ugaritic tablets — specific to the Peor region. The pattern of localized Baalim (Baal-Meon, Baal-Hazor, Baal-Gad, Baal-Berith) is attested archaeologically and in the Mesha Stele's ninth-century Moabite inscriptional record. Israel's "yoking" (*ṣāmad*, צָמַד) itself to Baal-Peor (v. 3) uses the verb of tethering oxen to a pole — a striking reversal of the "yoke of the Torah" theme that will develop in later rabbinic literature. Hosea 9:10 looks back on this incident with horror: "They came to Baal-peor and consecrated themselves to the thing of shame, and became detestable like the thing they loved."

The nature of the cult is debated. Psalm 106:28 calls Baal-Peor's worshipers those who "ate the sacrifices offered to the dead" — which has suggested to some scholars (Milgrom, Levine) that Baal-Peor involved a chthonic or necromantic element. Others have argued from the juxtaposition of sexual license and ritual meal for a fertility-cult reading familiar from Ugaritic, though the evidence for "sacred prostitution" in ANE religion has been revised downward by scholars like Mary Joan Winn Leith. What the text describes clearly is three stages: sexual relations with Moabite (and Midianite) women, meals at their sacrifices, and bowing down to their gods (vv. 1-3).

Archaeologically, the eighth-century **Deir ʿAlla inscription** (continuing its relevance to the Balaam cycle) may contain the only extra-biblical reference to the Peor event, though the text is fragmentary. Jo Ann Hackett's edition tentatively proposed a Combination II reading referring to a seer and a Moabite religious event; the reading is debated.

The principals in the central scene are named with precision. **Phinehas** (*pînĕḥās*, פִּינְחָס) is grandson of Aaron and son of Eleazar, putting him in the direct high-priestly line; his name may be Egyptian in origin (*pa-nehasi*, "the Nubian"), a plausible Mosaic-era onomastic trace that Hoffmeier and Kitchen have noted. **Zimri ben Salu** (*zimrî ben-sālûʾ*, v. 14) is identified as "chief of a father's house belonging to the Simeonites" — a senior tribal figure, not a commoner; his brazenness is exacerbated by his rank. **Cozbi bat Zur** (*kozbî bat-ṣûr*, v. 15) is "the daughter of Zur, who was the tribal head of a father's house in Midian." The naming is unusual. Women are rarely named in Torah narrative; Cozbi's name is given and her paternity is specified as royal — the incident is thus both a sexual apostasy and an inter-chiefly political act. The Midianites, Israel's on-and-off kin through Moses' marriage to Zipporah, are here explicitly enemies.

Three Hebrew terms carry weight in this chapter. First, *qinʾâ* (קִנְאָה, vv. 11, 13), "jealousy/zeal" — a charged word used of both YHWH's covenantal jealousy (Exod 20:5, Deut 4:24) and of human zeal for God. Phinehas is praised for being *qānōʾ ʾet-qinʾātî*, "jealous with my jealousy" — the human passion aligned with the divine. Second, *hōqaʿ* (הוֹקַע, v. 4), "to hang, impale, expose" — a rare verb describing judicial execution with exposed corpses, as in 2 Samuel 21:9 of the Gibeonites' execution of Saul's sons. The precise form of execution ("before the LORD, in the sun") is a ritualized public shame-execution. Third, *bĕrît šālôm* / *bĕrît kĕhunnat ʿôlām* (בְּרִית שָׁלוֹם / בְּרִית כְּהֻנַּת עוֹלָם, vv. 12-13), "covenant of peace" / "covenant of perpetual priesthood" — language that will reverberate through Malachi 2:5 (covenant of life and peace with Levi), Isaiah 54:10 (covenant of peace not shaken), and Ezekiel 34:25 and 37:26 (messianic covenant of peace).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Numbers 25 is a notorious redactional knot. Most critical scholars (Noth, Gray, Milgrom, Levine, Budd) see two (or more) originally distinct traditions spliced together. Verses 1-5, in which the women are **Moabite** and the offense is Baal-Peor worship, form one unit; verses 6-18, in which the woman is **Midianite** and the offense is the public sexual act plus apparently the Peor cult, form another. The seam at v. 6 is audible: "And behold, one of the people of Israel came and brought a Midianite woman…" The Moabite frame dominates vv. 1-5 and then disappears; the Midianite frame (continuing into the war against Midian in ch. 31) dominates vv. 6-18.

Wellhausen assigned vv. 6-18 to P (Priestly), citing the Aaronide interest in Phinehas, the "covenant of perpetual priesthood" language, and the priestly vocabulary. Noth followed this allocation. Milgrom's JPS commentary nuances but does not dispute the Priestly framing of Phinehas's commissioning. The first unit, on this reading, is older — a J/E account of Baal-Peor apostasy — and the Priestly editor sutured in the Phinehas story to establish the zealous priesthood's legitimacy.

The literary craft of the final form, however, is considerable. Robert Alter, Jacob Milgrom, and Baruch Levine all note that the pairing of units creates a dense semantic field: the general apostasy in vv. 1-5 provides the context against which the specific act in v. 6 is shocking (Zimri and Cozbi act *during* the weeping ordered as response to vv. 1-5); Moses has just commanded the judges to kill the idolaters (v. 5), and Phinehas acts on that command with greater decisiveness than any of the judges. The doubling of terror — general plague, specific atrocity — intensifies the narrative.

The chapter's climactic act — Phinehas's spear-thrust — is described in graphic economy: *wayyidqōr ʾet-šĕnêhem ʾēt ʾîš yiśrāʾēl wĕʾet-hāʾiššâ ʾel-qŏbātāh* (v. 8). "He pierced both of them, the man of Israel and the woman through her belly." The rare noun *qŏbâ* (קֹבָה) is a hapax, etymologically uncertain — possibly "tent-chamber," possibly the female pubic region (rabbinic reading). The ambiguity produces the graphic sense that Phinehas killed them in the act of intercourse, spearing them while coupled — a reading the Talmud and most medieval commentators adopt.

The "covenant of peace" awarded in vv. 12-13 is syntactically structured as divine speech interrupting the narrative; the reward is framed as permanent, pertaining to Phinehas's descendants forever. This establishes the Zadokite line (Ezra 7:1-5 traces the high priesthood through Phinehas to Zadok to Ezra), making this chapter foundational for Second Temple priestly politics.

Textually, the MT is relatively stable. The Samaritan Pentateuch has minor variants. The LXX preserves the Moabite/Midianite tension without smoothing it. The most substantive text-critical point is the casting of "Beth-peor" at v. 3 — some manuscripts have Baal-peor, some Beth-peor; the confusion reflects the cult's tight association with a specific cultic site.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Jewish readings

Phinehas is a deeply uncomfortable figure in rabbinic Judaism. The Bible clearly praises him (Ps 106:30-31 is the most exalted non-Torah reference), but the rabbis' theological and halakhic instincts rebelled at the precedent of private zealotry. The Babylonian Talmud, **Sanhedrin 82a-b**, offers one of the most extraordinary rabbinic discussions in the tractate. The rabbis read the biblical praise alongside a set of sharp qualifications: Phinehas's act was permitted only because Zimri was caught *in flagrante* (if he had withdrawn, no license to kill); the action was permitted only to a zealous prosecutor acting on impulse, not to anyone who decided afterward to kill on behalf of God (*hakol halakhah ve'ein morin ken*, "this is the halakhah but we do not rule this way" — a startling formula). If Zimri had turned and killed Phinehas first, Phinehas would have had no recourse — i.e., Phinehas's "right" was not a legal right but an extraordinary dispensation. The rabbis quarantined the precedent so tightly that in practical halakhah it cannot be imitated.

*Mishnah Sanhedrin* 9:6 classifies "one who has relations with a Gentile woman" and adds "zealots may attack him" (*qanāʾîm pōgĕʿîm bô*) — codifying Phinehas's act as halakhically attested but ritually hedged. The later tradition (Rashi, Ramban, Maimonides) debates whether this halakhic category is operative in practice; the consensus is that it is not, and that the ruling applies only in a narrow historical window with judicial sanction.

The Maccabean revolt invoked Phinehas as its charter hero. In **1 Maccabees 2:24-27**, when a Jew approaches the altar at Modi'in to sacrifice to pagan gods under Seleucid duress, Mattathias the priest sees it "and burned with zeal according to the law, as Phinehas did in the matter of Zimri son of Salu; and he ran and killed him upon the altar." Mattathias then calls out, "Let everyone who is zealous for the law and supports the covenant follow me." The Hasmonean self-understanding is Phinehas-shaped. Josephus's *Zealots* (Zealotai) in the First Jewish War (66-70 AD) took their name and program from Phinehas; Josephus uses *zēlōtai* with the Phinehas resonance explicit.

Medieval commentators struggle. Rashi accepts the text's praise but follows the talmudic qualifications. Ramban (Nachmanides) argues that Phinehas's "covenant of peace" is specifically designed to heal the breach created by violence — Phinehas has just murdered a tribal chief in cold blood, and needs divine protection from Zimri's clan's revenge. Ibn Ezra notes the difficulty of reconciling the praise with the normal prohibition against private execution. Maimonides in *Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Issurei Biah* 12:4-5 codifies the halakhic category with all its restrictions.

Modern Jewish scholarship processes Phinehas through the trauma of twentieth-century religious violence. Yeshayahu Leibowitz rejected zealotry in principle as idolatrous; Nehama Leibowitz's studies in Numbers treat Phinehas with considerable unease. Jonathan Sacks's *Covenant & Conversation* addresses the zealotry directly: the "covenant of peace" awarded Phinehas is, on Sacks's reading, not a reward for violence but a divine redirection — God is telling the zealot, "from now on, peace" — turning a man of the spear into a man of the altar. Avivah Zornberg reads the episode with psychoanalytic depth, focusing on the community's paralysis ("weeping") versus Phinehas's decisive action.

### Christian readings

Phinehas has been invoked in Christian tradition both for and against. The New Testament itself is silent on him by name — a noteworthy absence, given his prominence in the Second Temple imaginary. Paul's discussion of Abrahamic righteousness in **Romans 4** echoes Psalm 106:31's *wattēḥāšeb lô liṣdāqâ* ("it was counted to him as righteousness") from the Phinehas episode — but Paul applies it not to Phinehas's violent zeal but to Abraham's faith. This may be deliberate. Paul reorients the logic of imputed righteousness away from zealous action toward faith in God's promise. The silent rewriting is one of the most theologically loaded intertextual moves in Paul.

Patristic authors handle Phinehas carefully. Origen's *Homilies on Numbers* 20 reads Phinehas spiritually as the soul's zeal against its own vices — the "spear" is discipline, the "Zimri and Cozbi" are temptations within the self. This allegorization blunts the political edge and makes the story an ethics of self-mortification. Augustine in *Contra Faustum* 22.79 defends the Old Testament against Manichaean criticism by arguing Phinehas acted under special divine command; his act is not a model for Christians but a sign of God's authority to direct specific responses to apostasy. Ambrose in his ethical writings uses Phinehas as an example of zeal qualified by providence.

Aquinas in the *Summa* (II-II q. 108 a. 1-2 on vengeance, II-II q. 188 on the active life) treats Phinehas as an example of how zeal for God can, in extraordinary circumstances and under legitimate authority, justify violent response — but he hedges heavily. Aquinas is aware that the Phinehas precedent is dangerous, and he circumscribes it with conditions that most concrete cases will fail. Calvin in his commentaries on Numbers and Psalms is more directly favorable: Phinehas models holy zeal against idolatry, and Calvin commends his decisiveness against temporizing. The text's praise is read as genuine endorsement.

The **Crusades and the wars of religion** in Europe invoked Phinehas explicitly. Bernard of Clairvaux's preaching for the Second Crusade cites zealotry against infidels with Phinehas language. Protestant and Catholic combatants in the French wars of religion, the Thirty Years' War, and the English Civil War both invoked Phinehas to justify sectarian violence. By the eighteenth century, the Phinehas-narrative had been so weaponized that mainstream Christian thinkers began to recoil.

Modern Christian ethics has mostly rejected Phinehas as a model. Karl Barth, Dietrich Bonhoeffer, John Howard Yoder, Stanley Hauerwas, Miroslav Volf — the traditions disagree on pacifism but agree that Phinehas's specific act is not a template for Christian life. Walter Brueggemann treats the episode as a locus of biblical violence that must be confessed and read against the grain of the New Testament cross. Ephraim Radner's *Leviticus* commentary (Brazos) approaches the chapter via the motif of divine wrath and its mediation; Fretheim reads the violence within a broader canon of divine suffering and restraint.

The contemporary horror at Phinehas's act — shared, to greater or lesser degrees, by most modern Jewish and Christian interpreters — is itself a theological data point. The text praises what our moral intuitions condemn. This is the hermeneutical crisis of the chapter.

### Convergences and divergences

Jewish and Christian traditions converge on a deep uncomfortableness with Phinehas-as-model. They converge on the halakhic/ethical move to quarantine the precedent. The Bavli's "this is the halakhah but we do not rule this way" and Aquinas's heavy conditionals and modern Christian pacifism all function similarly: praise the text, forbid the imitation. They diverge in how they distribute the praise and the critique. The rabbis preserve more of the positive force (the perpetual priesthood, the covenant of peace, the zeal for Torah) while restricting the application; Christian tradition generally acknowledges the text's approval but is less willing to integrate it into ongoing practice. Paul's silent transfer of "counted as righteousness" from Phinehas's violence to Abraham's faith is a structural reorientation that most Christian ethics has followed.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions.

**Tension 1: When, if ever, is "zeal for God" legitimate as a ground for violence?** The text unambiguously praises Phinehas for being "jealous with my jealousy" and grants him a covenant of peace and perpetual priesthood. Psalm 106:30-31 reinforces the praise. But the halakhic tradition (Sanhedrin 82a) and the Christian tradition (Aquinas's heavy conditionals) hedge the precedent to near-impossibility. The biblical enthusiasm and the halakhic/ethical restriction sit in tension.

*Strong-endorsement position (Zealot tradition, Maccabean/Mattathias, some Crusader theology, some Reformation-era polemics):* Phinehas is a genuine model. There are moments — defined by public apostasy in the face of divine command, clear and unambiguous — when the human person is authorized to act with deadly force in defense of the covenant. The hedges are compromises driven by post-exilic powerlessness or modern ethical squeamishness; they do not reflect the text's actual teaching. This position is now mostly disavowed in mainstream traditions but has deep scriptural roots and was operative for long stretches of Jewish and Christian history.

*Strong-pacifist position (modern Christian pacifism, Yoder, Hauerwas, rabbinic "we do not rule this way," Yeshayahu Leibowitz):* Phinehas's act is *not* normative. The text's praise must be read canonically against the New Testament cross (for Christians) or against the rabbinic halakhah (for Jews). The covenant of peace given to Phinehas is God's way of stopping the violence, not rewarding it; it is a divine redirection of zeal into priestly service. To take Phinehas as a model is to misread the episode catastrophically. The twentieth century's religious violence has made this position increasingly the consensus.

*Steelman — the circumscribed emergency position (Aquinas, Ramban, classical rabbinic, most serious premodern readers):* Zealotry is in principle a legitimate category of moral response to covenant-threatening apostasy, but it is so narrowly conditioned in its legitimate application that, in practice, almost no one qualifies. The conditions Sanhedrin 82a lays out — caught in the act, zealous prosecutor acting spontaneously, no recourse if the guilty party defends himself — effectively restrict the category to the single historical event. Aquinas's conditions — legitimate authority, right intention, extreme necessity, proportion — similarly strangle the category. The premodern traditions wanted to say: yes, covenantal zeal can legitimate extraordinary response; no, you personally are not in a position to practice it. This is the steelman of the restrictive-endorsement tradition.

**Where this leaves us:** The text describes an action the canonical tradition praises but cannot, without destroying itself, normalize. Both Judaism and Christianity inherited the praise and the restriction together. The modern hermeneutical task is neither to dismiss the praise nor to legitimize the imitation, but to read the Phinehas episode as a description of what God was willing to use in a specific covenantal emergency, while recognizing that the New Testament cross and the rabbinic emphasis on Torah-study reorient the concept of zeal away from the spear entirely.

**Tension 2: "Counted to him as righteousness" — Phinehas's violence and Abraham's faith.** Psalm 106:31, of Phinehas: *wattēḥāšeb lô liṣdāqâ lĕdōr wādōr*, "and that was counted to him as righteousness from generation to generation." Genesis 15:6, of Abraham: *wattēḥāšeb lô ṣĕdāqâ*, "and he counted it to him as righteousness." The phrase is identical. Paul, in Romans 4, uses Genesis 15:6 to ground his doctrine of justification by faith — and does not mention Phinehas. This is one of the most theologically loaded silences in the New Testament.

*Pauline-faith-alone position (Protestant readings, Augustine, Luther, Calvin):* Paul knew both texts. He deliberately transferred the formula from Phinehas's violent zeal to Abraham's trusting faith. This is not an oversight; it is a reorientation. "Imputed righteousness" is, in Pauline theology, grounded in faith, not in zealous action. The silent exclusion of Phinehas from Romans 4 is a structural claim about what justifies.

*Rabbinic-both-count position (classical Jewish, some Catholic):* Both faith and zealous deed can count as righteousness. Phinehas's violence and Abraham's faith are both recognized by God. The Torah is capacious enough for both categories. Paul's reading selects one strand; the rabbinic tradition preserves both. This is not a Pauline versus Jewish conflict so much as two legitimate canonical emphases.

*Steelman — covenantal-faithfulness position (N. T. Wright, James Dunn, other "new perspective" readers, Ramban, some patristic):* "Counted as righteousness" in both Abraham and Phinehas names covenantal faithfulness, which manifests differently in different circumstances. Abraham's faithfulness took the form of trust in promise; Phinehas's took the form of covenantal zeal at a moment of ultimate breach. Both are forms of what Paul calls *pistis*. The Pauline shift is not from works to faith but from ethnic-marker "works of the Law" to the kind of covenantal faithfulness whose paradigm is Abraham's trust — which is neither an exclusion of Phinehas nor an endorsement of his violence but a claim about what being in right relation with God actually looks like in the messianic age. This position tries to integrate the rabbinic reading of both texts with Paul's specific deployment of Abraham.

**Where this leaves us:** The identical phrase applied to two radically different actions is not accidental; it is one of the Hebrew Bible's own interpretive keys. Paul's deliberate foregrounding of Abraham and silencing of Phinehas communicates a pastoral and theological orientation of the new covenant away from zealous violence and toward trust. Whether this is a total reorientation or a selective emphasis within a continuing tradition is the hermeneutical question that divides Protestant, Catholic, and Jewish readings. What is clear is that the chapter cannot be read without Psalm 106 and Romans 4 in the background, and the three texts together form one of the great canonical arguments about what righteousness looks like.

**Unresolved question:** How is the "covenant of perpetual priesthood" (v. 13) related to the Melchizedek priesthood of Psalm 110 and Hebrews 7? Phinehas's line — Aaronide, Zadokite — is promised perpetuity here. Psalm 110 promises a priesthood "after the order of Melchizedek, forever," which Hebrews reads as explicitly non-Aaronide and superior to the Levitical priesthood. The canonical tension between two "perpetual priesthoods" — the Phinehas-Zadok line and the Melchizedek line — is one of the deep structural problems of biblical theology. Qumran anticipated it with its dual messianism (priestly and royal). The Christian tradition resolves it by absorbing the Phinehas-Aaron priesthood into the Melchizedek-Christ priesthood; the rabbinic tradition, after the destruction of the Temple, effectively subordinated both to the work of Torah-study. The Phinehas covenant of perpetual priesthood is a promise the canonical text develops in directions Phinehas himself could not have foreseen.

## The Thread

Four non-obvious cross-references.

1. **Psalm 106:28-31 and Numbers 25** — the only major Old Testament meditation on the Peor event outside the Pentateuch reframes the violence theologically: "Then Phinehas stood up and intervened (*wayyaʿămōd pînĕḥās wayĕpallēl*), and the plague was stayed. And that was counted to him as righteousness, from generation to generation forever." The verb *pālal* ("intervene," or possibly "pray/judge") is nuanced; some rabbinic readings take it to mean Phinehas prayed as well as acted, softening the pure-violence profile. The Psalm's memory is the bridge between Numbers 25 and Romans 4, making the direct Pauline echo possible.

2. **Malachi 2:4-7 and Numbers 25:12-13** — "My covenant with him was one of life and peace, and I gave them to him. It was a covenant of fear, and he feared me…The law of truth was in his mouth, and no wrong was found on his lips…" Malachi's oracle on the corrupt priesthood of his day explicitly reaches back to the "covenant of peace" given to Phinehas/Levi. The priestly ideal that Malachi invokes against the priests of his time is the ideal Phinehas established. The post-exilic priesthood is measured by whether it still carries the covenant of peace given at Peor.

3. **1 Maccabees 2:24-27 and Numbers 25:7-8** — Mattathias's zealous killing of the apostate Jew at Modi'in is presented with explicit Phinehas language. Mattathias is Phinehas in Hasmonean guise. This intertext is structurally decisive for Second Temple Judaism: the Maccabean self-understanding, and by extension the Zealots of the first century, and by extension the anti-Roman uprising of 66-70 AD and Bar Kokhba in 132-135 AD, all flow from Phinehas's precedent. Numbers 25 is the root text of Jewish zealotry as a religious-political category.

4. **Romans 4 and Psalm 106:31** — the silent transfer. Paul uses the Genesis 15:6 formula — *elogisthē autō eis dikaiosynēn*, "it was counted to him as righteousness" — and never cites the parallel in Psalm 106 applying the identical phrase to Phinehas. This is one of the most theologically loaded silences in the New Testament, and it signals Paul's deliberate reorientation: covenantal righteousness in the messianic age is indexed to Abraham's trust, not Phinehas's spear. The early church preserved both texts; Paul made clear which one he meant to be foundational.
