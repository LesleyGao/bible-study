# Joshua 20: Six Gates Against the Hot Blood

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Joshua 20 drops the reader into an unusual moment: the military campaigns are over, the tribal allotments have been distributed, and now the Israelite polity must be configured for the ordinary life that follows war. What this chapter describes is a legal infrastructure — six fortified cities, three east of the Jordan and three west, designated as asylum for a person who has killed another human being unintentionally. The places named are real, identifiable, and geographically deliberate: Kedesh in the Upper Galilee (Tel Qedesh, Naphtali), Shechem in the central hill country (Tell Balata, Ephraim), Kiriath-arba / Hebron in the southern hill country (Tel Hebron, Judah); across the Jordan, Bezer on the Moabite tableland (location disputed but probably in the plateau east of the Dead Sea, from the tribe of Reuben), Ramoth in Gilead (likely Tell er-Rumeith or Tell al-Husn, from Gad), and Golan in Bashan (possibly Sahm el-Jaulan east of the Sea of Galilee, from Manasseh). The spacing is almost engineered. A manslayer almost anywhere in the land could reach one within a day's hard walk. Rabbinic tradition (Mishnah Makkot 2:4) later states that the roads to these cities were to be kept wide, clearly marked with "refuge, refuge" signs, and bridges maintained — a detail that likely reflects a post-monarchic imagination but captures the chapter's animating concern: the system must actually work under stress.

The setting belongs to the Iron Age I / early Iron Age II landscape of highland Canaan. If one reads the chapter as reflecting Late Bronze or early Iron Age realities (the high dating, around 1406 BCE, associated with a literal pre-monarchic conquest), Shechem's prominence fits neatly with the Amarna letters' attention to Lab'ayu of Shechem in the fourteenth century. If one reads with the low dating (c. 1200–1150 BCE, closer to the Merneptah Stele of 1208, which gives the first extrabiblical mention of "Israel"), the chapter reflects a retrojection of early Israelite legal consciousness into the period of settlement. Most critical scholars — following Martin Noth's 1943 *Überlieferungsgeschichtliche Studien* and Frank Moore Cross's 1973 dual-redaction model in *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* — place the composition of Joshua within the Deuteronomistic History (DtrH), with a Josianic edition around 622 BCE and an exilic edition after 587 BCE. On this view the asylum system described here is presented as Mosaic-Joshuan but codified and elaborated in the context of Deuteronomic centralization of sacrifice and the consequent need to reallocate the older, sanctuary-based asylum function to designated cities.

The material culture points to city gates of the type excavated at Gezer, Hazor, Megiddo, and Dan — four- or six-chambered gatehouses where elders sat to judge disputes, visible in the archaeological record from the tenth century onward. The chapter's description of the fugitive standing "at the entrance of the gate of the city" (20:4) and explaining his case to the elders maps onto this physical setting with surprising precision. The gate was simultaneously the civic courtroom, the economic marketplace, and the military choke point. Asylum law weaponized the same architecture for protection.

Several Hebrew terms carry the weight of the chapter. First, *rōṣēaḥ* (רֹצֵחַ), "manslayer" or "killer," in 20:3 — a term that ancient Hebrew, unlike modern English, does not resolve into "murderer" vs. "killer" without further qualification. The qualifier is *bišĕgāgâ* (בִּשְׁגָגָה), "by inadvertence," in Numbers 35:11 and echoed here. *Šĕgāgâ* (שְׁגָגָה) is the technical priestly and legal term for an unintentional act — Leviticus 4's sin offering covers *ḥēṭĕʾ bišĕgāgâ* (sin committed unknowingly), and the same conceptual category underwrites asylum. Intention is the axis on which the law turns. Second, *gōʾēl haddām* (גֹּאֵל הַדָּם), "avenger of blood" or more literally "redeemer of the blood," in 20:3, 5, 9. The *gōʾēl* is the nearest male kinsman charged with protecting the family's interests — ransoming enslaved relatives (Leviticus 25), marrying a childless widow (Ruth 4), and here, executing blood vengeance to prevent the shedding of a relative's blood from going unavenged. Third, *miqlāṭ* (מִקְלָט), "refuge" or "asylum," in 20:2 — from the root *qlṭ* meaning "to take in," a word that appears almost exclusively in these asylum texts. Fourth, *ʿēdâ* (עֵדָה), "congregation," in 20:6, 9 — the covenantal-legal assembly, the body before which the manslayer must stand for formal adjudication. And fifth, *kōhēn haggādōl* (כֹּהֵן הַגָּדוֹל), "the high priest," whose death terminates the manslayer's exile — a strange and generative provision explored below.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels matter here. The Code of Hammurabi §§206–214 distinguishes between intentional and unintentional wounding, imposing compensatory payment for accidental injury but capital or talionic punishment for intentional assault. Hittite Laws §§1–4 make a similar distinction, with §3 specifying that "if anyone strikes a free man or woman and he dies, but it is an accident, he shall bring the body" and pay compensation. Temple asylum — the idea that a sanctuary grounds inviolable refuge — is well attested in Hittite, Mesopotamian, and Greek contexts (the Greek *asylia* tradition at Olympia, Ephesus, and Delphi). What Israel's asylum system does differently, as Moshe Greenberg argued in his landmark 1959 essay "Some Postulates of Biblical Criminal Law," is separate asylum from temple cultus and distribute it across civic space, while insisting that intentional murderers cannot be ransomed (Numbers 35:31) — human life is not commutable to property. This is a legal-theological claim about the uniqueness of the *imago Dei*.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Generically, Joshua 20 is a legal-administrative report embedded within a narrative of land distribution. More precisely, it belongs to the sub-genre of executive fulfillment: Yahweh commanded, Moses transmitted, and now Joshua implements. The chapter is framed by divine speech (20:1–6), then a terse execution notice (20:7–8), then a summary theological rationale (20:9). This triadic structure — command, fulfillment, rationale — mirrors the priestly-Deuteronomic pattern familiar from Exodus 25–31 / 35–40 and from the tabernacle-construction narratives.

Literary devices include geographic chiasm: three cities west of Jordan listed north-to-south (Kedesh, Shechem, Hebron) in 20:7, then three east of Jordan listed south-to-north (Bezer, Ramoth, Golan) in 20:8. The effect is to draw a hexagonal mesh across the whole land, including Transjordan — a quiet claim that the two-and-a-half eastern tribes are fully integrated into the legal polity even though they live outside Canaan proper. The inclusion of the *gēr* (stranger, resident alien) in 20:9 — "for the people of Israel and for the stranger sojourning among them" — extends the protection across ethnic lines, a feature Jacob Milgrom emphasized in his *Leviticus* commentary as distinctive to the priestly-Holiness ethic.

The chapter sits at the structural hinge of Joshua. Chapters 13–19 distributed the land; chapter 20 specifies the refuge system; chapter 21 specifies the Levitical cities (of which the six refuge cities are a subset). The three together constitute the completed civic-religious infrastructure of a people now in possession of their land. The summary notice at 21:43–45 then declares that every promise has been fulfilled — a declaration that has to bear the weight of the unconquered territories admitted in Joshua 13:1 and Judges 1.

The most significant textual variant in the chapter involves the relationship between the Masoretic Text (MT) and the Septuagint (LXX). The LXX version of Joshua 20:4–6 is markedly shorter than MT, containing only the core legal stipulations without the expansive procedural description. Most text critics — including Emanuel Tov and A. Graeme Auld — argue that LXX here preserves an older, shorter form, and that MT has secondarily harmonized the passage toward the fuller Deuteronomic version in Deuteronomy 19. This is one of the clearer cases in Joshua where LXX reflects a *Vorlage* earlier than the proto-Masoretic text, and it matters for interpretation: the earliest recoverable form of Joshua 20 is a compact administrative notice; the fuller MT is a theological-literary expansion that harmonizes Joshua with Deuteronomy. Auld's *Joshua, Moses and the Land* (1980) developed this point as evidence that the Deuteronomistic connections in Joshua were strengthened during transmission.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The asylum system arrives in Joshua 20 already layered with history — Exodus 21:13 ("if he did not lie in wait, but God let him fall into his hand, I will appoint for you a place to which he may flee"); Numbers 35:9–34 (the priestly elaboration, where the high priest's death is specified and the prohibition on ransom articulated); Deuteronomy 4:41–43 (Moses sets apart the three Transjordan cities) and 19:1–13 (the command to set apart three in Canaan, with the provision for three more if the land expands). The trajectory runs through Kings (1 Kings 1–2, where Adonijah and Joab grasp the horns of the altar seeking asylum — and are denied, suggesting the civic refuge system had displaced altar asylum by the time of the Solomonic court narrative) and Chronicles (1 Chronicles 6:54–81 repeats the Levitical-city list).

**Jewish trajectory.** Second Temple sources show active engagement. Philo's *De Fuga et Inventione* allegorizes the cities of refuge as intellectual-spiritual destinations for the soul fleeing passion — Philo distributes the six cities between the Logos as the primary refuge and various subordinate divine powers. Josephus (*Antiquities* 4.7.4) reports the institution straightforwardly as Mosaic law, interested in its function as a rational criminal-justice distinction. Qumran's *Temple Scroll* (11QT 61:12–63:8) reformulates the asylum provisions with expansions, suggesting continued sectarian reflection on the system. Rabbinic material is unusually rich: the entire Mishnah tractate *Makkot*, and especially Makkot 2 (2:1–8), discusses every detail — who qualifies, what counts as *šĕgāgâ*, what happens on the roads, and why the high priest's death triggers release. Talmud Bavli Makkot 7a–13b elaborates with extensive case law: should the blind be granted refuge? what if the manslayer kills again unintentionally within the city? does asylum protect from civil liability as well as vengeance? Mishnah Makkot 2:6 offers the famous explanation for the high priest's death functioning as release: the high priest should have prayed for his generation such that no accidental deaths would occur, and so the accident is somehow on his account. Medieval interpretation runs through Rashi (who follows the Makkot tradition closely) and Ramban (who emphasizes the contemplative-penitential dimension of exile in the city of refuge). Maimonides's *Mishneh Torah*, *Hilkhot Rotzeach u-Shmirat Nefesh* 5–8, codifies the system in remarkable detail: the cities must be of medium size, not near borders, supplied with water and markets, their roads straight and their bridges maintained; the manslayer's teacher must go into exile with him so the manslayer may continue to study Torah (a striking humanitarian provision). The Rambam-Rashba correspondence preserves halakhic debate over contested cases. Modern Jewish thinkers — Buber on the community of the word, Jon Levenson on the distinctiveness of biblical legal anthropology, Moshe Greenberg's 1959 "Some Postulates of Biblical Criminal Law" (the foundational modern essay arguing that biblical law treats human life as non-commutable to property because humans bear the divine image), Moshe Weinfeld on the Deuteronomic redaction, Haim Cohn on the jurisprudence of intention, Bernard Jackson on comparative Semitic law — constitute a dense scholarly conversation that continues to treat Joshua 20 and its antecedents as a high point of ancient legal thought.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic interpretation is dominated by Origen's *Homilies on Joshua*, homilies 20–21 of which treat the cities of refuge extensively and allegorically. For Origen, the six cities correspond to six modes of Christ's sheltering work — Christ as wisdom, as strength, as Word, and so on — and the manslayer is the soul that has killed through ignorance (ignorantly partaking in sin) and flees to the refuge of Christ. Jerome follows Origen with less extravagance, and Augustine, while not producing a dedicated treatment of Joshua 20, weaves the refuge-city imagery into his reflections on sanctuary and on the church's role in protecting fugitives (*Letters* 151, where Augustine himself intercedes for men fleeing capital punishment). Aquinas treats the underlying ethical distinction in the *Summa Theologiae* II-II q.64 (on killing) and q.67–69 (on judgment and unintentional killing), refining the philosophical categories of *voluntarium* and *involuntarium* that shape Catholic moral theology. The medieval canon-law tradition of ecclesiastical sanctuary — developed across the *Decretum Gratiani* and codified at the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) — drew deliberately on Joshua 20 as scriptural warrant, though it transposed the refuge function back into the sacred building rather than the civic city. Calvin, in his *Commentary on the Four Last Books of Moses* and his treatment of civil law in *Institutes* IV.20, endorses the underlying logic — mercy for the involuntary, strict justice for the intentional — while sharply criticizing medieval sanctuary practice as a protection for criminals. Critical modern scholarship — Noth, Albright, Yehezkel Kaufmann, Benjamin Mazar, A. Graeme Auld, Richard Nelson's 1997 *Joshua* OTL commentary — debates the historicity and redactional layering of the asylum list. Contemporary voices include Walter Brueggemann on the subversive theopolitics of refuge; Christopher Wright's *Old Testament Ethics for the People of God* on the chapter's ethical contribution; Jeffrey Stackert's *Rewriting the Torah* (2007) on how Deuteronomy's asylum laws transform their priestly and Covenant-Code sources; Richard Bauckham's *The Bible and Politics* tracing the Bible-to-modern-sanctuary-movement lineage; and Jacqueline Lapsley's work on the ethical imagination of the Deuteronomic corpus. Pamela Barmash's *Homicide in the Biblical World* (2005) and Jonathan Burnside's *God, Justice, and Society* (2011) are essential recent monographs that situate the asylum system within a comparative ancient Near Eastern and contemporary legal-theoretical frame.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Justice against mercy — what does it mean that the manslayer must still leave home?**

The first position holds that the asylum system is primarily about mercy: it rescues the accidental killer from a vengeance system that cannot distinguish intention from outcome. On this reading, the cities of refuge are a great moral advance over the undifferentiated blood-feud and anticipate modern criminal-law distinctions between murder, manslaughter, and negligent homicide. Greenberg and most Jewish interpreters emphasize this.

The second position holds that asylum is primarily about justice — specifically, the cosmic demand that shed blood pollutes the land (Numbers 35:33) and must be answered. On this reading, the manslayer is not innocent; he is less guilty, but guilty enough that the land requires a real response. Exile from his ancestral plot is no small thing in an agrarian covenantal society; his fields, his kin, his place at the gate are all forfeited until the high priest dies. The *gōʾēl haddām* is not a primitive survivor of barbarism but a structural guarantor of the land's integrity. Milgrom develops this reading.

The steelman — and the least intuitive position — is that the asylum city is both a refuge and a prison, and this dual character is itself the theological payload. The city is what Milgrom called a "gilded cage": the manslayer is protected inside it and dead outside it. He cannot leave to visit his father's grave; he cannot go back to his field to bring in his harvest; the sons of the deceased know exactly where he is. The high priest's death as release marks the system's refusal to resolve the ambiguity through a fixed sentence. The duration is contingent on the life of a specific other person — someone the manslayer may never meet. This converts the manslayer's exile into a strange shared bond with the high priest, whose atoning life in the sanctuary is bearing weight for accidents that ought not have happened. The steelman claims that asylum is neither mercy nor justice but a distinctive third thing: a structured waiting on priestly mediation. Where this leaves us: justice and mercy do not here become a compromise but a choreography, and the choreography is priestly.

**Tension 2: The high priest's death as release — honor, accident, or atonement?**

Position one (the rationalist-honor reading): the high priest's death marks a generational reset. The death of the nation's chief representative is a civic watershed that fittingly discharges debts outstanding in that generation. Josephus and some medieval commentators lean this way.

Position two (the prayerful-negligence reading): Mishnah Makkot 2:6 — the high priest is culpable in some attenuated sense for the accidental deaths that occurred on his watch, because he should have prayed such deaths away. His death is his delayed accountability, and with his death the manslayer's count is cleared.

Position three (the atonement reading, most fully developed in Christian interpretation but anticipated in rabbinic material): the high priest's death atones. His life was the vehicle of atonement for the sanctuary; his death takes into itself the blood-debt that the manslayer could not expiate by any offering. This is the reading the Epistle to the Hebrews assumes when it says, "We who have fled for refuge might have strong encouragement to hold fast to the hope set before us" (Hebrews 6:18), and when it develops the Melchizedekian high priest in chapters 7–10.

The steelman here is position two — the rabbinic negligence reading — because it most resists the two modern temptations. It refuses the secular reduction that nothing happens at the high priest's death except the passing of time, and it refuses the Christianizing move that the high priest's death becomes straightforwardly an atoning sacrifice. It insists that priestly mediation is active rather than ritual, that it can fail, and that its failure has consequences visible in the ledger of accidents. Where this leaves us: asylum joins the manslayer's freedom to the high priest's intercessory life in a way that makes prayer and accident part of the same moral fabric.

One unresolved question: If accidents are reduced by prayer, and if the high priest's life is the chief locus of Israel's prayer, what accounts for the accidents that happen during the high priest's good years?

## The Thread

Numbers 35:9–34 (the priestly legal foundation, with its claim that blood pollutes the land and cannot be ransomed). Deuteronomy 19:1–13 (the Deuteronomic parallel, with the practical injunction to make the roads passable). 1 Kings 2:28–34 (Joab grasping the altar and being executed anyway — the collision of altar asylum with the new civic system). Hebrews 6:17–20 (the Christian reappropriation: "we who have fled for refuge," the manslayer's flight transposed into the language of the gospel's hope).
