# Isaiah 28 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Isaiah 28 opens the great *woe-oracle* section of First Isaiah (chapters 28-33), six successive oracles each introduced by *hoy* (הוֹי, "ah!" or "woe!") — at 28:1, 29:1, 29:15, 30:1, 31:1, and 33:1. After the cosmic eschatological vision of the Isaiah Apocalypse (chs 24-27), the prophet returns sharply to the eighth-century historical world: drunken Ephraim about to fall, Jerusalem's elite trafficking with Egypt, a Davidic king (Hezekiah) wavering between trust and pragmatism, and Assyria — *Asshur*, אַשּׁוּר — pressing in from the northeast as the dominant geopolitical fact.

The historical setting of chapter 28 is generally placed in the years immediately before or just after the fall of Samaria to Assyria in 722 BCE. The opening *woe to the proud crown of the drunkards of Ephraim* (28:1) presupposes a Northern Kingdom still standing but visibly tottering — perhaps the years 727-722 under the brief, chaotic reigns of Pekah, Hoshea, and the Assyrian conquest. Ephraim (אֶפְרַיִם), the dominant tribe of the Northern Kingdom, had been functioning as a synecdoche for the entire kingdom of Israel since the divided-monarchy period. The *rich valley* (גֵּיא שְׁמָנִים, *gei shemanim*) is most plausibly read as Samaria — the city was geographically perched on a hill above a fertile valley, and the *crown* image works precisely because of this topography. Joseph Blenkinsopp (Anchor Yale, 2000), Hans Wildberger (BKAT), and J. J. M. Roberts (Hermeneia, 2015) all read 28:1-13 as substantially Isaianic material from the late 720s, addressed to or about Samaria; chapters 28:14 onward shift addressee to Jerusalem's *scoffers* who *rule this people* — the political elite around Hezekiah making (in chapters 30-31) the disastrous turn toward Egyptian alliance.

Composite-authorship analysis: while the *Isaiah Apocalypse* (chs 24-27) is widely held to be late, chapters 28-33 are generally treated as core eighth-century Isaianic material — perhaps with later editorial framing but with substantive prophetic content traceable to Isaiah ben Amoz himself. John Goldingay (ICC, 2014) and Patricia Tull (Smyth & Helwys, 2010) accept a substantial Isaianic core in the woe-oracles; Christopher Seitz and Brevard Childs in the canonical-shape tradition emphasize that the woes function as the prophetic word of the eighth-century crisis, however edited. The conservative-evangelical line (Motyer, Oswalt) reads the unit as straightforwardly Isaianic. Even Wildberger and Blenkinsopp, who detect editorial layers, locate the bulk of chapter 28 in the eighth century.

The *Great Isaiah Scroll* (1QIsa^a) preserves chapter 28 with high fidelity to the Masoretic text — the famous *tzav la-tzav, qav la-qav* sequence (vv. 10, 13) is intact in the scroll, and the cornerstone verse (28:16) is also intact, with one notable variant we will discuss below.

Hebrew terms central to the chapter:

- *atarot ge'ut shikkorei Efrayim* (עֲטֶרֶת גֵּאוּת שִׁכּוֹרֵי אֶפְרַיִם, "the crown of pride of the drunkards of Ephraim," 28:1) — a triple-genitive construction of accumulating contempt.
- *avan bochan pinnat yikrat musad mussad* (אֶבֶן בֹּחַן פִּנַּת יִקְרַת מוּסָד מוּסָּד, "a tested stone, a precious cornerstone, of a sure foundation," 28:16) — densely poetic, the four stone-modifiers piled in apposition.
- *tzav la-tzav tzav la-tzav, qav la-qav qav la-qav, ze'ir sham ze'ir sham* (צַו לָצָו צַו לָצָו קַו לָקָו קַו לָקָו זְעֵיר שָׁם זְעֵיר שָׁם, 28:10, 13) — the famously obscure refrain.
- *berit et mavet, choze et she'ol* (בְּרִית אֶת מָוֶת חֹזֶה אֶת שְׁאוֹל, "covenant with death, agreement with Sheol," 28:15, 18).
- *ma'aseh zar / avodah nochriyyah* (מַעֲשֶׂה זָר / עֲבוֹדָה נָכְרִיָּה, "strange deed / alien work," 28:21) — YHWH's own work characterized as foreign to himself.

## Lens 2: The Literature Itself

Chapter 28 has a tightly disciplined three-part structure: (1) *woe to Ephraim* and the inverse-image of YHWH-as-true-crown (vv. 1-13), (2) *woe to the Jerusalem scoffers* who have made a covenant with death — and the cornerstone-foundation declaration (vv. 14-22), and (3) the closing *agricultural parable* about discriminating divine pedagogy (vv. 23-29). The chapter moves from north (Ephraim) to south (Jerusalem) to wisdom-instruction, and the agricultural parable retrospectively interprets the whole.

**The opening woe (vv. 1-6)** is one of the most aurally textured pieces of poetry in First Isaiah. The *crown of pride* worn by Ephraim — almost certainly the literal city-crown of Samaria — is contrasted in v. 5 with the *crown of glory and diadem of beauty* that YHWH himself becomes for the *remnant* (שְׁאָר עַמּוֹ, *she'ar ammo*). The image is precise: when human pride collapses, divine glory becomes the substitute crown. The repetition of *atarah / atarot* (crown) at vv. 1, 3, 5 functions as a structural marker.

**The drunken priests and prophets (vv. 7-13)** is one of the most savage indictments of religious leadership in the Bible. *They reel in vision, they stumble in giving judgment* (v. 7) — the priests cannot perform their juridical duty (priests adjudicated certain types of cases under Torah), and the prophets cannot deliver coherent oracles. *All tables are full of filthy vomit* (v. 8) — the table-imagery here may be the cult-tables of the temple, defiled with literal drunken vomit. The verse is shocking by design.

Then comes the chapter's most contested couplet, vv. 9-10. Two readings, both ancient.

**Reading A**: The *drunkards mock the prophet*. They sneer: *to whom will he teach knowledge — to babies just weaned from milk?* They are mocking Isaiah's preaching as childish, sing-song, baby-talk. The Hebrew *tzav la-tzav, qav la-qav* is then either gibberish (mock baby-talk) or refers to the Hebrew *aleph-bet*: *tzav* contains the letter *tzade*, *qav* contains the letter *qof* — the drunkards saying "he just teaches us A-B-C, A-B-C, like a kindergarten teacher." On this reading, the prophet's response in v. 11 is devastating: *fine — if you mock the simple Hebrew word of YHWH, you will hear instead the foreign tongue of the Assyrian invader.* The Babylonian-Assyrian conquest will be your unintelligible "foreign-language" lesson.

**Reading B**: The prophet is mocking the drunkards' speech. *Tzav la-tzav qav la-qav* is the slurred, repetitive babble of intoxication. The prophet reproduces what the drunken priests sound like. Verse 11's "by people of strange lips" then continues the mocking: their slurred drunken Hebrew has *already* become a "strange language" of incoherence.

Most modern scholars accept some version of Reading A — the drunkards mocking the prophet's pedagogy, the prophet turning the mockery back. The wordplay is sharper if *tzav* and *qav* are heard both as Hebrew words ("command," "line/measure") *and* as letters of the alphabet *and* as sounds approaching slurred speech. The Hebrew is intentionally polyvalent.

**The Jerusalem oracle (vv. 14-22)** is the chapter's theological heart. The *scoffers who rule this people in Jerusalem* — the political elite — have *made a covenant with death* (*berit et mavet*) and *with Sheol* (the Hebrew underworld). What does this mean? Three readings, none mutually exclusive:

(1) Literal cult-of-the-dead practices — necromancy, treaties with chthonic deities. The Ugaritic *Mot* (Death, mwt) was a god, and West Semitic culture had cult-of-the-dead traditions. Isaiah may be denouncing actual cultic practice.

(2) A *foreign-power treaty* described as a covenant with death because of its catastrophic consequences. The most plausible candidate is the emerging Egypt-alliance against Assyria, which chapters 30-31 will explicitly denounce. The "scoffers" think they have insurance against the *overwhelming whip* (the Assyrian invasion); Isaiah says their insurance is a death-pact.

(3) A *theological* characterization: by trusting in *lies* (*kazav*) and *falsehood* (*sheker*) as their refuge, they have effectively chosen the side of death over life. The Deuteronomic *life-or-death* choice (Deut 30:19) lies in the background.

Against the death-covenant Isaiah declares 28:16 — the cornerstone verse:

> *Behold, I am the one who has laid as a foundation in Zion,*
> *a stone, a tested stone, a precious cornerstone, of a sure foundation:*
> *"Whoever believes will not be in haste."*

The Hebrew piles up four genitival modifiers: *avan* (stone), *bochan* (tested), *pinnat yikrat* (precious cornerstone), *musad mussad* (founded foundation — paronomasia, intensifying). The closing line in Hebrew is *ha-ma'amin lo yachish* (הַמַּאֲמִין לֹא יָחִישׁ) — "the one who believes / trusts will not be in haste / will not panic / will not flee in alarm." The verb *chush* in this Hiphil form means hurried, panicked flight. The contrast is precise: the death-covenanters are in *haste* — desperate political maneuvering against the Assyrian threat; the one who trusts the foundation-stone is *not* in haste — calm, grounded, secure.

The *Great Isaiah Scroll* (1QIsa^a) reads slightly differently here — *lo yachush* with a *yod-ḥet-vav-shin* spelling that may suggest "will not be put to shame." The Septuagint follows this sense: *ou mē kataischynthē* — "shall not be put to shame." The variant matters because the New Testament (Romans 9:33; 1 Peter 2:6) quotes the *Septuagint* form: *will not be put to shame*. This is one of the most-discussed textual variants in OT-NT relations.

The *strange deed* / *alien work* of v. 21 — the Mount Perazim and Valley of Gibeon allusion — references David's victory against the Philistines (2 Sam 5:20; 1 Chr 14:11) and Joshua's victory at Gibeon (Josh 10). Both were occasions when YHWH fought *for* Israel. The shock of v. 21 is that this time YHWH will fight in the same manner — but *against* his own people. *Strange* and *alien* (*zar / nochriyyah*) are technical Levitical terms (cf. Lev 10:1, *strange fire*) — what doesn't belong, what is foreign to the proper sphere.

**The agricultural parable (vv. 23-29)** is one of the most lyrically beautiful sections in First Isaiah. The farmer does not plow forever; he plows, then sows; he distinguishes the seeds — dill, cumin, wheat, barley, emmer; he threshes appropriately to each — dill with a stick, cumin with a rod, wheat under cart wheels but not crushed forever. *This also comes from the LORD of hosts; he is wonderful in counsel and excellent in wisdom.* The parable is wisdom-instruction (*musar*) about divine pedagogy: YHWH does not punish forever, does not crush forever, applies the right discipline to the right plant at the right time. The farmer's craft is the metaphor for YHWH's own measured engagement with his people.

## Lens 3: Where This Sits in the Larger Story

Within the prophetic canon, chapter 28's cornerstone-image enters one of the most consequential intertextual streams in Scripture. Psalm 118:22 — *the stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone (rosh pinnah)* — provides the parallel image of the rejected-stone-now-foundational. Both Isaiah 28:16 and Psalm 118:22 use *pinnah* (corner) as the structural keyword. The two texts are almost always paired in subsequent reception.

**Jewish trajectory**. The rabbinic tradition reads Isaiah 28:16 in several distinct streams. The *Mishnah Yoma* (5:2) on the Day of Atonement ritual notes that the *Foundation Stone* (*Even ha-Shetiyah*) — the stone in the Holy of Holies upon which the Ark had stood — was the literal *foundation of the world*. Some rabbinic readings connect this directly to Isaiah 28:16: the Zion-foundation-stone is the cosmological pivot. The Targum Jonathan reads 28:16 messianically: "Behold, I appoint a king in Zion, a strong king, mighty and terrible." The messianic-political-king reading is one ancient Jewish line. The *ha-ma'amin lo yachish* — "the one who trusts will not be in haste" — has been a foundational text for Jewish *bitachon* (trust) tradition: the trust that doesn't panic, the faith that doesn't flee. Maimonides cites it in discussions of theological steadiness. The *covenant with death* image (28:15) gets picked up in rabbinic literature about the futility of treaties with foreign powers — the Talmudic discussions of when Jewish communities should or shouldn't negotiate with imperial overlords often gesture toward 28:15.

**Christian trajectory** runs through the New Testament and into the patristic and Reformation traditions. The cornerstone-Christology of the New Testament is one of the most thoroughly developed Old Testament-into-Christ readings:

- **Romans 9:33** — Paul quotes a *combination* of Isaiah 28:16 and Isaiah 8:14: *"Behold, I am laying in Zion a stone of stumbling, and a rock of offense; and whoever believes in him will not be put to shame."* Paul reads the cornerstone Christologically — Christ is the stone in Zion; faith-in-him is the *believing* of 28:16; the contrast is between those who trust and those who *stumble* (a different Isaiah text).

- **1 Peter 2:6-8** — Peter quotes Isaiah 28:16, Psalm 118:22, and Isaiah 8:14 together as a *catena* (chain) of stone-texts: *"Behold, I am laying in Zion a stone, a cornerstone chosen and precious, and whoever believes in him will not be put to shame."* The *will not be put to shame* form (following the LXX, not the MT *will not be in haste*) is the dominant New Testament version.

- **Ephesians 2:20** — *"built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the cornerstone (akrogōniaios)."*

- **Mark 12:10 / Matthew 21:42 / Luke 20:17** — Jesus himself quotes Psalm 118:22 (the rejected stone become cornerstone) at the climax of the parable of the wicked tenants — and the early church read the two stone-texts together.

The *cornerstone-Christology* became central to Christian self-understanding. Augustine treats Isaiah 28:16 extensively in *City of God* and the *Tractates on John*. The Reformation-era Protestant tradition emphasizes the *believing* (the *ma'amin* / *pisteuōn*) — sola fide — as the verse's center. Calvin's commentary on Isaiah is one of the most extensive early-modern treatments.

The MT-LXX variant matters here. The MT *will not be in haste* emphasizes *steadiness* in trust; the LXX / NT *will not be put to shame* emphasizes *vindication* of trust. Both readings are theologically rich; both have ancient pedigree; both have shaped distinct streams of devotional and theological reflection.

NT use of chapter 28 is striking in its breadth: 28:11-12 ("by people of strange lips") is quoted by Paul in 1 Corinthians 14:21 in his discussion of speaking in tongues; 28:16 is the cornerstone-text quoted in Romans 9:33 and 1 Peter 2:6; 28:22 is alluded to in Luke 21:35.

## Lens 4: What This Asks of Us — Tensions and Honest Reading

Three live tensions surface in chapter 28.

**First — the cornerstone-Christology question.** The New Testament's reading of 28:16 as referring to Christ has been one of the most foundational moves in Christian biblical interpretation. The Jewish tradition has read the verse messianically (Targum Jonathan), but typically with reference to *a* king or *the* Davidic monarchy in general, not specifically to Jesus of Nazareth. How honest readers across both traditions handle this divergence matters.

The Christian reading: the New Testament's *catena* of stone-texts (Isa 28:16 + Isa 8:14 + Ps 118:22) constitutes a coherent Christological-typological reading rooted in the actual Hebrew Bible's own messianic-foundation-stone language. Christ is the *eschatological* stone toward which the OT texts pointed.

The Jewish reading: the *ha-ma'amin lo yachish* refers to the trusting one in YHWH's covenant-promises generally; the messianic-king reading is one *application* but not the verse's primary reference. To collapse the verse into a single individual figure is to flatten the canonical horizon.

The steelman synthesis: both traditions agree that 28:16 is *messianically* available — that is, the verse points beyond its immediate eighth-century horizon toward a fuller fulfillment. The two traditions disagree about *which* messianic fulfillment is in view. Where this leaves us: the verse is, for Christians, foundational to cornerstone-Christology and cannot be read in good faith without the New Testament reception. For Jewish readers, the verse remains within an active messianic-trust tradition that has not been closed by Christian appropriation. Honest reading does not require choosing; it requires articulating the difference clearly.

**Second — the *tzav la-tzav* difficulty.** The interpretive history of vv. 9-13 is a microcosm of biblical-interpretive humility. The Hebrew is genuinely obscure; the polyvalence is intentional; the reader is invited to *hear* multiple registers simultaneously. This means any flat reading ("the verse means X") misses something essential. The verse is doing what it describes — the people who scorn the prophet's "simple" word will find his word becoming all the more impenetrable to them. The verse's obscurity is its theology. Modern readers should be honest about not fully understanding it — and should resist commentaries that pretend to total clarity.

**Third — the woe-oracle genre and its modern reception.** The *hoy*-oracles of chapters 28-33 (and similar units in Amos 5-6, Habakkuk 2, Micah 2) are funereal in form — *hoy* is the cry uttered at funerals (1 Kings 13:30; Jer 22:18). The prophet *mourns* the sinner as already dead. This is a confronting genre for modern readers shaped by therapeutic discourse: prophetic woe is not pastoral counseling, not "speaking the truth in love" in the usual sense. It is *lament* over those who have chosen a path that the prophet sees ending in destruction. The honest reading acknowledges the genre's harshness without apologizing for it; the Jewish and Christian traditions have both kept the woe-oracles in active liturgical use precisely because their *seriousness* is part of the prophetic message.

The agricultural parable of vv. 23-29 redeems the chapter's harshness. *He does not thresh forever* — YHWH's discipline is calibrated, not annihilating. The same God who pronounces *woe* over the drunkards of Ephraim is the master farmer who knows that dill needs a stick, not a cart wheel; who knows when the plowing should stop and the sowing should begin; who is *wonderful in counsel and excellent in wisdom*. The chapter's structure — woe, woe, parable — leaves us with the master farmer, not the destroyer.

Where this leaves us: chapter 28 is a chapter about *foundations* — what we build on, what we trust, what holds when the storm passes through morning by morning. The drunkards of Ephraim built on the rich valley; the scoffers of Jerusalem built on a covenant with death; the master farmer builds on an *avan bochan*, a tested stone. The closing question: *what is the cornerstone of your life that doesn't move when the overwhelming whip passes through?* The chapter does not soften the question; it just locates the answer in Zion.

## The Thread

Isaiah 28 is the chapter where the prophet stops singing apocalypse and starts naming names. The drunkards in the rich valley, the scoffers in Jerusalem, the political class trafficking with death — they are about to discover that the foreign tongue of the invader is what their slurred Hebrew was already becoming. Beneath them, in Zion, the tested stone has already been laid. The one who trusts will not be in haste. The master farmer is still threshing — but he does not crush forever.
