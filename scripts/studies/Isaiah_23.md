# Isaiah 23 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: Historical World

Isaiah 23 closes the OAN cluster (chs 13-23) with an oracle against Tyre and Sidon — the great Phoenician seafaring city-states on the Lebanese coast. Tyre, perched on an offshore island and protected by sea on three sides, was in the 8th-7th centuries BC the dominant maritime trading power of the eastern Mediterranean: founder of colonies from Carthage to Cádiz, source of the famous Tyrian purple dye, supplier of cedar to Solomon's temple (1 Kgs 5; 2 Chr 2), and the financial broker linking the Levant, Egypt, Cyprus, and the western Mediterranean. The chapter's sweep — Tarshish (probably Tartessos in southern Spain), Cyprus (Hebrew כִּתִּים, *Kittîm*), Sidon, Egypt, the Chaldeans — is the geographic reach of the Phoenician trade network itself.

The historical horizon is contested. The reference to Assyria's destruction of "the land of the Chaldeans" (v. 13) — most likely Sennacherib's 689 BC sack of Babylon — places the oracle plausibly in the late 8th century, consistent with Isaiah ben Amoz's lifetime. Tyre itself was besieged by Shalmaneser V (c. 725-722 BC), then by Sennacherib (701 BC), then by Esarhaddon (c. 671 BC), then notoriously for thirteen years by Nebuchadnezzar II (586-573 BC), and finally taken decisively by Alexander the Great in 332 BC after a seven-month siege in which he built a causeway from the mainland out to the island, forever altering the geography. Each of these has been proposed as the oracle's referent. The "seventy years" of v. 15 has been variously fitted: to the gap between Esarhaddon's 671 BC subjugation and the city's renewed prosperity under Ashurbanipal's successors, or more loosely to a generic generation of obscurity. Conservative readings (Oswalt, Motyer) keep an 8th-century horizon and read v. 13 as Isaianic foresight. Mainstream-critical readings (Wildberger, Blenkinsopp, Williamson) often see vv. 13-18 as later editorial additions, with the core oracle (vv. 1-14) Isaianic and the prose appendix (vv. 15-18) post-exilic.

The composite-authorship debate inherited from Bernhard Duhm's *Das Buch Jesaia* (1892) — First Isaiah (8th c. BC), Second Isaiah (chs 40-55, c. 540 BC), Third Isaiah (chs 56-66) — applies here to layer-level analysis within a single chapter. The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran (1QIsaᵃ, c. 125 BC) preserves Isaiah 23 in continuous form with the rest of the book, fixing whatever editorial development had occurred by the late Second Temple period. Brevard Childs's canonical approach brackets the dating question and reads the oracle as the canon presents it: a Phoenician-judgment oracle that closes the OAN cluster with a surprising note of restoration in vv. 17-18.

The chapter names two Phoenician cities — Tyre (Hebrew צֹר, *Ṣōr*, "rock," for the offshore island fortress) and Sidon (צִידוֹן, *Ṣîdōn*) — and one diaspora destination: Tarshish (תַּרְשִׁישׁ, *Taršîš*), the proverbial far-western trading hub, identified by Eusebius and Jerome with Tartessos in Iberia. The "ships of Tarshish" (v. 1) became a stock biblical phrase for ocean-going merchant vessels (1 Kgs 10:22; Ezek 27:25; Jonah 1:3).

## Lens 2: Literary World

Chapter 23 is mostly poetry (vv. 1-14) with a prose coda (vv. 15-18) — a structure typical of late editorial framing, where a poetic oracle is given a "what-comes-next" pendant. The opening **הֵילִילוּ אֳנִיּוֹת תַּרְשִׁישׁ** (*hêlîlû ʾŏniyyôt Taršîš*, "Wail, O ships of Tarshish," v. 1) is repeated almost verbatim in v. 14, forming a tight inclusio that brackets the poetic oracle. The Hebrew verb *yālal* (הֵילִילוּ, "wail") gives the chapter its sound — a long, drawn-out lament-cry like the English "howl."

The Hebrew literary features cluster around Tyre's identity as merchant-bride. The city is personified across three female figures: as **virgin daughter of Sidon** (v. 12, בְּתוּלַת בַּת־צִידוֹן, *bĕtûlat bat-Ṣîdôn*); as **mother who has not borne** (v. 4, the haunting line where the sea itself speaks: "I have neither labored nor given birth"); and as **forgotten prostitute** (v. 15-16, זוֹנָה נִשְׁכָּחָה, *zōnāh niškāḥāh*) singing the song-of-remembering. The transition from virgin-mother-prostitute is unsparing and theologically loaded: Tyre's commerce is figured as fornication with the nations, a charge that becomes standard prophetic shorthand for cosmopolitan capital cities (cf. Nineveh in Nahum 3, "Babylon" in Revelation 17-18, Jerusalem itself in Hos 1-3 and Ezek 16, 23).

The phrase **"the bestower of crowns, whose merchants were princes"** (v. 8, הַמַּעֲטִירָה, *hammaʿăṭîrāh*, "the crown-bestower") captures Tyre's strange political weight: a city that crowned colonial rulers, whose merchants outranked kings. The oracle's theological hinge is v. 9: "The LORD of hosts has purposed it, to defile the pompous pride of all glory, to dishonor all the honored of the earth" — לְחַלֵּל גְּאוֹן כָּל־צְבִי (*lĕḥallēl gĕʾôn kol-ṣĕvî*), "to profane the pride of all beauty/glory."

The "song of the prostitute" (vv. 16) is one of the strangest small-form genres embedded in Hebrew prophecy: a quoted street song, presumably a real Iron Age ditty about a sex worker walking the city with her lyre. The prophet borrows the popular song to make a metaphorical point about Tyre's reentry into the global trade economy after seventy years of obscurity.

The closing prose pivot (vv. 17-18) is theologically remarkable. Tyre, condemned in vv. 1-14 and exiled-in-time for seventy years, returns to her commerce — and her merchandise becomes **קֹדֶשׁ לַיהוָה** (*qōdeš laYHWH*, "holy to the LORD"). The trading wealth is repurposed: not stored, not hoarded, but supplying food and clothing "for those who dwell before the LORD." This is one of the earliest oracles in the Hebrew Bible to imagine Gentile commerce serving Yahweh's people — a motif Isa 60-66 and Zech 14 will later develop as the eschatological-pilgrimage theme.

## Lens 3: Canonical and Theological World

Tyre and Sidon traditions cluster across the canon with unusual density. Among the prophetic OAN sequences:

- **Ezekiel 26-28** — the longest prophetic treatment of Tyre, three chapters of dirge that include the famous "you were in Eden, the garden of God" passage (Ezek 28:13) addressed to the king of Tyre, a text Christian theology has long read alongside Isa 14 as a portrait of cosmic pride.
- **Joel 3:4-8** — Tyre and Sidon condemned for selling Judean captives to the Greeks.
- **Amos 1:9-10** — Tyre judged for forgetting "the covenant of brotherhood" (likely the Hiram-Solomon alliance).
- **Zechariah 9:2-4** — a brief Tyre oracle in the late prophets.

The combined weight of these traditions builds a portrait of Tyre as the canonical type of mercantile pride and global trade unaccountable to the God who made the sea. Isaiah 23 is the earliest and most economically focused of the cluster — it sees Tyre primarily as a trader, not as a king-figure (that's Ezekiel's emphasis) or as a slaver (Joel's emphasis).

The "seventy years" number is striking because it recurs across the prophetic canon: Jeremiah's seventy years for Babylonian exile (Jer 25:11-12; 29:10), Daniel's reflection on the same (Dan 9:2), Zechariah's seventy years (Zech 1:12; 7:5), and 2 Chr 36:21's retrospective. The number functions less as a precise calendrical prediction than as a generation-of-judgment marker — a "lifetime of one king," as Isa 23:15 itself glosses it.

The Jewish trajectory has read Isaiah 23 alongside Ezek 27-28 in meditations on the futility of accumulated wealth. The Talmud (b. Pesahim 119a; b. Megillah 6a) treats Tyre as a paradigm of the trading nations, and the Tyre-of-Yahweh-converted in vv. 17-18 became, for some midrashic readings, a glimpse of Gentile inclusion in the eschatological economy.

The Christian trajectory is shaped especially by the Gospel uses of Tyre and Sidon. Jesus deliberately enters their territory in **Mark 7:24-31**, the encounter with the Syro-Phoenician woman whose daughter is healed — a striking moment because Jesus is operating in classical OAN territory and granting the request of a Gentile woman from the cursed city. **Matthew 11:21-22** and **Luke 10:13-14** use Tyre and Sidon as a comparative shaming-point against Galilean cities: "Woe to you, Chorazin! Woe to you, Bethsaida! For if the mighty works done in you had been done in Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented long ago in sackcloth and ashes." This is a sharp inversion — the OAN-typed cities are now portrayed as more responsive than the elect cities of Galilee.

**Acts 27** records Paul's voyage past the Phoenician coast en route to Rome, and **Acts 21:3-7** records a stop in Tyre with a community of disciples already gathered there — the early church had taken root in the city Isaiah condemned. The pattern is consistent: where the OT prophets pronounce judgment, the NT Gospel pronounces unexpected ingathering. Isaiah 23:17-18 — Tyre's wealth becoming "holy to the LORD" — is one of the OT seeds for that Gospel pattern.

Augustine, in *City of God* 16-18, treats Tyre as one of the great earthly cities whose fall the Hebrew prophets foretold; Calvin's Isaiah commentary lingers on v. 9's "to defile the pride of all glory" as a key text for the theology of providence. In modern Catholic social teaching and Reformed political theology alike, Isaiah 23 has been a touchstone for thinking about global trade, financial power, and the moral status of merchant cities.

## Lens 4: Tensions and Honest Reckoning

**Tension 1: The Tyre-and-Sidon traditions across the canon.** Three positions on how to read the chapter alongside the parallel prophetic material: (a) The prophetic-cluster reading (most modern critical commentaries) treats Isa 23, Ezek 26-28, Joel 3:4-8, and Amos 1:9-10 as independent traditions that grew up around the same shared cultural memory of Phoenician commerce — overlapping but not derivative. (b) The canonical-typology reading (Childs, the Christian patristic tradition) treats Tyre as a developing canonical figure who becomes, by the end of the prophetic canon, a type for "mercantile global power that judges and is judged by Yahweh" — Revelation 18's Babylon being the culmination. (c) The Gospel-inversion reading (Bauckham, Wright) emphasizes the NT swerve where Jesus' actions in Tyre and Sidon (Mark 7; Matt 11) deliberately reverse the OT judgment, opening the formerly-cursed Gentile cities to the Kingdom. Steelman of position (a): the prophetic books really do show signs of independent traditions; the differences in emphasis (Ezekiel on the king, Joel on slavery, Isaiah on trade) suggest separate streams. Steelman of position (b): the canonical shape is real, and reading the prophets in isolation loses the cumulative force of the Tyre-typology. Steelman of position (c): the NT's Gospel logic genuinely inverts the OAN material, and missing this inversion produces flat readings of the Gospels. Where this leaves us: all three are partly right. Isa 23 belongs to a cluster of independent prophetic traditions; the canon shapes those traditions into a typology; and the Gospel reverses the typology in surprising ways. The honest reading holds all three.

**Tension 2: The "seventy years" number and the question of literal versus formulaic prophecy.** Three positions: (a) The literal reading takes the seventy years as a precise calendrical prediction — and is then forced to find a specific 70-year window that fits Tyre's history (no obvious candidate matches cleanly). (b) The formulaic reading treats seventy as a stock prophetic number for "a generation-of-judgment," paralleling Jer 25, Zech 1, Dan 9 — not predictive of a specific decade. (c) The text-internal reading notes that Isa 23:15 itself glosses the number as "the days of one king," softening any insistence on a precise count. Steelman of the literal reading: the prophets are willing to give specific numbers and dates, and treating every numerical prophecy as formulaic empties the tradition of historical accountability. Steelman of the formulaic reading: across the prophetic canon, "seventy years" really does function as a stock unit, and the text itself glosses it loosely. Where this leaves us: seventy years in Isa 23 is most plausibly formulaic — a generation-of-Tyrian-eclipse, not a calendrical prediction.

**Closing question:** When merchant wealth becomes "holy to the LORD" — neither hoarded nor stored, but flowing to feed those who dwell before God — what does that picture of consecrated commerce ask of cities like Tyre, then and now?
