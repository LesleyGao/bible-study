# Ezekiel 28: The King of Tyre, the Cherub in Eden, and the Fall of Satan

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political situation**: Chapter 28 contains **two distinct oracles to Tyre's monarch** plus a brief oracle against **Sidon** (28:20–24) and a closing restoration-promise to Israel (28:25–26). The first Tyre oracle (28:1–10) addresses the **prince of Tyre** (Hebrew *nāgîd ṣōr*, נְגִיד צֹר), and the second (28:11–19) addresses the **king of Tyre** (*melekh ṣōr*, מֶלֶךְ צֹר). The historical referent of both oracles is **Ittobaal III / Ethbaal III** of Tyre, who reigned c. 591–573 BC and was the king during Nebuchadnezzar's thirteen-year siege. He is named in **Josephus *Against Apion* 1.21** (drawing on the Tyrian historian Menander of Ephesus) and in the **Tyrian king-list**. The two-oracle structure has long puzzled commentators: are *prince* and *king* the same person, two different titles for the same monarch, or two different referents (the historical Ittobaal vs. a primordial-mythological figure)? **Greenberg (Anchor)** treats *nāgîd* and *melekh* as parallel titles for the same historical king. **Block (NICOT)** agrees but notes that the second oracle's mythological language opens onto something larger than the historical referent. **Zimmerli (Hermeneia)** treats the first oracle as historical (Ittobaal as the proud commercial monarch) and the second as drawing on a **primordial-mythological tradition** that *typifies* the king. The Sidon oracle (28:20–24) is brief and follows the Tyre material because Sidon is the second Phoenician city, twenty miles up the coast from Tyre; their political fortunes are intertwined.

**Material culture**: The most distinctive feature of chapter 28 is its **mythological-iconographic vocabulary** in 28:11–19. The *signet of perfection* (Hebrew *ḥôtām toḵnît*, חוֹתָם תָּכְנִית, 28:12), *Eden, the garden of God* (*ʿēden gan-elōhîm*, 28:13), *anointed guardian cherub* (*kerub mimshaḥ ha-sōkek*, 28:14), the *holy mountain of God* (*har qōdesh elōhîm*, 28:14), *stones of fire* (*avnê ʾēsh*, 28:14, 16), *blameless from the day you were created* (*tāmîm bidrāḵeyḵā*, 28:15) — this vocabulary belongs to **ANE primordial-mythology** rather than to historical-political prophecy. The **nine precious stones** of 28:13 (*sardius, topaz, diamond, beryl, onyx, jasper, sapphire, emerald, carbuncle*) are **nine** of the **twelve stones of the high-priestly breastplate** in Ex 28:17–20 and Ex 39:10–13 (LXX has all twelve in Ezek 28:13; MT has only nine). The breastplate-connection is theologically loaded: the king of Tyre is described in *priestly* iconographic vocabulary. The *cherub* identification — the king is *himself* a *kerub mimshaḥ* (anointed cherub) — is unparalleled in the Hebrew Bible. Cherubim elsewhere are throne-attendants (Ex 25:18–22, 1 Kgs 6:23–28, Ezek 10) or guardians of Eden (Gen 3:24); they are not identified with human kings. The *holy mountain of God* with *stones of fire* is mythological topography drawing on the **Ugaritic Mount Ṣaphan** (Mount Casius / Jebel al-Aqra, the dwelling of Baal in the Ugaritic Baal Cycle) and the **Mesopotamian Mount Mashu** (the cosmic mountain in the Gilgamesh epic, tablet 9). **Heiser (*The Unseen Realm*, 2015; *The Divine Council*, 2008)** and **Mullen (*The Assembly of the Gods*, 1980)** trace the *holy mountain / divine council* tradition through the Ugaritic, Mesopotamian, and Hebrew sources.

**Possible Phoenician royal-Eden myth**: **Greenberg (Anchor)** and **Zimmerli (Hermeneia)** both suggest that 28:11–19 may draw on an otherwise-unattested **Phoenician royal-Eden myth** — a Tyrian court tradition in which the king was figured as a paradisal-cherubic primordial being, ritually expelled and restored. Such a myth would parallel the **Adapa myth** (the Mesopotamian sage-figure who is offered immortality and refuses, attested in the El Amarna and Nineveh tablets) and the **Etana myth** (the king who ascends to heaven on an eagle) and possibly the lost **Tyrian Annals** referenced by Menander of Ephesus. The Phoenician royal ideology is fragmentarily attested in the **Karatepe inscription** (Azitiwadda of Adana, c. 720 BC, in Phoenician) and the **Yehimilk and Abibaal inscriptions** of Byblos. None of these supplies an exact parallel, but they establish the genre. **Block (NICOT)** is more skeptical of a specific Phoenician-myth source and reads the chapter as Ezekiel's own creative theological construction drawing on Genesis 2–3.

**Authorship & dating**: Ezekiel ben Buzi, exilic priest. Greenberg, Block, and Zimmerli all treat the chapter as Ezekielian; the question is the **literary unity** of 28:1–10 and 28:11–19. Greenberg treats them as a **deliberate diptych** by Ezekiel, the historical and the mythological aspects of the same king. Zimmerli identifies the second oracle as drawing on prior mythological tradition Ezekiel inherited and redeployed. The Sidon oracle (28:20–24) is generally accepted as Ezekielian. The restoration-promise (28:25–26) closes the OAN block's first half (chapters 25–28) by turning back to Israel. Date: 587/586 BC for the Tyre material, possibly slightly later for the Sidon and restoration material.

**Archaeological & textual evidence**: **The Eshmunazar sarcophagus inscription** (KAI 14, from Sidon, c. 480 BC) gives Phoenician royal burial conventions and divine-name usage. The **Tabnit sarcophagus inscription** (KAI 13, also Sidon, c. 5th c. BC) preserves the title *kōhēn ʿaštart* (*priest of Astarte*) for the Sidonian king — confirming that **Phoenician kings combined royal and priestly office**, a feature relevant to Ezekiel 28's priestly iconography for the king of Tyre. **The Ahiram sarcophagus inscription** (KAI 1, Byblos, c. 1000 BC) is the oldest substantial Phoenician royal inscription. **MT/LXX variants of 28:13**: MT lists *nine* stones; LXX lists *twelve* stones, matching the high-priestly breastplate. **Cooke (ICC)** and **Block (NICOT)** treat the LXX as harmonizing toward Exodus 28; **Greenberg (Anchor)** retains MT as the original. **4QEzek-a** is fragmentary at chapter 28.

**Key terms**: *nāgîd ṣōr* (נְגִיד צֹר, "prince of Tyre," 28:2 — the historical king); *melekh ṣōr* (מֶלֶךְ צֹר, "king of Tyre," 28:12 — the mythological figure or the same king under different aspect); *ḥôtām toḵnît* (חוֹתָם תָּכְנִית, "signet of perfection," 28:12 — a single Hebrew construct unparalleled elsewhere); *gan-elōhîm* (גַּן-אֱלֹהִים, "garden of God," 28:13 — non-Genesis name for Eden); *kerub mimshaḥ ha-sōkek* (כְּרוּב מִמְשַׁח הַסּוֹכֵךְ, "anointed covering / guardian cherub," 28:14 — the chapter's most disputed phrase); *avnê ʾēsh* (אַבְנֵי-אֵשׁ, "stones of fire," 28:14, 16 — the divine-mountain iconographic detail); *tāmîm* (תָּמִים, "blameless," 28:15 — the Genesis-Job-Levitical key term for moral integrity).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: **two distinct prophetic oracles** — the first (28:1–10) a *judgment oracle*, the second (28:11–19) a *qina* (lamentation, named explicitly in 28:12). Plus the brief *recognition-formula oracle* against Sidon (28:20–24) and the *restoration promise* to Israel (28:25–26). The chapter is structurally complex.

The literary architecture:

- A. First Tyre oracle: prince of Tyre (28:1–10)
  - A1. *I am a god* claim and divine refutation (28:2)
  - A2. Wiser than Daniel; wealth; pride (28:3–5)
  - A3. *Therefore* judgment: foreigners, swords, the pit (28:6–10)
- B. Second Tyre oracle: king of Tyre (28:11–19)
  - B1. *Signet of perfection*; Eden; precious stones (28:11–13)
  - B2. *Anointed guardian cherub*; holy mountain; stones of fire (28:14)
  - B3. *Blameless until unrighteousness was found* (28:15)
  - B4. Cast from the mountain; profaned (28:16–17)
  - B5. Fire from your midst; turned to ashes (28:18–19)
- C. Sidon oracle (28:20–24)
- D. Restoration promise to Israel (28:25–26)

The two Tyre oracles share a **central thematic pivot**: *pride leads to fall*. The first oracle expresses pride in *commercial-political* terms (*by your wisdom you have made wealth ... your heart has become proud*); the second expresses pride in *primordial-mythological* terms (*your heart was proud because of your beauty; you corrupted your wisdom for the sake of your splendor*). The two oracles are *parallel readings of the same king* — historical Ittobaal in his commercial pride, the *type-figure* of cosmic-paradisal pride.

The **literary structure of 28:11–19** is concentric:

- a. Signet, beauty (28:12)
- b. Eden, garden of God, precious stones (28:13)
- c. Anointed cherub, holy mountain, stones of fire (28:14)
- d. Blameless until unrighteousness (28:15)
- c'. Cast from holy mountain, destroyed by guardian cherub, removed from stones of fire (28:16)
- b'. Cast to the ground, exposed to kings (28:17)
- a'. Profaned sanctuaries, fire, ashes (28:18)

The **central rotation** at 28:15 (*blameless until unrighteousness was found*) is the chapter's crux. Hebrew *tāmîm hāyîtā bidrāḵeyḵā miyyom hibbārakhā* — *you were blameless from the day you were created*. This is **created-in-a-state-of-blamelessness** language; *until iniquity was found* implies that the iniquity *originated within* the cherub-figure, not from external corruption.

The **leitwort *ḥokmā* (wisdom)** appears five times in the chapter (28:3, 4, 5, 7, 12, 17). The first oracle praises Tyre's *commercial wisdom*; the second oracle revises: the wisdom is *corrupted for the sake of splendor* (28:17). The chapter offers a sustained meditation on **wisdom-without-humility-becomes-self-divinization**.

**MT/LXX variants**: 28:13 — MT has nine precious stones; LXX has twelve, harmonizing with Exodus 28:17–20. 28:14 — MT has *att-kerub mimshaḥ ha-sōkek* (*you were the anointed covering cherub*); LXX has *meta tou cheroub* (*with the cherub*) — implying the figure is *with* the cherub rather than *being* the cherub. The LXX variant is theologically momentous: the Hebrew identifies the figure *as* the cherub (the basis for the patristic Satan-as-cherub reading); the Greek implies the figure is a *human* placed *alongside* the cherub (the basis for the Jewish Adam-comparison reading). Modern critical scholarship is divided. **Greenberg (Anchor)** and **Block (NICOT)** retain MT *as* the cherub; **Cooke (ICC)** and **Eichrodt (OTL)** prefer LXX *with* the cherub. The **Targum Jonathan** sides with the LXX reading: *you were a king, anointed and great*, treating the figure as *human-royal* rather than cherubic-angelic.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Place in the canonical arc**: Chapter 28 is the climactic and most theologically saturated unit of the Tyre cycle. The chapter's primordial-mythological imagery has had an **outsized afterlife** in both Jewish and Christian theological tradition — out of all proportion to its function within Ezekiel itself. The chapter is the textual seedbed of the Christian doctrine of the **fall of Satan** as primordial cherub.

**Jewish trajectory**: **Targum Jonathan** reads 28:11–19 as a *literary comparison* of the king of Tyre to *Adam in Eden* — the king is *like* Adam, expelled from paradise for pride. This *Adam-comparison* reading became the dominant medieval Jewish position. **Rashi** explicitly takes the figure as the human king of Tyre, with the Eden language as poetic comparison. **Radak** offers a longer treatment but stays with the Adam-typology. **Ibn Ezra** is similar. The **Bavli** treats the chapter cursorily; **Pesiqta Rabbati 33** preserves an aggadic tradition that the king of Tyre was so proud he claimed direct divine descent from Tyre's god Melqart, and his fall is the demonstration that no human king can claim divinity. **Maimonides** (*Guide* 2.6) reads the cherub-language as figurative for *the rational soul*; in the *Guide* Maimonides systematically de-mythologizes the angelic-cherubic vocabulary of the Hebrew Bible. Modern Jewish: **Greenberg (Anchor)** treats the chapter as Ezekiel's own creative theological work, drawing on Genesis 2–3 to express the king of Tyre's pride; **Heiser (*The Unseen Realm*)** — though Heiser is Christian, his work draws heavily on Jewish Second-Temple-period sources — argues that the chapter draws on the broader **divine council / holy mountain** tradition that runs through the Ugaritic, Mesopotamian, and Hebrew Second-Temple sources.

**Christian trajectory**: The patristic **Satan-as-King-of-Tyre identification** runs through the major fathers. **Origen's *De Principiis* 1.5.4** reads 28:11–19 (alongside Isa 14:12–15, the *Lucifer / morning star* passage about the king of Babylon) as describing the **fall of the primordial cherub who became Satan**. Origen's reasoning: the language of *blameless from the day you were created until unrighteousness was found in you* (28:15) cannot apply to a human king (no human is *blameless from creation*); it must apply to a *created* being who fell from blamelessness — the angelic Lucifer-Satan of the Christian tradition. **Tertullian's *Adversus Marcionem* 5.11** invokes the same passage. **Jerome's Commentary on Ezekiel** (414) follows Origen and Tertullian. **Augustine in *City of God* 11.13–15** systematizes the doctrine: the angelic fall is presupposed in the Genesis serpent narrative, and Ezekiel 28 (with Isa 14) supplies the textual foundation. **Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job*** elaborates the cherubic ontology of the fallen Lucifer. The medieval angelological tradition (Pseudo-Dionysius's *Celestial Hierarchy*, Aquinas *ST* I.63 on the angelic fall, Bonaventure) builds on this foundation. **Calvin's *Institutes* 1.14.16** cites Ezekiel 28 and Isaiah 14 for the doctrine of Satan's fall: *Scripture clearly proves that the devils were created angels, but by their falling away they corrupted themselves and became instruments of perdition for others*. **Milton's *Paradise Lost*** draws on this tradition extensively — the fallen Lucifer of Books 1–6 is constructed in significant part from Ezekiel 28's cherubic imagery (the *jewel-encrusted throne*, the *holy mountain*, the *stones of fire* all reappear in *Paradise Lost*).

**The critical-mythological reading** (**Zimmerli (Hermeneia)**, **Block (NICOT)**, **Eichrodt (OTL)**, modern critical scholarship) treats 28:11–19 as **ANE-style mythological imagery** drawn from a **Phoenician royal-Eden myth** (possibly related to the Adapa or Gilgamesh paradise traditions) that Ezekiel redeploys against the king of Tyre. The *cherub* language is mythological iconography, the *holy mountain* is the ANE divine-council mountain (cf. Ps 48:2's *Mount Zaphon*), the *stones of fire* are the meteoric stones of mythological cosmology. The reading does not deny the chapter's theological force but locates it in *mythological-rhetorical* rather than *primordial-historical* terms.

**Heiser's *The Unseen Realm* (2015)** offers a third position: the chapter draws on the **divine council / holy mountain** tradition, the figure is a *cosmic-mythological* primordial being but not specifically *Satan* in the later Christian sense — belonging to the broader Watchers / divine council tradition rather than the Lucifer-fall tradition specifically.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The Satan-as-King-of-Tyre identification — patristic-Christian vs. critical-mythological vs. Jewish-literary readings.** This is the central interpretive crux.

**The patristic reading** (Origen, Tertullian, Jerome, Augustine, Calvin Institutes 1.14.16, Aquinas ST I.63, Milton) takes 28:11–19 as **the most direct biblical description of the primordial fall of Satan**. The reasoning is internal-textual: the figure is *created* (28:13, 15), *blameless from creation*, *unrighteousness was found in* him, *cast out* of the *holy mountain of God*, was a *cherub* — language no historical human king could literally satisfy. Combined with **Isaiah 14:12–15** and **Luke 10:18** (*I saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven*), Ezekiel 28 forms one third of the classical fall-of-Satan tradition. Steelman: the textual specifics — *anointed cherub*, *Eden the garden of God*, *blameless from creation*, *cast as profane from the mountain of God* — strain against any *purely human-historical* referent.

**The critical-mythological reading** (Zimmerli, Block, Eichrodt) reads the specifics as **ANE mythological imagery** Ezekiel redeploys rhetorically against the king of Tyre. Steelman: no other OT passage develops Satan-as-cherub theology, and Ezekiel's cherub imagery elsewhere (chs. 1, 10) is throne-bearing not fallen-being. Building a doctrine of Satan's fall on two passages that primarily address human kings is eisegesis.

**The Jewish-literary reading** (Targum Jonathan, Rashi, Radak, Ibn Ezra) takes the figure as the *human* king of Tyre compared to Adam in Eden. Steelman: the chapter is *internally* about the king of Tyre, the *prince* and *king* are the same person, the Adam-typology fits the proud-beauty-fall arc of Genesis 2–3, and there is no need to introduce *Satan* into a chapter that does not name him.

What's lost if the patristic reading is denied? Classical Christian theology of evil's *origin in created freedom* loses one of its three principal textual anchors (Isa 14, Luke 10:18 the others). Aquinas's angelic-sin metaphysics, Augustine's *two cities*, Calvin's account of evil's origin, Milton's literary theology — all are weaker without Ezekiel 28 read patristically. Steelman the patristic position: the canonical text *as it stands in MT* says what the patristic tradition takes it to say; the alternative readings are sophisticated reinterpretations that may be more historically accurate but cost theological power.

Where this leaves us: Ezekiel 28:11–19 is the densest single chapter for the *fall of Satan / origin of evil* doctrine in the Christian tradition. The unresolved question: is the cosmic-mythological reading (patristic-traditional) or the historical-rhetorical reading (Jewish-critical) the *original* sense of the text, and what *theological* weight should the original sense carry against the *received* canonical reading? The chapter does not answer; it puts the question on the table.

**Tension 2: Divine self-claim as definitive idolatry — *I am a god* (28:2, 9) and Christ's *I am* statements in John.** The first oracle's central indictment is the *prince of Tyre*'s declaration *I am a god, I sit in the seat of the gods, in the heart of the seas* (28:2). This is taken as definitive idolatry — the human king claiming divine status. Yet the New Testament's central Christological claim is *precisely* a divine self-identification: **Christ's *egō eimi* statements** in John (Jn 8:58, *before Abraham was, I am*; Jn 10:30, *I and the Father are one*; Jn 14:9, *whoever has seen me has seen the Father*).

The Christological problem: how is **Christ's divine self-claim** not the same as the **king of Tyre's**? Both involve a human being uttering divine self-identification. Both invoke direct claim to divine status. Yet one is **definitive idolatry meriting destruction**, the other is **the center of Christian confession**.

**Bauckham's *Jesus and the God of Israel*** (2008) and **Hurtado's *Lord Jesus Christ*** (2003) supply the standard scholarly Christological responses: Christ's divine self-claim is *vindicated* by his **resurrection** (Rom 1:4: *declared to be the Son of God in power according to the Spirit of holiness by his resurrection from the dead*); the king of Tyre's divine self-claim is *exposed* by his **destruction** (Ezek 28:8–10: *thrust you down to the pit ... die the death of the slain*). The criterion of legitimate vs. illegitimate divine self-claim is **vindication by the Father** — and only the *unique-Son* claim is so vindicated. **Boyarin's *The Jewish Gospels*** (2012) argues that the Johannine *I am* statements are intelligible only against the Second-Temple Jewish *binitarian* tradition that allowed for a *second power in heaven* (the *Memra*, the Wisdom-figure, the Son-of-Man); the *king of Tyre* claim violates *both* monotheism and binitarianism, while Christ's claim invokes the divinely-sanctioned binitarian structure.

The least intuitive position to a strict-monotheist Jewish reader is the **Christian Christological reading** that distinguishes Christ's *I am* from the king of Tyre's. The least intuitive to a confessional Christian is the **Jewish reading** (Boyarin) that the Christian *I am* tradition draws on Second-Temple binitarianism shared by Jewish writers of the period. Both can be steelmanned. The unresolved question: by what hermeneutic criterion does one distinguish *legitimate* divine self-claim from *idolatrous* divine self-claim? Resurrection-vindication is the standard Christian answer; the question of whether that answer is *internally compelling* or *externally imposed* remains.

Where this leaves us: Ezekiel 28 establishes the principle that **divine self-claim by a creature is the definitive idolatry**. The unresolved question: how does **Christ's** divine self-claim in John not fall under this principle, and what hermeneutic criterion mediates the distinction? The chapter does not answer; the New Testament's resurrection-Christology proposes the answer; and the conversation between Tyre's destruction and Christ's exaltation is the deepest theological dialogue Ezekiel 28 sets in motion.

## The Thread

The *anointed guardian cherub on the holy mountain of God* of Ezekiel 28:14 is read in **Origen *De Principiis* 1.5.4**, **Tertullian *Adv. Marc.* 5.11**, and **Augustine *City of God* 11.13–15** as the primordial Lucifer-Satan, and the chapter joins **Isaiah 14:12–15** as one of two textual anchors for the Christian doctrine of the angelic fall. The *Eden, the garden of God* of 28:13 reappears in **Genesis 2:8–17, 3:24** (the original Eden narrative with its guardian cherubim) and in **Revelation 22:1–5** (the New Jerusalem with its tree of life — Eden restored). The *I am a god* claim of 28:2, exposed by destruction, is dramatically inverted in **Philippians 2:6–11**: the *one who being in the form of God did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself ... therefore God has highly exalted him, and bestowed on him the name above every name*. Christ's *non-grasping* of equality vindicates the divine self-identification; the king of Tyre's *grasping* of it invites destruction. The *signet of perfection* (Hebrew *ḥôtām toḵnît*, 28:12) reappears in **Haggai 2:23** (Zerubbabel as YHWH's *signet*) and in **Songs 8:6** (*set me as a seal upon your heart*) — the *seal* imagery transfers from the king of Tyre, who profaned it, to the messianic restoration. **Milton's *Paradise Lost*** (Book 1, lines 84–270) draws Lucifer's character and architecture explicitly from Ezekiel 28: the throne, the gems, the *holy mountain*, the *stones of fire*, the original beauty, the falling — all are derived. The chapter has thus had a **double afterlife**: in patristic-Reformation theology as the primordial cherub-fall, and in literary-imaginative tradition as the architecture of every subsequent theological-poetic image of fallen majesty.
