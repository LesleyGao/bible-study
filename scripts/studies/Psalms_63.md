# Psalm 63 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

The superscription — *mizmor le-David behyoto bemidbar Yehudah* (מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד בִּהְיוֹתוֹ בְּמִדְבַּר יְהוּדָה), "a psalm of David, when he was in the wilderness of Judah" — is one of thirteen Davidic psalms with a biographical heading. Two periods of Davidic flight match: the years running from Saul (1 Sam 23-26, set in the Wilderness of Ziph and Maon) and the flight from Absalom (2 Sam 15-17, when David crossed the Jordan into the wilderness east of Jericho). Rashi and the medieval Jewish commentators placed the psalm in the Saul period; many modern interpreters (Goldingay, Tate) read v. 11's reference to "the king" as decisive for the Absalom period — the speaker himself is the king. Form-critical scholars from Gunkel forward have generally treated the superscription as a secondary editorial layer that catechizes the psalm into Davidic biography rather than fixing its origin. The cautious historical-critical position is agnostic: the psalm uses the wilderness as setting and metaphor, and the superscription anchors it in the Davidic narrative without dictating which episode.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 63 as a *Vertrauenspsalm* — a psalm of trust — though with elements of complaint (the enemies of v. 9-10) and a royal coda (v. 11) that have led some (Mowinckel, Eaton) to reclassify it as a royal psalm proper. The royal coda is the interpretive crux: is the psalm a personal-mystical meditation that ends with a courtly flourish, or a royal psalm whose mystical opening was retrojected onto the king? Hossfeld and Zenger (Hermeneia) read the psalm as originally individual and later given a royal reading in the Davidic redaction of Book II. John Eaton (*Kingship and the Psalms*, 1976) reverses this: the psalm was royal from the start, and the "I" of the opening is the king himself, performing a liturgical thirst at the sanctuary on behalf of the people.

The opening — *Elohim eli atta ashacharekka tzame'a lekha nafshi* (אֱלֹהִים אֵלִי אַתָּה אֲשַׁחֲרֶךָּ צָמְאָה לְךָ נַפְשִׁי), "O God, you are my God; earnestly I seek you; my soul thirsts for you" — is rich with verbal and grammatical density. *Ashacharekka* (אֲשַׁחֲרֶךָּ) is a piel cohortative from *shachar* (שָׁחַר), the same root as *shachar* (שַׁחַר, "dawn"). Most modern translators render "earnestly seek" (ESV), reading the verb as denominative from "morning" — to seek at dawn, then by extension to seek eagerly. The Targum reads this temporally — "I shall seek you at dawn" — which fed directly into the Christian morning-prayer tradition. *Tzame'a* (צָמְאָה, "thirsts") and the parallel *kamah* (כָּמַהּ, "faints / longs") in v. 1 form a paired physical metaphor: the body's thirst becomes the soul's longing. The "dry and weary land where there is no water" (*eretz tziyya ve-'ayef beli mayim*, אֶרֶץ צִיָּה וְעָיֵף בְּלִי מָיִם) is concrete Judean wilderness imagery — the *yeshimon*, the badlands east and south of Jerusalem where summer brings genuine dehydration.

Psalm 63 stands in Book II of the Psalter (Pss 42-72), the *Elohistic Psalter*, and uses *Elohim* exclusively for God (six times) — no YHWH at all. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of Psalm 63 in 11QPsa and 4QPsa with no significant variants from the Masoretic Text. The Septuagint translates *ashacharekka* as ὀρθρίζω πρὸς σέ ("I rise early to you"), unambiguously temporal — and this Greek translation is the form that entered Christian liturgy through the Vetus Latina ("ad te de luce vigilo") and Vulgate ("ad te de luce vigilo"). The morning-prayer reading is in part a translation history.

The thirst-and-water imagery has clear Ancient Near Eastern parallels. Egyptian and Mesopotamian hymns frequently depict the worshipper as a parched land or desiccated reed; the Mesopotamian *šuilla* prayers compare the supplicant to one wandering in the desert. But the specifically covenantal coloring — *Elohim eli atta*, "O God, *you are my God*" — pulls the psalm toward Israel's particular theology. The language echoes the *Shema* ("YHWH is our God") and the covenant formula ("I will be your God, you shall be my people," Exod 6:7).

## Lens 2: The Literature

Psalm 63's parallelism is among the Psalter's most studied. The Lowth categories all surface, but the dominant pattern is what later scholars (Adele Berlin, Robert Alter) have called *climactic* or *intensifying* parallelism. Verse 1 stacks three parallel clauses — *I seek you / my soul thirsts for you / my flesh faints for you* — each intensifying the bodily reach of the longing. The second line shifts from cognitive ("seek") to interior ("soul thirsts") to bodily ("flesh faints"). This is parallelism not as repetition but as descent into deeper layers of the self.

The strophic structure divides naturally into three movements. Verses 1-4 are the longing-and-vow strophe: thirst gives way to remembered sanctuary vision (v. 2) and the resolved praise (vv. 3-4). Verses 5-8 are the satisfaction-and-meditation strophe: the imagined feast ("fat and rich food," *chelev va-deshen*, חֵלֶב וָדֶשֶׁן), the night meditation, the clinging soul. Verses 9-11 are the imprecation-and-royal-coda strophe: the enemies' fate, the king's joy, the silencing of liars. The shift from the intensely personal first two strophes to the third's third-person plural and royal voice is abrupt enough that some critical scholars (Briggs, *ICC*) have proposed v. 11 as a later addition. Most contemporary commentators (Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate) defend the unity of the psalm: the imprecation is the dark side of the trust, and the royal coda is the public dimension of the personal confession.

Two key inclusios bind the psalm. The verb *nafshi* (נַפְשִׁי, "my soul / my life") appears at the start (v. 1, "my soul thirsts"), middle (v. 5, "my soul will be satisfied"), and again (v. 8, "my soul clings to you") and (v. 9, "those who seek my soul"). The same word that thirsts in v. 1 is sought for destruction in v. 9 — the enemies aim at the very thing that is reaching for God. The motion from *tzame'a nafshi* (my soul thirsts) to *tisba' nafshi* (my soul will be satisfied) to *davqah nafshi acharekha* (my soul clings to you) is the psalm's core trajectory: thirst, satisfaction, adherence.

The verb *davaq* (דָּבַק) in v. 8 — *davqah nafshi acharekha* (דָּבְקָה נַפְשִׁי אַחֲרֶיךָ), "my soul clings to you / cleaves to you" — is the same verb used in Genesis 2:24 for marital union ("a man shall leave his father and mother and cleave to his wife") and in Deuteronomy 10:20 and 11:22 for covenantal union with YHWH ("you shall fear YHWH your God; him shall you serve, and to him shall you cleave"). The choice of verb is loaded: covenantal cleaving, marital cleaving, the soul's clinging — all the same root. The medieval Jewish mystical tradition's *devekut* (דְּבֵקוּת, "cleaving"), the central category of Hasidic spirituality, is built on this verbal stem and on this verse.

The night-watch imagery in v. 6 — *ashmurot* (אַשְׁמֻרוֹת, "watches") — is technical: the night was divided into three watches in Hebrew reckoning (or four in the later Roman scheme). The bed-meditation language is unique in the Psalter for its tenderness, distinct from the night-tears of Psalm 6 or the night-songs of Psalm 42. The "shadow of your wings" (*betzel kenafekha*, בְּצֵל כְּנָפֶיךָ, v. 7) recalls the cherubim wings over the ark — the worshipper sheltered under the cultic image of divine presence — but also evokes the protective wings of the mother bird (Deut 32:11; Ps 91:4).

Notably absent: there is no *selah* in Psalm 63. The psalm flows without the standard liturgical break, which has led some scholars (Tate) to suggest it was composed as a single private meditation rather than for processional or antiphonal use.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

In Book II of the Psalter, Psalm 63 forms a tightly bound trust-cluster with Psalms 61-64, all attributed to David. Gerald Wilson and Nancy deClaissé-Walford see this cluster as part of the Davidic theological foundation that anchors Books I-II before the historical crisis of Books III-V. The placement of the wilderness-thirst psalm near the Elohistic Psalter's center of gravity gives the trust-language a particular liturgical weight: the king's longing for God in the wilderness is the people's longing for the temple in exile.

**The Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 63 reads the *midbar* (wilderness) as both literal and figurative — the soul's wilderness in any age. Rashi (11th c.) anchors the psalm in Saul's pursuit, with the "dry and weary land" as the historical Wilderness of Judah. Ibn Ezra (12th c.) reads more philosophically, treating the *tzame'a nafshi* as the soul's longing for the divine presence withdrawn during the speaker's separation from the sanctuary. Radak (David Kimchi, 13th c.) and Rabbeinu Bahya develop the verb *davaq* (cleaving, v. 8) into a foundational mystical category — *devekut*, the soul's adherence to God, which becomes the central spiritual aim of medieval Jewish mysticism and later, in transformed form, of Hasidism. The Ba'al Shem Tov and his school treat Psalm 63 as a charter text for *devekut* practice. Martin Buber, in *Hasidism and Modern Man* (1958), reads the *davqah nafshi* as the model of the I-Thou relation in its specifically religious form. Abraham Joshua Heschel cites the psalm in *God in Search of Man* (1955) as the locus classicus of the soul's *kavvanah* (intentional reaching). Jon Levenson and Nahum Sarna both read the wilderness-thirst as covenantal: the speaker is reliving the Exodus generation's thirst, with God as the rock that gives water. The psalm is recited daily in some traditions during *Birkot Hashachar* (the dawn blessings) — a liturgical placement that fits the *ashacharekka* / dawn-seeking reading.

**The Christian trajectory.** Athanasius's *Letter to Marcellinus* (4th c.) explicitly recommends Psalm 63 as a morning-prayer text: "if you wish to praise the Lord at dawn, sing this psalm." This recommendation, combined with the LXX's *de luce* translation, made Psalm 63 the foundational psalm of Christian morning worship for fifteen centuries. The psalm is appointed for **Lauds on Sunday** in the Roman Office (preserved through the 1962 *Liber Usualis* and into the post-Vatican II *Liturgia Horarum*); it appears at **Orthros (Matins)** in the Byzantine Rite as one of the Six Psalms of dawn; and the Anglican *Book of Common Prayer* assigns it for early-morning use. To pray Psalm 63 is to participate in a continuous tradition stretching from the desert fathers through Benedict's *Rule* (where it is appointed for daily Lauds) through the medieval cathedral office through Cranmer's reforms into the present day.

Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 62 (his numbering, our 63) is among his most affectionate commentaries: he reads the *desideravit te anima mea* ("my soul has thirsted for you") as the central confession of the church in via — the pilgrim soul's longing for the heavenly homeland. Cassiodorus (6th c.) develops the threefold strophic division and treats the *davqah* / cleaving as the goal of the contemplative life. Bernard of Clairvaux's *Sermons on the Song of Songs* (12th c.) draws repeatedly on Psalm 63's thirst-language in his bridal-mystical theology — the soul's thirst for the divine Bridegroom. Aquinas's lectures on the Psalms treat the *fat and rich food* (v. 5) as Eucharistic foretaste. Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* (1519-21) and his later sermons read the psalm as the believer's daily morning act of justifying faith — the soul's thirst answered by the Word and Sacrament. Calvin's 1557 commentary lingers on v. 3's "your steadfast love is better than life," reading it as the heart of the gospel's anthropology: God's *chesed* outweighs the natural good of life itself.

The mystical-contemplative tradition has made Psalm 63 a central text. John of the Cross's *Spiritual Canticle* and *Living Flame of Love* draw on the thirst-and-night imagery. Bernard of Clairvaux's *On Loving God* reads the *davqah* as the fourth and highest degree of love. Teresa of Ávila cites the psalm in *Interior Castle*. The Eastern Orthodox tradition treats Psalm 63 as a foundational text for hesychastic prayer and the practice of unceasing prayer — the *clinging soul* of v. 8 maps onto the goal of continuous *mneme Theou* (remembrance of God). The Carmelite, Cistercian, and Benedictine traditions all treat the psalm as central to their daily rule.

The 19th-20th-century critical tradition — Gunkel, Mowinckel, Westermann, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate, Wenham, Eaton — has converged on a reading that holds the personal-mystical and royal-cultic dimensions together. Walter Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*) treats Psalm 63 as a paradigm of "psalms of new orientation" — the trust spoken not in the absence of trouble but on the far side of it. Patrick Miller and William Brown both stress the bodily and sensory density of the psalm's God-language. Beth Tanner's *NICOT* contribution (2014) underscores the integration of thirst, sanctuary memory, and royal vow as a single liturgical movement. Ellen Davis's reading (in *Wondrous Depth*, 2005) emphasizes the night-meditation as a model of contemplative practice grounded in covenantal memory rather than mystical absorption.

The New Testament's appropriation of Psalm 63 is more allusive than citational. The Johannine "thirst" theology (Jn 4:14, 6:35, 7:37) draws on the same wilderness-thirst imagery, and Revelation 22:17's "let the one who is thirsty come" stands in the same tradition. Hebrews 6:5's "tasted the heavenly gift" echoes the satisfaction-language of v. 5. The Gethsemane scene's night-watch (Mt 26:38-46) and the resurrection appearances at dawn (Mk 16:2; Jn 20:1) gain liturgical depth when read through the morning-prayer reception of Psalm 63.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

Two tensions structure Psalm 63's theological field.

**Tension 1: desert spirituality versus royal-historical fugitive.** The mystical-monastic reading — the soul's thirst as the universal longing for God, the *midbar* as the dark night, the *davqah* as contemplative cleaving — has fifteen hundred years of Christian liturgical use behind it and the medieval Jewish *devekut* tradition besides. The historical-royal reading insists that the psalm is the actual prayer of an actual king on the run, that the wilderness is the Wilderness of Judah, that the enemies are Saul's forces or Absalom's, and that the royal coda is not a tacked-on flourish but the psalm's organizing key. A third position (Eaton, Hossfeld-Zenger, Tanner) holds these together: the psalm is *both* royal-historical *and* personally appropriable, because the king's prayer is offered as a model for the people. The Reformed tradition (Calvin, then later) tends to insist on the historical anchoring; the Catholic-monastic tradition (Augustine through Bernard) tends to allegorize freely; the Lutheran tradition (Luther, then Bonhoeffer in *Psalms: Prayer Book of the Bible*) tries to hold both.

The steelman of the desert-spirituality reading: the psalm itself moves quickly from external setting (v. 1's dry land) to interior posture (vv. 5-8's bed and night-watches), and the church's universal use of the psalm shows that its language is portable beyond its original setting. The steelman of the royal-historical reading: v. 11's first-person reference to "the king" (*ha-melekh*, הַמֶּלֶךְ) and the imprecation against political enemies (vv. 9-10) refuse to dissolve into pure inwardness — the psalm is a public prayer with political stakes. The steelman of the both/and: in the biblical theology, the king's prayer always represents the people, and the people's prayer always echoes the king's; there is no clean separation of the personal and the political.

**Tension 2: personal-mystical versus corporate-liturgical use.** Should Psalm 63 be prayed alone in the bed and night-watches, as the psalm itself seems to imagine (v. 6), or together at Lauds with the choir, as fifteen hundred years of Christian practice and parallel Jewish use have done? The mystical tradition (John of the Cross, the hesychasts, the Hasidic *devekut* school) has emphasized the solitary, intimate, contemplative reading. The cathedral office tradition (Benedict, Cranmer, Vatican II *Liturgia Horarum*) has insisted on the corporate reading: the psalm is best prayed together because the longing it voices is the church's longing, not just the individual's. A third position (Bonhoeffer, Brueggemann, Ellen Davis) argues that the psalm models a movement *between* solitude and assembly: the night-watches feed the morning office, and the morning office sends the worshipper back into the night to remember.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 63 refuses to choose. It is solitary and corporate, royal and personal, historical and portable, monastic and political, Jewish and Christian, ancient and current. Its bodily metaphors (thirst, faintness, cleaving, fat and rich food) ground the soul's longing in flesh; its night-watches ground the day in sleep; its royal coda grounds the personal in the public; its imprecation refuses to pretend the enemies are not real. The pastoral force is that the psalm gives the worshipper a language for longing without prescribing how that longing must be performed. To pray Psalm 63 at dawn, alone or with others, on the run or at the altar, in joy or in want — this is what the psalm has been used for, and what it continues to invite.

**Closing question:** When the psalm pairs *davqah nafshi acharekha* (my soul clings to you) with *ha-melekh yismach b'Elohim* (the king will rejoice in God) — when the most intimate verb of personal cleaving sits beside the public language of royal joy — what kind of self is the psalm shaping, and can that self still be prayed by people who are neither monks nor kings?

## The Thread

- Psalm 42-43 — the other great wilderness-thirst psalm ("as a deer pants for streams of water"), the Korah parallel to Psalm 63's Davidic
- Deuteronomy 11:22 — the *davaq* / cleaving covenant command that v. 8 echoes ("cleave to him")
- John 4:14 / 7:37 — Jesus's appropriation of the wilderness-thirst imagery in the living-water discourses
- Revelation 22:17 — the canonical resolution of the thirst-language ("let the one who is thirsty come")
