# Jeremiah 4: Un-Creation and the Foreskin of the Heart

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 4 escalates from the call-to-return rhetoric of chapter 3 into the apocalyptic vision of imminent catastrophe. The dating is contested but the historical referent is increasingly clear as the chapter progresses. The "destroyer of nations" who "has gone out from his place" (4:7) and the "disaster from the north" (4:6) point unambiguously to the Neo-Babylonian threat under Nebuchadnezzar II, who succeeded his father Nabopolassar in 605 BC and immediately defeated the Egyptians at Carchemish in the same year. The Babylonian Chronicle (BM 21946) records that campaign in detail. Most scholars (Holladay, Lundbom, Bright) date the bulk of chapter 4 to the years immediately after 605 BC — Jehoiakim's reign — when the Babylonian threat had become concrete and Judah's leadership was vacillating between Egyptian and Babylonian alignment. Some sections, particularly the apocalyptic un-creation vision of 4:23–28, may belong even later, possibly after the first deportation of 597 BC.

The geography of the threat is precise. The voice that "declares from Dan" (4:15) — the northernmost city of historical Israel, at the foot of Mount Hermon — and "proclaims trouble from Mount Ephraim" — the central highland north of Jerusalem — traces the conventional invasion route from Mesopotamia through Syria, down the Beqaa, past the Galilee, through the central highlands, and into Judah. The route is the same one Sennacherib followed in 701 BC and the one Nebuchadnezzar would take in 597 and 587. The reference to chariots "like the whirlwind" and horses "swifter than eagles" (4:13) reflects accurate intelligence about Neo-Babylonian military capability — the Babylonian army was a chariot-and-cavalry force on the Assyrian model, and the speed of the Babylonian campaigns from Carchemish onward (Nebuchadnezzar reached Hamath within weeks of Carchemish, and was at Jerusalem's gates by 597) justifies the imagery.

The "fortified cities" of 4:5 are the Judean hill-country fortresses — Lachish, Azekah, Mizpeh, and the smaller installations — to which the rural population would flee in time of invasion. The Lachish Ostraca, written from precisely this defensive system during the final siege of 587, attest to the desperate communications between these fortresses ("we are watching for the signal-fires of Lachish… for we cannot see Azekah," ostracon IV) and parallel the chapter's atmosphere of imminent collapse. The "standard toward Zion" (4:6) is the military signal post — the *nes*, a tall pole or banner used for assembling troops or guiding refugees — and the verse describes the actual emergency-mobilization protocol of late Iron II Judah.

The polemic against external beautification in 4:30 — "what do you mean that you dress in scarlet, that you adorn yourself with ornaments of gold, that you enlarge your eyes with paint?" — refers to specific late Iron II cosmetic practices. *Pukh* (פּוּךְ), the eye-paint mentioned, was kohl, a black eye liner made from galena or stibium that was widely used across Egypt and the Levant; the Egyptian khol-pots and applicators have been recovered in dozens of excavations. *Shani* (שָׁנִי), scarlet, refers to the dye produced from the kermes insect (*Kermes vermilio* or *K. ilicis*), the most prestigious red dye of the ancient Near East — the same dye later used for the temple veil. The verse is not an attack on cosmetics per se but on the geopolitical signaling of *foreign* beauty: the woman dressed for her foreign lovers, signaling alignment with Egyptian or Mesopotamian aesthetics, who has misjudged her audience.

The standard commentaries already named (Lundbom, Holladay, Brueggemann, Carroll, McKane, Bright, Stulman, Thompson, O'Connor) all give chapter 4 sustained attention, especially for the un-creation vision of 4:23–28, which Brueggemann reads as the prophetic counter-imagination's most radical move.

**Key terms.** *Tohu wa-vohu* (תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ, "without form and void," 4:23) — verbatim from Gen 1:2, the only other occurrence of the exact phrase in the Hebrew Bible. *Mul* (מוּל, "circumcise," 4:4) — the verb of physical circumcision applied here metaphorically to the heart. *Orlat levavchem* (עָרְלַת לְבַבְכֶם, "the foreskin of your hearts," 4:4) — *orlah* is the technical Hebrew term for the foreskin, used here as a metaphor of cardiac obtuseness. *Nes* (נֵס, "standard, banner," 4:6, 21) — the military signal post. *Aphar* (עָפָר, "dust"), and *zer'u rachachem* (זִרְעוּ לָכֶם לִצְדָקָה, "sow for yourselves righteousness," echoing Hos 10:12). *Niv u-nichum* (literally "I have spoken; I have purposed," 4:28) — the divine speech-act of irrevocable judgment.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 4 is one of Jeremiah's most diverse compositions, alternating among hortatory oracle (1–4), war alarm (5–8), prophetic complaint (9–10), divine response (11–12), invasion vision (13–18), prophetic anguish (19–22), un-creation vision (23–28), and the closing image of the laboring woman (29–31). The compositional layering is intricate, and the Mowinckel A/B/C scheme (poetic A material, biographical B, Deuteronomistic C) is harder to apply cleanly than in earlier chapters; most scholars (Holladay, Lundbom) treat the chapter as substantially Jeremianic with some prose-frame additions.

The un-creation vision of 4:23–28 is the chapter's most striking literary moment and one of the most radical theological compositions in the Old Testament. The structure is a fourfold "I looked, and behold" (*ra'iti ve-hineh*) — the same formula used in apocalyptic visions (Dan 7, Zech 1) — but the content moves backward through the days of creation:

- *I looked on the earth, and behold, it was tohu wa-vohu, and to the heavens, and they had no light.* This is Gen 1:2 reversed — the formless void before the divine "let there be light."
- *I looked on the mountains, and behold, they were quaking, and all the hills moved to and fro.* The dry-land creation of day three is undone.
- *I looked, and behold, there was no man, and all the birds of the air had fled.* The creation of humans (day six) and birds (day five) is undone.
- *I looked, and behold, the fruitful land was a desert, and all its cities were laid in ruins.* The cultural infrastructure built upon creation is undone.

The vision is creation in reverse. The same God who, in Genesis 1, brought order out of chaos can return creation to chaos. Brueggemann's reading is that this is the most radical move in prophetic literature: the divine judgment is not merely historical but cosmic, and the prophetic imagination is willing to envision *un-creation* as the ultimate consequence of covenant infidelity. The qualification of 4:27 — "yet I will not make a full end" — preserves the possibility of restoration, but the vision itself stands as a permanent theological possibility.

The literary device of *prophetic anguish* in 4:19–21 — "my anguish, my anguish! I writhe in pain! Oh the walls of my heart!" — is the first appearance of the *confessions of Jeremiah* tone (the pattern that will dominate chapters 11–12, 15, 17, 18, 20). It is often unclear in chapter 4 whether the speaker is Jeremiah or the personified Daughter of Zion or YHWH himself. The ambiguity is probably deliberate: the prophet's grief participates in the divine grief. Kathleen O'Connor (*Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*) develops this most extensively, reading Jeremiah as a book about traumatized prophetic speech in the face of national catastrophe.

The closing image of the laboring woman in 4:31 — "the daughter of Zion gasping for breath, stretching out her hands, 'Woe is me! I am fainting before murderers'" — uses the standard Hebrew metaphor of childbirth pain (*chil ka-yoledah*) for terror, but inverts the conventional valence. Childbirth normally signals new life; here it signals the violent end of life. The image will recur in Jer 6:24, 13:21, 22:23, 30:6, 49:24, and 50:43 — Jeremiah's most repeated metaphor of catastrophe.

The chapter's textual situation is generally stable across MT and LXX, with minor variants but no meaning-altering divergences. The un-creation vision of 4:23–28 is preserved identically in both traditions.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The metaphor of *heart-circumcision* in 4:4 has one of the most generative receptions in Scripture. The motif begins in Deut 10:16 ("circumcise therefore the foreskin of your heart") and Deut 30:6 ("the LORD your God will circumcise your heart"), and is echoed in Lev 26:41 ("if then their uncircumcised heart is humbled"). Jeremiah deepens it; Ezekiel develops it as the "new heart" promise (Ezek 36:26). The New Testament reception is foundational: Paul in Rom 2:29 makes heart-circumcision the criterion of true Jewishness ("real circumcision is a matter of the heart, by the Spirit, not by the letter"); Stephen in Acts 7:51 accuses the Sanhedrin of being "stiff-necked, uncircumcised in heart and ears"; Phil 3:3 calls Christians "the circumcision, who worship by the Spirit of God." The metaphor has been one of the most theologically productive in the canon, but also one of the most contested in Christian-Jewish reception.

The un-creation vision of 4:23–28 has its New Testament correspondences in the eschatological discourses of Mt 24, Mk 13, Lk 21 (the cosmic disturbances accompanying the Son of Man), and especially in the apocalyptic-cosmic disruption sequences of Rev 6:12–17 (the sixth seal: sun darkened, moon as blood, stars falling) and Rev 8 (the trumpets). Brueggemann argues that Jer 4:23–28 is the foundational Old Testament text for biblical apocalyptic — the moment when prophetic imagination crosses the threshold into envisioning cosmic dissolution.

**Jewish trajectory.** Heart-circumcision in the rabbinic tradition is generally treated as *aggadic* deepening of physical circumcision rather than as replacement. Bavli Yebamoth 71a discusses the circumcision of children with bleeding disorders — the legal-halakhic context in which heart-circumcision functions as the *kavanah* (intentionality) of physical circumcision rather than its substitute. Rashi on Jer 4:4 reads the verse as calling for the circumcision of the heart's stubbornness alongside physical circumcision, not in place of it. Daniel Boyarin's *A Radical Jew* (1994) and Krister Stendahl's *Paul Among Jews and Gentiles* (1976) both argue that Paul's reception of Jeremiah's heart-circumcision in Rom 2:29 is *not* as supersessionist as the later Christian tradition assumed — Paul is operating within a Second-Temple Jewish framework in which heart-circumcision was a known intensification of physical circumcision, not its replacement. The contemporary Jewish-Christian dialogue (Soulen, Kendall Soulen, Levenson) has continued to recover the non-supersessionist reading.

**Christian trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah* allegorizes the un-creation vision as the soul's experience of judgment and the heart-circumcision as the soul's purification. Jerome's commentary attends to the Hebrew text and to the Pauline reception. Augustine treats heart-circumcision as a key text for the doctrine of grace: the circumcision of the heart is something God *does to* the human, not something the human achieves (City of God 17.5; commentary on Rom 2). Calvin's reading is similar but more developed: the heart-circumcision of Jer 4:4 is the inward work of regeneration, prefigured in the law and accomplished by the Spirit. The Reformation tradition uniformly treats the verse as a foundational text for the doctrine of inward religion. The critical tradition has focused on the relationship between Jer 4:4 and Deut 10:16 / 30:6 — whether Jeremiah is dependent on the Deuteronomic formulations or vice versa.

**Where the traditions diverge.** Christian tradition has often read heart-circumcision as *replacing* physical circumcision — the new-covenant interiority supplanting the old-covenant exterior. Jewish tradition reads it as *intensifying* physical circumcision without replacing it. The Pauline reception, properly read, is closer to the Jewish position than the later supersessionist Christian tradition assumed. The un-creation vision of 4:23–28 is read by Christian tradition as proleptic of eschatological judgment fulfilled in the apocalyptic discourse and Revelation; Jewish tradition reads it as the prophetic counter-imagination's lament over the destruction of 587 BC, with Lamentations as the lyric counterpart. Critical tradition reads it as a proto-apocalyptic text, the threshold between classical prophecy and the apocalyptic literature that emerges in the Second Temple period.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Can divine judgment go behind creation?** The un-creation vision of 4:23–28 raises the most acute question in classical theism: is YHWH's judgment bounded by the divine commitment to creation, or can the judgment dissolve the very creation it judges? The Catholic creation theology (CCC 290–301; Aquinas ST I.44–47) holds that creation is a *positive* divine act expressing divine goodness, and that while God can judge creation, the judgment cannot ultimately undo creation because doing so would contradict the divine essence. The Catholic reading of Jer 4:23–28 typically emphasizes the qualifier of 4:27 — "yet I will not make a full end" — as the structural limit on un-creation. The Reformed tradition, with its emphasis on divine sovereignty, is willing to entertain the un-creation vision more directly: God can do anything that does not contradict the divine character, and the question is whether un-creation contradicts that character. Calvin's reading of the passage emphasizes the sheer freedom of the divine judgment without dwelling on metaphysical limits.

The Lutheran tradition's *Deus absconditus* — the hidden God — offers the most uncomfortable reading. Luther's *De servo arbitrio* distinguishes the God revealed in Christ (gracious, faithful, bound to the covenant) from the God hidden in majesty (unbounded, free, capable of anything). On this reading, the un-creation vision is a glimpse of the *Deus absconditus* — the God whose judgment can in principle return creation to *tohu wa-vohu*. The Lutheran insistence that the believer must flee from the hidden God to the revealed God in Christ is one response to the vision's terror. The Jewish tradition's *hester panim* (hidden face) tradition is the closest parallel: in moments like the Holocaust, God's face is hidden, and the prophetic counter-imagination's un-creation is the experience of the absent God.

The Eastern Orthodox tradition, with its strong creation-theology and its doctrine of the *logoi* of creation (Maximus the Confessor), reads Jer 4:23–28 as eschatologically transformative rather than annihilative — creation goes through a *passover* into restoration, and the un-creation language is metaphorical for the transformation. The steelman of the most uncomfortable reading is given by Carroll's redaction-critical approach: the vision is a literary device, not a theological claim about what God *can* do, and reading it as cosmology rather than rhetoric overinterprets the text. Where this leaves us: the un-creation vision marks a permanent possibility within prophetic theology that classical metaphysical theology has tried to bound but cannot entirely contain.

**Tension 2: Heart-circumcision as inward religion — supersessionism or intensification?** The verse "circumcise yourselves to the LORD; remove the foreskin of your hearts" (4:4) is one of the most contested loci in Christian-Jewish dialogue. The Christian tradition, especially after the Reformation, has often read it as the foundational Old Testament anticipation of the new-covenant interior religion that Paul announces in Rom 2:29 — the gospel as the spiritualization of the law. The supersessionist version of this reading takes physical circumcision (and by extension, all the cultic-ritual structures of Israelite religion) as superseded by the inward circumcision Christ accomplishes. Origen, Augustine, and the Reformers all participate in this trajectory to varying degrees, though with different emphases.

The Jewish counter-position, articulated by Boyarin, Levenson, Stendahl, and others, is that heart-circumcision in Jeremiah and Deuteronomy is *not* a replacement but an *intensification* of physical circumcision. The metaphor presupposes physical circumcision and demands that the same surgical-removal logic be applied to the heart's stubbornness. To read Jeremiah as advocating heart-circumcision *instead of* physical circumcision is anachronistic — the Jeremianic audience continued to practice physical circumcision and would have heard the metaphor as moral demand layered on covenantal practice, not replacement of it. Stendahl's *Paul Among Jews and Gentiles* argues that Paul's reception in Rom 2:29 is the same: Paul is not replacing physical circumcision with heart-circumcision but arguing that physical circumcision without heart-circumcision is insufficient — a position any Second-Temple Jewish moralist would have endorsed.

The contemporary Catholic reading, especially since *Nostra Aetate* (1965) and Pope Benedict XVI's writings, has retired the supersessionist version and recovered the non-supersessionist reception. Whether the recovery is fully theologically integrated, or whether the practice of Christian theology can fully shed the supersessionist habits of two millennia, is the live question. Where this leaves us: heart-circumcision is one of the most theologically generative metaphors in the canon, but its reception requires constant vigilance against the supersessionist habits that have distorted Christian readings of the Old Testament.

**One unresolved question.** If the un-creation vision (4:23–28) and the heart-circumcision call (4:4) sit in the same chapter, is the relationship causal? Is it that *only* heart-circumcision can prevent the un-creation — that the cosmic-scale judgment is the consequence of unhealed cardiac obtuseness, and that the inward and the cosmic are linked in a way that classical theism has been reluctant to articulate?

## The Thread

The Deuteronomistic background of heart-circumcision (Deut 10:16, 30:6) is the chapter's primary intertext for verse 4. The *tohu wa-vohu* of 4:23 is verbatim Genesis 1:2 — the only other occurrence in the Hebrew Bible — and the chapter is one of the strongest Old Testament examples of *intentional intertextuality*: Jeremiah cites Genesis to undo it. The laboring-woman image of 4:31 finds its inversion in John 16:21 (the woman who "has anguish, because her hour has come, but when she has delivered the baby, she no longer remembers the anguish"), where Jesus uses the same metaphor for the resurrection's reversal of the cross. And the Pauline reception of heart-circumcision in Rom 2:29 — properly read as intensification rather than supersession — links Jeremiah 4 directly to the new-covenant theology of Jeremiah 31:31–34, which Hebrews 8:8–12 quotes in the longest Old Testament citation in the New Testament.
