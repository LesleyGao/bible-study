# Exodus 5: Who Is YHWH?

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Exodus 5 is a throne-room scene and a brickyard scene, and the distance between them is the distance between sovereign speech and enforced silence. The chapter presumes a Ramesside court — the highly formalized audience hall, the layered bureaucracy of taskmasters (*nōgĕśîm*) and foremen (*šōṭĕrîm*), the record-keeping of labor quotas that characterized the New Kingdom economy. Pharaoh's question, "Who is YHWH?" (v. 2), is not a rhetorical flourish but a theological position consistent with how Egyptian royal ideology worked. Pharaoh was himself divine — the son of Ra, the living Horus — and he was the mediator between the gods and the land. To recognize another god's sovereignty over Egyptian subjects would have been a category-violation. Pharaoh's denial is structurally required by the ideology he embodies.

The material culture of brick-making is unusually well-attested. The Rekhmire tomb at Thebes (TT100, mid-fifteenth century BC) preserves extraordinary painted scenes of Semitic and Nubian laborers mixing mud, stamping bricks in wooden molds, stacking them to dry, and being supervised by overseers with rods. The inscription labels the workers "captives whom his majesty brought for the works of the temple of Amun." The technical process used Nile silt, chopped straw as a binder, and water — the straw gave tensile strength to what would otherwise be brittle mudbrick. Without straw, the bricks crack and crumble; archaeological analysis of Egyptian mudbricks confirms straw content as a structural necessity.

Papyrus Leiden I 348, a New Kingdom administrative document, contains a line that is often cited in Exodus commentary: "There is nobody to make bricks, and there is no straw in the district." The papyrus is a complaint from a supervisor about insufficient materials — the direct historical analogue for the labor conditions of Exodus 5. Kenneth Kitchen draws on this in *On the Reliability of the Old Testament* to argue the chapter reflects authentic Egyptian administrative practice. Papyrus Anastasi V describes similar shortages. The store-cities Pithom and Rameses (Exodus 1:11) have been identified with Tell el-Retaba and Qantir/Pi-Ramesses in the eastern Delta, where extensive Ramesside-era mudbrick construction has been excavated; Manfred Bietak's long-running excavations at Tell el-Dabʿa/Qantir have produced the material evidence of the building program.

Labor disputes in the ancient Near East are not unattested. The Deir el-Medina ostraca from the reign of Ramses III (c. 1155 BC) record what is generally regarded as the first recorded labor strike in history — the artisans who cut the royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings walked off the job because their grain rations had been delayed. They staged sit-ins at mortuary temples and eventually received their wages. This is not the same situation as Exodus 5 — Deir el-Medina was a privileged artisan community, not a conscripted slave workforce — but it establishes that labor protest was part of the Ramesside social world. The biblical narrative taps into real structures.

The foremen (*šōṭĕrîm*, שֹׁטְרִים) are a specific social category. These are Israelites (v. 14) who functioned as middle-management under Egyptian taskmasters — the class that bore the brunt of downward pressure from the state and upward pressure from their own people. When the quota cannot be met, the foremen are beaten; when they complain, they are told "you are idle" by Pharaoh himself. The structure is textbook coercive labor: make the oppressed police each other. The Hebrew root *štr* (שׁטר) means to record or to organize; in this context the *šōṭĕrîm* are the ones with writing implements, the accountants of enslavement.

Authorship and source layers: most critical scholarship assigns Exodus 5 primarily to J (the Yahwist), with possible Deuteronomistic glossing at verses 22–23 in Moses' lament. The chapter's vivid character sketches, its concrete details of the labor system, and its theological wrestle are characteristic J material. Rendtorff and Blum would speak of redactional layering rather than clean source partition. Schmid and Römer see the chapter as part of a deliberately constructed narrative of failed first confrontation, setting up the divine name revelation in chapter 6 (traditionally P) as the theological answer to Pharaoh's "Who is YHWH?" The architecture — failed encounter in chapter 5, divine name reaffirmation in chapter 6 — is too neat to be accidental.

Five terms deserve their original script. *Nōgĕśîm* (נֹגְשִׂים), taskmasters — from the verb *nāgas*, to drive or press, the same root as the debt collectors of Isaiah 58. *Šōṭĕrîm* (שֹׁטְרִים), foremen, the middle-management category. *Teven* (תֶּבֶן), straw; *qaš* (קַשׁ), stubble — the distinction matters in verse 12, because the people are sent to gather *qaš*, inferior material, in place of the *teven* they used to be given. *Nirpîm ʾattem* (נִרְפִּים אַתֶּם), "you are idle" (v. 17) — the word comes from a root meaning slack or relaxed, the rhetorical move of the oppressor who calls the oppressed lazy. And *hēraʿōtā* (הֵרֵעֹתָה), "you have done evil" (v. 22) — the verb Moses hurls at God, from the same root as *raʿ* (evil) that will mark the plagues.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre-wise, Exodus 5 is a confrontation narrative with an embedded labor dispute and a closing lament. It is the first of several confrontations between Moses and Pharaoh that will structure the plague cycle through chapter 12. The chapter's closing — Moses' lament in verses 22–23 — has the form and feel of a complaint psalm in prose. "Why?" (*lāmâ*), "Why did you...?", "You have not delivered..." — this is the vocabulary of Psalm 22, Psalm 44, Jeremiah's confessions. The prose lament is uncommon in the Pentateuch, and its placement here — at the end of the first failed audience — marks the narrative's first real crisis of faith.

Literary devices are precise. The opening exchange sets up a recurring call-and-refusal pattern. Moses and Aaron: "Thus says the LORD, the God of Israel, 'Let my people go.'" Pharaoh: "Who is the LORD... I do not know the LORD." The chapter will answer Pharaoh's question with the plagues, but here the question stands unanswered, and the text treats Pharaoh's ignorance as both the problem and the opportunity. Brueggemann has emphasized that the plague cycle is structured as theological pedagogy: Pharaoh will come to know YHWH by the end.

The structural downward spiral is deliberate. Moses asks; Pharaoh refuses. Pharaoh increases the burden. The foremen complain; Pharaoh refuses. The foremen confront Moses and Aaron; Moses complains to God. Each move makes things worse. The word *ʿăvōdâ* (עֲבֹדָה), labor/service/worship, punctuates the chapter: the word that will be the book's keyword is here firmly in Pharaoh's mouth — the people serve him, not YHWH. The reversal is the book's whole project.

Chiastic elements appear around the foremen's appeal (vv. 15–19): they go up to Pharaoh (A), Pharaoh refuses (B), they come down to meet Moses (A'). The symmetry emphasizes Pharaoh's immovable position at the center. The language of "cry" (*ṣāʿaq*, צָעַק) reappears from Exodus 2:23 — the people cried, God heard, God remembered. In chapter 5, the foremen cry to Pharaoh, and Pharaoh refuses. Theological implication: the human king cannot hear, and this refusal is what the divine King will answer.

Within the book's argument, Exodus 5 is the anti-chapter of chapter 3. Chapter 3 promised deliverance; chapter 5 delivers worse oppression. The narrative creates the problem that chapters 6–12 will solve. The failed first audience is theologically necessary — it forces the declaration that only the hand of God will move Pharaoh. Chapter 6 will reopen with the divine name revelation (the P account) and the reaffirmation of the covenant. The book is constructed to let deliverance come as gift, not as diplomatic success.

Textual variants. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of chapter 5 (4QExodᶜ, 4QpaleoExodm). The Samaritan Pentateuch has minor variants, mostly orthographic. The Septuagint is generally faithful in chapter 5 but softens some of the harsher language of Moses' lament — a pattern the LXX shows elsewhere when biblical speech edges toward accusation of God. The Targum Pseudo-Jonathan expands Pharaoh's answer with a defiant speech about Egyptian gods.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical arc, Exodus 5 sits at the hinge where promise meets initial failure. It echoes Genesis 3 (humanity's first encounter with death and curse) in its structural move from hope to catastrophe, and it foreshadows every prophetic confrontation with kings — Elijah and Ahab, Jeremiah and Zedekiah, Jesus and Pilate. The chapter establishes the pattern: the word of God meets power, power refuses, the faithful suffer, and God is asked to answer. Moses' lament in verses 22–23 is the first of many prophetic laments that will shape the canonical voice — Jeremiah's "Why has my pain been perpetual?" (Jer 15:18); Habakkuk's "How long, O LORD?" (Hab 1:2); the psalmist's "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Ps 22:1) — which Jesus himself will pray on the cross.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Second Temple readings read the chapter as a demonstration of Pharaoh's arrogance and God's patience. The Wisdom of Solomon 11–19 extensively elaborates the plagues as just measure-for-measure responses, with chapter 5's labor increase being the first outrage requiring response. Philo (*Life of Moses* 1.86–95) makes Pharaoh the personification of tyrannical impiety and Moses the exemplary philosopher-king. Josephus (*Antiquities* 2.13) narrates the audience with additional speeches.

Rabbinic readings in *Exodus Rabbah* 5 develop the characters. The foremen, beaten by taskmasters, become heroic figures — Exodus Rabbah identifies them as those who later became the seventy elders of Numbers 11, their suffering qualifying them for leadership. The Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael reads verses 22–23 carefully, noting that Moses' lament is bold speech to God that receives not rebuke but revelation. *Exodus Rabbah* says that Moses' accusation was the speech that earned him the further revelation of chapter 6.

Rashi reads Moses' "Why did you ever send me?" as genuine distress but also as a failure of faith that leads to the divine answer in chapter 6. Rashi's characteristic move is to take Moses' words seriously as dialogue rather than monologue — Moses speaks, and God responds. Ibn Ezra reads the chapter philologically: *nirpîm*, "idle," is the classic accusation of the exploiter, and Ibn Ezra draws parallels with Numbers 13's slanderous spies and Proverbs' sluggard. Ramban (Nachmanides) sees Pharaoh's increased burden as a test — a worsening condition that forces Israel to trust in God rather than in political negotiation.

Maimonides treats the chapter's material in legal and ethical register: the beating of the foremen illustrates the injustice of tyrannical rule that *Hilchot Melakhim* will address.

Modern Jewish readings are sharp. Nahum Sarna's JPS commentary treats the brick-and-straw details with philological and archaeological precision, documenting the Egyptian background. Jon Levenson reads the chapter as the initial collision between the God of Israel and the imperial apparatus — neither can coexist with the other. Abraham Joshua Heschel, in *The Prophets*, reads Moses' lament as exemplary prophetic voice — God's servant who speaks back to God on behalf of suffering people. Avivah Zornberg reads Moses' lament psychoanalytically: the prophet must absorb the people's accusation ("you have made us stink") and hand it to God as his own. Walter Brueggemann, whose engagement with Exodus has been decisive, reads Pharaoh not just as tyrant but as "the economic system of extraction" — Pharaoh personifies a political economy whose survival depends on unending labor.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** The patristic tradition read chapter 5 as the opening skirmish in the cosmic battle between divine and diabolical power. Origen (*Homilies on Exodus* 4) allegorizes Pharaoh as the devil increasing the soul's burden the moment it begins to listen to God. Augustine, in *City of God* 15.2, reads Pharaoh as the archetype of the earthly city's refusal of the divine city. Gregory of Nyssa, in *Life of Moses*, treats the increased burdens as the soul's experience of deepened struggle in the ascent to God.

Aquinas does not comment on chapter 5 as such, but his treatment of tyranny in *De Regimine Principum* and *ST* II-II q. 42 on sedition engages the structural question: when may the oppressed resist the king? Aquinas allows that tyrants may be resisted in certain conditions, though he limits the cases. The chapter provides raw material for later Christian reflection on resistance.

Luther, in his lectures on Exodus, reads Pharaoh as the archetype of law-in-its-terrifying-use — the law that demands and demands and never provides. Moses' preaching increases Israel's burden because the gospel word in a law-dominated world intensifies oppression before it liberates. Calvin, in the *Harmony*, reads the chapter as a lesson in God's hidden ways: God allows oppression to deepen precisely so that deliverance will be recognized as divine. Calvin's Moses is tested and taught through the failure; his lament is honest, not ideal.

Critical scholarship has treated chapter 5 as a narrative study in political economy. Brevard Childs's *Book of Exodus* reads the chapter as theologically about Pharaoh's refusal to recognize YHWH's sovereignty — a refusal that structures the entire plague cycle. Terence Fretheim's *Exodus* reads the chapter's theology of divine response: God works through Moses' lament, not around it. John Durham's *Exodus* (WBC) provides careful philological notes on the brick-making technical vocabulary. William Propp's Anchor Bible commentary gives the most detailed Egyptological background. Walter Brueggemann's *Theology of the Old Testament* and his *Exodus* essays read Pharaoh as the exemplar of "the totalizing system" that reduces human life to production; the chapter is not just about one king but about how extraction-economies operate. Göran Larsson's *Bound for Freedom* mines Jewish interpretive tradition. Gustavo Gutiérrez, in *A Theology of Liberation*, reads chapter 5 as the paradigm of how oppression responds to the announcement of liberation — with intensified oppression before collapse. Sugirtharajah's postcolonial reading foregrounds the chapter's critique of imperial labor extraction.

**Where the trajectories converge and diverge.** Both traditions treat the chapter as foundational — the setup that demands divine response. Both recognize the foremen's tragic position. The divergences concern emphasis. Jewish readings highlight Moses' bold speech to God and the rabbinic celebration of prophets who argue with God. Christian readings more often moralize Moses' lament as insufficient faith (Calvin) or allegorize the struggle (patristics). On Pharaoh, the Christian tradition tends toward typology (Pharaoh as Satan, Pharaoh as earthly city) while Jewish tradition tends toward political and moral particularity. Modern critical and liberation readings — both Jewish and Christian — converge in seeing Pharaoh as the embodiment of an extractive system, not merely a bad man.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author believe God was doing in Exodus 5? The chapter's claim is twofold. First, Pharaoh's ignorance of YHWH is the theological problem that the whole book exists to answer — Pharaoh asks "Who is YHWH?" and the plagues, the sea, Sinai, and the tabernacle are the long answer. Second, the worsening of conditions after the first audience is not a failure of the divine plan but its shape — deliverance will come as God's act, not as negotiation's success. The author's theological vision is that divine rule and imperial rule cannot coexist, that the God of the Hebrews will not accept "a three days' journey into the wilderness" as a compromise, and that the intensification of suffering is what makes the contest unmistakable.

**Tension 1: Why does God allow the burden to increase after sending the deliverer?** Verses 22–23 put the question into Moses' own mouth: "Why did you ever send me? For since I came to Pharaoh to speak in your name, he has done evil to this people, and you have not delivered your people at all."

- **Catholic teaching** (Catechism §§309–314 on providence and evil; Aquinas *ST* I q. 22 a. 2) holds that God permits evils only in view of greater goods. The deepening of oppression prepares Israel for the fullness of deliverance and the knowledge of God. The suffering is permitted, not willed.
- **Reformed Protestant readings** (Calvin *Institutes* I.17–18; Herman Bavinck) hold that God ordains the circumstances, including suffering, for his glory and his people's good. Pharaoh's worse-making is within God's sovereign plan that leads to the exodus. Calvin is not sanguine about suffering; he is sober about it.
- **Lutheran readings** (Luther on the *Deus absconditus*) emphasize the hiddenness of God. God appears to act against his people even while acting for them — the cross itself is the paradigm, and Moses' lament prefigures the lament of Christ.
- **Orthodox readings** (Lossky on divine economy; Gregory of Nyssa) see the intensification as pedagogical — the soul must know its incapacity before it can receive grace. The hardening of conditions is the hardening that reveals.
- **Jewish readings** (Rashi, Heschel, Levenson) treat Moses' lament as legitimate prophetic speech. God is not offended by the question. *Exodus Rabbah* reads Moses' accusation as the speech that opens chapter 6's deeper revelation. The worsening is real, the complaint is real, and the response is God's fresh self-revelation.
- **Liberation theology** (Gutiérrez, Boff) reads the pattern as structural: oppressive systems respond to liberation announcements with intensified oppression before they collapse. God's promise is not undone by intensification; it is confirmed.
- **Critical readings** (Fretheim) emphasize relational realism: God responds to human agency, and Pharaoh's free choice to intensify oppression is genuine. God does not simply manipulate the situation.

Steelman the least intuitive: the Reformed reading that God ordains the worsening. This seems theologically troubling to modern readers who want to protect God from responsibility for suffering. But Calvin is doing serious work. He refuses to make the exodus a product of human diplomatic cleverness; he insists that if God is sovereign, the circumstances of salvation are themselves God's gift. The worsening is not an obstacle God had to overcome but the canvas on which God paints. This is harder but more theologically consistent than the evasions — and it takes seriously the book's own claim (4:21; 5:22) that God announces the hardening in advance.

**Where this leaves us:** The text holds both divine sovereignty and the reality of human oppression without systematic resolution. The worsening is real; the promise is real; the question is whether faith can hold both.

**Tension 2: Pharaoh's question "Who is YHWH?" and the nature of divine self-disclosure.** Pharaoh's ignorance is not innocent. But is it culpable?

- **Catholic teaching** (CCC §§31–38; Vatican II *Dei Verbum*) holds that natural revelation makes God knowable in principle; culpable ignorance is the refusal of what is naturally available. Pharaoh's refusal is culpable because the signs (chapter 4) are offered and rejected.
- **Reformed Protestant readings** (Calvin on general and special revelation; the Westminster Confession) hold that all ignorance of God outside special revelation is culpable, and Pharaoh's specific refusal after being addressed by God's messenger compounds culpability.
- **Wesleyan readings** stress prevenient grace: God works in Pharaoh to make response possible, and Pharaoh's repeated refusal grieves but does not finally coerce.
- **Orthodox readings** (Gregory of Nyssa, Maximus) see ignorance of God as the fallen soul's default state, reversed only by grace. Pharaoh's ignorance is the human condition writ large.
- **Jewish readings** (Maimonides *Hilchot Teshuvah* 6 on the hardening) hold that Pharaoh's initial ignorance may have been innocent but becomes culpable through repeated refusal. Free will is preserved until it is forfeited by repeated sin.
- **Critical readings** (Brueggemann) read Pharaoh's question as the structural claim of empire — imperial power always denies the sovereignty of any god but its own.

Steelman the least intuitive: Brueggemann's structural reading. The question "Who is YHWH?" is not first a personal theological inquiry but an imperial speech-act. Pharaoh is not an individual asking a sincere religious question; he is the institutional voice of a system whose survival depends on recognizing no rival sovereignty. Read this way, Pharaoh's ignorance is both real and structural — he genuinely does not know YHWH because his office forbids him to.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter insists that imperial power and divine sovereignty are at genuine war, that Pharaoh's ignorance is both personal and systemic, and that the plagues will be answer, not argument.

One unresolved question: If prophetic work makes things worse before it makes them better, how can anyone tell, at the moment of worsening, whether they have been sent by God at all?

## The Thread

**Exodus 1:11 — The store-cities.** The bricks Israel made at Pithom and Rameses are the same bricks now demanded without straw. The archaeological layers at Tell el-Retaba and Qantir are the material residue of this chapter.

**Psalm 22:1 — "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"** Moses' "Why?" in verse 22 is the template for biblical complaint prayer. When you reach the cry of dereliction from the cross, remember it was Moses' language first.

**Romans 9:17 — "For this very purpose I have raised you up."** Paul quotes Exodus 9:16, but the theology is already in chapter 5: Pharaoh's refusal will serve the revelation of God's name. The hardening theology Paul uses is rooted here.

**Revelation 18 — The fall of Babylon.** The merchant lament over Babylon's fall is the structural inversion of Exodus 5: the imperial economy that extracted labor and bodies collapses when God acts. When you reach Revelation's economic critique, remember Pharaoh's brickyard.
