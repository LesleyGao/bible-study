# 2 Samuel 5: Bone and Flesh, Stone and Cedar

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens with what the History of David's Rise has been driving toward across two books: **all the tribes of Israel come to David at Hebron**. The acclamation formula they speak — *"behold, we are your bone and flesh"* (5:1) — is dense with covenantal vocabulary. The phrase *etzem* and *basar* (עֶצֶם וּבָשָׂר) for bone and flesh is the Hebrew Bible's standard idiom for kinship and shared identity (Genesis 2:23 of Adam and Eve, Genesis 29:14 of Laban to Jacob, Judges 9:2 of Abimelech to the Shechemites). The northern tribes are formally claiming David as kin and authorizing his rule by treaty rather than by conquest. The verse that follows — "in times past, when Saul was king over us, it was you who led out and brought in Israel" — concedes David's de facto military leadership even during the Saulide period and then quotes a divine oracle granting him the kingship: "you shall be shepherd of my people Israel, and you shall be prince (*nagid*, נָגִיד) over Israel."

The acclamation is structured as a **three-stage covenant**. First, the tribes come and speak the kinship formula (5:1-2). Second, the elders of Israel come "to the king" — the title is now used — at Hebron (5:3a). Third, David **cuts a covenant with them before YHWH** at Hebron, and they anoint him king over Israel (5:3b). The triple progression — speech, formal delegation, covenant-and-anointing — is a deliberately staged accession ceremony with constitutional implications. P. Kyle McCarter (Anchor Bible) and Walter Brueggemann both note the dual-authority structure: David rules by divine election (the oracle of 5:2) *and* by elder consent (the covenant of 5:3). The same dual structure appears in 2 Kings 11:17 (Joash) and arguably underwrites the prophetic critique of any king who treats his throne as unmediated by either party. The Iron Age I-II monarchical institution that the chapter is establishing is not absolutist; it is constitutional in form, however much its substance trended otherwise.

The reign formula at 5:4-5 is the standard dtr regnal summary: David was **thirty years old** at accession and reigned **forty years** total (a schematic generation-number; cf. Saul, Solomon, the wilderness period). Of those forty, **seven and a half years at Hebron over Judah** and **thirty-three years at Jerusalem over all Israel and Judah**. The chronological precision is one of the markers of the dtr historical work. The Hebron period archaeology has been investigated at **Tel Rumeida** (P. Hammond, Avi Ofer, Emanuel Eisenberg excavations); Iron Age IIA Hebron was a modest fortified town whose role as David's first capital is plausible but historically irrecoverable in detail.

**Jerusalem** in 5:6-9 is the chapter's central event. The pre-Davidic city was a small Jebusite stronghold occupying the **southeastern ridge** between the Kidron Valley and the Tyropoean Valley — the area called the **City of David**, perhaps 4-5 hectares in size. Excavations under Charles Warren (1867), Kathleen Kenyon (1961-67), Yigal Shiloh (1978-85), and especially **Ronny Reich and Eli Shukron** (1995-2010) have substantially clarified the pre-Davidic and early Israelite city. The water system was the city's lifeline: the **Gihon Spring** in the Kidron Valley provided water that the Jebusites had channeled and protected with massive Middle Bronze Age fortifications (the so-called "Spring Tower," redated by Reich-Shukron). The **Warren's Shaft system** was originally interpreted as a vertical climb that David's men could have used to enter the city; the Reich-Shukron redating shows that the vertical shaft was a natural karstic feature that may not have functioned as an access route in the Iron Age — the actual water-access route was a horizontal tunnel system. The implication: the **tsinnor** (צִנּוֹר) of 5:8 — the obscure word that has been translated as "water shaft," "windpipe," "trident," "grappling hook" — cannot be confidently identified with Warren's Shaft. The Hebrew word occurs only here and in Psalm 42:7 (where it means "channels" or "waterfalls"); etymological proposals abound. The most likely meaning is some component of the Jebusite water system that David's men exploited to gain entry, but the precise referent is lost. The text's own opacity should be honored.

The **"blind and lame"** taunt (5:6, 5:8) has generated extensive interpretive controversy. The Jebusites' boast that "the blind and the lame will turn you away" is read variously as: a magical-apotropaic ritual involving disabled persons stationed on the walls (Roland de Vaux, Yadin); a metaphorical boast that even disabled defenders could repel David; a legal-curse formulation invoking the prior agreement between Abraham and the locals (some rabbinic readings); or simply a contemptuous brag (Brueggemann, McCarter). David's answering line — "whoever strikes the Jebusites, let him get up the *tsinnor* to attack the lame and the blind, who are hated by David's soul" — and the etiological gloss "therefore it is said, 'the blind and the lame shall not come into the house'" (5:8b) has troubled commentators since antiquity. **Targum Jonathan** softens the line; rabbinic tradition (Bereshit Rabbah 38) reads it as referring to ritually disqualified priests; the contemporary disability-studies critique (Saul Olyan, Jeremy Schipper) reads it honestly as a problematic exclusion text whose interpretive afterlife in Levitical priesthood prohibitions (Lev 21:18) we cannot afford to elide. The text is doing political-symbolic work that produces real exclusionary effects later. Engage rather than smooth.

**Hiram of Tyre's** delegation (5:11) is the chapter's first signal of the international Phoenician trade relationship that will become central under Solomon (1 Kings 5). Cedars of Lebanon, Phoenician carpenters and masons — David is moving Jerusalem from a Jebusite stronghold to a royal capital with imported skilled labor and prestige timber. The Phoenician archaeological record at Sarepta, Tyre, and Sidon documents the metallurgical and architectural sophistication that David is importing.

The **Tel Dan Stele** (discovered 1993 by Avraham Biran's team at Tel Dan; Aramaic inscription dated paleographically to the late 9th c. BCE) is the most important extra-biblical witness to this chapter's central claim. The fragmentary stele, set up by Hazael of Damascus or his son, refers to **bytdwd** (בית דוד) — "the House of David" — as the polity ruling the southern kingdom. This is the first and only attestation of the Davidic dynasty by name in any extra-biblical inscription. Its discovery substantially shifted the maximalist-minimalist debate in Israelite historiography. Thomas Thompson, Niels Peter Lemche, and Philip Davies (the Copenhagen school) had argued that David might be largely or wholly fictional; the Tel Dan Stele established that within roughly a century of David's putative reign, his dynasty was named in inscriptional sources from a hostile neighboring kingdom. William Dever, Amihai Mazar, and William Schniedewind have developed a middle position: a historical David existed, ruled a small Iron Age IIA polity, and the biblical narrative magnifies and theologizes him.

A few Hebrew terms transform the chapter. ***Nagid*** (נָגִיד), translated "prince" or "leader" or "ruler-designate," is the term used in the divine oracle at 5:2. *Nagid* and *melekh* (king) are not synonymous in Samuel; *nagid* is the more theological term for the divinely designated leader (used of Saul at 1 Sam 9:16, 10:1; of David at 1 Sam 13:14, 25:30). Some scholars (Halpern, McCarter, Mettinger) propose that *nagid* originally designated the divinely-elected leader and *melekh* the institutional king; David is now both. ***Karat berit*** (כָּרַת בְּרִית), "to cut a covenant," is the standard Hebrew idiom for treaty-making (Genesis 15, Exodus 24, Joshua 24). The Hebron covenant of 5:3 is genuine treaty-form, not mere acclamation. ***Millo*** (מִלּוֹא), the place David built around in 5:9, is one of the chapter's archaeological puzzles. The word means "filling" and likely refers to a stepped terrace fill structure on the eastern slope of the City of David ridge — possibly the **Stepped Stone Structure** excavated by Kenyon and reinvestigated by Eilat Mazar and others. ***Tsinnor*** (צִנּוֹר), the water-shaft word, has been discussed above. ***Baal-perazim*** (בַּעַל פְּרָצִים), "lord of breakings-through," is the etiological place-name for David's first Philistine victory at 5:20 — *peretz* (פֶּרֶץ) wordplay; YHWH "broke through" enemies like a flood.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Ruth 5 is structured as five movements: the all-Israel acclamation (5:1-5), the conquest of Jerusalem (5:6-10), the Tyrian recognition and growth (5:11-12), the family expansion at Jerusalem (5:13-16), and the two Philistine battles (5:17-25). The five units form a deliberate progression from political legitimation to military consolidation, with the Jerusalem capture as the structural pivot.

The chapter is the **conclusion of the History of David's Rise** (Rost's proposed source running from 1 Sam 16 to 2 Sam 5). The source's plot architecture has been driving toward the moment when David is acclaimed king of all Israel and consolidates his throne in a new capital. McCarter's Anchor Bible commentary structures the source so that the chapter functions as its terminal cadence — every narrative thread that has been advancing since Samuel anointed David at Bethlehem now resolves into the kingship at Jerusalem. What follows in chapters 6-7 (the ark and the Davidic covenant) belongs to a different source-arc. Robert Polzin in *David and the Deuteronomist* reads the chapter as the dtr's deliberately consolidated arrival point.

Within the chapter, the literary craft is most concentrated in the **conquest of Jerusalem** (5:6-10). The Jebusite taunt about the blind and the lame, David's responding command, and the etiological gloss form a tight ironic cycle. The Jebusites use disability as boast; David inverts the boast into curse; the city falls anyway; the resulting saying becomes a marker of post-conquest exclusion. The narrator's terseness — six verses for the capture of what becomes the political-religious capital of Israel — is itself a literary signal. The narrator does not narrate the assault. The water-shaft strategy is named obliquely, the actual breach is unnarrated, and the next thing we hear is "David lived in the stronghold and called it the city of David." The contrast with the extended Goliath narrative (1 Sam 17) or the long Ammonite war narrative (2 Sam 10-12) is striking. Robert Alter (*The David Story*) reads the terseness as literary signal that the narrator wants Jerusalem's status established without dwelling on the violence. Brueggemann reads it as theological understatement: the city falls because YHWH has "established David king over Israel" (5:12), not because of any tactical brilliance worth narrating.

The two Philistine battles in 5:17-25 are paired in deliberate parallel: Philistines spread in the Valley of Rephaim, David inquires of YHWH, YHWH commands and David obeys. But the parallelism breaks at the second engagement: instead of the same divine command, YHWH gives a different tactic — "do not go up; go around to their rear and come opposite the balsam trees, and when you hear the sound of marching in the tops of the balsam trees, then rouse yourself, for then YHWH has gone out before you to strike down the army of the Philistines." The contrast teaches a lesson about divine guidance: even when the situation appears identical, the instruction may differ. Inquire each time. The "sound of marching in the tops of the balsam trees" is one of the most evocative theophany images in the historical books — divine warriors moving through the trees ahead of David, audible but unseen. Patrick Miller (*The Divine Warrior in Early Israel*) and Frank Cross's *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* place this image in the broader divine-warrior tradition (cf. Judges 5, Habakkuk 3, Psalm 68).

The textual evidence for the chapter is generally stable. **4QSam-a** preserves fragments of the chapter consistent with MT. The LXX has minor smoothings; the LXX of 5:8 ("blind and lame" line) reorders the syntax somewhat. The most significant textual issue is at 5:21, where MT reads "they left their idols (*atsabim*) there, and David and his men carried them away" while the parallel at 1 Chronicles 14:12 reads that David ordered them burned. The Chronicler's harmonization with Deuteronomy 7:5 (idols must be burned) is one of the clearer cases of Chronistic theological revision of Samuel.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, 2 Samuel 5 is the founding moment of the institutional polity that will dominate the rest of the historical books and serve as the political-theological backdrop for the prophets, the psalms, and the New Testament's Davidic-messianic claims. Jerusalem becomes Israel's capital here; the ark will arrive in chapter 6; the Davidic covenant in chapter 7; the Temple under Solomon in 1 Kings. The arc that runs from 2 Samuel 5 through the destruction of Jerusalem in 2 Kings 25 — and on through the prophetic literature, the post-exilic restoration, and the New Testament's claim that Jesus is "Son of David" — begins here.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Josephus in *Antiquities* 7.61-77 retells the chapter at length, smoothing the blind-and-lame difficulty and amplifying the architectural achievement. The Talmud (Yoma 22b, Yebamot 78b-79a) treats the Gibeonite-blind-and-lame question through midrashic harmonization with the Joshua 9 covenant; Pesachim 87a discusses the Jerusalem accession as fulfillment of divine promise. **Rashi** (11th c.) glosses the chapter philologically and follows the rabbinic harmonization on the blind-and-lame line. **Radak** (David Kimhi, 12th c.) gives extended attention to the *tsinnor* problem and the Jebusite taunt. **Ralbag** (Gersonides) extracts moral-political lessons (*to'aliyot*) about the proper accession of a king through tribal consent rather than mere conquest. **Abarbanel** (15th c.) develops the chapter as a foundational text in his political theory: the dual authorization (divine election plus tribal covenant) is the model of legitimate kingship, and any king who lacks either lacks half his title. **Yair Zakovitch** in modern Jewish scholarship attends to the intertextual play with the patriarchal narratives (David's "bone and flesh" echo of Adam and Laban). **Tikva Frymer-Kensky** notes the absence of women's voices in the accession narrative even as the chapter ends with David taking "more concubines and wives" in Jerusalem.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic Christianity read the Jerusalem conquest typologically: the city of God is captured by David and prefigures the heavenly Jerusalem won by Christ. **Origen's** homiletic fragments develop this. **Augustine** in *City of God* 17 treats the Jerusalem founding as a major moment in the *civitas Dei*'s historical embodiment. **Jerome** notes the etymology of Jerusalem (often glossed as "city of peace") and the irony of its conquest by violence. **Ambrose** in *De Officiis* uses the dual-authorization (divine and tribal) as a model of legitimate ecclesial authority. **Chrysostom**'s homilies on 2 Samuel develop the theme of YHWH's hand making David great. **Gregory the Great** in the *Moralia* uses the Philistine campaigns at the chapter's end as figures of the spiritual life's recurring battles. The medieval *Glossa Ordinaria* compiles patristic readings; **Bernard of Clairvaux** develops Jerusalem as figure of contemplative ascent.

The Reformation shifted register. **Calvin's sermons on 2 Samuel** treat the chapter as foundational for political theology: the covenant David cuts with the elders at Hebron is constitutional in form and rebuts both anarchic populism and absolutist monarchy. Luther's lectures attend to the typological reading of Jerusalem as figure of the church. The post-Reformation Pietist and Puritan tradition (Matthew Henry) develops the moral and devotional readings.

The critical period (Wellhausen, Noth, Gunkel) treated the chapter as evidence for the political consolidation of the early monarchy and the emergence of the dual Israelite-Judahite kingdom. The Rost (1926) framework placed it as the conclusion of the History of David's Rise. The discovery of the **Tel Dan Stele** in 1993 transformed the historiographical landscape; **Israel Finkelstein** and **Neil Asher Silberman** in *The Bible Unearthed* offer a minimalist-leaning archaeological reading of the chapter (small-scale Iron IIA Jerusalem, modest Davidic polity), while **Amihai Mazar**, **Eilat Mazar**, and **William Dever** offer more maximalist readings (substantial Davidic Jerusalem, supported by the Stepped Stone Structure and Large Stone Structure). **William Schniedewind** in *How the Bible Became a Book* offers a middle position. **Baruch Halpern's *David's Secret Demons*** reads the chapter politically: the dual-authorization formula is constitutional theater that legitimates a polity built by force. **McCarter's** Anchor Bible commentary develops the apologetic reading of the Jerusalem conquest. **Brueggemann** holds the constitutional and the apologetic readings in productive tension. The contemporary turn includes feminist readings (Frymer-Kensky on the chapter's silence about women and its closing note about concubines), disability-studies readings (Saul Olyan, Jeremy Schipper on the "blind and lame" exclusion), and postcolonial readings (Musa Dube on the Jebusite displacement) — all of which press questions the older traditions often passed over.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Both traditions read the chapter as foundational for Davidic-messianic theology — Jewish in the form of expectation of a Davidic restoration, Christian in the claim that Jesus is the heir of the Davidic line. They diverge on what to do with the Jerusalem conquest's exclusionary aftermath. Christian typological readings have often spiritualized away the violence; Jewish readings have often harmonized the blind-and-lame line through Levitical analogy. The contemporary turn — disability studies, postcolonial, feminist — has pressed both traditions to engage what the chapter's historical and political reality actually entailed for the displaced and excluded.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author believe God was doing in this chapter? The theological argument operates at two registers. The surface register attributes David's success directly to YHWH: "David became greater and greater, for YHWH the God of hosts was with him" (5:10), and "David knew that YHWH had established him king over Israel and exalted his kingdom for the sake of his people Israel" (5:12). The chapter ends with two military victories that YHWH directly authorizes and tactically directs. The narrator's surface theology is a classical providential theology of divine election, divine guidance, and divine victory.

The deeper register is more difficult. The chapter establishes a polity through violence (the Jerusalem conquest), through political negotiation (the Hebron covenant), through international alliance (Hiram of Tyre), and through dynastic expansion (more concubines and wives). The divine action is invoked but not narrated; the human action is narrated in detail. The same providential theology that operates in the chapter could equally be invoked of any successful Iron Age polity. The theological claim that this particular consolidation is YHWH's work is exactly the kind of claim that requires the Davidic covenant of chapter 7 to underwrite it. The chapter is doing theological work that depends on the canonical sequel to be fully intelligible.

**Tension One: How should we read the chapter's exclusion of "the blind and the lame"?** The text's etiological note that "the blind and the lame shall not come into the house" (5:8b) creates a real interpretive crisis. It can be read as a Jebusite taunt that David inverted, as a curse that became proverbial, or as the founding text for the Levitical priesthood prohibition (Lev 21:18) that excluded disabled persons from cultic service.

The traditional Jewish reading (Talmud Yebamot 78b-79a, Rashi, Radak) harmonizes the line with the Joshua 9 Gibeonite covenant: the blind and lame Jebusites were specifically those who tried to enforce the covenantal ban; the saying refers to them in particular, not to all disabled persons. The harmonization preserves the text from a sweeping disability-exclusion reading.

The patristic Christian reading often spiritualizes: the blind and lame are figures of unbelievers excluded from the church, or of unrepentant sinners. The medieval reading developed the spiritualization. Aquinas treats the chapter sparingly and does not engage the disability-exclusion question in depth.

The contemporary disability-studies critique (Saul Olyan in *Disability in the Hebrew Bible*, Jeremy Schipper in *Disability Studies and the Hebrew Bible*) presses the text honestly. The Levitical priesthood prohibition (Lev 21:16-23) excluded those with various physical disabilities from the most sacred priestly functions; the etiological note in 2 Samuel 5:8 and the cultic exclusion in Leviticus form a textual cluster that has shaped Jewish and Christian attitudes toward disability for millennia. The disability-studies reading does not dissolve the text but asks readers to engage rather than evade.

The position to steelman is the rabbinic harmonization. It is sometimes treated as evasive, but it does honor a feature of the text that disability-studies readings sometimes minimize: the Jebusite taunt is the explicit context. The blind and the lame in 5:6 are weapons in Jebusite mockery, and David's hatred is directed at those who used disability as boast against him. The harmonization preserves the text from a sweeping reading that the immediate context does not require, even if the cultic afterlife of the saying did broader exclusionary work.

**Where this leaves us:** The text is doing both — it preserves a specific etiology rooted in the Jerusalem conquest and it provides a textual hook that later cultic exclusion drew on. Honest reading holds both, and refuses to let the rabbinic harmonization dissolve the historical effects or let the disability-studies critique flatten the immediate context.

**Tension Two: Constitutional kingship or apologetic theater?** The Hebron covenant of 5:3 presents David's kingship as authorized by both divine oracle and tribal consent. The chapter is the earliest Israelite witness to a constitutional theory of monarchy in which the king is bound by covenant to the people he rules.

The Catholic and Reformed traditions both read the dual authorization as theologically and politically significant. Aquinas develops the principle that the king rules under law and covenant; Calvin makes the Hebron covenant a foundational text for resistance theory (the king is bound, the people may resist a covenant-breaker). Theodore Beza's *Right of Magistrates*, the Huguenot *Vindiciae contra Tyrannos*, and the Scottish covenanter tradition all draw on the dual-authorization model. **Abarbanel** in the Jewish tradition reads it similarly: the Hebron covenant is constitutional, and any king who lacks either divine mandate or popular ratification is illegitimate.

The contemporary apologetic reading (Halpern, McKenzie) reads the dual authorization as political theater. The History of David's Rise needs to legitimate David's accession against contemporary accusations that he conquered the throne; the formal three-stage acclamation is the narrative form that legitimacy requires. The covenant is real but politically motivated; the divine oracle is the apologetic's necessary supplement.

A mediating reading (Brueggemann, Polzin) holds both: the constitutional form is real, the apologetic motivation is real, both are doing work. To read the covenant only as constitutional theory or only as apologetic theater is to miss what the chapter is doing.

The position to steelman is the apologetic reading. It is sometimes resisted by traditional readers because it treats the surface theology as politically loaded. But the apologetic reading takes seriously a structural feature of the History of David's Rise: the source consistently legitimates David's path to the throne against accusations the contemporary audience would have made. The dual-authorization formula is exactly the kind of legitimation a contested accession needs. To read the formula as constitutional theory without noticing its apologetic function is to miss half the text's work.

**Where this leaves us:** The Hebron covenant is doing constitutional and apologetic work simultaneously. The constitutional legacy in later Jewish and Christian political theology is real; the apologetic motivation in the History of David's Rise is real. The chapter holds both without resolving which is primary.

The unresolved question this chapter leaves us with: When the founding text of a polity is also the apologetic that legitimates how the polity was founded, what does it mean to receive the founding text as constitutional inheritance — and what would be involved in receiving it without absorbing the apologetic uncritically?

## The Thread

**Joshua 15:63 and Judges 1:21.** Both texts note that Judah and Benjamin failed to drive out the Jebusites from Jerusalem in the conquest period; the Jebusites remained "to this day." David's capture of the city in 2 Samuel 5 is the long-deferred completion of the conquest. When you read those notices in Joshua and Judges, remember that the city remains Jebusite for two centuries after the formal conquest — and that David's capture is finally what changes the demographic.

**1 Chronicles 11:1-9.** The Chronicler's parallel account of the Jerusalem conquest names **Joab** as the first to climb the *tsinnor* and rewards him with the commandership ("Joab son of Zeruiah went up first, so he became chief"). The Samuel narrator omits this detail; the Chronicler adds it. The variant exemplifies the different politics of the two histories: Samuel keeps Joab in the wings while Chronicles foregrounds his role in the founding. When you read Chronicles, notice what each historian decides to credit Joab with.

**2 Chronicles 3:1.** The Chronicler identifies the Temple Mount as **Mount Moriah** — the same site as Abraham's near-sacrifice of Isaac in Genesis 22 — and locates Solomon's Temple on the **threshing floor of Ornan/Araunah the Jebusite**, the site David purchases at the very end of 2 Samuel (24:18-25). The Jerusalem captured in 2 Samuel 5 becomes the site of the Temple by the end of 2 Samuel and of the patriarchal binding by Chronicler reading. When you read 2 Chronicles 3:1, the city David captures here is being placed in a long sacred geography that runs from Abraham through David through Solomon.

**Matthew 1:1-17 and Luke 1:32-33.** The genealogy of Jesus traces him as "Son of David" through this chapter's establishment of the Davidic dynasty. The angel's announcement to Mary — "the Lord God will give to him the throne of his father David, and he will reign over the house of Jacob forever" — quotes the dynastic promise that begins in 2 Samuel 5 and is formalized in 2 Samuel 7. When you read the gospel infancy narratives, the throne being promised is the throne established at Hebron in 5:3.
