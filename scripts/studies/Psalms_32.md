# Psalm 32 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

The superscription reads "A *maskil* of David" (*le-David maskil*, לְדָוִד מַשְׂכִּיל). This is the **first occurrence of the term *maskil* in the Psalter** — it will recur thirteen times (Pss 32, 42, 44, 45, 52, 53, 54, 55, 74, 78, 88, 89, 142). The noun derives from the *hiphil* of *sakal* (שָׂכַל), "to be wise/instruct/give understanding." Its precise sense is debated — possibly "instructive psalm," possibly "skillfully crafted song," possibly a musical-technical term whose original meaning was already lost by the Septuagint period (the LXX translates with *synesis*, "understanding"). What is clear is that the redactor wanted readers to approach this poem in **didactic** posture: this is teaching, not just lament.

Form-critically, Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 32 as a hybrid: a *Danklied des Einzelnen* (**individual thanksgiving**) with strong **wisdom** and **didactic** elements. The opening *ashrei* formula (אַשְׁרֵי, "blessed/happy is...") signals a wisdom incipit familiar from Pss 1:1, 41:1, 112:1, and the wisdom literature broadly. Gunkel called Ps 32 a "wisdom-shaped thanksgiving" — the speaker is not just praising God for forgiveness but **instructing the congregation** in the dynamics of confession. By v. 8, the speaker has shifted into outright pedagogy ("I will instruct you and teach you").

Psalm 32 is the **second of the seven traditional Penitential Psalms** in the Christian tradition (Pss 6, 32, 38, 51, 102, 130, 143). The grouping was formalized by Cassiodorus in the sixth century (*Expositio Psalmorum*) and became liturgically central in the Western church — recited on Ash Wednesday, in Lenten observances, and traditionally on Fridays. Of these seven, Pss 32 and 51 are the two great explicit confession psalms; Ps 32 is the one that *theorizes* the experience while Ps 51 *enacts* it.

The Hebrew vocabulary cluster is the heart of the psalm. Three classic terms for sin appear in vv. 1–2:

- *pesha* (פֶּשַׁע, "transgression"): rebellion, willful breach of relationship — the Akkadian cognate is used of vassal kings in revolt.
- *chataah* (חֲטָאָה, "sin"): the standard term, etymologically "missing the mark."
- *avon* (עָוֹן, "iniquity"): twisted, crooked, perverse — guilt and its consequence together.

Three matching verbs of forgiveness:

- *nasa* (נָשׂוּי, "lifted/borne away," v. 1) — the priest "bears" sin in atonement (Lev 16:22).
- *kasah* (כְּסוּי, "covered," v. 1) — concealed from sight, like blood covered with dust.
- *lo-yachshov* (לֹא־יַחְשֹׁב, "does not count/reckon," v. 2) — a bookkeeping verb, the same root Paul uses in Romans 4 (*logizomai*).

The opening line in Hebrew is rhythmic and famous: *ashrei nesui-pesha kesui chataah* (אַשְׁרֵי נְשׂוּי־פֶּשַׁע כְּסוּי חֲטָאָה). The double *ashrei* (vv. 1, 2) is a wisdom signature.

The psalm is well attested in the **Dead Sea Scrolls** (4QPs-a, 4QPs-q, and others) without significant variation from the MT. The *Septuagint* (where it is numbered Ps 31) renders *ashrei nesui-pesha* as *makarioi hōn aphethēsan hai anomiai* — "blessed are those whose lawless deeds have been forgiven" — and this is the form Paul will quote in Romans 4:7–8.

Three *Selah* notations punctuate the psalm (vv. 4, 5, 7), making it one of the more heavily marked psalms for liturgical pause. The placement matters: *Selah* falls after the description of unconfessed sin's bodily toll (v. 4), after the moment of forgiveness (v. 5), and after the confession of God as hiding place (v. 7). The pauses mark the major theological beats.

ANE parallels include Mesopotamian "lifted-hand" prayers and Hittite confessional texts (Mursili II's plague prayers), but those texts confess ritual breach to identify and remove a curse. What is distinctive in Psalm 32 is the **interiority** — the toll of *unconfessed* sin on the body and the cure that comes through *spoken* confession to a covenant God who already knows.

## Lens 2: The Literature

The poem is a tightly built six-strophe structure built on a personal narrative arc:

- **Strophe 1 (vv. 1–2):** The wisdom incipit — double *ashrei*, the threefold sin/forgiveness vocabulary as theological frame.
- **Strophe 2 (vv. 3–4):** The pre-confession state — silence, bodily wasting, God's heavy hand. *Selah*.
- **Strophe 3 (v. 5):** The turn — confession in three parallel lines, immediate forgiveness. *Selah*.
- **Strophe 4 (vv. 6–7):** Generalization — call to all the godly, God as hiding place. *Selah*.
- **Strophe 5 (vv. 8–9):** The pedagogical turn — instruction to others, warning against stubbornness.
- **Strophe 6 (vv. 10–11):** Closing wisdom contrast and communal exhortation to joy.

Lowth's parallelism is dense throughout. Synonymous in v. 1 ("transgression is forgiven // sin is covered"), synthetic in v. 5 ("I acknowledged my sin // I did not cover my iniquity // I said, I will confess // and you forgave"), antithetic in v. 10 ("Many are the sorrows of the wicked // but steadfast love surrounds the one who trusts").

Several literary effects deserve notice. The verb *kasah* ("cover") appears twice with deliberate irony: in v. 1, sin that *God* covers is blessed; in v. 5, sin that *the speaker* tries to cover by silence is destructive. The cover that heals is the cover that comes from outside the self. Patrick Miller calls this "the irony of concealment."

The image of God as **hiding place** (*seter*, סֵתֶר, v. 7) plays on the same root: where sin once needed to be hidden by the speaker, now the speaker himself is hidden by God. The transition is from cover-up to cover-over, from concealment to refuge.

In v. 4, the phrase "my strength was dried up as by the heat of summer" uses the rare word *charvonei kayitz* (חַרְבֹנֵי קָיִץ) — the parched dryness of high summer. The body becomes a wadi in August. The image is physical and brutal.

The horse-and-mule metaphor in v. 9 (*sus-ufered ke'ein havin*, סוּס־וּפֶרֶד כְּאֵין הָבִין) is the only animal imagery in the psalm and lands with a thud after the personal confession — a deliberate turn from the speaker's "I confessed" to the listener's potential stubbornness. Don't be the donkey, the speaker says; learn from someone who already broke.

The psalm has no acrostic but does have an inclusio of joy: from "blessed" (*ashrei*, v. 1) to "rejoice and shout for joy" (*simchu...harnimu*, v. 11). The frame holds the dark middle.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**In the canonical Psalter.** Psalm 32 is bracketed by laments (Pss 31, 35) but functions as a thanksgiving and wisdom interlude. Hossfeld and Zenger treat it as part of the Pss 25–34 sub-collection oriented around refuge and trust, with Ps 32 supplying the **didactic core** — the explanation of how the trust-language of the surrounding psalms actually gets enacted in a sinner's life. Pss 32 and 51 are deliberately separated in the Psalter (they are the two great confession psalms) but read together they form the canon's complete account of confession — Ps 51 is the prayer, Ps 32 is the testimony afterward.

**The Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* takes the psalm's *maskil* designation as evidence that David wrote it for the **instruction of penitents**, and reads vv. 3–4 as David's own description of the period between his sin with Bathsheba and Nathan's confrontation. The Talmud (*Berakhot* 7a) cites the psalm in discussions of repentance. Rashi follows the Bathsheba reading. Ibn Ezra is more cautious about the historical anchor but agrees the psalm is fundamentally about *teshuvah*. Radak treats it as a teaching psalm for any penitent. Nachmanides connects the threefold sin vocabulary (*pesha*, *chataah*, *avon*) to the threefold structure of *teshuvah* — recognition, confession, return.

In modern Jewish thought, Martin Buber on the dialogical self reads vv. 3–5 as the I-Thou breakthrough — silence is monological, confession is dialogical. Abraham Joshua Heschel on prophetic confession treats Ps 32 as a model of how speech transforms guilt into relationship. Jon Levenson on the persistence of evil reads the psalm's quick resolution carefully, noting that the speaker does not deny the *consequences* of sin even as he celebrates forgiveness. Nahum Sarna emphasizes the psalm's wisdom-tradition framing. Avivah Zornberg on confession sees the psalm as performing the very *teshuvah* it describes — the act of speaking the poem is itself the confession. The psalm is recited in the Yom Kippur liturgy and in Ashkenazi *selichot*.

**The Christian trajectory.** Augustine famously identified Psalm 32 as **his own deathbed psalm**. According to Possidius's *Vita Augustini* (the contemporary biography), Augustine in his final illness had the psalm written out on parchment and pinned to the wall opposite his sickbed, and read it weeping in his last days. His *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Ps 32 is one of his great pieces — the sermon on the threefold sin vocabulary and the threefold forgiveness vocabulary became foundational for Western confessional theology.

Athanasius lists Ps 32 among the psalms to pray "when you have sinned and turn to repentance." Cassiodorus formalizes its place as the second of the seven **Penitential Psalms**. Aquinas in his Psalms commentary takes vv. 1–2 as the proof-text for the doctrine of justification, citing Paul's use in Rom 4 directly. Bonaventure mystically reads the *hiding place* of v. 7 as the wounded side of Christ.

Luther's exposition of Psalm 32 in his *Operationes in Psalmos* (1519–1521) — written as the indulgence controversy and the doctrine of justification by faith were crystallizing — treats the psalm as **the Pauline psalm**. Luther reads *the Lord counts no iniquity* (v. 2) through Romans 4 and makes Ps 32 a centerpiece of the Reformation's case against works-righteousness. The *simul iustus et peccator* formula — simultaneously just and sinner — is grounded partly here: the believer is "blessed" not because sin is absent but because God does not count it.

Calvin's 1557 Psalms commentary reads Ps 32 with strong Pauline accent, taking the psalm as didactic teaching on the dynamics of free grace. Calvin emphasizes that v. 5's confession is **response** to grace, not cause of it — the confession does not earn forgiveness but receives it.

Among modern critical interpreters: Gunkel and Begrich (thanksgiving with wisdom shape), Mowinckel (cultic confession liturgy), Westermann (declarative thanksgiving), Hans-Joachim Kraus (wisdom-thanksgiving hybrid, post-exilic in present form), Hossfeld and Zenger (didactic confessional psalm with deliberate threefold-vocabulary structure), John Goldingay (wisdom thanksgiving cautious about over-reading Pauline categories), Marvin Tate, Gordon Wenham. Walter Brueggemann puts Ps 32 in the "psalms of new orientation" — the speaker has been through the dark and emerged with hard-won theological understanding. Patrick D. Miller emphasizes the **bodily** dimension of unconfessed sin. William P. Brown attends to the metaphor cluster of cover/hide. Beth Tanner reads it pastorally for congregational confession.

**NT use.** Romans 4:6–8 quotes Ps 32:1–2 alongside Genesis 15:6 in Paul's argument for **justification by faith apart from works**. Paul's logic: just as Abraham was reckoned righteous before circumcision and apart from works of Torah, so David in Ps 32 describes blessing as the state of the one *to whom God does not count iniquity* — a person whose status before God is determined not by their own deeds but by God's reckoning. This is one of the load-bearing exegetical moves in Pauline soteriology and the Reformation tradition.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — Confession, Counting, and the Pauline Reading

**Tension 1: The Pauline-Reformation reading vs. the covenantal-confessional reading.** Romans 4 has so dominated Christian reception of Ps 32 that for many readers the psalm *is* a proof-text for justification by imputation. But the Israelite poem is a thanksgiving for forgiveness *that happened in response to confession*, in the context of an ongoing covenant relationship. The psalm assumes a real moral history (silence → confession → release), not a simple legal transaction.

- **Position A (Pauline-Reformation):** Augustine, Aquinas (in the Pauline mode), Luther, Calvin, classical Reformed and Lutheran orthodoxy. The psalm teaches *imputation* — God's not-counting (*lo-yachshov*) of iniquity is the same divine reckoning Paul develops in Rom 4. The blessing is forensic. Steelman: Paul, under the inspiration of the Spirit, identifies the deepest grammar of the psalm — the verb *chashav* (חָשַׁב) is bookkeeping language, and Paul reads it correctly as accounting language. The psalm's quick movement from silence to release is intelligible only if the release is an act of divine reckoning, not a moral process the speaker performs.
- **Position B (Covenantal-confessional / Catholic sacramental):** Cassiodorus, the medieval penitential tradition, Catholic sacramental theology, and the New Perspective on Paul (E. P. Sanders, *Paul and Palestinian Judaism*; James D. G. Dunn; N. T. Wright on justification). The psalm is about real confession in a real covenant, and Paul's use of it in Rom 4 is making an argument about *Gentile inclusion* in covenant blessing rather than about a contrast between faith and moral effort per se. Steelman: the psalm itself shows that forgiveness comes after the speaker *speaks* (v. 5) — there is a creaturely act of confession that the psalm refuses to skip. The Reformation reading is theologically powerful but partially abstracts from the psalm's own narrative grammar of speaking, being heard, being released.
- **Position C (Both/and, with Augustine and Wright):** Augustine himself (when read carefully) holds that confession is response to grace, not cause of it, but he also insists confession *must be made*. N. T. Wright (in *Justification* and *Paul and the Faithfulness of God*) argues that Rom 4's use of Ps 32 is about *covenant membership* defined by faith, which includes the confessional dynamic the psalm describes. Steelman: the psalm and Paul together teach that God's not-counting and the human confessing are not in competition — they are the two sides of the covenant relationship the psalm celebrates.

**Tension 2: The bodily wasting of vv. 3–4 vs. the rapid release of v. 5.** The psalm spends two verses on the brutal physical toll of unconfessed sin and one verse on the confession itself. Is the imbalance honest about the dynamics of repentance, or is it a poetic compression of what is in reality a longer psychological process? Augustine reads it as the genuine speed of grace once confession finally happens — the release is fast because God is fast. Modern psychological readings (Brueggemann, Tanner) note that the psalm preserves the slow buildup as a witness against quick-and-cheap confession. The literary form may be teaching that confession is fast but the *path* to confession is slow.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 32 is the canonical anatomy of the confessional moment. It tells the truth about silence — that hiding sin is a form of self-destruction, that the body knows what the mouth refuses to say, that God's hand is heavy on the unconfessed. And it tells the truth about release — that when the speaker finally says the words, the release is immediate, not earned but received, not a wage but a gift. Whether one reads the not-counting as forensic imputation or covenantal forgiveness, the psalm refuses two false alternatives: that confession is unnecessary (because grace) or that confession is meritorious (because work). The speaker confesses; God forgives; the speaker teaches. That is the order, and the Pauline reading and the Catholic sacramental reading both, at their best, hold it.

**Closing question:** What sin in your life right now is wasting your bones because you have not yet spoken it? And what would it cost — really cost — to break the silence?

## The Thread

- Psalm 32:1–2 is quoted in Romans 4:6–8, where Paul makes it (with Gen 15:6) the scriptural pillar of his argument for justification by faith apart from works of Torah.
- The threefold sin vocabulary (*pesha*, *chataah*, *avon*) recurs in the great atonement texts of the Hebrew Bible — Exod 34:6–7 (the divine self-revelation), Lev 16:21 (the Day of Atonement scapegoat), Isa 53:5–6 (the Suffering Servant who bears all three).
- *I will instruct you and teach you* (v. 8) makes the forgiven sinner into a teacher — the same dynamic at work in Luke 22:32 ("when you have turned again, strengthen your brothers") and 2 Cor 1:4 ("comfort those in any affliction with the comfort with which we ourselves are comforted").
- The psalm's status as Augustine's deathbed reading and as the second of the Seven Penitential Psalms made it foundational for the Western confessional tradition — and Luther's reading of it in 1519–1521 helped trigger the Reformation's recovery of imputed righteousness.
