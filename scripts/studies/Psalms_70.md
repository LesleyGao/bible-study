# Psalm 70 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 70 carries the brief, technical superscription *la-menatzeach le-David le-hazkir* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ לְדָוִד לְהַזְכִּיר, "to the choirmaster, of David, for the memorial offering / to bring to remembrance"). The unusual term *le-hazkir* — Hiphil infinitive of *zakhar* ("to remember, mention") — appears as a superscription only here and at Psalm 38:1. The form likely indicates a liturgical setting connected with the *azkarah* — the "memorial portion" of the grain offering described in Leviticus 2:2 and 24:7, the small handful burned on the altar to bring the worshipper "to remembrance" before God. Whether the *le-hazkir* signifies an actual cereal-offering accompaniment or is metaphorical for invoking divine remembrance, the term marks the psalm as a formal liturgical petition.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 70 as a *Klagelied des Einzelnen* (individual lament) — though it is the briefest such lament in the Psalter, just five verses. The psalm's defining textual feature is its character as a **doublet** of Psalm 40:13-17. The two passages are nearly identical, with a handful of minor variations: where Psalm 40 reads *YHWH* (the Tetragrammaton), Psalm 70 substitutes *Elohim* in three places, betraying the editorial hand of the Elohistic Psalter (Pss 42-83). Where Psalm 40:13 begins *retzeh YHWH le-hatzileni* ("be pleased, O LORD, to deliver me"), Psalm 70:1 opens with the more urgent *Elohim le-hatzileni chushah* ("O God, hasten to deliver me") — adding the urgency-verb *chushah* (חוּשָׁה, "make haste") at the very start.

The doublet phenomenon is one of the most important pieces of evidence for the editorial-redactional formation of the Psalter. Gerald Wilson's *Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* (1985) and David Mitchell's *The Message of the Psalter* both argue that doublets like Psalms 14/53, 40/70, and 108/57+60 reveal that the Psalter is a deliberately edited collection — psalms could be excerpted, recombined, and re-deployed for different liturgical-theological purposes. Psalm 70 is, on this reading, a liturgical excerpt of Psalm 40's closing petition, lifted and slightly modified to function as a stand-alone urgent prayer.

Hossfeld and Zenger, in their *Hermeneia* commentary, argue that Psalm 70 was deliberately positioned to introduce Psalm 71 — that the two psalms were paired as a single liturgical unit in the editorial design. Some Hebrew manuscripts and the Septuagint preserve traces of this pairing, with no superscription separating Psalm 71 from Psalm 70 in some witnesses.

The Hebrew opening *Elohim le-hatzileni, YHWH le-ezrati chushah* (אֱלֹהִים לְהַצִּילֵנִי יְהוָה לְעֶזְרָתִי חוּשָׁה, "O God, deliver me; O LORD, make haste to help me") deploys a chiastic intensifier: deity-name, deliverance-petition, deity-name, help-petition with urgency-verb at the climactic end. The verb *chushah* governs the whole psalm — it appears again at v. 5 in inclusio — making urgency the psalm's defining mood. This is the prayer of someone who cannot wait.

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve a fragmentary witness to Psalm 70 in 4QPs-a, with no major variants. The Septuagint follows the MT closely, preserving the Elohistic-substitution pattern. The Vulgate's rendering of v. 1 — *Deus, in adjutorium meum intende; Domine, ad adjuvandum me festina* — became the most-prayed line of Psalm 70 in Western Christian history, opening the Liturgy of the Hours every hour for over 1,500 years (see Lens 3).

## Lens 2: The Literature of the Text

Robert Lowth's parallelism categories appear concisely throughout. Synonymous parallelism dominates: v. 1 ("deliver me / help me"); v. 2 ("put to shame and confusion / turned back and brought to dishonor"); v. 4 ("seek you / love your salvation"); v. 5 ("you are my help / my deliverer"). Antithetic parallelism shapes the contrast between vv. 2-3 (the enemies' fate) and v. 4 (the seekers' fate). The brevity of the psalm sharpens its parallel structure — there is no fat, no narrative interlude, only paired urgent petition.

Strophically, the five verses divide cleanly:
- v. 1: Opening urgent petition (with *chushah*)
- vv. 2-3: Curse against enemies
- v. 4: Blessing on those who seek God
- v. 5: Closing urgent petition (with *chushah* echoed in *al te'achar*, "do not delay")

The inclusio is unmistakable: the psalm opens with "make haste" (*chushah*) in v. 1 and closes with the variant "do not delay" (*al te'achar*, אַל־תְּאַחַר) in v. 5. This urgency-frame is the formal signature of the psalm, making haste itself the petition's mood and content.

The chiastic structure is subtle but real:
- **A** (v. 1): Petition — make haste, deliver me
- **B** (vv. 2-3): Enemies — let them be ashamed
- **B'** (v. 4): Seekers — let them rejoice
- **A'** (v. 5): Petition — make haste, do not delay

The taunt "Aha, Aha!" in v. 3 (*he'ach he'ach*, הֶאָח הֶאָח) is an onomatopoeic Hebrew interjection of malicious satisfaction — appearing also in Psalm 35:21, 25 and Ezekiel 25:3, 26:2, 36:2. It is the sound of the enemy's gloating, captured in the very phonemes.

The psalm's central self-description — *va-ani ani ve-evyon* (וַאֲנִי עָנִי וְאֶבְיוֹן, "but I am poor and needy," v. 5) — uses the standard Psalter formula for the petitioner's standing before God: *ani* and *evyon* together describe the materially-and-existentially destitute, the *anawim* who form the recurring subject of Psalter prayer. This self-positioning is theologically constitutive: the prayer is heard *because* the petitioner stands among the *anawim*, not in spite of it.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Book II Placement.** Psalm 70 sits in the closing arc of Book II (Pss 42-72), within the Elohistic Psalter, two psalms before the Solomonic royal closure at Psalm 72. Its placement immediately before Psalm 71 — and the textual evidence that some MS traditions did not separate them — suggests the editor placed this urgent-doublet here as a liturgical bridge into the aged-voice lament of Psalm 71. Wilson's *Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* sees Books I-III as tracing the Davidic monarchy's trajectory; Psalm 70's brief urgent voice fits among the Davidic laments of Book II.

**Jewish Trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 70 reads the psalm of David in his various flights from enemies — Saul, Absalom, the Philistines. Rashi follows the Davidic-biographical reading. Ibn Ezra notes the doublet relationship to Psalm 40 and treats Psalm 70 as a liturgical excerpt for daily use. Radak emphasizes the urgency of the petition as a model for prayer in extremity. Nachmanides extends the psalm to Israel's collective experience of urgent need. The doublet phenomenon itself is treated in medieval Jewish commentary not as a textual problem but as evidence of David's deliberate liturgical craft — the same petition deployed twice for different settings.

In modern Jewish thought, the psalm's brevity and urgency have made it a prototype for the *piyyut* tradition (medieval Hebrew liturgical poetry) — short, urgent, formally constrained petitions. The psalm's *anawim* self-positioning has been important for Levenson's work on the spirituality of the destitute. The psalm appears in Jewish liturgy in various penitential contexts, particularly during *Selichot* (penitential prayer seasons before the High Holidays).

**Christian Trajectory.** The Christian liturgical reception of Psalm 70 is among the most distinctive in the Psalter. **Saint Benedict of Nursia**, in chapters 17-18 of his *Rule of Saint Benedict* (c. 540 CE), prescribed that every hour of the Divine Office should open with the Latin of Psalm 70:1 — *Deus, in adjutorium meum intende; Domine, ad adjuvandum me festina* ("O God, come to my assistance; O Lord, make haste to help me"). Benedict explicitly cited the psalm as the appropriate opening for monastic prayer because it positions the monk as a needy beggar, urgent for divine help, before any praise or petition begins. From the sixth century to the present, this single verse has opened every hour of the Divine Office in Western Christian monasticism — the most-prayed line of Psalm 70 in human history.

The same opening was preserved in the post-Vatican-II *Liturgia Horarum* (1971) and remains in use in the Anglican, Lutheran, and many Protestant breviary traditions. John Cassian, whose *Conferences* directly shaped Benedict, had earlier described Psalm 70:1 as the perfect short prayer — what Cassian called the *formula orationis*, a formula one could pray ceaselessly. Cassian's Conference 10 is one of the foundational texts of the Christian "short prayer" tradition that runs through the Jesus Prayer of Eastern Orthodoxy and into modern centering prayer.

Augustine's *Enarratio in Psalmum 69* (LXX numbering) reads Psalm 70 Christologically — Christ as the urgent petitioner, his Body the Church praying with him for deliverance. Athanasius treats the psalm as a model for prayer in distress. Cassiodorus and Aquinas follow the Augustinian lens. Luther's *Operationes* emphasizes the urgent-trust dimension — the psalm is brief because the petitioner cannot afford long words. Calvin (1557) handles the doublet relationship to Psalm 40 with characteristic philological honesty, suggesting that David adapted his earlier composition for fresh liturgical use.

Modern critical commentary — Gunkel, Mowinckel, Westermann, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate — has focused especially on the doublet phenomenon as evidence of Psalter-formation. Brueggemann places the psalm in his "disorientation" rubric. Patrick Miller and Beth Tanner have emphasized the urgency-formula and its liturgical legacy. The psalm's brevity has made it a frequent subject for studies of Hebrew prayer-form.

**NT Use.** Psalm 70 is not directly cited in the New Testament, but its urgency-formula echoes throughout the synoptic prayers — particularly Jesus' Gethsemane prayer ("Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me"). The "make haste" mode of prayer is the mode Jesus himself prays under extremity.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The doublet phenomenon and the question of Psalter formation.** Psalm 70 reproduces, with minor edits, the closing petition of Psalm 40. Three positions structure how to read this. The *single-author* position (much pre-critical and some traditional reading) holds that David composed both, deploying the same petition twice for different contexts. The doublet is intentional artistry. The *editorial-redaction* position (Wilson, Mitchell, Hossfeld-Zenger, most modern critical commentary) holds that the doublet is evidence of the Psalter's editorial formation — the closing of Psalm 40 was excerpted, modified for the Elohistic Psalter, and redeployed as Psalm 70 to introduce Psalm 71. The *liturgical-living-text* position (some recent canonical-criticism work, Brueggemann in places) treats the doublet as neither problem nor artifact but as the natural mode of liturgical text — psalms were prayed, re-prayed, adapted for new settings, and the canon preserves multiple deployments because each was used.

The strongest steelman for the editorial-redaction reading is empirical: the Elohistic-substitution pattern is too consistent to be coincidence, and the psalm's pairing with Psalm 71 in some MS traditions points to deliberate placement. The strongest steelman for the liturgical-living-text reading is theological: scripture is not less inspired for being shaped by use; the canon's character as a living liturgical document is part of its theological character. The honest assessment combines all three: David (or a Davidic poet) composed; an editor selected, modified, and redeployed; the Psalter we have is the product of generations of liturgical use.

**Tension 2: The monastic appropriation and the urgency-of-prayer theology.** The fact that Benedict's *Rule* prescribes Psalm 70:1 as the opening of every monastic hour for over 1,500 years constitutes one of the most sustained liturgical appropriations of any biblical verse. Three positions on its theological logic. The *neediness-as-default* position (the Cassian-Benedict tradition itself, retrieved by recent monastic theology) holds that the monk who begins each prayer with "make haste to help me" is positioned correctly before God: as needy, urgent, dependent. Prayer that begins from any other position is in danger of self-sufficiency. The *Reformed-protest* position (some Reformation-era critique, though more often of the *number* of monastic hours than of the opening verse itself) worries that fixed-formula prayer can become rote — that the urgency of "make haste" can become the mumbled background of unattended liturgy. The *integration* position (much recent ecumenical retrieval — Bonhoeffer's *Life Together*, modern Protestant breviary use, Catholic-Anglican liturgical reform) holds that the formula is healthy precisely because it positions the worshipper rightly, and the danger of routine is an inevitable risk of any sustained practice rather than a defect of the formula itself.

The contemporary application is strong. The *Deus in adjutorium* tradition is one of the few clearly continuous practices linking sixth-century monasticism, medieval Catholic-Eastern liturgy, Reformation-era Anglican daily office, modern post-Vatican-II Catholic prayer, and contemporary Protestant retrieval of the daily office. To pray Psalm 70:1 today is to pray with sixteen centuries of Christians.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 70 is not a great theological treatise. It is a hand thrust up out of deep water with one word: hurry. The psalm's smallness is the point. We do not always have the luxury of long prayer. When we do not, we have these five verses — and we have the company of every monk, every desperate Christian, and every aging believer who has prayed them for over 2,500 years.

**Closing question:** When you most need to pray, do you have anything shorter than Psalm 70 — and is shorter what you actually need?

## The Thread

The thread of Psalm 70 runs through Psalm 40:13-17 (its parent text), through Numbers 5:15 (the *azkarah* memorial-portion that gives the superscription its term), and through Mark 14:36 (Jesus' Gethsemane prayer in the same urgency-mode). Read these alongside the psalm and the through-line becomes clear: this is the prayer of a creature whose foothold has slipped, asking for the only thing that matters — that God hurry.
