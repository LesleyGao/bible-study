# Psalm 70 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 70 carries a compact superscription that compresses three layers of information about its origin and use: *la-menatzeach* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ, "to the choirmaster" or "for the director"), *le-David* (לְדָוִד, "of/for/concerning David"), and the unusual liturgical instruction *le-hazkir* (לְהַזְכִּיר, "for the memorial offering" or "to bring to remembrance"). This last term appears only twice in the Psalter — here and in the superscription of Psalm 38 — and links the psalm to the *azkarah* (אַזְכָּרָה), the portion of the grain offering burned on the altar to "remind" God of the worshipper (Lev 2:2, 9, 16; 24:7). The verb *zakar* (זָכַר) carries the specifically liturgical sense of cultic remembrance: a petitioner asks God to *zakar* him, to act on his behalf because his predicament has been brought before the divine throne. Mitchell Dahood (Anchor Bible, *Psalms II*) and Marvin Tate (Word Biblical Commentary) both read *le-hazkir* as a technical rubric tying the psalm to a moment within the offering ritual when the worshipper's case was actively pleaded.

The form-critical genre is unmistakable. Hermann Gunkel's foundational *Einleitung in die Psalmen* (1933) classified this as a *Klagelied des Einzelnen* — an individual lament — and modern form criticism has not seriously dissented. The components are textbook: invocation and cry for help (v. 1), petition against enemies (vv. 2-3), petition for the faithful (v. 4), and a closing self-description with renewed urgency (v. 5). What distinguishes Psalm 70 is that it contains almost nothing else. There is no extended description of suffering, no historical recital, no vow of praise. The psalm is stripped to lament's bone: *help, now*.

Dating is bound up with the most striking fact about the psalm: it is a near-verbatim doublet of Psalm 40:13-17. Compare the openings — Ps 40:13 reads *retzeh Yahweh le-hatzileni Yahweh le-ezrati chushah* ("be pleased, O Lord, to deliver me; O Lord, make haste to help me"), while Ps 70:1 reads *Elohim le-hatzileni Yahweh le-ezrati chushah* ("O God, deliver me; O Lord, make haste to help me"). The most consistent variation is the substitution of *Elohim* for *Yahweh* — a feature of what scholars since Friedrich Delitzsch have called the Elohistic Psalter (Pss 42-83), a sub-collection in which the divine name *Yahweh* has been systematically (though imperfectly) replaced by *Elohim*. Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger (Hermeneia, *Psalms 2*) argue that Psalm 70 represents an editorial reuse of Ps 40's lament-tail, lifted out and re-deployed as an independent prayer within the Davidic collection of Book II. This is the "doublet phenomenon" — the same liturgical material in different editorial settings, evidence that the Psalter was assembled from earlier collections that sometimes overlapped.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels run deep. Babylonian *šuilla* prayers ("hand-lifting" prayers) regularly open with imperatives to the deity to "hasten" and "incline" — compare the standard *qibâ qātka* and *šurriba ana ardīka* ("hasten to your servant") formulas in Akkadian penitential prayers. Egyptian execration prayers similarly invoke divine action against enemies. The psalm sits in this older liturgical stream while distinguishing itself by its monotheistic concentration: only one deity is addressed, and the petitioner's standing rests not on ritual technique but on covenant relationship — *ani ani v'evyon* (אֲנִי עָנִי וְאֶבְיוֹן, "I am poor and needy," v. 5).

The Hebrew text is well-preserved. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of the surrounding psalms in 4QPsa and 11QPsa, and while Ps 70 itself is fragmentary in the Qumran witnesses, the variants in 4QPsa for the doublet passage in Psalm 40 are minor and confirm the early circulation of both forms. The Septuagint (Ps 69 LXX, given the Greek numbering offset) translates faithfully, rendering *chushah* as *prosches* ("attend, hasten") and giving us the Latin Vulgate's *Deus, in adjutorium meum intende* ("O God, come to my assistance").

Book II of the Psalter (Pss 42-72) is, as noted, the heart of the Elohistic Psalter, and Psalm 70 sits near its end. Just two psalms later, an editorial colophon (72:20) declares "the prayers of David, son of Jesse, are ended" — a remark that retroactively frames Pss 42-72 as a Davidic collection moving toward a Solomonic conclusion. Psalm 70's compression and urgency, placed here on the threshold, function almost as a final cry before the royal psalm that closes the book.

## Lens 2: Literary Architecture

Robert Lowth's eighteenth-century discovery of Hebrew parallelism is the right starting point for reading Psalm 70's compact lines. Verse 1 is a textbook case of synonymous parallelism: *Elohim le-hatzileni* / *Yahweh le-ezrati chushah* — "God, deliver me / Yahweh, hasten to help me." The verbs *natzal* (rescue) and *azar* (help) intensify each other; the imperative *chushah* ("hasten") is held back to the second line and lands with metrical force. Verses 2-3 unfold in three carefully balanced bicola of synonymous parallelism, each pairing a verb of dishonor with a verb of reversal: *yevoshu v'yachperu* ("be ashamed and confounded") / *yissogu achor v'yikalmu* ("be turned back and disgraced") / *yashuvu al-ekev boshtam* ("turn back because of their shame"). The mockers' taunt *he'ach he'ach* (הֶאָח הֶאָח, "Aha! Aha!") closes the strophe with an onomatopoeic jolt — the same scornful exclamation appears in Ps 35:21, Ezek 25:3, and 26:2, always on enemy lips.

Verse 4 turns to antithetical parallelism with the petition's other audience: *yasisu v'yismechu* ("rejoice and be glad") in those who seek God, paired against the shame of those who seek the psalmist's life. The juxtaposition is editorially deliberate — the two groups (*mevakshei nafshi*, "those seeking my life," v. 2; *mevakshei-cha*, "those seeking you," v. 4) are defined by the object of their seeking. The closing acclamation *yigdal Elohim* (יִגְדַּל אֱלֹהִים, "God is great") — note the *Elohim* substitution here, where Ps 40 reads *yigdal Yahweh* — is liturgical climax language; cognate Semitic religious vocabulary stands behind the Arabic *Allahu akbar*.

Verse 5 is the inclusio's closing bracket. It returns to the first-person voice with *va'ani ani v'evyon* ("but I am poor and needy") and reprises the imperative *chushah li* ("hasten to me") that opened the psalm. The repetition is structural: the psalm begins and ends with *chushah*. James Kugel (*The Idea of Biblical Poetry*) would note that the second line of v. 5 escalates rather than merely echoes — *ezri u-mefalti atah* ("you are my help and my deliverer") and the closing *Yahweh al-te'achar* ("O Lord, do not delay") press the same petition with rising intensity.

The strophic structure is thus a five-verse chiasm of urgency:

- A — Cry for divine haste (v. 1)
- B — Petition against enemies (vv. 2-3)
- B' — Petition for the faithful (v. 4)
- A' — Cry for divine haste, intensified (v. 5)

The psalm has no *selah*, no extended praise section, no historical recital. The *qinah* meter (3+2 limping cadence) characteristic of lament is broken in places by the rapid 3+3 of imperatives. The text is built for liturgical reuse — short enough to be prayed hourly, structured tightly enough that no line is wasted.

## Lens 3: Within the Larger Story — Reception

The Jewish reception begins with the doublet's place in *Sefer Tehillim*. The rabbis of *Midrash Tehillim* (Buber edition) note the parallel with Psalm 40 and read the doubling as intensification: David's prayer is so urgent that it must be repeated, and the second repetition (Ps 70) presses past the praise framework of Ps 40 to bare urgency. *Rashi* (eleventh-century France) glosses *le-hazkir* by linking it to the *azkarah* offering and reads David as petitioning that God remember him *as if* he had brought a memorial offering — petition standing in for sacrifice. *Ibn Ezra* (twelfth century, Spain/France) is more philological, treating the psalm as a personal lament David repeated in extremity. *Radak* (David Kimhi) emphasizes the social setting of the *menatzeach* (choirmaster) and reads the psalm as one repeatedly used in the temple liturgy. *Ramban* (Nachmanides) connects the *le-hazkir* superscription to the corporate memory of Israel as a covenant people whose suffering itself functions as a memorial before God.

Modern Jewish scholarship continues this thread. Martin Buber's I-Thou framework finds in v. 1 a "primal address" that refuses any technique of prayer and rests entirely on direct relation. Abraham Joshua Heschel (*Man's Quest for God*) reads the psalm as an example of "the dignity of being needy" — the psalmist's confession of need is not weakness but the proper posture of the creature before the Creator. Jon Levenson and Nahum Sarna both situate it in the editorial development of the Psalter, with Sarna emphasizing the literary craftsmanship of the *Elohim* substitutions. Liturgically, the psalm has not entered standard Jewish daily prayer in the way some Davidic psalms have, but its themes echo throughout the *Avinu Malkenu* tradition and the High Holy Day petitions for divine haste in answering.

The Christian reception is dominated by one fact: the opening verse, in its Vulgate form *Deus, in adjutorium meum intende; Domine, ad adjuvandum me festina*, opens every hour of the Catholic Liturgy of the Hours and has done so for fifteen centuries. The provision is in *Regula* 18.1 of the Rule of St. Benedict (sixth century), where Benedict prescribes that every canonical hour (Lauds, Prime, Terce, Sext, None, Vespers, Compline, Vigils) open with this verse. John Cassian, in *Conferences* 10 (early fifth century), had already commended this verse as the perfect Christian short prayer — to be carried in the heart, prayed continuously, made the substance of *oratio iaculatoria* (the dart-prayer tradition that flowered into hesychasm and the Jesus Prayer). For the Catholic, Benedictine, and Orthodox traditions, this verse is the daily threshold of the worshipping life.

Augustine, in his *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on this psalm, treats it as the prayer of Christ in his members — the Body of Christ crying to its Head for haste. Athanasius lists Psalm 70 among prayers for use "when men intend evil against you." Cassiodorus (*Expositio Psalmorum*) reads it as the Church's perpetual prayer in pilgrimage. Aquinas (*In Psalmos*) follows Augustine: the psalm is *oratio Christi pro membris suis*. Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* (1519-1521) emphasizes the "naked faith" of the *pauper et inops sum ego* (poor and needy am I) as the posture in which the gospel meets the sinner. Calvin's 1557 commentary stresses that the doubling of Psalm 40 is not redundancy but the natural extremity of true distress, in which we cry the same words again and again.

Modern critical scholarship from Gunkel through Mowinckel (the cultic *Sitz im Leben*), Westermann (lament as foundational Israelite speech), Kraus (BKAT), Hossfeld-Zenger (the editorial Elohistic recension), Goldingay (treating Pss 70 and 71 as a paired unit; *BCOTWP*), Tate (WBC), and Wenham (*The Psalter Reclaimed*) has converged on reading Psalm 70 as a deliberately compressed lament whose function in the canonical Psalter is liturgical — a portable prayer for ongoing use rather than a one-time historical cry. Walter Brueggemann's *Spirituality of the Psalms* names this as the move from "orientation" to "disorientation": Psalm 70 is the disorientation cry stripped of all but its core. Patrick Miller (*They Cried to the Lord*) calls it "lament reduced to its essence." Beth Tanner (NICOT) and Bill Brown (*Seeing the Psalms*) note its place in the Psalter's editorial design just before the royal capstone of Psalm 72.

The New Testament does not directly cite Psalm 70, though the *kyrie eleison* tradition and the urgent prayers of Jesus in the Gospels (Mark 14:36; Matt 26:39) breathe the same air. The early church's liturgical use through Cassian and Benedict effectively absorbed the psalm into Christian worship at a depth few other psalms have achieved.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — What's at Stake

**Tension 1: The doublet phenomenon and what it reveals about the Psalter's formation.** Psalm 70 is, by any measurable standard, a near-replica of Ps 40:13-17. What does it mean that the canonical Hebrew Bible contains the same prayer twice in different editorial dress? *Position one — redactional reuse:* Hossfeld and Zenger argue this is straightforward editorial reuse — a later compiler took the lament-portion of Ps 40 (skipping its thanksgiving introduction) and slotted it into Book II as a stand-alone prayer, adjusting *Yahweh* to *Elohim* per Elohistic-Psalter convention. *Position two — earlier independent existence:* Some, like Goldingay, argue the lament may have circulated independently before being attached to the longer Ps 40 thanksgiving in Book I. *Position three — liturgical multiplicity:* John Goldingay also notes that ancient Israelite worship may simply have used the same prayer in multiple settings, and the doubled appearance in the canonical Psalter reflects that multiplicity rather than editorial laziness. Steelmanning each: the redactional view fits the Elohistic-Psalter evidence and the wider editorial signs of Book II; the early-independence view explains the slight variations that resist a clean copy hypothesis; the liturgical-multiplicity view honors how prayers actually function in worshipping communities. *Where this leaves us:* the doublet is best read as evidence that the Psalter is not a pristine collection of one-off compositions but the worship-book of a long-lived community. The same words were prayed in many settings, and the canonical text preserves that liturgical history. This is consonant with how Psalm 70 has actually functioned: not as a one-time historical cry but as a portable prayer for repeated use — Benedict's prayer, hourly, for fifteen centuries.

**Tension 2: The urgency-of-prayer theology vs. contemplative-stillness traditions.** Psalm 70 begins and ends with *chushah* — "hasten." The petitioner is not patient. The psalmist will not wait, and demands that God will not delay. This sits uneasily next to other strands of the biblical tradition that commend stillness: Ps 46:10 (*harpu u-de'u ki-anokhi Elohim*, "be still and know that I am God"); Isa 30:15 ("in quietness and trust shall be your strength"); Lamentations 3:26 ("it is good that one should wait quietly for the salvation of the Lord"). And it sits uneasily against the contemplative tradition — Cassian, the Cappadocians, Pseudo-Dionysius, the *Cloud of Unknowing*, the Centering Prayer movement — that has emphasized stilling the heart before God rather than pressing petition. *Position one — urgency as faith:* The lament tradition (Brueggemann, Miller, Westermann) reads urgency itself as an act of faith. To press God for haste is to take the covenant promise seriously enough to insist on its delivery. Silence in the face of suffering is theologically irresponsible; the psalmist's *chushah* is the proper response of the covenant partner. *Position two — stillness as deeper trust:* The contemplative tradition argues that maturity in prayer moves from petition to abiding presence; *chushah* belongs to the beginner, *be still* to the adept. *Position three — both/and (the Benedictine synthesis):* Benedict's monasticism, which prays *Deus, in adjutorium* eight times daily but in a settled rhythm, holds urgency and stillness together. The urgent cry is itself the entry into stillness; one prays *hasten* and then one rests. Steelmanning each: the urgency view honors the lament psalms' actual texture and resists a spiritualizing that mutes the cry of the suffering; the stillness view honors the deepening of prayer in maturity and the cosmic patience of God; the both/and view fits actual liturgical practice and honors the way real worshipping lives oscillate between them. *Where this leaves us:* Psalm 70 refuses to allow contemplation to become an alibi for not crying out. The psalm is on the side of the urgent. The contemplative tradition's wisdom is real, but it cannot displace the lament psalm's insistence that there are situations in which the only honest prayer is *do not delay*. The closing question is whether contemporary Christian worship — which has largely lost the lament tradition and moved its prayer life toward gratitude and presence — has thereby lost something the psalmist would have considered essential.

**Closing question:** If you had to pray one short prayer eight times a day for the rest of your life, would you choose this one — or would you reach for something gentler? And what does your answer reveal about what you believe is happening in your prayer life?

## The Thread

- **Psalm 38:1, 22** — the other *le-hazkir* psalm; the same urgency, the same memorial-offering frame, the same "make haste to help me, O Lord, my salvation."
- **Psalm 40:13-17** — the doublet source; reading the two together shows what is preserved when lament is stripped of its thanksgiving frame.
- **Lamentations 3:55-58** — "I called on your name, O Lord… you came near when I called on you; you said, 'Do not fear.'" The lament tradition's testimony that the urgent cry is heard.
- **Mark 14:36** — *Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me.* The Gospel's most urgent prayer carries Psalm 70's grammar of haste into the heart of the passion.
