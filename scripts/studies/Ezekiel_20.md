# Ezekiel 20: Israel's Apostasy in Long Retrospective and the Statutes That Were Not Good

## Lens 1 — Historical World

The chapter opens with one of Ezekiel's precise date formulas: *the seventh year, in the fifth month, on the tenth day of the month* (20:1). Reckoning from Jehoiachin's deportation in 597 BC, this is approximately **August 14, 591 BC** — about a year before the chapter 8–11 vision (September 592 in the canonical sequence) and about three years before Jerusalem's destruction. Ezekiel ben Buzi, priest at Tel-abib by the Chebar canal, is approached by a delegation of Judahite elders from the Babylonian *gōlâ* community who have come to *inquire of YHWH* (*lidrōš ʾet-YHWH*). The verb *dāraš* is technical: prophetic consultation, equivalent to consulting a recognized oracle. Ezekiel is now functioning as the *de facto* sanctioned channel to YHWH for the deportee community — a startling priestly-prophetic role for a man cut off from the Jerusalem Temple.

YHWH refuses the inquiry. Greenberg (Anchor 22A, 1983) reads this as the chapter's defining moment: the elders come expecting an oracle, and the prophet is given instead a long historical retrospective on Israel's apostasy. The refusal-to-be-inquired-of (vv. 3, 31) brackets the entire chapter, and what fills the bracket is the historical recital of Israel's fivefold rebellion: *in Egypt* (vv. 5–9), *in the wilderness, first generation* (vv. 10–17), *in the wilderness, second generation* (vv. 18–26), *in the land* (vv. 27–29), and *in the present* (vv. 30–31). Block (NICOT, 1997) treats this as Ezekiel's "anti-Pentateuch" — a shadow-history that retells the Heilsgeschichte through the lens of human rebellion rather than divine deliverance.

The chapter's chronological setting is significant. By 591 BC, Zedekiah's revolt against Babylon was being contemplated but had not yet begun (the revolt likely began c. 589). The Egyptian alliance temptation under Pharaoh Hophra was building. The elders' inquiry takes place in this volatile interval, and the chapter's repeated linkage of *Egypt* (vv. 5–9, 36) and *idolatry* and *covenant infidelity* is therefore not academic — it is current political theology, warning the deportees not to pin hopes on the Egyptian counterweight to Babylon that the homeland Judahite establishment is about to bet on.

The Al-Yahudu and Murashu archives (Pearce and Wunsch, CUSAS 28, 2014) document the elder-class structure of the Babylonian *gōlâ* directly: Judahite community leaders held administrative roles within the imperial system, and the chapter's *zĕqēnê yiśrāʾēl* (elders of Israel) corresponds to the kind of community council attested in those tablets. The historicity of the inquiry-event is therefore well-grounded.

The chapter's most disturbing single passage is **20:25–26**: *Moreover, I gave them statutes that were not good and rules by which they could not have life, and I defiled them through their very gifts in their offering up all their firstborn, that I might devastate them. I did it that they might know that I am the LORD.* The plain Hebrew reads YHWH as the *agent* of bad statutes — *ḥuqqîm lōʾ ṭôbîm ûmišpāṭîm lōʾ yiḥyû bāhem*. The grammar is unambiguous; the theological problem is acute. **Jon Levenson's *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son* (Yale, 1993)** treats this as the most direct biblical evidence that Yahwistic child sacrifice was a viable ancient practice — that Exodus 22:29's *the firstborn of your sons you shall give to me* originally meant exactly what it said, and that Ezekiel 20:25–26 is the OT's retrospective acknowledgment that this command, taken literally, was the *bad statute* God gave Israel as judicial hardening. The redirection at Genesis 22 (the binding of Isaac, the substitution of the ram) is, on Levenson's reading, the canonical move that reinterprets the original demand. The Topheth practice — denounced ferociously by Jeremiah (Jer 7:31, 19:5, 32:35) and 2 Kings 23:10's report of Josiah's destruction of the Topheth — is, in this reading, not a foreign Canaanite contamination but a Yahwistic practice the prophets repudiated. The Tophet at Carthage (Phoenician colonial child-sacrifice cemetery, archaeologically attested with thousands of urn-burials of infants) provides a Phoenician parallel that supports Levenson's broader thesis: the religious world from which Israel emerged practiced this, and the Bible's own most uncompromising voices know it.

The history of interpretation of 20:25–26 has been controversial. Rabbinic tradition (Sanhedrin 39a) softens the verse: *I gave them statutes that were not good* is read as YHWH allowing Israel to be bound by their own evil practices, or as YHWH giving them over to oppressive Gentile statutes after their rebellion. Rashi follows the Talmudic softening: the bad statutes are the laws of the nations under whom Israel will live in exile. Radak considers but does not endorse the literal reading. Ibn Ezra is more direct: the bad statutes are *what they perceived as commands from YHWH but were misreadings* — a proto-critical reading that anticipates modern interpretation.

Patristic-Christian tradition reads 20:25–26 as polemical hyperbole or as judicial hardening. Origen (*De Principiis* 3.1) and Jerome treat the bad statutes as divinely permitted consequences of human rebellion — God *gives over* the rebellious to the bad practices they have chosen, paralleled by Romans 1:24, 26, 28's threefold *paredōken* ("God gave them up"). Aquinas (*ST* I-II.79) develops this systematically: God permits evil but does not author it; the *bad statutes* are evils permitted under the divine economy of judicial hardening, not commands proceeding from divine goodness. Calvin's *Lectures on Ezekiel* preserves the Augustinian-Thomist reading: the bad statutes are God's *permissive* will operating to harden the rebellious, not his *preceptive* will commanding evil.

Modern critical scholarship (Greenberg, Levenson, Block, Zimmerli) takes the verse more literally. Greenberg reads it as an internal-Yahwistic acknowledgment that some of the laws received as Sinai-tradition were retrospectively recognized as inadequate or ruinous. Block reads it as judicial hardening but acknowledges the disturbing grammatical force. Levenson's reading is the most theologically uncompromising: the verse means what it says, and the canonical movement from Exodus 22:29 (firstborn given) through Genesis 22 (firstborn redeemed) through Ezekiel 20 (the bad statute acknowledged) to the prophetic-Christian reception (the bad statute repudiated) is the Bible's own internal critique of its earliest religious imagination.

Hebrew terminology is dense. *Ḥōq* (statute, vv. 11, 13, 16, 18, 19, 21, 24, 25), *mišpāṭ* (rule, vv. 11, 13, 16, 19, 21, 24, 25), *šābbāt* (Sabbath — Ezekiel's distinctive emphasis on Sabbath as covenant sign, vv. 12, 13, 16, 20, 21, 24), *gillûlîm* (idols, "dung-pellets," vv. 7, 8, 16, 18, 24, 31, 39), *šēm* (name, the *for-my-name's-sake* rationale that runs through the chapter, vv. 9, 14, 22, 39, 44), *bāmâ* (high place, v. 29, with the chapter's own folk-etymology *māh habbāmâ* "what is the high place?" → *bāmâ*). The *recognition formula* (*wîdaʿtem kî-ʾănî YHWH*) closes nearly every section (vv. 12, 20, 26, 38, 42, 44).

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter is one of Ezekiel's longest sustained discourses (49 verses in the MT, with the chapter division between 20 and 21 disputed; the LXX and many modern Bibles place 20:45–49 as 21:1–5). It is structured as a prophetic *Geschichtsentwurf* — a historical-theological retrospective — modeled formally on Deuteronomic-Levitical recitals (Deut 29, 32; Josh 24; 1 Sam 12; Neh 9; Ps 78, 105, 106) but inverted in tone. Where the Deuteronomic recital celebrates Israel's deliverance as the spine of identity, Ezekiel's recital catalogs Israel's serial rebellion as the spine of identity — the people that has rebelled in every place YHWH has placed them.

The chapter's structural signature is the threefold *Nevertheless I acted for the sake of my name* (vv. 9, 14, 22). Each cycle follows a pattern: Israel rebelled → YHWH considered destruction → YHWH withheld destruction *for my name's sake*. The *for-my-name's-sake* logic is Ezekiel's most distinctive theological move, anticipated by the chapter and developed across chapters 36 and 39. YHWH's faithfulness, in this theology, is rooted not in Israel's worthiness but in the integrity of the divine *šēm* — the divine reputation among the nations.

The leitwort is *mārâ* / *mōrēh* ("rebel, rebellious"), recurring across the chapter as the verb of Israel's relationship to YHWH. Israel rebels in Egypt, rebels in the wilderness, rebels in the wilderness (second generation), rebels in the land, and is in danger of rebelling in the present. The repetition is the argument: rebellion is not an episode but a structure.

The *Sabbath* emphasis is distinctively Ezekielian. Block notes that no other prophetic book elevates Sabbath observance to the same sign-of-covenant status that Ezekiel does. Verses 12, 13, 16, 20, 21, 24 form a Sabbath-thread through the recital. The choice is theologically precise: in exile, with no Temple and no land, the Sabbath becomes the portable sign of covenant identity. Ezekiel's priestly orientation (he is a priest before he is a prophet) reads Sabbath as the *axis mundi* of priestly-covenantal identity, and the chapter's repeated insistence that Sabbath-profanation was a *sign* of rebellion grounds the post-exilic Jewish elevation of Sabbath that runs through Nehemiah 13 and into rabbinic halakhah.

Textual variants are significant. The MT chapter division (20 ends at v. 49) differs from the LXX (which begins chapter 21 at MT 20:45). The Hebrew vv. 25–26 are textually well-preserved; the disturbing meaning is not a textual problem but an interpretive one. The LXX renders v. 25 as *kai egō edōka autois prostagmata ou kala kai dikaiōmata en hois ou zēsontai en autois* — an exact Greek rendering that does not soften the Hebrew. The Targum Jonathan paraphrases: *and I gave them over to their evil inclination, and they went and made for themselves statutes that were not good* — the Targum is the earliest attested softening of the verse, transferring agency from YHWH to Israel. The Hagigah restrictions do not apply to chapter 20.

The closing "parable" question (v. 49) is the chapter's literary self-reflection: *Ah, Lord GOD! They are saying of me, "Is he not a maker of parables?"* The prophet acknowledges that his own people receive his oracles as enigmatic riddles. The line is meta-prophetic — Ezekiel naming the difficulty of his own reception.

## Lens 3 — Larger Story

**Jewish reception** treats chapter 20 with great seriousness, especially vv. 25–26. The Talmudic discussion at b. Sanhedrin 39a addresses the verse directly: *Rabbi Eleazar said: When the Holy One gave statutes that were not good, He gave them in a manner that they could not live by them — namely, that they would be given over to the wicked nations.* The rabbinic move is to redirect the *bad statutes* away from any divine command toward a divine permission of Gentile oppression. This reading runs through medieval Jewish commentary (Rashi, Saadia Gaon) and into the modern period. Maimonides in *Hilkhot Avodah Zarah* (Laws of Idolatry) treats child sacrifice (Molekh-worship) as the paradigmatic forbidden practice, and his treatment of Ezek 20:26 is consistent with the Talmudic softening.

The chapter's *for-my-name's-sake* theology is foundational for Jewish *kiddush ha-Shem* (sanctification of the divine name) tradition. The post-Holocaust Jewish theological reflection (Emil Fackenheim, Eliezer Berkovits, Irving Greenberg) returns repeatedly to Ezekiel 20's logic: YHWH preserves the people not for their merit but for the integrity of his name among the nations. Fackenheim's *To Mend the World* (1982) treats this as the theological warrant for post-Shoah Jewish continuance.

The Sabbath theology of the chapter is canonical for Jewish liturgical practice. The *Lekha Dodi* hymn of the Sabbath-eve liturgy and the *Friday-night Kiddush* draw on Ezekiel's theology of Sabbath as covenant-sign.

**Christian reception** treats the chapter primarily as a recital of the divine economy of judgment-and-mercy that anticipates the gospel. Origen reads it allegorically: the rebellions in Egypt, wilderness, and land prefigure the human soul's serial rebellions, and the *for-my-name's-sake* logic anticipates the divine grace that saves not by merit but by the integrity of God's character. Jerome's *Commentariorum in Ezechielem* treats the chapter historically with allegorical extensions. Gregory the Great's *Homilies on Ezekiel* extends the *for-my-name's-sake* logic into pastoral theology: God's faithfulness in the moment of catastrophe rests on his own character, not on the worshipper's worthiness.

Calvin's *Lectures on Ezekiel* is exegetically meticulous on the chapter. Calvin reads vv. 25–26 as judicial hardening — God *gave them over* to bad statutes in the sense of permitting them to follow the ruinous practices they had already chosen. Calvin explicitly distinguishes God's *preceptive* will (what God commands) from God's *permissive* will (what God allows in judgment) and locates v. 25 firmly in the second category. This reading is decisive for the Reformed tradition's handling of the verse.

Modern critical Christian reception (Brevard Childs, *Biblical Theology of the Old and New Testaments* 1992; Walter Brueggemann, *Theology of the Old Testament* 1997) treats the chapter as a theologically demanding text whose force should not be softened. Childs reads Ezek 20 as the OT's most serious internal critique of its own religious history. Brueggemann reads it as an example of YHWH's *odd freedom* — a freedom that confounds standard divine-attribute frameworks.

The N. T. Wright tradition (*Jesus and the Victory of God* 1996; *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* 2013) reads Ezek 20:33's *I will be king over you* as a foundational text for the NT theology of the *kingdom of God*. The chapter's vision of restoration through judgment, exile, and royal divine reclaiming is, in Wright's reading, the OT template that Jesus's announcement of the kingdom directly invokes. The Lord's Prayer's *Your kingdom come* is, on this reading, the prayer that the Ezek 20:33 promise be realized.

**Where the readings diverge most sharply** is on vv. 25–26. The Talmudic softening (bad statutes = Gentile oppression) and the Augustinian-Calvinist judicial-hardening reading (bad statutes = God's permissive will) both protect divine impassible goodness. Levenson's reading (Yahwistic child sacrifice was real, and God's redirection through Genesis 22 and the prophets is the canonical correction) treats the verse with maximum theological honesty but at the cost of a more disturbing picture of Israel's religious history. Critical scholarship (Greenberg, Block, Zimmerli) lies between: the verse acknowledges that some of the practices Israel received as commands were retrospectively repudiated, but the implications for divine attribute theology are held open.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The bad-laws problem of 20:25–26.** What does it mean that Scripture itself records God-as-giver-of-bad-laws? The verse's plain Hebrew is *Moreover, I gave them statutes that were not good and rules by which they could not have life, and I defiled them through their very gifts in their offering up all their firstborn, that I might devastate them.* The plain reading: YHWH gave Israel bad statutes — *as judicial hardening*, in the language of Romans 1:24, 26, 28 (*paredōken autous*) — and these bad statutes included the practice of offering up the firstborn.

The five positions:
1. *Talmudic softening*: the bad statutes are the oppressive laws of the Gentile nations under whom Israel will live in exile; God did not give bad statutes, but gave Israel over to Gentile statutes. Sanhedrin 39a; Rashi; medieval Jewish commentary.
2. *Judicial hardening (Augustinian-Thomist-Calvinist)*: the verse expresses God's *permissive* will — God permitted Israel to follow ruinous practices as judgment, parallel to Romans 1:24, 26, 28. Aquinas *ST* I-II.79; Calvin *Lectures on Ezekiel*; Westminster Confession 5.4.
3. *Levenson's literal reading*: Yahwistic child sacrifice was real; Exodus 22:29 originally meant what it said; Ezekiel 20:25–26 is the OT's retrospective acknowledgment, and the Genesis 22 binding-of-Isaac is the canonical redirection. Levenson *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son* (1993).
4. *Progressive revelation*: divine pedagogy operates within human capacity at each stage; bad statutes were genuine commands at one stage, repudiated later as moral imagination matured. Origen's pedagogical reading; liberal Protestant developmental views (Charles Gore).
5. *Inerrantist redactional*: the verse must be read in context of the canonical witness against child sacrifice (Lev 18:21, 20:2–5; Deut 12:31; Jer 7:31, 19:5); the bad statutes are practices Israel adopted under surrounding nations' influence which YHWH let them follow as judgment. Block (NICOT); conservative evangelical commentary.

Steelman the least congenial. If drawn to Levenson, steelman judicial-hardening: the canonical witness against child sacrifice (Lev 18:21, 20:2–5; Deut 12:31; Jer 7:31, 19:5; Mic 6:7) is univocal, and *Ezekiel himself* repudiates the practice in the same chapter (20:31). If drawn to inerrantist readings, steelman Levenson: the canon's internal trajectory from Exod 22:29 through Gen 22 to the prophetic repudiation is itself the *resolution* of an earlier tension — and the something repudiated was Yahwistic, not foreign.

Where this leaves us: the verse forces a choice about how the canon's internal tensions are read. The choice has implications for biblical inerrancy, progressive revelation, and the ethical character of God. The choice cannot be evaded by translation; the Hebrew is unambiguous. What the reader does with the unambiguous Hebrew is the theological question.

**Tension 2: *I will be king over you* (20:33) and the kingdom of God.** The chapter's restoration promise begins with the divine-kingship oath: *As I live, declares the Lord GOD, surely with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm and with wrath poured out I will be king over you* (20:33). The phrase *ʾemlôk ʿălêkem* is one of the OT's clearest declarations of divine kingship, and it is invoked precisely at the moment of greatest apostasy — Israel saying *let us be like the nations* (20:32). The divine-kingship declaration is not predicated on Israel's worthiness; it is YHWH's response to Israel's most direct rejection of distinctive identity.

The five positions:
1. *Christian-Christological inaugurated eschatology*: the divine kingship promised in Ezek 20:33 is inaugurated in Jesus's announcement *the kingdom of God has come near* (Mk 1:15) and consummated at the Parousia. N. T. Wright *Jesus and the Victory of God*; G. E. Ladd *The Presence of the Future*.
2. *Reformed sovereignty*: divine kingship is timelessly real; the Ezek 20:33 declaration expresses what is always the case, with redemptive-historical realization. Calvin *Institutes* 2.15; Kuyper *Lectures on Calvinism*.
3. *Jewish malkhut shamayim*: divine kingship is the heart of Jewish liturgy (the *Shema*, the *Aleinu*, the *Malkhuyot* of Rosh Hashanah); the Ezek 20:33 promise is foundational for Jewish messianic-eschatological hope. Heschel *God in Search of Man*; Joseph Soloveitchik *The Lonely Man of Faith*.
4. *Liberation-political*: divine kingship is a counter-imperial claim; the *I will be king over you* declaration directly opposes the *like the nations* aspiration; the kingdom of God is the political alternative to Babylonian, Roman, and modern empire. Walter Brueggemann *The Prophetic Imagination*; Richard Horsley *Jesus and Empire*.
5. *Catholic kingship of Christ*: the divine kingship promised in Ezek 20:33 is realized in Christ's eternal kingship, celebrated liturgically in the feast of Christ the King; the kingdom is both already and not-yet; the social kingship of Christ has implications for political theology. Pius XI *Quas Primas* (1925); Henri de Lubac *The Splendor of the Church*.

Steelman the least congenial position. If you tend toward inaugurated eschatology, steelman the Jewish *malkhut shamayim* position: the kingship is liturgically present *now* in the worshipping community, and the over-emphasis on inauguration in Christ risks treating divine kingship as something that arrived only with the incarnation rather than something Israel always confessed. If you tend toward Jewish liturgical reading, steelman the Christological reading: the canon's own placement of Ezek 20:33 within a book that culminates in the chariot-throne vision of YHWH's return to the eschatological Temple (chapters 40–48) is itself an argument that the divine-kingship promise has an eschatological horizon, and the Christian reading of that horizon as Christological is in continuity with the canonical trajectory.

ONE unresolved question: When YHWH declares *I will be king over you* in the same chapter where he acknowledges giving statutes that were not good, what does it mean that the king who promises eschatological reign is the same one who has, by his own acknowledgment, hardened his people through bad laws? Is the eschatological kingship the *correction* of the earlier judicial hardening, or its *consummation*?

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references. First, **Romans 1:24, 26, 28** — the threefold *paredōken autous ho theos* ("God gave them up") — is Paul's most direct invocation of the Ezek 20:25–26 logic of judicial hardening, and the Pauline pattern of God-gives-over functions as the NT theological framework for reading the OT bad-statutes problem. The connection is rarely noted but is one of the strongest intertexts in the canon. Second, **Genesis 22's binding of Isaac** — Levenson's reading of the canonical redirection from firstborn-given to firstborn-redeemed; the Akedah is, on this reading, the OT's most direct response to the bad statute of Ezek 20:26, the moment where the divine demand for the firstborn is replaced by the divine provision of the substitute. Third, **Mark 1:15** — Jesus's announcement *the time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the gospel* — which is the most direct NT invocation of Ezek 20:33's *I will be king over you* combined with Ezek 18's call to repentance; the Markan opening recapitulates Ezekielian theology at its most concentrated.
