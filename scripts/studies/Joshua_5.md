# Joshua 5: Flint, Unleavened Bread, and the Drawn Sword

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 5 drops us onto the plains of Jericho (*ʿarvôt yĕrîḥô*) in the lower Jordan valley, roughly 250 meters below sea level, in the narrow band of arable ground where spring barley ripens weeks before it does in the central highlands. The political world assumed by the text is the southern Levant in the late second millennium: a patchwork of small city-states nominally tributary to Egypt, attested in the Amarna correspondence (EA 285-290, the Jerusalem letters of ʿAbdi-Ḫeba, mid-14th c. BCE). The Merneptah Stele (1208 BCE, Cairo Museum JE 31408) names "Israel" (*ysrỉꜣr*, determined as a people rather than a land) among Egypt's defeated enemies in Canaan, giving us the only firm terminus ante quem for a group called Israel in the region. "Amorites beyond the Jordan to the west" (5:1) here means highland peoples (contrast Gen 14's Mesopotamian Amorites); "Canaanites by the sea" are the coastal lowlanders, a geographic distribution that matches Egyptian usage in the Amarna age.

Authorship and dating follow the Deuteronomistic History problem. Noth's 1943 thesis that Deuteronomy–2 Kings is a single exilic work remains the frame. Cross's 1973 dual-redaction model locates a Josianic Dtr1 (c. 620 BCE) under an exilic Dtr2 (c. 550 BCE); most read Josh 5 as Dtr1 material with older cultic traditions embedded. Nelson's Hermeneia commentary treats the Gilgal circumcision etiology and the manna-cessation notice as independent pre-Dtr cult legends bound together to stage the conquest liturgically. The conquest-dating problem is sharp: high date c. 1406 BCE (1 Kgs 6:1's 480 years) places the event in Late Bronze IIA; low date c. 1200-1150 BCE places it at the LB/Iron I transition when the central hill country shows a settlement boom (Finkelstein's 1988 survey data).

The material culture fits either horizon. Flint knives (*ḥarvôt ṣūrîm*, 5:2-3) are archaic even for the LB; bronze and iron knives were ordinary. The text knows this and uses the archaism to evoke antiquity — Exod 4:25 (Zipporah) uses the same term. Circumcision was practiced widely in the Egyptian and Levantine sphere (Egyptian relief at Saqqara, Tomb of Ankh-ma-hor, 6th Dynasty; Jer 9:24-25 lists Egypt, Judah, Edom, Ammon, Moab as circumcising peoples) and conspicuously not by Philistines and most Mesopotamians — which is why Herodotus (*Histories* 2.104) thought the Syrians learned it from Egypt. The Passover-plus-new-grain sequence (5:10-12) assumes a functioning agricultural calendar in the Jordan valley in the month of Abib/Nisan.

Key terms:

- ***ḥerpat miṣrayim*** (חֶרְפַּת מִצְרַיִם, 5:9), "the reproach of Egypt." *Ḥerpâ* is social shame, not just insult; cognate with Akkadian *ḫerpu*. English "reproach" loses the bodily-communal weight. Whose reproach? Either the shame Egypt inflicted (slavery, uncircumcision of the wilderness generation) or the shame Egyptians would direct at a group claiming covenant status while uncut. Targum Jonathan reads the first; Rashi the second.

- ***Gilgāl*** (גִּלְגָּל, 5:9) with verb *gallôtî* (גַּלּוֹתִי, "I have rolled"). The root *gll* generates the noun *galgal* (wheel, rolling thing) and the place name. The wordplay is untranslatable in English. Gilgal has stood for Iron I stone-circle cult sites — Adam Zertal's "footprint" sites on Mt. Ebal and the Jordan valley (published 1980s-2000s) argued some of these are Joshua-era installations, contested by Israel Finkelstein and Aren Maeir.

- ***śar ṣĕbāʾ YHWH*** (שַׂר צְבָא־יְהוָה, 5:14), "commander of YHWH's host/army." *Ṣābāʾ* is a military muster; the title personalizes the cosmic army elsewhere summoned by "YHWH ṣĕbāʾôt." English "commander" is accurate but domesticates it; "marshal of the divine legions" would be closer.

- ***maṣṣôt*** and ***qālî*** (מַצּוֹת, קָלוּי, 5:11), "unleavened cakes and parched grain." *Qālî* is fresh barley ears roasted on embers — still eaten in the Levant as *freekeh* when harvested green. The detail fixes the month: barley ripens in the Jordan valley in Nisan/April.

- ***mûl šēnît*** (מוּל שֵׁנִית, 5:2), "circumcise a second time." LXX reads *ek deuterou* ("for the second time"), which generated midrashic speculation about a two-stage rite (cut plus *pĕriʿâ*, the later retraction); MT likely means "this generation in addition to the exodus generation."

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: etiological narrative embedded in conquest historiography, with a theophany (5:13-15) functioning as commissioning-vision. Robert Alter's narrative poetics apply: the chapter is built from three episodes (circumcision, Passover-manna, the man with the sword) joined by resumptive locative clauses (5:10 "encamped at Gilgal"; 5:13 "by Jericho"), which Trent Butler (Word Biblical Commentary) argues are editorial seams over originally independent traditions.

Literary devices:

The opening verse (5:1) mirrors 2:11 — "their hearts melted" — closing an inclusio around the Rahab-plus-Jordan-crossing unit (chapters 2-5) and setting up Jericho's collapse in chapter 6. The narrator reports enemy psychology without explaining how the narrator knows, a standard Hebrew omniscience convention. Verse 9's "today" (*hayyôm*) is one of Joshua's etiological formulas ("to this day," *ʿad hayyôm hazzeh*, appears 16x in the book) — Brevard Childs's 1963 *JBL* article argued these formulas are Deuteronomistic signatures; Burke Long (1968) refined this, distinguishing true etiologies from narrative tags.

The theophany scene (5:13-15) is deliberately written as an Exodus 3 echo. Both have a solitary leader confronted by a numinous figure; both feature the removal-of-sandals command with nearly identical phrasing (Exod 3:5: *šal-nĕʿālekā mēʿal raglêkā*; Josh 5:15: *šal-naʿalkā mēʿal raglekā*); both identify the ground as *ʾadmat-qōdeš*/*mĕqôm qōdeš* (holy ground/place). Yairah Amit reads this as intentional Joshua-as-second-Moses typology, programmatic for the book. The difference matters too: Moses is called *from* a bush; Joshua faces a drawn sword. The commissioner has changed uniforms.

The "Are you for us, or for our adversaries?" — "No" (5:13-14) exchange is a literary shock. Hebrew *lōʾ* ("no") is abrupt; LXX softens it (*οὐχί*, with the elaboration "but I am commander of the host"). The narrator refuses the binary Joshua offers. Nelson and L. Daniel Hawk (*Every Promise Fulfilled*, 1991) treat this as the chapter's theological hinge: the sacral-war tradition is not YHWH taking Israel's side but Israel being enlisted into YHWH's side, a distinction critical for any ethical reading.

Textual variants with meaning:

MT 5:2 has "circumcise the sons of Israel a second time" (*šēnît*); the Samaritan Pentateuch-aligned tradition and some LXX witnesses read differently, and 4QJoshᵃ, the Qumran manuscript Eugene Ulrich published in DJD 14 (1995), is fragmentary here but preserves elements that differ from MT in surrounding chapters. 4QJoshᵃ's famous feature — relocating Joshua's altar-building (MT 8:30-35) to immediately after the Jordan crossing, before Jericho — does not touch chapter 5 directly but reshapes its literary context. Emanuel Tov (*Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible*, 3rd ed.) takes 4QJoshᵃ as preserving an earlier edition; Nelson is more skeptical.

The LXX of Joshua runs about 5% shorter than MT overall. In chapter 5, LXX omits a phrase in 5:4-6 (the summary explanation of why circumcision was needed) that reads like an inner-biblical harmonization in MT. Lawrence Greenspoon's work on the Greek Joshua suggests the Hebrew Vorlage of LXX preserved an earlier form at several points, though not uniformly.

Location in book's argument: Chapter 5 is transitional. Chapters 1-4 move Israel across the Jordan; chapters 6-12 execute the conquest. Chapter 5 is the liturgical pause — covenant re-inscription (circumcision), covenant meal (Passover), and military commissioning (the commander) — without which the conquest narrative would be mere military report. Childs's canonical reading treats this as the deliberate theological framing of what follows: the wars are cultic, not political.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Canonically, Joshua 5 stands at the seam between the Pentateuchal wilderness and the land. Passover brackets the exodus: Exod 12 at departure, Josh 5 at arrival. Manna starts in Exod 16 and stops here (5:12). Circumcision as covenant sign goes back to Gen 17; the wilderness lapse is noted by the narrator without explanation. The chapter closes off Egypt and opens the land — theologically, this is arrival.

**Jewish trajectories:**

*Second Temple*: Qumran's 4Q378-379 (Apocryphon of Joshua, Carol Newsom, DJD 22) expands Joshua's role with psalm-like material and curse formulas against those who would rebuild Jericho. Philo allegorizes circumcision (*Special Laws* 1.1-11) as the excision of pleasure and pride. Josephus (*Ant.* 5.20-34) retells chapter 5 straightforwardly and soft-pedals the shock of the "No." Ben Sira 46:1-6 praises Joshua as "successor to Moses in prophecies."

*Rabbinic*: Yevamot 71b-72a asks why the wilderness generation was not circumcised and offers several answers: the wandering made it dangerous, and — R. Isaac's preferred reading — the north wind (*rûaḥ ṣĕpônît*) that would have healed wounds did not blow during the forty years. The preference for the north-wind explanation reads the suspension as divine providential withholding, not human failure. Bereshit Rabbah 46 informs later readings of Josh 5 as covenant-reconstitution. Mishnah Pesachim 9:5 distinguishes the Egyptian from the generational Passover; the Gilgal Passover generates halakhic discussion of circumcision as prerequisite for Passover participation (Exod 12:48).

*Medieval*: Rashi reads *ḥerpat miṣrayim* (5:9) as Egyptian mockery: "See the blood-star rising before them in the wilderness, a sign they will die there" (from Pirkei deRabbi Eliezer). Ibn Ezra, more sober, takes the reproach as shame of slavery and uncircumcision combined. Ramban stresses covenant renewal: entry into the land demands re-cutting of the covenant sign because the land is the covenant's material content. Radak identifies the commander as Michael. Abarbanel, writing after 1492, reads the drawn sword with new urgency. Maimonides (*Guide* 3.49) reads circumcision philosophically: it weakens lust and creates communal solidarity.

*Modern*: Martin Buber (*On the Bible*) reads the Jericho encounter as paradigmatic numinous address. Jon Levenson stresses the covenantal-cultic shape rather than the military. Moshe Weinfeld (*Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School*, 1972) situates Gilgal as a Dtr cult center retrojected into Joshua. James Kugel discusses the etiological layer. Avivah Zornberg reads circumcision as a covenant of "lack." Baruch Halpern (*The First Historians*, 1988) treats chapter 5 as historiographically credible cultic tradition. Rachel Havrelock (*River Jordan*, 2011) reads Gilgal as a borderland cult center whose theology is about boundary-crossing.

**Christian trajectories:**

*Patristic*: Origen's *Homilies on Joshua* 4-6 is the foundational Christian reading. The second circumcision typifies baptism; the stone/flint knife is Christ himself (citing 1 Cor 10:4); the manna's cessation signifies the letter yielding to the spirit; the commander with the drawn sword is explicitly Christ ("he who was then seen by Joshua is the same who now is seen by us"). This fuses with Justin Martyr's *Dialogue with Trypho* 61-62 (c. 160 CE), which identifies pre-incarnate Christ with the visible angel of YHWH. Jerome echoes this; Augustine (*City of God* 16.26-27) uses the Gilgal circumcision for baptismal regeneration; Theodoret's *Questions on Joshua* 7-9 resists over-allegorization.

*Medieval*: Aquinas (*Summa* III.70.2) cites Josh 5 in his discussion of circumcision as a sacrament of the Old Law superseded by baptism. The Glossa Ordinaria anthologizes Origen's allegorical readings and hardens them into a standard medieval typology: flint knife = Christ, unleavened cakes = sincerity of life, manna's cessation = law yielding to gospel.

*Reformation*: Luther's lectures on Joshua treat the second circumcision as prefiguring baptism's replacement of the fleshly sign but resists the wholesale allegorical method. Calvin (*Commentary on Joshua*, 1564) reads the chapter soberly: circumcision as covenant renewal, the commander as an angel (he rejects the full Christophany reading on the grounds that the text does not clearly demand it), the Jericho theophany as sacral-war confirmation. Calvin is theologically and exegetically cautious here, and his caution set the Reformed hermeneutical baseline. Menno Simons and the Anabaptists used the chapter to argue against infant baptism by distinguishing the cutting-of-flesh covenant from the voluntary adult covenant of the church.

*Critical*: Noth treats the chapter as etiological tradition aggregated in the Dtr frame. Von Rad (*Holy War in Ancient Israel*, 1958) reads the commander-of-the-host scene as classical sacral-war commissioning. Nelson's commentary (1997) is the standard critical treatment; Dozeman (Yale AB, 2015) reads the cult-historical layer as pre-monarchic; Butler (WBC, 2nd ed. 2014) stresses literary coherence over source division. Lawson Younger (*Ancient Conquest Accounts*, 1990) situates the ritual-military preparation within ANE conquest-account conventions.

*Contemporary*: Walter Brueggemann (*Divine Presence amid Violence*, 2009) wrestles with the juxtaposition of liturgy and war. Christopher Wright (*The God I Don't Understand*, 2008) uses 5:13-14 to argue the conquest is not God-taking-sides but God-having-his-own-side. John Goldingay reads the manna-cessation as ordinary-providence replacing extraordinary-providence. Richard Hess (*Joshua*, TOTC 1996), Gordon Wenham, and David Howard defend historicity cautiously. Terence Fretheim reads through divine vulnerability. Ellen Davis (*Opening Israel's Scriptures*, 2019) treats the chapter as theological-pedagogical. L. Daniel Hawk (*Joshua*, Berit Olam 2000) and Lori Rowlett (*Joshua and the Rhetoric of Violence*, 1996) apply rhetorical-critical methods. Robert Allen Warrior's 1989 essay "A Native American Perspective: Canaanites, Cowboys, and Indians" reads from the standpoint of the dispossessed; Musa Dube and Kwok Pui-lan extend the postcolonial critique.

**Convergence and divergence**: Both traditions read chapter 5 as covenant-reconstitution before conquest. Both take the theophany with theological seriousness. The major divergence: Christian tradition almost universally (Calvin excepted) reads the commander as pre-incarnate Christ; Jewish tradition reads him as Michael or another angel. A subtler divergence: Christian allegorization transposes circumcision into baptism and thus supersedes it; Jewish reading keeps circumcision central as ongoing covenant sign. Modern critical work on both sides has converged on literary-cultic readings, though postcolonial critique is more prominent in Christian-critical than in Jewish-critical scholarship, where the ethical questions take different shapes.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What is God doing? Two things simultaneously: reconstituting covenant identity through embodied rite, and refusing to be conscripted as a tribal deity. The drawn sword says "I am armed"; the "No" says "I am not yours." The liturgy (circumcision, Passover) and the theophany (the commander) are set back-to-back, and the reader is supposed to feel the join — the same God who heals the camp also arrives with a weapon.

**Tension 1: Covenant sign — continuity or supersession?**

- *Catholic (CCC 1150, 1964)*: The circumcision of the Old Covenant prefigures and is fulfilled in baptism; Josh 5's second circumcision typifies the renewal of grace in the sacramental economy. Trent (Session V) affirms that the ceremonial law has ceased but the prefigurative meaning endures.

- *Reformed*: Calvin and Westminster hold that circumcision and baptism are the same covenant, different administrations. Circumcision has ceased as rite; its substance (covenant of grace) continues in baptism, which is therefore proper to infants of believers. The Gilgal circumcision shows the covenant sign properly applied to the children of promise before they can act.

- *Lutheran*: Closer to Reformed on continuity of covenant substance; Luther's Small Catechism treats baptism as fulfilling the covenant sign without hardening the typology.

- *Wesleyan/Methodist*: Wesley read Josh 5 covenantally; Methodist tradition has historically been less polemical about the sign and more focused on means-of-grace theology.

- *Anabaptist*: Menno Simons and Schleitheim: the Gilgal circumcision is indeed covenant renewal, but that covenant was of flesh-and-land, and the new covenant is voluntary and spiritual. Infant baptism is thus a category error — circumcision was not voluntary; baptism must be.

- *Orthodox*: Circumcision prefigures baptism but also the "circumcision of the heart" (Rom 2:29; Col 2:11). Eastern tradition emphasizes that Jesus himself was circumcised (Luke 2:21, celebrated Jan 1), preserving continuity rather than pure supersession.

- *Jewish rabbinic*: *Bĕrît mîlâ* remains the covenant sign, not displaced. Josh 5 is exemplary covenant-maintenance, the wilderness lapse is an anomaly requiring explanation (Yevamot 71b), and entry to the land requires re-observance.

- *Critical*: The pre-Dtr circumcision traditions likely reflect cult-etiology for the Gilgal sanctuary; the Dtr editor weaves them into an identity-formation narrative for post-exilic Judah, for whom circumcision had become a boundary marker against Babylonian acculturation (Gen 17 is Priestly, late). The "second circumcision" phrase may reflect an inner-biblical harmonization.

*Steelman the Anabaptist*: The Anabaptist reading is the least intuitive to most modern Protestants, but it has the text's plain-sense on its side: the Gilgal circumcision is a rite applied to living persons after a period of conscious covenant-lapse, as a deliberate restoration. If the rite is read as straightforwardly as it presents itself — an act of embodied, communal, consequential re-entry into covenant — then its closest New Testament analogue is believer's baptism, not paedobaptism. The paedobaptist move requires a covenantal-structural argument that the text itself does not supply; the Anabaptist move requires only that the rite's form be read as formative.

*Where this leaves us*: The chapter refuses to settle the question. It says the sign was re-done before the land could be entered, and that the manna stopped when the produce began. Whether the sign has changed is a question the text doesn't know it's being asked.

**Tension 2: Whose side is God on? (the "No" of 5:14)**

- *Catholic (CCC 2302-2317 on just war; 839-845 on Israel)*: God's purposes encompass but are not reducible to any nation's project; the drawn sword belongs to God's justice, not Israel's interest. Israel's election does not collapse God's transcendence.

- *Reformed (post-Kuyper)*: Sphere sovereignty preserves the distinction; God is sovereign over all nations and chooses one for redemptive purpose without being that nation's partisan. Hawk's Reformed-inflected reading of "No" is representative.

- *Lutheran (two kingdoms)*: The God who appears with the sword operates in the kingdom of the left hand, which is God's own kingdom but not identified with any earthly regime. The commander's "No" is the two-kingdoms doctrine in narrative form.

- *Wesleyan*: Moral government theology — God's sword is always on the side of righteousness, and righteousness is not coextensive with any human army.

- *Anabaptist*: The sharpest reading. The commander's "No" is proto-pacifist: God will not be a partisan in human violence even when that violence is commanded. Yoder (*The Politics of Jesus*, 1972) and later John Nugent (*The Politics of Yahweh*, 2011) push this line hard, though not without internal debate about the chapter's continued endorsement of Israel's warfare.

- *Orthodox*: Divine providence is theandric; God works with and through human agency without being reducible to it. The scene is a warning against presumption.

- *Jewish rabbinic (Rashi, Sanhedrin 44b-related traditions)*: The angel rebukes Joshua for neglecting Torah study the night before battle — "you neglected the daily burnt offering and Torah; for which do you come?" The "No" becomes a pedagogical correction: God sides with covenant fidelity, not tribal success. This is remarkable; the rabbis use the scene to critique militarism.

- *Critical (Brueggemann, Rowlett, Warrior)*: The scene is the text's own internal protest against its ideology. Rowlett's rhetorical-critical reading: the "No" is a narrative safety valve that lets the text both command violence and question it. Warrior's postcolonial reading: the safety valve is inadequate; the narrative still ends with dispossession.

*Steelman the postcolonial critique*: It is easy to read the "No" as resolving the ethical problem — God is above the violence, therefore the violence is not compromising. But Warrior's point is that whatever the commander says in 5:14, the story continues in chapter 6 with the destruction of Jericho at God's command. The "No" does not prevent the *ḥerem*; it frames it. Ethical cleanliness requires not just a good disclaimer before the violence but a different outcome. The postcolonial reading holds that a God who says "No" and then endorses total war has not said "No" in any morally operative sense. This reading is unpopular because it is uncomfortable, not because it is weak.

*Where this leaves us*: The "No" is the best thing in the chapter and possibly in the book. It is also not enough to solve what comes next.

**An unresolved question**: If YHWH's army has its own side, and that side is neither Israel nor its adversaries, then who belongs to that army, and what does it mean that Israel is about to be told to do its work?

## The Thread

- **Exodus 3:1-6** — the burning bush. Joshua 5:13-15 is written in deliberate echo: sandals off, holy ground, solitary encounter. What shifts is the posture: Moses hides his face; Joshua falls to worship the commander. The leader has changed; the God has not, but the phase of the mission has — bush to sword.

- **Genesis 17:9-14** — the Abrahamic covenant of circumcision. Josh 5 re-enacts what Gen 17 institutes. That the wilderness generation had lapsed, and that the text does not explain why, suggests covenant is something that can be practically suspended without being theoretically abandoned — a harder doctrine than it first appears.

- **Hebrews 4:8-11** — "If Joshua had given them rest, God would not have spoken of another day." The author of Hebrews reads Joshua 5's arrival as incomplete; the land was given but the rest was not. Christian canonical reading tends to under-read this text; it is a significant qualifier on any triumphalist Joshua theology.

- **2 Kings 23:21-23** — Josiah's Passover, which the narrator says had not been kept "since the days of the judges who judged Israel." Some Dtr readings see this as a deliberate back-reference to Josh 5:10-11, the Gilgal Passover. If so, the Dtr framer is setting up Josiah as a second Joshua, entering a reconstituted land through a re-inscribed covenant — and the irony, from the exilic redactor's standpoint, is that it didn't last.
