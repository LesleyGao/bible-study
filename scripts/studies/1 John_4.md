# 1 John 4: The Test of Spirits and the Shape of Love

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The First Epistle of John is anonymous in the text itself. The voice that speaks is the same voice that introduces 2 and 3 John as "the elder" (*ho presbyteros*, ὁ πρεσβύτερος), and this self-designation has carried the burden of every authorship debate since the second century. The traditional attribution to John the Apostle in old age rests on Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1), Polycarp's allusions, and a passage in Eusebius (*Hist. Eccl.* 3.39.4) that preserves Papias's apparent distinction between *John the Apostle* and *John the Elder* — a distinction that has been pulled in opposite directions ever since. The critical consensus, advanced most influentially by Raymond Brown in *The Community of the Beloved Disciple* and his Anchor Bible commentary on the Johannine Epistles, treats the letter as the work of an anonymous community member writing in the wake of a painful schism (the *secessionists* of 2:19). Rudolf Bultmann's earlier source-critical reading, and Judith Lieu's *I, II, & III John* (New Testament Library) and *The Theology of the Johannine Epistles*, both situate the letter in Asia Minor, most likely Ephesus, in the 90s AD, written into a community fracture over Christology — the adversaries deny that Christ has come *en sarki* (ἐν σαρκί, "in the flesh," 4:2). Robert Yarbrough's *BECNT* commentary and Karen Jobes's *ZECNT* hold to traditional apostolic authorship while engaging the critical evidence carefully; I. Howard Marshall's *NICNT* commentary and John Stott's older *Tyndale* commentary likewise; C. H. Dodd's *Moffatt* commentary remains a touchstone for the literary reading.

Chapter 4 opens with the most explicit anti-Docetic confessional test in the New Testament. "Every spirit that confesses Jesus Christ has come *en sarki* is from God" (4:2). The phrase points directly into the early-Christological controversies that Irenaeus describes under the names of Cerinthus and the early Docetists (*Against Heresies* 1.26.1; Hippolytus *Refutatio* 7.33-35; Eusebius *Hist. Eccl.* 3.28). Cerinthus, on Irenaeus's account, taught that the heavenly *Christ* descended on the man Jesus at his baptism and departed before the crucifixion — a separation of the divine Christ from the human Jesus that the elder will challenge again in 5:6 ("not by the water only but by the water and the blood"). The Docetists more broadly held that Christ only *seemed* to take flesh. The elder's pneumatology in 4:1-6 is forensic: spirits are tested by their confession, and the test is the embodiedness of the incarnation. The "spirit of the antichrist" (*to tou antichristou*, 4:3) is a confessional pattern, not a person — a way of speaking and being in the world that refuses the scandal of the *en sarki*. The discernment-of-spirits idiom (*diakrisis pneumatōn*, cf. 1 Cor 12:10) becomes a long medieval-monastic tradition (*discernimentum spirituum*) developed by John Cassian, refined by Ignatius of Loyola in the *Spiritual Exercises*, and absorbed into Catholic spiritual direction as a stable practice.

From 4:7 the chapter turns to its great theme. *Agapē* (ἀγάπη) is used 27 times in the letter, 18 of those in chapters 4 and 5 — the densest concentration of love-vocabulary in the New Testament. Twice the elder writes *ho theos agapē estin* (ὁ θεὸς ἀγάπη ἐστίν, "God is love," 4:8 and 4:16), and these two clauses become the *locus classicus* for divine simplicity in classical theism — the claim that God's attributes are not parts but identical with God's essence (Augustine, *De Trinitate*; Aquinas, *Summa* I.13, I.20). Pope Benedict XVI's first encyclical, *Deus Caritas Est* (2005), takes 4:8 as its title. The propitiation language of 4:10 — *hilasmon peri tōn hamartiōn hēmōn* (ἱλασμὸν περὶ τῶν ἁμαρτιῶν ἡμῶν, "propitiation for our sins") — picks up the same word from 2:2 and locks the love-of-God into the cross. The verb *teleioō* (τελειόω) appears in the perfect-passive participle *agapē tetelei*ōtai (ἀγάπη τετελείωται, "love has been perfected," 4:12, 17, 18) — the perfective grammar matters: love is brought to its end, completed, in the abiding mutuality between God and the community. *Parrhēsia* (παρρησία, boldness or confidence, 4:17) is forensic-eschatological — confidence on the day of judgment. And *phobos* (φόβος, fear) and *agapē* are mutually exclusive in 4:18: "perfect love casts out fear, for fear has to do with punishment" — a verse Augustine, Bernard of Clairvaux, and John of the Cross developed into a whole theology of the *fear-of-the-Lord* / *love-of-God* progression, with John of the Cross's *dark night* the most rigorous treatment of the transition.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The letter's genre has resisted easy classification. It has no formal opening — no sender, recipient, or grace-greeting in the Pauline mold — and no formal closing. Bultmann proposed an underlying "Revelation Source" that the elder rewrote, a hypothesis no one quite accepts in its detail but which captured the document's layered, almost hymnic quality. Judith Lieu reads it as sustained meditation in lyric register, closer to a homily than a letter. Brown's term, "tractate," catches the same intuition — this is theological writing in epistolary clothing.

The literary structure of chapter 4 turns on two movements. The first (4:1-6) is forensic: a discernment-of-spirits oracle organized around the confessional test. The second (4:7-21) is expository, with *agapē* recurring as the structural drumbeat. The repetition is not redundancy. Throughout 1 John, four verbs and nouns — *agapē* (love), *zōē* (life), *koinōnia* (fellowship), *menein* (to abide, μένειν) — circle back in concentric patterns, and chapter 4 is the chapter where *agapē* and *menein* fuse: "whoever abides in love abides in God, and God abides in him" (4:16). The elder's prose is paratactic, almost antiphonal, and the rhythm carries the argument as much as the syntax does. C. H. Dodd called the style "spiral" — the letter circles back to its themes at greater depth each time, never quite progressing in linear fashion. Chapter 4 is the gravitational center of those spirals.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the New Testament canon, 1 John 4 is the densest love-theology and the sharpest anti-Docetic confession we have. It interprets the Gospel of John — the *en sarki* of 4:2 is the cousin of the *sarx egeneto* (σὰρξ ἐγένετο, "became flesh") of John 1:14, and the ethic of love-of-God-and-neighbor (4:20-21) is the explicit working-out of John 13:34's new commandment.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

The patristic reception of 1 John 4 begins with Polycarp's *Letter to the Philippians* 7, which quotes 4:2-3 against Docetism within a generation of the letter's likely composition. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.16.5) and Origen reach for it as a confessional plumb line; Cyril of Alexandria and Athanasius drew on the love-passages for Trinitarian and Christological argument. The most consequential patristic reading is Augustine's *Tractates on the First Epistle of John* — ten homilies preached during the Easter season — and especially Tractate VII, where the famous *dilige et quod vis fac* ("love, and do what you will") emerges from his exegesis of 4:8. The maxim is a commentary on what genuine charity entails, not a license: when love is the operative principle, the actions that flow from it are inherently shaped by God's own being. Augustine's *De Trinitate* makes equally extensive use of 4:8 and 4:16 in developing the Father/Son-as-Lover/Beloved/Love analogy that becomes the backbone of Western Trinitarian thought.

The medieval period builds upward from Augustine. Bernard of Clairvaux's *On Loving God* takes 1 John 4 as its scaffolding for the four degrees of love. Aquinas treats God's love systematically in *Summa* I.20-21 and the virtue of charity in II-II.23-46, with 1 John 4 the central biblical text. Catherine of Siena's *Dialogue* returns to the chapter constantly. Hildegard of Bingen and Bonaventure draw on it for affective theology, and John of the Cross, building on Teresa of Avila, develops 4:18's fear-and-love grammar into the most rigorous map of the soul's purification we have. The mystical tradition reads the chapter not as a sentimental love-text but as a diagnostic instrument.

The Reformation reception is more fraught. Luther seizes on the assurance theme — that the believer can know God's love and rest in it — and Calvin in *Institutes* III.2-3 develops the assurance of faith with 1 John 4 (and 5:13) as touchstones. Wesley's later treatment of *Christian perfection* leans on the *agapē tetelei*ōtai language of 4:12, 17, 18, though he is careful to insist (against caricature) that perfected love is not the absence of fault but the orientation of the affections. The triadic-witness verses of chapter 5 produced more controversy in the same period than chapter 4, but the love-passages were less contested.

The contemporary reception runs along several tracks. Brown's Anchor Bible commentary on 1 John (1982) is the standard critical reference; Lieu's *Theology of the Johannine Epistles* and her commentary are the most influential recent readings. Marshall (*NICNT*), Yarbrough (*BECNT*), Jobes (*ZECNT*), Stott (*Tyndale*), and Leon Morris all hold conservative-evangelical readings; Dodd, Bultmann, and Lieu represent the critical end. The Catholic *Catechism* incorporates 1 John 4 at §218-221 (God is love), §733-741 (the Spirit), §1604 (the love of spouses), and §1822-1829 (the virtue of charity). Benedict XVI's *Deus Caritas Est* is the most extended modern magisterial reading. In Eastern Orthodox theology — Vladimir Lossky, Sergei Bulgakov, John Zizioulas — 1 John 4 is the *theotic* charter: love is the divine *energeia* (energy) in which the creature participates without confusion, the basis for the essence-energies distinction's pastoral application.

### Jewish Scholarly Engagement

The proximate Jewish source for the chapter's love-theology is the Shema and the Holiness Code. Deuteronomy 6:4-9 commands love of God with whole heart, soul, and might; Leviticus 19:18 commands love of neighbor as oneself; Leviticus 19:34 extends that command to the resident alien. Jesus's summary of the Law in Mark 12:29-31 / Matthew 22:37-40 is the Christological hinge between the Hebrew *ahavah* tradition and the Johannine *agapē* tradition. Daniel Boyarin's *The Jewish Gospels* and Geza Vermes have argued that the early Jesus movement's love-theology is a recognizable inflection of Second Temple Jewish ethical reflection rather than a sharp departure. Adele Reinhartz's work on the Fourth Gospel raises the harder question of how the Johannine literature handles "the world" and "the Jews" — a problem that the elder's binary in 4:5-6 ("they are from the world… we are from God") inherits and that any honest reading must reckon with. Jewish-Christian dialogue around 1 John has tended to focus on the schism-language and the in-group / out-group framing, where the elder's pastoral situation produced rhetoric that, taken outside its original sectarian context, has been weaponized in later Christian history. Lawrence Schiffman's work on Second Temple boundary-markers is useful background.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What God is doing through this chapter, in the elder's own framing, is securing two things at once: the apostolic confession of the embodied Christ (4:2-3) and the love-shaped form of Christian life (4:7-21). The two are not separable. Truth and love, in the Johannine register, are two sides of one coin — and any spirituality that wants the love without the confession, or the confession without the love, has departed the apostolic witness.

### Tension 1: "God is love" — what kind of statement is this?

The clause *ho theos agapē estin* (4:8, 4:16) is grammatically simple and metaphysically vertiginous. The classical theist reading, articulated by Augustine in *De Trinitate* and developed by Aquinas (*Summa* I.13, I.20), takes the statement as an assertion of divine simplicity: in God, love is not a property added to essence but identical with essence. God does not *have* love; God *is* love. This is a hard saying. It implies that all of God's attributes are really one — that omniscience, omnipotence, justice, and love are not distinguishable parts but the one undivided divine essence considered under different aspects. The strength of this reading is its consonance with the broader Johannine claim that "God is light" (1:5) and "God is spirit" (John 4:24): each is an essence-statement, not a property-statement.

Analytic theism — William Lane Craig, Alvin Plantinga, Richard Swinburne — has pressed back. The classical doctrine of divine simplicity, the analytic critique runs, makes too many sentences synonymous: if God's love is identical with God's justice and both are identical with God's essence, then "God loves Israel" and "God judges Israel" become tautologies, and the differentiation between divine acts collapses. Plantinga in *Does God Have a Nature?* argued the point at length; Craig and others have proposed modified or weak versions of simplicity. The strength of this position is its philosophical care about identity-statements; the weakness is its reluctance to follow the patristic-medieval tradition into apophatic territory.

Process theology — Charles Hartshorne, John Cobb — takes 4:8 in a third direction. If God is love, and love is essentially relational, then God's being is essentially relational becoming. The classical attributes (immutability, impassibility, simplicity) misread the love-clause; what 4:8 demands is a panentheistic God who is genuinely affected by the world, who suffers with creation, who is in process. Hartshorne's *The Divine Relativity* makes the case rigorously. The steelman: the classical tradition's emphasis on impassibility creates real exegetical strain when reading the Hebrew prophets and the Passion narrative; if God is love, and love grieves, then a God who cannot grieve cannot love in the fullest sense. The cost is a metaphysical revision that most of the tradition has resisted as too high a price.

The Eastern Orthodox apophatic tradition (Lossky, Bulgakov, Zizioulas) offers a fourth reading. The essence-energies distinction, articulated most rigorously by Gregory Palamas, holds that God's essence is unknowable, but God's energies — including love — are God truly given to creation. On this view, *ho theos agapē estin* speaks of God-as-given, God-toward-us, without claiming to have grasped the divine essence. The strength is its respect for the apophatic limits the tradition recognized; the weakness, from a Western perspective, is that the essence-energies distinction can sound like a softened form of divine simplicity.

Open theism — Greg Boyd, Clark Pinnock — extends the process intuition with less metaphysical revision: God is genuinely loving, genuinely relational, genuinely affected, but does not exhaustively foreknow free creaturely choices. The steelman, again on the love-statement: if love entails risk, and God is love, then the future must be genuinely open. The cost is the doctrine of comprehensive foreknowledge that the tradition has held since Augustine.

**Where this leaves us:** the four readings are not equally probable, but each is responding to a real pressure in the text. The classical theist reading remains the majority position and the one most consonant with the Johannine grammar of "God is light," "God is spirit." But the analytic, process, Orthodox, and open-theist alternatives are not frivolous — each is trying to honor the claim that *love* is somehow primary to God, not derivative from a more abstract metaphysical inventory. The elder is not adjudicating these debates; he is making the assertion they all attempt to construe.

### Tension 2: Discernment of spirits (4:1-6) — confessional, charismatic, or mystical?

The Reformed cessationist reading takes 4:1-6 as a doctrinal-confessional test only. The "spirits" in question are teachers and prophets in the early church, and the test is the *en sarki* confession — orthodox Christology. The spiritual gift of *diakrisis pneumatōn* described in 1 Cor 12:10 belonged to the apostolic age and ceased with it; what remains is the catholic creed. B. B. Warfield's *Counterfeit Miracles* is the classic statement; many contemporary Reformed theologians (R. C. Sproul, John MacArthur with qualifications) hold versions of this view.

The Pentecostal-charismatic reading sees 4:1 as describing an ongoing spiritual gift. The discerning of spirits is a charism the Spirit gives to particular believers in particular moments, and it functions alongside but distinguishable from doctrinal-confessional tests. John Christopher Thomas's work on Pentecostal hermeneutics, and Wayne Grudem's *Systematic Theology* (which represents a moderate-evangelical openness to ongoing gifts), both develop this reading. The strength: it takes seriously the experiential dimension of the early-church practice and refuses to flatten *diakrisis* into propositional doctrine.

The Catholic Ignatian tradition reads 4:1-6 as the biblical warrant for the *Rules for the Discernment of Spirits* in the *Spiritual Exercises*. Discernment is a habituated practice, neither merely confessional nor merely charismatic, in which the soul learns to distinguish the consolations and desolations of different "spirits" (the Holy Spirit, the human spirit, the enemy). The Catholic charismatic renewal has developed this reading further. The mystical-Carmelite tradition (John of the Cross, Teresa of Avila) builds on it for the *dark night* — the discernment of which dryness is purgative and which is pathological.

**Where this leaves us:** the elder's immediate concern in 4:1-6 is plainly confessional — the test is the *en sarki* confession against Cerinthian Christology. But the broader tradition has rightly seen that confessional discernment is not the only kind of discernment Christians need. The Ignatian practice is not a substitute for the confessional test; it operates downstream of it, in the smaller decisions of the spiritual life. The charismatic gift is a third thing — episodic, given, not reducible to either the credal test or the trained spiritual sense. To read 4:1-6 well one needs all three.

**Closing question for chapter 4:** if love and fear are mutually exclusive (4:18), and most Christians live with fear most of the time, what does it mean — practically, not aspirationally — to be "perfected in love" before death? The mystics have one answer; the Reformers have another; Wesley a third. The text itself does not say.

## The Thread

Four threads run from 1 John 4 into the rest of the canon. John 1:14 — *kai ho logos sarx egeneto* (καὶ ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο, "and the Word became flesh") — is the most direct Christological grounding of the *en sarki* confession; the Gospel and the Epistle stand or fall together on this point. Deuteronomy 6:4-9 is the Shema, the source of the love-of-God command that the elder presupposes throughout. Leviticus 19:18 is the love-of-neighbor source that 4:20-21 makes inseparable from the love-of-God. And 1 Corinthians 13 — Paul's hymn to *agapē* — is the parallel passage that should be read alongside 1 John 4: where Paul writes a poem, the elder writes a meditation; where Paul defines love by what it does, the elder defines it by where it comes from. Read together they are the New Testament's two great love-passages, and neither is complete without the other.
