# Job 24: The Catalog of Unpunished Cruelty

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 24 opens with a question that indicts the governance of the cosmos — "Why are not times of judgment kept by the Almighty?" — and then delivers one of the most detailed ethnographies of rural injustice in the Hebrew Bible. Moving landmarks (24:2), seizing the flocks of dependents, driving away the donkey of the fatherless, taking the widow's ox in pledge (24:3), thrusting the poor off the road (24:4), forcing them into the wild to glean wheat from unfriendly fields (24:6), withholding clothing from them in the mountain cold (24:7-8), separating the nursing infant from its mother for debt-collection (24:9), working them naked through the olive harvest and winepress (24:10-11). This is not generic moralizing. It is a specific picture of agrarian predation in a clan-based society where power translated directly into seizure.

Archaeological work on Iron Age land tenure in the Levant confirms the social pathology Job describes. Boundary stones (*kudurru* in Mesopotamia, *gĕbûlāh* in Israelite Hebrew) have been recovered in large numbers from Babylonia and, in more fragmentary form, from the southern Levant. The Code of Hammurabi §§ 57-60 prescribes severe penalty for moving a boundary; Deuteronomy 19:14 and 27:17 pronounce a curse on the one who moves his neighbor's landmark. That Job here names landmark-moving (*yassîgû gĕbûlôt*, 24:2) as a signature wicked act means he is invoking a crime that every ancient Near Eastern law code treated as foundational. The Laws of Ur-Nammu, Lipit-Ishtar, and Eshnunna all contain provisions protecting widows, orphans, and pledge-takers. Ugaritic texts from Ras Shamra preserve in the Keret and Danel epics the royal ideal of the king as guardian of widow and orphan — a role Job himself claimed to have fulfilled in chapter 29. That the wicked in chapter 24 invert each of these protections shows the poet's fluent command of the ancient Near Eastern ethical lexicon.

The authorship and dating of Job remain contested. Marvin Pope, David Clines, and Carol Newsom make the case for post-exilic composition on the basis of Aramaisms, the developed figure of *ha-satan*, and the theological trajectory from Second Isaiah. Robert Gordis and Francis Andersen argue for earlier dating, pointing to the archaic phonology, the patriarchal milieu, and the absence of Torah or Temple references. The middle witness is Ezekiel 14:14, where Job is already a figure of proverbial righteousness by the sixth century BCE. Chapter 24's concrete sociology of agrarian predation could reflect any period from the mid-second millennium forward, but the sophisticated poetic elaboration of the dialogues points to the late monarchic or post-exilic period. The LXX of Job is approximately four hundred lines shorter than the MT; chapter 24 is one of the most cut sections in the Greek, and verses 18-24 in particular are either heavily abridged in the LXX or rearranged. This is relevant to one of the chapter's major scholarly cruxes: verses 18-24 sound theologically at odds with Job's usual position and more like a friend's speech.

The ancient Near Eastern comparanda are thick. *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi* and the *Babylonian Theodicy* both probe the prosperity of the wicked and the suffering of the innocent. Egyptian wisdom literature — the *Admonitions of Ipuwer*, the *Eloquent Peasant* — contain catalogs of social injustice strikingly parallel to Job 24. The *Eloquent Peasant* in particular, a Middle Kingdom text, rehearses the grievances of the marginal against the powerful with rhetorical precision similar to Job's.

Five Hebrew terms carry the chapter. גְּבוּלוֹת (*gĕbûlôt*, "landmarks, boundaries," גְּבוּלוֹת) in 24:2 is the technical term for the property markers whose displacement Deuteronomy curses. יָתוֹם (*yātôm*, "fatherless, orphan," יָתוֹם) and אַלְמָנָה (*'almānāh*, "widow," אַלְמָנָה) are the standing pair of vulnerable dependents across the Torah's social legislation. חֲבֹל (*ḥăbōl*, "pledge," חֲבֹל) in 24:3 and 24:9 is the legal term for the garment or ox held as collateral, the same word Eliphaz hurled at Job in 22:6. שׁוֹד (*šōd*, "violence, destruction," שֹׁד) underlies the atmosphere of predation. Finally, מִשְׁפָּט (*mišpāṭ*, "judgment," מִשְׁפָּט) in 24:1 returns from chapter 23, now not as Job's courtroom hope but as the missing schedule of divine adjudication — the *days* (*yāmāyw*, "his days," in 24:1) on which God was expected to act.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 24 is Job's second consecutive speech and the fullest catalog of social injustice in the book. It belongs to the third cycle, whose architecture is visibly deformed: Eliphaz's last speech in chapter 22 was followed by Job's response in 23-24, Bildad's truncated six-verse speech in chapter 25, then Job's extended responses in 26-27 — and Zophar never speaks a third time at all. Pope, Clines, Habel, and many others argue the text has been textually disturbed. Some treat chapter 24's verses 18-24 as originally part of a lost Zophar speech because their theology — that the wicked are ultimately snatched away by Sheol — sounds more like the friends than like Job. Others (Hartley, Andersen, Newsom) defend the received text, reading 24:18-24 as Job's ironic quotation of the friends' position, which he then refutes by the structure of his argument.

The poetic architecture of chapter 24 is a three-part structure. Verses 1-12 are the exploitation catalog: a documentary sweep of what the wicked do to the poor, delivered in present-tense habitual action and accumulating horror. Verses 13-17 are the nocturnal catalog: the wicked as creatures of darkness, dwelling in a timeworld inverted from the honest world. Verses 18-25 are the contested section, variously read as Job's ironic ventriloquism of the friends, a misplaced Zophar speech, or Job's own chastened acknowledgment that the wicked do eventually die. Each section uses distinctive poetic technique. The exploitation catalog works by verb-density: move, seize, drive, take, thrust, gather, glean, lie, cling, snatch, carry, tread — the wicked's actions pile on top of each other. The nocturnal catalog uses *chiasm*: rise before light (24:14), wait for twilight (24:15), dig through houses in the dark (24:16), and the centering insight "deep darkness is morning to all of them" (24:17). The contested section shifts to metaphors of water and tree and harvest — "swift are they on the face of the waters," "their portion is cursed in the land," "wickedness is broken like a tree," "cut off like the heads of grain."

The parallelism is dense and often synonymous. Verse 7 ("they lie all night naked, without clothing, and have no covering in the cold") builds in two stages. Verse 9's parenthetical (ESV uses parentheses) is grammatically anomalous and may be a glossator's insertion. Verse 12's last line ("yet God charges no one with wrong," or with a different emendation, "yet God sees no wrong") is one of the chapter's theological thunderclaps — the poet has let Job say out loud what the rest of the book has been circling. The Hebrew here, וֶאֱלוֹהַּ לֹא־יָשִׂים תִּפְלָה, admits at least two readings. Pope reads it as an indictment: "God pays no attention to the crime." The MT's pointing supports "God sees no wrong," which could mean either God finds nothing wrong with their behavior (sarcastic) or God imputes no wrong to them (accusatory). Clines and Habel lean toward the accusatory reading; Andersen defends a more neutral translation.

The legal vocabulary from chapter 23 returns but inverted. Where 23 asked for audience in the courtroom, 24 indicts the courtroom's failure to convene. The *mišpāṭ* Job sought in 23 is the *mišpāṭ* that is never administered in 24. The pledge-language Eliphaz used in 22 to accuse Job falsely is used in 24 to describe what the actual wicked do. The chapter works by a kind of rhetorical *tu quoque*: Eliphaz accused Job of oppressing widows and orphans, and Job responds by showing that the real oppressors of widows and orphans go free.

Scholars differ sharply on verses 18-25. Marvin Pope reorders them, attributing parts to Zophar. Clines in WBC argues they are Job quoting the friends' position in order to demolish it by the next stanza. Habel treats them as Job's ambivalent acknowledgment that the wicked do eventually die — but not when justice would require it. Andersen reads them straight as Job's own speech, noting that the final clause (24:25, "if it is not so, who will prove me a liar") frames the whole passage as Job's challenge. The textual disturbance is undeniable; how to resolve it is not.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Chapter 24 is one of the densest theodicy passages in Scripture. The Jewish trajectory of reading it is layered. The Talmud's *Bava Batra* 15a-16b touches the theodicy question in its discussion of Job, and the medieval Jewish commentators engage chapter 24 with unusual care. Rashi reads 24:1 as Job's lament that God does not publicize the days on which he has judged, so that the righteous cannot take comfort in knowing judgment has occurred. Ibn Ezra is characteristically grammatical, attending to textual difficulties in verses 18-24 and suggesting emendations. Maimonides in *Guide of the Perplexed* III.22-23 treats Job as a figure moving through stages of understanding providence; chapter 24 represents Job at the stage where he correctly perceives the absence of visible retribution but has not yet transcended the retribution framework. Nachmanides reads the chapter mystically, connecting the nocturnal catalog of 24:13-17 to the *qelipot*, the shells or husks of evil. Martin Buber reads chapter 24 as Job's refusal to accept the friends' theology of visible retribution and his insistence on naming what he sees. Jon Levenson's work on creation and the persistence of evil treats the chapter as evidence that Israelite religion does not claim the wicked are always visibly punished — this is a counter-narrative preserved in the canon against flattened retribution theology. James Kugel emphasizes the chapter's ethnographic precision and argues it reflects actual rural sociology of late monarchic Judah or post-exilic Yehud. Avivah Zornberg's literary-psychoanalytic reading attends to the oral, hungry, thirsting imagery of 24:10-11 — the wicked carry sheaves but are hungry, tread winepresses but suffer thirst — as the Tantalus image of the sufferer's condition.

The Christian trajectory on chapter 24 is equally layered. Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job* reads chapter 24 allegorically: the wicked of verses 13-17 are heretics who hate the light of truth; the poor of verses 1-12 are the church suffering under persecution. Origen reads the chapter eschatologically: the judgment-times not kept in this age will be kept at the end of all ages. Chrysostom's homilies emphasize the social-ethical weight of the catalog: the church is called to do the justice God defers. Augustine folds the chapter into his *City of God* framework: the earthly city permits what the heavenly city will rectify. Thomas Aquinas reads 24:1 as Job's correct intuition that divine providence is not always visibly manifest and defends it as theologically sound. John Calvin in his 1554-55 Job sermons preaches chapter 24 with unusual political force, naming the specific crimes of sixteenth-century landlords against their tenants and arguing that Job's catalog is transhistorical. Luther reads the chapter as evidence of *deus absconditus* — God permits the wicked to flourish precisely so the righteous may be refined by their patience. Critical scholarship — Wellhausen, Gunkel, von Rad — reads the chapter as a sharp wisdom-theological statement against any mechanical doctrine of retribution. Clines's WBC offers a detailed textual reconstruction and argues verses 18-24 are Job's quotation of the friends. Newsom reads the chapter as a performance of moral imagination, asking the reader to see what the friends refuse to see. Gustavo Gutiérrez in *On Job* treats chapter 24 as the paradigmatic liberation-theological text: God is present among the poor of verses 1-12, and the theology that refuses to see their suffering is the theology Eliphaz has been voicing. Ellen Davis in *Getting Involved With God* reads the chapter as a test for the preacher: whether one will speak the truths Job speaks or hide them. Susannah Ticciati reads the chapter as the climax of the disruption of retribution language. Norman Habel and John Hartley defend textual coherence. Francis Andersen treats the chapter with pastoral restraint, urging that Job's catalog not be sentimentalized but faced as theological reality.

The convergences across traditions are significant. Both Jewish and Christian readers have found chapter 24 the sharpest indictment of retribution theology in the canon. Both struggle with verses 18-25. Both recognize the chapter's close observation of rural injustice. The divergences are telling: Jewish readings tend to let the theodicy question stand open, Christian readings tend to press it toward eschatological resolution. Liberation theology reads the chapter as descriptive of the contemporary situation of the poor; classical theology reads it as a permanent feature of the saeculum.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

### Tension 1: Does God's non-intervention in injustice constitute divine endorsement, divine indifference, or divine patience?

Job's indictment reaches its peak in verse 12 — "yet God charges no one with wrong." The chapter forces a question about how to interpret divine restraint in the face of cataloged cruelty.

- **Catholic (Aquinas, Rahner, von Balthasar)**: Divine patience. God permits evil to persist within the *saeculum* for the greater good of free creaturely response and final judgment. Aquinas develops this in his treatment of divine providence; non-intervention is not absence. Evil remains evil and is not endorsed, but the divine economy takes the long view. This requires the doctrine of final judgment to be coherent; without it the position collapses.
- **Reformed (Calvin, Bavinck, Berkhof)**: Divine decree. The prosperity of the wicked and suffering of the poor are within the mysterious providence of God, who uses even the wicked for his purposes. Calvin in his Job sermons is unusually forceful here, preaching against both the oppressors and the temptation to read their prosperity as divine favor. The Reformed position holds divine sovereignty and creaturely responsibility in tension without resolution.
- **Lutheran (Luther, Forde, Jüngel)**: The hidden God. God permits and even appears to work through evil (*opus alienum*) for the sake of his ultimate proper work (*opus proprium*) of salvation. The tension is not resolved but held under faith.
- **Open Theist (Pinnock, Boyd)**: Divine self-limitation. God has bound himself to non-coercive action within a genuinely free creation; non-intervention reflects a covenantal commitment, not a decree. The chapter becomes evidence of real divine grief at creaturely evil.
- **Process (Whitehead, Cobb, Suchocki)**: Divine persuasion, not coercion. God is doing all God can; the prosperity of the wicked reflects the limits of persuasive power against resistant creatures. No final judgment closes the gap.
- **Jewish (Heschel, Fackenheim, Levenson)**: Divine pathos and the persistence of chaos. Heschel reads God as suffering alongside the poor, not distant from them. Levenson reads the chapter as acknowledging that creation is not yet complete; evil persists because the creative work is ongoing. Fackenheim after the Shoah insists the question not be foreclosed.
- **Liberation (Gutiérrez, Sobrino, Boff)**: Historical contingency and human responsibility. God's non-intervention is the space in which the church is called to act. The chapter indicts both the oppressors and the theologians who legitimate them. To read the chapter and not act is to join the oppressors.

The least-intuitive position to steelman is Process theology's — that God is doing all God can and cannot simply stop the wicked. The Catholic, Reformed, and Orthodox traditions all reject this as a compromise of divine omnipotence. Its steelman strength is that it takes chapter 24's horror at face value: the poet says "the dying groan, the soul of the wounded cries for help; yet God charges no one with wrong," and Process theology refuses to explain this away by appeal to eschatology or decree. Its cost is the classical divine attributes; its gain is an honesty about the present.

### Tension 2: Is the poor person in Job 24 present as moral example, as anthropological datum, or as christological figure?

The chapter's ethnography of the oppressed poor — naked in the cold, gleaning unfriendly vineyards, carrying sheaves hungry, treading winepresses thirsty — is strikingly sustained. How the poor person is functioning in the text matters for how the text is read and preached.

- **Catholic (Gutiérrez, Boff, the 1968 Medellín document)**: Christological. The poor person is the *sacramentum Christi*, the sign-presence of Christ in the world. Matthew 25 is the interpretive key; to encounter the hungry, the naked, the thirsty of Job 24 is to encounter Christ.
- **Reformed (Calvin, Kuyper)**: Moral example. The poor person is the figure through whose suffering the justice of God is displayed. Calvin preaches chapter 24 as a test of the church's obedience.
- **Orthodox**: Ascetic. The poor person is the one whose condition approaches the condition of Christ in his poverty; the worship of Christ in the poor is integral to the liturgical life.
- **Jewish (Heschel, Levinas)**: The prophetic face. Levinas especially reads the face of the poor as the site of ethical obligation that cannot be evaded. The poor person in Job 24 calls the reader to the infinite ethical demand.
- **Critical (Clines, Newsom)**: Anthropological. The poor person is the historical figure of ancient Near Eastern rural class-structure. The text documents what the poet observed. The theological readings risk abstracting the concrete sociology.

Where this leaves us: chapter 24 is the book of Job's most sustained indictment of the world-as-it-is, and the poet refuses to let his indictment be softened by any framework, including his own. The final verse is Job's challenge: "If it is not so, who will prove me a liar?" The book does not, in fact, prove him a liar. The divine speeches of chapters 38-41 will not revisit the social catalog of chapter 24. They will not restore the moved landmarks, return the widow's ox, clothe the naked glean-workers, or charge the wicked with wrong. The book's silence on this is its most difficult theological datum.

One unresolved question: if God's eventual response to Job will not address the sociology of chapter 24 directly, and if the book ends with Job's restoration but not with justice for the anonymous poor he catalogs, what does the book mean us to do with this silence? Is it the silence of an unaddressed remainder, an eschatological pointer to a judgment beyond the book, or an invitation to the reader to become the hand the book will not name?

## The Thread

- **Amos 2:6-8** — "They sell the righteous for silver, and the needy for a pair of sandals — they trample the head of the poor into the dust of the earth." The prophetic cousin to Job's catalog. Amos extends the ethnography of injustice into covenant lawsuit against Israel.
- **Psalm 73** — Asaph's parallel struggle with the prosperity of the wicked. The psalm resolves in the sanctuary vision (73:17); Job 24 offers no equivalent resolution.
- **Luke 16:19-31** — The rich man and Lazarus. The only parable in which Jesus names a poor character. The gulf between the wicked's prosperity and the poor's suffering is fixed at death, an eschatological answer to Job 24's theodicy.
- **James 5:1-6** — "Come now, you rich, weep and howl for the miseries that are coming upon you. Behold, the wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you kept back by fraud, are crying out against you." James writes inside the Job 24 tradition, applying its catalog to the first-century church's rich.
