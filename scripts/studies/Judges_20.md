# Judges 20: The Brother-War and the God Who Loses Twice

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The civil war of Judges 20 stages a portrait of pre-monarchic Israel as a fragile cultic confederation that can muster a national army but cannot administer justice within its own borders. The summons "from Dan to Beersheba, including the land of Gilead" (20:1) is a pan-Israelite formula that elsewhere defines the realm under David and Solomon (2 Sam. 24:2; 1 Kings 4:25); placing it in the period of the judges signals a confederation that already imagines itself as a single body politic but lacks any standing institution to enforce that imagination. The assembly meets at Mizpah, traditionally identified with Tell en-Nasbeh in the Benjaminite hills north of Jerusalem, a site William F. Badè excavated in the 1920s and 1930s and that Jeffrey Zorn has restudied. Mizpah's later prominence under Samuel (1 Sam. 7) and as the Babylonian provincial center under Gedaliah (2 Kings 25:23) suggests it was already a recognized assembly point with cultic legitimacy. The decision to inquire of YHWH at Bethel (20:18, 26-28) — not Shiloh, the official sanctuary of the Shiloh-Eli line that 1 Samuel will privilege — reflects the multi-sanctuary reality of Iron I religion. Bethel/Beitin was excavated by W. F. Albright and James Kelso, who identified Iron I cultic activity, though their dating remains contested by Israel Finkelstein.

Benjamin's marginal status is structurally crucial. The "son of the right hand" tribe occupies a narrow corridor between the Joseph tribes and Judah, controlling the central ridge route and Jerusalem before David. That seven hundred of its mustered men are *itter yad-yemino* (אִטֵּר יַד-יְמִינוֹ), "bound in the right hand," meaning left-handed slingers (20:16), is a dark joke at the expense of the tribe whose name means "right hand": Benjamin produces left-handers. The same condition appears in Ehud (3:15) and forms a recurring Benjaminite trope. The slingers' precision — "could sling a stone at a hair and not miss" — describes a specialist Iron Age light infantry comparable to the Balearic slingers later prized by Hellenistic and Roman armies; archaeological recovery of sling stones from Lachish, Megiddo, and Gibeah corroborates the prevalence of the weapon.

Tell el-Ful, three miles north of Jerusalem on the central ridge road, has been identified with Gibeah since Edward Robinson's 1838 survey. Albright excavated it in 1922-23 and 1933, identifying a fortress of "Period I" that he linked to Saul's citadel; Paul Lapp returned in 1964 and refined the stratigraphy. A burn layer in the Iron I phase has been read by some as archaeological residue of Judges 19-20, though Israel Finkelstein, Patrick Arnold, and others have challenged the identification, noting that destruction layers in the central hill country are common and not easily tied to specific narratives. The point is not that the chapter records bare history but that the geography is real and the tribal seams the narrator describes correspond to a recognizable Iron I landscape.

The chapter's military procedure follows the logic of *herem* (חֵרֶם), the war ban, applied to fellow Israelites — a theological scandal. *Herem* normally targets the Canaanite cities of the conquest (Deut. 7; Josh. 6-11); applying it to Benjamin (20:48 — the men of Israel "struck them with the edge of the sword, the city, men and beasts and all that they found") inverts the logic of national identity. The verb *naga'* (נָגַע) in 20:34, "disaster was close upon them" (literally "touched them"), echoes the same root used elsewhere for the touch of plague. The army's *qahal* (קָהָל), the assembly (20:1-2), is a covenantal term: this is not mere militia but a sacral gathering. The Levite in 20:4 narrates the Gibeah outrage with a conspicuous omission — he says nothing of having shoved the concubine out the door (19:25). Hebrew narrators are routinely sparing of inner judgment, but the gap is a critique rendered in silence. The verb *zanah* (זָנָה) used for the concubine in 19:2 — usually "to play the harlot" but possibly here "to be unfaithful" or even, per LXX A's "she was angry," to depart — has been retranslated by Pamela Tamarkin Reis and others to defuse the misogynist English rendering.

The casualty figures (400,000 mustered, 22,000 fallen, 18,000 fallen, 25,100 Benjaminites destroyed) are conventionally read by historical critics as *eleph* (אֶלֶף) ambiguity: the word means "thousand" but also "military unit," "clan," or even "chieftain." George Mendenhall's 1958 article and Colin Humphreys's later refinements suggest the original numbers are far smaller — perhaps 400 contingents, 22 dead per contingent — recovered as the Persian or Hellenistic redactors hardened the numbers into thousands. Whether or not one accepts that reduction, the magnitude is theological more than statistical: the entire armed force of a small confederation collapses against a single tribe twice before YHWH grants victory.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Judges 20 is intra-Israelite war narrative built on a tightly chiastic three-battle structure: inquiry → defeat (22,000), inquiry with weeping → defeat (18,000), inquiry with weeping, fasting, sacrifice → victory by ambush. Robert Alter has noted that biblical narrators love the pattern of three with intensification, and here the intensification is theological: each consultation deepens — first they "inquire" (20:18), then they "wept before the LORD" and inquired (20:23), then they "wept and sat there before the LORD and fasted that day until evening, and offered burnt offerings and peace offerings" (20:26). The escalation suggests that what looks like divine fickleness — YHWH says "go up" twice and Israel is twice slaughtered — is actually a pedagogy of repentance. The narrator stages the Israelites learning that war against their brother is not a weekend operation that the deity automatically endorses.

The third-battle ambush at Gibeah (20:29-48) is a deliberate literary echo of Joshua 8 and the conquest of Ai. The verbs, the staging, the smoke signal from the burning city, the feigned flight that draws defenders away from the gate — all replay the Ai pattern. Polzin in *Moses and the Deuteronomist* reads the parallel as deeply ironic: the conquest tactic that secured Israel's foothold in the land is now turned inward against an Israelite city. The narrator stages Israel doing to Benjamin what they once did to Canaanites; the moral register flips even as the military choreography stays identical. Robert Boling in the Anchor Bible commentary draws the same parallel and notes the deliberate intertextual marking.

The famous refrain — "in those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes," *yashar b'einav* (יָשָׁר בְּעֵינָיו) — does not appear in chapter 20 itself but is the editorial frame supplied at 17:6, 18:1, 19:1, and the closing 21:25. Yet chapter 20 is the structural pivot at which the absence-of-king refrain becomes the dominant interpretive lens: when the people act "as one man" (*ke'ish echad*, כְּאִישׁ אֶחָד, 20:1, 8, 11), they nearly destroy themselves. The unity is the wrong unity, achieved on the wrong terms, against the wrong enemy. Yairah Amit in *The Book of Judges: The Art of Editing* reads the threefold "as one man" repetition as ironic: the only thing this confederation does in unison is exterminate one of its own.

The Levite's edited speech in 20:4-7 is one of the chapter's most discussed literary moves. Meir Sternberg in *The Poetics of Biblical Narrative* identifies it as a textbook case of unreliable internal speech — the character lies by omission while the narrator preserves the omission for the reader who already knows the truth from chapter 19. Mieke Bal in *Death and Dissymmetry* presses this further: the Levite's first-person testimony erases the woman's body from the political process even as her literal dismembered body is what summoned the assembly. Susanne Scholz, Cheryl Exum, and Phyllis Trible have each followed this critique in different registers.

The textual situation is unusually unsettled. LXX A (Codex Alexandrinus) and LXX B (Codex Vaticanus) diverge substantially across chapters 20-21, especially on casualty numbers and the ordering of the Bethel inquiries. Natalio Fernández Marcos's Göttingen Septuagint edition treats Judges as one of the most textually unstable books in the Greek tradition. The Qumran fragments 4QJudg-a and 4QJudg-b are too short to settle the questions but tend to confirm a fluid Hebrew Vorlage. Pseudo-Philo's *Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum* 47 expands the war narrative with a long divine speech justifying the two defeats — read as evidence that the moral problem of the chapter was already painful to Second Temple readers.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc of Judges 20 is twofold: backward to the conquest pattern of Joshua 7-8 (Israel defeated until they purge sin, then victorious by ambush) and forward to the Saul narrative of 1 Samuel. The line is genealogical and geographical: Saul of Gibeah is a Benjaminite, son of the tribe nearly exterminated here. When 1 Samuel 11 introduces Saul rescuing the men of Jabesh-gilead from Nahash the Ammonite, the connection lit up by chapter 21 (Jabesh-gilead is destroyed to provide brides for Benjamin) is already in play. Saul's dynasty descends from women taken by force in the aftermath of this war.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Pseudo-Philo (*LAB* 47) significantly expands the divine speech, giving YHWH a long justification for the two failed battles: Israel had become as bad as the Benjaminites and needed to be purged before being granted victory. Josephus (*Ant.* 5.2-3) flattens the moral difficulty by reframing Israel's failures as test, then victory as reward. The Talmud (Bava Batra 109b) addresses the disputed identity of the Levite of Judges 17 (Jonathan, grandson of Moses, per one tradition) and uses the appendix as a case study in how priestly families can fail. Rashi on 20:18 reads the divine answer "Judah shall go up first" as deliberate ambiguity: God does not say Judah will win, only that Judah will go up first. The point, for Rashi, is that the people's question was poorly framed — they assumed victory and asked only about order. Radak (David Kimhi) follows similar lines, emphasizing that the divine response was honest if cryptic. Ralbag (Levi ben Gershon) introduces a more philosophical reading: the priestly oracle reveals only what is asked, and human moral responsibility cannot be outsourced to divine consultation. Maimonides in *Hilkhot Melakhim* (Laws of Kings and Their Wars) draws on Judges 20 for the laws of intra-Israelite war — when is it permissible? — and concludes only when there is a clear judicial process and a refusal to surrender the guilty. Yair Zakovitch and Yairah Amit in contemporary Israeli scholarship read the chapter as Judahite polemic against the Benjaminite/Saulide line, written or redacted from a Davidic perspective. Marc Brettler in *The Book of Judges* and *The Creation of History in Ancient Israel* makes the polemic argument explicit: the appendix is anti-Saul, anti-Benjamin, and the dtr framework is pro-Davidic propaganda working backward to delegitimize the Saulide claim.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Origen's Homily 9 on Judges allegorizes the war as the soul's battle against vice; the two defeats are the soul's failed first attempts at virtue, victorious only after fasting and sacrifice. Augustine in *Quaestiones in Iudices* (Question 49) wrestles directly with the divine permission of the two defeats: God allowed Israel to be defeated because they entered the war with overconfidence and without proper repentance for the larger national sin that produced a place like Gibeah. Ambrose draws on the chapter for early just-war ethics (*De Officiis*), arguing that Israel's slow progression — inquiry to weeping inquiry to fasting inquiry — is the model for legitimate war. Aquinas in *Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 40 builds on Ambrose, citing Judges 20 as evidence that even rightly-intentioned wars can fail without sufficient moral preparation. Calvin's commentary on Judges (1561) takes divine inscrutability head-on: the two defeats teach that providence is not transactional. Critical scholarship from Wellhausen onward broke the chapter into composite sources; C. F. Burney's 1918 commentary still influences contemporary work, and Boling and Trent Butler extend the source-critical work in the Anchor Bible and Word Biblical Commentary respectively. Daniel Block (NICOT) and Barry Webb (NICOT) take more synchronic theological readings; Lawson Younger attempts to recover preachable content without flattening the moral difficulty. Feminist criticism has been the most generative recent stream: Phyllis Trible's *Texts of Terror* opened the discussion with the unnamed concubine as paradigmatic victim of patriarchal narrative; Mieke Bal's *Death and Dissymmetry* gave the most sustained theoretical analysis of how the chapter sublimates violence against women into nation-building. Cheryl Exum, Susanne Scholz, Pamela Tamarkin Reis, Gale Yee, and Athalya Brenner have extended the critique; Carol Meyers in *Discovering Eve* contextualizes the female bodies within Iron Age social history.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Both Jewish and Christian streams are uncomfortable with the divine permission of the two defeats; both produce explanatory frameworks (Pseudo-Philo's purgation, Augustine's prerequisite repentance) that essentially say the same thing — Israel was not yet morally prepared to win. Both have produced just-war traditions that draw on the chapter's pattern of inquiry, weeping, and fasting. They diverge on the polemical reading: contemporary Israeli scholarship (Brettler, Amit, Zakovitch) sees the chapter as anti-Saulide Davidic polemic, while Christian commentary has historically taken the framing at face value. Feminist scholarship across both traditions — Trible (Christian), Reis (Jewish) — has been the most honest about the systematic violence against women that the chapter's military theology requires.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author believe God was doing? Two genuine tensions.

**Tension 1: Why does YHWH say "go up" twice and let Israel be slaughtered both times?** This is the chapter's theological scandal. Israel inquires of God at Bethel before the priestly oracle (20:18); God says "Judah shall go up first." Israel goes up; 22,000 die. They weep, inquire again; God says "Go up against them." They go; 18,000 more die. Only after a fast and sacrifice does God grant victory. The Catholic tradition, following Augustine and Aquinas, reads the two defeats as divine pedagogy: God permits failure to teach moral seriousness. The cause may be just but the disposition of the warriors is not yet purified, and entering a war of brother against brother in confidence rather than in grief is itself a sin. Where this leaves the Catholic reader: God's "yes" to a right cause is not a permission slip; legitimate war requires interior conversion that ritual inquiry alone cannot supply. Reformed Protestant readings (Calvin, more recently Daniel Block) lean toward divine inscrutability: God's ways are higher than ours, and the two defeats remind Israel and the reader that providence is not a transactional ledger; even right causes pursued in legitimate ways may be granted defeat for purposes beyond human accounting. Where this leaves the Reformed reader: trust without certainty, faith without forensic confidence in outcomes. Jewish tradition (Rashi, Radak, Ralbag) reads the divine speech as honest but cryptic: the people asked the wrong question. They asked "who shall go up first" assuming victory; God answered the question they asked. Where this leaves the Jewish reader: divine consultation reveals only what is asked, and human moral responsibility cannot be outsourced to oracles. The critical-historical position (Brettler, Amit, Soggin) reads the two defeats as a literary device of the deuteronomistic redactor: the chapter is staged as ritual escalation to demonstrate the inadequacy of the pre-monarchic cultic confederation. Israel is too disorganized, too informally led, too liturgically improvised to wage even a justified war effectively. Where this leaves the critical reader: the chapter's theological message is sociological — confederation cannot do what monarchy can, and that is the deuteronomistic point.

The position least intuitive to me is the Reformed inscrutability reading. Steelman: a God who is fully comprehensible is no God at all; the two defeats refuse to let the reader convert divine consultation into a vending machine. Every human framework that explains the defeats — pedagogy, wrong question, sociological inadequacy — runs the risk of domesticating the deity into a system the human mind can manage. Calvin's instinct to leave the inscrutability standing is theologically more rigorous than the explanatory frameworks that surround it. The two failed battles preserve the otherness of God against the reader's appetite for moral causality.

**Tension 2: Is intra-Israelite *herem* a moral disaster or a tragic necessity?** The verb in 20:48 — *naku 'iyr metim ad behemah* (the city was struck with men and beasts) — is the language of *herem*, the war ban normally reserved for the Canaanites of the conquest. Applying it to Benjamin is theologically scandalous. The Roman Catholic just-war tradition (Ambrose, Aquinas, recent Catholic Social Teaching) reads the chapter as a tragic necessity within a fallen polity: when a tribe shelters murderers and refuses judicial extradition, war becomes the last available form of justice. The horror of intra-Israelite ban is precisely the horror of war as such, exposed without the distance that "foreign enemy" usually provides. Where this leaves the Catholic reader: the chapter is a sustained exposé of war's intrinsic violence, including the possibility that even justified war becomes morally indistinguishable from atrocity in execution. Anabaptist and pacifist Protestant readings (John Howard Yoder, Stanley Hauerwas) read Judges 20 as the reductio ad absurdum of just-war reasoning: when the framework permits brother to slaughter brother, the framework has failed. Where this leaves the Anabaptist reader: the chapter is the deuteronomist's own argument against its system, a confession that the tools of national justice produce national catastrophe. The Jewish tradition (Maimonides on the laws of war) takes a middle position: intra-Israelite war is permitted only after exhausting judicial remedies and only against those who refuse extradition of clear criminals. Where this leaves the Jewish reader: the chapter is a case study in jurisprudence under impossible constraints. The feminist critique (Bal, Trible, Scholz) refuses the just-war framing entirely: every theological resolution that gestures toward "tragic necessity" is implicated in the disposability of the women who started the chapter (the dismembered concubine) and the women who will end the next chapter (the destroyed Jabesh-gilead wives, the Shiloh dancers). Where this leaves the feminist reader: the framework that produces this chapter is the same framework that requires female bodies to settle male political disputes, and no theological resolution that does not name that structure is honest.

The position least intuitive to me is the just-war reading. Steelman: in a world without functioning international or supratribal courts, the alternative to intra-confederation war is impunity for the men of Gibeah and continued predation on travelers. The just-war tradition does not endorse the war as good; it identifies the conditions under which war becomes the least bad option in a world where worse options are impunity for atrocity. The chapter's slow buildup — extradition demanded, refused, oracle consulted, weeping, fasting — is the slow march of a tradition trying to do the morally serious work that pacifism, in its purer form, refuses on principle. The just-war reading takes the chapter seriously as moral reasoning under emergency, not as endorsement of violence.

**Closing question that lands like a punch.** If God's two defeats were pedagogy and the third victory was reward, what does it mean that the reward was the near-extermination of a tribe and the moral catastrophe of chapter 21? What does it mean that the divine "yes" to a just cause produced a result so monstrous that the deuteronomist himself uses it as the closing argument for a different system of government?

## The Thread

Three cross-references, non-obvious.

When you get to **1 Samuel 11** and Saul of Gibeah rescues Jabesh-gilead from Nahash the Ammonite, remember that Saul's tribe was nearly destroyed in Judges 20, his hometown is the Gibeah whose men committed the original outrage, and his ancestral line runs through women taken by force from the Jabesh-gilead he later rescues. Saul's first act as king is repaying a debt his Benjaminite ancestors incurred at the moment of their near-extinction.

When you get to **Hosea 9:9 and 10:9**, where the prophet says Israel "has deeply corrupted themselves as in the days of Gibeah," remember that "the days of Gibeah" became proverbial for national catastrophe — the worst memory the northern prophetic tradition could summon. The chapter became cultural shorthand for total moral collapse, used by Hosea to indict the eighth-century kingdom.

When you get to **Joshua 7-8** and the failure at Ai followed by the ambush victory, notice that Judges 20 deliberately recapitulates the pattern: defeat, repentance, ambush victory by smoke signal. The narrator stages Israel doing to Benjamin what Joshua did to the Canaanites. The conquest tactic that won the land is now the tactic that destroys part of Israel. The intertextual mirror is the deuteronomist's quiet judgment on what the confederation has become.
