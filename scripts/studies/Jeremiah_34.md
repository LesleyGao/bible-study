# Jeremiah 34: Deror, the Cut Calf, and the Re-enslavement of the Liberated

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 34 has two distinct datable units. Verses 1–7 are positioned during the active Babylonian siege of Jerusalem, "when Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon and all his army… were fighting against Jerusalem and all of its cities" (34:1) — *Lachish and Azekah* (34:7) being identified as "the only fortified cities of Judah that remained." This dates the unit to a specific narrow window in 588/587 BC: after the loss of the Shephelah perimeter and before the final fall of Lachish. Verses 8–22 are positioned slightly later in the same siege period, around the brief Babylonian withdrawal to engage the approaching Egyptian relief force under Pharaoh Hophra (Apries), an event Jer 37:5 explicitly names. The slave-release covenant of 34:8–10 is best read as a desperation measure during the worst of the siege; the abrogation of 34:11 is best read as a calculated reversal once the Babylonian withdrawal made re-enslavement feel safe again.

The mention of *Lachish and Azekah* is one of the most precisely datable details in the Hebrew Bible. **Lachish Letter IV** — an ostracon recovered from the gatehouse of Lachish Stratum II — contains the line "we are watching for the signal-fires of Lachish according to all the signs my lord has given, for we cannot see Azekah." This corresponds exactly to Jer 34:7. The implication: when Lachish Letter IV was written, Azekah had already fallen, leaving Lachish as the last fortified city before Jerusalem itself. This is the only place in extant Iron Age Levantine epigraphy where a specific battlefield situation in the Hebrew Bible is independently confirmed by contemporary native Hebrew documentation. The Lachish Letters were excavated by James Starkey 1932–1938 and have been re-analyzed by Frank Moore Cross, Dennis Pardee, and André Lemaire. The letters use the same Paleo-Hebrew script, the same prophetic vocabulary, and a parallel idiom: Lachish Letter VI complains that the *prophets weaken the hands* of the soldiers — verbatim Jer 38:4.

Material culture: The covenant-cutting ritual of 34:18 — *the calf cut in two and passed between its parts* — is documented across the ANE. The closest parallel is the **Sefire Treaty** (Aramaic, mid-8th c. BC, between Bar-Ga'yah of KTK and Mati'el of Arpad), which prescribes "as this calf is cut up, so may Mati'el be cut up" if the treaty is broken. The **Mari archives** (18th c. BC) include the "ass-killing" ritual (*hayaru qatalu*) for treaty-cutting between West Semitic groups. The Hebrew idiom *karat berit*, "to *cut* a covenant," preserves the ritual etymology even where the ritual itself has fallen out of use. Genesis 15:9–17 is the canonical narrative case (Abraham, the heifer, ram, and turtledoves); Jer 34:18 is the only other place in the Hebrew Bible where the ritual is performed.

The slave-release legislation: Lev 25:8–55 (the Jubilee, every 50 years) and Deut 15:1–18 (the *shemittah*, every 7 years, with anti-poverty supplements) lie behind 34:14, which quotes Deut 15:12 nearly verbatim: *at the end of seven years each of you must set free the fellow Hebrew who has been sold to you and has served you six years*. The Code of Hammurabi §117 prescribes a *three-year* limit on debt-slavery for free Babylonians; the Jeremiah/Deuteronomy code is therefore *more restrictive* on the debtor (six years, not three) but applies the *deror* manumission only to *Hebrew* slaves, not foreign chattel slaves. This *limited* application has shaped the modern moral-historical debate.

Authorship and dating: Jeremiah ben Hilkiah with Baruch ben Neriah (cf. ch. 36). Lundbom (Anchor) takes both units (1–7, 8–22) as Jeremianic. Holladay (Hermeneia) treats 34:8–22 as substantially Jeremianic with a Deuteronomistic editorial frame (note the language overlap with Deut 15). Carroll (OTL) reads it as a heavily Deuteronomistic composition retrojecting later legal concerns into a Jeremianic memory. McKane (ICC), Bright. **Stulman** structures the chapter as a *covenant-violation* unit. **Kathleen O'Connor** (*Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*) reads it through the lens of trauma and betrayal: the abrogation is psychological as much as legal — the population's terror-driven repentance collapses the moment the immediate threat lifts.

Archaeological and textual evidence: The Lachish Ostraca (already cited); the **Babylonian Chronicle** (BM 21946) for Nebuchadnezzar's Levantine campaigns; the **Yehucal ben Shelemiah** bulla (Eilat Mazar 2005, City of David) names a Jeremianic-period official from Jer 37:3; the **Gedaliah ben Pashhur** bulla (Eilat Mazar 2008) names the official from Jer 38:1 who advocated Jeremiah's death. These bullae situate the chapter in a confirmed historical milieu of named Jerusalem officials.

Key terms: *deror* (דְּרוֹר, "release/liberty/manumission," 34:8, 15, 17) — the technical Jubilee-and-manumission word; cognate to Akkadian *andurarum*, the royal *anduraru* edicts of the Old Babylonian and Neo-Assyrian kings (e.g., Ammisaduqa, the Edict of Ammisaduqa c. 1646 BC, which canceled debts and freed debt-slaves); the *Mesha Stele* (mid-9th c. BC, Moabite Stone) uses the cognate term for a similar manumission. *karat berit* (כָּרַת בְּרִית, "cut a covenant," 34:13, 15, 18) — the technical phrase whose ritual etymology is exposed in the cut-calf ceremony. *qara' deror* (קָרָא דְרוֹר, "proclaim liberty," 34:8, 15, 17) — the same idiom of Lev 25:10 ("proclaim *deror* throughout the land"), the Jubilee phrase later quoted on the Liberty Bell. *eved 'ivri* (עֶבֶד עִבְרִי, "Hebrew slave," 34:9) — the technical legal category of Deut 15:12, distinguishing the brother-Israelite indentured servant from foreign chattel slavery.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: composite — biographical prophetic encounter (34:1–7), covenant-violation oracle with retrospective legal narration (34:8–22). Devices: *messenger formula* "Thus says the LORD" structuring the chapter (34:2, 4, 13, 17); *dual-fate prophecy* (34:2–5) — Zedekiah is told *both* "you shall not escape" (34:3) *and* "you shall die in peace" (34:5), a tension resolved by Jer 39:7 (Zedekiah's eyes are put out *after* he sees his sons killed, then he is taken to Babylon, where he presumably dies "in peace" relative to dying by the sword); *talionic punishment* (34:17) — *you have not obeyed me by proclaiming deror; behold, I proclaim deror to the sword, to pestilence, and to famine* — the same word used both for what was withheld and for the punishment, an inversion of the *jubilee-liberation* idiom into *cataclysmic-release*; the *cut-calf legal narration* (34:18) makes the ancient ritual visible as the shape of the curse.

The 34:18–19 list of those who passed between the calf parts is socially comprehensive: *the officials of Judah, the officials of Jerusalem, the eunuchs, the priests, and all the people of the land*. This list reads like a roster of every elite class in late-monarchic Jerusalem, and matches the post-fall list in Jer 52:10 of those killed by Nebuchadnezzar. The text is making a literary-prophetic claim: the political class that *cut the calf* will *be cut*.

MT-LXX: chapter 34 has one substantial divergence — verse 1 LXX lacks "and all the kingdoms of the earth under his dominion and all the peoples"; verses 18–22 are present in both but with minor differences. The chapter is largely stable across textual traditions, in contrast to chapter 33's massive divergence. Flag: 34:5 *as spices were burned for your fathers* — the practice of burning aromatics at royal funerals is attested at 2 Chr 16:14 (Asa) and 2 Chr 21:19 (Jehoram, where the spices are *not* burned, marking dishonor); the LXX preserves the same reading. Flag: 34:7 *Lachish and Azekah* — the Hebrew is unambiguous, and the Lachish ostracon parallel makes it one of the most historically anchored verses in the Hebrew Bible.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Place in canonical arc: the slave-release legislation (Exod 21:1–11, Lev 25, Deut 15) is given foundational covenantal status (34:13: *I myself made a covenant with your fathers when I brought them out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery*) and its violation is treated by Jeremiah as an attack on Israel's *founding identity*. The exodus narrative is structurally invoked: YHWH freed Israel from the *house of slavery* (*beit avadim*), and now Israel has become a *house of slavery* internally. Re-enslaving the freed is an inversion of the exodus.

Jewish trajectory: the Mishnah (*Kiddushin* 1:2, *Bava Qamma* 8) develops the rabbinic law of the Hebrew slave; the *eved 'ivri* category becomes complicated in the absence of the Jubilee (which the rabbis say was suspended after the exile of the trans-Jordanian tribes; cf. Bavli *Arakhin* 32b). **Rashi** on Jer 34 emphasizes the violation as a profanation of the divine name. **Radak** treats 34:5 ("you shall die in peace") as compatible with Zedekiah's blinded captivity in Babylon — *peace* meaning natural death. **Ibn Ezra** notes the Jubilee resonance. **Maimonides** in *Hilkhot Avadim* 1:7 codifies that anyone who buys a Hebrew slave and keeps him beyond the seventh year is in violation of an active commandment. **Abrabanel** reads the abrogation as the culminating act of the Judahite leadership before destruction. The **Liberty Bell** in Philadelphia (cast 1751) bears the inscription from Lev 25:10, "Proclaim liberty (*deror*) throughout the land unto all the inhabitants thereof" — the *deror* of Jeremiah 34 entered American civic consciousness through the Bell, which became an abolitionist symbol in the 1830s after William Lloyd Garrison's *Liberator* publicized it.

Christian trajectory: Patristic — **Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah*** (Hom. 14, partially extant) reads the slave-release/abrogation as a moral allegory of Christian backsliding. **Jerome** in his *Commentariorum* moralizes the cut calf as an image of those who break baptismal covenants. Medieval — **Aquinas** (*Summa* II-II, q. 100, a. 6) treats Jubilee-style manumission as belonging to the *judicial precepts* of the Old Law, abolished in the New (a position the Christian abolitionist tradition later contested). Reformation — **Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah*** (1563) reads chapter 34 as a sustained warning against political opportunism in religion: the Judahites repented under siege pressure, then reverted when pressure lifted. Critical — Duhm, Mowinckel, Holladay, Carroll. The chapter has been a key text in **liberation theology** (Gustavo Gutiérrez, *A Theology of Liberation*, 1971; Jon Sobrino) and in **Black liberation theology** (James Cone, *God of the Oppressed*, 1975). Christian abolitionists — Granville Sharp, William Wilberforce, Thomas Clarkson, Frederick Douglass, Sojourner Truth — drew explicitly on Jer 34 and Lev 25. Pro-slavery Christian theologians — **Robert Lewis Dabney** (*A Defense of Virginia and Through Her of the South*, 1867), **James Henley Thornwell** (*The Rights and the Duties of Masters*, 1850) — drew on Pauline household codes (Eph 6:5–9, Col 3:22–4:1, Philem) and argued that the *eved 'ivri* limitation in Jer 34 *implicitly authorized* permanent foreign chattel slavery. **Mark Noll** (*The Civil War as a Theological Crisis*, 2006) shows that the inability of nineteenth-century American Protestantism to resolve this exegetical conflict was a crisis of biblical authority, not a marginal political dispute. **Esau McCaulley** (*Reading While Black*, 2020) argues the abolitionist tradition's reading is exegetically superior because it reads the Old and New Testaments as a *single trajectory* against slavery culminating in Gal 3:28. **Willie Jennings** (*The Christian Imagination*, 2010) traces how the *failure* to read this trajectory produced the theological warrant for the Atlantic slave trade.

Where they diverge: Reformed natural-law jurisprudence (Calvin, Althusius) historically supported abolition through the *general equity* doctrine of the law (Westminster Confession 19.4). Catholic social teaching (*Sublimis Deus*, 1537; *In Plurimis*, 1888) condemned slavery in stages, drawing on natural law. Liberation theology reads the *deror* as a still-active divine demand for political-economic justice. Anabaptist and Hutterite tradition (Schleitheim Confession, 1527) refused civic participation but practiced internal community-of-goods, reading the *deror* as ecclesial rather than civic. Jewish reading (Maimonides, *Hilkhot Avadim*) preserves the *eved 'ivri* category as a halakhic category but treats the institution as effectively suspended in the absence of Jubilee.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Slavery and Scripture — the *eved 'ivri* limitation.** The Old Testament *both* assumes slavery as a normal social institution *and* contains the most radical anti-slavery legislation of the ANE (the *deror* of Lev 25, the *shemittah* of Deut 15, the *talion-deror* of Jer 34). The Christian abolitionist tradition reads this as a *trajectory*: the Hebrew Scriptures point beyond themselves toward the eschatological liberation announced in Isa 61 and quoted by Jesus in Luke 4:18 ("to proclaim liberty [*aphesin*, the LXX rendering of *deror*] to the captives"). The Christian pro-slavery tradition reads the *eved 'ivri* limitation as a *boundary*: only fellow-Israelites are subject to the seven-year release; foreign chattel slavery is permitted (Lev 25:44–46). The historical-critical reading agrees with the pro-slavery reading on the *original* meaning: Jeremiah's *deror* applied only to Hebrew slaves, and the chapter's outrage is that Judahites had enslaved fellow Judahites *as if they were foreigners*. Steelman the historical-critical position: the Bible's slave-release legislation is genuinely limited to in-group manumission and does not condemn foreign chattel slavery; reading it as a universal abolitionist mandate is a *theological development* in Christian and Jewish tradition that goes beyond what Jeremiah and Leviticus literally say. This is uncomfortable for modern readers but textually defensible. The reply (McCaulley, Cone, Wright): the Bible's legal materials are not a *static code* but a *moral trajectory*; the prophets press toward a *deror* that exceeds the legal code's letter (Isa 61, Jer 34, Luke 4); the New Testament radicalizes the trajectory by collapsing the in-group/out-group distinction (Gal 3:28). On this reading, *both* the literalist boundary-reading *and* the literalist trajectory-reading are exegetically defensible, but only the trajectory-reading produces moral coherence with the canonical whole. Where this leaves us: the question of what counts as *the* meaning of an ancient legal text — the original-historical meaning, the canonical meaning, or the morally-coherent trajectory meaning — is unavoidable, and the slave-release material in Jeremiah 34 is the place in Scripture where the question becomes most urgent.

**Tension 2: Covenant-cutting and the self-curse ritual.** Genesis 15:9–17 has YHWH *himself* (in the form of the smoking firepot and flaming torch) pass between the cut animals — a self-imposed curse: *may I become like these animals if I fail to keep my promise to Abraham*. Jeremiah 34:18–20 has the Judahite leadership pass between the cut calf — a self-imposed curse on themselves: *may we become like this calf if we break our covenant*. The text reports (34:21) that they will indeed be cut. New Testament covenant theology (Heb 9:15–22, esp. 9:18–22, "not even the first covenant was inaugurated without blood") draws on this material: the *new covenant* is *cut* in Christ's blood, which is to say, Christ *takes the self-curse upon himself* in the place of the covenant-breakers (Gal 3:13, "becoming a curse for us"). This produces three competing covenant theologies. Catholic *covenant-and-sacrament* theology (*Lumen Gentium*, *Sacrosanctum Concilium*) reads Christ's covenant-cutting as ongoing in the Eucharistic re-presentation. Reformed federalism (Westminster Confession 7) reads the covenant as a legal-juridical structure with Christ as the *covenant head* who fulfills the conditions and absorbs the curse. Anabaptist *believer's covenant* (Schleitheim) reads each individual's adult baptism as a personal covenant-cutting, with self-imposed accountability to the community. Jewish reading: *brit* is constitutive of Israel's identity; Genesis 15 and Jeremiah 34 are paradigmatic. Steelman the Anabaptist position: covenant is not an abstract legal structure but an embodied ritual commitment whose breach demands real consequences in the visible community — which is what Jer 34 dramatizes. Where this leaves us: the cut-calf ritual is one of the strangest images in Scripture and one of the most theologically generative; it produces both the self-curse logic of the Abrahamic covenant and the substitutionary logic of the cross.

**An unresolved question:** If the political class of Judah cut the calf and were cut, and if Christ took the self-curse onto himself, *what is the status of covenant-breakers in the New Covenant*? Are they *also* taken up into Christ's self-curse (the Reformed position), or do they remain under a covenant-breaking judgment that is ecclesially adjudicated (the Catholic and Anabaptist positions)? This is one of the live theological questions in contemporary discussion of church discipline, apostasy, and the perseverance of the saints.

## The Thread

The *deror* of 34:8, 15, 17 is the same word as Lev 25:10's Jubilee proclamation and Isa 61:1's "to proclaim liberty to the captives" — the verse Jesus reads at his inaugural sermon in Nazareth (Luke 4:18–19) and explicitly says "today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing." Jesus presents his ministry as a *deror* event: the Jeremiah 34 abrogation is *reversed* in Christ's proclamation of the year of the Lord's favor. The cut-calf ritual (34:18) draws on Gen 15:9–17 (the Abrahamic covenant) and feeds Heb 9:15–22 (the Christological covenant). The dual-fate prophecy of Zedekiah (34:3, 5) is fulfilled with extraordinary precision in Jer 39:5–7 and 52:8–11, which are the same event narrated twice in the canonical book — Zedekiah sees Nebuchadnezzar "eye to eye," then his sons are killed, then his eyes are put out, then he is taken to Babylon to die. The Lachish-Azekah detail (34:7) is independently confirmed by Lachish Letter IV — one of the most precise correlations between biblical prophecy and contemporary epigraphy in the entire Hebrew Bible. The slave-release theme threads through Philemon 16 ("no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, as a beloved brother") and Galatians 3:28 ("there is neither slave nor free… for you are all one in Christ Jesus") — the New Testament intensification of Jeremiah's outrage.
