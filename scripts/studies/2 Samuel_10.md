# 2 Samuel 10: Half a Beard and a Hired Army

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens with the death of **Nahash king of the Ammonites** and the succession of his son **Hanun**. The Ammonite throne sits at **Rabbah** — modern **Amman**, the capital of Jordan, where Iron Age remains have been excavated on the citadel hill (Jebel al-Qal'a). The Ammonite kingdom occupied the highlands between the Jabbok and Arnon rivers in central Transjordan, with Rabbah as its political center and a string of fortified towns guarding the trade routes east of the Jordan. The Ammonite Iron Age corpus includes the **Amman Citadel Inscription** (9th c. BCE) and a series of king-statues that demonstrate Ammonite political and artistic sophistication. The kingdom was a regional power throughout the Iron Age II until its absorption first by Assyria in the 8th c. BCE and finally by Babylon.

Nahash is the same king whose siege of Jabesh-gilead in 1 Samuel 11 launched Saul's career — there he threatened to gouge out the right eye of every man in Jabesh-gilead before Saul's spirit-empowered intervention. The chapter does not say when or how Nahash and David formed an alliance, but the divine clue at 10:2 — David's intent to deal *hesed* with Hanun "as his father dealt *hesed* with me" — implies that Nahash had aided David at some point during the Saulide-David war. Some scholars (P. Kyle McCarter, Anchor Bible) read the implied alliance as a counterweight to Saul: the king who threatened Saul's first political success aided Saul's eventual successor. Others (Walter Brueggemann, A. A. Anderson) note that the alliance is otherwise unrecorded and may be pure narrator inference. Either way, the chapter opens with David exercising the standard ANE royal protocol of condolence delegations to allied courts at moments of dynastic transition. Such delegations are well attested in Hittite, Assyrian, and Egyptian diplomatic correspondence — they are how Iron Age states maintained diplomatic continuity across royal generations.

The Ammonite princes' suspicion at 10:3 — "do you think, because David has sent comforters to you, that he is honoring your father? Has not David sent his servants to search the city and to spy it out and to overthrow it?" — is not paranoid invention. ANE diplomacy regularly used embassies as cover for intelligence gathering, and the Mari archive, the Amarna letters, and the Hittite vassal correspondence all preserve evidence of envoys functioning as spies. The princes' counsel to Hanun is bad politics — it manufactures the very war they fear — but it is rooted in real diplomatic suspicion. The chapter's irony is that Hanun's response (humiliating the envoys) creates the conflict that Hanun's princes claimed to be preventing.

The **humiliation of the envoys** at 10:4 is the chapter's most physically vivid moment. **"Hanun took David's servants and shaved off half their beards and cut off their garments in the middle, at their hips, and sent them away."** Both gestures are calculated symbolic violence with clear ANE meaning. **Beard-shaving** in ANE Near Eastern cultures (Hittite, Assyrian, Egyptian) was associated with mourning, slavery, and shame; an adult Israelite male's beard was a marker of dignity and status. Forcibly shaving half the beard — leaving the man visibly half-disgraced — was a calibrated insult: not full shame (full shaving) but partial disfigurement that would persist visibly until the beard regrew, marking the man as one who had been shamed in public. The **cutting of the garments at the hips** exposed the buttocks and possibly the genitals — the standard ANE shaming gesture against captives and defeated soldiers, well-attested in Assyrian palace reliefs (e.g., the Lachish reliefs from Sennacherib's palace at Nineveh, c. 700 BCE, depict Judahite captives stripped from the waist down). Both gestures together communicated: you are reduced to slave-shame; your manhood is publicly canceled; return home as a humiliation walking.

David's response at 10:5 — "Remain at Jericho until your beards have grown and then return" — is itself politically calibrated. The men cannot return to Jerusalem in their humiliated state without that humiliation rebounding on the king who sent them. **Jericho** in the Jordan Valley was a transitional space, neither fully home nor enemy territory, where the men could wait out the visible disgrace. The geographical detail is realistic; Iron Age Jericho was a small settlement at the time, attested archaeologically in the Iron Age IIA-B strata.

The military escalation that follows is the consequence of the diplomatic failure. **The Ammonites hire Aramaean coalition forces** — "the Aramaeans of Beth-rehob and Zobah, 20,000 foot soldiers, the king of Maacah with 1,000 men, and the men of Tob 12,000" (10:6). The geography is northern Transjordan and the Bekaa Valley: **Beth-rehob** (likely between the upper Jordan and Mount Hermon), **Zobah** (Aramaean kingdom in the Bekaa, the major regional power under **Hadadezer son of Rehob**), **Maacah** (small Aramaean kingdom in the Golan, possibly identified with the area around modern Quneitra), **Tob** (north of Gilead, location uncertain). The numerical totals (33,000 troops) are typical Iron Age narrative hyperbole; actual ANE armies were smaller, but the pattern of coalition warfare and mercenary hire is well-attested in the Mari letters, the Hittite military records, and the later Assyrian annals (Shalmaneser III's inscription on the battle of Qarqar in 853 BCE lists a similar Aramaean coalition against Assyria, including Israel under Ahab — direct continuity with the Aramaean coalition pattern of 2 Samuel 10).

**Joab's strategy** at 10:9-12 is the chapter's tactical centerpiece. Facing battle on two fronts (Ammonites at the city gate, Aramaeans in the open field behind), Joab divides his force: he takes the elite troops against the Aramaeans (the more formidable threat), gives the rest to **Abishai** (his brother) against the Ammonites, and pre-arranges mutual reinforcement. The "if the Aramaeans are too strong for me, then you shall help me, and if the Ammonites are too strong for you, then I will come and help you" (10:11) is ANE military doctrine — specifically the **mutual support tactic** in two-front engagements, attested in Hittite military texts and Assyrian battle narratives. Joab's accompanying speech — "be of good courage, and let us play the man for our people and for the cities of our God; and may YHWH do what seems good to him" (10:12) — is the chapter's most theologically loaded line. The combination of human resolve ("be courageous") with divine deference ("may YHWH do what seems good") is classical Israelite military theology: the battle is real and depends on courage, the outcome is YHWH's, and the warrior must do both at once. Compare Jonathan's similar formula at 1 Samuel 14:6 ("nothing can hinder YHWH from saving by many or by few") and Joab's later self-justification language. The line is one of the strongest theological statements Joab ever makes.

The Helam campaign in 10:15-19 expands the war into the Aramaean heartland. **Hadadezer** brings additional forces "from beyond the Euphrates" — Aramaean troops from the Khabur Valley and the upper Mesopotamian region (territories that Assyrian sources call **Bit-Adini** and **Bit-Bahiani**). The crossing of the Jordan and the engagement at **Helam** (location uncertain — possibly modern Alma in the Hauran, possibly further north) brings the campaign deep into Transjordanian and Aramaean territory. The numbers ("700 chariots, 40,000 horsemen") are again narratively hyperbolic — Iron Age chariot forces in this region typically numbered in the hundreds, not thousands, though the Battle of Qarqar inscription does record **2,000 chariots** for Ahab a century later. The chapter ends with the Aramaeans submitting to David and the Ammonites isolated, setting up the chapter 11-12 siege of Rabbah.

A few Hebrew terms transform the chapter. ***Hesed*** (חֶסֶד) — already encountered as Samuel's keyword — appears twice in 10:2 in David's own speech: "I will deal *hesed* with Hanun the son of Nahash, as his father dealt *hesed* with me." The vocabulary of covenant loyalty is being extended across an international diplomatic relationship, not just kinship or covenant within Israel. ***Ba'ash*** (בָּאַשׁ), "to stink, to become a stench," is what the Ammonites recognize they have become to David at 10:6. The verb is used in the Hebrew Bible of literal stench (Exodus 7:18 of the Nile, 1 Samuel 13:4 of Saul becoming "a stench" to the Philistines, 27:12 of David becoming a stench to the Israelites) and figuratively of irreversible diplomatic rupture. The Ammonites recognize the gesture is unforgivable. ***Zaqan*** (זָקָן), "beard," is the chapter's most concrete keyword, repeated twice (10:4, 10:5). The chapter is structured around the symbol of the masculine face publicly disfigured. ***Madon*** (מָדוֹן), "garment," is what is cut. Together: face and dress, the two surfaces of public male identity in Iron Age Israel, both forcibly disfigured. ***Yad*** (יָד), "hand," in the strategic dispositions of Joab is the standard military idiom (Joab "puts" his men into Abishai's "hand" — into his command).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is a tight three-movement narrative: the diplomatic insult (10:1-5), the Ammonite-Aramaean war (10:6-14), the Helam campaign (10:15-19). The structure mirrors the political logic — an act of personal insult escalates into regional war.

The chapter functions in the larger Samuel narrative as **the structural setup for chapters 11-12**. The Ammonite war it begins is the war during which David remains in Jerusalem ("at the time when kings go out to battle, David sent Joab" — 11:1) and during which the Bathsheba episode occurs. The siege of Rabbah that begins in 11:1 and concludes in 12:26-31 is the same Ammonite war whose origins this chapter narrates. The literary effect is significant: the reader who arrives at chapter 11's "but David remained at Jerusalem" already knows what war is being fought, against what enemy, at what stake. Chapter 10 is doing setup work that chapter 11 will exploit.

Within the chapter, the literary craft is most concentrated in two places. First, **the diplomatic insult sequence** (10:1-5) is structured as a triple irony: David sends *hesed*, Hanun's princes interpret it as espionage, Hanun acts on the misinterpretation, and the misinterpretation manufactures the espionage threat that wasn't there. The narrator's restraint — refusing to tell us whether David's intent was actually pure or whether the princes' suspicion had any basis — is itself the storytelling. Robert Alter (*The David Story*) and **Meir Sternberg** (*Poetics of Biblical Narrative*) both note this kind of narrator-managed ambiguity. The diplomatic mistake is a study in how political relationships can collapse through misreading.

Second, **Joab's speech at 10:12** is the chapter's literary high point. The combination of three short imperatives ("be strong, and let us be courageous for our people and for the cities of our God") with one open-ended deference ("may YHWH do what seems good to him") is rhythmically tight and theologically dense. The same Joab who will murder Amasa, who will kill Absalom against David's orders, who will be executed by Solomon at the altar — here speaks with theological clarity that the rest of the canon will rarely grant him. The chapter is the one place where Joab is something other than the political fixer.

The Ammonite-Aramaean coalition war is structured by **two-front-then-pursuit** narrative pattern: division of forces, mutual support pact, the more dangerous front engaged first, the secondary front collapses when the primary collapses, both wings flee, no pursuit because Joab returns to Jerusalem (10:14b — perhaps because of the seasonal close of the campaign year, perhaps because the chapter is hurrying toward its real subject). Then the Aramaean reinforcement and the Helam campaign repeat the pattern at larger scale: Aramaean reinforcement, Israelite mobilization, decisive battle, vassal submission. The chapter's military narrative is competent and condensed.

The chapter functions as part of the **summary war narrative** of 2 Samuel 8 and 10 — both chapters compress what must have been multi-year campaigns into brief reports. The Ammonite war specifically is treated more fully because it is the framing context for chapters 11-12; the Aramaean wars are treated more briefly because they are not the chapter's real subject.

There are no major textual variants of significance in the chapter. **4QSam-a** preserves fragments consistent with MT. The LXX has minor smoothings. The parallel at **1 Chronicles 19** is largely identical with minor numerical variations (1 Chron 19:18 reads "7,000 charioteers" where 2 Sam 10:18 reads "700 chariots"; 1 Chron 19:7 specifies "32,000 chariots and the king of Maacah and his people" where 2 Sam 10:6 has the more diversified coalition). The Chronicler's version is generally inflated; the Samuel version is closer to what most critical scholars (McCarter, A.A. Anderson, Sara Japhet on Chronicles) take as the older tradition.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, 2 Samuel 10 sits at the structural threshold between David's empire-building (chapter 8 is the summary war report; chapter 10 is the war-in-detail) and David's personal moral collapse (chapters 11-12). The chapter functions as the bridge: it establishes the war that creates the conditions for David's failure. The narrator places it deliberately so that the reader who knows what comes next reads the Ammonite war already framed by the Bathsheba episode it surrounds.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Josephus in *Antiquities* 7.114-129 retells the chapter at length, expanding the diplomatic exchanges and making the Ammonite princes' counsel more elaborate. The Talmud (Sanhedrin 96a, Niddah 47a) treats the chapter mainly for its incidental halakhic content; the rabbinic tradition is more interested in chapters 11-12 than in the war that frames them. **Rashi** glosses the chapter philologically; he attends carefully to the symbolic violence of the half-beard and the cut garments, treating the Ammonite gesture as the kind of irreversible insult that no reasonable diplomatic relationship could survive. **Radak** (David Kimhi) develops the geography of the Aramaean kingdoms and notes the diplomatic protocols. **Ralbag** (Gersonides) extracts moral-political *to'aliyot* about the dangers of bad counselors (Hanun's princes) and the necessity of military readiness in international relations. **Abarbanel** treats the chapter as a case study in how kingly *hesed* can be misread by foreign courts, and in how the king must respond to such misreading without sacrificing the dignity of his envoys. **Tikva Frymer-Kensky** notes that the chapter is one of the few that gives Joab a clearly admirable speech; **Yair Zakovitch** attends to the chapter's structural function as the framing of the Bathsheba narrative.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic Christianity treated the chapter mostly for its incidental moral lessons. Origen's homiletic fragments use Joab's speech ("may YHWH do what seems good to him") as a model of Christian providence-language. Augustine in *City of God* 17 mentions the Ammonite war briefly as part of David's empire-building but moves quickly to chapters 11-12. **Ambrose** in *De Officiis* uses the diplomatic insult as a study in how *injuria* (insult) can be more politically destructive than direct violence, and Joab's speech as a model of military piety. **Chrysostom**'s homilies on 2 Samuel develop the contrast between the diplomatic mistake (Hanun) and the diplomatic restraint (David sending the men to Jericho). **Gregory the Great** in the *Moralia* uses the half-beard motif allegorically — those who do not honor God's messengers shame themselves before the world. The medieval *Glossa Ordinaria* compiles the moral readings.

The Reformation shifted register. **Calvin's sermons on 2 Samuel** treat the chapter as a study in the proper exercise of royal *hesed* and the dangers of bad counsel. The Hanun episode becomes a case study in how rulers should evaluate the advisers around them. **Luther's** lectures attend more to the typological reading. The post-Reformation Pietist and Puritan tradition (Matthew Henry) develops the moral reading at length.

The critical period (Wellhausen, Noth, Gunkel) treated the chapter mainly for its political-historical content: the Ammonite-Aramaean coalition is read as evidence for the early monarchic period's regional politics. The Rost (1926) framework places the chapter at the start of the Court History. **McCarter's** Anchor Bible commentary develops the chapter as the structural setup for the Bathsheba narrative. **A. A. Anderson's** WBC commentary on 2 Samuel treats the chapter in detail. **Baruch Halpern's *David's Secret Demons*** reads the war narrative politically — the Aramaean campaigns are early Israelite imperial expansion, and the apologetic framing protects David from accusations of unprovoked aggression. **Steven McKenzie's *King David: A Biography*** offers similar analysis. **Brueggemann** holds the diplomatic and military analyses together with attention to the chapter's theological framing in Joab's speech. The contemporary turn includes feminist readings (the chapter's silence about women in war), postcolonial readings (Musa Dube on the imperial expansion), and military-historical readings (the Hittite parallels for two-front tactics).

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Both traditions read Joab's speech as theologically significant — the rare moment of theological clarity from the chapter's most morally compromised character. They diverge on how to weight the apologetic framing: traditional readings generally accept the chapter's surface narrative of justified Israelite response to Ammonite insult, while contemporary critical scholarship reads the chapter as part of the History of David's Rise / Court History's larger apologetic for Davidic imperialism. The shift is again from one mode of theological reading to another, both of which can hold Joab's speech as theologically real while reading the war narrative with different historical-critical eyes.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author believe God was doing in this chapter? The theological argument is the most condensed in Joab's speech at 10:12: human courage and divine deference held together. "Be strong, and let us be courageous for our people and for the cities of our God; and may YHWH do what seems good to him." The chapter's theology is operative rather than discursive — it is in the speech of the warrior preparing to fight, not in any narrator's commentary.

**Tension One: The diplomatic mistake — was Hanun's response paranoia or pattern recognition?** The chapter presents Hanun's princes as misreading David's *hesed* as espionage. But the History of David's Rise and the Court History are themselves apologetic narratives that present David's intentions in the best possible light. How should we evaluate the princes' suspicion?

The traditional reading (rabbinic, patristic, Reformation) takes the narrator's framing at face value. David's *hesed* was real; the princes' suspicion was paranoid; Hanun's response was both diplomatically and morally wrong. The chapter is a case study in how bad counsel destroys good relationships.

The contemporary critical reading (Halpern, McKenzie) reads the princes' suspicion more sympathetically. ANE diplomacy regularly used embassies as cover for intelligence gathering; David was building a regional empire; the princes were doing what royal advisers were supposed to do. Their counsel was tactically wrong (it manufactured the war they feared) but not unreasonable as a reading of the regional pattern. The chapter is then a case study in how imperial expansion creates the diplomatic distrust that leads to wars.

A mediating position holds both. David's *hesed* may have been sincere; the princes' suspicion may have been pattern recognition; both can be true; the diplomatic system can fail when sincere overture meets reasonable suspicion.

The position to steelman is the critical reading. The traditional reading underestimates how regional powers actually evaluated each other. ANE diplomatic correspondence (the Mari letters, the Amarna correspondence) is full of examples of overt friendly delegations that were also intelligence operations. Hanun's princes were not foolish or paranoid; they were applying standard diplomatic skepticism to a king whose regional ambitions were obvious.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter's diplomatic crisis is overdetermined. David's *hesed* and Hanun's princes' suspicion are both intelligible within the political grammar of the Iron Age Levant. The text refuses to specify whether the suspicion was justified, and the refusal is part of the theological argument: the chapter is interested in how diplomatic relationships fail, not in adjudicating whose fault the failure was.

**Tension Two: Joab's speech — operative theology or pious cover?** The speech at 10:12 is one of the strongest theological statements Joab makes in Samuel. He combines human resolve with divine deference in a formula that has been quoted in liturgy and military theology for centuries.

The traditional reading takes Joab's speech as genuine. He may be politically opaque elsewhere, but at this moment he speaks with theological seriousness. The Reformation tradition (Calvin) treats the speech as a model of how a soldier should approach battle — neither presuming on God nor abandoning effort.

The contemporary reading is more divided. Brueggemann holds that Joab is genuinely theologically articulate here even if morally compromised elsewhere — the same person can speak truly about God and act badly in the same hour. Halpern reads the speech more skeptically — Joab is a master rhetorician who knows what his soldiers need to hear. McKenzie holds something similar.

The Catholic and Reformed Protestant traditions both take the speech as a paradigm for the proper relationship between human action and divine sovereignty. Aquinas's *Summa* (II-II q. 64 on the conditions of legitimate war) cites similar language; the just-war tradition has used the formula as a model for the soldier's moral disposition. The Reformed Westminster Confession's account of providence (V) operates with similar language.

The position to steelman is the skeptical reading. Joab's record in Samuel makes it hard to read the speech as straightforward piety: he murders Abner, kills Amasa, kills Absalom against orders. To attribute genuine theological conviction to him here requires either a developmental account (he changed) or a dissociated account (he could speak truly even when acting badly). The skeptical reading takes seriously that Joab is the chapter's least trustworthy character and reads the speech as masterful battlefield rhetoric.

**Where this leaves us:** The speech is theologically substantive whether Joab means it sincerely or not. The formula — courage plus deference — can be true even on the lips of a morally compromised speaker. The text leaves the question of Joab's interior unresolved and asks the reader to assess the speech on its own terms.

The unresolved question this chapter leaves us with: When the same person can speak the deepest truth about God in the morning and commit murder in the afternoon, what does it mean to receive their theology — to learn from what they say while not absolving what they do?

## The Thread

**1 Samuel 11.** Nahash king of Ammon's siege of Jabesh-gilead launched Saul's career; the hesed-relationship implied at 2 Sam 10:2 between Nahash and David is one of the chapter's interpretive puzzles. When you reread 1 Samuel 11, remember that Nahash who threatened Saul's people seems to have aided David's flight — the politics of the Iron Age Transjordan ran in patterns the canonical narrative does not always make explicit.

**2 Samuel 11:1.** "In the spring of the year, the time when kings go out to battle, David sent Joab... but David remained at Jerusalem." The famous opening of the Bathsheba narrative depends on the reader knowing what war Joab is fighting. Chapter 10 establishes that war: the Ammonite-Aramaean coalition that David's army is now engaging at Rabbah. When you read 11:1, the war already has a history — and David's absence from it is set against the chapter 10 narrative of his army's actual campaign.

**1 Samuel 14:6.** Jonathan's "nothing can hinder YHWH from saving by many or by few" is the closest Samuel parallel to Joab's "may YHWH do what seems good to him." Both speeches combine military readiness with deference to divine outcome. When you reread 1 Samuel 14, hear Joab's chapter 10 speech in dialogue with Jonathan's — two warriors of different moral character speaking the same theological grammar.

**2 Samuel 12:26-31.** The Rabbah siege that begins in chapter 11 ends in 12:26-31, where Joab takes the city and summons David to lead the final entry (so that the city would not be named after Joab — a courteous political gesture). When you read the Rabbah conclusion, remember that the war that ends there began here, with a half-shaved beard and a cut garment, and ran through David's worst moral failure.
