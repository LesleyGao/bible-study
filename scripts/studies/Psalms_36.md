# Psalm 36 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 36 carries the superscription *lamnatzeach le-eved-Yahweh le-David* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ לְעֶבֶד־יְהוָה לְדָוִד, "to the choirmaster, of David, the servant of the LORD") — a notation rare in its construction. Only Psalm 18 shares the formula *eved Yahweh* ("servant of the LORD") in its heading. The phrase is not casual: in Hebrew Bible usage, *eved Yahweh* functions as a covenantal title borne by Moses (Deut 34:5), Joshua (Josh 24:29), and supremely the Suffering Servant of Isaiah 52-53 (*ebed*). Its presence over a Davidic psalm signals a piety self-understood as service rather than mere petition.

Form-critically the psalm resists clean classification. Hermann Gunkel, in his foundational *Einleitung in die Psalmen* (1933), classified individual psalms by *Gattung* (literary type) and *Sitz im Leben* (life-setting): hymn, individual lament, individual thanksgiving, royal psalm, wisdom psalm. Psalm 36 confounds the schema. Verses 1-4 read as wisdom-reflection on the *rasha* (רָשָׁע, "wicked"); verses 5-9 are pure hymnic praise of YHWH's *hesed* (חֶסֶד, "steadfast love"); verses 10-12 close with petition and imprecation. Gunkel called such hybrids *Mischgedichten* (mixed compositions). Sigmund Mowinckel, in *The Psalms in Israel's Worship* (1962), tied many to the autumn New Year festival; Hans-Joachim Kraus's *Psalmen* (BKAT) and the Hossfeld-Zenger commentary in the *Hermeneia* series treat Psalm 36 as a meditation that begins with wisdom-observation and rises to hymn — Goldingay (in his three-volume *Psalms*, BCOTWP) accepts the hybridity matter-of-factly: "the psalm comprises three quite different sections."

Dating is contested. The wisdom-vocabulary (*sakal*, "act wisely," v. 3) and the contemplative cadence have led some, including Mitchell Dahood (*Psalms*, Anchor Bible), to suggest a post-exilic sapiential setting. Others — Franz Delitzsch, Allen Ross — defend the Davidic ascription on lexical and structural grounds. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of Psalm 36 in 4QPsa and 4QPsc; the text aligns closely with the Masoretic tradition. The Septuagint (Psalm 35 in LXX numbering) translates *ne'um pesha* (the opening) as *phēsin ho paranomos* — "the lawless one says" — flattening the prophetic-oracle resonance.

The opening line is one of the most unusual in the Psalter: *ne'um pesha la-rasha be-kereb libbi* (נְאֻם־פֶּשַׁע לָרָשָׁע בְּקֶרֶב לִבִּי, "transgression speaks to the wicked deep in his heart"). The word *ne'um* is the prophetic-oracle formula — *ne'um Yahweh*, "oracle of the LORD," appears 365 times in Jeremiah alone. Here, with biting irony, it is *transgression* that issues the oracle, and the recipient is the wicked man's *own* heart. The Masoretic text reads *be-kereb libbi* ("in *my* heart") — a striking textual difficulty. Many manuscripts and the LXX, Syriac, and Targum read *libbo* ("his heart"), and most modern translations follow that reading. The MT's first-person pronoun, however, may preserve a deliberate strangeness: the psalmist hears the wicked's interior oracle as if it spoke within him.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels illuminate but do not exhaust the imagery. The "fountain of life" (*meqor chayim*, מְקוֹר חַיִּים) of v. 9 has Mesopotamian and Ugaritic antecedents — the Sumerian *ME* of life-giving waters, the Ugaritic *mbk nhrm* ("source of the rivers") at El's dwelling. The "shadow of your wings" (*tzel kenafekha*, צֵל כְּנָפֶיךָ) in v. 7 echoes both the cherubim over the ark and the protective-deity iconography common from Egyptian Isis to Persian winged sun-disks. The psalm Israelizes the imagery: it is *YHWH's* light by which we see light, *YHWH's* river of delights from which the faithful drink.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Robert Lowth's eighteenth-century discovery of *parallelismus membrorum* — the patterned correspondence between cola in Hebrew verse — finds a textbook display here. The synonymous parallelism of v. 5 is exemplary:

- "Your steadfast love (*hasdekha*), O LORD, extends to the heavens / your faithfulness (*emunatkha*) to the clouds."

*Hesed* and *emunah* are paired across the colon-break; *heavens* and *clouds* form a vertical merism reaching beyond the visible. Verse 6 escalates the parallelism into a four-fold structure (*righteousness / mountains*, *judgments / great deep*) before resolving with a universal claim: *adam u-vehemah toshia Yahweh* ("man and beast you save, O LORD") — the most expansive soteriological line in the Psalter, gathering the animal world into divine concern.

Strophic structure organizes the whole. Most modern commentators (Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate) divide the psalm into three movements:

- **Strophe 1 (vv. 1-4):** the interior oracle of the wicked — wisdom-observation in the indicative mood.
- **Strophe 2 (vv. 5-9):** the cosmic *hesed* of YHWH — hymnic praise rising in cosmic register (heavens, clouds, mountains, great deep).
- **Strophe 3 (vv. 10-12):** prayer and imprecation — petition for continuance of *hesed*, prayer against the foot of arrogance, and a closing perfect-tense vision of the evildoers fallen.

The middle strophe is structurally and theologically the heart. It builds in concentric movement: outer cosmos (heavens, clouds, mountains, great deep) → divine house (*beytekha*, v. 8) → inner sanctuary of light (v. 9). The structure mirrors the temple itself — outer court, holy place, holy of holies — and reads as a theological topography of *hesed*.

The triple repetition of *hesed* (vv. 5, 7, 10) functions as a verbal *inclusio* binding the central hymn to the closing petition. The wicked of vv. 1-4 has *no fear of God before his eyes*; the righteous of vv. 5-9 take refuge *in the shadow of your wings* — a deliberate antithetical structure pivoting on the question: what does one *see*?

The climax of v. 9 — *ki immekha meqor chayim be-orkha nireh-or* (כִּי־עִמְּךָ מְקוֹר חַיִּים בְּאוֹרְךָ נִרְאֶה־אוֹר, "for with you is the fountain of life; in your light we see light") — exhibits chiasmus and assonance simultaneously. The repetition *or … or* ("light … light") and the bracketing of *immekha* / *be-orkha* ("with you … in your light") create a compact theological epigram. There is no *selah* in this psalm — its progression is unbroken meditation rather than liturgical staging.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Within Book I of the Psalter (Pss 1-41), Psalm 36 sits between two laments (35 and 38) and serves as a meditative pause — Patrick Miller (*Interpreting the Psalms*) reads its placement as compositional respite, a hymnic island amid the Davidic struggles. Gerald Wilson's *The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* (1985) and the canonical-criticism school treat such placements as deliberate.

**Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 36 elaborates v. 9 — "in your light we see light" — as a teaching about the *or ha-ganuz*, the "hidden light" of creation reserved for the righteous in the world-to-come. Rashi reads v. 1 as the wicked's self-deceiving inner voice, glossing *ne'um pesha* as the "thought" (*machshavah*) that addresses the wicked in his innermost being. Ibn Ezra concentrates on the lexical strangeness of *be-kereb libbi*, accepting the MT's first-person and reading it as the psalmist's vivid empathic insight. Radak harmonizes the textual variants, treating both as legitimate. Nachmanides, more mystically, sees the *or* of v. 9 as the divine emanation that grants the soul its capacity for vision.

In modern Jewish scholarship Martin Buber, in *Right and Wrong: An Interpretation of Some Psalms* (1952), reads Psalm 36 as a meditation on the contrast between the wicked's interior monologue and the divine-cosmic immensity that breaks in upon the faithful. Abraham Joshua Heschel (in *The Prophets* and *Man Is Not Alone*) cites v. 9 as a biblical anchor for what he calls "radical amazement" — the disclosure that life and light are gifts of presence. Jon Levenson's *Sinai and Zion* and *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* situate the psalm within Israel's covenantal cosmology, where *hesed* is not abstract benevolence but treaty-loyalty cosmically extended. Nahum Sarna (*Songs of the Heart*) emphasizes the *meqor chayim* image as a deliberate theological counter to ANE fertility-spring mythologies. Avivah Zornberg's literary readings, particularly in *The Murmuring Deep*, would highlight the psychological strangeness of the opening "oracle of transgression" — the wicked's heart speaking like a prophet to itself.

Liturgically, Psalm 36 is recited in some Jewish traditions on the eve of Yom Kippur and is featured in the *Pesukei de-Zimra* in expanded Sephardic morning rites; v. 9 (*ki immekha meqor chayim*) appears prominently in Sabbath table-songs.

**Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 36 (Latin numbering 35) is one of his most theologically dense expositions. He reads v. 9 — *in lumine tuo videbimus lumen* — as a key text for what he calls the *illuminatio* of the mind: the inner light of God enables all genuine perception of truth, a cornerstone of his epistemology in *De Trinitate* and *De Magistro*. Athanasius, in his *Letter to Marcellinus*, recommends Psalm 36 for the soul tempted to despair at the prosperity of the wicked. Cassiodorus (*Expositio Psalmorum*) reads the three strophes as *vituperatio impii*, *laudatio divina*, and *deprecatio* — vituperation, praise, prayer.

Thomas Aquinas, in his *Postilla super Psalmos*, treats v. 9 as a touchstone for the doctrine of divine illumination, later developed in the *Summa Theologiae* (I, q. 12) on the beatific vision. Bonaventure's *Itinerarium Mentis in Deum* (1259) cites the verse as the scriptural ground for the soul's ascent through created light to uncreated Light. Eastern Orthodox theology — Gregory Palamas in the fourteenth century, drawing on the Cappadocians and Symeon the New Theologian — anchors its doctrine of the *uncreated light* of Tabor in this very verse: in God's light, not a created intermediary, the saints see light.

Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* (1519-1521) treats Psalm 36 as a portrait of self-deceived sin (vv. 1-4) and gospel comfort (vv. 5-9): the wicked's heart preaches damnation; God's *hesed* preaches salvation extending to the heavens. Calvin's 1557 commentary emphasizes the universal scope of v. 6's "man and beast you save" — divine providence as the secure foundation of cosmic order. Among modern critical interpreters: Gunkel notes the wisdom-and-hymn fusion; Mowinckel reads the hymnic core as cultic; Westermann's *Praise and Lament in the Psalms* treats vv. 5-9 as descriptive praise par excellence; Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate, Wenham (*The Psalter Reclaimed*), Walter Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*), Patrick Miller, William P. Brown (*Seeing the Psalms*), Beth Tanner, and Susan Gillingham (*Psalms Through the Centuries*) all engage v. 9 as a peak of psalmic theology of light.

**NT use.** While Psalm 36 is not directly cited in the New Testament, John 4:14 ("the water that I will give him will become in him a spring of water welling up to eternal life") and John 8:12 ("I am the light of the world") draw deeply from the imagery of vv. 8-9. Revelation 22:1's "river of the water of life" closes the canon with the same picture.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: A wisdom-and-hymn hybrid — does the structure cohere?** The form-critical question is whether vv. 1-4, vv. 5-9, and vv. 10-12 belong together. (a) **Compositional unity (Goldingay, Hossfeld-Zenger, Tate):** the strophes form a deliberate triptych — the wicked's interior darkness, the cosmic light of *hesed*, and the prayer that the worshipper's life come under that light rather than that darkness. (b) **Editorial composite (Gunkel, some Mowinckelians):** vv. 1-4 may have circulated as wisdom-fragment and vv. 5-9 as a hymn, joined liturgically. (c) **Liturgical-dramatic reading (Brueggemann):** the psalm enacts the *journey* of the worshipper *out of* the world the wicked imagines and *into* the world *hesed* makes — the sequence is the theology.

The strongest case for compositional unity (its steelman): the antithesis between *no fear of God before his eyes* (v. 1) and *in your light we see light* (v. 9) is too tight to be accidental. The wicked sees nothing because he refuses to see; the righteous sees because YHWH gives the light by which seeing is possible. The whole psalm pivots on the verb of perception. To split the strophes is to miss the argument.

**Tension 2: The divine-light theology — what kind of vision is meant?** Verse 9 has carried more theological weight than perhaps any other verse in the Psalter. (a) **Eastern Orthodox uncreated-light reading (Palamas, hesychast tradition):** the light *of* God is God's own *energeia*, uncreated, granted to deified humanity in mystical vision. (b) **Augustinian illumination (Augustine, Bonaventure, much of Western mysticism):** the verse grounds an epistemology — God is the inner light by which the mind perceives truth at all. (c) **Reformed covenantal reading (Calvin, Goldingay):** the verse is metaphorical praise of YHWH as life-source and revelation-giver, without committing to a particular metaphysics of vision. (d) **Jewish *or ha-ganuz* reading (*Midrash Tehillim*, Nachmanides):** the light is creation's primordial light, hidden for the righteous in the age to come — eschatological rather than mystical.

The steelman for the Augustinian reading: Augustine takes the verse at the maximum level of seriousness. He notices that the verse does not say "in your light *you* see" or "in your light *we shine*," but "in your light *we see light*." The light is at once medium and object. This is precisely the structure of any theology of revelation: God is what is seen, *and* what makes seeing possible. The verse refuses to let revelation be a transaction; it makes revelation a participation.

Where this leaves us: Psalm 36 confronts us with two interior worlds. In one, transgression speaks oracles to itself, and the eyes go blind through the heart's refusal. In the other, *hesed* extends to the heavens, *adam u-vehemah* are saved together, and the worshipper drinks from a river not his own. The psalm does not argue between these worlds. It shows them, and asks which one we live inside. The closing imprecation — *let not the foot of arrogance come upon me* — is the prayer of someone who knows that *both* possibilities remain live for him.

**Closing question:** When the psalmist says "in your light we see light," is he claiming that *any* genuine seeing — moral, intellectual, aesthetic — is a participation in God's own self-disclosure, or only that the worshipper sees God through God? And if the former: what does that imply about the wicked of vv. 1-4, who do see, but see falsely?

## The Thread

- **Psalm 1:** the *ashrei* / *rasha* contrast that frames Book I; Psalm 36's wicked is the man not blessed, whose plotting (v. 4) is the inverse of meditating on Torah day and night.
- **Psalm 27:1:** "the LORD is my light and my salvation" — the same *or*-theology in lament register; Ps 36:9 in hymnic register.
- **Psalm 73:** the lived problem Ps 36 names but does not develop — the prosperity of the wicked who *seem* to flourish; Asaph will go where Psalm 36 does not.
- **John 1:4-9 / Revelation 22:1-5:** the canonical resolution of the *or* and *meqor chayim* imagery — life-light and river-of-life converge in the Lamb.
