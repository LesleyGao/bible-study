# Ezekiel 43: The Return of the Glory

## Lens 1 — Historical World

Ezekiel 43 is the theological climax of the temple-vision unit of chapters 40–48 and arguably of the entire book. The dating in 40:1 places the vision in 573 BC, fourteen years after Jerusalem's destruction. Where chapters 40–42 measured an empty temple, room by room, with the silence of an absent owner, chapter 43 fills it. The kavod — the divine glory that had departed eastward over the Mount of Olives in the harrowing vision of 11:22–23, leaving the city to its judgment — returns from the same direction and re-enters by the east gate. "Behold, the glory of the God of Israel was coming from the east. And the sound of his coming was like the sound of many waters, and the earth shone with his glory" (43:2). The architectural tour reveals its purpose at this moment: the building was being prepared for an inhabitant.

Moshe Greenberg's Anchor Bible commentary stresses the precise reversal Ezekiel constructs: in 11:23 the kavod went "to the mountain that is on the east side of the city"; in 43:2 it comes "from the east" and re-enters by the east gate. The departure-line and the return-line are the same line, traversed in opposite directions. Greenberg reads this as Ezekiel's signature theological move — the catastrophe of the exile is not simply reversed; it is symmetrically undone, and the geometry of judgment becomes the geometry of restoration. Daniel Block's NICOT commentary develops the connection between 43:2's "sound of many waters" and 1:24's description of the cherubim's wings ("a sound like many waters, like the sound of the Almighty"), arguing that the prophet is given to recognize, by sound before by sight, that this is the same kavod he saw at his inaugural vision by the Chebar canal — a deliberate inclusio that frames the entire book.

Walther Zimmerli (Hermeneia) treats 43:1–12 as a redactional masterpiece, weaving the older throne-vision tradition of chapter 1 with the newer temple-vision tradition of chapters 40–42, the seam visible in v. 3's explicit cross-reference: "And the vision I saw was just like the vision that I had seen when he came to destroy the city, and just like the vision that I had seen by the Chebar canal." Zimmerli argues that the redactor's hand here is theological as well as literary: by binding the inaugural vision, the destruction-vision (chapters 8–11), and the restoration-vision into a single sequence, the prophet's career is given a narrative arc with kavod-departure as the turning point and kavod-return as the resolution.

Jon Levenson's *Theology of the Program of Restoration of Ezekiel 40–48* (1976) is the foundational modern study and treats chapter 43 as the chapter that *programs* the rest of the program. The promise of v. 7 — "this is the place of my throne and the place of the soles of my feet, where I will dwell in the midst of the people of Israel forever" — is, on Levenson's reading, the eschatological foundation that the rest of the architectural and territorial vision presupposes. Without this promise, the measurements of the previous three chapters and the territorial allotments of the next five would be merely intricate; with it, they are the working-out of an eternal indwelling. Kalinda Stevenson's *The Vision of Transformation* (1996) extends Levenson's reading by arguing that the kavod's return *transforms space itself* — the temple is not a container into which the kavod enters but a topography that becomes holy by virtue of being stood upon. Steven Tuell's *The Law of the Temple in Ezekiel 40–48* (1992) emphasizes the legal-cultic content of vv. 13–27, treating the altar instructions and the seven-day consecration ritual as a Persian-period cultic constitution that re-grounds Israel's worship after exile.

The comparative material is again rich. The Mishnah Middot describes the altar of the Second Temple in terms that intersect Ezekiel 43:13–17 — square, with horns, with steps facing east — though the dimensions differ. The Temple Scroll from Qumran (11QTemple, columns 12–13) prescribes altar measurements that are closer to Ezekiel's than to historical practice, suggesting that the Qumran community took 43:13–17 as authoritative for the eschatological altar. Maimonides in *Hilkhot Beit ha-Bechirah* discusses the altar's measurements and adjudicates between Ezekiel's specifications and the older Mosaic tradition, again giving Ezekiel priority for the eschatological future. The wider Ancient Near Eastern background — the Mesopotamian temple-foundation rituals, the seven-day consecration of pagan altars, the recurring motif of the deity's installation in the newly-built temple — provides typological parallels but Ezekiel's distinctive contribution is the binding of installation to *return*, presupposing the prior departure.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The literary form of Ezekiel 43 is a deliberate fusion of three Ezekielian genres. The opening vision-report (vv. 1–5) borrows from the throne-vision genre of chapter 1; the divine speech (vv. 6–12) is in the prophetic-disputation form Ezekiel has used throughout (the rebuke of Israel's past defilements, the conditional offer of restoration); the altar instructions (vv. 13–27) are in the cultic-prescription genre of Leviticus, and Tuell argues that this section is consciously written to read like Leviticus 8 (the consecration of Aaron and his sons). Block calls the chapter "the most generically diverse" in the temple-vision unit and reads the diversity as a deliberate signal that this chapter is the pivot — vision-report becomes divine-speech becomes cultic-prescription, the prophet's experience giving way to YHWH's instruction giving way to the ongoing life of the cult.

The chapter's most powerful literary moment is vv. 1–5. The prose accelerates: "Then he led me to the gate, the gate facing east. And behold, the glory of the God of Israel was coming from the east. And the sound of his coming was like the sound of many waters, and the earth shone with his glory." Three sensory channels open simultaneously — sight (the glory coming, the earth shining), sound (the many waters), and the prophet's bodily response (he falls on his face). The tour-narrative pace of the previous three chapters, with their patient cubit-counting, breaks open into a moment of revelation that is sensorially overwhelming. Greenberg notes the shift in Hebrew sentence rhythm: the long measured clauses of chapters 40–42 give way in 43:2 to short paratactic units, the prose mimicking the rapid succession of perceptions.

The naming-formula returns in v. 7, this time spoken not by the bronze-skinned guide but by YHWH from inside the temple: "Son of man, this is the place of my throne and the place of the soles of my feet, where I will dwell in the midst of the people of Israel forever." This is the third great naming-formula of the architectural unit — after 41:4's "this is the Most Holy Place" and 41:22's "this is the table that is before the LORD" — and it is the one that names not a piece of space or a piece of furniture but the *function* of the whole edifice: this is *the place of my throne*. Block reads the formula as a deliberate counter-statement to the earlier vision of the kavod's departure: the throne that left now identifies its destination, and the destination is *here*, *forever*.

Verse 7's promise of "forever" (le-olam) is repeated in v. 9 in conditional form — *if* Israel puts away her whoring and the dead bodies of her kings, then "I will dwell in their midst forever." Greenberg notes the conditional structure as theologically important: the eternal indwelling is not a unilateral promise overriding human response, but a covenantal commitment that nonetheless makes claims on Israel's purification. The chapter holds promise and demand together.

Textual variants are relatively minor in this chapter compared to chapters 40–42. The Septuagint omits or shortens parts of vv. 13–17 (the altar measurements), and the Masoretic text's terminology for the altar's parts — *cheq* (bosom), *azarah* (ledge), *har'el* and *ariel* (altar-hearth, with both forms appearing in vv. 15–16) — has provoked extensive scholarly debate. The word *ariel* (literally "lion of God") in v. 15 is a hapax legomenon in this sense and Greenberg suggests it preserves an ancient Near Eastern altar-name that Ezekiel inherits and re-purposes.

## Lens 3 — Larger Story

Jewish reception of Ezekiel 43 has been shaped by the chapter's promise of eternal divine indwelling. The rabbinic tradition reads the Shekinah's return in eschatological terms — at the messianic restoration, the kavod will return to a rebuilt temple, fulfilling Ezekiel 43. The Mishnah Tamid and the Mishnah Middot describe the Second Temple's altar in terms drawn partly from Ezekiel 43:13–17, even though the Second Temple was built without the kavod's manifest return; rabbinic tradition acknowledged that the Shekinah did not visibly indwell the Second Temple as it had Solomon's, and treated this as evidence that the Second Temple was a provisional reality awaiting eschatological completion. Maimonides codifies the future-temple theology in *Hilkhot Beit ha-Bechirah* and treats Ezekiel 43:7's promise as binding for the messianic age: a Third Temple will be built, the Shekinah will return, the indwelling will be eternal.

The Qumran community took Ezekiel 43 as foundational for its own self-understanding. The Temple Scroll's altar prescriptions (11QTemple 12–13) draw on 43:13–17, and the community's broader theology of an eschatological cult presupposes the kavod's eventual return to a restored temple. The Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice (4QShirShabb) describe an angelic liturgy in a heavenly temple that is, in some sense, Ezekiel's vision realized in the heavenly precincts — the kavod indwells the upper sanctuary while the earthly temple awaits.

Christian reception of Ezekiel 43 is dominated by the typological reading that makes Christ the eschatological kavod. The patristic tradition almost universally read 43:1–5 as fulfilled in Christ: the glory comes from the east, just as Christ enters Jerusalem from the Mount of Olives at the east in Matthew 21:1–11; the glory enters by the east gate, just as the medieval Christian tradition built churches with the altar at the east end and worship oriented *ad orientem*. Jerome's commentary on Ezekiel reads 43:2 as a Christological prophecy and treats the medieval *ad orientem* tradition of Christian church-building as the architectural memory of Ezekiel's vision. The "many waters" of 43:2 are linked to John 7:38 ("out of his heart will flow rivers of living water") and to Revelation 1:15 ("his voice was like the roar of many waters"), so that the kavod-return is read as the Christ-return, in his earthly ministry and in his eschatological coming.

The most direct Christological cross-reference is John 1:14: "And the Word became flesh and tabernacled among us, and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth." The Greek *eskenosen* (tabernacled) is widely recognized as a deliberate evocation of the tabernacle/temple-indwelling tradition; the *doxa* (glory) is the kavod. For the patristic and Reformation Christian tradition, Ezekiel 43:7's promise of eternal indwelling is fulfilled in the incarnation: the throne of God, the place of the soles of his feet, is now a human body, and the body becomes the church (Eph 2:21–22), and the church awaits its consummation in the new Jerusalem (Rev 21:22).

The Christian readings split sharply at the question of *literal future fulfillment*. The dispensational tradition — Charles Feinberg, John Walvoord, the older Scofield apparatus, J. Dwight Pentecost — argues that the typological fulfillment in Christ does not exhaust Ezekiel 43; a literal millennial temple will be built in Jerusalem, and the kavod will literally return to indwell it during the thousand-year reign. The amillennial Christian tradition — patristic, Reformed, much of Roman Catholic exegesis — argues that the Christological typological fulfillment *is* the fulfillment; there will be no future literal kavod-return because Christ is the kavod and the church is the temple. Revelation 21:22's "I saw no temple in the city" is the amillennial trump card: the eschatological consummation is temple-less because the temple is the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

The first sustained tension in chapter 43 is the question of the kavod's identity in the New Testament era. The Christian Christological reading takes John 1:14 as the definitive interpretation: the *kavod* of Ezekiel 43:2 is the *doxa* of the incarnate Word, and the eternal indwelling promised in 43:7 is realized in Christ's permanent union with humanity. The Jewish reading is that the Shekinah's return is a future messianic event, still awaited; the Second Temple's destruction in 70 AD did not invalidate the promise but only extended its waiting. The critical-historical reading treats the entire vision as a sixth-century BC theological aspiration that was substantially disappointed by the modesty of the actual post-exilic restoration and that has been reinterpreted theologically (by Jews as future, by Christians as Christological) precisely because it was not literally fulfilled in its own historical horizon.

Steelmanning the Jewish reading is important here, because the Christological reading can become triumphalist quickly. The Jewish counter is twofold. First, the kavod's return in Ezekiel 43 is described in unmistakably *spatial-architectural* terms — the glory enters by the east gate, fills the temple, takes up residence in the inner court — and the Christological reading has to substitute *bodily-incarnational* terms for these. The substitution is not unmotivated, but it is a substitution. Second, the eternal indwelling promised in 43:7 was supposed to come *with* the rebuilt temple, the restored cult, the gathered tribes, the territorial inheritance — the package described in the next five chapters. Christianity has had to detach the kavod from this whole package and relocate it inside a single human body; Judaism reads the package as an integrity and waits for it to arrive whole. Steelmanned, the Jewish position is not "rejecting Christ" but "preserving the integrity of the prophetic vision" — a vision that includes land, polity, and cult, and not only the personal Mediator.

The second tension is the meaning of "forever" in vv. 7 and 9 in light of 70 AD. The Second Temple's destruction by the Romans is the historical fact that haunts every Jewish and Christian reading of Ezekiel 43. If the kavod was supposed to dwell forever, what happened? The patristic Christian answer — that the kavod transferred to Christ and through him to the church, so that the temple's destruction freed the indwelling from its architectural container — has the elegance of typological coherence but the cost of treating the *forever* as conditional in a way the text does not obviously support. The dispensational answer — that the *forever* applies to a still-future millennial temple — has the cost of implying that the promise has been deferred for over two millennia. The Jewish messianic answer — that the Third Temple is still to come — has the cost of indefinite deferral. The critical answer — that the *forever* was sixth-century theological hyperbole — has the cost of dismissing the prophetic claim altogether. None of the four answers is cost-free.

The unresolved question, sharper than the tensions, is whether Ezekiel 43's promise of eternal indwelling and Revelation 21:22's "I saw no temple" are compatible or incompatible. If the eschatological consummation has no temple, in what sense did the kavod return *to a temple* forever? Either the temple of Ezekiel 43 is fulfilled in Christ such that the absence of a temple in Revelation is its perfection, or the temple of Ezekiel 43 still awaits literal fulfillment such that Revelation 21:22 describes a still-later state, or the two visions are programmatic alternatives that the canon has placed in tension without resolving. The Christian reader confronts this question without a clean answer.

## The Thread

Three cross-references illuminate Ezekiel 43 in non-obvious ways. First, Ezekiel 11:22–23 — the kavod's earlier eastward departure: "Then the cherubim lifted up their wings, with the wheels beside them, and the glory of the God of Israel was over them. And the glory of the LORD went up from the midst of the city and stood on the mountain that is on the east side of the city." Chapter 43:2's coming "from the east" is the precise reversal of this earlier departure, and the geometry of the two passages, taken together, makes the entire intervening narrative of judgment and restoration into a single great motion of leaving and returning. Second, John 1:14's "the Word became flesh and tabernacled among us, and we beheld his glory" inserts the kavod-tradition into the Christian account of the incarnation; the Greek *eskenosen* is a tabernacle-verb and the *doxa* is the kavod, so that the Fourth Gospel's prologue can be read as a meditation on Ezekiel 43:7 reconfigured around a person. Third, Revelation 21:22 — "And I saw no temple in the city, for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb" — completes the trajectory in a paradox: the eternal indwelling promised to the temple becomes, at the end, an indwelling without the temple, because the temple has been internalized into the divine presence itself. The wall of separation that Ezekiel 42:20 erected to make the holy and the common distinguishable is replaced, finally, by a city whose gates never close.
