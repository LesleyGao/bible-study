# 1 Thessalonians 4: The Trumpet, the Cloud, and the Welcome of the King

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Thessalonica in AD 50 was not a quiet provincial backwater. It was the political capital of the Roman province of Macedonia, a "free city" (civitas libera) that had backed Octavian and Antony at Philippi and reaped the political rewards of having chosen well. It governed itself through a council of magistrates called politarchs, an unusual title that nineteenth-century critics once doubted until inscriptional evidence (the Vardar Gate inscription, IG X 2,1 no. 126, and roughly thirty other Macedonian texts) demonstrated that Luke's vocabulary in Acts 17:6 was technically precise. The city sat at the hinge point of the Via Egnatia, the Roman trunk road running from Dyrrhachium on the Adriatic to Byzantium, which meant that any letter, rumor, or refugee from Italy could move through the city within weeks. It also meant that the imperial cult had a particularly heavy footprint in Thessalonica — temples to Roma and the Caesars, civic priesthoods, festival calendars organized around the Augustan house. Acts 17:7 records the political accusation that brought Paul into trouble there: "they are all acting against the decrees of Caesar, saying that there is another king, Jesus." The charge is not theological abstraction. It is the practical consequence of Paul's use of words like *kyrios*, *euangelion*, and *parousia* in a city whose civic life was saturated with imperial parallels.

That imperial-cultic backdrop is why chapter 4's eschatological climax sounds the way it does. When a Roman emperor or his legate undertook a *parousia* — an arrival in a provincial city — the citizens did not wait inside their walls for him to come find them. They processed out in formal *apantēsis* to meet the dignitary on the road, hailed him with acclamations, and escorted him back into the city. Cicero attests the technical use of the term (*Ad Atticum* 8.16.2), as does Polybius (*Histories* 5.26.8) and a string of inscriptions and papyri dating from the Hellenistic kings through the Caesars. Imperial coinage and inscriptions celebrating the emperor's *adventus* — the Latin equivalent — survive from Nero onward; Suetonius and Dio Chrysostom describe the ritual choreography. This is the cultural script Paul reaches for in 4:17 when he tells the Thessalonians that the dead in Christ will rise first and the living will be caught up "to meet the Lord in the air" (*eis apantēsin tou kyriou eis aera*). He is not describing an evacuation. He is describing a civic welcome.

The other half of the chapter's emotional weight comes from material culture around death. Greco-Roman funerary practice ran from elaborate sarcophagi to mass columbaria with cremation urns. The inscriptions are an archive of pagan despair. The Latin formula *non fui, fui, non sum, non curo* — "I was not, I was, I am not, I care not" — appears across the Roman world, sometimes abbreviated NF F NS NC (compare CIL VI 6244). Other tombs read *somnus aeternus*. The mystery religions offered something better — Eleusis, Isis, Mithras — but these were elite options that could not promise the body itself would return. Paul's pastoral problem in 4:13 ("that you may not grieve as others do who have no hope") is the difference between the gospel and the inscriptions on the tombs the Thessalonians walked past every day.

The Jewish apocalyptic literature Paul drew on supplied a different set of images: the trumpet of *1 Enoch* and the cosmic visions of *4 Ezra* and *2 Baruch*; the resurrection promise of Daniel 12:2 and 2 Maccabees 7; the descent of God in cloud-theophany from Sinai to Ezekiel. Qumran's *1QM* (the War Scroll) ordered eschatological battle by trumpet-blast, and *1QH* hymns approach resurrection language. Paul welds these materials to the Greco-Roman script of imperial *parousia* and produces the densest twelve-verse package of early Christian eschatology we possess.

The letter is universally accepted as authentically Pauline and almost certainly the earliest surviving Pauline writing, written from Corinth around AD 50–51. The dating is anchored by the Gallio inscription at Delphi, which fixes Paul's Corinth visit (Acts 18:12) within a six-month window. Textual witnesses are excellent: P46 (c. AD 200), Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, and Alexandrinus.

**Key terms.** *Hagiasmos* (ἁγιασμός, sanctification) — process and state, used three times in 4:3–7 in a way that makes holiness the will of God definitionally. *Porneia* (πορνεία) — the broad term for sexual immorality, distinct from *moicheia* (adultery). *Skeuos* (σκεῦος) — vessel, container, instrument; the contested word in 4:4. *Parousia* (παρουσία) — presence, arrival, official visit. *Apantēsis* (ἀπάντησις) — formal welcome of a visiting dignitary. *Harpagēsometha* (ἁρπαγησόμεθα) — "we shall be snatched up," from *harpazō*, source of the Latin *rapiemur* and so of the English "rapture." *Koimaomai* (κοιμάομαι) — to fall asleep, the early Christian euphemism for death.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre matters because 1 Thessalonians 4 is doing two things at once. The first half (4:1–12) is paraenesis — moral exhortation in the standard Hellenistic philosophical mode, the kind of letter Seneca or Epictetus might have written, with its repeated *parakaloumen* ("we urge"), its appeal to existing teaching ("just as you received from us"), and its call to make incremental progress ("that you do so more and more"). Abraham Malherbe's Anchor Yale commentary (2000) reads the whole letter through the lens of philosophical paraenesis and demonstrates how comprehensively Paul has absorbed and Christianized the conventions. The second half (4:13–18) shifts genre: Paul invokes "a word of the Lord" (*en logō kyriou*, 4:15) and produces an apocalyptic descent narrative complete with archangelic voice, divine trumpet, resurrection of the dead, transformation of the living, and gathering at the meeting place.

The peri formula structures the back half of the letter. *Peri de tēs philadelphias* (4:9), *peri de tōn koimōmenōn* (4:13), *peri de tōn chronōn kai tōn kairōn* (5:1) — "now concerning brotherly love," "now concerning those who are asleep," "now concerning the times and the seasons." These are answer-formulas, signaling that Paul is responding to specific questions or concerns Timothy has reported back from Thessalonica. Charles Wanamaker (NIGTC, 1990) and Ernest Best (Black's, 1972) treat 4:13–18 as Paul's pastoral answer to a community grieving members who had died before the parousia, presumably expecting Christ to return within the lifetime of the founding congregation. The novelty of the question — and Paul's evident need to teach rather than merely remind — suggests this is the first time the issue has come up, which would fit the very early date of the letter.

Within 4:13–18 itself, the literary devices are dense. The descent narrative in 4:16 stacks three sound-images — *keleusma* (a military or athletic command), *phōnē archangelou* (the voice of an archangel), *salpinx theou* (the trumpet of God) — in what reads almost like the staging note for a theophany. The trumpet is the Sinai trumpet (Exod 19:16) refracted through prophetic and apocalyptic tradition (Isa 27:13, Joel 2:1, Zech 9:14, 1QM throughout, 4 Ezra 6:23). The cloud-imagery (4:17) is Daniel 7:13 and the cloud of Exodus and the transfiguration. And the verb *harpagēsometha* — passive, divine agent — places the action firmly in God's hands rather than the believer's flight.

Within the book's argument, chapters 4–5 are the climactic section. Chapters 1–3 are autobiographical and pastoral, defending Paul's ministry and rejoicing at Timothy's report. Chapters 4–5 then turn to the questions the report has raised: sexual ethics (4:1–8), brotherly love and economic life (4:9–12), the dead in Christ (4:13–18), the timing of the day of the Lord (5:1–11), and communal life in light of the eschaton (5:12–22). The apocalyptic material is not bolted on; it is the answer to genuine pastoral need.

Textual variants worth flagging. In 4:4, the manuscripts are unanimous on *to heautou skeuos ktasthai en hagiasmō* — the question is not the text but its meaning (see Lens 4). In 4:11, the Western text adds "*idiais*" before "*chersin*" ("with your own hands") in some witnesses, possibly to emphasize the manual-labor exhortation against social-class objections; the longer reading is in P46 and Sinaiticus and is almost certainly original. In 4:17, the manuscripts agree on *eis apantēsin* — the word's technical sense is, again, contested in interpretation rather than text.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**The Christian interpretive trajectory.** Tertullian's *De Resurrectione Carnis* (c. 210, especially chapters 24–25) is the earliest extant systematic engagement with 1 Thess 4:13–18. Writing against Gnostic spiritualizers, Tertullian insists that the *salpinx* and the *anastasis* require a real body, that "the dead in Christ" means the same flesh that died, and that Paul's "we who are alive, who are left" includes the dead returning embodied. Origen's full commentary on 1 Thessalonians is lost, but fragments survive in catenae and in Jerome's translations. Chrysostom's *Homilies on 1 Thessalonians* (the parousia material is in Homilies 7 through 8) treats 4:13–18 as practical pastoral comfort, dwells at length on *apantēsis* as a triumphal procession, and reads Paul's "we who are alive" as a rhetorical inclusion rather than a personal prediction. Augustine's *City of God* 20 sets the basic pattern that would shape Western reading for a thousand years: a single second coming, a general resurrection, a single judgment, no millennial interregnum on earth — what later theologians would label amillennialism.

Joachim of Fiore's three-ages scheme pushed apocalyptic speculation in directions the Spiritual Franciscans took up. Aquinas read 1 Thess 4 as straightforward catechesis on the resurrection. The Reformation kept the chapter within the patristic-Augustinian frame: Calvin's *Commentary on 1 and 2 Thessalonians* (1551) is deeply non-millennial and treats *apantēsis* in something close to its civic sense. The traumatic exception was the Anabaptist Münster catastrophe of 1534–35, which left a permanent black mark on millennial enthusiasm.

The modern critical tradition begins with F.C. Baur and J.B. Lightfoot's *Notes on Epistles of St. Paul*, which treated the parousia material with careful attention to Greco-Roman context. Albert Schweitzer's *Mysticism of Paul the Apostle* (1931) pushed "consequent eschatology": Paul genuinely expected the return in his lifetime. Bultmann's *Theology of the New Testament* tried to demythologize the descent-narrative into existentialist categories. Oscar Cullmann's *Christ and Time* counter-proposed an "already/not-yet" reading. C.H. Dodd's "realized eschatology" tried to dissolve the future expectation into the present kingdom; George Eldon Ladd's *A Theology of the New Testament* responded with historic premillennialism. N.T. Wright's *Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003) and *Surprised by Hope* (2008) argue that 4:13–18 is precisely an *apantēsis* text and that the dispensational reading inverts the imagery.

Major commentaries: Malherbe (Anchor Yale 2000), Wanamaker (NIGTC 1990), Fee (NICNT 2009), Best (Black's 1972), Green (Pillar 2002), Richard (Sacra Pagina 1995), Gaventa (Interpretation 1998), Bruce (WBC 1982), Marshall (NCB 1983), Jewett (*The Thessalonian Correspondence*, 1986). The imperial-critical school — Donfried's *Paul, Thessalonica, and Early Christianity* (2002), Koester, Horsley's *Paul and Empire*, Harrison's *Paul and the Imperial Authorities at Thessalonica* (2011) — reframes the chapter as counter-imperial: *parousia* and *apantēsis* claim for Jesus the ceremonies Caesar monopolized. The apocalyptic-Paul school — Beker, Gaventa, Douglas Campbell, J. Louis Martyn — refuses to soften the cosmic disruptiveness of Paul's eschatology.

**The dispensationalist rapture trajectory.** John Nelson Darby in the 1830s separated 4:13–18 from 5:1–11 and claimed the first passage describes a secret pretribulational rapture of the church distinct from the visible second coming. The Scofield Reference Bible (1909) embedded this reading into millions of evangelical Bibles. Hal Lindsey's *The Late Great Planet Earth* (1970) sold tens of millions; LaHaye and Jenkins's *Left Behind* series (1995–2007) sold over sixty million. John Walvoord's Dallas Theological Seminary lineage codified the system. The novelty of this reading is the load-bearing fact: no Christian writer before Darby reads 1 Thess 4 as a secret rapture distinct from the parousia, and Catholic, Orthodox, mainline Protestant, Reformed, Lutheran, and Wesleyan traditions all reject it. Critical engagement from within evangelicalism comes from Ladd (historic premillennialism), Anthony Hoekema (*The Bible and the Future*, amillennial), Kim Riddlebarger, Gregory Beale, Wright, Ben Witherington III, Craig Blomberg, Andrew Perriman, and Loraine Boettner (postmillennial).

**The Jewish frame.** Paul's eschatology stands within Jewish apocalyptic — *1 Enoch* 14, *4 Ezra* 7, *2 Baruch* 30, Qumran's *1QM* and *1QH*. Flusser, Vermes, and John J. Collins (*The Apocalyptic Imagination*) have shown how thoroughly Pauline categories overlap with Second Temple Jewish ones. Boyarin reads Paul as a Jewish apocalyptic thinker working out an internal Jewish problem. The Pharisaic resurrection hope (Daniel 12:2, 2 Maccabees 7, *m. Sanhedrin* 10) sits behind 4:13–18; Maimonides' thirteenth principle of faith later codifies bodily resurrection as essential to Judaism. The Sadducean denial of resurrection and the rabbinic concept of *olam ha-ba* frame the Jewish cousin of Christian eschatology.

**Convergence and divergence.** Catholic, Orthodox, and most Protestant Christianity converge on a single visible parousia, a bodily resurrection, and a final judgment, and they share that frame with rabbinic Judaism on the question of resurrection. They diverge on the millennium, on the intermediate state, and on whether 4:13–18 is to be read as imperial *adventus* (in which Christ comes down to earth to stay) or as evacuation (the dispensational rapture). The novelty of the rapture in Christian history is the single most important fact for anyone trying to assess its claims.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What is God up to in this twelve-verse passage? Paul's claim is that the death and resurrection of Jesus has begun a public, bodily, cosmic restoration of creation, and that the parousia is the moment when that restoration becomes visible to everyone, dead and alive, and when the people of Jesus are vindicated as citizens of his kingdom rather than Caesar's. The trumpet is not for evacuation. It is for arrival.

**Tension 1: The "rapture" question.** Pretribulational dispensationalism (Darby, Scofield, Lindsey, LaHaye, Walvoord) reads 4:13–18 as describing a secret return of Christ to the air, where he meets the church, takes them with him to heaven, and then later (after seven years of tribulation on earth) returns publicly with them to inaugurate a literal millennium. The dispensational case rests on a strong Israel/church distinction (the church must be removed before God resumes his program with national Israel), on the imminence of the parousia (no signs precede it), on the absence of the church from Revelation 4–19, and on what the system reads as a textual gap between 4:17 ("caught up to meet the Lord in the air") and the visible coming described in Matthew 24:30 and 2 Thess 1.

The steelman, fairly stated: the dispensational reading takes seriously the imminence-language scattered through the New Testament, refuses to allegorize Israel into the church, holds out a literal future for ethnic Israel that aligns with Romans 9–11, and offers a coherent narrative for why God would protect his people from a wrath he is pouring out on unbelievers. Walvoord and Ladd both wrote with seriousness; the mass-market version is not the only version.

The objections, however, are weighty. The novelty argument is the heaviest: if the rapture is in 1 Thess 4, why did no Christian writer notice for eighteen centuries? *Apantēsis* in its Greco-Roman civic context (Cicero, Polybius, the inscriptions) consistently means going out to meet a dignitary in order to escort him in, not to leave with him; the same word in Matt 25:6 (the wise virgins go out to meet the bridegroom and accompany him to the wedding) and Acts 28:15 (Roman believers come out as far as the Forum of Appius to meet Paul and escort him to Rome) reinforces the directional reading. Wright, Witherington, Hoekema, Beale, and Riddlebarger all argue that the dispensational reading inverts the imagery by sending Christ back up. Historic premillennialism (Ladd) accepts a future millennium but rejects the secret rapture. Amillennialism (Augustine through Calvin and the Reformed mainstream) reads 4:13–18 as a single visible second coming. Postmillennialism (Boettner) shares the single coming and locates the millennium before it. Catholic teaching (CCC 668–82) rejects "millenarianism, especially the 'intrinsically perverse' political form of a secular messianism," and reads the parousia as one event. Orthodox theology, never having absorbed Western millennial debates, holds the same single-coming structure with a strong sacramental-cosmic emphasis (Schmemann, *For the Life of the World*; Lossky, *Mystical Theology*).

Where this leaves us: the cumulative case against the pretribulational rapture — its novelty, the imperial *apantēsis* parallel, the consistency of Christian tradition before 1830, the inversion problem with 4:17 — is heavy enough that a fair reading should give the dispensational system the burden of proof rather than the default position.

**Tension 2: Soul-sleep, intermediate state, or immediate beatitude.** Paul's word for the dead in 4:13–15 is *koimōmenōn*, "those who are sleeping." Does the metaphor reflect a doctrine of soul-sleep (the dead are unconscious until resurrection) or is it a euphemism for death that says nothing about the intermediate state? Luther sometimes spoke as if the soul slept until resurrection; Calvin's *Psychopannychia* (1542) was an early and ferocious attack on soul-sleep, written against Anabaptists. The Catholic position (CCC 1021–32) is the doctrine of the intermediate state, with particular judgment at death and souls in heaven, hell, or (for those needing purification) purgatory until the resurrection. Eastern Orthodox theology (Lossky, Florovsky, Pope Benedict XVI's *Spe Salvi*, which draws heavily on patristic and Orthodox sources) holds an immediate communion with Christ at death, with the resurrection as the bodily completion of an already-real fellowship. Reformed scholastic orthodoxy (Westminster Confession 32, Hoekema) holds an intermediate state without purgatory: the souls of the righteous go immediately to be with Christ and await the resurrection. Seventh-Day Adventists, Jehovah's Witnesses, and some Anabaptists hold soul-sleep as doctrinal.

The textual case for soul-sleep relies on *koimōmenōn* and on similar OT language (Daniel 12:2, "many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake"). The case against relies on Phil 1:23 ("my desire is to depart and be with Christ, for that is far better"), Luke 23:43 ("today you will be with me in paradise"), 2 Cor 5:6–8 ("away from the body and at home with the Lord"), and the patristic consensus from Polycarp and the martyr accounts onward that Christian dead are with Christ.

The steelman for soul-sleep is the holistic anthropology argument: if the human person is a unity rather than a body-with-detachable-soul, then conscious existence apart from the body may be theologically incoherent, and the resurrection then becomes the only meaningful continuation. Hans Walter Wolff's *Anthropology of the Old Testament* and James Barr's critique of Greek dualism support this anthropology, and serious modern theologians (Oscar Cullmann's *Immortality of the Soul or Resurrection of the Dead?*, 1955) have argued that the immortality-of-the-soul doctrine is a Hellenistic import that Christianity should jettison. Wright's *Resurrection of the Son of God* lands close to this: he uses "life after life-after-death" to insist that the resurrection, not the intermediate state, is the central Christian hope.

Where this leaves us: the patristic, Catholic, Orthodox, and Reformed traditions all maintain that the dead in Christ are with Christ now in some real sense, while the bodily resurrection remains the future hope; soul-sleep is a minority position with weak textual support but a serious anthropological argument behind it; and even within the traditional view, the language of "sleep" should keep us humble about how much we actually know of what death is like for those who die in Christ.

**Unresolved question.** If Paul really expected the parousia within his own lifetime — and 4:15 ("we who are alive, who are left") is hard to read otherwise — then either he was simply wrong about the timing while right about the substance, or the parousia category is being used in a way that does not collapse into a calendar date the way the Thessalonians (and we) want it to. Which?

## The Thread

**1 Corinthians 15:51–52** — Paul's other extended resurrection passage. Same trumpet, same transformation, same mystery, written perhaps three years later from a different city; the parallel anchors the consistency of his teaching against attempts to read 1 Thess 4 as a separate event from the parousia of 1 Cor 15.

**Matthew 24:29–31** — the Olivet discourse, with its trumpet and its gathering of the elect from the four winds. Whether this is Paul's "word of the Lord" or whether Paul and Matthew share a common synoptic-tradition source, the parallels are dense enough that the dispensational distinction between rapture and second coming has to argue past these texts rather than from them.

**Acts 28:15** — Roman believers come out as far as the Forum of Appius and the Three Taverns to meet Paul and escort him into Rome. Same word, *apantēsis*, used in the same civic-welcome sense. The verse is a small but important footnote on what *eis apantēsin tou kyriou eis aera* meant in ordinary Greek.

**Daniel 12:2–3** — "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt." The Pharisaic resurrection hope, with its sleep-and-waking imagery, sits directly behind Paul's *koimōmenōn* and his confidence that God will bring with Jesus those who have fallen asleep.
