# Psalm 53 — A Scholarly Study

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 53 is the most striking case of duplication in the Psalter. It is, almost word-for-word, a parallel of Psalm 14, with one decisive editorial difference: every occurrence of *YHWH* (יהוה) in Ps 14 has been replaced by *Elohim* (אֱלֹהִים) in Ps 53. This is the clearest single piece of evidence for the so-called **Elohistic Psalter (Pss 42–83)** — the editorial collection in which the divine name has been systematically edited toward the more general *Elohim*. Mainstream critical scholarship (Hossfeld and Zenger; Kraus; Goldingay; Tate) treats Ps 14 and Ps 53 as two recensions of one earlier psalm, with Ps 53 being the *Elohistic* reworking of an older composition that is preserved in its earlier form as Ps 14.

The superscription identifies the psalm as a Davidic *maskil* (מַשְׂכִּיל) — a wisdom-teaching song, the same designation that frames Pss 32, 42, 44, 45, 52, 54, 55, 74, 78, 88, 89, and 142. The added rubric *al-mahalath* (עַל־מָחֲלַת, "according to Mahalath") is one of several enigmatic musical or genre indicators in the Psalter (cf. *al-ayyeleth ha-shahar*, Ps 22; *al-shoshannim*, Pss 45, 69). Suggested glosses include "for sickness" (from *cholah*), "with flutes" (a musical instrument), and a melody-name. The Septuagint renders it *hyper Maeleth*, transliterating rather than translating, signaling that the meaning was already obscure to the LXX translators in the third or second century BCE.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 53 (and its twin Ps 14) as a hybrid of *Klagepsalm* (lament) and *Weisheitsdichtung* (wisdom poem). More recent form-critical work designates it a wisdom-lament, voicing communal anguish about pervasive human corruption while teaching the wisdom contrast of fool and righteous. The Hebrew *naval* (נָבָל, "fool," v. 1) is the same word that names Nabal of 1 Samuel 25 — the man whose name was his character. In wisdom literature *naval* is not the simpleton of Proverbs (*pethi*) or the obstinate scoffer (*letz*) but the morally bankrupt person who treats God as practically irrelevant. "There is no God" (אֵין אֱלֹהִים, *ein Elohim*) here is not metaphysical atheism — a category foreign to the ANE — but practical denial: God will not act, will not judge, does not see (cf. Ps 10:4, 11; 14:1).

The most significant variation between Ps 14 and Ps 53 falls at v. 5. Where Ps 14:5–6 reads "for God is with the generation of the righteous. You would shame the counsel of the poor, but the LORD is his refuge," Ps 53:5 reads instead: "For God scatters the bones of him who encamps against you; you put them to shame, for God has rejected them." Psalm 53's version introduces explicit military-camp imagery — "him who encamps against you" (חֹנָךְ, *chonakh*) — and the gruesome image of scattered bones. Hossfeld-Zenger argue that this points to a particular crisis behind the Ps 53 recension: a siege or military encampment against Jerusalem, perhaps from the late monarchic or exilic period, recasting the older Ps 14 reflection on universal human corruption into a specific cry against an attacking army.

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve Ps 53 fragments (4QPsa, 4QPsc), confirming the Masoretic text essentially as we have it, though with no surviving evidence of the v. 5 expansion's textual prehistory. The Septuagint of Ps 53 (LXX 52) follows the Hebrew closely. Importantly, the LXX of Ps 14 contains an extended insertion at v. 3 (drawn from Pss 5:9, 140:3, 10:7, 36:1, Isa 59:7–8) that became the basis of Paul's chain-citation in Romans 3:10–18. This LXX insertion is not present in MT Ps 14 nor in MT Ps 53; the Pauline citation thus draws on the LXX recension of Ps 14, which functionally overlaps with Ps 53.

Date: linguistic features of Ps 14 suggest pre-exilic origin; Ps 53's military-camp variation likely belongs to a late monarchic or early exilic redaction, with final Elohistic editing in the post-exilic period.

## Lens 2: Literature

Robert Lowth's parallelism is on full display in Ps 53. The first verse moves from a quoted speech-act ("the fool says in his heart, 'there is no God'") to its evidentiary backing ("they are corrupt") and its summary verdict ("there is none who does good"). The triadic movement — interior speech, observable behavior, theological evaluation — recurs throughout the psalm.

The poem is strophically organized in roughly four units, though the absence of *selah* in the MT of Ps 53 (in contrast to its presence in Ps 14) leaves the structure unmarked editorially:

- **Strophe I (v. 1):** The fool's interior speech and its moral consequence.
- **Strophe II (vv. 2–3):** God's downward gaze and its verdict — universal corruption.
- **Strophe III (v. 4):** Direct apostrophe to evildoers — "have those who work evil no knowledge?"
- **Strophe IV (vv. 5–6):** The reversal — terror seizes the encampers, salvation longed for from Zion.

The chiastic envelope is the contrast between *human corruption from below* (vv. 1, 3 — "they are corrupt, none who does good") and *divine vision from above* (v. 2 — "God looks down from heaven"). This vertical structure — the descending divine gaze surveying ascending human evil — is one of the most visual chiasms in the Psalter. William Brown (*Seeing the Psalms*) emphasizes this as a "cosmic-architectural" image: God peering down through the heavens at the *bnei adam* (בְּנֵי־אָדָם, "children of man"), an echo of Genesis 11:5 ("the LORD came down to see the city and the tower").

The phrase "in great terror, where there is no terror" (פָּחֲדוּ פַחַד לֹא־הָיָה פָחַד, *pachadu fachad lo-hayah fachad*, v. 5) is one of the great poetic shocks of the Hebrew Bible. The triple repetition of *pachad* (terror) creates a vortex: the wicked are terrified by a terror that has no objective referent. The poem implies that conscience or divine presence creates panic where human eyes see no enemy. Hossfeld-Zenger note this as the signature touch of the Ps 53 redactor: the older Ps 14 contains the line less emphatically.

The closing strophe's longing for "salvation from Zion" (יְשׁוּעוֹת מִצִּיּוֹן, *yeshu'ot mi-Tziyyon*, v. 6) creates an inclusio with the opening fool's denial of God: practical atheism gives way to messianic expectation. The poem moves from "there is no God" to "let Jacob rejoice, let Israel be glad."

Inclusio also operates at the divine-name level. The Elohistic redactor's decision to use *Elohim* throughout the poem — even where Ps 14 had *YHWH* — creates a sustained meditation on the universal God who looks down on all humanity, rather than on the covenantal Yahweh of Israel. The theological effect of the recension is to widen the lens from Israel-vs-the-nations toward humanity-as-such, while still longing for "salvation from Zion" in the final verse.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Psalm 53 sits in Book II of the Psalter (Pss 42–72) and within the Davidic Elohistic cluster (Pss 51–72). Its placement after Ps 52 (the imprecation against Doeg) and before Ps 54 (the prayer when the Ziphites betrayed David) integrates it into a sequence of psalms wrestling with human treachery — first one named informer (Doeg), then a meditation on universal corruption, then another set of betrayers (Ziphites). The editor's logic moves from the particular to the universal and back to the particular.

**Jewish trajectory.** The rabbinic reception of the Ps 14 / Ps 53 doublet generated significant exegetical reflection. *Midrash Tehillim* on both psalms treats *naval* as the prototype of the practical atheist — the one who reasons "no eye sees me," whose denial of God is moral rather than philosophical. Rashi explicitly identifies the *naval* as Nebuchadnezzar (in Ps 14) and as Titus (in Ps 53), reading the two recensions as prophetic of two different destructions of Jerusalem. Ibn Ezra is more philological, treating the two psalms as variant transcriptions of the same composition. Radak (David Kimchi) preserves both readings, noting the textual variations carefully — one of the earliest premodern observations of what we now call the Elohistic recension. The Targum on Ps 53 explicitly inserts "the wicked Sennacherib" as the encamping enemy at v. 5, applying the military imagery to the 701 BCE crisis. Modern Jewish readers — Buber, Heschel, Sarna, Levenson — give Ps 53 less attention than Ps 14, treating it as a recensional variant. Avivah Zornberg has reflected on the *naval*'s "internal speech" as a paradigm of self-deception in modern Jewish moral psychology. Liturgically, Ps 53 has no fixed weekday placement; it appears in some kabbalistic prayer cycles.

**Christian trajectory.** The Christian reception is dominated by Romans 3:10–12, where Paul cites the LXX of Ps 14 (whose extended chain-citation overlaps substantially with Ps 53) as scriptural proof of universal human sinfulness: "as it is written, 'None is righteous, no, not one; no one understands; no one seeks for God. All have turned aside; together they have become worthless; no one does good, not even one.'" Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* treats Ps 53 (LXX 52) and Ps 14 (LXX 13) together, emphasizing the universal scope of human corruption that grace alone overcomes. Cassiodorus follows Augustine. Aquinas in the *Catena Aurea* uses Ps 53 prooftextually for original sin.

The Reformers gave Ps 53 / Ps 14 a foundational role. Luther's *Operationes* makes universal sinfulness the hinge of justification by faith: the *naval* is everyone apart from grace. Calvin's 1557 commentary is unusually careful with the doublet, noting the textual differences and reading Ps 53's military-camp imagery as prophetic of Sennacherib's siege (echoing the Targum's reading independently).

Modern critical readers handle the doublet with editorial sophistication. Gunkel pioneered the recensional analysis. Mowinckel emphasized the cultic setting against attacking nations. Westermann classifies it among the laments. Kraus and Hossfeld-Zenger develop the most rigorous account of the Elohistic Psalter and Ps 53's place in it. Goldingay, Tate, and Wenham all give Ps 53 its own treatment, refusing to collapse it into Ps 14. Walter Brueggemann reads the psalm as a "psalm of disorientation" — a community looking at human reality and refusing to see it sentimentally. Patrick Miller and William Brown both note the cosmic-architectural imagery of God's descending gaze. Beth Tanner's recent commentary (with deClaissé-Walford and Jacobson) treats the recensional question pastorally: two communities, two crises, one truth.

**NT use.** Romans 3:10–12 is the signature New Testament citation, drawing from the LXX of Ps 14 with its expanded chain-citation that functionally overlaps with Ps 53. Paul's argument that "all have sinned" depends on this Psalm-text. The verse "they are corrupt, doing abominable iniquity; there is none who does good" (Ps 53:1) becomes one of the load-bearing scriptural pillars of the Pauline doctrine of human sin.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

Two tensions structure the theological significance of Psalm 53.

**Tension 1: The recensional question — what does it mean that we have two versions?** The doublet of Ps 14 / Ps 53 raises questions that traditional readers of the Bible find uncomfortable.

- **The harmonizing position (premodern Christian and Jewish):** The two psalms are independent compositions by David, addressing different historical situations. The variations are intentional and inspired. Both are equally authoritative.
- **The recensional position (mainstream critical):** Ps 14 and Ps 53 are two editions of one earlier psalm. The Elohistic redactor of Pss 42–83 reworked the older psalm for inclusion in his collection. Both versions were preserved because both became authoritative for different communities.
- **The canonical position (Brevard Childs, Hossfeld-Zenger):** The fact that *both* are in the Psalter is itself the canonical decision. The editors declined to harmonize. The scripture invites readers to hold both — to listen for *YHWH*-language and *Elohim*-language together, to hear the one truth in two voices.

Steelman of the canonical position: A scripture that contains its own variant readings, side by side, is teaching a hermeneutical lesson — that truth is not destroyed by recension, that canonical authority does not require univocity, that the two voices of the doublet enrich rather than threaten each other. The doublet itself is a model of how the canonical community handles textual diversity: not by erasure but by preservation.

**Tension 2: Universal sinfulness vs. specific oppressors.** The psalm sustains a tension that modern readers often miss. Vv. 1–3 indict humanity as such — "none who does good, not even one." V. 4, however, suddenly distinguishes "those who work evil" who "eat up my people" — a specific class of oppressors who consume God's people. Then v. 5 names "him who encamps against you" — a military enemy. The psalm holds together universal human corruption *and* specific oppressive enemies.

- **The Pauline reading (Rom 3):** The universal indictment is the dominant note. All are sinners; the distinction between oppressor and oppressed dissolves before God. This reading treats v. 4's "they who eat up my people" as part of the universal indictment rather than a separate category.
- **The original-context reading (Hossfeld-Zenger, Kraus):** The psalm originally addressed a community under siege. The "fools" who say there is no God are specifically the encamping enemies, the oppressors who reason that no divine judgment will fall on them. The universal-sounding language is rhetorical hyperbole within a particular crisis.
- **The both-and reading (Goldingay, Tanner, Brueggemann):** The psalm refuses to choose. It teaches that human corruption is universal *and* that specific oppressors must still be named. The community confesses its own sin while still crying out against the encamping enemy. The Pauline reading is true; the contextual reading is also true; the canonical psalm holds both.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 53 confronts a worshiping community with a hard reality and a harder hope. The hard reality is the psalm's bleak anthropology: God looks down and finds no one who does good. This is not pessimism but moral honesty. The harder hope is that the community continues to pray "Oh, that salvation for Israel would come out of Zion!" — that the same psalm that says "none who does good" still expects God's restoration of his people. The fool says "there is no God"; the worshiper, looking at the same evidence, says "let Jacob rejoice."

The closing question: When you read Ps 53, do you locate yourself among the *bnei adam* whom God surveys from heaven and finds wanting, or among "my people" whom the evildoers eat up? The psalm presses both identifications on every reader. The Pauline tradition insists on the first; the lament tradition insists on the second. The canonical psalm refuses to let you choose only one.

## The Thread

- **Psalm 14:** The earlier recension of the same psalm. Reading the two side by side reveals the Elohistic editor's hand and the canonical decision to preserve both.
- **Romans 3:10–12:** Paul's chain-citation of the LXX of Ps 14 (overlapping Ps 53) — the New Testament's most influential use of this material in the doctrine of universal human sin.
- **Genesis 11:5:** "The LORD came down to see the city and the tower" — the closest narrative parallel to Ps 53:2's image of God looking down from heaven on the children of man.
- **Isaiah 37 / 2 Kings 19 (Sennacherib's siege):** The traditional Jewish reading of v. 5's "him who encamps against you" — the encamping enemy whose bones are scattered.
