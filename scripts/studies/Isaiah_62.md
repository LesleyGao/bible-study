# Isaiah 62 — Go Deeper

## The Thread

Isaiah 62 is a chapter about names. A city that had been called *Forsaken* and a land called *Desolate* are renamed: *Hephzibah* — "my delight is in her" — and *Beulah* — "married." The names are not metaphors layered onto an unchanging reality. They are performative speech that changes the city. To be called by a new name in the Hebrew Bible is to be re-constituted, the way Abram became Abraham and Jacob became Israel. Zion is given a name that locates her not in shame but in covenantal-marriage with Yahweh.

The chapter follows the Spirit-anointed Jubilee-prophecy of Isaiah 61 and develops its bridegroom-imagery into a full marriage theology. The prophet refuses to keep silent. Watchmen on the walls give Yahweh no rest. The redeemed people walk on a highway prepared for them. The chapter is short, lyrical, and dense with names — and those names have echoed through Jewish liturgy and Christian hymnody for two thousand years.

## Lens 1 — Historical World

Isaiah 62 belongs to Third Isaiah (chs. 56-66), the post-exilic collection that mainstream-critical scholarship since Bernhard Duhm (1892) has distinguished from First Isaiah (chs. 1-39, eighth-century Jerusalem under Assyrian threat) and Second Isaiah (chs. 40-55, exilic Babylon, mid-sixth century). Trito-Isaiah is dated to the early Persian period, late sixth to fifth century BC, and addresses the disappointments of the rebuilt-Jerusalem community.

The mainstream-critical position (Claus Westermann, Joseph Blenkinsopp's Anchor Yale *Isaiah 56-66*, Paul Hanson's *The Dawn of Apocalyptic*, John Goldingay, Jacob Stromberg, Patricia Tull) reads chs. 60-62 as the literary core of Third Isaiah — three chapters of unmixed-restoration vision that may be earlier than the surrounding material and closer to the Second-Isaiah tradition. Hanson's social-conflict reading places these chapters with the visionary-prophetic faction that contested the priestly-hierocratic establishment of Persian-period Yehud. Stromberg's redactional analysis treats 60-62 as a deliberate centerpiece around which the rest of Trito-Isaiah was built.

The composite-authorship debate continues. The unified-authorship defense (J. Alec Motyer's *Prophecy of Isaiah*, John Oswalt's NICOT) argues that the eighth-century Isaiah ben Amoz could prophesy across centuries and that the book reads as a coherent literary-theological whole. Brevard Childs's canonical approach accepts the layered composition but reads the canonical-final-form as authoritative. The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa-a) from Qumran preserves all 66 chapters as one document by 125 BC, demonstrating that the *received* book was unified well before the New Testament era.

The historical setting of chapter 62 is plausibly the early restoration community wrestling with whether Yahweh has truly returned to Jerusalem. The post-exilic literature (Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi, Ezra-Nehemiah) shows a community in tension: the temple rebuilt in 516 BC was a pale shadow of Solomon's; the city walls were not rebuilt until Nehemiah in 445 BC; foreigners controlled the agricultural produce (cf. 62:8 — *I will not again give your grain to be food for your enemies*); the promised glory had not arrived. Into this disappointment the prophet refuses silence: Yahweh's righteousness *will* go forth as brightness.

The two new names in 62:4 are the chapter's signature. *Hephzibah* — Hebrew *Cheftzi-vah* (חֶפְצִי־בָהּ), literally *my-delight-in-her* — is the new name for Zion. *Beulah* — Hebrew *Be'ulah* (בְּעוּלָה), the passive participle of *ba'al* (to marry, to possess as husband) — means *married*, *bridal*. The renaming is paired with a renaming of what Zion had been called: *Azuvah* (עֲזוּבָה), *Forsaken*, and *Shemamah* (שְׁמָמָה), *Desolate*. The contrast is precise: from abandonment-language to delight-and-marriage language.

A historical curiosity: *Hephzibah* is also the name of the wife of King Hezekiah and mother of King Manasseh (2 Kgs 21:1) — meaning that for the eighth-century-Isaiah readers (under the unified-authorship view) the name carried a Davidic-royal resonance. For the Trito-Isaiah audience (under the critical view), it had become a name detached from any specific queen and operating purely as restoration-theology vocabulary.

## Lens 2 — Literature

Isaiah 62 is prophetic-poetry of the restoration-oracle type, with four short movements:

**Refusal of silence (62:1-5).** The speaker (the prophet, or by some readings Yahweh himself) declares he will not keep silent for Zion's sake. Vindication will come; the nations will see; Zion will receive a new name. The renaming is stacked: a *crown of beauty*, a *royal diadem*, *My Delight Is in Her*, *Married*. The marriage-imagery culminates in the striking double-simile of 62:5: as a young man marries a young woman, so your sons shall marry you (with the textual oddity that *banayikh* — your sons — could be repointed to *boneikh*, your builder, an emendation Westermann adopts) — and as a bridegroom rejoices over the bride, so shall your God rejoice over you.

**Watchmen-on-the-walls (62:6-7).** The speaker has set watchmen on Jerusalem's walls who never keep silent — Hebrew *ha-mazkirim et-Yahweh* (הַמַּזְכִּרִים אֶת־יְהוָה), *those who put the Lord in remembrance*. They are to give Yahweh no rest until he makes Jerusalem a praise in the earth. The image is bold: human intercessors *requiring* God to act, refusing to let him be silent.

**Yahweh's oath (62:8-9).** Yahweh swears by his right hand and his mighty arm that the agricultural-economic injustice of foreign exploitation will end. Grain and wine will not again feed enemies; those who labor will eat in the courts of Yahweh's sanctuary.

**The highway and the new names (62:10-12).** The chapter ends with Exodus-language — *prepare the way*, *build up the highway*, *clear it of stones*, *lift up a signal* — echoing the *prepare the way of the Lord* of Isaiah 40:3. The closing names: *The Holy People*, *The Redeemed of the Lord*, *Sought Out*, *A City Not Forsaken*.

The chapter's literary architecture is name-driven. Six names appear: two negative (Forsaken, Desolate) being repudiated, and several positive (Hephzibah, Beulah, Holy People, Redeemed of the Lord, Sought Out, City Not Forsaken). The Hebrew naming-convention treats names as performative — to give a name is to constitute a reality. The chapter is a renaming-ceremony.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Within the prophetic canon.** Isaiah 62 develops the marriage-covenantal imagery that runs through the prophets. Hosea 1-3 had used the marriage metaphor — Yahweh as husband, Israel as unfaithful wife — to indict the northern kingdom. Jeremiah 2-3 picked it up for Judah. Ezekiel 16 and 23 extended it with brutal indictment-poetry. Second Isaiah 54:5-8 had begun the *redemptive* turn — *your Maker is your husband* — and Third Isaiah 62 brings it to fullness: not just the husband-wife metaphor but a renaming-ceremony in which Zion is *Beulah*, married. The marriage theology is not new; the *names* are new.

**Jewish trajectory.** The medieval Jewish commentators read 62 as eschatological. Rashi treats the new names as future-restoration realities. Ibn Ezra reads them as the messianic-age reality. Radak emphasizes covenant-renewal. The Targum Jonathan paraphrases the chapter into messianic-restoration language. In Jewish liturgical tradition, Isaiah 62 contributes vocabulary to the *Lecha Dodi* hymn sung on Friday evening to welcome the Sabbath as bride — *Hitoreri hitoreri ki ba orech* (*Awake, awake, for your light has come*) draws on Isaiah 60 and 62 — and the bridegroom-bride imagery underlies traditional Jewish wedding liturgy, in which the seven blessings (*sheva brachot*) end with Jerusalem's joy (referencing Jer 33:11 with a Trito-Isaian backdrop).

The name *Hephzibah* itself was adopted as a Jewish female given name throughout the medieval and modern periods. The name *Beulah* did not become a common given name in Jewish use but in Christian use (especially American Protestant) became a popular nineteenth-century female name.

**Christian trajectory.** The marriage-imagery of Isaiah 62 became foundational for Christian ecclesiology. Paul's marriage-theology in Ephesians 5:22-33 — *husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church* — draws on the prophetic-marriage tradition Isaiah develops here. Revelation 19:7 (*the marriage of the Lamb has come*) and 21:2 (*the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband*) directly echo Isaiah 62's bridal-language. The *new Jerusalem as bride* of Revelation 21 is the eschatological completion of Isaiah 62's *Beulah* — the city Yahweh has named *Married* now arriving as the Lamb's bride.

In Christian hymnody, John Mason Neale's translation of Bernard of Cluny's twelfth-century Latin hymn *Hora Novissima* — *Jerusalem the Golden* — draws on Isaiah's Zion-imagery. Edward Mote's nineteenth-century hymn and the older *Beulah Land* (1876, Edgar Page Stites) both make *Beulah* a name for the Christian's destination — *I am dwelling on the mountain, where the golden sunlight gleams… I have reached the land of corn and wine, and all its riches freely mine; here shines undimmed one blissful day, for all my night has passed away. O Beulah Land, sweet Beulah Land, as on thy highest mount I stand…* The hymn made *Beulah* a synonym for the heavenly homeland in American Protestant piety, especially among nineteenth-century revivalist and gospel-music traditions.

John Bunyan's *Pilgrim's Progress* (1678) names *the country of Beulah* as the place pilgrims reach just before the Celestial City — *the contract of the bridegroom and the bride*. Bunyan reads Isaiah 62 as the experiential anticipation of heaven, the foretaste in the present life of the marriage-feast to come.

**New Testament use.** Isaiah 62:11 — *Behold, your salvation comes; behold, his reward is with him* — is echoed in Matthew 21:5 (the triumphal entry, fused with Zech 9:9) and Revelation 22:12 (*Behold, I am coming soon, bringing my recompense with me*). The closing-names of 62:12 (*The Holy People, The Redeemed of the Lord*) feed Petrine-ecclesiology: *you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession* (1 Pet 2:9).

The bridegroom-imagery of 62:5 enters Christology directly: Jesus presents himself as the bridegroom (Mark 2:19-20; Matt 25:1-13 — the parable of the ten virgins; John 3:29 — John the Baptist as the bridegroom's friend). The Christian-tradition reading: Christ *is* the bridegroom Isaiah 62 promised, and the church (or the eschatological new Jerusalem) is the bride.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Marriage-theology and modern reception.** The bridegroom-bride imagery is one of the most theologically rich and one of the most contested metaphors in scripture. On the rich side: it figures covenantal-faithfulness, mutual delight (62:4-5 — *the Lord delights in you*; *your God will rejoice over you*), and the asymmetric-but-loving relation between Yahweh and his people. Mainstream Christian theology — Catholic (CCC §757, §796 on the church as bride), Reformed (the Westminster Confession on the church as Christ's bride), Eastern Orthodox (the marriage-mystery as iconic of Christ-and-church) — has consistently embraced this metaphor as central to ecclesiology.

On the contested side: feminist theology (Phyllis Trible, Renita Weems, Athalya Brenner, Drorah Setel) has pointed out that the prophetic marriage-metaphor in its negative form (Hosea, Ezekiel 16, 23) deploys violent and sexualized punishment-language against the wife-Israel, with troubling implications for how readers absorb male-female-power-dynamics. The Isaiah 62 deployment is the *redemptive* end of the metaphor — Zion as delighted-in, rejoiced-over — and so escapes the worst of the indictment-form. But the asymmetry remains: Yahweh is husband, Zion is bride, and the metaphor encodes a hierarchical structure that modern readers handle differently.

The mainstream Catholic and Reformed response: the metaphor is irreducible and biblical, but it is *one* metaphor among many for the divine-human relation (parent-child, shepherd-flock, king-people, friend-friend). Reading it alongside the others avoids absolutizing the gender-asymmetry. Christian feminist theology (Sallie McFague, Elizabeth Johnson) has worked to recover the maternal-divine images (Isa 49:15; 66:13) as complement to the marital-paternal.

**Tension 2: Hephzibah as queen of Manasseh.** The biblical Hephzibah was wife of Hezekiah and mother of King Manasseh — the worst king of Judah by the Deuteronomistic Historian's evaluation (2 Kgs 21:1-18). To name Zion *Hephzibah* is to take a name with mixed historical resonance and load it with restoration-theology. The historical Hephzibah disappears from the narrative after one verse; the prophetic Hephzibah names the entire restored city. This is part of how prophetic-poetry works — it takes ordinary names and elevates them into theological vocabulary.

**Tension 3: The watchmen who give God no rest.** Isaiah 62:6-7 contains an extraordinary theological claim: Yahweh has appointed intercessors whose job is to *give him no rest until he establishes Jerusalem*. Jewish prayer tradition has read this as warrant for persistent intercession — the watchmen are the prayers of Israel demanding Yahweh's faithfulness. Christian tradition has read it as warrant for continual prayer (Luke 18:1-8 — the parable of the persistent widow; 1 Thess 5:17 — *pray without ceasing*). Both readings honor the boldness of the text: God *appoints* people to importune him.

The theological tension: does God need to be reminded? The answer in the Hebrew prophetic tradition is yes — not because Yahweh forgets, but because covenant-faithfulness requires the *people's* active participation in remembering. The watchmen do not inform God of facts; they hold him to his promises in a covenantal-relational sense. Christian theology of prayer has often domesticated this boldness; the text refuses to.

**Tension 4: Liberation read of 62:8-9.** *I will not again give your grain to be food for your enemies, and foreigners shall not drink your wine* — the agricultural-economic substance of the promise is concrete: an end to colonial-extractive injustice. Liberation theology and Catholic social teaching (CCC §2402-2409 on the universal destination of goods; *Evangelii Gaudium*) have read Isaiah 62:8-9 alongside 61:1-3 as part of a sustained prophetic critique of economic-extraction. Those who labor should eat. Foreigners-as-exploiters should not consume what locals produce. The theological force is uncomfortable in a global-trade economy where this pattern continues.

**Tension 5: The new name and the question of identity.** To be renamed in scripture is to be re-constituted (Abram → Abraham, Jacob → Israel, Simon → Peter, Saul → Paul). Isaiah 62 promises Zion a new name *the mouth of the Lord will give*. Christian baptismal tradition has read this typologically: Christians receive a new name in baptism (cf. Rev 2:17 — *a white stone, with a new name written on the stone*). The promise of being given a new name speaks to the deep human need not just for forgiveness but for *re-naming* — for being addressed differently than the names the world or our shame have given us.

**Where this leaves us.** Isaiah 62 is short and luminous. It is a chapter about names — about the difference between *Forsaken* and *My-Delight-in-Her*, between *Desolate* and *Married*. It builds the marriage-theology that runs from the prophets through Paul and Revelation. It commissions watchmen who refuse silence. It promises an end to economic exploitation. And it ends with a city the Lord has *sought out* — not abandoned.

The chapter sits in tension with itself in modern reading. The marriage-imagery is theologically central and pastorally complicated. The watchmen's *importunity* is biblically warranted and theologically bold. The economic substance of the promise is unfashionable in a world where extraction continues. And the names — Hephzibah, Beulah — have echoed in Jewish liturgy, Bunyan's *Pilgrim's Progress*, nineteenth-century gospel-music, and Christian hymnody right up to the present.

**Closing question.** If God's people are appointed to give him no rest until justice is done, what does it mean that we have so often gone silent? And what name does God want to give the cities, the communities, the souls we have written off as *Forsaken*?
