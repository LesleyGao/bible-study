# 1 Samuel 9: Lost Donkeys, Found King

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens with a tribal genealogy and the territorial geography of Benjamin, and both are weighted historical claims. The five-generation patrilineage (Saul → Kish → Abiel → Zeror → Becorath → Aphiah) is the longest such genealogy any character receives in the Samuel narrative, and it places Saul firmly in the social structure of an Iron Age I tribal aristocracy. The qualifier בֶּן־אִישׁ יְמִינִי גִּבּוֹר חַיִל *ben-ish yemini gibbor ḥayil* (9:1) — usually translated "a man of wealth" but more precisely "a mighty man of substance" — designates the kind of village patriarch who could outfit a small force of retainers, owned multiple draft animals (the lost donkeys are plural), and had economic standing in the highland clan system. This is not nobility in the later sense; this is the aristocracy of the unfortified hill village, the social class Lawrence Stager and David Schloen identified as the "patrimonial household" structure of Iron Age I Israel. Khirbet Qeiyafa under Yosef Garfinkel's excavation, with its central administrative building and four-room houses, gives material context for the kind of substantial-household economics behind Kish.

The geography of the donkey search (9:4) — Ephraim, Shalishah, Shaalim, Benjamin, Zuph — traces a circular route through the central highlands. Zuph is Samuel's family region (1 Sam 1:1, where Samuel's father Elkanah is identified as a Zuphite), and it is the ironic terminus of Saul's wandering: he is unwittingly arriving at the place that has been preparing him from before he set out. Saul's hometown is Gibeah of Benjamin, identified with Tell el-Ful three miles north of Jerusalem, where W. F. Albright's 1922–23 excavations exposed an Iron Age I fortress reasonably attributed to a Saulide political center. The sequence of place names in the search functions as a tribal map of a polity that does not yet exist; the chapter is taking the reader on a tour of the territory that will become the Saulide kingdom.

Three Hebrew terms govern the chapter. רֹאֶה *roʾeh* (9:9, 9:11, 9:18, 9:19) — "seer" — is the older title for the prophetic functionary, distinct from later נָבִיא *navi*. The narrator stops the action at 9:9 with an editorial gloss: "Formerly in Israel, when a man went to inquire of God, he said, 'Come, let us go to the seer,' for today's 'prophet' was formerly called a seer" (לְפָנִים בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל כֹּה־אָמַר הָאִישׁ בְּלֶכְתּוֹ לִדְרוֹשׁ אֱלֹהִים … כִּי לַנָּבִיא הַיּוֹם יִקָּרֵא לְפָנִים הָרֹאֶה). This historicizing aside is one of the most important historical-linguistic notes in the Hebrew Bible. McCarter (AB), Joseph Blenkinsopp (*A History of Prophecy in Israel*), and Robert R. Wilson (*Prophecy and Society*) all treat 9:9 as evidence that pre-monarchic Israel had multiple distinct religious offices — the *roʾeh*, the חֹזֶה *ḥozeh* (visionary, used of Gad in 2 Sam 24:11), and eventually the consolidated *navi* — and that these offices merged or were redefined during the monarchic period as classical prophecy emerged. The aside is the editor showing his work.

נָגִיד *nagid* (9:16) — "prince" or "leader-designate" — is the chapter's other technical term, and Samuel uses it deliberately instead of מֶלֶךְ *melekh*. Halpern (*The Constitution of the Monarchy in Israel*) and McCarter argue that *nagid* is the older sacral-charismatic title, distinct from the later *melekh*, denoting a YHWH-designated military deliverer rather than a dynastic monarch. The chapter is pre-monarchic in its theology: Saul is not yet *king*, he is *nagid*, the next in the line of *moshiaʿ*-judges who will save Israel from external threat. Halpern's reading is that the dtr inherited two terms from two different political-theological traditions — *nagid* from the tribal-charismatic tradition, *melekh* from the popular request — and the Samuel narrative preserves the distinction.

בָּמָה *bamah* (9:12, 9:13, 9:14, 9:19, 9:25) — "high place" — is the third governing term, and historically the most contested. Premonarchic and early-monarchic Israel had legitimate worship at multiple high places (cf. Solomon at Gibeon, 1 Kings 3:4) before the dtr's centralization theology condemned them. The high place at Ramah where Samuel presides is treated by the narrator without polemic. Iron Age I cultic installations — the bull site at Mount Manasseh excavated by Amihai Mazar, the Mount Ebal site excavated by Adam Zertal, and various open-air installations — give material context. The chapter is a window onto pre-Deuteronomic worship before centralization made all such sites theologically suspect.

The economy of the encounter has historical specificity: רֶבַע שֶׁקֶל כֶּסֶף *revaʿ sheqel kesef* (9:8), a quarter-shekel of silver, is the consultation fee. Iron Age I silver was weighed, not coined; the standardization of the *sheqel* as a weight unit is well attested in epigraphic and archaeological finds (the Yavneh-Yam ostracon, scale weights from Lachish and Tel Arad). A quarter-shekel is roughly 2.8 grams — the price of a chicken in some Mari texts, a modest professional fee. The detail is a flash of mundane economics in a chapter otherwise weighted with destiny.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Generically, this chapter is a folktale. Robert Alter, Susan Niditch (*A Prelude to Biblical Folklore*), and Hugo Gressmann all identified its structure: the unsuspecting hero sets out on a small errand, the errand becomes a pretext for divine appointment, an apparently chance encounter is the engineered destination. The structure is that of "the lost is found," and the lost is double — the donkeys are the surface object, the king is the deeper one. By the chapter's close, only one of these has actually been recovered (the donkeys are reported found, 9:20), and the other has been claimed without yet being installed. The narrator is enjoying himself.

The literary devices are concentrated. The chapter opens with a long descending genealogy (9:1) that culminates in Saul's physical introduction (9:2) — and the introduction is double-edged. Saul is "handsome" (טוֹב *ṭov*) and "from his shoulders upward taller than any of the people" (מִשִּׁכְמוֹ וָמַעְלָה גָּבֹהַּ מִכָּל־הָעָם). The narrator is not flattering. The repeated emphasis on Saul's physical impressiveness will be the chapter 16 contrast point when David is introduced and YHWH explicitly tells Samuel "the LORD looks not as man looks; man looks at outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart" (16:7). Saul looks like a king; David looks like a shepherd. The dtr is laying a trap.

The structure of the chapter is patient. Saul is given a long opening (9:3–10) of pure folktale — lost donkeys, anxious father, helpful servant, modest consultation. The reader knows from chapter 8 that a king is being sought and the elders' demand has been granted, but Saul does not. Then at 9:15–17 the narrator suddenly shifts perspective and reveals that YHWH had already alerted Samuel the previous day: "Tomorrow about this time I will send to you a man from the land of Benjamin." This dramatic-irony device — what Sternberg calls "narrative omniscience deployed to expose providence" — converts the entire opening folktale retroactively into theological setup. The donkeys were the mechanism; the appointment was the meaning.

Bar-Efrat and Fokkelman both note the chapter's repeated motif of being *led*: Saul does not choose his route, the servant suggests the seer, the maidens direct them to the high place, Samuel directs them to the meal, the cook produces the kept-aside portion. Saul is a passive subject through the entire chapter — and this passivity is itself a literary characterization, foreshadowing the indecisiveness that will mark his reign.

The textual variants are not dramatic but they are interesting. At 9:24, the MT specifies the cook took up "the leg and what was on it" (הַשּׁוֹק וְהֶעָלֶיהָ); the precise meaning of הֶעָלֶיהָ is debated. McCarter, following the LXX, reads it as "and the fat-tail" — a delicacy. Tsumura (NICOT) defends the MT and reads it as "and what was upon it." Either way, the priestly portion (Lev 7:32–34) of the right thigh is being given to Saul, a gesture that effectively signals priestly-charismatic recognition. The Septuagintal expansions throughout the chapter generally amplify the providential framing without changing the substance. 4QSam-a is fragmentary at key verses but where preserved aligns with the LXX more often than the MT.

Within the book's argument, chapter 9 is the chapter that begins what Wellhausen famously identified as the "pro-monarchic source" — the tradition that treats the institution of kingship as YHWH's gracious gift rather than human apostasy. The tonal shift from chapter 8 is dramatic: there the demand is rejection of YHWH's kingship; here the king is YHWH's hand-picked deliverer, given to save Israel from the Philistines because YHWH has *seen* and *heard* his people (9:16, language echoing the exodus prologue of Exod 3:7). The dtr has welded the two sources together without smoothing the contradiction, and Polzin reads this welding as the canon's deliberate constitutional ambivalence.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, 1 Samuel 9 is the chapter that introduces Saul, and through Saul the whole institution of Israelite monarchy. The folktale frame links it to two other "lost-and-found" providential episodes: Joseph's brothers selling him to traveling traders (Gen 37, the lost son who becomes a savior), and the servant of Abraham seeking Rebekah at the well (Gen 24, the chance encounter that fulfills the divine plan). The well-encounter motif is especially close — Saul and his servant meet maidens at the city water-source (9:11), the same setting where Eliezer meets Rebekah and Jacob meets Rachel and Moses meets Zipporah. Robert Alter's analysis of the "betrothal type-scene" in *The Art of Biblical Narrative* notes the parallel and reads its truncation as significant: Saul does not meet a bride at the well, he meets directions to a prophet. The biblical type-scene of romantic-providential encounter is here repurposed for political-providential encounter. The kingship narrative is being staged as a marriage, but with YHWH and Israel as the parties.

**Jewish trajectory.** Josephus (*Antiquities* 6.4.1–5) tells the chapter as a Hellenistic providence-tale, smoothing the folktale features and emphasizing Saul's modest character — Josephus is preparing his Roman readers to like Saul before the tragedy unfolds. The rabbis of *b. Berakhot* 48b and elsewhere debate Saul's height and beauty, generally treating it as divine gift rather than dramatic-ironic foreshadowing. Rashi reads the lost donkeys as providential mechanism with characteristic plain-sense brevity and notes that Saul's modesty in 9:21 ("Am I not a Benjaminite, from the least of the tribes?") is genuine, not feigned. Radak develops a more psychological reading: Saul's self-deprecation is the mark of someone who has not yet sinned — the chapter's Saul is the Saul-before. Ralbag, characteristically, reads the chapter as exemplifying the *natural* working of providence through ordinary means — donkeys, servants, a small fee — and reads the "seer" tradition as evidence of how prophetic knowledge worked in pre-monarchic Israel. Don Isaac Abarbanel gives the chapter unusually extended attention because of the political-theological stakes: he argues that YHWH's selection of Saul through the lost-donkey mechanism is itself a divine accommodation to Israel's request, a way of granting the king they wanted while preserving the divine prerogative of selection. Yair Zakovitch and Moshe Garsiel read the chapter's narrative artistry — especially the layered providence and the type-scene allusions — as evidence of sophisticated literary composition. Avivah Zornberg reads Saul's modesty as the chapter's emotional center: this is the only chapter in which we see Saul whole, unburdened by the kingship that will undo him. The "Saul of chapter 9" is, for Zornberg, the road not taken.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic readers tended to find Saul difficult and the chapter therefore awkward. Origen reads the lost donkeys allegorically as the lost senses or the lost gentile nations, recovered through prophetic mediation. Augustine in *City of God* 17.7 is brief on this chapter, treating Saul's election as the providential establishment of a typologically inadequate kingship, awaiting David. Chrysostom's homilies dwell on Saul's modesty in 9:21 as evidence that even a flawed king begins humble. Gregory the Great in *Moralia* uses the lost-donkey episode as figure of the soul wandering until God appoints a guide. Aquinas treats Saul's election in *De Regimine Principum* as a case study in providential political appointment. Calvin's sermons on 1 Samuel 9 are particularly attentive: he reads the chapter as evidence that *God's hand is in events that look entirely accidental*, and he draws pastoral encouragement that the most ordinary errand can be the means of divine appointment. He is gentle with Saul-of-chapter-9 in a way he will not be with Saul-of-chapter-15. Luther reads Saul's height and beauty as divine gift abused, and uses the chapter to warn against trusting outward gifts of nature without inward gifts of grace. Among critical scholars, Wellhausen famously identified this chapter as the spine of the pro-monarchic source, in deliberate contradiction to chapter 8's anti-monarchic source. Noth, Cross, and especially McCarter (AB) refined the source-critical analysis. Halpern (*The Constitution of the Monarchy*) developed the *nagid* / *melekh* distinction. Klein (WBC) and Hertzberg (OTL) provide the standard commentary readings. Brueggemann reads the chapter as canonical evidence that even the institution YHWH disapproves can become the institution YHWH inhabits — accommodation as providence. Polzin reads the chapter's pro-monarchic tone as the dtr's deliberate dialectical move against chapter 8. Eugene Peterson reads the chapter pastorally as the model of vocational discovery: the call comes through the small errand. Ellen Davis and John Goldingay attend to the chapter's quietness, the way the prophet's authority is exercised through hospitality rather than spectacle. Among feminist readers, the chapter's nameless maidens at the well (9:11–13) — knowledgeable, articulate, integrated into local cultic life — have been recovered by Phyllis Trible, Carol Meyers (*Discovering Eve*), and Susan Ackerman as evidence of women's normal participation in Israelite religious geography that the dtr's later centralization erased.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Was Saul YHWH's choice, or only YHWH's permission?** The chapter says clearly that YHWH revealed Saul to Samuel the day before (9:15–16) and identified him at sight (9:17). But chapter 8 had said the demand for a king was rejection of YHWH's own kingship. Is Saul YHWH's man or merely Israel's man, divinely tagged?

A *traditional Catholic* position would harmonize: YHWH genuinely chooses Saul as the providentially fitted instrument for Israel's deliverance from the Philistines (9:16), even within an institutional arrangement the people demanded against divine preference. Choice within concession is still choice.

A *Reformed* position with Calvin would read divine election here as providential ordering of human sin's consequences. Israel demanded a king; YHWH chose *which* king. The chapter is not endorsement of monarchy but accommodation that retains divine sovereignty over the institution.

A *Jewish* position, especially Abarbanel's, would emphasize the active divine selection — Saul is genuinely chosen, not merely tolerated — and read the chapter as evidence of YHWH's continued covenantal investment in Israel's politics even when the people's choices are flawed.

A *critical* position (Wellhausen, McCarter) would treat the chapter as the pro-monarchic source's theological argument *against* the anti-monarchic chapter 8: this source celebrates Saul as YHWH's chosen savior, and the contradiction with chapter 8 is unresolved because two traditions have been juxtaposed without harmonization.

A *narrative-theological* position (Brueggemann, Polzin) would say the canon is preserving both perspectives because both are theologically necessary — divine election operates within human choices, including bad ones, and the canon refuses to flatten the dialectic. *Where this leaves us:* Saul is chosen, but chosen for an institution God did not want — and the chapter establishes the pattern of every subsequent Israelite king, whose anointing is real but whose office is tragic.

**Tension 2: How can the same God who declared the king-demand a rejection of his kingship (8:7) now answer Israel's cry for deliverance by appointing one (9:16)?** The chapter quotes YHWH using the exodus language — "I have seen my people, because their cry has come to me" (כִּי רָאִיתִי אֶת־עַמִּי כִּי בָּאָה צַעֲקָתוֹ אֵלָי, 9:16) — but the deliverer he sends is the very institution he warned against.

A *Lutheran* or *Reformed* position would read this as classic divine accommodation: God works through means he does not approve, and the king is both gift and judgment. The same act of providence that delivers Israel will eventually be the institution that destroys her. Calvin's sermons hold this tension explicitly.

A *Catholic* position, with the natural-law tradition, would say the king is genuinely a *good* in the order of natural political wisdom — the chapter shows that monarchy can serve genuine ends — even if Israel's specific motive in demanding a king was disordered. The institution is morally neutral; the demand was distorted.

An *Orthodox* position would emphasize the *kenotic* dimension: God is willing to enter institutions he did not initiate, and the king's anointing is a provisional incarnation of YHWH's deliverance through a flawed instrument.

A *Jewish* position would generally hold the tension without resolving it. Abarbanel reads the chapter as showing that even when Israel chooses badly, YHWH does not abandon Israel — divine commitment outlasts institutional disappointment.

A *critical* position would treat the contradiction as evidence of the dtr's editorial juxtaposition of incompatible source traditions; the contradiction is canonical because the editing is.

A *liberation* or *political-theology* position (Walter Wink, Brueggemann's later work, Yoder) would read the tension as the canonical Bible's most honest statement about institutional power: God's "yes" to imperfect institutions does not endorse them; deliverance can come through means that themselves require eventual repentance. *Where this leaves us:* the chapter forbids us from triumphalism about any human institution YHWH has used. Even YHWH's chosen king is the beginning of a tragedy. Even YHWH's deliverance comes through means that will need their own undoing.

*Unresolved question:* If Saul is genuinely YHWH's man — chosen, anointed, sent to save — why does the canon's larger story require his destruction, and what does it mean to be elected for a role that cannot end well?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 24:10–27** — Eliezer at the well meeting Rebekah. The betrothal type-scene Saul's encounter at Ramah deliberately invokes and inverts; Saul finds not a bride but a kingdom.
- **1 Samuel 16:6–7** — "The LORD looks at the heart." The deliberate retrospective correction of the chapter's emphasis on Saul's height and beauty. The dtr is paying us back later.
- **2 Samuel 7:8** — Nathan to David: "I took you from the pasture, from following the sheep." The pattern of YHWH choosing a king through an ordinary errand — donkeys for Saul, sheep for David — is a literary motif the dtr reuses to mark divine election as recognizable.
- **Mark 14:13–15** — Jesus sends two disciples into Jerusalem who will meet a man carrying a water jar; he will lead them to the upper room already prepared. The flagged later passage where the providential-encounter pattern of 1 Sam 9 — the unsuspecting traveler led by a stranger to a prepared room and a kept portion — is restaged in the gospels for the Last Supper. The cook's reserved portion at Ramah and the upper room's prepared table are not unrelated.
