# Psalm 86 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 86 carries a rare and significant superscription: *tefillah le-David* (תְּפִלָּה לְדָוִד) — "A Prayer of David." It is **the only Davidic psalm in Book III** of the Psalter (Psalms 73-89), set among Asaphite and Korahite collections that otherwise dominate this stretch. Within the Korahite cluster of Psalms 84-85, 87-88, Psalm 86 sits as a deliberate Davidic interruption — and the editorial choice is not random. Where the surrounding Korahite psalms speak corporately (the dwelling place of God, the restoration of Jacob, the gathering of nations to Zion), Psalm 86 speaks in the voice of an individual servant. The structural placement makes the psalm read as the king's own personal prayer in the middle of the community's worship.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 86 as an **individual lament** (*Klagelied des Einzelnen*), more specifically a *Bittgebet* — a prayer of petition. It follows a recognizable lament pattern but with unusual features: heavy reuse of formulas from elsewhere in the Psalter and the Pentateuch (it has been called an *anthological* psalm because so many of its phrases appear elsewhere), a rare central hymnic section in the middle (vv. 8-10), and a structurally important quotation of the **attribute formula** of Exodus 34:6 in v. 15. Sigmund Mowinckel placed prayers of this kind in the cult of the suffering individual — a worshipper coming to the temple in distress and praying with the help of standardized liturgical language. Hans-Joachim Kraus, Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger, John Goldingay all read it as **post-exilic**, an anthology prayer that gathers the great phrases of older psalms and the Torah into a single composite supplication.

The dating question depends partly on what to make of the *le-David* superscription. Most modern critical scholars treat it as either traditional attribution rather than authorial claim, or as a literary device by which a post-exilic editor anchored an anthology psalm to the great pray-er of Israel's tradition. Marvin Tate (WBC) summarizes the consensus: the language is late, the anthological style suggests a learned scribe with deep knowledge of the Psalter and Torah, and the placement in Book III among Korahite psalms makes most sense as a post-exilic editorial choice to give David a voice in the book of crisis.

The most theologically loaded line is v. 15 — *ve-atah Adonai El rachum ve-channun erekh apayim ve-rav chesed ve-emet* (וְאַתָּה אֲדֹנָי אֵל רַחוּם וְחַנּוּן אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם וְרַב־חֶסֶד וֶאֱמֶת) — "But you, O Lord, are a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness." This is a near-direct quotation of **Exodus 34:6**, the *attribute formula* (Hebrew tradition: *shelosh esreh middot*, the **thirteen attributes of mercy**). The Exodus passage is the climactic theophany at Sinai after the golden calf — God passes before Moses and proclaims his name. The formula is repeated, with variations, throughout the Hebrew Bible: Numbers 14:18 (Moses pleading after the spies), Joel 2:13 (the prophet's call to return), Jonah 4:2 (Jonah's complaint that God is too merciful to Nineveh), Nahum 1:3 (the prophet of judgment on Assyria), Psalm 103:8, Psalm 145:8, Nehemiah 9:17. **Walter Brueggemann** in *Theology of the Old Testament* calls Exod 34:6 "the credo of God's character" — the central confession of who God is across the Hebrew Bible. Psalm 86:15 is the psalmist's deliberate appropriation of that credo as the warrant for personal prayer: *because* God is merciful and gracious in the foundational Sinai disclosure, *therefore* the praying servant has standing to ask.

Hebrew terms worth dwelling on. *Tefillah* (תְּפִלָּה) in the superscription is the standard Hebrew word for prayer; only five psalms carry this designation (Psalms 17, 86, 90, 102, 142). *Ani ve-evyon* (עָנִי וְאֶבְיוֹן) in v. 1 — "I am poor and needy" — pairs *ani* (afflicted, lowly) with *evyon* (needy, destitute); the pair is the standard self-description of the Psalter's righteous sufferer. *Chasid* in v. 2 (אֲנִי חָסִיד, "I am godly," more literally "loyal" or "covenant-faithful") is unusual as a self-designation — most translators soften it. *Ben amatekha* (בֶּן־אֲמָתֶךָ) in v. 16 — "the son of your maidservant" — is a striking idiom, possibly a form of self-humbling or possibly indicating membership in a household born to service (a *home-born servant*). The famous prayer of v. 11 — *yacheid levavi le-yir'ah shemekha* (יַחֵד לְבָבִי לְיִרְאָה שְׁמֶךָ) — "unite my heart to fear your name" — uses the verb *yachad* (to make one, unite) in the piel; the underlying noun *echad* is the same root as the *Shema*'s confession that "the LORD is one" (Deut 6:4). The petition is for the praying *self* to be unified into the *one*-ness that fits the *one* God. This single phrase has become one of the most influential lines in Western devotional literature.

The Septuagint (Psalm 85 LXX) renders the attribute formula of v. 15 with high fidelity: οἰκτίρμων καὶ ἐλεήμων, μακρόθυμος καὶ πολυέλεος καὶ ἀληθινός. The Greek tradition's frequent use of the formula in the Liturgy of St John Chrysostom and the Anaphora preserves it as a foundational Christian liturgical confession. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragmentary witnesses to Psalm 86; the text is broadly stable.

ANE parallels are limited because Psalm 86's anthological style is quite distinctive — but the prayer-formulary tradition of Mesopotamian *šuilla* prayers (which often reuse phrases from older liturgical compendia) provides a comparable cultural setting. The Israelite version is distinctive in that the source-pool being anthologized is the Hebrew Scriptures themselves — the psalm draws on Exodus 34, on Deuteronomy 6, on earlier psalms (Pss 25, 27, 40, 54, 56, etc.), creating a learned tapestry of canonical citation.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Psalm 86's structure is best read as a chiastic arrangement around the central hymnic affirmation of vv. 8-13. The outer frames are petitions; the center is doxology and confession.

- **A** (vv. 1-7): petition with eight imperatives — *incline, answer, preserve, save, be gracious, gladden, give ear, listen*; first hymnic ground (*for you, O Lord, are good and forgiving*, v. 5)
- **B** (vv. 8-10): central hymn — incomparability of God, eschatological vision of nations worshipping
- **A'** (vv. 11-13): petition with two imperatives — *teach, unite*; second hymnic ground (*great is your steadfast love*, v. 13)
- **C** (vv. 14-17): final lament — enemies, citation of attribute formula, three imperatives (*turn, give, save*) and a final imperative (*show*)

The center (vv. 8-10) is a remarkable hymnic interlude in an otherwise petitionary psalm. *Ein kamokha ba-elohim Adonai* (אֵין־כָּמוֹךָ בָאֱלֹהִים אֲדֹנָי) — "there is none like you among the gods" — opens an incomparability hymn that broadens to the eschatological vision of v. 9: *kol-goyim asher asita yavo'u ve-yishtachavu lefaneikha Adonai* (כָּל־גּוֹיִם אֲשֶׁר עָשִׂיתָ יָבוֹאוּ וְיִשְׁתַּחֲווּ לְפָנֶיךָ אֲדֹנָי) — "all the nations you have made shall come and worship before you." The petitioner's individual cry opens onto the universal horizon. This is structurally important: the ground of the prayer is not just God's character toward Israel but God's character toward the nations.

Lowth's parallelism is dense throughout. **Synonymous parallelism** is the dominant mode: "incline your ear, O LORD, and answer me" / "preserve my life… save your servant" (vv. 1-2). **Antithetic parallelism** organizes vv. 14-15 — the *insolent men* and *ruthless men* who *do not set you before them* (v. 14) versus *but you, O Lord, are a God merciful and gracious* (v. 15). The contrast is between human insolence and divine mercy. **Synthetic parallelism** drives vv. 11-13, where each line builds the next: teach me your way → that I may walk in your truth → unite my heart → I give thanks with my whole heart → I will glorify your name forever → for great is your steadfast love → you have delivered my soul.

The psalm uses **inclusio** at the level of divine titles: it opens with appeals to *YHWH* and *Adonai* in the first verses and closes with the same address in v. 17 (*atah YHWH azartani ve-nichamtani*, "you, LORD, have helped me and comforted me"). The name *Adonai* (אֲדֹנָי) appears seven times — a deliberately patterned frequency that marks the psalm as a prayer of *the servant to the master*.

The most distinctive literary feature is the **anthological style** itself. The psalm is, in a real sense, a composition *made of* other psalms and Torah passages. Verse 1's opening echoes Psalm 17:6 and Psalm 31:2; verse 5's *good and forgiving* echoes Psalm 25:7-8; verse 11's *teach me your way* echoes Psalm 25:4; verse 14's enemy-description echoes Psalm 54:3; verse 15's attribute formula is Exodus 34:6. The poet is not lacking originality — the originality is in the *combinatorial* art of bringing the canonical phrases into a single new prayer. This is itself a theology of prayer: the praying self is not alone but stands in a tradition of pray-ers whose words become the praying self's words.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

In the canonical shape of the Psalter, Psalm 86's placement is theologically significant. **Gerald Wilson** read Book III (Psalms 73-89) as the book of crisis in the Davidic covenant. Psalm 86 is the *single Davidic prayer* in that book — the king's own voice in the midst of national lament. Some commentators (David Mitchell among them) read the placement as deliberate: David himself, as paradigmatic servant, prays the prayer of the suffering people; the figure who founded the dynasty whose collapse is being mourned in Book III speaks in the voice of the *poor and needy*. The Davidic king is *anvi ve-evyon* — and that is the point. The kingship that survives the crisis is the kingship that prays this prayer.

In **Jewish reception**, Psalm 86 has had a profoundly important life through its v. 15 reuse of the **thirteen attributes of mercy** (*shelosh esreh middot ha-rachamim*). The thirteen-attribute tradition — counted by the medieval Jewish authorities from the Sinai theophany of Exodus 34:6-7 — is recited in the *Selichot* services of penitential days, on Yom Kippur, on fast days, and in the *neilah* service that closes the Day of Atonement. **Rashi** (11th c.) on Psalm 86 reads each verse with reference to the corresponding events in David's life. **Ibn Ezra** (12th c.) is more philological. **Radak** (David Kimhi, 13th c.) reads the psalm as fitting both David's biography and the experience of every Jew in exile. *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 86 reads the prayer for *yacheid levavi* (v. 11) as the prayer for *kavvanah* — the unified intentionality that proper prayer requires; the divided heart cannot pray. In the Hasidic tradition, **Rabbi Nachman of Breslov** treated v. 11's *unite my heart* as a foundational text for *yichudim* — the meditative practice of unifying scattered fragments of the soul. **Martin Buber** in *Tales of the Hasidim* records numerous Hasidic teachings on the divided heart and its restoration. **Abraham Joshua Heschel** in *God in Search of Man* reads the attribute formula tradition as the heart of Jewish theology — God as *El rachum ve-channun*. **Jon Levenson** in *Sinai and Zion* treats Exod 34:6 and its reuses as the central confession of the Hebrew Bible. **Nahum Sarna** notes the rhetorical structure of v. 15 as direct citation of the Sinai theophany.

In **Christian reception**, Psalm 86 has had two great strands of influence: the **divided heart** tradition (v. 11) and the **incomparability and conversion of nations** strand (vv. 8-10). **Augustine** in his *Confessions* and *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 85 (LXX) gives the verse 11 prayer one of its most famous treatments. Augustine reads his own life as a long struggle with a divided heart — *cor inquietum*, the restless heart — and v. 11's *unite my heart to fear your name* becomes one of his programmatic petitions. In *Confessions* X he prays repeatedly for the gathering of the scattered self. **Cassiodorus** in his *Expositio Psalmorum* follows Augustine. **Bernard of Clairvaux** in his sermons on the Song of Songs draws on v. 11 for the theology of the *united heart* in mystical love. **Thomas Aquinas** (in his commentary on the Psalms and in the *Summa* II-II q. 81 on devotion) treats v. 11's unification as foundational for the virtue of religion. **Luther** in his *Operationes in Psalmos* (1519-1521) preached Psalm 86 as a prayer of the church under the cross; v. 15's attribute formula is the gospel itself, the comfort to which the conscience flees. **Calvin** (1557 commentary) reads Psalm 86 with characteristic practical earnestness; v. 11 is read as the prayer for sanctification — the integrated heart that is the goal of Christian formation.

The verse 11 tradition flowered in **John Wesley's** doctrine of *entire sanctification* — Wesley repeatedly cited Psalm 86:11 as the scriptural ground for his teaching that God can and does unify the believer's heart in love of God in this life. The Wesleyan-Holiness tradition (Phoebe Palmer, Charles Finney, the late-19th-century holiness movement) drew on the verse extensively. The Catholic tradition's parallel concept of *integritas* — the integration of the moral life — drew on the same verse through Augustine and Aquinas. **Søren Kierkegaard's** *Purity of Heart Is to Will One Thing* is, in essence, an extended meditation on Psalm 86:11.

In modern critical scholarship: **Gunkel** classified the form, **Mowinckel** the cultic setting, **Westermann** the lament structure, **Kraus** the temple liturgy, **Hossfeld-Zenger** the anthological character, **John Goldingay** the canonical-theological reading, **Marvin Tate** (WBC) the philological detail, **Gordon Wenham** (*Psalms as Torah*) the moral instruction, **Jamie Grant** the Davidic placement in Book III. **Walter Brueggemann** treats Psalm 86 as a paradigmatic prayer of *trust in the credo* — the suffering individual leans on the disclosed character of God; the prayer is grounded in confession. **Patrick Miller** reads Psalm 86 as a *lectionary of canonical phrases* — a teaching prayer that shows the praying community how to assemble new prayers from the inherited treasury. **William Brown** notes the anthropological weight of v. 11's *unified heart* — the psalm understands the self as composite, in need of integration. **Beth Tanner** in the NICOT replacement volume draws out the theological argument of the structure.

In the **New Testament**, Psalm 86 is not directly quoted, but its theological grammar is everywhere. The attribute formula of v. 15 (echoing Exod 34:6) is woven into the New Testament confession of God's mercy — particularly in James 5:11 (*polysplangchnos* and *oiktirmōn* echoing the LXX of the formula), in 2 Corinthians 1:3 (the Father of mercies), in Ephesians 2:4 (rich in mercy). Verse 9's eschatological vision of the nations worshipping is fulfilled in Revelation 15:4 — *all nations shall come and worship before you* — which directly cites Psalm 86:9. Verse 11's *unite my heart* is the burden of the New Testament's calls to *single-mindedness* (James 1:8, *dipsuchos*, the divided soul).

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

Two tensions are worth holding.

**Tension 1: The unified heart — Christian sanctification, Jewish *kavvanah*, or anthropological observation?** Verse 11's *yacheid levavi le-yir'ah shemekha* has been read in radically different traditions. **Position A (the Augustinian-Wesleyan reading):** the prayer is for entire sanctification; God can unify the divided heart in love, completing the work of grace in this life. This reading sees the verse as the scriptural ground for a theology of perfection or *integritas* — the believer is called to undivided devotion, and grace can supply what nature cannot. **Position B (the Jewish *kavvanah* reading — Hasidism, Heschel, Buber):** the prayer is for unified intentionality in worship; the divided heart cannot pray, and the petition is that God would gather the scattered fragments of attention so that the praying self becomes a true unity. Wesley and the Hasidim differ on the metaphysics but agree on the existential reality — that the human heart is divided and that prayer asks God for unity. **Position C (the Kierkegaardian-anthropological reading):** the prayer is the *ongoing* lifelong petition of any self that is constituted by competing loves; unity is not an arrival but a discipline, and the verse is the prayer the human being prays repeatedly because the divided heart is the human condition.

The steelman of Position A: the New Testament does speak of believers being made *complete* (Matt 5:48; Heb 12:23); the Wesleyan reading has produced lives of remarkable integration, and there are moments in the Christian life that warrant the language of unification. The steelman of Position C: the Confessions of Augustine end without arrival; the praying self is always praying *because* it is divided; treating unity as a permanent state risks self-deception. The most defensible synthesis is that the verse is *both* a real petition for real grace *and* a recurring prayer that the self in time will need to pray throughout its life. The unified heart is given but not kept without continual asking.

**Tension 2: The attribute formula as warrant — covenant theology of mercy.** Verse 15's citation of Exod 34:6 raises the deep question of how the foundational covenant character of God functions in personal prayer. **Position A (Brueggemann, the credo reading):** Exod 34:6 is *the* central confession of the Hebrew Bible; every subsequent prayer that cites it is asking God to be God-as-disclosed. The attribute formula is performative, not descriptive — to recite it is to invoke it, to call God to be the God he has already named himself to be. **Position B (the more cautious reading):** the formula is a theological summary, not a magical formula; the petitioner's invocation does not bind God but acknowledges God; the warrant for prayer is God's free covenant grace, not the recitation. **Position C (the rabbinic-thirteen-attributes reading):** the formula is a sacred liturgical structure — its recitation is itself an act of repentance and a renewal of covenant; in the *Selichot* tradition, the community recites the thirteen attributes precisely because the recitation is the act of teshuvah.

The steelman of Position A: the Hebrew Bible does treat the formula as performatively powerful — Moses cites it in Numbers 14:18 to *change God's mind*, Joel cites it to call for repentance, Jonah cites it as protest against the same character. Position C is essentially the Jewish liturgical embodiment of Position A. The synthesis: the attribute formula is the warrant *because* it is the disclosed character; the praying servant of Psalm 86 is doing what every praying Israelite does — leaning on what God has said about himself.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 86 is the prayer of the divided heart leaning on the disclosed mercy. It is anthological — a tapestry of older phrases — and that is its theology: the praying self is never alone; it stands in the long line of pray-ers whose words become its words. It cites Exodus 34:6 because the foundational disclosure of God's character at Sinai is the warrant for every subsequent supplication. It prays for the unified heart because the heart's division is the human condition, not a temporary failure. It is the only Davidic psalm in the book of crisis because the king who survives the crisis is the king who prays *I am poor and needy*. The eschatological vision of v. 9 — that all nations shall come and worship — is woven into the personal prayer because the suffering servant's prayer participates in the cosmic horizon. The praying self and the praying nations are gathered into the same address.

**Closing question:** What would it mean for you to pray Psalm 86's *unite my heart to fear your name* not as a one-time petition for arrival but as the daily prayer of a self constituted by competing loves — and what would change if you treated the divided heart not as failure but as the normal human condition that always needs grace?
