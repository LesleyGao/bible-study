# Psalm 33 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 33 is one of the very few psalms in Book I (Pss 1–41) that lacks a superscription in the Masoretic Text. The Davidic core of Book I attaches *le-David* to almost every poem, which makes the silence here conspicuous. Several scholarly proposals try to explain it. The Septuagint *does* supply a superscription — *tō Dauid* — suggesting that early translators or scribes felt the absence and supplied it. The **Dead Sea Scrolls evidence** is decisive in a different direction: in **11QPs-q and 4QPs-q**, Psalms 32 and 33 are joined as a **single composition**, with no break between them. This suggests that the original liturgical unit may have been Pss 32+33 together, with Ps 33 functioning as a **hymnic appendix** to the *maskil* of confession in Ps 32 — confession leading naturally into communal praise. The MT eventually separated them but preserved the absence of a superscription as a memory of the original union. Hossfeld and Zenger argue persuasively for this reading.

Form-critically, Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 33 as a *Loblied / Hymnus* — a **hymn of praise** in the proper sense, and a near-perfect example of the genre. Gunkel's structural analysis identifies the classic three-part hymn: (1) **call to praise** (vv. 1–3), (2) **reasons for praise / *ki*-clauses** (vv. 4–19), (3) **closing trust expression** (vv. 20–22). Mowinckel saw the psalm as cultic and possibly festival-oriented (Sigmund Mowinckel, *The Psalms in Israel's Worship*) — perhaps connected to the autumn festival of the LORD's enthronement. Westermann classified it as a **descriptive hymn** (as opposed to a declarative thanksgiving) — a hymn that praises God for who he is and what he does in general, rather than for a single specific deliverance.

The Hebrew is musically attentive throughout. Verse 2 calls for *kinor* (כִּנּוֹר, "lyre") and *nevel asor* (נֵבֶל עָשׂוֹר, "harp of ten strings") — the standard pair of stringed instruments in temple worship. Verse 3 commands *shir chadash* (שִׁיר חָדָשׁ, "a new song"), a phrase that will recur across the Psalter (Pss 96:1, 98:1, 144:9, 149:1) and find its eschatological climax in Revelation 5:9 and 14:3. The "new song" is not new in the sense of innovation for its own sake but in the sense of a song that has not yet been composed because the deliverance it celebrates has not yet been received — a song proper to fresh acts of God.

The **creation theology** in vv. 6–9 uses two of the most theologically loaded Hebrew phrases. *Bidvar Yahweh shamayim na'asu* (בִּדְבַר יְהוָה שָׁמַיִם נַעֲשׂוּ, "by the word of the LORD the heavens were made," v. 6) is unmistakably an echo of Genesis 1, where God creates by speech (*vayyomer*). The parallel line *uveruach piv kol-tzva'am* (וּבְרוּחַ פִּיו כָּל־צְבָאָם, "and by the breath of his mouth all their host") pairs *davar* (word) with *ruach* (breath/spirit) in a way that became central to later trinitarian creation-theology — Word and Spirit cooperating in creation. Verse 9 makes the speech-act creation explicit: *ki hu amar vayyehi, hu-tzivvah vayya'amod* (כִּי הוּא אָמַר וַיֶּהִי, הוּא־צִוָּה וַיַּעֲמֹד), "for he spoke, and it came to be; he commanded, and it stood firm."

The anti-militarism in vv. 16–17 is striking in an Iron Age context. The verse names the three components of ANE military power — the king, the warrior, the war horse — and denies that any of them saves. *Ein hammelek nosha be-rov chayil* (אֵין הַמֶּלֶךְ נוֹשָׁע בְּרָב־חָיִל, v. 16), "no king is saved by a great army," would have been a direct critique of the dominant ANE royal ideology in which the king's army *was* the divine presence on earth. The phrase *sheker ha-sus li-tshuah* (שֶׁקֶר הַסּוּס לִתְשׁוּעָה, "the war horse is a lie/false-hope for salvation," v. 17) uses *sheker* — the same word for false witness in the Decalogue — to pronounce the war horse a *liar*. The cavalry promises and cannot deliver.

ANE parallels include Babylonian creation hymns (Enuma Elish) and Egyptian solar hymns, but Psalm 33 deliberately demilitarizes the genre: the creator is praised not for cosmic combat (Marduk vs. Tiamat) but for sovereign **speech**.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Psalm 33 has 22 verses, which has long invited speculation that it is a "broken acrostic" — the same number as the letters of the Hebrew alphabet (matching Pss 25, 34, 38, 103, 145, etc., which are full or partial acrostics). The verse-count without alphabetic structure suggests a **consciously alphabetic-aware composition** — the poet wrote 22 verses to evoke the completeness of an acrostic without actually executing the alphabetic constraint. Adele Berlin and others have noted this pattern as a way the Hebrew poets used acrostic-completeness symbolism even in non-acrostic poems. The 22-verse count signals **comprehensive praise**.

Lowth's parallelism is everywhere and clean. Synonymous in v. 6 ("by the word of the LORD the heavens were made // and by the breath of his mouth all their host"), antithetic in v. 10–11 ("the LORD brings the counsel of the nations to nothing // the counsel of the LORD stands forever"), synthetic in v. 9 ("he spoke, and it came to be // he commanded, and it stood firm").

The strophic structure tracks the form-critical analysis:

- **Strophe 1 (vv. 1–3):** Triple call to praise — *rannenu* (shout), *hodu* (give thanks), *zammeru / shiru* (sing/make melody).
- **Strophe 2 (vv. 4–5):** General reasons — God's word is upright, his work faithful, the earth full of his *chesed*.
- **Strophe 3 (vv. 6–9):** Creation by word.
- **Strophe 4 (vv. 10–12):** Sovereignty over the nations.
- **Strophe 5 (vv. 13–15):** God's all-seeing observation.
- **Strophe 6 (vv. 16–19):** Anti-militarism / true salvation.
- **Strophe 7 (vv. 20–22):** Communal trust closing with *yachalnu lakh* (we hope in you).

The psalm has no *Selah*, which is unusual for a hymn of this length — suggesting it was meant to be sung straight through as a single sustained act of praise.

A literary feature worth noting: the move from cosmic creation (vv. 6–9) to historical providence (vv. 10–12) to individual oversight (vv. 13–15) to particular deliverance (vv. 16–19) is a deliberate **zoom-in**. The same God who spoke the heavens into being is the God who watches the king's army and finds it insufficient and is the God who keeps the soul alive in famine (v. 19). The literary architecture argues that creation theology and pastoral providence are the same theology.

The closing word of the psalm, *yichalnu* (יִחַלְנוּ, "we have hoped/waited," v. 22), echoes the verb in v. 18 (*ha-meyachalim*, "those who hope") — an inclusio that names the proper response to everything the psalm has just said. The hymn ends not in shouting but in waiting.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**In the canonical Psalter.** The DSS witness and the catchword links suggest Pss 32 and 33 were originally a unit — confession of sin (Ps 32) flowing into communal hymn of trust (Ps 33). Hossfeld and Zenger see Pss 32–34 as a deliberate triad: the *maskil* of personal confession (32), the communal hymn (33), the acrostic of teaching (34). Within Book I, Ps 33 is one of the few hymns proper — the surrounding context is dominantly lament — making it a deliberate moment of communal doxology amid individual distress.

**The Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Ps 33 dwells on the creation theology and connects v. 6 to the Genesis account as proof of creation by *ma'amar* (utterance). The rabbis derived the **Ten Utterances of Creation** doctrine partly from this verse (*Pirkei Avot* 5:1, "By ten utterances the world was created"). Rashi takes the psalm as Davidic and reads v. 6 as proof against Aristotelian eternalism. Ibn Ezra, characteristically more literary, focuses on the parallelism and the musical superscription's absence. Radak emphasizes the anti-militarism as polemic against trust in foreign alliances.

In modern Jewish thought, Buber on the I-Thou of creation reads vv. 6–9 as God's address — creation itself as dialogical. Heschel on the prophetic critique of power treats the anti-militarism of vv. 16–17 as part of the Hebrew Bible's persistent **demystification of empire**. Levenson on creation and divine omnipotence (*Creation and the Persistence of Evil*) reads the psalm as part of the strand of Hebrew Bible creation-theology that proceeds **without combat** — God simply speaks. Sarna on Genesis cites Ps 33:6 as the paradigmatic biblical statement of *creatio per verbum*. Zornberg on creation imagery treats the psalm as a meditation on the gap between divine speech and human counsel.

In the Jewish liturgy, Psalm 33 is recited on **Rosh Hashanah** in some traditions and on Sabbath morning in others — the creation theology and the kingship theme align with both occasions. The Western Sephardi rite includes it among the festival psalms.

**The Christian trajectory.** Augustine in his *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Ps 33 reads the *new song* of v. 3 as the song of the new humanity in Christ — the *novum canticum* is the song of the redeemed who have come to know what the old creation could not yet know. He treats v. 6 (creation by word and breath) as a trinitarian text — the Word is Christ, the breath is the Spirit. Athanasius in his Christological controversies cites v. 6 as proof of the Word's eternal preexistence (*Contra Arianos* II.31).

Cassiodorus reads the psalm as a hymn of the church gathered. Aquinas in his Psalms commentary takes vv. 6–9 as a major proof-text for *creatio ex nihilo* — God's commanding word creates from nothing, with no pre-existing matter. **Maximus the Confessor** (7th c.), working out his theology of the *logoi* (the divine reasons inhering in created things), draws on Ps 33:6 to argue that every creature has its being-and-meaning rooted in the Word — each thing's *logos* is a participation in the Logos who spoke it. This becomes foundational for Eastern Orthodox theology of creation and for later Christian thought (in the West, this Maximian theology is recovered in Bonaventure and in 20th-century theologians like Hans Urs von Balthasar).

Luther on Ps 33 (in his lectures) reads the psalm as the church's song of trust under persecution — the anti-militarism of vv. 16–17 becomes the believer's confidence that the gospel does not need imperial power to advance. Calvin's commentary takes the psalm as Davidic and reads vv. 16–17 as polemic against political alliances; he applies it directly to the geopolitics of mid-16th-century Europe.

Among modern critical interpreters: Gunkel (paradigmatic hymn), Mowinckel (cultic, possibly festival), Westermann (descriptive hymn), Hans-Joachim Kraus (post-exilic hymn drawing on creation traditions and second Isaiah), Hossfeld and Zenger (originally connected to Ps 32; deliberately 22-verse), John Goldingay (hymn of God's universal sovereignty), Marvin Tate, Gordon Wenham (canonical reading: ethical implications of creation theology). Walter Brueggemann on creation and chaos puts Ps 33 in the "psalms of orientation" — this is the world ordered by sovereign speech. Patrick D. Miller on divine sovereignty. William P. Brown on biblical creation theology. Beth Tanner reads the psalm pastorally as antidote to political despair.

**NT use and echo.** Direct citations of Psalm 33 in the NT are few, but the conceptual presence is large. The Johannine prologue (*In the beginning was the Word*, Jn 1:1–3) is unintelligible without Ps 33:6 in the background — John is reading Ps 33 through Genesis 1 to identify Christ as the creating Word. Hebrews 11:3 (*by faith we understand that the universe was created by the word of God, so that what is seen was not made out of things that are visible*) is a near-direct paraphrase of Ps 33:6, 9. The "new song" tradition climaxes in Revelation 5:9 and 14:3 — the song of the redeemed before the Lamb. And the anti-militarism of vv. 16–17 echoes through Zech 4:6 ("not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit"), Mt 26:52 ("all who take the sword will perish by the sword"), and 2 Cor 10:4 ("the weapons of our warfare are not of the flesh").

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — Word, Counsel, and the Limits of the Sword

**Tension 1: Creation by word and the doctrine of *creatio ex nihilo*.** Psalm 33:6, 9 is the strongest single Hebrew Bible text for creation by sovereign divine speech. But the psalm does not explicitly say creation is *out of nothing* — it says God spoke and it came to be. Patristic and medieval Christian readers (Athanasius, Augustine, Aquinas) read the psalm with Genesis 1 and 2 Maccabees 7:28 to derive the formal doctrine of *creatio ex nihilo*.

- **Position A (Robust *creatio ex nihilo*):** Athanasius, Aquinas, classical Catholic and Reformed theology. The psalm's "he spoke and it came to be" presupposes that there was nothing prior to that speech for the speech to act upon. The contrast with Mesopotamian combat-creation myths (Enuma Elish) is precisely the absence of pre-existing chaos that God must defeat. Steelman: any reading of creation in which God works on pre-existing matter compromises divine sovereignty and creates an eternal dualism.
- **Position B (Creation as ordering, with creatio ex nihilo as later development):** Levenson, some critical scholarship. The Hebrew Bible itself is more interested in God's ordering of chaos than in the absolute origination question; *creatio ex nihilo* is a Hellenistic-period development crystallized in 2 Macc 7:28 and read back into Genesis. Steelman: the psalm celebrates God's sovereign **ordering**, not necessarily his creation from absolute nothing — and that ordering is the more biblical category.
- **Position C (Eastern Orthodox / Maximian):** Maximus the Confessor and the Eastern tradition. Creation is by Word and contains the *logoi* — every created thing has its meaning in participation with the Logos. *Ex nihilo* is true but incomplete; the deeper truth is that creation is **iconic**, every creature pointing to its divine source. Steelman: the psalm's pairing of *davar* (word) and *ruach* (breath/spirit) in v. 6 is doing theological work the West has often missed.

**Tension 2: The anti-militarism of vv. 16–17 and Christian engagement with state power.** The psalm flatly denies that the king is saved by his army or the war horse can rescue. Christian readers across the tradition have asked: what does this mean for Christian participation in war and statecraft?

- **Position A (Christian pacifism):** The Anabaptist tradition, John Howard Yoder (*The Politics of Jesus*), Stanley Hauerwas, and the historic peace churches read Ps 33:16–17 with Mt 26:52 and conclude the psalm forbids Christian reliance on military power. Steelman: the psalm is plain — the war horse is a *sheker*, a lie. The church that builds its security on military strength has trusted a liar.
- **Position B (Just war / Augustinian):** Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin, classical Christian just-war tradition. The psalm denies that the army *saves* in an ultimate sense, but does not deny that the magistrate may rightly bear the sword (Rom 13:4). Steelman: the psalm critiques **trust** in the army, not its **use** under God's authority. The king who trusts his army is condemned; the king who uses his army while trusting God is not.
- **Position C (Constantinian critique with discernment):** Brueggemann, Wright, much contemporary theology. The psalm's polemic against trusting military power applies sharply to Christian communities that have functionally identified the gospel with national or imperial security. The question is not whether to participate in any state but whether the church's trust is ultimately located there. Steelman: the psalm is honest about the seductiveness of military power and demands ongoing discernment about where the church's hope really sits.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 33 holds together a creation theology, a political theology, and a pastoral theology that the modern church often divides. The God who spoke the heavens into being is the God who frustrates the counsel of the nations and is the God who keeps the soul alive in famine. The cosmic and the political and the personal are one theology because there is one God doing all three. The psalm's persistent verb is **speak** — God speaks creation, God's word stands forever, the king's bluster is silenced, and the people respond by waiting. The hymn ends in *yichalnu lakh* (we have hoped in you), not in conquest or self-confidence but in the active waiting that is the right human posture before a sovereign creator.

**Closing question:** What is your war horse — what false hope are you trusting because the real source of help requires waiting? And what would change if you took the psalm seriously enough to dismount?

## The Thread

- Psalm 33:6 ("by the word of the LORD the heavens were made, and by the breath of his mouth all their host") is the conceptual ancestor of John 1:1–3 and Hebrews 11:3 — Christian identification of Christ as the creating Word is unintelligible without this verse.
- The "new song" of v. 3 recurs in Pss 96, 98, 144, 149 and finds its eschatological end in Revelation 5:9 and 14:3 — the song of the redeemed before the Lamb is the ultimate fresh song proper to the freshest deliverance.
- The anti-militarism of vv. 16–17 echoes in Zech 4:6, Mt 26:52, and 2 Cor 10:4 — the persistent biblical refusal to identify divine help with military power.
- The 22-verse alphabetic-aware structure connects Ps 33 to Pss 25, 34, 38, 103, 145 and to the broader biblical use of acrostic completeness as a sign that the praise being offered is **comprehensive** — A to Z, every letter, nothing left out.
