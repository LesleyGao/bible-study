# 1 Corinthians 16 — Collection, Travel, and Maranatha

## Lens 1: The Historical World

The closing chapter of 1 Corinthians is sometimes treated as administrative housekeeping appended to the great theological argument of chapter 15, but historically it is one of the densest passages in the Pauline corpus. In thirty-five economical verses, Paul establishes a pan-Mediterranean fundraising practice, sketches a travel itinerary that organized his Aegean ministry for two years, names co-workers whose households formed the social backbone of his churches, and closes the letter in his own hand with an Aramaic liturgical formula that has become one of the strongest pieces of evidence for very early high Christology. To read chapter 16 well is to see the apostolic mission as it actually operated.

The collection (*logia*, λογία, v. 1; *charis*, χάρις, "gift," in the parallel discussions of 2 Corinthians 8–9) is the central project Paul refers to. From Galatia (1 Cor 16:1), through Macedonia and Achaia (Rom 15:25–28; 2 Cor 8–9), and back through Asia, Paul has been organizing a financial offering from his Gentile churches for "the saints in Jerusalem." The decision to organize the collection traces back to the Jerusalem agreement of Galatians 2:10, where the Jerusalem leadership (James, Cephas, John) asked only that Paul "remember the poor" — and Paul says, "the very thing I was eager to do." Dieter Georgi's *Remembering the Poor: The History of Paul's Collection for Jerusalem* (German 1965, English 1992) reads the collection theologically as Paul's enactment of the eschatological pilgrimage of the nations to Zion, fulfilling Isaiah 60:5–7 ("the wealth of the nations shall come to you"), Micah 4:1–2, and Tobit 13:11. Bruce Longenecker's *Remember the Poor: Paul, Poverty, and the Greco-Roman World* (2010) situates Pauline almsgiving against the background of Greco-Roman euergetism: in the imperial economic order, civic giving (*euergesia*) flowed laterally between elite peers and downward only to political clients, almost never to the structural poor. Paul's instruction that "each of you" — whatever the household's resources — set aside a contribution to be given to people none of the donors had ever met, in another province, of a different ethnic identity, is a counter-cultural redistribution. David Downs (*The Offering of the Gentiles*, 2008) argues that Paul understood the collection cultically, as a Gentile sacrificial offering that ratified Jewish-Gentile unity in the Messiah.

The mechanics in 16:1–4 are striking. Paul instructs the Corinthians, like the Galatians, to set aside funds *kata mian sabbatou* (κατὰ μίαν σαββάτου), "on the first day of the week" (v. 2), proportional to prosperity, so that no special collection will be needed when he arrives. He will then send accredited messengers, with letters of recommendation, to carry the gift to Jerusalem. This is the earliest evidence in any Christian text for a regular Sunday gathering distinct from the Jewish Sabbath. It is also the earliest evidence for proportional, planned, decentralized Christian giving — a system of distributed accumulation rather than dramatic single-occasion offerings — and for accountable transmission of funds with the donor community appointing its own representatives. By the second century, Justin Martyr (*1 Apology* 67) describes a Sunday collection at the Eucharist that maps directly onto Paul's prescription.

Paul's travel plans (vv. 5–9) place him in Ephesus, intending to remain until Pentecost — a Jewish festival reckoning still natural to him — before going through Macedonia to Corinth, possibly to winter there. The "wide door (*thyra*, θύρα) for effective work" with "many adversaries" (v. 9) is corroborated by the riot at Ephesus in Acts 19:23–41 and Paul's reference to having "fought with beasts at Ephesus" in 1 Cor 15:32. Timothy is being sent ahead (v. 10) and Apollos has declined to come "now" (v. 12), a remark that is socially careful given the Apollos-Paul partisan factionalism Paul confronted in chapters 1–4. The specific naming of households — Stephanas (the first converts in Achaia, v. 15), Fortunatus, and Achaicus (v. 17), and the household of Aquila and Prisca with the church meeting in their house in Ephesus (v. 19) — gives a roster of the early urban Christian network. Wayne Meeks (*The First Urban Christians*, 1983) and Gerd Theissen (*The Social Setting of Pauline Christianity*, 1982) reconstruct from such names a portrait of moderately prosperous artisanal and freedman households whose homes hosted the *ekklēsia*. Aquila and Prisca, expelled from Rome under Claudius's edict of AD 49 (Suetonius, *Claudius* 25; Acts 18:2), have moved to Ephesus and are now hosts of a house-church there.

The most arresting historical feature of the chapter, however, is the Aramaic word in v. 22: *Maranatha*. Paul writes a Greek letter to a Greek-speaking congregation in a Roman colonial capital, and at the moment of closing he reaches for an Aramaic liturgical phrase that he assumes the Corinthians will recognize. It is a fossil — a piece of Aramaic-speaking Jerusalem Christianity that has traveled to a Hellenistic congregation and become embedded in the church's worship vocabulary. The Didache 10:6 preserves the same formula at the close of the eucharistic prayers, in identical liturgical position. Larry Hurtado (*Lord Jesus Christ*, 2003) and Richard Bauckham (*Jesus and the God of Israel*, 2008) have built on this datum the case that high Christology — devotional address of Jesus as "Lord" in a manner reserved in Jewish prayer for the God of Israel — was not a late, gradual, Hellenistic accretion but a feature of Aramaic-speaking Jewish Christianity within months or years of the resurrection. Oscar Cullmann's *Early Christian Worship* (1953) and Joachim Jeremias developed the same point earlier. The Aramaic survives in Paul's Greek letter precisely because it is too holy and too established to translate.

## Lens 2: Literature and Argument

The chapter follows the conventions of a Hellenistic letter closing — travel plans, commendations, greetings, autograph, final benediction — but Paul deploys those conventions in a way that is structurally significant for the letter as a whole. Hans-Josef Klauck (*Ancient Letters and the New Testament*, 2006) and Stanley Stowers have shown that ancient letters had standard closing formulae, but Paul characteristically loads the closing with theological weight. In 1 Corinthians 16 the conventions become carriers of pastoral and doctrinal pressure.

The chapter divides into six brief movements. Verses 1–4 give the collection instructions, opening with the formulaic *peri de* ("now concerning"), the same phrase Paul uses to introduce topics raised by the Corinthians' letter (cf. 7:1, 7:25, 8:1, 12:1) — suggesting they had asked about the collection. Verses 5–9 sketch travel plans. Verses 10–12 commend Timothy and explain Apollos's absence. Verses 13–14 contain a tight catechetical sequence of imperatives: "Be watchful, stand firm in the faith, act like men (*andrizesthe*, ἀνδρίζεσθε), be strong (*krataiousthe*, κραταιοῦσθε); let all that you do be done in love." Verses 15–18 commend the household of Stephanas. Verses 19–24 contain greetings, the holy-kiss instruction, the autograph signature, the *anathema*-Maranatha formula, and the closing grace and love.

The catechetical sequence in vv. 13–14 deserves attention. *Andrizesthe* — "act like men," literally "be courageous" — is the LXX vocabulary of Joshua 1:6 ("be strong [*ischye*] and courageous [*andrizou*]") and Deuteronomy 31:6–7. Paul is invoking the language Moses used to commission Joshua to take the land and using it for the Corinthians' spiritual courage. The five imperatives form a chiasm: the four virtues of watchfulness, faith, courage, and strength bracket the climactic instruction in v. 14: "let all that you do be done in love." Paul has spent the entire letter arguing that love is the measure (chapter 13), and the closing imperatives subordinate even courage and strength to it.

The Greek vocabulary of the chapter rewards close attention. *Logia* (collection, v. 1) is technical for a religious-civic levy or charitable fund. *Kata mian sabbatou* (v. 2) is grammatically significant: *mian* is a feminine numeral standing in for *prōtēn* (first), reflecting the Semitic idiom of counting days from Sabbath ("first day of the week" rather than "Sunday"); the phrase shows the synagogue-rooted timekeeping of early Christians even as they distinguish their gathering from the Sabbath. *Thyra* (door, v. 9) is Paul's standard metaphor for missional opportunity (cf. 2 Cor 2:12; Col 4:3). *Philēma hagion* (φίλημα ἅγιον), the holy kiss (v. 20, also Rom 16:16; 1 Thess 5:26), was a distinctive early Christian liturgical practice cementing kinship across social boundaries (Justin, *1 Apology* 65; Tertullian, *On Prayer* 18). *Anathema* (ἀνάθεμα, v. 22), the LXX word for the *herem* (the devoted thing, often the devoted-to-destruction of Joshua), here used of any who "has no love for the Lord." *Maranatha* (Μαράνα θα or Μαράν αθα), the Aramaic liturgical phrase, hinges on word division.

The autograph in v. 21 ("I, Paul, write this greeting with my own hand") is a forensic detail. Paul typically dictated to a secretary (see Romans 16:22 where Tertius identifies himself as the amanuensis) and signed in his own hand at the end, both as authentication and as a personal touch. The same authentication pattern appears in Galatians 6:11, 2 Thessalonians 3:17, and Philemon 19. For a letter whose authority Paul claims comes from "the command of the Lord" (14:37), the autograph mattered.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

The Jewish current of the chapter is densest at three points: the Maranatha formula, the Sunday gathering, and the collection.

*Maranatha* is two words in Aramaic, and word division is contested. *Marana tha* — vocative plus imperative — yields "Our Lord, come!" and reads as an eschatological prayer for the parousia. *Maran atha* — possessive plus perfect verb — yields "Our Lord has come" and reads as a confessional. The grammatical and Didache evidence weighs heavily toward the imperative reading: Didache 10:6 uses the formula at the close of the Eucharist as a prayer ("if anyone is holy, let him come; if anyone is not, let him repent. Maranatha. Amen."), and the immediate context in 1 Cor 16:22 (anathema on the unloving plus Maranatha) parallels the Didache's anathema-formula closely. Modern critical consensus (Cullmann, Fee, Thiselton, Hurtado, Bauckham) reads it as eschatological prayer. The formula's preservation in Aramaic in Greek-speaking Gentile contexts is the philological miracle: it indicates that the Aramaic-speaking Jerusalem church had developed a liturgical practice of addressing Jesus as *Mar* ("Lord") in petition — and that this practice was so foundational it migrated untranslated into the Greek-speaking mission. The Old Testament's reservation of *Maran/Adonai* for the God of Israel makes the Christological implication of the formula explicit.

The instruction to set aside funds *kata mian sabbatou* is the earliest evidence for the Christian Sunday. Whether by then Sunday had become a distinct gathering day or whether v. 2 still describes a *household* practice (set aside at home, on the first day of each week) preparatory to the eventual collection trip, the Sunday-after-Sabbath rhythm is in place. By Pliny's letter to Trajan (*Ep.* 10.96, c. AD 112) the Christians of Bithynia gather "on a fixed day before dawn" to sing hymns to Christ as to a god — the Sunday morning gathering attested as a fixed feature. Justin's *1 Apology* 67 (c. 155) gives the most detailed early description of the Sunday liturgy: gathering, reading, exhortation, common prayer, kiss, eucharistic offering, distribution to the poor. Ignatius's *Magnesians* 9 polemically distinguishes Christian Sunday observance from Jewish Sabbath practice. Constantine's edict of 321 made Sunday a civic day of rest. The Reformation produced a sustained debate on whether Sunday is a Christian Sabbath or a different kind of day. The Westminster Confession (21.7–8) and Larger Catechism articulate strict Reformed Sabbatarianism: the moral substance of the fourth commandment transferred from the seventh day to the first. The Lutheran Augsburg Confession (28.59) and Calvin's *Institutes* II.8 take a more moderate line: the ceremonial Sabbath is abolished, the moral element of regular rest and worship preserved, but Sunday observance is ecclesial custom, not commandment. The Anabaptist tradition resisted strict Sabbatarianism. Seventh-Day Adventists from the mid-nineteenth century onward (Ellen White, Joseph Bates) returned to Saturday observance, reading the Sunday move as a Constantinian imposition. The Catholic Catechism (CCC 2168–2173, 2174–2188) treats Sunday as the Christian Lord's Day in which Sabbath is fulfilled.

The collection theme has its richest patristic and medieval afterlife in the corporal works of mercy and ecclesial almsgiving. The early church developed structured almsgiving as central to Christian identity: Justin's *1 Apology* 67 describes the Sunday collection going to "orphans and widows, the sick, prisoners, strangers"; Cyprian's *De Opere et Eleemosynis* makes almsgiving redemptive (a position Augustine would moderate); Chrysostom's homilies on 1 Corinthians and 2 Corinthians return repeatedly to the duty of giving to the poor. The medieval Catholic tradition systematized the seven corporal works of mercy. The Reformation produced multiple positions: Lutheran proportional giving without the Catholic merit theology; Reformed tithing debates; Anabaptist mutual aid (Hutterite community of goods being one strong reading); Wesleyan generosity. Modern responses range from the Catholic Catechism's emphasis on the universal destination of goods (CCC 2402–2406) to the prosperity-gospel distortion that reads giving as transactional with God. Bruce Longenecker's reconstruction of early Christian giving has chastened both ends, showing that Pauline practice was structurally redistributive in a way that prosperity-gospel exchange theology is not.

The greetings and household commendations in vv. 15–20 connect to the larger Pauline theology of the *ekklēsia* as household. Paul calls the Corinthians "brothers" (*adelphoi*), instructs them to "be subject" (*hypotassēsthe*) to fellow workers like Stephanas, and prescribes the holy kiss as a liturgical greeting that crossed the social lines of slave/free, male/female, Jew/Gentile (cf. Gal 3:28). The patristic period elaborated the kiss as a regular liturgical feature (Tertullian, *On Prayer* 18) until medieval social anxieties moved it to the *pax* between same-sex liturgical participants and eventually to a passing of a *pax-board*. The Reformation generally retired the practice. Charismatic renewal and some contemporary Christian traditions have recovered the holy kiss or reformulated it as the passing of the peace.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

Three theological claims rise to the surface of this seemingly administrative chapter.

First, the collection is not optional charity but a constitutive act of ecclesial unity. Paul does not present the Jerusalem collection as one good cause among many; he treats it as the practical theology of Jewish-Gentile unity in the Messiah. Gentile believers, who have received the spiritual blessings of the gospel from the Jewish church (Romans 15:27), are paying back materially. The collection is *koinōnia* — partnership, common participation — made tangible. Dieter Georgi's reading of the collection as Paul's enactment of the eschatological pilgrimage of the nations to Zion captures something the text supports: Paul's enormous investment in this project across multiple letters and several years of ministry, culminating in his final journey to Jerusalem (Acts 21:17–26) at considerable personal risk, makes sense only if the collection bore theological weight. The Pauline gospel produces material redistribution across ethnic and geographic lines, or it has not produced what it claims to produce. This is not a proto-socialist reading of Paul. It is the observation that for Paul the gospel cannot be confessed in liturgy and denied in economics.

Second, the Sunday gathering is a temporal claim. The first day of the week is the day of resurrection. The Christian week begins where the Jewish week reaches its Sabbath rest; or, in the Augustinian-Catholic reading, the Christian week's first day *is* the eschatological Sabbath, the eighth day, the day after the seventh that points to the world to come. By gathering on the first day, the church makes a weekly confession that Jesus is risen and that history has a different shape than it would otherwise have. The Sabbatarian-versus-Lord's-Day debates are real, and reasonable Christians have settled them in different ways. The exegetical baseline of 1 Cor 16:2, however, is not a polemic in either direction; it is the calm assumption that "the first day of every week" is the natural rhythm of the Corinthian assembly. Christian time has a structure, and that structure is given by the resurrection.

Third, the closing formula stitches the entire letter together with a particular theological grammar. The juxtaposition of *anathema* and Maranatha in v. 22 — "if anyone has no love for the Lord, let him be accursed; our Lord, come" — is not a rhetorical excess. Paul has spent fifteen chapters arguing that the gospel produces a community ordered by love — that gifts without love are nothing (chapter 13), that knowledge without love puffs up (chapter 8), that the body's lowest member is honored equally (chapter 12). The closing curse on those who have *no love* for the Lord (the Greek *philei*, φιλεῖ, used here, the affectionate verb) is not a doctrinal excommunication but an ethical exposure: those who do not love Jesus are outside the community whose definition is love. The Maranatha that follows is the appeal to the Lord himself to come and resolve what the church cannot finally resolve on its own. Eschatology, ethics, and Christology converge in a single Aramaic word.

The chapter ends with grace and love (vv. 23–24), the closing benediction Paul uses to seal his letters, with the remarkable autograph "my love be with you all in Christ Jesus" — Paul rarely speaks this directly of his own affection. Given the harshness of much of the letter (the partisanship, the incest, the lawsuits, the disordered worship, the resurrection deniers), the closing word is not one of relieved valediction but of personal love that has refused, throughout, to leave the Corinthians to themselves. The chapter that begins with collection bookkeeping ends with the apostle's own affection. The two are not unrelated: the bookkeeping was always an act of love, and the love takes practical form in offerings, kisses, hospitality, courage, and the prayer that the Lord himself will come and finish what the apostle and his churches have begun.
