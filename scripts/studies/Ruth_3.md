# Ruth 3: Uncovering the Feet at Midnight

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter unfolds at a **threshing floor** (*goren*, גֹּרֶן) at the end of the harvest, and the location matters in ways modern readers easily miss. The threshing floor in Iron Age Israel was a flat, hard, exposed surface — typically on a windward hilltop just outside a town — where harvested grain was beaten with sledges or trampled by oxen, then winnowed by tossing it in the late-afternoon breeze so that the chaff blew away and the heavier grain fell back to the ground. It was the symbolic and economic center of the agricultural year. It was also a publicly liminal space: not inside the town, not yet domestic, communal but outside the protective walls. Threshing floors appear in the Hebrew Bible as places of judgment (Hosea 9:1-2), of theophany and altar (the threshing floor of Araunah/Ornan in 2 Samuel 24 / 1 Chronicles 21, which becomes the site of Solomon's Temple), and of sexual transgression (Hosea 9:1 explicitly accuses Israel of "loving a prostitute's wages on every threshing floor"). The location of Ruth's approach is, even before any action, charged with sexual implication and ritual weight at once.

Workers slept at the threshing floor during harvest to guard the grain heap from theft — a practical necessity that also explains why Boaz is alone at night with a pile of barley and a meal of bread and wine. The Mishnah (Peah 6:11, Ma'aserot 1:6) and later sources record this practice; Carol Meyers's archaeological work on Iron Age Israelite agriculture and Lawrence Stager's seminal essay on the Israelite family economy ("The Archaeology of the Family in Ancient Israel," BASOR 1985) document the threshing-floor labor system. Boaz at 3:7 has eaten, drunk, and his "heart is merry" (*vayyitav libbo*, וַיִּיטַב לִבּוֹ) — a Hebrew idiom for the warm, slightly inebriated contentment that follows a celebratory meal (cf. Judges 19:6, 19:9, 1 Samuel 25:36, Esther 1:10). He lies down at the edge of the grain heap and falls asleep. The narrator is setting the scene with deliberate care: a man warmly fed, alone, asleep, in the dark, at a place laden with sexual associations.

Naomi's instructions to Ruth are precisely calibrated. "Wash, anoint yourself (*sukh*, סוּךְ), put on your cloak (*simlah*, שִׂמְלָה)" (3:3) — the same three-part preparation Ezekiel 16:9-10 uses of YHWH preparing his bride and 2 Samuel 12:20 uses of David ending his mourning. The combination signals the end of mourning and the deliberate, formal presentation of a woman as marriageable. Ruth has been in Naomi's mourning shadow throughout chapter 1 and 2; chapter 3 begins by ending that liminal status. The cloak is also significant — *simlah* is the standard outer garment, and at 3:15 Boaz will fill the corner of that same garment with grain, an act loaded with quasi-betrothal symbolism.

The verb at the chapter's center is **uncovering** *margelot* (מַרְגְּלוֹת) — "the place of his feet." The Hebrew is plural and ambiguous; it occurs only here and in Daniel 10:6. The word *raglayim* (feet) is also the standard biblical Hebrew euphemism for the genitals (Isaiah 7:20 of pubic hair, Exodus 4:25 of Moses's son's genitals, Deuteronomy 28:57 of childbirth, possibly 2 Kings 18:27/Isaiah 36:12). Whether *margelot* in Ruth 3 means literally "the area at the foot of the bed" or euphemistically "the genital area" has been intensely debated. Edward Campbell's Anchor Bible commentary, Frederic Bush's WBC, Robert Hubbard, Jack Sasson, and Kirsten Nielsen each take different positions, generally landing on a deliberate ambiguity that the narrator has constructed precisely *because* the act stays inside that ambiguity. The narrator is letting the reader see what could be happening without specifying that it is. Phyllis Trible reads the scene as decorous courtship within a charged space; André LaCocque reads it as deliberately sexually transgressive within proper limits; Pamela Tamarkin Reis reads the entire scene as a calculated proposal that uses the language of sexual approach without crossing into sexual act.

The proposal Ruth makes at 3:9 is legally specific. "**Spread your wing** (*kanaph*) **over your servant, for you are a redeemer** (*go'el*)." The verb *paras* with *kanaph* (פָּרַשׂ כָּנָף) is the technical Hebrew idiom for a marriage proposal — Ezekiel 16:8 uses exactly this phrase of YHWH marrying personified Jerusalem ("I spread my wing over you and covered your nakedness"). Ruth is invoking, in formal language, both the protection imagery from Boaz's own blessing at 2:12 (where YHWH's wings sheltered her) and the legal vocabulary of marriage and redemption. She is also explicitly invoking Boaz's status as *go'el* — collapsing two distinct legal categories (kinsman-redeemer and levirate husband) into a single proposal. This conflation is itself a legal innovation; the Pentateuchal codes don't quite support it, but the book of Ruth treats them as continuous in a way the rabbis would later have to harmonize.

The "**six measures of barley**" Boaz pours into Ruth's cloak at 3:15 are a striking detail. The Hebrew text doesn't specify the measure — six *what*? The Targum on Ruth interprets as six *seahs* (which would be roughly two ephahs, about 88 liters or 50-65 kg — too much for a woman to carry alone). The Talmud (Bava Batra 14b) and Rashi suggest six *omers* (a smaller measure, more carryable). Most modern commentators read it as deliberately unspecified, a generous quantity meant to send Naomi a sign. The detail at 3:17 that Boaz says "you must not go back empty-handed (*reqam*, רֵיקָם) to your mother-in-law" deliberately echoes Naomi's own self-description at 1:21: "the LORD has brought me back empty (*reqam*)." The six measures of barley are, narratively, the first material reversal of the emptiness Naomi has carried since chapter 1.

A few Hebrew terms transform the chapter when you can hear them. ***Manoach*** (מָנוֹחַ), "rest," is what Naomi says she seeks for Ruth at 3:1 — a word with deep covenantal resonance (Deuteronomy 12:9-10 uses it of the rest YHWH gives Israel in the land; Ruth 1:9 uses it of the "rest" of a husband's house). Naomi is naming her project in covenantal language. ***Eshet hayil*** (אֵשֶׁת חַיִל), "worthy woman," is what Boaz calls Ruth at 3:11 — the same phrase that opens Proverbs 31:10. The Hebrew Bible uses *hayil* of warriors and substantial men (*gibbor hayil* of Boaz at 2:1); applied to a woman, it signals comparable substance and capability. The placement of Ruth as *eshet hayil* is striking because in the canonical order of the Tanakh (Hebrew Bible), Ruth follows Proverbs immediately, and the rabbinic tradition reads Boaz's speech as an inscription of Ruth into the category that the Proverbs poem will later extol. ***Davaq*** (דָּבַק) returns implicitly: Ruth's clinging to Naomi in chapter 1 is now her clinging to a future. ***Lailah*** (לַיְלָה), "night," frames the entire scene; the chapter is the only one in the book set at night, and the temporal marking is deliberate — what happens here is hidden, exposed only by Ruth's morning return and Boaz's gift.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Ruth 3 is the book's most artfully ambiguous chapter and the most literarily charged. The narrator's restraint reaches its peak: we are told what Ruth does (uncovers his feet, lies down), what Boaz does (startles, asks, blesses, instructs), and what is exchanged (a vow, a bundle of grain). We are not told what happens between the moment Ruth lies down at 3:7 and the moment Boaz wakes startled at 3:8. Everything theological, ethical, and legal that the chapter does depends on that gap.

The chapter is a tight three-part structure:
- **Frame opening (3:1-5):** Naomi instructs, Ruth assents.
- **The threshing floor (3:6-15):** preparation, descent, uncovering, exchange, gift.
- **Frame closing (3:16-18):** Naomi receives, interprets, predicts.

Within the threshing-floor scene, the encounter between Ruth and Boaz (3:8-13) is the chapter's literary core. It is structured as a triple exchange: Boaz's startled question and Ruth's identification (3:8-9), Boaz's blessing (3:10-11), Boaz's legal qualification (3:12-13). The pacing slows down at the legal qualification — Boaz must name that there is a nearer redeemer — to insert the legal complication that chapter 4 will resolve. The narrator could have left this out and allowed Boaz to redeem directly. The fact that the complication is introduced at all is a signal that the legal procedure matters to the book: redemption must go through the proper legal forms.

The chapter is laced with intentional **echoes** of other biblical scenes. The threshing-floor seduction recalls (and, depending on the reading, reverses) Genesis 19:30-38 — Lot's daughters lying with their drunken father in the cave, the scene that produces Moab. Ruth, the great-granddaughter of Moab (genealogically through generations), now appears at night to a sleeping man whose "heart is merry" with wine, and what happens between them produces the line of David. The narrator is constructing a typological inversion: where the Moabite origin was incest in the dark, the Moabite redemption is decorous proposal in the dark. Most modern commentators (Campbell, Bush, LaCocque, Trible, Sasson) note this intertextual play; whether the story affirms or corrects the Moabite origin is part of what the reader is being asked to weigh.

The chapter also echoes **Genesis 38** (Tamar and Judah). Tamar disguises herself, presents herself to Judah, and conceives Perez — the same Perez whose descendants the chapter 4 genealogy will trace. The Tamar story is referenced explicitly in the elders' blessing at 4:12. Robert Alter's *The Art of Biblical Narrative* identifies both echoes as deliberate. The book of Ruth is in continuous conversation with Genesis: the matriarchal pattern (foreign or marginal woman taking initiative to secure the line) runs from Tamar through Ruth into the Davidic genealogy.

The narrator's craft with **point of view** is also worth attending to. We see Ruth do what Naomi instructed; we see her at the threshing floor; we see Boaz wake. But we are inside no one's head. The narrator does not tell us Ruth's intent, Boaz's interpretation, or whether what happened between them is what either of them planned. The reader is forced to infer from the dialogue, and the dialogue itself is carefully framed: Boaz's "you have not gone after young men" (3:10) suggests he reads the encounter as a marriage proposal rather than a sexual encounter, but the very fact that he must name the alternative ("young men") signals that the alternative was readable in the situation. Meir Sternberg, in *The Poetics of Biblical Narrative*, treats this as a paradigm case of how the Hebrew Bible uses dialogue as the bearer of moral interpretation while leaving narrative reportage spare.

There are no major textual variants of significance in Ruth 3. The DSS Ruth fragments do not preserve large portions of this chapter, but what survives is consistent with the Masoretic text. The LXX renders the chapter with some interpretive smoothing — for instance, *margelot* is rendered with the Greek *pros podas autou* ("at his feet"), which fixes one reading of the ambiguity. The Aramaic Targum on Ruth substantially expands the dialogue, having Boaz interrogate Ruth about her motives and Ruth answer with extensive rabbinic-style legal argumentation. The Targum is reading the chapter back through the rabbinic harmonization of *go'el* and *yibbum*.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, Ruth 3 is the chapter where the book's two major plot questions converge. The personal question — will Naomi find rest, will Ruth find a husband — and the legal question — who will redeem the family land and continue the line — are fused at the threshing floor. The chapter resolves neither, but it makes their fusion visible. The book's quiet theological argument continues: the resolution will come not through theophany but through the slow working of legal procedure and human initiative.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** *Ruth Rabbah* 5-6 expands the chapter substantially, reading Naomi's instructions as a model of pious counsel and Ruth's actions as a model of modesty and obedience. The midrash works hard to defuse the chapter's sexual ambiguity: it has Ruth dressing in her finest only after she arrives at the threshing floor (so she would not attract attention on the road), and it has Boaz spend the entire night in fervent prayer rather than in sleep. The rabbinic tradition is uncomfortable with what the chapter could mean; the midrash domesticates it. Rashi follows the midrashic line, reading Ruth as wholly chaste and Boaz as wholly restrained. Ibn Ezra is more willing to acknowledge the surface meaning while denying any sexual contact. The Talmud (Sanhedrin 19b-20a) reads Boaz's age as advanced — making the encounter all the more clearly chaste — and treats his self-restraint as the measure of his righteousness. Maimonides treats Boaz as the model of what Israelite sexual ethics requires under conditions of opportunity. The Zohar reads the entire scene mystically: Ruth uncovering Boaz's "feet" is read as the unification of divine attributes. In modern Jewish scholarship, Avivah Zornberg attends to the chapter as a study in how women navigate a system in which their agency must be encoded as obedience to male authority. Tikva Frymer-Kensky reads Ruth's proposal as the boldest act in the book — she initiates, she names her terms, she claims the redemption right that the law does not strictly grant her. Yair Zakovitch's commentary develops the intertextual play with Genesis 19 and 38 as the chapter's central interpretive key.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic Christianity read Ruth's approach to Boaz typologically: the Gentile soul approaching Christ in faith, the church's unveiled approach to the bridegroom. Origen developed this in his Joshua homilies and in fragments preserved on Ruth; Jerome and Augustine extended it. Ambrose in *De Fide* and elsewhere read the unveiling of the feet as a sign of humility and the spreading of the wing as Christ's covering of the church with grace. The medieval *Glossa Ordinaria* compiled the typological readings; Bernard of Clairvaux's sermons developed the threshing-floor scene as a contemplative trope of the soul's nocturnal encounter with the bridegroom. The medieval Catholic tradition also treated the chapter through the lens of *consilium* (counsel) — Naomi's instruction as a model of older female wisdom guiding the younger toward marriage. The Reformation shifted register. Luther's prefaces praised the chapter as evidence of God's care for the marginalized through the orderly working of legal procedure rather than miracle. Calvin's sermons on Ruth (1554-55) gave sustained, surprisingly frank attention to chapter 3, defending Boaz's chastity and Ruth's modesty while acknowledging the apparent indecency of the situation; Calvin's reading is one of the more careful pre-modern engagements with the chapter's surface tension. The post-Reformation Pietist and devotional tradition (Matthew Henry, John Wesley) generally followed the typological-allegorical reading. The critical period (Wellhausen, Gunkel) treated the chapter as ethnographic evidence of ancient Israelite marriage customs and largely sidestepped the typology. The contemporary turn has been more willing to confront the chapter's sexual ambiguity directly. Phyllis Trible's *God and the Rhetoric of Sexuality* reads it as a women's narrative of agency under constraint. Athalya Brenner's *A Feminist Companion to Ruth* gathers feminist readings; Mieke Bal in *Lethal Love* treats Ruth as a paradigm of a woman who rewrites the story she is in. Pamela Tamarkin Reis ("Ruth: Beyond the Pale," 2003) reads the entire chapter as deliberately calculated proposal that uses sexual approach as a legal claim. André LaCocque's *Ruth: A Continental Commentary* defends a more sexually transgressive reading — the chapter is "scandalous" in proper measure, and the scandal is the point: the Moabite woman crosses the boundary of decorum to secure the line that produces David. Brueggemann's *Ruth: A Conversation* reads it as a study in how the marginal use ambiguity to claim what direct request cannot achieve. Robert Alter's translation and commentary attend to the literary craft.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Both traditions have, until quite recently, worked hard to defuse the chapter's sexual ambiguity — Jewish through halakhic and midrashic harmonization, Christian through allegorical and typological reading. Both traditions read Ruth as a model of righteous initiative under proper authority. They diverge on the legal question: Jewish reading takes seriously the conflation of *go'el* and *yibbum* and works to harmonize them within the law; Christian reading more often treats the conflation as a peculiarity of the narrative without legal weight. The contemporary turn — feminist and literary — has, across both traditions, been more willing to let the ambiguity stand and to read it as the chapter's central theological tool. The shift is not from chastity to indecency but from harmonization to honest reading.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author of Ruth believe God was doing in this chapter? The theology continues the book's distinctive method: YHWH does not act in this chapter at all. The divine name appears only in Boaz's blessing of Ruth (3:10) and in his oath formula (3:13). What happens — the proposal, the qualification, the gift, the prediction — happens entirely through human initiative, human response, and the institutions of kinship law. The book's argument is that this *is* the form of providence. The unspoken theological claim is that what the rabbis call *hashgachah* (providence) operates through the structure of kinship and the agency of those who choose to honor it.

**Tension One: How should we read Ruth's initiative — bold faithfulness, calculated risk, or sexual transgression in service of legal claim?** The chapter does not specify; the reader must.

The rabbinic Jewish reading treats Ruth's initiative as bold but chaste — she is the proselyte who, having chosen YHWH and YHWH's people in chapter 1, now chooses Boaz as the legal-covenantal vehicle of her incorporation. Her boldness is praised; the chastity is taken as given. *Ruth Rabbah* and the Talmud both work to make the encounter both daring and pure.

The patristic Christian reading allegorizes the boldness into the soul's pursuit of Christ. Ruth's approach to Boaz is the Gentile church's approach to the bridegroom; her uncovering of the feet is the unveiling of humility; the spreading of the wing is the granting of grace. The legal and sexual surface is dissolved into spiritual meaning.

The Reformation reading, especially Calvin's, takes the surface seriously and defends both the propriety of the encounter and the daring of Ruth's initiative as together a sign of how providence uses bold but ordered human action.

The contemporary feminist reading (Trible, Brenner, Pardes, Frymer-Kensky) reads Ruth's initiative as the chapter's main theological point: a woman with no legal standing in the patriarchal order takes the initiative that the male relatives have not taken, and her action is what makes redemption possible. The chapter is not despite her boldness but because of it. Mieke Bal in *Lethal Love* extends this: Ruth is rewriting her position in the story by acting outside the conventions that would have kept her invisible.

The contemporary literary reading (Alter, Sasson, LaCocque) treats the chapter's ambiguity as deliberate and theologically loaded. The narrator is not unable to specify whether something sexual occurred; the narrator is refusing to specify because the refusal is the theological move. Providence, in the book's vision, works through situations that cannot be morally tidied. To insist on chastity as a precondition for the chapter's theology is to miss what the chapter is doing.

The position to steelman is the rabbinic chastity reading. It is sometimes dismissed as harmonization, but it takes seriously the textual signal that Boaz wakes startled — *charad* (חָרַד), "to tremble in alarm" — at finding a woman, which suggests the situation surprised him rather than that it had progressed to consent. The chastity reading honors the dialogue's content: Boaz speaks to Ruth as one initiating a proper proposal, and Ruth's silence on what happened in the dark is consistent with nothing having happened. The reading also makes sense of why the legal procedure of chapter 4 is necessary: if a sexual claim had been established at the threshing floor, the city-gate procedure would be redundant.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter's ambiguity is a feature, not a problem. It allows the book to register the moral weight of Ruth's risk without requiring the reader to specify what the risk actually consisted in. The theology depends on the reader's engagement with that ambiguity rather than on its resolution.

**Tension Two: What is the legal status of Ruth's proposal?** The chapter introduces a legal innovation that the Pentateuchal codes do not quite anticipate. Ruth fuses *go'el* (kinsman-redemption of land, Lev. 25) and *yibbum* (levirate marriage, Deut. 25:5-10) into a single proposal. Boaz accepts the fusion in principle but qualifies it (there is a nearer redeemer). The book treats the fused obligation as legitimate. The Pentateuchal codes treat them as separate institutions.

The rabbinic Jewish reading harmonizes by extension: the *go'el* obligation in the land case can carry with it a marriage obligation when the family line is at stake. The Mishnah (Yevamot 4) and the Talmudic discussion work out the principles. The marriage of a deceased relative's wife by a *go'el* who is not a brother becomes a legitimate extension of the legal framework, though the rabbinic tradition is careful to note that this is a *de facto* practice rather than a *de jure* requirement.

The Catholic canonical reading absorbs the issue into the broader category of providence working through legal practice; the conflation is not a problem because the redeeming work of God can use overlapping institutions.

The Reformation reading (Calvin) treats the legal procedure as exemplary of how civil law and personal piety together create good order. The chapter is read as evidence that Israelite legal practice was more flexible than the Pentateuchal codes alone suggest, and that the flexibility was itself part of the wisdom of the law.

The critical reading varies. Form-critical and source-critical approaches (Wellhausen, Gunkel) sometimes treat the legal conflation as evidence that the book reflects a later, less differentiated practice or a literary harmonization. Robert Hubbard, Frederic Bush, and Jack Sasson read the chapter as preserving an authentic but informal practice that the legal codes do not capture in full. André LaCocque reads the legal innovation as part of the book's polemical project: the post-exilic narrator is showing how the law can and should be flexibly applied to incorporate the foreign woman.

The position to steelman is the legal-purist reading: that the conflation is irregular and that the chapter is taking liberties the Pentateuchal codes do not authorize. This reading is sometimes used pejoratively (the book is "innovating" or "departing from the law"), but it can also be read with respect: the book is showing that the law, properly applied, is more capacious than its strict letter, and that the spirit of *hesed* extends the institutions rather than violating them. This is not a soft reading but a serious one: it grants that something is happening here that the codes don't strictly mandate, and it asks whether the extension is faithful or unfaithful to the law's intent.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter is doing legal innovation in service of *hesed*. Whether one reads that as wisdom or as overreach depends on one's underlying view of how law and mercy relate. The book itself clearly takes the former view: Boaz's willingness to fuse the obligations is part of his praise.

The unresolved question this chapter leaves us with: When the procedures we inherit are not quite adequate to the situation we face, what authorizes someone like Ruth — a foreign widow with no legal standing — to claim that the law should be extended in her direction, and what authorizes someone like Boaz to agree?

## The Thread

**Genesis 19:30-38.** Lot's daughters lie with their drunken father in the cave; one of them conceives Moab, Ruth's distant ancestor. Ruth 3 is the deliberate inversion: a Moabite woman approaches a sleeping man whose "heart is merry" with wine, and what happens (or doesn't) produces, three generations later, the line of David. When you reread Genesis 19, notice how thoroughly Ruth 3 picks up its language — wine, sleeping man, woman lying down, the dark — and rearranges it.

**Genesis 38.** Tamar disguises herself, intercepts Judah, and conceives Perez. The chapter 4 elders' blessing will explicitly invoke Tamar; the threshing-floor scene of Ruth 3 sits in the matriarchal pattern that Tamar's story established. Both stories feature foreign or marginal women who take the initiative when male relatives have failed in their obligations. When you reread Genesis 38, remember that Perez — Tamar's son — is the ancestor of Boaz.

**Ezekiel 16:8.** "And I passed by you again and looked upon you, and behold, you were at the age for love, and I spread my wing over you and covered your nakedness; I made my vow to you and entered into a covenant with you, declares the Lord GOD, and you became mine." YHWH's marriage to Israel uses the exact phrase Ruth uses to Boaz at 3:9 — *paras kanaph*, "spread the wing." The human marriage proposal at the threshing floor uses the same Hebrew vocabulary as YHWH's covenantal marriage to Israel. When you read Ezekiel 16, you are reading the divine version of what Ruth proposes.

**Proverbs 31:10-31.** The hymn to the *eshet hayil*, the "worthy woman," uses exactly the phrase Boaz applies to Ruth at 3:11. In the canonical order of the Tanakh, Ruth follows Proverbs immediately, and the rabbinic tradition (Ruth Rabbah, Bava Batra 14b) reads the placement as deliberate: Ruth is the embodied case study of the abstract figure Proverbs 31 portrays. When you read Proverbs 31, remember that the only woman the Hebrew Bible actually calls *eshet hayil* in narrative is Ruth.
