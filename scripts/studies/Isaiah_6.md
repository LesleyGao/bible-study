# Isaiah 6 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Isaiah 6 opens with a date that does double duty: "In the year that King Uzziah died" (Isa 6:1). Modern chronologies place Uzziah's death somewhere around 740 or 739 BC, depending on how one harmonizes the regnal data of 2 Kings 15 and 2 Chronicles 26. That single phrase locates the prophet on the cusp of the Assyrian crisis. Tiglath-pileser III had begun his aggressive western campaigns in 745 BC, and the small kingdoms of the Levant — Aram-Damascus, Israel, Judah, Tyre, the Philistine city-states — were already feeling the gravitational pull of an empire whose appetite for tribute and territory was, at this point in the eighth century, essentially unprecedented. The throne-vision of Isaiah 6 is best read against that pressure: as Uzziah's long, prosperous, finally leprous reign ends, Isaiah is shown another throne, occupied by another King.

The book of Isaiah has been at the center of one of the longest-running disputes in modern biblical scholarship: the composite-authorship debate. Bernhard Duhm's 1892 commentary (*Das Buch Jesaia*) crystallized a tripartite division — First Isaiah (chs. 1–39, eighth-century Isaiah of Jerusalem under Ahaz and Hezekiah), Second Isaiah (chs. 40–55, an exilic anonymous prophet near 540 BC), and Third Isaiah (chs. 56–66, a postexilic continuation). That framework is still the mainstream-critical default, defended in different keys by Hans Wildberger, Joseph Blenkinsopp, John Goldingay, Christopher Seitz, J. J. M. Roberts, Patricia Tull, and Jacob Stromberg. Against it, Brevard Childs's canonical approach insists on reading the final form as a coherent theological whole regardless of compositional history, and conservative-evangelical commentators such as J. Alec Motyer and John Oswalt continue to argue, on stylistic and theological grounds, for substantial unity under a single eighth-century prophet. Isaiah 6 sits firmly in First Isaiah territory — almost no critic disputes its eighth-century core — but its placement in the canonical book (curiously, *not* in chapter 1) has been read as a deliberate editorial framing, possibly a memoir cycle (chs. 6–8) inserted to ground the whole book in a specific call.

The Hebrew of 6:3 is one of the most repeated lines in the entire Bible: *qādôš qādôš qādôš YHWH ṣĕbāʾôt* (קָדוֹשׁ קָדוֹשׁ קָדוֹשׁ יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת), "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts." Triple repetition in Biblical Hebrew functions as a superlative — "the holiest possible" — and *ṣĕbāʾôt* ("of hosts/armies") binds Yahweh's holiness to his cosmic-military rule. The seraphim themselves (שְׂרָפִים, *śĕrāpîm*, from the root שׂרף, "to burn") are the only place in the Hebrew Bible where heavenly beings carry this name; the same noun elsewhere designates the fiery serpents of Numbers 21 and the winged uraeus-like figures known from Egyptian iconography and from Iron Age Judean seal impressions. Othmar Keel's iconographic work (*Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel*) shows that two- and four-winged uraei appear on royal Judean seals from precisely Isaiah's century; Isaiah multiplies the wings to six and reassigns these throne-attendants to Yahweh's exclusive service. The "burning coal" (רִצְפָּה, *riṣpâ*) from the altar that touches Isaiah's lips (6:6–7) draws on temple ritual and on the wider ANE motif of mouth-purification rites known from Mesopotamian *mīs-pî* ("washing of the mouth") liturgies used to consecrate cult statues — except here the rite is done to a prophet, not to an idol.

The throne-vision genre itself has clear ANE parallels: the divine-council scenes in 1 Kings 22, the throne-room of Marduk in *Enuma Elish*, the throne of El at the source of the rivers in the Ugaritic *Baal Cycle*. What sets Isaiah 6 apart is the moral vector: the encounter does not produce wonder alone but unbearable shame ("Woe is me! For I am lost," 6:5) and then commission. The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsaᵃ) from Qumran Cave 1, dated paleographically to roughly 125 BC, preserves Isaiah 6 essentially as the Masoretic Text transmits it, with only minor orthographic variants — strong evidence for textual stability across more than a millennium. The hardening commission of 6:9–13 in 1QIsaᵃ reads the same in substance, which matters because the LXX of 6:9–10 (which Matthew, Mark, Luke, John, Acts, and Paul will all cite) softens the imperatives slightly into descriptive predictions. The Hebrew is the harsher version.

## Lens 2 — Literature

Chapter 6 is structured as a single, tightly built narrative scene with a sharp pivot. The opening (vv. 1–4) is a vision-report; the middle (vv. 5–7) is a purgation-and-cleansing oracle; the climax (v. 8) is a call-and-response; the conclusion (vv. 9–13) is the prophetic commission proper, given in poetry. This shape — vision → purgation → commission — has long been recognized as a "prophetic call narrative," parallel in structure to Moses (Exod 3), Jeremiah (Jer 1), and Ezekiel (Ezek 1–3), though each exemplar varies the sequence. Norman Habel's classic 1965 study identified a six-element form (divine confrontation, introductory word, commission, objection, reassurance, sign); Isaiah 6 adapts and compresses it.

The Hebrew literary craft is dense. The threefold *qādôš* answers, in heaven, to the threefold "Woe!" of Isaiah's response on earth. The seraphim cover their *faces* and *feet* (a euphemism for genitals in some Hebrew Bible passages, signaling complete modesty before holiness) and *fly* — a triple action that mirrors the triple holy. The coal touches Isaiah's *lips* because his confession was about his *unclean lips*; the cleansing is precisely targeted to the wound. The verbs of 6:10 — *hašmēn* ("make fat/dull"), *hakbēd* ("make heavy"), *hāšaʿ* ("smear shut") — are causative imperatives directed at the prophet himself, an extraordinary grammatical move. He is told to *do* the hardening through his preaching, not merely to predict it.

The closing image of v. 13 — the felled terebinth or oak whose stump (מַצֶּבֶת, *maṣṣebet*) remains, with the gloss "the holy seed is its stump" (זֶרַע קֹדֶשׁ מַצַּבְתָּהּ) — is one of the most contested verses in the Hebrew Bible. The line is missing or differently positioned in some Septuagint manuscripts, and Wildberger and Blenkinsopp both read the "holy seed" gloss as a postexilic editorial addition meant to recast the chapter as a remnant prophecy. Whether redaction or original, it functions inside the canonical book as the seed-bed of the *šĕʾār yāšûb* ("a remnant shall return") motif that flowers in chapters 7–11.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the Jewish trajectory, Isaiah 6 became the engine of one of the most important liturgical texts in synagogue worship: the *Kedushah* (קְדֻשָּׁה, "Sanctification"), recited in the standing prayer (*Amidah*) of every weekday and Sabbath service. The *Kedushah* takes Isaiah 6:3 ("Holy, holy, holy") and pairs it with Ezekiel 3:12 ("Blessed be the glory of the LORD from his place") to constitute the congregation as a kind of earthly choir answering the seraphic chant. Reuven Kimelman, in his comparative work on Jewish and Christian doxological traditions, has documented in detail how the *Kedushah* and the Christian *Sanctus* share a common second-temple matrix and developed in conversation with each other through the rabbinic and patristic centuries. Targum Jonathan paraphrases Isaiah 6:1 as "I saw the glory (*yĕqārâ*) of the LORD," substituting the rabbinic conviction that no human can see God directly — a substitution that will matter when we turn to John 12. Rashi (eleventh-century France) reads the hardening commission of 6:9–10 as descriptive ("they will not understand, because of their own choice") rather than causal, softening the predestinarian edge. Ibn Ezra and Radak follow similar lines. Abarbanel (fifteenth-century Spain) reads the chapter as Isaiah's *first* commission (against the rabbinic tradition that placed his call earlier), restoring the plain sense.

In the Christian trajectory, Isaiah 6 becomes a Christological text by way of John 12:39–41. After Jesus's public ministry has met largely with rejection, John writes: "Therefore they could not believe. For again Isaiah said, 'He has blinded their eyes…' Isaiah said these things because he saw his glory and spoke of him" (John 12:39–41). The "his glory" is, in John's grammar, Christ's. The early Christian reading of Isaiah 6 — that the *Lord* Isaiah saw on the throne was *the pre-incarnate Christ* — runs from Justin Martyr's *Dialogue with Trypho* through Origen and Athanasius, becomes standard in Augustine, and is codified in the Trinitarian liturgical reading of the chapter. Jerome's Vulgate transmitted the chapter to medieval Latin Christianity. Aquinas, in his Isaiah commentary, reads the seraphim as types of the highest angelic order. Calvin treats the chapter as a paradigm of the prophetic vocation and downplays the Christological identification of the throne-figure, though he allows it.

The hardening commission of 6:9–13 is one of the most heavily cited Old Testament passages in the New Testament. Jesus uses it to explain the parable-method (Matt 13:14–15; Mark 4:12; Luke 8:10), Acts uses it to close Paul's ministry to Rome (Acts 28:25–27), and Paul uses it in Romans 11:8 to interpret Israel's hardening within his larger argument about Jew and Gentile. The Trisagion enters Christian liturgy at the latest by the early fifth century — the *Sanctus* of the Roman Mass, the *Trisagion* of the Eastern Orthodox Divine Liturgy ("Holy God, Holy Mighty, Holy Immortal"), the Anglican and Lutheran Eucharistic prefaces. Handel's *Messiah* (1741) sets a different verse, but Mendelssohn's *Elijah* and many other oratorios bring the seraphic chant into the concert hall. The text has been musicalized more than perhaps any other single line of the Hebrew Bible.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Did Isaiah see the pre-incarnate Christ?** John 12:41 ("Isaiah saw his glory and spoke of him") is the textual hinge, and the question splits Christian and Jewish readers cleanly.

The *mainstream-Christian Trinitarian reading* (patristic consensus, Aquinas, Calvin, modern Reformed and Catholic commentators) is that the vision of Isaiah 6 is a vision of the Triune God, and that the "Lord" on the throne is — by John's interpretation — specifically the Son. Athanasius leans on this in his anti-Arian arguments; Augustine lets the entire Trinity inhabit the seraphic *qādôš qādôš qādôš*. The steelman is John 12 itself: a first-century Jewish-Christian author, writing in deliberate intertextual conversation with Isaiah, makes the identification.

The *mainstream-Jewish reading* (Targum Jonathan, Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Maimonides) is that no human directly sees God; Isaiah saw the *kavod* (glory), the *Shekhinah*, a mediated theophany. The "Lord of hosts" on the throne is YHWH himself, and the chapter offers no warrant for any Christological identification. The steelman is the Hebrew text itself, which gives no hint of inner-divine plurality, and the rabbinic conviction that prophetic visions of God are always mediated.

The *mainstream-critical reading* (Wildberger, Blenkinsopp, Goldingay, Roberts) brackets the Christological question and reads the chapter as an eighth-century Judean prophet's account of a temple-vision in which Yahweh appears as cosmic king. John's later Christological reading is treated as a New Testament hermeneutical move, intelligible within the trajectory of early Christian reflection but not as the historical referent of Isaiah's vision.

**Tension 2: Does God commission Isaiah to *prevent* repentance?** The hardening commission of 6:9–13 reads, in the Hebrew, as a series of causative imperatives directed at Isaiah: "Make this people's heart dull… lest they see… and turn and be healed." On the surface, God is sending a prophet to ensure that his audience will *not* turn.

The *Augustinian-Reformed sovereign-decree reading* (Augustine, Calvin, Jonathan Edwards, modern Reformed exegesis) takes this seriously: God in his sovereign freedom hardens whom he wills (Rom 9), and Isaiah 6 is a window into that hardening. The chapter does not embarrass this tradition; it grounds it. The steelman is the plain sense of the Hebrew imperatives and Paul's use in Romans 11:8.

The *Pauline-Christian reading via Romans 9–11* takes the hardening as real but provisional and instrumental. Hardening serves a larger redemptive purpose — "a partial hardening has come upon Israel, until the fullness of the Gentiles has come in" (Rom 11:25) — and is not the last word. The steelman is the canonical placement of Isaiah 6 itself: the chapter ends with "the holy seed is its stump," opening the door to remnant theology.

The *open-theist response* (Greg Boyd, Terence Fretheim, John Sanders) reads the imperatives as God's anguished prediction of what Isaiah's preaching will, in fact, produce given Judah's already-hardened state — not as God's primary intent but as a tragic, foreseen-but-not-decreed outcome. The steelman is the lament-quality of the surrounding context ("How long, O Lord?" 6:11) and the Hebrew Bible's wider portrayal of God grieving over judgment.

The *mainstream-Jewish reading* (Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Radak) tends to soften the causative force grammatically and to read the people's hardening as the *consequence* of their persistent unfaithfulness, with Isaiah's preaching exposing what is already true. Modern Jewish scholars such as Marvin Sweeney read 6:9–13 as ironic prophetic rhetoric — a rhetorical device that *names* the hardness in order to indict it.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter forces a Christian reader to hold two things together that resist one another. First, the Trisagion makes God's holiness the deepest fact about him — and holiness, here, is not moral perfection in the abstract but a consuming, world-shaking otherness that makes a prophet say "I am lost." Second, that same holy God commissions a prophet whose preaching will, at least in the immediate term, harden hearts. Christian liturgy has resolved this by setting the *Sanctus* in the Eucharist, the moment of greatest mercy: we sing the seraph-song right before we receive forgiveness. Whether that liturgical move *answers* the hardening problem or only *brackets* it is a fair question. Jewish liturgy, in the *Kedushah*, places the same line in the heart of the daily standing prayer, binding the worshiper's body to the angels' chant without asking the Christological question. Both traditions agree that Isaiah 6:3 is the verse you say when you have run out of other things to say. They disagree, more sharply than almost anywhere else in the Hebrew Bible, about whom you are saying it to.

**Closing question:** If the same God who is "holy, holy, holy" is also the God who hardens — and if the prophet's commission is to keep speaking even when he knows the speaking will harden — what is the theological account of preaching, and of teaching, and of any human attempt to communicate truth that would be honest about both the seraphic doxology and the smeared-shut eyes?

## The Thread

Isaiah 6 is the chapter that runs underground beneath every other call-narrative in the Bible. The seraphic *qādôš qādôš qādôš* surfaces in synagogue *Kedushah* and Christian *Sanctus*; the "Here I am, send me" surfaces in every missionary biography; the hardening commission surfaces every time Jesus explains a parable. The chapter's strangest gift may be that it refuses to choose between glory and judgment. The throne-room and the smeared eyes share the same vision.
