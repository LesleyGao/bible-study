# Job 36: Suffering as Instruction

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Job 36 opens Elihu's fourth and final discourse, which extends into chapter 37. At thirty-three verses, it is the longest of his speeches and introduces the chapter that is thematically and literarily most ambitious: the theology of disciplinary suffering, followed by the lyrical storm hymn that culminates in chapter 37 and prepares for Yahweh's theophany in chapter 38. The setting remains the assumed ash heap outside a settlement in the land of Uz — a region that most scholars locate in the northern Arabian or Edomite steppe, within the broader wisdom-cultural orbit of the ancient Near East. Elihu's claim in v. 3 that he will bring his knowledge "from afar" (מֵרָחוֹק, *merachoq*) may be a geographical boast (Edomite or Aramean wisdom traditions) or a rhetorical one (his knowledge reaches back to sources beyond his interlocutors).

The question of dating and authorship for the Elihu cycle takes on particular significance here because chapter 36 introduces the idea of suffering as divine pedagogy, a theology that develops substantially in post-exilic Jewish thought and is notably absent from the three friends' speeches. Marvin Pope placed the Elihu material firmly post-exilic, reading chapter 36's disciplinary theodicy as reflecting later Persian-period influences where Zoroastrian and proto-apocalyptic ideas of instructive suffering had begun to circulate. David Clines (WBC) sees the chapter as late but literarily sophisticated. Carol Newsom treats the disciplinary model as a distinctive post-exilic contribution to Jewish theodicy, which the book's editor added to complicate the original dialogue. Against these, Robert Gordis argues for originality, pointing out that instructive suffering is not inherently post-exilic (compare Deuteronomy 8:5). Francis Andersen defends the chapter as integral to the book's structure, noting that v. 15's image ("He delivers the afflicted by their affliction") is the book's most concise formulation of a genuinely new theodicy.

Archaeologically, chapter 36's theology of pedagogical suffering has striking parallels in the Akkadian *Ludlul Bēl Nēmeqi*, where the restored sufferer eventually understands his ordeal as Marduk's lesson. The *Babylonian Theodicy* offers a softer version, with the friend counseling that the gods' ways include discipline that is not identical to punishment. The Egyptian *Dialogue of a Man with His Ba* touches on suffering as existential reshaping. What distinguishes Elihu's presentation is the specific linkage between adversity and the opening of the ear (v. 10, v. 15) — a metaphor rooted in biblical covenantal language (Isaiah 50:4–5) where the servant's ear is opened morning by morning. The Qumran 11QTargum Job preserves portions of chapter 36, and where the Aramaic survives, it tends to soften the sharper pastoral edges of Elihu's warnings to Job. The Septuagint is notably shorter than the Masoretic Text throughout Job, and chapter 36 contains several verses where the Greek diverges significantly, particularly in the transition to the storm hymn.

Five Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. First, יָסַר (*yasar*), "to discipline" or "instruct" (implicit in v. 10, "he opens their ears to instruction"), is the root of Deuteronomy's covenantal pedagogy (Deut 8:5, "as a man disciplines his son, the LORD your God disciplines you"). Second, עֳנִי (*oni*), "affliction" (v. 8, v. 15), names the specific category of suffering that Elihu believes God uses instrumentally. Third, כֹּפֶר (*kofer*), "ransom" (v. 18), is a legal-cultic term referring to compensation or redemption; its use here in a context of warning Job has generated extensive debate since antiquity. Fourth, צַר (*tzar*), "distress" or "cramping" (v. 16), is paired with its opposite רַחַב (*rachav*), "broad place," to frame the before-and-after of God's alluring deliverance. Fifth, אַדִּיר (*addir*), "mighty" (v. 5), describes God's strength — but Elihu joins it immediately with "does not despise any" (לֹא יִמְאָס, *lo yim'as*), the same verb Job used in 7:16 to describe his own self-loathing. The lexical play is likely deliberate: Elihu claims that the God Job has accused of disregard does not, in fact, despise the sufferer.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 36 divides into four movements. Verses 1–4 are Elihu's self-introduction, with the striking self-description "one who is perfect in knowledge" (*temim de'ot*, v. 4) — a phrase that readers have found either self-important or self-evidently true, depending on their prior judgment of Elihu. Verses 5–15 develop the core theology: God does not despise; God disciplines the righteous through affliction; the godless refuse the instruction and perish. Verse 15 states the chapter's thesis: "He delivers the afflicted by their affliction and opens their ear by adversity." Verses 16–21 turn this teaching directly on Job, warning him that his present suffering is such an opportunity and that he must not, in rebellion, turn to iniquity. Verses 22–33 lift into a hymn to divine sovereignty in nature, introducing the storm imagery that chapter 37 will develop — the transition is deliberate and prepares the theophany.

The compositional questions about the Elihu cycle apply to this chapter with the usual force. Pope counted multiple Aramaisms, especially in v. 2 (תַּר, "bear with"), and the vocabulary for discipline in vv. 8–10. Clines (WBC) notes that the self-description in v. 4 is either ironically arrogant (the book's critique of Elihu) or straightforward claim to inspired wisdom (the book's endorsement). Claus Westermann saw the chapter as evidence that the Elihu cycle introduced a distinct theological voice into what had been a more unified dialogue. Newsom reads the chapter as a dialogic contribution — the voice of Persian-period pedagogy, which the book wants readers to examine and test.

Defenders of originality have focused on chapter 36's literary integration. Gordis argued the self-description in v. 4 is not arrogance but a conventional oath-like formula (compare the similar construction in v. 26 where God is described in the same way), signaling that Elihu speaks under a self-imposed standard of truthfulness. Robert Fyall makes the structural case most strongly: chapter 36 cannot be removed without breaking the transition to the Yahweh speeches, because the storm imagery beginning in v. 27 is precisely what will arrive in 38:1. Andersen points out that v. 15 introduces a genuinely new theological possibility — that suffering itself can be deliverance — which neither Job nor the friends have articulated and which the book seems to preserve rather than refute.

The distinctive Elihu vocabulary appears throughout. Verse 2 uses a rare piel of *katar* ("bear with") that the medieval commentators had to puzzle over. Verse 27's meteorological description uses technical terminology rarely found elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible. Verse 33's concluding phrase about the cattle sensing the storm has generated the broadest translation variation of any line in the book — from the ESV's "the cattle also declare that he rises" to more naturalistic readings about livestock sensing approaching weather. The textual difficulties concentrate, as they did in chapters 34 and 35, at the most daring theological turns.

The transition from instruction (vv. 1–21) to hymn (vv. 22–33) is the chapter's most important literary feature. Many critical scholars — Habel, Fyall, Newsom, Andersen — have observed that Elihu appears to *see* the storm approaching as he speaks. Verse 29's "thunderings of his pavilion" and v. 32's "covers his hands with the lightning" read as if spoken into a darkening sky. This literary device — a speaker whose oration is overtaken by the phenomenon he is describing — is unusual in ancient Near Eastern wisdom literature and suggests the author is preparing the Yahweh theophany with considerable craft. The storm that arrives in 38:1 ("then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind") has, on this reading, been visible for the whole of chapter 36:22–37:24.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the Hebrew Bible, chapter 36's theology of disciplinary suffering finds its strongest antecedent in Deuteronomy 8:5 ("as a man disciplines his son, the LORD your God disciplines you"). The framing is also embedded in Proverbs 3:11–12 ("My son, do not despise the LORD's discipline or be weary of his reproof, for the LORD reproves him whom he loves, as a father the son in whom he delights") — a passage Hebrews 12:5–11 will later quote as the canonical charter for disciplinary theodicy. Psalm 94:12 ("Blessed is the man whom you discipline, O LORD") echoes the same theology. But the book of Job is careful not to allow this framework to function as a comprehensive explanation; chapter 36 presents it, but chapters 38–42 do not confirm it as the final word.

The Jewish reception of Job 36 is remarkably positive by the standards of the Elihu cycle. The Bavli's discussion in Bava Batra 15a–16b situates Job in various historical moments — the patriarchal era, the exile, the period of the Judges — and the disciplinary theology of chapter 36 fits naturally with several of these. Rashi largely endorses Elihu's teaching in this chapter, treating the opening of the ear through adversity (v. 10, v. 15) as a genuine spiritual insight. Ibn Ezra follows similarly. The most developed medieval reading is Maimonides's in *Guide of the Perplexed* III.22–23: Maimonides holds up Elihu's fourth speech as the apex of the book's theological argument, and chapter 36 is where his case crystallizes. For Maimonides, the categories of discipline, instruction, and adversity — refined through the mediation of a revelatory figure (33:23–24) — supply a providence doctrine that respects both divine perfection and human experience. Nachmanides is more reserved but still engages the disciplinary framework seriously. The phrase "opens their ear by adversity" (v. 15) became a locus classicus in Jewish mystical and musar traditions, cited by Ba'al Shem Tov's disciples and later by the Musar movement of Rabbi Yisrael Salanter. Martin Buber, however, resists the framework: disciplinary theodicy can slide into spiritual tyranny, and Buber's reading of Job emphasizes the limits of such explanations. Jon Levenson (*Creation and the Persistence of Evil*) treats the Elihu theodicy as a legitimate voice within a book that preserves multiple voices without collapsing them. James Kugel highlights v. 15 as one of the book's luminous lines, noting how easily it can be weaponized by those who stand outside suffering. Avivah Zornberg draws on psychoanalytic categories to read Elihu's disciplinary theology as both insight and defense — a way of thinking that illuminates but also protects the speaker from the suffering before him.

The Christian trajectory is less uniformly positive. Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job* treats chapter 36 at length, and Gregory gives Elihu more credit here than in the earlier speeches. The theology of v. 15 — suffering as deliverance — becomes, in Gregory's reading, a preparation for the cross: affliction is not merely pedagogy but redemption. Gregory still faults Elihu's tone as arrogant, but the doctrinal content of chapter 36 he largely accepts. Thomas Aquinas in *Expositio super Iob* treats the chapter as a genuine theological advance and integrates Elihu's teaching into his broader doctrine of providence. John Calvin's Job sermons are especially full of chapter 36 material; Calvin's theology of sanctification-through-suffering owes a considerable debt to Elihu's fourth speech. Martin Luther was ambivalent, worried that disciplinary theodicy could be used against the terrified conscience. The nineteenth-century critics (Wellhausen, Gunkel) declared the chapter secondary and therefore non-normative, but von Rad in *Wisdom in Israel* nonetheless treated it as a representative voice of late wisdom theology worth taking seriously. Clines (WBC) accepts its literary integrity within the Elihu cycle while questioning its theological adequacy. Newsom's dialogic reading holds the teaching up for examination without endorsement. Norman Habel (OTL), John Hartley (NICOT), and Andersen (TOTC) each press the integrative case — that the book wants the disciplinary framework preserved as a partial truth. Ellen Davis in *Getting Involved with God* highlights v. 15 as a pastorally powerful but dangerous verse, true only in certain modes of address. Susannah Ticciati reads the chapter as disclosing something about divine pedagogy while warning against its generalization. Gustavo Gutiérrez in *On Job* remains critical: chapter 36, like the earlier Elihu speeches, addresses Job from above, and the book will need Yahweh's speech to rescue the suffering one from such well-meaning instruction.

The Maimonides–Gregory divide that has run through the Elihu cycle narrows here. Both figures give chapter 36 more credit than earlier speeches. Where Maimonides sees the book's theological peak, Gregory sees a chapter in which Elihu nearly gets it right. The disagreement continues over whether the instruction of v. 15 is the final word or a penultimate one awaiting Yahweh's correction.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

### Tension 1: Is suffering pedagogical?

Verse 15's claim — "He delivers the afflicted by their affliction and opens their ear by adversity" — is the strongest statement of disciplinary theodicy in the Hebrew Bible.

- **Maimonides / Aquinas / Calvin / Gregory (late): the framework is true.** Suffering really does function as instruction for those with ears to hear. The disciplinary reading is rooted in Deuteronomy and Proverbs, adopted into the sanctification theology of the New Testament (Hebrews 12), and becomes a central resource for Christian spirituality. Chapter 36 gives the framework its most concise formulation.

- **Buber / Gutiérrez / Kushner: the framework is a partial truth that becomes false when generalized.** Some suffering is genuinely pedagogical; some is simply destructive. The framework works for those already in a formative relationship with God; imposed on strangers, it becomes spiritual violence. Gutiérrez presses this — the cry of the poor is not primarily an educational opportunity.

- **Newsom / Ticciati / Davis (steelman of the least-intuitive position): *chapter 36 teaches a true thing that must remain internal to the sufferer's own testimony*.** The steelman: the disciplinary reading is true in the first person and false in the third person. A sufferer who looks back and says "through this I learned" speaks truly; an observer who says "this is what you are meant to learn" speaks falsely — even if both sentences have the same propositional content. Chapter 36 is preserved in the canon precisely because the book wants readers to see that the same theology that illuminates a sufferer's own testimony becomes a weapon when wielded as a diagnosis of another's pain. The steelman does not reject Elihu's doctrine; it restricts its legitimate speaker.

### Tension 2: What does it mean that the storm begins while Elihu is still speaking?

Many scholars observe that the storm imagery that dominates chapter 37 appears to describe a weather event unfolding in real time during Elihu's speech. This creates a genuine theological question about Elihu's role.

- **Classical interpolation reading (Pope, Westermann): the storm is a literary bridge, and Elihu is the bridge.** The redactor who inserted the Elihu material placed the storm hymn here precisely to set up the Yahweh theophany. Elihu does not see the storm; the editor writes Elihu as seeing it.

- **Traditional defender reading (Maimonides, Fyall, Andersen): Elihu prophetically perceives the coming theophany.** His speech is elevated because he is, in some sense, a participant in what God is about to do. His final words are overtaken by the divine voice, and this is right. The text does not mark his transition as failure; it marks him as the last human voice before the divine one.

- **Habel / Clines (steelman of the least-intuitive position): *Elihu is talking over the storm he should be listening to*.** The steelman of this view: the storm does arrive during Elihu's speech, and his continued talking through it is the critique. He describes the thunder, praises the lightning, and keeps lecturing Job — while the divine presence he claims to represent is arriving. Yahweh speaks in 38:1 after Elihu finally stops. The chapter is not showing us a prophet in the making; it is showing us a theologian who cannot recognize the limit of theology even when the theophany is already visible. On this reading, the book's treatment of Elihu is devastating precisely because it is subtle — he is not wrong in what he says about God and nature; he is wrong in failing to fall silent.

Where this leaves us: the book preserves the most refined theodicy in the ancient Near East and then lets it be eclipsed by a voice from the storm. One unresolved question: if disciplinary suffering is sometimes truly deliverance, how do we learn when we may speak it and when we must only let the sufferer speak it for themselves?

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references. First, Isaiah 50:4–5, the servant's opened ear — "morning by morning he awakens my ear to hear as those who are taught." Elihu's v. 10 and v. 15 use the same image of the opened ear, but applied from above; Isaiah's servant song applies it from within the servant's own testimony. The difference is precisely the one the steelman of Tension 1 identifies. Second, Hebrews 12:5–11, which quotes Proverbs 3 and articulates the New Testament's fullest endorsement of disciplinary suffering — a framework that has Elihu's chapter 36 as an ancestor and that operates under the same conditions of legitimate use. Third, Luke 22:44–46, where Jesus in Gethsemane prays in distress and the disciples sleep; if the disciples had awakened to instruct the suffering Christ about the discipline of adversity, they would have been exactly wrong in the way Elihu is exactly wrong. The canonical counterweight to Job 36 is not the absence of disciplinary theology but its restriction to the speaker who has already borne what they claim to understand.
