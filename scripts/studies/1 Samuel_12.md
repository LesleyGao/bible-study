# 1 Samuel 12: The Prophet's Farewell and the Wheat Harvest Storm

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

1 Samuel 12 belongs to a literary form well attested across the late Bronze and Iron Age Near East — the **public valedictory address** of an aging leader who is transferring authority while reasserting the terms under which authority was originally held. The historical setting is **Gilgal**, the same site where Saul's kingship has just been "renewed" in 11:14–15. Gilgal sits on the western edge of the Jordan Valley, north of the Dead Sea. Identifications vary — Khirbet el-Mefjir is one candidate, Khirbet en-Nitla another — but no excavation has produced an unambiguous Iron I cult center there. The textual centrality of Gilgal in 1 Samuel (it appears at every major Saul-Samuel turning point: 11:15, 13:7–15, 15:12–33) suggests it functioned in the early monarchic period as a covenantal-political assembly site analogous to **Shechem** in the older Joshua tradition (Joshua 24).

The chapter's setting is **wheat harvest**, mid-May to mid-June in the Cisjordanian climate. This timing detail is essential to the chapter's central event. The Levantine climate runs on a binary: rainy season October to April, dry season May to September. The agricultural calendar known from the **Gezer Calendar** (a tenth-century BCE schoolboy's tablet listing the months by their characteristic crops, found by R.A.S. Macalister in 1908) confirms what the modern meteorological record confirms: rain in May–June is meteorologically anomalous and economically catastrophic, since standing wheat must be cut, threshed, and stored dry. A storm at harvest can destroy a year's food. When Samuel summons thunder and rain in 12:17–18, the people understand the sign exactly: this is YHWH demonstrating, in the medium of the agricultural economy on which their lives depend, that He is displeased.

The political context is the **transition from charismatic deliverer-judges to dynastic kingship**. The earlier framework — judges raised up ad hoc by the Spirit (Othniel, Ehud, Deborah, Gideon, Jephthah, Samson) — operated through what Max Weber would call "charismatic authority," episodic and personal. Saul's installation marks the move toward "traditional authority" with an institutional, hereditary element. The chapter is Samuel's attempt to reconcile the two: he names himself the last of the deliverer-judges (12:11, where he includes himself with **Jerubbaal/Gideon**, **Barak**, and **Jephthah**), and he hands over to a king whose legitimacy is now public and constitutional rather than purely Spirit-given. This political sociology is implicit in the text but visible to anyone reading with comparative ANE materials in hand.

Five Hebrew terms structure the chapter. **Mashiach** (מָשִׁיחַ, "anointed one," 12:3, 5) appears here in its earliest political application to a reigning Israelite king, the term that will eventually generate the entire messianic theology of Judaism and Christianity. **Tsedaqoth YHWH** (צִדְקוֹת יְהוָה, "the righteous deeds of the LORD," 12:7) is a covenant-litigation phrase: Samuel is conducting a *rib*, a courtroom procedure in which YHWH's faithfulness is the evidence and Israel's faithlessness is the charge. **Shamuel** (שְׁמוּאֵל) himself — Samuel's name — means roughly "heard of God" and shadows the chapter's repeated demand to "obey the voice" (*shema'*, שָׁמַע) of YHWH. **Hithpallel** (הִתְפַּלֵּל, "to pray, intercede," 12:19, 23) is the priestly-prophetic intercessory verb that links Samuel to Moses (Num 11:2, Deut 9:20). And **niham** (נִחַם, "to relent, regret"), absent from this chapter but anticipated by it, will become the theological flashpoint of 15:11, 35.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: this is a **Deuteronomistic covenant-renewal speech**, formally similar to **Joshua's farewell at Shechem** (Joshua 23–24), **Moses's farewell in Deuteronomy** (esp. Deut 28–32), and the later **Ezra–Nehemiah** covenant ceremonies (Neh 9–10). Martin Noth identified this form as one of the Deuteronomistic Historian's signature compositional units: at every major transition in Israel's history (Moses to Joshua, Joshua to the judges, judges to monarchy, monarchy to exile), the Dtr inserts a long retrospective speech that re-binds the narrative to covenant obligation. **Dennis McCarthy** in *Treaty and Covenant* and **Richard Nelson** in *The Double Redaction of the Deuteronomistic History* have shown that this form is structurally indebted to ANE suzerain-vassal treaties, particularly the **Hittite treaties** of the late Bronze Age and the later **Vassal Treaties of Esarhaddon** (672 BCE). The treaty form has six standard elements: preamble, historical prologue, stipulations, witnesses, blessings/curses, and ratification ceremony. 1 Samuel 12 contains all six in compressed form.

The **historical prologue** (12:6–11) is a tight survey: Egypt, Sisera, the Philistines, Moab, then Jerubbaal, Barak, Jephthah, and (the surprise) Samuel himself. **Textual variant**: the MT reads "Bedan" (בְּדָן) where the LXX, Syriac, and likely 4QSam have "Barak" (בָּרָק). McCarter (Anchor Bible) and Klein (WBC) both adopt the Barak reading; "Bedan" is otherwise unknown to the Hebrew Bible and likely a scribal corruption of *bn dn*, "son of Dan" (a guess at Samson?), or simply a metathesis. The ESV adopts "Barak." The textual issue is meaning-affecting because Samuel listing himself alongside the named judges is the literary device by which he marks the closing of an era.

The **stipulations** (12:14–15, 20–25) are framed as conditional: *if* you fear the LORD … *if* you do not. The **witnesses** are doubled: YHWH and his anointed (12:5). The **sign** (12:16–18) — the harvest-storm theophany — functions as the ratification of the curses, an enacted threat of what disobedience produces. **Polzin** (in *Samuel and the Deuteronomist*) argues that this scene is the structural mirror of the burning bush: in Exodus 3, theophany inaugurates the covenant; in 1 Samuel 12, theophany re-inaugurates it on new institutional terms.

Literary devices include the **rhetorical questions of the *rib*** (12:3 — "Whose ox have I taken? Or whose donkey?"), which are formally identical to the prophetic-legal questions in Micah 6:1–8 and Isaiah 1:18. Samuel's self-vindication is not vanity; it is the legal demonstration that the prosecution has clean hands. The **chiasm** of 12:14–15 (positive/negative parallel of "if you fear … if you will not") and the **inclusio** between 12:1 ("I have obeyed your voice") and 12:25 ("you shall be swept away, both you and your king") frame the speech as a single argument. The **double "Now therefore stand still"** (12:7, 16) marks the speech's two halves — first the verbal indictment, then the visible sign.

Place in the book argument: 1 Samuel 12 is the **closing bracket** of the long debate over kingship that began in 1 Samuel 8. Brueggemann reads chapters 8–12 as a single complex unit — request for a king (8), private anointing (9–10), public lottery (10:17–27), salvation deliverance (11), covenantal ratification (12) — in which the Dtr both gives the people what they want and tells them, simultaneously, what it costs. The chapter does not condemn kingship outright; it conditionalizes it. From this point forward, every Israelite king will be measured against the standard set in 12:14–15.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical arc, Samuel's farewell at Gilgal stands in a clear lineage. **Moses** in Deuteronomy 28–32 sets the pattern: rehearse history, lay out conditions, name witnesses, predict failure, end with a song of YHWH's faithfulness. **Joshua** in Joshua 24 compresses the pattern at Shechem. **Samuel** in 1 Samuel 12 applies it to the inauguration of monarchy. **The Chronicler** later applies it to the inauguration of the Temple (1 Chr 28–29). The form is the Bible's way of marking institutional transitions as covenantal events rather than merely political ones.

The **Jewish interpretive tradition** has paid close attention to Samuel's self-vindication. **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 6.86–94) treats Samuel's "whose ox have I taken?" as the model of priestly-judicial integrity, contrasting it implicitly with the priests of his own first-century world. The **rabbis** (b. Berakhot 31b; b. Megillah 14a) cite Samuel as one of seven great prophets; his integrity in 12:3 is a recurring exemplum. **Rashi** glosses 12:3 with characteristic terseness: Samuel demands the people testify because a leader's reputation is the people's possession, not the leader's. **Radak** notes that Samuel's listing of himself among the judges (12:11) is not self-aggrandizement but a structural marker that the era of judges is ending with him. **Ralbag** treats the wheat-harvest sign as a rational demonstration: the people would not have believed words alone, so YHWH provided sensory evidence. **Abarbanel** offers an extended political-philosophical reading — Samuel, having warned against kingship in chapter 8, now models how a prophet relates to a king once kingship is in place: not as enemy, but as covenantal monitor. Modern Jewish readers — **Avivah Zornberg** on the psychology of prophetic intercession, **Yair Zakovitch** on the chapter's literary structure, **Moshe Garsiel** on the parallels to other ANE farewell speeches — have generally treated chapter 12 as a meditation on the relationship between religious leadership and political power, a perennial Jewish question.

The **Christian tradition** has read this chapter as foundational for Christian political theology. **Origen**'s homilies on 1 Samuel treat Samuel as the type of the apostolic leader: pastoral, intercessory, willing to confront yet committed to continued prayer for those he confronts. **Augustine** in *City of God* references Samuel's farewell as evidence that the people's choice of a king was a sin even though God permitted it — a paradigm of how God works through, but also against, human political ambition. **Chrysostom** focuses on 12:23 — *"far be it from me that I should sin against the LORD by ceasing to pray for you"* — making it a homiletical centerpiece on pastoral perseverance. **Gregory the Great**, *Pastoral Rule* II.5, cites Samuel as the archetype of the pastor whose authority comes from his refusal to take, illustrated by 12:3. **Aquinas** (*Summa* II-II, q.62 on restitution; II-II, q.184 on the religious life) cites Samuel's "whose ox have I taken?" as the standard of disinterestedness for those in spiritual office.

The **Reformers** found particular weight in 12:23. **Calvin's sermons on 1 Samuel** — chapters 56–58 in the Geneva sermon series — treat 12:23 as the ground of the Reformed pastoral office: ceasing to pray for one's congregation is itself a sin. **Luther**, who knew himself to be both prophet and political agitator, read Samuel's relationship to Saul as a model for his own relationship to the German princes: confront when necessary, intercede always. The chapter became a touchstone for the Reformed doctrine of the prophetic office.

**Critical scholarship** has been more interested in source and form. **Wellhausen** assigned chapter 12 to the late, anti-monarchic Dtr stratum. **Noth** confirmed it as a Dtr composition. **Cross** and **Nelson** placed it in the exilic redaction (Dtr2), reading 12:25 ("you shall be swept away, both you and your king") as transparent reflection on the Babylonian destruction of 586 BCE. **McCarter** (Anchor Bible) treats it as a Dtr composition that nevertheless preserves authentic memory of a covenant-renewal event at Gilgal. **Klein** (WBC) and **Hertzberg** (OTL) emphasize the speech's treaty-form structure and its theological function as the conditional charter of monarchy.

**Contemporary readers**. **Brueggemann** centers chapter 12 in his political theology of the Old Testament: kingship is *permitted but penalized*, granted but never quite normalized. **Polzin** structurally aligns the chapter with the burning bush as a covenantal theophany. **Eugene Peterson** in *Leap Over a Wall* draws pastoral lessons about the nature of leadership transitions. **Ellen Davis** highlights the agricultural sign as the moment when ecological reality enforces theological argument — wheat harvest matters, and YHWH speaks in the medium of the harvest. **John Goldingay** stresses the chapter's grace note: even after enumerating the people's sin, Samuel concludes with the promise that "the LORD will not forsake his people" (12:22). **David Gunn** in *The Fate of King Saul* notes the structural irony that Saul's reign begins under a covenant whose curses will eventually bury him. **Feminist readers** — **Trible**, **Exum**, **Bal**, **Bach** — have observed that the entire covenantal scene is conducted with no women present in the speaking roles; the conditional curse falls on a community whose women are silent participants.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Did Asking for a King Constitute Sin?** The chapter explicitly says yes (12:17 — "your wickedness is great, which you have done in the sight of the LORD, in asking for yourselves a king") and yet equally explicitly affirms that YHWH has set the king over them (12:13). Three positions: (a) **Permissive concession**, classically Augustinian and developed by the Reformed tradition: the request was sin because it expressed distrust of YHWH's direct kingship, but God in mercy permitted it and now works through the institution He did not originally desire. (b) **Developmental providence**, articulated by Aquinas and continued in much Catholic political theology: kingship is a legitimate development of Israel's political life; the *manner* of asking (rejecting YHWH) was sinful, but the *institution* itself is good and providentially ordained. (c) **Hardened-heart accommodation**, defended by Calvin and some modern Reformed thinkers (Wright, Goldingay): YHWH grants the request as a judgment in the form of a gift, similar to giving Israel quail in the wilderness — they get what they asked for, and what they got is its own punishment. Steelman: position (c) accounts best for the textual paradox that YHWH both names the request as sin and gives the king without retracting; it also makes the conditional structure of 12:14–15 coherent (the king becomes good or bad depending on covenant, not depending on whether asking was right). **Where this leaves us:** the Bible's ambivalence about kingship is not redactional confusion but theological precision. Human political institutions are real, divinely permitted, conditionally legitimate, and fundamentally penultimate.

**Tension Two: The Ethics of the Sign.** Samuel calls down a destructive thunderstorm at harvest as proof of the people's sin. Is this divine pedagogy or divine cruelty? Three positions: (a) **Necessary sign**, defended by Ralbag and most premodern commentators: the people would not have believed verbal warning; the storm is mercy in the form of fear, calibrated to produce the repentance recorded in 12:19. (b) **Performative violence**, argued by some feminist and ethical critics (Phyllis Trible's "texts of terror" approach extended): the storm destroys ordinary people's livelihood to demonstrate a theological point; the cost of the lesson is borne by farmers, not by Samuel or Saul. (c) **Sacramental sign**, advanced by Brueggemann and Davis: the storm is a real-world enactment of the covenantal curses (Deut 28:22–24); it is not capricious but contractually consistent — the people agreed to these terms when they accepted YHWH at Sinai and again at Shechem. Steelman: position (b) is morally serious and should not be dismissed; position (c) reframes the question by insisting that the storm is not extra-curricular punishment but the covenant working as advertised. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter wants us to feel the cost of the sign, not to explain it away. Samuel ends not with judgment but with intercession ("far be it from me that I should sin against the LORD by ceasing to pray for you," 12:23) — the prophet who calls the storm is the same prophet who will not stop praying through it.

**One unresolved question:** Samuel says the LORD "will not forsake his people, for his great name's sake" (12:22) and in the same speech says the people "shall be swept away, both you and your king" (12:25). How do we hold both? The exilic Dtr who probably gave us this chapter knew which prediction was about to come true.

## The Thread

- **Joshua 24** — Joshua's covenant-renewal at Shechem. Samuel at Gilgal is the deliberate mirror, marking the second major institutional transition in Israel's history with the same form.
- **Deuteronomy 28:22–24** — The covenantal curse of weather striking the harvest. Samuel's storm is not arbitrary; it is Deuteronomy 28 enacted in the wheat field.
- **Micah 6:1–8** — The classic prophetic *rib*. Micah's rhetorical questions ("with what shall I come before the LORD?") share the legal-courtroom form of Samuel's "whose ox have I taken?"
- **Acts 13:21** — Paul's speech in Antioch references "Samuel the prophet" giving Israel a king for forty years. The chronological number Paul cites is the figure missing from the Hebrew text of 1 Samuel 13:1 — **flag forward to that textual problem**, which we will hit in the next chapter.
- **2 Kings 25** — The destruction predicted in 1 Sam 12:25 ("swept away, both you and your king") arrives with the Babylonians. The Dtr framing of Samuel's farewell is meant to be read in light of how the story ends.
