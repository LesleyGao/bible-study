# Leviticus 3: Meat Shared with God

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The book continues under its Hebrew name *Wayyiqrāʾ* (וַיִּקְרָא), the opening "and he called" from 1:1, with Leviticus picking up directly from the completed tabernacle of Exodus 40. Chapter 3 stands in the opening worshipper-manual (Lev 1-5), specifically as the third of the three voluntary offerings — *ʿōlâ* (burnt offering, ch. 1), *minḥâ* (grain offering, ch. 2), *šĕlāmîm* (peace offering, ch. 3) — before the mandatory expiatory offerings of chs. 4-5. Lev 6-7 revisits the same offerings from the priest's procedural angle.

On dating, the critical field follows a familiar spectrum. Wellhausen (*Prolegomena*, 1878) placed the Priestly material (P) post-exilic, reading it as Second Temple practice retrojected into a Mosaic frame. Yehezkel Kaufmann (*The Religion of Israel*) and Menahem Haran (*Temples and Temple-Service*) argued for pre-exilic composition on linguistic, institutional, and terminological grounds. Jacob Milgrom's three-volume *Anchor Bible Leviticus* (1991-2000) — the standard critical commentary — makes the sustained pre-exilic case, pointing out that P's sacrificial system differs in concrete and telling ways from Second Temple practice documented in Chronicles and the Mishnah. Israel Knohl's *Sanctuary of Silence* (1995) distinguishes P from the Holiness School (H, Lev 17-26), arguing H represents a later priestly reform that reframes the older ritual material ethically. The peace offering legislation of Lev 3 is almost universally assigned to P proper; Lev 17's fat-and-blood prohibitions — closely related — fall to H.

The material culture of Lev 3 is pastoral. The offerings are drawn from the herd (*bāqār*, cattle, vv. 1-5) or flock (*ṣōʾn*, sheep or goats, vv. 6-16). Age and sex are unspecified ("male or female"), in contrast to the *ʿōlâ* which requires an unblemished male (Lev 1:3). The only universal requirement is *tāmîm* — "without blemish." Zooarchaeological study of Israelite cultic sites (bone assemblages at Dan, Arad, Beersheba, Shiloh, and Lachish) has confirmed that caprine and ovine species (sheep and goats) dominate Israelite offering deposits, with cattle a smaller fraction — a pattern consistent with the economy behind Lev 3, where flock animals predominate and cattle mark the wealthier offerer. Liora Kolska Horwitz and Justin Lev-Tov have published extensively on these deposits.

The offering's signature feature is its distribution. In the *ʿōlâ* the entire animal is burned; in the *ḥaṭṭāʾt* and *ʾāšām* the priests eat most of the meat; but in the peace offering only the fat and blood go to God, and the meat is consumed by the offerer, family, and guests in a shared meal (further specified in Lev 7:11-36). This is the uniquely communal sacrifice. The fat burned comprises specific visceral fat (*ḥēleb*): the fat covering the entrails, the fat on the entrails, the two kidneys with their loin fat, the long lobe of the liver (*yōteret* ʿal *ha-kābēd* — the caudate lobe in anatomical terms), and for sheep specifically the whole fat tail (*ʾalyâ*) — a feature of the fat-tailed sheep breed (*Ovis aries platyura*) indigenous to the Levant, whose massive tail stored fat reserves.

The categorical prohibition of v. 17 — "you shall eat neither fat nor blood" — has proven one of the most consequential lines in Levitical legislation. It functions as a permanent statute (*ḥuqqat ʿôlām*) that extends the altar's claim on visceral fat into the domestic kitchen. This becomes the halakhic basis for the kashrut laws on *ḥēleb*, still observed in rabbinic Judaism: the specific suet fat around the kidneys and entrails is forbidden for consumption; other animal fat (*šumān*, subcutaneous and intermuscular) is permitted. The blood prohibition is amplified in Lev 17:10-14 and Deut 12:23, and grounded theologically in the identification of blood with life (*nefeš*).

Ugaritic parallels are precise. The Ugaritic ritual texts KTU 1.39, 1.40, and 1.41 specify *šlmm* offerings — cognate with Hebrew *šĕlāmîm* — in which specific organ-fat is burned and the rest consumed. Dennis Pardee's *Ritual and Cult at Ugarit* (2002) collates this evidence and shows that the ritual vocabulary and practice have deep West Semitic roots. The *šlmm* at Ugarit is offered to a range of gods and serves communal functions similar to the Israelite version. The Punic Marseille tariff (CIS I 165, fourth-century BCE Carthage) distinguishes a *šlm kll* (whole peace offering) from *šlm ṣwʿt* — again with fat and specific portions assigned to priests and offerers. Where Israel's code differs is in restricting the cult to one sanctuary and in the fat-and-blood prohibitions, which have no direct Ugaritic or Punic parallel.

Key terms to carry:

- *Šĕlāmîm* (שְׁלָמִים) — peace offering, from the root *šlm* (wholeness, completeness, peace). The form is plural and may originally have been an abstract plural ("well-being offering").
- *Zebaḥ* (זֶבַח) — slaughter-sacrifice. *Zebaḥ šĕlāmîm* is the full phrase; *zebaḥ* on its own distinguishes the communal meal from the holocaust.
- *Ḥēleb* (חֵלֶב) — suet; the specific visceral fat.
- *ʾAlyâ* (אַלְיָה) — fat tail, proper to Near Eastern fat-tailed sheep.
- *Yōteret ʿal ha-kābēd* (יֹתֶרֶת עַל הַכָּבֵד) — lobe upon the liver; commonly rendered "caudate lobe."

Textual evidence is strong. 4QLev fragments preserve portions of the chapter in close agreement with MT. The LXX renders *šĕlāmîm* variously as *thysia sōtēriou* ("sacrifice of salvation") — a translation choice that will shape Christian reception, since *sōtēriou* carries soteriological weight in Greek. The Samaritan Pentateuch and Peshitta are close to MT.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is priestly ritual torah in casuistic form. The chapter is organized by animal type — cattle (vv. 1-5), sheep (vv. 6-11), goats (vv. 12-16) — with each section following the same template: presentation, hand-laying, slaughter at the tent entrance, blood dashed against the altar's sides, fat extracted, and fat burned on the altar. The result is a carefully terraced parallel structure.

Repetition is the chapter's main literary device. The same procedural steps recur three times with minor variation. The only substantive difference between the three subsections is the fat-tail (*ʾalyâ*) prescription for sheep — a species-specific adjustment, not a theological one. Milgrom reads the triplicate structure as pedagogical: ritual repetition internalizes the pattern.

Two framing elements stand out. The opening of each subsection begins with the *ʾim* ("if") protasis typical of casuistic law. The refrain *ʾiššeh rêaḥ nîḥōaḥ laYHWH* ("a food offering with a pleasing aroma to the LORD," vv. 5, 16) threads the unit to Lev 1 and 2, binding the three voluntary offerings into a single literary-theological complex. The chapter closes with two categorical statements (v. 16b and v. 17): "all fat is the LORD's" and "you shall eat neither fat nor blood." These shift register from casuistic to apodictic — a characteristic priestly move, where specific instructions climax in universal principles.

One structural oddity deserves attention. Unlike chapters 1 and 2, Lev 3 does not grade by wealth. Chapter 1 descends from bull to bird (with a pigeon option for the poor); chapter 2 offers grain for those without animals; chapter 3 lists three animal types but no explicit poor-man's option. Baruch Levine (*JPS Leviticus*) suggests this is because the peace offering is celebratory, not mandatory, and the poor could simply abstain. Milgrom counters that the absence of a poor-person option here reflects that this sacrifice requires a meal, and a meal requires an animal. The literary effect is to mark *šĕlāmîm* as a sacrifice of abundance — one offered when the offerer has enough to share.

Textual variants in the chapter are few. 4QLev-Levb shows only minor orthographic differences; the LXX renders the fat-portion list with Greek anatomical terms that occasionally do not map cleanly onto the Hebrew (the "long lobe of the liver" becomes *ho lobos ho epi tou hēpatos*). Mary Douglas in *Leviticus as Literature* (1999) argues that the anatomical specificity of the fat portions is not mere procedural fussiness but a deliberate mapping of the animal's interior onto sacred-spatial logic — the innermost organs to God, the outer flesh to the community — though her argument has not found universal assent.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Second Temple reception is rich. Philo (*Special Laws* 1.212-224) reads the peace offering as the offering of gratitude, naming it *sōtērion* following the LXX, and spiritualizes the fat as the "sweet and pleasant parts of the soul." Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.228-231) gives a procedural account, noting that the remaining meat must be consumed within a strict timeframe — a detail from Lev 7 that rabbinic tradition will systematize. The Temple Scroll (11Q19) develops the peace offering in festal contexts; Jubilees 22:3-6 places a peace offering at the heart of Abraham's covenant meal with Isaac and Rebekah.

Rabbinic treatment is codified in Mishnah Zevachim, the tractate on sacrifice. The tradition distinguishes three subclasses of *šĕlāmîm* (elaborated in Lev 7): *tôdâ* (thanksgiving), *nēder* (vow), *nĕdābâ* (freewill). Zevachim 5 sets out the procedural differences. The Mishnah's discussion of *ḥēleb* runs through Chullin — the massive kashrut tractate — where the suet-prohibition of Lev 3:17 is codified for post-Temple domestic practice. Sifra on Leviticus works through the chapter verse by verse. The Bavli (e.g., Pesachim 96, Zevachim 37a) engages the procedural detail extensively.

Rashi (on 3:1) reads *šĕlāmîm* as "that which brings peace (*šālôm*) into the world" — a midrashic etymology found in Sifra and Rabbah. Ibn Ezra treats the etymology more naturally, connecting it to wholeness. Ramban (Nachmanides) in his comment on 3:1 emphasizes that the peace offering alone returns meat to the offerer's table — making it the sacrifice of reciprocity par excellence, in which God and the worshipper share a meal. Ramban's comments on the fat prohibition draw out the idea that the richest part of the animal, which in ANE culture was the guests' portion, is reserved entirely for God; hospitality is thus inverted and theologized. Maimonides codifies *šĕlāmîm* law in *Hilchot Maaseh HaKorbanot* 9-11 and classifies its varieties systematically. Bahya ben Asher adds mystical resonances; Sforno emphasizes the moral dimension of communal celebration before God. Nehama Leibowitz (*Studies in Vayikra*) reads the peace offering as the grammar of joy in covenant; Jonathan Sacks (*Covenant and Conversation: Leviticus*) emphasizes the uniqueness of a sacrifice in which the worshipper eats with God, arguing this is the ancestor of Sabbath and festival meals.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Leviticus* treats the peace offering in Hom. 5 and develops the typological line that will dominate patristic reception: the *šĕlāmîm* is the sacrifice of Christ as *pax nostra* (Eph 2:14 — "he is our peace"). The LXX's *sōtērion* underwrites Origen's move; the peace offering becomes the offering of salvation. Cyril of Alexandria (*Glaphyra*) extends this reading to the Eucharist explicitly — the meat shared with God prefigures the eucharistic meal where the offerer partakes of the sacrifice. Augustine in *City of God* 10 and *On the Trinity* 4 develops the broader theology of sacrifice as communion, although he does not comment on Lev 3 at length. Chrysostom and Theodoret follow similar allegorical lines.

Medieval exegesis inherited these readings. Aquinas in *Summa Theologiae* I-II q. 102 a. 3 ad 6-8 treats the peace offering as figuring Christ's sacrifice insofar as Christ's death establishes peace between God and humanity — the Pauline *pacificator* theme of Col 1:20. The Glossa Ordinaria compiles the patristic readings. Hugh of St. Victor draws the Eucharistic connection at *De Sacramentis* 2.8. The medieval Latin tradition consistently associates *šĕlāmîm* with the mass, reading the shared-meal structure typologically.

Reformation exegesis shifted the register. Calvin's *Commentary on the Last Four Books of Moses* (1563) reads Lev 3 as a picture of fellowship with God through sacrifice, insisting that the shared meal teaches Israel "that God does not merely pardon us but makes us his table-companions." Calvin resists the Catholic Eucharistic-sacrificial extension: the peace offering is fulfilled in Christ's once-for-all sacrifice, not repeated in the mass. Luther's treatment is more occasional; Bullinger echoes Calvin. Reformed sacramental theology has generally read *šĕlāmîm* as a type of spiritual communion that the Lord's Supper signifies without repeating.

Critical scholarship since Wellhausen places Lev 3 within P's reconstructed system. De Vaux's *Studies in Old Testament Sacrifice* (1964), von Rad's *Old Testament Theology*, Childs, Milgrom, and Knohl form the modern critical spine. Among contemporary theological commentators, Ephraim Radner (Brazos) develops a figural reading that takes the Eucharistic connection seriously without repeating the Catholic-Reformed dispute in its sixteenth-century form. L. Michael Morales (*Who Shall Ascend*) reads the peace offering as the culmination of the worshipper-manual, the sacrifice in which access to God is completed in communion. Wenham (NICOT), Sklar (NIVAC), and Hartley (WBC) work the historical-grammatical middle; Brueggemann's *Theology of the Old Testament* reads *šĕlāmîm* as the grammar of Israel's "well-being" as covenant gift.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Both traditions recognize the peace offering as the sacrifice of communion — the moment at which the worshipper does not merely approach God but eats with God. Both traditions read this communal structure as theologically pregnant. The divergence lies in what the fulfillment looks like. For Jewish interpretation after 70 CE, the peace offering — like all sacrifice — is suspended, with its communal logic preserved in Sabbath and festival meals, in the *kiddush* and *havdalah*, in the sanctified table that Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai will call "the altar of the household" (after Hag 2 and Berakhot). For Catholic and Orthodox Christianity, the peace offering is fulfilled in the Eucharist, and the structure of sacrifice followed by communion meal is retained. For Reformed Christianity, the sacrifice is fulfilled on the cross once-for-all, with the Lord's Supper signifying but not repeating; for Lutheran theology the real presence is affirmed but not construed sacrificially. Each tradition preserves a piece of the ancient logic.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: If the peace offering expresses communion, why does God alone get the fat and blood — the most prized ANE portions?** The surface logic of sacrifice-as-communion suggests reciprocity: offerer and God share the animal. But the chapter insists on an asymmetry. The richest, most valuable portions (visceral fat, plus all blood) are exclusively God's. This creates a theological question: what kind of communion reserves its best for one party?

The Jewish halakhic reading (Rashi, Ramban, codified in Mishnah Zevachim) treats the fat-and-blood reservation as acknowledgment that God is the source. The offerer gets the meat because God is not a consumer; the offerer returns to God what is uniquely God's — the life-bearing blood and the richest fat — and receives back the sustenance. Ramban in particular emphasizes that this is the reverse of ANE patronal hospitality, where the lord receives the best portion; here God's "best portion" is symbolically consumed in the fire, returning to the air as smoke, while the offerer eats.

Milgrom's critical reading frames the asymmetry functionally: blood is life (Lev 17:11), and life belongs to God alone; fat is the premium portion in ANE economy and so marks God's preeminence. The reservation is boundary-marking, not communion-denying. Mary Douglas develops this in *Leviticus as Literature*: the innermost organs to God, the outer flesh to the people — a spatial theology where the animal's anatomy maps onto the sanctuary's structure.

A Catholic reading through Ratzinger (*Spirit of the Liturgy*) construes the asymmetry as the essential structure of sacrifice: the worshipper surrenders what is not owned and receives in return what cannot be taken. The blood is God's; the fat is God's; what the worshipper eats is grace. Orthodox theology through Schmemann (*For the Life of the World*) reads the asymmetry eucharistically — the offering given, transfigured, and returned is the logic of all sacramental life. Reformed theology (Calvin, Owen *Death of Death*) reads the reservation as pedagogical: the offerer is taught that fellowship with God is not barter, and that what God receives is never functionally "consumed" but wholly relinquished; the offerer's portion is gift, not wages.

The steelman is Milgrom's: the text does not think it is expressing metaphysics of communion. It is enforcing a clean line between what belongs to God (life, best portion) and what belongs to humans (food, daily provision). The asymmetry is not a theological paradox to resolve; it is a boundary to preserve. Readings that romanticize the sacrificial meal as mutual communion overread.

**Where this leaves us:** the peace offering is communion structured by asymmetry. The parties do not share equally, and that unequal sharing is the communion. This is a distinctive shape that both traditions end up preserving in different forms — in the Eucharist's elevation and reception, in the Sabbath blessing and meal, in every liturgical act where the best is returned to God and received back as food.

**Tension 2: Why does the universal prohibition on eating blood (v. 17) come attached to a sacrifice and not to the dietary laws of Lev 11?** The prohibition is absolute, perpetual, and binding in every dwelling — and yet it is placed inside the peace offering pericope rather than in the Kosher code. This placement is deliberate, and its deliberateness is itself the tension.

The Jewish halakhic reading, codified in Chullin, treats the Lev 3:17 prohibition as the foundational kashrut principle from which all blood-handling law (*melichah*, salting; *soaking*; draining at *sheḥitâ*) derives. Its placement in Lev 3 is no accident: the peace offering is where the community most directly encounters blood outside the altar, and the absolute prohibition is set where the temptation is strongest — the celebratory meal. Ramban draws this out, as does Rashi on Lev 17:11. The prohibition is cultic-dietary at once.

Milgrom's reading — developed at book length in his Anchor volumes — connects the blood prohibition to the life-identification of Lev 17:11 ("the life of the flesh is in the blood"). Blood is not merely a dietary substance but a life-bearing one; to consume it is to consume life, which belongs only to God. The placement in Lev 3 anchors the prohibition in the moment of greatest ritual intensity — the sharing of sacrificed meat — before the broader code elaborates its cosmological grounding. Baruch Levine in *JPS Leviticus* concurs, noting that the Lev 17 argument is presupposed rather than stated in Lev 3.

A Christian reading through Hebrews 9-10 sees the blood prohibition as the OT grammar within which the cross makes sense: Christ's blood, uniquely, is offered and — paradoxically — received by worshippers in the Eucharist (John 6:53-56). The absolute prohibition of the OT is precisely what makes the NT inversion shocking. Catholic theology (Aquinas ST III q. 73-83) treats the shift as a sign that a new covenant is at work; the blood Christians "consume" is not animal life but divine life. Reformed theology (Calvin on Heb 9-10) construes the cross as the final blood-shedding after which the prohibition is no longer literal but its principle — the preciousness of life — stands. Orthodox theology treats the Eucharistic blood as transfigured life offered back in communion.

The steelman, advanced by Mary Douglas and Jacob Milgrom together, is that the blood prohibition is the single most distinctive feature of Israelite cult — more so than monotheism or aniconism in its ANE contrast — and that its placement in Lev 3, not Lev 11, signals that the prohibition is cultic before it is dietary. This reading, less intuitive because it treats Lev 3 rather than Lev 11 as the kashrut cornerstone, better fits the textual placement and the ANE evidence.

**Where this leaves us:** the blood prohibition is where the peace offering's communion logic meets the sacrificial system's boundary logic. The worshipper eats with God but not what is God's — not the blood, not the visceral fat. The line is drawn most sharply where the temptation is greatest: at the celebratory meal. Christian tradition reads this line as preparing for the cross; Jewish tradition preserves it as living halakhah; both agree that the Levitical placement is freighted with meaning.

**Unresolved question:** if the peace offering's central feature is a meal shared with God, why is there no explicit mention of God's "eating" in the chapter — only the formulaic "pleasing aroma" — while the offerer's eating is elaborated in Lev 7? The text carefully avoids the feeding-the-god language it might most naturally use here, and the avoidance itself deserves more attention than it typically receives.

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references worth following:

- **1 Samuel 1:3-4** — Elkanah's annual *zebaḥ* at Shiloh, where he distributes portions to his wives and sons, is a peace offering family meal. The domestic shape of 1 Sam 1 makes visible what Lev 3 legislates: peace offerings were the ordinary ritual backbone of Israelite family religion, not marginal ceremony.
- **Ezekiel 43:27** — the restored-temple vision specifies that on the eighth day the priests will make *ʿōlôt ve-šĕlāmîm* — burnt offerings and peace offerings — as the inaugural cultic act. The peace offering is the sign that the sanctuary is fully operational. Ezekiel's vision reverses the direction of Lev 1-3: what ends the restoration is what began the worshipper-manual.
- **1 Corinthians 10:18-21** — Paul's argument against idol-meat turns on the peace-offering logic. "Those who eat the sacrifices participate in the altar" (*koinōnoi tou thysiastēriou*) — precisely the *šĕlāmîm* theology of Lev 3. Paul uses the Levitical grammar to construe participation, and to argue that the Corinthians cannot share both the Lord's table and demonic tables. The NT's most developed theology of sacrificial participation is built on Lev 3's shared-meal structure.
