# Mark 15: The Crucifixion — Veil Torn, Centurion Confessing

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Pilate's interrogation in 15:2 — "*sy ei ho basileus tōn Ioudaiōn?*" — turns the question of Jesus's identity from religious to political in a single sentence. Where the Sanhedrin asked, "Are you the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?" (14:61), Pilate translates the charge into an idiom Rome can prosecute: kingship is sedition; *Caesar is Lord*; competing claimants are crucified. The charge that Mark records on the *titulus* in 15:26 — *ho basileus tōn Ioudaiōn* — is, on Roman legal precedent, the charge for which a man could be hung on a cross under the *lex maiestatis*.

Pontius Pilate himself is among the best-attested figures in the New Testament. The **Pilate Inscription**, discovered at Caesarea Maritima in 1961 by Italian archaeologist Antonio Frova, names *Pontius Pilatus, Praefectus Iudaeae* — fixing his title as *praefectus* (later sources called him *procurator*, an anachronism). Philo (*Legatio ad Gaium* 38) describes him as "naturally inflexible, a blend of self-will and relentlessness." Josephus (*Antiquities* 18.3, 18.4; *Jewish War* 2.169–177) recounts three of Pilate's confrontations with Jewish religious sensitivity — the standards in Jerusalem, the aqueduct funded from the temple treasury, and a massacre of Samaritans that ended his prefecture in 36 CE. Pilate's anxiety to keep public order in Jerusalem during festival is historically documented; his unwillingness to release a man whose own religious leaders had handed him over fits the pattern of a governor who has already mishandled his province and cannot afford another riot. **Helen Bond** (*Pontius Pilate in History and Interpretation*) and **Raymond Brown** (*The Death of the Messiah*) are the foundational modern treatments.

***Barabbas*** (15:7) is named — *Bar-Abbas*, "son of the father." A textual variant attested in some Caesarean witnesses and known to Origen (*Commentary on Matthew* 121) reads "**Jesus Barabbas**" at Matthew 27:16–17 — the parallel scene — and probably stood in some early copies. Origen objected to it on the grounds that no sinner should bear the holy name *Iesous*; **Bruce Metzger** (*Textual Commentary*) and **Bart Ehrman** (*The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture*) judge that the longer name is likely original and was suppressed by orthodox copyists. The juxtaposition is searing: *Jesus son of the father* the insurgent goes free; *Jesus Son of the Father* the Messiah is crucified. Mark's Barabbas is a *stasiastēs* (15:7), an "insurgent" who had committed murder in the *stasis* — an actual Roman provincial uprising. He is, in Roman legal terms, the *lēstēs* (cf. 14:48) Jesus is not, but is condemned as.

The ***Praetorium*** (15:16) has two candidate locations. The **Antonia Fortress** at the northwest corner of the temple platform (the Franciscan tradition; Pierre Benoit, 1971) housed the Roman cohort during festivals. The **Herodian palace** on the western hill (defended by Shimon Gibson, *The Final Days of Jesus*; and most modern Israeli archaeologists) was Pilate's actual residence when up from Caesarea. The Via Dolorosa traces the Antonia route, but the historical path more probably ran from Herod's palace through the Gennath Gate to Golgotha. The *Lithostrotos*/*Gabbatha* pavement under the Convent of the Sisters of Zion (cf. John 19:13) has been re-dated to Hadrian's 2nd-century *Aelia Capitolina*.

***Simon of Cyrene*** (15:21) — Cyrene was the Jewish-populated capital of Roman Cyrenaica (modern Libya); Acts 6:9 mentions a Cyrenian synagogue in Jerusalem. Mark's notice that Simon is *the father of Alexander and Rufus* is the kind of detail that reads as eyewitness signature: Mark assumes his Roman audience knows these sons. **Richard Bauckham** (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*) makes Simon's family the prooftext for his case that Mark's named secondary characters were eyewitness sources whose families remained known in the early Christian community; Romans 16:13 greets a Rufus, and early-second-century tradition (Hippolytus, Eusebius) identifies him with Simon's son. If correct, this is concrete internal evidence that Mark wrote in Rome for a Roman audience.

***Golgotha*** (15:22) — Aramaic *Gulgalta*, "skull" — is now almost universally located at the **Church of the Holy Sepulchre** rather than the **Garden Tomb** popularized by Charles Gordon in 1883. The Holy Sepulchre site sits on a former stone quarry outside the Gennath Gate of the pre-70 walls, with first-century rolling-stone tombs cut into its limestone face. Hadrian built a temple to Aphrodite over it in 135 CE — unintentional preservation, since Helena identified the site by demolishing it in 326. The **Garden Tomb** is Second Temple period, but its skull-face is now regarded as a later quarrying artifact, and its single-chamber multi-bench typology does not match 15:46 (engage **Jerome Murphy-O'Connor** *The Holy Land*; **Shimon Gibson**).

The ***myrrhed wine*** (15:23) was an analgesic offered to crucifixion victims. *b. Sanhedrin* 43a records the rabbinic custom of giving condemned criminals "a grain of frankincense in a cup of wine" to dull the senses, citing Proverbs 31:6 — "give strong drink to him who is perishing." Jesus refuses it; he will go to the cross awake.

***Crucifixion*** itself — Greek *staurōsis* — has left us almost no firsthand survivor description. The 1968 discovery of an ossuary at **Giv'at ha-Mivtar** in north Jerusalem containing the heel bone of *Yehohanan ben Hagqol*, with an iron nail driven through the calcaneus and a fragment of olive wood — the only physical evidence ever recovered — confirmed that the feet were nailed sideways to the upright. Cicero (*Verrine Orations* 2.5.165) called it "*crudelissimum taeterrimumque supplicium*." **Martin Hengel**'s *Crucifixion* (1977) remains foundational.

Three Greek terms drive the chapter. ***Eloi Eloi lema sabachthani*** (15:34) — Aramaic of Psalm 22:1; Mark preserves the original liturgical voice rather than the Hebrew (*Eli Eli*) preserved in Matthew 27:46. The mishearing of *Eloi* as *Elias* (Elijah) makes sense only in Aramaic-speaking ears. ***eschisthē*** (15:38, "was torn") — same root as *schizō* in 1:10, completing the Markan inclusio. ***alēthōs houtos ho anthrōpos huios theou ēn*** (15:39, "truly this man was Son of God") — the centurion's confession; the grammatical absence of the article before *huios* opens the translation question discussed below.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Mark 15 is the load-bearing chapter of the Gospel's literary architecture. Two structural features dominate.

The **great Markan inclusio**: at Jesus's baptism in 1:10, the heavens are *schizomenous* — torn open — and the Spirit descends, and the voice declares, "You are my Son, the Beloved." At Jesus's death in 15:38, the temple veil is *eschisthē* — torn — from top to bottom, and the centurion declares, "Truly this man was Son of God." The two scenes are framed by the same verb-root (*schizō* / *eschisthē*) and the same Christological declaration (*huios theou*). **Joel Marcus** (Anchor Bible) and **Adela Yarbro Collins** (Hermeneia) read this as the structural key of Mark: the apocalyptic envelope opens at baptism, closes at death, and contains the entire Gospel as a single act of divine self-disclosure. The torn heaven and the torn veil are the *two* tearings of Mark; everything in between is what is exposed when God tears his own dwelling open. **R.T. France** (NIGTC), **Morna Hooker** (BNTC), Vincent Taylor, and **Donald Juel** (*A Master of Surprise*) all build on this structural reading.

The **two human confessions** of Jesus as *huios theou*: only two human characters in Mark confess Jesus as Son of God, and they are deliberately paired. Peter at Caesarea Philippi (8:29) confesses *sy ei ho Christos* ("you are the Christ") — a confession Mark immediately undermines by Peter's rebuke of Jesus's passion prediction (8:32–33). The centurion at the cross (15:39) confesses *alēthōs houtos ho anthrōpos huios theou ēn* — and Mark gives this confession the climactic position. The pattern is theologically pointed: a Jewish disciple in Galilee gets the *title* but misunderstands the *cross*; a Gentile soldier at the cross gets *both*. The **messianic secret** that **William Wrede** (*The Messianic Secret in the Gospels*, 1901) made famous — Jesus's repeated demands that demons and disciples not declare his identity — is *resolved at the cross*. Wrede's foundational thesis was that the secret was a Markan literary device to explain why Jesus had not been recognized as Messiah during his ministry; whatever its historical origin, narratively the secret breaks open here. The first human in Mark to call Jesus *Son of God* is a Roman centurion at the moment of his death.

A grammatical and theological debate attends 15:39. ***huios theou*** is anarthrous — "Son of God" or "*a* son of God"? **Earl Johnson Jr.** (*Mark 15:39 and the So-Called Confession of the Roman Centurion*) argued for a less-confessional reading: the centurion is admiring Jesus as a noble *vir divinus*, "a son of God" in the loose Hellenistic sense applied to heroes and emperors. **Jack Kingsbury** (*The Christology of Mark's Gospel*), **Yarbro Collins**, **Marcus**, and most modern commentators defend the full confessional reading: the anarthrous predicate before the verb is a Greek convention (Colwell's rule), not a sign of indefiniteness, and the narrative weight of the inclusio with 1:11 demands the full confession. **Frank Matera** (*The Kingship of Jesus*) reads it as the climactic recognition that organizes the entire Gospel.

The **temple veil** (15:38) — which veil? The temple complex had two veils: the outer *parokhet* between the *azarah* (court) and the *hekhal* (Holy Place), and the inner *parokhet* between the Holy Place and the *Devir* (Holy of Holies). **Daniel Gurtner** (*The Torn Veil*) is the most thorough modern treatment; he argues for the outer veil on the basis that it was visible from outside the temple (a Roman centurion could not have seen the inner one) and that Josephus describes the outer veil as a tapestry depicting the cosmos (*Jewish War* 5.212–213) — its tearing has cosmic-apocalyptic resonance. The minority position (most patristic commentators; some modern Catholic interpreters) reads it as the inner veil, granting access to the Holy of Holies and supporting the Hebrews 9–10 reading. Mark is ambiguous; the theological force survives either choice.

Markan signature features: ***euthys*** is conspicuously *absent* through most of chapter 15, slowing the narrative for the central crucifixion scene. The triadic structure governs the time markings — *third hour* (15:25), *sixth hour* (15:33), *ninth hour* (15:34) — and the threefold mockery (passersby, chief priests, fellow-crucified). The vocabulary of *paradidōmi* continues from chapter 14: Jesus is delivered to Pilate (15:1), delivered to be crucified (15:15) — programmatic Markan passion verb completing its arc.

A textual note: 15:28 ("And the Scripture was fulfilled which says, 'And he was numbered with the transgressors'") is omitted from the earliest Greek manuscripts (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus) and almost universally regarded as a later harmonization to Luke 22:37; modern critical editions (NA28, UBS5) bracket or omit it, which is why the ESV jumps from verse 27 to 29.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Psalm 22 is the chapter's structuring intertext. Mark builds the crucifixion scene as a tissue of Psalm 22 allusions: the casting of lots for the garments (15:24 / Ps 22:18), the wagging of heads (15:29 / Ps 22:7), the mocking demand "let him save himself" (15:30 / Ps 22:8), and the cry of dereliction itself (15:34 / Ps 22:1). **N.T. Wright** (*The Day the Revolution Began*) and **Joel Marcus** (*The Way of the Lord: Christological Exegesis of the Old Testament in the Gospel of Mark*) emphasize that Psalm 22 is not merely a psalm of dereliction but a psalm of vindication — verses 22–31 turn to praise and proclaim the kingdom of God to the nations. Jesus's citation of the first verse, on the Jewish principle of *shorthand citation* (cite the opening, invoke the whole), brings the entire arc of the psalm into the cross — including the ending where God's reign is proclaimed to the ends of the earth. Whether Jesus is doing this on the cross, or Mark is doing it in the framing, is a separate question; the literary fact stands.

Within Second Temple Judaism, the political and theological weight of crucifixion was distinctive. **E.P. Sanders** (*The Historical Figure of Jesus*) emphasizes that Jesus was crucified as a royal pretender — the Romans crucified for sedition, not blasphemy, and the *titulus* records the actual charge. **Geza Vermes** (*Jesus the Jew*; *The Passion*) and **Amy-Jill Levine** (*The Misunderstood Jew*) press hard against any reading that places collective Jewish guilt on the cross; the Sanhedrin acted within a particular political context, and the executioners were Roman. **Daniel Boyarin** (*The Jewish Gospels*) reads the messianic-suffering theme as already present in Second Temple Jewish thought (Isaiah 53, the Targums on Zech 12:10, 4Q541) — the cross is not an interpretive shock to Jewish messianism but a particular and contested instance of it.

The Christian interpretive trajectory has placed more theological weight on chapter 15 than on any other passage in the Gospels. **Origen** (*Commentary on Matthew*; *Contra Celsum* 7.55) reads the cross as cosmic exorcism. **Athanasius** (*On the Incarnation* §8–9) makes the death of Christ the means by which death itself is destroyed — *Christus Victor*. **Augustine** (*De Trinitate* 4.13–14; *On the Spirit and the Letter*) develops the cross as substitutionary mediation. **Gregory of Nazianzus** (*Theological Orations*) wrestles with the cry of dereliction: did the Father abandon the Son? Gregory's answer is no — the Son who cries out *prays* the abandonment as our representative; the dereliction is not the rupture of the Trinity but the obedience of the Son in the depths of human alienation.

**Anselm** (*Cur Deus Homo*, 1098) constructs the satisfaction theory: the cross repays the debt of human sin to divine honor. **Aquinas** (*ST* III q. 46–49; *Catena Aurea* on Mark 15) refines satisfaction into a richer doctrine of fitting sacrifice. **Calvin** (*Institutes* 2.16) systematizes penal substitution: Christ bears the curse in our place. **Luther** (*Heidelberg Disputation*; *Bondage of the Will*) develops the *theologia crucis* — the theology of the cross — against the *theologia gloriae*: God is hidden in the cross and revealed in suffering, not in power. **Bonhoeffer** (*Discipleship*; *Letters and Papers from Prison*) makes Luther's cross-theology existentially urgent: "only the suffering God can help."

**Jürgen Moltmann**'s *The Crucified God* (1972) is the foundational modern crucifixion theology and indispensable for chapter 15. Writing after Auschwitz and as the post-war German theological generation reckoned with the silence of God in the camps, Moltmann reads 15:34's cry of dereliction as the constitutive moment of Christian Trinitarian thought: at the cross, the Son is genuinely abandoned by the Father, and yet the Spirit's bond of love between them is the bond of their *separation*. The cross, Moltmann argues, is *where* the Trinity is most itself — God in God-forsakenness, love that goes the full distance into the absence of love. This is not a comfortable doctrine; Moltmann means it to be uncomfortable, and to make Christian faith answerable to the silence the survivors of the camps knew. **Hans Urs von Balthasar** (*Mysterium Paschale*) extends Moltmann into a Holy Saturday theology of Christ's descent into the absolute absence of God — the silence before resurrection.

**Karl Barth** (*Church Dogmatics* IV/1, §59) titles his crucifixion treatment "*The Judge Judged in Our Place*" — the cross is the eternal Yes of God spoken through and against the No of human sin; the judge who has the right to condemn assumes the condemnation himself. **N.T. Wright** (*The Day the Revolution Began*; *Mark for Everyone*) develops a covenantal-eschatological reading: the cross is the climactic act of the Israel-story, where Israel's vocation to bear the world's evil is fulfilled by Israel's representative.

**Ched Myers** (*Binding the Strong Man*) reads chapter 15 as the climax of the Gospel's anti-imperial witness: Jesus is crucified by Roman power as a political insurgent and is vindicated by the rending of the temple — both Roman empire and Jerusalem temple-state are unmasked. **Yoder** (*The Politics of Jesus*) and Bonhoeffer make the cross the ethical center of Christian discipleship.

**Raymond Brown**'s *The Death of the Messiah* (2 vols., 1994) is the most thorough modern Passion-narrative commentary — over 1,600 pages of historical, literary, and theological analysis of Mark 14–15 and parallels. **Frank Matera** (*The Kingship of Jesus*), **Joel Marcus** (AB), **Adela Yarbro Collins** (Hermeneia), **Morna Hooker** (BNTC) carry the modern critical work.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Was Jesus actually forsaken by the Father?**

The ***penal-substitution*** position (Calvin *Institutes* 2.16; Charles Hodge *Systematic Theology*; modern Reformed) reads 15:34 as the moment the Father pours out the wrath against sin onto the Son; the Son is *really* forsaken, bearing the curse Galatians 3:13 names. The strength: it takes the cry's plain force and the substitutionary logic of Isaiah 53 and 2 Corinthians 5:21 with full seriousness. The weakness: pressed too hard, it threatens the unity of the Trinity, suggesting opposition between Father and Son.

The ***Moltmann / Trinitarian-relational*** position (Moltmann *The Crucified God*; Balthasar *Mysterium Paschale*) reads the dereliction as a real event within the Trinity: the Son experiences abandonment by the Father, and the Spirit is the bond that holds them in their separation; God in God-forsakenness is *more* the Trinity, not less. The strength: it preserves the cry's full pathos while keeping the Trinity intact through pneumatological framing. The weakness: it requires careful formulation to avoid implying genuine rupture in the divine being.

The ***patristic / "shorthand citation"*** position (Gregory of Nazianzus; modern Wright, Hooker) reads the cry as Jesus citing Psalm 22 by its opening, invoking the entire psalm including its concluding vindication. The dereliction is real as suffering but *prayed* — the Son who quotes the psalm prays himself through abandonment toward vindication. The strength: it respects the psalm's complete arc and Jewish citation conventions. The weakness: it can soften the cry's protest into something too neat.

**Where this leaves us**: Mark refuses the systematic answer. The cry is preserved in Aramaic — the rawest possible form — and stands without theological commentary. The reader meets the dereliction as the chapter's center, not its solved problem. Any atonement theology earns its keep by what it does with this cry; any Christology earns its keep by whether it lets the cry be real.

**Tension Two: What does the centurion actually see?**

The ***full confessional*** reading (the dominant modern position; Marcus, Yarbro Collins, Matera, Kingsbury) takes 15:39 as the climactic Christological recognition of the Gospel. The centurion sees the manner of Jesus's death — the loud cry, the breath, the way he died — and confesses what no human in Mark has yet confessed: this is the Son of God. The strength: it completes the structural inclusio with 1:11 and resolves the messianic secret at the cross. The weakness: it requires reading a significant theological recognition into the brief statement of a Roman officer.

The ***minimal-Hellenistic*** reading (Earl Johnson Jr.; some history-of-religions commentators) reads the centurion as making an ordinary Roman *vir divinus* admiration: this was a noble man, *a* son of the gods. The strength: it respects the lower historical probability of a Roman officer reaching full Christological confession on Calvary. The weakness: it underweights Mark's structural framing, which has been preparing the reader for *exactly* this confession from 1:1 onward (where Mark titles his Gospel "*the gospel of Jesus Christ, Son of God*").

The ***ironic*** reading (Donald Juel; Frank Kermode) holds that the confession is true *as Mark frames it* even if the centurion did not understand his own words; the ironic gap between speaker and meaning is a Markan signature device. The Roman officer says the right words for the wrong reasons, and Mark's reader hears the truth the speaker does not.

**Where this leaves us**: the centurion is the first human in Mark to see clearly, and Mark places him at the cross. The Gospel that began with God tearing open the heavens for the Son ends — at the climax — with God tearing open the temple veil and a Roman pagan recognizing the Son. The Christology is not abstract. It is the Christology of *this* death.

**An unresolved question**: if the centurion sees and confesses, why is the centurion not given a name? Mark names Simon of Cyrene's sons but not the man who confesses Jesus as Son of God. Later tradition supplied the name *Longinus*. Mark's silence may be theological: the recognition is not personal but representative — the first Gentile sees, and the Gentile mission to which Mark writes is born at the cross of an unnamed soldier. Or it may be historical reticence — Mark did not know. The chapter does not say.

## The Thread

15:38's torn veil reaches back to 1:10's torn heavens — the Markan inclusio binding the Gospel as a single apocalyptic act. 15:34's cry of dereliction echoes Psalm 22 forward to Hebrews 5:7 ("with loud cries and tears") and Romans 8:32 ("he who did not spare his own Son"). 15:39's centurion confession completes the trajectory begun at 1:1 ("the gospel of Jesus Christ, Son of God") and at 1:11 (the Father's voice, "you are my Son") — the divine declaration is finally heard and echoed by a human voice, and that voice is a Roman pagan. The Gospel that began with the heavens torn for the Son ends with the veil torn for the world.
