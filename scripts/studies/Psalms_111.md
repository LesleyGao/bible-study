# Psalm 111 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 111 has no superscription. It opens with *halleluyah* (הַלְלוּ־יָהּ), "Praise the LORD!" — the imperative-plural call to praise that becomes a hallmark of Books IV-V of the Psalter and especially the closing doxological cluster (Pss 111-118, 146-150). The unsuperscribed *halleluyah* psalms in Book V form a recognizable sub-collection, often called the "Hallel collections," in which Pss 111 and 112 stand as a paired prologue to the great *Egyptian Hallel* (Pss 113-118).

In **Gunkel**'s form-critical taxonomy, Ps 111 is a **Hymnus** — a hymn of praise — and specifically a **Lehrhymnus** or *didactic hymn*, a hymn whose praise is structured to teach something. The teaching genre is ratified by the closing line: *reshit chokhmah yir'at Yahweh* (רֵאשִׁית חָכְמָה יִרְאַת יְהוָה), "the fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom" (111:10) — a sapiential motto that frames the praise as wisdom-teaching. The combination of hymn and wisdom-teaching is characteristic of late post-exilic compositions; the genre overlap reflects the period's integration of cultic praise with sapiential reflection.

Dating is post-exilic. Linguistic features — the late vocabulary, the wisdom-tradition framing, the acrostic form — all point to the Persian or early Hellenistic period (fifth to third century BCE). Most modern scholars (Hossfeld-Zenger, Allen, Goldingay, Tate) place the composition in the post-exilic temple's liturgical setting, where the second-temple community needed didactic praise to teach a generation that had not seen the first temple's glory.

The most distinctive feature of Ps 111 is its **alphabetic acrostic** form. Each *colon* (half-verse) begins with a successive letter of the Hebrew alphabet — twenty-two letters in twenty-two cola, distributed across ten verses. Verses 1-8 each have two cola; verses 9 and 10 each have three. The first colon of v. 1 (after the *halleluyah*) begins with **א** *aleph* (*odeh*, "I will give thanks"); the last colon of v. 10 begins with **ת** *tav* (*tehilato*, "his praise"). The acrostic runs A to Z without break.

Acrostic psalms are a specific late genre, found in Pss 9-10, 25, 34, 37, 111, 112, 119, 145, and outside the Psalter in Lam 1-4 and Prov 31:10-31. The form is constraining: the poet must begin each colon with a fixed letter, regardless of grammatical or rhythmic preference. The constraint produces a particular literary effect: the praise is *complete*, covering the alphabet from A to Z. **Adele Berlin** has written most influentially on the rhetoric of biblical acrostics, arguing that the form embodies *theological completeness* — A-to-Z stands for everything that can be said. The acrostic claims that the poem says all that needs to be said about its topic.

Ps 111 is paired with Ps 112. The two are sister-poems — both alphabetic acrostics with twenty-two cola in ten verses; both opening with *halleluyah*; both built around the keyword *tzedek/tzedaqah* (righteousness); both ending with eternal-praise / eternal-shame imagery. **Ps 111 praises YHWH; Ps 112 praises the one who fears YHWH.** The pairing is the key to reading either: the divine attributes praised in 111 are mirrored in the human virtues praised in 112. The God who is "gracious and merciful" (*chanun ve-rachum*, 111:4) is praised in 111; the human who is "gracious and merciful" (*chanun ve-rachum*, 112:4) is praised in 112. The humanity of the upright reflects the character of God. **Hossfeld-Zenger** and **Mitchell** treat the pairing as deliberate editorial composition — likely by the same author, certainly placed deliberately by the editor.

The keyword *zikkaron* / *zakhar* (memory, remember) recurs through the psalm. *Zekher asah le-niflotav* (זֵכֶר עָשָׂה לְנִפְלְאוֹתָיו), "he has caused his wondrous works to be remembered" (111:4). *Yizkor le-olam berito* (יִזְכֹּר לְעוֹלָם בְּרִיתוֹ), "he remembers his covenant forever" (111:5). Memory is the cultic action: God remembers, and Israel is taught to remember. The wondrous works that God *causes to be remembered* are the deliverances — the exodus, the covenant, the giving of the land. Ps 111 is partly a *zikkaron* psalm — a remembering hymn — that teaches the post-exilic community to keep the redemptive history alive in their cultic praise.

The closing line draws the wisdom tradition explicitly: *reshit chokhmah yir'at Yahweh, sekhel tov le-khol-osehem* (רֵאשִׁית חָכְמָה יִרְאַת יְהוָה שֵׂכֶל טוֹב לְכָל־עֹשֵׂיהֶם), "the fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom; / a good understanding have all those who practice it" (111:10). The first half is a near-citation of Prov 1:7 and Prov 9:10; the second half echoes Prov 13:15 and 15:32. The psalm is integrating the wisdom-tradition's central maxim into the temple's hymnic praise — claiming that the *fear of YHWH* and the *praise of YHWH* are the same posture, expressed in different idioms.

The DSS preservation of Ps 111 in 11Q5 (11QPs-a) shows minor textual variations but maintains the acrostic form intact — strong evidence that the acrostic was scrupulously preserved through transmission. The LXX (Psalm 110) struggles slightly with the acrostic (which Greek cannot reproduce) but renders the content faithfully.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Every literary feature of Ps 111 is shaped by the acrostic constraint. **Lowth's** parallelism is mostly synonymous in the bicola — the two cola of each verse repeat the same idea in different words. "Great are the works of the LORD, / studied by all who delight in them" (111:2). "Full of splendor and majesty is his work, / and his righteousness endures forever" (111:3). The synonymous parallelism is well-suited to the acrostic form because each colon must begin with a fixed letter — the poet is pushed toward conceptual repetition rather than rhetorical extension.

The acrostic distribution:
- v. 1a: **א** *aleph* — *odeh* (I will give thanks)
- v. 1b: **ב** *bet* — *be-sod yesharim* (in the company of the upright)
- v. 2a: **ג** *gimel* — *gedolim* (great)
- v. 2b: **ד** *dalet* — *derushim* (studied)
- v. 3a: **ה** *he* — *hod* (splendor)
- v. 3b: **ו** *vav* — *ve-tzidqato* (and his righteousness)
- v. 4a: **ז** *zayin* — *zekher* (remembrance)
- v. 4b: **ח** *het* — *channun* (gracious)
- v. 5a: **ט** *tet* — *teref* (food)
- v. 5b: **י** *yod* — *yizkor* (he remembers)
- v. 6a: **כ** *kaf* — *koach* (power)
- v. 6b: **ל** *lamed* — *latet lahem* (to give them)
- v. 7a: **מ** *mem* — *ma'ase* (works)
- v. 7b: **נ** *nun* — *ne'emanim* (trustworthy)
- v. 8a: **ס** *samek* — *semukhim* (established)
- v. 8b: **ע** *ayin* — *asuyim* (performed)
- v. 9a: **פ** *pe* — *pedut* (redemption)
- v. 9b: **צ** *tzade* — *tzivvah* (he commanded)
- v. 9c: **ק** *qof* — *qadosh* (holy)
- v. 10a: **ר** *resh* — *reshit* (beginning)
- v. 10b: **ש** *shin* — *sekhel* (understanding)
- v. 10c: **ת** *tav* — *tehilato* (his praise)

The progression is itself thematic. The first letters trace divine action and character: *I will give thanks* → *great* → *splendor* → *remembrance* → *gracious* → *food* → *power* → *works* → *trustworthy* → *established* → *redemption* → *holy*. The last letters move from divine attribute to human response: *beginning [of wisdom]* → *understanding* → *praise*. The acrostic structure carries the theological argument: the alphabetic completeness of God's works (א through ק) culminates in the alphabetic completeness of right human response (ר, ש, ת).

The **structural division** of the psalm follows the acrostic but tracks a thematic movement:
- **Vv. 1-3** — the praise of YHWH's character and works.
- **Vv. 4-6** — the redemptive history (covenant, food, inheritance of the nations).
- **Vv. 7-9** — the trustworthiness of the works and precepts; redemption.
- **V. 10** — the wisdom-coda: fear of the LORD as beginning of wisdom.

There is **inclusio** by way of the *halleluyah* opening and the *tehilato* closing — the psalm begins with "praise the LORD" and ends with "his praise [endures forever]." The whole composition is bracketed by praise.

The keyword density is striking. *Le-olam* (forever) appears in vv. 3, 5, 8, 9, 10 — the eternal duration of God's righteousness, covenant, precepts, redemption, and praise. The psalm's eternal-claim is asserted at every structural seam.

There is no *selah* — Book V's restraint. Prosody is mostly 3+3 stress per colon, with the last colon of v. 9 ("Holy and awesome is his name") slightly heavier and the wisdom-coda of v. 10 carrying the punch of the entire composition.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Jewish Reception.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Ps 111 reads the acrostic as a complete praise — A-to-Z covering the totality of YHWH's works. The midrash treats the wisdom-coda (v. 10) as the binding clause: the praise is not merely cultic but instructive — it teaches the reader that the *fear of YHWH* is the necessary posture for receiving all that has been praised.

**Rashi** reads the psalm straightforwardly as praise; he focuses particular attention on v. 10 and reads the wisdom-coda in the line of Prov 1:7 and 9:10. **Ibn Ezra** notes the acrostic form and treats it as an aid to memorization — the poet's gift to the community is a praise that can be learned by heart through the alphabetical order. **Radak** develops the keyword-density reading and emphasizes the *zikkaron* theme: God remembers his covenant; the community remembers God's works; the praise is the cultic act of mutual remembering.

In modern Jewish scholarship, **Sarna** in his JPS commentary on the Psalms developed the Pss 111-112 pairing as a deliberate editorial construction: 111 is the divine *imitatio* and 112 the human *imitatio Dei*. The grace and mercy attributed to YHWH in 111:4 are mirrored in the grace and mercy attributed to the upright human in 112:4, and the structural parallel teaches that human ethics flows from divine character. **Levenson** has emphasized that Ps 111's wisdom-coda integrates the wisdom and cult traditions in a way characteristic of the late Persian period — the temple's praise becomes a school of wisdom.

**Adele Berlin**'s essay on biblical acrostics has been definitive for modern reception. Berlin argues that the acrostic form does not merely impose a constraint; it *signifies* completeness. The poem says A through Z; the praise covers the alphabet; nothing is left out. The form is itself a theological claim about the comprehensiveness of God's praise. Berlin's reading is now the standard among most modern commentators. **Wenham** in *Psalms as Torah* makes Ps 111-112 central to his argument that the Psalter teaches ethics through embodied praise.

**Christian Reception.** **Augustine** (*Enarrationes* on Ps 110 LXX) reads Ps 111 as the praise of God's works — creation, redemption, and the gift of the law — and pairs his exposition with Ps 112 as the praise of the righteous human who responds to those works. Augustine's pairing treatment establishes the Christian interpretive convention of reading Pss 111 and 112 together. **Cassiodorus** continues the pairing and adds a Christological dimension: the *fear of the LORD* of v. 10 becomes the proper Christian posture before the incarnate Christ.

**Aquinas** treats Ps 111:10 as a foundational text for his treatment of the *gift of fear* (*timor*) in *ST* II-II.19 — one of the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit. The fear of the LORD that is the beginning of wisdom is, for Aquinas, both filial fear (reverence) and servile fear (awareness of judgment); the gift of fear in the Christian life integrates both into the proper posture of the soul before God. **Luther** (*Operationes*) reads the psalm as the great praise-and-wisdom hymn and emphasizes the integrative function of v. 10 — fear and praise are not opposites but the same posture in different modes. **Calvin** (1557) treats Ps 111 as the Christian's school of doctrine: each verse teaches something about God's character, and the acrostic structure teaches that the doctrine is comprehensive.

In modern critical scholarship, **Gunkel** classed Ps 111 as a hymn with wisdom features. **Mowinckel** placed it in temple cultic use. **Westermann** noted its didactic function. **Kraus** detailed the post-exilic dating and the sapiential integration. **Hossfeld-Zenger** developed the most influential reading: Pss 111-112 are deliberately paired and stand at a key transition point in Book V, where the great Hallel (113-118) is about to begin. The two acrostics function as a *prologue* to the Egyptian Hallel — a meditative-didactic gateway into the great festival praise. **Goldingay** and **Tate** follow this reading. **Mitchell** treats Pss 111-112 as part of Book V's didactic-eschatological program.

**Wenham** has developed the *Psalms as Torah* reading specifically through Pss 111-112, arguing that the Psalter teaches its singers to be like the God they praise — that the *imitatio Dei* implicit in 111-112 is the Psalter's central ethical move. **Brown** and **Tanner** have similarly emphasized the Psalter's ethical formation through embodied praise.

**Brueggemann** has argued that Ps 111's *fear of the LORD* is the antidote to Israel's chronic temptation to practical atheism — the temptation to live as if YHWH were not the relevant power. The wisdom-coda is not merely sapiential commonplace but a polemical claim against idolatry: only the *yir'at Yahweh* yields *chokhmah*; alternative wisdoms grounded elsewhere are not wisdom at all.

**NT Use.** Ps 111 is not directly quoted in the NT, but its language and themes saturate the gospels and epistles. The phrase "great are the works of the LORD" (111:2) feeds into the Lukan Magnificat ("he has done great things for me," Luke 1:49). The *gracious and merciful* couplet (*channun ve-rachum*, 111:4) is the standard divine-attribute formula that runs through Exod 34:6 into the gospels' presentation of Jesus. The wisdom-coda (v. 10) feeds the Pauline argument that "the foolish things of God are wiser than men" (1 Cor 1:25) — Paul stands in continuity with the wisdom-tradition that Ps 111 integrates with cultic praise.

The doxological framework of Ps 111 — beginning and ending with praise, structuring the alphabet of works between — is deeply influential on the Christian *Te Deum* and on the structure of Christian hymnody generally.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The acrostic constraint and theological economy.**

*Position A: Berlin's completeness reading.* The acrostic form is a theological claim. By covering the alphabet, the psalm asserts that its praise is *complete* — A through Z, exhaustive, comprehensive. The form tells the reader: nothing is left out; this is the totality of right praise. The constraint is not a limitation but a virtuosic expression of theological wholeness. The form *is* the theology.

*Position B: The poet's-craft reading.* The acrostic form is primarily an aid to memorization and recital. The Persian-period community needed liturgical material that could be learned and recited; the alphabetical order helped. The theological content is no different from a non-acrostic hymn; the form is mnemonic, not theological. On this reading, the acrostic is craft, not signification.

*Position C: The integrative reading (Wenham, Hossfeld-Zenger).* The acrostic is *both* — mnemonic and theological. The form serves memorization and serves the claim of completeness. The two functions are not in tension; the form's mnemonic utility is precisely what makes its theological claim effective. Through learning the alphabet of praise by heart, the singer internalizes the claim that praise is comprehensive. The form teaches what it embodies.

**Steelman:** Berlin's reading captures the theological dimension that the poet's-craft reading misses; the poet's-craft reading captures the practical dimension that Berlin's reading might overlook. The integrative reading holds them together: the form is craft *and* signification, mnemonic *and* theology. The acrostic is not arbitrary, and it is not merely formal.

**Tension 2: The wisdom-tradition's *fear of the LORD* and the Christian gospel.**

V. 10's *fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom* is the wisdom-tradition's defining maxim, present also in Prov 1:7, 9:10, Job 28:28. How does this maxim relate to the Christian gospel of grace?

*Position A: Continuity (Aquinas, Reformed orthodoxy).* The fear of the LORD is the proper Christian posture, integrated into the gospel through the gift of fear (Aquinas) and through the Reformed understanding of *coram Deo* awe. The gospel does not abolish the fear of the LORD; it transforms it from servile dread into filial reverence. The wisdom-coda remains operative in Christian discipleship.

*Position B: Discontinuity (some Protestant pietistic traditions).* The gospel of grace transcends the wisdom-tradition's fear of the LORD. "Perfect love casts out fear" (1 John 4:18); the Christian relates to God as *Abba* (Rom 8:15), not as one to be feared. Ps 111:10 belongs to the OT preparatory stage; the Christian moves through it to a higher posture of love.

*Position C: The integrative reading (mainstream Christian theology).* The fear of the LORD and the love of God are not opposites but two dimensions of the same posture. To love God truly is to fear him (in the sense of reverence, awe, recognition of his transcendence); to fear him truly is to love him (in the sense of trust, submission, obedience). Ps 111:10 stands in continuity with the gospel because the gospel's grace is the grace of the holy God who is rightly feared. The two are not in tension; they are both required.

**Steelman:** The integrative reading is right. The discontinuity reading reflects a sentimentalized version of grace that the NT itself rejects (cf. Heb 12:28-29, "let us offer to God acceptable worship, with reverence and awe; for our God is a consuming fire"). The continuity reading rightly preserves the wisdom-tradition's claim. The gospel transforms the fear of the LORD; it does not abolish it.

**Tension 3: The Pss 111-112 pairing — what does *imitatio Dei* mean?**

The pairing makes a strong claim: the human who fears the LORD reflects the LORD's character. The *channun ve-rachum* of 111:4 (gracious and merciful — God) becomes the *channun ve-rachum* of 112:4 (gracious and merciful — the upright human). What is the theological force of this?

*Position A: Strong *imitatio Dei*.* The human is called to be like God in his ethical character. Grace, mercy, righteousness, generosity — these divine attributes are also human virtues, and the upright human becomes them through the fear of the LORD. The *imitatio Dei* is the Psalter's central ethical move; Wenham's *Psalms as Torah* develops this strongly.

*Position B: Weak *imitatio Dei*.* The parallel between 111:4 and 112:4 reflects how the upright human *receives* God's grace, not how the human *becomes* like God. The character is divine, not transferable; the upright human receives the divine character as gift, not as imitation.

*Position C: The integrative reading.* The strong *imitatio Dei* is right but properly framed: the upright human becomes like God *because the fear of the LORD orients him to receive the divine character*. There is no contradiction between gift and imitation; the imitation is the form the gift takes in the human life.

**Steelman:** The integrative reading captures both the gift-character of grace and the genuine ethical formation that follows. Ps 111-112's pairing is not minor; it is the Psalter's clearest formal statement that praising God shapes the praiser into the likeness of God praised.

**Where this leaves us.** Ps 111 teaches that praise is structurally complete — A through Z, covering the alphabet of God's works. It teaches that praise integrates with wisdom — the *fear of the LORD* is the right posture for receiving the works praised. It teaches that the post-exilic community needed didactic praise — hymns that taught while they praised. It teaches that the human who fears the LORD becomes like the LORD whom he fears — the *imitatio Dei* is implicit in the praise. And it teaches that the Christian *Te Deum*, the Reformation's confessional hymnody, and the contemporary worship song stand in a tradition that goes back to this acrostic — the praise that says everything and tells the singer to become what he sings.

**Closing question.** If the alphabet of praise is the form Ps 111 chose to embody the claim that nothing is left out, what does it mean for our worship — full of refrains and reprises, often impressionistic and emotional — to learn again from a poem that started with א and reached ת before it would consent to stop?
