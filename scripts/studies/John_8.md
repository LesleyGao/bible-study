# John 8: The Adulteress, the Light, and "Before Abraham Was, I Am"

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

John 8 sits inside the Tabernacles narrative that began in chapter 7. The two great festival ceremonies — the water-pouring rite and the lamp-lighting in the Court of Women — frame the chapter's two principal sayings. Jesus's "I am the light of the world" (8:12) almost certainly engages the *menorot* of the Mishnah's *Sukkah* 5:2-4: four enormous golden candelabra, each fifty cubits high, illuminating the temple courts so brightly that "there was not a courtyard in Jerusalem that did not reflect the light of the place of water-drawing." Andreas Köstenberger (BECNT), Brown (Anchor Bible), and Mary Coloe (*God Dwells With Us*) all anchor the saying to this liturgical context. The geography is specific: 8:20 places Jesus "in the treasury" (ἐν τῷ γαζοφυλακίῳ) — the Court of Women, where the thirteen shofar-shaped offering chests stood, and where the great menorah ceremony was conducted. Jesus is teaching at the very site of the lamp-lighting.

The pericope adulterae (7:53-8:11) places its action at "the Mount of Olives" (8:1) and "the temple" (8:2) — the geography of Jesus's last week in the Synoptic gospels (Mark 11; Luke 19:47, 21:37). The action centers on the legal procedure for adultery prescribed in Deuteronomy 22:22-24 and Leviticus 20:10. By the late Second Temple period, capital jurisdiction had been removed from Jewish courts — Roman occupation, *jus gladii* — though the question is debated (Sherwin-White's *Roman Society and Roman Law in the New Testament* argued capital cases required Roman ratification; later scholars have qualified). The Mishnah's tractate *Sanhedrin* 7:4-5 specifies stoning for adultery but already shows rabbinic discomfort with the death penalty (the famous *Sanhedrin* 1:6 — a Sanhedrin that puts a man to death once in seven years is "destructive," and Rabbi Eleazar ben Azariah extends to once in seventy years). The trap of 8:5 ("Now in the Law, Moses commanded us to stone such women — so what do you say?") thus leverages a real legal tension: Mosaic command versus Roman jurisdiction versus rabbinic restraint.

The detail that Jesus "wrote with his finger on the ground" (8:6, 8:8) has produced more speculation than perhaps any Gospel detail. Patristic readings include: writing the sins of the accusers (Jerome), writing Jeremiah 17:13 ("those who turn away from you shall be written in the earth"), writing the Decalogue, or (Augustine) imitating God writing the law on stone tablets — but the writing is on dust, not stone. Chris Keith's *The Pericope Adulterae, the Gospel of John, and the Literacy of Jesus* (2009) is the foundational modern study. Keith argues the writing is a literacy-claim — ancient readers would have noticed that an unlettered Galilean was being shown to write, asserting a teaching authority beyond what his "lack of learning" (cf. 7:15) implied. Whether the gesture demonstrates literacy or invokes prophetic / scribal symbolism (Jeremiah 17:13), the text leaves the content opaque.

Three Greek terms anchor the chapter:

**ἐγώ εἰμι** (*egō eimi*, "I am") — used in absolute form (without predicate) at 8:24, 8:28, 8:58, in addition to the predicate "I am the light of the world" at 8:12. This is the chapter's center of gravity. The absolute *egō eimi* in 8:58 ("before Abraham was, I am") echoes the Septuagint's rendering of the divine name in Exodus 3:14 (LXX: ἐγώ εἰμι ὁ ὤν, "I am the one who is") and Isaiah 43:10 (LXX: ἵνα γνῶτε καὶ πιστεύσητε καὶ συνῆτε ὅτι ἐγώ εἰμι, "that you may know and believe and understand that I am [he]"). Catrin Williams's *I Am He: The Interpretation of Anī Hû in Jewish and Early Christian Literature* is the foundational study of how the Hebrew *ani hu* ("I [am] he") of Deuteronomy 32:39 and Second Isaiah became the Septuagint's ἐγώ εἰμι and then Johannine self-disclosure. Larry Hurtado's *Lord Jesus Christ* and Richard Bauckham's *Jesus and the God of Israel* both make this passage a key data point in early high Christology.

**φῶς τοῦ κόσμου** (*phōs tou kosmou*, "light of the world") — the second of the Seven I AM Sayings with Predicates (after "bread of life," 6:35). Light is rich Old Testament divine metaphor: Psalm 27:1, Isaiah 60:19-20 (the Lord shall be everlasting light), Habakkuk 3:4, Wisdom 7:26 (Wisdom as a "reflection of eternal light"). At Qumran, the Community Rule's two-spirits passage (1QS 3:13-4:26) draws light/darkness dualism in cosmic terms — Charlesworth's *John and Qumran* makes this a central comparison.

**ἐλεύθερος** (*eleutheros*, "free") and **δοῦλος** (*doulos*, "slave") at 8:32-36 — the famous "the truth will set you free" passage. Freedom in Greco-Roman political discourse meant manumission from slavery, but Jesus reframes it: the slavery is to sin, the freedom is the Son's gift. Greco-Roman parallels (Stoic *eleutheria* as moral freedom from passion; Philo's *Quod Omnis Probus Liber Sit*) provide context.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter's most pressing literary question is whether 7:53-8:11 — the *pericope adulterae* — belongs in the Gospel of John at all. The text-critical case is overwhelming and worth laying out carefully:

The earliest and best Greek manuscripts omit the passage entirely. **𝔓⁶⁶** (around 200 CE) and **𝔓⁷⁵** (early third century, the earliest substantial witnesses to John) lack it. **Codex Sinaiticus (ℵ)** and **Codex Vaticanus (B)** — both fourth century, the great majuscules of Christian biblical text — omit it. **Codex Alexandrinus (A)** and **Codex Ephraemi Rescriptus (C)** in their fifth-century state lack it (their text in the relevant section is largely lost, but the column space measurements indicate the passage was not present). **Codex Borgianus, Codex Regius, Codex Athous Laurensis, Codex Petropolitanus** all omit. Many manuscripts that include the passage mark it with asterisks or obeli to flag its dubious status.

The early versional evidence agrees: the Old Syriac (Sinaitic Syriac, Curetonian Syriac), the Sahidic Coptic, the Bohairic Coptic, the Armenian, and the early Georgian all omit. The earliest Latin tradition (Old Latin) is divided; Jerome's Vulgate includes it, citing his manuscripts.

Several manuscripts that do include the passage place it elsewhere: family 13 (the Ferrar group of medieval Greek manuscripts) places it after Luke 21:38; some manuscripts place it after John 7:36 or after John 21:25 or at the end of Luke. The "floating" character of the passage in the textual tradition is itself strong evidence that it was not original to any specific position.

Patristic citation is informative. Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.17) reports that Papias (early second century) "expounded another story about a woman accused before the Lord of many sins, which is contained in the Gospel of the Hebrews." This may or may not refer to our pericope. The Greek Fathers — Origen, Chrysostom in his *88 Homilies on John* (notably absent from his Johannine exposition), Cyril of Alexandria — do not comment on the passage in their commentaries, suggesting their texts lacked it. Augustine's *Adulterous Marriages* (De Adulterinis Coniugiis) 2.7.6 famously suggests that some scribes removed the passage to prevent license: "this proceeding probably scandalized some of weak faith, or rather of unsound enmity to the true faith." Augustine's apologetic notwithstanding, modern textual criticism has unanimously concluded the passage was not original to John.

Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* gives the standard modern judgment: the pericope is not part of the original text of John, but the story it preserves "is by all counts an authentic incident in the life of Jesus and is preserved by virtue of its inclusion (probably late) in the canonical Gospel." Bart Ehrman in *Misquoting Jesus* and elsewhere frames the passage as a striking case study in how textual transmission reflects theological investment. Chris Keith's *The Pericope Adulterae* makes the case that the passage was inserted into John (somewhere between the late second and fourth centuries) precisely because of its Christological-literacy claims, which gained polemical traction in fourth-century debates about Jesus's divine learning.

Yet the passage is canonical for almost every Christian tradition. Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran, and most Protestant traditions include it (often with critical notes); critical scholarly editions like NA28 and UBS5 print it in double brackets to signal textual-historical doubt while preserving canonical status. The Catholic *Sancta Mater Ecclesia* and Vatican-approved Bibles include it with footnotes. Reformed traditions vary — some print with cautionary notes, others (more rarely) omit entirely.

The pericope's content has authentic Jesus-tradition feel: the legal trap, the silent gesture, the surprising verdict that sends accusers away, the personal address to the woman ("neither do I condemn you"), the call to ongoing transformation ("go, and from now on sin no more"). Whatever its textual status, the church's reception of it as a true window onto Jesus's character has been remarkably durable.

The remainder of John 8 (8:12-59) is one of the gospel's most uncomfortable extended dialogues — and one of its most theologically charged. Structurally, it operates as a series of progressively heated exchanges: light-of-the-world saying and witness debate (8:12-20), going-away saying and "I am he" (8:21-30), Abrahamic descent and freedom (8:31-47), and the climactic "before Abraham was, I am" (8:48-59). The literary movement is downward, from public teaching to confrontation to attempted lynching.

The "I am" claims build through the chapter: predicate "I am" at 8:12 (light of the world), absolute "I am [he]" at 8:24 ("unless you believe that I am he, you will die in your sins") and 8:28 ("when you have lifted up the Son of Man, then you will know that I am he"), and the climactic absolute "I am" at 8:58 ("before Abraham was, I am" — *prin Abraam genesthai egō eimi*). Brown's Anchor Bible commentary, Catrin Williams's *I Am He*, and Bauckham's *Jesus and the God of Israel* read 8:58 as the gospel's most explicit Christological self-disclosure — the moment Jesus does not merely claim to be "from God" or "sent by God" but to share the eternal divine being. The crowd's response — picking up stones (8:59) — confirms the reading: blasphemy (Leviticus 24:16) was the prescribed legal ground for stoning, and only an explicit divine-name claim warranted that response.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Patristic engagement with John 8 was foundational for Trinitarian Christology. Origen's *Commentary on John* (Book 19, fragments of Book 20) treats 8:58 extensively as evidence of the Son's eternal generation and uncreated being — language that fed directly into the fourth-century Arian controversies. Athanasius cites 8:58 against Arius. Augustine's *Tractates on John* 35-43 walk through the chapter, especially Tractate 43 on 8:58 ("before Abraham was, I am"): Augustine reads the change of tense (Abraham *came to be*, *I am*) as decisive evidence of the Son's eternal divine being against any Arian temporal subordination. Chrysostom's *88 Homilies* (homilies 53-55) emphasize the dramatic narrative tension. Cyril of Alexandria's commentary makes 8:58 a pillar of his anti-Nestorian Christology. Aquinas's *Lectura super Joannem* synthesizes the patristic reading: the present tense of "I am" against the past tense of "was" is the linguistic hinge of eternal Christology.

The chapter's most painful patristic legacy lies elsewhere. The "you are of your father the devil" passage (8:44) became one of the most weaponized texts in Christian anti-Judaism. Chrysostom's homilies *Adversus Judaeos* (Against the Jews, 386-87 CE) — eight sermons preached in Antioch warning Christians against attending synagogue — leverage John 8:44 to characterize Jews as devil's children. The medieval blood libel, the pogroms of the Crusades, the Holy Week violence in medieval Europe, the language of the Inquisition, and ultimately the rhetorical scaffolding Christian Europe provided for the Holocaust — all draw rhetorical authority from texts like John 8:44.

Adele Reinhartz's *Cast Out of the Covenant: Jews and Anti-Judaism in the Gospel of John* (2018) is the foundational contemporary work on John's "the Jews" language, and her engagement with chapter 8 is unsparing. Reinhartz argues that John 8 is the chapter where the gospel's anti-Jewish potential becomes most acute — not because the Johannine community was ethnically distant from Judaism (it almost certainly was not), but because the rhetoric of expulsion and demonization, applied at the level of inner-Jewish polemic, became unrecognizable when later Christian readers no longer saw themselves as Jews. Her *Befriending the Beloved Disciple* models a self-aware Jewish reading of the gospel that neither evades nor simply reproduces its rhetoric.

Daniel Boyarin's *Jewish Gospels* and *Border Lines* place the chapter's high Christology within the late-Second-Temple Jewish "two powers in heaven" tradition that the rabbis later anathematized. Boyarin's argument: claims like 8:58 were not yet outside the bounds of Jewish theological possibility when John was written; they became so partly in response to Christian use of them. Amy-Jill Levine's *Misunderstood Jew* and her commentary work emphasize that John's rhetoric is inner-Jewish polemic, not anti-Semitism in the modern racial sense, but stresses the text's reception history requires Christian readers to engage with care.

The Roman Catholic Church's engagement with this trajectory runs through Vatican II's *Nostra Aetate* (Declaration on the Relation of the Church to Non-Christian Religions, 1965), which declared that "what happened in [Christ's] passion cannot be charged against all the Jews, without distinction, then alive, nor against the Jews of today" and "the Jews should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God." The Pontifical Biblical Commission's 2001 document *The Jewish People and Their Sacred Scriptures in the Christian Bible* offers an extensive theological reckoning with anti-Judaism in Christian Scripture. Mary Boys's *Has God Only One Blessing? Judaism as a Source of Christian Self-Understanding* (2000) and John Pawlikowski's work develop a constructive post-Holocaust Christian theology of Jewish-Christian relations. The 1985 Vatican *Notes on the Correct Way to Present the Jews and Judaism in Preaching and Catechesis* and the 1998 *We Remember: A Reflection on the Shoah* are official magisterial responses.

The Reformation engagement runs through Luther — whose *On the Jews and Their Lies* (1543) is a horrifying late-life document deploying John 8:44 explicitly — and Calvin, whose *Commentary on John* engages the chapter more soberly but does not escape supersessionist framing. Modern Lutheran theology has explicitly repudiated Luther's anti-Jewish writings (Lutheran World Federation, 1984; ELCA, 1994).

Twentieth-century Johannine commentary on chapter 8: Brown (Anchor Bible, foundational), Schnackenburg (HthKNT), Bultmann (existential reading; 8:58 is the eschatological moment of revelation breaking into time), Barrett (CGTC, careful Greek), Beasley-Murray (WBC), Köstenberger (BECNT), Carson (PNTC), Keener (two volumes, extensive Jewish-context work), Thompson (NTL, attentive to Trinitarian theology), Moloney (Sacra Pagina), O'Day (NIB), Newbigin (*The Light Has Come*). On the *pericope adulterae* specifically: Chris Keith's monograph is foundational; Jennifer Knust and Tommy Wasserman's *To Cast the First Stone: The Transmission of a Gospel Story* (2018) is the comprehensive recent study of the passage's transmission history. Sandra Schneiders's *Written That You May Believe* offers feminist engagement.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: How do post-Holocaust Christian readers receive John 8 — especially 8:44 — responsibly?**

*Position 1: Historicize the rhetoric.* The chapter reflects late-first-century intra-Jewish polemic between the Johannine community (likely Jewish-Christian) and other Jewish groups. Calling opponents "of the devil" was harsh but not racially anti-Semitic; rabbinic and sectarian polemic of the period (Qumran's "sons of darkness" language, for example) employed similar rhetoric. The text means in 90 CE what it cannot mean in 2026. Steelman: this is historically responsible reading; it refuses to project modern racial anti-Semitism backward; it preserves the text's first-century setting. Counter-steelman: historicizing does not absolve the text's reception; the passage has been taken to mean what its first-century rhetoric did not say, and Christian readers cannot pretend that history away.

*Position 2: Acknowledge canonical complicity.* The text, as canonical Scripture, has authorized centuries of violence. Christian reading must begin with confession of the harm and a hermeneutic of suspicion toward our own readings. Reinhartz's position: read with the awareness that the gospel's rhetoric, even in inner-Jewish polemic, became destructive when extracted from that setting. Steelman: this takes the church's actual history seriously; it refuses cheap Christian identification with the gospel's voice; it makes space for Jewish readers to be heard. Counter-steelman: if the text is canonical, it must be readable for Christian formation, not only deconstructed.

*Position 3: Christological reframing.* The chapter's "father" language is Christological before it is anti-Jewish. The Johannine question is "whose child are you?" and the answer is Christological — to be Abraham's true child is to receive the one Abraham rejoiced to see (8:56). The chapter's polemic is against a specific failure of Christological recognition, not against Jewishness as such. Steelman: this preserves the chapter's Christological logic without externalizing its critique to a racial-religious other. Counter-steelman: this can become apologetic evasion — saying the text is "really" Christological does not unwrite its rhetorical effect.

*Where this leaves us:* Post-*Nostra Aetate* Christian engagement with John 8 is necessarily layered. The historical reading (intra-Jewish polemic) is true but insufficient; the canonical-complicity reading (acknowledging reception history) is necessary but cannot be terminal; the Christological reframing has internal coherence but must be held with care. The most responsible Christian reading does not preach 8:44 without 8:58 — does not denounce "the Jews" without confessing that the same gospel says salvation is *of* the Jews (cf. 4:22). The text's painful potential cannot be unwritten; it can be read with the church's repentance and the long-overdue conversation with Jewish readers in the room.

**Tension Two: What does "before Abraham was, I am" (8:58) claim, and what kind of Christology does it require?**

*Position 1: Eternal divine identity.* The change of tense (Abraham *genesthai* — came to be; Jesus *egō eimi* — I am) plus the Septuagintal divine-name resonance (Exodus 3:14, Isaiah 43:10 LXX) plus the crowd's stoning response (Leviticus 24:16) together require reading 8:58 as a claim to participate in the eternal divine being of YHWH. This is the patristic-orthodox-Reformation consensus: Athanasius, Augustine, Cyril, Aquinas, Calvin, Bavinck, Barth, Bauckham, Hurtado. Steelman: the text's grammar, scriptural background, and dramatic resolution all point this way; alternative readings have to explain why the crowd took up stones if Jesus claimed only pre-existence rather than divine identity.

*Position 2: Pre-existence, not full divine identity.* Some scholarly readings (Dunn's earlier work in *Christology in the Making*; some Unitarian-Christian readings; certain process theologians) take 8:58 as a claim to pre-existence as Wisdom or as the heavenly Son of Man, without yet asserting full divine identity in the later Nicene sense. Steelman: high Christology developed gradually in early Christian thought; reading Nicene categories back into John may anachronize. Counter-steelman: Bauckham's *Jesus and the God of Israel* and Hurtado's *Lord Jesus Christ* have largely dismantled the "evolutionary" Christology hypothesis; very high Christology is present in earliest Christian sources (Phil 2:6-11 predates John).

*Position 3: Mystical participatory reading.* From Meister Eckhart through John of the Cross through some twentieth-century mystical theology, 8:58 has been read as Christ revealing not only his own eternity but the timeless reality of divine presence in which all souls participate. This reading risks dissolving the Christological particular into a generic mysticism; mainstream theology has resisted it.

*Where this leaves us:* The patristic-Reformation consensus on 8:58 is strong, and recent high-Christology scholarship has reinforced rather than weakened it. The text is the gospel's most explicit divine-identity Christology; everything in chapters 1, 5, 6, 10, and 14 funnels through this moment. What it requires is not metaphysical speculation in the abstract but recognition that the gospel's narrative claim — that the Word who was in the beginning with God (1:1) is now standing in the temple courts at Tabernacles — has reached its point of maximum clarity. The crowd's stones are the proper response if the claim is false; they are the canonical-historical confirmation that the claim was understood.

**One unresolved question:** What do we do with the *pericope adulterae*? Modern textual criticism is unanimous that it is not original to John. Many traditions still read it as Scripture. The story has shaped Christian moral imagination — the Pope's funeral readings, the Salvation Army's founding inspiration, countless homilies — for fifteen centuries. Should the church's lived reception override the text-critical verdict? Should the verdict override the reception? Most contemporary scholars answer "include with critical notes" — but this is a settlement, not a resolution. The question of how text-critical scholarship and canonical-ecclesial reception relate remains genuinely open, and the *pericope adulterae* is its most prominent specimen.

## The Thread

The "I am the light of the world" of 8:12 reaches back to the prologue's "in him was life, and the life was the light of men" (1:4) and forward to the man born blind in chapter 9, where Jesus repeats "while I am in the world, I am the light of the world" (9:5) and the discourse becomes the dramatic enactment of light coming and darkness retreating. The "before Abraham was, I am" of 8:58 is the climactic absolute *egō eimi* whose lower-key precursors run through 4:26 (to the Samaritan woman), 6:20 (on the water), 8:24 and 8:28; it will recur at 13:19 and reach its narrative climax at 18:5-6, when the arresting party falls to the ground at Jesus's "I am [he]" — the gospel's sustained construction of divine self-disclosure. The "if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed" (8:36) anticipates the Farewell Discourse's promise that the Spirit will guide the disciples into all truth (16:13). And the stones picked up at 8:59 — never thrown — anticipate the crucifixion that will be the actual "lifting up of the Son of Man" (8:28) by which the world will know who he is.
