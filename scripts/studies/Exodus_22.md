# Exodus 22: The Widow, the Orphan, the Stranger

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The material world behind Exodus 22 is Late Bronze to early Iron Age agrarian life — mixed pastoralism and cereal farming on the hill country of the southern Levant, with cultural memory reaching back into the Ramesside thirteenth century. Kenneth Kitchen places the Sinai and covenant traditions firmly within the second-millennium ANE legal world, noting that the case-law format of Exodus 21–23 (the so-called Covenant Code, *sēper habbĕrît*) shares its deep grammar with the Code of Hammurabi, the Laws of Eshnunna, the Middle Assyrian Laws, and the Hittite Laws. James Hoffmeier, working from the archaeology of the eastern Delta and northern Sinai, has argued that Israelite memory of Egypt retains specific Ramesside-era realia — the very texture of Ex 22:21 ("you were sojourners in the land of Egypt") presumes a community that remembers itself as *gērîm* in someone else's territory.

The material culture of the chapter is relentlessly practical. A man's ox wanders into another man's field. A fire lit for stubble-burning leaps to the neighbor's grain stacks. A cloak is taken in pledge against a loan. A donkey left with a neighbor for safekeeping dies "without anyone seeing it." These are the daily hazards of an agrarian economy where a family's entire capital might be three animals, a field, and the clothes on their backs. Carol Meyers' archaeological work on Iron Age Israelite households underlines how thin the margins were — a lost ox was not an inconvenience but a catastrophe.

The property-law material (vv. 1–15) sits almost verbatim alongside Hammurabi §§8, 120–126. Hammurabi §8 penalizes the theft of ox, sheep, donkey, pig, or boat at thirtyfold restitution if the owner is palace or temple, tenfold if a private citizen; Exodus prescribes fivefold for an ox and fourfold for a sheep (v. 1). The deposit laws (vv. 7–13) match Hammurabi §§120–126 so closely — down to the scenario of the depositor coming "before the god" to swear an oath (v. 8, Hebrew *hāʾĕlōhîm*, which LXX and the rabbis read either as God or as judges) — that Shalom Paul's landmark 1970 study *Studies in the Book of the Covenant in the Light of Cuneiform and Biblical Law* treats them as drawn from the same legal stream. Nahum Sarna in the JPS Exodus volume argues the Israelite version is distinctively theologized: the accused is brought not before human magistrates but before God himself.

The seduction law (vv. 16–17) addresses *mōhar*, the bride-price, attested at Nuzi, Mari, and in Middle Assyrian marriage contracts. The sorcery provision (v. 18) echoes Middle Assyrian Law A §47 and a long tradition of ANE magical prohibition; Egyptian magical papyri (the Pyramid Texts, the Book of the Dead, the execration texts) and the Hittite sorcery laws show that *mĕkaššēp/mĕkaššēpâ* were feared and regulated across cultures. Bestiality (v. 19) is condemned in Hittite Law §§187–200, though with startling distinctions the Israelite code refuses (Hittite law permits intercourse with horses and mules). Maximum fidelity to YHWH alone (v. 20) against ḥērem appears against a backdrop of pluralistic ANE cult — the Ramesside era was one of active Canaanite, Egyptian, and Mesopotamian religious exchange.

The widow-orphan-sojourner triad (vv. 21–24) is not uniquely Israelite — Hammurabi's prologue and epilogue boast of protection for the widow and orphan, and Ugaritic texts (the Aqhat and Keret legends) treat care of the vulnerable as the mark of the just king. What is distinctive is that Israel's God binds this ethic not to royal virtue but to collective memory: *you* were sojourners. The move from king-as-protector to people-as-former-victim reframes the ethic entirely.

On authorship, the critical consensus following Albrecht Alt's 1934 essay on the origins of Israelite law, developed by Albright, Cross, Mendenhall, Weinfeld, and Propp, holds the Covenant Code to be the oldest Israelite law collection — pre-monarchic or early monarchic. Cassuto argued for still greater antiquity and for substantial Mosaic authenticity. Dozeman sees the Book of the Covenant as a pre-priestly document later incorporated into the Sinai pericope.

Archaeological touchpoints: the Ketef Hinnom silver scrolls (seventh-century, preserving a priestly blessing close to Num 6:24–26) demonstrate that biblical legal-cultic formulas were in circulation pre-exile; the proto-Sinaitic inscriptions at Serabit el-Khadim show Semitic scribal activity in Sinai in the Late Bronze; Tel Arad's sanctuary gives a picture of Israelite cult before Josianic centralization.

Key Hebrew terms:

- *gēr* (גֵּר) — sojourner, resident alien; 92 OT occurrences, 36 in legal contexts
- *ʾalmānâ* (אַלְמָנָה) — widow; invariably paired with orphan (*yātôm*)
- *mōhar* (מֹהַר) — bride-price
- *mĕkaššēpâ* (מְכַשֵּׁפָה) — sorceress (feminine singular); masculine *mĕkaššēp* used in Deut 18:10
- *ḥannûn* (חַנּוּן) — compassionate; one of the 13 attributes of YHWH (Ex 34:6)

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is casuistic law — the conditional "kî/ʾim + imperfect" construction ("if a man…") — interleaved with apodictic commands ("you shall not…"). Alt's distinction between these two forms still structures scholarly reading. Casuistic law is the ANE lingua franca, shared with Mesopotamian collections; apodictic law is arguably the more distinctively Israelite form, and it dominates the chapter's second half. The editor has arranged the material so that the casuistic property laws yield, at verse 18, to a block of apodictic prohibitions that escalate from sorcery through bestiality through idolatry to social protection.

Jacob Milgrom argued that the apparent miscellany of vv. 16–31 coheres around the category of *consecration* — things and persons set apart for YHWH, from the firstborn (vv. 29–30) to the people themselves ("you shall be consecrated to me," v. 31). Robert Alter's translation notes treat the chapter as an anthology that nonetheless reveals an editor's mind: the triple repetition of concern for vulnerable classes (sojourner, widow, orphan, poor) functions as a structural refrain.

The literary device that most repays attention is the rhetoric of divine hearing. In verses 23 and 27, YHWH twice says "if they cry out to me, I will surely hear *šāmōaʿ ʾešmāʿ*." The Hebrew infinitive-absolute construction intensifies the verb — *I will really, absolutely, without fail hear*. In verse 27 it is even more personal: "for I am compassionate *ḥannûn*." Brueggemann has pointed out that this moves the law from third-person jurisprudence into first-person divine self-disclosure. The legislator becomes the avenger; YHWH's own character is the enforcement mechanism.

The cloak-in-pledge passage (vv. 26–27) is a small masterpiece of pastoral compression. In two verses the text moves from legal stipulation ("if ever you take your neighbor's cloak in pledge") to physical detail ("that is his only covering, and it is his cloak for his body") to rhetorical question ("in what else shall he sleep?") to divine pathos ("if he cries to me, I will hear, for I am compassionate"). No other ANE legal corpus moves through these registers in so few words.

Textual variants: the LXX numbering differs — Hebrew 22:1 appears as 21:37, shifting the whole chapter forward by one verse. The Samaritan Pentateuch harmonizes toward Deuteronomy 24. The Qumran 4QpaleoExodm preserves fragments of the Covenant Code with orthographic variants but no substantive divergences.

A structural observation from Cornelis Houtman (HCOT): the chapter is held together by the movement from the particular to the universal. It begins with specific animals (ox, sheep, donkey) and ends with consecration ("you shall be consecrated to me"). The agrarian particularity of the first verses becomes the covenantal identity of the last.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Canonically, Exodus 22 stands at the heart of the Book of the Covenant (Ex 20:22–23:33), the legal material that immediately follows the Decalogue and precedes the covenant ratification of Ex 24. It is the first substantial body of law Israel receives as a people — the constitution, as it were, of the newly freed slaves. The chapter's repeated appeal to the Egyptian experience ("you were sojourners") establishes a pattern the Pentateuch will echo in Leviticus 19, Deuteronomy 10, Deuteronomy 24. Care for the vulnerable is not supererogatory; it is the defining ethical consequence of the exodus itself.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory**:
- Second Temple: Philo devoted much of *Special Laws* 2–4 to exposition of the Covenant Code, reading the widow-orphan-sojourner laws as foundational to what he called the "fifth commandment" writ large (honor of parents extending to honor of the vulnerable). Josephus (*Antiquities* 4.266–290) paraphrases and generalizes the laws for a Greco-Roman audience, softening the sorcery and bestiality prohibitions. Jubilees 7:20 internalizes care for the poor into Noah's testament.
- Rabbinic: The Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael on Mishpatim is the primary rabbinic commentary and treats vv. 1–15 as the foundation of *dinei mamonot* (monetary law). The Bavli tractates Bava Kamma (on damages) and Bava Metzia (on bailments and loans) derive from these verses. The principle of paying fourfold or fivefold (v. 1) becomes a major topic at Bava Kamma 62b–79b, where the rabbis reason that fivefold restitution on an ox (a work animal whose labor was lost) versus fourfold on a sheep (where the thief "merely" had to carry it) shows the law's concern for the full economic loss. On v. 27 ("I am compassionate"), the Mekhilta cites this as one of the scriptural warrants for the doctrine of *middot* (divine attributes) elaborated at Ex 34:6. Maimonides' *Sefer HaMitzvot* locates numerous of the 613 commandments in this chapter: positive commands #197 (return pledged garment), #248 (lend to the poor); negative commands #234 (not to oppress the stranger), #256 (not to charge interest). His *Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Malveh v'Loveh* develops the interest prohibition into a full code of lending ethics.
- Medieval: Rashi emphasizes the plain sense and the halakhic derivation. Ibn Ezra attends to philology; on v. 28 ("you shall not revile God") he notes the ambiguity of *ʾĕlōhîm* — God, or judges — and sides with "judges." Ramban (Nachmanides) argues that the cloak-in-pledge law is not merely legal but devotional: YHWH identifies with the poor man's cry in a way that makes injustice a theological offense, not merely a social one.
- Modern: Nahum Sarna's JPS Exodus commentary is the standard reference; Jonathan Sacks preached and wrote extensively on the sojourner command as Judaism's "most reiterated commandment — 36 times, more than any other." Moshe Weinfeld's *Social Justice in Ancient Israel and in the Ancient Near East* (1995) locates the chapter in the ANE tradition of royal *mīšārum* proclamations but argues that Israel's law democratizes what had been royal prerogative.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory**:
- Patristic: Origen's *Homilies on Exodus* (especially 9–13) read the Covenant Code allegorically but not at the expense of its ethical force; the widow-orphan command for Origen is at once literal and figural. Augustine in *Quaestiones in Heptateuchum* on Exodus 22 works through the property laws and treats the sorcery prohibition as binding on Christians (a reading that would resonate devastatingly in later centuries). Chrysostom's homilies on 2 Corinthians and Acts repeatedly cite Ex 22:25 against usury. Ambrose's *De Tobia* is essentially an extended meditation on the interest prohibition.
- Medieval: Aquinas in *ST* II-II q. 78 ("On the sin of usury") grounds the medieval Catholic prohibition of interest in Ex 22:25 and Deut 23:19–20. The canonical prohibition lasted into the sixteenth century.
- Reformation: Calvin in his *Commentary on the Last Four Books of Moses* treats the Covenant Code as the specification of the moral law (particularly of the second table of the Decalogue). His reading of v. 18 ("you shall not permit a sorceress to live") defended literal application in civil jurisprudence — a position that shaped Geneva and, through later Puritanism, the Salem witch trials of 1692. Luther, in his 1525 *How Christians Should Regard Moses*, argued the Covenant Code was Israel's civil law, binding only insofar as it expressed natural law; he explicitly denied its direct applicability to Christian polities.
- Critical: Wellhausen treated the Covenant Code as the earliest law stratum (E source), predating the Deuteronomic and Priestly reforms. Martin Noth's *Exodus* commentary (OTL) presumes this stratigraphy. Propp's two-volume Anchor Bible *Exodus* (1999, 2006) provides the most exhaustive recent philological treatment. Dozeman (*Exodus*, Eerdmans Critical Commentary 2009) reads the code as a pre-priestly document integrated into the Sinai pericope through redaction.
- Contemporary: Walter Brueggemann in *Theology of the Old Testament* treats the sojourner command as the paradigm case of the OT's "subversive" ethical imagination. Christopher J.H. Wright's *Old Testament Ethics for the People of God* uses Ex 22 as a central case for what he calls the "paradigmatic" approach to OT ethics — not literal transfer, but analogical application. Terence Fretheim (*Exodus*, Interpretation) emphasizes YHWH's pathos in verses 22–27 as central to biblical theology. N.T. Wright cites the Egyptian-memory command as foundational to early Christian ethics toward outsiders.

**Where they converge and diverge**: Jewish tradition reads the chapter as enforceable, detailed civil and criminal law — the Mekhilta, the Bavli, Maimonides' code, and contemporary responsa literature all continue to derive *halakhah* from these verses. The cloak-return rule, the interest prohibition, and the widow-orphan protections remain practically operative in observant communities. Christian tradition has been more hermeneutically plural: Calvin's view treated the code as civil law for Christian magistrates (with catastrophic consequences in witch-hunting), Luther's and later Reformed covenant theology distinguished moral/civil/ceremonial and let the civil lapse, and Catholic moral theology (Aquinas) preserved the interest prohibition for over a millennium before abandoning it. Contemporary Christian ethics, especially Brueggemann, Wright, and Fretheim, has returned to the sojourner command as hermeneutically privileged — reading Ex 22:21 into immigration debate, refugee ethics, and the theology of welcome. Jewish and Christian interpretation converge strikingly on the cloak-in-pledge passage: both traditions treat it as the model instance of law that shades into pastoral theology.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is the sorcery death-penalty of v. 18 a binding ethical norm or a culture-bound provision?**

- Catholic: The *Catechism* (CCC 2117) treats all sorcery/magic as gravely sinful, but the death penalty provision is read as belonging to the civil law of ancient Israel, not the universal moral law (CCC 1965–1974 on the relation between old and new law; Aquinas *ST* I-II q. 102 on ceremonial and judicial precepts). Contemporary Catholic teaching rejects capital punishment almost entirely (CCC 2267 as revised by Francis).
- Reformed (Calvin): The verse bound Christian magistrates to suppress sorcery with lethal force. Calvin's Geneva executed witches on this warrant; his *Commentary on the Last Four Books* is explicit. Later Reformed theology (Westminster Confession 19.4) distinguishes moral and judicial law and treats the civil provisions as not binding "further than the general equity thereof may require" — leaving room for literal application by those who judged the equity continued.
- Lutheran: Luther read the Covenant Code as Israel's *Landrecht* (civil law), binding on Israel alone. His 1525 treatise *How Christians Should Regard Moses* argued that Christians owe obedience only to laws confirmed by the New Testament or natural law; the sorcery execution is neither.
- Orthodox: Church canons (Trullo 61, 65) prescribe penance, not execution, for sorcery. Orthodox interpretation treats Ex 22:18 as pedagogical — marking the seriousness of the offense — without transferring its civil sanction.
- Jewish (Maimonides): *Hilchot Avodah Zarah* 11:15 treats actual sorcery (distinguished from illusion) as a capital offense in a court with full Sanhedrin jurisdiction, but Maimonides adds that such courts have not existed since the destruction of the Temple and the death penalty is therefore unavailable.
- Critical: The provision reflects the ANE horror of hostile magic (Hittite Law §§44, 111; Middle Assyrian Law A §47) and should be read historically, not prescriptively.

Steelmanning the least intuitive position — Calvin's: the argument is that if God's law identifies sorcery as a capital offense, a magistrate who fails to punish it fails in his covenantal office. Calvin was not a proto-Enlightenment liberal; his premise was that civil authority is theologically accountable. The premise collapsed when post-Enlightenment societies rejected the theocratic framing, but within that framing, Calvin's reading is internally consistent.

**Where this leaves us:** Almost no contemporary Christian or Jewish tradition applies v. 18 literally. The near-universal judgment is that the culture-bound nature of the civil penalty is the right reading, and that the historical application of the verse — from medieval witch trials to Salem — is one of the gravest interpretive failures in the church's record.

**Tension 2: Does the interest prohibition of v. 25 forbid modern commercial lending?**

- Catholic: From Lateran II (1139) through the Council of Vienne (1311) through Aquinas (*ST* II-II q. 78), usury — understood as charging interest on a loan — was formally prohibited. The position shifted gradually through the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries as distinctions between productive capital (investment) and consumption loans hardened; Pius VIII in 1830 finally permitted Catholics to charge moderate interest. The *Catechism* no longer teaches the old prohibition.
- Reformed (Calvin): Calvin's 1545 letter to Claude de Sachin is the turning point. He argued that Ex 22:25 protects the poor, not commerce; that loans to the rich for productive purposes are of a different category; and that forbidding all interest would make modern economic life impossible. His distinction — no interest on loans to the poor, moderate interest on commercial loans — became mainstream Protestant teaching.
- Lutheran: Luther in his 1524 and 1540 sermons on usury held a stricter line than Calvin; he regarded commercial interest with deep suspicion to the end. Contemporary Lutheran ethics has largely followed Calvin.
- Wesleyan: Wesley's *Sermon 50* (*The Use of Money*) preserves the pastoral concern — "gain all you can, save all you can, give all you can" — but permits commercial interest as a matter of stewardship.
- Jewish (Maimonides): *Hilchot Malveh v'Loveh* 5:1 prohibits interest between Jews (*ribbit*) but permits it with non-Jews. The *heter iska* legal device (from the sixteenth century onward) recasts an interest-bearing loan as a joint venture, preserving the letter of the law. Modern Orthodox economies have operated with this framework for five centuries.
- Critical: The provision is a specifically agrarian, kin-based protection of the poor within a small-scale subsistence economy; it is neither a general monetary principle nor a blanket capitalism-critique.

Steelmanning the least intuitive — the medieval Catholic position: Aquinas argued from Aristotle that money is sterile (it does not naturally reproduce), so charging interest is charging for nothing — a kind of theft. The argument has been revived by contemporary Islamic finance and by theological economists who see compound interest at scale as structurally extractive. The medieval synthesis was not naive; it identified something about money-as-commodity that the modern consensus has not fully answered.

**Where this leaves us:** Every major tradition has moved away from a strict literal application of v. 25 to commercial loans, but every major tradition has preserved its pastoral core — loans to the poor are a different moral category, and the exploitation of the vulnerable through predatory lending remains under biblical indictment. The payday-loan industry is the case where the tension becomes unavoidable.

**An unresolved question**: Ex 22:20 prescribes *ḥērem* (devotion to destruction) for anyone who sacrifices to another god. Exodus 34, Deuteronomy 7, and the conquest narratives extend this logic. How does the canonical portrait of YHWH as "compassionate" (v. 27) — introduced in the very same chapter — relate to the portrait of YHWH as the God who commands *ḥērem*? The question is not resolved by any major tradition; it is the knot at which modern readers most often break from the ancient text.

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references:

- The cloak-in-pledge rule (vv. 26–27) reappears at Amos 2:8 — "they lay themselves down beside every altar on garments taken in pledge" — where Amos accuses Israel of violating precisely this command while performing cultic worship. The prophetic critique presumes the Covenant Code's authority centuries later. It reappears again in a 7th-c. BCE Hebrew ostracon from Meṣad Ḥashavyahu, where a laborer petitions for the return of his garment taken in pledge — real-world operation of Ex 22:26.
- The formula "I will surely hear" (*šāmōaʿ ʾešmāʿ*, vv. 23, 27) echoes the beginning of the exodus in Ex 3:7 — "I have surely seen the affliction of my people… and I have heard their cry." The same divine hearing that initiated the exodus now binds Israel's conduct toward others. The legal code is, structurally, the exodus in reverse: God heard your cry, now don't make others cry.
- Jesus' command in Matt 5:42 ("give to the one who begs from you, and do not refuse the one who would borrow from you") and the parable of the widow in Luke 18:1–8 ("will not God give justice to his elect who cry to him day and night?") are both, among other things, commentaries on Ex 22:22–27. The widow's cry to the unjust judge is the cry Ex 22:23 promised YHWH would hear.
