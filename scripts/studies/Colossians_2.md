# Colossians 2: The Cancelled Bond and the Paraded Powers

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The letter is addressed to a small Christian community in the Lycus Valley of western Phrygia, a tributary watershed feeding the Maeander River about a hundred miles inland from Ephesus. Colossae itself sat between the wealthier wool-finishing cities of Laodicea and Hierapolis, on a Roman road that connected the Aegean coast with the Anatolian plateau. By the late 50s or early 60s AD, the era in which most defenders of Pauline authorship place the letter, the valley had been thoroughly Hellenized for three centuries and Romanized for one, but its religious substrate remained densely Phrygian. The mother goddess Cybele (Magna Mater) still drew worshipers, the moon-god Mēn Karou had a sanctuary at nearby Attouda, and the mystery cult of Sabazios — a Phrygian deity assimilated to Dionysus — was active enough through the wider region that Cicero's contemporaries knew the rites by name. Stephen Mitchell's *Anatolia* (1993) and the older work of Carl Roebuck on Phrygian religious inscriptions establish that the Lycus Valley was a place where indigenous mystery religion, diaspora Judaism (Antiochus III had transplanted thousands of Jewish families to Phrygia in the late third century BC, per Josephus, *Antiquities* 12.147–53), and Greco-Roman civic cult coexisted and bled into one another.

The interpretive crux of the entire chapter is the identity of the "philosophy and empty deceit" (φιλοσοφία καὶ κενῆς ἀπάτης, *philosophia kai kenēs apatēs*) of 2:8. Five candidate reconstructions have dominated the scholarly literature.

- **Mystery cult / pagan syncretism.** Martin Dibelius's classic 1917 essay *Die Isisweihe bei Apuleius und verwandte Initiationsriten* read 2:18's *embateuō* (ἐμβατεύω, "entering in") as technical vocabulary borrowed from the Apollo-of-Klaros mystery cult inscriptions, where initiates were said to "enter" the inner sanctuary. On this reading the Colossian "philosophy" is a Phrygian mystery sect promising visionary experience and angelic encounter through ritual entry. Ralph Martin's commentaries elaborated the position.

- **Jewish-Hellenistic syncretism.** J. B. Lightfoot's 1875 commentary, still the most influential English-language treatment, read the false teachers as a proto-Gnostic offshoot of Essenism — a Jewish sect that had absorbed cosmological speculation about angelic intermediaries and ritualized purity. Sabbath, food laws, and circumcision (2:11, 16, 21) carry obvious Jewish weight. Günther Bornkamm in his 1948 essay *Die Häresie des Kolosserbriefes* and Eduard Lohse's Hermeneia commentary (German 1968, English 1971) refined this into a Jewish-Hellenistic synthesis that took mystery-cult language onto a Jewish frame.

- **Middle Platonic / Pythagorean.** Richard DeMaris (*The Colossian Controversy*, 1994) and Eduard Schweizer argued the *stoicheia tou kosmou* (στοιχεῖα τοῦ κόσμου, 2:8, 20) reflect Middle Platonic and Pythagorean cosmological speculation about the elemental constituents of the cosmos and the soul's ascent through them. The "philosophy" on this view is principally a Greek philosophical tradition with secondary Jewish and mystery overtones.

- **No coherent heresy.** Morna Hooker's 1973 essay "Were There False Teachers in Colossae?" argued that the language of 2:8–23 is preemptive rather than refutational. Paul writes against the *pull* of the Phrygian religious environment on Gentile converts, not against an organized rival teaching. The chapter then becomes pastoral inoculation rather than polemic.

- **Apocalyptic Jewish mysticism.** Fred Francis (1962) and more recently Loren Stuckenbruck (*Angel Veneration and Christology*, 1995) read 2:18's *thrēskeia tōn angelōn* (worship of angels) as a subjective rather than objective genitive — not worship *directed at* angels but human participation *with* angels in heavenly liturgy, a feature of merkabah-style Jewish mysticism documented in 1 Enoch, the Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice, and later Hekhalot literature.

Each reading anchors itself in a different fragment of the chapter's vocabulary. The material culture of the polemic is largely ascetic: 2:21's "do not handle, do not taste, do not touch" (μὴ ἅψῃ μηδὲ γεύσῃ μηδὲ θίγῃς) belongs equally to the Greco-Roman philosophical traditions that prized *enkrateia* (self-mastery) and to Jewish purity boundaries that policed contact with corpses, menstruants, and unclean foods. The Pauline authorship debate, treated more fully on Colossians 1, applies here too: those who doubt direct authorship locate the letter in the 70s or 80s as a Pauline-school product addressing a more developed syncretism. The internal evidence of chapter 2 — its specific Phrygian-mystery diction — fits either dating reasonably well.

Five Greek terms carry the weight of the chapter. *Stoicheia tou kosmou* (στοιχεῖα τοῦ κόσμου, 2:8, 20) means literally "the elements of the cosmos" and ranges in ancient usage from the four basic physical elements (earth, water, fire, air) through the alphabetic letters as building blocks to, by the Hellenistic period, personalized cosmic powers or stars governing human fate. *Cheirographon* (χειρόγραφον, 2:14) is a precise Greco-Roman legal-commercial term for a handwritten certificate of debt — papyrological parallels from Egyptian archives show debtors signing IOUs in their own hand, which the creditor held until repayment. *Embateuō* (ἐμβατεύω, 2:18) means "to enter in" or "to set foot in," and the Klaros inscriptions show it as terminus technicus for mystery initiation. *Apekdyomai* (ἀπεκδύομαι, 2:15) means "to strip off completely" — Paul uses it for both the believer's circumcision-as-removal-of-flesh in 2:11 and the Father's stripping of the powers in 2:15, a verbal echo binding the two scenes. *Plērōma* (πλήρωμα, 2:9) and the verbal *peplērōmenoi* (πεπληρωμένοι, 2:10) cover "the whole fullness" of deity dwelling in Christ bodily and the believer's having been filled in him — the same root activated in the same paragraph to bind Christology to participation.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre of Colossians 2 is polemical paraenesis: moral-pastoral exhortation deployed as a defensive perimeter against teaching the author opposes. It is distinct in tone from the doxological Christ-hymn of chapter 1 (1:15–20), which is celebratory and confessional, and from the household paraenesis of chapter 3, which is regulative for ordinary domestic life. The polemic is structured around a thesis statement at 2:6–7 ("as you received Christ Jesus the Lord, so walk in him"), a sharp warning at 2:8 ("see to it that no one takes you captive"), a Christological grounding at 2:9–15 (fullness, circumcision, baptism, cancelled bond, defeated powers), and a series of practical applications at 2:16–23 governed by diatribe-style imperatives: "let no one pass judgment on you... let no one disqualify you... why do you submit to regulations?"

Two literary set pieces deserve closer attention. The first is the imagery of the Roman triumph in 2:15: "He disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him" (ἐθριάμβευσεν αὐτοὺς ἐν αὐτῷ). The Roman *triumphus* was a public ritual of imperial humiliation — a victorious general processed through Rome with his captured enemy commanders chained behind his chariot, displayed to the city as proof of conquest, and then ritually executed. Paul takes the most powerful visual rhetoric of the imperial cult and inverts it. On the cross Christ is the one who appears to be paraded by Rome — beaten, displayed, crucified at a public crossroads. Paul says the visual was a feint: it was the cosmic powers, not Christ, who were stripped and exposed. The crucifixion is rewritten as a triumph in which the *triumphator* is the apparent victim. Brian Walsh and Sylvia Keesmaat (*Colossians Remixed*, 2004) press this anti-imperial dimension; N. T. Wright's commentary makes the same point more cautiously.

The second is the chiastic linkage of two covenant signs at 2:11–12. Circumcision and baptism are placed in parallel as paired removals of flesh and burials with Christ. The literal flesh-cutting of the Abrahamic sign is replaced by participation in the death-resurrection of Christ, with baptism functioning as the circumcision of the new covenant. This rhetorical pairing — circumcision *and* baptism — does not appear elsewhere in Paul with anything close to the same density, and as we will see in Lens 3 it has carried enormous downstream weight in baptismal theology.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the New Testament canon, Colossians 2 sits at the intersection of several major Pauline currents. The use of *stoicheia tou kosmou* in 2:8 and 2:20 finds its only other Pauline appearance at Galatians 4:3, 9, where Paul uses the term to describe the slavery of both pagan idolatry and Torah-as-cosmic-mediator, indicating he had a settled vocabulary for the cosmic-elemental subjugation that Christ had broken. The death-burial-resurrection logic of 2:11–12 directly mirrors Romans 6:1–11. The "cancelled the law" theme of 2:14 has its closest parallel at Ephesians 2:14–16, where the author speaks of "abolishing the law of commandments expressed in ordinances" — most scholars read these letters as products of the same author or circle. The food regulations that 2:16, 21 oppose belong to the same pastoral conversation as 1 Corinthians 8 and Romans 14.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

The patristic tradition seized on 2:14, the *cheirographon* nailed to the cross, as a foundational text for the Christian self-understanding over against Israel. Tertullian in *Against Marcion* (Book 5) and Origen on Romans both read the cancelled bond as the abrogation of the ceremonial obligations of the Mosaic law. Daniel Boyarin (*Border Lines*, 2004) and Andrew Jacobs have documented how this verse functioned in the second through fourth centuries as a textual lever for the gradual separation of Christian identity from Jewish identity. Chrysostom, with characteristic pastoral instinct, modified the trajectory: the bond cancelled was the bond of *our sins*, not the law as such — an early Greek attempt to preserve continuity with Israel's scripture while affirming the cross's juridical effect.

The medieval synthesis is largely the work of Aquinas. In the *Summa Theologiae* Ia-IIae qq. 99–105 he distinguishes the moral, ceremonial, and judicial dimensions of the Old Covenant law and argues that only the ceremonial precepts were nailed to the cross; the moral law abides as expression of natural reason, and the judicial law of Israel ceased to bind only as positive law for one nation. This three-fold distinction migrated into Reformed orthodoxy through Calvin and the Westminster Confession (chapter 19) and remains the dominant Protestant reading.

The Reformers leaned on 2:13–14 more heavily than perhaps any other passage outside Romans. Luther preached the *cheirographon* as the foundational image of justification — the indictment cancelled, the sinner declared free at the bar. Calvin's *Institutes* (II.7) follows the same line but with more care for the law's continuing third use as a guide for the regenerate. The Anabaptist reading takes a different route: Menno Simons and Balthasar Hubmaier read 2:11–12 as decisive against infant baptism. The text, they argued, requires conscious participation — the believer is *buried with him* and *raised with him through faith*, language they took to demand cognitive faith rather than passive incorporation.

The Catholic-versus-Protestant disagreement on 2:11–12 became one of the principal battle lines of baptismal theology. Catholic sacramental theology reads the circumcision-baptism parallel as warranting the analogical extension of infant baptism: as circumcision incorporated infants into the Abrahamic covenant, so baptism incorporates them into the new. Reformed paedobaptists (Calvin, John Murray's *Christian Baptism*) follow the same covenantal logic. Anabaptists and modern Baptists insist the "with him" of 2:12 implies adult cognition. The verses have been read both ways for five centuries, and the disagreement is largely structural rather than exegetical: each tradition reads the parallel through its prior commitment to a covenantal or a confessional ecclesiology.

The critical-historical period reframed the *stoicheia*. Rudolf Bultmann, in his program of demythologization, treated the cosmic powers of 2:8, 15, 20 as mythological idiom: the existential meaning was estrangement from authentic being, and the powers themselves were a first-century symbolic carapace requiring translation. J. Louis Martyn's apocalyptic Paul, developed in *Galatians* (1997) and *Theological Issues in the Letters of Paul* (1997), reads the powers as personal spiritual agencies opposed to God whose grip is broken precisely by the cross. Walter Wink's three-volume *Powers* trilogy (especially *Naming the Powers*, 1984) takes a middle path: the powers are real spiritual realities, but they are inseparable from the human institutions, ideologies, and structures through which they operate. N. T. Wright develops a similar position. Brian Walsh and Sylvia Keesmaat read 2:15 as anti-imperial commentary, and postcolonial readers have seen in 2:14–15 a hermeneutical model for resistance to colonial juridical orders.

### Jewish Scholarly Engagement

The cancellation reading of 2:14 has been a flashpoint in Jewish-Christian dialogue since the second century. Justin Martyr's *Dialogue with Trypho* (mid-second century) shows Trypho recognizing the supersessionist implication of texts like this and resisting it; the trajectory of Christian use of the verse from Tertullian forward has confirmed Trypho's worry. Modern Jewish scholarship has worked to recover Paul as a Jew within Judaism. Daniel Boyarin (*A Radical Jew*, 1994; *The Jewish Gospels*, 2012), Paula Fredriksen (*Paul: The Pagans' Apostle*, 2017), Mark Nanos (*The Mystery of Romans*, 1996), Pamela Eisenbaum (*Paul Was Not a Christian*, 2009), and Stanley Stowers have all contributed to what has come to be called the "Paul within Judaism" school.

On Colossians 2 specifically, Nanos and his colleagues argue that the polemic is directed not against Torah-observant Jewish life but against Gentile attempts to take on a syncretized pagan-Jewish religious order in a Phrygian context. The "handwriting" cancelled is not the Torah but a record of Gentile sins under the *stoicheia* — under pagan religious orders that had a juridical claim on Gentiles before Christ. Andrew Bandstra's older study *The Law and the Elements of the World* (1964) and Roy Yates's articles arguing the *cheirographon* is the bond of indictment rather than the legal code itself have been absorbed into this newer reading. Where contemporary scholarship has shifted is here: it is now less common to read 2:14 as straight law-abrogation, more common to read it as cancellation of an indictment, with the Torah-question itself a separate matter not directly settled by the verse.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The theological claim of the chapter is that, through the cross, God has stripped the cosmic powers of their juridical claim on humanity, made the believer a participant in Christ's death and resurrection, and thereby exposed the regulations of "the philosophy" as having "no value in stopping the indulgence of the flesh" (οὐκ ἐν τιμῇ τινι πρὸς πλησμονὴν τῆς σαρκός, 2:23). Two tensions structure the historical reception.

### Tension 1: What was nailed to the cross? (2:14)

Five reading altitudes have contended for the *cheirographon*.

- **The Lutheran-Reformed classical reading** treats the cancelled bond as the moral and ceremonial demands of the law in their condemning function. The law's accusing voice is silenced at the cross, and justification by faith depends on this silencing. Luther's *Galatians* lectures and the *Heidelberg Catechism* treat the verse this way.
- **The patristic-Catholic reading**, articulated already in Chrysostom and developed by the medievals, treats the cancelled bond as the indictment listing our specific trespasses, not the law as such. The moral substance of the law abides; what is nailed to the cross is the personal record of debt against each sinner.
- **Aquinas's three-fold reading** distinguishes within the Old Covenant law itself: ceremonial law abrogated, judicial law of Israel ceased to bind as positive law, moral law continues to oblige. This is the most influential medieval mediation and shapes Reformed orthodoxy through the Westminster tradition.
- **The Paul-within-Judaism reading** (Nanos, Eisenbaum, Stowers) treats the cancelled bond as the record of Gentile sins under the *stoicheia* and pagan religious orders. Paul, on this view, has nothing dismissive to say about Torah for Jews; the polemic is about Gentile entry into a syncretized religious system that pretended to mediate cosmic order.
- **The apocalyptic reading** (Martyn, Douglas Campbell, Susan Eastman) treats the bond as a cosmic juridical document — humanity's subjugation under the cosmic powers — that is publicly torn up in the apocalypse of the cross.

A fair steelmanning of the field: a reader convinced this is about Torah-abrogation should sit with the Nanos case that Paul never speaks dismissively of Torah-for-Jews and that his real target in 2:8–23 is Gentile syncretism. A reader convinced this is about a personal indictment should sit with the Lutheran case that the law accuses precisely as written demand and that the cancellation is not just of the indictment but of the demand's accusing power. A reader convinced this is cosmic should sit with the patristic case that Paul and Chrysostom alike understand sin as a personal liability against personal trespasses, not an abstract cosmic record.

The verse can be read at three altitudes — personal indictment, ceremonial code, cosmic ordinance — and each tradition has chosen the altitude that fits its larger theological system. The chapter itself does not arbitrate; it operates at all three altitudes simultaneously, which is part of what makes it generative.

### Tension 2: When does discipline become a snare? (2:20–23)

Paul's complaint is concrete: "do not handle, do not taste, do not touch... they have indeed an appearance of wisdom in promoting self-made religion (ἐθελοθρησκία) and asceticism and severity to the body, but they are of no value in stopping the indulgence of the flesh." The Greek *ethelothrēskia* is a Pauline coinage — "self-chosen worship," "religion of one's own willing" — and the polemic is sharp.

But the Christian tradition has not read this as a blanket condemnation of bodily discipline.

- The Catholic and Orthodox monastic traditions (Antony of Egypt, the Desert Fathers, Benedict of Nursia, John Climacus's *Ladder*) have understood asceticism as necessary spiritual training (ἄσκησις) when ordered to charity. Aquinas (*ST* II-II.88) defends religious vows when freely chosen and ordered to virtue. The Catholic reading of 2:23 takes Paul's target to be *self-made* religion (ἐθελοθρησκία), not embodied discipline as such.
- The Protestant Reformation drew the line elsewhere. Luther's 1521 *On Monastic Vows* cites this passage frontally against the monastic life. Calvin (*Institutes* IV.13) is more measured but reads 2:23 as a fundamental rebuke of works-righteousness asceticism.
- Contemporary theology has substantially rehabilitated *askēsis* on the Protestant side. Sarah Coakley's *Powers and Submissions* and *God, Sexuality, and the Self* argue contemplative practice and embodied discipline are essential to Christian formation and were lost at significant cost in Reformation and post-Reformation Protestantism. Rowan Williams's writings on Augustine and the desert fathers make a similar case. Stanley Hauerwas has argued more pointedly that Protestantism's loss of disciplined embodied practice has been catastrophic for Christian moral formation.

The chapter condemns asceticism unmoored from Christ and self-prescribed for spiritual rank; it does not condemn embodied discipline as such. The line is hard to hold, and traditions have policed it differently. The judgment about whether a given practice — a fast, a silence, a sexual abstinence, a regimen of food refusal — falls inside or outside Paul's polemic depends on whether it is held in connection to the Head (2:19) or detached from him as an independent achievement.

**A closing question to sit with**: when you next find yourself wanting to *do something* to make yourself acceptable — a rule, a fast, a small visible discipline — what would it cost to ask whether you have already been "filled in him" (2:10), and whether you have anything left to add?

## The Thread

- **Galatians 4:1–9** — Paul's parallel use of *stoicheia*, where the same vocabulary is deployed against a different occasion; reading the two passages in tandem shows Paul had a settled cosmology of liberation from the elements.
- **Romans 6:1–11** — the death-burial-resurrection logic of baptism, given fuller theological treatment than Colossians 2:11–12; hear Romans 6 underneath the Colossian compression.
- **Hebrews 7–10** — a parallel cancellation-of-cult argument worked from the high-priestly side rather than the cosmic-juridical side; same theological move, different rhetorical register.
- **Ephesians 2:14–16** — "abolishing the law of commandments expressed in ordinances," likely the same author or circle, addressing a different polemical setting; the texts illuminate one another.
