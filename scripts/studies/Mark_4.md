# Mark 4: The Word Sown, the Sea Stilled

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Mark 4 unfolds along the Sea of Galilee in the early-to-mid Tiberian period (Tiberius reigned 14–37 CE), under Herod Antipas's tetrarchy. The lake itself — *Yam Kinneret* in Hebrew, *Limnē Gennēsaret* in Greek — sits 211 meters below sea level and is roughly 21 km north-to-south by 13 km east-to-west. Its eastern shore lay in Philip's tetrarchy or in the Decapolis; its western and northwestern shores were Antipas's Galilee. The agricultural geography behind the parables is the Bekaa-like plain stretching west of the lake (the Plain of Gennesaret) and the limestone hill country rising toward Korazim and Safed. Galilean small-holder agriculture in the first century relied on terracing for vines and olives, with mixed grain (wheat and barley) on flatter soils. The Plain of Gennesaret was famously fertile; Josephus (*War* 3.516–521) describes it as supporting "every species of fruit, grapes and figs ten months in the year, and the rest of the fruits in succession throughout the entire year." The yields cited in 4:8 (thirty-, sixty-, hundredfold) are agricultural marvels — typical Galilean yields of wheat and barley were in the four-to-tenfold range, with exceptional fields reaching tenfold to twentyfold; David C. Sim and Joachim Jeremias (*The Parables of Jesus*) have argued that hundredfold is therefore eschatological-hyperbolic; K. D. White and others have suggested it falls within the upper range of unusually fertile zones; either way, the trajectory of the parable is from ordinary peasant frustration (path, rock, thorns) to extraordinary divine yield.

The sowing technique behind 4:3–8 is broadcast sowing — the sower walks the field flinging seed by hand from a sack, and ploughing follows the sowing rather than preceding it. (The Mishnah and Tosefta record both pre-plowing and post-plowing methods; first-century Galilean practice was likely post-plowing, which makes sense of the path losses, the rocky outcrops, and the surviving thorns.) The fishing economy behind 4:35–41 was the lake's other primary industry. The Magdala stone, found in the 2009 excavations of the Migdal synagogue (likely the earliest synagogue ever excavated, in continuous use through the first century), bears a menorah relief and reflects a community whose wealth came from fish-salting (Magdala / *Tarichaeae*, "the salting place"). The Kinneret Boat (or "Jesus Boat"), recovered from the lakebed near Kibbutz Ginosar in 1986 during a drought, is a 27-foot first-century fishing boat carbon-dated to between 100 BCE and 100 CE; it is built of cedar planks and Aleppo oak, with patches and repairs reflecting decades of use. It is the kind of boat that Jesus and the disciples would have used in 4:36.

The Sea of Galilee's microclimate generates the kind of squall described in 4:37. Cold air masses from the Mediterranean descend through the Wadi el-Hamam and other gorges; warm air rising from the lake basin (211 meters below sea level) collides with the cold descent, producing sudden violent storms. Local fishermen still warn of these. Mark's *lailaps megalē anemou* (4:37, "great windstorm") is meteorologically precise.

The "other side" (4:35) — where Jesus and the disciples are headed — is the eastern shore (the Decapolis / region of the Gerasenes). This is Gentile territory. The boat-crossing is therefore programmatic: the gospel is moving from Jewish to Gentile territory. The "other boats" (4:36) — a detail unique to Mark — suggests a small flotilla; some commentators have read this as evidence of Petrine memory (the kind of detail an eyewitness would include and a later editor would smooth out).

The audience hearing parables in 4:1–34 is mixed: Galilean peasants (the kind who sow grain), small-holder vineyard tenants, fishermen, day-laborers, and, increasingly, the curious from outside Galilee (3:7–8). The Aramaic-speaking village world is Mark's primary setting; Greek would have been used for trade and contact with administrative authorities.

Greek terms worth carrying: ***mystērion*** (μυστήριον, "secret/mystery" — 4:11; in the Septuagint and Daniel, an apocalyptic term for hidden divine purposes); ***parabolē*** (παραβολή, "parable" — translating Hebrew *māshāl*, which encompasses everything from proverb to riddle to allegory); ***siōpa, pephimōso*** (σιώπα, πεφίμωσο — 4:39, "be silent, be muzzled" — the same verb Jesus uses to silence demons in 1:25); ***deilos / pistis*** (δειλός / πίστις — 4:40, "afraid / faith"). The verb form *pephimōso* is striking — a perfect imperative passive ("be muzzled and stay muzzled") suggesting the storm is not just calmed but rebuked as one rebukes an unruly spirit.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 4 is the gospel's first sustained discourse, and its structure is carefully built. The chapter is bracketed by two boat scenes — the boat as pulpit (4:1) and the boat as crucible (4:35–41) — and inside the bracket runs a parable cycle: the Sower (4:3–9), its private interpretation (4:10–20), the lamp and the measure (4:21–25), the seed growing secretly (4:26–29), and the mustard seed (4:30–32). The sequence follows what Mary Ann Tolbert (*Sowing the Gospel*) reads as the gospel's master parable — the parable of the soils is the interpretive grid for the entire Markan narrative.

The kingdom theme is now densely worked. The phrase ***hē basileia tou theou*** (ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ, "the kingdom of God"), already announced at 1:14–15, is used five times in chapter 4 (4:11, 26, 30) and is the dominant subject of every parable except the lamp/measure cluster.

The literary problem of 4:11–12 is one of the most debated in Markan studies. Jesus says that the *mystērion* of the kingdom has been given "to you," but to "those outside" everything is in parables — and then quotes Isa 6:9–10 ("so that *hina* they may indeed see but not perceive…lest *mēpote* they should turn and be forgiven"). The Greek conjunction *hina* normally indicates purpose ("in order that"), and *mēpote* "lest." Read straightforwardly, this is a hard-predestinarian reading: the parables are spoken so that outsiders will not understand, lest they be forgiven. **Joachim Jeremias** (*The Parables of Jesus*, 1947) tried to soften this by arguing the underlying Aramaic *de* / *be-dīl* could be result rather than purpose; **C. F. D. Moule** and others have followed. **C. H. Dodd** (*The Parables of the Kingdom*) read it as the consequence rather than purpose. **N. T. Wright** (*Jesus and the Victory of God*) reads the citation as Jesus identifying his own ministry with Isaiah's: like Isaiah, Jesus speaks to a Israel that has refused to hear, and the parables function as judgment — not because Jesus wants to exclude but because the word is by nature parabolic and demands ears to hear. **Adela Yarbro Collins** (*Hermeneia*) reads the verse as Markan apocalyptic determinism in continuity with Qumran's predestinarian sectarianism (1QH, 1QS). **Joel Marcus** (*Anchor Bible*) reads the *hina* as genuine purpose but within a Jewish apocalyptic frame in which divine and human responsibility overlap. **Madeleine Boucher** (*The Mysterious Parable*) reads parables as inherently double — clear to those inside the interpretive community, opaque to those outside — by their literary nature.

The Sower itself sits at the heart of Mark's parabolic theology. **Adolf Jülicher's** 1888–89 *Die Gleichnisreden Jesu* is the foundational modern critical work on parables — Jülicher rejected allegorical exegesis (in which each element symbolically corresponds to something else) in favor of single-point readings (each parable makes one point). On Jülicher's reading, the Sower's allegorical interpretation in 4:13–20 must be a Markan or pre-Markan ecclesial expansion, not authentic Jesus tradition. **Joachim Jeremias** continued this line, arguing the interpretation reflects later Christian persecution-context vocabulary ("tribulation or persecution arises on account of the word"). The contemporary recovery of allegorical legitimacy, however — especially in **Klyne Snodgrass's *Stories with Intent*** (2008) and **Arland Hultgren's *The Parables of Jesus*** (2000) — has rehabilitated the synoptic interpretations. Snodgrass argues that the dichotomy Jülicher built between parable and allegory was overdrawn; ancient Jewish *meshalim* (e.g., Nathan's parable to David in 2 Sam 12) were often allegorical at multiple points. **John Dominic Crossan** (*The Dark Interval*; *In Parables*) reads parables as polyvalent and subversive — the Sower's force is its agricultural realism (most seed fails) coupled with its eschatological hope (some yields hundredfold).

***Euthys*** continues to drive the prose: 4:5 ("immediately it sprang up"), 4:15 ("Satan immediately comes"), 4:16 ("immediately receive it with joy"), 4:17 ("immediately they fall away"), 4:29 ("at once he puts in the sickle"). The drumbeat is now in the parables themselves — the agricultural rhythm of springing, scorching, falling.

The seed growing secretly (4:26–29) is **unique to Mark**. It appears in neither Matthew nor Luke. Its uniqueness has invited speculation that it preserves authentic Jesus tradition independent of the Q stream; or that it is a Markan composition encoding the gospel's "kingdom comes by hidden means" theology. The harvest verb *apostellō* (ἀποστέλλει, 4:29, "he puts in" or "sends out") echoes Joel 3:13 ("put in the sickle, for the harvest is ripe") and grounds the parable in apocalyptic harvest imagery (Rev 14:15 will reuse the same Joel allusion).

The storm-stilling (4:35–41) is staged with archetypal restraint. Jesus is asleep on the *proskephalaion* (προσκεφάλαιον, 4:38, the "cushion" — likely the helmsman's seat-cushion). The boat is filling; the disciples wake him. His command — "Peace, be still" (*siōpa, pephimōso*) — uses the demon-rebuke verb. The implicit YHWH-Christology is unmistakable: Ps 89:9 ("you rule the raging of the sea; when its waves rise, you still them"), Ps 107:23–32 (the storm-stilling psalm), Job 38:8–11 ("who shut in the sea with doors… and said, 'thus far you shall come, and no farther'"). The disciples' question — "who then is this?" (*tis ara houtos estin*, 4:41) — is the chapter's punchline. The reader knows; the disciples are still groping.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc behind chapter 4 reaches into the Genesis creation (the divine word that orders chaos and seas), the prophets' use of agricultural imagery (Isa 5's vineyard, Jer 4:3 "break up your fallow ground," Hos 10:12 "sow for yourselves righteousness"), the apocalyptic harvest imagery (Joel 3:13, Dan 7), and Israel's deepest sea-storm tradition — Ps 107, Ps 89, Jonah 1, the Red Sea crossing in Exodus 14–15.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** The *māshāl* tradition in rabbinic Judaism is the closest analog to Jesus's parabolic teaching. The earliest collected rabbinic *meshalim* are in *Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael*, *Sifra*, *Sifre*, and the Tannaitic Midrashim; by the medieval period, *meshalim* fill the homiletical Midrashim (Genesis Rabbah, Leviticus Rabbah, etc.). David Stern's *Parables in Midrash* (1991) demonstrated that the rabbinic *māshāl* form is structurally close to Jesus's parables: a "king" or "father" or "owner" stands for God; the human characters figure Israel or the nations; the *nimshal* (interpretation) typically follows. Geza Vermes (*Jesus the Jew*) read Jesus's parables as continuous with this Galilean charismatic-rabbinic milieu. Daniel Boyarin's *The Jewish Gospels* read the kingdom-of-God parables as authentically Jewish apocalyptic-restorationist preaching. Amy-Jill Levine's *Short Stories by Jesus* (2014) recovers the Jewishness of the parables from centuries of supersessionist Christian misreading — the Sower is not about church-versus-Jewish-rejection but about the kingdom's mysterious ratio of failure to fruitfulness within Israel itself.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** Origen's *Commentary on Matthew* (which preserves his most extensive parable exegesis, fragments on Mark) read parables allegorically at multiple levels — anagogical, moral, mystical. Augustine's *Quaestiones Evangeliorum* and his sermons read the four soils as types of human moral disposition. Bede's *Commentary on Mark* allegorized: the sower is Christ, the seed is the word, the four soils are four states of soul. The medieval allegorical tradition culminated in Aquinas's *Catena Aurea* on Mark, weaving together Origen, Chrysostom, Bede, and Theophylact. The Reformation broke from much of this allegorical apparatus — Calvin's *Harmony* read the Sower with restraint, focusing on the central point (the word's reception conditions) rather than on multiplied symbolic correspondences. Luther's 1525 *Sermon on the Sower* fixed the Reformation reading: the four soils are not predestinarian categories but a description of the diverse human response to the gospel.

Modern critical commentary on Mark 4 includes Vincent Taylor's foundational *The Gospel According to St. Mark* (1952); C. E. B. Cranfield (*CGTC*); R. T. France's *NIGTC* (the most thorough recent evangelical commentary); Joel Marcus's *Anchor Bible* (the most thorough critical-historical commentary); Adela Yarbro Collins's *Hermeneia*; Morna Hooker's *BNTC*. **Madeleine Boucher's *The Mysterious Parable*** (1977) and **Frank Kermode's *The Genesis of Secrecy*** (1979) re-thought the relation between parable, secret, and reading — Kermode argues that Mark's parables are constitutive of a hermeneutic that withholds even as it reveals.

**Literary and feminist readings.** **Mary Ann Tolbert's *Sowing the Gospel*** (1989) is the major book-length reading of Mark through the Sower as master parable: every subsequent character in Mark gets sorted into one of the four soils (path, rocky, thorny, good). Joanna Dewey, David Rhoads, and Donald Michie (*Mark as Story*) read the chapter as a narrative pivot — Jesus has consolidated the inside/outside distinction (4:10–12) and now begins the long teaching of those inside.

**Political and liberation readings.** **Ched Myers's *Binding the Strong Man*** reads chapter 4 as Jesus's narrative-political pedagogy: the parables conceal from imperial-religious power and reveal to the marginalized; the Sower's hundredfold yield is a peasant utopian dream against the extractive yield of the elites. Richard Horsley's *Hearing the Whole Story* reads the storm-stilling as a counter-imperial enactment — the chaos of the sea (in Hellenistic and Jewish apocalyptic, a frequent symbol for imperial power, cf. Dan 7:2–3 "the four winds stirring the great sea") is rebuked by Jesus.

**N. T. Wright's *Jesus and the Victory of God*** reads the Sower as Jesus's most direct statement that his ministry is the long-awaited return of YHWH to plant a renewed Israel — and that the planting will pass through failure (path, rock, thorns) to harvest. The harvest is eschatological but already in motion.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Why parables? — divine pedagogy or divine hardening?** The hard reading of 4:11–12 (Calvin's reading; Reformed sovereignty): God speaks in parables so that the elect understand and the non-elect are confirmed in their refusal. This is not arbitrary cruelty but the divine response to a long-standing Israelite pattern of refusal (Isa 6) — a judicial hardening. The Catholic position (Aquinas, *ST* I-II q. 79): God permits but does not cause hardening; the parable form is itself merciful, inviting interpretation. The Wesleyan-Arminian position: the parables address all hearers; the soils represent free human response, not predestined categories. The Orthodox position: the parable is *mysterion* — an icon-word that invites contemplation; understanding follows from spiritual disposition, not predetermination. The historical-Jesus reading (Sanders, Wright): Jesus is identifying with Isaiah, prophetically diagnosing Israel's deafness and announcing the kingdom anyway — the parables are judgment-and-invitation. The literary reading (Kermode, Boucher): parables are by their nature ambiguous; they sort hearers by their own engagement, not by an external decree. The liberation reading (Myers): the parables conceal from imperial-religious power and reveal to those willing to inhabit the kingdom from below; the *mystērion* is dangerous knowledge requiring trust. **Where this leaves us:** Mark refuses to soften 4:11–12. The disciples themselves are about to demonstrate (4:13, "do you not understand this parable?") that being inside is no guarantee of comprehension; the parable cuts through the inside/outside distinction even as it asserts it. Steelman the strongest predestinarian reading (Calvin's), and the Markan structure still complicates it: the disciples are *outside* in their incomprehension, and the chapter ends with Jesus rebuking their lack of faith (4:40). The line between inside and outside runs not through groups but through hearts.

**Tension 2: Faith versus fear — what does Jesus expect of disciples in the storm?** The disciples' question — "do you not care that we are perishing?" (4:38) — is one of the most pastorally honest sentences in Mark. Jesus's response — "why are you so afraid? have you still no faith?" (4:40) — implies he expected something different. The Reformed position: faith is trust in God's covenantal care; the disciples' fear evidences a deficient faith that must be progressively grown into Christ. The Catholic position: faith is the assent of the intellect and will to God's revelation; in the storm, faith would have meant trusting Jesus's presence even asleep. The Orthodox position: faith is the *energeia* of the Spirit in the soul, displaced by the *pathē* (passions, here: fear); the storm becomes ascetic-pedagogical. The Wesleyan position: faith is personal trust enabled by prevenient grace; the disciples' failure shows the need for the Spirit's deeper work. The liberation reading: faith is the courage to follow Jesus into the storm (the Decapolis crossing, 4:35) when the political-spiritual chaos rises — the storm is not just meteorological. The feminist reading: the disciples' fear is the patriarchal honor-shame fear of failure; faith is the trust that allows the boat to keep going. The Jewish-historical reading (Sanders): Jesus is performing as an apocalyptic prophet whose authority over chaos echoes the divine warrior of the Psalms. **Where this leaves us:** Jesus's question (4:40) is uncomfortable. The disciples have just heard the parables — they have been told the *mystērion* — and yet at the first storm they suspect Jesus does not care. Mark refuses to make faith easy. Faith in Mark is the courage that holds when the boat is filling, and it will be tested again at every subsequent storm — the demoniac, the loaves, the cross. Steelman the most demanding reading (the disciples should have done better), and Mark's pastoral tenderness remains: Jesus does not abandon them mid-storm; he rebukes the wind, then asks the question.

**Unresolved question:** If "the earth produces by itself" (4:28, *automatē*) — and the sower "knows not how" (4:27) — what is the role of human agency in the kingdom? Mark seems to insist on both: the word must be sown and heard (4:14, 23), and yet the growth is divine and mysterious. How tightly should we hold the tension between trust in the kingdom's *automatē* coming and the urgency of sowing and hearing?

## The Thread

Four threads anchor chapter 4 into the rest of Mark. **4:13 — "do you not understand this parable?"** — initiates the disciple-incomprehension theme that will mount through **6:52 ("they did not understand about the loaves, but their hearts were hardened"), 7:18 ("are you also without understanding?"), 8:17–21 (the Pharisees' leaven and the disciples' loaves), and 9:32 (they did not understand the second passion prediction)**. The disciples' incomprehension is structural in Mark — they are inside the *mystērion* but cannot quite see. **4:35–41 — the storm-stilling** — pairs with **6:45–52 (Jesus walks on the sea, with the same disciple-incomprehension footnote)**. The two boat scenes are deliberate Markan doublets; the second escalates from rebuking the storm to walking on the storm. **4:11 — *to mystērion tēs basileias*** — recurs as the unspoken question through the rest of the gospel and is finally enacted (rather than explained) at **15:38 (the temple veil torn) and 16:6 (the empty tomb)**. The kingdom's *mystērion* is not a code to be cracked but a death-and-resurrection to be witnessed. **4:30–32 — the mustard seed** — encodes the gospel's whole shape: the smallest of beginnings (a Galilean carpenter's word, a peasant movement, a crucified messiah) becomes the tree where the birds of the air find shelter (an echo of Dan 4:21 and Ezek 17:23, Gentile inclusion in the eschatological tree). By the final chapter the smallest seed has become the worldwide gospel — but Mark's last word, in the original ending at 16:8, is the women fleeing in fear and saying nothing to anyone. The seed is sown; what kind of soil is the reader?
