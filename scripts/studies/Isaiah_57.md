# Isaiah 57 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: Historical World

Isaiah 57 sits inside Third Isaiah (Trito-Isaiah, chapters 56-66), the post-exilic prophetic corpus addressed to the restoration community in Persian-period Yehud, roughly the late sixth to fifth centuries BC. On the mainstream-critical reading (Bernhard Duhm's tripartite division, defended in Joseph Blenkinsopp's Anchor Bible commentary, Claus Westermann's *Isaiah 40-66* OTL volume, Paul Hanson's *The Dawn of Apocalyptic*, John Goldingay's ICC work, Patricia Tull, Jacob Stromberg), the chapter belongs to anonymous post-exilic prophecy that is sharper in tone than Second Isaiah, more concerned with cultic-ethical failure inside the restoration community, and more conflicted about who counts as the people of God. The unified-authorship defense (Brevard Childs canonically; Alec Motyer and John Oswalt for full single-authorship) reads Isaiah 57 as eighth-century prophecy of Isaiah ben Amoz, with the idolatry-imagery referring to the high-place worship of Manasseh's reign or general syncretistic Baalism. The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran (1QIsa-a, ca. 125 BC) preserves the chapter as part of the unified 66-chapter book.

The chapter divides into three parts. The first (57:1-2) laments the death of the righteous, taken away while no one understands. The second (57:3-13) contains one of the most graphic idolatry-condemnations in the Hebrew prophetic corpus, charging the community with high-place worship, child sacrifice, sexual rites, and political alliance-making. The third (57:14-21) returns to restoration-promise, with the famous *peace, peace, to the far and to the near* that becomes load-bearing in New Testament Pauline theology.

The opening (57:1-2) is striking: *the righteous man perishes, and no one lays it to heart; devout men are taken away, while no one understands. For the righteous man is taken away from calamity; he enters into peace*. The Hebrew הַצַּדִּיק אָבָד (*ha-tzaddiq avad*) — *the righteous one perishes* — and the parallel אַנְשֵׁי־חֶסֶד (*anshei-chesed*, men of steadfast love) describe the death of the faithful in a community that does not notice. The second clause adds a startling theodicy: the righteous one is taken away *from* the coming calamity (מִפְּנֵי הָרָעָה, *mipnei ha-ra'ah*) — death is here construed as merciful preservation from a coming evil. This reading anticipates later apocalyptic and Wisdom-of-Solomon themes (Wisdom 4:7-15) about the early death of the righteous as divine kindness.

The idolatry-condemnation of 57:3-13 deserves close attention. The catalog includes:

- *burning with lust among the oaks, under every green tree* (57:5) — fertility-cult worship under sacred trees, a recurring prophetic concern (Hosea 4:13; Jeremiah 2:20; Deuteronomy 12:2)
- *slaughtering your children in the valleys, under the clefts of the rocks* (57:5) — child sacrifice (Hebrew שֹׁחֲטֵי הַיְלָדִים, *shochatei ha-yeladim*), associated with Molech worship in the Hinnom Valley (cf. 2 Kings 23:10; Jeremiah 7:31; 32:35)
- *the smooth stones of the valley* (57:6) — the Hebrew בְּחַלְּקֵי־נַחַל (*be-chalqei-nachal*, in the smoothnesses of the wadi) plays on *cheleq* (portion) — your portion is in the smooth-stones — likely cult-stones used in valley shrines
- *on a high and lofty mountain you have set your bed, and there you went up to offer sacrifice* (57:7) — high-place sexual rites
- *behind the door and the doorpost you have set up your memorial* (57:8) — household pagan icons displacing the Yahwistic mezuzah-Torah of Deuteronomy 6:9
- *you journeyed to the king with oil... you sent your envoys far off, and sent down even to Sheol* (57:9) — a doubled accusation: political alliance-making (likely with foreign powers) and necromantic consultation of the dead (cf. 1 Samuel 28; Isaiah 8:19; Deut 18:11)

The honest engagement with this material requires acknowledgment of its violence. The accusations of sexual immorality, child-sacrifice, and necromancy are framed in language of marital-betrayal (the community as Yahweh's adulterous wife — recurring prophetic metaphor from Hosea, Ezekiel, Jeremiah, Isaiah). The graphic sexualization of the idolatry critique — *you have uncovered your bed... you have loved their bed... you have looked on nakedness* (57:7-8) — has been read by feminist biblical scholars (Renita Weems, *Battered Love*; Athalya Brenner; Gerlinde Baumann) as pornographic-prophetic rhetoric that uses violence-against-women imagery to indict the community. This is honest territory. The text genuinely deploys this rhetoric. The mainstream critical reading does not soften this; it asks how the imagery functions and what it costs.

The middle of the chapter (57:14-15) makes a striking move. *Build up, build up, prepare the way, remove every obstruction from my people's way* (57:14) — the imagery directly recalls Isaiah 40:3 (*in the wilderness prepare the way of the LORD*), the great opening of Second Isaiah. *For thus says the One who is high and lifted up, who inhabits eternity, whose name is Holy: "I dwell in the high and holy place, and also with him who is of a contrite and lowly spirit"* (57:15). The Hebrew רָם וְנִשָּׂא שֹׁכֵן עַד וְקָדוֹשׁ שְׁמוֹ (*ram ve-nisa shokhen ad ve-qadosh shemo*) — *high and lifted up, dwelling forever, and Holy his name* — is exalted, transcendent language; immediately paired with דַּכָּא וּשְׁפַל־רוּחַ (*dakka u-shfal-ruach*, contrite and lowly of spirit). The God who *inhabits eternity* also dwells with the *contrite*. This bilateral-dwelling theology — God in the high place AND with the lowly — is one of the great theological articulations in the Hebrew Bible.

The chapter's most famous verse comes at 57:19: *peace, peace, to the far and to the near* (שָׁלוֹם שָׁלוֹם לָרָחוֹק וְלַקָּרוֹב, *shalom shalom la-rachoq ve-la-qarov*). This phrase will be picked up directly in Ephesians 2:17 — *and he came and preached peace to you who were far off and peace to those who were near* — applied by Paul to Christ's making peace between Jews (the *near*) and Gentiles (the *far*).

The chapter closes (57:20-21) with a sharp return to judgment: *the wicked are like the tossing sea... there is no peace, says my God, for the wicked*. This refrain — *no peace for the wicked* — is repeated nearly verbatim at 48:22, suggesting a structural marker dividing the corpus. Some critical scholars see this as an editorial seam.

## Lens 2: Literature

Isaiah 57 deploys multiple genres in succession. The opening (57:1-2) is lament-and-theodicy. The central section (57:3-13) is judgment-oracle in the form of legal-disputation: Yahweh prosecutes the community in court, listing charges. The closing section (57:14-21) returns to restoration-oracle and then closes with a wisdom-style judgment-refrain.

The most distinctive literary feature is the lurid sexualization of the idolatry critique. The Hebrew prophets routinely depict idolatry as adultery against Yahweh (Hosea 1-3; Jeremiah 2-3; Ezekiel 16, 23), but Isaiah 57:5-10 deploys particularly graphic imagery: bed-spreading, nakedness-uncovering, journeying-to-the-king-with-oil. The doubled use of *bed* (מִשְׁכָּב, *mishkav*) in 57:7-8 plays the literal cult-bed against the metaphorical betrayal-bed. The text wants the reader to feel scandalized, and feminist scholarship has rightly insisted that the cost of this rhetorical-effectiveness includes the casting of women's sexuality as the figure for unfaithfulness.

The structural pivot at 57:14 is masterful. The *build up, build up, prepare the way* echoes Isaiah 40:3 verbatim in vocabulary (Hebrew סֹלּוּ סֹלּוּ פַּנּוּ־דָרֶךְ, *sollu sollu pannu-darekh* — pile up, pile up, clear-the-way). This intertextual move is the editor's signal that even after the harsh idolatry-condemnation, Second Isaiah's restoration-vocabulary still applies. The way will be prepared.

The bilateral-dwelling theology of 57:15 deploys the rhetorical figure of merism — the Holy One who *inhabits eternity* (the highest) AND dwells with the *contrite and lowly spirit* (the lowest) — encompassing the totality. This rhetorical structure has become a load-bearing element of both Jewish and Christian piety: God is most-high AND with-the-lowly.

The *peace, peace, to the far and to the near* of 57:19 deploys another structural merism (far/near) and the doubled *shalom* — the repetition functioning to intensify and to encompass. The Hebrew pun-effect of *shalom shalom* paired with *rachoq ve-qarov* (far and near) gives the verse its musical-prophetic balance.

## Lens 3: Larger Story

In the prophetic canon, Isaiah 57 belongs to the prophetic-judgment tradition that runs through Hosea, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel — the recurring critique of the community's syncretistic worship, depicted in marital-betrayal imagery. The high-place idolatry critique is shared with the Deuteronomistic History (1-2 Kings, especially the evaluations of the kings of Israel and Judah by their faithfulness to centralized Yahwistic worship). The chapter's turn to the *contrite and lowly* in 57:15 anticipates the post-exilic sapiential development of humility-theology (Psalm 51, the penitential psalms; Wisdom literature's praise of the meek).

In Jewish trajectory, the *high and lifted up... and also with him who is of a contrite and lowly spirit* of 57:15 has been a foundational text for Jewish humility-theology and for the rabbinic doctrine of God's accessibility to the broken-hearted. The Talmud (*Bavli Sotah* 5a) discusses how Yahweh dwells with the contrite, and the medieval mystical tradition (the *Zohar*, Lurianic Kabbalah) develops the bilateral-dwelling theme into a theology of divine presence in the broken vessels. The early-death-of-the-righteous theme of 57:1-2 is read in *Bavli Mo'ed Qatan* 28a and similar tractates as theodicy: the righteous die early as a kindness, removed from coming evil.

The Lurianic doctrine of *tzimtzum* (divine self-contraction to make space for creation) draws part of its imaginative resonance from Isaiah 57:15's bilateral-dwelling: God who inhabits eternity contracts to dwell with the contrite. The medieval and modern Jewish mystical tradition has read this verse as the hinge between transcendence and immanence in divine being.

In Christian trajectory, Isaiah 57 has had several specific receptions. The *peace, peace, to the far and to the near* of 57:19 receives its most famous Christian appropriation in Ephesians 2:11-22, where Paul applies the verse to Christ's reconciliation of Jews and Gentiles. *He came and preached peace to you who were far off and peace to those who were near* (Eph 2:17) is, on the dominant New Testament scholarship reading (F.F. Bruce, *The Epistles to the Colossians, to Philemon, and to the Ephesians*; Andrew Lincoln, WBC Ephesians; Harold Hoehner; Frank Thielman), an explicit citation of Isaiah 57:19. The Pauline appropriation reads the *far* as Gentiles (cosmic outsiders), the *near* as Jews (covenant-near), and Christ as the *peace* who unites them. This is one of the clearest cases of New Testament Christological appropriation of Isaiah's restoration-vocabulary.

The *high and lofty One... who dwells with the contrite* of 57:15 has been a foundational text for Christian humility-theology. The medieval contemplative tradition (Augustine, Bernard of Clairvaux, the *Imitation of Christ*) draws explicitly on this verse, as does the Reformed tradition (Calvin's *Institutes* III.12 on humility). The verse functions in Christian piety as authorization for the entire tradition that locates divine presence in brokenness rather than triumph — what Luther called the *theology of the cross* against the *theology of glory*. Bonhoeffer's *Letters and Papers from Prison* (especially the *God lets himself be pushed out of the world onto the cross* passage) draws ultimately on this Isaianic bilateral-dwelling.

The early-death-of-the-righteous theme of 57:1-2 has shaped Christian funeral-theology and martyrology. Wisdom of Solomon 4:7-15 (*the righteous one, though he die early, will be at rest*) is in the Catholic deuterocanonical lectionary for the funeral of a child or young person, and the theology directly draws on Isaiah 57:1-2.

The high-place-idolatry tradition has had a complex modern Christian reception. In some streams, the imagery has been appropriated allegorically as critique of *modern idolatries* — consumerism, nationalism, technological worship. In other streams, the violent sexualization of the imagery has been a focus of feminist critique (Renita Weems and others arguing that the prophetic *marital-metaphor* tradition reinforces patriarchal violence). The mainstream critical position is to read the rhetoric in its ancient context (where it had specific cult-critical force) without endorsing its violence-against-women imagery as constructive theology.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

Three large tensions inhabit this chapter, and honest reading requires holding them.

The first tension is the *peace-to-far-and-near* and its Pauline appropriation. The mainstream critical reading sees 57:19 in its restoration-context — *peace* announced to the dispersed Jewish community, both those still in exile (*far*) and those returned to the land (*near*) — as the divine response after the harsh judgment of 57:3-13. The Pauline appropriation in Ephesians 2 extends the categories: *near* becomes Jews (covenant-near), *far* becomes Gentiles (cosmically far), and Christ becomes the unifying peace. This is a real theological move that the New Testament makes — and the move is not arbitrary, because the Hebrew text itself uses the *far/near* merism in a way that is structurally hospitable to wider extension. The Christian reading does not falsify the Hebrew text; it extends it. The honest engagement asks whether the extension preserves what the original meant or distorts it. Most New Testament scholarship (Lincoln, Hoehner, Thielman) finds the Pauline use a legitimate development of Isaiah's universalizing tendency that is already manifest in 56:7's *house of prayer for all peoples*.

The second tension is the *high-place idolatry tradition* and its modern reception. The mainstream critical reading takes the idolatry-condemnation seriously as a real cultural-religious critique of post-exilic syncretism: high-place worship, child-sacrifice, fertility rites, and necromancy were genuine practices in the historical context, and the prophetic critique was theologically pointed. The honest engagement, however, must reckon with the rhetorical violence of the imagery — particularly the sexualization of the idolatry critique in 57:7-8. Feminist biblical scholarship (Weems, Brenner, Baumann, Phyllis Trible's *Texts of Terror*) has rightly insisted that the prophetic *marital-metaphor* tradition, taken across the corpus (Hosea 1-3; Jeremiah 2-3; Ezekiel 16, 23; Isaiah 57), creates a theological problem: the figure of unfaithful-female-sexuality bears the weight of all spiritual betrayal. To read the text honestly is neither to dismiss the cult-critical content nor to endorse the sexualizing rhetoric uncritically. The text functions; the cost is real.

The third tension concerns the *bilateral-dwelling* theology of 57:15. The God who *inhabits eternity* AND dwells with *the contrite and lowly* — this is one of the great theological articulations in the Hebrew Bible, and it has shaped Jewish and Christian piety for two-and-a-half millennia. But the verse is not simple. The bilateral-dwelling means that divine transcendence is not compromised by divine immanence; the same God is high-and-holy AND with-the-lowly. For Reformed theology, this preserves what Calvin called the *finitum non capax infiniti* (the finite cannot contain the infinite) while affirming gracious accommodation. For Catholic theology, the verse anticipates the doctrine of God's intimate presence in the soul (Augustine's *interior intimo meo*). For Jewish mystical theology, the *tzimtzum* tradition draws on this bilateral-dwelling to articulate divine self-contraction. The tension is between transcendence and immanence — and the verse refuses to dissolve either pole.

Where this leaves us: Isaiah 57 is hard prophecy. It opens with the lament for the righteous taken away unnoticed, moves through graphic idolatry-condemnation, and closes with the great theological balance of bilateral-dwelling and the *peace, peace, to the far and to the near*. The text does not soften its violence, and it does not surrender its hope. The Pauline appropriation in Ephesians 2 reads the far/near merism Christologically; the Jewish mystical tradition reads the bilateral-dwelling as the hinge of transcendence and immanence; the modern critical reading insists on the rhetorical-violence cost. All three are pointing at real features of the text.

The closing question: who is the *righteous one* taken away while no one understands? Whose death goes unmarked in the present community? And what does it cost to claim, alongside Isaiah, that the high-and-holy God is also with the contrite — without thereby instrumentalizing brokenness as a path to divine favor?

## The Thread

Isaiah 57 sits between the radical-inclusion vision of chapter 56 and the prophetic-justice imperative of chapter 58. The chapter does the dark work that the surrounding chapters require: it acknowledges the cost of unfaithfulness, names the practices the community must abandon, and yet refuses to close the door on restoration. The *peace, peace, to the far and to the near* anticipates the universalism that Pauline theology will receive; the *high and holy with the contrite* gives the theological foundation for the prophetic-justice ethic that chapter 58 will demand. The harsh middle and the gracious frame of chapter 57 belong together — restoration and accountability are not separable.
