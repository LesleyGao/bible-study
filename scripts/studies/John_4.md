# John 4: At the Well — Living Water, the Samaritan Apostle, and the Second Sign

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jacob's well is the strangest archaeological landmark in the New Testament. Of all the place-names in the Fourth Gospel, this is the one with the most secure identification — what Jerome Murphy-O'Connor and the *Oxford Archaeological Guide to the Holy Land* call "the only New Testament site whose location has been continuously known and is virtually unanimously identified." The well sits today within the crypt of the Greek Orthodox Church of St. Photini in the modern Palestinian city of Nablus, at the foot of Mount Gerizim, a few hundred meters from Tell Balata — the archaeological mound of ancient Shechem. The well was excavated by Robert J. Bull (1956-1973) and earlier by H. Kerkhof; it is at least 35 meters deep, possibly originally over 41 meters; it has flowed continuously through Byzantine, Crusader, Ottoman, and modern periods. Pilgrim accounts from the fourth century onward (the Bordeaux Pilgrim of 333 CE; Egeria; Jerome) all locate it at this site. The continuity is genuinely unique among New Testament archaeology.

**Sychar** (4:5) — the town to which the woman returns — is most plausibly Askar, a village on the eastern slope of Mount Ebal a few hundred meters from the well; some scholars (older proposals) identified Sychar with Shechem itself. Jerome's *Onomasticon* (Eusebius's *Onomasticon* underlies it) treats Sychar as a corruption of Shechem; modern archaeological consensus, following James Pritchard's surveys and Murphy-O'Connor, identifies Sychar with Askar.

The Samaritan-Jewish enmity behind 4:9 ("for Jews have no dealings with Samaritans") has a specific second-temple history. The Samaritans were descendants of populations the Babylonian and Assyrian deportations had not removed and who had developed their own version of the Pentateuch (the Samaritan Pentateuch) and their own temple on Mount Gerizim. The Mount Gerizim temple was built in the late Persian period (probably late 5th century BCE, recent excavations under Yitzhak Magen confirm) and was destroyed by the Hasmonean John Hyrcanus around 128-110 BCE — an act of religious-political aggression that fixed Jewish-Samaritan hostility for centuries. **Reinhard Pummer's** *The Samaritans: A Profile* (2016) is the foundational contemporary scholarly synthesis; **Magnar Kartveit's** *The Origin of the Samaritans* (2009) treats the historical and textual questions; **Yitzhak Magen's** excavations on Mount Gerizim from 1982 onward have transformed our understanding of the Samaritan temple and its destruction. The Samaritan community survives today as roughly 800 people in two communities, on Mount Gerizim and in Holon, Israel.

The geographical detail in 4:6 — "It was about the sixth hour" (noon by Roman reckoning) — and 4:52 — the official's son recovers at "the seventh hour" — orient the reader chronologically. The "fourth month before harvest" of 4:35 is a calendrical or proverbial reference; if calendrical, it would set the chapter in roughly January-February (four months before the May-June barley harvest), though Brown and Schnackenburg note this is most likely a traditional saying rather than a chronological marker.

**Capernaum** (4:46), the home of the royal official, was a substantial fishing village on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee. Excavations under Virgilio Corbo, Stanislao Loffreda, and James Strange (Franciscan and University of South Florida teams) have uncovered the first-century synagogue beneath the surviving fourth/fifth-century one, and a domus-ecclesia identified as Peter's house with continuous Christian veneration from the first century onward.

Greek terms in the chapter:
- ***Hydōr zōn*** (ὕδωρ ζῶν, "living water") — 4:10-11. The double-meaning: literally "running / flowing water" (vs. cistern water); figuratively the eschatological gift of the Spirit.
- ***Pēgē*** (πηγή, "spring, fountain") — 4:14. A spring vs. a well; Jesus's gift produces spring-like welling.
- ***Egō eimi*** (ἐγώ εἰμι, "I am") — 4:26 — the first absolute *egō eimi* in the Gospel, anticipating the divine-name Christology of John 8:58 and beyond.
- ***Pneuma kai alētheia*** (πνεῦμα καὶ ἀλήθεια, "spirit and truth") — 4:23-24. The new mode of worship.
- ***Sōtēr tou kosmou*** (σωτὴρ τοῦ κόσμου, "Savior of the world") — 4:42 — a phrase used in imperial cult of the Roman emperor; here applied to Jesus by Samaritans, a striking provincial subversion.
- ***Basilikos*** (βασιλικός) — 4:46, 4:49. "Royal official" — a Herodian court figure, not a Roman.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The Samaritan-woman narrative is one of the longest single narrative units in any Gospel and the longest dialogue in the Fourth Gospel. R. Alan Culpepper's *Anatomy of the Fourth Gospel* and Mark Stibbe's *John as Storyteller* both treat it as a literary masterpiece — characterization, dialogue rhythm, deliberate ambiguity, and theological depth integrated.

**Robert Alter's** *The Art of Biblical Narrative* (1981) identified the **type-scene** as a fundamental structural device of biblical narrative — recurring plot patterns whose conventional features both shape audience expectation and create meaning through deviation. The "betrothal at the well" type-scene appears in **Genesis 24** (Abraham's servant meets Rebekah at a well; she draws water; family hospitality; betrothal arranged); **Genesis 29** (Jacob meets Rachel at a well; he waters her flock; betrothal); **Exodus 2:15-21** (Moses meets Zipporah at a well; he defends her from shepherds; betrothal). The conventions of the type-scene: a man arrives at a foreign well; meets a woman drawing water; an act of hospitality (water given or received); the woman runs home; a betrothal results.

John 4 deliberately invokes and subverts this type-scene. Jesus, weary, comes to a well. A woman arrives to draw water. He asks for a drink. But: she has had five husbands; the man she has now is not her husband; she runs to the town not to announce a betrothal but to announce a prophet who knew her secrets. The "betrothal" that occurs is theological-eschatological — the Bridegroom (foreshadowed in 3:29) meets his bride, who is the entire Samaritan town; "many Samaritans believed in him because of the woman's testimony" (4:39). **Jo-Ann Brant's** *Paideia* commentary treats the type-scene reading at length; **Sandra Schneiders's** *Written That You May Believe* makes it foundational; **Brown's** *Anchor Bible* treats the structural parallels carefully.

The **misunderstanding** device — Jesus speaks of "living water," the woman hears "running spring water" and protests that the well is deep and Jesus has no bucket — is the chapter's structural engine. As in chapter 3 with Nicodemus, the misunderstanding is generative: it provokes the deeper teaching.

**Brown's seven-signs schema** locates 4:46-54 (the official's son) as the second sign, completing the "Cana to Cana" frame (2:1-4:54) that runs from the first Cana sign through the discourses with Nicodemus, the Samaritan woman, and the disciples about the harvest, to the second Cana sign. Brown's structural insight: the central material (Nicodemus, the Samaritan woman, the official) presents three representative responses to Jesus, widening the circle of belief — Pharisaic Judaism, Samaritanism, and a probably-Gentile-or-at-least-non-Judean royal household.

The textual variants in chapter 4 are minor. The most significant is the parenthetical 4:9b ("for Jews have no dealings with Samaritans"), which is omitted by Sinaiticus and a few witnesses; NA28 retains it. The verb *synchrōntai* (συγχρῶνται) here is unusual and may mean "share vessels with" (a ritual-purity concern) rather than "associate with" generally.

The disciples' reaction in 4:27 ("they marveled that he was talking with a woman") is a striking narrative moment; rabbinic tradition (Mishnah *Avot* 1:5: "talk not much with womankind"; m. Sotah 3:4) cautioned against extended public conversation between rabbis and women, and Brown, Keener, and Schneiders all note that Jesus's behavior here violates Pharisaic propriety.

The "Savior of the world" acclamation in 4:42 is striking. The phrase *sōtēr tou kosmou* was a standard Roman imperial title — applied to Augustus, Nero, Hadrian, and others on inscriptions, coins, and in literature. **Adolf Deissmann's** *Light from the Ancient East* (1908/1927) was foundational on this; **Craig Keener's** commentary catalogs the imperial-cult background extensively. For Samaritans (a colonized provincial people) to apply this title to a Jewish prophet from Galilee is a quietly subversive act — a transfer of imperial honors to the executed-and-risen one.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish trajectory.** The chapter's most consequential statement for Jewish-Christian relations is 4:22: "you worship what you do not know; we worship what we know, for *salvation is from the Jews* (*hē sōtēria ek tōn Ioudaiōn estin*)." This is the most pro-Jewish sentence in the Fourth Gospel — and one of the most underread. **Adele Reinhartz's** *Cast Out of the Covenant* notes it pointedly: a Gospel with such pervasive negative use of "the Jews" also contains this irreducibly affirmative theological commitment. **Marianne Meye Thompson's** *NTL* commentary gives it substantial weight. The verse is not incidental; it is structural to the chapter's argument. Jesus is moving the locus of worship beyond both Mount Gerizim (Samaritan) and Jerusalem (Judean Temple), but the path of salvation runs *through* Israel.

The well itself (4:5-6) is "Jacob's well" — explicitly Patriarchal, explicitly the inheritance of all the children of Jacob, Samaritans included (the Samaritans claimed descent through Joseph, whose tomb is also in the vicinity at Tell Balata, where archaeological identification is also reasonably secure). **Daniel Boyarin's** *Border Lines* and the broader "parting of the ways" scholarship (James Dunn; Adam Becker and Annette Yoshiko Reed's *The Ways That Never Parted*) frame the chapter as a contest *within* the family of Abraham, not between two religions.

The **Targumic Memra** tradition (which Boyarin develops in *Jewish Gospels*) reads the *egō eimi* of 4:26 within the deeply Jewish tradition of divine-name speculation. Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Exodus 3:14 expands "I AM" with the Memra. **Catrin Williams's** *I Am He: The Interpretation of 'Anî Hû' in Jewish and Early Christian Literature* (2000) is the foundational study of this background; Williams shows that the Hebrew *anî hû* (אני הוא, "I AM" / "I am he") in Deutero-Isaiah (43:10, 25; 46:4; 48:12) was already a divine-name formula in late Second Temple Judaism, and the Septuagint's *egō eimi* renders this. Jesus's use of the absolute *egō eimi* in 4:26 is the first occurrence of this divine-name Christology in John; it will recur with increasing density (6:20; 8:24, 28, 58; 13:19; 18:5-8).

**Christian patristic trajectory.** **Origen's** *Commentary on John* (book 13 covers chapters 4-5) treats the Samaritan woman as an exemplar of philosophical-spiritual ascent: the dialogue moves from physical water to living water to messianic recognition to evangelical mission. **Augustine's** *Tractates on the Gospel of John* (tractates 15-16) treats her as a foundational figure for the church's catechumenate — the slow movement from need to recognition to confession. **John Chrysostom's** *Homilies on John* (homilies 31-35) preaches her as a model of repentance and witness. **Cyril of Alexandria's** *Commentary on John* makes the *egō eimi* of 4:26 a Christological keystone. **Aquinas's** *Lectura super Joannem* synthesizes the patristic readings.

The **Eastern Orthodox** tradition has a particularly developed reception: the woman is named **Photini** (Greek for "the enlightened one") or in Slavic tradition **Svetlana**, with a feast day (February 26 in Eastern reckoning), an extensive martyrology (she is said to have evangelized in North Africa and to have been martyred under Nero), and the Greek Orthodox church at Jacob's Well bears her name. The Orthodox tradition treats her as *equal-to-the-apostles* (*isapostolos*) — a title given to a small number of figures (Mary Magdalene, Helena, Photini, Nina of Georgia).

**Reformation.** **Calvin's *Commentary on John*** reads chapter 4 as a foundation for true worship — internal, spiritual, in spirit and truth, transcending the locative shrine-religion of both Gerizim and Jerusalem. Calvin's polemic against Catholic shrine-worship draws explicitly on 4:21-24. **Luther's** sermons on John 4 emphasize divine condescension — the Word who tabernacled among us now sits weary at a well, asking a despised woman for water. Reformation iconoclasm and the cleansing of pilgrimage-shrine practice in Protestant Europe drew theological warrant from this text.

**Mystical and devotional.** **Bernard of Clairvaux** preached on the Samaritan woman as a figure of the soul thirsting for Christ. **Eckhart's** sermons read the well as the depth of the soul where the Word springs up. **John of the Cross**'s *Spiritual Canticle* draws on living-water imagery. **Teresa of Avila's** *Interior Castle* uses the spring-of-water imagery from 4:14 for the contemplative interior life. **Hildegard of Bingen's** writings on the *viriditas* (greening power) of God draw on Johannine living-water imagery.

**Twentieth century commentaries.** **Brown** *Anchor Bible* treats the chapter as a literary masterpiece; **Bultmann** reads the Samaritan-mission as a symbol of universal reach beyond Judaism; **Dodd** treats 4:23-24 ("worship in spirit and truth") as foundational for his "realized eschatology" — the messianic hour is "now"; **Barrett**, **Schnackenburg**, **Beasley-Murray**, **Köstenberger**, **Carson**, **Keener**, **Thompson**, **Moloney**, **Brant**, **O'Day**, **Newbigin** all engage at length. **Lesslie Newbigin's** *The Light Has Come* makes chapter 4 foundational for missional ecclesiology — Jesus crossing ethnic and religious boundaries; the Samaritan woman as the first cross-cultural evangelist. **Ernst Haenchen** treats the chapter in his characteristic source-critical mode.

**Feminist trajectory** is unusually rich for this chapter. **Sandra Schneiders's** *Written That You May Believe* offers the most influential feminist reading: the woman is not a "fallen" or sexually-suspect figure (the conventional reading of "five husbands and one not your husband" as moral indictment) but a representative figure for Samaria itself — recall that Samaritans had been historically said to have worshiped five gods plus YHWH improperly (2 Kings 17:24-41), and the husbands-as-gods reading goes back to **Tertullian** (*Against Marcion* 4.35). Schneiders argues she is a sophisticated theological interlocutor, not a sinner being exposed; she becomes the first apostle to a non-Jewish people; her "Can this be the Christ?" (4:29) is an authentic christological confession in interrogative form. **Elisabeth Schüssler Fiorenza's** *In Memory of Her* makes her foundational for feminist reconstruction of early Christianity. **Adele Reinhartz** in *Befriending the Beloved Disciple* engages the chapter with attention to how its reception has both elevated and stereotyped the woman. **Gail O'Day's** *NIB* commentary integrates feminist reading with broader narrative-critical analysis. **Teresa Okure's** *The Johannine Approach to Mission* (1988) — by a Nigerian Catholic biblical scholar — reads the chapter as a missiological foundation from a Two-Thirds World perspective.

**Liberation theology.** **Gustavo Gutiérrez** and Latin American liberation readings emphasize Jesus's crossing of ethnic, religious, and gender boundaries — the despised Samaritan woman becomes the first evangelist; "Savior of the world" is acclaimed first by colonized provincials, not by imperial centers. **Jon Sobrino** reads the *sōtēr tou kosmou* of 4:42 as decolonial counter-confession to imperial cult.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension one: where, and how, does one worship God now?** "The hour is coming, and is now here, when the true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and truth" (4:23-24).

The **Catholic** tradition reads this as compatible with sacramental, locative worship: "in spirit and truth" does not abolish the body, the place, the rite, but interiorizes them — true worship is sacramental worship offered with the spirit's full engagement. *Sacrosanctum Concilium* (Vatican II) develops this. The **Eastern Orthodox** tradition similarly reads "spirit and truth" as the worship of the Spirit-led, truth-receiving believer integrated into the embodied liturgical life of the Church — the Divine Liturgy as the actualization of "spirit and truth." The **Reformed** tradition has historically read 4:23-24 as a critique of locative shrine-religion and as the warrant for Reformed simplicity in worship — no images, no pilgrimages, no holy places, only word and sacrament administered in spirit and truth. Calvin's *Institutes* 4.10 develops this. **Anabaptist** and **Quaker** traditions push further: no priesthood, no sacraments necessary, gathered worship in the Spirit. **Pentecostal/Charismatic** traditions read "in spirit" pneumatologically — worship is Spirit-led, ecstatic, gifted, not tied to liturgical form. **Liberation theology** reads "in spirit and truth" as worship in solidarity with the suffering — true worship is integrated with justice (citing Amos 5:21-24, Isaiah 58). **Pietist** and **devotional** traditions read it as worship of the heart — sincere, internal, free from formalism.

Steelmanned, the Catholic-Orthodox sacramental-liturgical reading takes seriously that "spirit and truth" does not negate body, place, or rite — God who took on flesh is properly worshiped in flesh-mediated ways. Steelmanned, the Reformed reading takes seriously the Johannine prediction of locative worship's transcendence ("neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem"). Steelmanned, the liberation reading takes seriously that prophetic critique is integral to true worship.

*Where this leaves us:* the verse establishes that worship is no longer geographically determined (Mount Gerizim or Jerusalem) without specifying a single normative form. Christian traditions have read it variously, and the variance is theologically productive rather than scandalous — the Church's worship is plural in form because the Spirit-and-truth is irreducibly free.

**Tension two: the woman's status — sinner exposed or theologian engaged?** "You have had five husbands, and the one you now have is not your husband" (4:18).

The **traditional Western reading** (Augustine, Calvin, much of subsequent Christian preaching) treats the woman as a moral failure, sexually compromised, brought to repentance by Jesus's prophetic exposure. Her "I have no husband" (4:17) is read as an evasive half-truth that Jesus penetrates. This reading underwrites a thousand sermons on conversion-through-confrontation.

The **revisionist feminist reading** (Sandra Schneiders, Schüssler Fiorenza, more recent commentary) argues:

(1) The text says nothing about her being divorced or unfaithful. In first-century Mediterranean culture, women rarely initiated divorce; she may have been widowed multiple times, or divorced by husbands for failure to bear children, or trapped in levirate succession. Her vulnerability is socially constructed, not morally indicting.

(2) Her response to Jesus is not evasive — it is theologically engaged. She raises the Mount-Gerizim-vs-Jerusalem question (4:20) — a substantive, sophisticated, and theologically central question, not a deflection.

(3) Her role in the chapter is sustained as theological dialogue partner, not penitent. She is the first character in the Fourth Gospel to receive the absolute *egō eimi* (4:26); she is the first evangelist to a non-Judean people; "many Samaritans believed because of her testimony" (4:39).

(4) The "five husbands" may be allegorical (Tertullian's reading: five gods of Samaria), narrative (a marker of social vulnerability), or historical without being moral. The text under-determines moral judgment.

The **Eastern Orthodox** tradition has long held a more positive reading — Photini as *isapostolos*, equal to the apostles, evangelist and martyr. **Liberation theology** reads her as a colonized woman whose theological agency is recognized by Jesus across the borders of gender, ethnicity, and religious history. **African and Asian feminist readings** (Teresa Okure, Kwok Pui-lan) have developed the reading further.

Steelmanned, the traditional moralizing reading takes seriously that the text's narrator does include the marital irregularity, presumably for some narrative reason, and that Jesus's revelatory naming of her history is a moment of genuine recognition. Steelmanned, the feminist reading takes seriously that the text never moralizes her history and that her function in the narrative is theological-evangelical, not penitential.

*Where this leaves us:* the chapter's reception has been shaped more by interpreters' default readings of women than by anything the text actually requires. The plain narrative function is theological-missiological. Whether the marital history is moral indictment or social vulnerability is exegetically under-determined; the certainty has flowed from Christian interpretive culture, not from the Greek text.

**Unresolved question:** what becomes of the Samaritan church the woman founds? The Acts narrative (Acts 8) has Philip evangelizing Samaria, with apparent assumptions of pre-existing readiness. Justin Martyr (a Samaritan by birth, second century) takes Samaritan Christianity for granted. By the fourth century, the Samaritan-Christian community was substantial enough to attract distinctive Byzantine ecclesiastical structures. Did the woman's community persist? The historical thread is suggestive but unrecoverable in detail.

## The Thread

The "living water" of 4:10-14 reaches forward to 7:37-39 ("if anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink... rivers of living water"), where the narrator interprets the saying as referring to the Spirit. The "Savior of the world" acclamation (4:42) reaches forward to 1 John 4:14 ("the Father has sent his Son to be the Savior of the world") and stands as one of the Gospel's most ecumenical confessions. The absolute *egō eimi* of 4:26 inaugurates a chain that climaxes in 8:58 ("before Abraham was, I am") and 18:5-8 (the soldiers fall back at Jesus's *egō eimi*). The "fields white for harvest" (4:35) prefigures the post-resurrection mission of chapter 21. The official's son sign (4:46-54) — the second of the seven signs — frames the Cana-to-Cana arc of chapters 2-4 with its parallel: at the first sign, Jesus turned water into wine in the bridegroom's honor; at the second, his word from a distance brings a son back from the brink of death. Both signs work through trust in his word ("do whatever he tells you," 2:5; "the man believed the word that Jesus spoke," 4:50). Watch the steady widening: Galilean wedding guests, then a Pharisaic teacher of Israel, then a Samaritan town, then a probably-Gentile royal household — the *kosmos* of 3:16 receiving its Savior in concentric expansion.
