# Deuteronomy 7: The Seven Nations and Election — Not Because You Were More Numerous

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Deuteronomy 7 sits within the first great sermon of Moses on the plains of Moab, the second-generation Israelites preparing to cross the Jordan. The book's Hebrew title is *Dĕbārîm* ("words"); "Deuteronomy" comes from the LXX's *Deuteronomion* ("second law"), a reading probably derived from 17:18's "copy of this law." The traditional structural map — 1:1-4:43 prologue, 4:44-11:32 first sermon, 12-26 law code, 27-28 blessings and curses, 29-30 covenant renewal, 31-34 closing — places chapter 7 in the heart of the sermon that begins with the Decalogue (5), builds to the Shema (6), and turns in chapter 7 to the hardest question for any people about to take land: what they are to do with those who live there.

Dating remains contested. De Wette (1805) placed Deuteronomy at 622 BC, identifying it as the scroll found under Josiah. Weinfeld (*Deuteronomy 1-11*, AB, 1991; *Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School*, 1972) saw in Deuteronomy's loyalty rhetoric the imprint of neo-Assyrian treaty and *adê* texts — especially the Esarhaddon Succession Treaty (672 BC), which demands exclusive devotion to a suzerain and lists curses in language that parallels Deut 28. Peter Craigie (NICOT, 1976), Kenneth Kitchen, and Meredith Kline argued the second-millennium Hittite vassal treaty is the closer fit — preamble, historical prologue, stipulations, document clause, witnesses, blessings/curses — which places the core document earlier. For chapter 7 specifically, the *ḥērem* warfare ideology has been compared to Mesha's Moabite Stone (ninth-century BC), where King Mesha devotes Israelite towns to Chemosh with cognate language. The ANE *ḥērem* institution was not a Hebrew innovation; it was how small states in the Iron Age narrated sacral warfare. Richard Nelson (OTL, 2002) and Duane Christensen (WBC, 2001) treat the chapter's core as reflecting a long memory of conquest idealism that the Deuteronomic school edited into its present program.

The seven nations listed in v. 1 — Hittites, Girgashites, Amorites, Canaanites, Perizzites, Hivites, Jebusites — are largely a literary-theological typology. The actual late-Bronze inhabitants of Canaan were far more diverse, and several of these names refer to groups better attested elsewhere (the Hittites proper were an Anatolian empire; "Hittites" in Canaan probably denotes local populations called by Egyptian or Hurrian names). Archaeologically, Iron I highland settlement — the so-called "Israelite" villages of the Shiloh, Bethel, and Shechem hill country, surveyed by Adam Zertal, Israel Finkelstein, and others from the 1970s onward — shows a rural, pastoral population emerging without evidence of the mass destruction Joshua-Judges suggests. This archaeological picture is one reason why many scholars (including Chris Wright, McConville, and Block) argue that Deuteronomy 7's rhetoric must be read as covenantal-theological, not as a reportage of campaigns.

The Ketef Hinnom silver amulets, discovered in 1979 southwest of Jerusalem and dated paleographically to the late seventh century BC, quote a form of the Aaronic blessing (Num 6) but also echo Deuteronomy's language of "keeping covenant and steadfast love" — the exact phrase of Deut 7:9. This is one of the earliest material witnesses to the theological vocabulary of the chapter. Qumran has yielded multiple copies of Deuteronomy (4QDeut-a through 4QDeut-n), making it the most abundantly attested Pentateuchal book at the site after Genesis.

Three Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. *Ḥērem* (חרם, v. 2) is the verb-and-noun complex translated "devoted to complete destruction" — the sacral ban, rooted in the idea that an object or enemy is transferred entirely to the deity and therefore removed from ordinary use. *Bāḥar* (בחר, vv. 6-7) — "chose" — is the election verb that Deuteronomy uses for YHWH's action toward Israel and, elsewhere, for the place YHWH chooses for his name. *Sĕgullâ* (סגלה, v. 6) — "treasured possession" — is a loan word with Akkadian cognates (*sikiltu*), referring to a royal treasury or private cache; it places Israel in the position of an Ancient Near Eastern king's personal treasure, distinct from the rest of his holdings.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is covenantal paraenesis bending toward two poles — a harsh opening on *ḥērem* warfare (vv. 1-5, 16-26) and a tender middle on election (vv. 6-16). The structural heart of the chapter is a chiasm: commands about the nations (vv. 1-5) frame election rhetoric (vv. 6-11), which frames promise (vv. 12-16), which is framed again by warfare instruction (vv. 17-26). Daniel Block and Christopher Wright both read the chapter's center of gravity at verses 7-8: "It was not because you were more numerous... but because the LORD loves you."

Literary devices cluster around the verb *ʾāhēb* ("love"). It appears in multiple voices: Israel is to love YHWH (implicit from chapter 6's Shema), YHWH loves Israel (v. 8), YHWH keeps covenant with those who love him (v. 9), YHWH will love-bless-multiply (v. 13). As Moran argued in 1963 and Weinfeld developed, this is treaty-*ahavah* — the language of covenantal loyalty. But the reversal in v. 8 — that YHWH's love has no prior ground in Israel's merit — is not standard treaty rhetoric. Suzerains do not typically announce that they chose a vassal out of affection unrelated to the vassal's qualifications. This is a striking moment where Deuteronomy adapts its genre to say something genre-transcending.

Merism shows up in v. 9's "to a thousand generations" paired with v. 10's "to their face" (immediate repayment) — a rhetorical stretch from maximal duration to immediate presence, making the covenant's steadfast love both long and near. Verse 7's startling self-deprecation — "you were the fewest of all peoples" — reverses the expected boast of a conquering people. Verse 22's "little by little" qualifies the language of total dispossession in v. 2, introducing a gradualism in tension with the absolute *ḥērem* command. McConville argues this internal tension is deliberate: the chapter's rhetoric refuses simple application.

Textual variants are modest. 4QDeut-k² preserves portions of 7:1-4 with MT-consistent text. The Samaritan Pentateuch expands v. 5 with language drawn from Exodus 23. LXX Deuteronomy slightly softens v. 2's "show no mercy" with a paraphrase. These minor witnesses show some early readers already grappling with the chapter's hard edges.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish reception.** *Sifre Deuteronomy* (piska 76-96) treats the chapter with careful legal attention. The rabbis read the prohibition of intermarriage (vv. 3-4) broadly, as a principle extended beyond the seven Canaanite nations (who in any case, the Talmud argues, no longer existed as identifiable peoples after Sennacherib's exilic mixings — *Berakhot* 28a, *Yadayim* 4.4). Maimonides codifies this in *Mishneh Torah*, *Hilchot Issurei Biah* 12, making the intermarriage prohibition a general principle while acknowledging the seven nations are gone. Rashi on v. 2 quotes the Sifre's "make no covenant" — *lō tikrōt lāhem bĕrît* — and links it to *lō tĕḥonnēm* (v. 2) in rabbinic tradition extended to not granting permanent settlement rights to idolaters in the land. Ibn Ezra takes v. 7 as the key: Israel is not elected for merit, a theme he traces through the book. Ramban on v. 8 links divine love to the oath to the patriarchs — election is ultimately anchored in Abraham.

Judah Halevi's *Kuzari* (1.95-109) treats this chapter's election rhetoric as the foundation of Israel's unique religious vocation: not a claim of racial superiority but of prophetic inheritance, inseparable from the land. Maimonides (*Guide* 3.32) interprets the gradual dispossession of v. 22 as divine pedagogy — God accommodates human capacity, changing practice by stages rather than all at once, a principle he extends to the whole sacrificial system.

Modern Jewish engagement has not shied from the chapter's hardest demands. Jeffrey Tigay's JPS commentary explicitly contrasts Deuteronomy 7's *ḥērem* with the more limited rules of 20:10-15; he takes the difference as theological — the seven nations are a special case, not a general doctrine of ethnic warfare. Jon Levenson (*Sinai and Zion*) places election in the context of covenantal particularity, arguing that universalism and particularism both are present in the Hebrew Bible. Nehama Leibowitz's *Studies in Deuteronomy* devotes careful essays to the chapter. Abraham Joshua Heschel preaches against the exploitation of election rhetoric for political power. Jonathan Sacks (*Covenant and Conversation*, "Va'etchanan" and "Eikev") treats election as responsibility rather than privilege. Yeshayahu Leibowitz notoriously argued that Jewish election imposes extreme humility, not superiority.

**Christian reception.** Patristic readings of Deuteronomy 7 are often allegorical — not evasion so much as the standard exegetical method. Origen's *Homilies on Joshua* and *Homilies on Numbers* (the latter touches on parallel material) take the seven nations as seven vices the soul must destroy: pride, avarice, lust, anger, gluttony, envy, sloth. This spiritual reading becomes standard through the medieval tradition. Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job* extends the trope. Augustine treats the *ḥērem* passages in *City of God* 1.21 and *Questions on the Heptateuch*, arguing that the wars of conquest were uniquely authorized divine acts under the old covenant, not templates. Aquinas (*ST* II-II q. 40 a. 1; q. 64 a. 6) argues that the *ḥērem* was a specific divine command, not a general principle of just war.

Reformation readings split. Luther's preaching on Deuteronomy treats the chapter's election (vv. 6-9) as an anchor for the Christian doctrine of unconditional grace — "not because you were more numerous" becomes a proof text for *sola gratia*. Calvin's *Commentary on Deuteronomy* and *Institutes* 3.21-24 read the chapter as foundational to the doctrine of election; Calvin does not soften the *ḥērem* but locates it in the specific history of conquest. Jonathan Edwards preaches on election with Deuteronomy 7:7-8 as recurrent text, making the chapter an evangelical centerpiece in his soteriology.

Critical scholarship reshapes the picture. De Wette's Josianic dating makes chapter 7 part of a seventh-century program to purify cult. Wellhausen's documentary hypothesis treats Deuteronomic theology as a distinctive late strand. Weinfeld's Assyrian parallels suggest the chapter borrows imperial rhetoric for anti-imperial ends. Norbert Lohfink and Thomas Römer treat the *ḥērem* rhetoric as largely theological fiction — no such campaigns happened; the rhetoric is edited memory aimed at Josianic reform. This critical reading, controversial in earlier decades, is now broadly accepted in the mainstream guild. Nelson (OTL) and Christensen (WBC) take this approach.

Contemporary ethical engagement is fraught. Regina Schwartz's *The Curse of Cain* (1997) argued that biblical monotheism is itself the root of violent exclusion. Philip Jenkins's *Laying Down the Sword* (2011) confronts the passages directly. Phyllis Trible's *Texts of Terror* (1984) does not treat Deuteronomy 7 but provides the hermeneutical vocabulary. R. S. Sugirtharajah's postcolonial readings ask whose interests the conquest narratives have served in modern colonial contexts. Walter Brueggemann (AOTC) names the chapter's violence honestly and refuses to domesticate it. Ellen Davis (*Opening Israel's Scriptures*) reads it within the land theology of Deuteronomy as a whole, where Israel is never owner but tenant.

Evangelical and confessional responses deserve care. Kenneth Kitchen (*On the Reliability of the Old Testament*) argues the *ḥērem* rhetoric is ANE hyperbolic convention and the archaeological minimalism fits Deuteronomic rhetoric's own "little by little" (v. 22). Christopher Wright (*The God I Don't Understand*, NIBC) presents three framings: the Amorites' "400-year" iniquity as divine patience finally exhausted (Gen 15:16); the uniqueness of the conquest as one-time covenantal judgment; and the eschatological reversal in which Israel's election exists for the nations' blessing (Gen 12:3). Paul Copan (*Is God a Moral Monster?*) defends the hyperbole reading and notes the command is religious (destroy altars, idols, asherim) more than ethnic. John Howard Yoder's *The Original Revolution* and *The Politics of Jesus* treat Deuteronomic warfare as subverted and recapitulated in Jesus' nonviolent messianism.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Both Jewish and Christian traditions affirm the election language of vv. 6-8 as foundational — Maimonides' unmerited covenant, Calvin's unconditional election, Luther's *sola gratia*, Sacks' responsibility of being chosen. Both traditions struggle with the *ḥērem* and produce similar moves: the rabbinic argument that the seven nations no longer exist; the patristic allegorization into vices; the critical argument that the rhetoric is theological memory not historical program; the ethical argument that the *ḥērem* is unique and not a template. They diverge on whether election is perpetual (Jewish, including modern religious Zionism) or has been universalized in Messiah (Christian). Paul's argument in Romans 9-11 explicitly works with Deuteronomy 7's election theology — "not because of works" (Rom 9:11) is a Pauline echo of "not because you were more numerous" — to construct a theology where Gentile inclusion does not erase Israel's election but depends on it. That Romans 9-11 argument is the place where Jewish-Christian conversation on Deuteronomy 7 most intensely continues.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: How should the *ḥērem* command be read?** The text demands total destruction of seven nations (v. 2) with no covenant and no mercy. Three families of reading compete.

Position A: Historical hyperbole. Kitchen, Copan, many evangelicals. ANE war rhetoric regularly speaks of total destruction where the archaeology shows limited campaigns; Egyptian, Assyrian, and Moabite inscriptions all do this. The command is real as religious separation (destroy cult objects, do not intermarry) but the genocidal surface is convention, not a literal campaign ledger.

Position B: Unique divine action, not template. Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin, Chris Wright. The *ḥērem* was one-time covenantal judgment on peoples whose wickedness had filled up (Gen 15:16's "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete"). It is not a principle of warfare and does not authorize any analogue. Reading it as template is a hermeneutical error.

Position C: Theological fiction retrojected. Nelson, Römer, Lohfink, much mainstream criticism. The rhetoric is seventh-century Deuteronomic program aimed at purifying late-monarchic Judean cult, using conquest memory rhetorically. Taking the command as historical campaign mandate misreads the genre.

The least intuitive, and hardest to steelman for a faith reader, is the theological-fiction reading. But it deserves serious engagement. The archaeology does not support the campaigns the command presupposes; Joshua's own narrative contradicts itself (wholesale destruction in ch. 10-11 vs. survival of peoples in ch. 13-23); and the Deuteronomic editorial fingerprints are pervasive. If the rhetoric is theological memory working late, then the question of the command's ethics shifts: what was the rhetoric *for*, if not licensing actual genocide? The most generous reading says the rhetoric aimed at cultic purity in Josiah's Jerusalem — keep the idols out of the house — but that still leaves the text with a stain no allegory fully removes.

**Where this leaves us:** Each position captures something real — ANE rhetorical convention, covenantal uniqueness, Deuteronomic editorial artistry — and each leaves residual difficulty. The honest reader should hold all three in view and refuse to domesticate the text with any single move. Brueggemann's discipline of not looking away is part of the chapter's theological work.

**Tension 2: What makes election moral?** If YHWH elects Israel "not because you were more numerous" (v. 7), on what grounds *is* election granted, and why is this not arbitrary preference?

Position A: Election is sheer grace. Luther, Calvin, Edwards. There is no ground in Israel; the ground is in YHWH's own love. "He loves you because he loves you" (v. 8, paraphrased). This is the classical Protestant reading and the heart of the doctrine of grace.

Position B: Election is purposive. Chris Wright, Bauckham, Sacks. Israel is chosen *for* the nations (Gen 12:3, Isaiah 49:6, Exodus 19:5-6's "kingdom of priests"); election is a commissioning, not a privilege. This reading makes election ethical by making it instrumental — chosen to bless others.

Position C: Election is relational-covenantal, anchored in promise. Ramban, Aquinas on divine covenants, Tigay. The ground is YHWH's oath to the patriarchs (v. 8b). Election is not arbitrary because it is promise-keeping; the moral grammar is fidelity.

The least intuitive is Position B — that election is instrumental rather than preferential. Here is its steelman: if "chosen" means burdened with a vocation for others' sake, then election is not privilege but service, and the logic of 7:6-8 is not tribalism but responsibility. The Hebrew *sĕgullâ* is a king's *treasured* possession, but a king's treasury exists for the realm's sake. Read this way, election answers the moral objection by dissolving it: Israel is not preferred over other nations; Israel is conscripted on behalf of them. The chapter's jarring juxtaposition of *ḥērem* and election then takes on a tragic irony — a people chosen to bless nations is commanded to destroy certain nations. The tension is not resolved in the chapter, and the subsequent canon (Isaiah, Paul) works it out over centuries.

**Where this leaves us:** All three readings — pure grace, vocation, covenant fidelity — are present in the text. Protestant tradition has overweighted A; Jewish tradition has held B and C together as covenantal purpose; Christian theology gains depth from holding all three.

**Unresolved question:** If the *ḥērem* rhetoric is read as hyperbole or as theological fiction, the moral sting is blunted — but what does that do to the authority of the command? If it is read as unique divine action or as literal mandate, the sting is preserved but the theological cost is enormous. Is there a reading that keeps the text's authority without either evading its ethics or authorizing its replication?

## The Thread

Four non-obvious cross-references. (1) Deuteronomy 7:9's "keeps covenant and steadfast love" is quoted nearly verbatim in Nehemiah 1:5, Daniel 9:4, and 1 Kings 8:23 — it is a liturgical formula that shaped post-exilic Jewish prayer across the centuries and is the cadence preserved on one of the Ketef Hinnom silver amulets from seventh-century Jerusalem. (2) Deuteronomy 7:6's "*sĕgullâ*" reappears in Exodus 19:5 and is picked up by Malachi 3:17 ("on the day I make up my treasured possession") and, strikingly, by 1 Peter 2:9 where Peter applies the language to the church — one of the New Testament's most direct transfers of Deuteronomic election vocabulary to a Gentile-inclusive body. (3) Paul's argument in Romans 9-11 works systematically through Deuteronomy 7's theology of unconditional election; Romans 9:11 ("not because of works but because of him who calls") is a clear echo of Deut 7:7-8. (4) Deuteronomy 7:22's "little by little" is quoted in Exodus 23:29-30, where the same gradualism appears; together they form the internal biblical qualifier to the *ḥērem* maximalism and are the textual basis for Maimonides' doctrine (*Guide* 3.32) of divine accommodation to human capacity — an interpretive move with enormous afterlife in both Jewish and Christian philosophy of religion.
