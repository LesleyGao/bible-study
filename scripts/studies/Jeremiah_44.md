# Jeremiah 44: The Last Oracle, the Queen of Heaven, and the Book's Ending in Egypt

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political situation**: Chapter 44 contains *the latest dated prophecy of Jeremiah's career* — the prophet's final canonical oracle, delivered in Egypt to the Jewish diaspora communities scattered across four named settlements: Migdol, Tahpanhes, Memphis (Hebrew Noph, Egyptian Mn-nfr), and the land of Pathros (Upper Egypt, the Egyptian *Pa-Ta-Resy*, "the southern land"). The chapter is plausibly dated to the late 580s or early 570s BC, perhaps a decade after the forced descent of chapter 43. Pharaoh Hophra (Apries, Wahibre) — named explicitly in 44:30 as the king to be given into the hand of his enemies — reigned c. 589–570 BC and was overthrown and killed by his general Ahmose II (Amasis) in a coup that Herodotus (*Histories* 2.161–69) describes in vivid narrative detail. The fulfillment of 44:30 occurs c. 570 BC, when Apries marched against Cyrene, lost his Egyptian troops to Amasis's revolt, and was eventually killed (Herodotus says strangled by the Egyptian crowd, c. 567 BC). The oracle's conditional logic — "as I gave Zedekiah into the hand of Nebuchadnezzar" — depends on the prophecy being delivered while Hophra still reigned, that is, before 570 BC.

**Material culture**: The four named diaspora settlements are all attested archaeologically. **Migdol** (Hebrew מִגְדֹּל, Egyptian *Mktr*) is the eastern Delta fortress identified with Tell el-Heir or Tell Qedua, excavated by Eliezer Oren beginning in 1972; it controlled the northern road of the Way of Horus into Sinai. **Tahpanhes** is Tell Defenneh (Petrie 1886, *Tanis II: Nebesheh and Defenneh*). **Memphis (Noph)** is the great traditional capital at Saqqara/Mit Rahina; Jewish funerary stelae in Aramaic and Hebrew from Saqqara, dated 4th–3rd c. BC, attest the long-running Jewish presence in Memphis through the Persian and Hellenistic periods. **Pathros** is Upper Egypt — the south country, including Thebes (No-Amon) and the southern reaches of the Nile valley. The most consequential archaeological corpus for chapter 44 is the **Elephantine papyri** from the Jewish military colony at Yeb (Aswan), discovered between 1893 and 1908 and edited principally in A. E. Cowley's *Aramaic Papyri of the Fifth Century B.C.* (Oxford, 1923) and Bezalel Porten's *Archives from Elephantine* (Berkeley, 1968). These Aramaic papyri document, *for the very Egyptian Jewish military diaspora chapter 44 addresses*, a continuing syncretistic cult: alongside YHWH (rendered *YHW* in the Aramaic papyri), the Yeb community worshipped *Anat-Yahu* and *Anat-Bethel* and made oaths by these compound deities. The Cowley 22 letter (the famous letter requesting the rebuilding of the YHW temple at Yeb) and especially Cowley 30 (the letter to Bagoas, governor of Judah) make clear that the Jewish military colony's cultic life was syncretistic in precisely the way Jeremiah's chapter denounces — *with documentary evidence dated only about 130 years after the prophet's preaching*. This makes chapter 44 one of the few prophetic oracles whose target audience is documentarily attested by extra-biblical evidence on the very point of denunciation.

**Authorship & dating**: The chapter is the longest sustained prose oracle in the Egyptian-diaspora unit (chapters 40–44) and contains the latest dated material in the canonical book. It is biographical/oratorical "B source" prose with characteristic Deuteronomistic vocabulary — *abominations*, *provoke to anger*, *walk in the law*, *the voice of the LORD*. The MT and LXX of chapter 44 differ in some details and in verse division; the LXX is generally shorter (the LXX of Jeremiah is famously about one-eighth shorter than the MT overall, and the prose chapters preserve some of that compression). Lundbom (Anchor) defends the chapter's substantial historicity. Holladay (Hermeneia) reads it through Deuteronomistic editorial framing. Carroll (OTL) is more skeptical and treats the women's speech as a literary set-piece. McKane (ICC), Bright, Stulman, Brueggemann, and especially Kathleen O'Connor (*Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*, 2011) read it as historically rooted; O'Connor's trauma-theology reading takes the women's speech with unusual seriousness as authentic theological argument from inside catastrophe.

**Archaeological & textual evidence**: The Elephantine papyri (Cowley 1923; Porten 1968; the more recent Porten-Yardeni *Textbook of Aramaic Documents from Ancient Egypt*) for the syncretistic cult Jeremiah denounces. Tell Qedua / Tell el-Heir for Migdol (Oren 1972ff.). Tell Defenneh for Tahpanhes. Saqqara funerary stelae for the Memphis diaspora. The Beni Hasan tomb paintings for the longue durée of Semitic settlement in Egypt. The Khirbet el-Qom tomb inscription ("Uriah… YHWH and his Asherah," 8th c. BC) and the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions (early 8th c. BC, Sinai) for the *pre-exilic* archaeological evidence of Israelite syncretism — directly relevant to the historicity question the women's speech raises. Herodotus *Histories* 2.161–69 for Hophra's overthrow.

**Key terms**: *meleket ha-shamayim* (מְלֶכֶת הַשָּׁמַיִם, "Queen of Heaven," 44:17–25) — the disputed cultic title; the consonantal text could also be read *melekhet*, "work/work-of," which a few medieval commentators preferred. *qatar* (קָטַר, "burn incense, make offerings," used eighteen times in this chapter) — the cult-act of pouring out drink offerings and burning incense to the goddess. *Tahpanhes* (תַּחְפַּנְחֵס), *Migdol* (מִגְדֹּל), *Noph/Memphis* (נֹף), *Pathros* (פַּתְרוֹס) — the four Egyptian Jewish diaspora settlements. *qiyyem* (קִיֵּם, "perform, fulfill," 44:25) — the same root used for fulfilling vows; YHWH's bitter command "perform your vows" turns the women's vow-language back as judgment.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is unusually structured for a prophetic oracle: it is a *dialogue*. Verses 1–14 are Jeremiah's opening oracle. Verses 15–19 are the people's collective answer — and crucially, the women's answer, with the men implicated as those "who knew that their wives had made offerings to other gods." Verses 20–28 are Jeremiah's response. Verses 29–30 are the closing sign-oracle with Hophra's death as confirmation. The dialogue structure is rare in the prophetic corpus; only Malachi's *disputation* form approaches it. This makes chapter 44 unusually valuable as a window into how the prophet's audience actually argued back — and the women's speech in 44:17–19 is one of the very few moments in the Hebrew Bible where the cultic *opposition* gets to articulate its own theological position in its own voice.

The women's argument (44:17–19) is theologically sophisticated. They make an *empirical* case: when we worshipped the queen of heaven, we had plenty; when we stopped, we lacked. They invoke *family tradition* — "we and our fathers, our kings and our officials." They invoke *the streets of Jerusalem* as the practice's setting (a claim the Khirbet el-Qom and Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions and the late monarchic prophetic critiques in Jer 7:16–20 corroborate). And they invoke *husbands' approval* (44:19) as the legal-religious frame within which their cult-action occurred. This is not the speech of *defeated* paganism; it is the speech of a coherent counter-theology with its own history, its own evidence, and its own explanation for the catastrophe. Jeremiah's response (44:21–28) does not engage the empirical claim directly; he reframes the historical record (the catastrophe was *because of* the syncretistic cult, not in spite of it) and counter-asserts divine sovereignty through the Hophra sign-oracle.

Devices include the dialogic structure, the formulaic four-place geographic frame (Migdol-Tahpanhes-Noph-Pathros), the heavy *qatar* repetition (eighteen times — driving home the cultic specificity), the bitter imperative *perform your vows!* (44:25), the divine self-oath "I have sworn by my great name" (44:26), and the closing fulfillment-sign tied to a named living Pharaoh. MT and LXX differ in some details — the LXX is somewhat shorter and orders the diaspora settlements differently. Carroll and others have argued some material is later editorial expansion.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Chapter 44 is the *canonical book's effective ending*. Chapter 45 is a brief private oracle to Baruch dated to a much earlier period (Jehoiakim's fourth year). Chapters 46–51 are the oracles against the nations, gathered from various dates. Chapter 52 is a historical appendix repeating 2 Kings 24–25. The narrative arc of Jeremiah's life and ministry concludes with chapter 44 — a final oracle to a syncretistic diaspora that refuses him, with no recorded conversion, no Babylonian repatriation, no resolution. This ending is *theologically scandalous*. The prophet of new covenant (Jer 31:31–34) ends preaching to a people who have explicitly rejected the LORD's name and who articulate a coherent counter-theology in defense of goddess worship. The prophet's final fate is unknown — Christian apocryphal traditions (the *Lives of the Prophets*, the *Paralipomena of Jeremiah*) report his stoning by his own people in Egypt, but the canonical text leaves him preaching into a void.

**Jewish trajectory**: Sirach 49:6–7 names Jeremiah's career in retrospect. The Bavli (Megillah 14b–15a, Bava Batra 14b–15a) discusses the prophet's authorship and life. Targum Jonathan paraphrases the women's speech, sharpening the theological condemnation. Rashi on 44:17 notes the *meleket ha-shamayim* reading dispute and treats the cult as Astarte. Radak develops the historical argument that the women's empirical claim (we prospered when we worshipped her) is *mistaken in its causal attribution* — the prosperity correlated with goddess-worship was not caused by it. Ibn Ezra is grammatically careful on the *meleket* / *melekhet* question. Abrabanel reads the women's speech as the diaspora's perennial temptation to attribute prosperity to syncretistic accommodation rather than covenant fidelity. The medieval Jewish prayer tradition's renunciation of *avodah zarah* (idolatry) in the *aleinu* prayer carries forward the chapter's theological stake. The Karaite-Rabbanite divide in the medieval period sharpens around precisely the kind of cultic heterogeneity chapter 44 documents.

**Christian trajectory**: Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah* (especially the homilies on the prose chapters) read the prophet's ending in Egypt as *kenotic* — the prophet who proclaimed YHWH's word ends emptied into the place of bondage, prefiguring Christ's apparent failure on the cross. Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah* (ad loc.) is geographically and historically focused; he discusses the four Egyptian settlements with characteristic care and reads Hophra's overthrow against Herodotus. Augustine in *De civitate Dei* uses the chapter's providence-doctrine. Aquinas (*Summa* II-II q.94 on idolatry) cites the chapter as a paradigm case of *mala consuetudo*, evil custom. Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah* spends extensive attention on the women's speech — Calvin treats it as the most candid expression of pagan rationality in the Hebrew Bible and uses it to argue that the *post hoc ergo propter hoc* causal reasoning of the women is the universal logic of fallen religion. Critical scholarship since Duhm has read the chapter as Deuteronomistic editorial framing of a difficult diaspora reality. Mark Smith (*The Origins of Biblical Monotheism*, 2001; *The Early History of God*, 2nd ed. 2002), Susan Ackerman (*Under Every Green Tree: Popular Religion in Sixth-Century Judah*, 1992), and Patrick Miller (*The Religion of Ancient Israel*, 2000) have used the Elephantine evidence and the inscriptional record to argue that pre-exilic Israelite religion was substantially syncretistic in its actual practice — a critical reframing that makes the women's empirical claim historically defensible. Albertz's two-volume *History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period* (1994) develops the same case at length. O'Connor's trauma-theology reading takes the women's speech as authentic theological argument from inside catastrophe — they are not simply pagans but theologically articulate participants in Judah's religious history who have drawn the wrong conclusion from the data.

**Where they diverge**: Christian classical tradition reads the women's speech as paradigmatic fallen theology — empirical reasoning unmoored from covenant. Jewish tradition reads it as the diaspora's recurring temptation. Critical scholarship reads it as historically representative of widespread Iron Age Israelite religion, with the inscriptional record (Khirbet el-Qom, Kuntillet Ajrud, Elephantine) providing direct documentary support. The divergence over the *historicity of pre-exilic syncretism* is one of the sharpest in modern Hebrew Bible scholarship, with consequences for how one reads Deuteronomistic monotheism — as normative-from-the-start (classical view) or as radical post-exilic achievement (Smith, Albertz, Miller).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: the historicity of pre-exilic syncretism.** The chapter's Lens-1 stake is unavoidable. The women in 44:17 say something that the inscriptional and papyrological record substantially confirms: goddess-worship and YHWH-worship co-existed in Iron Age Israel and Judah and persisted into the Egyptian diaspora through the Persian period. The Khirbet el-Qom tomb inscription's "Uriah… YHWH and his Asherah" (Late 8th c. BC), the Kuntillet Ajrud pithos inscriptions ("YHWH of Samaria and his Asherah," "YHWH of Teman and his Asherah," early 8th c. BC), the female pillar figurines pervasive in late monarchic Judah (Kletter's *The Judean Pillar-Figurines*, 1996), and most powerfully the Elephantine papyri's *Anat-Yahu* — all suggest that the women's claim "we and our fathers, our kings and our officials" did not invent a goddess cult but participated in a longstanding, popularly normative, often royally sanctioned practice. Mark Smith's *Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001) and *The Early History of God* argue that strict monotheism is a *post-exilic theological achievement* that Deuteronomistic and prophetic editors retroject onto the earlier history. The classical Christian reading (Augustine through Calvin) treats Old Testament monotheism as normative-from-Sinai and the syncretism as deviation. The Jewish tradition has space for both readings — the prophetic critique tradition (Hosea, Amos, Jeremiah) presupposes widespread popular deviation, while the rabbinic tradition's strong monotheism reads the deviation as *aveirah* (transgression) rather than as alternative theology. Steelman the women's position fairly: they were not making it up. The cult they defend was *historically real, royally sanctioned, and demographically widespread*. The prophet's verdict is not that they are confused about the historical record but that the historical record itself was already a long record of provocation. Levenson's *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son* (1993) develops the longue-durée of monotheism's emergence with comparable nuance. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter forces a hard question about whether canonical theology reads its own past accurately, or whether prophetic monotheism is itself a theological *act* that re-narrates a much messier history into a covenantal frame. Both readings have integrity; neither can be dismissed.

**Tension 2: the canonical book's ending in Egypt.** The book of Jeremiah, as a literary whole, ends — narratively, canonically, theologically — *not* in Babylon (where the redemptive future of Jer 24's good figs is supposed to germinate), *not* in restored Jerusalem (the abandoned land), but in *Egypt* (the *forbidden* re-entry into bondage). The prophet of new covenant ends preaching to a people who refuse him. There is no recorded repentance, no narrative resolution, no Babylonian-return scene to balance the Egyptian descent. This is theologically scandalous. The patristic tradition through Origen reads it typologically: the prophet's apparent failure prefigures Christ's apparent failure on the cross — both end with the word seemingly defeated and the speaker emptied. The Reformed tradition through Calvin reads the ending as the structural vindication of Jer 31:31–34's new covenant logic: the new covenant must come *from God*, not from human response, precisely because the human response in Jer 44 is so absolute a refusal. The Jewish tradition through Tisha B'Av's liturgy of lament without resolution embraces canonical endings that do not close — Lamentations ends "unless you have utterly rejected us," and the synagogue tradition reads it backward to soften the closure. The Orthodox tradition reads the ending through *kenosis* and the patristic theology of the apparently defeated word. Contemporary trauma theology (O'Connor especially) reads the ending as the prophet's vocational truth: prophets may *not* see resolution; their work may be to keep speaking into refusal. Steelman the position one might dismiss: that the canonical ending in Egypt is *itself* the theological point — the book refuses redemptive closure on the prose-narrative level so that the new covenant promise of chapter 31 cannot be cashed out by anything other than divine act. **Where this leaves us:** Jeremiah's canonical shape forces every reading tradition to confront the prophet's failure as theologically constitutive rather than incidental. The new covenant promise stands precisely because it must, given how chapter 44 ends.

**Unresolved question:** What does it mean that the canonical *prophet of new covenant* ends his career being told, by his own people, "we will not listen to you"? Does the failure of the prophet's word among its hearers function, theologically, as itself a kind of testimony? Is the book of Jeremiah ending in apparent failure in Egypt the indispensable counter-image without which Jer 31's promise of a covenant written on the heart could not carry its full weight?

## The Thread

The Queen of Heaven cult threads from Jer 7:16–20 (the same denunciation, in pre-exilic Jerusalem, with the same vocabulary of *making cakes for the queen of heaven*) into Jer 44 and forward to Acts 19:23–41's Artemis cult at Ephesus, where Paul confronts a deeply embedded female-deity civic religion with precisely the same structural challenge Jeremiah faces in Egypt — and where, like Jeremiah, the apostle is met with collective refusal. The forbidden return to Egypt threads from Deut 17:16 and Hos 8:13 into Jer 43–44's literal violation and forward to Matthew 2:13–15's redemptive reversal. The book's ending in apparent prophetic failure links to the Servant Songs' rejected servant (Isa 53:3, "despised and rejected"), to Ezekiel's audience that "hears your words and will not do them" (Ezek 33:32), and forward to John 1:11 ("his own people did not receive him"). The Hophra sign-oracle (44:30) links to Jer 39:5–7's Zedekiah judgment as YHWH's parallel handling of disobedient kings on both sides of the Jordan-Nile axis.
