# 1 Kings 11: The Apostasy and the Tearing of the Kingdom

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter records the dtr's central indictment of Solomon and introduces the three adversaries — Hadad of Edom, Rezon of Damascus, and Jeroboam of Ephraim — whose careers reshape the tenth-century geopolitical map of the Levant. The political-historical material in verses 14-40 is unusually dense with extra-biblical synchronisms.

Hadad the Edomite (verses 14-22) belongs to a recognizable Iron Age I-IIA pattern of Egyptian asylum for displaced Levantine royal houses; the Tale of Sinuhe (Twelfth Dynasty) and the later Bay/Beya of Late Bronze Age Ugarit attest the type. The narrative's claim that "Pharaoh king of Egypt... gave him in marriage the sister of his own wife, the sister of Tahpenes the queen" (11:19) is a precise piece of court-marriage diplomacy. Tahpenes (*Taḥpənēs*, תַּחְפְּנֵס) is plausibly Egyptian *Tȝ-ḥm.t-nsw*, "the king's wife," a title rather than a personal name; Kenneth Kitchen and Donald Redford have offered competing reconstructions placing her at the late Twenty-First Dynasty court (Siamun or Psusennes II), broadly contemporary with the Solomonic horizon. Edom itself in this period is documented archaeologically: the copper-mining sites of Khirbat en-Nahas in the Wadi Faynan, excavated by Thomas Levy and Mohammad Najjar, have produced Iron IIA radiocarbon dates that demonstrate substantial polity-level industrial activity in tenth-ninth-century Edom — confirming that "the royal house in Edom" (11:14) is not anachronistic.

Rezon (*Rəzôn*, רְזוֹן, also *Razon*) of Damascus (verses 23-25) provides a second synchronism. The text says he had "fled from his master Hadadezer king of Zobah" — the same Hadadezer defeated by David in 2 Samuel 8 and 10. Rezon is plausibly the founder of the Aramean Damascus dynasty that produced Hadad-Idri (the *Adad-idri* of Shalmaneser III's monolith, hence the Bar-Hadad of biblical Aramean kings). Wayne Pitard's *Ancient Damascus* and the more recent scholarship of K. Lawson Younger Jr. (*A Political History of the Arameans*) treat the Damascus polity as emerging precisely in this Solomonic-to-early-divided-monarchy window. The Tel Dan stele (mid-ninth century BCE), which references "the king of Israel" and "the house of David" in Old Aramaic, is the materialization of the conflict-relationship Rezon initiates.

Jeroboam son of Nebat (verses 26-40) belongs to the third archaeological synchronism. He flees "to Shishak king of Egypt" (11:40). Shishak (*Šîšaq*, שִׁישַׁק) is the biblical rendering of *Shoshenq I*, founder of the Twenty-Second (Libyan/Bubastite) Dynasty, whose 925 BCE Levantine campaign is recorded on the Bubastite Portal at the Karnak Temple — a relief listing some 154 toponyms across Cisjordan and the Negev, including identifiable Israelite and Judahite sites. This is the single most important external synchronism in the entire Solomonic-Jeroboamic narrative; it places the chapter's events within a tightly bounded chronological window (Shoshenq's reign c. 945-924 BCE, with the campaign in his twenty-first year). Kenneth Kitchen's *The Third Intermediate Period in Egypt*, Karol Mysliwiec's later work, and Israel Finkelstein's debates over the campaign's destructions at Megiddo, Rehov, and elsewhere all turn on the chapter's Shishak reference.

The cultic geography of the chapter is precise. The "mountain east of Jerusalem" (11:7) is the Mount of Olives; 2 Kings 23:13 later names it "*har ha-mašḥît*" (הַר הַמַּשְׁחִית), the "Mount of Corruption" — a punning intentional substitution for *har ha-mišḥâ*, "Mount of Anointing/Oil," that 2 Kings makes explicit when Josiah destroys what Solomon built. Chemosh (*Kəmôš*, כְּמוֹשׁ) is the chief Moabite deity, attested in the Mesha Stele (mid-ninth century BCE, Louvre) where King Mesha credits Chemosh with the recovery of Moabite territory from Israel. Milcom/Molech (*Milkōm*, מִלְכֹּם, with possible vocalization play on *Mōlek*) is the Ammonite chief deity; the text appears to deliberately confuse the personal name *mlk* ("king") with the deity to indicate the dtr's contempt. Heath Dewrell, John Day, and George Heider have all written on the Molech-Milcom complex and the disputed connection to child-sacrifice rites attested in the Tophet at Carthage and at South Phoenician sites. Ashtoreth (*ʿAštōret*, עַשְׁתֹּרֶת — likely a deliberate Masoretic vocalization of *ʿaštart* with the vowels of *bōšet*, "shame") is the Sidonian Astarte, well-attested in Ugaritic and Phoenician texts. Mark S. Smith's *The Early History of God* is the standard reference for the Levantine pantheon.

Hebrew terms beyond the deities. The "high places" (*bāmôt*, בָּמוֹת, verse 7) are the cultic platforms condemned throughout the dtr. *Sārâ* (שָׂטָן, "adversary," verses 14, 23, 25) is the same word later transliterated as "Satan"; here it carries no demonic charge but means "political opponent raised up by God." The "forced labor" (*mas*, מַס, verse 28) over which Jeroboam was set is the corvée system by which Solomon built the Millo and the Temple — the same institution Rehoboam refuses to lighten in chapter 12 and the proximate political grievance. *ʾôhăbîm* (אֹהֲבִים, "loved," verse 1-2) is treaty vocabulary as well as personal-affection vocabulary; William Moran's classic 1963 article ("The Ancient Near Eastern Background of the Love of God in Deuteronomy") demonstrated that ANE treaty texts use "love" for political loyalty — Solomon's "love" of foreign women may carry a treaty-political charge alongside the personal one.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is the dtr's structural climax of the Solomon source. Mordechai Cogan in the Anchor Bible volume calls it "the great indictment"; Marvin Sweeney (OTL) treats it as the inevitable terminus of the dialectical praise-and-condemnation begun in chapter 10. Genre-wise, the chapter is a hybrid of theological evaluation (verses 1-13), political-historical adversary-list (14-25, 26-40), and the regnal-closure formula (41-43) that becomes the structural backbone of all subsequent regnal accounts in 1-2 Kings.

The literary fingerprints of the dtr are everywhere. Verse 1's opening "King Solomon loved many foreign women" deliberately echoes Deuteronomy 7:3-4's prohibition on intermarriage with the Canaanite peoples and Deuteronomy 17:17's prohibition on multiplying wives. The numerical extravagance (700 wives, 300 concubines) is rhetorical hyperbole — Sternberg and Alter both note the figures function as totalizing condemnation, not as census record. The phrase "his heart was not wholly true to YHWH his God, as was the heart of David his father" (verse 4) introduces the comparative formula by which all subsequent kings will be evaluated against David — David becomes the dtr's measuring rod. The chapter establishes the regnal evaluation formula's full vocabulary: *šālēm ʿim YHWH* ("wholly with YHWH"), *hāyâ libbô ʾel YHWH* ("his heart was toward YHWH"), *ʿāśâ hayyāšār bə-ʿênê YHWH* ("did what was right in YHWH's eyes").

Robert Polzin in *David and the Deuteronomist* and the *1 Samuel-2 Kings* volumes argues that the chapter performs the dtr's signature compositional move: it stages the king's downfall as the predictable outworking of his wisdom-failure. Solomon's wisdom in chapter 10 cannot save him from the entanglements his diplomacy produces — knowledge without obedience is the dtr's recurrent dialectic. Walsh's *1 Kings* (Berit Olam) attends to the chapter's structural symmetry: the apostasy section (1-13) is matched by the three-adversary section (14-40), and the prophetic word of judgment (11-13) is paralleled by the prophetic word to Jeroboam (29-39).

The chapter's textual transmission is unusually complex, and this is *the* chapter for which LXX variants are meaning-affecting. The Old Greek of 3 Kingdoms 11 reorders Solomonic material substantially: in MT, the apostasy-narrative (1-13) precedes the adversary list (14-40); in LXX, elements of the apostasy material are differently distributed, and material about Hadad and Jeroboam appears in shifted positions. Most strikingly, LXX preserves what Julio Trebolle Barrera and Adrian Schenker call the "Alternative Jeroboam Narrative" — supplementary material about Jeroboam's flight and prophetic mandate that appears at 12:24a-z in LXX numbering and offers a substantially different account of the Jeroboam tradition's pre-history. Schenker's *Septante et texte massorétique dans l'histoire la plus ancienne du texte de 1 Rois 2-14* (2000) is the major treatment; Knoppers in *Two Nations Under God* and Anneli Aejmelaeus's work on LXX Kings build on it. The methodological upshot: at certain points the LXX preserves an earlier Hebrew Vorlage that the MT has reworked, and the variant ordering reflects competing dtr editorial logics.

The regnal-closure formula in 11:41-43 — "the rest of the acts of Solomon... are they not written in the Book of the Acts of Solomon? ... and Solomon slept with his fathers and was buried in the city of David his father, and Rehoboam his son reigned in his place" — is the inaugural instance of the formula that will close the account of every king for the next twenty-five chapters. The "Book of the Acts of Solomon" (*sēfer dibrê šəlōmōh*) is one of the dtr's three named source-citations alongside the "Book of the Acts of the Kings of Israel" and "Book of the Acts of the Kings of Judah" — a real or putative court-archive source, the kind of material Albrecht Alt and the Alt-Noth tradition treated as historiographically significant.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The chapter has been a battleground for interpretation in both Jewish and Christian traditions, and the stakes are unusually high because Solomon's status — wisest of men, builder of the Temple, recipient of YHWH's covenant promise — is so exalted that his apostasy raises foundational questions about covenant theology.

In Jewish tradition, the rabbis read Solomon's fall with a mixture of indictment and apology. *b. Shabbat* 56b records the famous dictum: "Whoever says Solomon sinned is mistaken" — a defensive reading that displaces the responsibility onto his wives ("his wives turned away his heart" is read literally — they sinned, not he). Other rabbinic voices in *b. Sanhedrin* 21b and the Mekhilta are far harsher, citing Solomon as a paradigm of how wisdom does not protect against sin. Josephus (*Ant.* 8.190-211) navigates the apostasy carefully, attributing it to advancing age and to "his uncontrolled passion for women." Rashi follows the Talmudic apologetic line where possible, but cannot avoid the text's plain reading on the high places. Radak (David Kimhi) is more willing to take the indictment at face value. Ralbag offers a rationalist gloss: Solomon's failure is intellectual as much as moral — he should have foreseen the political-religious consequences. Don Isaac Abarbanel, whose commentary on Kings is one of the great political-theological readings in the tradition, treats the chapter as a study in the corruption of legitimate kingship by accumulated wealth and treaty-marriages — an analysis informed by his own experience advising the courts of Portugal, Castile, and Naples. Abarbanel argues that Solomon's failure is structural-political: the very institutions that secured his peace (treaty marriages with surrounding kingdoms) carried the seeds of religious compromise, and the kingship's legitimacy could not survive that compromise. Avivah Zornberg's contemporary readings, Moshe Garsiel's literary work, and Yair Zakovitch's source-critical sensibilities all treat the chapter as the deliberately unresolved tragedy of Solomon.

In Christian reception the chapter has been pastorally awkward — the wisest of men becomes an idolater. Origen wrestles with whether Solomon could be saved given his apostasy and inclines toward yes (Solomon's wisdom and Temple-building tip the balance). Augustine in *City of God* 17.20 takes the harder line: Solomon "fell from the wisdom of God" and stands as a warning of how high a man may rise and still fall. Ambrose (*De Apologia David Altera*) and Chrysostom in his homilies treat Solomon's case with rhetorical pathos. Theodoret of Cyrus in his *Quaestiones in Reges* engages the chapter philologically and is more measured. Gregory the Great in *Moralia in Job* uses Solomon as the great cautionary example of pride preceding fall. Aquinas in the *Summa* (II-II, q. 162-165) takes Solomon's case as a question for moral theology: did he repent? Aquinas concludes the question is not decidable from Scripture, an unusually agnostic verdict.

The Reformers wrote extensively. Calvin's *Sermons on 1 Kings* (preached at Geneva 1561-1562, recently edited by Max Engammare) treat the chapter as the parade example of how spiritual gifts do not preserve from sin without continued vigilance — Calvin is uncharacteristically pastoral here, fearful that his hearers will conclude that Solomon's apostasy means election can be lost. Luther's lectures and table talk return to Solomon repeatedly; he tends to read Solomon's repentance into Ecclesiastes (the king's voice in 1:12) and read Ecclesiastes back into 1 Kings 11 as evidence that Solomon was finally saved despite the apostasy.

Modern critical scholarship is dominated by the dtr-redaction debates. Frank Moore Cross's double-redaction theory has the Josianic edition (Dtr1) presenting Solomon ambivalently — apostasy noted but bracketed — and the Exilic edition (Dtr2) sharpening the indictment to explain the destruction of Jerusalem. Richard Nelson's *The Double Redaction of the Deuteronomistic History* refines the source-critical claim. Mordechai Cogan's *I Kings* (AB) is the indispensable philological-historical reference, with detailed treatment of the LXX variants and ANE comparanda. Marvin Sweeney's *I & II Kings* (OTL) reads the chapter as the dtr's theological pivot — the reason for the kingdom's eventual destruction. Gary Knoppers's *Two Nations Under God* is the most theologically serious modern monograph on the chapter; Knoppers argues the dtr's evaluation of Solomon is more nuanced than the standard reading admits, with the apostasy rooted in the very structures of covenantal kingship rather than in personal moral failing alone. Brueggemann's *1 & 2 Kings* and Eugene Peterson's pastoral readings, alongside Iain Provan, Davis (*1 Kings: The Wisdom and the Folly*), and John Goldingay, all dwell on the chapter's pastoral and theological gravity. Jon Levenson's work on Davidic theology (especially *Sinai and Zion*) is essential for the "lamp" promise of verse 36 — David's lamp before YHWH in Jerusalem is a foundational text for messianic and zionic theology.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Is Solomon's apostasy a personal moral failure or a structural inevitability of the imperial monarchy?**

Position one: personal moral failure (much of the patristic and Reformation tradition). Solomon was given more than any other king — wisdom, wealth, peace, the Temple, a direct covenant promise. His fall is the failure of an individual will not to remain "wholly true to YHWH" (verse 4). The chapter is an indictment of his choices: he loved foreign women, he clung to them in love, he built the high places. The narrative makes the moral grammar explicit. Position two: structural-political inevitability (Knoppers, Brueggemann, Abarbanel). Solomon's apostasy is not separable from the imperial diplomatic-economic structures that funded his Temple-building. Treaty marriages with surrounding kingdoms were the standard ANE technology for securing peace and trade, and they came with cultic obligations — the foreign queens brought their gods. The dtr's indictment is therefore as much against the institution of imperial monarchy as against Solomon personally. The Law of the King in Deuteronomy 17 anticipated this and tried to forbid it — and was ignored.

Position three: the dialectical reading (Polzin, Sweeney). The chapter holds both readings in irresolvable tension. Solomon is personally responsible *and* trapped by structures he inherited. The dtr does not let either reading collapse into the other; the narrative refuses simple causal explanation.

Steelman of position two: the prohibitions of Deuteronomy 7 and 17 are themselves anti-imperial — they envision a kingship structurally distinct from surrounding ANE monarchies, with no horse-multiplication, no gold-accumulation, no wife-multiplication. The dtr knows this. Solomon's apostasy is the *predictable* result of running an empire by ANE standards while claiming covenant exclusivity. The structural reading is not exculpatory; it is the deepest critique.

Where this leaves us: position three is the most exegetically defensible, but the chapter's pastoral force depends on holding personal responsibility intact. The text refuses to let Solomon off the hook, but it also refuses to let the institution of monarchy off the hook. Both indictments stand.

**Tension Two: How should we read the YHWH-speech in verses 11-13 — particularly the limit "I will not tear away all the kingdom" for David's sake?**

Position one: covenant fidelity. YHWH's mercy to David's house (the "lamp" of verse 36) is unconditional — based on YHWH's prior commitment to David in 2 Samuel 7, not on Solomon's or his sons' fidelity. The Davidic covenant is treated as eternal in Psalm 89 and 132. The chapter shows YHWH honoring covenant even in the face of Solomonic apostasy. Position two: Zionic election. The reservation is "for the sake of David my servant and for the sake of Jerusalem the city that I have chosen" (verse 13). The election of Jerusalem operates alongside the Davidic covenant. Levenson treats this as the foundational logic of zionic theology — Jerusalem as YHWH's chosen city is itself a theological category that survives Solomonic and later failure. Position three: temporary preservation, eventual collapse. The reservation is genuinely temporary — "but not forever" (verse 39). The Davidic line will be afflicted; the final collapse of the Davidic monarchy in 587 BCE is foreshadowed here. The chapter is preparing the reader for Babylon.

Steelman of position one: 2 Samuel 7's covenant is unconditional; YHWH's promise to David ("your throne shall be established forever") is the load-bearing structure of the entire OT. The dtr cannot abandon it without making the whole DH incoherent. The reservation in verse 13 is YHWH being faithful to himself.

Where this leaves us: positions one and two are not opposed but layered — the Davidic covenant is unconditional in its core but operates through Jerusalem's election. The third position is also true and is part of the dtr's exilic horizon. The chapter does both: it preserves the covenant *and* signals the long arc toward judgment.

Unresolved question: does Solomon repent? The text does not say. Ecclesiastes is sometimes read as his repentance; the dtr says nothing. The chapter ends with "Solomon slept with his fathers" — neither saved nor damned by the narrator. The silence has theological weight: even the wisest king's eternal status is left to YHWH's hidden judgment.

## The Thread

The chapter looks back to Deuteronomy 7:3-4 (intermarriage prohibition), 17:14-20 (Law of the King), and 2 Samuel 7 (Davidic covenant), all of which it explicitly invokes. Forward, it sets up chapter 12's division of the kingdom (Ahijah's torn garment is the prophetic enactment of what chapter 12 narrates), 2 Kings 23:13 (Josiah destroying the high places Solomon built — three centuries later), and the entire "lamp of David" theme that runs through 1-2 Kings (1 Kings 15:4; 2 Kings 8:19). The Shishak synchronism (verse 40) prepares 1 Kings 14:25-28's invasion. The "but not forever" of verse 39 anchors the messianic hope that runs through the prophets (Isaiah 11, Jeremiah 23, Ezekiel 34, Zechariah 12) into the gospels (Matthew 1's genealogy resolves the chapter's tension by tracing the messianic line through Solomon's descendants to Christ). Augustine's *City of God* 17 reads the chapter as the founding moment of the two-cities trajectory — the earthly kingdom will fail, but the city of God endures.
