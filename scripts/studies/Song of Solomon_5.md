# Song of Solomon 5 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Song of Solomon 5 sits at the literary and emotional center of the *Shir HaShirim* (שִׁיר הַשִּׁירִים, "Song of Songs" — a Hebrew superlative construction meaning "the greatest of songs"). The book's superscription, *asher li-Shlomoh* (אֲשֶׁר לִשְׁלֹמֹה, "which is Solomon's," 1:1), has anchored traditional attribution to Solomon for two and a half millennia. Modern critical scholarship — Marvin Pope in the *Anchor Bible* commentary, Roland Murphy in *Hermeneia*, Othmar Keel in the *Continental Commentary*, Cheryl Exum in the *Old Testament Library*, Tremper Longman in the *NICOT*, and Michael Fox in *The Song of Songs and the Ancient Egyptian Love Songs* — generally dates the final form to the post-exilic Persian or Hellenistic period (roughly fifth to third century BC), based on Persian loanwords like *pardes* (פַּרְדֵּס, "garden/orchard," 4:13) and Greek-period syntactic features. The *li-Shlomoh* preposition is grammatically ambiguous: "by," "for," "about," or "in the manner of" Solomon. Phyllis Trible and Renita Weems have argued the female voice in the Song is too dominant for traditional male-Solomonic authorship to make sense as anything but literary convention.

Chapter 5 opens with the man's voice (5:1) — *bati le-gani achoti khallah* (בָּאתִי לְגַנִּי אֲחֹתִי כַלָּה, "I came to my garden, my sister, my bride") — completing the consummation language begun in chapter 4. The triple endearment *achoti* ("my sister"), *khallah* ("bride"), *ra'yati* ("my love"/"companion," 5:2) echoes Egyptian love poetry conventions. Michael Fox's seminal comparative work demonstrated that the Chester Beatty Papyri (c. 1300 BC) and the Papyrus Harris 500 use *snt* ("sister") as a standard term for a romantic beloved with no incestuous implication. The Egyptian *Love Songs of the New Kingdom* repeatedly stage scenes of nocturnal longing, locked doors, and beloveds wandering city streets — direct generic parallels to 5:2-8.

The dream-knock sequence (5:2-8) has Mesopotamian resonance as well. The Inanna-Dumuzi sacred-marriage texts include scenes of the goddess's beloved approaching her chamber, and Marvin Pope read 5:2 against this background, though most contemporary scholars (Keel, Exum, Longman) treat the parallels as motif-borrowing rather than ritual borrowing — the Song is human love poetry, not cult liturgy.

The woman's *waṣf* (وصف, an Arabic term scholars borrow from later Bedouin wedding poetry to describe the head-to-foot or foot-to-head body-praise genre) of the man begins at 5:10. Hebrew *dodi tzach ve-adom* (דּוֹדִי צַח וְאָדוֹם, "my beloved is radiant and ruddy"). *Tzach* connotes dazzling whiteness or brightness; *adom* is the ruddy red of David's complexion (1 Sam 16:12) and of Esau (Gen 25:25). This is the *only* extended female-describing-male body-poem in the Hebrew Bible — a fact Phyllis Trible underscored in *God and the Rhetoric of Sexuality* (1978) as a uniquely female-voiced moment in the canon.

## Lens 2: The Literature

The chapter is structured as a poetic dialogue with three voices: the man (*Dod*, 5:1), the woman (*Ra'yah*, 5:2-8, 10-16), and the daughters of Jerusalem chorus (*B'not Yerushalayim*, 5:9). The dialogue form — with no narrative connective tissue, no "and then he said" — is the Song's signature mode. Robert Alter, in *The Art of Biblical Poetry*, observed that the Song achieves its erotic intensity precisely by withholding the narrator's voice; the lovers speak directly, without mediation.

The dream sequence (5:2-8) employs deliberate disorientation. *Ani yeshenah ve-libbi er* (אֲנִי יְשֵׁנָה וְלִבִּי עֵר, "I slept, but my heart was awake," 5:2) signals dream-state. The beloved's voice — *kol dodi dofek* (קוֹל דּוֹדִי דוֹפֵק, "the voice of my beloved! He knocks") — is heard, not seen. The woman's hesitation (5:3, "I had put off my garment; how could I put it on?") is psychologically realist: she is half-asleep, reluctant, comfortable. By the time she rises (5:5), her hands drip with *mor over* (מֹר עֹבֵר, "liquid myrrh"), an erotically charged image (myrrh appears throughout the Song as anointing perfume), but the door is empty. *Nafshi yatz'ah ve-dabro* (נַפְשִׁי יָצְאָה בְדַבְּרוֹ, "my soul went out at his speaking," 5:6) — her very *nefesh* (נֶפֶשׁ, life-force) follows him into the night.

The watchmen scene (5:7) is structurally a doublet of 3:3, but where the earlier watchmen merely *find* her, these *beat* her (*hikkuni* הִכּוּנִי), *bruise* her (*ptsa'uni* פְּצָעוּנִי), and take her *redid* (רְדִיד, "veil" or "cloak"). The escalation is shocking. Cheryl Exum reads it as a dream-image of frustrated longing turning violent — the dreamer's interior anxiety projected onto the city. Trible reads it as a real-world reminder that female desire in public space carries danger, even within love poetry.

The *waṣf* (5:10-16) follows a head-to-feet pattern (head, locks, eyes, cheeks, lips, arms, body, legs, mouth) and uses architectural and material imagery — *paz* (פָּז, "fine gold"), *shesh* (שֵׁשׁ, "alabaster" or "marble"), *tarshish* (תַּרְשִׁישׁ, possibly "beryl" or "chrysolite"), *sappirim* (סַפִּירִים, "sapphires" or "lapis lazuli"). The man is described as a temple or palace — a body that is also architecture, monumental and adorned. The closing *zeh dodi ve-zeh re'i* (זֶה דוֹדִי וְזֶה רֵעִי, "this is my beloved and this is my friend," 5:16) lands the praise-poem on the relational note: he is not only erotic object but *re'a*, "companion" or "friend."

The first of the three adjuration refrains structuring the book — *hishba'ti etkhem b'not Yerushalayim* (הִשְׁבַּעְתִּי אֶתְכֶם בְּנוֹת יְרוּשָׁלִָם, 2:7, 3:5, 8:4) — does not appear here, but the woman's address to the daughters in 5:8 echoes the formula and prepares their question in 5:9.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Chapter 5 became a magnet for allegorical reading in both Jewish and Christian traditions. The canonical legitimacy of the Song was contested into the second century AD. Mishnah *Yadayim* 3:5 records Rabbi Akiva's famous defense: *kol ha-ketuvim qodesh ve-Shir HaShirim qodesh qodashim* ("all the writings are holy, but the Song of Songs is the Holy of Holies"). Akiva insisted that the Song never defiled the hands in dispute — meaning its sacred status was secure. The Song was placed among the *Megillot*, the five festival scrolls, and read at Passover, where its love-language was applied to the covenant between Yahweh and Israel coming out of Egypt.

The Aramaic *Targum Shir HaShirim* (probably seventh-eighth century AD) reread the entire book as the history of Israel from the Exodus through the messianic age. *Midrash Shir HaShirim Rabbah* allegorized verse by verse. Rashi (eleventh century) called the Song a metaphor for Israel in exile, with the woman as Israel longing for God. Maimonides, in the *Guide for the Perplexed* (3.51), used Song 5:2 — *I sleep but my heart is awake* — as an image of the philosopher's soul never resting from contemplation of the Divine. The *Zohar* and later kabbalistic tradition read the lovers' union as the union of *Tiferet* and *Shekhinah*, the masculine and feminine aspects within the divine. In modern Jewish scholarship, Robert Alter's translation, Marcia Falk's *The Song of Songs: A New Translation* (1990), Ariel and Chana Bloch's translation (1995), and Avivah Zornberg's literary readings have recovered the text as both human love poetry and sacred scripture, refusing to flatten either pole.

The Christian allegorical trajectory begins with Hippolytus of Rome (early third century) and reaches its first systematic form in Origen's *Commentary on the Song of Songs* and *Homilies on the Song* (mid-third century). Origen read the woman as either the Church (corporate allegory) or the individual soul (mystical allegory). The watchmen who beat her in 5:7 were, for Origen, the hostile powers persecuting the soul who seeks Christ. Gregory of Nyssa's fifteen homilies on the Song (c. 380) made the chapter central to his doctrine of *epektasis*, the soul's endless straining toward God — the beloved who *turns and is gone* (5:6) figures the inexhaustibility of divine reality.

Bernard of Clairvaux's eighty-six *Sermons on the Song of Songs* (1135-1153) — he died before reaching chapter 3 — established the medieval mystical reading. Bernard read 5:2 (*I sleep, but my heart is awake*) as the soul's contemplative repose; the knocking of the beloved is Christ's gracious approach, often unrecognized at first. The *Christus pulsator* ("Christ the knocker") motif fed directly into medieval iconography and, much later, into William Holman Hunt's painting *The Light of the World* (1851-1854, Keble College Oxford) — though Hunt's painting drew explicitly on Revelation 3:20 (*Behold, I stand at the door and knock*), the Johannine echo of Song 5:2 is unmistakable.

William of St. Thierry, Teresa of Avila in *The Interior Castle*, and especially John of the Cross in his *Spiritual Canticle* (a poem and commentary built directly on the Song's imagery) all used chapter 5 to articulate the soul's experience of Christ's apparent absence — what John called the *dark night*. Hadewijch of Antwerp (thirteenth century) and the Beguine tradition read the chapter through a feminine voice that speaks with shocking erotic immediacy of Christ.

Reformers softened but did not abandon allegory. Luther preferred a political reading (the bride as the faithful state). Calvin avoided the Song almost entirely. Pope John Paul II's 129 Wednesday catecheses (1979-1984), gathered as the *Theology of the Body*, recovered the literal-theological sense: human eros as iconic of trinitarian self-gift. Christopher West has popularized this reading. Modern critical Christian scholars — Phyllis Trible, Ellen Davis (*Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Songs*), Tremper Longman, Cheryl Exum, Renita Weems — have largely returned to a literal-poetic reading while acknowledging the legitimate trajectory of theological appropriation.

NT echoes: Ephesians 5:22-33 (Christ and the Church as bridegroom and bride); Revelation 19:7-9 and 21:2 (the marriage supper of the Lamb); John 3:29 (John the Baptist as friend of the bridegroom). The knocking of 5:2 stands behind Revelation 3:20, the verse evangelical tradition has often used apart from any allegorical machinery.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The central tension this chapter forces is hermeneutical. **Position one** — the dominant reading from Akiva to Bernard to John of the Cross — treats 5:2-8 as the soul's spiritual journey: the slowness to respond to grace (5:3), the pursuit through dark city streets (5:6-7), the violence of suffering (5:7), the praise of the absent Beloved that holds the soul together (5:10-16). On this reading, the Song is *primarily* about God; the human-erotic surface is a teaching aid, possibly even a divine accommodation to fleshly readers.

**Position two** — modern critical scholarship, from Trible and Exum to Longman and Fox — treats the chapter as human love poetry, period. The dream sequence is a psychologically realistic vignette of erotic longing: the half-awake hesitation, the missed moment, the searching, the praise that holds the absent lover in mind. Allegory, on this reading, is a later imposition that needed to justify the Song's canonicity in eras embarrassed by erotic literature in scripture.

**Position three** — the *both/and* reading, articulated explicitly by Ellen Davis, Pope John Paul II's *Theology of the Body*, and the older catholic tradition rightly understood — refuses the binary. The Song is human love poetry *and* therefore can speak about God, because human eros is itself a created image of self-giving love. Davis writes that the Song's place in canon is not despite its eroticism but *because* the canon's redemption story includes the redemption of the body. The literal sense and the allegorical sense are not rivals; the literal sense *grounds* the allegorical, and the allegorical *vindicates* the literal.

The watchmen-beating in 5:7 sharpens the test case. Origen's allegorical reading (the soul persecuted) is theologically generative but obscures the social fact of the verse: a woman alone at night seeking her lover is in danger from civic authorities. Trible and Exum insist this danger be *seen*. The both/and reading would say: yes, see the social fact; and also see how the canon's God identifies with vulnerable seekers, with the scourged and bruised, with those who lose their veil in the streets. The Song, read with chapters 53 of Isaiah and the passion narratives in mind, opens unexpected resonances.

Steelman the literal-only position: the canon is large enough to include human love poetry as itself revelatory without needing to be *about* something else. To insist on allegory is to flinch from the body. Eroticism in scripture is not a problem to be solved but a gift to be received — Davis, Trible, and West, however differently they would phrase it, converge here.

Steelman the allegorical-primary position: the Song's placement after Ecclesiastes in the Hebrew arrangement (and as the climactic *Megillah* read at Passover), its history of canonical reception, its disproportionate weight in the Jewish and Christian mystical traditions, all suggest the text's intended canonical work is theological. Bernard, John of the Cross, and Akiva were not making a category mistake.

**Where this leaves us**: chapter 5 stages the experience of love that is real and yet absent — the lover at the door, the lover gone before the door opens, the praise that fills the gap. Whether the gap is between a human lover and her beloved, or between the soul and the God who comes when not expected and is gone when sought, the chapter teaches the same pedagogy: *love is what you praise even when the beloved is missing*. The dream-image refuses to settle into either pure eros or pure allegory; it holds both in suspended attention.

**Closing question**: when the door opens and the beloved has turned away — what would your *waṣf* be? What praise can you speak in the absence?

## The Thread

The chapter's center is the failed meeting and the praise that follows it. The man comes to his garden (5:1); the woman dreams of his coming and misses him (5:2-8); she praises him to the daughters (5:10-16) and so re-establishes him in language. The thread is the strange logic of love: praise composed in absence is not a substitute for presence but a form of fidelity that summons it.
