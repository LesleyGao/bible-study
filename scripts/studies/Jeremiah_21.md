# Jeremiah 21: The Inverted Exodus

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jeremiah 21 drops the reader, with a kind of editorial violence, into the latest dramatic moment in the entire book. The chapter is set during the **siege of Jerusalem of 588–587 BC**, in the reign of Zedekiah, the last Davidic king. Yet the editors have placed it well before chapters that are dated decades earlier — the book of Jeremiah is famously *not* in chronological order, and Lundbom (Anchor Bible, vol. 21A) treats this dislocation as a deliberate compositional choice: chapter 21 is positioned as the gateway to a long royal section (chs. 21–24) that culminates in the Branch oracle and the basket-of-figs vision. The book has stopped narrating the prophet's life and started narrating the death of the kingdom.

The political situation in 588 was acute. Nebuchadnezzar II had already deported Jehoiachin, the queen mother, the princes, the craftsmen, and the smiths — some 10,000 in 597 BC, according to 2 Kings 24:14–16. The Babylonians had installed Zedekiah (Mattaniah) as a vassal. By 588 Zedekiah had broken the vassal oath, encouraged perhaps by Pharaoh Hophra, and Nebuchadnezzar's army had returned. The Babylonian Chronicle (BM 21946, in the British Museum) gives us the spine of the chronology: the surrender of "the city of Yehud" in 597 is recorded in cuneiform; the second campaign, ending with Jerusalem's destruction, is referenced in subsequent fragments. The **Lachish Ostraca** — twenty-one ink-on-pottery letters excavated by James Starkey in 1935 from the gate-room of Lachish Level III — record the desperate signal-fire communications of Judean officers as Nebuchadnezzar's army cut their watch towers off one by one. Letter IV famously says, "we are watching for the fire-signals of Lachish according to all the signs that my lord has given, for we cannot see Azekah." Jeremiah 34:7 names Lachish and Azekah as the last two fortified cities still resisting Babylon. Chapter 21 was spoken into that air.

The two emissaries Zedekiah sends are themselves freighted with significance. **Pashhur ben Malchiah** is *not* the Pashhur ben Immer of chapter 20 who beat Jeremiah and put him in the stocks; this is a different Pashhur, a member of one of the priestly courses (1 Chr 9:12), who reappears as one of Jeremiah's accusers in 38:1, demanding that the king put him to death for sedition. **Zephaniah ben Maaseiah** is a senior priest who reappears in 29:25 (he receives Shemaiah's letter from Babylon) and 37:3 (Zedekiah sends him to Jeremiah a second time during the siege); he is later executed by Nebuchadnezzar at Riblah after the city falls (52:24–27). The bullae bearing the names *Gemariah ben Shaphan*, *Jehucal ben Shelemiah*, *Pashhur*, and *Gedaliah* (the City of David excavations of Eilat Mazar, 2005–2008) anchor the entire late-monarchic court of Jeremiah in the dust of Iron II Jerusalem.

The hope that animates Zedekiah's inquiry is theologically specific: the king clearly has in mind **Hezekiah's deliverance from Sennacherib in 701 BC** (2 Kings 18–19; Isaiah 36–37), when the Assyrian army was decimated overnight and the prophet Isaiah's word was vindicated. *Perhaps the LORD will deal with us according to all his wonderful deeds* (21:2) is a plea for Zion-theology to repeat itself. Jeremiah's answer is the theological pivot of the book: it will not.

Key terms in this chapter operate at the intersection of grammar and theology. *Yad netuyah u-zeroa' chazaqah* (יָד נְטוּיָה וּזְרֹועַ חֲזָקָה, "outstretched hand and strong arm," 21:5) is the **exodus formula** — the very phrase used at Deut 4:34, 5:15, 7:19, and 26:8 to describe YHWH's deliverance of Israel from Egypt. Here it is used for the first time in the canon **against** Israel. *Derekh ha-chayyim ve-derekh ha-mavet* (דֶּרֶךְ הַחַיִּים וְדֶרֶךְ הַמָּוֶת, "the way of life and the way of death," 21:8) directly echoes Deuteronomy 30:15–19 and re-applies its choice in inverted political terms: surrender to the pagan empire = life; defense of the holy city = death. *Beit David* (בֵּית דָּוִד, "house of David," 21:12) opens the long royal section and frames the indictment within the covenant promise of 2 Samuel 7. *Mishpat va-tzedaqah* (מִשְׁפָּט וּצְדָקָה, "justice and righteousness," implicit in 21:12 *execute justice in the morning*) is the stem on which the entire critique of the Davidic house in chapter 22 will turn.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre matters enormously here. Verses 1–10 are **prose narrative-oracle** in the heavy Deuteronomistic style that McKane (ICC) attributed to a "rolling corpus" of editorial expansion. Holladay (Hermeneia) defended a stronger Jeremianic core. The opening superscription (*This is the word that came to Jeremiah*) is a prose formula that recurs across the book and signals an embedded oracle. Verses 11–14 then shift to **poetic oracle**, with the characteristic 3+2 qinah meter audible in the Hebrew of vv. 13–14. The chapter is bilingual in form: it begins as a courtly diplomatic exchange and ends as prophetic poetry.

The most striking literary device is the **inverted citation**. Verse 5 deliberately lifts the exodus formula and reverses its target. Verse 8 lifts the Deuteronomic two-ways formula and reverses its political content. This is not allusion; it is what Carroll (OTL) called "anti-canonical citation" — the prophet quotes Israel's own scripture against Israel. Brueggemann's reading in *A Commentary on Jeremiah: Exile and Homecoming* (1998) names this the prophetic act of "decanonization": the Jerusalem-temple ideology of Psalms 46, 48, and 76 is being unwritten in real time. Stulman (*Jeremiah*, AOTC) frames chapters 21–24 as the literary pivot from "the dismantling of the old order" to "the seeds of new beginning."

Textually, the MT and LXX of Jeremiah diverge dramatically (the LXX is roughly one-eighth shorter and the oracles against the nations sit in a different position). For chapter 21 specifically the differences are minor; the LXX has the same shape, and the Qumran fragments (4QJer-a, 4QJer-c) preserve a Hebrew Vorlage close to the MT for this section. The MT reading at 21:13 — *yoshevet ha-emeq tzur ha-mishor* ("inhabitant of the valley, rock of the plain") — is geographically peculiar (Jerusalem sits on hills, not in a valley) and has provoked Lundbom and Holladay to read it as ironic apostrophe: the city that imagined itself a fortress is being addressed as a complacent valley-dweller. Origen, in *Homilies on Jeremiah* (Homily I), already heard the irony: *the city that thought itself a high place is being shown a low one*.

A formal note on the lament-form parody in vv. 13–14: the meter and address-formula ("Behold, I am against you") are borrowed from the laments-against-foreign-nations genre (cf. Jer 50–51 against Babylon, Nahum against Nineveh) and turned against Jerusalem herself. The form-critical signal is unmistakable. The city is being addressed in the voice the prophets normally reserve for empires marked for destruction.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

This chapter sits at the spine of the canonical story. It is the oracle in which the Davidic-Zion theology of the Psalms and Isaiah's Zion oracles is most explicitly revoked. Where the canon goes from here depends on which tradition is reading.

In the Jewish trajectory, Sirach 49:6–7 ("they burned the chosen city of the sanctuary… by the hand of Jeremiah") already reads Jeremiah 21 as the prophet's authorization of the destruction. The Babylonian Talmud, **Bavli Megillah 14b**, lists Jeremiah among the prophets whose words concern the destruction — a category distinct from the consoling prophets. The **Targum Jonathan** to Jeremiah 21:5 softens the inverted-exodus violence by paraphrasing *outstretched hand* as "a plague from before me." Rashi reads the chapter through the lens of Deuteronomy 28's covenant curses; **Radak** (Rabbi David Kimchi) emphasizes that Zedekiah's emissaries were sincere, which heightens the tragedy: they hoped for a Hezekiah miracle and instead received a Sennacherib judgment in reverse. **Abrabanel**, writing in the shadow of his own expulsion from Spain in 1492, read the way-of-life / way-of-death formula as a paradigm for diaspora existence — surrender to the pagan empire is sometimes the path of survival; he reads Jeremiah's counsel here as proto-galut wisdom. The *churban* tradition (the destruction-of-the-Temple liturgy) integrates this chapter into the Tisha B'Av lectionary structure.

In the Christian trajectory, Origen (*Homilies on Jeremiah*, esp. Hom. I, IX) read the chapter typologically: Jerusalem under siege is the soul under temptation, and "the way of life" is the imitation of Christ's submission. Jerome's commentary on Jeremiah (*In Hieremiam Prophetam*, c. 415 CE) treats chapter 21 as a literal prophecy of the destruction and a figural prophecy of the destruction of 70 CE — a hermeneutical move that became standard. **Aquinas** (drawing on Jerome) reads 21:5 as evidence that divine *fortitudo* is an attribute exercised both for and against Israel, refusing the easy dichotomy between OT wrath and NT mercy. **Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah*** (1563), delivered in Geneva at the height of the Reformed view of providence, returns repeatedly to verse 5: God *himself* fights against his people, not as a stranger but as a covenant partner whose covenant has been spurned. The critical-historical tradition — Duhm (*Das Buch Jeremia*, 1901), Mowinckel — broke the chapter into pre-exilic Jeremianic core and Deuteronomistic redaction, but later scholars (Holladay, Lundbom) have largely vindicated a more unified, Jeremianic origin for the substance. **Kathleen O'Connor** (*Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*, 2011) reads the chapter through the lens of trauma theory: the inverted exodus is the linguistic signature of a community whose foundational story has been ruptured and who must now learn to speak the old words in a new key.

Where the trajectories diverge most sharply is on the question of Zion. Jewish readings preserve the *land* and the *city* as the irrevocable locus of covenant; Jeremiah 21 is a punishment within that covenant, not a withdrawal of it. Christian patristic readings, especially after Augustine, tended to spiritualize Zion — the earthly city is a figure of the heavenly Jerusalem, and her destruction prefigures the passing of the old covenant. The medieval and Reformation readings inherit this spiritualization. Modern post-supersessionist Christian readers (Soulen, Wright, Brueggemann) have tried to recover the theological seriousness of land in Jeremiah 21 without re-collapsing the covenant.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The inverted exodus.** What does it do to a triumphalist account of divine power when the same God who fought *for* Israel against Egypt now fights *against* Israel for Babylon? The Jewish *churban* tradition (especially the rabbis who composed the Tisha B'Av kinot) holds that God himself goes into exile with his people — the Shekhinah is among the deportees. The Catholic reading (drawing on Aquinas, *ST* II-II.30 on mercy and justice as both forms of divine love) preserves the unity of God's character: the same outstretched hand delivers and judges, because both are forms of covenantal fidelity. The Reformed reading (Calvin, Bavinck) absorbs verse 5 into a doctrine of providence in which divine action is never less than active, even in destruction. The Anabaptist / non-resistance reading (Yoder, *The Politics of Jesus*) finds in the surrender-counsel of v. 9 a precedent for refusing to fight even when fighting feels like covenant fidelity. The most uncomfortable reading is Brueggemann's in *Theology of the Old Testament* (1997): YHWH here is "torn" — exhibiting fidelity to his own holiness against his fidelity to his elect — and the prophet's task is to speak the rupture without resolving it. Heschel (*The Prophets*, 1962) called this *the divine pathos* — God's anger is itself a form of suffering. Steelmanning the least intuitive: the Anabaptist reading insists that triumphalist providence is a category mistake; God's power in Jeremiah 21 is exhibited not in delivering the city but in delivering the prophet who speaks its judgment. Where this leaves us: the inverted exodus refuses both the moralism that says "they got what they deserved" and the sentimentalism that says "God would never do that"; it forces a third theology in which fidelity and judgment are the same act seen from different sides.

**Tension 2: Surrender as faithfulness.** Verses 8–10 instruct individual Judeans that going out to the Babylonians is the way of *chayyim* — life. This is treason in the eyes of Zedekiah's military establishment, and Jeremiah will be imprisoned for precisely this message in chapter 38. Romans 13's submit-to-the-governing-authorities counsel and Acts 5:29's "we must obey God rather than men" are both at stake here; what makes Jeremiah's case theologically piercing is that the governing authority *to which faithful surrender is owed* is the **pagan empire**, not the Davidic king. Bonhoeffer's *Ethics* (esp. the chapter on the structure of responsible life) reads Jeremiah 21 as a paradigm for Christian political action under tyranny — there are moments when the visible institutional church is on the wrong side of God's action and only the prophetic minority sees it. Yoder (*Politics of Jesus*) reads the surrender-counsel as the OT root of Christian non-resistance: God's people survive empire by refusing to fight it on its own terms. The Catholic just-war tradition (Augustine, Aquinas, Suárez) is more uncomfortable with this verse — surrender to a pagan aggressor against a covenant-king feels like a violation of the political *bonum commune*. The Jewish reading, following Abrabanel and the later galut tradition, takes the chapter as the founding document of diaspora political theology: covenant fidelity sometimes requires accepting the loss of political sovereignty. Steelmanning the just-war reading: the integrity of Davidic kingship and the holiness of Jerusalem are *themselves* forms of covenant good, and to surrender them is to commit a sin against the very promises that make Israel Israel. Where this leaves us: Jeremiah 21 makes faithfulness *politically illegible*. The faithful look like traitors; the patriots look like covenant-keepers. The chapter forces us to admit that prophetic discernment cannot be reduced to a public ethic.

**One unresolved question:** If the way of life in 21:8 is surrender, does Jeremiah's counsel transfer to other moments when God's people are besieged? Or does the chapter's authority depend on a specific prophetic word that cannot be generalized — leaving each generation to ask, with no formula, whether the faithful response to imperial pressure is resistance or submission?

## The Thread

The inverted exodus formula of 21:5 is picked up directly in Lamentations 2:3–5, where the destruction of Jerusalem is described in language that mirrors Jeremiah's: *the Lord has become like an enemy*. The way-of-life / way-of-death formula of 21:8 is reused in 38:2 (Jeremiah's renewed counsel during the same siege), and Jesus's lament over Jerusalem in Luke 19:41–44 — *the days will come upon you when your enemies will set up a barricade* — recapitulates Jeremiah's posture in a new key. The theme of YHWH fighting against his own city resurfaces in Ezekiel 10–11 with the departure of the glory from the temple, the visual counterpart to Jeremiah's verbal oracle. And the surrender-counsel of v. 9 finds a New Testament echo in 1 Peter 2:13–17, where submission to pagan governing authority is reframed as a form of doxology — though without Jeremiah's specific political charge that the empire is *itself* the instrument of judgment on God's people.
