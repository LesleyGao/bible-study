# Daniel 11: The Linchpin of the Dating Debate

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Daniel 11 is, by a wide margin, the most historically detailed chapter in the entire Hebrew Bible. From verse 2 to roughly verse 35, it traces in compressed but identifiable form the political history of the Near East from the late Persian period through the Antiochene crisis of 167–164 BC — a span of nearly four centuries. The compression is uneven: the Persian period gets four kings in a single verse (11:2), Alexander gets one (11:3), the Diadochi struggle gets verse 4, and then the chapter slows down dramatically to narrate the Ptolemaic-Seleucid wars of the 3rd and early 2nd centuries with extraordinary granularity.

The "three more kings in Persia and a fourth far richer" (11:2) most naturally identifies as Cambyses, Pseudo-Smerdis (Bardiya), Darius I, and the famously wealthy Xerxes I, whose invasion of Greece in 480 BC is the historical event most plausibly described as "stirring up all against the kingdom of Greece." The "mighty king" of 11:3 is Alexander the Great (336–323 BC); the kingdom "broken and divided toward the four winds of heaven" (11:4) reflects the Diadochi settlement after Alexander's death — Cassander in Macedonia, Lysimachus in Thrace, Seleucus in Babylonia, Ptolemy in Egypt.

From 11:5 forward, the chapter narrates the Ptolemaic-Seleucid wars in dense detail. The "king of the south" is the Ptolemaic dynasty in Egypt; the "king of the north" is the Seleucid dynasty in Antioch. The "daughter of the king of the south" given in marriage (11:6) is Berenice, daughter of Ptolemy II Philadelphus, married to Antiochus II Theos around 252 BC under terms that required Antiochus to set aside his first wife Laodice — the marriage failed, Laodice poisoned both Berenice and Antiochus II, and the resulting Third Syrian War involved Berenice's brother Ptolemy III Euergetes ("a branch from her roots," 11:7) invading Seleucid territory in 246 BC and carrying off the Egyptian gods that Cambyses had taken to Persia centuries earlier (11:8 — the verifiable detail confirmed by the Adoulis inscription and Jerome's commentary).

The narrative tracks the wars of Antiochus III the Great (11:10–19) — his initial defeat by Ptolemy IV at Raphia in 217 BC, his eventual victory at Panium in 200 BC giving him control of Coele-Syria including Judea ("the glorious land" of 11:16), the political marriage of his daughter Cleopatra I to Ptolemy V to "destroy the kingdom" (11:17 — a strategy Cleopatra subverted by aligning with her husband), Antiochus III's defeat by Rome at Magnesia in 190 BC, the indemnity imposed by the Treaty of Apamea (11:18 — the "commander" who "put an end to his insolence" is Lucius Cornelius Scipio Asiaticus), and Antiochus III's death in 187 BC plundering a temple in Elam ("he shall stumble and fall, and shall not be found," 11:19).

Then Seleucus IV Philopator (11:20) sends his treasurer Heliodorus to plunder the Jerusalem temple — an episode dramatized in 2 Maccabees 3 — and is shortly afterward assassinated, "broken, neither in anger nor in battle" (his son Demetrius was a hostage in Rome). And then the chapter arrives at its central figure: "In his place shall arise a contemptible person" (11:21) — **Antiochus IV Epiphanes** (175–164 BC), who seized the throne by political maneuver rather than legitimate succession, who waged the Sixth Syrian War against Egypt (11:25–28), and whose 168 BC invasion of Egypt was halted by the Roman ambassador Gaius Popilius Laenas, who drew a circle in the sand around Antiochus and demanded he answer Rome's ultimatum before stepping out — the famous "Day of Eleusis" (11:30: "ships of Kittim shall come against him, and he shall be afraid and withdraw"). *Kittim* in Hebrew prophetic literature regularly denotes Mediterranean western powers; in this case unmistakably the Romans.

Frustrated by the humiliation in Egypt, Antiochus turned his rage on Judea. In 167 BC, his forces "profane[d] the temple and fortress, and... took away the regular burnt offering. And they... set up the abomination that makes desolate" (11:31). The historical correlate is the desecration described in **1 Maccabees 1:54** — Antiochus's altar to Zeus Olympios erected on the temple's altar of burnt offering, accompanied by the prohibition of circumcision, Sabbath observance, and Torah study, on pain of death. **2 Maccabees 4–6**, **Polybius's *Histories*** (especially Books 26 and 29), and **Livy** (Books 41–45) confirm the entire historical arc with parallel detail. The *maskilim* — "the wise among the people" (11:33–35) — are the loyal Jewish reformist intelligentsia who taught Torah at the cost of their lives during the persecution; they parallel the *hasidim* of 1 Maccabees 2:42, the proto-Pharisaic group that aligned with the Maccabean revolt while preserving its distinctively scholarly-pietist identity.

Then, at 11:36, the chapter shifts. The description of the king's "end" (11:36–45) — his self-exaltation, his disregard for ancestral gods, his honor of the "god of fortresses," his final campaign south against the king of Egypt followed by his death "between the sea and the glorious holy mountain" (11:45) — does **not** correspond to the historical Antiochus IV's actual death. The historical Antiochus died of disease in 164 BC at Tabae in Persia, far from Judea, while attempting to plunder a temple to fund his military expenses (1 Macc 6:1–16; Polybius 31.9). This precise-then-imprecise pattern at 11:36 is the chapter's most consequential textual feature.

Authorship and dating: chapter 11 is the linchpin of the Maccabean dating hypothesis. **John J. Collins** (Hermeneia) writes that the precise-prophecy-up-to-165-BC-then-imprecise-after pattern is "the most powerful single argument for Maccabean composition in the Old Testament." The critical consensus — Collins, **Carol Newsom** (OTL, 2014), **Maurice Casey** (*Son of Man*), **James Montgomery** (ICC), **S.R. Driver**, **Lester Grabbe**, **Philip Davies**, **Klaus Koch**, **Norman Porteous** — reads chapter 11 as composed sometime between Antiochus's desecration in late 167 BC and his death in late 164 BC, after the historical events of 11:1–35 but before those of 11:36–45 had failed to occur. The conservative tradition — **Stephen Miller** (NAC), **Joyce Baldwin** (TOTC, 1978), **Gleason Archer**, **Edward Young**, **John Walvoord**, **Leon Wood** — defends genuine 6th-century predictive prophecy and accounts for the post-v.36 divergence by reading 11:36–45 as a *prophetic shift* from Antiochus to a typologically-related future Antichrist (the standard dispensational and historic premillennial reading).

**Porphyry of Tyre** made the Maccabean argument first in his *Adversus Christianos* (3rd c. AD), preserved through **Jerome's *Commentary on Daniel*** (407, *contra Porphyry*), where Jerome quotes Porphyry: "the one who composed this book under the name of Daniel lied in order to revive the hope of his fellow countrymen." Jerome's response defends genuine prophecy but, in a remarkable concession, allows that 11:36–45 may indeed describe a future Antichrist beyond Antiochus.

Key Hebrew terms: *melekh ha-tzafon* (מֶלֶךְ הַצָּפוֹן, "king of the north," Seleucid); *melekh ha-negev* (מֶלֶךְ הַנֶּגֶב, "king of the south," Ptolemaic); *nivzeh* (נִבְזֶה, "contemptible person," 11:21, Antiochus IV); *Kittim* (כִּתִּים, the western seafaring power, 11:30, the Romans); *shiqquts meshomem* (שִׁקּוּץ מְשׁוֹמֵם, "abomination that makes desolate," 11:31); *maskilim* (מַשְׂכִּלִים, "the wise," 11:33, 35); *eloah ma'uzzim* (אֱלוֹהַּ מָעֻזִּים, "god of fortresses," 11:38, debated — possibly Zeus Olympios, possibly an abstract deity of force).

Archaeological-textual: the chapter's historical claims can be cross-referenced against the **Babylonian Astronomical Diaries**, the **Adoulis inscription** of Ptolemy III, the **Rosetta Stone** (Ptolemy V), Polybius, Livy, **1 and 2 Maccabees**, **Josephus *Ant.* 12**, and the Seleucid coinage. The agreement between the chapter and verifiable history through 11:35 is dense and detailed.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 11 is the longest sustained piece of *vaticinium ex eventu* in the Hebrew Bible — what Collins calls "history disguised as prophecy." The genre is well-attested in Second Temple Jewish apocalyptic and in Greco-Roman literature: prior history is narrated in the future tense as if predicted by an ancient seer, lending authority to the genuinely predictive material at the end. 1 Enoch's *Animal Apocalypse* (1 Enoch 85–90) and *Apocalypse of Weeks* (1 Enoch 93, 91:11–17), the *Sibylline Oracles*, and the Qumran *War Scroll* all employ the form. Collins, in his *Apocalyptic Imagination*, argues this is the standard apocalyptic mode in the period.

The chapter's literary structure is tight. It opens with a chronological superscription connecting it to chapter 10 (11:1), surveys the Persian and Hellenistic transition in compressed form (11:2–4), narrates the Ptolemaic-Seleucid wars at length (11:5–20), and then concentrates on Antiochus IV from 11:21 through 11:45 — half the chapter for one king. The structural emphasis is unmistakable: this king matters more than any other in the historical sweep. Within the Antiochus section, 11:21–35 narrates the historical career through the desecration and the *maskilim*'s suffering, while 11:36–45 describes the end in language that draws on standard apocalyptic-prophetic motifs (self-exaltation, blasphemy against the God of gods, military overreach, divine judgment) without obvious one-to-one historical correspondence.

The phrase *until the time of the end* (11:35) is one of the chapter's most contested literary signals. For the critical reading, it marks the end of the *vaticinium ex eventu* and the beginning of genuinely prospective material that turned out not to happen. For the conservative reading, it marks a shift in temporal horizon from the immediate Antiochene crisis to the eschatological future. The textual signal is real; the interpretation depends on prior framework.

The Old Greek and Theodotion versions of Daniel 11 differ in detail; the Old Greek's version of 11:36–45 contains additional material that some scholars (Albertz) read as a later post-164-BC editorial layer. The Hebrew Masoretic text is generally well preserved at Qumran (4QDan-c).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The chapter is the deepest engagement of the Hebrew Bible with the *clash of empires* as theological theme. The Daniel apocalyptic tradition takes seriously what the great pre-exilic prophets had begun to glimpse: that Israel's history is not merely Israel's, but is unfolded inside a structure of imperial succession that God ultimately judges. The chapter's tight numerical-chronological compression places the Antiochene crisis at the climax of the *kings of north and south*, treating the moment when Israel was forcibly compelled to violate Torah as the climactic confrontation between the powers of the age and the people of the holy covenant.

**Jewish Trajectory**: 1 Maccabees 1 reads chapter 11 as just-fulfilled. 2 Maccabees and Josephus (*Ant.* 12) both treat the chapter as describing the Antiochene crisis. The Talmudic tradition retained the chapter as cryptic history; **Saadia Gaon** worked through it carefully and identified the *contemptible person* as Antiochus while leaving the closing verses for messianic interpretation. **Rashi** and **Ibn Ezra** identified the historical figures with care. **Abrabanel** (*Mayanei ha-Yeshua*, 15th c.) wrote at length on Daniel and identified the kings; he read 11:36–45 as pointing to the future *gog u-magog* eschatological war. Modern Jewish scholarship — **Robert Gordis**, **Aaron Koller** — accepts the Maccabean reading.

**Christian Trajectory**: The patristic tradition handled the chapter in two distinct ways. **Hippolytus** (c. 200) read 11:21–35 historically as Antiochus while pushing 11:36–45 into the future as the Antichrist. **Jerome's *Commentary on Daniel*** (407) is the longest patristic engagement: Jerome works systematically through the chapter, identifies the Persian, Macedonian, Ptolemaic, and Seleucid figures with Greco-Roman historical sources, defends genuine prophecy against Porphyry, and — strikingly — concedes that 11:36–45 most plausibly describes a future Antichrist beyond Antiochus. **Theodoret of Cyrus** continued the historical-then-eschatological pattern. The medieval tradition — Aquinas, **Joachim of Fiore** — folded the chapter into its eschatological schemes. **Calvin's *Lectures on Daniel*** (1561) treated the chapter as one of the most important in the OT and worked through its historical identifications with care, defending genuine prophecy and reading 11:36–45 as continuing about Antiochus with eschatological resonance — Calvin was hesitant about a clean shift to a future Antichrist, but his reading allowed dual reference. **Luther's *Preface to Daniel*** (1530) read the *contemptible person* as Antiochus and 11:36–45 as a typological pointer to the papacy.

The dispensationalist tradition — **J.N. Darby**, **C.I. Scofield** (*Reference Bible*, 1909), **John Walvoord** (*The Prophecy Knowledge Handbook*), **Hal Lindsey**, **Tim LaHaye** — reads 11:1–35 as Antiochus and 11:36–45 as a future Antichrist arising during the Tribulation. The shift point at 11:36 is read as the chapter's evident pivot from the prior Antichrist-type to the eschatological Antichrist himself. **Stephen Miller**'s NAC commentary defends this reading carefully.

The critical tradition is unanimous on the Maccabean reading. Collins, Newsom, Casey, Driver, Montgomery, Davies, Grabbe, Porteous all read the chapter as composed during the desecration period itself, with 11:36–45 as the author's prospective expectation of how the Antiochene crisis would resolve — an expectation that the actual death of Antiochus in Persia (rather than Palestine) frustrated.

**Where they diverge**: The shift point at 11:36 is read by the patristic-Reformed-conservative tradition as the divinely intended point of transition from Antiochus-the-type to Antichrist-the-antitype. It is read by the critical tradition as the point at which the author's *vaticinium ex eventu* runs out of historical knowledge and the genuinely prospective material begins — material that turned out wrong about how Antiochus would die. The dispensational reading inserts a 2,000-year gap. The Calvinist reading allows dual reference without the gap. The Maccabean-critical reading takes the failure of 11:36–45 to fit the historical Antiochus's actual death as confirming the dating.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Prophecy vs. *vaticinium ex eventu* — the dating debate at its sharpest.** This is the chapter where the question is impossible to soft-pedal. Either Daniel 11:1–35 is supernatural predictive prophecy down to specific dynastic marriages, named Roman-legate humiliations, and the precise ritual character of Antiochus's desecration — all four centuries in advance — or it is a Maccabean-era composition projected back into the 6th century. Steelman the conservative defense: the same text that critical scholars treat as evidently *vaticinium ex eventu* through 11:35 was treated by the entire Christian tradition through the 18th century as genuine prophecy; the Christian doctrine of Scripture's inspiration is deeply tied to the possibility of genuine predictive prophecy; the textual evidence for Maccabean composition is circumstantial (no extant 2nd-century fragments demonstrably show editorial layering) and depends on philosophical assumptions about whether predictive prophecy is possible. Steelman the critical defense: the precision-then-imprecision pattern at 11:36 is *not* a minor anomaly but the single strongest piece of internal evidence in the OT for late composition; there is no plausible reading of 11:36–45 that maps to Antiochus's actual death (he died of disease in Persia, not in Palestine in a campaign against Egypt); the same author who knew the political details of the past four centuries with perfect accuracy suddenly becomes vague about an event that should have been the climax of the prophecy if it were genuinely predictive. Don't pretend the issue is small. The credibility of the entire Hebrew Bible's predictive-prophetic tradition — and the doctrinal implications for inspired scripture — turns on what one decides about Daniel 11. *Where this leaves us:* the textual evidence is what it is. What one makes of it depends on prior commitments about what scripture can and cannot do. Both readings have to be steelmanned without dismissive caricature.

**Tension 2: The king of the north in 11:36–45 — Antiochus, Antichrist, or literary topos?** The pivot at 11:36 has shaped two thousand years of Christian eschatology. The dispensational reading (Darby, Scofield, LaHaye) takes the verses as describing a future end-time figure who repeats Antiochus's pattern on a global eschatological scale; this reading underwrites 2 Thessalonians 2's "man of lawlessness" theology, much of Revelation 13's beast theology, and the entirety of Anglo-American premillennial-dispensational eschatology. **Newsom**'s OTL commentary offers a mediating reading: 11:36–45 is the author's eschatological expectation of how the Antiochene crisis would culminate, drawing on prior prophetic motifs (Ezekiel's Gog of Magog, Isaiah's downfall-of-the-tyrant theology, Joel's *day of the Lord*) — not a clean prediction of a future figure but a prophetic-poetic crystallization of how the contemporary tyrant should fall. The dispensational reading has shaped popular evangelical eschatology more decisively than perhaps any other passage in scripture; **Hal Lindsey's *Late Great Planet Earth*** (1970) and the *Left Behind* novels (LaHaye / Jenkins, 1995–2007) have translated the reading to tens of millions. Steelman the dispensational reading: the textual shift at 11:36 is real, the language of self-exaltation and blasphemy fits eschatological-Antichrist patterns that show up in 2 Thess 2 and Rev 13, and the chapter's evident structural emphasis on a single climactic king coheres with NT eschatology. Steelman the critical-Newsom reading: the *vaticinium ex eventu* genre regularly concludes with prospective eschatological material that does not literally occur; the author's literary intent is to crystallize the expected fall of the tyrant rather than predict a specific future figure; reading the verses as a future Antichrist is anachronistic. *Where this leaves us:* what one does with 11:36–45 shapes one's entire eschatology — whether the Christian tradition's expectation of a future Antichrist is solidly grounded in OT prophecy or is a typological extension that goes beyond what the text actually says. The stakes are high.

**The unresolved question:** If the chapter's predictive specificity peaks precisely where it would matter most — the death of the persecutor — and then misses the actual historical event, what does it mean to read a text as inspired? Is the inspiration in the words, in the typological pattern, in the moral truth about how tyrants meet their end, or in the call to the *maskilim* to teach Torah at the cost of their lives in any age?

## The Thread

**1 Maccabees 1:54** — "On the fifteenth day of Chislev, in the one hundred forty-fifth year [of the Seleucid era — December 167 BC], the king set up the abomination of desolation upon the altar of burnt offering." The historical correlate to Daniel 11:31, narrated by a witness within a generation. The phrase *abomination of desolation* is a direct quotation from Daniel.

**Mark 13:14 / Matthew 24:15** — "When you see the abomination of desolation spoken of by the prophet Daniel, standing where it ought not... let those who are in Judea flee to the mountains." Jesus picks up Daniel 11:31's phrase and applies it forward — to AD 70, to a future eschatological figure, or to both. The Synoptic Apocalypse's debt to Daniel 11 is foundational.

**2 Thessalonians 2:3–4** — "the man of lawlessness... who opposes and exalts himself against every so-called god or object of worship, so that he takes his seat in the temple of God, proclaiming himself to be God." The verbal echoes of Daniel 11:36 ("he shall exalt himself and magnify himself above every god, and shall speak astonishing things against the God of gods") are decisive. Pauline eschatology is built on Daniel 11.

**Polybius, *Histories* 29.27** — the eyewitness account of Popilius Laenas's circle in the sand at Eleusis (168 BC), the historical correlate to Daniel 11:30's "ships of Kittim." Reading the two passages side by side is one of the most striking exercises in OT historical reference.
