# 1 Chronicles 20 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

This eight-verse chapter is one of the shortest in 1 Chronicles, and it is also one of the most theologically loaded chapters in the entire Hebrew Bible — not for what it says but for what it does not say. The Chronicler, writing in the post-exilic Persian period (most likely fourth or late fifth century BC, in the small Yehud province under Persian administration), takes the same narrative material that 2 Samuel covers across three full chapters of dense and morally complex storytelling (2 Sam 11:1; 11:2–12:25; 12:26–31; with the Philistine giant material gathered separately in 2 Sam 21:18–22) and compresses it into eight verses. What he keeps: the campaign at Rabbah, the spoils, the giant-slayings. What he removes: the Bathsheba narrative, Uriah's death, Nathan's confrontation, David's repentance, the death of the first child, the birth of Solomon. The Chronicler has cut from his Davidic narrative the single most morally significant episode in 2 Samuel.

The historical setting begins with verse 1's chronological marker: *vayehi leʿet teshuvat hashanah leʿet tzeʾt ha-melakhim* (וַיְהִי לְעֵת תְּשׁוּבַת הַשָּׁנָה לְעֵת צֵאת הַמְּלָכִים, "and it came to pass at the time of the turn of the year, at the time when kings go out [to battle]"). This is verbatim 2 Sam 11:1's opening — and in 2 Samuel that line continues, *"David sent Joab and his servants with him and all Israel… But David remained at Jerusalem"*, which immediately leads into the Bathsheba narrative. The Chronicler keeps the framing — *"But David remained at Jerusalem"* (v. 1c) — but does not narrate what David did *while* remaining at Jerusalem. He simply moves directly to *"And Joab struck down Rabbah and overthrew it"* (v. 1d). The reader who knows 2 Samuel hears the omission as a roar; the reader who does not know 2 Samuel may not notice anything is missing.

Verse 2 contains a striking detail. The Chronicler says David took *ateret malkam* (עֲטֶרֶת מַלְכָּם, "the crown of their king") — but the consonantal Hebrew *malkam* (מַלְכָּם) can also be read as *Milcom* (מִלְכֹּם), the chief deity of Ammon (cf. 1 Kings 11:5, 33; Zeph 1:5). Williamson, Klein, and Knoppers all note that the Hebrew is genuinely ambiguous here, and many scholars (and several modern translations, e.g., NRSV "the crown of Milcom from his head") read the line as "David took the crown of *Milcom* from his/its head" — meaning the crown was lifted from the head of the Ammonite idol. The "talent of gold" weight (roughly 75–100 lbs depending on the talent-standard) is plausible only if the crown was not actually worn but mounted on a cult image. The chapter then says it was "placed on David's head" (v. 2) — a striking image either way: David crowned with either the king's diadem (after Hanun's deposition) or with the captured idol's diadem (the more theologically loaded reading).

The forced-labor note in v. 3 is contested. The Chronicler's text reads *vayasar baseragah uvachrutzei habarzel uvamegerot* (literally, "he set them to saws and iron picks and axes"). The parallel in 2 Sam 12:31 is debated in textual transmission and has been read by some scholars as describing torture (cutting people *with* saws), by others as describing forced labor (setting them *to work with* saws). The Chronicler's text seems to favor the forced-labor reading, and most modern translations (ESV, NIV, NRSV) take it that way in both Samuel and Chronicles. Williamson and Japhet defend the forced-labor reading as more contextually plausible (slave-labor for construction is a recognized ANE post-conquest practice).

The giant-slayings of vv. 4–8 parallel 2 Sam 21:18–22 closely. The figures: Sippai (Saph in 2 Sam 21:18), struck down by Sibbecai the Hushathite at Gezer (Gob in 2 Sam — likely a textual variant); Lahmi the brother of Goliath, struck down by Elhanan son of Jair (the parallel verse 2 Sam 21:19, in its present Masoretic form, says Elhanan killed *Goliath himself*, in apparent contradiction with 1 Sam 17 — the Chronicler has resolved the contradiction by reading *the brother of Goliath*, i.e., adding *achi* to the name; whether this is the Chronicler's harmonization or a recovery of an original reading is debated, with most critical scholarship favoring the harmonization view); a six-fingered, six-toed unnamed giant of Gath (described in the chapter as *ish midah* — איש מדה, "a man of measure," v. 6 — the standard Hebrew idiom for unusual stature) struck down by Jonathan son of Shimea (David's brother). The label *yelidei harafa* (יְלִידֵי הָרָפָא, "born to the Rapha," vv. 4, 6, 8) connects these figures to the *Rephaim* tradition — pre-Israelite giant peoples whose name appears in the Pentateuch and Joshua (Gen 14:5; Deut 2:11, 20–21; 3:11; Josh 12:4, 13:12).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The literary structure of the chapter is its theological argument. Two scenes: the close of the Ammonite war (vv. 1–3) and the catalogue of giant-slayings (vv. 4–8). Both are gathered from material the Chronicler's Vorlage handled in different parts of 2 Samuel — the Rabbah campaign concludes 2 Sam 12 (after Bathsheba), and the giant-slayings are tucked into 2 Sam 21 as part of the appendix material. The Chronicler has taken the *war frame* from one place and the *giants list* from another and stitched them together to bridge from the war chapters (1 Chr 18–19) directly to the David-and-the-temple-preparation chapters (1 Chr 21–29) — without ever passing through the Bathsheba episode.

The omission is the Chronicler's most consequential redactional decision in his entire Davidic narrative. He does not omit casually. He omits *exactly* 2 Sam 11:2–12:25, which contains: David's evening walk on the roof, his sight of Bathsheba bathing, the inquiry about her, the summons, the adultery, her conception, David's attempted cover-up, Uriah's refusal to go home to his wife while the army was in the field, David's command to Joab to put Uriah in the front line and withdraw, Uriah's death in battle, Bathsheba's mourning and remarriage, the LORD's anger ("the thing that David had done displeased the LORD," 2 Sam 11:27), Nathan's parable of the ewe lamb, David's confession ("I have sinned against the LORD," 12:13), the sentence ("the sword shall never depart from your house," 12:10), the death of the first child, David's worship at the news of the death, the conception of Solomon, and the divine love for Solomon ("Yahweh loved him… and called his name Jedidiah," 12:24–25). All of this is gone. The boundary of the cut is precise: the Chronicler keeps 2 Sam 11:1 (the war's opening) and 2 Sam 12:26–31 (the war's conclusion at Rabbah) but cuts everything in between.

This is not the only omission; we have already noted (in the chapter 18 commentary) that the Chronicler omitted the David-Saul-house material of 2 Sam 1–4, the Mephibosheth material of 2 Sam 9, and that he will go on to omit the Tamar/Amnon/Absalom/Sheba material of 2 Sam 13–20. But the Bathsheba omission is the most theologically loaded because Bathsheba is the *moral and theological center* of 2 Samuel's David — the moment that, in the prophetic-historical framework, makes intelligible everything that follows in David's life (the family disasters, the decline, the contested succession). Williamson, Japhet, Knoppers, Boda, Klein, McKenzie, and Braun all identify this as a — perhaps *the* — defining redactional decision of the Chronicler.

The chapter's brevity and matter-of-factness compound the effect. The reader who knows Samuel comes to v. 1's "But David remained at Jerusalem" and waits for the next sentence — and the next sentence is "And Joab struck down Rabbah." The narrative space where Bathsheba should be has been silently sealed.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canon, the relationship between Samuel and Chronicles becomes most acute here, and it has shaped reception in both Jewish and Christian traditions in ways worth tracing carefully.

In the **Jewish trajectory**, the Talmud and rabbinic tradition handle the Bathsheba narrative with notorious complexity. b. Shabbat 56a famously argues that *"whoever says David sinned is in error"* — proposing a defensive reading in which Uriah's prior conditional divorce (a halakhic fiction grounded in the practice of soldiers giving their wives conditional divorces before battle to prevent agunah status if they fell) meant Bathsheba was technically unmarried, Uriah was technically a rebel against the king for refusing David's command (so his death was not strictly murder), and David's repentance (Ps 51) was for less serious infractions. This rabbinic harmonization is striking precisely because it shows that the moral force of 2 Sam 11–12 was so strong that Talmudic tradition felt the need to explain it away. Modern Jewish scholarship — Levenson, Japhet, Greenberg — has largely rejected the harmonizing tradition and read 2 Samuel as the more theologically serious witness. Japhet in particular argues that the Chronicler's omission is honest theological selection, not endorsement of a David-without-sin doctrine, and that Chronicles must be read alongside Samuel rather than in place of it.

In the **Christian trajectory**, the reception is more troubled. Mainstream evangelical and Reformed scholarship has often expressed frank embarrassment with the Chronicler's omission — the worry being that Chronicles seems to *whitewash* a David whom Samuel and the Psalter (especially Ps 51, traditionally read as David's prayer of repentance after Nathan's confrontation) preserve in penitent moral complexity. Catholic and Orthodox traditions have generally been more comfortable with the Chronicler's theological-historiography mode, recognizing that ancient Near Eastern history-writing was always more theological than the modern documentary ideal, and that Chronicles' selectivity is consistent with that mode. The Childs canonical-criticism position has gradually become a de facto consensus across confessional lines: both Samuel and Chronicles are canonical; their differences are theologically meaningful; the Chronicler's omission does not erase Samuel's witness; the canon contains both because the church (and synagogue) need both.

The **New Testament** does not directly cite the Bathsheba episode but the Davidic line as it runs through Solomon (and thus through Bathsheba, who is named in Matt 1:6 as "the wife of Uriah" — Matthew's careful and pointed phrasing) is part of the Christological lineage. Matthew's Greek phrase *ek tēs tou Ouriou* ("from the [wife] of Uriah," Matt 1:6) is a small but striking inclusion: Matthew names Bathsheba *as Uriah's wife* in the genealogy of Christ, refusing to pretend the moral mess away. The genealogy of the Christ runs through Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, the wife of Uriah, and Mary — five women, four of them figures of ambiguity or scandal in the Davidic-line tradition. Matthew's inclusion of Bathsheba *under Uriah's name* is a quiet refusal of the Chronicler's omission.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

The Chronicler's distinctive theological agenda in this chapter, and in the omission that defines it, is *to construct an idealized David as the founder of post-exilic temple-worship — a David shorn of the moral failures that Samuel preserved — because the post-exilic community's theological need is for a legitimate, durable foundation for the cult that survives them*. The Chronicler is not a transparent historian. He is a theologian of the temple, working in narrative form, selecting what serves his project.

**Tension 1: The Bathsheba omission and what it reveals about the Chronicler's theology and method.** This is the central tension. Three positions, each held by serious scholars and traditions:

- *Position A — Honest theological selection (Williamson, Japhet, Knoppers, Boda, Klein, McKenzie, Braun, Childs):* The Chronicler is selecting from the Davidic record those elements relevant to his project: David as covenant-bearing temple-founder. He is writing for an audience that knows Samuel — for him to omit Bathsheba is not to deny Bathsheba; it is to choose not to retell her. The omissions are *visible* to the audience, and they constitute a theological choice about emphasis, not a deception. The David of Chronicles is a partial portrait, and the audience is expected to know that. Read alongside Samuel, the Chronicler's omissions throw into relief what each book is doing: Samuel preserves the moral history of the Davidic individual; Chronicles preserves the institutional theology of the Davidic dynasty. Both are needed; neither is sufficient alone.
- *Position B — Whitewashing distortion:* The Chronicler is constructing a David that is not the David of Samuel and is, by virtue of its omissions, *false to the canonical witness*. The Bathsheba narrative is the moral center of 2 Samuel; cutting it is not selection but distortion. The Chronicler's idealized David has been used and misused historically to authorize political and ecclesial figures who claim Davidic legitimation while excluding Davidic accountability. Some critical scholars and some evangelicals concerned about *celebrity-pastor culture* and political theology have voiced versions of this concern.
- *Position C — Theological-historiographical mode:* The Chronicler is writing in an ancient mode that does not distinguish "historical accuracy" and "theological vision" the way modern readers want to. To call him a "historian" or to call him a "theologian" is anachronistic; he is doing both at once, in a mode that took for granted the legitimacy of selecting and shaping historical material to disclose theological meaning. Christian readers — especially those formed by Catholic and Orthodox traditions — tend to be comfortable with this mode. Modern evangelical readers, formed by post-Enlightenment historiographical sensibilities, tend to find it harder to accept. The mode itself is neither inherently faithful nor unfaithful; it is the mode in which much of biblical narrative is composed, and learning to read it well requires patience with theological selectivity.

A fair steelman of Position B: the Bathsheba narrative does not just contain David's sin — it contains the *prophetic confrontation* that Israel's whole prophetic tradition models. Nathan's *atah ha-ish* (אַתָּה הָאִישׁ, "you are the man," 2 Sam 12:7) is the prophetic word that holds power accountable. Cutting it from Chronicles is not just cutting David's failure; it is cutting the prophetic-confrontation pattern that funds the entire Hebrew prophetic tradition. The cost is real, and Position A's "honest selection" defense has to reckon with what is lost when Nathan disappears. Even if Chronicles does not deny Nathan's word, by not re-narrating it the Chronicler has dimmed something the canon needs at full brightness.

A fair steelman of Position C: the Chronicler's mode is also the mode of John's gospel relative to the Synoptics, of Stephen's speech in Acts 7 relative to the Pentateuch, of much sermonic biblical reflection. Selective theological narration is a feature of the canon, not a bug, and Christian and Jewish traditions have learned to read it as such. The Chronicler is doing what the canon's other narrators do; the difference is one of degree, not of kind.

**Tension 2: How should we read Chronicles — *despite* the Bathsheba omission, or *with* it?** Brevard Childs's canonical-criticism approach insists that the canon's containing of *both* Samuel and Chronicles is itself the theological witness. The Chronicler's vision is part of the testimony, but it is not the only testimony. Samuel's morally complex David is also part of the testimony. To read either without the other is to miss the canonical depth.

- *Despite-the-omission reading:* The reader silently supplies what Chronicles omits, drawing on Samuel and the Psalms. David in Chronicles is "the same David" as in Samuel, with the moral history acknowledged even when not narrated. This reading is comfortable for those who want a coherent single Davidic portrait but tends to underplay the Chronicler's distinctive theological vision.
- *With-the-omission reading:* The reader takes the Chronicler's vision seriously as itself, recognizing that the Chronicler is offering a theological construction in which David's institutional achievements (temple-preparation, Levitical orders, dynastic founding) are foregrounded as the materially-relevant legacy for the post-exilic community. The omission is part of the message; the Chronicler is saying *for the purposes of grounding your worship now, this is the David you need to remember*. This reading takes Chronicles seriously as a distinct theological witness rather than collapsing it into Samuel.

The mature canonical position holds both readings together: Chronicles' vision is real and is what Chronicles offers; Samuel's vision is also real and is what Samuel offers; the canon expects readers to hold both. The Bathsheba is *not* in Chronicles; the Bathsheba *is* in Samuel; the canon contains both.

**Tension 3: Mainstream evangelical embarrassment versus Catholic/Orthodox comfort.** This division is real and worth naming. Evangelical scholarship — especially of a more historically conservative bent — has often expressed discomfort with the Chronicler's selectivity, sometimes attempting harmonizing readings that minimize the omissions, sometimes simply privileging Samuel as the more reliable historical record. Catholic and Orthodox traditions, more comfortable with allegorical, typological, and theological-historiographical reading modes, have generally received Chronicles' distinct vision with less anxiety. The honest engagement is to recognize that the Chronicler is doing what he does, that his distinctive vision is part of the inspired witness, and that wrestling with the omission is more faithful than either embarrassed minimization or uncritical acceptance.

**Where this leaves us:** The Chronicler has compressed three chapters of 2 Samuel into eight verses, and the eight verses are not the morally significant ones. The Bathsheba narrative is gone. Solomon's birth is unmentioned (the Chronicler's later chapters will simply have Solomon present, without explaining his maternal origin). Nathan's prophetic confrontation is silent. What remains is the war's victorious end, the spoils, the giant-slayings — the institutional-theological David, shorn of the moral history that 2 Samuel preserves. Whether the cost is acceptable depends on whether the canon's other witness — Samuel — is held in equal weight by the reader. If it is, then the Chronicler's vision is a legitimate theological selection that complements rather than replaces the fuller witness. If Samuel is forgotten, the Chronicler's vision becomes idealized propaganda. The canon contains both. The reader must read both.

**Closing question:** When sacred narrative selects what to remember and what to leave silent, what is the difference between *theological focus* and *historical falsification*? The Chronicler did not invent his David; he selected him. Samuel preserved a different selection. The canon refused to choose. What does it ask of us, that it preserves both?

## The Thread

It is spring, the time when kings go out to battle. Joab goes out. The army goes out. David remains at Jerusalem. In Samuel, this single sentence opens the moral catastrophe that will shape the rest of David's life. In Chronicles, the same sentence opens nothing. The next words are *"And Joab struck down Rabbah."* The roof, the bath, the summons, the cover-up, the murdered husband, the prophet's parable, the dying child, the second son who will be Solomon — all of it is gone. The Chronicler has built a David for the post-exilic temple — legitimate, victorious, the dynastic anchor. He has built him by what he kept and by what he did not. The reader who knows Samuel hears the omission as a roar. The reader who does not know Samuel hears nothing missing. The canon, which contains both books, asks us to be the reader who knows.
