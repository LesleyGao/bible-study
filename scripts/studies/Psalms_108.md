# Psalm 108 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 108 carries the superscription *shir mizmor le-David* (שִׁיר מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד), "A Song. A Psalm of David." But it is the Psalter's most distinctive composite: it is built almost verse-for-verse from material in two earlier psalms. **Verses 1-5 reproduce Ps 57:7-11, and verses 6-13 reproduce Ps 60:5-12.** Psalm 108 is the only canonical psalm constructed from sections of two other psalms, and that fact is the central interpretive datum for understanding it.

In **Gunkel**'s form-critical taxonomy, the source psalms have different forms. Ps 57 is a **Klagelied des Einzelnen** — an individual lament — whose closing portion (vv. 7-11) is the resolved-praise turn at the end of the lament. Ps 60 is a **Volksklage** — a communal lament — pleading after a military setback ("you have rejected us, O God"). Psalm 108, then, splices the *praise turn* of an individual lament onto the *military petition* of a communal lament. The composite functionally becomes a **mixed thanksgiving-and-petition hymn** — confidence first (the praise carried over from Ps 57), then the petition for victory (carried from Ps 60). This sequence is theologically deliberate: the resolved confidence comes first, the unresolved national distress second. The psalmist sings the praise *first*, even as the army has not yet won.

The composition almost certainly belongs to a relatively late stage of the Psalter's editorial life. Most modern critical scholars (Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Allen, Kraus) date Ps 108 to the post-exilic period and read it as evidence of how Davidic psalmody was *rearranged* by later editors to serve new liturgical purposes. The compositional move tells against the assumption that all "Davidic" psalms are simply early; some are late re-recensions. Goldingay calls Ps 108 the "key witness" that the Psalter's editors worked recensionally — splicing existing material into new compositions for the post-exilic temple's needs.

The most striking philological feature is the ***Elohim*-to-*Yahweh* re-recension**. Pss 57 and 60 belong to the *Elohistic Psalter* (Pss 42-83), the section of the Psalter notable for its preference for *Elohim* (אֱלֹהִים) over the divine name *Yahweh* (יהוה). When the editor of Ps 108 lifted material from Pss 57 and 60, he restored *Yahweh* in places where the source psalms read *Elohim*. Compare:

- Ps 57:9 reads *odekha ba-ammim, Adonai* (אוֹדְךָ בָעַמִּים אֲדֹנָי) — "I will give thanks to you among the peoples, O Lord."
- Ps 108:3 reads *odekha ba-ammim Yahweh* (אוֹדְךָ בָעַמִּים יְהוָה) — "I will give thanks to you among the peoples, O LORD."

The shift back to *Yahweh* in the doublet is editorially significant. It suggests Ps 108 was composed in a period when the *Elohim*-preference of Pss 42-83 was no longer the editorial fashion, and when the divine name was being re-emphasized — a feature of Persian-period liturgical sensibility. (The *Elohistic Psalter* itself is usually dated to a slightly earlier period when divine-name avoidance was preferred, perhaps in northern or diaspora contexts.) Hossfeld-Zenger detail this as one of several small but cumulative signs of late composition.

The opening line names the psalm's keynote: *nakhon libbi Elohim* (נָכוֹן לִבִּי אֱלֹהִים) — "my heart is steadfast, O God" (108:1). The verb *nakhon* (steadfast, fixed, established) is the same root used of David's throne being established (2 Sam 7:16), of God's throne being established (Ps 93:2), of right speech (Job 42:7-8). The psalmist's heart, like David's throne and like creation itself, is *fixed*. This is the language of covenantal stability — and the choice to begin the doublet here, at the resolved-confidence moment of the original lament, sets the tone for the whole.

The geographical inventory in vv. 7-9 is the divine speech from Ps 60: Shechem, the Valley of Succoth, Gilead, Manasseh, Ephraim, Judah, Moab, Edom, Philistia. This is the **idealized territory of Davidic Israel** — the central highlands, Transjordan (Gilead, Succoth), the northern tribes (Manasseh, Ephraim), Judah in the south, and the surrounding subjugated peoples (Moab, Edom, Philistia). The geography mattered: it described a kingdom that no longer existed in the late post-exilic period. To sing this geography in the second-temple liturgy was to make a messianic claim — to assert that the territorial promise still stood and would be fulfilled.

There is no DSS scroll preserving Ps 108 in usable form (11Q5 begins later in Book V). The LXX numbers it Psalm 107 and translates faithfully, including the doublet phenomenon — meaning the doublet was already set in the Hebrew Vorlage by the third-second century BCE.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Ps 108's literary identity is inseparable from its compositional history. To read the psalm as if it were an original unity is to miss its primary feature.

**Lowth's** parallelism operates at every level. The opening bicolon — "My heart is steadfast, O God! / I will sing and make melody with all my being!" (108:1) — is *synthetic* parallelism: the second colon extends the first by translating internal steadiness into external praise. The next bicolon — "Awake, O harp and lyre! / I will awake the dawn!" (108:2) — is a striking instance of *climactic* parallelism, building from instrument to cosmos: the singer wakes the harp, the harp wakes him, and together they wake the dawn. The image is unique to Ps 57:8 / Ps 108:2 and has been famous in poetic reception (Donne picked it up, as did Hopkins).

The **strophic structure** of Ps 108 follows two clear movements:

- **Strophe I (vv. 1-5)** — confidence and praise, drawn from Ps 57:7-11. Heart steadfast → instruments awake → praise among the nations → *chesed* above the heavens → *exalted, O God, above the heavens*.
- **Strophe II (vv. 6-13)** — petition for military victory, drawn from Ps 60:5-12. Save by your right hand → divine speech of territorial promise → *who will bring me to Edom?* → "you do not go out with our armies" → *with God we shall do valiantly*.

The hinge is v. 6, which the editor has cleverly positioned. The first strophe ended on "let your glory be over all the earth" (108:5). The second strophe opens "that your beloved ones may be delivered, / give salvation by your right hand and answer me" (108:6). The seam is invisible to the casual reader: the praise of v. 5 flows into the petition of v. 6 as if they were always one composition. The editor's craft lies precisely in making the seam disappear.

There is **inclusio** by way of *chesed* (108:4) and the closing confidence (108:13): *be-Elohim na'aseh chayil* (בֵּאלֹהִים נַעֲשֶׂה־חָיִל), "with God we shall do valiantly." The composition begins in resolved confidence and ends in resolved confidence, even though the middle introduces the unresolved petition of "have you not rejected us, O God? / You do not go out with our armies" (108:11). The arrangement teaches that confidence frames lament — not the other way around.

There is no *selah* in Ps 108 — striking, because Ps 57 (the source of vv. 1-5) has *selah* at its parallel position. The editor of Ps 108 has *removed* the *selah* markings of the source. This is consistent with the broader picture that *selah* falls out of editorial use by the late Persian period, and that Book V (where *selah* appears only sparingly) reflects this later style. The composite, in other words, is not a slavish copy but a stylistic update.

There is also a careful **chiasmus** in vv. 7-9. The divine speech — "I will divide up Shechem" — moves outward through Israelite territories (Shechem, Succoth, Gilead, Manasseh, Ephraim, Judah) and then to the conquered peoples (Moab, Edom, Philistia). The structure is: claim of central territory → core of the kingdom → subjugated periphery. The editor has preserved the structure of the divine speech intact from Ps 60.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Jewish Reception.** Rabbinic tradition was alert to the doublet phenomenon. **Rashi** notes simply that Ps 108 *combines* parts of Pss 57 and 60. The comment is matter-of-fact; the rabbis do not seem to find the composite phenomenon theologically troubling. **Ibn Ezra** takes the doublet as evidence that David himself recombined his own earlier psalms for new liturgical occasions — a position that preserves Davidic authorship at the cost of accepting compositional flexibility within David's own corpus. **Radak** suggests the recombination was post-Davidic, an editorial act for the post-exilic temple's liturgy when the petition of Ps 60 had gained new urgency under foreign domination.

*Midrash Tehillim* on Ps 108 reads it eschatologically — the geographic catalog of vv. 7-9 is a list of the territories Israel will reclaim in the messianic age. The midrash applies "Moab is my washbasin; / upon Edom I cast my shoe" (108:9) to the eschatological subjugation of the nations. This eschatologizing reading would be picked up in different idiom by Christian commentators.

In modern Jewish scholarship, **Levenson** has noted that Ps 108 is the Psalter's clearest evidence that the canonical form is the work of theologically alert editors, not just compilers. The recension preserves David's voice while updating it for a community for whom the territorial promises had become eschatological hopes rather than political descriptions. **Sarna** observes that the *Yahweh*-restoration in Ps 108 is part of a broader Persian-period editorial preference for the divine name — a preference visible also in Chronicles' rewriting of Samuel-Kings.

**Christian Reception.** **Augustine** (*Enarrationes* on Ps 107 LXX) handles the doublet by reading the composite Christologically: the praise of vv. 1-5 is Christ's confidence in the Father; the petition of vv. 6-13 is the church's prayer for victory over its enemies. Augustine reads "Edom" allegorically as the world's flesh-bound resistance to Christ. **Cassiodorus** develops the same Christological reading and adds an anaphoric note: the composite tells us that the church's liturgy is constructed by combining earlier prayer-traditions for new occasions.

**Aquinas** (lectures on the Psalms, *Postilla super Psalmos*) treats Ps 108 as straightforwardly Davidic and reads the territorial promise as referring to the universal kingship of Christ ("over Philistia I shout in triumph"). **Luther** (*Operationes*) stays close to the Christological reading and emphasizes the v. 13 conclusion — *with God we shall do valiantly* — as the right speech of Christian confidence in the spiritual battle.

**Calvin** (1557 commentary) takes the doublet seriously and is the first major Christian commentator to argue that Ps 108 is *post-Davidic* — that an editor composed it from the two earlier psalms. Calvin saw no theological problem in this; Davidic authorship was for him about the canonical voice and tradition, not necessarily individual penmanship.

In the modern critical tradition, **Gunkel** and **Mowinckel** treated Ps 108 as a textbook case of late editorial composition. **Westermann** observed that the doublet showed how lament-and-praise forms could be recombined for new liturgical purposes — the Psalter's forms are not rigid but recombinable. **Kraus** detailed the *Elohim*-to-*Yahweh* shift and dated the composition to the early post-exilic period. **Hossfeld-Zenger** developed the most sophisticated reading: Ps 108 stands at the head of the small Davidic collection within Book V (108-110), opening a cluster that will reach its climax in the royal-priestly Christology of Ps 110. They read Ps 108 as the editor's signal that Book V's Davidic recovery is *re-recensional* — taking earlier Davidic material and reassembling it for the eschatological-restoration program.

**Goldingay** has been the most insistent that Ps 108 is the key witness to how the Psalter was formed. The composition shows that the Psalter's editors (1) had access to fixed earlier psalms, (2) felt free to splice them, (3) preferred *Yahweh* in their re-recensions, and (4) used the splicing to create new theological compositions appropriate to the post-exilic situation.

In **NT use**, Ps 108 is not directly cited. But the language of the composite — particularly *odekha ba-ammim, Yahweh* (108:3, "I will give thanks to you among the peoples, O LORD") — feeds Paul's argument in Rom 15:9-12, where he chains together OT texts that promise Gentile praise of YHWH. Paul does not cite Ps 108 directly but cites Ps 18:49 (the parallel verse in another Davidic context). The thematic line — Davidic praise extending to the nations — runs through Ps 108 into Paul's missionary theology.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What the composite-text phenomenon means for Psalter formation.**

Ps 108 is the smoking gun that the Psalter's editors worked recensionally. Three positions on what to make of this:

*Position A: The traditional position — Davidic re-use.* Ibn Ezra and others have held that David himself recombined his own earlier psalms for new occasions. On this reading, the composite is evidence of liturgical flexibility within a single author's corpus. The doublet does not threaten Davidic authorship; it merely shows that David, like any working liturgist, repeated and rearranged his own material.

*Position B: The critical position — late editorial re-recension.* Calvin, Goldingay, Hossfeld-Zenger, and most modern scholars hold that Ps 108 was composed by a post-exilic editor who took fixed Davidic psalms and recombined them for the second-temple liturgy. The *Elohim*-to-*Yahweh* shift, the absent *selah*, the placement at the head of a Book V Davidic cluster — all these argue for late editorial composition. Davidic authorship is preserved as canonical voice rather than individual penmanship.

*Position C: The mediating position — Davidic tradition with editorial freedom.* The composite belongs to a Davidic *tradition* — a body of psalmic material associated with David's name and used in temple worship — within which editors were free to recombine. This sidesteps the binary of "David himself" versus "post-exilic editor" and reads the Davidic superscriptions as marking *the tradition the material belongs to*, not necessarily individual authorship.

**Steelman:** The mediating position is probably right. The Davidic superscription is theologically claiming the psalm for the Davidic tradition; the compositional features show late editorial work. The Psalter's editors did not see these as in tension. To attribute a composite to David was to say: this is the prayer of the Davidic king, the messianic figure whose voice is preserved in this tradition. The historical David's individual authorship was not the point.

**Tension 2: Doublet Christology — what does it mean that Christ's voice in the Psalter is constructed by the church's editors?**

If Ps 108 is the work of post-exilic editors splicing earlier material, and if Christian tradition reads the Psalter's Davidic voice as the voice of Christ, then a question arises: what does it mean that the Christ-voice in the Psalter is itself a *constructed* voice? Three positions:

*Position A: The traditional Christian reading — providential editing.* Augustine, Cassiodorus, Aquinas all assumed that the Psalter's editors worked under providence to produce the canonical text. The composite character of Ps 108 is not a theological problem because the Spirit who inspired David also inspired the editors who recombined his material. The Christ-voice that emerges from the canonical Ps 108 is the Christ-voice the church receives.

*Position B: The historical-critical reading — secular editing, theological reception.* The historical work of the editors was a normal scribal-liturgical activity; the theological appropriation by the church is a separate (and legitimate) act of reception. The doublet does not threaten the Christological reading because the Christological reading does not depend on individual Davidic authorship — it depends on the canonical text the church receives.

*Position C: The hermeneutical-deepening reading — the church's voice in Christ's voice.* If Ps 108 is the church's editorial composition, then when the church prays it as the voice of Christ, what is happening is that *the church's own constructed Christ-voice is the voice it then receives back as Christ's voice to the church*. This is not a vicious circle but a hermeneutical one: the church speaks the Christ-voice into existence editorially and then is shaped by what it has spoken. Brueggemann gestures toward this reading; it sits well with a more constructive theological view of canon-as-living-tradition.

**Steelman:** Each position serves a different theological purpose. Position A sustains traditional confessional reading; Position B preserves historical honesty; Position C opens a constructive theology of canon. They are compatible if held in proper relation: the church receives the canonical text as authoritative (A), acknowledges the historical complexity of its formation (B), and recognizes that the canon's voice is something the community both receives and participates in shaping (C).

**Where this leaves us.** Psalm 108 is the Psalter's most unambiguous evidence that the canonical text was *edited*. It tells us that the Davidic voice in Book V is, at least in this case, a deliberate editorial recombination. It tells us that the post-exilic community needed both confidence (vv. 1-5) and unresolved petition (vv. 6-13) and that the editor placed confidence first. It tells us that the *Elohim*-to-*Yahweh* re-recension reflects a Persian-period preference for the divine name. It tells us that the territorial geography of David's promised kingdom was sung in second-temple liturgy as eschatological hope, not political description. And it teaches the church that singing the Davidic voice always means singing a voice the community has helped shape — a fact more challenging to certain theological framings than it is comforting, but one the canon itself does not hide.

**Closing question.** If Ps 108 is the editor's deliberate splice — confidence first, then the petition that has not yet been answered — what does it mean for a community today to learn to begin in confidence, even when the army has not yet won?
