# Daniel 12: Resurrection, Numbers, and the Book's Climactic Word

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter is the climactic conclusion of the Daniel 10–12 vision-discourse and the climactic ending of the Hebrew book of Daniel. Its historical setting is identical to chapter 10's: "the third year of Cyrus king of Persia" (Dan 10:1), 537/536 BC, with Daniel on the bank of the Tigris. The angel's chronological revelation continues uninterrupted from chapter 11 into chapter 12; the chapter division is medieval, not original. The historical horizon described — "a time of trouble, such as never has been since there was a nation till that time" (12:1) — is, on the critical reading, the Antiochene crisis of 167–164 BC; on the conservative reading, an eschatological tribulation still future.

Twelve verses are the densest twelve in the entire Hebrew Bible. The chapter opens with Michael's eschatological intervention (12:1), proclaims the resurrection of the dead (12:2), promises astral glory to the *maskilim* (12:3), commands Daniel to seal the book (12:4), describes a heavenly scene with two angelic figures questioning the man in linen above the waters (12:5–7), records Daniel's confession of incomprehension (12:8), receives the closing dismissal and the famous chronological numbers (12:9–13).

The political-cultic horizon is the desecration of the temple by Antiochus IV in December 167 BC — "from the time that the regular burnt offering is taken away and the abomination that makes desolate is set up" (12:11). The phrase *the regular burnt offering* refers to the *tamid*, the twice-daily burnt offering required by Numbers 28:3–8 and Exodus 29:38–42, which constituted the core of Jerusalem temple worship. The cessation of the *tamid* under Antiochus is recorded in **1 Maccabees 1:45** ("[Antiochus] forbade burnt offerings and sacrifices and drink offerings in the sanctuary") and was experienced by the faithful as a catastrophe of cosmic proportions. The 1,290 days (12:11) and 1,335 days (12:12) are conventionally interpreted in connection with this desecration: 1,290 days = roughly 3.5 years (3 × 360 + 210), the same span as the *time, times, and half a time* of 12:7 expanded into days, with 1,335 days being a slightly longer period that adds 45 days to the rededication horizon.

The historical correlation: Antiochus's altar to Zeus Olympios was set up on the temple's altar of burnt offering on 15 Chislev 167 BC (1 Macc 1:54); the rededication of the temple under Judas Maccabeus occurred on 25 Chislev 164 BC (1 Macc 4:52–59), the founding moment of Hanukkah. The interval from the desecration to the rededication is approximately 1,150 days (just over 3 years), which fits Daniel 8:14's *2,300 evenings and mornings* (1,150 days under one reading) but does not exactly match Daniel 12:11's 1,290 or 12:12's 1,335. Critical scholars (Collins, Newsom) read these numbers as the author's prospective expectation of how the crisis would resolve, written during the desecration period itself, with the discrepancies between the various numerical schemes (8:14, 12:11, 12:12) reflecting the author's progressive recalculation as the desecration period stretched beyond initial expectations. **John Collins** in his Hermeneia commentary takes the discrepancies as confirming the Maccabean dating: a later author would have written numbers that fit the actual rededication exactly, while an author writing during the crisis would have produced numbers that approximated but did not exactly match the eventual outcome.

Material culture: the *tamid* was the daily heart of Jerusalem temple worship; its cessation was experienced as the world's interruption. The *abomination that makes desolate* (*shiqquts meshomem*) is the Hebrew of 11:31 and 12:11, which **1 Maccabees 1:54** transliterates into the Greek *bdelygma erēmōseōs* — the same phrase Jesus cites in Mark 13:14 / Matthew 24:15. The Greek *bdelygma* / Hebrew *shiqquts* root denotes ritual abomination; the *meshomem* / *erēmōseōs* root denotes desolation, the cessation of normal cultic life. **Émile Puech**'s work on the Qumran Daniel manuscripts demonstrates that the chapter was textually stable by the late 2nd century BC — fragments of Daniel 12 are preserved at 4QDan-c and 4QDan-d.

The closing image of two angelic figures on opposite banks of the river questioning a third figure above the waters (12:5–7) recalls and inverts the formal structure of chapter 10's vision: the man in linen is now in the position of authoritative teacher answering the angelic questioners. The raising of both hands toward heaven (12:7) is a doubled oath gesture — the standard oath posture (Gen 14:22, Deut 32:40) intensified.

Authorship and dating: Daniel 12 contains the OT's clearest resurrection passage (12:2) and its climactic chronological riddles (12:11–12). The critical consensus reads it as composed during the Antiochene crisis (between 167 and 164 BC). **Collins**, **Newsom**, **Maurice Casey**, **James Montgomery** (ICC), **R.H. Charles** (whose *Eschatology* remains foundational), **H.H. Rowley**, **Klaus Koch**, **Norman Porteous** all hold the late date. Conservative scholarship — **Stephen Miller** (NAC), **Joyce Baldwin** (TOTC), **Gleason Archer**, **Edward Young** — reads the chapter as 6th-century predictive prophecy.

Key Hebrew terms: *Mikhael ha-sar ha-gadol* (מִיכָאֵל הַשַּׂר הַגָּדוֹל, "Michael the great prince," 12:1); *yikitzu* (יָקִיצוּ, "shall awake," 12:2 — the technical resurrection verb); *chayyei olam* (חַיֵּי עוֹלָם, "everlasting life," 12:2 — the OT's only unambiguous occurrence); *dir'on olam* (דֵּרָאוֹן עוֹלָם, "everlasting contempt," 12:2 — *dir'on* is rare, paralleled only in Isa 66:24); *maskilim* (מַשְׂכִּלִים, "the wise," 12:3, 10); *qets* (קֵץ, "end," 12:4, 13); *moed moadim va-chetzi* (מוֹעֵד מוֹעֲדִים וָחֵצִי, "a time, times, and half a time," 12:7 — paralleled in Dan 7:25, Rev 12:14).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is the formal close of Daniel 10–12's vision-discourse and the structural close of the entire book. The literary form is **apocalyptic conclusion**: angelic command to seal the book (12:4, 9), divine oath about the duration of the crisis (12:7), prophetic dismissal of the visionary (12:13). The pattern parallels 1 Enoch's closing chapters and the conclusions of the Apocalypse of Abraham and 4 Ezra.

The resurrection passage in 12:2 is the chapter's literary climax. The Hebrew is precisely worded: *ve-rabbim mi-yeshenei admat-afar yaqitzu* — "and many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake." The *many* (*rabbim*) has been variously interpreted: as a partial resurrection of the righteous and the wicked of the immediate Antiochene crisis (Newsom, Collins), as a representative *many* indicating universal scope (most patristic-medieval reading), as a term for the totality of the dead in some Hebraic idioms. The verb *yaqitzu* ("shall awake") is the technical Hebrew resurrection verb that occurs also in Isaiah 26:19. The contrast — *some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt* — is the OT's unique unambiguous statement of *differentiated* resurrection, dividing the awakened into two destinies. **Jon Levenson**'s *Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel* (2006) is the major recent scholarly treatment and argues against reading this as Persian Zoroastrian importation.

The astral imagery in 12:3 — *the wise shall shine like the brightness of the sky above; and those who turn many to righteousness, like the stars forever and ever* — has been read as **astral immortality** (the wise becoming literal stars, a motif paralleled in 1 Enoch and in some Hellenistic-Jewish tomb inscriptions), as metaphorical glorification, or as the foundation for the later Christian beatific vision and rabbinic Olam Ha-Ba. **Daniel Boyarin** notes that the imagery sits at the intersection of indigenous Israelite resurrection theology and Hellenistic astral-eschatology. The Greek versions (Old Greek and Theodotion) handle the imagery differently — Theodotion preserves the more literal sense.

The chronological numbers are the chapter's literary mystery: a *time, times, and half a time* (12:7) corresponds to 7:25's same phrase and most plausibly equals 3.5 years; 1,290 days (12:11) = approximately 3.5 lunar-and-solar years on a 360-day prophetic calendar with intercalation; 1,335 days (12:12) = 1,290 + 45. The discrepancies among Daniel's various numerical schemes (8:14's 2,300 evenings and mornings, 9:24–27's seventy weeks broken into 7 + 62 + 1, 12:11's 1,290, 12:12's 1,335) have generated centuries of harmonizing calculation. The Old Greek and Theodotion preserve slight numerical variants. **The textual evidence suggests the numbers were already opaque to the earliest Greek translators.**

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The chapter establishes resurrection as a central theological category in Israel's scripture. Prior OT references to resurrection are debated. **Isaiah 26:19** ("Your dead shall live; their bodies shall rise") is read by some as earlier and more original than Daniel 12, by others as a later post-exilic gloss; **Levenson** argues both are organic developments from prior covenantal categories rather than Persian importations. **Ezekiel 37**'s vision of the dry bones is national-restoration metaphor on most readings, though some patristic and rabbinic readers treated it as literal future resurrection. **Job 19:25–27** ("I know that my Redeemer lives... in my flesh I shall see God") is grammatically obscure and disputed. **Psalm 16:10** ("you will not abandon my soul to Sheol") was read messianically and resurrectionally by 1st-century Christianity (Acts 2:27). But **Daniel 12:2 is the unambiguous OT resurrection text** — the one passage where bodies in the dust awake to differentiated eschatological destinies. **Wright**'s *The Resurrection of the Son of God* makes Daniel 12:2 the ground of pre-Christian Jewish resurrection theology that the apostolic preaching takes as already established.

**Jewish Trajectory**: 2 Maccabees 7's seven brothers martyrdom narrative is the explicit application of Daniel 12 — the brothers and their mother die in defiance of Antiochus's persecution, confessing resurrection hope. The Wisdom of Solomon (3:1–9), Psalms of Solomon 3, and 4 Ezra 7 develop differentiated-resurrection theology directly from Daniel 12. The Mishnah (Sanhedrin 10:1) makes belief in resurrection a defining Jewish doctrine: "All Israel has a portion in the world to come... but the following have no portion: he who maintains that resurrection is not a biblical doctrine." Rabbinic Judaism's *Olam Ha-Ba* doctrine cites Daniel 12 as foundational. **Daniel Boyarin**'s *The Jewish Gospels* and Levenson's *Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel* both insist that Christian resurrection theology inherits a fully formed Jewish doctrine rather than inventing one. **Saadia Gaon**, **Maimonides** (his Thirteen Principles include resurrection), **Abrabanel** all read Daniel 12 as the OT foundation of Jewish resurrection hope.

**Christian Trajectory**: 1 Corinthians 15 is the major Pauline development. Paul's argument — that Christ's resurrection is the *firstfruits* of the general resurrection — presupposes the Daniel 12 framework. The Synoptic Gospels' resurrection theology and the Johannine "I am the resurrection and the life" build on the same foundation. **Hippolytus** read Daniel 12 as both first-advent fulfillment and future eschatological resurrection. **Jerome's *Commentary on Daniel*** (407) treats 12:2 as the unambiguous proof-text for bodily resurrection against the Origenist tendency to spiritualize. **Augustine**'s *City of God* uses Daniel 12 in his eschatological scheme. The medieval tradition — Aquinas's *Summa Theologica* III Suppl., the Reformation confessions, the Catholic Catechism — all anchor resurrection doctrine on Daniel 12. **Calvin's *Lectures on Daniel*** (1561) reads 12:2 carefully as the foundation of differentiated resurrection. **Wright**'s *The Resurrection of the Son of God* is the major contemporary Protestant treatment.

The chronological numbers have generated radically divergent traditions. The *historicist* tradition (Joachim of Fiore, Reformation prophetic interpretation, William Miller's Adventism) read the days as years and applied them to ecclesiastical-political history culminating in their own time. The *futurist-dispensational* tradition (Darby, Scofield, Walvoord, LaHaye) reads the numbers as a future seven-year Tribulation. The *preterist* tradition (R.H. Charles in part, contemporary scholars like Kenneth Gentry) reads them as fulfilled in the Antiochene crisis or in AD 70. The *idealist* tradition reads them symbolically as recurrent patterns. The Maccabean-critical reading (Collins, Newsom) takes them as fulfilled in the original 2nd-century crisis.

**Where they diverge**: The resurrection passage is genuinely common ground — Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, Jewish, and most critical scholars agree that 12:2 teaches something like differentiated bodily resurrection (with Levenson's caveats about the originality of the doctrine within Israelite categories). The chronological numbers divide bitterly: dispensationalist futurism reads them as a future Tribulation; the critical-Maccabean reading takes them as originally fulfilled; historicist Adventism reads them as referring to AD 1843–44 (William Miller's calculation, the famous *Great Disappointment*); idealist readings demur entirely.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The origin of resurrection theology.** The standard critical view (developed in the late 19th and early 20th c. and still defended by **R.H. Charles**, **Klaus Koch**, **Émile Puech**, and many in the History-of-Religions school) is that bodily resurrection entered Jewish theology through Persian Zoroastrian influence during the post-exilic period — that Daniel 12 reflects Hellenistic-period Jewish encounter with Iranian eschatological dualism (the *frashokereti*, the renewal-of-the-world). On this view, resurrection is a relatively late doctrinal innovation in Israel, foreign to the indigenous Hebrew theological vocabulary. **Jon Levenson**'s *Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel* (2006) is the major recent challenge to this thesis. Levenson argues — carefully and with extensive textual analysis — that resurrection theology emerges organically from within Israelite covenantal categories: Sheol-and-deliverance language in the Psalms, national-restoration imagery in Ezekiel 37, the *teshuvah* and *tikkun* concepts in the prophets, the covenantal logic of God's faithfulness extending beyond death. Persian influence may have given the doctrine specific imagery and articulation but did not generate the underlying theological categories. Steelman the Persian-influence reading: the historical timing fits (Persian period and immediately after); the differentiated-resurrection-with-judgment scheme is recognizably Iranian; the absence of clear earlier OT resurrection texts suggests late development. Steelman Levenson's reading: Persian dualism is doctrinally and structurally different from Daniel 12's resurrection in important ways; the covenantal logic of resurrection (God's *hesed* extending beyond death) is internally Israelite; Isaiah 26 and the prophetic restoration tradition provide indigenous antecedents. For Christian readers, the doctrinal stake is significant: if resurrection is a late, Persian-influenced importation, the apostolic resurrection preaching is making a distinct historical claim about Christ's bodily resurrection without the deep biblical pedigree it appears to claim; if Levenson is right, the apostolic preaching draws on a doctrine deeply rooted in Israelite covenantal theology going back into the pre-exilic period in seed form. **Where this leaves us:** the question is unsettled in current scholarship. The text of Daniel 12:2 says what it says; the historical genealogy of the doctrine is genuinely contested.

**Tension 2: The chronological numbers — Maccabean specificity, eschatological future, or symbolic recurrence?** The numbers (1,290 days, 1,335 days, time-times-and-half-a-time) are the textual ground of Anglo-American evangelical end-times speculation as much as any other passage. The Maccabean reading (Collins, Newsom): the numbers are the author's prospective expectation of how the Antiochene desecration would resolve, written in 165–164 BC, tracking from the desecration of 15 Chislev 167 BC through projected vindication. The dispensational reading (Darby, Scofield, Walvoord): the numbers describe a future seven-year Tribulation, with the 1,290 and 1,335 days marking the second half of the Tribulation plus an additional period for the kingdom's establishment. The historicist reading (Miller, Adventist tradition): the numbers are days-as-years pointing to specific calendar dates in church history. The idealist reading: the numbers are symbolic of recurrent persecution-and-vindication patterns. The stakes for evangelical eschatology are enormous: Hal Lindsey's *Late Great Planet Earth* (1970), the *Left Behind* novels, and the entirety of dispensational popular eschatology rest on a futurist reading of these numbers and Daniel 9's 70th week. **Newsom**'s OTL commentary represents the mediating critical position: the numbers reflect the author's eschatological expectation of how the immediate crisis would resolve, a poetic-prophetic specification rather than a literal calendar; the discrepancies among Daniel's various numerical schemes (8:14, 9:24–27, 12:11, 12:12) suggest progressive recalculation as expectations were not exactly met. Steelman the dispensational reading honestly: the numbers' apparent precision suggests literal intent; the New Testament's apocalyptic eschatology (2 Thess 2, Rev 12–13) clearly draws on Daniel's framework as forward-looking. Steelman the Maccabean reading honestly: the numbers' actual fit to the historical Antiochene desecration is approximately right but not exact, suggesting a writer in the middle of the crisis projecting an expected resolution rather than a 6th-century prophet predicting a future one or a literal future fulfillment. *Where this leaves us:* the architecture of dispensational eschatology — from Lindsey through LaHaye through ongoing prophecy-conference culture — depends on a particular reading of these twelve verses' numerical riddles. If the Maccabean reading is right, that entire structure rests on a misreading; if the dispensational reading is right, the critical consensus has missed something significant about how prophecy works. This is not a small disagreement.

**The unresolved question:** If the dating debate is irresolvable but the resurrection promise is unambiguous, what does it actually mean to live as one of the *maskilim* in a time of crisis you cannot predict? The chapter's last word to Daniel — *Go your way till the end. And you shall rest and shall stand in your allotted place at the end of the days* (12:13) — does not solve the puzzle of when the end will come or how the numbers work. It tells the prophet to walk away from his own questions, trust the resurrection, and leave the calculation to God. The book ends not with a key to the chronology but with a dismissal that refuses to give one. Perhaps the calculation has always been the wrong question.

## The Thread

**Isaiah 26:19** — "Your dead shall live; their bodies shall rise. You who dwell in the dust, awake and sing for joy!" The closest OT parallel to Daniel 12:2, possibly earlier, possibly later. The shared verb *yaqitzu* / *yaqumun* and the shared dust imagery anchor an indigenous Israelite resurrection trajectory.

**2 Maccabees 7** — the seven brothers martyrdom narrative, the explicit Jewish application of Daniel 12 to the Antiochene persecution. "The King of the universe will raise us up to an everlasting renewal of life, because we have died for his laws" (2 Macc 7:9). The most direct evidence that 2nd-century Jewish faithful were reading and dying on the basis of Daniel 12.

**1 Corinthians 15:51–52** — "We shall all be changed, in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet... and the dead shall be raised incorruptible, and we shall be changed." Pauline resurrection theology presupposes Daniel 12 — the *many* who awake, the differentiation between life and shame, the general-resurrection framework. The apostolic preaching builds on Daniel.

**Mark 13:19 / Matthew 24:21** — Jesus's apocalyptic discourse cites Daniel 12:1 directly: "there will be great tribulation, such as has not been from the beginning of the world until now, no, and never will be." The Synoptic Apocalypse is Daniel 12's most consequential New Testament heir.

**Revelation 12:14** — "the woman was given the two wings of the great eagle so that she might fly from the serpent into the wilderness, to the place where she is to be nourished for *a time, and times, and half a time*." The Daniel 12:7 phrase enters Christian apocalyptic at its highest pitch. Whatever the original referent, the phrase becomes the church's measuring rod for eschatological tribulation.
