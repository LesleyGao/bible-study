# Psalm 41 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 41 carries the heading *lamnatzeach mizmor le-David* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד, "to the choirmaster. A psalm of David") and bears the distinction of **closing Book I** of the Psalter (Psalms 1-41). The structure of the Hebrew Psalter into five books — Pss 1-41, 42-72, 73-89, 90-106, 107-150 — is marked by closing doxologies at 41:13, 72:18-20, 89:52, 106:48, with the entire collection of Pss 146-150 functioning as the doxological close of the whole Psalter. Gerald Wilson's foundational *The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* (1985) established the canonical-criticism approach that reads these book-divisions as deliberate editorial structuring, and most subsequent scholarship — Patrick Miller, J. Clinton McCann, Nancy deClaissé-Walford (*Reading from the Beginning*), David Howard, Hossfeld-Zenger — has built on Wilson's framework. The Psalter is not a random anthology; its sequence and book-divisions carry theological weight.

Form-critically, Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 41 as an **individual lament** (*Klagelied des Einzelnen*) with thanksgiving elements. The lament structure is clear: complaint about illness (vv. 3-4, 8), enemies' malicious speech (vv. 5-7), the betrayal of an intimate friend (v. 9), petition for healing (v. 10), confession of trust (vv. 11-12). The opening *ashrei*-beatitude (v. 1) functions as a wisdom-introduction, and the closing doxology (v. 13) is editorial — the scribal seal closing Book I. Westermann's lament-typology (address, complaint, petition, confession of trust) fits the body of the psalm closely.

Dating is contested. The Davidic ascription is traditional. Mowinckel and the cultic school connect the sickness-and-friend-betrayal motifs to royal-Davidic crises (perhaps Absalom's revolt, with Ahithophel as the betraying intimate — 2 Sam 15-17). The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of Psalm 41 in 4QPsa and 11QPsa, with text largely matching the Masoretic. The Septuagint (Ps 40 LXX) preserves the doxology of v. 13 and reads v. 9's *megaduwon ha-akev* ("lifted his heel") as *epēren ep' eme pternismon* ("lifted up his heel against me") — a literal rendering that John 13:18 will quote.

The opening word *ashrei* (אַשְׁרֵי, "blessed / happy") is the same word that opens Book I at Psalm 1:1 — *ashrei ha-ish asher lo halakh ba-atzat resha'im* ("blessed is the man who walks not in the counsel of the wicked"). The *ashrei*-inclusio between Pss 1 and 41 has long been noted by canonical-critical readers. Patrick Miller calls this a "deliberate framing" of Book I; Gerald Wilson and J. Clinton McCann see it as a structural seal. Whether the framing is original or editorial, the textual datum is firm.

Hebrew terms drive the meaning. *Maskil el-dal* (מַשְׂכִּיל אֶל־דָּל, v. 1, "considers the poor") — the verb *sakal* in the hiphil means to act wisely, attend insightfully, with discernment; the *dal* is the socially weak or impoverished. The opening beatitude promises divine attention to the one whose attention is on the poor. *Ish shelomi asher batachti vo okhel lachmi higdil alai akev* (אִישׁ שְׁלוֹמִי אֲשֶׁר־בָּטַחְתִּי בוֹ אוֹכֵל לַחְמִי הִגְדִּיל עָלַי עָקֵב, v. 9, "my close friend in whom I trusted, who ate my bread, has lifted his heel against me") — *ish shelomi*, "the man of my peace," is the most intimate covenantal-friendship vocabulary; *higdil … akev*, "magnified the heel," is a powerful insult-gesture suggesting trampling, contempt, or the act of walking out and turning the back.

The *barukh*-doxology of v. 13 — *barukh Yahweh Elohei Yisrael me-ha-olam ve-ad ha-olam amen ve-amen* (בָּרוּךְ יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל מֵהָעוֹלָם וְעַד הָעוֹלָם אָמֵן וְאָמֵן) — is the editorial close of Book I. The doubled *amen ve-amen* signals liturgical assent. The same doubled-amen formula closes Book II (72:19-20) and Book III (89:52); Book IV's close at 106:48 has a single amen plus *halleluyah*.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Robert Lowth's parallelism patterns are dense throughout. The opening triplet (vv. 1-2) builds in synonymous parallelism: "the LORD delivers him / the LORD protects him and keeps him alive / he is called blessed in the land." The verbs accumulate the dimensions of divine care: deliver, protect, keep alive, bless.

Strophic structure has been variously analyzed. The most common division:

- **Strophe 1 (vv. 1-3):** the opening *ashrei*-beatitude — the wisdom-promise that the one who attends to the *dal* will be attended to in his own day of trouble.
- **Strophe 2 (vv. 4-9):** the lament proper — the worshipper's confession (v. 4), the enemies' malice (vv. 5-8), the climactic intimate-betrayal (v. 9).
- **Strophe 3 (vv. 10-12):** the petition and confession of trust — *raise me up* (v. 10), *I know* (v. 11), *upheld me … set me in your presence forever* (v. 12).
- **Editorial coda (v. 13):** the doxology closing Book I.

The structural pivot is verse 9, which functions as the climactic complaint. The intimate-friend's betrayal exceeds the enemies' malice in psychological gravity — the enemies are expected; the *ish shelomi* who ate my bread is the wound beneath the wound.

Inclusio operates at multiple levels. Within the psalm, the *Yahweh* address opens (v. 1, "the LORD delivers") and is paired with the closing *Adonai* / *Yahweh* of vv. 10, 13 — divine-rescue framing the whole. At the canonical level, the *ashrei* opening connects to Psalm 1:1's *ashrei*, framing all forty-one psalms of Book I. This canonical inclusio is one of the strongest pieces of evidence for deliberate editorial shaping of the Psalter.

There is no *Selah* in Psalm 41. Its movement is brief, focused, and uninterrupted — appropriate for a closing piece.

The phrase *higdil alai akev* (v. 9) is poetically dense. The verb *higdil* (to magnify, make great) usually has positive connotations; here it is ironically used of an act of contempt. The *akev* (heel) is the body-part associated with hostility from Genesis 3:15 onward (*ve-attah teshufenu akev*, "and you shall bruise his heel") — the heel is the place of vulnerability and the site of attack. The image of an intimate "lifting the heel" is gestural-symbolic: turning to walk away in contempt, or trampling.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Psalm 41 closes Book I with the *ashrei*-inclusio binding back to Psalm 1, and with the editorial doxology launching the reader into Book II. The position is structurally significant. Gerald Wilson's *The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* (1985) treats Pss 1-41 as a coherent Davidic collection bracketed by *ashrei*; Patrick Miller's *Interpreting the Psalms* extends the analysis; Nancy deClaissé-Walford's *Reading from the Beginning* and the later Wallace-Tanner-deClaissé-Walford commentary develop the canonical-shape readings further. David Howard's *The Structure of Psalms 93-100* and J. Clinton McCann's *A Theological Introduction to the Book of Psalms* extend the methodology to other portions of the Psalter.

**Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 41 develops v. 1's *maskil el-dal* into extended teaching on tzedakah and the eschatological reward of those who attend to the poor. Rashi reads v. 9 — *ish shelomi* — as Ahithophel, David's counselor who joined Absalom's revolt (2 Sam 15:12, 17:1-23). The traditional Jewish identification of Ahithophel as the "intimate betrayer" is widespread, though some midrashic sources also read the verse generally. Ibn Ezra reads more universally, treating the friend-betrayal as a pattern any sufferer might recognize. Radak engages both readings. Nachmanides develops the connection between sickness, sin, and intercession (vv. 4, 10) within his broader theology of divine providence and the body.

In modern Jewish scholarship: Nahum Sarna treats Ps 41 as a sickbed-lament with the closing doxology as editorial. Jon Levenson's work on the Psalter as Israel's worship-anthology engages the canonical-shape question. Buber, in *Right and Wrong*, attends to the psychology of intimate betrayal — the *ish shelomi*'s heel is the deepest wound the worshipper articulates, and the prayer for divine response is what stands between the wound and despair. Heschel emphasizes the wisdom-opening: the one who *attends to the poor* is the one whom YHWH attends. Avivah Zornberg's literary readings would attend to the strangeness of the worshipper's prayer in v. 10 — *raise me up, that I may repay them* — the imprecation embedded in the petition.

Liturgically, Psalm 41 features in some Jewish morning prayers and in traditions of recitation for the sick. The closing doxology is a beloved liturgical formula.

**Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* (Latin Ps 40) reads the entire psalm Christologically. The opening *ashrei*-beatitude becomes a portrait of Christ who supremely "considers the poor"; the sickbed becomes the cross; v. 9 — *ish shelomi* — is read of Judas; v. 13's doxology is the Triune blessing. Augustine's reading of v. 9 as Judas is grounded explicitly in John 13:18, where Jesus himself cites the verse. Athanasius recommends the psalm to those facing illness combined with social betrayal. Cassiodorus structures his commentary around the opening beatitude and the closing doxology.

Aquinas (*Postilla*) treats vv. 1-3 as a wisdom-beatitude on the *misericordia* extended to the poor, drawing on the same Matthew 25 framework that informs his treatment of the works of mercy. Bonaventure's social-spiritual writings echo the psalm's opening beatitude. Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 41 draws out the *coram Deo* dimension — the worshipper sick, betrayed, and sustained by YHWH's attention. Calvin's 1557 commentary engages the canonical-shape question (without using the modern terminology), noting the appropriateness of the closing doxology.

Among modern critical interpreters: Gunkel and Mowinckel; Westermann's lament-typology; Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate, Wenham (*The Psalter Reclaimed*), Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*), Patrick Miller, William P. Brown (*Seeing the Psalms*), Beth Tanner, Nancy deClaissé-Walford, Susan Gillingham (*Psalms Through the Centuries*) all engage the psalm with attention to its canonical position. Gerald Wilson's *Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* is the foundational methodological work for reading Ps 41 as Book I's closing seal. J. Clinton McCann's "Books I-III and the Editorial Purpose of the Hebrew Psalter" (in *The Shape and Shaping of the Psalter*, 1993) develops the argument further.

**NT use.** John 13:18 — *all' hina hē graphē plērōthē, ho trōgōn mou ton arton epēren ep' eme tēn pternan autou* ("but the Scripture will be fulfilled, 'he who ate my bread has lifted his heel against me'") — Jesus himself cites Ps 41:9 at the Last Supper, applying it to Judas's betrayal. This is one of the most direct Christological appropriations of an individual-lament verse in the Gospels. The use is striking: Jesus reads David's intimate-betrayal experience as type, and his own moment as antitype. The application makes the psalm canonically a Passion-anticipation alongside Pss 22, 31, 69.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The closing-Book-I structure — coincidence or canonical shaping?** The *ashrei*-inclusio between Pss 1 and 41 and the doxology of 41:13 raise the question of editorial intention. (a) **Coincidence reading (some older critical scholarship):** the two *ashrei*-openings are accidental; the doxology is a late liturgical addition without compositional significance. (b) **Editorial-shaping reading (Wilson, Miller, McCann, deClaissé-Walford, mainstream contemporary scholarship):** the inclusio and doxology are deliberate marks of editorial intentionality; Pss 1-41 form a coherent Davidic collection bracketed by Torah-meditation and wisdom-beatitude. (c) **Maximalist canonical reading (Childs' *Introduction to the Old Testament as Scripture*, the broader canonical approach):** the editorial shaping carries theological meaning — the entire Psalter is to be read as a literary-theological whole, with structural cues guiding interpretation. (d) **Cautious-skeptical reading (some recent critical voices):** patterns can be over-read; not every textual feature is editorially intentional; we should be careful not to project modern compositional design onto ancient editorial work.

The strongest steelman is the editorial-shaping reading. The data are not a single feature but a cluster: the *ashrei*-inclusio, the doxology, the predominantly Davidic ascription of Book I (Pss 3-41 are Davidic with a few exceptions), the contrast with the Korahite-Asaphite shape of Book II. Wilson's argument — that the Psalter has been deliberately edited as a story of David, the failure of the Davidic monarchy, and the eschatological hope of YHWH's kingship — is grounded in this kind of structural data. The closing of Book I with a sickbed-betrayal lament that ends in praise (and the editorial doxology) prepares the reader for Book II's deepening engagement with Davidic crises.

**Tension 2: The betrayer-Judas reading of v. 9.** John 13:18's application of Ps 41:9 to Judas raises the question of how original text and later application relate. (a) **Original-Davidic reading (most historical-critical scholarship):** v. 9 is David's complaint about a particular betraying friend, often identified as Ahithophel; the Christological application is later typology. (b) **Christological-typological reading (most patristic and medieval Christian interpretation):** Jesus' own use of the verse legitimates the typological reading; David's betrayal-experience prefigures Christ's. (c) **Mainstream evangelical-canonical reading (Carson, Beale, Bauckham):** the psalm has both an original-Davidic meaning and a Christological-typological extension authorized by Christ himself; both readings are scripture. (d) **Pattern-of-the-Innocent-Sufferer reading (Brueggemann, modern theological readers):** the psalm articulates the recurring pattern of the righteous one betrayed by intimate; David fits the pattern, and Jesus fulfills it; the pattern is what is typological, not the verse.

The strongest steelman is the both-readings position: the original Davidic meaning is the foundation, and Christ's own application is the authorized extension. The two are not in conflict. David's experience of intimate betrayal is real, and articulating it in scripture has its own theological purpose — articulating the pattern of innocent-sufferer-betrayed. When Jesus picks up that verse at the Last Supper and applies it to Judas, he is not erasing David's voice; he is fulfilling the pattern David's voice anticipated. John 13:18's *plērōthē* ("might be fulfilled") is the technical vocabulary of canonical-typological fulfillment, not of mere illustration.

**Tension 3: The imprecation embedded in petition (v. 10).** The prayer "raise me up, *that I may repay them*" combines healing-petition with vengeance-vow. The honest-with-imprecation tradition (Eric Zenger's *A God of Vengeance?*, Brueggemann's *Praying the Psalms*) refuses to soften the violence. The Christological reading (Augustine, Aquinas) sometimes spiritualizes; modern readers face the question directly. The most honest reading: the worshipper, sick and betrayed, prays for vindication and repayment, and the canon does not censor the prayer. What the canon does is *bring* the prayer to YHWH — refusing both the suppression of vengeance-feeling and the autonomous execution of vengeance. The prayer is the alternative to action.

Where this leaves us: Psalm 41 is the close of Book I. The forty-one psalms have been the David-collection, ranging from Torah-beatitude (Ps 1) through royal coronations (Ps 2), individual laments (3-7, 12-17, 22), thanksgivings (18, 30, 32, 34), wisdom (19, 37), penitentials (6, 32, 38), confidence (23, 27), and confession (51, though that's Book II). They close here with a sufferer betrayed, healed, and the editorial *amen ve-amen*. The closing is not triumph but covenant — *barukh Yahweh Elohei Yisrael me-ha-olam ve-ad ha-olam*. The God who has heard David through forty-one psalms is the God blessed forever. That is the seal of Book I, and the door to what follows.

**Closing question:** When Jesus quotes Ps 41:9 at the Last Supper and applies it to Judas, does he do so because the verse was always *about* him in some prior sense, or because he chooses in that moment to *take up* the David-pattern as his own — and what difference does the answer make to how we read the Psalter as scripture for the church?

## The Thread

- **Psalm 1:** the *ashrei*-opening that bookends Book I — Torah-meditation begins the journey, sickbed-betrayal ends it, both blessed in YHWH.
- **2 Samuel 15-17:** Ahithophel's betrayal of David in Absalom's revolt — the historical setting Rashi and the rabbinic tradition read into v. 9.
- **John 13:18:** Jesus' direct quotation of Ps 41:9 at the Last Supper, applying it to Judas — the canonical Christological seal of the verse.
- **Psalm 72:18-20 / 89:52 / 106:48 / 146-150:** the doxological closes of the other Psalter-books, with which 41:13 forms the structural pattern of the whole.
