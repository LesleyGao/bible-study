# Isaiah 38 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Isaiah 38 narrates Hezekiah's terminal illness, his weeping prayer, the fifteen-year extension granted by Yahweh, the sundial sign confirming the promise, and a poetic psalm of thanksgiving placed in Hezekiah's own mouth. The chapter parallels **2 Kings 20:1-11** with one striking divergence: Hezekiah's psalm (38:9-20) appears **only in Isaiah** and is absent from the Kings parallel. A briefer parallel appears in **2 Chronicles 32:24-26**, which compresses the episode and uniquely flags Hezekiah's pride afterward — a detail Isaiah and Kings omit. Three textual witnesses, three different shapes for the same event.

The mainstream-critical position (Wildberger, Blenkinsopp, Goldingay) treats Isaiah 38 as drawn from the Deuteronomistic narrative in 2 Kings, with the **psalm of Hezekiah** added as the distinctively Isaianic contribution — possibly an originally independent psalm of thanksgiving incorporated into the prophetic frame. Christopher Seitz's reading reverses the borrowing direction. Brevard Childs's canonical reading reads the chapter as paired with Isaiah 39: the king delivered personally (ch 38) and the king who fails to interpret his deliverance rightly (ch 39), forming the double conclusion to First Isaiah.

The chronology of chapter 38 is debated. The phrase "*in those days*" (38:1) is loose. The promise that Yahweh will deliver Jerusalem from Assyria (38:6) suggests events placed *before* the deliverance of chapters 36-37 — i.e., during the buildup to Sennacherib's 701 BCE campaign or earlier — though the chapter's narrative position after the deliverance creates an apparent inversion. The standard reconstruction (K. A. Kitchen, *On the Reliability of the Old Testament*; Edwin Thiele, *The Mysterious Numbers of the Hebrew Kings*) is that Hezekiah's illness occurred c. 712-711 BCE, with the fifteen-year extension carrying him to his death c. 697-687 BCE (depending on co-regency reconstructions), so the historical sequence is illness-then-deliverance, but the literary sequence is deliverance-then-illness for canonical-theological reasons.

Several elements connect to extra-biblical material:

The **figs as poultice** (38:21) reflects standard ancient Near Eastern medicine. Akkadian medical texts and Egyptian medical papyri document fig poultices for skin afflictions, abscesses, and boils. The Hebrew *shechin* (שְׁחִין), "boil" (38:21), denotes the same category of inflamed lesion that Job suffered (Job 2:7) and that struck Egypt as the sixth plague (Exod 9:9). Some scholars have speculated about anthrax or bubonic-plague-related buboes given the proximate timing to the Assyrian camp's mass mortality in chapter 37, though this is conjectural.

The **dial of Ahaz** (38:8) — Hebrew **ma'alot Achaz** (מַעֲלוֹת אָחָז) — is one of the most debated objects in the Hebrew Bible. The word *ma'alot* (מַעֲלוֹת) means "steps" or "ascents" — the same word used for the "Songs of Ascents" (Psalms 120-134) — and only by extension "degrees" of a sundial. Yigael Yadin and others have proposed it was a stepped solar device — possibly a flight of steps with a marker that cast a moving shadow as the day progressed. A device of this kind might be similar to the *clepsydra*-and-shadow installations known from later Egyptian and Babylonian astronomy. The translation of *ma'alot* as "degrees" reflects the influence of the Septuagint (*anabathmoi*, "steps") through later astronomical terminology.

Hezekiah's name **Chizkiyahu** (חִזְקִיָּהוּ), "Yahweh strengthens," takes on a thematic register in this chapter where the king's bodily strength fails. The verb *chazaq* (חזק), "to be strong, to grow firm," recurs in the chapter's prayer language. The repeated phrase "*the LORD, the God of David your father*" (38:5) anchors the deliverance in covenantal-dynastic terms — a connection that becomes central in the closing oracle (38:20): "*we will play my music on stringed instruments... at the house of the LORD.*"

The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran (1QIsaa, c. 125 BCE) preserves Isaiah 38 substantially intact, including the psalm. The Septuagint psalm shows some textual variation — particularly in the difficult lines 12-14 — but the substantive content tracks the Masoretic Text. The very difficulty of the Hebrew in 38:12-16 has long been read as evidence of the psalm's antiquity and authentic poetic register rather than secondary smoothing.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Isaiah 38 has a clean three-part structure broken by an unusual postscript. Part one (38:1-8) is a tight narrative of illness, prayer, oracle, and sign. Part two (38:9-20) is the psalm of Hezekiah — a thanksgiving lament unique to the Isaianic version. Part three (38:21-22) is a brief medical and confirmatory postscript, displaced from its narratively earlier position (compare 2 Kings 20:7-9, where the figs and the sign-question come in their natural sequence). The displacement may reflect a redactional preference for ending the chapter with the psalm's praise, or simply a textual rearrangement.

The **narrative frame** (38:1-8) is built on a single pivot: a divine word reversed by prayer. Isaiah arrives with the most absolute possible oracle — "*you shall die, you shall not recover*" (38:1). Hezekiah's response is striking: he turns his face to the wall, prays without intermediary, and weeps. He does not protest the justice of the verdict; he appeals to his prior faithfulness ("*remember how I have walked before you*"). Yahweh hears, sees the tears, and speaks again to Isaiah — a remarkable narrative sequence in which a prophetic oracle is *revised* through the prayer of the one against whom it was pronounced. The grant of fifteen years is precise: not unlimited, not vague, a measured extension. The deliverance from Assyria (38:6) is added to the personal grant — the body and the city are saved together.

The **sundial sign** (38:7-8) is the rarest kind of biblical sign — an astronomical/optical reversal. The shadow on the *ma'alot Achaz* moves backward ten steps. The verse is told twice (the announcement and the report), and the report is laconic: "*so the sun turned back on the dial the ten steps.*" Mainstream-critical scholarship treats the sign as theological-poetic — ancient narrative employs cosmic-scale imagery for covenantal signs (cf. Joshua's long day, Josh 10) without expecting natural-philosophical mechanism. Apologetic literature has occasionally proposed mechanism — atmospheric refraction, eclipse phenomena — but these proposals are generally rejected by both scientific and biblical-scholarly review. The sign's purpose is theological: a creation-scale token confirming a covenantal-personal promise. The contrast with chapter 7 is sharp: where Ahaz refused to ask for a sign and was given one anyway, Hezekiah does ask and receives one matched to the king who built the dial — Ahaz, his own faithless father.

**Hezekiah's psalm** (38:9-20) — the *miktav* (מִכְתָּב) of Hezekiah, his "writing" — is the chapter's distinctive literary contribution. It belongs to the genre of **thanksgiving psalm** (*todah*) following recovery from mortal illness, with structural and thematic parallels to Psalms 6, 30, 88, 116. The psalm divides into a lament-from-the-edge (38:10-14), a turning-point of address (38:15-17), a Sheol-theology stanza (38:18-19), and a closing vow of praise (38:20).

Several lines deserve particular attention. "*In the middle of my days I must depart; I am consigned to the gates of Sheol*" (38:10) — Hebrew *sha'arei she'ol* (שַׁעֲרֵי שְׁאוֹל). The image of Sheol as a city with gates appears in Job 38:17, Psalm 9:13, and the much later Pseudepigrapha. "*I shall not see the LORD... in the land of the living*" (38:11) is a cosmological assumption, not a complaint about death simpliciter — Sheol is a place where the dead exist in shadow but are cut off from active relation to Yahweh. The weaver image — "*like a weaver I have rolled up my life; he cuts me off from the loom*" (38:12) — is among the most concrete in biblical poetry, drawing on the everyday image of a finished tapestry being severed from the loom-warp. The lion image (38:13) is brutal: "*like a lion he breaks all my bones.*" The Hebrew here is difficult, with several emendations proposed; the violence of the image is undisputed.

The **theological turn** comes in 38:17: "*it was for my welfare that I had great bitterness; but in love you have delivered my life from the pit of destruction, for you have cast all my sins behind your back.*" The illness is reread retroactively as covenantal pedagogy — *for my welfare*, literally "for peace" (*l'shalom*, לְשָׁלוֹם). The casting of sins "behind your back" is a vivid Hebrew idiom: out of sight, out of accounting.

The Sheol-stanza (38:18-19) is the chapter's most theologically pressing line for later readers: "*Sheol does not thank you; death does not praise you; those who go down to the pit do not hope for your faithfulness. The living, the living, he thanks you.*" This is **pre-resurrection Hebrew anthropology** in its clearest form. There is no post-mortem participation in the praise of Yahweh. The grave is silent. Hezekiah's argument for life is precisely that he is more useful to Yahweh alive than dead — an argument that requires the absence of resurrection theology for its force.

The closing vow (38:20) returns to temple-worship vocabulary: songs played on stringed instruments, "*all the days of our lives, at the house of the LORD.*" The personal deliverance becomes communal liturgy.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Within the **prophetic canon**, Isaiah 38 connects to the small genre of prophet-king encounters around illness and recovery: Elijah and the Shunammite's son (1 Kings 17), Elisha and Naaman (2 Kings 5), Elisha and the Shunammite's son (2 Kings 4). The pattern is consistent: illness, prophet, prayer, recovery, witness. Isaiah 38 distinguishes itself by placing the king himself in the patient role and by including the king's own poetic response.

The **Jewish trajectory** has read Isaiah 38 as pivotal for two doctrines. First, **the efficacy of repentant prayer** (b. Berakhot 10a-b, b. Rosh Hashanah 16b): even a divine decree of death can be reversed when the king prays in tears. The Talmudic discussion of whether prayer can change a decree centers on this passage. Hezekiah's prayer becomes the paradigm of *teshuvah* power. Second, **the development of resurrection doctrine**: the very Sheol-theology that Hezekiah's psalm articulates — the silence of the dead before God — is precisely what later Jewish thought (Daniel 12, the Pharisees, the Mishnah's doctrines, and rabbinic Judaism) had to overcome. Jon D. Levenson's *Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel* (2006) treats this passage as one of the key texts showing the *trajectory* from pre-resurrection Hebrew anthropology to Second Temple resurrection-hope. The change is not denial of Hezekiah's lament but its retrospective qualification: what Hezekiah could not see, later Israel did.

Medieval Jewish commentators — Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Radak — work through the chronology, the dial of Ahaz, and the psalm's difficult lines. Maimonides cites Hezekiah's prayer in the *Mishneh Torah* under the laws of repentance.

The **Christian trajectory** has read the chapter both pastorally and christologically. The patristic tradition (Tertullian, Augustine) read Hezekiah's recovery as a type of resurrection — the *figura* of Christ raised on the third day. Calvin's *Commentary* treats the psalm as a model of evangelical lament: honest about death, honest about sin, honest about Yahweh's restoring action. The Reformers used the chapter against medieval purgatory-theology, citing 38:18 ("*Sheol does not thank you*") as evidence that the dead do not actively pray or praise.

Hezekiah's psalm has had a substantial liturgical afterlife. It appears in the **Septuagint as one of the Odes** appended to the Psalter, used in Eastern Orthodox Matins and various Western breviaries. Lutheran and Anglican traditions have used the psalm in services of healing and thanksgiving for recovery. Charles Wesley wrote a hymn ("Worship and Thanks and Blessing") drawing on the psalm.

**New Testament use** is largely echo rather than direct quotation. The phrase "*the gates of Sheol*" (38:10) lies behind Matthew 16:18's "*the gates of Hades*" — Christ's promise that they will not prevail against the church. Paul's "*for to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain*" (Phil 1:21) is a deliberate inversion of Hezekiah's "more useful alive" theology, made possible by the resurrection of Christ. Hebrews 11 lists Hezekiah-style figures in its faith catalogue (without naming him directly). Revelation's vision of those who praise God "*day and night*" (Rev 7:15) recasts Hezekiah's worry that the dead cannot praise into a vision of post-resurrection liturgy.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — and Tensions

The chapter's theological intent is double: Yahweh hears prayer, even the prayer that asks for revision of his own oracle; and life — embodied, breath-having, temple-worshiping life — is a covenantal good worth weeping for. But the chapter raises tensions modern readers must engage.

**Tension 1: The sundial sign and astronomical realism.** Mainstream-critical scholarship treats 38:8 as theological-poetic rather than astronomical-historical. The text gives no mechanism; the sign is a covenantal-particular act, not a cosmic-physical claim about the sun's motion. Apologetic literature has sometimes attempted naturalistic explanations, generally rejected by both scientific reviewers (the energetic implications of reversing earth's rotation are catastrophic) and by mainstream biblical scholarship. The cleaner reading — held by both confessional canonical readers (Childs) and historical-critical readers (Blenkinsopp) — is that the sign belongs to the same category as Joshua's long day or the darkness at the crucifixion: covenantally-particular, theologically-meaningful, and rendered in the cosmological idiom of its day rather than in modern astronomical terms.

**Steelman the rationalist position**: ancient peoples did not have our concept of a uniform physical cosmos, and biblical texts written within that framework should not be read as making claims our cosmology must adjudicate. The honest theological reading does not require either empirical defense or dismissive demythologization; it requires reading the text on its own terms.

**Tension 2: The Sheol-cosmology of the psalm.** The psalm states clearly that the dead do not praise Yahweh, that those who go down to the pit do not hope for his faithfulness (38:18). This is in tension with later resurrection-hope explicit in Daniel 12:2 and central to both rabbinic Judaism and Christianity. How can a canonical text say something its later canonical context corrects?

Levenson's response (in *Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel*) is that the relation between Hezekiah's anthropology and later resurrection-hope is **trajectorial**, not contradictory: Hebrew thought *develops* from a Sheol-bounded anthropology in the early monarchy to the explicit resurrection hope of late Second Temple literature, and the canonical preservation of both witnesses to the development. The honest reading is to take Hezekiah's psalm as expressing an authentic stage of biblical anthropology, not to harmonize it prematurely with later doctrine.

**Tension 3: The unique-to-Isaiah psalm and what it adds.** The psalm's absence from 2 Kings is striking. Why does the Isaianic version preserve it? One plausible reading (Goldingay, Seitz) is that the psalm functions in the Isaianic frame to give the king his *own voice* — to show that the king who has just been saved can articulate, in poetry, what the deliverance means. The book of Isaiah is dense with poetry; the psalm folds the historical narrative back into the prophetic register. The king becomes, briefly, his own psalmist. The literary effect is to make Hezekiah more than a recipient of deliverance — he becomes a witness and worshiper, a model for the reader who will turn to Second Isaiah's poetry of the suffering servant and the comforted exile.

**Tension 4: The Chronicler's note on Hezekiah's pride.** 2 Chronicles 32:25-26 records that "*Hezekiah did not make return according to the benefit done to him, for his heart was proud.*" Isaiah 38 does not include this critique. The reader who holds all three sources (Isaiah, Kings, Chronicles) sees a complex king: faithful in prayer, granted deliverance, but susceptible to the pride that the Chronicler flags and that Isaiah 39 will narratively expose in the Babylonian-envoys episode. The psalm of chapter 38 should be read with chapter 39 close at hand: the king who sang this song is the same king who, in the next chapter, shows the envoys all his treasure.

**Where this leaves us:** Isaiah 38 is the chapter where the king's body becomes the site of theology. Personal mortality, prayer, an extraordinary sign, a psalm with a theological frame the canon will eventually qualify, and a quiet medical postscript. The chapter's most enduring contribution is not the sign but the psalm — and within the psalm, not its anthropological assumptions but its theological turn: "*it was for my welfare that I had great bitterness*" (38:17). The pedagogy of suffering, the casting of sins behind God's back, the resolve to play music at the house of the LORD — these are the marks the chapter leaves on readers who have themselves wept against the wall.

The closing question worth carrying into chapter 39: **the king who prayed against death and the king who showed the envoys his treasure — is this the same king, and what does that tell us about the deliverance he received?**
