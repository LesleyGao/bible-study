# Ecclesiastes 11: Cast Your Bread Upon the Waters

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Ecclesiastes was composed in Persian-period Yehud, fifth or fourth century BC. Choon-Leong Seow's Anchor Bible commentary (1997) makes the case decisive on linguistic and economic grounds: Late Biblical Hebrew studded with Aramaisms, Persian loanwords (*pardes*, *pithgam*, *medinah*), and an economic register saturated with monetized commerce — coinage, contracts, inheritance, partnership — that fits the post-Achaemenid coinage reforms but ill fits Solomon's tenth-century court. Roland Murphy (Word), Tremper Longman (NICOT), Thomas Krüger (Hermeneia), James Crenshaw (OTL), Norman Whybray, Stuart Weeks, and Robert Gordis converge on Persian-period dating with disagreements measured in decades.

The Solomonic frame is a literary convention. Qohelet (קֹהֶלֶת, "assembler" or "convener") is a function-name; the epilogist who frames the book at 12:9–14 already treats Qohelet as a wisdom teacher distinct from Solomon. By chapter 11 the Solomonic guise has long been dropped. The book's spatial frame is *tahat ha-shemesh* (תַּחַת הַשֶּׁמֶשׁ, "under the sun"), and its keyword is *hevel* (הֶבֶל, "vapor," "the ungraspable"), which returns explicitly at 11:8 and 11:10.

Material culture matters in chapter 11 because the chapter's controlling images are commercial, agricultural, and embryological. The opening verse — *cast your bread upon the waters, for you will find it after many days* (11:1) — has been read for centuries as a generic counsel of charity, but Seow's reading (taken up by Krüger, Longman, and Whybray) is more specific: this is Mediterranean trade. The Persian-period Levantine economy was deeply embedded in maritime commerce — grain shipped from Egypt to the Aegean, oil and wine from Phoenicia, the Phoenician ports of Tyre and Sidon serving as commercial entrepôts. *Bread* (*leḥem*) is here metonymy for grain, the Persian-period staple commodity. *Cast upon the waters* is the actual practice of shipping goods in bottoms one did not personally control, with the expectation of long delays before profit returned (or did not). *Give a portion to seven, or even to eight* (11:2) is the diversification proverb of a merchant: split the cargo across multiple ventures, because *you know not what disaster may happen on earth*. This is realistic Persian-period maritime advice, not generic generosity.

The agricultural images of 11:3–6 are equally specific. *He who observes the wind will not sow, and he who regards the clouds will not reap* (11:4) is the language of Levantine dry-farming, where the rains begin in late October and the timing of the sowing window is critical. The over-cautious farmer who waits for ideal conditions misses the planting season entirely. *In the morning sow your seed, and at evening withhold not your hand* (11:6) is the same agricultural realism extended into a counsel for diversified labor. The embryological image of 11:5 — *as you do not know the way the spirit comes to the bones in the womb of a woman with child* — fits the broader ancient Near Eastern tradition of the womb as the locus of the mystery of life, with parallels in Job 10:8–11 and Psalm 139:13–16.

Textual witnesses include 4QQoh-a and 4QQoh-b at Qumran (the second-century BC fragments of the book), the LXX (an Aquila-school literal Greek translation), and the Targum Qohelet (the expansive orthodoxizing Aramaic paraphrase). The Hebrew of chapter 11 is reasonably stable; the chief textual difficulty is 11:5, where the MT *atzamim ba-beten ha-melaʾah* ("bones in the full womb") is rendered variously by the ancient versions, with some manuscripts and the LXX reading "spirit and bones" (the spirit and the bones being two distinct mysteries, both of which Qohelet names as inaccessible).

Hebrew terminology in this chapter intersects with the rest of the book. *Hevel* (הֶבֶל) returns explicitly at 11:8 and 11:10. *Yom ha-ḥoshekh* (יוֹם הַחֹשֶׁךְ, "the days of darkness") at 11:8 is a distinctive Qohelet phrase that prepares the imagery of chapter 12. *Mishpat* (מִשְׁפָּט, "judgment") at 11:9 is one of the chapter's most contested words — its presence in Qohelet is rare (cf. 3:17, 8:6, 12:14) and its theological weight is one of the chapter's open questions. *Yaldut* and *shaḥarut* (יַלְדוּת, שַׁחֲרוּת, "youth" and "the dawn of life") at 11:9–10 form a poetic doublet that becomes the bridge into chapter 12's old-age allegory.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels include the Egyptian *Instruction of Onkhsheshonqy* and the Aramaic Ahiqar, which share the diversification-and-prudence vocabulary of 11:1–2. The embryological image of 11:5 has a partial parallel in the Egyptian *Hymn to Aten* and in Mesopotamian birth incantations. The carpe-diem of 11:7–10 is the direct counterpart to chapter 9:7–10 and the broader ANE tradition of the *Old Babylonian Gilgamesh* Tablet X (Siduri's speech), the *Dialogue of Pessimism*, and the Egyptian *Harper's Songs*.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 11 is structured in two clear movements: the agency-under-uncertainty cluster (11:1–6) and the youth-and-judgment carpe-diem (11:7–10). The chapter is a hinge between the proverbial cluster of chapter 10 and the climactic old-age allegory of chapter 12.

The literary devices are tightly controlled. The first movement (11:1–6) builds through six imperatives — cast, give, observe, regard, sow, withhold-not — that progressively reorient the reader from passive observation (the over-cautious farmer who watches the wind) to disciplined diversified action (sow morning and evening). The structural argument is that *because* outcomes are unpredictable (you do not know what disaster will come, you do not know the way the spirit comes to the bones, you do not know which will prosper), action must be diversified rather than withheld.

The second movement (11:7–10) is the seventh and penultimate carpe-diem refrain in the book (after 2:24–26, 3:12–13, 3:22, 5:18–20, 8:15, 9:7–10). The address shifts from generic second person to the *young man* (*baḥur*, בָּחוּר), which prepares the contrast with the failing body of chapter 12. The structure is concentric: rejoice (11:7–8), remember (11:8b), rejoice (11:9a), know (11:9b), remove (11:10). The rejoice-and-judgment pair at 11:9 is the chapter's theological pressure point.

Hebrew literary devices include parallelism (densely throughout 11:1–6), the listing structure (seven, even eight; this or that or both), and what Robert Alter calls "the gnomic surface": short, hard, polished sayings followed by the freer prose of 11:5–6. The poem of 11:7–10 has the cadence of address-poem rather than proverb-cluster — Qohelet shifts here into a more direct vocative tone that prepares the climactic poem of 12:1–8.

The chapter's most contested literary question is verse 11:9b: *but know that for all these things God will bring you into judgment* (*da kiy ʿal kol-elleh yeviʾakha ha-Elohim ba-mishpat*). Crenshaw treats this clause as an editorial gloss imposed on Qohelet's carpe-diem to harmonize it with conventional retributive theology. Gordis, Murphy, Provan, and Seow defend it as integral to Qohelet's argument: Qohelet has acknowledged divine judgment elsewhere (3:17, 8:6, 12:14), and 11:9 is his honest admission that the carpe-diem is not absolute — it is the proper response to *hevel*, but it is held under the awareness that one is responsible to God. Fox treats the clause as integral but reads it more ironically: the judgment named here is the chastening fact of mortality itself, not a forensic eschatological judgment. The Targum Qohelet, predictably, expands 11:9 into a meditation on the world-to-come and the day of final reckoning.

Textual variants in chapter 11 are minor. 11:5 has the *spirit and bones* / *spirit … bones* divergence noted above. 11:9's *ba-mishpat* is read by some ancient witnesses as *ba-mishpaṭim* (the plural, "into judgments"), without significant change in meaning.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Ecclesiastes 11 stands at the wisdom-literature trajectory's most subtle moment. Chapter 9 has stated the mortalist horizon. Chapter 10 has cited the conventional wisdom and quietly tested it. Chapter 11 turns to the question: given *hevel* and *miqreh* (chance) and the unknowability of the future, *how do I act?* The answer is the chapter's distinctive contribution: not paralysis, not pure carpe-diem, but disciplined diversified action under acknowledged uncertainty.

**Jewish trajectory.** Sirach 14:11–19 is in conversation with Qohelet 11's carpe-diem and reframes it under explicit Torah-piety. The Wisdom of Solomon 2:1–9 quotes a Qohelet-like carpe-diem speech in the mouth of the ungodly precisely to refute it, then asserts the immortality of the soul. The Mishnah Avot 3:1, "Reflect on three things and you will not come into the power of sin: know whence you came, whither you are going, and before whom you must give an account," reframes Qohelet 11:9's judgment under explicit rabbinic ethical theology.

The rabbinic tradition reads chapter 11 with serious attention to its diversification ethics. Bava Metzia 42a applies *give a portion to seven, or even to eight* (11:2) to the diversification of investments — "a man should always divide his money into three: a third in land, a third in goods, and a third at hand" — making 11:2 the textual basis for ancient Jewish portfolio theory. Rashi reads 11:1's *cast your bread upon the waters* as charity (the dominant medieval reading); Ibn Ezra reads it as commerce (the philological reading that Seow recovers). Ralbag (Gersonides) reads philosophically. Sforno reads ethically. Maimonides cites Qohelet's epistemic humility (11:5) in the *Guide* (3.13–14, 3.51) as a meditation on the limits of human understanding of divine providence. Robert Gordis (*Koheleth: The Man and His World*, 1951) reads chapter 11 as Qohelet's mature counsel of disciplined action under uncertainty, with the judgment of 11:9 as Qohelet's hesitant acknowledgment of moral order. Michael V. Fox reads similarly, with more emphasis on the carpe-diem as the chapter's primary counsel.

Targum Qohelet expands 11:1 into a meditation on charity and the world-to-come; 11:5 into a meditation on God's providence over the unborn; 11:9 into the explicit doctrine of the day of judgment. These expansions are characteristic of the Targum's project of harmonizing Qohelet to rabbinic orthodoxy without altering the Hebrew text.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic reception read chapter 11 morally and allegorically. Gregory of Nyssa's *Homilies on Ecclesiastes* (c. 380) reads 11:1 (cast your bread upon the waters) as alms to the poor, allegorized as bread cast on the water of human variability — the standard Christian charity reading that dominated for fifteen centuries. Jerome's *Commentary on Ecclesiastes* (388) reads 11:1 similarly. Didymus the Blind's commentary, recovered at Tura in 1941, supplies an Alexandrian reading. Augustine cites 11:1 in his discussions of almsgiving. The medieval tradition (Hugh of St. Victor, Bonaventure, Aquinas, the Glossa Ordinaria) consolidated the charity reading, and 11:1 became one of the most-preached verses in Christian almsgiving theology.

The Reformation modified but did not abandon the charity reading. Luther's *Notes on Ecclesiastes* (1526/1532) reads chapter 11 vocationally — the diversified action of 11:6 is the Christian's confidence in vocation under God's providence. Luther read 11:9's judgment soberly: the young man is told to rejoice *and* to remember accountability. Calvin published no commentary on Ecclesiastes; the Reformed tradition's reading of 11:1 emerges through Beza, the Geneva Bible's marginal notes, and later Puritan exposition (Matthew Henry reads it as charity *and* commerce).

Critical scholarship from Delitzsch (1875) through Crenshaw, Murphy, Seow, Fox, Whybray, and Krüger has progressively recovered the commercial reading of 11:1–2 against the patristic charity reading. Seow's 1997 commentary is the locus classicus of this recovery. Tremper Longman's NICOT commentary reads chapter 11 as part of Qohelet's heterodox skeptic's discourse that the epilogist contains. Iain Provan (NIVAC) reads it as sincere wisdom counsel. Brevard Childs's canonical reading takes the chapter as integral to the canonical shape. Walter Brueggemann reads chapter 11 as counter-testimonial wisdom that names the disciplined risk-taking that life under YHWH's freedom requires. Ellen Davis reads chapter 11 as practical ethics for a community living under conditions of uncertainty.

**Where they diverge.** The deepest divides are over 11:1's referent (charity, commerce, or both) and 11:9's judgment (editorial gloss, sincere acknowledgment, or ironic naming of mortality). The reading you adopt of 11:9 — and you must adopt one — shapes how you hear the carpe-diem of 11:7–10.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Agency under uncertainty.** The chapter's controlling counsel — *cast your bread upon the waters … give a portion to seven, or even to eight … in the morning sow your seed, and at evening withhold not your hand* — is delivered together with the explicit epistemological agnosticism of 11:5: *as you do not know the way the spirit comes to the bones in the womb of a woman with child, so you do not know the work of God who makes everything*. How does one act when one does not know the outcome?

The Calvinist providential reading: sow because God secretly governs the harvest. Calvin's *Institutes* I.16–18 and the broader Reformed tradition read providence as the doctrine that gives human action its meaning under conditions of human ignorance. The farmer sows because God's secret government will determine which seed prospers; the merchant casts bread on the waters because God governs the seas. Action is meaningful because it is taken under providence, not because the actor knows the outcome. Read this way, Qohelet 11:1–6 is a doctrine of providence-from-below: the wise actor diversifies *because* he does not see what God sees, and the diversification is itself a confession of God's hidden government. This is the dominant Protestant Reformed reading. Steelmanned: it preserves both the epistemic humility (you do not know) and the theological confidence (God does), and it makes diversified action the proper expression of trust under providence.

The Pelagian-Wesleyan reading: sow because the moral order rewards diligence. The Arminian-Wesleyan tradition reads chapter 11 as natural ethics — the world rewards disciplined action and punishes sloth, and the wise observer notices this and acts accordingly. The judgment of 11:9 is then natural-moral judgment: God's just government is built into the structure of the world, and the young man's accountability is the moral law inscribed in creation. Read this way, Qohelet 11 is wisdom literature in its classic mode: the wise act diligently because the world's moral structure makes diligent action good. Steelmanned: this reading takes Qohelet's empiricism seriously — he is reporting what the wise observer sees — and connects it to a longer Christian moral tradition that takes natural law and human responsibility as constitutive of the moral life.

The Stoic-Epicurean parallel reading: sow because action is meaningful in itself. The classical pagan philosophical traditions provide a third way of reading 11:1–6 that resists both the Calvinist providence and the Wesleyan natural-law reading. The Stoic counsel — act in accordance with virtue regardless of outcome — and the Epicurean counsel — act for the modest goods of life under conditions of mortality — both have echoes in Qohelet. The chapter's emphasis on action under uncertainty has more in common with Stoic *kathekonta* (proper functions) and Epicurean *ataraxia* (tranquility) than with covenantal theology. Steelmanned: this reading takes seriously the international wisdom-literature character of Ecclesiastes and reads Qohelet as a thinker whose conclusions are partially independent of YHWH-frame theology.

The steelman of the position least native to the Reformed Christian reader is the third: the Stoic-Epicurean parallel. The chapter's vocabulary, structure, and counsel can be read substantially without the providence-doctrine. The wise actor diversifies because diversification is wise *under conditions of mortality*, not because providence will reward it. The carpe-diem follows because joy is the proper response to *hevel*, not because pleasure is divinely sanctioned. This reading does not require Qohelet to be a pagan; it requires only that Qohelet's wisdom is being practiced at the boundary where international wisdom converges with covenantal wisdom and where the convergence does not collapse into either. Brueggemann reads close to this; Fox reads close to this; Krüger reads close to this. Seow reads more conservatively but acknowledges the international parallel.

Where this leaves us: chapter 11 is the wisdom literature's most disciplined statement of agency under uncertainty. Whether you read it under Calvinist providence, Wesleyan natural law, or Stoic-Epicurean ethics, the practical counsel is the same: diversify, sow morning and evening, do not wait for the perfect conditions, do not refuse to act because you cannot see the outcome. The unresolved question is what *grounds* the counsel — whether the action is meaningful *because* of providence, *because* of the moral order, or *because* the alternative (paralysis) is unlivable under conditions of mortality.

**Tension 2: Youth and judgment.** Verse 11:9 is the chapter's theological pressure point: *rejoice, O young man, in your youth … walk in the ways of your heart and the sight of your eyes. But know that for all these things God will bring you into judgment.*

Crenshaw's ironic reading: the judgment clause is an editorial gloss imposed on Qohelet's carpe-diem to harmonize it with conventional retributive theology. The original carpe-diem was unqualified, and the editor — possibly the epilogist, possibly an earlier hand — added the judgment-warning to render the verse acceptable for canonical reception. Steelmanned: this reading explains the verse's syntactic awkwardness (the *but know that* clause sits oddly against the imperatives) and its theological tension with the surrounding carpe-diem.

Gordis-Murphy-Provan sincere reading: 11:9b is integral to Qohelet's argument. Qohelet has acknowledged divine judgment elsewhere (3:17, 8:6, and the epilogist closes the book at 12:14 with the same theme). The carpe-diem is not absolute — it is the proper response to *hevel*, but it is held under the awareness that the young man is responsible to God. Steelmanned: this reading preserves Qohelet's seriousness as a wisdom teacher and makes the carpe-diem a *disciplined* rather than a *libertine* counsel. The young man is told to rejoice *and* to remember accountability — and the rejoicing is genuine because the accountability is genuine.

Fox's middle reading: 11:9b is integral but ironic — the judgment named here is the chastening fact of mortality itself, not a forensic eschatological judgment. The young man's joy will be tempered by the simple fact of aging, sickness, and death (the chapter 12 allegory). Steelmanned: this reading takes the immediate context (the *days of darkness* of 11:8, the youth-as-vapor of 11:10, the old-age allegory of 12:1–8) as the controlling frame for what 11:9 means by judgment. The judgment is the body's failing, the mind's dimming, the dust returning to the earth — that is what the young man must remember.

The Christian reception has historically read 11:9 in the Gordis-Murphy direction: the carpe-diem is real, and the judgment is real, and the young man must hold both. Luther's *Notes on Ecclesiastes* read 11:9 as the doctrine of Christian liberty under accountability — Christians are free to enjoy the goods of life *and* are accountable to God for how they enjoy them. Calvin's rare references to Qohelet read similarly. The Catholic moral tradition (Aquinas, the Catechism) reads 11:9 as the natural-law ground for the moral theology of pleasure: pleasure is good when ordered to its proper end. The Orthodox tradition reads ascetically: the young man's joy is real but must be ordered toward the contemplative life.

The Reformed pleasure-ethic debate (especially in the seventeenth-century Puritan tradition and revived in twentieth-century discussions of Christian liberty) takes 11:9 as a key text. The question is whether Christian freedom means freedom *to* enjoy or freedom *from* the absolutizing of enjoyment. The Lutheran tradition has tended toward the former (Luther's robust theology of vocation, food, marriage, and beer). The Reformed tradition has tended toward the latter (the Westminster Larger Catechism's framing of pleasure under sanctification).

Where this leaves us: 11:9 sits at the center of the chapter's theological work. Whether it is editorial (Crenshaw), sincere (Gordis-Murphy-Provan), or ironic (Fox), the verse functions to hold the carpe-diem and the judgment together without collapsing either. The unresolved question — and it is a live one for contemporary Christian moral theology — is how a tradition that has often domesticated pleasure or feared it can recover a counsel of disciplined joy that is genuinely joyful and genuinely accountable.

## The Thread

Job 38:25–27 (*who has cleft a channel for the torrents of rain … to satisfy the waste and desolate land?*) shares chapter 11's epistemic humility about the unknowability of God's work in the natural world; the rain that falls and the seed that sprouts both lie outside the wise observer's predictive grasp. Proverbs 21:31 (*the horse is made ready for the day of battle, but the victory belongs to the LORD*) is the orthodox version of Qohelet 11:1–6 that Qohelet rewrites in the secular register of *miqreh*. Sirach 11:18–19 (*one becomes rich through diligence … and yet does not know what his lot will be*) is in direct conversation with Qohelet 11 and reframes the diversification ethic under explicit Torah-piety. James 4:13–17 (*you do not know what tomorrow will bring … you ought to say, if the Lord wills, we shall live and do this or that*) is the New Testament's direct reception of Qohelet 11:1–6: the same epistemic humility about the future, the same counsel of disciplined action, now framed under the kerygma of the Lord's will and the *atmis* (vapor) that picks up *hevel* at James 4:14.
