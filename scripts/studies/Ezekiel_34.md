# Ezekiel 34: The Shepherds Who Feed Themselves and the One Shepherd David

## Lens 1 — Historical World

Chapter 34 is the great pastoral oracle of the Hebrew Bible — *the most consequential pastoral chapter in the Old Testament*, in Block's phrasing (NICOT, 1998). Its placement in the *restoration block* (chs. 33–48) is deliberate. After the renewed watchman commission and the news of Jerusalem's fall in chapter 33, the prophet turns to indict the leadership whose failure produced the catastrophe and to announce the divine takeover that will reverse it. No date formula appears in the chapter, but its setting is post-fall — late 587 or 586 BC by the standard chronology of Albright and Wiseman — and its audience is the Tel-abib exilic community whose former kings, princes, priests, and prophets are now collectively indicted under the *ro'eh* metaphor. The four standard scholarly anchors — Greenberg (Anchor 22B, 1997), Block (NICOT, 1998), Zimmerli (Hermeneia, 1983), and Daniel Block's later monograph *Beyond the River Chebar* (2014) — converge on chapter 34 as the chapter in which Ezekiel's restoration theology first becomes explicitly *christological* in retrospect, although the chapter's plain sense is set fully within Israelite covenantal categories.

The shepherd metaphor is ancient Near Eastern royal-political idiom. The Code of Hammurabi (prologue, ca. 1750 BC) names the king as *re'um*, the shepherd who pastures the people; Hammurabi calls himself *re'um nibitum*, the called-one. The Egyptian *Instructions for King Merikare*, the Mari letters, and the Neo-Assyrian royal annals of Tiglath-Pileser III, Sargon II, and Ashurbanipal use *re'û kīttim* ("the just shepherd") as a standard royal honorific. The metaphor was not Israelite-distinctive; it was the standard ANE political vocabulary. Greenberg's Anchor commentary catalogs the material in detail.

Within the Hebrew Bible the metaphor has a developed earlier history. Genesis 48:15 names YHWH as Jacob's shepherd ("the God who has been my shepherd all my life long"); Psalm 23, attributed to David, is the most famous OT shepherd-text and presumes a royal-Davidide author writing of YHWH; Psalm 80:1 calls YHWH *the shepherd of Israel*; Isaiah 40:11 (the proto-Isaiah, post-exilic prophet — Second Isaiah by most reconstructions) presents YHWH as the shepherd who *gathers the lambs in his arms*; Jeremiah 23:1–6 indicts the false shepherds of Judah with vocabulary remarkably close to Ezek 34. Jeremiah and Ezekiel are likely in literary dialogue here — Greenberg argues that Ezek 34 expands and intensifies Jer 23, while Block argues that the two prophets independently apply a shared traditional rhetoric to overlapping historical material. Either way, the *Davidic shepherd* of 34:23–24 — *I will set up over them one shepherd, my servant David* — knits the chapter to Jer 23:5–6's *righteous Branch from David*, to Isa 11:1's *shoot from the stump of Jesse*, and to the broader prophetic Davidic-messianic expectation.

The Hebrew vocabulary is dense. *Ro'eh* (רֹעֶה, "shepherd") at 34:2, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 23 — the operative noun. The verb *ra'ah* ("to shepherd, to feed, to pasture") shifts in the chapter from indictment to promise: in vv. 2–10 the false shepherds *fail* to *ra'ah*; in vv. 11–16 YHWH *himself* *ra'ah*; in v. 23 the Davidide will *ra'ah*. The morphology *ʿēder* ("flock") at v. 12, 17, 31 grounds the metaphor materially. *Berît šālôm* (בְּרִית שָׁלוֹם, "covenant of peace") at 34:25 is one of the Hebrew Bible's three "covenant of peace" formulae, paralleling Numbers 25:12 (the priestly covenant with Phinehas) and Isaiah 54:10 (the everlasting covenant after exile). The *recognition formula* — *and they shall know that I am the LORD* — appears at 34:27, 30. The chapter ends (v. 31) with what Block identifies as a corrective gloss that secures the metaphor's tenor: *And you are my sheep, human sheep of my pasture* — the Hebrew *ʾādām attem* makes explicit that the *flock* is the *people*, in case the metaphor risked taking on independent life.

The historical-political content of the indictment is sharp. *You eat the fat, you clothe yourselves with the wool, you slaughter the fat ones, but you do not feed the sheep* (34:3). The vocabulary is derived from royal court taxation: *ḥēleb* (fat), *ṣemer* (wool), and *bĕrî'â* (fattened beasts) are royal revenues paid in kind, the standard pre-monetary fiscal flow of the Iron Age Levant. The Lachish ostraca and Arad letters confirm the same fiscal pattern under Zedekiah. The indictment is not allegorical-only; it is the prophet's specific charge that the Davidic monarchy's provisioning extracted rather than provided. The deportation is the result of the leadership's systemic failure of pastoral economy.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter is structured in five panels that move from indictment to promise to settlement. Verses 1–10 indict the false shepherds. Verses 11–16 announce YHWH's direct shepherding takeover. Verses 17–22 differentiate within the flock, judging between the *fat sheep and the lean sheep* (v. 20) and between *rams and male goats* (v. 17). Verses 23–24 announce the Davidide. Verses 25–31 promise the covenant of peace, the security from beasts, the showers of blessing, and the recognition formula's seal. The form is *Disputationsrede* in Greenberg's scheme — a layered disputation in which the prophetic voice cycles indictment, announcement, and promise — but the chapter's distinctive literary signature is its *allegory-with-tenor-disclosure* form: the metaphor is sustained throughout, and at v. 31 the tenor is named explicitly to prevent independent allegorical drift.

The *leitwort* of the chapter is *ra'ah*. Twelve occurrences in vv. 2–23. As in chapter 33 with *šûb*, the repetition is the argument. The shepherds *ra'ah* themselves; YHWH will *ra'ah* his sheep; the Davidide will *ra'ah* the flock. The verb's repeated subjects map the chapter's theological movement: from the false shepherds (subject of failed *ra'ah*) to YHWH (subject of *ra'ah* in vv. 11–16) to the Davidide (subject of *ra'ah* in v. 23). The chapter's structure is the verb's biography across three subjects.

Verses 11–16 are one of the OT's most arresting passages of divine first-person agency. The Hebrew is dense with the emphatic pronoun *ʾănî* — *I, I myself* — in v. 11 (*hinnî ʾănî*, "Behold, I myself"), v. 15 (*ʾănî ʾerʿeh*, "I myself will shepherd"), and v. 20 (*ʾănî ʾānî*, "I, I myself"). Block notes that the doubled emphatic *ʾănî* in v. 11 and v. 20 is a deliberate intensification rare even in Ezekiel's emphatic style. The literary effect is the divine displacement of the human shepherds: the I-myself construction marks YHWH's direct entrance into the pastoral office.

Form-critically, the chapter is a *Mahnrede* (admonition speech) layered with *Heilsorakel* (salvation oracle). The combination is distinctive. Greenberg compares its structure to Jer 23:1–8 and Zech 11, both of which deploy the shepherd metaphor in similar combinations of indictment and promise. The *covenant of peace* formula in v. 25 and the *showers of blessing* in v. 26 echo the priestly tradition (Num 25:12; Lev 26:4–6) and the prophetic salvation-oracle tradition (Isa 32:15–18; Hos 2:18–23) — Block reads vv. 25–31 as deliberate intertextual gathering of these earlier strands, the prophet recapitulating the salvation-tradition in its fullest available form.

Textual variants are minor. The MT is well preserved. The LXX renders v. 16's *the fat and the strong I will destroy* (MT *ʾašmîd*) as *I will guard* (Greek *phylaxō*) — a small but theologically loaded variant. Targum Jonathan and the Vulgate follow the MT. Modern critical editions (BHS, BHQ) note the LXX variant but retain the MT reading. The textual problem is small enough to be philological rather than redactional, but it has been deployed in twentieth-century debates about whether YHWH's judgment within the flock (vv. 17–22) is restorative or punitive — the LXX softens the judgment, the MT preserves it.

The chapter's most distinctive literary feature is its *prosopopoeia of the divine voice*. The *I-myself* of vv. 11–16 is sustained for six verses with no shift to third-person divine-speech-about-self; the prophet speaks the divine voice in the first person. Block argues that this is the chapter's literary signal that the human prophetic-pastoral vocation has been transcended: the I-myself voice is the shepherd's voice, not the watchman's voice; the relation of the prophet to the divine speaker is no longer mediation-by-warning but mediation-by-direct-speech.

## Lens 3 — Larger Story

**Jewish reception** treats chapter 34 as the central OT text on royal-political failure and the Davidic-messianic hope. The rabbinic tradition reads the *one shepherd, my servant David* (34:23) messianically — *Targum Jonathan* on 34:23 inserts the explanatory gloss *meshicha bar David* ("the messiah son of David"). The Bavli (b. Sanh. 98b) lists 34:23 among proof-texts for the messiah's name; Maimonides (*Hilkhot Melakhim* 11–12) cites it for the Davidic-messianic restoration; the Zohar (Pinchas) develops the *one shepherd* as the *tiferet* sefirah. Daniel Boyarin's *The Jewish Gospels* (2012) and Israel Knohl's *The Messiah Before Jesus* (2000) document the antiquity of the typology; the *Psalms of Solomon* 17 and Qumran *Damascus Document* CD-A 13:9 deploy Ezek 34's vocabulary. The shepherd-messiah typology is not a Christian retrofit; it is an inner-Jewish development the Christian tradition inherited.

**Christian reception is concentrated in John 10**. *I am the good shepherd* (Jn 10:11, 14) is the most explicit Ezekielian intertext in the New Testament. The discourse is structured around three Ezekielian vocabulary clusters: *the sheep have been scattered* (Ezek 34:5–6) becomes *that they should be one flock, one shepherd* (Jn 10:16); *I myself will search for my sheep* (Ezek 34:11) becomes *I lay down my life for the sheep* (Jn 10:15); the *one shepherd, my servant David* (Ezek 34:23) is implicitly identified with the Davidide Jesus. Andrew Lincoln's *The Gospel According to St. John* (BNTC, 2005), Raymond Brown's *The Gospel According to John I–XII* (Anchor, 1966), and N.T. Wright's *Jesus and the Victory of God* (1996) all treat the Johannine discourse as a deliberate, theologically loaded application of Ezek 34 to Jesus's self-presentation. Wright in particular argues that the discourse claims for Jesus the *I-myself* divine voice of Ezek 34:11–16: the Good Shepherd is not merely a Davidic figure; he is the divine shepherd of Ezek 34 acting in person.

Patristic and medieval reception develops the connection. Origen's *Homilies on Ezekiel* reads 34 christologically; Jerome's *Commentary on Ezekiel* makes the identification structural; Augustine's *Tractates on John* 45 treats Ezek 34 and John 10 as a single unit. Gregory's *Pastoral Rule* (590) is Ezek 34 read pastorally — the bishop's office articulated through the failed-shepherd indictment and YHWH's takeover. Aquinas (*ST* III.7–8) reads the chapter as Christ's claim to be both Davidic shepherd and YHWH-shepherd of Ezek 34:11.

The Catholic tradition develops the *cura pastoralis* into the *Petrine office*: the Pope as *pastor pastorum* (shepherd of shepherds), with explicit theological grounding in Ezek 34, John 10, and John 21 (*feed my sheep*). The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* 882 articulates the office of the Pope as shepherd in continuity with Ezek 34's shepherd-of-shepherds typology. The Reformed tradition contests the Petrine application but preserves the chapter's pastoral logic: Calvin (*Institutes* 4.3 on the offices of the church) reads the *plurality of elders* as the proper structural realization of pastoral oversight — the office is real, but it is collegial, not hierarchical-papal. Calvin's *Lectures on Ezekiel* on chapter 34 polemicizes explicitly against the Roman application: the *one shepherd* is Christ, not the Bishop of Rome, and human shepherds derive their office only as Christ's *under-shepherds*. The Anabaptist and free-church tradition (Menno Simons, John Smyth, the *Schleitheim Confession*) takes the chapter further: there are no proper *shepherds* in the church except Christ; all believers are *priests* (1 Pet 2:9), and pastoral oversight is communal and ad hoc.

The *Davidic shepherd* of v. 23 is the chapter's most contested reception point. Christian reading takes it christologically: Christ is the Davidide *and* the shepherd, identified in the genealogies (Matt 1:1; Luke 3:23–38), confessed in the Gospel narratives (the entry into Jerusalem, the Triumphal Entry, the Davidic acclamations of the crowd), and implicit in John 10's discourse. Jewish reading retains it messianically but as future expectation: the Davidic shepherd-messiah remains awaited; the second-temple, rabbinic, and modern Jewish theological traditions agree on the basic typology while differing on its candidate. Boyarin's *Jewish Gospels* argues that the typology was sufficiently developed in second-temple Judaism that the Christian application would not have been theologically surprising in form, however contested in content.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Divine shepherding versus human shepherds.** The chapter announces YHWH's direct takeover of the pastoral office — *I myself will search for my sheep* (v. 11), *I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep* (v. 15) — and then announces the Davidide as shepherd — *I will set up over them one shepherd, my servant David* (v. 23). Both subjects shepherd. How are they related, and how does the divine shepherding inhabit human ministry?

The five positions:
1. *Catholic Magisterium*: the Petrine office is the structural realization of the divine shepherding through Christ's commissioning of Peter (Matt 16:18–19; John 21:15–17); the Pope as *pastor pastorum* mediates Christ's ongoing shepherding to the universal church; the bishops in communion with the Pope mediate to the local churches. *Lumen Gentium* §22; *Catechism* §882; Joseph Ratzinger *Called to Communion*. The position locates the divine shepherding in a hierarchical structure of human officers in unbroken apostolic succession.
2. *Reformed plurality of elders*: the *one shepherd* is Christ; human pastoral oversight is genuinely commissioned but is *plural*, *collegial*, and *under-shepherding*; the office is real but distributed, with no single human officer claiming the *one shepherd* title. Calvin *Institutes* 4.3; James Bannerman *The Church of Christ*; Jonathan Leeman on Reformed ecclesiology. The position locates the divine shepherding in a collegial structure that prevents human absorption of the *one shepherd* office.
3. *Anabaptist priesthood of all believers*: *all* believers are priests (1 Pet 2:9); pastoral functions are communal; clerical hierarchy contradicts the priesthood's universality; the *one shepherd* is Christ alone, with no human structural mediation. *Schleitheim Confession*; John Howard Yoder *Body Politics*; Stuart Murray *The Naked Anabaptist*. The position locates the divine shepherding in the gathered congregation's collective discernment.
4. *Eastern Orthodox conciliar*: the bishops in council represent the divine shepherding, but the Spirit guides through the *consensus fidelium* (the consent of the faithful); the office is hierarchical but its authority is conciliar and pneumatological, not papal. Vladimir Lossky *Mystical Theology*; John Zizioulas *Being as Communion*; Kallistos Ware *The Orthodox Church*. The position locates the divine shepherding in the council's discernment, with the Spirit as the active subject.
5. *Pentecostal-charismatic prophetic*: the *one shepherd* is Christ acting *now* through Spirit-given charisms; pastoral office is real but functional, given by direct charism rather than formal commission; the structure is open to ongoing apostolic-prophetic disruption. *Apostolic Faith* tradition; Wayne Grudem *Are Miraculous Gifts for Today?*. The position locates the divine shepherding in present-tense charismatic giftedness.

Steelman the position you find least congenial. If you tend toward Reformed or Anabaptist accounts, steelman the Catholic Magisterium: a theology with no structural office of pastoral oversight at the universal-church level risks either congregational fragmentation (the Anabaptist outcome) or doctrinal drift over generations (the historical Reformed outcome on contested doctrines). If you tend toward Catholic accounts, steelman the priesthood of all believers: a theology that locates the *one shepherd*'s mediation in a single human office risks substituting the office for the shepherd — the very pattern Ezekiel indicts in 34:2–10.

**Tension 2: The Davidic shepherd.** *I will set up over them one shepherd, my servant David, and he shall feed them: he shall feed them and be their shepherd. And I, the LORD, will be their God, and my servant David shall be prince among them* (34:23–24). The promise is plain: a future Davidide will shepherd Israel, with YHWH as God and David as *nāśî* (prince). Christian reading identifies this Davidide as Jesus, citing the genealogies, the crucifixion *titulus regis Iudaeorum*, the Davidic acclamations in the Gospels, and the Johannine Good Shepherd discourse. Jewish reading retains the messianic typology but holds that the Davidic shepherd-messiah remains awaited.

The five positions:
1. *Christological fulfillment*: Christ is the Davidide and the shepherd, the *one shepherd* of v. 23 fully realized in his earthly ministry, his death, his resurrection, his ascension, and his ongoing reign. N.T. Wright *Jesus and the Victory of God*; Richard Hays *The Conversion of the Imagination*. The Christian position with sustained engagement with the OT promise structure.
2. *Christological inauguration with consummation pending*: Christ is the Davidide and shepherd inaugurated; the full pastoral promise of vv. 25–31 (the covenant of peace, the security from beasts, the showers of blessing, the never-suffering-reproach) awaits eschatological consummation. G.E. Ladd *A Theology of the New Testament*; G.K. Beale *The Temple and the Church's Mission*. The Christian *already-and-not-yet* position.
3. *Jewish messianic expectation*: the Davidic shepherd-messiah is awaited; the typology is biblical-canonical; the candidate has not yet appeared; the messianic age is future. Maimonides *Hilkhot Melakhim* 11–12; Marc-Alain Ouaknin; Gershon Cohen on the Davidic messiah. The position takes seriously the OT promise structure and the messianic hope without resolving it in any past candidate.
4. *Two-stage messiah (Jewish and some Christian readings)*: there are two messianic figures in the OT — *messiah ben Joseph* (the suffering one) and *messiah ben David* (the reigning one); both are anticipated; the Christian claim is that Jesus is both, but the second-stage realization remains future. Boyarin *Jewish Gospels*; Knohl *Messiah Before Jesus*. The position recognizes the typological complexity.
5. *Demythologized political*: the Davidic-shepherd promise is the prophet's articulation of restored political-pastoral leadership in restored post-exilic Israel; the messianic typology is post-Ezekielian midrash; the chapter is about political restoration, not eschatological figure. Walther Eichrodt *Theology of the Old Testament*; Brevard Childs *Old Testament Theology in a Canonical Context* on the limits of typological reading. The position reads chapter 34 within its sixth-century horizon.

Steelman the position you find least congenial. If you tend toward Christological fulfillment, steelman Jewish messianic expectation: the chapter's plain promise of vv. 25–31 — security from wild beasts, never-failing rains, no nations devouring — has not been realized in the post-Christian world; the typology may be valid while the consummation remains future, and a triumphalist reading risks evading the unredeemed character of present history. If you tend toward Jewish messianic expectation, steelman Christological fulfillment: the typology's specifics — the *one shepherd* who feeds the flock, the *prince* who is also the divine voice's *I-myself* — finds its closest historical fit in the figure of Jesus, and a refusal to consider the candidate may be theological fastidiousness.

ONE unresolved question: When YHWH says *I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep* (v. 15) and immediately also says *I will set up over them one shepherd, my servant David* (v. 23), how do these two statements relate? Christian reading collapses them in Christ: the divine shepherd and the Davidic shepherd are one person. Jewish reading distinguishes them: YHWH shepherds *through* the Davidide, who is the human officer of the divine shepherding. The Hebrew syntax permits both readings; the chapter does not adjudicate. The unresolved question is whether the OT promise structure invites Christological collapse or holds the divine and human shepherds in covenantal cooperation.

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references. First, **John 10:11–18** — *I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep* — the most explicit NT use of Ezek 34. The discourse claims for Jesus the *I-myself* divine voice of Ezek 34:11–16 and the *one shepherd* office of 34:23. Wright's *Jesus and the Victory of God* argues that the discourse's claim is so direct that the audience's response in 10:31 (*the Jews picked up stones again to stone him*) registers the recognition of Jesus's claim to the *I-myself* divine pastoral voice.

Second, **1 Peter 5:1–4** — *I exhort the elders among you… shepherd the flock of God among you, exercising oversight, not under compulsion, but willingly… not as those domineering over those entrusted to you, but being examples to the flock. And when the chief Shepherd appears, you will receive the unfading crown of glory* — which is Ezek 34 received pastorally into the church's elder-ministry. The phrase *those entrusted to you* (Greek *tōn klērōn*) and the *chief Shepherd* (Greek *archipoimenos*) deploy Ezek 34's structural logic into the office of the Christian elder, with Christ as the *archipoimēn* who mediates the divine shepherding through his under-shepherds.

Third, **Revelation 7:17** — *the Lamb in the midst of the throne will be their shepherd, and he will guide them to springs of living water, and God will wipe away every tear from their eyes* — which collapses the shepherd-Lamb identity in eschatological climax. The image is paradoxical: the *Lamb* is the *shepherd*; the slain becomes the slaughterer's protector; the sacrificial victim becomes the pastoral subject. The image draws on Ezek 34 (the shepherd's pastoral function), Isa 53 (the lamb-led-to-slaughter), and Ps 23 (the shepherd's leading), and the synthesis is uniquely apocalyptic. The Ezekielian shepherd-promise is consummated in a figure the prophet himself could not have anticipated: the shepherd who is also the lamb.
