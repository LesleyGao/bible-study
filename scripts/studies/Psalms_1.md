# Psalm 1: The Tree, the Chaff, and the Door into the Psalter

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Psalm 1 carries no superscription. This silence is itself an editorial fact. In a Psalter where seventy-three psalms bear Davidic attribution, a dozen are tied to Asaph, and several are credited to the sons of Korah, the absence of *le-david* over Psalm 1 is the redactors' way of pulling the reader aside before the music starts. The Septuagint manuscripts confirm this: many do not number Psalm 1, treating it as a preface rather than a song. Several Hebrew manuscripts go further and run Pss 1-2 together as a single composition, and the Western reading of Acts 13:33 quotes Ps 2:7 as occurring in "the *first* psalm" — likely preserving a textual tradition in which Pss 1 and 2 functioned as one programmatic gateway.

Form-critically, Psalm 1 is a **wisdom psalm** in Hermann Gunkel's *Einleitung in die Psalmen* (1933) classification — the same category that gathers Pss 37, 49, 73, 112, 119, 127, 128, and 133. Gunkel saw wisdom psalms as products of the post-exilic scribal classes rather than the temple cult proper, sharing a *Sitz im Leben* with Proverbs and the later strata of the wisdom tradition. Sigmund Mowinckel, who otherwise pushed for cultic origins of nearly every psalm in *The Psalms in Israel's Worship* (1962), conceded that the wisdom psalms — and Psalm 1 above all — represented "learned psalmography" produced by the same circles that compiled and shaped the canonical Psalter into its present five-book form. The dating most scholars now favor is **late post-exilic**, perhaps fourth century BCE, when the Torah had achieved scriptural authority and *meditating on Yahweh's torah day and night* had become both a literary ideal and a piety program. Gerald Wilson's *The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* (1985) made the now-standard case that Pss 1-2 were placed at the head of the collection precisely as the editor's hermeneutical key.

The material culture behind the imagery is agricultural and irrigated. The "tree planted by streams of water" (1:3) reflects Levantine horticulture in which a tree's survival depended on access to a *peleg* (פֶּלֶג), a channel of irrigated water diverted from a wadi or spring — not the natural rain-watered hillside vegetation that withered in the dry season but a deliberately situated, cultivated tree. The contrast with *motz* (מֹץ), "chaff," is the threshing floor: harvested grain heaped on a hilltop, tossed into the wind so the heavier kernel falls and the husk blows away. ANE parallels are abundant. The Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemope* (likely 12th-11th century BCE) opens with strikingly similar imagery — the silent, righteous man as a tree planted in the orchard, the hot-tempered as a tree in the open that loses its leaves. The Mesopotamian *Counsels of Wisdom* and the Sumerian *Instructions of Shuruppak* draw on the same constellation. Whether this is direct influence or shared cultural water-table is debated, but the motif of "two ways" — *the way of the righteous* and *the way of the wicked* — is at home across the entire ancient Near East before it becomes Israelite property.

Five Hebrew terms control the psalm.

- **אַשְׁרֵי** (*ashrei*, 1:1) — "happy / blessed / fortunate." Not the priestly blessing-word *barukh* (בָּרוּךְ) but a wisdom term, the same exclamation that opens twenty-six psalms and the Beatitudes' Greek *makarios* (μακάριος, Matt 5:3-11). It is congratulatory rather than performative — pointing rather than pronouncing.
- **תּוֹרָה** (*torah*, 1:2) — not narrowly "law" but instruction, the entire received teaching. By the late post-exilic period this almost certainly meant the Pentateuch in its emerging canonical form.
- **הָגָה** (*hagah*, 1:2) — to "meditate," but the verb is physical: it is the sound a lion makes growling over its prey (Isa 31:4), the moan of a dove (Isa 38:14). Torah meditation here is murmured, vocalized, ruminated — not silent contemplation.
- **פֶּלֶג** (*peleg*, 1:3) — irrigation channel, not natural stream.
- **מֹץ** (*motz*, 1:4) — chaff, the husk separated from the grain at threshing.

Textual evidence from the Dead Sea Scrolls reinforces the psalm's introductory function. The Great Psalms Scroll **11Q5 (11QPsa)** contains a notably different ordering of Book V and includes apocryphal compositions, but Psalm 1 is consistently first in the surviving fragments that begin Book I. The Septuagint numbering diverges from MT beginning at Pss 9-10 (which LXX unifies into Ps 9, pushing all subsequent numbers down by one through Ps 147), but Ps 1 holds its programmatic position in every major textual witness.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Psalm 1 is six verses and roughly seventy Hebrew words, but its architecture is extraordinarily controlled. Its parallelism ranges across all four of Robert Lowth's 1753 *De Sacra Poesi Hebraeorum* categories — synonymous, antithetic, synthetic, and climactic — sometimes in a single verse.

**Verse 1** is climactic, a triple-step descent: *walks not in the counsel of the wicked / nor stands in the way of sinners / nor sits in the seat of scoffers*. The verbs intensify in stillness — walk, stand, sit — while the nouns escalate in hostility — *resha'im* (wicked), *hata'im* (sinners), *letzim* (scoffers). James Kugel in *The Idea of Biblical Poetry* (1981) called this the "A is so, and what's more B" structure that he argued was the underlying logic of all biblical parallelism. Robert Alter's *The Art of Biblical Poetry* (1985) identified the same dynamic as "intensification" — the second and third lines do not merely repeat but heighten. Adele Berlin's *The Dynamics of Biblical Parallelism* (1985) showed how the morphological symmetry (verb + noun + adjective) reinforces the semantic crescendo.

**Verse 2** turns on antithetic parallelism (*but his delight is in the law of the LORD*) — the *ki im* hinge that defines the righteous over against verse 1's negation. Verses 3-4 form the central antithetic structure of the whole psalm: the righteous as **tree** planted by water, fruit-bearing, leaf-enduring, prospering — and then a single brutal hemistich, "the wicked are not so, but are like chaff." The asymmetry is intentional. The righteous get four lines of agricultural permanence; the wicked get one line and a verb of dispersion (*tiddefenu ruach*, "the wind drives away").

**Verses 5-6** close with synthetic parallelism, building the consequence: the wicked will not stand in the *mishpat* (judgment) nor in the *adat tzaddikim* (congregation of the righteous), *for* — the *ki* of grounding — Yahweh *yodea* (knows, with the relational weight of *yada* in Gen 4:1) the way of the righteous, while the way of the wicked *to'ved* (perishes, vanishes).

The psalm is bounded by an **inclusio**: *ashrei ha-ish* ("blessed is the man," 1:1) opens, and *derekh resha'im to'ved* ("the way of the wicked perishes," 1:6) closes — happiness contrasted with destruction, the man placed against the way. Susan Gillingham (*Psalms Through the Centuries*) and Patrick Miller (*Interpreting the Psalms*) both note that the chiastic frame organizes the psalm around a central axis — the tree of verse 3 — making the entire piece a poetic icon of two stable, mutually exclusive existential possibilities.

There is no *selah*, no superscription, no musical instruction. This silence matters: it tells the reader that Psalm 1 is not for singing in the cult but for reading and ruminating — the very *hagah* the psalm itself prescribes.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Placement in the Five-Book Psalter

The Masoretic Psalter is divided into five books, each ending with a doxology (Pss 41:13, 72:18-19, 89:52, 106:48, 150). Book I (Pss 1-41) is overwhelmingly Davidic. Gerald Wilson, building on Brevard Childs and amplified by Patrick Miller, J. Clinton McCann, and Beth Tanner, argued that Pss 1-2 form a deliberate **double-gate** to the entire collection. Psalm 1 is the *torah-gate* (the wise reader who meditates on instruction); Psalm 2 is the *messianic gate* (the king on Zion against whom the nations rage). To enter the Psalter you must pass under both — submit to torah and trust the anointed. That editorial framing makes Psalm 1 not merely the first psalm but the **hermeneutical key** to the other 149.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

The Qumran community's **11Q5/11QPsa** preserved a Psalter that included material absent from MT and arranged Book V differently, but the wisdom-Torah piety of Psalm 1 was deeply at home there — the Yahad's self-understanding as those who "meditate on torah day and night" (1QS 6:6-8) is almost a citation. **Midrash Tehillim** (compiled in stages between the late Talmudic and early medieval period) reads the "blessed man" as Adam, then as Abraham, then as anyone who studies Torah; the rabbis play on the absence of a name to make the verse universally claimable. **Rashi** (eleventh century) reads the three verbs of v. 1 — walk, stand, sit — as progressive entanglement in sin, glossing the "seat of scoffers" as the most dangerous because it is settled and habitual. **Ibn Ezra** (twelfth century) emphasizes the philological precision of *hagah* as vocalized murmuring, anchoring the medieval Jewish discipline of *girsa* (recitation-study). **Radak (David Kimhi)** and **Nachmanides** both treat the psalm as a meditation on free will and the soul's two paths. In the modern period, **Avivah Zornberg** (*The Beginning of Desire*, *The Murmuring Deep*) reads Psalm 1's *hagah* against the Hebrew Bible's broader pattern of inarticulate sound — moaning, growling, mourning — as the substratum of meaning beneath language. **Jon Levenson** in *Sinai and Zion* and **Nahum Sarna** in *Songs of the Heart* both stress that the psalm is not Pelagian works-righteousness but an account of how delight in Torah *forms* the self over time.

Liturgically, Psalm 1 is not part of the daily *Shir Shel Yom* cycle (Sun=24, Mon=48, Tue=82, Wed=94, Thu=81, Fri=93, Sat=92) and is not in *Hallel* or *Pesukei deZimra*. It functions instead as an opening to the entire book — recited at the conclusion of certain study sessions and on occasions when the Psalter as a whole is recited (during illness, mourning, or for the dying).

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

**Augustine's** *Enarrationes in Psalmos* opens with Psalm 1 read Christologically: "the man" is Christ, who alone walked not in the counsel of the wicked, the *lignum* (tree) is the cross, the *fructus* the redeemed nations. **Athanasius's** *Letter to Marcellinus* describes the Psalter as "a mirror of the soul" and treats Psalm 1 as the soul's first turning. **Cassiodorus** (sixth century) systematized the patristic reading. **Theodore of Mopsuestia**, by contrast, resisted the Christological allegorization and read the psalm as plain wisdom instruction — the Antiochene-Alexandrian split visible already in patristic Psalter commentary. **Hilary of Poitiers** built his entire Psalms commentary around Pss 1-2 as the porch.

**Aquinas's** *Postilla super Psalmos* (lectures, c. 1272-73) reads Psalm 1 fourfold — literal, allegorical, moral, anagogical — with the tree as Christ-cross-church-saint. **Martin Luther's** *Operationes in Psalmos* (1519-21), his second Psalms lecture cycle, made Psalm 1 the foundation for his theology of the Word: meditation on torah is *not* the law's accusing function but the gospel-formed delight of the believer in Christ's righteousness. **John Calvin's** 1557 commentary, in which he famously called the Psalter "an anatomy of all the parts of the soul," reads Psalm 1 as the saints' portrait — those whose lives have been reordered by election around the law of God.

The critical tradition has been shaped by **Hermann Gunkel** (form criticism, wisdom psalm classification), **Sigmund Mowinckel** (cultic origins, but conceding Ps 1's scribal *Sitz*), **Claus Westermann** (*Praise and Lament in the Psalms*, 1965), **Hans-Joachim Kraus** (*Psalmen*, BKAT, 1960), **Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger** (the Hermeneia three-volume commentary completed 2011), **John Goldingay** (three-volume Baker commentary, 2006-2008), **Marvin Tate**, **Leslie Allen**, and **Gerald Wilson**. Among contemporary readers, **Walter Brueggemann**'s *The Message of the Psalms* (1984) places Psalm 1 squarely in his "psalms of orientation" category — the well-ordered world where the righteous prosper and the wicked perish — and warns that this orientation is precisely what Pss 73 and 88 will tear apart. **Patrick Miller** (*The Lord of the Psalms*) reads Ps 1 as the editor's torah-piety frame; **William P. Brown** (*Seeing the Psalms*) attends to the visual-arboreal imagery; **Beth Tanner** (with deClaissé-Walford and Jacobson, *NICOT* commentary 2014) emphasizes the wisdom and royal frames as joint hermeneutical keys.

### NT Use

Psalm 1 is not directly quoted in the New Testament, though its imagery saturates the synoptic tradition. **Matthew 7:13-27** (the two ways, the two builders, the tree known by its fruit) is virtually a sermon on Psalm 1. **Jeremiah 17:5-8** contains an almost exact parallel of the tree-by-water imagery; the majority critical view (Holladay, Lundbom, Goldingay) is that **Psalm 1 depends on Jeremiah** rather than the reverse, given Jeremiah's likely earlier date and his specifically situated lament context. **Revelation 22:1-2** picks up the imagery for the eschaton: the tree of life by the river of the water of life, leaves for the healing of the nations — the tree of Psalm 1 fulfilled in cosmic scope.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author's vision is that there are two and only two existential trajectories — the *derekh tzaddikim* (way of the righteous) and the *derekh resha'im* (way of the wicked) — and that the difference is constituted by a sustained relational orientation: delight in and meditation on Yahweh's torah. The psalm does not say the righteous are without sin. It says they have not located their lives among the wicked, the sinners, and the scoffers. They are *transplanted* (the participle *shatul* in 1:3 is passive — they have been planted there, by another), and their fruitfulness is the natural consequence of where they stand. The wicked, by contrast, are not described as actively malicious; they are simply *not so* — rootless, wind-blown, dispersed.

### Tension One: The Tidy Retributive Theology vs. Lived Reality

The sharpest theological tension in Psalm 1 is internal to the Psalter itself: the psalm promises that the righteous prosper and the wicked perish, and Pss 37, 49, 73, and 88 spend hundreds of verses wrestling with the visible fact that this is not how the world works. Asaph in Ps 73 nearly loses his footing watching the wicked thrive. Job, that wisdom counter-text, demolishes the simple retributive grid the friends keep deploying. So what is Psalm 1 doing at the head of a collection that contains its own most acute counter-evidence?

- **Walter Brueggemann's reading**: Psalm 1 is "naive orientation" — the well-ordered world the wisdom tradition imagines and that the believer must inhabit before disorientation can do its work. The placement is dialectical, not dogmatic. The Psalter teaches you to start here, lose this, and arrive at re-orientation in the doxologies of Pss 146-150. This is the most influential contemporary reading.
- **Brevard Childs's canonical reading**: The placement makes Psalm 1 the hermeneutical horizon under which all subsequent psalms must be read. Even the disorientation of Ps 88 ("darkness is my closest friend") happens *within* the Psalter framed by Pss 1 and 150 — the lament is real, but it is bordered by the affirmation that the way of the righteous does not perish in Yahweh's knowing.
- **Reformed reading (Calvin, contemporary heirs like Sinclair Ferguson, Tremper Longman)**: The psalm is eschatological. The "judgment" of v. 5 is the final assize, not present prosperity. The retributive promise is not falsified by the wicked's temporary success because the verb *to'ved* (perish) speaks ultimately, not chronometrically.
- **Lutheran reading (Luther, Bonhoeffer's *Psalms: Prayer Book of the Bible*)**: The "blessed man" is finally Christ. No human being has actually walked, stood, and sat as Psalm 1 demands; the psalm describes a portrait we cannot achieve but that Christ inhabits and gives to those in him. The retributive grid is real but only fulfilled in the Messiah's perfect obedience and the believer's incorporation into him.
- **Wesleyan / Anabaptist reading**: The psalm is a description of the genuinely transformed life — the *ashrei* is offered to those who actively choose, who form habits of delight, who refuse the seat of scoffers. The grid is true on a generational and communal scale, even when individual cases seem to contradict.
- **Eastern Orthodox reading (Athanasius, contemporary writers like Patrick Henry Reardon)**: The psalm is liturgical-formative; daily recitation in matins shapes the soul into the tree by the water. The retributive frame is true because participation in Christ is true — the righteous *become* fruitful by participation, not by moral arithmetic.
- **Jewish reading (Sarna, Levenson, Zornberg)**: The psalm is descriptive of formation, not transactional reward. *Hagah* over time builds a life that is itself the blessing — the tree by water is not a payment for righteousness but a portrait of what righteousness *is* when it has had time to grow. Lived reality contradicts a vending-machine reading; it does not contradict a formation reading.
- **Critical / Academic reading (Gunkel, Mowinckel, Brueggemann)**: The psalm is a wisdom-school product reflecting a particular sapiential confidence, deliberately placed by editors to set a particular reading frame. The Psalter as a whole renders the simple confidence inadequate while preserving it as starting orientation.

**Steelmanning the least-intuitive position** — the Lutheran-Christological reading. To modern ears it sounds like overreach to find the historical Christ in a sixth-century scribe's wisdom poem. But the case is structurally serious. The psalm describes a man who *never* takes the wrong step at any of three escalating levels — never walks, never stands, never sits with the wicked. As a description of any historical individual it is implausible to the point of fiction. The psalm therefore either functions as an asymptote (an ideal toward which the reader strains) or it functions as a portrait of someone in particular. The early church, reading the Psalter in light of the resurrection, judged that the portrait fits exactly one figure. That judgment is not exegetical strain but a reading shaped by what they took to be the only fulfillment that holds the language together.

**Where this leaves us**: Psalm 1 is best read as a *programmatic invitation* rather than a transactional promise. It tells the reader entering the Psalter what the well-ordered world looks like — and the rest of the Psalter will tell you what to do when that world breaks. The retributive grid is not falsified by Pss 73 and 88; it is *deepened* by them. The way of the righteous does not perish, but it sometimes runs through Sheol.

### Closing Question

If Psalm 1 portrays a human being who never walks, stands, or sits with the wicked — and no historical reader has ever been that human being — what work is the psalm doing when it places such a figure at the door, asking us to enter a book of 150 prayers in his name?

## The Thread

- **Jeremiah 17:5-8** — the prophet's tree-by-water with cursed/blessed antithesis. Almost certainly the older text, which Psalm 1's late-post-exilic editor draws into a wisdom frame. The shared imagery of *ya'al* (cursed) trust in flesh vs. *barukh* (blessed) trust in Yahweh, the parched desert vs. the verdant tree, makes Jer 17 the closest scriptural cousin to Ps 1.
- **Matthew 7:13-27** — Jesus' two-ways teaching at the Sermon on the Mount's close. The narrow gate, the tree known by its fruit, the wise builder versus the fool: a cluster of Ps-1-saturated wisdom imagery now placed under the eschatological pressure of the kingdom.
- **Psalm 73** — the most direct internal challenge to Psalm 1's tidy retributive theology. Asaph "almost slipped" watching the wicked prosper; the resolution comes only when he enters the sanctuary and perceives the long arc.
- **Revelation 22:1-2** — the tree of Psalm 1 cosmically expanded: a single tree of life by the river of life, bearing twelve fruits, leaves for the healing of the nations. The wisdom-poem image becomes the New Jerusalem's center.
