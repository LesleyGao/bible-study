# Ecclesiastes 3: Eternity in the Human Heart

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 3 contains the book's most quoted poem (3:1–8) and its most theologically loaded single verse (3:11). The chapter sits within the Persian-period composition Choon-Leong Seow (Anchor, 1997) and the critical mainstream date to the 5th–4th centuries BC, and the chapter's vocabulary supports that dating. *'Et* (עֵת, "time") in the *appointed-time* sense of 3:1–8 is widespread in Hebrew but the *catalog form* — paired antitheses of human activity assigned to fixed times — has its closest ANE parallels in Egyptian *Onomastica* (catalog wisdom literature) and in Mesopotamian *menological* and *astrological* lists that paired actions with auspicious and inauspicious days. James Crenshaw (OTL, 1987) and Roland Murphy (Word, 1992) both see Eccl 3:1–8 as a Hebrew poetic adaptation of this ANE *kairological* tradition: not divination, but a meditation on the structure of human time.

The political and social world implied is again the small temple-state under imperial administration. The judicial complaint of 3:16 — "in the place of justice, even there was wickedness" — reflects exactly the corruption-of-courts theme that 5:8 will name explicitly as a feature of the bureaucratic provincial system; a high official is watched by a higher, and there are yet higher ones above. The mention of the breath/spirit (*ruach*) of man and beast in 3:19–21 belongs to a Persian-period anthropological vocabulary (Genesis 2:7's *nefesh hayyah*, Job 12:10, Psalm 104:29–30) that the rabbinic and Hellenistic period will sharpen into the immortality-of-the-soul question. Qohelet's *mi yode'a* (3:21, "who knows?") is the diagnostic tell: he writes before the doctrine of resurrection has become fixed in Jewish theology, and his agnosticism is therefore not a polemic against an established belief but an honest position in an unsettled debate. (Daniel 12:2's resurrection promise belongs to the same broad Persian/Hellenistic period and represents the position Qohelet does *not* share.)

The signature philological crux of the chapter is *ha-'olam natan be-libbam* (3:11) — "he has put *'olam* into their hearts." *'Olam* (עוֹלָם) in Biblical Hebrew has a semantic range from "long duration" to "perpetuity" to "remote time" (past or future) to "the world." The translation choices fan out wildly: KJV/ESV "eternity"; NRSV "a sense of past and future"; JPS "a sense of duration" (following Fox); the Targum "wisdom"; the Vulgate *mundum* ("the world," picking up the post-biblical Hebrew sense). Seow defends "eternity" against Fox's "duration"; Fox argues that Qohelet's *'olam* in this verse is the *consciousness of time* rather than the metaphysical reality of eternity; some critical scholars (Whybray) read it as "the world" and follow the Vulgate. The decision matters: if "eternity," then 3:11 grounds the most famous Christian gloss on the verse — Augustine's "you have made us for yourself, and our hearts are restless until they rest in thee" (*Confessions* 1.1) — even though Augustine never directly cites Eccl 3:11. If "duration" or "consciousness of time," then Qohelet is making a phenomenological observation about the human capacity to be *aware of* duration without grasping its totality, with no necessary metaphysical claim. Fox notes pointedly: there is no other passage in the Hebrew Bible where *'olam* clearly means metaphysical eternity in the Greek philosophical sense; the Septuagint's translation choices throughout the OT use *aiōn*, which itself shifts meaning in late antiquity.

Other key Hebrew terms: *yafeh* (יָפֶה, "beautiful/fitting," 3:11) — Seow argues this means "appropriate" or "fitting" rather than "beautiful," restoring the verse's connection to the time-poem (each thing is *fitting in its time*); *miqreh* (מִקְרֶה, "fate/event," 3:19) — the same word that hammered chapter 2 returns to assert beast-and-human equivalence in death; *ruach* (רוּחַ, 3:19, 3:21) — "breath/spirit/wind," with the deliberate ambiguity Qohelet exploits throughout (the same *ruach* humans share with beasts in 3:19 is the *ruach* whose direction at death is unknown in 3:21). And *helek* (חֵלֶק, 3:22) — "lot/portion" — closing the chapter with the same inheritance vocabulary that closed chapter 2.

Textual base: Qumran 4QQoh-a preserves portions of chapter 3, agreeing closely with MT. The LXX renders 3:11's *'olam* as *aiōn* (consistent with its general practice). The Targum to chapter 3 is heavily expansive: 3:21's question "who knows whether the spirit of man goes upward?" becomes in the Targum a confident answer ("the spirit of the righteous goes upward to heaven, and the spirit of the wicked goes down to Gehenna") — exactly the kind of theological domestication the rabbis applied to soften Qohelet's agnosticism.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is structurally one of the book's most carefully composed. The *Time Poem* of 3:1–8 — fourteen pairs of opposed times, organized into seven pairs of pairs (or, by Seow's analysis, two heptads) — is followed by a prose meditation on its meaning (3:9–15), a justice-and-judgment reflection (3:16–17), an anthropological meditation on death (3:18–21), and a closing carpe diem refrain (3:22). The poem itself is one of the most translated and adapted passages in the Hebrew Bible — Pete Seeger's 1959 song "Turn! Turn! Turn!" (the Byrds' 1965 hit) preserves the poem's text almost exactly, adding only the antiwar coda "I swear it's not too late." Seeger's gloss makes clear what most popular reception assumes: that 3:1–8 is a serene affirmation of cosmic harmony.

Fox's analysis (*A Time to Tear Down*, 1999) pushes hard against this reading, and most critical scholarship now follows him. The poem is not a celebration; it is, on Fox's reading, a *limitation*. The point of 3:9 — "what gain has the worker from his toil?" — placed immediately after the catalog is that the *human being does not get to choose* when to weep or laugh, plant or pluck up, kill or heal. The times come; the human is subject to them. Read this way, the poem is not Stoic resignation but an assertion of the human's *temporal heteronomy*: you live inside a time you did not author. Crenshaw, Murphy, and Seow all converge on this reading, with varying degrees of emphasis on whether the heteronomy is comforting (because God authors the times) or exhausting (because the human cannot align himself with them).

The poem's literary form deserves attention. Each line follows the structure *'et la-X ve-'et la-Y*, with X and Y semantic opposites. The fourteen pairs may be intentional (twice seven, the canonical wisdom number); the ordering is debated — some scholars find a chiastic structure, others find the poem's apparent randomness itself meaningful. Seow notes the poem moves from the basic pair (born/die) outward through agriculture, conflict, emotion, social practice (cast/gather stones may refer to fertility ritual or building practice — the rabbinic reading at *Shemot Rabbah* takes "cast stones" as marital relations), property, communication, and finally love/hate and war/peace. The cumulative effect is that the totality of human action falls under the *'et* category.

Devices: leitwort *'et* (twenty-eight occurrences in the poem alone — one for each phrase), inclusio (the poem opens with birth and closes with peace, framing creation and final order), the *yipheh be-'itto* echo (3:11 picks up the poem's "in its time" and applies it cosmically — God makes everything fitting *in its time*), and the *mi yode'a* refrain (3:21 — "who knows?" — Qohelet's epistemic signature). The chapter ends with the second carpe diem refrain (3:22) — "there is nothing better than that a man should rejoice in his work, for that is his *helek*; who can bring him to see what will be after him?" — which functions as a structural rhyme with 2:24–26.

The chapter contains the book's most concentrated theological vocabulary: *ha-elohim* (God) appears nine times in 3:10–18, the densest concentration in the book. The rhetorical effect is that the chapter is, paradoxically, the book's most *theological* chapter and its most *agnostic*: God is everywhere named and yet almost nowhere knowable. *Mi yode'a* (who knows?) at 3:21 is the chapter's refusal to close the gap.

Textual variant of significance: 3:21 — *mi yode'a ruach beney ha-adam ha-'olah hi' lema'lah u-ruach ha-behema ha-yoredet hi' lemattah la-arets*. The MT vocalization treats *ha-'olah* and *ha-yoredet* as definite articles with participles ("the [one] going up" and "the [one] going down") — a declarative form that asserts the destinations. But the alternative vocalization (favored by some manuscripts, the Targum, and many medieval Jewish commentators) treats them as interrogative *he* — "*does* the spirit of man go up and *does* the spirit of beast go down?" The interrogative reading makes Qohelet's agnosticism explicit. Most modern translations follow the interrogative reading on contextual grounds (the verse begins with *mi yode'a*) even where the MT pointing differs.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The time-poem and the *'olam*-in-the-heart verse have together generated more interpretive weight than perhaps any other passage in the wisdom corpus. The Jewish trajectory begins with the rabbinic puzzle. *Qohelet Rabbah* on 3:11 reads *'olam* as referring to the *love of the world* God has placed in human hearts — the *yetzer ha-ra*, the inclination toward the temporal that prevents humans from finding out what God has done. Maimonides (*Guide* 3.51) reads 3:11 philosophically: *'olam* is the rational soul's *capacity for the eternal*, which the body's *hevel* nature prevents from achieving union with the divine intellect. Ibn Ezra reads it grammatically and locally — *'olam* as "duration" — and resists the philosophical inflation. Rashi reads *'olam* as "the world" (following the rabbinic gloss): God has put love-of-the-world in human hearts, which is precisely why humans cannot grasp God's ways. Robert Gordis (*Koheleth*, 1951) reads *'olam* as the consciousness of duration — closer to Fox's later reading.

The Christian patristic reception is dominated by Gregory of Nyssa's *Homilies on Ecclesiastes* (the homilies cover 1:1–3:13, ending mid-chapter; Gregory's planned continuation does not survive). Gregory reads 3:11's *'olam* through the LXX's *aiōn* and through a Platonist anthropology: the soul has been *measured to* eternity, which is why the temporal cannot satisfy it. This is the major patristic gloss, and it underwrites the entire Christian *imago Dei* tradition that locates the human's distinguishing capacity in eternity-orientation. Augustine's *cor inquietum* of *Confessions* 1.1 belongs to this stream even though Augustine does not cite the verse directly. Jerome's commentary (388) reads 3:11 similarly. Hugh of St. Victor's *Homilies on Ecclesiastes* (12th c.) develops the eternity-in-the-heart line into a full anthropological doctrine. Aquinas (*ST* I-II q. 3) cites Eccl 3:11 in his treatment of the natural desire for the beatific vision.

The Reformation's most famous gloss on chapter 3 is Luther's *Notes on Ecclesiastes* (1526/1532). Luther reads the time-poem as a divine providential ordering and 3:11's *'olam* as the human capacity for fearing God — *aeternitatem*, but not in the philosophical sense; rather, the orientation toward divine government that even fallen humans cannot extinguish. Luther loved 3:14 ("whatever God does endures forever; nothing can be added to it, nor anything taken from it") as a sovereignty text. Calvin (silent on Ecclesiastes generally) leaves no commentary, but his *Institutes* draws on 3:11 obliquely in the famous *sensus divinitatis* passage (1.3.1): humans bear an awareness of God they cannot escape. Puritan readings (Cawdry, Bridges) develop the providential reading.

Critical scholarship from Delitzsch (1875) onward has tended to deflate the eternity-reading. Whybray, Crenshaw, and Fox all argue that the text supports something less than a metaphysical claim about the soul's structure: humans are aware of duration (past and future) without grasping totality. Seow occupies a middle position: *'olam* is "eternity" in the sense of "all time," and the verse is making a real claim about human cognition, but the claim is bounded — humans are aware of time-as-totality, not aware of God-as-eternal. The contemporary divisions are real: Iain Provan (NIVAC) recovers something close to the patristic reading; Peter Enns reads it as honest agnosticism; Ellen Davis emphasizes the verse's tension with 3:21's "who knows?"; Walter Brueggemann reads it as the human capacity to be *unsettled* by God.

The animal-death passage of 3:18–21 has a parallel reception history. Jewish reading historically softens it: the Targum supplies the missing reassurance (the righteous spirit goes up; the wicked goes down); *Qohelet Rabbah* reads it as God testing the children of Adam to humble them. Maimonides reads it philosophically — the rational soul (which beasts lack) is what survives, but the verse describes only the embodied creaturely *ruach*. Christian patristic and medieval reading allegorizes (3:18–21 describes the *carnal* human, not the *spiritual* one) or apologizes (Qohelet is here describing what the senses see, not what revelation knows). Reformation reading splits: Luther reads it as honest creatureliness; Calvin's silence is again a datum. Critical scholarship (Crenshaw, Whybray) takes the passage at face value as an ancient text that does not yet possess the doctrine of resurrection. Seow and Murphy read it as agnostic — Qohelet does not deny life beyond death but refuses to assert it. Longman (NICOT) reads it as the epilogist's setup for 12:7's resolution ("the dust returns to the earth as it was, and the spirit returns to God who gave it"), where the canonical narrator finally answers Qohelet's "who knows?"

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: *'Olam* in the heart — what kind of capacity?** The chapter's most theologically consequential verse, 3:11, asserts that God "has put *ha-'olam* into [human hearts], yet so that they cannot find out what God has done from the beginning to the end." The interpretive options are not minor variations; they assert different anthropologies.

The classical Christian reading (Augustine through Aquinas, articulated formally in Gregory of Nyssa) treats *'olam* as *eternity* — humans have been made for an eternal end, and their hearts are restless because the temporal cannot fulfill an eternity-shaped capacity. The verse becomes, on this reading, the OT's most direct anthropological warrant for the doctrine of the *imago Dei*: humans *uniquely* possess eternity-consciousness, which is why neither pleasure (chapter 2) nor wisdom (chapter 1) nor the rhythms of time (3:1–8) can satisfy. Augustine's "our hearts are restless until they rest in thee" is the canonical Christian gloss. The reading underwrites the natural-desire-for-God argument across Catholic theology (Aquinas, Henri de Lubac) and the *sensus divinitatis* across Reformed theology (Calvin's *Institutes* 1.3).

The Jewish reading (Maimonides through Gordis) reads *'olam* less as metaphysical eternity than as the *yetzer*, the *consciousness*, the *world-orientation* God has placed in humans. The verse describes a human capacity that is real but does not require the Christian inflation. Maimonides reads it intellectualistically: humans have rational souls that can grasp the eternal forms but are bounded by embodiment. Gordis reads it phenomenologically: humans are aware of duration (past and future) and ask the *meaning of time* questions that animals do not. The verse asserts a real difference between human and animal cognition without committing to a metaphysical doctrine of the soul.

The genuine steelman for Fox's secularizing reading is that Qohelet himself, two paragraphs later, asserts that humans and beasts have the *same fate* and the *same breath*, and that "who knows" whether human spirit goes up. If 3:11 had asserted metaphysical eternity-capacity, 3:19–21 would directly contradict it. Fox's coherent Qohelet has the human capacity to be aware of duration without grasping totality — which is precisely what 3:11 says ("yet so that he cannot find out what God has done") — but he refuses to conclude that this capacity entails any *metaphysical* eternity-orientation. The capacity is real; the metaphysics is not licensed by the text.

The critical scholar's question: is the Christian reading of 3:11 a *legitimate* development of an ambiguous text, or is it an *imposition* of a Hellenistic anthropology Qohelet himself does not share? Brevard Childs's canonical reading splits the difference: Qohelet's text does not require the eternity-anthropology, but the canon does — by the time the text is read alongside Genesis 1's *imago Dei*, the prophets' eschatological future, Daniel 12's resurrection, and the New Testament's resurrection theology, 3:11 acquires a richer meaning than its lexical bare bones support. *Where this leaves us*: 3:11 is not self-interpreting; it is a verse whose meaning depends on the canonical and theological context in which it is read, and the patristic reading is a possible but not necessary development.

**Tension 2: Human and animal continuity at death (3:18–22).** Qohelet's most provocative anthropological claim: "what happens to the children of man and what happens to the beasts is the same; as one dies, so dies the other. They all have the same breath, and man has no advantage over the beasts, for all is *hevel*. All go to one place. All are from the dust, and to dust all return. Who knows whether the spirit of man goes upward and the spirit of the beast goes down into the earth?" (3:19–21).

Position one (Crenshaw, Whybray): Qohelet is denying any meaningful afterlife distinction. The "who knows?" is rhetorical, equivalent to "no one knows," and the body's *return to dust* (echoing Gen 3:19) is the whole truth. Qohelet's anthropology is materialist within the limits of ancient Israelite cosmology — *Sheol*-bound, no resurrection, no separable immortal soul. The passage is therefore in genuine tension with the developing Jewish doctrine of resurrection (Daniel 12) and with Christian resurrection theology, and the canon includes both positions without resolving them.

Position two (Gordis, Seow): Qohelet's *mi yode'a* is genuine agnosticism, not denial. He is saying that *empirical observation* yields the same fate for human and beast — both die, both return to dust — but he does not foreclose the possibility that the human's *ruach* has a different end. His skepticism is honest, not polemic. The Christian doctrine of resurrection is something Qohelet does not yet possess, but the passage is not in active conflict with it.

Position three (Longman, Provan): the passage is set up by Qohelet to be answered later. At 12:7 the book ends with "the dust returns to the earth as it was, and the *ruach* returns to God who gave it" — which closes the loop Qohelet opened in 3:21. The epilogist (or the book's overall argument) gives the answer Qohelet had refused to give. The reading depends on whether 12:7 belongs to Qohelet or to the epilogist; Longman attributes it to the epilogist and reads the canonical voice as correcting Qohelet, while Seow reads 12:7 as Qohelet's own (perhaps grudging) acknowledgment of conventional belief.

The Catholic-Orthodox reading historically allegorizes (3:18–21 describes the *body* and the *empirical* condition; the *soul* is a separate question theological reasoning addresses). The liberation-theological reading (Gustavo Gutiérrez, drawing on Eccl 4 more centrally but applicable here) emphasizes the *equality* aspect: rich and poor, oppressor and oppressed, all return to dust, and Qohelet's leveling is itself a form of justice claim. Anabaptist non-resistant readings note that 3:18–21 undercuts any human pretension to ultimacy. *Where this leaves us*: this passage cannot be read as anything other than what it is — a creaturely refusal to claim more than empirical observation supports — and any teacher of this book has to decide whether the canon's later resurrection theology *answers* Qohelet or simply *coexists* with him.

**One unresolved question.** If God has put *'olam* into the human heart "yet so that he cannot find out what God has done from the beginning to the end" (3:11), then is the unsatisfiable capacity itself a *gift* (it makes us seek God) or a *cruelty* (it makes us aware of what we cannot reach) — and does the text give us any way to decide?

## The Thread

The non-obvious cross-references run in unexpected directions. *Genesis 2:7* and *Genesis 3:19* — humans formed from the dust, returning to dust — are the explicit subtext of Eccl 3:20 ("all are from the dust, and to dust all return"); Qohelet is reading the Eden narrative against any reading that smuggles in immortality. *Psalm 49:12, 49:20* — "man in his pomp will not remain; he is like the beasts that perish" — is virtually identical to Eccl 3:19; Seow argues the Psalm is one of Qohelet's likely sources, and the parallel is so close that the Psalm must be considered part of the book's wisdom-tradition substrate. *Daniel 12:2* — "many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt" — is the Persian/Hellenistic-period text Qohelet's "who knows?" stands beside; the two texts are roughly contemporary and represent the unresolved Jewish debate about resurrection that the New Testament will eventually settle. The forward echo to *Romans 8:20–22* — "the creation was subjected to *mataiotēti* [futility]… in hope that the creation itself will be set free from its bondage to corruption and obtain the freedom of the glory of the children of God" — picks up Qohelet's *hevel* (LXX *mataiotēs*) and gives the eschatological answer Qohelet does not give. And finally *Acts 17:26* — Paul at the Areopagus: "[God] made from one man every nation… having determined allotted periods (*kairoi*) and the boundaries of their dwelling place" — reads Eccl 3:1–8's *'et* poem under a missional theology: God's appointed *kairoi* are the structure within which the gospel addresses every nation, picking up Qohelet's time-as-given but reframing it as gospel opportunity.
