# 3 John 1: A Hospitality, A Power Grab, A Network of Friends

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

3 John is the shortest book in the New Testament — fifteen verses on a single sheet of papyrus, opening with the same self-designation as 2 John: "The elder (*ho presbyteros*, ὁ πρεσβύτερος) to the beloved Gaius, whom I love in truth" (v.1). The Greek vocabulary, cadence, and theological idiom match 2 John so closely that virtually all scholars treat the two as products of the same hand or, at minimum, the same Johannine school. The traditional identification, traced through Irenaeus's *Against Heresies* 3.16.8 and Polycarp's testimony, makes the elder John the Apostle in old age, writing from Ephesus in the 90s. The critical alternative, going back to F. C. Baur and refined in Raymond Brown's *Anchor Bible* commentary, Judith Lieu's *I, II, & III John* (NTL), and Lieu's earlier monograph *The Second and Third Epistles of John: History and Background* (1986), reads the elder as an authoritative tradition-bearer of the Johannine school but not the apostle himself. Eusebius (*H.E.* 3.39) preserves Papias's distinction between **John the Apostle** and a separate figure, **John the Elder**, which has fueled the alternative reading for two centuries. Marianne Meye Thompson, I. Howard Marshall (NICNT), and John Stott (TNTC) preserve the apostolic association while acknowledging the difficulty.

The recipient is Gaius (Γάϊος, v.1) — a name so common across the Greco-Roman world that the New Testament names at least four other figures who share it: **Gaius of Macedonia** (Acts 19:29, dragged into the Ephesian theater riot alongside Aristarchus); **Gaius of Derbe** (Acts 20:4, one of Paul's traveling companions on the collection-mission to Jerusalem); **Gaius of Corinth** (Romans 16:23, "host to me and to the whole church," and probably the same as 1 Corinthians 1:14, whom Paul personally baptized). The Gaius of 3 John is most plausibly a *fifth* Gaius, a member of an Asian Johannine community whose precise identity has not survived. Origen and Eusebius both decline to harmonize the Gaiuses; the homonymity is itself a reminder of how thinly the New Testament prosopography overlaps with the actual social density of first-century Christian house-church networks.

The dramatic interest of the letter is the conflict between three figures: **Gaius**, the faithful host whose love and hospitality have been "testified before the church" (v.6); **Diotrephes** (Διοτρέφης, v.9-10), who *philoprōteuōn* (φιλοπρωτεύων, v.9 — "loves to be first," a hapax legomenon found nowhere else in the New Testament); and **Demetrius** (Δημήτριος, v.12), whom the elder commends as having "received a good testimony from everyone, and from the truth itself." Diotrephes is the *first* named individual in the Christian canon to refuse apostolic authority. The elder writes that Diotrephes "does not acknowledge our authority" (*ouk epidechetai hēmas*, οὐκ ἐπιδέχεται ἡμᾶς, v.9), spreads "wicked nonsense" (*logois ponērois phlyarōn*, λόγοις πονηροῖς φλυαρῶν) against the elder's circle, refuses to welcome the brothers, and excommunicates those who would (v.10). Whether Diotrephes is also a heretic is not stated; the elder names only behavioral grievances, not doctrinal ones. Wayne Meeks (*The First Urban Christians*) and Adolf von Harnack both read Diotrephes as a representative figure in the late-first-century shift from itinerant-charismatic authority (the *Wandercharismatiker* of Gerd Theissen's *Sociology of Early Palestinian Christianity*) to settled, local, monepiscopal authority. The elder still travels and writes; Diotrephes controls a local door. The pattern that Ignatius will codify around 110 AD in his letters to the Asian churches — the bishop as the unitary local authority around whom the church gathers — is already visible in this brief letter as a tension. Whether Diotrephes is wrong because he resists apostolic authority (the Catholic reading) or wrong because of pride and lack of charity (the Protestant reading) is itself one of the contested theological questions the chapter surfaces.

The setting is the same network of Asian house-churches that frames 2 John. Itinerant teachers travel between congregations, depending on local hosts for *xenia* (hospitality) — a *xeneon* (guest-house), a meal, an introduction onward. Karl Donfried's work on early-Christian hospitality and Margaret MacDonald and Carolyn Osiek's research on the social texture of the early house-church (in *A Woman's Place: House Churches in Earliest Christianity*) make the social stakes vivid. Welcoming a teacher was a public act of recognition; refusing to welcome was a public act of denial. Diotrephes is doing both — refusing to welcome the elder's emissaries, and excommunicating any local member who would extend the welcome.

Several Greek terms reward attention. *Agapētos* (ἀγαπητός, "beloved") is applied four times to Gaius (vv.1, 2, 5, 11) — the form of address itself signaling the warmth that makes the contrast with Diotrephes sharper. The repetition functions almost like a refrain: each time the elder turns to a new section of the letter, he opens with *agapētos*, anchoring Gaius in the love that shapes the whole missive. *Euodousthai kai hygiainein* (εὐοδοῦσθαί σε καὶ ὑγιαίνειν, v.2 — "may you be in good health and prosper") is one of the most popularly cited verses in modern prosperity-gospel preaching, but historically it is a Hellenistic-Greek epistolary commonplace, the standard "I hope you are well" formula found in countless papyrus letters from the period. Stott, Marshall, and Brown all flag the verse as a greeting convention rather than a theological promise. *Synergoi tē alētheia* (συνεργοὶ τῇ ἀληθείᾳ, v.8, "fellow workers for the truth") names the role the elder asks Gaius to step into — not just hosting, but co-laboring in the gospel mission that the visiting brothers represent. *Hyper tou onomatos* (ὑπὲρ τοῦ ὀνόματος, v.7, "for the sake of the name") is striking: the brothers travel for the sake of *the name* — Christological shorthand that does not require the name to be specified. Origen and the patristic readers consistently identified *the name* with Jesus.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

3 John is a personal letter — a *commendatio* in Hellenistic-Greek epistolary form, recommending Demetrius (v.12) and contrasting him with Diotrephes. The opening (v.1) follows the standard sender-recipient-greeting pattern, with the elder's distinctive *agapētos / en alētheia* Johannine idiom. The body unfolds as a triadic-prosopographic structure: praise of Gaius (vv.2-8), denunciation of Diotrephes (vv.9-10), commendation of Demetrius (v.11-12). The closing (vv.13-15) follows standard Hellenistic conventions — a travel notice ("I hope to see you soon," echoing 2 John 12 almost verbatim), a peace greeting, and a request to "greet the friends, each by name" (*aspazou tous philous kat' onoma*, ἀσπάζου τοὺς φίλους κατ' ὄνομα).

Wayne Meeks's prosopographic method — reading early Christian documents for the social and political alignments they encode in their named figures — is uniquely productive on 3 John. The three named figures map a complete polity: a faithful patron-host (Gaius), a local power-broker who has captured the door (Diotrephes), a commended traveling teacher whom the elder is asking Gaius to receive (Demetrius). The letter is a bid to maintain a translocal network against a local capture. Diotrephes's refusal to receive *the brothers* is both ecclesial and political — it disconnects the local congregation from the broader apostolic web.

The vocabulary of *philoi* (φίλοι, "friends," v.15) is also literarily significant: the term is rare in the Johannine epistles but common in classical Greek epistolary friendship-letters and in the Gospel of John (15:13-15, where Jesus calls the disciples *philoi*). The closing phrase "greet the friends, each by name" carries the warmth of named, specific, embodied relationships against the cold, impersonal exercise of institutional power that Diotrephes represents. The contrast is the literary heart of the letter: friendship and naming on one side, anonymous expulsion on the other.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

Patristic reception of 3 John is sparse — the letter was among the *antilegomena* (disputed) listed by Eusebius in *H.E.* 3.25, mostly because of its brevity, personal character, and limited circulation. Origen knew it. Athanasius's 39th Festal Letter (367) lists it as canonical. The Western reception was secured through Jerome's Vulgate. Augustine engaged the letter only in passing.

Medieval reception fixed on the figure of Diotrephes as a stock example of ecclesiastical pride. The schoolmen used Diotrephes (alongside Simon Magus) as a paradigm of a leader corrupted by *amor sui*. Aquinas, in the *Summa Theologiae* II-II q.162 (on pride), draws on Diotrephes to illustrate how pride manifests as the desire for preeminence (*excellentia*).

Reformation readers found Diotrephes useful in polemics against papal authority — Luther and the Reformers identified Diotrephes with Rome, while Catholic apologists countered by identifying Diotrephes with the Reformers' rejection of episcopal authority. The interpretive question was the same one the patristic readers had skirted: was Diotrephes wrong because he resisted apostolic authority, or wrong because he was a power-grabber? The first answer leans Catholic; the second leans Protestant; both have textual support.

Critical and contemporary scholarship — Brown's *Anchor Bible* on the epistles, Lieu's *I, II, & III John* (NTL) and her earlier monograph, Marshall's NICNT, Stott's TNTC, Marianne Meye Thompson's *1-3 John* — treats 3 John as the most valuable surviving glimpse of Johannine-community sociology, alongside Diotrephes-as-evidence for the rising 2nd-century monepiscopal pattern that Ignatius's letters codify around 110 AD. Adolf von Harnack's classic work on the *Wandercharismatiker* (the wandering charismatic preachers of early Christianity) and Gerd Theissen's *Sociology of Early Palestinian Christianity* both read 3 John as documenting the structural tension between traveling charismatic authority and settled local authority — a tension that Christianity would resolve, eventually, in favor of the local bishop.

### Jewish Scholarly Engagement

Jewish-Christian dialogue scholarship has paid little attention to 3 John, since the polemic is intra-Christian and the figures are not connected to synagogue or Temple controversies. But Daniel Boyarin's broader work on early Christian-Jewish boundary formation is suggestive: the kind of network-of-friends sociology 3 John encodes — translocal, hospitality-driven, named-friend-by-named-friend — looks more like the early-rabbinic *chavurah* network than like the eventual monepiscopal Catholic structure. Both Christianity and rabbinic Judaism in the late-first and early-second centuries faced the same structural problem: how does a translocal religious movement coordinate authority across distance after the loss of a single central institution (the Temple, or — for the Christians — the apostolic generation)? 3 John shows one early Christian answer (the elder writing letters and sending emissaries through trusted hosts); Diotrephes represents a different, more institutional answer that would eventually win.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

God's purpose, as the elder configures it, is the formation of a church-network ordered through hospitality, faithful witness, and recognition of those who serve the gospel — and the protection of that network against the local capture of authority by figures whose love of preeminence dissolves the bonds of love. The chapter is the first canonical naming of a *power-grabbing local leader* and a foundational text for Christian self-critique on ecclesial pride.

### Theological tensions

**Diotrephes and ecclesiastical authority.** The chapter has been a battleground for centuries. The Catholic apostolic-succession reading argues Diotrephes is wrong precisely because he resists the apostolic authority of the elder — and that the verse vindicates the principle that local churches must remain in communion with apostolic teachers and their successors. The Protestant congregational reading observes that the elder appeals to no formal office; he writes as a charismatic-apostolic figure but does not claim a juridical right Diotrephes is bound to honor. On this reading, Diotrephes is wrong because of pride and lack of charity, not because he opposes apostolic authority *as such*. The Anglican via media holds the two readings together: apostolic authority matters, but so do the relational virtues, and Diotrephes fails on both. The rising 2nd-century monepiscopal pattern that Ignatius defends in his letters to Smyrna, Magnesia, and Tralles overshadows the conversation in retrospect — Ignatius would have written a very different letter to Diotrephes than the elder did, asserting episcopal authority where the elder appeals to friendship and witness.

**Prosperity gospel reading of v.2.** "Beloved, I pray that all may go well with you and that you may be in good health, as it goes well with your soul." Kenneth Hagin, Joel Osteen, and the broader Word-of-Faith movement have used this verse as a foundational proof-text for the claim that material prosperity and physical health are God's covenant promise to believers. The mainstream evangelical correction — articulated by Stott in TNTC and Marshall in NICNT, and echoed by virtually every academic commentary in print — observes that the verse is a Hellenistic letter-greeting commonplace, structurally identical to the *valetudo* and *prosperitas* wishes that open thousands of surviving papyrus letters from the period. To turn the verse into a theological promise is to confuse a greeting convention with revealed doctrine. Catholic and mainline Protestant readings reject the prosperity gospel framing on the same grounds. The verse is warm; it is not a theology of wealth.

**Closing question.** When apostolic authority has receded into letters and emissaries, and local leaders have begun to control the doors of the local church, on what does the church's coherence finally rest — on office, or on the named, particular friendships that "greet the friends, each by name" (v.15)? The elder writes as if the answer is friendship and witness. Two centuries later, the answer institutional Christianity gave was the bishop. The chapter does not adjudicate; it shows the moment before the choice was made. What it leaves visible is that whatever institutional answer the church eventually settles on, the ground beneath the institution is and remains the named-friend network the elder describes — and an institution that loses that ground has lost the thing it was built to protect.

## The Thread

Read 3 John alongside Acts 19-20 (the Asian-mission context Gaius and Demetrius emerge from — Paul's farewell to the Ephesian elders in Acts 20 takes place in the same provincial geography the elder is writing into); Ignatius of Antioch's *To the Smyrnaeans* (the early-2nd-century monepiscopal turn that Diotrephes anticipates and that Ignatius's letters codify around 110 AD); and 1 Timothy 5:17 (the *honor for elders* tradition — "Let the elders who rule well be considered worthy of double honor" — which gives the parallel Pauline framing of how local leadership is to be received). Together these texts triangulate the moment when traveling apostolic authority began to give way to settled local authority, and they let the reader see what was gained, what was lost, and what was at stake when the door of the local church became a door someone — Gaius, Diotrephes, the friends, the bishop — had to decide whether to open.
