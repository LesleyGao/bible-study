# Daniel 2: The Statue, the Stone, and the Four-Kingdoms Schema

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter is set "in the second year of Nebuchadnezzar" (v. 1) — a famous chronological knot, since chapter 1 has Daniel completing a three-year training first. Conservative interpreters (Edwin Thiele's accession-year reckoning, defended by **Stephen Miller** [NAC], **Joyce Baldwin** [TOTC, 1978], **John Whitcomb**) treat the second year as Nebuchadnezzar's second full regnal year, after a roughly nine-month accession year, which can be made to dovetail with chapter 1's three-year training. **John J. Collins** (Hermeneia, 1993) and **Carol Newsom** (OTL, 2014) read the seam as evidence of compositional layering: the court tales in chapters 1–6 do not all originate as a single sequence and the redactor has not smoothed every chronological edge.

Babylonian dream interpretation was a specialized, technical, and respected profession. The cuneiform manual *Zaqiqu* (also transliterated *Ziqīqu*) is a substantial dream-omen series organized by content of the dream and standard apotropaic responses; the *ummânu* — the scholarly courtiers translated as "wise men," "magicians," "enchanters," "Chaldeans" — were the bearers of that tradition, and the four-fold list in v. 2 (Hebrew *hartummim*, *ashshafim*, *mekhashshefim*, *kasdim*) tracks roughly onto known Mesopotamian divinatory specialties: dream-experts, conjurers, sorcerers, and the astronomical-astrological priesthood whose late-Achaemenid and Hellenistic activity is attested in the cuneiform astronomical diaries from Babylon. The royal demand that the experts produce the dream itself, not merely interpret a dream the king reports, is unusual but not unparalleled in the literature of "the king tests his courtiers" — comparable patterns appear in the Aḥiqar story and in Egyptian Demotic narratives. The threat of dismemberment and house-razing (v. 5) is consonant with attested Neo-Babylonian and Persian punitive conventions; the language is hyperbolic but not invented.

Two philological features carry the whole dating debate on their backs. **The Aramaic block begins at 2:4b**: "Then the Chaldeans said to the king *in Aramaic*…" The Masoretic text shifts language at this verse and continues in Imperial Aramaic until the close of chapter 7. The bilingual switch is itself a literary signal — a marker that the wisdom of the gentile court is conducted in the lingua franca of the empire — but it is also a source-critical seam: chapters 1 and 8–12 are Hebrew. Critical scholars from S.R. Driver (1891) through Collins read the Aramaic of Daniel as Imperial Aramaic with later (Eastern, possibly Hasmonean-period) features; conservatives counter that Imperial Aramaic was stable for centuries and that an exilic Daniel could plausibly write it. The second pivot is *raz* (רָז, Aramaic "mystery"), an Old Persian loanword (*raza*) that recurs nine times in chapter 2 (vv. 18, 19, 27, 28, 29, 30, 47) and supplies the chapter's key theological term. It is the same word Qumran's sectarian literature (1QpHab, 1QS, 4QInstruction) uses for the eschatological "mystery to come," *raz nihyeh*; and **Collins** has tracked the trajectory from Daniel 2 through Qumran into Paul's *mystērion* in Ephesians 3:3 and Romans 16:25. The chapter is the conceptual headwater of an entire vocabulary of New Testament apocalyptic.

Authorship and dating, addressed at length in the introduction to chapter 1, presses harder here than anywhere else in chapters 1–6. The four-kingdoms schema is the load-bearing apocalyptic framework of the whole book; if chapter 11 is *vaticinium ex eventu* (the case made by **Porphyry of Tyre** in the third century AD, preserved and contested by **Jerome's *Commentary on Daniel*** of 407, and developed by Bertholdt, Driver, **R.H. Charles**, **James Montgomery** [ICC, 1927], **H.H. Rowley**, Collins, Newsom, **Maurice Casey**, **Lester Grabbe**, **Philip Davies**, **Norman Porteous**), then the Stone of chapter 2 was originally a Maccabean expectation — the Hasmonean restoration in 164 BC — that subsequent reading rebound to the messianic age and, for Christians, to Christ. If chapter 11 is genuine prophecy (**Stephen Miller**, **Gleason Archer**, Whitcomb, Baldwin), then the Stone was always pointing further than Maccabees, and the four-kingdom sequence runs through Rome.

Archaeology supplies one external corroboration. The **Babylonian Chronicle** (BM 21946, the "Jerusalem Chronicle" published by D.J. Wiseman, 1956) places Nebuchadnezzar at Jerusalem in 605 and 597; **Berossus** (preserved in Josephus, *Against Apion* 1.19) confirms his rebuilding program at Babylon. The **Nabonidus Chronicle** and the **Cyrus Cylinder** (BM 90920, 539 BC) describe the fall of Babylon to Cyrus the Persian — the transition from the head of gold to the chest of silver in the patristic-Christian reading. **Berossus** also preserves the four-empire schema in a different key (Babylon-Mede-Persian-Macedonian); whether Daniel inherits the schema or reworks it independently is debated.

Key terms in the chapter: *raz* (רָז, "mystery"); *peshar* (פְּשַׁר, Aramaic "interpretation," cognate with the Qumran *pesher* genre); *malkut* (מַלְכוּת, "kingdom" — the leitwort of the entire book); *aharit yomayya* (אַחֲרִית יוֹמַיָּא, "the latter days," v. 28 — the Aramaic form of the Hebrew *aharit hayyamim*).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is a court tale of the Joseph type lifted into apocalyptic register. The opening crisis (a troubled dream) and the courtier-test pattern come straight from Genesis 41; Daniel's prayer and his confession that the interpretation belongs to God (v. 28) parallel Joseph's "It is not in me; God will give Pharaoh a favorable answer" (Gen 41:16). The chapter's literary innovation is the dream-content itself: the four-metal statue, struck by a stone "cut out by no human hand," ground to chaff, and replaced by a mountain that fills the earth. This is the first sustained apocalyptic image in the Hebrew Bible, and it inaugurates the four-kingdoms schema that recurs in chapter 7 and reverberates through Second Temple literature (1 Enoch's Animal Apocalypse, 4 Ezra 11–12, 2 Baruch 39–40, Sibylline Oracles 4) and into the New Testament book of Revelation.

The Aramaic block (2:4b–7:28) is famously chiastic — A. Lenglet (1972) mapped the structure and Collins, Newsom, and **Joyce Baldwin** all use it: chapters 2 and 7 are paired (four-kingdoms vision); 3 and 6 are paired (faithful resistance, deliverance from a death threat); 4 and 5 are paired (humbled kings). Chapter 2 is therefore the literary anchor of the chiasm. Within the chapter, the structure is itself layered: prose narrative frame (vv. 1–13 and 24–49) bracketing Daniel's poetic prayer (vv. 20–23, an extraordinary wisdom-doxology), bracketing the dream-recital (vv. 31–35) and its interpretation (vv. 36–45). The mid-chapter prayer is the chapter's theological keystone: it confesses that wisdom and might belong to God, that he changes times and seasons, that he removes and sets up kings — an explicit relativization of imperial sovereignty that prefigures the dream's interpretation.

The leitworter are *raz* (mystery — nine occurrences) and *galah* in its Aramaic form ("reveal"). The motif of the *stone not made by human hand* (*even di-la bidayin*, v. 34) is one of the most-cited images in subsequent biblical literature: it links to Isaiah 28:16 (the cornerstone in Zion), Psalm 118:22 (the rejected stone become the chief cornerstone), and forward to 1 Corinthians 10:4 ("the rock was Christ"), 1 Peter 2:7, and Mark 12:10. Its negative formulation — *not* by human hand — is the chapter's quiet political-theological claim: the kingdom that will replace the empires is not generated by imperial agency.

Canonical placement: Daniel sits in the *Ketuvim* (Writings) in the Hebrew Bible, between Esther and Ezra-Nehemiah; the LXX moved it to the prophets, and Christian Bibles have followed. The textual variants matter here too. The Old Greek (LXX) and Theodotion recensions of chapter 2 differ in some detail; the Old Greek is shorter at points and longer at others, and the Greek versions also include the **Greek Additions to Daniel** in chapter 3, removed by Jerome. Eight Daniel manuscripts at Qumran (4QDan-a through e, 1QDan-a, 6QDan) and the parallel **Prayer of Nabonidus** (4QPrNab, c. 100 BC) constitute the textual evidence base.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In canonical perspective, the chapter is the engine room of biblical apocalyptic. Babylon → another inferior kingdom → a third, of bronze → a fourth, of iron → the kingdom of God: the schema is condensed, ambiguous, and infinitely re-applicable. Three identifications of the four kingdoms have dominated.

The **patristic-Christian** identification, set by **Hippolytus of Rome's *Commentary on Daniel*** (c. AD 200, the earliest extant Christian biblical commentary), reads: Babylon → Medo-Persia (a single empire, since the Medes were absorbed into the Persian Empire under Cyrus) → Greece (Alexander and the Diadochi) → Rome. The Stone is Christ; his first advent is the kingdom that strikes and grinds the empires. The mountain that fills the earth is the Church; Rome continues, in some readings, to the end of history. **Jerome** consolidates this reading in his commentary of 407 against Porphyry. **Theodoret of Cyrrhus** continues it. **Calvin's *Lectures on Daniel*** (1561) defends it, with the Stone-as-Christ identification but a more cautious approach to the mountain's identification with any visible institution. Premillennial dispensationalist readings (Scofield, John Walvoord, *Daniel: The Key to Prophetic Revelation*) preserve the Babylon-Persia-Greece-Rome schema but push the Stone to Christ's *second* advent, taking the feet of iron and clay as a revived (or fragmented) Roman empire still future.

The **modern critical** identification, articulated by Bertholdt, S.R. Driver, **R.H. Charles**, Montgomery, Rowley, and now standard from Collins, Newsom, Casey, Grabbe, Davies, and Porteous, reads: Babylon → Media (treated as a separate empire, since Daniel 5:31 introduces "Darius the Mede" as ruler after Belshazzar) → Persia → Greece. The fourth empire is Greece, and the climax is the divided Diadochi (the iron-and-clay feet) and Antiochus IV Epiphanes (the eleventh horn of chapter 7). The Stone is the Maccabean restoration of 164 BC, or, in a more theological reading, the apocalyptic kingdom of God as imagined by the Hasidic *maskilim*. Two arguments anchor this reading: the textual presence of "Darius the Mede" as a Median ruler distinct from Cyrus (a figure not attested historically and best explained as the redactor's preservation of the four-kingdom schema he inherited); and the precise historical fit between chapter 11's narrative and Antiochus IV's career through 165 BC. **Steelman**: the critical reading is not merely incredulity about prophecy. It is the attempt to take seriously the book's own concentration of attention on the Hellenistic crisis (chapters 8, 9, 10–12 all focus on it) and on the figure who in chapter 11 is described in detail unmatched anywhere else in apocalyptic literature.

The **Jewish post-Temple** identification, traced through **Saadia Gaon** (10th c., the first major Jewish Daniel commentary), **Rashi**, **Ibn Ezra**, and **Abrabanel** (15th c.), preserves the Babylon-Persia-Greece-Rome schema but reads Rome as continuing through Christendom (and into Islamic and modern empires by typological extension); the Stone is the future messianic kingdom. **Daniel Boyarin's** reading in *The Jewish Gospels* argues that the four-kingdoms apocalyptic schema, including the Son-of-Man figure of chapter 7, is a pre-Christian Jewish messianic tradition that the gospels inherit rather than invent — a claim that has reframed the Christian-Jewish conversation about Daniel in the last twenty years. Modern Jewish scholars (Robert Gordis, Aaron Koller, Louis Finkelstein on the *maskilim*) now treat Daniel as resistance literature against Antiochus and read the Stone as the eschatological-messianic horizon of which the Maccabean victory was at most a foretaste.

Where the trajectories converge: Daniel 2 establishes the schema, supplies the imagery, and forces every subsequent reader to identify the kingdoms and the Stone. Where they diverge: on the identity of the four, and consequently on the nature, timing, and identity of the Stone. This divergence is the single most consequential interpretive decision in the book.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The identity of the four kingdoms.** The pre-critical Christian reading (Hippolytus, Jerome, Calvin) takes Babylon-Persia-Greece-Rome with Christ as the Stone of his first advent. Catholic teaching, articulated through Aquinas's commentary tradition and confirmed in modern magisterial readings (the Pontifical Biblical Commission's 1933 *Decretum* on Daniel — since substantially revised — and the more nuanced *Dei Verbum* approach of Vatican II), has retained this reading while permitting historical-critical engagement. Eastern Orthodox patristic exegesis (Theodoret, Ephrem the Syrian) preserves it. Magisterial Reformation Protestants — Luther's *Preface* (1530), Calvin (1561) — sit with it. The premillennial dispensational reading reframes the Stone as Christ's second advent and reads the iron-and-clay feet as a still-future revived Roman alliance; the eschatology is reorganized but the schema is retained.

The critical reading (Babylon-Media-Persia-Greece, with Antiochus IV and the Maccabean restoration as the climactic context) reads Daniel 2 as Hellenistic-period apocalyptic encouragement-literature for Jews under Seleucid persecution. **Steelman**: read in this register, the chapter is not less theologically important — it becomes the foundational testimony that the empires fall and that the kingdom of God is *not* the project of any imperial state. The Maccabean reading, far from deflating the chapter, gives it a sharp political edge that is sometimes lost in the Christian Christological reading. The Jewish post-Temple reading (Babylon-Persia-Greece-Rome, with the Stone still future) keeps the messianic edge but reads the Christian identification of the Stone with Jesus as a category mistake — the Stone "fills the whole earth" and "stands forever," and Rome did not end with Christ's first advent.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter has been read three significantly different ways for two thousand years, and the readings have shaped Christology, eschatology, and political theology along divergent paths. A reader who imagines they can simply *settle* the question is overconfident; what is available is a clearer view of what is at stake in each reading and a more honest account of what the chapter alone can and cannot adjudicate.

**Tension 2: The Stone "not made by human hands" — what kind of kingdom is this?** **Calvin's amillennial reading** is the most theologically integrated of the major Christian options: the Stone is Christ's first advent, the kingdom is *already* present in his church, the mountain that fills the earth is the church's gradual eschatological growth, and the iron-and-clay feet are read more as the unstable Hellenistic-Roman situation into which Christ came than as a still-future political alignment. **Premillennial dispensationalism** (Walvoord, *Daniel*, 1971; J. Dwight Pentecost) reads the Stone as Christ's second advent, the kingdom as a still-future political-theological reality inaugurated by the parousia, and the feet as the prophetic future. The **Catholic ecclesial reading** (Aquinas, contemporary magisterium) reads the Stone as Christ and the mountain as the church through history, with significant continuity with Calvin but stronger institutional identification. The **Anabaptist reading** (Yoder, *Politics of Jesus*) shares the Christological identification of the Stone but resists any identification of the mountain with imperial Christendom — the kingdom is *visible* in the gathered church only, not in any state-religious arrangement. **Jewish messianic readings** (Saadia, Abrabanel, modern Orthodox) hold the Stone is still future. **The critical reading** (Collins, Newsom, Davies) treats the Stone as the Hasidic-Maccabean expectation of God's eschatological kingdom against the empires, applied first to Hasmonean restoration and subsequently rebound to Christian and Jewish messianic horizons.

The line that nearly all these readings agree on is the chapter's negative claim: the kingdom is *not* made by human hands. Whatever else is contested, the chapter denies that the eschatological kingdom is the project of imperial action, including imperial-religious action. **N.T. Wright** has noted (in *Jesus and the Victory of God* and elsewhere) that Mark 12:10 — Jesus quoting Psalm 118 about the rejected stone — is part of a deliberate identification of Jesus with the Daniel 2 Stone; the gospels read the schema as climactically realized in the cross and resurrection. Whether one accepts that identification or holds the kingdom to be still future, the chapter's ascription of agency to God against the empires is the load-bearing claim.

**Where this leaves us:** the four readings have very different politics. The amillennial-Catholic reading tends toward institutional patience; the dispensational reading toward eschatological withdrawal; the Anabaptist reading toward separatist witness; the Jewish messianic reading toward this-worldly hope held in tension with patient endurance; the critical reading toward seeing apocalyptic as the literature of the powerless against the empires. Each reading does work the others do not. None is simply right.

**One unresolved question.** If the chapter denies that the kingdom is made by human hands — including imperial-religious hands — what does that mean for any project, ancient or modern, that proposes to *build* the kingdom by political, ecclesial, or technological means?

## The Thread

The Joseph parallel of Genesis 41 is the most direct: Pharaoh's troubled dream, the failure of the Egyptian *hartummim*, the Hebrew interpreter, the elevation to second-in-the-realm. Daniel 2 is structurally identical and theologically expanded.

Forward to the New Testament, three lines run from the Stone. First, **1 Corinthians 10:4**: "the rock was Christ" — Paul's typological identification, drawing on the wilderness-rock tradition (Ex 17, Num 20) and the Daniel 2 image. Second, **1 Peter 2:7** quoting Psalm 118:22: "the stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone" — the rejected-stone becomes the chief stone, layered on top of the Daniel 2 Stone-not-made-by-hands. Third, **Mark 12:10–11** in Jesus's parable of the wicked tenants: Jesus identifies himself as the rejected stone, fusing the Daniel and Psalm 118 streams. The conceptual line from the *raz* of Daniel 2 to Paul's *mystērion* (Eph 3:3, Rom 16:25, 1 Cor 2:7, Col 1:26) — the eschatological mystery now revealed in Christ — is a fourth thread that runs through Qumran's *raz nihyeh* and emerges in Paul's letters as a fully formed apocalyptic theological category.
