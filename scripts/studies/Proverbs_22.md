# Proverbs 22 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World — A Structural Hinge and the Egyptian Connection

Proverbs 22 is the most important structural seam in the entire book. Verses 1–16 close out the **Great Solomonic Sentence-Proverb Collection** (Pr 10:1–22:16) — over three hundred two-line sayings that form the bulk of the canonical book. Then at **22:17** a new collection begins: *"Incline your ear, and hear the words of the wise (divrê chakamim, דִּבְרֵי חֲכָמִים), and apply your heart to my knowledge."* This superscription signals a return to the longer **instruction** form (the *musar*, מוּסָר, of chs. 1–9) and runs through 24:22, where another short collection ("These also are sayings of the wise," 24:23) follows.

The chapter is therefore a **two-genre artifact**: sentence-proverbs (1–16) and the opening of an instructional discourse (17–29). Bruce Waltke and Michael V. Fox both treat 22:17 as the most consequential editorial seam in Proverbs after 1:1.

**The Egyptian connection — *The Instruction of Amenemope*.** This is the single most discussed comparative-literature finding in Old Testament wisdom scholarship. The *Instruction of Amenemope* is an Egyptian sebayit text, traditionally dated 12th–11th c. BC (some scholars push it later, into the 7th c.), that presents thirty chapters of fatherly instruction from one Amenemope, an agricultural overseer, to his son Hor-em-maakher. The text was acquired by E.A. Wallis Budge for the British Museum in 1888 and **fully published in 1923** (Budge, *Facsimiles of Egyptian Hieratic Papyri, Second Series*); the parallels to Proverbs 22:17–24:22 became immediately apparent. Adolf Erman's 1924 article *"Eine ägyptische Quelle der 'Sprüche Salomos'"* ("An Egyptian Source of the 'Proverbs of Solomon'") set off a century of scholarly debate.

The parallels are dense and unmistakable:
- **Pr 22:17–18** (*"Incline your ear, and hear the words of the wise… for it will be pleasant if you keep them within you"*) ≈ Amenemope ch. 1 (*"Give your ears, hear what is said… it is profitable to put them in your heart"*).
- **Pr 22:20** — *"Have I not written for you **thirty** (Hebrew kethiv שִׁלְשׁוֹם / qere שָׁלִישִׁים, *sheloshim*) **sayings** of counsel and knowledge"* — corresponds to the **thirty chapters** of Amenemope. The Hebrew is famously difficult; older translations rendered "excellent things" (KJV) or "thrice" (older readings of *shilshom*); the Amenemope discovery vindicated the reading *sheloshim* ("thirty"), now standard in modern critical translations.
- **Pr 22:22–23** (*"Do not rob the poor… for the LORD will plead their cause"*) ≈ Amenemope ch. 2 (*"Beware of robbing the wretched… he whose property is taken by violent hands… the moon-god marks his crime"*).
- **Pr 22:24–25** (*"Make no friendship with a man given to anger"*) ≈ Amenemope ch. 9 (*"Do not associate to thyself the heated man, nor visit him for conversation"*).
- **Pr 22:28** (*"Do not move the ancient landmark"*) ≈ Amenemope ch. 6 (*"Do not carry off the landmark at the boundaries of the arable land… do not encroach upon the boundaries of a widow"*).
- **Pr 22:29** (*"Do you see a man skillful in his work? He will stand before kings"*) ≈ Amenemope ch. 30 (*"As for a scribe experienced in his office, he will find himself worthy of being a courtier"*).

**Mainstream-critical position** (Fox, Murphy, Brown, Yoder, Kynes): Proverbs 22:17–23:11 either directly borrowed from Amenemope or both texts drew from a shared ANE wisdom-stock. The verbal parallels, the matching "thirty" structure, and the order of topics make the literary relationship undeniable. **Conservative-evangelical responses** divide: Waltke (NICOT) accepts the parallels as evidence that Israel's sages engaged with broader ANE wisdom; some older defenders argued Amenemope borrowed from Proverbs (Kevin J. Cathcart, John Ruffle), but this position is now a minority view because Amenemope's text-form, vocabulary, and Egyptian cultural-religious context appear to be original to Egypt.

**How this affects inspiration.** The Amenemope parallels do not embarrass a serious doctrine of Scripture — they **clarify** it. The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* (§107–108) teaches inerrancy in what God intends to communicate for the sake of salvation; Reformed doctrines of inspiration (Westminster, *sola scriptura*) likewise affirm that inspiration uses the human author's full cultural-linguistic toolkit. If Israel's sages absorbed and reframed Egyptian instruction, they were doing what Israel's poets did with Canaanite cosmology (Ps 29 ≈ Baal cycle), what Israel's prophets did with treaty-form (Hosea, Isaiah), what Paul did with Stoic moralism (Acts 17), and what John did with Hellenistic logos-philosophy. The reframing is decisive: where Amenemope speaks of *Re*, *Thoth*, the *moon-god*, and the abstract cosmic order *ma'at*, Proverbs names YHWH as the moral guarantor (Pr 22:19, 22:23). The Egyptian wisdom is taken up, baptized, and reordered around the covenant God.

## Lens 2: The Literature — Two Forms in One Chapter

**Verses 1–16: sentence-proverbs.** Like ch. 21, antithetic and synthetic two-line *meshalim*, semantically self-contained. The famous **22:1** — *"A good name (shem, שֵׁם) is to be chosen rather than great riches, and favor (chen, חֵן) is better than silver or gold"* — uses *shem* in its rich Hebrew sense: not merely reputation but the publicly known character that defines a person before God and community.

**22:6** — *"Train up (chanok, חֲנֹךְ) a child in the way he should go (al-pi darko, עַל־פִּי דַרְכּוֹ); even when he is old he will not depart from it."* This verse sits at the center of one of the chapter's hardest interpretive cruxes (treated below).

**Verses 17–21: superscription / prologue to the *Sayings of the Wise*.** The form shifts to first-person address ("I have made them known to you today, even to you"), longer stichs, and motivational language ("that your trust may be in the LORD"). This is *musar* — instruction from teacher to student — formally identical to Egyptian *sebayit* and to Pr 1:8–9:18.

**Verses 22–29: the first six of the "thirty sayings."** Most scholars count thirty discrete units across 22:17–24:22 (Fox lists them with chapter-by-chapter Amenemope correspondences). The form is **prohibition/admonition + motivation**: "Do not X… for / lest Y" (cf. 22:22–23, 22:24–25, 22:26–27, 22:28).

**Key Hebrew terms:**
- **דִּבְרֵי חֲכָמִים** (*divrê chakamim*, "words of the wise") — 22:17. The collection's title; the same phrase reappears at 24:23.
- **שָׁלִישִׁים / שְׁלֹשִׁים** (*shalishim / sheloshim*, "thirty") — 22:20. The *qere* reading, vindicated by Amenemope.
- **גְּבוּל עוֹלָם** (*gevul 'olam*, "ancient landmark/boundary") — 22:28. A boundary stone marking inherited land. To move it was a capital-level offense in ANE law (Deut 19:14, 27:17), and Amenemope's parallel (ch. 6) makes the same point: God protects the inheritance of the widow and orphan against land-grabbers.
- **מָהִיר בִּמְלַאכְתּוֹ** (*mahir bimla'kto*, "skillful in his work") — 22:29. *Mahir* connotes both speed and competence; Ezra 7:6 uses the same word for Ezra "the skillful scribe."

**Parallelism** in vv. 17–29 is more flowing than in chs. 10–22:16 — the instruction-form allows three- and four-line units, motivation clauses, and rhetorical questions ("Do you see a man skillful in his work?", 22:29).

## Lens 3: The Larger Story — Wisdom Canon, Jewish Trajectory, Christian Trajectory

**Within the wisdom canon**, Pr 22:17–24:22 is the clearest evidence that Israel's wisdom literature participated in an **international wisdom guild**. Ben Sira (Sirach) explicitly travels to learn from foreign sages (Sir 39:4). Job's friends are Edomites and Arabians; the Queen of Sheba comes to test Solomon's wisdom (1 Kgs 10). Wisdom in Israel is universalizing — what is true and good is true and good wherever found — even as it is anchored in *yir'at YHWH* (יִרְאַת יהוה, "fear of the LORD"). The Amenemope-Proverbs relationship is a window into how this worked in practice.

**Jewish trajectory.** Rabbinic tradition treats 22:6 as a foundational text on Jewish education — the obligation to instruct children in Torah from the earliest age (Mishnah *Avot* 5:21 lays out the curriculum by age). Pr 22:1's *shem tov* ("a good name") becomes a major rabbinic value: *Pirkei Avot* 4:13 lists three crowns (Torah, priesthood, kingship) and adds that "the crown of a good name surpasses them all." 22:9's *tov-'ayin* (literally "good of eye," generous) and 22:16's prohibition against oppressing the poor reinforce *tsedaqah* obligations central to rabbinic ethics. The medieval commentator Rashi reads 22:28 (the ancient landmark) as both literal-property law and metaphor for received halakhic tradition — moving the *gevul 'olam* means innovating against the received teaching of the fathers.

**Christian trajectory.** Pr 22:6 became the dominant text of **Christian parenting theology**, especially in evangelical circles, often read as a near-promise: train them young and they will not depart. James Dobson's *Dare to Discipline*, Tedd Tripp's *Shepherding a Child's Heart*, and the broader American evangelical parenting literature all anchor in 22:6. Pr 22:7's *"the borrower is the slave of the lender"* became the motto of Dave Ramsey's debt-elimination teaching and a cornerstone of Christian financial counsel.

**NT use.** Pr 22 is not directly quoted in the NT, but its themes are pervasive:
- Pr 22:8 (*"whoever sows injustice will reap calamity"*) anticipates Gal 6:7 (*"whatever one sows, that will he also reap"*).
- Pr 22:9 (*"whoever has a bountiful eye"*) is echoed in Mt 6:22–23's *"the eye is the lamp of the body… if your eye is **good** (haplous, generous)"* — the Greek *haplous* matching Hebrew *tov-'ayin* in cultural connotation.
- Pr 22:22–23 (do not rob the poor) shapes James 5:1–6's prophetic warning to the rich.
- Pr 22:29 (the skillful man before kings) prefigures 1 Tim 4:14–15's "let your progress be evident to all."

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent — Tensions, Positions, Steelman

Two tensions in Pr 22 deserve honest reckoning.

**Tension 1: OT-ANE wisdom borrowing and the doctrine of inspiration.** The Amenemope evidence is the most dramatic case of cross-cultural literary transmission in the OT. How should we hold it?

- *Mainstream-critical position.* The literary dependence is clear: Proverbs 22:17–23:11 borrows from, adapts, and re-religions Egyptian *sebayit*. This is a feature, not a bug, of how ancient texts were composed. Steelman: the evidence is overwhelming — the verbal parallels, the thirty-saying structure, the order of topics — and any account that ignores it is not honest with the data.
- *Conservative-evangelical accommodation* (Waltke, Longman, Kidner). Inspiration uses human cultural materials. Just as Solomon could "speak of trees, from the cedar to the hyssop" (1 Kgs 4:33) and write down what was true wherever he found it, Israel's sages absorbed Egyptian wisdom, tested it against the fear of YHWH, and reframed it under covenant. The doctrine of inspiration does not require *creatio ex nihilo* of every sentence; it requires that what is written is what God intends his people to know. Steelman: the *Catechism* (§107–108) and the *Westminster Confession* (I.4–5) both leave ample room for inspired use of pre-existing materials; nothing about Amenemope undermines either.
- *Older defensive position* — Amenemope borrowed from Proverbs. Steelman: this preserved a tidy chronology if Solomonic dating of all of Pr 22:17–24:22 was assumed. But the textual, cultural, and religious indicators in Amenemope are independently Egyptian; the position is now untenable.

**Where this leaves us:** The Amenemope parallels should make us *more* confident in the doctrine of inspiration, not less. They show inspiration as the actual historical phenomenon it was — a community of sages, embedded in an international wisdom culture, listening to truth wherever they could find it, and ordering everything under the fear of YHWH. The parallels also expose the boundary that mattered: where Amenemope grounds the moral order in *ma'at* and the gods of Egypt, Proverbs explicitly redirects: *"that your trust may be in the LORD"* (22:19). Same thirty sayings; different theological center. Inspiration is the redirection.

**Tension 2: "Train up a child" (22:6) — the parenting promise problem.** The traditional reading takes 22:6 as a near-promise: instruct your children in the right way and they will not depart from it as adults. But the Hebrew is ambiguous, and the proverb is a *mashal* (a generalization), not a guarantee.

- *Traditional-promise reading.* "Train up (chanok) a child in the way he should go" — the way of wisdom, the way of YHWH; even when old, he will not depart. This reading underwrites a great deal of Christian parenting confidence.
- *Mainstream-critical "bend-the-bow" reading.* The Hebrew *al-pi darko* literally reads "**according to his way**" — meaning his individual bent, temperament, or developmental stage. The proverb may be saying: train each child according to their own nature, so that the training takes; or it may be a darker observation: indulge a child in *his* way (whatever way he wants to go), and he will be locked into it as an adult. *Chanok* itself can mean either "dedicate/initiate" or "indulge"; the Egyptian-instruction parallels and the broader proverb-corpus do not disambiguate.
- *Genre-honesty reading* (Longman, Fox, Murphy). Whatever the precise translation, 22:6 is a proverb, not a promise. Proverbs work by stating tendencies — "this is generally how things go" — not iron-clad guarantees. The book itself elsewhere acknowledges that fools come from wise households (Pr 17:21, 17:25) and the wicked sometimes prosper (Pr 24:19–20). Christian parents who have lost children to addiction, unbelief, or rebellion have often been wounded twice — once by the loss, once by 22:6 used as accusation.

**Where this leaves us:** 22:6 is a proverb urging serious early instruction, not a contract. Whether the Hebrew points to "the right way" or "his own way," the chapter as a whole insists that **God is the moral guarantor**, not parental technique. The honest reading — supported by the broader genre and the rest of Proverbs — refuses to use 22:6 to shame parents whose children have walked away. The proverb tells us what to do; only God knows what will come of it.

**Closing question:** What would change if you read the wisdom of your own life — your habits of speech, your friendships, your handling of money — as a translation and reordering of the wisdom of the cultures around you, judged at last by the fear of the LORD? Where does Amenemope sneak in undetected, and where has the redirection happened?
