# 1 Chronicles 22 — David's Temple Preparations and the Disqualification of Blood

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 22 has no parallel in Samuel-Kings. It is *Sondergut* — material unique to the Chronicler — and the Chronicler's distinctive presence here is unmistakable. Sara Japhet (*I & II Chronicles: A Commentary*, 1993) reads chapters 22, 28, and 29 as a literarily integrated unit in which David is recast as the *initiator* of the temple project, while Solomon is reduced to the *executor*. Hugh Williamson (*1 and 2 Chronicles*, NCB, 1982) and Ralph Klein (*1 Chronicles*, Hermeneia, 2006) both note that this Davidic foregrounding has no equivalent in the Deuteronomistic History: in 1 Kings 5–7, Solomon does the planning, the procurement, and the building. The Chronicler shifts the center of gravity backward in time.

Why? Gary Knoppers (*1 Chronicles 10–29*, Anchor Yale Bible, 2004) argues that the Chronicler is theologically motivated to make David the *architect* and Solomon the *builder* — a pairing analogous to Moses and Joshua. Just as Moses prepared the law and the tabernacle pattern but Joshua led the conquest, David prepares the temple plans and materials but Solomon builds. This pairing strengthens the Davidic foundation while preserving Solomon's executive role. Roddy Braun (*1 Chronicles*, Word, 1986) calls this the Chronicler's "David-Solomon succession theology" and connects it to the post-exilic community's need for clear lines of legitimate religious authority. Mark Boda (*1-2 Chronicles*, Cornerstone, 2010) and Steven McKenzie (*1-2 Chronicles*, Abingdon, 2004) place this within a broader Persian-period pattern of temple-foundation legends written for restoration communities.

The Chronicler's writing context — late Persian Yehud, a province with a functioning Second Temple but no functioning monarchy — makes the David-as-temple-founder figure ideologically powerful. The community has temple but no king; the Chronicler reminds them that the temple is the *Davidic legacy*, that the absent monarchy still grounds the present cult.

The numbers in 22:14 are extraordinary. **100,000 talents of gold** and **1,000,000 talents of silver** are sums far exceeding any plausible historical accumulation. Klein and Knoppers both treat these as rhetorical hyperbole — the Chronicler is communicating *exceeding magnificence* (cf. v.5, "exceedingly magnificent, of fame and glory throughout all lands"), not auditing royal accounts. Williamson notes that ancient Near Eastern temple-building inscriptions routinely use such hyperbolic numbers; the figures are theological, not financial.

Hebrew terms: *bayit* (בַּיִת, "house" — used throughout for the temple); *menuchah* (מְנוּחָה, "rest, repose" — 22:9, the pivotal theological word for Solomon); *shalom* (שָׁלוֹם, "peace, completeness" — 22:9); *damim* (דָּמִים, "blood, bloodguilt" — plural intensive in 22:8); *Shlomo* (שְׁלֹמֹה, "Solomon," derived from *shalom*); *kohanim* (כֹּהֲנִים, "priests"); *miqdash* (מִקְדָּשׁ, "sanctuary, holy place" — 22:19). The wordplay between *Shlomo* and *shalom* in 22:9 is etymologically explicit — the name itself is a theological program.

## Lens 2 — Literature

Chapter 22 falls into three literary movements. Verses 1–5 are narrative summary: David designates the threshing-floor site (v.1, picking up directly from 21:28–30), gathers resident aliens (*gerim*, גֵּרִים) as labor, and amasses materials. Verses 6–16 are David's first charge to Solomon — a private speech, parental and royal at once. Verses 17–19 are David's charge to "all the leaders of Israel" — a public charge that mobilizes the whole nation around Solomon's task.

The David-to-Solomon speech (vv. 6–16) is rhetorically structured to mirror Mosaic-Joshua transition language. The phrase "Be strong and courageous. Fear not; do not be dismayed" (v.13) is a direct echo of Deuteronomy 31:6–8 and Joshua 1:6–9 — Moses to Joshua, the LORD to Joshua. The Chronicler is consciously typing Solomon as a new Joshua. Williamson and Knoppers both flag this as deliberate intertextual signaling.

The blood-shedding speech (vv. 7–10) is one of the most rhetorically loaded passages in Chronicles. The argument is this: David *desired* to build (v.7); the LORD *forbade* it because of bloodshed (v.8); the LORD *promised* a son who would be a *man of rest* (v.9); that son would build (v.10). The verbal chain — desire, prohibition, promise, fulfillment — is a compressed theology of vocation: not every calling is for every generation, even when the calling is righteous.

The repeated word *menuchah* (rest) in v.9 is theologically luminous. It is the same root as the *Sabbath rest* of Genesis 2 and the *promised rest* of Deuteronomy 12:9–10 ("the rest and the inheritance that the LORD your God is giving you"). Solomon, the man of *menuchah*, is positioned as the fulfillment of a Mosaic-Joshuan trajectory: Israel finally enters its rest under a king who builds the place where God will dwell.

Verse 14 contains the only first-person numerical accounting in the Chronicler's David-to-Solomon material: *100,000 talents of gold, 1,000,000 talents of silver*. The literary effect is staggering — David speaks like a patriarch turning over an estate of unimaginable scale.

The chapter closes with David's third movement (vv. 17–19), shifting from son to nation: *Now set your mind and heart to seek the LORD your God. Arise and build*. The same imperative *qum* (קוּם, "arise") used to Solomon in v.16 is now used to all Israel in v.19 — the Chronicler unites private and public vocation under one verb.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the Tanakh, 1 Chronicles 22 stands at the heart of the Chronicler's late-canonical reframing. The Jewish trajectory of *temple* runs: tabernacle (Exod 25–40); Solomonic temple (1 Kgs 6–8); destroyed (2 Kgs 25); rebuilt under Zerubbabel (Ezra 3–6); expanded by Herod; destroyed in 70 CE. Rabbinic Judaism, after 70 CE, transposes temple-theology onto Torah study, prayer (the *Amidah* with its temple-restoration petition), and acts of *gemilut chasadim* (lovingkindness). The Talmud (Berakhot 8a) substitutes the synagogue and house of study for the destroyed temple. David's preparations in 1 Chronicles 22 — the *bayit* he charged Solomon to build — become, in rabbinic memory, the site whose absence still organizes Jewish liturgy and longing.

The Christian trajectory transposes the temple onto Christ. John 2:19–21 — "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up... he was speaking about the temple of his body" — is the foundational New Testament reframe. 1 Corinthians 3:16–17 and 6:19 extend it: the church corporately and the believer individually are the temple. Ephesians 2:19–22 makes the temple-construction language explicitly ecclesiological: "built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the cornerstone." Hebrews 9–10 reads the temple cult as fulfilled in Christ's once-for-all sacrifice. Revelation 21:22 closes the canon: "I saw no temple in the city, for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb."

The David-Solomon, blood-shedder vs. man-of-rest typology has a particularly strong Christian reception. **Hebrews 4:8–11** is the keystone: "If Joshua had given them rest, God would not have spoken of another day later on. So then, there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God." Hebrews reads Solomon's *menuchah* as anticipatory — pointing to a deeper rest only Christ can give. The Chronicler's Solomon-as-rest-giver theology is thus folded into the New Testament's eschatological-Sabbath theology.

Patristic readers — Augustine in *City of God* XVII.8, John Chrysostom in his homilies on Hebrews — read David and Solomon as a typological pair: the warrior-father whose violence built the kingdom, and the peace-bearing son who built the house. Both, in patristic exegesis, point forward to Christ — but Christ is the *Solomon-son* who builds, and his building is his own body and the church. The Chronicler's David-Solomon transition becomes, in Christian reading, a figure of the Father-Son relationship in the economy of redemption.

NT explicit citation: 22:10 ("He shall be my son, and I will be his father") is conceptually echoed in Hebrews 1:5 (where the Davidic dynastic formula from 2 Samuel 7:14 is applied to Christ). 22:9's "man of rest" tradition undergirds Hebrews 4. Acts 7:46–47 (Stephen's speech) explicitly references David's desire and Solomon's building.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

The Chronicler's agenda in chapter 22 is the legitimation of the Davidic-temple project: David is the divinely sanctioned founder, Solomon the divinely sanctioned builder, and the whole nation the divinely sanctioned support. The chapter thus binds dynasty, temple, and people into one theological knot.

**Tension 1: David disqualified by bloodshed.** The text is unambiguous: *You have shed much blood and have waged great wars. You shall not build a house to my name, because you have shed so much blood before me on the earth* (22:8). What is the moral and theological weight of this disqualification?

Three positions deserve attention.

*Position A — Cultic-Holiness Reading (mainstream-critical and traditional Jewish: Japhet, Knoppers, the Talmudic and medieval Jewish commentators).* The disqualification is *cultic*, not *moral*. David's wars were holy wars, divinely sanctioned. He is not being condemned. But blood — even legitimately shed — incurs ritual impurity that disqualifies its bearer from temple construction. The temple is a place of *menuchah*, of completed rest; it cannot be built by the hands that achieved that rest through violence. The position takes the OT cultic logic seriously: holiness and bloodshed, even righteous bloodshed, do not mix in the same sacred space. Solomon's *peace-name* qualifies him precisely because he has not wielded the sword. Steelman: this position respects the inner logic of the priestly tradition and resists modern moralistic readings that turn David into a war criminal. It does not condemn David's wars; it simply marks them as belonging to a different liturgical order.

*Position B — Moral-Critique Reading (Anabaptist, Mennonite, contemporary Christian non-violence: John Howard Yoder, Stanley Hauerwas, Greg Boyd's *The Crucifixion of the Warrior God*, Brian Zahnd's *Sinners in the Hands of a Loving God*).* The disqualification *is* a moral judgment, however quietly. The God who forbids the bloody-handed king from building his house is the same God whose ultimate self-revelation in Christ is non-violent. David's wars may have been historically necessary, but they were not theologically clean. The Chronicler is registering a critique of warrior-kingship even within his Davidic legitimation. Steelman: this position takes the canonical trajectory seriously — from David's blood-disqualification through the prophetic critiques of royal violence (Hosea 1:4–5, the indictment of Jehu's Jezreel-blood) to Jesus's "those who take up the sword shall die by the sword" (Matt 26:52). It refuses to use 22:8 as bare cultic ritualism and instead reads it as a seed of Christological non-violence.

*Position C — Just War Reading (traditional Catholic and Reformed: Augustine, Aquinas, the Westminster divines, contemporary just-war theorists).* The verse acknowledges that even *just* war involves bloodshed that has spiritual consequences, but it does not delegitimize war itself. David's wars are repeatedly described as the LORD's deliverance (e.g., 22:18, "the LORD... has delivered the inhabitants of the land into my hand, and the land is subdued before the LORD"). The just-war tradition reads 22:8 as a sober acknowledgment of war's moral costs without an absolutist condemnation: war can be necessary and divinely sanctioned, and yet still leave its agents marked. Steelman: this position takes both the OT's repeated holy-war theology and 22:8's gravity seriously. It refuses to soften 22:8 into mere ritual and refuses to use it as a proof-text for absolute pacifism.

**Where this leaves us.** The text resists a clean reading in any one direction. The cultic frame (Position A) is most internally consistent with priestly literature; the Anabaptist-non-violence frame (Position B) is most theologically generative for Christian ethics; the just-war frame (Position C) is most coherent with the broader OT canon's repeated warrant for divinely sanctioned war. A serious theological reader probably needs to hold all three in tension — refusing both the temptation to make 22:8 a non-violent manifesto and the temptation to neutralize it as mere ritual.

**Tension 2: Solomon as rest-bringer and the Christological reframe.** Hebrews 4:8–11 explicitly says Joshua's rest was inadequate, and by extension Solomon's was too. The *menuchah* of 22:9 was historically temporary — the Solomonic peace lasted barely a generation; the kingdom split under Rehoboam; the temple was eventually destroyed. The New Testament reads this trajectory as evidence that the rest the OT promises requires a greater Solomon. **Catholic teaching** (CCC §661–662) reads Christ's ascension as the entry into the eschatological *menuchah*. **Reformed covenant theology** (Vos, Murray, Beale's *The Temple and the Church's Mission*) reads Solomon's temple as a typological pointer to Christ and the church. **Eastern Orthodox** liturgical tradition reads the temple-fulfillment Christologically and ecclesiologically together — Christ is both the new temple and the temple-builder.

This raises a question the Chronicler does not answer: if the *menuchah* of Solomon was historically incomplete, what does it mean that the Chronicler — writing centuries after Solomon, after the temple's first destruction, in a community without sovereign monarchy — still presents the David-Solomon-temple narrative as the foundational story? Is the Chronicler hopeful for a renewed Davidic-temple future? Hopeful for a transposed Davidic-temple meaning? Or simply preserving memory? Mainstream-critical readers tend toward the third; messianic-Jewish and Christian readers tend toward the first or second.

**Closing question.** If even David — the man after God's own heart — was disqualified from building God's house because of the blood on his hands, what does that tell us about the kind of hands God wants for sacred building? And if Solomon's rest proved temporary, where does the rest of the people of God finally rest?

## The Thread

A king accumulates more than he could ever spend, knowing he will not build the thing he loves most. He hands the project to a son not yet ready, with a name that means peace and a vocation that requires it. He tells his son and his nation the same thing: *be strong, fear not, arise and build*. He has shed blood and cannot build the place where blood is no longer shed. He knows it. He builds toward a rest he will not see. The Chronicler shows us a paradox at the heart of the Davidic legacy: the warrior who founds the kingdom of peace cannot enter the house of peace he has prepared. The temple, in the Chronicler's hands, is always a project handed forward — from David to Solomon, from Solomon to a kingdom that will lose it, from the kingdom to a community that will rebuild it, and finally, in Christian reading, from that community to a Son whose rest is unending. Every builder of sacred space in the chain is also, somehow, disqualified from completing it. The work is always passed forward. The rest is always still ahead.
