# Deuteronomy 27: The Ebal Altar and the Twelve Curses

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

After the long march of laws closes in chapter 26, Deuteronomy's voice shifts. Chapter 27 opens with an unusual grammatical move: "Now Moses *and the elders of Israel* commanded the people" (v. 1). For the first time since the book began, Moses is not the sole speaker. Verse 9 adds another enlargement: "Moses *and the Levitical priests* said to all Israel." This widening of the speaker base signals the chapter's ceremonial character — covenant is a communal act, not a personal one — and prepares for the liturgical drama to come.

The setting remains the plains of Moab (1:5; 29:1), east of the Jordan, but the chapter projects its action across the river and northward, forty-odd miles into the central hill country. Mount Gerizim (881 m) and Mount Ebal (940 m) face each other across a narrow east-west pass near the ancient city of Shechem. The valley between them forms a natural amphitheater — the acoustic properties are remarkable, and travelers from Jerome onward have noted that speech from one slope carries clearly to the other. Shechem itself was already ancient when Israel arrived. Abraham had built an altar there (Genesis 12:6-7); Jacob had purchased land and dug a well there (Genesis 33:18-20); Joshua would gather the tribes there for covenant renewal (Joshua 24). The geography was freighted with patriarchal memory before the covenant ceremony at the site began.

The chapter prescribes three actions: plastering great stones and inscribing the law on them (vv. 2-8), building an unhewn-stone altar on Ebal (vv. 5-7), and staging an antiphonal blessing-and-curse recitation between the two mountains (vv. 11-26). The plastered stones have direct ANE parallels. The eighteenth-century BCE stele of Hammurabi, the Mesopotamian *kudurru* boundary stones, the eighth-century BCE Tell Deir ʿAlla plaster inscriptions (themselves found only 25 miles from Moab), the Egyptian practice of lime-plastering for display — all are variants of the same monumental-display genre. Plaster over stone (*sîd*, שִׂיד, vv. 2, 4) gave a white, writable surface. The unhewn-stone altar, by contrast, reaches back to Exodus 20:25: iron tools desacralize stone. The two constructions coexist on the same mountain: a plastered display stele for the people's eyes and an unworked altar for God's address.

The archaeological centerpiece is Adam Zertal's excavation on Mount Ebal from 1982 to 1989. Zertal identified a large rectangular stone structure (about 9 x 7 meters) on the mountain's northern ridge, dated by pottery to the Iron I period (thirteenth-twelfth century BCE), with a ramp, a surrounding courtyard, and bone deposits of clean animals consistent with sacrificial use. Zertal published his claim that this was Joshua's altar from Joshua 8:30-35, which itself fulfills Deuteronomy 27:4-8. The identification generated sustained debate. Israel Finkelstein and Nadav Naʾaman argued the structure was a watchtower or Iron I farmstead, not a cultic site. In 2019, Scott Stripling announced the discovery of a small folded lead tablet from the excavation dump, allegedly bearing a proto-Hebrew inscription with the divine name; Christopher Rollston and most epigraphers have remained skeptical about both reading and date. What is not disputed: there *is* a substantial thirteenth-twelfth century BCE installation on Ebal of unusual character for the region. What it was and whether it bears on Deuteronomy 27 is contested.

On dating, the Ebal installation pulls toward a Mosaic or very early horizon for the tradition, though most critical scholars since de Wette have read Deuteronomy as a seventh-century composition drawing on older material. The Samaritan Pentateuch's reading of the altar site adds another layer: where MT Deuteronomy 27:4 reads "Mount **Ebal**," the Samaritan Pentateuch reads "Mount **Gerizim**." The Samaritans consider Gerizim the true sanctuary (John 4:20 — "our fathers worshiped on this mountain"). A fragmentary Dead Sea Scroll (4QDeut^f or related) reportedly preserves the Gerizim reading as well, complicating any simple assumption that MT preserves the earliest text. Three or four Hebrew terms ground the chapter: *ʾărûr* (אָרוּר, "cursed"), the passive participle that opens each curse; *ʾāmēn* (אָמֵן, "so be it," literally "firmly"), the communal response; *sîd* (שִׂיד, "plaster"); and *šĕlāmîm* (שְׁלָמִים, "peace offerings," v. 7), the eating-together sacrifice that caps the altar's construction.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 27 is a covenant ratification ceremony in literary form. The genre is closest to ANE treaty ratification and to later Israelite covenant-renewal texts (Joshua 8 and 24 most obviously). Its structure moves in three panels: inscribed law and altar (vv. 1-10), tribal arrangement on the mountains (vv. 11-13), and the Levitical antiphonal curse recitation (vv. 14-26).

The inscription of the law on plastered stones is a distinctive move. ANE treaties were often deposited in temples; here the law is put up for public display on the mountain itself, outside any sanctuary. The phrase *baʾēr hêṭēb* (בַּאֵר הֵיטֵב, v. 8, "very plainly" or "clearly engraved") gives permission for both reading and teaching — the public visibility of the law is part of its theology. Rashi reads the phrase as "in seventy languages," a Jewish tradition that the Torah on Ebal was universally legible; Patrick Miller reads it more soberly as the plain intelligibility of the revealed word.

The blessing-and-curse structure is the dominant literary device. Six tribes on Gerizim for blessing; six on Ebal for curse. Close readers since Abarbanel have noticed that the tribes are not distributed by birth order or camping arrangement: the six on Gerizim are the sons of Leah and Rachel (Simeon, Levi, Judah, Issachar, Joseph, Benjamin); the six on Ebal are the concubines' sons plus Reuben (who lost primacy in Genesis 49) and Zebulun. The "handmaid sons" on the curse side reinforces the theme that the curse mountain is the place of disadvantage recalibrated — though there is no ethical demotion; both mountains are mountains of Israel.

The twelve curses (vv. 15-26) are the chapter's most studied literary unit. They take the form: *ʾārûr* + participle or clause describing a prohibited act, followed by "and all the people shall say, Amen." The twelve clauses cluster thematically: (1) idolatry; (2) dishonoring parents; (3) moving a neighbor's landmark; (4) misleading the blind; (5) perverting justice for sojourner, orphan, widow; (6-9) sexual transgressions with father's wife, any animal, sister, mother-in-law; (10) secret murder; (11) bribery for blood; (12) the summary — "cursed be anyone who does not confirm the words of this law by doing them."

Von Rad, Hillers, and Weinfeld have all observed that the twelve curses focus overwhelmingly on *secret* sins — acts done *basseter* (בַּסֵּתֶר, "in secret," vv. 15, 24). Idolatry "set up in secret"; landmarks moved where nobody can see; the blind misled; a blow struck covertly. The curses protect the categories of persons most vulnerable to private malice — parents, the blind, the stranger, the orphan, the widow, the victim of bribery, the unknowing — and the list forms a kind of social conscience of offenses too hidden to prosecute. The Levites speak what human courts cannot reach.

The twelfth curse (v. 26) is summary rather than specific: "Cursed be anyone who does not confirm the words of this law by doing them." The LXX reads "in all the words of this law," adding *pasin* — a reading Paul inherits. The shift from particular prohibition to comprehensive covenant loyalty provides the final *amen* and the theological capstone that Paul will seize on in Galatians 3.

Textual variants are relatively modest apart from the Ebal-versus-Gerizim question in verse 4. The Septuagint, Samaritan Pentateuch, and 4QDeut fragments diverge on small points. The LXX's comprehensive reading of verse 26 (adding *pasin*) is exegetically significant and will reappear in Pauline reception.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Jewish Reception

**Second Temple.** Josephus (*Antiquities* 4.305-308) retells the ceremony at length, amplifying the altar and the curse liturgy. The *Temple Scroll* (11Q19) from Qumran reconfigures the sanctuary around a Jerusalem-centered ideology; it does not reproduce the Ebal altar because its polemic against non-Jerusalem sanctuaries runs the other way. The Samaritan community, however, took Deuteronomy 27 as liturgical charter; their reading of verse 4 places the altar on Gerizim and their temple on the mountain until its destruction by John Hyrcanus in 128 BCE. The sectarian dispute over the correct mountain outlasted the temples; it is still alive in John 4's conversation at Sychar, literally in the valley between Ebal and Gerizim.

**Rabbinic.** *Mishnah Sotah 7:5* preserves the earliest rabbinic description of the ceremony: six tribes on each mountain, the Levites between them, the ark in the center; the priests turn to Gerizim and bless, turn to Ebal and curse; every blessing and curse is spoken twice because the *amen* implies a prior declaration. *Sifre Deuteronomy* treats the twelve curses as overlapping with wider Torah prohibitions and notes the emphasis on secret sin. *Talmud Bavli Sotah 32a-37b* expands the account with midrashic detail. *Devarim Rabbah* draws homiletical lines from the curses to the themes of humility and communal responsibility. Rashi, as noted, reads *baʾēr hêṭēb* as "in seventy languages"; Ibn Ezra offers the more restrained "with perfect clarity." Ramban connects the twelve curses to unwritten sin — the whole category of private wrongdoing that evades the court but not God. Maimonides in *Guide* 3.39 treats Deuteronomy 27 under the rationale of curses as public performance making private sin socially visible.

**Modern.** Jeffrey Tigay's *JPS Torah Commentary* provides the most detailed contemporary Jewish commentary, including extensive comparative material. Nechama Leibowitz's *Studies in Devarim* walks through the classical commentators on the twelve curses. Yeshayahu Leibowitz reads the ceremony as a paradigm of the categorical obligation of the commandments. Jonathan Sacks returns repeatedly to the "secret sin" motif as Deuteronomy's ethical distinctive. Jon Levenson sees the blessing-and-curse structure as the theological frame for biblical covenantalism.

### Christian Reception

**Patristic.** Origen's *Homilies on Deuteronomy* treats the Ebal altar as a figure of Christ's sacrifice offered outside the sanctuary — a reading Hebrews picks up (13:12). Augustine's *City of God* reads the ceremony as the inaugural act of two cities, earthly and heavenly, ratified on two mountains. Chrysostom and Theodoret give short treatments. Cyril of Alexandria's *Glaphyra* on Deuteronomy is extensive: the two mountains are the two testaments, the blessing-and-curse the two economies.

**Medieval.** Aquinas, *ST* I-II q. 102 art. 5, treats the ceremony under the ceremonial law, with detailed rationales for the uncut stones and the Levitical antiphonal. Bernard of Clairvaux preached occasionally on the "cursed be he who does not confirm the words of this law," reading it morally as the call to persevering obedience.

**Reformation.** This is where Deuteronomy 27 becomes central. Luther's 1525 *Lectures on Deuteronomy* reads the twelve curses as the law's voice stripped of gospel: the Levites' twelve *amens* are what the Law sounds like when heard without Christ. Calvin's *Commentary on Deuteronomy* works through the curses clause by clause, emphasizing that the curse falls on anyone who does not keep "*all* things written in the book of the law." His treatment shapes Reformed federal theology; the "covenant of works" summarized as Deuteronomy 27:26 becomes the foil against which the "covenant of grace" is defined. Deuteronomy 27-30 is structurally foundational to the whole Reformed covenant-theology tradition (Johannes Cocceius, the Westminster divines, Jonathan Edwards).

**Pauline.** Galatians 3:10 cites Deuteronomy 27:26 (following the LXX with *pasin*): "Cursed be everyone who does not abide by all things written in the book of the law, to do them." Paul's argument runs: the law brings those under it under curse; Christ became a curse for us (Galatians 3:13, citing Deuteronomy 21:23); the blessing of Abraham now flows to the nations through Christ. This is among Paul's densest Old Testament arguments, and modern Pauline studies (E. P. Sanders, James Dunn, N. T. Wright's *The Climax of the Covenant*) have turned it over repeatedly. Wright in particular reads Galatians 3 as Paul reading Deuteronomy 27-30 as a sustained storyline: Israel is in exile-as-curse, Christ bears that curse, blessing flows to the Gentiles in fulfillment of the Abrahamic promise. On this reading, Paul is not allegorizing Deuteronomy 27 but narrating Israel's history through it.

**Critical.** De Wette, Wellhausen, and Weinfeld see chapter 27 as a later insertion into the Deuteronomic framework, possibly reflecting cultic traditions at Shechem being integrated into the Deuteronomic program. Richard Nelson (*OTL*) and Thomas Römer treat the chapter as redactional bridge. Delbert Hillers's *Treaty-Curses and the Old Testament Prophets* is the classic comparative study. Adam Zertal's Mount Ebal publications are the archaeological reference point, disputed but essential.

**Contemporary.** Daniel Block (*NIVAC*), J. Gordon McConville (Apollos), Christopher Wright, Peter Craigie (*NICOT*), Duane Christensen (*WBC*), Walter Brueggemann (*AOTC*), Dennis Olson (*Death of Moses*), and Patrick Miller all give extended treatment. Olson in particular frames chapter 27 as the transition from catechesis to ratification in his reading of Deuteronomy's generational structure.

### Where They Converge and Diverge

Both traditions read the chapter as covenant theology in performance. Both see the twelve curses as revealing the seriousness of covenant violation. The divergence is what the curses *do*. For Jewish tradition, they remain a perpetual call to responsibility: the covenant is alive, the curses are pedagogy, repentance and return are always available. For the Reformation, the curses culminate in Galatians 3: the law's voice on Ebal cannot be heard without the voice on Gerizim, and Christ is the One who takes the curse so the blessing can flow. The same twelve *amens* become in Jewish hearing the rhythm of covenant responsibility, and in Pauline hearing the sentence Christ bore. The Samaritan question — which mountain? — is a third, largely forgotten, strand of reception that complicates any assumption of a single "original" setting.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

### Tension 1: Who Owns the Curse — Human or Divine Speaker?

The Levites pronounce the curses; the people say *amen*. But whose curse is it?

**Position A: The curses are divine speech performed by human mouths.** Augustine, Calvin, Brueggemann: when the Levites speak, God's judgment is inaugurated. The *amen* is the people accepting divine verdict in advance. This reading underwrites Reformed covenantal theology: God's word has agency; the curse is not a wish but a sentence.

**Position B: The curses are communal self-obligation.** Rashi, Rambam in *Guide*, Leibowitz: the people invoke the curse upon themselves as a conditional self-sentence. The *amen* is not acceptance of external verdict but covenantal self-binding. The curses function more like the oath-curses of ANE vassal treaties — if I break this, let X happen to me.

**Position C (steelman for the least intuitive):** The curses are ritual performatives that constitute the community of the cursed-if-faithless. Mary Douglas's *Leviticus as Literature* and the work of anthropologists of ritual on oath-speech: saying *amen* twelve times is how the tribe *becomes* the community that is liable to these particular judgments. Without the ceremony, the people are not yet under the covenant's particular curses — they are human beings subject to general moral order, but not yet bearers of this specific covenantal liability. The liturgy makes them vulnerable to the curse in a new way. This is Durkheim via ritual theory; it sounds odd but it explains why the ceremony is necessary at all.

**Where this leaves us:** The three positions map onto different accounts of how words create obligations. The chapter probably contains elements of all three: divine verdict, human self-binding, and ritual constitution. The theological point is that covenant is not a contract in the modern sense; it is a speech-act that reconstitutes persons as covenantally liable. What the Levites pronounce and the people confirm is, in some real sense, a new ontological condition.

### Tension 2: The Curse on "Anyone Who Does Not Confirm All This Law"

Verse 26 generalizes. The preceding eleven curses are specific; the twelfth is total. Does the summary curse cover sins of weakness and ignorance, or only willful violation?

**Position A: Comprehensive — the curse falls on any violation.** Paul's reading in Galatians 3:10, following the LXX's *pasin*. Luther, Calvin, and classical Reformation exegesis: the law demands perfect obedience; no one fulfills it; all are cursed. This is the exegetical engine of the Reformation's doctrine of justification.

**Position B: Covenantal — the curse falls on covenant-breaking, not ordinary stumbling.** Most rabbinic readings, N. T. Wright, James Dunn: the ancient covenantal imagination distinguishes between the faithful Israelite who commits ordinary transgressions (and atones) and the covenant-breaker who abandons the law as a whole. Verse 26 means the second, not the first. The Reformation's reading is, on this view, a Paul-Luther overextension.

**Position C (steelman):** The verse is deliberately indeterminate, and its indeterminacy is theologically load-bearing. Jacob Milgrom, Jon Levenson, and in a different mode Brueggemann: the summary curse leaves open whether it covers "all" or "all-who-abandon" because the covenant itself contains a productive ambiguity. Any reading that forecloses the tension also forecloses the dynamic of repentance and return that the book's next chapters (30, 31) develop. Verse 26 is meant to hover.

**Where this leaves us:** The reading one takes of verse 26 largely determines one's theology of law and gospel. The Reformation read it comprehensively and got justification by faith; much of Second Temple and rabbinic Judaism read it covenantally and got a theology of repentance and atonement within the covenant. Contemporary Pauline studies (Wright, Dunn, and many others) have reopened whether the comprehensive reading is correct as exegesis of Deuteronomy, even if it is correct as exegesis of Paul.

### Unresolved Question

If the Samaritan Pentateuch preserves the older reading — if the altar was on Gerizim and the *blessing* mountain, not Ebal the *curse* mountain — then the entire MT-LXX-Christian tradition has inverted the site. The altar of sacrifice would belong not among the curses but among the blessings, and the shape of Deuteronomy 27 would be significantly different. Is the MT reading (Ebal altar, among the curses) theologically motivated — placing the sacrifice among the curses precisely because sacrifice addresses the curse — or is the Samaritan reading closer to the original? The question has textual, archaeological, and theological stakes, and none of them are currently settled.

## The Thread

- **Genesis 12:6-7** — Abraham's first altar in the land was at Shechem, "between Ebal and Gerizim." Deuteronomy 27 prescribes a covenant-ratification altar at the same site. The return to Shechem is not accidental; the generation entering the land ratifies the covenant at the place where the patriarchal promise of land was first given. Genesis 12 is the seed; Deuteronomy 27 is the rite; Joshua 24 is the fulfillment.
- **Joshua 8:30-35** — the explicit execution of Deuteronomy 27. Joshua builds the altar, inscribes the law, arrays the tribes, reads the blessings and curses. This is one of the very few cases in the Hebrew Bible where a command in Deuteronomy is reported as literally and fully fulfilled. The structural rhyme between Deuteronomy 27 and Joshua 8 is so exact that it is almost certainly literary.
- **Hebrews 13:11-13** — "the bodies of those animals whose blood is brought into the holy places... are burned outside the camp. So Jesus also suffered outside the gate." The epistle's claim that Christ suffered outside the camp picks up the Ebal altar's location — outside the future sanctuary, on the mountain of curse. Hebrews reads Christ's crucifixion through the geography of Deuteronomy 27, not the sanctuary of Leviticus.
- **John 4:19-24** — "Our fathers worshiped on this mountain, but you say that in Jerusalem is the place where people ought to worship." The Samaritan woman's question is a direct reference to Gerizim. Jesus's answer — "neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem... in spirit and in truth" — formally resolves the Gerizim-Ebal-Jerusalem triangle that Deuteronomy 27 opens. The geography of covenant worship passes through the Son who stands in the valley between.
