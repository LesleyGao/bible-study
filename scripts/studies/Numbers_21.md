# Numbers 21: Lift It Up on a Pole

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Numbers 21 is a hinge in a different direction from chapter 20. Where 20 closed out the founding generation, 21 opens the conquest. Israel wins its first battle against a Canaanite king (Arad), survives a venomous plague by looking at a bronze serpent, traverses Transjordan on an itinerary punctuated by two of the oldest poetic fragments in the Bible, defeats Sihon at the Jabbok, and defeats Og of Bashan. The wilderness journey ends here, in practice; from Numbers 22 on, the story is set in the plains of Moab opposite Jericho, preparing the entry.

The geographical stakes are specific. Arad (v. 1) is identified with Tel Arad in the northern Negev, extensively excavated by Yohanan Aharoni and subsequent teams; the archaeological problem is that the Early Bronze city at Tel Arad was destroyed long before any plausible Israelite entry, and the Iron Age fortress post-dates a Late Bronze conquest. Aharoni argued for a nearby Canaanite site (Tel Malhata or Tel Masos) as "the king of Arad"; Kitchen (*On the Reliability of the Old Testament*) accepts the displacement; Finkelstein reads the mismatch as evidence that the narrative was composed late. *Hormah* (חָרְמָה, v. 3), the name given to the destroyed site, plays on *ḥērem* — the root meaning "devoted, placed under ban."

The Transjordanian itinerary from Oboth to Pisgah (vv. 10–20) traces a path east of the Dead Sea, skirting Edom and Moab, moving through what is now west-central Jordan. The Arnon (*ʾarnôn*, modern Wadi Mujib, vv. 13–14) is a dramatic canyon system that cut the traditional border between Moab to the south and the Amorite polity centered on Heshbon to the north. Heshbon is generally identified with Tell Hesban (el-Mishash) or Jalul; extensive excavation at Tell Hesban by Siegfried Horn and later Lawrence Geraty showed limited Late Bronze remains and substantial Iron II occupation — a gap Finkelstein and Thomas Thompson cite as evidence against a Late Bronze Sihon, while Kitchen and James Hoffmeier argue that the archaeologically invisible pastoral Amorite polity could still have existed at or near the site. Bashan (v. 33) is the basalt plateau of the modern Golan and Hauran; Og's royal city Edrei is modern Daraa (southern Syria). The "King's Highway" (*derek ha-melek*) referenced throughout is the major Iron Age trade route along the Transjordan plateau.

Key Hebrew terms. *Ḥērem* (חֵרֶם, v. 2–3), "devoted to destruction" — a sacrificial category in which captured persons, animals, and property are entirely given over to the deity, forbidden from any secular use; the same word appears at Jericho in Joshua 6 and at Ai, and its theological logic is among the hardest in the Hebrew Bible. *Nĕḥāšîm śĕrāpîm* (נְחָשִׁים שְׂרָפִים, v. 6), "fiery serpents" — literally "burning snakes"; the adjective *śārāp* shares the root of the angelic *serafim* in Isaiah 6, and commentators have long noted the semantic overlap (serpent / burning / shining). *Nĕḥaš nĕḥōšet* (נְחַשׁ נְחֹשֶׁת, v. 9), "bronze serpent" — a Hebrew wordplay (*nāḥāš* "serpent" / *nĕḥōšet* "bronze") that the verse exploits untranslatably. *Mišpāṭ* in the poem of v. 17 — "princes" — is *śārîm*, "nobles"; *mĕḥōqēq* (v. 18) is "scepter" or "lawgiver's staff." *Sīḥôn* (סִיחוֹן) and *ʿôg* (עוֹג) are the two Amorite kings; *ʿôg* is later associated with the giants/Rephaim (Deut 3:11 records his iron bed).

Ancient Near Eastern parallels. The bronze serpent raises the sharpest comparison with Egyptian religion. The Egyptian snake goddess Wadjet (cobra), the Meretseger ("She Who Loves Silence") cobra-goddess of the Theban necropolis, and the healing snake-rods associated with Isis and Hathor all involve serpents as protective or healing figures; Karen Joines, in *Serpent Symbolism in the Old Testament*, documents the widespread Levantine use of bronze serpent figurines (excavated at Timna, Gezer, Hazor, and Megiddo). The Timna find — a small bronze serpent with gilded head, discovered by Beno Rothenberg in a Midianite shrine dated to roughly the thirteenth century B.C.E. — is the closest physical analogue to what Numbers 21 describes. The "Song of the Well" (vv. 17–18) parallels Sumerian and Ugaritic well-dedication formulas. The Heshbon ballad (vv. 27–30) is analyzed by Frank Moore Cross and P. D. Miller as preserving an authentic Amorite victory poem appropriated by Israel. The "Book of the Wars of the LORD" reference (v. 14) points to a lost extra-biblical Israelite source.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Numbers 21 is genre-heterogeneous. Within its thirty-five verses the reader encounters a brief conquest narrative (vv. 1–3), a narrative of sin-plague-intercession-cure (vv. 4–9), a travel itinerary (vv. 10–13, 18b–20), three poetic fragments of ancient pedigree (the Arnon fragment from the "Book of the Wars of the LORD" in vv. 14–15, the Song of the Well in vv. 17–18, and the Heshbon ballad in vv. 27–30), and two compressed conquest reports (Sihon in vv. 21–31, Og in vv. 32–35). Martin Noth identified the poetic fragments as among the oldest material in the Pentateuch; Frank Moore Cross's dating work (*Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*) placed the Song of the Well and the Heshbon ballad in the twelfth–eleventh centuries B.C.E., earlier than the narrative frame that incorporates them. Paul Hanson and David Noel Freedman followed Cross. More recently, Mark Smith (*The Origins of Biblical Monotheism*) and Ron Hendel treat these fragments as linguistic fossils from the pre-monarchic period.

The "Book of the Wars of the LORD" (*sēfer milḥămôt YHWH*, v. 14) is cited and then lost. The same phenomenon occurs at Joshua 10:13 ("Book of Jashar") and 2 Samuel 1:18 (also "Book of Jashar"). These lost sources are important: they indicate that the biblical writers knew they were quoting, and that they drew on a literary corpus of war poetry we no longer have.

Literary devices. The bronze-serpent episode is built on wordplay — *nāḥāš* (serpent) / *nĕḥōšet* (bronze) in v. 9 and the śārāp / serpent ambiguity in v. 6. The structure is classical Deuteronomistic: sin (complaint) → judgment (serpents) → intercession (Moses) → remedy (bronze serpent). The abrupt place-name Hormah (v. 3) turns the *ḥērem* vow into a toponym, a Hebrew narrative trick that fixes theology in geography. Cross's analysis of the Heshbon ballad argues it is originally an Amorite victory poem celebrating Sihon's conquest *over Moab*, which Israel then appropriates as explanation for why Heshbon is now available (Sihon had already taken it from Moab, so Israel taking it from Sihon does not violate the promise to spare Moab, Deut 2:9).

Textual variants are notable. 4QNumᵇ preserves fragments of the chapter, generally aligning with the MT. The SP expands slightly at the Sihon encounter. The LXX at v. 14 differs subtly: where the MT has "Waheb in Suphah," the LXX reads *tēn Zōob ephlogisen*, suggesting a different Vorlage or an interpretive struggle with a corrupted archaic Hebrew. The MT of v. 14 is famously difficult — "Waheb in Suphah, and the valleys of the Arnon" — and commentators from antiquity have disagreed about parsing. LXX renders "fiery serpents" of v. 6 as *opheis thanatountas* ("death-dealing serpents"), moving from a color metaphor to a function metaphor. The variant in v. 9 is also significant: where MT has "bronze serpent" (*nāḥāš nĕḥōšet*), the LXX retains the wordplay approximately (*ophin chalkoun*) but loses the phonetic echo.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish reception**: The bronze serpent receives a striking and theologically pointed Mishnaic comment. *Mishnah Rosh Hashanah* 3:8 asks directly: "Does the serpent cause life or death? Rather: when the people of Israel looked upward and subjected their hearts to their Father in heaven, they were healed; and if not, they perished." This is a classical rabbinic demagicalization — the cure is not in the bronze but in the turning-upward, the trust, the direction of the heart. Bavli *Nedarim* 31b–32a and Bavli *Avodah Zarah* 43b pursue the theme, debating whether Moses's making a sculpted image did not violate the Decalogue, and concluding that it did not because the object's function was not worship but a sign. The later fate of the bronze serpent is laid bare in 2 Kings 18:4: Hezekiah destroys a bronze serpent called *nĕḥuštān* that "the people of Israel had made offerings to until those days," and the Mishnah and Rashi both cite this to show that the serpent of Numbers 21 had been preserved through five centuries and then corrupted into an idol — proving, as Rashi glosses at 21:8, that *any* sign can become idolatrous if the heart turns to it rather than through it. Maimonides (*Guide* 3.37 and elsewhere) develops the demagicalization: the serpent was pedagogical, a visible focus for the act of faith; its material composition was immaterial. Ibn Ezra follows Maimonides. Ramban is more sacramentally inclined, allowing that the sign itself had real efficacy as divinely instituted means, though the efficacy terminated in God not in the bronze.

The *ḥērem* vow at Hormah (vv. 2–3) is treated in Mishnah *Arakhin* 8:4–6 as the foundational case of the category, alongside Joshua's Jericho. The ethics of *ḥērem* are debated through the medieval period (Rashi, Ramban, Maimonides) and sharply in modern Jewish ethics (Leibowitz, Michael Walzer in *In God's Shadow*). The serpents-as-punishment narrative is treated in Bamidbar Rabbah 19:22, which develops the reading that the *śārāp* language ties the punishment to the sin — Israel's "fiery" speech against God provokes "fiery" snakes.

The "Song of the Well" (vv. 17–18) is liturgically central in rabbinic tradition. *Mishnah Sukkah* 5:1 preserves the *Simḥat Beit ha-Shoʾevah* (joy of the water-drawing) ceremony connected to the festival of Sukkot; the rabbinic tradition (Bavli *Sukkah* 51a-b) connects this ceremony to the Numbers 21 well-miracle, and the "well that followed Israel" midrash (which Paul reshapes in 1 Cor 10:4) is already developed here. Tosefta *Sukkah* 3:11 describes Miriam's well.

Nehama Leibowitz's *Studies in Bamidbar* on this chapter emphasizes the connection between the mercy at the bronze serpent (Israel's complaint produces serpents, but God provides a remedy) and the severity at Meribah (Moses's failure produces permanent exclusion). Jonathan Sacks (*Covenant and Conversation*) reads Numbers 21 as the transition from a generation that complained to one that would sing — the two poetic fragments mark this change; Israel begins to have a poetic voice of its own. Avivah Zornberg's *Bewilderments* reads the serpent narrative psychoanalytically — the serpent is the return of the repressed fear of the wilderness; looking at the bronze image is a staged confrontation that integrates what would otherwise destroy.

**Christian reception**: John 3:14–15 places the decisive Christian citation in Jesus's own mouth: "As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in him may have eternal life." This is the only place in the synoptic-Johannine tradition where Jesus explicitly ties his own crucifixion to a Numbers text, and the typology is exact: both figures are raised up, both are looked upon, both yield life. Justin Martyr (*Dialogue with Trypho* 94) uses the bronze serpent to argue that not every made image is idolatrous, and that the serpent-on-pole foreshadows Christ on the cross. Tertullian (*Against Marcion* 2.22) follows. Origen's *Homilies on Numbers* 18 treats the chapter at length, reading the bronze serpent as Christ "made to be sin" (2 Cor 5:21) — not sinful but bearing the form of what caused death, so that looking at him removes what poisoned us. Athanasius uses the same typology in *On the Incarnation*. Augustine (*Tractates on John* 12) and Cyril of Alexandria (*Commentary on John*) develop the reading: the serpent's material (bronze, without venom, resembling the venomous without being venomous) is a perfect figure of the Incarnation (Christ in the likeness of sinful flesh, Rom 8:3). Aquinas (*ST* III q. 46 a. 4 ad 2; III q. 50 a. 1) makes the typology doctrinally structural, using it to explain both the fittingness of crucifixion and the nature of Christ's bearing of sin. Calvin (*Institutes* 2.16.6, and his commentaries on John 3 and Numbers 21) accepts the typology and treats it as paradigmatic of how Old Testament types work. Luther, in his sermons on John 3, develops the image at length and makes the bronze serpent the visual heart of his theology of the cross.

Critical scholarship (Noth, Cross, Wellhausen, Levine, Milgrom, Budd, Wenham, Ashley, Cole, Dozeman) has focused variously on the archaic poetry, the sources of the itinerary, the historicity of Sihon and Og, and the integration of the bronze serpent narrative with Hezekiah's 2 Kings 18 reform. Mary Douglas (*In the Wilderness*) reads the chapter as a turning — the wilderness of death (chapter 20) pivots to the war of possession (chapters 21–24). Walter Brueggemann reads the bronze serpent narrative as the clearest instance of the paradox that the sign of judgment becomes the vehicle of grace — a paradox Brueggemann sees at the heart of the prophetic tradition. Terence Fretheim (*Numbers*, Interpretation) focuses pastorally on the dynamics of complaint and remedy. Ephraim Radner, in *Leviticus* and elsewhere, draws the bronze serpent into a Christological theology of the sign; Chris Wright (*The Mission of God*) reads the chapter for its theology of divine accommodation. N. T. Wright, in *Jesus and the Victory of God* and *The Resurrection of the Son of God*, treats John 3:14 as one of the defining texts of early Christology.

**Convergence and divergence**: The bronze serpent is perhaps the single sharpest instance in the Torah where Jewish and Christian readings converge on hermeneutical strategy (both demagicalize, both read the sign as pointing past itself) and diverge on object (rabbinic tradition reads the upward look as generic trust in heaven; Christian tradition, following Jesus's own citation, reads it as Christological type). Both agree that the sign can become idolatrous — Hezekiah's destruction of *nĕḥuštān* is canonical warning in both traditions. Both preserve the sense that what heals is not the material but the act of looking in faith. The Mishnah's "when Israel looked upward and subjected their hearts to their Father in heaven, they were healed" and John 3:14 are closer in logic than most Jewish-Christian pairs of readings; what divides them is whether the upward look terminates in God directly or in Christ crucified.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: How can God command the making of a bronze serpent without violating the prohibition against images?** Rabbinic tradition (Mishnah Rosh Hashanah 3:8; Bavli Avodah Zarah 43b) resolves the tension by distinguishing signs from idols: the serpent is a pedagogical focus for faith, not an object of worship; its material form is incidental. Maimonides formalizes this as a theory of divine accommodation — God uses means appropriate to the people's developmental stage. A less intuitive position, sometimes associated with Ramban and reworked in Christian sacramental theology, holds that the bronze serpent does more than represent — it is a divinely instituted means through which grace actually flows, not because bronze has power but because God has chosen this object as the focal point for a real transaction of healing. Steelmanning this sacramental position: the narrative says looking at the serpent brings life; if the object were purely symbolic with no instrumental function, the same result would obtain from looking at any object God designated, but the text specifies *this* object and names it. 2 Kings 18:4, where the object remains identifiable five centuries later and is worshiped, confirms that something about this particular object was preserved — for better and then for worse. Read this way, the line between sign and sacrament is theologically real: Numbers 21 institutes a sign that is also, in its instituted function, a vehicle of divine action. **Where this leaves us**: The Mishnah's warning and the sacramental reading are both necessary — without the Mishnah, the bronze becomes idol (as at Hezekiah); without the sacramental recognition, the biblical and Christian reception is flattened. The text preserves both: the serpent truly heals, and the heart must look past it to God.

**Tension 2: What is the moral status of *ḥērem*?** The Hormah episode (vv. 1–3) is brief but decisive: Israel vows total destruction, receives victory, and completes the vow. The theological options. First, the classical Jewish and Christian reading: *ḥērem* is a divinely authorized act of sacral warfare, historically bounded to the conquest, and not a standing command. Maimonides restricts *ḥērem* to the seven Canaanite nations, all of which, he held, no longer exist; subsequent application is forbidden. Second, the modern ethical critique (Philip Jenkins, *Laying Down the Sword*; aspects of Walzer, Leibowitz, and much Christian pacifist theology): *ḥērem* narratives are morally intolerable as texts, and later Jewish and Christian traditions have wisely domesticated them. Third, a less intuitive position developed by Chris Wright (*The God I Don't Understand*), Richard Hays, and from a different angle Ephraim Radner: *ḥērem* is a theological category not primarily about violence but about unconditional devotion — what is placed under *ḥērem* is declared "not for human use," and the conquest narratives ritually enact on enemies what the priestly system normally enacts on sacrifices. Steelmanning this third position: *ḥērem* in the Torah binds Israel's own property just as it binds enemy property (Lev 27:28 prohibits the redemption of anything devoted); at Jericho, Achan's sin is retaining *ḥērem* for himself. The category is about total transfer to God, not primarily about who is killed. Read this way, the conquest *ḥērem* is the edge case of a broader logic, and the moral problem is real but not exhausted by the question of violence. **Where this leaves us**: The tension does not dissolve. The text does command the destruction of populations, and no theological reading removes that fact. But the category of *ḥērem* is denser than "divinely authorized slaughter" — it is, within the biblical world, a total act of devotion that can be enacted on any property, and the conquest narratives are the extreme application of a category that also disciplined Israel's own wealth. Holding all three readings in tension keeps the moral difficulty present without reducing the text to a single dimension.

**The unresolved question**: Why did the bronze serpent work? The text does not say. It does not say that faith activated it, it does not say that God's power alone healed, it does not say that the object was merely pedagogical. It says: God commanded, Moses made, the bitten looked, and they lived. The reader — Jewish, Christian, or modern — supplies the mechanism, and the text's silence on mechanism is what allows two millennia of competing readings.

## The Thread

Four non-obvious cross-references. First, 2 Kings 18:4 — Hezekiah destroys "the bronze serpent that Moses had made, for until those days the people of Israel had made offerings to it (it was called Nehushtan)." The object endures five centuries before becoming an idol, and Hezekiah's reform shows that the very sign given for healing could, given time, require destruction; the narrative is a warning against the sacralizing of instituted signs beyond their instituted function. Second, Wisdom of Solomon 16:5–14, Hellenistic Jewish reflection on Numbers 21: "for neither herb nor poultice cured them, but your word, O Lord, which heals all things" — Wisdom glosses the chapter exactly in the direction the Mishnah will formalize, insisting that God, not the bronze, is the true healer. Third, John 3:14–15, where Jesus's only direct Numbers-citation in the Gospels ("as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up") turns the chapter into the foundational typological site for the crucifixion; the verb in John is *hypsoō*, "to lift up / exalt," and it will recur in John 8, 12, and beyond as the signature verb of Jesus's passion. Fourth, 1 Corinthians 10:4 — "they drank from the spiritual Rock that followed them, and the Rock was Christ" — Paul picks up the "well that followed Israel" tradition present in Numbers 21:16–18 (the Song of the Well) and mapped in rabbinic midrash (Miriam's well, Tosefta Sukkah 3:11), and Christianizes it; the move shows how Paul is reading Numbers as layered — the well, the rock, the serpent, all become types of the one who sustains the people through the wilderness of the world.
