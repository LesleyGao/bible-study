# Jeremiah 31: The New Covenant and the Longest OT Quotation in the New Testament

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jeremiah 31 is the climax of the Book of Consolation and is the densest single chapter of restoration material in the prophetic corpus. The political situation behind it is more contested than most chapters in the book. Several internal features point toward an early Jeremianic horizon, possibly during **Josiah's reform period** (c. 622–609 BC): the explicit address to *Ephraim* (31:6, 9, 18, 20) and *Samaria* (31:5) — that is, to the northern kingdom destroyed in 722 BC — suggests a moment when the apparent collapse of Assyrian power opened the possibility of a north-south reunification under a Davidide. **Lundbom** (Anchor) argues this case strongly. **Holladay** (Hermeneia) reads the chapter's literary affinity with Hosea as evidence that Jeremiah, an Anathoth priest with possible northern connections, was deliberately appropriating northern prophetic vocabulary for a Josianic-period reunification appeal. The "watchmen on the hill country of Ephraim" (31:6) calling pilgrims to Zion fits the centralizing logic of Josiah's reform (2 Kgs 23). Other features point later: the new-covenant pericope (31:31–34) presupposes the failure of the Sinai covenant in a sense most natural after 587 BC; the rebuilding-of-Jerusalem oracle (31:38–40) presupposes the city's destruction.

The mediating consensus, held by **Bright**, **Thompson**, and **Stulman**, is that an authentic Jeremianic core dates to the late seventh century, and the new-covenant prose was either delivered by Jeremiah after 587 BC or expanded by editors. **Carroll** (OTL) treats most of the chapter as redactional. **Mowinckel**'s classical source-critical analysis assigned the new-covenant text to his "C" prose source, which he placed late. **Duhm** treated 31:31–34 as a post-exilic addition. The post-Holocaust scholarship — **Kathleen O'Connor** especially — has shifted the question away from precise dating and toward the trauma-survival function of the language.

The material culture spans the geography of the divided kingdoms. *Ramah* (31:15), where Rachel weeps, sits about five miles north of Jerusalem on the Benjamite plateau, the staging-ground for the 587 BC deportation (Jer 40:1). The "Tower of Hananel," "Corner Gate," "Horse Gate," and "Brook Kidron" of 31:38–40 are all locatable in late Iron II and early Persian Jerusalem topography; Nehemiah 3 maps the same circuit during the post-exilic rebuilding. The Mizpah-Anathoth-Ramah-Bethel-Shiloh axis is the geographical spine of the old northern Benjamite-Ephraimite territory.

The textual situation: MT and LXX again diverge in places, but the core new-covenant pericope (31:31–34) is stable across the textual traditions. The **Dead Sea Scrolls** preserve Jeremiah fragments at Qumran Caves 2, 4, and 7, including a scrap of 31; the **Damascus Document** (CD 6:19; 8:21; 19:33–34; 20:12) calls the Qumran community the *baʾei ha-brit ha-chadasha* — "those who entered the new covenant" — making Jer 31 the only OT new-covenant text to receive explicit pre-Christian Jewish appropriation, decades before the New Testament use.

Standard scholars: **Lundbom**, **Holladay**, **Brueggemann**, **Carroll**, **McKane**, **Bright**, **Stulman**, **O'Connor**, **Thompson**, **Duhm**, **Mowinckel**; for the new-covenant theology, **N.T. Wright**, **R. Kendall Soulen**, **Daniel Boyarin**, **Jon Levenson**.

Key terms: *brit chadasha* (בְּרִית חֲדָשָׁה, "new covenant," 31:31); *Rachel mevakkah ʿal-baneha* (רָחֵל מְבַכָּה עַל־בָּנֶיהָ, "Rachel weeping for her children," 31:15); *aharit ve-tikvah* (אַחֲרִית וְתִקְוָה, "future and hope," 31:17, echoing 29:11); *betulat Yisraʾel* (בְּתוּלַת יִשְׂרָאֵל, "virgin Israel," 31:4, 21); *ahavat olam* (אַהֲבַת עוֹלָם, "everlasting love," 31:3); *neqevah tesovev gaver* (נְקֵבָה תְּסוֹבֵב גָּבֶר, "a woman encircles a man," 31:22, the famously enigmatic phrase).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is structured as a sustained restoration-poem in seven movements: (1) the wilderness-grace and rebuilding oracle (31:1–6), (2) the gathering-from-the-north and shepherd oracle (31:7–14), (3) the Rachel-weeping pericope and its consolation (31:15–22), (4) the dream-vision interlude (31:23–26), (5) the sowing-with-seed-of-man and individual-responsibility oracle (31:27–30), (6) the new-covenant pericope (31:31–34), and (7) the cosmic-order and rebuilt-Jerusalem oracles (31:35–40). The literary affinity with Hosea — especially the marriage imagery and the Ephraim-as-firstborn language — is the strongest internal evidence for an early Jeremianic dating of the poetic core (Holladay's case).

The Rachel-weeping pericope (31:15) is among the most rhetorically powerful texts in the prophetic corpus: a five-line lament whose stark grief is answered by an unflinching salvation oracle (31:16–17, *keep your voice from weeping... they shall come back from the land of the enemy*). Rachel — the matriarch buried at Ephrath/Bethlehem (Gen 35:19) but here heard at Ramah (the staging-area of the deportation) — figures collective Israel's grief through the personal grief of the mother. **Brueggemann** treats this as the prophet's most sophisticated literary act: a maternal voice given canonical room to refuse premature consolation. **O'Connor** reads it as Jeremiah's most explicit trauma-pastoral move.

The new-covenant pericope (31:31–34) is the longest unified theological-prose oracle in the chapter and is structured chiastically: (A) days are coming, (B) new covenant unlike the Sinai covenant, (C) law within them, written on hearts, (D) covenant formula *I will be their God, they shall be my people*, (C') no need for didactic mediation, all shall know me, (B') forgiveness of iniquity, (A') sin remembered no more. The pericope's most distinctive feature is its negative reference to Sinai: *not like the covenant that I made with their fathers... my covenant that they broke* (31:32). This Sinai-was-broken framing is what makes the passage so theologically productive and so theologically dangerous.

The "woman encircles a man" of 31:22 is one of the most disputed phrases in the OT. **Lundbom** surveys the major proposals: a reversal of natural order signaling the new age (LXX, Jerome); a reference to Israel returning to YHWH (Calvin); Mariological prefiguration of the virgin birth (medieval Christian); a textual corruption (some critical commentators); a reference to the new covenant's interior dynamism (Holladay). No consensus exists.

The *I will write it on their hearts* of 31:33 echoes Deuteronomy 6:6 (*these words... shall be on your heart*) and Deuteronomy 30:6 (the circumcision of the heart) — the new-covenant text is in conversation with Deuteronomic theology, perhaps deliberately. The MT-LXX divergence is moderate; LXX of 31:31 reads *diatheke kaine* — the Greek phrase that becomes the New Testament's *kaine diatheke*, "new covenant" or "new testament," and gives the second part of the Christian Bible its name.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Jewish trajectory: Sirach 49:6–7 praises Jeremiah; Bavli Megillah 14b discusses prophetic chronology. The Targum Jonathan paraphrases the new-covenant text without reading it as a covenantal *replacement* — for the targum, "new covenant" means renewed and deepened. **Rashi** on 31:31 treats the new covenant as the renewed Torah-life of the messianic age, not a different Torah. **Radak** and **Ibn Ezra** read the language as the eschatological fulfillment of Sinai, not its abrogation. **Abrabanel**, after the Spanish expulsion, reads it as the still-future restoration. The Pesikta Rabbati 32–34 dwells on Jer 31 at length, treating Rachel-weeping (31:15) as the matriarchal intercession that finally moves God to mercy after Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Moses have failed.

The most striking pre-Christian Jewish reception is the **Damascus Document** of the Dead Sea Scrolls (CD 6:19; 8:21; 19:33–34; 20:12), where the Qumran community calls itself *those who entered the new covenant in the land of Damascus*. This is the only pre-Christian explicit appropriation of Jer 31:31 as a self-designation; the Qumran sect understood itself as a remnant inside Israel that had renewed the covenant in a sectarian setting.

Modern Jewish reception (Levenson, *Sinai and Zion*; Boyarin, *A Radical Jew*; *Border Lines*) reads the new covenant as the renewal and deepening of Sinai — never its replacement. Boyarin in particular has argued that the Pauline appropriation of Jer 31 (especially in 2 Cor 3 and Gal 4) does not require the supersessionist reading later Christian theology drew from it. **Heschel** treats 31:3 (*everlasting love*) as the foundational pledge that grounds Jewish covenantal theology.

Christian trajectory: **Hebrews 8:8–13 quotes Jer 31:31–34 verbatim — the longest OT quotation in the New Testament**. Hebrews follows LXX, which translates *brit chadasha* as *diatheke kaine*. Hebrews 8:13 deploys the verbal logic — *in speaking of a new covenant, he makes the first one obsolete* — that becomes the foundational text of Christian supersessionist theology. The patristic reception is dense: **Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah*** treats the new covenant as the spiritual law of the Gospel; **Jerome** reads similarly; **Augustine's *De Spiritu et Littera*** (412 AD) is one of the most influential single readings of Jer 31, identifying the law-written-on-the-heart with the interior gift of the Spirit, and grounding the entire Augustinian-Western soteriology of grace-against-Pelagianism in this text. **Aquinas** (*ST* I-II.106.1) develops the Augustinian reading as the *new law of grace*. **Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah*** treats the new covenant as substantially identical with the Sinai covenant — same substance, different administration. Calvin (*Institutes* 2.10–11) developed the famous unity-of-the-covenants argument that became Reformed federal theology. **Luther** read the new covenant antithetically against the Sinai law, in line with his law-gospel hermeneutic.

The foundational **Christian-Jewish dispute** turns on whether the new covenant *replaces* or *renews* Sinai. Classical Christian supersessionism, present in Hebrews 8:13's "obsolete" language, in Marcion's extreme version, in Justin Martyr's *Dialogue with Trypho*, in the medieval *adversus Iudaeos* tradition, and in Calvin's complicated handling, treats the new covenant as the cancellation of the old. Post-Holocaust Christian theology has substantially revised this. **Vatican II's *Nostra Aetate*** (1965) explicitly rejected supersessionism: God's covenant with the Jewish people remains valid. **R. Kendall Soulen** (*The God of Israel and Christian Theology*, 1996) has produced the most sustained anti-supersessionist Christian reading. **N.T. Wright** in *Jesus and the Victory of God* and *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* argues that the Pauline appropriation of Jer 31 is a *renewal* reading, not a replacement reading; the new covenant is the eschatological fulfillment of God's pledge to Israel, not a different pledge to a different people.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The first genuine tension is **the supersessionism question**, and it cannot be honestly avoided. Does the new covenant of Jer 31:31–34 *replace* Sinai or *renew* it? The text itself is ambiguous: *not like the covenant that I made with their fathers... my covenant that they broke* (31:32) sounds replacement-ish; *I will put my law within them* (31:33) sounds renewal-ish (the *Torah* is preserved, just relocated). The negative comparative could mean "different in kind from Sinai" or "Sinai itself, now interiorized." Hebrews 8:13's language — "obsolete... ready to vanish away" — pushes the replacement reading; Wright and Soulen argue Hebrews has its own first-century intra-Jewish polemical context and should not be lifted out of it as the controlling lens for Jeremiah.

Catholic post-*Nostra Aetate* reading: God's covenant with Israel remains. The new covenant fulfills Sinai without abrogating God's faithfulness to the Jewish people; *Romans 11:29* (the gifts and call of God are irrevocable) is the controlling Pauline text, not Hebrews 8:13. Reformed federal theology classically held a more substitutionary reading, though contemporary Reformed theology has substantially modified this. **Calvin's** unity-of-the-covenants argument (*Institutes* 2.10) is closer to renewal than replacement, although Calvin himself was unsparing toward contemporary Judaism. Lutheran reception (the law-gospel antithesis) tilts more strongly toward replacement; the Reformation polemical context made this difficult to nuance.

Steelman for the renewal reading: every element of the new covenant in Jer 31 is the Sinai covenant intensified. The covenant formula (*I will be their God, they shall be my people*, 31:33) is verbatim Sinai. The Torah is preserved (it is God's *torati*, my law, 31:33). The covenant partner is Israel and Judah (31:31) — not a new ethnic-religious community. Forgiveness of iniquity is already a Sinai provision (Lev 16, the Day of Atonement). The genuinely new elements are interiorization (law on hearts, not stones) and the universalizing of the knowledge of God (no need for catechetical mediation). Both are *intensifications* of Sinai, not its abolition.

Steelman for the replacement reading: the negative comparative (*not like the covenant... that they broke*) is hard to dilute. Hebrews 8:13's deployment is canonical for Christians. Sinai was a covenant of stone; the new covenant is a covenant of spirit. The deep grammar of New Testament theology repeatedly treats the new as superseding the old (2 Cor 3:6–18 is unmistakable). To dissolve this is to lose Christian distinctiveness.

Jewish reading offers a third path: the new covenant is the *renewal* of Sinai promised within the Sinai logic itself (Deut 30:1–6 already speaks of God circumcising the heart), and the Christian appropriation is a hermeneutical possibility but not the only one. **Boyarin** argues that first-century Jewish-Christian relations were less binary than later polemical historiography suggests. The *Damascus Document* shows that "new covenant" language was available in pre-Christian Judaism for sectarian self-designation without supersessionist implications. Where this leaves us: the post-Holocaust Christian theological consensus, the Catholic post-*Nostra Aetate* development, and the renewed appreciation of intra-Jewish covenantal diversity have made the older replacement reading harder to sustain without serious qualification. The Christian-Jewish dialogue on Jer 31 is one of the most important theological conversations of the contemporary period.

The second tension is **individual versus corporate responsibility** (31:29–30). The proverb *the fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge* expresses the intergenerational covenant logic of Exodus 20:5 (visiting iniquity to the third and fourth generations). Jeremiah and Ezekiel (Ezek 18) both abrogate it: *everyone shall die for his own iniquity*. Reformed federal theology (covenant of grace operating corporately through the church, original sin transmitted federally) reads this as a *qualification* of corporate responsibility, not its abolition; the Westminster Standards keep both. Wesleyan free-will theology (Wesley's *On Original Sin*, *Predestination Calmly Considered*) reads it as foregrounding individual responsibility — each person responds to grace personally. Catholic teaching (CCC 405) holds original sin as transmitted *by propagation, not imitation*, but final accountability as personal. Jewish reading (Maimonides, *Hilkhot Teshuvah*) is similarly twofold: covenantal solidarity coexists with individual responsibility for repentance.

Where this leaves us: Jer 31:29–30 does not abolish covenantal solidarity but reframes the locus of judgment. The new covenant operates corporately (it is made with the *house of Israel*) and individually (each one shall know YHWH from least to greatest). Both registers persist.

One unresolved question: if the law is written on the heart and all shall know YHWH from least to greatest, what theological status does *teaching* — catechesis, preaching, scriptural mediation — retain in the new-covenant age? Christian tradition has continued to teach for two millennia despite the verse promising teaching's redundancy.

## The Thread

The Rachel-weeping pericope (31:15) is taken up in **Matthew 2:18** as the prophetic resonance of Herod's slaughter of the innocents at Bethlehem; Matthew's typological move (a Bethlehem matriarch weeping for Bethlehem children) is one of his most striking citations. The new-covenant text (31:31–34) is quoted in **Hebrews 8:8–12** (the longest OT quotation in the NT) and again in **Hebrews 10:16–17**; it is alluded to in **Luke 22:20** (the cup at the Last Supper, *the new covenant in my blood*), in **1 Corinthians 11:25**, and most extensively in **2 Corinthians 3:3–18** (the ministry of the new covenant, of the Spirit, written on hearts of flesh). The "everlasting love" of 31:3 reverberates in **Romans 8:38–39** and **Ephesians 2:4–7**. The "I am a father to Israel, and Ephraim is my firstborn" (31:9) is appropriated in **2 Corinthians 6:18** and resonates with the firstborn theology of **Hebrews 12:23**. The covenant formula *I will be their God, they shall be my people* (31:33) culminates in **Revelation 21:3**.
