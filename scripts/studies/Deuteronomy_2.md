# Deuteronomy 2: Brothers in Seir, Lot in Moab

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Deuteronomy 2 describes Israel's movements through the Transjordan — the highland plateau east of the Jordan River and the Dead Sea — during the final year of the wilderness generation. The chapter treats a political geography that was real and contested across the Late Bronze and early Iron Ages: Edom in the south (the Seir range, running roughly from the Zered south toward the Gulf of Aqaba), Moab in the central plateau (between the Zered and the Arnon), Ammon north of the Jabbok, and the Amorite kingdom of Sihon of Heshbon occupying the territory between the Arnon and the Jabbok. Moses's itinerary moves from Kadesh-barnea in the Negev (v. 14) across the Arabah, around Edom, past Moab, up to the Arnon and the defeat of Sihon at Jahaz (v. 32).

The political situation at the time Deuteronomy narrates is the late second millennium BC; the political situation of the author(s), on standard critical reconstructions, is centuries later. Here the book's authorship and dating questions come into play. W.M.L. de Wette's 1805 dissertation identified Deuteronomy with the "book of the law" found in the temple during Josiah's reform in 622 BC (2 Kings 22–23), a hypothesis that became foundational for Julius Wellhausen's source-critical synthesis and still orients most critical scholarship. Moshe Weinfeld, in *Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School* (1972) and his Anchor Bible commentary *Deuteronomy 1–11* (1991), argued that Deuteronomy's rhetorical style, covenant ideology, and curse formulae bear unmistakable resemblance to neo-Assyrian vassal treaties, especially Esarhaddon's Succession Treaty (VTE, 672 BC), which would place the book's core in the late eighth or seventh century. Against this, Kenneth Kitchen (*On the Reliability of the Old Testament*, 2003) and Peter Craigie (NICOT 1976) argued that Deuteronomy's structure more closely fits the Hittite suzerain-vassal treaty form of the fourteenth–thirteenth centuries: preamble, historical prologue, stipulations, document clause, witnesses, blessings and curses. Meredith Kline pressed this typology as evidence of Mosaic antiquity. Jeffrey Tigay in the JPS commentary (1996) and Richard Nelson (OTL 2002) hold the middle — a multi-stage composition with a monarchic-era core, exilic supplements, and a long memory of earlier traditions. For Deuteronomy 2 specifically, the brother-peoples polemic (Esau, Moab, Ammon) and the military tour of the Transjordan sit loosely with either dating but are often read as responding to later Israelite political memories — including, perhaps, Moabite and Ammonite conflicts of the divided monarchy.

The archaeological picture of the Transjordan is famously contested. Nelson Glueck's mid-century surface surveys concluded that Edom and Moab were essentially unoccupied in the Late Bronze Age, casting doubt on a thirteenth-century exodus and conquest. Subsequent excavations — especially Thomas Levy's work at Khirbet en-Nahas in the Faynan copper district — revealed a substantial Iron I–II copper-smelting complex, with some evidence pressing the beginning of organized Edomite polity earlier than Glueck believed, though still probably after Moses's lifetime on any literal chronology. James Hoffmeier (*Ancient Israel in Sinai*, 2005) and Kitchen have emphasized seasonal pastoralism and chiefdoms that could exist below the threshold of visibility to surface archaeology. The Mesha Stele (mid-ninth century BC), discovered at Dhiban in 1868, is the single most valuable extra-biblical witness to Moabite religion, politics, and language, and its verbal echoes of Hebrew *ḥērem* warfare ("I devoted it to Chemosh") are striking parallels to v. 34 here.

Several Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. *Aḥîkem* (אֲחֵיכֶם), "your brothers," applied to the sons of Esau in v. 4, carries the weight of the entire Jacob-Esau narrative and marks a limit on Israelite territorial claims. *Yerušāh* (יְרֻשָּׁה), "possession" or "inheritance" (vv. 5, 9, 12, 19), is not restricted to Israel here: YHWH has given Seir to Esau, Ar to Moab, and the land of Ammon to the sons of Lot as *yerušāh*. This is extraordinary, theologically. *Rĕpāʾîm* (רְפָאִים) designates the autochthonous giant populations (v. 11, 20) — also called *ʾEmîm* by Moabites and *Zamzummîm* by Ammonites; the term appears in Ugaritic texts (KTU 1.161) for deified royal ancestors, and in Isaiah 14 and Job 26 for shades of the dead. *Ḥērem* (חֵרֶם), "devote to destruction" (v. 34), denotes total consecration of war-spoil — the same root used of Achan's sin (Josh 7) and of Saul's disobedience in 1 Samuel 15. Finally, *wayqaš ʾet-rûḥô* (וַיְקַשׁ אֶת־רוּחוֹ), "he hardened his spirit" (v. 30), applied to Sihon, deliberately echoes the Exodus idiom of Pharaoh's hardened heart (*qāšâ*, *ḥāzaq*) — Sihon is the Transjordan's Pharaoh.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Deuteronomy 2 belongs to what most commentators treat as Moses's first address (1:1–4:43), the historical prologue of the covenant document. Dennis Olson (*Deuteronomy and the Death of Moses*, 1994) reads the whole book as a catechism structured around Moses's impending death; Duane Christensen (WBC, 2001) reads it as a liturgical-poetic composition; Daniel Block (NIVAC, 2012) treats it as a series of sermons. In all these readings, chapter 2 is retrospective narrative serving an exhortative purpose — Moses telling the story so that the generation about to cross the Jordan understands both the scale of what YHWH has done and the limits of what has been authorized.

The chapter's genre is historical-homiletic narrative embedded in a covenant speech. It shares features with ANE royal inscriptions — the named kings, specified place names, captured cities, and the catalogue formula "we captured all his cities at that time" (v. 34) — but its cadence and theological interruptions (the Rephaim parenthesis, vv. 10–12, 20–23; the "hand of the LORD was against them" notice, v. 15) push it toward sermon rather than chronicle. J. G. McConville (Apollos, 2002) notes how the parenthetical ethnographies function rhetorically: they remind Israel that YHWH has displaced other giant-peoples for other nations before Israel even arrives, so that the coming Anakim in Numbers 13's report of the land should not terrify.

Deuteronomic phraseology is already audible: "the LORD your God has blessed you in all the work of your hands" (v. 7) anticipates the blessing language of chapter 28; "these forty years the LORD your God has been with you; you have lacked nothing" is classic Deuteronomic covenant memory. Weinfeld collected these formulae as diagnostic of a "Deuteronomic School," and Patrick Miller (Interpretation, 1990) showed how chapter 2 rehearses them in narrative mode.

The structural architecture is itineraric: a triad of nations passed by (Edom, Moab, Ammon) and a triad of victories offered — except Israel only fights the last of them (Sihon). The chiastic logic is that the God who constrains Israel from taking what belongs to Esau, Lot-by-Moab, and Lot-by-Ammon also promises what belongs to Sihon. The three-plus-one pattern does theological work: the war that comes in v. 24 is legitimated precisely by the three refusals preceding it. Sihon's refusal of safe passage (vv. 26–30), phrased in explicit Exodus language, is the narrative pivot.

Textual variants in the Transjordan narratives are significant. 4QDeutj and 4QDeutn (Qumran, 2nd–1st c. BC) preserve sections of Deuteronomy with some alignment to the proto-MT and some alignment to the Septuagint Vorlage. The Samaritan Pentateuch harmonizes Deuteronomy 2 with Numbers 20–21 more extensively than MT does. LXX Deuteronomy 2 adds a half-verse at v. 7 that heightens the providential language. None of these variants disturb the chapter's substance, but they show it was a living text whose details were carefully tracked.

A literary device worth noting: the parenthetical ethnographies read like editorial glosses, particularly the anachronistic "to this day" (v. 22). Many critical commentators (Nelson, Weinfeld, Römer in *The So-Called Deuteronomistic History*, 2005) take these as evidence of later editorial layers — the kind of detail that Abraham ibn Ezra, in the twelfth century, hinted at when he wrote, enigmatically, about verses that could not have been written by Moses himself ("and the Canaanite was then in the land"; Gen 12:6). The "Ibn Ezra problem" is an ancient acknowledgment, within the Jewish tradition, that Deuteronomy bears signs of editing.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

The Second Temple period read Deuteronomy 2 with live political stakes. Josephus's *Antiquities* Book 4 retells the Transjordan wars with embellishment appropriate to a Roman-era audience, emphasizing Sihon's treachery to justify Israelite conquest — Josephus had to explain to Greek readers why the bypassing of Edom and Moab was not simply pragmatic weakness. Philo of Alexandria, in *On the Life of Moses*, allegorizes the refusals to attack as disciplines of self-restraint. The Temple Scroll (11Q19) from Qumran rewrites Deuteronomic material into a utopian first-person divine voice, though the Transjordan sections are fragmentary.

The classical rabbinic tradition preserved detailed discussion in the midrashic and Talmudic sources. *Sifre Deuteronomy*, the tannaitic halakhic midrash, treats the brother-peoples' land grants as theologically significant in their own right — YHWH is not only Israel's God but the giver of lands to Esau and Lot as well. Rashi (eleventh c.) emphasizes the merit of Lot in sheltering the angels (Gen 19) as the reason his descendants received Ar and Ammon, and the merit of Abraham's loyalty to Esau's father Isaac as the reason Edom was spared. Ibn Ezra, more literal, simply notes that the nations had historical priority. Nachmanides (Ramban, thirteenth c.) wrestles with the moral theology of ḥērem — why Sihon's people deserved it and Moab's did not — concluding that Sihon's aggression in refusing passage forfeited protection.

Maimonides in the *Guide* (3.31–32) uses the Transjordan narratives to argue that ritual and political commands often have contingent, educational purposes rather than timeless rationale — a rationalist position with its own echoes in later critical scholarship. In the modern period, Jeffrey Tigay's JPS commentary (1996) gives a comprehensive philological and historical reading; Jon Levenson (*Sinai and Zion*, 1985) uses Deuteronomy's territorial theology to contrast with Zion theology; Nehama Leibowitz's *Studies in Devarim* reads the chapter as an ethical manual — even in war, boundaries of kinship and historical debt matter.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

Early Christian exegesis handled Deuteronomy 2 with some discomfort. Origen's *Homilies on Numbers* and scattered comments on Deuteronomy allegorize the defeat of Sihon as the conquest of the vices; Origen famously read ḥērem as spiritual warfare against sin, not as literal ethnic violence. Augustine, in *Questions on the Heptateuch* and *City of God* 1.21, argued that the wars were justified by direct divine command, a special-case ethic not generalizable. Chrysostom, preaching to a Christian empire, treated Sihon's hardening as a warning against imperial pride.

Medieval Christian reception, through the *Glossa Ordinaria* and Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* I-II qq. 98–105), used Deuteronomy to articulate the distinction between moral, ceremonial, and judicial law. The wars of conquest were classed as judicial, bound to a specific historical dispensation. Reformation commentators returned to the text with new intensity. Calvin's *Commentary on the Last Four Books of Moses* organizes the material by the Decalogue — a bold editorial move that subordinated narrative to law, but it meant Calvin had to fold the Transjordan stories into comments on idolatry, the sixth commandment, and civil government. His treatment of Sihon's hardening (on v. 30) is one of his strongest statements of double predestination outside the Romans commentary. Luther preached Deuteronomy in the 1520s, reading Sihon's refusal of peace as the self-destruction of unbelief.

Critical scholarship in the modern era has been dominated by the de Wette–Wellhausen lineage. Martin Noth's *Deuteronomistic History* thesis (1943) treated chapter 2 as part of the Deuteronomistic framework that extends through Joshua–Kings, constructed to justify the Davidic-Josianic kingdom and later to explain the exile. Weinfeld's Anchor Bible (1991) brings comparative ANE treaty material to bear on every detail. Thomas Römer's refinement of the Deuteronomistic thesis distinguishes Assyrian-era, Babylonian-exilic, and Persian-era editorial horizons.

Contemporary Christian commentators handle the chapter variously. Walter Brueggemann (AOTC, 2001) emphasizes the gift-character of land — for Moab and Ammon no less than for Israel. Patrick Miller (Interpretation, 1990) and Christopher Wright (NIBC, 1996) read the brother-peoples ethic as a critique of Israelite imperial self-understanding. Daniel Block (NIVAC) insists that the chapter's moral seriousness — the giants who cannot be defeated without YHWH, the brother-peoples who cannot be attacked despite Israel's power — resists both sentimental readings and cynical ones.

### Convergence and Divergence

Both traditions read Deuteronomy 2 as theologically sophisticated geography — YHWH is not a tribal deity whose power ends at Israel's borders. The idea that Esau, Moab, and Ammon received their lands *from YHWH* is striking in both rabbinic and patristic readings. Jewish tradition has stayed closer to the text's particularity — the actual peoples, the actual territories, the actual ḥērem law — partly because Jewish political life remained bound to the land of promise. Christian tradition has more readily allegorized or theologized the conquest, partly out of necessity after the dispersion of Jewish identity from land and partly because the church expanded as a non-territorial movement. The divergence becomes sharpest around ḥērem: Jewish tradition, even at its most troubled, has had to reckon with the commandment as commandment; Christian tradition, from Origen forward, has reached quickly for the spiritual key.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

### Tension 1: The Ethics of Ḥērem

The chapter's climactic military episode ends with an unambiguous statement: "We captured all his cities at that time and devoted to destruction every city, men, women, and children. We left no survivors" (v. 34). No modern reader gets past that sentence without discomfort.

**Reformed**: Calvin (on v. 34) and Edwards held that the ḥērem was a direct divine command rooted in the holiness of YHWH and the depth of Canaanite (and here, Amorite) corruption. The command is not generalizable; it was a unique exercise of divine judgment in which Israel functioned as instrument, analogous to the Flood. The theological force lies in the seriousness of sin and the reality of judgment.

**Catholic**: Aquinas (ST I-II q. 105) and the *Catechism* hold that the ḥērem commands belonged to the judicial law of Israel and are abrogated in Christ. The moral law — do not murder the innocent — remains; the judicial wars were a temporary divine dispensation. Benedict XVI in *Verbum Domini* (2010) urged that such passages be read in the context of progressive revelation culminating in the nonviolence of the cross.

**Jewish**: Maimonides (*Hilkhot Melakhim* 6–7) codifies the laws of war including the ḥērem, with a revealing detail: even the ḥērem wars required prior offer of peace. Rambam's requirement that Joshua offered peace to Canaanite cities before besieging them is an expansion that softens the brutality. Abarbanel and later commentators stressed that ḥērem applied only to seven specific nations and only under specific circumstances no longer operative. Modern Orthodox thinkers (Lichtenstein) and Conservative scholars (Levenson) have argued that the texts stand as testimony to a real historical moment without licensing modern imitation.

**Critical**: Weinfeld, Nelson, and Römer argue that ḥērem language is largely rhetorical — ideological Deuteronomic construction projected back onto the conquest from the viewpoint of seventh-century Judah or later. The theological intent is nationalistic self-definition in a period of Assyrian or Babylonian pressure, not a report of genocide.

**Steelman (critical position, least intuitive for traditional readers)**: If Weinfeld and Nelson are right that ḥērem is primarily a rhetorical construction of the Deuteronomic school, the text is not commanding historical genocide but constructing an ideology of purity in the face of foreign threat. The force of the text is psychological and political, not moral-prescriptive. The proper theological response is not to defend the command as ethical but to understand the social circumstances that produced such language — and to treat it as scripture in a chastened way, recognizing that God can speak truth through texts whose immediate historical function was ideological.

**Where this leaves us**: The chapter resists easy resolution. Traditional readings uphold divine sovereignty in judgment while restricting the command's scope; critical readings relocate the moral weight from ancient genocide to modern ideology. What cannot be avoided is that the text links YHWH's holiness to violence, and any theology of Deuteronomy has to reckon with that linkage rather than domesticate it.

### Tension 2: Brothers and Strangers — the Limits of Territorial Claim

The chapter is remarkable for what Israel is *not* permitted to do. YHWH has given Seir to Esau, Ar to Moab, the land of Ammon to the sons of Lot. Israel is to pay for food and water (v. 6) and pass through without contention. This is a political theology of national limits.

**Reformed**: Reformed political theology, especially in the Rutherford–Gillespie tradition (*Lex Rex*, 1644), read Deuteronomy's limits on royal and national power as enduring principles. The refusal to take Edomite or Moabite land establishes that even the elect nation operates under divine limitation. Calvin drew from this text arguments against wars of aggrandizement.

**Catholic**: Thomistic natural law sees in the brother-peoples material a confirmation of the *ius gentium* — a universal law binding all nations, including respect for existing possession. Pope Pius XII and later *Pacem in Terris* (John XXIII, 1963) drew on this tradition for modern Catholic teaching on national sovereignty.

**Wesleyan**: John Wesley's *Notes on the Old Testament* emphasize the economic dimension — Israel is to pay for what it consumes (v. 6). Wesleyan social ethics has developed this into a theology of respect for others' labor and provision.

**Jewish**: The rabbinic tradition celebrates this as evidence of YHWH's universality. Sifre Deuteronomy on 2:5 is explicit: YHWH is concerned with Esau as well as Jacob, with Lot as well as Abraham. Modern Jewish thinkers (Heschel, Sacks) draw from this a vision of particular election that does not deny the divine concern for the nations.

**Orthodox**: Eastern Christian readings (John Chrysostom, Theodoret of Cyrus) emphasize the providential care of God for all peoples — a theme Orthodoxy has developed into a broader theology of "the logoi of nations."

**Steelman (Jewish universalist reading, least intuitive for Christian supersessionism)**: Deuteronomy 2 is, if anything, an anti-imperial text. YHWH explicitly recognizes non-Israelite peoples as having land-inheritance from Him, with historical depth ("formerly..."). Israel's election is not zero-sum. The Christian reading that has, at various points, used Israel's conquest theology to justify colonial expansion has inverted the text's meaning. The proper reading is that YHWH is Lord of all nations and Israel's calling is bounded, limited, responsible.

**Where this leaves us**: The chapter's political theology is more nuanced than often read. Even at the height of conquest rhetoric, Deuteronomy insists that some lands are not for Israel. This qualifies every subsequent claim about "the land." It also creates a template for thinking about national limits — a template Rutherford, the Puritans, and modern Catholic social teaching have all drawn on.

**One unresolved question**: If YHWH gave Seir to Esau and Ar to Moab as inheritances, why did the same YHWH permit, through Israel's later history, the dispossession of those peoples (Obadiah against Edom, the Moabites eventually absorbed)? Is national inheritance from YHWH revocable in the same way Israel's is, or is there a double standard? The prophetic books leave this tension open.

## The Thread

Three cross-references that open the chapter outward:

First, the command to Sihon (vv. 26–30) with its offer of peaceful passage echoes *backward* to Numbers 20:14–21, where Edom refused passage — a story Deuteronomy 2 does not exactly retell but assumes the reader knows. The difference between Edom's refusal (which Israel accepted without war) and Sihon's refusal (which triggered war) turns on YHWH's specific permission in v. 24. The theology is not that refusal of Israel's passage is always a casus belli; it is that Sihon's refusal coincided with divine authorization.

Second, Sihon's hardening (v. 30) echoes forward to Joshua 11:20, where YHWH "hardened the hearts" of the Canaanite kings so they would come out to battle and be destroyed. This is not a one-off literary flourish; it is the Deuteronomic history's theology of providence — the *Heilsgeschichte* interpretation under which even enemy decisions are YHWH's instruments. Paul reaches for the same logic in Romans 9 when citing Exodus on Pharaoh.

Third, the brother-peoples ethic (vv. 4, 9, 19) reappears unexpectedly in Obadiah and in Amos 1:11 — prophetic oracles that denounce Edom specifically for violating brother-kinship with Israel. The refusal to attack Esau in Deuteronomy 2 becomes, in the prophetic tradition, a standard by which Edom's later hostility is judged. The ethical vector cuts in more than one direction.

Finally — a fourth thread — the Rephaim parentheses (vv. 10–12, 20–23) mark Deuteronomy 2 as a key text in biblical traditions about the giants. It links to Genesis 6:4 (the Nephilim before the flood), Numbers 13:33 (the Anakim in Hebron), Joshua 11–15 (Joshua's war against the Anakim), 2 Samuel 21:15–22 (David's heroes killing Rephaim descendants), and — most strikingly — Isaiah 14 and Job 26, where Rephaim denotes the dead in Sheol. Giants in Deuteronomy 2 are not curiosities; they are fragments of a pan-biblical tradition about primeval powers displaced by YHWH in favor of historical peoples — including peoples who are not Israel.
