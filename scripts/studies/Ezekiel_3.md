# Ezekiel 3: The Sweet Scroll, the Watchman, and the Imposed Silence

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political situation**: Continuing the **exilic period (593–571 BC)** call narrative. The setting is Tel-abib, by the Chebar canal, in the fifth year of Jehoiachin's exile (593 BC). The chapter has three movements: the completion of the scroll-eating commission (3:1–11), the prophet's transport to Tel-abib and seven-day silence (3:12–15), the watchman commission (3:16–21), and the imposed silence with cords and tongue-binding (3:22–27). Politically, this is six years before the destruction of Jerusalem (586 BC), and the exilic community at Tel-abib is in a state of *premature triumphalism*: from Jeremiah 28–29 we know the Babylonian community was being told by false prophets like Hananiah and Shemaiah that the exile would be over within two years and that Jehoiachin would return as king. Ezekiel's call inserts him into that contested prophetic landscape — the rebellious house is not just resistant to ethics; it is positively expecting a different message.

**Material culture**: Babylonian exilic community; the *Tel-abib* settlement (3:15) — Akkadian *Til-Abubi* (*"mound of the deluge"*), a place-name suggesting a tell built up over an older Mesopotamian site. The canal-irrigated Nippur landscape continues from chapters 1–2. The watchman imagery (3:17 — *tsofeh*, צֹפֶה) reflects the **fortified-city architecture** of the ANE: every walled city had elevated watchmen on the *migdal* (tower) responsible for sounding the *shofar* (trumpet) at the approach of an enemy. The watchman's failure to sound the alarm was a capital offense in Mesopotamian and Israelite practice (cf. 2 Kings 9:17–20; Hab 2:1; Jer 6:17). The *cords* and *binding* of 3:25 evoke either a literal restraint (some priestly retirement-from-circulation, perhaps a form of mourning seclusion) or a metaphor for divinely-imposed immobility; **Greenberg** and **Block** read the cords symbolically, **Zimmerli** considers a literal Babylonian-context restraint plausible. The **Murashu archive** and **Al-Yahudu tablets** continue to anchor the social setting — the exiles at Tel-abib are documented in the broader cuneiform record as a coherent settled community.

**Authorship & dating**: Ezekiel ben Buzi the priest (1:3), 593 BC. The chapter completes the inaugural call narrative. **Greenberg (Anchor)** treats chapters 1–3 as a single literary unit; **Block (NICOT)** breaks them at the end of chapter 3, with the watchman commission as the structural seam; **Zimmerli (Hermeneia)** identifies redactional layers — the watchman pericope (3:16–21) recurs nearly verbatim at 33:1–9, suggesting either deliberate inclusio or editorial relocation. **Odell** reads the chapter as the inaugural arc completed, with the imposed silence (3:22–27) as the bridge to the sign-act cluster of chapter 4. **Eichrodt (OTL)** stresses the priestly cast of the watchman role — a *priestly* watchman over Israel's covenantal life, not a military one. **Lapsley** and **Smith-Christopher** read the imposed silence trauma-theologically: the prophet's body registers what the community refuses to register.

**Archaeological & textual evidence**: 4QEzek-a is partially preserved at chapter 3 and agrees with MT in the surviving readings. LXX 3:12 has a famously contested variant: *ki berum* (*as the glory rose*) for MT *baruch kevod* (*blessed be the glory*) — a one-letter difference (ב/כ) with significant theological implications, which most modern critical scholars (including Zimmerli) emend toward LXX, though Greenberg and Block defend MT. The watchman ethic (3:17–21) develops material that runs back to Habakkuk 2:1, Jeremiah 6:17, Hosea 9:8, and forward to **Bavli Sanhedrin 73a–b** on the obligation to *prevent transgression* (*tochecha*, rebuke) and **Bavli Shabbat 54b** on collective responsibility (*kol Yisrael areivim zeh la-zeh*).

**Key terms**: *megillah* (מְגִלָּה, "scroll," 3:1–3); *devash* (דְּבַשׁ, "honey," 3:3 — *sweet as honey*); *tsofeh* (צֹפֶה, "watchman," 3:17 — the chapter's signature theological term); *damo mi-yadkha avakkesh* (דָּמוֹ מִיָּדְךָ אֲבַקֵּשׁ, "his blood I will require at your hand," 3:18, 20 — the *blood-requirement* clause that makes the watchman pericope foundational for pastoral ethics); *avot* (עֲבֹתִים, "cords," 3:25); *ilem* (אִלֵּם, "mute," 3:26 — the imposed silence).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: completion of the **prophetic call narrative** (3:1–15) followed by the **watchman commission** (3:16–21) and a third commissioning sequence with **imposed silence** (3:22–27). The chapter is structurally tripartite, and each section has its own genre: ingestion-vision, oracle of office, sign-act announcement. The cumulative effect is to *layer* Ezekiel's vocation: he is sent (ch. 2), nourished (3:1–3), commissioned to a hard audience (3:4–11), traumatized into silence (3:12–15), assigned the watchman office (3:16–21), and then *physically restricted* from speaking unless directly given words (3:22–27). The vocation tightens at every step.

The literary technique features the **leitwort** *bet meri* (*rebellious house*) one final time at 3:9, 26, 27 — closing the inclusio that opened at 2:5. The chapter inaugurates a new leitwort: *tsofeh* (watchman), which will return at 33:1–9 in expanded form. The **honey-sweetness** of the scroll (3:3) is a deliberate paradox — the content is *lamentation, mourning, and woe* (2:10), the audience is *rebellious*, and yet the scroll itself is *sweet*. Block and Greenberg both read the sweetness as the prophet's *delight in the divine word as such* — the content of judgment is bitter; the *fact of being addressed by God* is sweet. Revelation 10:9–10 will pick this up and split the difference: sweet in mouth, bitter in stomach.

The **seven-day silence** at Tel-abib (3:15) is striking. *Mashmim* (overwhelmed, stunned, desolate) is the participial form of the verb that elsewhere describes *desolated land* (Lev 26:31–35; Isa 49:8). Ezekiel sits among the exiles in the posture of the *desolated* — bearing in his body the condition of the people. This is the priestly intercessor or the suffering-with prophet. **Jeremiah's** equivalent is *Jer 8:18–9:2* (the prophet's lament). **Job's** comforters sit seven days in silence (Job 2:13). Ezekiel's seven days place him in the same gestural register: *the magnitude of catastrophe forecloses speech*.

The **watchman discourse** (3:16–21) is a tightly structured legal-pastoral oracle: four cases, balanced antithetically. The wicked who is unwarned (death, blood on the prophet); the wicked who is warned and refuses (death, blood on himself); the righteous who turns and is unwarned (death, blood on the prophet); the righteous who is warned and stays righteous (life). The structure is deliberately complete — the prophet is responsible for all four, and in two of the four his fidelity costs his life. The chapter ends with the **imposed silence** (3:25–27): cords, tongue-binding, voice-rationing. The prophet is reduced to opening his mouth only when the divine word is given — a theological condition that anticipates the silence-broken at 24:27 and 33:22, when news of Jerusalem's fall arrives and the seal lifts.

Textual variants beyond LXX 3:12 are minor. The MT/LXX agreement on the watchman pericope is striking and confirms it as ancient.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Place in the canonical arc: chapter 3 completes the inaugural call cycle and inaugurates the **watchman tradition** that runs through the rest of Ezekiel (33:1–9), through the New Testament (Acts 20:26 — Paul's *I am innocent of the blood of all*), and through the long pastoral-theological history of the church. It also inaugurates the **imposed-silence tradition** that links the prophet to Job's silent comforters, to Habakkuk's tower-watch, and to the contemplative-silence tradition of the desert fathers and the Christian mystics.

**Jewish trajectory**: Sirach 49:8 frames the legacy. The rabbinic tradition reads the watchman pericope as foundational for the *mitzvah of tochecha* (rebuke): one who fails to rebuke the sinning brother bears responsibility for the sin (**Bavli Shabbat 54b–55a**; **Sifra Kedoshim** on Lev 19:17 *thou shalt surely rebuke thy neighbor*). **Bavli Sanhedrin 73a–b** uses the watchman pericope to develop the doctrine of *lo ta'amod al dam re'ekha* (do not stand idly by the blood of your neighbor). Targum Jonathan glosses the *cords* of 3:25 as a metaphor for divine restraint. **Rashi** reads the scroll-sweetness as Ezekiel's *love of the divine command* irrespective of content. **Radak** notes the philological detail. **Maimonides** *Hilchot Teshuvah* incorporates the watchman pericope into the law of repentance-warning. The **medieval mystical tradition** reads the *seven-day silence* as a stage of *gerushim* (mystical-trance immobility) — the prophet's body becoming the site of vision-ingestion. **Levenson**: the watchman office is *priestly*, not military — the prophet stands in the gate of covenant-life. **Sommer**: the *Spirit lifted me up* moments (3:12, 14) are concrete *fluid-divine-self* operations. Modern Jewish ethics consistently makes the watchman pericope foundational: the moral weight of *being-in-position-to-warn*.

**Christian trajectory**: Patristic — **Origen's *Homilies on Ezekiel* (Hom. 3–6)** treats chapter 3 with great theological seriousness, especially the watchman pericope; he reads the *blood-requirement* as foundational for episcopal accountability. **Jerome's *Commentary on Ezekiel*** (414) develops the watchman as a figure for the bishop and the preacher. **Gregory the Great's *Homilies on Ezekiel*** (Book 1, Hom. 9–11) takes the watchman as the central figure of the *cura pastoralis* — the bishop is the watchman who must blow the trumpet, and the failure to blow makes him liable for the blood of his people. This is the source-text of Gregory's *Liber Regulae Pastoralis* and through it of all medieval pastoral theology. **Augustine** in the *Confessions* and in his sermons takes the watchman as the figure of the bishop in his own person: *I am terrified by what I am for you, but I am consoled by what I am with you* (*Sermon 340*) — directly inspired by the watchman pericope. Medieval — **Hugh of St. Victor**, **Richard of St. Victor *De Visione Ezechielis***, **Aquinas** (*ST* II-II q. 33 on fraternal correction draws on this chapter). Reformation — **Calvin's *Lectures on Ezekiel* (1565)** on chapter 3 is one of the most pastorally-directed sections of the work, and the watchman material is foundational for Reformed pastoral office (Westminster Confession 25–28 — the church and its ministry). Critical — Cooke (ICC); **Zimmerli (Hermeneia)** on the redactional history of the watchman pericope (chs. 3 and 33); **Greenberg (Anchor)**; **Block (NICOT)**. Contemporary — **Lapsley (*Can These Bones Live?*)** on the imposed-silence as priestly trauma; **Wilson** on prophetic role-conflict; **Wright** on the pastoral implications. **Bonhoeffer's *Spiritual Care*** is in the same tradition — the watchman as the embodied witness who must speak whether they hear or refuse to hear.

**Where they diverge**: the Jewish tradition has read the watchman pericope as foundational for the *mitzvah of tochecha* and the broader doctrine of communal responsibility (*areivut*); the Christian tradition has read it as foundational for episcopal/pastoral office (*cura animarum*); both share the conviction that being-in-position-to-warn carries irreducible moral weight, but they apply it to different institutional contexts. The Reformed tradition has split the difference, applying the watchman ethic to the preacher's congregational responsibility while resisting clericalization.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The watchman responsibility (3:16–21) — what is the moral weight of *unsuccessful* prophetic ministry?** The blood-requirement clause is one of the heaviest in Scripture: failure to warn makes the prophet *liable for* the death of the unwarned. Successful warning (warning followed by obedience) costs the prophet nothing extra; warning followed by refusal still saves the prophet's life; *not warning* is what is fatal.

**Augustine** (*De Spiritu et Littera*; *Sermon 340*) reads the watchman as the bishop in his existential terror: the office is a burden no one should accept lightly, and the *vocation* is more than a job — it is a *being-in-position* with permanent moral consequence. **Calvin** (*Institutes* 4.1, *Lectures on Ezekiel*) takes the watchman as the structuring image of the Reformed pastoral office: the preacher's responsibility is fidelity in delivering the warning; the congregation's responsibility is response. Neither can be transferred. **Wesley** in his sermons takes the watchman ethic as foundational for the itinerant preacher's accountability. **Karl Barth** (*CD* IV.3) reads the watchman as *the Christian witness* in general — every believer stands in some watchman-position with respect to those around them. **Bonhoeffer's *Spiritual Care*** brings the watchman ethic into modern pastoral practice. The **Catholic *cura pastoralis*** tradition makes the watchman the bishop's defining figure. The least intuitive position to a contemporary egalitarian reader may be the heavy-office reading — *the watchman is a clerical figure, not a universal one*. The steelman: the chapter speaks specifically to a *prophet-priest* in a *covenant-public* role, not to every individual believer. To universalize the watchman ethic without remainder is to flatten the canonical specificity. Augustine's terror is not a generic terror; it is the terror of the man who took up the office. *And the office is worth taking up — but only with eyes open*. Where this leaves us: the watchman pericope is one of the canonical foundations for the gravity of pastoral and prophetic vocation. It also, by structural symmetry, sets a ceiling: warned-and-refused costs the prophet *nothing* — fidelity is not a guarantee of outcome but is, when present, sufficient.

**Tension 2: Imposed silence (3:25–27) — why is Ezekiel *muzzled* until the fall of Jerusalem (33:21–22)?** The bound prophet, with cords on his body and his tongue clinging to the roof of his mouth, is given speech only when YHWH directly opens his mouth. The seven-year silence (broken only by oracular bursts) is theologically unsettling: God *withholds* the prophetic voice from a community that has not heeded it.

The theology of *prophetic silence-as-judgment* runs through the canon: **Amos 8:11–12** ("a famine of hearing the words of the LORD"); **Micah 3:6–7** ("the seers shall be disgraced and the diviners shall be put to shame"); **1 Samuel 3:1** ("the word of the LORD was rare in those days"). The withdrawal of prophetic voice is one of the gravest covenantal sanctions. The **Catholic contemplative-silence tradition** (the desert fathers, the *Cloud of Unknowing*, John of the Cross's *dark night*) reframes the silence positively: the soul is *purified* by the absence of the spoken word, and the deepest knowledge of God is wordless. The **Jewish *hester panim*** tradition (the hiding of the divine face — Deut 31:17–18; cf. Buber, Heschel, Fackenheim, Wiesel) takes silence as the constitutive religious problem of post-exilic and post-Holocaust Judaism: God is silent, and the silence itself must be theologically read. **Endo's *Silence*** (1966) translates this into the Christian-mission context — the apostate's experience of silence is the deepest spiritual problem the novel can name. The least intuitive position to an evangelical Protestant may be the *silence-as-judgment* reading. The steelman: the canonical text really does present silence as a divine action — not the absence of God but a particular mode of divine speech. To miss the silence is to assume that *speaking* is the only mode of presence. Where this leaves us: Ezekiel 3 ends with a prophet *bound*, and the binding is not punishment for Ezekiel but communication to Israel. The community's refusal to hear has earned them a bound prophet — and the binding *is* the prophecy. The body of the silent prophet is the message.

**The unresolved question**: If the silence of the prophet is itself a prophetic act, what should one do in seasons when God seems silent — assume judgment, assume contemplation, assume one is not listening, or assume something else? The chapter does not say.

## The Thread

The scroll *sweet as honey* in 3:3 connects to **Revelation 10:9–10** (sweet in mouth, bitter in stomach) and to **Psalm 119:103** (the Word as honey). The watchman pericope (3:16–21) reappears expanded at **Ezekiel 33:1–9**, is taken up by **Acts 20:26** (Paul: *I am innocent of the blood of all*), and stands behind the long pastoral-theological line from **Augustine's *Sermon 340*** through **Gregory's *Pastoral Rule*** to **Bonhoeffer's *Spiritual Care***. The imposed silence (3:25–27) is broken at **Ezekiel 33:21–22** when the news of Jerusalem's fall arrives — the canonical seal between covenant-warning and covenant-fulfillment, the moment when the prophet's body finally synchronizes with his message.
