# Psalm 3: Morning Prayer of a Hunted King

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Psalm 3 carries the first explicit superscription of the Psalter: *mizmor le-david bivrocho mippnei avshalom beno* — "A Psalm of David, when he fled from Absalom his son." It is the first of seventy-three Davidic-attributed psalms in the MT (LXX adds more), and the first of thirteen psalms with **historical superscriptions** that anchor the song to a specific moment in David's biography (the others are Pss 7, 18, 34, 51, 52, 54, 56, 57, 59, 60, 63, 142). The narrative referenced — David's flight from Absalom across the Kidron and up the Mount of Olives — is told in 2 Samuel 15-18, the most psychologically searching narrative in the Deuteronomistic History: a king betrayed by his beloved son, ascending the ridge weeping and barefoot, hearing curses thrown at him by Shimei, and forbidden by his counselors to fight in the battle that will end with Absalom dead and David hollowed out by grief.

Form-critically, Psalm 3 is an **individual lament** in Hermann Gunkel's *Einleitung in die Psalmen* (1933) classification. Gunkel grouped it with Pss 5, 6, 7, 13, 17, 22, 25, 26, 27, 28, 31, 35, 38, 39, 42-43, 51, 54-57, 59, 61, 63, 64, 69, 70, 71, 86, 88, 102, 109, 120, 130, 140, 141, 142, 143 — the largest single category in the Psalter, accounting for roughly a third of the book. Claus Westermann in *Praise and Lament in the Psalms* (1965) refined Gunkel's analysis and identified the canonical structural elements of an individual lament: (1) **invocation** of Yahweh by name; (2) **complaint** in three directions (against enemies, against self, against God); (3) **petition** for deliverance; (4) **motivation** — reasons God should act; (5) **vow of praise** or **assurance of being heard**. Psalm 3 contains all five elements in compressed form: invocation in v. 1a, complaint in vv. 1b-2, statement of trust in vv. 3-6, petition in v. 7a, motivation/imprecation in v. 7b, and confession of confidence in v. 8.

Date and *Sitz im Leben* are debated. The historical superscription claims a Davidic-monarchic context. Many critical scholars treat the superscriptions as later editorial assignments rather than authorial colophons — Gunkel and Mowinckel saw them as post-exilic glosses by editors who matched generic laments to remembered Davidic episodes. **Brevard Childs** in *Introduction to the Old Testament as Scripture* (1979) argued, in a now-influential essay on the superscriptions, that the historical headings belong to the *canonical text* — they were the editors' way of teaching the reader how the psalm was to be read, supplying narrative contexts that turned timeless laments into Davidic memoirs. Childs's point is not that the psalm was actually composed by David in 2 Sam 15 but that the canonical reading instructs us to read the psalm *through* that narrative. The contemporary scholarly consensus follows a moderate Childs-influenced position: the psalms with historical superscriptions are best understood as canonically situated, even when their original composition is unknown.

The material culture: the *holy hill* (*har kodsho*, 3:4) is Mount Zion, where the ark resided (David had brought it to Jerusalem in 2 Sam 6) and where Yahweh was understood to "answer" prayers. In the Absalom narrative, David has *left* the holy hill — he has crossed the Kidron and is east of the city, exposed and vulnerable, while Absalom occupies Jerusalem. The psalm's confession that Yahweh "answers from his holy hill" carries dramatic weight: the king has lost the city but not the God of the city. ANE parallels include Mesopotamian *šigû* and *eršahungu* lament prayers (the penitent prays to a personal god from a position of besieged exposure), Egyptian morning hymns to Re (rising-with-the-sun confession of survival), and Ugaritic ritual texts in which the king prays at dawn for protection.

Hebrew terms controlling the psalm:

- **סֶלָה** (*selah*, 3:2, 4, 8) — appears here for the **first time in the Psalter**. The word's meaning is among the oldest unsolved questions in Psalter scholarship. Major proposals: (1) musical-pause indicator (LXX *diapsalma*, "interlude"); (2) *salal* root meaning "to lift up" — call to raise voice or instruments; (3) Sigmund Mowinckel's proposal in *The Psalms in Israel's Worship* — *selah* signals a moment of cultic enactment, perhaps a prostration or a specific liturgical gesture; (4) *qere* / *qara* root meaning "to recite" or "repeat" — a call to amplify; (5) honest contemporary admission (Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay) that *we do not know*. The most defensible scholarly position is uncertainty with a probable musical or liturgical function.
- **כָּבוֹד** (*kavod*, 3:3) — "glory." A theologically dense word: Yahweh's *kavod* is his weighty manifest presence (Exod 33:18-23), the cloud over the tabernacle (Exod 40:34-35), the pillar of fire. To call Yahweh "my *kavod*" is to claim that the divine presence belongs to the speaker — a remarkably bold confession in a hunted king's prayer.
- **מָגֵן** (*magen*, 3:3) — "shield." The small round buckler used by foot soldiers, distinct from the larger *tzinnah*. The Genesis 15:1 promise — "I am your shield, your reward shall be very great" — establishes the metaphor; David picks it up here.
- **יְשׁוּעָה** (*yeshu'ah*, 3:8, also v. 2) — "salvation." From the root *yasha* (יָשַׁע), to deliver, save, rescue. This is the etymological root of the personal name *Yehoshua* (Joshua) and its later contracted form *Yeshua* — the Hebrew name behind Greek *Iesous*, English Jesus. The psalm's pivot — "no *yeshu'ah* for him in God" (the enemies' taunt, v. 2) becoming *la-Yhwh ha-yeshu'ah* (salvation belongs to Yahweh, v. 8) — is the controlling theological move of the entire prayer.
- **בָּרֲכָתֶךָ** (*birkhatekha*, 3:8) — "your blessing." The prayer turns at the end from individual petition to corporate intercession: *upon your people*. The hunted king ends with the well-being of the nation that is currently hunting him.

Textual evidence: Psalm 3 is preserved in Dead Sea Scrolls fragments **4QPsa** and **4QPsq** in forms substantially identical to MT. The LXX renders the psalm with minor variations and translates *selah* as *diapsalma*. The historical superscription is present in MT, LXX, and the Qumran witnesses — it was therefore a stable canonical feature by the late Second Temple period.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Psalm 3 is eight verses divided by three *selah* markers into four strophes of two verses each — the cleanest *selah* structure in the entire Psalter. The strophic shape:

- **vv. 1-2** — complaint about the enemies (closes with *selah*)
- **vv. 3-4** — confession of Yahweh as shield, prayer answered from the holy hill (closes with *selah*)
- **vv. 5-6** — confidence in sustenance through the night, fearlessness despite the surrounding multitudes
- **vv. 7-8** — petition for deliverance, imprecation against enemies, closing benediction (closes with *selah*)

Robert Lowth's parallelism categories appear throughout. Synonymous parallelism: "many are my foes / many are rising against me" (3:1). Climactic intensification: "many are my foes / many are rising against me / many are saying of my soul" (vv. 1-2) — the Hebrew word *rabbim* hammered three times. Antithetic: the enemies' "no salvation in God" (v. 2) against the psalmist's "salvation belongs to Yahweh" (v. 8) — the psalm is held together by this *inclusio* of contradicted claims about *yeshu'ah*. Synthetic: "I lay down and slept / I woke again / for Yahweh sustained me" (v. 5).

The **inclusio** between the enemies' taunt in v. 2 and the speaker's confession in v. 8 is the structural center of the psalm — the entire prayer is the move from enemy speech to divine truth. James Kugel and Robert Alter have both noted that lament psalms typically pivot from complaint to confidence at a structural midpoint; Westermann calls this the *Stimmungsumschwung* (mood-shift). In Psalm 3 the pivot is unusually fast — already at v. 3 ("but you, O LORD") — and the rest of the psalm is the unfolding of that pivot.

The psalm contains a rare *imprecatory* element in v. 7: "you strike all my enemies on the cheek; you break the teeth of the wicked." The cheek-strike (*hakkita lechi*) was an act of public humiliation — the enemy disarmed and disgraced. Breaking the teeth (*shinnei resha'im shibbarta*) renders the enemy unable to bite, unable to consume the speaker. The verbs are *qatal* (perfect-aspect), which can be read as "you have struck" (past, completed) or as a *prophetic perfect* declaring the future as already accomplished. Robert Alter, in *The Book of Psalms: A Translation with Commentary* (2007), translates as past completed action — the speaker is recalling Yahweh's prior deliverances as the ground of his current petition. The imprecation is concrete, physical, and undisguised.

The **morning psalm** tradition pairs Psalm 3 with Psalm 4 (the evening counterpart) on the strength of v. 5 — "I lay down and slept; I woke again, for Yahweh sustained me." In rabbinic and patristic tradition, Pss 3 and 4 became the morning-evening pair recited as part of personal devotion before and after sleep. The image of falling asleep in a war-camp east of Jerusalem with Absalom's army hunting the speaker, and waking the next morning still alive, is the concrete situational image behind v. 5.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Placement

Psalm 3 opens Book I after the programmatic gateway of Pss 1-2. The editorial logic is striking: Ps 1 promises that *the way of the wicked perishes*, Ps 2 declares Yahweh's anointed king as the agent of that judgment, and then Ps 3 — without warning — places the anointed king on the run from his own son, surrounded by enemies, taunted with the claim that *there is no salvation for him in God*. The Psalter's first move after its programmatic introduction is to plunge its messianic hero into the very disorientation Pss 1-2 seemed to rule out. **Walter Brueggemann's** orientation/disorientation/reorientation grid begins to operate immediately: Pss 1-2 are orientation, Ps 3 is disorientation, and the rest of Book I will work out the back-and-forth.

Gerald Wilson and the canonical-shaping school have noted that Pss 3-7 form a tight cluster of Davidic individual laments that establish the lament-shape of Book I. The Psalter is teaching its reader, almost from the first page, that being the anointed does not exempt one from being hunted.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve Ps 3 in **4QPsa** and **4QPsq** with the historical superscription intact, indicating that the Davidic-Absalom reading was canonical by at least the second century BCE. **Midrash Tehillim** on Psalm 3 expands the Absalom narrative imaginatively: the rabbis read v. 5 ("I lay down and slept") as a miracle — David's sleep amid mortal danger was Yahweh's gift, and the morning rising was a small resurrection. **Rashi** (eleventh century) reads the psalm strictly historically, anchored in 2 Sam 15-18, and treats v. 7's imprecation as referring to Absalom's actual death by Joab's spear. **Ibn Ezra** (twelfth century) follows Rashi but adds that David's prayer is a paradigm for any believer hunted by enemies. **Radak (David Kimhi)** treats *kavod* in v. 3 as Yahweh's protective glory hovering over the king. **Nachmanides** reads the psalm as the soul's prayer in any besieged condition. **Avivah Zornberg's** treatment of David in *The Murmuring Deep* draws on Psalm 3 as the paradigmatic moment of Davidic vulnerability — the king as one who has lost everything and prays from the dirt.

Liturgically, Psalm 3 is part of **kriat shema al ha-mitah** — the bedtime *Shema* prayer — in the Sephardic and some Ashkenazi rites, alongside Ps 4. The pairing of Pss 3 (morning) and 4 (evening) is preserved in Jewish bedtime liturgy with the pairing inverted — both psalms are recited together before sleep, as protection through the dark hours. Psalm 3 is **not** part of the *Shir Shel Yom* daily cycle (Sun=24, Mon=48, Tue=82, Wed=94, Thu=81, Fri=93, Sat=92).

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

**Augustine's** *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 3 reads the psalm Christologically: David fleeing Absalom is Christ persecuted by Judas (Augustine notes that Absalom-the-traitor-son maps onto Judas-the-traitor-disciple), and v. 5's "I lay down and slept; I woke again" is read explicitly as the **death and resurrection of Christ**. Augustine's reading became the standard patristic understanding — Pss 3 and 4 as resurrection psalms, Ps 3 as Easter morning's psalm — and shaped the Christian liturgical use of the psalm. **Athanasius's** *Letter to Marcellinus* recommends Ps 3 for the soul under persecution. **Cassiodorus** systematizes. **Aquinas** reads it fourfold.

**Luther's** *Operationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 3 treats v. 5 as the central verse — the believer's daily death-and-resurrection in baptismal-existential terms. **Calvin's** 1557 commentary reads the psalm as the model for the Christian under affliction: the immediate pivot from complaint to trust at v. 3 is the paradigm of how the believing heart moves under pressure. The historical-critical tradition (**Gunkel, Mowinckel, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate, Allen, Wilson**) has placed the psalm in the lament category and explored its cultic *Sitz*. **Brueggemann** (*The Message of the Psalms*, *The Costly Loss of Lament*) treats it as a paradigmatic individual lament and emphasizes the speed of its pivot to trust. **Patrick Miller**, **Beth Tanner**, and **William P. Brown** all build on the canonical-shaping reading to emphasize Ps 3's location as the first Davidic prayer of the Psalter.

**Liturgical use**: Psalm 3 is the **first psalm of Matins (the morning office) in the Roman Breviary** and in the Benedictine Rule (chapter 9 of the Rule of St. Benedict prescribes Ps 3 as the first psalm of Vigils, recited daily — "*Domine, quid multiplicati sunt*"). Bonhoeffer in *Psalms: The Prayer Book of the Bible* makes Pss 3-4 central to his treatment of the daily office. Christian morning prayer in the West has been opened with Psalm 3 for fifteen hundred years.

### NT Use

Psalm 3 is not directly quoted in the New Testament, but v. 8's *la-Yhwh ha-yeshu'ah* ("salvation belongs to Yahweh") echoes throughout Acts, Hebrews, and Revelation as the paradigmatic Christian confession.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author's vision is that the speaker — anointed, hunted, surrounded — has located *yeshu'ah* not in his own resources, not in armies, not in the city he has lost, but in Yahweh alone. The psalm's controlling move is the contradiction of the enemies' claim. They say: "no salvation for him in God." He says: "salvation belongs to Yahweh." Everything else in the psalm hangs on which sentence is true.

### Tension One: The Historical Reliability of the Davidic Superscriptions

Should we read Psalm 3 as actually composed by David during the Absalom episode, or as a generic individual lament canonically *associated* with that narrative by later editors?

- **Conservative-traditional reading (older confessional Protestant, traditional Jewish)**: David composed the psalm in or shortly after the Absalom flight. The superscription is authorial and historically reliable. The psalm is therefore a window into the actual interior life of the historical David at a specific moment.
- **Brevard Childs's canonical reading** (*Introduction to the Old Testament as Scripture*, 1979): the historical superscriptions are part of the canonical text and were placed there by editors to teach the reader how to read the psalm. Whether or not David actually composed it on that occasion is irrelevant to its canonical meaning. The editors are saying: *read this psalm through the lens of 2 Sam 15-18.* This is now the dominant scholarly position among canonical readers.
- **Mainstream historical-critical reading (Gunkel, Mowinckel, contemporary critical commentaries)**: the superscriptions are post-exilic editorial additions, often made by analogy or association rather than from historical knowledge. The psalms themselves are typically generic — composable on many occasions — and the editors retrofitted them onto Davidic episodes.
- **Pseudepigraphic-attributive reading (some critical scholars)**: the *le-david* attribution may not even mean "by David" but "for David," "concerning David," or "in the Davidic style." The Hebrew preposition *lamed* permits all these readings.
- **Conservative-evangelical mediating position (Goldingay, Longman)**: Davidic authorship of at least some Davidic-superscriptioned psalms is plausible; for others, association is more likely. Each psalm should be assessed individually. The superscriptions carry historical weight without being forensically reliable.
- **Jewish reading (Rashi, modern scholars like Sarna)**: the historical superscriptions are taken seriously as locating the psalm within the David narrative, but the Talmud (*Pesahim* 117a) already discussed the question of how David could have composed psalms about events that happened to him — the rabbis themselves treated the attributions with sophistication.

**Steelmanning the least-intuitive position** — Childs's canonical reading. To Western readers trained to ask "did this actually happen?" and "who actually wrote this?" the canonical reading sounds evasive — as if the historical question is being dodged. But Childs's point is more rigorous than evasion. He argues that we possess the psalm only as the canonical text has handed it to us, and the canonical text comes with its superscription attached. The editors have already made an interpretive decision: this psalm is to be read in the context of David's flight from Absalom. That editorial decision is not an extraneous gloss; it is *part of the text*. To strip the superscription off and read the psalm "in its original form" is to read a hypothetical reconstructed text that no community has ever received. Childs is defending the textual reality we actually have, not a positivist historical reconstruction we will never reach.

**Where this leaves us**: The honest position is that we do not know whether David composed Psalm 3 during the Absalom flight, but we *do* know that the canonical text instructs us to read it in that light. The question "what does the psalm mean?" is best answered not by adjudicating historical authorship but by reading the psalm with its superscription as the editorial hermeneutical key — which is also the way every Jewish and Christian reader for two and a half millennia has received it.

### Tension Two: The Imprecation of v. 7

"You strike all my enemies on the cheek; you break the teeth of the wicked." Is this acceptable Christian prayer?

- **Augustinian-spiritualizing reading**: the enemies are spiritual — sin, the devil, the principalities. The cheek-strike and teeth-breaking are real, but their object is not flesh and blood. This is the dominant patristic and medieval Christian reading.
- **Davidic-historical reading (Calvin, Rashi)**: the enemies are Absalom's forces, and the imprecation is a real prayer for their physical defeat — which 2 Sam 18 narrates Yahweh granting. The psalm is honest about what the king is praying for. Christian readers must reckon with this honesty rather than allegorize it away.
- **Brueggemann's reading** (*The Message of the Psalms*, *The Costly Loss of Lament*): the imprecation is the speaker's *raw rage* given to God in prayer rather than enacted in violence. The prayer is the alternative to the action. To pray "break their teeth" is precisely *not* to break their teeth oneself. The psalm is a model of how a hunted person processes murderous anger — by speaking it to God, not by acting on it.
- **Pacifist / Anabaptist reading (Yoder, Hauerwas)**: the imprecation is the cry of the victim demanding God's justice, and the Christian inherits it as the cry of the persecuted church. Praying it is licit; enacting it is not.
- **Critical-honest reading (Gunkel, Mowinckel, contemporary critical scholars)**: the imprecation reflects ancient Near Eastern royal ideology and the assumption that the king's enemies are God's enemies. It cannot be sanitized. Christian readers may pray it as the cry of the oppressed, but they must reckon with its embeddedness in royal-violence frameworks.
- **Erich Zenger's reading** (*A God of Vengeance? Understanding the Psalms of Divine Wrath*, 1994): imprecatory verses are *protests against violence* — they hand violence to God so that the speaker does not have to enact it. The cheek-strike and teeth-breaking are real, but they are entrusted to divine justice, not to the speaker's hand.
- **Liberation reading (Gutiérrez)**: the imprecation is the cry of the powerless who have nowhere else to take their grievance. In a world without justice, the imprecatory psalms are the protest-prayer of the oppressed. The colonizer who reads them as authorizing his violence has inverted them.

**Where this leaves us**: The imprecation in v. 7 is real and cannot be allegorized away. But Brueggemann and Zenger are right that the prayer is the alternative to the action — David in the canonical narrative does *not* personally strike Absalom; he forbids his men to harm "the young man Absalom" (2 Sam 18:5) and weeps when he hears of the death. The psalm's cheek-strike is therefore prayer-handed-over-to-God, not vengeance-enacted-by-the-king. The Christian who prays this verse honestly is acknowledging rage and giving it to God rather than acting on it.

### Closing Question

If Psalm 3 is the first prayer of the anointed king after the programmatic Pss 1-2 — and that prayer is uttered while the king is hunted, surrounded, and taunted that God has abandoned him — what does the editor mean by placing it here? Is the Psalter telling us that being anointed is what *gets* you hunted, or that being hunted is the only condition under which the anointed's prayer can be heard?

## The Thread

- **2 Samuel 15-18** — the Absalom narrative referenced by the superscription. The crossing of the Kidron, the ascent of the Mount of Olives barefoot and weeping, Shimei's curses, the battle in the forest of Ephraim, Absalom's death, David's grief in the gate. Without this narrative the psalm hovers; with it, the psalm is grounded in some of the most psychologically searching prose in the Hebrew Bible.
- **Psalm 4** — the evening-prayer counterpart. Pss 3-4 form a morning/evening pair in both Christian and Jewish bedtime liturgical traditions. Read together they bracket the day in Yahweh's keeping.
- **Psalm 22** — the Davidic lament that begins "my God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" — taking up the same complaint that v. 2's enemies make against the psalmist in Ps 3 and pushing it past the limit. Where Ps 3 contradicts the enemies' claim quickly (v. 3), Ps 22 lets it stand for twenty-one verses before the pivot.
- **Mark 4:35-41** — Jesus asleep in the storm-tossed boat. The disciples' panic ("teacher, do you not care that we are perishing?") and Jesus' sleep echo the same dynamic as Ps 3:5: the chosen one sleeps in danger because Yahweh sustains.
