# 1 Kings 15: A Lamp in Jerusalem, A Conspiracy at Gibbethon

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter is a synchronistic ledger covering, in compressed form, three reigns in Judah (Abijam, Asa, the closing notice for Asa) and two in Israel (Nadab, Baasha's accession). Chronology is hard. Asa's forty-one-year reign (15:10) overlaps four northern kings (Jeroboam, Nadab, Baasha, Elah, Zimri, Omri, into Ahab). **Edwin Thiele**'s system (*The Mysterious Numbers of the Hebrew Kings*) places Asa c. 911–870 BCE; **Gershon Galil** (*The Chronology of the Kings of Israel and Judah*) prefers slightly different brackets; **Mordechai Cogan** in the AB *1 Kings* notes that the synchronisms in chapter 15 are internally consistent if one allows non-accession-year reckoning in the north and accession-year reckoning in the south, which Thiele proposed and most commentators since have accepted with modifications.

The political theater is the **Ramah border standoff** (15:17). Ramah (modern er-Ram, about 8 km north of Jerusalem) sits astride the central ridge road and the Benjaminite-Ephraimite frontier. Baasha's fortification of Ramah is a strategic chokehold on Jerusalem's communication and trade. **Nadav Naʾaman** has discussed the long-running Israelite-Judahite frontier wars in the central Benjamin plateau; the strategic geography is unchanged from the Saul-David frontier of the previous century. Asa's response — buying off Ben-hadad I of Aram-Damascus to attack the Galilee from the rear — opens a second front. The text names the towns Aram strikes: **Ijon** (Tell ed-Dibbin in the Hula valley), **Dan** (Tel Dan, Biran's excavations), **Abel-beth-maacah** (Tell Abil el-Qamh, recent excavations by Robert Mullins and Naama Yahalom-Mack at Azusa Pacific / Hebrew University), and "all Chinneroth" — the western Galilee littoral including Tel Kinrot. The geographical specificity is characteristic of dtr's annalistic source-handling: this is military reportage, probably from the "Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Judah."

**Ben-hadad I** of Aram-Damascus is named with three-generation pedigree (15:18): "son of Tabrimmon, son of Hezion." The Aramaic name *Tabrimmon* contains the divine element **Rimmon** / **Hadad-Rammanu**, the storm god — paralleled in the **Melqart Stele / Bar-Hadad inscription** (originally read by Albright as a dedication by Ben-hadad I to Melqart, c. 9th c. BCE; the reading is now contested — **Wayne Pitard** has argued the dating and dynastic identification are uncertain, but the inscription remains an Aramaic royal text from the relevant horizon). The **Tel Dan Stele** (Biran 1993, Aramaic, late 9th c.) is by Hazael, Ben-hadad's later successor or relation — but it situates the Aramean royal house in the same diplomatic-military matrix the chapter assumes.

Material culture and cult: **Maacah's "abominable image for Asherah"** (15:13) is *miflṣt*, a hapax legomenon often translated "horrid thing" or "abomination" — possibly a phallic or fertility symbol, possibly a wooden carving. **Susan Ackerman** (*Warrior, Dancer, Beloved, Queen*) reads the queen-mother (*gəbîrāh*) role as the ritually-empowered patron of Asherah cult; Maacah is therefore not a private idolater but the *holder of an institutional cult office* whose abolition is a structural reform. The episode resonates with the Khirbet el-Qom and Kuntillet ʿAjrud "YHWH and his Asherah" inscriptions — Asherah was integrated into Iron II Yahwistic practice at popular and royal levels. Asa burns the image "at the brook Kidron" (15:13) — the same wadi where Josiah will later burn cult objects (2 Kgs 23:6, 12), establishing a literary parallel the dtr surely intends.

Hebrew terms. **נֵר** *nēr* (15:4) — "lamp" — the Davidic lamp motif (cf. 11:36, 2 Kgs 8:19, Ps 132:17), royal-ideological imagery probably drawn from the perpetual sanctuary lamp (Lev 24). **גְּבִירָה** *gəbîrāh* (15:13) — "queen mother," the highest non-royal office in the Judahite court; **Niels-Erik Andreasen** and **Susan Ackerman** are the standard references. **קְדֵשִׁים** *qədēšîm* (15:12) — "consecrated males," translated "male cult prostitutes" but the cultic-sexual content is debated (see ch. 14 commentary). **בָּמוֹת** *bāmôt* — high places — Asa removes Asherah-images but not the high places; the dtr will repeat this incomplete-reform note for Jehoshaphat, Joash, Amaziah, Azariah, and Jotham (the "good but incomplete" pattern, **Iain Provan** has tabulated). **חָלָה בְרַגְלָיו** *ḥālāh bə-raglāyw* (15:23) — "diseased in his feet" — possibly a euphemism (the LXX had a tradition of reading "feet" euphemistically), possibly literal (gout, peripheral vascular disease, leprosy). The Chronicler (2 Chr 16:12) sharpens this into a moral indictment: Asa "did not seek YHWH but the physicians."

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is **regnal-formula machinery** — what **Steven McKenzie** has called the "synoptic" structure of Kings — in dense form. Five reigns or partial reigns are accounted for in 34 verses. The dtr's formula is visible: accession synchronism, length of reign, mother's name (Judah only — a southern court archive feature absent from Israelite tradition), evaluation by the David-standard, source citation, death-and-burial notice, succession notice. **Cogan** has dissected the formula into its slots; the chapter is best read as a sequence of formula-slots interrupted by two longer narrative episodes (the Maacah cult-reform, 15:13; the Ramah-Aram war, 15:16–22).

Genre precision: this is **dtr historiography** in its most administrative mode. The longer Elijah-Elisha narratives are stylistic departures *from* this baseline; chapter 15 is the baseline. **Robert Polzin** has argued (*Samuel and the Deuteronomist*, *David and the Deuteronomist*) that the dtr's regnal formula is itself a theological device — the very repetition of the formula enacts the inevitability of judgment, the structural sameness of human kingship under covenant. To read chapter 15 as "boring" is to miss the point: the boredom is the theology.

The literary device of greatest interest is the **Davidic-lamp parenthesis** (15:4–5): "Nevertheless, for David's sake the LORD his God gave him a lamp in Jerusalem… because David did what was right in the eyes of the LORD and did not turn aside from anything that he commanded him all the days of his life, **except in the matter of Uriah the Hittite**." That final clause is the dtr's only explicit acknowledgment in the regnal-formula material that David's career contained one moral exception. **Walsh** (Berit Olam) reads this parenthesis as the dtr's careful theological balancing act: the Davidic dynasty persists because of David's overall fidelity, but the dtr will not pretend David was sinless. **Provan** notes that the dtr nowhere references David's other moral failures (the census, the deathbed enemies-list); only the Uriah affair survives as the canonical exception. **Brueggemann** writes that this is the dtr's "honest theological grammar": grace is not earned by perfection, but neither is fidelity erased by particular sin.

The **Maacah cult-reform** scene (15:13) is unusual in the regnal-formula material for its concrete narrative detail — *gəbîrāh*, *miflṣt*, brook Kidron. The compression is deliberate: a major cultic and political action (deposition of the queen mother) is reported in eight words of Hebrew. **Sweeney (OTL)** reads this as a deliberate literary signal: the cult-reform of a southern king is sufficient to merit narrative detail; the dynastic-termination violence in the north (Nadab's assassination, the extermination of Jeroboam's house) is reported in equally compressed but morally inverted form. The chapter is a study in narrative parallelism between southern reform and northern violence.

The **Nadab-Baasha sequence** (15:25–32) introduces the chapter's second longer episode and the dtr's first **dynastic extermination** report (the prospective curse from 14:10–11 now activated): "He left to the house of Jeroboam not one that breathed, until he had destroyed it, according to the word of the LORD that he spoke by his servant Ahijah the Shilonite" (15:29). The literary device is **prophecy-fulfillment notice** — the dtr's signature theological device, mapping every major political event to a prior prophetic word.

Textual variants: the LXX has variant chronological numbers in several verses; **4QKgs** preserves fragments. The Lucianic recension occasionally diverges. **Julio Trebolle Barrera**'s *Centena in Libros Samuelis et Regum* catalogues the variants. None is meaning-altering for the chapter's basic argument.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish trajectory.** **Josephus** (*Ant.* 8.286–303) preserves the Asa-Ben-hadad alliance with extra detail, treating Asa as an exemplar of pious kingship complicated by the political realism of mercenary diplomacy. The rabbis are mixed. *b. Sotah* 10b discusses Asa's foot disease as punishment for conscripting "even priests and Torah scholars" into the Geba-Mizpah construction project (15:22, "none was exempt"). **Rashi** glosses the *miflṣt* as a phallic image and emphasizes Asa's willingness to depose his own mother as a model of public virtue overriding private piety. **Radak** treats the Aramean alliance as a failure: better to trust YHWH for deliverance from Baasha than to bribe a Gentile king. **Ralbag** is more pragmatic: the alliance worked, and worked acts deserve qualified moral credit. **Abarbanel**, with his statesman's eye, treats chapter 15 as a case study in how reform is always partial — Asa removed the queen-mother's idol but not the high places, and the dtr is honest about the limit. **Yair Zakovitch** reads the Maacah deposition as the rare moment when the Hebrew Bible records a queen-mother removed by her own son for cultic reasons — a structural anomaly worth dwelling on. The Chronicler (2 Chr 14–16) expands this material substantially, adding the **Cushite invasion** under Zerah (2 Chr 14:9–15) and the prophet **Hanani's rebuke** (2 Chr 16:7–10) for the Aramean alliance — material the dtr does not include. **Sara Japhet**'s commentary on Chronicles is the standard reference for understanding how the Chronicler's expansion is itself a midrashic extension of the dtr's compressed Asa account.

**Christian trajectory.** **Theodoret of Cyrus** (*Quaestiones in Reges*) treats Asa as a positive type of the Christian ruler: zealous for purification, willing to depose a powerful family member when piety demands it, but limited by historical inheritance (the high places persist, as the residue of older sin). **Gregory the Great** (*Moralia*) reads the foot disease as a moral allegory: Asa's "feet" — his late-life walk, his praxis — falter even as his heart remains true; spiritual perfection is uneven across the life span. **Aquinas** cites Asa's deposition of Maacah in discussions of whether civil rulers may discipline their parents (*ST* II-II 102), concluding that the lower obligation (filial piety) yields to the higher (covenant fidelity). **Calvin's Sermons on 1 Kings** treat Asa across multiple sermons, reading the Aramean alliance as Asa's "Petrine moment" — the otherwise faithful king who, under pressure, leans on the wrong support. Calvin's pastoral instinct is sharp here: Asa is not condemned for the alliance; he is described, and the description carries its own warning. **Luther** uses the Maacah reform to argue against the early-modern toleration of "old idolatries" in newly Reformed territories: better the queen-mother be deposed than the image preserved.

Critical scholarship: **Cogan (AB)** sees in chapter 15 a careful interweaving of two annalistic sources (one Judahite, one Israelite) into the dtr's formulaic frame. **Sweeney (OTL)** emphasizes the chapter as the dtr's first deployment of the prophecy-fulfillment formula on the new template (Ahijah's word against Jeroboam, 14:10–11, fulfilled 15:29). **Frank Moore Cross** assigned the Davidic-lamp parenthesis (15:4–5) to **Dtr1** as part of the Josianic edition's promotion of Davidic theology. **Knoppers** (*Two Nations Under God*) reads the parallel structure of chapter 15 — Asa good but incomplete in the south, Nadab evil and exterminated in the north — as the dtr's argument for the systemic asymmetry of the two kingdoms: the south can produce good kings (because of David), the north cannot (because of Jeroboam). **Brueggemann** notes the irony that Baasha, whose accession fulfills YHWH's word against Jeroboam, is himself condemned for the very sins he was raised up to punish (16:1–4) — the dtr's prophetic instrument is itself indicted. **Eugene Peterson** writes that chapter 15 teaches that "purification has its limits" — Asa's heart is true, the reform is real, the high places remain. **Iain Provan** has tabulated the "good king with high-places-remaining" pattern across the Judahite list. **Robert Coote** (*In Defense of Revolution*) reads the Baasha-Nadab coup as a foreshadowing of the Jehu revolution: prophet-authorized regicide as legitimate political theology, a tradition the dtr inherits and complicates. Feminist scholarship: **Susan Ackerman** is the central interpreter of the Maacah deposition; **Athalya Brenner** has noted the unusual narrative density given to a queen-mother in this otherwise austere regnal material. **Tikva Frymer-Kensky** treats the *gəbîrāh* office as one of the few institutionalized female positions in Iron Age Judah and as therefore politically significant when removed.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The Davidic-lamp principle.** Verse 4 introduces the dtr's most theologically loaded sentence: "**Nevertheless, for David's sake the LORD his God gave him a lamp in Jerusalem.**" Three positions:

(a) **Strict Davidic covenant**: The promise to David in 2 Sam 7 is unconditional with respect to the dynasty's continuance, conditional only with respect to individual kings' chastisement. The lamp persists *because of David*, regardless of Abijam's worth (Walsh, classical Reformed and Catholic dynastic readings).

(b) **Dynastic conditional**: Even the "lamp for David" is contingent on continued covenant participation by the dynasty; Abijam survives because he is *within* the line, not because he merits, but the *line itself* is conditional (Sweeney's qualified reading).

(c) **Dtr theological retrojection**: The "lamp" formula is the dtr's seventh-century construction reading the survival of the south against the fall of the north and back-projecting the explanatory principle. The principle is doing the work of theodicy — explaining why YHWH let Jerusalem stand longer than Samaria (Cross, Nelson).

Steelman the third: the dtr is writing into a real historical asymmetry. The north fell in 722 BCE; the south lasted to 587. The "lamp" is not a naïve hereditary principle — it is the dtr's earned theological language for the fact that the southern dynasty did, empirically, last longer. To call this retrojection is not to dismiss it; it is to recognize that the language was forged in the act of historical interpretation.

**Where this leaves us:** the principle that grace can persist *for the sake of another* (here, for David's sake) is foundational to later biblical and Christian soteriological vocabulary. But the chapter is sober: the lamp persists; Abijam is not made righteous by its persistence. Grace and judgment coexist within the same lineage.

**Tension 2: The Aramean alliance.** Asa, evaluated as wholly true (15:14), bribes Ben-hadad to break a treaty and attack Israel. Three positions:

(a) **Realpolitik vindication**: The alliance worked, Israel withdrew, Judah survived. Outcomes matter; the dtr does not condemn (Ralbag, modern political-theological readers).

(b) **Quiet condemnation**: The dtr lets Asa's later foot-disease (15:23) stand as an oblique judgment; the Chronicler makes explicit what the dtr leaves implicit (Hanani's rebuke, 2 Chr 16:7–10).

(c) **Theological neutrality with structural limit**: The dtr reports without verdict; the alliance is neither commended nor condemned, and the reader is left to weigh the cost (Brueggemann, Peterson).

Steelman the second: the Chronicler is not adding a foreign moral; he is making explicit the dtr's implicit logic — when Judah leans on Aram, Judah forfeits the deeper trust in YHWH the dtr otherwise commends. Asa's late-life disease, on this reading, is not narrative coincidence.

**Where this leaves us:** the relationship between political pragmatism and covenant fidelity is the chapter's running unresolved problem. The alliance worked. The reform persisted. The high places remained. The king's feet failed him in the end. The dtr neither vindicates nor condemns. The reader is left holding the ambiguity. **Unresolved question: When does the use of foreign power as instrument of national survival become unfaithfulness — and is the chapter giving us a principle, or just a case?**

## The Thread

- **2 Samuel 7** — the foundational Davidic covenant, the source-text for the "lamp" theology of 15:4.
- **2 Samuel 11–12** — the Uriah affair, the only sin the dtr will name in his David-evaluation; 15:5 is the canonical citation point.
- **2 Chronicles 14–16** — the Chronicler's expanded Asa narrative, which adds Zerah the Cushite, Azariah son of Oded, and Hanani the seer; flagged for comparative reading.
- **2 Kings 23** — Josiah's reform, including the burning of cultic objects at Kidron (23:6, 12) — the deliberate dtr echo of Asa's earlier reform at the same wadi, signaling that reform is not novel but a recurring covenant possibility.
