# Matthew 22: Five Tests in the Temple Court

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Matthew 22 unfolds entirely within the precincts of Herod's Temple, in the days between Jesus's triumphal entry and the Olivet Discourse. The setting is most plausibly the **Court of the Gentiles** — the broad outer plaza of the *hierón* enclosed by Herod's colonnaded porticoes — where rabbis customarily taught their disciples and where any Jew could engage a teacher in disputation. Solomon's Portico along the eastern edge (cf. John 10:23, Acts 3:11) is the traditional location. Josephus (*Antiquities* 15.11.5) describes the Royal Stoa along the southern edge as a basilica with one hundred sixty-two columns in four rows; archaeological remnants of these columns lie among the destruction debris excavated by Mazar.

Each of the chapter's controversies is a recognizable form of first-century Jewish disputation. The **Pharisaic-Herodian** delegation pairs ideological opposites: Pharisees opposed Roman taxation in principle, Herodians benefited from it directly. Their joint approach is itself a tell — the test is calibrated to put Jesus in an impossible position. Either answer (yes or no on the tax) ends his ministry: yes alienates the Galilean crowds (the Zealot movement that would launch the war in 66 CE was already simmering — cf. Judas the Galilean's tax revolt of 6 CE that Josephus describes in *Antiquities* 18.1.1), no invites Roman charges of sedition. Jesus's request to *see* the coin and his question about its image (***eikōn***, εἰκών) and inscription is itself revelatory: the **Tiberian denarius** of the period bore Tiberius's portrait with the inscription "TI CAESAR DIVI AVG F AVGVSTVS" — "Tiberius Caesar, son of the divine Augustus, Augustus" — a text that any Pharisee should have considered idolatrous to carry into the Temple precincts. By producing the coin from their own pockets, the Pharisees expose their compromise.

The **Sadducees** appear only here in Matthew before the passion narrative. The aristocratic priestly party rejected the resurrection (Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.1.4: "they take away the belief of the immortal duration of the soul"; cf. Acts 23:8). Their hypothetical of seven serial levirate marriages (Deut 25:5-10) is a typical rabbinic *reductio* designed to expose the supposed absurdity of resurrection belief. Jesus's response cites Exodus 3:6 — a Pentateuchal text the Sadducees, who privileged the Torah over the Prophets and Writings, were obligated to take seriously.

The **lawyer** (νομικός) who asks the great-commandment question represents the Pharisaic project of distilling the 613 commandments of the Torah into ranked principles — a known rabbinic practice. The Talmud (b. Makkot 23b-24a) records Rabbi Simlai reducing the 613 to eleven (Psalm 15), then six (Isaiah 33), then three (Micah 6:8), then one (Habakkuk 2:4 — "the righteous shall live by faith"). Hillel's reported summary — "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor; this is the whole Torah" — is the closest first-century parallel to Jesus's answer.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter. ***Eikōn*** (εἰκών, "image/likeness") on the denarius echoes Genesis 1:26-27, the human bearing God's image — a connection Tertullian (*On Idolatry* 15) and modern interpreters from Cullmann to Wright have made: render Caesar's image to Caesar, render the image of God (yourself) to God. ***Anastasis*** (ἀνάστασις, "resurrection," literally "standing up again") is the Pharisaic-Christian theological term the Sadducees reject. ***Entolē megalē*** (ἐντολὴ μεγάλη, "great commandment") is the technical Pharisaic question of the highest-ranked commandment. ***Kyrios*** (κύριος, "Lord") in the Psalm 110:1 citation appears twice in the LXX rendering, translating both YHWH and *Adonai* in the Hebrew — the Christological wedge Jesus drives in the closing exchange. ***Phorós*** vs. ***kēnsos*** (κῆνσος, "tax/poll-tax," from Latin *census*) — the specific tax in question is the *kēnsos*, the imperial poll-tax instituted at Quirinius's census in 6 CE that triggered Judas the Galilean's revolt.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Matthew 22 forms the central panel of a **three-chapter triptych** of Temple controversy: chapter 21 stages the entry and prophetic confrontations, chapter 22 stages the rabbinic disputations, chapter 23 delivers the climactic prophetic invective. The five units of chapter 22 — the wedding feast parable, the tax question, the resurrection question, the great commandment, the David's-son question — are arranged as a careful chiasm of who is testing whom. Units 2-4 are tests *against* Jesus (Pharisees-Herodians, then Sadducees, then a Pharisaic lawyer); unit 5 reverses the dynamic (Jesus asks; the Pharisees cannot answer). Unit 1, the wedding feast parable, frames the whole as eschatological warning.

The **wedding feast parable** (vv. 1-14) shows Matthew at his most editorially distinctive. Compared with **Luke's Great Banquet** (Luke 14:15-24), which Q-source theorists treat as the parallel, Matthew's version is harsher in three ways. First, Matthew's host is a **king** preparing a **wedding feast for his son**, raising the eschatological-Christological stakes. Second, Matthew adds the violent rejection: the invitees not only refuse but seize, mistreat, and kill the servants (v. 6), prompting the king to send troops and burn their city (v. 7) — a verse that nearly every modern commentator (Davies/Allison, Luz, France) reads as a vaticinium ex eventu reflection of the 70 CE destruction of Jerusalem. Third, Matthew uniquely appends the **wedding-garment episode** (vv. 11-14), which functions almost as a separate parable. A guest brought in from the streets, then condemned for lacking proper attire — a story that sits uneasily with the inclusive sweep of vv. 8-10 ("both bad and good"). Davies/Allison and Luz both treat the wedding-garment unit as Matthew's redactional warning against cheap grace: the church gathered from the highways still faces eschatological scrutiny.

The **tax pericope** (vv. 15-22) is one of the most rhetorically perfect units in the synoptic tradition. Mark 12:13-17 contains essentially the same exchange; Matthew's redaction preserves it. The pairing of Pharisees and Herodians is itself an embedded historical signal — these two factions did not normally cooperate; their joint appearance signals coordinated political opposition. The **Greatest Commandment** unit (vv. 34-40) preserves Mark's pairing of Deuteronomy 6:5 (the **Shema** continuation: "love YHWH your God with all your heart and soul and might") with Leviticus 19:18 (love your neighbor as yourself), but Matthew uniquely adds: "**On these two commandments depend [κρέμαται, *krematai*, literally 'hangs'] all the Law and the Prophets**" (v. 40) — a hermeneutical claim that the entire Hebrew canon hangs from these two pegs.

The closing **Son of David** exchange (vv. 41-46) is the most rabbinically dense unit in the chapter. Jesus cites **Psalm 110:1** — "The LORD said to my Lord, sit at my right hand until I put your enemies under your feet" — and presses the puzzle: how can the Messiah be both David's son (a status of subordination — sons honor fathers, not vice versa) and David's Lord (a status of preeminence)? In the LXX the verse reads κύριος twice ("the Lord said to my Lord"), translating YHWH and *Adonai* respectively. Hagner (WBC) notes that this exchange becomes the single most-cited Hebrew Bible verse in the New Testament — Psalm 110:1 is quoted or alluded to over thirty times across the NT (Acts 2:34-35, Hebrews 1:13, 1 Cor 15:25, etc.) and forms the bedrock of early Christological reflection.

The closing line — "**from that day no one dared ask him any more questions**" (v. 46) — closes the disputation cycle. From chapter 23 forward, only Jesus speaks until his arrest.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** **Geza Vermes** and **David Flusser** (*Jesus*, 1968) read the great-commandment exchange as showing Jesus operating well within Pharisaic norms; the pairing of *Shema* and Lev 19:18 is found in similar form in the *Testament of Issachar* (5:2) and *Testament of Dan* (5:3) and is consistent with Hillel's hermeneutic. **Pinchas Lapide** (*The Sermon on the Mount*) emphasizes that nothing Jesus says here would have offended the Pharisaic mainstream. **Amy-Jill Levine** (*Short Stories by Jesus*, 2014) reads the wedding feast parable carefully — and resists the supersessionist reading. The original guests are not "the Jews"; they are specifically those who reject the king's invitation. The replacement guests "good and bad" gathered from the highways are not "the Gentiles" or "the Church" but represent the *unlikely* recipients (tax collectors, prostitutes — recall 21:31). For Levine, the parable's force is intra-Jewish: the establishment is rejecting the messianic invitation while the marginalized are accepting it.

On the **resurrection question**, **N. T. Wright** (*The Resurrection of the Son of God*, 2003) provides the magisterial study showing that Jesus's appeal to Exodus 3:6 stands in the developed Pharisaic mainstream. By the first century, the Pharisaic tradition — preserved in Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:1 ("All Israel has a share in the world to come") — had developed a robust resurrection theology. Wright shows the originality of Jesus's argument: Exodus 3:6's present tense ("I *am* the God of Abraham") implies the patriarchs presently live to God, therefore God will raise them.

On **Psalm 110:1**, **Daniel Boyarin** (*The Jewish Gospels*, 2012) and **Larry Hurtado** (*Lord Jesus Christ*, 2003) have shown that the Psalm 110:1 application to a transcendent figure was not unique to Christianity — *Melchizedek* texts at Qumran (11QMelchizedek), *1 Enoch*'s Son of Man traditions, and other Second Temple speculation were already exploring divine throne-sharing. **Richard Bauckham** (*Jesus and the God of Israel*, 2008) argues that early Christianity's distinctive move was to identify this exalted figure with the historical Jesus, situating him within the divine identity itself.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** The **patristic** reading of "render to Caesar" (vv. 21) ranges across a wide political spectrum. **Tertullian** (*On Idolatry* 15) takes the saying restrictively — Caesar gets only what bears Caesar's image (coins), while the human person, bearing God's image, belongs entirely to God. **Origen** (*Comm. on Matthew*) reads similarly. **Augustine** (*Letter* 138 to Marcellinus) develops a more constructive two-realms reading that Aquinas (*Catena Aurea*; *Summa* II-II q. 60) and the medieval tradition systematize. **Chrysostom**'s *Homily 70 on Matthew* gives the classic patristic exposition of the wedding-garment episode, identifying the garment with charity (works of love).

The **Reformation** sharpens the political reading. **Calvin**'s *Harmony of the Evangelists* and *Institutes* IV.20 develop a robust two-kingdoms theology grounded partly in this verse: the civil magistrate is divinely ordained within his sphere, and Christians owe taxes and obedience in temporal matters. **Luther**'s *Two Kingdoms* doctrine reads similarly. The **Anabaptist** tradition reads the verse very differently: **Menno Simons** and the radical Reformers see "render to Caesar" as drawing a much sharper line — Caesar's coin returns to Caesar, but the Christian's allegiance, body, and life belong wholly to God, requiring non-resistance and refusal of state violence. **John Howard Yoder** (*The Politics of Jesus*, 1972) developed the most sophisticated modern Anabaptist reading: the verse does not legitimate the state's claims but ironizes them — Caesar gets back his idolatrous coinage; God gets the human person.

Among modern commentators, **Davies/Allison** (ICC vol. 3) provide exhaustive historical-critical engagement; **Ulrich Luz** (*Hermeneia*) traces the *Wirkungsgeschichte* of "render to Caesar" from Constantinian establishment through medieval church-state arrangements through Reformation two-kingdoms doctrine through twentieth-century totalitarian crises — Bonhoeffer's, Barth's, and the Confessing Church's wrestling with the verse against National Socialism are part of its reception history. **France** (NICNT) and **Keener** (NICNT) emphasize that Jesus's answer is genuinely two-sided: it neither baptizes the state (against Caesar-worship) nor wholly delegitimizes it (against Zealot revolt). **Hagner** (WBC) and **Nolland** (NIGTC) round out the synoptic consensus.

On the wedding-garment episode specifically, **Klyne Snodgrass** (*Stories with Intent*, 2008) provides the fullest modern engagement. The "garment" has been read as the white robe of baptism (Tertullian), works of love (Chrysostom), faith (Calvin), Christ's righteousness (Luther), and good works grounded in faith (Wesley). Snodgrass's view: the parable is not a freestanding allegory but a warning that the eschatological invitation, however inclusive, still demands a transformed life.

**N. T. Wright** (*Jesus and the Victory of God*) reads the entire chapter as Jesus winning the public contest: he silences each questioner in turn, demonstrating his prophetic-messianic authority, and finally turns the question on his opponents. The chapter functions as Jesus's vindication in the Temple court before the Mount of Olives prophecy.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What does "render to Caesar" actually authorize?** Three positions deserve steelmanning. **(1) Two-kingdoms reading** (Augustine, Calvin, Luther, mainstream Reformed) — the saying establishes parallel legitimate authorities: civil government in its sphere (taxes, justice, public order) and God in his (worship, conscience, ultimate allegiance). The Christian renders to each what is owed. This reading underwrites most of Christian political thought from Constantine through Hooker to contemporary mainstream Christianity. **(2) Subversive-irony reading** (Tertullian, Yoder, Anabaptist tradition, much liberation theology) — the saying ironizes Caesar's claims: Caesar gets back the idolatrous coin he minted, but the human person, made in God's image (cf. Gen 1:27 echoing in *eikōn* of v. 20), belongs entirely to God. The saying is not balanced; it is weighted toward God's totalizing claim. **(3) Pragmatic-evasion reading** (some critical scholars including Crossan) — the saying is a clever rhetorical dodge that gives no real political guidance but extracts Jesus from a trap. **Where this leaves us:** the saying functions on multiple levels, and the church's political theology has oscillated between the two-kingdoms and subversive-irony readings depending on its relationship to imperial power. When the church has been comfortable with state power, it has read this as two-kingdoms; when persecuted (early centuries) or resisting tyranny (Confessing Church, civil-rights movement), it has read this as subversive irony. The text bears both readings; the church's situation determines which it hears.

**Tension 2: What does the wedding-garment scene (vv. 11-14) teach about grace and works?** Three positions. **(1) Augustinian-Reformed reading** — the garment is the imputed righteousness of Christ given to the elect; the man without it represents the reprobate who presume on grace they have not received. This reading underwrites the Reformed doctrine of perseverance and the warning against false assurance. **(2) Patristic-Wesleyan reading** — the garment is the transformed life of love and good works that should accompany the call. Chrysostom's identification of the garment with charity, Wesley's emphasis on sanctification, and modern Catholic teaching converge here: the gathered guest must still respond. **(3) Eschatological-judgment reading** (Davies/Allison, France) — the parable functions less as a doctrinal allegory and more as an eschatological warning to Matthew's church: the inclusive invitation does not exempt the recipient from final scrutiny. **Where this leaves us:** the closing aphorism — "many are called, but few are chosen" — has been a contested verse in every Christian generation. Calvin and Augustine read it as predestinarian; Arminius and Wesley read it as a warning that the called must persevere; Catholic tradition reads it as affirming both grace and merit. The parable does not adjudicate the systematic question; it warns the church that the Eucharistic table itself does not save.

**Unresolved question:** If "the kingdom of God will be taken from you and given to a people producing its fruits" (21:43) and "many are called but few are chosen" (22:14) sit together in adjacent chapters, what does Matthew teach about the security of the new community? Is the new "people" of 21:43 itself liable to the same judgment that fell on the original tenants — and if so, on what grounds?

## The Thread

Matthew 22 weaves Deuteronomy 6:5 (the *Shema*), Leviticus 19:18 (love of neighbor), Exodus 3:6 (God of the patriarchs), Genesis 1:26-27 (image of God, behind *eikōn* in v. 20), and Psalm 110:1 (David's Lord) into a single chapter. Psalm 110:1 becomes the most-cited Hebrew Bible text in the NT (Acts 2:34-35; 1 Cor 15:25-27; Hebrews 1:13, 5:6, 7:17; Eph 1:20-22; 1 Peter 3:22). The great-commandment summary echoes forward to Romans 13:8-10, Galatians 5:14, James 2:8 — all citing Lev 19:18 as the law's summary. The wedding-banquet imagery recurs at Revelation 19:7-9 (the marriage supper of the Lamb). Forward in Matthew, the resurrection theology grounded in Exodus 3:6 prepares the reader for chapter 28, and Psalm 110:1 returns explicitly at 26:64 in Jesus's reply to Caiaphas — the citation that secures his condemnation. Flag this: the Christological wedge Jesus drives in 22:41-46 is the same wedge that returns in the high priest's courtroom four chapters later.
