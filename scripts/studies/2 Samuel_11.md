# 2 Samuel 11: The King Who Stayed Home

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens with a temporal marker that doubles as an indictment: "In the spring of the year, the time when kings go out to battle" (*li-tshuvat ha-shanah, le-et tzet ha-melakhim*). In Iron Age IIA Levantine practice, military campaigns resumed after the winter rains ended and before the wheat harvest — roughly the period from late March to early June. Roads dried, water was available in seasonal wadis, and grain stores in besieged cities had not yet been replenished. To "go out" was the king's *job*. The chronicler of 1 Chronicles 20 will airbrush the affair entirely, jumping from the campaign opening directly to Rabbah's fall, but the Samuel narrator preserves the awkward fact: the king stayed home. Halpern (*David's Secret Demons*) reads this against ANE royal propaganda, where kings boast of personally leading every campaign; the Samuel narrative is structured as something closer to anti-propaganda.

Rabbah of the Ammonites is modern Amman. Excavations on the Amman citadel (Jebel al-Qal'a) and surveys of its lower terrace have revealed Iron Age fortifications, including a casemate wall system consistent with a tenth-century capital. The "city of waters" mentioned at 12:27 likely refers to control of the Wadi Amman springs. Joab's siege would have been a months-long affair: cutting off water, attempting sapping operations against gates and weak wall sections — exactly the kind of operation in which an exposed assault produces predictable casualties (Anderson, WBC).

Jerusalem at this stage was likely centered on the Stepped Stone Structure on the eastern slope of the City of David and the Large Stone Structure on its summit, which Eilat Mazar identified (controversially) as David's palace. Whatever one makes of that identification, the city was small — perhaps ten to twelve acres — and a flat-roofed royal residence on its highest terrace would have looked down over closely-packed houses with their own roof courtyards. The "roof" (*gag*) of 11:2 is not a leisure deck in any modern sense; it was the coolest place in the late afternoon and a normal site for sleeping, drying produce, and bathing. Bathsheba's bath was thus visible by the geometry of the city, not by exhibitionism — a point Sternberg, Bal, and Exum all press in different directions.

A few terms of art carry the chapter's weight. *Va-yishlach David* — "and David sent" — is the verbal pulse of the chapter. David sends Joab (v. 1), sends to inquire (v. 3), sends messengers to take Bathsheba (v. 4), sends to Joab to send Uriah (v. 6), sends Uriah out (v. 8), writes and sends a letter (v. 14), sends and brings Bathsheba (v. 27). The verb tolls. *Laqach* ("took," v. 4) is the same verb Samuel used in 1 Samuel 8:11–17 prophesying what a king will *take*. *Ha-Hitti* ("the Hittite") brackets Uriah's name with ethnic precision at vv. 3, 6, 17, 21, 24 — as if the narrator will not let the reader forget that the most covenantally faithful man in the chapter is the foreigner. *Mit-qaddeshet mi-tum'atah* — "purifying herself from her uncleanness" (v. 4) — is a parenthetical that does enormous narrative work: it rules out, in advance, the possibility that the child is Uriah's. *Ra ha-davar asher asah David be-eyney YHWH* — "the thing David had done was evil in the eyes of YHWH" (v. 27) — is the only theological comment in the entire chapter. The narrator's restraint is the model.

On dating and source: the story sits in what Leonhard Rost in 1926 isolated as the Court History or Succession Narrative (2 Samuel 9–20 + 1 Kings 1–2), which he argued was the oldest extended Hebrew prose narrative — possibly composed within a generation or two of the events, perhaps in Solomon's court. Gerhard von Rad called it "the beginning of history-writing in Israel." Rost's thesis has been refined (Gunn, McCarter, McKenzie) but never displaced. The textual situation is complicated by 4QSamᵃ from Qumran, which preserves variants in the messenger's report (vv. 22–23) where MT seems compressed; many critical editions and McCarter's Anchor Bible reconstruct a fuller Vorlage in which the messenger first delivers the bad news straight, *then* reports Uriah's death — a sequence that further exposes David's calculation in his reply.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is court historiography in a register so spare it has been compared to Thucydides. There are almost no adjectives, no internal monologue, no editorial commentary until the final clause. Meir Sternberg's reading in *The Poetics of Biblical Narrative* established this chapter as the textbook case for how biblical narrative withholds information to implicate the reader: we are told *what* David sees and *what* David does, but never told what he thinks; we are told that Bathsheba was bathing and that she conceived but never told what *she* thought; we are told Uriah refused to go home but given two mutually compatible explanations (piety, suspicion) and forced to choose. The chapter is built almost entirely from external action and direct speech.

Structurally the chapter is a chiasm of "sending" verbs around the central pivot of Uriah's refusal. The opening (vv. 1–5) compresses the affair into five verses of pure narrative momentum — saw, sent, took, lay, conceived. The middle (vv. 6–13) slows radically as David tries three times to maneuver Uriah into his own bed; the pace itself dramatizes the cover-up's failure. Then (vv. 14–25) the violence is again compressed into staccato actions: wrote, sent, set, fell, died, sent, told. The final coda (vv. 26–27) is a brief domestic resolution that the last clause sabotages.

Among the devices: the *repetition with damning variation*. Uriah's speech in v. 11 — "the ark and Israel and Judah dwell in booths, and my lord Joab and the servants of my lord are camping in the open field; shall I then go to my house, to eat and to drink and to lie with my wife?" — is a triadic articulation of solidarity that exactly inverts what David has just done (he is at home, eating, drinking, lying with another man's wife). Robert Alter (*The David Story*) calls this "an accusation by indirection so devastating that the reader wonders whether Uriah has guessed." Then comes the *Joab inset* (vv. 19–21): Joab anticipates David's military objection (why approach the wall?) and pre-empts it with a precise historical reference — Abimelech and the millstone at Thebez (Judges 9:53). The point is not just military self-defense; it is Joab signaling, in coded form, that he understands the *real* reason for the assignment. Joab the field commander is now Joab the political accomplice, and the reader watches him become indispensable to David in a way David will spend the rest of his life regretting.

Bar-Efrat's narrative analysis highlights the *deflected verbs*: David never himself kills Uriah, but every verb in the killing chain runs through David's hand — wrote, sent. Fokkelman's *Narrative Art and Poetry in the Books of Samuel* II attends to the chapter's negative theology: the divine name appears only at the chapter's last word, framing the entire account as a thirty-six-hour blank in which God seems silent. That silence is itself the indictment.

Textual variants are meaning-affecting. At v. 4, MT reads simply "she came to him and he lay with her"; some Greek and Qumran witnesses include or amplify the parenthetical about her purification, which several modern critics (McCarter; Cross's students) have argued was original. At v. 22, 4QSamᵃ preserves a longer messenger speech that more closely follows Joab's instruction, suggesting MT has lost a line by haplography. Anderson (WBC) and Tsumura (NICOT) discuss but do not resolve.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, this chapter is the hinge of the David story. Up to chapter 10, David's career has been a near-uninterrupted ascent — the slingstone, the harp, the loyalty of Jonathan, the cave of Adullam, the kindness to Mephibosheth, the conquest of Jerusalem, the Davidic covenant of chapter 7. From chapter 11 forward, the trajectory inverts. The "sword shall not depart from your house" prophecy of 12:10 is the structuring principle of every scene that follows: Amnon's rape of Tamar (13), Absalom's murder of Amnon (13), Absalom's revolt and death (15–18), Sheba's revolt (20), the Adonijah affair (1 Kings 1–2). Rost saw the entire Court History as built around the question "Who will succeed David?" — and chapter 11 is the moral wound from which that question bleeds.

**Jewish trajectory.** Josephus (*Antiquities* 7.130–158) retells the chapter at length but mutes its starkness: he stresses that David acted "out of love" and emphasizes the genuine repentance to come. The rabbinic tradition divides itself sharply. Shabbat 56a contains the famous (and famously strained) defense: "Whoever says David sinned is mistaken." The argument runs that all soldiers in David's army gave conditional bills of divorce before battle, so Bathsheba was technically unmarried when David took her, and Uriah's refusal to obey the king's order made him a rebel deserving of execution. The Talmud itself records this position; later rabbinic commentary, especially Rashi and Radak, accepts that David sinned but emphasizes the speed and totality of his repentance. Ralbag (Gersonides) is more philosophically frank about the moral failure. Abarbanel, writing in fifteenth-century Iberia, stages the most thorough moral reckoning, refusing the Shabbat 56a defense and calling the act *murder* (*retsicha*) rather than mere causation. Avivah Gottlieb Zornberg, in her recent work on the David narratives, reads David's sending as a study in the king's will displacing all other agency. Yair Zakovitch and Moshe Garsiel have written on the chapter as a study in royal abuse of administrative power.

**Christian trajectory.** Origen treated Bathsheba allegorically, reading her name (often glossed as "daughter of the oath") as the soul drawn to the heavenly king; this is exegetically thin and morally evasive. Augustine's *City of God* 17.20 is more honest: he treats David's sin as real and grave, and treats his repentance as the fundamental Christian model — not because the sin is excusable, but because the kingly capacity to be truly broken open by the prophetic word is itself the miracle. Ambrose dedicated two whole treatises to David, *De Apologia Prophetae David* and *De Apologia Prophetae David Altera*, arguing that the integrity of David as a prophet survives because of, not despite, his confrontation with his own sin; Ambrose stages David as the patient who, having been wounded, becomes capable of healing others. Chrysostom's homilies make David's penitence a homiletic touchstone. Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job* uses David's example as a study in the slow corrosion of ease — the "leisure of kings" that becomes the seedbed of the gravest sin. Aquinas (*Summa* II-II, q. 154) cites the chapter in his treatment of adultery and treats David's status as "man after God's own heart" as referring to his repentance, not his behavior. Calvin's sermons on 2 Samuel are notably severe; he refuses any softening of the narrative and reads David as a warning to every magistrate.

In modern critical reading, Wellhausen, Cross, and McCarter (Anchor Bible) treat the chapter as Court History in roughly Rost's sense; A. A. Anderson (WBC 2 Samuel) gives it the most thorough recent commentary form. Brueggemann (Interpretation 2 Samuel; *David's Truth*) reads the chapter as the moment the narrative refuses to flatter power. Polzin (*David and the Deuteronomist*) reads David's "sending" as the perversion of the prophetic word. Eugene Peterson (*Leap Over a Wall*) holds the moral plainness of the narrative against later attempts to soften it. Davis and Goldingay treat it as the dark mirror to chapter 7. The chapter has been a major site for feminist criticism: Cheryl Exum's *Fragmented Women* analyzes Bathsheba's complete textual silence — she speaks exactly two words ("I am pregnant") — as the narrative's quiet acknowledgment of erased agency; Mieke Bal's *Lethal Love* and Alice Bach's *Women, Seduction, and Betrayal* press this further. Tikva Frymer-Kensky's *Reading the Women of the Bible* treats Bathsheba as the first of the maligned royal mothers.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: How much does Bathsheba "do"?** The text is almost aggressively non-committal. Position A (older, dominant in Christian tradition through the medieval period and reinforced in some popular modern preaching): Bathsheba is at least partially complicit — she bathes where she might be seen, she comes when summoned, she does not protest in the text. Position B (rabbinic Shabbat 56a in extreme form): Bathsheba was already legally available and her assent is irrelevant. Position C (modern feminist reading, Trible–Exum–Bal–Bach): there is no consent in any meaningful sense available to a subject whose king sends armed messengers to take her; her silence is the silence of someone the narrator declines to interview. Steelman of C: the verb structure of vv. 3–4 is governed entirely by David — he sees, he inquires, he sends, he takes, he lies; the only verb governed by Bathsheba in the active is *va-tashav le-vetah* ("she returned to her house") and *va-tishlach va-tagged* ("she sent and told"). Even the "purification" notice is structurally placed to clear the king's paternity question, not to characterize her. **Where this leaves us:** The text neither convicts nor exonerates Bathsheba; it leaves her in a deliberate moral fog while it convicts David. The asymmetry is itself the theology — power is what the chapter is about, and power's first effect is that the powerful person's interiority is the only one we can see.

**Tension 2: What does the narrator's restraint mean?** Position A (Sternberg): the narrator's silence until v. 27 is a pedagogical strategy — the reader is forced to render the moral judgment that the narrator refuses to render *for* us, and only then is the reader's verdict ratified by the divine verdict. Position B (Brueggemann): the silence is itself a theological move — God is hidden through the chapter precisely because the chapter is about a regime in which God's voice is not consulted; the absence of YHWH's name is a structural depiction of a polity that has placed itself outside covenant. Position C (Polzin): the silence is the Deuteronomist's irony — the reader knows from Deuteronomy 17 what a king is forbidden to do (multiply wives, multiply horses, multiply silver and gold); the chapter is a slow-motion violation of all three, and the narrator's quiet is the quiet of evidence being entered into the record. Steelman of C: the chapter is bracketed by Deuteronomic categories that the reader is expected to supply. **Where this leaves us:** Probably all three are true at once — the silence is pedagogical, theological, and juridical. What it is *not* is endorsement.

**Unresolved question:** Why does the narrative not record any conversation between David and Bathsheba? They have at least one extended encounter (v. 4), and the next chapter will reveal a second encounter and a marriage that produces Solomon. But not one word passes between them in the text. Is this the silence of trauma, of complicity, of indifference, of editorial decorum, or of something the narrator has decided we cannot be permitted to overhear?

## The Thread

Cross-references: Deuteronomy 17:14–20 (the law of the king — multiplication of wives and the king's heart "lifted up above his brothers") sits behind the entire chapter as the unspoken indictment. Genesis 38 (Judah and Tamar) is the closest narrative parallel for sexual transgression complicated by who is "more righteous than I" — and it will echo again in 2 Samuel 13. 1 Samuel 25 (Nabal, Abigail, and the sheep-shearing feast) introduced the David-takes-another-man's-wife pattern in a far more flattering register, which makes its inversion here more devastating. Psalm 51 carries the chapter's traditional liturgical afterlife, though its superscription points to chapter 12 rather than 11.

Flag forward: 2 Samuel 12 (Nathan's parable and the fourfold sentence — every term of which becomes a chapter title in 13–18); 2 Samuel 23:34, 39 (Eliam and Uriah listed among David's Thirty — the chapter's understated forensic exhibit); 2 Samuel 15:12 and 16:23 (Ahithophel's defection — explicable on the genealogy, since Eliam, Bathsheba's father, is Ahithophel's son, and the most trusted royal counselor goes over to Absalom because the king has dishonored his granddaughter); 1 Kings 1–2 (Bathsheba reappears as the queen mother who secures Solomon's succession — the silenced figure of chapter 11 turns out to have been only deferred). The chapter's resolution does not occur within the chapter; it does not occur within the book.
