# Psalm 105 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 105 carries no superscription in the Masoretic Text, but its first fifteen verses are quoted verbatim in 1 Chronicles 16:8–22 as part of the composite worship-psalm David delivers at the installation of the Ark of the Covenant in the tent on Mount Zion. The Chronicler's composite at 1 Chronicles 16:8–36 stitches together Psalm 105:1–15, Psalm 96:1–13, and Psalm 106:1, 47–48 — three separate psalms now woven into a single liturgical performance. This editorial layering is one of the clearest windows we have into how the Psalter's individual texts moved through Israel's worshiping life. Whether Psalm 105 was composed *before* the Chronicler and excerpted into his composition, or composed *from* the Chronicler's composite as a freestanding text, is debated; the majority view is that Psalm 105 in roughly its current form predates Chronicles and was the source for the excerpt, with both then circulating in tandem.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 105 as a *Geschichtspsalm*, a "history psalm" or historical recital — the form that takes the canonical narrative of Israel's salvation history and re-presents it in lyric form for the worshipping congregation. The form-critical category groups it with Psalms 78, 106, 135, 136, and the historical sections of Psalms 77 and 114; within that group, Psalms 105 and 106 form the most deliberate pair, as we will see. Sigmund Mowinckel located the historical recital within the cultic celebration of the New Year festival, where the people's history was reenacted and reaffirmed. Hans-Joachim Kraus, Erhard Gerstenberger, John Goldingay, and Frank-Lothar Hossfeld with Erich Zenger all retain some version of the historical-recital classification while adding nuance about the post-exilic shaping.

Dating is contested. The pre-exilic *core* — the tradition of recital itself — is clearly old; Joshua 24's covenant-renewal recital and Deuteronomy 26's "wandering Aramean" creed show the form working in the seventh century or earlier. The *psalm* in its present form, however, almost certainly belongs to the late pre-exilic or post-exilic period, with the editorial integration into Book IV of the Psalter happening after the Babylonian exile. The Chronicler's use of it places a firm terminus around 400 BC at the latest. The vocabulary is classical Hebrew with no decisive late forms, but the theological emphasis on Abrahamic covenant (rather than Mosaic-Sinaitic) and on the *land* as inheritance fits a community for whom Land has become a question — i.e., a community in or after exile.

Among the **Dead Sea Scrolls**, Psalm 105 appears in 4QPse and 11QPsa, with text largely consistent with the Masoretic Text. The 11QPsa scroll, with its distinctive ordering and inclusion of additional psalms, places Psalm 105 in close proximity to other historical recitals, suggesting that the Qumran community read these psalms as a cluster.

The Hebrew vocabulary is dense with covenant language. Verse 8's *zākhar lə‛ôlām bərîtô* (זָכַר לְעוֹלָם בְּרִיתוֹ, "he remembers his covenant forever") is the controlling theological claim of the psalm — *zākhar*, "remember," is the verb that does the most theological work in Israel's covenant theology, because divine remembering is divine acting. Verse 15's *al-tigə‛û bimšîḥāy ûvinvî'ay 'al-tārē‛û* (אַל-תִּגְּעוּ בִמְשִׁיחָי וּבִנְבִיאַי אַל-תָּרֵעוּ, "Touch not my anointed ones, do my prophets no harm") applies the term *māšîaḥ* — "anointed" — to the patriarchs, which is unusual and has been read by Christian exegetes as a typological foreshadowing and by Jewish exegetes as evidence of the patriarchs' prophetic-priestly status. Verse 23's *yiśrā'ēl yāqōv gār bə'ereṣ-ḥām* (יִשְׂרָאֵל יָקֹב גָר בְּאֶרֶץ-חָם, "Israel came to Egypt; Jacob sojourned in the land of Ham") uses "Ham" as a synonym for Egypt, picking up the Genesis 10 Table of Nations.

The **ancient Near Eastern** context for the historical recital form includes Mesopotamian *naru* literature (royal autobiographies disguised as ancient inscriptions), Hittite covenant treaties with their historical prologues, and Egyptian royal annals. The closest formal parallel is the Hittite suzerainty treaty's historical prologue, where the great king recites the gracious deeds he has done for the vassal as the basis for the treaty's stipulations. Psalm 105 is essentially this prologue made into hymn: God's deeds for Abraham, Joseph, Israel-in-Egypt, Israel-in-the-wilderness — recited as the basis for the people's covenantal obligation in verse 45 ("that they might keep his statutes and observe his laws").

## Lens 2: The Literature

Robert Lowth's parallelism is everywhere; the psalm uses synonymous parallelism heavily — "make known his deeds among the peoples / sing to him, sing praises to him" — as suits the recital form, where repetition is part of the rhetorical drive. James Kugel's revision again applies: *A*, and what is more, *B*. The escalation pattern fits a hymn meant to gather momentum.

The **strophic structure** tracks the Pentateuchal narrative with remarkable fidelity. After an opening call to praise (vv. 1–6), the psalm moves through:

- Patriarchal covenant (vv. 7–11): the covenant with Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, with land as inheritance
- Patriarchal protection (vv. 12–15): few in number, sojourners, divinely shielded
- Joseph in Egypt (vv. 16–22): famine, slavery, fetters, exaltation
- Israel in Egypt (vv. 23–25): fruitful, hated, oppressed
- Moses, Aaron, plagues (vv. 26–36): the signs in the land of Ham
- Exodus (vv. 37–38): silver, gold, no stumbling
- Wilderness providence (vv. 39–41): cloud, fire, quail, manna, water from rock
- Reason and goal (vv. 42–45): the remembered promise, the inherited land, the kept statutes

Notice what is **absent**: there is no Sinai, no golden calf, no Korah, no Baal of Peor, no Meribah-as-failure, no death in the wilderness, no sin of the spies. The wilderness is purely a scene of divine providence. This is structurally the *positive* historical recital — God's faithfulness without Israel's failure — and its absences are deliberate. The complementary recital that includes the failures is Psalm 106, the next psalm. The pair is editorially designed.

The psalm has no *selah*, like Psalms 104 and 106 — the long historical-creational hymns of Book IV's close move continuously without pause, perhaps because the historical narrative does not pause.

The opening **inclusio** is more thematic than verbal: the psalm opens with a call to "remember the wondrous works that he has done" (v. 5) and ends with "he remembered his holy promise" (v. 42). The remembering is bilateral — Israel remembering God's works, God remembering his promise — and the two remberings are the engine of the covenant.

The plagues sequence (vv. 28–36) is striking for what it includes and excludes. Eight plagues are listed (darkness, blood, frogs, flies/gnats, hail, locusts, firstborn), and the order differs from both Exodus 7–12 and Psalm 78. The order in Psalm 105 puts darkness first, where Exodus has it ninth. Several explanations have been proposed: an alternative tradition, a literary reordering for poetic effect, or a theological emphasis (darkness as the inversion of the creation light of Genesis 1:3). Whatever the reason, the variant order is one of the clearer signs that the historical psalms are *interpretations* of the canonical narrative, not flat repetitions of it.

The Hebrew word *mōsheh ‛avdô* (מֹשֶׁה עַבְדּוֹ, "Moses, his servant," v. 26) and the parallel *‛avdô* applied to Abraham (v. 6) and Joseph implicitly (the entire Joseph-as-slave narrative of vv. 17–22) connect the psalm's central figures through a shared title. The "servant" title in Isaiah 40–55 — the *‛eved Yahweh* tradition — has roots in this kind of patriarchal-prophetic application, and Psalm 105's use is part of that traditioning stream.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Psalm 105 sits in **Book IV of the Psalter (Psalms 90–106)**, and within Book IV it forms with Psalm 106 the editorial diptych that closes the book. The structural logic is sharp. Book IV opens with the Mosaic prayer of Psalm 90 — "Lord, you have been our dwelling place in all generations" — and answers the Davidic crisis of Psalm 89 by relocating Israel's hope from the Davidic throne to the Mosaic-creational covenant. The *Yahweh-mālāk* psalms (93, 95–99) proclaim divine kingship as the foundation that survives human kingship's collapse. Psalms 103–104 hymn divine *ḥesed* and divine creation as the cosmic and personal grounds of trust. Then Psalms 105–106 deliver the historical recital that *applies* these doctrines to Israel's actual story.

Within that diptych, Psalm 105 is the *positive* recital: God's covenant fidelity from Abraham through the Conquest. Psalm 106 is the *negative* recital: Israel's covenant infidelity through the same period and into exile. The two psalms together answer the implicit question of Book IV: if the LORD reigns and the covenant endures, why is Israel in exile? Psalm 105's answer: because God's faithfulness is constant, the exile cannot be the last word. Psalm 106's answer: because Israel's failure is real, the exile is *just*. The pair holds both claims and refuses to dissolve either.

The **Jewish trajectory** treats Psalm 105 as a foundational liturgical text. The *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 105 reads the patriarchs' designation as *məšîḥāy* ("my anointed ones," v. 15) as evidence of their priestly-prophetic status, a point that becomes significant in Second Temple priestly genealogy debates. Rashi reads the verse straightforwardly: God protected the patriarchs from Pharaoh, Abimelech, and the Canaanite kings. Ibn Ezra is more lexically careful and notes the broad use of *māšîaḥ* for any divinely chosen agent. Radak (David Kimchi) emphasizes the covenantal pattern: God's promise to Abraham works itself out through every generation, and the psalm is the hymn of that working-out. Nachmanides reads the historical sequence as a Kabbalistic mapping of the *sefirot*, with each patriarch corresponding to a different divine attribute.

Liturgically, Psalm 105 is recited on the second day of Passover in some traditions and figures in the Pesukei d'Zimra of Sabbath morning prayers in Sephardic rites. The Chronicler's use of vv. 1–15 in 1 Chronicles 16 made the psalm permanently associated with the festival of the Ark and, by extension, with celebrations of God's saving deeds in Israel's history. Modern Jewish thinkers — Buber, Heschel, Levenson, Sarna — have read Psalm 105 less prominently than Psalms 23, 90, or 137, but the text remains central to the *credal recital* tradition. Walter Brueggemann's *Israel's Praise* analyzes Psalms 105 and 106 as the paradigmatic examples of *credal recital* — the lyric performance of Israel's confessional history — and traces the form back through Deuteronomy 26's "wandering Aramean" creed and Joshua 24's covenant renewal.

The **Christian trajectory** is less concentrated than for Psalms 22, 51, or 110, but real. Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 104 (LXX) reads the patriarchs typologically as figures of Christ and the church, with Joseph in particular as a Christ-figure (sold by his brothers, descending into the pit, raised to glory, saving his people through bread). Cassiodorus follows this Augustinian line. Aquinas treats the psalm in passing in the *Catena Aurea* and elsewhere. Luther's exposition emphasizes God's covenant fidelity as the ground of justification by faith. Calvin's commentary on Psalm 105 is sober and historical; he reads the psalm as the textbook of providence, with each episode in patriarchal and exodus history showing how God's hidden hand works through human means.

Modern Christian scholarship has paid increasing attention to Psalm 105 as a window into Israel's *credal* identity. Gerhard von Rad's *Old Testament Theology* used the historical psalms as evidence for the centrality of the *Heilsgeschichte* recital in Israel's theology. Walter Brueggemann's *Israel's Praise* and *The Psalms and the Life of Faith* read Psalm 105 as the "psalm of orientation" par excellence — the recital that affirms God's reliable governance of Israel's story. James Mays, J. Clinton McCann, Patrick Miller, William P. Brown, Beth Tanner, and Hossfeld-Zenger all converge on the psalm's importance for understanding Israel's liturgical-confessional identity. Goldingay's three-volume Psalms commentary devotes careful attention to Psalm 105's relationship to Pentateuchal traditions and to its pair with Psalm 106. David Mitchell's canonical-shape work treats Psalms 105–106 as the historical-confessional answer to the Davidic-failure question of Books I–III.

The **New Testament** does not quote Psalm 105 at length, but Stephen's speech in Acts 7 is the paradigmatic Christian historical recital and bears clear formal kinship: Stephen rehearses Abraham, Joseph, Moses, and the wilderness in the same recital style. Hebrews 11's catalogue of faith is a related form. Paul's argument in Romans 4 about Abraham's prior righteousness depends on the kind of patriarchal-priority emphasis Psalm 105 makes (covenant established with Abraham, vv. 8–11). The historical-recital form is part of how the New Testament tells its story.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The first hard tension is **the relationship between the positive recital of Psalm 105 and the negative recital of Psalm 106**. Three positions stake out the territory.

The first reading sees the two psalms as **complementary halves of a single confession**. On this reading, Psalm 105 is what Israel says in praise — God has been faithful — and Psalm 106 is what Israel says in repentance — we have been unfaithful. The two together form the bilateral covenant theology that holds both divine fidelity and human failure without dissolving either. This is essentially the canonical-shape reading of Wilson, Mitchell, and McCann, and it is the dominant scholarly position. The strength is its faithfulness to the editorial pairing. The weakness is that it can flatten the two psalms into mere "sides" of a single argument when each is doing its own theological work.

The second reading sees Psalm 105 as **liturgical idealization** — the historical recital cleaned up for cultic purposes, where the failures are deliberately suppressed because the cult requires the *positive* version of the story. On this reading, the gap between Psalm 105 and the actual canonical narrative (which includes the calf, Korah, Meribah, the spies) is not theological complementarity but liturgical convention: praise psalms tell the story for praise, lament psalms tell the story for lament, and the "real" story is more honest than either alone. Brueggemann edges this way when he distinguishes psalms of *orientation* from psalms of *disorientation*. The strength is its honesty about what cultic recital actually does. The weakness is that it can make Psalm 105 sound dishonest when the text itself does not seem to be hiding anything — it knows there are other psalms.

The third reading sees Psalm 105 as **theological argument by selection** — the psalm is making a specific claim by choosing what to include. The claim is that the *foundation* of Israel's existence is God's covenant fidelity from Abraham forward, and that this foundation is logically and chronologically prior to anything Israel does or fails to do. Even the wilderness providence is rehearsed without the failures, because in this psalm the question is not "did Israel obey?" but "did God provide?" The answer is yes. The strength is its respect for the psalm's integrity as a discrete theological statement. The weakness is that it can underplay the editorial work that pairs it with Psalm 106.

**Where this leaves us**: the psalms are best read as both complementary and individually integral. Psalm 105 is doing real theological work on its own — affirming covenant priority — and is also positioned as the first half of a diptych with Psalm 106. The complementarity is not flattening if each psalm is allowed its own voice within the larger conversation.

The second hard tension is **the relationship between cult-recital and historical-confession**. Brueggemann's *Israel's Praise* argues that Israel's *credal recital* — the rehearsal of the saving deeds in cultic worship — is the original form of biblical theology, prior to systematic doctrine. Psalm 105 is one of the clearest examples. But the question is sharper than it appears. Three positions.

The first reading treats the recital as **historical reportage in poetic form**. The psalm tells what happened, with poetic compression and selection, but its claims are descriptive: God did these things, in this order, with these results. Conservative evangelical readings (Hoffmeier, Kitchen, Walton in some moods) lean here, with attention to how the recital corresponds to the Pentateuchal sources.

The second reading treats the recital as **memory-making rather than reporting**. The community is not so much remembering what happened as making the past present through the act of recital. The recital *constitutes* Israel as the people of this story; without the recital, the story does not function. Mowinckel's cultic theory and the Scandinavian school edge this way. The strength is its attention to the social-formative function of the text. The weakness is that it can collapse the historical referent into pure performance.

The third reading treats the recital as **theological interpretation of historical tradition**. The events happened (the recital is not pure invention), but the recital is a particular *reading* of those events, with selection, ordering, and theological emphasis as part of its rhetorical work. This is the mainstream critical position, and it allows for both the historical anchor and the interpretive freedom. Childs, von Rad, Brueggemann (in his more careful moments), and Hossfeld-Zenger all hold versions of this. The strength is its hermeneutical realism. The weakness, from a conservative angle, is that it can soften the historical claim more than the text itself does.

**Where this leaves us**: the recital is *interpretive narration*, anchored in historical tradition but freely shaped for confession. The variant plague order, the absence of the calf, the prominence of Joseph (who gets seven verses to Moses's eleven verses including the plagues) — these are theological choices in service of the psalm's argument, and reading them as such is not skepticism but exegesis.

The third tension is **the Chronicler-Psalter relationship**. 1 Chronicles 16:8–36 takes Psalm 105:1–15, sets it alongside Psalm 96 and Psalm 106:1, 47–48, and presents the composite as the song David delivers at the Ark's installation. Three readings.

The simple reading is that **Chronicles excerpted from existing psalms** that were already in liturgical use, treating them as Davidic compositions for narrative purposes. This is the majority view and fits the Chronicler's general interest in Davidic liturgical foundation. The weakness is that it requires Psalms 105, 96, and 106 in roughly current form to predate the Chronicler.

A second reading is that the Chronicler's composite is **the original**, and Psalms 105, 96, and 106 were later disaggregated from it to form three separate psalms. This is a minority view but has been defended by some scholars who note the seamlessness of the Chronicler's composition.

A third reading is that **both directions of dependence are real**: there was an old Davidic-Asaphite tradition of liturgical recital, the Chronicler's composite reflects one moment in that tradition, and the three psalms reflect later developments and stand-alone uses of the same material. The Psalter's editing into five books is itself part of a long process, and the Chronicler's redaction is one node in that process rather than a single fixed point. Most contemporary scholars (Knoppers, Klein, Hossfeld-Zenger) hold versions of this view.

**Where this leaves us**: the Chronicler-Psalter relationship is a window into how Israel's liturgical texts moved, were excerpted, were recombined, and were re-circulated. Psalm 105 in its current form is the product of that movement, not a static composition.

**Closing question**: If Psalm 105 affirms that God remembers his covenant *forever* (v. 8) and acts on that memory through every generation, what does it mean to be a community whose primary act of worship is to *remember* in return — and what is the difference between remembering that constitutes a people and nostalgia that merely consoles them?

## The Thread

Psalm 105 is the positive historical recital that answers Book IV's central question — if the Davidic throne is in ruins, what holds Israel in being? — by reaching back past David to Abraham. The covenant with Abraham is older than the monarchy and outlives it. The God who fed Joseph in the pit, brought Israel out of Egypt, and gave the land to a wandering people has not been defeated by the exile. The psalm clears the ground for Psalm 106's confession of failure by establishing that the foundation has not failed. From Abraham forward, the covenant runs; what Israel does with it is the question Psalm 106 will answer.
