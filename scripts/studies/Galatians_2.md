# Galatians 2: The Table That Decided the Church

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Two scenes anchor this chapter, and both are about who eats with whom. The first (2:1–10) is a private summit in Jerusalem between Paul, Barnabas, Titus, and the "pillars" — James, Cephas, and John. The second (2:11–14) is a public meal in Antioch where Cephas stops eating with Gentile believers when "men from James" arrive. The argument of the entire letter — and arguably the church's entire later self-understanding — turns on those two table scenes.

Antioch on the Orontes was the third largest city of the empire after Rome and Alexandria, with perhaps 250,000 inhabitants. Josephus (Jewish War 7.43–62) attests a substantial Jewish population settled there since Seleucus I, and Antioch was where Jesus-followers were first called "Christians" (Acts 11:26). Diaspora synagogue communities had developed accommodations for sharing meals with God-fearing Gentiles (the Aphrodisias stele lists theosebeis alongside Jews; Josephus, Jewish War 7.45, claims Antiochene Jews "made many of the Greeks perpetually their associates"). Some Pharisaic schools insisted on rigorous separation; others were porous. The "men from James" likely represent a stricter halakhic position — perhaps responding to rising Zealot pressure in Judea in the mid-to-late 40s, when accommodations to Gentiles were politically dangerous for Jerusalem Jewish-Christians.

The dating crux remains. The South Galatian theory (favored by F.F. Bruce in his 1982 NIGTC, N.T. Wright, Longenecker, Moo) places Galatians 2:1–10 as the same event as Acts 11:30 (the famine-relief visit, c. AD 46) or as a separate private meeting before Acts 15. Galatians is then dated c. AD 48–49, before the Jerusalem Council. The North Galatian view (Lightfoot, Martyn, Schoeps) identifies Galatians 2:1–10 with Acts 15 itself and dates the letter to the early-to-mid 50s. The reason this matters: if Galatians 2 is Acts 15, why doesn't Paul cite the Council's apostolic decree (Acts 15:23–29) against the agitators? Most South Galatian theorists answer that the decree had not yet been issued when Paul wrote. Either chronology requires us to read Galatians 2:11–14 (the Antioch incident) as later than 2:1–10 — and the question of whether Antioch came before or after Acts 15 affects how we read Cephas's behavior. If Antioch is before Acts 15, the Council resolved the issue Paul confronts here. If Antioch is after Acts 15, Cephas knew better and lapsed anyway.

Roman provincial governance frames the dispute. Antioch was capital of Syria, governed by an imperial legate. Jewish communities had politeuma status under Augustus and Claudius — exempt from imperial cult participation and free to follow ancestral customs. To eat with Gentiles was not illegal, but it could compromise the Jewish-Christian community's standing within the Antioch synagogue and its protections under Roman law. The pressure on Cephas was not theological abstraction; it was whether his community would remain inside or outside the synagogue's legal umbrella.

The "circumcision party" (hoi ek peritomēs, 2:12) is contested. Longenecker reads this group as the "false brothers" of 2:4. Martyn reads them as stricter Jewish-Christians who maintained boundary-marker observance without necessarily demanding Gentile circumcision. Mark Nanos (The Irony of Galatians, 2002) takes them as Torah-observant Jewish-Christians who insisted on full halakhic table separation. Paul's gospel says these hierarchies are abolished in Christ; the "men from James" say the boundary remains. Cephas — who in Acts 10:9–48 had received a vision telling him "what God has made clean, do not call common" — backs away from his own earlier conviction.

The textual transmission of chapter 2 raises one of the great cruxes of New Testament Greek. The phrase **pistis Christou** (πίστις Χριστοῦ, 2:16, 2:20) can be construed as objective genitive ("faith in Christ" — the traditional reading defended by Dunn, Schreiner, R. Barry Matlock) or subjective genitive ("the faithfulness of Christ" — defended by Richard Hays in The Faith of Jesus Christ, 1983, and supported by Wright, Campbell, Martyn, de Boer, Hooker). The grammar of Paul's Greek does not decide it; both are possible. The theological consequences are substantial. P46, our earliest manuscript, gives no help — it transmits the disputed phrase exactly as we have it. The variant at 2:5 (Western text omitting "to whom") would, if accepted, reverse Paul's account of Titus and concede the agitators' demand.

Greek terms. **Erga nomou** (ἔργα νόμου, "works of the law") appears six times in Paul, all in Galatians and Romans. Qumran's 4QMMT (Miqsat Ma'ase Ha-Torah, published in full 1994) used the same Hebrew phrase for halakhic boundary practices distinguishing the sect — a major data point for the New Perspective. **Pistis Christou** (πίστις Χριστοῦ) — the genitive crux. **Dikaioō** (δικαιόω, "to justify") — courtroom language. **Hupokrisis** (ὑπόκρισις, 2:13) — theatrical "play-acting." **Sunesthiō** (συνεσθίω, 2:12, "to eat with") — the verb at the table.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

In the Betz rhetorical scheme, chapter 2 sits across two structural seams. Verses 1–14 complete the narratio (the autobiographical account of facts) begun in 1:12. Verses 15–21 are the propositio — the formal thesis statement of the letter, which the rest of the argument (chapters 3–4 probatio, 5–6 exhortatio) will defend.

The narratio is structured as two paired vignettes. The Jerusalem episode (2:1–10) shows Paul's gospel ratified by the pillars without modification. The Antioch episode (2:11–14) shows Paul defending that same gospel against Cephas in public. The pairing is rhetorical: Paul has been faithful when others — even Cephas, even Barnabas — have wavered. The Jerusalem episode is told with diplomatic care (note Paul's hedge "those who seemed to be pillars" — repeated three times in 2:6, 2:9 — which is hard to read as anything but ironic). The Antioch episode is told with confrontational force.

The propositio (2:15–21) is one of the densest theological passages in the New Testament. The "we" Paul deploys in 2:15 ("we ourselves are Jews by birth") is rhetorically loaded. He continues to address Cephas through verse 21, but the address has slipped into theological speech that the Galatian readers are meant to overhear. The triple repetition of the contrast — "not justified by works of the law but through pistis Christou" three times in 2:16 alone — is hammer rhetoric. The chiastic structure of 2:19–20 (I died to the law / so that I might live to God / I have been crucified with Christ / it is no longer I who live / but Christ who lives in me / and the life I now live / I live by faith) is one of Paul's most carefully crafted sentences.

The diatribe style emerges in 2:17 ("Is Christ then a servant of sin? Certainly not!" — mē genoito, μὴ γένοιτο). This is the Stoic-Cynic preacher's rhetorical question-and-rejection, deployed by Paul also in Romans 6:2; 7:7; 11:1. The function is to surface and crush an objection.

Verse 14's rebuke to Cephas ("If you, though a Jew, live like a Gentile and not like a Jew, how can you force the Gentiles to live like Jews?") is one of the chapter's interpretive cruxes. It is not clear whether Paul's quotation of his own speech ends at verse 14 or continues into the propositio of 2:15ff. Calvin took it to end at 14; many modern commentators (Betz, Martyn) extend it into 2:21. The decision affects how we read the audience of 2:15–21 — Cephas at Antioch, or the Galatians, or both.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** No chapter of the New Testament has produced more patristic argument than Galatians 2:11–14. The dispute between Augustine and Jerome over the Antioch incident, conducted by letter from approximately AD 394 to 405 (Augustine's Letters 28, 40, 71, 75, 82; Jerome's responses 75, 81, 105), is one of the foundational debates of Latin theology. Jerome, following Origen and Chrysostom, argued that Peter and Paul staged the confrontation pedagogically — Peter pretended to lapse so that Paul could publicly defend the gospel for the benefit of onlookers. Augustine was scandalized: this would make the apostles liars and the inspired text a mere stage drama. He insisted that Peter's hypocrisy was real, his rebuke was real, and Paul's correction was real. Augustine's reading eventually prevailed in the West and became the foundation for the Catholic doctrine that Petrine authority is not impeccable in conduct, only in office.

Marius Victorinus's Latin commentary (c. 362) treated the incident as plain rebuke; Ambrosiaster and Theodore of Mopsuestia followed similar lines. John Chrysostom, preaching in Antioch itself, leaned toward Jerome's pedagogical reading — perhaps reluctant to let his city's apostle stand condemned. Aquinas's Galatians lectures (c. 1265) generally followed Augustine. Luther's 1535 commentary made Galatians 2:16 the heart of the Reformation, reading the Antioch incident as proof that even Peter could fall from the gospel and need correction. Calvin's commentary (1548) was substantively similar but more measured.

The Council of Trent's Decree on Justification (Session VI, January 1547) condemned the Lutheran formula. It taught that justification is a real interior renewal (not merely a forensic declaration), that the justified can grow in righteousness through cooperation with grace, and that faith is necessary but not alone (canons 9, 11, 24). The Lutheran-Catholic disagreement on Galatians 2:16 hardened for four centuries.

The Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification, signed by the Lutheran World Federation and the Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity on October 31, 1999 (the anniversary of Luther's 95 Theses), articulated a "differentiated consensus": the condemnations of the sixteenth century do not apply to the partner church's contemporary teaching, even though doctrinal differences in emphasis remain. The World Methodist Council joined in 2006, the Anglican Communion in 2016, and the World Communion of Reformed Churches in 2017.

The New Perspective on Paul (Sanders, Dunn, Wright) revolutionized the reading of 2:16 in the late 20th century. Dunn's signature claim, made in his 1982 Manson Lecture and developed in his Romans WBC and his 1993 Galatians BNTC, is that "works of the law" (erga nomou) refers specifically to Jewish boundary markers — circumcision, food laws, Sabbath — not human moral effort generally. The 4QMMT parallel reinforced this. On this reading, Paul is not refuting medieval works-righteousness; he is refuting ethnic privilege. N.T. Wright extends the argument: justification is fundamentally about who counts as a member of God's people, not about how an individual stands before God's tribunal.

The Lutheran-Reformed counter-response (Stephen Westerholm's Perspectives Old and New on Paul, 2004; Thomas Schreiner's Faith Alone, 2015; Frank Thielman; Simon Gathercole's Where Is Boasting?, 2002) argues the New Perspective is right that Second Temple Judaism was a religion of grace but wrong to limit "works of the law" to boundary markers. D.A. Carson, Peter O'Brien, and Mark Seifrid co-edited Justification and Variegated Nomism (2001, 2004) to argue this.

The pistis Christou debate cuts across these party lines. Richard Hays's 1983 dissertation (The Faith of Jesus Christ) argued for the subjective genitive: 2:16 means we are justified through Jesus's own faithfulness, not our faith in him. Wright, Campbell, Martyn, and de Boer have endorsed this. Dunn and Schreiner defend the objective genitive. The shift matters: if pistis Christou is subjective, the entire propositio is christologically grounded in Christ's faithful obedience (echoing Romans 5:18–19 and Philippians 2:5–11). If objective, the propositio is anthropologically grounded in human believing.

The apocalyptic Paul school (Martyn's 1997 AYB Galatians) reads 2:19–20 as the heart of the gospel's apocalyptic structure: the believer is co-crucified with Christ and lives in the new creation. The "I" that has died is the cosmic-anthropological self that belonged to the old age.

**Jewish scholarly critique and its evolution.** Joseph Klausner's From Jesus to Paul (1939) and Hans Joachim Schoeps's Paul (1959) treated Galatians 2 as Paul's break with Judaism and the founding of a new religion. Both saw Paul as misreading Torah's actual function in Jewish life.

Daniel Boyarin's A Radical Jew (1994) reread Galatians 2 as Paul's universalist allegorical move within Hellenistic Judaism: the body's particularity (circumcision) is dethroned in favor of a spiritual universal. Boyarin reads Paul sympathetically as a Jewish thinker but sees the move as still problematic for Jewish particularity.

Mark Nanos's The Irony of Galatians (2002) argues Paul never opposed Torah for Jewish-Christians — including Cephas. The Antioch incident is about whether Gentile believers can join Jewish-Christian table fellowship without becoming Jews. Cephas's withdrawal forces them into a second-class status pressuring conversion. Paul's rebuke is for halakhic compromise, not Torah observance as such. Pamela Eisenbaum (Paul Was Not a Christian, 2009) and Paula Fredriksen (Paul: The Pagans' Apostle, 2017) extend this: Paul thought the eschaton imminent, and his task was to bring Gentiles to the God of Israel without making them Jews.

Amy-Jill Levine has noted how Galatians 2 has been read through anti-Judaic lenses — Cephas the Jew vs. Paul the Christian, Judaism the law vs. Christianity the gospel — and how that framing distorts the text. Post-Holocaust theological reckoning, including Nostra Aetate (1965), the World Council of Churches' 1988 statement, and the Pontifical Biblical Commission's The Jewish People and Their Sacred Scriptures in the Christian Bible (2001), has tried to recover a non-supersessionist reading.

**Convergence and divergence.** All schools agree the chapter records a real conflict. They diverge on what was at stake. Was it (a) Lutheran justification by faith vs. medieval works-righteousness avant la lettre (Augustine, Luther, Westerholm)? Was it (b) ethnic privilege vs. universal Abrahamic family (Wright, Dunn)? Was it (c) halakhic table fellowship within Judaism (Nanos, Eisenbaum)? Was it (d) cosmic apocalyptic invasion of the old age (Martyn)? The text supports something of all four — which is why the chapter remains contested.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did Paul believe God was doing? He believed that the death and resurrection of the Messiah had created a new community in which Jew and Gentile sit at the same table on the same basis. To withdraw from that table is to nullify the cross (2:21). The act of God Paul is defending is the establishment of one mixed eschatological people whose unity is constituted by Christ's faithfulness and made visible in shared meals. The theology is enacted when bread is passed.

**Tension 1: What "works of the law" means and what justification undoes.** When Paul says no one is justified by works of the law (2:16), what is he ruling out?

The Lutheran-Reformed reading (Westerholm, Schreiner, Carson) takes "works of the law" as all human moral and religious effort to merit standing before God. Justification is the forensic declaration of righteousness on the basis of Christ's imputed righteousness, received through faith. The propositio of Galatians 2:15–21 is the foundation of this entire framework, and its application is universal: whether Jew or Gentile, no one earns God's favor.

The Catholic reading (Trent Session VI; CCC 1987–2029; Joint Declaration 1999) holds that justification is a real interior renewal — the infusion of sanctifying grace that makes the sinner actually righteous, not merely declared so. Faith is the root and beginning of justification but operates through love (Galatians 5:6). "Works of the law" excluded by Paul are pre-grace works performed apart from Christ; works done in grace are themselves grace's fruit and contribute to growth in justification. The 1999 Joint Declaration affirmed that Lutherans and Catholics could now confess together that "by grace alone, in faith in Christ's saving work and not because of any merit on our part, we are accepted by God" (§15).

The Orthodox reading (Lossky, Florovsky, John Romanides) frames justification within the larger arc of theosis. The forensic categories of the West are not the primary frame; the question is participation in divine life. "Works of the law" name a reduction of communion with God to legal contract. Justification and sanctification are not separable moments but aspects of a single transformative union.

The New Perspective reading (Dunn, Wright) takes erga nomou specifically as the boundary markers (circumcision, kashrut, Sabbath) that distinguished Jew from Gentile. Justification is the verdict that someone is a member of the eschatological covenant family — and Paul's claim is that this verdict is rendered through Christ's faithfulness (or faith in Christ), not through ethnic markers.

The Paul Within Judaism reading (Nanos, Eisenbaum, Fredriksen) takes "works of the law" as halakhic boundary markers that, if required of Gentile believers, would force them to convert to Judaism. Paul does not oppose Torah observance for Jews; he opposes its imposition on Gentiles.

The steelman of the Catholic position: Paul's own language — "the life I now live in the flesh I live by faith in the Son of God" (2:20), "if righteousness were through the law, then Christ died for no purpose" (2:21) — describes a real ontological union with Christ that produces real righteousness. The Lutheran reading reduces this union to a legal fiction. Trent's condemnation of merely forensic justification has more textual support in the chapter than Lutheran tradition has wanted to admit. Where this leaves us: the 1999 Joint Declaration has narrowed but not closed the gap. The chapter genuinely sustains both a forensic-declarative reading and a participationist-transformational reading. Paul is doing something each tradition has captured part of and neither has captured whole.

**Tension 2: The pistis Christou question and what kind of faith justifies.** Is 2:16's pistis Christou "faith in Christ" (objective genitive) or "the faithfulness of Christ" (subjective genitive)?

The traditional reading (Dunn, Schreiner, Matlock, most translations including the ESV) takes the objective genitive: we are justified by our faith placed in Christ. This preserves the centrality of human faith as the appropriating instrument of salvation and aligns with the Reformation's hermeneutic of receptive faith.

The subjective-genitive reading (Hays, Wright, Campbell, Martyn, de Boer, Hooker) takes pistis Christou as Christ's own faithfulness — his obedient, trusting, covenant-keeping life and death. On this reading, Galatians 2:16 says we are justified by the faithfulness of Christ (and we also believe in Christ as our response). The Christology becomes the ground; human faith becomes the response, not the cause. Hays argues this fits Paul's elsewhere usage (Romans 3:21–26; Philippians 3:9), the parallel construction of pistis Abraam (the faith of Abraham) in Romans 4:16, and the larger narrative of Christ's obedient self-giving in Philippians 2.

The steelman of the subjective-genitive reading: it dethrones human faith from being the meritorious ground of justification (which it was always uncomfortable being) and re-grounds salvation in Christ's own action. It also better fits Paul's flow: the Antioch incident is about Christ's faithfulness creating a mixed community, not about whether each individual exercises sufficient faith. The objective-genitive steelman: Paul's other formulations (Romans 1:16; 10:9–10) are unambiguous about human faith's instrumental role, and the subjective genitive risks making salvation impersonal. Where this leaves us: both are grammatically possible. Paul may have been deliberately or unconsciously playing on the ambiguity. The English translation tradition defaults to objective; the subjective is gaining ground.

**One unresolved question.** If the cross has actually torn down the Jew-Gentile boundary at table (2:14), why does the New Testament elsewhere preserve so much of that boundary in practice — Acts 21:20 has James telling Paul about thousands of Torah-observant Jewish believers, Acts 16:3 has Paul circumcising Timothy, and Romans 14 has Paul accommodating both observant and non-observant believers without forcing the issue? What is the actual content of the unity Galatians 2 demands, and how does it differ from the unity Romans 14 envisions?

## The Thread

**Acts 10:9–48.** Cephas's vision of the unclean animals on the rooftop in Joppa, his command to enter Cornelius's house, his witness of Gentile reception of the Spirit — all this happened before Galatians 2:11–14. When Cephas withdraws from the table at Antioch, he is acting against his own earlier received revelation. Paul's rebuke is grounded in a memory of what Cephas himself had once seen.

**Genesis 12:1–3 and Genesis 15:6.** Galatians 2's propositio sets up the Abrahamic argument that explodes in chapter 3. When Paul says no one is justified by works of the law, he is preparing to argue from Genesis 15:6 that Abraham was reckoned righteous by faith — before circumcision, before Torah. When you get to Galatians 3:6, remember 2:16.

**Romans 3:21–4:25.** Paul's argument in Galatians 2:16 reappears in expanded form in Romans, where the Abrahamic argument becomes the structural center of his theology. Romans 3:22's pistis Iēsou Christou repeats the genitive crux. When you get to Romans 4, remember Galatians 2: this is the doctrine being argued against opponents in Galatia, then re-presented to a church Paul has not founded.

**Acts 15:1–35.** The Jerusalem Council's decree — that Gentile believers need not be circumcised, but should abstain from food sacrificed to idols, blood, things strangled, and sexual immorality — is conspicuously absent from Galatians 2. Whether Acts 15 had not yet happened, or Paul considered it irrelevant, or Luke and Paul remember different events, is one of the great chronological puzzles of the New Testament. The decree's compromise on food restrictions does not match Galatians' radical assertion of free table fellowship — and that mismatch is part of the church's enduring difficulty with this chapter.
