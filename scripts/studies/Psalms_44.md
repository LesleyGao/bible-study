# Psalm 44 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 44 carries the superscription *lamnatzeach livnei-Qorach maskil* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ לִבְנֵי־קֹרַח מַשְׂכִּיל), "To the choirmaster. A *Maskil* of the Sons of Korah." Like Pss 42–43, it is a Korahite *maskil* — a "skillful" or "instructive" composition associated with the Levitical guild whose pedigree runs through Numbers 26:11 and 1 Chronicles 6. Where Pss 42–43 are individual lament, Ps 44 is its **communal counterpart**: a national lament voiced in the first-person plural — *we have heard*, *our fathers told us*, *we have not forgotten you*, *we are killed all the day long*.

Form-critically, Ps 44 is a paradigmatic **community lament** in Gunkel's *Klagelied des Volkes* category — perhaps the clearest example in the Psalter, alongside Ps 74 and Ps 79. The form runs: address-and-historical-recital (vv. 1–8), complaint-against-God (vv. 9–16), protestation-of-innocence (vv. 17–22), and petition (vv. 23–26). Mowinckel placed Ps 44's *Sitz im Leben* in a **public day of fasting and lament** following military defeat — the kind of cultic occasion described in 1 Kings 8:33, 2 Chronicles 20:3–13, Joel 1–2, and the post-exilic fasts catalogued in Zechariah 7–8. The community gathered, the priests led, the temple singers sang.

Dating is contested. Earlier critical scholarship favored a Maccabean date (mid-second century BCE), reading the protestation of innocence in vv. 17–22 as fitting the persecutions under Antiochus IV. More recent scholarship (Hossfeld–Zenger, Goldingay, Kraus) tends toward a **late preexilic or exilic** origin: the language of "scattered among the nations" (v. 11) and "made us a byword among the nations" (v. 14) fits the post-722 northern dispersion or the post-587 Babylonian exile. The Korahite ascription, the Elohistic vocabulary (this psalm uses *Elohim* throughout, no YHWH), and the editorial placement in Book II all point to a redaction at home in the late-monarchic-to-postexilic Levitical singer tradition.

Hebrew terms worth marking:

- *kemo-tzon le-tivchah* (כְּמוֹ צֹאן לְטִבְחָה), "as sheep for slaughter" (44:11). The verb *natan* + the simile renders Israel as livestock destined for the knife. The phrase recurs more sharply in v. 22 — *ki-aleikha horagnu kol-hayom nechshavnu ke-tzon tivchah* (כִּי־עָלֶיךָ הֹרַגְנוּ כָל־הַיּוֹם נֶחְשַׁבְנוּ כְּצֹאן טִבְחָה), "for your sake we are killed all the day long; we are regarded as sheep to be slaughtered."
- *yemin-kha u-zero'akha ve-or panekha* (יְמִינְךָ וּזְרוֹעֲךָ וְאוֹר פָּנֶיךָ), "your right hand, your arm, and the light of your face" (44:3). Triadic stack of Exodus-conquest theology.
- *urah lamah tishan Adonai* (עוּרָה לָמָּה תִישַׁן אֲדֹנָי), "awake! why are you sleeping, O Lord?" (44:23). One of the most theologically scandalous lines in the Hebrew Bible — the imperative to wake God. Use of *Adonai* here is one of the rare non-*Elohim* divine names in the psalm.
- *al-tizkach* and *qumah ezratah lanu* (אַל־תִּזְנַח … קוּמָה עֶזְרָתָה לָּנוּ), "do not reject … rise up to our help" (44:23, 26). Imperatives directed *at* God.

The DSS evidence: 4QPs(c) contains fragments of Ps 44 consistent with the MT. The LXX numbers it Ps 43 and translates *kemo-tzon tivchah* as *hōs probata sphagēs* (ὡς πρόβατα σφαγῆς) — the exact Greek phrase Paul will later quote in Romans 8:36.

The psalm sits in the **Elohistic Psalter** (Pss 42–83); Ps 44 uses *Elohim* about a dozen times and YHWH-as-tetragrammaton not at all (v. 23 has *Adonai*, a substitute). This is consistent with the Korahite-cluster pattern.

## Lens 2: Literature

The poem is built in four clearly demarcated movements:

- **vv. 1–8 — Historical-recital**: God's past saving deeds, the conquest, the planting of Israel "by your right hand."
- **vv. 9–16 — Complaint**: the abrupt *aph-zanachta* ("but you have rejected") shifts the tense and the mood; God is now subject of verbs of *rejection, disgrace, scattering, selling, mocking.*
- **vv. 17–22 — Protestation of innocence**: "all this has come upon us, though we have not forgotten you" — the Deuteronomic theology of *tit-for-tat* national punishment is explicitly *refused.*
- **vv. 23–26 — Petition**: the imperative-cluster — awake! arise! rise up! redeem!

The poem's most striking literary device is the **second-person address that reverses direction at v. 9**. Through vv. 1–8 the "you" is the God of triumph: *atah hatzaltanu*, "you have saved us." From v. 9 forward the "you" is the God of disaster: *atah zanachta*, "you have rejected us." The pronouns do not change; the verbs do. Berlin's grammatical-parallelism analysis here is especially incisive: the same syntactic frame — "you-verb-us" — is filled with opposite contents. Wenham reads this as the rhetorical heart of the psalm: *the God we praise and the God we accuse are the same God*. There is no second power, no Marcionite alibi.

Lowth's parallelism categories are dense throughout. The historical-recital uses synonymous parallelism with intensification: "you with your own hand drove out the nations // but them you planted." The complaint uses **antithetical parallelism with surprise** — "you have made us like sheep for slaughter // and have scattered us among the nations" — where the second colon does not soften but compounds the first. The protestation of innocence uses **conditional parallelism**: "if we had forgotten the name of our God // or spread out our hands to a foreign god, // would not God discover this?" — a rhetorical *if-then* that places the burden of explanation on God himself.

There is a *Selah* at v. 8, marking the boundary between the historical-recital-and-trust section and the complaint. The shift across the *Selah* is the most violent transition in the Psalter: from "in God we have boasted continually" to "but you have rejected us." The musical pause buys the singers a breath before the accusation begins.

Westermann's lament-form template is fully visible: invocation, complaint (here doubled — both *thou-complaint* against God and *we-complaint* about the people), protestation of trust *and* innocence (the latter is unusual), and petition. What is missing is the typical "vow of praise" that closes most laments. Ps 44 ends on the imperative *gola'enu lema'an chasdekha* — "redeem us for the sake of your steadfast love." There is no anticipated praise. The psalm leaves the singer mid-prayer, hands lifted, mouth open.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Placement.** Ps 44 sits in the Korahite cluster (Pss 42–49), inside Book II, inside the Elohistic Psalter. The cluster opens with the individual longing of Pss 42–43, deepens into the corporate lament of Ps 44, then moves to royal celebration (Ps 45) and Zion-confidence (Pss 46–48). The editorial sequence is theologically loaded: communal lament precedes royal hope and Zion-praise. Hossfeld–Zenger argue this is deliberate — the Korahite editors wanted the question of *why have you rejected us* asked before the songs of *God is our refuge* are sung. Lament earns the right to praise.

**Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Ps 44 dwells on v. 22 — *aleikha horagnu kol-hayom*, "for your sake we are killed all the day long." Rabbinic tradition reads the verse as the pre-emptive martyrology of Israel: "for your sake" makes suffering covenantal-sacrificial, not punitive. The midrash explicitly cites the Hadrianic persecutions (Bar Kokhba, 132–135 CE) as the verse's later fulfillment. Rashi reads the protestation of innocence as the cry of the righteous remnant in any generation. Ibn Ezra and Radak both grapple with the theological scandal: how can a covenant-faithful people suffer? Their answer is that God's purposes exceed the simple Deuteronomic schema. Nachmanides quotes v. 22 in his disputation at Barcelona (1263).

In modern Jewish reception, **Ps 44 has become the principal post-Holocaust psalm**. Emil Fackenheim's "614th commandment" — that Jews must not give Hitler a posthumous victory by abandoning the covenant — is a direct theological extension of the protestation of innocence in vv. 17–18. Dorothee Sölle's *Suffering* (writing across the Christian-Jewish boundary) cites Ps 44 as the canonical text of innocent corporate suffering. Levenson's *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* takes the awakening-imperative of v. 23 as central: the believer's prayer is to *wake God up*, not to make peace with absence. Sarna emphasizes that the psalm refuses theodicy — it does not explain suffering, it accuses. Heschel reads the psalm as the prophetic posture of arguing with God. Liturgically, parts of Ps 44 are recited on certain communal fast days, and the psalm has been incorporated into post-Holocaust Yom HaShoah liturgies in some communities.

**Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarratio* on Ps 43 (his numbering of our 44) reads the suffering church as the community in vv. 17–22 — innocent of apostasy, yet handed over to slaughter. Augustine cites Romans 8:36 explicitly and reads the psalm Christologically: the suffering church is the body of the suffering Christ. Cassiodorus and Aquinas develop the same line. Luther preached on Ps 44 during the Peasants' War aftermath; he heard the protestation of innocence as the cry of the persecuted gospel-believer. Calvin (1557) — writing as French Protestants were being burned — applied vv. 17–22 directly to the Reformed martyrs of his day. The psalm became the **principal Reformed lament** during the wars of religion, the Huguenot persecutions, the Covenanter risings. The metrical psalter tradition (Geneva, Genevan, Scottish) sang Ps 44 frequently.

Modern critical commentary: Gunkel established the form-critical reading; Mowinckel placed it cultically; Westermann mapped the lament-form. Kraus's *Psalmen* commentary remains the standard German-critical reference; Goldingay and Tate provide the standard English-critical readings. Hossfeld–Zenger's Hermeneia volume is exhaustive on the Korahite cluster. Brueggemann makes Ps 44 a flagship of his "psalms of disorientation" and argues that the absence of *vow of praise* is a deliberate refusal: the psalm will not let the singer pretend to a resolution she has not received. Soong-Chan Rah's *Prophetic Lament* and Eberhard Bons's German-Catholic readings both develop the lament-as-protest tradition. Wenham, Anderson, Miller, Brown, Tanner, and Gillingham contribute chapters.

**NT use.** Paul quotes Ps 44:22 verbatim in **Romans 8:36** — *kathōs gegraptai hoti heneken sou thanatoumetha holēn tēn hēmeran, elogisthēmen hōs probata sphagēs* — inside his "more than conquerors" passage. The quotation is theologically loaded. Paul does *not* explain the verse away; he affirms it. *We are killed all the day long.* The Pauline answer is not that suffering disappears but that nothing — neither death nor life nor angels nor rulers — can separate the suffering from the love of God in Christ. Hebrews 11:32–38 also draws on Ps 44's protestation-of-innocent-suffering tradition.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

The psalm presses two tensions worth holding.

**Tension one: Innocent corporate suffering vs. Deuteronomic theology.** The Deuteronomic schema (Deut 28; the framework of Joshua-Judges-Kings) holds that national disaster follows national sin. Ps 44 explicitly *refuses* this schema. Vv. 17–22 are a sustained protestation: we have not forgotten you, our heart has not turned back, our steps have not departed from your way — *and yet* you have broken us. Five mapped positions on what to do with this:

- *Catholic*: Ps 44 voices the suffering church-in-history, the *ecclesia patiens*, whose innocent suffering is folded into the paschal mystery. Aquinas's reading: the merits of the head flow to the members; the church suffers redemptively in union with Christ.
- *Protestant Reformed (Calvin, Edwards)*: the psalm voices the elect under the cross — the *theologia crucis* in which the believer's apparent abandonment is the very sign of God's hidden work. Calvin reads vv. 22–23 as a call to faith that holds on against appearances. The Reformed tradition has typically refused to soften the protestation.
- *Orthodox*: the psalm is martyrological — the church as the suffering body sharing in the *kenosis* of Christ; the slaughter of the saints fills up what is lacking in the afflictions of Christ (Col 1:24).
- *Jewish (Levenson, Sarna, Fackenheim)*: the psalm is post-Holocaust scripture — the protest-prayer of an innocent covenant people that refuses theodicy and demands divine action. Fackenheim's 614th commandment is its modern extension.
- *Critical / liberation (Brueggemann, Soong-Chan Rah, Sölle)*: lament-as-protest is a permanent and necessary mode of biblical faith; the church's liturgy must include the *thou-complaint* against God or it falsifies the gospel. The psalm authorizes the speech of the wronged.

The least intuitive position, for a pious-Christian ear, is the lament-as-protest reading. Steelman: Ps 44 does not merely complain about enemies; it accuses *God*. *You* have rejected us. *You* have sold your people for a trifle. *You* have broken us. To soften these accusations into "permitted us to be tested" is to lose the psalm's theological work. The Bible authorizes the believer to say *to* God what cannot be true *about* God — and to keep covenant with God in the saying. This is not faithlessness; it is the opposite: it is the faith that refuses to leave the relationship even when the relationship is the source of the wound.

**Tension two: Romans 8:36 — Pauline appropriation.** Paul cites *aleikha horagnu kol-hayom* without dissolving its scandal. The Christian movement of the verse is not "we *were* sheep for slaughter, *now* in Christ we are not"; it is "we *are* killed all the day long, and *nothing* in all creation can separate us from God's love." Mapped positions:

- *Augustinian*: Christ is the suffering psalmist who takes Israel's protestation of innocence and makes it true; the church then participates in his innocent sacrificial suffering.
- *Reformed (Calvin)*: the protestation belongs to the elect-in-Christ; the *for your sake* of v. 22 is now the *for your sake* of cross-bearing discipleship.
- *Catholic / Orthodox martyrological*: the verse is the charter of Christian martyrdom; the church's slaughtered ones are not failures but witnesses.
- *Jewish*: the verse remains primarily Israel's prayer; Paul's Christian appropriation is real but secondary, and the Jewish post-Holocaust reading is theologically prior.

**Where this leaves us:** Ps 44 ends with *gola'enu* — "redeem us." No vow of praise. The poem does not resolve. The Christian who recites this psalm — and the Jew who recites it — is taught that the appropriate prayer of the covenant people in catastrophe is not *thank you* but *wake up.* Romans 8 does not erase Ps 44; it stretches it. *We are killed all the day long* and *nothing can separate us* are the same sentence in two languages.

**Closing question.** If the most-quoted line of Ps 44 in the New Testament — *as sheep to be slaughtered* — was lifted by Paul without softening, what does the contemporary church lose when it quietly drops the protestation-of-innocence and the awake-O-Lord imperatives from its corporate prayers?

## The Thread

- Deuteronomy 28 — the covenantal blessings-and-curses framework that Ps 44 explicitly contests.
- Romans 8:35–39 — Paul's quotation and the "more than conquerors" answer.
- Hebrews 11:35–38 — those of whom the world was not worthy; the protestation-of-innocent-suffering tradition.
- Revelation 6:9–10 — "how long, O Lord, holy and true, before you judge?" — the awake-imperative carried into the Apocalypse.
