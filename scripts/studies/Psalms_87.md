# Psalm 87 — Going Deeper

Psalm 87 is one of the shortest psalms in the Psalter and arguably one of the most theologically explosive. In seven verses the Korahite poets manage to register the great enemy-empires of Israel — Egypt, Babylon, Philistia, Tyre, Cush — as native-born citizens of Zion. It is the moment in the Hebrew Bible where particularist national-election theology cracks open and something universalist shines through. Christian readers have heard in it the seed of Paul's "Jerusalem above is our mother"; Jewish readers have heard the cosmic Zion of the prophets; both have struggled to say exactly what is happening when God leans over the registry of nations and says, of an Egyptian or a Babylonian, "this one was born here."

## Lens 1: Historical World

**Superscription.** *Livne-Korach mizmor shir* (לִבְנֵי־קֹרַח מִזְמוֹר שִׁיר) — "Of the Sons of Korah. A Psalm. A Song." The doubled designation *mizmor shir* (a "psalm-song") occurs only a handful of times in the Psalter and tends to mark psalms intended for cultic singing, often in liturgical procession. The Sons of Korah were a Levitical guild assigned to temple music (1 Chr 6:31–38; 2 Chr 20:19); eleven psalms bear their name (42–49, 84–85, 87–88), and most of them cluster around Zion-theology and the longing for the temple courts. Psalm 87 is their high point.

**Gunkel's form-critical genre.** Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 87 as a *Zionslied*, a Song of Zion, alongside Psalms 46, 48, 76, 122, and 137. The Songs of Zion celebrate Jerusalem not merely as a political capital but as the cosmic dwelling-place of YHWH and the navel of the world. What distinguishes Ps 87 from the others is its outward orientation: where Ps 48 and 76 narrate the defense of Zion *against* the nations, Ps 87 narrates the inscription of the nations *into* Zion. Gunkel himself called it eschatologically charged and unusually difficult; later form critics (Mowinckel, Kraus, Hossfeld–Zenger) have generally followed his Zionslied designation while debating whether the psalm reflects a specific cultic procession (perhaps a pilgrimage festival) or a more abstract theological vision.

**Date.** The text gives no firm anchor. The list of nations — Egypt (under the cipher *Rahab*), Babylon, Philistia, Tyre, Cush — could fit anywhere from the late monarchy through the post-exilic period. The fact that Babylon appears prominently has led most commentators (Kraus, Tate, Goldingay) to favor a sixth-century or post-exilic date, when Babylon had become the paradigmatic enemy. Hossfeld and Zenger place it in the Persian period, reading it as a deliberate post-exilic reframing of Zion theology in light of diaspora realities. A pre-exilic date is not impossible, but the universalist sweep coheres best with the prophetic horizons of Second and Third Isaiah (Isa 2:2–4; 19:18–25; 56:6–8; 60:1–14; 66:18–21), which most scholars place in or after the exile.

**ANE parallels.** Mesopotamian and Egyptian hymnody routinely celebrates the royal city as the cosmic mountain, the navel-of-the-earth, the meeting-place of heaven and earth. Babylon's *Enuma Elish* makes Babylon itself the dwelling-place built for the gods after creation; Egyptian hymns to Thebes ("the city of cities") use comparable language. Psalm 87 borrows this ANE city-hymn vocabulary — *the holy mount*, *the city of God*, *founded by him* — but inverts its political logic. In ANE hymnody the foreign nations are subjugated *under* the cosmic city; in Ps 87 they are registered *within* it as native citizens.

**Hebrew terms.** A handful of words carry the freight.

- *Yesudato beharerei-qodesh* (יְסוּדָתוֹ בְּהַרְרֵי־קֹדֶשׁ, v. 1) — literally "his foundation is in the holy mountains." The plural *harerei-qodesh* echoes the cosmic-mountain motif.
- *Rahab* (רַהַב, v. 4) — "tumult," "raging one"; a chaos-monster name (Job 9:13, 26:12; Isa 51:9; Ps 89:10) used here as a poetic cipher for **Egypt**. Critically, this is *not* the Rahab of Jericho (Josh 2), whose name is a different Hebrew word (*Rachav*, רָחָב, "broad"). The chaos-Rahab here is the primordial sea-monster slain at creation; calling Egypt by this name evokes the Exodus crossing of the Red Sea (cf. Isa 51:9–10).
- *Ish ve-ish yullad-bah* (אִישׁ וְאִישׁ יֻלַּד־בָּהּ, vv. 5–6) — "this one and that one were born in her." The doubled *ish ve-ish* is idiomatic Hebrew for "every single one"; the passive *yullad* ("was born") is the universalist hinge.
- *Yitspor bi-khtov ammim* (יִסְפֹּר בִּכְתוֹב עַמִּים, v. 6) — "He records as he registers the peoples." The verb *safar* (count, register) and the noun *ammim* (peoples) together evoke a divine census, a heavenly birth-registry.
- *Kol-mayanai bakh* (כָּל־מַעְיָנַי בָּךְ, v. 7) — "all my springs are in you." *Mayan* is a fountain or spring; the metaphor is of Zion as the source of life-giving water (cf. Ps 36:9; Isa 12:3; Ezek 47:1–12).

**DSS / LXX.** Psalm 87 is preserved (fragmentarily) in 11QPsa and 4QPsa. The Qumran witnesses offer no major variants from the MT. The LXX (Ps 86 in its numbering) reads broadly the same, though it slightly softens some of the universalist language by translating *yullad* with γεγένηται ("has come to be") rather than the more concrete "was born." The Vulgate follows the LXX; Augustine read Ps 87 in this Greek-derived form, which shaped his "Jerusalem above" exegesis.

## Lens 2: The Literature

**Lowth's parallelism.** Robert Lowth's three categories — synonymous, antithetic, synthetic — are all on display, though Ps 87 leans heavily on synthetic parallelism (the second line completes or extends the first rather than restating it). "The LORD loves the gates of Zion / more than all the dwelling places of Jacob" (v. 2) is classically synthetic: the second colon does not echo the first but elevates Zion above the rest of Israel's geography. "Glorious things of you are spoken / O city of God" (v. 3) intensifies through direct address.

**Strophic structure.** Most commentators divide the psalm into three strophes marked by the two *selah* notations:

1. **Strophe 1 (vv. 1–3):** Foundation and praise of Zion — the cosmic city.
2. **Strophe 2 (vv. 4–6):** The registry of the nations — universalist core.
3. **Strophe 3 (v. 7):** Liturgical climax — singers, dancers, springs.

The two *selah* markers fall at the end of strophes 1 and 2 (vv. 3, 6), suggesting musical rest-points before each new movement. The third strophe is brief and processional, the kind of closing one would expect at the climax of a temple liturgy.

**Inclusio.** The psalm opens with Zion's *foundation* (*yesudato*, v. 1) and closes with Zion's *springs* (*mayanai*, v. 7) — a foundation-to-fountain arc that frames the city as both rooted in primordial holiness and overflowing with life. The opening "founded" and the closing "all my springs are in you" form a thematic inclusio: the city YHWH founded is the city from which all life flows.

**Chiasmus.** Within the central strophe (vv. 4–6), commentators have noted a chiastic structure organized around the registry-formula:

- A "I mention Rahab and Babylon… Philistia, Tyre, Cush" (v. 4a)
- B "This one was born there" (v. 4b)
- C "Of Zion it shall be said" (v. 5a)
- B' "This one and that one were born in her" (v. 5b)
- A' "The LORD records as he registers the peoples: 'this one was born there'" (v. 6)

The chiasm centers on the divine speech-act: it is YHWH who establishes Zion (v. 5c) and YHWH who keeps the registry (v. 6). The nations are spoken about; YHWH is the speaker.

**Selah.** The two *selah* markers (vv. 3, 6) likely indicate musical interludes or congregational responses. Their placement reinforces the strophic division and slows the reader at exactly the two moments most laden with theology: the praise of Zion as "city of God" and the divine registry of the nations.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Book III closes at Psalm 89, but Psalm 87 sits inside a Korahite cluster** (Pss 84–85, 87–88) that frames the closing arc of Book III with intense Zion-longing followed by the desolation of Pss 88–89. Gerald Wilson's canonical-shape reading (*The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter*, 1985) traces a deliberate editorial arc through Books I–III that plots the rise, peak, and tragic collapse of the Davidic-messianic kingdom. Psalm 87, with its vision of Zion as mother of nations, sits at the high-water mark of that arc — a final glimpse of what Zion was supposed to be — before Pss 88–89 plunge the reader into darkness and the failure of covenant. David Mitchell (*The Message of the Psalter*, 1997) extends Wilson's reading eschatologically, arguing that the editor preserved Ps 87 precisely because its universalist vision points beyond the historical collapse to a restored Zion at the end of the age.

**Jewish trajectory.** Rabbinic tradition wrestled hard with the universalism of Ps 87. *Midrash Tehillim* on Ps 87 takes the registry-language seriously: God will, in the messianic age, register the nations among the citizens of Zion, and even the great enemy-empires will be inscribed there. Rashi reads the psalm eschatologically — the future Zion will receive the proselytes and converts of the nations. Ibn Ezra is more cautious, suggesting the registry refers specifically to Israelite captives born in foreign lands who are nonetheless counted as native to Zion. Radak (David Kimchi) splits the difference: the literal reference is to diaspora Jews born abroad, but the eschatological horizon includes the nations who will turn to YHWH. Modern Jewish thinkers — Martin Buber on the dialogical Zion, Abraham Joshua Heschel on prophetic universalism, Jon Levenson on the cosmic mountain (*Sinai and Zion*, 1985), Nahum Sarna on the registry-motif — generally read Ps 87 as a counter-weight to the more particularist strands of biblical theology, a reminder that the election of Israel was for the sake of the nations. Liturgically, Ps 87 is recited in some Jewish traditions on the Three Pilgrimage Festivals and on Yom Yerushalayim.

**Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Ps 87 (Ps 86 in his Vulgate numbering) is one of the foundational texts for the "two cities" theology that flowers in *De Civitate Dei*. Augustine reads "this one was born there" as the spiritual birth-certificate of every citizen of the heavenly Jerusalem — Egyptian, Babylonian, Philistine, all who are born from above are inscribed in Zion's registry. Athanasius treats the psalm Christologically, with Zion as the figure of the Church drawing in the nations. Cassiodorus and Aquinas extend the Augustinian reading. Luther reads Ps 87 as a hymn to the gospel-Word that creates citizens of Zion through faith. Calvin (*Commentary on the Psalms*) emphasizes the gathering of the nations as the work of God's free grace, with the registry as a figure of election. Among twentieth- and twenty-first-century commentators, Gunkel and Mowinckel (form-critical), Westermann (theological), Kraus (historical-critical), Hossfeld and Zenger (canonical), Goldingay (literary-theological), Tate (Word Biblical Commentary), Wenham (canonical), Brueggemann (theological imagination), Patrick Miller (theology of the Psalms), William Brown (poetic and ecological), Beth Tanner (feminist-theological), and David Mitchell (eschatological-canonical) all converge on the recognition that Ps 87 is among the most universalist texts in the Hebrew Bible, even as they differ on whether the universalism is realized, eschatological, or rhetorical.

**NT use.** The most direct echo is Galatians 4:26 — "the Jerusalem above is free, and she is our mother" — which Paul develops in his Hagar/Sarah allegory and which clearly draws on the Zion-as-mother tradition crystallized in Ps 87. Hebrews 12:22–24 ("you have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem") draws on the same well. Revelation 21–22's vision of the New Jerusalem with the nations bringing their glory into her and her gates never shut belongs to the same trajectory. None of the NT writers cites Ps 87 explicitly, but the cluster of imagery — Zion as mother, the nations registered as citizens, the springs of life flowing from the city — saturates the apostolic vision of the Church.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Universalist Zion vs. particularist election.** Ps 87 sits awkwardly alongside texts like Deut 7:6–8 ("the LORD has chosen you to be a people for his treasured possession, out of all the peoples") or the herem-warfare texts where Egypt and the Philistines are precisely the enemies to be expelled. How can the same God who fought Pharaoh at the Red Sea register Egypt as a citizen of Zion?

- *The realized-universalism position* (often associated with classical liberal Protestant readings, but also with some Jewish universalists like Buber and Heschel) takes the psalm as evidence that biblical religion always contained a universalist core, and that the particularist texts are local or polemical rather than foundational.
- *The eschatological position* (Wilson, Mitchell, Hossfeld–Zenger, much of patristic Christian reading) treats the universalism as a future horizon: in the age to come, the nations will be gathered into Zion; in the present age, Israel's election remains constitutive. The tension is temporal, not contradictory.
- *The dialectical position* (Levenson, Brueggemann, Miller) refuses to resolve the tension. Election and universalism are both real; both are operative; the canon holds them together without dissolving either. Levenson's *Sinai and Zion* argues precisely this: Zion is at once the election of one people *and* the cosmic mountain to which all nations stream.

The steelman for the dialectical position is that the canon as we have it does not collapse the tension. Ps 87 stands; so does Deut 7. Reading either one without the other distorts the witness. The strongest argument for dialectical preservation is that the prophetic texts most analogous to Ps 87 (Isa 19:23–25 — "Blessed be Egypt my people, and Assyria the work of my hands, and Israel my inheritance") explicitly hold particular and universal together without resolution.

**Tension 2: Cosmic-eschatological Zion vs. literal-territorial Jerusalem.** Modern Christian readings of Ps 87 split sharply along this fault line.

- *The literal-territorial position* (much contemporary Christian Zionism, some dispensationalist readings) takes the psalm to refer to the actual geographical Jerusalem, which will, in the millennial kingdom, be the political capital of a restored Davidic regime to which the nations will pay tribute.
- *The cosmic-eschatological position* (Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin, most mainline Protestant and Catholic readings, Reformed covenant theology) treats Zion as a typological figure that finds its fulfillment in the heavenly Jerusalem and in the Church as the gathered people of God from every nation.
- *The both-and position* (N. T. Wright, much New Testament theology, some recent Catholic exegesis) holds that the cosmic-eschatological reading is primary but does not require the abolition of geographical Israel; the New Jerusalem comes *down* (Rev 21:2) to a renewed earth, including a restored Jerusalem within it.

The steelman for the cosmic-eschatological reading is that the NT itself reads the OT Zion texts this way — Galatians 4, Hebrews 12, Revelation 21 all treat the heavenly Jerusalem as the fulfillment, not as a parallel reality to the earthly one. The literal-territorial reading must explain why the apostolic witness so consistently spiritualizes (or, more accurately, eschatologizes) the Zion tradition.

**Where this leaves us.** Psalm 87 is a registry-poem. It does not argue; it announces. God is leaning over the book of nations and writing in the names of his enemies as native-born. That is what the poem says, and however we adjudicate the surrounding theological tensions, that announcement stands. The Christian who hears in it the gospel-promise that Gentiles are co-heirs has not misread it; the Jew who hears in it the eschatological gathering of the nations has not misread it. Both are reading what the text is doing. What we cannot do, after Ps 87, is treat the people of God as a closed registry. The book is open, and someone is still writing.

**Closing question.** If God's registry of Zion includes the names of the empires that destroyed her, what does it mean that we, the readers, are also being written in — not by our own pen, but by his?
