# Jeremiah 27: The Yoke of the King of Babylon

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jeremiah 27 opens with a textual difficulty: the MT reads "in the beginning of the reign of Jehoiakim" (27:1), but the chapter's content — Zedekiah on the throne (27:3, 12), envoys plotting an anti-Babylon coalition, the temple vessels already in part deported (27:18–22) — fits unmistakably in the **fourth year of Zedekiah, 594/593 BC**. The MT reading is almost certainly a scribal error caused by the parallel opening of chapter 26 (Jehoiakim) and 28 (28:1, "fourth year of Zedekiah"). Lundbom, Holladay, Bright, and McKane all emend to Zedekiah; the LXX simply omits the verse, suggesting an earlier text that did not include the corruption. Zedekiah, installed by Nebuchadnezzar in 597 BC after the first deportation that took Jeconiah, the queen mother, the nobility, and the first batch of temple vessels to Babylon, has now been on the throne about four years.

The political situation is precisely datable to 594/593 BC. Babylonian Chronicle entries record uprisings within Babylon itself in Nebuchadnezzar's tenth year (winter 595/594 BC), and the Levantine vassal states evidently read those internal troubles as an opportunity. By Zedekiah's fourth year, **envoys from Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre, and Sidon had assembled in Jerusalem** for what was clearly an anti-Babylon coalition meeting (27:3). The historical setting is corroborated by external evidence: the Tyrian king-list shows political turbulence in Sidon and Tyre during these years; the Mesad Hashavyahu ostracon from earlier in this period attests to the extent of regional vassal-treaty diplomacy; and Ezekiel 17:11–21, written from the exilic side, references the same revolt-conspiracy and its eventual collapse. The plot ultimately fizzled — by 594 BC Zedekiah even traveled to Babylon (Jer 51:59) to reaffirm his loyalty, and the anti-Babylon coalition dissolved without the rebellion that came seven years later.

Material culture is dense in this chapter. The **wooden yoke-bars** (*motot*, 27:2) Jeremiah makes are agricultural-pastoral implements — the cross-piece that ties two oxen together at the shoulder for plowing. ANE iconography from Mesopotamia and the Levant routinely depicts the yoke as a political-vassalage symbol: Esarhaddon's vassal treaties speak of subjects "bearing the yoke" (Akkadian *nīru*), and a relief from Tiglath-Pileser III shows defeated kings literally yoked at the neck. Jeremiah's sign-act exploits a metaphor every observer would have understood. The **temple vessels** (27:16, 18–22) — *kelei bet-YHWH* — were the bronze pillars Jachin and Boaz, the molten sea, the stands (*mekhonot*), and the smaller liturgical vessels. The first deportation in 597 BC had taken some; the rest would go in 587 BC.

Authorship and dating: chapter 27 is a sign-act narrative widely treated as authentically Jeremianic, with Baruch as the likely scribal mediator. Carroll reads heavy redaction. The MT/LXX divergence in the chapter is significant though less structural than at 25:13: the LXX is shorter, omits "Nebuchadnezzar my servant" in 27:6, and condenses the messenger formulae. McKane's ICC carefully maps which omissions are likely original and which represent later MT expansion. The dropping of "my servant" in the LXX appears to be a softening — a scribal reaction against the appellation's scandal, the same impulse Targum Jonathan would later exhibit on 25:9.

Key terms anchor the message. *Motah / motot* (מוֹטָה, "yoke-bar," 27:2 and throughout chs. 27–28) is the literal-symbolic centerpiece. *Mosrot* (מוֹסֵרוֹת, "straps" or "bands," 27:2) are the leather binding-thongs that secure the yoke. *Avadi Nevuchadnetzar* (עַבְדִּי נְבוּכַדְנֶאצַּר, "*my servant* Nebuchadnezzar," 27:6) returns from 25:9 and will return again at 43:10 — the trio of occurrences in the MT, all in MT-only material relative to LXX. *Sheker* (שֶׁקֶר, "lie / falsehood," 27:10, 14, 15, 16) is the technical Jeremianic word for false-prophetic word; the chapter accuses the temple-prophets of "prophesying *sheker*." *Kelei* (כְּלֵי, "vessels," 27:16, 18–22) are the temple's sacred objects, and the chapter's closing pericope on their fate sets up Daniel 5 directly.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 27 is a **sign-act narrative** with three distinct addresses radiating from a single performed sign. Jeremiah makes the wooden yoke-bars and wears them around his own neck. He sends word to the foreign envoys (27:3–11), then speaks identically to Zedekiah (27:12–15), and finally to the priests and people of Jerusalem (27:16–22). The message is the same in all three: do not listen to the prophets who promise quick liberation; submit to Babylon and live. Sign-acts are a recognizable prophetic genre (Isa 20, Ezek 4–5, Hos 1–3) — embodied prophecy, where the prophet's body becomes the message. Jeremiah's yoke is one of the most famous in the corpus and sets up its dramatic destruction in chapter 28.

Stulman's structural analysis treats chapters 27–29 as a unit: 27 is the international yoke message, 28 is the Hananiah confrontation, and 29 is the letter to the exiles in Babylon. All three center on the *test of true vs. false prophecy*. The literary device of the **threefold repetition** (envoys, king, people) gives the chapter its rhetorical density: the same theology of divine-imperial instrumentality is delivered at three rungs of social stratification, and the rhetorical argument scales accordingly.

The chapter is also notable for its **deliberate echo of Genesis 1–2**: 27:5 reads "*It is I who by my great power and my outstretched arm have made the earth, with the men and animals that are on the earth, and I give it to whomever it seems right to me*." Lundbom calls this "the Genesis claim" — Jeremiah grounds the political assertion (Babylon gets the kingdoms) in a creation-theology assertion (YHWH gives lands to whomever he wills). The "outstretched arm" (*zero'a netuyah*) language is Deuteronomic exodus-vocabulary (Deut 4:34, 5:15) deliberately retooled: the same arm that brought Israel out of Egypt now hands the land over to Babylon.

The MT/LXX divergence in this chapter is the textbook case of *MT-expansion*. Verses 7, 13, 14a, 17, 21 are absent or radically shorter in the LXX. Carroll reads these as MT theological glosses; McKane more cautiously reconstructs an earlier shorter form. Either way, the LXX preserves the message without the MT's "Nebuchadnezzar my servant" reinforcement.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish Trajectory.** The yoke as political-religious symbol generates rich rabbinic afterlife. Bavli Berakhot 13a develops the *ol malkhut shamayim* — the "yoke of the kingdom of heaven" — as the metaphor for Torah-observance, and explicitly contrasts it with the *ol malkhut basar va-dam*, the yoke of earthly empire. Mishnah Avot 3:5 picks up the same image: anyone who takes upon himself the yoke of Torah is freed from the yoke of empire. Jeremiah 27's wooden yoke is the literary background. Targum Jonathan paraphrases the chapter cautiously, again softening "my servant Nebuchadnezzar" by syntactic redirection. Rashi reads the chapter through the lens of *anavah* (humility): the yoke is borne not as defeat but as discipline. Radak focuses on the temple-vessels question, anticipating Cyrus's restoration in Ezra 1. Ibn Ezra notes the textual problem at 27:1. Abrabanel reads the chapter as a paradigm for diaspora politics: submit to the host empire while clinging to covenant.

**Christian Trajectory.** The yoke of 27:2 receives a counter-typology in Matthew 11:28–30 — "*take my yoke upon you, and learn from me… for my yoke is easy*." Origen explicitly reads this connection in his *Homilies on Jeremiah*: Jeremiah's heavy political yoke gives way to Christ's gentle covenant yoke. **Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah*** spends substantial space on the chapter, especially on the temple-vessels promise (27:22) and its fulfillment in Ezra-Nehemiah; Jerome reads the vessels' restoration as a typological pledge of resurrection. Aquinas, in his lectures on Jeremiah, treats the chapter as a doctrine of providence: God assigns kingdoms (cf. Dan 4:17). Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah* makes 27:5–6 a foundational text for political theology — God is the *real* assigner of polities, and political authority is therefore *delegated*. Romans 13:1–7 ("there is no authority except from God") is read against this Jeremianic background.

In modern criticism, Duhm read the chapter as heavily Deuteronomistic. Mowinckel placed it in his Source C. Holladay defends a high degree of authentic Jeremianic kernel with later Deuteronomistic editing. Carroll reads the entire chapter as a post-587 theological construction. Bright and Lundbom defend historical reliability. Brueggemann reads the chapter as the most concentrated **theology of empire** in the prophetic corpus: God can give kingdoms, and that giving is *not* a permanent endorsement (Babylon's own time will come, 27:7). **Kathleen O'Connor's *Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*** reads the yoke trauma-theoretically: the prophet bears the burden physically as a way of *naming* what the community refuses to feel.

**Where they diverge.** Patristic and medieval readings emphasize providence and the typological reversal in Christ's "easy yoke." Reformation readings (Calvin, Luther) extract a doctrine of magistracy. Anabaptist readings (Yoder, Hauerwas) emphasize Jeremiah's *political quietism* under empire as a normative posture for the church-against-empire — the Christian church is not the kingdom and should not pretend to be a kingdom. Jewish reception holds the yoke as covenant-discipline rather than mere submission. Liberation theology questions whether 27:6 can ever be a normative theology of empire, given how it has been weaponized to justify imperial Christianity.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: international politics and theology.** Jeremiah counsels *submission* to a pagan empire as YHWH's will. The Christian reception has split sharply along confessional lines. Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah* and Luther's two-kingdoms doctrine support submission to legitimate political authority on the basis of Romans 13, with Jeremiah 27 as the prophetic background. The Lutheran-Reformed reading is that submission to legitimate authority — even foreign, even imperial, even pagan — is the Christian's vocation, and active resistance is reserved for the rare case of magistrate-against-God commands.

The Anabaptist tradition (Yoder's *Politics of Jesus*) treats Jeremiah 27 as *the* prophetic political ethic for the people of God in dispersion: don't seek liberation by political-military means; bear the yoke; live within empire without becoming empire. This is the *dispersion-ethic* reading. Modern Christian Zionism *rejects* the application of 27 to contemporary Israel — submission to the empires of the twentieth century would have meant non-survival. Liberation theology (Gustavo Gutiérrez, Jon Sobrino) questions whether submission can ever be a faithful response under conditions of structural injustice; the cross becomes the model, but the cross is *also* a form of submission-unto-death.

Steelman the liberation-theology critique. Jeremiah 27's submission-counsel works *because Jeremiah is also denouncing Judah's own ruling class*. The prophet does not submit to *every* power; he submits to a specific historical empire because the alternative (military revolt by an unprepared vassal state) would be catastrophic. To extract from Jeremiah 27 a *general* ethic of submission to empire is to ignore the prophet's own critique of empire (cf. ch. 50, the oracle against Babylon). Submission was strategic, contextual, and time-limited (27:7 — "until the time of his own land comes"). When Jeremiah's submission-ethic has been generalized into Constantinian Christianity's *quiet* under empire, it has betrayed the prophet's own internal limit.

Where this leaves us: the chapter offers a theology of *strategic* submission that resists generalization. To read 27 as a perpetual political ethic is to forget 50–51, where the yoke is finally broken.

**Tension 2: the temple vessels.** What does it mean that the *holy vessels* themselves are carried to Babylon (27:16, 21–22)? The vessels were ritually consecrated objects; their physical presence in pagan space raises a sharp theological question. Daniel 5 dramatizes their profanation by Belshazzar; Ezra 1:7–11 records Cyrus's restoration of the very same vessels. The Catholic and Orthodox sacred-object tradition reads the vessels as carrying the residue of the *qodesh* — they retain holiness even in exile, and their eventual restoration is a vindication of sacramental theology. Reformed iconoclastic tradition (especially the radical Reformation) reads the vessels' departure as warning *against* sacred-object piety: God's holiness is not in the bronze but in the people. The vessels go because piety has collapsed into idolatry-of-place.

Jewish reception (Bavli Yoma 21b on the *anointing oil* and the temple vessels) reads the vessels' fate as the deepest possible measure of exile — when the very furniture of communion is gone, communion itself is in question.

Steelman the Reformed-iconoclastic position. The chapter's narrative arc is precisely that the vessels' presence in Jerusalem did *not* save Jerusalem. The temple's sacred-object piety became part of what Jeremiah was sent to attack. To make 27:22's promise of *restoration* into a doctrine of sacred-object continuity is to ignore that the vessels were taken in the first place because piety had failed. The vessels' return under Cyrus is a mercy, not a vindication of object-theology. Cite **Levenson's *Sinai and Zion*** on the genuine theological work done by sacred-object traditions, but note Levenson himself acknowledges the prophetic critique of misplaced confidence in temple objects (Jer 7:4, the "deceptive words: this is the temple of the LORD").

Where this leaves us: the vessels matter. They are not nothing. But they cannot save the city, and Jeremiah's chapter is at pains to make that clear.

**Unresolved question.** If Nebuchadnezzar is YHWH's servant *for a season* (27:7), what counts as the legitimate end of that season? Jeremiah 50–51 announces it; Daniel 5 dramatizes it; Cyrus enacts it. But the structural question — who decides when a divinely-instrumentalized empire has outlived its mandate? — has no programmatic answer in the text. That ambiguity is one of the deepest cracks in the providence tradition. Hananiah will arrive in chapter 28 claiming to know exactly when the yoke breaks ("within two years," 28:3), and his confidence about *the timing* of empire's end is precisely what marks him as the false prophet. The chapter quietly insists that *knowing the season* is a divine prerogative the prophet does not seize.

The chapter's two tensions — strategic submission to empire and the theology of sacred objects in exile — sit unresolved alongside each other. The same prophet who tells Zedekiah to bear Babylon's yoke also promises that the temple vessels will return (27:22). Submission is therefore not despair, and the vessels' temporary residence in pagan space is not their final word. Both claims hold simultaneously, and Christian political theology has often had to choose one and lose the other.

## The Thread

The yoke of Jeremiah 27:2 echoes forward into Matthew 11:28–30 — Christ's "easy yoke" is the deliberate counter-image to Babylon's iron one (which Hananiah's broken wooden yoke will become in 28:13). The temple vessels of 27:18–22 reappear in Daniel 5 (Belshazzar's profanation), Ezra 1:7–11 (Cyrus's restoration), and arguably in 2 Corinthians 4:7 (Paul's "treasure in jars of clay") — the vessel-in-exile trope runs deep. The "outstretched arm" creation theology of 27:5 echoes Deuteronomy 4:34 / 5:15 (the Exodus arm now retasked) and forward to Romans 13:1 (the same providence that gave Babylon its season gives Rome its). And the "Nebuchadnezzar my servant" appellation of 27:6, third occurrence in the MT, prefigures Cyrus the *anointed* (Isaiah 45:1) — both texts offering the same scandal: pagan emperors as instruments of YHWH.
