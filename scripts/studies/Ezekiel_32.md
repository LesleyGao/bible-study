# Ezekiel 32: The Dragon in the Seas, the Cosmic Darkening, and the Necropolis of Empires

## Lens 1 — Historical World

Ezekiel 32 closes the Egypt cluster (chs. 29–32) and contains two oracles, both dated. The first (vv. 1–16) is dated *the twelfth year, in the twelfth month, on the first day*, which corresponds to **March 3, 585 BC** by the standard chronology — about nineteen months after the fall of Jerusalem (August 587 BC). The second (vv. 17–32) is dated *the twelfth year, in the twelfth month, on the fifteenth day* — most likely an editorial shortening of *the twelfth year, in the first month, on the fifteenth day* (so MT manuscripts and the Targum), which would correspond to **April 27, 586 BC**, or alternatively (following LXX) the same day as the first oracle, two weeks later. **Daniel Block** (NICOT) and **Walther Zimmerli** (Hermeneia) accept the MT reading with a textual emendation; **Moshe Greenberg** (Anchor Yale) defends the MT date as it stands and treats the two oracles as bookends across the year. Either way, the chapter belongs to the period after Jerusalem's fall, when Ezekiel's prophetic horizon had pivoted from threat to consolation and the Egypt-oracles function as theological housekeeping — closing accounts with the empire that had failed Judah and asserting that no imperial power escapes the same eventual reckoning.

The historical context is the post-Jerusalem fallout. **Pharaoh Hophra (Apries)** still ruled Egypt at this point (he would not be overthrown by Amasis until 570 BC), but his political position had been gravely weakened by the failure of his Jerusalem-relief intervention and by the ongoing pressure from Nebuchadnezzar's western front. The Babylonian invasion of Egypt referred to in 29:17–20 and 30:10 lay still in the future (568/567 BC, after Amasis had taken the throne). The chapter therefore looks forward, theologically and historically, to events that Ezekiel would not live to see fulfilled in the form he predicted (cf. the prophecy-amendment of 29:17–20).

The remarkable feature of Ezek 32:17–32 is the **descent-to-Sheol vision** with its catalog of fallen empires. **Assyria** fell to Babylon and the Medes in 612 BC (Babylonian Chronicle BM 21901). **Elam** is the highland kingdom east of Babylonia, briefly mentioned in OT prophets as a coming victim (cf. Jer 49:34–39); the Achaemenid Persian rise would absorb Elam by the mid-sixth century, with the Elamite capital **Susa** becoming a Persian winter capital (the Elamite linguistic evidence is preserved in the Behistun inscription, in royal Achaemenid trilingual texts, and in the Persepolis Fortification tablets). **Meshech-Tubal** (a paired toponym for the Anatolian peoples; in **Herodotus** and Assyrian inscriptions these are *Mušku* and *Tabal*, the Phrygians and the Tabalians of central-eastern Anatolia, prominent in the Late Bronze and Iron Age trade in metals; cf. Gen 10:2; Ezek 27:13). **Edom** (the kingdom south of Judah, with its capital at Bozrah; well-attested in Edomite seals, the Tel ʿAroʿer ostraca, and in **Itzhaq Beit-Arieh's** excavations of the Edomite border-fortress at Tell el-Kheleifeh). **The princes of the north** (likely the Aramean kingdoms and Phoenician states north of Judah). **The Sidonians** (the great Phoenician maritime power, whose coastal city archaeology is preserved at modern Saida). The catalog functions as an imperial roll-call of recently-fallen or coming-to-fall powers, all of whom *spread their terror in the land of the living* (a recurring phrase in vv. 23–32) and now share the underworld with Egypt.

The geography of vv. 22–30 is precise and reflects authentic Iron Age geopolitical knowledge. The *uttermost parts of the pit* (v. 23) is the deepest Sheol-stratum, where the most violent and feared empires lie. The catalog is hierarchical: Assyria first (the empire whose fall Egypt had recently witnessed), Elam second, Meshech-Tubal third, Edom fourth, the princes of the north and the Sidonians last. **Block** notes that the catalog moves from greatest geographic distance (Assyria, Elam, Anatolia) to nearest neighbors (Edom, Sidon), which inverts the more usual prophetic catalog-order and creates the literary effect of Pharaoh-Egypt arriving at a Sheol already populated by the entire ANE imperial roster.

Hebrew vocabulary worth flagging: *tannin* (תַּנִּין, "dragon, sea-monster") in v. 2, the same chaoskampf-trope deployed in 29:3; *šəʾôl* (שְׁאוֹל, "Sheol"); *bôr* (בּוֹר, "pit"); *tahtiyyôt ʾāreṣ* (תַּחְתִּיּוֹת אָרֶץ, "the world below," literally "the lower parts of the earth") in v. 18 and 24; the recurring *māgôr ḥittîṯām bə-ʾereṣ ḥayyîm* (מָגוֹר חִתִּיתָם בְּאֶרֶץ חַיִּים, "their terror in the land of the living"); *ʿărēlîm* (עֲרֵלִים, "the uncircumcised"), used here as a shame-term meaning *those who lie in dishonored death*.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter divides into two extended *qina* (lament) units. The first (vv. 1–16) is a formal lamentation over Pharaoh as the *tannin* in the seas, with the cosmic-darkening trope of vv. 7–8 as its rhetorical climax. The second (vv. 17–32) is the **descent-to-Sheol** vision with its *necropolis* catalog of fallen empires.

The first lament re-deploys the *tannin* trope of 29:3 but with intensification. Where 29:3 cast Pharaoh as *the great dragon that lies in the midst of his streams*, 32:2 escalates: *you consider yourself a lion of the nations, but you are like a dragon in the seas*. The contrast is biting — Pharaoh imagines himself as *kəfîr gôyîm* (lion of the nations, a royal-iconographic title common in Egyptian and Mesopotamian self-presentation) but is actually *tannin ba-yammîm* (dragon in the seas, a chaoskampf identification). **Greenberg** notes that the rhetorical move conscripts the Egyptian self-image of Pharaoh-as-lion (countless reliefs from the Narmer Palette through the Ramessides depict the king as a lion crushing enemies) and inverts it: the king is not the order-imposing lion but the chaos-monster requiring suppression.

The **net-and-fishing** imagery of vv. 3–6 echoes 29:4–5 but extends it. *I will throw my net over you with a host of many peoples, and they will haul you up in my dragnet* — the imagery is of communal hunting, with YHWH as the master-fisher and the *many peoples* (the Babylonians and their coalition) as the net-haulers. The carcass-disposal imagery of vv. 4–6 (birds of the heavens settling on the body, beasts of the earth gorged, mountains strewn with flesh, valleys filled with carcass, ravines drenched with blood) is unusually graphic even by Ezekielian standards. **Block** treats this as the *banquet of corpses* trope that recurs in apocalyptic contexts (cf. the messianic banquet inversion in Rev 19:17–18).

The **cosmic-darkening** trope of vv. 7–8 is one of the chapter's most theologically loaded passages. *I will cover the heavens and make their stars dark; I will cover the sun with a cloud, and the moon shall not give its light. All the bright lights of heaven will I make dark over you, and put darkness on your land*. The trope echoes Joel 2:31 (*the sun shall be turned to darkness and the moon to blood*), Isa 13:10 (the day-of-YHWH against Babylon: *the stars of the heavens and their constellations will not give their light*), and Amos 8:9 (*I will make the sun go down at noon, and darken the earth in broad daylight*). The trope's NT inheritance is dense: **Matthew 24:29** (*the sun will be darkened, and the moon will not give its light, and the stars will fall from heaven*) explicitly cites this language for the *parousia*; **Mark 13:24**, **Luke 21:25**, **Revelation 6:12–13** all deploy the trope.

The second lament's **descent-to-Sheol** vision (vv. 17–32) is one of the OT's most extended underworld scenes. The literary structure is a *necropolis tour*: Pharaoh is conducted (or rather, sent down) to Sheol, where he encounters each fallen empire in its grave. The repeated structural phrase is *X is there, and all her multitude, her graves all around it, all of them slain, fallen by the sword*. The cumulative effect is a *Spoon River Anthology* of imperial dead — each empire's grave with its company arrayed around it, each empire described in identical formula, the formula's repetition mimicking the underworld's leveling effect. **Greenberg** notes that the literary form here is unique in the OT: nowhere else does an OT prophet provide such an extended underworld catalog. The closest formal parallel in the OT is Isa 14's descent-to-Sheol of the king of Babylon (Isa 14:9–11 *Sheol beneath is stirred up to meet you when you come*), but Isa 14 is shorter and less catalogic. The closest ANE parallels are the **Mesopotamian *Descent of Inanna/Ishtar*** (the Sumerian *Inanna's Descent to the Underworld*, c. 2000 BC, and its Akkadian *Ishtar's Descent* counterpart) and the **Egyptian *Book of the Dead*** with its judgment-of-the-soul scenes. Ezekiel's underworld is structurally similar (a hierarchical realm with stratified locations) but theologically distinct: it is YHWH's underworld, not a polytheistic realm of divine inhabitants.

A textual variant: 32:17 lacks a month-specifier in MT (just *the twelfth year, in the twelfth month, on the fifteenth day* would be impossible if the first oracle is also dated to the first day of the twelfth month — the descent-vision would precede the lament). LXX reads *the first month*, which most modern critical commentaries (including Block) accept as an emendation. **Greenberg** defends MT and reads the second oracle as the same day as the first.

## Lens 3 — Larger Story

In Jewish reception, Ezekiel 32's descent-to-Sheol vision became a primary text for the development of OT-and-Second-Temple eschatology. **Targum Jonathan** paraphrases the underworld vocabulary with a Second Temple eschatological vocabulary: *bə-ʾereṣ ḥayyîm* (in the land of the living) becomes *bə-ʾarʿā de-ḥayyê* (in the land of the living-ones), and *šəʾôl* is rendered *šeʾôl*, with the eschatological judgment of the dead implied in the paraphrase. The Talmudic tractate **Sanhedrin 91a–b** cites Ezek 32 in connection with the doctrine of the resurrection, treating the *uncircumcised* (vv. 19, 21, 24, 26, 28, 29, 30, 32) as a category that does not include the righteous dead. The medieval rabbinic discussion (Rashi, Radak, Ibn Ezra) wrestles with the ontology of Sheol: are the *speakers* of v. 21 (*the mighty chiefs shall speak of them, with their helpers, out of the midst of Sheol*) actually conscious, or is this poetic personification?

Christian reception has been theologically rich. **Origen's** lost commentary read the descent-to-Sheol vision as a typological prefiguration of Christ's descent to the dead (the *descensus ad inferos* tradition that became the Apostles' Creed's *descended into hell*). **Jerome's** *Commentariorum in Ezechielem libri XIV* extended Origen's reading and connected the Pharaoh-in-Sheol scene with the Lazarus-and-the-rich-man parable of Luke 16:19–31, where the deceased *speak* to one another across the bosom of Abraham. **Augustine's** *Enchiridion* treats Sheol as the universal pre-Christ underworld with multiple compartments. **Calvin's** *Praelectiones in Ezechielem* is more cautious about Sheol's ontology, treating the chapter's underworld imagery as poetic representation of the death-state without commitment to the patristic compartment-theology.

The **OT Sheol theology** has been the focus of major modern scholarship. **Jon Levenson's** *Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel: The Ultimate Victory of the God of Life* (2006) is the standard recent treatment, arguing that the OT Sheol is initially undifferentiated dust-existence (cf. Job 10:21–22; Ps 88:10–12) but develops by the late prophetic period (Ezek 37, Daniel 12) into a more articulated death-theology that allows for resurrection. **Philip Johnston's** *Shades of Sheol: Death and Afterlife in the Old Testament* (2002) provides the systematic treatment. **N. T. Wright's** *The Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003) traces the developmental arc from dust-Sheol through prophetic underworld articulation to Daniel 12's resurrection-doctrine and its NT inheritance. The interpretive question is whether Ezek 32 represents the *speaking-dead* version of Sheol theology (more articulated, closer to Daniel 12 and the NT) or a *poetic-personification* version (more figurative, closer to Job and Psalms).

The **cosmic-darkening** trope's NT inheritance has been studied by **N. T. Wright** (*Jesus and the Victory of God*, 1996) in close conversation with **R. T. France's** *The Gospel of Mark* commentary. Wright argues that Mark 13's Olivet Discourse — *the sun will be darkened, and the moon will not give its light* (Mk 13:24) — deploys the OT cosmic-darkening tropes typologically, predicting both the fall of Jerusalem in 70 AD and (in a typological-not-literal sense) the end-of-the-age judgment. The dispensationalist reading (cf. **John Walvoord's** *The Revelation of Jesus Christ*) takes the cosmic-darkening as future-literal eschatological events; the preterist reading (**R. C. Sproul's** *The Last Days According to Jesus*) takes them as already-fulfilled in the 70 AD destruction; the typological reading (Wright, **George Eldon Ladd**) preserves both registers without flattening either.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — OT Sheol theology and the consciousness of the dead.** The chapter's descent-vision presents Pharaoh entering Sheol and being met by previously-fallen empires whose *mighty chiefs shall speak of them* (v. 21). The patristic-Christian reading (Origen, Jerome, Gregory) treats this as describing genuinely conscious post-mortem existence, with the dead speaking and gathering. The 1 Sam 28 *witch of Endor* episode — where Samuel actually speaks from the dead — is the closest OT precedent, and the Lazarus-and-rich-man parable (Lk 16:19–31) is the closest NT extension of the trope. The modernizing reading treats the speech as poetic personification, with the *mighty chiefs* speaking as a literary device for political-historical commentary on imperial collapse rather than as a metaphysical claim about post-mortem consciousness. **Levenson's** treatment in *Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel* attempts a theological mediation: the OT Sheol is not a doctrine but a developing imagination, and Ezek 32 is one of the texts that pushes the imagination toward articulation, anticipating the Daniel-12 resurrection-doctrine without yet stating it. The unresolved theological question is whether contemporary Christian readers should affirm the patristic *anima separata* doctrine (the soul exists consciously in an intermediate state) or the more cautious approach that treats Ezek 32's underworld as figurative prophetic critique of empires.

**Tension 2 — The cosmic-darkening trope and the political-cosmic ambiguity.** *I will cover the heavens and make their stars dark* (32:7–8). Is this *political-historical* metaphor for the fall of an empire (the *light goes out* on Egypt as a geopolitical image) or *cosmic-eschatological* prediction of literal sun-and-moon-darkening events? The full range of Christian readings is (a) preterist: the cosmic-darkening was metaphorical and was fulfilled in the fall of Egypt and the 70 AD fall of Jerusalem in Matt 24's reuse; (b) dispensationalist literal-future: the cosmic-darkening predicts a literal future tribulation event; (c) typological (Wright, Ladd): the trope describes both political-historical fall-of-empire events and ultimate eschatological cosmic events, with the same divine action terminating in different scales. **N. T. Wright's** treatment in *Jesus and the Victory of God* is the most rigorous contemporary defense of the typological view: the OT prophets used cosmic-disturbance language for fall-of-empire events because the fall of an empire *is* a cosmic event from the standpoint of those whose ordered world it organizes; the NT inherits the language and applies it to both Jerusalem-70 and the eschaton, with no flattening required.

**Unresolved question.** *When Pharaoh sees them, he will be comforted for all his multitude* (32:31). Why is *comfort* (וְנִחַם, *wə-niḥam*) the verb deployed for Pharaoh's reaction in Sheol? Is it sardonic — the cold comfort of being one fallen empire among many? Or is it theologically substantive — Sheol is not a place of torment but of fellowship-in-defeat? The chapter declines to specify, and Block reads the verb as deliberately ambiguous: Pharaoh's comfort is at once the false comfort of imperial company and the residual community-in-death that Sheol allows. The verse is one of the OT's most theologically subtle moments about post-mortem existence.

## The Thread

Four non-obvious cross-references. (1) **Isaiah 14:9–11** — *Sheol beneath is stirred up to meet you when you come*. The closest formal parallel to Ezek 32's descent-vision; the king of Babylon's reception in Sheol mirrors Pharaoh's, and the two texts together are the OT's primary witnesses to a *speaking-Sheol* tradition. (2) **Daniel 12:2** — *and many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt*. The first explicit OT resurrection-doctrine; Ezek 32's *uncircumcised* lying *in the world below* is one of the texts that prepared the imaginative ground for Daniel's articulation. (3) **Matthew 24:29 / Mark 13:24 / Revelation 6:12–13** — the cosmic-darkening trope's NT destination. The *day of clouds* of Ezek 30:3 and the cosmic-darkening of Ezek 32:7–8 are the deepest OT substrates of the synoptic Olivet Discourse and of John's apocalyptic visions. (4) **Revelation 12:9 and 20:2** — *the great dragon was thrown down, that ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan*. The *tannin* of Ezek 29:3 and 32:2 finds its canonical destination in John's identification of the cosmic-sea-monster as the spiritual-political principle behind every imperial pretension. The forward arc from Ezek 29 and 32 to Rev 12 and 20 is one of the densest single threads in the canon's apocalyptic theology, and the descent-to-Sheol of Ezek 32 anticipates Rev 20's *the sea gave up the dead that were in it, Death and Hades gave up the dead who were in them*.
