# Psalm 73 — The Theodicy Psalm

## Lens 1: Historical World

Psalm 73 opens Book III of the Psalter (Pss 73-89) and stands at the head of the Asaphite cluster (Pss 73-83). The superscription, *mizmor le-Asaph* (מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף), assigns the psalm to Asaph, identified in 1 Chronicles 16:5 and 25:1-9 as a Levitical chief musician appointed by David and the eponymous head of a guild of temple singers. The Chronicler describes the Asaphites as those who "prophesied with lyres, harps, and cymbals" (1 Chr 25:1), a category that aligns with the prophetic and didactic tone of Pss 73-83. Whether Asaph himself composed Psalm 73 or whether the superscription points to a guild tradition is debated, but most commentators (Goldingay, Hossfeld-Zenger, Tate) treat the Asaphite collection as a corpus shaped within a Levitical singing tradition, plausibly carrying material from the late monarchic period through the exile and beyond.

Hermann Gunkel's form-critical taxonomy classifies Psalm 73 as a *Weisheitspsalm* — a wisdom psalm — though with strong elements of individual thanksgiving and confessional testimony. Sigmund Mowinckel and later Klaus Seybold modified this slightly, recognizing that the psalm's didactic frame (presupposition, problem, resolution, confession) functions as a stylized cult-confession, perhaps recited by a single worshipper before a community. Erhard Gerstenberger's *Psalms, Part 1* and *Part 2* placed the psalm within the genre of *individual reflection on retribution*, while Walter Brueggemann famously called it the centerpiece of "the costly loss of lament" — the psalm in which a worshipper begins inside the theodicy crisis and emerges with a re-oriented faith.

The dating is contested. Mowinckel and Kraus tilted toward a pre-exilic temple setting given the centrality of the *miqdash* (sanctuary) in v. 17. Hossfeld and Zenger in their *Hermeneia* commentary place the final form in the post-exilic Second Temple period, when wisdom-style theodicy had become a major intellectual concern (cf. Job, Ecclesiastes, the Wisdom of Solomon). The reference to *miqdeshei-El* in v. 17 is in the plural construct (literally "sanctuaries of God"), which Goldingay reads as a poetic plural and Tate as referring to the temple precincts as a complex, not multiple shrines. The Septuagint renders it as *to hagiastērion tou theou*, a singular, confirming the early Jewish reading of a single sanctuary.

Hebrew terms anchor the psalm. The opening *akh tov le-Yisra'el Elohim* (אַךְ טוֹב לְיִשְׂרָאֵל אֱלֹהִים, v. 1) — "truly God is good to Israel" — pivots on *akh*, an asseverative particle meaning "surely," "only," or "yet." The same particle reappears at v. 13 (*akh riq zikkiti levavi*, "surely in vain I have kept my heart pure") and v. 18 (*akh be-chalaqot tashit lamo*, "surely you set them in slippery places"). The threefold *akh* structures the psalm's argument: a confession of faith, a confession of doubt, and a renewed confession of faith. The crisis at v. 2 — *ka-m'at natayu raglai* (כִּמְעַט נָטָיוּ רַגְלָי, "as for me, my feet had almost stumbled") — uses the verb *natah* (to stretch, incline, turn aside), the same root used in Deut 5:32 of turning aside from the commandments. The speaker is describing not casual doubt but a near-apostasy.

The structural turn at v. 17 is the psalm's hinge: *ad-avo el-miqdeshei-El, avinah le-acharitam* (אַד־אָבוֹא אֶל־מִקְדְּשֵׁי־אֵל אָבִינָה לְאַחֲרִיתָם), "until I came into the sanctuaries of God; then I discerned their end." The verb *bin* (to discern, understand) and the noun *acharit* (end, latter state, outcome) together signal a wisdom-tradition turn — the same vocabulary appears in Prov 14:12 and Ecclesiastes 7:8. The famous v. 25 — *mi-li va-shamayim ve-immekha lo chafatzti va'aretz* (מִי־לִי בַשָּׁמָיִם וְעִמְּךָ לֹא־חָפַצְתִּי בָאָרֶץ) — "whom have I in heaven but you, and on earth I desire nothing besides you" — uses the verb *chafetz* (to delight, desire), which carries strong covenantal and erotic resonance in the Song of Songs and the prophets. The closing v. 28 — *qarav et-Elohim li tov* (קָרַב אֱלֹהִים לִי־טוֹב) — "for me, drawing near to God is good" — completes an inclusio with v. 1: *tov*, good, frames the entire psalm.

ANE parallels help illuminate the genre. The Babylonian *Ludlul bēl nēmeqi* ("I will praise the lord of wisdom") and the *Babylonian Theodicy* both wrestle with the prosperity of the wicked, but neither resolves through cult-encounter as Psalm 73 does. The Egyptian *Dialogue of a Man with His Ba* circles the same problem with greater pessimism. Psalm 73's solution — that the resolution comes not through philosophical argument but through entering sacred space — is distinctively Israelite and is mirrored only loosely in the Joban speeches from the whirlwind (Job 38-41), which also resolve a theodicy through encounter rather than explanation.

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of Psalm 73 in 4QPs(a) and 11QPs(d). The text is largely consistent with the Masoretic Text. The Septuagint (LXX Ps 72) renders v. 1 as *Hōs agathos tō Israēl ho theos* — "How good is God to Israel" — replacing the asseverative *akh* with an exclamatory adverb, slightly softening the argumentative force. The Vulgate of Jerome (*iuxta Hebraeos*) restored the asseverative reading: *Verumtamen bonus Israel Deus*.

## Lens 2: Literature

Robert Lowth's parallelism is on full display. The opening bicolon — "Truly God is good to Israel, / to those who are pure in heart" — uses *synonymous narrowing*, where the second line specifies the first: "Israel" is glossed as "those who are pure in heart," redefining covenantal belonging in moral-spiritual terms rather than ethnic ones. This narrowing is itself an argument, anticipating the wisdom-tradition's reframing of who counts as God's people.

The psalm divides into a clear strophic structure. Strophe I (vv. 1-3): the thesis and the crisis. Strophe II (vv. 4-12): the empirical description of the wicked. Strophe III (vv. 13-15): the temptation to apostasy. Strophe IV (vv. 16-17): the cult-encounter pivot. Strophe V (vv. 18-22): the perception of the wicked's end. Strophe VI (vv. 23-26): the renewed confession. Strophe VII (vv. 27-28): the closing testimony. The ESV's stanza breaks reflect roughly this division. The threefold *akh* (vv. 1, 13, 18) marks the major hinges; the fourfold *ani* / *imadi* ("but as for me," vv. 2, 22, 23, 28) marks the speaker's interior trajectory.

The chiastic shape is striking. The psalm opens with *tov* (v. 1) and closes with *tov* (v. 28); it opens with *Yisra'el* (the corporate "Israel") and closes with *li* (the personal "for me"); it opens with the assertion of God's goodness and closes with the confession of having drawn near to God. The center of the chiasm is v. 17 — the entry into the sanctuary. This is the literary fulcrum: every line before it leans toward despair, every line after it leans toward confession.

There is no *selah* in Psalm 73. The absence is striking, since Asaphite psalms often use *selah* heavily (cf. Ps 77 with four *selahs*). The unbroken flow may reinforce the testimonial-confessional character: the speaker does not pause for liturgical refrain but presses through to the resolution.

Inclusio and key-word repetition do significant work. The verb *natah* (to incline) at v. 2 ("my feet had almost inclined / stumbled") plays against *qarav* (to draw near) at v. 28, the trajectory from inclination-away to drawing-near. The word *lev* (heart) recurs five times — at vv. 1, 7, 13, 21, 26 — tracking the moral state of the speaker and the wicked. In v. 1, the heart is pure (*bar levav*); in v. 7, the wicked's heart "overflows with follies"; in v. 13, the speaker laments having kept his heart clean in vain; in v. 21, his soul is "embittered" and he is "pricked in heart"; in v. 26, his heart fails (*kalah*) but God is *tzur levavi* — "the rock of my heart." The word does the theological work of the psalm.

The metaphor cluster of vv. 18-20 — slippery places, ruin, dream, phantoms — depends on the dual meaning of *chalaqot* (smooth places, slippery ground, but also the smoothness of a flatterer's tongue). The wicked who once stood on the heights now slip; the speaker who almost slipped (v. 2) now stands. The literary inversion is exquisite. The phantom imagery in v. 20 (*tselem*, image, semblance) anticipates Ecclesiastes 7:14 and Job 20:8 — the standard wisdom-tradition trope of the wicked as a vanishing dream.

Verse 25's "whom have I in heaven but you" is one of the most quoted lines in the Psalter. The Hebrew structure — *mi-li va-shamayim* / *ve-immekha lo chafatzti va'aretz* — is a parallelism of total possession: nothing in heaven, nothing on earth, only God. Bonhoeffer cited this verse in his *Letters and Papers from Prison* as the touchstone of his theology of God-as-the-only-final-good.

## Lens 3: Larger Story

Gerald Wilson's *The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* (1985) argued that Books I-III of the Psalter (Pss 1-89) plot the rise, reign, and crisis of Davidic kingship, with Ps 89's lament closing the sequence as a breaking-point: the Davidic covenant has failed, the throne is cast down. Psalm 73 opens Book III at the head of the Asaphite cluster, and within Wilson's reading it sets the tone for the whole Book — a Book that begins with theodicy crisis (Ps 73), passes through community lament for the destroyed temple (Ps 74), and ends with the rending question of where the *chasdei Adonai ha-rishonim* (the "former lovingkindnesses of the Lord") have gone (Ps 89:50). Book III is part of the Elohistic Psalter (Pss 42-83), which prefers *Elohim* over *YHWH* — and Psalm 73 reflects this, using *Elohim* (God) almost exclusively until v. 28, where *Adonai YHWH* finally appears in the closing testimony. The shift to the divine name marks the worshipper's restoration to direct covenantal address.

Within Jewish tradition, Psalm 73 has been read primarily as a wisdom-confession about the *olam ha-zeh* and *olam ha-ba* — this world and the world to come. The *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 73 reads v. 17's "sanctuary" as referring not only to the Temple but also to the eschatological sanctuary, where the wicked's final fate will be revealed. Rashi's commentary glosses *acharit* (end) as the final judgment. Ibn Ezra reads more philosophically: the entry into the sanctuary is the moment when wisdom turns from empirical observation to faith-knowledge. Radak emphasizes the moral psychology — that envy of the wicked corrodes the heart and that the cure is contemplation of divine providence. Nachmanides draws the line to the question of resurrection and the immortality of the soul: v. 24's "afterward you will receive me to glory" was, for him, evidence of the soul's reception by God after death.

In modern Jewish thought, Martin Buber treated Psalm 73 in his *Right and Wrong: An Interpretation of Some Psalms*, arguing that the psalm's central insight is that "to be near God" is not a state of philosophical certainty but a relational mode — the *I-Thou* answer to the *I-It* problem of theodicy. Abraham Heschel used the psalm as a touchstone of "depth theology" — theology that arises not from speculation but from encounter. Jon Levenson, in *Sinai and Zion* and *Creation and the Persistence of Evil*, reads Psalm 73 as confessing the *partial* not *total* victory of God in history: the wicked's prosperity is real, but its outcome is not. Avivah Zornberg, in her literary-rabbinic readings, treats the psalm's pivot at v. 17 as a moment of *tehom*-encounter — the worshipper plunges into the depths and emerges with a transformed perception.

In Christian tradition, Psalm 73 has been a central text for theodicy and for the doctrine of the eschatological reversal. Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 72 (LXX numbering) is one of his most personal psalm-commentaries: he reads the speaker's near-stumbling as an autobiographical analog to his own pre-conversion crisis, and the entry into the sanctuary as the moment of catechesis and baptism. Athanasius treated the psalm as a model of the soul's training. Cassiodorus emphasized its didactic structure. Aquinas drew on the psalm in *Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 36 (on envy), citing v. 3 as the locus classicus on the moral danger of envying the prosperous wicked.

Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* and his later lectures on Psalm 73 give the psalm a strongly Christological reading — the speaker's near-stumbling is the *anfechtung* (the spiritual crisis) that drives the believer to Christ. Calvin's 1557 commentary on the Psalms is more pastoral: he reads the psalm as a guide for ministers tempted to abandon their calling when the world rewards the unrighteous. Hermann Gunkel and Sigmund Mowinckel placed the psalm in their respective form-critical schemata as a wisdom-thanksgiving hybrid. Claus Westermann, in *Praise and Lament in the Psalms*, used Psalm 73 as the chief example of the "movement from plea to praise" within a single psalm. Hans-Joachim Kraus and Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger's *Psalmen* commentary trace the editorial placement at the head of Book III as a deliberate theological framing: the Davidic crisis of Book III is opened with a theodicy, signaling that the historical crisis (loss of king and temple) is also a wisdom crisis (the failure of standard retribution-theology).

John Goldingay, Marvin Tate, Gordon Wenham, J. Clinton McCann, Beth Tanner, and David Mitchell have all contributed to recent commentary. Mitchell's *The Message of the Psalter* reads Book III's opening as oriented toward an eschatological vindication. Brueggemann, in *The Message of the Psalms* and *Theology of the Old Testament*, places Psalm 73 at the center of the costly loss of lament: the psalmist refuses to short-circuit the crisis with easy piety and refuses to short-circuit faith with easy despair. Patrick Miller, in *Interpreting the Psalms*, reads the psalm as a meditation on the inner life of the covenant.

The New Testament does not directly cite Psalm 73, but its grammar shapes Pauline expressions. Romans 8:38-39 — "neither death nor life... will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus" — echoes v. 25's totalizing devotion. 2 Corinthians 4:16-18 — the "inner self being renewed day by day" while the outer wastes — parallels v. 26's "my flesh and my heart may fail, but God is the strength of my heart and my portion forever." Jesus' beatitude on the pure in heart (Matt 5:8 — "blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God") aligns with v. 1's *bar levav*.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

The psalm forces two tensions on every reader.

The first tension is theodicy itself. Psalm 73 belongs alongside Job, Ecclesiastes, and Habakkuk as one of the Hebrew Bible's frontal engagements with the prosperity of the wicked. Crenshaw, in *Theodicy in the Old Testament*, organizes biblical theodicies into roughly three categories: the *retributive* (suffering = consequence), the *educative* (suffering = formation), and the *eschatological* (vindication is deferred). Psalm 73 sits primarily in the eschatological category — but with a wisdom-tradition modification: the eschatology is not a future vindication so much as a *present perception of the end* gained through cult-encounter.

A first position takes the **classical theistic** reading: God is omnipotent, omniscient, omnibenevolent, and the prosperity of the wicked is a real but temporary surface phenomenon that will be reversed. Augustine's privation-of-good doctrine fits here: evil is not a substance but a lack, and God's providence governs the temporary asymmetry. Aquinas formalized this in the *Summa*, arguing that evil is permitted for a greater good. Plantinga's free-will defense gives the philosophical version: God permits the prosperity of the wicked because the goods of free moral agency outweigh the costs.

A second position is the **soul-making** reading associated with Irenaeus and modern theologians like John Hick. On this reading, the psalmist's near-stumbling and reorientation are themselves the point: the crisis forms the soul. The wicked's prosperity is not a glitch in the system but a feature of the moral universe that requires the worshipper to develop the kind of trust that does not depend on visible reward. The cult-encounter at v. 17 is the moment when the worshipper realizes that the goods of the spiritual life are themselves the goods being offered — not as compensation for present suffering but as their own end.

A third position is **process theology and modern protest theology**. David Ray Griffin and the process tradition argue that the classical theistic reading purchases divine sovereignty too cheaply: if God is omnipotent and the wicked still prosper while the righteous suffer, then either God is not as good as the psalm claims or not as powerful. Process theology proposes a God whose power is persuasive rather than coercive, and whose participation in the world is genuine suffering-with rather than transcendent oversight. The psalm's *qarav et-Elohim li tov* — drawing near to God is good — is read by process theologians as a relational claim, not a metaphysical one. Greg Boyd's "warfare worldview" makes a related move: the cosmic conflict is real, the outcomes are not predetermined, and the psalm's vindication is one moment within a still-unfolding battle. David Bentley Hart's universalist position pushes a different direction: the eschatological end of the wicked in Psalm 73 must be read in light of the larger biblical witness to the gathering-in of all, and "perish" (*avad*) does not necessarily mean ontological annihilation.

To steelman process theology against classical theism: the classical reading must explain why the asymmetry is so often unmitigated within history. Pious answers — that God permits evil for soul-making, or that the eschatological balance corrects everything — work within the system but require holding several premises simultaneously. The process tradition asks whether a God who could end the prosperity of the wicked but does not is more morally intelligible than a God who genuinely cannot and is engaged in real persuasive work alongside creatures. Both readings can claim Psalm 73 — the classical reads v. 17's "end" as eschatological vindication, the process reads v. 28's "drawing near" as the relational good that is itself the answer to the theodicy, not a stopgap until vindication arrives.

The second tension is the *cult-encounter* solution. The psalm does not resolve the theodicy by argument; it resolves by entry into sacred space. This is theologically generative and theologically unsettling. Generative, because it takes seriously that worship is not merely a response to truth but a means of perceiving it; the *miqdash* is not just a building but a place where reality is differently visible. Unsettling, because the resolution is unavailable to anyone outside that cult-encounter — to the worshipper without a sanctuary, the psalm appears to leave them in vv. 13-16 forever.

A first position reads the cult-encounter as **sacramental epistemology**: knowledge of God's justice is not simply propositional but is mediated through liturgical participation. Brueggemann, Dietrich Ritschl, and the post-liberal tradition (Hans Frei, George Lindbeck) have all defended versions of this. A second position reads it as **mystical immediacy**: the sanctuary is a metonymy for the soul's encounter with God, which can occur anywhere. Bernard of Clairvaux, John of the Cross, and the mystical tradition incline this way. A third position reads it as **historical contingency**: the psalmist had a temple, modern readers do not, and the psalm's resolution is therefore not directly transferable; the contemporary reader must improvise an analog (corporate worship, contemplative prayer, the liturgical year). Each reading has costs. The sacramental position risks making the psalm unavailable to private believers; the mystical position risks dissolving the specifically corporate-cultic dimension; the historical-contingency position risks reducing the psalm to a museum piece.

Where this leaves us: Psalm 73 is the Psalter's most candid acknowledgment that faith can be lost over the ordinary fact that the unrighteous live well. It refuses to deny the empirical observation, refuses to silence the speaker's near-apostasy, and refuses to settle the tension by a tidy doctrine. Its resolution is more demanding than any doctrine: it asks the reader to walk into the place where God is named and to let perception be re-formed there. The closing — *qarav et-Elohim li tov* — is not a conclusion in the philosophical sense but a confession in the existential sense. The psalm leaves it to each reader to find their *miqdash*.

The closing question for any reader: where, in your own life, is the place that re-orients perception? When envy of the prosperous wicked rises, what is the analog of the sanctuary — the practice, the body, the room, the company — in which what you can see changes, and you discern *acharit*?

## The Thread

From the threshold of the sanctuary, the psalmist looks back at his near-fall and forward to the wicked's slippery places, and concludes that drawing near to God is the only good worth wanting in heaven or on earth.
