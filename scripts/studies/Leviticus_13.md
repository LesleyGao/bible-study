# Leviticus 13: When the Priest Declares Clean

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter the rabbis call *Negaʿim* ("Afflictions") is the longest sustained technical passage in *Wayyiqrāʾ* (וַיִּקְרָא), fifty-nine verses of diagnostic criteria. It addresses what the older English translations called "leprosy" and what Hebrew calls *ṣāraʿat* (צָרַעַת), and it is among the most often misread passages in the Pentateuch because of that translation. Modern medicine knows *ṣāraʿat* is not Hansen's disease. Hansen's disease (*Mycobacterium leprae*) is poorly attested in the ancient Near East before the Hellenistic period — the earliest confirmed skeletal evidence is 2nd century BC, and the DNA evidence from pre-exilic Levantine contexts is essentially absent. Whatever *ṣāraʿat* is, it covers a constellation of skin conditions — possibly including psoriasis, vitiligo, fungal infections, ringworm, certain forms of eczema, and some severe skin pathologies — along with, by extension, fabric mildew (vv. 47-58) and, in chapter 14, house mold. The unifying feature is not a single disease entity but the *visible irruption of the surface*. Something white, something deep, something spreading, something with raw flesh appearing where skin should be.

The chapter sits, structurally, in the middle of the purity sequence: animals (ch. 11), postpartum (ch. 12), skin (13), cleansing ritual for skin (14), genital discharges (15), with chapter 16's Day of Atonement drawing it all together. Composition-critically it belongs to the Priestly source; the technical diction and the absence of hortatory rhetoric make it one of the clearer P passages. Wellhausen dated it late; Milgrom argued for pre-exilic composition, citing the archaic terminology of hair and skin categories and the absence of late features. Knohl assigns it to P rather than H. The chapter's voice is almost entirely clinical. There is no "be holy, for I am holy"; there is only "the priest shall examine."

Material culture matters extraordinarily here. The Egyptian Papyrus Ebers (c. 1550 BC), a medical papyrus preserved in Leipzig, contains extensive sections on skin conditions (§§712-738 on various eruptions; §§847-877 on growths) with descriptions strikingly close to Leviticus 13 — whitening, depth, raw flesh, spreading — but the Papyrus Ebers prescribes *treatment* (unguents, incantations, dietary adjustment), while Leviticus 13 prescribes *declaration*. This is the chapter's most important comparative fact. Mesopotamian medical texts (the *Diagnostic Handbook* compiled by Esagil-kin-apli in the 11th century BC, the *Šumma ālu* omen collections) also treat skin conditions diagnostically, but with causal explanations (demon, curse, divine anger) and prescribed therapies. The Hittite *Lāzi* and related ritual texts contain references to skin affliction but treat it ritually rather than medically. The distinctive move of Leviticus 13 is to *refuse therapy* within the priestly system. The priest is not a physician. He examines, isolates, reexamines, and pronounces *ṭāhôr* (טָהוֹר, clean) or *ṭāmēʾ* (טָמֵא, unclean). The afflicted person may well be healed — by time, by natural means, by a physician outside the priestly system — but the priest's work is declarative, not therapeutic.

Milgrom makes this point definitively in AB 3: the priest of Leviticus 13 is functioning as a *boundary-keeper*, not a healer. The authority he exercises is pronouncement, not cure. This is why the chapter's criteria are so meticulous — seven days of isolation, then examination, then possibly seven more days — and why the diagnostic language attends to appearance (*mar'e*, "sight") rather than cause. What matters is whether the condition violates the integrity of the skin in specified ways, not what produced it.

Key Hebrew terms. *Ṣāraʿat* (צָרַעַת) — the ritual-diagnostic category; the traditional English rendering "leprosy" is misleading and most recent translations (NRSV, CEB, Milgrom's own) render "scaly affliction" or retain the Hebrew. *Neteq* (נֶתֶק, v. 30) — the specific affliction of the scalp or beard, probably a fungal condition. *Baheret* (בַּהֶרֶת, v. 2), *śĕʾēt* (שְׂאֵת, v. 2), and *sappāḥat* (סַפַּחַת, v. 2) — three named surface lesions whose exact medical referents are debated. *Miḥyat bāśār ḥay* (מִחְיַת בָּשָׂר חַי, "raw flesh," v. 10) — the diagnostic marker that immediately triggers the unclean pronouncement; literally "the life of raw flesh." The paradox of the Hebrew is striking: "the life" of raw flesh makes the skin *dead*. The body that has opened is contagious.

On the rabbinic etymology that links *ṣāraʿat* to *môṣîʾ raʿ* (מוֹצִיא רָע, "one who brings forth evil," i.e., the slanderer): Bavli *Arachin* 15b develops this reading by deriving *mĕṣōrāʿ* (the afflicted person) from *môṣîʾ raʿ* via consonantal play. It is not a historical etymology — the Hebrew verb *ṣ-r-ʿ* is distinct from *y-ṣ-ʾ raʿ* — but a midrashic etymological reading. It reads the skin affliction as the physical manifestation of verbal sin, which the rabbis found confirmed in Miriam's *ṣāraʿat* after slandering Moses (Num 12) and in Gehazi's *ṣāraʿat* after his deception (2 Kings 5:27). The reading is pervasive in rabbinic literature and in medieval Jewish piety, and its ethical payload is considerable: the tongue that wounds in private causes a public sign that cannot be hidden.

The textual evidence for the chapter is strong. 4QLev-a, 4QLev-b, 4QLev-e, and 4QLev-g all preserve portions of Leviticus 13, and 11QpaleoLev preserves most of the chapter in paleo-Hebrew script. Variants are minor, almost all orthographic. The LXX translates smoothly but struggles with the lesion terminology — the Greek *lepra* (λέπρα) does not precisely map the Hebrew and has bequeathed the English mistranslation "leprosy." The Peshitta and SP follow MT.

Archaeological evidence for the social reality of Leviticus 13's isolations is sparse but suggestive. Second Temple period texts describe separate quarters for the afflicted outside the camp or outside the city, and Josephus (*War* 5.227-228, *Against Apion* 1.281-282) discusses the treatment of those with skin afflictions in Second Temple Judaism. The Copper Scroll from Qumran (3Q15) mentions an area reserved for *mĕṣōrāʿîm*. Medieval Christian leprosariums (from the 4th century onward) take their social architecture, if not their disease concept, from Leviticus 13.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Leviticus 13 is a legal taxonomy organized by diagnostic criteria. Its structural spine is the examination-reexamination sequence: the priest looks (*wĕrāʾâ*, "and shall see," the chapter's governing verb, appearing more than twenty times), makes an initial judgment, isolates the person for seven days (*hisgîr*, "and shall shut up"), looks again, and then either declares clean, isolates for another seven days, or declares unclean. The pattern repeats for each category of affliction (general skin, post-boil skin, post-burn skin, scalp/beard, cloth).

The sections: general skin eruptions (vv. 1-8), chronic skin eruptions (vv. 9-17), post-boil eruptions (vv. 18-23), post-burn eruptions (vv. 24-28), scalp and beard *neteq* (vv. 29-44), the social protocol of the afflicted (vv. 45-46), and mildew in cloth (vv. 47-58). The cloth section appended to the human diagnostics is not an afterthought but a structural statement: the same diagnostic logic applies to surface-irruption wherever it appears. This is the *textile* equivalent of the skin and anticipates chapter 14's house mold — eruption of the surface of a textile, eruption of the surface of a wall, eruption of the surface of a body, all under the same ritual category.

Verses 45-46 are the chapter's narrative punctum:

"The leprous person who has the disease shall wear torn clothes and let the hair of his head hang loose, and he shall cover his upper lip and cry out, 'Unclean, unclean.' He shall remain unclean as long as he has the disease. He is unclean. He shall live alone. His dwelling shall be outside the camp."

The prose here breaks the clinical register. The chapter has been pure procedure up to this point; verses 45-46 switch to the afflicted person's embodied performance. The torn clothes and loose hair are the signs of mourning (Lev 10:6 prohibited Aaron and his surviving sons from exactly these signs); the *mĕṣōrāʿ* is put in the posture of the bereaved, mourning his own ritual death. The cry *ṭāmēʾ ṭāmēʾ* ("unclean, unclean") is the only speech the chapter requires of the afflicted, and it is performative self-categorization. The final clause, *bādād yēšēb* ("he shall live alone"), uses *bādād*, the same word Lamentations 1:1 uses for Jerusalem after the destruction: *ʾêkâ yāšĕbâ bādād*, "How lonely sits the city." The narrative rhyme is deliberate or at least powerful: the solitary mourner-city and the solitary mourner-leper share a Hebrew word.

Mary Douglas reads the chapter as the clearest statement of priestly boundary-thinking: the afflicted's skin has broken its proper integrity, and the person must be placed outside the camp's integrity until the skin's integrity is restored. The outside of the body, in priestly theology, is the boundary that keeps the person a person. When that surface erupts, the person is literally no longer bounded, and ritual space must be reconfigured to mark the new state. Douglas's *Purity and Danger* and *Leviticus as Literature* both return to chapter 13 as foundational.

The chapter's governing leitwort is *ṭāhôr*/*ṭāmēʾ*, the pronouncement the priest makes. Verse 3 introduces the key verb *wĕṭimmĕʾô hakkōhēn* ("and the priest shall pronounce him unclean") — *ṭimmēʾ* in the piel is a declarative, not a causative. The priest does not *make* the person unclean; he *declares* the person to be in the unclean state. Similarly in verse 6, *wĕṭihărô hakkōhēn* ("and the priest shall pronounce him clean") is declarative. Ibn Ezra and Milgrom both emphasize this grammar: the priest is not an agent of cleanness but its authorized interpreter. The person's state precedes the priest's judgment; the priest recognizes it and pronounces it.

Textual variants in the chapter are minor. 4QLev-a at verse 31 has a small orthographic variant; the LXX clarifies *neteq* as a fungal scalp condition; the Peshitta adds a descriptive gloss at verse 45. None of this materially changes the reading.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** The Mishnah devotes an entire tractate, *Negaʿim* ("Afflictions"), fourteen chapters long, to the practical halakhah of Leviticus 13-14. It is among the most technically developed tractates in the whole Mishnah, and it has no corresponding Gemara in either Talmud — the tractate exists only in Mishnah and Tosefta, its contents being chiefly diagnostic rather than halakhically live after the Temple's destruction. Sifra's running commentary on Leviticus 13 is extensive. Bavli *Arachin* 15b-16a develops the *lāshōn hā-rāʿ* (slander) reading at length. Bavli *Horayot* 10a, *Shabbat* 97a, and several other passages collect the rabbinic readings of Miriam's *ṣāraʿat*.

The dominant rabbinic reading, by the period of the *Arachin* discussion, is that *ṣāraʿat* is the punishment for slander — in modern terms, a somatization of verbal sin. The evidence is drawn inner-biblically from Miriam (Num 12:10, who becomes *mĕṣōraʿat* immediately after speaking against Moses), Uzziah (2 Chr 26:19-21, who contracts *ṣāraʿat* on his forehead when he presumes to enter the Temple), Gehazi (2 Kings 5:27), and the rabbinic traditions about Korach (Num 16-17). The rabbis extend the causal list: *ṣāraʿat* can punish arrogance, slander, murder, perjury, theft, illicit sexual union, and stinginess (Bavli *Arachin* 16a). The extension is moralizing, and it has attracted criticism (even from within the tradition) for its implicit claim that visible affliction marks moral fault — a claim John 9:2-3 directly rebukes in the New Testament.

Rashi follows the rabbinic consensus: the chapter is about the slanderer, and the priest's declaration is a moral as well as ritual act. Ibn Ezra is more cautious, emphasizing the literal diagnostic reading and noting that the connection to slander is homiletical rather than textual. Ramban integrates the two: *ṣāraʿat* is a supernatural affliction sent by God in response to serious sin, and the diagnostic criteria distinguish true *ṣāraʿat* from ordinary skin disease. Maimonides, in *Hilchot Tumʾat Tzaraʿat* and in *Guide* 3.47, strongly emphasizes the moral dimension and argues that *ṣāraʿat* is not a natural disease at all but a miraculous sign; he explicitly denies that natural skin conditions are in view. Sforno reads the chapter as a pedagogy of repentance mediated through the body. Rabbi Samson Raphael Hirsch reads the priest's role symbolically: the priest, by declaring unclean, enacts God's concern for the afflicted's restoration; isolation is not abandonment but held space for healing. Rabbi Aryeh Kaplan's late 20th-century reading integrates the medical and ethical dimensions.

Nehama Leibowitz's *Studies in Vayikra* surveys the rabbinic positions with characteristic precision and argues that the slander-reading, whatever its homiletical value, should not exhaust the chapter. Jonathan Sacks in *Covenant and Conversation: Leviticus* develops the Hirsch line: the isolation of the *mĕṣōrāʿ* is ultimately a form of care, a kind of hospitalization that separates for healing rather than rejects. Avivah Zornberg reads the chapter's silence (the afflicted person says only "unclean, unclean") as a ritual-narrative loss of speech that mirrors the sin of slanderous speech.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** The chapter's centerpiece in the New Testament is Mark 1:40-45 (and parallels Matt 8:2-4, Luke 5:12-16), where Jesus heals a *leprosus* by touching him. The narrative is structured to reverse the contagion logic of Leviticus 13: normally, the unclean contaminates the clean; in Mark 1, the clean purifies the unclean by touch. Jesus then sends the healed man to the priest "for a testimony to them" (Mark 1:44), deliberately activating the priestly diagnostic system that Leviticus 13-14 establishes. The Gospels do not abolish the Levitical framework; they work within it while inverting its direction of contagion.

Luke 17:11-19, the ten lepers (one of whom is a Samaritan, the grateful one), extends the reversal. Matthew 11:5 cites cleansing of lepers as one of the messianic signs. The parallel of *ṣāraʿat* and skin affliction runs through the Gospels' theology of Jesus's ministry to the margins.

The Patristic reception is extensive. Origen's *Homilies on Leviticus* 8 treats chapter 13 with care, reading the priest's diagnostic work as a type of pastoral examination and the different categories of affliction as types of different spiritual conditions. This allegorical mode dominates the Christian reception for more than a thousand years. Gregory of Nazianzus's *Oration* 14, "On Love of the Poor" (c. AD 373), is one of the greatest early Christian texts on the treatment of those with skin afflictions — Gregory preaches to fourth-century Cappadocia about the social exclusion of lepers and argues, against the prevailing theological assumption that the afflicted were being punished by God, that they are icons of Christ and the test of the community's compassion. Gregory's brother Basil founded the *Basileias* outside Caesarea, an institution that included a leprosarium. John Chrysostom followed in the same pastoral register.

Augustine's treatment of the chapter is briefer; in *Contra Faustum* and the *Questions on the Heptateuch*, he distinguishes ceremonial from moral law and reads the chapter as ceremonial shadow fulfilled in Christ. Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I-II q. 102 a. 5 on ceremonial precepts, treats the chapter with care: its literal sense is the preservation of Israel from contagious skin conditions; its allegorical sense is Christ's cleansing of the heart; its moral sense is that the Christian community must examine sin carefully and mercifully. In II-II q. 32 on mercy and q. 30 on misericordia, Aquinas returns to the treatment of lepers as a test case for Christian love.

The medieval period institutionalized the chapter in the leprosarium system. By the 12th century, there were roughly 2,000 leprosaria across Europe, and the rituals of induction often drew explicitly on Leviticus 13-14. In some regions, the *mĕṣōrāʿ*'s civil status was that of the ritually dead — a "mass for the living" was celebrated for the newly diagnosed. St. Francis of Assisi's encounter with the leper — recorded in Thomas of Celano's *First Life* — is the most famous medieval Christian reenactment of Mark 1, a reversal of disgust into recognition.

The Reformation generally read the chapter typologically (Calvin) or allegorically (Luther in his sermons) as a figure of the soul's condition under sin. Calvin's *Commentaries on Leviticus* treats chapter 13 briefly and moves to Christological fulfillment. Luther's sermons on *ṣāraʿat* emphasize the priest's declarative function — *ṭāhôr*/*ṭāmēʾ* — as a type of the pastor's proclamation of grace and judgment through the Word. The declarative reading of the priest in Leviticus 13 became, for Luther, an analogue to the declarative function of the gospel word: sin and forgiveness are pronounced, not produced, by the one who speaks.

Modern critical scholarship reorients around the medical question. John Wilkinson's work on biblical leprosy (1977 and later) argued decisively that *ṣāraʿat* is not Hansen's disease. Milgrom's AB 3 places the chapter in its ancient Near Eastern medical-ritual context and argues for the priest as boundary-keeper rather than diagnostician. Gordon Wenham's NICOT, Jay Sklar's Tyndale, John Hartley's WBC, Ephraim Radner's *Leviticus* (Brazos), and L. Michael Morales's *Who Shall Ascend the Mountain of the LORD?* all develop the ritual-category reading. Jonathan Klawans's *Impurity and Sin in Ancient Judaism* is indispensable for understanding why the rabbinic slander-reading, however theologically rich, is categorically distinct from the chapter's own concern. Christopher Wright's *Old Testament Ethics* addresses the social exclusion element.

Susan Sontag's *Illness as Metaphor* (1978) remains a critical touchstone for thinking about what happens when a disease becomes a moral category — her critique of the cultural metaphorization of tuberculosis and cancer applies with force to the rabbinic slander-reading of *ṣāraʿat*. Nancy Eiesland's *The Disabled God* and more recent disability theology have engaged chapter 13 as a key site for thinking about bodily difference and liturgical space; the chapter's concern with exclusion — whatever its original function — has long-term effects on how communities form around the differently embodied. Amy-Jill Levine has written critically about Christian use of chapter 13 in ways that have stigmatized Jewish purity practice; Rachel Adler has reclaimed the category of *tumʾat mēt* (corpse impurity) and adjacent purity states as phenomenological rather than moral categories.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Jewish and Christian traditions converge on the chapter's moral-typological reading in the pre-modern period. The rabbinic tradition read *ṣāraʿat* as punishment for slander; the Christian tradition read it as a type of the soul under sin. Both traditions treated the chapter as revealing something about the moral condition of the afflicted. The modern critical consensus, across both traditions, has moved sharply away from this reading, insisting on the chapter's ritual rather than moral character. The convergence on the question of care is striking: Gregory of Nazianzus's *Oration* 14, the rabbinic obligation of *biqqur ḥôlîm* (visiting the sick) extended to those with skin afflictions in rabbinic practice, Francis's kiss, and contemporary disability theology all press toward the same conclusion — that the afflicted are not to be abandoned to their category. The divergence is on whether the category itself is abrogated (mainstream Christian reading, following Mark 1) or transformed (mainstream rabbinic reading, which retains the laws as diagnostically live while recognizing their practical suspension after the Temple).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is *ṣāraʿat* a disease or a ritual category?** Three positions.

The *medicalized* reading: *ṣāraʿat* is a set of actual diseases (psoriasis, fungal infections, various skin pathologies) that the ancient priestly system addresses diagnostically. On this reading the chapter is an early form of public health — isolation of the contagious, reexamination for improvement, reintegration after recovery. Maimonides, at the literal level, and many modern readers lean this way.

The *Milgrom ritual-category* reading: *ṣāraʿat* is a ritual state signaled by certain skin signs but distinct from disease as such. The priest is not a physician and does not treat; he declares, shuts up, reexamines, and pronounces. The person may recover (by natural means, by time, by outside care) or not, but the priest's system tracks ritual state, not disease progression. The chapter's diction (*ṭimmēʾ*/*ṭihēr* as declarative piel) supports this reading.

The *rabbinic moralized* reading: *ṣāraʿat* is a supernatural sign, divinely imposed as punishment for specific sins (slander, arrogance). The chapter's diagnostic procedure is not medicine but discernment of the sign. Maimonides defends this at *Guide* 3.47; the rabbinic tradition from Bavli *Arachin* 15b forward develops it.

Steelmanning the least intuitive: the rabbinic moralized reading is the hardest to defend against modern sensibilities, because it appears to treat the afflicted as guilty of sin and thereby to justify social exclusion on moral grounds. But it has a more defensible core. If one reads the moralized tradition against the background of Gregory of Nazianzus's *Oration* 14 and the rabbinic obligation of visiting the sick, the moralized reading was never in serious practice license to abandon the afflicted. What it was, rather, was a refusal to treat suffering as meaningless. The rabbinic tradition insisted that embodied affliction connects to something in the person's moral and spiritual life — not as simple punishment but as a summons to reckoning. That reading's failure mode is callousness; its best mode is the recognition that bodily life and spiritual life are not separable. Pastoral practice in both traditions has struggled to hold the latter without collapsing into the former.

*Where this leaves us:* the three readings are not fully compatible. The medicalized and the ritual-category readings can be combined (the priest addresses ritual category, the category correlates with certain actual skin conditions), and Milgrom does combine them. The moralized reading sits in tension with both: it adds a causal claim that the text itself does not explicitly make. The Torah's own silence on the cause of *ṣāraʿat* — the chapter says nothing about why the affliction appears — may be more instructive than any of the three positions. The priest diagnoses the state, not the source.

**Tension 2: Is the isolation of the *mĕṣōrāʿ* (vv. 45-46) stigmatizing exclusion or boundary-marking care?** Three positions.

The *stigma* reading: the ritual of torn clothes, loose hair, covered lip, and the self-identifying cry "unclean, unclean" is the text's own inscription of the afflicted's social death. Reading 13:45-46 as stigma is not a hostile reading — it is what the words say — and the long history of leper-exclusion in both Jewish and Christian societies bears it out. Susan Sontag's critique of the social production of disease-meaning applies.

The *boundary-care* reading: the isolation is not social death but ritual containment for the sake of eventual reintegration. The whole structure of chapter 13 bends toward the possibility of declaration clean; the seven-day and fourteen-day reexaminations are designed to *release* the afflicted when possible. The ritual of verses 45-46 is a marker of transitional state, not of permanent rejection. Rabbi S. R. Hirsch, Sacks, and Radner all emphasize this dimension.

The *liminal-theological* reading: the afflicted person occupies the same social position as the mourner (torn clothes, loose hair) and is held between the world of the living and the world of the dead. This is a thresholding, and it is neither stigma nor care purely but recognition of the real threshold the afflicted has crossed. Douglas's reading in *Leviticus as Literature* and Klawans's in *Impurity and Sin in Ancient Judaism* both support this.

Steelmanning the least intuitive: the boundary-care reading is counterintuitive against the plain sense of the self-identifying cry, which feels unmistakably like stigma. But the counterreading has text-internal support. The priest is obligated to reexamine after each seven-day period; the chapter's reintegration procedure in chapter 14 is elaborate and includes a sacrifice of atonement and restoration to the camp; the system is designed to be *passed through*, not terminated in. A society that abandoned the afflicted would not need diagnostic reexamination. The care reading is the reading that takes chapter 14 seriously as the resolution chapter 13 promises.

*Where this leaves us:* the chapter contains both dimensions. The stigma is real — the liminal state is hard, the social distance is enforced — and the care is real — the reexamination is mandated, the reintegration is prepared. Both Jewish and Christian pastoral traditions at their best have tried to hold these together; both at their worst have collapsed into the stigma without the care.

**One unresolved question:** If the priest in chapter 13 is declarative rather than causal, and if he does not heal but only pronounces, what is the priesthood *for* when the afflicted person is outside the camp? The chapter leaves the priest's presence during the affliction unspecified. Chapter 14 will describe the rite by which the priest goes out to meet the healed person, but during the affliction itself — during the seven days, the second seven days, the potentially longer period of confirmed *ṣāraʿat* — the afflicted is alone, and the priest is not with him. Whether the priest should be with him, and what the priest's pastoral obligation to the afflicted is during that period, the chapter does not say. The rabbinic and Christian traditions will each develop answers, and their answers will diverge.

## The Thread

Four non-obvious cross-references.

First: Numbers 12:10-15, Miriam's *ṣāraʿat* after speaking against Moses, is the Torah's own inner-biblical exegesis of chapter 13. Miriam becomes *mĕṣōraʿat kaššeleg* ("leprous, like snow"), is shut outside the camp for seven days (the period of chapter 13's initial isolation), and is restored when the people wait for her. The rabbinic slander-reading draws its primary warrant from Numbers 12, and the narrative gives the chapter a moralized register that the chapter itself does not carry explicitly.

Second: 2 Kings 5, Naaman the Aramean commander, is chapter 13's most surprising engagement. Naaman, a Gentile, is healed of *ṣāraʿat* by washing in the Jordan at Elisha's command — the priesthood is nowhere present, the Israelite ritual system is bypassed, and a foreign commander becomes clean. The counterpart, 2 Kings 5:27, is Gehazi, who inherits Naaman's *ṣāraʿat* through his own greed. The pairing is the prophetic tradition's running comment on the priestly system: the afflicted Gentile outsider becomes clean, the afflicted Israelite insider becomes unclean.

Third: Isaiah 53:4, "surely he has borne our griefs (*ḥolāyēnû*) and carried our sorrows (*makʾōbênû*), yet we esteemed him stricken (*nāgûaʿ*), smitten by God (*mukkēh ʾĕlōhîm*) and afflicted (*mĕʿunneh*)," uses *nāgûaʿ* — the same root as *negaʿ*, the affliction of chapter 13. The rabbinic and early Christian reading of Isaiah 53 as describing a *mĕṣōrāʿ* — a "leper messiah" — goes back to Bavli *Sanhedrin* 98b (which names the messiah *ḥivrāʾ de-bê rabbî*, "the leper-scholar of the rabbi's household") and to Origen's engagement with that tradition. The *mĕṣōrāʿ* of chapter 13 becomes the suffering servant of Isaiah 53.

Fourth: Mark 1:40-45 reverses chapter 13's contagion. Jesus stretches out his hand and touches (*hēpsato*) the leper; in Levitical logic the touch should contaminate Jesus, but instead the leper is cleansed. Mark's theological claim is precise: the purity of the one who touches is stronger than the impurity of the one touched. This is not the abolition of Leviticus 13's categories but their reversal, and the reversal requires the categories to be intact in order to have the force Mark intends. The chapter is therefore not rendered obsolete by Mark 1 but activated as the backdrop against which the gospel's radical gesture registers.
