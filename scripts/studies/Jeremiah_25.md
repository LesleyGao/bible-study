# Jeremiah 25: The Cup of Wrath and the Servant Nebuchadnezzar

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jeremiah 25 carries one of the most precisely datable superscriptions in the prophetic corpus: "the fourth year of Jehoiakim the son of Josiah, king of Judah (that was the first year of Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon)" (25:1). This synchronism places the chapter in **605/604 BC**, the very year of the **Battle of Carchemish**, where Nebuchadnezzar shattered Pharaoh Necho's army on the Euphrates and inherited the western empire of the recently fallen Assyrians. The Babylonian Chronicle (BM 21946), preserved in the British Museum and edited definitively by D. J. Wiseman in 1956, is the single most important extra-biblical witness to this period, and it confirms with day-by-day precision that Nebuchadnezzar received word of his father Nabopolassar's death while campaigning in the west, raced back to Babylon for his accession to the throne, and within weeks returned to subjugate "the whole of Hatti-country" — the Levant, Judah included. Jeremiah 25, in other words, is dated to the *historical hinge* of the late Ancient Near East. The world has just changed: Egypt is no longer a credible patron, Assyria is gone, and a new imperial order has been imposed almost overnight. Lundbom's Anchor commentary and Holladay's Hermeneia volume both treat this synchronism as the chronological anchor of the entire book.

The material culture of Late Iron II Judah hangs in the background: the small Judean polity, perhaps no more than 50,000 souls, is suddenly a vassal of an empire whose siege apparatus, deportation logistics, and provincial administration would within two decades destroy its capital. Lachish Ostraca IV and VI, written in the closing months before 587 BC, capture Judah's military commanders watching the signal-fires of their fellow garrisons go out one by one — material confirmation of Jeremiah's prophetic chronology. The bullae of Baruch ben Neriah ("belonging to Berekyahu son of Neriyahu the scribe"), of Gemariah ben Shaphan, of Jehucal ben Shelemiah, and of Pashhur ben Immer — all unearthed in the City of David — locate Jeremiah's named opponents and supporters in the dirt of the actual royal quarter.

The chapter is a Jeremiah ben Hilkiah composition, mediated through Baruch the scribe (cf. ch. 36). But chapter 25 is also where the **MT/LXX divergence becomes structurally decisive**. The Septuagint of Jeremiah is roughly one-eighth shorter than the Masoretic Text, and at 25:13 the LXX inserts the entire block of Oracles Against the Nations (chapters 46–51 in MT order) immediately after the line "everything written in this book, which Jeremiah prophesied against all the nations." The LXX thus uses 25:13 as the *gateway* to the OAN, making Jeremiah 25 the climactic center of the book. The MT, by contrast, scatters the OAN to the end (chs. 46–51) and treats ch. 25 as a transitional summary. This is the single most important text-critical decision in Jeremiah studies: the two ancient editions present radically different architectures. McKane's ICC commentary maps the divergence in painstaking detail; Carroll's OTL volume reads the redaction-critical implications hard, treating the LXX order as closer to an earlier compositional layer; Bright, Lundbom, and Stulman are more cautious but acknowledge the LXX preserves what is likely an older arrangement.

Key terms anchor the chapter's theological argument. *Kos chamat* (כּוֹס חֲמַת, "cup of wrath," 25:15) draws on a stock prophetic image (Isa 51:17, Ezek 23:31–34, Hab 2:16) but Jeremiah extends it cosmically — every nation drinks. *Shiv'im shanah* (שִׁבְעִים שָׁנָה, "seventy years," 25:11–12; cf. 29:10) becomes the most-cited prophetic numeral in the Hebrew Bible's afterlife. *Avadi Nevuchadnetzar* (עַבְדִּי נְבוּכַדְנֶאצַּר, "*my servant* Nebuchadnezzar," 25:9) is the appellation that has scandalized exegetes for two and a half millennia: the same noun YHWH applies to Moses (Num 12:7), David (2 Sam 7:5), and the Suffering Servant of Isaiah 52–53. *Sheshach* (שֵׁשַׁךְ, 25:26) is the *atbash* cipher (substitution by the alphabet read backward) for *Bavel* — Babylon — a coded reference whose presence in the MT but absence from some LXX manuscripts has fueled redaction debates since Duhm. *Cherev* (חֶרֶב, "sword," 25:16, 27, 29, 31) functions as the verbal hinge of the cup-pericope.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Jeremiah 25 is structurally the **fulcrum of the book**. Lundbom and Holladay treat verses 1–14 as the closing summary of the first half of Jeremiah's preaching (the "twenty-three years" of 25:3 brackets the ministry from Josiah's thirteenth year to this fourth year of Jehoiakim); verses 15–38 inaugurate the universal-judgment material that culminates in the OAN. The chapter combines two genres: a prose superscription with Deuteronomistic cadences (vv. 1–14, complete with the formulaic "you have not listened" — *lo' shema'tem* — repeated four times) and a poetic-oracular cup-vision (vv. 15–38) whose ending in 25:30–38 climbs into pure judgment-poetry. Stulman's *Order Amid Chaos* reads the chapter as the deliberate *peripeteia* of the Jeremianic narrative — the moment when the camera pulls back from Judah and reveals the global stage.

The cup-of-wrath sign-act of 25:15–29 is a *visionary* sign-act rather than a performed one: Jeremiah does not literally trek through Pharaoh's court with a goblet, as he will later wear a literal yoke before the envoys (ch. 27). The list of nations who drink (vv. 18–26) reads like a counter-clockwise tour of the ANE — Egypt, Uz, Philistia, Edom-Moab-Ammon, the Phoenician coast, the Arabian tribes, Elam and Media, finally "all the kings of the north." The *symmetrical* structure — Jerusalem first (v. 18), Babylon last (v. 26) — frames the entire pericope as God's even-handed judgment on covenant insider and pagan outsider alike.

The closing poem (vv. 30–38) deploys two stock metaphors: YHWH as roaring lion (cf. Amos 1:2, Hos 11:10) and the kings as "shepherds" who will be slaughtered like a "choice vessel." The shepherd-imagery looks both backward to Jer 23:1–4 (the indictment of Judah's failed shepherds) and forward to Ezekiel 34. The MT/LXX divergence at v. 13 must be flagged in any honest reading: the LXX's transition is "*and Jeremiah prophesied against the nations: Elam…*" launching directly into the OAN. The MT preserves the verse but redirects to the universal cup-vision.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish Trajectory.** The seventy-years prophecy of 25:11–12 generated a rich exegetical afterlife. Sirach 49:6–7 (early second century BC) lauds Jeremiah as the prophet "consecrated in the womb" who foretold the city's destruction; the writer assumes the seventy-years prophecy's literal fulfillment in 539/538 BC. Bavli Megillah 14b lists Jeremiah among the seven prophets who prophesied to gentile nations, citing this very chapter. Targum Jonathan paraphrases 25:9 to soften the "my servant Nebuchadnezzar" — replacing the appellation with a circumlocution about Nebuchadnezzar serving as YHWH's instrument, an interpretive gesture that sidesteps the scandal. Pesikta Rabbati 26 reads the chapter as the moment of maximum prophetic burden — the prophet who must announce destruction even to nations not his own. Rashi reads "seventy years" as running from the destruction of the Temple (586 BC) to its rebuilding under Darius (516 BC) — exactly seventy. Radak (David Kimhi) reads it more elastically as 605–539 (sixty-six years rounded). Ibn Ezra notes the calculation problem and inclines toward Rashi's reckoning. Abrabanel, writing in Iberian exile after 1492, reads the chapter messianically — every Jewish exile recapitulates the seventy years of Babylon, and the eschatological return is still pending.

**Christian Trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah* (Homily 14 in particular) reads the cup of wrath Christologically: the cup that Christ asks to pass from him in Gethsemane is *this* cup, the cup Israel and the nations should have drunk. **Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah*** (early fifth century) is the most extensive patristic engagement with chapters 25–28; Jerome lived in Bethlehem, knew the Hebrew and the LXX, and explicitly grappled with the textual divergence at 25:13. He sided with the Hebrew order. Aquinas, in his lectures on Jeremiah, follows Jerome's chronology and reads "my servant Nebuchadnezzar" through the lens of *common providence*: God raises up even pagan rulers as instruments. Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah* (delivered 1560–1564, published posthumously) makes 25:9 the locus classicus for his doctrine of the *secret providence* of God: "Although the Babylonians acted wickedly, yet God's will was fulfilled in their wicked work." The *Institutes* 1.16–18 cite this chapter implicitly throughout the providence treatise.

In the modern critical period, Duhm's 1901 commentary treated the chapter as heavily redacted (assigning much of it to a "Deuteronomistic" hand); Mowinckel's 1914 source-critical division of Jeremiah into Sources A (poetry), B (biographical narrative), and C (Deuteronomistic prose) places ch. 25 squarely in C. Holladay rebuilds toward authenticity. Carroll, more skeptical, reads the chapter as a post-exilic theological summary projected backward. Lundbom's Anchor volume defends extensive Jeremianic authenticity. Brueggemann's *A Commentary on Jeremiah: Exile and Homecoming* reads the chapter as the **most theologically scandalous summary in the prophetic corpus** — a prophet announcing that YHWH employs the *empire that destroys the Temple* as his very *servant*. **Kathleen O'Connor's *Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*** reads the cup-of-wrath imagery through trauma theory: the universal cup names the suffering as universal precisely so that Judah is not abandoned in its particular agony.

**Where they diverge.** The seventy-years calculation is contested across traditions. Christian patristic-historical readings (Jerome, Calvin) take it as fulfilled in 605–539 or 586–516 BC. Jewish exilic readings (Rashi, Radak) split between Temple-to-Temple and deportation-to-return. Dispensationalist readings, drawing on Daniel 9's seventy weeks, treat the prophecy as eschatologically open. Reformed amillennialism reads it as historically fulfilled and typologically completed in Christ. The calling of Nebuchadnezzar "*my servant*" is theologically more divisive: Calvin reads it through providence; Anabaptist tradition (Yoder's *The Politics of Jesus*) reads it as a prophetic *critique* of the equation between empire and divine will, not an endorsement; modern liberation theology (Gustavo Gutiérrez) reads it as a warning that God can use empire *against* itself. The MT/LXX divergence, finally, has its own confessional gravity: Catholic and Orthodox traditions historically privileged the LXX, Protestant traditions the MT — a difference that here actually changes the canonical shape of the book.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Nebuchadnezzar as *YHWH's servant*.** Does YHWH employ pagan empires as agents of his will, and what work does this do? The Reformed tradition, following Calvin, reads 25:9 as the high-water mark of the doctrine of *providential causality*: the king of Babylon, though acting from his own ambition and cruelty, is genuinely God's instrument. *Institutes* 1.18.4 famously argues that God's hidden will and the wickedness of secondary agents are not in contradiction. Augustine in *City of God* 5.21 had already grounded Rome's rise in providence by the same logic. Aquinas (*Summa* I.22.3) develops this through the metaphysics of primary and secondary causality. The Lutheran two-kingdoms doctrine inherits the framework: Babylon belongs to the left-hand kingdom, ruled by God through the magistrate's sword.

The Anabaptist tradition resists. John Howard Yoder's *Politics of Jesus* and Stanley Hauerwas after him read "my servant Nebuchadnezzar" as a *strategic concession* under exile — not a normative endorsement of empire as a vehicle of God. Brueggemann pushes the tension further: the appellation is *covenantally scandalous*, deliberately chosen to break Judah's confidence that covenant ensures protection. Modern Christian Zionism inherits a version of this through reverse: it reads divine instrumentality cautiously, knowing how it has been weaponized.

Steelman the Anabaptist position: the sequence of YHWH-as-judge texts in Jeremiah and Habakkuk is structured so that *every* empire eventually drinks the cup it is used to pour (note that 25:12 already announces Babylon's own punishment). The "servant" language is therefore *not* a permanent endorsement but a transitional designation — Babylon is YHWH's servant *for a season*, and that season ends with judgment on Babylon itself. To read 25:9 as a general theology of empire is to misread the rhetorical arc: the cup goes to Jerusalem first (v. 18) but reaches Babylon last (v. 26).

Where this leaves us: providence and protest are not opposites. Jeremiah holds both — Babylon is God's servant *and* God's defendant — and Christian theology has often collapsed one into the other.

**Tension 2: the seventy years and Christian reuse.** Should 25:11–12 be read historically (586/539 BC fulfillment) or apocalyptically (still operative in eschatology)? Daniel 9:24, written either in the late sixth century or, on critical readings, in the Maccabean period, *reapplies* Jeremiah's seventy years as seventy *weeks of years* — 490 years — extending the prophecy's clock into eschatology. 2 Chronicles 36:21 gives it a Sabbatical reading: the seventy years let the land enjoy its sabbaths (cf. Lev 26:34–35). Zechariah 1:12 and 7:5 still pray over the seventy years in the Persian period.

Dispensationalism inherits Daniel's apocalyptic reading and locates the final seven of the seventy weeks in a future Tribulation; Reformed amillennialism reads the seventy weeks as fulfilled in Christ's first advent. Catholic and Orthodox tradition reads the seventy years historically and the seventy weeks Christologically, terminating in the cross. Jewish reception, Rashi and Radak especially, holds the historical reading while leaving messianic timing to the eschaton.

Steelman the apocalyptic position: 2 Chronicles 36:21's Sabbatical interpretation makes the seventy years *typological*, not merely historical. The seventy is the number of completed exile — and exile-as-such is not over. Even after the return, Ezra 9:8–9 confesses Israel is still slaves. Daniel 9 is therefore not a misreading; it is an extension of the typology. To insist on a closed historical fulfillment is to assume that the exilic condition was solved in 539 BC, which the post-exilic literature itself denies.

Where this leaves us: the seventy is at once literal-historical (a Babylonian age that ended in 539 BC) and typological (a prophetic shorthand for the unfinished business of exile). Christian readings that pick only one usually miss the other.

**Unresolved question.** If YHWH can call Nebuchadnezzar *my servant*, what prevents the appellation from being weaponized — by Christendom, by empire, by every regime that has called itself God's instrument since? The text gives the title and immediately announces its expiration (vv. 11–12); is that built-in obsolescence enough to keep the title from becoming an ideology, or has the history of Christian empire shown that it isn't?

## The Thread

The seventy years of 25:11–12 echo forward into Daniel 9:24's seventy weeks of years, into 2 Chronicles 36:21's Sabbatical theology of the land's rest, and into Zechariah 1:12 / 7:5's still-petitioning prayer over the unfinished exile. The cup of wrath of 25:15 echoes into Mark 14:36, where Christ in Gethsemane prays "let this cup pass from me" — N. T. Wright reads the gospel cup as Israel's cup of wrath drained finally by the Messiah on her behalf. The Jeremiah twenty-three-year ministry summary of 25:3 ("from the thirteenth year of Josiah … but you have not listened") frames the *covenantal lawsuit* tradition that runs from Deuteronomy 32 through Hosea 4:1 to Romans 1:18–32. And the universal indictment of 25:31 ("the LORD has an indictment against the nations… he is entering into judgment with all flesh") prefigures Acts 17:31 and Revelation 20:11–15: the day appointed when the cup passed in Gethsemane is finally poured out.
