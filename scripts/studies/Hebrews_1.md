# Hebrews 1: God Has Spoken in His Son — The Exordium and the Angel-Catena

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Hebrews opens in a world where the question of how God speaks — and to whom, and through what mediators — was unsettled across both Jewish and Greco-Roman religious imaginations. The Roman empire of the mid-first century, under the late Julio-Claudians and the Flavians, was a religiously plural environment in which the imperial cult was rising, mystery religions were flourishing, and the Jewish Diaspora communities of Alexandria, Antioch, Asia Minor, and Rome maintained synagogues that were both centers of Torah-instruction and points of cultural negotiation with their host cities. The political horizon hangs over Hebrews. If the homily was composed before 70 AD, the Jerusalem temple was still functioning and the Levitical sacrificial system was a living institution; if it was composed after 70, the destruction of the temple under Titus had collapsed the institutional frame within which the author's argument was originally legible. Most scholars place Hebrews somewhere between the late 60s and the 90s. The present-tense verbs describing priestly activity (7:27, 8:3-4, 9:6-7, 10:1-3, 13:10-11) push some readers toward a pre-70 date; others, including Paul Ellingworth (NIGTC) and Harold Attridge (Hermeneia), argue the present tense is generic or describes the tabernacle pattern of Exodus rather than the historical Jerusalem temple, leaving a post-70 reading viable. A reasonably firm terminus ad quem comes from *1 Clement* (c. 96 AD), which appears to know Hebrews — *1 Clem* 36 quotes Heb 1:3-13 closely.

The material culture of the audience is Hellenistic-Jewish. The author writes in the most polished Greek in the New Testament. He (or she) quotes the LXX exclusively — never the Masoretic Hebrew — and operates fluently within the literary conventions of Greek rhetorical training: periodic sentences, alliteration, paronomasia, and the formal device of *synkrisis* (comparison). The Alexandrian Jewish-Platonic tradition exemplified by Philo (*De Confusione Linguarum*, *De Opificio Mundi*, *De Vita Mosis*) is one plausible formation context: Philo's Logos-theology, his contrast of heavenly archetype and earthly copy, and his allegorical reading of the tabernacle all shadow the moves Hebrews makes. James W. Thompson (*The Beginnings of Christian Philosophy: The Epistle to the Hebrews*, 1982) argued for a strongly Philonic-Platonic background; Lincoln Hurst (*The Epistle to the Hebrews*, 1990) pushed back, contending that apocalyptic Jewish thought — the heavenly archetype as eschatological reality, not Platonic ideal — is the better frame.

Authorship is the most candidly unsettled question in the New Testament. **The text never names its author.** Eastern tradition leaned toward Paul; the Western canon hesitated for centuries, and Hebrews was only formally ratified at the Council of Hippo (393) and the Third Council of Carthage (397). Origen's verdict has never been improved upon: "Who wrote the epistle, in truth God knows" (Eusebius, *HE* 6.25.14). Modern proposals are educated guesses, none certain — Apollos (Luther's intuition; defended by William Manson), Barnabas (Tertullian, *De Pudicitia* 20), **Priscilla (Adolf von Harnack, "Probabilia über die Adresse und den Verfasser des Hebräerbriefes," 1900)**, Silas/Silvanus, Clement of Rome, Luke, Aquila. What can be said with confidence is that the author is a Hellenistic-Jewish Christian of high rhetorical training, a second-generation believer (2:3 says salvation was "attested to us by those who heard" the Lord), and one whose theological imagination is steeped in the LXX.

The textual evidence is rich. **P46 (c. 200) places Hebrews directly after Romans, not at the end of the Pauline corpus** — a canonical-historical fact that signals how seriously P46's tradition took Hebrews' Pauline associations even while the West remained skeptical. Sinaiticus (א), Vaticanus (B), Alexandrinus (A), Ephraemi (C), and Bezae (D) where extant all preserve the text well. P13 contains parts of chapters 2-5 and 10-12. **11QMelchizedek**, the Qumran fragment (1st c. BC), provides crucial Second Temple context for the high-Christological framework Hebrews will deploy more explicitly in chapter 7: there Melchizedek is a heavenly *elohim* figure who executes eschatological judgment.

Greek key terms in this chapter: ἀπαύγασμα *apaugasma* "radiance" (1:3); χαρακτήρ *charaktēr* "exact imprint" (1:3); ὑπόστασις *hypostasis* "being/substance" (1:3); φέρων *pherōn* "upholding/bearing" (1:3); κρείττων *kreittōn* "superior" (1:4 — a thematic word that recurs throughout the book).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre first. Hebrews is not primarily a letter; the author calls it a *λόγος παρακλήσεως* (*logos paraklēseōs*) "word of exhortation" in 13:22 — the same idiom Acts 13:15 uses for the synagogue homily Paul delivers in Pisidian Antioch. This is decisive. **Hebrews is best understood as a sermon or homily with a brief epistolary postscript (13:22-25).** Lawrence Wills (*The Form of the Sermon in Hellenistic Judaism and Early Christianity*, 1984) and Hartwig Thyen (*Der Stil der jüdisch-hellenistischen Homilie*) have mapped the form: an alternation of expository sections (drawing on scripture) and hortatory sections (driving home the implications). Chapter 1 functions as an exordium — a formal opening that announces the theme, establishes the speaker's authority, and conditions the audience to hear what follows.

The opening sentence (1:1-4) is one of the most carefully constructed periods in the New Testament. It is a single Greek sentence of seventy-two words, alliterative on pi (*polymerōs kai polytropōs palai*), syntactically symmetrical (the past speaking of God in the prophets balanced against the eschatological speaking in the Son), and theologically dense — within four verses the author has named seven attributes of the Son: appointed heir, agent of creation, radiance of glory, exact imprint of being, upholder of the universe, purifier of sins, and exalted king at God's right hand. Albert Vanhoye (*La Structure littéraire de l'Épître aux Hébreux*, 1963/1976) read this exordium as the announcement of the chiastic structure that organizes the entire homily; the seven attributes are not a random list but the program of what the rest of the book will expound.

The dominant rhetorical move from 1:5 onward is *synkrisis* — formal comparison. The audience is being shown that the Son is greater than the angels, and the proof comes through a chain of seven OT citations (a *catena*): Ps 2:7 (1:5a), 2 Sam 7:14 (1:5b), Deut 32:43 LXX (1:6), Ps 104:4 (1:7), Ps 45:6-7 (1:8-9), Ps 102:25-27 (1:10-12), and Ps 110:1 (1:13). The catena is held together by an *a fortiori* logic ("to which of the angels did God ever say...?") and by the contrastive particle *de* ("but of the Son he says..."). The citations are uniformly LXX; in 1:6 the quotation of Deut 32:43 ("let all God's angels worship him") follows the LXX rendering, which differs from the Masoretic Text — and is now confirmed by the Qumran fragment **4QDeut^q**, which preserves an earlier Hebrew Vorlage that included precisely this clause. The LXX is not an error here; it is a witness to a Hebrew text-form the MT did not preserve.

Locate the chapter in the book's argument: chapter 1 is the announcement; chapter 2 is the first warning (so great a salvation, drift, neglect); chapters 3-4 develop the Moses comparison and the rest-warning from Psalm 95; chapters 5-7 introduce and unfold the Melchizedek high-priesthood; chapters 8-10 expound the new covenant and the once-for-all sacrifice; chapter 11 catalogs the heroes of faith; chapters 12-13 close with sustained exhortation. Chapter 1's exordium contains in compressed form the entire trajectory: from "God has spoken" the rest of the homily unfolds.

The Philonic-Platonic vs. apocalyptic background debate matters here because of the verbal echoes in 1:3. *Apaugasma tēs doxēs* directly echoes **Wisdom of Solomon 7:25-26**, where divine Wisdom is "the radiance *apaugasma* of eternal light, a spotless mirror of God's working, an image of his goodness." The Logos and Sophia speculations of Hellenistic Judaism are unmistakably present. Whether they enter Hebrews via Philonic Platonism (Thompson) or via apocalyptic-Wisdom traditions (Hurst) is the live debate.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical NT arc — Israel's story → Jesus → the church — Hebrews 1 sits at the hinge between the prophetic word given to Israel and the eschatological Son-speech given to the church. The "many and various ways" of God's prior speaking are not denigrated; they are gathered up and surpassed by the single decisive Son-speech "in these last days." The chapter places Christ at the apex of OT scriptural witness: he is the one of whom Ps 2, 2 Sam 7, Deut 32, Ps 45, Ps 102, and Ps 110 finally speak.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic readers gravitated to Hebrews 1 for high-Christological proof. Origen, the first to treat Hebrews at length, recognized its Pauline echoes while admitting the style was different — and was the first to record the anonymity verdict. **Chrysostom's homilies on Hebrews** treat 1:1-4 as the dogmatic charter for a divine-Son Christology that does not collapse the Father-Son distinction. **Athanasius's use of Heb 1:3 in the Arian controversy was decisive**: *charaktēr tēs hypostaseōs* — "exact imprint of his being" — was deployed against any reading that made the Son ontologically lesser than the Father. The Greek word *hypostasis* in Heb 1:3 is pre-Trinitarian — it means "underlying reality" or "substance" — but the post-Nicene Cappadocians (Basil, the two Gregorys) retrieved it as the technical term for "person" within the Trinity (over against *ousia* "substance"). The semantic journey from Heb 1:3 to the Cappadocian formulation is one of the critical chapters in Trinitarian doctrine. Cyril of Alexandria and Theodoret of Cyrus continued the line, the former mining 1:3 for the unity of subject in Christ, the latter for the distinction of natures.

Medieval theology returned again and again to chapter 1. Aquinas's *Commentary on Hebrews* (a late, polished work) reads 1:1-4 as a christological summa: appointment, creation, illumination, providence, purification, exaltation. The Glossa Ordinaria, which compiled the patristic tradition for the medieval classroom, foregrounded Athanasius's reading. Bernard of Clairvaux's preaching tradition, while focused more on Hebrews 3-4, kept the exordium in view as the ground of all Christology.

The Reformers were divided on the book as a whole. Luther famously moved Hebrews to the back of his Bible because of the apostasy passages, but he never doubted the depth of its Christology. Calvin's commentary on Hebrews remains a classic: **Calvin reads Heb 1:3 *hypostasis* as foundational to Reformed Trinitarianism**, glossing the term with characteristic precision. The Lutheran scholastics deployed 1:3 against Socinianism and any unitarian rereading. The Wesleyan tradition, while less invested in Trinitarian polemic, embraced 1:1-4 as the warrant for sustained worship of Christ.

Critical scholarship since the nineteenth century has approached Hebrews more cautiously. F.C. Baur and the Tübingen school treated it as a relatively late, mediating document. **Adolf von Harnack's 1900 essay "Probabilia"** advanced the case for Priscilla as author — an argument that has won supporters and critics in roughly equal measure. Bultmann and the Bultmannians saw Hellenistic-religious-mysticism in the background. **Ernst Käsemann's *Das wandernde Gottesvolk* / *The Wandering People of God* (1939)** read Hebrews through a Gnosticizing lens, with the heavenly journey of the soul as background; that reading has been largely abandoned but its rigor was influential. Contemporary scholarship is dominated by Ellingworth (NIGTC), Attridge (Hermeneia), William Lane (WBC), Luke Timothy Johnson (NTL), Craig Koester (Anchor Yale), Gareth Lee Cockerill (NICNT), Peter T. O'Brien (Pillar), and David deSilva (*Perseverance in Gratitude*, socio-rhetorical). On the high-Christology question, **Larry Hurtado (*Lord Jesus Christ*)** and **Richard Bauckham (*God Crucified* / *Jesus and the God of Israel*)** have argued that Hebrews 1 evidences a Christology that includes Jesus within the unique identity of YHWH from the earliest stages of Christian thought.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** The Jewish reception of Hebrews has historically been wary. The book quotes the LXX exclusively, and several of its readings depend on LXX-MT divergences (1:6 quoting Deut 32:43 LXX is the paradigm case). For centuries, Jewish polemic treated this as evidence of Christian textual carelessness; modern scholarship — including the Qumran finds (4QDeut^q vindicating the LXX reading at Deut 32:43) — has complicated that polemic. Daniel Boyarin, Mark Nanos, and Pamela Eisenbaum represent a generation of Jewish scholars who read NT documents as intra-Jewish productions before they became Christian against Jewish. Eisenbaum's *The Jewish Heroes of Christian History* (1997) takes up Hebrews 11 specifically; it reads the catalog as a Christianizing of Jewish heroes. Where contemporary Jewish scholarship still pushes back is on supersessionist readings of Hebrews 8-10 — the new covenant language, on Jewish hearings, has historically been weaponized.

11QMelchizedek (Qumran, 1st c. BC) offers the single closest Second Temple parallel for Hebrews' high-Christological framework. Although Hebrews 1's catena focuses on the Son and not on Melchizedek explicitly, the apocalyptic-heavenly frame within which Melchizedek is honored as an *elohim* figure at Qumran is exactly the conceptual habitat in which Hebrews' Son-Christology becomes legible.

**Where this leaves us.** The dialogue is active. Hurtado, Bauckham, and Hays have made high-Christology readings of Hebrews 1 dominant in Anglophone scholarship; Dunn-style developmental readings persist; and Jewish-Christian conversation, energized by Qumran and by post-Holocaust theology, has moved past polemic toward shared philological work.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Pre-existence and Wisdom-Christology.** Heb 1:1-4 attributes to the Son a series of prerogatives that, in earlier Jewish literature, were ascribed to personified Wisdom (Wisdom of Solomon 7:25-26; Sirach 24; Proverbs 8) and to the divine Logos (Philo). Does this entail that the Son is pre-existent and personally divine, or is the language idiomatic Wisdom-personification that does not entail an independent pre-existent person? Bauckham's high-Christology reading (*God Crucified*) holds that the Son is included in YHWH's unique identity from eternity; Hurtado (*Lord Jesus Christ*) reaches similar conclusions through devotional-historical analysis. The Catholic tradition (CCC 461-463) and the Reformed confessions (Westminster 8) agree: Heb 1:1-4 is a foundational text for the eternal generation and full divinity of the Son. Eastern Orthodoxy, formed by the Cappadocians, has taken 1:3 as its scriptural lodestar.

The least intuitive position — and the one to steelman — is **James Dunn's developmental reading in *Christology in the Making* (1980).** Dunn argued that Heb 1's Wisdom-language need not be understood ontologically; it can be read as the application of personified-Wisdom idiom (a literary device, not a metaphysical claim) to Christ, marking him out as the locus of God's saving activity without yet asserting a pre-existent divine person. On Dunn's reading, the metaphysical Christology of Nicaea is a legitimate development from but not strictly entailed by Hebrews 1. The strength of Dunn's case is its philological caution: Wisdom in Wisdom of Solomon and Sirach is *grammatically* personified but no Jew of the period thought Wisdom was a separate divine person. To attribute pre-existent personhood to the Son on the basis of Wisdom-language alone is to read Nicene categories backward into a first-century text. The weakness is that Hebrews does more than apply Wisdom-language: it has the Son *speak* and *act* and be *worshiped* (1:6); the language ceases to be metaphor and becomes address. Most contemporary scholars (Bauckham, Hurtado, Fee, Wright) have judged the high-Christological reading the more compelling, but Dunn's discipline still chastens premature dogmatic conclusions.

**Where this leaves us:** Hebrews 1 is one of the strongest textual warrants in the NT for divine-Son Christology, and the post-Nicene tradition has read it that way responsibly; but Dunn's caution about reading later metaphysics back into first-century idiom remains a discipline worth keeping.

**Tension 2: Christological exegesis of the Old Testament.** Hebrews 1:5-14 reads seven OT texts — written about Davidic kings, about angels, about Israel — as speaking directly about the Son. Is this a legitimate reading of the OT, or is it a Christianizing imposition on texts that have their own integrity in their original Israelite settings? The Catholic tradition (CCC 115-118) distinguishes the literal sense from the spiritual senses (allegorical, moral, anagogical) and grants the spiritual senses real exegetical authority. Henri de Lubac's *Medieval Exegesis* and Hans Urs von Balthasar's biblical theology both defend a *sensus plenior* — a fuller sense intended by the divine author that exceeds the human author's awareness. The Reformed tradition (Calvin, Westminster Confession 1) is more restrained: the literal-historical sense is primary, and Christological readings must be grounded in the text's typological structure rather than in allegory cut loose from the text.

Critical-historical scholarship has often treated Hebrews' catena as anachronistic — as reading post-resurrection Christology back into psalms about Davidic kings. **Brevard Childs's canonical approach** offers a mediation: the Christological reading is legitimate within the canonical context of the church's scripture, even if the historical-critical reading of each psalm in its original setting yields a different sense. Jewish scholars (Levenson, Boyarin) push back harder: these texts have a plain Jewish sense, and the Christological reading is exegetical violence done from outside.

The least intuitive position — and the one to steelman — is the **Jewish-critical objection that Hebrews' catena imposes meaning rather than discovers it.** Steelmanned: Psalm 2 is a royal-coronation psalm for an Israelite king; its "you are my Son" is enthronement language adopted from Egyptian and Mesopotamian royal ideology. Psalm 45 is a wedding psalm for an Israelite king. Psalm 102 addresses YHWH about his eternity, not a divine son. To read these as direct addresses to a pre-existent divine Son is to bend the texts to a frame they did not originally inhabit. The strength of this objection is its respect for the primary historical setting; the weakness is that the OT itself sets up typological structures (the Davidic covenant looks beyond any particular king; the royal psalms look toward an ideal anointed) that invite the Christological reading from within. The Catholic *plenior sensus*, the Reformed typological reading, and Childs's canonical approach all wrestle honestly with the tension.

**Where this leaves us:** Hebrews 1's catena is a paradigm of early-Christian Christological exegesis — beautiful, theologically powerful, and exegetically contestable. The honest reader holds the original Israelite settings of each psalm and the canonical Christological reading in productive tension.

**One unresolved question:** If the Son is the one through whom God has spoken decisively "in these last days," what does it mean — for Christians, for Jews, for the philosophical theology that has tried to think God for two millennia — that the form of that speaking was a flesh-and-blood man who was tortured to death by an empire, and that the radiance of God's glory chose to pass through that body and that death rather than through the prophets' words alone?

## The Thread

- **John 1:1-18** — the Johannine prologue and the Hebrews exordium are the NT's two great high-Christological openings, and their verbal echoes (Logos as agent of creation, glory, the contrast with prior witnesses) are unmistakable. They were composed independently in different communities and yet converge.
- **Colossians 1:15-20** — another seven-attribute hymnic Christology (image of God, firstborn of creation, agent of creation, head of the body, firstborn from the dead, fullness, reconciler). The structural parallel to Heb 1:1-4 suggests a shared pre-literary tradition of Christological hymnody in the early church.
- **Wisdom of Solomon 7:25-26** — the deuterocanonical text Heb 1:3 most directly echoes. The verbal parallel (*apaugasma*) is one of the cleanest cross-references between a NT text and a Second Temple Jewish text.
- **Hebrews 12:18-29** (later in the homily) — the eschatological speaking of God at Sinai and at the heavenly Zion. Chapter 1's "God has spoken" reaches its climax in chapter 12's "see that you do not refuse him who is speaking" — the exordium and the peroration are bound together.
