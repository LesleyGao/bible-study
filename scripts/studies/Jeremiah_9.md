# Jeremiah 9: The Weeping Prophet, the Wailing Women, and the Boast That Reorders the World

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter sits in the closing decades of the Judean monarchy, somewhere between the death of Josiah at Megiddo (609 BC) and the first Babylonian deportation (605). Jeremiah's ministry, called in the thirteenth year of Josiah (c. 627 BC), spans five kings — Josiah, Jehoahaz, Jehoiakim, Jehoiachin, Zedekiah — and three deportations: 605, 597, 587/6. The political world in which Jeremiah 9 was preached is a world of doomed maneuver. Josiah's son Jehoahaz lasts three months before Pharaoh Necho deports him to Egypt. Jehoiakim, installed by Necho, switches loyalty to Babylon after Carchemish (605) and then revolts. Jeremiah preaches into a court that still believes in survival by alliance, while the prophet insists that the only survivable posture is submission to the very imperial power his hearers most fear.

The material culture is Late Iron II Judah. The Lachish ostraca, excavated in 1935–38 from the gatehouse of Stratum II at Tell ed-Duweir, are roughly contemporary with Jeremiah 34:7's note that only Lachish and Azekah remained as fortified cities. Letter IV's plaintive line — "we are watching for the signals of Lachish... we cannot see Azekah" — preserves the texture of the very collapse Jeremiah 9 anticipates. The Jerusalem bullae corpus has put names from the prophet's circle in our hands: Baruch ben Neriah (the scribe of Jer 36), Gemariah ben Shaphan (Jer 36:10), Jehucal ben Shelemiah and Pashhur ben Immer (both opponents of Jeremiah, Jer 38:1), all attested on clay sealings excavated by Eilat Mazar in the City of David and by Yigal Shiloh's earlier expedition. The Anathoth-to-Jerusalem geography matters: Jeremiah is a priest from a small Benjaminite village three miles northeast of the capital, an outsider speaking inside the temple precinct.

Mourning customs underwrite the whole second half of the chapter. ANE iconography — Egyptian tomb paintings of professional mourners with raised arms, Mesopotamian *kalu*-priest laments, Ugaritic ritual texts — shows a recognized class of female lament-performers. The Hebrew term in Jer 9:17, *meqonenot* (מְקוֹנְנוֹת, "wailing women"), points to a guild trained to perform the *qina* (קִינָה), the limping 3:2 lament rhythm. Cutting flesh, shaving heads, wearing sackcloth — practices Deuteronomy 14:1 prohibits but the prophets repeatedly assume — were part of this professional mourning vocabulary.

Authorship and dating remain contested. The traditional view — Jeremiah son of Hilkiah of the priestly family at Anathoth, dictating to Baruch ben Neriah (Jer 36) — is preserved by Sirach 49:6–7 and the rabbinic tradition. Critical scholarship since Bernhard Duhm (1901) has identified at least three layers: poetic oracles (Source A, mostly authentic), biographical prose by Baruch (Source B), and Deuteronomistic prose sermons (Source C, redactional). Sigmund Mowinckel's tripartite source theory remains influential. **Jack R. Lundbom (Anchor Bible 1999–2004)** defends a more conservative attribution; William Holladay (Hermeneia 1986/89) traces an evolving Jeremianic corpus. Robert P. Carroll (OTL 1986) reads the book as largely a post-exilic Deuteronomistic composition projecting itself back onto a prophetic figure. William McKane (ICC) speaks of a "rolling corpus." Walter Brueggemann reads Jeremiah as theological witness regardless of layer; Louis Stulman emphasizes the book's structural design; Kathleen O'Connor (*Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*, 2011) reads it through trauma theory.

The MT–LXX divergence is structurally critical for the entire book: the Septuagint is roughly one-eighth shorter than the Masoretic Text and arranges the oracles against the nations differently. The Qumran evidence is decisive — 4QJer-b (and 4QJer-d) preserves a Hebrew Vorlage that matches the LXX, while 4QJer-a tracks the MT. Two Hebrew editions circulated side by side at Qumran. Chapter 9 is less acute on this front than chapter 10 will be, but the textual situation should be in the reader's mind.

Key terms to hold: *qina* (קִינָה), the dirge meter; *meqonenot* (מְקוֹנְנוֹת), the professional mourning women; *bin* (בִּין), the verb of discernment in 9:12 and again in the boasting verse 9:24 (*haskel weyado'a*, "to understand and to know"); *shuv* (שׁוּב), the great Jeremianic verb of "turning" or "returning" that runs through the book; and *milah ba-basar* (מִלָּה בַבָּשָׂר), circumcision in the flesh, which 9:25–26 uses to set up the famous opposition between bodily mark and the uncircumcised heart.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is a tightly woven sequence of poetic oracles in *qina* meter, framed at the front by the prophet's own grief (vv. 1–6) and at the back by the rhetorical hammer of the boasting oracle (vv. 23–24) and the heart-circumcision coda (vv. 25–26). The genre is mixed: prophetic lament (vv. 1–2, 10), oracle of judgment (vv. 7–11), Deuteronomistic prose explanation (vv. 12–16), summons to the mourning guild (vv. 17–22), wisdom-shaped oracle (vv. 23–24), and a closing oracle against the circumcised-but-unfaithful (vv. 25–26). Brueggemann reads this layering as deliberate — poetry's pain framed by prose's verdict — and Stulman calls it a "conversational" structure where multiple voices argue inside the chapter itself.

The rhetoric is dominated by leitwort. Three times the people "do not know" (*lo yada'uni*, vv. 3, 6) Yahweh, only for the boasting oracle to demand a knowing that *boasts*: "let him who boasts boast in this, that he understands and knows me" (*ki im bezot yithallel hammithallel haskel weyado'a oti*, v. 24). The pun is structural. The deceit-tongue triplet (vv. 3, 5, 8) builds a forensic indictment by sound alone. The wailing oracle (vv. 17–22) modulates the *qina* meter into a self-conscious meditation *on* lament — call the women, teach your daughters a dirge — until lamentation becomes a transmitted craft, a curriculum for survival. Then the unforgettable image: *ki ala mavet bechalonenu*, "for death has come up into our windows" (v. 21), where Death is personified as a household intruder, a thief or perhaps a *demon* climbing in the way Mot or Resheph might in Ugaritic mythology, though the Hebrew demythologizes the figure into pure dread.

The *qina* (3:2 limping) meter dominates the chapter, especially vv. 17–22. This is the same meter that runs through Lamentations and through Ezekiel's dirges over Tyre. The fact that Lamentations is traditionally attributed to Jeremiah on the strength of Jer 9:1 is itself a literary judgment — the post-biblical tradition recognized the *qina* voice and named its master.

Inclusio and frame: the chapter opens with the prophet's tears (v. 1) and closes with the threat to circumcise the heart (v. 25–26). Tears for the slain become surgery for the living. The literary movement is from grief to verdict to curriculum to cosmic reordering. Verse 24's three rejected boasts — wisdom, might, riches — read as a catalogue of the ruling-class identity Jeremiah's hearers actually trusted, and the verse's final clause names the *only* ground that survives: knowing Yahweh, "who practices steadfast love, justice, and righteousness in the earth." Hesed, mishpat, tzedaqah — the triad runs back to Mosaic covenant and forward through Hosea 6:6 and Micah 6:8. The boasting oracle, in form, is closer to wisdom literature than prophecy; its rhythm anticipates Proverbs 8 and Job 28.

Canonical position: Jeremiah is the second of the Latter Prophets in the Hebrew Bible (after Isaiah, before Ezekiel and the Twelve), and the chapter's grief-and-verdict pattern is foundational for the entire prophetic corpus's later reception.

Textual note for ch. 9: the divergences are smaller than in ch. 10. The Aramaic verse problem (10:11), the missing 10:6–8 in LXX — those crises do not appear here. Jeremiah 9 is comparatively stable in textual transmission. It should still be read knowing that the *book* it sits in is one of the Hebrew Bible's two great test cases (with Daniel) for the canonical-pluralism question.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the Hebrew canon, Jeremiah 9 looks both directions. Backward to Deuteronomy 28's curses, where exile, sword, and "scattering among the nations" are the explicit covenant penalty (Deut 28:64); to Hosea's tears for the northern kingdom; to Amos's verdict on a court that "knows not how to do right." Forward to Lamentations — whose alphabetic dirges are unimaginable without Jeremiah 9's meqonenot summons — and to Ezekiel's lament-oracles. The book's own famous "new covenant" passage (Jer 31:31–34) will recover the verb *yada* ("they shall all know me") that 9:3, 6 uses to mark the people's alienation, completing an arc the book itself sets up.

**Jewish trajectory.** Sirach 49:6–7 names Jeremiah explicitly: "they handled him cruelly, who had been consecrated a prophet from the womb." The Bavli (Bava Batra 14b–15a, Megillah 14b) attributes Jeremiah to Jeremiah and lists him among the prophets whose words were canonized. Targum Jonathan paraphrases the Hebrew in Aramaic and softens difficult metaphors. The chapter has a major liturgical home: Jer 9:22–23 (in Hebrew numbering, our 9:23–24) is the *haftarah* for the Ninth of Av in some traditions and recurs in Tisha B'Av lamentation. The medieval commentators read closely: Rashi glosses *meqonenot* with reference to the contemporary Jewish mourning practice of his day; Radak (David Kimhi) emphasizes the prophet's grief as covenantal fidelity; Ibn Ezra defends the boasting verse as wisdom-tradition; Abrabanel reads the chapter politically, fitting it to the late monarchy's diplomatic crises. Pesikta Rabbati and the Yalkut bring Jer 9 into Tisha B'Av memory, fusing the chapter with Lamentations into a single liturgical mourning.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic: **Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah*** (the 14 Greek homilies surviving from c. 240, plus the Latin homilies translated by Jerome) read the prophet typologically — Jeremiah's grief prefigures Christ's tears over Jerusalem (Lk 19:41), the boasting verse anticipates Pauline theology, and the mourning women are figures of the church's lament for the unfaithful. **Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah*** (incomplete at his death in 420) treats the chapter philologically with reference to the Hebrew, defending the LXX where it diverges. Medieval reception — Rupert of Deutz, Aquinas (citing Jeremiah extensively in the Summa on the prophetic office, II-II.171–174). Reformation: **Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah* (1563, his largest OT work, four folio volumes)** reads Jer 9 as testimony to the depth of human depravity and the prophetic call to lament alongside Yahweh. Luther preached on the chapter in his Jeremiah lectures. Critical: Duhm, Mowinckel, Skinner, Holladay, Carroll. Contemporary: Lundbom (Anchor), Brueggemann (*Theology of the OT*; the Jeremiah commentary in 2 vols.), **Kathleen O'Connor (*Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*, 2011)** reading the prophet as a witness to disaster who refuses premature consolation, Christopher J.H. Wright on the ethical-political dimension.

**Where Jewish and Christian readings diverge.** Christian tradition reads vv. 23–24 forward to Paul's *en kyrioi kauchasthai* in 1 Cor 1:31 and 2 Cor 10:17 ("let him who boasts, boast in the Lord") and finds in it a foundational text for theology of grace against works-righteousness. Jewish tradition reads the same verse as the deepening of Mosaic *teshuvah*: the boast in *daat* of Yahweh's hesed-mishpat-tzedaqah is the *halakhic* life rightly lived. The Christian Christological reading of vv. 1–6 — Jeremiah's tears as type of Christ's — has no Jewish counterpart; Jewish reception locates the tears entirely in covenantal grief and Tisha B'Av memory. Critical scholarship reads both traditions as theologically determined retrojections and locates the chapter's meaning in late-monarchy Judean experience.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Whose tears are these?** The chapter opens with weeping that is grammatically the prophet's — "Oh that my head were waters" — but the speaker shifts within the chapter without warning. By v. 10 it is Yahweh who takes up "weeping and wailing for the mountains." The classical Jewish and Christian interpretive question: does Jeremiah's grief disclose Yahweh's *own* grief, or is it Jeremiah's emotional response *over against* a sovereign God who remains impassible? Abraham Joshua Heschel's *The Prophets* (1962) gave the modern paradigmatic answer — the prophet shares God's pathos; the prophet's tears *are* God's tears made audible. Eastern Orthodox tradition has long sustained a *theopaschite* sympathy theology, in which God genuinely suffers with the people through the incarnate Word. Catholic tradition, especially since Jürgen Moltmann's *The Crucified God* (1972, though Moltmann is a Protestant), has substantially absorbed this view, against the older scholastic doctrine of divine impassibility (*Deus impassibilis*), which Aquinas defends in *Summa* I.9 and which Calvin defends in *Institutes* 1.13.1: God uses anthropomorphic language as accommodation to creaturely capacity, not as disclosure of divine emotion.

The Reformed impassibility reading deserves a fair hearing. If God genuinely *grieves* in the chronic, exhausted way Jer 9:1 implies, then either God knew this would happen (and grieves performatively, which sounds like bad faith) or did not know (which sacrifices omniscience to preserve pathos). Calvin's accommodation theory cuts the knot: Yahweh speaks in human emotional terms because we cannot understand otherwise, but the underlying divine reality is the unmoved love that *governs* the grief without being subject to it. Brueggemann pushes the other way: "the prophet does not perform grief on God's behalf; the prophet is the medium through which God's grief becomes audible." Open theism (Pinnock, Sanders, Boyd) goes further still — Yahweh genuinely does not control the future and genuinely suffers loss.

Where this leaves us: the impassibility reading is the more coherent metaphysics but the less honest reading of Jeremiah 9's grammar. The chapter's pronouns refuse to stay still. Whether we resolve that into accommodation or into divine pathos may be the most important christological-anthropological question the prophets force.

**Tension 2: The boasting verse and Pauline reception.** Jer 9:23–24 is the source-text for 1 Cor 1:31 and 2 Cor 10:17, both quoting Jeremiah explicitly: *ho kauchomenos en kyrioi kauchastho*. The interpretive question is what Paul thinks the Jeremiah text *does*. The traditional Christian reading (Augustine through Luther): the verse abrogates Jewish ethnic privilege — wisdom, might, and riches stand for *kauchema kata sarka*, "boasting according to the flesh," which for Paul includes circumcision, Torah-observance, and Israelite descent (Phil 3:4–6). The text underwrites the Reformation's *sola gratia*: no works-boasting, only Christ-boasting.

The "new perspective on Paul" — E.P. Sanders (*Paul and Palestinian Judaism*, 1977), James Dunn, N.T. Wright (*Justification*, 2009) — has substantially revised this reading. Krister Stendahl had argued already in 1963 that Paul's "boasting" critique is not against works-righteousness in the Lutheran sense but against ethnic-boundary maintenance. Daniel Boyarin (*A Radical Jew*, 1994) reads Paul as critiquing the Jewish particularism while remaining a Jewish thinker. On this reading, Jer 9:23–24 is *deepened* in Paul, not abrogated: knowing Yahweh's hesed-mishpat-tzedaqah is now extended to Gentiles, and the boast in the Lord is the boast of those incorporated into covenant by faith.

The supersessionist reading should be steelmanned: Paul *does* contrast boasting in flesh with boasting in the Lord, and the Jer 9:25–26 coda about uncircumcised hearts is itself a critique of unredeemed Israel from inside the canon. Paul is reading the Hebrew Bible against its own covenant identity, and the boasting verse is one of his pillars. The new-perspective reading should be steelmanned in turn: Paul never says circumcision is bad, only that it is not the boundary of belonging; he says "circumcision is nothing, and uncircumcision is nothing, but keeping the commandments of God" (1 Cor 7:19), which is closer to deepening than abrogating Jer 9:25–26.

Where this leaves us: Paul reads Jer 9:23–24 as foundational and reads Jer 9:25–26 as already pointing toward an inward-circumcised Israel that is not coextensive with ethnic Israel. Whether this is supersessionist or covenant-deepening probably depends on whether one believes Romans 11 means what it says about all Israel being saved.

The unresolved question: if Yahweh's grief is genuine in Jer 9, then the boasting verse — *boast in this, that you understand and know me* — invites the boaster into a knowing that includes shared grief. To know Yahweh is, on this reading, to weep with Yahweh over the slain of the daughter of one's people. But what kind of religion has *grief over judgment* as its central identity claim? Not the religion of consolation we usually preach.

## The Thread

Deuteronomy 28:36–37, 64–68 — the Mosaic curse-catalogue of exile, sword, and scattering, which Jer 9:15–16 explicitly activates: "I will scatter them among the nations." The covenant is not breaking; it is doing exactly what it warned. Lamentations in its entirety — the alphabetic dirges traditionally ascribed to Jeremiah on the strength of 9:1 — should be read alongside this chapter as the same voice in the next register. 1 Corinthians 1:31 and 2 Corinthians 10:17, where Paul cites Jer 9:23–24 as the foundation of his boasting theology, with Romans 2:28–29 as the Pauline gloss on Jer 9:25–26's heart-circumcision. Hebrews 8:8–12 quotes Jer 31:31–34 in full as the locus classicus of the new covenant, and Hebrews's argument is unintelligible without the alienation-from-knowing that Jer 9 establishes. Forward also to Lamentations 1–2, where the *meqonenot* of Jer 9:17 finally take the stage and sing — and to the Tisha B'Av liturgy, where the chapter is read every year as Jewish memory of the same wound.
