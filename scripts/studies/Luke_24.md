# Luke 24: Resurrection, Emmaus, and the Opened Mind

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The empty tomb of Luke 24 stood, on the strongest archaeological consensus, on the site now occupied by the **Church of the Holy Sepulchre**. First-century Jerusalem tombs of the kind Luke describes — *kokhim* (loculi) cut into limestone, with rolling-stone closures — have been excavated by the dozens around the city; the **Garden Tomb** site north of Damascus Gate, by contrast, has Iron Age tombs centuries too early. The 1965 excavations beneath the Holy Sepulchre by **Virgilio Corbo** and **Charles Coüasnon** confirmed the site as a first-century quarry with hewn tombs, with the rock now enclosed in the Edicule. The 2016–2017 restoration by Antonia Moropoulou's team at the National Technical University of Athens removed the marble cladding and exposed the limestone burial slab; mortar samples were dated by optically stimulated luminescence to approximately 345 CE, consistent with the construction date of Constantine's original basilica. The continuity of the site's veneration from the early Jewish-Christian community through Hadrian's deliberate desecration (a Venus temple was built over it in the 130s, possibly preserving the location through the very effort to obliterate it) and into the fourth-century basilica is one of the strongest cases in biblical archaeology for a single venerated site.

The **rolling stone** of 24:2 has clear archaeological parallels. Most first-century Jerusalem tombs used square plug-stones (*golel*); rolling discoid stones are rarer and tend to be associated with wealthier burials. The Tomb of the Kings (actually the first-century tomb of Queen Helena of Adiabene), the so-called Tomb of Herod's Family, and the Herodian tomb at Hyrcania all have rolling stones, and Joseph of Arimathea's tomb — described as "cut in stone, where no one had ever yet been laid" (23:53) — fits the wealthy-Jerusalemite profile.

The **Emmaus** of 24:13 is the most contested geographical identification in the Gospels. Luke gives the distance as ***hexēkonta stadia*** (sixty stadia, about seven miles or eleven kilometers) from Jerusalem. Four candidates have serious advocates. **Imwas / Nicopolis**, eighteen miles west of Jerusalem on the road to the coast — the Byzantine and patristic identification, attested by Eusebius's *Onomasticon* and Jerome — is too far for Luke's stadia, though some Western manuscripts of Luke 24:13 read 160 stadia (about 19 miles), which would fit. **Qiryat-Anavim** (Abu Ghosh), about seven miles west of Jerusalem on the Roman road to Joppa, was identified as Emmaus by the Crusaders, who built a substantial church on the site that still stands. **El-Qubeibeh**, about seven miles northwest of Jerusalem, was identified by the Franciscans in the medieval period and excavated by **Bellarmino Bagatti**, who found a Roman-era village with first-century pottery. **Motza** (the Hebrew Bible's Mozah, Joshua 18:26), about three miles west of Jerusalem, has been advocated by **Carsten Thiede** and others. **Joel Green** (NICNT), **Joseph Fitzmyer** (Anchor Bible), and most modern commentators conclude that the geographical uncertainty is genuine and may itself be thematically significant: Luke's Emmaus is a humble, undistinguished village whose name has been almost lost to history, which suits the theological point that the risen Christ meets disciples not at the temple or in glory but on the dusty road home.

**Bethany** (24:50), where the ascension takes place, is well-identified at modern al-Eizariya on the eastern slope of the Mount of Olives. The tomb of Lazarus — preserved as a venerated site since at least the fourth century — and the medieval Church of Lazarus (now succeeded by the Franciscan Church of St. Lazarus, 1953) mark the village.

Greek terms central to the chapter: ***ouk estin hōde alla ēgerthē*** (οὐκ ἔστιν ὧδε ἀλλὰ ἠγέρθη, "he is not here, he has risen," 24:6). ***Lēros*** (λῆρος, "idle tale / nonsense," 24:11) — the apostles' dismissive verdict on the women's report. ***Ophthalmoi autōn ekratounto*** (ὀφθαλμοὶ αὐτῶν ἐκρατοῦντο, "their eyes were kept," 24:16) and the inverse ***diēnoichthēsan oi ophthalmoi autōn*** (διηνοίχθησαν οἱ ὀφθαλμοὶ αὐτῶν, "their eyes were opened," 24:31). ***Diērmēneuen*** (διηρμήνευεν, "he interpreted," 24:27). ***Pneuma sarka kai ostea ouk echei*** (πνεῦμα σάρκα καὶ ὀστέα οὐκ ἔχει, "a spirit does not have flesh and bones," 24:39). ***Anepheri eis ton ouranon*** (ἀνεφέρετο εἰς τὸν οὐρανόν, "he was carried up into heaven," 24:51).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Luke 24 is structured as four scenes — the empty tomb (1–12), Emmaus (13–35), Jerusalem appearance (36–49), and ascension (50–53) — that together form the most theologically dense resurrection narrative in the canonical Gospels. Each scene is governed by a Lukan pattern of failed-then-opened recognition: the women remember, the Emmaus disciples have their eyes opened, the eleven are convinced by physical evidence, and finally Jesus "opens their minds to understand the Scriptures" (24:45).

**The empty tomb (24:1–12).** Luke's account features Lukan distinctives at three points. First, the angelic figures are "**two men in dazzling apparel**" (24:4) — Luke alone has two (Mark has one young man, Matthew one angel). The doubling parallels the two men at the ascension (Acts 1:10) and at the transfiguration (9:30, Moses and Elijah), constructing a Lukan-Acts angelological pattern. Second, the women named in 24:10 are **Mary Magdalene, Joanna, and Mary the mother of James** — a list distinctive in including Joanna (cf. 8:3, "Joanna the wife of Chuza, Herod's household manager"), connecting the resurrection witness back to the women who had funded Jesus's ministry. Third, the apostles' dismissal of the women's testimony as ***lēros*** (24:11) is uniquely Lukan. **Schüssler Fiorenza** in *In Memory of Her* and **Turid Karlsen Seim** in *The Double Message* read this Lukan candor as both honoring and complicating the Lukan portrayal of women: Luke does not erase the women's primary witness, but he records the male disciples' refusal to accept it. The Mishnaic and Josephan record (Josephus, *Antiquities* 4.219) that women's testimony was inadmissible in court provides the cultural background — and makes the historical-apologetic argument advanced by **N.T. Wright** in *Resurrection of the Son of God* and **Richard Bauckham** in *Gospel Women* striking: no first-century author fabricating a resurrection account would have placed women as primary witnesses. Their presence in all four Gospels is among the strongest reasons to take the empty-tomb tradition as historically rooted rather than fabricated.

**The Emmaus road (24:13–35)** is the most theologically rich resurrection narrative in the New Testament. It is uniquely Lukan and has been called the "fifth Gospel within the Gospel" (Bovon). The structure is exquisite: a slow build of misrecognition (the disciples explain the events to the unrecognized Jesus), the Christological reframe (Jesus reinterprets the events through the Scriptures), the hospitality (the disciples urge the stranger to stay), and the recognition in the breaking of bread. **Cleopas** is named (24:18) — the only one of the two disciples identified. **Richard Bauckham** in *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* (2006) argues that named figures in the Gospel resurrection narratives are best explained as eyewitness sources whom the early church knew by name; Cleopas may be the same as Clopas in John 19:25, husband of one of the Marys at the cross, and possibly the brother of Joseph (Hegesippus, in Eusebius *Ecclesiastical History* 3.11; 4.22.4, identifies Clopas as the brother of Joseph and uncle of Jesus). The other disciple's name is unknown — perhaps his wife (the Mary of John 19:25), perhaps a male companion, perhaps deliberately left open as an invitation to identification.

The Christological hermeneutic of 24:25–27 is **programmatic Lukan-Acts theology**: "**O foolish ones, and slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken! Was it not necessary that the Christ should suffer these things and enter into his glory? And beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself.**" Luke is asserting that the entire Old Testament — Torah, Prophets, and (implicitly, completed at 24:44) Psalms — is to be read Christologically. **Christopher Seitz** in *Figured Out: Typology and Providence in Christian Scripture* and **Richard Hays** in *Echoes of Scripture in the Gospels* and *Reading Backwards* both treat Luke 24 as the foundational warrant for figural-Christological reading of the Old Testament. **Brevard Childs** in *Biblical Theology of the Old and New Testaments* makes Luke 24:27, 44 the hermeneutical anchor for canonical biblical theology.

The **breaking of bread** moment (24:30–31) — "when he was at table with them, he took the bread and blessed and broke it and gave it to them. And their eyes were opened, and they recognized him" — uses the same fourfold liturgical formula as the Last Supper (22:19): *took, blessed, broke, gave*. The Eucharistic typology is unmistakable. **Augustine** in *Sermon* 234 and *Tractate on John* 26 made the Emmaus recognition the foundational image for Eucharistic encounter: "*nos quoque in panis fractione cognoscimus Dominum*" ("we too recognize the Lord in the breaking of bread"). **Aquinas**'s Eucharistic hymns (*Pange lingua*, *Adoro te devote*) draw on this typology. **Joseph Ratzinger / Benedict XVI**'s *Sacramentum Caritatis* and *Jesus of Nazareth: Holy Week* read Emmaus as the foundational pattern of Christian liturgy: word followed by sacrament, opened Scripture followed by opened eyes.

**The Jerusalem appearance (24:36–49)** features the strongest emphasis on **bodily resurrection** in the New Testament. Jesus eats fish (24:42–43), shows hands and feet (24:39–40), and explicitly states "**a spirit does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have**." Against any docetic, vision-only, or "spiritual-resurrection" reading, Luke insists on the embodied continuity of the risen Christ. **N.T. Wright**'s *The Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003), 817 pages of sustained argument, treats this scene as central to the case that Christian resurrection-faith is not a metaphor but a claim about a genuinely transformed but genuinely embodied risen one. The scriptural-hermeneutical climax — "**then he opened their minds to understand the Scriptures**" (24:45) — repeats and intensifies the Emmaus pattern. The **mission commission** of 24:47 — "repentance and forgiveness of sins should be proclaimed in his name to all nations, beginning from Jerusalem" — is programmatic for Acts; the geographical pattern (Jerusalem → Judea/Samaria → ends of the earth) is then explicit at Acts 1:8.

**The ascension (24:50–53)** is uniquely Lukan in the Gospels (the longer ending of Mark 16:19 is a second-century addition). Luke briefly narrates it here at Bethany — "lifting up his hands he blessed them. While he blessed them, he parted from them and was carried up into heaven" — and then re-narrates it more elaborately at Acts 1:6–11. The Lukan **temple inclusio** is theologically significant: the Gospel that opened with Zechariah in the temple (1:8–23) closes with the disciples "continually in the temple blessing God" (24:53). The temple-frame is Lukan-distinctive and underscores the continuity of the Christian movement with Israel's worship.

A textual note: 24:51's "and was carried up into heaven" is **textually disputed**. The phrase is omitted in the original hand of Sinaiticus and Codex Bezae and a few Old Latin witnesses; it is included in P75, Vaticanus, the corrector of Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus, and the majority. The omission would leave the Lukan ascension implicit at this point (with the explicit narration only at Acts 1). **Metzger**'s *Textual Commentary* gave the inclusion an "A" rating; modern critical editions print the words.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The resurrection narratives draw on multiple Old Testament textures. The "third day" pattern (24:7, 21, 46) echoes Hosea 6:2 ("after two days he will revive us; on the third day he will raise us up") and may echo Jonah's three days (Jonah 1:17), which Jesus had earlier cited (11:29–32). The **bodily resurrection** itself stands in continuity with Jewish apocalyptic resurrection-hope developed especially in Daniel 12:1–3, Isaiah 25–27, and the inter-testamental literature (2 Maccabees 7; 1 Enoch 22, 51, 91, 102–104). **N.T. Wright**'s *Resurrection of the Son of God* traces this background exhaustively. **George Nickelsburg**'s *Resurrection, Immortality, and Eternal Life in Intertestamental Judaism* is the foundational study.

The Christological reading of the Old Testament asserted in 24:25–27, 44–47 is not unique to Luke but is most explicit there. Mark, Matthew, and John all use Old Testament citations Christologically; Paul (1 Cor 15:3–4, "according to the Scriptures") presupposes a Christological reading; but only Luke makes the hermeneutic itself the climactic teaching of the risen Christ. This Lukan emphasis became the foundation for two millennia of Christian Old Testament interpretation. **Henri de Lubac**'s monumental *Medieval Exegesis* (originally 1959–64, English translation 1998–2009) traces the Christological-figural tradition; **Hans-Georg Gadamer** in *Truth and Method* engages it philosophically; **Christopher Seitz**, **Brevard Childs**, **Richard Hays** carry it into contemporary theology.

Patristic readings of the Lukan resurrection are extensive. **Origen** (*Commentary on Matthew*; fragments on Luke; *De Principiis* 2.10) developed the Christological hermeneutic as the foundation for his entire allegorical method. **Athanasius** (*On the Incarnation* 26–32) drew on Luke 24's bodily emphasis to argue that the resurrection is the ground of the divinization of human nature. **Cyril of Alexandria** (*Commentary on Luke*, Homilies 154–157) argued from Luke 24 against any docetic Christology. **Augustine**'s sermons on the resurrection (especially *Sermons* 232–260) are the most extensive patristic engagement; *Sermon* 234 on Emmaus inaugurates the Eucharistic-recognition tradition; *City of God* 22.5–8 argues from the resurrection appearances to the credibility of bodily resurrection. **Aquinas** (*Summa* III q. 53–59; *Catena Aurea* on Luke 24) systematized the medieval consensus on the *qualitates* of the risen body: *impassibilitas, subtilitas, agilitas, claritas* — properties that explain how Jesus could pass through doors (subtility) yet be touched and eat fish (genuinely embodied).

The Reformation and modern engagement is rich. **Luther**'s sermons on Easter and the Emmaus disciples are the doctrinal foundation of his preaching theology — the Word that walks beside us on the road, opening the Scriptures, is the Word that comes to us in proclamation. **Calvin**'s *Harmony of the Evangelists* on Luke 24 is masterful at the bodily-resurrection question. **Karl Barth** (*Church Dogmatics* III/2 §47; IV/1 §59; IV/2 §64) treats the resurrection as the foundational event of revelation — God's verdict on the cross — and Luke 24 as central. **Jürgen Moltmann**'s *Theology of Hope* (1964) makes the resurrection the foundation of Christian eschatology. **Hans Urs von Balthasar**'s *Mysterium Paschale* treats Luke 24 (along with John 20–21) as the climax of the dramatic-paschal arc. **Wolfhart Pannenberg**'s *Jesus — God and Man* (1968) makes the resurrection the historical-theological foundation of Christology. **Eugene Peterson**'s *Christ Plays in Ten Thousand Places* and *The Pastor* engage Emmaus as the foundational pattern of pastoral ministry.

Foundational resurrection-specific scholarship: **N.T. Wright**, *The Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003) — the most comprehensive contemporary defense of bodily resurrection. **Dale Allison**, *Resurrecting Jesus: The Earliest Christian Tradition and Its Interpreters* (2005) — sympathetic but more skeptical than Wright. **Markus Bockmuehl**'s essays on resurrection. **Gerd Lüdemann**, *The Resurrection of Jesus: History, Experience, Theology* (1994) — the most influential modern counter-reading, arguing the appearances are best explained as visionary-psychological experiences. **Pinchas Lapide**, *The Resurrection of Jesus: A Jewish Perspective* (1983) — striking in that an Orthodox Jew accepts the historicity of the resurrection while not accepting Jesus as Messiah.

Major Lukan commentators on chapter 24: **Joseph Fitzmyer** (Anchor Bible) on the Greek, redaction, and patristic reception. **Joel Green** (NICNT) is theologically rich, especially on the hermeneutical pattern. **John Nolland** (Word) is patient on the textual variants. **Darrell Bock** (BECNT) defends a conservative-evangelical reading. **I. Howard Marshall** (NIGTC) is philologically meticulous. **Luke Timothy Johnson** (Sacra Pagina) reads Luke 24 as the literary climax that opens onto Acts. **François Bovon** (Hermeneia) is invaluable on the *Wirkungsgeschichte*. **Robert Tannehill**'s *The Narrative Unity of Luke-Acts* shows how Luke 24 functions as the hinge.

Jewish readings: **Pinchas Lapide**'s acceptance of resurrection-historicity is striking. **David Flusser** read the resurrection narratives as broadly continuous with Pharisaic-apocalyptic resurrection-faith. **Geza Vermes** in *The Resurrection* and **Amy-Jill Levine** read the narratives as theologically generative even where they disagree on historicity. **Daniel Boyarin** in *The Jewish Gospels* engages the broader Jewish messianic-apocalyptic context.

Feminist readings: **Schüssler Fiorenza**'s *In Memory of Her* recovers the women as primary resurrection witnesses; **Turid Karlsen Seim**'s *The Double Message* engages the Lukan tension between honoring and marginalizing women's testimony; **Ben Witherington III**'s *Women and the Genesis of Christianity* and **Richard Bauckham**'s *Gospel Women* both develop the historical-apologetic reading.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: How "physical" is the resurrection body, and what is its relationship to the body that died?** A first position, the strong-physicalist reading defended by **N.T. Wright** in *Resurrection of the Son of God*, takes Luke 24:39–43 as decisive: the risen body is a transformed but genuinely physical body, the same body that was crucified, now with new properties (capable of passing through doors, of being unrecognized) but still with flesh and bones, capable of eating fish. The resurrection is not a metaphor for spiritual renewal or community ongoing; it is a claim about an empty tomb and a transformed corpse. A second position, the spiritual-resurrection reading developed by **Rudolf Bultmann**, **Willi Marxsen**, and **Gerd Lüdemann**, takes the bodily-emphasis passages as later apologetic accretions and the original resurrection-experience as visionary-psychological. The disciples' experience of "the Lord lives" was real and transforming; the empty-tomb tradition is a later objectification. A third position, the dialectical-symbolic reading developed in different ways by **Rowan Williams**, **Sarah Coakley**, and **John Polkinghorne**, holds bodily and spiritual together: the resurrection is a genuinely physical event but one that stands at the edge of natural-historical categories, an eschatological reality breaking into history rather than fitting comfortably within it. A fourth position, the early Christian-mainstream reading from **Athanasius** and **Aquinas** through **Calvin** and **Barth**, holds bodily resurrection as ontologically real but transformed: the *qualitates* of the risen body (impassibility, subtility, agility, clarity) make it the same yet different. Steelmanning each: Wright takes seriously Luke's explicit anti-docetic insistence and the empty tomb's centrality; Bultmann/Lüdemann take seriously the historical-critical difficulty of resurrection-as-public-event and the genuine variety of appearance-traditions; the dialectical reading takes seriously both poles; the patristic-medieval reading takes seriously the church's confessional tradition. *Where this leaves us:* Luke 24:39 is the most explicit anti-docetic statement in the New Testament, and the church's resurrection-faith has historically depended on its taking the empty tomb and the physical resurrection seriously. But the risen body is not the resuscitated body; it is the eschatological-transformed body, and the ways in which it differs from ordinary embodied life (passing through doors, vanishing, sometimes unrecognized) are themselves part of the narrative's witness. Resurrection is neither metaphor nor mere resuscitation; it is the inauguration of a new mode of embodied life.

**Tension 2: What does Jesus mean when he says "the Christ should suffer and on the third day rise"… is "necessary" (24:26, ***edei***)?** A first position, the classic substitutionary-atonement reading of much Reformed theology (Calvin, *Institutes* II.16; Charles Hodge; **John Stott**'s *The Cross of Christ*), reads the *necessity* as the necessity of God's justice meeting God's mercy: the cross is necessary because human sin requires a divine-substitutionary atonement. A second position, the Christus-Victor reading of **Gustaf Aulén**'s *Christus Victor* (1931) and many of the Greek Fathers, reads the necessity as the necessity of cosmic conflict: the cross is necessary as the means of victory over death, sin, and the demonic powers. A third position, the Anselmian satisfaction reading of *Cur Deus Homo*, reads it as the necessity of restoring God's honor and the moral order. A fourth position, the **scripture-fulfillment** reading suggested most directly by Luke's own context (24:25–27, 44–47), reads the necessity as the canonical-narrative necessity: Jesus must suffer and rise because this is what the Scriptures have said the Messiah must do, and God's faithfulness to Israel's prophets requires this fulfillment. A fifth position, **N.T. Wright**'s reading in *The Day the Revolution Began* (2016), holds these together: the cross is necessary as the climactic act of God's covenant faithfulness, the means by which Israel's exile is ended, the powers are defeated, and the new creation is inaugurated. Steelmanning: substitution takes seriously human sin and divine justice; Christus Victor takes seriously cosmic-spiritual conflict; satisfaction takes seriously the moral-order question; scripture-fulfillment takes seriously Luke's explicit framing; Wright's integration takes seriously the canonical narrative as a whole. *Where this leaves us:* Luke's own ***edei*** (it was necessary) is most directly grounded in his canonical-Christological hermeneutic rather than in any single atonement theory. The Lukan Christ argues from the Scriptures that the Messiah must suffer and rise, but he does not argue *why* the Scriptures said this. The deeper why is what the church's atonement theologies have tried to answer, and the variety of those theologies suggests that no single reading exhausts the meaning.

One unresolved question: if recognition of the risen Christ requires both Scripture and sacrament — both opened minds and broken bread — what does that mean for our knowing of God in any age? Emmaus suggests that the risen Lord is not coercively self-evident; he meets us through the texts of Israel and the meal of the church, and apart from those mediations our eyes remain "kept from recognizing him." The implications for natural theology, for evangelism, and for the place of the Eucharist in Christian formation are profound but not fully systematizable.

## The Thread

Luke 24 closes the temple inclusio that opened the Gospel: Zechariah serves in the temple (1:8–23), and the disciples are "continually in the temple blessing God" (24:53). The "third day" of 24:7, 21, 46 looks back to 9:22, 18:33, and 24:7, completing the pattern of passion-prediction. The "**clothed with power from on high**" of 24:49 reaches forward to Acts 1:8 and 2:1–4. The "beginning from Jerusalem" of 24:47 sets up the geographical structure of Acts 1:8 — Jerusalem, Judea and Samaria, the ends of the earth. The breaking of bread at Emmaus echoes the Last Supper (22:19) and reaches forward into Acts 2:42, 46 (the disciples "breaking bread in their homes") and 20:7, 11 (Paul at Troas). The two men in dazzling apparel at the tomb (24:4) are doubled by the two men at the ascension (Acts 1:10) — the angelological symmetry binds Gospel and Acts. And the ***paradidōmi*** that ran through the passion is summarized one last time at 24:7, 20: the Son of Man was "delivered into the hands of sinful men" — the verb completes its arc, and what was given over is given back, raised, opening Scripture, breaking bread, and ascending in blessing.
