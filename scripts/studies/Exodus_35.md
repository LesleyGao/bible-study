# Exodus 35: Whose Heart Stirred Him

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Exodus 35 pivots from theophany to workshop. Whatever dating framework one adopts, the chapter is about organizing a sanctuary-building project among a mobile people. The traditional Ramesside-era setting (thirteenth century BCE) lines up with a Late Bronze Age material culture well documented from Egypt, Canaan, and northern Syria. The critical tradition, following Wellhausen, assigns chapters 35-40 to the Priestly source (P) and dates the material substantially later, with Julius Wellhausen himself reading P as a post-exilic retrojection of Second Temple cultic order into the wilderness. Jacob Milgrom and Menahem Haran, working in different modes, have argued against a wholesale late dating: Milgrom sees the ritual grammar of P as preserving genuinely old priestly traditions; Haran's *Temples and Temple Service in Ancient Israel* argues the tabernacle description has too much First Temple architectural memory to be sheer invention. Israel Knohl's *The Sanctuary of Silence* distinguishes P proper from a later "Holiness School" (H), refining the picture further.

Workshop practice in the ancient Near East provides the material setting. Metalworkers in Late Bronze and Iron Age Syria-Palestine worked gold by hammering (cold-working), annealing over charcoal fires, and casting in clay and lost-wax molds. Silver and bronze followed similar techniques. The Uluburun shipwreck (off the Turkish coast, c. 1320 BCE, excavated by George Bass and Cemal Pulak) preserved the tool-kit of a Late Bronze Age long-distance trader: copper oxhide ingots, tin ingots, unworked glass ingots in cobalt and turquoise, raw ivory, and ostrich eggshells — precisely the material palette of Exodus 35's contribution list (metals, dyes, precious stones). Textile production occurred on warp-weighted looms, with dyes derived from murex shellfish (Tyrian purple) and from plant sources (madder red, woad blue). The "blue and purple and scarlet" yarns (*tĕkēlet*, *ʾargāmān*, *tôlaʿat šānî*) of vv. 6 and 23 correspond to known ANE dye technology; archaeological finds at Timna, Tel Shiqmona, and Tel Kedesh have recovered dye installations.

The chapter's detailed mention of women's spinning (vv. 25-26) is not decoration. Carol Meyers' *Discovering Eve: Ancient Israelite Women in Context* and *Households and Holiness* argue that spinning and weaving were the core female contributions to the domestic economy in Iron Age Israel and were strongly skilled labor — the noun *ḥakmat-lēb* ("wise of heart," v. 25) is technical. The text names specifically women who spun goats' hair (v. 26), a coarser fiber requiring different handling than wool or linen; the distinction is artisanally real. The Timna tent-shrine excavated by Beno Rothenberg in the Arabah (fourteenth-twelfth centuries BCE) preserved a small Midianite-era shrine with copper serpent and textile remains — the closest physical analog to a portable Bronze Age desert sanctuary. Yohanan Aharoni's excavation at Arad uncovered a Judahite sanctuary with altar, ante-chamber, and holy-of-holies, structurally paralleling tabernacle layout.

Authorship and dating follow the P-source framework. Chapters 35-40 are the execution narrative that parallels the instruction narrative of Exodus 25-31, with deliberate repetition and structural mirroring. Childs' *Exodus: A Commentary* treats the two halves as a literary unity whose doubling is theologically intentional, whatever source history one posits. John Durham's WBC volume and Cornelis Houtman's Kok Pharos commentary provide comprehensive historical-critical and literary readings. The LXX ordering for chapters 35-40 diverges substantially from the MT — a fact documented by John W. Wevers' *Notes on the Greek Text of Exodus* and Anneli Aejmelaeus' work on the Greek Pentateuch — with the LXX material both shorter and reorganized. Whether the LXX reflects an older Hebrew Vorlage or represents translator initiative is one of the classic textual problems in Exodus.

The economy of voluntary offering (*nĕdābâ*, cf. *tĕrûmat YHWH* in vv. 5, 21, 24, 29) has its own ANE context. Temple endowments in Mesopotamia were typically royal or taxation-driven; the Sumerian and Akkadian vocabulary for temple provisioning features obligatory contributions, royal grants, and tithe-like *sattukku* offerings. Voluntary, general-public contribution on the scale Exodus 35 describes is comparatively unusual in the ANE record and is theologically weighted.

Key Hebrew terms: *qāhāl* (קָהָל) — "assembly, congregation" (v. 1); *nĕdîb-lēb* (נְדִיב-לֵב) — "generous of heart" (v. 5); *nĕśāʾô libbô* (נְשָׂאוֹ לִבּוֹ) — "whose heart lifted him up" (v. 21); *tĕrûmâ* (תְּרוּמָה) — "contribution, uplift-offering" (v. 5); *ḥakmat-lēb* (חַכְמַת-לֵב) — "wise of heart," skilled (v. 25).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 35 is a public proclamation followed by a collective response. The structural skeleton is characteristic of P: Moses speaks the command (vv. 1-19), the people act (vv. 20-29), and named artisans are designated (vv. 30-35). The command itself is bracketed with the Sabbath (vv. 2-3) — a deliberate placement. The very long construction narrative will not begin until chapter 36; before it begins, the chapter asserts that work, even sacred work, is subordinated to rest. Balentine's *The Torah's Vision of Worship* reads the Sabbath bracketing as the theological hinge of the whole tabernacle account: worship must be structured so that it does not consume those who offer it. Fretheim's *Exodus* (Interpretation Commentary) concurs.

The literary strategy uses repetition with variation. "The LORD has commanded" (v. 1, v. 4, v. 10, v. 29) operates as an anaphoric refrain that will become the structural backbone of chapters 35-40 ("as the LORD commanded Moses" recurs some nineteen times across the construction narrative). Childs reads the refrain as the literary marker of fidelity: every element is not merely built but built-as-commanded. The doubling between instruction (chs. 25-31) and execution (chs. 35-40) is itself a literary device. Exodus 25-31 ends with the Sabbath (31:12-17); Exodus 35 begins with the Sabbath (vv. 1-3). Jon Levenson, in *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* and his work on temple theology, reads this symmetry as encoding a larger claim: the tabernacle is a microcosm, a built cosmos, and its completion in chapter 40 will match the language of Genesis 2's completion of creation.

The lexical specificity of the willing-heart terminology is worth attention. Three overlapping phrases are used: *nĕdîb-lēb* (v. 5), *nĕśāʾô libbô* (v. 21), and *nĕdābat libbô* (v. 29, literally "the freewill of his heart"). Each describes interior motion — the heart is "generous," "lifted up," "moved." The vocabulary shapes the contribution as precisely *not* extracted. Umberto Cassuto's *Commentary on Exodus* and Nahum Sarna's JPS volume both highlight the literary contrast with Exodus 32, where the golden calf's gold was demanded from the people with an imperative ("break off the rings of gold"). Here, against that foil, the gold comes because hearts move. Zornberg's *The Particulars of Rapture* makes the inversion a centerpiece: the calf's gold was reflexive and collective; the tabernacle's gold is interior and individuated.

The naming of Bezalel (v. 30) and Oholiab (v. 34) is also a literary move. Named craftsmen are unusual in ANE temple accounts, which tend to foreground the royal patron. Bezalel's tribe (Judah) and Oholiab's tribe (Dan) bracket the social spectrum — Judah the foremost, Dan one of the peripheral. Bezalel's genealogy "son of Uri, son of Hur" grounds him in the leadership stratum already named at Exodus 17:10 and 24:14. The verb *milleʾ* (v. 31) — "filled" with the Spirit of God — is priestly-consecratory vocabulary used for ordination; here it is used for craft. The movement is striking: Spirit-filling for artisanship, not only for prophecy or priestly office. V. 34's note that God "inspired him to teach" embeds pedagogy into the craft from the start.

Textual variants are substantial. The LXX order for 35-40 differs from MT, as noted. Verse 11's list of tabernacle components is shorter in LXX than MT. These variants illustrate the fluidity of the tabernacle description in its early transmission — a reminder that the "completed" P narrative was itself still in literary motion well into the Hellenistic period.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** Second Temple sources attend to the chapter. Philo's *Life of Moses* 2.71-108 treats the tabernacle construction as an allegorical embodiment of the cosmos. Josephus, *Antiquities* 3.102ff, narrates the workmanship. 4Q364-367 (4QReworked Pentateuch) and 4Q158 rework the construction sections. Ben Sira 45 praises the priestly craftsmanship that follows in Aaron's wake.

Rabbinic tradition reads the voluntary contribution as the counter-narrative to the golden calf. Exodus Rabbah 48 and 49 develop an extensive midrashic comparison: in the calf episode, the people rushed to give; in the tabernacle, they also rushed — the impulse was the same, but the object had changed. The midrash treats the contribution as teshuvah in action. The Sabbath command that opens the chapter is the text from which the rabbis derive the categories of forbidden labor (*avot melakhot* — Shabbat 49b, 73a): the thirty-nine categories of work are enumerated from the labor needed to build the tabernacle, the juxtaposition in Exodus 35 making the inference: whatever was needed to build the tabernacle is precisely what must cease on the Sabbath. This is the rabbinic warrant for one of the most elaborated bodies of Jewish law.

The fire prohibition of v. 3 became a historic sectarian dividing line. Karaite Jews read the prohibition absolutely — no fire may burn in a Jewish home on Shabbat, including pre-lit fire. Rabbinic tradition, reading this verse against Ex 16:23, permits fire lit before Shabbat to continue burning (hence the Sabbath candles of Friday night). The Karaite-Rabbinic split over this verse is one of the sharpest halakhic divergences in Jewish history.

Medieval commentators layer the reading. Rashi (v. 2) ties the capital-punishment clause to a broader halakhic apparatus. Ibn Ezra works grammatically. Ramban reads the chapter's placement after the calf as theologically decisive: the tabernacle exists because the calf failed, and the materials and labor are Israel's *correction*. Maimonides' *Hilchot Shabbat* systematizes the derived prohibitions. Maimonides' *Hilchot Klei HaMikdash* and *Hilchot Tefillah* read the artisan tradition as permanent. Judah Halevi's *Kuzari* treats the chapter as evidence of Israel's covenantal distinctiveness.

Moderns extend the reading. Nahum Sarna in the JPS Commentary foregrounds the free-will offering. Heschel's *The Sabbath* uses the Sabbath-first placement in Exodus 35 as his exegetical keystone: the tabernacle in space is flanked by the tabernacle in time. Jonathan Sacks, in *Covenant and Conversation*, reads the chapter as the theology of shared labor in community. Jon Levenson and Carol Meyers draw the material-culture picture tight. Michael Fishbane's *Biblical Interpretation in Ancient Israel* traces the intertextual work the chapter performs within later biblical literature.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** Patristic readings allegorize the contribution and the craftsmanship. Origen's *Homilies on Exodus* 13 reads the materials as virtues, the artisans as spiritual teachers. Gregory of Nyssa treats the tabernacle construction as the architecture of the soul. Augustine's *Questions on the Heptateuch* on this chapter and the *City of God* references handle the material soberly. Bede's commentary *On the Tabernacle* offers a verse-by-verse spiritual reading that dominated medieval monastic reception.

Medieval scholasticism handles the chapter primarily in relation to the moral law/ceremonial law grid. Aquinas (*ST* I-II q. 101-103) explores the purpose of ceremonial law in the old dispensation, citing the tabernacle construction as instrumentum of worship that foreshadows Christ's body as the true temple (with John 2:19-21 as hermeneutical key). Bonaventure develops the allegorical reading.

The Reformation divides over the chapter's implications for worship. Luther is comparatively light-handed, treating the tabernacle as preparatory and abrogated. Calvin's *Commentary on Exodus* reads the chapter as establishing two principles that will become Reformed constants: first, that worship must follow divine command precisely (the regulative principle — later systematized by the Westminster Divines); second, that free, willing-hearted giving is the proper posture for sacred work. The tension inside Calvinism between these two — strict form and willing-hearted freedom — structures much Reformed debate about worship from the seventeenth century forward.

Critical biblical scholarship treats the chapter as primary evidence for P-source theology. Wellhausen's hypothesis placed 35-40 at the heart of exilic priestly retrojection; Noth, von Rad, and Frank Moore Cross refined the picture. Brevard Childs' *Exodus* reads the doubling of instruction and execution as canonical theology in its own right. Terence Fretheim reads the chapter as theology of human participation in divine creation. Thomas Dozeman's *Commentary on Exodus* (Eerdmans Critical Commentary) lays out the source-critical picture in detail. Samuel Balentine's *The Torah's Vision of Worship* treats chapters 35-40 as the Torah's architectural theology of worship — not merely regulations but a vision. Walter Brueggemann's *Theology of the Old Testament* reads P's tabernacle theology as a construction of order against the chaos of exile. Christopher Wright integrates the ethical implications missionally.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Convergence: both traditions read Exodus 35 as a foundational text for the theology of worship — its material, its voluntariness, its structured craft. Both agree that Sabbath framing the construction work is theologically significant. Both agree that willing hearts are constitutive, not optional. Divergence: Jewish tradition reads the chapter as foundational halakhic text for Sabbath labor prohibition and for the *permanent* priestly ritual infrastructure. Christian tradition reads the same chapter as typological — the tabernacle is preparatory to the body of Christ as true temple (John 2, Heb 9-10). The Reformation added an internal Christian divergence: Calvin's regulative principle (only what is commanded is permitted in worship) versus the Lutheran, Catholic, and Orthodox normative-principle traditions (what is not forbidden is permitted).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: voluntary offering and commanded obligation.** The chapter mixes two categories that look opposed. The contribution is a freewill gift of the willing heart (vv. 5, 21, 22, 29); it is also what "the LORD has commanded" (vv. 1, 4, 10, 29). Is the gift free or required? How do the traditions hold both?

- *Jewish reading (Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Matnot Aniyim)*: the gift is doubly real — commanded in frame but free in substance. God commands that an offering be brought; the individual's heart determines the specific contribution. The *obligation to give* is mandatory; the *willingness to give* is the measure of the offering's acceptability. Both categories are preserved.
- *Catholic reading*: Aquinas (*ST* II-II qq. 80-87) on religion as virtue treats worship as owed to God in strict justice and as given in love. The framing of obligation does not eliminate freedom; free cooperation with grace is itself the form of the fulfilled obligation. CCC 2581-2583 reads Moses' ministry as the pattern.
- *Reformed reading*: Calvin reads the regulative principle (worship must follow divine command) as protective, not restrictive. Within the form God commands, the *willingness of the heart* is what God accepts. Michael Horton's *Pilgrim Theology* echoes this: God's command structures the possibility of genuine gift.
- *Lutheran reading*: Luther distinguishes sharply between the works of love offered out of gratitude (post-gospel) and works performed under compulsion (law-driven). The Exodus 35 contribution is a paradigm case of gratitude-driven labor — Israel after the calf is forgiven Israel, not extorted Israel.
- *Wesleyan reading*: Wesley's sermons on sanctification read willing-heartedness as evidence of the sanctified will — the heart conformed to God no longer feels obligation as imposition. Wesleyan holiness tradition makes Exodus 35 a template for the mature Christian life.
- *Orthodox reading*: Lossky and Schmemann read the chapter's coexistence of command and free offering as deeply ecclesial — the Eucharistic offering is both commanded and freely offered; the liturgy does not suppress but structures freedom.
- *Critical reading*: Balentine and Fretheim treat the coexistence as a theological achievement of the P writer, not a tension to be resolved. Obligation and voluntariness are co-constitutive in covenantal worship.

*Steelman the least intuitive — Calvin's regulative principle.* To non-Reformed ears the principle sounds restrictive and even legalistic — only what is commanded may be done in worship. But Calvin's rationale is anti-priestly in a specific sense: without the regulative principle, worship drifts into what pleases the worshipers or aggrandizes the clergy, and the willingness of the heart is coopted. The regulative principle was in its historical context a democratizing move that protected common worshipers from the aesthetic and financial demands of clerical innovation, by binding the form to what the text commands and freeing the heart within it.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter will not let us separate freedom and form. Traditions that emphasize one without the other distort the text. The willing heart contributes within the commanded frame; outside the frame there is sentiment without worship, and inside the frame without the heart there is worship without offering.

**Tension 2: worship and freedom in form — the regulative principle debate.** Related but distinct from the first tension: does worship follow only what Scripture commands, or what Scripture does not forbid?

- *Reformed regulative principle (Calvin, Westminster Confession 21)*: worship includes only elements Scripture commands. Exodus 35 establishes this: Bezalel makes exactly what is commanded, no more. Elements not commanded — candles, incense (outside the prescribed), images — are excluded.
- *Normative principle (Lutheran, Catholic, Anglican)*: worship may include anything Scripture does not forbid. Exodus 35 establishes a floor (what must be done) but not a ceiling. The New Testament church has liberty within the Spirit's guidance.
- *Orthodox reading*: tradition develops organically under the Spirit; liturgy is received, not constructed. The tabernacle's detail establishes that God cares about worship's form; subsequent ecclesial liturgy is the analogous form of the new covenant.
- *Jewish reading*: halakhic development (the *takkanot* of the rabbis) follows an organic analog to the normative principle within a robustly commanded core. The Sabbath labor categories derived from the tabernacle are commanded; the precise expression varies across communities.
- *Critical reading (Balentine)*: the text itself does not adjudicate later sectarian debates; the tabernacle worship theology is *vision*, and later traditions construct different theologies of worship from that vision.

*Steelman the least intuitive — the Orthodox received liturgy.* To Reformed ears, the Orthodox liturgy looks like a monumental accretion unauthorized by Scripture. But the Orthodox claim is subtler: the tabernacle's form was received by Moses from God, not invented; the Church has received its liturgy likewise, through apostolic and patristic transmission under the Spirit. The continuity between tabernacle-receiving and liturgy-receiving is the theological warrant, not an argument from silence or from general freedom.

**Where this leaves us:** Exodus 35 establishes that God cares about worship's form and that form is given, not invented. Where traditions differ is on whether the tabernacle's form is a unique, closed template whose new-covenant analog is the Word alone, or a pattern of received-and-transmitted liturgy whose analog is the ongoing worship of the Church.

*Unresolved:* If the willingness of the heart is what the chapter repeatedly emphasizes, why does it open with the death penalty for Sabbath violation (v. 2)? The severity sits uneasily with the voluntariness that dominates the rest of the chapter. Is the Sabbath the one place where the willing heart *must* coincide with the commanded form, on pain of death — and if so, what does that teach about the rest?

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references.

First: 1 Chronicles 29:1-9. David's preparation for the Temple construction. The lexical and thematic echo of Exodus 35 is direct: David commands, the leaders and people give willingly (*nĕdābâ*, v. 5, v. 6, v. 9, v. 14, v. 17), David rejoices that "with a whole heart they have freely offered." The Chronicler is rewriting Exodus 35 into Solomonic prehistory, making the Temple the explicit sequel of the tabernacle's voluntary economy.

Second: 2 Corinthians 8-9. Paul's collection for the Jerusalem poor. "Each one must give as he has decided in his heart, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver" (9:7). The vocabulary of willing-hearted giving is Exodus 35 translated into Greek. Paul is reading the chapter as his warrant for voluntary contribution in the new covenant, not as abrogated by it.

Third: Genesis 2:2-3. The Sabbath-bracketing of the tabernacle construction (Exodus 31:12-17 closes instruction; Exodus 35:1-3 opens execution) deliberately echoes God's own cessation of creative work on the seventh day. Levenson's *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* reads the tabernacle as microcosm, a "built cosmos," its completion paralleling creation's. The link runs through the Sabbath: human sacred-building work, like divine creative work, is measured by its rest.
