# Isaiah 66 — Go Deeper

## The Thread

Isaiah 66 is the last chapter of one of the most consequential books in human religious history. It closes Isaiah, and it closes hard. The chapter contains the strongest maternal-divine image in the Hebrew Bible — *as one whom his mother comforts, so I will comfort you* — and one of the harshest final lines in the canon: *they shall go out and look on the dead bodies of the men who have rebelled against me; for their worm shall not die, their fire shall not be quenched*.

Both of these are Isaiah's gift to the rest of scripture. The maternal-comfort image has shaped Christian and Jewish theology of God's tenderness for two and a half thousand years. The worm-and-fire image is the principal Old Testament source of Jesus's *Gehenna* teaching, and through Mark 9:48 has shaped the entire Christian doctrine of hell.

The chapter also contains an anti-temple oracle that Stephen quotes on his way to martyrdom, a vision of the universal-mission to Tarshish and Lud and the coastlands, the staggering claim that Yahweh will take Gentiles *for priests and Levites*, and the new-heavens-and-new-earth promise carried over from chapter 65. The Jewish liturgical tradition does not end its public reading of Isaiah on 66:24; the synagogue goes back and re-reads 66:23 to avoid closing on the dark note. That liturgical decision is itself a piece of theology — a question about how the Hebrew Bible's ending should be heard.

This is where Isaiah ends. After sixty-six chapters that have moved from Assyrian crisis to Babylonian exile to post-exilic restoration to cosmic new-creation, the last word is the marriage of mother-comfort and unquenched-fire. We sit with the tension. We do not get to skip it.

## Lens 1 — Historical World

Isaiah 66 belongs to Third Isaiah (chs. 56-66). The mainstream-critical position since Bernhard Duhm's 1892 *Das Buch Jesaia* dates Trito-Isaiah to the late sixth or fifth century BC, post-exilic Yehud. The unified-authorship defense (J. Alec Motyer, John Oswalt) maintains the eighth-century Isaiah ben Amoz could prophesy across centuries; Brevard Childs's canonical approach honors the layered composition while reading the canonical-final-form as authoritative. The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa-a) preserves all 66 chapters as one document by 125 BC.

The mainstream-critical reading (Claus Westermann's *Isaiah 40-66*, Joseph Blenkinsopp's Anchor Yale *Isaiah 56-66*, Paul Hanson's *The Dawn of Apocalyptic*, John Goldingay, Jacob Stromberg, Patricia Tull) treats Isaiah 66 as the climactic chapter of Third Isaiah, gathering and concluding the major themes of the post-exilic prophetic vision. Hanson reads the chapter as the visionary-prophetic faction's final declaration against the hierocratic-priestly establishment; Stromberg's redactional analysis treats 66 as deliberately mirrored with chapter 1 to bracket the entire book.

Indeed the inclusio between Isaiah 1 and Isaiah 66 is striking. Chapter 1 began with: *the ox knows its owner... but Israel does not know* (1:3); a critique of empty sacrifice (*what to me is the multitude of your sacrifices? I have had enough*, 1:11-15); Sodom-and-Gomorrah comparison (1:9-10); the threat that rebels will be consumed (1:31). Chapter 66 ends with: a critique of empty sacrifice (66:3); Yahweh enthroned over heaven and earth questioning the temple (66:1); the universal-mission to all flesh (66:23); rebels consumed by unquenched fire (66:24). The book opens and closes on similar notes — Isaiah is a single literary structure however many hands shaped it.

The historical setting of chapter 66 is post-exilic Yehud, after the second temple was rebuilt (516 BC) but in a community where the rebuilt-temple had not delivered the promised glory. The opening anti-temple oracle (66:1-2) is sharp: *what is the house that you would build for me?* Some critical scholars (Westermann) read this as a polemic against the temple-building project itself — a Trito-Isaiah voice that questioned whether the Yehudite community should be focusing on temple-construction at all. Others (Blenkinsopp) read it as a polemic against an *attitude* toward the temple — against treating the rebuilt-house as if it could contain Yahweh.

The cultic-syncretism critique (66:3, 66:17) continues the polemic of chapter 65 — pig's flesh, abominations, mice, secret-garden rituals. The post-exilic community continued some of the religious-practices that Deuteronomistic reformers had tried to eliminate.

The maternal-divine image of 66:13 uses Hebrew *ke-ish asher immo tenachamenu ken anokhi anachemkhem* (כְּאִישׁ אֲשֶׁר אִמּוֹ תְּנַחֲמֶנּוּ כֵּן אָנֹכִי אֲנַחֶמְכֶם) — *as a man whom his mother comforts, so I myself will comfort you*. The simile is precise: God comforts as a mother comforts. This is not God *as* mother (though chapter 49:15 — *can a woman forget her nursing child... yet I will not forget you* — moves close); it is God whose comfort *resembles* a mother's. The maternal-image is reinforced by the Jerusalem-as-mother imagery of 66:7-13: Jerusalem in labor, Jerusalem nursing, Jerusalem's consoling-breast, the carrying-on-the-hip and bouncing-on-the-knees. The mothering happens at multiple levels — Jerusalem mothers her children; Yahweh mothers Israel; both maternal-images converge.

The closing-verse imagery of 66:24 uses Hebrew *toleatam lo tamut ve-ishaam lo tichbeh ve-hayu de-raon le-khol basar* (תּוֹלַעְתָּם לֹא תָמוּת וְאִשָּׁם לֹא תִכְבֶּה וְהָיוּ דֵרָאוֹן לְכָל־בָּשָׂר) — *their worm shall not die, their fire shall not be quenched, and they shall be an abhorrence to all flesh*. The image is not eternal-conscious-torment in the original setting — it is the post-mortem indignity of unburied corpses being consumed by maggots and burned, which in the ancient Near East was the ultimate humiliation. The setting is plausibly the Hinnom Valley (*Gehinnom* / Gehenna) outside Jerusalem, the burning trash-heap that became the New Testament word for hell.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter is a montage rather than a single linear argument. The major movements:

**The anti-temple oracle (66:1-4).** Heaven is Yahweh's throne; the earth is his footstool. The temple cannot contain him. The God who acted out the Exodus and split the sea will not be domesticated by sacred-architecture. What Yahweh values: *the humble and contrite in spirit who tremble at my word*.

**The mother-Jerusalem birth oracle (66:5-9).** A startling image: Jerusalem gives birth before being in labor, delivers children before pain comes. The post-exilic community's restoration is figured as miraculous childbirth. Yahweh asks: *shall I bring to the point of birth and not cause to bring forth?* Once Yahweh has begun the labor, he will not abandon it.

**The maternal-comfort vision (66:10-14).** Rejoice with Jerusalem; nurse from her consoling-breast; be carried on her hip and bounced on her knees. *As one whom his mother comforts, so I will comfort you.* The maternal-divine image runs alongside the mother-Jerusalem image.

**The fiery-judgment oracle (66:15-17).** Yahweh comes in fire, with chariots like a whirlwind, fury, flame, sword. Those who sanctify themselves with cultic-syncretism in the gardens will *come to an end together*. The judgment-language is severe.

**The universal-mission vision (66:18-21).** *I will gather all nations and tongues.* Survivors will be sent to Tarshish (Spain or Tartessos in the western Mediterranean), Pul (uncertain — possibly Put / Libya), Lud (Lydia in Asia Minor), Tubal (eastern Anatolia), Javan (Greece), the coastlands far away. The Gentile-nations will be brought to Jerusalem as an offering. And — astonishingly — *some of them I will take for priests and for Levites*. The post-exilic priestly-tradition reserved priesthood for Aaronic descent and Levitical service for tribal Levi; this oracle promises Yahweh will take Gentiles into the priesthood.

**The new-creation continuity (66:22-23).** As the new-heavens-and-new-earth shall remain, so shall your offspring and your name. From new-moon to new-moon and Sabbath to Sabbath, *all flesh shall come to worship before me*.

**The dark close (66:24).** They shall go out and look on the dead bodies of those who rebelled. Worm undying. Fire unquenched. Abhorrence to all flesh.

The literary architecture is dense and somewhat asymmetric. The chapter does not march toward resolution; it accumulates. It is more like the closing of a symphony with multiple themes returning rather than a linear argument leading to a conclusion. The juxtaposition of maternal-comfort (66:13) and unquenched-fire (66:24) within eleven verses is not an editorial accident; it is the chapter's deliberate theology.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Within the prophetic canon.** Isaiah 66 closes the book with all major Trito-Isaiah themes: temple critique, cultic-purity polemic, maternal-divine imagery, the new-creation, the universal-mission, and final judgment. The inclusio with Isaiah 1 brackets the entire book. The new-heavens-and-new-earth language (66:22) reinforces 65:17. The maternal-image (66:13) builds on 49:15.

**Jewish trajectory — the synagogue's reading-tradition.** Here is one of the most theologically interesting facts about how Jews and Christians have read the Hebrew Bible's ending differently. In the Jewish liturgical tradition, when Isaiah 66 is read publicly in the synagogue (it appears as a haftarah portion, including for *Shabbat Rosh Chodesh* — the new-moon Sabbath, drawing on 66:23), the reader does not end on 66:24. Instead, after reading 66:24, the reader goes back and re-reads 66:23: *from new moon to new moon, and from Sabbath to Sabbath, all flesh shall come to worship before me*. The dark note is not the last note heard.

The Talmud (b. *Megillah* 31b) discusses this practice — books that end on a hard note are not allowed to end the public reading on that note. The same liturgical principle applies to several other texts: Isaiah 66, Malachi 4 (*lest I come and strike the land with utter destruction*), Ecclesiastes 12 (*the conclusion of the matter — fear God*), and Lamentations (*for if you have utterly rejected us...*). In each case, the synagogue re-reads the second-to-last verse aloud after the last, so that the public-reading ends on hope. The Hebrew technical name for this is *shen* — to repeat the previous verse.

This is itself a theology. The Jewish tradition has decided, deliberately and liturgically, that the dark final-verse of Isaiah is true (it is in scripture, it is read) but should not be the *last word* heard by the worshiping community. The hope is the last word. The judgment is real but penultimate. This is a precise theological claim made through liturgical-practice rather than through doctrinal-statement.

The maternal-divine image of 66:13 has shaped Jewish prayer-tradition. The *Shechinah* (the dwelling-presence of God) is grammatically feminine in Hebrew, and Jewish mystical-tradition (the Kabbalah, the *Zohar*) developed an extensive theology of the divine-feminine drawing in part on Isaiah 66:13 and 49:15. The mother-Jerusalem imagery feeds into the *Tisha B'Av* lamentation-tradition (Jerusalem as bereaved-mother) and into the consoling-Jerusalem of Sabbath-and-festival prayer.

The universal-mission vision (66:18-21) and the priests-and-Levites-from-the-Gentiles claim raised difficult questions in classical Jewish interpretation. Some commentators (Rashi) read it conservatively — Yahweh will take Gentiles for *service* in some ancillary capacity. Others (Ibn Ezra, Radak) read it more boldly as messianic-age inclusion of God-fearers. The text remains one of the most universalist verses in the Hebrew Bible.

**Christian trajectory.** Stephen quotes Isaiah 66:1-2 in his speech in Acts 7:49-50 immediately before he is stoned: *the Most High does not dwell in houses made by hands, as the prophet says: heaven is my throne, and the earth is my footstool*. Stephen's appropriation of the anti-temple oracle is consequential — it becomes one of the New Testament's strongest critiques of localizing-divine-presence. The Christian theology of the church-as-temple (1 Cor 3:16-17; Eph 2:19-22; 1 Pet 2:4-5) draws on this Isaian-Stephen tradition.

The maternal-divine imagery has been received in Christian tradition with some ambivalence. The earliest fathers (Clement of Alexandria, Origen) cited 66:13 as evidence of God's tender-feminine compassion. Medieval Christian mystics (Julian of Norwich's famous *Christ as our Mother* in the *Revelations of Divine Love*; Anselm's *Prayer to St. Paul* with its image of *Christ as mother*) developed maternal-divine theology drawing on this and similar texts. The Catechism of the Catholic Church §239 affirms: *God transcends the human distinction between the sexes... He is neither man nor woman: he is God. He also transcends human fatherhood and motherhood, although he is their origin and standard*; the CCC cites Isa 66:13 and 49:15 as warrants for the *maternal* dimension of God.

Modern Christian feminist theology (Phyllis Trible's *God and the Rhetoric of Sexuality*, Sallie McFague's *Models of God*, Elizabeth A. Johnson's *She Who Is*) draws extensively on Isaiah 66:13 and 49:15 to recover the maternal-divine images that the predominantly-paternal mainstream Christian tradition has often muted. Mainstream Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran, and Reformed denominations have officially affirmed God's transcendence of gender while continuing to use predominantly-paternal liturgical-language; revisions to liturgical-language to include maternal-divine images have varied across traditions and remain contested in some.

**The Mark 9 / Gehenna text.** Jesus quotes Isaiah 66:24 directly in Mark 9:48 — *where their worm does not die and the fire is not quenched*. The Greek text of Mark 9:48 is essentially a direct quotation of the LXX of Isaiah 66:24. The quotation is part of Jesus's *Gehenna* teaching (Mark 9:43-48) — better to enter life maimed than to be thrown into Gehenna where the worm does not die and the fire is not quenched. The Hinnom Valley (*ge-Hinnom*) outside Jerusalem, with its history as a site of child-sacrifice (Jer 7:31, 19:5-6) and later as a burning trash-heap, became the New Testament's word for the place of final judgment.

This is one of the principal Old Testament sources of Christian doctrine of hell. The other major-source is Daniel 12:2 (*some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt*). Jesus's repeated use of *Gehenna* language (Matt 5:22, 5:29, 5:30, 10:28, 18:9, 23:15, 23:33; Mark 9:43, 9:45, 9:47; Luke 12:5) draws on Isaiah 66:24 as background.

The major Christian-theological options on hell:

(a) *Eternal-conscious-torment* — the traditional and majority Christian view, held by mainstream Catholic, Reformed, Lutheran, Methodist, and Eastern Orthodox traditions. The dead-bodies-with-undying-worm imagery of Isaiah 66:24 is read as eternally-extending suffering of the unredeemed.

(b) *Annihilationism / conditional-immortality* — a minority but historically present view, held by some early-church figures and by John Stott (the late evangelical British theologian, in his *Essentials* dialogue with David Edwards), Edward Fudge (*The Fire That Consumes*), Clark Pinnock, and others. The fire and worm of Isa 66:24 destroy completely; the *abhorrence to all flesh* is the ongoing memory of the consumption rather than continuous-conscious-suffering.

(c) *Universalism / apokatastasis* — held by Origen and some patristic writers; condemned by some councils but recently revived in mainstream evangelical and Catholic-leaning circles by David Bentley Hart (*That All Shall Be Saved*), Robin Parry (writing as Gregory MacDonald, *The Evangelical Universalist*), Brad Jersak. The universalist reading treats the judgment-language as severe-but-purgative — even *Gehenna* is for restoration rather than final-loss.

Each of these views can engage Isaiah 66:24 and Mark 9:48 honestly. The eternal-conscious-torment view treats the language at face-value. The annihilationist view emphasizes the destruction-language: the worm consumes and the fire burns until the corpse is gone; *eternal* describes the *finality* not the *duration of suffering*. The universalist view treats the imagery as the language-of-judgment-as-purgation pointing to ultimate-restoration.

**New Testament use beyond Mark 9 and Acts 7.** Romans 9-11 draws on Isaiah's universal-mission vision. The new-heavens-and-new-earth of 66:22 is echoed in 2 Pet 3:13 and Rev 21:1. Revelation 21:24-26 picks up the gathering-of-all-nations vision. The maternal-comfort of 66:13 informs 2 Cor 1:3-4 (the God of all comfort) and the mother-of-us-all language of Gal 4:26.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The maternal-divine and Christian theology of God.** The mainstream Christian tradition has affirmed both that God transcends gender and that God's primary self-revelation in scripture is in paternal-language (Father, Son, the Holy Spirit traditionally pronounced as masculine in Greek and Latin). Isaiah 66:13 (and 49:15, and the *El-Shaddai* etymology associating God with the breast / nurturing — though this is contested in scholarship) provides the strongest maternal-counterpoints. Modern feminist theology has argued that recovering these maternal images is essential to a fully-orbed Christian theology of God; mainstream Catholic and Reformed responses (CCC §239 and various Reformed treatments) have affirmed the maternal-dimension while preserving the predominantly-paternal liturgical-language.

The honest reading: scripture itself uses both. Reducing God to either male or female projection is idolatry. The Christian liturgical-tradition has historically privileged the paternal; the Jewish mystical-tradition has more readily used the feminine (*Shechinah*); the modern feminist-theological recovery is genuinely scriptural without being merely-ideological. The simile-precision of Isaiah 66:13 (*as a man's mother comforts him, so I will comfort you*) preserves both the simile-grammar (Yahweh is *like* a mother, the comparison is not collapsing into identification) and the boldness of the comparison.

**Tension 2: The Mark 9 / hell-text and the structure of final-judgment.** The eternal-conscious-torment / annihilationist / universalist debate is not new, but it has intensified in modern evangelicalism. The mainstream Catholic position (CCC §1033-1037 on hell) holds eternal-conscious-torment but interprets it as the soul's free self-exclusion from communion with God rather than divine-inflicted-punishment. The mainstream Reformed position holds eternal-conscious-torment more directly (Westminster Confession 33.2). Eastern Orthodox theology distinguishes between the *energy* of God's love which is experienced as bliss by the redeemed and as fire by the unrepentant — the same divine fire is heaven for some and hell for others.

David Bentley Hart's universalism rests in part on the argument that Isa 66:24 / Mark 9:48 do not require eternal-conscious-torment: the imagery describes the perpetual-destruction-of-evil rather than the perpetual-suffering-of-the-evil. John Stott's annihilationism similarly distinguishes between the *eternality* of the consequence and the *duration* of conscious-suffering. The traditional reading takes the imagery at face-value: undying worm, unquenched fire.

Steelman the traditional view: scripture's strongest hell-language deserves to be heard. To soften it is to risk false-comfort and to weaken the moral-stakes of the gospel. The Catholic hell-as-self-exclusion preserves divine-justice and human-freedom.

Steelman the annihilationist view: scripture also speaks of *destruction* and *perishing* (Matt 10:28 — *fear him who can destroy both soul and body in hell*). The combined OT-NT picture supports complete-destruction of evil rather than its eternal-perpetuation.

Steelman the universalist view: the love of God revealed in Christ is incompatible with eternal-conscious-torment. The *all things* language of 1 Cor 15:28 (*God may be all in all*), Col 1:20 (*through him to reconcile to himself all things*), and the apocatastasis tradition deserve serious-weight. Isa 66:24 must be read alongside the universal-worship of 66:23 (*all flesh shall come to worship before me*).

The text itself does not decide between these positions. The Christian tradition has held all three in some form, with the majority view historically being eternal-conscious-torment. The current debate is healthy and unresolved.

**Tension 3: The synagogue's re-reading of 66:23.** The Jewish liturgical practice of going back to re-read 66:23 after 66:24 is itself a question for Christian readers. The Christian Bible ends Isaiah on 66:24 (the dark verse). The Jewish public-reading-tradition refuses to do so. What does this say about how each tradition has chosen to *hear* the Hebrew Bible's ending?

One way to take the Jewish liturgical-decision: it is a hermeneutical-judgment that the canonical-text is true (the dark verse is in the scroll, is read, is honored as Torah-tradition) but the *ending* — the way the community-sends-itself-home — should be hope. This is a piece of pastoral-theology built into the liturgy itself.

A Christian reader can learn from this. Christian scripture-reading has often privileged literalist-completeness (read the chapter to the end, and end where the chapter ends) over the kind of pastoral-shaping the Jewish tradition embeds in liturgy. The Christian Bible-reading-tradition does have parallel practices in lectionaries (some difficult passages are appointed for reading on penitential days, others for festal days, and the curatorial-shaping is real even if less explicit). But the synagogue's *shen* — the deliberate re-reading of the second-to-last verse — has no exact Christian equivalent.

What might the practice teach? Perhaps that the canonical-text's authority does not require the canonical-ending to function as the *last word* in a liturgical sense. Perhaps that hope, not judgment, is the proper-frame for sending the community out. Perhaps that the Jewish tradition has wrestled with these texts longer and more communally than any Christian tradition, and there is wisdom in their pastoral-practice that Christians should honor.

**Tension 4: The closing of Isaiah and the closing of canon.** Isaiah closes the prophetic books in some Christian Old Testament arrangements (Latter Prophets in Hebrew arrangement; in Christian arrangement Isaiah is followed by Jeremiah, Lamentations, Ezekiel, the Twelve, and then in Catholic arrangement the deuterocanonicals, before the New Testament begins with Matthew). The position of Isaiah 66 in the larger canonical-narrative places it as a hinge: the Hebrew Bible's vision of new-heavens-and-new-earth, universal-mission, maternal-comfort, and final-judgment all flow forward into the New Testament's gospel and eschatology.

The deliberate juxtaposition of universal-mission (*all flesh shall come to worship*) and final-judgment (*the worm shall not die*) in the closing-verses is theologically important. Isaiah does not close on a one-sided note. The chapter holds the universal-scope of God's redemptive-mission alongside the genuine-stakes of moral-rebellion. Christian theology that emphasizes one without the other does not match the chapter's ending.

**Where this leaves us.** Isaiah 66 is a chapter of extraordinary range. In ninety verses (the chapter is twenty-four; combined with chapter 65 the unit is about ninety) it covers temple-critique, mother-Jerusalem birth, maternal-divine comfort, fiery-judgment, universal-mission, the priests-and-Levites-from-the-Gentiles claim, the new-heavens-and-new-earth promise, and the unquenched-fire conclusion. It contains language Stephen quotes, Jesus quotes, and the Apocalypse quotes. It contains the Hebrew Bible's strongest maternal-divine image and the Hebrew Bible's primary source-text for Christian hell-doctrine.

The chapter ends Isaiah by refusing to choose between comfort and judgment. The God who comforts as a mother is also the God who comes in fire. The community gathered from all nations to worship will look on the bodies of those who rebelled. The maternal-comfort and the unquenched-fire are not alternatives; they are two faces of the same Yahweh. The chapter does not flinch.

And yet the synagogue, when it reads this chapter aloud, deliberately ends on 66:23 — *all flesh shall come to worship before me*. The dark verse stands in scripture but does not have the last spoken word. That liturgical-decision is a piece of theology Christian readers should hear and consider.

**Closing question.** When the maternal-comfort of Yahweh and the unquenched-fire of Yahweh stand together at the end of one of the most consequential books in human religious history — and when one tradition has chosen to re-read the second-to-last verse aloud rather than end on the last — what does it mean to *close* a sacred text? Is the canonical-final-word the only word? Or does faithfulness sometimes mean knowing how to send a community home?

Isaiah's last sentence is judgment. Isaiah's penultimate sentence is universal-worship. The book ends with both. We are required to hold both. We do not get to choose only one — but neither, perhaps, do we get to pretend the choice does not matter.
