# Ecclesiastes 10: Dead Flies and the King's Bird

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Ecclesiastes was composed in Persian-period Yehud, sometime in the fifth or fourth century BC. Choon-Leong Seow's Anchor Bible commentary (1997) makes the linguistic case decisive: the Hebrew of the book is Late Biblical Hebrew with significant Aramaic influence, including Persian loanwords (*pardes*, *pithgam*, *medinah* in the sense of imperial province). The economic vocabulary fits a monetized provincial economy after the Achaemenid coinage reforms, and the political horizon — kings, princes, courts, and the unsettled status of the rich and the slave — fits the Persian satrapal world rather than the tenth-century Davidic monarchy. Roland Murphy (Word), Tremper Longman (NICOT), Thomas Krüger (Hermeneia), James Crenshaw (OTL), Norman Whybray, and Stuart Weeks converge on this dating with disagreements measured in decades.

The Solomonic frame (*the words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem*, 1:1) is a wisdom-literary convention. Qohelet (קֹהֶלֶת, "assembler" or "convener") is a function-name. The epilogist who frames the book at 12:9–14 already treats Qohelet as a wisdom teacher distinct from Solomon, and the Solomonic guise is dropped after chapter 2. The book's signature spatial frame *tahat ha-shemesh* (תַּחַת הַשֶּׁמֶשׁ, "under the sun") and its keyword *hevel* (הֶבֶל, "vapor," "the ungraspable") situate the book inside a particular epistemic stance: the wise observer in a small province under imperial power, drawing conclusions about life from what can be seen rather than from inherited theological certainty.

Material culture matters in chapter 10 because the chapter's images are workshop and household images. *Dead flies in the perfumer's ointment* (10:1) presupposes a perfume industry — Persian-period Levantine balsam and resin trade is well-attested in the Aramaic ostraca from Idumea and the Persepolis fortification tablets. The agricultural and construction images of 10:8–9 — digging a pit, breaching a wall, quarrying stones, splitting logs — are images of small-holder Levantine labor with its routine hazards (snake bites in field walls are a real and recurrent danger in Mediterranean agriculture). The roof-sinks-in image of 10:18 presupposes the typical Levantine flat-roof house with packed earth and beams that required annual maintenance — a leaking roof is a winter hazard in the rainy season and a structural problem if neglected through summer. The serpent-charmer of 10:11 (literally *baʿal ha-lashon*, "lord of the tongue") fits the Egyptian and Mesopotamian profession of snake-handler well-attested from the second millennium onward.

Textual witnesses include 4QQoh-a and 4QQoh-b at Qumran, the LXX (Aquila-school literal Greek), and the Targum Qohelet (the expansive Aramaic paraphrase that consistently orthodoxizes the book). The Hebrew of chapter 10 is reasonably stable; the chief textual difficulty is 10:1, where the MT *zevuvey mavet* ("flies of death," likely meaning dead flies) is rendered variously by the ancient versions, and 10:10's *hakshir* ("succeed," literally "set right") is a hapax that the LXX renders by paraphrase.

Hebrew terminology in this chapter intersects with the rest of the book. *Sikluth* (סִכְלוּת, "folly") in 10:1 returns from 1:17, 2:3, 2:13, 7:25, where it has functioned as the structural opposite of *ḥokhmah* (חָכְמָה, "wisdom"). *Yitron* (יִתְרוֹן, "surplus") returns negatively at 10:11 — the snake-charmer's profit vanishes if the snake bites first. The wisdom literature's two conventional terms for the wise (*ḥakham*, חָכָם) and the fool (*kesil*, כְּסִיל) frame the chapter, and Qohelet's deployment of these terms here is the closest he comes anywhere in the book to the diction of Proverbs.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels are relevant but more diffuse than in chapter 9. The proverbial structure of chapter 10 has affinities with the Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemope* (especially the warnings about court conduct), the *Instruction of Ankhsheshonqy* (proverbial collections), and the broader Mesopotamian *Sumerian Proverbs* and *Counsels of Wisdom*. The political-prudence theme of 10:4, 10:16–17, and 10:20 fits the international wisdom literature on serving the king — a corpus that runs from Old Kingdom Egypt through Ahiqar (Aramaic, Persian-period, found at Elephantine). The boomerang doctrine (he who digs a pit falls into it, 10:8) is a near-universal proverbial trope; the Hebrew Bible has parallels at Proverbs 26:27 and Psalm 7:15. Whether 10:8–9 is invoking the orthodox wisdom doctrine or undermining it is one of the chapter's contested questions.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 10 reads as the most Proverbs-like chapter in Ecclesiastes — a cluster of short sayings (*meshalim*) loosely organized around themes of folly, court conduct, work, and speech. Crenshaw treats this as evidence that the chapter is editorially compiled from a proverb-collection, perhaps by the epilogist or a later hand. Seow defends its integrity as Qohelet's own deployment of conventional wisdom genres in service of his own argument. Murphy and Provan agree with Seow: the chapter shows Qohelet working *within* the wisdom tradition, using its forms even as he twists them.

The chapter's structure is loose but discernible. Verses 1–4 cluster around the small thing that ruins the great (dead flies, a little folly, the fool's lack of sense, the wise man's calmness before the angry ruler). Verses 5–7 are a thematic insertion — the world-upside-down observation that Qohelet has made before (8:14), here applied to political order: slaves on horses, princes walking. Verses 8–11 cluster around the dangers of work and the limits of action, with the boomerang proverb at the head and the snake-charmer at the foot. Verses 12–15 turn to the speech and labor of the fool. Verses 16–20 turn to the king and political prudence: the woe-and-blessing pair (16–17), the leaking roof (18), the secular-sounding aphorism on bread, wine, and money (19), and the final warning not even to *think* a curse against the king (20).

Hebrew literary devices include parallelism (densely throughout), proverbial chiasm (10:2's right-hand–left-hand pair, 10:16–17's woe-and-blessing pair), inclusio (the wise man's lips of favor at 10:12 inverted into the fool's speech-troubles at 10:13–14), and what Robert Alter calls "the gnomic surface": short, hard, polished sayings that resist explanation. The serpent appears twice (10:8, 10:11) as a small-scale leitwort. Money is named once (10:19), and the chapter's only direct YHWH-vocabulary is absent — a feature shared with the bulk of Qohelet.

The chapter's most contested literary question is whether 10:8–9 are quoted to *affirm* the boomerang doctrine (the orthodox reading: actions have consequences) or quoted to *undermine* it. Michael V. Fox in *A Time to Tear Down* reads the verses ironically: Qohelet quotes the conventional wisdom and then immediately deconstructs it at 10:11 (the snake-charmer's wisdom fails when the snake bites first) and 10:14 (no man knows what is to be). On Fox's reading, the chapter is doing what Job's friends do in reverse — mouthing the orthodox doctrine while the surrounding context discloses its failure. Murphy and Provan resist this reading as over-ironizing.

Verse 19 is the chapter's most striking line: *bread is made for laughter, and wine gladdens life, and money answers everything*. The Hebrew *ha-kesef yaʿaneh et ha-kol* — literally "money answers/responds-to all" — has been read variously: cynically (Crenshaw: Qohelet's secular-sounding shrug), realistically (Murphy: an observation, not a recommendation), and ironically (Fox: a quoted folly that the surrounding context exposes). The Targum, predictably, reads it as a description of the unrighteous, not Qohelet's own counsel.

Textual variants in chapter 10 are minor. 10:1's *yavʾish yabbiaʿ shemen roqeaḥ* is awkward Hebrew that some commentators emend to read *zevuvey mavet yavʾishu* ("dead flies stink"). 10:10's *hakshir* is a hapax that may be Aramaized; the LXX reads it freely. None of these variants alter the chapter's argument.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Ecclesiastes 10 stands at a particular point in the wisdom-literature trajectory: between Proverbs' confident retributive theology and the carpe-diem of chapter 11, between the mortalism of chapter 9 and the editorial-frame address of chapter 12. Chapter 10 is the chapter where Qohelet most resembles Proverbs and most quietly undermines it — or quietly extends it, depending on which scholar you read.

**Jewish trajectory.** Ben Sira (Sirach) absorbs Qohelet 10's themes — the wise tongue, the foolish servant, the king's anger — and reinscribes them under explicit Torah-piety. Sirach 8:1–2 ("do not contend with a powerful man, lest you fall into his hands") is the Sirach version of Qohelet 10:4. The Wisdom of Solomon, written later and more assertively, constructs an explicit king's court (chs. 6–9) under which the wise rule justly — the corrected counter-image of Qohelet's "folly set in many high places" (10:6).

The rabbinic tradition reads chapter 10 in the context of court wisdom and political prudence. Rashi reads 10:8 (digging the pit) as an ethical warning; Ibn Ezra reads philologically and reads 10:8–9 as observations rather than doctrines. Ralbag (Gersonides) reads philosophically. Sforno reads ethically. The Targum Qohelet expands 10:20 ("a bird of the air will carry your voice") into a meditation on the omnipresent surveillance of God — a striking pre-modern reading that turns the political observation into a theological one. Maimonides cites Qohelet's political prudence in the *Guide* (3.51) as practical philosophy. Robert Gordis (*Koheleth: The Man and His World*, 1951) reads chapter 10 as Qohelet's deliberate engagement with the conventional wisdom material, neither endorsing nor rejecting it but reframing it under the larger argument of *hevel*. Michael V. Fox reads more ironically.

The canonization debate in Mishnah Yadayim 3:5 and the report in Shabbat 30b ("the Sages sought to suppress the Book of Qohelet because its words contradict one another") is partly about chapter 10 — the proverbs that mouth orthodox wisdom seem to contradict the carpe-diem and mortalism of the surrounding chapters. The rabbinic resolution was canonical: the book's beginning and end are *divrei Torah* (words of Torah), and the contradictions are educational, not disqualifying.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic reception read chapter 10 allegorically. Gregory of Nyssa's *Homilies on Ecclesiastes* (c. 380) reads 10:1 (dead flies in the perfumer's ointment) as the small sin that corrupts the soul's virtue — a reading that became influential in monastic ascetic theology. Jerome's *Commentary on Ecclesiastes* (388) reads the chapter as worldly counsel that the spiritual reader transcends through ascetic vocation. Didymus the Blind's commentary, recovered at Tura in 1941, supplies an Alexandrian moralizing reading. Hugh of St. Victor and Bonaventure read in the Jerome tradition. Aquinas in his Lectura on Ecclesiastes reads chapter 10 as natural ethics that points toward supernatural fulfillment.

The Reformation broke with allegory. Luther's *Notes on Ecclesiastes* (1526/1532) reads chapter 10 vocationally — the chapter's wisdom is for the conduct of one's calling under God's providence. Luther read 10:19 (*money answers everything*) realistically and ironically: it is not Qohelet's counsel but his observation that the world runs on money, an observation the Christian must hear without surrendering to. Calvin published no commentary on Ecclesiastes; his silence on the book is itself a datum of Reformation reception.

Critical scholarship from Delitzsch (1875) through Crenshaw, Murphy, Seow, Fox, and Whybray reads chapter 10 as proverbial material that Qohelet either compiled, edited, or appropriated. Tremper Longman's NICOT commentary (1998) reads the chapter as part of the heterodox skeptic's discourse that the epilogist contains. Iain Provan (NIVAC) reads Qohelet as a sincere wisdom teacher within the orthodox tradition. Brevard Childs's canonical reading takes the proverbial chapter as integral to the canonical shape. Walter Brueggemann reads Qohelet's proverbs as counter-testimonial wisdom. Ellen Davis reads the chapter as practical ethics for a post-imperial people.

**Where they diverge.** The deepest divide on chapter 10 is whether the proverbs are sincere (Murphy, Provan, Gordis: Qohelet works within the wisdom tradition) or ironic (Fox: Qohelet quotes orthodoxy in order to expose it) or editorially imposed (Crenshaw: the chapter is later proverb-collection material added to the book). The reading you adopt shapes how you hear the rest of the book — particularly whether the carpe-diem of the next chapter is grounded in observed wisdom or in Qohelet's residual hope for some moral order.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The relation to Proverbs.** Does chapter 10 prove Qohelet *uses* conventional wisdom, *cites it ironically*, or is it editorially imposed?

The Murphy-Provan reading — Qohelet works within the wisdom tradition — is the most powerful. Steelmanned: Qohelet is not a heterodox skeptic but a Persian-period wisdom teacher whose distinctive contribution is to take the wisdom tradition seriously enough to test it against observation. Chapter 10 is what wisdom looks like after it has passed through the *hevel* refrain. The proverbs are real, they are accurate as far as they go, and they are inadequate as a complete account of the world. The wise man's mouth wins favor (10:12) — usually. The fool's labor wearies him (10:15) — usually. He who digs a pit falls into it (10:8) — sometimes, not always; and the snake-charmer who is bitten first (10:11) shows the limit. The chapter, on this reading, is wisdom-tradition stuff used honestly: it is what the tradition can deliver, no more and no less. Qohelet is neither denying it nor pretending it is sufficient. This is the reading that best fits the book as wisdom literature.

The Fox ironic reading is also strong. Steelmanned: Qohelet's deployment of orthodox wisdom in chapter 10 is inseparable from its limits in chapter 9. The boomerang doctrine of 10:8 cannot be heard innocently when the chapter immediately preceding has said *the same event happens to the righteous and the wicked*. Qohelet's quotation is not endorsement; it is sober citation followed by quiet undermining (the snake who bites before being charmed; the fool whose labor wearies him; the upside-down political world of 10:5–7). This reading takes Qohelet's seriousness to consist in his refusal to be naïve about the wisdom tradition's confidence.

The Crenshaw editorial reading is the weakest of the three but not absurd. Steelmanned: chapter 10's proverbial diction does sit awkwardly with the carpe-diem and mortalism of the surrounding chapters, and the most parsimonious explanation is later editorial expansion under the influence of Proverbs. The book's canonical shape is a multi-voiced text, and chapter 10 is one voice.

The Catholic tradition has historically read chapter 10 in the Murphy-Provan direction (natural wisdom that points toward grace, in Aquinas's framework). The Protestant Reformed tradition has read in the Provan direction (covenantal wisdom under providence). The Lutheran tradition has read in the Luther direction (vocational counsel for the Christian in the world). The Orthodox tradition has read it ascetically (Gregory of Nyssa). Modern Jewish thought has tended toward the Gordis-Murphy reading (Qohelet as honest sage). Critical scholarship has been more open to the Fox ironic reading.

Where this leaves us: the steelman of the Murphy-Provan position is that wisdom literature *is* a tradition of disciplined observation, and Qohelet's contribution is not to reject the tradition but to extend it by adding the element of *miqreh* (chance) and *hevel* (vapor) that Proverbs underweights. Chapter 10 is wisdom doing its real work — not pretending to predict outcomes but training the wise observer to live well in a world where outcomes are not guaranteed.

**Tension 2: Political theology — *do not curse the king even in your thoughts*.** Verse 20 — *even in your thoughts, do not curse the king, nor in your bedroom curse the rich, for a bird of the air will carry your voice* — is one of the strangest verses in the Old Testament. Read most literally, it is a warning about Persian-period political surveillance and the prudent silence of the small-holder. Read theologically, it is a basis for Romans 13 (*let every soul be subject to the higher authorities*) and 1 Peter 2:13–17 (*honor the king*).

The Catholic political tradition, drawing on Augustine's *City of God* and the medieval doctrine of the two swords, has read 10:20 as a warning about political prudence and the duty of obedience to legitimate authority, with significant qualifications about tyranny (Aquinas's discussion of unjust laws, *Summa Theologiae* I-II.96). The Reformed tradition, drawing on Calvin's *Institutes* IV.20 (on civil government), has read it as a doctrine of obedience under providential ordination, with the famous Calvinist exception that lesser magistrates may resist a tyrannical sovereign (developed by Theodore Beza, Junius Brutus, and the Scottish Covenanters). The Lutheran tradition, drawing on Luther's two-kingdoms doctrine, has read it as the Christian's submission to temporal authority while keeping conscience free.

The radical Reformation broke with this. The Anabaptist Schleitheim Confession (1527) read the New Testament's apparent endorsement of civil authority as compatible with the Christian's separation from political power — the king is to be obeyed and avoided. The Mennonite tradition extended this. John Howard Yoder's *The Politics of Jesus* (1972) read Romans 13 in light of Jesus's own political nonviolence and argued that the New Testament's apparent endorsement of civil authority is more limited and more subversive than mainstream Christian political theology has admitted.

Liberation theology has read 10:20 from below. Gustavo Gutiérrez and the broader Latin American tradition note that the verse comes from a position of subjugation under imperial power. The Persian-period Yehudite who is told not even to *think* a curse against the king is the colonized small-holder under imperial surveillance. Read from that position, the verse is descriptive (this is what the powerless must do to survive) rather than prescriptive (this is what wisdom commends). Walter Brueggemann's *Theology of the Old Testament* reads chapter 10 as counter-testimonial wisdom that names the political-economic asymmetry that the dominant theological voices in Israel mute.

The political-theology question is unavoidable: how do you read 10:20 from a position of political privilege (where the verse functions as an endorsement of the existing order) versus from a position of oppression (where the verse functions as a description of the conditions of survival)? The two readings are not symmetrical. The first reading naturalizes the existing distribution of power. The second reading exposes it.

Where this leaves us: chapter 10's political-prudence material is read very differently depending on the political position of the reader. The unresolved question — and it is one the Christian church has not resolved — is whether 10:20 is wisdom or accommodation. If it is wisdom (Murphy, Provan, the mainstream tradition), it endorses prudent silence under imperial authority. If it is accommodation (Brueggemann, Yoder, Gutiérrez), it describes the conditions under which the powerless survive while leaving open the question whether faithful witness might require a different posture.

## The Thread

Proverbs 26:27 (*whoever digs a pit will fall into it; if anyone rolls a stone, it will come back on him*) is the orthodox boomerang doctrine that Ecclesiastes 10:8 quotes; the question is whether Qohelet endorses or undermines it. Sirach 8:1–2 gives the Sirach reframing of court prudence and provides a Jewish wisdom-tradition reading of Qohelet 10:4 under explicit Torah-piety. Ahiqar (the Aramaic wisdom text from Elephantine) shares the political-prudence vocabulary of "do not curse the king" with Qohelet 10:20 — the closest Persian-period parallel and evidence that this is shared imperial-court wisdom. Romans 13:1–7 picks up the political-theology trajectory of Qohelet 10:20 and reframes it under the doctrine of providence; the Christian reception of 10:20 is essentially a long argument over how to hold these two texts together without naturalizing imperial power.
