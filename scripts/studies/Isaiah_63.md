# Isaiah 63 — Go Deeper

## The Thread

Isaiah 63 opens with a watchman's startled question: *Who is this who comes from Edom, in crimsoned garments from Bozrah?* The answer is Yahweh himself — alone, his clothing splattered with blood, marching from the south after a battle no human witnessed. *I have trodden the winepress alone*, he says. *Their lifeblood spattered on my garments*. The image is one of the most disturbing in the Hebrew Bible: God as a solitary blood-soaked warrior who has just trampled enemies underfoot.

And then the chapter pivots. Without warning, the prophetic voice drops the warrior-vision and begins reciting Yahweh's *steadfast love*. The Exodus is remembered — the dividing of the sea, the Holy Spirit in Israel's midst, the days of Moses. But then the lament breaks: *Where are your zeal and your might?* The God who once split the sea seems hidden, withdrawn. The chapter ends as a community-lament begging for the heavens to be torn open.

This is not a chapter that resolves. It holds the bloody-warrior and the absent-comforter together in a single text, and it leaves the reader staring at the gap.

## Lens 1 — Historical World

Isaiah 63 belongs to Third Isaiah (chs. 56-66), the post-exilic collection that mainstream-critical scholarship dates to late sixth or fifth century BC, addressing the disappointments of the rebuilt-Jerusalem community. The mainstream-critical position (Westermann, Blenkinsopp's Anchor Yale *Isaiah 56-66*, Hanson's *The Dawn of Apocalyptic*, Goldingay, Stromberg, Tull) reads chs. 63-64 as a community-lament unit responding to a specific crisis — most likely the slow rebuilding of Jerusalem, the continued Persian-political subjection, or the sense that the promised glory of restoration has not arrived.

The composite-authorship debate persists. Bernhard Duhm's 1892 *Das Buch Jesaia* argued for the three-Isaiah division that has structured critical scholarship since. The unified-authorship defense (Motyer, Oswalt) maintains that the eighth-century prophet could speak across centuries. Brevard Childs's canonical approach holds the layered composition together with the canonical-authoritative final form. The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa-a) from Qumran preserves the entire 66 chapters as one document by 125 BC.

The chapter divides naturally into two distinct units:

**63:1-6 — The Edom-Bozrah oracle.** Edom (Hebrew *Edom*, אֱדוֹם) was Israel's southern neighbor and historic enemy, descended from Esau. *Bozrah* (בָּצְרָה) was a major Edomite city. Critical scholarship has long noted the wordplay — *botzer* (בּוֹצֵר) means *one who gathers grapes* / *vintager*, and *Bozrah* sounds like *botzer*; the same root is at play in the wine-treader image. After the Babylonian destruction of 586 BC, Edom was widely regarded in Israel as having taken advantage of Judah's collapse — looting, betraying refugees, claiming territory. Obadiah, Jeremiah 49, Ezekiel 35, Psalm 137 (*remember, O Lord, against the Edomites*) all carry intense anti-Edom polemic in the post-exilic period. Edom became, in the prophetic literature, a symbol for the *enemy* in the largest sense — not just the historical kingdom but the powers that oppress Yahweh's people.

**63:7-19 — The community lament.** A separate genre and voice. The first-person plural ("we") recites Yahweh's mighty acts of the Exodus, then turns to lament: God's compassion is *held back*; the sanctuary is trampled; the people seem to be those over whom Yahweh has never ruled. The unit continues into chapter 64 (the verse-division here is a Christian-Bible convention; the lament unit runs 63:7 through 64:12 and is sometimes called the *Penitential Prayer of Trito-Isaiah*).

The lament has a striking detail: *Where is he who put in the midst of them his Holy Spirit?* (63:11). The phrase *Holy Spirit* (Hebrew *ruach qodsho*, רוּחַ קָדְשׁוֹ) appears here and at 63:10 (*they grieved his Holy Spirit*) — and almost nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible (Ps 51:11 is the only clear parallel). The Holy-Spirit language we associate with the New Testament has its Hebrew roots in this Trito-Isaiah lament, which Paul will echo in Ephesians 4:30 (*do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God*).

The text-critical issue at 63:9 is famous. The Masoretic Text reads *lo tzar* (לֹא צָר) — *he was not their adversary*. But by ancient scribal tradition (the *qere*), the word is read *lo tzar* (לוֹ צָר) — *to him there was distress*, i.e., *in all their affliction he was afflicted*. The ESV follows the qere. The reading is theologically charged — a God who suffers with his people — and has been important to twentieth-century theology of divine suffering (e.g., Jürgen Moltmann's *The Crucified God*).

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter combines two distinct literary forms.

**The dialogic vintner-oracle (63:1-6).** A short dramatic dialogue. A watcher asks the question; Yahweh answers. The *winepress* is a standard biblical-prophetic image for divine judgment — Joel 3:13, Lamentations 1:15 (*the Lord has trodden as in a winepress the virgin daughter of Judah*), and ultimately Revelation 14:19-20 and 19:13-15. The image is grim: the press crushes the grapes; the juice runs red; here the juice is the lifeblood of the trampled. The wine-and-blood symbolic equation is ancient and runs deep. The structural feature: Yahweh says *alone* (Hebrew *levaddi*, לְבַדִּי) — *I have trodden the winepress alone*. No human warrior accompanies him; no army shares the work. The salvation is unilateral.

**The historical-recital lament (63:7-14).** A shift to community-praise: *I will recount the steadfast love of the Lord* (Hebrew *chasdei Yahweh*, חַסְדֵי יְהוָה). The verse is the standard psalm-language of remembered-mercy. The recital walks through Exodus history — Yahweh as Savior, the angel of his presence, the rebellion in the wilderness, the dividing of the sea, the Holy Spirit in their midst. The literary technique is anamnesis — *remembering forward* — rehearsing the past to ground a present plea.

**The lament proper (63:15-19).** The voice turns from praise to plea. *Look down from heaven and see*. The *stirring of your inner parts* (Hebrew *hamon me'eikha*, הֲמוֹן מֵעֶיךָ — literally *the rumbling of your bowels*) is the visceral-compassion-language. Where is it? Why is it withheld? The ancestors are far away (*Abraham does not know us, Israel does not acknowledge us*) — but Yahweh is *Father*: Hebrew *atta avinu* (אַתָּה אָבִינוּ), *you are our Father*. The chapter ends with the bitterest line: *we have become like those over whom you have never ruled, like those who are not called by your name*. The crisis-language reaches all the way back: as if the covenant had never happened. As if Yahweh had abandoned them so completely that they could no longer be told from the nations.

The Christian chapter-division at 63:19/64:1 is awkward — the cry that closes 63 (*tear open the heavens and come down*) is in the Hebrew text 63:19b and 64:1. The plea for divine descent runs across the chapter break.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Within the prophetic canon.** The Edom-vintager oracle of 63:1-6 develops a long prophetic tradition of Edom-judgment (Obadiah; Jer 49:7-22; Ezek 25:12-14; 35:1-15; Amos 1:11-12; Mal 1:2-5). Edom became a cipher for the worst of national-enmity. The community-lament of 63:7-64:12 belongs to a wider Hebrew-Bible lament tradition — Psalm 44, 74, 79, 80, 89; Lamentations; Habakkuk's questioning. Israel's faith never lost the right to ask why.

**Jewish trajectory.** The medieval Jewish commentators (Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Radak) read the Edom-vintager oracle eschatologically — as Yahweh's final judgment on the nations that oppressed Israel. By the rabbinic period, *Edom* in Jewish interpretation came to symbolize Rome, and later Christian Rome, and eventually any enemy power that had broken the covenant-people. The *trampling of the winepress* was held in Jewish liturgy as Yahweh's eventual vindication.

The community-lament of 63:7-64:12 entered Jewish liturgical use, particularly during periods of communal distress. The *Avinu Malkeinu* prayer tradition, recited on the High Holy Days and fast days, draws on the Father-language of 63:16 (*you are our Father*) and the petition for divine compassion. The verse *for you are our Father, though Abraham does not know us* is sometimes read as the boldest prayer in Jewish tradition — claiming Yahweh as Father directly, without intermediary, when even the patriarchs cannot be invoked.

**Christian trajectory.** The Edom-vintager oracle entered Christian art and devotion in disturbing form. Medieval Christian iconography depicts Christ as the *vintner* whose robes are dyed in his own blood (or in the blood of his enemies, depending on the tradition). The image of the *crimsoned warrior* runs through Christian eschatology, military symbolism, and (more troublingly) crusader theology. The Christian appropriation of the Edom-vintager oracle has had a complicated and sometimes destructive history — used to justify violence in the Crusades, in conquest theology, and in white-Christian-nationalist symbolism. Honest Christian readers must acknowledge how this text has been weaponized.

The community-lament of 63:7-64:12 entered Christian liturgical use through the Advent season. The cry *oh that you would tear open the heavens and come down* (63:19/64:1) became the central Advent-prayer — the plea for the incarnation, the coming-down of God into history. The traditional Advent hymn *O Come, O Come, Emmanuel* (Latin *Veni, Veni, Emmanuel*, twelfth-century antiphons) draws on this longing. Christian Advent theology reads the incarnation itself as Yahweh's answer to the lament: he *did* tear open the heavens and come down — in the womb of Mary.

**New Testament use.** The Edom-vintager oracle is the direct source of Revelation 19:13-15. The rider on the white horse, *clothed in a robe dipped in blood*, whose name is *the Word of God*, *will tread the winepress of the fury of the wrath of God the Almighty*. The image is unmistakably Isaiah 63:1-6 read Christologically — the lone warrior, the bloodied robe, the trampling of the winepress. Christian eschatology takes the disturbing-Yahweh image and applies it to the returning Christ.

The Holy-Spirit language of 63:10-11 enters New Testament theology directly. Paul's *do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God* (Eph 4:30) echoes 63:10. Stephen's speech in Acts 7:51 — *you stiff-necked people, uncircumcised in heart and ears, you always resist the Holy Spirit* — recalls the same text.

The Father-language of 63:16 (*you are our Father*) is part of the OT background to Jesus's *Abba* prayer (Mark 14:36), the Lord's Prayer (Matt 6:9 — *our Father*), and Pauline adoption-theology (Rom 8:15; Gal 4:6). The Israelite tradition of addressing Yahweh as Father — present here in Trito-Isaiah and in a few other places (Deut 32:6; Mal 2:10) — flowers in Jesus's intimate-paternal address.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The bloody-warrior image and Christian non-violence.** Isaiah 63:1-6 is one of the texts that most challenges any non-violent reading of God. *I trod them in my anger and trampled them in my wrath; their lifeblood spattered on my garments, and stained all my apparel.* The image is brutal. There is no softening; the text intends to disturb.

The Christian tradition has handled this in several ways. The dominant patristic and medieval reading: this is Yahweh's righteous judgment on cosmic evil, eventually Christ's eschatological judgment on the powers that oppress the saints. Augustine, Origen, Jerome read it Christologically. Aquinas affirmed divine-judgment as an attribute of justice. The Reformers (Calvin) read it as Christ's judgment to be revealed at the parousia.

Modern Christian non-violence advocates (Greg Boyd's *Crucifixion of the Warrior God*, Brian Zahnd's *Sinners in the Hands of a Loving God*, Walter Wink's *Engaging the Powers*) have argued that the violent-Yahweh imagery must be read through the lens of Christ's cross — where Christ *absorbs* violence rather than inflicting it. Boyd's cruciform-hermeneutic reads the OT divine-violence texts as accommodation to the human-projection of violence onto God; Christ is the corrective. The reading is theologically bold and has been criticized (Paul Copan, Thomas McCall) as too quickly setting aside the text's plain claims.

Mainstream Catholic teaching (CCC §2302-2317 on the legitimacy of just-war) has historically held divine-judgment alongside Catholic just-war ethics. Reformed and Lutheran traditions affirm both divine-judgment and Christian peace-making. The tension is unresolved in the tradition; honest theology cannot pretend the text is anything other than what it is.

Steelman the divine-judgment reading: a God who never judges is a God indifferent to evil. The Holocaust, slavery, genocide, child trafficking — these cannot be left unaddressed in a moral universe. The bloody-warrior image is the prophetic vocabulary for Yahweh's *moral seriousness* — the assurance that systemic evil will not prevail forever.

Steelman the cruciform-reading: the cross is where divine-power is revealed as self-giving love, not coercive violence. To read Isaiah 63 without the cross is to risk creating an idol — a violent God who legitimates human violence. Christian readers have done this, with terrible consequences. The cross requires us to re-read the OT divine-violence texts.

**Tension 2: The Spirit-grieved (63:10) and divine pathos.** The line *they rebelled and grieved his Holy Spirit; therefore he turned to be their enemy* contains a startling theological claim: God can be *grieved*. Abraham Heschel's *The Prophets* built a theology of divine-pathos on this and similar texts: God is not the unmoved-mover of Greek philosophy but the deeply-affected lover of his people. This view has been pressed further by twentieth-century theologians (Moltmann, Kazoh Kitamori's *Theology of the Pain of God*). Classical-theism (Aquinas, the Westminster Confession) has affirmed divine-impassibility — God does not literally suffer or change — and read the *grieving* language as anthropomorphic-accommodation.

The contemporary debate continues. Open-theism (Greg Boyd, John Sanders) reads the grieving-God passages literally. Reformed-classical-theism (Paul Helm, James Dolezal) reads them analogically. Catholic and Eastern Orthodox traditions tend toward classical-theism with significant nuance.

**Tension 3: *Why do you make us wander* (63:17).** *O Lord, why do you make us wander from your ways and harden our heart, so that we fear you not?* The verse asks God why he is the cause of his people's straying. This is the *theological* question of the chapter and one of the most uncomfortable verses in the Hebrew Bible. The same theological problem appears in Pharaoh's hardening (Exod 4-14), Romans 9:18 (*he hardens whom he wants to harden*), and the Augustinian-Calvinist tradition of monergism.

The Reformed-Augustinian reading takes it at face value: God in his sovereignty hardens whom he wills, even his own people, for purposes the people cannot see. The Arminian-Wesleyan and Catholic readings emphasize human responsibility and read the divine-hardening as God's just response to prior human rebellion. The mainstream-critical reading (Westermann, Blenkinsopp) treats it as the lament-rhetoric of a community pushed past explanation: when nothing else makes sense, the question becomes *why are you doing this to us?*

**Tension 4: The Advent-cry and Christian longing.** *Oh that you would tear open the heavens and come down* (63:19/64:1) became the foundational Advent prayer. Christian Advent reads Christmas as the answer: God *did* tear open the heavens. But the lament is bigger than Christmas. The plea continues — for the second-Advent, for the present-suffering of the church under persecution, for every situation where Yahweh seems hidden. Christian liturgy has wisely *kept* the Advent-lament alive even after Christmas, refusing to treat the incarnation as resolving every divine-hiddenness.

**Where this leaves us.** Isaiah 63 is a hard chapter. The bloody-warrior of Edom and the absent-Comforter of the lament are held together in a single text without resolution. The chapter refuses easy theology. It insists that Yahweh is *both* the moral-warrior who will not let evil prevail *and* the seemingly-withdrawn God whose people cry out for him to come down.

Christian theology has tried to resolve the tension at the cross — where Yahweh's judgment-on-evil and Yahweh's coming-down-into-suffering are united in one event. Whether this resolution holds depends on how seriously we take the text's own refusal to flatten its two halves. Reading the chapter forces a choice: do we honor the text's insistence on holding warrior and absence together, or do we domesticate one half to comfort ourselves?

**Closing question.** When Yahweh's robe is stained with the lifeblood of his enemies and Yahweh's people cry that the heavens are silent — what kind of God is this? And if Christ is the answer, is he the answer because he absorbed the bloody-winepress into himself, or because he is the one *coming* in crimson garments still to do the trampling? The chapter does not tell us how to choose.
