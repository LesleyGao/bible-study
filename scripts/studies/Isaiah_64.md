# Isaiah 64 — Go Deeper

## The Thread

Isaiah 64 is the second half of one of the great communal-laments of the Hebrew Bible. It begins with a cry that has shaped Christian Advent for centuries — *oh that you would rend the heavens and come down* — and ends with two questions to which the chapter offers no answer: *will you restrain yourself at these things, O Lord? Will you keep silent, and afflict us so terribly?* The chapter contains the famous *we are the clay, you are the potter* metaphor and the equally famous (and theologically explosive) *all our righteous deeds are like a polluted garment*.

Isaiah 64 is a prayer that knows it might not be answered. The community looks at the burned-out temple and ruined Jerusalem, recites Yahweh's mighty acts of the past, confesses sin, calls Yahweh *Father* and *Potter*, and then asks: are you going to do something? The chapter is the lament-floor of the Hebrew Bible's faith — the place where a people's trust is reduced to a pleading question.

## Lens 1 — Historical World

Isaiah 64 is the continuation of the lament that began at 63:7. The Christian Bible chapter-division at 63:19/64:1 is awkward — the cry *tear open the heavens and come down* is in the Hebrew text 63:19b, and the chapter division falls in the middle of a single literary unit. Most scholars treat 63:7-64:12 as one extended community-prayer.

The lament belongs to Third Isaiah (chs. 56-66). The mainstream-critical position since Bernhard Duhm's 1892 *Das Buch Jesaia* dates Trito-Isaiah to the late sixth or fifth century BC, post-exilic Yehud — the period after the Babylonian return when the temple had been rebuilt (516 BC, on a much-reduced scale) and the city slowly recovered. The unified-authorship defense (J. Alec Motyer, John Oswalt) maintains the eighth-century Isaiah ben Amoz could prophesy across centuries; Brevard Childs's canonical approach honors the layered composition while reading the canonical-final-form as authoritative. The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa-a) preserves the entire 66 chapters as a single document by 125 BC.

Westermann, Blenkinsopp, Hanson, Goldingay, Stromberg, and Tull all read 63:7-64:12 as a single lament-unit reflecting a community in crisis. The crisis-language of 64:10-11 is concrete: *your holy cities have become a wilderness; Zion has become a wilderness, Jerusalem a desolation; our holy and beautiful house, where our fathers praised you, has been burned by fire*. The reference is most plausibly to the destruction of Solomon's temple in 586 BC by the Babylonians — but the lament-form was preserved (and perhaps re-used) in the post-exilic community when the rebuilt-but-modest second temple seemed inadequate to the promised glory.

A historical complication: if the lament refers to a *burned* temple, and the second temple stood by 516 BC, the original setting may be the exile itself, before the rebuilding. The lament may have been preserved by Trito-Isaiah's circle and re-deployed in the post-exilic period as a continuing-cry of a community whose restoration felt incomplete. Blenkinsopp leans toward an exilic origin re-edited; Goldingay treats it as an authentic post-exilic lament responding to ongoing distress (perhaps the events around Nehemiah's wall-rebuilding in the mid-fifth century when the city was still vulnerable).

The specific phrasing of *all our righteous deeds are like a polluted garment* uses Hebrew *ke-veged iddim* (כְּבֶגֶד עִדִּים). The word *iddim* (עִדִּים) is widely understood by lexicographers as referring to a *menstrual* or *soiled* garment — the term carries cultic-impurity weight. Some critical translations render it *menstrual cloth* (NRSV: *like a filthy cloth*; NJPS: *like a filthy rag*; the King James reads *filthy rags*). The lament uses ritual-impurity vocabulary to describe even the community's *best* deeds. The Hebrew is uncomfortable on purpose.

The clay-and-potter metaphor of 64:8 — Hebrew *atta avinu atta yotzreinu va-anachnu ha-chomer* (אַתָּה אָבִינוּ אַתָּה יֹצְרֵנוּ וַאֲנַחְנוּ הַחֹמֶר) — *you are our Father, you are our potter, we are the clay* — reuses an image that runs through Jeremiah 18 (the potter's house), Genesis 2:7 (Yahweh forming Adam from the dust), and Isaiah 29:16. The Father-Potter combination here is unusual: Yahweh is at once parent and craftsman.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter is communal-lament poetry, the second movement of a single lament-unit running from 63:7. The literary structure of the entire prayer (63:7-64:12) is roughly:

1. Recital of mighty acts (63:7-14)
2. Petition for divine presence (63:15-19a)
3. The cry for descent (63:19b-64:3)
4. The hidden-God complaint (64:4-7)
5. The Father-Potter petition (64:8-9)
6. The ruin-description (64:10-11)
7. The closing questions (64:12)

**The cry for descent (63:19b-64:3).** *Oh that you would rend the heavens and come down*. Hebrew *lu-qarata shamayim yaradta* (לוּא קָרַעְתָּ שָׁמַיִם יָרַדְתָּ). The verb *qara* (to tear, to rend) is forceful — the same root used for tearing garments in mourning. The image is theophany-language: the mountains would quake (cf. Sinai, Exod 19), the fire would kindle the brushwood, the nations would tremble. The plea recalls past theophanies (*you came down, the mountains quaked at your presence*) and asks for them again.

**The hidden-God complaint (64:4-7).** A theological-meditation: from of old no one has heard or seen a God like Yahweh, *who acts for those who wait for him*. But then the confession: *we have sinned*. The community's sin is *long-standing* — they have been in sin a long time. The polluted-garment line lands here. The lament moves from confession (*we have all become like one who is unclean*) to despair (*there is no one who calls upon your name, who rouses himself to take hold of you*) to indictment-of-God (*for you have hidden your face from us, and have made us melt in the hand of our iniquities*). This is a lament that does not blame the people without also asking why God has hidden himself.

**The Father-Potter petition (64:8-9).** *But now, O Lord, you are our Father; we are the clay, and you are our potter*. The shift to *but now* (Hebrew *ve-atta*, וְעַתָּה) is the lament's pivot. After confession comes appeal-to-relationship: you made us; we are yours; please remember.

**The closing questions (64:12).** *Will you restrain yourself at these things, O Lord? Will you keep silent, and afflict us so terribly?* Hebrew *ha-al-eleh tit'apaq Yahweh? techeshe u-ta'aneinu ad me'od?* The lament does not end in confidence. It ends in question. The Hebrew *tit'apaq* (you will restrain yourself) and *techeshe* (you will keep silent) — two verbs naming Yahweh's *non-action*. The whole prayer has been to break Yahweh's silence, and the prayer ends without knowing whether it has succeeded.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Within the prophetic canon.** The communal-lament form is widely attested: Psalm 44, 74, 79, 80, 89; Lamentations; Habakkuk's questioning. Israel's prayer-tradition preserved the right to lament — to question Yahweh boldly without losing covenant-status. Isaiah 64 belongs to this stream and contributes specific images (the rending-heavens, the polluted-garment, the clay-and-potter) that resonate forward.

**Jewish trajectory.** Medieval Jewish commentators read the chapter as the prayer of exilic and post-exilic Israel — and, by extension, of every later generation in distress. Rashi reads the lament as ongoing Jewish reality. The Targum paraphrases the chapter into messianic-restoration vocabulary, projecting the answer to the lament forward into the messianic age.

The clay-and-potter metaphor of 64:8 became central to Jewish liturgy. The Yom Kippur evening service and selichot prayers contain a prayer that begins *ki hineh ka-chomer be-yad ha-yotzer* (*as the clay in the hand of the potter*), drawing directly on Isaiah 64 and Jeremiah 18. The piyyut (liturgical poem) compares the people to clay, stone, glass, cloth, silver, iron, anchor — each handled by the divine craftsman who shapes and reshapes. The lament-tradition — including the polluted-garment confession and the why-do-you-hide-your-face complaint — runs through Jewish penitential prayer, the Tisha B'Av lamentations, and the Holocaust-era theological literature (Eliezer Berkovits, Emil Fackenheim).

The Hebrew Bible's lament tradition resists Christian appropriation that flattens it into pre-Christian-incompleteness. Jewish tradition has continued to pray Isaiah 64 across two thousand years of additional history — the destruction of the second temple in 70 AD, expulsion from Spain in 1492, the Khmelnytsky massacres, the Shoah. The lament is not a document about the past; it is an ever-present prayer.

**Christian trajectory.** The cry *oh that you would rend the heavens and come down* (63:19b/64:1) became the central Advent prayer. Christian liturgical tradition reads the incarnation as the answer: God *did* tear open the heavens — at the baptism of Jesus, where Mark explicitly uses the verb *schizō* (*the heavens being torn open*, Mark 1:10), the same Greek word the LXX uses for *qara* in Isaiah 64:1. Mark's gospel begins by signaling that the lament's answer has arrived. The same verb returns at the cross: the temple curtain is *torn* (Mark 15:38) — the heavens-torn-open at Jesus's baptism is matched by the curtain-torn at his death.

Mainstream Christian Advent theology (Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, Anglican, Reformed liturgical traditions) reads Isaiah 64:1 as the OT lament that the New Testament gospel answers. Christmas means: the heavens *have been* torn open. The Advent hymn *O Come, O Come, Emmanuel* draws on this longing.

The clay-and-potter image enters Christian theology through Romans 9:21 — *has the potter no right over the clay, to make out of the same lump one vessel for honorable use and another for dishonorable use?* Paul's appropriation in the predestination-context of Romans 9 turns Isaiah's pastoral *we are the clay* into a more confrontational *the potter does what he wills*. The shift in tone has been controversial. Reformed theology (Calvin, Jonathan Edwards, John Piper) reads Romans 9:21 as straightforward sovereignty-language consistent with Isaiah 64:8. Catholic and Arminian readings emphasize the cooperative relational character of the OT image (where the clay *trusts* the potter) over a more deterministic reading of the Romans appropriation.

**The polluted-garment text (64:6) and Reformed total-depravity.** This is one of the most theologically loaded verses in the Hebrew Bible for Reformation theology. *All our righteous deeds are like a polluted garment*. John Calvin's *Institutes* (Book III) cites the verse as proof that even the *good works* of the unregenerate are tainted. The Heidelberg Catechism (Q. 62: *Why can our good works not be the whole or part of our righteousness before God?*) draws on the verse. The Westminster Confession (chapter XVI on good works) cites it. The Puritan and Reformed-evangelical preaching tradition has used Isaiah 64:6 as the foundation-text for the doctrine of *total depravity* — that nothing humans do, even our best, is acceptable to God apart from Christ.

Catholic theology has been more graded. The Catechism of the Catholic Church (CCC §1700-1709 on the dignity of the human person; §405-409 on the consequences of the fall) affirms that human nature is *wounded* by sin but not *totally* corrupted. The image of God remains. Good works performed by grace are genuinely good. The Catholic reading of 64:6 takes it as the lament's confession of corporate sin in a specific context — not as a comprehensive metaphysical claim about every human deed in every circumstance. The Council of Trent's *Decree on Justification* (1547) carefully distinguished between the unregenerate state and the renewed life of grace.

Eastern Orthodox theology, with its *theosis*-doctrine, reads human nature as called to participation in divine life. The polluted-garment language describes the fallen condition; baptism and the sacraments begin the restoration of the divine image.

Wesleyan-Arminian traditions (John Wesley's *The Scripture Way of Salvation*) affirm prevenient grace — God's grace working before conversion enables genuine human response. The polluted-garment language is read in confessional context, not as metaphysical-anthropology.

The exegetical question: is 64:6 a comprehensive statement about human moral capacity, or is it the lament-language of a community confessing communal-sin in a specific historical moment? The Hebrew-context reading (Westermann, Blenkinsopp, Goldingay) leans toward the latter. The Reformation-systematic reading takes the verse as warrant for a broader doctrine. Both readings have textual support; the difference is hermeneutical.

**New Testament use.** The polluted-garment imagery feeds Paul's clothing-theology (Phil 3:9 — *not having a righteousness of my own*, *Rom 3:21-26 — the righteousness of God apart from law). The clay-and-potter image returns in Romans 9:20-21 and 2 Tim 2:20-21. The cry to rend-the-heavens is answered Christologically in Mark 1:10. The Father-language (*you are our Father*) enters into the Lord's Prayer (Matt 6:9).

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The polluted-garment hermeneutic.** Whether 64:6 supports a comprehensive doctrine of total depravity or a contextual confession of communal sin shapes major theological questions. The Reformed reading produces a robust doctrine of grace — humans contribute nothing to salvation, all is gift. The Catholic-Orthodox-Wesleyan reading produces a more cooperative anthropology — grace does not annihilate nature, and human response under grace is genuinely virtuous.

Steelman the Reformed reading: the verse, taken at face-value, says *all* righteous deeds are like a polluted garment. The text does not qualify this. To read it as merely contextual is to weaken its force. Pastoral experience confirms: human moral self-righteousness is one of the most spiritually corrosive realities, and the polluted-garment language is a needed corrective.

Steelman the Catholic-Orthodox-Wesleyan reading: the verse is communal-lament confession, not a metaphysical anthropology. Read alongside Genesis 1:26-27 (humans in God's image), Psalm 8 (humans crowned with glory), Matthew 25 (the genuinely-righteous works of the sheep), and the New Testament's affirmation of cooperative grace, the verse cannot bear the entire Reformed-doctrinal weight.

A more ecumenical reading: the lament confesses that *in the present crisis*, even the community's best deeds are tainted by the sin that has brought them low. The verse is true in its context and points to a broader truth without needing to be the sole foundation of an entire systematic-theology of human nature.

**Tension 2: The hidden-face problem (64:7).** *For you have hidden your face from us, and have made us melt in the hand of our iniquities*. The verse contains a startling syntactic possibility: *you have made us melt in the hand of our iniquities*. Some translations soften this (NIV: *you have given us over to our sins*); the Hebrew *va-temugenu be-yad avoneinu* (וַתְּמוּגֵנוּ בְּיַד עֲוֺנֵנוּ) literally means *and you have melted us in the hand of our iniquities*. Yahweh has actively given the people over to be undone by their own sins.

This is the *hidden-face* (*hester panim*) doctrine in Jewish theology — the periods when God's face is hidden and his protective presence withdrawn. Jewish post-Holocaust theology (Eliezer Berkovits's *Faith After the Holocaust*, Martin Buber's *Eclipse of God*) draws on this language. The hidden-face is not divine-absence in the strong sense but the painful experience of divine non-intervention. Christian theology has parallel resources in the *deus absconditus* (hidden God) tradition — Luther especially — which acknowledges the genuine darkness of God's apparent silence.

**Tension 3: The rending-the-heavens theophany.** The plea for divine descent runs through scripture. Mark's gospel makes the bold claim that the plea is answered: at Jesus's baptism, the heavens are torn. But Christian theology has generally maintained that the *full* answer to the lament is still future — the second-Advent will be the full theophany the chapter prays for. Christian Advent-prayer therefore prays Isaiah 64 *both* in remembrance of the incarnation *and* in expectation of the parousia.

The Jewish reading-tradition continues to pray the lament forward without the Christian incarnation-fulfillment. The lament remains open. From a Christian perspective, this is the ongoing question of Jewish-Christian dialogue: are we praying for the *first* coming or the *second*, and what does it mean that one community has placed the dividing-line at Christmas while the other has not?

**Tension 4: The closing questions (64:12).** The lament ends in interrogation, not assurance. *Will you restrain yourself, O Lord? Will you keep silent?* The chapter does not give an answer. The next chapter (Isaiah 65) opens with what some commentators read as *Yahweh's reply* to the lament — *I was ready to be sought by those who did not ask for me; I was ready to be found by those who did not seek me*. But the answer in chapter 65 is partial and surprising; it does not match the lament-question one-to-one.

The lament's open-ending is theologically important. Hebrew prayer-tradition refuses to close the door on divine-mystery. The community can pray, confess, recite, plead — and still not know whether the answer will come. This is not faithlessness; it is faith in its lament-form.

**Where this leaves us.** Isaiah 64 is a chapter of unresolved cries. The community recites Yahweh's mighty acts, confesses sin in extreme language, claims the Father-Potter relationship, and ends without knowing whether God will speak. The Christian Advent tradition reads the chapter as fulfilled (the heavens have been torn) and as ongoing (we still pray for the parousia). The Jewish tradition reads it as the perpetual prayer of a people who have known many silences. The Reformation reads it as the foundational confession of human moral inadequacy. The Catholic and Orthodox traditions hold the confession in tension with the divine image still bearing in human persons.

The chapter teaches us that lament is not faithlessness. The community that prays *will you keep silent?* is the community still praying — still calling Yahweh *Father*, still claiming the potter-clay relationship, still expecting the heavens to open.

**Closing question.** When the polluted-garment confession is over and the clay has called out to the potter, when the cry for the heavens to tear has been spoken into a sky that does not crack — what are we supposed to do with the silence between the question and the answer? The chapter ends without telling us. Perhaps that silence is itself part of the prayer.
