# John 2: Cana and the Cleansing — Wine, Body, Temple

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

A Galilean wedding in the late 20s of the first century is a multi-day, village-wide affair. Mediterranean honor-shame culture, on which Kenneth Bailey, Bruce Malina, and Jerome Neyrey have written extensively, makes the running-out of wine in 2:3 not a minor inconvenience but a public catastrophe for the bridegroom's family. Wine in a Mediterranean village wedding is a marker of hospitality, abundance, and patronal honor; its failure shames the host's lineage and would be remembered for generations. Craig Keener's two-volume commentary catalogs the rabbinic, papyrological, and ethnographic evidence: weddings ran seven days (cf. Tobit 11:18); the bridegroom and his family bore the cost; the *symposiarchos* or "master of the feast" (*architriklinos*, ἀρχιτρίκλινος) supervised the wine. Six stone jars holding twenty or thirty gallons each (2:6) — between 120 and 180 gallons total, conservatively 450 to 680 liters — would be far in excess of any normal village wedding's needs, and the *stone* material is technically required: Mishnah *Kelim* 10:1 specifies that stone vessels (unlike clay or wood) do not contract ritual impurity, which is why purification jars are stone.

**Cana** itself is contested. The traditional pilgrimage site Kafr Kanna, on the road from Nazareth to Tiberias, has hosted a Franciscan wedding-church since the seventeenth century, but its archaeological case is weak. **Khirbet Qana**, nine kilometers north of Sepphoris in the Beit Netofa valley, is the more probable site. Douglas Edwards led excavations there from 1998, and Tom McCollough continued the work; they uncovered a first-century Jewish village with mikvaot, stone vessels (significantly), a synagogue, and Byzantine-era pilgrim graffiti in a cave-shrine that explicitly identifies the site as the wedding location. Most contemporary archaeology of Galilee — Jonathan Reed, Mordechai Aviam — accepts Khirbet Qana as the more probable identification.

The chapter's other major archaeological setting is the **Jerusalem Temple** (2:13-22). The reference in 2:20 — "It has taken forty-six years to build this temple" — gives us a chronological anchor. Herod the Great began the Temple reconstruction in roughly 20-19 BCE according to Josephus (*Antiquities* 15.380); forty-six years from that point puts Jesus's first Passover at roughly 27-28 CE, broadly consistent with a ministry beginning around that time. The Temple complex covered approximately 36 acres (the Temple Mount platform Herod created remains the largest such platform in the ancient world); Leen Ritmeyer's archaeological reconstructions and the Jerusalem Archaeological Park (Mazar excavations from the 1960s onward) have substantially clarified its layout. The "money-changers" (*kollybistai*, κολλυβισταί) and the sellers of sacrificial animals would have operated in the Court of the Gentiles, the outermost precinct, where Robinson's Arch and the Hulda Gate steps brought pilgrims into the Temple complex. The half-shekel Temple tax (Exod 30:13; Mishnah *Sheqalim* 1:3) had to be paid in Tyrian shekels (because of their silver purity), so money-changing was operationally necessary; the question Jesus raises is not the necessity of the trade but its location and spirit.

Greek terms structure the chapter. ***Sēmeion*** (σημεῖον, "sign") in 2:11 is the Johannine programmatic term for Jesus's revelatory miracles — Brown counts seven of them. ***Doxa*** (δόξα, "glory") in 2:11 — "manifested his glory" — picks up 1:14. ***Hōra*** (ὥρα, "hour") in 2:4 — "my hour has not yet come" — is a theological term in John for the moment of crucifixion-glorification; it recurs in 7:30, 8:20, 12:23, 13:1, 17:1. ***Naos*** (ναός, "sanctuary, temple-shrine") in 2:19-21 is distinct from the broader *hieron* (ἱερόν, the whole Temple complex) used in 2:14 — Jesus speaks of the inner sanctuary, then the narrator translates: "he was speaking about the temple of his body" (*peri tou naou tou sōmatos autou*).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter contains two of the Gospel's most distinctive units: **the first sign** (2:1-11) and **the Temple cleansing** (2:13-22). Both are foundational for Brown's seven-signs schema and for the Johannine literary structure that Bultmann, Dodd, and Brown all foundationally identified.

The Cana narrative is told with extraordinary economy. The opening — "On the third day" (2:1) — has been read by Brown and Schnackenburg as the climax of an opening week (a creation-week structure: 1:19, 1:29, 1:35, 1:43 mark four sequential days; "the third day" from the last marker brings us to day seven), echoing Genesis 1's seven-day creation. R. Alan Culpepper's *Anatomy of the Fourth Gospel* and Mark Stibbe's narratological readings press this further: John's narrative architecture is rigorously deliberate. The Cana sign is presented as inaugural — "*archē tōn sēmeiōn*," "the first of his signs" (2:11) — and the second sign (4:46-54) returns to Cana, framing the entire sequence between two Cana signs, what Brown calls the "Cana to Cana" structure of John 2-4.

The mother of Jesus is unnamed — she appears only as "his mother" or "woman" (*gynai*, γύναι, 2:4). Brown's *Anchor Bible* commentary works through the *gynai* address with care: it is not disrespectful in koine Greek (Jesus uses it again from the cross to his mother in 19:26), but it is unusual for a son to address his mother this way. Brown reads it as deliberate: Mary is being relocated from biological-maternal relation to discipleship. The Catholic tradition has read 2:1-11 as the foundation for Marian intercession (Mary brings the need to Jesus; Jesus responds despite his initial demurral); Aquinas, the medieval mariological tradition, and *Lumen Gentium* §58 of Vatican II all treat Cana as a mariological keystone. Reformed and Reformation readings have often read the *gynai* address as Jesus distancing himself from a Marian mediation theology. The text under-determines both readings.

The Temple cleansing's **chronological displacement** is the most consequential source-critical question in the chapter. Mark, Matthew, and Luke all locate the cleansing in Passion week, immediately before the crucifixion (Mark 11:15-19 and parallels). John locates it at Jesus's first Passover, three years before the crucifixion. The harmonization options:

(1) **Two cleansings**: Jesus cleansed the Temple twice — once at the start of his ministry, once at the end. D.A. Carson's *PNTC*, Köstenberger's *BECNT*, and Leon Morris all defend this reading, citing the differences in detail (the whip of cords appears only in John; the prophetic citation differs).

(2) **One event, Johannine relocation**: The synoptics preserve the historical chronology; John has relocated the cleansing for theological-narrative reasons, placing it programmatically at the beginning of Jesus's public ministry as a signal that Jesus's mission is to replace the Temple. Raymond Brown, Bultmann, Keener, Beasley-Murray, and the bulk of historical-critical scholarship adopt this reading. The narrative function is clear: by moving the cleansing forward, John makes explicit from the start the claim that the synoptics develop slowly: "destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up" (2:19), which the narrator immediately glosses (2:21) as referring to Jesus's body.

(3) **One event, synoptic relocation**: A minority position holding that John preserves the historical chronology and the synoptics have telescoped the cleansing into Passion week.

Most contemporary critical commentators favor (2), but the evangelical commentary tradition (Carson, Köstenberger, Morris) defends (1).

The **misunderstanding** literary device — programmatic in John — appears here in 2:19-22. Jesus's interlocutors hear *naos* literally; the narrator unveils the figurative meaning. This is the same device that will structure the Nicodemus dialogue in chapter 3 (*anōthen* — born "again" or "from above") and the Samaritan-woman dialogue in chapter 4 (living water as flowing water or as eschatological gift). Culpepper, Brown, and Mark Stibbe all treat misunderstanding as a hermeneutical key to the Fourth Gospel.

Textual variants in the chapter are minor. The most consequential is the addition of the bridegroom's reply in some Western witnesses, but the *editio critica maior* and NA28 follow the shorter, more difficult Alexandrian text.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Cana sign sits at the convergence of multiple Jewish and Christian interpretive trajectories.

**Jewish trajectory.** Wine and abundance in Jewish messianic expectation is a deep tradition. **Amos 9:13** ("the mountains shall drip sweet wine"), **Isaiah 25:6** ("on this mountain the LORD of hosts will make for all peoples a feast of rich food, a feast of well-aged wine"), **Joel 3:18**, and **2 Baruch 29:5** (in which the messianic age is described as a time when "each vine shall have a thousand branches, and each branch shall produce a thousand clusters") all establish a wine-of-the-messianic-age tradition. Daniel Boyarin's *Jewish Gospels* and Marianne Meye Thompson's *NTL* both develop the connection: Cana is not a magic trick but a messianic acted parable — the inauguration of the eschatological banquet. The six stone purification jars (2:6) carry a particular weight in this reading: the water of Jewish ritual purification is transformed into the wine of messianic celebration. Adele Reinhartz's caution applies here: this transformation can be (and has been) read supersessionistically — Christianity replaces Judaism — but it can also be read as Boyarin reads it: the eschatological fulfillment of Jewish hope from within Jewish theological imagination.

The Temple cleansing's theological background is **Zechariah 14:21** ("there shall no longer be a trader in the house of the LORD of hosts on that day") and **Malachi 3:1-3** ("the Lord whom you seek will suddenly come to his temple... he will purify the descendants of Levi"). The disciples remember Psalm 69:9 ("zeal for your house will consume me," John 2:17). N.T. Wright's *Jesus and the Victory of God* reads the cleansing as a prophetic acted-parable of judgment — not primarily a "purification" of trade but an announcement that the Temple, in its current form, is under divine judgment. E.P. Sanders's *Jesus and Judaism* (1985) was foundational here: Sanders argued the cleansing was *the* historical action that triggered Jesus's arrest, an enacted prophecy of Temple destruction.

**Christian patristic trajectory.** **Origen's** *Commentary on John* (book 10 treats the cleansing) reads the Temple as the soul into which Christ enters to drive out unworthy thoughts; this allegorical reading sets the template for medieval mystical interpretation. **Augustine's** *Tractates on the Gospel of John* (tractate 10 treats Cana) reads the six water jars as the six ages of the world before Christ; the wine is the gospel's eschatological completion of biblical history. **John Chrysostom's** *Homilies on John* (homilies 21-23) treats Cana with characteristic pastoral attention to the *gynai* address and Jesus's apparent demurral. **Cyril of Alexandria's** *Commentary on John* makes Cana a Christological keystone — the divine Logos's authority over creation manifest in the transformation. **Aquinas's** *Lectura super Joannem* synthesizes the patristic readings.

**Reformation.** **Calvin's *Commentary on John*** reads Cana as an attestation of Jesus's divine authority and a defense of marriage as honorable (against monastic-celibate readings). Calvin is suspicious of the mariological reading and reads the *gynai* address as Jesus's distancing of himself from any maternal claim on his ministry. Calvin on the cleansing reads the Temple as God's house defiled and Jesus's action as restoration — a reading that played a substantial role in Reformed iconoclasm and the cleansing of Catholic worship.

**Mystical and devotional.** **Bernard of Clairvaux** preached on Cana repeatedly — the wine as the transformation of the affections by Christ's love. **Eckhart** read the six jars as the six powers of the soul filled by grace. **Teresa of Avila** and **John of the Cross** read Cana as the mystical marriage of the soul with Christ.

**Twentieth century commentaries.** Brown, Barrett, Bultmann, Dodd, Schnackenburg, Beasley-Murray, Köstenberger, Carson, Keener, Thompson, Moloney, Brant, O'Day, Newbigin all treat both episodes at length. **Bauckham's** *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* notes the unusually specific detail of "six" jars and Cana's precise location as eyewitness markers. **Lesslie Newbigin's** *The Light Has Come* reads Cana as the inauguration of the new creation — wine of the messianic age — and the Temple cleansing as the announcement that the body of Christ replaces the locative shrine, a theme central to Newbigin's missional ecclesiology.

**Liberation and feminist trajectory.** **Sandra Schneiders's** *Written That You May Believe* reads Cana with attention to the mother of Jesus as a model disciple ("do whatever he tells you," 2:5) — the first character in the Gospel to enact the response that John commends. Jon Sobrino and Gustavo Gutiérrez read the Temple cleansing as a paradigm of prophetic critique of religious commerce — Jesus's action as solidarity with the poor pilgrims being economically exploited at the Temple gates.

**Mediterranean cultural reading.** **Kenneth Bailey's** work on Mediterranean honor-shame culture reads Cana through the lens of the bridegroom's threatened public dishonor: Jesus's sign is a *socially* salvific act, restoring the bridegroom's honor by anonymously providing the missing wine. The master of the feast praises the bridegroom (2:9-10), not knowing the source — which preserves the host's honor while revealing Jesus's glory only to those who know the secret (2:11: "his disciples believed in him").

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions structure the chapter.

**Tension one: what is a "sign," and what does belief based on signs mean?** The chapter ends with a striking note (2:23-25): "many believed in his name when they saw the signs that he was doing. But Jesus on his part did not entrust himself to them" — the same verb in both clauses (*pisteuō*, πιστεύω). The Greek pun is exact: people *believed in* Jesus, but Jesus did not *believe in / entrust himself to* them. **Catholic** and **Eastern Orthodox** sacramental theology has often read the seven signs as foundational for sacramental imagination — sign and reality, water and wine, bread and body, all connected through real-symbolic transformation; *Sacrosanctum Concilium* and *CCC* 1145-1152 develop this. **Reformed** theology, especially in the line from Calvin through Bavinck and Vanhoozer, reads signs as *attestations* of Jesus's identity but warns against a "sign-based" faith that demands ongoing spectacle (2:24-25 as the cautionary note). **Pentecostal/Charismatic** theology reads the signs as paradigmatic of the Spirit's continuing work — every sign in John 2-11 points forward to the church's signs-and-wonders ministry. **Wesleyan** theology emphasizes the sign as occasion for the sanctifying transformation of the heart (the wine as figure for the transformation of the affections). **Bultmann** reduced the signs to ciphers for existential decision — the historical event evaporates, but the kerygmatic call remains. **Käsemann** read the signs as evidence of Johannine "naive docetism" — the divine Logos doing god-like things. Steelmanned, the sacramental reading takes seriously what John 2:11 says: the sign *manifested his glory* and his disciples *believed*. Steelmanned, the Reformed cautionary reading takes seriously what 2:24-25 says: faith based only on spectacle is faith Jesus does not trust. Steelmanned, the Pentecostal reading takes seriously that the signs are normatively presented as a sequence to be continued in the church's mission.

*Where this leaves us:* the Johannine sign is neither raw miracle nor mere symbol but revelatory action whose meaning requires the disciples' interpretive framework. Faith that responds *only* to spectacle is precarious; faith that recognizes the sign *as* sign — as glory manifest in flesh — is the response the Gospel commends.

**Tension two: what is the Temple now?** The cleansing-followed-by-the-saying-about-the-body (2:19-22) is the Gospel's first programmatic claim that Jesus's body replaces the Jerusalem Temple as the locus of divine presence. The interpretive options span the tradition:

The **Catholic** tradition reads this Christologically and ecclesiologically: Jesus's body is the new Temple, and the Church as the body of Christ continues this Temple-presence in time and space; the Eucharist is the sacrificial center of this new Temple. The **Eastern Orthodox** tradition reads similarly but with deeper emphasis on the iconographic and liturgical continuity — the Temple's furniture (the veil, the holy of holies, the incense) is fulfilled and transposed in the Divine Liturgy. The **Reformed** tradition reads more spiritually: the new Temple is Christ's body; access is by faith; locative shrine-religion is over. **Lutheran** theology, with its strong real-presence Eucharistic theology, holds together the Temple-replacement claim with sacramental embodiment. **Wesleyan** theology focuses on the inner Temple — the believer's body as the temple of the Holy Spirit (1 Cor 6:19), continuous with John 2:21. **Liberation theology** (Jon Sobrino, Sobrino's *Christ the Liberator*) reads the cleansing as the foundational political-theological act: God's presence is no longer in a hierarchically controlled shrine but in the body of the suffering servant, and by extension in the bodies of the suffering poor. **Feminist theology** (Schneiders, Elizabeth Johnson) reads the Temple-replacement as theologically liberating from purity codes that systematically excluded women from the inner courts — though Reinhartz's caution applies: this can also tip into supersessionism. **Jewish readings** (Boyarin, Reinhartz) note the painful afterlife of this passage in Christian anti-Judaism: "house of trade" (2:16) became Christian polemic against Jewish religious life as such. After 70 CE, the historical Temple is gone; rabbinic Judaism developed its own theology of post-Temple presence (Torah-study and prayer as functional substitutes for sacrifice; the Shekhinah as accompanying Israel into exile, b. Megillah 29a); Christianity claimed the Temple-fulfillment language. Both communities, Boyarin argues, are responding to the same trauma with different theological imaginations.

*Where this leaves us:* the Temple-replacement claim is irreducibly Christological in John and has been read responsibly *and* irresponsibly across Christian history. The text says what it says — Jesus's body is the new Temple — but how Christians have used that claim against living Jewish neighbors is not the text's fault and not its inevitability.

**Unresolved question:** if Jesus's body is the new Temple, and the Church is the body of Christ, what is the moral and theological status of Christian sacred-space practice — pilgrimage, church buildings, holy sites, including the very Khirbet Qana commemorations and Holy Sepulchre archaeology that ground this very chapter's history? The Reformed iconoclastic answer is one thing; the Eastern Orthodox sacred-space theology is another; the Quaker simplicity tradition another still. The question lives in the text without resolution.

## The Thread

The Cana sign reaches back to the messianic-banquet imagery of Isaiah 25 and the wedding-language of Hosea 2 and forward to the wedding feast of the Lamb in Revelation 19:7-9. The "my hour has not yet come" of 2:4 is the first occurrence of a programmatic Johannine motif that climaxes in 12:23, 13:1, 17:1 — the hour of glorification on the cross. The Temple cleansing reaches forward to John 4:21-24 (worship neither on Mount Gerizim nor in Jerusalem) and to the sustained Temple-replacement Christology of John 7-10, where Jesus appropriates the Temple festivals (Tabernacles, Dedication) to himself. The "destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up" (2:19) anticipates the resurrection narrative of chapter 20 and is echoed in the false-witness charges at Jesus's trial (Mark 14:58 and parallels — though John omits this charge at the trial itself, having already disclosed its meaning here in chapter 2). Watch for the recurring water imagery (2:6 jars; 3:5 born of water; 4:14 living water; 5:7 pool of Bethesda; 7:38 rivers of living water; 9:7 pool of Siloam; 13:5 footwashing; 19:34 blood and water from his side) — water is the chapter-spanning matter that the Logos transforms.
