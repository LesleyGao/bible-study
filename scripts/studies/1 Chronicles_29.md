# 1 Chronicles 29 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

This is the chapter that closes 1 Chronicles, and the Chronicler invests it with theological weight that no chapter since the Davidic covenant in 1 Chronicles 17 has carried. Four movements unfold in sequence: David's personal gift and the freewill offerings of the leaders (verses 1–9), David's great prayer (verses 10–19), Solomon's enthronement and the kingdom's transition (verses 20–25), and the Chronicler's editorial closing on David's reign (verses 26–30).

The opening movement places the assembly of chapter 28 still in session. David addresses them again, this time with a financial appeal. The Hebrew is striking: he calls the temple a *bira* (בִּירָה), translated "palace" — a Persian-period loanword (from Old Persian *bârā*, "fortress, citadel") that the Chronicler uses three times here (verses 1, 19) and that occurs in Nehemiah 1:1, 2:8 of the Persian governor's residence. Sara Japhet flags this as one of the clearest linguistic markers of the Chronicler's late post-exilic milieu: he reaches for the Persian-administrative word for a citadel-residence to describe the temple, because for him the temple *is* Yahweh's royal residence.

David then announces personal donations on a staggering scale: 3,000 talents of gold of Ophir and 7,000 talents of refined silver (verse 4). At ancient near-eastern weights this is roughly 100 tons of gold and 240 tons of silver — an obviously stylized figure. Hugh Williamson and Gary Knoppers both treat the numbers as theological-rhetorical rather than auditable. Their function is to establish a baseline of royal generosity that the leaders are then invited to match. The leaders give 5,000 talents and 10,000 darics of gold (the *daric*, Hebrew *adarkonim*, אֲדַרְכֹּנִים — another Persian-period coin, anachronistic for David's reign but native to the Chronicler's), 10,000 talents of silver, 18,000 talents of bronze, 100,000 talents of iron, plus precious stones (verses 7–8).

Verse 9 captures the theological point: *ki be-lev shalem hitnaddevu la-Yahweh* (כִּי בְּלֵב שָׁלֵם הִתְנַדְּבוּ לַיהוָה, "for with a whole heart they had offered freely to the LORD"). The verb *nadav* (נָדַב, "to offer willingly, freely") and the noun *nedavah* (נְדָבָה, "freewill offering") run through the temple-funding passages of Exodus 25:2 and 35:21–29 as well. The Chronicler is drawing the same parallel he drew with the *tavnit* in chapter 28: as Moses's tabernacle was funded by Israel's freewill offering, so David's temple is funded by the same kind of voluntary, whole-hearted devotion. Steven McKenzie reads this as a deliberate liturgical doublet — the temple has the same theological pedigree as the tabernacle, including its method of finance.

The second movement is David's prayer (verses 10–19), one of the most exalted prayers in the Hebrew Bible. *Yours, O LORD, is the greatness and the power and the glory and the victory and the majesty* (verse 11). The Hebrew piles five attributes: *ha-gedullah ve-ha-gevurah ve-ha-tiferet ve-ha-netzach ve-ha-hod* (הַגְּדֻלָּה וְהַגְּבוּרָה וְהַתִּפְאֶרֶת וְהַנֵּצַח וְהַהוֹד). These five terms appear together nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible. The prayer continues: *for all that is in the heavens and in the earth is yours; yours is the kingdom, O LORD, and you are exalted as head above all*. Hebrew *lekha Yahweh ha-mamlakhah ve-ha-mitnasse le-khol le-rosh* (לְךָ יְהוָה הַמַּמְלָכָה וְהַמִּתְנַשֵּׂא לְכֹל לְרֹאשׁ). The kingdom is *Yahweh's*. David, having sat on what the Chronicler calls "the throne of the LORD" (28:5; 29:23), now confesses publicly that the throne, the kingdom, and the glory belong entirely to God.

Verse 14 turns from doxology to humility: *but who am I, and what is my people, that we should be able thus to offer willingly? For all things come from you, and of your own have we given you* (Hebrew *ki-mimekha ha-kol u-mi-yadkha natanu lakh*, כִּי מִמְּךָ הַכֹּל וּמִיָּדְךָ נָתַנּוּ לָךְ). The grammar is precise: everything is from Yahweh, and what we have given is *from your hand* — already yours before we offered it. Mark Boda has called this verse the theological center of the chapter: even the most lavish gift to God is itself a gift *from* God being returned.

Verse 15 is one of the great pilgrim verses of the Hebrew Bible: *we are strangers before you and sojourners, as all our fathers were. Our days on the earth are like a shadow, and there is no abiding*. The Hebrew *gerim ve-toshavim* (גֵּרִים וְתוֹשָׁבִים, "strangers and sojourners") echoes Abraham's self-description at Sarah's burial (Genesis 23:4) and Leviticus 25:23, where Yahweh says, "the land is mine; for you are strangers and sojourners with me." That David, the king at the height of his administrative power, with the entire kingdom assembled before him and tons of gold piled at his feet, confesses *gerim ve-toshavim* — that is the Chronicler's most piercing theological move in the whole book. The polity matters; the polity is also temporary.

The prayer's intercession (verse 19) prays for Solomon: *grant to Solomon my son a whole heart* (*lev shalem*, לֵב שָׁלֵם, the same phrase from verse 9). Roddy Braun and Williamson note that this is the prayer the Chronicler will eventually answer with mixed verdicts — Solomon will build the temple but, in the longer Deuteronomistic memory, will not keep his heart whole. Chronicles itself softens that arc considerably (2 Chronicles 1–9 omits much of Solomon's later apostasy from 1 Kings 11), but verse 19 is asking for something the historical record will only partly grant.

The third movement narrates Solomon's enthronement. Verse 22 says they made Solomon king *shenit* (שֵׁנִית, "a second time"). This is the Chronicler's only acknowledgment that there was an earlier accession event. Whether he is harmonizing implicitly with 1 Kings 1 or marking that the brief private anointing of Kings is here ratified publicly is debated. Knoppers reads it as a tacit canonical acknowledgment. Verse 23 then states what the Chronicler has been building toward: *Solomon sat on the throne of Yahweh as king* (*kisseh Yahweh*, כִּסֵּא יְהוָה). The throne is Yahweh's. The king is regent.

The fourth movement is the closing summary. David reigned forty years, seven in Hebron and thirty-three in Jerusalem. He died "at a good age, full of days, riches, and honor" (verse 28). The Chronicler refers the reader to three sources: "the Chronicles of Samuel the seer, and the Chronicles of Nathan the prophet, and the Chronicles of Gad the seer" (verse 29). These are documentary sources the Chronicler claims to be drawing on; whether they are real archives or stylized references to the Samuel-Kings tradition (which the Chronicler clearly does have access to) is a long-standing debate among Steven McKenzie, Knoppers, and Williamson.

## Lens 2: The Literature

The chapter is a composite of four genres in tight sequence: speech of appeal (verses 1–5), narrative of communal response (verses 6–9), prayer (verses 10–19), and enthronement-and-closing-formula (verses 20–30). The prayer is the literary peak of the entire book of 1 Chronicles. It is composed in elevated parallelism, draws on language from Psalms (compare Psalms 8, 24, 145), and reaches a height of sustained doxology that the Chronicler does not match anywhere else. Mark Boda calls it the "theological climax" of 1 Chronicles, and the placement at the very end of the book signals that everything in the preceding twenty-eight chapters — genealogies, ark narratives, military campaigns, administrative rosters, succession scene — has been moving toward this confession: the kingdom is Yahweh's. Once that confession is offered, the book can close. David can die.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

The chapter has the most consequential New Testament afterlife of any passage in 1 Chronicles. The doxology of verse 11 — *yours is the kingdom and the power and the glory* — is the textual basis for the doxological ending of the Lord's Prayer that appears in some manuscripts of Matthew 6:13 ("for thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, forever, Amen"). The history is worth being honest about. Bruce Metzger, in *The Text of the New Testament: Its Transmission, Corruption, and Restoration*, lays out the textual evidence: the doxology is absent from the earliest and best Greek manuscripts of Matthew (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus), absent from the parallel in Luke 11:2–4, and is widely judged by modern critical scholarship to be a *liturgical accretion* added in early Christian worship and eventually copied into the gospel manuscripts. The Textus Receptus, Erasmus's compiled Greek text from which the Geneva Bible and the King James Version translated, retains it. Modern critical editions (Nestle-Aland, UBS) print Matthew 6:13 without it or in brackets.

The substantive source of that liturgical doxology, however, is unmistakably 1 Chronicles 29:11. Early Christian worshipers ending the Lord's Prayer with "for thine is the kingdom, the power, and the glory" were, whether they knew it or not, citing David's prayer at the close of his reign. The Didache (chapter 8, late first or early second century) already has the doxology in the Lord's Prayer, evidence that this liturgical pairing is very old. So the historical reality is layered: the doxology is *not* original to Jesus's words as recorded in Matthew, but it *is* deeply biblical, drawn from a Davidic prayer that the synagogue and the early church both knew. The pastoral question — whether to say it, write it, sing it — is therefore not "is it scripture?" (1 Chronicles 29:11 is) but "is it Matthew?" (probably not).

The "strangers and sojourners" motif of verse 15 has its own afterlife. Hebrews 11:13–16 takes it up explicitly, applying it to the patriarchs: "they acknowledged that they were strangers and exiles on the earth... they desired a better country, that is, a heavenly one." 1 Peter 1:1 addresses its readers as *parepidēmois* ("sojourners") and 1 Peter 2:11 calls them *paroikous kai parepidēmous* ("sojourners and exiles") — the Greek translates the Hebrew *gerim ve-toshavim* directly. Early Christian self-understanding as *paroikia* (the source of our word "parish" — a community of resident aliens) is rooted in this verse, transmitted through Hebrews and 1 Peter. When the church called itself a *paroikia* it was, knowingly or not, situating itself in David's prayer.

Robert Wilson and others have made the canonical-shape observation that 1–2 Chronicles function as the *closing book* of the Hebrew canon in the order preserved by the Babylonian Talmud and most Jewish Bibles (Ketuvim, with 1–2 Chronicles last). The Hebrew Bible therefore opens with Adam (1 Chronicles 1) and closes with Cyrus's edict permitting the exiles to return (2 Chronicles 36:23). Both endpoints — Adam and the return — are moments of beginning. The canonical shape of the Hebrew Bible is therefore not a story that ends in conclusion but a story that closes by opening outward, and 1 Chronicles 29 sits very near the heart of that shape: David's prayer of *gerim ve-toshavim* is the theological idiom in which the post-exilic community is being asked to live.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The Chronicler's closing agenda is to seal his theological vision: David's reign was a transient stewardship of a kingdom that belongs to Yahweh, the temple is funded by freewill offering returned from God's own gifts, the people of God are *gerim ve-toshavim*, Solomon sits on Yahweh's throne, and the post-exilic community inherits this entire framework. Three tensions deserve careful naming.

**The first tension: the Lord's Prayer doxology.** What does it mean that the most-recited prayer in Christian history ends with words probably not original to Jesus's lips, drawn substantively from David's prayer at the close of 1 Chronicles?

**Position 1: KJV-only / Textus Receptus traditionalism.** The doxology is part of received scripture as it has been read in English-speaking Christianity for four centuries, and removing it amounts to a sectarian tampering with what God has providentially preserved. On this view, the doxology is Matthew, full stop.

**Position 2: Mainstream critical-text view.** The doxology is not original to Matthew. It is a liturgical accretion. Honest text-critical practice prints it in brackets or in a footnote. This is the position of nearly all modern Bible translation committees.

**Position 3: Liturgical-canonical reading.** The doxology is biblical (1 Chronicles 29:11), ancient (Didache 8), beloved, and has shaped Christian worship for nearly two millennia. The pastoral and liturgical practice of saying it is therefore not corrupt; it is canonical in a different sense — it joins Jesus's prayer to David's prayer in a way the Holy Spirit appears to have blessed across centuries. Brevard Childs and many liturgical theologians take this view.

**Steelman of Position 3.** The strongest case is that early Christian worship, including the Didache community, was already saturated with the Hebrew Scriptures. When they ended Jesus's prayer with David's doxology, they were performing a deeply intra-canonical act — recognizing that Jesus's "your kingdom come" finds its native home in the prayer of the king who first publicly confessed the kingdom is *Yahweh's*. The accretion is not an error; it is a recognition. To strip the doxology in the name of textual purity is to amputate a theological gesture that connects Jesus to David in exactly the way the New Testament repeatedly does.

**Where this leaves us on this tension.** The honest pastoral answer is to say the doxology when praying, knowing it is liturgical not Matthean, and to teach its origin in 1 Chronicles 29:11. The text-critical fact does not have to undo the worshiping practice; it can illuminate it.

**The second tension: pilgrim ecclesiology.** Verse 15 — *we are strangers and sojourners* — has carried Christian self-understanding through every period when the church has been politically vulnerable, and it has been quietly forgotten in every period when the church has been politically comfortable. The Pauline-Petrine *paroikia* tradition takes the verse as paradigmatic: the church is always sojourning. But Christian establishment from Constantine forward has often muted the verse, treating it as applicable only to martyr-eras.

**Position 1: Establishment Christianity** — the long medieval and early modern arrangement in which the church holds civil authority — softens the *gerim ve-toshavim* claim. Yes, every Christian dies and goes to a heavenly country, but in the meantime the church builds cathedrals and runs states.

**Position 2: Anabaptist-pilgrim ecclesiology** — Hauerwas, Yoder, the longer Mennonite tradition, with roots in patristic *paroikia* — insists that the verse names the church's permanent posture, not just an interim one. The church is *gerim ve-toshavim* always, and arrangements that obscure that fact are spiritually corrosive.

**Position 3: Augustinian two-cities reading.** The City of God and the City of Man both run through history. Christians live in both. The verse names the truth that our citizenship is finally elsewhere, but it does not require withdrawal; it requires a clear-eyed dual loyalty in which the deeper loyalty is to the heavenly city.

**Steelman of Position 2.** The strongest case for the strict pilgrim ecclesiology is that the New Testament writers — themselves working with 1 Chronicles 29:15 in their bones — describe the church consistently as sojourning. Hebrews 11 and 13:14 ("here we have no lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come"). 1 Peter 1:1 and 2:11. The Christian establishment patterns of later centuries, however productive in some ways, are deviations from the New Testament's native idiom. The verse is naming a truth about the church's location in time that no political success can erase.

**The third tension: the canonical shape itself.** What does it mean that the Hebrew Bible (in its Tanakh ordering) closes with the Chronicler's idealized founding-narrative and Cyrus's permission to return? The post-exilic community is being handed a story whose closing note is not exilic lament but a return-edict, framed by an idealized memory of Davidic kingship. Robert Wilson and others have noted how unusual this is. The Bible does not end where its history-line ends. It ends earlier, at the edict of return, even though the post-exilic community already knows what happened next — modest second temple, Persian then Greek then Roman overlords, no Davidic king. The canonical shape is making a claim: the *meaningful* terminus is the moment of return, not the disappointing reality that followed.

For a post-exilic Yehud reader, the message is implicit but powerful: the kingdom you do not have is still Yahweh's; the temple you are building, modest though it is, is still funded by the freewill offering of God's people; you are *gerim ve-toshavim* in your own land, but so was David at the height of his glory; the Davidic line is dormant but its theology — Yahweh's kingdom, Yahweh's throne, Yahweh's house — is fully alive.

For a Christian reader, the same canonical shape acquires another layer. Jesus inherits the unfinished business of the Chronicler's vision. He is the son of David who actually does sit on the throne of Yahweh, the king who builds a true temple (John 2:19–22), the Solomon-figure who gives true rest (Hebrews 4:8–11). The early church's ending the Lord's Prayer with David's doxology is the liturgical recognition of that connection.

**Honest reckoning with the Chronicler's project.** It is fair to ask whether the Chronicler's idealizing impulse is a kind of pious fiction, papering over the messy reality of Israel's actual history. Williamson, Japhet, Knoppers, and McKenzie are all clear that the Chronicler is not writing what we would call objective history; he is writing theological historiography for a particular post-exilic audience. The question for the contemporary reader, Jewish or Christian, is whether that theological historiography is honest enough to be useful. The chapter answers in two ways. First, with the *gerim ve-toshavim* confession of verse 15: the Chronicler's most exalted moment is also his most humble, and a reader who notices verse 15 cannot accuse the Chronicler of triumphalism without missing his point. Second, with verse 14's *for all things come from you, and of your own have we given you*: the Chronicler explicitly attributes every gift back to God. Whatever idealization is happening in the founding-narrative, the theology of giving and receiving is realist and self-aware.

**Where this leaves us.** The book closes with David alive in prayer, then Solomon enthroned, then David dead "at a good age, full of days, riches, and honor," and finally a footnote pointing to other chronicles. The Chronicler does not end with triumph; he ends with a citation. The story he has told is one chapter in a larger record. The post-exilic reader is being told: live in the unfinished business of this story. Worship the God whose kingdom this is. Give back from what was already his. Confess your sojourner status. And know that the temple, the kingdom, and the throne all belong to Yahweh, who outlives every king who has ever sat on it.

**Closing question.** If you knew that the most exalted thing you said in your life was the prayer in which you confessed yourself a sojourner, what would change about how you spent the parts that were not the prayer? David's day of greatest wealth, greatest authority, and greatest public glory was the day he stood up and said: I am a stranger, and the gold I am giving was already yours. Most of us do not have his platform. None of us have a different theology of giving available to us.

## The Thread

David ends his reign on his feet, with a kingdom assembled around him, gold piled at his feet, and his own death close at hand — and what he says is that the kingdom and the gold and the breath are Yahweh's, that he and his people are *gerim ve-toshavim*, and that the next king will sit on a throne that was never David's to begin with. The book closes; the post-exilic community inherits the prayer; the early church will end Jesus's prayer with David's words. The Chronicler has finished his first volume. The throne is still Yahweh's.
