# Hebrews 13 — Final Exhortations, the Unchanging Christ, and Outside the Camp

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Hebrews 13 is the document's only properly epistolary chapter — and even here the epistolary frame is brief, attached to the end of what the author himself calls a λόγος παρακλήσεως (*logos paraklēseōs*, "word of exhortation," 13:22). The text's anonymity remains intact: Origen's "in truth God knows who wrote the epistle" (Eusebius, *Historia Ecclesiastica* 6.25.14) is still the operative verdict in modern scholarship. Tertullian's Barnabas, Luther's Apollos, and Adolf von Harnack's 1900 Priscilla hypothesis all retain advocates without consensus. Paul Ellingworth (NIGTC), Harold Attridge (Hermeneia), William L. Lane (Word Biblical Commentary), and Craig Koester (Anchor Yale) all converge on a high-rhetoric Hellenistic Jewish-Christian author writing for a community of Roman Jewish-Christians, dating between the mid-60s and early 90s AD.

Three internal data points orient the chapter historically. First, the closing greeting at 13:24 — οἱ ἀπὸ τῆς Ἰταλίας (*hoi apo tēs Italias*, "those from Italy") — is grammatically ambiguous. The construction can mean either "those *from* Italy [now writing to you in Italy]" or "those *of Italy* [writing to you elsewhere from Italy]." Most modern commentators (Lane, Lindars, F. F. Bruce) read it as Italian Christians abroad sending greetings home to Rome, which fixes Roman destination as the most plausible hypothesis. This reading gives Hebrews its single best historical anchor. Second, the mention of Timothy's release (13:23) — Τιμόθεον τὸν ἀδελφὸν ἡμῶν ἀπολελυμένον — places the author within the Pauline circle, though without committing to Pauline authorship; Timothy could be released from imprisonment or simply released from a journey. Third, the call to "obey your leaders" (13:17) and to "remember your leaders, those who spoke to you the word of God" (13:7) reflects an established but still-pre-monarchical-episcopate ecclesial structure, consistent with mid-to-late first-century Roman Christianity.

The chapter's structure betrays its dual character. Verses 1-21 are sermonic continuation — exhortations that round out the homily. Verses 22-25 are pure epistolary postscript: the author names his work, mentions Timothy, sends greetings, and closes with "Grace be with all of you." Lawrence Wills and Hartwig Thyen's analyses of the synagogue-sermon genre note this pattern of sermon-with-letter-postscript as characteristic of homiletic letters circulated in Hellenistic-Jewish networks.

Key Greek terms cluster densely in the chapter: φιλαδελφία (*philadelphia*, "brotherly love," 13:1); φιλοξενία (*philoxenia*, literally "love of strangers," translated "hospitality," 13:2); θυσιαστήριον (*thysiastērion*, "altar," 13:10); ἔξω τῆς παρεμβολῆς (*exō tēs parembolēs*, "outside the camp," 13:13); θυσία αἰνέσεως (*thysia aineseōs*, "sacrifice of praise," 13:15); λόγος παρακλήσεως (13:22); οἱ ἀπὸ τῆς Ἰταλίας (13:24).

## Lens 2: The Literary Architecture

Verses 1-7 issue a tightly compressed series of ethical imperatives. Brotherly love (φιλαδελφία) "let it continue" — the author assumes the community already practices it. Hospitality (φιλοξενία) is given an angelological warrant: "thereby some have entertained angels unawares" — an unmistakable reference to Genesis 18 (Abraham at the oaks of Mamre) and Genesis 19 (Lot at Sodom), with the Greek λεληθότες (*lelēthotes*, "without knowing it") suggesting the entire Hellenistic-Jewish theology of *theoxeny*, the visit of the divine in disguise. The injunction to "remember those in prison, as though in prison with them" (13:3) carries unmistakable resonance with the prior persecution recalled in 10:32-34. The marriage exhortation (13:4) and the contentment exhortation (13:5) are pieced together from LXX citations — Deuteronomy 31:6 ("I will never leave you nor forsake you") and Psalm 118:6 LXX ("the Lord is my helper, I will not fear; what can man do to me?"). The author is composing in the texture of the Septuagint.

Verse 8 supplies the chapter's theological hinge: Ἰησοῦς Χριστὸς ἐχθὲς καὶ σήμερον ὁ αὐτὸς καὶ εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας — "Jesus Christ, the same yesterday and today and forever." The line functions structurally as a hinge between the leader-imitation paragraph (v. 7) and the warnings against strange teaching (vv. 9-14). It is also one of the major divine-immutability texts in the New Testament, and we will treat it in Lens 4.

Verses 9-14 form a tightly bound theological unit. The warning against "diverse and strange teachings" (διδαχαῖς ποικίλαις καὶ ξέναις) and the contrast between the heart "strengthened by grace" and "foods, which have not benefited those devoted to them" — most commentators read this as a polemic against either ongoing participation in temple cuisine or against early Jewish-Christian dietary scrupulosity (cf. Rom 14; Col 2). What follows is one of the most textually contested passages in the entire epistle. ἔχομεν θυσιαστήριον ἐξ οὗ φαγεῖν οὐκ ἔχουσιν ἐξουσίαν οἱ τῇ σκηνῇ λατρεύοντες — "we have an altar from which those who serve the tent have no right to eat" (13:10). The verse has been the textual battlefield of Reformation Eucharistic theology for five centuries, and we will treat it in Lens 4.

Verses 11-14 develop the Yom Kippur typology. On the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:27), the carcasses of the bull and the goat whose blood the high priest carried into the Most Holy Place were burned outside the camp. Jesus, similarly, "suffered outside the gate" (ἔξω τῆς πύλης, *exō tēs pylēs* — Jerusalem's wall, the place of execution at Golgotha) "to sanctify the people through his own blood." The pivot then comes: "let us therefore go out to him outside the camp, bearing the reproach he endured" (13:13). The exhortation is more radical than is usually noticed. The "camp" (παρεμβολή) is a wilderness-Israel image — the encamped people of God — and the call to go *outside* is the call to depart from the very community-structure that organizes Israelite identity. The accompanying line "for here we have no lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come" (13:14) seals the eschatological-pilgrim frame already developed in 11:13-16. Modern theological reception has been particularly intense on these verses: liberation theologians (Gustavo Gutiérrez, Jon Sobrino, Leonardo Boff) read "outside the camp" as the call to leave centers of religious-political power for solidarity with the marginalized; Stanley Hauerwas and Will Willimon's *Resident Aliens* and John Howard Yoder's *The Politics of Jesus* read it as the foundational charter for a counter-cultural ecclesiology.

Verses 15-16 reframe Christian worship sacrificially: a "sacrifice of praise" (θυσία αἰνέσεως — itself a Septuagintism, cf. Lev 7:12 LXX, Ps 49:14 LXX) and the "doing good and sharing." The author has converted the entire sacrificial cult into the ethics-and-doxology of the Christian community.

The great benediction at 13:20-21 is one of the most magnificent in the New Testament. "Now may the God of peace, who brought again from the dead our Lord Jesus, the great shepherd of the sheep (τὸν ποιμένα τῶν προβάτων τὸν μέγαν), by the blood of the eternal covenant, equip you with everything good that you may do his will." The shepherd-image draws on Isaiah 63:11 LXX ("the shepherd of the sheep") and Ezekiel 34, and the "blood of the eternal covenant" recalls the Last Supper words.

Verses 22-25 close the document. The author asks the readers to "bear with my word of exhortation, for I have written to you briefly" — a wry self-description for a thirteen-chapter homily. Timothy is named. Greetings are sent. Grace closes the text.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story — Jewish and Christian Trajectories

Three trajectories deserve sustained attention.

First, the *philoxenia* tradition — entertaining angels unawares — became the foundational text for the entire Christian theology of hospitality. The Rule of St. Benedict (chapter 53) cites Hebrews 13:2 directly, mandating that every guest be received "as Christ" — a discipline that shaped European monastic civilization for fifteen centuries. The Eastern Orthodox tradition of the *xenia* icon (the three angels at Mamre, most famously rendered by Andrei Rublev c. 1411-1425) reads Hebrews 13:2 backward into Genesis 18, identifying the three visitors at Mamre as a Trinitarian prefiguration. Jewish reception of Genesis 18 in *Genesis Rabbah* and the Talmud (Bava Metzia 86b) develops a parallel hospitality ethic — *hakhnasat orchim* ("welcoming guests") — without the Trinitarian gloss. Modern Christian recovery of the theme by Henri Nouwen (*Reaching Out*) and Christine Pohl (*Making Room*) has placed *philoxenia* at the center of contemporary moral theology.

Second, the Yom Kippur typology and the *exō tēs parembolēs* trajectory have shaped Christian self-understanding from Tertullian's *De Fuga in Persecutione* through the radical-Reformation Anabaptists to twentieth-century liberation theology. The pattern is recurring: each generation of Christian movements that experienced themselves as marginal (the early martyrs, the Anabaptists, Black Pentecostals, the base-ecclesial communities of Latin America) has read Hebrews 13:13 as their charter. The shadow-side, articulated by mainline Protestant and Catholic theologians, is that "outside the camp" can become its own form of ecclesial pride — a charter for sectarianism that mistakes its own marginality for prophetic faithfulness.

Third, the closing benediction's "great shepherd of the sheep" trajectory connects Hebrews to the Johannine "good shepherd" (John 10), to 1 Peter 5:4 ("the chief Shepherd"), and to the Petrine-Roman pastoral office ("feed my sheep," John 21). The image became one of the controlling metaphors for Christian pastoral theology, from Gregory the Great's *Regula Pastoralis* through Calvin's emphasis on the *officium pastoris* to modern pastoral theology.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — Two Tensions

### Tension 1: Divine Immutability and the Unchanging Christ (13:8)

"Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever." The Greek is austere: ὁ αὐτός — *the same*. The verse has anchored five distinct theological architectures.

The classical-theist tradition reads 13:8 as a Christological extension of divine immutability. Aquinas in *Summa Theologiae* I q.9 (*De Dei Immutabilitate*) treats immutability as a corollary of divine simplicity and pure act: God cannot change because there is no potentiality in God to actualize. Boethius's *Consolation of Philosophy* V.6 supplies the famous *totum simul* eternity — the all-at-once life that is interminable, perfect possession of life. Anselm in *Proslogion* 22 makes the same move. The Roman Catechism and CCC 202 retain the doctrine. The Reformed scholastic tradition (Francis Turretin, *Institutio Theologiae Elencticae* III.11; Westminster Confession 2.1, "without parts or passions, immutable") inherits the same architecture. Stephen Charnock's *Discourses Upon the Existence and Attributes of God* and modern Reformed dogmatician John Webster (*God Without Measure*) defend the classical doctrine.

Process theology challenges all of this. Whitehead's *Process and Reality*, Charles Hartshorne's *The Divine Relativity*, and John B. Cobb's developments argue that classical immutability is incoherent with the relational God of biblical narrative — a God who covenants, hears, responds, and grieves cannot be immutable in the strong sense. God is unchanging in *character* but genuinely affected by the world.

Open theism (John Sanders's *The God Who Risks*, Greg Boyd's *God of the Possible*, Clark Pinnock) presents a more biblically conservative version of the same critique: God is unchanging in nature, character, and ultimate purposes, but God genuinely takes risks and genuinely responds to creaturely free choices. The future is partly open even to God.

Karl Barth's actualism in *CD* II/1 §31 reframes the question. Barth retains the language of constancy rather than static immutability: God is unchangeably the One who acts in history, and the act-in-history is itself eternal. The economic Trinity is the immanent Trinity. Christ's life-death-resurrection is not a temporal episode imposed on a static God; it is the eternal act of God's self-giving.

Eleonore Stump's contemporary Thomism (*Aquinas*, 2003) supplies a careful defense: classical immutability does not mean *static* or *unresponsive*. The eternal one act of God is responsive to every creaturely act in the eternal simultaneity. God's immutable life *includes* responsiveness without containing temporal succession.

Each architecture has pastoral consequences. The classical reading offers an unshakeable rock — Christ is the *same*, and the believer's life is grounded in divine constancy. The process-open reading offers a God who is genuinely with us in our suffering, genuinely affected by our prayers. Steelman the Process / Open theist reading: classical immutability has, at times, obscured the biblical God's genuine relationality, has been used to dismiss biblical anthropomorphism as mere accommodation, and has produced pastoral coldness in real contexts of theodicy. The honest reading of 13:8 in its homiletic setting is probably more pastoral than metaphysical: Christ is reliable, the same Lord who saved the fathers will save you, the One you trust does not change his loyalties.

### Tension 2: The Altar of 13:10 — Eucharistic Sacrifice and the Reformed Cross

The second tension is older than the first and has divided Christianity for five centuries. ἔχομεν θυσιαστήριον — "we have an altar" — and the question is what this altar is.

The Catholic Eucharistic reading is articulated definitively at Trent, Session 22 (1562, *Decretum de Sanctissimo Missae Sacrificio*) and reaffirmed in CCC 1382-1383 and 1410: the Christian altar is the Eucharistic altar, identical with the heavenly altar of Hebrews 9 — the once-for-all sacrifice of Christ re-presented (not repeated) in the Mass. "Those who serve the tent" are those still bound to the Levitical-Mosaic worship who cannot eat from this Christian altar. The Eastern Orthodox tradition reads similarly: the Anaphora is sung at the altar that is also the heavenly *thysiastērion*, with the bread and wine consecrated as the very Body and Blood of Christ (Lossky, Schmemann, Zizioulas). For both Catholic and Orthodox traditions, 13:10 is one of the New Testament's clearest endorsements of an altar-centered Eucharistic ecclesiology.

The Reformed tradition reads 13:10 in nearly the opposite direction. Calvin in *Institutes* IV.18 mounts a sustained polemic against the Mass as sacrifice: the Christian altar of Hebrews 13:10 is the cross of Christ, and any priestly re-offering at a physical altar misunderstands the once-for-all character of Christ's sacrifice (Heb 9:25-28; 10:10-14). Heidelberg Catechism Q. 80 — composed in 1563 in direct response to Trent — calls the Roman Mass "an accursed idolatry" (*ein vermaledeite Abgötterei* in the original German). B. F. Westcott, Geerhardus Vos, and modern Reformed exegetes (G. K. Beale, D. A. Carson) read 13:10 as anti-cultic rather than pro-Eucharistic. The "altar" is the heavenly reality of Christ's sacrifice; the "those who serve the tent" are those still attached to Levitical worship; and the Christian community feeds on Christ by faith, not at a physical altar.

The Lutheran middle reading distinguishes itself from both. The Augsburg Confession (Article 10) and the Formula of Concord (Article 7) reject the Catholic Mass-as-sacrifice while affirming the *real presence* of Christ's body and blood in the Eucharist (sacramental union, *unio sacramentalis*). Luther's reading of 13:10 treats the altar as Christ himself, with the Eucharist as the location where Christ's body and blood are truly present *for the believer's benefit* without the Mass being a re-offering of his sacrifice.

The Anglican tradition navigates between Catholic and Reformed via Articles XXVIII-XXXI of the Thirty-Nine Articles. Lancelot Andrewes and the Caroline divines retained altar-language while rejecting transubstantiation; the Tractarians (Pusey, Newman pre-conversion) attempted a recovery of altar-centered worship within Anglicanism that finally drove Newman to Rome.

What does *thysiastērion* require? The Greek itself does not decide the question. Bruce Metzger's careful philological work shows that *thysiastērion* in the Septuagint and in Hellenistic Jewish writings can refer to either a physical altar or a metaphorical sacrificial reality. The Reformed reading is exegetically defensible. The Catholic reading is also exegetically defensible. The deeper question — what *kind* of community Hebrews envisions, and what *practices* it expects — sits beneath the textual surface and resists a tidy resolution.

**Closing question:** When the author tells us Jesus suffered "outside the gate" and calls us to "go to him outside the camp, bearing the reproach he endured" — is that summons compatible with our settled altars, our established communities, our recognized leaders? Or does the chapter's quiet center, beneath the unchanging Christ and the contested altar, finally lie in the unsettling exhortation to leave the camp itself?

## The Thread

Chapter 13 closes the homily by collapsing all the prior architecture into ethics and worship. The unshakeable kingdom that arrived in 12:28 must now show itself in *philadelphia*, *philoxenia*, marital fidelity, prison-solidarity, contentment, leadership-imitation. The unchanging Christ of 13:8 anchors a community that lives by grace, not by foods. The altar from which we eat is outside the camp where Christ suffered, and the city we seek is the one not yet here. The author's *logos paraklēseōs* ends with a benediction that turns the entire epistle's high Christology — the Son through whom God made the world, the High Priest who entered once for all, the *archēgos* who pioneered our faith — into a single pastoral wish: that the God of peace would equip us with everything good. The grace at the end is not decoration. It is the same grace that has carried the document from chapter one — the grace by which the unchanging Christ continues to speak.
