# Joshua 2: The Canaanite Who Saw

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The scene is set at Shittim (*Šiṭṭîm*, שִׁטִּים, "Acacias"), the last Transjordanian encampment of Israel described in Numbers 25 and 33:49, probably Tell el-Hammam or Tell el-Kefrein in the plains of Moab. From there Joshua dispatches two unnamed *mĕraggĕlîm* ("spies," though the Hebrew more literally means "ones going about on foot") to the fortified Canaanite city-state of Jericho. In the political geography of the Late Bronze/Iron I transition (roughly 1200–1150 BCE on the low chronology favored by Finkelstein and most contemporary archaeologists, or c. 1406 BCE if one follows the high-chronology reading of 1 Kings 6:1), Jericho (Tell es-Sultan) would have been one of many small Canaanite city-states operating under the fraying hegemony of New Kingdom Egypt. The Amarna Letters from the mid-fourteenth century preserve the panicked correspondence of such city-state rulers to Pharaoh — Abdi-Heba of Jerusalem, Biridiya of Megiddo — complaining of the *ʿapiru* (a sociological category of displaced persons often invoked in discussions of early Israel). The Merneptah Stele of 1208 BCE, with its boast that "Israel is laid waste, its seed is not," is the earliest extrabiblical reference to Israel as a people group in Canaan, a fixed point around which all dating arguments revolve.

Jericho's own archaeology is contested and famous. John Garstang in the 1930s dated a destruction layer to around 1400 BCE and proclaimed a match with the biblical account. Kathleen Kenyon's careful stratigraphic work in the 1950s overturned this, finding no substantial Late Bronze occupation at Tell es-Sultan — only a small, unfortified settlement at the time the conquest would have occurred on either chronology. Bryant Wood in 1990 argued for a Middle Bronze II redating of Garstang's "City IV," but his proposal has not gained acceptance in mainstream Syro-Palestinian archaeology. This historical-archaeological gap is fundamental to how modern readers handle the book: the text's Jericho and the dirt's Jericho do not coincide.

Rahab (*Rāḥāv*, רָחָב, "broad" or "wide") is introduced as an *ʾiššâ zônâ* (אִשָּׁה זוֹנָה, "a prostitute woman"). The Septuagint translates with *pornē*, and Jewish tradition from the Targum onward sometimes softened the term to "innkeeper" (*pundaqîtā*) — Rashi famously follows this line, though Kimchi (Radak) and others retain the sexual meaning. The historical plausibility is high: taverns run by women who offered food, lodging, and sex were a well-documented feature of ANE urban economies, attested in the Code of Hammurabi §108–111 (the *sābîtu* tavern-keeper). A house "built into the wall" (*baqqîr haḥômâ*, בַּקִּיר הַחוֹמָה, v. 15) fits Bronze Age casemate wall construction, where the outer and inner walls were joined by partitions forming chambers — a known architectural feature though not excavated at Jericho specifically in the relevant period.

Key Hebrew terms worth watching. *Ḥesed* (חֶסֶד) appears twice in Rahab's negotiation (vv. 12, 14) — the covenantal, steadfast kindness that Nelson Glueck made a career out of, and which Katharine Sakenfeld has since refined as "a freely-chosen act of loyalty within an existing relationship." Rahab uses it of her own act and asks it in return, transacting covenant language from inside a Canaanite house. *Nāmōg* (נָמוֹג, "melt," vv. 9, 24) is the same verb Moses sings in the Song of the Sea (Exod 15:15): "the inhabitants of Canaan melted away." The Reed Sea has now reached Jericho in rumor. *Yirʾâ* (יִרְאָה, "fear," v. 9) is the Deuteronomic fear-of-YHWH inverted onto the Canaanites — they have it, Israel is supposed to have it, the reader is meant to notice. *Tiqwat ḥûṭ haššānî* (תִּקְוַת חוּט הַשָּׁנִי, "cord of the scarlet thread," v. 18) uses *tiqwâ*, which in every other biblical occurrence means "hope" (Jer 29:11; Prov 23:18). English cannot hold both senses; Hebrew collapses the cord and the hope into one word.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is DtrH narrative prose with strong wisdom-tale features. Robert Alter reads chapter 2 as a classic Hebrew "type-scene" of the foreign helper (with parallels in the Egyptian midwives of Exodus 1 and Jael of Judges 4), and Yairah Amit identifies it as a *hidden polemic* that uses a Canaanite voice to speak Deuteronomistic theology. The craft is dense. The chapter opens and closes with Joshua son of Nun receiving intelligence (vv. 1, 23–24) — an inclusio framing the middle dialogue. Inside it, Rahab's confession in vv. 9–11 forms a triadic chiasm: "I know the LORD has given you the land (A) / fear has fallen / inhabitants melt (B) / we heard about the Sea and the Amorite kings (C) / our hearts melted (Bʹ) / the LORD your God is God above and beneath (Aʹ)." The rhetorical climax is the most sweeping monotheistic statement by a non-Israelite in the Hebrew Bible before Nebuchadnezzar's prostration in Daniel 4.

Wordplay runs throughout. Rahab's name ("broad") is paired with her house on the broad city wall; when she sends the spies "into the hills" (*hāhārâ*), she sends them into the very land the fear has melted. The *ḥûṭ* (cord) that lowers them down the wall (v. 15) becomes the *tiqwâ* (cord/hope) that marks her window (v. 18) — the instrument of descent becomes the instrument of salvation. The *petaḥ* (opening/door) imagery is thick: the gate closes on Jericho (v. 5, 7), the window opens for the spies (v. 15), and the door of Rahab's house becomes the threshold of life and death (v. 19).

Intertextually the chapter reaches in three directions. Backward, to Genesis 38 and Tamar, who also uses a scarlet cord (*šānî*, the same rare word) to mark the firstborn — Jewish tradition (Tanhuma, Numbers Rabbah) links the two scarlet threads as a genealogical hint. Forward, to Ruth 1:16 — another Moabite/Canaanite woman uses near-identical covenant language ("your God will be my God"). And latest, to Matthew 1:5, where Rahab appears in Jesus's genealogy alongside Tamar, Ruth, and Bathsheba. The narrator has structured chapter 2 to read differently in retrospect.

Placement in the book's argument is pointed. Chapters 1 and 3–4 are official, liturgical, ark-centered. Chapter 2 is nocturnal, covert, and full of lies. L. Daniel Hawk's *Every Promise Fulfilled* argues the chapter deliberately disrupts the conquest script: the first Canaanite encountered is saved, not destroyed; the first confession of YHWH in the land comes from a prostitute's mouth; the *herem* is compromised before it has begun. The book opens by undermining its own ideology.

On textual variants: LXX Joshua is roughly five percent shorter than MT and occasionally reorders material. In chapter 2, the LXX lacks the redundant "and hid them" in v. 4 and condenses the spies' reply in vv. 17–20. 4QJosh^a preserves little of chapter 2 directly, but its relocation of the Mt. Ebal altar to before Jericho (a reordering attested against MT) confirms that the Joshua text was still fluid in the late Second Temple period. Emanuel Tov's *Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible* treats Joshua as a textbook case where the shorter, sometimes differently-ordered LXX preserves an earlier Hebrew recension.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc from creation through exile, Joshua 2 sits at a hinge. The *nāmōg* ("melt") from the Song of the Sea (Exod 15) is being fulfilled: the promise of Genesis 15:16 that the iniquity of the Amorites would be "full" is being cashed in. Yet the fulfillment is interrupted by a counter-movement — a Canaanite blessed into covenant. This is the same structural irony Jon Levenson identifies across the Hebrew Bible: election always bends toward inclusion, but the mechanism is jagged.

**Jewish trajectories.** In Second Temple literature, 4Q378–379 (the Apocryphon of Joshua) fragments from Qumran rework Joshua's farewell material but are thin on Rahab. Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.1.2–7) significantly cleans up the story — he calls Rahab's establishment an "inn" and removes the deception from her speech, part of his broader apologetic for a Roman audience. Philo does not treat her directly but his allegorical method would later license the Christian Fathers. The rabbinic reception is astonishingly rich. Megillah 14b lists Rahab among the four most beautiful women ever to live (with Sarah, Abigail, and Esther) and says she became a proselyte, married Joshua himself, and was the ancestress of eight prophets including Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and the prophetess Huldah. Zevachim 116b makes her confession a model proselyte's declaration. Sifre Numbers 78 puts her in the line of priests. Bereshit Rabbah and Ruth Rabbah pair her with Ruth as exemplary gentile converts whose faith surpasses that of native Israelites.

Medieval Jewish commentary splits along interpretive lines. Rashi (11th c. France) on v. 1 reads *zônâ* as *pundaqîtā* ("innkeeper") — a softening that was already in the Targum — though elsewhere he acknowledges the sexual sense. Radak (David Kimchi, 13th c.) holds to the plain meaning (*zônâ* = prostitute) and says her repentance is the point precisely because of the depth from which she rose. Ramban (Nachmanides) focuses on the oath and asks how the spies could swear loyalty to a non-Israelite city-dweller in light of Deuteronomy 7's prohibition on covenant with Canaanites; he resolves it by arguing Rahab had already converted in her heart, which is why the *herem* did not apply. Abarbanel emphasizes the political realism of the scene. Maimonides in the *Mishneh Torah* (Hilkhot Melakhim 6:1–5) uses the Rahab episode to codify that any Canaanite who accepted the seven Noahide laws and renounced idolatry was to be spared — Rahab becomes a case law.

Modern Jewish readers work in a different register. Martin Buber (*Prophetic Faith*) reads her confession as an I-Thou moment that breaks the tribal wall. Jon Levenson foregrounds the theological scandal: the covenant's first beneficiary in the promised land is outside the covenant. Avivah Zornberg (*The Murmuring Deep*) finds in Rahab's speech the "voice from below," the repressed Canaanite interiority that the conquest narrative usually silences. Tikva Frymer-Kensky's *Reading the Women of the Bible* reads Rahab as an agent, not a trope, and refuses the rabbinic softening that makes her palatable by making her a convert. Rachel Havrelock's *River Jordan* situates the chapter in the book's larger geography of crossing and boundary.

**Christian trajectories.** The Fathers built a typological mountain on the scarlet cord. Clement of Rome (*1 Clement* 12, c. 96 CE) is the earliest extant reading: "by faith and hospitality Rahab the harlot was saved... they gave her a sign, that she should hang forth from her house a scarlet cord, thereby showing beforehand that through the blood of the Lord there should be redemption." Justin Martyr (*Dialogue with Trypho* 111) and Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* IV.20.12) develop this. Origen's *Homilies on Joshua* (especially Homily 3) is the foundational Christian commentary: Rahab's house becomes the Church, the scarlet cord the blood of Christ, the gathering of her family the ingathering of the nations. "Let him who would be saved come into the house of this one-time prostitute... Let him carry the scarlet sign." Jerome cites the Matthew 1 genealogy to defend the legitimacy of Gentile inclusion. Augustine (*City of God* 16.35; various letters) uses Rahab to argue that faith precedes law — the Canaanite woman has what the Israelite is supposed to have.

Medieval Christian reception carries the typology forward. The *Glossa Ordinaria* reproduces Origen. Aquinas (*Summa* II-II q. 110, a. 3 ad 3) addresses the ethical problem head-on: was Rahab's lie sinful? He concedes yes (lying is always disordered) but says God rewarded her faith, not her deception — a nuanced reading later Reformers sharpened. Hugh of St. Victor develops the Church-as-Rahab ecclesiology. The Reformation period saw sharp debate. Luther in his 1525 sermons on Joshua made Rahab a figure of faith-alone (*sola fide*) — she is justified by trusting YHWH's power, not by works. Calvin in the *Commentaries on Joshua* defends her faith while more sternly naming her lie sinful: her faith was real but mixed with weakness; God accommodated. Menno Simons and the Anabaptists used her as a model for non-Israelites entering covenant community through confession.

Critical scholarship shifts the frame. Wellhausen saw the chapter as a late, anti-conquest redaction. Noth's DtrH framework made it part of the Deuteronomist's theological project. Frank Cross's dual redaction assigned the Rahab material to Dtr1 (Josianic) with its concern for right confession. Richard Nelson's commentary (OTL) reads chapter 2 as a carefully constructed irony inside the conquest ideology. Thomas Dozeman (Eerdmans Joshua) emphasizes the gender and ethnicity inversions. Trent Butler (Word) works the form-critical layers. Contemporary commentators split further: Richard Hess (TOTC) defends the historicity; L. Daniel Hawk reads the chapter as subversive counter-narrative; Carolyn Pressler foregrounds the complicity of Rahab's own body in the rescue; Robert Warrior's 1989 essay "Canaanites, Cowboys, and Indians" forced the field to reckon with Rahab from the perspective of the destroyed — she is the exception that proves, and hides, the rule. Musa Dube and Kwok Pui-lan extend this postcolonial reading: a Canaanite woman is made useful to the imperial project and then absorbed into its genealogy.

Where Jewish and Christian traditions converge: both make Rahab a model of faith that transcends ethnic boundary. Both are troubled by her lie and find ways to honor it. Both make her genealogically significant (rabbinic prophets descend from her; Matthew's messianic line runs through her). Where they diverge, and why: the scarlet cord. For Jewish tradition it is a sign, a practical mark of covenant, and nothing more. For Christian tradition from Clement forward it becomes the blood of Christ — a typological reading that Jewish interpreters reject as imposing New Testament categories onto the plain sense. The divergence is not ornamental: it is the entire hermeneutical question of whether the Hebrew Bible's signs point forward to Christ or sit within their own theological logic.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What is God up to here? Chapter 2 is doing something the rest of Joshua struggles to do: representing divine sovereignty as operating from inside the condemned population, not only against it. Rahab speaks the book's theology better than Joshua does. The question of why the narrator gave the best confession to a prostitute is the chapter's theological center. Two genuine tensions.

**Tension 1: The moral status of Rahab's lie.** She deceives the king of Jericho's men (vv. 4–5) and the text neither rebukes nor explains her. The later tradition has to.

- *Catholic (CCC 2482–2486; Aquinas)*: Lying is intrinsically disordered because speech is ordered to truth. Rahab's lie is a *mendacium officiosum* (helpful lie) — still objectively sinful but diminished in gravity, and God rewards the underlying faith, not the lie itself. This is the classical position.
- *Reformed (Calvin)*: Real faith mixed with real weakness. God accommodates human frailty; the faith is imputed as righteousness, the lie remains sin. Calvin refuses to call the lie good but refuses to deny her faith. Later Reformed (Witsius, Turretin) sharpened this into doctrine of concursive divine action through imperfect human means.
- *Lutheran (Luther)*: Rahab shows *sola fide* in its purest form — she has no law, no circumcision, no works to plead. The deception is irrelevant to her justification; faith grabs the promise and God counts it.
- *Anabaptist*: Menno Simons and later Anabaptists read the chapter as evidence that covenant community is entered by confession, not birth — the lie is subordinate to the confession.
- *Jewish rabbinic*: The lie is *piqquaḥ nefesh* (preservation of life), which overrides almost all commandments. Rahab's deception is *not* a moral compromise; it is Torah-obedience in its highest form. Sanhedrin 74a's principle that one may violate most commandments to save a life applies here. This is perhaps the sharpest divergence from Christian moralizing over the lie.
- *Critical/academic*: The narrator shows no interest in the ethical question. It is a wisdom tale — the trickster who outwits the powerful is celebrated across ANE literature. The question of lying was imported later.

*Steelman of the position I find least intuitive (the Aquinas view that the lie was sinful even though it saved lives)*: truthfulness is not a rule among rules but the precondition of all relational reality — including the reality of God's own speech. To license lying in extremity is to concede that human flourishing sometimes requires the corruption of the very medium through which God reveals. Aquinas's position is not squeamish; it is metaphysical. A world where lying is sometimes virtuous is a world where speech has no fixed ontological status, and once that is gone, divine revelation itself becomes negotiable. The rabbinic principle of *piqquaḥ nefesh* is right about life but possibly wrong about the category it uses.

*Where this leaves us:* Rahab's lie is a permanent disturbance in any moral system that wants both clean principles and covenant loyalty in extremis. The chapter will not resolve it for us.

**Tension 2: The ethics of selective rescue and the *herem*.** Rahab and her household are saved while Jericho is destroyed (ch. 6). The chapter's rescue only makes the surrounding slaughter more visible.

- *Traditional apologetic (Copan, Flannagan)*: Rahab's salvation proves the *herem* was not racial or territorial but moral — any Canaanite who turned to YHWH was spared. The *herem* targeted a corrupted religious-social system, not a people. Rahab is the evidence.
- *Divine Command critical (Douglas Earl, Eric Seibert, Jerome Creach, John Collins)*: The *herem* remains ethically indefensible even with Rahab. Earl reads Joshua as mythic-theological rather than historical — the *herem* is an ideological construct, not a divine mandate to be defended. Seibert calls it "disturbing divine behavior" and says the reader's conscience is the beginning of theological honesty. Creach's *Violence in Scripture* argues the narrative itself is critiquing the ideology even as it reports it. Collins in *Does the Bible Justify Violence?* says quietly: yes, it does, and we have to decide whether to follow.
- *Reformed (Calvin, contemporary Goldingay)*: The *herem* is God's sovereign judgment on a specific people at a specific moment; Rahab is the exception that shows judgment is never total. Goldingay (*Israel's Faith*) adds a pastoral note: the conquest narratives trouble us because they should.
- *Anabaptist/pacifist*: The *herem* cannot be reconciled with the non-violent ethic of Jesus. Rahab's rescue is the trajectory to which the texts point; the violence is the part to be left behind.
- *Jewish rabbinic*: The *herem* is time-bound and people-bound to the seven Canaanite nations, and Maimonides (*Hilkhot Melakhim* 5:4–5) notes that by his day those nations were untraceable — the law could no longer be applied. Rahab's rescue is evidence of the built-in exception always available.
- *Postcolonial (Warrior, Dube, Kwok Pui-lan)*: Rahab is a collaborator. The question is not whether she is saved but whether her salvation is purchased at the cost of her people's erasure. The narrative uses her confession to justify their destruction. The chapter is not evidence that the *herem* is moral; it is evidence that empire always finds a native willing to speak for it.

*Steelman of the postcolonial position*: Robert Warrior's 1989 essay asked the field to read Joshua from the ground under the collapsing wall. Every apologetic that cites Rahab has to ignore the 99% of Jericho for whom there is no cord. The theological point is not that Rahab's faith saved her — it is that the narrator made the destruction of her people narratable only by first giving us a Canaanite we could love. This is the structural logic of conquest literature everywhere, and the Bible's version is not innocent of it.

*Where this leaves us:* The chapter resolves nothing about the *herem*. It makes the ethical problem worse by humanizing one Canaanite, which casts a shadow over every other one. Readers who feel this shadow are reading correctly.

*One unresolved question:* If the narrator could hear the Canaanite confession and preserve it with this much care, why could the narrator not hear any other Canaanite voice in the book — and what does our theology become when we notice that silence?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 15:16** — "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete." Joshua 2 is the hinge when the text claims the iniquity is full. Rahab's presence inside a condemned city is the counterpoint that complicates the judgment timetable.
- **Exodus 15:14–16** — the Song of the Sea prophesies Canaanites "melting" (*nāmōg*) and "trembling." Rahab in 2:9–11 quotes the song back to the spies; the mythology of one generation has become the testimony of another. Listen for this word in chapter 5 when the kings of the Amorites hear the crossing report.
- **Matthew 1:5** — when you reach the opening of the New Testament and see "Salmon the father of Boaz by Rahab," remember this rooftop. The scarlet cord is already in the genealogy.
- **Hebrews 11:31 and James 2:25** — both New Testament writers cite Rahab for opposite arguments (faith and works, respectively). When you get to James, remember that the Jewish Rahab tradition has no trouble holding faith and works together because the rabbinic reading already did.
