# Psalm 99: The Threefold Holy and the Priest-Prophets Who Were Answered

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Psalm 99 carries no superscription, no attributed author, and no narrative occasion. The Hebrew tradition received it as a liturgical text whose voice belongs to the gathered community rather than to a named individual, and the form-critical work of Hermann Gunkel in the early twentieth century placed it firmly within a recognizable genre: the enthronement hymn. Together with Psalms 93, 95, 96, 97, and 98, it forms a tightly bound cluster scholars call the *Yahweh-malak* psalms, named for the opening declaration *Yahweh malakh* (יְהוָה מָלָךְ, "the Lord reigns"). Psalm 99 is the cluster's closer and its most theologically dense member — the only one to weave the threefold *qadosh* refrain through its strophes.

Sigmund Mowinckel, building on Gunkel in his *Psalmenstudien* (1921-24), proposed that these psalms were composed for and used in an annual New Year enthronement festival in pre-exilic Jerusalem, a Yahweh-festival paralleling the Babylonian *akitu* in which the king-god's reign was ritually re-acclaimed. Most contemporary scholars (Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay) treat the Mowinckelian festival as conjectural in its details but credit the underlying instinct: these psalms breathe the air of cultic acclamation, of a community that gathered to declare Yahweh's kingship aloud. The most likely date for Psalm 99's final form is post-exilic, in the period when the *Yahweh-malak* cluster was assembled as Book IV's theological response to the loss of the Davidic monarchy.

Three Hebrew phrases anchor the psalm's theology. The opening line, *Yahweh malakh yirgezu amim* (יְהוָה מָלָךְ יִרְגְּזוּ עַמִּים, "the Lord reigns; let the peoples tremble," 99:1), names the genre and sets the affective register: the proper response to divine kingship is corporeal trembling, *ragaz*, a verb used elsewhere for the shaking of mountains and the quaking of nations. The refrain *qadosh hu* (קָדוֹשׁ הוּא, "holy is he," 99:3, 5) and its expanded form *qadosh Yahweh Eloheinu* (קָדוֹשׁ יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵינוּ, "the Lord our God is holy," 99:9) closes each strophe. And the picture of Yahweh *yashav keruvim* (יֹשֵׁב כְּרוּבִים, "enthroned upon the cherubim," 99:1) draws directly on the temple-cult imagery of the ark of the covenant, where two cherubim faced each other above the *kapporet* and Yahweh was understood to dwell invisibly between them (Exod 25:18-22; 1 Sam 4:4; 2 Sam 6:2). The psalm's material world is the inner sanctuary; its political world is the post-exilic community wrestling with whether Yahweh still reigns when no Davidide sits in Jerusalem.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Robert Lowth's eighteenth-century insight into Hebrew parallelism opens the psalm's architecture immediately. The opening couplet — "the Lord reigns; let the peoples tremble! / He sits enthroned upon the cherubim; let the earth quake!" — pairs synonymous halves so that *peoples* and *earth* expand outward, and *tremble* (*ragaz*) and *quake* (*nut*) intensify the somatic response. The whole psalm operates with this intensifying parallelism: justice and equity in 99:4, Moses-Aaron-and-Samuel in 99:6, forgiving-yet-avenging in 99:8.

The structural signature is the threefold *qadosh* refrain, which divides the psalm into three strophes of unequal length:

- **Strophe I (vv. 1-3):** Yahweh's universal kingship, exalted over all peoples — closes with *qadosh hu*.
- **Strophe II (vv. 4-5):** Yahweh's righteous kingship in Jacob, justice and equity — closes with *qadosh hu*.
- **Strophe III (vv. 6-9):** Yahweh's particular dealings with Moses, Aaron, and Samuel — closes with *qadosh Yahweh Eloheinu*.

This threefold-holy structure is one of the most distinctive features in the Psalter. Its only close parallel is Isaiah 6:3, where the seraphim cry *qadosh qadosh qadosh Yahweh tseva'ot* (קָדוֹשׁ קָדוֹשׁ קָדוֹשׁ יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת, "holy, holy, holy is the Lord of hosts"). Psalm 99 distributes across three strophes what Isaiah compresses into a single seraphic cry, but the underlying liturgical instinct — that Yahweh's holiness must be acclaimed three times — is the same. Hossfeld-Zenger argue that Psalm 99 deliberately echoes Isaiah 6's vision and adapts it for congregational use.

The psalm's most striking literary move arrives in 99:6: *Mosheh ve-Aharon be-khohanav u-Shmuel be-qor'ei shemo* (מֹשֶׁה וְאַהֲרֹן בְּכֹהֲנָיו וּשְׁמוּאֵל בְּקֹרְאֵי שְׁמוֹ, "Moses and Aaron were among his priests, Samuel also was among those who called upon his name"). Three named figures stand together as Yahweh's intercessors — and Moses is here described as a priest (*kohen*) alongside Aaron, an attribution found nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible. The Pentateuch consistently distinguishes Moses (prophet, lawgiver, *ish ha-Elohim*, "man of God") from Aaron (priest); Psalm 99 collapses that distinction. The psalm then telescopes time, declaring that "they called to the Lord, and he answered them," and the recital ends with a remarkable theological summary in 99:8: Yahweh was both *El nose hu lahem* (אֵל נֹשֵׂא הוּא לָהֶם, "a forgiving God to them") and *noqem al-alilotam* (נֹקֵם עַל־עֲלִילוֹתָם, "an avenger of their wrongdoings"). Forgiveness and judgment named in a single verse, of the same persons.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Gerald Wilson's *The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* (1985) and David Mitchell's *The Message of the Psalter* (1997) read Book IV (Psalms 90-106) as the canonical-shape response to Book III's catastrophic close, where Psalm 89 grieves the apparent collapse of the Davidic covenant after the exile. Book IV's answer is Mosaic and theocratic: it opens with Psalm 90, the only psalm attributed to Moses, and centers on the *Yahweh-malak* cluster, which insists that Yahweh's kingship — not the Davidic monarchy — is the foundation that endures. Psalm 99, closing the cluster and recalling Moses, Aaron, and Samuel by name, brings the argument to its climax: when there is no king on the throne in Jerusalem, the people remember that Yahweh sits enthroned on the cherubim, and that he answered the priest-prophets who called upon his name.

**The Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 99 dwells on the threefold *qadosh*, reading each instance as marking a distinct dimension of divine holiness — in heaven, on earth, and forever. The medieval commentators Rashi, Ibn Ezra, and David Kimchi (Radak) each read 99:6 as drawing the priest-prophet line tightly: Moses functions as priest because he consecrated Aaron and offered sacrifice before the priesthood was formally instituted (Exod 24, 29). Liturgically, the threefold-holy structure became one of the building blocks of the *Kedushah*, the sanctification recited in the Amidah, which combines Isaiah 6:3, Ezekiel 3:12, and Psalm 146:10 into a congregational acclamation of Yahweh's holiness. Reuven Kimelman's work on the development of the *Kedushah* traces how rabbinic Judaism wove the biblical threefold-holy traditions into a daily liturgy that has shaped Jewish prayer for nearly two millennia. In the modern period, Martin Buber's *Kingship of God* engaged the *Yahweh-malak* psalms directly; Abraham Joshua Heschel's *The Prophets* and Jon D. Levenson's *Sinai and Zion* read Psalm 99's holiness theology as the connecting thread between the Sinai theophany and the Zion-temple cult.

**The Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* reads the threefold *qadosh* trinitarianly, finding Father, Son, and Spirit in each refrain — a reading Cassiodorus elaborates and Aquinas (in his *Postilla super Psalmos*) cautiously refines. The critical-scholarly tradition runs from Gunkel and Mowinckel through Claus Westermann, Hans-Joachim Kraus, Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger, and John Goldingay, each of whom has produced major commentaries that wrestle with the psalm's cultic setting and theology. Contemporary American scholarship — Walter Brueggemann, Patrick Miller, William P. Brown — reads the psalm canonically and theologically, asking what it means for a community to acclaim divine kingship in the face of historical loss. The threefold *qadosh* is one of the principal Old Testament roots of the Christian liturgical *Sanctus*, which entered the eucharistic prayer of the early church (already attested in the late-first-century *Apostolic Tradition*) and remains central to the Roman Mass and the Eastern Divine Liturgy. Reginald Heber's 1826 hymn "Holy, Holy, Holy, Lord God Almighty" carries the same acclamation into Protestant hymnody.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The psalmist's vision is concentrated and unified: Yahweh reigns from the holy mountain, enthroned upon the cherubim; he is served by intercessors — Moses, Aaron, Samuel — who called upon his name and were answered; and the proper response of the gathered community, of Israel, and of all the peoples is trembling, exaltation, and the threefold acclamation of his holiness. The author wants the worshipper to feel both the seismic distance of divine otherness and the astonishing fact that this Holy One answers when called.

**Tension 1: Holiness and approachability.** The threefold *qadosh* hammers home divine otherness. *Qadosh* in the Hebrew Bible names what is set apart, separated, dangerous to touch — the ground at the burning bush, the mountain at Sinai, the inner sanctuary where only the high priest enters once a year. Yet Psalm 99 places at the structural center of its third strophe the claim that Moses, Aaron, and Samuel "called to the Lord, and he answered them" (99:6). The Holy One who makes the earth quake is also the God who picks up the phone. How does the Old Testament hold these together? Walter Brueggemann's *Theology of the Old Testament* names this the dialectic of "holiness and presence," arguing that Israel's distinctive theological achievement is refusing to collapse one into the other. Eastern Orthodox theology articulates the same balance through its apophatic-cataphatic distinction: God's essence is unknowable (*ousia*), but his energies (*energeia*) are genuinely communicable. Reformed theology speaks of the simultaneous reverence and intimacy proper to the believer; Catholic theology develops a doctrine of mediation in which holiness becomes approachable through the priesthood of Christ and, derivatively, the saints. **Where this leaves us:** Psalm 99 will not let us choose. It insists the same God who makes nations tremble is the God who answered Moses by name, and it will not soften the first claim to make the second more palatable.

**Tension 2: Moses as priest (99:6).** The Pentateuch's normal categories place Moses in the prophetic-mediatorial line and Aaron in the priestly-sacrificial line, with the two functions deliberately separated. Yet 99:6 calls Moses a *kohen*. John Goldingay's commentary on Psalm 99 reads this as a deliberate retrospective: the psalmist is not making a technical claim about Moses's priestly ordination but recognizing that in the period before the Aaronic priesthood was institutionalized, Moses functioned in the priestly office — sprinkling blood at the covenant ceremony (Exod 24:6-8), consecrating Aaron and his sons (Exod 29; Lev 8), and interceding for Israel after the golden calf (Exod 32:30-32). Patrick Miller's *Religion of Ancient Israel* notes that the boundaries between prophet, priest, and king in early Israel were more porous than later canonical categories suggest. The Christian theological tradition develops this triple office into the doctrine of Christ's *triplex munus* — prophet, priest, and king — most fully articulated in Calvin's *Institutes* II.15, where Christ alone holds all three offices undividedly. **Where this leaves us:** Psalm 99 hints at what later theology would systematize: that the deepest mediation between God and his people is not parceled out across separate institutions but unified in a single mediator who calls upon the divine name and is answered.

**Closing question:** What does it mean to worship a God whose holiness makes the earth quake but who, when called by name, has always answered?

## The Thread

- **Isaiah 6:1-7** — The seraphic threefold *qadosh*, the parallel acclamation that Psalm 99 distributes across its three strophes; together they form the Old Testament foundation of the Christian *Sanctus*.
- **Exodus 32:30-32** — Moses as intercessor for Israel after the golden calf, the priestly-mediatorial function that Psalm 99:6 retrospectively names.
- **1 Samuel 7:5-12** — Samuel as intercessor at Mizpah, where he called to the Lord on Israel's behalf and the Lord answered with thunder; the third name in Psalm 99:6's recital.
- **Psalm 100** — The doxological coda to the *Yahweh-malak* cluster, turning the trembling acclamation of Psalm 99 into joyful thanksgiving as the worshipper enters the gates.
