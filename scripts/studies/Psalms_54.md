# Psalm 54 — A Scholarly Study

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 54 carries one of the most narratively specific superscriptions in the Psalter: "To the choirmaster: with stringed instruments. A *Maskil* of David, when the Ziphites went and told Saul, 'Is not David hiding among us?'" The reference points to the twin betrayal narratives of 1 Samuel 23:19–24 and 26:1, where the Ziphites — inhabitants of the Judean wilderness south of Hebron — twice report David's hiding place to Saul. Unlike Doeg's active violence (Ps 52), the Ziphites' offense is informational: they are not killers but informants. They volunteer geography. Their crime is the disclosure that lets Saul's troops close in.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 54 as a brief *individuelle Klagepsalm* — an individual lament — with all the standard form-critical components compressed into seven verses: invocation (vv. 1–2), complaint (v. 3), expression of trust (v. 4), petition / curse (v. 5), and vow of praise with realized confidence (vv. 6–7). The poem's brevity is itself a feature; Mowinckel and Westermann note that Ps 54 is the most economical full-cycle lament in the Psalter, demonstrating how compact the form could become.

The Davidic *maskil* designation — a wisdom-teaching song — places it in the editorial cluster of Pss 52, 54, 55, 74, 78, 88, 89, 142. The musical rubric *bi-neginot* (בִּנְגִינוֹת, "with stringed instruments") situates the psalm in temple performance with harps or lyres. The Septuagint renders the rubric *en hymnois* ("with hymns") — a less specific musical setting that suggests the LXX translators understood *neginot* as a general performance category.

The Hebrew petition of v. 1 is theologically dense: *Elohim be-shimkha hoshi'eni u-bi-gevuratkha tedineni* (אֱלֹהִים בְּשִׁמְךָ הוֹשִׁיעֵנִי וּבִגְבוּרָתְךָ תְדִינֵנִי, "O God, save me by your name, and vindicate me by your might"). Two divine instrumentalities are paired: the *name* (*shem*, שֵׁם) and the *might* (*gevurah*, גְּבוּרָה). In the ANE worldview, the name was the locus of effective power; to be saved "by the name" of a deity is to invoke the deity's full operational presence. Mesopotamian incantations regularly call on the *šumu* of a god to act; Egyptian ritual texts speak of the *ren* (true name) as the source of efficacy. But where ANE name-magic operates by invocation as compulsion, the Psalmist's "save me by your name" is petition as trust. The name is not a lever to compel God but a relational reality to invoke.

The poem belongs to **Book II of the Psalter (Pss 42–72)** and to the **Elohistic Psalter (Pss 42–83)**. Psalm 54 names *Elohim* in vv. 1, 2, 3, 4 and switches to the *Tetragrammaton* (*YHWH*) in v. 6 ("I will give thanks to your name, O LORD") and the unnamed pronoun in v. 7. This mixed pattern is characteristic of the Elohistic redaction, which was not absolute — divine-name traces sometimes survive editing.

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of Ps 54 in 4QPsa, with no significant variants from the Masoretic Text. The Septuagint follows the MT closely except for the *bi-neginot* rendering noted above.

ANE parallels: the closest formal cousin is the Akkadian *šuilla* prayer to a personal god in time of betrayal — "they have surrounded me, they have plotted against me" — invoking the god's *šumu* (name). But the Psalter's distinctive note is the conviction that the divine name *vindicates* (*tedineni*, from *din*, judicial verdict), not merely rescues. The psalmist asks not just for survival but for divine forensic recognition.

Date: linguistic features place Ps 54 in classical Biblical Hebrew. Most critics (Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate) treat it as a pre-exilic composition; the Davidic superscription is editorial framing rather than authorial attribution.

## Lens 2: Literature

Robert Lowth's parallelism is on full display in this brief psalm. Verse 1's two cola — "save me by your name / vindicate me by your might" — exemplify synonymous parallelism with intensification. Verse 3's "strangers have risen against me / ruthless men seek my life" intensifies *zarim* (strangers) into *aritzim* (ruthless men), pressing the threat from anonymity into menace.

The poem's structure is bipartite, hinged on the *selah* after v. 3:

- **Strophe I (vv. 1–3):** Petition and complaint. The speaker invokes God's name and might, then describes the threat — *zarim* (זָרִים, strangers / outsiders) and *aritzim* (עָרִיצִים, ruthless / tyrannical ones).
- **Strophe II (vv. 4–7):** Confidence and vow. The *behold* (הִנֵּה, *hinneh*, v. 4) marks the rhetorical pivot: God is helper, the speaker promises sacrifice, and v. 7 declares deliverance as already accomplished.

The chiastic envelope is divine-name framing: "save me by your *name*" (v. 1) and "give thanks to your *name*" (v. 6). The petition that opens the psalm becomes the praise that closes it; the *name* invoked in crisis becomes the *name* honored in deliverance. This is one of the tightest name-inclusios in the Psalter.

The textual variant at v. 3 deserves attention. The Masoretic Text reads *zarim* (זָרִים, strangers), but several Hebrew manuscripts and the parallel passage in Ps 86:14 read *zedim* (זֵדִים, "insolent ones"). Goldingay and Hossfeld-Zenger note that *zarim* is the more difficult reading and likely original; the change to *zedim* may have been to ease the reading, since calling the Ziphites "strangers" requires explanation — they were fellow Judahites, not foreigners. The hardness of *zarim* may itself be the interpretive key: betrayal makes kinsmen into strangers.

The terms in v. 3 — *zarim* and *aritzim* — appear in Lowth's "synonymous-with-intensification" pattern, but the semantic move is significant. *Zarim* describes the relational distance ("they are not my people"); *aritzim* describes the moral character ("they are oppressive, tyrannical"). The first is about who they are; the second is about what they do. Together they construct the betrayer as both alienated and violent.

The phrase *u-le-eyay nichtah ve-oyvay* (וּבְאֹיְבַי רָאֲתָה עֵינִי, "my eye has looked in triumph on my enemies," v. 7) literalized is "my eye has gazed on my enemies." Modern translators add "in triumph" to capture the implication of looking down on the defeated, but the Hebrew is restrained. The speaker simply says he has *seen*. The seeing is the vindication.

*Selah*, that uncertain term, here punctuates the complaint just before the pivot to confidence. Liturgically, it may have signaled a musical pause that allowed the worshiper to hold the threat before God before turning to trust.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Psalm 54 sits in the Davidic Elohistic cluster of Book II (Pss 51–72), placed after Ps 53's universal lament over human corruption and before Ps 55's lament over a betraying friend. The editorial sequence Ps 52–54 — Doeg the active killer, then universal corruption, then the Ziphite informers — constructs a typology of human treachery in the speech-violence register that runs through this part of the Psalter.

**Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 54 reads the Ziphites as the prototype of betrayers within the covenantal community — fellow Judahites who informed against David. The midrash extends the betrayal forward into Jewish history, applying the psalm to informers (*moserim*) in every generation. Rashi follows this line, glossing the Ziphites as the model of the *moser* — the Jew who turns over fellow Jews to gentile authorities, a category of profound concern in medieval rabbinic ethics. Ibn Ezra is more philological, parsing *zarim* and *aritzim* and noting the textual variant. Radak emphasizes the Davidic historical setting and reads the psalm as David's prophetic confidence in Yahweh's vindication. The Targum personalizes the *aritzim* as Saul's officers. In modern Jewish thought, Heschel and Buber both reflect on the moral category of the informer — Buber's *Right and Wrong* contrasts the Ziphite report ("Is not David hiding among us?") with the prophetic word that defends the persecuted. James Sanders has written on the canonical-historical superscriptions as Israel's "remembered Bible" — the way David's life becomes a frame within which later worshipers find their own crises. Liturgically, Ps 54 is recited in some Sephardic and Yemenite communities as a daily protective psalm; in Ashkenazi tradition, it appears in the *Shir shel Yom* cycle in some rites.

**Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 54 (LXX 53) reads the Ziphites christologically — they prefigure the Pharisees and Judas, those within the covenant community who betrayed Christ to outside authorities. Augustine's most striking move is his exegesis of "save me by your name": he reads it as a prayer to be saved by the *name of Jesus*, the divine name made flesh. Cassiodorus follows Augustine. Aquinas's *Postilla* preserves the moral reading: the *zarim* are the disordered passions in the soul; the *name* is the saving name of Christ.

The Reformers turned the psalm toward concrete persecution. Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* reads it as a model prayer for the persecuted believer who cannot rely on neighbor or prince. Calvin's 1557 commentary stays close to the Davidic narrative, warning Genevan readers that informants and betrayers operate within the church as within the world.

Modern critical readers extend the conversation. Gunkel established the form-critical reading. Mowinckel emphasized the cultic setting — Ps 54 was likely performed in a temple ritual of vindication and thanksgiving. Westermann classifies it among the individual laments that move from plea to praise. Kraus and Hossfeld-Zenger develop the most rigorous treatment of the Elohistic Psalter and Ps 54's place in it. Goldingay's *Psalms 42–89* reads the psalm pastorally: how does a worshiping community pray when its own people have informed against it? Tate's *Word Biblical Commentary* catalogs the lexical features. Wenham's *Psalms as Torah* reads it as moral formation — the psalm trains believers to entrust vindication to God rather than self-vengeance. Walter Brueggemann's *Message of the Psalms* treats Ps 54 as a model of "orientation to disorientation to new orientation." Patrick Miller's *They Cried to the Lord* notes the divine-name theology. Beth Tanner (with deClaissé-Walford and Jacobson) emphasizes the canonical-narrative function of the superscription.

**NT use.** Psalm 54 is not directly cited in the New Testament. Its theology of the divine name as saving instrument resonates with Acts 4:12 ("there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we must be saved") and Phil 2:9–11 (the name of Jesus before which every knee will bow). Augustine's name-of-Jesus reading is not explicit in the text but extends a trajectory the New Testament itself develops.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

Two tensions structure the theological work of Psalm 54.

**Tension 1: The historical superscription and the Augustine spiritualization.** The psalm's superscription locates it in a specific historical moment — the Ziphites' betrayal of David. But Augustine and most premodern Christian readers treat the enemies as spiritual: disordered passions, demonic powers, or general evil.

- **The historical-critical position (Hossfeld-Zenger, Kraus, Goldingay):** The superscription is editorial; the psalm originally addressed concrete enemies. Modern readers must take the historical specificity seriously. To spiritualize is to evade the political and communal reality of betrayal.
- **The Augustinian position:** Reading the psalm only historically traps it in David's biography. The Christian tradition reads it as a prayer for every persecuted soul, with the enemies expanded to include spiritual forces. The *name* invoked is the name of Christ, which the original psalmist did not yet know.
- **The canonical-historical synthesis (Childs, Sanders, Tanner):** Both readings are right. The historical superscription invites the reader into David's specific crisis, then offers the prayer to every later worshiper facing analogous betrayal. The psalm is *both* about the Ziphites *and* about the disordered passions, *both* about Saul's troops *and* about the spiritual forces of wickedness.

Steelman of the historical-critical view: To read Ps 54 only spiritually is to make it impossible for actual victims of actual informers to pray it. The persecuted Christian in a totalitarian state, the dissident reported to the secret police, the church member exposed to a hostile authority — all of these find Ps 54 unbearably specific. To allegorize their enemies into "the passions" is to silence their cry. The Augustinian reading is theologically rich; it is also politically dangerous when it dominates exclusively.

**Tension 2: Imprecation against the Ziphite betrayers.** Verse 5 prays "He will return the evil to my enemies; in your faithfulness put an end to them" (וּבַאֲמִתְּךָ הַצְמִיתֵם, *u-va-amitkha hatzmitem*). The verb *hatzmitem* — "destroy them, annihilate them" — is harsh. The psalmist asks God's *faithfulness* (*emet*) to be the instrument of their destruction.

- **The Lewis dilemma (*Reflections on the Psalms*):** C. S. Lewis acknowledged the imprecatory psalms make Christians uncomfortable but defended their honesty. We feel these things; pretending we do not is dishonest. The psalms teach us to bring our worst feelings to God rather than acting on them.
- **The Brueggemann / Zenger position:** The imprecation is a prayer, not an action. By naming the desire for the betrayers' destruction in worship, the worshiper renounces personal vengeance and entrusts judgment to God. The act of praying "destroy them" is the act of *not* killing them.
- **The pacifist Christian critique (some Anabaptist readers):** The imprecation is morally untenable for Christians who follow Jesus' command to love enemies. Such psalms must be read against the grain — as describing what fallen humans feel but not what they should pray.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 54 is the prayer of a person whose own community has turned informant. The Ziphites were not foreign invaders; they were fellow Judahites who used local knowledge to harm a neighbor. The psalm's theological gift is not to suppress this betrayal but to bring it to God's name. The petition "save me by your name" is the act of refusing to save oneself by the same instruments of speech-violence the betrayers used. The Ziphites speak words to Saul; the psalmist speaks words to God. Both are speech-acts; only one is the act of trust.

The closing question: When you read Ps 54, do you locate yourself as the persecuted David, the Ziphite informer, or the Saul who acts on the report? The psalm has space only for the first — but most lives, examined honestly, contain moments of all three. Praying this psalm requires acknowledging when you have been the persecuted, *and* when you have been the informer, *and* when you have acted on others' reports.

## The Thread

- **1 Samuel 23:19–24 and 26:1:** The Ziphites' twin betrayals of David — the narrative behind the superscription.
- **Psalm 86:14:** The parallel verse using *zedim* rather than *zarim* — the textual variant that illuminates the lexical choice in Ps 54:3.
- **Acts 4:12 and Philippians 2:9–11:** The New Testament's development of the divine-name theology that Ps 54 invokes — "save me by your name."
- **Romans 12:19:** "Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord" — the apostolic articulation of the principle that Ps 54:5 enacts: entrust judgment to God rather than seizing it oneself.
