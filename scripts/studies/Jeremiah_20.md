# Jeremiah 20: Pashhur, the Stocks, the Seducing God, and the Cursed Birth

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 20 opens with a name and an institutional title: *Pashhur the priest, the son of Immer, who was chief officer in the house of the LORD*. The political context is again Jehoiakim's reign (609–598 BC), with this incident probably falling in the years immediately after the temple sermon of chapter 7 and the broken-flask sign-act of chapter 19 (though some commentators place the chapter slightly earlier, perhaps under Jehoiakim's third or fourth year). The temple establishment had moved from rhetorical opposition (the *plot against Jeremiah* of 18:18) to physical action: arrest, beating, public humiliation in stocks at the upper Benjamin Gate of the temple complex.

The role of Pashhur is precisely specified in the Hebrew: *paqid nagid be-bet YHWH* (פָּקִיד נָגִיד בְּבֵית יְהוָה, "chief overseer in the house of the LORD"). The title combines two terms: *paqid* (administrative officer, with an etymology related to *paqad* — to muster, to oversee, to call to account) and *nagid* (leader, chief). Pashhur held the temple police office, with authority to arrest, discipline, and detain. The combination of titles places him at the top of the temple's internal-discipline hierarchy, second only to the chief priest. The prosopography is independently attested: a clay bulla bearing the inscription *belonging to Pashhur ben Immer* was recovered in the City of David excavations directed by Eilat Mazar (the bulla, sometimes called the "Pashhur bulla," is contemporary with Jeremiah; the impression preserves the elaborate Hebrew script and theophoric name structure typical of late-monarchic priestly seals). The *Immer* family is named in 1 Chronicles 24:14 as the sixteenth priestly course; Ezra 2:37 lists 1,052 returnees from the *Immer* family after the exile. Pashhur the chief officer of the temple was a real, archaeologically attested person in late seventh-century Jerusalem.

The material culture of v. 2 — the *mahpekhet* (מַהְפֶּכֶת) at the upper Benjamin Gate — is also specific. The *mahpekhet* (from the root *hpk*, "to overturn, to twist") is usually translated *stocks* but is more probably a *contortion frame* in which the prisoner's body was bent into an unnatural posture (head, hands, and feet locked into wooden slots that forced a stooped or crouching position). Such devices are attested in Mesopotamian and Egyptian iconography. The upper Benjamin Gate was on the north side of the temple complex, the gate through which traffic from Benjamin (and thus from Anathoth, Jeremiah's own town) passed — a deliberate location choice for public humiliation: any countryman of Jeremiah's coming up to the temple would see him there.

Authorship and dating: chapters 17–20 are widely treated as a structural unit climaxing in 20:14–18, the last and most extreme of Jeremiah's confessions. Lundbom defends the entire unit as Jeremianic; Holladay argues that 20:7–13 is a coherent confession but 20:14–18 is a separate fragment of similar voice; Carroll argues for a more layered redaction. Kathleen O'Connor's *Jeremiah: Pain and Promise* (2011) is the indispensable contemporary reading — O'Connor reads chapters 17–20 as *trauma literature*, the testimony of a community whose world has shattered and whose religious tradition is having to find language for survival under impossible conditions. Her trauma-informed reading is particularly necessary for chapter 20 because the chapter pushes prophetic speech to a place that classical Christian and Jewish theology has often been unwilling to follow.

Archaeological and textual evidence for chapter 20: the Pashhur ben Immer bulla, already noted; the Lachish Ostraca, especially Letter III's reference to "the prophet" whose words "weaken the hands of the people," which fits the social profile chapter 20 narrates; the late-Iron-Age gate-architecture excavated in the Ophel and the City of David; and the comparable confession-form material in Job 3, which the chapter's closing curse on birth (vv. 14–18) parallels so closely that the literary relationship has been debated since Duhm.

Key Hebrew terms: *Pashhur* (פַּשְׁחוּר) — possibly Egyptian-origin name, "son of Horus" or similar, a name attested on multiple seal impressions. *Magor-missabib* (מָגוֹר מִסָּבִיב, "Terror on Every Side," 20:3) — a Jeremiah-signature phrase (cf. 6:25; 20:10; 46:5; 49:29; Lamentations 2:22; Psalm 31:14). *Mahpekhet* (מַהְפֶּכֶת, "stocks/contortion frame," 20:2). *Pittiani* (פִּתִּיתַנִי, 20:7) — second-person perfect of *pth* with first-person object suffix, "you have deceived/seduced me"; the same verb is used for *seducing* a virgin in Exodus 22:15 (Heb 22:16) and for the kind of sexual or rhetorical inducement that makes the seduced party complicit (cf. Hosea 2:14, *I will allure her*). *‘Esh bo‘eret ‘atzur be-‘atzmotai* (אֵשׁ בֹּעֶרֶת עָצוּר בְּעַצְמֹתָי, "a burning fire shut up in my bones," 20:9). *Naqam* (נָקָם, "vengeance," 20:10, 12). *‘Arur ha-yom* (אָרוּר הַיּוֹם, "cursed be the day," 20:14) — the imprecation formula on a cosmic scale.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 20 has three sections of escalating intensity. (1) The prose narrative of Pashhur's action and Jeremiah's renaming oracle (vv. 1–6). (2) The fifth and most extreme of Jeremiah's confessions (vv. 7–13). (3) The cursed-birth lament (vv. 14–18) — the closing of the unit and, by general critical agreement, the climax of the prophetic confession-form in the Hebrew Bible.

The prose narrative of vv. 1–6 is structured as action–response. Pashhur acts (beats, locks, releases); Jeremiah responds with a renaming oracle (*the LORD does not call your name Pashhur, but Magor-missabib*). The renaming inverts power: the temple official who has the institutional authority to discipline the prophet is rhetorically renamed by the prophet using YHWH's name. The oracle then specifies Pashhur's coming exile (*To Babylon you shall go, and there you shall die*) and accuses him of false prophecy (*you and all your friends, to whom you have prophesied falsely*).

The confession of vv. 7–13 is the chapter's literary heart. Its structure is recognizable: address (v. 7), complaint (vv. 7–8, 10), interior turmoil (v. 9), confidence (v. 11), petition (v. 12), praise (v. 13). What distinguishes this confession from the four earlier ones is the verbal assault on YHWH himself in v. 7. *Pittitani YHWH va-‘epat* — *you have seduced me, YHWH, and I was seduced* — uses *pth* in the piel, the same verb used for seducing a betrothed virgin in Exodus 22 and for the rhetorical-spiritual inducement of Hosea 2:14. The verb places YHWH in the syntactical position of the seducer and Jeremiah in the syntactical position of the seduced; the prophet accuses God of having drawn him into prophetic vocation under false pretenses, since the labor has produced not vindication but mockery (*I have become a laughingstock all the day; everyone mocks me*). Then, with no transition, the famous fire-in-the-bones image of v. 9: when the prophet tries to stop speaking, the divine word burns inside him until he cannot hold it. The confession ends, again with no smooth transition, in the praise interlude of v. 13 — *sing to the LORD; praise the LORD! for he has delivered the life of the needy from the hand of evildoers*. The juxtaposition of the seduction-charge (v. 7) and the praise (v. 13) is part of what makes this confession so theologically productive: the chapter does not resolve the contradiction; it sets it in poetry and lets it stand.

The cursed-birth lament of vv. 14–18 is structurally separate but literarily continuous. The *‘arur ha-yom* formula echoes Job 3:1–11 with such precision that the literary relationship is unmistakable. *Cursed be the day on which I was born; let that day not be blessed... why did I come out from the womb to see toil and sorrow?* The motif of *the man who brought the news* (v. 15) is unique to Jeremiah; Job 3 has no parallel. The wish *that he had killed me in the womb, so my mother would have been my grave, and her womb forever great* (v. 17) is the most extreme version of the un-being motif in the Hebrew Bible — a wish not just for death but for never having been born.

Genre-wise, vv. 7–13 is a confession in the *qina*-related lament tradition; vv. 14–18 is a *birth-curse* of a kind otherwise paralleled only in Job 3 (and indirectly in the *‘oh that I had died in the womb* of Job 10:18–22). Stulman calls vv. 14–18 the "anti-creation" speech: the prophet who has been called by YHWH from before he was formed in the womb (cf. Jer 1:5) now wishes the womb had been his grave. The chapter folds back upon the call narrative.

The MT-LXX divergence in chapter 20 is again moderate; the LXX preserves the unit substantially intact but with a few minor compressions in the prose framing of vv. 1–6.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the Jewish trajectory, chapter 20 is read primarily through the figure of Jeremiah as the suffering prophet par excellence. Sirach 49:7 names Jeremiah as the one *who suffered evil at their hands*. Bavli Megillah 14b includes Jeremiah among the seven prophets to the gentile nations and treats his sufferings as exemplary. Pesikta Rabbati 26 develops a haggadic narrative around Jeremiah's protest at the destruction of the temple, drawing on the chapter's confessional material. Maharsha (Rabbi Shmuel Eidels, 17th c.) on Bavli Gittin 56b treats the *pittitani* of v. 7 as a covenant-suit (*riv*), reading Jeremiah's accusation against God as a legitimate covenant-grievance procedure rather than a personal insult — God has made covenant promises and the prophet is invoking them. Rashi reads the *pittitani* more conservatively as *you have persuaded me*, softening the seduction undertone; Radak preserves the harder reading; Ibn Ezra and Abrabanel both retain the strong reading of *pth*. Jewish post-Holocaust reception has been especially intense around chapters 17–20: Wiesel's *Messengers of God* (1976) treats Jeremiah as the prophet of the Shoah; Fackenheim's *To Mend the World* draws on the chapter's confessional material; Levinas reads the *pittitani* as the testimony of the survivor whose covenant fidelity persists *as accusation*.

In the Christian trajectory, Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah* 18 and 19 treat chapter 20 typologically — the prophet bound in stocks at the temple gate prefigures Christ bound and beaten at the high priest's house; the *pittitani* of v. 7 prefigures the *my God, my God, why have you forsaken me* of Mark 15:34 (Psalm 22:1); the cursed-birth lament prefigures *it would have been better for that man if he had not been born* (Mark 14:21) and the eschatological lament of *they will say to the mountains, fall on us* (Luke 23:30). Jerome's commentary largely follows Origen. Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah* on chapter 20 is one of the more theologically strained units of the *Lectures*: Calvin defends the legitimacy of the confession's intensity but reads *pittitani* as *you have persuaded me beyond my strength*, reducing the seduction-charge to a strong expression of overwhelming divine call. The Reformed *imitatio Christi* tradition reads the chapter's suffering as a pattern Christians must expect to share. The Anabaptist martyr tradition — preserved in *Martyrs Mirror* (van Braght, 1660) — repeatedly cites Jeremiah 20 as a pattern for Anabaptist witnesses tortured and killed; the chapter became central to a tradition that needed prophetic precedent for the costliness of nonviolent witness.

Critical scholarship has read chapter 20 in successive ways. Duhm treated the confessions as *spiritual diary* — direct access to the inner life of the prophet. Mowinckel and Bright read them as Deuteronomistic constructions in the prophet's voice. Holladay returns to a Duhm-like reading but with redactional sophistication. Brueggemann reads the confessions as *prayer-as-prayer*, irreducible to either historical biography or theological dogma. O'Connor's trauma reading is the contemporary watershed: the chapter is *survivable speech under unbearable conditions*, and its theological status is precisely as testimony from inside a wound, not as theology offered from a position of safety.

Where the trajectories diverge most sharply is on what to do with v. 7. The Christian *imitatio Christi* tradition can absorb the verse if it is read as a typological prefigurement of the cry of dereliction. The Jewish covenant-suit tradition can hold the verse as legitimate prophetic speech inside an ongoing relationship. The trauma-theological tradition (O'Connor, Brueggemann) reads the verse as the testimony of survival itself: the prayer that accuses God is the prayer that *remains in covenant*, because the alternative is silence or apostasy, and the prophet has chosen neither. The classical Reformed and Catholic theological tradition has more difficulty: divine impassibility and the goodness of God seem at first to be embarrassed by *pittitani*, and Calvin's softening of the verb in the *Lectures* is, to a contemporary reader, a sign of dogmatic discomfort.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The first tension is the seduction-charge of v. 7. *Pittitani YHWH va-‘epat*: *you have seduced me, YHWH, and I was seduced*. Heschel in *The Prophets* (1962) treats the verse as the high-water mark of his thesis on prophetic *pathos*: the God of Israel is genuinely vulnerable to the prophet's accusation because the prophetic relationship is *real* relation, not a one-way emanation; the prophet's protest matters to God because God has chosen relation as the mode of divine self-disclosure. Classical Reformed impassibility (Calvin *Institutes* 1.13.1) holds that God is not affected by creatures and that prophetic *pathos*-language is anthropomorphic accommodation. Jewish midrash (Maharsha, the Tosafot tradition) reads the verse as a covenant-grievance procedure (*riv*) within an established legal-relational framework; the prophet has standing to bring the grievance because the covenant has given him standing. Trauma theology (O'Connor) reads the verse as *survivable speech*: the prayer that accuses is the prayer that remains, and the alternative is to walk away from the relationship altogether. The least intuitive reading to a contemporary Western Christian is the Maharsha covenant-suit reading: the prophet is not impiously accusing God but lawfully invoking covenant standing; the seduction-charge is a juridical move within a relationship the prophet still trusts. To steelman this fairly: it places the accusation in the same conceptual register as Abraham's *shall not the judge of all the earth do right?* (Gen 18:25) and Moses's intercession at Sinai (Ex 32:11–14); Hebrew Bible piety accommodates legal-style protest because the covenant is genuinely two-sided. Where this leaves us: the contemporary Christian reader who recoils at *pittitani* should consider whether the recoil is exegetical or dogmatic; the verse stands in the Hebrew Bible's settled witness, and its presence is not an embarrassment but a permission.

The second tension is the cursed-birth lament of vv. 14–18 and its relationship to Christ's cry from the cross. Bonaventure in *Itinerarium* and the broader Catholic *suffering servant* tradition read the chapter christologically: the prophet who curses the day of his birth prefigures the Christ who cries *eli, eli, lama sabachthani*; the parallel is taken to license the legitimacy of human despair within the life of faith because Christ has assumed it. Reformed *imitatio Christi* under affliction (Calvin's reading, Bunyan's *Grace Abounding*, Spurgeon's pastoral writings on depression) develops a similar pattern: the believer who curses the day will discover, with Jeremiah, that he is in the company of the canonical witnesses and ultimately of Christ himself. The Anabaptist martyr tradition (*Martyrs Mirror*) reads the chapter as the testament of nonviolent resistance: the prophet does not strike back at Pashhur, does not retaliate, but bears witness from inside the system that has crushed him. Jewish post-Holocaust theology (Wiesel, Fackenheim) reads the chapter as testimony of the *survivor who curses but does not abandon*: the prayer cursing birth is prayer, not apostasy; it is spoken inside the covenant, not outside it. Steelman the trauma-theological position (O'Connor): the chapter does not need a christological resolution to be theologically meaningful; the suffering speaks in its own register, and Christian readers who rush to typological closure may foreclose what the text is trying to do — testify to a God who can be accused without ceasing to be God. Where this leaves us: the chapter offers itself as either a christological prefiguration (the dominant Christian reading) or a survivable speech inside the covenant (the dominant Jewish and trauma-theological reading); both readings are honorable, and the same reader can hold both.

One unresolved question presses through the chapter and confronts every reader. If even Jeremiah accused God of seducing him — and remained in the covenant — what does it mean to remain in covenant with a God you have *not* accused? The chapter's silence on this is louder than its speech.

## The Thread

Three cross-references run from this chapter. The cursed-birth lament of 20:14–18 runs into Job 3:1–11 (the canonical parallel: *let the day perish on which I was born*) and onward into Christ's cry from the cross in Mark 15:34 quoting Psalm 22:1 — three canonical moments of un-being that hold the place where the language of faith and the language of despair are no longer separable. The *fire in the bones* of 20:9 (*there is in my heart as it were a burning fire shut up in my bones, and I am weary with holding it in*) runs into Luke 24:32 (*did not our hearts burn within us while he talked to us on the road?*) — the prophetic burn becomes the Emmaus burn, and what was held under torture becomes what is shared at table. And the *Magor-missabib* renaming of 20:3 — *the LORD does not call your name Pashhur, but Terror on Every Side* — runs into the broader prophetic tradition of YHWH renaming the powerful (Daniel and his companions in Babylon, Saul becoming Paul, Simon becoming Peter, the seven churches of Revelation 2–3 each receiving a new judgment-name) and reaches its eschatological form in Revelation 2:17, *to the one who conquers I will give a white stone, with a new name written on the stone that no one knows except the one who receives it*. The prophet who was renamed by YHWH in his call narrative (Jer 1:5, *before I formed you in the womb I knew you*) ends his confession-cycle by renaming his persecutor and cursing the day of his own naming — and the canonical river runs from there to a final renaming that this side of glory none of us yet know.
