# Psalm 28 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 28 carries the brief superscription *le-david* (לְדָוִד, "of David"), the standard Davidic attribution found across most of Book I (Psalms 1-41). As with neighboring psalms, contemporary critical scholarship (Goldingay, Hossfeld-Zenger, Tate) treats the *lamed*-attribution as editorial — a reflection of how the post-exilic compilers framed the psalm rather than evidence of literal Davidic authorship. The Septuagint adds no additional historical-setting note for Psalm 28 (in contrast to Psalm 27's LXX expansion), suggesting it was received as a generalized prayer of distress rather than tied to a specific Davidic episode.

Form-critically, in **Hermann Gunkel's** classification, Psalm 28 is an **individual lament** (*Klagelied des Einzelnen*) that pivots into a **thanksgiving** (*Danklied*) midway through. This pivot at v. 6 — *baruch Yahweh ki shama qol tachanunai* (בָּרוּךְ יְהוָה כִּי־שָׁמַע קוֹל תַּחֲנוּנָי, "Blessed be the LORD! For he has heard the voice of my pleas for mercy") — is one of the clearest examples in the Psalter of what **Joachim Begrich** in his classic 1934 study identified as the **"certainty of being heard" (*Erhörungsgewissheit*)** transition. Begrich proposed that such mid-psalm shifts reflect a cultic moment when a priest or temple prophet pronounced a *Heilsorakel* ("oracle of salvation") to the petitioner; the psalm's tonal shift from petition to praise marks the worshipper's response. **Westermann** developed this into his standard movement-from-plea-to-praise model. Whether or not the cultic-oracle hypothesis holds historically, the literary pivot is undeniable.

The Hebrew vocabulary deserves attention. The opening verb *eqra* (אֶקְרָא, "I call") is the standard lament-psalm address-verb. The plea *al-techerash mimmenni* (אַל־תֶּחֱרַשׁ מִמֶּנִּי, "do not be silent toward me," 28:1) uses the verb *charash* (חָרַשׁ), which means to be deaf or to keep silent — a striking anthropomorphic plea that God not "play deaf." Divine silence as crisis is a recurring biblical motif (Pss 35:22, 39:12, 83:1, 109:1; Hab 1:2). The petitioner's fear is concrete: *vedimiti im-yordei vor* (וְנִמְשַׁלְתִּי עִם־יוֹרְדֵי בוֹר, "I shall become like those who go down to the pit") — *yordei vor* is one of the standard Hebrew euphemisms for the dead, those who descend into Sheol's pit (compare Pss 30:3, 88:4; Isa 38:18).

The image of God as **rock** runs through the psalm. *Tsuri* (צוּרִי, "my rock," 28:1) draws on the standard divine-rock metaphor (Deut 32:4, 30; 1 Sam 2:2) — Yahweh as the immovable foundation, the high refuge, the cliff one can hide behind. This is paired with shield language in 28:7 (*magen*, מָגֵן). The phrase *devir qodshekha* (דְּבִיר קָדְשֶׁךָ, "your most holy sanctuary," 28:2) is technically the inner sanctum of the temple — the holy of holies. The petitioner's gesture of *be-nas'i yadai* (בְּנָשְׂאִי יָדַי, "when I lift up my hands") toward this *devir* is the standard Israelite prayer-posture (compare 1 Kgs 8:22, 38; Ps 134:2).

The psalm's most disturbing verse — at least to modern ears — is 28:4: *ten lahem kefolam ukhroa ma'aleleihem* (תֶּן־לָהֶם כְּפָעֳלָם וּכְרֹעַ מַעַלְלֵיהֶם, "give to them according to their work, and according to the evil of their deeds") — the **imprecation**. This is a *lex talionis* prayer: requite them precisely as they have requited others. The verb *gemul* (גְּמוּל, "due reward, recompense") in the second half of the verse names the principle directly. We will return to this in Lens 4.

The psalm closes with a corporate turn: *hoshi'ah et-amekha uvarekh et-nachalatekha ure'em venase'em ad-ha-olam* (הוֹשִׁיעָה אֶת־עַמֶּךָ וּבָרֵךְ אֶת־נַחֲלָתֶךָ וּרְעֵם וְנַשְּׂאֵם עַד־הָעוֹלָם, "save your people and bless your heritage; be their shepherd and carry them forever," 28:9). The **shepherd** metaphor (*ra'ah*, רָעָה) for Yahweh's relationship to Israel is foundational across the Hebrew Bible (Gen 48:15; Pss 23, 80; Isa 40:11; Ezek 34) and underlies the kingly-shepherd ideal in ancient Near Eastern royal ideology — Mesopotamian and Egyptian kings routinely styled themselves as shepherds of their people. That an individual lament concludes with a corporate-shepherd petition has occasioned scholarly comment: the speaker may be the king, or the editorial frame may have generalized a personal psalm into a national one (Mowinckel, Hossfeld-Zenger).

Textually, the MT of Psalm 28 is stable; the LXX rendering is straightforward. There are minor variants in 28:8 — the MT *l'amo* ("for his people") versus some manuscripts and the LXX reading *le'ammo* — that do not affect interpretation.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Psalm 28 is nine verses of compact Hebrew poetry exhibiting Lowth's parallelism throughout:

- **Synonymous**: "Hear the voice of my pleas for mercy, / when I cry to you for help" (28:2).
- **Antithetic**: implicit between the wicked who "speak peace... while evil is in their hearts" (28:3) and the speaker whose interior matches his speech.
- **Synthetic**: "The LORD is my strength and my shield; / in him my heart trusts, and I am helped" (28:7) — each line advances the previous.

The strophic structure breaks into two halves with a clear pivot:

- **Strophe 1 (28:1-2)**: Address — call to Yahweh, plea against silence.
- **Strophe 2 (28:3-5)**: Petition and imprecation — do not lump me with the wicked; give them their due.
- **PIVOT (28:6)**: Blessed be the LORD — the petitioner's certainty that the prayer has been heard.
- **Strophe 3 (28:7)**: Personal thanksgiving.
- **Strophe 4 (28:8-9)**: Corporate enlargement — Yahweh as the strength of his people, the saving refuge of his anointed; petition for the whole people.

This shape — lament, pivot to thanksgiving, corporate enlargement — is sometimes called the **"answered-prayer"** (*aniyat tefillah*) movement and is paradigmatic for Israelite prayer.

Several literary devices unify the whole:

- **Vocabulary echoes between halves**: "Hear the voice" (28:2, lament) and "he has heard the voice" (28:6, thanksgiving). What is asked in distress is granted in answer.
- **The "work/works" wordplay (28:4-5)**: the wicked's *ma'aseh yedeihem* ("work of their hands," 28:4) is set against the *ma'aseh Yahweh* ("works of the LORD," 28:5) which they ignored. The two "works" are juxtaposed as a moral verdict.
- **The "rock-shield-strength-shepherd" chain**: 28:1 (rock), 28:7 (strength, shield), 28:8 (strength, refuge), 28:9 (shepherd) — a cumulating series of divine titles.
- **The *anointed* (*meshicho*) in 28:8**: this is the only explicitly royal vocabulary in the psalm, and it ties the personal lament to Davidic kingship and to the broader hope-in-the-anointed-king tradition that runs through Pss 2, 18, 20, 21, 45, 72, 89, 110, 132.

The psalm has no *selah* markers. Some commentators (Tate, Goldingay) see a chiastic embedding around the central pivot, but the linear lament-to-thanksgiving structure is more obvious.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Placement in Book I.** Psalm 28 sits in the cluster of Davidic prayers (Pss 25-30) that move between confidence, integrity, distress, and thanksgiving. Specifically, Psalm 28 follows the trust-and-lament dialectic of Psalm 27 and prepares for the great theophany hymn of Psalm 29 and the thanksgiving of Psalm 30. The editorial placement suggests an arc: Psalm 27's "wait for the LORD" — Psalm 28's anguished call and answered prayer — Psalm 29's storm-glory of Yahweh — Psalm 30's thanksgiving for healing. The cluster is theological progression: waiting, hearing, beholding, recovering.

**Jewish trajectory.** The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of Psalm 28 consistent with the MT. *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 28 emphasizes the "do not be silent" plea: God's silence is the worst of all afflictions, worse than active opposition, because it leaves the petitioner without orientation. **Rashi** reads the imprecation in 28:4 as David's prayer against Doeg the Edomite and other enemies — specific, not generalized — and stresses the *measure-for-measure* (*middah keneged middah*) principle of biblical justice. **Ibn Ezra** notes the pivot at v. 6 and treats the psalm as a model of how prayer is answered while the petitioner is still on his knees. **Radak** highlights the corporate close, reading 28:8-9 as evidence that the king's prayer becomes the people's prayer.

In modern Jewish thought, **Sarna** (*Songs of the Heart*) emphasizes the prayer-posture of lifted hands toward the *devir*. **Levenson** treats the psalm as evidence of how individual and corporate prayer interpenetrate in Israelite religion — the king's lament expands organically into the nation's. **Buber** reads the "do not be silent" plea as the fundamental religious cry, the demand for divine speech that makes the human-divine encounter possible at all.

**Christian trajectory.** **Augustine**'s *Enarratio in Psalmum 27* (LXX numbering = Ps 28 MT) reads the psalm christologically: the speaker is Christ in his passion, crying out from the cross-experience of divine silence (echoing Ps 22). Augustine **spiritualizes the imprecation**: the wicked who are torn down (28:5) are vices and demonic powers, not human enemies; the prayer is for God's just dismantling of evil itself. **Cassiodorus** treats the psalm as a model of penitential prayer that moves from contrition to confidence. **Aquinas** in his Psalms commentary handles the imprecation through the distinction between the wish for justice (legitimate) and the wish for personal vengeance (illegitimate).

**Luther**'s *Operationes in Psalmos* read 28:1's "lest I become like those who go down to the pit" as the crisis of *Anfechtung* — the trial of faith in which divine silence threatens to drag the believer into despair. For Luther, the psalm is a model of how the conscience cries out in *tentatio*. **Calvin**'s 1557 commentary stresses the **covenantal frame** of the imprecation: David is not pursuing personal vengeance but appealing to the covenant's promise that the Judge of all the earth will do right (Gen 18:25). The wicked of 28:3-5 are the covenant-breakers whom God himself has pledged to oppose. Calvin's reading aims to defuse the moral scandal by re-locating the prayer within the covenantal courtroom.

**Modern critical scholarship.** **Gunkel** classified the psalm as an individual lament with thanksgiving conclusion. **Mowinckel** and **Begrich** developed the cultic-oracle hypothesis around the v. 6 pivot. **Westermann** used Psalm 28 as a paradigm of his lament-to-praise movement. **Kraus** (*Psalmen 1-59*) emphasizes the shepherd-king vocabulary at the close. **Hossfeld-Zenger** read the psalm's corporate ending as evidence of its post-exilic editorial reshaping into a community psalm. **Goldingay** (*Psalms*, 2006) emphasizes the realism of the psalm — the petitioner is genuinely in danger, the prayer is genuinely raw, the shift to praise is genuinely answered.

**Brueggemann**'s framework places Psalm 28 squarely in **disorientation moving to new orientation**. He treats the imprecation in 28:4 as **lament-as-protest** — the prayer that names injustice and demands divine response is theologically essential, not embarrassing; the alternative is silent acquiescence in evil. **Patrick Miller** highlights the "voice" motif (the petitioner's voice, the LORD's voice, the answered voice) as the psalm's organizing thread. **William P. Brown** notes the **gesture-and-posture** dimension: lifted hands toward the inner sanctuary, the body's enacted prayer. **Beth Tanner** sees the psalm as a model of how individual prayer becomes corporate intercession.

**New Testament use.** Psalm 28 is not directly cited in the NT, but its themes echo. The cry against divine silence anticipates Mark 15:34 and the Gethsemane prayers (Heb 5:7). The "shepherd" close (28:9) reverberates in John 10 and 1 Peter 5:4. The "anointed" in 28:8 anticipates messianic application.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The imprecation in 28:4.** "Give to them according to their work, and according to the evil of their deeds." How do Christians and thoughtful readers handle the prayer for divine retribution against enemies?

**Position 1a (Augustine, Christian-allegorical).** The "wicked" of 28:3-5 are demonic powers and the vice-residue in the believer's own soul; the imprecation is a prayer for God to destroy evil itself, not human enemies. This is the dominant patristic reading, and it preserves the psalm for Christian liturgy at the cost of treating its surface meaning as not-quite-literal.

**Position 1b (Calvin, Reformed-covenantal).** The imprecation is an appeal to the covenant Judge against covenant-breakers. The petitioner is not seeking personal vengeance but echoing God's own commitment to justice. The prayer is appropriate within a covenantal framework where the wicked are objectively the enemies of the covenant. This reading retains the surface meaning but limits its scope.

**Position 1c (Brueggemann, lament-as-protest).** Imprecation is theologically necessary. The psalmist refuses to pretend that injustice is acceptable; he names it and demands divine response. The alternative — pious silence in the face of evil — is moral and theological failure. The imprecation is the cry of victims who refuse to be quiet, and the church loses something essential when it bowdlerizes the Psalter. Erich Zenger's *A God of Vengeance? Understanding the Psalms of Divine Wrath* (1996) is the standard development of this reading.

**Position 1d (Aquinas, distinction-based).** A wish for *justice* (that wrongs be righted by the proper authority) is legitimate; a wish for *personal revenge* is illegitimate. The imprecation is the former, properly addressed to God who is the legitimate authority. Romans 12:19 ("vengeance is mine, says the Lord") undergirds this: the believer renounces personal vengeance precisely by handing it over to God.

**Steelmanning the simple-objection reading**: Even granting all these distinctions, Jesus' explicit teaching in Matthew 5:44 ("love your enemies, pray for those who persecute you") and Luke 23:34 ("Father, forgive them") pulls in the opposite direction. A serious Christian reader has to acknowledge that the imprecation creates real friction with the Sermon on the Mount, and that Christian appropriation of Psalm 28 cannot simply mimic its retributive surface. The patristic spiritualization may have been too glib, but the impulse — to read these prayers in light of the cross — is not arbitrary.

**Where this leaves us on the imprecation.** The four positions are partially complementary. Brueggemann is right that the church loses something when it sanitizes the lament tradition; victims of evil need permission to name evil and demand divine response. Calvin is right that the prayer belongs within a covenantal framework rather than being raw vengeance. Aquinas is right that there is a real distinction between justice and revenge. Augustine is right that Christians read the psalm in light of Christ. The most defensible synthesis: pray Psalm 28's imprecation honestly when you have been genuinely wronged, addressing it to God as the just Judge; trust that God's response to evil includes both judgment and the cross; do not pretend the prayer is anything other than what it is.

**Tension 2: Divine silence (28:1).** "Do not be deaf to me, lest I become like those who go down to the pit."

**Position 2a (Mystical-via-negativa, John of the Cross).** Divine silence is a stage in spiritual maturation — the *dark night* in which the soul's attachment to consolations is purified. The petitioner's experience of silence is real but not finally negative.

**Position 2b (Lament-tradition, Brueggemann).** Divine silence is genuine crisis, not a higher pedagogy. The psalmist is right to protest it. To soften the protest is to misread the psalm.

**Position 2c (Christological).** Divine silence finds its deepest expression in Holy Saturday — God's silence over the dead Christ. The psalm's "lest I become like those who go down to the pit" is unwittingly answered by Christ's actual descent. This reading (developed in different ways by Hans Urs von Balthasar and Karl Barth) connects the psalm's anguish to the heart of the gospel.

**Where this leaves us on tension 2.** Silence in the Bible is genuinely ambiguous — sometimes pedagogy, sometimes crisis, sometimes divine restraint, sometimes the unbearable absence that drives the prayer in the first place. Psalm 28 takes silence as crisis, and the rest of the canon does not contradict that reading even when it adds others.

**Closing question.** When have you prayed against someone's silence — God's, or another person's — and what did you actually want them to say?

## The Thread

- **Psalm 22:1-2** — "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?... by night, but I find no rest" — the supreme expression of the divine-silence crisis Psalm 28 addresses.
- **Romans 12:19** — "Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord" — Paul's reframe of imprecation: the believer hands the prayer for justice over to God rather than executing it personally.
- **Hebrews 5:7** — "In the days of his flesh, Jesus offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, to him who was able to save him from death" — the Gethsemane echo of Psalm 28's anguished call.
- **Revelation 6:10** — "How long, O Lord, holy and true, before you will judge and avenge our blood?" — the martyrs' imprecation that shows Psalm 28's prayer remaining live in the New Testament.
