# Jeremiah 17: The Engraved Heart, the Tree by the Stream, and the Sabbath Gate

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The political situation behind Jeremiah 17 is the late Judean monarchy in its final agonies. Most of the material in chapters 17–20 is best dated to the reign of Jehoiakim (609–598 BC), a vassal king installed by Pharaoh Necho II after the brief reign and execution of Jehoahaz. The Battle of Carchemish in 605 BC (recorded in the Babylonian Chronicle, BM 21946) had recently shifted the Near Eastern balance from Egypt to Babylon, and Judah found itself a small kingdom oscillating between empires while internal life proceeded as if nothing fundamental had changed. The high places, the Asherim, the trade conducted through Jerusalem's gates on the Sabbath — Jeremiah's targets in this chapter — describe a society going about ordinary religious and economic business under the shadow of an empire that would, within two decades, dismantle it.

The material culture of this world is late Iron Age II Judah. Domestic and royal architecture had matured, the lmlk-stamp jar tradition had given way to rosette-stamp jars used by the late seventh-century administration, and sealed bullae — small clay impressions made by carved seals — were proliferating in administrative centers. The cache of bullae recovered from the City of David includes the seals of figures named in Jeremiah's circle: Baruch ben Neriah (the prophet's scribe), Gemariah ben Shaphan, Jehucal ben Shelemiah (Jer 37:3), and the priest Pashhur ben Immer who appears in chapter 20. Lesser-known but indispensable for situating this period are the Lachish Ostraca (Lachish IV, Stratum II), Hebrew letters scratched on potsherds from the very last years before Jerusalem's fall — military correspondence that confirms the Babylonian siege pattern Jeremiah describes and that uses the characteristic late-Judean orthography of his book.

Authorship and dating of Jeremiah is famously contested. The traditional position attaches the book to Jeremiah ben Hilkiah, a priest from Anathoth, with Baruch ben Neriah as his scribe (Jer 36 narrates the dictation, burning, and re-dictation of the scroll). The MT and LXX of Jeremiah differ substantially — the LXX is roughly one-eighth shorter and orders the foreign-nation oracles differently — and the Qumran fragments (4QJer-a, 4QJer-b, 4QJer-d) attest both textual traditions, suggesting two parallel editions in late Second Temple Judaism. Critical scholarship since Mowinckel has divided the book into source layers (poetic A oracles, Deuteronomistic prose B and C). Of the major commentators, William Holladay (Hermeneia) defends an essentially Jeremianic core; Robert Carroll (OTL) reads the book as a Deuteronomistic literary construct; Jack Lundbom (Anchor) defends Jeremianic origin for most material including the prose; William McKane (ICC) proposes a "rolling corpus" of accreted tradition; Walter Brueggemann reads canonically while granting redaction; and Louis Stulman emphasizes the book's coherence as exilic theology of unraveling. For chapter 17 specifically, the prose Sabbath sermon of vv. 19–27 is widely regarded by critical scholars as later (Carroll, Holladay flag it as Deuteronomistic or post-exilic) while Lundbom argues for substantive Jeremianic origin reworked by Baruch.

Archaeological and textual evidence relevant to chapter 17 includes the late-Iron-Age Tel Dan inscriptions and Mesha Stele as parallels for inscribed-stone royal language (the "engraved with iron pen, with a point of diamond" image of 17:1); the Ketef Hinnom silver amulets recovered by Gabriel Barkay in 1979 (late seventh century BC, contemporary with Jeremiah and inscribed with a form of the priestly blessing of Numbers 6:24–26, evidence of textual piety in the very Hinnom valley Jeremiah denounces in chapter 19); and the gates of Jerusalem themselves — the People's Gate and the Sabbath gate of 17:19 likely refer to gates in the eastern wall whose foundations have been partially excavated in the City of David and Ophel.

Key Hebrew terms structure the chapter. *Baruk ha-gever* (בָּרוּךְ הַגֶּבֶר, "blessed is the man," 17:7) — *gever* is the strong word for a man, contrasting with *adam* and *enosh*; the same construction opens Psalm 1 and the Beatitudes (LXX *makarios*). *Arur* (אָרוּר, "cursed," 17:5) is the antithesis, the standard imprecation formula of Deuteronomy 27. *Et* and *shamir* (עֵט and שָׁמִיר, "iron pen" and "diamond/adamant point," 17:1) — *shamir* in later rabbinic legend becomes the supernatural worm that cuts the temple stones. *‘Aqov ha-lev* (עָקֹב הַלֵּב, "deceitful is the heart," 17:9) — *‘aqov* shares the root with Jacob (the supplanter) and means "twisted" or "crooked." And *Shabbat* (שַׁבָּת, 17:21–27) — the cessation-day, the chapter's climactic legal-religious term.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Jeremiah 17 is a deliberately heterogeneous chapter. It opens with a stark inscriptional metaphor (17:1–4), pivots to a wisdom poem in Psalm-1 cadence (17:5–8), gives a proverbial unit on the heart and on ill-gotten wealth (17:9–11), turns to a temple-confession addressed to YHWH (17:12–13), erupts into the third of Jeremiah's "confessions" (17:14–18), and closes with a long Deuteronomistic-style sermon on Sabbath observance at the city gates (17:19–27). Form-critically the chapter is a microcosm of the book's genres: prophetic oracle, wisdom poem, liturgical confession, lament, and sermon-as-sign-act.

The wisdom unit of 17:5–8 is the most famous literary feature of the chapter. Its parallelism with Psalm 1 is so close that the question of literary direction (which is older?) has occupied commentators since Duhm. Holladay argues Jeremiah is the source and the psalm dependent; others reverse the relation; the most defensible view is that both draw on a common stock of late pre-exilic Zion-wisdom imagery in which the *tree planted by water* served as a stable metaphor for covenant fidelity. The image itself depends on the agricultural reality of Judah: a tree whose roots reach the wadi survives the *qadim* drought wind, while a *shrub* (*‘ar‘ar*, 17:6) in the wilderness withers. The contrast of *cursed/blessed* turns the unit into a wisdom *makarism* anchored in covenant theology.

The verse 17:9 is one of the most quoted lines in the prophetic corpus and one of the most contested. *‘Aqov ha-lev mikol* — "the heart is more crooked than anything." The form is comparative; the meaning is "the heart is the most twisted of all things." The next colon, *we'anush hu*, uses a participle from *anash* (incurable, mortally sick, the same root as the place-name Enosh and the noun *makka anusha*, "incurable wound," in Jer 30:12). The verse stands alone, without explicit literary context, and functions as a wisdom maxim — which is why it has been so easily detached and used as a freestanding theological proof-text.

Jeremiah's third confession (17:14–18) is shorter than the first two (11:18–23; 12:1–6) and the later ones (18:18–23; 20:7–18) but is recognizably the same form: address to YHWH, complaint about adversaries, plea for vindication, imprecation. *Heal me, O LORD, and I shall be healed; save me, and I shall be saved* (17:14) is a chiastic parallelism in which divine action and human result are bound together — a Hebrew form of the *passivum divinum* that Bonhoeffer noticed in the Beatitudes.

The Sabbath sermon (17:19–27) is the longest sustained Sabbath unit in the prophetic corpus. Its prose register, formulaic *koh amar YHWH*, conditional *if-then* rhetoric, and concern with covenant continuity all mark it as Deuteronomistic in style. Critical readers (Carroll, Duhm) treat it as a late insertion; Holladay reads it as a genuine Jeremianic sermon recast by Baruch in Deuteronomistic idiom; Lundbom defends substantial Jeremianic content. Whatever the redactional history, the sermon's placement is theologically purposeful: it follows the confession's appeal for personal vindication with a public, civic appeal for national vindication keyed to a single visible practice — gate-Sabbath observance.

The MT-LXX divergence in chapter 17 is relatively modest compared to other parts of Jeremiah. The LXX preserves verses 1–4 in a slightly different order and omits a few cola in the Sabbath sermon, suggesting the prose section was still fluid in the period when the LXX *Vorlage* was copied.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Jeremiah's place in the canonical arc is the hinge of pre-exilic prophecy. He stands between the Assyrian-era prophets (Isaiah, Amos, Hosea, Micah) and the exilic-postexilic ones (Ezekiel, Second Isaiah, Haggai, Zechariah). Chapter 17's twin themes — the deep-set sinfulness of the human heart and the covenant claim on Sabbath — anticipate two major exilic theological developments: the new-covenant promise of Jeremiah 31 (in which YHWH writes the *torah* on the heart, undoing the iron-pen engraving of 17:1) and the Sabbath as exilic identity-marker (Ezekiel 20; Isaiah 56, 58).

In the Jewish trajectory, Jeremiah 17 is read primarily through the Sabbath sermon and through the heart maxim. Sirach 49:6–7 already lists Jeremiah among the great prophets whose words shaped Israel's memory of the destruction. Bavli Megillah 14b includes Jeremiah among the seven prophets who prophesied to the gentile nations. Targum Jonathan paraphrases the *‘aqov* of 17:9 with *megalgel* ("revolving, turning"), softening the absoluteness of the indictment by reading it as a description of human inconsistency rather than an essential corruption. Rashi reads 17:9 as referring to the wicked specifically, not all humanity ("the heart of the wicked is more deceitful than all things"); Radak similarly limits the scope; Ibn Ezra reads it as a general observation about how the heart deceives even its own owner; Abrabanel develops a more philosophical reading in which the heart's deceit is the function of the appetitive faculty resisting the intellective one. The Jewish dual-yetzer tradition — the *yetzer ha-tov* (good inclination) and *yetzer ha-ra* (evil inclination) of Berakhot 61b — provides a rich anthropology that does not require reading Jeremiah 17:9 as a universal claim of total depravity.

In the Christian trajectory, Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah* (preserved in twenty Greek and fourteen Latin homilies via Jerome) treats chapter 17 in homily 17, applying the cursed/blessed contrast typologically: the cursed shrub is the Jew under the law without Christ, the blessed tree is the Christian planted by the waters of baptism — a supersessionist reading whose hermeneutical violence Origen's modern readers must register honestly. Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah* is more philological and lighter on allegory but inherits much of Origen's framework. Aquinas does not write a sustained commentary on Jeremiah but cites 17:9 in *ST* I-II.81–83 on original sin and 17:5 on the trust-in-man problem in *ST* II-II.129. Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah* (Geneva, 1563, delivered as classroom lectures and published posthumously) treats 17:9 as the *locus classicus* for total depravity and forms the exegetical backbone of *Institutes* 2.1–2.3. The Reformed dogmatic tradition's reading of "total depravity" depends heavily on this verse plus Romans 3 and Genesis 6:5; the Lutheran tradition uses 17:9 similarly but with more emphasis on simul iustus et peccator.

Critical scholarship (Duhm, Mowinckel, Holladay, Carroll) has historically been suspicious of dogmatic readings of 17:9 and has tended to read the verse as wisdom anthropology rather than systematic anthropology. Contemporary commentators (Lundbom, Brueggemann, O'Connor) read the chapter more whole, attentive to its movement from the engraved heart of v. 1 to the new-covenant heart of chapter 31.

Where they diverge: the Christian trajectory reads chapter 17 as pointing forward to a christological resolution (Jesus's controversies over Sabbath in Mark 2–3 happen against precisely this Jeremianic background — *the Son of Man is lord even of the sabbath* answers a long-running prophetic argument about how to keep it). The Jewish trajectory reads chapter 17 within the cycle of the Tisha B'Av kinot and the Sabbath haftarah cycle: Sabbath itself is the cosmic sign of redemption, not a problem requiring christological resolution. Critical-redactional readings bracket both trajectories and ask what the text meant in its successive editorial layers.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two genuine tensions structure this chapter.

The first is the anthropology of 17:9. Calvin's reading is straightforward: the heart is *‘aqov mikol* — more deceitful than anything else — and *anush*, incurably sick. There is no inner reservoir of moral capacity that survives the fall; whatever good appears in the unregenerate is "common grace" or "civic righteousness," not saving virtue. Total depravity, in the Reformed sense, does not mean every human is as bad as possible; it means no part of the human is untouched by sin. This is the workhorse reading from *Institutes* 2.1.8–9 and 2.3.1–5. The Wesleyan and Catholic positions accept the seriousness of fallenness but insist on prevenient grace: God's prior action restores enough of the *imago Dei* that the will, while wounded, is not annihilated — Aquinas's *ST* I.93–95 and Wesley's *Predestination Calmly Considered* (1752) both develop this. The Eastern Orthodox tradition (Lossky, *The Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church*) reads original sin as inheritance of mortality and corruption rather than inherited guilt, and reads the heart in Jeremiah 17:9 as the heart in need of *theosis*. The least intuitive position to a modern Western Christian is the Jewish dual-yetzer reading: the heart contains both the *yetzer ha-tov* and the *yetzer ha-ra*; the *yetzer ha-ra* is not pure evil but the appetitive drive necessary for life ("were it not for the *yetzer ha-ra*, no man would build a house, marry, or beget children" — Bereshit Rabbah 9:7); Jeremiah 17:9 describes the result of letting the *ra* dominate, not an essential property of the heart. To steelman this: the dual-yetzer reading better fits the actual Hebrew of 17:9 (a comparative observation about the heart's *‘aqov*-ness, not an ontological claim), better fits the wisdom-form of the verse (proverbs describe rather than dogmatize), and better fits the canonical context (Deuteronomy 30:11–14 insists the commandment is *not too hard* and is *near you, in your mouth and in your heart*; the heart is not categorically incapable of obedience). Where this leaves us: the Reformed reading has the homiletical sharpness, but the Jewish reading has the better claim on the verse's plain sense; the Catholic and Orthodox positions hold a careful middle.

The second tension is the Sabbath sermon (17:19–27). The Sabbatarian-Reformed position treats Sabbath as part of the moral law, transferred to the Lord's Day; the Westminster Confession 21.7 explicitly reads Genesis 2 plus the Decalogue as establishing a perpetual *one in seven* obligation, with Christian Sunday as its New Covenant form. The Lutheran and Catholic position is that Sabbath as Sabbath is ceremonial-Mosaic and abrogated; Sunday is a distinct ecclesial institution rooted in the resurrection rather than in transferred Sabbath law (Aquinas *ST* II-II.122.4; Luther's *Large Catechism* on the third commandment). The Anabaptist tradition has more variability: some Anabaptist readings (e.g., Yoder's broader political theology) read Sabbath as anti-empire — a refusal to participate in the productive economy that the prophetic literature consistently arraigns. The Jewish reading, articulated most powerfully in Heschel's *The Sabbath* (1951), reads Sabbath as a *cathedral in time*, a cosmic-redemptive sign that anticipates the messianic age; here Jeremiah 17 is read not as a finger-wagging legal sermon but as a prophetic appeal to remember what Sabbath is *for* — the rhythm of creation that resists the empire's logic of perpetual production. Steelman the Heschel position: Jeremiah is not legalistically demanding gate-closure; he is asking whether Judah will recognize that its political-economic survival is bound up with whether it can remember a different time. Where this leaves us: the surface-level question (is Sabbath law binding on Christians?) is downstream of a deeper question (what is Sabbath for?), and on the deeper question Heschel and the Reformed sabbatarians agree more than either would admit.

One unresolved question presses through the chapter. If the heart is engraved with sin in 17:1 and only YHWH can write a new covenant on the heart in 31:33, what kind of agency does the human have between those two moments? The chapter offers no clean answer.

## The Thread

Three cross-references run from this chapter through the canon. The Sabbath sermon (17:19–27) is the prophetic background to Jesus's controversies in Mark 2:23–3:6, where *the sabbath was made for man, not man for the sabbath* answers the Jeremianic question about how to keep Sabbath without making it a death-trap. The blessed tree of 17:7–8 — *like a tree planted by water, that sends out its roots by the stream* — runs into Psalm 1, into Ezekiel's vision of the river-fed trees in 47:12, and finally into Revelation 22:2 where the *tree of life* by the river bears twelve manner of fruit. And the engraved heart of 17:1 (sin written with an iron pen on the tablets of the heart) finds its inverse in Jeremiah 31:33, *I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts* — the new covenant that 2 Corinthians 3:3 transfers to the Christian assembly, *written not with ink but with the Spirit of the living God, not on tablets of stone but on tablets of human hearts*. The chapter that begins with the iron pen ends, at the canon's far edge, with the Spirit's writing.
