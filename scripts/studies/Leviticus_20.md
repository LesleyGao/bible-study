# Leviticus 20: Their Blood Is upon Them

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Leviticus 20 revisits the material of chapter 18 but in a different register: 18 is apodictic prohibition ("You shall not"), 20 is casuistic with penalty ("If a man… he shall surely die"). Israel Knohl's *The Sanctuary of Silence* (1995) assigned both chapters to the Holiness School (H) but noted that 20's penalty-focused formulation makes it the rhetorical peak of the code's enforcement apparatus, where the covenant's most serious violations are given their ritual and judicial weight. Jacob Milgrom's Anchor Bible *Leviticus 17–22* treats 20 as a deliberate literary mirror of 18, intended to be read alongside it — two angles on the same ethic, with 18 articulating the boundary and 20 specifying the cost of breach. For Knohl, H's distinctive project was to take P's ritual purity system and extend its seriousness into the social and sexual spheres; 20's capital penalties are what that extension looks like when cashed out in civic terms.

The chapter presupposes a small, face-to-face legal community. "The people of the land" (*ʿam hāʾāreṣ*, v. 2, v. 4) are the ones who execute the sentence — not a king's officers, not a priestly tribunal, but the assembled community. This is the same institutional world Ruth and the elders at the city gate inhabit: village-scale adjudication by adult male heads of household. The stoning penalty (v. 2, v. 27) enacts communal judgment in the most literal way: everyone throws, so everyone participates, and no single person bears the blood. Similar practices are attested in Mesopotamian and Hittite contexts, though the biblical rhetoric is distinctive in making communal participation a point of principle rather than a procedural convenience.

The Molech prohibition (vv. 1–5) has the same cultural-archaeological background as Leviticus 18:21. Larry Stager's excavations at the Tophet of Carthage (1976–1979) and the subsequent work of Lawrence Stager and Joseph Greene recovered thousands of urns containing the cremated remains of infants and very young children alongside stelae bearing dedicatory formulas. Whether *mōlek* in Hebrew names a deity (the older Eissfeldt-modified view) or an offering-type (from the Punic *molk* inscriptions) remains debated. Phoenician-Punic evidence strongly suggests ritual child sacrifice was practiced in related West Semitic religion; the Hebrew polemic maps onto this. Later biblical texts (2 Kings 23:10; Jer 7:31; 32:35) place such practice in the Valley of the Son of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, suggesting the prohibition had real purchase.

Mediums and necromancers (vv. 6, 27) are framed through the verb *zānâ*, "whoring," the standard biblical metaphor for covenant infidelity. The practice is vividly illustrated in 1 Samuel 28, where Saul — who had himself earlier enforced Leviticus 20:27 by expelling the mediums — consults the woman of Endor to raise Samuel's shade. The narrative presumes the practice existed, was prohibited, and was practiced anyway. Ugaritic and Mesopotamian parallels abound: the Akkadian *muluggu* ritual for consulting ghosts, the Ugaritic *rapiʾum* texts on ancestor cult.

The sexual catalog of vv. 10–21 draws on the incest list of chapter 18 but rearranges it around graded penalties. Death is specified for the most central violations (father's wife, daughter-in-law, male-male intercourse, woman-and-her-mother, bestiality). The lesser penalty of being "cut off" (*kārēt*) or of "bearing iniquity" or of dying childless applies to the more peripheral cases. This grading is itself evidence of careful legal thinking — the text is not simply maximalist but distinguishes seriousness.

ANE incest law comparanda, as with chapter 18, include Hittite Laws §§189–195, Middle Assyrian Laws A §§40–59, and Code of Hammurabi §§154–158. The biblical catalog is more systematic and more restrictive than any of these. Hittite and Assyrian law permit certain unions Leviticus forbids (stepmother in some post-death cases, sister-in-law in others). The death penalty for adultery (v. 10) has close parallels in Hittite §197 and Middle Assyrian A §§13–16, though in Assyrian law the husband retained discretion to pardon. In Leviticus, the judicial process is communal and the husband's discretion is absent; this is a striking restriction of private patriarchal power.

The menstrual prohibition (v. 18) reappears from 18:19, here with the added penalty of *kārēt*. The phrase "he has made naked her fountain" (*meqôrāh heʿĕrâ*) uses distinctive language — *māqôr* is the "spring" or "source" of blood — pulling the prohibition into the category of ritual-boundary violations that disrupt the body's sacred economy of blood.

The "blood guilt upon them" refrain (*dĕmêhem bām*, vv. 9, 11, 12, 13, 16, 27) is legal-technical: it means that no bloodguilt redounds on the executioners, because the condemned has forfeited the protection of the blood-avenger system. This is the same idiom used at Josh 2:19 for Rahab's pact.

The land-vomit theology returns at v. 22, explicitly tying the penalty apparatus to the covenant geography: the purpose of judicial enforcement is to keep the land from vomiting Israel as it vomited the Canaanites.

Key Hebrew terms: *mōt yûmāt* (מוֹת יוּמָת), "he shall surely be put to death"; *kārēt* (כָּרֵת), "cutting off" — the divine sanction distinct from judicial execution; *tōʿēbâ* (תּוֹעֵבָה), "abomination," v. 13; *tebel* (תֶּבֶל), "perversion," v. 12; *zimmâ* (זִמָּה), "depravity," v. 14; *dĕmêhem bām* (דְּמֵיהֶם בָּם), "their blood is upon them"; *ʿam hāʾāreṣ* (עַם הָאָרֶץ), "people of the land."

Manuscript witnesses: MT, SP (close tracking), 4QLevᵇ and 4QLevᵈ fragments, LXX (which occasionally softens; the Greek for v. 13 uses *bdelygma* and renders the death sentence with *thanatousthōsan*).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter's architecture is carefully organized. It opens with Molech and necromancy (vv. 1–6), pivots through a holiness preamble (vv. 7–8) and a filial piety command (v. 9), moves into the graded sexual catalog (vv. 10–21), resumes the land-vomit theology and holiness frame (vv. 22–26), and closes with a return to mediums and necromancers (v. 27). The inclusio — necromancy at opening and closing — brackets the whole chapter as a warning against covenant infidelity of the sort that draws Israel across the boundary that distinguishes it from the nations.

The penalty gradation is literarily precise. The fullest form is *mōt yûmāt*, "he shall surely be put to death," with the method sometimes specified (stoning for Molech and necromancy; burning for the daughter-and-mother case of v. 14). A reduced form is *kārēt*, translated "cut off from their people," which the rabbinic tradition understood as a divine rather than judicial sanction — a shortening of life or denial of offspring administered by God, not by the court. A still lesser form is "they shall bear their iniquity" (vv. 17, 19) or "they shall die childless" (vv. 20, 21). The grading matters both for legal seriousness and for literary effect: the text builds from the most heinous violations to the less grave.

Milgrom argued that Leviticus 20 is stylistically later than Leviticus 18, suggesting H reshaped earlier material with added penalties. Knohl read the two chapters as companion pieces by the same school, with 18 articulating the norm and 20 giving enforcement. The stylistic evidence — formulaic *mōt yûmāt*, the "blood upon them" refrain, the specific method-of-execution notes — does suggest a legal-judicial editorial hand distinct from pure apodictic composition.

Jerome T. Walsh has drawn attention to a subtle shift in verse 13's grammar: where 18:22 addresses only the male ("you shall not lie with a male"), 20:13 specifies both parties ("both of them have committed an abomination; they shall surely be put to death"). Walsh argued this suggests 20:13 extends the prohibition to both partners in a way 18:22 did not — a claim Saul Olyan and others have contested on philological grounds. The plural verb in 20:13 may simply reflect the casuistic form's general practice of stating consequences for all parties to the act.

The literary relationship to Deuteronomy is worth noting. Deut 21:18–21 applies the death penalty to a rebellious son in a procedure that resembles Lev 20:2's communal stoning; Deut 22:22 parallels Lev 20:10 on adultery. The shared penalty apparatus suggests Deuteronomy and H are operating in overlapping legal traditions, though with significant literary independence.

Textual variants are minor. LXX's rendering of v. 13 has been foundational for later Greek-speaking debate (the word *arsenokoitēs* in 1 Cor 6:9 and 1 Tim 1:10 appears to be a Pauline coinage built from the LXX of Lev 18:22 and 20:13, pairing *arsen* "male" with *koitē* "bed"). This is one of the most philologically important bridges from Leviticus into New Testament sexual ethics and was developed influentially by David Wright and subsequently contested by Dale Martin and others.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** The Mishnah built on Leviticus 20 to develop the whole structure of Jewish capital jurisprudence. Mishnah Sanhedrin 7 lists the four modes of judicial execution (stoning, burning, decapitation, strangulation) and Sanhedrin 7:4 assigns specific offenses from Lev 20 to each. The striking thing about rabbinic treatment is how strenuously it restricted, rather than expanded, the penalty apparatus.

Mishnah Makkot 1:10 preserves a foundational dispute: "A Sanhedrin that executes once in seven years is called destructive. Rabbi Eleazar ben Azariah says: once in seventy years. Rabbi Tarfon and Rabbi Akiva say: had we been on the Sanhedrin, no one would ever have been put to death." The rabbinic method was to erect such rigorous evidentiary requirements (two eyewitnesses, prior warning, explicit acknowledgment by the accused of the warning) that judicial capital punishment became nearly impossible. Bavli Sanhedrin 40b–41a develops the procedural requirements in detail. Bavli Sanhedrin 54a–56b engages the sexual catalog specifically. The result is a Jewish legal tradition that affirms the text's seriousness while hollowing out its application — the penalties teach the gravity, not the prescribed practice.

The concept of *kārēt* received extensive treatment. Mishnah Keritot 1:1 lists thirty-six offenses that incur *kārēt*, most of them from Leviticus 18 and 20. The rabbinic understanding distinguished judicial execution (the court's responsibility) from *kārēt* (God's direct action — either premature death, denial of offspring, or exclusion from the world to come). This two-track system handled Lev 20's distinction between capital offenses and *kārēt*-only offenses.

Maimonides codified the whole apparatus. *Hilchot Issurei Biah* 1 catalogs the forbidden sexual relations; *Hilchot Sanhedrin* 14–15 addresses capital procedure; *Hilchot Avodah Zarah* 6 addresses Molech. His *Guide for the Perplexed* 3.41 offers a philosophical rationale: capital sanctions reflect the gravity of the offenses in theory, while evidentiary restrictions prevent miscarriage in practice.

Medieval commentary on the chapter is dense. Rashi reads verse by verse, often drawing on Sifra's expansion. Ibn Ezra attends to syntactic nuance, and on v. 13 preserves the philological debate. Ramban (Nahmanides) in his Leviticus commentary offers an extensive theology of the Land of Israel's sensitivity to sexual transgression — a theme he shared with chapter 18 but developed further here. Sforno and Hirsch continue the tradition.

Modern Jewish treatment remains committed to rabbinic restrictiveness. Jacob Milgrom's Anchor Bible commentary reads the death penalties as rhetorical gravity markers — the text signals the offenses' seriousness by assigning the most severe penalty without necessarily requiring that it be carried out. Baruch Levine's JPS Leviticus treats the chapter historically. Nahum Sarna engages the literary parallel to chapter 18. Rabbi Jonathan Sacks's *Covenant and Conversation* develops the rabbinic reading: the Torah teaches through law the values a society should hold sacred, whether or not the penalties are ever imposed. Rabbi Steven Greenberg's *Wrestling with God and Men* engages v. 13 and the philological work of Olyan and Milgrom for an affirming Orthodox Jewish reading; this has been contested within Orthodoxy.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic engagement with Leviticus 20 worked primarily through the lens of Augustine's (and before him, already in Tertullian's) distinction between moral, ceremonial, and judicial law. Augustine in *Contra Faustum* and *City of God* argued that the moral content of the sexual prohibitions remained binding on Christians while the judicial penalties lapsed with the cessation of Mosaic civil polity. Chrysostom's homilies read the chapter primarily for moral instruction. Origen's *Homilies on Leviticus* 11 allegorized the penalties as spiritual consequences.

Thomas Aquinas formalized the tripartite distinction in *Summa Theologiae* I-II qq. 99–105: moral precepts are universal and continuing, ceremonial precepts were fulfilled in Christ, judicial precepts governed Israel's civic polity and have lapsed (though their equity continues to inform natural justice). On Aquinas's scheme, Lev 20's prohibitions remain binding as moral law, while its specific capital sanctions are judicial and do not bind contemporary Christian states as matters of revealed law — though the offenses' gravity may justify civil penalties derived from natural-law reasoning. Aquinas in *Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 154 analyzes the sexual offenses in detail.

Reformation thought diverged on the civil use. Luther distinguished moral and civil uses of the law and generally held that Christian magistrates were not bound to apply Mosaic penalties, though the moral content remained. Calvin, in his *Commentary on the Last Four Books of Moses* and in the *Institutes* 4.20, held a more robust view of the continuing civil use: the moral content of Lev 20 informed Geneva's civil law, and some penalties were applied (adultery and blasphemy were capital offenses in Geneva). The Reformed tradition produced the most extensive Protestant engagement with the civil enforcement question. Anabaptists (Menno Simons, the Schleitheim Confession) rejected the civil use entirely, insisting that the sword was outside the church's vocation.

Seventeenth-century Puritan political theology carried Calvin's view into the Massachusetts Bay Colony, which adopted the "Body of Liberties" (1641) with capital statutes drawn partly from Leviticus 20. This became the high-water mark of Christian theocratic enforcement and was gradually abandoned over the following centuries.

John Wesley's sermons engage the chapter through the lens of grace — the penalties reveal what sin deserves and what Christ has borne. The evangelical tradition has broadly followed this reading.

Modern critical scholarship (Wellhausen, Noth, Milgrom, Knohl) treats the chapter historically. Walter Brueggemann, in *Theology of the Old Testament* and in smaller studies of Leviticus, reads the severity as moral seriousness — a community that takes these offenses so gravely is making a statement about the sanctity of bodily, familial, and covenantal bonds. Terence Fretheim's work on Pentateuchal theology frames the chapter within Israel's vocation. Chris Wright's *Old Testament Ethics* reads the penalty structure as signaling priorities rather than prescribing practice. Richard B. Hays's *The Moral Vision of the New Testament* treats the moral content as continuing in the New Testament's sexual ethic. Robert Gagnon engages the chapter extensively in *The Bible and Homosexual Practice*.

Affirming Christian readings (Vines, Brownson, Wink) apply the same hermeneutic they bring to Lev 18: the capital penalty signals honor-shame-patriarchal categories that modern Christian ethics need not inherit; the moral content, if isolable from those categories, should be examined on its own terms. Jerome Walsh's philological work on v. 13 has been used in this debate.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Jewish and Christian traditions converge in refusing the chapter's capital penalties as contemporary practice — rabbinic Judaism through procedural restriction, Christianity through Aquinas's tripartite distinction and the long abandonment of theocratic enforcement. They diverge on the moral content: both traditions historically affirmed the sexual prohibitions as continuing; contemporary progressive streams in both traditions have disputed specific elements. The debate is not exegetical so much as hermeneutical — how do we hold together a text whose seriousness we affirm and whose penalty structure we reject?

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Moral, judicial, ceremonial — and what continues to bind?**

The Augustinian-Thomistic tripartite distinction is the central Christian framework for handling Leviticus 20. It allows the tradition to affirm the moral weight while releasing the judicial penalties. But the distinction is not self-evident from the text, and it has critics.

- **Catholic teaching** (CCC 1965–1974; Aquinas *Summa Theologiae* I-II qq. 99–105; *Veritatis Splendor* 43–53) embraces the tripartite division and treats Lev 20's moral content as continuing, its ceremonial-cultic elements as fulfilled in Christ, its judicial penalties as lapsed but informative for civil prudence.
- **Reformed teaching** (Calvin *Institutes* 2.8; Westminster Confession 19.3–5) accepts the tripartite division but emphasizes the continuing general equity of the judicial precepts — their underlying principles inform Christian civil polity even after the specific penalties lapse. Theonomy (Rushdoony, Greg Bahnsen) revived a more maximalist reading of the judicial precepts' continuing authority.
- **Lutheran teaching** is more reserved about systematic categorization, focusing instead on the distinction between law and gospel. Lev 20's penalties function within the "first use" of law (civil restraint) and the "second use" (convicting of sin), not as a blueprint for Christian society.
- **Wesleyan teaching** incorporates the tripartite distinction while emphasizing the sanctifying function of the moral content.
- **Orthodox teaching** similarly accepts the moral-ceremonial-judicial distinction but integrates it with sacramental and theosis frameworks.
- **Jewish teaching** has no analog. All 613 commandments remain formally binding on Jews, with rabbinic halakha determining what is operative. The rabbinic solution is not to distinguish moral from judicial but to distinguish what can be practiced (without Temple, Sanhedrin, or sovereign authority) from what cannot. Maimonides in *Hilchot Sanhedrin* spells this out.
- **Critical scholarship** (Milgrom, Knohl) treats the penalty apparatus as historically specific and leaves contemporary application to communities.

The least intuitive position is theonomy's — that the specific judicial penalties of Lev 20 remain normative for Christian civil authority today. Steelmanned: if Scripture is God's word and if God revealed these penalties as just, then the burden of proof falls on those who would set them aside; the tripartite distinction, on theonomy's reading, is a post-Constantinian accommodation to the fact that Christian political authority waned, not a principled hermeneutic. Rushdoony's *Institutes of Biblical Law* articulates this view at book length; Bahnsen's *Theonomy in Christian Ethics* refines it. Most of Reformed theology rejects theonomy, but the rejection requires explaining why the tripartite distinction is principled rather than convenient — an explanation that is not trivial.

**Where this leaves us:** Jewish and Christian traditions broadly agree that the capital penalties of Leviticus 20 do not bind contemporary civil authority. They disagree about why — rabbinic procedural restriction, Thomistic tripartite distinction, Lutheran two-kingdoms, Anabaptist rejection of the civil use, theonomic reassertion — and these disagreements have political consequences.

**Tension 2: Are the death penalties rhetorical gravity markers or actual prescriptions?**

- **Milgrom's reading**: the penalties signal the offenses' gravity, and the rabbinic procedural restrictions (which he partially reconstructs as already present in the late Second Temple period) were part of the original legal design. The text teaches values through severity, not execution.
- **Brueggemann's reading**: the severity reflects a community's moral seriousness; the penalties are what a society says when it wants to mark an offense as covenant-defining.
- **Traditional Jewish reading** (Maimonides; rabbinic): the penalties are real, but the procedural requirements make them nearly impossible to apply.
- **Traditional Christian reading** (Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin): the penalties were real prescriptions for Mosaic Israel and have lapsed with the Mosaic civil polity, though their moral gravity continues.
- **Theonomic reading** (Rushdoony, Bahnsen): the penalties remain normative.
- **Critical scholarship** (Knohl, Walter Houston): the penalties functioned as ideology before they functioned as practice; their rhetorical weight was primary.
- **Contemporary progressive readings** (Brueggemann in other modes; Walter Wink): the penalty apparatus belongs to ancient honor-shame and patriarchal social logic and is not normative; the moral content, where applicable, must be re-examined without the penalty scaffolding.

The least intuitive position may be Milgrom's — that ancient Israel's own legal tradition never intended literal enforcement. Steelmanned: ancient law-codes across the ANE (including Hammurabi) routinely prescribed penalties that procedural and social practice softened; the rabbinic restrictions are not innovations but unfolding of constraints already implicit; and the Torah's rhetorical style — emphatic *mōt yûmāt*, formulaic repetition — is legal rhetoric, not administrative instruction.

**Where this leaves us:** Whether we read the penalties as real prescriptions that lapsed or as rhetorical markers that never required full enforcement, the practical result — no contemporary Jewish or mainstream Christian community applies the penalties — is the same. The hermeneutical question is how to hold the text's severity while declining its civic form.

**Unresolved question:** If the capital penalties of Leviticus 20 are not binding, what exactly is the moral content we are claiming to retain? Traditional readings answer: the prohibitions themselves. Progressive readings answer: the underlying values of covenant seriousness, bodily dignity, and protection of the vulnerable. But these answers diverge sharply on specific cases (adultery, same-sex relations, mediums, blasphemy), and the disagreements are not going to be resolved by exegesis alone.

## The Thread

First: Saul and the witch of Endor (1 Sam 28). Saul had himself enforced Lev 20:27 by expelling the mediums and necromancers from Israel. His consultation of the woman of Endor is a deliberate narrative reversal: the king who enforced the Holiness Code violates it at the moment of greatest desperation. The story is not just about Saul's failure; it is about the law's force when its enforcer turns. The chronicler notes (1 Chr 10:13) that Saul's necromancy was among the reasons for his death — the *kārēt* of Lev 20:6 visibly enacted.

Second: Paul's *arsenokoitēs*. In 1 Cor 6:9 and 1 Tim 1:10, Paul uses a term that appears to be coined from the LXX of Lev 18:22 and 20:13 — *arsen* ("male") plus *koitē* ("bed"). The word is philologically disputed (David Wright's work defends the Levitical derivation; Dale Martin's work contests it), but the plausible derivation makes Paul's vice-lists a direct uptake of the Leviticus 20 prohibition. This is the central bridge from Leviticus into the NT's sexual ethic, and the contemporary debate about same-sex relations runs through this philological question.

Third: Hebrews 10:26–31. The NT's most explicit re-deployment of the Lev 20 framework appears in Hebrews' warning against deliberate sin after knowledge of the truth. The "fearful expectation of judgment" echoes the "blood is upon them" refrain; the insistence on the terror of covenant breach maps onto Lev 20's penalty logic. Hebrews does not lift the Leviticus penalties into the church, but it retains their rhetorical weight: covenant seriousness is not diminished in Christ; it is intensified.

Fourth: Jeremiah 7 and 32. Jeremiah's Tophet oracles explicitly indict Judah for Lev 20:2's Molech offense and threaten the land-vomiting of Lev 20:22 as imminent reality. The prophet reads Leviticus 20 as the code that Judah has broken, and the exile becomes the fulfilled penalty. For Jeremiah, Leviticus 20 is not theoretical; it is the explanatory framework for national catastrophe.
