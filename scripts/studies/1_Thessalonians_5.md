# 1 Thessalonians 5: Pax et Securitas and the Counter-Slogan of the Day

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The hinge of 1 Thessalonians 5 is verse 3, and verse 3 is a piece of imperial propaganda turned inside out. *Eirēnē kai asphaleia* — "peace and security" — is the Greek translation of *pax et securitas*, the Roman state slogan that announced what the empire claimed to deliver. The phrase is attested across Julio-Claudian and Flavian numismatics. *RIC* (Mattingly and Sydenham, *The Roman Imperial Coinage*) catalogs *Pax* and *Securitas* as personified deities on the reverses of denarii and sestertii from Augustus through Vespasian and beyond; *Securitas Augusta* and *Securitas Publica* legends multiply under Nero and Galba. Inscriptions across the Greek East repeat the formula in dedications to emperors who have brought *eirēnē* to a particular province or city. Thessalonica's politarchs presided over a city whose civic identity was bound to that promise: as a "free city" (civitas libera) confirmed in inscriptions like the Vardar Gate text (IG X 2,1 no. 126), Thessalonica had backed the right side at Philippi and lived inside the Pax Romana with material privileges.

When Paul writes that "while people are saying 'peace and security,' then sudden destruction will come upon them as labor pains come upon a pregnant woman, and they will not escape" (5:3), he is picking up a coin or a placard and turning it over. Karl Donfried's *Paul, Thessalonica, and Early Christianity* (2002) argues this is the single most explicitly counter-imperial sentence in the Pauline corpus. Helmut Koester, James Harrison (*Paul and the Imperial Authorities at Thessalonica*, 2011), and Richard Horsley (*Paul and Empire*, 1997) read the entire chapter through this verse: the day of the Lord stands over against the day of Caesar, and the assembly that hails Jesus as *kyrios* in counter-imperial *apantēsis* is a subversive presence in any city built around the imperial cult.

The political situation explains the urgency. Acts 17:7 records the charge against the Thessalonian believers: "they are all acting against the decrees of Caesar, saying that there is another king, Jesus." James Harrison has shown that the loyalty oaths sworn by provincial cities to the imperial house were the political glue of provincial life. To proclaim a competing *kyrios*, *parousia*, *euangelion*, and *eirēnē* was to court accusations of *maiestas* — treason against the imperial majesty — and the charge was not idle. The Thessalonians had already been mob-attacked, dragged before the politarchs, and forced to post bond (Acts 17:5–9). Paul's eschatology in chapters 4–5 is the consolation of a church already paying the political cost of refusing the imperial slogan.

Material culture sits behind the chapter at multiple points. The "thief in the night" image (5:2) draws on standard urban anxieties about night-time burglary in unwalled neighborhoods; Plutarch and Seneca use the trope. The light/darkness dualism (5:4–8) parallels both Greco-Roman moral philosophy (the Stoic and Cynic contrast of the *phōs* of reason against the *skotos* of vice) and Jewish apocalyptic dualism, especially the Qumran sectarians' "sons of light" and "sons of darkness" in *1QS* (the Community Rule, with its Two Spirits doctrine in cols. 3–4) and *1QM* (the War Scroll). The armor metaphor in 5:8 — breastplate of faith and love, helmet of the hope of salvation — picks up Isaiah 59:17 (the LORD as warrior) and is recognizable to anyone who has watched a Roman legionary on parade in a provincial capital. The "holy kiss" (5:26) reflects Greco-Roman household greeting practice baptized into ekklēsia liturgy; by the second century it is a fixed liturgical element (Justin, *First Apology* 65).

The trichotomous formula in 5:23 — "spirit and soul and body" (*pneuma kai psychē kai sōma*) — sits at the boundary of Jewish whole-person anthropology and Greek partitive psychology. Hans Walter Wolff's *Anthropology of the Old Testament* and James Barr's critique of Greek-influenced biblical anthropology have argued for decades that Hebrew *nephesh*, *ruach*, and *basar* are not separable parts but overlapping perspectives on the unified person. Whether Paul is anthropologically Hebrew with Greek vocabulary or genuinely partitive is one of the open questions of the chapter.

**Authorship, dating, manuscripts.** Authentically Pauline, written from Corinth around AD 50–51, anchored by the Gallio inscription at Delphi. Textual witnesses are excellent: P46 (c. AD 200), Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Alexandrinus. Verse 9's hēmas/hymas variant (whether "us" or "you" are not destined for wrath) is the chapter's main meaning-affecting variant; the better-attested reading is *hēmas*.

**Key terms.** *Hēmera kyriou* (ἡμέρα κυρίου) — the day of the Lord, a prophetic technical term. *Eirēnē kai asphaleia* (εἰρήνη καὶ ἀσφάλεια) — peace and security, the Roman slogan. *Pneuma kai psychē kai sōma* (πνεῦμα καὶ ψυχὴ καὶ σῶμα) — spirit and soul and body. *Prophēteia* (προφητεία) — prophecy. *Philēma hagion* (φίλημα ἅγιον) — holy kiss.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

1 Thessalonians 5 layers four genres in twenty-eight verses. The first eleven verses (5:1–11) are apocalyptic eschatology in the same register as 4:13–18, picking up a continuation peri formula (*peri de tōn chronōn kai tōn kairōn*, 5:1) that signals Paul is still answering questions Timothy brought back from Thessalonica. Verses 12–22 are a paraenetic chain — a string of imperatives in classic Hellenistic moral-letter form, of the kind Seneca writes to Lucilius — but Christianized by their orientation toward eschatological vigilance. Verses 23–24 form a wish-prayer (*optative* mood: "may the God of peace himself sanctify you"). Verses 25–28 are an epistolary closing with greetings, an oath-charge to read the letter aloud to the assembled congregation, and a grace formula.

Within 5:1–11 the device-stack is dense. The thief-in-the-night image (5:2) is a *parabolē* with synoptic parallels (Matt 24:43, Luke 12:39, 2 Pet 3:10, Rev 16:15) that almost certainly draws from a Jesus tradition Paul knows. The labor-pains image (5:3, *ōdin*) is Old Testament prophetic standard (Isa 13:8, Jer 4:31, Mic 4:9–10) and Jewish apocalyptic standard (the "messianic woes" or *chevlei mashiach* of rabbinic literature). The light/darkness dualism (5:4–7) and the armor (5:8) intersect with Romans 13:11–14 and Ephesians 6:10–17, suggesting a stable Pauline metaphor-set. The "we will live with him whether we are awake or asleep" of 5:10 deliberately echoes 4:13–17 to bind the two passages together against any reading that would treat them as describing different events.

The paraenetic chain in 5:12–22 is the clearest example in the Pauline corpus of stacked imperatival exhortation: nine main verbs in 5:14 (admonish, encourage, help, be patient...) followed by sixteen short imperatives in 5:15–22. Charles Wanamaker (NIGTC, 1990) reads the chain as Paul drawing on standard Hellenistic *parainesis* lists, while Abraham Malherbe (Anchor Yale, 2000) reads it as deeply philosophical, comparable to lists in Seneca and Epictetus. The triad "rejoice always, pray without ceasing, give thanks in all circumstances" (5:16–18) became, in the patristic tradition, the heart of the case for unceasing prayer.

The wish-prayer in 5:23 stands grammatically apart — *autos de ho theos tēs eirēnēs* ("now may the God of peace himself") — and tightens the chapter's argument by tying sanctification (the language of 4:1–8) to the parousia (the language of 4:13–18). Paul is signing off with a unified theological vision: God's people are being made holy *for* the day, *in light of* the day.

Within the book's argument, 5:1–11 finishes the eschatological section that began at 4:13, 5:12–22 transitions back to community life, and 5:23–28 closes the letter with prayer, greetings, and the charge to read aloud (5:27) — a striking instruction that suggests Paul anticipated factions or absent members and wanted the whole community held accountable to the same text.

**Textual variants.** 5:9 — *hēmas* ("us") versus *hymas* ("you") for "God did not destine for wrath" — most major manuscripts read *hēmas*, including P46, and the editorial choice is settled in NA28 and most modern translations. 5:25 — some Western witnesses add *kai peri* ("and concerning [us]"), softening the bare imperative; not original. 5:27 — the variant on whether "all the brothers" (*pasin tois adelphois*) or "all the holy brothers" (*pasin tois hagiois adelphois*) is original is settled toward the shorter reading.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** Origen's now-lost commentary engaged 5:23 on the *pneuma/psychē/sōma* question. Tertullian (*De Resurrectione Carnis*) treated the chapter alongside chapter 4 as a unified argument for bodily resurrection. Chrysostom's *Homilies on 1 Thessalonians* 9–11 work through 5:1–28 with characteristic pastoral attention; on 5:17 ("pray without ceasing") he argues that unceasing prayer is the disposition of the heart toward God across all activities, not literal verbal continuity — a reading Cassian and the desert tradition will pick up. Augustine's *City of God* 20 deploys 5:2–3 against millenarian chronological speculation: the day of the Lord is unknown, and the church should not pretend otherwise. Theodoret of Cyrus, Ambrosiaster, and Pelagius (whose commentary survives in fragments) all treat 5:23 within the patristic anthropological frame.

The Eastern monastic tradition built directly on 5:17. Cassian's *Conferences* 9 and 10 turn unceasing prayer into the architecture of monastic life, and the Hesychast tradition culminating in Gregory Palamas treats 5:17 as the foundation of the Jesus Prayer practice. *The Way of a Pilgrim* in nineteenth-century Russia is a vernacular outworking of the same exegesis. Western monasticism through Benedict and the Carthusians read the verse similarly though without the same dogmatic apparatus.

The Reformation read the chapter against medieval purgatorial and visionary traditions. Calvin's *Commentary on 1 and 2 Thessalonians* (1551) treats 5:1–11 as warning against false security and 5:12–22 as the practical shape of Christian community; on 5:19–22 he argues the test of prophecy is conformity to Scripture, and uses the verse to discipline both Catholic visionary claims and Anabaptist enthusiastic excess. Luther uses 5:8 in his theology of faith as armor and 5:21 ("test everything") as a Scripture-principle text. The Anabaptists drew on 5:21 as a charter for continuing prophetic discernment, a tradition the Quakers later inherited.

The cessationist/continuationist debate that runs from the Reformation to the present rests heavily on 5:19–22. B.B. Warfield's *Counterfeit Miracles* (1918) is the classic Reformed-cessationist case: the gifts of the Spirit ceased with the apostolic age. John MacArthur's *Strange Fire* (2013) is a contemporary version. On the other side, the Wesleyan-Holiness tradition, the Pentecostal movement (Azusa Street, 1906, and forward), and the charismatic renewal of the 1960s through 1980s read 5:19–22 as a permanent church charter. Wayne Grudem's *The Gift of Prophecy in the New Testament and Today* (1988) and Sam Storms's writings argue a continuationist case from within Reformed theology. Catholic and Orthodox sacramental theologies hold a synthesis: charismatic gifts continue but are integrated with sacramental and ecclesiastical order.

Modern critical commentaries: Malherbe (Anchor Yale 2000), Wanamaker (NIGTC 1990), Fee (NICNT 2009), Best (Black's 1972), Green (Pillar 2002), Richard (Sacra Pagina 1995), Gaventa (Interpretation 1998), Bruce (WBC 1982), Marshall (NCB 1983), Jewett (*The Thessalonian Correspondence*, 1986). The imperial-critical reframe of 5:3 by Donfried, Koester, Harrison, and Horsley has reshaped the field: any commentary written after 2000 that takes Pauline Studies seriously has to engage *pax et securitas*. The apocalyptic-Paul school (Beker, Gaventa, J. Louis Martyn, Douglas Campbell) continues to insist on the irreducibly cosmic and political dimension of Paul's eschatology.

**The Jewish frame.** The day of the Lord (*yom YHWH*) is OT prophetic vocabulary — Amos 5:18–20, Isaiah 13:6–13, Joel 2:1–11, Zephaniah 1:14–18, Malachi 4:1–6. Paul Christologizes the term without removing its Israel-shaped roots; the day is now the day of *kyrios Iēsous*, but the structure (judgment, vindication, restoration) is recognizable from prophetic literature. The light/darkness dualism in 5:4–8 is paralleled almost exactly in *1QS* col. 3–4 (the Two Spirits doctrine: God appoints two spirits, of light and of darkness, in which all humanity walks) and *1QM* (the War Scroll's "sons of light" against "sons of darkness"). David Flusser, Geza Vermes, and John J. Collins (*The Apocalyptic Imagination*) document the parallels in detail; Daniel Boyarin frames Paul's appropriation as inner-Jewish reflection on a shared apocalyptic-dualist vocabulary. The "messianic woes" pattern of labor pains preceding the messianic age is rabbinic standard (Mishnah *Sotah* 9:15, the Talmudic *chevlei mashiach* discussions in *b. Sanhedrin* 97–99). Maimonides codifies a more sober eschatology in *Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Melachim* 11–12, but the apocalyptic strand never fully disappears in Judaism.

**Convergence and divergence.** Christian and Jewish eschatologies converge on the day of the Lord as a future divine intervention with judgment and restoration as its content; they diverge on whether the messiah has come once already and on the identity of *kyrios*. Within Christianity, the major traditions converge on a single visible parousia and bodily resurrection and diverge sharply on (a) whether 5:1–11 describes the same event as 4:13–18 (Wright, Reformed, Catholic, Orthodox: yes; dispensational: no, the rapture and the day are distinct), (b) the millennial question, and (c) the cessation or continuation of prophetic gifts.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What is God up to in this chapter? Paul claims that the death and resurrection of Jesus has begun a public divine action that will be completed at his parousia, that the people of Jesus are already citizens of the day even while the night still lasts, and that the practical shape of life-in-the-day is sanctified community oriented toward the *eirēnē* God gives rather than the *eirēnē* Caesar promises.

**Tension 1: Trichotomy versus dichotomy versus holism — what is the human person?** 5:23's *pneuma kai psychē kai sōma* has been read three ways. The trichotomist tradition (some early fathers, especially Irenaeus in *Against Heresies* 5.6, and a stream that runs through Origen, certain Eastern monastic writers, and modern voices like Watchman Nee and a wide swath of popular evangelicalism) treats spirit, soul, and body as three constitutive parts of the human person. The dichotomist tradition (Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin, Reformed orthodoxy through Hodge and Berkhof) reads body and soul as the two genuine constituents and treats *pneuma* and *psychē* as overlapping perspectives on the inner life. The holistic-Hebrew tradition (Hans Walter Wolff, James Barr, Joel Green, modern OT and biblical-theology scholarship) argues that biblical anthropology is irreducibly whole-person and that any partitive reading imports Greek metaphysics that Paul did not hold; on this view 5:23 is a rhetorical totality formula meaning "every part of you, your whole self, however we name the parts."

The steelman for trichotomy is patristic and pastoral: Irenaeus and the Eastern fathers needed *pneuma* as the locus of grace-bearing capacity in the human person — what the Spirit indwells, what theosis transforms, what marks the human as imago dei in a way distinct from the merely animal *psychē*. Without that distinction, the theological grammar of *pneuma hagion* indwelling *pneuma anthrōpou* (Rom 8:16) loses purchase. Watchman Nee's *The Spiritual Man* applies this to spiritual formation: distinguishing soulish from spiritual experience as a practical pastoral discipline.

The objection from holism is anthropological: if the person is a unity, then dividing them into parts produces theological problems (which part is saved? which part rises? which part is the "real" me?), and the immortality-of-the-soul doctrine that partitive anthropologies tend to support is Hellenistic rather than biblical. Cullmann's *Immortality of the Soul or Resurrection of the Dead?* (1955) makes this case forcefully. Wright's *Resurrection of the Son of God* lands close to it. The objection from dichotomy is that 5:23 is a single verse doing rhetorical work in a wish-prayer; a doctrine of human nature should not be built on such a slender textual base, especially when Paul elsewhere uses *pneuma* and *psychē* interchangeably.

Where this leaves us: the holistic reading has the strongest exegetical support and the dichotomist reading has the strongest dogmatic tradition; a careful theology probably needs the dichotomist resource of an embodied soul that survives death (to make sense of intermediate-state texts) without the trichotomist over-specification or the holist refusal of any inner-outer distinction at all.

**Tension 2: Continuationism versus cessationism on prophecy and Spirit gifts.** 5:19–22 is the textual heart of the question. "Do not quench the Spirit. Do not despise prophecies, but test everything; hold fast what is good. Abstain from every form of evil." Cessationists (Warfield, MacArthur, Reformed scholastic majority) argue that Paul's instruction was binding for the apostolic age but that prophetic gifts ceased with the closing of the canon and the death of the apostles; the church's discernment now operates by Scripture-as-norm rather than ongoing prophecy. Continuationists (Wesleyan, Pentecostal, charismatic, Wayne Grudem, Sam Storms) argue that 5:19–22 is a permanent charter: the gifts continue, the church must not quench the Spirit, and prophecy still operates under the test of conformity to Scripture and edification of the body. Catholic and Orthodox traditions hold a third position in which the charismatic continues but is integrated with sacramental, episcopal, and magisterial order — Vatican II's *Lumen Gentium* 12 explicitly affirms continuing charisms in the church, and the Catholic Charismatic Renewal from 1967 onward institutionalized this.

The steelman for cessationism (the position least intuitive in much of contemporary global Christianity, where Pentecostal-charismatic forms dominate) is theological: if the canon is closed and Scripture is sufficient, ongoing prophetic claims either redundantly repeat Scripture or contradict it. Warfield's argument is that the apostolic-foundational miracles served the unique purpose of attesting revelation, and once that revelation is complete the function ceases. The pastoral concern is real — abuses in continuationist circles, false prophecy, manipulation, the trauma of "words from the Lord" used to control people. A cessationist church protects its members from such abuse by structural unwillingness to accept the category at all.

The objection is exegetical and historical. 5:19–22 reads as a permanent instruction without temporal limit. The cessationist case requires a theological argument from outside the text (canon-closure, apostolic uniqueness) to constrain the text's plain force. Historically, the patristic, medieval, and global-southern church have all understood prophecy and Spirit-gifts as continuing realities (with appropriate discipline), and the cessationist position is largely a Western Protestant scholastic development. Grudem's argument that NT prophecy is fallible-but-authoritative — distinct from canonical OT prophecy — preserves Scripture's unique authority while allowing 5:19–22 to retain its plain meaning.

Where this leaves us: a careful reading of 5:19–22 does not support cessationism on its own terms; the cessationist case depends on theological commitments that have to be argued separately. The continuationist position, when paired with Paul's "test everything" discipline, is the more natural reading of the text, even where it requires hard pastoral work to discern abuse from genuine gift.

**Unresolved question.** If the day of the Lord comes "like a thief" — unannounced, indeterminable in timing — how should a community that genuinely expects it shape its life so that the expectation does neither work (paralyzing apocalyptic urgency) nor disappear into routine (de facto cessationism about the parousia itself)?

## The Thread

**Joel 2:28–32** — the prophetic background for both the day of the Lord and the Spirit-gifts including prophecy. Peter at Pentecost (Acts 2:17–21) cites Joel to interpret what is happening; Paul in 1 Thess 5 inherits the same prophetic texture, holding the day-of-the-Lord and the prophetic-Spirit themes together as Joel does.

**Romans 13:11–14** — the same wake-from-sleep, armor-of-light imagery as 5:4–8. Written perhaps seven years later, the persistence of the metaphor cluster across the Pauline corpus suggests a stable theological vocabulary rather than ad hoc imagery.

**1 Corinthians 14:1–5, 29–33** — Paul's most extended treatment of prophecy in the assembly, with explicit instructions to "let two or three prophets speak, and let the others weigh what is said." The "test everything" of 5:21 has its institutional outworking in the Corinthian instructions.

**Revelation 16:15** — "Behold, I am coming like a thief! Blessed is the one who stays awake." The Jesus-tradition behind 5:2 surfaces again at the close of the canon, and the early Christian eschatological vocabulary of vigilance proves remarkably stable from 50 to 95 AD across very different authors.
