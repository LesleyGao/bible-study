# Titus 1: The Liar's Island and the Trustworthy Word

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Crete in the first century was a senatorial province of the Roman Empire, joined administratively to Cyrenaica since 27 BC under Augustus. Its capital was Gortyn on the central southern plain, with Knossos still inhabited as a minor city of antique fame and Phaistos and Cydonia as continuing population centers. The island sat at a maritime crossroads between Greece, Egypt, and Asia Minor, with a long pre-Roman history of mercenary export, archery, and ship-pirating that had given Cretans a reputation for violence and untrustworthiness across the Greek-speaking world. Polybius (*Histories* 6.46-47) had already complained that Cretans were so given to "shameful greed" and "civic faithlessness" that they were unique among Greeks; Plutarch (*Aemilius Paullus* 23) and Livy (44.45) carried the same trope forward into the Roman period. The proverb κρητίζειν *krētizein* — "to act like a Cretan," i.e., to lie — was a fixed expression. Crete also hosted a substantial Jewish diaspora; Josephus's first wife was a Cretan Jewess (*Life* 427), and *Antiquities* 17.327 references Cretan Jews receiving a pseudo-Alexander pretender. Philo (*Legatio ad Gaium* 282) lists Crete among the diaspora communities. The Cretan Jewish presence is the necessary backdrop for the letter's repeated targeting of "the circumcision party" (1:10) and "Jewish myths" (1:14).

Material culture on Crete in the first century was layered. Beneath every Roman city sat the ruins of Minoan palace civilization — Knossos's frescoed throne room, the labyrinth of subsidiary chambers — which had collapsed more than a millennium earlier but had bequeathed the island its mythological self-presentation as the birthplace of Zeus and the home of Minos. Roman urbanization brought aqueducts, theaters, basilicas, and imperial-cult shrines (the Caesareum at Gortyn is attested epigraphically). Coastal trade in olive oil, wine, and wool flowed through Phalasarna, Itanos, and Hierapytna. The island's archaeology shows continued operation of indigenous sanctuaries (the Idaean Cave, Mount Dikte) alongside imperial cult installations.

The authorship and dating of Titus stand at the center of one of the longer-running debates in modern New Testament criticism. F. C. Baur's Tübingen school first denied Pauline authorship of all three Pastoral Epistles in 1835; H. J. Holtzmann systematized the case in 1880, and Adolf Harnack agreed. The pseudonymity argument rests on five pillars: (1) vocabulary — Titus alone contains roughly thirty-three hapax legomena, and the Pastorals as a group contain a vocabulary departing significantly from the undisputed letters; (2) developed church order with named *presbyteroi* and *episkopoi*; (3) the mission situation — Paul leaving Titus on Crete — fits no point in the Acts narrative, which records only a coastal stop in passing (Acts 27:7-12); (4) the polemic against "myths and genealogies" matches second-century anti-gnostic vocabulary; (5) close stylistic kinship with 1-2 Timothy. Hans Conzelmann and Martin Dibelius (Hermeneia commentary), Lewis Donelson, Norbert Brox, and Margaret MacDonald have argued for pseudonymity, dating the letter c. 90-130 AD. Defending Pauline authorship are William Mounce (Word Biblical Commentary), I. Howard Marshall (International Critical Commentary), Philip Towner (NICNT), George Knight (NIGTC), Luke Timothy Johnson (Sacra Pagina), Donald Guthrie, and Gordon Fee. Jerome Quinn (Anchor Yale) takes a moderating position, allowing for an amanuensis and post-Acts release of Paul, with a Cretan mission in the mid-60s. The interpreter who reads Titus 1 must hold this debate openly: the letter's force is not contingent on any single resolution, but its historical setting is.

The textual witnesses to Titus are more limited than to most Pauline letters. Papyrus 32 (P32), dated c. 200, is the earliest and contains portions of Titus 1:11-15 and 2:3-8 — making it the oldest surviving fragment of any Pastoral Epistle. Codex Sinaiticus (א), Codex Alexandrinus (A), and Codex Bezae (D, where extant for the Pastorals) are the chief uncials. Codex Vaticanus (B), one of the two great fourth-century witnesses, famously lacks the Pastoral Epistles entirely — its New Testament breaks off at Hebrews 9:14, with Titus, Timothy, and Philemon absent. Bart Ehrman in *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* and elsewhere has argued that this absence is suggestive of canonical ambivalence in some Eastern circles, while Daniel Wallace and Bruce Metzger have argued it is most likely the result of damaged or missing exemplar quires rather than ideological exclusion. The ambiguity remains.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter. ἀψευδής *apseudēs* (1:2) — "never-lying," a rare Hellenistic predicate of deity that Paul (or pseudo-Paul) deliberately deploys before introducing the Cretan-liar trope. πρεσβύτερος *presbyteros* (1:5) and ἐπίσκοπος *episkopos* (1:7) — appearing in close succession, possibly synonymously, raising the church-order question. ἴδιος προφήτης *idios prophētēs* (1:12) — "their own prophet," qualifying Epimenides as a recognized voice. πάντα καθαρὰ τοῖς καθαροῖς *panta kathara tois katharois* (1:15) — "all things are pure to the pure," the chapter's pivot toward Christian liberty.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre of Titus belongs to a cluster Hellenistic readers would have recognized. Luke Timothy Johnson has argued that the Pastorals are best read as *mandata principis* — royal commission letters in which a sovereign instructs a delegated officer about how to govern in his absence. The form was familiar from the Ptolemaic and Seleucid courts, with extant parallels in papyri instructions to provincial officials. Pliny the Younger's *Epistles* X (correspondence with Trajan from Bithynia) preserves the same shape: an officer in a province writes to a superior, or a superior writes to officer, on questions of governance, judicial procedure, and personnel. Abraham Malherbe and Benjamin Fiore have located Titus also within the Greco-Roman paraenetic tradition — the moral exhortation literature of Stoics and Cynics that Seneca deployed in his *Epistulae Morales* and that Musonius Rufus, Epictetus, and Dio Chrysostom developed for popular ethical formation. Titus is more compressed than 1-2 Timothy; some have called it a précis of pastoral instruction.

The chapter opens with the longest salutation in any Pastoral Epistle — three full verses (1:1-3) before reaching the addressee in 1:4. Paul styles himself δοῦλος θεοῦ καὶ ἀπόστολος Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ *doulos theou kai apostolos Iēsou Christou*: "slave of God and apostle of Jesus Christ." The combination is unique: Paul elsewhere uses "slave of Christ Jesus" (Rom 1:1, Phil 1:1) but never "slave of God" alone. The rhetorical effect is to cast the apostolic office in the language of theocratic delegation — a form proper to a *mandata* letter — while introducing the chapter's controlling theological thread: God ὁ ἀψευδὴς θεός, "the God who does not lie" (1:2), promised eternal life "before the ages began" (πρὸ χρόνων αἰωνίων *pro chronōn aiōniōn*). The lexical choice of *apseudēs* is deliberate. The word is rare in the New Testament — appearing only here — and its placement in the salutation, several verses before the Cretan-liar polemic of 1:12, sets up a structural antithesis: God, who never lies, has commissioned an apostle to a people whose poet-prophet says they always lie.

The body of the chapter operates by two literary devices common to Pastoral Epistles. First is the qualification list, here applied to elders/overseers (1:5-9). The list parallels 1 Timothy 3 closely but is distinct: Titus 1's list emphasizes that the overseer must "hold firm to the trustworthy word" so as to "rebuke those who contradict it" (1:9), a polemical edge sharper than 1 Timothy 3's more domestic register. Second is the vice list (1:10-16), with sharply rhetorical color: ἀνυπότακτοι *anypotaktoi* "insubordinate," ματαιολόγοι *mataiologoi* "empty talkers," φρεναπάται *phrenapatai* "deceivers." Embedded in the vice list is the Epimenides citation (1:12), a quotation that functions as both polemical ammunition and literary marker.

Within the letter's argument, Titus 1 establishes the diagnostic frame. The opponent is named, the structural remedy is identified (sound elders/overseers), and the criterion of judgment — purity, sound doctrine, faithful word — is set in place. Chapters 2 and 3 will then develop the positive paraenesis on Christian household and civil life, anchored in the credal fragments of 2:11-14 and 3:4-7.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical New Testament arc, Titus 1 stands at the bridge between the Pauline missional letters and the post-apostolic concern with institutional consolidation. The qualification of elders in 1:5-9 echoes Acts 14:23 ("they appointed elders in every church") and prefigures the more developed church order of Ignatius and the *Didache*. The Cretan polemic in 1:10-16 echoes Galatians' anti-Judaizing concern but in a Hellenistic Diaspora setting where the polemic is filtered through Greek poetic citation rather than Jewish-Christian theological argument.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** John Chrysostom's six homilies on Titus, preached in Antioch or Constantinople in the late fourth century, treat Paul's praise and rebuke of Cretans as a model of pastoral candor — Chrysostom emphasizes that the apostle is willing to speak the truth even at the cost of offense, and reads 1:15 ("to the pure all things are pure") as a statement about the moral disposition of the believer, not about ritual purity per se. Jerome's *Commentarius in Epistolam ad Titum* engages the Epimenides citation extensively, defending Paul's use of pagan poetry as continuous with apostolic preaching at Athens (Acts 17:28). Theodore of Mopsuestia, in his Antiochene historical-critical mode, takes the *episkopos*/*presbyteros* terminology as functionally identical at this stage of development. Augustine repeatedly cites 1:15 in his anti-Manichaean writings to argue that creation as such is good and that purity is a matter of the will, not of matter.

Medieval reception consolidated around Aquinas's *Super Epistolas S. Pauli Lectura* on Titus, where the qualification list of 1:6-9 is read as a charter for the canonical office and where 1:15 is glossed through the lens of moral theology — Aquinas distinguishing the per se purity of created things from their per accidens defilement when used disordered (*Summa Theologiae* I-II q.102 on the ceremonial law's abrogation). The Glossa Ordinaria preserves both Jerome's and Augustine's readings. Medieval canon-law tradition drew on 1:10-11 ("they must be silenced") for ecclesiastical disciplinary authority.

Reformation reading sharpened around two issues. Luther's preface to Titus praises the letter as "a short epistle, but a model of Christian doctrine" and reads 1:15 as a flagship verse for justification by faith — purity is not ritual but is wrought by faith. Calvin's commentary on Titus is more measured; he treats the Cretan polemic as a salutary model of how Christian ministers must occasionally speak harshly, and he reads the Epimenides citation as evidence that Paul "did not refuse to be aided by their testimony" — i.e., that pagan wisdom can serve gospel ends when subordinated to revealed truth (an early articulation of common-grace epistemology that Bavinck would later develop in *Reformed Dogmatics*). Anabaptist tradition (Schleitheim Confession 1527; Menno Simons) was suspicious of such accommodations and read the chapter's purity language more strictly.

Critical scholarship, beginning with Baur and Holtzmann and culminating in Dibelius-Conzelmann (Hermeneia, 1972) and the work of Lewis Donelson and Norbert Brox, treated the chapter as a window onto early-second-century church organization. The "Jewish myths" of 1:14 were variously identified as gnostic-tinged Jewish-Christian teaching or as proto-rabbinic haggadah. Contemporary scholarship — Mounce, Marshall, Towner, Knight, Quinn, Collins — has been more willing to defer judgment on the precise opponent profile while taking the church-order data seriously as evidence of either late-Pauline or early-post-Pauline development.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** Early Jewish-Christian polemics responded to Titus 1's "circumcision party" and "Jewish myths" language as unfair caricature; rabbinic literature does not engage Titus directly, but the broader pattern of Christian anti-Jewish polemic reading back into Titus has been examined by Daniel Boyarin in *Border Lines* and by Paula Fredriksen in *When Christians Were Jews*. The modern Paul-within-Judaism school (Krister Stendahl, E. P. Sanders, Mark Nanos, Boyarin) has argued that the Pastorals' anti-Jewish vocabulary represents a later development and is a primary marker distinguishing the Pastorals from undisputed Pauline letters, where the relationship to Israel is more dialectical (Romans 9-11). The implication: if Titus is post-Pauline, the chapter's "Jewish myths" polemic reflects a later, harsher boundary-drawing rather than Paul's own self-understanding within Israel.

**Where this leaves us:** Critical and confessional readers can broadly agree that Titus 1 reflects an environment in which Cretan-Jewish opponents were teaching something the writer judged to be deceptive and economically motivated. The deeper questions — about authorship, about whether Paul or a Pauline tradition is speaking, and about what the "Jewish myths" actually were — remain unsettled, but the chapter's pastoral logic does not depend on their resolution.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Pagan philosophy and poetry as sources for Christian truth.** Paul (or pseudo-Paul) cites Epimenides of Knossos, a sixth-century BC Cretan poet-seer, calling him ἴδιος αὐτῶν προφήτης *idios autōn prophētēs* — "their own prophet" — and pronouncing his testimony "true." The same pattern appears at Athens (Acts 17:28, citing Aratus). The Catholic natural-law tradition systematized by Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* I-II q.91-94) holds that human reason, by participating in eternal law, can apprehend genuine moral truth even apart from special revelation; the use of pagan poetry as authoritative on this view is unobjectionable, since the same divine reason undergirds both. The Reformed common-grace tradition, articulated by Calvin (*Institutes* II.2.15-18) and developed by Abraham Kuyper and Herman Bavinck, argues that the Spirit illumines all human knowing, so that pagan wisdom contains genuine insight by divine permission, though it cannot save. Eastern Orthodoxy's *logos spermatikos* doctrine, articulated by Justin Martyr and developed by Clement of Alexandria, Maximus the Confessor, and Vladimir Lossky, holds that the seeds of the Logos are present in pagan philosophy. Anabaptist and certain post-liberal traditions (Stanley Hauerwas, John Howard Yoder) have been more suspicious, reading philosophical accommodation as a Constantinian compromise. Karl Barth's emphatic *Nein!* (1934) to Emil Brunner explicitly rejected natural theology in any form: Barth held that all genuine knowledge of God comes through Christ alone, and that the pretension to find God through pagan poetry is a form of idolatry. The Barthian rejection deserves to be steelmanned. Barth was responding to a German Christianity that had already begun to find revelation in nation, blood, and Aryan culture; the natural-theology project, however refined, looked to him like a permission slip for cultural-religious syncretism. To say that Epimenides spoke truly because all truth is God's truth is, on Barth's view, dangerously close to making cultural intuition a co-authority with the gospel. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter does not resolve the question. Paul cites Epimenides but immediately subordinates the citation to the prior frame of "the trustworthy word as taught" (1:9) and the salvific truth committed to the apostle (1:1-3). Whether that subordination authorizes a Thomistic/Reformed embrace of natural theology or only a Barthian use-and-discard remains an open theological question.

**Tension 2: Purity and Christian liberty.** πάντα καθαρὰ τοῖς καθαροῖς (1:15) — "to the pure all things are pure" — has functioned as a flagship verse in nearly every debate over Christian liberty since the Reformation. The Catholic tradition, while affirming the goodness of creation against Manichaeism (Augustine, *Contra Faustum*) and against ritualist Judaizing, has nevertheless preserved a robust ritual and sacramental theology of purity in the Eucharistic discipline, in confession before communion (CCC 1385-1387), and in the various canonical fasting traditions. Reformed Christian liberty, articulated by Calvin (*Institutes* III.19) and codified at Westminster (Confession 20), reads 1:15 with Romans 14 and 1 Corinthians 8 as authorizing freedom in matters not explicitly bound by Scripture, with the caveat of conscience and edification. Lutheran two-kingdoms theology distinguishes the inner freedom of the conscience from the outer ordering of civil and ceremonial life; Luther's *On the Freedom of a Christian* (1520) reads 1:15 as a proclamation that no external thing defiles the believer. Anabaptist tradition embraced a stricter simplicity of life, less because of ritual purity concerns than because the gathered community's nonconformity required visible distinction. Eastern Orthodoxy preserves an extensive fasting calendar (Wednesdays and Fridays, four major fasts annually) on the principle that fasting is ascetic formation, not ritual purification per se. Jewish kashrut tradition continues unbroken, and modern Jewish scholarship has been pointed about the tendency of Christian readings of 1:15 to flatten kashrut into mere ritualism. **Where this leaves us:** "to the pure all things are pure" is not an antinomian statement; the second clause ("to the defiled and unbelieving, nothing is pure") immediately re-locates purity in the agent's disposition. The verse names a real reorientation but does not adjudicate which traditions of fasting, sacramental discipline, or moral abstention have correctly preserved its inner logic.

**One unresolved question:** When Paul calls Epimenides "their own prophet" and pronounces the testimony of a six-hundred-year-dead pagan oracle "true," is he opening the canon by a hairsbreadth — making space for ongoing recognition of truth wherever it speaks — or is he closing it with finality, claiming the right to weaponize even pagan voices in service of the apostolic word he has already received?

## The Thread

**1 Timothy 3:1-7** parallels the elder/overseer qualifications of Titus 1:6-9, but with a more interior and domestic register; the comparison sharpens the question of whether the two letters reflect the same office at different developmental stages or two different communities' practice.

**Acts 17:28** — Paul's citation of Aratus and Epimenides at the Areopagus is the closest canonical parallel to Titus 1:12 and the only other clearly attested Pauline use of pagan poetry; together they form the textual basis for any natural-theology argument from Paul.

**Romans 14:14** ("nothing is unclean in itself, but it is unclean for anyone who thinks it unclean") is the undisputed-Pauline twin of Titus 1:15 and is the verse most often used to argue that 1:15's logic is genuinely Pauline rather than a later development.

**Mark 7:19** ("thus he declared all foods clean") roots the purity-reorientation in Jesus' own teaching, which Paul presupposes in Romans 14 and which Titus 1:15 distills into proverbial form. The Markan parenthetical, often suspected of being editorial, pairs with Titus 1:15 as the New Testament's two most compressed statements of the purity reorientation.
