# Jeremiah 36: The Scroll, the Knife, and the Bullae of Baruch

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 36 is one of the most precisely datable chapters in the Hebrew Bible: *the fourth year of Jehoiakim the son of Josiah, king of Judah* (36:1) = 605/604 BC, the year of the **Battle of Carchemish**, in which Nebuchadnezzar II decisively defeated the Egyptian army of Necho II and inherited the Levantine sphere as Babylon's tributary zone. The Babylonian Chronicle (BM 21946, the *Nebuchadnezzar Chronicle*) records the campaign in detail: in his accession year (Nisan 605–Nisan 604) Nebuchadnezzar took Carchemish; in his first regnal year he campaigned through the Hatti-land (Syria-Palestine) and received tribute from "all the kings"; in 603 he campaigned again. Jehoiakim's *fourth* year (605/604) is therefore the moment at which the Babylonian threat to Judah materialized. The reading of the scroll occurs *in the fifth year, in the ninth month* (36:9) = December 604 / January 603 BC, the *fast* called perhaps in response to Nebuchadnezzar's 604 sack of Ashkelon, which had refused to submit (Babylonian Chronicle BM 21946 obverse line 18; archaeologically confirmed at Tel Ashkelon by Lawrence Stager's excavations).

Material culture: ANE scribal practice in the Iron II / early Neo-Babylonian period. Hebrew scribes used **papyrus** (imported from Egypt) and **leather** scrolls, written in iron-gall or carbon ink with a reed pen. The **Lachish Letters** (carbon ink on potsherds, late 7th c. BC) preserve the same script, idiom, and vocabulary as Jeremiah. The **Wadi Daliyeh papyri** (4th c. BC, slightly later) preserve the leather-scroll form. **Scribal tools** are attested at Lachish (an inkwell, lots of styli), Arad (a complete scribal kit), and Kuntillet Ajrud. The *megillah* (scroll) form is the standard pre-codex book — long sheets sewn or glued end-to-end and rolled around two staves. The **knife** with which Jehoiakim cuts the scroll (36:23) is the *ta'ar ha-sopher*, the *scribe's knife* used to scrape errors and trim margins (cf. the rabbinic discussion of the *ta'ar ha-sopher* in Bavli *Bava Batra* 14b, *Megillah* 16b). The detail that *the king himself* — not a servant — handles the scribe's knife and *cuts* the scroll is rhetorically devastating: the king performs the scribe's gesture in inverse, *destroying* rather than preserving text.

The **winter house** of 36:22 — *the king was sitting in the winter house, and there was a fire burning in the fire pot before him* — refers to the Iron Age palace heating system: a winter wing (often partially below grade or enclosed in heavy stone) with a *brazier* (Hebrew *ach*; Akkadian *kinunum*) burning charcoal for warmth. Archaeological parallels exist at the **Persepolis Apadana** (later, but architecturally continuous), at **Tell Halaf**, at **Hazor**, at **Megiddo's** Iron II palaces. The "ninth month" (36:9) = Kislev (November-December), explicitly cold-season; the brazier is in active use.

The **named officials** in the chapter constitute the most archaeologically attested cohort in any prophetic narrative. **Baruch ben Neriah** (36:4, 32) — the **Baruch ben Neriah bulla** (clay seal-impression) reading *l-Brkyhw bn Nryhw h-spr* ("Belonging to Berechyahu son of Neriyahu the scribe") was identified in 1975 by **Nahman Avigad** in a private antiquities collection. A second bulla, attributed to the same individual and bearing a partial fingerprint (presumed to be Baruch's), was identified in the 1990s. The bullae are unprovenanced (which has produced legitimate scholarly suspicion of forgery — Yuval Goren and Christopher Rollston have raised concerns), but the **Yigal Shiloh** excavations in the City of David's *Burnt Room* (Area G) recovered a *provenanced* archive of bullae from the 586 BC destruction layer that includes **Gemariah ben Shaphan** (cf. Jer 36:10, 11, 12, 25) — *l-Gmryhw bn Spn*. The Gemariah bulla is *unambiguously authentic and provenanced*, and Gemariah's chamber is precisely where Baruch reads the scroll publicly. The **Yehuchal ben Shelemiah** bulla (Eilat Mazar, 2005) names the official from Jer 37:3, 38:1; the **Gedaliah ben Pashhur** bulla (Mazar, 2008) names the official from Jer 38:1. The **Jehudi ben Nethaniah** of 36:14, 21 has been tentatively linked to a bulla but the identification is debated. The **Elishama the secretary** of 36:12, 20–21 is named in the **Lachish ostraca** (Lachish Letter III mentions an Elishama). The **Achbor**, father of **Elnathan** (36:12), appears as the official sent by Josiah to Huldah in 2 Kgs 22:12. The **Shaphan** family — Shaphan, Ahikam, Gemariah, Micaiah, Elasah, Gedaliah — runs as a *named scribal-political dynasty* through Jeremiah and Kings, anchoring the prophetic narrative in a documented Jerusalem elite.

Authorship and dating: chapter 36 is the foundational text for understanding the *production* of the book of Jeremiah itself. The text explicitly names Baruch as scribe (36:4) and reports a *first scroll* (burned), a *second scroll* with *additions* (36:32) — so the book of Jeremiah's own narrative tells us that *what we have* is a *re-written, expanded* corpus. **Karel van der Toorn** in *Scribal Culture and the Making of the Hebrew Bible* (Harvard, 2007) makes chapter 36 his central case study for understanding biblical book-production: prophetic-scribal collaboration, dictation, public reading, royal repudiation, expansion. Van der Toorn argues the chapter is *the* OT window onto how prophetic books were physically made. **William Schniedewind** in *How the Bible Became a Book* (Cambridge, 2004) similarly foregrounds Jer 36 as the *scribalization* turning point. Lundbom (Anchor) treats the chapter as substantially Baruchian/Jeremianic. Holladay (Hermeneia) similarly. Carroll (OTL), more skeptical, reads it as a *Deuteronomistic* literary construction. McKane (ICC), Bright, Stulman. **Kathleen O'Connor** reads it as the trauma narrative of the prophet whose *words* are killed and yet *return*.

Key terms: *megillah* (מְגִלָּה, "scroll," 36:2, 4, 6, 14, etc.) — the standard term, occurring 17 times in the chapter (a density unmatched in the OT). *sopher* (סֹפֵר, "scribe," 36:10, 12, 20, 26, 32) — the technical term used for both Baruch and the royal officials Elishama and (later) Shaphan. *deyo* (דְּיוֹ, "ink," 36:18) — the only OT occurrence, archaeologically confirmed by ink residue on Lachish ostraca. *ta'ar ha-sopher* (תַּעַר הַסֹּפֵר, "scribe's knife/razor," 36:23) — the technical scribal tool; rabbinic literature continues to use the term. *ach* (אָח, "fire pot/brazier," 36:22, 23) — the Iron Age heating apparatus; only here in the OT.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: **the most extended scribal-process narrative in the Hebrew Bible**. The chapter is structured as a four-act drama: (1) *dictation and inscription* (36:1–8) — YHWH commands, Jeremiah dictates, Baruch writes; (2) *first public reading* (36:9–10) — Baruch reads to the assembled fast-day worshippers in Gemariah's chamber; (3) *escalating institutional response* (36:11–19) — Micaiah reports to officials, officials hear and react with fear, officials warn Baruch and Jeremiah to hide; (4) *royal destruction and divine response* (36:20–32) — Jehoiakim cuts and burns the scroll, YHWH commands a *second scroll* and pronounces judgment on Jehoiakim. The chapter's literary structure is a sustained meditation on *what happens when a king destroys a prophetic text*: the text is *more* — not less — than what was burned.

Devices: *parallel readings* — the scroll is read three times (in public, before the officials, before the king), with progressively narrower audiences and progressively darker responses. *contrast of responses* (36:16, 24) — the *officials* are afraid (*pachad*) when they hear; the *king and his servants* are *not afraid* (*lo-pachadu*) and *do not tear their garments* (the standard mourning gesture; cf. Josiah's response to the discovered scroll in 2 Kgs 22:11). The contrast with Josiah is direct and devastating: Josiah, hearing the scroll of Deuteronomy, tore his garments and led national repentance; Jehoiakim, his son, *cut and burned* the scroll. *Visual irony* (36:23) — the king cuts the scroll *with a scribe's knife*, performing the inverse of the scribal vocation. *Theological reversal* (36:32) — *and many similar words were added to them* — the burning *produces a longer text*. The destruction is undone with *interest*. *Interlinking with chapter 26* — the same fast-day institution, the same temple-court dynamics, the same official-class debate; chapter 26 (the temple sermon trial) is a structural rhyme with 36 (the scroll-burning) — both are *what happens when prophetic words enter institutional space*.

MT-LXX: chapter 36 is largely stable, with minor variants. LXX numbers chapter 43. Flag: 36:30 *he shall have none to sit on the throne of David* — historically problematic, since Jehoiakim's son Jehoiachin *did* sit on the throne briefly (3 months) before deportation. The classical rabbinic resolution (Bavli *Sanhedrin* 38b, *Bereshit Rabbah* 95) is that the verse refers to *no permanent or successful* dynastic continuation, fulfilled in the brief and impotent reign of Jehoiachin and the absence of any Davidic king after Zedekiah. The Christian resolution invokes the *symbolic* throne of David occupied eschatologically by Christ (Luke 1:32–33). Flag: 36:30 *his dead body shall be cast out to the heat by day and the frost by night* — historically unverified; Jeremiah does not narrate Jehoiakim's death; 2 Kgs 24:6 says only that "Jehoiakim slept with his fathers." 2 Chr 36:6 says Nebuchadnezzar bound him in fetters to take him to Babylon, but the outcome is unstated. The prophecy of 36:30 may have been fulfilled in some manner not recorded, or the text preserves a non-fulfilled prophetic word — a theological problem the rabbinic tradition addresses (Bavli *Bava Batra* 15a).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Place in canonical arc: chapter 36 is the *foundational text* for Jewish and Christian theologies of Scripture as a *written* phenomenon. Before this chapter, prophetic word is largely *spoken* (oral oracles preserved in memory and recited); after this chapter, prophetic word is *inscribed*, *publicly read*, and *survives material destruction*. **William Schniedewind** dates the *scribalization* of Israelite religion to precisely this period (late 7th / early 6th c. BC) — the Deuteronomic scroll discovery (2 Kgs 22), Jeremiah's scroll (Jer 36), the Lachish letters as a documented scribal milieu. The implication: the *Bible as Book* is a product of the late-monarchic scribal class, of which Baruch is the named exemplar.

Jewish trajectory: **Sirach 49:6–7** (early 2nd c. BC) names Jeremiah but does not foreground Baruch; the apocryphal book of *Baruch* and the *Letter of Jeremiah* (deuterocanonical/apocryphal) extend Baruch's traditional authorship into Second Temple Jewish literature. **2 Baruch** (the *Syriac Apocalypse of Baruch*, late 1st c. CE) and **3 Baruch** (the *Greek Apocalypse of Baruch*, 1st-3rd c. CE) make Baruch a major pseudonymous figure of post-70 AD apocalyptic literature — *Baruch* becomes the patron of Jewish responses to the second temple destruction. **Bavli *Megillah* 14b** lists Baruch among the prophets, and **Bavli *Sotah* 13b** treats him as Jeremiah's successor. **Targum Jonathan** to Jer 36 makes Baruch's scribal role explicit and respected. **Rashi** treats Baruch as honored prophetic disciple. **Radak** focuses on the textual question — what was on the scroll? — and notes the chapter's implication that the canonical book of Jeremiah includes *additions* beyond what was first written. **Maimonides** in the *Mishneh Torah*, *Hilkhot Yesodei ha-Torah* 7:1–7, treats Jer 36 as paradigm of how prophecy enters into authoritative written form. **Abrabanel** (15th c.) develops the chapter's anti-tyrannical implications in the context of the Spanish expulsion.

Christian trajectory: Patristic — **Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah*** (Hom. 21, partially extant in Latin via Jerome) treats the second scroll as a type of the resurrection: the destroyed text *rises* with additions, prefiguring Christ. **Jerome's *Commentariorum in Jeremiam*** treats chapter 36 as foundational for the doctrine of *inspired Scripture*: the text, even when burned, returns. Jerome himself, as the translator of the Vulgate, read his own scribal vocation through Baruch's. Medieval — **Aquinas** (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 171–174 on prophecy) cites Jer 36 on the relation between prophetic inspiration and scribal mediation. Reformation — **Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah*** (1563) reads chapter 36 as the warrant for the Reformed doctrine of Scripture: God's word *cannot be silenced* by political power, and the burning of the scroll prefigures every subsequent attempt to suppress Scripture (the burning of Tyndale's English New Testament in 1526, Wycliffe Bibles, etc.). Calvin's reading became foundational for English Puritan theologies of Scripture under persecution. **Luther** similarly. Critical — Duhm (1901), Mowinckel (*Komposition*, 1914), Holladay (Hermeneia, 1989), Carroll (OTL, 1986). Contemporary — Lundbom (Anchor, 2004), Brueggemann, **Karel van der Toorn** (*Scribal Culture*, 2007), **William Schniedewind** (*How the Bible Became a Book*, 2004), **Kathleen O'Connor** (*Pain and Promise*, 2011).

Where they diverge: chapter 36 is the foundational text for **Christian theologies of Scripture and inscription**. The Catholic tradition (*Dei Verbum* 11, Vatican II): "the books of Scripture must be acknowledged as teaching solidly, faithfully and without error that truth which God wanted put into sacred writings for the sake of salvation" — inspiration includes the *scribal mediation* of Baruch, and the Magisterium guards the faithful transmission of the inscribed text. The Reformed tradition (Westminster Confession 1.8): the *original autographs* are inspired; subsequent transmission is *providentially preserved*. The chapter pressures the autograph doctrine because it shows *the original autograph being burned* and a *re-written, expanded* second scroll becoming the canonical text. The Eastern Orthodox tradition: Scripture lives in the Tradition of the Church, and Jer 36 illustrates the scribal-ecclesial mediation by which Scripture is preserved. **Critical *canonical-process* readings** (Brevard Childs, *Introduction to the Old Testament as Scripture*, 1979; James Sanders, *Torah and Canon*, 1972) take Jer 36 as the *paradigmatic biblical case* of canon-as-process: text grows, expands, is re-written — and the *expansion* is what becomes canonical. **Karel van der Toorn**'s *Scribal Culture* synthesizes the empirical case: the Bible is the product of a scribal class whose practices Jer 36 makes visible.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The scroll-burning king and prophetic Scripture — the doctrine of inspiration.** Scripture's *self-narrative* records its *re-writing under attack*, and the king's burning of the scroll is *included* in the reproduced scroll. This produces a complex theological puzzle. High-doctrine inspiration (Catholic *Dei Verbum* 11; Reformed Westminster Confession 1; evangelical *Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy*, 1978) holds that the inspired text is *the text we have*. Canonical-process readings (Childs, Sanders, van der Toorn) hold that the text *grew* and that the *growth itself* is inspired — the *second scroll* of Jer 36:32 (*and many similar words were added to them*) is the canonical text, not the first scroll. The Catholic tradition of the *Magisterium* protecting Scripture provides a relatively coherent answer: inspiration is mediated through the scribal-ecclesial process, and the canonical text is the inspired text precisely as the Church has received it. The Reformed *autograph* doctrine has more difficulty: if the autograph is what Jeremiah originally dictated to Baruch, *that scroll was burned*; the canonical text is the *second*, *expanded* scroll, which makes the autograph doctrine's locus of inspiration unclear. The evangelical *inerrantist* tradition (Warfield, Henry, Geisler) typically resolves this by treating the *second* scroll as the autograph, since *that* is what was ultimately preserved.

Steelman the canonical-process position: Scripture, in its own self-presentation, is the product of a *living scribal-prophetic process* in which God's word grows under historical pressure. The destruction of the first scroll is not an accident but a *constitutive* event: the *expansion* of the second scroll (36:32) is divine response to historical assault. On this reading, the doctrine of inspiration must absorb the empirical fact that biblical texts grew, were edited, were re-written — and that the *growth itself* is inspired. This is uncomfortable for static-deposit theories of revelation but *more honest* about how the text actually came to be. Where this leaves us: the doctrine of inspiration in light of Jer 36 is *not* the simple deposit-and-preservation model; it is something more like *prophetic-scribal-ecclesial collaboration under historical pressure*, and the resulting Scripture bears the marks of the process that produced it.

**Tension 2: The role of the scribe — Baruch as named, archaeologically attested, and central.** Baruch ben Neriah is one of the rare biblical figures with direct (if contested) archaeological attestation via the **bullae**. The provenanced *Gemariah ben Shaphan* bulla in particular — found in the Burnt Room of Area G in the City of David, in the destruction layer of 586 BC — places the chapter in a documented historical milieu. Baruch is the *human* mediator of divine revelation: he writes the scroll, reads it publicly, hides with Jeremiah, returns to write the second scroll. What does this mean for the relation between *human scribal agency* and *divine inspiration*?

The Catholic *concursus divinus* tradition (Aquinas, *ST* II-II.171–174; Leo XIII, *Providentissimus Deus*, 1893; Pius XII, *Divino Afflante Spiritu*, 1943) holds that God works *through* the full human capacities of the inspired author — the scribe's literary craft, historical knowledge, and theological insight are *not* overridden but *taken up* into inspiration. The Reformed *Spirit-and-Word* doctrine (Westminster Confession 1.4–5; Bavinck, *Reformed Dogmatics* 1.405–448) similarly emphasizes the Spirit's superintendence of the human writer. Modern critical readings of biblical scribes as *authorial agents* (van der Toorn, Schniedewind) press further: scribes are not transparent stenographers but creative redactors with theological agendas; the prophetic text is partially *their* composition. The Jewish *sopher* tradition (Ezra-Nehemiah, the post-biblical *Sopherim*) preserves the scribe as a venerated authoritative office. Steelman the critical position: Baruch is *not* a passive transcriber. Jer 36 narrates him reading publicly, negotiating with officials, writing a second scroll *with additions* — Baruch is co-authorial. The Catholic-Reformed *concursus* tradition can absorb this without contradiction; the strict *dictation* theory of inspiration cannot. Where this leaves us: the *human* mediation of divine revelation is structurally embedded in Scripture itself, and Jer 36 makes the scribe's authorial agency *visible* in a way that few other biblical texts do.

**An unresolved question:** If the second scroll of Jer 36:32 contains *additions* not in the first, and if the canonical book of Jeremiah is the descendant of *that* second scroll, *what is the textual locus of inspiration*? Is the *original* dictation inspired, the *expansion* inspired, the *redacted final form* inspired — or all three, in different ways? And what does this imply about the LXX-MT divergences (cf. ch. 33:14–26's MT-only material, possibly itself a later expansion)?

## The Thread

The scroll-burning of 36:23 threads forward into Acts 19:19 (the Ephesian magic-book burning, where converts *voluntarily* burn their books — the inverse of Jehoiakim's *involuntary* victim-burning); Foxe's *Book of Martyrs* on the Tyndale and Wycliffe Bible burnings; the Nazi book-burnings of 1933; the Tianjin Bible-burnings under the Cultural Revolution. The Baruch-Jeremiah collaboration prefigures the Mark-Peter collaboration in early Christian tradition (Eusebius, *Hist. Eccl.* 3.39, citing Papias on Mark as Peter's *hermeneutēs*). The *concealment* of Jeremiah and Baruch (36:26, *the LORD hid them*) recurs in 1 Kgs 17:3 (Elijah at the Cherith), 1 Kgs 18:13 (Obadiah hiding 100 prophets in caves), and the *crypto-prophet* tradition continued in Maccabean literature. The Davidic-throne prophecy of 36:30 *(he shall have none to sit on the throne of David)* finds its canonical resolution in Luke 1:32–33's Davidic Christology (Christ as the *true* heir on whom the Jeconiah/Jehoiakim curse does not finally close the line). The scribal-knife image (36:23) inverts in Heb 4:12 (*the word of God is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword*) — the sword that *cuts* belongs not to the king destroying the text but to the text itself, cutting into the human heart.
