# Exodus 29: Blood on the Ear, the Thumb, the Toe

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Exodus 29 is the ritual that makes a man a priest. It is a week-long, blood-saturated, meticulously staged ceremony, the elements of which map onto well-documented ANE priestly installation rites without being reducible to any single one. The closest parallels are the *pīt pî* ("mouth-opening") and *mīs pî* ("mouth-washing") rituals of Mesopotamian divine-image consecration — week-long ceremonies involving multiple animal sacrifices, anointings, and clothing changes, documented in tablet collections from the Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian periods. Michael Hundley's *Keeping Heaven on Earth* (2011) and Victor Hurowitz's work on temple dedication rituals have catalogued the ANE parallels in detail. The seven-day duration (29:35) reappears in Mesopotamian ceremonies; the sequence of washing, vesting, anointing, and blood-application is paralleled in Hittite priestly consecration. What is distinctive in Exodus 29 is not the ritual grammar, which is regional, but the theological content — it is *YHWH* who is consecrating, and the priests are installed into service of a God who has no image to open its mouth.

Political context: if pre-exilic, the chapter reflects an early stage of Aaronic priestly self-definition, probably in tension with the broader Levitical category (which Deuteronomy 18 will describe differently). If Wellhausen is right, the chapter codes Second Temple priestly prerogatives back into wilderness foundations. Haran argued that the specific ritual grammar preserves features too archaic to be late invention — the "ram of ordination" (*ʾêl millūʾîm*, v. 22) is a technical term that does not appear elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible except in Leviticus 8 (the execution of this command) and in a few very archaic-looking contexts. Milgrom's *Leviticus 1–16* (Anchor Bible) provides the authoritative analysis of the sacrificial system here.

Material culture: the sacrificial animals — one bull, two rams without blemish (v. 1) — reflect standard ANE sacrificial practice. Bulls were the most costly victims, reserved for the highest-stakes offerings; rams were the standard adult male sheep for significant ceremonies. The unleavened bread, cakes mixed with oil, and wafers smeared with oil (v. 2) parallel the bread offerings in Mesopotamian texts. Fine wheat flour (*sōlet ḥiṭṭîm*) was the highest-grade flour, not the coarser barley of common bread.

The anointing oil (*šemen ha-mišḥâ*, v. 7) — whose composition is specified in Exodus 30:22–25 as a fragrant mixture of myrrh, cinnamon, sweet cane, cassia, and olive oil — is used to anoint Aaron (v. 7) and then, mixed with blood, sprinkled on Aaron and his sons and their garments (v. 21). Anointing as a rite of royal and priestly installation is paralleled across the ANE; in Israelite practice, kings (1 Samuel 10:1; 16:13; 1 Kings 1:39) and priests were anointed, making *māšîaḥ* ("anointed one") the technical term that would eventually generate the Messianic category.

The blood rituals are the chapter's archaeological and comparative richest material. Blood on the horns of the altar (v. 12) — we already encountered the horns in chapter 27 — is attested in Leviticus 4 for various sin offerings. The ordination's most distinctive blood rite is verse 20: blood on the right earlobe, right thumb, and right big toe of each priest. This triadic rite has genuine ANE comparanda (a Hittite *šarlatta-* ritual involves analogous triadic applications), but the biblical symbolism is striking: ear for hearing God's word, hand for doing God's work, foot for walking God's way. Leviticus 14:14 applies the *same* three-point rite to the cleansed leper — the restored leper is ritually treated as a re-consecrated person entering the community, on priestly analogy. Milgrom made much of this parallel: the consecrated priest and the cleansed leper are both crossing a threshold into full standing.

The "filling of the hands" (*millēʾ yād*, vv. 9, 22, 29, 33, 35) is the chapter's technical term for ordination. The Hebrew literally means to fill the priest's hands with the sacrificial portions — and the LXX translates it *teleioō* ("complete, perfect"), which Hebrews will pick up in its Christological argument. Baruch Levine and Jacob Milgrom both emphasize that *millēʾ yād* is an ANE idiom meaning "to install in office" with parallels in Akkadian; it does not mean (pace some older commentators) that the priest's hands are literally filled for the first time, but that he is authorized to receive sacrificial portions as his ongoing right. The etymology drives the semantics.

The slaughter locations matter archaeologically. The bull's flesh, skin, and dung are burned *outside the camp* (v. 14) — the first of many such "outside the camp" disposals in the sacrificial system (Leviticus 4:12, 21; 16:27). Hebrews 13:11–13 will pick up this geographic detail and make much of it theologically. The *ḥaṭṭāʾt* (sin offering) bull of verses 10–14 and the *ʿōlâ* (burnt offering) ram of verses 15–18 represent two of the five main sacrificial categories later codified in Leviticus 1–7. The second ram, the "ram of ordination" (vv. 19–28), has elements of the peace offering (*šĕlāmîm*) — the priests eat it — but with distinctive blood rites.

The concluding *ʿōlat tāmîd* (vv. 38–42) — the continual burnt offering of two lambs daily, morning and evening — establishes the permanent liturgical rhythm that will define Israelite Temple worship for the next 1,200+ years. The two lambs appear in Numbers 28:3–8 with additional specifications; their termination under Antiochus IV in 167 BCE is the great desecration of Maccabean literature (Daniel 8:11–14; 11:31; 12:11). Daily morning and evening sacrifice was the basic rhythm of the Jerusalem Temple until 70 CE.

Key Hebrew terms: *millēʾ yād* (מִלֵּא יָד), "fill the hand," idiom for ordination; *ʾêl millūʾîm* (אֵיל מִלֻּאִים), ram of ordination; *ḥaṭṭāʾt* (חַטָּאת), sin offering / purification offering (Milgrom's preferred translation); *ʿōlâ* (עֹלָה), burnt offering (literally "ascent" — what goes up entirely in smoke); *šĕlāmîm* (שְׁלָמִים), peace/wellbeing offering; *tĕnûpâ* (תְּנוּפָה), wave offering; *šemen ha-mišḥâ* (שֶׁמֶן הַמִּשְׁחָה), anointing oil; *ʿōlat tāmîd* (עֹלַת תָּמִיד), continual burnt offering; *šākan* (שָׁכַן), "dwell" (v. 45 — the verbal root of *miškān*, *šĕkînâ*).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Exodus 29 is the narrative and theological climax of the tabernacle instructions. The centrifugal movement from ark to court, which organized chapters 25–27, is complete; chapter 28 dressed the priest; chapter 29 installs him. The chapter then ends with the most explicit theological statement of the entire tabernacle section: "I will dwell among the people of Israel and will be their God... that I might dwell among them" (vv. 45–46). The whole project has been driving toward this verb *šākan*, "dwell." Every specification of cubit and curtain, every stone and thread, has been engineered to answer one question: how can a holy God dwell in the midst of an unholy people? Chapter 29's answer is the sustained liturgy of blood, washing, vesting, and ordination.

Genre: ritual instruction with narrative embedding. The chapter is almost entirely second-person imperatives directed to Moses ("you shall take... you shall bring... you shall wash... you shall kill..."). The rhetorical effect is to locate Moses as the ordaining priest *before* there is a priesthood — he vests Aaron, he performs the slaughter, he applies the blood. Moses is the primordial priest, who installs Aaron in a role he himself partly enacts. This narrative-theological claim (Moses as first priest) is crucial for Deuteronomy's understanding of the Mosaic office and for later Jewish theology of Moses's unique status.

Literary devices: the sevenfold occurrence of sacrificial acts (the bull, two rams, ordination over seven days, daily offerings) participates in the pervasive seven-symbolism of chapters 25–31. The inclusio formed by verses 1 and 46 — the chapter opens with "consecrate them" (*qaddēš*) and closes with "I am the LORD their God, who brought them out of Egypt that I might dwell (*šākan*) among them" — frames the ordination as the purpose toward which the exodus itself was directed. This is a stunning theological claim: Israel was not rescued from Egypt merely for freedom, or even for covenant at Sinai, but *for indwelling*.

The verbal frequency of "hands" is notable. Aaron and his sons "lay their hands" on the victim (vv. 10, 15, 19); their "hands are filled" at ordination; the portions are placed "on the palms" (vv. 24, 25) to be waved. Hands are the chapter's choreographic center: they identify with the victim, they receive the sacrificial portion, they are filled by YHWH. Leviticus 8 will replay all this narratively; Exodus 29 is the script.

A careful structural feature: the phrase "the priesthood shall be theirs by a statute forever" (v. 9), the ordination garment succession of verses 29–30, and the "throughout your generations" of verse 42 all point toward the *perpetuity* of the priestly office. This is the chapter's claim to permanence. Whatever is said about its supersession in later tradition, the text itself claims an eternal ordinance.

Textual variants: the LXX of Exodus 29 is close to MT but with occasional divergences (the Greek translator clarifies some obscure Hebrew technical terms). The Samaritan Pentateuch adds execution material synchronizing with Leviticus 8. DSS fragments preserve portions consistent with MT. The LXX's translation of *millēʾ yād* as *teleioō* ("perfect, complete") is linguistically interesting — it shapes Hebrews 7:19's polemic that "the law made nothing perfect" (the same verb), implicitly critiquing the LXX's terminology while using it.

An important narrative-theological feature: Leviticus 8–9 will later narrate the actual performance of this ordination. Exodus 29 is command; Leviticus 8 is execution. The repetition is not redundant but a P literary-theological device emphasizing that what God commanded, Moses did. The command-execution pattern is a consistent P feature throughout the tabernacle material and is found in ANE temple-building narratives (Hurowitz): divine command, human execution, divine approbation.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Aaron's ordination establishes a priesthood that will function until 70 CE — roughly 1,250 years (on a fifteenth-century Exodus) or 900 years (on a thirteenth-century) of continuous succession. Every high priest, from Aaron through Zadok's line through Hasmoneans and Herodian appointees to Phannias ben Samuel in 70 CE, traces his office to this ceremony. 1 Chronicles 24 provides the priestly courses; Josephus preserves the succession lists.

The sacrificial vocabulary here defines the rest of the Pentateuch. Leviticus 1–7 systematizes the types (burnt, grain, peace, sin, guilt); Leviticus 8–9 narrates the actual ordination; Leviticus 10 records Nadab and Abihu's death in their first week of service. Leviticus 16 describes the Day of Atonement. The *ʿōlat tāmîd* (29:38–42) becomes a narrative pulse through the OT — referenced as constitutive worship (2 Chronicles 2:4; 13:11), lamented when suppressed under Antiochus (Daniel 8:11–14; 11:31), and recorded as ceasing on 17 Tammuz 70 CE in Josephus (*Wars* 6.2.1), a date still fasted in Jewish practice.

The NT picks up the priestly thread primarily in Hebrews. Hebrews 7 contrasts the Aaronic priesthood (mortal, multiple, sacrificing often) with Christ's Melchizedekian priesthood (immortal, singular, sacrificing once). Hebrews 9:11–14 reads Christ as entering the heavenly Holy of Holies with his own blood. Hebrews 13:11–13 makes an extraordinary move: "the bodies of those animals whose blood is brought into the holy places by the high priest as a sacrifice for sin are burned *outside the camp* [Exodus 29:14]. So Jesus also suffered *outside the gate* in order to sanctify the people through his own blood. Therefore let us go to him outside the camp and bear the reproach he endured." The geography of 29:14 becomes the geography of discipleship.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Philo (*Life of Moses* 2.140–160; *Special Laws* 1.228–238) reads the ordination rite allegorically — the ear represents learning, the hand action, the foot progress in virtue. Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.205–207) describes the ceremony with historical reconstructive interest. Ben Sira 45:6–22 eulogizes Aaron; Jubilees 21:16 elaborates; the Temple Scroll 11Q19 provides an alternate scheme.

Rabbinic: Mishnah Menachot on the daily burnt offering; Mishnah Tamid on the dawn liturgy; Zevachim on sacrificial procedure; Sifra works through Leviticus 8's execution of this chapter's command. Maimonides' *Mishneh Torah* (*Hilchot Maaseh HaKorbanot*, *Hilchot Temidin u'Musafin*) codifies sacrificial laws in extraordinary detail for a hypothetical restored Temple. Medieval: Rashi on the blood rites; Ibn Ezra on the ritual grammar; Ramban on ordination as continuing Sinai (the priest enacts what happens on the mountain). Maimonides' *Guide* 3.32 argues the whole system was accommodation to a people habituated to idolatrous sacrifice; its spiritual center is prayer.

Modern: Milgrom's three-volume *Leviticus* is indispensable; Baruch Schwartz, Knohl, and David Wright on priestly theology; Gary Anderson's *Sin: A History* on atonement; Carol Meyers on archaeological dimensions.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Leviticus* is the foundational patristic work — ordination, blood, fat, seven days each given Christological meaning. Cyril of Alexandria's *Glaphyra on Exodus* develops the typology. Augustine's *City of God* 10 articulates the Christian sacramental vision. Aquinas (*Summa* I-II q. 102 a. 4) systematizes why sacrifices are fitting and how they find fulfillment in Christ. The medieval Mass is celebrated as participating in Christ's eternal high priesthood.

Reformation: Calvin's *Institutes* 2.15 (prophetic, priestly, kingly offices of Christ) is foundational — Christ alone exercises the true priestly office; human ministry does not participate in it sacrificially. Luther rejects the Mass as sacrificial repetition. Zwingli more radically removes altar from worship.

Contemporary: N. T. Wright on temple and sacrifice in Paul; Morales on ordination and graded sanctity; Fretheim on divine vulnerability in sacrifice; Brueggemann on 29:45–46 as the climax of the exodus; Jon Levenson's *Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son* on sacrificial theology's substructure.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Jewish and Christian traditions both take Exodus 29's ordination as establishing genuine priesthood, genuine atonement, and genuine indwelling. They diverge on whether priesthood continues (Jewish: yes, quiescent; Catholic/Orthodox: yes, transformed in Christ; Reformed: no, fulfilled in Christ alone), whether sacrifice continues (Jewish: awaiting restoration; Catholic: re-presented in Eucharist; Reformed: once-for-all completed; Anabaptist: never necessary), and whether the indwelling of YHWH in verse 45 is fulfilled in the Church (Christian majority) or in the ongoing covenant people (Jewish majority).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What does blood *do*? Is the chapter's saturation in blood symbolic, substitutionary, or sacramentally effective?**

*Catholic (sacramental):* Blood effects atonement because it is, according to Leviticus 17:11, "the life" — and only life can make atonement for life. The Aaronic ordination's blood rites, fulfilled in Christ's blood, continue sacramentally in the eucharistic cup. This is not symbolism; it is participation in the living blood of Christ (CCC 1333, 1364).

*Reformed (penal substitution):* Blood signifies the life forfeited by sin. The priest is ordained by the blood of a ram as a prefigurement of Christ, whose blood alone actually atones. Aaronic ordination blood is type; Christ's blood is substance. The ceremony pedagogically trained Israel for the one once-for-all substitutionary death.

*Levitical-phenomenological (Milgrom):* Blood purifies the sanctuary from the pollution generated by sin. Sin is not primarily debt but contamination; blood is not primarily substitution but detergent. The ordination's blood rites clean the priest and altar for service; this is neither juridical nor pedagogical, but ritual-ontological.

*Allegorical-Philonic:* Blood stands for life given; the ordination represents the consecrated soul. The literal blood is unnecessary once the soul is consecrated. This is Philo's broad tendency, and it resurfaces in some Alexandrian Christian and medieval monastic readings.

*Anabaptist-moral:* The blood rites testify to the seriousness of consecration but do not effect atonement in any ontological sense. God desires mercy, not sacrifice (Hosea 6:6). The whole system is didactic.

*Jewish (rabbinic):* After 70 CE, prayer and Torah study substitute for sacrificial blood (Berakhot 26b reads the three daily prayers as replacing the three daily sacrifices). The blood was once operative; it is now memorial and hopeful.

*Steelman the Levitical-phenomenological (Milgrom):* The text repeatedly uses purification vocabulary (*ṭihēr*, *kippēr*) for what blood does. The altar itself is said to be purified and made holy (29:36–37); an altar is not a sinner requiring substitution, but it can be polluted and cleansed. The priests' garments are sprinkled with blood and oil (29:21) — clothing cannot receive penal substitution but can receive ritual cleansing and consecration. Milgrom's reading, controversial as it seemed when first published, has textual data on its side.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter's blood vocabulary is more purificatory than penal. Penal substitutionary readings can be built on other biblical texts (Isaiah 53; Leviticus 17:11; Romans 3), but Exodus 29's own logic is closer to Milgrom's ritual-ontological pattern. This does not abolish Christian atonement theology; it complicates any reading that collapses the whole sacrificial system into a single juridical pattern.

**Tension 2: Is the daily *ʿōlat tāmîd* (continual burnt offering, vv. 38–42) the chapter's climax, or does the indwelling formula of v. 45 eclipse it?**

*Rabbinic:* The *tāmîd* is the liturgical spine of Israel's life. Berakhot 26b derives the three daily prayers from the daily offerings. The constancy of morning and evening worship is the chapter's enduring legacy. Verse 45's indwelling is real but grounded in the ongoing *tāmîd* — God dwells where worship is continuous.

*Reformed-biblical-theological:* Verse 45 is the climax of the chapter, the book, and the exodus itself. "I will dwell among them" is the purpose toward which everything else points. The *tāmîd* is instrumental to this indwelling; it is not the end, but the ordered means.

*Catholic:* Both are climactic and both are fulfilled — the daily Mass is the new *tāmîd*, and it is precisely in the Mass that Christ's indwelling is sacramentally realized. The two are not in tension; they are unified in eucharistic theology.

*Anabaptist:* The indwelling of verse 45 is realized in the gathered community, not in any ritual repetition. The *tāmîd* has passed; the indwelling continues.

*Steelman the Rabbinic:* If the indwelling could be separated from the daily offering, why would the text so carefully specify *both*, and why would the formula for meeting with God be tied so explicitly to the location "at the entrance of the tent of meeting... where I will meet with you" (v. 42)? The indwelling is not generic; it is located in an ongoing liturgical rhythm. Rabbinic Judaism's insistence that prayer must replace sacrifice takes the text's logic seriously: you cannot have indwelling without ordered, continual approach.

**Where this leaves us:** Verse 45 is indeed the climax, but it is climactic *within* the ritual structure, not apart from it. The indwelling is grounded in ordered approach; God does not dwell with Israel abstractly but in a community that sustains morning and evening worship. Christian traditions have taken different routes to honor this — daily Office, daily Mass, daily prayer — but none can escape the grammar without loss.

**Unresolved question:** If the chapter's climactic promise is "I will dwell among them... they shall know that I am the LORD their God, who brought them out of the land of Egypt that I might dwell among them" (vv. 45–46), then the exodus itself was for the sake of divine indwelling. What becomes of this theology when the place where God chose to dwell — the Tabernacle, then the First Temple, then the Second — is destroyed and not rebuilt? Is the promise migrated (Christian: into Christ, into the Church), fulfilled-and-deferred (Jewish: awaiting the third Temple), or was it always conditional and now transmuted? The three answers cannot be reconciled, and each makes claims the others cannot accept.

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references.

*First,* the three-point blood rite of 29:20 (right ear, right thumb, right big toe) is applied in Leviticus 14:14 to the cleansed leper. The parallel is startling. The priest entering service and the leper re-entering community are ritually equated: both are crossing from outside to inside, both have their organs of hearing, doing, and walking consecrated. The consecration of the priest and the restoration of the outcast are structurally the same rite. Jesus' ministry to lepers (Mark 1:40–45) and his authorization of the cleansed leper to present himself to the priest (v. 44) reads inside this grammar: the leper restored is the priest re-consecrated.

*Second,* the "outside the camp" disposal of the sin offering bull (29:14) will recur through the Pentateuch (Leviticus 4:12, 21; 16:27) and receive its Christian reapplication in Hebrews 13:11–13, where Jesus crucified outside Jerusalem's gate is read as the fulfillment of the sin offering burned outside the camp. "Let us go to him outside the camp, bearing the reproach he endured" (Hebrews 13:13). The place of disposal becomes the place of discipleship. When Jesus is crucified at Golgotha (Matthew 27:32–33, outside the city wall), the geography of Exodus 29:14 is being enacted.

*Third,* the climactic "I will dwell among the people of Israel and will be their God" (29:45) is quoted twice in the NT — at 2 Corinthians 6:16 ("As God said, 'I will make my dwelling among them and walk among them, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people'") and at Revelation 21:3 ("Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. He will dwell with them, and they will be his people, and God himself will be with them as their God"). The Exodus 29 formula is the biblical indwelling promise, invoked at both the apostolic Church's threshold and the new creation's arrival. When Revelation sees the holy city descending, its first words identify the city as the fulfillment of the tabernacle's ordination. From Aaron's anointing to the cubic New Jerusalem, one sentence does the theological work: "I will dwell among them."
