# Deuteronomy 4: No Form, Only a Voice

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Deuteronomy 4 is the theological climax of Moses's first address (1:1–4:43) and, on many readings, the climax of the book's historical prologue as a whole. The chapter sits in the plains of Moab (v. 46), looking backward to Horeb and forward to exile and return. Its setting is liminal: the generation that saw the Baal-peor apostasy (v. 3; cf. Num 25) is about to cross the Jordan. The chapter's political geography is still Transjordan — Beth-peor, the land of Sihon and Og, Aroer on the Arnon, Mount Hermon — but its temporal geography spans from "the day that God created man on the earth" (v. 32) to "the latter days" when Israel will be scattered (v. 30).

The chapter's visionary scope — creation to exile to return — is exactly what makes its dating the most contested in the book. W.M.L. de Wette's 1805 dissertation connecting Deuteronomy to Josiah's reform (622 BC) would already have placed Deuteronomy 4 in the seventh century, but for most critical scholars even de Wette's dating is too early for this chapter. Richard Nelson (OTL 2002), Moshe Weinfeld (*Deuteronomy 1–11* AB 1991), and Thomas Römer (*The So-Called Deuteronomistic History*, 2005) all argue that 4:25–31 — with its clear forecast of exile and restoration — presupposes the Babylonian exile and is best dated to the sixth century or later. The verb *hĕpîṣ* (v. 27) — "he will scatter" — is exactly the exilic technical term used in Jeremiah 9:16 and Ezekiel 11:17. The vocabulary of "seek with all your heart and soul" (v. 29) — *dāraš* with *bĕ-kāl lĕbāb-kā û-bĕ-kāl napšĕ-kā* — becomes a signature of post-exilic covenantal piety (Jer 29:13; Ezra-Nehemiah).

Against this, Peter Craigie (NICOT 1976) and Daniel Block (NIVAC 2012) argue the exile-and-return prediction fits prophetic foresight, not literary retrojection, and that the chapter's structure — with its careful argument from Horeb to creation to the future — is too tightly integrated to be a secondary exilic supplement. Kenneth Kitchen points to the chapter's heavy use of treaty-form language (witnesses heaven and earth, v. 26 — a standard Hittite and neo-Assyrian treaty motif).

Jeffrey Tigay (JPS 1996) and J. G. McConville (Apollos 2002) take middle positions: the core belongs to the Deuteronomic school's monarchic-era composition, but vv. 25–31 show either exilic editorial supplementation or deliberate prophetic framing that became newly relevant in exile. Weinfeld's comparative work with neo-Assyrian treaties — especially Esarhaddon's Succession Treaty (VTE, 672 BC) — shows that some of the chapter's language fits seventh-century, not earlier, conventions.

Archaeologically, the chapter's Horeb theophany is unrecoverable, but the iconographic polemic of vv. 15–19 responds to a rich Near Eastern repertoire of divine images: Assyrian winged bull-deities, Canaanite seated-god bronzes (the El statuette from Megiddo), Egyptian animal-headed gods (Horus the falcon, Anubis the jackal), astral-cult worship (the sun-disk Aten, the moon-god Sin of Harran, the Ishtar-Venus cult). The sequence in vv. 16–19 — male/female, earth animals, birds, creeping things, fish, astral hosts — mirrors the Genesis 1 creation sequence, but inverted: what was made by God is not to be made *as* God. The astral-worship polemic (v. 19) — "when you see the sun and the moon and the stars" — is particularly resonant with Assyrian religion.

Key Hebrew terms: *tĕmûnâ* (תְּמוּנָה, vv. 12, 15, 16), "form, likeness" — the chapter's polemical keyword. Israel saw *no tĕmûnâ* at Horeb, only *qôl* (קוֹל), "a voice." *Pesel* (פֶּסֶל, v. 16), "carved image," from the root meaning "to hew"; this becomes the classical Hebrew word for an idol. *Kôr barzel* (כּוּר בַּרְזֶל, v. 20), "iron furnace," metaphor for Egyptian bondage — a smelting-furnace in which slag is separated from refined metal. *ʾēš ʾōkĕlâ* (אֵשׁ אֹכְלָה, v. 24), "consuming fire" — the searing divine presence. *ʾēl qannāʾ* (אֵל קַנָּא, v. 24), "jealous God" — *qannāʾ* shares a root with *qinʾâ*, the passionate zeal of covenant exclusivity. And *bĕkāl lĕbābĕkā û-bĕkāl napšĕkā* (בְּכָל־לְבָבְךָ וּבְכָל־נַפְשְׁךָ, v. 29), "with all your heart and all your soul" — the signature Deuteronomic phrase that reappears in the Shema (6:5).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Deuteronomy 4 is formally a sermon embedded in the covenant document. Structurally it divides into three movements: (1) exhortation to obey the law as wisdom (vv. 1–8); (2) the memory of Horeb and the polemic against images (vv. 9–24); (3) the prophetic forecast of exile and return, framed by the cosmic claim of YHWH's uniqueness (vv. 25–40); followed by the cities-of-refuge appendix (vv. 41–43) and the transitional superscription to the second address (vv. 44–49).

The chapter's rhetoric is masterful. Patrick Miller (Interpretation 1990) analyzes it as a tightly structured argument moving from "your eyes have seen" (v. 9) through "you saw no form" (v. 15) to "know therefore today" (v. 39) — an epistemological climb from memory to faith. The device is what classical rhetoric would call *amplificatio*: Moses keeps raising the stakes. The argument culminates in the cosmic interrogation of vv. 32–34: has any people ever heard a god speaking out of fire and lived? Has any god ever taken a nation from the midst of another nation?

The Deuteronomic phraseology here is dense. "With all your heart and with all your soul" (v. 29); "he is a merciful God" (v. 31); "know therefore today, and lay it to your heart" (v. 39); "that it may go well with you" (v. 40); "a people of his own inheritance" (v. 20) — all are diagnostic of what Weinfeld identified as the Deuteronomic School's distinct rhetorical vocabulary. Dennis Olson (*Deuteronomy and the Death of Moses*, 1994) notes how the chapter models catechetical transmission: "make them known to your children and your children's children" (v. 9).

The iconographic polemic of vv. 15–19 is a carefully constructed literary unit. The sequence follows Genesis 1 — human (male/female), beast, bird, creeping thing, fish, astral hosts — with deliberate irony: the Creator's work cannot become the pattern for graven images of the Creator. Ronald Hendel and Benjamin Sommer (*The Bodies of God and the World of Ancient Israel*, 2009) have analyzed how Deuteronomy radicalizes aniconism beyond Exodus's prohibition of images by adding the rationale that Israel *saw no form* — theological aniconism grounded in historical memory.

Textual variants are significant. 4QDeutj (Qumran) preserves portions of Deuteronomy 4 with some variation from MT; Papyrus Nash (second or first century BC, discovered in Egypt in 1898) contains the Decalogue and Shema but not chapter 4 itself, though it witnesses to the centrality of the Decalogue-Shema complex in early Jewish liturgy. The Septuagint translates *tĕmûnâ* (form) as *homoiōma* (likeness) — the same word used in the New Testament for the incarnation in Rom 8:3 and Phil 2:7, creating interesting translational resonances. Weinfeld notes that the Samaritan Pentateuch makes several harmonizations at vv. 41–43, aligning with the cities-of-refuge list in Joshua 20–21.

The most important literary feature of the chapter may be its temporal scope. Verse 32 deliberately invokes creation ("the days that are past, which were before you, since the day that God created man on the earth") and v. 30 projects to "the latter days" (*bĕʾaḥărît hayyāmîm*) — a phrase that later becomes the prophetic-apocalyptic marker for end-time. The chapter thus brackets all of history between creation and eschaton, placing Israel's Horeb experience at the pivot point.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

Deuteronomy 4's aniconism became the Jewish tradition's charter for imageless worship. The claim that Israel "saw no form" was taken as the decisive warrant for the prohibition on representing the divine. Philo of Alexandria (*On the Decalogue* 66–81) developed this philosophically: God has no form because God is not a body. Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.5–6) explains to Roman readers why Jewish religion differs from Greco-Roman visual piety by citing this chapter. The Dead Sea Scrolls community, austerely aniconic, took Deuteronomy's "no form" seriously.

The rabbinic tradition is extensive. *Sifre Deuteronomy* on chapter 4 explores every phrase; *Mekhilta* on the parallel Exodus material draws on Deuteronomy 4's logic. Rashi (eleventh c.) emphasizes that Israel's direct encounter with a voice-without-form places it in a unique epistemic position: its monotheism is not philosophical but witnessed. Ibn Ezra notes the anticipated exile-and-return structure without flinching from its apparent prophetic foresight. Nachmanides (Ramban) gives v. 29 ("from there you will seek the LORD... and find him") as warrant for the possibility of repentance even in exile — a text of enormous importance for medieval Jewish life.

Maimonides (*Guide of the Perplexed* 1.1) quotes Deuteronomy 4:12, 15 as the foundational texts for divine incorporeality — one of his Thirteen Principles of Faith. The Jewish tradition's intellectual architecture of God — non-corporeal, non-imagable, approached through word rather than sight — rests substantially on this chapter. Judah Halevi (*Kuzari*) reads v. 32 ("ask now of the days that are past") as the argument from historical uniqueness: only Israel's revelation meets the epistemic standard of direct collective experience.

Modern Jewish thought has returned repeatedly to this chapter. Heschel (*God in Search of Man*, 1955) treats the voice-without-form as the beginning of the prophetic tradition. Jon Levenson (*Sinai and Zion*, 1985) analyzes the cosmic scope of vv. 32–39 as a theological uniqueness claim. Michael Fishbane (*Biblical Interpretation in Ancient Israel*, 1985) traces the chapter's inner-biblical reuse of Exodus 19–20 material. Yeshayahu Leibowitz read the chapter's aniconism as the categorical difference between Judaism and every idolatrous religion. Aviva Zornberg's work engages the chapter's traumatic memory-discipline.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

Patristic reception of Deuteronomy 4 was dominated by two themes. First, the aniconic material was foundational to early Christian resistance to Greco-Roman image-worship. Origen (*Contra Celsum*), Tertullian (*On Idolatry*), Clement of Alexandria, and Eusebius all cite chapter 4 against pagan idolatry. Athanasius (*On the Incarnation*) has to carefully distinguish the Word's becoming flesh from the making of images forbidden here. Second, the chapter's cosmic uniqueness claim ("the LORD is God; there is no other besides him," v. 35, 39) became the basic text for Christian monotheism in dialogue with polytheism.

Byzantine iconoclasm controversies made Deuteronomy 4 central for several centuries. Iconoclast theologians cited v. 15 ("you saw no form") as decisive proof against images. John of Damascus (eighth c.), in his three *Apologies for the Holy Images*, had to argue carefully that Deuteronomy's prohibition applied to the invisible divine essence, not to the visible incarnate Christ. The Seventh Ecumenical Council (Nicaea II, 787 AD) ruled with John against the iconoclasts, but Deuteronomy 4 remained a text to be reckoned with. The Eastern Orthodox tradition eventually settled into a carefully distinguished theology: icons of Christ are permitted because the invisible became visible; images of the Father are not.

The medieval Western tradition followed a different path. Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* I-II q. 99 a. 3) discussed the image-prohibition as ceremonial-pedagogical, suited to Israel's context but not absolutely binding on the church. The *Glossa Ordinaria* allegorized the aniconism as a disciplining of the spiritual senses. Medieval devotional culture — crucifixes, statuary, stained glass — developed largely without feeling the pressure of Deuteronomy 4, though Bernard of Clairvaux and later Cistercian reformers cited the chapter in critiques of ornate decoration.

The Reformation retrieved Deuteronomy 4 with full force. Calvin's *Institutes* 1.11–12 is an extended meditation on this chapter (together with Exodus 20). Calvin cites v. 15 repeatedly as the decisive text: because God presented himself to Israel without form, no image can properly represent him. Calvin's iconoclasm — and the waves of image-breaking that swept Reformation Geneva, Zurich, the Netherlands, Scotland, and England — rested theologically on Deuteronomy 4. Luther was less severe but still critical; Zwingli and the Swiss Reformed were severe indeed. Calvin's *Commentary on the Last Four Books of Moses* (on 4:15–18) is as pointed as anything he ever wrote on Catholic devotional practice.

Critical scholarship from de Wette onward has treated chapter 4 as a theological high point but a compositionally layered one. Wellhausen treated the whole as late. Martin Noth's *Deuteronomistic History* (1943) places 4:1–40 as the transitional frame between the historical prologue and the book's deuteronomic core. Weinfeld's AB commentary provides exhaustive ANE parallels. Thomas Römer distinguishes exilic and post-exilic horizons within the chapter, with vv. 25–31 typically seen as post-587 BC supplement.

Contemporary Christian commentary is varied. Walter Brueggemann (AOTC 2001) emphasizes the chapter's radical theology of God's word. Patrick Miller (Interpretation 1990) analyzes the rhetorical architecture. Christopher Wright (NIBC 1996) develops the missiological claim of vv. 6–8 — Israel's law as witness to the nations. Daniel Block (NIVAC 2012) gives a pastoral-theological reading. McConville (Apollos 2002) treats the chapter as the theological heart of Deuteronomy. N.T. Wright cites the chapter's exile-return structure as foundational for Second Temple Jewish hope and therefore for NT theology.

### Convergence and Divergence

Jewish and Christian traditions converge on Deuteronomy 4's monotheistic claim and its aniconic polemic. Both traditions have, at various points, drawn image-prohibition arguments from this chapter — Jews continuously, Christians in iconoclast waves. Both traditions have treated vv. 32–39 as a foundational text for the uniqueness of divine revelation.

They diverge sharply on the incarnation question. Jewish tradition preserves "you saw no form" as categorical: God has no form and cannot be seen. Christian tradition, especially in the Byzantine-Orthodox and later medieval West, developed the claim that in Christ the formless assumed form. The iconoclast controversy shows how much tension this creates; the Protestant Reformation shows how much tension survived into modernity. Richard Hays's *Echoes of Scripture* (2016) has pressed contemporary Christians to reckon honestly with the Jewish reading rather than simply supersede it.

The exile-return structure of 4:25–31 has had different trajectories too. Jewish tradition has lived with it continuously as a map of diaspora hope. Christian tradition, after the first century, often read it as fulfilled in Christ's return from the "exile" of human sin, flattening its territorial specificity. Contemporary Jewish and Christian scholarship (Levenson, N.T. Wright, Hays) is recovering the text's literal-political weight.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

### Tension 1: No Form and Yet the Voice — Can God Be Represented?

The chapter's insistence that Israel "saw no form" but "heard a voice" (vv. 12, 15) generates the tension that runs through Jewish and Christian theology for three millennia.

**Reformed**: Calvin's position (Institutes 1.11) is that no image can properly represent God; images lead to idolatry; therefore images in worship are prohibited. Reformed iconoclasm is not a historical accident but a theological consequence of Deuteronomy 4 read strictly. Jonathan Edwards preached within this tradition, though with greater attention to typology.

**Lutheran**: Luther took a more moderate position — images are not forbidden per se, only their worship; Lutheran churches retained crucifixes, altars, and artwork that Reformed churches destroyed. Luther's *Large Catechism* on the First Commandment draws on Deuteronomy 4 but reads the prohibition as against false worship rather than all representation.

**Catholic**: The Catholic tradition, formalized at Trent (Session 25, 1563) and carried into the modern *Catechism* (#2129–2132), distinguishes sharply between the worship due to God alone (*latria*) and the veneration of images (*dulia*). Images are pedagogical, not objects of worship. Trent cited Deuteronomy 4 but interpreted it as against idol-worship, not against religious imagery as such.

**Orthodox**: Eastern Orthodox theology, worked out most fully by John of Damascus against iconoclasm, distinguishes the invisible divine essence (which cannot be imaged) from the incarnate Christ (who can). Icons are theological statements about incarnation, not idols. Deuteronomy 4 is honored; it applies to what God was before the incarnation, not to what God has become in Christ.

**Jewish**: Jewish tradition holds the text's literal force. *Tĕmûnâ* is not a limitation but an absolute: God cannot be depicted. Maimonides's Thirteen Principles of Faith codify divine incorporeality as a matter of belief. Synagogue architecture, Torah scroll veneration, and Jewish visual culture have developed elaborately without ever representing God.

**Critical**: Nelson, Weinfeld, and Benjamin Sommer argue that Deuteronomy radicalizes the aniconism of earlier Israelite religion; the chapter represents a particular theological development, not the whole Hebrew Bible's position. The "body of God" is visible in earlier strata (Exodus 24, Isaiah 6); Deuteronomy's aniconism is a theological innovation with polemical edge.

**Steelman (Orthodox incarnational reading, least intuitive for Protestant iconoclasts)**: If the reason Israel saw no form at Horeb was that YHWH had not yet taken visible form in Christ, then the Orthodox position is not a betrayal of Deuteronomy 4 but its eschatological fulfillment. The "no form" of Horeb is preserved as the proper worship of the invisible divinity; what has changed is that the invisible has become visible in Christ, making a new kind of image possible. This steelman requires one to accept the incarnation as real and therefore as changing the image question — but within that acceptance, the Orthodox reading is theologically coherent.

**Where this leaves us**: The tension is real and has shaped three millennia of religious practice. The stakes are not merely aesthetic. Reformed iconoclasm, Catholic iconography, Orthodox veneration, and Jewish aniconism all claim fidelity to Deuteronomy 4. The tension cannot be resolved without taking a position on the incarnation itself.

### Tension 2: Can a Book Predict Its Own Future? The Exile Forecast

Verses 25–31 forecast Israel's idolatry in the land, YHWH's scattering of Israel among the nations, and Israel's repentance and restoration "in the latter days." Critical scholarship reads this as exilic-era composition. Traditional reading takes it as prophetic foresight.

**Reformed**: The traditional Reformed position is that the Holy Spirit can speak through Moses about events centuries future; the exile forecast is real prophecy. Calvin and the Westminster tradition take vv. 25–31 as one of several Mosaic prophecies (cf. Deut 28, 30) that were fulfilled in exile.

**Catholic**: Catholic tradition generally holds that Deuteronomy's substance is Mosaic while allowing editorial supplementation. The *Pontifical Biblical Commission* documents of the early twentieth century upheld Mosaic substance; later documents (*Dei Verbum*, 1965) allow for the complexity of biblical composition without denying prophetic character.

**Wesleyan / Arminian**: Wesley treats the forecast as genuine prophecy. The Wesleyan tradition has been comfortable with prophetic predictive material.

**Jewish**: Traditional Jewish reading takes vv. 25–31 as Mosaic prophecy; medieval commentators (Rashi, Ramban) treat it as such. Modern Jewish scholars have entertained the critical reading without abandoning the traditional. The text's liturgical function in Jewish life — a source of exilic hope — is preserved either way.

**Critical**: Weinfeld, Nelson, Römer, and most modern scholars treat vv. 25–31 as an exilic or post-exilic insertion. The technical vocabulary (*hĕpîṣ*, "scatter") matches Jeremiah and Ezekiel; the theology of seeking God "with all your heart" matches post-exilic piety. This is not prophecy predicted but prophecy retrojected.

**Steelman (critical reading, least intuitive for traditional believers)**: The text's rhetorical power comes precisely from its exilic composition. The audience is a community that has experienced scattering and is hoping for restoration; the "Moses" who speaks is the Deuteronomic-school author giving voice to the founder in terms the exilic generation needs to hear. This is not deception; it is a recognized genre of ancient literature (pseudepigraphic prophecy). The text's theological depth rests on the truth it tells about YHWH's faithfulness, not on the chronological accuracy of the prediction.

**Where this leaves us**: The question of whether this is prophecy or retrojected composition has been asked since the medieval period (Ibn Ezra's "secret of the twelve") and remains unresolved. What cannot be denied is the theological claim: exile and return are not outside YHWH's covenant faithfulness. Jewish and Christian communities in exile — Babylonian, Roman, medieval, modern — have drawn life from this text regardless of its compositional history.

**One unresolved question**: If vv. 19's allocation of the heavenly bodies to the nations ("things that the LORD your God has allotted to all the peoples under the whole heaven") is taken seriously, does YHWH permit other peoples to worship astral deities as legitimately their own? The text's syntactic ambiguity is famous; critical readings (Nelson) see a vestigial polytheism here that Deuteronomy both preserves and repudiates.

## The Thread

Three cross-references that open the chapter outward:

First, v. 19's "allotted to all the peoples" connects to Deuteronomy 32:8–9, where YHWH "divided the nations" and "fixed the borders of the peoples according to the number of the sons of God" (following the DSS and LXX reading). The connection opens onto a Second Temple theology — especially in the Enoch literature and Paul's "principalities and powers" — in which the nations have their own heavenly rulers, subordinate to YHWH but distinct. The Jewish and early Christian traditions (Justin Martyr, Origen) developed this into a rich angelology of nations.

Second, "you will seek the LORD your God and you will find him, if you search after him with all your heart and with all your soul" (v. 29) is picked up almost verbatim in Jeremiah 29:13 ("you will seek me and find me, when you seek me with all your heart") — in Jeremiah's famous letter to the exiles in Babylon. The Deuteronomic promise becomes Jeremiah's consolation. And it returns in Matthew 7:7, "seek and you shall find." The line of the promise runs from Horeb through Babylon to the Sermon on the Mount.

Third, "the LORD is God in heaven above and on the earth beneath; there is no other" (v. 39) is quoted by Rahab in Joshua 2:11 — the Canaanite prostitute who shelters the Israelite spies. The monotheistic confession of Deuteronomy 4 migrates across the Jordan before Israel does, placed in the mouth of a non-Israelite woman in the book of Joshua. The text's claim that YHWH alone is God is not only Israel's claim; it is the claim that, in Rahab, crosses a boundary.

Finally — a fourth thread — the "consuming fire" of v. 24 is quoted in Hebrews 12:29, at the climax of the New Testament's most sustained engagement with Deuteronomic theology. The author of Hebrews, having contrasted Sinai with Zion, warns the community: "our God is a consuming fire." The line of continuity — not discontinuity — between the two testaments runs through this sentence.
