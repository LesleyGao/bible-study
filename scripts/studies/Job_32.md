# Job 32: The Young Man Who Arrives Late

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Job 32 introduces one of the most disputed figures in Hebrew literature: Elihu ben Barachel the Buzite, of the family of Ram. He appears out of nowhere. The prologue (chapters 1–2) names Job, the three friends Eliphaz, Bildad, Zophar, the satan, Job's wife, his children — but no Elihu. The epilogue (42:7–9) names Eliphaz, Bildad, Zophar again as those rebuked by God and requiring Job's intercession — but no Elihu. He speaks for six chapters (32–37), at the longest sustained stretch of any character other than Job, and then vanishes without comment. This absence bracketing his presence is the central historical-critical puzzle of the book.

The name is carefully specified. *Elihu ben Barachel ha-buzi mi-mishpachat ram* (אֱלִיהוּא בֶן־בַּרַכְאֵל הַבּוּזִי מִמִּשְׁפַּחַת רָם, 32:2). *Elihu* means "He is my God" or "My God is he" — a confessional name. *Barachel* means "God blesses" or "El blesses." *Buzi* points to the Buzites, descendants of Buz, the second son of Nahor, Abraham's brother (Genesis 22:21). Buz is mentioned as a nation in Jeremiah 25:23 alongside Dedan and Tema — Edomite and North Arabian territory, overlapping with the assumed setting of Uz. The Buzite connection places Elihu in the same Transjordanian/North Arabian wisdom milieu as Job himself and the three friends (Eliphaz the Temanite, Bildad the Shuhite, Zophar the Naamathite). The "family of Ram" is uncertain; some rabbinic traditions identified Ram with the Ram of Ruth 4:19 (ancestor of David) to tie Elihu into Israelite lineage, but this is homiletic.

Historically, the speech of a young man to elders violates ancient Near Eastern honor protocol. The detail that Elihu "had waited" (*hochah yahwah*, חִכָּה, 32:4) is not incidental; the text is making a point about propriety stretched to breaking. Pope (Anchor Bible) compared the motif to Egyptian and Mesopotamian "contest" literature where a young sage confronts older ones — parallels in the *Instruction of Ptahhotep* and in various Sumerian disputations. But the closer parallel is perhaps the Akkadian *Dialogue of Pessimism* and the *Babylonian Theodicy*, in which inherited wisdom is tested and sometimes overturned.

The dating debate intensifies here. Critical scholars who see the Elihu speeches as a *later insertion* — Pope (1965, Anchor), Clines (WBC III), Newsom (*Contest of Moral Imaginations*, 2003), Westermann (*The Structure of the Book of Job*, German 1956, English trans. 1981), Fohrer, Pfeiffer, Driver-Gray — cite five main pieces of evidence:

First, Elihu is absent from the prologue and epilogue. If he were an original character, the careful narrative frame should have included him.

Second, his Hebrew differs. Studies by Snaith, Gordis, and more recently by Tur-Sinai show higher incidence of Aramaisms in the Elihu speeches (*aḥawa* for "I will tell," *millah* for "word," *maʿbed* constructions). Whether Aramaisms indicate late date or dialect or author-specific style is contested; the point is the Elihu section reads differently.

Third, Elihu quotes and cites Job by name (33:8–11; 34:5–9), something no other character does. He quotes Job as if from a text, suggesting he was inserted into a text that already existed.

Fourth, the theological content of Elihu's speeches — particularly the theodicy-as-instruction motif in chapter 33 — has been read as a softening or correction of the bolder original book.

Fifth, YHWH in the whirlwind engages Job and the three friends but never Elihu, as if Elihu were not part of the original dialogue.

Defending the originality of Elihu: Gordis (*The Book of Job*), Fyall (*Now My Eyes Have Seen You*), Andersen (TOTC), Habel (OTL, who treats the tension carefully), and more recently Hartley (NICOT). Their arguments: the differences can be explained as characterization (the poet giving Elihu a distinctive voice), Elihu's silence at theophany is deliberate irony, and the narrative frame may reflect Elihu's youth/junior status (too junior to be named). The debate remains genuinely open.

Key Hebrew terms: *ruach ʾEl* (רוּחַ אֵל, "spirit of God," 32:8) and *nishmat shadday* (נִשְׁמַת שַׁדַּי, "breath of the Almighty," 32:8) — Elihu's claim that understanding comes from divine inspiration, not merely age, is a pneumatological novelty in the book. *Charon ʾaf* (חָרָה אַף, "his anger burned," four times in 32:2–5) — the chapter's emotional key signature. *Daʿa* (דֵּעָה, "opinion" or "knowledge," 32:6, 10, 17) — the three-fold declaration of intent to speak. *Chemer* (חֵמֶר, wine, 32:19) — the image of fermentation about to burst the skin, a rich ancient viticulture metaphor.

Archaeologically, the image of new wineskins bursting is authentic. Ancient wineskins were goat or sheep skins sewn together; fermentation gas built up in fresh wine, and old skins stretched or split. The same image appears in Mark 2:22. Wine-press installations with fermentation basins have been excavated at sites throughout Iron Age Judah and the Transjordan.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Job 32 is structurally distinctive on two counts. First, it is the only sustained prose insertion in the middle of the poetic body of the book. Verses 1–6a are prose narrative; the prose resumes only at 42:7. Critical scholars have taken this shift as further evidence of secondary insertion: the narrator has been absent since chapter 2, and the sudden return to narrative prose to introduce Elihu is read as editorial seaming. Clines (WBC III) provides a detailed structural comparison showing that 32:1–6a reads more like the prologue narrator than like the surrounding poetic dialogue.

Second, the chapter is remarkably *self-conscious about speech*. Elihu spends almost the entire poetic section (32:6b–22) justifying his speaking rather than actually saying anything substantive. Newsom observes that if you remove the meta-discourse, very little of Elihu's content begins until chapter 33. The effect is a prolonged throat-clearing — which some readers (Gregory the Great, Calvin) took as evidence of arrogance and others (Gordis, Habel) as literary craft, establishing character before content.

Alter (*Wisdom Books*) notes the chapter's deft use of the young-and-old trope. Elihu's "I am young in years, and you are aged" (*tsaʿir ani leyamim*, צָעִיר אֲנִי לְיָמִים, 32:6) sets up the reversal: age should carry wisdom, but the spirit is the real source. This is a wisdom-tradition commonplace (cf. Sirach 25:3–6, Eccl 4:13), and Elihu is deploying it to unseat his elders rhetorically.

The wineskin image (32:18–20) is unusually vivid. *Hinneh vitni keyayin lo yippateah* (הִנֵּה־בִטְנִי כְּיַיִן לֹא־יִפָּתֵחַ, "my belly is like wine that has no vent," 32:19) — Elihu frames his speech as a physiological necessity. Good (*In Turns of Tempest*) reads this wryly: Elihu presents his speaking as a bodily discharge, which can be read either as sincere urgency or as embarrassing self-revelation. The poet keeps the ambiguity alive.

The LXX handles chapter 32 without significant shortening — unlike many other chapters of Job, where the Greek is dramatically compressed, the Elihu section is rendered nearly in full, suggesting that by the time of the LXX translator (second century BCE) the Elihu material was established in the received text.

Literarily, the chapter prepares the reader for four speeches to follow (chs 33, 34, 35, 36–37). It establishes Elihu as angry, young, claiming divine inspiration, rhetorically self-conscious, and (importantly) *not anticipated by any previous mention*. The question of whether this is a defect or a feature is the heart of the critical debate.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Elihu has divided the tradition. The Jewish reception was initially warm and became more complex over time.

The Testament of Job (first century BCE/CE) does not give Elihu a prominent role, suggesting that the pseudepigraphon's author found the figure less interesting than the three friends. *Bava Batra* 15b–16b discusses Job's origins but does not dwell on Elihu, though later rabbinic tradition did identify him (Rashi, following an earlier view) with Balaam or with a prophet of unusual standing. Rashi glosses Elihu's speeches moderately, taking them as a legitimate if youthful contribution. Ibn Ezra similarly reads Elihu as genuine.

The pivotal Jewish reading is Maimonides in *Guide of the Perplexed* III.22–23. Maimonides identifies five theories of providence in Job: that of the common view (Job himself, before his crisis); of Eliphaz (classical retributive theology); of Bildad (providence through repentance); of Zophar (providence through inscrutable divine wisdom); and — most significantly — of Elihu. Elihu's contribution, as Maimonides reads it, is the theory of *providence through divine warning*: God sends dreams, illnesses, and suffering as *instruction*, not punishment, to turn the sufferer back from pride and preserve him from the pit (the reference is chiefly to 33:14–30). Maimonides treats this as the most philosophically adequate position in the book, though still short of the whirlwind's final correction. This favorable reading of Elihu became canonical in much of the Jewish philosophical tradition — Gersonides and Abarbanel follow it — and it is one of the great arguments against the critical position that Elihu is a secondary insertion. Maimonides finds the Elihu speeches doing theological work no other voice in the book does.

Zornberg in her midrashic readings continues this tradition, treating Elihu as the voice of a newer, more inward understanding of God's dealings with the human soul.

In Christian tradition, the reception has been more negative, at least in the medieval period. Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job* treats Elihu as the archetype of arrogant youth — a Pharisee-before-Christ, claiming spiritual authority without the experience to warrant it. Gregory's reading turns on Elihu's boast of divine inspiration (32:8) combined with the subsequent rebuke of Job; Gregory sees this as vain. The Gregorian reading shaped medieval exegesis and was the default in Western tradition through the Reformation. Chrysostom and the Antiochene tradition were somewhat milder but similarly skeptical.

Aquinas, following Gregory partially, treats Elihu more carefully. In *Expositio super Iob ad litteram*, Aquinas distinguishes between what Elihu says well (his insights into divine providence and instruction) and what he overreaches (his confidence in his own youth's inspiration). Aquinas leaves room for a partial defense of Elihu's content while criticizing his posture.

Calvin in his sermons is famously harsh on Elihu: he treats him as a puffed-up young man whose content, while partly sound, is vitiated by his self-congratulatory preface. The bursting-wineskin metaphor Calvin handles with particular irony. Luther was more divided; Wellhausen and Gunkel in the historical-critical tradition returned to the secondary-insertion theory, which has remained the mainstream critical position into the twentieth century.

Modern critical scholarship has refined rather than overturned this. Newsom, in *Contest of Moral Imaginations*, offers perhaps the most sophisticated reading: whether or not the Elihu speeches are secondary in terms of compositional history, they function in the received text as a deliberate *delay*. Elihu postpones the theophany, and the postponement is itself meaningful — it forces the reader to confront whether any human speech could substitute for God's. Habel (OTL) reads Elihu as a foil whose speeches the theophany will transcend. Clines (WBC III) is more severe, holding to the secondary-insertion thesis and reading Elihu's content as theologically thin and rhetorically pompous. Hartley (NICOT) defends Elihu's integrity more strongly, arguing that the speeches advance the argument. Fyall (*Now My Eyes Have Seen You*) reads Elihu as preparatory for the whirlwind, arguing his theology aligns with (rather than contradicts) YHWH's. Gutiérrez is more restrained; he focuses on Job's voice and leaves Elihu to the side. Davis (*Getting Involved with God*) offers a pastoral reading in which Elihu's interruption is read as the voice of the community breaking into the dialogue, for better or worse.

The scholarly consensus as it currently stands: the majority of critical scholars continue to read Elihu as a later insertion, while a vocal minority (Gordis, Fyall, Andersen, Hartley) defend his originality. The truth may be that the material passed through multiple hands — an original Elihu section expanded, or an inserted section that nevertheless developed a theologically serious role.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is Elihu an Arrogant Youth or a Prophetic Voice?**

- *Gregorian reading* (Gregory, Calvin, much of the Christian medieval tradition): Elihu is the embodiment of unearned spiritual confidence. His wordy self-introduction, his claim to divine inspiration, his burning anger at his elders — all mark him as the kind of voice Wisdom warns against. The narrative frames him for a fall, even if God does not rebuke him explicitly.
- *Maimonidean reading* (Maimonides, Gersonides, Abarbanel, Fyall, partial Aquinas): Elihu represents a real theological advance. The theory of providence-as-instruction (dreams, suffering as divine warning) is the most philosophically adequate position offered by any human speaker in the book. The narrative's ambiguity about him — neither rebuked nor vindicated — reflects his genuinely intermediate status.
- *Steelman the least intuitive — Newsom's literary-functional reading*: The question of Elihu's sincerity or adequacy misses the point. Elihu functions to postpone the theophany, and the postponement is itself theologically generative. By the time YHWH finally speaks, the reader has been exposed to *every human theodicy available* — retributive, reformist, mystical, instructional — and found them all wanting. Elihu's role is to exhaust the human options so that when God speaks, we hear it as something genuinely other. Whether Elihu is arrogant or inspired is secondary to whether his six chapters successfully empty out human confidence so that divine speech can be heard on its own terms.

**Tension 2: Does Spirit Trump Age? (32:7–9)**

- *Traditional wisdom reading*: Age brings experience; experience brings wisdom. Elihu's elevation of spirit (*ruach*) over age is an inversion that the rest of the Hebrew Bible rarely warrants. Proverbs consistently privileges the wisdom of the elderly. Elihu is therefore standing against the grain of the tradition — and his doing so is marked by the narrator's repeated note of his anger.
- *Prophetic reading* (Gordis, Habel, Fyall): The Hebrew Bible does recognize that *ruach* can override inherited structures. Samuel speaks before Eli, Jeremiah is too young, David is the least of Jesse's sons. Elihu is invoking a real prophetic principle, not inventing one. His claim is not that age is worthless but that it is not the only pathway to wisdom — a claim the Hebrew Bible affirms elsewhere.
- *Steelman the least intuitive — the epistemic reading* (implicit in Newsom, Ticciati): Elihu's claim about *ruach* is not finally about the young-versus-old quarrel at all. It is a *claim about where human understanding comes from* — that wisdom is not a cumulation of experience but an inbreathing. This is a profoundly different epistemology from the proverbial one, and it sets up the whirlwind theophany (where knowledge comes from direct divine disclosure, not from dialogue or accumulated learning) in a way that neither the three friends nor Job has been able to. If we read Elihu as right about the *source* of knowledge while still inadequate about its *content*, we have a more nuanced account of his place in the book.

**Where this leaves us:** Whether Elihu is arrogant or inspired, whether his speeches are original or inserted, whether his content is advance or obstacle — these questions remain genuinely open in the scholarly literature. What is clear is that the narrator frames him carefully: angry, youthful, self-conscious, claiming inspiration, and unaccountable (neither rebuked nor commended at the end).

*One unresolved question*: If God never addresses Elihu directly in the whirlwind speeches, and never rebukes him in the epilogue, what is the narrative theology of his silence? Is God's non-response approval, indifference, or irrelevance?

## The Thread

- **1 Samuel 3** — the young Samuel hearing God while the aged Eli sleeps. The non-obvious parallel: the Hebrew Bible has a pattern of the *young voice that overturns accumulated authority*, and Elihu is invoking this pattern (rightly or self-servingly is the question).
- **Numbers 11:24–29** — the spirit resting on the seventy elders, and Moses' wish "would that all the LORD's people were prophets." The wish that spirit would democratize prophecy is exactly what Elihu claims has happened to him; the claim is older than Pentecost.
- **Jeremiah 1:6–8** — "I do not know how to speak, for I am only a youth." The called-young-prophet tradition; Elihu is either its heir or its parody.
- **Acts 2:17** — "your sons and your daughters shall prophesy… your young men shall see visions." The Pentecost citation of Joel imagines a future where age no longer gates the spirit. Whether Elihu is a premature glimpse of that future or a cautionary tale about claiming it too soon is perhaps the sharpest way to frame the enduring question about his role.
