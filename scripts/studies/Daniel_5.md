# Daniel 5: The Writing on the Wall and the Fall of Babylon

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The narrative places us inside the throne room of Babylon on what tradition has fixed as the city's last night — October 12, 539 BC by most reconstructions — when Cyrus the Great's general Gubaru (or Ugbaru, the name appears with both spellings in the Nabonidus Chronicle) entered the city. The political situation behind the chapter is the collapse of the Neo-Babylonian Empire. Nabonidus, the actual reigning king, had spent roughly a decade at the oasis of Tema in Arabia, leaving administration of Babylon proper to his son Belshazzar. The Cyrus Cylinder, recovered from the foundations of the Esagila temple in 1879 and now in the British Museum, frames the Persian conquest as a peaceful restoration in which the gods of Babylon turned against an impious Nabonidus and welcomed Cyrus. The Nabonidus Chronicle, an Akkadian cuneiform text that gives a year-by-year administrative account of Nabonidus's reign, records that Belshazzar was placed in charge of the army and that "the king died not" — meaning Nabonidus survived the fall, was deported, and Belshazzar fell.

This is the textual key to the most famous historical problem in the Hebrew Bible. Daniel 5 calls Belshazzar the son of Nebuchadnezzar (5:2, 11, 13, 18, 22) and treats him as the reigning king of Babylon. Both descriptions are inaccurate by strict genealogical and constitutional standards. Belshazzar was the son of Nabonidus, not Nebuchadnezzar; he held the office of co-regent or crown prince, not king. From antiquity until 1854, when J. G. Taylor's excavations at Ur turned up cylinders naming Belshazzar alongside his father Nabonidus, the figure was known nowhere outside Daniel — a fact that made nineteenth-century critics confident he was invented. The cuneiform recovery vindicated Daniel's knowledge of his existence even as it complicated Daniel's account of his lineage. **Donald J. Wiseman**, in *Nebuchadrezzar and Babylon* (1985), argued that "son" in Aramaic *bar* and Hebrew *ben* commonly designates a successor or descendant in royal contexts, and that Belshazzar's mother (the queen-figure of 5:10–12) may have been a daughter of Nebuchadnezzar — making him a grandson by blood. **John J. Collins** in his Hermeneia volume (*Daniel*, 1993) and **Carol Newsom** in the Old Testament Library volume (*Daniel*, 2014) treat the genealogy as a literary conflation by a later redactor who collapsed the Neo-Babylonian dynasty into Nebuchadnezzar's name because he was the only Babylonian king remembered by post-exilic Jewish tradition. **Lester Grabbe**'s *A History of the Jews and Judaism in the Second Temple Period* (2004) reads the slippage as a marker of distance from the events themselves.

The temple vessels that anchor the chapter's moral logic (5:2, 23) were taken by Nebuchadnezzar in 587 BC, when the Babylonians sacked Jerusalem and burned the Temple. Their use as drinking cups at a state banquet is not casual sacrilege but ritual provocation. Babylonian banquet protocol — the *qarītu*, attested in the royal records — involved libations to the state pantheon; using *qodesh qodashim* vessels (קֹדֶשׁ קָדָשִׁים, "holy of holies" implements) for that purpose pulled the God of Israel into a contest of pantheons that the chapter then resolves on his terms. The material culture is exact even where the genealogy is loose.

The dating of the book of Daniel itself — the most consequential issue in Old Testament source criticism — runs through this chapter. The conservative position, defended by **Stephen Miller** in the *New American Commentary* (1994), **Joyce Baldwin** in the *Tyndale Old Testament Commentary* (1978), and **Gleason Archer** in his *Survey of Old Testament Introduction*, holds that Daniel was composed in the sixth century BC by the historical Daniel, with the apparent errors resolvable through accession-language conventions and unrecovered evidence. The critical position, dominant in the academy since **Porphyry of Tyre**'s third-century *Against the Christians* (preserved by Jerome) and consolidated by S. R. Driver, R. H. Charles, H. H. Rowley, and James Montgomery's *International Critical Commentary* (1927), holds that the book in its present form was redacted under the Maccabean crisis (167–164 BC), with the court tales of chapters 1–6 representing earlier traditions and the visions of 7–12 reflecting the Antiochene present. Collins, Newsom, and Grabbe extend this consensus with attention to the Aramaic block (2:4b–7:28), which they read as an originally independent collection that was bilingually framed by a Hebrew redactor.

The textual situation is unusually rich. Eight Daniel manuscripts were recovered from Qumran (1QDan-a, b; 4QDan-a through e; 6QDan; pap6QDan), all consistent with the Masoretic text and dating from roughly 125 BC to 50 AD — close enough to a Maccabean composition to make the tightness of transmission notable. The Old Greek (LXX) version of Daniel diverges significantly from the Masoretic text and Theodotion's revision; for chapters 4–6 the divergence is large enough that scholars speak of two distinct recensions. The **Greek Additions to Daniel** — the Prayer of Azariah and Song of the Three Young Men, Susanna, and Bel and the Dragon — survive only in Greek and Latin and are canonical in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles, deuterocanonical or apocryphal in Protestant ones. The **Prayer of Nabonidus (4QPrNab)**, recovered from Qumran Cave 4, tells a parallel tradition in which Nabonidus himself (not Nebuchadnezzar) is afflicted with a skin disease at Tema and is healed by a Jewish exorcist — strong evidence that the figure of Nebuchadnezzar in Daniel 4 absorbed traditions originally about Nabonidus, which strengthens Newsom's reading of dynastic conflation here in chapter 5.

A note on key terms. The writing on the wall — *mene mene tekel parsin* (מְנֵא מְנֵא תְּקֵל וּפַרְסִין) — works as Akkadian-Aramaic monetary weights (mina, mina, shekel, half-shekels) in surface meaning, and as a verbal pun on the roots *mny* (to number), *tql* (to weigh), and *prs* (to divide / and Persia, *paras*) in Daniel's interpretation. The pun on *peres / paras* — divided and Persia — is the hinge: empire and judgment collapse into one word. The temple vessels are *qodesh qodashim*, the most heavily marked category of holiness in Israel's sacrificial vocabulary; their misuse here is what the prophets called *me'al*, sacrilegious appropriation, the same vocabulary used of Achan in Joshua 7. The "Most High God," *El Elyon*, is the term Daniel chooses for his addresses to gentile kings throughout the Aramaic block — a deliberately translatable name that refuses both pagan-pantheon assimilation and Hebrew-only particularism.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is a court tale of judgment, the dark sibling of the court tale of vindication. Genre conventions are tight: a royal feast, a portent, the wise men who fail, the foreign sage who succeeds, and a reward whose terms are then reversed by external history. The pattern parallels Joseph before Pharaoh (Genesis 41), Daniel himself before Nebuchadnezzar in chapters 2 and 4, Esther's confrontation at the second feast (Esther 7), and reaches forward to Mark's account of Herod Antipas's birthday banquet (Mark 6) and the death of Herod Agrippa I in Acts 12. **W. Lee Humphreys**, in his 1973 *Journal of Biblical Literature* essay "A Lifestyle for Diaspora," named this the "tale of the courtier" subgenre and traced its function as resistance literature in dispersion communities.

The chapter sits at the structural pivot of the Aramaic block. **Albin Lenglet**'s 1972 chiastic analysis of Daniel 2–7, accepted by Collins and most subsequent commentators, pairs chapters 2 and 7 (four-kingdoms visions), chapters 3 and 6 (miraculous deliverance from execution), and chapters 4 and 5 (humbled kings — Nebuchadnezzar restored, Belshazzar destroyed). Chapter 5 is therefore the dark counterpart of chapter 4: the king who would not learn from the king who did. The narrator marks this with verbal echoes — "your father" is repeated eight times in Daniel's speech (5:11, 13, 18), and the phrase "you knew all this" (5:22) is the pivot on which Belshazzar's fate turns. He is judged not for ignorance but for refusal of available knowledge.

The narrative deploys peripeteia in classical form. The king begins enthroned at the head of a thousand; he ends a corpse the same night. The reversal is foreshadowed by the disembodied hand, accelerated by the failure of the wise men, and made inevitable by Daniel's reading. The narrator's pacing is unusually quick for Daniel — most of the chapter is dialogue, with only the writing-on-the-wall vision and the closing notice of Belshazzar's death given as omniscient narration. The royal-decree form that closes chapters 3, 4, and 6 is conspicuously absent here; there is no theological proclamation from Belshazzar, only his death.

Canonical placement is significant. Daniel sits in the *Ketuvim* (Writings) of the Hebrew Bible, between Esther and Ezra-Nehemiah, where its function is post-exilic theology of survival. In Christian Bibles since the Septuagint it has been moved into the prophets, after Ezekiel, where its function is reframed as forward-looking apocalyptic. The shift changes how the reader inhabits chapter 5: as Wisdom literature about how the diaspora navigates pagan power, or as Prophecy about how empire ends. The textual variants are real. The Old Greek of chapter 5 includes a long preface summarizing the chapter and rearranges the sequence of Daniel's speech; Theodotion's version, which became the dominant Christian text, hews closer to the Masoretic order.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc here is the theology of empire judged at table. The fall of Babylon was the foundational political event of the post-exilic Jewish imagination — the sign that the gods of the nations are not gods, that the God of Israel rules over the rise and fall of empires, that exile is reversible. Isaiah 13–14, Isaiah 47, and Jeremiah 50–51 are the prophetic expectation; Daniel 5 is the narrative realization. **Jeremiah 51:39** — "I will prepare them a feast and make them drunk … so that they may sleep a perpetual sleep and not wake" — sits behind the chapter as a likely literary source. The four-kingdoms schema that orders Daniel as a whole begins to land here: Babylon's end is the first transition, with Persia explicitly named.

The Jewish trajectory of the chapter is rich. **The Prayer of Nabonidus (4QPrNab)** preserves an alternative tradition that bears on the figure of Belshazzar's father. Josephus, *Antiquities* 10.11, paraphrases Daniel 5 and supplements it with **Berossus**, the Babylonian priest-historian preserved in Josephus's *Against Apion* — Josephus identifies Belshazzar with Nabonidus's son Naboandelos, which is the closest ancient source to recognizing the dynastic fact while preserving the narrative. **1 Maccabees 2:59–60** invokes Daniel and his three friends as paradigms of resistance under Antiochus, but does not cite chapter 5 directly. The Babylonian Talmud, *Sanhedrin* 92b–94a, treats Belshazzar's punishment at length and reads the chapter as a parable of imperial pride; *Megillah* 11b discusses the timing of his death; *Yoma* 77a uses the chapter to discuss the angelic role in judgment. **Saadia Gaon**'s tenth-century commentary, **Rashi**, **Ibn Ezra**, and especially **Don Isaac Abrabanel**'s extensive fifteenth-century commentary all read the chapter as a paradigm of imperial sacrilege judged. Modern Jewish scholarship — **Robert Gordis**, **Aaron Koller** (*Esther in Ancient Jewish Thought*, 2014, with comparative attention to Daniel) — emphasizes the diaspora-resistance reading. **Daniel Boyarin**, while focused on chapter 7, situates the chapter within a tradition of Jewish counter-imperial theology that does not require borrowed Christian categories.

The Christian trajectory begins with **Hippolytus of Rome's *Commentary on Daniel*** (c. 200 AD), the earliest extant Christian biblical commentary, which reads Belshazzar as a type of the Antichrist desecrating the holy. **Jerome's *Commentary on Daniel*** (407 AD), composed explicitly to refute Porphyry, reads the chapter both historically (as the fall of Babylon to Cyrus) and typologically (as the church's vindication over pagan power). **Theodoret of Cyrus** in the fifth century follows Jerome. The medieval reception — **Nicholas of Lyra**, **Aquinas** in his *Commentary on Daniel*, **Joachim of Fiore**'s apocalyptic schema — keeps the chapter in the rotation of imperial-judgment texts. **Luther's *Preface to Daniel*** (1530) reads the chapter through the four-monarchies frame and uses Belshazzar's banquet as a figure of papal sacrilege. **Calvin's *Lectures on Daniel*** (1561, two volumes) is exegetically careful, defends the historical reliability of the chapter against early-modern critics, and reads the writing on the wall as a paradigm of God's judgment on civil powers that abuse sacred things.

The critical trajectory begins with Porphyry, runs through **Bertholdt** (1806), **S. R. Driver**'s *Introduction to the Literature of the Old Testament* (1891), **R. H. Charles**'s *Critical and Exegetical Commentary on Daniel* (1929), **James Montgomery**'s *International Critical Commentary* (1927), **H. H. Rowley**'s *Darius the Mede and the Four World Empires in the Book of Daniel* (1935), and into the contemporary consensus represented by Collins, Newsom, **Maurice Casey** (*Son of Man*, 1979), **Philip Davies** (*Daniel*, 1985), and Grabbe. The contemporary critical reading treats chapter 5 as a court tale of probably late Persian or early Hellenistic origin, redacted into the Maccabean book, with the historical inaccuracies as evidence for the redactor's distance from sixth-century events.

Where the trajectories diverge is on the historical-error problem. The conservative reading (Wiseman, Miller, Baldwin, Archer) treats the genealogical "son" as conventional accession language and reads the chapter as substantially historical. The critical reading treats the inaccuracy as one piece of converging evidence — alongside the linguistic Aramaic of the Persian-and-later period, the Persian and Greek loanwords, the apparent unfamiliarity with sixth-century Babylonian administration, and the precision of the Antiochene prophecies in chapters 7–11 — for a Maccabean composition.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The first tension is between prophetic and apocalyptic theologies of judgment. The prophetic tradition (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Amos) reads judgment as the consequence of covenant breach, mediated through historical agents and reversible by repentance. The apocalyptic tradition (Daniel, parts of Zechariah, the Enochic literature) reads judgment as a pre-determined cosmic-political transition in which God's verdict has already been written and human agents only execute it. Chapter 5 sits between the two. Belshazzar is offered the precedent of his "father" Nebuchadnezzar's repentance (5:18–22) — the prophetic possibility — but the writing has already been inscribed (5:24). **Walter Brueggemann**'s reading of the prophetic tradition (*Theology of the Old Testament*, 1997) emphasizes the open future under Yahweh's judgment; Collins's reading of the apocalyptic tradition (*The Apocalyptic Imagination*, 1984) emphasizes the closed determinism. The Catholic tradition has historically held both in tension through the doctrine of conditional prophecy (the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §2823 and Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* II-II q. 174 a. 2 on prophecy as foreknowledge that may be revoked); the Reformed tradition under Calvin emphasizes the determinism without softening it; the Eastern Orthodox tradition reads the chapter through the lens of *energeia* — divine action expressed in created time — and refuses the dichotomy. Jewish readings, from the Talmud through Abrabanel, tend to preserve the prophetic-conditional reading: Belshazzar could have repented and did not. The critical reading is uninterested in the dichotomy and reads the chapter as Maccabean polemic projected into the past.

Steelman the determinist reading. The text gives Belshazzar no scene of decision. The hand writes before any speech of his is reported; Daniel's interpretation is a post-hoc explanation, not a sermon calling for repentance. The reward Belshazzar offers Daniel is fulfilled (5:29) — and is meaningless, because Belshazzar dies the same night (5:30). The narrator stages a world in which the human response to divine judgment is theatrical irrelevance. This is theologically discomfiting in a way the conditional-prophecy reading smooths over too quickly. Where this leaves us: chapter 5 is a hard text for any tradition that wants the future open all the way down; it forces a distinction between the open future of Jeremiah and the sealed future of apocalyptic.

The second tension is the historical-error problem and what it does to the doctrine of Scripture. If "son of Nebuchadnezzar" is genealogically wrong, what is the cost? The Catholic position, articulated in *Dei Verbum* §11 (Vatican II, 1965), holds that Scripture teaches "without error" the truth God willed for the sake of salvation, leaving room for incidental historical compression. The Eastern Orthodox tradition, expressed by **Father John Behr** and **Andrew Louth**, similarly distinguishes the salvific intent of the text from incidental historical detail. Protestant traditions split. The conservative evangelical position, represented by Miller, Archer, and the Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy (1978), treats the genealogy as accurate under the convention that *bar* / *ben* designates a successor or descendant, and treats any other reading as a concession that erodes the doctrine. The mainline Protestant position, represented by **Brevard Childs**'s canonical approach (*Introduction to the Old Testament as Scripture*, 1979) and the Yale school, treats the discrepancy as canonically incorporated and theologically inert — what matters is the function of Belshazzar in the canonical narrative, not his bloodline. Jewish readings have never required strict genealogical inerrancy; the rabbinic tradition is comfortable with literary economy. Critical scholarship is uninterested in the doctrinal stakes. Steelman Wiseman's reading. *Bar* in royal Aramaic does cover successor relationships in attested texts; the Assyrian and Babylonian royal annals routinely call later kings "son" of much earlier dynastic founders. Belshazzar's mother as a daughter of Nebuchadnezzar would make him a grandson by blood — and the queen-figure of 5:10–12 is plausibly that mother, with first-hand knowledge of Daniel's role under Nebuchadnezzar. The reconciliation is not absurd. Where this leaves us: the chapter forces a decision about what kind of accuracy a sacred text owes its reader, and the answer is not obvious from the text alone.

The unresolved question. The hand writes; the wise men fail; Daniel reads. But who is the writing for? Belshazzar dies before he can repent, the Babylonians are conquered before they can mourn, and the kingdom passes to a Darius the Mede who is barely identifiable in the historical record. The chapter ends without anyone — except the reader — having understood what happened. Why does God write at a banquet that ends in conquest, when the writing changes nothing in the night? Unless the writing was always for us.

## The Thread

The non-obvious cross-reference is **Esther 1**, where Ahasuerus's six-month banquet at Susa is the dark inversion of Belshazzar's. Both chapters open with imperial feasts that establish a king's pride; both turn on the misuse or display of objects taken from the conquered (Persian banquet vessels in Esther; Jerusalem temple vessels in Daniel); both move from feast to crisis through a textual artifact (the writing on the wall; the irrevocable Persian decree). Esther 1 is the comedy of pride that recovers; Daniel 5 is the tragedy of pride that does not. Read together, they map the diaspora's doubled-vision of imperial banquets — the place where Jews are at greatest risk and where God acts most decisively.

The second is **Mark 14:22–25**, the institution of the Lord's Supper, which is the deliberate counter-banquet to Daniel 5. Where Belshazzar drinks wine from temple vessels and dies, Jesus pours wine and calls it the blood of a new covenant for the forgiveness of sins. Where Belshazzar's banquet ends with a hand writing judgment, the Last Supper ends with Jesus blessing the cup that becomes the cup of his own judgment borne for others. The intertext is structural, not verbal — Mark's audience would have heard the resonance.

The third is **Revelation 18**, the song over fallen Babylon, which is Daniel 5 universalized into apocalyptic. John's Babylon is "the great city" — Rome explicitly, every empire by extension — and her fall, like Belshazzar's, comes "in one hour" (Rev 18:10, 17, 19). The temple vessels of Daniel 5 become, in Revelation, the cup of Babylon's fornication that her kings have drunk; the writing on the wall becomes the angel's cry that Babylon has fallen. The chapter is the seed of one of the most enduring apocalyptic images in the Christian tradition.

The fourth is **Hesiod's *Works and Days*** and the Persian *Bahman Yasht*, both of which preserve the four-ages or four-kingdoms schema in non-Israelite traditions. Daniel did not invent the schema; he received it from a wider Near Eastern apocalyptic stream and turned it on the empires that produced it. The writing on the wall is the moment when that schema becomes, for the first time, a verdict.
