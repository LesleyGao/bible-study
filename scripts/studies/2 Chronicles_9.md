# 2 Chronicles 9 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The Chronicler — anonymous, writing in Persian-period Yehud most plausibly between the late fifth and mid-fourth centuries BC — closes the Solomon narrative in chapter 9 with the visit of the queen of Sheba, a tableau of imperial wealth, and a brief death notice. Sara Japhet (*I & II Chronicles*, OTL, 1993) treats this chapter as the structural climax of the Chronicler's idealized Solomon: the wisdom that God promised in 1:7–13 is now publicly recognized by a foreign monarch, and the wealth that accompanied that wisdom is on full display. Ralph Klein (*2 Chronicles*, Hermeneia, 2012) emphasizes that the chapter functions as the *final flourish* of the Solomonic idealization — the very last thing the reader sees of Solomon is glory, not collapse. Hugh Williamson (*1 and 2 Chronicles*, NCBC, 1982) and Gary Knoppers (in his Solomon essays and the AB volumes) lay out the parallel material in 1 Kings 10–11 and document what is preserved, what is rearranged, and — most importantly — what is omitted. Steven McKenzie (*1–2 Chronicles*, AOTC, 2004), Mark Boda (*1–2 Chronicles*, CBC, 2010), and Louis Jonker (*Defining All-Israel in Chronicles*, 2016) read the chapter as Persian-period wisdom-and-empire theology: a Davidic king ruling justly draws the nations to himself, and his death is honored, not judged.

The Hebrew vocabulary illuminates the scene. The visiting monarch is מַלְכַּת שְׁבָא (*malkat sheva*, "queen of Sheba"). Sheba is identified by most modern scholarship with the kingdom of Saba in southern Arabia (modern Yemen), a major incense-trade polity in the early first millennium BC; Kenneth Kitchen, Stuart Munro-Hay, and others have laid out the archaeological case. The Ethiopian tradition will also identify Sheba with Aksum (Ethiopia), and the controversy over Sheba's geography is itself a piece of reception history. The "hard questions" with which she tests Solomon are חִידוֹת (*ḥidot*), riddles — the same word used of Samson's wedding riddle (Judg 14) and of the proverbs of the wise (Prov 1:6). The Chronicler is staging a wisdom contest in the form recognizable across the ancient Near East. The queen's astonished phrase "there was no more breath in her" is לֹא־הָיָה בָהּ עוֹד רוּחַ (*lo-haya bah od ruaḥ*) — a Hebraism for being utterly overwhelmed. Her doxology in v. 8 is striking: "Blessed be Yahweh your God, who has delighted in you and set you on *his* throne as king for Yahweh your God." The phrase עַל־כִּסְאוֹ (*al-kis'o*, "on his throne") is theologically dense: the throne is Yahweh's, and Solomon sits on it as Yahweh's regent. This is a Chronicler-distinctive theology of kingship; the parallel in 1 Kgs 10:9 has the queen bless Yahweh for setting Solomon "on the throne of Israel," not on Yahweh's own throne. The Chronicler has elevated the throne to a divine seat (compare 1 Chr 28:5; 29:23).

A direct comparison with 1 Kings 10:1–11:43 is essential. The Chronicler preserves the queen's visit (with minor changes), the wealth catalogue, the throne description, the trade with Tarshish and Ophir, and the death notice. He omits — *entirely* — 1 Kings 11:1–13, the long passage in which Solomon takes seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines, his foreign wives turn his heart from Yahweh, he builds high places for Chemosh and Molech on the Mount of Olives, and Yahweh announces that the kingdom will be torn from his son. He also omits 1 Kings 11:14–40, the rise of three adversaries (Hadad the Edomite, Rezon of Damascus, and Jeroboam son of Nebat), and the prophetic word of Ahijah of Shiloh announcing the kingdom's division — though, tellingly, he keeps a *reference* to "the prophecy of Ahijah the Shilonite" in v. 29. The Chronicler knew the Kings material; he chose not to narrate it. His Solomon dies in glory; Kings' Solomon dies under judgment.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter divides into four panels. Verses 1–12 are the *queen-of-Sheba scene*, a fully developed narrative with travel, dialogue, doxology, and gift exchange — the only fully developed narrative episode in the Chronicler's late-Solomon material. Verses 13–21 are the *wealth catalogue*: 666 talents of gold per year, 200 large shields, 300 small shields, the great ivory throne, the gold drinking vessels, and the triennial Tarshish fleet. Verses 22–28 are the *imperial summary*: Solomon excels all kings of the earth, the kings of the earth seek his wisdom, and tribute flows in. Verses 29–31 are the *death notice* — Solomon reigned forty years, slept with his fathers, was buried in the city of David, and was succeeded by Rehoboam.

Hebrew literary features include hyperbolic accumulation (the queen's "very great retinue," "very much gold," "no more breath in her"), rhetorical doublet ("Happy are your men! Happy are these your servants!"), and inventory rhetoric (gold, silver, ivory, apes, peacocks). The 666 talents of gold per year (v. 13) sits in canonical resonance with Deut 17:17, where the king is forbidden to "multiply silver and gold for himself" — a connection many readers, ancient and modern, have heard, and which Christian readers since at least Irenaeus have also heard echoed in Rev 13:18. The Chronicler does not flag the resonance, but the canonical reader cannot avoid it.

The throne description in vv. 17–19 is one of the chapter's most vivid set-pieces: ivory overlaid with gold, six steps, twelve lions on the steps, two lions beside the armrests, "nothing like it was ever made for any kingdom." Numerologically, twelve lions on the steps corresponds to the twelve tribes; the Chronicler is staging the throne as a microcosm of all Israel under Davidic kingship.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the Hebrew canon, where 2 Chronicles is the *last book* of the Tanakh, the queen of Sheba's visit and Solomon's death sit near the canonical center of the Chronicler's idealized monarchy narrative. Her doxology — "Blessed be Yahweh your God" — gives the Chronicler what he wants: foreign-monarch acknowledgment that Israel's God is the source of Israel's king's wisdom and rule.

The reception of the queen of Sheba is one of the richest in all of Scripture. **Jewish reception** in the Talmud (b. Bava Batra 15b) discusses whether the *queen of Sheba* is a reference to a literal monarch or to a kingdom; some rabbis prefer the latter to avoid suggesting Solomon entertained a foreign queen. The *Targum Sheni to Esther* contains an extensive legendary expansion in which the queen is named, riddles are exchanged, and Solomon's hoopoe bird is the messenger. Rashi (eleventh century) and Radak (twelfth–thirteenth centuries) treat the historical reading straightforwardly. Abarbanel (fifteenth century) reads her doxology as evidence of Solomon's universal wisdom. Sara Japhet, Isaac Kalimi (*The Reshaping of Ancient Israelite History in Chronicles*, 2005), and other modern Jewish scholars have placed the visit firmly in the Chronicler's wisdom-and-kingship theology.

**Christian reception** has been even richer. Jerome treats her as a type of the church drawn to Christ from the ends of the earth. Origen and other patristic writers read the visit allegorically: the queen, a Gentile, comes to Solomon, the wisdom-king, prefiguring the Gentile church coming to Christ. Cassiodorus continues this typology. Aquinas (*Summa* III, q. 36, a. 1) cites the visit as a type of Gentile inclusion. Calvin's 1561 commentary reads the queen's doxology as evidence of natural revelation drawing the nations to Israel's God. Modern critical scholarship (Williamson, Japhet, Klein, Knoppers, Boda, McKenzie, Jonker) has handled the visit historically while preserving its theological resonance.

**Ethiopian Orthodox reception** has produced one of the most distinctive traditions in world Christianity. The fourteenth-century *Kebra Nagast* ("Glory of Kings") narrates that the queen of Sheba was Makeda, queen of Aksum, and that she bore Solomon a son named Menelik I, who became the founder of the Ethiopian Solomonic dynasty and brought the Ark of the Covenant from Jerusalem to Aksum. The *Kebra Nagast* is not Scripture for the Ethiopian Orthodox Church but is a foundational text of national-religious identity, and the Solomonic dynasty (last reigning monarch: Haile Selassie, deposed 1974) traced its legitimacy to this account. The tradition is obviously legendary in its later elaborations, but the historical kernel of trade and diplomatic contact between southern Arabia / the Horn of Africa and Solomon's Israel is plausible and is taken seriously by mainstream archaeology.

**Islamic reception** treats the queen as Bilqis. The Qur'an (Surat al-Naml 27:22–44) narrates the visit at length, with the hoopoe bird as Solomon's messenger, the testing with the throne, and Bilqis' submission to the God of Solomon. Bilqis is not a sinner figure in Islamic tradition; she is a wisdom-figure who recognizes the truth and submits.

**African Christian reception** beyond Ethiopia has often elevated the queen as an emblem of African presence in the biblical narrative — a corrective to centuries of European Christian art that depicted her as European. The recovery of the queen as African is a real piece of contemporary biblical theology in African and African-diasporic churches.

The most important **New Testament reception** is the saying of Jesus in Matt 12:42 / Luke 11:31: "The queen of the South will rise up at the judgment with this generation and condemn it, for she came from the ends of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon, and behold, something greater than Solomon is here." Jesus places himself in the position of one greater than Solomon, and the queen — a Gentile, a woman, a foreigner — becomes a witness against an unrepentant generation. The trajectory the Christian tradition has read is: a Gentile queen recognized Solomon's wisdom; how much more should Israel and the nations now recognize Christ.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

The Chronicler's agenda in this chapter pivots on one massive editorial decision: he ends the Solomon narrative on the queen-of-Sheba peak, the wealth catalogue, and a peaceful death notice. He omits Solomon's apostasy, foreign wives, idolatry, and prophetic judgment. The shape of his theological vision is therefore: David and Solomon are the founders of an idealized covenant monarchy, and the templates they lay down are the templates for Israel's life forever.

The first tension worth steelmanning is the **Solomon-apostasy omission**. Three positions are live in serious scholarship.

*Position one — Chronicler theological retouching as historiographic deception.* The most pointed critical position holds that the Chronicler has deliberately suppressed material that would damage his idealized portrait. 1 Kings 11 is not a minor detail; it is the theological hinge on which the rest of Kings turns (the kingdom is divided *because* of Solomon's sin), and to remove it is to misrepresent the canonical record. This position is held by some critical scholars who treat Chronicles as ideologically driven historiography and treat the omissions as falsifications.

*Position two — Chronicler theological retouching as legitimate alternative historiography.* Sara Japhet, Hugh Williamson, Mark Boda, and Gary Knoppers have argued that the Chronicler is doing theology-through-historiography, not falsifying. Ancient historiography did not require comprehensive coverage; it required theological focus. The Chronicler's focus is the unconditional aspect of the Davidic covenant and the temple as the ongoing site of presence; Kings' focus is the conditional aspect and prophetic judgment. Both are canonical; both are needed. The Chronicler can assume his readers know Kings (he refers them to "the prophecy of Ahijah the Shilonite" in v. 29, knowing they will recognize the source of the missing apostasy material). His silence is not denial; it is selection.

*Position three — Catholic/Orthodox comfort with theological historiography.* The Catholic and Eastern Orthodox traditions have generally been more comfortable than mainstream Protestant evangelicalism with the recognition that Scripture contains different theological retellings of the same events. The Chronicler's idealized portrait is read as a *theological icon* of David and Solomon, which is to say: an icon makes theological claims about its subject that strict historical photography would not make, and the icon is theologically true even where it is historically selective. The Catholic tradition has long read the four Gospels this way, and applies the same hermeneutic to Chronicles vs. Kings. This position avoids the embarrassment-management of position one and the apologetic strain that sometimes accompanies position two.

The mainstream-evangelical reckoning with this omission has often been awkward. Evangelical commentators have tried to argue that the Chronicler "assumes" Solomon's apostasy because he knew his readers knew Kings; while this is plausible, it does not relieve the question of why he chose to leave the apostasy out of his own narrative. The more honest evangelical positions (recently Russell Moore, John Goldingay, Mark Boda) acknowledge the editorial decision and try to understand it theologically rather than explain it away.

The second tension is the **666 talents of gold**. Verse 13 reports that Solomon's annual gold income was 666 talents. Deuteronomy 17:17 forbids the king to multiply silver and gold. Many ancient and modern readers have heard a deliberate canonical signal: Solomon, even in his apex, is in violation of the kingship law. The Chronicler does not narrate this tension, but his preservation of the number — when he could have rounded or omitted it — leaves the canonical resonance in place. Christian readers since Irenaeus have also heard the number echoing in Rev 13:18 ("the number of the beast is 666"); whether the Apocalypse intends the Solomonic resonance is debated, but the canonical pattern of empire-corrupting-by-wealth is real. The Chronicler's idealized portrait, in other words, is not as airbrushed as a casual reading suggests; the very number he preserves carries judgment in its hand.

Where this leaves us: chapter 9 is the closing tableau of the Chronicler's idealized Solomon, and it is also a chapter that has produced one of the most generative reception histories in Scripture — Ethiopian Orthodox, Islamic, African Christian, patristic Christian, rabbinic Jewish, and modern critical readings all converge on this strange visit. The queen of the South came; she saw; she blessed Yahweh; she went home. Jesus tells us she will rise at the judgment.

The closing question worth sitting with: what does it mean that the foreigner blesses Yahweh more clearly than the Chronicler's own narrator does — and what does the Christian tradition's claim that "something greater than Solomon is here" ask of us?
