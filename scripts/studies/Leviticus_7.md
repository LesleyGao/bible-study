# Leviticus 7: Fat, Blood, Wave, and Heave

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Leviticus, *Wayyiqrāʾ* (ויקרא) in Hebrew — named from its opening word, "and he called" — continues directly from Exodus 40's account of the glory-cloud taking up residence in the newly-completed tabernacle. Chapter 7 stands at the close of the priest's manual that began at 6:8, and its final two verses (vv. 37–38) explicitly round off the whole sacrificial block that has run from chapter 1: "This is the law of the burnt offering, of the grain offering, of the sin offering, of the guilt offering, of the ordination offering, and of the peace offering, which the LORD commanded Moses on Mount Sinai." Chapter 7 therefore functions as the colophon to Leviticus 1–7 as a whole, the moment before the narrative of chapters 8–10 picks up the ordination that chapter 7:37 has just named (the *millûʾîm*, "ordination offering," otherwise unmentioned in Leviticus 1–7 but paradigmatic for what is about to happen in chapter 8).

The dating debate at the book level runs from Wellhausen's *Prolegomena* (1883), which placed the entire Priestly source in the post-exilic period, through Yehezkel Kaufmann's mid-twentieth-century counter-argument for the antiquity of P, to Haran's and Milgrom's pre-exilic core and Knohl's PT/H distinction. Chapter 7 is one of the texts Knohl assigns largely to PT, in part because the priestly-portion regulations (vv. 32–36) assume a functioning sanctuary economy in which priests are dependent on sacrificial dues rather than on a centralized temple treasury — an arrangement that fits early Iron Age Israelite shrine economics better than the centralized Second-Temple establishment.

The material culture of chapter 7 is strikingly concrete. Sacrificial fat (*ḥēleb*, חֵלֶב) is specified with anatomical precision: the fat tail (*ʾalyâ*, אַלְיָה) of the fat-tailed sheep (a breed attested archaeologically in the Iron Age Levant by faunal remains at Lachish and Tel Miqne), the fat covering the entrails, the two kidneys and the fat upon them, and the long lobe of the liver (*yôteret hakkābēd*, יֹתֶרֶת הַכָּבֵד). This liver-lobe is the same organ Mesopotamian diviners inspected in extispicy (compare the Ras Shamra liver models and the Assyrian *bārû* texts); the prohibition on eating it, and the command to burn it, may reflect a polemical repurposing of the primary Mesopotamian divination organ into a sacrificial-portion that belongs only to Yahweh. Milgrom (AB *Leviticus 1–16*, 1991, 204–218) argues this is not mere parallel but pointed counter-appropriation. The breast (*ḥāzeh*, חָזֶה) and the right thigh (*šôq hayyāmîn*, שׁוֹק הַיָּמִין) are named as priestly portions, matching the distribution patterns attested in the Punic sacrificial tariffs from Marseille and Carthage (KAI 69, 74), the Ugaritic ritual texts (KTU 1.39, 1.41), and Hittite *Instructions for Temple Officials* (CTH 264), where cultic functionaries likewise receive specified cuts of the sacrificial animal. Leviticus 7 participates in a broader Near Eastern temple-economy pattern while inflecting it with its distinctively Yahwistic fat-and-blood prohibitions.

Archaeologically, the four-horned altar at Tel Arad and the sanctuary complex at Tel Beersheba both show installation features — channels for blood, ash receptacles, and storage rooms — consistent with the operations chapter 7 assumes. Manuscript evidence includes 4QLev-Num fragments that preserve portions of Leviticus 7 with only minor orthographic variation from MT; LXX renders the technical vocabulary with Greek cultic terms that would shape patristic reading (*sōtēriou* for *šĕlāmîm*); the Samaritan Pentateuch and the Peshitta substantially agree with MT.

Five Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. *ʾāšām* (אָשָׁם, "guilt offering," v. 1) is the reparation offering for desacralizing or misappropriating what belongs to Yahweh. *Šĕlāmîm* (שְׁלָמִים, "peace offerings," v. 11) is the shared-meal offering (variously translated "well-being," "communion," "peace"), divided three ways: thanksgiving (*tôdâ*, תּוֹדָה, v. 12), vow (*nēder*, נֵדֶר, v. 16), and freewill (*nĕdābâ*, נְדָבָה, v. 16). *Piggûl* (פִּגּוּל, "tainted," v. 18) names the offering whose sanctity has been violated by being eaten past its permitted window — a term that reappears in Leviticus 19:7, Isaiah 65:4, and Ezekiel 4:14, becoming a near-technical designation for cultically-voided holy food. *Tĕnûpâ* (תְּנוּפָה, "wave offering," v. 30) and *tĕrûmâ* (תְּרוּמָה, "contribution/heave offering," v. 32) name two modes of priestly-portion designation that Milgrom has argued should be understood as *elevation* offerings — lifting the portion toward Yahweh as a gesture of presentation, not as the swinging-motion of older translations.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Leviticus 7 is priestly *tôrâ* — instruction to Aaron and his sons on the back-end mechanics of the sacrificial system — and the chapter shows the distinctive formal markers of its genre. The "this is the law of..." formula (*zōʾt tôrat*, זֹאת תּוֹרַת) introduces each unit: the guilt offering (v. 1), the peace offerings (v. 11), and the whole-system colophon (v. 37). The triple colophon of v. 37, naming all the offerings together, is both literary summation and inclusio: it closes the sacrificial block that opened in Lev 1:2 with Yahweh's first address "when any of you brings an offering to the LORD." Wenham (NICOT, 1979) and Milgrom both note that the colophon's inclusion of *millûʾîm* — not treated in the preceding chapters but central to chapter 8 — functions as a literary hinge pointing forward to the ordination narrative.

Literary devices include the graded time-limit on peace-offering meat (vv. 15–18): *tôdâ* must be eaten the same day; *nēder* and *nĕdābâ* may be eaten for two days; anything remaining on the third day is *piggûl* and must be burned. Mary Douglas (*Leviticus as Literature*, 1999) reads this temporal gradation as structurally parallel to the spatial gradation of holiness: the more intense the sanctity (thanksgiving is offered at the moment of specific divine deliverance), the shorter the window of permitted consumption — holiness is not only spatial but temporal. The leavened-bread exception at v. 13 is a telling literary anomaly. Leviticus 2:11 forbids leaven in grain offerings; Lev 7:13 requires *leavened* loaves (*leḥem ḥāmēṣ*, לֶחֶם חָמֵץ) as part of the thanksgiving offering. Some of these loaves go to Yahweh via the priest's share, not to the altar fire. Milgrom and Baruch Levine (JPS *Leviticus*, 1989) take this as one of the chapter's most revealing literary seams: the anomaly suggests an older thanksgiving-offering tradition in which leaven had a legitimate place, later harmonized with the broader anti-leaven cultic principle by confining the leavened loaves to priestly consumption rather than altar-burning.

Another literary feature is the chapter's rhetorical placement of the fat-and-blood prohibitions (vv. 22–27) between the peace-offering rules and the wave-and-heave priestly portions. Why this placement? Sklar (TOTC, 2014) suggests the prohibitions sit here because peace offerings are the *only* offerings from which worshippers — not just priests — eat the meat, and therefore the only occasion on which lay Israelites handle sacrificial flesh directly; the fat-and-blood prohibition is addressed to them at the moment it becomes practically relevant. The chapter's closing colophon (vv. 37–38) mentioning "Mount Sinai" and "the wilderness of Sinai" is the only time in Leviticus 1–7 that the Sinai setting is made explicit; this is a literary tethering device linking the priestly torah back to the mountain of revelation.

Textual variants in chapter 7 are minor but worth noting. LXX at v. 15 renders *yôm qorbānô* as "the day of his offering" with Greek eucharistic overtones that medieval Christian exegetes would exploit. 11QpaleoLev preserves portions of Lev 7 in archaizing paleo-Hebrew script, testifying to the chapter's textual stability into the Hellenistic period. SP and Peshitta concur substantively.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Philo (*Special Laws* 1.212–223) reads the peace offering allegorically as the offering of "complete" worship, its three subtypes corresponding to three species of gratitude. Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.9.3–4) describes the priestly portions with antiquarian care. The Temple Scroll (11Q19, cols. 19–23) expands the peace-offering regulations with additional ritual detail, harmonizing Leviticus 7 with Numbers 28–29's calendrical sacrifices for an idealized future Jerusalem sanctuary.

Rabbinic literature works the chapter intensively. Mishnah *Zevachim* (especially chapters 5–6, 10) codifies the priestly portions and the slaughter-locations for each offering type; Mishnah *Menachot* treats the grain-and-loaves requirements of the thanksgiving sacrifice; Bavli *Zevachim* 36a–b develops the *piggûl* rules at length, working out precisely at what moment of wrong intention a sacrifice becomes invalidated. *Sifra* on Leviticus 7 handles the leavened-bread exception by noting that the leavened loaves go to the priest, not to the altar — preserving the "no leaven on the altar" principle while allowing leaven in the priestly portion. Rashi on v. 13 explains that the thanksgiving offering has leavened loaves because "the person giving thanks has much to tell" and the additional bread signifies abundant speech before Yahweh; Rashi on v. 12 distinguishes the four kinds of deliverance that obligate a thanksgiving offering — crossing the sea, crossing the desert, recovering from illness, being released from prison — a list Bavli *Berakhot* 54b preserves and that remains the basis for the contemporary *Birkat HaGomel* blessing in Jewish liturgy.

Ibn Ezra gives plain-sense commentary on the priestly portions. Ramban reads the fat-and-blood prohibition as both dietary and theological: fat is the seat of the soul's appetite, blood is the seat of its life, and both belong to Yahweh alone. Maimonides (*Hilchot Maʿaseh HaKorbanot* 9–13) systematizes the offerings into a complete halakhic code, distinguishing most-holy offerings (eaten only by priests, only in the sanctuary court) from lesser-holy offerings (peace offerings, which lay Israelites may eat throughout Jerusalem). Sforno and Bahya add moral-spiritual commentary on the thanksgiving offering as the archetype of covenantal gratitude.

Modern Jewish scholarship — Milgrom (AB), Knohl (*Sanctuary of Silence*), Baruch Levine (JPS), Sarna, Leibowitz (*Studies in Leviticus*), Sacks (*Covenant and Conversation: Leviticus*), and Zornberg (*The Hidden Order of Intimacy*) — has recovered the chapter's historical and literary complexity. Sacks in particular has developed the thanksgiving-offering motif as the paradigmatic Jewish posture before God, anchoring it in chapter 7.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic readers approach chapter 7 typologically. Origen (*Homilies on Leviticus* 5) reads the fat-and-blood prohibition as the prohibition against carnal desire and carnal self-assertion; the priestly wave and heave offerings become figures of Christ's self-elevation toward the Father. Cyril of Alexandria (*Glaphyra on Leviticus*) reads the thanksgiving offering with its leavened bread as the eucharistic thanksgiving of the church, in which Christ's body — leavened, risen — is offered as the fulfillment of the Levitical *tôdâ*. Augustine (*Questions on the Heptateuch*, on Lev) treats the blood prohibition as preserving the sacred sign-character of blood for the coming atonement in Christ. Theodoret offers plain-sense commentary alongside typology.

Medieval Latin readers condensed this tradition. Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* III q. 65 on priestly character, and *ST* I-II q. 102 on the ceremonial precepts of the Old Law) draws on Leviticus 7 to distinguish the priest's portion from the layperson's portion as a type of the Church's sacramental economy. The thanksgiving offering with its leavened bread receives particular attention: Joseph Ratzinger (*The Spirit of the Liturgy*, 2000) has argued that the Jewish tradition — preserved in rabbinic sources such as Leviticus Rabbah 9:7 — that *only the tôdâ* among the offerings would continue in the messianic age, and that *only* the leavened-bread offering would survive, is the deep structural background for the Christian Eucharist. The Eucharist as *eucharistia* (thanksgiving) is the messianic-age continuation of the Levitical *tôdâ*, and the Christian use of leavened bread (retained in Eastern churches, debated in the medieval West) continues the structural logic of Leviticus 7:13. Whether one credits the tradition Ratzinger cites or not, the typological pull of chapter 7 toward eucharistic theology is hard to miss.

Reformation readers divided. Luther on Leviticus treats the chapter as type fulfilled in Christ, with particular emphasis on the thanksgiving offering as figure of the believer's gratitude. Calvin's *Commentary on the Last Four Books of Moses* on Lev 7 handles the priestly portions as signs of the minister's dependence on the congregation while insisting that the ceremonial typology is fulfilled and abrogated in Christ.

Critical scholarship from Wellhausen through Noth, de Vaux (*Ancient Israel*, 1961), von Rad, Childs, and Milgrom has excavated the compositional history. Contemporary theological commentary — Radner (*Leviticus*, Brazos, 2008), Morales (*Who Shall Ascend the Mountain of the Lord?*, 2015), Wenham (NICOT), Sklar, Hartley (WBC), Kiuchi, Brueggemann, and T. Desmond Alexander — has recovered the chapter's theological density. Morales reads the peace offering as the archetype of communion meal, the covenant dinner table between God and his people.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Jewish tradition treats the priestly portions of Leviticus 7 as a continuing marker of *kohen* status: in many traditional communities, *kohanim* are still given the first *ʿaliyâ* and still perform the priestly blessing, and the memory of the *tôdâ* is liturgically preserved in *Birkat HaGomel*, the blessing of deliverance recited publicly after surviving danger. Christian tradition — especially Catholic and Orthodox — has drawn the *tôdâ* into eucharistic theology, reading the leavened-bread thanksgiving offering as the type that finds its fulfillment in the Mass or Divine Liturgy. Protestant traditions have generally emphasized the universal priesthood and the believer's own thank-offering (Rom 12:1, Heb 13:15) as the continuing reality, while rejecting sacerdotal continuation. The wave-and-heave priestly portions of Lev 7:30–34 become, in each tradition, a different image: rabbinic preservation, Catholic offertory, Protestant gratitude.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Why leaven in the thanksgiving offering?** Leviticus 2:11 forbids leaven on the altar; Lev 7:13 requires leavened loaves with the thanksgiving peace offering. What does the exception mean theologically?

The Catholic position (CCC 1328–1332 on the Eucharist as *eucharistia*; Ratzinger, *The Spirit of the Liturgy*) reads the *tôdâ* with its leavened bread as the only Levitical offering whose structure persists into the messianic age, and as the structural type of the Christian Eucharist — leavened bread figures the risen, living Christ offered in thanksgiving. The Reformed position (Calvin; Owen on Hebrews) reads the leavened-loaf exception as a pedagogical accommodation of Israel's ordinary bread into the sacred economy, without reading eucharistic typology into it; Christ's thanksgiving is typologically present but not eucharistically continued in the way Catholic theology claims. The Lutheran reading (Luther on Leviticus; Augsburg Confession Art. 10 on the real presence) splits the difference: the thanksgiving-offering type supports the sacramental reality of Christ's body in the supper without requiring sacerdotal continuation of the Aaronic priesthood. The Wesleyan line (Wesley on Lev 7 and his *Treatise on the Lord's Supper*) reads the thanksgiving offering as a figure of the believer's eucharistic thanksgiving, with Wesley's own characteristic emphasis on sanctification. The Anabaptist tradition (Schleitheim; Yoder) relocates the thanksgiving meal to the common table of the gathered church, dissolving both altar and sacerdotal priesthood. The Orthodox reading (Schmemann, *For the Life of the World*) sees the leavened *tôdâ* as the archetype of the eucharistic thanksgiving in which all creation is offered back to God; Zizioulas emphasizes the communal-ecclesial character. Jewish readings (Maimonides; Leibowitz) treat the leaven as a theological statement that the thanksgiving of deliverance — unlike atonement — can include the ordinary substance of daily bread, because deliverance restores the ordinary. Critical scholarship (Milgrom, Knohl) treats the leaven as a survival of an older thanksgiving tradition later harmonized with anti-leaven altar theology by restricting the leavened portion to priestly consumption.

The least intuitive position here may be the Jewish Leibowitz-style reading: the leaven is not a concession or a survival but a theological statement in its own right. Atonement requires the removal of fermentation — the removal of the accumulating "old" that has corrupted. Thanksgiving for deliverance, by contrast, celebrates the restoration of the ordinary, which is inherently fermented, aged, leavened. The *tôdâ*'s leavened bread is not an exception to the rule but the rule's complement: a theology of deliverance that affirms the goodness of the ordinary restored, not only the ordinary cleansed. This reading makes intelligible why the *tôdâ* alone has leaven, without importing eucharistic categories.

**Where this leaves us:** the leaven of Leviticus 7:13 is a theological puzzle that forces every tradition to a decision about how to handle the relationship between the Levitical system and the continuing worship life of the faithful. Whether one reads eucharistic continuation (Catholic, Orthodox), sacramental typology (Lutheran), ethical-gratitude continuation (Reformed, Wesleyan, Anabaptist), or halakhic preservation (Jewish), the text does not let the reader treat the *tôdâ* as just another sacrifice.

**Tension 2: What do wave and heave mean?** Leviticus 7:30–34 specifies that the breast is to be "waved" (*tĕnûpâ*) and the right thigh is to be "heaved" or "contributed" (*tĕrûmâ*). Both become priestly portions. What is the distinction?

The Catholic position (drawing on Aquinas *ST* III q. 22 on Christ's priesthood, and on older Catholic biblical commentators) reads *tĕnûpâ* as lateral motion toward the four quarters (asserting Yahweh's dominion over all space) and *tĕrûmâ* as vertical elevation (asserting Yahweh's heavenly authority); both are priestly acts that figure Christ's cosmic lordship. The Reformed position (Calvin, *Commentary on Leviticus*) treats both terms as modes of presentation to Yahweh that designate priestly portions, without attributing specific symbolic meaning to the motion; Calvin is cautious about speculative elaboration. The Lutheran tradition generally follows Calvin here. The Wesleyan line treats them as figures of the believer's whole-life offering. The Anabaptist reading sees the whole wave-and-heave machinery as irrelevant to the post-Levitical community. The Orthodox reading (Schmemann) preserves the liturgical-cosmic meaning: the waved breast and heaved thigh are liturgical gestures that gather creation and lift it to God. Jewish readings — Maimonides (*Hilchot Maʿaseh HaKorbanot* 12) and Sforno — preserve the older rabbinic understanding of *tĕnûpâ* as horizontal motion and *tĕrûmâ* as vertical motion. Milgrom has reshaped the modern conversation by arguing, on the basis of comparative Near Eastern evidence (Akkadian *našûm* ana eli*, "to lift up before"), that both terms denote *elevation* — presentation-by-lifting — rather than one being horizontal and one being vertical. The rabbinic distinction, on Milgrom's reading, is a later Masoretic-era systematization. Baruch Levine agrees.

The least intuitive position is probably Milgrom's. It overturns centuries of rabbinic and Christian iconography (the priest sweeping the breast in four directions, elevating the thigh skyward) in favor of a single elevation-ritual. The steelman: Milgrom has demonstrated that *hēnîp* throughout Ugaritic and Akkadian cognates denotes vertical elevation toward a deity, that the term is applied in Leviticus to objects (like the Levites themselves in Numbers 8:11) that cannot be waved horizontally, and that the two-direction-versus-four-direction distinction appears nowhere in the biblical text itself but only in later rabbinic systematization. If Milgrom is right, the wave-and-heave is a single presentation-gesture that the priest performs with two different portions, not a choreographed double ritual. This is the reading most contemporary scholarship has accepted.

**Where this leaves us:** whether one reads wave-and-heave as two distinct gestures or one elevation-gesture, the theological point is the same — the priestly portion is not taken by the priest but given by Yahweh through a liturgical act of presentation. The priest eats at Yahweh's table, not from his own hand.

**Unresolved question:** If the thanksgiving offering alone survives into the messianic age (the tradition Ratzinger cites from Leviticus Rabbah 9:7), and if its leavened bread persists as a theological marker of deliverance restored to the ordinary, then what happens to the fat-and-blood prohibitions — do they persist, transform, or lapse, and on what basis can a tradition decide?

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references. First: the *piggûl* concept of Lev 7:18 reappears in Isaiah 65:4 as a description of apostate Israelites eating "broth of tainted things" in their idolatrous feasts — the prophet is indicting post-exilic Jews for turning the Levitical taxonomy inside out. Second: the thanksgiving offering of Lev 7:11–15 with its day-of-offering time limit reappears structurally in Exodus 12:10's Passover (eaten the same night, nothing left until morning) and in the manna regulations of Exodus 16:19–20 (nothing kept until morning, or it breeds worms); the common logic is that deliverance-gifts must be consumed in their time, not stored. Third: the priestly breast-and-thigh portion of Lev 7:30–34 reappears negatively in 1 Samuel 2:12–17, where Eli's sons violate the rules by seizing meat before the fat is burned — the Eli-sons narrative is framed around a precise inversion of Leviticus 7, which becomes the measure of priestly corruption.
