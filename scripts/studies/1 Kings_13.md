# 1 Kings 13: The Man of God, the Old Prophet, and the Lion

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter is set at the Bethel altar Jeroboam has just established (the *bāmâ*-complex of 12:32-33). The "man of God" (*ʾîš ha-ʾĕlōhîm*, אִישׁ הָאֱלֹהִים) is the chapter's preferred designation for the Judahite prophet — a term Joseph Blenkinsopp's *A History of Prophecy in Israel* and David Petersen's *The Roles of Israel's Prophets* both treat as denoting an itinerant charismatic figure rather than a court-attached *nābîʾ*. The terminology of the chapter is unusually careful: the southerner is consistently *ʾîš ha-ʾĕlōhîm* until the closing verses, while the northerner is *nābîʾ zāqēn* (נָבִיא זָקֵן, "old prophet"). Both are prophets, but the lexical distinction signals different social locations — the Judahite as an outsider sent on a single oracular errand, the Bethel prophet as a settled, sons-bearing, donkey-owning local figure. James Crenshaw's *Prophetic Conflict* and Karel van der Toorn's *From Her Cradle to Her Grave* and *Family Religion in Babylonia, Syria, and Israel* situate the chapter within the broader ANE category of conflicting prophetic claims and the social embedding of regional prophets.

The "Josiah by name" prediction (verse 2) is the chapter's most striking historiographical feature. Josiah of Judah (reigned c. 640-609 BCE) destroys the Bethel altar in 2 Kings 23:15-20, exhuming bones from surrounding tombs and burning them on the altar to defile it permanently — explicitly fulfilling 1 Kings 13:2's prophecy. The naming of Josiah some three centuries before his birth is, for traditional reading, miraculous foreknowledge; for the dtr-redactional reading, it is the strongest single piece of evidence for a Josianic edition of the DH. Frank Moore Cross's double-redaction theory (Dtr1 = Josianic, Dtr2 = Exilic) makes much of this verse: a Josianic redactor has inserted his patron's name into an older prophetic-confrontation tradition, retrojecting the legitimation of Josiah's reform back to Jeroboam's foundational moment. Richard Nelson's *The Double Redaction of the Deuteronomistic History* refines the argument; Knoppers in *Two Nations Under God* engages it carefully. The "houses of the high places that are in the cities of Samaria" (verse 32) is anachronistic for the Jeroboamic period — Samaria as a city is founded by Omri in 1 Kings 16:24 — and the phrase "cities of Samaria" most plausibly reflects post-722 BCE Assyrian-period vocabulary, again pointing to dtr redactional layering.

The lion-and-donkey tableau (verses 24-28) is materially recognizable. Lions were extant in the Levantine ecosystem through the late Iron Age and into the Crusader period; the last attested Asiatic lion in Palestine was killed near Megiddo in the twelfth or thirteenth century CE. Lion-attack accounts are well-attested in ANE literature (compare 1 Kings 20:36, 2 Kings 17:25-26, and the Assyrian royal-hunt reliefs from Nineveh's palace of Ashurbanipal, where the lion is the paradigmatic dangerous beast). The tableau in 13:28 — lion and donkey standing beside the body, the lion not eating, not attacking the donkey — is presented as miraculous: the lion functions as YHWH's agent of judgment but does not behave as a normal predator. Ancient readers would have recognized the unnaturalness immediately; this is the chapter's most explicit theophanic signal.

The "oak" (*ʾēlâ*, אֵלָה, verse 14) under which the man of God sits is the standard sacred-tree marker of Levantine cultic and assembly geography (cf. Genesis 35:4-8 — "the oak of weeping" near Bethel; Joshua 24:26 — the oak at Shechem; Judges 6:11 — Gideon's oak). The ironic juxtaposition is that the Judahite prophet, having denounced Bethel's apostate cult, is sitting under an oak of the kind that often marked sacred sites. The narrative is showing the man of God in a vulnerable cultic-geographic location.

A few Hebrew terms repay attention. *Mizbēaḥ* (מִזְבֵּחַ, "altar," repeated as direct address in verse 2 — "O altar, altar"). The vocative repetition is rare in prophetic speech and parallels the Lord's address to Moses ("Moses, Moses" in Exodus 3:4) and Samuel ("Samuel, Samuel" in 1 Samuel 3:10) — the rhetorical force is solemnity-by-doubling, a device Robert Alter has documented across biblical prose. *Yābēš* (יָבֵשׁ, "withered," verse 4) — used of Jeroboam's hand; the same root for desiccation that runs through Ezekiel's vision of dry bones (Ezekiel 37) and the prophetic curse-language. *Šākar* (שָׁכַר) and the lying speech of verse 18 — the old prophet's words "an angel spoke to me by the word of the LORD" are explicitly marked by the narrator with *kiḥēš lô*, "he lied to him." The narrator's editorial intervention is uncharacteristically direct; usually biblical narrative leaves moral evaluation to the reader. Here the narrator's hand is on the scale.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is one of the most genre-puzzling units in 1-2 Kings. It is at once: a prophetic sign-act (verses 1-10), a parable of prophetic disobedience (verses 11-32), and a closing dtr formula (verses 33-34). Its theological framing of prophet-versus-prophet conflict has invited extensive scholarly attention, much of it acknowledging the chapter's resistance to easy interpretation.

Walter Brueggemann in *1 & 2 Kings* (Smyth & Helwys) calls the chapter "the dtr's most strange and unsettling meditation on prophetic obedience." Marvin Sweeney (*I & II Kings*, OTL) treats it as a wisdom-style cautionary tale embedded within historical narrative. Mordechai Cogan (*I Kings*, AB) is more reserved, treating it as a tradition with deep folkloric roots that the dtr has incorporated for its predictive Josiah-content. Richard Nelson (*First and Second Kings*, Interpretation) pushes hardest on the chapter's strangeness — it presents a true oracle delivered by a faithful prophet, who is then deceived by another prophet's lie about angelic communication, eats forbidden food, and is killed by a lion; the deceiving prophet survives, mourns him, and is vindicated as the bearer of a future-fulfilling oracle. The chapter offers no explicit moral-theological resolution.

The literary devices are remarkable. The chapter's center is the speech of verse 18: "I also am a prophet as you are, and an angel spoke to me by the word of the LORD, saying, 'Bring him back with you into your house that he may eat bread and drink water.' But he lied to him." The phrase "by the word of the LORD" (*bid-bar YHWH*) has been the chapter's leitmotif since verse 1; here it is weaponized as the vehicle of a falsehood. The repeated formula — used five times in the chapter as the marker of authentic prophetic speech — is now spoken by a deceiver. The narrator's terse "but he lied to him" (*kiḥēš lô*) places the moral evaluation at the structural center. Robert Polzin in *David and the Deuteronomist* and James Walsh in his *1 Kings* (Berit Olam) both note that the chapter's literary architecture is built around the corruption of the formula by which the chapter has been verifying truth.

The chapter is also rich in narrative rhyme. The man of God is told three things: do not eat bread, do not drink water, do not return by the way you came. He observes the third (he goes another way out of Bethel — verse 10) and violates the first two (he eats and drinks at the old prophet's house — verse 19). The narrator emphasizes the partial obedience: he kept the geographical instruction and broke the dietary one, and the dietary breach is what is fatal. The implicit theology is that prophetic instruction must be obeyed in its entirety, not selectively.

Karel van der Toorn (*Family Religion*) and James Crenshaw (*Prophetic Conflict*) have read the chapter as a meditation on the criteria for distinguishing true from false prophecy. Crenshaw's analysis is especially incisive: the chapter does not give the man of God any way to verify the old prophet's claim. The Bethel prophet appeals to angelic mediation (*malʾāk* — "messenger/angel" — language common in Zechariah and other later prophets), to peer-prophetic standing ("I also am a prophet as you are"), and to plausible hospitality custom. The Judahite prophet has only the original instruction. The lesson, on Crenshaw's reading, is that a prior direct word from YHWH cannot be overridden by subsequent indirect mediation, even from another prophet — the original word stands.

Textual transmission is relatively stable for this chapter compared to chapters 11-12. The LXX of 3 Kingdoms 13 follows MT closely, with only minor variations. The chapter does not have the "Alternative Jeroboam" supplementary material that LXX 12 carries; it appears to have been a stable narrative tradition in both transmission lines. Trebolle Barrera and Aejmelaeus's work confirms the relative textual stability.

The chapter's regnal-closure formula (verses 33-34) returns to the Jeroboam frame — "After this thing Jeroboam did not turn from his evil way" — and previews the dynasty's destruction (1 Kings 14:10-11, 15:29). The literary effect is to embed a strange prophetic episode within the ongoing dtr indictment.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In Jewish tradition the chapter has produced extensive midrashic and commentarial reflection. *b. Sanhedrin* 89a and 104a treat the chapter as a study in the dangers of prophet-against-prophet conflict; the Talmudic discussion focuses on whether the man of God should have known to disregard the old prophet's claim. Rashi engages the chapter carefully, treating the lying prophet's eventual oracle (verses 20-22) as evidence that even a deceiver can be a vehicle of true prophecy when YHWH wills — a theologically delicate position. Radak (David Kimhi) reads the chapter as a study in prophetic discernment; he treats the man of God's failure as instructive, not damning. Ralbag offers a rationalist reading: the man of God should have applied the test of internal coherence — a true prophet does not contradict an earlier explicit word from YHWH.

Don Isaac Abarbanel reads the chapter as a meditation on the political-theological function of prophets in a corrupt cultic regime: the man of God comes from outside the Bethel system, delivers his oracle, and must depart without participating in any of the cult's hospitality structures. The dietary prohibition is not arbitrary; it is symbolic non-participation. His failure is therefore not just personal disobedience but a failure of his prophetic office's symbolic separation from the apostate cult. Abarbanel's reading anticipates modern political-theological readings (compare Brueggemann, Walter Wink). Yair Zakovitch's source-critical work and Avivah Zornberg's contemporary readings have continued to engage the chapter's irresolution.

In Christian reception the chapter has been famously difficult. Origen's homilies on Kings (preserved in part) treat the chapter typologically: the man of God is a type of the prophet, the old prophet a type of the false teacher, the lion a type of judgment. Augustine in *City of God* 17 and elsewhere acknowledges the chapter's difficulty and treats it as a warning against prophets who add to or modify the original divine word. Ambrose treats it briefly. Chrysostom's homilies engage the deception theme. Theodoret of Cyrus offers measured philological commentary. Gregory the Great in *Moralia* uses the chapter as a meditation on the perils of believing pious-sounding falsehoods. Aquinas in the *Summa* (II-II, q. 172 on prophecy) engages the chapter's epistemology — how does one verify a prophetic claim? — and concludes that the prior direct word stands.

The Reformers found the chapter pastorally weighty. Calvin's *Sermons on 1 Kings* preached at Geneva treat the chapter at length; Calvin uses the man of God's failure as a warning against the corruption of pious community by false teaching, and as a meditation on the discipline required to hold to an original divine word in the face of plausible-sounding contradictions. Luther's lectures and table talk return to the chapter as evidence that prophetic standing does not preclude moral failure — even genuine prophets can lie, and the believer must hold to the original word.

In modern critical scholarship, the chapter has provoked some of the most sustained theological reflection in 1-2 Kings commentary. Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* II/2 (the doctrine of election) treats the chapter at remarkable length as a meditation on the unsearchable judgments of God — Barth was haunted by the chapter's apparent injustice and treated it as one of the OT's deepest meditations on divine sovereignty. Walter Brueggemann's *1 & 2 Kings* treats the chapter as the dtr's most uncompromising statement of the cost of prophetic disobedience. Mordechai Cogan's *I Kings* (AB) is the philological-historical reference; Marvin Sweeney's *I & II Kings* (OTL) engages the chapter's redactional layers. Richard Nelson's *First and Second Kings* (Interpretation) and his earlier *Double Redaction* offer the major redaction-critical treatment. Iain Provan, Davis (*1 Kings: The Wisdom and the Folly*), Goldingay, and Eugene Peterson have all written pastorally on the chapter's irresolution. Karel van der Toorn (*Family Religion*) and James Crenshaw (*Prophetic Conflict*) are the foundational treatments of the prophetic-deception theme. Robert Polzin's *David and the Deuteronomist* offers the literary-critical reading that has been most influential among biblical-theology readers.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Why does the man of God die for a relatively small disobedience while the deceiving prophet lives, prospers, and even bears a true oracle?**

Position one: divine sovereignty is unsearchable (Barth, Brueggemann). The chapter's refusal to explain the asymmetry is the theological point. YHWH's judgments are not subject to human accounting. The man of God dies because he disobeyed the explicit word; the old prophet lives because that is YHWH's prerogative. The chapter resists the moral-accounting theology that would expect punishment proportional to offense.

Position two: prophetic vocation entails heightened responsibility (Calvin, Aquinas, much of the Reformation tradition). The man of God is not punished for a generic disobedience but for the failure of his specific prophetic office — having delivered a true oracle, his subsequent compromise undermines the oracle's credibility. The asymmetry of judgment reflects the asymmetry of office: the prophet who has just spoken with public authority is held to a higher standard than the prophet who is merely a private deceiver. Calvin develops this as the burden of prophetic faithfulness.

Position three: the chapter is the dtr's parable about the cult of Bethel itself, with the prophets functioning as figures (Polzin, the political-theological readers). The man of God represents the southern, Jerusalem-temple, faithful prophetic tradition; the old prophet represents the northern, Bethel-cultic, accommodated prophetic tradition. The asymmetric outcome is not about the individual prophets but about the sustainability of the two cultic systems — the southern prophet is dispensable but his oracle endures (Josiah will fulfill it three centuries later); the northern prophet survives but only to bury his Judahite counterpart and acknowledge the Judahite oracle's truth. The chapter is a parable about the fate of the two kingdoms, not a moral story about the two men.

Steelman of position one: the chapter resists every reading that would make it morally legible. The man of God has been deceived; he has acted in good faith on what appeared to be divine instruction; he has eaten bread and drunk water — actions that in any other context would be unremarkable hospitality. The disproportion between his offense and his fate is the chapter's central feature. The dtr is forcing the reader to confront divine sovereignty without the consolation of moral-accounting theology. Barth saw this clearly.

Where this leaves us: position one is the most exegetically defensible. Position three is illuminating as a literary reading of the chapter's place in the larger DH; position two has pastoral force but tends to soften the chapter's theological hardness. The most honest reading holds that the chapter is genuinely difficult and was meant to be — it is the dtr's meditation on the unsearchable judgments of God, and any easy resolution betrays its purpose. The chapter sits in the canon as a permanent provocation against theologies that demand divine action be proportionate to human deserving.

**Tension Two: How does the chapter address the problem of how to discern true from false prophecy?**

Position one: the prior direct word stands (Aquinas, Crenshaw). The man of God has received an explicit instruction directly from YHWH. The old prophet's claim of subsequent angelic mediation cannot override the prior direct word. The lesson is hermeneutical: when later claimed revelation contradicts earlier explicit revelation, the earlier stands. This becomes a major theme in later prophetic conflict (Jeremiah versus Hananiah in Jeremiah 28; the "lying spirit" of 1 Kings 22).

Position two: the chapter offers no test, only a tragedy (Brueggemann, Barth). The man of God had no way to verify or falsify the old prophet's claim in real time. The chapter is honest about the epistemological poverty of the prophetic situation: prophets often cannot tell true claims from false in the moment, and the consequences fall regardless. This is a sobering reading but matches the text.

Position three: the test is fidelity to the original sending (Calvin, the Reformation tradition). The man of God was sent on a specific mission with specific instructions; his obligation is to complete that mission as instructed. Hospitality, peer-prophetic claims, even angelic-mediation claims do not override the original commission. Faithfulness to the original sending is the test.

Steelman of position three: prophetic authority is not free-floating; it is sent. The man of God's vocation in the chapter is bounded by his commissioning instructions. To accept the old prophet's hospitality is to accept Bethel's symbolic legitimacy, which is precisely what his mission was to deny. The dietary prohibition is theologically loaded; it is not arbitrary. Calvin's reading is theologically rich.

Where this leaves us: positions one and three are complementary — the prior direct word stands, *and* the prophet's vocation is bounded by the original commissioning. Position two sits alongside both as honest acknowledgment that even with these criteria the prophet may not have certainty in real time. The chapter's pastoral teaching: hold to the original word, even at the cost of plausible-sounding contradiction.

Unresolved question: what is the spiritual or moral state of the old prophet at the chapter's end? He lies, deceives a peer to his death, mourns him, and asks to be buried beside him so that his bones will be spared in the eventual Josianic destruction (verse 31 in light of 2 Kings 23:18). Is he repentant? Cynically self-protective? A complex figure whose mixed motives the dtr leaves uncategorized? The text does not say.

## The Thread

The chapter looks back to 1 Kings 12:32-33 (Jeroboam's altar establishment) and the Mosaic prophet's authority traditions of Numbers 12 and Deuteronomy 13 (the test of false prophecy) and 18 (the prophet like Moses). Forward, the chapter sets up 2 Kings 23:15-20 (Josiah's destruction of the Bethel altar, fulfilling verse 2's prophecy by name three centuries later — the most extended single fulfillment-prophecy span in the OT); 1 Kings 22 (the lying spirit and Micaiah's confrontation with the four hundred prophets); Jeremiah 28 (Hananiah versus Jeremiah, with Hananiah dying within the year for false prophecy); and the New Testament's concern with false prophecy (Matthew 7:15-23, 1 John 4:1, Revelation 2:20). The lion-as-judgment motif runs through 2 Kings 17:25-26 (lions as judgment on the resettled Samaritan population) and into Daniel 6 (the lions Daniel survives — an inversion of the 1 Kings 13 motif). The chapter's hermeneutical principle — prior direct word stands against subsequent indirect mediation — is foundational for the canonical-doctrinal logic by which later revelation is judged against earlier (compare Galatians 1:8 — "if we or an angel from heaven should preach to you a gospel contrary to the one we preached to you, let him be accursed," a near-verbal echo of 1 Kings 13's situation).
