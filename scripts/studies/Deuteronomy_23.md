# Deuteronomy 23: Open Palms to the Sojourner

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Deuteronomy 23 belongs to the middle span of the Deuteronomic Code and is delivered, in the book's dramatic frame, on the plains of Moab where Moses addresses a new generation about to cross the Jordan. The Hebrew title of the book, *Dĕbārîm* ("words"), is taken from its opening; the chapter treats the standing of the Israelite assembly (*qāhāl YHWH*), the purity of the military camp, the treatment of escaped slaves, prohibitions of cultic prostitution and interest-taking, the law of vows, and the traveler's right to eat from a neighbor's field. Whether the chapter originates with Moses in the Late Bronze / Early Iron transition (defended by Kenneth Kitchen and Meredith Kline on the basis of Hittite treaty parallels) or reflects Josianic reform literature (de Wette 1805; Wellhausen; now in more nuanced form Moshe Weinfeld), the material culture reflected is consistent with settled Israelite village life intersected by seasonal warfare, itinerant labor, and long-distance trade.

The chapter's opening legislation about who may enter "the assembly of YHWH" (vv. 1–8) reflects a concrete institution. The *qāhāl* is not simply the ethnic people Israel but its cultic and possibly military-political assembly — the gathering with voice and standing. Mesha Stele-era Moab, Ammonite settlements east of the Jordan, and Edomite polities in the Negev are archaeologically well-attested neighbors whose populations intermarried and traded with Israel; the legislation here draws boundaries around who can assimilate into full covenantal standing and on what timeline. Egyptian stelae and Amarna correspondence document the longstanding Egyptian administrative presence in Canaan, which gives texture to the "you were a sojourner in his land" rationale (v. 7).

The camp-purity legislation (vv. 9–14) assumes military encampments of the sort documented in ANE iconography — tented arrangements, designated latrine zones, purification procedures. The Hebrew *yātēd* (translated "trowel" in v. 13) is a tent-peg-like implement; the digging-and-covering procedure is remarkably close to latrine discipline prescribed in 1QM (the Qumran War Scroll). The nocturnal emission rule (vv. 10–11) uses the technical expression *miqrēh lāylâ*, a ritual-impurity category further developed in Leviticus 15 and in the later Mishnah tractate *Mikvaʾot*.

The fugitive-slave law (vv. 15–16) must be read against its ancient Near Eastern backdrop. Code of Hammurabi §§15–20 prescribes death for anyone who harbors a fugitive slave or assists escape. The Laws of Eshnunna and the Hittite Laws are only marginally less severe. Ancient Near Eastern extradition treaties — including the Hittite-Egyptian treaty preserved at Karnak and Boghazköy — regularly feature clauses requiring the return of fugitives. Against this uniform regional policy, Deuteronomy 23:15–16 is strikingly counter-cultural: not only must you not return him, he chooses his own town and must not be oppressed. Archaeologically, the Iron Age Levantine towns that Deuteronomy envisions would have had slave populations, debt-bondsmen, and refugees fleeing regional conflict.

The prohibition on cultic prostitution (vv. 17–18) uses the Hebrew terms *qĕdēšâ* and *qādēš*, technical words for sacred-sex personnel attached to Canaanite and Mesopotamian temple establishments (cf. Akkadian *qadištu*). Whether such figures were primarily prostitutes in the modern sense or cultic functionaries of a broader sort is debated (Karel van der Toorn has pushed back on assuming a straightforward sex-worker identification). The paired phrase "wages of a dog" (v. 18) is now generally read, with Nelson and Tigay, as either a contemptuous term for a male cultic prostitute or as a literal prohibition on offering money from disreputable sources at the sanctuary.

The interest law (vv. 19–20) uses *nešek* — literally "bite" — for interest on money, food, or any loan. The ANE context is dense with commercial lending; tablets from Larsa, Nuzi, and Ugarit preserve interest rates typically running 20–33% for silver and higher still for barley. Deuteronomy's prohibition on charging *nešek* to a fellow Israelite is therefore a sharp deviation from regional commercial norms, preserved and extended in Leviticus 25:36–37.

Key Hebrew terms: *qāhāl YHWH* (assembly, vv. 1–8); *pĕṣûaʿ dakāʾ* (crushed of testicles, v. 1); *mamzēr* (forbidden union / illegitimate, v. 2); *maḥăneh qādôš* (holy camp, v. 14); *qādēš / qĕdēšâ* (cultic personnel, v. 17); *nešek* (interest, vv. 19–20); *nēder* (vow, vv. 21–23).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter alternates apodictic commands ("you shall not") with casuistic case framing ("if a man becomes unclean"). Patrick Miller's framing of Deuteronomic law as "preached law" applies particularly well here: motive clauses ("because they did not meet you with bread and water," v. 4; "because the LORD your God walks in the midst of your camp," v. 14) moralize and narrativize the statutes in a way that the Code of Hammurabi's terser formulations never attempt.

The internal organization has been debated. Calum Carmichael (*The Laws of Deuteronomy*; *Law and Narrative in the Bible*) argues that Deuteronomy 23 still belongs under the seventh commandment's orbit — that is, sexual and boundary purity — and reads the individual laws as narrative-triggered by episodes in Genesis and Numbers (the Moab/Ammon exclusion keyed to Lot's daughters in Gen 19; the Edom/Egypt clauses keyed to Jacob/Esau and the Exodus; the slave refuge law possibly keyed to Hagar). Stephen Kaufman's Decalogue-correlation scheme places the chapter straddling commandments seven and eight. Dennis Olson (*Deuteronomy and the Death of Moses*) is more cautious about forcing a Decalogue grid and reads the chapter as a thematic cluster on "the holy camp" and its boundaries. Georg Braulik works with a chiastic internal architecture organized around the camp-holiness center. Most commentators agree the chapter is topically clustered (membership — camp holiness — slave refuge — cultic integrity — economic ethics — vows — hospitality), even if the micro-ordering remains disputed.

Literary devices are notable. The tenfold repetition of *qāhāl YHWH* in the opening six verses functions as an incantatory marker. The motive clauses stack narrative memory (Balaam) on top of ethnographic identity. The "holy camp" framing (v. 14) supplies a theological center: YHWH's presence walks in the midst, which both motivates purity and reframes it as a consequence of divine nearness rather than a ritual precondition. The closing two verses on gleaning-in-passage (vv. 24–25) strike a conspicuously generous note after the restrictive membership rules, rounding the chapter with a vision of neighborly trust.

Textually, 4QDeut fragments preserve parts of the chapter with only minor variants. The Samaritan Pentateuch occasionally harmonizes motive clauses with parallel passages in Numbers. The Septuagint renders *qāhāl* with *ekklēsia* — a translation of enormous downstream consequence for early Christian self-understanding (when Paul writes "church" he is translating the same word that here excludes Moabites). Verse numbering differs between the Hebrew and English traditions: the Hebrew Bible treats the "father's wife" verse as 23:1 (where ESV prints it as 22:30), shifting subsequent verses.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish reception.** Philo (*Special Laws* 1) devotes extended attention to the assembly-exclusion rules, reading the bodily-integrity requirements as metaphors for moral integrity and interpreting the Ammonite/Moabite exclusion as an indictment of inhospitality. Josephus (*Antiquities* 4) harmonizes the exclusions with his Greco-Roman audience. The Qumran *Temple Scroll* (11QT 39–40; 45–47) rewrites and extends the camp-purity legislation, imposing an even stricter boundary around the envisioned sanctuary precinct — excluding the blind, the lame, and the menstruant from the Holy City, not merely the assembly. The *Damascus Document* (CD 14) extends exclusion categories further. 4QMMT touches on several of this chapter's concerns as part of its broader halakhic polemic.

The Mishnah's *Yevamot* 8 and Tosefta *Yevamot* work out exactly who counts as a "Moabite" or "Ammonite" for the exclusion; the famous halakhic principle "Moabite but not Moabitess" — authorizing Ruth's descendants in the Davidic line — appears in Mishnah *Yevamot* 8:3 and is central to rabbinic readings of the Ruth narrative. Rabbi Yohanan in the Bavli works out the *mamzēr* rules with casuistic precision in *Yevamot* and *Kiddushin*. Rashi glosses the Ammonite/Moabite exclusion with the Sifre Deuteronomy tradition, emphasizing the moral rather than strictly ethnic offense. Ibn Ezra engages philological problems in *pĕṣûaʿ dakāʾ*. Ramban reads the fugitive-slave law as a universal humanitarian principle applicable beyond Israel. Maimonides (*Hilchot Issurei Biah*; *Hilchot Melachim*) systematizes the membership rules; he controversially argues that in the post-Sennacherib dispersal the genealogical identities of ancient Moabites and Ammonites are effectively unrecoverable, effectively rendering the exclusion inoperable (*Hilchot Issurei Biah* 12:25). Sforno emphasizes the Balaam backstory as the moral root of the exclusion. Nehama Leibowitz pays close attention to the Edom/Egypt positive case as a principle of moral memory: gratitude outlasts old injury. Jonathan Sacks treats the chapter's membership rules as a worked example of covenantal pluralism — concentric circles of belonging rather than binary inclusion. Tikva Frymer-Kensky engages the cultic-prostitution passage within broader reconsiderations of female religious roles in Israel and its neighbors.

Isaiah 56:3–8 — "let not the eunuch say, 'Behold, I am a dry tree' … I will give in my house and within my walls a monument … an everlasting name" — stands as an explicit reversal of Deut 23:1, a reversal Jewish tradition reads as eschatological and which the Christian book of Acts (8:26–40, the Ethiopian eunuch) reads as inaugurally fulfilled.

**Christian reception.** Origen reads the chapter allegorically; Chrysostom engages the interest prohibition in his homilies on usury. Augustine treats the Moabite/Ammonite exclusion as a lesson about divine election surpassing genealogy (the Ruth narrative, in Augustine's hands, demonstrates Christ's lineage through precisely the excluded stock). Aquinas (*ST* II-II q. 78) develops a full treatment of usury grounded partly in Deuteronomy 23, distinguishing the natural-law and ceremonial dimensions of the prohibition.

The Reformation inherited two lively debates from this chapter. Luther's position on usury was restrictive, rooted in Deuteronomy 23 and in Aquinas, but he distinguished between personal loans (charitable) and commercial lending (more permitted). Calvin famously broke with the mainstream tradition in his *Letter on Usury* (1545), arguing that *nešek* applied to exploitative lending to the poor and did not forbid commercial interest between willing parties — an interpretive move that had enormous consequences for Reformed capitalism (Max Weber, *The Protestant Ethic*; R. H. Tawney's *Religion and the Rise of Capitalism*, with important qualifications by Brad Gregory). The medieval European Jewish moneylending phenomenon, often framed as an outworking of the foreigner-exception clause (v. 20), was a defensive economic adaptation to Christian exclusion of Jews from craft guilds; Salo Baron and Yuval Harari have emphasized the complicated interplay of law and social constraint.

On the fugitive-slave law (vv. 15–16), Christian abolitionist readers in the nineteenth century seized on the text with singular force. Frederick Douglass, in speeches and in the *North Star*, cited Deuteronomy 23:15 against the 1850 Fugitive Slave Act as a biblical refutation of enforced return. Lewis Tappan and Theodore Dwight Weld deployed the text in American abolitionist pamphlet literature. The text stood as a rebuke not merely to the institution of slavery in general but specifically to the legal machinery of returning escaped persons — making Deuteronomy 23 one of the most politically consequential passages in nineteenth-century American religious discourse.

Modern critical commentary — Weinfeld, Tigay (JPS), Nelson (OTL), Craigie (NICOT), McConville (AOTC), Block (NIVAC), Wright — converges on treating the chapter as a Deuteronomic reworking of older Israelite traditions in a humanitarian direction. Brueggemann (*Deuteronomy*, AOTC) reads the fugitive-slave and interest laws as elements of an alternative "neighborly economy." David Instone-Brewer, better known for divorce and remarriage scholarship, has written on the cultural and legal backdrop of usury in Second Temple Judaism. Amy-Jill Levine and Judith Hauptman remind Christian readers that the chapter's rabbinic afterlife, particularly on the Ammonite/Moabite question via Ruth, is a major thread of internal Jewish self-correction.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Christian and Jewish traditions converge on the moral force of the fugitive-slave law and on the Isaiah-56 trajectory regarding eunuchs. They diverge most sharply on the interest prohibition. Jewish tradition has maintained a developed apparatus (*hetter iska*) that legally restructures commercial loans to satisfy the Deuteronomic prohibition in practice. Christian tradition, after Calvin's intervention, largely abandoned any general prohibition on interest, leaving only moralistic critiques of "usury" in the narrow sense of exploitative lending. On cultic prostitution (vv. 17–18), both traditions read the text as excluded from any contemporary sanctuary practice, though with different framings: Jewish tradition tends to moralize the offense, Christian tradition tends to historicize it. On Moabite/Ammonite exclusion, the rabbinic "Moabite not Moabitess" ruling and the Christian reading of Ruth-to-David-to-Jesus (Matt 1:5) tell the same essential story with different rhetorical registers: the covenant is genealogically narrower than ethnic Israel and yet, paradoxically, ethnically wider. The Ethiopian eunuch of Acts 8 (from the excluded class of v. 1) and the Samaritan woman of John 4 (from a population whose covenantal standing was contested) sit in the Christian canon as narrative commentary on Deuteronomy 23's boundaries being rewritten.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Holy boundaries, or moral hypocrisy?** The assembly-membership rules (vv. 1–8) have always strained readers. Three positions deserve a hearing.

*Position A — Covenant integrity.* Block, Craigie, and Wright read the exclusions as protective of the *qāhāl*'s covenantal identity. The eunuch prohibition reflects the cultural valence of bodily wholeness at the sanctuary (cf. Lev 21:17–23); the Moabite/Ammonite exclusion is grounded in specific historical offenses (Balaam, inhospitality); the Edomite/Egyptian inclusion reflects remembered kinship and remembered hospitality. The rules make ethical, not arbitrary, distinctions.

*Position B — Late exilic boundary-marking.* Weinfeld and Nelson historicize the exclusions within post-monarchic identity consolidation. The concerns about *mamzēr* lineages (v. 2) and foreign assemblies may reflect Ezra–Nehemiah-era debates about mixed marriage. On this reading the exclusions are less timeless ethics than historically contingent community defense.

*Position C — Prophetic reversal already inscribed.* Isaiah 56:3–8 names eunuchs and foreigners and explicitly reverses the Deut 23 exclusion, and does so within the same canonical stream. On this reading Deuteronomy 23 is *meant* to generate its own critique: the exclusion belongs to one covenantal moment whose resolution is the widening of the assembly. Acts 8 and 10 (and Galatians 3:28) complete the trajectory.

**Steelman for Position C (least intuitive to readers who want the Torah to say one thing consistently).** The unsettling claim here is that the Torah expects its own provisions to be transcended — not abrogated, but outgrown. Deuteronomy 23:1 is not a mistake that Isaiah corrects; it is a historically concrete rule that names a real anxiety (bodily integrity, lineage continuity) in a real setting, and Isaiah 56 honors that anxiety by promising the eunuch "a name better than sons and daughters." The Christian reading that sees Acts 8's Ethiopian eunuch as fulfillment and the rabbinic reading that reads Ruth's Moabite inclusion as halakhic restraint on the plain sense of v. 3 are performing the same interpretive move: honoring the text by letting it unfold.

**Where this leaves us.** The three positions all carry real weight. If we hold them together: Deuteronomy 23 marks a real covenantal boundary (A), in a particular historical moment (B), within a canonical trajectory that already anticipates its widening (C). The assembly has borders; those borders mean something; those borders are not the last word.

**Tension 2: Radical economic humanitarianism, or ethnic double standard?** The fugitive-slave law (vv. 15–16) is among the most remarkable legal innovations in the ANE corpus; the interest law (vv. 19–20) and the gleaning-in-passage law (vv. 24–25) extend the same humane logic. But the interest law explicitly permits charging the foreigner what one may not charge the brother. Three readings:

*Position A — Concentric covenantal ethics.* Wright and Block read the distinction as an organic feature of covenantal life: the covenant community owes one another forms of generosity that cannot be legally enforced on all parties at all times. The foreigner-exception is not hostile permission to exploit; it is the absence of a covenantal mutual obligation.

*Position B — Commercial realism.* Tigay and Weinfeld read the foreigner clause as a pragmatic acknowledgment that international trade requires interest-bearing instruments; prohibiting interest across the board would have made Israel economically unviable. The brother-foreigner distinction encodes the difference between a loan to a struggling neighbor (charitable) and a commercial investment with a traveling merchant (contractual).

*Position C — Moral asymmetry to be critiqued.* Brueggemann is less comfortable with the easy justifications. The same chapter that mandates refuge for the fugitive slave — striking *any* ANE distinction between insider and outsider — permits a different treatment of the foreign borrower. The text is not internally consistent on the reach of its own humanitarianism, and the trajectory from Deuteronomy through James 5:4, Paul's collection for the Jerusalem poor, and the prophetic critiques of economic oppression is the tradition's own self-correction.

**Steelman for Position C (least intuitive to harmonizing readers).** The critical reading has this going for it: the chapter's own logic pushes toward universalism when applied to the slave (no distinction between insider and outsider), then retreats to distinction when applied to the lender. If the fugitive-slave law is unambiguously a moral advance over Hammurabi — and it is — then the foreigner-interest exception should be read as the unfinished work of the same humanitarian impulse. Calvin's re-reading of *nešek* as specifically exploitative lending (not all interest) is a contemporary attempt to honor the text's trajectory rather than its letter. A sober reading concedes that Deuteronomy 23 does not arrive at moral completeness on economic ethics; it starts the motion.

**Where this leaves us.** The chapter's radical fugitive-slave law and its concentric-but-incomplete interest law together pose the question of whether covenant ethics is inherently universal or inherently particular. The answer the canonical trajectory suggests is that it is *particular in shape* (the covenant is with Israel) and *universal in direction* (it moves toward the nations). The interest law's foreigner clause is a halting station on a longer road.

**Unresolved question.** Who counts as a "brother" when the text says "you shall not charge your brother interest" (v. 19)? If Ruth's inclusion teaches that covenantal kinship can be adopted across ethnic lines, does that widening also redraw the foreigner-brother economic distinction — or do those categories operate on different logics, with economic boundaries more stubborn than cultic ones?

## The Thread

Four cross-references worth carrying with this chapter:

1. **Deut 23:1 and Isaiah 56:3–8 / Acts 8:26–40.** The eunuch exclusion is explicitly and lyrically overturned by the prophet ("I will give them an everlasting name that shall not be cut off"), and the Christian Scripture enacts the reversal narratively. The Ethiopian eunuch reads Isaiah 53 in his chariot — a passage not far from the eunuch promise of Isaiah 56 — and is baptized. The canonical arc is a worked example of how Torah's boundaries are meant to be honored, remembered, and finally widened.

2. **Deut 23:3 and Ruth 4:13–22 / Matthew 1:5.** The Moabite exclusion stands in the Deuteronomic text; the narrative of Ruth answers it in the form of a genealogy leading to David and (in Matthew's reading) to Jesus. The rabbinic "Moabite not Moabitess" ruling and the Christian Matthean genealogy perform the same theological operation — letting a narrative widen what a statute had narrowed.

3. **Deut 23:15–16 and Philemon.** The fugitive-slave law is the Old Testament's most counter-cultural statute. Paul's letter to Philemon, sending Onesimus back (in a profoundly transformed relational configuration, "no longer as a slave but as more than a slave, as a beloved brother"), sits in interesting tension with the Deuteronomic command not to return him. The early abolitionist polemical tradition read Philemon in the light of Deuteronomy 23, insisting that the gospel fulfillment cannot be more restrictive than the Mosaic ground.

4. **Deut 23:24–25 and Mark 2:23–28.** The right of the traveler to pluck grain by hand from a neighbor's field is presupposed by the Gospel scene of Jesus's disciples satisfying hunger on the Sabbath. The Pharisees' objection is not that they have stolen grain — Deuteronomy 23:25 permits the plucking — but that they have done it on the Sabbath. The economic generosity of the Deuteronomic law is intact; the sabbath dispute operates on another axis. A careful reading of the Gospel scene depends on knowing the Deuteronomic right the disciples are exercising.
