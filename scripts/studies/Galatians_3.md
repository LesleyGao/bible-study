# Galatians 3: Abraham, the Curse, and the Family Without Borders

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

When Paul accuses the Galatians of being "bewitched" (3:1, baskainō, βασκαίνω), he is reaching for a word from the everyday folk-magic of the Mediterranean world. The "evil eye" was a real concern in Greco-Roman Galatia, attested in apotropaic amulets, household phylacteries, and curse tablets (defixiones) excavated across Asia Minor. The verb baskainō was used technically for casting the evil eye. Paul is suggesting that something occult and malign has happened to a community he had visited and seen receive the Spirit. This is not metaphor in a flat modern sense; it is the cultural register of his audience.

The audience itself remains the South-vs-North Galatian dispute (see chapter 1 analysis), but chapter 3 makes the South Galatian destination more textured. The cities of Pisidian Antioch, Iconium, Lystra, and Derbe had Jewish populations old enough that Genesis was a familiar text in synagogue worship; new God-fearer Gentile converts would have heard the Abraham stories read aloud and would have known that Abraham was the father of the Jewish people. The agitators' argument almost certainly traded on this: if you want to be Abraham's children and inherit Abraham's promises, you must do what Abraham did — be circumcised. Paul's response in chapter 3 is to retell the Abraham story from a different angle, focusing on Genesis 15:6 (Abraham's faith reckoned as righteousness) before Genesis 17 (the circumcision covenant).

The chronology Paul deploys in 3:17 — "the law, which came 430 years afterward" — follows the Masoretic Text of Exodus 12:40 (LXX has the 430 years cover the whole patriarchal period from Abraham). Paul more often quotes the LXX, but here his counting follows MT or a tradition shared with Jubilees 47–50.

The "guardian" of 3:24–25 (paidagōgos, παιδαγωγός) is a household role, not a teacher. The paidagōgos was typically a slave assigned to a freeborn boy from age six or seven until adolescence, charged with disciplining him, accompanying him to school, and protecting him. Plato (Lysis 208c) and Plutarch describe paidagōgoi as figures children both feared and depended on. The paidagōgos could beat the boy with a rod for misbehavior, and his role ended at the boy's coming of age.

The material world of "no longer Jew or Greek, slave or free, male and female" (3:28) is the world of Roman household codes, slave law, and the lex Iulia de maritandis ordinibus. Each of these binaries had legal force. Jewish exemptions from imperial cult, slave inability to own property or marry legally, and the patria potestas of male heads of household are the social facts behind Paul's claim. Wayne Meeks's seminal essay "The Image of the Androgyne" (1974) showed that the male-and-female pair (arsen kai thēly, ἄρσεν καὶ θῆλυ, echoing Genesis 1:27 LXX) belongs to a baptismal formula already circulating in Pauline communities.

P46 (Chester Beatty II, c. AD 175–225), our earliest substantial Galatians manuscript, preserves chapter 3 in good condition. Codex Sinaiticus and Vaticanus confirm the reading. The major textual variants in chapter 3 are minor — alternative orderings of "Christ Jesus / Jesus Christ," the question of whether Galatians 3:14 reads "the promise of the Spirit" or "the blessing of the Spirit." The pistis Christou question reappears at 3:22.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter. **Paidagōgos** (παιδαγωγός, 3:24–25) — household guardian-slave. **Epangelia** (ἐπαγγελία, "promise," used eight times in the chapter) — covenantal-promissory language. **Katara** (κατάρα, "curse," 3:10, 13) and the cognate verb **epikataratos** (ἐπικατάρατος, "cursed") — the language of Deuteronomic covenant sanctions. **Sperma** (σπέρμα, "seed/offspring," 3:16, 19, 29) — the term Paul forces into a singular reading despite its collective semantic range. **Eis Christon** (εἰς Χριστόν, 3:24, 27) — "into Christ," the prepositional phrase that names baptismal incorporation.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 3 opens the probatio in the Betz rhetorical scheme (3:1–4:31). After the propositio of 2:15–21, Paul now defends the thesis with a series of arguments. The chapter falls into roughly six movements: experiential argument from the Galatians' own reception of the Spirit (3:1–5); scriptural argument from Abraham (3:6–9); curse-and-redemption argument (3:10–14); legal argument from contract and covenant (3:15–18); the function of the law (3:19–22); the paidagōgos figure and the baptismal formula (3:23–29).

The argument is a virtuoso display of Jewish midrashic technique adapted to a Gentile audience. Paul strings together OT citations: Genesis 15:6 (3:6), Genesis 12:3 / 18:18 (3:8), Deuteronomy 27:26 (3:10), Habakkuk 2:4 (3:11), Leviticus 18:5 (3:12), Deuteronomy 21:23 (3:13), Genesis 12:7 / 13:15 / 17:7 (3:16). The connection of texts by shared verbal links is the rabbinic technique of gezerah shavah; Paul uses it to argue that "those of faith" (hoi ek pisteōs) are Abraham's true heirs.

The curse-blessing structure of 3:10–14 deploys the Deuteronomic sanctions framework. Deuteronomy 27–30 sets the blessings and curses of the covenant: blessing for obedience, curse for transgression. Paul argues that no one keeps "all things written" (Deuteronomy 27:26), so all under the law are under the curse — and Christ has redeemed by becoming a curse himself (Deuteronomy 21:23, on the body of an executed criminal hung on a tree). The reading depends on a particular inner-canonical move: importing Deuteronomy 27:26 onto everyone under Torah and Deuteronomy 21:23 onto Christ's crucifixion. N.T. Wright (The Climax of the Covenant, 1991) read 3:10–14 not as a generic claim about human inability but as a specific claim about Israel's exile-curse: the curse is the historical condition of unfaithful Israel, and Christ takes it on representatively.

The famous "seed... is Christ" argument of 3:16 is a notorious case of Paul exploiting a singular noun whose plain Hebrew sense (zera, זרע) is collective, like the English "offspring." Paul knows this — he uses sperma collectively at 3:29 ("if you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's sperma"). The argument at 3:16 is therefore not philological naïveté but rhetorical-typological move: the singular collective is filled by Christ as the corporate representative head, with the church then included as members of Christ.

The "law was added because of transgressions" of 3:19 has been read three ways. (a) The law was added to define and reveal sin (traditional view, Augustine). (b) The law was added to provoke and intensify sin (Luther's "second use"). (c) The law was added to restrain transgressions (Calvin's "first use"). The Greek charin (χάριν, "for the sake of") is ambiguous between purpose and reason and supports any of these readings.

The phrase "ordained through angels by an intermediary" (3:19) reflects a Second Temple tradition (Jubilees, Josephus Antiquities 15.5.3, Acts 7:38, 53, Hebrews 2:2) that Sinai was mediated through angelic intermediaries. Apocalyptic readers (Martyn) take this as a covert downgrading: Torah is from angels, the promise directly from God. Longenecker and Moo see it as neutral observation.

The baptismal formula of 3:27–28 ("for as many of you as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ. There is neither Jew nor Greek...") is widely held to be a pre-Pauline liturgical formula Paul is citing rather than coining. The triadic structure (Jew/Greek, slave/free, male and female) appears in shorter form at 1 Corinthians 12:13 and Colossians 3:11, with variations. The slight grammatical anomaly — the first two pairs use oude (neither/nor) while the third uses kai (and) — echoes the Genesis 1:27 LXX phrasing arsen kai thēly.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** Patristic readings focused on Abraham-Christ typology and the curse-of-the-tree theme. Marius Victorinus (c. 362) emphasized cosmic soteriology. Jerome read 3:13 as substitutionary. Augustine engaged 3:28 in De Genesi ad Litteram 3.22, generally reading "no male and female" as eschatological without abolishing present role distinctions. Chrysostom treated the paidagōgos image as a pedagogical metaphor. Theodore of Mopsuestia read the law's pedagogical role as preparation for Christ. Aquinas (Galatians lectures c. 1265) integrated 3:10–14 into substitutionary atonement. Luther's 1535 commentary made 3:13 ("Christ became a curse for us") perhaps his most beloved verse — the great exchange. Calvin similarly read substitutionary atonement and emphasized the law's third use (regula vitae).

The Reformation also wrestled with 3:28. Most Reformers read the verse coram Deo (before God) as abolishing distinctions soteriologically without abolishing them in the orders of creation (church, household, society). Anabaptists more often pressed the verse toward visible egalitarianism in the gathered community.

Modern critical scholarship on 3:10–14 has been transformed by Wright's reading of it as Israel's exile curse, by Hays's argument (Echoes of Scripture in the Letters of Paul, 1989) that Paul is reading Habakkuk 2:4 within its larger canonical resonance, and by Sanders's reframing of "covenantal nomism."

The apocalyptic Paul school (Martyn AYB Galatians 1997, Beverly Gaventa, Douglas Campbell, Martinus de Boer) reads chapter 3's law as a temporary cosmic enslaver — one of the stoicheia tou kosmou (4:3) — rather than a benevolent guardian. On Martyn's reading, the paidagōgos of 3:24 is not warmly preparatory but tyrannically enslaving; the verse echoes 4:3's language of enslavement under elemental powers. The grammar of 3:23 (synkleiomenoi, "imprisoned") supports this reading.

The Lutheran-Reformed mainstream (Westerholm, Schreiner ZECNT 2010, Moo BECNT 2013, Frank Thielman) reads chapter 3 as the foundation of the doctrine of justification by faith alone: 3:10–14 establishes the curse-bearing substitutionary atonement; 3:22 establishes universal sinfulness; 3:24 establishes the law's pedagogical preparation for Christ.

The New Perspective (Sanders, Dunn 1993 BNTC, Wright) reads chapter 3 as Paul's argument for an Abrahamic family without ethnic boundary markers. Justification by faith is not primarily about how guilty individuals find peace but about who counts as a member of God's covenant people. Wright's Climax of the Covenant (1991) and Justification (2009) make 3:10–14 a redemptive-historical argument about Israel's exile and Christ's representative bearing of it.

The Paul Within Judaism school (Nanos, Eisenbaum, Fredriksen, Caroline Johnson Hodge's If Sons Then Heirs, 2007) reads chapter 3's Abraham as the ancestor through whom Gentiles are adopted into the family of God without becoming Jews. Hodge develops the kinship anthropology: Abrahamic descent in 3:29 is a real participation in a real ethnic-fictive lineage made possible by Christ as the singular sperma.

Feminist readings of 3:28 are a major modern subfield. Krister Stendahl's small but influential book The Bible and the Role of Women (1966) argued the verse abolishes hierarchy in the orders of redemption and demands its abolition in church order. Phyllis Trible, Beverly Gaventa, Gordon Fee (Discovering Biblical Equality, 2004), and Ben Witherington III have developed the egalitarian reading. The complementarian counter-reading (Schreiner, Carson, John Piper, Wayne Grudem) holds that 3:28 abolishes salvific distinctions but preserves role distinctions in church and household, citing 1 Corinthians 11:3, 1 Timothy 2:11–14, and Ephesians 5:22–33.

**Jewish scholarly critique and its evolution.** Genesis 15:6 was the keystone for both Paul and the rabbis, but the readings diverge sharply. The rabbinic Abraham (Genesis Rabbah 39, b. Nedarim 32a, b. Yoma 28b, Maimonides Hilkhot Avodah Zarah 1) is the Torah-observer-before-Sinai, who in some traditions kept the entire Torah by intuition (Genesis Rabbah 64.4). Rashi's commentary on Genesis 15:6 reads Abraham's faith as itself a meritorious act in the covenant of obedience. The rabbinic Abraham makes proselytes (Genesis Rabbah 39.14 imagines him converting Gentiles). For the rabbis, faith and works are not opposed; faith is the trust that motivates obedience.

Paul's Abraham, by contrast, is the pre-Sinai, pre-circumcision Gentile-believing Abraham, justified by faith before any work of Torah. The hermeneutical move is to treat the chronological priority of Genesis 15 over Genesis 17 as theologically decisive: faith comes first, then circumcision, then Torah.

Joseph Klausner and Hans Joachim Schoeps treated this Abraham as Paul's hellenized misreading. Daniel Boyarin's A Radical Jew (1994) reread Paul as a thoroughly Jewish thinker doing Hellenistic-allegorical exegesis: Paul is universalizing Abrahamic descent by allegorizing the body. Boyarin sees this as both genuinely Jewish (a midrashic reading) and problematic (it dethrones Jewish particularity). James Kugel (How to Read the Bible, 2007) and Jon Levenson (The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son, 1993; Inheriting Abraham, 2012) have detailed the Jewish Abrahamic traditions Paul's reading both draws on and reconfigures.

Mark Nanos and Caroline Johnson Hodge argue that Paul's Abraham is not a polemic against Judaism but an argument for Gentile inclusion as a separate-but-related family. Pamela Eisenbaum (Paul Was Not a Christian, 2009) makes 3:28 about Gentile adoption into Abrahamic descent without becoming Jews. Amy-Jill Levine has shown how this chapter's typology has historically fueled supersessionist readings — Jewish Abraham replaced by Christian Abraham — and how careful readings can recover something more faithful to Paul.

**Convergence and divergence.** All trajectories agree the chapter is foundational for Pauline theology. They diverge on whether (a) it teaches forensic substitutionary atonement and the law's preparatory pedagogy (Lutheran-Reformed); (b) it announces the apocalyptic invasion that ends Torah's cosmic-enslaving function (Martyn); (c) it argues for an Abrahamic family without ethnic boundary markers (New Perspective); (d) it inscribes Gentiles into Israel's promises while leaving Jewish particularity intact (Paul Within Judaism); or (e) it abolishes ethnic-social-gender hierarchies in the new creation (egalitarian feminist).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did Paul believe God was doing in this chapter? He believed God had unveiled (back to apokalypsis from chapter 1) the singular sperma of Abraham — the Messiah Jesus — and through him had opened the Abrahamic promise to the nations. The Spirit poured out at the Galatian conversion was the empirical evidence that God had already received them into Abrahamic blessing. The chapter is not abstract soteriology; it is the defense of an empirical and eschatological reality: these Gentile believers have received the promised Spirit, therefore they are already Abraham's children. To require circumcision now would be to revoke what God had already given.

**Tension 1: The function of the law and the meaning of the paidagōgos.** Was the law a benevolent custodian preparing for Christ, or a tyrannical jailer from whom Christ liberates?

The traditional Reformed reading (Calvin, Schreiner, Moo) takes the paidagōgos positively. The law was God's pedagogical instrument, training Israel and exposing sin until Christ came. Calvin's "third use of the law" extends this positively into the Christian life as the rule of holy living. The law and the gospel are not opposed; they are sequential moments in one redemptive economy. The Catechism of the Catholic Church (CCC 1961–1986) similarly affirms the law as gift, fulfilled in Christ but not abolished.

The Lutheran reading (Luther's 1535 commentary, Westerholm) reads the paidagōgos more harshly. The law's pedagogy is to drive sinners to despair so they will flee to Christ. The law is good in its function but functionally lethal apart from Christ. The Lutheran "second use" of the law (lex semper accusat — the law always accuses) is grounded in this reading. Luther was reluctant to give the law a positive role in Christian life beyond accusing.

The apocalyptic Paul reading (Martyn AYB Galatians, Beverly Gaventa, Douglas Campbell, de Boer) goes further still. The paidagōgos is part of the cosmic-enslaving stoicheia tou kosmou (4:3). The law is associated with the angels of Sinai (3:19), with imprisonment (3:22–23), with the elemental powers from which Christ has rescued. The law is not merely a stage in salvation history but a power from which liberation is needed.

The New Perspective reading (Wright, Dunn) reframes the question. The law was given to Israel, not the human race; its function was to mark out Israel as God's covenant people. The "curse of the law" of 3:10–13 is Israel's exile curse, borne by Christ representatively. With the Messiah's coming, the boundary-marking function ends and the family expands.

The Catholic teaching on the law in Aquinas's Summa I-II.99–108 makes a richer threefold distinction: moral law (decalogue), ceremonial law (cultic), judicial law (civil). Christ fulfills and abrogates the ceremonial and judicial; the moral law remains binding as the natural law promulgated. This distinction was rejected by Luther and Calvin's more unified view of Torah but reaffirmed by Trent.

The Orthodox reading (Lossky, Florovsky) reads the law within the larger arc of theosis: Torah is a partial participation in the divine life, fulfilled and transcended in the union with Christ that the Spirit makes possible.

The steelman of the apocalyptic reading: the chapter's verbs of imprisonment (synkleiomenoi 3:22, ephrouroumetha 3:23), the angelic mediation that downgrades Torah's directness to God (3:19–20), and the explicit naming of the law as "guardian" (paidagōgos, a slave-figure) all weigh heavily on the negative side. The positive Reformed reading has to soften too many of Paul's words. Where this leaves us: Paul's evaluation of Torah is more ambivalent than the Reformed mainstream has wanted to admit and more positive than Martyn's Marcionizing tendency suggests. Romans 7:12 — the law is holy, just, and good — sits in the same Pauline corpus and resists any flat reading of Galatians 3.

**Tension 2: Galatians 3:28 — what does the abolition of distinctions mean?**

The egalitarian reading (Stendahl, Witherington, Gordon Fee, Beverly Gaventa, Phyllis Trible) takes 3:28 as a structural claim about the new creation. The triadic abolition — ethnic, social, and gender — is consistent and total. The verse demands its enactment in church order: women in leadership, slaves freed, ethnic boundaries down. The grammatical echo of Genesis 1:27 LXX (arsen kai thēly) signals that the gendered hierarchy introduced by the fall is reversed in Christ.

The complementarian reading (Schreiner, Carson, Piper, Grudem) accepts that 3:28 abolishes salvific distinctions — all are equally heirs in Christ — without abolishing role distinctions in the orders of creation (church and household). 1 Corinthians 11:3, 1 Timothy 2:11–14, and Ephesians 5:22–33 establish role differentiation that must be read in concert with 3:28.

The Catholic teaching (Inter Insigniores 1976, Ordinatio Sacerdotalis 1994; CCC 1934–1948) similarly affirms equal dignity in baptismal personhood while preserving differentiated roles in priestly office. The Orthodox reading is similar.

The Paul Within Judaism reading (Hodge, Nanos) takes 3:28 as a claim about ethnic adoption into Abrahamic family — Gentiles in Christ are no longer outside, no longer Greek-as-pagan — without abolishing Jewish-Christian ethnic identity in any flat way.

The apocalyptic reading (Martyn, Gaventa) reads 3:28 as new creation: the cosmic categories that structured the old age have been undone, and the new creation makes claims that exceed any present social order's capacity to embody.

The steelman of the complementarian position: the same Paul who wrote 3:28 wrote 1 Corinthians 11 and 14 and (in disputed authorship) the Pastorals. If 3:28 abolishes role distinctions structurally, Paul's other practice in his churches contradicts the principle, which is implausible. The reading that 3:28 abolishes salvific status while preserving differentiated calling holds together better with the rest of the Pauline corpus. Where this leaves us: 3:28 unmistakably announces a reality that has not yet been fully embodied. Whether the gap between announcement and embodiment is permanent (complementarian) or eschatological-call-to-be-actualized (egalitarian) is the live question. The history of the church's expansion of who counts (Gentiles included, slavery abolished as Christian institution after long delay, women's roles still contested) suggests the announcement has demonstrated more force than its early embodiments did.

**One unresolved question.** If Paul argues from Genesis 15:6 that Abraham was justified by faith before circumcision (Genesis 17), and this proves faith is prior to ethnic boundary markers — what theological work is left for Genesis 17's covenant of circumcision once Christ has come, and is its retention within Israel a remnant grace, an obsolete sign, or something the Pauline argument has not finally settled?

## The Thread

**Genesis 15:6.** The single keystone verse Paul cites at 3:6 is the same verse the rabbis treat as central — but they read Abraham's faith as itself a meritorious act of obedience, while Paul reads it as faith reckoned as righteousness apart from works. When you get to Romans 4, remember 3:6: this is the verse Paul builds his entire Abrahamic theology on, twice.

**Habakkuk 2:4.** "The righteous shall live by faith" (3:11) is Paul's other keystone, cited also at Romans 1:17 and quoted in Hebrews 10:38. The original Habakkuk context is the prophet's struggle with God's use of Babylon — the just person waits faithfully for vindication. Paul reads the verse as the principle of justification.

**Deuteronomy 21:23.** "Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree" (3:13) names the scandal of the cross at its most acute. In Second Temple readings (4QpNah, Temple Scroll), this verse was applied to crucifixion. For a Pharisee, "Christ crucified" was prima facie evidence of God's curse — which is exactly why the cross was, as Paul says elsewhere (1 Corinthians 1:23), a stumbling block to Jews. Paul's move at 3:13 is to take that scandal head-on: yes, Christ became a curse, and that is precisely the redemptive event.

**Genesis 12:3.** "In you shall all the nations be blessed" (3:8) is the Abrahamic promise as Paul reads it: a promise of Gentile inclusion, "preached beforehand" to Abraham. When you get to Acts 3:25, remember 3:8: Peter cites the same verse to the Jerusalem crowd, but with a different rhetorical edge. The Abrahamic blessing of the nations is the through-line of the entire New Testament.
