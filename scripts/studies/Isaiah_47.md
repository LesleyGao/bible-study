# Isaiah 47 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Isaiah 47 sits at the structural heart of Second Isaiah's anti-Babylon material. On the mainstream-critical reading (Westermann, Blenkinsopp, Goldingay, Whybray, Tull), the chapter was composed by an anonymous prophet in Babylon around 545–540 BC, in the brief and electrifying interval between Cyrus's rise and his entry into Babylon in October 539 BC. Bernhard Duhm's 1892 commentary fixed this dating as the standard for over a century. The unified-defense tradition (Motyer, Oswalt, Young) reads the chapter as predictive prophecy from the eighth-century Isaiah ben Amoz; Childs's canonical-criticism reads chapter 47 as part of the unified prophetic word of "Isaiah" without forcing a single compositional setting. What every reading recognizes is that chapter 47 is a *taunt-song* (Hebrew *mashal*), a literary form well-attested from Israel's neighbors, addressed to a personified Babylon on the eve of her fall.

The historical context behind the chapter is the rapid disintegration of the Neo-Babylonian Empire in the 540s BC. Nabonidus, the last Babylonian king, had spent ten years at the desert oasis of Tema, neglecting the Marduk cult in Babylon and alienating the city's priesthood — a fact recorded in the **Nabonidus Chronicle** (BM 35382) and in the polemical **Verse Account of Nabonidus** (BM 38299). When Cyrus's army reached the Babylonian heartland in 539 BC, the city fell with minimal resistance. The Greek historians Herodotus (*Histories* 1.190–191) and Xenophon (*Cyropaedia* 7.5) preserve the tradition that Cyrus's army diverted the Euphrates and entered the city under its walls during a festival night — embellishment that overlays the more sober chronicle account. The **Cyrus Cylinder** presents the conquest as Marduk's judgment on Nabonidus and Cyrus's peaceful liberation of Babylon. Isaiah 47 sees the same fall from a third angle: as Yahweh's reckoning on the empire that had destroyed Jerusalem.

The textual witness is firm: the **Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa^a)** from Qumran preserves the full chapter with only orthographic variation from the Masoretic Text. The chapter was settled in its received form by the late second century BC.

The Hebrew vocabulary is built on a single literary device — **personified Babylon as a deposed queen, then a stripped slave, then a widow.** The chapter's signature designation is *batulat-bat-Bavel* (בְּתוּלַת־בַּת־בָּבֶל), *virgin daughter of Babylon* (47:1). The same epithet appears in 47:5 as *bat-Kasdim* (*daughter of the Chaldeans*) and in 47:1 as *gevirah* (גְּבִירָה), *queen-mistress of kingdoms*. These titles draw on the same ancient Near Eastern convention that gives us *bat-Tziyon* (*daughter of Zion*) for Jerusalem in the prophetic and lament literature: cities are imagined as women, and the fate of the city is dramatized through the body of the personified woman.

Two doublets carry the chapter's theological weight. **Babylon's claim** in 47:8, 10 — *ani ve-afsi od* (אֲנִי וְאַפְסִי עוֹד), *I am, and there is no one besides me* — is a deliberate parody of the divine self-declaration in 45:5, 6, 18, 21, 22 (*ani Yahweh ve-ein od*, *I am the LORD, and there is no other*). Babylon has stolen Yahweh's signature line. **Yahweh's response** in 47:4 names himself the *go'el* — *our Redeemer, the LORD of hosts is his name, the Holy One of Israel* (Hebrew *go'alenu Yahweh tzeva'ot shemo, qedosh Yisra'el*). *Go'el* is the kinsman-redeemer language of Israelite family law (Leviticus 25, Ruth 4), now applied to Yahweh's vindication of his exiled people.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Chapter 47 is a single, sustained taunt-song against personified Babylon, structured in five parts. **Verses 1–4** open with the deposition: come down from the throne, sit in the dust, the queen has become a slave-girl. **Verses 5–7** indict Babylon's hubris and her cruelty to Israel: *I gave them into your hand; you showed them no mercy*. **Verses 8–9** announce the doom: the things she said could not happen — widowhood and loss of children — will come on her in a single day. **Verses 10–11** name the specific charge: *you said, "no one sees me,"* and the disaster will come without warning. **Verses 12–15** mock Babylon's astrologers and sorcerers: let them stand forth and save you.

The taunt-song (*mashal*) is the ancient Near Eastern literary form for ridicule of fallen powers, with parallels in the Egyptian "Prophecy of Neferti," the Mesopotamian *Lament for Ur*, and inside the Hebrew Bible in Numbers 21:27–30, Isaiah 14 (against the king of Babylon), Isaiah 23 (against Tyre), Ezekiel 27 (against Tyre), and Nahum (against Nineveh). Isaiah 47 is, alongside Nahum, the most sustained example of the form in the prophetic literature.

The literary architecture exploits the gendered city-personification convention to maximum effect. Babylon is imagined as a queen who must come down from the throne (47:1), who must take up the slave-woman's millstone (47:2), who must remove her veil and uncover her legs to wade through the river (47:2–3) — the iconography of a captive woman taken into exile. The convention is harsh and is meant to be: this is the pictorial language of imperial overthrow as the ancient world knew it.

The two-part *I am, and there is no one besides me* of verse 8 and verse 10 functions as the chapter's pivot. Babylon has not only oppressed Israel; she has claimed for herself the divine prerogative of self-existence and incomparability. The chapter's fundamental charge is not cruelty but blasphemy. The astrology-and-sorcery section in verses 12–13 is concrete: *those who divide the heavens, who gaze at the stars, who at the new moons make known what shall come upon you*. This is recognizable as Babylonian celestial divination — the practice attested in cuneiform texts like *Enuma Anu Enlil*, the great Babylonian astrological compendium. The chapter's final image is searing: the astrologers are like stubble that the fire consumes; *no coal for warming oneself is this, no fire to sit before* (47:14).

## Lens 3: Inside the Larger Story

**Within the prophetic canon**, Isaiah 47 belongs to the cluster of oracles-against-the-nations that punctuate the prophetic books. Its closest companions are Isaiah 13 (also against Babylon, in First Isaiah), Isaiah 14:4–23 (the king-of-Babylon taunt), Jeremiah 50–51 (the longest prophetic anti-Babylon material, with extensive verbal contact with Isaiah 47), and the entire book of Nahum (against Nineveh). The chapter's nearest narrative companion in the New Testament is Revelation 17–18, which lifts the personified-Babylon convention directly out of the prophetic tradition to indict Rome.

**Jewish trajectory**: The chapter became a foundational text for Jewish reflection on the fall of empires and the limits of imperial pride. The rabbinic tradition reads chapter 47 alongside Esther and Daniel as part of the larger biblical-historical argument that empires that abuse Israel come to ruin. *Babylon* in this trajectory becomes a typological designation for any oppressive empire — Rome in the Talmudic period (where *Bavel* is sometimes a code-word for Rome), and later for any persecuting power. The *go'el* declaration of 47:4 became one of the central kinsman-redeemer texts in Jewish liturgical theology. Modern Jewish biblical scholarship (Levenson, Sommer) reads the chapter as a deeply anti-imperial text, one that takes the side of the dispossessed against the boasting of the powerful.

**Christian trajectory**: The chapter's most enduring afterlife in Christian tradition is the *Babylon* archetype that runs through Revelation. *Mystery, Babylon the great, mother of prostitutes* (Revelation 17:5) is built on the literary convention Isaiah 47 helped establish. Augustine in *City of God* uses *Babylon* as his term for the *civitas terrena*, the city of self-love, in opposition to the *civitas Dei*, the city of God. The Reformers (Luther's *Babylonian Captivity of the Church*, Calvin's commentaries) drew on Isaiah 47 typologically to indict Rome — for Luther, against the medieval papal church; the same trope was later inverted in Catholic polemic against Reformation centers. The chapter's anti-astrology polemic (47:12–13) was cited heavily in Christian engagement with astrology and divination through the Patristic period (Augustine's *Confessions* 4.3, *On Christian Doctrine* 2.21–24) and in Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, Q. 95). Catholic teaching on divination is summarized in CCC §2115–2117, which draws on the prophetic anti-divination tradition (including Isaiah 47) to forbid all forms of recourse to occult means of knowing the future.

**NT echoes**: The most direct echoes are in Revelation 18, where the *fallen, fallen is Babylon the great* (Revelation 18:2) and the *I sit as queen, and I am no widow, and I shall never see grief* (Revelation 18:7) directly mirror Isaiah 47:7–8. Paul's polemic in 1 Corinthians 1–2 against the *wisdom of this age* echoes the indictment of Babylon's *wisdom and knowledge* in Isaiah 47:10. The *no one sees me* of Babylon (47:10) is the negative pole that frames Jesus' teaching on the Father who *sees in secret* in Matthew 6.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — Where the Tensions Sit

Three tensions deserve honest engagement.

**First, the gendered taunt-song and modern critical reception.** Isaiah 47 personifies Babylon as a woman who is stripped, exposed, and sexually shamed. Feminist biblical scholarship (Phyllis Trible, *Texts of Terror*, 1984; Athalya Brenner, *A Feminist Companion to the Latter Prophets*; Carol Newsom; Susan Ackerman) has pressed hard on the moral question of how prophetic literature gendered enemies. The pattern across the prophets is that female-personified enemy cities are violated, exposed, and shamed, while male-personified enemy kings are dethroned and killed; the gendering is not incidental but structural. The honest steelman of the critique is that the prophetic taunt-song convention drew on, and reinforced, a cultural script in which a defeated city's humiliation was imagined through the violated body of a woman. The honest counter from defenders of the canonical tradition (Iain Provan, Daniel Block, Alec Motyer) is that the prophet is not endorsing the script; he is using a recognizable literary convention to indict an empire that had visited literal sexual violence on the women of Jerusalem in 586 BC. The image is no harsher than what Babylon's army actually did, and the chapter is not a celebration but a prophetic vindication of those who had been so violated. Both observations are true. The chapter cannot be made comfortable for a modern reader — it should not be — and it must also be read in the genre and context in which it was given. The prophetic taunt-song is a form that took the rhetoric of imperial conquest and turned it back against the conqueror. That does not exempt the form from critique; it does mean that critique should engage the form on its own terms.

**Second, the anti-astrology polemic and modern reception.** Verses 12–13 mock Babylon's astrologers — *those who divide the heavens, who gaze at the stars, who at the new moons make known what shall come upon you* — as utterly powerless to save Babylon from her doom. The polemic is sharp and specific: this is not generic critique but engagement with the actual celestial-divination practices of Babylon, which are well-documented in cuneiform sources. Catholic teaching (CCC §2115–2117) takes Isaiah 47 as one of the foundational texts in its prohibition of astrology, divination, horoscopes, and recourse to mediums. Jewish halakhic tradition (Maimonides, *Hilkhot Avodah Zarah* 11:8–16) has similarly forbidden astrology and divination, with Isaiah 47 in the warrant-stack. The honest steelman of a sympathetic reading of ancient astrology is that Babylonian celestial divination was, in its own context, a serious intellectual project that contributed to the development of mathematical astronomy. Isaiah 47 is not engaging that achievement on its scientific merits. The prophet's claim is theological: the future is not something to be read off the stars but something Yahweh declares (recall 46:10 — *declaring the end from the beginning*). The contrast is between *Yahweh as the speaker of the future* and *the astrologers as readers of signs* — and the prophet's argument is that the readers cannot save themselves, while the speaker can save his people.

**Third, the *I gave them into your hand* of 47:6.** *I was angry with my people; I profaned my heritage; I gave them into your hand; you showed them no mercy.* This verse is theologically dense and resists every easy reading. (a) On the **Reformed sovereignty** reading (Calvin, Edwards), Yahweh genuinely *gave* Israel into Babylonian hands as judgment, but Babylon's cruelty was its own free moral act and remains fully culpable — God's sovereign use of an instrument does not excuse the instrument. (b) On the **mainstream-critical** reading (Westermann, Goldingay), the verse is the prophet's theological reckoning with the otherwise unbearable fact of the exile: *Yahweh did this, and yet Babylon's overreach is now being judged*. (c) On the **two-witnesses** reading running back to the Patristic period, both moves of the verse are simultaneously affirmed: God's judgment is real, and the human agent's cruelty is genuinely culpable. The honest position is that 47:6 refuses to absolve Babylon by appeal to divine sovereignty. Yahweh's anger with his people did not license Babylon's cruelty to the aged; the *yoke exceedingly heavy* (47:6) is the specific charge for which Babylon is now being judged. The text holds together what later theology has often tried to keep apart — God's sovereign use of a wicked agent and the agent's full moral responsibility.

**Where this leaves us**: Isaiah 47 is the chapter that puts limits on imperial confidence. Babylon said *I am, and there is no one besides me*, and the chapter's announcement is that this self-deification is the precise charge that brings the empire down. The chapter is brutal in its imagery and unsparing in its mockery. It also tells the truth: empires that abuse the weak, claim divine status for themselves, and trust their charts and counselors to save them, end up sitting in the dust.

**Closing question**: Babylon's two great errors were saying *I am, and there is no one besides me* and saying *no one sees me*. Which of these errors is more characteristic of the imperial confidence of our own time, and what would it look like to repent of it?
