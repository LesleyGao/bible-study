# Ecclesiastes 2: The Royal Experiment

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 2 is the centerpiece of what Choon-Leong Seow (Anchor, 1997) calls the "royal autobiography" or "fictive royal testament" — a literary form Qohelet borrows from a Mesopotamian and Egyptian convention in which a king (real or imagined) reflects on the failure of his own ambitions. The closest ANE parallels Seow cites are the *Cuthean Legend of Naram-Sin* (a Sargonic king's pseudonymous deathbed reflection on imperial overreach) and the Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemhat I* (a posthumous warning from a murdered pharaoh). Within this convention, Solomon is the natural Israelite candidate; 1 Kings 3–11 had already supplied the canonical inventory of unsurpassed wisdom, building projects, gardens, treasure, and women. Qohelet adopts the persona without ever naming Solomon, and at 2:9 strikes the precise note the convention requires: "I became great and surpassed all who were before me in Jerusalem."

The vocabulary of 2:4–8 is itself the strongest dating evidence for the book. The catalog of royal works includes *bayit* (houses), *kerem* (vineyards), *gan* (gardens), *pardes* (parks — Persian *pairi-daēza*, the loan that the Septuagint will use to translate Eden's *gan* and that gives English "paradise"), *bereka* (pools), *ya'ar* (forest), and an inventory of male and female slaves, livestock, silver, gold, "the treasure of kings and provinces" (*segullat melakhim ve-ha-medinot* — *medinot* being a Persian administrative term for satrapal districts), male and female singers, and *shiddah ve-shiddot* (2:8) — a notorious philological crux usually translated "concubines" or "harem" but which Seow (with the Targum's *karkei karkin*, "carriages") reads as a Persian-period luxury good. Every one of these terms belongs more comfortably in the world of Achaemenid Yehud than in the 10th-century united monarchy. Seow, Fox, Murphy, and Crenshaw concur: the *content* of Solomon's world has been refurnished in 5th–4th century BC vocabulary.

The economic and social world implied is significant. The royal builder commands male and female slaves (*'avadim ve-shefahot*) including those born in the house — the standard ANE three-generation slavery vocabulary — alongside herds and flocks. Silver and gold are not merely possessed but *gathered* (*kanas*), a verb that Seow notes is characteristic of Persian-period economic prose for the accumulation of monetized wealth. The "treasure of kings and provinces" reflects a tributary system; the "singers, men and women" reflect court musical culture analogous to that of the Persian court (compare Esther's banquets). The harem reference (whatever *shiddah* finally means) reflects Achaemenid royal practice. The portrait is not Solomon's Jerusalem but a Persian-period imagining of what unlimited royal power *would* look like, projected onto a Solomonic frame.

The textual basis is again well-established. The Qumran witnesses (4QQoh-a, 4QQoh-b, c. 175–150 BC) preserve substantially the MT for chapter 2; the LXX renders literally; the Targum to Eccl 2 is heavily expansive, transforming the royal experiment into a moral exemplum (the Solomon of Targum Qohelet repents at 2:11). Jerome's Vulgate fixes *vanitas* in 2:11 as the chapter's interpretive keyword for the Latin West.

Key Hebrew terms: *simchah* (שִׂמְחָה, "joy/pleasure," 2:1, 2:10) — the first appearance of what will become a central theological category; *amal* (עָמָל, "toil/labor," six times in this chapter) — Qohelet's signature word for human productive effort, with overtones of suffering (cf. Job's *'amal*); *yitron* (יִתְרוֹן, 2:11, 2:13) returning from the prologue's diagnostic question; *miqreh* (מִקְרֶה, "fate/event/chance," 2:14, 2:15) — a metaphysically loaded term that Seow notes is virtually unique to Qohelet in the Hebrew Bible; and *helek* (חֵלֶק, "portion/share," 2:10, 2:21) — the language of inheritance, here applied to what one gets *out of* one's labor before death takes it.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 2 unfolds in four clearly demarcated movements. The pleasure experiment (2:1–11): Qohelet tries laughter, wine, building, gardens, slaves, treasure, music, harem, declares the result *hevel u-re'ut ruach*. The wisdom-folly meditation (2:12–17): finding that "more gain in wisdom than in folly" still does not save the wise from the same *miqreh* as the fool, Qohelet "hated life." The toil-and-inheritance lament (2:18–23): you cannot guarantee whether the heir who inherits your accumulated wealth will be wise or fool, so the wisdom you put into accumulation is itself *hevel*. And the first carpe diem refrain (2:24–26): "There is nothing better for a person than that he should eat and drink and find enjoyment in his toil."

The carpe diem refrain is the book's most consequential literary device — and the most contested. It will recur eight times across Ecclesiastes (2:24, 3:12–13, 3:22, 5:18, 8:15, 9:7–9, 11:8, 11:9), often after a stretch of pessimistic reasoning, and the question of whether these refrains are *pious affirmations* or *ironic resignations* is the major interpretive division in the book. The 2:24 form reads: "There is nothing better for a man than that he should eat and drink and find enjoyment in his toil. This also, I saw, is from the hand of God." Seow and Murphy treat this as a genuine theological assertion: enjoyment is a gift, named so by Qohelet himself. Fox and Crenshaw read it as resigned: *en tov*, "there is nothing better" — a comparative that could equally mean "there is no other option." The Hebrew *gam-zoh ra'iti ki mi-yad ha-elohim hi*' is sometimes translated "this also I saw is from the hand of God" (theological affirmation) and sometimes "even this I saw — that it comes from God's hand" (sardonic concession — even pleasure is not autonomous).

The literary architecture is intentional. The chapter performs the breakdown of the wisdom project from inside. Qohelet *applies* wisdom, *acquires* wisdom, *uses* wisdom in his pleasure experiment ("my wisdom remained with me," 2:9), and the wisdom is what generates the diagnosis that *all* of it — including the wisdom — is *hevel*. The verb sequence is striking: Qohelet *makes* (fourteen first-person *qal* and *piel* verbs in 2:4–8, which the Bible commentator Robert Alter calls "the most relentlessly first-person passage in the Hebrew Bible") and then *considers* (*panah*, 2:11, 2:12), and the considering is what ruins the making.

Devices include: leitwort *amal* (toil) hammered through the chapter; the "I" pronoun *ani* repeated for emphasis (2:1, 2:9, 2:11, 2:12, 2:14, 2:18, 2:20); the *miqreh-ehad* construct (2:14, "one fate") as the metaphysical hammer; and the inheritance-vexation passage (2:18–21) deploying *sin'ti* ("I hated") twice and *ya'ash* ("I gave up to despair") in the most emotionally exposed verbs Qohelet uses. The chapter ends with *gam-zeh hevel u-re'ut ruach* (2:26) — the same formula that closed the experiment at 2:11 and the wisdom-folly meditation at 2:17, three uses of *hevel u-re'ut ruach* structuring the chapter's three despairs.

Textual variant of note: 2:25 — *mi yokal u-mi yahush huts mimmenni* ("who can eat or who can hasten apart from me"). The MT *mimmenni* ("from me") is awkward; LXX, Peshitta, and Vulgate read *mimmennu* ("from him," referring to God), which most modern translations follow. The variant matters theologically: if the MT is right, Qohelet is briefly speaking *as God* (or quoting God); if the versions are right, Qohelet is asserting that no one can eat or enjoy apart from God.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The royal experiment of Eccl 2 sits inside a canonical conversation about the wisdom-and-wealth of kings. 1 Kings 3–11 narrates the Solomonic original: the divine grant of wisdom, the temple and palace projects, the gardens of *pardesim* (1 Kgs 4:33's catalog of Solomon's botanical knowledge), the silver "as common as stones in Jerusalem" (1 Kgs 10:27), the seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines, and the eventual collapse of the project into idolatry and dynastic fracture. The *Deuteronomic* king-law (Deut 17:14–20) had warned against exactly this: a king must not multiply horses, wives, or silver and gold. Eccl 2 reads, in effect, like Solomon writing the post-mortem the books of Kings refused to write — what it was actually like, from inside, to violate the king-law and discover that the violation produced *hevel*.

The Jewish interpretive trajectory takes the royal experiment seriously as Solomonic, even when the rabbis privately knew the chronology problem. The Targum to Eccl 2 turns Solomon into a repentant king who has now learned the lesson; *Qohelet Rabbah* and the *Bavli* (Gittin 68a–b, Sanhedrin 20b) elaborate folk-legend material about Solomon's loss and recovery of the throne, reading Eccl 2's "I gave my heart up to despair" as autobiographical for the demoted king. Maimonides (*Guide* 3.51) reads the chapter as Solomon's philosophical recognition that *empirical* satiation cannot satisfy the rational soul. Ibn Ezra reads carefully but takes the persona at face value. Robert Gordis (*Koheleth*, 1951) modernizes: the royal experiment is a *literary device* by which Qohelet names the universal human experience that even the maximally resourced life produces no surplus. Ibn Caspi and Ralbag both read Eccl 2 as an ethical demonstration of the futility of bodily goods.

Christian patristic reading allegorizes hard. Gregory of Nyssa's *Homilies on Ecclesiastes* (Homily 3 covers 2:1–11, Homily 4 covers 2:12–26) treats each item in Solomon's catalog as a class of vain desires the soul must renounce: vineyards as drunkenness, gardens as carnal beauty, the harem as concupiscence, the silver and gold as avarice. Jerome's commentary (388, addressed to Blesilla) reads the same way — the chapter is a *contemptus mundi* document. Hugh of St. Victor and the *Glossa Ordinaria* extend the allegory; Bonaventure adds a Franciscan inflection (the chapter validates voluntary poverty); Aquinas reads Eccl 2 in the *Summa* (II-II q. 119) as the locus classicus for the vice of avarice.

The Reformation breaks differently. Luther's *Notes on Ecclesiastes* (1526/1532) loves chapter 2 and reads it as gospel: the royal experiment proves *sola gratia* — works (even royal works) cannot produce justification, and the *hevel* is precisely what drives the heart to grace. Luther reads 2:24's "this is from the hand of God" as the chapter's evangelical center: the gift of joy in toil is *Gabe*, not *Aufgabe*, gift not task. Calvin again is silent. Puritan readings (Cawdry, Bridges) extract sober moral lessons. Critical scholarship from Delitzsch (1875) onward distinguishes the descriptive from the prescriptive: Eccl 2 *describes* what unlimited resources produce, without prescribing renunciation.

The most consequential modern division is over the carpe diem refrain. Murphy (Catholic, Word, 1992), Provan (Reformed, NIVAC), and Ellen Davis read 2:24–26 as a genuine theological affirmation — joy in eating, drinking, and meaningful work is a divine gift that Qohelet recommends. Fox (*A Time to Tear Down*) and Crenshaw (OTL) read the same verses as resigned hedonism — *given that* nothing else works, you might as well eat. Longman (NICOT) reads with the epilogist-as-disapproving frame: 2:24–26 is Qohelet's failed conclusion, which the canonical narrator ultimately corrects at 12:13. Peter Enns reads the refrain as both/and: a real but partial good in a *hevel* world. Walter Brueggemann reads it as covenantal: the gift-language reasserts a creator-creature relationship that the experiment had threatened to dissolve.

The deep convergence: Jewish, Catholic, and Lutheran readings all want to make Eccl 2 *constructive* — the royal experiment teaches us something positive about creaturely life. The deep divergence: how much of that constructive teaching is *Qohelet's* and how much is the *epilogist's* (or the canon's). Longman's reading represents the limit case where Qohelet's voice is *opposed* to the canonical voice; Gordis represents the limit case where they coincide.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Was the royal experiment a *demonstration* of the futility of the wisdom-and-wealth life, or a *failure* of pursuit by Qohelet himself?** This may be the most consequential interpretive choice in the chapter, because it determines whether the reader is meant to *learn from* Qohelet's experiment or *learn against* it.

The demonstration-reading (Gordis, Murphy, Seow, Fox, the Jewish mainstream and most critical scholarship) takes Qohelet at his word in 2:9 — "my wisdom remained with me." The royal experiment is *competently executed*. Qohelet really did acquire the gardens, the slaves, the silver, the wisdom; he really did "keep his heart from no pleasure" (2:10) while remaining a wisdom practitioner; and the discovery that *even this* yields *hevel* is therefore a discovery about the *limits of the wisdom-and-wealth life as such*, not a discovery about Qohelet's particular failures. On this reading, Eccl 2 is a controlled experiment whose negative result is a negative result for the whole class of human projects it represents.

The failure-reading is articulated most aggressively by Tremper Longman (NICOT, 1998). On Longman's view, the epilogist of 12:9–14 disapproves of Qohelet, and the royal experiment of chapter 2 is the centerpiece of the disapproval: Qohelet has tried to find meaning *under the sun* — the very phrase that names autonomy from God — and has predictably failed. The catalog of royal pleasures is presented not as a representative human pursuit but as an idolatrous one (note the Deuteronomic king-law violations); its failure is moral, not metaphysical. The "this is from the hand of God" of 2:24 is, on this reading, a piece of orthodoxy Qohelet bumps into and then immediately retracts ("this also is *hevel*," 2:26), which proves that even Qohelet's positive moments are unstable.

The genuine steelman for Longman's view is that Ecclesiastes really does have a *frame* (1:1–11 + 12:9–14 are third-person; the body is first-person), and the frame really is more pious than the body. Christian readers from Gregory of Nyssa onward have read the body through the frame. The Jewish *Bavli Shabbat 30b* admits that the rabbis "sought to suppress the book… because its words are mutually contradictory" — precisely the textual datum Longman exploits. If the frame and the body disagree, the canonical voice is the frame's, and the frame's voice condemns the experiment.

The counter-steelman for the demonstration-reading: the chapter's energies are too fully invested in Qohelet's first-person exploration for the experiment to be presented as merely *bad* exploration. Qohelet is treated as a serious investigator, and his findings — including the carpe diem refrain — are integrated into the book's developing argument rather than abandoned as failed positions. Fox and Seow argue that the book's theological work is done *by* Qohelet, not *against* him. *Where this leaves us*: chapter 2 is genuinely undecidable on this question without committing to a prior framework about the relationship of frame to body, and the book seems to want it that way.

**Tension 2: Is the carpe diem refrain a *gift acknowledgment* or *resigned hedonism*?** Eccl 2:24–26 is the first occurrence of what will become the book's most repeated formula. The interpretive options are theologically distinct and the chapter's stakes are high.

The gift-acknowledgment reading (Murphy, Provan, Ellen Davis, Lutheran tradition) takes "this is from the hand of God" (2:24) as a real theological assertion: ordinary embodied life — eating, drinking, meaningful work — is a divine *Gabe*. The Reformed/Lutheran inflection emphasizes *common grace*: the gift is real even for those outside covenant, even in a *hevel* world. The Catholic inflection (extending Aquinas on the natural goods) reads the same passage as the locus classicus for the *integrity of secular goods* — they are real goods, properly ordered, when received as gifts rather than grasped as projects. Iain Provan adds a creation-theological dimension: the eat-drink-work gift restores the creature-Creator relationship Genesis 1–2 had instituted, *despite* the Fall's *hevel*.

The resigned-hedonism reading (Fox, Crenshaw, Whybray) reads *en tov ba-adam* ("there is nothing better for a man") as a comparative of resignation. *Given* that the universe yields no *yitron*, *given* that the wise dies like the fool, *given* that the heir may waste the inheritance — the only viable course is small-scale satisfaction. The "from the hand of God" is not a confident theological claim but Qohelet's slightly tired acknowledgment that even this minimal good is not autonomously available. The Hellenistic parallels (Stoic *ataraxia*, Epicurean modest pleasure, Siduri's tavern speech to Gilgamesh) are decisive on this reading: Qohelet is recommending what an honest pagan would recommend.

The third position — Brueggemann, Enns, Davis — reads the refrain *covenantally*: the gift-language reasserts the creature-Creator relationship that the experiment had threatened to dissolve, but the gift is bounded by *hevel*; it is real and small and not eschatologically extensible. This reading splits the difference: Fox is right that the refrain does not solve the problem, but Murphy is right that it is a real theological affirmation rather than mere resignation. *Where this leaves us*: 2:24 is not settled by the lexicon alone — it requires a reading of Qohelet's overall project — and any teacher of this book has to make a choice that cannot be hidden behind translation.

**One unresolved question.** If "to the one who pleases [God] God has given wisdom and knowledge and joy, but to the sinner he has given the business of gathering and collecting, only to give to one who pleases God" (2:26) — which appears to assert exactly the retributive logic Qohelet elsewhere denies — then is this verse Qohelet's *concession* to the conventional wisdom he has otherwise dismantled, the *epilogist's* gloss, or Qohelet's *bitter irony* (the "sinner" gathers wealth he won't enjoy, just as Qohelet has)?

## The Thread

The chapter's most productive cross-references run sideways into the canonical wisdom debate. *Proverbs 13:22* — "a good man leaves an inheritance to his children's children, but the sinner's wealth is laid up for the righteous" — sets up exactly the retributive frame Qohelet's 2:26 echoes; Eccl 2's prior verses (2:18–21) had just denied that you can know the moral character of your heir at all. *Job 27:13–17* — "the wicked man's portion… he may pile up silver like dust, but the just will wear it" — uses identical imagery; Job affirms the retributive logic Qohelet doubts, then gets his own demonstrative experience that complicates it. *Psalm 49:10* — "the wise die; the fool and the stupid alike must perish and leave their wealth to others" — is Eccl 2:14–18 in psalmic compression, and Seow argues this Psalm is one of Qohelet's likely sources. The forward echo to *Luke 12:16–21*, the parable of the rich fool ("you fool! This night your soul is required of you, and the things you have prepared, whose will they be?"), takes Eccl 2:18–21's heir-anxiety and dramatizes it under eschatological judgment — Jesus reads Qohelet's *hevel* into a parable about treasure stored up *eis Theon*. And *Sirach 14:3–4, 14:11–19* (Ben Sira) explicitly reworks Eccl 2:18–21, softening the heir-anxiety into a recommendation to enjoy the gift before death — Sirach knows Qohelet, and his moralizing edits show what an orthodox second-century BC sage thought needed correcting.
