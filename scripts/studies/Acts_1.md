# Acts 1: The Ascension, the Lot, and the Programmatic Geography of Witness

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Acts opens in the reign of Tiberius (14–37 CE), in a Jerusalem still operating under the prefecture system that would shortly pass to Caligula (37–41) and then Claudius (41–54). The events of Acts 1 — Jesus's post-resurrection appearances, the ascension from the Mount of Olives, and the gathering in an upper room — sit in the brief window between Passover c. 30 (or 33) CE and Shavuot of the same year, while Pontius Pilate was still prefect, Caiaphas was still high priest, and Herod Antipas still tetrarch in Galilee. The Sanhedrin under Caiaphas (whose ossuary, discovered in 1990 in the Peace Forest south of Jerusalem and bearing the inscription *Yehosef bar Qayafa*, anchors the priestly family in archaeological reality) governed temple affairs; Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes, Zealots, and the more amorphous Herodians comprised the religious-political ecology into which the Jesus movement was emerging. Joachim Jeremias's *Jerusalem in the Time of Jesus* remains the foundational reconstruction of the city's economy, demography, and class structure, and Eilat Mazar's City of David excavations have continued to refine our picture of first-century Jerusalem topography.

The "upper room" (***hyperōon***, ὑπερῷον, 1:13) of 1:13 fits the well-documented architectural pattern of Jerusalem priestly and well-to-do houses with a second-story chamber suitable for gatherings — Jeremias calculated such rooms could hold several dozen people. Christian tradition since the fourth century has located this upper room on the western hill (today's "Mount Zion"), at the site of the Cenacle, though the present Crusader-era structure cannot be tied directly to the first century. The ascension on the Mount of Olives (***to oros tōn elaiōn***, τὸ ὄρος τῶν ἐλαιῶν, 1:12) places the event on the eschatologically loaded ridge of Zechariah 14:4, where the Lord "will stand on the Mount of Olives" on the day of his coming — a deliberate Lukan placement that Tannehill reads as binding ascension to parousia. The "Sabbath day's journey" (about 2,000 cubits, c. 1.1 km) reflects rabbinic *eruv* halakhah codified in the Mishnah (*Eruvin* 4–5), a precise topographic note characteristic of Luke.

The Field of Blood / **Akeldama** (***Hakeldama***, Ἁκελδαμά, 1:19, transliterated from Aramaic *ḥaqal dema*) has been identified since the Byzantine period with a site in the Hinnom Valley south of the Old City, where Crusader-era charnel houses still stand over earlier burial grounds — the location accords with the tradition of a potter's field (cf. Matt 27:7) used for burial of strangers.

Textually, Acts is the book where the textual-critical stakes are highest. **P74** (seventh century, Bodmer collection) is our earliest substantial witness; the great fourth-century uncials Vaticanus (B) and Sinaiticus (ℵ) preserve the Alexandrian text, while **Codex Bezae (D)**, fifth century, preserves the so-called **Western Text**, which in Acts runs roughly 8.5% longer than the Alexandrian — the most significant textual divergence in the New Testament. Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary* and Eldon Epp's *The Theological Tendency of Codex Bezae Cantabrigiensis in Acts* are the foundational guides; Acts 1 itself contains comparatively minor Western expansions, but the question shadows the entire book.

A few Greek terms repay attention. ***Anelēmphthē*** (ἀνελήμφθη, 1:2, 11, 22), "was taken up," is the Septuagintal verb used for Elijah's translation in 2 Kings 2:11 — Luke is signaling typology, not merely chronicling. ***Martyres*** (μάρτυρες, 1:8, 22), "witnesses," still carries here its primary forensic-testimonial meaning rather than the later technical sense of "martyr"; the semantic drift from witness-by-word to witness-by-blood is one of Acts's narrative arcs. ***Klēros*** (κλῆρος, 1:17, 25, 26), "lot/share/inheritance," becomes the operative term for Matthias's selection. And ***epiphanestatēn*** is not in the chapter, but ***basileia*** (βασιλεία, 1:3, 6) — the kingdom Jesus speaks of for forty days, the kingdom the disciples ask about restoring — is the disputed center.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Henry Cadbury's *The Making of Luke-Acts* (1927) established the foundational claim that Luke and Acts form a single two-volume work in the tradition of Greco-Roman historiography — a *bios*-plus-history hybrid addressed to an unknown patron Theophilus and following the prefatory and structural conventions of Hellenistic historians. Loveday Alexander's *The Preface to Luke's Gospel* refined this by locating the Lukan prefaces in the more specific genre of scientific and technical prose (medical, mathematical, military manuals), softening Cadbury's straight-line analogy to Thucydides and Polybius. Daniel Marguerat's *The First Christian Historian* presses further: Acts is apologetic historiography, written to legitimate the church to itself and to outsiders. Richard Pervo's *Profit With Delight* (and later *Acts: A Commentary* in Hermeneia, 2009) makes the controversial counter-case that Acts is closer to ancient novel than to historiography — entertaining, episodic, fictionalizing — and on that basis Pervo dates Acts to the early second century, against the more widely held 80–90 CE consensus.

The opening verses of Acts 1 enact Lukan literary signatures with unusual density. The address to Theophilus (1:1, "in the first book, O Theophilus") explicitly inverts back to Luke 1:3, creating the inclusio that justifies reading the two volumes as one work — Cadbury's structural claim turns on this verse. The forty-day post-resurrection period (1:3) is unparalleled in the other Gospels and creates the chronological space Luke needs between resurrection and Pentecost, but it sits in apparent tension with Luke 24:50–53, where the ascension reads as occurring on Easter day itself. Cadbury, Tannehill, and Marguerat all argue that the discrepancy is narrative-theological rather than historical: Luke 24 closes one volume with a heaven-ascent that gives the Gospel its rounded ending; Acts 1 opens the second volume with the same event re-narrated in expanded form to launch the new arc. Bruce, Marshall, and Hemer attempt harmonization (the Luke 24 account telescopes events for narrative compression); critical scholars from Haenchen onward treat the two accounts as Luke's deliberate, theologically motivated redoubling.

Acts 1:8 — "you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you, and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth" — is the **programmatic verse**, and on this Tannehill's *The Narrative Unity of Luke-Acts* vol. 2 is foundational. The geography of the verse maps the structure of the book: Acts 1–7 in Jerusalem; 8–12 in Judea and Samaria; 13–28 in the diaspora reaching Rome (the *peras tēs gēs*, "end of the earth," that for first-century Mediterranean readers might mean Spain, Ethiopia, or simply Rome as the imperial center). The five major narrative panels Tannehill identifies, each closing with a summary statement, are visible already in the close of chapter 1 (the gathering and prayer in 1:14) and will recur with slight variation through the book.

The "**we passages**" (16:10–17, 20:5–21:18, 27:1–28:16) are absent from Acts 1 but are the primary internal evidence for the traditional authorship debate that begins to be relevant the moment one asks who wrote 1:1. Vernon Robbins's *By Land and By Sea* argued that the first-person plural is a sea-voyage convention rather than authorial signature; Stanley Porter's *The Paul of Acts* defends the traditional reading; Bauckham's *Eyewitnesses* extends the argument that the "we" tracks an actual companion source, possibly the Lukan author himself. The chapter's listing of the eleven (1:13) and its careful enumeration of "120" persons in 1:15 reflects what Marguerat calls Luke's "documentary aesthetic" — the accumulation of names, numbers, and topographical data that lends apologetic-historical weight.

Speeches comprise roughly 30% of Acts, and Peter's address in 1:16–22 is the first. Cadbury's classical thesis was that Luke composed his speeches in the manner of Thucydides (1.22), supplying what *would have been said* with rhetorical fidelity to the situation; Marshall and Witherington argue Luke also preserves pre-Lukan tradition, especially in the kerygmatic speeches; Marguerat and Conzelmann emphasize the speeches' Lukan-theological function. Peter's first speech is short and procedural — citing Psalm 69:25 and Psalm 109:8 — but it sets the pattern of scripture-citation-then-application that will dominate the longer speeches to come.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical arc, Acts 1 is the hinge. The forty days of resurrection appearances and kingdom-teaching close the Gospel narrative; the ten days of waiting open onto Pentecost; and 1:8's geography programs the whole book. Luke's redactional decisions here — re-narrating the ascension, expanding the post-resurrection window, framing the apostolic mission as Spirit-empowered witness — are doing canonical work, knitting the Gospel to the missionary expansion that Paul's letters (already in circulation by Luke's writing on most datings) had assumed without narrating.

**Jewish interpretive trajectories**. The early Jewish encounter with the apostolic movement is preserved obliquely in rabbinic literature: Babylonian Talmud *Sanhedrin* 43a, 107b, and Tosefta *Hullin* 2:24 contain the disputed early references to Jesus and his followers, all hostile. The **birkat ha-minim**, the synagogue prayer-curse against heretics canonized in some form by the late first or early second century, marked a formal point of separation; Lawrence Schiffman's *Who Was a Jew?* and Daniel Boyarin's *Border Lines* both argue, though with different emphases, that the church-synagogue split was slower, messier, and later than older Christian scholarship assumed. Adele Reinhartz and Amy-Jill Levine (*The Misunderstood Jew*, *Jewish Annotated New Testament*) have pressed contemporary Christian exegesis to read Acts 1 — and its restoration-of-Israel question (1:6) — without flattening the Jewish hope into a mere foil for Christian fulfillment. Pamela Eisenbaum and Matthew Thiessen's *A Jewish Paul* extend this work into the Pauline corpus and reshape how we read the apostolic movement's self-understanding as still Jewish in Acts 1.

**Christian patristic reception**. **John Chrysostom's 55 Homilies on the Acts of the Apostles**, preached in Constantinople around 400 CE, is the foundational and longest patristic engagement with the book; Chrysostom on Acts 1 emphasizes the ascension as enthronement, the apostolic continuity through Matthias, and the disciplined waiting of the eleven as a model for Christian patience. **Origen** engages the ascension in *Contra Celsum* and *De Principiis*; **Augustine** treats Acts in scattered places (especially *De Doctrina Christiana* and the anti-Donatist works); **Jerome's** *De Viris Illustribus* preserves authorship traditions. **Bede the Venerable's** *Expositio Actuum Apostolorum* (early eighth century) is the most extensive medieval Acts commentary and reads chapter 1 with characteristic typological care, mapping Elijah and Elisha onto Christ and the apostles.

**Reformation and modern**. Calvin's *Commentary on Acts* is foundational for Reformed reading, and on chapter 1 Calvin works hard on Matthias's selection — reading the lot as a one-time apostolic-foundation event rather than a normative procedure. Luther's lectures on Acts are less developed but resonate with Calvin on this point. **Karl Barth** in *Church Dogmatics* IV/3.2 reads Acts 1:8 as the church's commission to bear witness, with the ascension grounding the church's life in the lordship of the risen Christ; Bonhoeffer's *Discipleship* picks up the witness theme; Bultmann's existentialist reading demythologizes the ascension while preserving its kerygmatic function.

**Contemporary commentaries** form a dense conversation: F.F. Bruce's NICNT (foundational evangelical-historical), C.K. Barrett's two-volume ICC (foundational critical), Joseph Fitzmyer's Anchor Bible, Beverly Gaventa's Abingdon, Luke Timothy Johnson's Sacra Pagina (literary-theological), Craig Keener's four-volume encyclopedic commentary (foundational for ancient-context detail), Ben Witherington's socio-rhetorical commentary, Eckhard Schnabel's ZECNT, Hans Conzelmann's Hermeneia (foundational redaction-critical), Ernst Haenchen's classic, Richard Pervo's late-dating Hermeneia, David Peterson's Pillar, Daniel Marguerat's Continental commentary, and I. Howard Marshall's TNTC. Loveday Alexander's *Acts in Its Ancient Literary Context* and Marguerat's *The First Christian Historian* anchor the literary-historiographical work; Tannehill's *Narrative Unity* and Robert Brawley's writings anchor the narrative-literary.

**Pentecostal and charismatic** scholarship reads Acts 1 forward into the Pentecost paradigm: Roger Stronstad's *Charismatic Theology of St. Luke*, Robert Menzies's *Empowered for Witness*, and Gordon Fee's *God's Empowering Presence* press the case that Lukan pneumatology is distinct from Pauline and frames Spirit-baptism as missional empowerment, beginning already in 1:5 and 1:8.

**Liberation and feminist** readings press the unfinished work of the chapter. Justo González's *Acts (Belief)* reads the apostolic poverty and the gathering at Olivet in continuity with Luke's prophetic emphases on the marginalized; Allan Boesak's South African readings push the witness-and-power language toward the politics of resistance. Schüssler Fiorenza's *In Memory of Her* and Beverly Gaventa's commentary press hard on 1:14's "the women, and Mary the mother of Jesus, and his brothers" — the women are listed but unnamed except for Mary, and the all-male slate of two candidates for the twelfth apostle (1:23) raises the question of whose witness counts. Turid Karlsen Seim's *The Double Message* analyzes Lukan gender-rhetoric across Luke-Acts with particular care.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The kingdom and its restoration.** When the disciples ask in 1:6, "Lord, will you at this time restore the kingdom to Israel?", they are asking the question Luke does not directly answer. Three positions:

*Dispensationalist*: the kingdom restoration to ethnic Israel is real and future; Jesus does not deny it but defers it; the church age is parenthetical. Engage Charles Ryrie's *Dispensationalism* and the Scofield tradition. *Supersessionist-realized*: the kingdom is being restored, but not to ethnic Israel as such — it is being given to a renewed Israel that will include Gentiles; the church *is* the eschatological Israel. Augustine and Calvin fall broadly here, as does the older Reformed tradition. *Post-supersessionist / two-covenant or single-covenant inclusivist*: the kingdom is being extended to Gentiles without abrogating God's covenant with Israel; the question of 1:6 is reframed but not voided. Engage Marguerat, Reinhartz, Thiessen.

The strongest version of each: dispensationalists are right that the disciples' question is real and Jesus does not contradict the premise; the realized-eschatology reading is right that 1:8 reframes the question by extending witness "to the end of the earth" rather than restoring a Davidic polity; the post-supersessionist reading is right that Acts as a whole refuses to write Israel out of its own story (cf. Romans 9–11; Acts 28:17–28).

*Where this leaves us*: 1:8 is not a denial of 1:6 but a redirection. The kingdom Jesus inaugurates is restored — but its geography is the world, its citizens both Jew and Gentile, its means the Spirit-empowered word rather than the sword. Luke does not resolve whether the future holds something more for Israel; Paul will be left to wrestle that question.

**Tension 2: Apostolic succession and the lot.** Matthias's selection by lot in 1:15–26 is the last lot-casting (***klēros***) recorded in the New Testament, and it sits at the center of a long Christian disagreement. *Catholic*: the apostolic office continues through episcopal succession; Matthias is the inaugural instance of a continuing pattern, even if the specific procedure (lot) belongs to the inaugural moment. Engage *Lumen Gentium* 3, 18–22. *Reformed*: the apostolic office was a foundational, non-repeatable office for the inscripturation period; Matthias completes the twelve (a typological number tied to the tribes of Israel) but inaugurates no continuing succession; ministry continues through plural eldership. Engage Calvin's commentary on 1:23–26 and the Westminster tradition. *Anabaptist / congregationalist*: ministry is a function of the local gathered church; the lot in Acts 1 is a unique moment, not a paradigm; congregational discernment under the Spirit is the continuing form.

The strongest version of each: the Catholic case rests on the structural fact that Acts gives Matthias an office to fill, not just a witness role; the Reformed case rests on the absence of any further lot-casting after Pentecost (notice that the Spirit is already the operative agent in chapter 13, where Paul and Barnabas are set apart by prayer and laying on of hands without lot); the Anabaptist case rests on the chapter's emphasis on prayer (1:14, 1:24) over institutional procedure.

*Where this leaves us*: Acts itself shows the lot dissolving as the operative method once the Spirit comes. Whatever one makes of apostolic succession, 1:24's prayer — "you, Lord, who know the hearts of all, show which one of these two you have chosen" — locates the choice in God, not in the procedure. The lot is a way of getting human will out of the way at a moment when the Spirit has not yet been poured out; after Pentecost, the Spirit's direct guidance replaces it.

*One unresolved question*: did Peter act prematurely in arranging Matthias's selection before Pentecost? Some readers (notably G. Campbell Morgan, F.B. Meyer in the older devotional tradition) have argued Paul was God's true twelfth apostle and Matthias an impatient mistake; mainstream commentary rejects this on textual grounds (Luke narrates the selection without disapproval and includes Matthias in "the eleven apostles" at 1:26 and in "the twelve" at 6:2). But the question of whether the church's first decision was right one is itself Luke's invitation to read the rest of the book.

## The Thread

The promise of Spirit-baptism in 1:5 reaches forward immediately to **Acts 2:1–4** (Pentecost), and its echo recurs at **Acts 10:44–47** (Cornelius) and **Acts 19:1–7** (Ephesian disciples) — the four "Pentecosts" of Acts that Pentecostal theology reads as paradigmatic. The programmatic geography of 1:8 maps the structure of the book: Jerusalem (chapters 2–7), Judea and Samaria (8–12), and the ends of the earth (13–28). The ascension and the promise that "this Jesus will come in the same way" (1:11) reaches forward to the parousia texts of 1 Thessalonians 4 and Revelation 1:7, and reaches backward to Daniel 7:13–14, where the son of man comes with the clouds and is given dominion. The lot-casting of 1:26 reaches backward to Proverbs 16:33 ("the lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the Lord") and to the priestly lot for the goats on Yom Kippur (Lev 16:8); flag forward to the ordination of the seven in Acts 6 and the setting apart of Paul and Barnabas in Acts 13 to see how the church's discernment evolves once the Spirit is given.
