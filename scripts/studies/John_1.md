# John 1: In the Beginning Was the Word

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The Fourth Gospel reaches us from a world already pulling apart. If we date the Gospel's final form to the 90s of the Common Era, as most critical scholarship does, we are reading a document composed under the late Flavians (Domitian's reign ended 96 CE) and finalized perhaps under Nerva or early Trajan. This places the community in the long aftermath of the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE — a generational rupture for both Jews and the followers of Jesus, who had not yet sorted themselves into the discrete religions that later centuries would call Judaism and Christianity. Daniel Boyarin's *Border Lines: The Partition of Judaeo-Christianity* has reframed this period decisively: there was no clean wall between "Jew" and "Christian" in the late first century, but rather a gradual, contested, painful sorting that Boyarin calls "the partition" — and the Fourth Gospel sits inside that contest, not after it.

The most consequential historical-critical hypothesis for reading John was J. Louis Martyn's *History and Theology in the Fourth Gospel* (1968), which proposed that the Gospel reflects a "two-level drama": the surface story tells of Jesus, but the subtext refracts the experience of a Jewish-Christian community expelled from synagogue fellowship. Martyn anchored this in the **birkat ha-minim** — the "blessing against heretics" added to the Eighteen Benedictions, traditionally associated with the Yavneh academy under Gamaliel II around 85-90 CE. The Cairo Geniza version curses *notzrim* (Nazarenes) and *minim* (heretics). John 9:22, 12:42, and 16:2 speak of being made *aposynagōgos* (ἀποσυνάγωγος, "out of the synagogue") — a term unattested elsewhere. Martyn's reconstruction has been substantially modified — Steven Katz, Reuven Kimelman, and Daniel Boyarin have all argued that the birkat ha-minim was not originally a formal Jewish-Christian expulsion mechanism, and Adele Reinhartz in *Cast Out of the Covenant* warns that Martyn's framework can underwrite a supersessionist reading of the Gospel — but the broader observation that the Johannine community is in painful tension with a synagogue from which it has recently parted remains a working consensus.

Qumran has reshaped how we read John's dualism. The discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls in 1947 rendered obsolete the older view (Bultmann's) that John's light/darkness, truth/lie, sons-of-light/sons-of-darkness vocabulary was Hellenistic gnosticism imported into a Jewish text. The *Community Rule* (1QS 3:13-4:26) — the "two spirits / two ways" passage — uses precisely this vocabulary in unmistakably Jewish-sectarian Hebrew. James Charlesworth's *John and Qumran* and Mary Coloe's work on Johannine sectarianism have established that John's dualism is at home in late Second Temple Palestinian Judaism, not in later Hellenistic religion.

The geography of chapter 1 is itself contested. **Bethany across the Jordan** (1:28 — *Bēthania peran tou Iordanou*, Βηθανία πέραν τοῦ Ἰορδάνου) is distinct from the Bethany near Jerusalem and is most plausibly identified with Wadi al-Kharrar / Bethany Beyond the Jordan in modern Jordan, where excavations under Mohammed Waheeb since 1996 have uncovered Byzantine-era pilgrimage churches commemorating the baptism site. Origen, writing in the third century, knew a textual variant *Bēthabara* (Βηθαβαρά) and reported visiting the site. **Bethsaida** (1:44), Andrew, Peter, and Philip's hometown, is most plausibly et-Tell on the northeast shore of the Sea of Galilee — Rami Arav's excavations since 1987 have uncovered a first-century fishing village, though some argue for el-Araj instead. **Nazareth** (1:45-46) was a small Galilean village whose modest archaeological footprint (Ken Dark's recent work; the so-called "Nazareth House" excavations) explains Nathanael's sneer in 1:46.

Greek terms structure the chapter. ***Logos*** (λόγος, "word, reason, account") in 1:1 is the most contested noun in the Gospel. ***Skēnoō*** (σκηνόω, "tabernacle, pitch tent") in 1:14 — "the Word became flesh and *tabernacled* among us" — evokes the Sinai *miškān* (מִשְׁכָּן) and the Septuagint's translation of God's glory dwelling with Israel. ***Monogenēs*** (μονογενής, 1:14, 1:18) — "only-begotten" or "unique," contested in the Arian controversy. ***Charis*** (χάρις, "grace") and ***alētheia*** (ἀλήθεια, "truth") in 1:14, 1:17 — likely a translation of the Hebrew pair *ḥesed* (חֶסֶד) and *ʾemet* (אֱמֶת), the Sinai self-disclosure of YHWH (Exod 34:6).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

What kind of book begins like this? Richard Burridge's *What Are the Gospels?* established the consensus that the canonical Gospels are Greco-Roman *bioi* (lives), but John strains that genre more than the synoptics. Wayne Meeks's landmark essay "The Man from Heaven in Johannine Sectarianism" (*JBL* 1972) argued that John reads as the founding mythography of a sectarian community — a text whose insider/outsider dynamics, riddles, and double-meanings function as a "sectarian code" comprehensible to insiders and opaque to outsiders. R. Alan Culpepper's *Anatomy of the Fourth Gospel* (1983) inaugurated literary-critical Johannine scholarship by analyzing John as a coherent narrative whole, complete with implied author, narrator, plot, and characterization, regardless of the sources behind it.

The **prologue** (1:1-18) is the most theologically dense opening in the New Testament — perhaps in any work of ancient literature. Rudolf Bultmann famously hypothesized that 1:1-18 is a pre-existing logos-hymn (likely from a baptist-sectarian or proto-gnostic milieu) that the evangelist has redacted and incorporated, citing the rhythmic structure, the staircase parallelism (*klimax*), and what Bultmann saw as awkward seams where the John-the-Baptist references (1:6-8, 15) are inserted into hymnic material about the Logos. Ernst Käsemann's *The Testament of Jesus* (1968) argued that the prologue's "we have seen his glory" (1:14) signals not an incarnational humbling but a "naive docetism" — the Logos walks the earth as a god striding through. Raymond Brown's *Anchor Bible* commentary (1966-1970) offered the most influential synthesis: the prologue is a hymn, likely Johannine-community in origin, structured as a chiasm or staircase moving from cosmic creation (1:1-5) to the Word's reception (1:9-13) to incarnation and witness (1:14-18). C.K. Barrett's *Gospel According to St. John* (CGTC, 1955/1978) resisted Bultmann's source-criticism and read the prologue as evangelist-composed throughout. Most contemporary commentators — Andreas Köstenberger (BECNT), D.A. Carson (PNTC), Marianne Meye Thompson (NTL), Craig Keener — accept some hymnic substrate but treat the prologue as integral to the Gospel's argument.

The **seven signs structure** that Brown, Bultmann, and Dodd all identified begins, in narrative time, in chapter 2 (water-to-wine), but the prologue functions as the hermeneutical key to all of them: the signs are revelations of *doxa* (1:14, "we have seen his glory"). Brown's enumeration: (1) water-to-wine 2:1-11; (2) nobleman's son 4:46-54; (3) lame man 5:1-15; (4) feeding the 5,000 6:1-15; (5) walking on water 6:16-21; (6) man born blind 9:1-41; (7) raising of Lazarus 11:1-44. The cleansing of the Temple in chapter 2 is not numbered as a sign — Brown reads it as a separate "prophetic action."

The **calling of the first disciples** (1:35-51) is one of the most distinctive Johannine narrative units, with no synoptic parallel. Where Mark, Matthew, and Luke have Jesus call the fishermen at the Sea of Galilee with no prior acquaintance, John presents a chain-call mediated through the Baptist's testimony: two of John's disciples follow Jesus (one is Andrew; the other unnamed and traditionally identified as the Beloved Disciple — Bauckham's *The Testimony of the Beloved Disciple* makes much of this); Andrew brings Peter; the next day Philip is called and brings Nathanael. This sequence, with its Aramaic name-translations ("Rabbi which means Teacher" 1:38; "Messiah which means Christ" 1:41; "Cephas which means Peter" 1:42), strongly suggests a Greek-speaking community for whom Aramaic is no longer mother-tongue.

**Misunderstanding** as Johannine literary device begins here in seed form (Nathanael in 1:46) and will become programmatic by chapter 3. The use of *amēn amēn* (ἀμὴν ἀμήν, "truly, truly") doubled in 1:51 — twenty-five times in John, never doubled in the synoptics — is a Johannine signature. Textual variants in the prologue are minimal but consequential: 1:18 reads either *monogenēs theos* (μονογενὴς θεός, "only-begotten God" — papyri P66, P75, Sinaiticus, Vaticanus) or *ho monogenēs huios* (ὁ μονογενὴς υἱός, "the only-begotten Son" — Alexandrinus, Western text, most Byzantine). Bart Ehrman's *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* argues the *theos* reading is a proto-orthodox heightening; most contemporary critics (NA28, UBS5) accept *monogenēs theos* as original.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Reading the prologue forces us into both Jewish and Christian interpretive trajectories at once. **Daniel Boyarin's** *The Jewish Gospels: The Story of the Jewish Christ* (2012) is foundational. Boyarin argues that the high Christology of John 1 — a divine Logos pre-existent with God, through whom all things were made — is not a Hellenistic corruption of pristine Jewish monotheism but a thoroughly Jewish Second Temple theology. Boyarin's evidence: the **Targumic Memra** tradition. In the Aramaic Targums (Neofiti, Pseudo-Jonathan, Onkelos), the Hebrew "YHWH" is regularly rendered "the *memra* (מימרא) of YHWH" — the Word of YHWH as a quasi-personified divine agent who creates, reveals, and saves. Targum Neofiti on Genesis 1:1 reads "from the beginning, by *memra* the Lord created the heavens and the earth." John Ronning's *The Jewish Targums and John's Logos Theology* (2010) develops this exhaustively. Martin McNamara's earlier *Targum and Testament* established the Targumic background. The point: John 1's Logos is not a Hellenistic intrusion into Judaism but emerges from within Jewish theological imagination already in motion.

Wisdom literature is the second Jewish backdrop. **Wisdom 7:25-26** describes Wisdom (Sophia, *ḥokmah*) as "a breath of the power of God, a pure emanation of the glory of the Almighty... a reflection of eternal light, a spotless mirror of the working of God." **Sirach 24** has Wisdom proceeding from the mouth of the Most High, dwelling in the heights, and finally *tabernacling* in Jacob (24:8) — exactly the verb John 1:14 uses for the Logos. **Proverbs 8:22-31** has Wisdom present at creation, beside God like a master craftsman. Raymond Brown's *Anchor Bible* commentary, Marianne Meye Thompson's *NTL*, and John Ashton's *Understanding the Fourth Gospel* (1991, 2nd ed. 2007) all develop the Wisdom-Christology reading thoroughly. Ashton in particular — a scholar who began as a relatively conservative Catholic exegete and became more radical over his career — argues that John is best read as a Jewish apocalyptic-mystical text whose Logos-Wisdom Christology is unintelligible apart from Second Temple Jewish speculation about divine intermediaries.

**Adele Reinhartz** has been the most consistent voice warning against Christian readings of John 1 that obscure its inheritance and its dangers. Her *Befriending the Beloved Disciple* (2001) reads John as a "friend" with whom one must argue; her more recent *Cast Out of the Covenant: Jews and Anti-Judaism in the Gospel of John* (2018) is unsparing about the supersessionist potential of 1:17 ("the law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ") and the entire prologue's framing of the Logos's coming to "his own" who "did not receive him" (1:11). Reinhartz urges readers to engage these texts with full awareness of their post-Holocaust afterlife.

Patristic interpretation of John 1 is the foundation of Nicene Christology. **Origen's** *Commentary on John* (early third century, partially surviving — books 1, 2, 6, 10, 13, 19, 20, 28, 32) is the most extensive patristic Gospel commentary we possess and devotes book 1 entirely to the prologue. Origen reads *logos* through Wisdom and through Philonic intermediation — the Logos as image of the Father, eternally begotten, the *archē* through which all things are made. **Augustine's** *Tractates on the Gospel of John* (124 tractates, ca. 406-420) is foundational for Western Christology — Augustine's reading of "the Word was God" against the Arians, his linking of Logos to *ratio* and *verbum* in his trinitarian theology of *De Trinitate*, his programmatic use of John 1:14 as the keystone of Christian doctrine. **John Chrysostom's** *88 Homilies on John*, **Cyril of Alexandria's** *Commentary on John* (foundational for Eastern Christology and the basis of Chalcedonian formulations on the unity of Christ's person), **Theodore of Mopsuestia**, and **Aquinas's** *Lectura super Joannem* all cluster around the prologue. The **Council of Nicaea (325)** — *homoousios*, the Son "of one substance" with the Father — is unintelligible without John 1.

Reformation: **Calvin's *Commentary on the Gospel According to St. John*** (1553) reads the prologue with characteristic precision against both Catholic-mediation and Anabaptist-spiritualizing readings; Calvin's account of 1:13 ("born... not of the will of man but of God") becomes a locus for his doctrine of regeneration. **Luther's lectures** on John, while less systematic, return repeatedly to 1:14 as the heart of the Gospel.

Mystical tradition: **Meister Eckhart's** German sermons read John 1 as the eternal "birth of the Word in the soul" — a dangerous reading that drew an inquisitorial bull (*In agro dominico*, 1329) but whose imaginative force shaped Tauler, Suso, and the Theologia Germanica. **John of the Cross**, **Bernard of Clairvaux**, and **Hildegard of Bingen** all treat 1:14 as the foundation of mystical Christology.

Twentieth century: **Karl Barth's** *Church Dogmatics* makes John 1 programmatic — the Word as God's self-revelation, against natural theology. **Rudolf Bultmann's** *The Gospel of John* (German 1941, English 1971) was the most transformative single commentary of the century; Bultmann's existentialist reading, his source-criticism, and his demythologizing program shaped a generation. **C.H. Dodd's** *The Interpretation of the Fourth Gospel* (1953) read John in dialogue with Hellenistic religious discourse (Hermetic literature, Philo) and proposed a "realized eschatology." **Raymond Brown's** *Anchor Bible* commentary (1966-1970) and *The Community of the Beloved Disciple* (1979) remain the foundational Anglophone resources. **C.K. Barrett**, **Schnackenburg's** *HthKNT* (3 vols), **Ernst Haenchen**, **Beasley-Murray's** *WBC*, **Köstenberger's** *BECNT*, **Carson's** *PNTC*, **Keener's** two-volume commentary, **Marianne Meye Thompson's** *NTL*, **Francis Moloney's** *Sacra Pagina*, **Jo-Ann Brant's** *Paideia*, **Gail O'Day's** *NIB*, and **Lesslie Newbigin's** *The Light Has Come* — the missional reading that became foundational for non-Western Johannine reception — all treat the prologue at length. **Bauckham's** *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* and *The Testimony of the Beloved Disciple* press the case that the prologue's "we have seen his glory" (1:14) is a literal eyewitness claim.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions structure the chapter.

**Tension one: how is the Word "with God" and also "God"?** The Greek of 1:1 — *kai theos ēn ho logos* (καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος) — has the predicate noun *theos* anarthrous (without the article) and fronted. The *Catholic* and *Reformed* mainstream (and most modern translations) read this as a qualitative predication: the Word *is divine in nature*, of one substance with the Father. The **Watchtower Society's** New World Translation famously renders "the Word was *a god*" — reading the anarthrous *theos* as indefinite, supporting an Arian/Jehovah's-Witness Christology in which the Logos is a created divine being. Greek grammarians (Daniel Wallace, *Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics*; E.C. Colwell's 1933 rule) overwhelmingly reject the indefinite reading: Colwell's rule states that definite predicate nouns preceding the verb commonly drop the article. The **Eastern Orthodox** tradition reads through the Cappadocians: the Logos is *homoousios* with the Father, distinct in *hypostasis*. The **Wesleyan/Arminian** tradition holds the Nicene line. **Modalist** and **Oneness Pentecostal** readings collapse the distinction between Father and Word into a single divine person manifesting in modes — the ancient Sabellian heresy reborn. The **Jewish** reading, with Boyarin, is striking: a Second Temple Jew need not be scandalized by the Logos's divinity if she is reading within the Memra and Wisdom traditions. What separated rabbinic Judaism from Johannine Christianity was not whether God could have a divine Word, but whether *this man, Jesus of Nazareth*, was that Word incarnate. Steelmanned, the Arian/Watchtower position has the linguistic anomaly of *theos* anarthrous to point to and the legitimate worry about the philosophical coherence of two divine persons; steelmanned, the Sabellian position protects monotheism most rigorously; steelmanned, the Nicene/Chalcedonian mainstream argues that only this reading does justice to both the prologue's distinction-with-identity grammar and to the worship-practice of the New Testament churches that pray *to* Jesus while remaining monotheist.

*Where this leaves us:* the grammar of 1:1 resists both Arian subordination and Sabellian collapse. The Logos is "with" (*pros*, πρός — face-to-face) God *and* is God. The Nicene reading is doing what the text actually requires — but only if we hold the philosophical machinery of "person" and "substance" lightly, recognizing it as fourth-century vocabulary applied to first-century Jewish theological imagination.

**Tension two: incarnation — humbling or unveiling?** "And the Word became flesh and tabernacled among us" (1:14). Is this a kenotic descent (Phil 2:7) — the eternal Word emptying himself to take on creaturely limitation, suffering, mortality? Or is it a theophanic unveiling — the Word's eternal glory becoming visible through the medium of flesh? **Ernst Käsemann** in *The Testament of Jesus* read John as "naively docetic" — the Logos walks the earth as a god, his humanity a thin veil. The **Catholic** and **Reformed** mainstream insists on a robustly incarnational reading: real humanity, real flesh, real death. The **Eastern Orthodox** tradition reads through Cyril of Alexandria's "one incarnate nature of God the Word" formula (*mia physis tou theou logou sesarkōmenē*), insisting on the unity of the person while distinguishing two natures. **Lutheran** Christology develops the *communicatio idiomatum* — the properties of each nature predicated of the whole person. **Liberation theology** reads 1:14 as the divine self-identification with material existence, the political-theological foundation for solidarity with the embodied poor (Gustavo Gutiérrez, Jon Sobrino's *Christ the Liberator*). **Feminist theology** (Sandra Schneiders, Elizabeth Johnson's *She Who Is*) presses the Wisdom-Christology background: the Logos is *Sophia* incarnate, and the "flesh" of 1:14 is human flesh in its full sexed and gendered particularity. Steelmanned, Käsemann's reading sees what is genuinely strange about Johannine Christology — Jesus's serenity, his prior knowledge, his control of his own death; steelmanned, the kenotic mainstream reads 1:14 as the Gospel's anti-docetic anchor, the verse that prevents the prologue from floating off into pure metaphysics.

*Where this leaves us:* John holds tension where the synoptics rest in narrative. The Jesus who weeps at Lazarus's tomb (11:35) and bleeds on the cross is also the Jesus who knows what is in man (2:25) and lays down his life of his own accord (10:18). The Fourth Gospel's incarnation is not a divine slumming but a divine self-disclosure that is *only* possible through real flesh.

**Unresolved question:** if the Logos is the agent of all creation (1:3) and the true light "that enlightens everyone" (1:9), what is the salvific status of those who have not encountered the incarnate Word — the religious other, the unevangelized, the pre-Christian sage? Justin Martyr's *spermatikos logos* tradition, Karl Rahner's "anonymous Christians," Lesslie Newbigin's missional reading, and Jacques Dupuis's *Toward a Christian Theology of Religious Pluralism* (which drew Vatican censure) all wrestle with this. The prologue invites the question without resolving it.

## The Thread

The Logos of John 1 reaches back to Genesis 1:1 ("In the beginning God created" — and it was *by speaking* that he created) and to Proverbs 8 (Wisdom present at creation). It reaches forward to John 14:6 ("I am the way, and the truth, and the life"), to Colossians 1:15-20, to Hebrews 1:1-3, and to Revelation 19:13 ("his name is called The Word of God"). The "tabernacling" of 1:14 (*eskēnōsen*) anticipates the Temple cleansing in 2:13-22, where Jesus's body replaces the Temple, and the Samaritan-woman conversation in 4:21-24, where worship is no longer bound to a place. The Lamb-of-God acclamation (1:29, 1:36) flags forward to the Passion chronology in chapters 18-19, where Jesus dies as the Passover lambs are slaughtered. The Jacob's-ladder allusion in 1:51 ("you will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man") is the chapter's last word and the Gospel's first programmatic claim: this man is the place where heaven and earth meet — the new Bethel.
