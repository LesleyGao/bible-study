# 1 Chronicles 10 — The Death of Saul

## Lens 1 — Historical World

After nine chapters of genealogies, the Chronicler's narrative finally begins — and it begins not with David, not with the institution of the monarchy, not with the founding of Jerusalem, but with the death of Saul. This is the Chronicler's most dramatic editorial decision in the entire book. Samuel-Kings devotes twenty-two chapters (1 Samuel 9-31) to Saul's reign — his anointing, his rise, his wars, his complicated relationship with David, his collapse into paranoia and necromancy. The Chronicler reduces all of that to fourteen verses, and the fourteen verses cover only the death. Saul's reign, in Chronicles, is essentially nothing but its end.

The book is anonymous; rabbinic tradition (b. Bava Batra 15a) attributes it to Ezra, but modern scholarship dates composition to Persian-period Yehud, between roughly 400 and 350 BC. Sara Japhet's *I & II Chronicles* (OTL, 1993), Gary Knoppers's *I Chronicles 10-29* (Anchor Bible, 2004), Hugh Williamson's *1 and 2 Chronicles* (NCBC, 1982), Ralph Klein's *1 Chronicles* (Hermeneia, 2006), Roddy Braun (WBC, 1986), Mark Boda (*A Severe Mercy: Sin and Its Remedy in the Old Testament*, 2009), and Steven McKenzie all date the work to the late Persian period and treat it as the work of an anonymous Levite, possibly a temple singer.

The chapter parallels 1 Samuel 31:1-13 closely — verses 1-12 follow Samuel almost word for word — but verses 13-14 are the Chronicler's editorial supplement, his own theological commentary on Saul's death. This is the Chronicler's signature compositional method: take a Deuteronomistic source (Samuel-Kings), reproduce the bulk of it, and add focused theological framing that redirects the meaning of the borrowed material. Williamson, in his classic *Israel in the Books of Chronicles* (1977), called this method "interpretive recension."

The historical setting of the death itself is real and recoverable. Mount Gilboa rises about 500 meters above the Jezreel Valley, on the southeastern flank. The Philistine army, advancing north through the coastal plain, would have engaged Israelite forces who had taken the high ground — a defensive position that became a kill zone when Philistine archers (v. 3) outranged the Israelite spearmen. The Philistines' use of *temple of Dagon* (v. 10) for displaying Saul's head, and *temple of their gods* for his armor, fits Philistine religious geography. Dagon was the chief god of the Philistine pentapolis, with temples attested at Ashdod (1 Sam 5:1-7) and likely Gaza. The men of Jabesh-gilead's recovery of the body (vv. 11-12) fulfills a debt — Saul's first great deed as king had been the rescue of Jabesh-gilead from Nahash the Ammonite (1 Sam 11), and the Trans-Jordanian town now repays the favor at his death.

The Hebrew terminology that controls the chapter is concentrated in verses 13-14 and centers on a single root: *ma'al* (מַעַל). The verse opens *vayyamat Sha'ul b'ma'alo asher ma'al ba-YHWH* — "Saul died for his ma'al that he ma'aled against YHWH." *Ma'al* is the Chronicler's signature theological term, appearing more than thirty times in 1-2 Chronicles and only rarely in earlier biblical literature. It denotes a specific kind of cultic-religious offense: a *breach of trust*, a *treacherous unfaithfulness*, a violation of sacral obligation. Knoppers and Boda both note that *ma'al* in Chronicles is not just "sin" generically but a technical term for the violation of holy boundaries — the kind of offense that disqualifies one from cultic access. Saul's *ma'al*, the Chronicler specifies, was twofold: he did not keep YHWH's command (an allusion to the Amalekite affair of 1 Samuel 15) and he consulted a medium (*lish'ol b'ov*, the necromancy of 1 Samuel 28).

The other key Hebrew term is *darash* (דָּרַשׁ), "to seek, inquire of." *V'lo darash ba-YHWH* in verse 14 — "and he did not inquire of YHWH" — is the Chronicler's signature verb for legitimate cultic-prophetic consultation. *Darash YHWH* is what David does (1 Chr 14:10), what Solomon does, what the good kings do. Saul *darash* a necromancer instead.

Archaeologically, the Saulide story-cycle has serious historical roots. The site of Tell el-Ful (likely Gibeah of Saul) shows late-eleventh-century Iron Age I fortification consistent with the Saulide period. The Philistine military archaeology — chariot-and-archer-based units pushing north into the Jezreel Valley — fits the eleventh-century coastal-plain Philistine expansion attested at Ekron, Ashdod, and Tell Qasile. The Chronicler's compressed account is not making up the death of Saul; it is presupposing widely known traditional material and interpreting it.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter is built as narrative-plus-commentary, a form the Chronicler will use throughout the book. Verses 1-12 are the borrowed Samuel narrative; verses 13-14 are the Chronicler's editorial verdict. The narrative section divides into four scenes. Scene one (vv. 1-7): the battle on Mount Gilboa, the death of Saul's three sons, Saul's wound, his suicide, his armor-bearer's suicide, the rout of Israel, the Philistine occupation of abandoned cities. Scene two (vv. 8-10): the Philistines' discovery of the body, the stripping, the head displayed in Dagon's temple, the armor displayed elsewhere. Scene three (vv. 11-12): the men of Jabesh-gilead's nighttime recovery of the body, burial under the oak, seven-day fast. Scene four (vv. 13-14): the Chronicler's verdict.

The Hebrew literary technique most worth marking is the Chronicler's *editorial compression*. Where 1 Samuel 31:13 says "they fasted seven days" and that is the end of the chapter, the Chronicler adds his commentary in verses 13-14. The commentary uses three repeated key terms — *ma'al* twice (verbal and nominal), *darash* twice (negated and contrasted), *YHWH* four times — packed into 28 Hebrew words. The density of the verdict is striking against the slower narrative pace of verses 1-12. The Chronicler is signaling that the *meaning* of the death is not in the narrative details but in the theological judgment that follows.

The most significant literary detail in the borrowed Samuel material is the Chronicler's small adjustment in verse 6: where 1 Samuel 31:6 says "Saul and his three sons and his armor-bearer and all his men died together that day," 1 Chronicles 10:6 says "Saul died, and his three sons, *and all his house*, all together." The Chronicler has changed "all his men" to *kol beito*, "all his house." Williamson, Klein, and Knoppers all note this: the Chronicler is making Saul's death a *dynastic* event, not just a military one. It is not just that Saul and his soldiers died; the *house* of Saul died together. This sets up the Chronicler's theological argument: the Saulide dynasty did not survive him because *YHWH put Saul to death and turned the kingdom over to David* (v. 14). The genealogical continuation of Saul's line in 8:33-40 and 9:35-44 documents Saul's family, but the *house* in the dynastic sense ended at Gilboa.

The Hebrew literary feature of inclusio is at work here. The chapter opens with *Plishtim nilchamim b'Yisra'el* ("the Philistines fought against Israel," v. 1) and closes with *vayyasev et ha-m'lukhah l'David ben Yishai* ("and turned the kingdom over to David the son of Jesse," v. 14). The Philistines win the battle; YHWH transfers the kingdom. The literary shape is a battle that becomes a transition.

## Lens 3 — Larger Story

In the Hebrew Bible's canonical order Chronicles is the last book, and the death of Saul as the *opening* narrative of the book has a striking effect: the Tanakh's last narrative voice begins with a king's failure and a kingdom's transfer. The rabbinic reception of this chapter is rich. The Talmud (b. Sanhedrin 102b-103b, b. Yoma 22b) discusses Saul's death extensively, and the rabbis preserved a complicated portrait — Saul as a tragic but ultimately culpable king. Rashi, commenting on verse 13, identifies Saul's two failures specifically: the Amalekite affair (1 Sam 15) and the medium of En-dor (1 Sam 28). Radak (David Kimhi) develops the *darash* contrast: Saul sought guidance from a forbidden source instead of from YHWH. Don Isaac Abarbanel reads the chapter as the foundational case of *measure-for-measure* judgment in the Hebrew Bible: Saul sought a forbidden voice, so YHWH ended his life and his line. The medieval Jewish commentators converge on the reading that Saul's death is *deserved* and *immediate*, but they also preserve the tragic dimension — the men of Jabesh-gilead's grief is real, the seven-day fast is real, and the loss is felt.

Modern Jewish scholarship engages the chapter at a different level. Sara Japhet's commentary treats the Chronicler's editorial verdict as an example of *immediate retribution*, the theological framework that distinguishes Chronicles sharply from the Deuteronomistic History. Isaac Kalimi (*The Reshaping of Ancient Israelite History*, 2005) shows how the Chronicler's compression of Saul's reign serves a *presentist* historiography: the past is interpreted from the standpoint of the post-exilic community's concerns. Jon Levenson's broader work on biblical theology emphasizes the Chronicler's de-tragicizing of Saul: where Samuel allowed Saul's failure to feel ambiguous and partly the work of YHWH's "evil spirit," the Chronicler's cleaner verdict assigns the death entirely to Saul's *ma'al*.

The Christian trajectory has wrestled with this chapter intensely. Jerome translated the chapter without major comment but noted in his prologue the difference between Chronicles' brief Saul-narrative and Samuel's longer one. Augustine, in *City of God* 17.5-7, treated Saul's death as an example of providential judgment. Aquinas cited verse 13 in arguments about the gravity of consulting forbidden sources for guidance. Calvin's 1561 commentary on the chapter is one of his most pointed: "Whoever does not seek God in his afflictions is utterly condemned, however much he may pretend to piety."

The most important modern Christian engagement with this chapter is Frank Moore Cross's *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* (1973), which placed the Chronicler's *immediate retribution* in contrast to the Deuteronomistic History's *delayed retribution*. The Deuteronomist (Cross argued) saw the exile as the cumulative consequence of generations of unfaithfulness, with judgment delayed until the Manasseh era and finally falling on Jehoiakim's generation. The Chronicler, by contrast, sees judgment falling immediately and personally on the offender. Saul dies for his *own* sin in 1 Chronicles 10; he does not die for some accumulated national debt. Mark Boda's *A Severe Mercy* (2009) develops this theme extensively: the Chronicler's retribution theology is not punitive but pedagogical — God's judgments fall quickly and visibly so that the people can learn and turn.

The New Testament's engagement with Saul's death is sparse but significant. Acts 13:21-22 paraphrases the Chronicler's verdict almost directly: "God gave them Saul... for forty years. And when he had removed him, he raised up David to be their king." Paul, preaching in Pisidian Antioch, takes the Chronicler's framework — Saul removed, David raised up — as a paradigm for understanding God's sovereign choice. The contrast between the failed king who sought a medium and the faithful king who inquired of YHWH becomes, in Paul's preaching, a typological frame for understanding Jesus' messianic claim.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

The Chronicler's distinctive theological agenda surfaces in this chapter with unusual sharpness. Three commitments converge: *immediate retribution* (judgment falls personally and quickly), *Davidic-temple centrality* (the kingdom transfer to David is the chapter's narrative pivot, and David's reign will be the institutional foundation for the temple), and *cultic-religious orthodoxy* (Saul's *ma'al* is specifically about consulting a forbidden source instead of *darash*-ing YHWH). The chapter is the Chronicler's manifesto in narrative form. Saul's reign — the entirety of it — is reduced to its end, because the end is the moment that matters theologically: a king who broke faith, sought guidance from the dead, and was removed by God so that David could be raised up.

The chapter sits inside one of the deepest tensions in biblical theology — the tension between *immediate* and *delayed* retribution. The Deuteronomistic History tends toward delayed: the consequences of unfaithfulness accumulate over generations, and the exile is the long-deferred reckoning for centuries of cumulative *avon*. The Chronicler tends toward immediate: each generation, even each king, faces judgment for its own sin. The Saul-narrative is the paradigm case. In Samuel, Saul's death is bound up with David's flight, with Jonathan's friendship, with the Philistine threat over decades, with a complex web of human and divine action. In Chronicles, Saul died because he was unfaithful, and the kingdom was transferred. Cause and effect are tight.

Three theological positions on this material are alive in the tradition. The first, the *immediate-retribution* reading, takes the Chronicler at his word and treats his theology as paradigmatic. The strength of this reading is that it preserves moral seriousness and personal accountability; the weakness is that it can collapse into a flat reward-punishment scheme that ignores the complexity of human suffering and divine timing. Job stands as the canonical refutation of any flat immediate-retribution theology, and the Chronicler's own community lived under the long shadow of an exile that no single generation could be said to have *deserved*.

The second position, the *delayed-retribution* reading, treats the Chronicler's theology as a useful pastoral framework but reads its sharpness in light of the broader Deuteronomistic and prophetic witness. Cross's *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* takes this line: the Chronicler's retribution theology is *one* voice in the canon, balanced by the Deuteronomist's longer view and by the Job-Ecclesiastes-Lamentations recognition that suffering exceeds desert. The strength of this reading is its canonical breadth; the weakness is that it can dilute the Chronicler's pointed theological clarity.

The third position, attempted by Williamson, Knoppers, Japhet, and Boda, is a both-and: the Chronicler's immediate retribution is a *pedagogical* theology — a framework for the post-exilic community to take its own faithfulness seriously — without being a *cosmic* theology that claims to explain all suffering. Boda's *A Severe Mercy* makes this case strongly: the Chronicler's quick, personal judgments are God's mercy in disguise, because they teach the community to turn before catastrophic accumulation occurs. The strength of this reading is that it accounts for both the Chronicler's sharpness and the canon's broader witness; the weakness is that it can soften the chapter's verdict in ways the Chronicler himself does not authorize.

To steelman the immediate-retribution reading: the Chronicler is writing for a community that had just experienced the catastrophic, long-delayed judgment of the Babylonian exile. His theology is precisely a *response* to that experience — a pastoral reorientation toward present accountability so that the catastrophe does not recur. To live as if every generation, every king, every leader, every individual must answer for their own *ma'al* is not naive; it is the wisdom of a community that knows what cumulative unfaithfulness costs.

To steelman the delayed-retribution reading: lived experience does not always fit the Chronicler's frame. Faithful people suffer; unfaithful people prosper. Job's friends took the Chronicler's logic and used it to torment a righteous sufferer. The canon's full voice resists any framework that promises clean cause-and-effect between human action and divine response.

Where this leaves us: the Chronicler's verdict on Saul is not just about Saul. It is about how communities of faith make sense of catastrophe, leadership failure, and institutional collapse. The Chronicler refuses the temptation to externalize blame — Saul did not die because the Philistines were strong, or because times were hard, or because the Spirit had departed inexplicably; Saul died for his *ma'al*. The closing question is whether modern communities of faith, facing their own institutional failures and leadership collapses, can hold the Chronicler's seriousness about *ma'al* alongside the canon's broader recognition that not every suffering is a verdict and not every catastrophe is a consequence. To learn to ask "did we *darash* YHWH?" is the Chronicler's pastoral gift — even when the answer cannot resolve every question.

## The Thread

From Mount Gilboa to Dagon's temple to Jabesh-gilead's oak to the Chronicler's two-verse verdict — the kingdom transfers because Saul broke faith. The Chronicler's compression turns twenty-two chapters of Saul's reign into a single death-narrative because what the post-exilic community most needs to learn from Saul is not how he lived but why he ended. *Ma'al* costs everything; *darash YHWH* is the alternative.
