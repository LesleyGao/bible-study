# Revelation 22: The Tree, the Face, and the Last Greek Word of the Bible

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The closing chapter of the Apocalypse — and of the Christian canon — is also the chapter where the historical-critical, the codicological, and the text-critical converge most uncomfortably. To read 22 historically is to read a text whose final form has been more contested than almost any other passage in the New Testament.

On the standard late-date hypothesis (Aune, Yarbro Collins, Koester, Bauckham), the Apocalypse was composed c. 95–96 AD under Domitian; on the early-date alternative (Gentry, Robinson) c. 65–69 AD under Nero. Either way, the chapter's *parousia* expectation — "I am coming soon" repeated three times (22:7, 12, 20) — anchors the book in earliest Christianity's eschatological pulse. The Aramaic *maranatha* preserved in Greek transliteration (1 Cor 16:22, *Didache* 10:6) and the Greek *erchou kyrie Iēsou* of 22:20 are the same prayer in two languages, attesting that the longing for Christ's return crossed linguistic boundaries from the earliest decades of the movement. The "delay of the parousia" — the perceived gap between earliest Christian expectation of imminent return and lived continuance of history — became a structural feature of New Testament eschatology (treated by Albert Schweitzer in *Quest of the Historical Jesus*, more recently and more carefully by Dale Allison and N. T. Wright).

Material culture: the river flowing from the throne (22:1) draws on Ezekiel 47, Joel 3:18, and Zechariah 14:8 — but the throne-as-source replaces the temple-as-source, and the river is *bright as crystal*, evoking the Roman aqueduct aesthetic any reader in Ephesus would recognize. The tree of life (22:2) draws on Genesis 2:9, Ezekiel 47:12 (the trees on either bank bearing monthly fruit, leaves for healing — almost verbatim), and Hellenistic-Jewish wisdom traditions (Sirach 24). The "name on the foreheads" (22:4) echoes Exodus 28:36–38's high-priestly diadem inscribed with YHWH's name; the eschatological people now collectively bear what only the high priest bore. The "morning star" (22:16) draws on Numbers 24:17, Isaiah 14:12, and Roman imperial iconography of Venus the morning star associated with Julius Caesar's deification.

**Manuscript evidence — the chapter where text criticism most matters.** P47 (Chester Beatty III, third century, our earliest substantial Greek witness for Revelation) ends at 17:2 and *does not cover* chapter 22. The major witnesses for these closing verses are Codex Sinaiticus (ℵ, fourth century), Codex Alexandrinus (A, fifth century, the best Revelation witness), Codex Ephraemi Rescriptus (C, fifth century, partial — and lacunose for parts of Revelation 22), and the body of Greek minuscules. Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* and B. F. Westcott's earlier *Introduction to the Greek New Testament* are the indispensable references.

The notorious problem: when Erasmus prepared the first published Greek New Testament (Basel, 1516), he was working under publisher pressure with whatever Greek manuscripts he could locate in Basel. For Revelation he had a single twelfth-century minuscule borrowed from Johann Reuchlin (designated 1r, also known as 2814), which was *missing the final leaf* containing Revelation 22:16–21. Erasmus, rather than wait for another manuscript, *back-translated the missing verses from the Latin Vulgate*. This produced several readings unsupported by any Greek manuscript that have nonetheless been preserved in the Textus Receptus (the lineage of Greek editions descending from Erasmus through Stephanus and Beza), and from there into the King James Version (1611). The most famous of these is in 22:19: where every Greek manuscript reads *xylou tēs zōēs* ("tree of life"), Erasmus's back-translation from the Vulgate gave *biblou tēs zōēs* ("book of life"), which is the KJV reading. Other variants in the closing verses (notably in 22:17, 21) trace to the same source. F. H. A. Scrivener catalogued these in the nineteenth century; Daniel Wallace and the Center for the Study of New Testament Manuscripts continue the textual work today.

Three key Greek terms anchor the chapter. *Erchou* (ἔρχου, "come" — present imperative) is repeated three times in 22:17 and once in 22:20 in the form *erchou kyrie Iēsou* ("come, Lord Jesus"); the Bible ends with the church's invocation. *Maran-atha* (μαράνα θά, Aramaic for "our Lord, come"), preserved in 1 Corinthians 16:22, is the Aramaic-language sister of John's Greek *erchou*. *Xylon zōēs* (ξύλον ζωῆς, "tree of life," 22:2, 14, 19) — the Genesis 2 term — is the chapter's deepest intertextual marker.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 22 closes the apocalypse and the canon. Its literary work happens at three levels at once: it concludes the New Jerusalem vision begun in chapter 21, it closes the entire book with epistolary frame (greetings, blessings, warnings, benediction), and it returns to themes from the very opening of the canon (Genesis 2–3).

The dominant intertextual operation is *return to the beginning*. Genesis 2:9–10 — "out of the ground the Lord God made to grow every tree... a river flowed out of Eden to water the garden" — is taken up directly in 22:1–2: the river of the water of life flowing from the throne, the tree of life on either side. Genesis 3:24 — the cherubim with flaming sword guarding the tree of life from fallen humanity — is reversed: the tree is now accessible, its leaves "for the healing of the nations" (22:2). Genesis 3:17 — "cursed is the ground because of you" — is reversed at 22:3: "no longer will there be anything accursed" (*katathema*, the cognate of LXX Gen 3:17's *epikataratos*). The whole biblical canon is thus framed as an inclusio: paradise lost in the opening chapters, paradise regained and amplified in the closing.

Ezekiel 47:1–12 supplies the river-and-trees imagery directly. Where Ezekiel's river flows from under the threshold of the temple, John's flows from the throne of God and the Lamb — the throne *is* the temple, as 21:22 had already announced. Ezekiel 47:12's "their leaves for healing" is taken almost verbatim. Zechariah 14:7's "there shall be no night" is repeated in 22:5. Daniel 12:9's instruction to Daniel to "seal up the vision until the time of the end" is reversed at 22:10: "do not seal up the words of the prophecy of this book, for the time is near." John's eschatological consciousness is more compressed than Daniel's; the time has arrived.

Literary devices: the chapter is structured by a triple repetition of "I am coming soon" (*erchomai tachy*, 22:7, 12, 20) and a sevenfold blessing pattern reaching its seventh in 22:7 ("blessed is the one who keeps the words of the prophecy of this book") and 22:14 ("blessed are those who wash their robes"). Inclusio with the book's opening: 22:13's "I am the Alpha and the Omega, the first and the last, the beginning and the end" answers 1:8's identical self-identification. The book's last word — the form of the benediction in 22:21 — is *amen*, which had been the church's response since Paul.

**Textual variants in the closing verses.** Beyond the Erasmus problem already detailed, three variants in 22:14 deserve discussion. The reading "those who wash their robes" (*hoi plynontes tas stolas autōn*) is found in Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus, Ephraemi, and the best minuscules and is the modern critical text's reading. The Textus Receptus (and KJV) reads "those who do his commandments" (*hoi poiountes tas entolas autou*) — a Byzantine reading found in the majority of late minuscules. The two readings sound alike in Greek (*plynontes / poiountes*; *stolas / entolas*) and the variant likely arose from auditory copying error early in the transmission, with each tradition then preserving one. The theological stakes have been argued in both directions: faith-by-baptismal-cleansing versus works-by-obedience. Bauckham (*Climax of Prophecy*) and Aune (*Word*) both argue strongly for *plynontes tas stolas* as original. The question of whether the closing curse of 22:18–19 ("if anyone adds to or takes from the words of this prophecy") refers to (a) Revelation only, (b) the entire emerging biblical canon, or (c) the immediate prophetic-apocalyptic disclosure has been argued for centuries; mainstream critical and confessional scholarship (Aune, Beale, Mounce, Osborne) holds that the warning, by parallel with Deuteronomy 4:2 and 12:32 and Proverbs 30:5–6 (which similarly close their immediate texts), refers to the book of Revelation only.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Chapter 22 has been read across all four major schools (preterist, historicist, futurist, idealist/eclectic) and across the millennial spectrum, but the most contested questions here are different from chapter 20. They are: the canonicity and authority of Revelation itself, the meaning of the closing curse, and the eschatology of imminent expectation.

The patristic period received Revelation slowly and unevenly. The Western church (Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian) accepted it from the second century; the Eastern church was more cautious — Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.25) lists it among the disputed books, and Cyril of Jerusalem omits it from his canon list. The Council of Carthage (397) confirmed it for the Latin West; the Greek East accepted it gradually but never made it part of the Orthodox lectionary. Origen's allegorical reading turned the river and tree into spiritual symbols. Augustine (*City of God* 22.30) reads chapter 22 as a vision of the eschatological Sabbath, the eternal rest of the saints. The medieval Western tradition (Bernard, Aquinas) follows Augustine, with the closing curse read as applying to the canonical Scriptures generally.

The Reformation produced two strikingly different responses. Luther was famously suspicious of Revelation in his 1522 preface (he placed it, along with Hebrews, James, and Jude, at the end of his New Testament with a separate preface noting his doubts), then later softened his judgment. Calvin wrote no commentary on Revelation. Erasmus's textual decisions about the closing verses — described above — became, against his intent, the foundation of the Textus Receptus and KJV tradition. The Westminster Confession received Revelation as canonical without question.

The nineteenth and twentieth centuries split. Premillennial dispensationalism (Darby, Scofield, Walvoord, Pentecost, Ryrie) reads "I am coming soon" as referring to the rapture-tribulation-millennium-eschaton sequence, with "soon" understood as imminent in the prophetic sense (any moment, but with no time committed). Historic premillennialism (Ladd, Mounce, Osborne) reads "soon" as eschatologically imminent without the dispensational timetable. Amillennialism (Hoekema, Beale) reads "soon" as the inaugurated-and-consummated eschatology already begun at the cross-resurrection. Postmillennialism (Warfield, Boettner) reads "soon" as the trajectory of gospel triumph.

The major twentieth-century critical commentaries — Charles, Caird, Beasley-Murray, Boring, Aune (Word), Bauckham, Koester (Anchor Yale), Yarbro Collins — read the imminent expectation as a real first-century apocalyptic conviction whose non-fulfillment in the obvious sense became a theological problem the early church had to absorb (cf. 2 Peter 3:8–10's reflection on the delay). They distinguish the *what* (the parousia hope) from the *when* (the temporal expectation), holding that the former remains theologically essential even where the latter requires reframing.

Liberation readings (Pablo Richard, Allan Boesak, Brian Blount) read 22 as eschatological hope of vindication: the river of life flowing freely without payment (22:17 — *dōrean* "without price") is direct economic critique of the Roman commercial system in chapter 18; the open invitation "let the one who is thirsty come" is universal grace against imperial gatekeeping. Feminist readings (Tina Pippin, Catherine Keller, Schüssler Fiorenza) note the persistence of the Bride imagery into the chapter's invocation (22:17 — "the Spirit and the Bride say, Come"), with both critique and reclamation: the Bride is not merely passive object but active inviter, joining the Spirit in the call. Eastern Orthodox eschatology (Bulgakov) reads the chapter as the eschatological consummation of the divine-human communion, with the seeing of the face (22:4) as the *theōsis* fulfilled.

**Jewish Scholarly Engagement.** The river and tree of paradise are deep Jewish tradition. *4 Ezra* 8:52 envisions the tree of life prepared for the eschatological righteous; *2 Baruch* 73:6 imagines the messianic reign with healing and restoration. The Qumran *Hodayot* (1QH 16) speak of the eschatological tree planted by the community. Rabbinic tradition continues: *b. Berakhot* 17a describes the world to come as the righteous "sitting with their crowns... enjoying the radiance of the *Shekhinah*" — a tradition closely parallel to 22:4's seeing of the face. The *Sefer ha-Razim* and the merkavah-mystical tradition develop the throne-river imagery further. The fundamental disagreement remains christological: is the throne shared with the Lamb (22:1, 3), or does the eschatological throne belong to the One God alone? Beyond that christological divide, Christian and Jewish eschatologies of paradise restored are remarkably continuous. Daniel Boyarin (*Border Lines*), Adele Reinhartz, and Amy-Jill Levine have insisted on this continuity against supersessionist readings.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** All major Christian traditions affirm the canonicity of Revelation (despite Eastern slowness and Lutheran reservation) and read 22 as eschatological consummation. The deepest disagreements: (1) the closing curse — does it apply to Revelation only, to the whole canon, or to oral teaching? (2) the imminent expectation — is "soon" psychological (as Christ-time, near every generation), prophetic-imminent, or temporally specific? (3) the exclusion of "dogs and sorcerers" (22:15) — eschatological permanent fact, or rhetorical-pastoral warning? (4) the textual question — which form of 22:14, and which form of 22:19? Within Judaism, the chapter's invocation of Christ as morning-star and root of David is the prior disagreement; once that is bracketed, the eschatology of paradise restored is shared territory.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the seer believe God was doing? Two genuine theological tensions structure the chapter.

**Tension 1: To what does the closing curse apply — Revelation only, the whole Bible, or all theological teaching?** The text reads: "I warn everyone who hears the words of the prophecy of this book: if anyone adds to them, God will add to him the plagues described in this book, and if anyone takes away from the words of the book of this prophecy, God will take away his share in the tree of life and in the holy city, which are described in this book" (22:18–19). The fundamentalist reading takes this as applying to the whole biblical canon: any modification of any biblical text incurs the curse. The traditionalist reading takes it as applying to all authoritative ecclesial teaching. The mainstream critical-confessional reading (Aune, Beale, Mounce, Osborne, Catholic Catechism's prudential silence on the question) takes it as applying *only to the book of Revelation*, by parallel with Deuteronomy 4:2 ("you shall not add to the word that I command you, nor take from it") and Proverbs 30:5–6 — Hebrew Bible parallels that similarly close their *immediate* texts and were not understood as canonical-totality warnings. Liberation readings note that 22:18–19 has been weaponized historically to prevent reform of imperial-Christian teaching; the curse warns against distorting *the prophetic vision of judgment on empire*, not against reform itself.

The strongest case for the broad application is that the church for many centuries read the verse as applying to all canonical Scripture, and that this reading has given the Christian tradition its strong textual conservatism. The strongest case for the narrow application is the parallel with Deuteronomy and Proverbs, the wording "this book" (singular, *touto biblion*), and the historical fact that when Revelation was written, the New Testament canon as such did not yet exist — there was nothing called "the Bible" in the modern sense for the curse to refer to. The Erasmus / Textus Receptus history, ironically, demonstrates that the very tradition most invested in textual conservatism has itself transmitted readings (the "book of life" of 22:19 KJV) that resulted from precisely the kind of modification the verse warns against.

The dispensationalist position deserves to be steelmanned. Its core claim is that prophetic Scripture is a tightly sealed disclosure whose authority depends on its preservation, and that any allegorizing or symbolizing of the prophetic timeline constitutes a "taking away" from the words of the prophecy. One does not have to accept the dispensational hermeneutic to recognize that the question — what counts as *honoring* a prophetic text? — is a serious one that any interpretive tradition must answer.

*Where this leaves us:* the closing curse applies most directly to the book of Revelation itself; its broader application to the canon is a later theological inference; and it speaks to the seriousness of receiving prophetic Scripture without licensing the textual-political weaponization the verse has sometimes suffered.

**Tension 2: "I am coming soon" — psychological imminence, eschatological inauguration, or unfulfilled temporal expectation?** The text says it three times in the chapter and frames the entire book with imminent expectation. The traditional Western dogmatic reading (Augustine, Aquinas, the Reformers, contemporary Catholic and Reformed orthodoxy) reads "soon" as eschatologically imminent in the sense that no event must intervene before the parousia — Christ is "always coming soon" because no historical scheduling stands between us and his return. The dispensationalist reading (Walvoord, Pentecost) reads "soon" as imminent in the prophetic sense that the rapture could happen at any moment, with the seven-year tribulation and millennial kingdom following. The historical-critical reading (Schweitzer in his strongest form, more carefully Aune, Yarbro Collins, Bauckham) reads "soon" as a real first-century temporal expectation that did not happen as expected, and treats the church's subsequent reframing — beginning with 2 Peter 3:8–10's "with the Lord one day is as a thousand years" — as a theological adjustment to the delay. N. T. Wright's reading distinguishes the destruction of the temple in 70 AD as one fulfilled "coming" of the Son of Man (Matt 24, Mark 13) from the eschatological consummation that remains future, holding both as real comings without flattening them into one. Liberation readings hold the imminent expectation as eschatological hope of the oppressed: the empire's vindication will come, *and soon* — even if "soon" by God's clock is a longer span than human suffering can easily bear. Eastern Orthodox eschatology dissolves the temporal question into liturgical time: every Eucharist is a "coming," and the parousia is the consummation of what has already been arriving.

The strongest case for taking "soon" temporally is that the early church evidently expected the parousia within their lifetimes (1 Thess 4:15, 1 Cor 7:29, Rev 22 itself), and intellectual honesty requires acknowledging that this expectation was not literally fulfilled. The strongest case for the dogmatic-theological reading is that "soon" as used in apocalyptic literature consistently bears compressed-perspective sense — God's relation to time is not measured by human chronology — and that the New Testament itself begins to thematize this in 2 Peter 3 and Hebrews 11:13–16. The dispensationalist position deserves steelmanning: its insistence on prophetic literalism has at least the virtue of taking the text's plain sense seriously.

*Where this leaves us:* the imminent expectation was real, the disappointment was real, and the church's reframing of "soon" was theological work done in response to a genuine pastoral and intellectual problem — not a betrayal of the text but a faithful struggle with it.

**One unresolved question.** If the river of the water of life flows freely without payment (22:17 — *dōrean*, "as a gift, without price"), and "the leaves of the tree are for the healing of the nations" (22:2), and "outside are the dogs and sorcerers and the sexually immoral and murderers and idolaters and everyone who loves and practices falsehood" (22:15) — what is the relationship between the radical openness of the invitation and the apparent finality of the exclusion, and is "outside" temporary or permanent?

## The Thread

**Genesis 2:9–10 / Genesis 3:24 → Revelation 22:1–3.** The grandest biblical inclusio. Genesis opens with a garden, a river, and a tree of life from which fallen humanity is excluded by cherubim with flaming sword; Revelation closes with a city in which the river still flows, the tree is replanted on both banks, and the cherubim with flaming sword are absent — the curse is undone, the access restored. The whole canon between them is the narration of that restoration.

**Ezekiel 47:1–12 → Revelation 22:1–2.** Ezekiel's eschatological river-from-the-temple, with trees on either bank bearing monthly fruit and leaves for healing, is taken up almost verbatim — but the source is now *the throne of God and of the Lamb*, not the temple. The transformation is christological-trinitarian: the throne replaces the sanctuary, the Lamb is co-source of life with the Father.

**Numbers 24:17 / Isaiah 11:1 → Revelation 22:16.** Christ identifies himself as "the root and the descendant of David, the bright morning star" — fusing Numbers 24:17's star of Jacob, Isaiah 11:1's root of Jesse, and the cosmic star imagery used by Roman imperial propaganda for Caesar. The morning star, named for Venus, the brightest light just before dawn, becomes a christological title.

**1 Corinthians 16:22 / Didache 10:6 → Revelation 22:20.** The Aramaic *maranatha* preserved in Paul and the *Didache* is the Greek *erchou kyrie Iēsou* of John's closing verse — the church's earliest eschatological prayer in two languages. The Bible ends with the church's invitation to Christ, not with a doctrinal statement, not with a moral conclusion, but with a single Greek imperative: *come*.
