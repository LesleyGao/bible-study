# Revelation 22: The Tree Replanted, the Face Unveiled

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Authorship and dating.** John of Patmos — almost certainly not the apostle of the Fourth Gospel — writing to the seven churches of Roman Asia. Mainstream dating, anchored by Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 5.30.3, c. AD 180), places the vision at the end of Domitian's reign, around AD 95 — the consensus across Aune's WBC, Bauckham, Beale, Koester. A respectable minority (J.A.T. Robinson, Kenneth Gentry) argues a Neronian date in the late 60s. Chapter 22 is the book's epilogue, mixing one final vision (vv. 1-5) with prophetic-epistolary closing material (vv. 6-21).

**Structural function.** Chapter 22 closes the canonical Bible. The intertextual saturation per square inch is the highest in scripture. Almost every line reaches back, deliberately, to specific Genesis and Exodus motifs and reverses or fulfills them.

**The river of water of life (22:1).** *Potamon hydatos zōēs lampron hōs krystallon, ekporeuomenon ek tou thronou tou theou kai tou arniou* (ποταμὸν ὕδατος ζωῆς λαμπρὸν ὡς κρύσταλλον, ἐκπορευόμενον ἐκ τοῦ θρόνου τοῦ θεοῦ καὶ τοῦ ἀρνίου) — flowing from the throne of God *and of the Lamb*. The trinitarian implication of that *kai* is sharp: the Lamb shares the throne of God, and the river that flows is the river of life that proceeded once from Eden. The four headwaters of Genesis 2:10-14 — the river that went out of Eden to water the garden — culminate here. The Ezekiel 47:1-12 source is even more direct: a river issuing from the threshold of the temple, growing as it flows, with fruit-bearing trees on each bank whose leaves are *for healing*. Zechariah 14:8 ("living waters shall flow out from Jerusalem") and Joel 3:18 ("a fountain shall come forth from the house of the Lord") fill out the prophetic background. The Old Testament eschatological river-from-the-sanctuary tradition culminates in 22:1.

**The tree of life (22:2).** *Xylon zōēs* (ξύλον ζωῆς) — the explicit return to Genesis 2:9 and 3:22-24. The tree from which Adam and Eve were barred at the close of Genesis 3 ("the LORD God sent him out from the garden of Eden... He drove out the man, and at the east of the garden of Eden he placed the cherubim and a flaming sword that turned every way to keep the way to the tree of life") is now in the city center. The barrier is removed. Twelve kinds of fruit, monthly — abundance and twelve-tribes symbolism. The leaves *eis therapeian tōn ethnōn* (εἰς θεραπείαν τῶν ἐθνῶν) — for the healing of the nations — is the chapter's most universalist note, continuing 21:24-26's procession of the nations into the city. Bauckham reads this as the climax of the conversion-of-the-nations theme: the leaves are *for* (εἰς, purposive) the healing.

**"No more curse" (22:3).** *Kai pan katathema ouk estai eti* (καὶ πᾶν κατάθεμα οὐκ ἔσται ἔτι). The word *katathema* is rare — a hapax in the New Testament — and is the explicit reversal of the curse-formula of Genesis 3:14-19. The chiastic *protology-to-eschatology* arc closes here: creation → fall (curse) → covenant → Christ → uncursed new creation. The single Greek word does the work of unwinding the entire post-Eden narrative. (Some manuscripts have *katanathema*; the meaning is the same.)

**"They shall see his face" (22:4).** *Opsontai to prosōpon autou* (ὄψονται τὸ πρόσωπον αὐτοῦ). This is the chapter's most theologically dense single phrase. It is the direct overturning of Exodus 33:20 — *ou gar mē idēi anthrōpos to prosōpon mou kai zēsetai* in the LXX, "no one shall see me and live." Moses, in the cleft of the rock, was permitted only to see God's *back*; his face was hidden. The Aaronic blessing of Numbers 6:25 — "the LORD make his face shine upon you" — was a partial mitigation of the prohibition. Psalm 27:8's "your face, LORD, do I seek" was the prayer's longing. 1 Corinthians 13:12's "now we see in a mirror dimly, but then face to face" is the Pauline anticipation. 1 John 3:2's "we shall see him as he is" closes the New Testament's strand. The entire trajectory of the *visio Dei* — the beatific vision tradition that Augustine develops in *City of God* XXII, that Aquinas anatomizes in *Summa* I.12, that Bonaventure and Catherine of Siena and Hildegard of Bingen take into the mystical-contemplative register, that the Eastern Orthodox tradition reads through *theōsis* and Gregory Palamas's essence-energies distinction — anchors here. The face hidden since Eden is seen.

**"His name on their foreheads" (22:4)** is the counterpoint to 13:16's mark of the beast and the seal of the saved at 7:3 and 14:1. The community marked by the divine name now stands with the divine name visible on their foreheads — high-priestly imagery (the high priest wore "Holy to the LORD" on his forehead-plate, Exod 28:36-38) democratized to the entire community.

**Maranatha (22:20).** The Greek text reads *Erchou kyrie Iēsou* (ἔρχου κύριε Ἰησοῦ) — "Come, Lord Jesus" — but stands behind it the Aramaic *marana tha* (מרנא תא, μαραναθα), "Our Lord, come!" Paul preserves the Aramaic original at 1 Corinthians 16:22, and the *Didache* 10:6 preserves it as a closing eucharistic acclamation: *maran atha. amēn.* This is the earliest Christian liturgical petition we possess, almost certainly older than any Greek New Testament document. That it is preserved in Aramaic is the most powerful internal evidence we have that the earliest Aramaic-speaking Jewish-Christian community already addressed the risen Jesus as *mar*, "Lord" — the title used in the Aramaic targumim for YHWH. The high Christology was there from the beginning.

**The curse on adding or subtracting (22:18-19).** *Ean tis epithēi ep' auta... ean tis aphelēi apo tōn logōn tou bibliou tēs prophēteias tautēs.* This is the direct formal echo of Deuteronomy 4:2 ("you shall not add to the word that I command you, nor take from it") and Deuteronomy 12:32. The curse-formula was a known ancient Near Eastern colophon — also found in *1 Enoch* 104:10-11 and the Letter of Aristeas 311 — meant to protect the integrity of a particular text. Despite later Reformation-era deployment as a *sola scriptura* warrant against post-canonical accretions and against modernist scholarship, the original referent is almost certainly limited to *this prophecy* — the book of Revelation itself, the *biblion tēs prophēteias tautēs* — not to the entire New Testament canon, which had not yet been closed when John wrote. Bauckham, Aune, Beale, and Koester are all clear on this point. The hermeneutical-rhetorical force was anti-modification of the prophet's own words.

**"I am the Alpha and the Omega" (22:13)** closes the inclusio with 1:8 and 21:6. Three appearances of the formula bracket the book.

**Key Greek terms.** *Therapeia* (θεραπεία, healing — 22:2); *prosōpon* (πρόσωπον, face — 22:4); *katathema* (κατάθεμα, curse — 22:3); *xylon zōēs* (ξύλον ζωῆς, tree of life — 22:2, 14, 19); *maranatha* (μαραναθα — Aramaic preserved through 22:20).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

**Genre.** The chapter mixes apocalyptic vision (vv. 1-5), prophetic oracle (vv. 6-7, 12-13, 16), epistolary closing with grace-greeting (v. 21), and liturgical petition (v. 17, 20). The fusion is unusual and deliberate. Revelation begins (1:4-6) with epistolary opening and ends (22:21) with epistolary closing — fully Pauline in form — *but enclosing* an apocalypse. The book is a circular letter of apocalyptic content, sent to seven actual churches (1:11), and the closing chapter performs that hybrid genre to the very last verse.

**The closing inclusio.** Verse 6's "These words are trustworthy and true" matches 21:5's "these words are trustworthy and true" and looks back to 1:1's "to show his servants what must soon take place." Verse 7's "Behold, I am coming soon" matches 1:3 and 22:12. Verse 8's "I, John, am the one who heard and saw these things" matches 1:9. Verse 13's "I am the Alpha and the Omega" matches 1:8 and 21:6. Verse 16's "I, Jesus, have sent my angel" matches 1:1. The entire closing chapter is a deliberate ring-composition with the opening chapter; the book is, formally, a single sustained vision bracketed by mirrored frames.

**The Old Testament intertextual density.** Beyond the four primary sources (Gen 2-3, Ezek 47, Exod 33, Deut 4/12), shorter echoes ride through the whole chapter: Isaiah 55:1's free water ("Come, everyone who thirsts" → 22:17's "let the one who is thirsty come"), Daniel 12:4's instruction to seal the words (here pointedly *un-*sealed at 22:10 — the eschaton is near, the prophet's words are open), Genesis 3:24's flaming sword (here disarmed by the open gates of 22:14), Numbers 6:24-26's priestly blessing (the face-shining now consummated). Bauckham's *The Climax of Prophecy* documents this density at length — Revelation does not allude lightly; it composes by recombination of the prophetic inheritance.

**The "blessed" (μακάριος) formula.** The seven *makarios* sayings of Revelation (1:3, 14:13, 16:15, 19:9, 20:6, 22:7, 22:14) reach completion here. Verse 7: "Blessed is the one who keeps the words of the prophecy of this book." Verse 14: "Blessed are those who wash their robes." This is the apocalyptic-prophetic register of the Sermon on the Mount's Beatitudes, recast.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Patristic.** Irenaeus's *Against Heresies* 5.32-36 reads Revelation 21-22 against Marcionite and Gnostic dualism: matter is good, the body will rise, the new earth is continuous with this one. Augustine's *City of God* XXII develops the *visio Dei* of 22:4 as the eternal blessed life. Origen's *De Principiis* 3.6 frames the *apokatastasis* hypothesis (universal restoration), drawing on universal-scope passages including 22:2's healing of the nations.

**Medieval.** Aquinas's *Summa* I.12 is the locus classicus for the beatific vision. *Benedictus Deus* (1336) defined it dogmatically: the souls of the just see the divine essence "by an intuitive vision and even face to face." Dante's *Paradiso* canto 33 is the imaginative consummation of 22:4. Bonaventure's *Itinerarium Mentis in Deum*, Catherine of Siena's *Dialogue*, and Hildegard of Bingen's *Scivias* render the vision in mystical-affective register.

**Reformation.** Luther reads the chapter christologically — the Lamb sharing the throne, the morning star (22:16) as Christ — but trims the medieval beatific-vision apparatus. Calvin's *Institutes* III.25 affirms the heavenly inheritance with deliberate reserve, holding the *visio* as relational-encounter rather than ontological-vision in the Thomist sense. Anabaptists (Hubmaier, Pilgram Marpeck) read 22:14's "wash their robes" as ecclesial discipline anticipated in the gathered congregation.

**Modern critical.** Bauckham's *Theology of the Book of Revelation* and *The Climax of Prophecy* are indispensable. N.T. Wright's *Surprised by Hope* reorients evangelical eschatology around new creation. Adela Yarbro Collins, Christopher Rowland, Brian Blount, Wilfrid Harrington, William Hendriksen, George Caird, Robert Mounce, Eugene Boring across commentary traditions; Jürgen Moltmann's *The Coming of God* gives the most expansive systematic treatment. Catherine Keller's ecological-feminist reading takes the river-and-tree imagery into present political commitment. James VanderKam and John Collins on the Jewish apocalyptic background.

**Contemporary Catholic and Orthodox.** *Lumen Gentium* 48-51 and *Gaudium et Spes* 39 frame the eschatology. CCC §§1023-1029 collects the doctrine of the beatific vision; §§1042-1050 the new creation. Pope John Paul II in *Dominum et Vivificantem* and Pope Benedict XVI in *Spe Salvi* meditate on 22:4 as the horizon of Christian hope. Eastern Orthodox theology — Lossky, Bulgakov — reads 22:4 through *theōsis* and Palamas's essence-energies distinction. Sarah Coakley on contemplation; J. Todd Billings on the Reformed retrieval; Stanley Hauerwas's critique of the thinness of evangelical eschatological imagination.

**Four interpretive schools, applied to 22.** *Preterist:* the river, tree, and unveiled face are present realities of the new-covenant church inaugurated at AD 70 — we live in this now (Chilton, Gentry). *Historicist:* the chapter is the future culmination toward which the church has been moving across history, with the present church anticipating it. *Futurist (dispensational):* the chapter is the literal future eternal state, sometimes located after a literal millennium (Walvoord, LaHaye); the river, tree, and city are spatially real. *Idealist:* the chapter's realities are the eschatological breaking-in, anticipated and consummated in the church's worship and life now.

**Jewish scholarly engagement.** The Genesis 2-3, Ezekiel 47, and Zechariah 14 sources are also the proximate sources of rabbinic eschatology. *Olam Ha-Ba* (the world to come — *Mishnah Sanhedrin* 10:1, *Pirke Avot* 4:21), the renewed *gan eden* (*Bereshit Rabbah* 21:9, *Pesikta Rabbati* 41), the river that will flow out of the Holy of Holies in messianic times (*Targum Pseudo-Jonathan* on Ezek 47, *B. Sanhedrin* 100a) — the Jewish-Christian eschatological grammar is shared. Daniel Boyarin's *Border Lines* and *The Jewish Gospels* argue exactly this. The healing of the nations (22:2) parallels Isaiah 19:24-25 (Egypt and Assyria as God's people alongside Israel) and the rabbinic universalist trajectory of the *seven Noahide laws* for Gentiles (*B. Sanhedrin* 56a). The single sharp Christian-Jewish divergence in this chapter is the throne of *God and of the Lamb* (22:1, 22:3) — the binitarian-trinitarian throne that Jewish monotheism does not share. Jon Levenson and John Goldingay model how the dialogue can honor both the shared rootage and the genuine divergence.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What God is doing in Revelation 22 is closing the canonical arc by reversing the closure of Genesis 3. The flaming sword that kept Adam from the tree of life is gone. The tree itself is in the city center. The river that flowed out of Eden runs through the streets. The face of God, hidden from Moses in the cleft of the rock, is seen. The curse that fell on the ground in Genesis 3 is unsaid. The covenant formula that was promised across the entire Hebrew Bible — *they shall be my people and I will be their God* — is consummated. This is the canonical inclusio at its most radical: the Bible ends by undoing the close of its third chapter.

### Tension 1: The Beatific Vision (22:4)

**The Catholic-Aquinas tradition.** The *visio beatifica* is the soul's culminating intellectual-volitional encounter with the divine essence itself. Aquinas in *Summa* I.12 and III, Suppl., systematizes this: the saints, by the *lumen gloriae* (the light of glory infused by grace), are enabled to see God's essence directly — not just God's effects, not just images of God, but God *as God is*. *Benedictus Deus* (1336) made this dogmatic: the just souls in heaven see the divine essence "by an intuitive vision and even face to face." CCC §1028 affirms it. Dante's *Paradiso* 33 is the imaginative crystallization. Steelmanned: this reading takes 22:4's *opsontai to prosōpon autou* with maximum ontological weight. The face is *seen*, not just encountered. The longing of every prayer in the Psalms — "your face, LORD, do I seek" — receives its consummate answer: not metaphor, not approximation, but actual *vision* of God-as-God-is.

**The Eastern Orthodox tradition: theōsis and the uncreated light.** Gregory Palamas's essence-energies distinction (*Triads*, fourteenth century, against Barlaam) holds the divine *essence* (*ousia*) forever unknowable, but the divine *energies* (*energeiai*) — God's actions, presences, manifestations — knowable, communicable, participable. The light of Mt. Tabor was uncreated divine energy made visible. *Theōsis* — deification — is the creature's participation in the divine energies. Vladimir Lossky's *Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church* and Sergei Bulgakov's *The Bride of the Lamb* are the indispensable modern statements. On this reading, 22:4 is the eternal seeing of the uncreated light — not the grasping of the divine essence (which would dissolve creature into Creator) but eternal participation in the divine energies. The face is real, the vision is real, but the abyss of the essence remains beyond.

**The Reformed and evangelical reading.** More relational-encounter than ontological-vision in the Thomist sense. Calvin in *Institutes* III.25 hesitates before the speculative apparatus. J. Todd Billings represents the contemporary Reformed retrieval. The face is met in the person of the risen Christ (the only proper *prosōpon* of God for human beings); the encounter is covenantal, personal, communal — not principally a metaphysical event. Steelmanned: this reading honors the Christ-mediated character of the vision (the throne is of God *and of the Lamb*, 22:1) and resists the Platonic-philosophical baggage that the medieval beatific-vision tradition arguably carried. Hauerwas's running critique that evangelical eschatology has been too thin to hold up under pastoral pressure is a goad here, not a counter-argument: the Reformed-evangelical line needs to develop its account of 22:4, not abandon it.

*Where this leaves us:* the three traditions are doing different things, but they converge on the central scriptural fact — the face of God is seen — and they diverge on what *seeing* means. The convergence matters more than the divergence.

### Tension 2: "Do not add or take away" (22:18-19)

**The narrowly literal reading.** The original referent is *this prophecy* — the book of Revelation itself, the *biblion tēs prophēteias tautēs* — not the entire New Testament or the entire Christian Bible. The form is a known ancient Near Eastern colophon (Deut 4:2, Deut 12:32, *1 Enoch* 104:10-11, Letter of Aristeas 311) protecting a specific text. Bauckham, Aune, Beale, and Koester all read it this way. Steelmanned: the reading honors the historical-exegetical evidence that the New Testament canon was not yet closed when John wrote, and avoids retrojecting later canonical concerns onto the ancient text.

**The expansively-canonical reading.** The Reformation deployment of 22:18-19 as warrant for *sola scriptura* and against post-biblical accretions (whether Catholic tradition or modernist scholarship). The reading reaches beyond the original referent because it sees in 22:18-19 a principle: scriptural texts are inviolable. Steelmanned: this reading captures something theologically true about how the church has always treated scripture — as canonically closed, as not subject to free editorial revision — even if the verse's original scope was narrower.

**The Catholic-functioning hermeneutical reading.** *Dei Verbum* 9-10: the deposit of revelation is the Word of God in scripture and tradition together, interpreted authoritatively by the magisterium. 22:18-19 applies to the revealed deposit but accommodates the magisterium's interpretive authority — which is not "addition" but authentic reading. Steelmanned: this reading honors both the inviolability of scripture and the historical fact that scripture has always been interpreted within an interpretive community; pure *sola scriptura* without an interpretive community is, on the Catholic view, a fiction.

*Where this leaves us:* the verse's original referent is narrow, but the principle has reasonably been extended to the whole canon — provided the extension is recognized as principled-extension rather than as a literal reading of John's intent. The deeper question is what counts as "adding" or "taking away" — and this is where the hermeneutical traditions visibly diverge.

**Closing question.** If the gates of the new Jerusalem are never shut (21:25), and outside are "the dogs and the sorcerers" (22:15), and the Spirit and the bride still say "Come" (22:17) — then is the boundary between inside and outside a wall, or a door, or an invitation? The book ends with Jesus saying "Surely I am coming soon" and the church saying "Come, Lord Jesus" — but the only wall mentioned in the closing vision is one whose gates never close.

## The Thread

- **Genesis 2:9-10 + 3:22-24** — the original tree of life and the river out of Eden; the flaming sword that closes Genesis 3. The inclusio that 22:1-3 closes: the river is back, the tree is back, the curse is gone, the gates are open.
- **Ezekiel 47:1-12** — the river-from-the-sanctuary source. Read in full alongside Revelation 22; the verbal dependence (water flowing, fruit-bearing trees on each bank, leaves for healing) is direct and sustained.
- **1 Corinthians 16:22** — the *maranatha* parallel. The earliest Aramaic Christian prayer preserved, also in *Didache* 10:6 — evidence that the highest Christology was there from the beginning, in the very earliest layer of the Aramaic-speaking Jewish-Christian community.
- **Exodus 33:20** — "no one shall see me and live." The prohibition that 22:4 lifts. Read with Numbers 6:25 (the priestly blessing), Psalm 27:8 (the prayer's longing), 1 Corinthians 13:12 (the Pauline "face to face"), 1 John 3:2 ("we shall see him as he is"). The *visio Dei* trajectory of the entire Christian Bible terminates here.
