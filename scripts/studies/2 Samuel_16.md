# 2 Samuel 16: Curse, Counsel, and the Roof

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter unfolds across three distinct topographic and political stations: the descent eastward from the Mount of Olives summit (Ziba's encounter), the Benjaminite village of Bahurim (Shimei's cursing), and the rooftop in Jerusalem (Absalom's appropriation of the concubines). Each setting is materially specific and politically charged.

**Bahurim** (בַּחֻרִים) is a Benjaminite village on the eastern slope of the Mount of Olives ridge, identified by most scholars (Mazar; Aharoni, *Land of the Bible*) with Ras et-Tmim or nearby Khirbet Almit, on the route descending toward the Jordan. It appears earlier in the Court History as the village where Paltiel weeps when Michal is taken from him (3:16) and will reappear in 17:18 as the location of the well that hides David's messengers. That a Saulide loyalist like Shimei son of Gera (Gera being a Benjaminite clan name; cf. Judges 3:15) lives here is geographically natural: the Benjamin–Judah fault line ran through this corridor, and Saulide partisans had not been pacified by David's southern consolidation.

The provisions Ziba brings (16:1) are catalogued with unusual specificity — two saddled donkeys, two hundred loaves (*lechem*, לֶחֶם), one hundred *tzimmuqim* (raisin clusters, צִמֻּקִים), one hundred *qayitz* (summer fruits — figs, dates; קַיִץ), and a *nevel yayin* (skin of wine). These match the standard ANE provisions list for a fleeing royal household and parallel Abigail's gift to David in 1 Sam 25:18 — the parallel is almost certainly intentional. Iron Age IIA storage and transport vessels matching this inventory are well attested at sites like Tell el-Ful and Tell en-Nasbeh (Aharoni; Mazar).

The cursing scene introduces several technical Hebrew terms. Shimei calls David an *ish ha-damim* (אִישׁ הַדָּמִים, "man of bloods" — the plural intensifies the charge of bloodguilt; cf. 2 Sam 21:1's famine for Saulide blood) and an *ish ha-beliya'al* (אִישׁ הַבְּלִיַּעַל, "worthless man," literally "man of belial," with sinister overtones — *beliya'al* in 1 Sam 2:12 of Eli's sons; later Qumran demonologized; cf. McCarter's discussion). Abishai's slur "*ha-kelev ha-met ha-zeh*" (הַכֶּלֶב הַמֵּת הַזֶּה, "this dead dog") is standard ANE court invective for a despised inferior; Mephibosheth uses the same self-deprecation in 9:8.

The rooftop tent (*ohel*, אֹהֶל) of 16:22 is the chapter's most archaeologically interesting setting. Iron Age IIA roofs in Judah were flat, plastered, accessible by external stair, and used for sleeping in summer, drying produce, and ritual (cf. 1 Sam 9:25-26; Jer 19:13). The pitching of a tent on the palace roof for Absalom's public consummation with the ten concubines weaponizes domestic architecture — what is normally a place of shelter and intimacy becomes the visible stage of dynastic claim. The "in the sight of all Israel" (16:22) means literally that: Jerusalem's hillside topography meant the palace roof was visible from much of the surrounding city. The act was a public coronation by sexual conquest.

The ANE practice underlying Ahithophel's counsel is well documented. Possession of a predecessor's harem was the formal claim of succession across the ancient Near East — Hittite, Assyrian, and earlier Israelite (compare Reuben taking Bilhah in Gen 35:22; Abner taking Rizpah in 2 Sam 3:7; Adonijah's request for Abishag in 1 Kings 2:22, which Solomon reads as treason and executes him for it). The act made succession legally irreversible. Ahithophel knew exactly what he was prescribing: not adultery for shock value, but legal severance.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 16 deepens the architecture of the Court History (Rost 1926) by establishing **three narrative threads that will run in parallel** through chapters 17-19: Ziba/Mephibosheth (resolved in 19:24-30), Shimei (resolved in 19:16-23 and again in 1 Kings 2), and Ahithophel/Hushai (resolved in 17:23). Fokkelman's literary commentary on Samuel notes that the chapter functions as a triptych — three encounters, each testing David's judgment under stress, each leaving an unresolved residue.

**Genre and structure.** The chapter is historiographic prose with strong dialogue dominance — only the rooftop scene (16:21-22) is narrated rather than spoken. Sternberg's "scenic preference" governs the design: each of the three encounters is a contained dramatic scene with entry, exchange, and exit. Bar-Efrat's analysis of biblical narrative time observes that the chapter compresses geographically dispersed events into a continuous reading flow, while signaling spatial transitions with terse markers ("a little beyond the summit," "when David came to Bahurim," "now Absalom and all the people").

**Devices.** The Ziba scene (16:1-4) operates by **strategic ambiguity**. The narrator does not tell us whether Ziba is lying about Mephibosheth — that resolution is deferred to 19:24-30, and even there the narrator refuses to adjudicate definitively. Alter and Sternberg both treat this as a textbook case of biblical narrative's "fraught reticence" — the reader is given two competing testimonies (Ziba's now, Mephibosheth's later) and David's hasty judgment between them, but never authorial commentary. The chapter's ethics depend on this withholding.

The Shimei scene is built on **lexical density around blood**. *Damim* (bloods) appears twice in 16:7-8, framing the curse. The verb *qalal* (קָלַל, "curse") and its derivatives appear seven times in 16:5-13 — the dense repetition makes Shimei's curse the auditory atmosphere through which David walks. Hebrew narrative rarely uses such concentrated lexical hammering; when it does (cf. the seven uses of *kavod*/glory in 1 Sam 4), it is signaling theological weight. Polzin reads the seven *qalal*s as a counter-blessing: Shimei's curse is the inverted Aaronic benediction David is being made to receive.

David's response (16:10-12) is one of the most theologically dense speeches in the Court History. The Hebrew *ulai* (אוּלַי, "perhaps") in 16:12 is the chapter's quiet hinge — David does not claim to know YHWH's purpose; he holds open the possibility that the curse is providential. Alter notes that *ulai* is the Hebrew of theological humility — the same word Abraham uses in Genesis 18 ("perhaps fifty righteous"). Sternberg's *Poetics of Biblical Narrative* dwells on this verse as an example of "epistemic restraint" — David refuses to project certainty onto providence.

**The chapter's literary high point** is Ahithophel's counsel at 16:21 and the narrator's parenthetical 16:23: "*Now in those days the counsel that Ahithophel gave was as if one consulted the word of God*." This aside is one of the rare moments the narrator breaks frame to evaluate a character — and the evaluation is double-edged. Ahithophel's counsel is oracular in quality and demonic in content. The verse sets up the irony of 17:14, where YHWH directly nullifies "the good counsel of Ahithophel." Polzin reads the pair as a meditation on the limits of human wisdom against divine purpose.

**Textual variants.** 4QSama preserves several minor variants in this chapter, including a slightly different formulation of Shimei's curse and a different word order in 16:23. Tov's analysis suggests the Qumran witness here reflects an Old Palestinian tradition closer to what the LXX translator used. McCarter's AB commentary adopts several 4QSama readings against MT.

**Place in the book argument.** Chapter 16 is the **fulfillment chapter** for Nathan's prophecy of 12:11-12: "*I will take your wives before your eyes and give them to your neighbor, and he shall lie with your wives in the sight of this sun. For you did it secretly, but I will do this thing before all Israel and before the sun.*" The verbatim repetition of "in the sight of all Israel" / "before this sun" makes 16:22 a citation of 12:11-12. Fokkelman calls this the "echo chamber" technique — the narrator forces the reader to hear Nathan's voice through Ahithophel's mouth. The Bathsheba episode is now publicly costed.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish trajectory.** Josephus (*Antiquities* 7.207-211) softens the Shimei scene — he attributes the curse to madness and emphasizes David's restraint as exemplary monarchic virtue. He omits the rooftop scene almost entirely, telescoping it into a brief mention of Absalom following Ahithophel's counsel; the omission is consistent with Josephus's general pattern of softening sexual content for his Greco-Roman audience.

The rabbis treat the Shimei encounter at length. **Sanhedrin 107a**, building on the same David-flight tradition that grounds Psalm 3, sees David's acceptance of Shimei's curse as a moment of voluntary atonement — David receives the humiliation as the chastisement Nathan announced. Rashi on 16:10 paraphrases: "Even though I know I am not guilty of Saul's blood, perhaps the Holy One has put it in his mouth that I should be humbled." The midrash *Pesikta Rabbati* expands this into a meditation on the spiritual discipline of accepting unjust accusation when it serves a higher purpose. Radak (Kimhi) reads David's *ulai* as theological prudence — the king does not presume to know providence's content. Ralbag focuses on the political wisdom of restraint: a fleeing king who kills cursing subjects creates more enemies.

**Abarbanel** is again the standout. His commentary on 16:1-4 reads Ziba's deception as a textbook example of how royal authority is manipulated by intermediaries during crisis. Abarbanel — who served as treasurer to Alfonso V of Portugal and later advised the Catholic Monarchs of Spain before the 1492 expulsion — knew firsthand how courtiers exploit royal absence. His judgment on David's snap decision to grant Ziba all of Mephibosheth's lands is sharp: the king who judged so wisely in the first half of his reign now judges hastily under stress. Abarbanel sees in this a pattern: stress degrades judicial faculty, and a king must build institutions that compensate for it. His reading of the rooftop scene is equally political: Absalom needed an act of irreversibility to bind Israel to him, and Ahithophel — who knew David would never forgive — provided one. (Some Jewish tradition reads Ahithophel's motive as private vengeance for Bathsheba his granddaughter; Abarbanel allows the reading but emphasizes the political logic.)

Modern Israeli scholarship: Yair Zakovitch reads the rooftop as the precise inversion of David's rooftop in 11:2 — David saw Bathsheba bathing on her roof and took her; now Absalom takes David's concubines on a roof. The chiastic geometry is intentional. Zornberg's psychological reading dwells on David's strange passivity in the Ziba and Shimei scenes — he is a man emptied of agency by grief. Garsiel's pun-analysis notes that Shimei's name (שִׁמְעִי, "Yah hears") is bitterly ironic against his curse — the *hearer* speaks, and Yah hears the curse too.

**Christian trajectory.** Origen (fragmentary) reads Shimei typologically as the Jewish opposition to Christ; the patristic supersessionist reading is uncomfortable but historically present. **Ambrose** (*Apologia David* and *De obitu Theodosii*) develops the chapter's central christological figure: David's silent acceptance of Shimei's curse prefigures Christ's silence before false accusers (Mt 26:62-63; 27:12-14). The figure is theologically substantial — both kings absorb unjust verdict without retaliation, and both cite providence as their reason. **Augustine** (*City of God* 17) extends the typology, reading Ahithophel's counsel-and-suicide arc (which begins in 16:23 and culminates in 17:23) as a figure of Judas. Augustine is careful: David is not Christ, but the Davidic plot is shaped by the same providence that will shape the Passion plot.

Chrysostom (homilies on 2 Samuel) emphasizes David's *ulai* as exemplary Christian humility under suffering. Gregory the Great (*Moralia*) cites the Shimei scene as paradigmatic for the contemplative life: the wise soul accepts unjust accusation rather than defending itself. Aquinas (*Summa* II-II, q. 76 on cursing) cites David's restraint as evidence that the prudent ruler distinguishes between curses one must answer and curses one must absorb.

**Calvin's** sermons on 2 Samuel 16 (preached 1562) are extensive and theologically incisive. He treats the chapter under three heads: (1) Ziba's deception illustrates how slander prospers in royal absence and why magistrates must hear both sides — Calvin is sharp on David's haste here; (2) Shimei's curse and David's restraint together demonstrate the doctrine of providence's "double agency" — Shimei sins genuinely and YHWH uses the sin justly; (3) the rooftop scene is the visible payment for the secret sin of 2 Sam 11, and Calvin uses it to argue for the public consequences of private wickedness in any covenanted ruler. Luther's *Lectures* are briefer but similar.

**Critical-historical.** Wellhausen treated chapters 15-19 as the J source's dramatic core. Cross's Harvard school placed the underlying narrative very early — perhaps tenth-century court material. McCarter's AB commentary defends a tenth-century date for the core; A.A. Anderson (WBC) is similar; Hertzberg (OTL) is the older standard. **Halpern's** *David's Secret Demons* and McKenzie's *King David* both read the chapter as political apologetic — every action the narrator records is one that protects David's later legitimacy. Halpern is especially sharp on the rooftop scene: by leaving the concubines, David made Absalom commit an irreversible offense that legitimized later disqualification, while himself remaining (legally) an aggrieved party.

**Contemporary.** Brueggemann reads David's restraint of Abishai as the chapter's theological center — the king who refuses to silence his accuser is the king who has learned to live under the prophetic word. Polzin's Deuteronomistic reading sees the chapter as the Court History's most intentional staging of providence-through-failure. Eugene Peterson (*Leap over a Wall*) dwells on Bahurim as the place where David learned to listen to curses. Davis and Goldingay both treat David's *ulai* as a model of Christian epistemic humility. **Feminist criticism** is sharpest here: Exum, Bal, and especially **Brenner** treat the rooftop scene as the chapter's moral indictment. The ten concubines have no name, no voice, no consent, and no agency. They are political objects in both 12:11 and 16:22, and 20:3 confirms their lifelong sequestration "as widows." Brenner's reading is uncompromising: the text uses women's bodies as currency in male political plots, and the prophetic word itself participates in this. Whether 12:11 is critique or endorsement is the chapter's most uncomfortable interpretive question.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is Shimei's curse from YHWH or not?**

Position A (David's "perhaps"): The curse may be providential — David refuses to foreclose the possibility. The text validates this stance through David's later restoration.

Position B (Shimei is a free moral agent who genuinely sins): David's restraint is exemplary precisely because Shimei *isn't* speaking for YHWH — he is a malicious Benjaminite and David refuses to add his blood to Saul's house. This is Calvin's "double agency" reading: Shimei sins genuinely and YHWH uses the sin justly.

Position C (the curse is partially correct): David *is* a man of bloods (Uriah; the Saulide handover in 21:1-9; the unauthorized census soon to come). Shimei's accusation contains truth, even if his motives are corrupt. The chapter's theology accepts that enemies sometimes speak true judgments.

Steelman of C: David himself never denies the charge. He says "let him curse," not "his curse is false." The narrative does not vindicate David against Shimei's content, only against Shimei's right to speak.

**Where this leaves us:** Calvin's double agency is the most coherent reading. Shimei's curse is morally his own act (and David's later charge to Solomon to execute Shimei in 1 Kings 2:8-9 confirms David did not consider him innocent), but its content is providentially permitted because it contains truth David needs to hear. The chapter teaches a difficult discipline: receiving accurate accusations from impure mouths.

**Tension 2: Was David right to grant Ziba Mephibosheth's estate?**

Position A (Ziba was telling the truth; David judged correctly under pressure; Mephibosheth's later defense in 19:24-30 is self-serving). Some rabbinic readings (e.g., portions of Shabbat 56a) take this view — Mephibosheth genuinely hoped for restoration and was caught.

Position B (Ziba lied; David judged hastily; the chapter's silence is indictment, not vindication). Most contemporary critics (McCarter, Brueggemann, Davis) read it this way.

Position C (Halpern's political reading): David knew Ziba was probably lying but had no time to investigate, needed the provisions, and made a deal — the later "divide the land" verdict in 19:29 is David's confession that he can't tell.

Steelman of C: David's later "divide the land" is precisely what one says when one has been unable to resolve a factual dispute and wants both parties placated. It is the verdict of a king who knows he was hasty and refuses to reverse himself fully.

**Where this leaves us:** The text's silence is the answer. Biblical narrative knows how to vindicate or condemn; it does neither here. The reader is meant to feel David's compromised judgment without being told what to conclude. The chapter teaches that crisis-decisions taken under pressure leave permanent residues that even subsequent restoration cannot fully clean.

**One unresolved question:** What ethical weight does the narrator's parenthetical evaluation of Ahithophel (16:23 — counsel "as if one consulted the word of God") bear? Is the narrator endorsing his wisdom, or setting up the irony of 17:14? The answer probably depends on whether biblical wisdom literature can be amoral — whether brilliant counsel that produces evil ends counts as wisdom at all. Proverbs 1-9 says no. The Court History seems less sure.

## The Thread

The chapter sets up cross-references that structure the rest of the unit and the larger canonical arc:
- **16:3-4 (Ziba's accusation)** → 19:24-30 (Mephibosheth's counter-defense; David's "divide the land").
- **16:5-13 (Shimei's curse)** → 19:16-23 (Shimei's begging and pardon) → 1 Kings 2:8-9, 36-46 (Solomon's execution per David's deathbed charge).
- **16:21-22 (rooftop concubines)** → 12:11-12 (Nathan's prophecy fulfilled verbatim) → 20:3 (concubines' lifelong sequestration).
- **16:23 (Ahithophel's counsel as oracle)** → 17:14 (YHWH defeats his counsel) → 17:23 (Ahithophel's suicide).

**Christological typology.** The patristic tradition reads David's silent acceptance of Shimei's curse (16:9-12) as a Passion figure: the king who absorbs unjust accusation without retaliation, citing the Father's permission. **Mt 26:62-63**: "But Jesus remained silent." **Mt 27:12-14**: "But he gave him no answer, not even to a single charge." **Lk 23:34**: "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do." Ambrose develops the parallel most fully; Augustine extends it. The figure is real — both kings move under unjust accusation toward what looks like defeat, both refuse to weaponize their authority, both cite providence as their reason. But the differences hold: David accepts Shimei's curse as possibly deserved; Christ is innocent. David is on his way to political restoration; Christ is on his way to death. Typology that holds the asymmetry rather than collapsing it is the only kind worth doing here.

What 2 Samuel 16 leaves us with is the exact public spectacle Nathan promised. The roof of Bathsheba's seduction has become the roof of Absalom's appropriation. Providence has reached the bedroom. David walks east into the wilderness, weary and refreshed at the Jordan; Absalom enters Jerusalem and lies with the concubines under the Judean sun. The two scenes happen in the same hours. The Court History is showing us the grammar of consequence: every secret sin produces a public bill, and the bill comes due in the architecture of one's own house.

Chapter 17 will move from spectacle to strategy — from Ahithophel's symbolic counsel to his tactical one. And the narrator will tell us, finally, who is actually moving the chess pieces.
