# Daniel 1: Diet, Names, and the Architecture of Diaspora

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The opening verse situates the chapter inside one of the most heavily attested events in the Hebrew Bible: the Neo-Babylonian campaigns against Judah at the turn of the sixth century BC. Nebuchadnezzar II (r. 605–562 BC), fresh from his victory over Pharaoh Necho at Carchemish, marched south and reduced Jerusalem to a tributary in 605 BC, deporting royal hostages and temple vessels — the deportation Daniel 1 narrates. The **Babylonian Chronicle** (BM 21946, the so-called "Jerusalem Chronicle" published by D.J. Wiseman in 1956) confirms in cuneiform terms what 2 Kings 24 and Jeremiah 25 narrate from Judah's side: the second deportation followed in 597, the destruction of the city in 587/6. The Babylon to which Daniel and his companions were taken was at the apex of its imperial self-presentation — Esagila, the rebuilt ziggurat Etemenanki, the *bit hilani* royal palace, the Ishtar Gate (now reassembled in the Pergamon Museum), and the gardens that Berossus (preserved by Josephus, *Against Apion* 1.19) credits to Nebuchadnezzar's homesick Median queen. The "land of Shinar" of v. 2 is the archaizing biblical name for Babylonia, deliberately echoing Genesis 11 and tagging the empire as the heir to Babel.

The court training described in vv. 3–5 is not generic. To learn "the literature and language of the Chaldeans" was to be inducted into the Babylonian *ummânu*, the scholarly class of literate court experts who kept the cuneiform corpus — the omen series *Enuma Anu Enlil* (celestial divination), the dream-interpretation manual known to Assyriologists as *Zaqiqu*, the medical-diagnostic *SA.GIG*, the *Enuma Elish* creation epic, and the literary classics. A Judean youth marked out for the Babylonian wisdom tradition was being groomed for the very technical skill set the rest of the book showcases: dream interpretation, oneiromantic discernment, mantic wisdom. The "royal portion" of v. 5 — Hebrew *pat-bag* (פַּת־בַּג) — is not a Hebrew word at all but an Old Persian loanword (*patibaga*), one of the philological clues that has fueled the dating debate for two centuries: a sixth-century narrator setting a story in Babylon could plausibly know the term, but its presence (alongside Greek loanwords for instruments in ch. 3) tilts most critical scholars toward a later editorial hand.

Authorship and dating is, as **John J. Collins** notes in his Hermeneia commentary (1993, the standard critical reference), the most contested introductory question in Old Testament scholarship. The conservative position — held by **Stephen Miller** (NAC, 1994), **Gleason Archer**, **John Whitcomb**, and **Joyce Baldwin** (TOTC, 1978, the careful bridge text) — defends sixth-century composition by Daniel himself, treats the predictions of chapter 11 as genuine prophecy, and accounts for the linguistic features by appeal to imperial Aramaic in the eastern diaspora. The critical consensus — Collins, **Carol Newsom** (OTL, 2014), **Maurice Casey**, **Lester Grabbe**, **Norman Porteous**, **Philip Davies**, **R.H. Charles** (1929), **James Montgomery** (ICC, 1927), **H.H. Rowley** — argues that the book was finalized during the Maccabean crisis under Antiochus IV Epiphanes (167–164 BC). Three converging lines drive that conclusion: the eerie precision of chapter 11 down to about 165 BC and its abrupt failure thereafter (the *vaticinium ex eventu* argument); the presence of Persian and Greek loanwords; and the references to the *maskilim* (מַשְׂכִּלִים, "the wise"), which match the reformist Hasidic intelligentsia of the Maccabean era. The argument is not modern. **Porphyry of Tyre**, the third-century Neoplatonist, made it in the lost fifteenth book of his *Against the Christians*; **Jerome** preserves and tries to refute it in his *Commentary on Daniel* (AD 407). Few interpretive controversies have run this long.

Archaeology supplies a second wedge. The **Nabonidus Chronicle** and the inscriptions of Nabonidus's reign show that Belshazzar (Daniel 5) was co-regent under his father Nabonidus, not the son of Nebuchadnezzar — an apparent historical "error" Daniel sustains. More striking still, the **Prayer of Nabonidus** (4QPrNab, found in Cave 4 at Qumran, dated paleographically to about 100 BC) tells the story of *Nabonidus* — not Nebuchadnezzar — afflicted by an ulcer/madness during a seven-year retreat to Tema in the Arabian desert and healed through a Jewish *gazer* (diviner). The parallel to Daniel 4 is structurally exact and the personalia rebound onto the more famous king. For Collins, Newsom, and Grabbe this is the strongest single argument that the Daniel court tales are literary reworkings of older Babylonian traditions. The **Aramaic block** of the Masoretic text — chapters 2:4b through 7:28 in Imperial Aramaic, with chapters 1 and 8–12 in Hebrew — is the major source-critical seam visible on the page. Eight Daniel manuscripts have surfaced at Qumran (4QDan-a through e, 1QDan-a, 6QDan, plus 4QPrNab as parallel tradition), making Daniel one of the better-attested OT books in the scrolls. The Greek tradition forks into the **Old Greek** recension and **Theodotion**, which are radically different in chapters 4–6 and which the church eventually replaced with Theodotion in most Christian use.

A handful of key terms recur across the book and are introduced here. *Maskilim* (מַשְׂכִּלִים, "the wise/instructors"), the implied audience of Daniel and the term Louis Finkelstein and others identify with the Maccabean-era pietist scribes. *Pat-bag* (פַּת־בַּג, the Persian "royal portion"). *Hartummim* (חַרְטֻמִּים, "magicians") — an Egyptian-loan term carried over from Joseph's Egyptian court (Gen 41:8) and applied here to Babylonian diviners, a deliberate cross-canonical echo. And anticipating chapter 2: *raz* (רָז, Aramaic "mystery"), itself a Persian loanword.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Daniel 1 is a textbook **diaspora court tale** — the genre **Susan Niditch** (*Underdogs and Tricksters*) and **W. Lee Humphreys** ("A Life-Style for Diaspora," JBL 1973) defined as the form in which a young foreign hero rises in a foreign court, is tested, vindicated, and exalted. Joseph in Genesis 37–50 is the canonical prototype; Esther, Tobit, and the Aramaic Story of Aḥiqar are the closest parallels. The form is comforting and didactic at once: it tells exiles that faithfulness is compatible with success, that the foreign king can be both threat and patron, and that providence works through palace politics. Chapters 1–6 belong to this genre; with chapter 7 the book turns sharply to apocalyptic.

The chapter's literary devices are economical. The chiastic structure of the larger Aramaic block (chs. 2–7), first mapped by A. Lenglet (1972) and adopted by Collins, has chapter 2 paired with chapter 7 (four kingdoms), 3 with 6 (miraculous deliverance), and 4 with 5 (humbled kings) — chapter 1, in Hebrew, sits as a Hebrew bookend with chapters 8–12. Leitwort patterning is heavy: *malkut* ("kingdom") appears more than fifty times in the book, *raz* ("mystery") nine, and the pairing of *yada* ("know") and *bin* ("understand") is the keystroke of the wisdom register. The chapter's typology runs through the Joseph story — vegetables and water echo Joseph's similar test of fidelity; Daniel's name change parallels Joseph's renaming as Zaphenath-paneah (Gen 41:45); the appearance "fatter and fairer" recalls Joseph's flourishing in Pharaoh's house. **Collins** notes that this is not subtle: the redactor wants the reader to read Daniel through Joseph's lens and to read Babylon as the new Egypt.

Two decisions about canonical placement are worth noticing. In the Hebrew Bible, Daniel sits in the *Ketuvim* (Writings) — alongside Esther, Ezra-Nehemiah, and Chronicles — not the *Nevi'im* (Prophets). This is a Jewish canonical decision that Christian readers rarely register. The Septuagint and Vulgate moved Daniel to the prophets, where it remains in Christian Bibles. The mid-textual question is the textual variant problem: the Old Greek and Theodotion recensions diverge sharply, and the **Greek Additions to Daniel** (the Prayer of Azariah and the Song of the Three Young Men in chapter 3, Susanna, Bel and the Dragon) are present in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles, removed by Jerome under his *hebraica veritas* principle, and rejected by the Reformers as apocryphal. The Catholic Daniel is meaningfully longer than the Protestant Daniel.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, chapter 1 inaugurates the second great empire-encounter of biblical Israel. Egypt was the first; Babylon is the second; the four-kingdoms schema that emerges in chapters 2 and 7 will sequence Babylon → Medo-Persia → Greece → Rome (or, in the critical reconstruction, Babylon → Media → Persia → Greece). Daniel 1 reads as the prologue to that schema and as exile theology in narrative form: the temple vessels deposited in the treasury of a foreign god (v. 2) are the unresolved theological wound the rest of the canon — through Ezra, Haggai, Zechariah, and the New Testament — will attempt to heal.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** Second-Temple readers picked up Daniel almost immediately. **1 Maccabees 2:59–60** cites Daniel 3 and 6 as exemplars of resistance martyrdom; **4QPrNab** sits beside the canonical Daniel at Qumran as a related tradition; **Josephus** retells chapter 1 in *Antiquities* 10.10. Rabbinic engagement is dense — Bavli Sanhedrin 92b–94a treats Nebuchadnezzar's fate, Megillah 3a discusses Daniel's role in the exilic identity formation, the Targums of Daniel circulate in Aramaic paraphrase. **Saadia Gaon's** Daniel commentary in the tenth century is the first major Jewish treatment, defending sixth-century authorship and reading the four kingdoms as Babylon-Persia-Greece-Rome with messianic edge. **Rashi**, **Ibn Ezra**, and **Abrabanel** (whose extensive fifteenth-century commentary remains a touchstone) extended the line. Modern Jewish scholars — **Robert Gordis**, **Louis Finkelstein** on the *maskilim*, **Aaron Koller**, **Daniel Boyarin** in *The Jewish Gospels* — have read Daniel as resistance literature against Antiochus and have reclaimed the "son of man" tradition (chapter 7) as a Jewish messianic schema rather than a Christian invention.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** **Hippolytus of Rome's *Commentary on Daniel*** (c. AD 200) is the earliest extant Christian commentary on any biblical book — a fact often missed. Hippolytus reads the four kingdoms as Babylon-Persia-Greece-Rome, identifies the Stone of Daniel 2 as Christ, and inaugurates the millennarian tradition. **Jerome's *Commentary on Daniel*** (AD 407) is in part written to refute Porphyry's late-dating thesis; Jerome preserves Porphyry's argument while contesting it. **Theodoret of Cyrrhus** continues the patristic line. Medieval engagement runs through **Nicholas of Lyra**, **Thomas Aquinas**, and **Joachim of Fiore**, whose twelfth-century apocalyptic reworking shaped later Reformation chiliasm. **Luther's *Preface to Daniel*** (1530) identifies the Pope as the little horn of Daniel 7; **Calvin's *Lectures on Daniel*** (1561, two volumes) is a remarkable late work that defends sixth-century authorship and reads the chapter as a model of pious civil engagement. The critical line — Bertholdt (1806), **S.R. Driver** (*Introduction to the Literature of the Old Testament*, 1891), Charles, Montgomery, Rowley — reaches Collins, Newsom, and Casey today.

Where the trajectories converge: that Daniel 1 is foundational diaspora theology, that the chapter sets the literary template for the book, that Daniel and his companions are exemplars of a particular kind of fidelity. Where they diverge: on dating (sixth century vs. Maccabean), on the meaning of the four kingdoms (Rome continuing into the modern era vs. Antiochus IV as the climax), and on the typological-Christological reading (Stone as Christ in Hippolytus vs. Stone as the Maccabean restoration in Collins). The dating debate is the single most theologically loaded scholarly disagreement on the book — because if the book is sixth century, the predictions are predictive; if Maccabean, they are *vaticinia ex eventu*, and the genre is closer to apocalyptic encouragement-literature than to prophecy in the Isaianic sense.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two genuine tensions in this chapter deserve patient handling.

**Tension 1: Assimilation versus resistance.** Daniel and his three companions accept the foreign training, accept the foreign names (Hananiah → Shadrach, Mishael → Meshach, Azariah → Abednego, Daniel → Belteshazzar — three of the four new names embed Babylonian theophoric elements; Belteshazzar invokes Bel, Abednego is "servant of Nebo"), and accept the offer of court office. They draw the line at food. The chapter is therefore not a parable of separatist refusal but of *partial assimilation under principled limits*. The question of where to draw the line is the question Jewish diaspora communities have asked every generation since.

The Catholic position, articulated in Vatican II's *Lumen Gentium* and *Gaudium et Spes*, treats cultural participation as a positive good provided the moral ecology is preserved — the Daniel model maps cleanly: education, language, civic office, with conscience-bound limits at the points where idolatry and direct cooperation in evil enter. The classical Reformed position, traced through Calvin's *Institutes* 4.20 and Kuyper's sphere sovereignty, similarly affirms cultural engagement under Christ's lordship; the line is drawn where the magistrate commands what God forbids. The Lutheran two-kingdoms doctrine permits robust civic participation while distinguishing temporal from spiritual authority, and historically has been more reticent about resistance. The Anabaptist tradition — **John Howard Yoder** in *The Politics of Jesus* — reads the chapter quite differently: even the partial assimilation is suspect, and the food refusal points to a deeper logic of exilic non-conformity that ought to extend further than Daniel himself extends it. Jewish readings — **Erich Gruen** on Hellenistic Jewish identity strategies, Daniel Boyarin on borderlines, **Aaron Koller** on Esther — have mapped a spectrum from full concealment (Esther) through accommodating prominence (Joseph, Daniel) to outright refusal (the Maccabean martyrs). The critical reading, with Collins and Newsom, sees the chapter as *programmatic for the Maccabean reader*: Daniel models the strategy of rigorous fidelity in the small things (kashrut as boundary marker) so that fidelity in the large things (refusal to eat the meat of Antiochus's pig) can be sustained.

The least intuitive position to steelman is the Anabaptist one. To the modern reader Daniel's compromises look reasonable, even admirable. Yoder's argument is that the reasonableness is itself the trap: the names will follow Daniel into the throne room, the language will shape his categories, the office will entangle him with the very imperial machinery the prophets unmask. The food line, on this reading, is not a heroic stand but a minimum, an indication of how far the assimilation has already gone before resistance even begins. The Anabaptist asks a pointed question: how much of what Daniel accepted should we accept? **Where this leaves us:** there is no canonical algorithm for the assimilation–resistance tradeoff. The chapter offers a model, not a rule, and the model itself is partial; readers in different empires will draw the line in different places, and the chapter does not relieve them of the burden.

**Tension 2: The dietary refusal — what is actually being defended?** The standard Jewish reading is that Daniel observes kashrut: the king's table likely included non-kosher meat and wine that had been libated to the gods. **Collins** argues that the issue is not principally dietary law but idolatry: the Babylonian royal portion would have been libated before idols (a practice attested in Akkadian ritual texts), so eating it would be an act of cultic participation. **Newsom** and others note the anomaly: vegetables and water can also be offered to gods, so the distinction will not strictly hold; the deeper logic is symbolic boundary-marking, an embodied refusal to be incorporated into the king's body politic on the king's terms.

The Catholic Council of Trent affirmed continuity between the moral content of Old Testament law and the church's life while teaching that ceremonial law (including dietary law) is fulfilled and superseded in Christ — *Decree on Justification*, sessions 6 and 7. The Reformed line through **Calvin's *Institutes*** 4.10 reads dietary law as ceremonial and abrogated, while preserving the principle that conscience must not be bound to indifferent things. The Adventist tradition has taken a contrary line, preserving health-law observance as ongoing witness. Acts 10–15 — Peter's vision and the Jerusalem Council — is the New Testament hinge: the Gentile mission requires releasing the food-law boundary, but Paul retains the idolatry-related concern in 1 Corinthians 8 and 10.

The Maccabean reader hears chapter 1 differently again. In 167 BC Antiochus's commissioners forced Jews to eat pork on pain of death; **2 Maccabees** 6–7 narrates the resulting martyrdoms. To a reader in 165 BC, Daniel 1 is not a domestic moral tale but a charter document for refusal. The question "what is being defended in 1:8?" has no single answer because the chapter is doing several things at once: ritual boundary, idolatry refusal, identity preservation, political-theological signal. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter resists being reduced to a single moral lesson, and the long history of competing readings is itself evidence that the text was always more dense than any single application. The ceremonial law has been fulfilled in Christ for Christian readers, but the structural question — what embodied disciplines preserve the people of God in an absorptive empire — remains live, and the answer in any given setting is closer to wisdom than to rule.

**One unresolved question.** If Daniel accepted the Babylonian name, the Babylonian education, and the Babylonian office, and drew his line at the Babylonian table, what is the line for a reader inside the empire that has thoroughly remade that reader's appetites — the empire whose books, languages, offices, and tables we have already accepted without registering them as foreign?

## The Thread

The Joseph parallel is the deepest substrate: Genesis 39–41 (favor in a foreign court, dream interpretation, exaltation, names changed) is the literary substrate Daniel 1 reworks. **Esther** offers the contrasting model: full concealment of Jewish identity, no recorded resistance to the king's table, salvation through hidden providence rather than open confession — read together, Esther and Daniel define the spectrum of diaspora possibilities.

Forward to the New Testament, the chapter opens two threads. First, **1 Corinthians 8 and 10** rework the food-and-idolatry question for the Gentile churches: the categorical distinction loosens, the underlying concern (no participation in idolatry, no occasion for stumbling) survives. Second, the deportation of the temple vessels in v. 2 is the loose end the Stone of Daniel 2 — and, in the patristic reading, **1 Corinthians 10:4** ("the rock was Christ") and **1 Peter 2:7** ("the stone the builders rejected") — eventually resolves: the empire's seizure of the vessels is not the last word on the temple's God.
