# Psalm 104 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 104 carries no superscription in the Masoretic Text — it stands anonymous, untethered from any specific Davidic crisis or cultic festival. The Septuagint supplies one (*tō Dauid*, "Of David"), but this is editorial smoothing, not historical witness. The psalm's silence about its own origin is part of its meaning: it is a hymn to the Creator that wishes to belong to no particular biographical moment. Hermann Gunkel classified it cleanly as a *Hymnus*, the praise-form par excellence, and within that genre as the supreme example of the *Schöpfungslied* — the creation song. Sigmund Mowinckel located it in the *Yahweh-malak* festival cluster, where Yahweh's kingship is celebrated as kingship over the cosmos he made and sustains. Most modern critics (Hans-Joachim Kraus, Frank-Lothar Hossfeld, Erich Zenger, John Goldingay) have followed some version of this reading: the psalm belongs to the temple liturgy of the Second Temple period in its present form, but its raw materials are far older.

Dating is genuinely difficult. The vocabulary is largely classical Biblical Hebrew, with no clear Aramaisms forcing a late date, but the editorial pairing with Psalm 103 (matching opening and closing inclusio, *bārakhî nafshî et-Yahweh* / בָּרֲכִי נַפְשִׁי אֶת-יְהוָה, "Bless the LORD, O my soul") suggests a deliberate post-exilic redaction that placed the two together. Most scholars settle on a long compositional history: pre-exilic core, exilic or post-exilic editorial framing, integration into Book IV of the Psalter as part of the *Yahweh-malak* answer to the Davidic crisis of Books I–III.

The single most important historical-critical observation about Psalm 104 is its parallels with the **Great Hymn to the Aten**, the fourteenth-century BC Egyptian hymn associated with Pharaoh Akhenaten's solar monotheism. The parallels are extensive and specific. Both hymns describe the sun setting and lions emerging from their dens; both describe humans going out to their labor at sunrise; both describe ships on the sea, birds in their nests, and animals receiving their food in due season; both depict the deity providing for cattle and wild creatures alike. James Allen, the Egyptologist, has documented the verbal and structural correspondences in detail. Brevard Childs, Patrick Miller, Cyrus Gordon, and Mark Smith have all argued that some form of dependence — direct or mediated — is the most economical explanation. The question is what kind of dependence.

There are two main positions. The **direct-borrowing** view holds that an Israelite scribe (perhaps in the late second millennium, perhaps later through Phoenician or Canaanite intermediaries) knew the Aten Hymn or a tradition derived from it and rewrote it as a hymn to Yahweh. The **shared-tradition** view holds that the Aten Hymn and Psalm 104 both draw on common ancient Near Eastern stock — solar imagery, animal-list praise, providence theology — without one being directly dependent on the other. Mark Smith's intermediate position is probably the most defensible: there is a real genealogical relationship through the Levantine sphere, but it is not a simple copy-paste, and the theological reframing in Psalm 104 is so thorough that "appropriation" rather than "borrowing" is the better word.

The Hebrew vocabulary is dense with creation-language. Verse 1's *hôd wəhādār lāvāštā* (הוֹד וְהָדָר לָבָשְׁתָּ, "you are clothed with splendor and majesty") uses royal-investiture language; verse 2's *‛ōteh-’ôr kassalmâ* (עֹטֶה אוֹר כַּשַּׂלְמָה, "covering yourself with light as with a garment") and *nôteh šāmayim kayrî‛â* (נוֹטֶה שָׁמַיִם כַּיְרִיעָה, "stretching out the heavens like a tent") construct a portable tabernacle-cosmos, with Yahweh wearing light and pitching the sky. Verse 26's *liwyātān zeh-yāṣartā ləśaḥeq-bô* (לִוְיָתָן זֶה-יָצַרְתָּ לְשַׂחֶק-בּוֹ, "Leviathan, which you formed to play in it") is the single most striking de-mythologization in the Hebrew Bible: the chaos-monster of Ugaritic Baal-cycle theology, the seven-headed serpent Lōtan, has been demoted to Yahweh's bath-toy. Verse 30's *təšallaḥ rûḥăkhā yibbārē'ûn* (תְּשַׁלַּח רוּחֲךָ יִבָּרֵאוּן, "you send forth your Spirit, they are created") uses *bārā'* — the same verb as Genesis 1:1, the verb reserved in the Hebrew Bible almost exclusively for divine creating.

Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, Psalm 104 appears in 4QPsd, 4QPse, 11QPsa, and 2QPs, with text that is largely consistent with the Masoretic tradition. The Septuagint's Psalm 103 (LXX numbering) follows closely. There are no major textual disturbances, which is unusual for a long psalm and itself a sign of how central this text was to Second Temple worship.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Robert Lowth's parallelism is everywhere in Psalm 104, but the psalm's deeper architecture is its **strophic correspondence to the seven days of Genesis 1**. This is one of the oldest exegetical observations on the psalm and remains the most fruitful. Verses 1–4 correspond to Day 1 (light, heavens). Verses 5–9 correspond to Day 2 and the gathering of waters, Day 3a. Verses 10–18 expand Day 3b (vegetation, springs, trees, animals fed by them). Verse 19–23 correspond to Day 4 (sun and moon as time-markers). Verses 24–26 correspond to Day 5 (sea creatures, Leviathan, ships). Verses 27–30 correspond to Day 6 and the providence over all living things. Verses 31–35 form the doxological closure, corresponding loosely to the Sabbath rest in which the Creator "rejoices in his works" (104:31) — a striking inversion of Genesis 2's rest, where here the rest is *active enjoyment* rather than cessation.

The opening and closing **inclusio** — *bārakhî nafshî et-Yahweh* — frames the entire psalm and pairs it with Psalm 103, which uses the same frame. This is editorial work of high intelligence: Psalm 103 is the hymn of God's covenant mercy to humans, Psalm 104 is the hymn of God's providential mercy to all creation. The pair forms a diptych on divine *ḥesed* in two registers, individual-historical and cosmic-creational.

The psalm has no *selah*, which is itself notable. Where Psalm 105 uses none and Psalm 106 uses none either, the long historical-creational hymns of Book IV's close move continuously. Psalm 104's flow is wave-like, each strophe ending with a small cadence and the next picking up the rhythm.

The parallelism is Lowth's "synonymous" type in places — "the wild donkeys quench their thirst / Beside them the birds of the heavens dwell" — but more often it is "synthetic" or "progressive": the second line extends the first rather than restating it. James Kugel's revision of Lowth applies well here. "*A*, and what is more, *B*" — God lays the beams of his chambers on the waters, **and what is more**, makes the clouds his chariot, **and what is more**, rides on the wings of the wind (104:3). The escalation is part of the doxological intensification.

Two image clusters dominate. The first is **clothing**: light as garment (v. 2), the deep as garment over the earth (v. 6), splendor and majesty as garment (v. 1). The Creator wraps himself and the world in fabric, and the world is something *worn* rather than merely made. The second is **water**: waters covering, fleeing, set within boundaries, springing from valleys, watering mountains, filling the sea. Water is the medium through which the Creator's providence becomes visible, and the psalm spends more verses on water than on any other element.

Verse 35's imprecation — "Let sinners be consumed from the earth, and let the wicked be no more!" — is the single most jarring literary turn in the psalm. After thirty-four verses of cosmic celebration, the closing wish for the eradication of the wicked feels like a tear in the fabric. Some critics have proposed it as a later editorial addition; the manuscript evidence does not support this. The verse must be read as part of the original design, and the question is what to do with it.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Psalm 104 sits in **Book IV of the Psalter (Psalms 90–106)**, the Mosaic-creational book that answers the Davidic-covenant collapse of Book III. Gerald Wilson's canonical-shape thesis, refined by David Mitchell and others, reads Books I–III as the rise, reign, and ruin of the Davidic monarchy, climaxing in the desolation of Psalm 89's "How long, O LORD?" Book IV opens with Psalm 90's prayer of Moses and structures itself around the *Yahweh-mālāk* psalms (93, 95–99) — "the LORD reigns" — as the answer to the failed kingship of David. Psalm 104 is part of this Mosaic-creational answer: when human kings fail, the Creator-King remains, and his kingship is older and more secure than any throne in Jerusalem.

Within Book IV, Psalm 104 pairs with Psalm 103 to form the doxological hinge before the historical recital of Psalms 105–106. The movement is: cosmic *ḥesed* (103) → cosmic *creation* (104) → covenant-historical fidelity (105) → covenant-historical failure (106). Psalm 104 is the ground from which the historical psalms launch their argument: the God who feeds the wild donkeys can be trusted to feed Israel, and the God who set boundaries for the sea can be trusted to set Israel's exile in proper limits.

The **Jewish trajectory** treats Psalm 104 as the signature *Berakhi Nafshi* psalm. The *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 104 dwells on the strophic correspondence to Genesis 1, anticipating the modern critical observation by a millennium and a half. Rashi reads verse 4's "he makes his messengers winds, his ministers a flaming fire" as referring to the angels — a reading the Letter to the Hebrews 1:7 inherits. Ibn Ezra is more grammarian than allegorist and focuses on the Hebrew syntax. Radak (David Kimchi) emphasizes the natural-philosophical reading: God's providence as the regular order of the cosmos, knowable through reason as well as revelation. Nachmanides treats the psalm as a Kabbalistically loaded text where the *ruach* of verse 30 is the upper *Shekhinah* descending into creation. Liturgically, Psalm 104 is recited on Rosh Chodesh (the New Moon) in many traditions and during the Sabbath afternoon service in others, and a portion is woven into the Hallel cycle in some Sephardic rites.

Among modern Jewish thinkers, Martin Buber treats the psalm as the supreme example of the I–Thou relation extended cosmically: every creature is addressed by the Creator, and the human's task is to enter that universal address. Abraham Joshua Heschel reads it through his "radical amazement" lens — the psalm is the textual equivalent of the wonder that is the proper religious response to existence. Jon Levenson's *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* uses Psalm 104 as evidence that biblical creation theology is not the closed-system creation-from-nothing of later philosophical theology but a creation that holds chaos at bay through ongoing royal vigilance — Leviathan is *demoted* but not *destroyed*. Nahum Sarna's commentary stresses the psalm's rejection of the deification of nature: the sun and moon are not gods, only time-markers.

The **Christian trajectory** is equally dense. Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 103 (LXX) reads verse 30's *ruach* as the Holy Spirit and finds the Trinity already present in the creation account: the Father creates, the Word orders, the Spirit gives life. Athanasius cites Psalm 104:30 in his *On the Incarnation* as evidence for the Spirit's role in creation, a key text for the Cappadocian articulation of pneumatology. Cassiodorus continues Augustine's allegorical-Trinitarian reading. Aquinas in the *Summa* uses Psalm 104:24 ("In wisdom have you made them all") as a key proof-text for divine wisdom as the formal cause of creation. Luther's exposition treats the psalm as a sermon on the Second Article of the creed expanded into creation, his characteristic move of finding the gospel in every Old Testament text. Calvin's commentary is one of his finest: he reads the psalm as the schoolroom of the *theatrum gloriae Dei*, the theater of God's glory, where every creature performs its part in the praise.

Modern Christian scholarship has rediscovered Psalm 104 as the key text for **eco-theology**. William P. Brown's *The Seven Pillars of Creation* devotes a chapter to Psalm 104 and reads it as a theology of *creation as temple* — Yahweh's chambers laid on the waters, the heavens stretched as the temple's curtain. Catherine Keller's *On the Mystery* uses verse 26's playful Leviathan as evidence for a non-dualistic, non-domination creation theology. Norman Wirzba's work on agrarianism and Christian ecology cites Psalm 104 repeatedly. Most consequentially, Pope Francis's 2015 encyclical *Laudato Si'* explicitly cites Psalm 104 as a foundational text for Catholic creation theology and the Church's response to the ecological crisis. Walter Brueggemann's *The Message of the Psalms* reads the psalm as "world-making praise" — language that builds a habitable cosmos against the chaos of disordered desire. Patrick Miller, William P. Brown, Beth Tanner, J. Clinton McCann, and Hossfeld-Zenger all converge on the psalm's centrality to biblical creation theology.

The **New Testament** quotes Psalm 104:4 ("he makes his messengers winds, his ministers a flaming fire") in Hebrews 1:7 as part of the argument that the angels are servants while the Son is Lord. The Septuagint translation reads the verse as referring to angels (a defensible reading of the Hebrew), and the writer of Hebrews builds on that. Beyond this direct citation, Psalm 104's creation-by-Word and creation-by-Spirit theology informs the Johannine prologue (John 1:1–4) and the Pauline cosmology of Colossians 1:15–20, where Christ is the agent in whom all things hold together — a christological extension of the *bəḥākhmâ* ("in wisdom," 104:24) of Psalm 104.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The first hard tension is the **Akhenaten parallel and what it does to the doctrine of revelation**. If Psalm 104 reworks the Great Hymn to the Aten — even at several removes through shared Levantine tradition — then the Hebrew Bible's most luminous creation poem is partly indebted to the religious imagination of fourteenth-century BC Egypt. Three positions stake out the territory.

The first is **polemical appropriation**: the psalmist knew (directly or indirectly) the Aten material and deliberately rewrote it to attack the solar monotheism of Egypt. On this reading, the sun in Psalm 104 is *demoted* to a clock (104:19), and Leviathan, which in Egyptian and Canaanite traditions threatens the sun's daily journey, is *demoted* to a creature playing in the sea. The psalm is doing to Aten what Genesis 1 does to the Babylonian *Enuma Elish*: cannibalizing its rival's poetry to make a counter-claim. James Allen, Mark Smith, and most recently Brent Strawn lean toward versions of this view. The strength of the position is that it takes both the parallels and the differences seriously: there is real genetic relationship, *and* there is real polemical reframing.

The second is **shared-tradition**: there is no genealogy, just a common ancient Near Eastern pool of solar-providence imagery. On this reading, the Aten Hymn and Psalm 104 are independent witnesses to a widespread regional consensus about how to praise a high god. The strength of this view is its parsimony — no transmission history needs to be reconstructed. Its weakness is that the verbal and structural parallels are unusually specific. James Hoffmeier and a few evangelical scholars hold versions of this position, sometimes in service of a stronger doctrine of inspiration.

The third is **divine condescension**: the parallels are real, the borrowing is real, and this is exactly what we should expect of a God who speaks through human poets in human cultures. On this reading, the psalm's relationship to Aten is no more scandalous than the Septuagint's use of Greek philosophical vocabulary or the New Testament's use of imperial cult language. Calvin's accommodation theology, applied to the question of comparative religion, lands here. Mark Smith's later work, with its emphasis on "translatability" between ancient religions, edges in this direction.

**Where this leaves us**: a chastened confidence. The psalm is neither pure revelation falling from heaven nor a derivative pastiche; it is what divine inspiration through human language always looks like — a borrowed vocabulary turned to a new purpose. To pretend the parallels do not exist is dishonest scholarship. To use the parallels to dissolve the psalm's claim is bad theology. The polemical-appropriation reading honors both and is the most defensible.

The second hard tension is **the imprecation of verse 35**. After thirty-four verses of cosmic celebration that includes lions, donkeys, badgers, storks, and Leviathan in the great congregation of praise, the psalmist asks that "sinners be consumed from the earth, and the wicked be no more." Three positions on what to do with this.

One reading takes the verse as a **prayer for moral restoration of the cosmos**. The psalm has been celebrating the order of creation; the wicked are the disorder within that order; the prayer is that creation's beauty be matched by humanity's righteousness, even at the cost of the wicked's removal. C. S. Lewis's *Reflections on the Psalms* edges this way. The strength is that it integrates the imprecation into the psalm's theology of cosmic order. The weakness is that it underplays the violence.

A second reading takes the verse as **honest psychological residue**: the psalmist, having celebrated cosmic harmony, cannot help noticing that human evil disrupts that harmony, and the prayer is the unfiltered human response. Brueggemann's general approach to imprecation — the psalms give voice to the unedited human heart, and that is part of their gift — applies. The strength is its honesty about Scripture's literary realism. The weakness is that it can become an alibi for never reckoning with the prayer's actual content.

A third reading takes the verse as **theologically problematic but textually authoritative** and uses it as an occasion for confession. In Christian worship, the verse can be prayed only with the New Testament's reframing: Christ prays "Father, forgive them" from the cross, and the wicked from whom we ask deliverance turn out to include ourselves. Augustine reads the verse this way in *Enarrationes*: pray for the *consumption of the wicked* by praying for the *conversion of the sinner*, since when the wicked become righteous, the wicked have indeed been consumed. The strength is that it takes the verse seriously while refusing to weaponize it. The weakness is that it requires a specifically Christian hermeneutical move that Jewish readers rightly resist as a Christianizing override.

**Where this leaves us**: the verse is part of the psalm and cannot be excised. It teaches that cosmic celebration without moral seriousness is sentimentality, and that the praise of creation is not the praise of *every* human action within creation. Whether the verse is prayed as a wish for moral renewal, as honest residue, or as occasion for confession, it must be prayed with care. The eco-theological recovery of Psalm 104 sometimes elides verse 35; this is a mistake. The full psalm includes the cry that human evil end, and that cry is part of what makes the celebration trustworthy rather than naive.

The third tension is **verse 30 and the Spirit**. *Təšallaḥ rûḥăkhā yibbārē'ûn wə-təḥaddēš pənê 'ădāmâ* — "you send forth your Spirit, they are created, and you renew the face of the ground." Christian Trinitarian readers, from Athanasius to Aquinas to Barth, have heard the Holy Spirit here, and have used the verse as a key text for the Spirit's role in creation and ongoing renewal. Jewish readers and most modern critical scholars hear *rûach* in its Hebrew sense of breath, wind, life-force — the divine animating power that is not yet personalized as the third person of the Trinity. Both readings are exegetically responsible; they belong to different theological frameworks.

**Where this leaves us**: the Hebrew text supports both readings, and the difference between them is the difference between two great traditions of reading Scripture. A Christian reading that hears the Spirit here is not imposing on the text — the Spirit is already there as life-breath, and the question is whether life-breath is to be personalized. A Jewish reading that hears life-breath is not denying the text — it is preserving the text's first horizon. The psalm has been generative for both traditions, and that generativity is itself a sign of the text's depth.

**Closing question**: If the praise of creation includes a prayer that human evil end (v. 35), and if the praise of creation is the response of all creatures who eat from God's hand (vv. 27–28), what would it mean to live as a creature among creatures whose breath is on loan and whose end is repentance — neither owners of the cosmos nor tourists in it, but participants in a song whose first verse and last verse both belong to the Creator?

## The Thread

Psalm 104 is the ground beneath the historical recitals that follow. Before Psalms 105–106 narrate Israel's covenant fidelity and infidelity, Psalm 104 establishes that the God of Israel is the Creator of all that lives, that his hand feeds the donkey and the lion before it ever fed Joseph or Moses, and that the cosmos is a temple in which every creature has a part to sing. The Davidic crisis of Books I–III, the Mosaic answer of Book IV — all of it is held within this larger frame: the LORD reigns because the LORD made, and the LORD made not for ownership but for the joy of the creatures who eat from his hand.
