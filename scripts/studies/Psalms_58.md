# Psalm 58 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 58 is the most violent prayer in the Hebrew Psalter, and the historical world it inhabits is the world of the corrupt court, the rigged tribunal, the king's officer who takes bribes and grinds the poor. The superscription is identical to Psalm 57's: "To the choirmaster: according to Do Not Destroy. A Miktam of David." The melody-marker *al-tashchet* (אַל־תַּשְׁחֵת, "do not destroy") again names the tune; *miktam* (מִכְתָּם) again tags the genre — perhaps "inscription," perhaps "golden poem," perhaps a poem worthy of being chiseled into stone (the LXX renders it *stēlographia*). What is missing in Psalm 58 is any historical incident in the superscription: no cave, no flight, no Saul. The psalm is a free-standing protest against unjust judges.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 58 as a *Fluchpsalm* — a curse-psalm — within the broader category of imprecatory or vengeance laments. Sigmund Mowinckel, more provocatively, argued that the curse-psalms had a near-magical cultic function: they were performed in the temple to release covenantal curses against named enemies, and the precise verbal forms had performative force. Claus Westermann placed Psalm 58 among the laments where the petition has hardened into imprecation. Hans-Joachim Kraus and Erhard Gerstenberger emphasize the social setting: the speaker has been victimized by the legal system, and the prayer is a cry for the *only* court that can correct the verdict — the court of God.

The opening word is contested. The Hebrew *ha-umnam elem tzedeq tedabberun* (הַאֻמְנָם אֵלֶם צֶדֶק תְּדַבֵּרוּן) reads literally "do you indeed, in silence, speak righteousness?" — but most modern translations (and the ESV) emend *elem* (silence) to *elim* (אֵלִים, "gods" or "mighty ones"), giving "Do you indeed decree what is right, you gods?" The same plural divine-council noun appears in Psalm 82, where God stands in judgment of the *elohim* who have failed to defend the orphan and the poor. The reference is best understood as the divine-council-of-rulers — earthly judges who hold delegated divine authority and who have abused it. Compare 2 Chronicles 19:6 ("you judge not for man but for the LORD") and Exodus 21:6 / 22:8 where *elohim* may refer to human judges acting in God's name.

Psalm 58 sits within Book II of the Psalter, the Elohistic Psalter (Psalms 42–72), where *YHWH* has been replaced with *Elohim* in most contexts. Psalm 58 is unusual in that *YHWH* still appears in v. 6 ("tear out the fangs of the young lions, O LORD") — one of the residual cracks in the Elohistic redaction. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of Psalm 58 (4QPsa, 4QPsb), with no major textual deviation, and the Septuagint preserves the same imprecatory shape, smoothing some of the more violent metaphors but keeping the structure intact.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels are abundant. The "venom of the serpent" imagery in v. 4 echoes Egyptian execration texts in which enemy names were inscribed on clay pots and ritually smashed. The "deaf adder that stops its ear" alludes to a known ancient practice of snake-charming and to a folk belief that some snakes pressed one ear to the ground and covered the other with their tail to escape the charmer's pipe. The blood-bathing image in v. 10 echoes Mesopotamian victory hymns in which the king "washes his weapons in the blood of his enemies." But the psalm subverts: it is *God* who acts, not the king.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Lowth's parallelism here is pressed to its hardest edge. The opening accusation in vv. 1–2 is paired by antithetical and synthetic parallelism: judges who claim to "decree what is right" actually "devise wrongs in their hearts" and "deal out violence with their hands." Heart and hand, decree and act, are bound by a tight rhetorical chiasm that exposes the gap between official claim and lived reality.

The structure is three strophes of imprecation framed by accusation and resolution. Strophe 1 (vv. 1–5) accuses: the judges are corrupt; they are estranged from the womb; they are venomous serpents who refuse to hear correction. Strophe 2 (vv. 6–9) curses: seven distinct images of divine breaking — broken teeth, torn fangs, water poured out, blunted arrows, dissolving snails, stillborn children, pots swept away before they boil. The pile-up of violent images is deliberate; this is a rhetoric of saturation. Strophe 3 (vv. 10–11) anticipates resolution: the righteous see vengeance, bathe their feet in blood, and humanity declares "surely there is a God who judges on earth." Two *selahs* (vv. 5, 9) divide the poem.

The most confronting Hebrew phrase is in v. 10: *yirchatz pe'amav be-dam ha-rasha* (יִרְחַץ פְּעָמָיו בְּדַם הָרָשָׁע — "he will bathe his feet in the blood of the wicked"). The verb *rachatz* is the standard verb for ceremonial washing; *pe'amav* means "his feet" (literally "his footsteps," from the same root as the verb "to step"). The image is of feet so thoroughly soaked in the slain that walking becomes a kind of bath. Modern readers recoil. Ancient readers would have heard echoes of the warrior's blood-bath after victory, and also (Patrick Miller notes) of the priest's ritual washing in the temple court — the language is liturgical and martial at once.

The seven curses in vv. 6–9 form a chiasm of dissolution: from solid (teeth, fangs) to liquid (water, snail-slime) to vapor (pot-heat swept away). The imagery moves through the four classical elements — earth, water, air, fire — undoing the wicked at every level of being. The final image, *terem yavinu siroteichem atad kemo-chai kemo-charon yis'arennu* (v. 9), is among the hardest verses in the Psalter to translate ("sooner than your pots can feel the heat of thorns, whether green or ablaze, may he sweep them away!") — the syntax may be deliberately broken, mimicking the chaos it invokes.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Psalm 58 sits inside the Davidic *miktam* cluster (Psalms 56–60) as the imprecatory pivot. Surrounding psalms cry from personal danger; Psalm 58 turns the cry outward, toward the systemic violence of corrupt power. Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger read the cluster as a deliberate David-anthology in which the king's prayer-life is shown to include not only personal lament (57, 59) and military protest (60) but also prophetic indictment of the courts (58).

Jewish reception has wrestled with Psalm 58 from antiquity. The *Midrash Tehillim* reads the *elim* of v. 1 as the angels of the nations who fail to judge righteously, anticipating Daniel 10. Rashi reads it as the judges of Israel who have abandoned Torah; Ibn Ezra reads them as foreign rulers; Radak reads them as both. The medieval exegetes do not flinch from the violence; they read v. 10 as eschatological — the righteous rejoice not in cruelty but in the final triumph of justice. Modern Jewish thinkers go further. Martin Buber, writing after the Shoah, argued that the imprecatory psalms preserve the dignity of the victim's protest — to silence them is to silence the cry of the murdered. Abraham Joshua Heschel saw in the prophetic-imprecatory tradition the divine *pathos* — God's own grief at injustice. Jon Levenson, in *Creation and the Persistence of Evil*, treats Psalm 58 as evidence that biblical monotheism has never been a polite theism: the God of Israel is the God who breaks the teeth of oppressors. Nahum Sarna highlights the courtroom imagery as foundational to Israelite jurisprudence. Liturgically, Psalm 58 is rarely used in synagogue worship and has historically been associated with private petitions for justice rather than public recitation.

Christian reception has been more fraught. Augustine (*Enarrationes* 58) reads the psalm allegorically: the corrupt judges are the heretics, and the dissolving snail is the heretical word that devours its own substance. Athanasius treats the psalm as a prayer Christians may offer against the demons. Cassiodorus emphasizes its eschatological dimension. Aquinas, treating the question whether Christians may pray for vengeance (*Summa* II-II q.83), distinguishes between desiring private revenge (forbidden) and desiring the public vindication of God's justice (permitted) — and reads Psalm 58 in the latter sense. Luther in his *Operationes* and Calvin in his 1557 commentary both treat the psalm as the prayer of the persecuted church against unjust rulers, and Calvin specifically reads "bathing the feet in blood" as a metaphor for victory rather than literal carnage.

Modern Christian engagement is where the honest wrestling lives. C.S. Lewis, in *Reflections on the Psalms* (chapter 3, "The Cursings"), wrote: "the hatred is there — festering, gloating, undisguised — and also we should be wicked if we in any way condoned or approved it … The reaction of the Psalmists to injury, though profoundly natural, is profoundly wrong." Walter Brueggemann, in *The Costly Loss of Lament* (1986) and *Praying the Psalms*, took the opposite view: the church's sanitization of the imprecatory psalms — their omission from the Liturgy of the Hours after Vatican II, their abridgment in the Anglican Book of Common Prayer, their absence from most modern hymnals — represents a theological failure to take seriously the cries of the victims of history. Erich Zenger's *A God of Vengeance? Understanding the Psalms of Divine Wrath* (1994/1996) made the most sustained Christian argument: the imprecations are not bloodthirsty curses but *protests against impunity* — the refusal to accept that the wicked will go unjudged. Renita Weems, Wilda Gafney, and the womanist tradition have recovered Psalm 58 as the legitimate cry of those who suffer institutional injustice — the Black church, victims of trafficking, the abused — for whom the polite prayers of the privileged are no prayer at all. Patrick Miller, William Brown, and Beth LaNeel Tanner all stress that to pray Psalm 58 is to refuse to be reconciled to evil. Hossfeld and Zenger, Goldingay, Tate, and Wenham all stand within this protest reading.

The New Testament does not quote Psalm 58 directly, but Revelation 6:9–11 ("how long, O Lord, holy and true, before you will judge and avenge our blood?") is its clearest echo, and Romans 12:19 ("vengeance is mine, says the Lord") may be read as the apostolic *internalization* of the Psalm 58 cry — the believer hands the imprecation to God rather than executing it.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The central tension is unavoidable: how does a Christian, formed by the Sermon on the Mount, pray a psalm that asks God to break teeth and bathe feet in blood? Three positions deserve a fair hearing.

(1) The *Lewis position*: the imprecations are emotionally honest but morally wrong — natural human cries that the New Testament transcends. Christians may study them as evidence of sub-Christian feeling but should not pray them as their own. Steelman: Jesus explicitly contrasts "you have heard it said, hate your enemy" with "but I say to you, love your enemies" (Matthew 5:43–44); the trajectory of revelation moves *away* from imprecation toward forgiveness.

(2) The *Zenger / Brueggemann / liberation position*: the imprecations are not bloodthirsty rage but protest against impunity, and the Christian who refuses to pray them is actually refusing to take seriously the suffering of the victims. The blood-bath of v. 10 is not a wish for personal cruelty but a vivid metaphor of the final overthrow of systemic evil. Steelman: the Psalter is the prayerbook of Jesus — he prayed it whole, including these psalms — and the New Testament's "vengeance is mine" *presupposes* rather than abolishes the cry for justice. To silence Psalm 58 is to leave the victims unprayed-for.

(3) The *Augustinian / Aquinas position*: the imprecations are legitimate when read eschatologically and corporately — they ask God to judge the structures of evil, not to harm specific persons; they pray for the final defeat of all that resists God's reign, which is also the prayer "thy kingdom come." Steelman: this reading preserves the full Psalter while integrating it into a Christological frame in which the wicked who must be broken are first of all the powers within the worshiper himself, and only then the powers in the world.

**Where this leaves us:** the imprecatory psalms are not optional. They are the prayer of the abused servant, the trafficked child, the family of the murdered, the believer in the prison cell. To excise them from worship is to silence the victims of history. To pray them is to refuse to accept that injustice is the last word and to entrust vengeance to the only judge who can render it without becoming the next oppressor. The closing question: when you read v. 10, can you hear it as the cry of the murdered child of God rather than as your own desire for blood — and can you bring it to the altar without pretending you do not feel it?
