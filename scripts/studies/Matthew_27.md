# Matthew 27: Pilate, the Cross, and the Verse That Has Done the Most Damage

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The day moves from the priests' early-morning council (27:1) through the Roman trial, the crucifixion, and burial — a Friday of the Passover week, the eve of Sabbath. The transfer to Pilate (27:2) is a forced jurisdictional handoff: Jewish authorities under Roman occupation could try and condemn but could not execute (cf. John 18:31; *y. Sanhedrin* 1:1, 7:2). Capital authority — *ius gladii* — belonged to the prefect.

Where the trial happened is contested. Luke 23 calls the location *praetorium*, Mark and Matthew speak of the Roman governor's residence in Jerusalem. Two candidates compete. The *Antonia Fortress* sat at the northwest corner of the Temple Mount, garrisoning the Roman cohort that watched over festival crowds; this is the traditional location, and the *Lithostrōton* (John 19:13) was identified by Père Vincent in the 1930s with pavement stones excavated under the Sisters of Zion convent. Subsequent excavation by Pierre Benoit and others showed those pavement stones are Hadrianic (post-135 CE), not Herodian, so the stones themselves are not the trial floor. The current scholarly majority — including Shimon Gibson (*The Final Days of Jesus*, 2009) and Joan Taylor — locates Pilate's praetorium at *Herod's Palace* on the western hill, near the modern Citadel-Jaffa Gate area, where the prefect resided when in Jerusalem (Philo, *Embassy to Gaius* 299; Josephus, *Jewish War* 2.301; recent excavation under the Tower of David by Amit Re'em in 2001 exposed Herodian-period walls consistent with the palace). The processional route (the Via Dolorosa) from Antonia to Golgotha is medieval Franciscan tradition; if the trial was at Herod's Palace, the actual route ran east-northeast, not west-east.

Golgotha — Aramaic *gulgalta*, "skull" (27:33) — is the second contested site. The Church of the Holy Sepulchre, built by Constantine in 326-335 CE atop a Hadrianic temple to Aphrodite, sits on a quarry that lay outside the city walls in 30 CE (the Second Wall ran east of it; Magen Broshi and Hillel Geva have confirmed this from excavation). A first-century rolling-stone tomb was found inside the church complex, and the rock outcrop identified as Golgotha is geologically a quarry residual, consistent with Matthew's "place" rather than a hill. The Garden Tomb (Gordon's Calvary), proposed by Charles Gordon in 1883 and beloved of Protestant pilgrims, is geologically possible but archaeologically unconvincing — the tomb itself is Iron Age, recut, not first-century. The scholarly consensus, including Gibson, Joan Taylor, and Jodi Magness, sits firmly with the Holy Sepulchre.

Crucifixion practice in the first century is illuminated by a single archaeological find: the **Giv'at ha-Mivtar crucified man**, discovered in 1968 by Vassilios Tzaferis in a tomb in northeast Jerusalem. The bones of *Yehohanan ben Hagqol*, a Jewish man of about twenty-five, were found in an ossuary with a 4.5-inch iron nail still driven through the heel-bone (calcaneus), with olive-wood fragments — the wood of the cross-piece — still attached. Joseph Zias and Eliezer Sekeles re-examined the find in 1985, correcting initial misreadings; their conclusion stands: nails were driven through heel bones laterally (not feet frontally), arms were probably tied to the patibulum (cross-piece) by ropes with possibly a single nail through forearm bones (not palms — palms tear under body weight). This is the only known archaeological evidence of a first-century crucifixion in Judaea. It corresponds with Cicero's description of crucifixion as "the most cruel and disgusting penalty" (*Against Verres* 2.5.165) and with Josephus's reports of its mass use during the Jewish revolts.

Three Greek terms shape the chapter. **Πραιτώριον** (*praitōrion*, 27:27) is itself a Latin loanword (Latin *praetorium*) for the governor's residence and judicial seat. **Γολγοθᾶ** (*Golgotha*, 27:33), preserved in Aramaic with Greek translation **κρανίου τόπος** (*kraniou topos*, "place of a skull"). And the cry **Ηλι Ηλι λεμα σαβαχθανι** (*Eli Eli lema sabachthani*, 27:46) is striking: Mark 15:34 has the Aramaic *Eloi Eloi*, but Matthew gives the first word in Hebrew (*Eli*) and the rest in Aramaic. This is why the bystanders' confusion ("this man is calling Elijah," 27:47) makes phonetic sense in Matthew — *Eli* sounds enough like *Eliyahu* to mishear — but in Mark's Aramaic *Eloi*, the mishearing is harder. France and Davies/Allison treat Matthew's redaction as a deliberate move toward Hebrew Psalter quotation; the cry is Psalm 22:1, the opening of the psalm whose later verses (22:18) Matthew has already invoked at the dividing of the garments.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Matthew is reading Mark 15 closely but adding five distinctive blocks of material that shape this chapter's reception more than any other. First, the **Judas pericope** (27:3-10) — uniquely Matthean. Judas, seeing Jesus condemned, "changed his mind" (μεταμεληθεὶς, *metamelētheis*, 27:3 — not the deeper *metanoia* of repentance; Davies and Allison stress the lexical distinction), throws the silver into the sanctuary, and hangs himself. The chief priests buy the *agros tou kerameos*, the potter's field, called from then on *Akeldama*, "Field of Blood." Acts 1:18-19 gives a different account: Judas himself buys the field, and dies by bursting open in it. Harmonization attempts (Augustine: hanged, then fell and burst; modern: two distinct traditions of Judas's grim end) cannot fully reconcile the accounts. Matthew's editorial signature is the citation: "Then was fulfilled what had been spoken by the prophet Jeremiah" (27:9), but the cited material is mostly from Zechariah 11:12-13 with strands of Jeremiah 32:6-9 and Jeremiah 19. Davies and Allison treat this as Matthew's *gezera shawa* — composite scriptural citation under the dominant prophet's name (Jeremiah, the prophet of the potter). It is not a slip; it is a midrashic technique.

Second, **Pilate's wife's dream** (27:19) — uniquely Matthean. "Have nothing to do with that righteous man, for I have suffered much because of him today in a dream." Coptic Christian tradition later names her Procula (the Apostolic Constitutions; the Greek Orthodox Church canonizes her as Saint Procla, June 27). Matthew uses the dream to amplify Pilate's hesitation and to underscore the moral indictment.

Third, **Pilate's hand-washing** (27:24) — uniquely Matthean. The gesture invokes Deuteronomy 21:6-7, the ritual of the elders who wash their hands over an unsolved murder to disclaim blood-guilt. Pilate appropriates a Jewish ritual to deflect responsibility — and Matthew lets the irony stand.

Fourth, the verse: **"His blood be on us and on our children"** (27:25 — uniquely Matthean). I will treat this carefully under Lens 3 and 4 because of its reception, but here as a literary observation: Matthew has constructed a tight inclusio with 23:35 ("all the righteous blood shed on earth, from the blood of righteous Abel...") and 23:36 ("all these things will come upon this generation"). The chapter 27 acclamation, in Matthean composition, is read as the people accepting the consequence the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE will then enact. Matthew is writing in the 80s CE, looking back at the catastrophe; the verse is a retrojective theodicy of that catastrophe within his community's intra-Jewish polemic. It is, in modern scholarly judgment (Davies/Allison, France, Levine, Brown, Pawlikowski), a Matthean theological-historical construction, not a transcript.

Fifth, the **earthquake, opened tombs, and raised saints** (27:51b-53) — uniquely Matthean. Mark has the temple curtain rent. Matthew adds the seismic-apocalyptic eruption: tombs opening, "many bodies of the saints who had fallen asleep were raised, and coming out of the tombs after his resurrection they went into the holy city and appeared to many." Davies and Allison stress the apocalyptic-eschatological grammar (Ezek 37, Dan 12:2, Zech 14:4-5). Donald Senior (*The Passion of Jesus in the Gospel of Matthew*, 1985) and Raymond Brown (*Death of the Messiah* II.1118-1140) treat it as Matthean theological symbolism: the death of the Messiah is the eschatological turning point, anticipating the general resurrection. Mike Licona (*The Resurrection of Jesus*, 2010) provoked an evangelical-inerrantist controversy by proposing that 27:52-53 should be read as "apocalyptic" rather than literal-historical; Norman Geisler and others demanded retraction, and Licona lost a faculty position. The reception of Licona's proposal is itself a window onto contemporary evangelical hermeneutic anxiety.

The **centurion's confession** (27:54) — "Truly this was the Son of God" — Matthew adds the seismic context absent from Mark, plus "those who were with him." Matthew converts Mark's individual exclamation into a collective Gentile recognition, anticipating 28:19's Gentile mission.

The chapter closes with **Joseph of Arimathea's burial** (27:57-61) and the **uniquely Matthean guard at the tomb** (27:62-66). The latter is set up to be paid off in 28:11-15: the guard's report and the priests' bribe to spread the "stolen body" story. Matthew is building an apologetic structure against a Jewish-polemical claim circulating in his community ("this story has been spread among the Jews to this day," 28:15).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Read along the canonical arc, Matthew 27 is the convergence of multiple Old Testament streams: the Suffering Servant of Isaiah 52-53 (silent before his accusers, despised, pierced for our transgressions), the righteous sufferer of Psalm 22 (the cry of dereliction; the divided garments; the mocking onlookers), the rejected shepherd of Zechariah 11 and 12 (the thirty pieces; the looking on him whom they have pierced), and the apocalyptic-cosmic darkness of Amos 8:9 ("I will make the sun go down at noon") and Joel 2:31. The passion narrative is a tapestry of citation and allusion, in which the death of Jesus is read as the fulfillment-rupture of Israel's prophetic legacy.

**Jewish interpretation** approaches 27:25 with the wounds of two millennia. Amy-Jill Levine (*The Misunderstood Jew*; *The Jewish Annotated New Testament*) argues that Matthew's verse, in its first-century context, was an intra-Jewish polemic — Matthew, himself a Jew writing in a Jewish-Christian community, was registering disagreement with the synagogue authorities who had rejected the Messianic claim. The "us and our children" was a generational claim, not a perpetual curse. Rosemary Radford Ruether's *Faith and Fratricide* (1974) traces the trajectory by which patristic writers (notably Melito of Sardis's *Peri Pascha*, ca. 165 CE, with its devastating "the king of Israel was killed by an Israelite hand") and medieval Christian preaching converted this verse into the theological scaffolding of Christian anti-Judaism — the deicide charge, the blood libel, the medieval pogroms, the inquisitions, and ultimately the Holocaust. John Pawlikowski (*Christ in the Light of the Christian-Jewish Dialogue*, 1982) and Mary Boys (*Has God Only One Blessing?*) have built post-Holocaust theology on Christian repentance for this hermeneutical crime. *Vatican II's Nostra Aetate* (1965) explicitly rejected the collective guilt reading: "what happened in his passion cannot be charged against all the Jews, without distinction, then alive, nor against the Jews of today." The 2001 Pontifical Biblical Commission document *The Jewish People and Their Sacred Scriptures* extends this with detailed textual treatment. The Levine-edited *Jewish Annotated New Testament* (2nd ed. 2017) is the standard contemporary Jewish-scholarly edition.

E. P. Sanders (*Jesus and Judaism*, 1985) and Geza Vermes (*Jesus the Jew*) read the historical events behind the passion as a Roman political execution for sedition, with priestly cooperation but not "the Jews" as a corporate entity. Daniel Boyarin (*The Jewish Gospels*, 2012) places the Christological grammar of the Passion within Jewish messianic possibilities of the Second Temple period.

**Christian interpretation** has read this chapter through the lenses of atonement, theodicy, and the cry of dereliction. Patristically, Origen treats the cosmic darkness as the heavens themselves recoiling from the Creator's death. Chrysostom's homilies dwell on the centurion's confession as the prototype Gentile believer. Augustine's *De Consensu Evangelistarum* labors over the Judas-death harmonization with Acts 1. Aquinas's *Catena Aurea* compiles the patristic gloss; his *Summa* III q. 46-49 systematizes the satisfaction theory of atonement with the Passion as its enacted center. The Reformation re-reads the cross. Luther's *Heidelberg Disputation* (1518) makes the cross the criterion of all theology — *theologia crucis* against *theologia gloriae*. Calvin's *Institutes* 2.16 gives a magisterial treatment of Christ's suffering, including (controversially) the descent into hell read as the spiritual anguish of Gethsemane and Calvary, not a literal post-mortem journey.

The twentieth century re-read the cross under the shadow of catastrophe. Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* IV/1 §59 — "the Judge judged in our place" — is the most extended Reformed-modern treatment of the substitutionary atonement enacted in Matthew 27. Jürgen Moltmann's *The Crucified God* (1972) reads the cry of dereliction (27:46) as a Trinitarian event: the Father suffers the death of the Son, the Son suffers abandonment by the Father, and the Spirit is the bond of love that holds the rupture without resolving it — a theology written explicitly for the post-Auschwitz church. Hans Urs von Balthasar's *Mysterium Paschale* extends the Trinitarian reading to Holy Saturday: the Son's descent into the silence of God. Dietrich Bonhoeffer's *Letters and Papers* — "only the suffering God can help" — is a meditation born from his cell at Tegel under Nazi sentence. Eberhard Jüngel's *God as the Mystery of the World* makes Calvary the centerpiece of his metaphysical theology.

The major commentaries, again, are Davies and Allison's *ICC* volume 3, Ulrich Luz's *Hermeneia* volume 4 (where the reception-historical work on 27:25 is especially extensive and harrowing), R. T. France's *NICNT*, Craig Keener's *NICNT*, Donald Hagner's *WBC*, and John Nolland's *NIGTC*. For the Passion as a coherent narrative, Raymond Brown's two-volume *The Death of the Messiah* (1994) is the unmoved foundation — every textual, historical, theological, and reception question thoroughly adjudicated. John Dominic Crossan's *Who Killed Jesus?* (1995) supplies a counter-reading insisting the Passion narratives are "prophecy historicized" rather than "history remembered" — Brown's *Death* methodologically rejects this. Joel Green's *The Death of Jesus* (1988) is the narrative-theological mediation. Donald Senior's *The Passion of Jesus in the Gospel of Matthew* (1985) is the standard Matthean-passion monograph.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The cry of dereliction — what is happening at 27:46?** "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" Three positions structure the dispute. *Penal substitutionary* (Calvin, Charles Hodge, J. I. Packer's *Knowing God*, John Stott's *The Cross of Christ*, the Reformed confessional tradition): the Father turns his face away because the Son has become "sin for us" (2 Cor 5:21), and the cry is the experiential reality of the wrath of God borne in the place of the elect. The forsakenness is real; the rupture in the divine economy is the cost of substitution. *Trinitarian-suffering* (Moltmann, *The Crucified God*; Balthasar, *Mysterium Paschale*; Eberhard Jüngel): the cry is not the Father turning away but the Son sharing the abandonment of the godforsaken. The Father suffers the loss of the Son, the Son the absence of the Father, and the Spirit holds the rupture without dissolving it. The cross is a Trinitarian event in which God enters the godforsaken depths of human reality. *Davidic-canonical* (Davies/Allison, France, Bockmuehl, Wright): the cry is the opening line of Psalm 22, a psalm that begins in dereliction and ends in vindication ("from the horns of the wild oxen you have rescued me... all the ends of the earth shall remember and turn to the Lord," Ps 22:21, 27). To cite the first line is to invoke the whole — the cry is anguished but it is also a confession of trust in eventual deliverance, the Davidic prayer of the righteous sufferer who will be vindicated. **Where this leaves us:** the three readings are not mutually exclusive at every point. Penal-substitutionary and Trinitarian-suffering both insist on the reality of the abandonment; they differ on whether the Father is the one who abandons or the one who shares the abandonment. The Davidic-canonical reading insists on the textual frame the cry occupies. **Unresolved:** can the divine essence be fractured even briefly in the Trinitarian event of the cross, or is the rupture entirely on the side of the assumed humanity? Patristic Trinitarian orthodoxy (against Patripassianism) said no; Moltmann's modern reading risks pressing toward yes. The question continues to divide Western theology.

**Tension 2: "His blood be on us and on our children" (27:25) — what do we do with this verse?** This is the single verse in Matthew that has done the most damage in Christian history. Three honest positions can be steelmanned. *Pre-Vatican II "self-curse" reading* (the patristic-medieval consensus that has been rejected but must be named to be repented): the verse is a Jewish self-imprecation, accepted by God, that explains the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE and the diasporic suffering of the Jewish people thereafter, and (in its worst applications) that legitimates ongoing Christian persecution of Jews as the providential consequence of their forefathers' acclamation. This reading is theologically and morally insupportable; the church has explicitly rejected it. *Matthean-redactional reading* (Davies/Allison, France, Levine, Brown, Senior, Pawlikowski): the verse is a redactional construction of Matthew's community in the 80s CE, looking back at the destruction of the Temple and constructing a theodicy from within an intra-Jewish polemical context. The "us and our children" was a single-generation acclamation, not a perpetual curse — and even as redaction, it does not correspond to the historical event but theologizes it. *Liberation-theological reading* (James Cone's *The Cross and the Lynching Tree*, 2011, in proximate dialogue with this passage; Pawlikowski; Ruether): the verse must be read against itself. The blood of the Messiah, freely poured out, cannot rest on anyone as condemnation. The cup of 26:28 is "for the forgiveness of sins"; the same blood cannot be both saving and damning. The verse, properly read, refutes its own historical misuse. **Where this leaves us:** *Nostra Aetate* (1965) and PCBC 2001 articulate the church's official repentance and re-reading. The pastoral and homiletical task remains to teach this verse with the full weight of its reception history disclosed. **Unresolved:** is repentance enough? What does ongoing Christian-Jewish reconciliation require of the church beyond official statements? Levine, Pawlikowski, and Boys are still writing into that question.

## The Thread

Matthew 27 is the death pole of the inclusio that opened in Matthew 1: the child named *Yeshua* "for he will save his people from their sins" (1:21) now pours out the blood "for the forgiveness of sins" (26:28; the program enacted in chapter 27). The cry of 27:46 invokes Psalm 22, the psalm whose later verses (22:18) Matthew has already deployed at the dividing of the garments and whose closing chord — universal worship of the Lord — anticipates the Great Commission of 28:19. The earthquake of 27:51-54 anticipates the earthquake of 28:2 at the resurrection — Matthew is signaling the eschatological hinge. The guard at the tomb (27:62-66) sets up the bribe of 28:11-15. Watch in chapter 28 for the verse that bookends the Gospel: 28:20 — "I am with you always, to the end of the age" — the Emmanuel of 1:23 fulfilled at the last word.
