# 2 Chronicles 28 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

The Chronicler's account of Ahaz (Hebrew *Achaz*, אָחָז, "he has grasped") is one of the book's most theologically loaded indictments and one of its most surprising narrative inversions. The parallel in 2 Kings 16 is itself dark — Ahaz is the apostate king who introduces a Damascene altar into the Jerusalem temple, sacrifices his son in the fire, and submits to Tiglath-Pileser III as a vassal — but the Chronicler, writing in Persian-period Yehud (Sara Japhet, Hugh Williamson, Gary Knoppers all converge on a fifth-to-fourth-century BCE date with internal disagreements), takes the Kings indictment and *expands* it in three directions while simultaneously inserting a story Kings does not have at all: the Oded narrative (28:9-15) in which northern Israelites, of all people, become the chapter's moral protagonists.

The expansion of Ahaz's sin is calibrated. Where Kings says Ahaz "burned his son as an offering" (2 Kgs 16:3), the Chronicler reads "burned *his sons*" (28:3, plural — *vayiv'er et-banav ba'esh*) and locates the act in *the Valley of the son of Hinnom* (*gei ben-Hinnom*) — the place name that becomes, by the time of Jeremiah and the rabbinic tradition, *gehinnom*, the source-word for the New Testament *Gehenna*. The Chronicler is doing two things at once: he is intensifying the horror (sons, plural) and naming the place that will function in later biblical and post-biblical literature as the topographic shorthand for the worst of Israelite apostasy. Jer 7:31 and 19:5 — "they have built the high places of Topheth, which is in the Valley of the son of Hinnom, to burn their sons and their daughters in the fire" — directly extend the Chronicler's vocabulary. Jon Levenson's *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son* has shown that the practice of child sacrifice in ancient Israel and Phoenicia is more historically attested than older scholarship admitted; the prophetic and historiographical literature condemning it was condemning something real, not constructing a strawman. The Chronicler's Ahaz is a king who actually did what the prophets said the worst kings did.

The chapter's most extraordinary literary move, however, is the Oded story (28:9-15). After Pekah of Israel kills 120,000 Judeans in a single day (28:6) and the northern army takes 200,000 Judean women and children captive (28:8), the prophet *Oded* — name meaning "restorer" or "lifter" — meets the returning army at Samaria. His speech is unique to Chronicles. He reframes the victory as judgment ("because the LORD, the God of your fathers, was angry with Judah, he gave them into your hand"), but he then rebukes Israel for excess: *but you have killed them in a rage that has reached up to heaven* (28:9). He commands the release of the captives. Then four named Ephraimite chiefs — *Azariah the son of Johanan, Berechiah the son of Meshillemoth, Jehizkiah the son of Shallum, and Amasa the son of Hadlai* (28:12) — stand against the returning soldiers and refuse to let the captives enter Samaria. The soldiers leave the captives and spoil before the assembly. The named chiefs then take the captives, *clothe all who were naked, give them sandals, provide them with food and drink, and anoint them, and carrying all the feeble among them on donkeys, bring them to their kinsfolk at Jericho, the city of palm trees* (28:15).

This is, in literary terms, the Chronicler doing something almost without parallel in his ideological framework. The Chronicler has spent the entire book treating the northern kingdom as schismatic and theologically illegitimate. He has refused to narrate northern history as such, treating northern kings only as offstage threats to Judah. He has consistently held that the legitimate temple, priesthood, and davidic line are in Jerusalem. And then, in the middle of his harshest indictment of a Judean king, he gives us four named Ephraimites and an unnamed northern prophet doing what no Judean character does in the chapter: showing mercy to the captives, clothing the naked, feeding the hungry, anointing the wounded, and providing donkey-transport for the feeble. Ralph Klein, Mark Boda, and Williamson all agree that this passage is theologically unprecedented inside the Chronicler's own ideological architecture. The author who has structurally privileged Judah is here, deliberately, locating moral seriousness in the north.

The political background completes the indictment. Ahaz, faced with the Syro-Ephraimite coalition (Aram and Israel jointly threatening Jerusalem, the situation Isaiah 7 narrates in detail), turned to Assyria. The Chronicler reports the appeal to Tiglath-Pileser (28:16, 20) but adds the editorial verdict that the Assyrian king *afflicted him instead of strengthening him* (28:20, *vayatzer lo velo' chazaqo*) — a Hebrew wordplay between *tsar* (afflict, oppress) and *chazaq* (strengthen) that captures the political irony precisely. Ahaz paid Assyrian tribute from the temple treasury (28:21) and got Assyrian oppression in return. Assyrian records (the Annals of Tiglath-Pileser III, lines preserved at Calah) confirm the tribute, naming "*Yauhazi* of Yaudi" — Ahaz of Judah — among the western vassals. The historical layer corroborates the theological layer.

The chapter ends with three accelerating offenses: Ahaz sacrifices to the *gods of Damascus that had defeated him* (28:23 — the Chronicler's signature irony, the same logical fallacy Amaziah committed with the Edomite gods three chapters earlier), cuts up the temple vessels and shuts the doors of the temple (28:24), and builds altars in every corner of Jerusalem and high places in every city of Judah (28:25). The Chronicler ends the reign with the deliberate notation that *they did not bring him into the tombs of the kings of Israel* (28:27). Like Uzziah the leper before him, Ahaz the apostate is denied the standard royal burial. The dynastic coherence has cracked.

## Lens 2: The Literature Itself

The chapter is structurally unusual. Most Chronicles royal narratives have a single moral arc — ascent and fall, or fall and recovery. Ahaz has only fall, with a single luminous interruption. The structure is: (A) Ahaz's apostasy and military disasters (vv. 1-7); (B) the Oded interlude in the north (vv. 8-15); (C) Ahaz's escalating apostasy and Assyrian humiliation (vv. 16-27). The B section is structurally and tonally inverted from A and C. In A and C, Judah is the apostate, the victim of Yahweh's anger, and the place where temple vessels are smashed. In B, the *north* is the location of prophetic speech, named chiefs of integrity, and the only acts of mercy in the chapter. The literary effect is theological vertigo. The Chronicler is forcing his reader to recognize moral truth coming from the place his ideology has located as illegitimate.

The Hebrew of v. 15 — describing the Ephraimites' care for the captives — uses a sequence of verbs that has caught the attention of scholars for two millennia: *hilbishu* (clothed), *hin'ilum* (gave sandals), *vaya'akhilum vayashqum* (fed and gave drink), *vaysukhum* (anointed), *vayenahalum bachamorim* (carried them on donkeys), *vayevi'um Yericho* (brought them to Jericho). The verb *nahel* with donkeys is the same verb used for Yahweh's *gentle leading* of the flock in Isaiah 40:11 and Psalm 23:2; the anointing (*sukh*) is the priestly verb for caring for the dignified body. The Chronicler is not just describing first aid; he is using a vocabulary saturated with shepherding and priestly resonance. The Ephraimites are doing what kings and priests are supposed to do.

The location *Jericho, the city of palm trees* (28:15) is also non-accidental. Jericho sits at the literal border between northern and southern territory, in the Jordan valley below sea level — a meeting point. The Ephraimites do not simply release the captives at the border; they accompany them all the way to Jericho. The journey itself is the act of mercy.

The closing irony of Ahaz's reign — *in the time of his distress he became yet more faithless to the LORD — this same King Ahaz* (28:22, *uv'et hatser lo hosif lim'ol baYHWH hu hammelekh Achaz*) — is one of the Chronicler's most characteristic emphatic constructions. The phrase *zeh hammelekh Achaz* ("this same King Ahaz") is a Hebrew idiom of editorial astonishment. *This* is the king. *This* is who Judah has. The Chronicler refuses to let the reader move past the indictment.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Within the canon, Ahaz is the proximate cause of two enormous prophetic moments. Isaiah 7-9 — including the *Immanuel* sign (Isa 7:14) and the great messianic oracle of Isaiah 9:6-7 — was preached *to Ahaz* during the Syro-Ephraimite crisis. The prophetic literature's most celebrated christological texts emerge in confrontation with this king. The Chronicler does not narrate the Immanuel sign, but his Persian-period reader knew that the king who burned his sons in the Valley of Hinnom was the king to whom Isaiah said *the virgin will conceive and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel*. The juxtaposition is staggering. A king who sacrificed his sons heard the promise of a son who would not be sacrificed.

In Jewish reception, the Oded story (28:9-15) became, in the rabbinic tradition, a paradigm of *gemilut chasadim* (acts of loving-kindness). The Talmud (b. Sotah 14a) and later midrashic collections cite the Ephraimite chiefs as exemplars of clothing the naked, feeding the hungry, and accompanying the wayfarer — the traditional list of *chasadim*. The story functioned as a quiet rebuke to any rabbinic anti-Samaritan or anti-northern prejudice: even at the moment of greatest fracture between the kingdoms, the Chronicler shows the north acting more righteously than the south. Maimonides cites the chapter (in *Hilkhot Avadim*) as the scriptural anchor for the rabbinic prohibition against enslaving fellow Israelites taken in inter-Israelite war. The Chronicler's Oded is, in this rabbinic reading, the founder of a halakhic tradition.

In Christian reception, the Oded story has had an arresting second life. N. T. Wright, in *The Mission of God* and elaborated in his Jesus-and-Israel scholarship, argues that *the parable of the Good Samaritan* (Luke 10:25-37) deliberately echoes 2 Chronicles 28:9-15. The structural parallels are striking. In the parable, a man is robbed and beaten on the road from Jerusalem to *Jericho* — the same Jericho. A priest and a Levite from Jerusalem pass by; a *Samaritan* — a northerner — stops, *clothes* the wounds with oil and wine (the priestly anointing verbs), puts him *on his own animal* (the donkey-transport of 28:15), and *brings him* to safety. The Lukan parable's vocabulary — bandaging, anointing, donkey-transport, journeying to a destination of safety — maps onto the Chronicler's vocabulary with a precision that is unlikely to be coincidental. Jesus is reaching back into the canonical memory of the *one place* in the Hebrew Bible where a northerner is the moral hero, and saying: this is the kind of neighbor you are called to be. Walter Brueggemann, who has independently traced this connection, calls it "the parable's deepest scriptural echo." The Good Samaritan is, in this reading, the *fulfillment of the Oded tradition* — the northerner whose mercy crosses the geography of schism.

The child-sacrifice element of Ahaz's apostasy has had a different reception history. Patristic writers (Origen, Jerome, Augustine) cited 28:3 as the OT's most explicit indictment of pagan child sacrifice and used it polemically against Roman infant exposure. The medieval and early-modern Jewish tradition (Levenson's research has illuminated this) treated the *Akedah* (Genesis 22) and the prophetic condemnations of Topheth as parts of a single canonical conversation about sacrifice, substitution, and the limits of religious obligation. The Chronicler's Ahaz is the *negative pole* of that conversation — the king who, having heard the Akedah, did the opposite of Abraham. Where Abraham was stopped, Ahaz proceeded. Where Abraham received a substitute, Ahaz substituted his sons for himself.

The New Testament's *Gehenna* (Matt 5:22, 5:29-30, 10:28, 18:9, 23:15, 23:33; Mark 9:43-48; Luke 12:5; Jas 3:6) takes its place-name and its theological charge from the Valley of the son of Hinnom that the Chronicler names as Ahaz's sacrificial site. The geography is the same. The location where Ahaz burned his sons becomes, by Jesus's day, the topographic image of judgment. The Chronicler's specificity — *Valley of the son of Hinnom* rather than just "the high places" — is what makes Gehenna possible as a New Testament concept.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The Chronicler's agenda in this chapter is harder to summarize than in most of the book because the chapter is doing two things simultaneously: indicting Ahaz with maximum severity and disrupting the reader's tribal moral geography by locating mercy in the schismatic north. The tension is the theology.

**Tension 1: The Oded story and the Christian-Jewish dialogue around the Good Samaritan.** What does it mean that the Chronicler — the most pro-Jerusalem author in the Hebrew Bible — locates moral integrity in northern Israel at the moment of greatest sectional violence? And what does it mean that Jesus's most famous parable echoes this passage? Three positions are worth taking seriously. The first — call it the *N. T. Wright Israel-and-Jesus reading* — argues that the Oded-Good Samaritan link is part of Jesus's larger argument that the kingdom of God reorganizes the people of God across the old categories, that mercy is the new boundary marker, and that the Chronicler had already broken the tribal frame the parable then completes. The second — the *Brueggemann prophetic-imagination reading* — argues that the Oded story is the Chronicler's own internal critique of his ideology, the moment the text turns on its own assumptions, and that this self-disruption is part of what makes the Hebrew Bible theologically alive rather than ideologically closed. The third — the *honest-supersessionism worry*, articulated in Jewish-Christian dialogue scholarship by R. Kendall Soulen, Jon Levenson, and others — pushes back: any Christian use of the Oded-Good Samaritan link must be careful not to turn the passage into a supersessionist argument that "the church has replaced Israel" or that "Samaritans are Christians-in-disguise." The Chronicler is not anticipating the church; he is doing internal Jewish theology that the church then, very legitimately, picks up and extends. Where this leaves us: the Wright reading is exegetically powerful and probably correct in identifying the deliberate Lukan echo. But the dialogue caution is right that the move must not flatten the Hebrew Bible into a Christian prologue. Both can be true: the Chronicler is doing something prophetically self-disrupting, *and* Jesus is extending it, *and* the extension does not erase the original.

**Tension 2: Child sacrifice, the *Akedah*, and the limits of religious obligation.** Ahaz burned his sons. The text reports it, condemns it, and moves on. But the reader cannot quite move on, because the Chronicler's canon also contains the *Akedah* (Gen 22), in which a father is told to sacrifice his son and is praised for being willing. What is the relationship between these two scenes? Three honest positions: The *prophetic-condemnation* reading, articulated in Jeremiah 7:31, 19:5 ("which I did not command, nor did it come into my mind"), holds that child sacrifice was always a non-Yahwistic practice that prophets and historiographers consistently condemned, that the Akedah is the foundational counter-narrative (Abraham was *stopped*; the substitution was provided), and that Ahaz's act is the violation of exactly the principle the Akedah established. The *substitutionary-typology* reading, articulated by Levenson and developed in much Christian theology, holds that the OT contains a real and disturbing strand of *beloved-son-sacrifice* theology that runs from the Akedah through the firstborn redemption laws (Exod 13:11-15) through the prophetic critiques and into the Christian reading of Christ as the unsubstituted beloved son who actually was sacrificed. The *honest-canonical* reading, articulated by Ellen Davis and Phyllis Trible, simply admits that the OT contains traditions that condemn child sacrifice and traditions that come uncomfortably close to legitimating it, and that the canon does not resolve the tension neatly. Where this leaves us: the Chronicler's narrative force is unambiguous — Ahaz's act is horror, full stop. But honest readers cannot pretend the *Akedah* does not exist or that the prophetic literature settles the question without remainder. The most pastorally responsible position is probably to hold the prophetic condemnation as load-bearing while admitting that the canon's full conversation about sacrifice, substitution, and beloved sons is genuinely complicated — a complication the Christian tradition has tried to resolve in the cross and the Jewish tradition has held in the Akedah's *aqedah* tension as an unresolved meditation.

The closing question, then: the chapter ends with Hezekiah waiting in the wings, the temple doors shut, and four Ephraimite chiefs walking captives to Jericho. Where in your own life does the moral seriousness you need most come from a place your tribal categories had ruled illegitimate? And what would it cost you to receive it from there?
