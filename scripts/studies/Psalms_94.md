# Psalm 94 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 94 stands at a striking position in Book IV. Anonymous in the Masoretic Text and lacking a superscription, it interrupts the *Yahweh malak* enthronement cluster: Psalm 93 declared the eternal kingship; Psalm 95 will summon Israel to worship the King; but Psalm 94 — placed between them — turns to the dark question that any declaration of divine sovereignty must answer. *If the LORD reigns, why do the wicked rule? If his throne is established, why are the widow, the sojourner, and the fatherless slaughtered with impunity?* The psalm's opening is among the harshest in the Psalter: *El-neqamot Yahweh El-neqamot hofia* (אֵל־נְקָמוֹת יְהוָה אֵל נְקָמוֹת הוֹפִיעַ, "O LORD, God of vengeance, O God of vengeance, shine forth!" 94:1). The doubling of *El-neqamot* (God of vengeances — note the plural form) is liturgically deliberate, an invocation that summons the Judge-King.

Form-critically, Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 94 as a hybrid — primarily a *Klagelied des Volkes* (community lament) with strong wisdom-instruction features and individual-thanksgiving elements in its closing strophes. The psalm shifts genre as it shifts perspective: corporate lament and imprecation (vv. 1–7), wisdom address to the wicked and rebuke of fools (vv. 8–11), beatitude and instruction of the disciplined (vv. 12–15), individual testimony of divine help (vv. 16–19), and corporate confidence in divine justice against unjust rulers (vv. 20–23). Westermann and Hossfeld-Zenger both note that the psalm's hybrid form is theologically purposeful: lament must be answered by wisdom, and wisdom must be tested by lament. The psalm is doing the work of integrating Israel's protest with Israel's instruction.

Within the canonical structure of Book IV, Psalm 94's placement between Psalms 93 and 95 — within the *Yahweh malak* cluster — is one of the most theologically pointed editorial decisions in the Psalter. Wilson's *Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* (1985) reads it as the necessary darker counterpoint to the enthronement declarations: the cluster cannot proclaim divine kingship without honestly facing the question of unpunished wickedness. Hossfeld-Zenger and McCann emphasize this canonical interpretation. Mitchell reads Psalm 94 as the lament-eschatological hinge of the cluster — the psalm that asks how long until the King's justice is enacted on earth.

Dating is uncertain. Some scholars (Goldingay, Tate) lean post-exilic, reading the "wicked rulers" of v. 20 as Persian or Hellenistic governors who frame injustice by statute. Others (Hossfeld-Zenger) see a more general setting and decline to specify. The reference to "wicked rulers framing injustice by statute" (*kisse hawwot ḥober alekha yotzer amal alei-ḥoq* — "throne of destruction allied with you, framing trouble by law," v. 20) suggests a context where Israel's people suffer under unjust legal-political authorities — a setting plausible across multiple periods.

ANE parallels are significant. The "vengeance of God" theme has Mesopotamian and Ugaritic precedents — divine retribution on behalf of the oppressed is a stock ANE theological motif, especially in the prologues to law codes (Hammurabi, Lipit-Ishtar) where the king-god is invoked as the avenger of the poor. The Hebrew *neqamah* (vengeance) is not personal vendetta but legal-cosmic redress; the *El-neqamot* is the God who restores cosmic order by punishing wickedness. The wicked's denial that "the LORD does not see; the God of Jacob does not perceive" (v. 7) directly echoes the practical atheism Job's friends ascribe to the wicked (Job 22:13–14) and Isaiah's denunciation (Isa 29:15). The vocation of Yahweh as judge of the earth has direct Ugaritic and Mesopotamian parallels.

Jewish liturgical placement is significant. *Tamid* 7:4 in the Mishnah identifies Psalm 94 as the *shir shel yom* — the song of the day — for Wednesday (the fourth day of the week). The fourth day in Genesis 1 is when God creates the sun, moon, and stars — the heavenly bodies that govern time and pronounce, in their orderly courses, the rule of God. The Mishnah's pairing of Psalm 94 with the fourth day reads the heavenly luminaries as witnesses to divine cosmic justice — an arresting connection between cosmic order and the cry for vengeance. Psalm 94 is recited on Wednesday morning in observant Jewish prayer.

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve Psalm 94 in 4QPsᵇ and 11QPsᵃ with text largely conforming to the MT. The Septuagint (LXX 93) preserves the daily-psalm tradition, attaching it to "the fourth day of the week" (*Tetradi sabbatōn*).

Hebrew terms worth lingering over: *El-neqamot* (אֵל־נְקָמוֹת, "God of vengeances," with the abstract plural intensifying); *hofia* (הוֹפִיעַ, "shine forth," theophanic appearance language used in Deut 33:2 and Pss 50:2, 80:1); *ad-matai* (עַד־מָתַי, "how long," the lament-formula); *ki lo yittosh Yahweh ammo* (כִּי לֹא־יִטֹּשׁ יְהוָה עַמּוֹ, "for the LORD will not forsake his people," v. 14); *ashrei ha-gever asher teyasrennu Yah* (אַשְׁרֵי הַגֶּבֶר אֲשֶׁר תְּיַסְּרֶנּוּ יָּהּ, "blessed is the man whom you discipline, O LORD," v. 12); *kisse hawwot* (כִּסֵּא הַוּוֹת, "throne of destruction" or "wicked rulers," v. 20); *yatzar amal alei-ḥoq* (יֹצֵר עָמָל עֲלֵי־חֹק, "framing trouble by statute," v. 20).

## Lens 2: The Literature

Psalm 94 unfolds in five movements: imprecatory lament (vv. 1–7), wisdom rebuke (vv. 8–11), beatitude and instruction (vv. 12–15), individual testimony (vv. 16–19), and confidence-with-imprecation (vv. 20–23). The structure mirrors the psalm's theological journey: from cry of outrage through theological-pedagogical correction to personal testimony of help and finally to renewed confidence in divine justice.

Lowth's parallelism is dense throughout. The opening doubling — *El-neqamot Yahweh El-neqamot hofia* — uses repetition for liturgical-theological intensification. Verses 3–4 deploy a triple *ad-matai* (how long), the classic lament-formula. Verse 7 names the wicked's blasphemy in synonymous parallelism: "the LORD does not see / the God of Jacob does not perceive." Verses 8–11 then refute the blasphemy with rhetorical questions: "He who planted the ear, does he not hear? He who formed the eye, does he not see?" The argumentation is deeply biblical — God's creative agency in forming the senses is the proof of his sensory awareness; the maker of the ear cannot be deaf, the maker of the eye cannot be blind. This is among the most piercing pieces of theological logic in the Psalter.

The structural diagram reveals a chiastic shape:
- A: Imprecation against wicked rulers (vv. 1–7)
- B: Wisdom rebuke / God sees (vv. 8–11)
- **C: Beatitude / discipline / *the LORD will not forsake his people* (vv. 12–15)**
- B': Individual testimony / God helped me (vv. 16–19)
- A': Imprecation against unjust rulers / God will wipe them out (vv. 20–23)

The chiastic center — vv. 12–15 — is the theological heart: *ki lo yittosh Yahweh ammo* (the LORD will not forsake his people). Erich Zenger and Walter Brueggemann both emphasize this: the imprecatory bookends are theologically contained within the central confession of covenantal faithfulness. The vengeance-cry is not raw revenge but the petition that the covenantal God act as he has promised — that he not abandon his heritage to oppression.

Verse 11's striking aphorism — *Yahweh yodea mahshevot adam ki-hemma havel* (the LORD knows the thoughts of man, that they are but a breath) — is famously quoted by Paul in 1 Corinthians 3:20, and it deploys the *hevel* of Ecclesiastes (vapor, breath) for the wicked's thoughts.

The closing strophe (vv. 20–23) returns to the imprecation but transforms it: the personal testimony of vv. 16–19 has changed the register. The God who has been "my stronghold" and "my rock of refuge" (v. 22) is the same God whose justice will fall on those who frame injustice by statute (v. 20). The imprecation is now grounded in personal experience of divine help.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

In the Jewish trajectory, Psalm 94 has held a steady liturgical place as the Wednesday *shir shel yom*. *Midrash Tehillim* connects the imprecation to the Exodus and the eschatological judgment of Israel's oppressors. Rashi reads the psalm as the cry of the exiles under foreign domination — the wicked rulers of v. 20 are the Roman and earlier foreign powers who frame injustice by statute. Ibn Ezra emphasizes the wisdom-pedagogical sections: God who formed the senses cannot be ignorant. Radak balances lament and wisdom — the psalm teaches the worshiper to hold both protest and trust. Nachmanides connects the psalm to the persistent question of the wicked's prosperity, reading the chiastic center as the answer: God will not forsake his people, even when his vengeance tarries.

In modern Jewish thought, Buber reads the psalm dialogically — Israel's cry remains a real address to a real Thou, and the divine "not forsaking" of v. 14 is not a cliché but a relational pledge. Heschel, in *The Prophets*, draws on Psalm 94 for his theology of divine pathos — God is not indifferent to oppression but burns with it. Levenson notes the psalm's role in the canonical structure of Book IV: enthronement (Ps 93) cannot stand without lament (Ps 94). Sarna and Zornberg both emphasize the psalm's wisdom-honesty about lived suffering.

In the Christian trajectory, Psalm 94 has been pastorally weighty but theologically fraught. Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 93 (LXX) reads the imprecation Christologically: the church suffers under unjust rulers, and the *El-neqamot* is the God who will judge the world in righteousness through Christ. Augustine's reading is restrained — the vengeance is God's, not ours, and the church waits. Cassiodorus and Aquinas follow this pattern. Aquinas takes v. 12 (the beatitude of divine discipline) as a foundational text for the theology of suffering as pedagogical. Luther reads the psalm pastorally, identifying with the lament of the persecuted church under hostile authorities. Calvin (1557 *Commentary*) emphasizes the wisdom-rhetorical force of vv. 8–11: the wicked's claim that God does not see is the deepest folly.

The major modern theological-pastoral retrieval of Psalm 94 has come through Walter Brueggemann's seminal essay *The Costly Loss of Lament* (1986) and Erich Zenger's *A God of Vengeance? Understanding the Psalms of Divine Wrath* (1996). Both argue that the church's tendency to silence the imprecatory psalms has been spiritually impoverishing: imprecation is not raw revenge but the cry of the wronged turned over to God for justice. Zenger argues that the imprecation is the alternative to vigilante justice — the worshiper hands the desire for vengeance to God rather than enacting it. Brueggemann, in *The Message of the Psalms*, reads Psalm 94 as Israel's "psalm of disorientation" turning toward "new orientation" through the central confession of covenantal faithfulness.

NT engagement with Psalm 94 is significant. Paul quotes v. 11 in 1 Corinthians 3:20 ("the Lord knows the thoughts of the wise, that they are futile"). The vengeance theology of Psalm 94 is taken up in Romans 12:19 — *emoi ekdikēsis, egō antapodōsō, legei kyrios* ("vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord"; cf. Deut 32:35). Paul's quotation of this kind of theology is *prescriptive* for Christian non-retaliation: precisely *because* God is the *El-neqamot*, the believer is to refuse personal vengeance and entrust the matter to God. This is the same logic that runs through Psalm 94 itself — the *imprecation* is the alternative to retaliation, not its license. Hebrews 10:30 makes the same connection: "vengeance is mine, I will repay." Revelation 6:9–11 (the souls under the altar crying *ad-matai*, "how long") draws directly on Psalm 94's lament.

Modern scholarship — Gunkel, Mowinckel, Westermann, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate, Wenham, Brueggemann, Miller, Brown, Tanner, Mitchell — has converged on three points: the psalm's hybrid form (lament-wisdom-thanksgiving), its canonical placement as the necessary dark counterpoint to the *Yahweh malak* enthronement, and the theological seriousness of its imprecation. C. S. Lewis's *Reflections on the Psalms* (1958) wrestled honestly with the imprecatory psalms; Lewis was uncomfortable with them but recognized their honesty about evil. More recent scholarship (Brueggemann, Zenger, Ellen Davis) has made the case that the imprecatory psalms are theological gifts to the church.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

Two tensions sit at the heart of this psalm and its reception.

The first is the **vengeance-theology and Christian non-retaliation tension**. Can a Christian pray Psalm 94 — with its cry "shine forth, O God of vengeance" — alongside Jesus's command to love enemies (Matt 5:44) and Paul's injunction to never repay evil for evil (Rom 12:17–21)? Three positions exist. (1) The dispensational/discontinuity reading: the imprecatory psalms belonged to an earlier dispensation; the Christian, under the new covenant, prays only for enemies and does not invoke vengeance. (2) The classical/integrated reading (Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin, the magisterial tradition): the imprecation is rightly prayed by the believer when properly understood — the prayer turns vengeance over to God rather than enacting it personally; Romans 12:19 quotes precisely this kind of theology *as the basis* for Christian non-retaliation. The believer's love of enemies and the believer's prayer for divine justice are not contradictory but complementary. (3) The Brueggemann-Zenger-Davis recovery: the imprecatory psalms are theological-pastoral gifts that the church has tended to suppress to its impoverishment; they give voice to the cry of the oppressed and refuse to silence it; they hand the desire for vengeance to God rather than enact it; they are essential to honest prayer in a violent world. The strongest steelman of the dispensational reading is its straightforward reading of Jesus's love-of-enemies command; the strongest steelman of the classical reading is its careful integration of Old and New Testament theology of justice; the strongest steelman of the Brueggemann-Zenger recovery is its pastoral honesty about evil and its refusal to spiritualize injustice. **Where this leaves us:** Psalm 94's imprecation is rightly prayed by the church when prayed *toward* God rather than *by* the believer; the cry "shine forth, O God of vengeance" is not the believer's sentence on the wicked but the believer's plea that the rightful Judge act. Romans 12:19 confirms rather than contradicts this — the believer refuses personal vengeance precisely *because* God is the *El-neqamot*.

The second tension is the **pedagogical-discipline reading of v. 12** ("blessed is the man whom you discipline"). The verse moves from imprecation to beatitude — the same God whose vengeance falls on the wicked also disciplines those he loves. Three positions are visible. (1) The traditional pedagogical reading (Aquinas, Calvin): suffering is divine pedagogy, formative of the saints; the *yasar* (discipline) of v. 12 is the beatitude of being shaped by God. (2) The Joban-protest reading: the discipline-of-the-righteous theology can be weaponized to spiritualize undeserved suffering; Job rebukes precisely this move when his friends use it. (3) The integrative reading (Brueggemann, Bonhoeffer): divine discipline is real but is not a license to dismiss the suffering of the oppressed; the psalm holds beatitude and imprecation together precisely so that the discipline-of-the-righteous theme cannot be detached from the imprecation-against-injustice theme. **Where this leaves us:** v. 12's beatitude must be read in the context of vv. 1–7's imprecation; God disciplines those he loves and judges those who oppress them, and these are not the same act seen from different angles.

A third, smaller tension worth naming: the **wicked-rulers-framing-injustice-by-statute** of v. 20. The Hebrew *yotzer amal alei-ḥoq* literally describes those who legislate injustice — who use legal statute to enact oppression. The phrase has been potent in modern liberation theology, civil rights theology (Martin Luther King Jr. citing the prophets and psalms in *Letter from Birmingham Jail*), and the Black church's use of imprecatory psalms (cf. Walter Brueggemann's and Allen Dwight Callahan's work). It speaks directly to the question of unjust law — and Psalm 94 names this category and declares that God will not be allied with such "thrones."

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 94 is the necessary dark counterpoint within Book IV's enthronement cluster. Where Psalm 93 declares the LORD reigns, Psalm 94 asks: when, how long, until your reign is visibly enacted on earth? The answer, at the chiastic center, is the covenantal pledge: *the LORD will not forsake his people*. The imprecation is real, the discipline is real, the help is real, and the divine *neqamah* is rightly entrusted to God who alone judges the earth.

**Closing question:** If Romans 12:19 quotes "vengeance is mine, says the Lord" *as the basis* for Christian non-retaliation, what would it look like for you, this week, to pray Psalm 94's imprecation honestly — handing the desire for vengeance to God — rather than either suppressing it or enacting it?

## The Thread

Psalm 94 is the dark counterpoint within Book IV's enthronement cluster. It cries *El-neqamot* and asks "how long," it rebukes the fools who think God does not see, it pronounces beatitude on those whom God disciplines, and at its chiastic heart it confesses that the LORD will not forsake his people. The imprecation is real, the discipline is real, the help is real, and the divine *neqamah* is rightly entrusted to God who alone judges the earth — which is precisely why Romans 12:19 cites this kind of theology as the basis for Christian non-retaliation.
