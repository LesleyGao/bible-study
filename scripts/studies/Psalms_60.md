# Psalm 60 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 60 is a community lament after military defeat. The superscription is the longest in Book II: "To the choirmaster: according to Shushan Eduth. A Miktam of David; for instruction; when he strove with Aram-naharaim and with Aram-zobah, and when Joab on his return struck down twelve thousand of Edom in the Valley of Salt." Each phrase opens a window. *Shushan Eduth* (שׁוּשַׁן עֵדוּת — "Lily of Testimony") is almost certainly a melody-name, like *al-tashchet* in Psalms 57–59 — perhaps a song originally connected to a covenant ceremony. *Miktam* (מִכְתָּם) repeats the genre tag of the Davidic cluster (16, 56–60). The unique addition is *le-lammed* (לְלַמֵּד — "for instruction" or "to teach"), which, like the inscription on 2 Samuel 1:18 ("the song of the bow … to teach the children of Judah"), signals that this is a psalm meant to be memorized by the people. It is national pedagogy in song.

The historical reference is to David's wars in 2 Samuel 8 and 2 Samuel 10. *Aram-naharaim* (אֲרַם נַהֲרַיִם — "Aram of the two rivers," the upper Mesopotamian region around Haran) and *Aram-zobah* (the Aramean kingdom in central Syria) form an alliance against David. Joab, David's general, defeats Edom in the Valley of Salt (south of the Dead Sea), killing twelve thousand. The number in 2 Samuel 8:13 is eighteen thousand; some commentators harmonize by suggesting two separate engagements. The historical scenario is plausibly the period after a *partial* defeat — the army has suffered reverses, the land has "quaked" (v. 2), but the king is preparing to counter-attack into Edom (v. 9).

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 60 as a *Volksklagelied* (community lament) — distinct from the individual laments in his form-critical taxonomy. The community-lament genre typically opens with corporate complaint ("you have rejected us"), recalls God's saving acts, includes an oracle of salvation (here vv. 6–8, "God has spoken in his holiness"), and closes with renewed petition and trust. Sigmund Mowinckel saw the psalm as a cultic prayer for use in covenant-renewal liturgies after national defeat. Hans-Joachim Kraus treats it as a "lament of the people in time of war." Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger argue the psalm was edited for the post-exilic community, where the loss of land and exile gave the imagery of "broken defenses" and "shaken land" renewed urgency.

The divine speech in vv. 6–8 — *Elohim dibber be-qodsho* (אֱלֹהִים דִּבֶּר בְּקָדְשׁוֹ — "God has spoken in his holiness") — has the form of a covenantal-territorial oracle. God claims Shechem, Succoth, Gilead, Manasseh, Ephraim, Judah as his own (the heart of the promised land), then Moab, Edom, Philistia as conquered subordinates. Some commentators (Goldingay, Tate) suggest the oracle quotes an older covenant text, perhaps drawn from a Davidic conquest-liturgy.

Psalm 60 sits inside Book II of the Psalter (the Elohistic Psalter, Pss 42–72), where *YHWH* has been replaced with *Elohim*. The substitution is particularly striking in this psalm: the opening *Elohim zenachtanu* (אֱלֹהִים זְנַחְתָּנוּ — "O God, you have rejected us," v. 1) places the covenant name *Elohim* directly against the community's experience of rejection. Joel Burnett has argued that the Elohistic redaction in Book II reflects a moment in Israel's history (perhaps post-722 northern displacement, or post-587 exile) when the divine name *YHWH* felt too intimate, too dangerous, or too compromised to use, and the more general *Elohim* opened space for renewed prayer. The Dead Sea Scrolls (4QPsa) preserve fragments of Psalm 60 with no major variants, and the Septuagint preserves the same lament-oracle-petition structure.

A literary fact of major importance: verses 5–12 of Psalm 60 are essentially identical to Psalm 108:6–13. Combined with the Psalm 57:7–11 = Psalm 108:1–5 doublet, this means Psalm 108 is a deliberate composite of the praise-half of Psalm 57 and the lament-half of Psalm 60 stitched into a new whole. Hossfeld and Zenger treat this as one of the clearest evidences of editorial composition in the Psalter.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Lowth's parallelism here works in a tightly structured petition-oracle-petition shape. Strophe 1 (vv. 1–5) is communal lament. The verbs come in agonized parallel: *zenachtanu* (you have rejected us), *peratztanu* (you have broken us), *anafta* (you were angry), *hir'ashtah eretz* (you made the land quake), *petzamtah* (you tore it open). The synonymous parallelism piles up the disasters until they reach the most disorienting image: *hishqitanu yayin tar'elah* (הִשְׁקִיתָנוּ יַיִן תַּרְעֵלָה — "you have given us wine to drink that made us stagger," v. 3), the staggering-wine of divine wrath that surfaces also in Isaiah 51:17 and Jeremiah 25:15.

Strophe 2 (vv. 6–8) is divine oracle. The shift is dramatic: the speaker stops complaining and quotes God speaking. The geography of vv. 6–8 — Shechem, Succoth, Gilead, Manasseh, Ephraim, Judah, Moab, Edom, Philistia — moves from the patriarchal-promised-land core (Shechem is where Abraham first received the land-promise, Genesis 12:6–7; Succoth is Jacob's first stop after Penuel, Genesis 33:17) outward through the tribes and then over the surrounding nations. The territorial sweep is breathtaking. Moab as "washbasin" (a vessel for washing feet, the most menial domestic object), Edom as the place where God "casts his shoe" (a gesture of claiming property, compare Ruth 4:7), Philistia as the conquered enemy over whom God shouts in triumph — these are images of total reversal.

Strophe 3 (vv. 9–12) is renewed petition: "who will bring me to the fortified city? Who will lead me to Edom?" The question hangs in the air, then answers itself in v. 11–12: vain is human help, but with God we will do valiantly. The closing line *be-Elohim na'aseh chayil ve-hu yavus tzareinu* (בֵּאלֹהִים נַעֲשֶׂה־חָיִל וְהוּא יָבוּס צָרֵינו — "with God we shall do valiantly; it is he who will tread down our foes") is a confession that combines covenantal trust with martial intent.

One *selah* divides the psalm at v. 4. The structure is therefore lament-pause-petition-oracle-petition, and the *selah* sits at the moment when a "banner for those who fear you" is raised in the midst of disaster. The banner — *nes* (נֵס) — is both literal military standard and metaphorical sign of refuge.

The chiasm of rejection bookends the psalm: v. 1 ("O God, you have rejected us") and v. 10 ("have you not rejected us, O God?") form an *inclusio* that frames the divine oracle in the middle. The lament *brackets* the oracle of victory, refusing to let the oracle dissolve the protest. This is mature lament-theology: the people hear God's voice and still hold the question of rejection in their mouths.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Psalm 60 is the final psalm in the Davidic *miktam* cluster (Psalms 56–60), and it is the only one in the cluster that prays as a community rather than an individual. The cluster's progression is striking: terror (56), refuge (57), imprecation (58), surveillance (59), national defeat and protest (60). Hossfeld and Zenger read this trajectory as deliberately curated by the editors of Book II — the *miktam* psalms together form a David-anthology that teaches the prayer-postures of the king through every species of crisis.

The doublet with Psalm 108 has already been noted; here it deserves a second look. Psalm 108 takes the praise-half of Psalm 57 (vv. 1–5 there = vv. 7–11 here) and joins it to the lament-half of Psalm 60 (vv. 6–13 there = vv. 5–12 here). This means Psalm 108 begins with confident praise ("my heart is steadfast") and *ends* with lament ("have you not rejected us?"). Psalm 60 begins with lament and ends with confidence. Whichever order the redactor chose, the same building blocks are recombined to make different liturgical music. Erhard Gerstenberger calls this "cultic improvisation"; Goldingay calls it "editorial reuse"; Hossfeld and Zenger see in it the canonical Psalter's openness to redactional life.

Jewish reception has read Psalm 60 within the Davidic conquest narratives. The *Midrash Tehillim* connects the staggering-wine of v. 3 to the cup of judgment given to Israel in exile. Rashi reads each toponym in vv. 6–8 within its biblical-historical context — Shechem and Succoth as patriarchal sites, Moab and Edom as eternal hostile neighbors. Ibn Ezra, ever the literalist, focuses on the historical superscription and the campaign in 2 Samuel 8. Radak emphasizes the prayer's pedagogical function (*le-lammed*) — this is a psalm Israel must learn, because every generation will face national crisis. Modern Jewish thinkers have variously engaged the psalm. Martin Buber heard the lament-confidence dialectic as a paradigm of the I-Thou prayer in time of national distress. Abraham Joshua Heschel treated the staggering-wine of v. 3 as expressing the divine *pathos* in covenantal anger. Jon Levenson, in his work on covenant theology, reads the territorial oracle as deeply tied to the unconditional Davidic-Abrahamic land-promise. Nahum Sarna has written on the geography of the oracle as a key to understanding Israelite covenant theology. Liturgically, Psalm 60 has been associated with prayers in time of national crisis — the *Ne'ilah* prayers of Yom Kippur in some traditions, and special petitions during war.

Christian reception has the most complicated record of any psalm in this cluster. Augustine (*Enarratio* on Psalm 59 in Latin numbering) reads the territorial oracle christologically: Shechem, Succoth, Gilead are the gentile nations gathered into the church; Moab and Edom are the conquered powers of sin and death. Athanasius treats the staggering-wine of v. 3 as a figure of the eucharistic cup, paradoxically. Cassiodorus emphasizes the threefold lament-oracle-petition as a model of liturgical movement. Aquinas reads the psalm as a typological prayer of Christ as the new David leading his people into the inheritance. Luther in his *Operationes* makes the psalm a paradigm of the *theologia crucis* — God appears against his people before he appears for them. Calvin's 1557 commentary draws out the providential reading: God's apparent rejection is pedagogical, not final, and the oracle in vv. 6–8 is the secret strength of the people in time of defeat.

The modern critical conversation runs through Gunkel, Mowinckel (cultic war-lament), Westermann (lament moves to praise), Kraus (the prayer of the people in war), Hossfeld and Zenger (compositional layers and the Psalm 108 doublet), Goldingay (pastoral-theological), Tate (*Word Biblical Commentary*), Wenham (canonical-ethical). Walter Brueggemann places Psalm 60 in his "psalms of disorientation" with strong elements of "new orientation," and treats v. 1's *zenachtanu* ("you have rejected us") as a paradigm of credible Israelite protest — the willingness to address God in language that holds him to covenant accountability. Patrick Miller, William Brown, and Beth LaNeel Tanner have all engaged the psalm.

The harder modern question is the territorial oracle's afterlife. The militaristic geography of vv. 6–8 — God claiming Shechem, Manasseh, Ephraim, Judah, while Moab and Edom and Philistia are washbasin and shoe and shouting — has been appropriated in deeply problematic ways. American manifest-destiny theology of the nineteenth century explicitly read the oracle as authorizing westward expansion; the Afrikaner Voortrekker theology of the Great Trek read it as authorizing settler claims to South African land; Christian Zionism in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries reads it as authorizing modern Israeli territorial expansion. Walter Brueggemann's *The Land* and Norman Habel's *The Land Is Mine* both wrestle with how the territorial promises of the Hebrew Bible are inherited by Christian readers without becoming a charter for colonial violence. Liberation theologians (Gustavo Gutiérrez, Naim Ateek), Palestinian Christians (Mitri Raheb), and postcolonial scholars (Musa Dube, R.S. Sugirtharajah) have all argued that the psalm's territorial language must be read with extreme care — as the prayer of a small, threatened community holding onto covenantal hope, not as a master text for imperial expansion.

The New Testament does not quote Psalm 60 directly, but Romans 8:31–37 ("if God is for us, who can be against us?") echoes the trust-in-defeat pattern, and Paul's "more than conquerors" language in Romans 8:37 is in the same theological register as v. 12's "with God we shall do valiantly."

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The central tension in Psalm 60 is the gap between protest and triumph. The psalm opens with rejection ("you have rejected us, broken our defenses"), is interrupted by an oracle of total territorial dominance, and ends with renewed petition that itself rests on covenantal confidence. How does the church pray a psalm that holds these together?

Three positions deserve a fair hearing.

(1) The *covenantal-territorial position* (held by parts of the Reformed tradition, by Christian Zionism in modern form, and historically by various settler theologies) reads the oracle in vv. 6–8 as a permanent claim — God's land is God's land, and the oracle authorizes the people of God to take possession. Steelman: the oracle is God's own speech, not the psalmist's; the territorial promises in Genesis 12, 15, 17 are unconditional; and the New Testament does not abolish them but extends them.

(2) The *spiritualizing-Christological position* (Augustine, Aquinas, much of Reformation exegesis) reads the territorial oracle as a typological prefigurement — the conquest of Shechem, Moab, Edom is figural for the conquest of sin, death, and the powers; in Christ the gentile nations are gathered without territorial violence. Steelman: Jesus refuses the kingdoms of the world from Satan's hand (Matthew 4:8–10); his kingdom is "not of this world" (John 18:36); the New Testament universally spiritualizes territory, e.g. Hebrews 11:13–16, "they desired a better country."

(3) The *protest-and-pedagogical position* (Brueggemann, Habel, liberation theology) reads the psalm as the prayer of a *defeated, small, threatened community* holding onto covenantal hope through honest protest. The oracle is not a charter for conquest but a defiant declaration that the God who seems to have abandoned the people still claims the land that once was theirs. The psalm refuses both despair and triumphalism; it is the prayer of a remnant. Steelman: the psalm's structure brackets the oracle with lament — v. 1 and v. 10 both confess "you have rejected us" — which means the oracle is heard in the mode of clinging hope, not victorious certainty. The territorial language must be read as covenantal protest, not imperial program.

**Where this leaves us:** the psalm simultaneously authorizes the cry of the defeated to demand God's presence and refuses to be flattened into a charter for conquest. The closing question: when you pray "with God we shall do valiantly," can you hold that confession together with the lament that brackets it — or does the trust dissolve the protest, or the protest dissolve the trust?
