# Jeremiah 23: The Branch and the Divine Council

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jeremiah 23 is the theological pivot of the kings-collection. After the four-king indictment of chapter 22, the chapter opens with a sweeping woe against "the shepherds" — a pastoral metaphor that in the ancient Near East was technical political language for kings. The Mari letters (eighteenth century BC), Hammurabi's prologue, and Egyptian royal inscriptions all use *shepherd* as a royal title. By calling the Davidic kings "shepherds who destroy and scatter," Jeremiah is invoking a millennium of ANE political vocabulary and turning it against the dynasty he has just condemned.

The chapter does not have a single dating formula, but its placement and content suggest it was assembled during the late reign of Zedekiah, sometime between 597 and 586 BC, when the deportation of 597 had already removed the elite to Babylon. Verses 7–8 — *they shall no longer say "As the LORD lives who brought up the people of Israel out of the land of Egypt," but "As the LORD lives who brought up… out of the north country"* — presuppose an audience that already knows what exile from the north means. The Babylonian Chronicle (BM 21946) confirms the 597 deportation; the Weidner ration tablets (Vorderasiatisches Museum, Berlin) confirm the survival of Jehoiachin and his court in Babylon. Jeremiah is preaching to a community that knows, from contemporary cuneiform records, that some of its own people are being fed barley rations in the imperial capital.

The Branch oracle of 23:5–6 is the textual climax. The Hebrew is dense and architecturally precise: *hineni meqim le-David tzemach tzaddiq u-malakh melekh ve-hiskil ve-asah mishpat u-tzedaqah ba-aretz* (הִנְנִי מֵקִים לְדָוִד צֶמַח צַדִּיק וּמָלַךְ מֶלֶךְ וְהִשְׂכִּיל וְעָשָׂה מִשְׁפָּט וּצְדָקָה בָּאָרֶץ) — *I will raise up for David a righteous Branch, and he shall reign as king and deal wisely, and shall execute justice and righteousness in the land*. The verb *meqim* ("raise up") is the standard Davidic-promise verb of 2 Samuel 7:12. The noun *tzemach* ("Branch, sprout, growth") is a botanical metaphor for dynastic survival from a stump — and the **Karatepe inscription** (Phoenician, eighth century BC) uses cognate vocabulary for legitimate dynastic succession. The throne-name *YHWH-tzidqenu* (יְהוָה צִדְקֵנוּ, "the LORD is our righteousness," 23:6) is an unprecedented construction in the Hebrew Bible: a human Davidic descendant is named with the Tetragrammaton-plus-noun. It deliberately puns on **Zedekiah**, whose name means "YHWH-is-my-righteousness" (*Tzidqi-yahu*) — the failed king's throne-name is reversed into the future Branch's true name. The pun is one of the most pointed in the prophetic corpus: the king who carries the right name has betrayed it; the Branch who is coming will *be* it.

The Branch oracle stands at the head of a stream of "Branch" texts. **Isaiah 11:1** uses *netzer* (a different Hebrew word for shoot) but with overlapping function. **Zechariah 3:8 and 6:12** explicitly identify the *tzemach* as the messianic-priestly figure. The **Damascus Document** (CD 7:18–21) from the Qumran library (Cave 4 fragments published by Vermes and Charlesworth) interprets Numbers 24:17 alongside *tzemach* texts as messianic. The **4QFlorilegium** (4Q174) cites 2 Samuel 7 and treats the Davidic shoot as eschatological. By the late Second Temple period, "Branch" had become a recognized messianic technical term.

The polemic against false prophets (23:9–40) has a specific historical referent: the rival prophets who, during the same siege Jeremiah was preaching surrender, were preaching deliverance — *peace, peace, when there is no peace* (cf. 6:14, 8:11). Hananiah ben Azzur (chapter 28) and Shemaiah of Nehelam (chapter 29) are named in subsequent chapters. The polemic is not abstract; it is the prophetic argument inside a community whose institutional prophets had a different read on the geopolitical situation than Jeremiah did. Pashhur ben Immer of chapter 20, who beat Jeremiah and put him in the stocks, is the institutional priest-prophet against whom Jeremiah's voice is being measured.

Key terms in this chapter: *tzemach tzaddiq* (צֶמַח צַדִּיק, "righteous Branch," 23:5), *YHWH-tzidqenu* (יְהוָה צִדְקֵנוּ, "the LORD our righteousness," 23:6), *sod YHWH* (סוֹד יְהוָה, "the council of the LORD," 23:18, 22), *massa YHWH* (מַשָּׂא יְהוָה, "the burden of the LORD," 23:33–40 — punning on *massa* meaning both "oracle" and "burden"). The *sod* terminology is technical: it refers to the heavenly council of divine beings who deliberate around YHWH (cf. 1 Kings 22:19–22; Job 1–2; Psalm 82). The prophet's claim is that he has stood in that council and the false prophets have not.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is composed of multiple discrete oracles that have been edited into a sequence: the shepherds-oracle (1–4), the Branch oracle (5–6), the new exodus formula (7–8), the lament over the prophets (9–15), the false-prophets oracle (16–22), the omnipresence oracle (23–24), the dreams-and-word polemic (25–32), and the *massa* / "burden" oracle (33–40). Lundbom (Anchor) treats these as carefully arranged rather than randomly assembled, with the Branch oracle as the rhetorical center.

The Branch oracle itself is two verses of architecturally tight Hebrew. The first verse establishes the king's *function* (justice and righteousness); the second establishes his *name* (YHWH our righteousness). The form is an oracle of promise, but it is set against the immediate background of the kings-collection, so its rhetorical effect is contrast: *that* dynasty has failed, *this* descendant will succeed. McKane (ICC) read 5–6 as a late insertion; Holladay (Hermeneia) and Lundbom defended its Jeremianic origin. The verse appears in slightly different form at 33:14–16 — a doublet that has been treated by critical scholarship as evidence of compositional history. The MT and LXX of 23:5–6 are essentially identical; the LXX places the equivalent of 23:7–8 at the end of chapter 23, but the Branch verses themselves are textually stable.

The polemic against the false prophets is a tour de force of prophetic rhetoric. The prophet himself enters the oracle with a personal lament — *my heart is broken within me; all my bones shake; I am like a drunken man* (23:9) — a rare moment of subjective speech in the prophetic corpus, comparable only to Habakkuk's prologue and Jeremiah's own confessions (chapters 11–20). The polemic deploys six distinct tests for distinguishing true from false prophecy: moral character (vv. 11–14, the prophets commit adultery), institutional locus (v. 11, *even in my house I have found their evil*), divine commission (v. 21, *I did not send them*), participation in the *sod* (vv. 18, 22, *if they had stood in my council*), the dream / word distinction (vv. 25–28, dreams are "straw," the word is "wheat"), and the rhetorical ventriloquism test (v. 31, prophets who *use their tongues and declare "declares the LORD"*). The cumulative effect is a sustained reflection on the epistemology of prophetic authority — perhaps the most concentrated such reflection in the OT.

The *massa* / "burden" wordplay (vv. 33–40) is one of the most intricate in the Hebrew Bible. *Massa* means both "oracle" (the standard prophetic genre-marker, cf. Isa 13:1, Nah 1:1) and "burden" (a load that is carried). When the people ask, *what is the massa of the LORD?* — meaning, "what is the LORD's oracle?" — the prophet's answer turns on the pun: *you are the massa*, you are the burden, and the LORD will cast you off. The pun has a polemical edge: the people are using *massa* to demand entertainment-prophecy, and the prophet refuses the term entirely. McKane treated this section as a late prose addition; Lundbom and Holladay defended its Jeremianic origin and identified the wordplay as characteristic of the prophet's own style.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Branch oracle is one of the most consequential messianic texts in the Old Testament, and the trajectory of its interpretation is the trajectory of the divergence between Jewish and Christian readings of Scripture itself.

In the Jewish trajectory, the *tzemach* of 23:5 became a recognized messianic title. The **Targum Jonathan** to Jeremiah 23:5 paraphrases *Branch* as *Messiah* (*meshicha*) and the name of 23:6 as the name by which the Messiah will be called. The **Babylonian Talmud at Berakhot 7b** discusses the divine names and the messianic name, with reference to Jeremiah 23:6. The **Pesikta Rabbati** (a homiletic midrash, redacted ninth century but containing earlier material) treats the *tzemach* as the eschatological Davidic king. **Rashi** (eleventh century) reads the verse plainly as messianic but resists the Christian inference that the name *YHWH-tzidqenu* signals the Messiah's divinity; for Rashi, the name signifies that *in the days of the Messiah, YHWH's righteousness will be revealed*. **Radak** follows Rashi. **Abrabanel** (fifteenth century, in his commentary on Jeremiah and in the *Mashmia Yeshuah*) develops the most sustained Jewish messianic reading: the Branch is the Messiah ben David; the name *YHWH-tzidqenu* is given to the Messiah but signifies God's vindication through him, not God's identity with him; the prophecy is unfulfilled and awaits the messianic age. **Maimonides** (*Hilkhot Melakhim* 11–12) treats the messianic king as a human Davidic descendant who will gather Israel and rebuild the temple, with the *YHWH-tzidqenu* name as honorific.

In the Christian trajectory, the Branch oracle was christological from the patristic period. **Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah*** (Hom. XVII–XVIII, surviving only in fragments and Latin translation) treats the *tzemach tzaddiq* as Christ, the *YHWH-tzidqenu* name as proof of Christ's divinity. **Jerome** in his Latin commentary (*In Hieremiam*) makes the same identification. The Latin liturgy preserved the oracle in the great O Antiphons of Advent: **"O Radix Iesse"** (December 19) — *O Root of Jesse* — explicitly fuses Isaiah 11 and Jeremiah 23. **Aquinas** (*ST* III.36.1) and **Calvin** (*Institutes* 1.13.10–11) both cite *YHWH-tzidqenu* as a Trinitarian prooftext for Christ's divine name. **Handel's *Messiah*** (1741) sets adjacent passages in its Christmas section. The contemporary critical Christian reading (Brueggemann, Stulman, O'Connor) preserves the messianic horizon while distinguishing prophetic foresight from Christian fulfillment claims.

The hermeneutical fault line is the name *YHWH-tzidqenu*. **Daniel Boyarin's *Border Lines* (2004) and *The Jewish Gospels* (2012)** argue that first-century Judaism could conceive of a divine-messianic figure within monotheism — the so-called "binitarian" or "two powers in heaven" tradition that the rabbis later anathematized but that was once a live option. Boyarin reads texts like Daniel 7's "one like a son of man" and Jeremiah 23:6's *YHWH-tzidqenu* as evidence that the Christian christological reading is not foreign to second-temple Judaism but is one possible inheritance of it. **Larry Hurtado** (*Lord Jesus Christ*, 2003) and **Richard Bauckham** (*Jesus and the God of Israel*, 2008) argue that NT high christology emerged within Jewish monotheism rather than against it — an argument that resonates with Boyarin's from a Christian direction. The contemporary Jewish response (Kugel, Levenson) tends to insist that the messianic age and the messiah's identity must be distinguished and that *YHWH-tzidqenu* names the age, not the agent.

The polemic against false prophets opens a separate trajectory. Jewish tradition (Tosefta Sanhedrin 14, Bavli Sanhedrin 89a–b) developed a halakhic framework for distinguishing true and false prophecy that draws on Deuteronomy 13 and 18 alongside Jeremiah 23. The Christian patristic tradition (Origen, Augustine, Aquinas) used Jeremiah 23 as the primary OT source for the discernment of spirits. The Reformation tradition (Calvin's *Institutes* 1.7–9) draws on Jeremiah 23:21–22 to argue that the *internal testimony of the Spirit* must accompany the external word. **Patrick D. Miller** (*The Religion of Ancient Israel*, 2000) treats *sod YHWH* in 23:18, 22 as one of the strongest pieces of evidence for a divine-council framework in pre-exilic Israelite religion. **Mark Smith** (*The Origins of Biblical Monotheism*, 2001) traces the divine council from Ugaritic texts through the Hebrew Bible, with Jeremiah 23 as a late but important witness. **Michael Heiser** (*The Unseen Realm*, 2015) builds an entire theological framework around the divine council, using Jeremiah 23 alongside Psalm 82 and Deuteronomy 32:8–9.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The Branch oracle's Christological vs. Jewish messianic reading.** Both traditions accept that the verse is messianic. They divide on whether *YHWH-tzidqenu* is a name that signifies the Messiah's divinity (Christian patristic-Reformation reading) or a name that signifies God's righteousness vindicated through the Messiah (Jewish reading from Rashi onward). The Christian reading treats Calvin's *Institutes* 1.13.10–11 and Aquinas's *ST* III.36.1 as exegetically grounded — the construction *YHWH*+predicate-noun (parallel to *YHWH-yireh* in Gen 22, *YHWH-shalom* in Judg 6, *YHWH-nissi* in Exod 17) is normally a place-name or altar-name, but here it is a personal name applied to a human descendant of David, and that semantic anomaly is what bears the christological weight. The Jewish reading (Rashi, Radak, Abrabanel) treats the name as a *theophoric* honorific signifying that the Messiah's reign will be the moment when God's righteousness is finally revealed in history, without any inference of identity-of-essence between the Messiah and YHWH. **Boyarin's binitarian thesis** complicates the standard contrast: first-century Judaism, before the rabbinic consolidation, could entertain divine-messianic readings of texts like Jeremiah 23:6 as one *internal* possibility, not as a foreign Christian imposition. Steelmanning the most uncomfortable readings on each side: the Jewish reading at its strongest insists that Christian christology has read its own conclusions into the verse, missing the more austere monotheism of the prophet himself; the Christian reading at its strongest insists that the verse's grammatical anomaly cannot be read away, and that the patristic-Reformation tradition tracked something the verse really says. Where this leaves us: Jeremiah 23:5–6 is one of the OT texts where the Jewish-Christian hermeneutical divergence cannot be papered over and yet is also the kind of text where Boyarin's work has shown the divergence to be more recent and more contingent than the polemics of either side admit.

**Tension 2: The divine council (*sod YHWH*) and prophetic epistemology.** Jeremiah 23:18 and 22 claim that the prophet's authority depends on having *stood in the council of the LORD*. The implication is that there is a divine council, that some prophets have access to it and others do not, and that this access is the *criterion* of true prophecy. The conservative reading (Calvin, Aquinas, the magisterial Protestant tradition) treats *sod* metaphorically: it signifies the prophet's intimate communion with God, not a literal council of divine beings. The critical reading (Patrick Miller, Mark Smith, Michael Heiser) treats *sod* literally: pre-exilic Israelite religion presupposed a council of divine beings (the *bnei elohim* of Job 1, the host of heaven of 1 Kings 22, the *elohim* of Psalm 82) over which YHWH presides, and Jeremiah 23 is one of the texts that takes this council for granted. The implications for **messenger-Christology** in the New Testament are non-trivial: if the OT presupposes a divine council in which prophets and angelic figures participate, then NT christology — Hurtado, Bauckham, Boyarin — has a richer Jewish substrate to draw on than the older "high christology vs. Jewish monotheism" framing allowed. Steelmanning the most uncomfortable reading: the Heiser / divine-council reading is uncomfortable for traditional monotheism because it suggests a polytheistic background that the canonical text never quite cleanses; but the texts themselves resist the cleansing, and the conservative metaphorical reading may be importing later doctrinal commitments. Where this leaves us: Jeremiah 23 forces a confrontation between the canonical text's apparent presupposition of a divine council and the doctrinal monotheism that Jewish and Christian traditions have both confessed. The two are not necessarily incompatible — strict monotheism can include heavenly beings as creatures, not deities — but the texts themselves do not always make the distinction the doctrines demand.

**One unresolved question:** If the Branch oracle is genuinely messianic, does its fulfillment lie in a single historical figure (Jewish messianic future, Christian fulfillment in Jesus) or in the recurring emergence of just kingship in history (a more sober reading that Brueggemann sometimes flirts with), and is there a way to read it that takes the prophet's word seriously as more than political idealism without forcing it into either a christological or a messianic-future grid?

## The Thread

The Branch (*tzemach*) of 23:5 is picked up directly in Zechariah 3:8 (*Behold, I will bring my servant the Branch*) and 6:12 (*the man whose name is the Branch, for he shall branch out from his place and build the temple of the LORD*) — the Zechariah passages are after the return from exile and apply the Jeremianic title to Zerubbabel and the high priest Joshua, with messianic-eschatological overtones. The name *YHWH-tzidqenu* finds its NT counterpart in 1 Corinthians 1:30 — *Christ Jesus, who became to us… righteousness from God* — where Paul takes the Jeremianic name and applies it to Christ, an exegetical move that depends on the Christian reading of Jeremiah 23:6. The polemic against false prophets is recapitulated in 2 Peter 2:1 (*there will be false teachers among you*) and 1 John 4:1 (*test the spirits*), both of which presuppose the Jeremianic test of stand-in-the-council. The shepherds-oracle of 23:1–4 is taken up directly in Ezekiel 34 (the most extended OT extension of the shepherd metaphor) and in John 10's good-shepherd discourse, where Jesus identifies himself as the shepherd whose coming Jeremiah and Ezekiel had prophesied.
