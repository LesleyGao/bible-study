# Ezekiel 38: Gog of Magog and the Trouble of Apocalyptic Identification

## Lens 1 — Historical World

Ezekiel 38–39 is the most extensively debated passage in the book. The chapter introduces a foreign ruler named Gog from the land of Magog, "the chief prince of Meshech and Tubal" (38:2), who in the latter years assembles a coalition of nations and marches against the restored people of Israel. The historical-geographical references — Meshech, Tubal, Persia, Cush, Put, Gomer, Beth-togarmah — anchor the prophecy in a specific Iron Age horizon, but the temporal reference ("after many days," "in the latter years") pulls the oracle into the eschatological future.

The geographical names can be located, with varying degrees of certainty, in Assyrian and Babylonian sources. Meshech and Tubal correspond to *Mushki* and *Tabal* in Assyrian royal inscriptions from Tiglath-Pileser I onward (eleventh century BC) — Anatolian peoples occupying what is today central and eastern Turkey. Block (NICOT, 1998) and Greenberg (Anchor, 1997) treat this identification as secure: Meshech is Phrygia or its neighbor, Tubal is Cilicia/Cappadocia. Beth-togarmah is the Til-garimmu attested in the same Assyrian sources, located in eastern Anatolia. Gomer is the Cimmerians (*Gimirrai* in Akkadian), the nomadic people who swept across Anatolia in the seventh century BC. Persia is Persia. Cush is Nubia/Sudan, Put is Libya. Magog itself is unidentified in extra-biblical sources; Josephus (*Antiquities* 1.6.1) identifies Magog with the Scythians, a guess that has shaped much subsequent interpretation but rests on no contemporary evidence.

The figure of Gog is more elusive. The closest extra-biblical analogue is the Lydian king *Gugu* (Gyges, reigned c. 680–644 BC), attested in the annals of Ashurbanipal as a ruler who first sought Assyrian help against the Cimmerians and then turned against Assyria and was destroyed. Some commentators (Greenberg cautiously, Postell more boldly, Heiser as a possibility) have proposed that Ezekiel reuses the name Gugu as a typological figure — the archetypal northern ruler who threatens and is destroyed. This is plausible but not provable. Block treats Gog as a *literary-symbolic* construction without specific historical referent: the chief of the chief, the archetype of the cosmic enemy.

The dating of the oracle is also debated. The chapter is undated, which is unusual in Ezekiel; most major oracles in the book carry precise datings. Greenberg places the oracle late in Ezekiel's ministry, after the chapter 36–37 promises of restoration; Zimmerli (Hermeneia, 1983) raised the possibility of a later editorial layer, since the apocalyptic-eschatological style stands somewhat apart from the rest of the book. Block defends Ezekielian authorship and treats the oracle as part of the original restoration block.

What is certain is the *historical theological problem* the oracle addresses: if the restoration of chapters 36–37 brings Israel home, why does the Bible then narrate yet another invasion? Ezekiel 38–39 confronts the reader with a "not-yet" within the "already" — the restoration is real, but it is not the final state. The text deliberately situates the Gog event "after many days" and "in the latter years" (38:8) — pushing it beyond the historical horizon of the immediate post-exilic return. This is the seed of biblical *apocalyptic* eschatology, the move that will flower in Daniel and Revelation.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter is structured as a series of seven divine speeches, each opening with the formula "Thus says the Lord GOD" or its variant, addressed to or about Gog. Block lays out the structure: (1) summons of Gog, 38:1–9; (2) Gog's evil scheme, 38:10–13; (3) Gog's advance, 38:14–16; (4) prior prophecy and divine vindication, 38:17–23. The divine voice oscillates between addressing Gog directly ("I am against you, O Gog") and announcing his fate to a third party — a rhetorical move that makes Gog the object of YHWH's action, never the subject.

The image of "hooks in your jaws" (38:4) is a deliberate allusion to Ezekiel's earlier oracles against Egypt (29:4) and Tyre's allies (cf. 19:4, 9, where the metaphor describes captured kings). The Assyrian iconography is well known: defeated kings led by hooks through their lower lips, a humiliation depicted on reliefs from Khorsabad and Nineveh. Block argues that Ezekiel deliberately reuses the imagery of the great oppressor empire (Assyria) to describe what YHWH will do to the eschatological enemy: the tools of the empire will be turned against the empire itself.

The phrase "uttermost parts of the north" (*yarkətê ṣāpôn*, 38:6, 15; 39:2) carries cosmological as well as geographical weight. In Canaanite mythology, the *ṣāpôn* (Mt. Zaphon, modern Jebel al-Aqra) was the dwelling of Baal — the divine mountain of the north. Psalm 48:2 retains this language for Mount Zion: "beautiful in elevation, the joy of all the earth, Mount Zion, in the far reaches of the north" (*yarkətê ṣāpôn*). When Ezekiel speaks of Gog coming from the *yarkətê ṣāpôn*, he is invoking not just a geographical direction but a cosmic-mythological location — the realm associated with chaos, foreign gods, and the threat to the divine order. The eschatological battle is thus framed as a cosmic confrontation, not merely a geopolitical one.

The literary genre of Ezekiel 38–39 is *proto-apocalyptic*. John J. Collins (in *The Apocalyptic Imagination*, 2016) treats Ezekiel 38–39 as one of the precursors to the apocalyptic genre proper, alongside Isaiah 24–27 and Zechariah 9–14. The features that mark the genre are present: cosmic enemies, eschatological timing, divine intervention with cosmic disturbances (earthquake, hailstones, fire and sulfur in 38:19–22), a focus on the universal acknowledgment of YHWH (38:23). The genre is fully developed in Daniel 7–12 and inherited by Revelation. Block resists the genre label, preferring to describe the chapter as eschatological prophecy, but the line between eschatological prophecy and proto-apocalyptic is fuzzy at best.

The intertextual echoes are dense. The earthquake of 38:19–20 echoes the theophany at Sinai (Exod 19:18). The fire and sulfur of 38:22 echoes Sodom (Gen 19:24). The summons of every man's sword against his brother (38:21) echoes the defeat of the Midianites in Judges 7:22 and the Moabite-Ammonite invasion in 2 Chronicles 20:23. The divine warrior who fights for Israel without Israel having to fight is a deliberately archaic motif, drawing the eschatological future back into the language of the holy-war traditions.

The merchant peoples of 38:13 — Sheba, Dedan, the merchants of Tarshish — function as a literary chorus. They do not fight; they ask. Their question ("Have you come to seize spoil?") is rhetorically ambivalent — possibly admiring, possibly accusing — and serves to mark Gog's motive as plunder, the lowest possible motive for invasion in the prophetic moral vocabulary.

## Lens 3 — Larger Story

The reception history of Ezekiel 38 is one of the most contested in the entire Bible. The chapter has been read in radically different ways by every major interpretive tradition.

In rabbinic Judaism, the war of Gog and Magog is one of the standard markers of the messianic age. The Talmud (b. Sanhedrin 97a, b. Berakhot 13a) treats Gog-Magog as a future eschatological battle that will precede or accompany the coming of the Messiah, son of David. The Targum to Ezekiel renders the chapter in explicitly eschatological terms. The Jewish liturgy preserves prayers asking to be spared "the war of Gog and Magog" (cf. the *Aleinu* and certain *piyyutim*). Maimonides (*Hilkhot Melakhim* 12:2) cautions against speculative dating but treats the Gog war as a legitimate part of the messianic horizon. The Lurianic Kabbalah developed elaborate cosmologies around the Gog-Magog motif. The chapter has become foundational to Jewish messianic expectation, shaping responses to historical crises from the Crusades to the Holocaust to the establishment of the State of Israel.

In Christian patristic reception, the dominant reading is Augustinian. *City of God* 20.11 identifies Gog and Magog with all the nations gathered by the Antichrist at the end of history — drawing, of course, on Revelation 20:8, which reuses the names. Augustine resists any specific geographical identification: "These are not, as some suppose, certain barbarian nations, located in some remote part of the world." He reads them allegorically as "the nations of the world." Jerome's commentary on Ezekiel takes a similar line. The medieval Antichrist tradition (cf. Adso of Montier-en-Der, tenth century; the *Play of Antichrist*, twelfth century) inherits and develops this identification.

The *Russian-Gog* identification, which has dominated American Protestant popular culture for a century, is a relatively recent invention. The seventeenth-century English Puritan commentator John Mede first proposed that "Rosh" in 38:2 — translated "chief prince" in the ESV but read as a proper noun by some — referred to Russia. The interpretation was popularized in the United States by Cyrus Scofield's *Reference Bible* (1909) and reached saturation in Hal Lindsey's *The Late Great Planet Earth* (1970) — a book that sold over thirty-five million copies. Lindsey, Tim LaHaye (the *Left Behind* series), Charles Ryrie, John Walvoord, and J. Dwight Pentecost all built dispensational eschatologies around the identification of *Rosh-Meshech-Tubal* with Russia-Moscow-Tobolsk.

The *lexical* problem with this reading is severe. Hebrew lexicographers (BDB, HALOT) treat *rōš* in 38:2 as an adjective ("chief, head") modifying "prince," not as a proper noun. The Septuagint reads it as an adjective. The Vulgate reads it as an adjective. Daniel Block (NICOT) provides the most thorough technical refutation, arguing that the *Russia* reading rests on a phonetic similarity that is etymologically baseless: the Russian state takes its name from the Rus' (Scandinavian Vikings, ninth century AD), and there is no philological connection between "Rus'" and Hebrew *rōš*. Moscow and Tobolsk are similarly unrelated to *Meshech* and *Tubal*. The dispensational reading, in Block's judgment, is "linguistically untenable."

Despite its lexical failure, the dispensational reading has had massive cultural and political influence. American Christian Zionism, energy policy debates about Russia and Iran, Cold War apocalypticism, and modern responses to wars in the Middle East have all been shaped by readers who have been told that Ezekiel 38 predicts a specific contemporary geopolitical event. John Hagee's *Jerusalem Countdown* (2006) and Joel Rosenberg's *The Ezekiel Option* (2005) extended the tradition into the twenty-first century. The cultural influence of the reading on actual American foreign policy — through evangelical voters, congressional aides, and sympathetic policymakers — has been substantial.

Among critical and scholarly readings, three positions are represented. The *preterist* reading (e.g., Daniel Postell) identifies Gog with Gyges of Lydia, treats the prophecy as fulfilled in some Iron Age event, and reads the eschatological language as poetic-prophetic hyperbole. The *symbolic-apocalyptic* reading (Block, Greenberg) treats Gog as a literary figure for the cosmic enemy, the eschatological adversary who is *not* a specific historical individual but the type of all who oppose YHWH's people. The *historical-prophetic* reading (Walvoord, dispensationalist) treats Gog as a specific future ruler whose identification awaits historical fulfillment.

Catholic and Orthodox traditions have generally followed the Augustinian allegorical line, though with varying degrees of openness to literal-future readings. The Reformation traditions split — Calvin (had he reached the chapter) would likely have followed Augustine; Luther's anti-Turkish polemics tied the chapter to contemporary Ottoman aggression; later Protestant traditions diverged into preterist, historicist (linking Gog to successive empires), and futurist (dispensational) camps.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1: who is Gog?** The four major identifications cannot all be right. Augustine's allegorical reading sacrifices the chapter's specificity. The dispensational *Russia* reading rests on indefensible philology. The preterist *Gyges* reading evacuates the eschatological force of "in the latter years." The symbolic-apocalyptic reading risks treating the text as so general that it makes no concrete predictive claim. The strongest reading — the one defended by Block, Greenberg, and most critical scholarship — is that Gog is a *deliberately constructed apocalyptic-symbolic figure* that draws on real geographical elements (Anatolia, Persia, Cush, Put) to give the eschatological enemy a concrete shape, but that does not refer to a specific identifiable individual or modern nation. The chapter functions theologically as *apocalyptic typology*: it teaches the reader to recognize the pattern of cosmic opposition to God's restored people without committing the reader to a specific predictive identification. The dispensational reading must be steelmanned: the chapter's specificity (named peoples, named directions, named timing) does push toward concrete identification, and the impulse to ask "who?" is exegetically legitimate. The dispensational answer, however, fails on linguistic and historical grounds.

**Tension 2: the post-restoration enemy.** The deepest theological puzzle of chapter 38 is structural. Chapters 36–37 have just promised that YHWH will gather his people, give them a new heart, place his Spirit within them, set his sanctuary in their midst forever. Why, then, does chapter 38 introduce yet another enemy? The puzzle is the same as the puzzle of Revelation 20: why is Satan released after the millennium? Why is there a "not yet" inside the "already"? Three readings have been offered. The *dispensational* reading places the Gog war in a specific eschatological slot — between the rapture and the second coming, or between the millennium and the eternal state. The *amillennial-typological* reading treats the Gog war as a recurring pattern within history: every time God's people are restored, a Gog rises against them, and the cycle will be definitively closed only at the eschaton. The *Wright-ian* reading (cf. *Surprised by Hope*, 2008) treats the Gog war as the literary expression of the deep tension at the heart of biblical eschatology: God's victory is real and decisive, but the world has not yet caught up with the victory; the resurrection has begun in Christ, but the bodily transformation of the cosmos awaits the new creation. The post-restoration enemy is the literary form of this tension — a way of saying that the *not yet* is real, even after the *already* has begun.

**One unresolved question:** Does Ezekiel 38 require an *identifiable historical event* in the future for its claims to be true, or can the chapter's truth be *typological* — meaning that the pattern it sketches (cosmic enemy, divine victory, universal acknowledgment) repeatedly manifests in history without any single event being *the* event? The answer one gives shapes the entire framework of biblical eschatology and continues to divide the church.

## The Thread

The "hooks in your jaws" of 38:4 connect Ezekiel 38 backward to Ezekiel 29:4 (the same image used against Pharaoh) and forward to 2 Kings 19:28 / Isaiah 37:29 (the same image used against Sennacherib). The image creates a typological chain: every great empire that opposes YHWH's people — Egypt, Assyria, Gog — meets the same fate, captured and led away by hooks. The reader who knows the chain reads Gog as the eschatological summary of all such empires.

The "fish of the sea and the birds of the heavens and the beasts of the field and all creeping things" of 38:20 reverses the Genesis 1 creation order. In Genesis 1 these creatures are made; in Ezekiel 38 they tremble at the divine approach. The Gog event is a creation-shaking event — not merely a geopolitical event but a cosmic one. The chain runs forward to Romans 8:22 ("the whole creation has been groaning") and to 2 Peter 3:10 ("the heavens will pass away with a roar").

The "dwelling securely" of 38:8, 11, 14 is a deliberate inversion of the curse formulas of Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy 28. Israel under judgment dwells without security; Israel restored dwells without walls. The walls that no longer stand in 38:11 are not absent because Israel is unprotected but because Israel is protected by YHWH's direct presence. The image will recur in Zechariah 2:4–5 ("Jerusalem shall be inhabited as villages without walls… for I will be to her a wall of fire all around") and in Revelation 21:25 ("its gates will never be shut by day, and there will be no night there"). The walled city of the ancient Near East is replaced, eschatologically, by the city protected by God himself.

The "fire and sulfur" of 38:22 link Gog to Sodom (Gen 19:24) and forward to Revelation 14:10 and 20:9. The biblical archetype of divine judgment by fire — first used against the cities of the plain — becomes the standard figure for the eschatological judgment of the nations. Gog's defeat is described in language that the reader of Genesis already knows.
