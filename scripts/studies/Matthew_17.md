# Matthew 17: The Mountain of Light, the Coin in the Fish's Mouth

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens "after six days" — a deliberate narrative bridge from the Caesarea Philippi confession of chapter 16 — with Jesus taking Peter, James, and John "up a high mountain by themselves." The geographic question of which mountain has shaped two thousand years of Christian liturgical practice and pilgrimage and is, even now, unsettled.

The **Byzantine tradition**, fixed by the fourth century and consolidated by Cyril of Jerusalem and Jerome, identifies the site as **Mount Tabor** (Hebrew *Har Tavor*) in lower Galilee — a 575-meter freestanding dome rising sharply from the Jezreel valley. The Tabor identification has the strength of ancient pilgrim tradition and visual drama: Tabor is genuinely conical and isolated, a mountain that *looks* like a numinous mountain. The Franciscan Basilica of the Transfiguration at the summit, designed by Antonio Barluzzi and consecrated in 1924, sits on Crusader and Byzantine foundations. The Greek Orthodox monastery occupies the eastern crest. Both communities celebrate the **Feast of the Transfiguration on August 6**.

But Tabor sits roughly 75 miles south of Caesarea Philippi, in territory that Matthew's narrative has just left behind, and excavations and Josephus's *Vita* 188 indicate that Tabor was a fortified Hasmonean-Herodian site at the time of Jesus, occupied by Galilean defensive works that complicate the "alone" of the narrative. **Mount Hermon**, by contrast — the 2,814-meter snowcap that towers directly behind Banias / Caesarea Philippi — is the immediate geographical antecedent of the chapter, the dominant high mountain of the region, snow-capped through summer (which gives obvious force to "his clothes became white as light"), and the natural reading of "high mountain" in the strict sense. Most modern critical commentators — **France NICNT**, **Davies/Allison ICC**, **Hagner WBC**, **Keener NICNT** — favor Hermon or one of its lower spurs (perhaps Mt. Jebel esh-Sheikh's southern foothills) over Tabor. **Nolland NIGTC** is more cautious but acknowledges Hermon's textual fit. The Tabor identification appears to be a Byzantine-era pilgrim devotional choice, not a first-century geographical memory.

The mountain matters because of what mountains *do* in the Old Testament substrate. **Sinai** — Exodus 19, 24, 34 — is where Moses ascends, where YHWH descends in cloud, where Moses's face shines so brightly on his return that he must be veiled (Exod 34:29-35). **Horeb** is where Elijah hears YHWH in the still small voice (1 Kings 19). The two figures who appear with Jesus are precisely the two figures associated with mountain-theophany in the Hebrew scriptures: **Moses** (Torah, prophet *par excellence* of Deut 18:15) and **Elijah** (the eschatological returner of Mal 4:5-6, who never died). Matthew 17:2's report that Jesus's "**face shone like the sun**" — an addition Matthew makes to Mark 9:2-3, which mentions only the clothes — explicitly assimilates Jesus to Moses-on-Sinai while exceeding the Mosaic precedent. Moses *reflected* divine glory; Jesus emits it.

A few Greek terms anchor the chapter. **metemorphōthē** (μετεμορφώθη, "was transfigured") is the rare verb that gives the event its name; the LXX background is thin and the philosophical Greek background is rich (Ovid would use the cognate *metamorphosis* for shape-shifting). **doxa** (δόξα, "glory") is implicit but not explicit in Matthew's account — Luke 9:31 makes the *doxa* explicit. **skēnas** (σκηνάς, "tents" or "tabernacles") is what Peter offers to build, and the word is the Septuagint's term for the wilderness Tabernacle and for the Feast of Booths (*Sukkot*) — Peter is reaching for liturgical vocabulary, perhaps reading the moment as the eschatological *Sukkot*. **didrachmon** (δίδραχμον, "two-drachma piece") and **statēr** (στατήρ, "shekel," a four-drachma coin) are the technical numismatic terms of the temple-tax pericope.

The **temple tax** of 17:24-27 deserves its own historical setting. The half-shekel temple tax derives from **Exodus 30:13**, where Moses commands every male over twenty to pay a half-shekel as ransom for his life and as support for the sanctuary service. By the late Second Temple period, **m. Shekalim 1.1** specifies that the tax was due on the first of Adar each year, with collection booths set up throughout Palestine, with payment due by the fourteenth. The two-drachma sum corresponds to the half-shekel; the **statēr** Peter retrieves equals exactly two payments. Pharisees endorsed the tax universally; the **Sadducees** at the Temple actively encouraged it; the **Essenes** at Qumran (4Q159) appear to have read Exodus 30 as a one-time payment, not annual, and may have refused to pay. In 70 CE, after the destruction of the Temple, **Vespasian** redirected the tax to the temple of **Jupiter Capitolinus** in Rome — Josephus *Jewish War* 7.6.6 — institutionalizing the levy as the **fiscus Iudaicus**, a humiliating poll-tax on Jews throughout the empire that Domitian would later sharpen. Matthew is the only Gospel to record this pericope (it appears nowhere else in the NT), and the fact has fed a long-running debate about Matthew's date and audience: writing for a community living through or in the recent shadow of the *fiscus Iudaicus*, the question "does your teacher pay the tax?" carries fresh political weight. **Davies/Allison** treat the question carefully; **France NICNT** argues for genuine pre-70 dominical tradition; **Hagner WBC** notes the *fiscus* implication for Matthean audience.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Within Matthew's five-discourse architecture (Bacon 1918), chapter 17 sits in the narrative bridge between the Caesarea Philippi confession (chapter 16) and the **Community Discourse of chapter 18** — the **fourth of Matthew's five major discourses**. The narrative function of chapter 17 is christological-pedagogical: the Transfiguration vindicates Peter's confession with a divine voice; the failed exorcism exposes the disciples' faith deficit and prepares the Community Discourse's instruction in faith and forgiveness; the second passion prediction returns the Jerusalem-bound trajectory; the temple-tax scene establishes Jesus's freedom-with-condescension that will frame the *ekklēsia*'s relation to civil and religious authority.

Synoptic parallels are dense. **17:1-9** parallels Mark 9:2-10 and Luke 9:28-36, with Matthean additions ("his face shone like the sun"; the disciples falling on their faces; Jesus's reassuring touch — all liturgical-theophanic intensifications). **17:10-13** parallels Mark 9:11-13. **17:14-21** parallels Mark 9:14-29 (much longer in Mark, more compressed in Matthew) and Luke 9:37-43a. **17:22-23** parallels Mark 9:30-32 and Luke 9:43b-45. **17:24-27** is **uniquely Matthean** — it appears nowhere else in the NT.

Two textual variants matter. **Verse 21** ("**but this kind never comes out except by prayer and fasting**") is missing from the proto-Alexandrian witnesses (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus original hand) and bracketed or relegated to footnote in modern critical editions. Most scholars regard it as a harmonization with Mark 9:29, which itself is textually contested (Mark's reading is "by prayer," with "and fasting" added by some witnesses). **Bruce Metzger** in his *Textual Commentary* assesses both as later expansions reflecting the church's developing ascetic practice. The ESV reflects this critical judgment by omitting the verse from the main text, which is why the chapter you're reading skips from v. 20 to v. 22. **Bart Ehrman's** *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* is a useful general survey of how scribal practice in the patristic period sometimes served theological agendas.

A second variant of interest is in 17:20, where some witnesses read "unbelief" (*apistia*) for "little faith" (*oligopistia*) — a Matthean rather than Markan word; *oligopistia* is one of Matthew's favorite Christological-pedagogical terms (cf. 6:30, 8:26, 14:31, 16:8), and the textual evidence supports the *oligopistia* reading.

The narrative of the **failed exorcism** at the foot of the mountain has a long literary pedigree as a "descent from glory" type-scene. **Origen** noted the parallel with Moses descending from Sinai to find the people worshipping the golden calf (Exod 32). **Davies/Allison** make the same comparison: glory-on-the-mountain juxtaposed with faithlessness in the valley.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Transfiguration has had one of the deepest receptions in Christian theology, particularly in the **Eastern Orthodox** tradition. Beginning in the patristic period (**Gregory of Nyssa**'s mystical exegesis, **Maximus the Confessor**'s Christology) and culminating in the fourteenth-century **hesychast controversy**, the Transfiguration became the doctrinal anchor of a whole theology of divinization — *theōsis*. **Gregory Palamas**, defending the hesychast monks of Mount Athos against the criticisms of **Barlaam of Calabria**, argued that the light Peter, James, and John saw on the mountain was not a created phenomenon but the **uncreated energies** of God — a real participation in the divine life accessible to the deified human being. The councils of Constantinople of 1341, 1347, and 1351 affirmed Palamas's position. The Orthodox **Feast of the Transfiguration on August 6** is the liturgical center of this theology; the icon — Christ in a mandorla of geometric light, Moses and Elijah flanking, the three apostles flung backward — is the visual summary of the dogma. The patristic and Byzantine reception is summarized concisely in **John Meyendorff's** *St. Gregory Palamas and Orthodox Spirituality* and at greater length in **Andrew Louth's** work on the Greek Fathers.

The **Western** reception is somewhat thinner. **Augustine** treats the Transfiguration as anticipation of the resurrection-glorified body. **Aquinas** in *Summa Theologiae* III.45 follows Augustine and treats the Transfiguration as a teaching event for the apostles, anticipating Christ's risen state. The Reformation traditions, while not denying the Transfiguration, have been less inclined to develop its theology of light; **Calvin** in his *Harmony* treats it as confirmation of Christ's divine glory and a foretaste of the Resurrection, and is wary of any reading that would import an Eastern theology of created/uncreated energies.

The **Jewish-historical** trajectory is illuminating in a different register. **Geza Vermes** in *The Religion of Jesus the Jew* notes the Mosaic typology embedded in the scene — the six days, the cloud, the voice, the mountain — and reads it as a thoroughly Jewish prophetic-mountain theophany. **E.P. Sanders** locates the messianic-prophetic vision within first-century Jewish expectations of a Mosaic prophet (Deut 18:15-22, "a prophet like me"). **Daniel Boyarin** in *The Jewish Gospels* reads the divine voice ("This is my beloved Son... listen to him") as a binitarian moment that has Jewish precedent in Daniel 7's Son of Man theology. **Amy-Jill Levine** consistently warns Christian readers against reading the Transfiguration as superseding Moses and Elijah; the text has them present, talking with Jesus, not displaced by him. **The Cambridge Companion to Second-Temple Judaism** is a useful general background.

The **2 Peter 1:16-18** passage is the NT's only other Transfiguration testimony, and it bears its own weight: "**we did not follow cleverly devised myths when we made known to you the power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, but we were eyewitnesses of his majesty. For when he received honor and glory from God the Father, and the voice was borne to him by the Majestic Glory, 'This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased,' we ourselves heard this very voice borne from heaven, for we were with him on the holy mountain.**" Whether or not 2 Peter is by Peter himself (the consensus is pseudonymous, late first or early second century), this text indicates that the Transfiguration was central to early Petrine memory and apologetics — and **Bauckham's** work in *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* and his *2 Peter* commentary makes this case carefully.

The **temple-tax pericope** has had a more modest but theologically important reception. The early Fathers (Origen, Chrysostom, Jerome, Augustine) read the pericope as an instance of Christ's freedom from the law combined with his condescension to avoid scandal — a model for Christian engagement with civil and religious obligations. **Aquinas** in his *Lectura* uses the passage as part of his argument about the obligation of Christ and Christians toward civil authority. The passage was a touchstone in Reformation debates over **Christian freedom and adiaphora**: things indifferent in themselves but to be done or avoided to avoid scandal. **Calvin** in his *Harmony* and *Institutes* III.19 develops this systematically. The "fish with the coin in its mouth" — the only Matthean miracle — is most often read in the modern critical commentaries (**Davies/Allison**, **Luz Hermeneia**, **France NICNT**) as a parabolic-symbolic narrative rather than a strictly historical report; the action is told but not narrated as actually completed (Peter goes off-stage; we don't see him catch the fish). Whatever the genre, the theological point is that Christ pays — for himself and for Peter — out of God's provision, not out of obligation to the Temple system whose Lord he is.

The **mustard-seed faith** logion (17:20) is paralleled at Luke 17:6 and informs the larger Synoptic tradition on faith-as-power. The "mountain-moving" image is rabbinic-hyperbolic (b. Sanhedrin 24a, b. Berakhot 64a — "mountain-uprooter" as honorific for the great rabbis). **Davies/Allison** trace the parallels carefully.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: What is the light of the Transfiguration?**

This is the deepest Christological question of the chapter. At least three positions deserve to be steel-manned.

The **Orthodox / Palamite position** holds that the light Peter, James, and John saw was the **uncreated energy** of God — not a created phenomenon, not a metaphorical illumination, but a real and direct participation in the divine life made visible to the apostles by a divine adjustment of their perceptive faculties. The Transfiguration is therefore not a unique miracle but the paradigmatic vision of *theōsis* — the deified life made visible. Steel-manned, this reading insists that any "metaphorical" reading hollows out the texture of the scene, makes the apostles' terror unintelligible, and severs the Transfiguration from the patristic theology of divine grace as participation. Palamas's distinction between God's essence (unknowable, incommunicable) and energies (knowable, communicable) is the metaphysical scaffolding.

The **Western Catholic / Thomist position** is more cautious about the metaphysics. Aquinas treats the Transfiguration as a divinely caused supernatural phenomenon — real light, but a created light produced by divine action, anticipating the glory of the resurrection body and the Beatific Vision. The vision is graced, not natural to the apostles' faculties; the Beatific Vision is the proper end-state, not *theōsis* in the Eastern sense. Steel-manned, this reading worries that the Eastern essence/energies distinction risks compromising divine simplicity, and prefers a developed theology of created grace.

The **Reformed position** is more reserved still. Calvin treats the Transfiguration as a confirmatory miracle — a vision granted to strengthen apostolic faith in advance of the Passion — and is uninterested in developing a theology of participation in divine light. The Reformed worry about Marian apparitions, Eastern monastic visions, and contemplative excesses produces a hermeneutic that reads the Transfiguration *backward* into resurrection rather than *forward* into mystical theology. Steel-manned, this reading insists on the eschatological-resurrection horizon as the theological key.

**Where this leaves us:** the text supports a strong **glory-Christology** — Jesus's *being* shines through, and the disciples are appropriately undone by it. The text does not, on its own, settle the metaphysical question of how that light relates to created/uncreated, divine essence/energy, or the apostles' perceptive faculties. The exegesis underdetermines the metaphysics. The Eastern tradition has done more theological work with this passage than the Western, and that asymmetry alone is worth acknowledging.

**Tension Two: The disciples' faith failure.**

Down the mountain, the disciples cannot cast out the demon. Jesus's response is harsher than the parallel chapters suggest at first reading: "**O faithless and twisted generation, how long am I to be with you? How long am I to bear with you?**" The reply implicates the disciples themselves, not just the surrounding crowd. Then the diagnosis: "**because of your little faith.**" Then the apparently incompatible promise: "**if you have faith like a grain of mustard seed, you will say to this mountain, 'Move from here to there,' and it will move, and nothing will be impossible for you.**"

The **literal-charismatic reading** holds that Jesus means what he says: faith of a particular quality and kind, however small in measure, has unrestricted power. This is the reading of large portions of the Pentecostal-charismatic tradition and of much patristic exegesis (Origen, Chrysostom). Steel-manned, it asks: by what hermeneutical authority do we soften "nothing will be impossible for you" into a warm metaphor?

The **paradigmatic-spiritual reading** holds that "moving mountains" is rabbinic hyperbole for great learning or spiritual achievement, not literal geological displacement, and that the saying must be read as paradigmatic of the kingdom-power working through faith. Davies/Allison favor this reading. Steel-manned, it asks: if literal mountain-moving is what Jesus means, why is there no record in the apostolic Acts of any such literal mountain-displacement?

The **eschatological reading**, advanced in part by Wright and others, locates "this mountain" in the immediate Caesarea Philippi / Hermon context and reads the saying as a coded reference to Mount Zion, with mountain-moving as a coded image for the eschatological reordering of Israel that the Kingdom-of-God movement was bringing. Steel-manned, this reading asks: doesn't the immediate setting of the Caesarea Philippi confession, with all its political and topographic charge, demand a more historically situated reading?

**Where this leaves us:** the text is severe about faith's necessity and exalted about faith's power. Any reading that makes the failure innocuous or the promise abstract has lost the chapter's edge. **Bonhoeffer** is right that what Jesus calls "little faith" includes the faith of his closest disciples: the diagnosis is for the inside of the church, not the outside. The unresolved residue is what scale of mountain-moving the disciples should expect.

**The unresolved question:** the temple-tax pericope leaves a hard ecclesial-political question on the table. Jesus is sovereign over the Temple; the sons are free; he is not under obligation. Yet he pays — to avoid scandal, *hina mē skandalisōmen autous* (so that we might not cause them to stumble). What governs the church's posture toward authorities — religious or civil — that have no real claim on it? Pay to avoid scandal? Refuse to pay because the claim is illegitimate? The Anabaptists, Quakers, and later Christian-pacifist traditions read the tax-question one way; the Lutheran two-kingdoms tradition another; the Constantinian establishment tradition a third. The text gives a particular answer in a particular case, and leaves the principle to be worked out.

## The Thread

The chapter's cross-references reach in several canonical directions. **Exodus 24:15-18** and **Exodus 34:29-35** — Moses on Sinai, the cloud, the radiant face — are the explicit Mosaic substrate for the Transfiguration; Matthew adds "his face shone" to assimilate Jesus to Moses while exceeding him. **1 Kings 19:9-18** — Elijah at Horeb, the still small voice — is the Elijah substrate. **Daniel 7:9-14** — the Ancient of Days and the Son of Man — is the apocalyptic substrate of the divine voice. **Deuteronomy 18:15** — "a prophet like me from among your brothers" — stands behind "listen to him."

Look forward to the **Community Discourse of chapter 18**, where the binding-and-loosing of 16:19 is generalized to the assembly and the failed-faith diagnosis of 17:20 is folded into the church's life of mutual forgiveness; to the **Olivet Discourse of chapters 24-25**, where the eschatological themes of 17 are extended; to the **passion narrative of 26-27**, where the Transfiguration's glory is hidden under cross and shroud; and to the **resurrection of 28**, which is the Transfiguration vindicated. Flag forward to **2 Peter 1:16-18** — the NT's only other eyewitness testimony of the mountain — and to **2 Corinthians 3:7-18**, where Paul's veil-of-Moses theology turns the Transfiguration outward: "we all, with unveiled face, beholding the glory of the Lord, are being transformed (*metamorphoumetha* — the same Greek root) into the same image, from one degree of glory to another."
