# Psalm 7: The Trap That Closes on the Trapper

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Psalm 7's superscription is one of the strangest in the Psalter: *shiggaion le-david asher shar la-YHWH al-divrei-Kush ben-Yemini* (שִׁגָּיוֹן לְדָוִד אֲשֶׁר־שָׁר לַיהוָה עַל־דִּבְרֵי־כוּשׁ בֶּן־יְמִינִי), "A *shiggaion* of David, which he sang to the LORD concerning the words of Cush, a Benjaminite." Two of those terms remain unresolved.

*Shiggaion* (שִׁגָּיוֹן) appears only here and at Habakkuk 3:1 in the Hebrew Bible. The verbal root *shaga* means "to err, wander, reel" — and on that basis some have suggested "wild, irregular hymn," "dithyramb," or "song of lamentation." Akkadian *šegû* names a type of cultic lament, which is the parallel many critical scholars (Kraus *BKAT*, Hossfeld-Zenger) cite. John Goldingay (*Psalms*, BCOTWP) admits straightforwardly that the meaning is uncertain and that we should not pretend otherwise. The LXX renders it *psalmos*, simply "psalm" — abandoning the obscure term rather than rendering it.

"Concerning the words of Cush, a Benjaminite" presents the second mystery: there is no Cush the Benjaminite anywhere else in the Hebrew Bible, no narrative episode involving him, no clue to the occasion. Medieval rabbinic interpretation took two paths. Rashi and Radak (David Kimhi) identified Cush with Saul himself — Saul was a Benjaminite (1 Sam 9:1), and "Cush" (literally "Cushite," dark-skinned) was read as an ironic kenning for Saul's striking appearance, perhaps his fairness inverted. *Midrash Tehillim* preserves a related tradition. Modern critical scholars take the reference as genuinely lost — perhaps a piece of Davidic court tradition outside the canonical Samuel narrative, perhaps a later editorial gloss attaching the psalm to the David-Saul cycle.

Form-critically, Psalm 7 is what Hermann Gunkel (*Einleitung in die Psalmen*) classified as an *individual lament with strong imprecatory and judicial elements*. The petitioner is accused of wrongdoing (vv. 3-5) and asks YHWH to convene a court (vv. 6-9), to judge him by integrity, and to turn the violence of the wicked back upon the wicked (vv. 12-16). Mowinckel (*The Psalms in Israel's Worship*) located such psalms in a sanctuary procedure where the falsely accused entered the temple, swore an oath of innocence, and submitted to divine judgment — a kind of sacral trial-by-ordeal preserved structurally in 1 Kings 8:31-32 (Solomon's prayer at the temple's dedication: "if a man sins against his neighbor and is made to take an oath, and the oath comes before your altar in this house, then hear in heaven and act"). The conditional self-curse of vv. 3-5 ("if I have done this... let the enemy pursue my soul") is exactly the form such an oath would take.

ANE parallels are dense. The Mesopotamian *dingir.shà.dib.ba* prayers — laments to a personal god during illness or false accusation — share both the confessional structure and the *boomerang* image (the curse returns to its sender). Egyptian execration texts inscribed names of enemies on bowls that were then ritually shattered. Ugaritic Baal-cycle imagery of the divine warrior arising for combat (Baal vs. Yamm, Baal vs. Mot) underlies the call "Arise, O LORD, in your anger" (v. 6) — the *qumah YHWH* formula that also appears in Numbers 10:35 (the Ark moving) and across the Psalter.

Three Hebrew terms anchor the psalm. *Shophet tzaddik* (שׁוֹפֵט צַדִּיק, "righteous judge," v. 11) — a forensic title that frames the entire poem as a courtroom proceeding. *Bochen libbot u-khlayot* (בֹּחֵן לִבּוֹת וּכְלָיוֹת, "tester of hearts and kidneys," v. 9) uses the verb *bachan* — to assay metals — and the somatic pair *lev/kelayot* (heart/kidneys) which in Hebrew anthropology designates the seat of intention and conscience. *Bor* (בּוֹר, "pit," v. 15) is the hunter's trap that snares its own digger.

Textually, Psalm 7 is preserved only fragmentarily in the Qumran psalms manuscripts. The MT and LXX agree on the chapter division (LXX/MT divergence does not begin until Pss 9-10).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The psalm runs seventeen verses in five movements: refuge plea (vv. 1-2), oath of innocence with conditional self-curse (vv. 3-5), summons to divine judgment (vv. 6-9), confidence in God as judge (vv. 10-11), and the great image cluster of the wicked's self-defeat (vv. 12-16) before the closing vow of praise (v. 17).

Hebrew parallelism organizes the lines, and Psalm 7 makes especially heavy use of what Lowth called *synthetic parallelism* — where the second line completes or extends the first rather than mirroring it (Robert Alter, *The Art of Biblical Poetry*, prefers "the consequential second line"). Verse 14 is a textbook example: "Behold, the wicked man conceives evil / and is pregnant with mischief / and gives birth to lies" — three stages of a single gestational metaphor advancing through time. Adele Berlin's *Dynamics of Biblical Parallelism* notes how grammatical and lexical patterning interact; here a verb chain (*yechabbel*, *harah*, *yalad*) ties the lines into a sequence that has narrative force.

The most distinctive literary feature is the *retributive boomerang* of vv. 14-16. The wicked person digs a pit and falls into it; his violence comes down on his own skull. Jerome F. D. Creach and others have called this the *Tat-Folge-Schicksal* — the "deed-consequence-fate" pattern that Klaus Koch identified in his 1955 essay "Gibt es ein Vergeltungsdogma im Alten Testament?" ("Is there a retribution doctrine in the Old Testament?"). Koch argued that biblical "retribution" is not a juridical penalty externally applied but an internal logic by which evil deeds carry their own destruction. Psalm 7 is one of his prime exhibits: God does not so much punish the wicked as let the wicked's own structure collapse on him.

Three repeated terms structure the poem: *tzedek/tzaddik* (righteousness/righteous) sounds five times; *shaphat* (judge) and its derivatives sound seven times; *shuv* (return) appears at the boomerang of v. 16. The seven-fold occurrence of judging-vocabulary in a poem that asks for a judgment is a poetic intentionality, not coincidence.

Westermann's lament structure is largely visible: invocation, complaint, petition, motivation, vow of praise (v. 17). The *Stimmungsumschwung* (turn from lament to confidence) falls between verse 9 and verse 10, with verse 10 ("My shield is with God") functioning as the hinge. Begrich's hypothesis of an unspoken priestly oracle of assurance (*Heilsorakel*) fits the seam.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Psalm 7 sits in Book I (Pss 1-41) inside the early-Davidic lament cluster (Pss 3-7) that follows the wisdom-Torah preface of Psalms 1-2. Gerald Wilson (*The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter*) treated the cluster as the Psalter's introduction to the *suffering king* motif: David in trouble, asking God to judge.

The Jewish interpretive trajectory begins with *Midrash Tehillim*, which expands the Cush-as-Saul reading and treats the psalm as David's protest under unjust pursuit. Rashi reads the conditional self-curse of vv. 3-5 as referring to the episode in 1 Samuel 24, where David spared Saul in the cave and was nonetheless slandered as plotting against him. Ibn Ezra parses the difficult Hebrew of vv. 4-5 with grammatical precision but declines to resolve the historical reference. Radak develops the typological reading in which David stands for Israel under unjust nations. In contemporary Jewish thought, Avivah Zornberg (*The Murmuring Deep*) reads the psalm as the cry of the falsely accused conscience that nonetheless submits itself to examination. The psalm appears in the Sephardic liturgy of *Mussaf* on Purim (the festival of Esther) — the connection being divine reversal, the boomerang of Haman's gallows.

The Christian trajectory unfolds in heavyweight commentary. Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 7 reads Cush allegorically as the devil and the speaker as the church under persecution; the wicked who "conceives evil and gives birth to lies" is the prototype of all spiritual deception. Cassiodorus follows Augustine. Aquinas in his *Postilla super Psalmos* treats v. 9 ("you who test the minds and hearts") as a major proof-text for divine omniscience. Bernard of Clairvaux preached on the boomerang of v. 16 as a parable of self-destructive sin. Calvin (*Commentary on the Psalms*, 1557) takes the imprecation seriously as a theological problem and argues that David's curses are not personal vindictiveness but an appeal to God's just government — they would be illegitimate from a private individual but are legitimate as a king's plea for the kingdom's order. Luther treats the psalm under his theology of the *theologia crucis*: the suffering one who appeals to God's righteousness against false accusation is figural for Christ at trial.

Modern critical scholarship — Gunkel, Mowinckel, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate (WBC), deClaissé-Walford-Jacobson-Tanner — converges on the form-critical reading and largely brackets the Cush identification. Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*) places it in *disorientation* with strong protest energy. Patrick Miller (*They Cried to the Lord*) emphasizes that the psalm refuses to choose between protest and trust; the speaker holds both.

The single most important contemporary reframing is Erich Zenger's *A God of Vengeance? Understanding the Psalms of Divine Wrath* (*Ein Gott der Rache? Feindpsalmen verstehen*, 1994). Zenger reads the imprecatory psalms — and Psalm 7 is among the milder ones — as the cry of the powerless against actual oppressors. The curse, he argues, is the protest of the violated who have nowhere else to go; to take the prayer out of the powerless's mouth and call it un-Christian is itself a kind of violence. Beth Tanner and J. Clinton McCann have built on this reading: the imprecation belongs to the conversation between God and the suffering, not to the relations between humans.

The New Testament does not quote Psalm 7 directly, but the boomerang logic recurs at Galatians 6:7 ("whatever a man sows, that will he also reap") and Romans 12:19 ("Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord") — where Paul appropriates the psalm's deepest move: vengeance is not the believer's prerogative but God's, which means the believer can release it.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author's theological vision is that *justice is real and God will enforce it*. The psalm refuses two false comforts. It refuses the comfort of pretending the speaker has not been hurt — the foes are real, the lion-tearing is real, the false accusation is real. And it refuses the comfort of taking matters into the speaker's own hands — the sword is whetted, but it is God's sword (v. 12). What God is doing is convening a court (v. 6), examining hearts and kidneys (v. 9), and arming Himself for judgment. The speaker's role is to submit to that examination ("judge me, O LORD, according to my righteousness," v. 8) and to wait.

The first theological tension is the imprecation. The speaker asks God's anger to rise (v. 6), to whet a sword and bend a bow (v. 12), and to let the wicked's violence return on his own skull (v. 16). How should this register in a tradition that confesses "love your enemies" (Matt 5:44)?

- The classical Christian allegorical reading (Augustine, Aquinas) directs the curse against spiritual enemies — sin, the devil, the flesh — and away from human persons. This rescues the psalm for liturgy at the cost of evading its plain sense.
- The Reformed reading (Calvin) accepts the literal sense but locates the imprecation in the speaker's role as *covenantal complainant*: David is not a private actor but represents the kingdom under God's covenant, and his prayer for justice is a prayer for the covenant's vindication.
- The Lutheran reading (Bonhoeffer, *Psalms: The Prayer Book of the Bible*) reads imprecation Christologically: only Christ can pray these psalms in their fullness, because only Christ can bear the wrath in his own body; the church prays them in him.
- The protest reading (Erich Zenger, Beth Tanner, Brueggemann's late work) restores the psalm to the powerless: imprecation is the language of those who cannot defend themselves and have no recourse but God. To censor it is to silence the violated.
- The Jewish reading (Levenson, Sarna) treats imprecation as part of the covenantal back-and-forth — God is not protected from the violated's anger; the prayer is *to* God, asking him to act, and the speaker's posture is submission to whatever judgment God renders.
- The Eastern Orthodox tradition tends to read these psalms ascetically: the enemies are the *logismoi*, the intrusive thoughts of the spiritual life, and the curse is interior warfare.

The least-intuitive position to steelman is Zenger's. It says: a devotional tradition that prays Psalm 7 only after spiritualizing the curse has actually erased the prayer of the violated — and in doing so has made the church's liturgy unsafe for the abused, who need permission to say *what was done was wrong, and may God set it right*. The literal sense is not sub-Christian. It is the prayer Christianity has too often refused to let its wounded pray.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 7 is the prayer of the falsely accused who submits to God's examination and refuses to do violence with his own hand. Its imprecation is not blessed but it is heard, and any reading that mutes it has stopped praying with the historical Israel and started using Israel's words for safer purposes.

A second tension is the oath of innocence in vv. 3-5. The speaker stakes his life on his integrity ("if I have done this... let the enemy trample my life"). This is not the posture of the penitent (Psalm 6) or of the universal sinner (Psalm 51). What does it mean for a tradition that confesses "all have sinned" (Rom 3:23) to pray a psalm of righteousness-protested? Calvin and Bonhoeffer answer: only one person has prayed these words without qualification, and the church prays them in him. Zenger and the protest tradition answer: the falsely accused has the right to protest specific innocence even while remaining a sinner generally — the psalm is about *this* charge, not about all charges.

**Closing question:** When the speaker asks God to "test the hearts and kidneys" of the wicked (v. 9), is he certain the same examination, applied to himself, would clear him — and if he is, what theological cost does that certainty carry?

## The Thread

- 1 Kings 8:31-32 — Solomon's prayer at the temple dedication, prescribing exactly the oath-and-judgment ritual that Psalm 7 enacts.
- Numbers 10:35 — "Arise, O LORD, and let your enemies be scattered," the *qumah YHWH* formula echoed at v. 6.
- Romans 12:19 — Paul's appropriation of the boomerang logic: "Vengeance is mine, I will repay."
- Galatians 6:7 — "whatever a man sows, that he will also reap"; the *Tat-Folge-Schicksal* in Pauline dress.
