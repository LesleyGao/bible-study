# Nehemiah 4: Trowel and Sword, and the Prayer We Wish He Had Not Prayed

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 4 marks the moment when the project's external opposition shifts from rhetoric to military threat. The ridicule of 2:19 has matured into a coordinated conspiracy: Sanballat the Horonite (governor of Samaria, north), Tobiah the Ammonite (east), Geshem the Arab and the Qedarite confederation (south), with the Ashdodites (the Philistine coastal plain, west) added to the list at 4:7. Yehud is now ringed on all four cardinal points, and the chapter's sense of encirclement is exact. Williamson and Blenkinsopp both read this geopolitical specificity as authentic memoir; the author is naming the actual political map of his moment.

Note that the Hebrew chapter divisions differ from the English. In the Masoretic Text, what appears as Nehemiah 4:1–6 in English Bibles is the conclusion of chapter 3 (3:33–38), and the Masoretic chapter 4 begins at what English numbers 4:7. The result is that the rhetorical-ridicule scene (Sanballat's mockery) and the military-threat scene (the conspiracy) belong to two different chapters in Hebrew tradition. This division is preserved in many Jewish editions and reflects a literary judgment that the move from ridicule to conspiracy is structurally significant — the redactor of the Masoretic text wanted these scenes separated.

The composition of the opposing coalition tells us about Persian-period regional politics. Sanballat had Samaritan provincial troops (the *ḥêl Šomerōn*, "army of Samaria," 4:2). Tobiah commanded Ammonite trans-Jordanian forces. Geshem chiefed the Qedarite Arab confederation. The Ashdodites were the coastal city's military with Philistine continuity. All four were officially under Persian satrapal authority and could not legally have launched formal warfare — but the chapter's threat is *laʿăsôt-lô tôʿâ* (4:8), "to cause confusion": irregular harassment, sabotage, fast strikes. This is asymmetric warfare in fifth-century BC mode.

The chapter's military reorganization (4:13, 4:16–23) is one of the Hebrew Bible's most concrete pictures of fifth-century militia organization. Defenders are stationed in the wall's topographically vulnerable lowest points (4:13), deployed *lemišpĕḥōt* (לְמִשְׁפָּחוֹת, "by clans"), preserving the kinship-based military unit attested throughout the Hebrew Bible. Equipment: swords, spears, bows — the standard fifth-century BC set, with bows likely the composite Persian type. At 4:16 shields and *širyōnîm* (coats of mail) are added; mail was expensive and suggests trained soldiers among the civilian builders.

The famous "with one hand each" passage (4:17–18) deserves its own note. The Hebrew is *baʾaḥat yādô ʿōśâ ḇam-melāʾḵâ wĕʾaḥat maḥăzeqet haššālaḥ* — "with one his hand he was doing the work, and one was holding the weapon." Williamson's philological note on this verse is influential. He observes that the picture some translations give — each man simultaneously building with one hand while wielding a weapon with the other — is impossible (ancient masonry cannot be done one-handed) and not what the Hebrew actually says. The Hebrew is more naturally read distributively: among the burden-bearers (those carrying stones), each had one hand for the load and one hand for a weapon. The actual masons and trowel-workers, on the other hand, had their swords *ḥăgûr ʿal-motnāyw* (4:18) — *belted at the side* — while building. The iconic image of "the man with sword in one hand and trowel in the other" is a theological cartoon; the actual military arrangement Nehemiah describes is more sensible and more interesting. Throntveit, Blenkinsopp, and Janzen all follow Williamson on this point.

Persian-period archaeology has yielded a few specific bits of corroborating evidence for the period. Arrowheads of the Persian-era type (small, three-flanged, often Scythian-style) have been found at multiple Yehud sites; the bronze trumpet-mouthpieces from contemporary contexts give us a sense of what 4:18's *šôpār* signaling system looked like. The excavations at Lachish and at Mizpah have produced Persian-period administrative seal impressions that help locate the named officials. The *yhwd* and *yhd* stamp impressions on storage jars continue to attest the provincial fiscal apparatus, which would have funded — or at least underwritten — the militia work.

The duration described at 4:21 is significant. *Mēʿălôt haššaḥar ʿad ṣēʾt hak-kôḵābîm* — "from the break of dawn until the stars came out." This is essentially the working hours of the fifth-century laborer, sunrise to nightfall. With half the men on guard duty at any given time, the actual masonry workforce is halved, and yet the wall was completed in fifty-two days (6:15). The numbers imply intense, sustained work under siege conditions — consistent with the chapter's report that "neither I nor my brothers nor my servants nor the men of the guard who followed me, none of us took off our clothes" (4:23).

Key Hebrew terms: *ʿālah ʿărûḵâ* (the wall's "joined-together" state at half height, 4:6); *meleʾḵâ* (מְלָאכָה, "work, project"), used some sixteen times across chapters 3–6 as the Memoir's running term for the building project; *qāšar* (קָשַׁר, "to conspire," 4:8), the same root used of treasonable plots in Kings; *šālaḥ* (שֶׁלַח, "weapon, dart" — 4:17, 4:23 — a relatively rare weapon-noun); *šôpār* (שׁוֹפָר, "trumpet," the ram's horn used for military signaling and later for liturgical purposes); and *zākar* (זָכַר, "remember") at 4:14 — "Remember the Lord, who is great and awesome" — which deliberately echoes the prayer-vocabulary of chapter 1.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter's genre is military memoir with embedded prayer. The form has Akkadian and Egyptian parallels — royal campaign annals interleaved with petitions to deity — but the Hebrew Memoir's distinctive feature is the first-person voice that preserves both the military detail and the inward register simultaneously.

The chapter is structured in three movements: (1) Ridicule and the imprecatory prayer (4:1–6 English, 3:33–38 Hebrew), (2) Conspiracy and military reorganization (4:7–14), (3) Ongoing vigilance and the trowel-sword arrangement (4:15–23). The climactic moment is 4:14, the public address: "Do not be afraid of them. Remember the Lord, who is great and awesome, and fight for your brothers, your sons, your daughters, your wives, and your homes." The phrase *hag-gādôl wĕhan-nôrāʾ* ("the great and the awesome") deliberately echoes the prayer of 1:5 — Nehemiah is preaching his earlier prayer back to the people. Throntveit notes this *Stichwort* repetition as the literary signature of the Memoir's interior coherence: the courtier's private prayer becomes the governor's public exhortation.

The literary device most worth attention is the imprecatory prayer at 4:4–5. *Šĕmaʿ ʾĕlōhênû kî hāyînû ḇûzâ* — "Hear, O our God, for we are despised. Turn back their taunt on their own heads and give them up to be plundered in a land where they are captives. Do not cover their guilt, and let not their sin be blotted out from your sight." This is the curse-prayer in its starkest form, structurally identical to imprecatory psalms (Psalm 69, 109, 137). Williamson reads the prayer as an authentic memoir element, not a later editorial insertion: its raw quality fits the cupbearer's voice and would not have been smoothed out by later piety. Calvin's *Lectures on Nehemiah* spend extensive time defending the prayer; Augustine had earlier offered the standard patristic move (imprecations are prophetic announcements of judgment, not personal vengeance — *Enarrationes in Psalmos* 108); modern scholars including Erich Zenger (*A God of Vengeance? Understanding the Psalms of Divine Wrath*, 1996) have defended the imprecations as psychologically honest and theologically necessary. The chapter forces the reader into this difficulty.

The "we built the wall" sentence at 4:6 is structurally pivotal. The whole project was reported in the third person in chapter 3; here it is reclaimed in the first-person plural. *Wan-niḇneh ʾet-ha-ḥômâ wat-tiqāšer kāl-ha-ḥômâ ʿad-ḥeṣyāh wayhî lēb lāʿām laʿăśôt* — "And we built the wall, and all the wall was joined to its half, and the people had a heart to do." The Hebrew *lēb laʿām* ("a heart for the people") is one of the Memoir's most concise expressions of communal will.

The "Remember" exhortation at 4:14 introduces the chapter's deliberate inversion of the imprecation. The same verb (*zākar*) that was used in 4:5 to ask God to *not* forget the enemies' guilt is now used in 4:14 to ask the people to remember God. The chapter holds these two memorial gestures in tension: God is to remember the enemies' guilt; the people are to remember God's greatness. The doubled use of *zākar* is a literary signature.

The chapter sits between the work-register of 3 and the internal economic crisis of 5. Chapter 4 is the *external* threat; chapter 5 will be the *internal* threat. The memoir has structured itself in concentric circles of crisis: foreign mockery, foreign military threat, internal economic collapse, foreign assassination attempt (chapter 6). Each chapter raises the stakes one degree.

Textual variants: 4:23's last clause is corrupt in the Masoretic text. The Hebrew *ʾîš šilḥô ham-māyim* — literally "a man his weapon the water" — has long puzzled commentators. Some emend to *ʾîš yāmîn ʿal-yad* (each at his right hand), others to *ʾîš šilḥô ʿalâw* (each his weapon upon him). The LXX appears to have read a different Vorlage. The ESV's "each kept his weapon at his right hand" is a reasonable smoothing. The point is unaffected: the men slept armed.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The post-exilic restoration arc reaches a moment of crisis here. The wall has gone up to half height (4:6); the moment when fortifications are most vulnerable is precisely when they are partially complete — too high to ignore, too incomplete to defend. The chapter's military theology is therefore not abstract but situational: God acts in the *partial* moment, when the work is begun and not yet finished.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Sanhedrin 19b uses Nehemiah 4 as the paradigm of *šemîrâ*, communal vigilance — Nehemiah's organization of the watch is read as the prototype of urban communal-defense responsibilities. Bava Batra 7b takes 4:13–14 as evidence that fortification of cities is a religious duty incumbent upon all residents, with the corollary halakhic obligation to share the cost. The imprecatory prayer at 4:4–5 has occasioned less rabbinic discussion than one might expect — perhaps because the rabbinic tradition was already comfortable with imprecation in the Psalms and did not find Nehemiah's prayer particularly difficult. Rashi reads 4:4 contextually: Nehemiah's curse is appropriate to the enemies' specific offense and is bounded by the offense. Ibn Ezra notes that the prayer is structured as an appeal to divine justice, not personal revenge. Maimonides' *Hilkhot Melakhim* uses 4:14 ("fight for your brothers, your sons, your daughters, your wives, and your homes") as a paradigm of *milḥemet ḥôvâ* (defensive war as religious obligation). Sara Japhet has written on the chapter's careful preservation of the gendered language of household — Nehemiah's exhortation names sons *and* daughters, wives *and* homes, which Japhet reads as inclusive of the *bayit* (household) as the basic covenantal unit. Tamara Eskenazi treats the chapter's *we*-voice as the climax of the corporate protagonist's emergence: chapter 3 names the body, chapter 4 fights for it. Jacob Wright's *Rebuilding Identity* reads the chapter polemically: the military reorganization is also an identity reorganization, sealing the *qahal* off from the surrounding peoples in a way that has long-term cultural consequences.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic readings allegorize the wall-and-sword imagery as the spiritual life. Origen's homilies on Joshua treat the prayer/work pattern as paradigmatic of *praxis* and *theoria* together. Bede's *In Ezram et Neemiam* allegorizes trowel and sword as preaching and discipline, the dual labor of the pastor. Calvin's *Lectures on Nehemiah* are particularly engaged. Calvin defends the imprecatory prayer at length: it expresses *covenant zeal*, not personal vengeance — Nehemiah identifies opposition to rebuilding as opposition to God and asks God to vindicate his own honor. Calvin also reads the chapter's military theology as paradigmatic of the godly magistrate's sword-bearing office (Romans 13). Luther treats the chapter as exemplary of *zwei Reiche*: the same Christian who prays the Sermon on the Mount bears arms when his city is threatened. Wellhausen reads it as the post-exilic theocracy's slide into militarized communalism. Williamson articulates the philological details; Blenkinsopp gives sustained theological reflection. David Janzen reads the imprecation as the speech-act of a community without sovereignty asserting moral standing. Walter Brueggemann has written on imprecation as the necessary speech of the powerless. Erich Zenger's *A God of Vengeance?* defends the imprecations as theologically required: a God who never receives the cry of the oppressed is not the biblical God.

**Convergence and divergence.** Both traditions accept that the chapter raises a hard ethical question. They diverge on its answer. The dominant Christian reading from the Reformation forward has tried to neutralize the imprecation by reading it as covenant-zeal, prophetic-announcement, or psychological-honesty — all moves that protect the prayer from being read as personal vengeance. The Jewish tradition is more comfortable with the imprecation as a legitimate part of covenant life: the *qahal* may pray its enemies' overthrow because the *qahal*'s flourishing is bound to the covenant's vindication. Where they converge: both traditions read 4:14 as the chapter's centerpoint, and both traditions take the household-defense commission as the legitimate ground of the militia organization.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author and redactor believe that God acts through the partially built wall, the armed builders, and even the imprecatory prayer. The theology is incarnational in a specific Hebrew Bible sense: God is not pulled out of the rubble by miracle; God is present in the rubble being moved by named persons whose hands hold both stones and weapons.

**Tension 1: The imprecatory prayer (4:4–5) — what does the Christian reader do with it?** The prayer asks God *not* to cover the enemies' guilt, *not* to let their sin be blotted out, to give them up to plunder. This is not a soft prayer.

*Catholic*: the *Catechism* (CCC 2262) cites Jesus' "love your enemies" as the new law that fulfills and surpasses the old. Aquinas in *Summa* II-II q.83 a.8 distinguishes between cursing in the *modus orationis* (as a prayer of imprecation calling for divine justice) and cursing in the *modus iustitiae* (as a personal act of vengeance); the former is permissible, the latter is sin. The imprecation in 4:4–5, read as appeal to divine justice, falls within the permissible category. The *Liturgy of the Hours* in 1971 famously suppressed the cursing psalms (109 in particular) on pastoral grounds, a decision still debated. *Reformed*: Calvin defends the imprecation explicitly. The prayer expresses zeal for God's covenant honor, not personal animosity. The Westminster tradition reads imprecation as the speech of those who have no other recourse against tyranny — a reading developed by Puritan covenanters who themselves prayed imprecations against persecuting kings. *Lutheran*: Luther's lectures on the imprecatory psalms read them similarly as covenant-prayer, but with a Lutheran twist — the imprecation reveals the *Anfechtung*, the spiritual struggle, of the believer who genuinely wants the kingdom and therefore wants the kingdom's enemies undone. Bonhoeffer's *Prayerbook of the Bible* defends the cursing psalms as Christian prayer through the mediation of Christ's own outcry on the cross. *Wesleyan*: more uncomfortable with imprecation; Wesley's notes on Nehemiah 4 soften the prayer significantly, reading it as appeal for God's prevention rather than judgment. *Anabaptist*: the most uneasy. The Anabaptist tradition reads the New Testament's love-of-enemies as a sharp break from imprecatory practice; Yoder's *Politics of Jesus* would press hard against any reading that legitimates the chapter's prayer as Christian model. The question becomes whether the Old Testament imprecations are *abrogated* by Christ or *fulfilled* in his prayer for forgiveness. *Orthodox*: the Eastern liturgical tradition includes some imprecatory texts but emphasizes their eschatological reading — the curses are fulfilled in the cross and the final judgment, not in present revenge. *Jewish*: Rashi reads the prayer contextually as appropriate to the offense; Ibn Ezra notes the appeal to divine rather than human justice; modern Jewish thinkers like Levenson have defended the imprecations as theologically necessary precisely because the alternative — pretending the oppressed have no standing to cry for justice — is a kind of religious silencing of the powerless. *Critical*: Janzen and Brueggemann read the imprecation as the speech of the powerless asserting moral agency. The least intuitive position is the strong Anabaptist abrogation reading; steelmanning it: the New Testament's transformation of enemy-relation in the cross does seem to render *prayer for the enemies' destruction* into a complicated act, and the Christian who prays the imprecatory psalms must do so through Christ — that is, while remembering that Christ prayed *forgive them, for they know not what they do* over those who killed him. Where this leaves us: the chapter does not let us neatly absorb the imprecation. The reader is left with a prayer the church has alternately defended, suppressed, and re-recovered, and there is no settled answer.

**Tension 2: The trowel and the sword — does godly work require armed defense?** Nehemiah's organization deploys half the workers as guards; even the masons sleep armed. The chapter normalizes a paradigm of *defended* construction, and the question is whether this is contextual emergency or normative paradigm.

*Catholic*: the just-war tradition (Augustine's *City of God* 19, Aquinas' *Summa* II-II q.40, the Catholic social teaching of *Pacem in Terris* and *Gaudium et Spes*) defends defensive force for the protection of the innocent and the flourishing of the common good. Nehemiah 4 is read within this tradition as a paradigm of justified defense. The CCC (2308–2317) preserves the just-war framework while emphasizing the duty of peacemaking. *Reformed*: the godly magistrate bears the sword (Romans 13); the chapter is paradigmatic. Calvin and the Westminster tradition both treat defensive force for the protection of family and city as obligatory under the second commandment. *Lutheran*: the *zwei Reiche* doctrine separates the kingdom of the right hand (church) from the kingdom of the left (state) and assigns the sword to the latter; Christian participation in defensive force is permitted in the secular kingdom. *Wesleyan*: ambivalent; Wesley himself accepted just-war reasoning but later Holiness movements have leaned pacifist. *Anabaptist*: pacifism is the historic and current position; the Schleitheim Confession (1527) rejected the sword for the regenerate, and Yoder's *Politics of Jesus* makes the most articulate twentieth-century defense. The Anabaptist reads Nehemiah 4 as Old Testament covenant-of-the-flesh continuing in Israel, *not* binding on the New Testament church. *Orthodox*: a generally just-war position with a strong emphasis on the sacramental/penitential character of even defensive killing — soldiers historically required confession after battle. *Jewish*: *milḥemet ḥôvâ* (obligatory war) and *milḥemet rĕshût* (discretionary war) are halakhically distinct; defense of the household and the *qahal* is uncontroversially obligatory. *Critical*: Wright reads the chapter as a militarization of identity that has long-term consequences for Jewish self-understanding. The least intuitive position to a Christian who has internalized the sermon-on-the-mount tradition is the strong Reformed/Catholic just-war reading; steelmanning it: a faith that refuses defense of the innocent is a faith that has outsourced its own protection to non-believers, which is morally incoherent unless the pacifist accepts (as Yoder himself argued) the full martyrological consequence — and even then, what about the *sons and daughters* of 4:14? Where this leaves us: the chapter forecloses on cheap pacifism (the work cannot continue without the guard) and forecloses on cheap militarism (the prayer of 4:4–5 and the *we built the wall* of 4:6 hold the building, not the killing, as the work). Both moves are made.

**One unresolved question.** If Nehemiah's prayer in 4:4–5 was the speech of a community without sovereignty appealing to divine justice — and if the chapter's structure makes that prayer indispensable, not embarrassing — then what does it cost the post-Constantinian Christian church, which *has* had sovereignty for sixteen centuries, to keep praying it without noticing the ways it has used the prayer to baptize its own coercion?

## The Thread

**Psalm 137:7–9** — The Babylonian Captivity's most notorious imprecation ("happy is he who takes your little ones and dashes them against the rock") sits in the same imprecatory family as Nehemiah 4:4–5, and any reader who works through Nehemiah's prayer should know that the Hebrew Bible's tradition of imprecation is the speech of the conquered and the displaced, not of the conquerors. Read together, Psalm 137 and Nehemiah 4 prevent the simple division of Old Testament imprecation as primitive ethics.

**Matthew 5:43–48 / Romans 12:14–21** — Jesus' "love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" and Paul's "bless those who persecute you, do not curse them" are the New Testament texts that any Christian reading of Nehemiah 4 must hold in mind. The relation is not abrogation but tension; the Christian reader is asked to live in the question of how the imprecation is *fulfilled* in Christ rather than how it is *erased*.

**Ephesians 6:10–17** — Paul's spiritual armor — the breastplate of righteousness, the shield of faith, the helmet of salvation, the sword of the Spirit — is a deliberate retrieval of Nehemiah 4's trowel-and-sword image in spiritual register. Paul reads the post-exilic militia imagery and converts it for the church. When you reach Ephesians 6, remember Nehemiah 4: Paul has read it, and the armor is not invented but reapplied.

**Acts 4:23–31** — The early church's prayer in the face of persecution from the Sanhedrin combines petition for boldness with appeal to God against the conspiring rulers ("Why do the nations rage?"). The structural similarity to Nehemiah 4 is striking: an enemy threat, a prayer that names the threat without softening it, a continuation of work despite vulnerability. The Christian community's first crisis after Pentecost is met with a prayer that quotes Psalm 2 in much the same posture as Nehemiah 4 quotes the covenant.
