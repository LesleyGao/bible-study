# Romans 7: The Wretched I and the Conscience of the West

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

By the time Paul dictates Romans 7 to Tertius in Corinth in the winter of 56–57 CE, he has spent more than two decades arguing about the Torah. The chapter is the most intricate piece of Pauline writing on the Law in the entire corpus, and it is written into a Roman house-church mix where the question is not academic. Returning Jewish believers, displaced by Claudius's edict of 49 CE and now back in Rome after his death in 54, were re-entering communities that had matured under Gentile leadership during their absence. The negotiation over what the Torah means for a mixed church was happening at every shared meal.

Two pieces of social-historical context shape how the chapter would have landed. The first is Roman **marriage law**, which provides the analogy of 7:1–6. *Manus* marriage (in which the wife came under her husband's *patria potestas*) was already dying out by Paul's day, replaced by *sine manu* marriage in which the wife remained legally under her father. But the foundational principle stayed: the marital bond ended only with death, and a widow could remarry without scandal. Roman audiences would have recognized the analogy as a familiar legal commonplace. Jewish audiences would have read it through the Mishnaic principle (formalized later but already operative) that death dissolves obligation: *mōt geres* — death divorces. The analogy works in both legal frames.

The second is the dominant moral-psychological framework of Greco-Roman antiquity: the Stoic-Platonic struggle of reason against the passions. Stanley Stowers (*A Rereading of Romans*) and Troels Engberg-Pedersen (*Paul and the Stoics*) have argued that 7:14–25, with its agonized "I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate," draws directly on the topos of *akrasia* — moral incontinence — best known from **Euripides**' Medea ("I know what evils I am about to do, but anger is master of my plans," *Medea* 1078–80) and Ovid's Medea ("I see and approve the better course; I follow the worse," *Metamorphoses* 7.20–21, *video meliora proboque, deteriora sequor*). **Epictetus** (*Discourses* 2.26.1) makes the same observation as a Stoic: every wrongdoer does what he does not really wish. Paul's audience — Greek-speaking, philosophically literate, Roman — would have heard 7:14–25 as a recognizable reworking of the *akrasia* topos.

Three Greek terms repay attention. ***Sarx*** (σάρξ, "flesh") appears repeatedly through 7:5, 7:14, 7:18, 7:25. *Sarx* is one of Paul's most contested words. It does not mean the physical body (that is *sōma*, σῶμα) and it is not Greek dualism's matter-versus-spirit. It is closer to "fallen humanity" or "human existence apart from the Spirit" — though some passages (Galatians 5) extend it to the realm of evil desires. **Ernst Käsemann** read *sarx* as the cosmic Power-frame for the human being under Sin's reign. ***Egō*** (ἐγώ, "I") — the famous *I* of 7:7–25, used emphatically and repeatedly — is the chapter's central interpretive crux. Who is the "I"? ***Eph hō hēmarton*** echoes from chapter 5; here in 7:7–13 sin (*hamartia*, ἁμαρτία) is personified as an active agent that "seized an opportunity" (*aphormē*, ἀφορμή — military terminology for a base of operations) and "killed me." Sin is not a state in this chapter; it is a beachhead, an army, a deceiver.

Material culture point: Tertius wrote on papyrus, almost certainly using a reed pen with iron-gall ink. Codex P46 (c. 200 CE), our earliest substantial witness to Romans, preserves this chapter with only minor variants. The Vaticanus and Sinaiticus readings agree at the substantive level. The textual tradition is stable.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Romans 7 is structured in three movements:

**7:1–6** is the marriage analogy: the believer has died to the Law through the body of Christ and so is now free to belong to another. The legal logic is *qal vāḥōmer*-style. Paul stretches the analogy to breaking — strictly speaking, it is the *spouse's* death that frees the survivor, and Paul has the *believer* die through the body of Christ — but the rhetorical force lands: death severs obligation.

**7:7–13** is the **Israel-and-the-commandment narrative**, told in a first-person *prosōpopoiía* (impersonation, speech-in-character). The "I" speaks autobiographically, but it is the autobiography of someone — not necessarily Paul himself. The Genesis 3 echoes are deliberate and dense: "sin, seizing an opportunity through the commandment, *deceived* me and through it *killed* me" (7:11) reuses the Septuagint's vocabulary for the serpent in Eden (Genesis 3:13 LXX, *ho ophis ēpatēsen me*, "the serpent deceived me"). The "commandment that promised life" (7:10) is best read against Leviticus 18:5 and Deuteronomy 30:15–20, where Torah is the way of life. **Stanley Stowers** reads 7:7–13 as the speech of a *Gentile* facing Torah: the Greco-Roman moral self confronted with the *do not covet* commandment and discovering that the very prohibition activates desire. **N.T. Wright** reads 7:7–13 as the speech of *Israel*: Israel received Torah at Sinai (the commandment "came," 7:9), Sin took occasion through the commandment, and Israel recapitulated Adam's fall. Both readings are textually coherent; both have ancient predecessors. Origen read the "I" as Adam; Augustine in his early *Propositions on Romans* (c. 394) read it as the unregenerate human; mature Augustine (after 396) read it as the Christian.

**7:14–25** is the **wretched-self passage**, written in present tense throughout. The shift from past tense (7:7–13) to present tense (7:14–25) is itself an interpretive crux. The grammar drives a real argument: the past-tense narrative seems to describe a one-time fall (Adamic / Israelite), while the present-tense agon describes an ongoing experience. The chapter ends with the cry "Who will deliver me from this body of death?" and the grateful answer "Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!" before the final summary in 7:25b that re-states the divided condition.

The literary devices stack:

***Mē genoito*** appears at 7:7 and 7:13 — the chapter is built on the diatribal refusal of two false conclusions ("the Law is sin" and "what is good produced death in me"). Paul wants neither.

The ***repetition of *nomos**** ("law," νόμος) — the word appears 23 times in the chapter, and Paul uses it in at least four senses: the Mosaic Torah, the principle or rule, the law-of-the-mind, the law-of-sin-in-the-members. Cranfield's ICC carefully distinguishes them. The chapter's argument cannot be tracked without sorting them out.

The ***agonistic verbs of 7:14–25*** — *agnoō* (do not understand), *katergazomai* (carry out), *poiō* (do), *prassō* (practice), *thelō* (will), *misō* (hate), *antistrateuomai* (wage war against), *aichmalōtizō* (take captive) — pile up to create the sustained sense of internal warfare. The vocabulary is military and judicial.

***Prosōpopoiía*** — speech-in-character — was a standard rhetorical device taught in the *progymnasmata* (preliminary rhetorical exercises) of Theon, Hermogenes, and later Aphthonius. **Stanley Stowers** argues forcefully that 7:7–25 is *prosōpopoiía*: Paul speaks in the voice of someone other than himself, and the audience would have recognized this immediately. The first-century Roman audience trained on rhetorical convention would not have read 7:14–25 as Paul's autobiography.

Käsemann's commentary makes the rhetoric central: the chapter is not introspection but theological characterization. Jewett's Hermeneia rhetorical analysis classifies 7:7–25 as a *digressio* (digression) within the larger argument, defending the Torah from the implication that it is somehow evil.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The deep story Paul is telling here begins with Genesis 3 and the entry of Sin and Death into the world; runs through Sinai and the giving of Torah; and ends with the cosmic deliverance accomplished by Christ.

The **Jewish interpretive trajectory** read Genesis 3 and the giving of Torah very differently from the way Romans 7 reads them. In rabbinic Judaism, Torah is the *antidote* to the *yetzer hara*, not its occasion. The classic statement (*Sifre Deuteronomy* 45; *b. Kiddushin* 30b) has God say: "I created the *yetzer hara*, but I created Torah as its remedy." Torah is the gift, the path, the medicine. Paul's claim that the commandment *killed me* (7:11) would have struck a Jewish reader as inverting the relationship. **Daniel Boyarin** (*A Radical Jew*) reads Romans 7 as Paul's attempt to articulate, in a deeply Jewish vocabulary, the Christian conviction that something has changed: not that Torah is bad (Paul insists it is "holy and righteous and good," 7:12) but that without the Spirit, Torah cannot achieve its end.

The **Christian interpretive trajectory** runs through one of the great identity-shifts in Western theology. **Origen** (*Commentary on Romans*, mid-3rd c.) read the "I" of 7:14–25 as the unregenerate human or as Paul speaking in character — explicitly *not* as the Christian. The Greek-speaking Eastern fathers — **Chrysostom** (*Homilies on Romans* 12–13), **Theodore of Mopsuestia**, **Theodoret** — overwhelmingly followed Origen: the wretched "I" is the pre-Christian or unregenerate human; the Christian has been delivered. Chrysostom is emphatic: the chapter cannot describe the Christian, because that would contradict everything Paul says in chapter 6 about freedom from sin's dominion.

**Augustine** is the pivot. In his early **Propositions on Romans** (394) and **Ad Romanos** (394–95, unfinished), Augustine read the "I" as the unregenerate person under Law without grace — the standard patristic reading. But around 396, in **Ad Simplicianum**, Augustine changed his mind. He came to read 7:14–25 as the Christian's continuing battle. By the time of the Pelagian controversy (411–418), the mature Augustine made 7:14–25 the centerpiece of his anti-Pelagian anthropology: the regenerate Christian still struggles, still finds nothing good in the flesh, still cries "wretched man" — and only the eschatological resurrection delivers fully. Augustine's *Confessions* (397–400) is the autobiographical performance of this reading.

**Pelagius** (*Commentary on Romans*, c. 405–410) — whose commentary survives complete and is one of the great underappreciated documents of patristic exegesis — read 7:14–25 in the older Greek sense: the wretched "I" is the human under Law without grace, *not* the Christian. Pelagius's exegesis is sober and careful; his theology of grace is what the Western church judged inadequate. The exegetical question and the theological question must be distinguished. Augustine's mature reading gave the West a chapter for the *simul iustus et peccator*; Pelagius's reading kept the Eastern emphasis on the radical newness of regeneration.

**Medieval theology** synthesized via Aquinas (*Lectura super Romanos*; *Summa* I-II, q. 109): the regenerate Christian is delivered from the dominion of sin (chapter 6) but retains the *fomes peccati* (the kindling of sin), so 7:14–25 describes the ongoing struggle with concupiscence. This is the Augustinian reading domesticated.

The **Reformation** doubled down on Augustine. **Luther's *Lectures on Romans* (1515–16)** read 7:14–25 as the heart of the Christian's existential reality: *simul iustus et peccator* — at the same time righteous (in Christ) and a sinner (in self). Luther's existential reading is one of the most influential Christian readings of any text in history. **Calvin's *Commentary* (1540)** agrees: the "I" is the regenerate Paul, and 7:14–25 is the believer's continuing battle against indwelling sin. The Reformed tradition systematized this in the doctrine of *remaining corruption* (Westminster Confession 13.2).

**Wesley's *Notes on the New Testament* (1755)** broke with the Augustinian-Reformed reading and recovered the Greek patristic position: 7:14–25 describes the *unregenerate* (or the Jewish believer under Law before Christ), and chapter 8 describes the Christian's actual condition. This reading is foundational for the Wesleyan-Holiness tradition's doctrine of entire sanctification — a real and attainable freedom from the divided condition of 7:14–25.

The transformative 20th-century intervention is **Krister Stendahl**'s 1963 essay "**The Apostle Paul and the Introspective Conscience of the West**" (republished in *Paul Among Jews and Gentiles*, 1976). Stendahl argued that the Augustinian-Lutheran reading of Paul, in which the apostle is the prototype of the introspective tortured conscience, is a *Western projection* on a text that belongs to a different mental world. Paul, Stendahl argues, did not have a guilty introspective conscience (Philippians 3:6 — "as to righteousness under the law, blameless"). The "I" of Romans 7 is not Paul's autobiography of inner torment; it is a rhetorical construction, perhaps the speech of someone else, perhaps a generic figure. Stendahl's essay reframed the entire question and made it impossible to read Augustine's reading as obvious.

The four modern interpretive schools all stake out positions:

The **Old Perspective** (Cranfield ICC, Moo NICNT, Schreiner) reads 7:14–25 as the regenerate Christian's continuing struggle with indwelling sin — the Augustinian-Lutheran reading. Cranfield's careful argument is that the present tense is not stylistic but real; that the *delight in the Law of God* of 7:22 is something only the regenerate possess; and that the cry of 7:24 followed by the gratitude of 7:25 places the speaker on this side of redemption.

The **New Perspective** (Sanders, Dunn WBC, **N.T. Wright**) reads 7:7–13 and 7:14–25 as **Israel under Torah** — the speech-in-character of Israel before Messiah. Wright (*Climax of the Covenant*) makes the case at length: Israel received Torah, Sin took occasion, Israel recapitulated Adam's failure, the Law became "captive" (7:23) under Sin's exploitation. The wretched cry of 7:24 is Israel's cry, and the deliverance is the Messiah. The chapter is an Israel-recapitulation reading of Adam's plight and the Law's tragic role.

The **Apocalyptic Paul** school (Käsemann, Martyn, **Beverly Gaventa**, **Douglas Campbell**) reads 7:14–25 as the description of the human under the *cosmic Power of Sin*. The "I" is not autobiographical but typological: the human-under-Sin's-dominion regardless of identity. The chapter's vocabulary — Sin "dwelling in me," Sin "waging war," "taking captive" — is the language of cosmic invasion. The deliverance is not moral progress but the apocalypse of grace announced in chapter 8. Käsemann's commentary is foundational here.

The **Paul Within Judaism** school (Mark Nanos, **Pamela Eisenbaum**, **Matthew Thiessen**, **Paula Fredriksen**, drawing on **Stowers**) reads 7:7–25 as the speech of a *Gentile* — possibly a Gentile God-fearer or proselyte — confronting Torah. Stowers's reading is the most developed: the *prosōpopoiía* is the voice of a Gentile attempting to live by Torah and discovering that without the eschatological pneuma promised to the Jewish people, the Gentile cannot. The chapter is Paul's argument that Gentiles need *Christ*, not Torah-observance, because Torah was not given for Gentiles in the first place. Thiessen (*A Jewish Paul*) develops this further: the wretched cry is the cry of the Gentile who has tried to live by Torah without being a Jew. Fredriksen and Eisenbaum extend the argument: Paul's mission is to bring Gentiles into the eschatological people of God *as Gentiles*, and Romans 7 is part of that argument.

Each reading has internal coherence; each reflects a different account of who Paul thought he was writing to and what he thought the Law was for. The chapter's ambiguity is a feature, not a bug — it is what allowed the chapter to function as the most-quoted passage in Christian anthropology for sixteen centuries.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions thread through this chapter, and on each there are at least three serious historical positions.

**Tension One: Who is the "I" of 7:14–25?**

*Position One: Paul as Christian / regenerate believer* (mature Augustine, Calvin, Luther, Reformed orthodoxy, Cranfield, Moo). Strength: 7:22 ("I delight in the Law of God in my inner being") sounds like the regenerate; 7:25 thanks God through Christ. Weakness: contradicts the freedom-from-sin claims of chapter 6 and the no-condemnation claim of chapter 8.

*Position Two: Paul as pre-Christian Jew / unregenerate human* (Origen, Chrysostom, Theodoret, Pelagius, Wesley, the Greek patristic mainstream). Strength: matches chapter 6 and chapter 8 without contradiction; matches the philological observation that the *delight in Torah* of 7:22 is an entirely Jewish trope (Psalm 1, Psalm 119); avoids the Augustinian projection of Western introspection. Weakness: must explain the present tense and the depth of the agonized first-person voice.

*Position Three: Adam / generic human under Sin* (Käsemann, Martyn, modern apocalyptic). Strength: matches the Genesis 3 echoes of 7:7–13; reads the chapter as cosmically anthropological rather than autobiographical. Weakness: must explain the specificity of the *delight in Torah* note, which is hard to fit into a generic-human reading.

*Position Four: Israel under Torah* (N.T. Wright, Dunn, New Perspective). Strength: integrates the chapter into a coherent Israel-recapitulation narrative; matches Wright's overall reading of Romans as the story of Israel's vocation; explains the Torah-specificity of the speech. Weakness: requires a strong corporate-personality reading of "I" that some find strained.

*Position Five: Generic Gentile in face of Torah* (Stowers, Thiessen, Fredriksen, Paul Within Judaism). Strength: takes seriously Paul's audience (Gentile believers in Rome); explains the *akrasia* topos as a Greco-Roman convention; integrates with the broader argument that Gentiles do not need Torah. Weakness: must explain how a Gentile without Torah came to *delight* in it (7:22) — possibly via the God-fearer category.

*Steelman the Stendahl-Stowers position*: The Augustinian-Lutheran reading rests on a translation of Paul into a mental world he did not inhabit. Paul does not show the introspective self-loathing the West reads back onto him (Philippians 3:6). The first-century rhetorical convention of *prosōpopoiía* makes the autobiographical reading philologically naive. When Augustine read his own conversion narrative back into Paul, he produced one of the most influential mistakes in Western theology. Stendahl's argument is that we should let Paul be Paul.

*Where this leaves us:* The chapter is genuinely under-determined. The strongest cumulative case is probably some combination of *Israel under Torah* (Wright) for 7:7–13 and *humanity under the dominion of Sin* (Käsemann) for 7:14–25 — that is, the chapter narrates what *Sin and Law combined* do to anyone, with Israel as the paradigm case. But this is contested ground, and one's conclusion shapes one's entire account of Christian sanctification. The Wesleyan tradition is right that if Romans 7 describes the Christian, the New Testament's promises of freedom from sin's dominion (chapter 6, chapter 8) are seriously diluted. The Reformed tradition is right that an honest believer's experience matches the chapter's description more closely than a triumphalist reading suggests. The Eastern patristic tradition is right that Paul's gospel announces something genuinely new and not just a more honest version of the old struggle. The Stendahl-Stowers school is right that the Western introspective reading is a projection. All four insights need to be held together.

**Tension Two: What is the role and effect of Torah?**

*Position One: Torah is good and necessary, but exposes sin* (mainstream Reformed, Cranfield, Moo). The Law shows us our sin, drives us to Christ, and serves as the rule of life for the redeemed. 7:12 ("the Law is holy") and 7:14 ("the Law is spiritual") are taken at full strength. Strength: respects Paul's affirmations of Torah. Weakness: must account for 7:5 ("the sinful passions, *aroused by the law*").

*Position Two: Torah is co-opted by Sin and becomes Sin's instrument* (Käsemann, Apocalyptic). Torah is good, but Sin (the cosmic Power) hijacks Torah to multiply transgression. The Law itself is not the problem; Sin's exploitation of it is. Strength: makes sense of Paul's both/and (Law is holy / Law produces death). Weakness: requires a robust reading of Sin as Power.

*Position Three: Torah is for Israel only; Gentiles do not need it; trying to put Gentiles under Torah is the problem* (Paul Within Judaism). Strength: integrates Paul's mission to Gentiles; explains why Paul's polemic is so sharp; matches Galatians. Weakness: must explain the universal-sounding language of Romans 7 ("I" rather than "you Gentiles").

*Position Four: Torah is fulfilled and superseded in Christ* (Lutheran, dispensationalist). Strength: matches the marriage analogy of 7:1–6. Weakness: too quickly displaces what Paul still affirms about Torah.

*Steelman the Apocalyptic position*: 7:5, 7:8, 7:11, 7:13 all say that Sin used the commandment, deceived through the commandment, killed through the commandment. The dynamic in the chapter is not Law-versus-grace but Sin-using-Law-against-itself. Käsemann's reading is exegetically tighter than the Lutheran "Law condemns; grace saves" frame, because it preserves Paul's insistence that the Law is good while explaining why it produces death.

*Where this leaves us:* The Law is not Sin (7:7), is holy (7:12), is spiritual (7:14), and is delighted in by the inner being (7:22). But the Law also "came in to increase the trespass" (5:20), and Sin "seized opportunity through the commandment" (7:8, 7:11). The cleanest reading is that Torah is good but vulnerable to Sin's hijacking; that Gentiles in particular cannot live by Torah without the Spirit; and that what Christ accomplishes is not the abolition of Torah's demand but the fulfillment of its goal through the Spirit (8:4). The chapter is not Paul against Torah; it is Paul against any human attempt to live by Torah without the eschatological gift of the Spirit.

**One unresolved question:** If 7:25b ("with my mind I serve the Law of God, but with my flesh the Law of sin") is the believer's continuing condition, what real difference does the Spirit of chapter 8 make? Is the divided condition lifted, transformed, or merely differently illuminated? The Wesleyan tradition reads chapter 8 as the genuine resolution — the Spirit really does liberate. The Reformed tradition reads chapter 8 as the *eschatological* horizon under which the divided condition continues. The Apocalyptic reading reads chapter 8 as the announcement of cosmic regime-change still working its way into bodies that groan. The text does not adjudicate. It moves to chapter 8 and lets the announcement do its work.

## The Thread

Romans 7 should be read alongside Galatians 2:19–20 ("through the Law I died to the Law"), Galatians 3:19–25 (the function of Torah as paidagōgos), and Philippians 3:4–9 (Paul's pre-Christian self-assessment). The chapter looks back to chapter 5 (Adam's act and the entry of Sin) and chapter 6 (death-to-sin and freedom from dominion); it looks forward to chapter 8 (no condemnation, life in the Spirit) as the deliverance announced in 7:25. The Genesis 3 echoes of 7:7–13 connect to the cosmic-Adam story of 5:12–21. The wretched cry of 7:24 is answered by 8:1 ("there is therefore now no condemnation"). The marriage-released-by-death analogy of 7:1–6 is fulfilled in the adoption-and-Spirit of 8:14–17. Flag forward: chapter 8 is the *answer* to chapter 7 — the deliverance, the Spirit, the no-condemnation, the no-separation. The decision one makes about who the "I" of chapter 7 is determines what chapter 8 finally announces.
