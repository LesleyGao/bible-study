# Ecclesiastes 6: The Stillborn, the Mouth, and the Limits of Knowledge

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 6 is short — only twelve verses — but it occupies a structural position in the book that Crenshaw (OTL) has called the *fulcrum of despair*: chapters 1–6 set out the problem (life is *hevel*, the gain of toil cannot be located), and chapters 7–12 pivot toward qualified responses (the wisdom of the "better than" clusters, the carpe-diem refrain returned to repeatedly, the closing meditation on aging and death). The chapter accordingly intensifies rather than resolves the earlier material; its rhetorical pressure is the highest in the book to this point.

The Persian-period setting (5th–4th c. BC) for which Seow, Krüger, Whybray, and Fox have argued is supported in chapter 6 by the economic vocabulary (*nekasim*, "possessions," 6:2 — Late Biblical Hebrew, attested in 1 Chronicles 27:31 and post-exilic texts) and by the social setting of the unenjoyed-rich-man scenario (6:1–6). The Persian-period Yehud landscape, with a small administrative elite drawing wealth from temple, taxation, and provincial trade while a large laboring population lived close to subsistence, frames the contrast that 6:1–6 develops. The phrase *tahat ha-shemesh* ("under the sun") in 6:1 and 6:12 brackets the chapter and locates the discussion firmly within the ANE wisdom-tradition framework of the realm of the living.

The Solomonic frame is by now backgrounded in the book's reception — the speaker is Qohelet (קֹהֶלֶת), the *assembler/convener*, a literary persona whose voice ranges across the book under the framing of the epilogist (12:9–14). The Solomonic claim of 1:1, while still rhetorically active, has receded behind the persona of the experiential observer who has *seen* (*rāʾîtî*, 6:1, the sixteenth occurrence in the book) what life under the sun produces.

Six Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. First, *raʿah ḥōlāh* (רָעָה חוֹלָה, "grievous evil," 6:2) — the same phrase used in 5:13 and 5:16; the rhetorical chain links the unenjoyed-rich-man of chapter 6 to the wealth-poem indictment of chapter 5. Second, *nepeš* (נֶפֶשׁ, "soul" / appetite, 6:2, 6:3, 6:7, 6:9) — the chapter's key term, used four times in twelve verses; the *nepeš* is the seat of life-desire, hunger, and longing, and Qohelet's claim is that the *nepeš* is never satisfied. Third, *nēpel* (נֵפֶל, "stillborn child," 6:3) — the term occurs only here and in Job 3:16 and Psalm 58:9; it carries the weight of an unfulfilled life that has nevertheless escaped the bitterness of an unfulfilled fulfillment. Fourth, *tāqîp̄* (תַּקִּיף, "stronger one," 6:10) — a rare Aramaic loanword meaning "powerful one" or "tyrant"; this is one of the chapter's two oblique designations of God, and the only term used. The Aramaism reinforces the Persian-period dating. Fifth, *yitron* (יִתְרוֹן, "advantage," 6:8 and 6:11) — the chapter's two appearances of the book's central economic question. And sixth, *ṣēl* (צֵל, "shadow," 6:12) — the human life as a passing shadow, an image with deep roots in the Psalms (Psalm 39:6, 102:11, 109:23, 144:4) and in the ANE wisdom tradition.

Two ANE parallels are central. First, the Egyptian *Dialogue of a Man with His Ba* (c. 2000 BC), which contains the line "Death is to me today like a sick man's recovery, like going outdoors after confinement" — a meditation on death-as-release that anticipates the stillborn comparison of 6:3–6. Second, Gilgamesh Tablet X, where Siduri tells Gilgamesh that humanity's lot is mortality and the gods have kept eternal life for themselves — a parallel to the closing meditation of 6:10–12 on the inability to dispute with the one stronger than the human.

The textual witnesses are stable. 4QQoh-a (mid-second century BC) preserves portions of chapters 5–7, including small fragments of chapter 6 that align with MT. LXX is close to MT throughout. Targum Qohelet expands the stillborn-comparison passage with explicit moralizing about resurrection and afterlife, attempting to soften Qohelet's claim that the stillborn is better off — a clear case of interpretive discomfort.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 6 falls into three units. The unenjoyed-rich-man scenario (6:1–6) develops the most provocative comparative in the book. The *all-toil-is-for-the-mouth* aphorism block (6:7–9) restates and tightens the chapter 5 wealth poem's themes. The closing philosophical meditation (6:10–12) crystallizes the chapter into explicit epistemological agnosticism.

The unenjoyed-rich-man (6:1–6) is constructed as a sustained *qal vaḥomer* — the rhetorical "how much more" argument familiar from rabbinic literature. The scenario escalates: a man receives wealth, possessions, and honor (the triad of the well-favored Persian-period elite, with verbal echoes of Esther 1:4 and 2 Chronicles 17:5); he lacks nothing of all that he desires; *yet* God does not give him power to enjoy them; *yet* a stranger consumes them. The scenario is then escalated again: the man fathers a hundred children, lives many years, has many days, *yet* his *nepeš* is not satisfied with the good things, *yet* he has no burial. To this Qohelet adds the comparative: a stillborn child is better off than he. And one final escalation: even if the man should live a thousand years twice over, *yet* enjoys no good — *do not all go to the one place?*

The escalations are deliberate. Each clause raises the stakes; each *yet* (Hebrew *vĕ*) functions as a counter-thrust. The rhetorical force is that no quantity of years, no quantity of children, no quantity of possessions can compensate for the absence of enjoyment. The stillborn becomes the foil: the child has no consciousness, no name, no remembrance — but neither has the unenjoyed-rich-man, in the end, given that all go to the *one place* (Sheol, the realm of the dead). The comparison is not romantic; it is brutal. It is also a deliberate inversion: in the standard ANE worldview, a long life, many descendants, and proper burial were the markers of divine blessing (Job 5:26, Genesis 25:8, Genesis 35:29); Qohelet is dismantling all three.

Crenshaw and Seow note that the rhetorical escalation in 6:3–6 echoes 4:2–3 ("the dead are better off than the living, and better than both is the one who has not been"). Chapter 6 intensifies that earlier statement by adding the specific case of the unenjoyed-rich-man. The book is climbing the ladder of despair through repeated returns to the comparative.

The aphorism block (6:7–9) is structured around three short observations. *All the toil of man is for his mouth, yet his appetite is not satisfied* (6:7) — the mouth and the *nepeš* mark the two registers of consumption: the mouth eats, the *nepeš* longs, and neither is filled. *What advantage has the wise man over the fool?* (6:8) — the question that the book has posed since 2:13–17 returns, now without consolation. *Better is the sight of the eyes than the wandering of the appetite* (6:9) — the proverb is sometimes called Qohelet's *bird-in-the-hand* maxim, but its weight is heavier: what is in front of you, what your eyes can see, is better than the *halok hannepeš*, the *wandering of the soul*, the unfulfilled longing for what is not yet possessed. Fox reads this as Qohelet's clearest formulation of the renunciation of acquisitive desire.

The philosophical meditation (6:10–12) is the chapter's climax. Verse 10 is one of the most contested verses in the book: *whatever has come to be has already been named, and it is known what man is, and that he is not able to dispute with one stronger than he*. The naming-language echoes Genesis 2:19–20 (Adam naming the animals) but with the agency reversed: man is not the namer but the named. The "one stronger than he" (*tāqîp̄ mimmenû*) is the chapter's first oblique reference to God, a Persian-period Aramaism whose force is *tyrant* or *overpowerer*. The image is of the human as the named and overpowered creature, unable to dispute. Verse 11 returns to the *more-words-more-hevel* refrain. Verse 12 closes with the chapter's most despairing question: *who knows what is good for man while he lives the few days of his vain life?* And the parallel: *who can tell man what will be after him under the sun?* The two questions are linked: not only does the human not know what is good in life, but the human does not know what will follow life. Crenshaw treats this as the structural midpoint of the book's despair (chs. 1–6 = problem; chs. 7–12 = qualified responses). The chapter ends with explicit philosophical agnosticism — the most direct in the book to this point.

As a literary unit, chapter 6 is one of the most compact and rhetorically tight in Ecclesiastes. The escalations of 6:1–6, the aphorism cluster of 6:7–9, and the philosophical close of 6:10–12 are bound together by the *nepeš* leitwort and the *one place* / *one stronger than he* imagery of finitude. The chapter does what it does economically and brutally.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical wisdom arc, chapter 6 sits at the apex of Qohelet's pessimistic curve. Proverbs has nowhere to put the unenjoyed-rich-man — the retributive logic does not have a category for the man who receives the marks of blessing without the capacity to enjoy them. Job has the friends, who would explain the unenjoyed wealth as divine punishment for hidden sin, and Job himself, who would protest the explanation. Qohelet does neither: he simply observes the case, declares it *hevel*, and moves on. The phenomenon is bracketed neither within retribution nor within protest; it stands as datum.

In Jewish interpretive trajectory, the stillborn-comparison passage (6:3–6) has been the chapter's most contested material. Qohelet Rabbah expands the passage at length, attempting to soften it through allegory: the stillborn is read as the unborn soul of the Torah-faithful, while the unenjoyed-rich-man is the faithless. Targum Qohelet inserts explicit afterlife material, arguing that the stillborn enjoys an eschatological reward that the unenjoyed-rich-man forfeits. Both readings are interpretively defensive. Among the medieval commentators, Rashi reads the stillborn-comparison as conditional: it is true only of the man who lacks faith; the faithful man, even if unenjoyed in life, has eternal compensation. Ibn Ezra is more philological and lets the comparison stand. Ibn Caspi (fourteenth century) reads the chapter as a deliberate shock-text designed to provoke philosophical reflection. Ralbag reads the chapter Aristotelianly: the unenjoyed-rich-man is the man whose contemplative faculty is blocked by acquisitive obsession. Sforno reads the closing question of 6:12 as Qohelet's invitation to revelation: human reason cannot know what is good, therefore divine revelation is required. Maimonides in *Guide* III.51 cites 6:12 (the question of what is good for man) as motivating his own apophatic theology — the human cannot know God's purposes, only the human's own ignorance. Modern Jewish reception, particularly after the Holocaust, has read the stillborn-comparison passage with renewed gravity. Emil Fackenheim's *To Mend the World* and Elie Wiesel's reflections on theodicy after Auschwitz draw the unborn-better-than-the-living motif into the post-Holocaust framework: the children who were not allowed to live are better off than the survivors who must live with the memory. The reception is searing and Qohelet's verses become, in this register, prophetic rather than philosophical.

In Christian interpretive trajectory, Gregory of Nyssa's *Homilies on Ecclesiastes* (c. 380) does not extend to chapter 6 in extant form, but his discussion of the stillborn motif in his *On Infants' Early Deaths* (a treatise written to console grieving parents) draws on Qohelet 6 to argue that the stillborn child is not lost but is brought into a different form of existence — the patristic instinct is to soften the comparison through afterlife theology. Jerome's commentary on Ecclesiastes treats the chapter as sustained meditation on the futility of acquisitive desire, with extended attention to the *nepeš*-language. Didymus the Blind reads the chapter allegorically (the unenjoyed-rich-man is the soul that has acquired knowledge but cannot be transformed by it). Origen treats the closing question of 6:12 as motivating the move from natural reason to divine wisdom.

Aquinas reads the chapter alongside his discussion of the *summum bonum*: the unenjoyed-rich-man has temporal goods without the rectitude of will to enjoy them, which Aquinas treats as evidence that no temporal good is the *summum bonum*; only God can be that. Bonaventure reads the chapter mystically: the soul is the unenjoyed-rich-man until it is reordered to God. Luther's *Notes on Ecclesiastes* reads the chapter strongly through gospel-precursor lenses: the unenjoyed-rich-man is the man under law without grace, who has the marks of righteousness without the joy of righteousness; the stillborn-comparison becomes a meditation on the futility of works-righteousness. Calvin's silence on Ecclesiastes leaves a Reformed gap that later Reformed commentators (Bullinger, Cocceius) attempted to fill.

In modern critical commentary, Crenshaw treats the chapter as the book's structural fulcrum — the lowest point of Qohelet's despair before the partial recovery of chapters 7–12. Murphy reads the chapter as Qohelet's clearest articulation of the limits of human happiness. Seow attends carefully to the philological difficulties of 6:10 and reads the *tāqîp̄* passage as one of the book's most direct theological statements: God is the overpowering one against whom the human cannot dispute. Fox reads the chapter as Qohelet's clearest articulation of *consciousness of injustice* — the unenjoyed-rich-man is the case of arbitrary fate, with no theological explanation available. Longman reads the chapter as Qohelet's most despairing voice, awaiting the epilogist's correction. Provan reads more positively, drawing the chapter into a contemporary theology of contentment.

Where the trajectories converge: virtually all interpreters across traditions agree that the chapter intensifies the book's pessimistic curve. Where they diverge: whether the unenjoyed-rich-man scenario is theologically explicable (Calvinist providence, Augustinian *restless hearts*, Maimonidean apophatic theology) or whether it must be left as datum (Fox's "consciousness of injustice," Tamez's structural critique). The stillborn-comparison passage is the pressure point at which traditions either domesticate or confront the text.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two genuine tensions deserve sustained attention.

The first is **the unenjoyed gift**. Qohelet's claim in 6:1–2 is striking: *God gives* wealth, possessions, and honor — but *God does not give* the power to enjoy them. Both the giving and the not-giving are attributed to God. The gift and the withholding come from the same hand. Three readings compete.

The Calvinist reading (developed by Charles Hodge, Robert Lewis Dabney, and the broader Princeton tradition) treats the unenjoyed gift as evidence of providential discrimination: God grants temporal goods to many, but the spiritual capacity to enjoy them only to some. The asymmetry tracks the doctrine of common grace versus saving grace. The unenjoyed-rich-man is a vivid case of common grace without saving grace — temporal blessing without the regenerated heart. Hodge in his *Systematic Theology* (Vol. 2) cites Qohelet 6 as evidence that temporal goods are insufficient for human flourishing apart from regeneration.

The Augustinian reading (drawing on the *Confessions* I.1 — *fecisti nos ad te, et inquietum est cor nostrum donec requiescat in te*, "Thou hast made us for Thyself, and our heart is restless until it rests in Thee") treats the unenjoyed-rich-man as the existential case of the *restless heart*: the man has the goods but not the rest. The unenjoyed-rich-man is not the case of providential discrimination; he is the case of every human, who, lacking the proper ordering toward God, cannot enjoy any temporal good. Augustine reads 6:7 — "all the toil of man is for his mouth, yet his appetite is not satisfied" — as direct evidence of the *cor inquietum*. The scenario is universal, not particular.

Fox's reading is the third option: the unenjoyed gift is *arbitrary fate*. There is no theological explanation. Some men have wealth and enjoyment; some have wealth without enjoyment; some have neither. The distribution is, from the human standpoint, opaque — the *one stronger than he* (6:10) does not explain. Fox's reading takes Qohelet at his word: the case is *raʿah ḥōlāh*, a *grievous evil*, and the language of evil resists theological explanation.

Steelmanning the Augustinian reading: the universal scope of Qohelet's *nepeš*-claim in 6:7 — *all the toil of man is for his mouth, yet his appetite is not satisfied* — does seem to push toward universal application rather than particular discrimination. Every human's appetite is unsatisfied, not just the unenjoyed-rich-man's. The chapter, read with attention to the universality of the *nepeš*-claim, supports the Augustinian reading more straightforwardly than the Calvinist (which requires the discrimination of common from saving grace) or the Fox (which has to insist on arbitrary fate against the evidence of universality).

Where this leaves us: the Augustinian reading has the strongest textual claim within Qohelet's argument; the Calvinist reading requires additional theological argumentation to specify the discrimination; the Fox reading has to suppress the universality of the *nepeš*-claim. But all three readings agree that the unenjoyed-rich-man is not a problem the chapter solves.

The second tension is **the stillborn comparison** (6:3–6). The most disturbing comparative in the OT — better not to have lived than to live without enjoyment — generates strikingly different responses across traditions. The Catholic tradition has had to manage the verses through theology of unbaptized infants (the limbo question, *Limbus Infantium* in medieval scholasticism, recently softened in the 2007 International Theological Commission document *The Hope of Salvation for Infants Who Die Without Being Baptized*); the Catholic instinct is to insist that the stillborn child is not in worse case than the unenjoyed-rich-man because of God's salvific will toward infants. The Calvinist tradition, with its doctrine of double predestination, has had to manage the verses through the framework of decretal sovereignty: the stillborn is in better case than the unenjoyed-rich-man if and only if the stillborn is among the elect (an unanswerable question this side of the eschaton). The Jewish tradition has been variably uncomfortable: Maimonides reads the verses metaphorically; Targum Qohelet inserts afterlife material; modern Jewish reception (Fackenheim, Wiesel) has confronted the verses with the gravity of post-Auschwitz reflection. The post-Auschwitz Jewish reception of the unborn-better-than-the-living motif is particularly searing: Wiesel and Fackenheim have read the verses not as philosophical comparison but as descriptive of the survivor's condition — those who were not allowed to live were spared the bitterness of having to remember.

Steelmanning the Wiesel-Fackenheim reading: the chapter's escalation in 6:3–6 builds toward the question of *6:6: do not all go to the one place?* — and that question, in the post-Auschwitz framework, is read with terrible directness. If all go to the one place, then the stillborn and the unenjoyed-rich-man and the survivor and the murdered share the same end, and the only meaningful distinction is whether they have endured the bitterness of consciousness. The Jewish tradition's discomfort with the verses is itself a marker of how seriously the verses must be taken.

Where this leaves us: the chapter does not soften, does not allegorize, does not explain. The stillborn-comparison stands as one of the OT's most direct meditations on the conditional character of life's value. Theological readings that insist on resolution (Catholic infant-theology, Calvinist decretal sovereignty) require importing theological structure that the chapter itself does not provide; theological readings that confront the comparison without resolution (Wiesel, Fackenheim, Brueggemann) take the chapter at its word.

**Where this leaves us at the chapter's end**: the closing question of 6:12 — *who knows what is good for man while he lives the few days of his vain life?* — is the chapter's punch. Not "what is good for man" but "*who knows*?" The epistemic question precedes the substantive one. If no one knows what is good for man, then how can the unenjoyed-rich-man's case be evaluated, the stillborn-comparison adjudicated, the *one stronger than he* contested? Qohelet's answer is silence. The chapter's lingering question: *if even the wise man has no advantage over the fool with respect to the question of what is good, then what is wisdom for?*

## The Thread

Cross-references for the thread, non-obvious. The stillborn motif (6:3) tracks against Job 3:11–19 — Job's curse of his birth, where he longs to have been a *nēpel* — but Qohelet generalizes Job's particular cry into a universal comparative. The naming-language of 6:10 (*whatever has come to be has already been named*) inverts Genesis 2:19–20 (Adam as the namer) and anticipates Isaiah 45:9–10 (the clay against the potter, the human against the *one stronger*) — the canonical thread runs from human-as-namer (Genesis) through human-as-named (Qohelet) to human-as-clay (Isaiah). The closing question of 6:12 — *who can tell man what will be after him?* — picks up directly in Sirach 18:6 ("when a man has finished, then he is just beginning") and Wisdom of Solomon 3:1–9 ("the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God"); both are pious corrections of Qohelet's agnosticism, and both date from the inter-testamental period when the apocalyptic imagination of resurrection was developing. James 4:14 — "your life is a *vapor* (*atmis*) that appears for a little while and then vanishes" — echoes the *ṣēl* (shadow) of 6:12, applied not to the rich man but to the planning merchant. The forward thread from Qohelet 6 through the wisdom inter-testamental literature to James is the canonical movement from agnostic *hevel* through pious correction toward apocalyptic resolution.
