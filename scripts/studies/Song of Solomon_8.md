# Song of Solomon 8 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Song of Solomon 8 closes the *Shir HaShirim* (שִׁיר הַשִּׁירִים, "Song of Songs" — a Hebrew superlative construction parallel to *qodesh qodashim*, "Holy of Holies") with one of the most quoted passages in all of Hebrew literature: *azzah kha-mavet ahavah qashah kish'ol qin'ah* (עַזָּה כַמָּוֶת אַהֲבָה קָשָׁה כִשְׁאוֹל קִנְאָה, "love is strong as death, jealousy fierce as Sheol," 8:6). The book's superscription, *asher li-Shlomoh* (אֲשֶׁר לִשְׁלֹמֹה, 1:1), traditionally attributes authorship to Solomon. Modern critical scholarship — Marvin Pope (*Anchor Bible*), Roland Murphy (*Hermeneia*), Othmar Keel (*Continental Commentary*), Cheryl Exum (*Old Testament Library*), Tremper Longman (*NICOT*), Michael Fox (*The Song of Songs and the Ancient Egyptian Love Songs*), Robert Alter — generally dates the final form to the post-exilic Persian or Hellenistic period (fifth to third century BC) on the basis of Persian loanwords (*pardes*, פַּרְדֵּס), Greek-period syntactic features, and northern dialect markers in some sections. Older pre-exilic poetic kernels likely underlie the redacted whole.

Chapter 8 contains *the* contested word in the entire book: *shalhevetyah* (שַׁלְהֶבֶתְיָה, 8:6) — translated in the ESV as *the very flame of the LORD*. The word appears nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible. Two questions hang on it: how to parse it morphologically, and whether it constitutes the Song's *only* mention of the divine name. The standard Hebrew reading takes *shalhevet* (שַׁלְהֶבֶת, "flame") + *-yah* (יָה) as either an intensifying suffix ("a great flame," "a mighty flame") or as a theophoric ending — the short form of YHWH (יהוה) attached to the noun, yielding *flame of Yah / Yahweh*. The Masoretic text writes it as one word; some Hebrew manuscripts and the Aleppo Codex separate *shalhevet-yah*. Marvin Pope, Robert Alter, and Cheryl Exum lean toward the divine reading: this is, almost, the only mention of God's name in the Song. Tremper Longman, more cautiously, allows it as superlative-intensifier with possible theophoric resonance. Either way, the text *flickers* between human and divine — and that flicker is itself canonically significant.

The seal-imagery — *simeni kha-ḥotam al-libekha kha-ḥotam al-zero'ekha* (שִׂימֵנִי כַחוֹתָם עַל־לִבֶּךָ כַּחוֹתָם עַל־זְרוֹעֶךָ, "set me as a seal upon your heart, as a seal upon your arm," 8:6) — has rich ANE background. Othmar Keel's *Continental Commentary* documented seal cylinders and stamp seals as marks of personal identity and ownership in Israel and Mesopotamia; the seal carried name, image, social position. Many Hebrews wore seals at the neck (over the heart) or on the right arm. The bride asking to be a *seal* on her beloved's heart and arm is asking for the most intimate possible identification — to *be* his identity-mark. The phylactery (*tefillin*) parallel — *bind these words... as a sign on your hand and as frontlets between your eyes* (Deut 6:8) — is unmistakable to any Jewish reader.

The phrase *Ba'al Hamon* (בַּעַל הָמוֹן, "lord of the multitude," 8:11) names Solomon's vineyard. Whether Ba'al Hamon is a real place or a poetic toponym ("the place of much fruit") is debated; some scholars identify it with Balamon in Judith 8:3. The thousand silver pieces (*elef kesef*, 8:11) reflects Solomon's extravagant wealth (1 Kings 10:14-29).

Major scholars whose work intersects this chapter: Marvin Pope, Roland Murphy, Robert Alter, Cheryl Exum, Tremper Longman, Othmar Keel, Michael Fox, Phyllis Trible, Renita Weems, Ellen Davis, Ariel and Chana Bloch, Marcia Falk.

## Lens 2: The Literature

The chapter is a compressed anthology of voices and scenes that gather the book's threads. The opening (8:1-4) is the woman's voice, expressing the wish that her beloved were like a *brother* (*aḥ*, אָח) so she could kiss him publicly without social shame — a culturally specific complaint about the constraint that her erotic love must be hidden from public view. The line *mi yittenkha ke-aḥ li yoneq shedei immi* (מִי יִתֶּנְךָ כְּאָח לִי יוֹנֵק שְׁדֵי אִמִּי, "oh that you were like a brother to me, who nursed at my mother's breasts") uses the *mi yitten* idiom of impossible wish, common in Hebrew poetry (Job 14:13, Ps 55:6).

The third adjuration refrain — *al ta'iru ve-al te'oreru et-ha-ahavah ad she-tehpatz* (אַל תָּעִירוּ וְאַל תְּעוֹרְרוּ אֶת־הָאַהֲבָה עַד שֶׁתֶּחְפָּץ, "do not stir up or awaken love until it pleases," 8:4, also 2:7, 3:5) closes the threefold structuring device that runs through the book. Cheryl Exum has argued these refrains form the book's primary architectural skeleton; the third occurrence, in shortened form, signals literary closure.

The wilderness-question of 8:5 — *mi zot olah min ha-midbar mitrappeqet al dodah* (מִי זֹאת עֹלָה מִן הַמִּדְבָּר מִתְרַפֶּקֶת עַל דּוֹדָהּ, "who is this coming up from the wilderness, leaning on her beloved?") — echoes 3:6 (*who is this coming up from the wilderness like columns of smoke?*) and frames the woman's emergence as a return from the wild liminal space into settled love. The reference to the *apple tree* (*tappuaḥ*, תַּפּוּחַ) at 8:5 picks up the woman's earlier praise (2:3) and adds the mysterious birth-line: *shamah ḥibbelatkha immekha* (שָׁמָּה חִבְּלַתְךָ אִמֶּךָ, "there your mother was in labor with you"). The lover is rooted in the same place where his mother bore him — love grounded in origin.

The seal-passage (8:6-7) is the book's theological climax. The progression *azzah kha-mavet ahavah // qashah kish'ol qin'ah // reshafeha rishpei esh shalhevetyah // mayim rabbim lo yukhlu le-khabbot et-ha-ahavah // u-neharot lo yishtefuha* moves from death and Sheol (the personalized *netherworld*) to fire (the *reshef* — flames of pestilential or divine fire, cf. the Canaanite god Reshef) to *shalhevetyah* (the flame-of-Yah) to floodwaters (*mayim rabbim* — the chaos-waters of Genesis 1, the deluge, the cosmic threat). Robert Alter notes the chiastic structure: love faces death, jealousy faces Sheol, fire flashes against waters, and love prevails. The closing economic-counterfactual — *if a man offered for love all the wealth of his house, he would be utterly despised* (8:7b) — ranks love above transactional value.

The little-sister dialogue (8:8-10) is one of the book's most playful exchanges. Brothers debate how to protect their *aḥot qetannah* (אָחוֹת קְטַנָּה, "little sister") — *if she is a wall, we will build on her a battlement of silver; if she is a door, we will enclose her with boards of cedar* (8:9). The wall/door pair is debated: *wall* as chastity-image, *door* as accessibility-image. The woman replies, *ani ḥomah* (אֲנִי חוֹמָה, "I am a wall," 8:10), claiming her own status — and her breasts are *migdalot* (מִגְדָּלוֹת, "towers"). The architectural body-imagery from 7:1-9 is reclaimed by the woman herself.

The Solomon-vineyard scene (8:11-12) plays Solomon's massive commercial vineyard at Ba'al Hamon (yielding a thousand silver pieces) against the woman's *karmi sheli le-fanai* (כַּרְמִי שֶׁלִּי לְפָנָי, "my own vineyard is before me"). She declines to be a part of Solomon's portfolio; he can keep his thousand, the keepers can have their two hundred, but her vineyard is *hers*. Tremper Longman and Cheryl Exum both read this as the book's quiet anti-Solomonic move: the Song attributed to Solomon ends with the woman declining Solomon's market.

The closing two verses (8:13-14) restore the lover-beloved exchange. He: *let me hear your voice* (*hashmi'ini*, הַשְׁמִיעִינִי). She: *beraḥ dodi u-demeh-lekha li-tzvi o le-ofer ha-ayyalim al harei vesamim* (בְּרַח דּוֹדִי וּדְמֵה לְךָ לִצְבִי אוֹ לְעֹפֶר הָאַיָּלִים עַל הָרֵי בְשָׂמִים, "flee, my beloved, and be like a gazelle or young stag upon the mountains of spices"). The Song ends not on resolution but on motion — the beloved running, the woman calling him onward. Robert Alter, Cheryl Exum, and Ellen Davis all note this open-ended close as the book's deliberate refusal of narrative closure.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

The Song's canonical legitimacy was secured against late first-century opposition by Rabbi Akiva. Mishnah *Yadayim* 3:5: *kol ha-ketuvim qodesh ve-Shir HaShirim qodesh qodashim* — "all the writings are holy, but the Song of Songs is the Holy of Holies." Akiva also said: *if all the writings are holy, the Song of Songs is the Holy of Holies; the day on which the Song of Songs was given to Israel is worth more than all the rest of creation*. Akiva's defense rested on the allegorical reading — the Song as covenant-love between Yahweh and Israel — and chapter 8 was central to that defense. The *love is strong as death* passage (8:6-7) was read as the Sinai-bond that not even death (the Egyptian sea, the wilderness, exile) could quench.

The *Targum Shir HaShirim* (probably seventh-eighth century AD) read 8:6-7 as the love-bond between Israel and Yahweh established at Sinai, holding through exile. *Shir HaShirim Rabbah* allegorized verse by verse. Rashi (eleventh century) read *shalhevetyah* explicitly as the divine flame at Sinai. The fact that the Song was assigned to Passover among the *Megillot* — read in the synagogue during the festival of redemption from Egypt — pressed the *love stronger than death* phrase into immediate liturgical-historical service: the love that brought Israel through the Red Sea is the love stronger than death, and *mayim rabbim* (the *many waters* that cannot quench love, 8:7) echoes the same phrase at Exodus 15:10. Maimonides cited 8:6 as the highest model of the soul's love for God (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 10:3). The *Zohar* and Lurianic kabbalah read 8:6 as the *seal* of *Tiferet* upon *Shekhinah*, and *shalhevetyah* as the fire-of-Yah that unites the *sefirot*. Avivah Zornberg's modern literary readings, Marcia Falk's translation, Ariel and Chana Bloch's translation with commentary, and Robert Alter's translation in *The Hebrew Bible* (2018) all foreground 8:6-7 as the Song's theological climax, with *shalhevetyah* as the canonical text's almost-but-not-quite naming of God.

The Christian allegorical trajectory begins with Hippolytus and reaches systematic form in Origen's *Commentary on the Song of Songs*. Origen read 8:6 as *the soul sealed by Christ*. Gregory of Nyssa's *Homilies on the Song* used 8:6 to articulate the soul's everlasting fixity in love of God. Augustine used 8:6-7 in *City of God* and *Confessions* — *strong as death* applied to the martyrs whose love overcame their fear. Bernard of Clairvaux died at Sermon 86 on chapter 3, but the Cistercian and Victorine continuations carried the allegorical reading to the close: William of St. Thierry, Aelred of Rievaulx, Hugh of St. Victor. John of the Cross's *Spiritual Canticle* — a Castilian poem and commentary built directly on the Song — reaches its climax in the soul's *flame of love* (*llama de amor viva*), explicitly drawing on *shalhevetyah*. Teresa of Avila's *Interior Castle*, Mechthild of Magdeburg, Hadewijch of Antwerp all built their bridal mysticism on chapter 8.

The Reformers softened allegory. Luther preferred a political reading. Calvin avoided the Song. James Durham's Puritan commentary (1668) preserved the Christ-soul allegory. The nineteenth century brought modernist criticism (W. Robertson Smith, Ernest Renan) that read the book as secular love poetry; Franz Delitzsch's 1875 commentary preserved Christological reading.

The most influential modern Christian reading is Pope John Paul II's *Theology of the Body* (Wednesday catecheses, 1979-1984), which devoted multiple sessions to chapter 8 and articulated the *spousal meaning of the body*: human eros as iconic of trinitarian self-gift, with *love strong as death* as direct testimony to the resurrection-love of Christ for the Church. Christopher West has popularized this. Sarah Coakley's *God, Sexuality, and the Self* (2013) develops a sophisticated systematic theology in which 8:6-7 figures the Spirit's binding of the believer to the trinitarian life. Modern critical Christian scholars — Phyllis Trible, Ellen Davis, Tremper Longman, Cheryl Exum, Renita Weems — have variously recovered the literal-poetic sense while honoring the allegorical trajectory.

NT echoes are dense: Ephesians 5:22-33 (Christ and the Church as bridegroom and bride); Revelation 19:7-9 and 21:2 (the marriage supper of the Lamb); Romans 8:38-39 (*neither death nor life... shall separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord* — almost certainly echoing Song 8:6-7); Ephesians 5:25 (*Christ loved the Church and gave himself for her* — love stronger than death enacted); 1 Corinthians 13:7 (*love bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things*); 2 Corinthians 1:22 and Ephesians 1:13-14 (the Spirit as *seal* upon believers — direct echo of *simeni kha-ḥotam*). The seal of 8:6 has functioned across the Christian tradition as the baptismal seal, the sacramental seal, the Spirit's seal.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

Chapter 8 closes the book and forces a theological accounting. The principal tensions cluster around three nodes: the *love-as-strong-as-death* claim (8:6-7), the *shalhevetyah* divine-name flicker (8:6), and the *canonical purpose* of the Song's ending.

**Position one** — the dominant Jewish-Christian allegorical reading — treats 8:6-7 as the canonical climax that *requires* a theological referent. Human love does not, in fact, conquer death; only divine love does. The Song's closing claim is therefore intelligible only as covenant-love (Akiva's reading), Christ-Church love (Origen, Bernard, Pope JP II), or soul-mystical union (John of the Cross, Teresa of Avila). *Shalhevetyah* is the canonical text *almost* naming God — the implicit-becoming-explicit moment, where the Song's deliberately God-less surface admits its true subject. The seal-imagery, the death-defeating love, the unquenchable fire — these are theological language by necessity. The Romans 8:38-39 echo confirms the apostolic reception: Paul read 8:6 as Christ's love.

**Position two** — modern critical scholarship — treats 8:6-7 as a hyperbolic claim *about human love*. The Song asserts that erotic-covenantal love between two human beings is, at its best, of the same order of cosmic seriousness as death itself — and that no flood, no fire, no economic transaction can quench it. *Shalhevetyah* is most likely a superlative-intensifier ("a mighty flame"), not a divine reference. The Song's deliberately God-less surface is *the point*: the canon contains a book that celebrates human love without theological propaganda, and this is itself a theological achievement. Phyllis Trible, Ellen Davis, Cheryl Exum, Tremper Longman variously articulate this position.

**Position three** — the *both/and* reading — articulated by Pope John Paul II, Sarah Coakley, Ellen Davis (in different ways), and Avivah Zornberg in Jewish key — treats the literal-erotic and the theological-covenantal as not rivals but as the same grammar at different levels. Human covenant-love *participates* in the kind of love it figures — God's covenant-love is not separate from the marital covenant-love that images it; the two are sacramentally bound. *Shalhevetyah* hovers deliberately between the divine-fire and the human-fire because the Song is teaching that the categories are not finally separable. Romans 8:38-39 is not Paul reading allegory *into* the Song but Paul reading the Song *as it is*: human love at its highest *is* the language for divine love, because divine love is what creates and sustains the conditions for human love at all.

Steelman position one: only divine love is, in fact, strong as death. To make 8:6-7 mean human love is to falsify it; human lovers die, and their love dies with them. The text's canonical meaning *demands* a theological referent. The unbroken Jewish-Christian allegorical reception across two millennia is not embarrassment but discernment.

Steelman position two: the canon's decision to include unembarrassed human love poetry is the theological move. To read the Song *only* allegorically is to rob it of the work it is doing. The Song affirms that *eros* — between two human bodies, in mutual desire, with Genesis 3:16 reversed — is good, sacred, scriptural. To allegorize this away is to refuse the gift. Tremper Longman and Cheryl Exum convincingly show that the Song's literal sense is itself canonically rich.

Steelman position three: the binary is false. Marriage is sacramental. Eros is iconic. The Song's *shalhevetyah* hovers because the categories hover. Pope JP II and Ellen Davis are right that the Song's place in canon is the canon's affirmation that human bodies, in covenant, *are* the alphabet of divine love.

The *canonical purpose* question — what the Song's ending is for — receives different answers. Akiva: it is the Holy of Holies, sealing covenant. Bernard: it is the soul's marriage to Christ, completed in eternity. JP II: it is the spousal meaning of the body, anchoring all theology of marriage. Trible: it is Eden restored, the canon's correction of Genesis 3. Davis: it is the redemption of the body, included in the redemption story. Sarah Coakley: it is the Spirit's binding of the believer to the trinitarian life. None of these readings cancel the others; the chapter's dense, almost-overstuffed close (seal, fire, floods, vineyard, gazelle, mountains of spices) seems designed to support all of them at once.

The closing image — *flee, my beloved... like a gazelle... upon the mountains of spices* (8:14) — refuses narrative resolution. The book does not end with consummation, marriage ceremony, or stability; it ends with the lover running and the beloved calling him onward. This is itself theologically loaded. The Christian tradition has read this as the soul's eternal pursuit of God (Gregory of Nyssa's *epektasis*), the church's ongoing longing for Christ's return (*Maranatha*), the Spirit and the Bride saying *Come* (Rev 22:17). Jewish tradition has read it as Israel's ongoing covenant journey, the redemption not yet consummated. The literal reading sees lovers in motion, love still in process. The book closes open.

**Where this leaves us**: chapter 8 is the canonical decision to place at the end of an erotic love poem the words *love is strong as death*. Whatever interpretive trajectory we take, we are accountable to that decision. The canon includes a book that says *love wins against death*, and it places the saying not in a prophet's vision or a psalmist's prayer but in the mouth of a woman speaking to her beloved. *Shalhevetyah* hovers, and the canon lets it hover. The seal is requested but not yet imposed. The gazelle runs.

**Closing question**: if love is, in fact, strong as death — and the canon is willing to say so — what kind of life is the canon asking us to live? And what difference does it make that the saying is in the mouth of human lovers, not in the mouth of God?

## The Thread

The thread of chapter 8 — and of the entire Song — is the ungraspable, unquenchable, undisposable nature of love. The lovers ask for the seal (8:6) and never quite receive it; they request *love strong as death* and the book ends with the beloved fleeing toward mountains of spices (8:14). The Song closes by refusing closure. Human love at its most intense flickers toward divine love (*shalhevetyah*), and divine love at its most intense flickers toward the human bodies through which we have learned to imagine it. The canon ends this book by sealing the unseal-able and by sending the beloved away — and by doing so, teaches that love is what is most real, most enduring, and most refusing-to-be-completed in our experience. *Many waters cannot quench it.*
