# 2 Peter 2: The Synoptic Problem of the Catholic Epistles

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Any reading of 2 Peter 2 has to begin where the chapter itself begins: with the question of authorship and source. 2 Peter as a whole is the most contested NT book on authorship, and chapter 2 is the chapter where the contestation bites hardest. This is because nineteen verses of 2 Peter 2 reproduce, often nearly verbatim, material that also appears in the Letter of Jude — so closely that one of the two letters must be drawing on the other or both must be drawing on a common source. This parallel is the closest thing the Catholic Epistles have to the Synoptic Problem in the Gospels.

The state of the question has consolidated. The vast majority of modern scholars — Richard Bauckham (*WBC*, 1983), Jerome Neyrey, Bultmann, Peter Davids, Tom Schreiner, even traditionalists like Doug Moo — hold that 2 Peter borrows from Jude rather than the reverse. The reasons are textual and theological: Jude is shorter and more pungent, 2 Peter expands and softens; Jude cites by name the non-canonical works *1 Enoch* (Jude 14) and the *Assumption of Moses* (Jude 9), while 2 Peter scrubs both citations, retaining only the angelic-imprisonment imagery (2:4) without the Enochic source attribution; and the secondary direction generally moves from sharper to smoother, from controversial to less controversial. The minority view, represented historically by Charles Bigg's 1901 ICC commentary and a few traditionalist defenders, argued for Jude borrowing from 2 Peter — but this requires explaining why Jude would isolate the most provocative apocalyptic citations and add the non-canonical references, which runs against the grain of editorial habit. Tommy Wasserman's text-critical work and Peter Davids's commentary both confirm the dominant priority of Jude.

This direction-of-dependence question matters for dating. If 2 Peter borrows from Jude, then 2 Peter postdates Jude. Jude itself is variously dated — most often to the 60s or 70s, occasionally later. If 2 Peter borrows from a Jude already in circulation, then 2 Peter is plausibly later than the Petrine martyrdom under Nero (c. 64-67). On the pseudonymity reading championed by Bauckham, Käsemann, Vögtle, and Neyrey, 2 Peter is a *testament* — a Jewish-Christian farewell discourse in the genre of *Testament of Moses*, *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs*, and the closing speeches of Moses, Joshua, and David — written by an admirer in the Petrine tradition somewhere between the 80s and the 130s. The early church itself was hesitant: Origen knew of doubts (preserved in Eusebius, *Hist. Eccl.* 6.25.8), Eusebius classed 2 Peter among the *antilegomena* (*Hist. Eccl.* 3.25), and full canonical acceptance came only in the late fourth century with Athanasius's 39th Festal Letter (367) and the Council of Carthage (397).

Evangelical defenders of Petrine authenticity — Michael Green, Tom Schreiner, Doug Moo, behind them Charles Bigg — handle the Jude parallel by accepting Jude's priority but arguing that Peter (or his amanuensis, since 1 Peter 5:12 already concedes Silvanus's hand for that letter) borrowed from Jude in the late 60s. The vocabulary differences from 1 Peter follow from the different amanuensis. The testament genre fits Peter's known impending death (1:14). Schreiner's *NAC* commentary remains the most thorough recent defense of authenticity. A mediating position (Witherington) holds that the Petrine voice was authorized through pseudonymous testamentary form acceptable in Jewish-Christian milieux. The point to register, in any case, is that no responsible reading of chapter 2 can ignore that ~70% of its content is shared with Jude.

The chapter's typological catalog also drinks deep from Jewish exegetical tradition that goes well beyond the Hebrew Bible. The angel-imprisonment reference (2:4) — "if God did not spare angels when they sinned, but cast them into hell and committed them to chains of gloomy darkness to be kept until the judgment" — is a Watcher-tradition reference. The originating text, Genesis 6:1-4, is laconic: "the sons of God saw the daughters of man… and took as their wives any they chose." The expansion of this brief text into a full mythology of fallen angels who cohabited with women, taught humans forbidden arts (sorcery, weaponry, cosmetics), fathered the giant Nephilim, and were imprisoned awaiting judgment is the work of *1 Enoch* 6-16, a Second Temple Jewish text dated to roughly the third or second century BC. The verb in 2:4 is also striking: *Tartarōsas* (ταρταρώσας), "having cast into Tartarus" — the only NT use of the Hellenistic underworld term. *Tartaros* is classical Greek; it appears in Hesiod and Homer as the deep place beneath Hades where the Titans are bound. By the writer's day the term had been absorbed into Jewish-Christian apocalyptic via *1 Enoch*. The writer reaches for a Greek mythological term to describe an Enochic event drawn from a Hebrew Bible verse. The layering is itself the history of Jewish-Christian thought.

The Sodom example (2:6-8) similarly runs through a Jewish exegetical lens. Genesis 19 says nothing about Lot being "righteous" or "tormented in his righteous soul." That development is post-biblical Jewish: *Wisdom of Solomon* 10:6-9 and 19:17 portray Lot as a righteous man rescued by Wisdom; Philo defends Lot's character; the Jewish tradition gives 2 Peter the Lot it cites. Balaam (2:15-16) likewise rests not on bare Numbers 22-24 but on a rich exegetical development in Philo's *De Vita Mosis*, Pseudo-Philo's *Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum*, the rabbinic *Midrash Tanchuma Balak*, and beyond. Balaam in this stream is the archetype of the prophet who sells his gift for money, the false teacher *par excellence*. The talking donkey detail (2:16) preserves the Numbers narrative's most striking miracle and reads it as the moment a senseless creature outshines a faithless seer.

The animal imagery of 2:22 — "The dog returns to its own vomit, and the sow, after washing herself, returns to wallow in the mire" — combines a Septuagint citation (Prov 26:11) with a Greco-Roman proverb. Bauckham and others identify the sow proverb as a known Hellenistic saying (likely a version preserved in *Story of Ahiqar* and elsewhere). The combination is characteristic of the chapter: Hebrew Bible plus Jewish development plus Greco-Roman wisdom, all in the service of a sustained vituperatio against the false teachers. Other key terms reward attention: *pareisaxousin* (παρεισάξουσιν, 2:1, "to bring in alongside, sneak in") names the false teachers' method — they enter not by frontal assault but by stealth; *miasmou epithymia* (μιασμοῦ ἐπιθυμία, 2:10, "lust of defiling passion") is a defilement-and-desire compound that draws on Levitical purity vocabulary; *katara tekna* (κατάρας τέκνα, 2:14, "children of a curse") is a Semitism in Greek dress, the kind of construction that points to a writer at home in Septuagintal idiom.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter belongs to the Jewish-Christian *psogos* or vituperatio — a sustained denunciation in the Greco-Roman rhetorical tradition. The form is well attested; Philo, Plutarch, and Stoic moralists all wrote against opponents in catalogs of vice and animal comparison. Within the testament genre, the prediction of false teachers is a conventional feature (compare *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs*, where dying patriarchs warn their children of coming corruption). Chapter 2 occupies the central section of the testament where the speaker, having recalled his own ministry (chapter 1), warns of the crisis to come.

The structural skeleton tracks Jude 4-19 closely. After the announcement of the false teachers (2:1-3, paralleling Jude 4), the writer offers three OT typological exempla of judgment — angels, Flood, Sodom (2:4-9) — paralleling Jude's similar but differently ordered triad (Israel in the wilderness, angels, Sodom). The denunciation proper (2:10-22) catalogs the teachers' vices: arrogance, blasphemy of angels, sensuality, greed. The Balaam exemplum (2:15-16) parallels Jude 11. The closing animal proverbs (2:22) move beyond Jude with their own rhetorical punch.

The two most striking literary features are the *type-list* and the *invective catalog*. The type-list (2:4-9) is a single periodic sentence in Greek — a long protasis with three or four "if" clauses (*ei gar*, "for if") followed at last by an apodosis ("then the Lord knows how to rescue the godly"). The construction lets the writer pile up examples of ancient judgment without committing to the rescue conclusion until the end, building rhetorical pressure across verses. The invective catalog (2:10-22) is loose and accumulative, piling vivid metaphors: "waterless springs," "mists driven by a storm," "irrational animals, creatures of instinct," eyes "full of adultery," hearts "trained in greed." The compound "blots and blemishes" (*spiloi kai mōmoi*) at 2:13 is a striking inversion of cultic-purity language; the false teachers are themselves the defilement at the love feasts. Bauckham observes that the rhetoric here is not random but follows the conventions of the Hellenistic *psogos*.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The patristic reception of 2 Peter 2 was more straightforward than the rest of the letter, since the chapter served immediately useful pastoral purposes. Once the canonical question was settled, fathers like Cyril of Alexandria, Bede, and the medieval glossators read the chapter as a manual against heresy: the typological exempla (angels, Flood, Sodom, Balaam) became standard catechetical material on God's pattern of judgment, and the descriptive vocabulary supplied terms of art for ecclesiastical denunciations of false teachers across Christian history. Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* II-II) draws on the chapter in his treatment of heresy and apostasy. The Reformation polemics in both directions — Catholic against Protestant, Protestant against Catholic — found ready ammunition in 2:1-3 and 2:18-22. Calvin reads the chapter as a sober warning to the visible church about the persistence of corruption; Luther, despite his suspicion of 2 Peter overall, deploys the chapter against the Roman teachers he opposes.

The most theologically interesting patristic afterlife of chapter 2 is the angel-imprisonment tradition. The Watcher mythology that 2 Peter 2:4 presupposes — and that Jude 6 cites more explicitly — was widespread in early Christianity. Justin Martyr, Athenagoras, Tertullian, and Clement of Alexandria all engage *1 Enoch* and treat the fallen-angel tradition as authoritative background to the New Testament. The *Book of the Watchers* (1 Enoch 1-36) was widely read in the early church. With time, however, especially after Augustine, the Western tradition turned away from the Enochic mythology. Augustine in *City of God* 15.23 expresses skepticism about the Genesis 6 reading that produced the Watcher mythology, preferring the "sons of Seth" interpretation. The Enochic books fell out of the Western canon entirely. Only in Ethiopia did *1 Enoch* survive as canonical — and there it remains so today, in the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church.

The contemporary recovery of *1 Enoch* in scholarly biblical studies — through the Dead Sea Scrolls discoveries, through the work of George Nickelsburg (whose Hermeneia commentary is the standard), James VanderKam, John Collins, Annette Yoshiko Reed, and others — has reopened the question of how Christian readers should engage Enochic background. 2 Peter 2:4 and Jude 6 both presuppose it. Reading them without that background flattens the text.

The Reformation read 2:1's "denying the Master who bought them" (*ton agorasanta autous despotēn arnoumenoi*) as a critical battleground for the doctrine of atonement. The natural reading of "the Master who bought them" suggests that the false teachers were in some sense purchased by Christ — which on a strict particular-redemption Calvinism is awkward, since the elect cannot finally fall. Doug Moo and Tom Schreiner from the Reformed angle have argued that the language is phenomenological — these teachers profess faith and *appear* to be bought, but their apostasy reveals they were never truly redeemed. Wesley's *Notes Upon the New Testament* and the broader Arminian tradition read the verse straightforwardly as evidence of universal atonement and the real possibility of falling away. Catholic readings (with their universal-atonement-with-particular-application) sit closer to the Wesleyan reading. The verse continues to be cited on every side of the Calvinist-Arminian debate.

### Jewish Scholarly Engagement

The Jewish scholarly engagement with chapter 2 is concentrated on the typological exempla. *1 Enoch* 6-16 (Nickelsburg's Hermeneia commentary, VanderKam's *The Book of Enoch: A New Translation*) supplies the Watcher background. The Lot-as-righteous tradition is a *Hellenistic Jewish* development (*Wisdom of Solomon* 10:6, 19:17; Philo's *De Abrahamo*) absent from the Genesis 19 narrative itself. The Balaam-as-archetype tradition is a *rabbinic and Hellenistic Jewish* development (*Midrash Tanchuma Balak*; Pseudo-Philo's *Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum*; Philo's *De Vita Mosis*) that generates the figure of the prophet-for-hire 2 Peter cites. Daniel Boyarin's broader argument that early Christian interpretation participates in a shared Jewish exegetical tradition applies acutely here: 2 Peter 2 is unintelligible without the Second Temple Jewish exegetical world, and recognizing this complicates older Christian narratives of Christianity as cleanly separable from Judaism.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The theological work of chapter 2 is severe. God is the God of judgment — historically demonstrated in the imprisoning of angels, the flooding of the world, the burning of Sodom — and the same God will act against the false teachers. But woven through the typology is also rescue: Noah was preserved, Lot was rescued, and the Lord *knows how* to rescue the godly from trials (2:9). The chapter holds together the threat and the hope. The disturbing element, picked up by every commentator, is that the false teachers are described as people who have *known* the way of righteousness (2:21) and have escaped the defilements of the world *through the knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ* (2:20) — and have now turned back. Two tensions follow.

### Tension 1: The Watcher tradition and *1 Enoch* as background to canonical Scripture

The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo position is that *1 Enoch* is canonical Scripture and should be read alongside the New Testament; 2 Peter 2:4 is then citing canonical material, just as Jude 14-15 does explicitly. This is not a fringe position; the Ethiopian church has continuously held it for over a millennium and a half.

The patristic-recovery position, exemplified by Justin, Athenagoras, Tertullian, and Clement of Alexandria, treated *1 Enoch* as authoritative background even when not formally canonical. On this view, contemporary readers should at minimum read *1 Enoch* as a primary source for the imaginative world 2 Peter assumes. The work of VanderKam, Collins, and Reed makes this an increasingly mainstream scholarly stance.

The medieval-Western suppression position, following Augustine's skepticism in *City of God* 15.23, treats Genesis 6 as the "sons of Seth" rather than fallen angels and reduces the Watcher tradition to a discarded curiosity. This is the dominant Western Christian inheritance for most of the second millennium and remains the default in much of Reformed and evangelical exegesis.

The contemporary critical recovery — represented by Reed's *Fallen Angels and the History of Judaism and Christianity* — reopens the question and argues that the Christian rejection of *1 Enoch* was historically contingent and theologically costly: it severed early Christianity from a body of literature its New Testament writers presupposed.

The least intuitive position for a Western Protestant is the Ethiopian one. Steelmanned: the New Testament writers cite *1 Enoch* (Jude 14-15) and presuppose its mythology (2 Pet 2:4, Jude 6, possibly 1 Pet 3:19-20), and a canon that excludes the very text its New Testament cites is internally awkward. The Ethiopian church preserves the consistency that the Western tradition lost.

**Where this leaves us**: The contemporary reader, even one who does not adopt the Ethiopian canon, has to read *1 Enoch* alongside Jude and 2 Peter 2 to understand the text in front of her. The flattened reading — angels sinned, were imprisoned, full stop — strips the chapter of its imaginative register.

### Tension 2: "Denying the Master who bought them" — particular or universal redemption?

The strict Reformed (particular-redemption) position, defended by Doug Moo and Tom Schreiner from a Reformed angle, reads 2:1's "the Master who bought them" phenomenologically: the false teachers professed Christ, appeared to be redeemed, but their apostasy reveals they were never truly elect. The "buying" language is descriptive of their profession, not their actual standing. *Despotēs* in the verse is paired with "Master" rather than "Lord" precisely to emphasize the master-slave relation of false profession.

The Arminian/Wesleyan position, advanced in Wesley's *Notes Upon the New Testament*, reads the verse straightforwardly: Christ genuinely purchased these teachers, who have now apostatized. The verse stands as biblical evidence that those genuinely redeemed can fall, and against the strict particular-redemption claim. Roger Olson has been the most articulate contemporary Arminian voice on this verse.

The Catholic position, which holds universal atonement with particular application, sits closer to the Wesleyan reading: Christ died for all (CCC §605), and these teachers were genuinely included in the redemptive purchase, but the application of redemption requires perseverance, which they lacked.

The least intuitive position for an evangelical Calvinist is the Wesleyan: Steelmanned, the verse simply says what it says — the Master *bought* them — and forcing the language to mean "appeared to buy" is special pleading driven by prior commitments, not by the grammar. The grammar of *agorasanta autous*, an aorist active participle, is a real action of a real subject on a real object. Reformed readings of the verse have to do work that Arminian readings do not.

**Where this leaves us**: 2:1 is one of the verses where the natural sense of the Greek and the systematic commitments of a tradition pull against each other. Both sides read the verse, and both have to bend something to keep the verse and their system in alignment.

### Closing question

If 2 Peter 2 is borrowing extensively from Jude, and Jude is citing *1 Enoch* and the *Assumption of Moses* as authoritative, then the chain of authority in the chapter runs: Genesis 6 → 1 Enoch → Jude → 2 Peter. Where, on that chain, is the line between inspired Scripture and the literature it draws on? The chapter does not answer this; it only forces it.

## The Thread

- Jude 4-13 — the parallel passage; reading them side by side reveals which is the more compressed and which the expansion, and the careful reader will see how 2 Peter scrubs the explicit *1 Enoch* and *Assumption of Moses* citations Jude retains.
- 1 Enoch 6-16 — the Watcher tradition that supplies the angelic-imprisonment mythology of 2:4; without this background the verse hovers in mid-air.
- Genesis 6:1-4 — the originating four verses about "sons of God" and "daughters of man" that Second Temple Judaism expanded into the entire Watcher mythology.
- Numbers 22-24 — the Balaam narrative the chapter reuses, including the talking donkey of 2:16 and Balaam's hire-for-prophecy that 2:15 names.
