# Exodus 10: The Wind That Swallows the Land

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

By the time the narrative reaches Exodus 10, the Egyptian political order imagined by the text is already staggering. If one accepts the Ramesside dating defended by Kenneth Kitchen (*On the Reliability of the Old Testament*) and James Hoffmeier (*Israel in Egypt*; *Ancient Israel in Sinai*), the scene unfolds in the thirteenth century BC, under one of the later Nineteenth-Dynasty pharaohs — most often Rameses II or his son Merneptah. The Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BC), the earliest extrabiblical mention of "Israel" as a people in Canaan, constrains the exodus to a period earlier than that inscription; Bryant Wood and others still defend an earlier 15th-century date. What matters for Exodus 10 is the shape of Egyptian monarchy in this era: a centralized, divinized court, where the pharaoh is the son of Ra, the living Horus, and the guarantor of *maʿat* — cosmic order, justice, and agricultural abundance. The plague sequence is a systematic dismantling of that guarantee.

The two plagues in this chapter strike at the heart of Egyptian material life. The eighth plague, *ʾarbeh* (אַרְבֶּה), a swarm of locusts, devastates what remains of the grain, flax, and orchards after the hail of chapter 9. Anyone living in the eastern Mediterranean in the Late Bronze Age knew locust plagues as episodic catastrophes that could reduce a nome to famine for a season; Ugaritic and Mesopotamian omen texts treat them as signs of divine displeasure. The east wind (*rûaḥ qādîm*, רוּחַ קָדִים) in v. 13 and the west wind (*rûaḥ-yām*, literally "sea wind") in v. 19 match the known meteorology of the khamsin, the desert wind that blows out of the Arabian and Sahara deserts in spring and can carry insects, dust, and blistering heat across the Nile delta. The precise and reversible directionality is the kind of detail Hoffmeier uses to argue for genuine Egyptian memory in the tradition.

The ninth plague, *ḥōšek* (חֹשֶׁךְ), a three-day darkness "that may be felt" (v. 21), targets Ra (and by extension Amun-Ra of Thebes, the dominant state cult of the New Kingdom). Because Pharaoh is the earthly manifestation of Horus and the son of Ra, an eclipse of the solar principle for three days is theologically a public humiliation of the throne itself. Scholars since the nineteenth century have proposed several natural correlates: an extreme khamsin loading the atmosphere with aeolian dust, a volcanic ash fall from the Thera/Santorini eruption (dated variously between c. 1628 and c. 1500 BC, problematic for Ramesside timelines), or a simultaneous solar eclipse (Kitchen is skeptical of the Thera correlation; Hoffmeier prefers khamsin). The Egyptian calendar month of *Abib* (אָבִיב, "ears of grain" — the Canaanite name for what later Judaism calls Nisan) places the plague in March–April, precisely the khamsin season.

Material culture in the chapter is mostly agricultural. Hebrew and Egyptian houses alike would be flat-roofed mudbrick with small high windows; a dense locust invasion would fill even these through openings never sealed in a normal year (v. 6). Pharaoh's court in v. 7 is recognizably the pharaonic audience chamber, where *sarisim* and viziers ("Pharaoh's servants") whisper counsel. The word *môqēš* (מוֹקֵשׁ, "snare," v. 7) is a hunter's trap, a loanword and concept well known from Egyptian fowling iconography.

On authorship and sources, Exodus 10 is generally assigned by Richard Elliott Friedman (*The Bible with Sources Revealed*) principally to J (the Yahwist), with the opening vv. 1–2 reflecting a later redactional framework (often attributed to P or to the Deuteronomic redactor) because of its catechetical cast — "that you may tell in the hearing of your son and of your grandson." Brevard Childs (*The Book of Exodus: A Critical, Theological Commentary*) and William H. C. Propp (*Exodus 1–18*, Anchor Bible) both note that the plague cycle is remarkably unified despite its composite origin; the redactor has flattened the seams. The Samaritan Pentateuch adds an expansion in v. 2 harmonizing with earlier announcements, and LXX smooths some ambiguous idioms (the phrase "deal harshly" in v. 2 is rendered *empaiksa*, "I mocked," preserving a note of divine irony).

Archaeologically, the plagues find their most disputed parallel in the Ipuwer Papyrus (Leiden I 344), a Middle Kingdom lament that describes the Nile turned to blood, darkness, and social chaos. Most Egyptologists date the underlying composition to the late Middle Kingdom (c. 1850–1600 BC), well before any Ramesside exodus, so its value is literary rather than documentary. The Elephantine papyri from the fifth century BC, especially the Passover Papyrus of 419 BC, show diaspora Jewish communities observing at least the unleavened bread rites; no such specific evidence anchors the plagues themselves.

Key terms: *ʾarbeh* (אַרְבֶּה, locust); *rûaḥ qādîm* (רוּחַ קָדִים, east wind); *ḥōšek* (חֹשֶׁךְ, darkness); *yām-sûp* (יַם־סוּף, Sea of Reeds/Red Sea); *kābēd* (כָּבֵד, "hardened" in the sense of heavy, weighty — applied to Pharaoh's heart).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Exodus 10 functions as the penultimate crescendo of the plague narrative, two blows that strip Egypt of its material and cosmic resources before the final, human blow of chapter 11–12. The chapter is composed of two plague pericopes and two court scenes, arranged in a tight A–B–A–B lattice: announcement (vv. 1–6), negotiation (vv. 7–11), execution and appeal (vv. 12–20); then announcement/execution compressed (vv. 21–23), negotiation and rupture (vv. 24–29). Robert Alter (*The Five Books of Moses*) highlights the deliberate acceleration: chapter 10 shrinks the standard announcement–execution–pleading template to force the reader toward the final threshold. Propp notes the symmetry of the east wind and west wind as a narrative chiasmus: the same divine breath that brings devastation blows it out again into *yām-sûp*, the very Red Sea where Pharaoh's army will later be swept.

The hardening vocabulary is deliberately varied, a feature Umberto Cassuto (*A Commentary on the Book of Exodus*) and Nahum Sarna (*JPS Torah Commentary: Exodus*) have both mapped. Three verbs alternate across the plague cycle: *ḥāzaq* (חָזַק, "to strengthen"), *kābēd* (כָּבֵד, "to make heavy"), and *qāšâ* (קָשָׁה, "to harden"). In Exodus 10, the narrator uses *kābēd* at v. 1 ("I have hardened his heart") and *ḥāzaq* at vv. 20 and 27. Terence Fretheim (*Exodus*, Interpretation) reads this as the escalation of divine activity: what Pharaoh began with his own heaviness (earlier chapters use the middle voice, "Pharaoh's heart was heavy"), YHWH now intensifies. The theological load of these verbs cannot be flattened into one translation, which is why English "hardened" obscures the Hebrew's texture.

The chapter's internal rhetoric culminates in the courtiers' astonishing confession: "Do you not yet understand that Egypt is ruined?" (*hā-ṭerem tēdaʿ kî ʾābdâ miṣrāyim*; v. 7). This is the first internal Egyptian dissent. The literary function is to isolate Pharaoh: even his own administrators have capitulated, and his continued refusal is now personal pathology rather than policy. Alter calls this "a small masterpiece of court realism."

In terms of genre, Exodus 10 blends plague narrative, courtroom drama, and a nascent form of etiology (the locust and darkness plagues become referenced in later texts — Deuteronomy 28:38, Joel 1–2, Wisdom of Solomon 17 — as paradigms of covenantal curse). The catechetical frame in vv. 1–2 ("that you may tell in the hearing of your son and of your grandson") prefigures the explicit catechetical rubrics in Exodus 12:26–27 and 13:8, 14, linking the plague story to liturgical transmission.

Within the book's argument, chapter 10 completes the dismantling of the created Egyptian order. Fretheim has argued persuasively that the plagues are a systematic "un-creation" reversing Genesis 1: the Nile (primal waters) turned to blood, the land overrun by swarms (reversing Gen 1:20–25), the luminaries darkened (reversing Gen 1:3–18). When the darkness falls in v. 22, what is undone is the first act of creation itself — *yĕhî ʾôr*. Jon Levenson (*Creation and the Persistence of Evil*) reads this as YHWH demonstrating lordship over the chaos that Egyptian theology claims to hold at bay; the exodus is therefore cosmic combat, not merely ethnic liberation.

Textual variants worth flagging: LXX Exodus 10:5 reads "locusts" with a slightly different distribution of verbs; the Samaritan Pentateuch again harmonizes with a fuller announcement paralleling v. 1–2. 4QExodᵇ and 4QpaleoGen-Exodˡ preserve only fragmentary remains of the chapter, consistent with the MT.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, Exodus 10 stands between the covenant with Abraham (whose promises include both land and national identity) and the final assault on Pharaoh's divine sonship in Exodus 11–12. It is the last chapter before the plague-of-firstborn pivot that will reframe the entire story as a firstborn-for-firstborn exchange.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** Second Temple writers were fascinated by the chapter's wind and darkness. Josephus (*Antiquities* 2.305–314) rationalizes the plagues in the idiom of a Greco-Roman historian, treating the locusts as a meteorological event intensified by providence. Philo (*Life of Moses* 1.120–128) allegorizes the darkness as the spiritual condition of the unrepentant soul — an early form of what later Christian writers will call *tenebrae interiores*. Wisdom of Solomon 17 elaborates the darkness of Exodus 10 into a full-blown meditation on the psychological terror of the wicked: "For the lawless, when they supposed to lie hidden with their secret sins, were scattered, sore troubled with spectres, under a dark veil of forgetfulness" (Wis 17:3). This text is crucial; it transforms the plague into moral psychology in a way the Exodus author did not explicitly intend.

The rabbinic tradition read the chapter with characteristic narrative inventiveness. The *Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael* on Exodus 10 and *Exodus Rabbah* famously interpret the three-day darkness as both a punishment on Egyptians and a cover for Israelite internal drama: during those days, the wicked Israelites who refused to leave Egypt died and were buried, so that the Egyptians would not see the scale of the Hebrew losses. This midrashic maneuver solves a pastoral problem (why is there no mention of Israelite dissent at the exodus?) while preserving the text's emphasis on divine discrimination between peoples (v. 23: "all the people of Israel had light where they lived"). Rashi draws on this Mekhilta and adds a second reason: Israel used the darkness to scout out Egyptian household valuables, preparing for the "plundering" of chapter 11. Ibn Ezra, more rationalist, insists the darkness was a physical, not merely psychological, phenomenon and explicates the Hebrew *wĕyāmēš* ("and let it be felt") as aerosolized dust so thick it had tactile density. Ramban (Nahmanides) reads the locusts and darkness as complementary assaults on the Egyptian pantheon, coordinated to shame Osiris (grain and resurrection) and Ra (sun) in succession — a tradition picked up much later in modernity by Sarna and Umberto Cassuto.

Modern Jewish scholarship has foregrounded the chapter's theological politics. Avivah Zornberg (*The Particulars of Rapture*) reads Pharaoh's "I have sinned against the LORD your God, and against you" (v. 16) as a paradigmatic false confession — a performative act that cannot be repentance because it lacks the cognitive reframing that Hebrew *šûb* requires. Moshe Greenberg and Nahum Sarna both treat vv. 1–2 as the theological key to the whole cycle: the plagues' purpose is not merely to compel Pharaoh but to generate a story that can be told to "your son and your grandson."

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** The earliest Christian commentary on the plagues comes from Origen, whose *Homilies on Exodus* (c. AD 240s) reads the darkness allegorically as the condition of the soul apart from the light of Christ, leaning on Wisdom of Solomon 17. Augustine (*Quaestiones in Heptateuchum* and scattered sermons) wrestles with the hardening language in vv. 1, 20, 27, developing what will become his doctrine of double predestination, where the hardening is neither a mere permission nor a forced imposition but a mysterious divine withdrawal. Gregory of Nyssa (*Life of Moses* 2.79–83) makes the three-day darkness a figure of the "free choice of evil"; for him, darkness is the natural habitation of a soul that has refused the light, and the Egyptians' immobility ("nor did anyone rise from his place for three days") is the paralysis of vice. Jerome's *Commentary on Joel* uses Exodus 10's locust imagery to frame Joel 1–2 as a covenantal re-application.

Medieval commentators (Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I-II q. 79 a. 3; Bonaventure) used the chapter as a test case for divine permission of evil, arguing that God's hardening of Pharaoh is not the infusion of malice but the withholding of efficacious grace. Reformers diverged sharply: Calvin (*Institutes* II.4; commentary on Exodus) insists the hardening is an active divine decree, a teaching pressed by later Reformed scholastics; Luther (in the *Bondage of the Will* and scattered Exodus expositions) agrees but emphasizes that Pharaoh's hardening is also his own act, held together by the paradox Luther calls *Deus absconditus*. Arminian readers (in the Wesleyan trajectory) reverse the order: God hardens only after Pharaoh has hardened himself, using v. 27 as a response to v. 28's rupture.

Critical scholarship since Julius Wellhausen has primarily engaged the chapter on source-critical grounds (J with P framing) and on comparative-religion grounds (the plagues as deicide against the Egyptian pantheon). Childs emphasizes the final canonical form as more than the sum of its sources; Propp's Anchor Bible commentary treats the chapter as a coherent literary unit despite diachronic complexity. Walter Brueggemann (*Theology of the Old Testament*) reads the plagues as YHWH's "counter-testimony" against the Egyptian empire's self-legitimating myth.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Jewish and Christian traditions agree that Exodus 10 depicts a god who is lord of both meteorology and morality and whose judgment is communicable — narratable to children. They diverge on the status of Pharaoh: for the rabbis, Pharaoh is an archetype of tyrannical power to be dismantled historically and liturgically; for much of the patristic and Reformation tradition, Pharaoh is also, or primarily, an allegory of the hardened soul or the reprobate. The midrash's claim that wicked Israelites died in the darkness has almost no Christian analogue and produces a very different theology of covenant: judgment is internal to the community, not only external.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The hardening of Pharaoh's heart.** Who hardens Pharaoh in Exodus 10? The text is deliberately ambiguous. V. 1 says *YHWH* has hardened Pharaoh's and his servants' hearts; v. 20 and v. 27 repeat this. But in chapter 9 and again in the broader arc, Pharaoh is said to harden his own heart. The theological stakes are enormous: Is YHWH the author of evil in Pharaoh's soul? Does Pharaoh retain genuine freedom?

The Catholic position, developed by Augustine and systematized by Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* I-II q. 79 a. 3), distinguishes between God's antecedent will (that all be saved) and God's permissive will (that hardening be allowed as a just response to prior sin). The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* 311 states that God "in no way, directly or indirectly, causes moral evil." Aquinas reads v. 1 as God withdrawing the grace Pharaoh had resisted — a withdrawal that is just because it responds to prior rejection. Ratzinger (*Introduction to Christianity*) frames this within a broader doctrine of God's self-restraint before human freedom.

The Protestant Reformed position (Calvin; more recently Michael Horton, *The Christian Faith*) reads the hardening as active divine decree. Calvin insists that softening the text into mere permission evacuates the biblical claim. God hardens Pharaoh not because Pharaoh deserves it only (though he does) but because God ordains to reveal his power through that hardening (Rom 9:17, quoting Ex 9:16). Horton steelmans this: the offense to modern sensibility is the point — the text insists on divine sovereignty even when it is uncomfortable.

The Wesleyan/Arminian position (John Wesley, *Notes on the Old Testament*; more recently Roger Olson) reverses the order. Pharaoh hardens himself in the earlier plagues (chs. 7–9), and YHWH's hardening in ch. 10 is a *judicial* response, ratifying a choice already made. The "I will harden" in v. 1 is not an initiating action but a confirmation of Pharaoh's prior trajectory.

The Jewish tradition (Rashi, Maimonides, *Hilchot Teshuvah* 6:3) largely anticipates the Wesleyan reading. Maimonides explicitly argues that God's hardening of Pharaoh's heart is punishment for prior unrepented sin — God removes the capacity for repentance as a judicial act, not as a first mover. Levenson (*The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*) would add that the hardening serves a larger dramatic purpose: without it, the plague cycle cannot reach the firstborn climax, which is the theologically essential moment. The steelman here is that the "hardening" language must be read within a Semitic idiom where secondary causation is routinely ascribed to God; modern fretting over the passage may reflect Enlightenment intuitions foreign to the text.

The critical/academic position (Childs, Propp, Fretheim) sees the hardening language as a post-hoc theological framework imposed on an older narrative in which Pharaoh's obstinacy is his own. Fretheim reads the hardening as the narrator's way of protecting YHWH's power: if Pharaoh could simply have relented after plague three, the plague sequence would look inefficient. The hardening is literary, not metaphysical.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter will not let us choose between divine sovereignty and human freedom; it insists both are operative in Pharaoh's face. The honest reader holds the tension rather than dissolves it.

**Tension 2: The moral status of divine devastation.** Exodus 10 ends with total agricultural ruin: "Not a green thing remained, neither tree nor plant of the field, through all the land of Egypt" (v. 15). Ordinary Egyptians — not only Pharaoh — suffer. Is YHWH's conduct just?

The Orthodox tradition (Schmemann, *For the Life of the World*; Florovsky) frames the plagues as a liturgical un-making of false worship. The devastation of Egyptian agriculture is not cruelty but the exposure of a regime whose abundance was always dependent on divine provision it refused to acknowledge. When the Egyptian pantheon fails, what is revealed is what was always true: these "gods" did not feed Egypt.

The Reformed position (Calvin, Horton) accepts the moral weight of collective suffering and grounds it in federal theology: Pharaoh is the representative of Egypt, and Egypt suffers with and in its king. The steelman is that modern readers, saturated with individualist moral categories, may be ill-equipped to judge a covenantal/federal moral universe; scripture's own moral grammar includes corporate responsibility.

The Wesleyan/Arminian tradition emphasizes the genuine opportunity for repentance offered to Egyptian commoners and courtiers throughout the plague cycle. Wesley highlights v. 7 — Pharaoh's servants beg him to relent — as proof that the plagues had their intended pedagogical effect; those who heeded the warnings could flee with Moses (cf. Ex 12:38's "mixed multitude"). The suffering was not arbitrary; it was a summons.

The Jewish tradition, especially in Heschel (*The Prophets*) and Zornberg, treats the plagues as theodicy-in-reverse. The normal question of theodicy asks why the innocent suffer; the plague narrative asks why the guilty are spared so long. Heschel reads v. 7 ("do you not yet understand that Egypt is ruined?") as the narrator forcing the reader to recognize that mercy has been extended to the point of absurdity. The devastation is evidence of mercy deferred, not of mercy absent.

The critical/academic position (Fretheim, Brueggemann) treats the plagues as a theological polemic against imperial systems. The devastation of Egypt is the narrative's refusal to let empire go on functioning after it has enslaved the weak. Brueggemann's steelman: any reading that domesticates the plagues misses their subversive force. The text is unapologetically anti-imperial.

The Lutheran tradition (Luther, *Large Catechism*; Bonhoeffer, *Ethics*) holds the paradox: God is both the giver of all good and the judge of all evil, and the distinction between these two works is known only to God. Luther's *Deus absconditus* is here: God's work in Exodus 10 is wrathful *sub contrario*, but its ultimate aim is salvation — not merely of Israel but of the cosmos, which must be rid of the Pharaonic ordering of life.

**Where this leaves us:** The text does not apologize for divine wrath, but it also does not celebrate it. The devastation is presented as tragic necessity in the face of a sustained, systematic refusal; whether modern readers find that persuasive depends on whether we accept the premise that Pharaoh's regime is the kind of evil that requires un-making.

**Unresolved question:** Why does the narrative give us the dissent of Pharaoh's servants (v. 7) and not their fate? If they "saw that Egypt was ruined," did they flee with the mixed multitude in chapter 12? The text is silent, and that silence is theologically generative.

## The Thread

Four non-obvious cross-references thread through Exodus 10.

First, the three-day darkness in vv. 22–23 resonates with the three-day darkness over Jerusalem at the crucifixion (Matthew 27:45, Mark 15:33, Luke 23:44). Early Christian commentators (Melito of Sardis, Origen) heard this echo; Melito treats Good Friday as a cosmic reenactment of the plague of darkness, with the judgment falling now on the new Egypt, which is sin and death itself. The darkness of Exodus 10 lasts three days; the grave of Jesus lasts three days; both end in exodus — out of Egypt, out of the tomb.

Second, the east wind of v. 13 and the west wind of v. 19 prefigure the east wind that divides the sea in Exodus 14:21. The same verb *nāhag* ("to drive") is used of the winds and, eventually, of YHWH driving the sea back. The rûaḥ that fills chapter 10 is the same rûaḥ that hovers over the waters of creation (Gen 1:2) and that will breathe over the dry bones of Ezekiel 37 — a divine breath that undoes what Egypt has built and rebuilds what Israel will become.

Third, the locusts of v. 14 — "such a dense swarm of locusts as had never been before, nor ever will be again" — are explicitly echoed in Joel 1:2–4 and Joel 2:2 ("their like has never been from of old, nor will be again after them, through the years of all generations"). Joel reapplies the Exodus plague to Israel itself as a covenantal curse come home; the prophet's point is that Israel is now the Egypt of its own story. This is the hermeneutic that allows Jeremiah and Ezekiel to read the Babylonian conquest as a reverse exodus.

Fourth, Pharaoh's declaration in v. 28 — "Get away from me; take care never to see my face again" — is literarily answered in Exodus 33:20, when YHWH tells Moses, "you cannot see my face, for man shall not see me and live." The chapter that begins with Pharaoh claiming the prerogative of the face that cannot be seen ends the book of Exodus with that prerogative returned to its rightful owner. The seeing of faces, across the book, becomes a grammar of sovereignty.
