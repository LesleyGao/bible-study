# Proverbs 18 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Proverbs 18 belongs to the great Solomonic sentence-proverb collection (10:1–22:16) that Bruce Waltke (NICOT) reads as the theological core of the book. Within it, chapter 18 has a recognizable thematic spine: words and their consequences, friendship and isolation, the contrast between trust in Yahweh and trust in wealth. Michael Fox (Anchor Bible) notes that the chapter's structural center is the pair of verses 10–11 — the *name of Yahweh* as strong tower set deliberately against the *rich man's wealth* as strong city. Roland Murphy (Word Biblical Commentary) and Tremper Longman (Baker) both treat this pairing as one of the most theologically pointed juxtapositions in the entire collection: two cities, two refuges, only one of them real.

Verse 10 — *migdal-oz shem Yahweh, bo-yaruts tsaddiq ve-nisgav* (מִגְדַּל־עֹז שֵׁם יְהוָה בּוֹ־יָרוּץ צַדִּיק וְנִשְׂגָּב), "the name of Yahweh is a strong tower; into it the righteous runs and is set on high" — uses the Hebrew *migdal oz* (מִגְדַּל־עֹז), literally "tower of strength." The image draws on the iron-age Levantine fortified city: a stone watchtower at the corner of the wall, the highest point in the city, the last refuge when the wall was breached. Christine Roy Yoder (Abingdon OT Commentary) observes that the *shem Yahweh* — the *Name* — is treated here as itself the refuge, not merely as a label for the refuge. The verb *yaruts* ("runs") is dynamic, aspectual: the righteous *runs into it* — there is movement, urgency, action. The verb *nisgav* ("is set on high") is in the niphal stem, which in this construction has a passive-divine sense: the Name lifts the runner up.

This Name-theology has deep roots. Exodus 3's revelation of *YHWH* (יהוה), Deuteronomy's repeated references to "the place where Yahweh will cause his Name to dwell" (12:5, 11; 14:23), and the Aaronic blessing's threefold pronouncement of the Name (Numbers 6:24–26) all stand behind this proverb. William Brown (*Wisdom's Wonder*) argues that Proverbs 18:10 is the wisdom tradition's most explicit appropriation of Israel's covenantal Name-theology — the *shem* is not a magical formula but the active presence of the covenant God.

Verse 11 — *hon ashir qiryat uzzo, u-ke-chomah nisgavah be-maskito* (הוֹן עָשִׁיר קִרְיַת עֻזּוֹ וּכְחוֹמָה נִשְׂגָּבָה בְּמַשְׂכִּתוֹ), "the wealth of the rich is his strong city, and like a high wall *in his imagination*" — uses the deliberately deflating *be-maskito* (בְּמַשְׂכִּתוֹ), "in his imagination" or "in his mental picture." The word *maskit* elsewhere refers to imagery, sometimes carved imagery (Leviticus 26:1, Ezekiel 8:12). Fox and Waltke both highlight the irony: the wealth *looks* like the tower of verse 10, but only as a mental construction. The form mimics the content. The tower of Yahweh's Name is real refuge; the wall of wealth exists only in the rich man's head. Mark Boda (work on Israel's narrative theology) notes the prophetic edge: this is wisdom's quiet challenge to the urban fortified order that ancient kings built and trusted.

Verse 21's *mavet ve-chayyim be-yad-lashon* (מָוֶת וְחַיִּים בְּיַד־לָשׁוֹן), "death and life are in the hand of the tongue," uses the Hebrew *yad* (hand) idiomatically for "power" or "agency." The construction is characteristic of the wisdom tradition's high view of speech — words are not soft, ornamental, or peripheral; they are agents in the world, capable of both destroying and creating. Will Kynes (*Obituary for "Wisdom Literature"*) emphasizes that this verse should not be read as an isolated nugget; it is the climax of a long chapter on speech ethics and must be read in context.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels illuminate the chapter. The Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemope* contains parallel material on the dangers of hasty speech (chapters 4 and 9) and the wisdom of friendship. The Akkadian *Counsels of Wisdom* warns against quarrelsome speech and praises the friend who endures. But Proverbs 18 introduces a distinctive theological move: the contrast between *Name* and *wealth* as competing refuges has no clear ANE parallel. As Waltke notes, this is wisdom doing covenantal theology — taking the secular categories of fortified-city and tower and reading them through Yahweh.

Verse 19's *ach niphsha mi-qiryat-oz* (אָח נִפְשָׁע מִקִּרְיַת־עֹז), "a brother offended is harder than a strong city," uses *qiryat-oz* — "fortified city" — the same root as verse 11's *qiryat uzzo*. The chapter is layered with city-imagery: tower (v. 10), wall (v. 11), and now the fortifications of an offended brother's heart. Fox argues this is deliberate design: the chapter explores what is and is not actually fortified. The Name is. Wealth is not. An offended brother's heart, sadly, often is.

Verse 24's *yesh ohev davek me-ach* (יֵשׁ אֹהֵב דָּבֵק מֵאָח), "there is a lover/friend who sticks closer than a brother," uses the verb *davaq* — "cleave," the same word used of marriage in Genesis 2:24. Friendship at this level is covenantal. Longman reads verse 24 as the chapter's relational climax, set against the chapter's opening warning (v. 1) about the one who *isolates himself* (the niphal of *parad*, "separate").

## Lens 2: The Literary Genre

Proverbs 18 is sentence-proverb literature with strong thematic clustering. Verses 1–2, 6–8, 13, 17, 19–21, 23 all concern speech; verses 10–11 form the chapter's theological pivot; verses 1, 19, 24 frame the chapter with reflections on friendship and isolation. James Kugel (*Idea of Biblical Poetry*) called this kind of clustering "thematic resonance" — the proverbs are not random aphorisms but a curated collection meant to be read as a sequence.

The chapter uses every major form of Hebrew parallelism. Verse 10–11 is one of the most striking examples of *thesis-and-counter-thesis* in the corpus. The two verses use parallel grammatical structures and the same imagery (city, fortification, height) to make opposite theological claims. Adele Berlin (*Dynamics of Biblical Parallelism*) reads this as the wisdom tradition's most rhetorically efficient device: the form trains the reader to see two competing world-pictures simultaneously and to discriminate between them.

Verse 4's *mayim amukim divrei pi-ish, nachal nove'a meqor chokhmah* (מַיִם עֲמֻקִּים דִּבְרֵי פִי־אִישׁ נַחַל נֹבֵעַ מְקוֹר חָכְמָה), "deep waters are the words of a man's mouth, a bubbling brook is the wellspring of wisdom," uses two distinct water-images that scholars have long debated. Are deep waters good (depth, mystery) or bad (treachery, hidden danger)? Yoder argues both readings are right: human speech *can* be either, and the proverb names the ambiguity. The *bubbling brook* — *nachal nove'a* — is unambiguously good; it springs up, gives life, never stagnates. The implicit ethic: let your speech be the bubbling brook, not the deep treacherous water.

Verses 20–21 form a tight unit on the *fruit of the lips*. The image is agricultural: speech is sown and harvested. Verse 20 — *mi-peri pi-ish tisba bitno* (מִפְּרִי פִי־אִישׁ תִּשְׂבַּע בִּטְנוֹ), "from the fruit of a man's mouth his stomach is satisfied" — pairs with verse 21's *those who love it will eat its fruit*. Brown calls this "embodied speech ethics" — words are food, and you eat what you say.

The chapter contains memorable proverbial epigrams that have entered English usage: *the one who states his case first seems right* (v. 17 — used in legal pedagogy), *a fool takes no pleasure in understanding but only in expressing his opinion* (v. 2 — quoted in countless modern essays), *death and life are in the power of the tongue* (v. 21).

Verse 8's *divrei nirgan ke-mitlahamim* (דִּבְרֵי נִרְגָּן כְּמִתְלַהֲמִים), "the words of a whisperer are like delicious morsels," uses a rare verb that may mean either "delicious bites" or "wounding blows." The Septuagint went one way, the Vulgate another. Either reading works thematically — gossip both wounds and feels delicious. Murphy notes that the wisdom tradition's polysemy is often deliberate; the Hebrew preserves the ambiguity that translation tends to flatten.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Proverbs 18:10's *Name of Yahweh as strong tower* echoes through the canon and becomes the seedbed for one of the richest devotional traditions in both Judaism and Christianity. The Psalter is full of Name-theology that runs parallel to the proverb: Psalm 20:1 ("the Name of the God of Jacob protect you"), Psalm 124:8 ("our help is in the Name of Yahweh"), Psalm 91:14 ("because he knows my Name, I will protect him"). Isaiah 50:10 — "let him trust in the Name of Yahweh and rely on his God" — could be read as direct exposition of Proverbs 18:10.

In Jewish reception the proverb sits at the heart of *kavannah* (intentional prayer) and the use of the Name in liturgy. The substitution of *Adonai* and *Hashem* for the Tetragrammaton in synagogue practice is not a denial of the proverb but a heightened acknowledgment of it: the Name is so powerful that direct utterance is reserved. The medieval mystical tradition, especially the *Sefer Yetzirah* and later Kabbalistic literature, developed elaborate Name-theologies whose root, in part, is texts like Proverbs 18:10. Hasidic teaching emphasized *running into the Name* in moments of distress — Rabbi Nachman of Breslov's practice of *hitbodedut* (solitary spoken prayer to God) draws on this proverb's image of urgent flight to refuge.

Christian devotional tradition has been particularly drawn to Proverbs 18:10. Martin Luther's *Ein feste Burg* ("A Mighty Fortress Is Our God") is essentially a hymnic exposition of the proverb crossed with Psalm 46. Charles Spurgeon's hymn "*The Name of Jesus Is a Strong and Mighty Tower*" makes the Christological move explicit: the *shem Yahweh* of the proverb is, for Christian devotion, the Name of Jesus (Philippians 2:9–11, "the Name above every name"). The Catholic *Catechism* (§§432–435) treats the divine Name as a constitutive element of prayer; the Eastern Orthodox tradition's *Jesus Prayer* — "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me" — is itself a continuous running into the Name as tower. Reformed liturgy, Wesleyan revival hymnody (Charles Wesley's "Jesus, the Name High over All"), and modern evangelical worship all draw deeply on this proverb.

Verse 21's *death and life are in the power of the tongue* finds its New Testament expansion in James 3:1–12 — the most sustained New Testament treatment of speech ethics. James's image of the tongue as a small fire that sets a forest ablaze, as a rudder that turns a great ship, as a fountain that should not pour out both fresh and salt water — all of this elaborates the wisdom tradition's claim that speech is consequential beyond its physical scale. Jesus's own teaching in Matthew 12:34–37 — "out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks... by your words you will be justified, and by your words you will be condemned" — is wisdom-tradition theology absorbed and intensified.

Here the chapter requires honest engagement with a contemporary distortion. Prosperity-gospel teachers — Kenneth Hagin, Kenneth Copeland, Joel Osteen, and the broader Word of Faith movement — proof-text Proverbs 18:21 to claim that believers can "speak things into being." The argument: if death and life are in the power of the tongue, then by speaking healing, wealth, or success aloud, the believer activates a creative force that produces those outcomes. This reading is exegetically and theologically untenable, and the mainstream evangelical response — articulated clearly by D.A. Carson, Michael Horton, John Piper, and the Gospel Coalition's body of work — is straightforward. The proverb is wisdom literature, not a metaphysical formula. *Be-yad-lashon* ("in the hand of the tongue") names speech as a powerful moral instrument, not a creative ontological force. The "fruits" the speaker eats are the relational, social, and spiritual consequences of their speech — friends made or lost, reputations built or ruined, trust earned or squandered. The verse stands beside, not in tension with, the chapter's broader counsel: hear before you answer (v. 13), restrain your speech (cf. 17:27–28), let your words be a bubbling brook of wisdom (v. 4). Read in context, Proverbs 18:21 is a sober warning, not a magical formula. Costi Hinn (*God, Greed, and the (Prosperity) Gospel*) — himself a recovering Word of Faith preacher — has argued that the misreading of this verse is one of the prosperity gospel's most damaging hermeneutical moves.

Verse 22 — *matsa ishah matsa tov, va-yapheq ratson me-Yahweh* (מָצָא אִשָּׁה מָצָא טוֹב וַיָּפֶק רָצוֹן מֵיְהוָה), "he who finds a wife finds a good thing and obtains favor from Yahweh" — anchors a long Jewish and Christian tradition of marriage as covenant gift. The Talmud (*Yevamot* 62b) cites this verse in its discussion of the obligation to marry. Christian marriage liturgies in nearly every tradition draw on this verse alongside Genesis 2:18.

Verse 24's *friend who sticks closer than a brother* has been read by Christian devotional tradition (especially in hymnody — "What a Friend We Have in Jesus") Christologically. The wisdom tradition itself does not require a Christological reading, but the verse does articulate a relational ideal — friendship-as-covenant — that the New Testament identifies as fulfilled in Christ's friendship with his disciples (John 15:13–15).

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The chapter's central tension is *what counts as refuge*. Verses 10 and 11 stand side by side: the Name of Yahweh is a strong tower; the rich man's wealth is a strong city *in his imagination*. The steelman of the wealth-as-refuge reading: wealth genuinely does protect against many of life's contingencies — illness, hunger, legal vulnerability, the predatory power of others. To pretend it does not is naive, and the wisdom tradition itself elsewhere acknowledges the protective function of wealth (10:15 — "the wealth of the rich is his strong city" — almost identical phrasing). The steelman of the Name-as-refuge reading: every protection wealth offers terminates at the same boundary — death, judgment, the deepest forms of moral vulnerability — at which the Name takes over. The wisdom tradition does not deny that wealth has functional power; it denies that wealth has *ultimate* power.

The chapter sets the two readings against each other and forces a choice about *the imagination*. *Be-maskito* ("in his imagination") is the proverb's interpretive key. The rich man's wall is real to him because he has constructed the mental architecture in which it counts as ultimate. The righteous, by contrast, runs into the Name — an act that requires a different imagination. Brown calls this "wisdom's pedagogy of the imagination" — the chapter is not finally about money or words or friends but about which mental architecture the reader inhabits.

The second tension is *speech as creative or destructive*. Verse 21's "death and life in the power of the tongue" sits between the prosperity-gospel temptation (speech as creative ontological force) and the modern dismissal (speech as ethically neutral expression). The wisdom tradition steers between both. Speech is consequential — verses 6–8 and 13 trace the destructive cascade of foolish speech, and verses 4, 15, 20 trace the constructive yield of wise speech. But speech is not creative ex nihilo; it is responsive to reality, and its power is moral and relational, not metaphysical. Murphy's reading: speech is potent because it is the fruit of the heart, and the heart is potent because it is weighed by Yahweh (cf. 16:2). The chain is heart-speech-consequence, not speech-creation.

Where this leaves us: Proverbs 18 trains the imagination to see clearly. It asks what we are actually trusting (Name or wealth), what we are actually doing with our words (giving life or death), and what kind of friend we are actually being (the gossiping whisperer of v. 8 or the one who sticks closer than a brother in v. 24). The chapter's gift is its refusal to flatter. The rich man's imagined wall is named for what it is. The fool's mouth is named for what it is. And the Name of Yahweh is named for what it actually is — the only refuge that holds when every fortification fails.

The closing question: when the wall in your imagination is breached — when the financial security, the reputation, the relationship that has felt like your tower turns out to have been *be-maskito*, in your head — what Name do you actually run into?
