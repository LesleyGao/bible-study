# 2 Samuel 20: The Trumpet at Gilgal and the Wise Woman of Abel

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 20 unfolds in the immediate political wake of Absalom's defeat, in a moment when the kingdom David has hammered together is visibly cracking along its oldest seam: the divide between Judah in the south and the northern tribes that had once followed Saul. Sheba son of Bichri is a Benjaminite, that is, a member of Saul's tribe, and his rallying cry — "we have no portion in David, no inheritance in the son of Jesse" — is not the ranting of an isolated malcontent but a slogan with a long political afterlife. The same words reappear almost verbatim at 1 Kings 12:16, when the northern tribes finally make their secession from Solomon's son Rehoboam permanent. The chapter is therefore a window into the structural fragility of the so-called "United Monarchy," a polity that scholars from Albrecht Alt to Baruch Halpern have argued was less a unified nation-state than a personal union held together by David's charisma, his army, and his ability to play tribal factions against each other.

The geography is precise and politically loaded. The action begins at Gilgal in the Jordan Valley, the historic site of Israel's entry into the land under Joshua and a recurring center of tribal assembly. From there the movement runs west to Jerusalem, then northwest to Gibeon (Tell el-Jib, excavated by James Pritchard between 1956 and 1962, where a massive cylindrical pool cut into bedrock — likely the "great pool" of 2 Sam 2:13 — and dozens of wine-press installations document a substantial Iron II town), and finally up to Abel of Beth-maacah on Israel's far northern frontier near the modern Lebanese border. Abel has been identified with Tell Abil el-Qameh, where excavations led by Robert Mullins and Nava Panitz-Cohen since 2013 have uncovered a large fortified Iron II city with imposing public architecture, including a monumental building destroyed in the late ninth or early eighth century — material confirmation that Abel was a city of real political weight when the biblical narrator calls it "a city that is a mother in Israel." The pursuit of Sheba thus traces, in microcosm, the geographic logic of David's kingdom: a southern royal core projecting force northward through chokepoints (Gibeon) toward a fractious frontier (Abel).

Three Hebrew terms repay close attention. The first is *beliyya'al* (בְּלִיַּעַל), used to introduce Sheba in verse 1: rendered "worthless" by ESV, it carries connotations of social and ritual disorder — literally "without profit" or "without yoke," a man who stands outside the legitimate covenantal order. The same epithet is used of Eli's sons in 1 Samuel 2:12 and of Nabal in 1 Samuel 25:25; by Second Temple times it had become a quasi-personal demonic name (Belial in the Qumran scrolls and 2 Cor 6:15). The second is *ḥakhamah* (חֲכָמָה), "wise woman," a near-technical title in the David narratives. The unnamed woman of Abel is the second of two such figures — the first being the wise woman of Tekoa whom Joab himself coached in 2 Samuel 14. Claudia Camp and Carole Fontaine have argued that these figures preserve the memory of a recognized social-political role of female sage-arbiter in early Israelite town life, parallel to the Mesopotamian *naditu* and the Egyptian "wise woman" of village dispute resolution. The third is the phrase *ir va-em b'yisrael* (עִיר וְאֵם בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל), "a city that is a mother in Israel" — a striking metaphor in which the city is personified as a maternal figure whose destruction would be a matricide of the covenant heritage. The image both elevates Abel's status (it is no mere village but a metropolis with traditional juridical authority — "let them but ask counsel at Abel, and so they settled a matter") and rebukes Joab's siege as a violation of Israelite kinship.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Generically, chapter 20 sits at the structural seam between two distinct compositions. Leonhard Rost's foundational 1926 study *Die Überlieferung von der Thronnachfolge Davids* identified a "Succession Narrative" or "Court History" running from roughly 2 Samuel 9 (or 11) through 1 Kings 2, written with a striking psychological realism, novelistic dialogue, and theological restraint that distinguish it from the rest of the Deuteronomistic History. On Rost's reading — modified but largely preserved by R. N. Whybray, Charles Conroy, and David Gunn — chapter 20 closes the main narrative arc of the Court History before the editorial appendix of chapters 21-24 interrupts; the Succession Narrative then resumes at 1 Kings 1 with the Adonijah-Solomon contest. Chapter 20 is thus the natural climax of the Absalom cycle's aftermath: the Sheba revolt is the last gasp of organized northern resistance to David, and the chapter ends with a final cabinet list (vv. 23-26) that mirrors and updates the earlier list at 2 Samuel 8:16-18, signaling a structural close.

The chapter is built on tight verbal and thematic parallelism. The "trumpet" (*shofar*) frames the action: Sheba blows it at the start (v. 1) to summon revolt, and Joab blows it at the end (v. 22) to dissolve the siege — sound brackets that mark the rise and fall of the rebellion. Joab's murder of Amasa explicitly recalls his murder of Abner in 2 Samuel 3:27: in both cases Joab takes the rival commander aside under pretense of friendly greeting (the fraternal kiss, the question "Is it well with you, my brother?"), and in both cases drives a sword into the belly. Robert Alter, in *The David Story*, calls this "Joab's signature": the calculated theatricality of the gesture, its public visibility, its function as warning to anyone tempted to displace him. J. P. Fokkelman, in volume 3 of his four-volume literary commentary on Samuel, traces the way the narrator's camera lingers on Amasa's body wallowing in the road until removed — a visual emblem of how Joab's violence stops the body politic in its tracks until it is hidden.

The wise-woman scene is a textbook instance of what Meir Sternberg in *The Poetics of Biblical Narrative* calls "scenic narration": dialogue does the entire load of characterization. The woman never gives her name; her authority is purely rhetorical and traditional. She invokes a proverbial ancient practice ("they used to say in former times, let them but ask counsel at Abel"), positions herself as the voice of the city's covenantal status ("peaceable and faithful in Israel"), and brings Joab to negotiate by appealing to the YHWH-heritage rationale rather than to military threat. Her speech is structured as a chiasm: A) ancient counsel, B) my peaceable status, C) the heritage of YHWH at risk, B') Joab's denial, A') concrete settlement — Sheba's head for the city's life.

Textual notes are substantial. The Lucianic recension of the LXX preserves a longer reading at verse 18 with an explicit reference to Dan as a co-arbitrating city alongside Abel — a reading P. Kyle McCarter's Anchor Bible commentary considers possibly original. Verse 8's description of Joab's sword "falling out" is grammatically obscure in the MT and may, as A. A. Anderson suggests in the Word Biblical Commentary, be a deliberate device by Joab — the sword falls into his off-hand so that Amasa does not see it — rather than an accident. The MT's precise term for Sheba (a *Bichri*, a clan name) and the play on *Bichrites* in v. 14 has occasioned suggestions that "Bichri" should be vocalized as *bekhori*, "first-born," tying the revolt to claims of tribal seniority — though most modern critics, including David Tsumura in NICOT, treat the clan reading as primary.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc of chapter 20 looks both backward and forward. Backward, the slogan "we have no portion in David" knits the Saul cycle to the Jeroboam cycle: the same tribal grievance that animated Saul's house animates Sheba and will animate the secession of the ten tribes under Jeroboam. Forward, the chapter's casual brutalities — Joab's murder of Amasa, the public abandonment of the ten concubines to a living widowhood — set in motion themes the Court History will resolve in 1 Kings 2, where the dying David instructs Solomon to settle scores with Joab.

Within Jewish interpretation, Josephus in *Antiquities* 7.11 dramatizes the chapter as a study in disordered loyalties, framing Joab as a man whose service to the king is inseparable from his ungovernable ambition. The rabbis in tractate Sanhedrin (49a-b) wrestle with the legitimacy of Joab's actions: they ask whether Joab's killing of Amasa was murder or licit execution of a tardy commander, and conclude — after multiple positions are ventilated — that it was murder, but that Joab was not subject to court execution because his service to David shielded him until David's deathbed instruction. Rashi reads Sheba as the type of the schismatic, while Radak (David Kimhi) develops the political reading: the slogan reveals that the wound between north and south was never healed, only suppressed. Abarbanel, writing in fifteenth-century Iberia and Italy with the eye of a court counselor who had served the kings of Portugal and Naples, treats the Amasa murder as a case study in the structural problem of what to do with a "necessary villain" — a general too useful to dismiss and too dangerous to keep. Yair Zakovitch, in modern Israeli scholarship, has argued that the wise woman of Abel preserves a real institutional memory of female arbitration that the later Deuteronomistic editors largely effaced.

Christian interpretation has paid less attention to chapter 20 than to its more dramatic neighbors, but the patristic tradition mined the wise woman as a type of the church mediating between Christ and unrepentant cities — Origen and Gregory the Great both develop variations on this allegory. Augustine in *City of God* 17.20 reads the Sheba revolt as evidence that the earthly city even within Israel is always at risk of fragmenting along the lines of self-interest, and contrasts this with the unity of the city of God grounded in the love of God rather than tribal loyalty. Calvin's commentary on 2 Samuel reads the chapter as a study in the providence of God working through the moral compromises of David's court; he is unsparing on Joab but also notes the political reality that David could not act without him. Among modern critics, Walter Brueggemann in his Interpretation commentary highlights the way the chapter exposes the cost of David's kingship: the women — the ten concubines — bear the silent weight of David's choices; the wise woman of Abel saves a city by negotiating with the man who has just murdered his rival in cold blood. Eugene Peterson in *Leap over a Wall* draws a pastoral lesson from the wise woman: that wisdom often takes the form of refusing to let large powers crush small communities under abstract necessities.

Feminist readings have unearthed real depth here. Phyllis Trible's broader project on "texts of terror" finds the ten concubines of verse 3 a paradigmatic case: David takes them with him into Jerusalem at the start of his reign, leaves them to keep house when he flees Absalom, finds them publicly violated by his son on the palace roof (16:22), and now consigns them to a living-widowhood imprisonment "until the day of their death" — provided for materially but stripped of name, agency, and conjugal life. They are the silent corollary of the Davidic political project. Esther Fuchs and Cheryl Exum (especially in *Tragedy and Biblical Narrative*) have read this scene as a quiet horror that the narrative refuses to dramatize — precisely because the political-male perspective controls the camera. The wise woman of Abel offers the counter-image: a woman who, unnamed, nonetheless acts and speaks and saves, modeling an alternative political ethics rooted in negotiation rather than spectacle.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The chapter generates two enduring theological tensions.

The first concerns *the legitimacy of political violence by indispensable agents*. Joab is the man who does what David cannot afford to be seen doing: he kills Abner, he kills Absalom against David's explicit order, he kills Amasa, he will later be killed himself at Solomon's command. Three positions sit on the table. The first, articulated by Augustine and developed by Aquinas in his treatment of just authority (*Summa* II-II q. 64), holds that a sovereign may use morally compromised agents in service of public order without himself becoming morally complicit, provided he ultimately holds them to account; David's deathbed instruction to settle accounts with Joab vindicates rather than indicts the prior arrangement. The second, sharpened by Calvin and recently by Walter Brueggemann, holds that the David-Joab relationship is a structural moral compromise from which no theology of clean kingship can escape: David benefits at every stage from Joab's violence even while ostensibly disapproving of it, and the text refuses to absolve him. The third, advanced by Baruch Halpern in *David's Secret Demons*, reads the entire Court History as in part an apologetic written to deflect the suspicion that David himself ordered the killings Joab carried out — Joab as scapegoat, the source-document equivalent of plausible deniability. Steelmanning Halpern: the slogan "every man to his tents, O Israel" is exactly what one would chant if one believed David had quietly orchestrated a series of inconvenient deaths and was now trying to scrape together a kingdom on the backs of those murders. **Where this leaves us:** the text neither absolves David nor condemns him; it presents the indispensable-villain problem as the unsolved cost of statecraft in a fallen world, and it is the modern reader, not the ancient narrator, who must decide whether such a cost is ever justifiable.

The second tension concerns *the moral standing of negotiated peace versus principled resistance*. The wise woman of Abel achieves peace by handing over a single life — Sheba's head — to spare a city. Three positions. First, the pragmatic-realist tradition (drawing on Augustine on the just war, modern figures like Reinhold Niebuhr): in a world of finite goods and finite power, the lesser-evil calculus is itself a form of moral seriousness; the wise woman saves more lives than she sacrifices, and her action is morally praiseworthy. Second, the principled-pacifist tradition (Anabaptist readings, John Howard Yoder): handing over a fugitive to be executed is collaboration with a violent power, even if it spares the city; the wise woman has compromised the city's prophetic witness for its physical survival. Third, a mediating reading developed by Brueggemann and Ellen Davis: the woman acts within the political order she actually inhabits, neither sanctifying violence nor refusing to engage with it; her wisdom is the wisdom of one who knows that a city's life is not her own to gamble with. Steelmanning the pacifist position: the text never tells us Sheba was guilty of more than political dissent; it never gives him a trial; his head is delivered as an exchange, not a sentence. The narrator describes the wise woman's act with apparent approval, but the moral question lingers. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter validates negotiated mediation as a form of wisdom, but does not collapse the distinction between wisdom and righteousness — the woman's settlement saves lives, but it does not erase the troubling fact that a man's life was the currency.

One unresolved question: what does it mean that the entire Sheba episode is sandwiched between the silent imprisonment of David's concubines (v. 3) and the formal cabinet list (vv. 23-26)? The narrator gives us a kingdom whose political structure looks orderly on paper — commanders, recorders, secretaries, priests — but whose women have been hidden away and whose principal field general has just murdered his rival in the road. The form of order is restored; the substance is contested.

## The Thread

Chapter 20 connects to several large biblical movements. First, the "we have no portion in David" slogan reappears at 1 Kings 12:16 as the formal speech of the northern secession under Jeroboam — chapter 20 is the political dress rehearsal for the schism that will define Israelite history for the next two centuries until the fall of Samaria in 722 BCE. Second, the wise woman of Abel is paired with the wise woman of Tekoa in 2 Samuel 14, and together they form a small but distinct biblical tradition of female political arbitration that includes Deborah (Judges 4-5), Huldah (2 Kings 22), and arguably the unnamed prophetic woman in Isaiah 8. Third, Joab's murder of Amasa is the third in a sequence — Abner (3:27), Absalom (18:14), Amasa (20:10) — and the third killing is what finally seals Joab's death sentence in David's deathbed instruction at 1 Kings 2:5-6. Fourth, the cabinet list of vv. 23-26 anticipates the more elaborate Solomonic cabinet of 1 Kings 4, and the inclusion of "Adoram" over the forced labor (*mas*) flags a fault line — corvée labor — that will become the explicit grievance triggering the secession in 1 Kings 12. The chapter is small in profile but structurally load-bearing: the kingdom that Solomon will inherit has its political fractures already mapped here.
