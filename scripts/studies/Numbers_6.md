# Numbers 6: The Name I Will Put on Them

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Numbers 6 sits in the opening Sinai block of *Bĕmidbar* (Numbers 1–10:10), where Israel has been counted, camped in tribal order around the tabernacle, and organized as a ritual polity on the brink of departure. The chapter has two distinct legislative units that most modern critical scholars — Gray in the ICC, Budd in the Word Biblical Commentary, Noth in his classic commentary — assign to the Priestly stratum (P), while more conservative commentators such as Gordon Wenham (Tyndale) and Timothy Ashley treat the material as integrally Mosaic with later redactional polish. Milgrom in his JPS Torah Commentary argues that the Nazirite legislation preserves a remarkably archaic institution, older than the monarchic period, because it functions without a temple, without a fixed sanctuary bureaucracy, and without the class distinctions that later priestly writings presuppose. The institution presumes a world in which a common Israelite — male or female — could assume a temporary, self-imposed consecration that rivaled the holiness of the priesthood itself. Baruch Levine in the Anchor Bible commentary notes that the Nazirite's three prohibitions map onto three distinct domains (diet, body, mortality) that together construct an anti-priest mirror: where the priest is born into holiness, the Nazirite vows into it; where the priest must avoid corpses only on duty, the Nazirite must avoid them always; where the priest may drink wine off-duty, the Nazirite may not.

The political setting is a camp in motion, and the material culture is domestic rather than urban. A Nazirite's consecration would have been performed with ordinary household provisions — fresh grapes, dried raisins, wine, vinegar, a razor — all of which the chapter forbids in concrete vocabulary. The shaving of the head (v. 18) and the burning of the hair "on the fire that is under the sacrifice of the peace offering" presuppose the tabernacle's outer altar and the suite of animal sacrifices standard in Leviticus 1–7. Archaeologically, the Nazirite institution is difficult to recover directly — hair does not survive — but the completion ritual's sacrificial economy (lamb, ewe, ram, unleavened bread, grain, drink offerings) maps onto Late Bronze and early Iron Age Levantine votive practice attested at sites like Shiloh and Lachish. Kitchen and Hoffmeier have argued that votive dedications of this scale are entirely plausible for wilderness-period Israel given the wealth extracted from Egypt (Exod 12:36).

Five Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. *Nāzîr* (נָזִיר) derives from the root *nzr* meaning "to separate, consecrate, set apart," and is cognate with *nēzer* (נֵזֶר), the "crown" or "diadem" worn by the high priest (Exod 29:6) and later the king (2 Sam 1:10). The Nazirite's long hair is literally a "crown of God on his head" (v. 7, *nēzer ʾĕlōhāyw*). *Pelāʾ* (פָּלָא) in v. 2, translated "special vow," is the same root that produces the noun for "wonder" or "extraordinary act"; Rashi glosses it as "separated and distinguished from others by his vow." *Tĕnûpâ* (תְּנוּפָה), the "wave offering" in v. 20, describes the ritual motion of elevating and presenting offerings before YHWH — Milgrom reconstructs it as a horizontal, not vertical, motion, a presentation rather than an elevation. *Birkat kōhănîm* (בִּרְכַּת כֹּהֲנִים), though not a phrase inside the chapter itself, is the technical rabbinic name for the priestly blessing of vv. 24–26 — "the blessing of the priests." And *šālôm* (שָׁלוֹם) in v. 26, far richer than English "peace," denotes wholeness, completeness, integration of relationships — the climactic gift the blessing names.

The chapter's archaeological crown jewel is the Ketef Hinnom silver scrolls. In 1979, Gabriel Barkay excavated a burial cave on the southern shoulder of Jerusalem's Hinnom Valley, and in Chamber 25 of Cave 24 he found two rolled silver amulets so fragile that it took three years to unroll them. Inscribed in paleo-Hebrew script, they preserve an abbreviated form of the priestly blessing of Numbers 6:24–26. Paleographic analysis dates them to the late seventh or early sixth century BC — pre-exilic — which makes them the oldest extant fragments of biblical text by roughly four centuries (predating the earliest Dead Sea Scroll manuscripts). The find decisively undermines Wellhausen's assumption that priestly material was entirely post-exilic; at minimum the priestly blessing was in liturgical use in First Temple Jerusalem. Sarna, Milgrom, and more recently William Schniedewind have all leaned on Ketef Hinnom to argue for the deep antiquity of at least portions of the priestly tradition. The scrolls were pierced and worn — amulets, not manuscripts — which tells us the blessing was a piece of lived piety, not merely liturgical script.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre of Numbers 6 is priestly legislation with an embedded liturgical text. The first unit (vv. 1–21) is casuistic law in the classic "if … then" form: "when either a man or a woman makes a special vow" introduces the protasis; the prohibitions and rituals form the apodosis. Within the legal material is a subordinate case (vv. 9–12) handling defilement — casuistic law nested within casuistic law, a signature priestly literary habit. The second unit (vv. 22–27) is a liturgical formula embedded in narrative frame, what form critics call a *Segensspruch*, a blessing-speech, preserved with its own distinctive structure.

The priestly blessing itself is a tightly crafted poem. Three lines, each addressing YHWH as subject and Israel as second-person-singular object ("you"), each naming two divine acts joined by *wĕ-*: bless-and-keep, make-face-shine-and-be-gracious, lift-countenance-and-give-peace. The line lengths ascend: three Hebrew words, five, seven. Jonathan Sacks observes this ladder of increasing amplitude, the blessing swelling as it progresses. The Tetragrammaton (YHWH) appears three times — once at the head of each line — and the phrase *ʿal-* ("upon") governs the final line. Budd counts fifteen words in total (3 + 5 + 7), a numerical structure that may intentionally echo the fifteen steps of the temple ascent (later Mishnah Middot 2:5). The sound patterns are dense: the guttural *yĕbārekĕkā* / *yišmĕrekā* / *wîḥunnekā* / *wîśem* forms an alliterative chain of second-person suffixes that knit the blessing into a single unified utterance. Translators uniformly note how the Hebrew's compactness defeats English — where Hebrew has two words, English requires five or six.

Numbers 6 is also structurally placed. Mary Douglas, in *In the Wilderness*, argued that Numbers has a ring composition, with ritual legislation balanced around narrative sections. Chapter 6 stands at a hinge: the Nazirite law, which democratizes holiness to the laity, immediately precedes the priestly blessing, which delegates the divine name to priests who pronounce it on the laity. The movement is from lay consecration to priestly mediation — two complementary ways the holy enters common Israel. The chapter's concluding verse (v. 27), "So shall they put my name upon the people of Israel, and I will bless them," functions as a theological summary that Radner in his Brazos commentary calls the "governing legend" of the entire Sinai section: the Name of YHWH rests on Israel, and Israel becomes the bearer of the Name among the nations.

Textually, Numbers 6 is well attested but not without variants. 4QNum-b (4Q27) from Qumran Cave 4 preserves fragments of the chapter with occasional orthographic differences and expansions characteristic of the Samaritan-affiliated tradition. The Septuagint translates *nāzîr* with *hēgismenos* ("the one consecrated") and generally follows the Masoretic Text closely for the blessing, though it adds the divine name at the end of each line ("The LORD bless you, the LORD keep you"), preserving a liturgical expansion. The Samaritan Pentateuch inserts an extended harmonization at v. 22. The Ketef Hinnom silver scrolls offer a shorter form: "May YHWH bless you and keep you; may YHWH make his face shine upon you and grant you peace" — missing the middle clause's "be gracious" and the third line's "lift up his countenance." Whether this represents a shorter Vorlage or an abbreviation for amulet use is debated; Ada Yardeni argues for liturgical compression, Schniedewind for a genuinely alternative textual tradition. Either way, the core blessing is demonstrably pre-exilic.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** The Nazirite institution persists as a living category throughout the Second Temple period. Philo of Alexandria (*On the Special Laws* 1.247–254) reads the Nazirite vow allegorically as the soul's consecration to God, with wine symbolizing sensual pleasure, hair as the crown of virtuous thought, and corpse-contact as moral pollution. Josephus in *Antiquities* 4.72 and 19.294 mentions Nazirites actively in first-century Jerusalem, including Agrippa I paying for the completion sacrifices of destitute Nazirites. The Qumran community's interest is limited — 4Q270 (4QD-e) regulates against false Nazirite vows — but the Temple Scroll (11Q19) treats the completion rituals with expanded detail. The priestly blessing itself was recited daily in the Second Temple by the priests from a platform (Mishnah Tamid 7:2).

The rabbinic reception is massive. Mishnah Nazir is a full tractate of nine chapters devoted entirely to this chapter's legislation — who may vow, for how long, how duration is reckoned, what constitutes defilement, what ambiguous wordings count, the inheritance of offerings, women's and slaves' vows, and the infant Samuel's lifelong Nazirite status. Sifre Numbers and Bamidbar Rabbah provide running midrash. The Bavli Nazir and Bavli Nedarim elaborate extensively. The rabbinic position is divided: Rabbi Shimon held the Nazirite to be a sinner (because the sin offering in v. 11 implies sin in renouncing wine, which God permitted); Rabbi Eleazar held him holy (because the text itself calls him *qādōš*, "holy," in v. 8). This debate anchors the entire Jewish theology of asceticism — whether self-denial honors or insults the Creator. Maimonides in Hilchot Nezirut (Mishneh Torah, Book of Asseveration) preserves the full halakhic apparatus and sides tentatively with Rabbi Shimon, cautioning that unnecessary vows are not praiseworthy, but allowing the Nazirite vow as legitimate when undertaken for a defined spiritual purpose.

Medieval Jewish exegesis is rich. Rashi on v. 2 explains *pelāʾ* as "he separates himself to sanctify himself." Ibn Ezra treats the Nazirite as a voluntary prophet-like figure — "one who draws near to God as the priests draw near." Ramban (Nachmanides) offers the most theologically ambitious reading: the Nazirite, by renouncing wine and letting hair grow, returns to an edenic condition — untamed, uncovenanted-by-priesthood, directly consecrated. He reads the sin offering at the end of the vow not as atonement for the Nazirite's excess asceticism but as sorrow at *leaving* the state of holiness. Sforno adds that the long hair visibly testifies to the Nazirite's vow in public, making holiness witnessable. Nehama Leibowitz, the great modern Israeli teacher, develops Ramban: the Nazirite demonstrates that holiness is accessible to every Jew, not only the priesthood. Jonathan Sacks frames the chapter as Torah's answer to the philosophical question of whether spirituality is for the few or the many — the answer is both, with the priestly blessing extending priestly holiness over the entire community.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Origen's Homilies on Numbers devotes his sixth homily to Numbers 6, reading the Nazirite as a figure of the ascetic Christian — the one who renounces the "wine" of worldly pleasure, lets "grow long" the interior hair of meditation, and avoids the "corpse" of sin. For Origen the shaving at the end of the vow is a figure of laying aside merely exterior religious display once perfection is reached. Jerome follows Origen and adds the John the Baptist connection: Luke 1:15 identifies John as one who "shall drink no wine or strong drink, and he will be filled with the Holy Spirit, even from his mother's womb," a clear Nazirite echo. Augustine does not treat the chapter systematically but uses the priestly blessing (vv. 24–26) pervasively as a Trinitarian text — three divine acts, one divine Name. Cyril of Alexandria's *Glaphyra* on Numbers makes this explicit: the triple *YHWH* of the blessing prefigures the Trinity, and the three clauses map to creation (Father blesses and keeps), redemption (Son makes face shine and is gracious), and sanctification (Spirit lifts countenance and gives peace). This Trinitarian reading becomes standard in the medieval West.

Aquinas addresses the Nazirite directly in *Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 88 (on vows) and q. 186 a. 6 (on religious counsels). For Aquinas, the Nazirite vow is the Old Testament paradigm for all voluntary religious commitments; monastic vows of poverty, chastity, and obedience are in continuity with this Mosaic pattern. The Nazirite demonstrates that God receives free self-consecration above and beyond the covenant's minimum demands. Bonaventure develops the same argument in his *Apologia Pauperum*. The Catholic tradition thus preserves a direct institutional descent: monasticism as Nazirite reborn.

The Reformation breaks this. Luther attacks monastic vows directly in *De Votis Monasticis* (1521), arguing that permanent vows are contrary to Christian liberty and that Numbers 6 describes a *temporary, case-specific* consecration rather than a perpetual state. Calvin in his commentary on Numbers accepts the historical institution but is skeptical of its ongoing Christian applicability: "The ceremony has ended with the shadow." Protestants generally abandon the Nazirite institution as a live category, retaining only the priestly blessing. Wesley, by contrast, draws from the chapter a doctrine of "entire consecration" — a voluntary, total surrender to God — that becomes a Methodist and later Holiness-movement cornerstone.

Critical scholarship in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries reframes everything. Wellhausen treats Numbers 6 as late Priestly invention, a post-exilic projection onto Mosaic history. Noth moderates this but keeps the institution in the exilic period. Milgrom reverses the trend, arguing from internal evidence and from Ketef Hinnom's pre-exilic dating of the priestly blessing that the Nazirite law is ancient. Levine agrees. Dennis Cole in the NAC and Gordon Wenham in the Tyndale commentary restore a pre-exilic Mosaic core. Radner (Brazos) reads the chapter theologically as Christian Scripture: the Nazirite is a figure of Christ, the supreme "separated one," and the priestly blessing is the definitive benediction that the Church now extends in Christ's name.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Jewish and Christian traditions agree on three things. First, the Nazirite is an extraordinary lay consecration — not a clerical office. Second, the priestly blessing is theologically dense: YHWH's face, YHWH's name, and YHWH's peace rest on Israel through priestly mediation. Third, the institution feeds a long afterlife — Samson (Judg 13:5, lifelong by angelic announcement), Samuel (1 Sam 1:11, dedicated by Hannah's vow), John the Baptist (Luke 1:15, implicit), and Paul, who takes a vow in Acts 18:18 and completes the purification of four Nazirites in Acts 21:24–26 — the latter passage demonstrating that the Jerusalem church under James was entirely comfortable with Mosaic Nazirite practice well into the apostolic era. They diverge on whether the institution continues in salvation history. Catholic tradition extends it into monasticism; Reformed tradition allegorizes it into general Christian consecration; Jewish tradition preserves it in minority practice (rare but not extinct — there are modern Nazirites in Israel). The priestly blessing itself converges: it is recited every Sabbath in Ashkenazi synagogues, daily in Sephardi, and forms part of the Catholic Mass and most Protestant benedictions. The blessing has outlived the institution.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is voluntary asceticism a gift to God or an insult to creation?** The Nazirite's three prohibitions each renounce something God pronounced good — wine gladdens the heart (Ps 104:15), the body is God's gift, burial of the dead is a pious duty. Rabbi Shimon's position in the Talmud is blunt: the Nazirite brings a sin offering because he sinned against himself by denying what God permitted. The Reformed critical position — Calvin and behind him much of Protestant thought — agrees with the thrust: asceticism for its own sake is a species of works-righteousness, a denial of creation's goodness. The Jewish majority position (Rabbi Eleazar, Maimonides' reluctant allowance, Ramban's edenic reading) affirms the vow as legitimate provided it is purposeful and time-bound. The Catholic tradition (Aquinas, Bonaventure) treats voluntary renunciation as a higher counsel, not a command, but a genuine good — the Nazirite foreshadows the religious life. The Orthodox tradition treats the Nazirite as a monastic prototype; the entire monastic movement of the desert fathers is explicitly linked to the Nazirite precedent in writers like Cassian. The Wesleyan position treats entire consecration as normative for the sanctified Christian. The Lutheran position, following Luther's 1521 treatise, is skeptical: permanent vows bind the conscience unbiblically; temporary dedications are acceptable but superfluous. Critical scholars (Budd, Levine) tend to read the chapter sociologically — asceticism as boundary-marking rather than theology.

Steelmanning the least intuitive: the Reformed/Lutheran critique has real force. Nothing in the chapter presents the Nazirite as ideal; the text carefully regulates rather than commands the vow; the sin offering at completion troubles any heroic reading; and the biblical Nazirite narratives (Samson's debauchery, Samuel's institutional prophet role, John's prison death) are ambiguous at best. A close reading does not obviously underwrite monasticism or works-righteousness asceticism.

**Where this leaves us:** Numbers 6 regulates rather than mandates the vow, which means the text itself refuses to resolve the tension between creation's goodness and voluntary renunciation. It treats both simultaneously — the vow is real, genuine, and consecrating; the completion brings the Nazirite back to ordinary goodness, wine again permitted. Ascetic traditions read the consecration as paradigmatic; creation-affirming traditions read the return as paradigmatic. Both are in the text.

**Tension 2: Can the divine Name be placed on creatures, and if so, by whom?** The chapter's final verse — "So shall they put my name upon the people of Israel, and I will bless them" — makes the priest a bearer of the divine Name and a channel of divine blessing. Catholic theology reads this as the foundation of sacerdotal mediation: ordained priests place the Name on the people, and the people are blessed ex opere operato. Orthodox theology reads it similarly, with the blessing pronounced at every Divine Liturgy. Reformed theology reads the priesthood typologically: Christ is now the one who places the Name, and the ordained minister is a servant-herald rather than a sacramental priest. Lutheran theology occupies the middle ground: the minister pronounces the blessing in Christ's office, but the efficacy is Christ's, not the minister's. Jewish theology treats the blessing as functionally reserved to kohanim (male descendants of Aaron), pronounced publicly in the synagogue, and the rabbinic tradition has been quite protective of this — non-priests are not to raise their hands in the *dukhan* (priestly blessing posture). The Wesleyan and broader evangelical positions have largely democratized the blessing to anyone speaking benediction. Critical scholars (Budd, Gray) note that v. 27 clearly says YHWH Himself blesses — the priests are conduits, not agents; the power is wholly divine.

Steelmanning the least intuitive: the Jewish position that only kohanim may pronounce the blessing has deep textual warrant. The chapter addresses Aaron and his sons specifically (v. 23), the Ketef Hinnom scrolls were presumably priest-deposited, and the Talmud ties the blessing to Temple and synagogue office. Christian practice, which has any ordained minister or even any Christian pronouncing the words, may be reading Gentile inclusion and priesthood-of-all-believers themes onto a text that does not obviously carry them.

**Where this leaves us:** The blessing's power is God's — v. 27 is unambiguous — but its pronouncement is delegated. Who inherits that delegation after Christ is the theological question that divides communions. Catholic and Orthodox: ordained priests. Reformed and Lutheran: ordained ministers as heralds. Jewish: kohanim. Wesleyan and evangelical: widely democratized. The text forces a decision about continuity and discontinuity between Aaronic and Christian ministry.

**Unresolved question:** If the priestly blessing places the Name on Israel (v. 27), and the Name is Jesus (Phil 2:9, Matt 28:19), then what precisely does it mean to say the Church now *bears* that Name, and what happens when the Church blesses the world in that Name — is the Church priest, prophet, or both?

## The Thread

The chapter's deepest cross-reference is Luke 1:15, where the angel tells Zechariah — a serving priest — that his son John "will drink no wine or strong drink" and will be filled with the Spirit from his mother's womb. John is a Nazirite-by-birth like Samson and Samuel, and the announcement comes to a priest who happens to be pronouncing the Numbers 6 blessing at that very incense service. The blessing and the Nazirite converge in a single moment.

Second: Acts 21:23–26, where James asks Paul to pay for four Nazirites' completion offerings. This is the apostolic church, a generation after the resurrection, still funding Numbers 6 rituals at the Second Temple. The early Jerusalem church did not read the Mosaic cultic law as abolished; it read it as completed in Christ and still available to those who wished to observe it.

Third: 2 Corinthians 3:18, "we all, with unveiled face, beholding the glory of the Lord, are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another." Paul is reading the Numbers 6 priestly blessing Christologically — YHWH's face shining on us — and extending it beyond Israel to the Church. The face of the LORD is the face of Jesus, and the blessing is now universal.

Fourth: Revelation 22:4, the final vision of the redeemed: "they will see his face, and his name will be on their foreheads." This is the eschatological fulfillment of Numbers 6:25 ("make his face shine upon you") and 6:27 ("put my name upon the people"). The priestly blessing reaches its completion in the beatific vision.
