# 1 Samuel 17: Goliath, the Valley of Elah, and the Most Famous Textual Crux in the Hebrew Bible

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter unfolds along a precise geographical seam: the **Valley of Elah** (*Emek ha-Elah*, עֵמֶק הָאֵלָה), a broad east-west wadi cutting through the Shephelah from the coastal plain inland toward the Judean hill country. The valley's modern Hebrew name preserves the ancient one (*elah* = terebinth tree, still scattered across the slopes). Anyone standing today at the floor of the valley between Tel Azekah on the north and the ruined town of Socoh on the south will see exactly the topography the narrator describes (v. 3): two facing ridge lines with a dry streambed between, a natural amphitheater for confrontation. The site is approximately fifteen miles southwest of Jerusalem and roughly twenty miles inland from the Philistine pentapolis. The strategic logic is transparent: control of the Elah Valley meant control of one of the few corridors into the Judean uplands.

This geographical specificity has become the focus of intense recent archaeology. **Khirbet Qeiyafa**, excavated by Yosef Garfinkel of Hebrew University from 2007 to 2013, sits on the northern ridge directly overlooking the valley floor at the precise location demanded by v. 1. Garfinkel dates the casemate-walled fortress to the late eleventh and early tenth century BCE on the basis of carbon-14 from olive pits and a coherent ceramic horizon, and he identifies it as a Judahite frontier fortress facing Philistine Gath. The identification is contested: Israel Finkelstein and Aren Maeir argue the site could be Canaanite or Philistine, and the chronological precision required to call it specifically "Davidic" is debated. Garfinkel proposes the site is biblical Sha'arayim (cf. v. 52), reading the dual ending as referring to the fortress's two gates—one of the few such double-gated Iron IIA sites known. The **Qeiyafa ostracon**, found in 2008, is a five-line inscription in proto-Canaanite script whose decipherment is still disputed; Émile Puech and Garfinkel read it as a Hebrew text with social-justice content, which would push attested Hebrew literacy back into the period of the chapter. Christopher Rollston is more cautious about the language identification. Whatever the verdict, Qeiyafa establishes that the Elah corridor was militarily contested in the chapter's reconstructed period.

**Tell es-Safi/Gath**, excavated since 1996 by Aren Maeir, is the home city of Goliath. The site is enormous—forty-five hectares in the Iron IIA, making it the largest Philistine city of the period and probably the largest urban site in the southern Levant outside Egypt at that time. The 2005 discovery of a horned altar with two horns (rather than the four standard in Israelite worship) and the 2010 discovery of an Iron IIA siege trench (the earliest known siege earthwork in the Levant) confirm Gath's significance. Most striking: a tenth-century ostracon from Gath bearing two non-Semitic personal names—**ALWT** and **WLT**—linguistically related to "Goliath" (*Golyat*, גָּלְיָת). The names are probably Anatolian or Aegean in origin, consistent with the broader hypothesis that the Philistines were a Sea People of western Anatolian or Mycenaean origin who arrived during the Bronze Age collapse around 1175 BCE. The Goliath name-cluster is therefore real, attested at Gath, and the chronological window fits.

The material culture of Goliath's armor (vv. 5–7) is precise and culturally telling. Scale armor (*shiryon qaskaśim*, שִׁרְיוֹן קַשְׂקַשִּׂים, "armor of scales") is well attested in Late Bronze and early Iron Age contexts—Mycenaean and Neo-Hittite parallels include the Dendra panoply and depictions on the Hittite king Šuppiluliuma II's reliefs. The "weaver's beam" simile for the spear shaft (v. 7) is the signature of the *qōrôt 'ōrgîm* technology—heavy weighted javelins of the type known from Achaean and later Greek contexts (the Greek *amentum* or throwing-loop javelin). The greaves on the legs (*mitschot*, מִצְחוֹת, v. 6) are not standard Levantine equipment but are characteristic of Aegean and Greek hoplite armor. **Goliath is dressed like a Mycenaean.** The narrator is precisely depicting a culturally alien warrior, equipped in a tradition foreign to the Israelite hill country. The bronze head weight—five thousand shekels—is roughly fifty-seven kilograms (125 pounds), which strains physical credibility; the iron spearhead at six hundred shekels is closer to seven kilograms, still substantial. The MT numbers may be deliberately heroic-epic, not literal.

Three Hebrew terms repay attention. First, **Goliath's height "six cubits and a span"** in MT (v. 4) is roughly nine feet nine inches; LXX and 4QSam-a read "four cubits and a span"—about six feet nine inches—still tall but not gigantic, well within the range of attested unusually large skeletons. The textual difference is materially significant: MT makes Goliath a giant in the heroic mode, LXX makes him a credibly large warrior. **Clyde Billington** and others have argued the LXX reading is original. Second, *cherpah* (חֶרְפָּה, "reproach," v. 26) is the key theological term: Goliath's challenge is not a military problem but a *cherpah* against Israel and against YHWH. The verb *charaph* (חָרַף), "to defy/insult," is YHWH-language; Goliath has unwittingly committed sacrilege. Third, **'arelim** (עֲרֵלִים, "uncircumcised," vv. 26, 36)—David's chosen epithet for Goliath—activates the boundary marker that separates Israel from the Philistines. Circumcision was practiced by most regional peoples (Egyptians, Edomites, Moabites, Ammonites) but conspicuously not by the Philistines, the Sea People newcomers. The slur is ethnically and theologically loaded: Goliath is outside the covenant community in a literal bodily sense.

The **single-combat convention** that drives the chapter's plot has Hittite and Greek parallels. The Hittite text known as the **Apology of Hattušili III** describes a champion combat in the thirteenth century BCE; the Iliad's combats between Paris and Menelaus (book 3) and Hector and Ajax (book 7) follow the same form. Roland de Vaux (*Les Institutions de l'Ancien Testament*) catalogues the convention. The principle: rather than commit two armies to costly engagement, each side sends a single warrior whose duel determines the outcome. The convention saves blood when both sides accept it, but its acceptance depends on cultural prestige norms that the narrative shows fraying—the Israelites are paralyzed not because they reject the convention but because Saul, the only Israelite of comparable physical stature (1 Sam 9:2), refuses to step forward.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

This chapter is the single most famous textual crux in the Hebrew Bible. The Greek Septuagint (LXX-B, the Vaticanus form) is approximately 45% shorter than the Masoretic Text, omitting verses 12–31, 41, 50, and 55–58. The omitted material includes: David's family background and trip from Bethlehem (vv. 12–31), the description of Goliath's approach with his shield-bearer (v. 41), the summary statement that David prevailed without a sword (v. 50), and the bizarre concluding scene where Saul does not recognize David despite having taken him into his service in chapter 16 (vv. 55–58). Without the omitted material, the chapter reads as a continuous, coherent battle narrative; with it, the chapter contains internal contradictions that have troubled readers since antiquity.

The contradictions are real. In 16:18 David is described as "a man of valor, a man of war"; in 17:33 Saul calls him "but a youth" with no military experience. In 16:21–22 David is Saul's armor-bearer and personal favorite; in 17:55–58 Saul does not even recognize him. In 16:14–23 David is summoned to court because of his musical skill; in 17 he is a shepherd unknown to the king. The LXX omissions remove all of these contradictions cleanly. **The Qumran scroll 4QSam-a**, dating to the late second or early first century BCE, preserves fragments that align with the LXX form rather than the MT, demonstrating that a shorter Hebrew Vorlage was in circulation in the Second Temple period.

Two main critical positions have crystallized. **Position A—the LXX preserves the older, shorter text** (championed by Wellhausen 1871, refined by Stoebe, advocated by Tov, McCarter, and Lust): The MT chapter is composite, an editorial weaving together of two originally independent David-and-Goliath narratives. The shorter LXX form preserves one of these source narratives in approximately its original state; the MT has incorporated a second tradition (the "David from Bethlehem" version, vv. 12–31, 55–58), leaving the contradictions visible. Tov's textual-critical work at Hebrew University and McCarter's Anchor Bible commentary both argue this position with full apparatus. **Position B—the LXX is a deliberate abridgment of a longer original** (championed by Dominique Barthélemy, Stephen Pisano, David Gooding, and the so-called "Hebrew University" scholars resistant to LXX priority): The MT preserves the original with all its narrative tensions, which were tolerated by the Hebrew tradition and softened by the Greek translator who edited for narrative coherence. Pisano's *Additions or Omissions in the Books of Samuel* (1984) provides the strongest case for the abridgment hypothesis.

The 4QSam-a evidence has shifted the balance toward Position A in mainstream textual criticism: a Hebrew Vorlage close to the LXX shape is now demonstrably attested, not merely hypothesized. But Position B is not dead; the question of whether 4QSam-a represents a textual tradition older than MT or merely a parallel tradition is genuinely contested, and Pisano-style readers continue to argue that the LXX translator could have abridged a longer Hebrew. Emanuel Tov's *Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible* (3rd ed., 2012) is the standard methodological treatment.

A second textual problem haunts the chapter: **2 Samuel 21:19** attributes the killing of Goliath to **Elhanan son of Jaare-oregim of Bethlehem**. The Hebrew is unambiguous: "Elhanan the son of Jaare-oregim, the Bethlehemite, struck down Goliath the Gittite, the shaft of whose spear was like a weaver's beam." 1 Chronicles 20:5, written later, harmonizes the difficulty by making Elhanan kill "Lahmi the brother of Goliath." The Chronicler's reading is almost certainly secondary, achieved by reparsing *bet ha-lachmi* ("the Bethlehemite") into *Lachmi* (a name) and supplying the explanatory "brother of." The straightforward reading is that two different traditions about who killed Goliath circulated, and one was attached to David secondarily. McCarter notes that the David tradition may have absorbed an originally Elhanan story, and Halpern in *David's Secret Demons* makes the absorption central to his apologetic reading: David's official biography needed a giant-killing.

The literary form of the MT chapter is a **heroic combat narrative** with strong folktale architecture: youthful hero, derisive opponent, ineffective elder authority (Saul), comic equipment scene (the oversized armor), unconventional weapon (sling), decisive single blow, head-trophy. Robert Alter, in *The David Story*, draws particular attention to the speech-act asymmetry: Goliath roars, curses, threatens; David speaks theology. The contrast is the chapter's central rhetorical engine. Sternberg analyzes the careful management of point of view—we see Goliath through Israelite eyes (terror), through David's eyes (a creature like a lion or bear), and through the narrator's eyes (his armor catalogued in obsessive detail). Fokkelman's literary commentary identifies a careful chiastic structure centered on David's theological speech in vv. 45–47.

Devices to mark: **the forty days** (v. 16) is a conventional period (cf. flood narrative, Moses on Sinai, Elijah's journey, Jesus's wilderness) signaling completed testing. **The "five smooth stones"** (v. 40) has spawned innumerable allegorical readings; the simpler reading is that David carries ammunition for backup shots. **The sling** itself was a serious military weapon: Judges 20:16 describes seven hundred Benjaminite slingers who could "sling a stone at a hair and not miss." Recent ballistics studies by Eitan Hirsch and others, drawing on military experiments with reconstructed ancient slings, calculate that an experienced slinger could deliver a stone at over thirty meters per second—comparable to a modern handgun bullet at the muzzle. A frontal head impact at that velocity, on an unarmored area (Goliath's helmet covered the crown but probably not the forehead), would concuss or kill.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc forged in this chapter is Israel's claim that YHWH wins his own battles through unlikely instruments. The chapter is cited or echoed across the Tanakh and the New Testament: Psalm 144:10 ("who saves David from the cruel sword"), 1 Maccabees 4:30 ("you crushed the attack of the mighty warrior by the hand of your servant David"), Sirach 47:4–7 (Ben Sira's panegyric), and the New Testament's repeated typology of Christ as the unexpected combatant. Hebrews 11:32–34 gathers David among the heroes "who through faith conquered kingdoms."

**Jewish interpretive trajectory**: **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 6.171–192) retells the chapter at length, harmonizing the contradictions by adopting essentially the MT sequence but smoothing the Saul-recognition problem. Josephus emphasizes David's piety and downplays Goliath's curse. The rabbinic tradition is fascinated by Goliath's lineage. **Sotah 42b** identifies Goliath as a son of Orpah (Ruth's sister-in-law who returned to Moab), making him in effect Ruth's nephew and a Davidic kinsman—the cosmic balance is restored when Ruth's descendant kills Orpah's son. The midrashic genealogy is exegetical play, not historical claim, but it captures the rabbinic taste for pulling figures into family relations. **Rashi** glosses v. 4's "champion" (*ish ha-benayim*, אִישׁ הַבֵּנַיִם, "man of the between") as one who fights in the gap between two armies, and treats Goliath's height in the MT form without softening. **Radak** addresses the Saul-doesn't-recognize-David problem by suggesting Saul knew David but did not know Jesse, his father; the question is genealogical, not personal. **Ralbag** rationalizes: David's appearance had changed during his time away from court, and the king reasonably needed clarification of his lineage. **Abarbanel**'s commentary on chapter 17 is particularly rich. He treats the chapter politically, focusing on Saul's failure of leadership: as the man chosen for his stature, Saul should have been the natural champion against Goliath; his refusal to step forward is the public manifestation of his rejection in chapter 15. Abarbanel reads David's victory not as a private heroism but as a public investiture—the people see that YHWH is with David in the way YHWH is no longer with Saul, and the political transfer begins. **Zornberg** treats David's refusal of Saul's armor as a refusal of the inherited self: David must fight as he is, not as the king's mimic. **Zakovitch** unpacks the doublet structure showing how 1 Sam 17 has absorbed an Elhanan tradition. **Garsiel** maps the chapter's chiasm and the careful narrative management of David's introduction, treating the doublet not as a problem but as a deliberate retelling.

**Christian interpretive trajectory**: **Origen**, in his *Homilies*, allegorizes Goliath as the devil and the five stones as the five books of Moses—the foundational typological reading that will dominate medieval Christian commentary. **Augustine**, in commenting on Psalms 143–144, treats David as a type of Christ contending with the devil, and the felled Goliath as Satan defeated at the cross. **Ambrose**, in *De Officiis Ministrorum* book 1, makes David's refusal of Saul's armor the paradigm for ministerial humility: the Christian leader does not put on borrowed authority but acts in the strength God has given. Ambrose specifically uses David here as the model for episcopal courage. **Chrysostom**, preaching on this chapter to the Antiochene congregation, draws moral conclusions about youthful piety: David's confidence is not bravado but theological clarity. **Gregory the Great**, in the *Moralia* on Job, reads David's combat as the soul's struggle against pride, with Goliath as the prideful self that must be felled by the stone of contrition.

**Reformation**: **Calvin**, in his *Sermons on 1 Samuel*, lingers on v. 47 ("the battle is the LORD's"). For Calvin this is the chapter's theological center: the entire elaborate combat exists so that the LORD's salvation might be visibly distinguished from human armament. Calvin reads David's speech as the model Christian profession of faith—what matters is not technique but who the deliverance is from. Luther in *Lectures on Genesis* and elsewhere treats David's confidence as the model of *fiducia*—the trust that does not calculate odds but rests in the promise.

**Critical**: Wellhausen first articulated the doublet hypothesis. McCarter's Anchor Bible commentary remains the standard critical treatment; he reads the MT as composite and provides a parallel-column comparison of MT and LXX. Klein in WBC catalogues the apparatus exhaustively. Tov's *Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible* uses the chapter as the textbook case for evaluating major MT/LXX divergence. Halpern in *David's Secret Demons* reads the chapter as ideological apologia: the giant-killing narrative is constructed to provide David with a foundational heroic moment that legitimates his rise. Steven McKenzie in *King David: A Biography* (2000) treats the historical David as a more morally compromised figure whose foundational combat may be a later embellishment. Israel Finkelstein in *David and Solomon* (2006, with Silberman) reads the entire David tradition as a Josianic-era construction projecting a great Davidic past for ideological purposes.

**Contemporary**: Brueggemann reads v. 47 as the theological pivot: "the battle is the LORD's" is the criterion against which all subsequent Davidic warfare must be measured, and which David himself will violate. Polzin treats the chapter as the narrator's setup of the David-Saul comparison: David acts theologically, Saul cowers; David trusts, Saul calculates. Eugene Peterson, in *Leap over a Wall*, reads the chapter as a meditation on the difference between adolescent confidence and theological courage, against the typical American sermonic moralization. **Walter Moberly** has a careful theological reading of David's speech in v. 45–47 as the chapter's true climax—the verbal proclamation matters more than the physical victory. **Davis** and **Goldingay** offer accessible expository treatments. **Feminist readings** (Exum, Trible, Bal) note the absence of women and the masculine economy of honor; **Mieke Bal** treats the chapter as a study in the construction of heroic masculinity through the spectacle of body-against-body combat. **Susan Niditch**, in *War in the Hebrew Bible* (1993), categorizes Goliath's combat under the "ban of utter destruction" theology while noting its single-combat reduction of total-war ideology.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**First tension: How is YHWH's victory related to David's skill?** The chapter insists the battle is the LORD's (v. 47), and yet David is depicted as a competent slinger who chooses good ammunition and aims for an exposed point.

*Position 1 (radical divine sovereignty, classical Reformed):* David's skill is itself a gift; the entire causal chain from his shepherd's training to the stone's trajectory is YHWH's working. The "battle is the LORD's" formula evacuates any space for human agency as ultimate cause. David's competence is real but instrumental.

*Position 2 (cooperative providence, scholastic):* YHWH's working operates through David's prudence, courage, and skill, not despite them. David is a real moral and physical agent whose excellences are the means by which YHWH wins. The chapter is not anti-skill; it is anti-the-wrong-skill (Saul's armor, Goliath's bronze panoply).

*Position 3 (literary-rhetorical, Brueggemann):* The chapter is making a polemical point against a specific kind of military theology, the kind that locates security in armament. The "battle is the LORD's" formula is meant to disrupt the politics of bronze-and-iron, not to deny human agency in any abstract sense. The text's positive theology is about *which* preparation counts, not whether preparation counts.

Steelman of 3: The narrator carefully shows David choosing the right weapon for his actual capacities (the sling he has trained on, not the sword he has not), and refusing the armor that would make him fight as someone he is not. This is not a counsel of unpreparedness; it is a counsel of fitting one's preparation to one's vocation. The chapter is theologically subtle: YHWH's victory does not bypass David's skill, it operates through David's refusal to substitute someone else's skill for his own.

*Where this leaves us:* Position 3 captures the chapter's literary structure most precisely; Position 2 captures its anthropological seriousness; Position 1 captures the boundary at which the chapter wants to leave the reader (the final causation lies with YHWH). The pastoral implication is unstable: the chapter neither commends prudent military preparation nor condemns it; it commends prudent vocational fidelity, which in David's case happens to include sling training.

**Second tension: The status of the conquered.** Goliath is a *cherpah*-bearer who must die; his army is to be food for birds (v. 46). What is the theology of the herem-style annihilation language?

*Position 1 (covenantal-canonical, classical):* Goliath represents the structural threat to YHWH's people and YHWH's name; the slaughter is the legitimate eschatological judgment in microcosm. The death of the giant is the type of the death of every uncircumcised reproach against the covenant.

*Position 2 (apologetic-historical, McCarter, Halpern):* The annihilation language is apologetic intensification, not literal program. The narrator wants to justify David's combat as a comprehensive victory over the Philistine threat, but the actual chapter shows a routed army fleeing—not a slaughtered one (vv. 51–53). The language is heroic-epic, not military instruction.

*Position 3 (critical, Niditch, Crouch):* The chapter participates in ancient Near Eastern total-war ideology—the same ideology visible in Assyrian royal inscriptions—and Christian and Jewish readers must reckon with the violence of the tradition we have inherited. The theological work is not to defend the language but to read it as evidence of the ancient war-imagination from which our scriptures have not been preserved.

Steelman of 3: The chapter's "I will give the dead bodies of the host of the Philistines this day to the birds" (v. 46) is the precise rhetoric Assyrian kings used in their annals (cf. the inscriptions of Ashurnasirpal II and Sennacherib). Recognizing the rhetorical convention does not absolve us of reading it; it tells us where in the ancient war-imagination this chapter is located.

*Where this leaves us:* The first two positions can hold the chapter's specifically theological work (the *cherpah* is real, YHWH's vindication is real, the apologetic intensification is recognizable as such), but Position 3 names what the first two often suppress: the language of the chapter is not unique to Israel and not free of the ideologies that surrounded Israel. Ethical theological reading must do all three things at once.

*Unresolved question:* If David's victory is "so that all the earth may know that there is a God in Israel" (v. 46), what is the relationship between battlefield outcomes and theological proof? The chapter assumes the victory will be epistemically probative; the rest of the Old Testament shows YHWH's people losing battles regularly. Does the chapter offer an answerable theology of when victory teaches what?

## The Thread

David's chosen epithet for Goliath, "uncircumcised" (vv. 26, 36), will return as the signature insult of David's Philistine campaigns—it appears in Saul's bride-price demand in 18:25 (the two hundred foreskins) and in David's lament over Saul in 2 Sam 1:20 ("lest the daughters of the uncircumcised exult"). The "battle is the LORD's" formula (v. 47) returns in 2 Chronicles 20:15 (Jehoshaphat against the Moabite coalition) and is implicit in the Pauline armor of God passage (Eph 6:10–17). David's bringing Goliath's head to Jerusalem (v. 54) is anachronistic—Jerusalem is still a Jebusite city until 2 Sam 5—and is one of the strongest internal indications that the chapter has been edited from a later perspective. Cross-references: Genesis 14 (Abraham's combat with the four kings, the prior heroic-combat type); Judges 7 (Gideon's three hundred, the prior unlikely-instrument type); 2 Samuel 21:15–22 (the four giant-killings, where Elhanan kills "Goliath" and the textual problem becomes acute); Psalm 144:10 ("who saves David from the cruel sword"); 1 Maccabees 4 (Judah Maccabee's deliberate Davidic typology against Antiochid forces); Hebrews 11:32–34 (David among the heroes of faith). Flag forward: chapter 18 will trace the immediate political consequences—Jonathan's covenant, Saul's spear, the women's victory song—the chapter that turns a battlefield triumph into a court crisis.
