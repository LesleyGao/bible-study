# Song of Solomon 4 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 4 contains the Song's first fully developed *waṣf* — the descriptive love-poem that catalogues the body of the beloved part by part. The form is named (with a touch of anachronism) after the later Arabic *waṣf* poetic genre, but the practice is older than Arabic poetry by at least two millennia. **Egyptian love songs from the Chester Beatty Papyri** (c. 1300 BC) and the Cairo Love Songs include extended catalogues of the beloved's body — eyes, hair, lips, breasts, thighs — and these are the closest formal antecedents to chapters 4, 5, and 7 of the Song. Othmar Keel's commentary documents the parallels in detail, and Michael Fox's *The Song of Songs and the Ancient Egyptian Love Songs* remains the standard reference. The man's *waṣf* of the woman fills 4:1–7; he then turns to direct address (4:8–11) and finally to the famous *garden locked* metaphor (4:12–15). The chapter closes with the woman's two-line answer in 4:16, in which she invites him into the garden.

The opening Hebrew *hinach yafah ra'yati hinach yafah* (הִנָּךְ יָפָה רַעְיָתִי הִנָּךְ יָפָה) — "behold, you are beautiful, my love, behold, you are beautiful" — repeats the formula from 1:15, this time at the head of an extended catalogue. *Yafah ra'yati* (יָפָה רַעְיָתִי), "my beautiful one, my love," is the man's signature address to her throughout the Song. The doubled *hinach* (behold!) is a Hebrew exclamatory intensifier; the man is not describing, he is exclaiming.

The catalogue itself moves from head down, ending at the breasts in 4:5. *Eyes like doves* — the dove (*yonah*) recurs through the Song as an image both of beauty and of the gentleness of the beloved. *Hair like a flock of goats leaping down the slopes of Gilead* — the long-haired Mediterranean goats are typically dark, glossy, in moving streams down the hillside. *Teeth like a flock of shorn ewes* — newly washed, white, paired ("each bearing twins, none missing its young," 4:2), evoking the symmetry of paired upper and lower teeth. *Lips like a scarlet thread* — the dye-thread (*chut ha-shani*) was a costly Mediterranean import, the same dyestuff used for high-priestly garments. *Cheeks like halves of a pomegranate* — the rosy-pinkish blush of the inner pomegranate flesh. *Neck like the tower of David* with shields — the architectural simile evokes a Jerusalem-fortification image; the meaning may be the woman's noble bearing, or the necklaces stacked in rows. *Breasts like two fawns, twins of a gazelle, grazing among the lilies* — the gazelle imagery returns, here in pastoral feminine form; the comparison emphasizes softness, paired symmetry, and the vulnerability of young animals.

The *waṣf* is, by ANE convention, more *associative* than *anatomical*. Marvin Pope and Othmar Keel both insist that the similes are not direct visual comparisons (the woman's neck is not literally tower-shaped, her teeth are not literally sheep-shaped) but evocative associations meant to fire imagination. Robert Alter renders the genre in his *Strong As Death Is Love* commentary as "rhapsody"; the function is praise, not anatomy.

The geographical references in 4:8 — *Lebanon, Amana, Senir, Hermon* — name the high northern mountains of the Levant. Hermon (*chermon*) is the great snowcapped mountain northeast of the Sea of Galilee, sacred in Canaanite religion; Senir is the Amorite name for the same range; Amana is a peak in the Anti-Lebanon. The "dens of lions" and "mountains of leopards" (4:8) are not metaphorical: Levantine lions and leopards survived into the early Iron Age and are attested in Mesopotamian and biblical hunting narratives.

The spice catalogue of 4:13–14 is the most extensive in the Song: *henna* (*kofer*), *nard* (*nerd*), *saffron* (*karkom*), *calamus* (*qaneh*), *cinnamon* (*qinnamon*), *frankincense* (*levonah*), *myrrh* (*mor*), *aloes* (*ahalot*). These were the international luxury commodities of the ancient world; nard came from the Himalayas, cinnamon from South Asia (probably via Indian traders), aloes from East Africa or India, frankincense and myrrh from southern Arabia, saffron from Persia. The catalogue reads like the manifest of a Persian-period or Hellenistic luxury merchant. Most of these substances were used as perfumes, anointing oils, and pharmaceuticals; many had specifically erotic associations in ANE love-poetry.

The chapter's pivotal Hebrew phrase — the most-discussed in the chapter — is in 4:12: *gan na'ul achoti chalah, gal na'ul ma'yan chatum* (גַּן נָעוּל אֲחֹתִי כַלָּה, גַּל נָעוּל מַעְיָן חָתוּם), "a garden locked is my sister, my bride, a spring locked, a fountain sealed." *Gan na'ul* is the masculine *gan* (garden) plus the passive *na'ul* (locked, bolted). The image is at once architectural (a walled garden with a locked door) and bodily — a meditation on female sexuality as enclosed, protected, and hers to open. Marvin Pope's Anchor Bible commentary, Othmar Keel's Continental Commentary, and Phyllis Trible's *God and the Rhetoric of Sexuality* all read the *gan na'ul* as a controlled, dignified erotic image of the woman's body and sexuality. The woman herself answers in 4:16 by inviting the wind to blow on the garden and inviting her beloved to "come to his garden" — *yavo dodi le-gano* (יָבֹא דוֹדִי לְגַנּוֹ) — and to eat its choicest fruits. The reciprocity is striking: he calls her a locked garden, and she opens it to him.

## Lens 2 — Literature

Chapter 4 is the most formally unified passage in the Song. Where chapters 1–3 oscillate among voices, 4:1–15 is sustained male speech to the female beloved, with her single brief response in 4:16. The chapter is built in three movements: (1) the body-*waṣf* of 4:1–7; (2) the invitation to come down from Lebanon and the praise of the woman's love itself in 4:8–11; (3) the garden-and-spice metaphor of 4:12–15. The woman's two-line response in 4:16 closes the unit and bridges into chapter 5.

The *waṣf* form will return at 5:10–16 (the woman's *waṣf* of the man), 6:4–10 (the man again), and 7:1–9 (the man's most famous *waṣf*, moving foot-to-head). Chapter 4 establishes the form. Cheryl Exum, Roland Murphy, and Robert Alter have all written on the Song's *waṣf* tradition; the closest comparative materials are the Egyptian poems and the Sumerian Inanna-Dumuzi hymns, but the Song's *waṣf*-poems are tonally distinct — more associative, more pastoral, more ecological than their cultic ANE parallels.

The repeated address *achoti chalah* (אֲחוֹתִי כַלָּה), "my sister, my bride," in 4:9, 4:10, 4:11, 4:12, and 5:1, is a deliberate formula. The *sister-bride* terminology is paralleled in **Egyptian love poetry**, where *sister* (*sn.t*) is a standard term of endearment between lovers regardless of biological relation; the Egyptian usage establishes that the term is amorous, not incestuous. *Chalah* (bride) marks the betrothal/wedding context. The five-fold repetition of *achoti chalah* across 4:9–5:1 is one of the chapter's structural pillars.

The *gan na'ul* of 4:12 generates an extended garden metaphor that runs through 4:13–15 and 4:16 — orchards, fountains, flowing streams from Lebanon, north and south winds. The metaphor's literary economy is that the woman's body, the garden, and the natural world fold into a single image. The economy is then sealed, in 5:1 (the next chapter's opening verse, which is structurally part of the chapter 4 unit), by the man's "I came to my garden, my sister, my bride" — the consummation announced in pastoral language.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Chapter 4 has been one of the most allegorically active passages in both Jewish and Christian reception, and one of the most controversial in modern interpretive recovery.

**Jewish reception.** The *Targum Shir HaShirim* and *Midrash Shir HaShirim Rabbah* read the *waṣf* of 4:1–7 as Yahweh's praise of Israel's righteous attributes — eyes as the Sanhedrin, teeth as the warriors of Israel, neck as the temple, breasts as Moses and Aaron (or, in some readings, the two tablets of the Law). Rashi standardized these readings. The *gan na'ul* of 4:12 was read as Israel's covenantal fidelity — a people set apart, sealed to their God. The garden-spices were read as the sacrificial system or the prayers of the righteous. Maimonides's more philosophical reading interpreted the *gan na'ul* as the soul's protected interior, the inviolate core of the rational being's relation to God. The mystical Kabbalistic tradition read the chapter as an esoteric description of the divine attributes, with the woman as the *Shekhinah*.

**Christian reception.** Origen's homilies and commentary on the Song treat 4:1–7 as Christ's praise of the Church's virtues, then secondarily as Christ's praise of the individual sanctified soul. The *gan na'ul* became one of Origen's most extended images for the soul's inviolate relation to Christ. Hippolytus extended the reading. **Bernard of Clairvaux** preached on chapter 4 in the central years of his sermon-cycle (Sermons 40 onward); he had not yet reached the chapter's later verses when he died at Sermon 86. Bernard read the *waṣf* as Christ's praise of the contemplative soul, working slowly through each image. The "tower of David with a thousand shields" became, in Bernard, the soul fortified by the merits of Christ; the breasts as twin fawns became the soul's virtues; the *gan na'ul* became the contemplative interior, sealed against the world.

The Catholic Marian tradition adopted the *gan na'ul* as a typological image for the **Virgin Mary**. From the patristic period onward, *hortus conclusus* (the Latin Vulgate's "garden enclosed") became a standard Marian title in liturgy, art, and theology. Medieval Books of Hours frequently illustrated Mary in a walled garden, with the *Annunciation* set within an enclosed garden symbolic of her perpetual virginity. The image fed directly into late-medieval and early-modern Christian art (think of fifteenth-century Flemish paintings).

**John of the Cross's** *Spiritual Canticle* reworks chapter 4's garden imagery into a sustained mystical poem; the bride's invitation to the wind to blow on the garden becomes, in John, the soul's invitation to the Holy Spirit. **Teresa of Avila** in *Interior Castle* uses the locked-garden image for the soul's protected center where God dwells.

**Modern critical recovery.** Phyllis Trible, Marvin Pope, Othmar Keel, Cheryl Exum, Renita Weems, Chana Bloch, and Tremper Longman have all argued — from different theological starting points — that the chapter's eroticism cannot be allegorized away without doing violence to the text. The *waṣf* is a celebration of the woman's body. The *gan na'ul* is a sustained meditation on female sexuality. The garden-and-fountain imagery is deliberately and sensually erotic. To read chapter 4 *only* as Christ-Church or Yahweh-Israel allegory is to lose what the canonical text is doing in its plain sense.

The **New Testament's** bridegroom-Christology draws unmistakably on this love-poetry tradition. Ephesians 5:22–33 takes the marriage of Christ and the Church as the deepest mystery, and the Pauline grammar — Christ loving the Church and giving himself for her, the Church responding in joy — echoes (without citing) the Song's bridegroom-bride dialogue. Revelation 19:7–9 announces the marriage supper of the Lamb; Revelation 21:2 sees the New Jerusalem coming down "as a bride adorned for her husband." John 3:29 puts the bridegroom-rejoicing in John the Baptist's mouth. The Song's theological substrate runs through these texts even when they are not direct citations.

**Modern Christian and Jewish reception of the eroticism.** Pope John Paul II's *Theology of the Body* lectures (1979–1984) treated the Song extensively, and chapter 4 in particular, as the Hebrew Bible's most explicit theological account of the goodness of embodied sexual love within marriage. The Catholic recovery of the literal-erotic reading without abandoning the allegorical-typological tradition has been one of the most theologically consequential moves of the late twentieth century. Christopher West has popularized this work, sometimes controversially.

The reception has been more uneven in Protestant settings. Some streams of evangelical *purity culture* in the 1990s and 2000s simultaneously claimed the Song as a celebration of married sexuality and reduced it to a list of permitted-versus-forbidden behaviors. Renita Weems and Cheryl Exum have written about how the Song has been deployed both to liberate Christian women's sexuality and to police it. The *gan na'ul* of 4:12 has been particularly weaponized in some popular Christian writing as a slogan for premarital virginity, often in ways that ignore both the woman's own agency in opening the garden in 4:16 and the canonical tradition's allegorical readings.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

Chapter 4 is where the Song's literal eroticism becomes most explicit, and where the ancient and modern interpretive traditions are most visibly in tension. Three distinct theological positions, with steelman force:

**Position 1 (literal-erotic, with affirmation of bodily love):** The chapter celebrates the body of the beloved, the goodness of erotic desire, and the sealed-and-then-opened gift of female sexuality, all within the integrity of love. The man's *waṣf* models a way of seeing the beloved with delighted, particular attention; the *gan na'ul* and the woman's "let my beloved come to his garden" model the reciprocity of consensual, joyful sexual love. This is the canon's answer to every shame-tradition that would teach Christians or Jews that the body is dangerous and desire is dirty. Phyllis Trible, Ellen Davis, Tremper Longman, Cheryl Exum, John Paul II's *Theology of the Body*, Christopher West (in his more careful moments) all argue versions of this reading.

**Position 2 (allegorical, with the Christian and Jewish mystical traditions):** The chapter is the love-poem of Christ for his Church, of Yahweh for Israel, of God for the soul. The body-images point beyond themselves to attributes of the people of God, the Church's holiness, the soul's interior. The *gan na'ul* is the mystical interior of the redeemed person, sealed by the Spirit. The Marian *hortus conclusus* extends the allegory to the figure of perfect human responsiveness to God. Origen, Bernard, John of the Cross, Teresa, the Targum, Rashi all argue versions of this reading. To collapse this trajectory is to lose two thousand years of interpretive tradition that took the Song's superlative status with full seriousness.

**Position 3 (both/and, integral reading):** The literal celebration of bodily love and the allegorical reading of God's love are not in competition; they are layered, and the canon requires both. The literal reading without the allegorical reduces the Song to anthology of erotic poetry; the allegorical without the literal flattens the canonical scandal that the Bible places this much body, this much desire, this much female agency at the center of its love-theology. Robert Alter, Ellen Davis, Mark Boda, the post-Vatican-II Catholic position, much of the better recent Protestant scholarship.

The chapter's particular tension — the one chapter 4 puts on the table more sharply than any other passage of the Song — is the **eroticism made explicit**. The man calls the woman a locked garden and then describes her body in spices; she answers by inviting the wind to blow and her lover to come and eat. To pretend this is anything other than a sustained sexual metaphor is to refuse to read the Hebrew. To pretend it is *only* a sexual metaphor is to refuse the canonical and traditional weight the Song has carried for two millennia of synagogue and church.

The honest reckoning is that **shame-traditions, in both Jewish and Christian settings, have often been unable to bear chapter 4**. Some streams of patristic-medieval allegory removed the body from the text; some streams of modern evangelical purity-culture moralism removed the woman's agency. Both moves are textual evasions. The post-Vatican-II Catholic recovery, the modern Jewish literary readings (Alter, the Blochs, Falk), the feminist hermeneutical recovery (Trible, Exum, Weems), and the better recent evangelical readings (Davis, Longman) have together insisted that the canon means what it says: the body is good, desire is good, female sexual agency is good, and the love between God and his people is most aptly figured by exactly this kind of love — embodied, particular, mutual, joyful, sealed and then opened.

The chapter also raises an unavoidable question about the **language of "no flaw" or "altogether beautiful"** in 4:7 — *kullach yafah ra'yati u-mum ein bach* (כֻּלָּךְ יָפָה רַעְיָתִי וּמוּם אֵין בָּךְ). The phrase echoes priestly-sacrificial language (the unblemished offering, *tamim*), and Jewish allegorical readings have leaned into the echo: the bride is acceptable, holy, fit for the divine presence. Christian allegorical readings (Origen, Bernard, Augustine in his Song allusions) read this as the Church being made spotless by Christ — language picked up directly in Ephesians 5:27. The literal reading insists this is the man's ecstatic perception of his beloved, not a clinical medical claim. The both/and reading hears both: human love at its best perceives the beloved as flawless, and divine love makes its beloved actually so.

**Where this leaves us:** Chapter 4 is the Song's most concentrated meditation on the goodness of embodied love and the goodness of God's love for his people, held together in a single sustained metaphor. The man delights in the body of the beloved; the woman seals and then opens her garden to him; the spices flow; the wind blows. To inherit this chapter well is to refuse both the disembodied allegory that strips the Song of its body and the body-only literalism that strips the Song of its theological depth. The canon insists on both.

**Closing question:** If the chapter ends with the woman saying *let my beloved come to his garden, and eat its choicest fruits*, what does it mean that the Bible places this voice — sealed, then willingly opened — at the climax of its meditation on love between persons and between God and his people?
