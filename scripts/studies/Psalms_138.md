# Psalm 138 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 138 carries the superscription *le-David* (לְדָוִד, "of/for/about David"), and its placement is freighted with editorial intentionality. It opens what scholars call the **final Davidic cluster** (Psalms 138-145), the second-to-last collection of Davidic psalms in the Psalter, gathered deliberately near the doxological close of Book V. Whatever the historical relation of these psalms to the figure of David himself — and the question is genuinely contested, with conservatives like Gerald Wilson noting the plausibility of Davidic provenance for individual psalms while critical scholars from Hermann Gunkel onward have read the superscription as a literary-canonical signal rather than a historical claim — the editorial fact remains: the editors of the Psalter wanted the great hallel-and-doxology section (Pss 146-150) to be preceded by *David's* voice, returning one last time to give thanks and to plead.

In Gunkel's form-critical taxonomy, Psalm 138 is a clear **individual thanksgiving** (*Danklied des Einzelnen*, *todah*-psalm). The opening *odekha be-khol-libbi* ("I give you thanks with my whole heart") signals the genre instantly: a worshiper who has cried out and been answered now stands in the temple courts to fulfill a vow of public praise. The internal evidence aligns: vv. 1-3 narrate the answered prayer, vv. 4-6 widen to universal praise, and vv. 7-8 acknowledge ongoing trouble while affirming Yahweh's covenant fidelity. This is not a hymn of pure adoration nor a pure lament; it is the structurally distinct *todah*, the song sung when the deliverance has already arrived and the offering is being brought.

Dating is uncertain. Mowinckel's school placed thanksgiving psalms in pre-exilic temple worship, where individual *todah* offerings (Lev 7:12-15) were accompanied by spoken or sung testimony. Hossfeld and Zenger, by contrast, read 138 as a post-exilic composition: the universalist theme of v. 4 ("all the kings of the earth shall give you thanks") fits the Persian-period horizon when Yahwism was reckoning with the existence of foreign empires that had become, in some prophetic readings, instruments of divine purpose. The phrase *neged elohim* (נֶגֶד אֱלֹהִים, "before the gods") in v. 1 remains the most contested phrase in the psalm, and its interpretation tilts the dating discussion. If *elohim* refers to a divine council of subordinate spiritual beings — the reading defended in different forms by Mark S. Smith, Michael Heiser, and earlier by Mitchell Dahood in the Anchor Bible — the psalm's mythological imagination feels older, drawing on Israel's polemic-against-the-pantheon strand visible in Pss 82, 89, and Deut 32. If *elohim* refers to human rulers (Calvin's reading, building on the Targum's *malakhayya*, "angels," but also tracing through the rabbinic tradition that read *elohim* in Exod 22:28 as judges), the psalm fits comfortably anywhere in the monarchic or post-monarchic period.

The Hebrew vocabulary is rich. *Chesed ve-emet* (חֶסֶד וֶאֱמֶת, "steadfast love and faithfulness," v. 2) is the great covenantal pair, the divine attributes proclaimed at Sinai in Exod 34:6 and echoed throughout the Psalter. *Hirhavtani be-nafshi oz* (הִרְהִבְתַּנִי בְנַפְשִׁי עֹז, v. 3, "my strength of soul you increased," literally "you have made me bold/strong in my soul with strength") uses the rare verb *rhb* (to embolden, to make audacious) — the same root behind *Rahab*, the chaos-monster of Pss 87:4 and 89:10. The text whispers: when Yahweh answered, he poured the strength-of-Rahab into the worshiper's nephesh.

ANE parallels include the Mesopotamian *šuilla* prayers and the Egyptian thanksgiving stelae from Deir el-Medina, where workers narrate personal deliverance from sickness or social disgrace and credit a named deity. The form is not unique to Israel; what is distinctive is the universalizing horizon — not "my god heard me" but "the LORD answered me, and *all the kings of the earth* will hear and sing."

Textually, Psalm 138 is well attested. The Septuagint (LXX 137) renders *neged elohim* as *enantion angelōn* ("before the angels"), which Augustine, the Vulgate, and the medieval Christian tradition will follow. The Targum reads *malakhayya* ("angels/messengers"). The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of this psalm in 11QPsa (the Great Psalms Scroll from Cave 11), where Psalm 138 appears in a different sequence than the Masoretic Text — a reminder that the canonical ordering of the late Davidic cluster was still in flux at Qumran in the late Second Temple period.

## Lens 2: The Literature in Front of Us

The psalm divides cleanly into three strophes of three verses each (vv. 1-3, 4-6, 7-8), a tight architecture that rewards close reading. Robert Lowth's classification of Hebrew parallelism — synonymous, antithetic, synthetic — finds all three modes here. **Synonymous parallelism** dominates v. 2: "I bow down toward your holy temple // and give thanks to your name." **Antithetic parallelism** structures v. 6: "though the LORD is high, he regards the lowly, // but the haughty he knows from afar" — the cosmic-vertical contrast (high/lowly) yielding to the relational-distance contrast (regards/knows from afar). **Synthetic** or constructive parallelism builds v. 7: each line advances the thought without semantic mirroring.

Several literary devices anchor the poem. There is an **inclusio** of personal address: the psalm opens with the singer's *odekha* ("I give you thanks") and closes with the singer's plea *al-tereph ma'ase yadekha* ("do not forsake the work of your hands"). The first-person voice frames the universal middle. There is a **deliberate widening lens**: v. 1 (I), vv. 4-5 (all the kings of the earth), v. 6 (the high and the lowly as cosmic categories), vv. 7-8 (back to I). The structure mimics a pilgrim raising his eyes from his own answered prayer to the global horizon and back — a movement that Walter Brueggemann, in *The Message of the Psalms*, calls the "thanksgiving-becomes-witness" trajectory: private deliverance becomes public testimony becomes cosmic doxology.

The phrase *higdalta al-kol-shimkha imratekha* (הִגְדַּלְתָּ עַל־כָּל־שִׁמְךָ אִמְרָתֶךָ, v. 2c, "you have exalted above all things your name and your word" or, with different construal, "you have exalted your word above all your name") is one of the most syntactically debated lines in the Psalter. The Masoretic accents push toward "above your every name (i.e., your reputation), you have exalted your word." Calvin, Luther, and the Reformers seized on this reading — *the word above the name* — as a theological warrant for *sola scriptura*. Hossfeld and Zenger, more cautious, render it as the doubled object: "you have magnified your word above your whole name," reading both *shem* and *imrah* as Yahweh's self-revelation. The ambiguity is itself poetically generative.

## Lens 3: The Psalm Within the Larger Story

**Book V placement.** Psalm 138 stands at the head of the final Davidic cluster (138-145), which Gerald Wilson in *The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* read as a deliberate canonical arrangement: Book V's structure moves through Pss 107-118 (the "Egyptian Hallel" frame), Ps 119 (the great Torah-meditation pivot), the Songs of Ascents (120-134), the Hallelujah collection (135-136), the exile lament (137), and then *David returns* (138-145) before the final Hallelujah cascade (146-150). David, the figure who opens the Psalter (Pss 1-2 plus the predominantly Davidic Book I), returns at the end to speak the last individual voice before the collective doxology takes over. Psalm 138 is the threshold of that return.

**Jewish trajectory.** The Dead Sea Scrolls evidence (11QPsa) places 138 in a fluid late-Davidic arrangement, suggesting the cluster's editorial coherence was still being settled. *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 138 dwells on the *neged elohim* phrase, reading the *elohim* as the seventy nations' angelic patrons (drawing on Deut 32:8 LXX) — David sings before the heavenly representatives of the nations, anticipating the day when *all* the kings will praise. Rashi follows the Targum's "before the angels"; Ibn Ezra raises the philosophical reading that *elohim* here refers to judges or rulers; Radak weaves both. Modern Jewish scholarship has been particularly drawn to v. 6 ("though the LORD is high, he regards the lowly"): Abraham Joshua Heschel, in *The Prophets*, treats this verse as a paradigmatic statement of divine pathos — God's transcendence not despite but precisely *expressed in* his attention to the lowly. Jon Levenson and Nahum Sarna both note the universalist horizon of vv. 4-5 as one of the Psalter's clearest expressions of the prophetic hope that the nations would come to acknowledge Yahweh. Liturgically, Psalm 138 appears in the Sephardic *Pesukei de-Zimra* tradition and in select Hasidic *tikkun* compilations.

**Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on 137 (Vulgate numbering of MT 138) reads *in conspectu angelorum* ("in the sight of the angels," following the LXX) as the church's praise heard by the heavenly host — the worshiping community joining the seraphim. Cassiodorus's *Expositio Psalmorum* picks up the same angelic framing. Aquinas in his Psalms commentary harmonizes the angelic and judicial readings. Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* zeroes in on v. 2c — *super omne nomen tuum magnificasti eloquium tuum*, "you have magnified your word above your every name" — as a foundational text for the Reformation's elevation of Scripture over tradition. Calvin, characteristically, reads *neged elohim* as "before kings and rulers" and warns against angelological speculation. Among modern critical scholars, Gunkel classifies it as *Danklied*; Mowinckel locates it in cultic *todah* settings; Westermann emphasizes the testimony-genre; Hans-Joachim Kraus reads it as post-exilic; Hossfeld and Zenger highlight its universalist-eschatological horizon; John Goldingay in his three-volume *Psalms* commentary defends a flexible historical horizon; Gordon Wenham in *Psalms as Torah* reads the psalm as ethical formation toward humility; Walter Brueggemann reads it as a "psalm of new orientation" following lament; Patrick Miller emphasizes the psalm's intercessory implications; William P. Brown highlights the cosmic-imaginative widening; and Beth Tanner, in the NICOT volume she co-authored with Nancy deClaissé-Walford and Rolf Jacobson, reads 138 as the inaugural David-return that frames the entire final cluster.

**NT use.** Psalm 138 is not directly cited in the New Testament, but its themes resonate widely: the theme of God regarding the lowly (v. 6) echoes through the Magnificat (Luke 1:48-52), and the universalist horizon of vv. 4-5 underwrites Pauline reflection on the nations' inclusion (Rom 15:9-12).

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent — Tensions, Positions, and Where This Leaves Us

**Tension 1: What does *neged elohim* mean?** The phrase has become a small battleground for how we read Israel's theological imagination.

*Position A: Divine council.* Mark S. Smith (*The Origins of Biblical Monotheism*), Michael Heiser (*The Unseen Realm*), and Mitchell Dahood (*Anchor Bible Psalms III*) read *elohim* as members of Yahweh's heavenly council — the *bene elohim* of Job 1, Ps 82, Deut 32:8 LXX. On this reading, David sings his thanks in the cosmic court, declaring Yahweh's superiority before subordinate divine beings. *Steelman:* the divine-council reading takes seriously the Psalter's polytheistic backdrop; it lets Pss 82, 89, and 138 speak in the same theological idiom rather than forcing harmonization with a fully articulated later monotheism; and it preserves the high-tension drama of Yahweh-among-the-elohim that the Hebrew syntax most naturally suggests.

*Position B: Human rulers / judges.* Calvin, following the rabbinic tradition that read *elohim* as judges (cf. Exod 22:28), takes the phrase as "before the rulers/judges" — David testifies in the public courts of human power. *Steelman:* this reading has rabbinic and patristic precedent, fits the v. 4 mention of "kings of the earth," and preserves the strict-monotheist reading of the Psalter favored by classical Reformed and rabbinic interpreters.

*Position C: Angels.* The LXX, Vulgate, Targum, Augustine, and most medieval Christian commentators read *elohim* as angels. *Steelman:* the angelic reading is the unanimous voice of the early translations; it preserves the heavenly-court resonance without committing to a polytheistic backdrop; and it continues to shape Christian liturgy where Psalm 138 is sung "in the sight of angels."

**Tension 2: The universalist hope.** Verses 4-5 declare that "all the kings of the earth shall give you thanks." Is this a confident eschatological prophecy, a missional hope, or a literary-rhetorical universalism? Christopher Wright (*The Mission of God*) reads it as missionally generative — the psalm provides theological grammar for the church's universalist horizon. Hossfeld and Zenger read it as Persian-period eschatology: the post-exilic community projecting its hope of vindication onto the global stage. Brueggemann reads it as a counter-imperial speech act: precisely *because* foreign kings dominate the worshiper's present, the psalm dares to claim that those kings will, at the eschaton, themselves bow.

**Where this leaves us.** Psalm 138 holds together two postures we usually separate: the intimate ("on the day I called, you answered me") and the cosmic ("all the kings of the earth shall give you thanks"). The intimate is not a private consolation; it is the seed of the cosmic. The cosmic is not an abstract theology; it grows from one person's answered prayer. The psalm refuses the modern split between personal devotion and political-eschatological hope.

The closing line is one of the most quietly devastating in the Psalter: *ma'ase yadekha al-tereph* — "do not forsake the work of your hands." The same hands that *yatzar* the worshiper in the womb (the next psalm will say so) are pleaded with not to abandon what they have made. The psalm of thanksgiving ends not in completed praise but in continuing dependence. The deliverance was real; the trouble is not yet finished.

**Closing question.** When you give thanks for an answered prayer, do you let the gratitude widen — to the kings of the earth, to the divine council, to the cosmic horizon — or does it stay as small as your own relief? Psalm 138 suggests that authentic *todah* refuses to stay private. What would it mean to sing your thanksgiving "before the gods"?
