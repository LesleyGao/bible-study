# Leviticus 22: Unblemished, for My Acceptance

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Leviticus 22 continues the Holiness Code (Lev 17–26), the block that Israel Knohl famously isolated as the work of a distinct "Holiness School" (H) writing in dialogue with, and often editorially over, the earlier Priestly (P) material. The chapter's signature cadence — *ʾănî YHWH mĕqaddiškem*, "I am the LORD who sanctifies you" (vv. 9, 16, 32) — is the stamp of H. Jacob Milgrom's three-volume Anchor Bible commentary (1991–2001) reads H as a late pre-exilic Judahite priestly circle, probably under Hezekiah, aware of prophetic ethics (especially Isaiah) and deliberately extending priestly holiness to the laity. Julius Wellhausen had placed the whole Priestly stratum after the exile; Menahem Haran and Avi Hurvitz answered with linguistic arguments for a pre-exilic core (archaic orthography, absence of Aramaisms), a position largely vindicated by the Dead Sea Scrolls' confirmation of Second Temple familiarity with the text. Knohl's refinement — that H redacted and supplemented P — now commands broad assent.

Politically, the chapter presupposes a functioning sanctuary with a priestly caste dependent on prebends: the *tĕrûmāh* (contributed or "heave" portions), the breast and thigh of peace offerings, firstfruits, tithe of tithes. These are the priests' food (v. 7: *kî laḥmô hûʾ*, "for it is his food"). In a subsistence economy without coinage for most transactions, a priest's household ate what came to the altar. That material fact — priests live off sanctuary income — drives the entire chapter. Who counts as "household"? Who may eat? What condition must the eater, and what condition must the offering, be in?

The material culture is priestly standards. An Israelite *ʿōlāh* (burnt offering) or *šĕlāmîm* (peace offering) had to be *tāmîm* — whole, entire, unblemished. The chapter's vice-list of disqualifying defects (vv. 22–24) is strikingly concrete: *ʿawweret* (blindness), *šābûr* (fracture), *ḥārûṣ* (mutilation/cut), *yabbelet* (wen or wart), *gārāb* (scab), *yallepet* (skin eruption), and crushed, torn, or severed testicles. These are not abstractions; they are the inventory of a shepherd's working knowledge, the things one sees in a flock pen at dusk. Archaeologically, Iron Age Israelite faunal assemblages at sites like Tel Dan, Tel Beersheba, and the City of David confirm that sheep, goats, and cattle dominated sacrificial-adjacent deposits, with sheep typically slaughtered in their second year — consistent with the eighth-day-onward rule in v. 27. The Ketef Hinnom silver amulets (late seventh century BC, Gabriel Barkay) preserve the priestly blessing of Num 6, giving us independent, pre-exilic material evidence that priestly liturgical language was alive on the tongue of lay Jerusalemites before 586.

ANE parallels matter. The Ugaritic KTU 1.40 (the *šaḥr wa-šalim* ritual) and the Hittite instructions to temple officials (CTH 264, edited by Jared Miller) both require physical integrity of offerings and ritual purity of priests; Egyptian temple protocols at Edfu and Dendera likewise mandate that only unblemished bulls bear the sacred stamp. What distinguishes Leviticus is not the principle but its relentless moral framing — the refrain "I am YHWH" (seven times in this chapter) binds physical integrity to covenantal holiness rather than to magical efficacy. Baruch Levine (JPS Leviticus) calls this the "ethicization" of the cult.

Manuscript evidence is unusually rich here. 4QLev-b and 4QLev-d (4Q24, 4Q26) preserve fragments of chs. 21–23 with minor orthographic variants; 11QpaleoLev-a (11Q1), the paleo-Hebrew Leviticus scroll from Qumran Cave 11, covers portions of chs. 22–27 and shows a text very close to the Masoretic. The Samaritan Pentateuch harmonizes v. 28 (the dam-and-young prohibition) with parallel pentateuchal animal laws; the LXX occasionally softens the vice-list with Greek medical vocabulary.

Key Hebrew terms: *qōdeš* / קֹדֶשׁ (holy thing, the sanctified portion); *tāmîm* / תָּמִים (whole, without blemish, the same word used of Noah and of the covenantal ideal in Gen 17:1); *ṭāmēʾ* / טָמֵא (unclean); *kārēt* / כָּרֵת (the dread "cutting off" penalty of v. 3); *ḥillēl* / חִלֵּל (to profane, the verb governing the whole chapter's concern).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is priestly casuistry — case law stitched onto apodictic frames. Verses 1–9 govern the priest's own body (he may not eat holy things while unclean); verses 10–16 govern the priest's household (who among his dependents counts); verses 17–25 govern the offering itself (unblemished); verses 26–30 catch residual rules about animals (eighth-day minimum, no dam-and-young same day, thanksgiving consumed same day); and verses 31–33 close with a theological envelope. The chapter is deliberately concentric: eater and eaten must both be whole. Mary Douglas's structural reading (*Leviticus as Literature*, 1999) highlights how this mirrored symmetry runs through the whole book — the human body, the sacrificial animal, and the tabernacle are isomorphic.

Literary devices: H's signature "I am YHWH" refrain (vv. 2, 3, 8, 9, 16, 30, 31, 32, 33) — Knohl counts these in hundreds across chs. 17–26 and argues they function like a musical *ritornello*. The verb *ḥillēl* ("profane") and its antonym *qiddēš* ("sanctify") braid through the chapter: the priest must not profane what YHWH sanctifies. Note also the inclusio: the chapter opens (v. 2) and closes (v. 32) with "do not profane my holy name." Everything in between is commentary on that single imperative.

A neglected literary feature is the movement in vv. 10–13 through kinship categories — lay person, foreign guest, hired worker, purchased slave, house-born slave, daughter married out, widowed daughter returned. The text is drawing a concentric diagram of the priestly household. Who eats with you? That, and not merely lineage, defines membership. A purchased slave eats; a hired worker does not. The economic and legal categories map precisely onto sacred eligibility. Baruch Levine notes that this is the fullest picture of a priestly *bêt ʾāb* we have in the Torah.

Textual variants: MT v. 22's list uses six defect terms; LXX renders with fewer, and 11QpaleoLev preserves a reading close to MT. V. 23's *śārûaʿ wĕqālûṭ* (usually rendered "part too long or too short") is genuinely obscure — Rashi: an animal with one limb longer than the other; Ibn Ezra: stretched or contracted. Milgrom suggests a congenital deformity distinguishable from acquired mutilation, which is why such an animal may be a freewill offering but not vow-fulfillment.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** In the Second Temple period, Philo (*Special Laws* 1.166–193, 1.260–272) read the unblemished-offering laws allegorically — the blemishes of the soul disqualify one more thoroughly than any bodily defect — while insisting on the literal observance. Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.228–230, 3.278–279) summarized the rules with Hellenistic decorum, stressing priestly dignity for a Roman readership. *Jubilees* 21 repackages them as Abraham's deathbed charge to Isaac, moving the priestly standards back into patriarchal memory. The Temple Scroll (11Q19) columns 52–53 sharpen the dam-and-young rule (v. 28) into a prohibition on slaughter of a pregnant animal and codifies a three-day rather than one-day limit on thanksgiving meat — the Qumran community characteristically raising the bar.

Rabbinic treatment runs through Mishnah and Tosefta Zevachim, Menachot, and especially Chullin (which codifies the dam-and-young rule of v. 28 — *ʾôtô wĕʾet-bĕnô* — as one of the Torah's negative precepts binding even outside the sanctuary, on profane slaughter). Sifra on Leviticus provides a verse-by-verse tannaitic midrash; the Bavli extends it in Zevachim 35b–37a (on eating holy things in uncleanness) and Bekhorot 37a–45b (on disqualifying blemishes, extrapolated to priests in Lev 21 and by analogy here). Rashi on v. 27 explains the eighth-day rule as the minimum for viability (a neonate was considered a *nēpel*, a non-viable, until the eighth day); he links this explicitly to the eighth-day circumcision. Ibn Ezra glosses *śārûaʿ* and *qālûṭ* with cautious philology; Ramban on v. 28 writes one of the classic medieval arguments for divine compassion as the rule's rationale — "Scripture did not permit destructive cruelty, to slaughter the young in the sight of the mother, for animals have great anxiety over this." Maimonides in the *Guide for the Perplexed* (3.48) takes the same line: "this law is meant to teach us the virtue of compassion … for the mother's anguish over her young is the same in all animals as in man." Sforno reads the whole chapter as a training in *hiddûr mitzvah* — beautification of the commandment, offering the best.

Modern Jewish interpreters: Milgrom's commentary (AB vol. 3) is magisterial on the zoology of the defect list and the economics of priestly prebends; Baruch Levine's JPS volume reconstructs the priestly household; Yeshayahu Leibowitz reads the unblemished-offering demand as the categorical refusal of "second-best" in service to God; Jonathan Sacks (in *Covenant and Conversation: Leviticus*) draws from the dam-and-young rule a paradigm of ethical restraint even within permitted activities; Avivah Zornberg and Louis Jacobs probe the psychological weight of *tāmîm* as a whole-person, not only whole-animal, demand.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic readers moved quickly to the unblemished Lamb of 1 Peter 1:19 and Hebrews 9:14 ("Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered himself without blemish to God"). Origen's *Homilies on Leviticus* (12–13) reads ch. 22 christologically and morally — the unblemished offering is Christ; the fit eater is the cleansed soul. Augustine (*Contra Faustum* 18, 22) defends the literal observance as pedagogy for a carnal people, now superseded. Chrysostom's homilies on Hebrews assume the Levitical defect-list as the negative from which Christ's sinlessness is positive. Gregory the Great returns often to *tāmîm* / *immaculatus* as the priest's inner condition.

Medieval Christendom: Aquinas (*ST* I-II q. 102 a. 3–5) treats the ceremonial observances systematically, reading the unblemished animal as signifying Christ's innocence and the priestly purity laws as signifying ministerial fitness — a framework that undergirds Tridentine and post-Tridentine thought on priestly impediments (*irregularitates*). Bernard of Clairvaux in his sermons on the Song and on consecration draws the priest's purity directly from Lev 21–22.

Reformation: Luther's Leviticus lectures (1544) read the chapter evangelically — the law demands the unblemished, the sinner has no such offering, Christ provides what we cannot. Calvin's *Commentary on the Four Last Books of Moses* harmonizes the legal material and handles ch. 22 under the rubric of priestly sanctity, drawing ethical consequences for ministers without reinstituting ceremonial rules. The Reformed tradition's regulative principle — worship limited to what God commands — has a Levitical pedigree here.

Critical modern: Wellhausen's *Prolegomena* (1883) used this block to argue for late P; Martin Noth's ATD *Leviticus* carefully separated legal strata; Brevard Childs's canonical reading folds priestly standards into the one sanctifying work of God; Gordon Wenham's NICOT (1979) is the best accessible Protestant commentary; Jay Sklar's ZECOT and John Hartley's WBC extend Wenham. Leigh Trevaskis and L. Michael Morales (*Who Shall Ascend the Mountain of the Lord?*) read Leviticus as ascent narrative, with ch. 22 guarding the altar-table at its center. Ephraim Radner's Brazos commentary reads the chapter as ecclesiology.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Jewish tradition keeps the unblemished-animal rules live as halakhah; they still govern kosher slaughter's checks for blemish (*ṭĕrêpāh* — the animal torn or fatally defective). The priestly-eating rules are dormant but not dead: the category of *terumah*, holy contribution, resurfaces whenever a Temple economy is imagined. The dam-and-young rule is a binding negative commandment (*lōʾ taʿaśeh*) in every halakhic code. Christian tradition, with few exceptions (Seventh-Day Adventist dietary readings, certain Ethiopian and Syriac Christian kashrut survivals), allegorizes and internalizes the rules — the unblemished offering is Christ, the fit eater is the sanctified believer. Both traditions converge, however, on the chapter's deep grammar: the holy is not infinitely transferable. It has eligibility rules. A community defined by what it eats at the sanctuary table is a community with a boundary — and the rabbis and the church fathers each wrestle with the same question of who sits at that table.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is the unblemished-animal rule about God's aesthetic dignity or about moral pedagogy for the offerer?** Position A (Maimonides, *Guide* 3.46; much of medieval allegory) reads the rule as pedagogical — God does not need unblemished flesh, but the human habit of offering the best trains generosity and counters stinginess. Position B (Nachmanides on Lev 1:9, and in more sophisticated form Milgrom) reads it as cultic realism — YHWH is the great King and the offering is literally royal tribute; one does not send the blind and lame to the imperial court. Position C (Wenham, Radner) reads it as sacramental ontology — the offering participates in the reality it represents; a blemished offering would mis-signify the holy and corrupt the symbolic grammar. The least intuitive steelman is C: in a sacramental register, the physical condition of the sign is not decorative but constitutive. Hebrews 9:14 depends on this — Christ's sinlessness is not a biographical accolade but the condition of efficacious offering. *Where this leaves us:* these are stacked, not opposed. The rule trains the offerer (A), it honors the King (B), and it preserves the symbolic integrity of the offering (C). What it cannot be is indifferent.

**Tension 2: The eligibility rules for eating holy things (vv. 10–13) draw hard lines — slave in, hired worker out; married daughter out, widowed daughter (without child) in. Is this an arbitrary ritual code, a coherent moral economy, or a structure we now judge unjust?** Position A (classical rabbinic and most premodern Christian readers) treats the rules as a coherent economy of household belonging — the purchased slave is a permanent member, the hired worker is a transient, the married daughter has joined another house. Position B (modern historical-critical readers from Wellhausen to present) sees a patriarchal artifact: a woman's eligibility is defined through her attachment to a male head-of-house, and the slave is a property-class whose eating is an extension of the owner's right. Position C (Ephraim Radner, Christopher Wright, and a strand of Jewish feminist readings, e.g., Judith Plaskow's *Standing Again at Sinai*) holds both — the rules are historically embedded and ethically limited by their time, yet they also encode a principle (the holy is tied to covenantal belonging, not to wage-labor or transient presence) that remains generative when the institutions are transformed. The least intuitive steelman is C's double move: the rules are, and are not, the point. *Where this leaves us:* the chapter forces a question modern liberalism answers too quickly — what does it mean to belong to a community deeply enough to eat its holy food? Market exchange (the hired worker) is not enough; only household membership is. That principle survives the passing of its ancient household forms.

*Unresolved question:* If the unblemished-offering rule is about sacramental integrity rather than aesthetic preference, what is the ecclesial analogue? Does it underwrite a high view of eucharistic fitness (1 Cor 11), or — more uncomfortably — does it underwrite historical Christian practices of excluding the disabled from ministry (canonical *irregularitas* in Catholic and some Orthodox traditions), which mapped priestly-body rules onto human priests? The chapter will not let us simply pick the first answer and ignore the second.

## The Thread

1. *Tāmîm* / תָּמִים threads from Gen 6:9 (Noah is *tāmîm*) through Gen 17:1 (Abram: "walk before me and be *tāmîm*") to Lev 22:21 (the peace offering must be *tāmîm*) and on to Ps 15:2 (the one who may ascend God's hill walks *tāmîm*), climaxing in Heb 9:14 (Christ offered himself *amōmos*, LXX's Greek for *tāmîm*). The word binds patriarch, sacrifice, and Messiah.

2. The eighth-day rule for animals (v. 27) rhymes with eighth-day circumcision (Gen 17:12, Lev 12:3). The same liminal week — the move from neonate to full member — governs both. Philo and the rabbis saw this connection; modern readers often miss it.

3. The dam-and-young prohibition (v. 28) is linked by the rabbis to the mother-bird rule (Deut 22:6–7) and, surprisingly, to the thrice-repeated prohibition against boiling a kid in its mother's milk (Ex 23:19, 34:26, Deut 14:21) — a triad of "do not collapse the generative boundary" rules. Jacob Milgrom traces the common logic; Jonathan Sacks extends it to the modern ethics of reverence for generativity itself.

4. The "cutting off" penalty *kārēt* in v. 3 is an eschatological verdict in rabbinic thought (excision from the world to come), but in its earliest priestly usage probably means removal from the cultic community and the lineage. The ambiguity opens into Paul's agonized use of *anathema* in Rom 9:3 — a covenantal exclusion that the apostle is willing to bear for his kin.
