# Isaiah 44 — Go Deeper

Isaiah 44 contains two of the most consequential passages in the entire book. The first is the **idol parody** (44:9–20), the most extended and merciless satire of pagan religious manufacturing in the Hebrew Bible: a man cuts down a tree, burns half of it to roast meat and warm himself, and then bows down to the other half as a god. The second is the explicit **naming of Cyrus** (44:28) — *who says of Cyrus, he is my shepherd, and he shall fulfill all my purpose; saying of Jerusalem, she shall be built, and of the temple, your foundation shall be laid* — the single passage that has done more than any other to push critical scholarship toward the composite-authorship reading of Isaiah, since it requires either an eighth-century prophet naming a Persian king some 150 years before he ruled or a sixth-century exilic prophet recognizing the conqueror at his door. Around these two pillars the chapter weaves promises of restoration, declarations of monotheism, and the most concentrated celebration of Yahweh as creator and redeemer in Second Isaiah.

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Second Isaiah's setting is the late Babylonian exile, around 545–540 BC, just before Cyrus the Persian conquered Babylon in 539 BC and issued his decree allowing exiled peoples to return home. The mainstream-critical consensus (Westermann, Blenkinsopp, Goldingay, Melugin, Tull, R.N. Whybray, James Muilenburg) reads chapters 40–55 as the work of an anonymous exilic prophet writing in Babylon. Conservative-evangelical scholars (Motyer, Oswalt, E.J. Young) defend eighth-century Isaianic authorship by appeal to prophetic foreknowledge. Brevard Childs's canonical-criticism brackets the historical question and reads the book as canonically unified.

This chapter contains **the strongest single argument** for composite authorship in the entire book of Isaiah. In 44:28 Cyrus is named explicitly, decades before any reasonable reading of the historical Isaiah of Jerusalem's lifetime. The historical Isaiah son of Amoz was active during the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah (Isaiah 1:1), roughly 740–700 BC. Cyrus II of Persia was born around 600–590 BC and rose to power in 559 BC. The temporal gap between the historical Isaiah's death (around 700 BC, with traditional martyrdom under Manasseh) and Cyrus's naming as Yahweh's *shepherd* is more than 150 years.

Three positions deserve hearing on this question. The conservative-evangelical position holds that this is a paradigm case of predictive prophecy: God revealed the name of the Persian liberator to Isaiah of Jerusalem 150 years in advance, precisely to demonstrate divine foreknowledge against the false gods of the nations (so Motyer, Oswalt, Young; the rabbinic tradition has parallel readings, e.g. *Yoma* 4a). The mainstream-critical position holds that this is a paradigm case for dating Second Isaiah to the late exilic period: prophets generally speak to their contemporaries, the genre of *trial-speech* against the gods specifically demands prediction-and-fulfillment within knowable historical span, and the natural reading of 44:28 is that the prophet names Cyrus because Cyrus is the conqueror at the door (so Duhm, Westermann, Blenkinsopp, Goldingay; the medieval Jewish commentator Ibn Ezra hinted at this reading in the twelfth century). The canonical-criticism position (Childs) brackets the historical question: whatever the chapter's compositional origin, the synagogue and church received it as Isaiah's, and the canonical shape connects the prediction-of-Cyrus material back to First Isaiah's threatenings and forward to Third Isaiah's restoration struggles.

Steelmanned: a thoughtful evangelical can grant that Second Isaiah may have been composed in the exile while still affirming inspiration; a thoughtful critical scholar can grant the canonical legitimacy of reading the chapter as Isaianic prophecy without surrendering historical method; the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §107–108 and the Reformed *sola scriptura* tradition can both accommodate the critical position because both root inspiration in the church's reception of the canonical text rather than in any specific theory of authorship.

The ancient Near Eastern parallel is the **Cyrus Cylinder** (539–538 BC, British Museum), a clay barrel inscription discovered in 1879 that records Cyrus's conquest of Babylon and his policy of returning displaced peoples and restoring their gods to their home temples. The Cylinder's Cyrus claims to have been chosen by the Babylonian high god Marduk; Second Isaiah's Cyrus is chosen by Yahweh. The historical fact of Cyrus's restoration policy — corroborated by the Cylinder, by Herodotus, by Xenophon's *Cyropaedia*, by the Babylonian Chronicle, and by Ezra 1:1–4 / 2 Chronicles 36:22–23 — vindicates the prophet's claim regardless of how one resolves the dating question.

The Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. *Yəšurûn* (יְשֻׁרוּן), *Jeshurun* (44:2), is a poetic name for Israel meaning roughly *the upright one*; the term appears only four times in the Hebrew Bible (Deuteronomy 32:15, 33:5, 33:26, and Isaiah 44:2), making its use here a deliberate echo of the Mosaic blessing tradition. *Hāʾanî hûʾ* (הַאֲנִי הוּא), *I am he* (44:6), and *ʾanî riʾšôn waʾanî ʾaḥărôn* (אֲנִי רִאשׁוֹן וַאֲנִי אַחֲרוֹן), *I am the first and I am the last* (44:6) — echoed in Revelation 1:17, 22:13. *ʿăṣabbîm* (עֲצַבִּים), *idols* (44:9), the contemptuous noun for cult-images, related to a root meaning *to grieve* or *to be a burden*. *Pesel* (פֶּסֶל), *carved image*, the standard term for an idol in Exodus 20:4. *Ləḵōrēš* (לְכוֹרֶשׁ), *of Cyrus* (44:28), the Hebrew rendering of the Old Persian *Kûruš*; the name Cyrus appears here and at 45:1, 45:13. *Rōʿî* (רֹעִי), *my shepherd* (44:28), uses the royal-shepherd imagery applied throughout the ancient Near East to kings; the term will be applied later to David's line (Ezekiel 34) and in the New Testament to Christ (John 10).

## Lens 2 — Literature

Isaiah 44 contains several distinct literary units. Verses 1–5 are a **salvation-oracle** addressed to Jacob/Israel, with the *fear not* assurance and the promise of pouring water and Spirit on the descendants. Verses 6–8 are a **trial-speech** declaration of Yahweh's uniqueness (*I am the first and I am the last; besides me there is no god*). Verses 9–20 are the **idol parody**, a prose section embedded in the surrounding poetry — a deliberate literary shift, since Hebrew poetry's normal compression and parallelism would not serve the parody's leisurely satirical accumulation of detail. Verses 21–23 return to poetry with a salvation-oracle and a hymnic call to praise. Verses 24–28 are a creator-redeemer doxology that culminates in the Cyrus oracle.

The **idol parody** of vv. 9–20 is one of the most extended satirical passages in the Hebrew Bible. Its rhetorical strategy is deflation by minute description. The prophet does not denounce idolatry abstractly; he describes the manufacturing process step by step. The ironsmith works the cutting tool. The carpenter stretches the line, marks with a pencil, shapes with a plane, marks with a compass. The man cuts down the tree and watches it grow. He uses half the wood for cooking and warmth, *and the rest of it he makes into a god, his idol, and falls down to it and worships it. He prays to it and says, deliver me, for you are my god!* The parody's force is that it puts the reader inside the idolater's actions and asks them to see what they themselves are doing. *No one considers, nor is there knowledge or discernment to say, half of it I burned in the fire; I also baked bread on its coals; I roasted meat and have eaten. And shall I make the rest of it an abomination? Shall I fall down before a block of wood?* (44:19).

The literary form here is what classical rhetoric called *deinōsis* (intensification) and *enargeia* (vivid presentation), and what later European satire would call *reductio*. The parody is so concrete that one cannot read it without seeing the absurdity. Modern interpreters (Blenkinsopp, Goldingay) note that the parody is somewhat unfair to actual Babylonian religion, which did not naively identify the cult-image with the god; the divine presence was understood to dwell in the image after a complex ritual of *mouth-washing* (mīs pî) and *mouth-opening* (pīt pî) that effectively translated the divine presence into the wood and gold. The prophet's parody collapses this distinction for rhetorical force. This raises an important honest engagement question, taken up below.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

For Jewish tradition, Isaiah 44 has been read as one of the great monotheistic chapters. The Targum Jonathan paraphrases v. 6 to emphasize Yahweh's eternal sovereignty. Rashi reads the idol parody as straightforward polemic against the gods of the nations. Ibn Ezra, who had hinted at composite-authorship readings of Second Isaiah, treats the Cyrus reference primarily as the historical liberator, though he stops short of the full critical position. Radak (Kimhi) reads the chapter as decisive evidence of Yahweh's foreknowledge over against the gods. Abarbanel develops the chapter's monotheism at length. Modern Jewish thinkers — Levenson, Sarna, Boyarin — read the chapter as a high point of biblical monotheism, while modern Jewish biblical scholarship has generally accepted the composite-authorship reading. The verse 44:5 — *another will write on his hand, the Lord's* — has been read in some Jewish traditions as anticipating proselyte-conversion.

For Christian tradition, the chapter has had several major appropriations. The *I am the first and I am the last* of 44:6 is taken up directly by Revelation 1:17 and 22:13 in Christological declarations applied to Christ. The pouring out of the Spirit in 44:3 — *I will pour my Spirit upon your offspring* — is read in Acts 2 (Peter at Pentecost) as fulfilled in the Spirit's outpouring on the church. The Cyrus oracle of 44:28 has been central to Christian apologetic discussions of predictive prophecy from Eusebius and Origen onward. Justin Martyr, Origen, Jerome, Aquinas, Calvin all develop the chapter's monotheism and idol-parody Christologically and pastorally. Modern critical Christian commentary (Westermann, Blenkinsopp, Goldingay, Brueggemann) reads the chapter first in its sixth-century BC setting and treats Christological readings as theological appropriation.

The **Cyrus oracle** has its own remarkable post-biblical reception. Josephus in *Antiquities* 11.5–7 reports a tradition that when the high priest met Alexander the Great, he showed him this passage, prompting Alexander's favorable treatment of the Jews — a story most modern historians treat as legendary, but which testifies to the oracle's status. Cyrus has been the model of a *righteous Gentile ruler* in Jewish thought; in Christian thought, especially through the Reformation and into the present, the oracle has been used (and at times dangerously misused) to identify particular contemporary rulers as God's anointed instruments. The pattern of Christian-political theology invoking the *Cyrus type* has continued in modern American evangelicalism, which is a sobering reminder that the oracle's typological resonance can serve very different theological agendas.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

Two tensions run through Isaiah 44 and must be named.

The first is the **Cyrus-naming as the strongest single argument for composite authorship**. The honest engagement with this question is the central interpretive challenge of Second Isaiah. Three positions deserve hearing. The conservative-evangelical position defends Isaianic unity through prophetic foreknowledge: God revealed Cyrus's name to Isaiah of Jerusalem 150 years in advance. The mainstream-critical position holds that the natural reading of the oracle places the prophet in the late exilic period, with Cyrus's rise as contemporary geopolitical commentary. The canonical-criticism position brackets the historical question and treats the canonical Isaiah as the inspired text regardless of compositional history. Steelmanned: a thoughtful evangelical can grant exilic composition while still affirming inspiration; a thoughtful critical scholar can grant the canonical legitimacy of reading Isaiah as a unified theological witness; both *Catechism* §107–108 and Reformed *sola scriptura* can accommodate the critical position. The most important point is this: whichever position one takes, the theological force of the oracle remains. The God of Israel is sovereign over the rise and fall of empires, and that sovereignty extends even over a foreign king who does not know him.

The second tension is the **idol parody and modern interfaith concerns**. The parody is sharp; its rhetorical force depends on collapsing the distinction between the cult-image and the god the worshipper believed dwelt in it. As an interpretation of actual Babylonian religious phenomenology, the parody is unfair; ancient Mesopotamian theology had developed sophisticated accounts of how a god *inhabited* an image without being identical to it. As a polemic against the human tendency to build small, manageable gods out of materials we control, the parody is enduring. Three positions deserve hearing. The traditional reading (medieval Jewish, patristic, Reformation, much modern evangelical) takes the parody at face value as theological truth: idolatry is inherently absurd, and the parody's exposure of it is appropriate. The interfaith-sensitive reading, exemplified by Vatican II's *Nostra Aetate* (1965), holds that polemics against ancient Babylonian religion should not be transferred without nuance to modern dialogue with Hindus, Buddhists, or other traditions whose use of images is theologically more sophisticated than the parody's caricature suggests. The literary-rhetorical reading holds that the parody is doing rhetorical-pastoral work for an exiled people surrounded by Babylonian religious display — its function is to give the exiles a way to maintain their distinctive faith — and that the parody's polemical force should be received as such, not pressed into philosophical-comparative work it was not designed to do. Steelmanned: a thoughtful traditionalist can grant the legitimacy of interfaith nuance without abandoning the parody's polemic against false worship; a thoughtful interfaith dialogue partner can grant the parody's pastoral force in its original setting without surrendering to its caricature of other traditions.

Where this leaves us: a chapter whose two pillars are the most extended satire of human-made gods in the Hebrew Bible and the explicit naming of a foreign emperor as Yahweh's shepherd. The juxtaposition is the chapter's argument: the gods who must be carried by their worshippers are nothing, and the God who carries his people uses even Cyrus the Persian to bring them home.

Closing question: If the prophet's parody works by making the reader see what the idolater is doing — burning half the wood for warmth and bowing down to the other half — what would it mean to apply that same scrutiny to the things our own hands have made and now cannot live without?
