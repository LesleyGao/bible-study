# Proverbs 28 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World — Rulers, the Poor, and Torah Within Wisdom

Proverbs 28 belongs to Hezekiah's collection (chapters 25-29), the second great Solomonic anthology compiled, according to 25:1, by *anshei Chizqiyyah* — "the men of Hezekiah" — in the late eighth century B.C.E. While chapters 25-27 cluster around courtly imagery and friendship, chapters 28-29 shift register: Roland Murphy and William P. Brown both observe that this final pair of chapters returns to the more atomized aphoristic style of chapters 10-22 but with a markedly heightened *political and economic* concern. The wicked ruler, the oppressed poor, the rich who are *chakham be-einav* (חָכָם בְּעֵינָיו, "wise in their own eyes"), and the dynamics of wealth and Torah-observance dominate the chapter.

This concentration is not accidental. Bruce Waltke (NICOT *Proverbs*) reads chapters 28-29 as carrying a stronger Deuteronomic resonance than the rest of the book — the noun *torah* (תּוֹרָה, "instruction / law") appears in 28:4, 28:7, 28:9 (and again in 29:18) with what Waltke argues is a self-conscious echo of the Deuteronomic legal corpus. Michael V. Fox (Anchor Bible *Proverbs 10-31*) is more cautious: *torah* in Proverbs ordinarily means parental and sapiential instruction, not the Pentateuchal Torah. The honest mainstream-critical reading sits between them: the chapter is using a term that the Hezekiah-era editors could plausibly have heard with both resonances, and the historical context of the late eighth century — the rise of prophetic critique under Amos, Hosea, Micah, and Isaiah, all of whom denounce ruling-class oppression of the poor — frames the chapter's intensified social-justice register.

The chapter's repeated juxtaposition of *tzaddiq* (צַדִּיק, "righteous") and *rasha* (רָשָׁע, "wicked") — verses 1, 12, 28 form a frame around the chapter — is classic wisdom-binary, but the *content* of *tzaddiq* in this chapter is unusually social. The righteous in Proverbs 28 are not abstractly virtuous; they are people who *do not oppress*, who *give to the poor* (28:8, 27), who *hate unjust gain* (28:16), who *walk in integrity* before the vulnerable (28:6). Christine Roy Yoder calls this the chapter's "social-prophetic edge" — the Hezekian editors are reading Solomonic wisdom under the influence of an emerging prophetic conscience.

Hebrew vocabulary worth noting: *moda* (מוֹדֶה, "confessing," from *yadah* יָדָה in the Hiphil) at 28:13 — "whoever confesses and forsakes [his transgressions] will obtain mercy" — is the same root used throughout the Psalter for liturgical confession (cf. Psalm 32:5; Psalm 51 conceptually) and in Daniel 9 and Nehemiah 9 for corporate confession. Will Kynes (*An Obituary for "Wisdom Literature"*) has argued that the older modern category-distinction between "wisdom" and "Torah-piety" in the Hebrew Bible was overdrawn; passages like Proverbs 28:13 show wisdom and confessional piety operating in the same conceptual world.

## Lens 2: Literature — Sentence Proverbs with a Political Frame

Proverbs 28 is built almost entirely from two-line sentence proverbs, but the editorial sequencing creates a sustained political-moral register. The chapter's opening (28:1) is one of the most quoted single lines in all Hebrew wisdom:

> נָסוּ וְאֵין־רֹדֵף רָשָׁע וְצַדִּיקִים כִּכְפִיר יִבְטָח
> *nasu ve-ein-rodef rasha ve-tzaddiqim ki-khefir yivtach*
> "The wicked flee when no one pursues, but the righteous are bold as a young lion."

The Hebrew *kefir* (כְּפִיר) is specifically a young lion in its prime — not a *ari* (the generic lion) nor a *layish* (the old lion), but the strong-young-lion at its peak of confidence. The image is psychological: the wicked carry their own pursuers in their consciences (cf. Lev 26:36, "the sound of a driven leaf shall put them to flight"), while the righteous walk with the settled boldness of clear ground beneath them. Tremper Longman observes that the verse is the chapter's *thesis statement* — the moral universe has structure, and that structure is felt internally before it manifests externally.

The chapter's structural center, the verse most frequently cited in Jewish and Christian devotional traditions, is 28:13:

> מְכַסֶּה פְשָׁעָיו לֹא יַצְלִיחַ וּמוֹדֶה וְעֹזֵב יְרֻחָם
> *mechasseh pesha'av lo yatzliach u-moddeh ve-ozev yeruchcham*
> "Whoever conceals his transgressions will not prosper, but he who confesses and forsakes them will obtain mercy."

The two participles — *mechasseh* ("concealing") and *moddeh* ("confessing") — are syntactically opposed, and the verb pair *moddeh ve-ozev* ("confesses and forsakes") is the key. Confession alone is insufficient; confession-and-forsaking is what triggers *yeruchcham* (יְרֻחָם, "will be shown mercy," a Pual passive of *racham*, the verb whose nominal cognate *rachamim* means "compassions" / "tender mercies"). This is the wisdom tradition's most concentrated single-verse account of repentance. William P. Brown notes that the verse runs structurally parallel to Psalm 32:5 ("I acknowledged my sin to you, and I did not cover my iniquity... and you forgave the iniquity of my sin") and shares its theology of confession as the breaking of self-deception.

The chapter has notable literary clusters. Verses 3, 8, 11, 15-16, 27 form a wealth-and-poverty thread: the poor man who oppresses the poor is "a beating rain that leaves no food" (a striking image of natural force turned destructive against its own kind); the rich man "wise in his own eyes" can be "found out" by the poor man with understanding (28:11 — a remarkable democratic-epistemological claim); the wicked ruler over a poor people is "a roaring lion or charging bear" (28:15). Mark Boda observes that the lion image is doing inverse work to its appearance in 28:1 — the *tzaddiq* is bold like a *kefir*, but the unjust ruler is destructive like a roaring *ari*. Same animal, opposite valence, depending on whether the strength is righteous or oppressive.

Verses 4, 7, 9 form a *torah* cluster. The contrast in 28:9 is sharp: "If one turns away his ear from hearing the *torah*, even his prayer is an abomination" (גַּם־תְּפִלָּתוֹ תּוֹעֵבָה, *gam-tefillato to'evah*). The same word used in Leviticus and Deuteronomy for the most serious cultic violations — *to'evah*, "abomination" — is here applied to the prayer of the disobedient. This is a wisdom-prophetic synthesis: Amos 5:21-24 ("I hate, I despise your feasts... let justice roll down") makes the same move, and 28:9 condenses it into a single line.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story — Jewish and Christian Reception

**Verse 13 in Christian sacramental and devotional tradition.** The verse is one of the most cited Old Testament texts in the entire Christian theology of confession. The First Letter of John 1:9 ("If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness") is the New Testament's clearest echo of Proverbs 28:13's logic — confession plus the divine action of mercy. The Catholic *Catechism of the Catholic Church* (CCC §1422-1498) places Proverbs 28:13 within its theology of the sacrament of penance, which it formalizes in the four classical elements: contrition (*contritio cordis*), confession (*confessio oris*), satisfaction (*satisfactio operis*), and absolution. The Reformed tradition rejects sacramental private confession but retains the theology of Proverbs 28:13 in its doctrine of *general confession*: the *Heidelberg Catechism* Q&A 56 on the forgiveness of sins, the *Westminster Confession* 15 on repentance unto life, and the prayers of confession in Reformed liturgies all stand downstream of this verse.

**Jewish liturgical tradition.** Proverbs 28:13 sits at the heart of the *Yom Kippur viduy* (וִדּוּי, "confession") — the alphabetic-acrostic confession of sins recited on the Day of Atonement. The two great confessional formulae of Yom Kippur — *Ashamnu* ("we have trespassed") and *Al Chet* ("for the sin") — operationalize 28:13's theology: confession in the plural ("we"), naming the specific transgression, and the implicit forsaking through the day's fasting and reorientation. *Mishneh Torah* (Maimonides, *Hilkhot Teshuvah* — Laws of Repentance) treats *moddeh ve-ozev* (confession-and-forsaking) as the structural core of *teshuvah*: the penitent must verbally articulate the sin (*viduy devarim*), commit to forsake it, and ideally be tested in the same circumstance and not return to it. Maimonides' framework remains the dominant Jewish theology of repentance and is rooted explicitly in Proverbs 28:13.

**Verse 1 in Christian preaching tradition.** The image of the righteous *bold as a lion* has been a homiletic centerpiece from Chrysostom through Spurgeon. Chrysostom uses it in his *Homilies on Romans* to describe Paul's frankness before authorities; Augustine cites it on the consciences of the wicked in the *Confessions* (Book II); Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 128) draws on it for his account of fortitude. In modern Black Christian preaching, verse 1 has been used powerfully to ground civil rights resistance — Martin Luther King Jr. drew on the lion-image rhetoric in several sermons.

**Verse 9 and the prophetic-wisdom synthesis.** The "even his prayer is an abomination" verse has been read in conversation with Isaiah 1:10-17 and Amos 5:21-24 in both Jewish and Christian traditions. Abraham Joshua Heschel's *The Prophets* makes 28:9 a hinge text in his account of the prophetic mind — religious devotion divorced from justice is not partial obedience but inverted obedience, and wisdom literature's editors knew this.

**Verse 8 and the medieval prohibition of usury.** "Whoever multiplies his wealth by interest and profit gathers it for him who is generous to the poor" was foundational to the medieval Christian (and earlier rabbinic) prohibition of usury. Aquinas's treatment of usury in *Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 78 cites this material. Modern Christian and Jewish reflection on debt-finance, predatory lending, and economic justice continues to draw on this tradition.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — Tensions, Steelman, and Where This Leaves Us

The central theological question Proverbs 28 poses is **what does confession-and-mercy actually require, and whose mercy is it?**

**Tension one: Catholic sacramental confession vs. Reformed general confession.** The Catholic position (CCC §1422-1498) holds that Christ instituted the sacrament of penance (Jn 20:22-23) and that ordinary mortal sins should be confessed to a priest who, acting *in persona Christi*, declares absolution. The biblical warrant draws on Proverbs 28:13 (confession-and-forsaking), James 5:16 ("confess your sins to one another"), Matthew 16:19 and 18:18 (the keys), and John 20. The steelman: confession is harder when it remains internal, and the discipline of articulating sin to another human being who stands sacramentally for the church is part of what makes the *forsaking* in Prov 28:13 actually happen.

The Reformed position rejects the sacramental-priestly mediation but retains the practice of confession — both general (in liturgy) and particular (between believers, James 5:16). Calvin's *Institutes* III.4 makes the case that confession is biblically required but that no priestly absolution is needed beyond the announcement of the gospel. The steelman: the priesthood of all believers means that the confessional encounter Proverbs 28:13 envisions is between the sinner and God, with human accountability serving but not mediating the divine mercy.

The honest critical reading does not resolve this tension. Proverbs 28:13 itself does not specify *to whom* the confession is made. Both Catholic and Reformed traditions can claim the verse, and what matters more theologically is that *both* require the *forsaking* — the verse is unambiguous that confession without amendment of life is not what Proverbs is talking about.

**Tension two: 28:9 and the relation between worship and justice.** "If one turns away his ear from hearing the *torah*, even his prayer is an abomination." This verse has been preached (rightly) as a warning that worship without obedience is empty. It can also be misused — to invalidate the prayer of the imperfect, the broken, the failing. The chapter does not invite that reading: 28:13 immediately offers the path of confession-and-mercy. The honest synthesis is that 28:9 names a serious danger (the cultivated, settled refusal to hear what God commands), and 28:13 names the path through it (confession-and-forsaking received with mercy). The two verses must be read together, not against each other.

**Tension three: the chapter's strong rhetoric against the rich.** Verses 6, 8, 11, 15-16, 20, 22, 25 form a sustained critique of wealth-accumulation and unjust power. The steelman of the conservative reading is that the chapter targets *ill-gotten* wealth specifically (verses 8, 16, 20, 25 all explicitly mention injustice or hastiness or greed). The steelman of the social-prophetic reading, voiced by writers like Walter Brueggemann, is that the cumulative effect of the chapter is to indict wealth-accumulation as such when it operates in indifference to the poor (verse 27). Both readings have textual warrant. The honest critical reading is that the chapter is more uncomfortable with wealth than the comfortable bourgeois reception of Proverbs has often allowed, and that 28:11's claim — that the rich man is "wise in his own eyes" but the poor man with understanding can "find him out" — is a specifically *epistemic* judgment that wealth distorts self-perception in ways that poverty does not.

**Where this leaves us:** Proverbs 28 is the wisdom tradition's most concentrated synthesis of *Torah-instruction*, *prophetic justice*, and *personal repentance*. Its central verse on confession-and-mercy (28:13) has shaped Jewish and Christian liturgy for two and a half millennia. Its sustained critique of unjust rulers and accumulated wealth gives the chapter a sharper political edge than is often acknowledged.

**Closing question:** What in your life is currently being *concealed* — held internally rather than confessed-and-forsaken? And what would it cost you, concretely, to move from *mechasseh* to *moddeh ve-ozev*? The chapter is unambiguous that mercy waits on the second move.

## The Thread

Proverbs 28 closes Hezekiah's collection alongside chapter 29. It runs a sustained line through *torah*-piety, prophetic justice, and personal repentance, with 28:13 — the confession-and-forsaking verse — at its theological center. The chapter has shaped Jewish *teshuvah*, Christian sacramental and Reformed confessional practice, and the wisdom-prophetic critique of unjust rulers across two millennia.
