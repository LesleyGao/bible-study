# Acts 20: Eutychus, Miletus, and the Pauline Farewell

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Acts 20 carries Paul through a sweeping arc of the eastern Mediterranean: out of Ephesus after the silversmiths' riot, through Macedonia and Greece, back through Macedonia to Troas, then by coastal hops down the Aegean to Miletus, where he summons the Ephesian elders for a final speech. The geography is dense with archaeology. **Troas** (Alexandria Troas) was a major Roman colony on the western coast of Asia Minor, founded by Antigonus and refounded by Augustus; the harbor mole and bath-gymnasium complex have been mapped by the Turkish-German excavations under Elmar Schwertheim, and the third-story window from which Eutychus fell would have belonged to one of the multi-story insulae characteristic of imperial-period Roman colonial housing — buildings whose remains are still visible in the city's residential quarter. **Miletus**, by Acts 20:17, was already in slow decline as the Maeander silted up its harbors, but its theater (capacity ~15,000), the Delphinion sanctuary, and the Roman-era harbor monument all stood when Paul anchored offshore. The "Jewish inscription" from the Miletus theater (engaged by Louis Feldman; CIJ 748) — an inscribed seat reading τόπος Ἰουδέων τῶν καὶ Θεοσεβῖον ("place of the Jews who are also God-fearers") — testifies to the Jewish presence Paul could plausibly tap as he sent for the Ephesian elders nearby.

The chapter sits inside the **second of three "we" passages** (20:5–21:18). The shift from third-person to first-person plural ("we sailed away from Philippi," 20:6) has anchored debates about Acts' authorship and sources for nearly two centuries. **Henry Cadbury** treated the "we" as a Lukan literary device; **F.F. Bruce** (NICNT) and **Joseph Fitzmyer** (AB) read it as evidence of an authentic eyewitness travel-diary; **Pervo** (Hermeneia) and **Marguerat** treat it as a sophisticated Hellenistic convention for travel narrative (cf. the *Periplus* genre, the Egyptian portion of Lucian's *Verae Historiae*). **Vernon Robbins**'s sea-voyage thesis — that "we" is a generic convention of ancient sea narratives — has been heavily contested by **Susan Marie Praeder** and **Stanley Porter**, both of whom showed Robbins's parallels do not actually deploy "we" the way Acts does. The most defensible synthesis (Bruce, Fitzmyer, Keener) is that Luke had access to a travel-source — perhaps his own notes — which he integrated rather than smoothed away.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter's historical world. ***presbyteroi*** (πρεσβύτεροι, 20:17) — "elders" — was a term with deep Septuagintal roots (the Mosaic seventy elders, Num 11) that synagogue communities had carried into the Diaspora; epigraphic evidence from Aphrodisias, Sardis, and Stobi attests synagogue *presbyteroi* across the empire. ***episkopoi*** (ἐπίσκοποι, 20:28) — "overseers" — was a Hellenistic civic office (financial-administrative oversight in Greek cities; cf. *IG* II² 1368) that Paul applies to the same men he just called *presbyteroi*. ***ekklēsia tou theou*** (ἐκκλησία τοῦ θεοῦ, 20:28) — "church of God" — is Pauline shorthand (1 Cor 1:2; 1 Thess 2:14) that bridges the LXX *qahal YHWH* and the Greek civic assembly. ***haima tou idiou*** (αἵματος τοῦ ἰδίου, 20:28) — "his own blood" — is the textual crux discussed under Lens 4. ***agraphon*** (the technical term for a saying of Jesus not in the canonical Gospels, applied to "It is more blessed to give than to receive," 20:35) — engaged by **Joachim Jeremias** in *Unknown Sayings of Jesus* — anchors the chapter to the early Jesus-tradition outside the four Gospels.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Acts 20 is one of the most carefully constructed chapters in Luke's second volume. The chapter pivots on a major Lukan structural device: the **farewell discourse**. Paul has given one missionary-foundation speech (Pisidian Antioch, 13:16–41) and one Areopagus speech (17:22–31); the Miletus address (20:18–35) is the third major Pauline speech and the only one addressed to fellow Christian leaders. **Conzelmann** (*Hermeneia*) read it as Lukan composition modeled on Greco-Roman *peristasis* catalogs and Jewish testamentary literature (Gen 49, Deut 32–33, Joshua 23–24, the *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs*). **Pervo** sharpened the testamentary reading: like a dying patriarch, Paul reviews his ministry, predicts post-departure crisis, and commends the community to God. **Tannehill** (*Narrative Unity*) shows the speech's most striking literary achievement: it is the **only** Acts speech that closely echoes Pauline epistolary vocabulary — *douleuō tō kyriō* (20:19), *taupheinophrosynē* (20:19), the "course" / *dromos* metaphor (20:24), the inheritance among the sanctified (20:32) — to such a density that even **Bruce** and **Barrett** (skeptical of speech-as-fabrication readings) concede it shows Luke working close to authentic Pauline tradition.

The chapter participates in the larger **Pauline-Jesus parallelism** that **Tannehill** documented across Luke-Acts. Paul, like Jesus, is on a journey to Jerusalem under the constraint of the Spirit (20:22); he predicts his own coming suffering (20:23, paralleling the Lukan passion predictions); he knows he will not see the disciples again (20:25, 38, paralleling Luke 22); he commits the community to God (20:32, paralleling Luke 23:46). **Charles Talbert** has shown the parallelism is not incidental ornament but the structural backbone of Luke's two-volume christological-ecclesial argument: the apostle's path conforms to the Master's. **Marguerat** (*The First Christian Historian*) sharpens the point: in Acts 20, Luke is not merely narrating Paul's last speech to Asia, he is constructing the **paradigm of post-apostolic ministry** for the third generation — the generation that will not have Paul.

The chapter is also a **literary bridge into Paul's three Acts conversion narratives**. Chapter 9 has already given the third-person narrator's account; chapter 22 will give Paul's first-person account in Aramaic to the Jerusalem crowd; chapter 26 will give Paul's first-person account before Agrippa. Engaging the variants is unavoidable for any reader of chapter 20: the Miletus speech is the last moment Paul speaks freely as a missionary before he is taken into custody. Western Text variants in chapter 20 are relatively modest — Codex Bezae lengthens the description of the night at Troas and slightly recasts 20:15 — but the **20:28 textual crux** (discussed under Lens 4) is among the most consequential variants in the entire NT.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Pauline collection-tour from Macedonia and Achaia is one of Acts 20's quieter but most significant theological currents. Paul has a delegation (20:4) representing the very communities to which he had sent the great collection-letters (Romans 15:25–28; 1 Cor 16:1–4; 2 Cor 8–9). **David Downs** (*The Offering of the Gentiles*) reads the collection as Pauline-ecclesial *cultic offering* — Gentile firstfruits to the mother-church. **Stephen Joubert** (*Paul as Benefactor*) reads it through Greco-Roman benefaction patterns: Paul is constructing reciprocal solidarity between Gentile churches and Jerusalem. **Dieter Georgi** (*Remembering the Poor*) reads it as Paul's bid to demonstrate that Gentile faith bears the legitimate fruit of *tzedakah*, fulfilling the eschatological pilgrimage of Isaiah 60. Luke barely names the collection in Acts (24:17 alone), but the delegation list in 20:4 — Berea, Thessalonica, Derbe, Lystra, Asia — is the receipt.

**Jewish interpretive trajectories** illuminate Acts 20 unevenly. **Pamela Eisenbaum** and **Matthew Thiessen** (*A Jewish Paul*) read the chapter's tearful farewell against the grain of Christian-supersessionist readings: Paul's "course" (20:24) is not a Christian career but a Jewish prophet's vocation to the nations, and the *ekklēsia tou theou* (20:28) is the eschatological Israel-and-the-nations gathered around Israel's Messiah. **Paula Fredriksen** (*Paul: The Pagan's Apostle*) is even more pointed: when Paul tells the elders "I am innocent of the blood of all" (20:26), he is not declaring Christian-mission completion but a Pharisaic-prophetic discharge of the Ezekiel 33 watchman charge. **Adele Reinhartz** has urged Christian readers to notice how often Lukan farewell-prophecies of "fierce wolves" (20:29) have been historically retooled into anti-Jewish polemic — a hermeneutical caution not directed at the text but at its reception.

**Christian interpretive trajectories** are richer still. **Chrysostom**'s *55 Homilies on Acts* devoted homilies 43–45 to chapter 20 and read the Miletus speech as the paradigmatic *episcopal* charge — clerical formation. **Bede** in his *Expositio Actuum Apostolorum* read 20:28 as the foundational text for the unity of the local pastor with the universal *ekklēsia*. **Calvin**'s *Commentary on Acts* used 20:17 and 20:28 together as the keystone of Reformed church polity: the same persons are called *presbyteroi* and *episkopoi*, and therefore the post-apostolic separation of "bishop" from "presbyter" is a human innovation. **Luther** read the agraphon at 20:35 as proof of *sola scriptura*'s practical limits — Scripture itself attests Jesus said things outside Scripture, so the church receives a deposit larger than the page even while being normed by it. **Karl Barth**'s *CD* IV/3.2 used 20:24 ("if only I may finish my course") for his theology of vocation. **Howard Marshall** (*Pastoral Epistles*) made Acts 20:17–35 the foundational pre-Pastoral text for early-church order: the wolves Paul predicts (20:29) are precisely the false teachers the Pastorals address, and the *episkopos* qualifications of 1 Tim 3 and Titus 1 are the working-out of the Miletus charge.

The major contemporary commentary tradition — **Bruce** (NICNT), **Barrett** (ICC), **Fitzmyer** (AB), **Gaventa** (Abingdon), **Johnson** (Sacra Pagina), **Keener** (4 vols), **Witherington III**, **Schnabel** (ZECNT), **Conzelmann** (Hermeneia), **Haenchen**, **Pervo** (Hermeneia), **Peterson** (Pillar), **Marguerat** — converges on three judgments about chapter 20: (1) the speech is closer to genuinely Pauline material than any other Acts speech; (2) the *presbyteros* / *episkopos* identification is decisive against monarchical-episcopate retrojection; (3) the textual crux at 20:28 is among the most significant in Acts. **Edward Schillebeeckx** (*Ministry*) read the same passage from the Catholic side, conceding the original identity but defending the historical legitimacy of the church's later differentiation under **Newman**'s *Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine*. The Anabaptist tradition (engaged through **Yoder**) read 20:33–35 — Paul's manual labor and refusal of patronage — as the founding charter of bivocational ministry.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The textual and Christological crux of 20:28.** The verse, in the ESV's rendering, has Paul tell the elders to shepherd "the church of God, which he obtained with his own blood." The Greek phrase *tēs ekklēsias tou theou hēn periepoiēsato dia tou haimatos tou idiou* admits at least three readings.

The first reading takes *theou* as the antecedent of *idiou*: "the church of God, which he obtained with his own blood." This is the reading of P74, Sinaiticus, and Vaticanus and is the text printed by NA28/UBS5. **Athanasius** read it this way and used it as Nicene-Christological ammunition against the Arians: God's own blood — i.e., the incarnate Son's blood, predicated of the divine subject under the *communicatio idiomatum*. **Theodoret** of Cyrrhus resisted, fearing patripassianism. **Bruce Metzger** (*Textual Commentary*) defended the reading as original. The danger, as **Bart Ehrman** (*The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture*) has argued, is that the verse is so theologically charged that scribal "improvement" in either direction is plausible.

The second reading takes the Western Text variant in Bezae and a few minuscules: "the church of the Lord (*kyriou*) which he obtained with his own blood." This neatly avoids the patripassian worry and aligns with Pauline epistolary usage (Paul never elsewhere calls the church *ekklēsia tou theou* and speaks of God's blood). **Haenchen** preferred this reading on internal grounds.

The third reading parses *tou idiou* as a substantive — "the blood of his own [Son/Beloved]." This makes the genitive christological without making it patripassian: God obtains the church with the blood of his own (Son). **Witherington**, **Marguerat**, and increasingly **Schnabel** prefer this construal; **F.F. Murphy-O'Connor** and **Bruce** have entertained it.

Steelmanning each: the first reading is the *lectio difficilior* and most likely original — early scribes would have softened, not hardened, patripassian-sounding language. The second reading is the most theologically conservative and best matches Pauline usage of *ekklēsia*. The third reading dissolves the theological scandal without textual emendation by exploiting an attested Pauline idiom (Rom 8:32: *tou idiou huiou*).

**Where this leaves us:** the original text is almost certainly *theou* with *tou idiou*, and the most defensible parsing is the third — "the blood of his own [Son]." The verse remains the strongest single-verse witness in Acts to the high Christology of Paul: the church is gathered around an Israel's-Messiah whose blood is spoken of with possessive divine attribution, even before Nicaea has hammered out the grammar.

**Tension 2: Whether 20:17–28 mandates a particular church polity.** The same persons are called *presbyteroi* (20:17) and *episkopoi* (20:28). This is the most discussed church-government text in Acts.

The Reformed-Presbyterian position (Calvin, **Marshall**, **Bruce**, **Schnabel**) reads the identification as binding: the New Testament knows no monarchical bishop distinct from the body of presbyters; the post-apostolic emergence of the single bishop (Ignatius of Antioch, c. 110) is a deviation requiring Reformed correction. The **Free Church / Anabaptist** position pushes further: even the *presbyter* office is a community function, not a sacerdotal order, and Paul's manual-labor pattern (20:33–35) deauthorizes any clerical caste.

The Catholic-Orthodox position (Newman, Schillebeeckx, increasingly **Raymond Brown** in his later work) concedes the original identity but argues that the monarchical episcopate is **legitimate organic development** — the Spirit guiding the church to differentiate offices as the apostles died and the inter-church communion required a single point of accountability. **Newman**'s *Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine* is the foundational defense: a doctrine or polity is legitimately developed if it preserves the type, the principles, and the consequences of its origin.

A third position — Anglican / "moderate episcopate" — reads Acts 20 as showing that the early church had a **flexible** order (presbyter-bishops in some cities, prophets in others, apostles overseeing) and that no single polity is divinely mandated; episcopate is *bene esse* (good for the church) but not *esse* (essential to its being).

Steelmanning each: the Reformed reading has the cleanest exegesis of 20:17–28; the Catholic reading has the cleanest historical account of how the church actually moved from Acts to Ignatius without rupture; the Anglican reading captures the actual diversity of New Testament practice.

**Where this leaves us:** Acts 20 settles less than each tradition wishes it settled. It rules out one thing decisively — the claim that the New Testament itself prescribes a *jure divino* monarchical bishop above presbyters — but it does not rule out the Catholic developmental defense, nor does it mandate a particular Reformed polity. What it *does* mandate is content: the *episkopoi* are charged with attentive shepherding, doctrinal vigilance, and freedom from financial avarice (20:33–35).

**One unresolved question:** Paul says the elders will see his face no more (20:25, 38). The Pastoral Epistles, however, presuppose a post-Acts Pauline ministry in Asia Minor (1 Tim 1:3, 2 Tim 4:13). Did Paul return to Asia after a first Roman release (the traditional reading from Eusebius forward), or is the Lukan prediction unfulfilled in a way the church has spent two millennia harmonizing? The historical answer would change how we read the urgency of the Miletus charge.

## The Thread

Acts 20:28 ties forward to **1 Peter 5:1–4** (the *episkopountes* charge to elders, with the same Christological warrant) and to the **Pastoral Epistles** as the working-out of the Miletus prediction of wolves. The agraphon at 20:35 ("It is more blessed to give than to receive") ties forward to **2 Cor 8–9** and back into the Synoptic tradition's blessing-formulas (Luke 6:38). The Pauline-Jesus parallelism — journey to Jerusalem under Spirit-constraint, knowing of suffering — flags forward to chapters 21–28, where it will become the chapter-by-chapter scaffolding of the rest of Acts.
