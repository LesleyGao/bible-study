# 1 Samuel 6: The Cows Knew the Way

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter sits inside what Leonhard Rost in 1926 isolated as the Ark Narrative — a self-contained source running through 1 Samuel 4–6 and rejoining at 2 Samuel 6. Politically, the Iron Age I Philistine pentapolis (Ashdod, Gaza, Ashkelon, Gath, Ekron) is at the height of its coordinated power, and the unique Hebrew political vocabulary applied to its rulers — סְרָנִים *səranim*, "lords" or "tyrants" (6:4, 6:12, 6:16, 6:18) — is itself a historical fingerprint. The word is unrelated to ordinary Semitic terms for ruler and almost certainly derives from an Aegean/Anatolian root cognate with Greek τύραννος *tyrannos*. Trude Dothan and Lawrence Stager have read this lexical loan as confirmation of the Sea Peoples migration narrative independently attested in the Medinet Habu reliefs of Ramesses III. Aren Maeir's ongoing excavations at Tell es-Safi/Gath and Seymour Gitin's at Tel Miqne-Ekron have produced the material correlate: locally produced Mycenaean IIIC:1b "Philistine Monochrome" pottery, hearths of Aegean type, pig bones in atypically high proportions, and Linear-related potter's marks — a population whose material world only later assimilated to Canaanite norms.

The two cult objects sent back are the chapter's defining image. The Philistine diviners (קֹסְמִים *qosəmim*, 6:2) — a class condemned in Deut 18:10 but here treated as competent professional ritual technicians — prescribe an אָשָׁם *asham*, a "guilt offering," consisting of five golden tumors and five golden mice. The Hebrew עֳפָלִים *ʿofalim* (6:4–5, 6:11, 6:17) is a hapax-adjacent term whose root means "to swell" or "to rise"; the Masoretes were uncomfortable enough that the qere replaces it everywhere with the more delicate טְחֹרִים *təḥorim* ("hemorrhoids" or "swellings"). Whether the Philistine plague was bubonic plague (so J. B. Geyer in *VT* 1981, comparing the rodent vector and groin swellings) or a hemorrhoidal epidemic of dysenteric origin, the *ʿofalim* are unmistakably bodies marked by the divine hand. The עַכְבָּרִים *ʿakbarim*, "mice" or "rodents" of 6:4–5, 6:11, 6:18, complete the diagnosis: rodents that "ravage the land" (6:5).

The cult logic is sympathetic-magic apotropaism, and it has direct ANE parallels. Hittite plague rituals attributed to Mursili II prescribe sending a ritually loaded animal or effigy out of the territory bearing the plague away with it; the Ambazzi and Uhhamuwa rituals (CTH 391 and CTH 410) involve substitute figurines crafted to draw off pestilence. Mesopotamian *namburbi* texts likewise neutralize ominous evil through likeness. The five-by-five symmetry to the Philistine pentapolis is itself ritual logic: the offering must match the polities afflicted. The new עֲגָלָה חֲדָשָׁה *ʿagalah ḥadashah* (6:7) and unyoked milch cows are ritually neutral instruments — never used for profane labor, never trained to a yoke that would predispose them to a path. Separating the calves at home creates the test: maternal instinct points one way, the ark another. The cows go straight, and the diviners get their answer.

Beth-shemesh, where the cart arrives, is Tel Beit Shemesh in the northern Shephelah, excavated by Mackenzie (1911–12), Grant (1928–33), and the long-running Bunimovitz–Lederman project (1990–present). Their stratum 7 destruction, dated to the late eleventh century by the excavators, sits roughly in the right horizon for the chapter and shows clear evidence of a Canaanite-Israelite border town. The "great stone" (6:14, 6:18) functions as etiological landmark — a megalith pressed into liturgical service as altar (the cart-wood becomes fuel, the cows the burnt offering), echoing Manoah's altar-rock in Judg 13 and prefiguring David's at Araunah's threshing floor.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Generically, this is theologized historiography with an embedded ritual etiology, but the chapter's most distinctive literary feature is its perspective: nearly the entire narrative is told from the Philistine point of view. Robert Alter notes that the Hebrew narrator is willing to ventriloquize the religious reasoning of foreigners with a remarkable absence of polemic; the priests and diviners speak coherent, even theologically literate, lines (6:3, 6:5–6). The reference to Egypt and Pharaoh (6:6) is framed as Philistine memory — they know the exodus story — and serves a double duty: it links this episode typologically to the plagues, and it lets the narrator extract Israel's central confession from gentile mouths. Meir Sternberg's analysis of foreign perspective in the Hebrew Bible reads this as the narrator's deliberate ironic strategy — *the pagans get it, and Israel will not*.

Structurally the chapter completes Rost's Ark Narrative arc with elegant symmetry. The ark moves: Aphek (4:1) → Ashdod (5:1) → Gath (5:8) → Ekron (5:10) → Beth-shemesh (6:12) → Kiriath-jearim (6:21–7:2). Each station ends with disaster; the ark is finally received by Israelites with no less destructive consequence. Fokkelman's narrative analysis (*Narrative Art and Poetry in the Books of Samuel*, vol. IV) underlines how the cart's straight-line motion — "neither to the right nor to the left" (6:12, deliberately echoing Deut 5:32 and 28:14) — visually images divine sovereignty over animal instinct.

The textual variants here matter. The death notice at 6:19 — וַיַּךְ בְּאַנְשֵׁי בֵית־שֶׁמֶשׁ … שִׁבְעִים אִישׁ חֲמִשִּׁים אֶלֶף אִישׁ, "He struck of the men of Beth-shemesh… seventy men, fifty thousand men" — is one of the more notorious cruxes in the Hebrew Bible. The numbers are syntactically asyndetic (no "and") and grossly disproportionate. Josephus (*Antiquities* 6.1.4) reads only seventy. Several LXX manuscripts read seventy and harmonize away the larger figure. The MT may preserve a textual conflation; McCarter (AB) reconstructs an original "seventy men, fifty out of every thousand" or simply seventy with a marginal gloss accreted into the text. Emanuel Tov treats the verse as a textbook case of textual corruption obscured by unusually conservative scribal transmission. The chapter is also one of the verses where 4QSam-a is unfortunately fragmentary at the critical locus, so the variant cannot be adjudicated from Qumran. The reason for the strike is itself textually unsettled — MT says they "looked upon" the ark (וַיִּרְאוּ בַּאֲרוֹן), several LXX witnesses suggest "the sons of Jechoniah did not rejoice with the men of Beth-shemesh," lending different theological color.

Within the book's larger argument, chapter 6 closes the demonstration that began at Aphek: YHWH does not need Israel's military to defend his honor, and Dagon's house is no shelter from him. The ark is not a talisman; it is a presence. This sets up chapter 7's return-to-covenant scene and the great pivot at chapter 8 — the demand for a king "like the nations." The chapter is also the literary fulcrum on which the question of *holiness as place* turns: the ark cannot stay at Beth-shemesh, and in 7:1 it is exiled to Kiriath-jearim, where it will sit for twenty years until David finally moves it to Jerusalem in 2 Sam 6 — and the Uzzah catastrophe will replay 6:19 nearly beat for beat.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, 1 Samuel 6 is the hinge between exodus typology and monarchy. The Philistines themselves invoke the exodus (6:6), and the narrator stages their guilt offering as a kind of inverted Passover: the cows depart bearing the wrath that the firstborn bore in Egypt; the ark is "released" the way Israel was released. The chapter also looks backward to Numbers 4 and 7 (the rules of ark transport — Levitical bearers, never carts) and forward to 2 Samuel 6, where David repeats the Philistines' mistake of putting the ark on a new cart and learns the Levitical lesson at the cost of Uzzah's life. The cross-canonical claim is dense: God is portable but not domesticable, returnable but not retrievable.

**Jewish trajectory.** Josephus (*Ant.* 6.1.1–4) tells the story with careful Hellenistic decorum, smoothing the magic and reducing the dead at Beth-shemesh to seventy, and frames the diviners' counsel as a kind of natural-philosophy prudence rather than augury. The rabbis of *b. Avodah Zarah* 24b debate the cows' miraculous behavior — Rabbi Yohanan reads their lowing (וַיִּגְעוּ *vayyigʿu*, 6:12) as song, citing a wordplay that lets them "sing" praise. Rashi (eleventh-century France) glosses the *ʿofalim* with characteristic medical realism and reads the death of the men of Beth-shemesh as punishment for cultic irreverence — "they were threshing and did not stop their work upon seeing the ark." Radak (David Kimhi, thirteenth century) is more interested in the diviners' theological sophistication; he is willing to credit them with genuine knowledge of YHWH's deeds in Egypt as common Mediterranean knowledge. Ralbag (Gersonides) reads the test of the cows philosophically as proof against mere coincidence — the very contrast YHWH stages against the Philistines' "if not, then we shall know that it happened to us by coincidence" (6:9). Don Isaac Abarbanel, writing in late-fifteenth-century Iberia and Italy with one eye on contemporary Christian polemic, gives the chapter unusually extended attention: he treats the entire ark-return episode as a polemical demonstration that *God needs no human stratagem to defend his honor*, and he reads the Philistines' offering as a lesson in pagan instinct toward true religion that should embarrass complacent Israelite priests. Yair Zakovitch and Moshe Garsiel both attend to the etiological function of the Eben-stone tradition and the wordplay between Beth-shemesh ("house of the sun") and the divine action that takes place there. Avivah Zornberg, with her characteristic psychological reading, stresses the ambivalence of Israel's reception: a homecoming that immediately becomes a rejection — the men of Beth-shemesh do not want what they have received.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic readings tend to typologize. Origen reads the cart as the soul carrying divine mystery and the Philistine return as the involuntary witness of paganism to Christ. Augustine in *City of God* 17.4–7, working through the Samuel material as part of his prophetic-history scheme, treats the ark's return as a figure of the church's emergence from gentile hands. Gregory the Great in *Moralia* uses the unyoked cows as a type of preachers who must travel a path they have never been trained on. Aquinas (*ST* II-II) cites the Beth-shemesh strike as a case study in the natural duty of reverence (*religio*) toward holy things. Calvin's sermons on 1 Samuel — preached in 1561 at Geneva and recovered in modern critical editions by Max Engammare — read the chapter as a sustained meditation on the futility of treating God as a tool: the Philistines learn that "they had not to do with a dead idol but with a living God," and the men of Beth-shemesh learn that mere proximity to the holy is no protection. Luther's marginal glosses treat the *ʿofalim* with characteristic earthiness and read the chapter as a warning against superstitious veneration of relics. Among critical scholars, Wellhausen, Noth, Frank Moore Cross, and McCarter (AB) all worked the chapter as part of the Ark Narrative — Cross's argument for an early independent source (the *kavod* theology preceding the dtr framework) shapes the modern academic reading. Klein (WBC) and Hertzberg (OTL) develop the source-critical case in commentary form. Brueggemann reads the Philistines as inadvertent confessors and the Beth-shemeshites as the chapter's tragic subject — a holiness that judges those nearest to it. Eugene Peterson treats the ark's homecoming as the Bible's most underrated argument that worship that does not reckon with God's strangeness becomes lethal. Ellen Davis and John Goldingay both read the chapter pastorally as a warning against domestication. Among feminist readers, Phyllis Trible, J. Cheryl Exum, and Ilana Pardes have noted the chapter's near-total exclusion of female perspective except for the milch cows themselves — a striking absence that Mieke Bal's narratological work would push to the surface as an interpretive datum.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Why are the Philistines theologically lucid and the Israelites theologically inept?** The chapter inverts the expected Israel/nations polarity. The diviners get the diagnosis right; the Beth-shemeshites die of mishandled holiness.

A *Catholic* reading (in continuity with Aquinas and the conciliar tradition on natural religion) would say the Philistines are exercising the *praeambula fidei* — preambles of faith available to natural reason and conscience, here unusually well-formed by pagan ritual instinct. The Beth-shemeshites' fault is failure of *religio*, the virtue that orders due reverence to the divine.

A *Protestant Reformed* reading, anchored in Calvin's sermons, would resist the natural-religion framing: the Philistines are not virtuous pagans but coerced witnesses. Their guilt offering is extorted by plague, not freely offered. Their lucidity is the lucidity of judgment. The Beth-shemeshites are punished because chosen-people status confers no immunity — covenantal proximity raises the standard, not lowers it.

An *Orthodox* reading, drawing on Chrysostom and the broader patristic typology, would emphasize that the ark is already a veiled sacrament of the Incarnation — uncreated holiness mediated through created matter. The Philistines have stumbled upon the truth that the holy is *real*, not symbolic; the Beth-shemeshites have presumed familiarity with what cannot be familiar.

A *Jewish* reading, especially in Abarbanel's vein, would treat the inversion as polemic against Israelite complacency — the very point of the narrative is to embarrass insiders who imagine that proximity to the cult is protection.

A *critical* reading (Brueggemann, Polzin) would say the inversion is the dtr's deliberate literary strategy: by putting orthodox confession in pagan mouths, the narrator universalizes YHWH's claim and indicts Israel's parochialism. *Where this leaves us:* the chapter refuses to confine theological insight to ethnic Israel and refuses to confine theological catastrophe to outsiders. Holiness recognizes neither passport.

**Tension 2: Is YHWH the kind of God who strikes seventy of his own people for looking?** The Beth-shemesh slaughter (6:19) is morally hard, and the textual instability of the number does not make it easier — even seventy is a horror. What kind of holiness *requires* this?

A *traditional Catholic-Orthodox* position would treat the strike as an ontological consequence of holiness rather than a punitive choice — the ark is not a conscious agent striking sinners but a presence whose nearness to unconsecrated handling is intrinsically lethal, the way uninsulated current is lethal. Lev 10 (Nadab and Abihu) and 2 Sam 6 (Uzzah) work the same logic.

A *Reformed* position, with Calvin and Luther, would refuse the ontological softening and call the strike judicial — God's free judgment on cultic disobedience. The Beth-shemeshites are Israelites; they should have known the Levitical handling rules of Numbers 4. To read this as morally hard is to import modern sentimentality into a covenantal framework.

A *Jewish* position, citing Rashi's "they did not stop their work" reading and the rabbinic emphasis on context, would read the strike as proportional to specific cultic violation — irreverence in the moment, not arbitrary divine touchiness. Some streams (Abarbanel) suggest the gloating or gawking of a triumphal crowd over the ark's return.

A *critical* position (Klein, McCarter) would treat the verse as reflecting an early *kavod*-theology — pre-monotheistic in tone, where divine presence carries a quasi-physical mana that destroys the unconsecrated. The strike is not chosen by a moral agent; it is the byproduct of unmediated presence. The dtr edited but did not erase the older theology.

A *feminist or postcolonial* reading (Trible, Bal, Phyllis Bird) would press the moral question without resolving it: a God whose holiness is lethal to sixty-eight or sixty-nine bystanders for the sin of looking is a God whose theology must be read with both reverence and protest. The narrator does not editorialize; we cannot pretend to. *Where this leaves us:* the chapter neither softens the ark's lethality nor sanitizes God's character. It poses the problem and moves on, and the moving on may be the point — biblical holiness does not negotiate with our moral comfort, and we are left with the harder task of distinguishing reverent fear from theological complicity.

*Unresolved question:* If the ark of God can level its own welcoming committee on the day of its homecoming, what does it mean that this same God will eventually consent to be carried in the body of a Galilean carpenter who can be mishandled all the way to a cross — and what changed?

## The Thread

- **Numbers 4:15** — "They must not touch the holy things, lest they die." The Levitical handling protocol the Beth-shemeshites violate. The whole chapter assumes you remember this.
- **2 Samuel 6:6–7** — Uzzah's death. David repeats the Philistine error of carting the ark and learns the Beth-shemesh lesson personally. The chapters are mirror images.
- **Acts 19:13–16** — The seven sons of Sceva, gentile exorcists who try to use Jesus' name as a talisman and are beaten naked out of the house. The New Testament's Philistine-pentapolis story.
- **John 1:14** — "The Word became flesh and *tabernacled* (ἐσκήνωσεν *eskēnōsen*) among us." The flagged later passage where the holiness that killed at Beth-shemesh becomes a body that can be touched, embraced, and finally executed — without anyone being struck dead for it.
