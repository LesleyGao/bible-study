# Jeremiah 7: The Temple Sermon and the Den of Robbers

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jeremiah 7 is the first of the great prose sermons in the book and the most consequential prophetic address in pre-exilic Judah. The parallel narrative at Jeremiah 26 dates the sermon to "the beginning of the reign of Jehoiakim son of Josiah" — almost certainly 609 BC, in the months immediately after Josiah's death at Megiddo and the Egyptian-installed accession of Jehoiakim. The sermon nearly costs Jeremiah his life: chapter 26 records the priests and prophets seizing him and demanding his execution; he is saved only by the intervention of certain elders who cite the precedent of Micah of Moresheth (Mic 3:12) and by the patronage of Ahikam son of Shaphan (Jer 26:24), brother of Gemariah son of Shaphan whose seal impression has been excavated in the City of David.

The sermon's setting is the temple gate (v.2) — almost certainly the New Gate of the inner court (cf. Jer 26:10, 36:10), the public-facing access point where Jeremiah would have been addressing pilgrims arriving for one of the three pilgrimage festivals. The political moment is the highest pitch of late-Judean nationalist confidence: Josiah's reform had concentrated worship at the Jerusalem temple (2 Kgs 22–23, c. 622), the Assyrian empire had collapsed (Nineveh fell in 612), and the inviolability of Zion-theology — drawing on the deliverance under Hezekiah from Sennacherib in 701 (Isa 36–37) — had hardened into the *hekal YHWH* slogan Jeremiah quotes in v.4.

Material-cultural correspondences accumulate at every point in the chapter. **Shiloh** (v.12, 14) — the prior tabernacle site twenty miles north of Jerusalem — was destroyed in the late 11th century BC, almost certainly in connection with the Philistine victory and ark-capture of 1 Samuel 4. Excavations at Tel Shiloh under Israel Finkelstein (1981–84), Avi Ofer, and most recently Scott Stripling (2017 onward) have confirmed major Iron I destruction layers. Jeremiah's appeal — "go now to my place that was in Shiloh" — is not a rhetorical flourish; the ruin was visible. **Topheth** (v.31) sat in the Valley of the Son of Hinnom (Hebrew *Gei Ben-Hinnom*, Greek *Gehenna*), south and southwest of the City of David. The eighth-century BC Phoenician-Punic *tophet* at Carthage and the smaller tophets at Motya, Tharros, and Sulci have produced thousands of urn-burials of infant remains; the Carthaginian inscriptions explicitly name the deities Baal Hamon and Tanit. Whether Topheth at Jerusalem involved actual child sacrifice (the text's plain claim, paralleled at 2 Kgs 23:10, 16:3, 21:6, Isa 30:33) or symbolic dedication-by-fire (a minority scholarly view) is contested, but the textual witness across multiple genres and centuries is unanimous. The **Queen of Heaven** cult (vv.18, and again at 44:17–25) is most plausibly identified with Astarte/Ishtar; the family-worship description — children gather wood, fathers kindle fire, women knead dough for cakes (Hebrew *kavvanim*, possibly star-shaped wafers attested in Mesopotamian Ishtar cult) — places the cult inside Judean households, not at illicit fringe sites.

The bullae continue to align with the chapter's social world. The Pashhur ben Immer seal impression (Eilat Mazar, 2008) belongs to the priestly faction Jer 20 will name as Jeremiah's persecutor; the Gemariah ben Shaphan bulla (Yigal Shiloh, City of David, 1982) and the Ahikam-Shaphan family more broadly belong to the scribal faction that protects him at Jer 26:24.

Standard commentaries on the temple sermon: Lundbom's Anchor volume gives the maximalist Jeremianic-authorship reading; Holladay's Hermeneia volume reads vv.1–15 as a Jeremianic core with Deuteronomistic expansion; Carroll's OTL reads it as substantially Deuteronomistic; McKane's ICC argues for "rolling corpus" composition. Walter Brueggemann's *To Pluck Up, To Tear Down* takes the sermon as the paradigmatic instance of prophetic deconstruction.

Key Hebrew terms: *hekal YHWH* (הֵיכַל יְהוָה, "temple of the LORD," v.4) — the threefold slogan. *Me'arat paritsim* (מְעָרַת פָּרִצִים, "den of robbers/violent ones," v.11) — the phrase Jesus quotes at the temple cleansing. *Shilo* (שִׁילוֹ, v.12, 14) — the historical-precedent reference. *Toʿevah* (תּוֹעֵבָה, "abomination," v.10) — the Deuteronomic vocabulary of cult-violation. *Topheth* (תֹּפֶת, v.31) — likely related to Aramaic *taphya*, "fireplace/hearth," via the Hinnom child-sacrifice site. *Melekhet ha-shamayim* (מְלֶכֶת הַשָּׁמַיִם, "Queen of Heaven," v.18) — distinct from *malkat* by a single vowel and possibly intentionally ambiguous between "queen" and "service/work" of heaven.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The temple sermon is the longest and most theologically programmatic prose unit in chapters 1–10. Form-critically it exhibits the classic features of Mowinckel's C source: long sentences, Deuteronomistic vocabulary (*amend your ways and your deeds*, *the way that I commanded*, *stiff-necked*, *did not listen, did not incline the ear*), the historical recital of Israel's persistent disobedience from Egypt forward (vv.22–26), and the conditional logic of obedience-leading-to-blessing-disobedience-leading-to-curse that runs through Deuteronomy 28. The MT vs. LXX divergence is modest in this chapter; the LXX is slightly shorter at vv.1–2 and at the chapter's seams but preserves the substance of the sermon.

The chapter divides into five movements. Verses 1–15: the temple sermon proper, with the *hekal YHWH* deconstruction (vv.4, 9–11) and the Shiloh precedent (vv.12–15). Verses 16–20: the prohibition of intercession and the Queen of Heaven indictment. Verses 21–26: the rejection of empty cult and the historical recital. Verses 27–29: Jeremiah's commission to speak to a people who will not listen, with the lamentation imperative. Verses 30–34: the Topheth oracle and the Valley of Slaughter renaming.

Devices accumulate. The threefold *hekal YHWH, hekal YHWH, hekal YHWH* of v.4 mocks the liturgical *kadosh, kadosh, kadosh* (Isa 6:3); the people have replaced YHWH's holiness with the temple's identity. The Shiloh-comparison of vv.12–14 is a rhetorical *qal vahomer* (light-to-heavy) argument: if YHWH destroyed his own prior dwelling-place, what makes you think he will spare this one? The "den of robbers" of v.11 is morally ironic: a *me'arat paritsim* is not where robbers commit crimes but where they hide afterward; the temple has become the safe-house of those whose lives are committed to violation. The *hashlikhi nizrekh* ("cut off your *nezer*/consecrated hair") of v.29 is a Nazirite-lament image, signaling that consecration itself has been broken.

Most consequentially, the rejection-of-cult passage at vv.21–22 — *"add your burnt offerings to your sacrifices and eat the flesh. For in the day that I brought them out of the land of Egypt, I did not speak to your fathers or command them concerning burnt offerings and sacrifices"* — is the most disputed single verse in the prophetic corpus on the question of cult and ethics, and the rabbinic, medieval, Reformation, and modern-critical traditions read it sharply differently.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The temple sermon's canonical reach is unmatched in Jeremiah. The single most consequential reception of any Jeremiah verse anywhere in the New Testament is Jesus's citation of Jer 7:11 at the cleansing of the temple — Mark 11:17 // Matthew 21:13 // Luke 19:46 — *"is it not written, 'My house shall be called a house of prayer for all the nations'? But you have made it a den of robbers"* (combining Isa 56:7 with Jer 7:11). The temple-cleansing scene is structurally framed by Mark as Jesus's enactment of Jeremiah 7: he stands in the temple, he indicts its corruption, he predicts its destruction (Mark 13:2). The historical Jesus, on the most plausible reading (E.P. Sanders, N.T. Wright, Dale Allison), saw himself standing in continuity with Jeremiah and possibly typologically *as* Jeremiah (cf. Matt 16:14, "some say… Jeremiah"). The temple destruction in 70 AD was read by the early church (and by Josephus, *War* 6.300–309) as the second fulfillment of the Jeremian threat — the new Shiloh.

Jewish reception is layered. Sirach 49:6 names Jeremiah as the one who suffered at the hands of the people. Bavli Yoma 9b cites the temple destruction as caused by *sin'at hinam* (causeless hatred) — a reading that absorbs Jer 7's ethical-cultic indictment. Bavli Megillah 14b and Lamentations Rabbah connect Jer 7 to the Tisha B'Av liturgy. Rashi reads vv.21–22 (the "I did not speak about burnt offerings") as polemical: God commanded *the obedience that should have accompanied* sacrifices, not the sacrifices themselves. Radak develops this. **Maimonides' *Guide of the Perplexed* 3.32 reads the rejection-of-cult passage with breathtaking radicalism**: sacrifices were a *concession* (Hebrew *taqqanah*) to the people's habituation in Egyptian polytheistic ritual; God did not originally desire them but accommodated to human weakness, with the long-term goal of weaning Israel toward direct worship through prayer (a reading that anticipates the post-70 AD synagogal-prayer-for-sacrifice substitution). Maimonides' reading is one of the most consequential single hermeneutical moves in medieval Jewish thought; it has shaped everything from Reformation cult-critique through modern Jewish liturgical theology. Abrabanel pushes back, reading vv.21–22 as polemical hyperbole within a cult-affirming Torah frame. The medieval Tisha B'Av liturgy uses Jeremiah 7 extensively; the *kinot* (lamentation poems) draw heavily on its imagery.

Christian reception is similarly weighty. **Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah*** (c. 240) reads the temple sermon allegorically: the temple is the soul, the sacrifices are spiritual offerings, and the *hekal YHWH* slogan is the self-deception of the unconverted heart. **Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah*** treats the chapter at length; Jerome reads vv.21–22 polemically (parallel to Hosea 6:6) and applies the Shiloh-precedent typologically to the 70 AD destruction. Augustine cites Jer 7 against Pelagian confidence in cultic righteousness. Aquinas engages vv.21–22 in *Summa Theologiae* I-II.103 on the ceremonial law, reading them as declarations of priority (obedience over sacrifice) rather than abolition. **Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah* (1563, his largest Old Testament commentary)** reads the temple sermon as the Reformation's prophetic precedent: the *hekal YHWH* slogan is the medieval doctrine of *ex opere operato* — the assumption that the institutional church automatically delivers grace — and Jeremiah's deconstruction is precisely the Reformation move. Luther preached on Jer 7 in similar terms.

The critical tradition has read Jer 7 (especially vv.21–22) as foundational evidence for what **Julius Wellhausen** called "ethical monotheism" — the alleged prophetic-religion-without-cult that Wellhausen took to be the original true religion of Israel before the priestly redactors imposed sacrificial law. Wellhausen's reading (*Prolegomena to the History of Israel*, 1878/83) was challenged by Yehezkel Kaufmann (*The Religion of Israel*, English ed. 1960) and is now generally rejected in its strong form, but the question of how Jer 7:21–22 relates to the Pentateuchal cult law remains live. Lundbom, Holladay, Carroll, and McKane each handle it differently. Kathleen O'Connor's trauma-informed reading (*Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*, 2011) takes the chapter's relentless rhetoric as therapeutic disclosure.

Where the traditions diverge: the Reformation reads the temple sermon as a charter for cultic critique; the Catholic and Orthodox traditions read it as a critique of *empty* cult that nonetheless presumes a true cult; the Jewish tradition reads it through the post-70 AD substitution of prayer for sacrifice (Maimonides) or as polemical hyperbole (Abrabanel); the critical tradition reads it as evidence for the development of Israelite religion. Christological reading: Jesus stood in the temple gate as Jeremiah had, and the temple fell (again).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The temple sermon's repudiation of cult.** Verses 21–22 — *"I did not speak to your fathers or command them concerning burnt offerings or sacrifices"* — is one of the most theologically loaded verses in the Old Testament. The Wellhausen reading (1878) takes it at face value as evidence that the original prophetic religion was ethical, not cultic, and that the Pentateuchal sacrificial system was a later priestly construction; this reading drove much of late-19th-century liberal Protestant theology. **Maimonides' reading** (*Guide* 3.32) takes it almost as radically: sacrifices were a divine *concession* to Egyptian-trained habituation, not a positive command; the long arc of religion is toward prayer and ethical worship. The Catholic Magisterial reading (CCC §§1539–1547 on the priesthood, §§2099–2100 on sacrifice) preserves cultic theology but reads vv.21–22 as a prophetic critique of cult divorced from ethical obedience — parallel to Hosea 6:6, Amos 5:21, Mic 6:6–8. The Reformed reading (Calvin, Owen, Hodge) takes vv.21–22 as proto-Reformation polemic against ritualism. The Lutheran reading is similar but emphasizes the law-gospel distinction: the cult was always meant to point beyond itself to Christ, and vv.21–22 expose any cult that does not. The Eastern Orthodox tradition reads vv.21–22 through the patristic typology of cult-fulfilled-in-Christ: the Eucharist is not the same as Levitical sacrifice and is not subject to the same critique. **Brueggemann's reading** in *The Prophetic Imagination* takes the temple sermon as the paradigm of prophetic deconstruction: the *hekal YHWH* slogan is the religious ideology that legitimizes the royal-imperial consciousness, and the sermon's task is to dissolve that ideology. The steelman of the radical (Wellhausen / Maimonides) reading: vv.21–22 read straight do appear to deny that God commanded sacrifices at the Exodus; if the verse is hyperbole, where does the hyperbole stop? The polemical-hyperbole reading (Calvin, Aquinas, Abrabanel) is the historical mainstream but it works only by importing the Pentateuchal cult-law assumption that vv.21–22 may be designed to call into question. **Where this leaves us:** every defense of religious institutional structure — Catholic, Orthodox, Jewish, Reformed — must walk past Jer 7:21–22 with full attention. The verse does not abolish cult, but it forbids cult from being the *guarantor* of relationship. The institutional apparatus of religion can do many things; one thing it cannot do is replace obedience.

**Tension 2: The prohibition of intercession.** "Do not pray for this people, or lift up a cry or prayer for them, and do not intercede with me, for I will not hear you" (v.16, repeated at 11:14 and 14:11) is the most extreme prophetic statement on the limits of intercession in the Old Testament. The Catholic intercessory tradition — the saints, Mary as Mediatrix, the priesthood praying *in persona Christi* — must reckon with the possibility that intercession can be theologically *closed*. The Catholic reading typically holds that v.16 represents a particular historical moment of judgment, not a permanent ontological possibility; intercession remains the church's vocation, but there are points (analogous to mortal sin without repentance, or to the "sin against the Holy Spirit" of Mark 3:29 // Matt 12:31–32) where intercession reaches its theological limit. The Reformed reading takes v.16 through the doctrine of providential decree: God has already determined the outcome, and the prohibition of intercession is a sign of decretive finality. The Lutheran reading is similar but sits less comfortably with absolute decretal logic. The Jewish tradition reckons with v.16 through the *teshuvah* limit-cases: the rabbinic discussion at Yoma 86a–b on the kinds of sin that Yom Kippur cannot atone for; the Talmudic discussion at Berakhot 10a of Hezekiah's "even if a sharp sword is on your neck, do not despair of mercy" — held against Jeremiah's prohibition. The Eastern Orthodox tradition tends to read v.16 within a synergistic frame: the door of *metanoia* is never absolutely closed, but God may ratify a self-chosen closure. The 1 John 5:16 distinction between sin "leading to death" (for which "I do not say you should pray") and other sin engages the same structural problem. **The steelman of the radical reading**: if Jer 7:16 means what it says, intercession has theological boundaries, and prophetic authority can include the authority to *refuse to pray*. This is not a comfortable doctrine for any tradition that has built its pastoral practice around the unrestricted availability of intercession. **Where this leaves us:** v.16 forces every tradition to specify what intercession is for. If intercession can secure pardon for the unrepentant, v.16 forbids it; if intercession is the prayer that asks God to draw the elect or convert the persistent sinner, the question becomes whether God reserves the right to ratify a heart's persistent self-closure — and v.16 says he sometimes does.

The unresolved question that lands hardest: at v.31 — the Topheth-and-child-sacrifice indictment — Jeremiah quotes God saying "*which I did not command, nor did it come into my mind*" (Hebrew: *velo 'altah 'al libbi*, "neither did it ascend into my heart"). The phrase asserts that the Molech-and-Topheth practice was so foreign to YHWH's character that even imagining it was beyond him. But if YHWH is the omniscient God of biblical theism, what does it mean for something *not to come into his mind*? The phrase is an anthropomorphism, certainly — but its rhetorical force depends on it being read as more than mere hyperbole. The Augustinian-Thomist tradition reads it analogically: God knows all things, but child sacrifice was foreign to his salvific will. The open-theist reading (Pinnock, Boyd) takes the verse as evidence that the future is genuinely open to God. The rabbinic reading often softens it. The hardest reading — the one Jeremiah's language presses — is that there are evils so deep that even attributing them to divine permission, much less divine providence, reaches the limit of what the biblical writers could bear. What does it mean to confess God's sovereignty over a history that includes Topheth, Auschwitz, and the Hinnom valleys still operating around the world? The chapter does not answer.

## The Thread

The cross-references that radiate from chapter 7 are unusually dense. First, Deuteronomy 12 (the centralization of worship) and 28 (the curses) sit behind the entire sermon — vv.5–7's conditional-obedience language (*if you truly amend… then I will let you dwell*) is straight Deuteronomistic. Second, Psalm 78:60–64 — "*he forsook his dwelling at Shiloh, the tent where he dwelt among mankind*" — is the Psalmist's commemoration of exactly the Shiloh-precedent Jeremiah cites at vv.12–14; Jer 7 reads as the prophetic application of Ps 78's historical theology. Third, Jesus's citation of v.11 at the temple cleansing (Mark 11:17 // Matt 21:13 // Luke 19:46) is the most consequential New Testament use of Jeremiah; combined with Jesus's predictions of the temple's destruction (Mark 13:2), it positions the events of 70 AD as the structural recurrence of 587 BC. Fourth, the new covenant of Jeremiah 31:31–34 — "*not like the covenant I made with their fathers when I took them by the hand to bring them out of Egypt*" — picks up the Egypt-and-disobedience recital of 7:22–26 and resolves it: the covenant that was repeatedly broken will be replaced by one written on the heart. And fifth, forward to Lamentations 4:11–12 — "*the LORD gave full vent to his wrath; he poured out his hot anger… kings of the earth did not believe… that foe or enemy could enter the gates of Jerusalem*" — the post-587 commemoration of the *hekal YHWH* slogan's empirical refutation. Read 7:14's threat through Lam 4:11–12, and the temple sermon stops being a warning. It becomes a coroner's report.
