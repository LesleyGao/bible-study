# Mark 9: Glory on the Mountain, Failure in the Valley

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Mark 9 hinges on a topographical question that 1st-century readers would have parsed instinctively but that has divided readers ever since: which "high mountain" did Jesus climb? Tradition from at least the 4th century (Cyril of Jerusalem; later Jerome) fixed on **Mt. Tabor**, a striking dome rising 575 meters out of the Jezreel Valley near Nazareth. The Byzantine basilica there, rebuilt by Crusaders and again by Franciscans in 1924, sits atop foundations excavated to reveal 4th-century pilgrim structures. But the narrative geography of Mark 8-9 places Jesus near **Caesarea Philippi** at the base of **Mt. Hermon** (2,814 meters), a snow-capped massif on the northern border. Joel Marcus (Anchor Bible) and R.T. France (NIGTC) both argue Hermon is the more probable setting, since Tabor is over 60 miles south and was, in the 1st century, fortified and inhabited (Josephus, *War* 4.1.8 records Hermon's slopes as remote pagan territory), making the "by themselves" of 9:2 implausible. Adela Yarbro Collins (Hermeneia) hedges, noting that Mark's "high mountain" is more theological signal — the mountain of revelation — than geographical claim.

The Greco-Roman material culture of the chapter is dense. The boy with the unclean spirit who "**foams** (***aphrizei***, ἀφρίζει) and **grinds his teeth** (***trizei tous odontas***, τρίζει τοὺς ὀδόντας) and **becomes rigid** (***xērainetai***, ξηραίνεται)" presents a clinical description that ancient medical writers (Hippocrates, *On the Sacred Disease*) would have classified as ***epilēpsia***. Matthew 17:15 in fact uses ***selēniazetai*** ("moonstruck"), the standard ancient Greek euphemism. Mark, characteristically, refuses the medical category and stays with the demonological frame — a reminder that 1st-century Galilean villagers and Roman physicians could observe the same convulsions and reach radically different ontologies. Levine's *Misunderstood Jew* warns against the Christian habit of treating "Jewish demonology" as primitive while reading Greek medical writing as proto-scientific; both are folk taxonomies of suffering.

Three Greek terms anchor the chapter's theology. ***Stilbonta*** (στίλβοντα, 9:3) — Jesus's clothes "gleaming," a participle Mark uses nowhere else, drawn from LXX vocabulary for celestial bodies (Daniel 12:3, the righteous shining like stars). ***Metamorphōthē*** (μετεμορφώθη, 9:2) — "he was transfigured," the same verb Paul uses in Romans 12:2 and 2 Corinthians 3:18 for the moral-eschatological transformation of believers. The lexical link is structurally important: what happens to Jesus in concentrated form on the mountain is what happens to disciples extensively over time. ***Geenna*** (γέεννα, 9:43-48) — "hell," a Greek transliteration of Aramaic ***Gei-Hinnom***, the Valley of the Sons of Hinnom, the steep ravine on the southwestern flank of Jerusalem. Archaeology of the Wadi er-Rababi (the modern site identification) shows it was used for refuse disposal in the Second Temple period; biblical memory (2 Kings 23:10, Jeremiah 7:31, 19:5) records it as the site of child sacrifice to Molech under Ahaz and Manasseh, defiled by Josiah's reforms. By the 1st century, Gehenna had become a metonym for eschatological punishment in apocalyptic literature (1 Enoch 27, 4 Ezra 7), though the precise content of that punishment — torment, destruction, purification — was unsettled and would remain so. Daniel Boyarin emphasizes that no monolithic 1st-century Jewish doctrine of hell existed; the imagery was malleable.

The ***denarion*** does not appear in Mark 9, but the social world of "**a cup of water**" (9:41) does. Roman Galilee in the 60s CE was a society of patron-client networks where small acts of hospitality (the offered cup) registered patron-deference relations. Jesus inverts the logic: the cup given to "**you who belong to Christ**" generates reward not from the human patron but from God. That phrasing — "belong to Christ" (***Christou este***, Χριστοῦ ἐστε) — is so close to Paul's "of Christ" formula (1 Corinthians 1:12, 3:23) that Marcus, like Bultmann before him, suspects redactional layering: an early Pauline-style identity marker dropped into a Jesus saying.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Mark 9 is the structural pivot of the gospel. The chapter falls in the central panel of Mark's tripartite design: chapters 1-8 establish Jesus's identity (climaxing in Peter's confession at Caesarea Philippi, 8:29), chapters 8:31-10:52 form the journey-to-Jerusalem section organized around three passion predictions (8:31, 9:31, 10:33-34), and chapters 11-16 narrate the passion itself. The **second passion prediction** (9:31) sits at the chapter's hinge. Mary Ann Tolbert (*Sowing the Gospel*) reads each passion prediction as triggering a discipleship-failure cycle: Jesus predicts suffering, the disciples misunderstand, Jesus teaches the way of the cross. The pattern is so tight in 9:30-37 (prediction → silent argument about who is greatest → child-as-model teaching) that it functions as a parable enacted by the disciples themselves.

Markan literary signatures saturate the chapter. ***Euthys*** (εὐθύς, "immediately") appears at 9:15, 20, 24 — Mark's signature breathless adverb, here marking the urgency of the boy's father, the spirit's reaction, and the cry of imperfect faith. The historical present surfaces at 9:2 ("**leads them up**"), 9:5 ("**says to him**"), and elsewhere — Mark's narratival immediacy that Matthew and Luke routinely smooth into past tense. The **Transfiguration scene** itself is constructed as a textbook theophany, deliberately echoing Sinai: "**after six days**" (9:2) parallels Exodus 24:16 (Moses waits six days before the cloud calls him on the seventh); the cloud overshadows (cf. Exodus 40:34-38); the divine voice declares ("**This is my beloved Son; listen to him**" — fusing Psalm 2:7's royal sonship with Deuteronomy 18:15's prophet-like-Moses oracle).

Frank Kermode's *Genesis of Secrecy* reads the **Messianic Secret** motif (Wrede 1901) as reaching its sharpest expression at 9:9, where Jesus charges Peter, James, and John to tell no one "**until the Son of Man had risen from the dead**." Mark's narrator notes that the disciples kept silent, "questioning what this rising from the dead might mean" (9:10). The disciples are inside the secret and yet outside its meaning — a structural feature that Kermode treats as the gospel's defining hermeneutical irony. Werner Kelber (*Oral and Written Gospel*) sees the Transfiguration as evidence that Mark inherited a body of oral epiphany traditions and used them precisely to undercut the Christology of glory in favor of the theology of the cross — the voice on the mountain says "**listen to him**," and the next thing Jesus says is that he will be killed.

A textual variant of major historical interest sits at 9:29: "**this kind cannot be driven out by anything but prayer**." A wide swath of later manuscripts (including Sinaiticus's corrector, the Byzantine tradition, the Vulgate) add "**and fasting**." Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary* and modern critical editions (NA28, UBS5) judge "and fasting" a secondary scribal addition reflecting the rise of monastic ascetic theology in the 3rd-4th centuries. The shorter reading ("only by prayer") is supported by the earliest witnesses (Sinaiticus*, Vaticanus). Verses 44 and 46 ("where their worm does not die and the fire is not quenched"), repeating verse 48, are also widely judged secondary harmonizing additions and are absent from the best witnesses — which is why most modern translations skip from verse 43 to verse 45 to verse 47.

Rhoads, Dewey, and Michie (*Mark as Story*) note Mark 9:33-50 as a tightly woven cluster of catchword-linked sayings: "in my name" (9:37, 38, 39, 41), "stumble/cause to sin" (9:42, 43, 45, 47), "fire" (9:43, 48, 49), "salt" (9:49, 50). Mark or his tradition appears to have transmitted these as a memorized teaching unit, organized for oral retention.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The **Transfiguration** has generated two thick interpretive trajectories. The Christian tradition reads it as proleptic glory — a momentary unveiling of the Son's divine identity, anticipating resurrection. **Origen** (*Commentary on Matthew* 12.36-43) reads Moses and Elijah as the law and the prophets, both subordinated to Jesus and dissolved when "**they no longer saw anyone with them but Jesus only**." **Augustine** (*Sermon* 78) treats the three tents Peter wanted to build as a typology of three insufficient theologies (legalism, prophetism, christological isolation), corrected by the divine voice. **Bede** (*Commentary on Mark*) reads the six days as the six ages of world history culminating in the seventh-day eschaton. **Aquinas** (*Catena Aurea*) collects all three threads.

The Eastern Christian trajectory diverges decisively. The Feast of Transfiguration (August 6) became, by the late Byzantine period, the doctrinal centerpiece of **Hesychast** theology. **Gregory Palamas** (1296-1359), defending the monks of Mt. Athos against the Calabrian philosopher Barlaam, argued that the light Peter, James, and John saw was the **uncreated light** of God's energies — genuinely divine, genuinely visible, neither symbolic nor metaphorical. Barlaam, working from a more Western Augustinian-Aristotelian framework, insisted any visible light must be created. The councils of Constantinople (1341, 1347, 1351) sided with Palamas; the **essence-energies distinction** became permanent Eastern dogma, and the Transfiguration became its proof text. **Calvin** (*Harmony*) walks the Western middle path: the light was real but accommodated to human eyes, a sign rather than the divine essence itself. **Luther** read the Transfiguration as comfort for the suffering church — the cross is the public face, but the glory is real.

The Jewish reception is sparse — the scene has no Jewish-tradition parallel — but **Daniel Boyarin** (*Jewish Gospels*) argues that the Markan Transfiguration is intelligible only against Daniel 7's vision of the Ancient of Days and the **Son of Man**: the cloud, the divine voice, the supernatural illumination are Danielic apocalyptic furniture, drawn from a Jewish tradition that already held space for a divine-human figure. **Geza Vermes** (*Jesus the Jew*) reads the scene as a post-Easter glorification narrative read back into Jesus's lifetime; **E.P. Sanders** (*Historical Figure*) treats it as one of the gospel's least historically accessible scenes precisely because the literary craft is so self-evidently theological.

The **"I believe; help my unbelief"** (9:24) of the boy's father may be the most universally cited verse of Mark 9 in pastoral history. **Augustine** (*Confessions* 1.5) treats it as the prayer of every honest catechumen. **Bernard of Clairvaux** uses it as a liturgical refrain. **Calvin** (*Institutes* 3.2.17) uses it as proof that faith and doubt coexist in the regenerate; faith is not the absence of unbelief but trust under siege. **John Wesley** preached on it repeatedly; his journal entries cite it during periods of doubt about his own assurance. **Karl Barth** (*Church Dogmatics* IV/1) treats it as the paradigmatic prayer of the church, which is always simultaneously believing and unbelieving. **Bonhoeffer** (*Discipleship*) uses it to puncture cheap-grace optimism: faith is not a feeling of certainty but a costly act under uncertainty.

The ***Geenna*** sayings (9:43-48) generate sharp ongoing trajectories. **Origen** (*De Principiis* 2.10) and the broader Alexandrian tradition lean toward purgative or ultimately restorative readings, eventually condemned at the Fifth Ecumenical Council (553) as ***apokatastasis***. **Augustine** (*City of God* 21) entrenches the eternal-conscious-torment reading that becomes Western orthodoxy. **Aquinas** systematizes it. **Edward Fudge** (*The Fire That Consumes*, 1982) and the **conditionalist/annihilationist** trajectory argue that the worm-and-fire imagery from Isaiah 66:24 — Mark's direct citation — depicts the **destruction** of the dead, not their everlasting torment; the worms eat **corpses** in Isaiah, and the fire consumes. **Steve Gregg** (*All You Want to Know About Hell*) maps three positions (eternal torment, conditional annihilation, evangelical universalism), each with patristic roots. **Daniel Boyarin** notes that 1st-century Jewish Gehenna was not yet doctrinally fixed and that Jesus's Gehenna sayings exploit imaginative ambiguity rather than codifying a system.

**Joel Marcus (AB)** treats the **second passion prediction** as evidence of Mark's tightly engineered "way of the cross" theology — the disciples cannot understand the prediction because they have not yet understood that the Son of Man's glory and the Son of Man's death are the same event. **Morna Hooker (BNTC)** stresses the disciples' fear (9:32 — they were "**afraid to ask**"); fear, not stupidity, governs their incomprehension. **Vincent Taylor** (1952) noted the prediction's brevity compared with 8:31 and 10:33-34 and treated this as evidence that 9:31 is the most primitive form of the saying. **Ched Myers** (*Binding the Strong Man*) reads the entire 8:22-10:52 section as a sustained political critique of the disciples' Davidic-restoration messianism; the child-as-model teaching (9:33-37) is Jesus's anti-imperial pedagogy. **N.T. Wright** (*Mark for Everyone*) reads the failed exorcism (9:14-29) as Mark's parable of the post-Easter church: the Master is on the mountain, the disciples are in the valley, the world is in agony, and only prayer keeps the connection live.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions sit at the heart of Mark 9.

**Tension 1: The relation of glory and the cross.** The Transfiguration shows the disciples Christ in glory; Jesus immediately predicts his death. Mark refuses to let the two scenes settle into a smooth synthesis. **Position A (Lutheran theology of the cross)** — best articulated by **Luther** and developed by **Bonhoeffer** — argues that the glory is real but veiled; the cross is the primary revelation, and any theology of glory unmoored from the cross becomes triumphalism. **Position B (Eastern hesychast)** — Palamas, Maximus the Confessor, the Philokalia tradition — argues that the glory is the goal, the **theōsis** (deification) toward which the cross is the means; the divine light on the mountain is the eschatological telos of the Christian. **Position C (Reformed-Calvinist accommodation)** — Calvin, B.B. Warfield, more recently Richard Bauckham — argues that the glory is genuinely Christ's but accommodated to disciples who could not have borne the unmediated reality; the Transfiguration is pedagogical, not the ontological climax of revelation.

The steelman position for the Eastern view is its capacity to take seriously the ***telos*** of Christian life. If the cross is the only framework, what is the resurrection for? Luther himself acknowledged that the theology of the cross presupposes glory, even if hidden. Where this leaves us: Mark refuses both pure cross-mysticism and pure glory-mysticism. The Transfiguration is real; the disciples come down the mountain; the boy is convulsing in the valley. The structure is itself the theology.

**Tension 2: Hell, the worm, the fire — what do the Gehenna sayings teach?** **Position A (Eternal Conscious Torment)** — Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin, the Westminster Confession, contemporary defenders like J.I. Packer and Tim Keller — argues that 9:48's "**fire is not quenched**" entails the unending conscious suffering of the lost; God's holiness demands infinite penalty for sin against an infinite being. **Position B (Conditional Immortality / Annihilationism)** — Edward Fudge, John Stott (carefully, in *Essentials*), Anthony Thiselton — argues that Isaiah 66:24, the verse Jesus cites, depicts corpses being consumed; the worm and fire are agents of destruction, not preservation; "eternal" describes the irreversibility of the destruction, not its duration. **Position C (Evangelical Universalism / Restorationism)** — Origen, Gregory of Nyssa, more recently Robin Parry (*The Evangelical Universalist*), David Bentley Hart (*That All Shall Be Saved*) — argues that the fire is purgative; Gehenna sayings function pedagogically and apocalyptically, not as a final dogmatic statement.

The steelman for the conditionalist position is its closer fit with the actual Old Testament text Jesus cites and with the Hebrew imagination of death-as-destruction rather than death-as-conscious-existence. The steelman for the universalist position is its theological fit with Romans 5, 1 Corinthians 15:22-28, and the patristic restoration tradition. Where this leaves us: Mark gives us terrifying imagery, not a doctrine. Jesus is not in 9:43-48 doing systematic eschatology; he is using maximalist rhetoric to convey that what is at stake in causing little ones to stumble is, in some real sense, ultimate. Reading Mark for a worked-out doctrine of hell is a category error; reading Mark for a refusal to trivialize sin is correct.

The unresolved question: if Jesus identifies John the Baptist as the returning Elijah (9:13) but John was beheaded by Herod's military-political apparatus, how does that retroactively reframe the messianic-precursor expectation? Has the prophesied "restoration" already failed, or has it succeeded in a form unrecognizable to those expecting it? Mark leaves the question hanging.

## The Thread

Mark 9:7's **divine voice** ("**This is my beloved Son; listen to him**") echoes back to Mark 1:11 (the baptismal voice) and forward to the centurion's confession at the cross (15:39). The voice on the mountain, the voice at the river, and the human confession at the foot of the cross form a Markan trinity of recognition.

Mark 9:31's **second passion prediction** chains to Mark 8:31, 10:33-34, and ultimately to 14:41 (the betrayal scene): the language of the Son of Man being "delivered into the hands of men" surfaces precisely as Judas arrives.

Mark 9:35's "**if anyone would be first, he must be last of all and servant of all**" resurfaces in 10:43-45, where it is grounded explicitly in the ***lytron*** (ransom) saying. The child of 9:36 prefigures the children Jesus blesses in 10:13-16.

Mark 9:42-50's **stumbling-block warnings** flag forward to Mark 14:27 ("**you will all fall away** [skandalisthēsesthe]"), where the twelve themselves become the stumbling little ones. The warning rebounds on the warners.
