# Isaiah 51 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Isaiah 51 belongs to the central drumbeat of **Second Isaiah** (chs. 40-55), the exilic prophetic corpus addressed to Judeans displaced to Babylon in the wake of Jerusalem's destruction in 587 BC. Most mainstream-critical scholars (Westermann, Blenkinsopp, Goldingay, Roy Melugin, Patricia Tull, Whybray, Muilenburg) date Second Isaiah to the closing years of the Babylonian empire, roughly 545-540 BC, when the rise of Cyrus the Persian had begun to make a return to Zion thinkable. The unified-defense tradition (Motyer, Oswalt, with Childs's canonical reading bracketing the historical question) reads chapters 40-55 as the work of the eighth-century Isaiah ben Amoz projecting forward. The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran (1QIsaᵃ, c. 125 BC) preserves the entire book as a single continuous text — including chapter 51 — without any compositional seam between chapters 39 and 40 or anywhere within Second Isaiah itself. For this study I follow the mainstream-critical placement: chapter 51 is exilic prophecy spoken to a community trying to remember whether their God can still act.

The chapter divides into three rhetorical *attentas* (Hebrew **שִׁמְעוּ אֵלַי**, *shim'u elai*, "listen to me," vv. 1, 4, 7) followed by a dramatic shift at verse 9 where the *prophet himself* turns and addresses Yahweh's arm in vocative imperative — *"Awake, awake!"* — followed by Yahweh's responsive *"I, I am he who comforts you"* in verse 12, and a closing summons to Jerusalem in verses 17-23. The chapter's heart is its triple imperative *uri-uri* / *hit'oreri hit'oreri* (**עוּרִי עוּרִי**, **הִתְעוֹרְרִי הִתְעוֹרְרִי**) — *Awake, awake!* — first addressed to Yahweh's arm (v. 9), then to Jerusalem (v. 17), and (in the next chapter) to Zion (52:1). The pattern is rhetorically deliberate: the prophet first calls Yahweh to wake, then calls his people to wake, then in 52:1 calls Zion to put on her splendid garments. The exiles, in this prophet's hearing, are sleeping under the weight of Babylonian power, and the prophet stages a liturgical awakening.

Verse 1's reach back to **Abraham and Sarah** is no incidental flourish. *"Look to the rock from which you were hewn, and to the quarry from which you were dug"* — Hebrew **הַבִּיטוּ אֶל־צוּר חֻצַּבְתֶּם וְאֶל־מַקֶּבֶת בּוֹר נֻקַּרְתֶּם** (*habitu el-tsur chutsavtem ve-el-maqevet bor nuqartem*) — uses quarry imagery to invoke the patriarchal origins. *He was but one when I called him* (v. 2): the appeal is to a pattern. Yahweh's history with Israel began with one barren couple and a bare promise, and the exilic remnant — small, scattered, doubtful — should look not to its present numbers but to the precedent of Abraham. This is *Heilsgeschichte* (salvation-history) used pastorally: the past is a warrant for the future. The same mode appears in the rib of 50:1-3 and the *new exodus* announcements throughout Second Isaiah (43:16-21; 48:20-21).

The chapter's most arresting historical-religious feature is its **mythological vocabulary** in verses 9-10. The prophet calls on Yahweh's arm to act *as in days of old* (**כִּימֵי קֶדֶם דֹּרוֹת עוֹלָמִים**, *kimei qedem dorot olamim*), and the deeds named are *cutting Rahab in pieces*, *piercing the dragon* (**הַמַּחְצֶבֶת רַהַב מְחוֹלֶלֶת תַּנִּין**, *hamachtsevet Rahab mecholelet tannin*), and *drying up the sea*. These are not merely Exodus references; they are echoes of the **ancient Near Eastern Combat Myth** in which a creator-god defeats a chaos-monster (Marduk vs. Tiamat in the Babylonian *Enuma Elish*; Baal vs. Yamm/Lotan in the Ugaritic Baal Cycle) to establish cosmic order. Frank Moore Cross's *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* and Mark S. Smith's *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* document the way Israelite poetry repeatedly reuses Combat-Myth imagery, demythologized but unmistakable. **Rahab** (a poetic name for a chaos-sea-monster, distinct from the woman in Joshua 2) and **tannin** (the dragon, cognate to Ugaritic *tnn*) are the chaos-figures defeated at creation. By invoking them in the same breath as the drying of the Reed Sea, the prophet collapses three events into one: creation, exodus, and the imminent new exodus from Babylon are a single act of Yahweh's arm. The chaos-power Babylon claims is older than Babylon and was already defeated *kimei qedem*, in the days of old.

## Lens 2: The Literature

The chapter's prophetic poetry is structured by repeated calls to attention and dramatic vocative shifts. The threefold *shim'u elai* in verses 1, 4, 7 is a rhetorical scaffold: the first call summons *those who pursue righteousness* and grounds them in Abraham; the second summons *my people, my nation* and orients them outward to the *coastlands* who wait for Yahweh's arm; the third summons *those who know righteousness* and tells them not to fear the reproach of man, because the moth will eat their accusers like wool — an image taken straight from 50:9. The triple call moves the audience inward (origins), outward (mission to nations), and forward (durability of righteousness against ephemeral oppression).

Verse 9's vocative shift is one of the most dramatic moments in Second Isaiah. The prophet — who has been ventriloquizing Yahweh — suddenly turns and speaks *to* Yahweh: *"Awake, awake, put on strength, O arm of the LORD!"* Hebrew **עוּרִי עוּרִי לִבְשִׁי־עֹז זְרוֹעַ יְהוָה** (*uri uri livshi-oz zero'a Yahweh*). The verb **עוּר** (*ur*, "to awaken") is here in the qal imperative feminine singular, addressed to **זְרוֹעַ** (*zero'a*, "arm"), which is grammatically feminine. The prophet treats Yahweh's arm almost as a quasi-personified agent — a pattern that will reappear in 53:1, where the *arm of the LORD* is *revealed* in the suffering Servant. The doubled imperative *uri uri* mirrors creation-language and lament-psalm formulas (cf. Psa 44:23, *"awake, why are you sleeping, O Lord?"*; Psa 35:23). The prophet is calling Yahweh to act with the same arm that defeated Rahab and dried the sea.

The Combat-Myth lines in 9-10 use participial constructions to telescope time: *the one cutting Rahab... the one piercing the dragon... the one drying up the sea*. The participles are not past-tense report; they are descriptive titles of who Yahweh is. The same arm is the same arm. Verse 11 then bursts into a vision of return: *"the ransomed of the LORD shall return and come to Zion with singing"* — a verse so closely paralleled in 35:10 that scholars debate whether one is borrowing from the other or both draw from a shared liturgical formula. **פְּדוּיֵי יְהוָה** (*peduyei Yahweh*, "the redeemed of the LORD") is the technical term for Israelites whom Yahweh has bought back — the language of kinsman-redemption (cf. Lev 25; Ruth 4) projected onto national restoration.

Verse 12 inaugurates Yahweh's response with the emphatic doubled pronoun: *"I, I am he who comforts you"* — Hebrew **אָנֹכִי אָנֹכִי הוּא מְנַחֶמְכֶם** (*anokhi anokhi hu menachemkhem*). The reduplication is intensification — *It is none other than I myself who comforts*. The construction echoes 43:11 and 43:25 and reinforces the divine-self-identification formula that pervades Second Isaiah. The rhetorical point: the people called Yahweh's arm to wake; Yahweh answers that he was never asleep — he is *the comforter*, and they have *forgotten* him while fearing *the wrath of the oppressor* whose wrath is itself fading (*and where is the wrath of the oppressor?*, v. 13).

The closing scene (vv. 17-23) introduces one of the most theologically charged metaphors in the prophets: the **cup of staggering**. Hebrew **כּוֹס תַּרְעֵלָה** (*kos tar'elah*) — sometimes rendered *cup of reeling* — is the cup Jerusalem has drained to its dregs. The metaphor of Yahweh's wrath as a cup that the nations and Israel must drink runs through Jeremiah 25:15-29, Ezekiel 23:31-34, Habakkuk 2:16, Psalm 75:8, and Lamentations 4:21. Drinking the cup means absorbing the full weight of divine judgment. In verse 22 Yahweh declares the inversion: *"I have taken from your hand the cup of staggering... and I will put it into the hand of your tormentors."* The cup does not vanish — it is reassigned. Babylon will drink what Jerusalem drank.

The image of *making your back like the ground for them to pass over* (v. 23) is brutal and concrete: ancient conquest iconography depicted kings literally placing their feet on the necks of conquered enemies (cf. Joshua 10:24). The exiles have lain face-down for the Babylonians to walk on. That posture is now ending.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

The chapter knits together several major prophetic trajectories. **Creation and exodus** are fused in verses 9-10: the chaos-monster defeated at the beginning of the world is the same chaos-monster defeated at the Reed Sea, and both are the precedent for the new exodus from Babylon. This fusion is a Second Isaiah hallmark and shapes how later biblical and Jewish tradition reads creation as itself an act of redemption — a thread Jon Levenson develops in *Creation and the Persistence of Evil*. The prophetic canon picks up the theme: Daniel 7's chaos-beasts rising from the sea, Revelation 12-13's dragon and sea-beast, and Revelation 21:1's *"the sea was no more"* all draw from the Combat-Myth grammar Isaiah 51 deploys.

The Jewish reception of Isaiah 51 has emphasized its **Zion-restoration** and **Abrahamic-rootage** themes. The chapter is read in synagogues as part of the *Haftarah of Consolation* (the seven prophetic readings between Tisha B'Av and Rosh Hashanah, drawn from Isaiah 40-66), where it sits in a sequence of restoration oracles that follow the lament for Jerusalem's destruction. Rashi (1040-1105) reads the *cup of staggering* as Jerusalem's historical suffering and the inversion in verse 22 as eschatological, awaiting the messianic age. Modern Jewish scholarship — Levenson, Michael Fishbane on inner-biblical exegesis, Benjamin Sommer on the *Prophet Reads Scripture* — emphasizes how Second Isaiah works by reactivating earlier traditions (Genesis 12, Exodus 15, the chaos-myth fragments embedded in Psalms) and projecting them forward.

The Christian trajectory has read the chapter through a series of inflection points. The *arm of the LORD* tradition (Hebrew *zero'a Yahweh*, Greek *brachion Kyriou* in the LXX) becomes a Christological title in early Christian reading. Justin Martyr (*Dialogue with Trypho* 114), Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.6.1), and Tertullian read *the arm of the LORD* as a circumlocution for the pre-incarnate Christ — the agent of Yahweh's deeds. Paul's language in Romans 1:16 — *"the gospel is the power of God for salvation"* — and 1 Corinthians 1:24 — *"Christ the power of God"* — moves in the orbit of this tradition. The line of reception runs from *zero'a Yahweh* in 51:9 to *zero'a Yahweh* in 53:1 (*to whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed?*) to the New Testament identification of Jesus as the *arm* by which God acts. John 12:38 cites Isaiah 53:1 in exactly this Christological register.

The **cup-of-staggering** trajectory is even more striking in its New Testament reception. The cup-of-wrath metaphor running through Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Isaiah 51, and Habakkuk forms the background of Jesus' Gethsemane prayer: *"Father, if you are willing, remove this cup from me; nevertheless, not my will, but yours, be done"* (Luke 22:42; cf. Mark 14:36 *"remove this cup from me"*; Matthew 26:39). The Gospel writers are not casually using *cup* as a metaphor for suffering; they are activating the Hebrew prophetic tradition in which *the cup* is specifically the cup of divine wrath. The Christian-typological reading is that Christ drinks the cup that Isaiah 51 said Israel had drained — and in drinking it, drains it for those whom he represents. Revelation 14:10 and 16:19 then redirect the cup back onto the unrighteous nations, completing the inversion Isaiah 51:22 began.

The **comfort** language of verse 12 (*anokhi anokhi hu menachemkhem*) reaches back to 40:1 (*"comfort, comfort my people"*) and forward to the entire Second Isaiah arc. Jewish commentary tradition treats *the Comforter* as one of Yahweh's signature titles. In Christian reading, the Greek *paraklētos* (comforter / advocate) of John 14-16 echoes this Isaianic vocabulary, though the New Testament does not directly cite Isaiah 51:12.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

The chapter's theological core is the question of whether **Yahweh's past acts can be trusted as warrant for his future acts**. The exiles have evidence: Jerusalem destroyed, the temple ash, the elite deported, the promises apparently dormant. The prophet's answer is not new evidence but a redirection of attention. *Look to the rock from which you were hewn* (v. 1) — look at how it has always been: one barren couple, then a multitude. *Was it not you who cut Rahab in pieces?* (v. 9) — look at what the arm has always done. The pastoral logic is that hope rests not on the visible present but on the rememberable past as it discloses the character of Yahweh.

The deepest tension is that the prophet *prays for Yahweh to wake* (v. 9) and *Yahweh responds that he is the comforter, and they should not have been afraid* (v. 12). Who has been asleep? The text is honest: from one angle, it is Yahweh's arm that needs awakening; from another, it is the people who have *forgotten the LORD* (v. 13). Both vectors are present. This is not contradiction; it is the genuine ambiguity of faith under pressure. Israel laments and prays as if Yahweh has slept (Psa 44:23-26 says it openly), and Yahweh answers as if Israel has slept. Both are true to the experience of the exile.

The **Combat-Myth backdrop** is theologically delicate. Mainstream-critical scholars (Cross, Smith, Levenson) have shown that Israel did not invent its monotheism in cultural isolation; it appropriated the chaos-combat grammar of its neighbors and bent it toward exclusive Yahwistic worship. *Rahab* and *tannin* are not, in Isaiah 51, foreign gods Yahweh defeated — they are demythologized chaos-figures, more like personified powers of disorder than rival deities. But the imagery still bears the marks of its origin. The honest reading is that Israelite poetry could and did speak in mythological cadences without thereby endorsing a pantheon. The prophet uses the Combat-Myth grammar because it is the most powerful available language for the cosmic stakes of what Yahweh is about to do — and because Babylon's *Enuma Elish*, which celebrates Marduk's defeat of Tiamat, is the very imperial mythology against which the exiles are being addressed. The prophet is saying: *your captors' creation-story is wrong. Yahweh, not Marduk, cut the chaos-monster in pieces. The exodus you remember is the same victory.* This is polemic on the Babylonians' own theological terrain.

The **arm of the LORD** theme will become Christological in Christian reading, and it is worth being honest about how that move works. In Isaiah 51:9 the arm is invoked as Yahweh's own agency. In Isaiah 53:1 the arm is *revealed* in the suffering Servant. The New Testament reads this trajectory as culminating in the incarnation: the arm of the LORD is finally revealed in Jesus, who is the *power of God* (1 Cor 1:24). Jewish readers will note, rightly, that this Christological move is an interpretive overlay rather than an explicit feature of Isaiah 51 itself. The chapter speaks of Yahweh's arm without specifying any incarnational referent. The Christian reading is canonically and theologically motivated, not arbitrary; the Jewish reading is textually grounded and refuses to read into the chapter a content it does not contain. Both are honest readings of a text whose imagery is rich enough to sustain multiple trajectories.

The steelman for the **strict-historical** reading: the chapter is exilic prophecy addressed to a specific moment, and its imagery should be heard primarily within that horizon. The arm that wakes is the arm that brings the exiles home from Babylon. To read the chapter primarily as Christology is to flatten its first meaning. The steelman for the **canonical-Christological** reading: the chapter's imagery — the arm, the cup, the comforter — is taken up by later prophetic and apostolic writers in patterns that suggest the text itself was generative of trajectories that exceed any single historical moment. Reading Isaiah 51 *only* as a 540 BC moment misses the way Scripture reads itself forward.

**Where this leaves us:** Isaiah 51 is a chapter about waking up — Yahweh's arm, the people's faith, Jerusalem itself. Its theological power is its insistence that the same God who defeated chaos at creation, who dried the sea at the exodus, will redeem the exiles, and that nothing in the present (no oppressor's wrath, no apparent silence) cancels that pattern. For the Christian reader, this same chapter feeds forward into the Gospel passion narrative through the cup-of-wrath imagery and into a Christological understanding of the *arm of the LORD*. For the Jewish reader, the chapter is a foundational text of the *consolation* tradition and an enduring resource for hope in the face of historical catastrophe.

**Closing question:** the prophet calls Yahweh's arm to wake and is answered with the reminder that the people have *forgotten* their Maker. Which is harder — to believe that God has not yet acted, or to remember what God has already done?

## The Thread

Chapter 51 is the threshold across which Second Isaiah moves from servant-suffering (chs. 49-50) to the climactic announcement of redemption (chs. 52-55). The chapter prepares the ground: it summons the exiles to look back to Abraham, to look up to Yahweh's arm, to look forward to return; it transfers the cup of staggering from Jerusalem's hand to her tormentors'; it stages the awakening that chapter 52 will complete. *Awake, awake* in 51:9 is addressed to Yahweh's arm; *Awake, awake* in 51:17 is addressed to Jerusalem; *Awake, awake* in 52:1 will be addressed to Zion to put on her splendid garments. The triple imperative is one continuous liturgy. And then in 52:13 the Servant will appear again, *high and lifted up* — and the arm of the LORD that the prophet called to wake here will be revealed there, in a form no one expected.
