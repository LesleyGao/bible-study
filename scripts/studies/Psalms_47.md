# Psalm 47 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 47 carries a double-tagged superscription typical of the Korahite collection in Book II of the Psalter: *lamnatzeach livnei-qorach mizmor* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ לִבְנֵי־קֹרַח מִזְמוֹר), "To the choirmaster. A Psalm of the Sons of Korah." The Korahites were a Levitical guild traceable in the Chronicler's narrative to the singers and gatekeepers of the Second Temple (1 Chr 6, 9, 26), whose ancestor — the Korah of Numbers 16 — had famously rebelled against Moses. The collection preserved under their name (Pss 42–49, 84–85, 87–88) presents a remarkable historical irony: a guild bearing the name of Israel's archetypal rebel sings the kingship of God most ardently. Whatever the eighth-to-sixth-century compositional history, the psalm in its received form belonged to a working temple choir that performed it as praise.

Hermann Gunkel placed Psalm 47 squarely within the **enthronement psalms** (*Thronbesteigungspsalmen*), and Sigmund Mowinckel, his student at Oslo, made this short text the linchpin of his celebrated reconstruction of an annual *autumnal enthronement festival* of Yahweh. Mowinckel grouped Psalms 47, 93, and 95–99 as the cultic core of a New Year ceremony in which Yahweh was symbolically re-enthroned each year, paralleling the Babylonian *Akitu* festival in which Marduk's ascendance was annually recapitulated. The decisive piece of evidence for Mowinckel was the verbal phrase *YHWH malak* (יהוה מָלָךְ) in 93:1, 97:1, and 99:1, which he translated dynamically — "Yahweh has become king" — rather than statically, "Yahweh reigns." On Mowinckel's reading, Psalm 47 records the climactic moment of the procession: *alah Elohim bi-truah, YHWH be-qol shofar* (עָלָה אֱלֹהִים בִּתְרוּעָה יהוה בְּקוֹל שׁוֹפָר), "God has gone up with a shout, the LORD with the sound of a trumpet" (47:5). The verb *alah* ("go up, ascend") is not the language of theophany descending but of a king ascending the throne.

The Mowinckel hypothesis remains debated. John Goldingay, in his three-volume *Psalms* commentary, treats the festival as a plausible *Sitz im Leben* but cautions that the textual evidence for the festival's actual existence is thin and largely circular: the psalms presuppose the festival; the festival is reconstructed from the psalms. Hans-Joachim Kraus, in *Theology of the Psalms*, prefers a "royal Zion festival" centered on Davidic kingship and divine residence rather than annual re-enthronement, and questions whether *malak* should be read inceptively at all. James Mays, in *The Lord Reigns*, defends a modified Mowinckelian reading: the psalms do envision a cultic enactment, but what is celebrated is recognition of an eternal kingship, not its annual re-creation. Most contemporary scholarship lands somewhere between Goldingay's caution and Mays's defense — there was likely some festival celebrating divine kingship, but the *Akitu* parallel was probably overpressed.

The shouting and trumpet-blast in 47:1, 5 fit either reconstruction. *Truah* (תְּרוּעָה) is the same noun used for the New Year shofar-blast (Lev 23:24, Num 29:1) and for the war-cry that brought down Jericho (Josh 6:5). The peoples are summoned to *tiqu kaph* (תִּקְעוּ־כָף, "clap hands") and to *hari'u* (הָרִיעוּ, "shout") — both verbs at home in coronation and in liturgy. When 1 Kings 1:39–40 narrates Solomon's anointing, the people *tiqu ba-shofar* and *vayyariu kol-ha'am simchah gedolah* — they blew the trumpet and shouted with great joy. Psalm 47 transposes that same coronation grammar onto Yahweh.

A note on the **Book II Elohistic Psalter**. Psalms 42–83 use the generic *Elohim* in preference to the divine name *YHWH* — an editorial peculiarity that has shaped Psalm 47 directly. *Elohim alah bi-truah* (47:5) almost certainly originated as *YHWH alah bi-truah*, with the divine name retained in 47:5b ("the LORD with the sound of a trumpet") because the parallel demanded it. Comparing 47 with 96, 97, 98, and 99 — all using *YHWH* freely — gives a sense of what the Elohistic editor was doing. The Greek translators of the Septuagint render both *YHWH* and *Elohim* with *Kyrios* and *Theos* respectively, blunting the distinction. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve Psalm 47 only fragmentarily (4QPsa contains portions of Pss 5–69 with Ps 47 attested), but where preserved the Qumran text supports the Masoretic shape.

The ancient Near Eastern context for Yahweh's enthronement is dense and polemical. In the Babylonian *Enuma Elish*, Marduk is enthroned after defeating Tiamat. In the Ugaritic Baal Cycle, Baal is enthroned on Mount Zaphon after defeating Yamm. Psalm 47 borrows the iconography — divine kingship after victory, peoples gathered, throne, shout — and removes the mythological combat. Yahweh's victory in 47:3 is not over chaos-monsters but historical: he "subdued peoples under us." The psalm's polemic is quiet but real: the king of the gods is the God of Abraham, not of Babylon's pantheon, and his enthronement does not need annual re-creation through cultic combat-mythology.

## Lens 2: The Literature in Front of Us

Psalm 47 is short and tightly built — nine verses in two strophes, with *selah* dividing them after verse 4. The first strophe (vv. 1–4) summons the peoples and grounds the praise in Yahweh's past acts; the second strophe (vv. 5–9) celebrates the ascension and present reign. Robert Lowth's tripartite parallelism is everywhere visible:

- **Synonymous**: "Clap your hands, all peoples! / Shout to God with loud songs of joy!" (v. 1)
- **Synthetic**: "God has gone up with a shout, / the LORD with the sound of a trumpet" (v. 5) — the second line completing what the first introduces
- **Synonymous (intensifying)**: "Sing praises to God, sing praises! / Sing praises to our King, sing praises!" (v. 6) — a four-fold imperative *zammeru* (זַמְּרוּ) echoing through the verse like a hammered drum

The poem's most striking literary device is the **inclusio of universal address**. The psalm opens with *kol ha'amim* (כָּל־הָעַמִּים, "all peoples") and closes with *megine eretz l'Elohim* (מָגִנֵּי־אֶרֶץ לֵאלֹהִים, "the shields of the earth belong to God") — the rulers/protectors of all nations subordinated to Israel's God. Within that frame the audience expands and contracts: peoples (v. 1) → us, Jacob (vv. 3–4) → God's ascent (v. 5) → King of all the earth (v. 7) → nations and princes gathered (vv. 8–9). The geographical scope of praise is total.

The chiastic center sits at verses 5–7, with the trumpet-blast of v. 5 and the *zammeru-zammeru* of vv. 6–7 framing the declaration "God is the King of all the earth." Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger, in their three-volume Hermeneia commentary, propose a ABA' structure: praise summons (1–4) / ascension hymn (5–7) / kingship affirmation (8–9). The *selah* after v. 4 marks an audible musical pause — origin disputed (Mowinckel: a cue for cymbals; others: a textual marker meaning "lift up [the voices]"; Septuagint *diapsalma* = interlude).

Wordplay deepens the poetics. The verb *alah* in v. 5 ("God has gone up") rhymes textually with *Elyon* (עֶלְיוֹן, "Most High") in v. 2, and with *meod na'alah* (מְאֹד נַעֲלָה, "highly exalted") in v. 9 — an *ascent* root threaded through the psalm. The verb *malak* (rule) in v. 8 sits next to *melekh* (king) in vv. 2, 6, 7 — kingship lexicon saturating every strophe. The Hebrew is not subtle. It is liturgical — designed to be heard, repeated, joined in.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

In the canonical shape of the Psalter, Psalm 47 sits near the close of the Korahite cluster of Book II. The collection runs Pss 42–49, with 50 (Asaphite) marking a transition before the great Davidic block beginning at Psalm 51. Within the Korahite collection, 46 ("God is our refuge"), 47 ("God is the king"), and 48 ("Mount Zion ... the city of the great king") form a tight Zion-and-kingship trilogy. Read together, they move from Yahweh's protection of Zion against the nations (46) to his enthronement over the nations (47) to the celebration of Zion as cosmic center (48). The Korahite editors composed a small theological argument in three movements.

**Jewish reading trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 47 emphasizes the sevenfold "sing praises" (*zammeru*) of vv. 6–7, linking it to seven shofar blasts on Rosh Hashanah and to seven heavens. The psalm became one of the central Rosh Hashanah texts in Jewish liturgy: in the Ashkenazic and Sephardic rites, Psalm 47 is recited seven times immediately before the shofar blowing (*tekiat shofar*) at the morning service. The *truah* of v. 5 is the *truah* of the New Year. Rashi (eleventh-century, Troyes) reads "He chose our heritage for us" (v. 4) as the land of Israel and "the pride of Jacob whom he loves" as the Temple — Zion remains the locus of divine election. Ibn Ezra (twelfth-century, Spain) takes the psalm prophetically: the future ingathering of nations to acknowledge Israel's God. Radak (David Kimchi, thirteenth-century, Provence) reads it both historically — the era of Jehoshaphat or Hezekiah — and eschatologically. Nachmanides extends the eschatological reading: the universal kingship envisioned here will be realized in the messianic age. Modern Jewish scholarship — Robert Alter's translation in *The Book of Psalms*, Jon Levenson on divine kingship in *Sinai and Zion*, Nahum Sarna's *On the Book of Psalms* — emphasizes the Israelite polemic against ancient Near Eastern myth and the political theology of a single deity over all nations. Avivah Zornberg, in her literary readings, lingers on the strangeness of the *truah*-shout — the human voice as participation in divine ascent.

**Christian reading trajectory.** Augustine, in his *Enarrationes in Psalmos*, reads Psalm 47 as the fundamental Christological psalm of the ascension. *Ascendit Deus in iubilo, Dominus in voce tubae* — the Latin Vulgate of v. 5 — names what happens on the fortieth day after Easter. For Augustine, the universal kingship of v. 8 is the kingship of the risen Christ, and the gathering of the princes of the peoples in v. 9 is the gathering of the Gentile church around the Jewish patriarchs. Athanasius, in his *Letter to Marcellinus*, prescribes Psalm 47 for the believer celebrating the resurrection. Cassiodorus elaborates the Trinitarian ascension. Aquinas's commentary on the Psalms (incomplete; he died working on Psalm 54) preserves the ascension reading but folds in the *Regnum Christi*: the eternal kingship inaugurated at the resurrection. Luther, in his *Operationes in Psalmos*, hears in v. 5 the *Erhebung Christi* — Christ's exaltation in glory, but reads also the *truah* as the gospel preached to the nations. Calvin, in his 1557 Psalms commentary, characteristically refuses pure allegory: the psalm celebrates Yahweh's actual rule over Israel and the nations; it does not "directly" prophesy Christ's ascension but typifies it. The line from Augustine to Calvin is the line from full Christological allegory to typological caution.

The liturgical settlement is broad. **Ascension Day** in the Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran, and Eastern Orthodox traditions includes Psalm 47 as a central reading or antiphon. The Catholic Liturgy of the Hours assigns Psalm 47 to Wednesday Lauds in Week I — every other week, the church prays this enthronement song at sunrise. Modern Christian commentary continues both lines. Hossfeld and Zenger's Hermeneia treats the psalm primarily on its own terms as a Zion-and-kingship hymn but acknowledges Christian reception. James Mays's *The Lord Reigns* (Westminster, 1994) makes the case that the *YHWH-malak* group is the theological heart of the Psalter — the reign of God, not the human king, is the central confession. Walter Brueggemann, in *The Message of the Psalms*, places Psalm 47 in the "psalms of new orientation" — psalms that move past lament into renewed praise. Marvin Tate (Word Biblical Commentary) sees the psalm as celebrating universal sovereignty under conditions of historical trial.

The New Testament's most direct echo of Psalm 47:5 is Ephesians 4:8, where Paul, drawing on Psalm 68:18, writes "When he ascended on high, he led a host of captives." The verb *anabainō* in Ephesians (and in Acts 1:9 of the Lord's actual ascension) becomes the New Testament's standard counterpart to the *alah* of 47:5. Hebrews 1:3, 8:1, 12:2, and the entire Christological superstructure of Hebrews assume an enthronement of Christ "at the right hand of the Majesty" — a New Testament rereading of the divine enthronement Psalm 47 first proclaimed.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

Two tensions are worth naming honestly.

**First: the Mowinckel question — annual re-enthronement, or eternal kingship recognized?** Mowinckel's reading of *malak* as inceptive ("has become king") and his reconstruction of an autumnal enthronement festival have shaped a century of Psalms scholarship. The implications are not trivial. If Yahweh was annually re-enthroned, the cult played a constitutive role in maintaining divine kingship — what Mowinckel called *Kultmythos* effective in the world. If Yahweh's kingship is eternal and the cult merely *acknowledges* it, the relationship between liturgy and reality is acknowledgment, not constitution. Three positions:

- *Mowinckel and successors* (Aubrey Johnson, partially James Mays): There was an autumnal festival; the cult enacted divine enthronement. The strongest steelman is the *Akitu* parallel, the verbal grammar of *malak*, and the overwhelming presence of enthronement language across Pss 47, 93, 95–99. If you saw seven psalms about divine kingship in a culture surrounded by enthronement festivals, the *prima facie* case for cultic re-enthronement is strong.
- *Mainstream caution* (Goldingay, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger): Some festival celebrating divine kingship is plausible; *Akitu* parallels are overpressed; *malak* is better translated "is king" or "reigns"; the psalms acknowledge eternal kingship rather than constitute it. The strongest steelman is the canonical theology of an eternally sovereign deity who does not require annual re-creation, and the slim direct evidence for an Israelite *Akitu*-style festival.
- *Modified defense* (Mays): The festival existed, but what it celebrated was recognition, not re-enactment. *Malak* is performative — like saying "I now pronounce you king" at a coronation that ratifies what was already true.

**Second: ascension-Christological reading vs. Israelite covenantal reading.** Augustine's reading of v. 5 as Christ's ascension has shaped Christian liturgy for sixteen centuries. The Jewish reading hears v. 5 as Yahweh's enthronement over Israel and the nations, climactically realized in the messianic age. Three positions:

- *Strong Christological*: Psalm 47 is *about* the ascension of Christ; the Old Testament saint sang what was fulfilled on the fortieth day. Steelman: New Testament authors read the Psalter Christologically (Heb 1, Acts 2); Christian liturgy across traditions has unanimously assigned this psalm to Ascension; Augustine's reading is theologically rich and ecclesially load-bearing.
- *Strong covenantal*: Psalm 47 celebrates Yahweh's universal kingship over Israel and the nations, period. Christian appropriation is supersessionist if it forecloses the psalm's ongoing meaning for the synagogue. Steelman: the Korahite singers were not Christians; the psalm has its own historical horizon; Jews have prayed it on Rosh Hashanah for two thousand years; the psalm's "God of Abraham" is not "God of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and *the church*."
- *Typological / both-and*: The psalm celebrates Yahweh's universal kingship; that kingship is climactically expressed in the resurrection-ascension of Christ; Christians and Jews read the same text at different liturgical moments without canceling each other. Steelman: this is the position of Calvin, of much modern Catholic biblical theology (post-*Nostra Aetate*), and of Hossfeld-Zenger.

**Where this leaves us.** The psalm itself does not adjudicate. What it does is summon. The imperative *tiqu kaph* — "clap your hands" — is given to *kol ha'amim*, all peoples, regardless of which liturgical calendar they keep. The trumpet-blast of v. 5 is heard differently from the synagogue on Rosh Hashanah and from the Lateran on Ascension Day, but in both places it summons recognition of a kingship that does not require us to constitute it. Whether we read the *alah* as Yahweh ascending the cosmic throne, as the messianic king ascending Mount Zion, or as Christ ascending to the right hand of the Father, the psalm asks the same thing: that the peoples take notice, and shout.

The closing question. If the kingship celebrated in Psalm 47 is real — if the shields of the earth genuinely belong to God — what would change in our political imagination, our reading of headlines, our citizenship of any earthly polity, if we actually believed it on a Tuesday afternoon in April, not just on Ascension Day or Rosh Hashanah?

## The Thread

- **Psalm 47:5** — "God has gone up with a shout" — *alah Elohim bi-truah*. The textual hinge of every reading: cultic, Christological, eschatological.
- **1 Kings 1:39–40** — Solomon's coronation: *tiqu ba-shofar*, the people shouted with great joy. The political grammar Psalm 47 transposes onto God.
- **Acts 1:9; Ephesians 4:8** — "He ascended" — the New Testament reread of Yahweh's ascent in Christ.
- **Mishnah Rosh Hashanah and Tractate Tamid** — Psalm 47 as the seven-times-recited shofar prelude; the Jewish liturgical home of this text.
