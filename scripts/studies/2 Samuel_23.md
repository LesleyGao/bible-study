# 2 Samuel 23: Last Words and the Name at the End of the List

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter is structured in two markedly different units: David's "last words" oracle (vv. 1-7) and the catalogue of mighty men (vv. 8-39). Both belong to the Samuel appendix's chiastic arrangement (chs 21-24) and together with chapter 22 form the appendix's poetic and military core.

The **superscription at vv. 1-2** is one of the most theologically loaded headers in the Hebrew Bible. The Hebrew is *neum david ben yishai u-neum ha-gever huqam al meshiach Elohey Yaakov u-ne'im zemirot Yisrael* — "the oracle of David son of Jesse, the oracle of the man who was raised on high, the anointed of the God of Jacob, the sweet psalmist of Israel." The word *neum* (נְאֻם) is the technical Hebrew for **prophetic oracle** — "thus says YHWH" formula vocabulary, used hundreds of times in the prophetic books to introduce divine speech. Its application to a Davidic composition is striking: the composition is not just royal poetry but prophetic oracle. The Hebrew Bible's other major use of *neum* with a personal subject is the Balaam oracles (Numbers 24:3, 24:15), where Balaam introduces his oracles with "the *neum* of Balaam son of Beor." David is being placed in the prophetic line. The framing supports the rabbinic and Christian traditions of attributing prophetic status to David.

The four-line title — son of Jesse, raised on high, anointed of the God of Jacob, sweet psalmist of Israel — covers David's full identity: his ancestry (son of Jesse), his political elevation (raised on high — *huqam al*, possibly meaning "raised above" or "established on high"), his covenantal status (anointed of the God of Jacob), his musical-poetic vocation (sweet psalmist — *ne'im zemirot Yisrael*, possibly "favorite of the songs of Israel" or "sweet of the strains of Israel"). The four-fold designation is one of the most concentrated character-identifications in the Hebrew Bible. **P. Kyle McCarter** (Anchor Bible) and **A. A. Anderson** (WBC) both read the superscription as part of an originally independent oracle composition that the appendix editor preserved.

The **oracle proper** at vv. 3-7 contains some of the densest theological-political content in Samuel. The opening *ruach YHWH dibber bi u-millato al-leshoni* (רוּחַ יְהוָה דִּבֶּר בִּי וּמִלָּתוֹ עַל-לְשׁוֹנִי) — "the Spirit of YHWH speaks through me; his word is on my tongue" — is the standard prophetic-inspiration formula. The word *millato* (his word) is the rare Aramaic-influenced form (cf. Psalm 19:5, Psalm 139:4 — Late Biblical Hebrew). The word *aelohim Yisrael* (the God of Israel), the second divine name in v. 3, prepares the divine pronouncement.

The pronouncement itself (vv. 3b-4) is a vision of just rule: "When one rules justly over men, ruling in the fear of God, he dawns on them like the morning light, like the sun shining forth on a cloudless morning, like rain that makes grass to sprout from the earth." The image of the just ruler as morning light is one of the Hebrew Bible's most evocative pieces of royal theology. Compare Malachi 4:2 (the sun of righteousness rising with healing in its wings), Isaiah 60:1-3 (the dawning of light on Jerusalem), Hosea 6:3 (YHWH coming like the morning rain). The vocabulary of dawn, sunlight, and rain on grass is agricultural-cosmological — the just ruler's effect on his realm is like the natural conditions that bring forth life.

The Davidic-covenantal claim at vv. 5-7 is the chapter's most theologically loaded section. "Does not my house stand so with God? For he has made with me an everlasting covenant (*berit olam*, בְּרִית עוֹלָם), ordered in all things and secure (*aruchah ve-shemurah*, עֲרוּכָה וּשְׁמוּרָה)." The vocabulary of "everlasting covenant" links directly to 2 Samuel 7 (the Nathan oracle); it is the same theology in poetic form. The promise is structurally and politically loaded: ordered (the legal-covenantal vocabulary of arrangement), secure (the military-defensive vocabulary of preservation). The contrast in vv. 6-7 — "but worthless men (*beney beli'aal*, בְּנֵי בְלִיָּעַל) are like thorns thrown away" — gives the negative pole. The thorn imagery comes from Iron Age agricultural reality: thorny brush is too dangerous to gather by hand and must be burned in place; the violent must be eliminated by means appropriate to their nature.

The **catalogue of mighty men** at vv. 8-39 is the chapter's longer second movement. The catalog has clear literary-political function: it is the official Davidic court roll of the elite warriors who built the kingdom. Such catalogs are well-attested in ANE royal contexts — the Egyptian royal correspondence preserves lists of standing officers, the Assyrian royal annals frequently list senior military officers with deeds of valor, and Hittite royal documents preserve similar elite-warrior rolls. The Davidic catalog has direct parallels at 1 Chronicles 11:10-47 (with substantial expansions) and 1 Chronicles 27:1-15 (David's monthly division commanders). The 1 Chronicles parallel is generally treated as a later expansion of the same catalog tradition; the 2 Samuel version is closer to what most critical scholars take as the older form.

The catalogue is structured in three tiers. **The Three** (vv. 8-12 + the implied superlative) — Josheb-basshebeth (the Hebrew is corrupt; possibly Ishbaal in original; LXX has different name; likely textual error from MT), Eleazar son of Dodo, Shammah son of Agee. Each is associated with a single legendary deed (Josheb-basshebeth killing 800 with a spear, Eleazar's sword sticking to his hand from extended battle until his hand "clung to the sword," Shammah defending a lentil field against the Philistines). **The Three's Water Episode** at vv. 13-17: three of "the thirty" (a different group than the Three? or the Three again? — the Hebrew is ambiguous) break through the Philistine garrison at Bethlehem to bring David water from the well by the gate; David refuses to drink it and pours it out as a drink-offering to YHWH. The episode is a striking ethical-cultic moment that **Robert Alter** (*The David Story*) and **Brueggemann** treat as one of David's most morally clear gestures. **The Two Higher** (vv. 18-23) — Abishai (Joab's brother) and Benaiah son of Jehoiada — distinguished from the Thirty but not quite reaching the Three. **The Thirty** (vv. 24-39) — the catalog of senior officers, ending with the count "thirty-seven in all."

The catalog's geography preserves valuable historical evidence about the demographic composition of David's military elite. The names range across Israelite tribes (Judahites, Benjaminites, Ephraimites, Gadites), include foreign mercenaries (Uriah the **Hittite**, Zelek the **Ammonite**, Naharai the **Beerothite** — the same Beerothite community whose other members assassinated Ish-bosheth in chapter 4), and include several whose tribal affiliation is unclear. The mixture is consistent with what we know of Iron Age military formations — citizen levy combined with foreign mercenary elite. **Baruch Halpern** in *David's Secret Demons* and **Steven McKenzie** in *King David: A Biography* both develop this analysis.

The catalog's closing position of **Uriah the Hittite** at v. 39 is the chapter's most discussed literary-theological feature. After the count "thirty-seven in all," the final name is the Hittite husband of Bathsheba whom David murdered in chapter 11. The placement of his name at the end of the list — preserved in the official roll of the Davidic elite — is the narrator's quiet but devastating indictment. The catalog of those who built the kingdom ends with the man the king killed. **McCarter**, **Halpern**, **Brueggemann**, and **Alter** all note the placement; the convergence is striking.

A few Hebrew terms transform the chapter. ***Neum*** (נְאֻם), "oracle," at vv. 1-2 — the technical prophetic-inspiration vocabulary applied to a Davidic composition. ***Berit olam*** (בְּרִית עוֹלָם), "everlasting covenant," at v. 5 — the Davidic covenant in covenantal vocabulary. ***Aruchah ve-shemurah*** (עֲרוּכָה וּשְׁמוּרָה), "ordered and secure," at v. 5 — the legal-administrative vocabulary of the covenant's permanence. ***Beney beli'aal*** (בְּנֵי בְלִיָּעַל), "worthless men," at v. 6 — the standard biblical Hebrew for the morally and politically dangerous. ***Gibborim*** (גִּבּוֹרִים), "mighty men," is the chapter's designation for the Davidic elite — the technical military vocabulary for the senior warrior class. ***Ariel*** (אֲרִיאֵל) at v. 20 (Benaiah killing "two *ariels* of Moab") is etymologically obscure — possibly "lion of God" (so traditional reading), possibly an architectural term (the *ariel* of Isaiah 29:1-2 may refer to Jerusalem as "altar-hearth"); the precise reference is debated.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter has two structurally distinct units: the oracle (vv. 1-7) and the catalog (vv. 8-39). They function differently in literary terms: the oracle is poetic and theological; the catalog is prose and historical-archival. Their juxtaposition is part of the appendix's chiastic arrangement.

The **oracle (vv. 1-7)** is one of the appendix's two poetic centers (with chapter 22). Its placement at the structural pivot of the chiasm (after the heroic catalogue of 21:15-22 and before the longer catalogue of 23:8-39) gives it theological weight. The oracle's content — vision of just rule, claim of everlasting covenant, indictment of the worthless — is a kind of theological summary of the Davidic theology that the rest of the appendix presupposes. **Brevard Childs** in his canonical-criticism work treats the appendix's poetic core (chapters 22 and 23:1-7) as the appendix's hermeneutical key. **Walter Brueggemann's *First and Second Samuel*** reads the two poems together as the theological frame for reading both the appendix and the entire book of Samuel.

The oracle's archaic features (the rare Aramaic-influenced *millato*, the rare verb *huqam al*, the dense theological vocabulary) have led some scholars to date it early — possibly to David's own period or shortly after. **Cross** and **Freedman** and others have placed it within the archaic Hebrew poetry corpus. The mainstream position holds that the oracle preserves authentically early material even if the final form has been updated.

The **catalogue (vv. 8-39)** is structurally a prose archival list with embedded narrative episodes. The structure is hierarchical: the Three (each with a deed), the Three's water episode (separate honor), the Two Higher (Abishai and Benaiah), the Thirty proper. The hierarchical structure mirrors ANE royal court protocol — closest associates above, distinguished officers next, the larger elite officer corps below. The deed-citations within the catalog (Josheb-basshebeth's 800, Eleazar's sword sticking to his hand, Shammah's lentil field, the water from the well, Abishai's 300, Benaiah's lion-in-a-pit and Egyptian-with-a-spear) function as etiologies for the warriors' rank — they are the deeds that earned them their place in the official memory.

The **water-from-Bethlehem episode** at vv. 13-17 is the catalog's most literarily developed moment. The setting — David at the cave of Adullam, Philistine garrison at Bethlehem, three of the thirty break through to bring water from the well by the gate — places the episode chronologically in the early outlaw period (1 Samuel 22). David's longing speech ("oh, that someone would give me water to drink from the well of Bethlehem that is by the gate") is rhetorical, and the three take it literally; the gap between figurative wish and literal compliance is the engine of the episode. David's response — refusing to drink the water and pouring it out as drink-offering — recasts the gesture: the water that men risked their lives to obtain cannot be used as ordinary refreshment but must be returned to YHWH. The phrase **"shall I drink the blood of the men who went at the risk of their lives?"** identifies the water with the blood, and pouring it out as drink-offering is the appropriate cultic disposal of what is now blood-equivalent. The episode is morally and cultically dense — David honors the sacrifice by refusing to consume it. **Brueggemann**, **Alter**, and **Eugene Peterson** (*Leap over a Wall*) all develop the episode as one of David's most theologically clear gestures.

The catalogue's **closing position of Uriah the Hittite** at v. 39 is the chapter's most discussed literary-theological feature. The count formula "thirty-seven in all" comes after Uriah's name. The placement is striking precisely because Uriah's name does not need to be there for the count to work — the catalog could have ended with another Hittite or omitted Uriah entirely. The narrator preserves Uriah's name in the official Davidic warrior roll knowing that the canonical reader has read chapter 11. The juxtaposition is the narrator's silent indictment: the official roll of the Davidic elite includes the man David murdered to take the man's wife. **Brueggemann** treats this as one of the canonical Samuel's most quietly devastating placements. **Halpern** and **McKenzie** read it similarly: the catalog preserves Uriah's name precisely to make the canonical point.

The chapter sits at a precise structural position in the Samuel appendix. The chiastic arrangement (per Childs, Brueggemann, Exum, Koch): A-Saulide bloodguilt (21:1-14), B-giant-killings list (21:15-22), C-David's Song (22), C'-David's last words (23:1-7), B'-mighty men list (23:8-39), A'-census plague (24). The chapter's two units occupy the C' and B' positions. The chapter is structurally the mirror-image complement of chapter 22 + 21:15-22, deepening and developing the same themes.

Textual evidence: **4QSam-a** preserves fragments of the chapter, with notable variants in the catalogue. The corrupt MT name "Josheb-basshebeth" at v. 8 is widely emended to "Ishbaal" (which appears in 1 Chron 11:11 as "Jashobeam") on the basis of LXX evidence; the corruption likely arose from scribal protection of the *baal* element in the personal name (compare the Eshbaal/Ish-bosheth pattern in chapter 2). The **1 Chronicles 11:10-47 parallel** has substantial expansions and some variant names; **Sara Japhet** on the Chronicler's editorial method treats the Chronicler's expansions as the standard form of the warrior-list tradition by post-exilic times.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, 2 Samuel 23 functions as the official Davidic catalog of the warriors who built the kingdom and as the theological summary of Davidic kingship through the oracle of last words. The catalog's preservation of the thirty-seven names — including Uriah's — has shaped how Jewish and Christian traditions have remembered the Davidic court.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Josephus in *Antiquities* 7.305-318 retells the chapter in summary, treating the oracle and the catalog separately. The Talmud (Sanhedrin 19b, 20a) discusses the Three and the Thirty as the paradigm of righteous warriors; the rabbinic tradition treats the catalog's preservation of Uriah's name as the narrator's moral judgment on David. **Rashi** (11th c.) glosses the difficult Hebrew of the oracle and develops the rabbinic readings of the catalog. **Radak** (David Kimhi) provides extensive commentary on the textual cruxes (the Josheb-basshebeth/Ishbaal name, the Three vs. Thirty distinction) and on the catalog's geography. **Ralbag** (Gersonides) extracts moral *to'aliyot* about leadership, loyalty, and the placement of Uriah's name. **Abarbanel** (15th c.) treats the chapter as a study in royal-elite relationships and the proper memory of those who serve. **Yair Zakovitch** in modern scholarship attends to the Uriah-placement intertext. **Avivah Zornberg** has not written extensively on this chapter; **Tikva Frymer-Kensky** notes the catalog's masculinist frame and the absence of women's names entirely.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic Christianity read the oracle (vv. 1-7) extensively and the catalog less. **Origen's** fragments treat the just-ruler vision as figure of Christ's just kingship. **Augustine** in *City of God* 17 develops the just-ruler oracle at length — the morning-light image becomes a christological figure. **Ambrose** in *De Officiis* uses the oracle as model of just episcopal leadership. **Chrysostom**'s homilies attend to David's refusal to drink the water as model of self-restraint. **Gregory the Great** in the *Moralia* uses the same episode as figure of how the spiritual leader receives and disposes of the sacrifice of others. The medieval *Glossa Ordinaria* compiles patristic readings. **Bernard of Clairvaux** develops the just-ruler oracle for ecclesial application. **Aquinas** treats the oracle in the *Summa* discussion of just kingship.

The Reformation shifted register. **Calvin's** sermons on 2 Samuel give substantial attention to both units. The oracle's vision of just rule becomes a model for Christian magistracy. The water-episode is treated as a model of how leaders should receive sacrificial service. The Uriah placement is acknowledged as the narrator's indictment. **Luther's** lectures attend more to the typological reading. The post-Reformation Pietist and Puritan tradition (Matthew Henry) develops both units at devotional length.

The critical period (Wellhausen, Noth, Cross) treated the chapter as preserving authentically early material. **Cross's** archaic Hebrew poetry corpus places the oracle within the early monarchic poetic tradition. **McCarter's** Anchor Bible commentary develops both the textual issues of the catalog and the theological structure of the oracle. **A. A. Anderson's** WBC commentary is detailed on both units. **Baruch Halpern's *David's Secret Demons*** reads the catalog as historical evidence about Davidic court composition (the foreign mercenary presence, the geographic distribution of the warriors); the Uriah placement is the narrator's silent moral commentary. **Steven McKenzie** offers similar analysis. **Walter Brueggemann's *First and Second Samuel*** reads the chapter's two units as the appendix's theological core. **Robert Polzin** reads it literarily within the dtr's argument. **Robert Alter's *The David Story*** treats the catalogue's structure and the water-episode at length. **Jan Fokkelman**'s four-volume literary reading provides exhaustive close analysis. The contemporary turn includes feminist readings (the absence of women, the unnamed women of the warriors' households), military-historical readings (the catalog's evidence for Iron Age IIA military organization), and reception-historical studies of how the catalog has shaped later memory.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Both traditions read the just-ruler oracle as theologically important and develop it for political-theological application. They diverge on the messianic interpretation of the everlasting covenant — Jewish reading awaits messianic fulfillment in a future Davidic descendant, Christian reading sees fulfillment in Jesus. They converge on the moral significance of the Uriah placement — both traditions treat it as the narrator's quiet indictment. They diverge on the interpretation of the water-episode — Jewish tradition often treats it as halakhic case study (proper disposal of consecrated material), Christian tradition often treats it as moral exemplum (the leader's restraint).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author/editor believe God was doing in this chapter? The theology operates on multiple registers. The oracle (vv. 1-7) advances the high theological claims: David is prophet, his words are YHWH's words, his house has an everlasting covenant, the worthless are doomed. The catalog (vv. 8-39) is mostly bare archive but contains one explicitly theological note ("YHWH brought about a great victory that day" at vv. 10 and 12) and one implicit theological judgment (the placement of Uriah at the end of the list). The chapter's theology has to be reconstructed from both registers.

**Tension One: David's claim to prophetic inspiration (vv. 1-3) — authentic prophetic vocation or royal ideology dressed in prophetic vocabulary?** The oracle's framing assigns *neum* (prophetic oracle) status to David's last words and claims that "the Spirit of YHWH speaks by me; his word is on my tongue." The framing places David alongside Moses, Balaam, and the canonical prophets as a divinely-inspired speaker.

The traditional reading (rabbinic, patristic, Reformation) takes the framing at face value. David is genuinely inspired; his words are genuinely YHWH's. The rabbinic tradition includes David among the prophets (Sotah 48b lists prophets through whom the holy spirit spoke); the Christian tradition treats David as type of Christ and his oracles as proto-prophetic. The Davidic authorship of the Psalms (which the canonical traditions affirm at varying degrees) draws on the same framework.

The contemporary critical reading is more divided. **Halpern** and **McKenzie** read the prophetic framing as part of the broader royal-ideology tradition: ANE kings regularly claimed divine inspiration for their pronouncements (compare the Sumerian king-list, the Egyptian royal oracles, the Mesopotamian *ṣalmu* royal-image vocabulary). The framing is real religious vocabulary, but it is also legitimating ideology. **Brueggemann** holds the two readings together: the framing is theologically substantive *and* politically functional. **Polzin** reads the dtr placement as deliberate canonical irony — David's claim to prophetic inspiration sits in tension with the moral failures the canonical reader has just read about.

The position to steelman is the contemporary critique. The traditional reading sometimes sidesteps the structural similarity between Davidic royal-prophetic claims and the broader ANE royal-divine-inspiration tradition. The critique asks the right question: when a king's pronouncements are framed as divine speech, what work is the framing doing politically? The answer is not necessarily that the framing is false — religious traditions can be both authentic and politically functional — but the framing's political function should not be elided.

**Where this leaves us:** The oracle's claim to prophetic inspiration is part of David's broader scriptural status as a divinely-inspired speaker. The claim is theologically substantive in the traditions that receive it; it is also part of an ANE pattern of royal-divine framing that has political consequences. Both are real.

**Tension Two: The placement of Uriah's name at v. 39 — narrator's indictment, archival completeness, or canonical reconciliation?** The chapter's most discussed literary-theological feature is the closing position of Uriah the Hittite in the warrior-roll.

The traditional reading (rabbinic, patristic, Reformation) generally treats the placement as the narrator's quiet indictment. The catalog of those who built the kingdom ends with the man David killed; the canonical reader who has read chapter 11 cannot miss the juxtaposition. The placement is moral commentary by silence.

The contemporary critical reading (Halpern, McKenzie, Brueggemann) develops this further: the placement is structurally deliberate. The catalog could have omitted Uriah, or buried him in the middle of the list, or marked his name with a parenthetical note. None of these moves was made. Uriah stands at the end of the list, and the count formula ("thirty-seven in all") falls after his name. The narrator/editor knew what they were doing.

A canonical-reconciliation reading (some patristic and rabbinic readings, some Christian devotional traditions) holds that the placement honors Uriah by preserving his name in the official roll despite the king's failure. The catalog refuses to erase the man David killed; the official memory of the Davidic court includes the wronged. The placement, on this reading, is not just indictment but also vindication — the wronged is named alongside the elite who built the kingdom.

The position to steelman is the canonical-reconciliation reading. It is sometimes treated as too generous to the canonical text, but it takes seriously what the placement actually does: it preserves Uriah's name in the official memory. The text could have erased him; it did not. The preservation is a form of justice — incomplete, but real. The wronged are not allowed to be forgotten.

**Where this leaves us:** The Uriah placement is doing both indictment and preservation. The narrator's silent commentary on David is real; the canonical text's preservation of Uriah's name in the official Davidic roll is also real. The two readings are not exclusive.

The unresolved question this chapter leaves us with: When the official memory of a regime preserves the name of someone the regime killed, what does it mean for the canonical text to honor the wronged through the very list that the wronger compiled — and what would it mean to receive that honor without using it to absolve the wronger?

## The Thread

**2 Samuel 7.** The Davidic covenant of "everlasting covenant" oracle in 23:5 is the same theology Nathan announced in 2 Samuel 7. The two texts together form the theological architecture of Davidic dynasty in the Hebrew Bible. When you reread 2 Samuel 7, the "everlasting covenant... ordered in all things and secure" of 23:5 is its poetic restatement.

**1 Samuel 22 and 2 Samuel 5:17-25.** The water-from-Bethlehem episode (23:13-17) places David at the cave of Adullam (1 Samuel 22 setting) with Philistines garrisoning Bethlehem (the political situation of the early outlaw period). The episode is dated to before David's accession; its placement in the appendix collects an early heroic memory that the canonical narrative did not include in chronological order. When you reread 1 Samuel 22, hold the water-episode as the kind of incident that the cave-period generated.

**2 Samuel 11.** Uriah the Hittite, the husband of Bathsheba, the man David murdered. The placement of his name at the end of the warrior-roll in 23:39 is the quiet canonical indictment that the Court History's narrative built. When you reread 2 Samuel 11, remember that Uriah's name remains in the official Davidic memory at 23:39 — the canonical text does not let David off, even at the appendix's conclusion.

**1 Chronicles 11:10-47.** The Chronicler's parallel of the warrior-list expands the catalog with additional names and deeds, omits some of the Samuel material (notably the more morally complicated stories), and presents the official Davidic roll in a more fully developed form. When you read the Chronicler's version, notice what the Chronicler chooses to preserve and what to leave out — including whether Uriah's placement is preserved with the same canonical force.
