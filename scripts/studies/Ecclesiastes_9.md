# Ecclesiastes 9: The Dead Know Nothing

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Ecclesiastes is almost certainly a Persian-period composition, written sometime in the fifth or fourth century BC in the small temple-province of Yehud. Choon-Leong Seow's Anchor Bible commentary (1997) marshals the strongest case: Late Biblical Hebrew studded with Aramaisms, a vocabulary that includes Persian loanwords (*pardes*, "park" or "garden," at 2:5; *pithgam*, "edict," at 8:11), and an economic register saturated with the language of money, contracts, inheritance, and commerce that fits the monetized economy of the Persian empire after Darius's coinage reforms but ill fits Solomon's tenth-century court. Roland Murphy (Word), Tremper Longman (NICOT), Thomas Krüger (Hermeneia), James Crenshaw (OTL), Norman Whybray, and Stuart Weeks all converge on a Persian or early Hellenistic dating, with disagreements measured in decades rather than centuries. The Solomonic frame — *the words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem* (1:1) — is a literary convention. Qohelet (קֹהֶלֶת, "assembler" or "convener of the assembly") is a function-name; the epilogist who frames the book at 12:9–14 already treats Qohelet as a wisdom teacher distinct from Solomon, and the Solomonic guise is dropped after chapter 2.

Material culture of Persian-period Yehud — the small population, the disrupted temple economy, the dependence on imperial power, the mixed coinage finds at Tel Mikhmoret, the Aramaic Persian-period correspondence at Elephantine — frames the world Qohelet writes from. This is a writer whose horizon is no longer the confident Davidic monarchy of Proverbs, no longer even the prophetic certainty that judgment will fall and restoration come. It is the horizon of a small province where time and chance happen to all, where wisdom is real but unappreciated, and where the dead are simply gone.

Textual witnesses include 4QQoh-a and 4QQoh-b at Qumran (the second-century BC fragments confirm a Hebrew text very close to the Masoretic), the LXX (a literal Greek translation, often credited to the school of Aquila), and the expansive Targum Qohelet (later, orthodoxizing — it inserts references to Torah, repentance, and the world-to-come at points where the Hebrew is theologically scandalous). The Hebrew text of chapter 9 is reasonably stable; the chief textual difficulty is the syntax of 9:1–2, where the MT and LXX diverge slightly in how they parse the rhythmic series "righteous … wise … the same event happens to all."

Hebrew terminology in this chapter carries the book's whole argument. *Hevel* (הֶבֶל, conventionally "vanity" but more accurately "vapor," "breath," "the ungraspable") does not appear in the strict refrain form here, but its conceptual presence saturates 9:1–6. *Yitron* (יִתְרוֹן, "surplus," "what's left over after the accounting") is the question Qohelet has been asking since 1:3 — what surplus does a man have for all his toil under the sun? — and 9:11 ("the race is not to the swift") is a meditation on the conspicuous absence of *yitron*. *Tahat ha-shemesh* (תַּחַת הַשֶּׁמֶשׁ, "under the sun"), the book's signature spatial frame, is repeated three times in this chapter alone. *Sheol* (שְׁאוֹל, the realm of the dead) appears at 9:10 in the sharpest mortalist statement in the Old Testament. *Miqreh* (מִקְרֶה, "happening," "chance," "what befalls one") at 9:11 names the contingency that erodes the wisdom-tradition's confidence that the wise prosper and the foolish fall.

The closest extant ancient Near Eastern parallel to anything in chapter 9 is the famous speech of Siduri the alewife to Gilgamesh in Tablet X of the *Old Babylonian Gilgamesh*: when you, O Gilgamesh, fill your belly with good things, day and night make merry, day and night dance and play, let your garments be clean, let your head be washed, may you bathe in water, gaze on the little one who holds your hand, let your wife enjoy your repeated embrace — for this is the lot of mankind. The verbal and structural overlap with Ecclesiastes 9:7–9 is one of the closest ANE-to-biblical parallels in the entire Hebrew Bible. Seow treats it as a probable literary echo (Qohelet read or absorbed the Mesopotamian skeptical wisdom tradition), Michael V. Fox in *A Time to Tear Down and a Time to Build Up* treats it as evidence of an independent existential pressure on which both texts converge, and Brevard Childs treats it canonically (Qohelet receives the wisdom of the nations and rewrites it under the frame of fear of God). The *Dialogue of Pessimism* and the *Dialogue of a Man with His Ba* are the broader ANE tradition into which this chapter fits.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 9 is structured in four clear movements: the meditation on death and the common fate (9:1–6), the carpe-diem refrain in its fullest biblical form (9:7–10), the time-and-chance reflection (9:11–12), and the parable of the poor wise man and the besieged city with its proverbial coda (9:13–18). The chapter is one of the most architecturally controlled in the book.

Qohelet's literary devices are on full display. The leitwort is *miqreh* — the same noun that opens at 2:14 ("one fate happens to them all") returns here doubled and intensified ("the same event happens to the righteous and the wicked"). The *carpe diem* refrain is the sixth and most elaborated of the seven "enjoy your portion" passages in the book (2:24–26, 3:12–13, 3:22, 5:18–20, 8:15, 9:7–10, 11:7–10), and 9:7–10 is the crescendo. Qohelet's command form — *go, eat your bread with joy* — is the most direct second-person address in the book and the most dense imperative cluster (eat, drink, let your garments be white, let oil not lack, enjoy life, do with your might).

The poem of 9:11–12, with its hammering anaphora ("not to the swift … not to the strong … not to the wise … not to the intelligent … not to those with knowledge"), is one of the great Hebrew set-pieces. Robert Alter calls it "a small masterpiece of disabused worldly observation." The negative parallelism is reinforced by the simile of 9:12 — *like fish taken in an evil net, like birds caught in a snare* — turning the abstract into a snapshot of the trap closing on a creature that did not see it coming.

The parable of 9:13–18 functions almost as a counter-narrative to the wisdom literature's confidence: a poor wise man saves a besieged city by his wisdom, and yet *no one remembered that poor man*. The aphorism "wisdom is better than weapons of war, but one sinner destroys much good" (9:18) lands with the bitter cadence of someone who has watched it happen.

Textual variants in chapter 9 are minor but worth flagging. The MT of 9:2 lacks the phrase "and the bad," which the LXX, Peshitta, and Vulgate all include — most commentators (Seow, Crenshaw, Murphy) restore "the good and the bad" to balance the series. At 9:4 the kethiv-qere distinguishes between *yebuḥar* ("who is chosen") and *yeḥubbar* ("who is joined") — the qere reading preserves the proverbial sense of being among the living. None of these variants alter the chapter's meaning materially.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Ecclesiastes sits at the third corner of the wisdom triangle: Proverbs articulates the confident retributive theology (the wise prosper, fools perish), Job stages the breakdown of that theology under the pressure of innocent suffering, and Ecclesiastes accepts the breakdown as the actual structure of the world under the sun. Chapter 9 is where this triangulation comes most sharply into focus — *one event happens to the righteous and the wicked* is the doctrinal opposite of *the righteous shall not be moved* (Proverbs 10:30).

**Jewish trajectory.** Ben Sira (Sirach 14:11–19, 41:1–4) reads as a direct response to Qohelet — Sirach absorbs the carpe-diem theme and the meditation on death, but reinscribes them under Torah-piety and the fear of the Lord. The Wisdom of Solomon (1:16–2:24, c. 1st c. BC) goes further, putting a Qohelet-like skeptical speech in the mouth of "the ungodly" precisely in order to refute it; the book then asserts the immortality of the soul and divine judgment as the answer Qohelet did not give. This is the Jewish wisdom tradition explicitly correcting Qohelet's mortalism. Mishnah Yadayim 3:5 records the canonization debate between the schools of Hillel and Shammai over whether Qohelet "defiles the hands" (the technical phrase for canonical status); Hillel's school prevailed in admitting the book. Talmud Shabbat 30b records that the Sages "sought to suppress the Book of Qohelet because its words contradict one another, but they did not suppress it because its beginning is words of Torah and its end is words of Torah" — the rabbinic awareness that the book's framing (the epilogist's call to fear God and keep the commandments) is what saved its canonical status.

Qohelet Rabbah (the Midrash on Ecclesiastes) and the Targum Qohelet both work hard to orthodoxize 9:5–6 and 9:10. Where Qohelet writes *the dead know nothing*, the Targum supplies "the wicked dead in their lifetime know nothing of the world to come." Where Qohelet writes *there is no work or thought or knowledge or wisdom in Sheol*, the Targum reads "there is no work in this world for one who has gone down to Sheol." Both glosses preserve the rabbinic doctrine of *olam ha-ba* (the world to come) by relocating the verse's negation into the human's experiential standpoint while leaving the metaphysics of resurrection intact.

Maimonides (*Guide for the Perplexed* 3.51) reads Qohelet as a meditation on intellectual immortality: the soul's union with the active intellect, not the body's persistence, is what survives. Ibn Ezra reads philologically and reads the carpe-diem as a description of how unphilosophical people experience life, not as Qohelet's own counsel. Rashi reads with the Targumic glosses and harmonizes Qohelet to rabbinic doctrine. Ralbag (Gersonides) reads philosophically with Maimonides. Sforno reads ethically. Robert Gordis (*Koheleth: The Man and His World*, 1951) reads as a humanistic Jewish thinker, defending the integrity of Qohelet's skepticism and refusing to soften it. Michael V. Fox (*A Time to Tear Down*, 1999) reads Qohelet as a thinker who has worked through and partially abandoned traditional wisdom but continues to commend pleasure and labor as God-given gifts.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic reception is shaped by allegorical reading. Gregory of Nyssa's *Homilies on Ecclesiastes* (c. 380) reads Qohelet as Christ the assembler-of-the-Church and the carpe-diem passages as references to the Eucharist (the white garments, the oil, the bread, the wine). Jerome's *Commentary on Ecclesiastes* (388) was written for a Roman widow named Blesilla and reads the book as preparing the reader for the contempt of the world and ascetic vocation; the commentary became the dominant medieval lens. Didymus the Blind's commentary, recovered at Tura in 1941, supplies an Alexandrian allegorical reading. Origen fragments survive. Hugh of St. Victor and Bonaventure read in the Jerome tradition. Aquinas in his Lectura on Ecclesiastes reads Qohelet as describing the natural insufficiency of created goods and pointing toward the supernatural fulfillment in beatitude. The Glossa Ordinaria preserves the patristic synthesis.

The Reformation broke with allegory. Luther's *Notes on Ecclesiastes* (delivered 1526, published 1532) reads the book as a treatise on Christian vocation: Qohelet is teaching contentment with one's God-given calling and a critique of the human heart's restless self-justification. Luther's reading of 9:7–10 is the locus classicus of Lutheran vocation theology — God has already approved your bread and wine; eat with joy, work with your might, in your station. Calvin published no commentary on Ecclesiastes (his silence on the book is itself notable; he preached on it only briefly).

Critical scholarship from Franz Delitzsch (1875) through Crenshaw, Murphy, Seow, Fox, and Whybray takes the book's skepticism seriously as a voice of post-exilic Israelite intellectual life. Tremper Longman's NICOT commentary (1998) takes the strongest Christian-canonical position that the epilogist *disapproves* of Qohelet — the body of the book is a frame-tale of a heterodox skeptic whose voice the epilogist contains and corrects. Iain Provan (NIVAC) reads Qohelet as a sincere wisdom teacher within the orthodox tradition. Brevard Childs's canonical reading takes the canonical shape (Qohelet plus epilogist) as the inspired form. Walter Brueggemann reads Qohelet as a counter-testimonial voice within Israel's witness, refusing premature theological closure.

**Where they diverge.** The deepest divide is whether Qohelet is teaching or being contained — whether 9:5–6's *the dead know nothing* is canonical Israelite wisdom that subsequent revelation extends and corrects (the rabbinic, Maimonidean, mainstream Christian reading), a heterodox voice that the epilogist quarantines (Longman's reading), or a legitimate voice in a polyphonic canon that the canon itself refuses to silence (Childs, Fox, Brueggemann).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Mortalism and the afterlife.** Qohelet's *the dead know nothing, and they have no more reward, for the memory of them is forgotten* (9:5) and *there is no work or thought or knowledge or wisdom in Sheol, to which you are going* (9:10) are the sharpest mortalist statements in the Hebrew canon. Daniel 12:2 (*many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life and some to shame and everlasting contempt*) is the first unambiguous Old Testament resurrection text, and it is later than Qohelet by at least a century. The New Testament resurrection theology — 1 Corinthians 15, Revelation 20 — is built on the Daniel-Maccabean trajectory, not on Qohelet.

The reformed-Lutheran-Calvinist soul-sleep tradition (developed in radical Reformation thought, surviving in Seventh-day Adventism and some Lutheran traditions) reads 9:5–6 as confirming that the dead are unconscious until the resurrection, and reads the New Testament's *sleep* metaphor (1 Cor 15:6, 1 Thess 4:13–18) as reinforcing this. The Catholic intermediate-state tradition, drawing on Luke 16:19–31 (the rich man and Lazarus), 2 Maccabees 12:43–46 (prayer for the dead), and Philippians 1:23 (*to depart and be with Christ*), reads the soul as conscious and either with God or in purgatory between death and resurrection. The Orthodox tradition reads similarly, with its own emphasis on the toll-houses tradition.

The steelman of the mortalist reading is that Qohelet is doing his work *honestly within the epistemic standpoint of pre-apocalyptic Israelite religion*. The doctrine of resurrection had not yet emerged. To soften 9:5–6 by appeal to later revelation is anachronistic. Qohelet's silence on personal afterlife is the silence of his entire tradition; what he denies is not afterlife metaphysically but afterlife as object of any knowledge available to the wise observer under the sun. Seow reads this as Qohelet's epistemological discipline: he refuses to claim what he cannot know.

The steelman of the canonical reading (Childs, Provan) is that the canonical shape places Qohelet in dialogue with Daniel and the New Testament rather than against them. Qohelet's mortalism functions as a *negative limit* — it tells us what cannot be said about the dead from the standpoint of *tahat ha-shemesh*. Resurrection theology is a *positive limit* added by later revelation. The two are not contradictions but a structured silence and a structured speech.

Where this leaves us: Qohelet's denial of knowledge of the afterlife sits inside the canon alongside Daniel's affirmation of bodily resurrection and the Pauline kerygma. Christian theology has historically integrated them by distinguishing the standpoint of natural human observation (Qohelet) from the standpoint of revealed eschatology (Daniel, the New Testament). The unresolved question: if Qohelet's discipline is the discipline of speaking only what can be observed under the sun, what does it mean that the canonical shape preserves his discipline alongside the apocalyptic disclosure that breaks it? Has Christian preaching too readily collapsed Qohelet into resurrection hope, losing the negative theology Qohelet was teaching?

**Tension 2: The carpe-diem alongside Gilgamesh.** Ecclesiastes 9:7–10 and Siduri's speech in Gilgamesh Tablet X are nearly verbal twins. Either Qohelet borrowed (Seow's view: the book is in conversation with Mesopotamian skeptical wisdom and reframes it under YHWH's frame), Qohelet's text and Gilgamesh independently express the same human existential pressure under the same condition of mortality (Fox's view: the parallel is structural, not genealogical), or both texts are taken into the canonical shape and given a new frame (Childs's reading: whatever the genealogy, the canonical Qohelet is the one given to us).

The Catholic and Orthodox reception traditions read 9:7–10 sacramentally — the white garments are baptism, the oil is chrismation, the bread and wine are the Eucharist. Gregory of Nyssa is the locus classicus. The Reformed tradition resists the sacramental reading and reads it vocationally: the bread, wine, garments, and oil are the ordinary goods of human life that God has approved. Luther and Calvin (in his rare comments) read it as the doctrine of vocation and contentment.

The Jewish tradition reads 9:9 — *enjoy life with the wife whom you love* — as a foundational text on marriage as gift; both Talmud and medieval ethical literature cite it. The mortalist horizon (9:10) is harmonized with rabbinic *olam ha-ba* doctrine through the Targumic glosses. Maimonides reads the carpe-diem as the proper attitude for the unphilosophical many, not the ultimate counsel of wisdom; the philosopher seeks intellectual immortality.

Where this leaves us: the carpe-diem in chapter 9 is more concrete, more bodily, more domestic than anywhere else in the book. It commends bread, wine, white garments, oil, marriage, work — the texture of a particular human life — as God-given portion under conditions of finitude. The unresolved question: if the carpe-diem is what Qohelet commends in the face of the mortalist horizon, and if the Christian tradition has historically read either through it (sacramentalism) or past it (vocational contentment), what would it mean to read it as Qohelet wrote it — without dissolving the horizon and without relocating the joy elsewhere?

## The Thread

Job 12:23 (*he makes nations great, and he destroys them; he enlarges nations, and leads them away*) shares Qohelet's sense of the unpredictability of *miqreh* and the limits of wisdom-as-prediction; Qohelet 9:11 is the wisdom-tradition's version of the same observation. Proverbs 21:31 (*the horse is made ready for the day of battle, but the victory belongs to the LORD*) is the orthodox version that Qohelet 9:11 secularizes by replacing "the LORD" with "time and chance." Sirach 41:1–4 picks up Qohelet 9:1–6 and reframes it under Torah-piety: death is not a horror to those who fear the Lord. James 4:14 (*you are a mist that appears for a little time and then vanishes*) translates Qohelet's *hevel* into Greek (*atmis*, "vapor") and brings the book's central image into the New Testament — the only direct New Testament echo of the *hevel* refrain, and proof that the canonical shape is bigger than Qohelet but cannot be read without him.
