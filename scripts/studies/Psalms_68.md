# Psalm 68 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 68 carries the superscription *la-menatzeach le-David mizmor shir* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ לְדָוִד מִזְמוֹר שִׁיר, "to the choirmaster, of David, a psalm, a song"). The Davidic attribution and the *mizmor shir* designation place the piece in the cultic-musical orbit of the temple, but the language itself is among the most archaic in the Psalter. Frank Moore Cross, in *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* (1973), assigned Psalm 68 to the same stratum of very-early Hebrew poetry as Exodus 15 (the Song of the Sea), Deuteronomy 32-33 (the Song and Blessing of Moses), and Judges 5 (the Song of Deborah). The features that mark this stratum — preserved archaic verbal forms, scarcity of the definite article, retained energic *nun* endings, and dense parataxis — are all present in Psalm 68.

The psalm is also notoriously, almost proverbially difficult. Mitchell Dahood's *Anchor Bible* commentary on the Psalms catalogued the chapter's hapax legomena and proposed extensive Ugaritic and Phoenician parallels for its contested vocabulary. S. R. Driver called it the most difficult psalm in the Psalter. Some critics — Albright most famously — proposed that Psalm 68 is a catalogue of opening incipits from older Israelite war-songs, stitched together liturgically rather than composed as a continuous piece. Whether or not Albright's incipit theory holds, the composite character of the psalm is widely accepted.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 68 as a *Hymnus* of the divine warrior, drawing on cultic-procession traditions. Patrick Miller's monograph *The Divine Warrior in Early Israel* (1973) treats Psalm 68 as one of the centerpiece texts of the warrior-theophany pattern: God arises (v. 1, *yaqum Elohim*, יָקוּם אֱלֹהִים — the formula that opens the Ark in Numbers 10:35), the enemies scatter, the cosmos shakes, and the divine king processes to his sanctuary.

The psalm's Sinai-cluster (vv. 7-8) reads almost as a citation of Judges 5:4-5 — Deborah's song. The Hebrew of v. 8, *eretz ra'asha af-shamayim natfu mi-pnei Elohim, zeh Sinai mi-pnei Elohim Elohei Yisrael* (אֶרֶץ רָעָשָׁה אַף־שָׁמַיִם נָטְפוּ מִפְּנֵי אֱלֹהִים זֶה סִינַי מִפְּנֵי אֱלֹהִים אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל), echoes Judges 5:4-5 in vocabulary and meter. The phrase *zeh Sinai* ("this/the One of Sinai") is itself archaic — a divine epithet that may correspond to the Egyptian-attested toponym *yhw3 in the land of the Shasu* (twelfth-century BCE Egyptian texts mentioning "YHW of the Shasu" near Edom/Midian). That extra-biblical attestation, while debated, suggests that "the One of Sinai" was a recognized epithet for Israel's God in the wider Late Bronze Age Levant.

The Elohistic Psalter's signature is everywhere: *Elohim* dominates over *YHWH*, though the Tetragrammaton breaks through at moments of high intensity (vv. 4, 16, 26). The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of Psalm 68 in 4QPs-a, with no major variants from the Masoretic Text. The Septuagint, however, presents a fascinating divergence at v. 18 that becomes load-bearing for New Testament Christology (see Lens 4).

ANE parallels are dense. The "rider on the clouds" epithet of v. 4 (*la-rokhev ba-aravot*, לָרֹכֵב בָּעֲרָבוֹת) corresponds almost word-for-word to Ugaritic Baal's epithet *rkb 'rpt* ("rider on the clouds") in the Ugaritic Baal Cycle (KTU 1.2.iv). The divine-warrior procession to Mount Zaphon in the Baal Cycle parallels the divine procession to Zion in vv. 15-18. Marduk's procession in the Babylonian *Enuma Elish* is structurally similar. What is distinctive is not the form — it is broadly West-Semitic — but the historical-narrative content: this divine warrior actually went out at the head of an actual people through an actual wilderness.

## Lens 2: The Literature of the Text

Robert Lowth's parallelism categories carry only so far in Psalm 68; the chapter strains his system because it shifts genre several times. Synonymous parallelism dominates the opening theophany (v. 1: "his enemies shall be scattered / those who hate him shall flee"; v. 2: "as smoke is driven away / as wax melts"). Antithetic parallelism shapes v. 3 ("the wicked shall perish / the righteous shall be glad"). Synthetic parallelism builds the divine-warrior catalogue (vv. 7-10). And staircase parallelism — where each line picks up and advances the previous — drives the climactic procession (vv. 24-27).

Strophically, the psalm divides into roughly seven movements:
- vv. 1-3: Theophanic opening (the *yaqum Elohim* formula)
- vv. 4-6: God as defender of the marginalized
- vv. 7-10: The wilderness march
- vv. 11-14: The conquest and the spoils
- vv. 15-18: The procession to Zion
- vv. 19-23: The salvation oracle
- vv. 24-31: The cultic procession in the sanctuary
- vv. 32-35: Universal hymn

Three *selah* markers (vv. 7, 19, 32) punctuate the major transitions. The inclusio is structural: the psalm opens with God arising and enemies scattering (v. 1) and closes with God giving power and strength to his people (v. 35) — the same divine warrior pattern bookending the piece. There is no neat chiasmus; the form is processional rather than concentric.

The crux interpretum is verse 18: *alita la-marom shavita shevi laqachta matanot ba-adam* (עָלִיתָ לַמָּרוֹם שָׁבִיתָ שֶּׁבִי לָקַחְתָּ מַתָּנוֹת בָּאָדָם, "you ascended on high, you led captive a captivity, you received gifts among/in the man"). The Hebrew *laqachta* ("you received/took") versus the LXX *edōkas dōmata* ("you gave gifts") — the textual divergence that becomes load-bearing for Ephesians 4:8 — is the single most discussed translation problem in the Psalter. The Aramaic Targum on Psalm 68 already harmonized the text to a Moses-receiving-Torah scenario: "you ascended to the firmament, Moses the prophet, you led captivity captive, you taught the words of Torah, you gave gifts to the children of men." The Targum's reading bridges to the LXX's "gave," providing critical context for Paul's later citation.

Striking imagery accumulates: smoke driven away, wax melting, the dove's wings of silver and pinions of gold (v. 13 — perhaps a battle standard or a poetic figure for spoils), the chariots numbering "twice ten thousand, thousands upon thousands" (v. 17), the singers and tambourine-playing virgins in procession (v. 25), and Cush stretching out her hands (v. 31). The sensory density is enormous — visual, kinetic, auditory — making the psalm read as a pageant rather than a meditation.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Book II Placement.** Psalm 68 sits near the close of Book II of the Psalter (Pss 42-72), within the Elohistic Psalter, three psalms before the Solomonic royal psalm that closes the Book. Its divine-warrior theology stands in the trajectory that Wilson identified: Books I-III tracing the rise and crisis of monarchy, with the warrior-king Yahweh undergirding the human Davidic monarchy.

**Jewish Trajectory.** The Aramaic *Targum on Psalm 68* is unusually expansive, particularly on v. 18, applying the ascension imagery to Moses on Sinai receiving and giving Torah. *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 68 extends this Mosaic-revelation reading and links the psalm to Shavuot (the festival commemorating Sinai). Rashi reads v. 18 of Moses ascending Sinai. Ibn Ezra notes the textual difficulties and offers cautious philological glosses. Radak proposes that the psalm celebrates David's bringing of the Ark to Jerusalem (cf. 2 Sam 6) — the procession of v. 24 read as the Ark procession. Nachmanides combines the Ark-procession reading with a messianic-eschatological horizon.

In modern Jewish scholarship, Levenson treats Psalm 68 as a key text for the divine-warrior tradition's persistence into liturgical use; Sarna, in his JPS materials, emphasizes the wilderness-Sinai cluster; Heschel reads the psalm's "father of the fatherless" (v. 5) as the moral signature of the warrior-God — the same God who scatters armies cares for widows. The psalm is recited liturgically on Shavuot in many traditions, anchoring it to the Sinai-revelation festival.

**Christian Trajectory.** Christian reception centers overwhelmingly on **Ephesians 4:8**, where Paul cites Psalm 68:18 in the form *anabas eis hupsos ēchmalōteusen aichmalōsian, edōken domata tois anthrōpois* ("when he ascended on high, he led captivity captive, he gave gifts to men") — applied to the ascended Christ giving gifts (apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors, teachers) to the Church. Augustine's *Enarratio in Psalmum 67* (LXX numbering) reads the psalm Christologically throughout: the divine warrior is Christ, the procession is the resurrection-and-ascension, the gifts are the Spirit's charisms. Athanasius and Cyril of Alexandria read v. 18 as one of the strongest Old Testament witnesses to the ascension. Cassiodorus and Aquinas extend the Augustinian reading. Luther's *Operationes* treats the psalm as a celebration of Christ's victory over death, hell, and the devil — a reading that fed directly into Lutheran *Christus Victor* atonement theology. Calvin (1557) handles the textual problem at v. 18 with characteristic philological care, suggesting that "received" and "gave" are reconcilable: a king who receives tribute then distributes it.

The Pauline application has generated centuries of exegetical engagement. Andrew Lincoln (*Word Biblical Commentary* on Ephesians) traces how Paul read the verse through the Targum-mediated tradition — receiving-in-order-to-give. Markus Bockmuehl notes that Paul's textual base for v. 18 is closer to the Targum than to either MT or LXX, which reveals something important about the Pauline use of Jewish-Aramaic interpretive tradition. Susan Docherty's work on Hebrews-and-Pauline LXX use treats Ephesians 4:8 as a paradigm case of New Testament authors using Septuagintal-and-targumic forms of OT texts as live theological sources.

The modern critical commentary tradition is rich on Psalm 68 specifically. Goldingay's *Psalms* commentary devotes extensive attention to the translation difficulties. Tate's *Word Biblical Commentary* and Hossfeld-Zenger's *Hermeneia* commentary handle the Sinai-cluster and the procession traditions. Kraus's *Biblischer Kommentar* set the modern critical framework. Brueggemann places the psalm within his "orientation" rubric (the warrior-King is reliable). Tanner emphasizes the liturgical-processional setting.

**NT Use.** Beyond Ephesians 4:8 (citing 68:18), Psalm 68 echoes in the imagery of Christ's ascension across the New Testament — the angelic chariots of Acts 1:9-11, the captivity-led-captive language of Colossians 2:15. Revelation's divine-warrior imagery (Rev 19:11-16) draws on the same theophanic stratum. The Christian liturgical use is widespread: Psalm 68 appears in many Western traditions on Ascension Day and Pentecost.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The textual difficulty as theological problem.** Psalm 68 is the most textually difficult psalm in the Psalter — and the New Testament's load-bearing citation of it (Eph 4:8) departs from the Masoretic Hebrew. Three positions structure the discussion. The *strict-MT* position (some Reformed and Jewish scholars) holds that "received gifts" is the original and that Paul has either misread or paraphrased loosely; on this reading, Christian use of the verse is interpretive rather than exegetical. The *LXX-and-Targum* position (Lincoln, Bockmuehl, Docherty) holds that Paul is reading a textual tradition different from MT, with the Targum and LXX preserving an interpretive line that makes "gave" a legitimate rendering — receiving-and-distributing as a single royal action. The *bidirectional* position (Calvin, much modern Christian scholarship) holds that the Hebrew itself can support both readings: kings receive tribute precisely in order to redistribute, so "received" and "gave" describe two phases of one royal motion. The strongest steelman for the strict-MT position is exegetical honesty: the Hebrew says "received," not "gave," and Christians should not pretend otherwise. The strongest steelman for the LXX-and-Targum position is historical: Paul's textual base is what it is, and Christian theology has lived with translational mediation since the LXX itself. The strongest steelman for the bidirectional position is canonical: both readings illuminate the same royal-divine pattern, and the canonical text is generative rather than narrowly determinate.

**Tension 2: The divine warrior and modern violence-readings.** Psalm 68 is unambiguously martial. Verse 23 — "that you may strike your feet in their blood, that the tongues of your dogs may have their portion from the foe" — is one of the more graphic violence-images in the Psalter. Three positions on its theological status. The *historical-distancing* position (Brueggemann, parts of the *Hermeneia* tradition) reads the divine-warrior tradition as a culturally-conditioned theological idiom that the canon itself relativizes by later trajectories — by Isaiah's servant, by Jesus' refusal of the sword. On this reading, Christians can read Psalm 68 as ancient liturgy without being bound to its violence-imagery. The *typological-Christological* position (Augustine, Aquinas, much classical Christian exegesis) reads the divine-warrior as Christ defeating sin, death, and the devil — the violence is real but its enemies are cosmic-spiritual, not ethnic. The *unflinching-historical* position (much modern Jewish reading, James Barr in different ways) refuses to spiritualize the violence: ancient Israel really did imagine God scattering enemies in actual battle, and this is part of the canonical witness whether comfortable or not. Patrick Miller's *Divine Warrior* monograph leans in this direction.

The honest assessment is that the psalm contains all three layers. There was a real military-cultic context in which it functioned. The canonical trajectory does relativize the warrior-imagery toward eschatological judgment and toward Christ's nonviolent enactment. And the violence-imagery does not simply disappear — it remains, demanding theological reckoning rather than embarrassed silence.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 68 is a procession we are tempted to either spiritualize away or take too literally. Both moves miss what the psalm is doing. The God who scatters enemies is the same God who is "father of the fatherless and protector of widows" (v. 5). The warrior-king and the defender of the marginalized are not two gods; they are one. Either side without the other becomes a different deity.

**Closing question:** When the psalm calls God "father of the fatherless" in the same breath as describing him scattering kings, can you hold both — or do you reach for only one half of the picture?

## The Thread

The thread of Psalm 68 runs through Numbers 10:35 (the Ark formula "Arise, O Lord, and let your enemies be scattered" — the opening of v. 1), through Judges 5:4-5 (Deborah's parallel theophany), and through Ephesians 4:8 (Paul's ascension-citation of v. 18). Read these alongside the psalm and the through-line becomes clear: the divine warrior who marched out before Israel is the same one Christians confess as ascended above all heavens.
