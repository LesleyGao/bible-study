# Psalm 69 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 69 carries the superscription *la-menatzeach al-shoshannim le-David* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ עַל־שׁוֹשַׁנִּים לְדָוִד, "to the choirmaster, according to Lilies, of David"). The musical-tune designation *al-shoshannim* ("upon Lilies") appears also in Psalms 45, 60, and 80, suggesting a known melody to which the psalm was set. The Davidic attribution is contested by modern critical scholarship: the explicit references to "Zion" and "the cities of Judah" being rebuilt (vv. 35-36) have led most commentators since Gunkel to read at least the closing strophe as exilic or post-exilic, while the bulk of the lament could be earlier. Hossfeld and Zenger date the core composition to the late pre-exilic period with exilic-redaction additions; Goldingay treats the psalm as a single composition reflecting the post-monarchic hope for Judean restoration.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 69 as a *Klagelied des Einzelnen* (individual lament) — the longest and most theologically complex such lament in the Psalter, second only perhaps to Psalm 22 in scope and in later Christian-Christological appropriation. The psalm follows the standard lament form (invocation, complaint, petition, vow of praise) but expands each element to extraordinary length and adds the most severe imprecation in the Psalter outside Psalm 137.

The Hebrew opening *hoshi'eni Elohim ki va'u mayim ad-nafesh* (הוֹשִׁיעֵנִי אֱלֹהִים כִּי בָאוּ מַיִם עַד־נָפֶשׁ, "save me, O God, for the waters have come to my neck") deploys the classic ANE drowning-as-distress metaphor. Mesopotamian *šuilla* prayers use identical imagery — Marduk-Re prayers speak of the petitioner sinking in floodwaters and crying out for divine rescue. The Ugaritic Baal Cycle also exploits the chaos-waters / Yamm motif, where being overwhelmed by water signifies cosmic-existential threat. Psalm 69 takes this stock metaphor and personalizes it: the speaker is not a king facing chaos-monsters but an individual facing social-religious persecution.

The Hebrew *cherpah shavarah libbi* (חֶרְפָּה שָׁבְרָה לִבִּי, "reproach has broken my heart," v. 20) is among the most tender phrases in lament literature. The verb *shavar* ("to break") in the Qal perfect with *lev* ("heart") as object is rare; the closer parallels are in Lamentations and in Jeremiah. The psalm's emotional vocabulary — *cherpah* (reproach, vv. 7, 9, 10, 19, 20), *kelimah* (dishonor, vv. 6, 19), *busha* (shame, v. 19) — draws on the rich shame-honor lexicon of ancient Mediterranean culture, where reputation was a currency of survival.

The Elohistic Psalter's signature is partial here: while *Elohim* dominates, the Tetragrammaton breaks through repeatedly (vv. 6, 13, 16, 31, 33), suggesting the editor's substitution was applied less mechanically than in some psalms of Book II. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragmentary witnesses to Psalm 69 in 4QPs-a and 11QPs-c, with no major textual variants from the Masoretic Text. The Septuagint preserves the imprecations intact, with no editorial softening.

The composite social setting is striking. The speaker mentions "those who sit in the gate" mocking him (v. 12) — the gate being the seat of legal-civic authority, suggesting public legal trouble. The accusation "what I did not steal must I now restore?" (v. 4) sounds like a forensic dispute over property. The wearing of sackcloth, fasting, and weeping in vv. 10-11 indicates a public mourning that has become an object of mockery rather than sympathy. This is the social experience of a person whose religious devotion has become a target.

## Lens 2: The Literature of the Text

Robert Lowth's parallelism categories appear throughout: synonymous parallelism dominates the drowning-imagery (v. 1: "save me, O God / for the waters have come to my neck"; v. 2: "I sink in deep mire / I have come into deep waters"); antithetic parallelism shapes the contrast between the speaker and the wicked (v. 22-23 against v. 32-33); synthetic parallelism advances the imprecation (vv. 22-28).

Strophically, the psalm divides into roughly seven movements:
- vv. 1-4: Opening cry — the drowning metaphor
- vv. 5-12: The shame-and-reproach complaint
- vv. 13-18: Central petition for deliverance
- vv. 19-21: The reproach and the bitter offering
- vv. 22-28: The imprecation (the seven curses)
- vv. 29-33: Vow of praise and turn to thanksgiving
- vv. 34-36: Final cosmic hymn and Zion-restoration

The structural turn at v. 13 (*va-ani tefilati lekha YHWH*, וַאֲנִי תְפִלָּתִי לְךָ יְהוָה, "but as for me, my prayer is to you, O LORD") is the classic lament-pivot — the moment where the speaker turns from describing distress to addressing God in petition. Claus Westermann identified this *aber* ("but") moment as structurally constitutive of the lament form.

The seven curses in vv. 22-28 form a tightly structured imprecation — perhaps the most concentrated curse-sequence in the Psalter. Each curse targets a specific dimension of life: their table (sustenance), their eyes (perception), their loins (vitality), the divine indignation (cosmic standing), their camp (dwelling), their punishment (judicial reckoning), their inscription in the book of the living (covenantal status). The imprecation is comprehensive — it asks God to undo the wicked at every level.

Verse 21 — *va-yitnu be-varuti rosh ve-li-tzma'i yashquni chometz* (וַיִּתְּנוּ בְּבָרוּתִי רֹאשׁ וְלִצְמָאִי יַשְׁקוּנִי חֹמֶץ, "they gave me gall for my food, and for my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink") — is the verse that the four canonical Gospels collectively activate at the crucifixion. The Hebrew *rosh* literally means "poison" or "bitter herb" (perhaps wormwood-derived); the LXX renders *cholē* (gall). *Chometz* means "vinegar" or sour wine — a poor person's drink, sometimes used to dull pain or quench thirst.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Book II Placement.** Psalm 69 sits near the end of Book II (Pss 42-72), within the Elohistic Psalter, three psalms before the Solomonic royal closure. Its placement among the Davidic laments contributes to the Book's shaping of the Davidic voice as suffering-and-vindicated. Wilson's *Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* sees Books I-III as tracing the rise and crisis of the Davidic monarchy; Psalm 69's lament-tone fits this trajectory.

**Jewish Trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 69 reads it of David in his Saul-pursued period, with some traditions extending the psalm to the destruction of the First Temple and the suffering of righteous Jews under foreign domination. Rashi reads the psalm of David but allows messianic-eschatological resonance. Ibn Ezra emphasizes the Davidic biographical setting. Radak applies the psalm to the exilic experience of Israel as a whole. Nachmanides extends this to a corporate-eschatological reading: the psalm voices the suffering of Israel through history. The imprecations are read in Jewish tradition as cries against the historical persecutors of the covenant people.

In modern Jewish thought, Levenson, Sarna, and Buber have variously emphasized the psalm's social-shame dimension and its place in the Jewish liturgical-suffering tradition. Adele Reinhartz, Daniel Boyarin, and other modern Jewish scholars have engaged especially with the New Testament's Christological appropriation of the psalm — particularly because that appropriation has historically generated anti-Jewish readings (see Lens 4). The psalm appears in some Jewish liturgical traditions on Tisha B'Av (the fast commemorating the destruction of the Temples) and on fast days more generally.

**Christian Trajectory.** Psalm 69 is one of the most-cited psalms in the New Testament — alongside Psalm 22 (the dereliction-cry psalm) and Psalm 110 (the messianic enthronement psalm), it forms the triad of psalms most heavily invoked for early Christian Christology. The major NT citations:

- **Psalm 69:9a** — *qin'at beitkha akhalatni* ("zeal for your house has consumed me") — cited in **John 2:17** as the disciples' interpretive lens for Jesus' temple-cleansing action.
- **Psalm 69:9b** — "the reproaches of those who reproached you fell on me" — cited in **Romans 15:3** as Paul's Christological lens for Christ-bearing-the-reproach.
- **Psalm 69:21** — "they gave me poison for food and vinegar to drink" — activated across all four Passion narratives: **Matthew 27:34** (the offered drink before crucifixion), **Matthew 27:48** / **Mark 15:36** / **John 19:29** (the sponge of vinegar offered on the cross). The Johannine version is most explicit: "Jesus, knowing that all was now finished, said (to fulfill the Scripture), 'I thirst.'"
- **Psalm 69:22-23** — the imprecations — cited in **Romans 11:9-10** as Paul's framing of Israel's stumbling in unbelief.
- **Psalm 69:25** — "may their camp be a desolation" — cited in **Acts 1:20** of Judas Iscariot.

Augustine's *Enarratio in Psalmum 68* (LXX numbering) reads Psalm 69 as the voice of Christ-and-his-Body — the *totus Christus*, head and members, suffering and praying together. Athanasius treats the psalm as a Christological lens. Cassiodorus reads it Christologically throughout. Aquinas, in the *Postilla*, structures his commentary around the Christological citations. Luther's *Operationes* treats Psalm 69 as a chief witness to Christ's suffering — the psalm spoken from the cross. Calvin, in his 1557 commentary, emphasizes that the psalm is David's first, Christ's by typological extension, and the Church's by participation.

Modern critical commentary — Gunkel, Mowinckel, Westermann, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate, Wenham — has largely treated Psalm 69 as a complex individual lament with Davidic-and-corporate-Israelite resonance. Brueggemann's category of "disorientation" psalms places Psalm 69 prominently. Patrick Miller has emphasized the psalm's reproach-and-shame vocabulary; William P. Brown its imagery-density; Beth Tanner its lectionary-Christological prominence.

Christian liturgical use is dense. Psalm 69 is among the most-used psalms in Holy Week — Western, Eastern, and Reformed traditions all anchor it to Maundy Thursday, Good Friday, or both. The Liturgy of the Hours and the Anglican/Episcopal *Book of Common Prayer* historically cycled the psalm through Lenten hours. In modern revisions, however, both the Catholic *Liturgia Horarum* (1971) and several Protestant breviaries have explicitly omitted vv. 22-28 — the imprecation. This editorial decision is itself theologically significant (see Lens 4).

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The imprecation in a Christologically-applied psalm.** Psalm 69 is one of the most heavily Christologically-applied psalms in the canon — and it contains the harshest sustained imprecation outside Psalm 137. The same psalm whose words Christians believe Jesus enacted on the cross also contains "let their eyes be darkened... pour out your indignation upon them... let them be blotted out of the book of the living." Three positions structure the discussion.

The *liturgical-pruning* position (modern Catholic *Liturgia Horarum*, several Protestant breviaries) treats the imprecations as inappropriate for Christian liturgical use and removes them. The justification: Christian prayer is shaped by the Sermon on the Mount's "love your enemies" command (Matt 5:44); to pray imprecation against enemies is to pray against the gospel.

The *whole-canon* position (Brueggemann, C. S. Lewis in *Reflections on the Psalms*, Eric Zenger in *A God of Vengeance?*, much of the recent retrieval movement) insists on praying the entire psalm, imprecations included. The argument: imprecation gives liturgical voice to legitimate human rage at injustice; suppressing it is not Christian charity but evasion. To pray the imprecations is to entrust one's anger to God rather than acting on it. Lewis defended the imprecatory psalms as honest where pious sentiment is dishonest. Zenger's monograph mounts the most sustained case.

The *Christological-substitution* position (parts of the Reformed tradition) holds that Christ has absorbed the imprecation in his cross-bearing, so the psalm's curses are now retrospectively read as falling on Christ rather than on enemies. On this reading, the imprecations remain in the psalm but are reread through the cross.

The strongest steelman for the liturgical-pruning position is moral: imprecation has historically generated anti-Jewish, anti-heretical, and political-vengeance readings, and Christians have responsibility for the use to which we put our prayers. The strongest steelman for the whole-canon position is psychological-pastoral: rage is real, suppressed rage is dangerous, and the psalms model how to pray rather than enact rage. The strongest steelman for the Christological-substitution position is canonical: the cross does change how Christians read every text.

**Tension 2: The Pauline use of Psalm 69 in Romans 11 and the post-Holocaust question.** Paul applies Psalm 69:22-23 — the imprecation — to "the rest of Israel" who did not believe in the Messiah (Rom 11:7-10). The history of Christian use of this Pauline application is grim. The "darkened eyes" of Romans 11:10, derived from Psalm 69:23, became a stock anti-Jewish trope in patristic, medieval, and Reformation polemics — visible in stained glass (the blindfolded Synagoga figure in medieval cathedrals), in liturgy, and in centuries of contempt-tradition that fed into the Holocaust.

Three positions on the post-Holocaust reading. The *full-revaluation* position (some Catholic post-Vatican-II scholarship; the *Pontifical Biblical Commission* 2001 document; the Lutheran-Catholic dialogues on Romans) reads Romans 9-11 in light of Romans 11:25-26 — "all Israel will be saved" — as ultimately a hope for Israel rather than a curse against it. The imprecation-citation in Rom 11:9-10 is qualified by the larger argumentative arc. The *honest-historical* position (Reinhartz, Boyarin, parts of post-supersessionist Christian scholarship) acknowledges that whatever Paul meant, the Christian use of the text has been catastrophic, and Christian theology must own that. The *retrieval-with-honesty* position (Levenson, Brueggemann engaging Levenson) reads the psalm in its Jewish particularity first, then engages the NT use without requiring it to be the one true reading.

The honest assessment: Christian Christological reading of Psalm 69 is neither optional nor unproblematic. It is canonically warranted (the NT cites the psalm) and historically dangerous (the cite has been weaponized). Reading well requires holding both.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 69 is not a comfortable psalm. Its rage is real, its longing is real, its later application to Christ on the cross is real, and the harm done by its weaponized use is also real. To read it honestly is to refuse the pious move of softening any of these. The psalm is broken-hearted — and a broken heart prays in registers we wish we did not need.

**Closing question:** When Christians pray the words "they gave me vinegar to drink" through Christ's cross, do they also pray the words "let their eyes be darkened" — and if not, on what grounds are some verses ours and others not?

## The Thread

The thread of Psalm 69 runs through John 2:17 (zeal for your house) and John 19:28-29 (the vinegar at the cross), through Romans 11:9-10 (Paul's use of the imprecation), and through Lamentations 3:14 (the parallel "byword to all my people" complaint of Jeremiah). Read these alongside the psalm and the through-line becomes clear: this is the prayer of a sufferer under reproach, prayed by David, prayed by Israel in exile, prayed by Christ on the cross, and prayed by the Church when it learns to suffer alongside its Lord.
