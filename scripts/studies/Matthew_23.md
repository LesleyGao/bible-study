# Matthew 23: The Seven Woes and Their Long Shadow

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Matthew 23 stages a public denunciation in the Temple courts, most plausibly the Court of the Gentiles where Jesus has been teaching since chapter 21. The audience is mixed — "the crowds and his disciples" (v. 1) — and the addressees are explicitly the scribes (γραμματεῖς) and Pharisees (Φαρισαῖοι). The historical-critical question that shadows every responsible reading: *which* scribes and Pharisees? Jesus's contemporaries in the 20s-30s CE, or the rabbinic-Pharisaic establishment that consolidated at **Yavneh (Jamnia)** after the 70 CE destruction, with whom Matthew's community was in painful intra-Jewish conflict at the time of the gospel's composition (most plausibly 80-90 CE)?

The Pharisees of Jesus's day were one Jewish group among several — Josephus (*Antiquities* 17.2.4) numbers them at "more than six thousand," a small but influential lay-piety movement. Their power was concentrated in synagogues and study circles, not in the Temple establishment (which the Sadducees controlled). After 70 CE, with Temple and Sadducean priesthood gone, the Pharisaic movement evolved into the rabbinic tradition that would produce the Mishnah (c. 200 CE) and ultimately the Talmuds. **Anthony Saldarini**'s *Matthew's Christian-Jewish Community* (1994) has shown this is decisive context: Matthew's polemic is intra-Jewish, between two groups (Christ-following Jews and proto-rabbinic Pharisaic Jews) competing to be the legitimate post-Temple Israel.

Several details ground the historical setting. **Moses' seat** (καθέδρα Μωϋσέως, v. 2) — a literal stone seat in synagogue assemblies. Archaeologists have recovered actual carved Moses seats at the **Chorazin synagogue** (4th c. CE basalt example, currently in Israel Museum) and from the **Hammath Tiberias** and **Delos** synagogues. The seat marked the position of authoritative teaching. **Phylacteries** (φυλακτήρια, v. 5; Hebrew *tefillin*) — the leather boxes containing Deut 6:4-9, 11:13-21, Exod 13:1-10, 13:11-16 worn on forehead and arm during prayer. **Tefillin from the Qumran caves** (1st c. BCE-CE) recovered from Cave 4 at Qumran show what Jesus would have seen, including the leather straps and miniature parchments. **Fringes** (κράσπεδα, v. 5; Hebrew *tzitzit*) — the tassels commanded in Numbers 15:38-39 and Deuteronomy 22:12, worn on the corners of the outer garment. Jesus himself wore them (cf. Matt 9:20; 14:36 — the woman with the hemorrhage touched his *kraspedon*).

**Whitewashed tombs** (τάφοι κεκονιαμένοι, v. 27) — a vivid Passover-pilgrimage detail. Jewish law (Num 19:16) imposed seven days of impurity on anyone touching a tomb. To prevent inadvertent pilgrim contact, tombs around Jerusalem were lime-washed each spring on the 15th of Adar (m. Sheqalim 1:1; m. Maaser Sheni 5:1). Excavated tomb complexes in the **Kidron Valley** and the **Mount of Olives** — including the Iron Age and Second Temple **Tomb of the Sons of Hezir**, the **Tomb of Zechariah**, the **Pillar of Absalom** — would have been visibly whitewashed during Passover. The metaphor's force depended on this seasonal ritual.

**Phylactery-broadening** and **fringe-lengthening** (v. 5) — these are not invented critiques. The Talmud itself (b. Sotah 22b) includes an internal Pharisaic critique of the "shoulder Pharisee" (who carries his good deeds on his shoulder for display) and the "make-good Pharisee" (who advertises piety). The ancient Pharisaic tradition was capable of intra-tradition self-critique; Jesus stands within rather than wholly outside this discourse.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter. ***Ouai*** (οὐαί, "woe!") — the prophetic-formula interjection that opens each judgment, echoing Isaiah 5:8-23 (six woes), Habakkuk 2:6-19 (five woes), and similar Hebrew prophetic invective. ***Hypokritēs*** (ὑποκριτής) — "hypocrite," from Greek theatre, originally "one who answers" or "actor"; Matthew uses the term twelve times, more than any other NT writer. ***Geenna*** (γέεννα, vv. 15, 33) — Gehenna, from Hebrew *gē-Hinnōm* (Valley of Hinnom), south of Jerusalem, associated with child sacrifice in 2 Kings 23:10 and Jeremiah 7:31, by Jesus's day a metaphor for final judgment. ***Diülízontes ton kōnōpa, tēn de kámēlon katapínontes*** (διϋλίζοντες τὸν κώνωπα, τὴν δὲ κάμηλον καταπίνοντες, v. 24) — "straining out the gnat, swallowing the camel" — possibly an Aramaic wordplay on *qamla* (gnat/louse) and *gamla* (camel), preserved in the Greek as a memorable rabbinic-style aphorism. ***Aphíetai hymin ho oikos hymōn érēmos*** (ἀφίεται ὑμῖν ὁ οἶκος ὑμῶν ἔρημος, v. 38) — "your house is left to you desolate" — the verb *aphíetai* in present passive ("is being left/abandoned") with prophetic force, echoing Jeremiah 22:5 and Ezekiel's vision of God's glory departing the Temple in Ezekiel 10-11.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Matthew 23 is the **longest sustained polemic** in the New Testament and one of the most carefully structured. Eight or nine units (the count depends on the textual question of v. 14) form a recognizable formal pattern. After the introduction (vv. 1-12) come the woes themselves: **seven** in the standard Nestle-Aland critical text, **eight** if v. 14 ("Woe to you... you devour widows' houses") is original. Verse 14 is absent from major early manuscripts (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Bezae) and is widely judged a scribal harmonization from Mark 12:40/Luke 20:47. The seven-woe count parallels the **seven blessings of the Beatitudes** in chapter 5, framing Matthew's gospel between two seven-fold pronouncements — one of blessing, one of woe.

The structure of the woes is deeply rabbinic. Each opens with the formula "Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites!" except the third (v. 16) which substitutes "Woe to you, blind guides!" The seven woes can be paired thematically: the first two (vv. 13, 15) concern obstructing access to the kingdom; the third and fourth (vv. 16-22, 23-24) concern misplaced priorities in legal observance; the fifth and sixth (vv. 25-26, 27-28) concern the inside/outside metaphor of inner corruption masked by outer purity; the seventh (vv. 29-32) concerns the building of prophets' tombs while perpetuating the killing of prophets.

The chapter then escalates from woes to **prophetic threat** (vv. 33-36) — "you serpents, brood of vipers" echoes John the Baptist's invective at 3:7 and Jesus's own at 12:34. The "blood from Abel to Zechariah" survey of martyred righteous (v. 35) is rabbinically precise: Abel is the first murder victim in Genesis, and **Zechariah son of Jehoiada** (2 Chronicles 24:20-22 — at the *end* of the Hebrew canon as ordered in the Tanakh) is the last righteous martyr. Hebrew Bible order, not Christian Old Testament order, is presupposed. The "son of Barachiah" identification is famously contested — this is **Zechariah the prophet** (the post-exilic figure of Zechariah 1:1, son of Berechiah), not the priestly Zechariah of 2 Chronicles 24. Davies/Allison and France treat this as a Matthean conflation, possibly intentional (combining the two Zechariahs into a single martyrological figure).

The chapter closes with the **lament over Jerusalem** (vv. 37-39) — distinctively Matthean (Luke's parallel at 13:34-35 places it earlier in Jesus's ministry; Matthew positions it as the climactic cap to the woes). The maternal-bird image — "as a hen gathers her brood under her wings" — echoes Deuteronomy 32:11 (the eagle stirring its nest, hovering over its young) and Ruth 2:12 (taking refuge under YHWH's wings) and Psalm 91:4. The image is unmistakably *feminine* divine imagery — God as mother bird — and one of the most striking such images in the synoptic Jesus tradition. Julian of Norwich's medieval mystical reading of Christ as Mother (*Showings*, ch. 60) draws explicitly on this verse.

**"Your house is left to you desolate"** (v. 38) — most modern commentators (Davies/Allison, Luz, France, Hagner) read "house" as the Temple, with the saying functioning as a *vaticinium ex eventu* statement reflecting the 70 CE destruction. The verb *aphíetai* (present passive) carries the connotation of divine abandonment — God's glory departing, as Ezekiel 10-11 had described in the lead-up to the Babylonian destruction.

**Textual variants** worth noting: v. 14 (the widows-houses woe) — likely scribal addition; v. 19 ("you blind men!") in some witnesses simply "you blind"; the Zechariah son of Barachiah identification differs across some patristic citations.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Matthew 23 is the most reception-historically charged chapter in the gospel. **Ulrich Luz**'s *Hermeneia* commentary devotes more than a hundred pages to its *Wirkungsgeschichte* — the history of its effects. The honest reading requires holding three things at once: what the text says, what its first hearers would have heard, and what two thousand years of Christian use has done with it.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** **Anthony Saldarini**'s *Matthew's Christian-Jewish Community* (1994) is foundational. Saldarini reads Matthew 23 as the polemic of a Jewish-Christian sect (Matthew's community) against the proto-rabbinic Pharisaic movement that was consolidating at Yavneh and beginning to define Jewish identity in ways that excluded Christ-following Jews. The polemic is intra-Jewish — not Christianity against Judaism but one Jewish movement against another, in painful family conflict. Both groups were claiming legitimate continuation of Israel after the Temple's destruction.

**Daniel Boyarin** (*Border Lines: The Partition of Judaeo-Christianity*, 2004) extends Saldarini's argument: Matthew 23 was written before "Christianity" and "Judaism" had become separable categories. Only retroactively, after the Christian-Jewish "partition" hardened in the second through fourth centuries, did the chapter come to read as one religion attacking another. **Krister Stendahl**'s *The School of St. Matthew* (1954) earlier established the picture of Matthew's community as a "school" engaged in scribal interpretation — itself a Jewish phenomenon.

**Amy-Jill Levine** (*The Misunderstood Jew*, 2006; *Jewish Annotated New Testament*) is the indispensable contemporary voice. Levine insists that Matthew 23 must be read with full awareness of how Christian preaching has used it: to caricature Jews as legalistic, hypocritical, externally pious but internally corrupt. She argues that this caricature has functioned for two millennia as the theological infrastructure for Christian anti-Judaism. The Pharisees of Matthew 23 are not "the Jews"; they are specific first-century leaders, and even within first-century Pharisaism the movement was internally diverse and capable of self-critique. To read Matthew 23 today as a description of "Jewish religion" is, on Levine's account, to misread both the text and its history.

**Pinchas Lapide** and **Geza Vermes** have shown that much of what Jesus says here would have resonated within first-century Pharisaic self-critique. The Talmud's typology of seven kinds of Pharisees (b. Sotah 22b) — including the "shoulder Pharisee" who advertises piety and the "blood-letting Pharisee" who closes his eyes to avoid looking at women — is a recognizable Pharisaic *internal* tradition of polemic against showy religion. Jesus speaks within rather than wholly outside the Pharisaic moral imagination.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** The reception is more troubling. **Patristic** readings of Matthew 23 frequently extended the woes from specific first-century leaders to "the Jews" generally. **Chrysostom**'s *Eight Homilies Against the Jews* (386-387 CE), preached in Antioch, draw heavily on Matthew 23 — and stand as one of the most rhetorically violent anti-Jewish texts in the patristic corpus. The Roman Catholic Church has formally repudiated their use. **Augustine**'s teaching in *Adversus Judaeos* and *City of God* is more measured but participates in the trajectory. **Aquinas**'s *Catena Aurea* compiles the patristic chorus.

The **Reformation** intensifies the polemic. **Luther**'s 1543 *On the Jews and Their Lies* is a notorious example, drawing on Matthew 23 to justify expulsion and persecution. The Lutheran World Federation, in its 1984 statement, formally rejected this strand of Luther's writings and apologized for their use.

The **post-Holocaust correction** has been the most significant theological development. **Vatican II's *Nostra Aetate*** (1965) explicitly rejected the deicide charge and reaffirmed Romans 11's teaching that God's covenant with Israel is irrevocable. The **Pontifical Biblical Commission's 2001 *The Jewish People and Their Sacred Scriptures in the Christian Bible*** went further, providing detailed exegetical guidance for reading polemical NT texts (including Matthew 23) without anti-Jewish application. **Rosemary Radford Ruether**'s *Faith and Fratricide* (1974), **Mary C. Boys**'s *Has God Only One Blessing?* (2000), and **John Pawlikowski**'s decades of work on Christian-Jewish relations have built the constructive theological infrastructure for responsible reading. The **PCBC 2015** document *The Gifts and the Calling of God Are Irrevocable* (commemorating the 50th anniversary of *Nostra Aetate*) further deepened the Catholic position.

Among modern commentaries: **Davies/Allison** (ICC) provide exhaustive historical-critical analysis with full attention to the chapter's polemical edge. **Ulrich Luz** (*Hermeneia*) gives the most sustained reception-history engagement, explicitly tracing the chapter's use in Christian anti-Judaism. **R. T. France** (NICNT) and **Donald Hagner** (WBC) are clear that the polemic targets specific first-century leaders, not Jews as such. **Daniel Harrington** (Sacra Pagina), writing as a Catholic Jesuit, brings *Nostra Aetate* sensibilities directly into the commentary tradition. **John Nolland** (NIGTC) and **Craig Keener** (NICNT) emphasize the intra-Jewish character of the polemic.

**N. T. Wright** (*Jesus and the Victory of God*) reads Matthew 23 within his broader narrative of Jesus as Israel's true prophet announcing judgment on a corrupt Temple establishment. The lament over Jerusalem (vv. 37-39) is not Christianity rejecting Judaism but the prophet Jesus weeping over the city he loves and longs to gather.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: How should the church read polemic in scripture given the chapter's documented role in authorizing anti-Jewish violence?** Three positions deserve steelmanning. **(1) Plain-sense reading** — the woes are Jesus's authoritative pronouncement of judgment on those who claim spiritual authority while obstructing it. The text's polemic is divinely sanctioned and must be preached as such, while its targets are understood historically (specific first-century leaders) rather than ethnically (Jews as a people). This is the position of most evangelical commentary today (Carson, Keener, France) and most magisterial Catholic teaching since *Nostra Aetate*. **(2) Hermeneutics-of-suspicion reading** — given the documented history of Christian use of Matthew 23 as authorization for Jew-hatred, the chapter must be read with explicit awareness of and resistance to its weaponization. This is the position developed by Levine, Boys, Pawlikowski, and the broader post-Holocaust corrective tradition; it is also the position of *Nostra Aetate* and PCBC 2001 in their explicit guidance for Christian preaching. **(3) Canon-critical reading** — the chapter's polemic must be balanced against Romans 9-11's teaching of Israel's irrevocable election; reading Matthew 23 in isolation distorts the canon's larger witness. **Where this leaves us:** these positions are not mutually exclusive but successive lenses. The honest preacher today reads the chapter (1) plainly as divine judgment on hypocritical religious leadership *of any era including the present church*, (2) suspiciously aware of how the chapter has been misused, and (3) canonically alongside the witness of Paul on Israel's enduring covenant.

**Tension 2: What is the relationship between v. 36 ("all these things will come upon this generation") and vv. 38-39 ("your house is left to you desolate... you will not see me again until you say, 'Blessed is he who comes...'")**? Three positions. **(1) Definitive-judgment reading** — the desolation is final; the Temple destruction in 70 CE confirms God's judgment; the closing citation of Psalm 118:26 functions as a future eschatological condition that may never be met. This is closer to the supersessionist tradition. **(2) Conditional-restoration reading** (Wright, France, broadly preterist) — the desolation is real but not final; v. 39 holds open the possibility of Jewish recognition of Messiah at the eschaton, echoing Romans 11:25-26 ("all Israel will be saved"). The "until" of v. 39 carries genuine future force. **(3) Already-not-yet reading** — the desolation describes both the 70 CE destruction (already) and an ongoing condition that awaits eschatological reversal (not yet); the church reads v. 39 as anticipating Israel's recognition of Christ, while granting that this recognition lies beyond present polemic. **Where this leaves us:** the post-Holocaust church increasingly reads v. 39 as a hope rather than a closed door. *Nostra Aetate*'s rejection of the deicide charge and PCBC 2001's affirmation of Israel's enduring election require reading the verse against any interpretation that consigns the Jewish people to final divine rejection. Whether v. 39 itself supports or merely accommodates this reading is contested, but the theological context now demands it.

**Unresolved question:** Can a Christian sermon today preach Matthew 23 as Jesus preached it — directly, polemically, by name — without participating in the chapter's documented anti-Jewish reception history? Or does the reception history now permanently constrain how the chapter can be preached? The Catholic and mainline Protestant answer has tended toward the latter; the evangelical answer has tended toward the former, with cautions; neither answer is wholly satisfying.

## The Thread

Matthew 23 weaves Isaiah 5's six-woe oracle, Habakkuk 2's five-woe oracle, Deuteronomy 32:11's eagle-and-young image, Ruth 2:12's wings-of-refuge image, Psalm 91:4's protective wings, Genesis 4 (Abel) through 2 Chronicles 24 (Zechariah) — a survey of the entire Hebrew Bible's testimony to martyred righteous. The "blood of righteous Abel" connects to Hebrews 12:24 (Christ's blood "that speaks better than the blood of Abel"). The hen-and-chicks lament reverberates forward to Revelation 12:14 (the woman given eagle's wings) and backward to Exodus 19:4 (God carrying Israel on eagle's wings out of Egypt). Forward in Matthew, "your house is left to you desolate" sets up 24:1-2 immediately following — the disciples emerging from the Temple to admire its stones, Jesus replying that not one stone will remain upon another. Flag this: the lament-prophecy of 23:37-39 transitions directly into the Olivet Discourse of chapters 24-25, with the Temple's coming destruction as the throughline. Also flag: the "blood on us and on our children" cry at 27:25 — the verse that Levine, Boys, and *Nostra Aetate* engage as the most weaponized passage in Christian anti-Jewish history — is set up rhetorically by the bloodguilt language of 23:35.
