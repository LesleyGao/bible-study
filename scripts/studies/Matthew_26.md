# Matthew 26: The Anointing, the Cup, and the Fall of Peter

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Matthew 26 unfolds across roughly thirty-six hours in late Nisan, around 30 or 33 CE, in and around Jerusalem at the height of Passover pilgrimage. Josephus estimates the city's population swelled from perhaps 80,000 residents to several hundred thousand during the festival (*Jewish War* 6.420-427 — his numbers are inflated but the order of magnitude stands). Pontius Pilate, prefect of Judaea from 26-36 CE, would have descended from Caesarea Maritima with auxiliary troops to maintain order. The high priest who hosts the plot in 26:3 is named: Joseph, called Caiaphas, who served unusually long (18-36 CE) under four prefects, suggesting close cooperation with Roman authority. The 1990 discovery of an ornate first-century ossuary in Jerusalem's Peace Forest, inscribed *Yehosef bar Qayafa* (Joseph son of Caiaphas), is widely accepted by archaeologists Zvi Greenhut, Ronny Reich, and David Flusser as belonging to the high-priestly family of the Gospels — perhaps to Caiaphas himself — and the bones inside (a man around sixty among others) lend a startling tangibility to the figure who sat in judgment of Jesus.

The Passover meal of 26:17-30 is staged in an ἀνάγαιον (*anagaion*, upper room — Mark 14:15), a feature of the wealthier houses on the western hill, what later tradition would call Mount Zion. Lithic remnants of such houses, with their stone vessels (Jewish purity concerns made stone preferred over pottery, since stone was impervious to ritual impurity per *m. Kelim* 10:1), have been excavated in the Herodian Quarter by Nahman Avigad. The Gethsemane scene (26:36-46) takes place in a κῆπος (*kēpos*, garden) on the Mount of Olives, across the Kidron Valley — most likely an olive grove with a press (the name גַּת שְׁמָנִים, *Gat Shemanim*, means "oil press"). A grove of ancient olives still stands at the traditional site adjacent to the Church of All Nations; carbon-14 dating on three of them by Italian botanists in 2012 returned dates between 1092 and 1166 CE — these particular trees are medieval, but olives regenerate from rootstock, and the genetic uniformity Petruccelli's team observed suggests a single ancient parent.

Three Greek terms in this chapter carry historical and theological weight beyond translation. **Πάσχα** (*Pascha*, "Passover," 26:2) is itself a Greek transliteration of Aramaic *pascha* and Hebrew *pesach* — the meal commemorating Exodus deliverance, with its own ritual choreography of unleavened bread, bitter herbs, four cups of wine, and *Hallel* psalms (Ps 113-118, the very psalms Jesus and the disciples likely sang on the way to Gethsemane in 26:30). **διαθήκη** (*diathēkē*, "covenant," 26:28) carries the freight of Sinai (Exod 24:8 — "the blood of the covenant") and Jeremiah's "new covenant" (Jer 31:31). And **εἰς ἄφεσιν ἁμαρτιῶν** (*eis aphesin hamartiōn*, "for the forgiveness of sins," 26:28) is a Matthean addition not found in Mark 14:24 or Luke 22:20 — a deliberate redactional move binding Eucharistic blood to the program announced at Jesus's naming in 1:21 ("he will save his people from their sins").

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Matthew is following Mark 14 with characteristic fidelity but shapes the material with characteristic distinctives. The chronological frame ("after two days the Passover is coming," 26:2) is sharpened from Mark's "it was two days before"; the chief priests gather "in the palace of the high priest, whose name was Caiaphas" — Mark omits the name. Matthew tightens the plot in 26:1-5 around the deliberate conspiracy of named adversaries. Then comes the anointing at Bethany (26:6-13), where an unnamed woman pours costly ointment over Jesus's head. Matthew, like Mark, refuses to identify her; Luke's parallel (7:36-50) describes a different scene, a "sinful woman" anointing Jesus's feet earlier in the ministry, while John 12:1-8 names her Mary of Bethany, sister of Lazarus and Martha. Davies and Allison treat this as a redactional and traditional cluster: probably two distinct anointings remembered in overlapping streams, with John's tradition naming Mary and supplying the Lazarus context. Matthew's commendation is extraordinary: "wherever this gospel is preached in the whole world, what she has done will be told in memory of her" (26:13). Elisabeth Schüssler Fiorenza built her foundational feminist hermeneutic *In Memory of Her* (1983) on the irony that this woman, whose act Jesus declares the gospel's permanent companion, has lost her name in the telling — Fiorenza reads the silence as evidence that early ecclesial transmission systematically suppressed women's apostolic agency even as it preserved Jesus's commendation of it.

The Judas pericope (26:14-16) and its closing detail — **τριάκοντα ἀργύρια** (*triakonta argyria*, thirty pieces of silver) — is uniquely sharpened by Matthew. Mark records simply that the priests "promised to give him money." Matthew names the price, almost certainly invoking Zechariah 11:12-13, where the rejected shepherd's wage is thirty pieces thrown to the potter in the house of the Lord. The figure also echoes Exodus 21:32, the price of a slave gored by an ox. France notes that Matthew's redaction is christological: the rejected Messiah is priced as a slave, and the prophetic shape of Zechariah hangs over the whole sequence.

The Last Supper (26:17-30) presents Matthew's signature redactional move. Where Mark 14:24 has simply "this is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many," Matthew adds **εἰς ἄφεσιν ἁμαρτιῶν** — "for the forgiveness of sins." This is not decoration. It is a structural keystone. In 1:21 the angel commands Joseph to name the child Jesus "for he will save his people from their sins." In 3:6 John baptizes "for the confession of sins" — but neither John's baptism nor Jesus's preaching of the kingdom has yet effected what 1:21 promised. Now, at the Supper, the program is fulfilled: the cup is the means by which the saving from sins announced at the beginning is finally accomplished. Joachim Jeremias, in his foundational *The Eucharistic Words of Jesus* (English ed. 1966), argued that the words of institution preserve a pre-Pauline Aramaic substratum and that Jesus understood his death as the eschatological Passover sacrifice inaugurating the new covenant — Matthew makes this Jeremianic interpretation explicit.

Gethsemane (26:36-46) is then the human face of that eucharistic resolution. Matthew, more than Mark, foregrounds Jesus's struggle: "began to be sorrowful and troubled... my soul is very sorrowful, even to death" (26:37-38). The petition is threefold; the prayer's second iteration (26:42) tightens the language: "if this cannot pass unless I drink it, your will be done." The threefold sleep of the disciples is set in deliberate contrast. The arrest (26:47-56) carries Matthew's distinctive insertion that "all who take the sword will perish by the sword" (26:52) — absent from Mark — a saying that shaped John Howard Yoder's *The Politics of Jesus* and the broader Anabaptist nonresistance tradition.

The Sanhedrin trial (26:57-68) climaxes in Jesus's most explicit Christological self-disclosure: "from now on you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming on the clouds of heaven" (26:64). The fusion of Psalm 110:1 (enthronement at God's right hand) with Daniel 7:13 (the Son of Man coming on clouds) is, as Davies and Allison and France both note, a stunning compression of Israel's eschatological grammar. Caiaphas's tearing of his garments is the canonically prescribed response to blasphemy heard (*m. Sanhedrin* 7:5). Peter's denials (26:69-75) bracket the trial inside its courtyard, and the crowing rooster — whether the literal bird or the gallicinium, the Roman trumpet sounded at the third watch (Hagner) — marks the Petrine collapse that the resurrection narrative will need to undo.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Read along the canonical arc, Matthew 26 is the hinge where the Mosaic Passover of Exodus 12 becomes the eucharistic Passover of the church, and where the Suffering Servant of Isaiah 53 — "poured out his soul to death" — is enacted at table before being enacted at the cross. The cup that Jesus prays might pass (26:39) recalls the prophetic cup of wrath in Isaiah 51:17-23, Jeremiah 25:15-29, and Ezekiel 23:31-34: the cup Israel had drunk in exile, which the Son of Man now drinks in Israel's place.

Jewish interpreters approach this chapter with the cautions earned by reception history. Amy-Jill Levine (*The Misunderstood Jew*, 2006; *Jewish Annotated New Testament*, 2nd ed. 2017) reads the Sanhedrin trial against the legal procedures of *m. Sanhedrin* and finds the Matthean account historically problematic — capital trials at night, on a feast, with capital judgment rendered in a single sitting, all violate the Mishnaic procedural norms. Levine argues, with E. P. Sanders (*Jesus and Judaism*, 1985) and Geza Vermes (*Jesus the Jew*, 1973), that the historical event was likelier a swift Sadducean priestly hearing oriented toward delivering Jesus to Pilate on a charge Rome would prosecute — sedition, not blasphemy — and that Matthew's narrative shape carries the polemical residue of his community's later conflict with the synagogue. Daniel Boyarin (*The Jewish Gospels*, 2012) goes further, arguing that the Son of Man Christology of 26:64 is itself within the range of late Second Temple Jewish messianic expectation drawn from Daniel 7 and the Parables of Enoch — Jesus's claim is not the introduction of a foreign deity but the activation of an already-extant Jewish messianic possibility.

Christian interpretation of this chapter has been shaped, layer upon layer, by the patristic and medieval engagement with the Eucharist and Gethsemane. Origen reads the cup of 26:39 typologically as the cup of suffering Christ shares with the martyrs (*Exhortation to Martyrdom*). John Chrysostom's *Homilies on Matthew* 80-83 dwells on the human anguish of Gethsemane as proof against docetism — the Son of God really did sweat, really did fear, really did pray — and on the Eucharist as the bond of the church. Augustine's *De Consensu Evangelistarum* harmonizes the anointing accounts (he treats the synoptic and Johannine anointings as distinct events). Aquinas's *Catena Aurea* compiles the patristic glosses without forcing them into systematic synthesis. The Reformation pivots to the Supper. Calvin's *Harmony of the Evangelists* and *Institutes* 4.17 articulates a spiritual presence: Christ's body remains in heaven, but the Spirit unites the believer to that body in the communion. Luther's *Small Catechism* and the *Marburg Colloquy* (1529) defend a real bodily presence "in, with, and under" the bread. The Catholic tradition, via Trent (1551) and *CCC* 1373-1377, holds substantial transubstantiation; the Anabaptists, with Zwingli, hold memorial. These are not merely doctrinal differences but readings of Matthew 26:26-28 — what does **τοῦτό ἐστιν** (*touto estin*, "this is") mean?

Twentieth-century theology has read Gethsemane as the heart of Christology. Dietrich Bonhoeffer's *Cost of Discipleship* and his *Letters and Papers from Prison* return repeatedly to Gethsemane: the disciples sleep while Jesus prays, and Bonhoeffer reads this as the constitutive moment of the church — Christians are those called to "watch with Christ in his hour of agony in the world." Karl Barth, in *Church Dogmatics* IV/1 §59, treats Gethsemane as the moment where the Son's perfect obedience is tested and confirmed, the place where "thy will be done" becomes the determining motion of the divine economy. Hans Urs von Balthasar's *Mysterium Paschale* (1969) makes the cup of Gethsemane the inner counterpart of the cross — the will surrendered before the body is broken. Jürgen Moltmann's *The Crucified God* (1972), though weighted toward Mark 15:34, reads Gethsemane as the trinitarian event in which the Son anticipates the abandonment of Calvary and the Father bears the agony of his Son's surrender.

Among critical commentaries, Davies and Allison's *ICC* volume 3 (1997) is foundational — every redactional and intertextual move is mapped against Mark, against the LXX, against the Pseudepigrapha. Ulrich Luz's *Hermeneia* volume 4 carries the reception-historical project for which his commentary is famous. R. T. France's *NICNT* (2007) and Craig Keener's *NICNT* (1999) supply readable evangelical synthesis. Donald Hagner's *WBC*, John Nolland's *NIGTC*, and Daniel Harrington's *Sacra Pagina* round out the technical roster. For the Passion narrative as a whole, Raymond Brown's *The Death of the Messiah* (2 vols, 1994) remains foundational — exhaustively detailed on every textual, historical, and theological question the chapter raises. John Dominic Crossan's *Who Killed Jesus?* (1995) supplies a counter-reading insisting the historical events have been heavily reshaped by prophetic-typological midrash; Joel Green's *The Death of Jesus* mediates between Brown and Crossan with a narrative-theological synthesis.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The Eucharistic words — what is happening at the table?** Matthew has Jesus say of the bread, "this is my body," and of the cup, "this is my blood of the covenant, poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins." Four positions have hardened over the church's history. *Roman Catholic* (Trent 1551, *CCC* 1373-1377) holds that the substance of bread and wine becomes the substance of Christ's body and blood while accidents (color, taste, shape) remain — transubstantiation. *Lutheran* (Luther's *Small Catechism*, the *Formula of Concord*) holds sacramental union: the body and blood are present "in, with, and under" the elements without conversion of substance. *Reformed* (Calvin's *Institutes* 4.17) holds spiritual real presence: Christ's body is in heaven, but the Spirit lifts the believer to feed on Christ in the communion. *Anabaptist/memorialist* (Zwingli, the *Schleitheim Confession*) holds the Supper as a memorial sign of an already-finished redemption. Each position can steelman itself from this text. The Catholic emphasizes the realism of **ἐστιν** ("is") and the priestly-sacrificial vocabulary of "blood... poured out for many" (echoing Isa 53:12). The Lutheran emphasizes the simplicity of Christ's words against scholastic refinement. The Reformed emphasizes the Holy Spirit's pneumatological work and the heavenly session of Christ's body. The memorialist emphasizes "do this in remembrance of me" (Luke 22:19; absent in Matthew but contextually presumed) and the New Testament's hermeneutical use of figural language. **Where this leaves us:** Matthew 26:26-28 itself underdetermines the dispute. What Matthew himself is doing — adding "for the forgiveness of sins" — is binding the Supper to his Christology and atonement program (back to 1:21), not adjudicating Aristotelian metaphysics. The fight is real but the text predates the categories the fight requires.

**Tension 2: Gethsemane and the will of the Son — does Jesus pray to escape what he has come to do?** "My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will" (26:39). Three classical positions structure the debate. *Two-wills Christology* (the Sixth Ecumenical Council, Constantinople III, 681 CE, against monotheletism, drawing on Maximus the Confessor's *Disputation with Pyrrhus*): Christ has both a divine will and a human will, perfectly united but distinct, and Gethsemane displays the human will's natural recoil from death subjected and conformed to the divine will. *Dyothelite kenoticist* (some 19th-c. Lutherans, Charles Gore, P. T. Forsyth): the incarnate Son has voluntarily emptied himself of certain divine prerogatives (Phil 2:7), so the prayer is genuine human petition with genuine ignorance of outcome until resolved in surrender. *Barthian-actualist* (Barth, *CD* IV/1 §59): Gethsemane is the event in which the eternal decision of the Son to be for us is enacted in time, the moment where "thy will be done" is the human-divine word that re-establishes the covenant. Bonhoeffer adds a fourth reading from *Letters and Papers*: Gethsemane is where Christ enters the suffering of the world to such depth that he becomes weak and powerless, and only the suffering God can help. **Where this leaves us:** the church has insisted, against monothelitism, that the fear of the cup is real, the will to escape is real, and yet the surrender is also real. To deny Gethsemane's anguish is to deny Christ's humanity; to deny the surrender is to misread the petition. **Unresolved:** does the Son's "if it be possible" mean that, in some sense, an alternative was open? Theologians have disagreed for sixteen centuries; Matthew preserves the question without answering it.

## The Thread

Matthew 26 closes the loop on the program announced in chapter 1: the child named **Ἰησοῦς** because "he will save his people from their sins" (1:21) now pours out his blood "for the forgiveness of sins" (26:28). The cup of Gethsemane recalls the prophetic cup of wrath in Isaiah 51:17 and Jeremiah 25:15, drunk now by the Son of Man in Israel's place. The Son of Man enthroned at God's right hand and coming on clouds (26:64) takes its grammar from Psalm 110:1 and Daniel 7:13, the two texts that sustain the Christology of Hebrews 1:3 and Revelation 1:7. Peter's denial here will be undone in 28:7's word to "tell my disciples and Peter" — Mark's Galilee tradition that Matthew preserves — and finally in John 21's threefold restoration. Watch in chapter 27 for Matthew's redactional intensification: the Judas-Field of Blood narrative (uniquely Matthean), Pilate's wife's dream, the hand-washing, and the verse — 27:25 — that has done more damage in Christian-Jewish history than any other single sentence in Scripture.
