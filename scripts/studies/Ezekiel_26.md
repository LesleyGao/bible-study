# Ezekiel 26: Tyre, the Bare Rock, and the Problem of Partial Fulfillment

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political situation**: **Dated explicitly to the eleventh year, on the first day of the month** (26:1) — the Hebrew text omits which month, and the dating has been one of the most disputed in Ezekiel. **Greenberg (Anchor)** and **Block (NICOT)** read the eleventh year as 587/586 BC, the year of Jerusalem's fall, with the oracle following hard upon the city's destruction. The **omission of the month** is treated by Greenberg as ancient textual loss; some commentators conjecturally restore *the eleventh month* (Cooke ICC). Either way, the chapter is the first of four oracles against Tyre running through chapters 26, 27, and 28 (with 28:1–10 and 28:11–19 being the two distinct oracles to the prince and the king of Tyre respectively). **Tyre** in 586 BC was an island-fortress city about half a kilometer offshore from the mainland, ruled by **Ittobaal III / Ethbaal III** (c. 591–573 BC, attested in Josephus *Against Apion* 1.21 and the **Tyrian king-list** preserved in Menander of Ephesus quoted by Josephus). Tyre had been the dominant Phoenician maritime power for several centuries, controlling the western Mediterranean trading network through its colonies — most famously **Carthage** (founded c. 814 BC according to traditional dating, archaeologically confirmed to mid-9th c. BC). Tyre's offense in 26:2 is identical in structure to the offenses of the four neighbors of chapter 25: *Aha, the gate of the peoples is broken; it has swung open to me. I shall be replenished, now that she is laid waste*. Tyre rejoiced at Jerusalem's fall because **Jerusalem was a competing trade-axis** controlling the King's Highway and the inland routes; with Jerusalem destroyed, Tyre expected commercial advantage.

**Material culture**: Phoenician-Tyrian archaeology is rich and partially excavated. **Patricia Bikai's *The Pottery of Tyre*** (1978) established the ceramic chronology that anchors all subsequent Phoenician archaeology. **Maria Eugenia Aubet's *The Phoenicians and the West*** (3rd ed. 2001) is the comprehensive synthesis of Phoenician material culture and trade-network archaeology. The **Tell el-Burak excavations** (Phoenician settlement just south of Sidon, ongoing since 2001) and the **Sidon American University excavations** have produced extensive material for the Tyrian-Sidonian sphere. The **Phoenician-Punic inscriptions** (KAI 1–73 in the standard collection of Donner and Röllig) supply linguistic and cultic detail. The **Tyrian temple of Melqart** is attested by the **Pyrgi tablets** (gold tablets from Etruria, 5th c. BC, in Phoenician and Etruscan, dedicating a temple to Astarte-Uni) and the **Cádiz Melqart-temple tradition** in Spain. **The Eshmunazar sarcophagus inscription** (from Sidon, c. 480 BC) gives Phoenician royal burial conventions. The **Cape Gelidonya wreck** (c. 1200 BC, off southwestern Turkey) and the **Uluburun wreck** (c. 1300 BC, off southwestern Turkey) were both excavated by **George Bass and the Institute of Nautical Archaeology**, and yielded Phoenician-style cargo (copper, tin, glass, amber, hippopotamus ivory) confirming the Late-Bronze-Age maritime infrastructure that Tyre inherited and expanded.

**Authorship & dating**: Ezekiel ben Buzi, exilic priest. **Greenberg (Anchor)**, **Block (NICOT)**, and **Zimmerli (Hermeneia)** all treat chapter 26 as Ezekielian, dated 587/586 BC. The chapter's literary unity is generally accepted. **Cooke (ICC 1936)** identified the prose conclusion (26:19–21) as a separate oracle but the four-oracle Tyre cycle (26, 27, 28:1–10, 28:11–19) is structurally coherent. **Zimmerli** identifies the *bare rock* (Hebrew *ṣeḥiaḥ sela'*, צְחִיחַ סֶלַע, 26:4, 14) as the chapter's central image and traces its repetition across the unit. The **partial-fulfillment problem** raised in 29:17–20 (where Nebuchadnezzar is *given Egypt* as compensation because *neither he nor his army got anything from Tyre*) confirms an internal Ezekielian dating — the partial-fulfillment oracle is dated to *the twenty-seventh year* (29:17 = 571 BC), implying that chapters 26–28 were composed before, and chapter 29:17–20 added later as a redactional response.

**Archaeological & textual evidence**: **The Babylonian Chronicle** records Nebuchadnezzar's siege of Tyre as lasting **thirteen years** (586–573 BC), making it the longest siege in ancient Near Eastern record. The **Tyrian king-list** preserved by Menander of Ephesus (in Josephus *Against Apion* 1.21) confirms the duration. The siege ended in **negotiated submission** rather than conquest: Tyre acknowledged Babylonian suzerainty but the island fortress was not breached, and Nebuchadnezzar withdrew without sacking the city. Modern archaeology (Bikai 1978; Aubet 2001) confirms **substantial urban continuity** at Tyre through the late 6th c. BC. The fuller destruction came under **Alexander the Great in 332 BC**, whose seven-month siege included building a stone-and-rubble **causeway** from the mainland to the island — using the *rubble of mainland Tyre* — and his subsequent storming of the island, killing 8,000 defenders and selling 30,000 into slavery. The causeway is still extant; modern Tyre is now connected to the mainland by the silted-up remnant of Alexander's mole. The classical sources for Alexander's siege are **Quintus Curtius Rufus *History of Alexander* 4.2–4** and **Arrian's *Anabasis* 2.16–24**, with additional detail in **Diodorus Siculus 17.40–46** and **Plutarch's *Life of Alexander*** 24–25.

**Key terms**: *ṣeḥiaḥ sela'* (צְחִיחַ סֶלַע, "bare rock," 26:4, 14 — the chapter's central prediction-image); *mishtaḥ ḥaramim* (מִשְׁטַח חֲרָמִים, "place for spreading nets," 26:5, 14 — the chapter's most concrete physical image); *sho'er gam* (שׁוֹעֵר גַּם, the *gate of the peoples*, 26:2 — Tyre's commercial-rival language for Jerusalem); *Nebuchadrezzar melekh-Bavel melekh melakhim* (נְבוּכַדְרֶאצַּר מֶלֶךְ-בָּבֶל מֶלֶךְ מְלָכִים, "Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon, king of kings," 26:7 — the only OT use of the *king of kings* title for a foreign sovereign, the same Akkadian *šar šarrāni* later applied to Persian, Parthian, and ultimately Christological referents); *yorde bor* (יוֹרְדֵי בוֹר, "those who go down to the pit," 26:20 — the *Sheol* descent vocabulary).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: **prophetic oracle of judgment**, structurally tripartite. The chapter has three movements: (a) the announcement (26:1–6), (b) the Nebuchadnezzar oracle (26:7–14), and (c) the lamentation by the maritime nations (26:15–21).

The literary architecture:

- A. Date-formula and announcement (26:1–6)
  - A1. Tyre's *Aha!* over Jerusalem (26:2)
  - A2. Many nations like waves (26:3)
  - A3. The bare rock prediction (26:4–5)
  - A4. Daughters on the mainland killed (26:6)
- B. Nebuchadnezzar oracle (26:7–14)
  - B1. King of kings comes from the north (26:7)
  - B2. Siege wall, mound, roof of shields (26:8–9)
  - B3. Horses, dust, breached gates (26:10–11)
  - B4. Plunder, walls thrown into the sea (26:12)
  - B5. Music silenced, bare rock again (26:13–14)
- C. Lamentation by maritime nations (26:15–21)
  - C1. Coastlands shake (26:15)
  - C2. Princes of the sea step down from thrones (26:16–17)
  - C3. Coastlands tremble (26:18)
  - C4. Descent into the pit (26:19–21)

The *bare rock* prediction (Hebrew *ṣeḥiaḥ sela'*) bookends the chapter's first two sections (26:4 and 26:14), creating a poetic inclusio. The image is concrete and architectural: Tyre, scraped of its soil, becomes a *flat exposed rock* used by fishermen for *spreading their nets to dry*. The image is so specific that **Greenberg (Anchor)** treats it as the chapter's interpretive key — the prediction is not generic destruction but *this specific topography*: an island-rock cleared of buildings, used by surviving Mediterranean fishermen.

The **Nebuchadnezzar oracle** (26:7–14) is filled with **siegecraft vocabulary**: *siege wall* (Hebrew *dayeq*, דָּיֵק), *mound* (*soleloh*, סֹלְלָה), *roof of shields* (*ṣinnah*, צִנָּה), *battering rams* (*qoboleh*, קֹבְלוֹ), *horses* (*sus*), *wagons* (*galgal*), *chariots* (*rekhev*). The vocabulary is technically accurate to Neo-Babylonian siege practice as known from the **Lachish reliefs** (Sennacherib's palace at Nineveh, depicting the 701 BC siege of Lachish with siege ramps, battering rams, and shield-walls — the visual standard for ANE siegecraft), and from Babylonian texts such as the **Nineveh siege records** and the **Esarhaddon prism**.

The **lamentation by maritime nations** (26:15–21) shifts genre to *qina* (lament). The princes of the sea *step down from their thrones*, *remove their robes*, *strip off their embroidered garments*, *clothe themselves with trembling*, *sit on the ground*. The mourning gestures are exact: removing royal regalia, sitting in the dust, audible lament. The same mourning sequence appears in **Job 2:8–13** (Job sitting on the ash-heap), in **Lamentations 2:10** (the elders of Zion sit on the ground in silence), and in **Jonah 3:6** (the king of Nineveh removes his royal robe and sits in ashes). It is the standard ANE royal-mourning sequence.

The **descent into the pit** (26:19–21) is the **Sheol-imagery** that recurs in Ezekiel 32:17–32 (the great Sheol-roster of fallen nations: Asshur, Elam, Meshech-Tubal, Edom, the princes of the north, Sidon, Egypt). The vocabulary — *those who go down to the pit*, *the people of old*, *the world below*, *ruins from of old* — is the Hebrew underworld-language, paralleling the Mesopotamian *Erṣetu* (the *Land of No Return* of the **Descent of Ishtar** and **Gilgamesh tablet 7**).

**MT/LXX variants**: 26:7 — MT and LXX agree on *Nebuchadrezzar*. The *king of kings* title (*melekh melakhim*, מֶלֶךְ מְלָכִים) is preserved in both. 26:20 — MT has *I will set beauty in the land of the living*, but the LXX reads *I will not give beauty in the land of the living*, which **Cooke (ICC)** prefers as the harder reading. **Greenberg (Anchor)** retains MT and reads *beauty* as the restored Israel set in the land in contrast to Tyre's descent. The textual question is theologically minor.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Place in the canonical arc**: Ezekiel 26 inaugurates the **Tyre cycle** (chs. 26–28), the most extended OAN unit in the book. The Tyre material is structurally parallel to the **Egypt cycle** (chs. 29–32), and together the two cycles dominate the OAN block. The placement of Tyre between the four-neighbors oracles of chapter 25 and the Egypt oracles of 29–32 is geographically coherent: northwest neighbor (Tyre), then southwest neighbor (Egypt). Tyre's commercial dominance and Egypt's political dominance are the two great non-Mesopotamian power-centers of Ezekiel's world; both are judged.

**Jewish trajectory**: **Sirach 49:8** singles out Ezekiel's vision; the OAN material is preserved without elaboration. **Targum Jonathan** of chapter 26 reads the *bare rock* prediction eschatologically — Tyre will be destroyed in the *messianic age*. **Rashi** identifies the historical Nebuchadnezzar campaign and notes that the *thirteen-year siege* failed to reduce Tyre fully — a problem he attributes to the people's unworthiness for full deliverance. **Radak** elaborates with characteristic philological care: the *bare rock* refers to the *island-rock*, which Radak knows from medieval geographic tradition is still partially inhabited, and he suggests the prophecy is *cumulative* — fulfilled progressively across multiple destructions. **Pesiqta Rabbati** preserves an aggadic tradition that Tyre's destruction is the precondition for Israel's flourishing — *no two cities in the world prosper at once*; Tyre's fall makes room for Jerusalem's restoration.

**Christian trajectory**: Patristic — **Origen's *Homilies on Ezekiel*** allegorizes Tyre as *the world* and the *bare rock* as *the soul stripped of its corruptions*. **Jerome's Commentary on Ezekiel** (414) treats the prophecy historically and notes the **Alexander fulfillment**: Jerome read **Quintus Curtius and Arrian** and explicitly identified Alexander's causeway-building with Ezekiel 26:12 (*your stones and timber and soil they will cast into the midst of the waters*). Jerome's identification became the standard Christian apologetic reading. **Tertullian** (*Adversus Marcionem* 3.13) cites Tyre's destruction as proof of Old Testament prophetic accuracy. **Calvin's *Lectures on Ezekiel*** (1565) treats the chapter exhaustively; Calvin grapples honestly with the partial-fulfillment problem and concludes that *Nebuchadnezzar's siege accomplished what was decreed against the mainland city, while the island Tyre was finally destroyed only later under Alexander*. Calvin's two-stage reading became the dominant Reformed position. Critical — **Cooke (ICC)**, **Eichrodt (OTL)**, **Zimmerli (Hermeneia)**. Contemporary — **Greenberg (Anchor)**, **Block (NICOT)**, **Aubet** on Phoenician material culture, **Stieglitz** on Tyrian archaeology.

**Where they diverge**: the **patristic-typological** reading (Origen) treats Tyre as the symbol of the *secular world* judged by God. The **Jerome-Calvin historical-cumulative** reading treats Nebuchadnezzar's siege and Alexander's destruction as *together* fulfilling the prophecy, a position embraced by classic conservative apologetics (**Lewis Sperry Chafer** in *Systematic Theology* 4.222–223; **John Walvoord** in *Every Prophecy of the Bible*; **Hugh Ross** in *Why the Universe Is the Way It Is*). The **critical-historical** reading (Zimmerli, Block) takes the *partial-fulfillment* as an exegetical fact: 29:17–20 explicitly acknowledges that Nebuchadnezzar *did not get* Tyre, and **the prophecy fails on Ezekiel's own terms unless reinterpreted**. The Catholic reading (taking the partial-fulfillment seriously) treats prophecy as *typological-progressive* rather than literally-determinist — Nebuchadnezzar partial, Alexander further, eschatological judgment final.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The partial-fulfillment problem — does prophetic accuracy require literal-historical match, and what is the theological status of 29:17–20's explicit acknowledgment that Nebuchadnezzar did not fully fulfill chapter 26?** This is the classic apologetic crux in Ezekiel. The texts say what they say: 26:7–14 predicts that **Nebuchadnezzar will destroy Tyre**, scrape its soil, throw its stones into the sea, leave it a bare rock, and 29:17–20 (sixteen years later) acknowledges that **Nebuchadnezzar's army labored hard against Tyre but got nothing** — and YHWH compensates Nebuchadnezzar with Egypt instead.

**Conservative literal-cumulative** reading (Block in NICOT; Walvoord, Chafer): the prophecy is *cumulative across history* — Nebuchadnezzar accomplished one phase (the destruction of mainland Tyre and the long siege), Alexander accomplished a second phase (the literal causeway-building, the literal scraping of the rock, the 332 BC island-storming), and the final eschatological phase is yet future. The *bare rock* prediction is satisfied by Alexander; the *Nebuchadnezzar* prediction is satisfied by the thirteen-year siege and the negotiated submission. **Hugh Ross** and **Josh McDowell** treat Ezekiel 26 as a paradigm case of **fulfilled prophecy apologetics**, citing the Alexander causeway as exact-literal fulfillment of 26:12 (*they will cast your stones and timber and soil into the midst of the waters*). **Lewis Sperry Chafer** (*Systematic Theology* vol. 4) gives the dispensational reading: prophecy can be fulfilled by *successive agents* across time. **Steelman**: the canonical text is internally honest. 29:17–20 *itself* acknowledges that Nebuchadnezzar did not fully accomplish 26:7–14, and the **Alexander causeway** is a remarkable historical fact that *exactly matches* the language of 26:4 (*scrape her soil from her*) and 26:12 (*your stones and timber and soil they will cast into the midst of the waters*). This is not retrospective creative reading; the text's specificity is unusual.

**Critical-mythopoeic** reading (Zimmerli in Hermeneia; Eichrodt; modern critical scholarship): the **prophecy did not literally come true under Nebuchadnezzar**, the *Tyre still stands* problem of 29:17–20 is *Ezekiel's own honest acknowledgment*, and the chapter must be read as **prophetic-rhetorical** rather than predictively-determinist. **Steelman**: the canonical text *itself* names the problem in 29:17. If the *book* of Ezekiel admits the prediction failed in its primary literal sense and reassigns it (Egypt as compensation), then any reading that defends Ezekiel 26 as literal-determinist contradicts the book's own internal redaction. The Alexander-fulfillment reading is **post hoc apologetic** that the text itself does not authorize — it is read *back* into the prophecy two and a half centuries after Ezekiel's compositional horizon. The honest critical reading respects the text's own admission.

The least intuitive position to a literal-prophecy apologist is **the critical-redactional reading that 29:17–20 acknowledges the prophecy's primary failure**. The least intuitive to a critical scholar is **the cumulative-fulfillment reading that the Alexander causeway exactly satisfies the bare-rock prediction**. Both can be steelmanned. The unresolved question is not which is correct — the texts admit ambiguity — but **what *kind* of accuracy prophecy is held to**: literal-determinist, cumulative-typological, or mythopoeic-rhetorical.

Where this leaves us: Ezekiel 26 forces a confrontation with the *meaning* of prophetic fulfillment. The unresolved question: when the canonical text *itself* admits a prediction has not been fulfilled in its primary sense (29:17–20), what theology of prophetic accuracy survives? The chapter does not answer; it puts the question on the table.

**Tension 2: Prophetic literalism vs. typology — does the *bare rock* prediction require Alexander's specific causeway-building, or is it sufficient that Tyre's island glory ended?** This is closely related to Tension 1 but distinct: even granting that the prediction is *somehow* fulfilled, what *kind* of fulfillment satisfies the canonical demand?

**Reformed-Calvin tradition** insists on *literal-grammatical-historical* exegesis (Calvin Institutes 1.7–9, *Lectures on Ezekiel* preface) but acknowledges *progressive-historical* fulfillment for OAN material. **Catholic four-senses tradition** (Aquinas *ST* I.1.10) — literal, allegorical, moral, anagogical — allows that the *literal* sense may be satisfied progressively while the *anagogical* (eschatological) sense remains forward-looking. **Dispensational hermeneutics** (Scofield, Ryrie, Walvoord) insists on strict-literal fulfillment in dispensational stages. **Critical-typological** reading (Childs, *Biblical Theology*, 1992; Goldsworthy) reads OAN material as *typology* — the *bare rock* is the symbol of *every* commercial-imperial city's eventual fall, not a topographic prediction.

The least intuitive position to a literalist is the **typological-symbolic** reading that takes Tyre as a symbol of *every* commercial-imperial city. The steelman: **Revelation 18** explicitly uses Tyre's fall as the *type* of Babylon-the-great's fall, and the New Testament's appropriation of Ezekiel 26–28 in Revelation 18 is *not* literalist — Babylon-the-great is *Rome*, the Roman commercial empire, and the *bare rock* imagery is *transposed* not *re-applied*. If the canonical NT itself uses Ezekiel 26 typologically, then typology is the *internal-canonical* hermeneutic.

Where this leaves us: Ezekiel 26 establishes the principle that *the fall of the great commercial-imperial city* is the type of every commercial empire's eschatological accountability. The unresolved question: how much of the *bare rock* prediction is satisfied by Alexander, and how much remains forward-looking toward the eschatological *fall of Babylon-the-great*? The chapter does not draw the line; it puts the question on the table.

## The Thread

The *bare rock* image of Ezekiel 26:4, 14 is taken up in **Revelation 18:21–24** (*a mighty angel took up a stone like a great millstone and threw it into the sea, saying, "So will Babylon the great city be thrown down with violence"*) — Babylon-the-great's fall imitates Tyre's, the millstone-into-the-sea imagery directly drawing on Ezekiel 26:12. The *king of kings* title applied to Nebuchadnezzar in 26:7 is reversed in **Revelation 17:14, 19:16** (*Lord of lords and King of kings* applied to the Lamb) — the imperial title transferred to Christ. The *gate of the peoples* phrase (26:2 — Tyre's name for Jerusalem) is theologically inverted in **Revelation 21:25** (*its gates will never be shut by day, and there will be no night there*), where the New Jerusalem becomes the gate of *all* peoples. **Bauckham's *The Climax of Prophecy*** (1993) traces this Tyre→Babylon→New-Jerusalem inversion as a foundational feature of Revelation's compositional architecture: Ezekiel 26 supplies the language and the trajectory; Revelation 18–22 closes the loop. The *thirteen-year siege* of 586–573 BC, the longest in ancient record, also has an unexpected post-history: the **Tyrian colony of Carthage** outlasted the metropolis and itself fell to Roman destruction in 146 BC — and **Augustine in *City of God*** explicitly draws the parallel between Tyre and Carthage as the two great commercial empires whose fall vindicates the prophetic OAN tradition.
