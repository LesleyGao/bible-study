# 2 Chronicles 16 — Asa's Late Failure and the Foot Disease

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

If chapter 14 shows Asa as the Chronicler's first model king and chapter 15 supplies the rubric of *seeking the LORD*, chapter 16 shows the rubric being failed by the very king who articulated it. The Chronicler — anonymous, post-exilic, writing in the Persian period for a community without a king — uses Asa's late reign to teach his readers a hard lesson: a faithful start does not guarantee a faithful finish, and the same king can both *seek* and *forsake* in different seasons of one life. Sara Japhet, Hugh Williamson, Gary Knoppers, Steven McKenzie, Ralph Klein, Mark Boda, and Louis Jonker all read the chapter as the closing panel of a deliberately constructed Asa triptych in which chapter 16 inverts chapter 14 almost point for point.

The synoptic relationship to Kings is again the load-bearing context. The parallel is **1 Kings 15:16–24**. Kings narrates the Baasha conflict and the Ben-hadad alliance (1 Kgs 15:17–22) in language closely paralleling 2 Chronicles 16:1–6, mentions Asa's foot disease in old age (1 Kgs 15:23), and notes his death (1 Kgs 15:24). What Kings does **not** include — and what the Chronicler **adds** — is the prophetic rebuke by Hanani the seer (vv. 7–10), Asa's imprisonment of the prophet, his cruelties against some of the people, and the explicit theological framing of the foot disease as failure to seek Yahweh (v. 12). These additions transform a brief Kings notice into a chapter-long theological argument.

The chronology has long puzzled commentators. Asa is said to be in his **thirty-sixth year** (v. 1) when Baasha attacks — but Baasha himself died in the twenty-sixth year of Asa's reign according to 1 Kings 15:33–16:8. Three solutions have been proposed. (1) The traditional harmonization (Edwin Thiele, John Bright with sympathy) reads "thirty-sixth year" as counting from the division of the kingdom rather than from Asa's accession; on that count it would be roughly Asa's sixteenth year, which fits Baasha's lifespan. (2) The textual-error reading (Williamson, Japhet, with Klein noting LXX variants) suggests the numbers in vv. 1 and 12 should be read as twenty-sixth and twenty-ninth respectively, with scribal corruption. (3) The Chronicler-as-theological-author reading (Knoppers, McKenzie) takes the chronological dissonance as evidence the Chronicler has organized his Asa material thematically rather than chronologically: chapter 14's *ten years of rest*, chapter 15's *no war until the thirty-fifth year*, and chapter 16's *thirty-sixth year* attack form a literary frame whose internal numbers are stylized rather than annalistic. None of these positions resolves all the data; the chronological problem is real.

The historical context of the alliance is well-attested. **Baasha** (בַּעְשָׁא) ruled the northern kingdom from roughly 909–886 BCE; **Ben-hadad I** (בֶּן־הֲדַד, *Ben-Hăḏaḏ*, "son of Hadad," referring to the Aramean storm god) ruled Damascus during a period when Aram-Damascus was the dominant power in Syria. The Aramean Stele of Tel Dan, discovered in 1993, attests a "house of David" in this general historical period and provides external confirmation that Aramean-Israelite-Judahite triangulation in the ninth century BCE was a real geopolitical reality. **Ramah** (הָרָמָה, *hāRāmâ*) sat about five miles north of Jerusalem on the main road north — a Baasha border fort there could effectively choke Judahite trade and travel, exactly as the chapter describes. The cities Ben-hadad takes — **Ijon** (עִיּוֹן), **Dan** (דָּן), **Abel-maim** (אָבֵל מַיִם, possibly Abel-beth-maacah), and the **store-cities of Naphtali** — trace a precise geographical line through the upper Galilee and the Jordan valley headwaters, exactly the corridor an Aramean army from Damascus would press to relieve pressure on Judah's northern border.

The pivotal Hebrew vocabulary returns. Hanani says (v. 7) **בְּהִשָּׁעֶנְךָ עַל־מֶלֶךְ אֲרָם וְלֹא נִשְׁעַנְתָּ עַל־יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ** (*bəhiššāʿenəḵā ʿal-meleḵ ʾĂrām wəlōʾ nišʿantā ʿal-Yahweh ʾĕlōheḵā*) — "because you relied on the king of Syria and did not rely on the LORD your God." The verb **שָׁעַן** (*šāʿan*, "to lean upon, to rely") is the same verb Asa himself used in his prayer in 14:11 (*we rely on you*). The Chronicler is showing a king who used to lean on Yahweh now leaning on a foreign king — the same posture, transferred to the wrong object.

Verse 9's famous line — *for the eyes of the LORD run to and fro throughout the whole earth* — uses the participle **מְשׁוֹטְטוֹת** (*məšôṭṭôṯ*, "roaming, ranging") of the verb **שׁוּט** (*šûṭ*). The verb appears in Job 1:7 and 2:2 of Satan *roaming* the earth; in Zechariah 4:10 of the *seven eyes of the LORD* that *range through the whole earth*. The Chronicler is drawing on a stock theological image of divine surveillance and aid, redeploying it as an indictment: the eyes that *range to give strong support* found Asa not relying.

The **foot disease** of verse 12 has been the subject of long medical and theological speculation. The Hebrew **חָלָה אָסָא בְּרַגְלָיו** (*ḥālâ ʾĀsāʾ bəraḡlāyw*, "Asa was diseased in his feet") is non-specific; ancient and medieval commentators (Josephus, *Antiquities* 8.12.6; some rabbinic sources) speculated about gout, edema, peripheral vascular disease, leprosy of the feet, or ergot-related conditions. The **Septuagint** translates with similar non-specificity. The Chronicler's interest is theological, not diagnostic: the king *did not seek the LORD, but sought help from physicians* (**רֹפְאִים**, *rōp̄əʾîm*). The verb is again **דָּרַשׁ** (*dāraš*) — the same verb that runs through the entire Asa cycle. He once *sought* Yahweh and prevailed; now he *seeks* physicians and dies.

The funeral in verse 14 is unusually detailed. Asa is buried *in the tomb he had cut for himself*; he is laid on a *bier filled with various kinds of spices* (**בְּשָׂמִים וּזְנִים**, *bəśāmîm ûzənîm*, "spices and varieties"); a *very great fire* (**שְׂרֵפָה גְדוֹלָה עַד־לִמְאֹד**, *śərēp̄â ḡəḏôlâ ʿaḏ-limʾōḏ*) is made in his honor. This is **not cremation** — Israelite practice strongly opposed cremation — but a public funeral pyre of incense and aromatic woods, a formal honor shown to a king. Compare Jeremiah 34:5, where Yahweh promises Zedekiah *they shall make a burning for you*, indicating this was a recognized honorific. The chapter ends with full funerary honors despite the tarnished late reign — the Chronicler's notice that Asa, even in failure, was still a Davidic king.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter is structured as a four-act tragedy: the failed alliance (vv. 1–6), the prophetic confrontation (vv. 7–10), the disease (vv. 11–12), and the funeral (vv. 13–14). The literary movement is downward — from a king at the height of his power making a wrong political choice, through prophetic word rejected, through bodily decline, to death.

Comparison with chapter 14 is the chapter's primary literary tactic. Chapter 14 began with the Cushite invasion: *a million men*, an army Asa could not defeat by arithmetic. Chapter 16 begins with a much smaller threat: a fort five miles from Jerusalem. In 14, Asa prayed and Yahweh fought. In 16, Asa empties the temple treasury and hires a Damascene king. The Chronicler is staging a deliberate contrast. Hanani's speech in vv. 7–9 makes the contrast explicit: *were not the Ethiopians and the Libyans a huge army with very many chariots and horsemen? Yet because you relied on the LORD, he gave them into your hand*. The prophet is performing the chapter's own literary work — pointing the reader from chapter 16 back to chapter 14 and asking what changed.

The prophet's name — **Hanani** (חֲנָנִי, *Ḥănānî*, "Yahweh has been gracious") — is significant. He reappears in 1 Kings 16:1 as the father of Jehu the prophet who rebukes Baasha; the Chronicler's audience would have known the family. His title here is **חֹזֶה** (*ḥōzeh*, "seer") rather than the more common **נָבִיא** (*nāḇîʾ*, "prophet"). Williamson notes that Chronicles uses several prophetic titles with apparent equivalence; Japhet suggests *ḥōzeh* may carry the connotation of visionary insight rather than oracular pronouncement. Either way, his role is the chapter's literary fulcrum.

Asa's response — *put him in the stocks* (**מַהְפֶּכֶת**, *mahpeḵeṯ*) — is **the first time a king of Judah persecutes a prophet in Chronicles**. This narrative milestone matters. The Chronicler will later record Joash killing the prophet Zechariah (24:20–22); Manasseh will, in tradition (martyrdom of Isaiah, *Ascension of Isaiah*), execute Isaiah; Jeremiah will be imprisoned by Zedekiah's officials. The trajectory from Asa to the destruction of the temple runs through king-after-king rejecting prophetic word. Asa is the first.

Verse 10's *Asa inflicted cruelties upon some of the people at the same time* — the Hebrew **וַיְרַצֵּץ אָסָא מִן־הָעָם** (*wayraṣṣēṣ ʾĀsāʾ min-hāʿām*, with the verb **רָצַץ**, *rāṣaṣ*, "to crush, oppress") — is grim and laconic. The Chronicler does not specify the cruelties but the verbal root is the same one used of an oppressed reed (Isa 36:6) or a crushed enemy. A king who once protected his people now crushes some of them. The literary geometry inverts.

The disease in v. 12 functions literarily as an *enacted parable*. The verb *seek* (**דָּרַשׁ**, *dāraš*) appears twice: he did not *seek* the LORD, but *sought* physicians. The repetition of the same verb with two different objects is the chapter's tightest literary device, perfectly inverting the chapter 14 prayer.

The closing funeral, opulent and honorific, lets the king out gently. The Chronicler's literary judgment is calibrated: Asa was a king who began well and ended badly, and both halves are recorded. The bier of spices and the great fire mark him as Davidic; the disease and the prophet in stocks mark him as fallen. The reader is asked to hold both.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Within the canon**, chapter 16's themes recur with striking consistency. The pattern of *foreign-alliance-as-failure-to-trust-Yahweh* runs through Isaiah 7 (Ahaz and Assyria), Isaiah 30–31 (Hezekiah's Egypt advisors), and Hosea 7:11 (*Ephraim is like a dove, silly and without sense, calling to Egypt, going to Assyria*). The persecution-of-prophets motif runs through Jeremiah, the *Lives of the Prophets* tradition, Stephen's speech in Acts 7:52 (*which of the prophets did your fathers not persecute?*), and Jesus's lament over Jerusalem (Matt 23:37, Luke 13:34).

The verse that has had the most extensive reception history is **16:12**: *yet even in his disease he did not seek the LORD, but sought help from physicians*. This verse has functioned as one of the touchstones of Christian and Jewish reflection on medicine. The verse reads on its surface as a condemnation of seeking medical help, and various sectarian movements have read it that way.

The **Christian Science** position (Mary Baker Eddy, *Science and Health*, 1875) treats verses like 16:12 as proof texts for the rejection of medicine in favor of prayer-based healing. **Jehovah's Witnesses** apply the verse in a more limited way — generally accepting medical care but rejecting blood transfusions on different scriptural grounds. **Some strands of Pentecostal-Charismatic faith-healing tradition** have read 16:12 as evidence that recourse to medicine reveals lack of faith.

The mainstream Christian reading — **Catholic, Orthodox, Reformed, Lutheran, Anglican, mainstream evangelical** — explicitly rejects this interpretation. The Catechism of the Catholic Church §1500–1525, in its treatment of the sacrament of the anointing of the sick, draws on Sirach 38:1–15 as the explicit Old Testament theology of medicine: *honor the physician with the honor due him, according to your need of him, for the LORD created him… and from God a physician gets wisdom* (Sir 38:1–2). Calvin in his commentary on 2 Chronicles 16 reads the verse not as forbidding medicine but as condemning *exclusive* trust in physicians without prayer — Asa's sin was not seeking physicians, it was *not seeking the LORD* in addition. Williamson and Japhet both argue this is the obvious sense of the Hebrew: the verse does not say *Asa sought physicians instead of the LORD because that is wrong*; it says *Asa did not seek the LORD, but sought physicians*, with the emphasis on the *failure to seek the LORD*. **Three positions** thus emerge clearly: (1) **the strict-rejection reading** (Christian Science, parts of faith-healing tradition) — medicine is itself the problem; (2) **the limited-application reading** (some sectarian groups) — particular medical interventions but not the practice as a whole; (3) **the both-and reading** (mainstream Catholic, Orthodox, Reformed, Lutheran, Anglican, mainstream evangelical) — prayer and medicine are complementary, both gifts of God, and the verse condemns exclusive reliance on physicians without God, not medicine itself.

The honest steelman of position 3: the Old Testament does have texts that affirm medicine (Jer 8:22 implies its existence and value; Ezekiel 30:21 references bandaging; Isaiah 1:6 names medical care positively); the post-canonical Sirach 38 explicitly affirms physicians as God's gift; Jesus himself uses medical analogies positively (*it is not the well who need a physician*, Mark 2:17, Luke 4:23); Luke is identified as the *beloved physician* (Col 4:14). The grammatical and theological context of 2 Chronicles 16:12 is **exclusive reliance**, not medical care as such.

In **Jewish reception**, the medieval halakhic tradition (Rambam, who was himself a physician) reads 16:12 as condemning failure to pray, not seeking medical help. Maimonides explicitly argues in *Mishneh Torah* and in his medical writings that consulting physicians is religiously obligatory, and that Asa's failure was theological neglect of God, not the consultation of physicians. The rabbinic discussion in b. Berakhot 60a and elsewhere broadly endorses medicine while insisting on prayer alongside.

The New Testament does not quote 2 Chronicles 16 directly, but the *seek-or-forsake* binary that runs through the Asa cycle stands behind passages like Hebrews 3:7–4:13 (*today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts*), where present faithfulness is urged precisely because past faithfulness does not guarantee future endurance.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

The Chronicler's intent is to show his post-exilic audience that *seeking the LORD* is not a one-time achievement but a continual practice. Asa sought in chapters 14–15 and forsook in chapter 16. The same king. The same seek-vocabulary. The trajectory is reversible, and the chapter is the Chronicler's pastoral warning that early faithfulness can collapse late.

Two tensions deserve sustained attention.

**Tension one: Asa's disease and the *seek-not-physicians* verse.** Already explored across positions in Lens 3; the theological question that remains is *why this verse troubles modern readers more than it troubled premodern ones*. The premodern reading (Maimonides, Calvin, Aquinas in his treatment of secondary causality) had no difficulty distinguishing *exclusive* reliance on physicians from medicine itself. The modern reading inherits a post-Enlightenment context in which medicine has become more powerful and the boundary between *trust in God* and *trust in medicine* feels more zero-sum. **Three positions on the modern application:** (1) the *both-and* mainstream — pray and seek medical care, recognizing both as God's provision; (2) the *medicine-as-suspect* sectarian — treat 16:12 as warrant for limiting or rejecting medical care; (3) the *seek-the-LORD-first* pastoral — prayer comes before, alongside, and after medical decisions, but the chapter's emphasis falls on the *failure to seek God*, not on the legitimacy of medicine. Steelman of position 1: every other relevant biblical text affirms medicine, and the grammar of 16:12 itself locates Asa's failure in the *not seeking* rather than in the *seeking physicians*. Steelman of position 2 is harder to construct — the position generally relies on isolating 16:12 from the broader canon and from premodern readings, and the cost in human suffering historically associated with rejection of medical care has been substantial.

**Tension two: the deteriorating-king pattern and the persecution of prophets.** The Chronicler will record this pattern again — Joash starts well and ends badly (ch. 24), Uzziah starts well and is struck with leprosy for cultic overreach (ch. 26), Hezekiah starts gloriously and ends with pride (ch. 32), Manasseh starts wickedly and ends repentantly (ch. 33), Josiah starts gloriously and ends in a politically reckless death (ch. 35). Asa is the template. **Three positions** on what the pattern teaches: (1) the *late-life-vulnerability* reading (Williamson, Japhet, Knoppers) — the Chronicler is documenting that even the most faithful kings are vulnerable in late reign, that pride or fatigue or political calculation erodes faithfulness, that the final years of leadership require special vigilance; (2) the *mixed-portrait* reading (McKenzie, Boda) — the Chronicler refuses tidy character assessments and prefers to show kings in their full complexity; (3) the *literary-construct* reading (some critical scholars) — the pattern is a Chronicler invention to teach his audience, with the historical reality possibly more uniform than the constructed pattern allows. Steelman of position 1: the pattern fits real human experience of leadership decline; the warning is pastorally apt for any community whose leaders are growing old.

The persecution of Hanani inaugurates a darker theological note: when prophetic word collides with royal will, royal will often wins in the short term and loses in the long term. The Chronicler is clear that Asa's imprisonment of Hanani was wrong, and that the disease and the wars that followed were divine response. The reader is asked to take the prophetic word seriously even when — especially when — it is being rejected by power.

**Where this leaves us.** A king who once embodied *seeking the LORD* dies having forsaken it. The chapter resists both a glib *he-was-saved-because-he-once-believed* and a harsh *he-was-condemned-because-he-failed-late*. The Chronicler keeps both halves. He records the failure plainly and the funeral honors fully. The reader is asked to hold the tension and to ask whether, at the end of one's own life, the *seeking* will be present-tense or past.

**Closing question.** If even Asa — whose covenant renewal at Pentecost is one of the high points of the Hebrew Bible — could end his reign imprisoning a prophet and dying without seeking, what does it ask of someone who has had a powerful season of faithfulness and now faces the possibility that the *seeking* could erode without their noticing, and what would Hanani's voice — five miles outside the gate, costing the temple treasury — sound like in their life today?

## The Thread

The verb *seek* — *dāraš* — appears once at the chapter's center (v. 12) doing the inverse of what it did in chapter 14. The same king, the same verb, the wrong object. *He did not seek the LORD, but sought physicians* — the Chronicler's most compressed statement of how the seeking-life can fail in the end.
