# 1 Samuel 19: Teraphim, Naioth, and the Naked King

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter spans three locations and four social institutions: the royal court at Gibeah, David's private dwelling (probably also at or near Gibeah, granted as part of his royal status as son-in-law), the prophetic guild at Naioth, and the unnamed "great well at Secu" (v. 22) where Saul makes his final stop. Each location carries its own institutional grammar.

**The royal household and its dependents.** Saul's order in v. 1—"to Jonathan his son and to all his servants" to kill David—reveals the structure of the early Israelite court. The *avadim* (עֲבָדִים, "servants/officials") were the king's personal entourage, comprising bodyguards, retainers, court officers, and family clients who functioned as the executive arm of royal power. The Tel Dan stele (Aramaic, ninth-century BCE; Biran 1993, 1995) preserves the formula *bytdwd* ("house of David"), which refers precisely to this kind of royal patrimonial household—the king's *bayit* as the institutional core of the regime. Saul's command-and-control runs through the *avadim*, and Jonathan is technically among them as crown prince. The fact that Jonathan can act publicly to defend David (vv. 4–5) and Saul listens (v. 6) reflects the relatively informal access patterns of an early monarchy still operating along clan-network lines.

**The private dwelling and its women.** Michal's window-escape (vv. 11–12) presupposes a typical Iron Age Israelite "four-room house" with a flat roof and small clerestory windows on the upper story. Such houses are excavated extensively at sites like Tell Beit Mirsim, Tell el-Far'ah North, and Hazor; the windows were small (often less than half a meter square) and high enough above ground level to require letting a person down by rope or cloth. The escape mechanism Michal employs (*va-toridem ba'ad ha-chalon*, "she let him down through the window") is the same architectural feature Rahab uses in Joshua 2:15 and Paul will later use at Damascus (Acts 9:25). The window-escape is a recurring narrative type-scene because Iron Age domestic architecture made it possible.

**The teraphim.** This is the chapter's most archaeologically loaded detail. *Terafim* (תְּרָפִים, v. 13) are household figurines—plural in form but ambiguous in number. Karel van der Toorn's foundational article "The Nature of the Biblical Teraphim in the Light of the Cuneiform Evidence" (*CBQ* 1990) argues, on the basis of cuneiform parallels with the Akkadian *tarpû*, that teraphim were ancestor figurines, representations of deceased family heads kept in the household for veneration and consultation. The term appears in Genesis 31 (Rachel's theft from Laban), Judges 17–18 (Micah's domestic shrine and the Danite seizure), Hosea 3:4 (where they are listed as a normal feature of Israelite religious practice), and Zechariah 10:2 (where they are condemned as deceptive divinatory devices). Teraphim are recovered archaeologically as small (typically eight-to-twenty-centimeter) anthropomorphic figurines, often clay, often female; the so-called "Judean pillar figurines" excavated by hundreds at sites including Jerusalem, Lachish, and Ein-Gedi may be one form of teraphim, though the identification is contested (Othmar Keel, Christoph Uehlinger, and Raz Kletter have all weighed in).

The chapter's matter-of-fact mention of teraphim in the house of David and Michal is theologically remarkable. **The royal son-in-law's house contains household idols.** The narrator passes over this without comment. Several conclusions follow. First, household figurine veneration was common enough in pre-exilic Israel that even a "favored of the LORD" household had them. Second, the Deuteronomistic editors who shaped the book did not bowdlerize the detail (compare 1 Chron 11–12, where the equivalent material about David is sanitized). Third, Michal's manipulation of the teraphim—using one as a body-double—shows the figurine is roughly human-scaled or at least sufficiently human-shaped to deceive at a distance. This actually argues for a substantial figurine, perhaps closer to a wooden ancestor-image than to the small clay figurines, which would be too obvious as decoys. **Theodore Lewis** has argued that some teraphim were near-life-sized.

**The Naioth prophetic guild.** *Nayyot* (נָיוֹת, vv. 18–19) appears nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible; its etymology is uncertain (perhaps from *naveh*, "habitation," giving "the dwellings"—a settlement of prophets near Ramah). The chapter shows a corporate prophetic *chevel* (חֶבֶל, "band, group") under Samuel's headship engaging in *navi* (נָבִיא) ecstatic activity. **Susan Niditch** in *Oral World and Written Word* (1996) and **Robert Wilson** in *Prophecy and Society in Ancient Israel* (1980) have analyzed these prophetic guilds against ANE parallels: the **Mari letters** (early second millennium) attest the **muḫḫûm** (ecstatic prophet) and **āpilum** (responder/spokesperson), while the Phoenician Wenamun inscription describes a possessed youth from Byblos prophesying ecstatically. The phenomenon described—group ecstasy, music-induced (cf. 2 Kings 3:15, where Elisha calls for a musician), and contagious—is well attested across the ancient Near East and Mediterranean world. Comparative material includes the Greek **mantis** tradition, the Anatolian **GUDU/UR.GI7** ecstatic priests, and later the corybantic and bacchic possession traditions.

Three Hebrew terms repay attention. First, **terafim** (above). Second, *vayitnabbe* (וַיִּתְנַבֵּא, "he prophesied," v. 20)—the hithpael of *navi*, the reflexive/durative form indicating the ecstatic-behavioral dimension of prophecy as opposed to the spoken-word dimension; the same form is used of Saul in 10:10 (the etiology to which 19:24 returns). Third, *vayitpashshet* (וַיִּתְפַּשֵּׁט, "and he stripped himself," v. 24), again the reflexive—Saul's nakedness is self-inflicted in the grip of the Spirit, not done to him. Public nakedness in the ancient Near East signified extreme abasement: prisoners of war were paraded naked (cf. Isa 20:4), and the act was the ultimate humiliation. Saul as the king lying naked all day and all night is the icon of his humiliation.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter has four episodes in escalating sequence: Jonathan's intercession (vv. 1–7), the third spear-throw (vv. 8–10), Michal's ruse (vv. 11–17), the Naioth flight (vv. 18–24). Each episode shows David escaping Saul through a different agent or mechanism: a brother's eloquence, his own agility, a wife's cunning, a prophet's territory. The narrator builds redundant deliverance—David is saved four times in one chapter—to underscore the providential pattern that will define David's exile years.

The Naioth episode is the chapter's most artistically remarkable piece, and it depends on a careful piece of internal allusion. The phrase *ha-gam Sha'ul ba-nevi'im* ("Is Saul also among the prophets?", v. 24) is the same proverb whose etiology was given at 10:11–12, when the young Saul, freshly anointed by Samuel, fell in with a prophetic band and joined their ecstasy. There the proverb expressed surprise at Saul's elevation; here it expresses pity at his shame. The narrator is closing a circle that opened in chapter 10. Saul's kingship began with prophetic ecstasy as inauguration; it is now revisited by prophetic ecstasy as humiliation. **David Gunn** in *The Fate of King Saul* makes this the chapter's literary climax: the doubled etiology marks Saul's symbolic unmaking, the formal reversal of his anointing.

**Robert Alter** notes the careful inversion of Saul's bodily situation. At his first prophetic episode (ch. 10), Saul is among the prophets as one elevated by them. Here he is laid low by the same Spirit. The same divine charisma that once made him elevates him now flattens him. **Polzin** treats the chapter as the demonstration that the LORD does not fail Saul prophetically—the Spirit will still come upon him—but the coming of the Spirit is no longer a coronation, it is a stripping.

The **Michal teraphim ruse** (vv. 11–17) is one of the chapter's most carefully constructed pieces. The narrative parallels Rebekah's deception of Isaac (Gen 27)—a wife/mother engineering the escape or success of the favored one against the kin's authority. **Bal** and **Exum** both note the parallel; **Frymer-Kensky** in *Reading the Women of the Bible* expands it. The literary mechanism of the goats-hair pillow (*kvir ha-izzim*, כְּבִיר הָעִזִּים, v. 13) at the head of the bed is precisely echoed in Genesis 27:16 (Jacob's goatskin disguise) and is probably a deliberate intertext—Michal as a Rebekah-figure. The teraphim is the body-double for David, the goats-hair is the hair-double; the deception is doubly textured.

A pivotal detail: when Saul confronts Michal (v. 17) about the deception, Michal lies—"He said to me, 'Let me go. Why should I kill you?'" David made no such threat. The narrator does not comment on the lie; we are left to read it. Two readings are possible. (1) Michal is protecting herself—claiming that David coerced her removes her culpability and shifts blame to David, who is safely fled. (2) Michal is protecting David—the alibi gives Saul a face-saving narrative ("David threatened her") that requires no further family rupture. Both readings work. **Adele Berlin**'s *Poetics and Interpretation of Biblical Narrative* (1983) treats Michal's lie as the inception of her characterization as a complex political agent who will resurface in 2 Samuel 6 as the only person willing to publicly criticize David.

Textual variants: The MT and LXX are largely in agreement for chapter 19, with minor differences. The LXX softens "harmful spirit from the LORD" in v. 9 to "evil spirit of God." 4QSam-b preserves fragments that align with MT.

A literary device worth marking: **the threefold escalation** of v. 21 (three sets of messengers, all caught up in prophesying) followed by the king himself going. The three-plus-one pattern (cf. Elijah's three captains in 2 Kings 1) is conventional for showing escalated divine resistance.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical thread woven here is the **David exile pattern**, which will dominate from chapter 19 through chapter 31 and find its narrative resolution in 2 Samuel 1–5. David spends approximately a decade as a fugitive, gathering a private army, taking refuge with Philistines and at Adullam and Engedi, and emerging only at Saul's death. This chapter is the launch of that exile. The Naioth episode is also the chapter's contribution to the **Spirit-and-prophecy** thread, which began at chapters 10–11, is partially explored at chapter 16 (the Spirit transfer between Saul and David), is suspended for the Goliath narrative, and now returns with full force.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory**: **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 6.218–224) retells the Naioth episode while softening the prophetic ecstasy into more dignified categories—Saul "fell into a fit of ecstasy" and "lay still." Josephus is uncomfortable with the public nakedness and downplays it. The rabbinic tradition is fascinated by the teraphim. **Pirke de-Rabbi Eliezer** 36 makes the teraphim into a head-shaped oracular device; the bizarre *teraphim*-as-mummified-firstborn-head tradition (probably late) shows the rabbinic effort to explain how a teraphim could deceive Saul's messengers as a body. **Targum Pseudo-Jonathan** translates *teraphim* as *tzalmanaya*—"images" or "icons"—without the polemical edge that the term acquired in Deuteronomistic discourse. **Rashi** glosses the teraphim as "human-formed images" and accepts that David's house contained one. He does not condemn Michal for possessing it, treating its deceptive deployment as the morally relevant act. **Radak** is similar, though he notes that the teraphim's presence in David's household is theologically anomalous and may reflect Michal's habits from her father's house. **Ralbag** rationalizes: teraphim were originally astrological calculation devices and only later became cult-objects, so Michal's possession was not necessarily idolatrous. **Abarbanel** is sharpest on the political reading. He argues that Saul's hostility in this chapter has crossed from psychological pathology (chs. 16, 18) into deliberate state policy: Saul has now formally enrolled the *avadim* in the assassination project, making it a public-order matter and not a private quarrel. Abarbanel reads Jonathan's intercession as a temporary halt to a policy that Saul will quickly restart, and he treats Saul's oath in v. 6 ("as the LORD lives, he shall not be put to death") as a perjury that further deteriorates Saul's standing. **Zornberg** reads Saul's nakedness at Naioth as his confrontation with the unmediated self he has been fleeing. **Zakovitch** maps the doubled etiology of "Is Saul also among the prophets?" **Garsiel** catalogues the verbal echoes between this chapter's Naioth episode and chapter 10's Gibeah episode, treating them as deliberate inclusio.

**Christian interpretive trajectory**: **Origen**, in his *Homilies*, allegorizes Saul's stripping as the soul's involuntary exposure under divine judgment—Saul stripped because he was no longer his own. **Augustine**, in commenting on the Spirit's coming upon Saul's messengers, distinguishes between the Spirit as charism (which can come upon anyone for divine purposes) and the Spirit as sanctifying grace (which abides with the elect). On Augustine's reading, Saul's messengers prophesy genuinely, but their prophecy is not a sign of election; it is the Spirit's circumstantial deployment for the rescue of David. **Gregory the Great**, in the *Moralia*, treats Saul's nakedness as the type of the soul stripped of dignity by its long resistance to grace—the body's nakedness is the visible icon of the spiritual nakedness Saul has long inhabited. **Chrysostom**, preaching on Saul, draws moral lessons about the dangers of unchecked anger and the way pursuing an enemy delivers one into divine judgment. **Aquinas**, in the *Summa* (II-II q. 172, on the gift of prophecy), uses Saul's case to distinguish the *donum prophetiae* from sanctifying grace—prophetic gifts can occur in the unworthy. **Calvin**, in his *Sermons on 1 Samuel*, returns repeatedly to the Naioth episode as the icon of God's continued sovereignty even over those who oppose him: even Saul's pursuing army is conscripted to praise God. Calvin reads the chapter's structure as a demonstration of providential overruling—every Saulide instrument is turned against Saul's own purpose. Luther in his lectures on the Spirit treats the Saul-prophesying episode as the canonical proof that the Spirit's manifestations are not always the Spirit's elective indwelling—a lesson Luther applies to the Anabaptist enthusiasts of his day.

**Critical**: Wellhausen treated the chapter's two etiologies of "Is Saul also among the prophets?" as evidence of two parallel sources, with the Naioth version possibly the older. Cross's double-redaction model places the chapter in the early Dtr1 layer with later prophetic interpolations. McCarter in the Anchor Bible commentary provides the standard apparatus and reads the chapter as the climax of the History of David's Rise's first major movement (chs. 16–19). Klein's WBC commentary catalogues the textual variants and the Hebrew vocabulary. Karel van der Toorn's work on teraphim has shaped contemporary critical reading of vv. 13–16. **Susan Niditch** in *Ancient Israelite Religion* (1997) treats the chapter as evidence that household religion (teraphim, ancestor cults) coexisted with state religion through the monarchic period. **Brian Schmidt** in *Israel's Beneficent Dead* (1994) explores the ancestor-cult dimensions.

**Contemporary**: Brueggemann (Interpretation) treats the chapter as the demonstration that Saul cannot escape the LORD's judgment even when Saul tries to circumvent it through political assassination—the very Spirit Saul has lost intervenes through David's deliverance. Polzin reads the chapter as the narrator's most pointed irony: Saul's pursuit of David delivers Saul himself naked into the prophetic enclave, exposing what the king has become. Eugene Peterson reads the Naioth episode as a meditation on the unpredictable interruption of the holy. Davis offers an expository treatment. **Feminist** readers focus heavily on Michal. **Cheryl Exum** in *Tragedy and Biblical Narrative* treats Michal's ruse as her moment of agency, contrasted with her later silencing in 2 Samuel 6. **Phyllis Trible** notes Michal's protection of David and David's failure to protect Michal in return (David never returns for her until 2 Sam 3:14, when he demands her back as political property). **Mieke Bal** reads Michal's lie to Saul as the narrative's recognition that protecting her husband requires sacrificing her relationship with her father—Michal pays a real price that David does not. **Brenner** and **Bach** examine the gender dynamics of Michal's bedroom strategy. **Frymer-Kensky** in *Reading the Women of the Bible* makes Michal one of her primary case studies of women acting decisively in male political crises.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**First tension: The status of household religion.** The chapter passes without comment over the presence of teraphim in David's household—a household marked by the LORD's favor.

*Position 1 (Deuteronomistic-canonical, classical):* The teraphim are illegitimate religious objects; the narrator's silence is an embarrassment, not an endorsement. The chapter's neutrality reflects the historical reality that pre-Deuteronomistic Israel tolerated household figurines, but the canonical redactor was uncomfortable enough to leave the detail in for verisimilitude while not commenting positively. Hosea 3:4 and Zechariah 10:2 are the proper canonical interpretation.

*Position 2 (descriptive-historical, van der Toorn, Niditch):* Teraphim were a normal feature of Israelite household religion through the monarchic period; the chapter's silence is genuine non-judgment. Israelite religion was multilayered—state cult (Jerusalem temple, eventually), regional cult (high places), and household cult (ancestor figurines, family altars)—and the layers were not in conflict for ordinary practitioners. The chapter shows household religion as part of the cultural fabric.

*Position 3 (mediating, Schmidt, Lewis):* The teraphim were ancestor figurines whose veneration coexisted with YHWH worship in pre-exilic Israelite practice without being theologically equivalent to idolatry. The Deuteronomistic critique developed later in response to specific reform agendas; reading the critique back into pre-Deuteronomistic narratives misrepresents the actual religious situation.

Steelman of 2: The text really does pass over the teraphim without comment. If the narrator wanted to condemn, the apparatus was available (cf. the editorial condemnations of high places throughout 1–2 Kings). The silence is not embarrassment; it is non-judgment, which is itself the historically accurate report of a religious culture in which household figurines were unremarkable.

*Where this leaves us:* Position 2 captures the historical situation; Position 1 captures the canonical trajectory; Position 3 mediates productively. The reader who takes both seriously will neither sanitize the chapter (Michal had no teraphim) nor moralize it (Michal sinned by having teraphim) but will read it as a window into a religious world more complex than the canonical surface usually suggests.

**Second tension: The Spirit's coming upon Saul at Naioth.** The chapter's culminating event—Saul stripped and prophesying—poses the question: what is the relationship between the Spirit's manifestation and Saul's spiritual condition?

*Position 1 (charism distinct from grace, Augustinian/Thomistic):* The Spirit's coming on Saul at Naioth is a charismatic gift commissioned for a specific purpose (rescuing David), not a sign of restored relation with YHWH. Saul prophesies, but this is no more salvific for him than Caiaphas's prophecy is salvific for Caiaphas in John 11:51. The episode shows the Spirit's instrumental use of any vessel for the LORD's purposes.

*Position 2 (mercy-and-judgment fused, Calvinist):* The Spirit's coming is simultaneously merciful (it spares David) and judicial (it humiliates Saul). The two are inseparable in this episode, which is why the rescue of David takes the form of Saul's degradation. The chapter performs the double-edged action of the same divine Spirit—life-giving to one, exposing to the other.

*Position 3 (tragic-pneumatological, Gunn, Brueggemann):* The Spirit's coming on Saul is a final unmaking, a stripping that is both divine compassion (the Spirit briefly reaches Saul) and divine sentence (the touch leaves him exposed and unrobed). The episode resists clean theological category; it is the proper subject of lament, not analysis.

Steelman of 3: The chapter's literary force depends on its refusal to resolve. If we read it as Position 1, the pathos disappears (Saul is just an instrument); if we read it as Position 2, the strangeness disappears (the rescue/exposure becomes a clean providential machine). The actual shape of the episode is troubling, and the reader who lets it stay troubling has been more faithful to the text than the reader who has theologized it into coherence.

*Where this leaves us:* The pastoral implication is that the chapter denies us the comfort of clean theology. The same Spirit that has elevated David here un-makes Saul; both actions are real, both are the LORD's. The chapter's witness is that the line between the elevating and the un-making is finer than we wish.

*Unresolved question:* Why is Saul not killed at Naioth? The Spirit who has come upon him could conceivably kill him (cf. Uzzah in 2 Sam 6, struck down for touching the ark; Nadab and Abihu in Lev 10). Instead Saul is stripped and shamed, and lives to pursue David many more times. What theology of withheld judgment is the chapter implying? The text refuses to say.

## The Thread

The proverb "Is Saul also among the prophets?" (v. 24) is itself the chapter's deepest cross-reference, sending the reader back to 10:11–12 and the inauguration narrative. The Spirit-on-Saul motif also connects to chapter 16, where the Spirit departed; the chapter shows the Spirit returning briefly, but for an unmaking purpose. Michal's deception with the teraphim links structurally to Rachel's teraphim theft in Genesis 31:34–35 (a wife concealing teraphim from her father against her father's authority); the parallel suggests Michal's act is the type of woman's protective subversion within patriarchal structures. The teraphim also appear in Judges 17–18 (Micah's shrine and the Danite migration), Hosea 3:4 (listed among normal Israelite religious accoutrements), and Zechariah 10:2 (condemned). David's flight to Samuel at Naioth begins the long Ramah/Naioth phase of David's exile and gathers Samuel's prophetic protection over David one last time before Samuel's death (which occurs in 25:1). Cross-references: Genesis 31:34–35 (Rachel and the teraphim); Joshua 2:15 (Rahab's window, the prior escape type-scene); 1 Samuel 10:9–13 (Saul's first prophetic ecstasy, the inclusio partner); 2 Samuel 6:16–23 (Michal's later confrontation with David and her childlessness, the closing of her arc that begins here). Flag forward: chapter 20 will provide the formal Jonathan-David covenant ceremony at the field, the final intimate parting, and the mechanism of the arrow signal—the chapter that turns David's exile from incidental flight into permanent separation.
