# Ezekiel 48: The Symmetrical Land and the Name of the City

## Lens 1 — Historical World

Ezekiel 48 is the closing chapter of the closing vision and the closing chapter of the book. After the river-from-the-temple of chapter 47 and the boundary list of 47:13–23, chapter 48 returns one final time to the territorial allotment, distributing the twelve tribes in horizontal east-west bands across the land — seven north of the central holy district (Dan, Asher, Naphtali, Manasseh, Ephraim, Reuben, Judah), then the *terumah* with the sanctuary at its center, then five south of it (Benjamin, Simeon, Issachar, Zebulun, Gad). The chapter concludes with the twelve gates of the city, three on each side, named for the twelve tribes (48:30–34), and the climactic naming: "And the name of the city from that time on shall be, The LORD Is There" — *YHWH Shammah* (יְהוָה שָׁמָּה, 48:35).

Greenberg, in the Anchor Bible, calls 48 "the consummation of the book," and notes that the closing inclusion (the name of the city) deliberately answers the abandonment narrative of chapters 8–11 (the *kavod* departing the temple). Block, in NICOT, treats the chapter as the structural climax of the entire book of Ezekiel: every oracle of judgment, every theophanic vision, every legal program, every exilic lament has been working toward a single end — the restoration of God's dwelling among his people — and 48:35 is the achievement. Zimmerli's Hermeneia commentary identifies the chapter's literary signature as the deliberate departure from the historical-geographic tribal allotments of Joshua 13–19: Ezekiel's tribes are arranged with mathematical symmetry, ranked by maternal lineage in a specific order (Leah and Rachel descendants closer to the center, the descendants of the handmaids — Bilhah and Zilpah — at the extremities), and given equal east-west bands without regard to topography, terrain, or the historical conquest. The allotment is *idealized*, not historical.

The pivotal scholarly resource is again Jon Levenson's *Theology of the Program of Restoration of Ezekiel 40–48* (1976). Levenson reads chapter 48 as the spatial culmination of the program: the *terumah* of chapter 45, the sanctuary of chapters 40–43, the river of chapter 47, all are now placed in their final canonical position in the territorial geometry. The priests (the sons of Zadok, 48:11) receive the most-holy portion adjoining the sanctuary; the Levites receive the parallel portion (48:13–14); the city sits on a 5,000-cubit strip; the prince's lands flank east and west (48:21–22); and the twelve tribes occupy parallel horizontal bands. The geography is theology: distance from the sanctuary maps onto degrees of priestly access, and the symmetry encodes the Ezekielian principle that holiness is graded but the people are equal — every tribe receives "one portion" (*ehad*, אֶחָד, repeated thirteen times in the chapter).

Kalinda Stevenson's *The Vision of Transformation* reads 48 as the climax of Ezekiel's territorial rhetoric: the prophet who began with the *kavod* leaving the temple now ends with the city's permanent presence-name. Steven Tuell's *The Law of the Temple* situates the chapter in the Persian-period theological imagination as a constitutional draft that informed without literally directing the Yehud province's emerging polity. Tuell argues that the chapter's idealized geography signals its character as a theological program rather than a literal blueprint: the Persian-period restoration could not actually rearrange Israel's tribes into east-west bands across the rift valley, and the visionary knew it.

Three older Israelite traditions stand behind the allotment. First, the Joshua 13–19 tribal allotments — the historical conquest geography — are deliberately reorganized; Ezekiel's land is the historical land transfigured. Second, the priestly traditions of Numbers 18 and 35, in which Levi has no land but receives offerings and cities, are honored in 48:13–14, where the Levites receive a *terumah* portion adjoining the priestly portion. Third, the holiness gradient of the priestly source (Lev 1–16) is here made spatial: the priests live closest to the sanctuary, the Levites in the next ring, the city in the next, the tribes beyond. The *Mishnah Middot* preserves analogous holiness gradations of the Herodian temple complex; *Maimonides* in *Hilkhot Beit ha-Bechirah* codifies them as halakhically permanent. The *Temple Scroll* (11QTemple) from Qumran inherits the Ezekielian gradation but redraws it for its own visionary city. Tuell argues that all three traditions read Ezekiel 48 as constitutional rather than literal — a conceptual standard against which historical polities were measured.

The closing verse — "the LORD is there" — is the canonical key. Greenberg notes that the formula *YHWH Shammah* deliberately echoes the *kavod*'s departure in 11:23 (the glory ascending east of the city) and the *kavod*'s return in 43:1–5 (the glory entering through the east gate). The book's structural inclusion is the *kavod*'s journey: out, in vision, and home. The city's permanent name is the achievement of that journey.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter is built around three literary devices. First, *enumeration*: the formula "Adjoining the territory of X, from the east side to the west, Y, one portion" is repeated twelve times (48:1–7, 23–28), producing a relentless metronomic rhythm. The Hebrew *peah ehat* (פֵּאָה אֶחָת, "one portion") at the close of each tribal verse is the chapter's drumbeat. The cumulative effect is geometric chant.

Second, *symmetry*. The seven tribes north of the *terumah* are arranged in a sequence — Dan (handmaid), Asher (handmaid), Naphtali (handmaid), Manasseh (concubine-descended via Joseph's son), Ephraim (similar), Reuben (Leah's eldest), Judah (Leah's fourth, but messianically central) — that places the more "marginal" tribes at the northern extremity and Judah immediately north of the holy center. South of the holy center: Benjamin (Rachel, beloved younger), Simeon (Leah's second), Issachar (Leah's ninth), Zebulun (Leah's tenth), and Gad (handmaid). The arrangement does not follow Joshua's geography or birth order or any single principle uniformly, but Block argues it reflects a deliberate liturgical ordering that places Judah and Benjamin (the southern kingdom's tribes) immediately flanking the sanctuary.

Third, the closing *city-gate enumeration* (48:30–34) lists three gates per side, named for the twelve tribes. Levi, who received no tribal allotment in the territorial plan (Levi has the *terumah* not a tribal band), receives a city gate; conversely, Joseph receives one gate (not two as in the tribal allotment, where Manasseh and Ephraim divide Joseph's portion). The gate list thus restores a symmetrical twelve at the urban scale. Block reads this as a deliberate canonical signal: the sanctuary's territorial geometry is hierarchical and graded, but the city's gates are equal.

The closing inclusion of the book — the *kavod* leaves the city in 11:23 and is given back the city in 48:35 — is the literary device that overarches the entire 48-chapter book. The departing glory of chapters 8–11, the desolation oracles, the exilic laments, the foreign-nations oracles, the dry-bones vision, the Gog oracles, the temple vision, the river — all are working toward the closing two-word Hebrew name: *YHWH Shammah*. The book ends, literally, with God's presence as the city's name.

Textually, 48 is among the more stable chapters in the closing vision. The LXX preserves substantially the same text with minor toponymic variants. The closing verse — "the name of the city from that time on shall be, The LORD Is There" — is identical in the MT and the LXX (rendered *kyriou ekei*, κυρίου ἐκεῖ), with the Hebrew preposition *shammah* (שָׁמָּה, the directional/locative form, "thither" or "there") preserved as the more archaic form rather than the simpler *sham* (שָׁם). Greenberg argues that the directional form deliberately invokes the original abandonment: the *kavod* went *thither*, eastward, in 11:23, and the city is now named after the destination of return.

## Lens 3 — Larger Story

In rabbinic tradition, Ezekiel 48 is treated alongside the rest of the closing vision as the messianic blueprint. *Bavli Bava Batra* 122a discusses the future allotment of the land in the messianic age and treats Ezekiel 48 as the operative text. Maimonides, in *Hilkhot Melachim* 11 and 12, codifies the closing vision as the messianic future: the Third Temple will follow Ezekiel's plan, the tribal allotments will follow Ezekiel 48, and the messianic king (the Ezekielian *nasi*) will reign under Torah. The name *YHWH Shammah* became central to the rabbinic *Shekinah* tradition: the divine presence that abandoned the First Temple, that hovered without entering the Second, will permanently dwell in the third. *Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer* and *Bereshit Rabbah* repeatedly invoke the name as the eschatological reversal of the divine departure. Levenson, in *Sinai and Zion* and in his Ezekiel monograph, treats the *YHWH Shammah* tradition as the theological taproot of subsequent Jewish presence-theology.

In Christian readings, the chapter has generated three major interpretive families. *Dispensational* readers (J. Dwight Pentecost, *Things to Come*; Charles Feinberg, *The Prophecy of Ezekiel*; Charles Dyer; Walvoord) read 48 as describing a literal future millennial restoration — the twelve tribes literally re-allotted, the temple literally rebuilt on Ezekiel's plan, the prince literally enthroned under Christ. *Amillennial / typological* readers (Patrick Fairbairn; Iain Duguid; Daniel Block himself, despite his historical-critical training) treat 48 as eschatological-symbolic: the symmetry of the allotment is the eschatological perfection of the people of God under Christ, the city's gates named for the tribes anticipate the New Jerusalem's twelve gates of Revelation 21:12, and *YHWH Shammah* is fulfilled in the Incarnation and consummated in the eschaton. *Critical-historical* readers (Tuell, Stevenson, the consensus of contemporary scholarship) treat the chapter as a Persian-period theological program that informed but was not implemented in the post-exilic restoration.

The intertextual debt of Revelation 21 to Ezekiel 48 is profound and deliberate. Revelation 21:12: "It had a great, high wall, with twelve gates, and at the gates twelve angels, and on the gates the names of the twelve tribes of the sons of Israel were inscribed." Revelation 21:16: "the city lies foursquare, its length the same as its width." The directly Ezekielian features are the twelve tribal gates, the foursquare layout (Ezek 48:16, 20, 30–34), and the implicit measuring activity (Rev 21:15–17 echoes Ezek 40–48's measuring man). But Revelation 21:22 introduces a striking transformation: "And I saw no temple in the city, for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb." Where Ezekiel's city *contains* the temple at its center, John's city *is* the temple — or, rather, the temple has been so fully realized in Christ that no separate building remains. Bauckham, in *The Climax of Prophecy*, treats this as the climactic transformation of the Ezekielian program: the city named "The LORD Is There" becomes the city in which the LORD is everywhere, with no architectural mediation needed.

The *YHWH Shammah* tradition has shaped four streams of presence-theology. Catholic and Orthodox sacramental presence theology — the *real presence* in the Eucharist (Trent, Session 13; the Orthodox doctrine of *epiclesis*) — reads the closing name as the canonical warrant for the church as the locus of divine dwelling, with Ephesians 2:19–22 ("being built together into a dwelling place for God by the Spirit") as the apostolic interpretation. Reformed Word-and-Spirit dwelling (Calvin, *Institutes* 1.13.6 on the Spirit; *Institutes* 4.17 on the Lord's Supper as real but spiritual presence) reads *YHWH Shammah* as fulfilled in the Spirit's indwelling of the church. Anabaptist *gathered church* theology (Menno Simons; the Schleitheim Confession) treats the gathered congregation as the present locus of *YHWH Shammah*. Jewish *Shekinah* tradition treats the divine indwelling as a permanent expectation realized partially now and fully in the messianic age. The Christian eschatological reading (Rev 21:3 — "the dwelling place of God is with man, and he will dwell with them") treats *YHWH Shammah* as anticipated now (in Christ, in the Spirit, in the church) and consummated then (in the New Jerusalem).

The Genesis-to-Revelation arc of divine presence is the canonical scaffolding behind chapter 48. Genesis 3:8 portrays "the LORD God walking in the garden in the cool of the day" — God dwelling with humanity in unmediated familiarity. The exile from Eden inaugurates the long story of presence-mediated. The patriarchal theophanies, the burning bush, the Sinai cloud, the tabernacle, the Solomonic temple — all are stages of presence-mediated. The exile of the *kavod* in Ezekiel 8–11 is the climax of presence-withdrawn. The return of the *kavod* in Ezekiel 43 is the inaugurated reversal. *YHWH Shammah* is the prophetic pledge. The Incarnation — "the Word became flesh and dwelt (*eskenosen*, "tabernacled") among us" (Jn 1:14) — is the inaugurated fulfillment. Pentecost is the Spirit's poured presence. Revelation 21:3 — "the dwelling place of God is with man" — is the consummation. Block, in NICOT, calls the *YHWH Shammah* of Ezekiel 48:35 "the canonical pivot," the moment when the prophetic promise of presence becomes the irrevocable name of the eschatological city.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

**Tension 1: idealized symmetry against historical messiness.** The east-west bands of Ezekiel 48 are mathematically perfect, geographically impossible, and spiritually programmatic. Does Ezekiel describe a *literal future restoration* (the dispensational reading), an *eschatological-symbolic* vision of perfected community (the amillennial-typological reading), or a *post-exilic theological program* that informed without literally directing the Yehud restoration (the critical reading)? To steelman the dispensational reading: the chapter's specificity is striking. Naming twelve tribes by name, listing twelve gates by name, locating the prince's lands precisely between Judah and Benjamin, dimensioning the holy district at 25,000 cubits square — these are not the vague gestures of typological poetry but the cadastral specifications of an actual map. Pentecost and Feinberg argue that the only honest reading of such specificity is literal; if Ezekiel is symbolic here, biblical literalism collapses everywhere. The amillennial counter-argument (Block, Duguid) is that the symbolism is *built into* the specificity: the very impossibility of fitting east-west bands across the Jordan rift signals a non-literal intent. The critical position threads the needle: the chapter is a Persian-period constitutional program, neither literally enacted nor purely typological, generating the conceptual standard for restoration.

**Tension 2: *YHWH Shammah* as the book's culmination.** What does it mean that Ezekiel ends with a *name*? The Catholic and Orthodox sacramental tradition reads *YHWH Shammah* as the canonical warrant for the church-as-temple — Christ really present in the Eucharist, the Spirit really indwelling the congregation, Ephesians 2:19–22 as the apostolic gloss. The Reformed tradition reads it as the canonical warrant for Word-and-Spirit dwelling: *YHWH Shammah* is fulfilled in the Spirit's indwelling of the believer (1 Cor 3:16) and the gathered church (1 Cor 6:19), with Calvin's *Institutes* 4.17 carefully distinguishing real spiritual presence from physical localization. Anabaptist tradition reads it as the warrant for the gathered-church model — wherever two or three are gathered, the city's name applies. Jewish *Shekinah* tradition treats the name as the permanent eschatological pledge that the divine presence, withdrawn in 587 BC, will return permanently in the messianic age. The Christian eschatological reading harmonizes Revelation 21:22 with Ezekiel 48:35: the city named "The LORD Is There" is the city in which "the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple" — the prophetic promise of presence becomes the consummated reality of unmediated dwelling.

**Unresolved.** If the *kavod* left in chapters 8–11 and returned only in vision, what does it mean to live in the *between* — between Pentecost's down-payment of the Spirit (the *arrabon*, ἀρραβών, of Eph 1:14, 2 Cor 1:22, 5:5) and the eschatological *YHWH Shammah* of the city's permanent name? The church confesses, on the Reformed and Catholic and Orthodox readings alike, that the Spirit truly indwells the gathered people now; yet Paul also says we "groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies" (Rom 8:23). Augustine, in *City of God* 19, names the *between* as the *peregrinatio* — the church's pilgrimage between inaugurated and consummated dwelling. Calvin's *Institutes* 3.25 names it the *expectation*. The Anabaptist tradition lives the *between* as gathered church amid worldly exile. The Jewish tradition lives it as ongoing *galut* (exile) under partial *Shekinah*. Ezekiel 48 does not resolve the question; it pledges the resolution. The book ends with a name and a promise, and the canonical reader is left in the long *between* that the name has not yet finished filling.

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references illuminate chapter 48. First, **Genesis 11:1–9, the tower of Babel**: the city humans build for themselves, with a name to "make a name for ourselves" (Gen 11:4), is the canonical antitype of Ezekiel's city, whose name is given by God: *YHWH Shammah*. Babel's self-naming city is scattered; Ezekiel's God-named city is gathered. The whole canonical project of city-theology — from Babel through Babylon to the New Jerusalem — runs through these two named cities. Second, **Matthew 1:23**, the angel's announcement of *Immanuel* (עִמָּנוּאֵל, "God with us"): Matthew's gospel opens with the very name that Ezekiel's book closes with, and the New Testament's first announcement is that the Ezekielian pledge is now incarnate. Third, **Revelation 22:3–4**, the consummation: "his servants will worship him; they will see his face, and his name will be on their foreheads." The closing of the canonical Bible inverts and fulfills the closing of Ezekiel: in Ezekiel, *the city* bears the name "The LORD Is There"; in Revelation, *the people* bear his name on their foreheads. The thread these three texts share is that the canonical Bible is, from beginning to end, a story of names — names humans give themselves, names God gives, names exchanged in covenant — and Ezekiel's closing two-word Hebrew name *YHWH Shammah* is the prophetic fulcrum on which the whole arc turns: God will be there, and his there will be everywhere.
