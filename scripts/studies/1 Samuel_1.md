# 1 Samuel 1: The Woman Whose Lips Moved Without Sound

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The opening of 1 Samuel sets us in the closing decades of the Iron I period (roughly the late twelfth through mid-eleventh centuries BCE), in the hill country of Ephraim, where Israelite settlement consisted of small unwalled villages clustered around terraced agriculture and the occasional regional sanctuary. The political situation was the tribal league at its breaking point. Judges had ended in fragmented violence ("in those days there was no king in Israel"), Philistine pressure was intensifying along the coastal plain, and the central sanctuary at Shiloh — perhaps twenty miles north of Jerusalem — was the closest thing the highland tribes had to a unifying institution. Israel Finkelstein's excavations at Khirbet Seilun have shown that Iron I Shiloh was a substantial cult center with monumental architecture and large pillared storehouses, destroyed violently in the mid-eleventh century BCE. That destruction is part of the silent backdrop to all of 1 Samuel: the sanctuary where Hannah weeps will not exist by the time her son is old. Jeremiah will later invoke Shiloh's ruin as a warning to Jerusalem (Jer. 7:12-14, 26:6-9).

The material culture of the chapter is dense. Elkanah is from Ramathaim-zophim, a doublet name perhaps preserving an Aramaized form, and he is identified as an Ephrathite — a clan term that elsewhere attaches to Bethlehem (and thus, eventually, to David). His annual pilgrimage with two wives reflects the sacrificial economy of the period: the family travels to a regional shrine, slaughters a peace offering (*zevach shelamim*), and the meat is distributed as portions (*manot*) to household members. The "double portion" Hannah receives is the *manah achat appayim* of v. 5 — literally "one portion of two faces" or possibly "one portion of the nostrils," a notoriously difficult phrase that the LXX, MT, and 4QSam-a render in incompatible ways. McCarter (Anchor Bible) follows 4QSam-a in reconstructing a reading that emphasizes Hannah's distress; Tsumura (NICOT) defends a more traditional rendering. Either way, the household economy of meat-sharing is the social engine that makes Peninnah's provocation public and humiliating.

The vow Hannah makes is a Nazirite vow imposed on the unborn child: "no razor shall touch his head" (v. 11). 4QSam-a expands the formula to include "and he shall not drink wine or strong drink" — a fuller Nazirite formula matching Numbers 6 — which the MT lacks. This is one of dozens of cases in 1-2 Samuel where the Qumran witnesses preserve a longer text closer to the LXX Vorlage. The classic studies by Frank Cross, Eugene Ulrich, and Emanuel Tov have made the textual situation of Samuel a paradigmatic case: the MT here is shorter, frequently corrupt, and any serious reading of the book must engage three witnesses simultaneously.

A few Hebrew terms repay attention. **חַנָּה** (*Channah*, "Hannah") shares the root *ḥnn* — to show favor, to be gracious — and the chapter plays on it at every turn (Eli's blessing in v. 17, "the God of Israel grant your petition," uses *natan*, but the whole arc is about *ḥen*, divine favor toward the unfavored). **שָׁאוּל** (*sha'ul*, "asked-for") in v. 20 is the etymological pun on Samuel's name — "I have asked him from YHWH" — but the etymology actually fits the name *Sha'ul* (Saul) far better than *Shemu'el* (Samuel). This is the famous source-critical puzzle: many scholars (going back to Hugo Gressmann and Karl Budde, with sophisticated treatments by McCarter and Antony Campbell) have proposed that 1 Samuel 1 was originally a birth narrative for Saul, later transferred to Samuel as the editorial frame demoted Saul and elevated Samuel. The pun is too good and too specific to ignore. Finally, **הֵיכָל יְהוָה** (*heikhal YHWH*, "the temple of the LORD," v. 9) is striking: Shiloh is called a *heikhal* rather than the expected *mishkan* (tent) or *ohel mo'ed* (tent of meeting) — implying a permanent structure, which the archaeology supports. The terminology fights the later Pentateuchal insistence that there was no temple before Solomon.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre matters here, and the genre is **annunciation type-scene** — what Robert Alter, in *The Art of Biblical Narrative*, identified as the recurring biblical pattern in which a barren matriarch (Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, the mother of Samson, Hannah, the Shunammite, Elizabeth) receives a promised son who will become a deliverer. Alter's point is that recognizing the convention is the precondition for hearing the variation. Hannah's scene is the latest and most psychologically interior of the matriarchal type-scenes in the Hebrew Bible. Sarah laughs from inside a tent, eavesdropping. Rachel demands children of Jacob with a near-suicidal "give me children, or I die." Hannah does something none of them does: she goes to the sanctuary alone, prays in silence, and bargains directly with God without male mediation. Yair Zakovitch and Moshe Garsiel both stress how Hannah's silent prayer is a literary innovation — the narrator gives us interior speech that is not externalized, which is rare in Hebrew narrative.

The chapter is structured by a series of misreadings. Elkanah misreads Hannah's grief ("am I not more to you than ten sons?" — perhaps the most tone-deaf line in the Hebrew Bible). Peninnah misreads Hannah's barrenness as fair game for cruelty. Eli misreads Hannah's silent lips as drunkenness — the priest who cannot tell prayer from intoxication is a damning character note that prepares the indictment of his house in chs. 2-4. Adele Berlin and Shimon Bar-Efrat have both observed how the narrator uses these triple misreadings to isolate Hannah: every human reader fails her, and only YHWH "remembers" (v. 19, *vayizkereha*) — the same verb used of YHWH remembering Noah, Abraham, and the enslaved in Egypt. Hannah is being placed in the company of the founding figures whose memory God preserves.

The literary placement of the chapter is programmatic. Polzin's *Samuel and the Deuteronomist* reads chs. 1-3 as an extended overture: the corruption of Eli's house, the silence of the divine word, the rise of a faithful prophet. Hannah's barrenness mirrors Israel's: the nation has lost prophetic voice, the priesthood is rotten, and only the cry of an outsider — a woman the priest mistook for a drunk — pries open a new chapter. Jan Fokkelman's massive *Narrative Art and Poetry in the Books of Samuel* notices that the chapter forms an inclusio with the weaning scene at the end: Hannah leaves Shiloh barren, returns with a son, and the verb of giving (*natan*) and asking (*sha'al*) shuttles between her and YHWH until the child is "lent" — *sha'ul* — back. The whole chapter is a chiastic exchange organized around a single root.

Textual variants in this chapter are not minor. The MT of 1:23 has Elkanah say "may the LORD establish *his word*" — but 4QSam-a and the LXX both read "may the LORD establish *what comes out of your mouth*," meaning Hannah's vow. McCarter, Klein (WBC), and most modern critical scholars accept the longer reading: Elkanah is endorsing Hannah's vow, not invoking some prior divine promise that the text never records. The MT here looks like a haplography or a theologically motivated softening. Similarly, 1:24 — MT says "a three-year-old bull" (*par meshullash*), 4QSam-a and LXX say "a three-year-old bull," but with different number agreements that affect whether one or three bulls are sacrificed. This is the Samuel textual situation in miniature: every other verse a meaningful divergence, no single witness reliable.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical arc, 1 Samuel 1 begins the transition from the chaos of Judges to the monarchy. Hannah's son will anoint Saul and David. Her song in chapter 2 will preview the royal-eschatological theology that culminates in 2 Samuel 7 and ripples into the Psalms and the prophets. But the canonical placement disguises a more interesting interpretive history.

**Jewish readings.** Josephus, in *Antiquities* 5.10, smooths Hannah's silent prayer into something more decorous and removes the more humiliating details of Peninnah's provocation. The rabbis of Berakhot 31a-31b famously made Hannah's prayer the *halakhic* template for all Jewish prayer: from her silent moving lips comes the rule that the *Amidah* must be said quietly, with lips moving but voice unheard. From her bargaining tone comes the legitimacy of demanding things of God. The Talmud reads her prayer as bordering on insolence — she essentially threatens God — and lets her get away with it, because grief gives her standing. Pesikta de-Rav Kahana 26 expands her into a model of righteous suffering. Rashi follows the rabbinic tradition closely; Radak (David Kimhi) is more attentive to the Hebrew oddities and notes the Saul-Samuel etymology problem with characteristic restraint. Ralbag (Gersonides), reading philosophically, treats the chapter as a study in how providence works through ordinary causation. **Abarbanel**, the great fifteenth-century Iberian commentator, reads the whole opening of Samuel as a critique of how easily Israel substitutes ritual access (Eli's sanctuary) for actual relationship to God — a remarkably modern political-theological reading. Avivah Zornberg, in our own century, gives Hannah a psychoanalytic depth: the silent prayer is the voice that has been so long suppressed that it no longer needs to be externalized.

**Christian readings.** The patristic and medieval Christian tradition was preoccupied with reading Hannah typologically as a figure of Mary and the Church. Origen, in his fragmentary homilies on Samuel, treats Hannah's prayer as the soul's interior turn toward God; Gregory the Great in the *Moralia* uses her grief as the model for compunction. Augustine in *City of God* 17 reads Hannah's song (which we will get to in ch. 2) as the first overtly messianic prophecy in the Former Prophets, and Hannah herself as the type of the Church bearing a son who will judge. Chrysostom's homilies on Hannah — five surviving sermons — are pastoral masterpieces that treat her as the ideal of female piety. Calvin's sermons on 1-2 Samuel (a substantial, undertranslated body of work — the Banner of Truth edition is partial) read Hannah with characteristic rigor: her vow is legitimate, her grief is real, and Eli's misreading is a warning to all clergy who assume they can diagnose souls by appearance. Luther's lectures on Samuel are sparser but emphasize Hannah's faith as exemplary — *sola fide* enacted before Israel had a king.

The critical tradition fragments the chapter. Wellhausen treated 1 Samuel 1 as a relatively late composition retrojecting later piety; Noth folded it into the deuteronomistic frame; Cross and McCarter have given it a more conservative dating, taking the textual evidence of 4QSam-a seriously and treating the Saul-Samuel pun as evidence of an originally separate Saulide birth narrative. McKenzie and Halpern read the chapter as part of the tendentious editorial program that retrofits a Samuel-centered prophetic legitimacy onto the monarchy.

**Feminist readings** have done the most fertile recent work here. Phyllis Trible's early essays and Cheryl Exum's *Fragmented Women* both treat Hannah as a rare case where biblical narrative gives a woman interiority and agency without immediately punishing her. Esther Fuchs has been more critical, arguing that Hannah's story still subordinates female experience to the production of a male hero. Carol Meyers, working from social-historical evidence about Israelite women's religious lives, has shown how unusual it is to find a woman praying alone at a sanctuary in this period — Hannah is doing something most Israelite women did not do, and the narrator notices. Tikva Frymer-Kensky in *Reading the Women of the Bible* gives Hannah perhaps the most generous reading: she is the first person in the Bible to address God in spontaneous personal prayer, without a script.

Where do the traditions converge? Almost universally, on the silent prayer as a turning point in religious history — Jewish and Christian readers alike treat Hannah as the inventor of inward, voluntary, personal prayer. Where do they diverge? On what to make of the priest who got it wrong. Jewish readings tend to make Eli pitiable; Christian readings tend to make him a warning; critical readings tend to make him programmatic — the priesthood was already failing before Samuel arrived.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author believe God was doing here? Two genuine tensions surface.

**Tension 1: The closed womb.** Verse 5 says plainly "the LORD had closed her womb." Verse 6 says it again. The narrator wants us to know this is not biology but providence. But why? The Catholic tradition, following Augustine, tends to read closed wombs as occasions for divine display — the barrenness exists so that the deliverance can be unmistakable. The Reformed tradition (Calvin, and contemporary Reformed readers like John Goldingay in his *Old Testament Theology*) reads it as a more troubling statement about divine sovereignty over suffering: God genuinely afflicted this woman in order to set up the answer. Orthodox readings, drawing on Chrysostom, tend to soften the causation — God permits, does not will, the barrenness. Jewish readings vary: the rabbis in Berakhot 31b read Hannah's barrenness as a deliberate divine provocation to elicit her prayer (she would not have prayed otherwise), which gives the suffering a pedagogical purpose but raises uncomfortable questions about a God who afflicts to instruct. Critical readings (Brueggemann's *First and Second Samuel* is sharpest here) accept the theological scandal: the narrator believes God closes wombs and opens them, and the modern reader has to either accept that or read against the grain. The position I find least intuitive — and which I think is actually the strongest — is the rabbinic one: that God afflicted Hannah to draw out a prayer that became a paradigm. It is monstrous if true, but it takes the text at its word and refuses to soften the divine character. Where this leaves us: a God who is described as the agent of a woman's worst suffering and her greatest joy, with no narrative apology offered for either.

**Tension 2: The vow that bargains with God.** Hannah strikes a deal — give me a son, I give him back. Catholic moral theology has historically been comfortable with vows as legitimate religious acts (Aquinas treats them in *Summa* II-II q.88 as acts of *latria*); Protestant reformers were more nervous, with Calvin warning against rash vows even while accepting Hannah's as paradigmatic. Orthodox spirituality, particularly in the monastic tradition, treats Hannah's vow as a model of self-offering — the child she gives up is the deepest possible offering. Jewish tradition is famously cautious about vows (the entire Yom Kippur liturgy of *Kol Nidrei* is about annulling them) but reads Hannah's as exemplary precisely because she keeps it without flinching. The critical reading, sharpened by feminist scholars, asks the harder question: what does it mean that the price of Hannah's deliverance is the loss of her child? Carol Meyers and Frymer-Kensky both note that Hannah pays a price no man in the chapter pays. Where this leaves us: a portrait of bargaining prayer in which the bargain is real, the cost is real, and the text neither celebrates nor critiques it — it simply reports.

One unresolved question to leave with: if Hannah had not vowed away her son, would the LORD have remembered her?

## The Thread

When you get to **Luke 1:46-55**, remember Hannah's song — but more importantly remember Hannah's silent prayer. Mary's *Magnificat* is the obvious echo of Hannah's hymn, but the deeper echo is structural: a woman, alone, addressed by God outside the regular channels of priestly authority, becoming the hinge of a new era. **Genesis 30:22** ("God remembered Rachel") uses the same verb and the same plot beat — keep an ear out for the verb *zakhar* across the matriarchal narratives; it always marks the turn from suffering to deliverance. **Judges 13** (the annunciation of Samson) is the immediately preceding annunciation type-scene and the closest formal parallel — but notice what Hannah has that Samson's mother lacks: a name, a voice, and a prayer of her own. And when you get to **1 Kings 8:22-53**, Solomon's prayer at the temple dedication — notice that Solomon stands at the *heikhal* and prays with his hands lifted; Hannah sat in the *heikhal* and prayed with her lips alone. The text remembers her posture and inverts it. The first prayer in the new sanctuary echoes the silent prayer in the old one.
