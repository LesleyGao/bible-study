# Acts 8: Samaritan Spirit, Simon's Money, and the Eunuch's Pool

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The persecution that scatters the Jerusalem church in 8:1 takes place in roughly 33–35 CE, immediately following Stephen's execution. The selectivity of the persecution — "they were all scattered throughout the regions of Judea and Samaria, except the apostles" — is one of the most diagnostic historical details in Acts. Hengel, Bruce, and Schnabel read the asymmetry as evidence that the persecution targeted specifically the **Hellenist** (Greek-speaking) circle around Stephen, whose temple-critical theology had drawn the temple authorities' attention, while leaving the Aramaic-speaking Hebraist circle around the Twelve, who continued to attend the temple, comparatively undisturbed. Haenchen drew the same conclusion more sharply: Luke's "except the apostles" is a Lukan smoothing of a fact the Hellenist source-tradition would have stated more harshly — the apostles stayed because the persecution was not aimed at them. The asymmetry confirms that the Hellenist-Hebraist divide of chapter 6 was *theological*, not just linguistic.

The geography of the chapter is the second stage of Acts 1:8 — Judea and Samaria. **Samaria** as a political-religious entity in the first century is the long shadow of the Assyrian destruction of the Northern Kingdom in 722 BCE and the subsequent settlement of Mesopotamian peoples on the land (2 Kings 17), the building of the rival Samaritan temple on **Mount Gerizim** in the late Persian or early Hellenistic period, and the destruction of that temple by the Hasmonean John Hyrcanus in **128 BCE**. **Reinhard Pummer's** *The Samaritans: A Profile* and **Magnar Kartveit's** *The Origin of the Samaritans* are the foundational reference works on this background, and Yitzhak Magen's archaeological excavations at Mount Gerizim from 1983 onward have confirmed both the scale of the Samaritan temple precinct and the Hasmonean destruction layer. The Samaritan Pentateuch tradition — preserving a Hebrew Pentateuch text-form distinct from both the proto-Masoretic and the Septuagint Vorlage, with significant theological variants (most famously the tenth-commandment insertion legitimizing Mount Gerizim as the divinely chosen sanctuary) — frames the Samaritan religious identity Philip is preaching into. By the first century, Samaritans and Judeans were mutually hostile but recognizably co-religionist (both held the Pentateuch as scripture, both practiced circumcision, both rejected the rest of the Hebrew Bible's prophetic-historical canon as Samaritans construed it), making the Samaritan mission an intra-Israelite extension before it is anything else.

The Ethiopian eunuch's geography is equally specific. "Ethiopia" in Greco-Roman usage typically denotes the **Kingdom of Meroë** (also called Kush or the Kushite Empire), a Nubian state on the Upper Nile centered at the city of Meroë (modern Sudan, north of Khartoum), which flourished from roughly 300 BCE to 350 CE. **László Török's** *The Kingdom of Kush* is the standard reference. The detail that the official serves "**Candace**, queen of the Ethiopians" reflects accurately that *Candace* (Greek *Kandakē*) was a Meroitic dynastic title for queen-mothers (not a personal name), attested in Greek and Latin sources from Strabo to Pliny and corroborated by Meroitic inscriptions and by the pyramidal royal tombs at Meroë and Begarawiyah. The eunuch's role "in charge of all her treasure" places him as a senior court financial official; **Brittany Wilson's** *Unmanly Men: Refigurations of Masculinity in Luke-Acts* situates the eunuch within the broader Greco-Roman category of court eunuchs as trusted royal servants whose physical alteration enabled their access to royal women's quarters and treasuries.

Five Greek terms structure the chapter. ***Mageia*** (μαγεία, 8:11) and ***mageuōn*** (μαγεύων, 8:9) — "magic," "practicing magic" — denote a category of Greco-Roman ritual specialist that overlapped with but was distinct from priestly, philosophical, and prophetic roles; the term carried both technical and pejorative valences. ***He dynamis tou theou hē kaloumenē megalē*** (ἡ δύναμις τοῦ θεοῦ ἡ καλουμένη μεγάλη, 8:10) — "the Power of God that is called Great" — is a divine title attested in Samaritan and Hellenistic-syncretic sources for an emanation or hypostasis of the high God. ***Argyrion*** (ἀργύριον, 8:20) — "silver," "money" — bears the weight of Peter's curse, "may your silver perish with you." ***Eunouchos*** (εὐνοῦχος, 8:27, 8:34, 8:36, 8:38, 8:39) — "eunuch" — repeats five times in fourteen verses, foregrounding the figure's identity at exactly the moment Philip refuses the Deuteronomic exclusion (Deut 23:1) in favor of the Isaianic inclusion (Isa 56:3–5). ***Ti kōlyei me baptisthēnai*** (τί κωλύει με βαπτισθῆναι, 8:36) — "what prevents me from being baptized" — uses the same verb (*kōluō*) Peter will use at 10:47 of Cornelius and 11:17 of the Spirit's gift to Gentiles, programmatic for the Lukan refusal of barriers to baptism.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 8 is structured as the first Lukan-narrative implementation of Acts 1:8 stage 2 — the Gospel moves from Jerusalem into Judea and Samaria. The chapter's two halves (Philip in Samaria, 8:4–25; Philip and the eunuch, 8:26–40) are bracketed by Saul's persecution (8:1–3) and Saul's continued threat (continued in 9:1), making chapter 8 a structural pause in the Saul-narrative that introduces Philip — one of the Seven from chapter 6 — as the second Hellenist protagonist after Stephen. **Tannehill's** *Narrative Unity* notes that Philip's role here mirrors Stephen's: a Hellenist deacon-evangelist whose theological trajectory pushes the church past the temple-cult frame. Philip will reappear at 21:8 as "Philip the evangelist, one of the Seven," now resident in Caesarea with four prophesying daughters — a programmatic Lukan signature that the Samaritan and eunuch missions of chapter 8 produced a settled Hellenist-Christian community on the coastal plain.

Textual variants in chapter 8 are substantial. **Verse 37** — "And Philip said, 'If you believe with all your heart, you may.' And he replied, 'I believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God'" — is **absent from the earliest and best manuscripts** (P45, P74, Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Alexandrinus) and is preserved only in the Western text and later majority-text witnesses. The verse is almost certainly a second-century baptismal-creed insertion preserving early Christian baptismal liturgy, not original to Luke. Its absence is theologically significant: in Luke's original narrative, no confession of faith is recorded between the eunuch's question ("what prevents me?") and his baptism, sharpening the narrative's emphasis on the *removal of barriers* rather than the imposition of new requirements. **Pervo** (Hermeneia), **Metzger** in his *Textual Commentary*, and the NA28/UBS5 critical apparatus all treat 8:37 as inauthentic. Most modern English translations (NRSV, NIV, ESV) relegate it to a footnote.

The **Simon Magus** narrative (8:9–24) is one of the most theologically charged episodes in Acts. Luke's framing — Simon "previously practiced magic," "amazed the people of Samaria," was called "the Power of God that is called Great" — places Simon in a recognizable Greco-Roman category (the wandering wonder-worker with claims to divine status) but with specific Samaritan religious overtones. The post-canonical reception of Simon Magus is enormous: **Justin Martyr's** *First Apology* 26 (mid-second century) reports that Simon was honored as a god in Rome with a statue inscribed *Simoni Deo Sancto* (a claim Justin probably misread from a *Semoni Sanco Deo*, an inscription to the Sabine deity Semo Sancus); **Irenaeus's** *Against Heresies* 1.23 makes Simon the founder of all Gnosticism; the apocryphal *Acts of Peter* dramatize Simon's flying contest with Peter in Rome. Whatever the historical Simon was, the figure became the patristic prototype of the heretical-magician-claiming-divine-power, and the term **simony** entered Christian moral vocabulary as a permanent technical term for the sale or purchase of ecclesial offices, sacraments, or spiritual gifts. Medieval simony controversies — the **Simoniacal heresy** debates of the eleventh century, the reform programs of **Pope Gregory VII**, the **Council of Reims (1049)** — directly invoked Acts 8:20 as scriptural ground for prohibition.

The **Ethiopian eunuch** (8:26–40) is read by Luke as a programmatic figure in the chapter's second half. **Brittany Wilson's** *Unmanly Men* argues that the eunuch's gender-and-body status is doing real theological work — not merely incidental detail. Five repetitions of *eunouchos* in fourteen verses press the point. The Isaianic passage the eunuch is reading — Isa 53:7–8 in the LXX — is itself loaded: only a few chapters later in the same scroll (Isa 56:3–5), the eunuch is explicitly admitted to the eschatological community: "Let not the eunuch say, 'Behold, I am a dry tree.' For thus says the Lord: 'To the eunuchs who keep my Sabbaths... I will give in my house and within my walls a monument and a name better than sons and daughters.'" Philip's evangelism is doing what Isaiah promised — and the Deuteronomic exclusion (Deut 23:1, "no one whose testicles are crushed... shall enter the assembly of the Lord") is being deliberately set aside.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Jewish interpretation of the Samaritan and eunuch narratives in Acts 8 is shaped by two prior interpretive traditions. **Lawrence Schiffman's** *Who Was a Jew?* and the broader rabbinic literature on Samaritans (the Mishnaic and Talmudic *Kuthim* tractates) document the gradual rabbinic narrowing of Jewish identity that excluded Samaritans by late antiquity, even as first-century evidence is more porous. The eunuch's status in Jewish law turns on the tension between Deut 23:1's exclusion and Isa 56's promise of inclusion — a tension internal to the Hebrew canon that the rabbis would resolve in various directions. **Adele Reinhartz** and **Amy-Jill Levine** caution against reading Luke's Samaritan and eunuch narratives as simple Christian universalism overcoming Jewish particularism: the Samaritans Philip evangelizes are themselves a co-religionist community claiming Mosaic descent, and Isaiah 56's vision of eunuch-inclusion is *Jewish* prophecy. Luke's narrative is implementing an internal Jewish vision, not abolishing Jewish boundaries from outside.

The Christian interpretive trajectory begins with the Patristic identification of the Ethiopian eunuch as the founder of African Christianity. **Eusebius's** *Ecclesiastical History* 2.1 reports the eunuch as the first Gentile convert and the evangelist of Ethiopia. **Irenaeus's** *Against Heresies* 3.12.8 already reads the episode this way. The **Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church** preserves a foundational tradition that traces its apostolic origin to this eunuch, whom Ethiopian tradition names *Bachos* or *Qinaqis*. The historical reliability of this specific genealogy is debated — most modern historians date the establishment of Ethiopian Christianity to the fourth-century mission of Frumentius under King Ezana of Aksum — but the symbolic weight of Acts 8 in Ethiopian self-understanding is enormous. **Origen's** *Commentary on Romans* and **Jerome** both invoke the eunuch as a model convert. **John Chrysostom's** Homilies on Acts (Homily 19 covers the eunuch) reads the episode as the pattern of catechetical instruction: the eunuch is reading scripture, Philip explains it, baptism follows. **Bede** preserves this reading in *Expositio Actuum*. The medieval iconography of the eunuch — black-skinned, royally robed, in a chariot, holding a scroll — became a major motif in European church art, including Rembrandt's 1626 *The Baptism of the Eunuch*, an early canvas in which the painter situates the scene in a deliberately naturalistic landscape.

The **Acts 8:14–17 sacramental crux** — the Samaritans believed and were baptized but had not received the Spirit until Peter and John laid hands on them — became the dogmatic battleground between traditions. **The Catholic confirmation theology** (CCC 1285–1321) reads the laying-on of apostolic hands as a sacrament distinct from baptism, transmitting the gift of the Spirit through episcopal ministry; this reading was developed by **Cyprian** in the third century, codified by the medieval Western church, and reaffirmed at Trent. **Eastern Orthodox chrismation** preserves the substantive theological claim — there is a distinct seal of the Spirit in addition to baptism — while binding the two rites tightly together; **Alexander Schmemann's** *Of Water and the Spirit* gives the foundational Orthodox treatment. **Reformed theology** (Calvin's *Institutes* IV.19, the Westminster Confession) refuses the sacramental separation: the Spirit is operative in baptism, and Acts 8 records a unique transitional event in which the apostolic authentication of the Samaritan mission required a publicly visible Spirit-bestowal that does not establish a permanent two-stage initiation pattern. **Pentecostal subsequence theology** (preserved by **Roger Stronstad's** *Charismatic Theology of St. Luke* and **Robert Menzies's** *Empowered for Witness*) reads Acts 8 as one of several Lukan paradigms — alongside Acts 19's Ephesian disciples — for a Spirit-baptism distinct from regeneration, undergirding the classical Pentecostal doctrine of subsequence. **William Atkinson's** *Baptism in the Spirit* surveys the modern Pentecostal-charismatic debate. Among the major contemporary commentators, **Bruce** (NICNT) and **Schnabel** (ZECNT) hold the Reformed-transitional reading; **Fitzmyer** (Anchor Bible) the Catholic-sacramental reading; **Keener** (4 vols) gives the most extensive comparative treatment; **Witherington III** reads the episode as a unique apostolic-validation moment; **Pervo** and **Marguerat** read it as Lukan editorial patterning rather than dogmatic precedent.

Liberation and feminist scholarship have given the eunuch episode renewed attention. **Justo González's** *Acts (Belief)* treats the eunuch as a paradigmatic figure for the church's reach across racial, geographic, and gender-status boundaries. **Allan Boesak** in his South African writings invoked Acts 8 as scriptural ground for the church's anti-apartheid witness. **Brittany Wilson's** *Unmanly Men* and **Sean Burke's** *Queering the Ethiopian Eunuch* push contemporary readings of the eunuch as a figure outside the Greco-Roman gender-binary whose inclusion in the church refuses bodily-status purity codes. **Beverly Gaventa** treats the chapter as the second of three Lukan barrier-crossing narratives (Samaritan, eunuch, Cornelius) that programmatically dismantle exclusionary patterns. **Schüssler Fiorenza's** feminist hermeneutic asks why women converts (the "men and women" baptized at 8:12) are mentioned and then disappear from the narrative — Luke names Simon Magus and the eunuch but not the Samaritan women.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions structure the chapter. **First**, the **two-stage baptism-Spirit pattern** of 8:14–17. The **Catholic-sacramental reading** treats the laying-on of apostolic hands as a permanent sacramental institution distinct from baptism, scripturally grounded here. The **Reformed-transitional reading** treats it as a unique moment of apostolic authentication in which the Spirit's gift was *publicly* delayed to ratify the Samaritan mission to the Jerusalem leadership, not a normative pattern for subsequent church practice. The **Pentecostal-subsequence reading** treats it as one of the Lukan paradigms for a Spirit-baptism distinct from conversion-initiation. The steel-man for the Catholic reading is the consistency of episcopal-confirmation tradition from Cyprian onward and the apparent narrative weight Luke gives to the apostolic laying-on. The steel-man for the Reformed reading is that Acts elsewhere depicts the Spirit given at or before baptism (Cornelius in chapter 10, the Pauline pattern), and 8:14–17 functions specifically to *validate* the Samaritan mission to the Jerusalem leadership who had not seen it. The steel-man for the Pentecostal reading is that Luke does seem to distinguish between baptism in water and Spirit-reception across multiple narratives, and theological flattening loses something. **Where this leaves us:** Luke is recording an apostolic-validation event that became the seed of three legitimate but distinct sacramental trajectories. The narrative will not collapse into one tradition's doctrine without remainder. Each reading captures something the others miss — the Catholic captures the apostolic-sacramental dimension, the Reformed captures the transitional-validation dimension, the Pentecostal captures the experiential-pneumatic dimension.

**Second**, the **eunuch's ambiguous status** as first Gentile convert. The traditional reading (Eusebius, Irenaeus, Bede) treats the Ethiopian as the first Gentile convert, narratively prior to Cornelius in chapter 10. The **modern critical reading** (Bruce, Fitzmyer, Marshall, Schnabel) often distinguishes: the eunuch is a **God-fearer** or perhaps a **proselyte** (he had come to Jerusalem to worship, was reading Isaiah), so his conversion is in continuity with Jewish piety rather than the boundary-crossing event of Cornelius. The **Wilson/Burke reading** refuses the binary altogether: the eunuch is a figure whose identity (Ethiopian, eunuch, court official, scripture-reader) is irreducibly multiple, and the Lukan narrative is precisely about the church's encounter with such irreducibility. The steel-man for the traditional reading is that Luke makes the eunuch the climax of chapter 8 and the first major non-Jewish convert in the narrative. The steel-man for the modern critical reading is that Luke clearly sets up Cornelius in chapter 10 as the *programmatic* Gentile breakthrough requiring apostolic ratification, suggesting the eunuch was understood as a different category. **Where this leaves us:** the eunuch is a transitional figure whose conversion does not require apostolic ratification (Philip simply baptizes him, no question is raised) precisely because he is already within the orbit of Jewish worship. Cornelius's conversion *will* require ratification because he stands fully outside that orbit. Luke is staging a graduated narrative of barrier-crossing, and the eunuch is the figure who shows that the barriers within Israel's own traditions (Deut 23:1) are already being overcome from within (Isa 56) — preparing for the fuller Gentile question of chapter 10.

**Unresolved question**: was Philip's evangelism of the Samaritans and the eunuch authorized by the Twelve, or did the Hellenist circle's mission outrun apostolic sanction and force the apostles to ratify what Philip had already done? The Lukan narrative reads as ratification-after-the-fact (Peter and John arrive after the Samaritan baptisms; no apostolic figure is present at the eunuch's baptism), suggesting the Hellenist mission was already pushing the church's geographic and ethnic frontier independently of the Twelve.

## The Thread

Philip the evangelist of chapter 8 reappears at 21:8 as Luke's host in Caesarea, providing a narrative bookend for the Hellenist-coastal-plain mission. The "what prevents me from being baptized?" of 8:36 echoes forward into Peter's "can anyone withhold water for baptizing these people?" at 10:47 and "who was I that I could withstand God?" at 11:17 — Luke is constructing a programmatic barrier-removal pattern. The Samaritan mission's apostolic ratification at 8:14–17 anticipates the Jerusalem Council's ratification of the Gentile mission in chapter 15, establishing a Lukan pattern in which Hellenist initiative is followed by apostolic confirmation. The Ethiopian eunuch's reading of Isaiah 53 and Philip's exposition of it ("beginning with this Scripture he told him the good news about Jesus") is the narrative mirror of Luke 24:27, the Emmaus road exposition by the risen Christ — Luke is signaling that Christian preaching is a mode of scriptural reading inherited from Jesus himself. Finally, Saul's persecution at 8:1–3 will set up the conversion narrative of chapter 9 — Luke is keeping Saul on stage.
