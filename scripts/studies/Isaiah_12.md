# Isaiah 12 — The Song at the Well

Isaiah 12 is a brief and exquisite hymn — only six verses — that closes the opening movement of the book of Isaiah. After the towering visions of chs. 1-11, with their indictments, judgments, messianic promises, and peaceable-kingdom imagery, the prophet stops to sing. The chapter is the shortest in Isaiah and one of the most quietly important: it functions as a doxological hinge between the messianic-Davidic vision of ch. 11 and the oracles against the nations that begin in ch. 13. It also resurrects, almost line for line, the Song of the Sea from Exodus 15, claiming that the people of Israel will sing the same song again on the far side of a new Exodus.

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Isaiah 12 belongs to the literary block of First Isaiah's opening unit (chs. 1-12). Most scholars working in the diachronic tradition (Hans Wildberger, Joseph Blenkinsopp, John Goldingay, Marvin Sweeney) treat the chapter as a redactional conclusion to chs. 1-11, possibly composed late in the book's editorial history to provide a doxological seal on the messianic vision of ch. 11 before the oracles-against-the-nations section opens in ch. 13. The phrase *ba-yom ha-hu* (בַּיּוֹם הַהוּא, "in that day") in 12:1 and 12:4 echoes 11:10 and 11:11, tying ch. 12 explicitly to the eschatological horizon of the preceding chapter. Conservative-evangelical commentators (J. Alec Motyer, John Oswalt) treat the chapter as integral to the original Isaianic composition. Brevard Childs's canonical-criticism approach focuses less on dating and more on the chapter's literary function as a hinge in the canonical shape of Isaiah.

The historical world presupposed by ch. 12 is the same broad world of First Isaiah: Judah in the late 8th century BC, under Assyrian pressure, with the northern kingdom recently fallen (722 BC) and Jerusalem narrowly surviving. But the *narrative voice* of the chapter is not the prophet speaking to his contemporaries — it is the future redeemed people speaking after the messianic age has dawned. *You will say in that day* opens the chapter; the speakers are the people on the far side of God's deliverance, looking back. The historical anchor is 8th-century Judah; the rhetorical stance is eschatological.

The Hebrew of 12:2 is the chapter's most-quoted line: *hinneh El yeshu'ati evtach ve-lo efchad ki-ozzi ve-zimrat Yah Yahweh va-yehi-li li-shu'ah* (הִנֵּה אֵל יְשׁוּעָתִי אֶבְטַח וְלֹא אֶפְחָד כִּי־עָזִּי וְזִמְרָת יָהּ יְהוָה וַיְהִי־לִי לִישׁוּעָה). The clause *ki-ozzi ve-zimrat Yah va-yehi-li li-shu'ah* (for Yah is my strength and my song, and he has become my salvation) is a near-direct quotation of Exodus 15:2, the opening line of the Song of the Sea that Moses and Miriam sang on the far shore of the Red Sea after the Egyptians drowned. The same line appears again in Psalm 118:14. Three places in the canon — Exodus 15, Isaiah 12, Psalm 118 — share this exact formula, and the intertextual pattern is hard to miss: the line is a deliberate signature of Exodus deliverance, and Isaiah 12 reinvokes it to claim that what Yahweh did at the Red Sea, Yahweh will do again.

The phrase *ma'ayanei ha-yeshu'ah* (מַעַיְנֵי הַיְשׁוּעָה, the wells of salvation) in 12:3 is unique to this verse in the Hebrew Bible. The image is of joyful drawing of water from springs — *u-she'avtem mayim be-sasson mi-ma'ayanei ha-yeshu'ah*, "with joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation." This single line generated an entire stream of Jewish liturgical and ritual practice. The Mishnah (*Sukkah* 4:9-5:4) describes the *Simchat Beit ha-Sho'evah* (שִׂמְחַת בֵּית הַשּׁוֹאֵבָה, the Rejoicing of the House of Water-Drawing), the joyous water-libation ritual at the Temple during Sukkot, which the rabbis grounded in Isaiah 12:3. The Talmud (*b. Sukkah* 50b-51a) preserves the saying that *one who has not seen the rejoicing at the place of the water-drawing has never seen rejoicing in his life*.

The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran (1QIsa^a, ca. 125 BC) contains Isaiah 12 with only minor orthographic variants from the Masoretic Text — confirming the chapter's textual stability by the late Second Temple period.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels exist in the broad genre of royal-victory hymns, where a king (or his god) is praised after a military deliverance, but Isaiah 12 is distinct in placing the song in the mouth of the redeemed *people* rather than the king and in framing it as the future eschatological response to God's salvation rather than a present celebration of past victory.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Isaiah 12 is poetry, structured as two short hymnic units (vv. 1-3 and vv. 4-6), each opening with the formula *ve-amarta ba-yom ha-hu* (and you will say in that day). Verse 1 is singular *ve-amarta* (you, masculine singular, will say); verse 4 is plural *va-amartem* (you, plural, will say). The shift from singular to plural — from the individual worshiper to the gathered community — is a deliberate literary movement. The redeemed first speak as one, then together.

The first hymn (vv. 1-3) opens with a confession of God's turned-away anger: *odkha Yahweh ki-anafta bi yashov apkha u-tenachameni* (I will give thanks to you, Yahweh, for though you were angry with me, your anger turned away, that you might comfort me). The Hebrew root *nacham* (נחם, comfort) anticipates the great opening of Second Isaiah — *nachamu nachamu ammi*, "comfort, comfort my people" (40:1) — and thus already foreshadows the book's later movement.

Verse 2's *ki-ozzi ve-zimrat Yah* uses the short divine name *Yah* (יָהּ), the truncated form of YHWH that appears in *halleluyah* (praise Yah). The double divine name in the verse — *Yah* and *YHWH* — is a poetic intensification, doubling down on the identity of the savior.

The bridge verse (12:3) about drawing water from the wells of salvation moves from confession to action: salvation is something to be *drawn*, like water — sustaining, daily, joyful. The Hebrew *u-she'avtem* (and you will draw) places the action in the mouth of the future people; the Hebrew *be-sasson* (with joy) intensifies the affective tone.

The second hymn (vv. 4-6) shifts outward in scope. Verse 4 commands proclamation *ba-ammim* (among the peoples) and the making known of God's deeds *ba-aretz* (in all the earth, v. 5). The horizon expands from individual gratitude to international witness. The closing verse (12:6) addresses the *yoshevet Tziyyon* (the inhabitant of Zion, feminine singular — the city personified) and commands her to *tzahali va-ronni* (cry aloud and sing for joy), *ki-gadol be-kirbekh kedosh Yisrael* (for great in your midst is the Holy One of Israel). The climactic divine title *kedosh Yisrael* (קְדוֹשׁ יִשְׂרָאֵל, the Holy One of Israel) is one of Isaiah's signature names for God — appearing roughly twenty-six times in the book and forming a thematic refrain that ties the whole prophetic collection together.

The chapter's structure is an inversion of Israel's typical lament-form. A lament moves from cry to confession to plea; Isaiah 12 moves from thanksgiving to witness to praise. It is the *answer* to all the laments of chs. 1-11, placed proleptically on the lips of the redeemed.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the prophetic canon, Isaiah 12 belongs to the cluster of *new-Exodus* texts that run through the prophets — Isaiah 11:15-16, 35:8-10, 40:3-5, 43:16-21, 51:9-11, 52:11-12; Jeremiah 16:14-15 and 23:7-8; Hosea 2:14-15; Micah 7:15. The Exodus from Egypt is the paradigmatic salvation event of Israel's memory, and the prophets repeatedly reach back to it as the template for what God will do again. Isaiah 12 makes the link explicit by quoting the Song of the Sea: the future deliverance will warrant the same song.

In Jewish trajectory, Isaiah 12:3 — *with joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation* — became the textual root of one of the most beloved festivals of Second Temple Judaism. The Water-Drawing Festival of Sukkot (*Simchat Beit ha-Sho'evah*) involved a procession with water from the Pool of Siloam, a libation poured on the altar in the temple, and a night of music, dancing, and torch-lit celebration in the Court of the Women. The Mishnah and Talmud preserve detailed descriptions; the festival was so central to late Second Temple piety that the Talmud could say one who had not seen it had never seen rejoicing.

The festival's Sukkot setting is also the probable background for one of the most famous claims in the Gospel of John. In John 7:37-39, *on the last day of the feast, the great day, Jesus stood up and cried out, "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink. Whoever believes in me, as the Scripture has said, 'Out of his heart will flow rivers of living water.'"* The "last day" of Sukkot was the climactic day of the Water-Drawing Festival; commentators from the patristic period through modern Johannine scholarship (Raymond Brown, Andrew Lincoln, Craig Keener) have argued that Jesus's offer of *living water* deliberately invokes Isaiah 12:3 and the Sukkot ritual that grew out of it. The wells of salvation are now Christ.

In Christian trajectory, Isaiah 12 became a staple of the church's hymnody. Isaiah 12:2 is the source of the Latin canticle *Ecce Deus salvator meus* (Behold, God is my salvation), used in the Roman Breviary and in Anglican Morning Prayer. The chapter is included in the lectionary readings for Advent, Easter Vigil, and various other feasts. The Easter Vigil of the Western liturgical tradition reads Exodus 14-15 (the Red Sea crossing and the Song of the Sea) as one of the great vigil readings, with Isaiah 12 sometimes used as the responsorial canticle — joining the original Exodus song to its prophetic echo and to the resurrection.

Charles Wesley wrote a hymn paraphrasing Isaiah 12 (*Ye saints of God, his praises sing*); Frederick Faber's *I'll praise my Maker while I've breath* draws on its language. Handel did not set Isaiah 12 directly in *Messiah*, but the chapter's mood of post-deliverance praise saturates the work's third part.

The Christian reception generally reads Isaiah 12 as a song of the redeemed church on the far side of Christ's saving work — a song the church already sings in its present worship, anticipating the eschatological day when it will be sung in fullness. Augustine, in his commentary on the Psalms, treats Isaiah 12 alongside Psalm 118 (which shares the *the LORD is my strength and my song* line) as expressions of the unity of God's saving work across the testaments.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

Two tensions sit at the heart of Isaiah 12, and they are smaller than the tensions of ch. 11 — but they matter.

The first tension is **the chapter's anger-and-comfort theology**. Verse 1 opens with: *I will give thanks to you, O Yahweh, for though you were angry with me, your anger turned away, that you might comfort me.* The redeemed people thank God for the *removal* of his anger. Three positions are worth holding side by side.

The classical Christian reading (Augustine, Calvin, evangelicals) treats this as a confession of substitutionary or satisfactional theology: God's righteous anger against sin was real and had to be turned away; in Christ, the anger is propitiated and the comfort begins. Steelmanned: the verse explicitly frames the divine anger as past and the divine comfort as present; this fits the New Testament pattern of Christ's death as propitiation (Rom 3:25, 1 John 2:2, 4:10).

The mainstream-critical / progressive reading (Brueggemann, Goldingay) treats the language of God's anger and turned-away anger as relational rather than juridical. The anger is the disrupted relationship — God's grief at Israel's covenant failures — and the turning of anger is not a transaction but a reconciliation. Steelmanned: the Hebrew *anaf* (אָנַף, to be angry) is paired throughout the prophets with relational and covenantal language, not juridical-legal language; the *comfort* (*nacham*) of v. 1 is the same word used for personal consolation, not for legal exoneration.

The Jewish-traditional reading takes the verse as a model of *teshuvah* (return / repentance). The angered God turns away anger when the people return; the comfort flows from the restored covenant. Steelmanned: the framework is repentance and return, deeply rooted in the prophetic literature, and does not require a Christological reading.

The second tension is **the chapter's universalism**. Verse 4 commands the people to *make known among the peoples* God's deeds; verse 5 says the news should be made known *in all the earth*. Three positions.

The covenant-particularist reading takes the universalism as instrumental: Israel's role is to witness God's salvation *of Israel* to the nations, not to suggest that the nations themselves are direct objects of the same salvation. The nations hear; Israel is saved. This is one strand of rabbinic interpretation.

The universalist reading (favored by many Christian commentators and by inclusive strands of modern Jewish thought) takes the universalism more strongly: the wells of salvation are open, and the proclamation among the peoples is invitation, not mere announcement. Steelmanned by Isaiah's broader trajectory in chs. 19, 25, 56, and 66, where the nations come to Zion and are included in God's saving work.

The eschatological-deferred reading takes the universalism as reserved for the messianic age: in the present, the witness is partial; in the day of full salvation, the proclamation reaches everyone, and the response is universal. Steelmanned: this fits the *ba-yom ha-hu* framing of the chapter — the universalism is set in the future day, not in the present.

Honest reading also notices that the chapter is short, joyful, and almost suspiciously serene after the heavy theology of chs. 1-11. The chapter is a deliberate breath, a doxological exhale before the oracles against the nations begin in ch. 13. It is not naive — it sits inside a book that has already named human violence, divine wrath, and the long judgment that will fall on Babylon, Moab, Egypt, and the rest. The song of ch. 12 is sung *after* the judgment has done its work. The wells of salvation are reached only on the far side of the wilderness.

Where this leaves us: Isaiah 12 is the pause between the messianic vision of ch. 11 and the oracles of judgment that begin in ch. 13. It is the rehearsal of a song the people will sing one day; the prophet places it in advance, like a score handed out before the concert. The Exodus song will be sung again, but louder, with more singers, and with the nations as the audience. The wells of salvation are open, and there is a bucket waiting.

What does it mean to learn the song now, in advance of the day for which it is written?
