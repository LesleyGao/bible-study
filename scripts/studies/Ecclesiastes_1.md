# Ecclesiastes 1: The Wind on Its Circuits

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The book opens with a superscription that the consensus of critical scholarship reads as a literary fiction rather than a historical claim. "The words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem" (1:1) and the first-person royal claim of 1:12 — "I the Preacher have been king over Israel in Jerusalem" — establish a Solomonic *frame*, but the linguistic evidence places the composition centuries after Solomon. Choon-Leong Seow's Anchor Bible commentary (1997) marshals the most thorough case for a Persian-period date (5th–4th century BC): the Hebrew is heavily Aramaized, includes Persian loanwords (*pardes* in 2:5, *pithgam* in 8:11), and reflects an economic vocabulary tied to the monetized administrative system the Achaemenids imposed on Yehud. Michael V. Fox (*A Time to Tear Down and a Time to Build Up*, 1999) and Roland Murphy (Word, 1992) concur, with a minority — including Tremper Longman (NICOT) and Stuart Weeks (*Ecclesiastes and Scepticism*) — willing to push later into Hellenistic Yehud (3rd century BC) on the basis of supposed Greek philosophical resonance.

The political world of the implied composition is therefore not the united monarchy but the small temple-state of post-exilic Judah, in which a wealthy land-owning class and a remote imperial administration set the terms of life. The references to oppression that "had no comforter" (4:1), the cynical observation that "if you see in a province the oppression of the poor and the violation of justice and righteousness, do not be amazed at the matter, for the high official is watched by a higher, and there are yet higher ones over them" (5:8), and the bitterness about kings whose succession is decided by court factions (4:13–16) all read more naturally against the Persian satrapal apparatus than against Solomonic absolutism.

The title itself is a feminine participle: *qohelet* (קֹהֶלֶת), "assembler" or "convener," from the root *qhl* — to gather an assembly. Seow argues this is a literary persona, not a personal name; the Greek *Ekklesiastes* (the Septuagint's translation, from which the English title descends) renders the same idea as "the one who addresses the assembly." Whether the persona is meant to evoke Solomon-as-king-convener or Solomon-as-wisdom-teacher is one of the book's productive ambiguities. The frame narrative — superscription in 1:1, first-person opening in 1:12, and the third-person epilogue at 12:9–14 — is what enables Brevard Childs's canonical reading: there is an *epilogist* who presents Qohelet's words at a deliberate interpretive distance.

The textual base is unusually well-attested for a wisdom book. Two Qumran scrolls — 4QQoh-a and 4QQoh-b, dated paleographically to roughly 175–150 BC — already display substantially the Masoretic text, which means the book was stable and accepted at Qumran within at most a century or two of its likely composition. The Greek versions (an idiosyncratic literal Aquilan-style translation), Jerome's Vulgate, and the Targum (heavily expansionist, transforming Qohelet's skepticism into orthodox sage-discourse) round out the textual witnesses. Comparative ancient Near Eastern context is essential: the Mesopotamian *Dialogue of Pessimism*, the Egyptian *Dialogue of a Man with His Ba*, and the Old Babylonian *Gilgamesh* Tablet X — Siduri's tavern speech to the bereaved hero ("Fill your belly with good things; day and night, night and day, dance and be merry…") — supply the closest generic analogues. Ecclesiastes is not an isolated voice in the ancient world but a Hebrew entry in a long-running ANE literary conversation about the limits of wisdom in a world that does not reliably reward it.

The chapter's signature vocabulary is dense and recurs throughout the book. *Hevel* (הֶבֶל, literally "vapor" or "breath") appears five times in the prologue alone (the famous *havel havalim* of 1:2 — "vanity of vanities" — uses the Hebrew superlative construction, like *qodesh qodashim*, "holy of holies"); it occurs 38 times in the book overall. *Yitron* (יִתְרוֹן, "profit," "surplus," "what remains over") makes its first appearance in 1:3 — the book's diagnostic question, "What *yitron* does a man have from all his toil?" — and recurs ten times. *Tahat ha-shemesh* (תַּחַת הַשֶּׁמֶשׁ, "under the sun") appears 29 times across the book, beginning in 1:3, and is itself a Phoenician/West Semitic idiom that Seow notes is otherwise rare in the Hebrew Bible. *Ra'yon ruach* (רַעְיוֹן רוּחַ), translated "striving after wind" (1:14, 17), uses an Aramaic loanword *ra'yon* (a clue to the late dating) paired with *ruach*, the same word that means "spirit" and "wind" — an inevitable double entendre Qohelet exploits throughout. The book's final keyword, *'olam* (עוֹלָם), will not arrive until 3:11, but its first echo is here: 1:4's "the earth remains forever" (*la-'olam*).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre-wise, Ecclesiastes belongs to a hybrid category that James Crenshaw (OTL, 1987) and Norman Whybray have called *skeptical wisdom* or *philosophical reflection*. It is not the optimistic two-ways *mashal* of Proverbs (act-consequence retributive wisdom), nor is it the disputation drama of Job (where the question of innocent suffering is staged as a dialogue and resolved, however ambiguously, by divine speech). The closer ANE analogues are the Egyptian "harper songs" and Mesopotamian skeptical wisdom, both of which already mix carpe-diem advice with cosmic pessimism. The frame narrative — superscription (1:1), first-person opening (1:12), and third-person epilogue (12:9–14) — creates what Childs calls the *canonical* shape: an editor presents Qohelet's reflections without endorsing every line of them.

Chapter 1 is itself a tightly composed unit comprising three movements. The motto-thesis (1:2): *havel havalim hakkol havel* — five Hebrew words, a chiastic frame around the verb-less *hakkol*, declaring the book's diagnostic conclusion before its argument. The cosmological prologue (1:3–11): a meditation on the cycles of generations, sun, wind, and water, which Fox calls "the most metaphysically ambitious passage in the book." And the royal autobiography opening (1:12–18): the introduction of Qohelet as Solomon-figure who has applied wisdom to "all that is done under heaven" and concluded that the project itself is *ra'yon ruach*.

The literary devices are dense. *Leitwörter*: the chapter introduces the four governing keywords (*hevel*, *yitron*, *tahat ha-shemesh*, *ra'yon ruach*) that will pulse through every chapter. The inclusio is one of the most precise in the canon: the motto of 1:2 — *havel havalim, amar qohelet, havel havalim hakkol havel* — is restated almost verbatim at 12:8 — *havel havalim, amar haqqohelet, hakkol havel*. Whatever happens between, the book's thesis is unaltered. The cosmological poem of 1:4–7 employs participles that translate as durative-iterative — the sun is *constantly* rising and setting, the wind *eternally* circling — which is why the famous Pete Seeger / Byrds adaptation of 3:1–8 ("Turn, Turn, Turn") catches Qohelet's grammar even when it sentimentalizes his thesis.

The most arresting compositional choice is 1:8, which Seow calls the "literary key" to the entire book: *kol ha-devarim yege'im, lo' yukhal 'ish ledabber, lo' tisba' 'ayin lir'ot, ve-lo' timmale' 'ozen mishemo'a* — "All things are wearisome; a man cannot utter it; the eye is not satisfied with seeing, nor the ear filled with hearing." Two readings compete. The traditional rendering ("all things are full of weariness") gives a metaphysical claim about reality. Seow argues for the alternative — "all *words* are wearisome" (the same Hebrew root *davar* serves both "thing" and "word") — which makes the verse a meta-commentary on the wisdom project itself: the act of *speaking about* the world exhausts speech without exhausting the world. On either reading, this verse carries the prologue's heaviest semantic load.

Within the canon, Ecclesiastes belongs to the *Megillot*, the five festival scrolls (Song of Songs, Ruth, Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, Esther). It is read in synagogues at *Sukkot*, the autumnal harvest festival — a calendrical placement that itself constitutes a hermeneutic, juxtaposing Qohelet's "all is vapor" with the *sukkah*'s temporary booth, the festival's ritual rehearsal of human transience. Textual variants in chapter 1 are minor; the LXX and MT agree closely, and the Targum's expansive softenings ("vapor of vapors said Qohelet, the *prophet* — all is vapor in *this world below*") signal not textual corruption but interpretive anxiety.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Ecclesiastes occupies a specific node in the canonical wisdom arc. Gerhard von Rad's *Wisdom in Israel* (1972) framed it as the third movement: Proverbs articulates conventional retributive wisdom (the wise prosper, the foolish perish), Job stages the protest (a righteous man suffers), and Ecclesiastes voices the skeptic's diagnosis (the categories themselves don't carve nature at its joints — the wise and the fool both die, and the same fate meets them). Two further canonical interlocutors complete the circle. Sirach (Ben Sira), writing perhaps 180 BC, appears to know and react to Qohelet — softening his pessimism into a more orthodox sage-piety. The Wisdom of Solomon (1st c. BC, Greek Diaspora Judaism) is in part a deliberate pious correction of Qohelet, putting an idealized Solomon's mouth around an immortality-of-the-soul argument that Qohelet expressly refuses.

The Jewish interpretive trajectory is haunted by canonization anxiety. *Mishnah Yadayim 3:5* records that "the school of Shammai says: Qohelet does not defile the hands [is not canonical], and the school of Hillel says: it does defile the hands" — using the technical idiom by which the rabbis registered the holy-book status of a scroll. The *Bavli Shabbat 30b* preserves the candid reasoning: "the Sages sought to suppress the book of Qoheleth because its words are mutually contradictory… but they did not suppress it because its beginning is words of Torah and its end is words of Torah." The frame narrative — the orthodox-sounding superscription and the orthodox-sounding epilogue — is what saved the scroll. *Qohelet Rabbah*, the major rabbinic midrash, and *Targum Qohelet*, the Aramaic paraphrase, both represent the long-running rabbinic project of *domesticating* Qohelet by interpolating eschatological judgment, Torah-piety, and afterlife clauses where the Hebrew has none. Maimonides reads Qohelet philosophically (*Guide* 3.51 treats the book as an exercise in the limits of human apprehension), Ibn Ezra reads it grammatically and contextually, and Robert Gordis's *Koheleth: The Man and His World* (1951) — still the dean of modern Jewish commentaries — argues for an authentically pious Qohelet whose skepticism is the wisdom of mature faith.

The Christian interpretive history is strikingly different. The major patristic treatment is Gregory of Nyssa's *Homilies on Ecclesiastes* (c. 380, eight homilies on chapters 1–3), which reads "vanity" allegorically as the soul's distance from God; vanity is everything that is *not* God. Origen wrote a commentary now lost. Jerome's Latin commentary (388, addressed to the Roman matron Blesilla as a *memento mori*) translated *hevel* as *vanitas* — the rendering that the Vulgate canonized and that Wycliffe carried into English. Didymus the Blind's catechetical commentary, discovered at the Tura cache in 1941, supplies a fourth major patristic witness. Hugh of St. Victor in the 12th century continued the allegorical line; the *Glossa Ordinaria* multiplied senses; Bonaventure and Aquinas wrote on the book.

The Reformation diverged sharply. Luther's *Notes on Ecclesiastes* (lectures 1526, published 1532) read *hevel* as the futility of human striving against grace — the book became, for him, a anti-works-righteousness gospel, and he loved it. Calvin, by contrast, never published a commentary on Ecclesiastes; the silence parallels his silence on Esther and is its own datum about which biblical books his theology found intractable. Puritan readings (Robert Cawdry; Charles Bridges, 1860) turned the book toward sober devotional use. Critical scholarship from Franz Delitzsch (1875) onward — through Whybray, Crenshaw, Murphy, and culminating in Seow's Anchor — has rehabilitated Qohelet as a *serious philosopher* rather than a believer to be apologized for. The most provocative recent move is Tremper Longman's NICOT (1998), which reads the epilogist as actively *disapproving* of Qohelet: the frame narrator, on Longman's reading, is presenting Qohelet's pessimism as a foil against which the closing exhortation ("fear God and keep his commandments," 12:13) is meant to land.

The deep question across both traditions is therefore whether the book *teaches* skepticism or merely *contains* it. Jewish reading historically domesticates Qohelet (Targum, Maimonides, Gordis); Christian patristic reading allegorizes (Gregory of Nyssa); Luther reads anti-works gospel; Longman reads repudiation. A genuinely modern reading — Fox, Seow, Crenshaw — accepts that the book is *really* skeptical and that its canonical inclusion is a remarkable testimony to the breadth of biblical theology.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Does the prologue *teach* despair or *diagnose* it?** When Qohelet declares *havel havalim hakkol havel*, is this the book's instruction or the book's premise? Robert Gordis (Jewish, *Koheleth*, 1951) reads Qohelet as a pious sage whose apparent skepticism is the disciplined honesty of mature faith — the book diagnoses what life looks like *under the sun* precisely so the reader will reach for what lies *above* it. Roland Murphy (Catholic, Word) reads similarly: Qohelet's *hevel* is a critical instrument, not a creed; the book reveals the limits of unaided human reasoning so as to leave room for revelation. The Reformed/Calvinist providential reading (e.g., Iain Provan, NIVAC) takes the cosmic prologue (1:4–7) as a subtle praise of common grace — generations come and go, but God's earth abides, and God's gifts (eat, drink, find joy in toil) are real even in a world we cannot ultimately decipher. Lutheran readings (extending Luther's 1526 lectures) take *hevel* as the *simul-iustus* condition: humans live perpetually in a vapor world that drives them to grace.

The genuine steelmanning belongs to the position that the book *really does* teach despair — or, more precisely, that it teaches a sober non-religious naturalism that the canonical frame did not erase. Michael Fox (*A Time to Tear Down*) renders *hevel* as "absurd" and reads Qohelet as articulating a coherent existentialist position: the universe is intelligible enough to be observed but not coherent enough to yield meaning. James Crenshaw (OTL) goes further: "Qohelet's God is unknown, distant, and indifferent." The book's God acts (3:11, 3:14) but does not communicate; humans are spectators of cosmic regularities they did not author and cannot interpret. Crenshaw and Fox argue that the carpe diem refrains are not pious affirmations but weary concessions — eat and drink because there is nothing else *to* do.

The strongest version of this naturalist reading is that Ecclesiastes is the canon's honest moment. The book of Job at least gives us a divine voice from the whirlwind; the prophets at least promise eschatological vindication; even Lamentations holds out *hesed* (Lam 3:22). Ecclesiastes withdraws all of these. The cosmic prologue (1:4–11) is the most disenchanted passage in the Hebrew Bible — closer to Lucretius and Marcus Aurelius than to Isaiah. *Where this leaves us*: any reading of Ecclesiastes that does not feel the force of the despair-teaching position has not actually read the book; the canonical frame makes the skepticism *bearable*, but it does not make it *false*.

**Tension 2: Eternal return or teleological history?** The cosmological prologue (1:4–11) presents a universe of perpetual cycles — generations come and go, the sun rises and sets, the wind circles, the rivers run to the sea but the sea is not full, "what has been is what will be" (1:9), "there is nothing new under the sun." This is striking against the larger Hebrew canon. Genesis 1 narrates a forward-moving cosmos that begins ("in the beginning"); the Deuteronomic histories narrate Israel's life as forward movement under covenant; Daniel 7 stages history as a forward sequence of kingdoms ending in the Son of Man's vindication. The Christian canon will extend this teleology: Romans 8's groaning creation, 1 Corinthians 15's resurrection, Revelation 21's new heavens and new earth. *Time* in the rest of the Bible is going somewhere.

Qohelet's prologue, on its face, denies this. The closer parallels are Greco-Roman: Stoic *ekpyrosis* (the eternal recurrence of cosmic cycles), Hesiodic ages, the cyclical cosmology of late Hellenistic philosophy. Brevard Childs's canonical reading argues the apparent contradiction is the point: the canon places Qohelet *next to* Genesis and the prophets so that his "nothing new under the sun" stands in deliberate dialectical tension with the canon's larger telos. Seow agrees in substance: 1:4–11 names the phenomenology of life-under-the-sun; the canon's larger arc names a hope that is precisely *not* under the sun. Comparative-religion scholarship (Crenshaw, Whybray) is less hospitable: the prologue is what it is, and the cyclical worldview is genuinely in tension with the rest of Scripture; canonical reading is the editor's resolution, not Qohelet's.

The Catholic and Orthodox traditions handle this through the doctrine of *natural* knowledge — Qohelet articulates what reason unaided by revelation can see; the canon's larger story articulates what revelation adds. Reformed readings tend to see the prologue as an indictment of human autonomy ("under the sun" = without reference to God) rather than a metaphysical claim. Jewish readings (Gordis, Fox) generally accept the cyclical phenomenology as honest description and locate consolation in the *hevel*-bracketed gifts (eat, drink, work) rather than in eschatological future. *Where this leaves us*: 1:9–10's "nothing new under the sun" is not a slogan to embroider on a tea towel; it is a serious metaphysical claim about cyclical futility that the canon does not retract but does *contextualize*.

**One unresolved question.** If Qohelet's prologue is correct that "the eye is not satisfied with seeing, nor the ear filled with hearing" (1:8), then *what kind of being* is the human being — and is that capacity to be unsatisfied a divine gift, a divine curse, or simply a fact about creatures who happen to be aware of their own duration without being able to grasp its totality?

## The Thread

The most productive cross-references are the non-obvious ones. *Job 14:1–2* — "Man who is born of a woman is of few days and full of trouble. He comes out like a flower and withers; he flees like a shadow and continues not" — uses the same vapor-imagery as Qohelet but in service of a different question (innocent suffering, not cosmic futility). *Sirach 14:11–19* (Ben Sira) explicitly echoes Qohelet's "eat and drink" but adds a moralizing frame: enjoy the gift, but remember Sheol. Most striking is the forward echo to *James 4:14* — "you do not know what tomorrow will bring. What is your life? For you are an *atmis* [vapor] that appears for a little time and then vanishes" — where the Greek *atmis* translates the same conceptual space as Hebrew *hevel*, and James (writing in a sapiential idiom himself) brings Qohelet's vapor into the New Testament's eschatological frame. Finally, *Romans 8:20* — "the creation was subjected to *mataiotēti*" — uses the LXX's translation of *hevel* (*mataiotēs*) to describe the cosmos's bondage to futility, with Paul adding what Qohelet would not: "in hope" (Rom 8:20–21). Paul reads Qohelet's prologue as cosmologically true and eschatologically penultimate — the New Testament's most direct theological response to *havel havalim hakkol havel*.
