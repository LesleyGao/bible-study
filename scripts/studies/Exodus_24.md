# Exodus 24: Under His Feet, a Pavement of Sapphire

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Exodus 24 stages the ratification of the Sinai covenant — the climactic moment toward which all of Exodus 19–23 has moved. The historical world is that of a second-millennium BCE covenant-making ceremony, a scene whose constituent elements — sacrifice, blood rite, covenant meal, written document, ascent of a sacred mountain — can be paralleled from the Mari archives (eighteenth-century BCE), from the Alalakh covenant texts, from Hittite international treaties (fourteenth to thirteenth century BCE), and from cognate ritual texts at Ugarit.

George Mendenhall's 1954 BA article and Moshe Weinfeld's *Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School* established that the structure of Israelite covenant-making parallels Hittite suzerainty treaties: preamble, historical prologue, stipulations, blessings and curses, document clause, witnesses, ratification ceremony. Exodus 24 contains the ratification. Kitchen in *On the Reliability of the Old Testament* argues the form fits the Late Bronze Age Anatolian-Syrian tradition, not the first-millennium Neo-Assyrian treaties. The dating matters: if the form is second-millennium, the Sinai narrative has deep historical roots.

Covenant blood rites are attested in Mari (the "killing of the donkey" ceremony, ARM II 37) and at Alalakh. What Ex 24:6–8 adds is a bifurcation: half the blood thrown against the altar, half thrown on the people. Propp in his Anchor Bible *Exodus* (vol. 2, 2006) treats this as without exact ANE parallel; the two-sided application binds YHWH and Israel into a single covenantal blood-fellowship.

The covenant meal on the mountain (vv. 9–11) has closer parallels. Shared eating after treaty ratification is standard in ANE diplomacy — the Mari texts describe "eating the oath" (*akālu māmītam*). What is extraordinary here is that the meal is eaten *in God's presence*: "they beheld God, and ate and drank." No known ANE parallel involves the human parties eating in the visible presence of the patron deity.

The ascending ladder of access — people at the foot, seventy elders partway up, Moses alone at the top — corresponds to a spatialized hierarchy attested in Mesopotamian *ziggurat* theology and Egyptian sanctuary architecture. The archaeology of Levantine high places (Arad, Dan, Tel Rehov) shows similar concentric graduated access.

Nadab and Abihu (v. 1, 9) appear here as honored participants — they will later die in Leviticus 10 for offering "unauthorized fire." The juxtaposition is deliberate on the final editor's part. The seventy elders (v. 1, 9) are the foundational council that reappears in Numbers 11 (where Moses' spirit is distributed among them) and echoes down into the later Sanhedrin, Jesus' seventy(-two) disciples of Luke 10, and rabbinic institutional memory. Bezalel and Oholiab, the tabernacle craftsmen, have not yet been introduced — they enter at Exodus 31.

On authorship: Exodus 24 is among the most compositionally complex chapters in the Pentateuch. Source critics from Wellhausen through Noth, Propp, Richard Elliott Friedman, and Dozeman identify at least three strands. Verses 1–2 and 9–11 (the mountain theophany with the seventy, and the meal) are commonly assigned to J or a proto-J source; vv. 3–8 (the blood rite and the Book of the Covenant) are assigned to E; vv. 15–18 (the cloud and glory *kābôd*) are P. Friedman's *The Bible with Sources Revealed* displays the chapter with its presumed strata color-coded. The redactor has woven them into a single dramatic arc — from ratification on the plain through ascent and meal to withdrawal into the cloud.

Sinai as sacred mountain echoes the ANE topos of the divine mountain — Zaphon (Baal's mountain, north of Ugarit), Hursag (Mesopotamian cosmic mountain), the Egyptian *akhet*. Jon Levenson's *Sinai and Zion* (1985) is the foundational study of how Israelite tradition located divine encounter on the mountain. Archaeological touchpoints: the proto-Sinaitic inscriptions at Serabit el-Khadim document Semitic presence in the Sinai in the Late Bronze; the precise location of Mount Sinai remains disputed — Jebel Musa (the traditional site, with Byzantine attestation), Jebel al-Lawz (in Midian, favored by some recent scholars), or an unidentified location.

Key Hebrew terms:

- *sēper habbĕrît* (סֵפֶר הַבְּרִית) — Book of the Covenant (v. 7)
- *dam habbĕrît* (דַּם הַבְּרִית) — blood of the covenant (v. 8)
- *naʿăśeh wĕnišmāʿ* (נַעֲשֶׂה וְנִשְׁמָע) — "we will do and we will hear/obey" (v. 7)
- *kābôd YHWH* (כְּבוֹד יְהוָה) — the glory of the LORD (vv. 16, 17)
- *libnat hassappîr* (לִבְנַת הַסַּפִּיר) — pavement of sapphire (v. 10)

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter divides into four movements: the summons to ascend (vv. 1–2), the ratification ceremony at the foot of the mountain (vv. 3–8), the ascent and theophanic meal (vv. 9–11), and Moses' further solitary ascent into the cloud (vv. 12–18). The structure is chiastic around the meal: summons → ceremony → **meal** → ascent → cloud.

The progressive vertical structure — people, elders, Moses alone — is one of the chapter's primary literary devices. Brevard Childs (*Exodus*, OTL) identifies it as the dominant compositional principle. The vertical axis is matched by a temporal one: six days of cloud, seventh day of summons (v. 16), forty days and nights on the mountain (v. 18). The sevens and forties are liturgical numbers — seven days of cloud-covering as creation-echo, forty as the wilderness generation's length, Elijah's fast at Horeb (1 Kgs 19), Jesus' wilderness temptation.

Verse 10's description of the pavement under God's feet — *libnat hassappîr* — is among the most striking theophanic images in the OT. The text refuses to describe God; it describes the floor. Robert Alter's translation note observes that the text "steadfastly refrains from representing the deity; the very indirection of the report — we are shown only the pavement under the feet — is itself a theological statement." The word *sappîr* probably denotes lapis lazuli rather than modern sapphire (Loretz, Propp); the sense is a transparent deep blue, "like the very heaven for clearness." The image is taken up again at Ezekiel 1:26 (the sapphire throne), Isaiah 6 (the train of God filling the temple), Daniel 7 (the Ancient of Days), and Revelation 4 (the sea of glass before the throne) — the foundational scriptural image of the divine court.

Verse 7's formula *naʿăśeh wĕnišmāʿ* — "we will do and we will hear" — places doing before hearing. This inversion becomes, in rabbinic tradition, the signature pedagogical principle of Torah: obedience precedes full understanding. The Bavli at Shabbat 88a elaborates this into a midrash on the angels' praise of Israel. Robert Alter notes the Hebrew would more naturally say "we will hear and we will do"; the reversal is deliberate and theologically loaded.

The blood rite (vv. 6–8) is structured as a tripartite distribution: half the blood against the altar, reading of the Book, half the blood on the people. The reading of the Book is placed *between* the two blood-throws, as if to say that the covenant document and the covenant blood together bind the parties. The formula "behold the blood of the covenant" (*hinnēh dam habbĕrît*) returns verbatim at Mark 14:24 in the Last Supper institution — one of the most exact OT-NT quotations in the Synoptics.

The ascent-with-companions followed by Moses-alone (vv. 9–13) reflects an intentional literary technique: partial communion for the seventy, total communion for Moses. The elders see and eat; Moses alone will enter the cloud and receive the tablets.

The cloud and glory of vv. 15–18 deploy classic Priestly theophanic vocabulary. *Kābôd YHWH* — the "weight" or "glory" of the LORD — appears thirteen times in Exodus, most densely in chapters 24, 33, and 40. The image of "devouring fire" (*ʾēš ʾōkelet*, v. 17) is echoed at Deuteronomy 4:24 and Hebrews 12:29. The six-days-of-cloud-then-seventh-day-summons structure echoes the creation week.

Textual variants: LXX adds minor clarifications in v. 10 (the "like" before "pavement" is expanded). The Samaritan Pentateuch harmonizes with Deut 5. 4QpaleoExodm preserves fragments of vv. 11–14. Targum Onkelos famously softens v. 10, "they saw the God of Israel," to "they saw the glory of the God of Israel" — a theological paraphrase Rashi follows.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Exodus 24 sits at the structural center of the Sinai pericope (Ex 19–40). The giving of the covenant reaches its climax here; what follows is the tabernacle instructions (Ex 25–31), the golden calf rupture (Ex 32–33), the renewed covenant (Ex 34), and the tabernacle's construction (Ex 35–40). The chapter also marks the canonical pivot point: Israel has become the covenant people, and the remainder of Exodus works out what it means for God to dwell among them.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory**:
- Second Temple: Philo's *Life of Moses* 2.154–158 treats the ascent of Moses and the seventy as a philosophical *anabasis*. Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.75–88) dramatizes the theophany. Jubilees 6 elaborates the covenant meal. The Dead Sea Scrolls' 4QVisions of Amram (4Q543–549) shows the seventy-elders tradition in apocalyptic development.
- Rabbinic: The Mekhilta on Mishpatim and the Bavli at Shabbat 88a treat *naʿăśeh wĕnišmāʿ* (v. 7) as Israel's defining response — the angels, hearing Israel say "we will do" before "we will hear," crown each Israelite with two crowns (rabbinic midrash). On v. 10, Bava Batra 75a and Chagigah 14b contain extraordinary statements: the sapphire pavement is preserved under God's throne as a remembrance of Israel's suffering in Egypt (one midrashic tradition reads the *lĕbēnâ*, brick, as a pun on the bricks of Egyptian slavery — so the "pavement under his feet" is literally the bricks Israel laid, now transformed into sapphire). On v. 11, the rabbis debate whether the elders erred in "feasting" during theophany — some traditions see this as the root of their later deaths at Num 11 and Lev 10. Maimonides in *Guide* 1.4, 1.5 treats the vision of v. 10 as intellectual apprehension, not sensory sight — the seventy "saw" God in the mind, not with the eyes. The *Merkabah* mystical tradition (Gershom Scholem's *Major Trends in Jewish Mysticism*) locates its origins here: the pavement-under-the-feet is proto-throne-vision, precursor to Ezekiel 1 and the Hekhalot literature.
- Medieval: Rashi follows Onkelos' reading ("saw the glory"). Ibn Ezra preserves the plain-sense "saw God" but adds philosophical qualifications. Ramban polemicizes against both: the elders saw God truly, though not in essence; their "eating and drinking" was cultic, not merely ordinary. Ramban treats the pavement as a veil of concealment — what can be perceived is the floor; the divine person remains hidden.
- Modern: Sarna's JPS Exodus is the standard treatment. Nehama Leibowitz's *Studies in Shemot* collects classical Jewish readings on the chapter. Levenson's *Sinai and Zion* treats Ex 24 as the paradigm theophany; his *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* reads the chapter alongside Ezekiel 1. Avivah Zornberg's *The Particulars of Rapture* gives an extensive psychoanalytic-literary reading of the blood rite and the meal. Jonathan Sacks on *naʿăśeh wĕnišmāʿ* made it a centerpiece of Jewish pedagogy in his *Covenant and Conversation* series.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory**:
- Patristic: Origen's *Homilies on Exodus* 12 reads the blood rite typologically — the blood on altar and people prefigures Christ's blood both offered to the Father and applied to believers. Gregory of Nyssa's *Life of Moses* 2.152–170 treats the ascent into the cloud as mystical union — the *gnophos* (darkness) into which Moses enters becomes the paradigm of apophatic ascent. Pseudo-Dionysius in *Mystical Theology* 1 makes the Sinai darkness the founding image of the entire apophatic tradition.
- Medieval: Aquinas in *ST* III q. 60 treats the blood rite as a "sacrament" of the Old Law, efficacious by its signifying of Christ's blood; *ST* I q. 12 uses v. 11 in the discussion of whether God can be seen in this life. Bonaventure's *Itinerarium Mentis in Deum* treats the six stages of ascent in imitation of the six days of cloud.
- Reformation: Calvin's *Commentary on the Last Four Books* on Ex 24 treats the blood rite as typological of the Eucharist and the meal as a sacramental foretaste; what the elders saw was a "form" accommodated to human weakness, not the divine essence. Luther reads the blood of the covenant straightforwardly Christologically, with Hebrews as his guide.
- Critical: Wellhausen assigned vv. 1–2, 9–11 to J, vv. 3–8 to E, vv. 12–18 to P. Noth, Childs, Propp, and Dozeman preserve substantially this stratigraphy.
- Contemporary: Brueggemann treats the chapter as the paradigm of "costly covenant." Fretheim (*Exodus*, Interpretation) emphasizes the theophanic meal as foundational to OT sacramental theology. N.T. Wright reads Jesus' "blood of the covenant" as a deliberate claim to enact Ex 24 at a new scale. L. Michael Morales's *Who Shall Ascend* treats the Sinai pattern as the template for Leviticus. Jon Levenson's *Sinai and Zion* is the single most important modern treatment.

**Where they converge and diverge**: Jewish and Christian traditions converge on the centrality of the blood rite, the meal, and the ascent, but diverge fundamentally on their forward reference. Rabbinic tradition extends the pattern forward into Sinai-pilgrimage theology, *Merkabah* mysticism, and eschatological expectation — the meal of vv. 9–11 becomes the messianic banquet (Isa 25:6), celebrated proleptically at every Sabbath table. Christian tradition extends the pattern forward into Eucharistic theology — Jesus' "blood of the covenant" (Mark 14:24) quotes Ex 24:8 verbatim, and the meal on the mountain becomes the paradigm of Christian communion. Gregory of Nyssa's mystical-ascent reading (darkness and unknowing) diverges from rabbinic readings, which typically emphasize clarity of revelation rather than apophatic obscurity. Maimonides' anti-anthropomorphic reading of v. 10 (intellectual vision) converges with the Cappadocian apophatic tradition in denying sensory vision, though from opposite philosophical starting points. Both traditions agree that Ex 24 is the high point of OT theophany; both read it as prefigurative, but the figures differ.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Did the seventy elders actually *see* God, or was the vision mediated, accommodated, or intellectual?**

- Catholic: *ST* I q. 12 a. 11 (Aquinas): God in his essence cannot be seen by any creature in this life; the vision of Ex 24:10 was a theophany mediated by a created likeness. The *Catechism* (CCC 2144, 2779) preserves the creator-creature distinction rigorously. Only the *visio beatifica* of the blessed in heaven can see God in essence.
- Reformed (Calvin): Calvin's commentary on Ex 24:10 reads the vision as "an accommodated symbol" — the elders saw a manifestation fitted to human capacity, not God's essence. Later Reformed theology (Turretin, Bavinck) develops this as the doctrine of *theophania* — divine self-manifestation in created forms.
- Lutheran: Luther's reading is more realist than Calvin's; the elders "truly saw God" insofar as God chose to be seen, through the Son as the eternal image. Luther's Christological reading of OT theophanies makes every such vision a pre-incarnate encounter with the Son.
- Orthodox: The Hesychast tradition, especially Gregory Palamas' distinction between God's essence (*ousia*) and energies (*energeiai*), reads the elders as seeing the divine energies — genuinely God, but not God's essence. Palamas' 1338 *Triads* cite Ex 24 explicitly.
- Jewish (Maimonides): *Guide* 1.4–5 treats the vision as purely intellectual. The elders "saw" with the mind, not the eyes; their error was to mix the intellectual ascent with corporeal eating and drinking, which is why Rabbi Tanhum says they deserved death and were spared only by God's mercy (v. 11).
- Jewish (Ramban): Ramban explicitly rejects Maimonides' intellectualism. The elders saw God in a prophetic vision, in God's "measure" or "glory" — a real, though mediated, seeing. The eating and drinking was cultic and appropriate, not profane.
- Critical: The text is a Yahwistic theophany report preserving an older oral tradition; its theological claim — that God was seen and that the elders survived — is deliberately paradoxical, resisting both doctrinal systematization and historical literalization.

Steelmanning the least intuitive — Palamas' essence/energies distinction: the claim is that Scripture everywhere says God is seen (Ex 24, Ex 33:11 "face to face," Num 12:8, Isa 6, Ezek 1) and everywhere says God cannot be seen (Ex 33:20, John 1:18, 1 Tim 6:16). Both must be true. The essence/energies distinction is not a philosophical imposition on the text but the conceptual apparatus that holds the text's two-sided claim together. Without it, you must either over-spiritualize (Maimonides, Aquinas) or accept contradiction. The distinction has been contested by Catholic theologians (especially in response to Rome's engagement with Palamism in the twentieth century), but it remains the Orthodox solution to the textual paradox.

**Where this leaves us:** Every tradition preserves the paradox of v. 11 ("they beheld God, and ate and drank") rather than dissolving it. Each offers a different conceptual tool — accommodation (Calvin), mediation through the Son (Luther), essence/energies (Palamas), intellectual vision (Maimonides), created likeness (Aquinas). The text outruns every tool. The theophany is genuine; the seeing is real; God's transcendence is preserved.

**Tension 2: Is the "blood of the covenant" of v. 8 fundamentally continuous with or fundamentally discontinuous from Jesus' words at the Last Supper?**

- Catholic: The Eucharist is the sacramental renewal of the one sacrifice of Christ, which itself fulfills and supersedes the Sinai rite (CCC 1362–1372). The "blood of the covenant" at Mark 14:24 deliberately cites Ex 24:8, establishing typological continuity within eschatological newness. Aquinas' *ST* III q. 60, q. 73–83 develops this theology.
- Reformed (Calvin): Calvin's *Institutes* 4.17 treats the Eucharist as the sign and seal of the same covenant ratified at Sinai — one covenant under two administrations. The blood of the covenant at Sinai and at the Supper are the same covenant's blood, typologically anticipated and fully realized.
- Lutheran: Luther reads v. 8 with Hebrews 9:18–22 as the locus classicus of Christ's substitutionary atonement. The Sinai blood is efficacious only in its forward reference to the cross.
- Orthodox: The liturgy of John Chrysostom explicitly cites Ex 24 in the *anamnesis*; the Eucharist is the *typos* of which Sinai was a partial foreshadowing.
- Wesleyan: John Wesley's sermon *The Means of Grace* and Charles Wesley's Eucharistic hymns ("Victim Divine, Thy Grace We Claim") invoke the Sinai blood as typological substrate.
- Jewish: The Sinai covenant stands on its own terms. The blood rite of Ex 24 binds Israel to YHWH in a relationship that needs no further ratification. The NT application of the formula to Jesus is a theological claim Jewish tradition rejects, though the historical fact of the quotation (Mark 14:24) is not disputed.
- Critical: The Markan institution formula almost certainly goes back to the historical Jesus (cf. Joachim Jeremias, *The Eucharistic Words of Jesus*, 1966); Jesus read his own death as enacting Ex 24 for a renewed Israel. This is a historical claim distinct from the theological claim that the reading is correct.

Steelmanning the least intuitive — the Jewish "stands on its own terms" reading: the Sinai covenant at Ex 24 is eternal (Jer 33:20–21 treats the covenant as inviolable). If the Sinai blood rite fully accomplishes what it claims, it requires no supplement. Christian supersessionism misreads the text by insisting on insufficiency where the text insists on adequacy. Post-Holocaust Christian theology (late Barth, Nostra Aetate, Moltmann) has taken the argument increasingly seriously.

**Where this leaves us:** Christian tradition universally reads Mark 14:24 as claiming Ex 24:8 for Jesus. Jewish tradition universally reads Ex 24:8 as a self-sufficient covenantal ratification. The gap between these readings is the gap between the traditions themselves. Contemporary Jewish-Christian dialogue has not closed it and perhaps cannot close it; what dialogue has achieved is clarity about what is actually at stake — not a misreading of the text but two different readings of what kind of text Ex 24 is.

**An unresolved question**: Verse 11 says "he did not lay his hand on the chief men of the people of Israel; they beheld God, and ate and drank." The verse presupposes that God *could have* and perhaps *should have* struck them dead. What, exactly, has Israel been spared from? The text names the restraint without explaining the threat. The question — what makes the vision of God mortally dangerous, and why the elders were spared — remains among the most unresolved questions in biblical theophany.

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references:

- The "blood of the covenant" (v. 8) returns at Zechariah 9:11 ("because of the blood of my covenant with you, I will set your prisoners free"), at Mark 14:24 verbatim, and at Hebrews 9:18–22. The verse is one of the most theologically freighted cross-reference chains in scripture.
- The pavement of sapphire (v. 10) echoes forward at Ezekiel 1:26 (the throne "like sapphire"), Isaiah 6:1–4 (the train of God filling the temple), Daniel 7:9–10 (the Ancient of Days), and Revelation 4:6 (the sea of glass). The single image is the seed of the entire biblical tradition of divine throne-vision, which becomes, in post-biblical Judaism, the foundation of *Merkabah* mysticism.
- The "we will do and we will hear" of v. 7 echoes inversely at Deuteronomy 5:27 ("we will hear and do"). At Sinai, doing precedes hearing; at Horeb's retelling, hearing precedes doing. Rabbinic tradition preserves both as complementary — Torah is both prior obedience and responsive understanding.
