# Acts 22: Defense in Aramaic, Roman Citizen by Birth

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Acts 22 stages a courtroom drama on the steps of the **Antonia Fortress**, the Roman cohort's barracks at the northwestern corner of the Temple Mount. Josephus (*War* 5.5.8 §238–245) describes the fortress's four corner towers, its dominating height over the Temple precinct, and the staircase by which soldiers could descend into the outer courts. The chapter's opening verses — Paul standing on those steps, motioning for silence, addressing the crowd — mark the architectural seam between Jewish-Temple authority and Roman provincial enforcement. The cohort that intervened was likely the **Cohors I Italica** (the same unit Cornelius served in, Acts 10:1) or a successor auxiliary unit; the standard Roman provincial cohort at full strength was 480 men, expandable to 1,000 in a *cohors miliaria*. The tribune (*chiliarchos*, equivalent to a Roman *tribunus militum*), later named Claudius Lysias (23:26), commanded that cohort.

Three sets of historical realia are in play. First, **Tarsus**, Paul's birthplace (22:3). Strabo (*Geographica* 14.5.13) describes Tarsus as a major Hellenistic-philosophical center, comparable to Athens and Alexandria for Stoic philosophy; the city was Cilicia's metropolis with substantial Jewish population. Tarsus's Roman-citizenship grant traces to civic-loyalty rewards, possibly under Antony or Augustus, with citizenship potentially extended to specific local-elite families — the most defensible reconstruction of Paul's family history is that an ancestor received citizenship through such a civic grant or as a freedman of a Roman patron in Cilicia (engaged by **A.N. Sherwin-White** in *Roman Society and Roman Law in the New Testament*, **Martin Hengel** in *The Pre-Christian Paul*, and **W.M. Ramsay**).

Second, the **Gamaliel education** (22:3). Rabban Gamaliel I (the Elder) was the grandson of Hillel and a leading first-century *nasi* of the Sanhedrin; the Mishnah (m. *Sotah* 9:15) records that "when Rabban Gamaliel the Elder died, the glory of the Torah ceased." Paul's claim to have been "educated at the feet of Gamaliel" places him in the most prestigious Pharisaic-rabbinic lineage of the Second Temple period. **Bruce** (NICNT) and **Hengel** treat this as historical; **Marguerat** and **Pervo** are more cautious, treating it as a Lukan claim about Paul's Pharisaic credentials. The "feet of" idiom (cf. Luke 10:39, Mary at Jesus's feet) is the standard rabbinic formula for a master-disciple relationship.

Third, **Roman citizenship law**. Paul's citizenship by birth (22:28) — *ego de kai gegennēmai* — places his family in the small minority of provincial Romans whose citizenship predated the late-first-century empire-wide expansions. The protections were specific: under the *Lex Porcia* (early second century BCE) and *Lex Julia* (49 BCE; reaffirmed by Augustus), a Roman citizen could not be flogged or scourged without trial, and any uncondemned citizen had the right of *provocatio* — appeal to higher Roman authority. **Cicero**'s *In Verrem* II.5.165–167 contains the famous denunciation of the Sicilian governor Verres for crucifying the Roman citizen Publius Gavius: *facinus est vincire civem Romanum, scelus verberare* ("it is a crime to bind a Roman citizen, a scandal to flog him"). The tribune's fear in 22:29 — that he had already bound a Roman citizen — is legally precise. **Sherwin-White** is the foundational study of these legal-procedural details and treats Acts as remarkably accurate at this technical level.

Greek terms anchor the chapter. ***Hebraïdi dialektō*** (Ἑβραΐδι διαλέκτῳ, 22:2) — the language of Paul's address. ***Nazōraios*** (Ναζωραῖος, 22:8) — Jesus self-identifies as "Jesus of Nazareth" within Paul's speech; the term carries the Davidic-*nēṣer* (Isa 11:1) wordplay. ***ho dikaios*** (ὁ δίκαιος, 22:14) — "the Righteous One" — Christological title shared with Acts 3:14, 7:52. ***epikalesai to onoma*** (ἐπικαλέσασθαι τὸ ὄνομα, 22:16) — "calling on the name" — the LXX formula (Joel 2:32 / 3:5 LXX) Paul will quote at Rom 10:13.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Acts 22 contains the **second of Paul's three Acts conversion narratives**. Chapter 9 is the third-person narrator's account; chapter 26 will be Paul's first-person speech to Agrippa. The variations among the three are among the most discussed Lukan compositional features in the entire book.

The most famous variant: in 9:7 Paul's companions "stood speechless, hearing the voice (*akouontes tēs phōnēs*) but seeing no one"; in 22:9 the companions "saw the light but did not hear (*tēn de phōnēn ouk ēkousan*) the voice of the one who was speaking to me." **F.F. Bruce** (NICNT), following classical Greek grammarians, argues for harmonization via case: in 9:7 *phōnēs* is genitive (they heard the sound), in 22:9 *phōnēn* is accusative (they did not understand the articulate words). This distinction is real in classical Greek but less consistently maintained in koine, and the harmonization has been challenged by **Daniel Wallace** and **Stanley Porter** as overreading. **Marguerat** and **Pervo** read the variation as Lukan literary technique: the three accounts are calibrated to their audiences, not redundant transcripts. **Keener** (4 vols) is more sympathetic to a deliberate-variation reading: ancient historiography permitted such adjustment without falsification.

Other variants: in chapter 9 Ananias's role is brief (laying on hands, Saul's healing); in chapter 22 Ananias's role is expanded with explicitly Jewish-piety credentials ("a devout man according to the law, well spoken of by all the Jews," 22:12) — a feature suited to the Jerusalem audience. In chapter 9 the Gentile mission is announced to Ananias (9:15); in chapter 22 it is announced directly to Paul in a Temple-trance vision (22:17–21) absent from chapter 9. **Tannehill** reads the additions as audience-tailoring, not contradiction: each retelling foregrounds what is rhetorically needed.

The chapter's most striking literary pivot is the audience's response. The crowd listens through the autobiographical account, the conversion, even Paul's expanded Jewish-piety credentialing of Ananias. The fracture point is precise: 22:21 — "Go, for I will send you far away to the Gentiles." That single word — *ethnē* (the nations, Gentiles) — triggers the riot. **Marguerat** identifies this as Luke's structural argument: the issue between Paul and his Jerusalem accusers is not Torah, not Temple, not even Jesus-as-Messiah, but **Gentile inclusion as Gentiles** — the eschatological extension of Israel's covenantal hope to the nations without requiring Gentile circumcision. The riot's trigger word is also the chapter's theological center.

The **Western Text** (Codex Bezae) at 22:9 expands "did not hear the voice" to "they did not hear the voice **of him speaking to me** but were afraid"; at 22:26 Bezae adds an exclamation from the centurion. These expansions reinforce that the second-century Western tradition was actively reading Acts as edifying-explanatory narrative.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Paul's autobiographical narrative in Acts 22 is one of the most contested texts in the **"Paul-within-Judaism"** debate. Three trajectories.

The traditional Christian reading, with **Augustine**'s *Confessions* as its template, treats Paul's Damascus-road experience as a **conversion** — a turning from Jewish religion to Christian faith. This reading dominated Christian interpretation from the patristic era through the Reformation and beyond. **Luther**'s *Lectures on Galatians* used Paul's Damascus-road narrative as the paradigm of the law-condemned conscience finding gospel-grace. **Calvin**'s *Commentary on Acts* read 22:3–16 as the conversion of a self-righteous Pharisee to the gospel of free grace.

The **Krister Stendahl** rereading (*Paul Among Jews and Gentiles*, 1976) is the watershed. Stendahl argued that the term "conversion" mistranslates the Damascus-road experience into Augustinian-introspective categories foreign to first-century Judaism. Paul did not "convert" from one religion to another; he received a **prophetic call** within Israel's own scriptural tradition (the parallels with Isaiah 6, Jeremiah 1, and Ezekiel 1–3 are explicit in the heavenly-voice / blinding-light / vocational-commissioning structure). Paul's "I am a Jew" (22:3) and his Pharisee self-identification (23:6) are not residual identity claims from a discarded past; they are present-tense identity descriptions.

The **Pamela Eisenbaum** / **Matthew Thiessen** / **Paula Fredriksen** "Paul-within-Judaism" school radicalizes Stendahl. **Eisenbaum** (*Paul Was Not a Christian*) reads Paul's Acts 22 narrative as the **prophetic-vocational call of a Jewish prophet to the eschatological Gentile nations** — the same vocation Isaiah 49:6 announces (the servant as light to the Gentiles). **Thiessen** (*A Jewish Paul*) reads Paul's continuing Jewish piety (Nazirite vow in chapter 21, Pharisee identification in chapter 23) as dispositive: Paul never left Judaism. **Fredriksen** (*Paul: The Pagan's Apostle*) frames the entire Pauline mission as Jewish-eschatological: the Messianic age has dawned, and the nations are now being gathered to Israel's God *as nations* — without circumcision, without Jewish ritual incorporation, but worshiping Israel's God through Israel's Messiah.

**Adele Reinhartz** and **Amy-Jill Levine** add a critical caution: Christian readers must read 22:21–22 carefully. The crowd is not enraged at Paul's Christology, his Damascus-road claim, or even his Jesus-language. They are enraged at the Gentile-mission announcement. To read 22:22 as Jewish rejection of Christ misreads the text; the issue is the eschatological-Israelite question of *whether the Gentiles are included as Gentiles*.

**Christian interpretive trajectories**: **Chrysostom**'s *Homilies on Acts* (homilies 47–48) read chapter 22 as the paradigm of conversion-narrative as evangelistic strategy: testimony precedes argument. **Bede** read 22:14 ("the God of our fathers") as Paul's ground for the continuity of the church with Israel. **Calvin** used 22:16 ("be baptized and wash away your sins") to navigate the relation of baptism to faith — for Calvin, the baptism is the visible sign sealing the prior inward work, not the cause of it. **Karl Barth** used 22:6–11 in *CD* IV/2 as a paradigm of christological-objective revelation that is not derived from inner experience but encounters the subject from outside. **N.T. Wright** (*Paul: A Biography*) has argued for an integrative reading: Paul's Damascus-road experience is genuinely transformative (Stendahl is wrong to dissolve it into mere call) but operates **within** Israel's eschatological framework (the older Christian conversion-from-Judaism reading is wrong to extract it from that framework).

The major contemporary commentary tradition — **Bruce** (NICNT), **Barrett** (ICC), **Fitzmyer** (AB), **Gaventa** (Abingdon), **Johnson** (Sacra Pagina), **Keener** (4 vols), **Witherington III**, **Schnabel** (ZECNT), **Conzelmann** (Hermeneia), **Haenchen**, **Pervo** (Hermeneia), **Peterson** (Pillar), **Marguerat** — divides on the historicity of the Temple-trance vision (22:17–21). **Bruce** and **Keener** treat it as authentic Pauline tradition that Luke deploys here for rhetorical reasons. **Pervo** treats it as Lukan composition designed to motivate the rest of Acts's narrative arc. **Marguerat** holds the middle: the vision functions theologically whether or not it has historical bedrock.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Whether the Damascus-road experience is conversion or call.** The terminological question is theologically substantive. Three positions.

The first (older Augustinian-Reformation, **Luther**, **Calvin**, traditional evangelicalism) reads it as conversion: Paul moves from one religious system (Judaism) to another (Christianity); the experience is paradigmatic of every Christian conversion. The strength: it takes seriously the radical reorientation of Paul's life. The weakness: it imposes anachronistic religious-system categories on a Second Temple Jewish prophet whose self-understanding was continuous with Israel's covenantal-eschatological tradition.

The second (**Stendahl**, **N.T. Wright**, the "New Perspective on Paul") reads it as call within Israel: Paul receives a prophetic-vocational commission to bring Israel's eschatological hope to the nations. The strength: it preserves the prophetic-call structure of the narrative (parallels to Isaiah 6, Jeremiah 1) and the continuity of Paul's Jewish identity. The weakness: it can underdescribe the genuine reorientation — Paul did not formerly believe Jesus was the Messiah and now does, and that is a substantive doxastic change.

The third (**Eisenbaum**, **Thiessen**, **Fredriksen**, "Paul-within-Judaism") reads it as a prophetic call to the Gentile mission with **no** rupture from Judaism whatsoever; Paul never converted because there was nothing to convert from. The strength: it is the most consistent with Paul's continuing Jewish-ritual practice in Acts and with the strongest reading of his own letters' Jewish self-identification (Phil 3:5). The weakness: it can flatten the genuinely christological dimension of the experience — the risen Jesus is the speaker, and Paul's encounter with him is not reducible to a continuation of pre-existing Jewish vocation.

**Where this leaves us:** "call" is closer to the first-century textual sense than "conversion," but the experience is irreducibly christological — Paul learns the Messianic-eschatological hope of his own tradition has a name and a face, and that the Gentiles are being included now without first becoming Jews. The N.T. Wright integrative reading is the most defensible: a transformative encounter that operates entirely within Israel's covenantal framework but reveals that framework's eschatological horizon has arrived.

**Tension 2: Roman citizenship and Christian witness under the state.** Paul invokes his Roman citizenship in 22:25 to avoid scourging. Three readings of what this implies for Christian engagement with state power.

The first (older mainline Protestant, post-Constantinian Catholic) reads it as warrant for Christian use of every legitimate civic protection: Paul models the prudential use of citizenship rights, and Christians may rightly invoke law, sue in courts, and benefit from state structures. The second (Anabaptist / Yoder / **Hauerwas**) reads it as a problematic concession in Acts that should not be normative: Paul's invocation of citizenship privilege secures his life at the cost of solidarity with non-citizen sufferers (cf. the Jerusalem disciples beaten and killed without recourse); the gospel calls Christians to renounce, not deploy, civic privilege. The third (**O'Donovan**, **Volf**, integrationist) reads it as a model of differentiated witness: civic structures are penultimate goods that may be deployed in service of the church's witness, but never as the primary identity-conferring framework.

Steelmanning each: the first preserves the integrity of Acts's narrative endorsement of Paul's strategy; the second preserves the cruciform shape of the gospel; the third tries to hold both.

**Where this leaves us:** Acts 22 endorses Paul's tactical use of citizenship without endorsing citizenship as primary identity. Paul's first words to the crowd are "I am a Jew" (22:3); his last words about citizenship are tactical (22:28). The hierarchy of identity is clear. The Anabaptist caution against treating civic privilege as a default Christian entitlement remains theologically serious, even if Acts itself does not foreclose tactical deployment.

**One unresolved question:** If Paul's Damascus-road experience is "call" rather than "conversion," what theological category captures the analogous experience of Gentile believers in Acts? They are converting from polytheism to the worship of Israel's God through Israel's Messiah — that is religious conversion in the strongest sense. Does the Jewish-Paul / Gentile-convert asymmetry imply a structural two-track ecclesiology (Jewish believers continuing Torah-observance, Gentile believers as new Gentile worshipers of Israel's God), or does Christian unity eventually flatten the asymmetry?

## The Thread

Acts 22's conversion narrative ties forward to **Acts 26** (the third retelling, before Agrippa) and back to **Acts 9** (the original third-person account); the variations among the three are the textbook example of Lukan compositional technique. The Roman-citizenship invocation (22:25–29) ties forward to **Acts 25:11** (Paul's appeal to Caesar) and ultimately to **Acts 28** (Rome). The Temple-trance vision (22:17–21) ties forward to the Pauline apocalyptic experiences of **2 Cor 12:1–10**. And the riot-trigger word *ethnē* (22:21) ties forward to **Romans 9–11** as Paul's most extended theological wrestling with the Gentile-inclusion question that nearly cost him his life on the Antonia steps.
