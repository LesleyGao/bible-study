# Isaiah 27 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Isaiah 27 closes the *Isaiah Apocalypse* (chapters 24-27), the four-chapter eschatological unit that sits as a self-contained theological capstone within First Isaiah (chapters 1-39). The chapter is structured around four occurrences of the formula *bayyom ha-hu* (בַּיּוֹם הַהוּא, "in that day") in vv. 1, 2, 12, and 13 — a phrase that has organized the entire Apocalypse and now drives it to its closing scenes: the slaying of the cosmic chaos-monster, the restored vineyard, the gleaning of scattered Israel, and the trumpet-summoned return to Zion.

The dating of chapters 24-27 has been the most contested compositional question in modern Isaianic scholarship for over a century. Bernhard Duhm in 1892 placed the unit in the second century BCE as a Maccabean "proto-apocalypse" — a position now largely abandoned but which set the terms of the debate. The mainstream-critical line (Hans Wildberger in his BKAT volume; Joseph Blenkinsopp in the Anchor Yale; John Goldingay in the ICC; Joseph Roberts in Hermeneia; Patricia Tull) settles on a late-Persian to early-Hellenistic date — broadly fifth to third century BCE — based on linguistic features, the developed eschatological orientation, and what is taken to be presupposed familiarity with the experience of exile and return. Jacob Stromberg's *Isaiah After Exile* (Oxford, 2011) emphasizes how chapters 24-27 read as a deliberate literary capstone composed to bring chapters 1-23 to theological resolution, drawing on Isaianic vocabulary and imagery throughout.

The conservative-evangelical line — Alec Motyer (1993) and John Oswalt (NICOT, 1986) — argues for substantial Isaianic authorship on the grounds that the chapter's vocabulary, its use of the *vineyard* metaphor (which directly answers chapter 5), its diction around YHWH-as-warrior, and its orientation toward Assyrian-era concerns all point to an eighth-century origin. Brevard Childs and Christopher Seitz, working in the canonical-shape tradition, argue that whatever the actual compositional history, the canonical placement of chapters 24-27 functions as Isaiah's own theological summation — and the text should be read with that canonical claim taken seriously.

The textual witness is unusually solid. The *Great Isaiah Scroll* (1QIsa^a) from Qumran, dated paleographically to c. 125 BCE, preserves chapter 27 with only minor orthographic variation from the Masoretic text — confirming that whatever the date of original composition, the chapter was textually stable and fully canonical by the late Second Temple period.

Hebrew terms central to the chapter:

- *Liwyatan* (לִוְיָתָן, "Leviathan") — the cosmic sea-serpent, attested in Job 41, Ps 74:14, Ps 104:26, and notably in the Ugaritic Baal Cycle (KTU 1.5.I) where the parallel monster is called *Lōtan* (𐤋𐤕𐤍).
- *nachash bariach* (נָחָשׁ בָּרִיחַ, "fleeing serpent") and *nachash aqallaton* (נָחָשׁ עֲקַלָּתוֹן, "twisting serpent") — both phrases find direct parallels in the Ugaritic Baal Cycle's description of *Lōtan*: *bṭn brḥ* and *bṭn ʿqltn*.
- *tannin* (תַּנִּין, "dragon, sea-monster") — the third term of v. 1, often paired with Leviathan in chaos-mythology contexts (Ps 74:13, Job 7:12).
- *kerem chemed* (כֶּרֶם חֶמֶד, "pleasant vineyard," 27:2) — directly echoing and answering the *kerem* of Isaiah 5:1-7 (the *Song of the Vineyard*).
- *shofar gadol* (שׁוֹפָר גָּדוֹל, "great trumpet," 27:13) — the eschatological trumpet of ingathering, drawn into the Jewish liturgy of *Rosh Hashanah* and the *Shofar Shel Mashiach* tradition.

The chapter's mythological backdrop is the ancient *Combat Myth* — the cosmogonic narrative attested across the ancient Near East in which a deity defeats a chaos-monster (Marduk vs. Tiamat in Babylon's *Enuma Elish*; Baal vs. Yam and Lōtan in Ugaritic literature; YHWH vs. Rahab/Leviathan/the sea in Israelite poetry). Frank Moore Cross's *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* (Harvard, 1973) and Mark S. Smith's *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (Oxford, 2001) and *The Early History of God* (Eerdmans, 1990) are the foundational treatments. The Ugaritic parallel is so close in 27:1 that direct literary inheritance, not merely shared cultural pool, is virtually certain.

## Lens 2: The Literature Itself

Chapter 27 is structured by the four "in that day" formulas, but it operates by a more subtle internal logic: cosmic victory (v. 1), vineyard restoration (vv. 2-6), measured discipline (vv. 7-11), eschatological gleaning (vv. 12-13). Each scene takes up and inverts a specific earlier image in First Isaiah.

**The cosmic-victory opening (v. 1)** is one of the most archaically-charged verses in the Hebrew Bible. The threefold designation — *Leviathan the fleeing serpent, Leviathan the twisting serpent, the dragon (tannin) that is in the sea* — replicates the threefold mythological taxonomy of the Ugaritic Combat Myth almost verbatim. Cross argued that Isaiah 27:1 preserves a fragment of *very ancient* Israelite cosmogonic poetry, recycled here as eschatological imagery. The point is theological: what YHWH did *in illo tempore* (at creation) he will do *at the end* — the cosmic chaos that the world has been straining against will be definitively defeated. Note also the threefold *will punish / will slay* construction: this is final, not provisional, victory. The word for "sword" — *cherev qashah ve-gedolah ve-chazaqah* (חֶרֶב קָשָׁה וּגְדוֹלָה וַחֲזָקָה, "hard, great, and strong sword") — uses three modifiers, mirroring the three names for the monster.

**The vineyard song (vv. 2-6)** is the chapter's structural and theological hinge. Isaiah 5:1-7 had given us the *original* Song of the Vineyard: YHWH planted a vineyard, tended it carefully, expected good grapes, got wild grapes, and pronounced devastating judgment — *I will remove its hedge, and it shall be devoured; I will break down its wall, and it shall be trampled down* (5:5). Chapter 27 deliberately reverses this. Now the vineyard is *kerem chemed* (the *pleasant* vineyard); now YHWH is its *keeper* (*notzer*, נֹצֵר); now he waters it *every moment*; now no thorns and briers can challenge it. The *I have no wrath* of v. 4 is one of the most striking lines in First Isaiah — the same God who pronounced judgment in chapter 5 now declares his disposition has resolved into protection. Verse 5 offers the alternative for the briers: *let them lay hold of my protection, let them make peace with me, let them make peace with me* — the doubled *yaaseh shalom li yaaseh shalom li* (יַעֲשֶׂה שָׁלוֹם לִי יַעֲשֶׂה שָׁלוֹם לִי) echoing the doubled *shalom shalom* of 26:3.

**The measured-discipline section (vv. 7-11)** addresses one of the prophetic corpus's most difficult questions: has YHWH's punishment of his own people been just? The answer in v. 7 is *no, not in the same measure* — Israel has not been struck as Israel's enemies were struck. Verse 8's *Measure by measure, by exile you contended with them* (Hebrew *be-sa'ssa'ah be-shalchah teribennah*, with *sa'ssa'ah* an obscure term sometimes rendered "in measured doses") locates the exile itself within YHWH's *measured* judgment — calibrated, not catastrophic; disciplinary, not annihilating. Verse 9 contains the chapter's most morally serious claim: *the guilt of Jacob will be atoned for* (*kuppar avon Yaakov*) when *all the stones of the altars* are crushed and the *Asherim* (אֲשֵׁרִים, the wooden cult-poles of Canaanite goddess-worship) are uprooted. Atonement here is achieved through the demolition of false-worship infrastructure — a theology of national repentance figured architecturally.

**The closing trumpet (vv. 12-13)** brings the Apocalypse to its eschatological climax: *from the river Euphrates to the Brook of Egypt* — the maximum biblical range of the Promised Land — YHWH will *thresh out the grain* (the verb *chavat*, חבט, used of beating olives or grain to separate the kernels), gleaning the scattered people *one by one*. Then the *shofar gadol* — the great trumpet — sounds, and *those who were lost in the land of Assyria* and *those who were driven out to the land of Egypt* — the two great diasporas of the eighth and sixth centuries — return to *worship the LORD on the holy mountain at Jerusalem*. The chapter, and the Isaiah Apocalypse, ends in worship.

The poetry is dense with intertextual links to earlier Isaianic material: the vineyard answers chapter 5; the *day of the east wind* (v. 8) recalls Isaiah's own use of east-wind judgment imagery; the *holy mountain* of v. 13 picks up the Zion theology of chapters 2 and 11; the *shofar* is the same instrument that summons the worshipers in 18:3.

## Lens 3: Where This Sits in the Larger Story

Within the prophetic canon, the *Combat Myth* runs as a deep current under the Hebrew Bible's surface. Job 41 describes Leviathan in extended cosmological detail; Psalm 74:13-14 sings of the heads of the sea-monsters crushed in the wilderness (a reading that fuses Combat Myth with Exodus); Psalm 89:10 names *Rahab* (the parallel chaos-monster) as having been cut to pieces; Habakkuk 3:8-15 retells creation-as-victory-over-the-sea in YHWH's coming. The chaos-creature is never simply an evil to be vanquished — it is the *backdrop* of order, the residual disorder that creation itself implies. Jon Levenson's *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* (Princeton, 1988) is the foundational modern treatment: in Israelite cosmology, creation is not an absolute beginning but an ongoing victory of order over chaos, and Isaiah 27:1 is an eschatological reactualization of the originating victory.

The **Jewish trajectory** receives Isaiah 27 in several distinct streams. The early rabbis read v. 13 (*the great trumpet*) into the eschatological liturgy of *Rosh Hashanah* — the trumpet of the New Year became also the trumpet of messianic ingathering, with the *Shofar Shel Mashiach* tradition. The *Mishnah Sanhedrin* and later Talmudic discussions treat the gathering of the lost from Assyria and Egypt as a specifically *eschatological* event: the *kibbutz galuyot* (gathering of the exiles), which became one of the central petitions of the *Amidah* daily prayer. Leviathan in rabbinic literature undergoes a striking transformation: in the *Bavli* (Bava Batra 74b-75a), Leviathan becomes the eschatological banquet — the righteous in the world to come will feast on Leviathan's flesh, while his hide becomes the canopy (*sukkah*) of the messianic banquet. The mythological adversary is domesticated into the eschatological menu — a theological move whose meaning is debated (some readings see it as celebrating the conclusive victory; others as a kind of hopeful playful imagination).

The **Christian trajectory** picks up the Combat Myth and reframes it Christologically. Revelation 12 — the great red dragon, the ancient serpent, the deceiver — explicitly identifies the chaos-monster as Satan (*the great dragon was thrown down, that ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan*, Rev 12:9). Revelation 20:2 repeats the identification at the climactic binding of Satan. The slaying of Leviathan becomes, in the New Testament, the cosmic backdrop to Christ's defeat of the devil — Hebrews 2:14 (*through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil*) draws on this whole tradition. Patristic exegesis (Origen, Jerome, Augustine) reads Isaiah 27:1 Christologically as anticipating the cross-victory over Satan. The vineyard imagery of vv. 2-6 is taken up by Jesus in the parable of the wicked tenants (Mark 12:1-12; Matt 21:33-46) — and in John 15:1-8, Jesus identifies *himself* as the true vine, with his Father as the keeper (*ho georgos*). The shofar of v. 13 reappears in 1 Corinthians 15:52 (*the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised*) and 1 Thessalonians 4:16 (*with the trumpet of God*) as the eschatological summons.

NT use of chapter 27 is more allusive than direct: Revelation 12-13 draws heavily on the Leviathan-and-dragon imagery; the *vineyard* parables of the synoptic gospels assume Isaianic vineyard theology; Romans 11:25-27 (Paul on Israel's eventual restoration) draws on the gleaning-and-ingathering image of 27:12-13.

## Lens 4: What This Asks of Us — Tensions and Honest Reading

Three tensions sit on the surface of chapter 27.

**First — the Combat Myth and what it means for monotheism.** The fact that Isaiah 27:1 reproduces Ugaritic mythology almost verbatim has unsettled readers since the Ugaritic tablets were deciphered in the 1930s. Does Israel's God *fight* a real chaos-creature? Is Leviathan a *being*, or a metaphor, or a poetic personification?

The *demythologized* reading (some forms of liberal Protestant scholarship, and to a degree the rabbinic *Sanhedrin* allegorizations) treats Leviathan as poetic personification of the forces of disorder — historical empires, the threatening sea, anything chaotic. On this reading, the verse is sublimated mythology — myth used as poetic language about real-world realities.

The *cosmic-realist* reading (Levenson, Cross, Mark Smith, and a great deal of Christian patristic and apocalyptic tradition) takes the Combat Myth more seriously: the chapter's claim is that there *are* cosmic forces of disorder, that creation is not their absence but their defeat, and that the eschatological completion of creation requires a final defeat. This reading does not require *literal* belief in a sea-monster, but it does require taking seriously the claim that the world's persistent dysfunction has cosmic and not merely social-historical roots.

The steelman synthesis: Israel's distinct theological move is *not* the elimination of the Combat Myth but its *subordination*. In Babylon, the Combat Myth is fundamental — Marduk's victory over Tiamat *constitutes* Marduk's kingship. In Israel, YHWH is already king before the Combat begins (Ps 93, *the LORD reigns*); the Combat is an episode within YHWH's ongoing rule, not a foundation of it. This means Israel can *use* the Combat Myth (as Isaiah 27 does) without granting it cosmological priority. The mythological language is real but not constitutive. Where this leaves us: chapter 27 reminds us that the world is *not* in our hands and not even in its own hands; the chaos that creation has been struggling against will be definitively defeated, but on YHWH's calendar, not ours.

**Second — the question of measured judgment.** Verse 7's *has he struck them as he struck those who struck them?* and v. 8's *measure by measure* raise a hard pastoral question: is Israel's experience of suffering really *measured* against its enemies' worse fate? This is a comparative theology of suffering, and it cuts in two directions. On one hand, the verse is a *comfort* — your suffering is not without limit, not without proportion, not without an end. On the other hand, it can be a *cruelty* if applied carelessly to the suffering of innocent victims. The honest reading: the chapter is speaking to the corporate experience of *Israel as covenant people* — a community in covenant with YHWH, whose discipline is internal to that relationship. It is not a general theodicy of all human suffering. The covenantal frame is essential and should not be dropped.

**Third — the vineyard restoration and the question of judgment's reversibility.** Chapter 5 had pronounced devastating judgment on the vineyard: *I will remove its hedge*. Chapter 27 declares *I have no wrath*. Has YHWH changed his mind? Or had judgment been provisional all along?

The Reformed-confessional reading emphasizes that judgment in chapter 5 was real and was carried out (the destruction of Israel and Judah), but that YHWH's covenant-faithfulness extends *beyond* judgment into restoration. The judgment is real; the restoration is also real; the second does not negate the first. The Catholic-Augustinian reading similarly sees the discipline-then-restoration pattern as fundamental to God's mode of dealing with sinful humanity — *poena medicinalis* (medicinal punishment). The Jewish reading emphasizes *teshuvah* — repentance and return — and v. 9's atonement-through-altar-demolition fits this: the people's turn from idolatry is the precondition for restored relationship.

Where this leaves us: chapter 27 reminds us that judgment, in the prophetic theology, is *never* the final word. The vineyard that was abandoned is the vineyard that is restored. The *kerem chemed* of 27:2 is the same *kerem* of 5:1 — different chapter, different season, same vineyard.

The closing question: *what do we do with a tradition that uses the language of mythological combat to talk about real evil?* The chapter's answer is to take the mythological imagery seriously without absolutizing it: chaos is real, but YHWH is more real; the dragon is dangerous, but the sword is harder, greater, stronger; the vineyard was abandoned, but the keeper has returned and the briers can either burn or sue for peace. The shofar of v. 13 is, in the chapter's logic, the sound the gleaned-and-restored people hear when the long combat is over and the vineyard has come into its full fruit.

## The Thread

Isaiah 27 closes the Apocalypse by reaching all the way back to the cosmic combat at creation and forward to the eschatological gleaning of every scattered exile. The slaying of Leviathan, the restoration of the vineyard, the demolition of the altars of false worship, the great trumpet — these are all one event under different aspects: the day when YHWH's long contest with chaos comes to its final, measured, and definitive end.
