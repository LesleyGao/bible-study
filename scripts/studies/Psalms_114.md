# Psalm 114 — A Scholarly Study

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 114 is anonymous and stands without superscription in the Masoretic Text — a striking absence given that it is one of the most overtly Exodus-themed psalms in the Psalter. It belongs to Book V (Pss 107–150) and to **the Egyptian Hallel** (*Hallel Mitzri*, Pss 113–118). In rabbinic Passover practice, codified in Mishnah Pesahim 10, **Psalm 114 is the second psalm sung before the meal**, immediately after Ps 113, over the second cup of wine, following the *maggid* recital of the Exodus narrative. This places Ps 114 at the structural heart of the Seder: after the Haggadah retells the story in prose, the community sings the story in verse. The synoptic Gospels' note that Jesus and the disciples "sang the hymn" before going to the Mount of Olives (Mark 14:26 / Matt 26:30) refers in the rabbinic period to the post-meal portion (Pss 115–118), but the same evening's earlier liturgy would have included Pss 113 and 114.

In Hermann Gunkel's form-critical taxonomy, Ps 114 is best classified as a **historical hymn** (*Geschichtspsalm*) — but a deeply unusual one. Where the longer historical hymns (Pss 78, 105, 106, 135, 136) develop the Exodus narrative across many verses with explicit recitals, Ps 114 compresses the entire arc of Egypt → Sea → Wilderness → Jordan → Land into eight verses of compact, mythopoetic poetry. Sigmund Mowinckel called it a *kultlyrik* of the Passover festival. Claus Westermann (*Praise and Lament in the Psalms*) treated it as a unique fusion of hymnic and narrative modes, where the recital is so foreshortened that historical event passes directly into cosmic imagery. Hans-Joachim Kraus (*Psalmen*, BKAT, 1978) reads it as a *Festlied* — a festival song — designed for liturgical performance at the Passover.

The psalm is widely dated to the post-exilic period (sixth to fourth centuries BCE) on the basis of (a) its lapidary, near-elliptical style typical of late Hebrew poetry, (b) the phrase *me-am lo'ez* in v. 1 (a hapax in this exact form), and (c) its inclusion in the Hallel cluster. Some scholars (Erich Zenger, in *Hossfeld–Zenger*, *Psalms 3*, Hermeneia, 2011) argue for an earlier core that has been redacted into the Hallel, but the text as we have it presupposes the post-exilic liturgical context.

The opening line, *be-tzeit Yisrael mi-Mitzrayim beit Ya'aqov me-am lo'ez* (בְּצֵאת יִשְׂרָאֵל מִמִּצְרָיִם בֵּית יַעֲקֹב מֵעַם לֹעֵז, "when Israel went out from Egypt, the house of Jacob from a people of strange language," 114:1), pairs *Yisrael* with *beit Ya'aqov* in a parallel that runs throughout the psalm. The phrase *me-am lo'ez* is unusual: the verb *la'az* (לעז) means "to speak unintelligibly, in a foreign tongue" — the same root that gives medieval Jews the term *La'az* for the European vernaculars (Old French, Old Spanish, Old Italian) glossing biblical and Talmudic Hebrew in commentaries (cf. Rashi's *La'az* glosses). The psalm thus characterizes Egypt by its *otherness of speech*, not its political might, and Israel's deliverance is from a people whose language she did not share. This is an anthropological-cultural framing of slavery, not a military one.

Hebrew terms reward attention. The verb *nas* (v. 3, "fled" — *ha-yam ra'ah va-yanos*) is the language of military rout: the sea flees from Yahweh as a defeated army flees a victorious king. The Jordan *yissov le-achor* ("turns back," v. 3) — a hapax phrasing for the Jordan crossing of Joshua 3. The mountains *raqdu ke-eilim* ("skip like rams," v. 4) and the hills *kivnei-tzon* ("like lambs"); the verb *raqad* is the verb of festal dancing, used of David before the ark (2 Sam 6:14, 16). The closing *ha-hofkhi ha-tzur agam-mayim chalamish le-ma'yano-mayim* (הַהֹפְכִי הַצּוּר אֲגַם־מָיִם חַלָּמִישׁ לְמַעְיְנוֹ־מָיִם, v. 8) compresses the rock-water miracles of Exodus 17 and Numbers 20 with extraordinary economy — *tzur* (rock) and *chalamish* (flint) parallel, *agam* (pool) and *ma'yan* (spring) parallel.

ANE parallels: the cosmic-conflict imagery of the sea fleeing has roots in the Canaanite Baal-Yam (sea) myth (cf. the Ugaritic *Baal Cycle*) and the Babylonian Marduk-Tiamat conflict (*Enuma Elish*). But Ps 114 deliberately demythologizes: the sea does not fight Yahweh; it simply *sees* and flees. The sea is not a god to be defeated but a creature responding to its Maker. The personification is poetic-rhetorical, not mythological. DSS evidence: 4QPs(b) preserves portions of the Hallel cluster; the LXX joins Ps 114 with Ps 115 as a single composition (LXX Ps 113), part of the broader Greek-Hebrew numbering divergence in Books IV–V.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Psalm 114 is one of the most elegantly constructed compositions in the Psalter. Its eight verses fall into four perfectly paired strophes of two verses each, with relentless parallelism in every line. Robert Alter (*The Book of Psalms*, 2007) calls it "a small lyric masterpiece, a kind of poetic miniature in which the entire history of the Exodus is compressed into a single, dazzling tableau."

The strophic structure is mathematically clean:
- **Strophe A (vv. 1–2)**: the historical claim — Israel left Egypt; Judah became sanctuary, Israel dominion.
- **Strophe B (vv. 3–4)**: the cosmic response — sea fled, Jordan turned back, mountains and hills skipped.
- **Strophe C (vv. 5–6)**: rhetorical interrogation — what ails you, sea, Jordan, mountains, hills?
- **Strophe D (vv. 7–8)**: theological resolution — tremble, earth, at the Lord who turns rock into water.

The pattern is **A (history) → B (cosmos) → C (interrogation) → D (theological reveal)**. Strophe C echoes B verbatim in question form, which is one of the boldest poetic moves in the Hebrew Bible: the poet stops the action, addresses the natural elements directly, and demands an explanation. Then Strophe D delivers the answer not by naming Yahweh's act of deliverance but by naming Yahweh himself — *mi-lifnei adon chuli aretz* ("tremble, O earth, at the presence of the Lord," v. 7).

Robert Lowth's parallelism categories appear in textbook form. Synonymous parallelism dominates: "Israel went out from Egypt / the house of Jacob from a people of strange language" (v. 1); "Judah became his sanctuary / Israel his dominion" (v. 2); "the sea looked and fled / Jordan turned back" (v. 3); "the mountains skipped like rams / the hills like lambs" (v. 4). Synthetic parallelism builds the closing strophe: "tremble, O earth ... who turns the rock into a pool" (vv. 7–8).

There is no *selah* in Ps 114, consistent with the Book V Hallel context. The whole psalm functions as a single unbroken liturgical performance — the Seder community would not pause but carry the song through to Ps 115.

The most striking literary feature is the **personification of nature** in vv. 3–6. The sea "sees" (*ra'ah*) — what does the sea see? The text does not say. The Targum supplies the ark; midrashic tradition supplies Joseph's coffin (since Joseph's bones were carried with the people, Exod 13:19); some Christian readers supply Christ proleptically. The poet's silence is deliberate. The mountains "skip like rams" — a verb of festal joy applied to inanimate creation. The whole cosmos is treated as a responsive participant in the Exodus event, not merely its backdrop.

The **inclusio** of Yahweh's *presence* is implicit but crucial: the psalm never names Yahweh in the first six verses (the Tetragrammaton is absent — note this carefully) and only resolves the suspense in vv. 7–8 by naming *adon* (Lord) and *Eloah Ya'aqov* (the God of Jacob). The withholding of the Name is a piece of rhetorical genius: the entire cosmos responds to a presence the psalm refuses to name, until the climactic moment. This is *paranomasia* at the level of theology.

A subtle chiastic shape organizes the whole: **Egypt (out, v. 1) → Sea/Jordan flee (v. 3) → Jordan/Sea questioned (v. 5) → Rock turned to water (v. 8)**. The water imagery brackets the psalm — Israel comes *out* through the Sea, comes *into* a land where the rock yields water — and the Jordan crossing in the middle joins the two ends. The mountains/hills in vv. 4 and 6 frame the central interrogation.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Within Wilson's canonical-shape reading of Book V (*The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter*, 1985), Pss 113–114 form the *pre-meal* unit of the Hallel and rehearse the Exodus narrative as the foundational paradigm of Israel's identity before the Seder community drinks the second cup. The Hallel cluster as a whole (113–118) traces an arc: praise of Yahweh's universal kingship (113), Exodus narrative (114), anti-idol polemic (115), thanksgiving for personal deliverance (116), universal call to praise (117), royal entrance liturgy (118). David Mitchell (*The Message of the Psalter*, 1997) reads this cluster as the post-exilic community's eschatological-messianic anticipation: each Passover rehearses not only past Exodus but coming redemption.

The **Jewish trajectory** is exceptionally rich. Mishnah Pesahim 10 places Ps 114 immediately after the *maggid* (the Haggadah's narrative core) and before the meal proper. The Talmud (b. Pesahim 117a) discusses the proper division of the Hallel and the precise blessings to recite. *Midrash Tehillim* reads Ps 114 as a meditation on the cosmic significance of the Exodus: the sea fled because it recognized its Creator; the mountains skipped because they witnessed Sinai. Rashi reads *me-am lo'ez* as "a people whose speech was hard upon them" — emphasizing the Egyptian-Hebrew linguistic alienation. Ibn Ezra notes the rhetorical device of the unnamed Yahweh through vv. 1–6 as a deliberate mystery the psalm withholds until v. 7. Radak treats vv. 3–4 as poetic personification, not literal cosmic seismic event. The medieval *Haggadah* preserves the singing of Ps 114 over the second cup as a fixed element of the Seder, a practice continued in Ashkenazi, Sephardi, Mizrahi, and Yemenite communities. Modern Jewish thought engages Ps 114 as the lyrical heart of the Seder: Buber on the Exodus as encounter; Heschel on the prophetic memory; Levenson (*Sinai and Zion*, 1985) on the link between Exodus and Sinai; Sarna (*Exploring Exodus*, 1986) on the Exodus as Israel's foundational metaphor. The link to *La'az* in medieval Jewish exegesis is itself a kind of Diaspora reception: every commentator writing in Old French or Old Spanish glossing Hebrew was, in a small way, re-inhabiting the *me-am lo'ez* condition.

The **Christian trajectory** runs along multiple lines. Augustine (*Enarrationes in Psalmos*) reads Ps 114 typologically: Egypt is the world, the sea is baptism, Jordan is Christ's baptism, and the entry into the land is the church's eschatological inheritance. Cassiodorus develops the typology more elaborately. Aquinas treats the personification of nature theologically — the cosmos is responsive to its Creator's saving acts. Luther's reading emphasizes the cosmic scope of the Exodus and finds in v. 7 ("tremble, O earth") a call to repentance before the holy God. Calvin reads the psalm as a celebration of God's providence and power over creation. **The most striking Christian liturgical reception is the *Exsultet* of the Easter Vigil**, the great Latin proclamation sung at the lighting of the Paschal candle, which weaves Exodus typology — "this is the night when you brought our fathers out of Egypt" — directly into the celebration of Christ's resurrection. The Catholic Holy Saturday and the Orthodox Pascha both sing Exodus themes through Ps 114's lens. Among the Reformers, Calvin's Geneva Psalter set Ps 114 to a memorable tune that became a staple of Reformed worship; the Bay Psalm Book (1640) included it among the colonial American Puritan repertoire. **Dante's Divine Comedy** opens its *Purgatorio* (II.46) with the souls singing *In exitu Israel de Aegypto* — the Vulgate's incipit of Ps 114 — as a typology of the soul's passage from sin to grace. Dante later, in the *Letter to Cangrande*, uses Ps 114 as his standard example of the four-fold sense of Scripture (literal, allegorical, moral, anagogical).

Among modern critical readers: Hans-Joachim Kraus reads the psalm as a *Passa-Lied* (Passover song). John Goldingay (*Psalms 90–150*, BCOTWP, 2008) emphasizes the personification of nature as a theological claim about creation's responsiveness to its Maker. Walter Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*, 1984) treats it as the lyrical center of the Hallel and the heart of Israel's narrative theology. James Mays, Patrick Miller, and Beth Tanner all stress the compactness — that the psalm achieves what longer recitals do not. Hossfeld–Zenger emphasize the deliberate withholding of the Tetragrammaton as a rhetorical-theological move. Erich Zenger reads vv. 7–8 as the climactic theological revelation: the cosmos trembles before the *adon* who turns rock into water — a Yahweh whose power is creative-life-giving, not merely destructive.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The central theological tension of Psalm 114 lies in its **personification of nature**. The sea sees and flees, the Jordan turns back, the mountains and hills skip. Are these poetic figures, mythological residues, or claims about the metaphysical structure of creation?

**Position 1: The poetic-personification reading (Calvin, Radak, most modern critical scholars).** The personification is a literary device, not a metaphysical claim. The poet ascribes agency to natural elements as a rhetorical means of magnifying Yahweh's act. The sea did not literally "see" anything; the verbiage dramatizes the awesome power of the Exodus event. Steelman: this reading respects the poetic genre and avoids reading later metaphysics into a Hebrew lyric. The personification's force is in its imaginative power, not in any claim about whether seas have eyes.

**Position 2: The mythopoetic-residue reading (Frank Cross, *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*, 1973; Mark Smith, *The Early History of God*, 2002).** Ps 114 preserves elements of the older Canaanite cosmic-conflict myth, in which Yahweh-as-warrior defeats the chaos-sea (Yam) and establishes order. The "fleeing sea" is a demythologized echo of the Baal-Yam combat. Steelman: there is real evidence of cosmic-conflict imagery throughout the Hebrew Bible (Pss 74, 89, 93; Isa 51; Hab 3), and Ps 114 stands in this stream. The deliberate demythologization — the sea simply flees, with no fight — is itself a polemical use of the older myth: Yahweh is so much greater than the Canaanite gods that there is no contest.

**Position 3: The cosmic-creational reading (Goldingay, Hossfeld–Zenger, the broader Hebrew Bible's creation theology).** The personification is neither merely poetic nor mythological residue but a genuine theological claim that creation is not inert. Mountains can skip, seas can flee, because creation is a responsive participant in the relationship between God and his people. Steelman: this reading aligns Ps 114 with Job 38–41 (where the sea is Yahweh's swaddled child, the morning stars sing), with Romans 8:19–22 (creation groaning), and with the broader prophetic tradition of cosmic response (Isa 35; Joel 2). The psalm makes a quietly radical metaphysical claim: salvation is cosmic in scope, not merely human.

**Position 4: The Christological-typological reading (Augustine, Dante, the Easter Vigil tradition).** Ps 114 is the Exodus made song, and the Exodus is the type of which Christ's death and resurrection are the antitype. The sea fleeing is baptism; the Jordan turning back is the Jordan at Christ's baptism; the rock yielding water is the rock from which living water flows (cf. 1 Cor 10:4 on the rock as Christ; John 7:37–39 on the water from Christ). Steelman: this reading rests on a long Christian tradition with deep roots in 1 Corinthians 10 and John's Gospel, and it does not deny the literal-historical sense; it adds the typological sense to it. Dante's choice of Ps 114 as his *locus classicus* for the four-fold sense was not arbitrary.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 114 is the lyrical heart of the Hallel and one of the most artistically perfect pieces in the Psalter. Its theological wager is that the Exodus is so cosmically significant that the natural world responds — that creation itself is summoned to *tremble* (v. 7) before the God who acts to redeem his people. The withholding of the Tetragrammaton through six verses, then its delayed naming in v. 7, performs a small theological revelation: the cosmos responds first; the people who know the Name speak it last, and only as the answer to a question the sea and the Jordan have already answered with their bodies.

The closing question is whether we, in our late-modern disenchanted reading of nature, can still hear what the psalm assumes — that the rock yielding water in Numbers 20 is not a one-time miracle but a sample of who Yahweh is. The verb in v. 8 is *participial* (*ha-hofkhi*, "the one who turns") — not a past tense. Yahweh turns rocks into pools. Yahweh turns flint into springs. The Exodus is not a finished event but a perpetual divine signature, and the Hallel is the song that teaches Israel — and the church, sitting at the Last Supper — to read the world that way. When Jesus and the disciples sang this song before going to Gethsemane, they were rehearsing a theology of cosmic deliverance whose deepest test was about to come.
