# Exodus 39: The Work Was Finished

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Exodus 39 describes the making of the priestly vestments and the climactic delivery of the completed tabernacle to Moses. Every element answers to an earlier command in Exodus 28, and the chapter closes with Moses inspecting, approving, and blessing the work. To read the chapter historically is to read it against the vast ANE archive of priestly costume, sacred inventory, and royal-temple commissioning.

The ephod (*ʾēpôd*, אֵפוֹד), breastpiece (*ḥōšen*, חֹשֶׁן), robe of the ephod (*mĕʿîl*, מְעִיל), tunics, turbans, sash, and holy crown (*nēzer haqqōdeš*, נֵזֶר הַקֹּדֶשׁ) constitute a priestly costume kit with striking ANE parallels. Mesopotamian priestly garments — documented in Neo-Assyrian temple texts and preserved in Ur III and Kassite reliefs — included ceremonial aprons, shoulder ornaments with deity-symbols, and headbands engraved with divine names. The Emar ritual texts (14th-13th c. BC) describe the installation of the *entu*-priestess of Baal with a dressing sequence mirroring Exodus 28-29: linen undergarment, colored outer robe, belt, headdress, inscribed ornament.

Egyptian priestly vestments are especially pertinent. The pectoral plates of New Kingdom priests featured twelve stones set in gold filigree, often in four rows of three, representing cosmic order; Tutankhamun's pectorals are the most celebrated but the pattern is pervasive. The high priest of Amun at Karnak wore a linen robe with gold-threaded embroidery. Diadems inscribed with sacred names — the Egyptian *wer*-diadem — provide formal precedent for the *ṣîṣ*, the gold plate inscribed "Holy to the LORD" fastened to the turban. Kenneth Kitchen has emphasized these parallels as part of his Ramesside-dating argument; Othmar Keel reads the breastpiece stones as a visual cosmology.

The twelve stones in four rows of three (vv. 10-13) are difficult to identify precisely. The Hebrew terms map onto ancient semiprecious stones whose modern equivalents are debated. Sardius (carnelian), topaz, emerald/carbuncle, sapphire (probably lapis lazuli), jacinth, agate, amethyst, beryl, onyx, jasper. The LXX, Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.166-172), Rashi, and Ibn Ezra differ. The stones were widely traded in the late Bronze Age across Mesopotamia, Egypt, and the Levant; caches from Ugarit, Byblos, and Mycenae confirm availability.

The golden bells and pomegranates on the hem (vv. 25-26) have Levantine parallels. Small gold bells (*paʿămôn*) appear at Byblos and in Egyptian priestly contexts. Pomegranate (*rimmôn*) decorations are among the most common cultic motifs — genuine pomegranate-decorated cultic objects are attested at Arad and Ta'anach.

The colophon phrase "the tabernacle, the tent of meeting" in verse 32 combines *miškān* (dwelling) and *ʾōhel môʿēd* (tent of meeting) — two terms that scholars since Wellhausen have treated as indicating underlying sources. Frank Moore Cross, and more recently Knohl and Baruch Schwartz, have argued that P synthesizes the two terminologies.

Authorship and dating: Priestly source throughout. The final form of the execution narrative (Ex 35-40) corresponds item-for-item to the command narrative (Ex 25-31), with the striking exception of the LXX rearrangement. John William Wevers and Anneli Aejmelaeus have documented the Old Greek's shorter and reordered text of Ex 35-40; the ephod description in particular appears in different sequence. Emanuel Tov has treated the case as unresolved between translator compression and a variant Hebrew Vorlage.

Archaeological footnote: the "Priestly Blessing" silver amulets from Ketef Hinnom (Gabriel Barkay, 1979) date to the late 7th century BC and preserve in tiny inscribed silver the Aaronic blessing of Numbers 6:24-26 — material evidence that the priestly-garment ideology had real Iron Age currency, not only post-exilic retrojection.

Key Hebrew terms: *kohēn gādôl* (כֹּהֵן גָּדוֹל), "high priest"; *ṣîṣ* (צִיץ), "plate" or "flower," the gold rosette-plate; *qōdeš la-YHWH* (קֹדֶשׁ לַיהוָה), "Holy to the LORD," the engraved inscription; *wattēkel* (וַתֵּכֶל), "was finished" (v. 32), the exact verbal root used in Gen 2:1; *wayĕbārek* (וַיְבָרֶךְ), "and he blessed" (v. 43), the exact verb used in Gen 2:3.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Two literary forces drive this chapter: the sevenfold refrain "as the LORD had commanded Moses," and the Genesis-1 echo at the end.

The refrain (*kaʾăšer ṣiwwâ YHWH ʾet-Mōšeh*) appears seven times in Exodus 39: verses 1, 5, 7, 21, 26, 29, 31, each marking the completion of one element of the priestly costume. Robert Alter has called this kind of structured repetition "the poetics of narrative completion" — the same rhythmic marking that Hebrew narrative reserves for moments when obedience is the whole point. Each vestment-component meets its mandate; the refrain is the seam stitched into the text. In verse 32 a broader summary takes over: "the people of Israel did according to all that the LORD had commanded Moses; so they did" — doubled, emphatic. In verse 42: "According to all that the LORD had commanded Moses, so the people of Israel had done all the work." In verse 43: "as the LORD had commanded, so had they done it." The chapter thus intensifies its refrain as it moves toward the blessing.

The structural parallel to Genesis 1-2 is the chapter's most discussed literary feature and has become foundational to modern tabernacle scholarship. Verse 32: *wattēkel kol-ʿăbōdat miškan ʾōhel môʿēd* — "Thus all the work of the tabernacle of the tent of meeting was finished." Genesis 2:1: *wayĕkullû ha-šāmayim wĕhā-ʾāreṣ* — "Thus the heavens and the earth were finished." Same root (*klh*). Verse 43: "And Moses saw all the work, and behold, they had done it... Then Moses blessed them." Genesis 1:31: "And God saw everything that he had made, and behold, it was very good." Genesis 2:3: "So God blessed the seventh day and made it holy." Same verbs: *rāʾâ* (see), *hinnēh* (behold), *bārak* (bless). The cluster of lexical links is not accidental. Peter Enns, Jon Levenson, and Terence Fretheim have all pointed to these as the clearest evidence of a deliberate cosmogonic parallel.

Levenson's argument in *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* and earlier work: the tabernacle is *miqdāš* as microcosm. What God did in creation, Israel has now replicated in the sanctuary. The construction of the tabernacle is the renewal of cosmos after the near-rupture of the golden calf (Ex 32). Fretheim, in his Interpretation commentary, pushes the same argument toward pastoral theology: creation is not simply background for Exodus; Exodus is the renewal of creation. Samuel Balentine (*The Torah's Vision of Worship*) reads the cosmic parallel ecclesially: worship is how creation is kept alive. L. Michael Morales (*Who Shall Ascend the Mountain of the Lord*) treats Ex 39-40 as the pivot by which the whole Pentateuch moves from creation-lost-in-Eden to creation-recovered-in-sanctuary.

The rabbinic tradition derived from verse 32 (and the surrounding labor-language) the thirty-nine categories of forbidden Sabbath labor (the *melakhot*, Bavli Shabbat 49b). The argument: the tabernacle work is the paradigm of human making; Sabbath refrains from exactly those categories because the tabernacle work images God's creative labor. The connection between tabernacle and Sabbath is thus embedded in the rabbinic conceptual structure of Jewish life.

Genre: priestly installation narrative, a subtype of the broader construction-completion form. Verses 33-41 constitute a presentation inventory — the completed tabernacle brought to Moses, item by item. The listing mirrors the order of instruction in Ex 25-30 but telescopes it. The inspection (v. 43) is a classical ANE element of royal/divine commissioning: the builder presents his work, the master inspects and approves.

Alter has noted the artistry of the ephod-description: the hammering of gold leaf into threads (v. 3) is an unusual technical detail — elsewhere unattested in the Hebrew Bible — and reads as a specification of how gold was woven into fabric. The *pȃz*-thread technique is documented archaeologically from Egyptian New Kingdom tombs (gold-thread embroidery on linen). The literary effect is to make the reader pause over the artisans' craft: a moment of wonder at the technique itself.

Textually, the LXX here inserts chapter 39's content at different points in its Exodus account, and some material from MT Ex 39 appears earlier or not at all in the Old Greek. This is one of the best-known divergences in Septuagint studies.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** Ben Sira 45:6-13 praises Aaron's vestments at length — "he put on the robe of honor." Wisdom of Solomon 18:24 treats the garments as cosmic symbol: "upon the long robe was the whole world." Philo (*Life of Moses* 2.109-135; *Special Laws* 1.82-97) elaborates: the breastpiece stones are the zodiac, the blue robe the air, the golden bells the spheres' music. Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.179-187) adopts Philo's cosmic typology for a Roman audience.

Rabbinic interpretation works on several fronts. Mishnah Yoma details the high priest's vestments on the Day of Atonement. The sevenfold refrain in Ex 39 is read as evidence that the tabernacle-work parallels the seven days of creation. Exodus Rabbah 50-52 develops the parallel elaborately: Moses's blessing at v. 43 corresponds to God's blessing at Genesis 2:3. Bavli Shabbat 49b derives the thirty-nine forbidden Sabbath *melakhot* from tabernacle labor. Pesikta d'Rav Kahana treats the completion as a cosmic milestone.

Rashi on v. 43 preserves the blessing: "May the Shekhinah rest upon the work of your hands" — a direct anticipation of Ex 40:34. Ibn Ezra works philologically. Ramban presents the tabernacle as a portable Sinai. Maimonides (*Guide* 3.45) reads the vestments rationalistically. The Zohar develops the vestments as kabbalistic correspondences between priestly body and supernal world. Sarna's JPS commentary treats the refrain as liturgical; Sacks reads the chapter as the restoration of cosmos after the calf; Zornberg dwells on the blessing as the closing of a long arc from Pharaoh's decree to the glory about to descend.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** Patristic writers read the priestly vestments typologically as figures of Christ. The Letter to the Hebrews (especially chapters 7-10) establishes the basic Christian reading: Christ as high priest, his body as the veil, his sacrifice as the ultimate entry into the Holy of Holies. Origen (*Homilies on Exodus* 9) reads each vestment as a figure of a virtue of Christ. Gregory of Nyssa (*Life of Moses* 2.180-200) reads the garments as stages of the virtuous soul's ascent. Chrysostom on Hebrews develops Christ-as-priest in detail. Jerome's *Letter 64 to Fabiola* is an extended allegorical meditation on the priestly garments: the linen tunic is purity, the ephod is apostolic ministry, the breastpiece is memory of the church's members, the gold plate "Holy to the LORD" is confession.

Medieval readings extend Jerome. Aquinas (*Summa* I-II q. 102, a. 5, obj. 10) treats the priestly vestments as ceremonial precepts that figure Christ. Bonaventure's *Itinerarium* uses the garments as a ladder of contemplation. Bede's *On the Tabernacle* is an elaborate typological commentary.

The Reformation split the tradition sharply. Luther's *Lectures on Exodus* reads the vestments as figures of Christ's self-donation, but insists they are fulfilled and therefore abolished. Calvin's *Harmony of the Law* is careful: the vestments taught Israel about Christ, but the church has no priestly vestments because Christ is priest. The Puritan tradition and especially Jonathan Edwards (*Images and Shadows*) treats every stone and thread as a type.

Critical scholarship since Wellhausen has read the vestments as Second Temple priestly ideology retrojected. Menahem Haran's work on the priestly cult, Milgrom's on P, and Israel Knohl's on the Holiness School have complicated Wellhausen's dating. Brevard Childs's canonical reading reads the chapter as Scripture: the question is what the text does in the canon, not whether a Moses-era high priest wore exactly this ephod.

Contemporary scholarship — T. Desmond Alexander, G. K. Beale, L. Michael Morales, N. T. Wright — reads the chapter inside the biblical-theological argument about priesthood, presence, and new creation. Alexander's *From Eden to the New Jerusalem* treats the sanctuary-priesthood complex as the backbone of biblical eschatology. Beale's *The Temple and the Church's Mission* argues that the priestly garments inaugurated in Ex 39 point toward Christ's priesthood and the church's priesthood.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Both traditions read the chapter as the narrative completion of the sanctuary, and both read the Genesis-1 parallels as deliberate. Jewish tradition extends priestly identity forward into rabbinic leadership, synagogue service, and the ongoing priestly-blessing ritual; the garments will be needed again when the Temple is rebuilt. Christian tradition reads the garments as fulfilled in Christ, whose body is the new temple and whose priesthood is eternal. The gold plate "Holy to the LORD" fixed to the high priest's forehead is read in Jewish tradition as the priestly office; in Christian tradition (and in Rev 22:4's "his name will be on their foreheads") as the eschatological identity of the redeemed. Both traditions see the sevenfold refrain as something like liturgy embedded in narrative; both see Moses's blessing at v. 43 as climactic.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What does the Genesis-1 parallel mean? Is the tabernacle a new creation, or is it something less?**

*Catholic* (CCC 1094, 1179; Joseph Ratzinger *The Spirit of the Liturgy*): the tabernacle is indeed a microcosm, and the sanctuary re-presents creation sacramentally. The Mass stands in continuity: every eucharistic celebration is a renewal of cosmos. Creation is fulfilled in worship.

*Reformed* (Michael Horton *People and Place*; Richard Gaffin; G. K. Beale): the tabernacle is a typological new creation, pointing forward to Christ and to the eschaton. The parallel is real but provisional — creation is not renewed in the tabernacle in any ontological sense; the tabernacle is a sign anticipating the new creation that breaks in with Christ's resurrection.

*Lutheran* (Luther's *Lectures on Genesis* and on Exodus; Oswald Bayer): the tabernacle is promise-embodied. It is not a new creation in itself but the concrete place where God's promise meets his people. The Genesis parallel is preaching: what God did on the cosmic scale he now does on the scale of a tent.

*Orthodox* (Schmemann *For the Life of the World*; Zizioulas *Being as Communion*; Florovsky): the tabernacle is the original liturgical cosmos. Creation is intrinsically doxological; the sanctuary is creation's truth disclosed. The Genesis-1 parallel is not metaphor but ontology.

*Jewish* (Ramban; Levenson *Creation and the Persistence of Evil*; Sacks; Heschel *The Sabbath*): the tabernacle is indeed a new creation, not merely figuratively. Creation was disrupted by human sin (the calf); the tabernacle re-establishes cosmic order. Heschel's argument that the Sabbath is a cathedral in time and the tabernacle a cathedral in space stands in this line.

*Critical* (Fretheim *Exodus*; Balentine *The Torah's Vision of Worship*; Meyers): the parallel is the text's central theological claim — the redactors are saying that worship is creation, that without sanctuary the world is incomplete.

Steelman for the least intuitive position — Reformed typology that calls the parallel "real but provisional": if the parallel were more than typological, then Christ would not be needed to fulfill it, since the tabernacle would already be new creation. Therefore the parallel must be genuine (not a mere literary trick) and at the same time awaiting its fulfillment.

*Where this leaves us:* the parallel is unmistakable; the disagreement is about its ontological weight. Whether one reads it sacramentally, eschatologically, typologically, or ontologically, the tabernacle at the end of Exodus 39 stands in the position creation occupies at the end of Genesis 1. Moses blesses, as God blessed.

**Tension 2: What does priestly mediation mean after the high priest puts on his robes?**

*Catholic* (Aquinas III q. 22; CCC 1539-1547 on ordained priesthood): the high priestly garments inaugurate an ordained priesthood that will continue in the apostolic ministry. Christ is the eternal High Priest, and the church's priests participate in his priesthood sacramentally. The vestments of Ex 39 are fulfilled and continued.

*Reformed* (Calvin *Institutes* 4.6, 4.18; the priesthood of all believers): Christ alone is priest in the new covenant. The vestments of Ex 39 are fulfilled in Christ and abolished for the church; no human priesthood mediates between God and the believer. The priestly office is generalized to the whole people.

*Lutheran* (Luther *The Freedom of a Christian*; *On the Babylonian Captivity of the Church*): all believers are priests (1 Peter 2:9), but a functional pastoral office is retained by order for the sake of preaching and sacrament. The vestments fall, but the office of Word and sacrament continues.

*Orthodox* (Schmemann; Florovsky): the priesthood is ecclesial and sacramental, and the vestments of the Divine Liturgy deliberately echo the Exodus pattern. The Orthodox priest's stole, cross, and crown preserve visual continuity with the Aaronic vestments.

*Jewish* (Maimonides *Mishneh Torah Hilkhot Klei ha-Mikdash*; the tradition of the *kohanim*): the priestly office is inherited, remains in force, and will be reinstated when the Temple is rebuilt. The garments themselves await remaking.

*Critical/historical* (Milgrom; Haran; Knohl; Jonathan Klawans *Purity, Sacrifice, and the Temple*): priestly mediation in Ex 39 is the Priestly source's vision of holiness-management and access-control, which stands in tension with prophetic critiques of cult (Isaiah 1, Amos 5).

Steelman for the least intuitive position — the Jewish awaiting of rebuilt-temple priesthood: if the tabernacle genuinely inaugurates a creation-renewing priesthood in continuity with Sinai, then its suspension (post-70 CE) is a theological emergency, and its restoration is an eschatological necessity. This is not nostalgia but cosmological seriousness.

*Where this leaves us:* every living tradition retains some form of priestly memory. Christian traditions differ sharply on whether the priesthood continues in persons or dissolves into Christ's unique priesthood. Jewish tradition holds the memory active. The golden plate "Holy to the LORD" is read very differently across these positions — as ongoing ordained office, as Christological witness, as eschatological expectation, as whole-people identity.

**One unresolved question:** If the work "was finished" in Ex 39:32 with the lexical signature of Genesis 2:1, and if Moses's blessing in Ex 39:43 parallels God's blessing in Genesis 2:3 — why is the Sabbath not mentioned here at all? The parallel seems incomplete. Why does Exodus defer the Sabbath-completion and go instead in Ex 40 to the erection and to the glory filling the tent? What does the creation-pattern's deliberate non-completion mean for how we read the end of Exodus?

## The Thread

**Zechariah 3** — Joshua the high priest stands before the angel of YHWH, filthy garments are removed, clean vestments are placed on him, including a clean turban. The prophet stages a visual redo of Exodus 39. The restoration of the priesthood after exile is described in the grammar of Ex 39: vestments, turban, service.

**Isaiah 61:10** — "I will greatly rejoice in the LORD... for he has clothed me with the garments of salvation; he has covered me with the robe of righteousness, as a bridegroom decks himself like a priest with a beautiful headdress." The prophet reads Aaronic vestment-investiture eschatologically: Israel will be clothed in priestly salvation.

**Hebrews 7-10** — the letter's central argument reworks Ex 39. Christ as high priest wears no linen ephod; his body is the veil (Heb 10:20), his blood the atoning sacrifice. The vestments of Ex 39 are neither denigrated nor retained; they are transfigured into the Christological reality.

**Revelation 21:22** — "I saw no temple in it, for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb." The vestments of Ex 39 had their ultimate fulfillment in a city with no temple and therefore no high priest in ephod and breastpiece. And yet Rev 22:4 says of the redeemed: "his name will be on their foreheads." The gold plate "Holy to the LORD" affixed to Aaron's turban has become, in John's vision, the inscription on every believer's brow. The work was finished in Ex 39, and in Rev 22 it is still being finished, on a different surface.
