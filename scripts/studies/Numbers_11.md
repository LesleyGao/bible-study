# Numbers 11: The Fire of Taberah, the Graves of Craving

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Numbers, *Bĕmidbar* (בְּמִדְבַּר) in Hebrew, pivots in chapter 10 from Sinai instruction to journey narrative. Chapter 11 is therefore the first narrative episode *in motion* — and it is a disaster. The book's macro-structure (Sinai 1:1–10:10; journey to Kadesh 10:11–14; wilderness 15–19; journey to Moab 20–21; plains of Moab 22–36) puts this chapter in what Dennis Olson famously named the "generation of the wilderness" section: the old generation that came out of Egypt will die off, chapter by chapter, before Numbers is done. The first death — by divine fire at Taberah and by plague at Kibroth-hattaavah — happens here.

Authorship and dating again shape interpretation. Wellhausen and Noth assigned much of Num 11 to the older Yahwist (J) or the Elohist (E), rather than the Priestly (P) source that dominates chs. 1–10. The reasoning: Num 11 uses the tetragrammaton YHWH freely, portrays God anthropomorphically ("his anger was kindled"), and shows Moses in psychological distress — features more typical of J/E than of P. Martin Noth (*Numbers*, 1968) treated the chapter as two originally independent traditions (the elders-with-spirit tradition and the quail-plague tradition) woven together by a late redactor. Thomas Dozeman (*Exodus*, 2009; forthcoming Numbers) and Baruch Levine (*Numbers 1–20*, AB, 1993) accept the composite theory but emphasize the artistic integration. Against the documentary-hypothesis frame, Kenneth Kitchen (*On the Reliability of the Old Testament*, 2003) notes that the complaint-form of v. 4–6 — with its specific food list — matches Late Bronze Age Egyptian laborers' ration-complaints attested in Deir el-Medina ostraca and Papyrus Anastasi IV, where workers grumble specifically about quality of beer, fish, and garlic rations.

The food list itself is historically specific and telling. The *cucumbers* (*qiššuʾîm*, קִשֻּׁאִים), *melons* (*ʾăbaṭṭiḥîm*, אֲבַטִּחִים), *leeks* (*ḥāṣîr*, חָצִיר), *onions* (*bĕṣālîm*, בְּצָלִים), and *garlic* (*šûmîm*, שׁוּמִים) of v. 5 are precisely the Nile-Delta garden vegetables that are archaeologically attested in Egyptian laborer diets from the Old Kingdom onward. Onions and garlic in particular were staple Egyptian foods — Herodotus (*Histories* 2.125) famously reports that onions, garlic, and radishes were inscribed on the Great Pyramid as the rations paid to the workers. Hoffmeier (*Israel in Egypt*, 1996) and Nili Shupak have both argued that the specificity of this list is a detail too Egyptian to have been invented from exilic Judah. The complaint is *about* Egyptian food because the complainers remember Egyptian food.

The "mixed multitude" or "rabble" of v. 4 is *ʾăsapsuf* (אֲסַפְסֻף) — a hapax legomenon (word occurring only once in the Hebrew Bible), doubled from the root *ʾāsap* (אָסַף, "to gather"), so literally "the gathering of gatherings" or "the collection-of-collectors." The likely referent is the *ʿēreb rab* (עֵרֶב רַב), the "mixed multitude" of Exod 12:38 — non-Israelite foreigners who joined the Exodus. Rashi at Num 11:4 specifies that the *ʾăsapsuf* *are* the *ʿēreb rab*, the gentile hangers-on who originated the craving and pulled the Israelites along. Ibn Ezra qualifies this, suggesting the term is pejorative for *any* disorderly segment of the camp. Ramban reads it as a sociological marker: the least-integrated segment of the community is where moral contagion begins.

*Taberah* (תַּבְעֵרָה, v. 3) is the place-name formed from the root *bāʿar* (בָּעַר, "to burn"), so "Burning." *Kibroth-hattaavah* (קִבְרוֹת הַתַּאֲוָה, v. 34) is "Graves of Craving" — *qĕbārôt* (קְבָרוֹת) from *qeber* (קֶבֶר, "grave") plus *taʾăwâ* (תַּאֲוָה, "craving, lust"). Both names are etiological. Neither site has been securely identified archaeologically, though various proposals (e.g., the Ruweisat Ridge east of Jebel Musa) have been advanced on the basis of the general Sinai itinerary. Kitchen and Hoffmeier note that the Late Bronze Age Sinai route is genuinely fragmentary in the archaeological record, and place-name preservation through Bedouin tradition is unreliable; the etiologies are likely older than our ability to locate them.

Manna (*mān*, מָן) in v. 7–9 is described with two comparisons: like coriander seed and like *bĕdōlaḥ* (בְּדֹלַח, "bdellium"), a fragrant resin from the *Commiphora* genus of Arabia and East Africa — the same substance mentioned alongside gold and onyx in Gen 2:12. The comparison is tactile, not scalar: bdellium is amber-pearl in color and waxy in texture. The etiological folk etymology of manna (*mān hûʾ*, "what is it?" Exod 16:15) has generated a long list of naturalistic explanations: tamarisk-insect honeydew (F. S. Bodenheimer, "The Manna of Sinai," *Biblical Archaeologist* 10, 1947), lichen (Allegro), the sap of tamarix mannifera. Milgrom, Levine, and Cole all treat these as reductive — the biblical narrative's theological point is that *what* manna was matters less than *where* it came from (dew from heaven, daily).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is a chiasm in crisis form. The outer frame is the divine-fire/divine-plague pair (vv. 1–3 and vv. 31–34), both keyed to the complaint-ignites-wrath pattern. The inner material contains Moses' own complaint-crisis (vv. 10–15), which mirrors the people's complaint but inverts its object: the people complain about God; Moses complains about the people. At the theological center sit the seventy elders and the Eldad-Medad pericope (vv. 16–30), where the Spirit is poured out.

Genre-wise, Num 11 is classic *murmuring narrative* — what German scholarship names *Murrgeschichte*. George Coats's monograph *Rebellion in the Wilderness* (1968) identified ten such narratives in the Pentateuch (Exod 14, 15, 16, 17; Num 11, 13–14, 16, 20, 21, 25), each with the same basic shape: initial complaint, divine response, partial resolution with residual judgment. Coats observed that the complaints move from *legitimate* (thirst, hunger) in Exodus to *illegitimate* (craving, contempt) in Numbers. Num 11:1–3 is the pivot — the people complain about their "misfortunes" (*rāʿ*, רָע, generically "evil, trouble"), without specifying what the misfortune is, and divine fire responds. The vague complaint is precisely what marks it as illegitimate.

The literary centerpiece is Moses' prayer in vv. 11–15, often called the most anguished speech by any prophet in the Hebrew Bible. Its form is the individual lament, compressed: accusation ("why have you dealt ill with your servant?"), protestation of innocence ("did I conceive all this people?"), petition (v. 15, "kill me at once"). The phrase *mōh śĕnēʾtî* (Moses' actual wording in v. 15, with the idiom "kill me at once... that I may not see my wretchedness") is structurally identical to Jeremiah's lament in Jer 20:14–18 and Elijah's in 1 Kgs 19:4. Brueggemann (*The Prophetic Imagination*, 1978) treats Num 11:11–15 as the foundational text for the Israelite prophetic type: the prophet who cannot bear the burden God has placed on him, and who prays to die.

Eldad and Medad (vv. 26–30) function as a literary surprise. The text has already named the seventy elders as the authorized recipients of the Spirit; two men who did not come to the tent receive it anyway. Their names are built from the same consonantal base — *ʾEldād* (אֶלְדָּד, "God has loved") and *Mēdād* (מֵידָד, "beloved" or "object of love") — a paronomastic pair, almost certainly intentional. The scene is narratively economical: six verses, three characters (Eldad/Medad as a unit, the young informer, Joshua, Moses), one punchline. Moses' response in v. 29 — *ûmî yittēn kol-ʿam YHWH nĕbîʾîm kî-yittēn YHWH ʾet-rûḥô ʿălêhem* (וּמִי יִתֵּן כָּל־עַם יְהוָה נְבִיאִים כִּי־יִתֵּן יְהוָה אֶת־רוּחוֹ עֲלֵיהֶם), "Would that all YHWH's people were prophets, that YHWH would put his Spirit upon them!" — is arguably the most democratic single line in the Pentateuch.

Textual variants: the LXX expands v. 26's description of Eldad and Medad's names and adds minor clarifying phrases; the Samaritan Pentateuch is substantively close to the MT. 4QNumᵇ is fragmentary for this chapter but supports the MT at several points. The Targum Pseudo-Jonathan adds a famously speculative midrashic expansion, naming the specific prophecies Eldad and Medad uttered (concerning the war of Gog and Magog) — an elaboration that will be taken up in rabbinic literature (Bavli Sanhedrin 17a).

Key Hebrew term: *mitʾōnĕnîm* (מִתְאֹנְנִים, v. 1, Hithpolel of *ʾānan*, אָנַן) is variously translated "complained" (ESV), "began to murmur" (JPS), "were like complainers" (Milgrom). The verb's intensity comes from the stem — it indicates self-involved, performative grumbling. The "fire of YHWH" (*ʾēš YHWH*, אֵשׁ יְהוָה) in v. 1 is the same phrase used for Nadab and Abihu's destructive encounter in Lev 10:2; the parallel implies that the outskirts-of-camp burning is a measured judgment, not total consumption.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish readings** of Numbers 11 concentrate on three loci: the complaint, the seventy elders, and Eldad-Medad.

In the Second Temple period, Philo (*On the Change of Names* 23) reads the manna allegorically as divine wisdom, the "bread of heaven," and treats the quail-craving as the soul's rebellion against wisdom in favor of lower pleasures. Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.296–299) narrates the quail plague with vivid detail, blaming the multitude's "insolence" and emphasizing Moses' intercessory role.

Rabbinic tradition on the seventy elders is extensive. Bavli Sanhedrin 17a derives from Num 11:16–25 the constitutional basis for the Sanhedrin of seventy-one (the seventy elders plus Moses, the prototype for the supreme court of seventy-one rabbis). Maimonides (*Mishneh Torah, Hilkhot Sanhedrin* 1:3) codifies this: the Great Sanhedrin of seventy-one in the Temple is the institutional descendant of the Num 11 seventy. This is a major piece of Jewish political theology — the authority of the rabbinic court traces its legitimacy directly to the Spirit-gifted elders of the wilderness.

Rabbinic readings on Eldad and Medad are plural. Bavli Sanhedrin 17a debates why they remained in the camp: Rabbi Shimon held they humbled themselves, feeling unworthy to be numbered among the seventy; Rav Nachman said they were chosen by lot but the lot did not fall to them, and so the Spirit came on them in camp as compensation. Sifre Numbers 95 emphasizes that their prophecy continued (unlike the seventy, who prophesied "only once") — making them, in rabbinic reading, greater prophets. Rashi at v. 26 follows the Bavli's humility interpretation; Ramban notes that the text itself does not specify their prophecy's content, and urges restraint in speculation.

On Moses' death-wish in vv. 11–15, the Bavli Berakhot 32a treats Moses as a model of *raising one's voice* to heaven — the foundational text for Jewish prayer as protest. Nehama Leibowitz (*Studies in Bamidbar*, 1980) devotes an essay to the psychological truth of these verses: a leader at the breaking point is permitted, even required, to speak plainly to God. Jonathan Sacks (*Covenant and Conversation: Numbers*, 2017) reads the episode as a management-theoretic turning point: Moses learns he cannot lead alone, and the seventy elders are the birth of distributed leadership in Israel.

Avivah Zornberg (*Bewilderments*, 2015) gives one of the most penetrating modern Jewish readings of Num 11. She reads the quail as an image of what the people actually want — not meat, but the return to Egyptian oral satisfaction, the pre-weaning regression. God gives them what they crave, and it kills them. The wilderness is the place where Israel must be weaned, and chapter 11 is the failure of weaning. The Graves of Craving become the cemetery of a people who could not let Egypt die inside them.

**Christian readings** work the same material through different frames. Origen's *Homilies on Numbers* 16 treats Num 11 at length. The mixed multitude (*ʾăsapsuf*) becomes, for Origen, the type of the unconverted heart still present within the baptized community — those who walk with the church but crave the leeks and onions of the world. The seventy elders are the seventy disciples of Luke 10, on whom Christ breathed the Spirit; the quail plague prefigures the judgment on those who receive the gifts of God without gratitude. Origen's reading is explicitly typological and explicitly moral: every Christian soul has a *ʾăsapsuf* within.

Augustine in *De Civitate Dei* 16.43 and in his *Enarrations on the Psalms* (on Ps 78, which retells Num 11) reads the quail as the image of God giving sinners what they demand, in judgment. Augustine's phrase, echoed by Pascal, is that the worst punishment God can give is to answer an ill-framed prayer. This reading enters the Reformation: Calvin on Num 11 (*Harmony of the Last Four Books of Moses*) cites Augustine directly and extends the lesson — to desire rightly is the hardest Christian discipline, and the people's craving is the very opposite of faith.

Aquinas in *Summa Theologiae* I-II q. 68–70 discusses the gifts of the Holy Spirit, drawing from Num 11 and Isa 11 as the two Old Testament roots of the Spirit's descent on human beings. The seventy elders receiving a portion of the Spirit that was on Moses becomes, for Aquinas, the basis for sacramental theology of episcopal ordination — the Spirit is communicated through the laying-on of hands in hierarchical succession. Luther rejects this hierarchical reading sharply: in his 1525 Pentateuch lectures, he treats the Spirit's distribution as an anti-hierarchical sign, with Moses' "would that all were prophets" as a direct anticipation of the priesthood of all believers.

The critical tradition from Wellhausen through Noth, Gray, Budd, Levine, Dozeman, and Cole generally treats Num 11 as a composite text whose final form integrates two originally independent traditions — the seventy-elders-with-Spirit tradition (which underwrites the Sanhedrin) and the quail-plague tradition (a cautionary etiology). Wenham (*Numbers*, Tyndale, 1981) and Cole (*Numbers*, NAC, 2000) accept the composite theory but emphasize the chapter's theological unity: the twin judgments (fire and plague) frame the one act of grace (Spirit on the seventy). Mary Douglas (*In the Wilderness*, 1993) reads the chapter structurally: the camp's "edges" are where the complaint originates and where the fire burns, while the Spirit descends at the center (the tent of meeting). The geography is theology.

Walter Brueggemann (*The Prophetic Imagination*, 1978, and *Theology of the Old Testament*, 1997) foundationally reads Num 11 — especially Moses' "would that all were prophets" — as the root text of Israelite democratic prophetism, the claim that the Spirit is not proprietary. This is the chapter that makes Joel 2:28 possible: "your sons and daughters shall prophesy." John Goldingay (*Old Testament Theology*, vol. 1, 2003) treats Num 11 as an episode of divine self-limitation: God accepts Moses' complaint, distributes the burden, and does not punish Moses' death-wish. Chris Wright (*The Mission of God*, 2006) reads the mixed multitude as an early sign of the nations included in covenant — even in judgment, their presence complicates any tribal reading of Israel.

Ephraim Radner (*Leviticus*, Brazos, 2008) and the Brazos series tradition more broadly read the chapter as the church's own self-diagnosis: the church always contains a *ʾăsapsuf*, and its cravings will always return it to Egypt unless the Spirit is poured out beyond the authorized seventy. Terence Fretheim (*Pentateuch*, 1996) emphasizes the anthropological point: the God of Num 11 is a God who can be argued with, who responds to Moses' complaint by redistributing authority, who listens to a leader at the breaking point.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Jewish and Christian traditions converge on reading the seventy elders as a constitutional moment: the Spirit is portable, distributed, and institutional. They diverge on *what institution* is founded. For Jewish tradition, the institution is the Sanhedrin and, by extension, rabbinic authority. For Christian tradition, the institution is the church in its Spirit-filled totality (the Pentecost reading) or the ordained episcopate in apostolic succession (the Aquinas reading). The Eldad-Medad pericope is the hinge: Jewish tradition reads them as humble elders who received the Spirit as compensation; Pentecostal and charismatic Christian tradition reads Moses' "would that all were prophets" as the direct promissory note that Acts 2 cashes. On the quail plague, both traditions converge on the warning: the danger is not the craving itself but the heart behind it.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What is the relationship between complaint and faith?** Numbers 11 is the Bible's densest single chapter on this question, because it contains three distinct complaint-speakers — the people, Moses, and by extension the prophetic tradition Moses inaugurates. Three positions:

The *complaint-as-sin* position (Calvin, most of the Reformed tradition, Augustine in places) reads the people's complaint as spiritual sickness. To grumble is to reject God's providence; to crave what God has not given is to distrust the goodness of what God has. Moses' complaint is different in kind (he addresses God directly; the people address each other) but still fragile, and God's response to Moses — "is the LORD's hand shortened?" — is a mild rebuke.

The *complaint-as-prayer* position (Brueggemann, Claus Westermann, Zornberg, Sacks) reads Moses' complaint specifically as legitimate prayer. The Psalter is full of complaint; the laments of Jeremiah and Job are holy protest; the cry of Moses here is the prototype. On this reading, the people's complaint fails not because it is complaint but because it is addressed to no one — it is grumbling, not prayer. Moses models the alternative.

The *steelman for the least intuitive view*: the *complaint-as-formation* position (Origen, Aquinas, Radner). The function of the wilderness is to *produce* complaint — to draw out what is latent in the human heart so that it can be addressed. God does not prevent Israel from complaining because the complaint itself is diagnostic. The *ʾăsapsuf* within each person must speak in order for it to be healed (or, in some cases, killed). The steelman force: neither the complaint-as-sin view nor the complaint-as-prayer view reckons seriously with the fact that the wilderness is God's chosen curriculum, and that Israel's cravings are not a mistake in the program but the very thing the program is designed to surface.

**Where this leaves us:** the three views can be held in tension if we distinguish the *what* (complaint is unavoidable human expression) from the *how* (addressed to God and willing to be answered, or grumbled among oneselves and resistant to answer) and the *to what end* (killing the craving so the covenant can live, or nursing the craving until the craving kills the crave-r). Numbers 11 is less a condemnation of complaint than a clinic on it.

**Tension 2: What does the distribution of the Spirit mean for authority?** The chapter makes two moves that stand in tension. Moses receives the Spirit, some is taken from him and given to seventy — suggesting a limited, zero-sum transfer. But then Eldad and Medad receive the Spirit without having gone to the tent at all, and Moses exclaims "would that all were prophets" — suggesting an unlimited, non-zero-sum outpouring. Three positions:

The *limited-transfer* position (Aquinas on hierarchical orders, much patristic/medieval tradition): the Spirit is genuinely communicated through authorized institutional channels. The seventy are ordained; the Spirit is their grace of office. Eldad and Medad are an exception proving the rule — a special divine intervention, not a pattern. Authority flows through ordained channels.

The *unlimited-outpouring* position (Luther, radical Reformation, Pentecostal traditions): Moses' "would that all were prophets" is the theological heart of the chapter, and Eldad-Medad are the paradigm, not the exception. The Spirit blows where it wills; institutional authority is always provisional; every believer is a potential prophet.

The *steelman for the least intuitive view*: the *both-and* position (Milgrom's "distributed charisma," Sacks on "the prophetic moment as the breaking-open of leadership," Radner on the church's integrated gifts). On this view, the text deliberately holds both: the Spirit is genuinely communicated through ordained authority (the seventy at the tent) *and* genuinely irrupts beyond ordained authority (Eldad and Medad in camp). The two are not competitors but complements. The steelman force: neither the high-ordination reading nor the radical-spiritualist reading can account for the full chapter; only a both-and structure can. Institutions without irruption become stagnant; irruption without institutions becomes chaos.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter resists the "either ordained or charismatic" dichotomy it is often pulled into. The seventy *and* Eldad-Medad are both genuine. Moses' response to Joshua — "are you jealous for my sake?" — suggests that the deepest threat to faithful leadership is not disorder but envy.

**Unresolved question:** If God gave Israel quail as *punishment* — answering their prayer in wrath — what does this imply about the rest of our prayers? How do we know which of our cravings God will mercifully refuse, and which God will devastatingly grant?

## The Thread

Four non-obvious cross-references:

(1) Joel 2:28–29 ("I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh; your sons and your daughters shall prophesy... even on the male and female servants in those days I will pour out my Spirit") is the direct prophetic amplification of Num 11:29. Joel cashes the check Moses wrote. Acts 2:17, on Pentecost, reads Joel and Moses together — the Spirit on Eldad and Medad becomes the Spirit on all flesh.

(2) Ps 78:18–31 retells the quail plague in poetic form, treating it as the paradigmatic episode of Israel "testing God in their heart by demanding the food they craved." The psalm's verdict — "he gave them what they craved, but while the food was still in their mouths, the wrath of God rose against them" — becomes the Bible's own interpretive gloss on Num 11.

(3) 1 Kgs 19:4 places Elijah under the broom tree praying "it is enough; now, O LORD, take away my life" — a direct literary echo of Moses' "kill me at once" in Num 11:15. The Elijah narrative consciously types itself on Moses' breaking point, down to the forty days and nights at Horeb (1 Kgs 19:8, the same mountain).

(4) 1 Cor 10:6 cites the wilderness cravings directly: "these things took place as examples for us, that we might not desire evil as they did." Paul names the *ʾăsapsuf*-type sin of Num 11 as a standing warning for the Corinthian church, with the quail graves as a literal burial-ground for misdirected desire. The theological category Paul uses — *epithymia kakōn* ("craving for evil") — is a direct Greek translation of *taʾăwâ*, the root of Kibroth-hattaavah.
