# Ezekiel 6: Mountains, High Places, and the Scatology of Idolatry

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 6 belongs to the same pre-587 BC oracle cycle as chapter 5, delivered in Babylonian exile (Tel-abib by the Chebar canal, in the Nippur countryside) probably between 593 and 588 BC. The audience sitting before Ezekiel is the deported elite — scribes, priests, royal household, artisans — already settled in the *al-Yahudu* and Murashu-region communities documented by the **Al-Yahudu tablets** (Pearce & Wunsch, *Documents of Judean Exiles and West Semitics in Babylonia*, 2014). The oracle's referent, however, is back home: the highlands of Judah and Israel, the *bamot* (high places) on the hilltops where Iron II Israelite religion actually happened.

The political moment is fragile. Zedekiah is Nebuchadnezzar's installed king, but pro-Egyptian factions in Jerusalem are pushing rebellion (cf. Jer 27–28; Lachish Letters 3, 6). Ezekiel preaches against the Babylonian-exile audience's hope that the Judean homeland is intact and the temple sacrosanct. Chapter 6 closes that escape route: not only the city but the whole *land* — every ravine, every hill, every wilderness from Dan to "Riblah" (6:14, almost certainly to be vocalized as *Diblah* per Greenberg and Block, a southern desert toponym pairing with the northern reach of the inheritance) — will be swept.

The material-culture frame is the *bamah* installation. Archaeology has now given us a precise picture of pre-exilic Israelite cultic architecture. The Tel Dan high place (Iron Age IIA/B), the Arad sanctuary's *holy of holies* with its standing-stone *massebot* and incense altars, the Ḥorvat Qitmit Edomite shrine, and the **Khirbet el-Qom** and **Kuntillet ʿAjrud** inscriptions ("YHWH and his Asherah" — *brkt 'tkm lyhwh šmrn wl'šrth*) collectively document that pre-exilic Israelite religion was overwhelmingly *bamah*-religion, frequently syncretistic, often Yahwistic-but-pluralistic (YHWH alongside Asherah, El-amalgams, sometimes Baal). **Mark Smith** (*The Origins of Biblical Monotheism*, 2001; *The Early History of God*, rev. 2002), **William Dever** (*Did God Have a Wife?*, 2005), **Patrick Miller** (*The Religion of Ancient Israel*, 2000), and **Susan Ackerman** (*Under Every Green Tree*, 1992 — the title itself drawn from Ezekiel 6:13) have established this as scholarly consensus: the prophets were not describing a marginal aberration from a pre-existing strict monotheism; they were polemicizing for a not-yet-normative monotheism against the *normative* religious practice of Iron II Israel and Judah.

The *gilulim* term (גִּלּוּלִים, 6:4–5, 13) — Ezekiel's signature contemptuous label for idols, used 39× in the book — is etymologically debated. **Block** (NICOT) follows the long-standing reading that links *gilulim* to *gel/gallu* (Akkadian *gallu*, or the Hebrew root *gll* "round / lump") with strong scatological connotation: "dung pellets," "round droppings." The deliberate scatological insult is consistent with the chapter's overall rhetoric. **Greenberg** (Anchor) is more cautious about the etymology but agrees on the contemptuous register. *Bamot* (בָּמוֹת, "high places," 6:3, 6, 13) and *to'ebot* (תּוֹעֲבוֹת, "abominations," 6:9, 11) round out the chapter's polemical lexicon. *Ḥamanim* (חַמָּנִים, "incense altars" or "sun pillars," 6:4, 6) is plausibly a kind of small standing-altar attested archaeologically in Phoenician and Aramean contexts.

Authorship: same priestly hand and same exilic dating as chapter 5; Cooke (ICC), Eichrodt (OTL), Zimmerli (Hermeneia), Greenberg (Anchor), Block (NICOT), and Odell all date the core of chapters 1–24 to Ezekiel's own ministry 593–571 BC. Torrey's pseudonymous theory has no purchase on this material.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 6 is a *gerichtswort* (judgment word) split into two oracles separated at v. 11. The first (vv. 1–10) addresses the mountains directly — a **prophetic apostrophe**, common in Hebrew prophecy but elevated here into a structural device: the land itself is the addressee, summoned as both witness and victim. The second (vv. 11–14) introduces a *clap-and-stamp* sign-act: the prophet performs a derisive gesture to externalize divine fury, then announces the sword-famine-pestilence triad.

The leitwort architecture is dense. *Bamot* (high places) appears 4× across vv. 3, 6 (twice in v. 6); *gilulim* (idols) 5×; *to'ebot* (abominations) 2×; the recognition formula *yada' ki ani YHWH* ("they shall know that I am the LORD") closes both oracles (vv. 7, 10, 13, 14) — Ezekiel's signature *Erkenntnisformel*, identified by Zimmerli as the book's central structuring claim. The chapter uses it four times in fourteen verses. Greenberg counts the recognition formula 72× across the book; chapter 6 has the highest density per verse outside chs. 38–39.

The phrase *under every green tree and under every leafy oak* (6:13) — *taḥat kol 'eṣ ra'anan we-taḥat kol 'elah 'avuttah* — is a stock prophetic phrase (cf. Deut 12:2; 1 Kgs 14:23; 2 Kgs 17:10; Jer 2:20; 3:6, 13) for Canaanite-style hilltop-and-grove cult. Ezekiel deploys it as inherited polemical idiom, signaling continuity with Deuteronomistic theology. Susan Ackerman titled her 1992 monograph on sixth-century popular religion after this verse.

Genre: oracle against the land, with embedded sign-act (clap-stamp) — a sub-form of the *symbolische Handlung* category. The structural unity of chapters 1–24 as judgment-cycle, 25–32 as oracles-against-nations, and 33–48 as restoration is visible in microcosm: chapter 6's land-judgment will eventually be answered by chapter 36's mountain-restoration, both addressing *the mountains of Israel* directly. The pairing is deliberate.

Text-critical notes: 6:4 has minor MT-LXX variants on the verb for cutting down the *ḥamanim*; LXX collapses the term toward "wooden images." 6:14 reads MT *me-ha-midbar diblatah* ("from the wilderness to Diblah/Riblah") — the place name is unattested as a toponym in the south, leading Greenberg, Block, and Zimmerli to treat *Diblah* as a corruption of *Riblah* (the northern Syrian site where Nebuchadnezzar held Zedekiah's tribunal in 2 Kgs 25:6) or, more likely, an unidentified southern site naming a deliberate north-to-south totality. The 4QEzek-a Qumran fragments do not preserve chapter 6 directly but confirm MT readings around it.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish trajectory.** *Targum Jonathan* paraphrases the chapter with characteristic moralizing, glossing *gilulim* directly as "filthy idols" — preserving the etymological insult. **Rashi** focuses on the recognition formula: the knowing-the-LORD that comes through judgment is itself the chapter's central act of mercy, because to know YHWH at all is the precondition of repentance. **Radak** treats the *bamot* references with attention to the Deuteronomic centralization theology — the chapter's denunciation presupposes Deuteronomy 12. **Ibn Ezra** notes the Diblah/Riblah crux at v. 14 and reads it as a southern toponym. **Maimonides** in *Guide* 3.29–30 treats idolatry historically: Sabian astral-worship inheritances explain the "high place" cult and the *ḥamanim* (sun pillars). For Maimonides, prophetic anti-idolatry rhetoric is a pedagogical instrument against an actually-prevalent error, not a hyperbolic flourish — a position that aligns surprisingly well with twentieth-century archaeology.

In modern Jewish scholarship: **Greenberg**'s Anchor commentary stresses the chapter's *covenantal-pedagogical* shape — punishment as the means by which the people come to *know* YHWH. **Jon Levenson** reads the mountain-rhetoric against Zion theology: the land's elevation was supposed to be sacred geography (Zion as the cosmic mountain); chapter 6 desacralizes the alternative high places to relocate sacrality at the temple mount. **Sommer** (*The Bodies of God*) is collateral to chapter 6 but the *kavod* / divine-fluidity theology in his work clarifies why YHWH can be present at the temple and absent from the *bamot* without spatial contradiction.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic: **Origen**'s *Homilies on Ezekiel* reads chapter 6 allegorically — the high places as the elevations of pride, the *gilulim* as the idols of the heart, the *under every green tree* as the false consolations of pagan philosophy. **Jerome**'s *Commentary on Ezekiel* (414) splits historical and typological registers: the chapter applies literally to 587 BC and typologically to the church's purification through tribulation. Jerome's anti-pagan polemic, reading the chapter into the late-fourth-century Roman context, is striking — Symmachus's altar-restoration controversy was within recent memory. **Gregory the Great**'s *Homilies on Ezekiel* (593, the most influential medieval treatment) reads the chapter pastorally: the preacher's task is to identify the *bamot* of the heart and demolish them. Gregory's tropological reading dominated medieval reception.

Medieval: **Hugh of St. Victor** and **Aquinas** both use the chapter in treatments of idolatry as a vice (*ST* II-II.94 takes idolatry as the most serious sin against religion; the *bamot* are illustrative). Reformation: **Calvin**'s *Lectures on Ezekiel* (1565, incomplete — chs. 1–20 covered, so chapter 6 is included and substantial) is severe. Calvin reads the chapter as the foundation of his radical iconoclasm: *Institutes* 1.11 ("It is unlawful to attribute to God a visible form, and… all who set up idols revolt against the true God") is grounded in Ezekiel-language. The Genevan and Reformed image-stripping of the 1540s–60s descends from this exegesis. Calvin's argument: any visible representation of God *is itself* a *gilul*, a "dung-pellet," because finite matter cannot bear divine glory. The polemic logic is borrowed directly from Ezekiel.

Catholic and Orthodox response: the **Second Council of Nicaea (787)** distinguished *latreia* (worship, due to God alone) from *proskynesis / dulia* (veneration, given to icons of Christ and saints) — a distinction the iconoclasts rejected and Reformed exegesis rejected again in the sixteenth century. The **Council of Trent** Session 25 (1563) reiterated Nicaea II's image-theology against the Reformers. The contemporary Catholic Catechism (CCC 2129–2132) maintains the distinction: idolatry is forbidden; veneration of icons that *passes through* to the prototype is not. Anabaptist iconoclasm went further than Calvin — Karlstadt at Wittenberg in 1522, the Münster radicals in 1534 — reading Ezekiel 6's demolition as a present-tense ecclesial mandate.

Modern: **Tillich**'s *idolatry-as-ultimate-concern-with-the-non-ultimate* (Systematic Theology I, 1951) and **Brueggemann**'s prophetic-imagination readings re-encode the chapter's polemic for ideology critique. The contemporary literature on "the idols of the market" (Walter Wink, *Engaging the Powers*) extends Ezekiel 6 into political theology — *gilulim* as ideologies of nation, capital, technology.

Critical scholarship: Cooke (ICC), Zimmerli (Hermeneia), Greenberg (Anchor), Block (NICOT), Odell, Lapsley. **Mark Smith** and **Susan Ackerman** are essential for the archaeological-religious context — the chapter cannot be read responsibly without their work.

**Where Jewish and Christian readings diverge.** Christian readings — patristic through Gregory through Calvin — take the chapter's anti-idol logic and universalize it ecclesially: every Christian heart is a potential *bamah*. Jewish readings tend to keep the chapter located: this is about the historical *bamot*, the Iron II syncretism, and the path back through repentance to the temple. Calvin and the Reformed tradition stand out as the most aggressive in collapsing the historical referent into a present-tense iconoclastic mandate.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — Idolatry as scatology.** Ezekiel's *gilulim* is the most aggressive prophetic anti-idol language in the Hebrew Bible. He uses the word 39 times, more than the rest of the OT combined. If Block's etymology is right (and even Greenberg's more cautious gloss preserves the contempt), Ezekiel is calling Israel's gods *turds*. This is not refined theology. It is a calculated rhetorical violence.

The Christian iconoclast tradition takes this rhetoric and runs. Calvin's *Institutes* 1.11.4: "I am not so superstitious as to think that all visible representations of every kind are unlawful. But as sculpture and painting are gifts of God, what I insist for is, that both shall be used purely and lawfully." But within the church the use is constrained: "everything respecting God which is learned from images is futile, not to say false." Geneva's image-stripping in the 1540s applied this. The Münster radicals went further. Knox in Scotland after 1559. The Puritan iconoclasm of the 1640s English Civil War is genealogically downstream of Ezekiel 6.

The Catholic and Orthodox response (Nicaea II 787; Trent Session 25; the contemporary CCC) distinguishes the *idol* from the *image*: an idol is worshipped in itself; an image is a window through which veneration passes to its prototype. This distinction depends on the *gilulim* of Ezekiel 6 being something genuinely different from icons of Christ — different in ontological status (false god vs. true incarnation) and different in liturgical function (substitution vs. transparent reference).

Steelman the least intuitive reading: the Catholic-Orthodox case is stronger than Reformed polemic typically allows. The argument runs: if God himself takes a body in the incarnation, then matter is no longer in principle excluded from carrying divine reference. The image of Christ on the icon is theologically possible because the body of Christ in the womb of the Virgin was theologically actual. The *gilulim* of Ezekiel 6 are forbidden because they are *false*, not because they are *material*. Modern Christian-Jewish dialogue (Marc Boulanger, David Novak) has grown more cautious about flat anti-image polemic.

Modern *idolatry-as-ideology* readings (Tillich, Brueggemann, Wink) extend the chapter's logic into critique of nationalism, racism, capitalism, technocracy. The contemporary force of the chapter is rarely directed at literal statues; it is directed at the *gilulim* of late-modern life — ideologies that demand worship and offer salvation. *Where this leaves us:* Ezekiel's polemic has a sharper edge than any sub-tradition has fully absorbed. Calvin's reading is the most rhetorically faithful; the Catholic-Orthodox image theology is the most ontologically careful; the modern ideology readings extend the polemic to its most consequential present-day target.

**Tension 2 — The high places' archaeological reality.** The Khirbet el-Qom and Kuntillet ʿAjrud inscriptions, the Tel Dan and Arad sanctuaries, the *bamot* at Megiddo and Tel Reḥov — pre-exilic Israelite religion was the religion the prophets opposed. **Mark Smith**'s *Origins of Biblical Monotheism* and *Early History of God* document this: a Yahwistic-pluralistic state cult, often with Asherah, syncretic with surrounding Canaanite-Phoenician practices, only gradually narrowed into the strict monotheism that Ezekiel and Deuteronomistic theology demand. Susan Ackerman's *Under Every Green Tree* maps the popular religion of late-monarchic Judah and finds it almost everywhere as *bamah*-religion.

This creates a hermeneutical problem. If pre-exilic religion was syncretistic *as the norm*, then Ezekiel 6 is not denouncing a deviation from a previously-pure religion. Ezekiel 6 is *fighting for* a normative monotheism that did not yet exist as norm. The prophetic critique is not retrospective enforcement of an established standard; it is forward construction of the standard itself.

Catholic and Orthodox traditions read this with a robust doctrine of *prophetic charism* — the prophet sees rightly because the Spirit speaks, regardless of whether the surrounding culture had reached the same understanding. The Reformed tradition similarly grounds prophetic authority in the divine word over against majority practice. Critical scholarship (Smith, Dever, Miller, Ackerman) is more agnostic about the metaphysics but rigorous about the sociology: monotheism *won*, and the prophets like Ezekiel were the rhetorical engine of that victory. Steelman the least intuitive: the *prophet-against-the-people* model in Ezekiel 6 is not a sociological accident. It is the structural shape of revelation — divine word disrupting the equilibrium of practice. The chapter's force depends on the gap between what most Israelites *did* and what Ezekiel says they *should have known*. *Where this leaves us:* the chapter is more radical than a flat reading suggests. Ezekiel is not preserving a tradition; he is *constituting* one, against the present practice and toward a future the people cannot yet imagine.

The unresolved question that lands hard: if the prophetic polemic constitutes the norm rather than reflecting an existing one, what happens when modern Christians and Jews invoke "biblical religion" against contemporary culture? Are we standing where the prophets stood — speaking forward into a future norm — or are we standing where the *bamah*-builders stood, naming our syncretism as ancient and assuming it is faithful?

## The Thread

- **Deut 12:2** — "you shall surely destroy all the places where the nations whom you shall dispossess served their gods, on the high mountains and on the hills and under every green tree." Ezekiel 6's vocabulary is borrowed verbatim; the chapter is a Deuteronomistic theology applied to Israel's own land.
- **2 Kgs 23** — Josiah's reform, the demolition of the *bamot*, the burning of the Asherah, the desecration of the priests' bones on the altars. Ezekiel 6:5 (bones scattered around altars) is a haunting echo of Josiah's program reversed — the people's bones now where the priests' bones were laid to defile pagan altars.
- **Hos 4:13** — "they sacrifice on the tops of the mountains and burn offerings on the hills, under oak, poplar, and terebinth, because their shade is good." A century earlier, the same critique against the same religion. Hosea names what Ezekiel will demolish.
- **Acts 17:29** — Paul on the Areopagus: "we ought not to think that the divine being is like gold or silver or stone, an image formed by the art and imagination of man." The Pauline *gilulim* polemic transposed into Greek philosophical idiom — Ezekiel's contempt translated for Athens.
