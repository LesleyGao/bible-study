# Psalm 124 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 124 is the fifth in the **Songs of Ascents** (*Shir HaMa'alot*, שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת) — the fifteen-psalm pilgrim collection (Pss 120–134) within Book V (Pss 107–150). The superscription is *shir ha-ma'alot le-David* (שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת לְדָוִד, "a song of ascents. Of David") — one of four Songs of Ascents bearing a Davidic attribution (Pss 122, 124, 131, 133). As with Ps 122, the Davidic attribution is contested: most modern critical scholars (Gunkel, Mowinckel, Kraus, Hossfeld–Zenger) read it as a post-exilic editorial assignment, while the conservative tradition (Delitzsch, with caveats Wenham) defends at least a Davidic milieu for the underlying material. The psalm's content — communal thanksgiving for deliverance from existential threat — fits multiple historical settings (Davidic-era military rescue, Hezekiah's deliverance from Sennacherib in 701 BCE, return from Babylonian exile). Several Linguistic features of the Hebrew (the relative particle *she-* in v. 1's *lulei Yahweh she-hayah lanu* rather than the older *asher*; the particle *azai* in vv. 3–5; the late vocabulary) point modern critics toward a post-exilic dating. The collection's editorial setting is post-exilic regardless of the individual psalm's earlier life.

Gunkel's form-critical genre is **community thanksgiving song** (*Danklied des Volkes*) — the corporate equivalent of the individual thanksgiving (*todah*). The psalm has the classic *todah* structure: a call to give thanks (here a call for Israel to confess God's deliverance, vv. 1–2), an account of distress (vv. 3–5), a praise of the deliverer (v. 6), a vivid metaphor of escape (v. 7), and a closing creator-confession (v. 8). Sigmund Mowinckel placed *todah* psalms in the actual cult of the Jerusalem Temple — the worshipers brought a thank-offering, sang the testimony, and the community heard it as faith-formation. Hans-Joachim Kraus (BKAT) develops the same setting; Hossfeld–Zenger and Goldingay agree on the genre though they debate the dating.

The opening Hebrew is one of the most striking constructions in the Psalter: *lulei Yahweh she-hayah lanu yomar-na Yisrael lulei Yahweh she-hayah lanu be-qum aleinu adam* (לוּלֵי יְהוָה שֶׁהָיָה לָנוּ יֹאמַר־נָא יִשְׂרָאֵל לוּלֵי יְהוָה שֶׁהָיָה לָנוּ בְּקוּם עָלֵינוּ אָדָם, "If it had not been the LORD who was on our side — let Israel now say — if it had not been the LORD who was on our side when people rose up against us...", vv. 1–2). The Hebrew particle *lulei* (לוּלֵי) is a **counterfactual conditional** — "had it not been," "if not," "but for." The construction is rare and emphatic in biblical Hebrew. The doubled *lulei*, with the embedded summons "let Israel now say" (*yomar-na Yisrael*), creates an antiphonal liturgical structure: the cantor begins, calls the congregation to repeat, and the congregation completes the line. The psalm is **liturgically scripted** — it is meant to be sung communally with response.

The counterfactual structure of vv. 1–5 unfolds in cascading metaphors. **First metaphor**: enemies as predatory beasts. *Azai chayyim bele'unu* (אֲזַי חַיִּים בְּלָעוּנוּ, "then they would have swallowed us up alive," v. 3). The verb *bala* (בָּלַע, swallow) evokes Numbers 16:32 (the earth swallowing Korah and his followers) and Jonah 1:17 (the great fish swallowing the prophet) — the swallow-alive image is the disappearance of the swallowed, a death that leaves no body to bury. **Second metaphor**: enemies as floodwaters. *Azai ha-mayim shetafunu nachlah avar al-naphshenu azai avar al-naphshenu ha-mayim ha-zedonim* (אֲזַי הַמַּיִם שְׁטָפוּנוּ נַחְלָה עָבַר עַל־נַפְשֵׁנוּ אֲזַי עָבַר עַל־נַפְשֵׁנוּ הַמַּיִם הַזֵּידוֹנִים, "then the waters would have swept us away, the torrent would have gone over our soul, then over our soul would have gone the raging waters," vv. 4–5). The flood imagery is rich in ANE resonance: the Babylonian *Atrahasis* and *Gilgamesh* flood traditions, the Genesis 6–9 Noah narrative, the Hebrew prophetic flood-as-judgment metaphors (Isa 8:7–8 — Assyria as the river that overflows). The compound *ha-mayim ha-zedonim* (the proud / raging / arrogant waters) personifies the floodwaters with the same word — *zedon*, root *zud* — used of human pride in v. 5. The waters are *insolent* — they presume to overwhelm the people of God.

The thanksgiving turns at v. 6: *barukh Yahweh she-lo netananu teref le-shineihem* (בָּרוּךְ יְהוָה שֶׁלֹּא נְתָנָנוּ טֶרֶף לְשִׁנֵּיהֶם, "blessed be the LORD, who has not given us as prey to their teeth"). The image returns to predatory beasts — *teref* (prey, torn flesh) and *shen* (tooth) name the lion's mouth. **Third metaphor**: the people as a bird in a fowler's snare. *Naphshenu ke-tsippor nimletah mi-pach yoqshim ha-pach nishbar va-anachnu nimlatnu* (נַפְשֵׁנוּ כְּצִפּוֹר נִמְלְטָה מִפַּח יוֹקְשִׁים הַפַּח נִשְׁבָּר וַאֲנַחְנוּ נִמְלָטְנוּ, "our soul has escaped like a bird from the snare of the fowlers; the snare is broken, and we have escaped," v. 7). The *pach* (פַּח) is the spring-loaded trap of the bird-catcher (*yaqush*); the metaphor recurs in Pss 91:3 and 141:9. The doubled *nimlatnu* (we have escaped) is the psalm's emotional pivot — the people remember the moment of release.

The closing v. 8 is the **creator-confession formula** that recurs across the Songs of Ascents: *ezreinu be-shem Yahweh oseh shamayim va-aretz* (עֶזְרֵנוּ בְּשֵׁם יְהוָה עֹשֵׂה שָׁמַיִם וָאָרֶץ, "our help is in the name of the LORD, the maker of heaven and earth"). Compare Ps 121:2 (*ezri me-im Yahweh oseh shamayim va-aretz*) and Ps 134:3 (*ye-varekhekha Yahweh mi-Tziyon oseh shamayim va-aretz*). The formula is the collection's theological anchor: the deliverer is the creator, and the maker of heaven and earth is not threatened by floods or swallowed by beasts.

The Septuagint numbers Ps 124 as Ps 123 (LXX one behind MT), and the **Dead Sea Scrolls** (11Q5, the Great Psalms Scroll) preserve the psalm with text substantially aligned to the MT.

## Lens 2: The Literature Itself

The psalm divides into **three strophes**: the counterfactual confession (vv. 1–5), the blessing-and-escape (vv. 6–7), and the creator-confession (v. 8). The first strophe is dominated by the doubled *lulei*; the second turns to *barukh* (blessed); the third closes with *ezreinu* (our help).

Robert Lowth's parallelism is virtuosic here. **Synonymous parallelism** with intensification structures vv. 1–2: the doubled *lulei* statement repeats with an intensifying clause ("when people rose up against us"). **Synthetic parallelism** carries vv. 3–5 forward through the cascading metaphors of swallow / flood / raging waters — each line builds on and intensifies the previous. **Climactic / staircase parallelism** (the technique of repeating words from one line into the next while extending the thought) is most visible in vv. 4–5: "then the waters would have swept us / nachlah avar al-naphshenu / azai avar al-naphshenu ha-mayim ha-zedonim." The phrase *avar al-naphshenu* repeats verbatim across the line break, with the second occurrence picked up by the closing *ha-mayim ha-zedonim*. This is the staircase technique that gives the Songs of Ascents their characteristic stepwise rhythm.

The psalm exhibits a **chiastic-thematic structure**:
- A. Counterfactual: had it not been the LORD (vv. 1–2)
-     B. Beasts swallowing (v. 3)
-         C. Floodwaters overwhelming (vv. 4–5)
-     B'. Beasts' teeth, prey rescued (v. 6)
- D. Bird escaping fowler's snare (v. 7)
- A'. The LORD as help, maker of all (v. 8)

The metaphor-pairs (beasts → flood → beasts → bird) intermesh with the conceptual frame (counterfactual at start, creator-confession at end). The bird-and-snare image at v. 7 is a fresh metaphor — the previous predator-beasts and floods named what *would* have happened; the bird-and-snare names what *did* happen and *did not* hold.

The repetition of *naphshenu* (our soul, our life) — three times across vv. 4, 5, 7 — concentrates the threat and the rescue at the same locus. The *nephesh* is the throat (the body part through which one breathes and swallows), the appetite, the life-force, the very self. The waters went over the *naphshenu*; the *naphshenu* escaped like a bird.

The repetition of *azai* (אֲזַי, "then" — the apodosis of the counterfactual) — three times in vv. 3, 4, 5 — drives the cascade. The doubled *nimlatnu* (we have escaped) at v. 7 is the rhetorical climax: the verb is the only verb in the psalm to be doubled in the indicative-perfect sense, marking the moment of transition from past-threat to present-relief.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story (Jewish and Christian Trajectories)

Within Book V's editorial sequence, Ps 124 follows the upward gaze of Ps 123 with a remembrance of past deliverance. The pilgrim community has arrived at the city (Ps 122), turned eyes upward in lament (Ps 123), and now stops on the staircase to sing the *if-not-for-the-LORD* — the past mercies that establish the basis for present trust. The structure of the Songs of Ascents collection is itself the structure of Christian and Jewish pilgrim spirituality: confession, gaze, gratitude, blessing.

In **Jewish reception**, Ps 124 has been a centerpiece of communal thanksgiving for as long as Jews have been singing the Psalter. Its counterfactual *lulei* opening makes it the natural song for moments when the community escapes destruction. **Rashi** (11th c.) applies the psalm to the various deliverances of Israel — from Pharaoh, from Sennacherib, from Haman, from the medieval pogroms; the psalm functions for him as a portable theology of rescue. **Ibn Ezra** emphasizes the historical specificity of the threat and rescue. **Radak** (David Kimchi) develops the metaphor-cascade as a literary device for naming the totality of threat. **Midrash Tehillim** on Ps 124 elaborates each metaphor — beasts, flood, fowler — as figures for distinct types of historical persecutors. The psalm is recited in many Jewish communities at *Hallel ha-Gadol* and on festival days associated with deliverance. After 1945, the psalm's *lulei* counterfactual took on weight that medieval commentators could not have anticipated; the post-Holocaust Jewish question — *what if?* and *we have escaped* — finds particular resonance here, even as the psalm's confidence about deliverance has had to be held alongside the unanswerable theodicy of those who did not escape.

In **Christian reception**, Ps 124 has functioned as a corporate thanksgiving across every era of the church's life. **Augustine**'s *Enarrationes* on Ps 123 (Latin numbering) reads the swallowing beasts and overwhelming waters as the powers of sin and the flesh from which Christ delivers the soul; the bird-from-snare metaphor becomes a figure of resurrection. **Cassiodorus** systematizes the allegorical reading. **Aquinas** treats the psalm as evidence of God's providential rescue. **Martin Luther** wrote a celebrated metrical paraphrase, **"Wär Gott nicht mit uns diese Zeit"** (1524) — "Were God Not with Us at This Time" — set as a chorale that became one of the most beloved Reformation hymns; J.S. Bach drew on it for cantata BWV 14. **Calvin**'s 1557 commentary on Ps 124 reads the doubled *lulei* as a model for how the church should rehearse its history of rescue — the corporate memory of past mercies as the fuel for present courage. The Genevan Psalter set the psalm to a melody that traveled across the Reformed world. The psalm has been used at thanksgiving days in nearly every European country following deliverance from war, plague, and famine. Modern critical scholarship — **Hermann Gunkel** (community thanksgiving), **Sigmund Mowinckel** (festival cult), **Claus Westermann** (lament-praise dialectic), **Hans-Joachim Kraus** (BKAT), **Frank-Lothar Hossfeld** and **Erich Zenger** (Hermeneia), **John Goldingay** (Baker), **Gordon Wenham** (the Psalter's shape), **Walter Brueggemann** (the testimony of the rescued), **Patrick Miller**, **William Brown**, and **Beth Tanner** — converges on the genre and the post-exilic dating while debating particulars. **Eugene Peterson**'s *A Long Obedience in the Same Direction* devotes a chapter to Ps 124 under the title "Help" (in his sequence; he treats it alongside Ps 121 as the twin help-confessions).

The **New Testament** does not directly cite Ps 124, but its theology of corporate rescue informs Paul's "if God is for us, who can be against us?" (Rom 8:31) and the *we are more than conquerors* passage (Rom 8:37–39); the apostle's recital of his many escapes (2 Cor 11:23–33) is structurally a *lulei* counterfactual; Acts 12's account of Peter's miraculous prison release embodies the bird-from-snare metaphor; and the church's continuous tradition of thanksgiving for deliverance descends directly from Ps 124's pattern.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — How does the *lulei* counterfactual work as theology?** The counterfactual structure of vv. 1–5 is a powerful rhetorical device, but it raises a hard theological question: does the *if-not-for-the-LORD* logic imply that those who *were* swallowed, *were* swept away, *were* devoured by the fowler — for whom the counterfactual did not hold — were somehow *not* on the LORD's side? The post-Holocaust Jewish question and the post-9/11 Christian question both press on this point. **Three positions** stand in the literature. **(1) The historical-particular reading** (Brueggemann, Hossfeld–Zenger): the psalm is the testimony of *one* community in *one* deliverance, and it makes no universal claim about all deliverances; the song is true to its singers without being totalizing. The community that escaped sings; the communities that did not escape are not silenced, they sing different psalms (the laments). The Psalter's polyphony — including Ps 88's unrelieved darkness and the laments of Pss 13, 22, 44, 74, 79 — guarantees that Ps 124's testimony does not erase others' experience. **(2) The eschatological reading** (Augustine, much Christian tradition): the *lulei* points beyond every immediate historical rescue to the ultimate rescue from the powers of sin and death; even those who were physically devoured are eschatologically rescued by Christ; the counterfactual is true at the deepest layer. **(3) The covenantal-corporate reading** (Levenson, Goldingay): the *we* of the psalm is the covenant people across generations, and the survival of the people-as-a-whole through every threat (Egypt, Babylon, Hellenism, Rome, the medieval pogroms, the Shoah) is the corporate rescue the psalm names; individual loss is real and grievous but does not falsify the corporate testimony. *Steelman for each:* The historical-particular reading honors the Psalter's polyphony and refuses to weaponize this psalm against grieving communities. The eschatological reading honors the deepest layer of biblical hope. The covenantal-corporate reading honors the actual structure of Hebrew thought — the *we* is real even as individuals come and go. *Where this leaves us:* The psalm offers a true testimony in the mouth of one community, on the basis of one deliverance, without claiming to be the only psalm. It must be sung alongside Ps 88, not instead of it. When this community escapes, *this* community sings *this* song — and the song stays available for the next community whose escape gives them grounds to use it.

**Tension 2 — What does corporate thanksgiving owe to the present moment?** Ps 124 is past-tense throughout the body of the psalm — the threat is described as past (vv. 1–5 counterfactual; vv. 6–7 past indicatives). The closing v. 8 alone is present: *ezreinu be-shem Yahweh oseh shamayim va-aretz* ("our help is in the name of the LORD, maker of heaven and earth"). The shift from past testimony to present confession is the engine of the psalm's theology: remembered rescue grounds present trust. **Two positions** on the contemporary use. **(1) The memory-as-fuel reading** (Brueggemann's "core testimony"): communities sing past rescues to fuel present hope; the testimony is performative, not merely descriptive. **(2) The doxological-formation reading** (Eugene Peterson, much pastoral tradition): rehearsing past mercies *forms* the soul that must face present and future threats; thanksgiving is a discipline that conditions perception. *Steelman for each:* Both readings agree that past testimony is for present formation; they differ on the mechanism. The reading that takes both seriously: the *lulei* counterfactual is a memory device for the soul that has not yet faced its next threat; v. 8 makes the explicit transfer — the past *was* God; the present *is* God; the future *will be* God; the maker of heaven and earth is the same throughout.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 124 names the corporate practice of remembering rescue as a discipline of present trust. The Songs of Ascents pilgrim has confessed exile (Ps 120), traveled the road (Ps 121), arrived at the gates (Ps 122), turned eyes upward (Ps 123), and now sits down on the staircase to rehearse what God has done. The psalm refuses the false modesty of forgetting and the false triumphalism of universalizing. It says: *here is what happened to us; here is what would have happened if God had not been with us; here is the name on which we still call.* The pilgrim's progress is not just upward but **backward and forward simultaneously** — the next step on the ascent is taken on the strength of the steps already taken in past deliverances.

The closing v. 8 — *our help is in the name of the LORD, maker of heaven and earth* — has been so taken into the bones of Christian liturgy that many readers do not recognize it as a quotation. It opens countless services as a versicle and response. The Songs of Ascents have lent the church's worship some of its most enduring confessional formulas, and v. 8 is among the deepest.

The psalm leaves us with a question shaped by the gap between past testimony and present threat: *what past rescue do you need to rehearse in order to face this week's actual fear?*

What has the LORD already brought you through that you have not recently said *barukh Yahweh* about?
