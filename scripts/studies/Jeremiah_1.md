# Jeremiah 1: The Call That Begins Before Birth

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The opening verses of Jeremiah are unusually specific about chronology, and that specificity matters. The book locates the prophet's first hearing of the divine word "in the thirteenth year" of Josiah son of Amon (1:2), which by standard reckoning is 627 BC, and tells us his ministry continued through the reigns of Jehoiakim (609–598) and "until the end of the eleventh year of Zedekiah" (587/6 BC) — a span of roughly forty years that traverses the catastrophe of late Judean history. The world Jeremiah inherited at his call was politically deceptive. Josiah had begun his reform movement around 622 BC, prompted by the discovery of "the book of the law" in the temple (2 Kgs 22), and Judah was enjoying the last shudder of Assyrian decline; Nineveh would fall in 612 to the Medes and Babylonians. But Egypt under Pharaoh Necho II would kill Josiah at Megiddo in 609 (2 Kgs 23:29; 2 Chr 35:20–24), and from that moment Judah was caught between Egypt and the rising Neo-Babylonian Empire. Nebuchadnezzar II's victory over Necho at Carchemish in 605 BC (confirmed by the Babylonian Chronicle, BM 21946) tipped the balance decisively eastward, and the three deportations followed in 605, 597, and 587/6.

Jeremiah belongs to Anathoth, a priestly village roughly three miles northeast of Jerusalem in the territory of Benjamin, traditionally identified with Abiathar's place of exile under Solomon (1 Kgs 2:26). That priestly genealogy is meaningful: Jeremiah comes from a line associated with the older Shilonite priesthood, displaced by the Zadokites who controlled the Jerusalem temple. Some scholars (notably William Holladay) have suggested this background helps explain Jeremiah's unease with Jerusalem's temple ideology and his readiness, in chapters 7 and 26, to threaten the temple's destruction — he does not stand inside the Zadokite establishment.

The material culture of late Iron II Judah survives Jeremiah's prophecy with unusual density. The Lachish Ostraca, discovered at Tel Lachish in 1935 and 1938, are letters from a Judean garrison commander written on potsherds during the final Babylonian siege; ostracon IV reports, "we are watching for the signal-fires of Lachish… for we cannot see Azekah," a statement that maps almost word-for-word onto Jer 34:7's note that the Babylonian army was fighting "against Lachish and Azekah, for these were the only fortified cities of Judah that remained." Three bullae — small clay seal impressions — name figures who appear in Jeremiah's narrative: "Belonging to Berechyahu son of Neriyahu the scribe," almost certainly Baruch ben Neriah, Jeremiah's amanuensis (Jer 36:4); a bulla naming Gemariah son of Shaphan (Jer 36:10); and one identified with Pashhur son of Immer (Jer 20:1). No prophetic book has more named historical figures confirmed by epigraphic finds.

The textual situation of Jeremiah is the most complex of any prophetic book. The Masoretic Hebrew text runs to fifty-two chapters; the Septuagint Greek is one-eighth shorter and relocates the oracles against the nations from chapters 46–51 to a position after 25:13. The Qumran caves preserve both traditions: 4QJer-a and 4QJer-c match the MT, while 4QJer-b matches the LXX *Vorlage*. The standard scholarly position, articulated by Emanuel Tov, J.G. Janzen, and J.A. Thompson, is that the LXX preserves an earlier, shorter recension and the MT represents a later, expanded edition. The implication for chapter 1 is mild — the call narrative is stable across both traditions — but it shapes how one reads the book as a whole: Jeremiah is literally a book of multiple editions.

The standard modern commentaries are Jack R. Lundbom's three-volume Anchor (1999–2004), William Holladay's two-volume Hermeneia (1986, 1989), Walter Brueggemann's *A Commentary on Jeremiah*, Robert P. Carroll's heavily redaction-critical OTL (1986), William McKane's two-volume ICC, John Bright's Anchor (1965), Louis Stulman, and J.A. Thompson's NICOT.

**Key terms.** *Davar YHWH* (דְּבַר־יְהוָה, "the word of the LORD") is the call formula and frames the entire chapter — it appears in 1:2, 1:4, 1:11, 1:13. *Shaqed* (שָׁקֵד, "almond") and *shoqed* (שֹׁקֵד, "watching") form the punning wordplay of 1:11–12 — the consonants ש-ק-ד carry both, and the almond's distinction in Israel is that it blossoms first in late winter, the earliest "watcher" of the agricultural year. *Anatot* (עֲנָתוֹת, the priestly village). *Yada* (יָדַע, "I knew you" in 1:5) — a relational rather than merely cognitive verb. And the six leitmotif verbs of 1:10: *natosh* (pluck up), *natats* (break down), *abad* (destroy), *haras* (overthrow), *banah* (build), *nata* (plant).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 1 is a tightly composed call narrative belonging to a recognizable prophetic-call genre. Norman Habel's classic 1965 form-critical study identified six elements that recur across the call narratives of Moses (Exod 3–4), Gideon (Judg 6), Saul (1 Sam 9–10), Isaiah (Isa 6), Jeremiah, and Ezekiel (Ezek 1–3): divine confrontation, introductory word, commission, objection, reassurance, and sign. Jeremiah's call hits every beat. The confrontation: the word comes in 1:4. The commission: "I appointed you a prophet to the nations" (1:5). The objection: "I do not know how to speak, for I am only a youth" (1:6). The reassurance: "I am with you to deliver you" (1:8). The sign: the touching of the mouth (1:9), and then the two visions of almond branch and boiling pot (1:11–14). The form-critical pattern is so deeply embedded that Jeremiah's reluctance is not psychological but generic — the prophet *must* refuse, because the form requires it.

Genre-wise, Jeremiah as a book is a hybrid. Sigmund Mowinckel's 1914 source analysis distinguished three strata: A (poetic oracles, mostly authentic), B (biographical narrative in prose, sometimes attributed to Baruch), and C (Deuteronomistic prose sermons of disputed authenticity). Chapter 1 contains the superscription (1:1–3, prose), a poetic call oracle (1:5, 7–10), prose narrative of the visions (1:11–16), and a closing oracle of fortification (1:17–19) that is itself part poetry, part prose. The chapter is, in effect, a microcosm of the book's compositional layering.

The two visions in 1:11–14 work by different mechanisms. The almond-branch vision is *paronomasia* — Hebrew wordplay. *Shaqed* (almond) and *shoqed* (watching) share the root ש-ק-ד; the vision turns on a pun, and the LXX translator, working without access to the Hebrew sound, had to abandon the wordplay entirely. (The Greek has *karuinēn baktrian*, "rod of nut-tree," and then must explain the *shoqed* connection prosaically.) The boiling pot vision (1:13–14) is straightforwardly visual — a *sir nafuach*, a tilted cauldron facing south, away from the north, with its contents about to spill southward — and it announces the geographical theme that will dominate the book: judgment from the north, which means in concrete terms the Neo-Babylonian army that will arrive via the standard invasion route through the Beqaa, Damascus, and the Galilee.

The leitmotif of 1:10 — the six verbs of plucking up and planting — recurs at 12:14–17, 18:7–10, 24:6, 31:28, 42:10, and 45:4. Holladay treats this as the structural skeleton of the entire book: every major movement reactivates the verbs in different proportions, and the four-to-two negative-positive ratio of 1:10 (four verbs of destruction, two of restoration) inverts in chapter 31, where building and planting predominate. Read this way, the call narrative is not a self-contained vignette but the keynote that the book will modulate.

The closing image of 1:18 — *ir mivtsar* (fortified city), *amud barzel* (iron pillar), *chomot nechoshet* (bronze walls) — uses the language of military architecture against the prophet's own body. Jeremiah is *made* a fortress, against the very institutions (king, officials, priests, people of the land) he will denounce. This inversion of normal political architecture, where the prophet becomes a structure stronger than the city he prophesies against, is a literary signature of the book.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Jeremiah stands in the canonical arc as the bridge prophet between the kingdom and the exile. He inherits the Deuteronomic theology of Josiah's reform (the curses of Deut 28 hover over every oracle of judgment), watches that reform fail to alter the trajectory of the nation, and prophesies through the destruction of the temple and the deportations. His ministry frames the books of Lamentations (which Jewish tradition assigns to Jeremiah, hence its placement in the LXX after Jeremiah), Daniel (whose prayer in chapter 9 cites the "seventy years" of Jer 25:11–12 and 29:10), and the Deuteronomistic History (whose theology of covenant infidelity-leading-to-exile reads almost as a narrative gloss on Jeremiah's preaching).

The New Testament debt to Jeremiah is concentrated in the new-covenant passage of Jer 31:31–34, which Hebrews 8:8–12 quotes in full — the longest Old Testament citation in the New Testament. But chapter 1 also has reception. *Before I formed you in the womb I knew you* (1:5) becomes a typological resource for prenatal call narratives: Isaac (Gen 18, 21), Samson (Judg 13), Samuel (1 Sam 1), John the Baptist (Lk 1), and Paul, who explicitly echoes Jer 1:5 in Gal 1:15 ("when he who had set me apart before I was born and called me by his grace"). The hand-touching-mouth in 1:9 parallels Isaiah 6:6–7 (the seraph with the burning coal) and Ezek 2:8–3:3 (the eaten scroll), constituting a small typology of prophetic ordination by oral consecration.

**Jewish trajectory.** Sirach 49:6–7 praises Jeremiah as "the prophet who was consecrated in his mother's womb to pluck up and ruin and destroy, and likewise to build and to plant" — the earliest reception, already pulling from chapter 1 specifically. The pseudepigraphic *Lives of the Prophets* preserves traditions of Jeremiah's death by stoning in Egypt. Bavli Megillah 14b counts Jeremiah among the forty-eight prophets. Targum Jonathan to the Prophets supplies the standard Aramaic paraphrase, and Pesikta Rabbati uses Jeremiah heavily for Tisha B'Av lamentation. The medieval commentators — Rashi (eleventh-century Champagne), Radak (David Kimchi, twelfth–thirteenth-century Provence), Ibn Ezra, and Abrabanel — provide the standard rabbinic readings. Modern Jewish engagement runs through Yochanan Muffs, Moshe Greenberg, Jon Levenson, and Benjamin D. Sommer (*The Bodies of God*).

**Christian trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah* (c. 240, fourteen Greek homilies surviving) is the major patristic treatment, intensely allegorical and Christological, reading Jeremiah's prenatal calling as a type of Christ's eternal generation. Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah*, left incomplete at his death in 420 (books 1–6 survive, covering Jer 1–32), is the most extensive patristic commentary; Theodoret's commentary follows in the fifth century. The medieval reception runs through Rupert of Deutz, Peter Lombard, and Aquinas. The Reformation produced Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah* (1563, five volumes — his largest single Old Testament work) and Luther's substantial preaching; both treat the call narrative as central to the doctrine of vocational election. Critical scholarship begins with Bernhard Duhm's 1901 commentary, foundational for source criticism, followed by Mowinckel's 1914 A/B/C theory, John Skinner's *Prophecy and Religion* (1922), and the modern critical commentaries already named (Lundbom, Holladay, Carroll, McKane). Contemporary readings include Brueggemann's, Stulman's, Kathleen O'Connor's *Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*, Christopher J.H. Wright, J. Andrew Dearman, J.A. Thompson's NICOT, and Tremper Longman.

**Where the traditions diverge.** Christian tradition reads Jeremiah's call Christologically — the suffering prophet typologically anticipates Christ, the prenatal consecration prefigures the Incarnation, the new covenant is fulfilled in the Eucharistic blood. Jewish tradition reads Jeremiah as the canonical prophet of *teshuvah* (return) and as the voice of Tisha B'Av lamentation; the call narrative grounds the dignity of prophetic vocation but is not Christologically loaded. Critical tradition focuses on the editorial layering — how much of chapter 1 is original Jeremianic material and how much is Deuteronomistic redaction — and Carroll, in particular, reads the prenatal-calling motif as a late theological retrojection rather than autobiographical report.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Prenatal calling and human freedom.** The verse *Before I formed you in the womb I knew you, and before you were born I consecrated you; I appointed you a prophet to the nations* (1:5) is one of the most contested loci of theological anthropology in the Old Testament. The Reformed tradition reads it as a textbook locus for unconditional election: God's choice precedes not only any merit but any existence, and Calvin (Institutes 3.21–24) explicitly cites Jer 1:5 alongside Eph 1:4 to argue that election is logically prior to creation. The Catholic position, articulated by Aquinas (ST I.23) and reaffirmed at Trent and again in *Lumen Gentium*, holds that God's foreknowledge does include predestination but that grace operates through (not against) human freedom; Aquinas distinguishes *praedestinatio* (which is real and prior) from *praescientia* (foreknowledge) such that the divine decree includes human cooperation. The Eastern Orthodox tradition, working from Maximus the Confessor and the Greek Fathers, prefers the language of *synergeia* (cooperation) and reads 1:5 as describing God's eternal *logos* for each person, which the person actualizes by free response. The Anabaptist and Wesleyan-Arminian readings, citing Arminius and later Wesley, distinguish foreknowledge from foreordination: God *knows* Jeremiah before forming him but does not *cause* his prophetic identity in a way that overrides his freedom; the *yada* of 1:5 is relational, not deterministic.

The steelman of the Reformed position is sometimes underplayed. The verb *yada* in 1:5 is the same verb used of the LORD's "knowing" Israel in Amos 3:2 ("you only have I known of all the families of the earth"), where it plainly carries elective force, and of God's "knowing" Moses face to face in Exod 33:17. To translate *yada* here as bare cognitive foreknowledge, as the Arminian reading sometimes does, drains the verb of its covenantal weight. The Reformed insistence that the verse is *electing* knowledge is exegetically defensible. But the steelman of the Arminian reading is also sharper than usually credited: even on the strongest electing reading, the call narrative *includes Jeremiah's objection* (1:6), and the LORD does not override that objection but answers it with assurance (1:8). The shape of the call narrative itself — refusal, reassurance, commission — assumes that the prophet's response is not pre-coerced. Where this leaves us: the text holds prior election and lived freedom in a tension neither tradition fully resolves, and the way one weighs them tracks one's broader doctrine of grace.

**Tension 2: The reluctant prophet and the structure of vocation.** Every major prophetic call begins with refusal. Moses pleads inability ("I am slow of speech," Exod 4:10). Isaiah confesses uncleanness (Isa 6:5). Jonah flees. Jeremiah pleads youth ("I am only a *na'ar*," 1:6 — *na'ar* could mean anything from a small child to a young man, though in priestly contexts it sometimes denotes a junior priest). Ezekiel falls on his face. The pattern is so consistent that scholars have long asked what it is *doing*. One reading, going back to Augustine and developed in Reformed theology, is that the refusal demonstrates total depravity: the unaided will resists the divine call, and only grace overcomes resistance. A second reading, more characteristic of Jewish tradition (Heschel, *The Prophets*), is that the refusal is the *appropriate* response to the divine summons — the prophet who does not feel the weight of the office is unfit for it; reluctance is moral seriousness. A third reading, anthropological (Habel, Westermann), is that the form-critical pattern simply mirrors the social reality of prophetic charisma — call narratives encode the legitimating function of the office, and the refusal authenticates the prophet against accusations of self-aggrandizement.

The Lutheran tradition reads the reluctance through the lens of *theologia crucis*: the prophet's "I am only a youth" is the proper recognition that God works through weakness, and the divine answer ("I am with you") is not a denial of the weakness but a promise to inhabit it. The Catholic tradition reads it through the doctrine of grace perfecting nature: Jeremiah's youth is real, and God's calling does not bypass it but consecrates it. Where this leaves us: prophetic refusal is not a flaw to be apologized for but a structural feature of how God's word enters the world, through reluctant, inadequate human voices that nonetheless become "fortified cities, iron pillars, bronze walls" (1:18).

**One unresolved question.** If God's word truly performs itself — if the LORD is "watching over my word to perform it" (1:12) — then in what sense did Jeremiah's forty years of preaching *fail*? The temple fell, the city was destroyed, the people were deported, and Jeremiah himself died in exile in Egypt. Was the "watching" the keeping of the word of judgment, the fulfillment of the boiling-pot vision, and not the saving of the people? If so, the chapter ends with a darker promise than it sounds: God's word is reliable, but its reliability may be the reliability of catastrophe.

## The Thread

The Deuteronomic curses of Deut 28 stand behind Jeremiah's call as the legal substrate: the boiling-pot vision of 1:13–14 is the narrative enactment of Deut 28:49, "the LORD will bring a nation against you from far away, from the end of the earth, swooping down like the eagle." The leitmotif of 1:10 — *to pluck up and to break down, to destroy and to overthrow, to build and to plant* — is reactivated at the structural pivot of Jeremiah 31:28 ("as I have watched over them to pluck up and break down… so I will watch over them to build and to plant"), where the negative four-to-two ratio of chapter 1 inverts. And the prenatal-consecration formula of 1:5 echoes forward into Lk 1:15 (John the Baptist filled with the Spirit "from his mother's womb") and Gal 1:15 (Paul "set apart from my mother's womb"), making Jeremiah's call a typological seedbed for New Testament vocation narratives without requiring the supersessionist reading the later tradition sometimes imposed on it.
