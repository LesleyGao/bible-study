# 2 Kings 18: Hezekiah's Reform and the Rabshakeh at the Wall

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Hezekiah son of Ahaz reigned approximately **715–686 BCE**, with the chronological complications of a possible coregency with his father (Edwin Thiele's reconstruction in *The Mysterious Numbers of the Hebrew Kings*, 1965, remains a touchstone; Galil and Cogan-Tadmor refine differently). His reign coincides with the apex of Neo-Assyrian power under **Sargon II** (722–705) and **Sennacherib** (705–681), and the chapter telescopes nearly his entire reign before delivering its hammerblow: **Sennacherib's third campaign of 701 BCE** against Judah.

The archaeological dossier for this chapter is the richest of any in the Hebrew Bible. **Sennacherib's Annals** survive in three near-identical prism copies: the **Taylor Prism** (British Museum, K.1675a, c. 691 BCE), the **Oriental Institute Prism** (Chicago, OIM A2793, c. 689 BCE), and the **Jerusalem Prism** (Israel Museum, c. 690 BCE). The 701 campaign account is the longest sustained narrative concerning a Judahite king in any extrabiblical source: Sennacherib boasts of conquering 46 fortified Judahite cities, deporting 200,150 people (the figure is rhetorical Assyrian inflation, but the order of magnitude registers real demographic catastrophe), and shutting up Hezekiah *kīma iṣṣūr quppi*, "**like a caged bird**," in Jerusalem his royal city. Strikingly, Sennacherib **never claims to have taken Jerusalem** — among the most celebrated argumenta-ex-silentio in ancient history. The standard scholarly editions are Daniel Luckenbill's *Annals of Sennacherib* (1924) and more recently Eckart Frahm's *Einleitung in die Sanherib-Inschriften* (1997).

The **Lachish Reliefs** — discovered by Austen Henry Layard in 1845 in the Southwest Palace of Sennacherib at Nineveh, now in the British Museum — provide the visual counterpart. The reliefs depict in extraordinary detail the siege of Lachish: the ramped assault, the battering rams, the Judean defenders, the lines of deportees with their possessions, the impalement of captives. Lachish is the only Levantine city Sennacherib chose to commemorate so monumentally — confirming the biblical detail of v. 14 that Hezekiah negotiated with the king "at Lachish." **David Ussishkin's** Lachish excavations (Tel Aviv University, multiple seasons from 1973 onward; *The Renewed Archaeological Excavations at Lachish*, 5 vols., 2004) confirmed the **Level III destruction layer** with massive Assyrian siege debris, including the siege ramp itself — one of the few Iron II Assyrian siege ramps surviving. Yosef Garfinkel's continuing work at the site has further refined the chronology.

For Hezekiah's reform program, multiple archaeological synchronisms register: **Tel Arad**'s sanctuary, with its altar dismantled and buried in late 8th-c. context (Yohanan Aharoni; Ze'ev Herzog, *Tel Aviv* 29, 2002), is widely read as evidence of a Hezekian centralization initiative, though the dating is disputed. **Tel Beersheba**'s horned altar, found dismantled and reused in storehouse walls, similarly fits a late-8th-c. reform horizon (Aharoni). The **LMLK seal impressions** (the *l-melek*, "belonging to the king," jar handles, totaling over 2,000 examples from sites across Judah) cluster precisely in the late 8th c. and reflect Hezekiah's military-logistical preparation for the Assyrian threat — provisioning fortified cities with standardized storage jars (Andre Lemaire's classic typology; Oded Lipschits's recent re-analyses).

The most direct extrabiblical confirmation of Hezekiah by name is the **Hezekiah Bulla**, a clay seal impression reading *l-ḥzqyhw [bn] 'ḥz mlk yhdh* — "Belonging to Hezekiah [son of] Ahaz, king of Judah" — featuring a winged sun-disk iconography. Multiple bullae have surfaced; **Eilat Mazar's 2009–2015 Ophel excavations** in Jerusalem yielded a controlled-archaeological example, the strongest contextual provenance to date.

Three Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. **בָּטַח** (*bāṭaḥ*, "to trust"; vv. 5, 19, 20, 21, 22, 24, 30) — the verbal root threading through the chapter; "trust" is the theological battleground. The Rabshakeh uses it nine times in the speech to dismantle every possible object of Judahite trust. **נְחֻשְׁתָּן** (*Nĕḥuštān*; v. 4) — Hezekiah's contemptuous coinage for the bronze serpent, a diminutive-pejorative meaning roughly "bronze-thingy"; one of the most sophisticated ironic neologisms in the Hebrew Bible. **רַבְשָׁקֵה** (*Rabshākēh*; vv. 17 onward) — a title not a name, from Akkadian *rab šāqê* ("chief cupbearer," senior court official), confirmed by Assyrian administrative texts.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is generically composite. Vv. 1–8 is a regnal evaluation of unusual fulsomeness — the dtr's most positive verdict on any Judahite king after David. Vv. 9–12 is a recapitulation of the Northern Kingdom's fall, structurally placed to pair Hezekiah's first decade with the Northern catastrophe — the implicit question being whether Judah will repeat the pattern. Vv. 13–16 is a brief annalistic account of Sennacherib's invasion and Hezekiah's tribute payment (a passage often labeled **Account A** in critical scholarship, after Bernhard Stade's 1886 isolation of three-source theory). Vv. 17–37 is the elaborate **Rabshakeh narrative** (**Account B**, with its parallel running through ch 19), a literarily sophisticated diplomatic-rhetorical set-piece.

The **Rabshakeh's speech** is one of the most carefully composed pieces of dialogic rhetoric in the Hebrew Bible. Brevard Childs (*Isaiah and the Assyrian Crisis*, 1967), Peter Machinist ("The Rab Šāqēh at the Wall of Jerusalem," *HUCA* 60, 2000), and Mordechai Cogan-Tadmor have all read it as authentic Neo-Assyrian rhetorical construction — its ideology and topoi match Sargonid royal propaganda exactly. Machinist's analysis identifies multiple stock Assyrian rhetorical moves: the *unde-fertis-confidentiam* opening ("on what do you trust?"), the dismissal of Egyptian alliance as "broken reed," the claim of divine commission ("the LORD said to me"), and the catalog of conquered gods. The speech is delivered in deliberate **Hebrew (the "language of Judah")** so the people on the walls can hear — and the officials' plea to switch to Aramaic (v. 26) is a classic moment of diplomatic register-control attempted and refused. The Rabshakeh's escalation to overt psychological warfare ("eat their own dung and drink their own urine") is the violation of normal diplomatic protocol.

Devices: **structural mirroring** binds the chapter to ch 19 through the Rabshakeh's two-stage rhetoric (taunting Hezekiah, then bypassing him to address the people directly), which will be answered in two stages by Isaiah's two oracles. The use of **threefold repetition of "trust"** in vv. 19–24 is not accidental — Robert Alter (*The Art of Biblical Narrative*, 1981; updated translation *Ancient Israel*, 2013) reads it as deliberate rhetorical erosion. The **silent populace** (v. 36) is a striking inversion of the standard biblical pattern of crowd response — Hezekiah has commanded silence, and the silence becomes itself a form of resistance.

**Textual-critical note — the Isaiah 36–39 // 2 Kings 18–20 parallel.** This is one of the most studied parallel-text problems in the Hebrew Bible. The narratives are nearly word-for-word identical for chs 18–19, with several significant divergences. The **dominant scholarly view** (Cogan-Tadmor, Sweeney, Christopher Seitz *Isaiah 1–39*, OTL 1993, Williamson *The Book Called Isaiah*, 1994) is that **2 Kings 18–19 preserves the older form of the Sennacherib material**, which was incorporated into Isaiah at a relatively late editorial stage. The major evidence: 2 Kgs 18:14–16 (the tribute pericope, often called **Account A**) is **absent from Isaiah 36** — strong evidence that Isaiah's editor abridged the Kings text, not vice versa. Account A's omission cleans up the theological narrative for Isaiah's purposes (downplaying Hezekiah's earlier capitulation to focus on the deliverance). Conversely, **the Hezekiah song in Isaiah 38:9–20 is widely regarded as older than the parallel material in 2 Kings 20** — the Kings narrative has dropped the song. So the parallel works in both directions: Kings preserves the older narrative; Isaiah preserves the older song. Klaus Smelik, Antti Laato, Ehud Ben Zvi, and Francolino Gonçalves have all written extensively on the source-critical question. **Bernhard Stade's** classic three-source analysis (Account A: vv. 13–16; Account B1: 18:17–19:9a, 19:36–37; Account B2: 19:9b–35) remains the framework most scholars work from, even when modifying it.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish trajectory.** Josephus (*Ant.* 9.276; 10.1–23) supplies extensive Hezekiah material, harmonizing Kings, Chronicles, and Isaiah, and offering Hellenistic moralization on the king as exemplar of trust in God. **Sirach 48:17–25** memorializes Hezekiah alongside Isaiah in a hymn of praise to the great Israelite figures. The rabbinic tradition is correspondingly rich. **b. Berakhot 10b** preserves the famous tradition that Hezekiah hid the Book of Cures so that people would pray to God rather than rely on remedies — read theologically as the king's commitment to direct trust in YHWH. **b. Sanhedrin 94a–95b** has an extensive Hezekiah-Sennacherib midrash, including the legend that **Hezekiah was a candidate to be the Messiah** but was disqualified for failing to sing praise after the deliverance (a reading that links chs 18–20 tightly to messianic expectation). The tradition of the **bronze serpent destroyed** — Nehushtan — is the subject of debate: was Hezekiah right to destroy a Mosaic relic? **m. Pesahim 4:9** records the rabbinic assessment that he was praised for it. **Rashi** on 18:4 emphasizes Hezekiah as the model iconoclast whose courage exceeded that of his predecessors. **Radak** focuses on the fulsomeness of v. 5 and the question of how Hezekiah's praise relates to David's. **Ralbag** treats the Rabshakeh's speech as a sophisticated philosophical attack that requires the answer of trust as a counter-argument, not merely an emotional response. **Abarbanel** is fascinated by the diplomatic-political mechanics: he reads the Hebrew/Aramaic register dispute as the classical case of how propaganda is weaponized against a population. **Avivah Zornberg**'s reading of the Hezekiah cycle dwells on the king's psychology — particularly the contrast with his father Ahaz's failure of trust. **Yair Zakovitch** and **Yairah Amit** have emphasized the literary artistry of the Rabshakeh narrative as a deliberately constructed contrast with the Ahaz narrative of ch 16.

**Christian trajectory.** The patristic tradition is voluminous on Hezekiah, far more than on most Judahite kings. **Origen** treats Hezekiah's reform as type of the church's purification. **Augustine** (*City of God* 18.36) treats the deliverance as evidence of providential history. **Theodoret of Cyrus** has detailed commentary on the Rabshakeh's speech, reading it as a textbook on how the world tempts the church to abandon trust. **Chrysostom**'s **homilies on the Sennacherib episode** are extensive and remained foundational for Eastern Christian preaching on the deliverance theme. **Ambrose** treats Hezekiah as a model Christian ruler. **Gregory the Great** (*Moralia in Job*) draws on the chapter in his theology of suffering and trust. **Aquinas** cites Hezekiah's reform among examples of legitimate royal action against idolatry (*ST* II-II q. 94). **Calvin's sermons** on 2 Kings 18 (Geneva sermons, transcribed by his secretaries; preserved in the Calvin Translation Society's 19th-c. editions) make the chapter the centerpiece of his theology of *trust* — the Rabshakeh's "on what do you trust?" becomes for Calvin the question that defines theological existence. Calvin spends extended sermons on the destruction of the bronze serpent as a model for Reformation iconoclasm. **Luther**'s commentary on the Hezekiah material similarly emphasizes trust as the defining theological category.

Critical scholarship: **Cogan and Tadmor (AB *II Kings*)** is foundational; **Hobbs (WBC *2 Kings*)** treats the chapter with attention to the diplomatic and rhetorical detail; **Sweeney (OTL *I & II Kings*)** integrates the chapter into the Josianic framework. **Christopher Seitz**'s *Zion's Final Destiny: The Development of the Book of Isaiah* (1991) is the major work on the Kings/Isaiah parallel from the Isaian side. **Brevard Childs**'s *Isaiah and the Assyrian Crisis* (1967) is the classical critical study of the Sennacherib material. **Peter Machinist**'s *HUCA* article on the Rabshakeh is the foundational analysis of the Assyrian rhetorical context. **Mordechai Cogan's *The Raging Torrent*** anthologizes the prism inscriptions in accessible form. **Brueggemann's *1 & 2 Kings*** gives the chapter substantial theological treatment, focusing on the trust-rhetoric. **Iain Provan**, **Dale Ralph Davis**, **Goldingay** all give extended commentary. **Marvin Sweeney's *King Josiah of Judah*** treats Hezekiah as anticipating the Josianic reform program — a precursor whose achievements ground Josiah's later more thorough reform.

The literary reception extends beyond commentary. **Lord Byron's "The Destruction of Sennacherib"** (1815) — though the destruction itself is in ch 19 — is the great anglophone literary monument to the deliverance, and its opening line "The Assyrian came down like the wolf on the fold" remains the most famous line in English-language poetry on a biblical episode. The reception of the bronze serpent — Nehushtan — through Christian typology (Numbers 21:8–9 read as type of the cross, John 3:14) makes Hezekiah's destruction of it theologically delicate: the symbol that became idolatrous is the same symbol Christ identifies with himself.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is Hezekiah's reform — including the destruction of the bronze serpent — pious zeal or destructive iconoclasm?**

*Position A (the dtr's verdict — pious zeal):* The chapter's evaluation in vv. 4–6 is unequivocal: Hezekiah's destruction of the high places, the pillars, the Asherim, and Nehushtan is the reason "there was none like him among the kings of Judah." The dtr places Hezekiah's reform precisely as the answer to the catalog of offenses in ch 17. Calvin reads this with full enthusiasm: Hezekiah is the proto-Reformer.

*Position B (destruction has theological cost):* The bronze serpent was a Mosaic artifact (Num 21:8–9) — destroying it was not just dismantling Canaanite cult, but eliminating a piece of Israelite tradition. Some readers (Brueggemann is gentle on this; some feminist scholars sharper) note that reform-by-destruction has costs, including the loss of legitimate symbolic resources alongside the corrupt ones.

*Position C (mediating — destruction reveals the prior corruption):* Provan and Davis argue the destruction is necessary precisely *because* the people had corrupted a legitimate object into an idol. Hezekiah is not destroying tradition; he is destroying what tradition had become.

**Steelman of the most uncomfortable position:** Position B. There is something theologically jarring about a Davidic king breaking apart a Mosaic relic and renaming it Nehushtan ("bronze-thingy") — a calculated act of contempt. The Numbers 21 narrative explicitly attributes the serpent's making to YHWH's command. Hezekiah's iconoclasm is therefore not just anti-idol but anti-relic; it implies a theology in which sacred objects, even those originating in divine instruction, have no enduring sanctity. This is theologically far-reaching. The Reformation drew on this moment to authorize iconoclasm against ecclesiastical tradition, with consequences (and losses) that are still debated.

**Where this leaves us:** Hezekiah's reform raises the perennial question: when does reverence for tradition become idolatry, and when does iconoclasm itself become a kind of impiety? The chapter does not resolve the tension; it dramatizes one moment in which the king chooses destruction, and the dtr endorses the choice.

**Tension 2: Is the Rabshakeh's claim to act on YHWH's commission (v. 25) a deceptive ploy, a partially true theological insight, or a genuine prophetic fragment?**

*Position A (deceptive ploy):* Most traditional readers — patristic, medieval, much modern conservative scholarship — read the Rabshakeh's claim as Assyrian propaganda exploiting Hezekiah's reform (the high places he removed) for psychological warfare. The Rabshakeh has gathered intelligence and is using it manipulatively.

*Position B (theologically substantive — Assyria as YHWH's instrument):* But Isaiah himself (Isa 10:5–6) identifies Assyria as "the rod of my anger," YHWH's instrument of judgment. Read against Isaiah, the Rabshakeh's claim is **partially true** — Assyria *is* fulfilling a divine role, even if as judgment-instrument rather than as Hezekiah's expected outcome. Brueggemann presses this hard: the chapter shows that imperial actors can speak partial truths about God that the people of God must reckon with.

*Position C (the speech as theological mirror):* The Rabshakeh's speech functions narratively as a mirror to Hezekiah's faith. The questions it asks — on what do you trust? where is your god? — are real questions that the deliverance must answer in real terms, not merely dismiss as imperial propaganda.

**Steelman of the most uncomfortable position:** Position B. The Hebrew Bible is willing to put theological insight in the mouths of Israel's enemies (Balaam, Cyrus, Nebuchadnezzar in Jeremiah). The Rabshakeh, in this reading, is an Assyrian official who has accidentally spoken a fragment of prophetic truth: YHWH did indeed bring the Assyrian against the land — though not for the destruction of Jerusalem the Rabshakeh intends. The chapter's tension is precisely that the enemy speaks something true about God in service of something false about God's purposes.

**Where this leaves us:** Theology is rarely cleanly partisan. The chapter shows that the test of trust is not whether the enemy's claims are absolutely false but whether one can hear what is true in them and still trust that God's purposes exceed what the enemy understands.

**Unresolved question:** Why does the dtr include the awkward tribute-and-temple-stripping pericope (vv. 14–16) at all, when it complicates the narrative of unwavering Hezekian trust? Some scholars (Stade, Childs) argue the inclusion is precisely the dtr's commitment to historical honesty even at theological cost — the king who later resists Sennacherib first paid him off with Temple gold. The tension between Account A and Account B is theological as well as text-critical: it is the difference between a Hezekiah who is humanly compromised and rises to faith, and a Hezekiah who is theologically pristine throughout.

## The Thread

Read 2 Kings 18 against **Isaiah 30–31** for the prophetic context — Isaiah's denunciations of the Egyptian alliance ("woe to those who go down to Egypt for help") date to exactly this period and form the prophetic counterpart to Hezekiah's eventual repudiation of trust in Pharaoh. Read it against **Isaiah 36** for the parallel narrative — and note the absence of vv. 14–16 there. Read it against **2 Chronicles 29–32** for the Chronicler's expanded Hezekiah, with its extensive Passover and reform material. Read it forward into **2 Kings 19** for the deliverance, into **Isaiah 22:9–11** for the prophet's later critique of Jerusalem's wall-building and weapon-stockpiling. And read **Numbers 21:8–9** alongside **John 3:14** to see what it means for the bronze serpent — destroyed in v. 4 — to be raised again, typologically, on a different cross.
