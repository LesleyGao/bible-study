# 2 Corinthians 1: The God of All Comfort and the Faithfulness of the Yes

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

- **Political/social situation**: Roman Corinth—Colonia Laus Iulia Corinthiensis, refounded by Julius Caesar in 44 BC after Mummius's destruction in 146 BC—was by the AD 50s the capital of the senatorial province of Achaia. Paul had founded the church there c. AD 50-51 (Acts 18; the Gallio inscription at Delphi, *Fouilles de Delphes* III.4 286, dates Gallio's proconsulship to AD 51-52 and remains the firmest peg in Pauline chronology). 1 Corinthians was written from Ephesus c. AD 53-54. Between 1 Cor and 2 Cor, Paul made a "painful visit" (2:1) and dispatched a now-lost "tearful letter" (2:4); 2 Corinthians itself was composed from Macedonia c. AD 55-56 after the Asian crisis referred to in 1:8-11. The social fabric of Roman Corinth—Bruce Winter's *After Paul Left Corinth* (2001), Gerd Theissen's *Social Setting of Pauline Christianity* (1982), and Wayne Meeks's *First Urban Christians* (1983) are essential—was a status-saturated patron-client world with a stratified Christian assembly. Cicero's friend, the freedman Erastus, named in the famous pavement inscription (ERASTVS PRO AEDILIT[AT]E S P STRAVIT, still in situ near the theater), is a likely candidate for the Erastus of Rom 16:23. Dale Martin (*The Corinthian Body*, 1995) argues that Corinthian opponents of Paul valued public oratorical display and physical presence—the very things Paul concedes he lacks—so the apologetic register of 2 Corinthians is calibrated to a city that judged speakers by ethos performed.
- **Material culture**: The Asian affliction of 1:8 sits inside a culture in which travel by sea was genuinely dangerous (the second-century stowed-away grain-ship loss off Malta is only the most famous case), in which Roman judicial penalty often ended in death (Acts 16:22-24, 22:25-29), and in which the bēma (judicial bench) Paul had stood before in Corinth (Acts 18:12-17) was still visible in the forum. The "sentence of death" of 1:9 (*apokrima tou thanatou*) is a forensic term—as Margaret Thrall (ICC, 1994) and Murray Harris (NIGTC, 2005) note, it refers to the formal decision of a court, not generic peril. Whether or not the Asia event was a literal trial, the language is borrowed from courtroom finality.
- **Authorship & dating**: Authentic Pauline. The huge issue is literary unity. Major partition theories: (1) chapters 10-13 as separate—either the very "tearful letter" (Hausrath 1870, Kennedy 1900) or a later post-2 Cor 1-9 letter (F.F. Bruce, Furnish, Ralph Martin, Francis Watson)—motivated by the radical tonal shift from reconciliation to attack; (2) 2:14-7:4 as an apology inserted into a 1:1-2:13 + 7:5-16 thanksgiving frame (Bornkamm, Welborn); (3) 6:14-7:1 as a non-Pauline interpolation reflecting Qumran-style purity language (Murphy-O'Connor, Fitzmyer; defended as Pauline by Stockhausen and Beale); (4) chapters 8 and 9 as separate collection-appeal letters (Semler, Windisch, Hans Dieter Betz). Defenders of unity—Philip Hughes, C.K. Barrett, Murray Harris, Linda Belleville, Ben Witherington, Fredrick Long—stress that no manuscript preserves any partitioned form. P46 (c. AD 200), Sinaiticus, and Vaticanus all carry 2 Corinthians whole. The partition debate is purely text-internal and inferential.
- **Archaeological & textual evidence**: P46, the Chester Beatty Pauline papyrus, is again our earliest substantial witness, with the great fourth-century uncials Sinaiticus and Vaticanus close behind. The Gallio inscription anchors the chronology. The bēma at Corinth and the Erastus pavement remain the touchstone material remains.
- **Key terms**: *paraklēsis* (παράκλησις, "comfort/encouragement/exhortation"—the verb *parakaleō* is densely repeated in 1:3-7), *thlipsis* (θλῖψις, "affliction, pressure"), *arrabōn* (ἀρραβών, "earnest, down payment"—a Semitic loanword from commercial law), *exaporeō* (ἐξαπορέω, "to be utterly at a loss, to despair"), *sphragizō* (σφραγίζω, "to seal"), *nai* and *amēn* (ναί, ἀμήν, the Greek and Aramaic affirmations Paul juxtaposes at 1:20).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

- **Genre**: Apostolic letter with a heavy admixture of apologetic self-defense. Frank Hughes (*Early Christian Rhetoric and 2 Corinthians 1989*) and Witherington read the chapter as the *exordium* and *narratio* of a forensic-deliberative argument—Paul is establishing ethos (1:3-11), narrating recent events (1:8-11, 1:15-2:13), and laying the ground for the propositions to come. Margaret Thrall, by contrast, reads the chapter more letter-formally, with 1:3-11 as a reformed Jewish *berakhah*-thanksgiving and 1:12-2:13 as an apologetic *narratio*.
- **Devices**: 1:3-7 deploys dense paronomasia and concatenation on the *paraklēsis* root—ten cognates in five verses, more than anywhere else in the Pauline corpus. The Hellenistic-Jewish *berakhah* opening ("Blessed be the God and Father…") is unparalleled in the undisputed Paulines except Eph 1:3 (which the disputed-letter debate complicates). 1:8-11 narrates with deliberate vagueness—Paul names neither the place specifically beyond "Asia" nor the precipitating event. 1:17 piles up rhetorical questions in diatribe style. 1:18-22 climaxes in an extraordinary doxological-soteriological run: "the Son of God Jesus Christ… was not Yes and No, but in him it is always Yes; for all the promises of God find their Yes in him; that is why it is through him that we utter our Amen to God." 1:21-22 strings four participles describing God's action in believers: *bebaiōn* (establishing), *chrisas* (anointing), *sphragisamenos* (sealing), *dous arrabōna* (giving the down payment).
- **Locate in argument**: Chapter 1 sets the relational and theological foundation for the entire letter. The *paraklēsis* doxology grounds Paul's apostolic authority not in oratorical power but in shared suffering and divine consolation—a frame that will return at 4:7-12 (treasure in jars of clay), 6:3-10 (the catalog of hardships), 11:23-33 (the great peristasis catalog), and 12:7-10 (the thorn). The defense of Paul's changed travel plans (1:15-2:4) is the immediate occasion: opponents at Corinth had charged that his plan-changes proved his unreliability ("Yes-yes and No-no at the same time," 1:17). Paul's answer is that God's faithfulness in Christ—the great Yes-and-Amen of 1:20—is the only register in which his own integrity finally has to be measured.
- **Textual variants**: 1:1 has a few patristic witnesses harmonizing "saints in the whole of Achaia" toward 1 Corinthians' formula; the readings are not meaningfully different. At 1:6-7 the order of clauses varies among major manuscripts (P46 vs Sinaiticus vs Vaticanus differ), but the meaning—mutual participation in suffering and comfort—is constant in every reading. 1:10's threefold deliverance (past, present, future) is firm; the future-tense variant at 1:10b ("he will yet deliver") is exegetically interesting but textually settled. 1:14's "day of our Lord Jesus" has minor variation in the divine names. 1:22's *arrabōn* is uncontested—but its theological weight will reappear at 5:5 and (in disputed Paul) Eph 1:14.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory**

- Patristic: John Chrysostom devoted thirty homilies to 2 Corinthians; Homily 2 on 1:3-7 is the great patristic meditation on consolation as ministerial credential—the comforted comforter as paradigm of pastoral office. Tertullian's *Ad Martyras* uses 1:5-7 to encourage the persecuted; *De Pudicitia*, polemically, deploys the chapter against laxist readings of forgiveness. Origen's lost commentary survives only in catenae fragments, but his reading of *paraklēsis* in dialogue with Isa 40:1 sets a hermeneutical precedent. Theodoret of Cyrus and Ambrosiaster both read 1:8-11 against the background of Asian persecution narratives. Augustine cites 1:22's *arrabōn* of the Spirit repeatedly across the *Tractates on John* and *De Trinitate* 4 as evidence for the inseparability of justification and sanctification.
- Medieval: Aquinas's *Super Secundam Epistolam ad Corinthios* (lectures at Paris) reads 1:5 ("we share abundantly in Christ's sufferings") through the lens of the Mystical Body, and 1:22's *pignus* (the Vulgate's rendering of *arrabōn*) as the seed of the doctrine of created grace. The *Glossa Ordinaria* preserves the patristic synthesis.
- Reformation: Calvin's 1546 commentary on 2 Corinthians is foundational. He reads 1:3-7 as the headwaters of his theology of *communio in passionibus Christi*—a view Bonhoeffer will later radicalize. Luther reads 1:8-9 ("we despaired of life itself… that was to make us rely not on ourselves but on God who raises the dead") as a paradigm of *Anfechtung*—the formative trial that drives the soul out of self-reliance to faith. The Protestant tradition's instinct that suffering has *formative* not *meritorious* function is forged in this chapter. The Wesleyan and Pietist traditions read 1:22 as the textual basis for the inner witness of the Spirit (Romans 8:16 supplies the second leg).
- Modern critical: Margaret Thrall's two-volume ICC (1994, 2000) is the most comprehensive commentary in any language. Victor Furnish's Anchor Yale (1984) is the standard for partition-friendly readings. Ralph Martin's WBC (1986; revised 2014) sets a partition-aware but theologically rich middle ground. Murray Harris's NIGTC (2005) carries the weight of the unity defense. Frank Matera's NTL (2003), Linda Belleville's IVP (1996), Paul Barnett's NICNT (1997), Scott Hafemann's NIVAC, and Ben Witherington's *Conflict and Community in Corinth* (1995) round out the contemporary field. On apocalyptic Paul, J. Louis Martyn, Beverly Gaventa, and Douglas Campbell read 1:8-11 as a microcosm of the Pauline cruciform pattern—death-and-resurrection as the shape of apostolic life.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory**

- The *berakhah* formula ("Blessed be the God and Father…") at 1:3 is straight synagogue liturgy. The Eighteen Benedictions / Amidah open with a structurally identical *Barukh atah Adonai* formula, and the Mishnaic *Berakhot* tractate codifies the practice. Paul prays as a Jew. Daniel Boyarin (*A Radical Jew*, 1994) and Pamela Eisenbaum (*Paul Was Not a Christian*, 2009) emphasize that Paul never abandons Jewish liturgical idiom—the *berakhah* opening of 2 Cor 1 is a clinching exhibit. The "Father of mercies" (*pater tōn oiktirmōn*) maps directly onto *Av ha-rachamim*, a stock Jewish liturgical epithet still embedded in the Sabbath Mussaf service.
- The "comfort" vocabulary echoes Isaiah 40:1 ("Comfort, comfort my people, says your God"—*nachamu nachamu ami*), which in the Jewish liturgical year inaugurates the Seven Sabbaths of Consolation between Tisha b'Av and Rosh Hashanah. Paul is reading apostolic suffering through the prophetic Isaian map of exile-and-return.
- The Yes-and-Amen pattern of 1:20 sits inside Jewish prayer practice. *Amen* is the Aramaic ratification of corporate prayer (b. Berakhot 47a treats answering Amen as more meritorious than reciting the blessing oneself). Paul's claim that the Christ-event is the "Yes" to which the church now answers "Amen" is a christologically intensified version of standard Jewish prayer-rhythm. Mark Nanos and Paula Fredriksen read the move as an *expansion* of Jewish prayer-grammar, not a replacement of it.
- The *arrabōn* of the Spirit (1:22) draws on Tg. Onkelos and rabbinic commercial law, where the *eravon* (Hebrew, ערבון, from which the Greek loanword) is a deposit binding both parties to the full transaction. Genesis 38:17-18 (Tamar's pledge from Judah) is the OT *Sitz im Leben*. The metaphor is commercial-Jewish, not Greco-Roman.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge**: They converge on suffering as formative—both Jewish and Christian readings agree that the comfort which arises from affliction is theologically generative, not incidental. They diverge on the christological inflection of 1:20: Christian readings hear the Yes-and-Amen as a Trinitarian-mediated prayer-grammar in which Christ is the divine Yes; Jewish and Paul-Within-Judaism readings hear it as Paul claiming the Messiah-event has ratified the covenantal promises without dissolving the covenantal frame. They diverge again on the *arrabōn*: Christian tradition (especially Latin) treats the Spirit-pledge as the seed of created grace, sacramental seal, or initial sanctification; Jewish-grammar readings keep the commercial-eschatological force—a binding deposit on a future inheritance still outstanding.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What Paul believed God was doing in this chapter is best captured as the *grounding of apostolic credibility in shared suffering and divine faithfulness*. The Corinthians had begun to suspect Paul of fickleness—a weakness in a culture that read changeable plans as a defect of character. Paul's answer is not to defend his itinerary in administrative terms but to relocate the question entirely: the only "Yes" that finally counts has been said by God in Christ, and Paul's whole life is now an attempt to live inside that Yes, including the Yes that runs through suffering. Two genuine theological tensions emerge.

**Tension 1: Suffering and divine consolation.** 1:3-7 is the densest theology of suffering in the Pauline corpus, and 1:8-11 grounds it in autobiographical near-death experience. What kind of work is suffering doing in the divine economy?

- *Catholic*: redemptive suffering. *Catechism* 1502, 1521 read the anointing of the sick as conforming the sufferer to the passion of Christ; John Paul II's *Salvifici Doloris* (1984) formulates the position systematically: suffering, taken up in Christ, becomes a co-participation in the redemption already accomplished—not adding to Christ's sufficient sacrifice but extending its application through the Mystical Body (echoing Col 1:24's "filling up what is lacking in Christ's afflictions"). 1:5 ("we share abundantly in Christ's sufferings") is the proof-text.
- *Reformed* (Calvin Inst. III.8; Berkhof; Murray): sovereign suffering. God permits affliction within his providence; affliction sanctifies, drives the believer to Christ, and witnesses to the world. Calvin's reading of 1:9 ("that was to make us rely not on ourselves but on God who raises the dead") is paradigmatic—suffering's telos is faith, not merit. The Reformed reject any compensatory or meritorious account of suffering.
- *Lutheran*: *theologia crucis* (Heidelberg Disputation 1518). God's hidden self-revelation runs through suffering, weakness, and the cross. 2 Cor 1:8-9 is the apostolic instance of *Anfechtung*—the trial that strips creaturely self-reliance. Suffering does not earn anything; it is the form God's grace takes when it cracks open self-sufficiency. Bonhoeffer (*Cost of Discipleship*; *Letters and Papers from Prison*) inherits and radicalizes this—God's strength in weakness is not a paradox but a polemic against religious triumphalism.
- *Wesleyan*: formative suffering. Affliction perfects sanctification. Wesley read 1:5-7 against Romans 5:3-5 (suffering produces endurance, character, hope). The Pietist tradition (Spener, Francke) emphasizes the inner witness of the Spirit precisely in trial; the *arrabōn* of 1:22 is read as the experiential confirmation that survives and indeed deepens in *thlipsis*.
- *Eastern Orthodox*: theosis through suffering. The ascetic tradition (Maximus the Confessor, the Philokalia) reads suffering as the crucible of *theōsis*—participation in the divine life conformed to Christ's pattern. Lossky stresses that suffering in itself is not redemptive; suffering joined to Christ's voluntary kenosis is.
- *Liberation theology* (Gutiérrez *A Theology of Liberation*; Sobrino *Christ the Liberator*; Dorothee Soelle *Suffering*): a sharp critique of any theology that valorizes suffering. God is not glorified in suffering as suffering. God is present in solidarity with the oppressed who suffer because of unjust structures. The proper Christian response to 1:3-7 is not romanticizing affliction but resisting the structures that produce it. Soelle in particular distinguishes between suffering that can be alleviated (which Christians must oppose) and suffering that remains (which faith bears).
- *Critical/academic*: J. Louis Martyn and Beverly Gaventa read 1:8-11 apocalyptically—the apostolic *thlipsis* is participation in the Messianic woes, the birth pangs of the new creation, not pedagogical chastening.

The position least intuitive to most modern Western readers is the Catholic doctrine of redemptive suffering, especially in its strong form. *Steelmanned*: the doctrine is not a divine accountancy in which the believer's pain is added to Christ's. It is a metaphysics of the Body of Christ in which the redemption Christ has accomplished historically is *applied* and *extended* through the lived suffering of the members—because the Body is one organism animated by one Spirit, and what one member endures is, ontologically, the whole Body's. *Salvifici Doloris* explicitly excludes any addition to Christ's atoning sufficiency. What it claims is a participatory ontology of the church in which suffering, when taken up in faith, becomes a site at which redemption is communicated. The Reformed instinct that this risks a transactional view is fair, but the Catholic instinct that the alternative leaves suffering meaningless—mere providential pedagogy without participatory weight—is also fair. *Where this leaves us*: 1:5 ("we share abundantly in Christ's sufferings") will not yield to a single account. Paul has refused systematization; what he has insisted on is that the comforted comforter is the only kind of minister there is.

**Tension 2: The Spirit as *arrabōn*—earnest, pledge, down payment.** 1:22 is one of three Pauline texts on the Spirit-as-pledge (also 5:5 and the disputed Eph 1:14). What kind of "first installment" is this?

- *Catholic* (CCC 1302-1305 on Confirmation): the Spirit given at baptism is sealed and strengthened in the sacrament of Confirmation. The *arrabōn* is initiatory; Confirmation is the perfecting seal. The Eastern Catholic and Orthodox practice of chrismation immediately following baptism preserves the unity of the seal more visibly than the delayed Western Confirmation.
- *Reformed* (Calvin Inst. III.1, IV.14-15; Westminster Confession): the Spirit's seal at conversion is the application of election. The *arrabōn* of 1:22 is a single, comprehensive pledge that secures the elect in Christ; it is not a stage. The Reformed read 1:22 against Eph 1:13-14 as a unified pneumatology of the believer.
- *Lutheran*: the Spirit is given at baptism. The *arrabōn* is sacramental and forensic—pledge of the future inheritance grounded in baptismal incorporation. The Lutheran tradition resists making the seal a separable second experience.
- *Wesleyan-Pentecostal*: the Spirit is initially given at conversion, but a fuller experiential seal—the "second blessing" (Wesleyan) or "baptism in the Spirit" (classical Pentecostal)—is a distinct subsequent experience. 1:22 is read in tandem with Acts 2 and the Spirit-baptism passages of Acts to argue for two stages. The charismatic renewal (Catholic and Protestant) has softened the two-stage claim into a continuum but retained the experiential emphasis.
- *Eastern Orthodox*: the Spirit is given at chrismation and progressively appropriated through synergistic cooperation in the divine life. The *arrabōn* is not a deposit awaiting later transfer but a real participation in the eschatological gift, foretasted now.
- *Critical/academic*: Gordon Fee (*God's Empowering Presence*, 1994) reads Pauline pneumatology as fundamentally experiential and eschatological—the *arrabōn* is the eschatological future already breaking into present life through the Spirit, not a second-stage anything. James Dunn's *Baptism in the Holy Spirit* (1970) argues against Pentecostal subsequence on Pauline grounds.

The position least intuitive to magisterial Protestants is the classical Pentecostal doctrine of subsequence. *Steelmanned*: classical Pentecostalism (Charles Parham, William Seymour at Azusa Street, the Assemblies of God *Statement of Fundamental Truths*) is not claiming that the Spirit is absent at conversion. It is claiming that the New Testament narrative pattern (Acts 2, 8, 10, 19) repeatedly shows believers receiving a *further* outpouring of the Spirit subsequent to initial faith, and that Paul's *arrabōn* language—a *first* installment, with more to come—coheres with that pattern. The Pentecostal point, sociologically and theologically, is that the magisterial Protestant settlement risks a textually thin pneumatology that has flattened New Testament charismatic life into a forensic seal. The critique cuts. What the Pentecostal reading struggles to accommodate is Paul's clear identification of the Spirit's indwelling with conversion itself (Rom 8:9, 1 Cor 12:13). *Where this leaves us*: 1:22 will not settle the order-of-application questions Western theology has fought over for four centuries. It will, however, ground every position in the same Pauline conviction that the Spirit is God's own pledge that the future is already underwritten.

**One unresolved question**: Paul stakes his apostolic credibility on a God whose every promise is "Yes" in Christ (1:20), even while he himself has just had to defend a changed travel plan that looked from the outside like an unreliable Yes-and-No. What does it mean to live as someone whose ordinary plans must yield to an apostolic obedience that may, from the world's standpoint, look exactly like the fickleness Paul is being accused of—and how would a community whose central claim is that God always says Yes in Christ recognize the difference between holy plan-revision and ordinary unreliability?

## The Thread

- **1:3 ("Blessed be the God and Father…") → Eph 1:3 / 1 Pet 1:3 / Jewish *berakhah***: All three NT *berakhah* openings borrow the synagogue *Barukh atah Adonai* formula. Paul prays as a Jew throughout. Reading 1:3 without the liturgical background flattens it into general gratitude rather than the inheritance of Jewish prayer-grammar that it actually is.
- **1:5 ("we share abundantly in Christ's sufferings") → Col 1:24 / Phil 3:10 / 2 Cor 4:10**: The same theological instinct—apostolic suffering as participation in the cruciform shape of Christ's life—threads through Paul. The strong reading at Col 1:24 ("filling up what is lacking in Christ's afflictions") clarifies what is and is not at stake: not the sufficiency of the atonement but the extension of cruciform pattern through the Body.
- **1:8-9 ("we despaired of life itself… God who raises the dead") → Romans 4:17-25**: The "God who raises the dead" formula appears in Paul's account of Abraham's faith. 1:8-9 makes the apostolic experience an enacted version of the patriarchal pattern—God's identity as resurrection-God is what makes faith in extremis possible.
- **1:20 ("all the promises of God find their Yes in him") → Acts 13:32-33 / Rom 9:4-5 / Gal 3:16**: The fulfillment-of-promise axis runs through the whole Pauline-Lukan kerygma. 1:20 is the most concentrated single statement—the covenantal promises (Abrahamic, Davidic, prophetic) are gathered into the Christ-event as their ratifying Yes. The Jewish-Christian seam at this verse is the seam at which all Pauline theology either holds or splits.
- **1:22 (*arrabōn* of the Spirit) → 2 Cor 5:5 / Eph 1:14 / Rom 8:23**: The Spirit-as-down-payment thread runs through Paul, with Rom 8:23's "firstfruits of the Spirit" supplying a parallel Greek metaphor (*aparchē*) for the same eschatological reality—what is given now is the binding pledge of what is still to come.
