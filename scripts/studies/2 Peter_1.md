# 2 Peter 1: The Last Will of an Apostle

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

No book in the New Testament is more contested on the question of authorship than 2 Peter, and any honest reading must begin there. The letter opens with "Simeon Peter, a servant and apostle of Jesus Christ" (1:1) — a self-presentation that, if taken at face value, places the document in the hand of the chief apostle in the mid-60s, on the eve of his Neronian martyrdom. But beginning with Origen, who already in the third century knew of doubts (preserved in Eusebius, *Historia Ecclesiastica* 6.25.8), and Eusebius himself, who classifies 2 Peter among the *antilegomena* — the "spoken-against" writings whose canonical status was still being debated (*Hist. Eccl.* 3.25) — the book has lived under suspicion. Full canonical acceptance came late, only in the fourth century, after Athanasius's 39th Festal Letter (367) and the Council of Carthage (397).

Modern critical scholarship has sharpened the question. Most modern critical scholars, including the evangelically committed Richard Bauckham in his magisterial *Word Biblical Commentary* on Jude and 2 Peter (1983), read the letter as a *pseudonymous testament* — an instance of the well-attested Jewish genre of farewell discourse in which a revered figure, on the verge of death, leaves final instructions to those who follow. The arguments for pseudonymity are cumulative: the Greek vocabulary and style differ sharply from 1 Peter (which was itself written, according to 1 Pet 5:12, with the help of Silvanus); 2 Peter 2 borrows extensively, often verbatim, from Jude 4-19; the reference to Paul's letters as "scriptures" alongside "the other scriptures" in 3:15-16 reflects a moment when the Pauline corpus had begun to circulate as something like inspired writ — a development most scholars locate after Peter's death; and the entire posture toward parousia delay (3:3-9) addresses a question that grew acute only as the apostolic generation died off. Anton Vögtle, Jerome Neyrey, Ernst Käsemann, and Bultmann all argued for a date somewhere between the 80s and the 130s, with composition by an admirer writing in the Petrine tradition.

Evangelical defenders of Petrine authenticity — Michael Green, Tom Schreiner (whose *New American Commentary* on 1-2 Peter and Jude is the most thorough recent defense), Doug Moo, and the older work of Charles Bigg — answer point by point. The vocabulary differences between 1 and 2 Peter are explicable on the assumption of a different amanuensis, since 1 Peter itself acknowledges Silvanus's hand. The reference to Paul's letters need not imply a closed canon; it could mean simply that Paul's writings already enjoyed authoritative status in some Christian communities. The testament genre is a perfectly natural form for a man who knows his death is near (1:14), and farewell discourse in Scripture has authentic precedents (Moses in Deut 31-33, Joshua 23-24, Paul in Acts 20). On this view the letter dates to c. AD 64-67. A mediating position, advanced by Ben Witherington and others, allows that a Petrine voice may have been authorized through pseudonymous testamentary form — a literary convention acceptable in Jewish-Christian milieux where the genre conventions were known.

The first chapter is where the testamentary character is clearest. "I think it right, as long as I am in this body, to stir you up by way of reminder, since I know that the putting off of my body will be soon, as our Lord Jesus Christ made clear to me. And I will make every effort so that after my departure you may be able at any time to recall these things" (1:13-15). The vocabulary is unmistakably valedictory: the body as a tent (*skēnōma*) to be put off, "departure" (*exodos* — the same word Luke uses for Jesus's death at the Transfiguration, Luke 9:31), the apostolic concern that the deposit of teaching survive the apostle's voice. The reference to the Lord's prediction of Peter's death looks back to John 21:18-19. Whether Peter wrote this himself in his last months or a disciple wrote in his name, the genre is testament: a man giving his last words.

The Transfiguration recollection in 1:16-18 is the most famous Petrine appeal to historical witness in the New Testament. "We did not follow cleverly devised myths" — *sesophismenois mythois* — "when we made known to you the power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, but we were eyewitnesses of his majesty." The word for eyewitnesses, *epoptai* (ἐπόπται), is suggestive: in Greco-Roman religion it was the term for initiates admitted to the highest grade of the mystery cults, those who had "seen" the sacred objects. Here it is wrenched from the mystery vocabulary and applied to historical witness on a particular mountain. Three Synoptic accounts (Mark 9:2-8, Matt 17:1-9, Luke 9:28-36) describe the event the writer recalls; the voice from the cloud — "This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased" — is a near-quotation of the Markan and Matthean voice, with minor differences in word order that have generated their own text-critical literature.

Perhaps the single most theologically loaded phrase in the chapter is *theia physis* (θεία φύσις) in 1:4 — "partakers of the divine nature." The phrase is Hellenistic philosophical language, not Septuagintal, and it is virtually unique in the New Testament. *Aretē* (ἀρετή, "virtue, excellence") in 1:3 and 1:5 is similarly Stoic in flavor; this is one of the few NT passages to use the word in its Hellenistic philosophical sense. *Epignōsis* (ἐπίγνωσις, 1:2-3, 8) is a Petrine favorite, denoting full or relational knowledge as opposed to bare *gnōsis*. *Prophētikon logon* (προφητικὸν λόγον, 1:19) names the prophetic word as a stable deposit. Taken together, the chapter's vocabulary indicates a writer fluent in Greco-Roman moral and religious idiom, ready to deploy it in the service of Christian ethical and metaphysical claims.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The whole letter is structured as a *testament* — the ancient Jewish genre exemplified in the *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs*, the *Testament of Moses*, the *Testament of Job*, and within the canonical Scriptures by the closing speeches of Moses (Deut 31-33), Joshua (Joshua 23-24), David (1 Kings 2), and Paul (Acts 20). The conventional features include an announcement of imminent death; a recall of the speaker's ministry and authority; warnings about future false teachers; predictions of crisis; ethical exhortation; and a vision of the future age. 2 Peter has every one of these features, distributed across its three chapters. Chapter 1 supplies the announcement of death (1:13-15), the recall of ministry (1:16-18 Transfiguration), and the foundational ethical exhortation (1:5-11). Chapter 2 supplies the prediction of false teachers, with the typological catalog. Chapter 3 supplies the apocalyptic vision. The unity of design across the letter is one of the strongest arguments — used by both sides — for its careful literary composition.

The Greek of 2 Peter is more elevated and self-consciously rhetorical than the Greek of 1 Peter — a fact that cuts both ways in the authorship debate. Within chapter 1, two literary structures stand out. The first is the so-called *sorites* or chain-of-virtues (1:5-7): faith → virtue → knowledge → self-control → steadfastness → godliness → brotherly affection → love. The form is Stoic, the *catena*-of-virtues is well attested in Hellenistic moral literature (compare the lists in Philo, Seneca, and the Stoic handbooks), but the Christian termini are unmistakable: *pistis* on one end, *agapē* on the other. The chain ends not at *eudaimonia* or apatheia but at love — the distinctively Christian crowning virtue. The second is the rhetorical *inclusio* of 1:3-4 with 1:11: "his divine power has granted to us all things that pertain to life and godliness… so that you may become partakers of the divine nature" answers to "there will be richly provided for you an entrance into the eternal kingdom" — the divine self-giving at the start finds its consummation in the divine kingdom at the end.

The testamentary terminology in 1:15 — *meta tēn emēn exodon* ("after my departure") — picks up the same word Luke 9:31 uses of Jesus at the Transfiguration; the writer of 2 Peter is signaling that his own death stands in some imitative relation to his Lord's. The cluster of mystery-cult vocabulary (*epoptai*, "majesty") followed by the prophetic-word vocabulary (1:19-21) sets up the chapter's central literary contrast: the apostolic witness rests on something seen and heard, *and* on a "prophetic word more fully confirmed" — the seen and the written together form the deposit being handed on.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The patristic reception of 2 Peter is a peculiar story. As noted, the book's canonical reception was the slowest of any New Testament writing. Origen, the great Alexandrian, knew of doubts but wrote on the letter; Eusebius classed it among the *antilegomena*; Jerome included it in the Vulgate but acknowledged the controversy. Once accepted, however, certain passages of chapter 1 became theologically load-bearing. The Transfiguration testimony of 1:16-18 served as a key Christological warrant alongside the Synoptic accounts in the Chalcedonian and pre-Chalcedonian debates over the identity of Jesus.

The phrase *theia physis* in 1:4 became the locus classicus for the doctrine of *theōsis* — deification — in the Greek-speaking East. Athanasius's famous formula in *De Incarnatione* 54 — "He became man that we might become god" — draws on this verse and on the broader logic of participation. The Cappadocians (Gregory of Nyssa, Basil) developed it. Gregory Palamas in the fourteenth century built the entire essence-energies distinction on the trajectory: we cannot participate in the divine essence, but we genuinely partake of the divine energies, and 2 Pet 1:4 is the key text. Vladimir Lossky's *Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church* and the writings of John Meyendorff and John Zizioulas restored this doctrine to broader theological awareness in the twentieth century, and even Catholic engagement (Norris Clarke, Gilles Emery on Aquinas's metaphysics of participation) has rehabilitated something like a participatory ontology in dialogue with Orthodoxy. The Catechism of the Catholic Church §460 cites 2 Peter 1:4 explicitly: "the Word became flesh to make us 'partakers of the divine nature.'"

The Reformation read 1:4 with caution. Luther, who was famously skeptical of 2 Peter (in the 1522 NT preface he placed it, along with Hebrews, James, Jude, and Revelation, in his sub-canonical group), worried about the Catholic deification reading. Calvin defended the letter cautiously and treated *theia physis* as a relational, covenantal "partaking" rather than ontological participation. Reformed orthodoxy fully accepted the canon but kept the participation language in narrow channels. Wesley and the Methodist tradition, in contrast, grasped 1:4 as warrant for Christian perfection — sanctification reaches a real participation in the divine life, even if not divinization in the strict Orthodox sense.

The critical era turned the question on its head. Käsemann's 1952 essay "Eine Apologie der urchristlichen Eschatologie" famously called 2 Peter "the worst writing in the New Testament" — a polemical claim aimed at what Käsemann saw as a retreat into "early Catholic" apologetic for parousia delay. Bauckham's 1983 *WBC* commentary is the major rehabilitation: 2 Peter as a sophisticated, historically located testament with its own theological integrity, not a fall from the apocalyptic purity Käsemann romanticized. Vögtle and Neyrey developed the testament-genre reading; Tommy Wasserman and Peter Davids have done major work on the textual evidence.

### Jewish Scholarly Engagement

On the testament genre itself, the deepest comparands are *Jewish* — the *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs* especially, but also the *Testament of Moses* and *Testament of Job*. George Nickelsburg's work on Jewish literature between the Bibles, James VanderKam's studies of pseudepigrapha, and Annette Yoshiko Reed's writing on shared apocalyptic-testamentary forms help locate 2 Peter within the literature its first audience would have recognized. Daniel Boyarin's broader argument about the inseparability of early Jewish and Christian apocalyptic forms applies here: the genre is not Christian or Jewish but Jewish-and-Christian, shared.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What the writer is doing in chapter 1 is surprisingly compressed. Across twenty-one verses he sets out the gift (divine power, divine promises, divine nature, 1:3-4), the response (the chain of virtues, 1:5-11), the testamentary occasion (1:12-15), and the foundation of the deposit (apostolic eyewitness plus prophetic word, 1:16-21). The God of 2 Peter 1 is the God who has *already* given everything needed for life and godliness — past tense, completed action, *dedōrētai* (1:3) — and whose ongoing work is to bring the believer through to the eternal kingdom. Two theological tensions warrant unpacking.

### Tension 1: *Theia physis* — what does it mean to "partake of the divine nature"?

The Eastern Orthodox tradition reads 1:4 ontologically and participatorily but draws the essence-energies distinction to avoid pantheism: we genuinely become "by grace what God is by nature," but only in his energies, not his unknowable essence. Lossky, Meyendorff, Zizioulas, and behind them Palamas and the Cappadocians, ground this entire spirituality in this single verse.

The Catholic tradition follows Aquinas (*ST* I-II.112-114) on the metaphysics of grace: sanctifying grace (*gratia creata*) is a real created participation in the divine life, an "accidental" form raising the soul to a connaturality with God — not a transformation of the divine essence into us or vice versa, but a real ontological elevation. CCC §460 makes this the defining frame for Christian salvation.

The Reformed tradition, alarmed at any reading that blurs the Creator-creature distinction, has typically taken "partakers" in a relational and covenantal sense. We share in what God *gives* — his promises, his life, his Spirit — without ourselves participating in any divine substance. Calvin reads it through union with Christ; the partaking is mediated through the person of the Mediator and remains thoroughly personal rather than ontological.

The Wesleyan/Methodist reading meets in the middle: there is a genuine transformation — Christian perfection, the renovation of the heart — that goes beyond mere imputation but does not reach to ontological deification. Wesley's *Notes Upon the New Testament* on this verse describe a real participation in God's holiness through love.

The least intuitive position to a Reformed or evangelical ear — Orthodox *theōsis* — is in fact the position with the deepest patristic roots and the most direct connection to the actual vocabulary of 1:4. Steelmanned: *theia physis* is a phrase from Hellenistic philosophical theology in which the divine nature is something one might genuinely come to share in. The writer uses it precisely because his Greek-speaking audience would hear the philosophical resonance. He does not say we become God; he says we become *partakers* — *koinōnoi* — and the essence-energies distinction is the most careful guard against the misreading that would swallow our creatureliness.

**Where this leaves us**: the verse will not let any tradition collapse it. Read it too thinly and you get something less than the text actually says. Read it too thickly and you efface the Creator-creature distinction Scripture elsewhere protects. The phrase *partakers* is the hinge — and partakers is genuinely participatory but not annihilating.

### Tension 2: Prophecy and "no prophecy of Scripture comes from someone's own interpretation" (1:20-21)

The Catholic magisterial reading is that the verse warrants a teaching authority above the individual reader: Scripture cannot be privately interpreted at will, and the Magisterium is the authentic interpreter (*Dei Verbum* §10, CCC §85). The plurality of contradictory Protestant readings is, on this view, exactly what 1:20 was warning against.

The Protestant *sola scriptura* reading takes the verse to mean that the *origin* of prophecy is not in private human imagination — "no prophecy was ever produced by the will of man, but men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit" (1:21). The verse is about prophetic *production*, not interpretation. Reading the verse with the next, the natural sense is that prophecy comes *from* God, not that the church alone reads it.

The Pentecostal and charismatic reading takes 1:21 as warrant for ongoing prophetic gift, since the Spirit who carried along the prophets remains active. The chapter validates ongoing prophecy as long as it follows the same Spirit-borne pattern.

The historical-critical reading agrees with the Protestant grammatical move: *epilyseōs* (ἐπιλύσεως, "interpretation, unloosing") in 1:20 most naturally describes the prophet's own act of producing the prophecy from himself, not the reader's later interpretation. The verse is about origin, full stop.

The least intuitive — but most exegetically restrained — reading is the historical-grammatical one: the verse is not, despite its later use, an answer to the Reformation question of who interprets Scripture. It is an answer to a more primitive question: where does prophecy come from? The answer is: from God, not from the prophet's own initiative. Steelmanned, this reading exposes how often theological tradition reads later debates back into earlier texts.

**Where this leaves us**: 1:20-21 cannot bear the weight of either a Roman Catholic doctrine of magisterial interpretation or a Protestant doctrine of perspicuity, even if both traditions have leaned on it. The verse is more modest and more important than either reading: it locates the authority of prophecy in the prior speaking of God.

### Closing question

If 2 Peter is genuinely a testament — whether dictated by Peter on the eve of his death or composed in his name by a faithful disciple — what is the theological status of the genre itself? Does the truth of the deposit depend on the historical particularity of the speaker, or does it rest on the authority of the deposit *as such*? This is the question 2 Peter forces, and Christianity has not entirely settled.

## The Thread

- Mark 9:2-8 / Matt 17:1-9 / Luke 9:28-36 — the Transfiguration accounts the apostolic testimony recalls (1:16-18); Luke uniquely names the event the disciples witnessed Jesus's *exodos*, the same word 2 Peter 1:15 uses of his own departure.
- Athanasius, *De Incarnatione* 54 — "He became man that we might become god"; the canonical *theōsis* formula reading 1:4.
- 1 John 3:2 — "we shall be like him"; the parallel New Testament logic of Christian transformation.
- Numbers 24:17 — "a star shall come forth out of Jacob"; the source of the "morning star" image in 1:19, and an ironic foreshadow given that the Balaam prophecy comes from the very figure 2 Peter 2:15 will hold up as the archetype of false prophecy for hire.
