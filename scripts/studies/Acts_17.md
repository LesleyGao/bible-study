# Acts 17: Thessalonica, Berea, and the Areopagus

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Acts 17 traces a route that any first-century traveler in Macedonia would recognize: the **Via Egnatia**, the great east-west Roman highway that connected the Adriatic to Byzantium. Amphipolis and Apollonia (17:1) were stations on it, roughly a day's march apart. **Thessalonica**, capital of the senatorial province of Macedonia after 146 BCE, was the largest city Paul had yet entered—a free city (*civitas libera*) governed by ***politarchai*** (πολιτάρχαι, the "city authorities" of 17:6, 8). The term was once thought to be a Lukan invention; it has now been documented on more than 30 inscriptions from Thessalonica and the surrounding region (the Vardar Gate inscription, second century CE, is the most famous), and the find was already cataloged in the early epigraphic surveys (E.D.B. Burton's 1898 article remains a standard reference). The discovery is a small but representative case of Lukan precision: where the language could have been generic (*archontes*), Luke uses the technically correct local term.

**Berea** (modern Veria), about 50 miles southwest, was off the Egnatia and quieter; its synagogue is mentioned but no archaeology has firmly identified its location, though a synagogue inscription from a later period attests continuity of Jewish presence.

**Athens** dominates the chapter. Paul's wait there places him in the most thickly intellectual city of the empire. The chapter's three named locations are real and identifiable: the **synagogue** (location uncertain; Athens had a Jewish community, attested in a Hellenistic-era proseuche inscription); the ***agora*** (the Athenian Agora, excavated since 1931 by the American School of Classical Studies, with the Stoa of Attalos as its principal preserved monument); and the ***Areios Pagos*** (Ἄρειος Πάγος, "Mars Hill," 17:19). The Areopagus is both a physical place—the rocky outcrop northwest of the Acropolis—and an institution, the Athenian council that, by the Roman period, had jurisdiction over religious and educational matters, including the licensing of new teachers. Whether Paul stood on the rock itself or before the council in its meeting place (possibly the Stoa Basileios in the Agora) is debated. **Bertil Gärtner's** *The Areopagus Speech and Natural Revelation* (1955) and **Bruce Winter's** work on Athenian religious adjudication argue for the council interpretation; the rock's traditional identification, while ancient, may be more pious than evidentiary.

The chapter's archaeology of religion is the heart of its texture. The **altar "to an unknown god"** (*agnōstō theō*, ἀγνώστῳ θεῷ, 17:23) is attested by **Pausanias** (*Description of Greece* 1.1.4)—"and altars of gods called unknown" near the Phaleric port—and by **Philostratus** (*Vita Apollonii* 6.3), who refers to altars "even to unknown gods" at Athens. Diogenes Laertius (1.110) tells of Epimenides of Crete advising the Athenians during a plague to release sheep on the Areopagus and sacrifice each to whatever god the sheep lay down nearest, with anonymous altars erected for the unknown ones. The plural "unknown gods" of the inscriptions and the singular of Acts 17:23 do not quite line up; either Paul (or Luke) elides the plural for rhetorical compression, or there were both kinds of altars in Athens. No altar with this exact inscription has yet been recovered, but the cultural practice is securely attested.

The chapter quotes two Greek poets. "**In him we live and move and have our being**" (17:28a) is most plausibly from **Epimenides** of Crete (sixth century BCE), specifically his lost *Cretica*; a Syriac fragment preserved by the ninth-century Bishop Isho'dad of Merv attributes the line to Epimenides. "**For we are indeed his offspring**" (17:28b) is the opening of **Aratus's** *Phaenomena* (line 5; third century BCE), an astronomical poem whose proem invokes Zeus as the god "in whom we are all his offspring." Aratus was Cilician—Paul's near-countryman—which gives the citation a quiet local resonance.

The two philosophical schools named at 17:18 are the **Epicureans** and the **Stoics**, the dominant intellectual movements in first-century Athens. **Epicureans** (school founded by Epicurus c. 307 BCE in the Garden) held that the gods exist but are uninvolved with human affairs, that the soul dies with the body, that pleasure (rightly understood) is the good. **Stoics** (founded by Zeno c. 300 BCE in the Stoa Poikile) held a pantheistic-monistic theology in which divine *logos* permeates the cosmos, virtue is the only good, and the wise person lives "according to nature." **A.A. Long's** *Hellenistic Philosophy* and **Cicero's** *De Natura Deorum* are the standard guides; Diogenes Laertius (Books 7 and 10) preserves the school's primary sources. Paul's speech engages both schools—his transcendent creator God refuses Epicurean detachment, and his judgment-and-resurrection refuses Stoic cyclical materialism—but it borrows the Stoic poetic vocabulary to make contact.

The Greek term ***to theion*** (τὸ θεῖον, "the divine," 17:29) is itself a Hellenistic philosophical idiom, characteristic of Stoic and middle-Platonic religious discourse. Its use signals that Paul is meeting his audience in their own register; he does not say *ho theos* ("the god") at this point but the more abstract neuter "the divine."

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Acts 17 is the most rhetorically self-conscious chapter in Acts. The Areopagus speech (17:22-31) is one of three major Pauline speeches in the book, paralleled by the synagogue address at Antioch of Pisidia (Acts 13) and the farewell at Miletus (Acts 20). Each is rhetorically attuned to its audience. Where Antioch addresses Diaspora Jews and God-fearers in scriptural-historical mode, Athens addresses Greco-Roman pagans in philosophical mode, and Miletus addresses church elders in pastoral-prophetic mode. The trio shows Luke's awareness that gospel proclamation is rhetorically variable.

The Areopagus speech is structured as a classical *deliberative oration* in miniature: *exordium* (17:22-23, captatio benevolentiae through the unknown-god altar), *propositio* (17:24-25, the creator God), *narratio* (17:26-27, providential history), *probatio* (17:28-29, citation of the poets), and *peroratio* (17:30-31, the call to repentance and the announcement of judgment through the resurrection). **George Kennedy's** *New Testament Interpretation Through Rhetorical Criticism* (1984) reads the speech as a model of accommodation; **Loveday Alexander** has shown that its philosophical citations are rhetorically deployed rather than genuinely doxographic—Paul does not endorse Stoic theology, he uses Stoic poetry to point toward something else.

The speech has been a battleground in Acts scholarship over the **historicity of the Lukan speeches** since at least Martin Dibelius's 1939 essay "Paul on the Areopagus." Dibelius read the speech as a Lukan composition reflecting Hellenistic-Jewish missionary convention, with little or no genuine Pauline content. **Bertil Gärtner** answered Dibelius point by point, defending the speech's continuity with Pauline thought (e.g., Romans 1:18-32). The discussion has continued through **Conzelmann**, **Haenchen**, **Pervo** (skeptical) and **Bruce**, **Hemer**, **Witherington**, **Keener** (defending a substantial Pauline core). The middle position, well represented by Marguerat: Luke composes the speech in his own voice, but the rhetorical strategy is consistent with how Paul actually engaged Gentile audiences (cf. 1 Thessalonians 1:9-10's "you turned to God from idols").

The chapter also continues Luke's deployment of **Western Text variants**. Codex Bezae expands 17:15 ("but he passed by Thessaly, for he was prevented from preaching the word to them") and 17:34 names "an Athenian woman of high standing" instead of Damaris, perhaps reflecting unease about a named woman convert. As elsewhere, the variants smooth or domesticate.

The Berean detail at 17:11—"examining the Scriptures daily to see if these things were so"—has become programmatic for a particular Protestant ecclesial sensibility. American evangelicalism has named churches, schools, and Bible-study programs after the Bereans, and the verse functions as a rallying cry for a lay-driven, scripture-checking piety. The Lukan compliment—"more noble" (*eugenesteroi*)—is rhetorically charged: Luke contrasts the Berean Jews favorably with the Thessalonican ones, but the compliment cuts both ways. As Tannehill observes, the Bereans' nobility consists precisely in their willingness to test apostolic preaching against Scripture; this is not docile reception but active discernment.

The political charge against the missionaries at 17:6-7—"these men who have turned the world upside down... saying that there is another king, Jesus"—is the most explicitly imperial-counter-claim language in Acts. Luke writes the riot scene with deliberate political shock. **N.T. Wright's** *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* (2013) reads the charge as rhetorically representative of how the gospel actually sounded to Roman ears: not as a private religious option but as a competing kingdom. The verb *anastatōsantes* (translated "turned the world upside down") is strong—it means to overturn or stir up sedition, and it appears in similar political contexts in Hellenistic literature.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Areopagus speech is one of the most-discussed passages in the entire New Testament, and its interpretive history runs from the Patristic period through 20th-century systematic theology to contemporary debates over religious pluralism.

**Patristic readings** generally treated the speech as a model for engaging pagan culture. **Justin Martyr's** *Apology* and *Dialogue with Trypho* developed the *logos spermatikos* doctrine—the idea that fragments of divine truth were scattered among the philosophers—citing Acts 17 as warrant. **Clement of Alexandria's** *Stromata* extended this generously, claiming that philosophy was "a schoolmaster" preparing the Greeks for Christ. **Chrysostom's** 38th homily on Acts is more cautious: he praises Paul's accommodation but underscores that the Athenians ultimately mocked or deferred—the speech is a model of method without a model of triumphant outcome. **Augustine's** *City of God* engages Platonism extensively but distinguishes carefully between the philosophers' partial truths and the gospel's revelation.

The **Reformation** sharpened the question. **Calvin's** commentary reads 17:27-28 as evidence that all humans have a *sensus divinitatis*—an innate awareness of God—but insists that this awareness is wholly insufficient apart from special revelation; the Athenians' unknown-god altar is proof of their ignorance, not their proximity to God. **Luther** is similarly cautious about natural theology, though he accepts that the heathen know God in a "general" sense.

The chapter became the focal text in the most consequential 20th-century **natural theology debate**. **Emil Brunner's** *Nature and Grace* (1934) argued that Acts 17 demonstrates a *Anknüpfungspunkt*—a "point of contact" between gospel and human reason rooted in the *imago Dei*. **Karl Barth's** response, the famous ***Nein!*** (1934), rejected this with characteristic violence: there is no such point of contact, no general revelation that enables natural theology, no way from human reason to God; Paul's speech in Acts 17 is not the foundation of natural theology but its disconfirmation, since the Athenians scoff at the resurrection. Barth treated the text in *Church Dogmatics* II/1 §26 at length, arguing that Paul cites the poets only to indict Athenian religion, not to commend it. The Brunner-Barth debate divided 20th-century theology and continues to shape Reformed and Lutheran systematics.

**Catholic theology** (Vatican I's *Dei Filius*, 1870) affirmed natural theology dogmatically: "God, the source and end of all things, can be known with certainty from the consideration of created things, by the natural power of human reason." Vatican II's *Lumen Gentium* 16 extended this in a pastoral key, affirming that those who seek God with a sincere heart can find salvation through paths known to God. **Karl Rahner's** "anonymous Christian" thesis builds on this trajectory, with explicit appeal to Acts 17:27.

**C.S. Lewis** in *Mere Christianity* echoes a generous natural theology consistent with the Anglican-Thomistic stream: humans share a common moral law, point toward but cannot reach the gospel, and require the specific revelation of Christ. **Bertil Gärtner's** *The Areopagus Speech and Natural Revelation* remains the most careful defense of a moderate natural-theology reading: Paul's speech presupposes general revelation but does not rest content with it, since it culminates in the call to repentance and the announcement of resurrection-judgment.

**Jewish interpretive trajectories** have been less prolific on Acts 17, but **Daniel Boyarin** and **Pamela Eisenbaum** have read the speech within its broader Hellenistic-Jewish missionary tradition. The Wisdom of Solomon (esp. ch. 13) anticipates Paul's argument: Gentiles fail to deduce the creator from creation, and their idolatry is condemnable but understandable. **Paula Fredriksen** has emphasized that Paul's mission to Athens is *Gentile* mission—the speech is for non-Jews, and its rhetorical strategy fits Paul's vocation as apostle to the nations rather than as a proto-Christian polemicist against Judaism.

**Contemporary commentary** on the speech is rich. **Bruce's** NICNT defends the speech's substantial Pauline character; **Barrett's** ICC analyzes its rhetorical structure with surgical precision; **Fitzmyer**, **Gaventa**, **Johnson**, **Keener**, **Witherington**, **Schnabel**, **Pervo**, **Peterson**, **Marguerat** all give it sustained treatment. **Tannehill's** narrative reading places the speech within Acts's overall theological program of Gentile inclusion. **Justo González's** liberation reading critiques the speech's ambiguous outcome (some mocked, some deferred, only a few believed) as a warning against assuming that gospel accommodation produces gospel reception.

**Dionysius the Areopagite** (17:34) became the focus of a remarkable later trajectory: a fifth- or sixth-century corpus of mystical theological writings was attributed to him (the **Pseudo-Dionysian corpus**: *The Divine Names*, *Mystical Theology*, *Celestial Hierarchy*, *Ecclesiastical Hierarchy*). **Andrew Louth's** *Denys the Areopagite* (1989) is the standard scholarly study. Through the Latin translations of John Scotus Eriugena (ninth century) and the engagement of Aquinas and the medieval mystics, Pseudo-Dionysius became one of the most influential mystical theologians in Christian history—an extraordinary afterlife for a single named convert in Luke's narrative.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: natural theology and special revelation.** Does Paul's Areopagus speech ground a positive natural theology, or does it expose the inadequacy of natural religion? **Three positions**: (1) **Catholic-Thomistic** (Vatican I, Rahner, Aquinas): the speech assumes natural reason can know that God exists, and the Athenian altar is evidence of this universal human awareness, however dimmed. Special revelation completes but does not contradict the natural light. (2) **Barthian**: there is no natural theology; the speech cites the poets to indict, not to commend, and the Athenians' final scoff at the resurrection demonstrates that human reason cannot reach the gospel from below. (3) **Mediating Reformed** (Bavinck, Berkouwer, Plantinga): general revelation is real but suppressed (Romans 1:18-21); the speech presupposes a *sensus divinitatis* but immediately moves to special revelation as alone sufficient for saving knowledge. The steelman of all three: (1) the speech does cite Greek poets favorably and does treat the unknown-god altar as a connecting point; (2) the speech's rhetorical climax is the resurrection, and Athens largely rejects it; (3) Paul's premise (the creator God is not far from each one) and his conclusion (you must repent in light of the resurrection) hold together only if general revelation is real but insufficient. **Where this leaves us**: the speech refuses both pure natural theology (the gospel is not deducible from creation alone) and pure rejection of natural theology (the gospel addresses an audience that already has *some* awareness of God). The category that does justice to the text is preparatory revelation that points beyond itself.

**Tension 2: the political theology of "another king, Jesus" (17:7).** **Three positions**: (1) **Two Kingdoms** (Lutheran-Reformed): Christ's kingship is spiritual and does not directly contest Caesar's temporal authority; the riot in Thessalonica reflects misunderstanding, not a structural conflict. (2) **Imperial counter-claim** (N.T. Wright, John Howard Yoder, Richard Horsley): the gospel does claim Jesus as *kyrios* against Caesar, and the Thessalonican charge is substantively correct, even if Paul's response is non-violent. (3) **Apocalyptic** (Käsemann, J. Louis Martyn): the kingship of Jesus is the inbreaking of God's apocalyptic rule that relativizes all human polities, including Roman; the conflict is real but is not adequately described as a competition between two kingdoms of the same kind. The steelman: the charge is real (Jesus is *kyrios*), the conflict is real (Christians cannot worship Caesar), but the kingdom Jesus brings is not interchangeable with Caesar's—it is of a different order. **Where this leaves us**: the gospel is politically incandescent in a way the modern privatized religion has often forgotten; but the politics of the gospel is non-violent, non-revolutionary in the conventional sense, and oriented toward the resurrection-judgment that is its true vindication.

**Unresolved question**: what does it mean that Athens, the city most prepared philosophically to hear the gospel, largely rejected it (17:32)? The speech has been treated by some interpreters as a strategic failure—Paul retreats to a simpler proclamation at Corinth (1 Cor 2:1-5: "I decided to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ and him crucified"). Others read 17:32-34 as a typical missionary outcome: some mock, some defer, some believe. The chapter does not resolve whether sophisticated cultural engagement is gospel strategy or gospel danger.

## The Thread

The Areopagus speech's "in him we live and move and have our being" (17:28) anticipates **Colossians 1:17** ("in him all things hold together") and **Romans 11:36** ("from him and through him and to him are all things"). The "times of ignorance God overlooked" (17:30) belongs to the same theological vocabulary as **Romans 3:25** ("in his divine forbearance he had passed over former sins"). The judgment-by-the-appointed-man (17:31) anticipates **Romans 2:16** and **2 Timothy 4:1**—the resurrection is the divine warrant that the appointed judge has been confirmed. The Berean Scripture-checking commendation (17:11) prepares the canonical pattern by which apostolic preaching is validated against prior revelation, a hermeneutical posture that runs through 1 Corinthians 15:3-4, 2 Timothy 3:16, and ultimately the church's reception of the New Testament canon itself.
