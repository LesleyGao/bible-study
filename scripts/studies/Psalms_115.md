# Psalm 115 — A Scholarly Study

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 115 is anonymous and stands without superscription in the Masoretic Text. It belongs to Book V (Pss 107–150) and to **the Egyptian Hallel** (*Hallel Mitzri*, Pss 113–118). In the rabbinic Passover Seder, codified in Mishnah Pesahim 10 and elaborated in b. Pesahim 117–118, **Psalm 115 opens the post-meal portion of the Hallel** — sung over the third and fourth cups of wine, after the *birkat ha-mazon* (grace after meals). Pss 113–114 frame the meal's beginning; Pss 115–118 frame its end. When the Synoptic Gospels report that Jesus and the disciples "sang the hymn" before going to the Mount of Olives (*hymnēsantes exēlthon*, Mark 14:26 / Matt 26:30), the most natural referent is precisely this post-meal Hallel: Pss 115, 116, 117, 118. Jesus likely sang Psalm 115 — with its anti-idol polemic, its trust-refrain, and its closing affirmation that "we will bless the LORD from this time forth and forevermore" — within hours of his arrest.

The **textual situation is famously complex**. The LXX, followed by the Vulgate, treats Hebrew Pss 114 and 115 as a single composition, numbered LXX/Vulgate Ps 113. Then Hebrew Pss 116:1–9 and 116:10–19 are split into LXX Pss 114 and 115. The LXX-Vulgate-Hebrew numbering divergence runs from Pss 9–10 (joined in LXX) through to Pss 147 (split in LXX). Pre-1969 Catholic Bibles followed the Vulgate numbering (Douay-Rheims, the older Roman Breviary); post-Nova Vulgata and modern Catholic editions (the Liturgia Horarum, the New American Bible, the Jerusalem Bible) revert to the Hebrew numbering, often retaining the Vulgate numbering in parentheses. For our purposes, "Psalm 115" denotes the Masoretic Hebrew composition vv. 1–18.

In Hermann Gunkel's form-critical taxonomy, Ps 115 is a **communal liturgy of trust** with hymnic and didactic elements. It blends the genres of communal lament (the apologetic question "where is their God?" in v. 2 echoes Pss 42:3, 79:10), anti-idol polemic (vv. 4–8, in the *Götzenpolemik* tradition), trust-refrain (vv. 9–11), priestly blessing (vv. 12–15), and hymnic affirmation (vv. 16–18). Sigmund Mowinckel located it in temple liturgy with antiphonal performance — a precentor/cantor leading and a congregation responding. Erhard Gerstenberger (*Psalms 2*, FOTL, 2001) reads the trust-refrain (vv. 9–11) as plainly responsorial: the cantor calls Israel, then the priests, then the proselytes, and each group responds with the refrain *betach ba-Yahweh* ("trust in the LORD").

The psalm is dated to the post-exilic period (sixth to fourth centuries BCE), partly on the basis of (a) its sustained anti-idol polemic, which becomes a major literary form in Second Temple Judaism (cf. Ps 135:15–18; Isa 44:9–20; Jer 10:1–16; Wisdom of Solomon 13–15; Bel and the Dragon), and (b) the post-exilic concern with diasporic identity reflected in v. 2's mocking question from the nations. The redactional placement in the Hallel cluster confirms a Second Temple liturgical setting.

The opening line, *lo lanu Yahweh lo lanu ki le-shimcha ten kavod* (לֹא לָנוּ יְהוָה לֹא לָנוּ כִּי־לְשִׁמְךָ תֵּן כָּבוֹד, "not to us, O LORD, not to us, but to your name give glory," 115:1), is one of the most famous lines in the Psalter. Its rhetorical doubling — *lo lanu lo lanu* — has become a touchstone of Reformed theology (Calvin made it a motto; the Knights Templar adopted it in Latin, *Non nobis Domine non nobis sed nomini tuo da gloriam*, as their official motto).

The central anti-idol section (vv. 4–8), *atzabbeihem kesef ve-zahav ma'aseh ydei adam* (עֲצַבֵּיהֶם כֶּסֶף וְזָהָב מַעֲשֵׂה יְדֵי אָדָם, "their idols are silver and gold, the work of human hands," 115:4), uses the noun *atzab* (עָצָב) — literally "image, fashioned thing," from the root *atz-v* meaning "to shape, fashion, form" but with an unmistakable wordplay on the homophonous *etzev* meaning "pain, sorrow." Idols are quite literally "things of pain" — the noun is a pun. The catalogue of senseless features (mouths-don't-speak, eyes-don't-see, ears-don't-hear, noses-don't-smell, hands-don't-feel, feet-don't-walk, throats-make-no-sound) is rhetorical-cumulative: each new sense organ named adds to the comic-tragic absurdity of an object that has the form of life without its function. The killer line is v. 8: *kemohem yihyu oseihem kol asher botech bahem* (כְּמוֹהֶם יִהְיוּ עֹשֵׂיהֶם כֹּל אֲשֶׁר־בֹּטֵחַ בָּהֶם, "those who make them become like them; so do all who trust in them"). This is an anthropological claim: idolaters are progressively conformed to the images they worship, becoming as deaf and blind and mute as their gods.

ANE parallels: anti-idol polemic exists in Egyptian and Babylonian wisdom traditions, but the *those-who-make-them-become-like-them* logic is distinctively Israelite, and it runs as a thread through Isaiah 44, Jeremiah 10, and into the Apocrypha. DSS evidence: 11QPs(a), the Great Psalms Scroll from Qumran, preserves much of Book V; portions of Ps 115 are extant. The LXX joins Ps 115 with Ps 114 as noted. Targum Psalms expands the anti-idol catalogue with intensified polemic against Roman imperial cult.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Psalm 115 is one of the most architecturally elaborate compositions in the Psalter, organized as an antiphonal liturgy with five distinct movements:

- **Movement 1 (vv. 1–3): Doxological prologue and apologetic question.** Israel offers glory to Yahweh's name; the nations mock; the answer comes immediately: "our God is in the heavens; he does all that he pleases."
- **Movement 2 (vv. 4–8): The anti-idol polemic.** A mocking inventory of idol senses.
- **Movement 3 (vv. 9–11): Threefold trust-refrain.** Israel, house of Aaron, those who fear the LORD — three calls, three refrains.
- **Movement 4 (vv. 12–15): Priestly blessing.** Yahweh has remembered, will bless — same threefold structure, with the priestly blessing language echoing Numbers 6.
- **Movement 5 (vv. 16–18): Hymnic-cosmological closing.** Heavens belong to Yahweh, earth to humans; the dead are silent, but we praise.

Robert Lowth's parallelism categories appear throughout. Synonymous parallelism dominates the trust-refrain ("O Israel, trust in the LORD! / He is their help and their shield" — repeated three times in vv. 9–11). Antithetic parallelism structures the great cosmological closing: "the heavens are the LORD's heavens, / but the earth he has given to the children of man" (v. 16); "the dead do not praise the LORD ... / But we will bless the LORD" (vv. 17–18). Synthetic parallelism builds the anti-idol catalogue (vv. 4–7), each line adding a new sense organ.

The **threefold structuring** is the psalm's signature literary device. Three groups are addressed in vv. 9–11 (Israel, house of Aaron, those who fear the LORD); three groups are blessed in vv. 12–13 (same triad, with "small and great" added); three sense pairs are mocked in vv. 5–7 before the cumulative summary. The triadic structure has been read by some commentators as reflecting concentric circles of Second Temple Israel: the people, the priests, the proselytes (*yir'ei Yahweh* in this period being a near-technical term for Gentile God-fearers, attested in Acts 10 with Cornelius and in synagogue inscriptions).

There is no *selah* in Ps 115, consistent with the Book V Hallel context.

The **anti-idol polemic (vv. 4–8) is itself a parody of priestly liturgy**. The structure mirrors the ritual call-and-response form, but each "answer" is silence. *Ozen lahem ve-lo yishma'u* — "they have ears, but do not hear." The sevenfold sense catalogue (mouths, eyes, ears, noses, hands, feet, throats) deliberately enumerates seven, the number of completion: idols are *completely* senseless. The capstone v. 8 then completes the thought: idol-makers and idol-worshippers undergo a corresponding completion — they become *completely like* their idols.

The closing strophe (vv. 16–18) contains a striking cosmological-anthropological claim: *ha-shamayim shamayim la-Yahweh ve-ha-aretz natan li-vnei adam* ("the heavens are Yahweh's heavens, but the earth he has given to the children of man," v. 16). This is creation theology in compact form — Yahweh is sovereign over heaven, but has entrusted the earth to humanity as stewards. It has been read alongside Genesis 1:28 and Psalm 8 as a triad of biblical creation-stewardship texts.

The famous v. 17, *lo ha-metim yehallelu Yah ve-lo kol-yordei dumah* ("the dead do not praise the LORD, nor do any who go down into silence"), reflects the early Hebrew Bible's *Sheol* anthropology: the realm of the dead is silent, separated from the praising community of the living. *Dumah* (דּוּמָה, "silence") is itself a name for the underworld in Ps 94:17. This is *not* the developed resurrection theology of Daniel 12 or 2 Maccabees 7 — it is the older Hebrew view, and the closing v. 18 *anachnu nevarech Yah me-atah ve-ad olam* ("but we will bless the LORD from this time forth and forevermore") is the living community's defiant response.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Within Wilson's canonical-shape reading of Book V (*The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter*, 1985), Pss 115–118 form the post-meal portion of the Hallel and rehearse the trust-and-deliverance theology that grounds Israel's continuing identity in the post-exilic period. Hossfeld–Zenger (*Psalms 3*, Hermeneia, 2011) and Mitchell (*The Message of the Psalter*, 1997) read the Hallel as the post-exilic community's eschatological-messianic anticipation, with Ps 115's anti-idol polemic functioning as the boundary-marker between Yahweh-faith and the surrounding pagan milieu.

The **Jewish trajectory**. Mishnah Pesahim 10 and b. Pesahim 117–118 prescribe Ps 115 over the third and fourth cups at the Seder. *Midrash Tehillim* on Ps 115 elaborates the anti-idol polemic and connects v. 1 to the Maccabean martyrs who died "for the sake of the Name." Rashi reads *lo lanu* as the prayer of the exilic community: not for our merit but for your Name's sake. Ibn Ezra notes the threefold structure of the trust-refrain and identifies the *yir'ei Yahweh* as Gentile proselytes attached to the synagogue. Radak (David Kimhi) develops the anti-idol polemic in dialogue with medieval Christian iconography — a pointed, sometimes dangerous exegesis given his historical context. Modern Jewish thought finds rich resources in Ps 115. Buber on the *Name*-theology; Heschel on the prophetic critique of idolatry; **Jonathan Sacks** (*Not in God's Name*, 2015) on v. 8 as the foundational anthropological insight that we become what we worship; **Levenson** on the Sheol theology of v. 17 as authentic biblical anthropology, not to be hastily Christianized; Sarna on the Hallel as the Passover's lyrical heart. The **Templar-Knights motto** *Non nobis Domine* and its later Catholic-and-Protestant adoption (Henry V's victory hymn at Agincourt sang *Non nobis* per Shakespeare's *Henry V* IV.viii) shows the verse's wide religious afterlife.

The **Christian trajectory**. Augustine (*Enarrationes in Psalmos*) reads Ps 115 as the church's confession against Roman idolatry; the *non nobis Domine* he treats as the formula of all Christian humility. Cassiodorus elaborates the anti-idol polemic. Aquinas distinguishes the worship of images (*latreia*) from veneration (*dulia*) using Ps 115 as one of the touchstone texts in the medieval debate over icons. The Byzantine **Iconoclast Controversy** (eighth–ninth centuries) made Ps 115 a battleground: iconoclasts cited vv. 4–8 against the iconodules, who responded that images of Christ and saints, properly venerated, do not fall under the polemic because Christ's incarnation makes images representational rather than constitutive. The Second Council of Nicaea (787) settled this distinction for the Catholic and Orthodox traditions. **The Reformation reopened it**: Luther was relatively moderate (he objected to image *worship* but not to instructional pictures); Calvin was sharper, citing Ps 115 frequently and calling the human heart *idolorum fabrica*, "a factory of idols" (*Institutes* I.xi.8). The Puritans took the polemic furthest, with the Westminster Larger Catechism citing Ps 115 in its exposition of the Second Commandment. **Calvin's Geneva Psalter** set Ps 115 to a singing tune; the *Bay Psalm Book* (1640) and the Scottish Metrical Psalter both preserved congregational versions. The **Knights Templar** adopted *Non nobis Domine* as their official motto from the Latin Vulgate; the same Latin became Henry V's victory anthem at Agincourt (1415), preserved in Shakespeare and set memorably by Patrick Doyle for Kenneth Branagh's 1989 film. **Bach** wove *Non nobis Domine* into multiple compositions; the canon attributed to William Byrd remains standard repertoire in Anglican choirs to this day.

Among modern critical readers, Hans-Joachim Kraus (*Psalmen*, BKAT) treats Ps 115 as a community trust-liturgy. John Goldingay (*Psalms 90–150*, BCOTWP, 2008) emphasizes the threefold-trust structure and reads the anti-idol polemic as both ancient Near Eastern critique and timeless theological warning. Walter Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*, 1984; *The Prophetic Imagination*, 1978) develops Ps 115:8 — *those who make them become like them* — into a sustained critique of consumer-capitalist idolatry, arguing that modern persons are "produced" by the commodities they worship. Stanley Hauerwas extends this in *Resident Aliens* (1989, with Willimon). **Greg Beale's *We Become What We Worship: A Biblical Theology of Idolatry* (2008)** is the most extensive modern monograph on the v. 8 logic, tracing it from Ps 115 through Isa 6, Hos 4, Rom 1 into the New Testament. Beth Tanner, James Mays, and Patrick Miller emphasize the antiphonal liturgical structure. Hossfeld–Zenger emphasize the redactional placement of Ps 115 between the Exodus rehearsal of Ps 114 and the personal thanksgiving of Ps 116 as theologically deliberate: the post-Exodus community must learn to trust the LORD precisely against the surrounding idolatrous nations.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The central theological tension of Psalm 115 lies in its **anti-idol anthropology** of v. 8: *those who make them become like them; so do all who trust in them*. Is this rhetorical mockery, ontological claim, or social-formational diagnosis? And how does this ancient polemic against silver-and-gold idols translate to a culture that has largely shed the literal artifacts but retained, perhaps in intensified form, the underlying patterns?

**Position 1: The classical iconoclast reading (Calvin, the Reformed tradition, the Iconoclast Council of Hieria 754).** Ps 115:4–8 forbids religious images of any kind. The human heart is, in Calvin's phrase, a *idolorum fabrica*, a factory of idols, and any representational worship inevitably collapses into idolatry. Steelman: the polemic is unequivocal in its targets (silver and gold images); the New Testament continues the polemic (1 John 5:21; 1 Cor 10:14); the *those-who-make-them-become-like-them* logic implies that any habituation to image-worship deforms the worshipper. The Reformed iconoclast reading is the most consistent and the most demanding application of the text.

**Position 2: The classical iconodule reading (John of Damascus, Theodore the Studite, Second Council of Nicaea 787, the Catholic and Eastern Orthodox traditions).** Ps 115's polemic is against pagan idols representing false gods, not against Christian images of the true God incarnate or his saints. The Incarnation changes the metaphysics of representation: the Word made flesh has dignified visible form, and images of Christ are not in the same category as the *atzabbim* of Ps 115. The *latreia/dulia* distinction (worship reserved to God; veneration extended to images and saints) preserves the polemic's force while permitting iconography. Steelman: the New Testament does not, in fact, prohibit Christian iconography; the Council of Hieria was rejected by the catholic church; and millennia of Eastern and Western Christian art have proved theologically defensible. The polemic targets *false gods*, not *all images*.

**Position 3: The anthropological-sociological reading (Brueggemann, Hauerwas, Beale, Sacks).** The deepest claim of Ps 115 is not metaphysical but anthropological: *we become what we worship*. The literal silver-and-gold idols of the ancient world have been replaced by other objects of ultimate concern — money, technology, nation, self, sex, ideology — and the formational logic remains. Worship is the most powerful shaping practice of human life, and Ps 115:8 is its anthropology. Steelman: this reading honors the polemic's force without getting trapped in the iconoclast/iconodule debate. Greg Beale traces the logic across the canon and into modernity: from Pharaoh's hardened heart in Exodus to Isaiah's senseless people in Isa 6 (*hashmen lev ha-am ha-zeh*) to Romans 1:18–32 to the present. The contemporary form of the polemic asks: what are the "factories of human hands" (*ma'aseh ydei adam*, v. 4) producing, and how are we being produced *by* them?

**Position 4: The Eucharistic-trust-refrain reading (the Hallel context).** Ps 115 is sung at the Passover Seder over the third cup of wine — the cup that Christian tradition (Luke 22:20) identifies with "the new covenant in my blood." The trust-refrain (vv. 9–11) is sung in the immediate context of the meal, after which (in the Last Supper sequence) the community goes out to sing the rest of the Hallel and proceed to the Mount of Olives. Steelman: read this way, Ps 115's anti-idol polemic and trust-refrain function as the negative and positive theology of the Passover meal: against the idols, *trust in the LORD*. The Christological reception (Christ as the one who, on the night before his death, sang this exact text) places Ps 115 at the heart of Christian Eucharistic theology — though the four major Christian Eucharistic positions (Catholic Real Presence, Lutheran consubstantiation, Reformed memorial-spiritual, Eastern Orthodox sacramental) construe the relationship differently.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 115 is the most concentrated theological statement in the Psalter on the question of *what we worship and what worship does to us*. Its claim is that the relationship between worshipper and object is never one-way: we project onto our gods, but our gods then form us. The Reformed and the Orthodox-Catholic traditions disagree sharply about the application to Christian iconography, and that disagreement is a real theological fault line. But the deeper claim — that human beings are progressively conformed to whatever they hold as ultimate — runs across the disagreement and applies to every age, including those (like ours) which imagine themselves to have moved beyond idolatry only because they have lost the vocabulary for it.

The closing question is what *we* are making with our hands — what *atzabbim* of silver and gold and silicon and code we are forming day after day — and whether we have noticed that we are becoming like them. The trust-refrain's threefold call (Israel, priests, fearers of the LORD) names every category of belonger, leaving no one outside its scope. *Betach ba-Yahweh*: trust in the LORD. The alternative is not trust in nothing but trust in something else, and that something else will, by v. 8's logic, eventually look like us — or, more disturbingly, we will eventually look like it. Jesus sang this song the night he was betrayed.
