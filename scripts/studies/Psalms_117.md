# Psalm 117 — A Scholarly Study

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 117 is **the shortest psalm in the Bible — only two verses, twenty-nine words in the Hebrew, sixteen Hebrew words in the body proper after the opening *halleluyah*.** It is also, by the same accounting, the shortest *chapter* in the Christian Bible by a wide margin (the next-shortest, 2 John, runs thirteen verses). The psalm is anonymous and stands without superscription in the Masoretic Text apart from the opening *halleluyah*. It belongs to Book V (Pss 107–150) and to **the Egyptian Hallel** (*Hallel Mitzri*, Pss 113–118). In the Passover Seder codified in Mishnah Pesahim 10, Ps 117 is sung with the post-meal Hallel-block (Pss 115–118) over the third and fourth cups of wine. Its brevity does not exempt it from the Hallel; it is a fully integrated Hallel psalm and is included in every traditional Hallel recitation, whether the long *Hallel ha-Gadol* or the abbreviated *Hallel ha-Mitzri* or the truncated *Hallel of Ne'imah* used on Rosh Chodesh and the latter days of Passover. The Hallel is the Hallel because Pss 113–118 are sung as one liturgical unit; remove Ps 117 and the unit is incomplete.

In Hermann Gunkel's form-critical taxonomy, Ps 117 is a **descriptive hymn** (*Hymnus*) in its purest, most distilled form. The structure of the descriptive hymn — (a) call to praise, (b) reasons for praise (introduced by *ki*, "for/because"), (c) renewed call to praise — is exhibited in Ps 117 with textbook clarity, compressed into just two verses. Sigmund Mowinckel and Claus Westermann both treated Ps 117 as a model specimen of the genre. Hans-Joachim Kraus (*Psalmen*, BKAT, 1978) reads it as a *Schluss-Hymnus* — a closing hymn — possibly used as a doxology to conclude a longer liturgy. Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger (*Psalms 3*, Hermeneia, 2011) read it as a deliberately compressed centerpiece in the Hallel's symmetrical architecture: 113 (universal kingship and reversal) → 114 (Exodus narrative) → 115 (anti-idol trust) → 116 (individual thanksgiving) → **117 (universal call to praise — the structural pivot)** → 118 (royal entrance liturgy). On this reading, Ps 117 is not an afterthought but the deliberate hinge of the Hallel.

The psalm is dated to the post-exilic period (sixth to fourth centuries BCE) on the basis of (a) the universalist scope ("all nations ... all peoples"), characteristic of post-exilic Yahwism's diasporic horizon (cf. Isa 49:6, 56:7; Mal 1:11), (b) its placement in the Hallel cluster, and (c) the lapidary, gnomic style typical of late Hebrew poetry. Some scholars have proposed it was composed specifically *as* a Hallel pivot rather than redacted into one.

The opening line, *halelu et-Yahweh kol-goyim shabchuhu kol-ha-ummim* (הַלְלוּ אֶת־יְהוָה כָּל־גּוֹיִם שַׁבְּחוּהוּ כָּל־הָאֻמִּים, "praise the LORD, all nations! Extol him, all peoples!" 117:1), uses two distinct pairings for non-Israelite humanity: *goyim* (גּוֹיִם, "nations" in the political-territorial sense) and *ummim* (אֻמִּים, "peoples" — a less common Hebrew noun, perhaps slightly archaic, paralleled in Gen 25:23). The verb *shibach* (שִׁבַּח, "extol, praise") is itself a relatively rare praise-verb in the Psalter, found only in Pss 63, 65, 145, 147, and here — its rarity gives the line a distinctive lexical color. The combination *halelu et-Yahweh kol-goyim* sounds the universalist note in its strongest possible form: every *goy*, every nation, is summoned to the praise of Israel's God.

The reason follows in v. 2: *ki gavar aleinu chasdo ve-emet Yahweh le-olam* (כִּי גָבַר עָלֵינוּ חַסְדּוֹ וֶאֱמֶת־יְהוָה לְעוֹלָם, "for great is his steadfast love toward us, and the faithfulness of the LORD endures forever"). The verb *gavar* (גָּבַר, "to be mighty, prevail") is the same root as *gever* (man, hero) and *gevurah* (might) — Yahweh's *chesed* (steadfast covenant love) "prevails" or "is mighty" *aleinu* (toward us, over us). The *chesed ve-emet* pairing is one of the most theologically loaded couplets in the Hebrew Bible (Exod 34:6; Pss 25:10, 26:3, 40:11, 86:15, 89:14), the standard Yahwistic self-description.

The pivot from "all nations" (v. 1) to "his steadfast love toward *us*" (v. 2) is the psalm's most surprising rhetorical move. The *aleinu* presupposes Israel as the *us*, and the nations are summoned to praise because of Yahweh's *chesed* toward Israel — not, strictly, because of Yahweh's *chesed* toward themselves. This is the universalist-yet-particularist signature of post-exilic Yahwism: the nations are invited into a praise that depends on Israel's covenantal history, not displacing it.

ANE parallels: the universalist call to praise is rare in pre-exilic ANE hymnography but appears in late Babylonian and Persian-period inscriptions. The closer biblical parallels are Isaiah 40–55, Ps 96, Ps 100, and Ps 148. **NT use is decisive**: Romans 15:11 quotes Ps 117:1 in Paul's central argument that Christ's mission has unified Jew and Gentile into a single worshipping people. DSS evidence: 11QPs(a) preserves Ps 117 within the Hallel cluster.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Psalm 117 is the most architecturally compressed composition in the Psalter, and yet it exhibits every feature of the descriptive-hymn genre with textbook precision. The structure is:

- **v. 1a**: Call to praise: *halelu et-Yahweh kol-goyim*
- **v. 1b**: Parallel call to praise: *shabchuhu kol-ha-ummim*
- **v. 2a**: Reason (introduced by *ki*): *ki gavar aleinu chasdo*
- **v. 2b**: Parallel reason: *ve-emet Yahweh le-olam*
- **v. 2c**: Closing call to praise: *halleluyah*

Robert Lowth's parallelism categories appear with miniature perfection. Verse 1 displays synonymous parallelism in its purest form: *halelu / shabchuhu* (parallel imperative verbs of praise); *kol-goyim / kol-ha-ummim* (parallel universalist objects). Verse 2 displays staircase parallelism, where the second line builds on the first: *gavar aleinu chasdo* (his *chesed* prevails over us) → *emet Yahweh le-olam* (the *emet* of the LORD endures forever). The first line names *chesed* and locates it spatially (*aleinu*, "over us"); the second line names *emet* and locates it temporally (*le-olam*, "forever"). Spatial and temporal universals are paired: chesed prevails *here*; emet endures *always*.

The opening and closing *halleluyah* form a perfect inclusio. There is no *selah* — selah would be ridiculous in so short a psalm. The poetic register is gnomic: each line is a complete proverb-like utterance.

Robert Alter (*The Book of Psalms*, 2007) calls Ps 117 "a small jewel — a complete hymn in two verses, exhibiting in miniature the genre's full structural integrity." Alter notes the symmetry: two universalist calls, two universalist reasons, framed by *halleluyah*. Hossfeld–Zenger emphasize the redactional function: Ps 117 serves as the hinge of the Hallel, the structural pivot at which the Egyptian-particular focus of Pss 113–116 opens out toward all nations before Ps 118 returns to the temple-entrance liturgy.

**A talmudic curiosity**: the Babylonian Talmud (b. Berakhot 9b) preserves a tradition that Ps 117 was *added* by a later editor as a single verse, then expanded — but the consensus rabbinic and modern critical view treats it as a unified two-verse composition. The brevity has prompted ingenious midrashic exegesis: *Midrash Tehillim* on Ps 117 elaborates that the psalm's brevity matches its theological scope inversely — the shortest psalm contains the largest claim (universal praise).

There is one unusual textual feature: many medieval Hebrew manuscripts preserve Ps 117 with vocalization indicators suggesting it was read in a single liturgical breath, without internal pause. The Aleppo Codex preserves it with a spacing that emphasizes its self-contained brevity.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Within Wilson's canonical-shape reading of Book V (*The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter*, 1985), Ps 117 stands as the structural pivot of the Hallel cluster. Mitchell (*The Message of the Psalter*, 1997) reads it as the eschatological-universalist hinge: the post-exilic community, in singing the Hallel at Passover, anticipates a coming day when the praise of Yahweh will be sung by every *goy* and every *am*.

The **Jewish trajectory**. The Hallel includes Ps 117 in its liturgical recitation; Pesahim 117–118 in the Babylonian Talmud preserves debates about precise ordering and divisions. *Midrash Tehillim* on Ps 117 elaborates extensively despite the psalm's brevity, treating it as a rhetorical *kol va-chomer* — if so brief a psalm contains so universal a call, how much more the longer hymns of Israel. Rashi reads *kol-goyim* as referring to the future eschatological participation of the nations in Israel's praise; the *aleinu* of v. 2 secures the psalm as Israel's, while the *kol-goyim* opens it toward the world. Ibn Ezra emphasizes the *chesed ve-emet* pairing as the foundational divine attributes (echoing Exod 34:6). Radak (David Kimhi) treats v. 2 as the messianic-age fulfillment of God's covenant with Israel, witnessed by all peoples. Modern Jewish thought engages Ps 117 in dialectic. Buber's *I and Thou* meditates on the move from particular (*aleinu*) to universal (*kol-goyim*); Heschel finds in the *chesed ve-emet* couplet the prophetic theology of God's character; Levenson (*The Universal Horizon of Biblical Particularism*) treats Ps 117 as a key text in the question of how Israel's particular election relates to the nations' eventual participation; Sarna emphasizes the post-exilic context in which Diasporic Israel had to formulate a theology of the nations.

The **Christian trajectory** is dominated by **Paul's quotation in Romans 15:11**. In Romans 15:7–13, Paul builds his climactic argument that Jew and Gentile alike are unified in Christ by stringing together four Old Testament prooftexts: Ps 18:49 (= 2 Sam 22:50), Deut 32:43, Ps 117:1, and Isa 11:10. Ps 117:1 is the third of the four, and it functions structurally as the most explicit universalist text in the chain: *praise the LORD, all you Gentiles, and let all the peoples extol him* (Paul follows the LXX αἰνεῖτε τὸν κύριον, πάντα τὰ ἔθνη). For Paul, Ps 117 is exegetical proof that Israel's Scripture itself anticipated the inclusion of the nations in the worship of the God of Israel — that the gospel's Gentile mission is not a deviation from biblical theology but its predicted fulfillment. The Pauline reception is foundational: every subsequent Christian theology of mission, Gentile inclusion, and the church's universal scope rests on this passage and its prooftext.

Augustine (*Enarrationes in Psalmos*) reads Ps 117 as the church's universal song, the hymn of the *Catholica* in its etymological sense (κατά + ὅλος, "according to the whole"). Cassiodorus develops the universalist theme. Aquinas treats v. 2's *chesed ve-emet* as the foundational divine attributes underlying the church's mission. Luther's reading emphasizes the pure *gratia* of the verse: God's *chesed* prevails over us — not because of our merit but by the strength of his covenant. Calvin treats Ps 117 as the eschatological fulfillment of Abraham's covenant: in Abraham all the nations would be blessed (Gen 12:3), and Ps 117 names the day when all the nations praise. Calvin's commentary on this psalm, despite its brevity, runs to substantial length precisely because of its theological density. **N. T. Wright** (*The Mission of God*; *Paul and the Faithfulness of God*, 2013) treats Ps 117 as a key text in the biblical theology of mission: Israel was always called to be the people through whom blessing came to the nations, and Ps 117's *kol-goyim* is the lyrical heart of that vocation. **Christopher Wright** (*The Mission of God*, 2006) follows similar lines.

Liturgically, Ps 117 has had a remarkable Christian afterlife. It is set frequently in church music (Bach's *Lobet den Herrn, alle Heiden* BWV 230 — a motet for double chorus on Ps 117); it appears in Anglican and Catholic Lauds; it is the shortest Psalter chapter and is often used as a quick congregational doxology. The Catholic Liturgy of the Hours assigns it weekly in Lauds. Mendelssohn set it; Handel quoted it; Renaissance and Baroque settings abound.

Among modern critical readers, Hans-Joachim Kraus reads Ps 117 as the structural climax of the Hallel's universalism. John Goldingay (*Psalms 90–150*, BCOTWP, 2008) emphasizes the move from *aleinu* (particular) to *kol-goyim* (universal). Beth Tanner emphasizes the post-exilic missiology. Walter Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*, 1984) treats Ps 117 as a "doxology of orientation" extended outward to the nations. James Mays and Patrick Miller emphasize the lyric integrity of the two-verse composition. Hossfeld–Zenger argue that the psalm's redactional placement is theologically deliberate: at the Hallel's pivot, the Passover community sings the universalist horizon precisely because the Exodus narrative is not merely a tribal myth but a paradigm of God's character.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The central theological tension of Psalm 117 lies in its **universalism-and-particularism**: the nations are called to praise Yahweh in v. 1, but the *reason* given in v. 2 is Yahweh's *chesed* toward *us* (Israel). How are these two held together, and what does the relationship imply for biblical theology of mission, Israel-and-the-church, and contemporary religious pluralism?

**Position 1: The Pauline-mission reading (Augustine, Calvin, Wright, Christopher Wright).** Ps 117 is the lyrical heart of biblical missiology. The nations are summoned to praise because Yahweh's *chesed* toward Israel is *for* the nations — Israel's election is instrumental, not exclusive. Paul's appeal to the verse in Romans 15:11 is not a strained allegorization but a faithful reading: from the call of Abraham forward, Israel exists for the blessing of the nations, and Ps 117 is the eschatological promise that the nations will eventually join the praise. Steelman: this reading honors Paul's apostolic exegesis, integrates Ps 117 into the broader canonical narrative (Gen 12:3 → Isa 49:6 → Ps 117 → Rom 15), and grounds the church's missionary identity in Israel's Scripture rather than in supersessionism.

**Position 2: The supersessionist temptation (which most contemporary scholars resist).** Ps 117's call to *kol-goyim* could be read as displacing Israel — the nations come, Israel recedes. Steelman of the *resistance*: the *aleinu* of v. 2 is structurally indispensable. The nations are called to praise *because* of Yahweh's *chesed* toward Israel, not in place of it. Any reading that erases the *aleinu* contradicts the syntax. The post-Holocaust generation of Christian readers (Wright, Hauerwas, Brueggemann, Cardinal Walter Kasper) have insisted that Israel's particularity is permanent, not temporary — Ps 117 enlarges the worshipping community, it does not replace its founding member.

**Position 3: The eschatological-universalist reading (Levenson, Sarna, the Jewish tradition broadly).** Ps 117 envisions a future age in which the nations join Israel's praise — but this is an *eschatological* hope, not a present reality. The Hallel sings what *will be* on the day of the LORD, when, in Zechariah's vision (Zech 14:16), all the nations come up to Jerusalem to worship. Steelman: this reading respects the post-exilic horizon of Ps 117 and integrates it with the broader eschatological tradition of the Hebrew Bible (Isa 2:2–4; Mic 4:1–4; Zech 14). It avoids both supersessionist and triumphalist applications.

**Position 4: The chapter-form / canonical-form reading.** Ps 117's status as the shortest *chapter* in the Bible has prompted reflection on the contingent nature of chapter and verse divisions. The chapter divisions were introduced by Stephen Langton in the early thirteenth century; the verse divisions in the Hebrew text by the Masoretes and in the New Testament by Robert Estienne (Stephanus) in 1551 and 1555. The "chapter" status of Ps 117 is therefore a medieval-modern accident — the Masoretic division of psalms is older but still post-biblical. Steelman: this reading treats the brevity as theologically significant despite its accidental form. The shortest chapter contains the largest claim. Whether this is intentional design or fortunate accident, the canonical effect is the same: the briefest form, the universal scope.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 117 is a small text bearing extraordinary weight. In two verses, it establishes the universalist horizon of biblical worship, names the divine attributes (*chesed ve-emet*) that ground that horizon, and roots the universal call in the particular covenant with Israel. Paul reads it as the prooftext for the Gentile mission. Christian liturgy has sung it for two thousand years as the lyrical concentration of the universal church. Jewish liturgy has sung it for as long as the Hallel has existed as the affirmation that the God of Israel is the God of all peoples — but is the God of Israel first, and the *aleinu* of v. 2 is non-negotiable.

The closing question is whether the church remembers, when it sings *halelu et-Yahweh kol-goyim*, that the *us* of v. 2 is older than the *all* of v. 1. The nations are invited into the Hallel because of God's *chesed* toward Israel. To sing this psalm is to take a stand against any theology that imagines Christian universalism as freestanding, as if the Gentile praise of Yahweh did not stand on Jewish covenantal foundations. Two verses, twenty-nine words — and an entire theology of God, Israel, the nations, and the church, compressed into a hymn small enough to be sung in a single breath.
