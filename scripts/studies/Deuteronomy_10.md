# Deuteronomy 10: The Ark and the Widow

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Deuteronomy 10 sits inside Moses' long second speech on the plains of Moab, and it functions as a hinge between the wrathful memory of Horeb (chapters 9) and the great covenantal exhortation of chapter 11. The chapter opens with Moses cutting the second set of stone tablets and making an ark of acacia wood (*ʾărôn ʿăṣê šiṭṭîm*, v. 3). The linguistic and material world here is thoroughly second-millennium Levantine: acacia (šiṭṭâ) is the hard-grained desert wood that ancient Egyptian furniture makers also prized, and the physical act of re-inscribing covenantal tablets after breakage has firm parallels in Hittite vassal treaty practice, where broken tablets were re-engraved to signal a restored relationship between suzerain and vassal. Moshe Weinfeld's *Deuteronomy 1–11* (Anchor Bible) catalogs these parallels with unusual care, showing how the language of covenant renewal in Deuteronomy echoes the formulas of Hittite and neo-Assyrian treaties.

The second unit (vv. 6–9) is a famous puzzle. It reads as a parenthesis inserted into Moses' speech, reporting that Aaron died at Moserah, that Eleazar took his place, and that at that time YHWH set apart the tribe of Levi to carry the ark and minister in his name. The difficulty is that Numbers 20:22–29 and Numbers 33:38 locate Aaron's death at Mount Hor, not Moserah. The itinerary Beeroth Bene-Jaakan → Moserah → Gudgodah → Jotbathah also runs in the opposite direction from Numbers 33:31–33. Jeffrey Tigay's *JPS Torah Commentary: Deuteronomy* treats this as evidence of an independent early tradition about the wilderness stations; Weinfeld and Richard Nelson (OTL) read it as a Deuteronomistic editor's compressed summary serving a theological function — to pair Aaron's death with the consecration of the Levites for a new stage of service. Peter Craigie (NICOT) and Daniel Block (NIVAC) argue for a harmonization, proposing that Moserah could be the district that includes Mount Hor. Whichever view one holds, the purpose of the insertion is clear: the covenant survives the death of its original priest because the Levitical order has been established, and the ark has a home.

Key Hebrew terms shape the chapter's thrust. Verses 12–13 pose the famous question *mah YHWH ʾĕlōhêkā šōʾēl mēʿimmāk* — "what does YHWH your God require of you?" — answered with five infinitives: to fear (*lĕyirʾâ*), to walk (*lāleket*), to love (*lĕʾahăbâ*), to serve (*laʿăbōd*), and to keep (*lišmōr*). This is the beating heart of Deuteronomic piety. Verse 16 commands Israel to *māltem ʾēt ʿorlat lĕbabkem* — circumcise the foreskin of your heart — which treats physical circumcision as a type for inward dedication. Verse 19 delivers the Torah's most-repeated refrain, *waʾăhabtem ʾet hagēr* — love the stranger — grounded in the memory *gērîm hĕyîtem bĕʾereṣ miṣrāyim* (you were sojourners in Egypt). The Sifre and later rabbinic tradition will count this command as appearing thirty-six (so Rabbi Eliezer) or forty-six (so others) times across the Torah, a statistic that becomes a theological claim about the weight of the duty.

Archaeology illuminates the material world. The acacia ark Moses describes is simple; the far more elaborate gold-overlaid ark of Exodus 25 belongs to a later, Priestly framing. Martin Noth, Richard Friedman, and others have argued that Deuteronomy preserves an older, Ephraimite memory of a plainer cultic object. High places (*bāmôt*) and pillars (*maṣṣēbôt*) — the targets of chapters 7 and 12 — are attested across Iron Age Canaan, from Tel Dan's cultic platform to the stelae of Gezer. The world of this chapter is not mythological; it is furniture, wood, stone, and the concrete practice of a people who know the taste of Egyptian dust.

Authorship debates cluster around the whole of Deuteronomy and are sharpened by chapter 10's parenthesis about the ark and the Levites. W. M. L. de Wette's 1805 dissertation identified the scroll found by Hilkiah in 2 Kings 22 with Deuteronomy, anchoring the book in the Josianic reform of 622 BCE. Julius Wellhausen's *Prolegomena* refined this into the four-source Documentary Hypothesis, treating Deuteronomy as a seventh-century product. Against this stand Peter Craigie, Kenneth Kitchen, and Meredith Kline, who argue from the treaty form that Deuteronomy fits a second-millennium Hittite model better than a first-millennium Assyrian one. The ark references in Deuteronomy 10 work for either camp: as a Mosaic original it reports what happened; as a later composition it preserves and interprets older tradition. The chapter is mostly at ease with both readings.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre is the key to reading Deuteronomy 10 rightly. The book as a whole is Moses' farewell sermon, framed as a series of catechetical addresses. Within that frame, chapter 10 is paraenesis — exhortation — structured around recapitulation (vv. 1–11), parenthesis (vv. 6–9), and theological crescendo (vv. 12–22). It is sermonic before it is legal. Patrick Miller's Interpretation commentary on Deuteronomy emphasizes how the book's rhetorical texture invites the hearer into a memory that is simultaneously past and present: "you were sojourners in Egypt" is addressed to a generation that was not, in biological terms, in Egypt at all, yet the liturgical *you* collapses time.

Literary devices are dense. The chapter turns on a set of striking metaphors. Circumcision of the heart (v. 16) breaks the ritual marker loose from its biological site and re-locates it in the inner life, a move that Jeremiah 4:4 and 9:25 will seize upon and that Paul will read as load-bearing (Romans 2:29; Philippians 3:3). The ladder of divine majesty in verses 14 and 17 — heaven, the heaven of heavens, the earth with all that is in it; God of gods and Lord of lords, great, mighty, awesome — builds an escalating cosmic frame, then immediately falls to the lowest social rungs: the fatherless, the widow, the sojourner. This is a characteristically Deuteronomic move: the highest God cares about the lowest person. Walter Brueggemann's *Deuteronomy* (AOTC) names this as the "downward mobility of YHWH's attention."

The five infinitives of verse 12 form a literary signature. Each verb is separately attested as a core Deuteronomic term, and together they constitute what McConville's *Deuteronomy* (Apollos) calls the grammar of covenantal response. The triplet at the end of verse 12 — heart, soul, and (implicitly, through the Shema nearby) might — anchors the chapter to Deuteronomy 6:4–9. The chapter is, in this sense, the first full elaboration of what loving God with all one's heart looks like as social practice.

Textual variants are modest but instructive. The Samaritan Pentateuch shows a different itinerary order in verses 6–7, smoothing the tension with Numbers 33. The Septuagint (LXX) preserves the MT order but with small lexical shifts; the Qumran fragments from 4QDeut (especially 4QDeut^f and 4QDeut^j) agree broadly with MT. The Nash Papyrus, a second-century BCE Hebrew text from Egypt, preserves the Decalogue in a form that suggests the tablets-making narrative of Deuteronomy 10 was already understood as canonical in Second Temple Judaism. Nothing in the variants overturns the chapter's argument; the textual tradition is stable.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish reception** begins in the Second Temple. Philo of Alexandria, in *De Specialibus Legibus* and *De Virtutibus*, reads the command to love the stranger as the apex of the Torah's ethics, and the circumcision of the heart as the true target of the physical rite. Josephus, writing for a Roman audience, softens the polemical edges but preserves the chapter's centrality. The Qumran community's Temple Scroll (11Q19) rewrites large stretches of Deuteronomy but leaves chapter 10's paraenetic heart largely intact; the community's asceticism took the inward circumcision seriously (1QS on the yahad's purity). The halakhic letter 4QMMT cites Deuteronomy repeatedly to distinguish the sect's practice from the Jerusalem priesthood.

Rabbinic tradition treasures Deuteronomy 10. The Sifre on Deuteronomy (a tannaitic midrash, third century CE) devotes extended attention to verse 12's "what does YHWH require." The Mekhilta on Exodus cross-references the love-the-sojourner refrain. Devarim Rabbah (Deuteronomy Rabbah) gathers homiletical expansions: the widow and orphan are loved because they have no one else; the sojourner is loved because the Holy One himself was, in a sense, "a sojourner" in the wilderness with Israel. Rashi (eleventh-century Troyes) comments on verse 12 with a famously laconic remark: though the question "what does YHWH require" looks difficult, in fact "everything depends on fear" — *hakōl bîrʾâ* — and from that all the other demands flow. Ibn Ezra reads the circumcision of the heart as removal of obstruction, not addition; Ramban (Nachmanides) presses further, treating it as prophetic promise that anticipates Deuteronomy 30:6. Maimonides' *Guide of the Perplexed* 3.32–33 uses chapter 10 to argue that the Torah's ceremonial commands aim at a rational, ethical end — the love of God and the stranger — and he expands the theme in *Hilchot Deʿot* and *Hilchot Matanot Aniyim*.

Modern Jewish readings remain vigorous. Jeffrey Tigay's *JPS Torah Commentary: Deuteronomy* reads chapter 10 as the moral climax of the book's opening movement. Jon Levenson's *Sinai and Zion* locates verse 14's cosmological claim — "heaven and the heaven of heavens, the earth with all that is in it" — within the Zion-cosmos tradition. Jonathan Sacks preaches verse 19 as the Torah's most demanding ethic: love the one who is not you, because you were once not-you. Nehama Leibowitz's *Studies in Deuteronomy* gathers the classical Jewish voices around verse 16's heart-circumcision as the Torah's inwardness principle. Abraham Heschel, in *God in Search of Man* and *The Prophets*, names the orphan-widow-sojourner triad as the divine pathos localized in law. Moshe Weinfeld's *Deuteronomic School* traces the social-ethical vocabulary of the chapter into the later prophetic corpus, especially Jeremiah.

**Christian reception** reads the chapter Christologically from the start. Origen's *Homilies on Deuteronomy* (most preserved in Rufinus' Latin) treats the second tablets as a figure of the Word re-inscribed after Adam's fall; the ark of acacia is a type of Christ, humanity unified with divinity; the circumcision of the heart is the baptismal renewal. Augustine, in *City of God* 16 and scattered sermons, returns again and again to the stranger-love command as the extension of charity beyond ethnic boundary, grounding his theology of the two cities in the pilgrim identity of the people of God. Chrysostom's homilies on the Pauline epistles lean on Deuteronomy 10:16 whenever he argues that the Christian life is inward before it is outward.

Aquinas' *Summa Theologiae* I-II qq. 101–105 treats the ceremonial, judicial, and moral precepts of the Old Law systematically. Deuteronomy 10's moral core — fear, walk, love, serve, keep — he reads as natural-law material preserved and elevated; the circumcision of the heart he treats (II-II q. 32 on mercy, and in the *Commentary on Romans*) as the analog of interior grace. The Reformation renews the chapter's weight. John Calvin's *Sermons on Deuteronomy* (delivered at Geneva 1555–56) spent more time on chapter 10 than on almost any other single chapter, reading verse 12 as the heart of sanctified religion and verse 16 as a prophecy fulfilled in regeneration. Luther's lectures on Deuteronomy (1525) treat the chapter as a pure gospel reading — law that drives to grace.

Critical scholarship from de Wette onward generally places Deuteronomy 10 late: either Josianic or exilic. Martin Noth's *Deuteronomistic History* framework treats chapters 1–11 as the introduction to the code. Thomas Römer's *The So-Called Deuteronomistic History* (and *The Invention of God*) traces the chapter's love-the-sojourner ethic to a Persian-period reflection on diaspora. Richard Nelson (OTL) and Duane Christensen (WBC) offer the fullest recent critical treatments. Contemporary Christian commentary — Brueggemann (AOTC), Patrick Miller (Interpretation), Chris Wright (*Deuteronomy* in Understanding the Bible Commentary), Block (NIVAC), McConville (Apollos) — converges on the chapter's ethical weight while differing on date and composition.

**Where Jewish and Christian readings converge and diverge** is illuminating. On love of stranger and care for widow and orphan, they agree almost entirely, and modern Jewish–Christian ethical conversation (Heschel, the Niebuhrs, Levinas reading Deuteronomy) treats the chapter as common ground. On circumcision of the heart they diverge sharply. Paul's Romans 2:29 reading — "circumcision is a matter of the heart, by the Spirit, not by the letter" — grounds a supersessionist tradition that Jewish readers have long contested. Rashi, Ibn Ezra, and Ramban all resist the dissolving of physical circumcision into a metaphor; for them the command in Deuteronomy 10:16 adds an interior dimension without replacing the outer sign. The modern Jewish–Christian Bible (Oxford) prints Deuteronomy 10 with parallel commentary that highlights exactly this fault line. Maimonides and Aquinas, across the divide, converge on the reading that the commandment's purpose is ethical and interior; they differ on whether the external sign remains binding.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What does YHWH require?** Verse 12 poses the question as if the answer were simple, and then the chapter gives five infinitives, eleven verses of expansion, and a cosmic frame. The tension is between the minimalism of the question's rhetoric and the maximalism of its answer.

*Position A (the Deuteronomic simplicity):* What YHWH requires is finally one thing — the love of God with all one's heart, from which all else follows. The five infinitives are a single act described from five angles. Calvin, Sacks, and Brueggemann read the chapter this way. The requirement is not a checklist but a reorientation.

*Position B (the halakhic fullness):* What YHWH requires is the full Torah, concretely enumerated, and the five infinitives are a preview of the specific commandments to follow in chapters 12–26. Maimonides' entire *Mishneh Torah* is an answer to Deuteronomy 10:12–13, which he uses as a structuring text. The Sifre already reads the chapter this way.

*Position C (the prophetic minimalism — steelman):* What YHWH requires is what Micah 6:8 says — to do justice, love mercy, and walk humbly. The chapter's cosmic frame (vv. 14, 17) and its concrete social ethic (vv. 18–19) mean that the ritual apparatus of the Torah is instrumental; the real requirement is the ethical core, and the rest is scaffolding. This is the Reform Jewish reading (Kaufmann Kohler, Hermann Cohen), and it has Christian analogs in the social gospel tradition. It is the least intuitive because it seems to reduce the Torah; its strength is that the chapter itself moves from heaven of heavens to the widow's food and clothing, and the prophetic tradition (Isaiah 1, Amos 5, Micah 6) reads Deuteronomy exactly this way. To steelman it: the text itself stacks verses 17–19 as the climax, not the five infinitives; the answer to "what does YHWH require" is, in the chapter's own movement, "that you love the sojourner."

**Where this leaves us:** the three positions are not rivals so much as nested. The minimal answer (Position C) gives the end; the maximal answer (Position B) gives the means; the unifying answer (Position A) gives the disposition. The chapter refuses to let any of the three collapse into the others.

**Tension 2: Is circumcision of the heart a command or a gift?** Verse 16 reads as an imperative: "circumcise therefore the foreskin of your heart." But Deuteronomy 30:6 announces it as a divine promise: "YHWH your God will circumcise your heart." Jeremiah 4:4 commands it; Jeremiah 31:33 and Ezekiel 36:26 promise it.

*Position A (imperative):* The text commands; Israel must do it. Rashi and Ibn Ezra read verse 16 as Israel's moral task. Luther sometimes reads it this way — as law that exposes inability. The command is real; obedience is demanded.

*Position B (promise):* The text commands what only God can give. Augustine reads verse 16 through 30:6 — the command awakens the desire, which God alone fulfills. Calvin develops this into a full doctrine of regenerative grace. Ramban, on the Jewish side, reads 30:6 as the eschatological fulfillment of 10:16.

*Position C (synergy — steelman):* Maimonides and Aquinas argue that the command and the gift are not rivals; the command summons the will, and grace (or divine assistance, for Maimonides) enables the response. This is least intuitive because it seems to split a difference that both Protestant and rabbinic traditions have wanted to keep sharp. But the chapter itself holds command and gift together: verse 15 — "YHWH set his heart in love on your fathers" — grounds verse 16's imperative in prior divine election. The grammar is sovereign grace eliciting free response, and the chapter refuses to resolve it in either direction. Steelmanned: the synergist reading is not a compromise but the plain sense of the text's ordering.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter will not let us choose between command and gift. To read only the imperative is to miss the gospel beneath it; to read only the promise is to miss the urgency of the demand. The text holds both.

**An unresolved question:** does "love the sojourner because you were sojourners in Egypt" (v. 19) universalize to all human beings, or remain specifically Israelite in scope? The rabbinic tradition has debated it for two millennia; the Christian tradition has debated it in every century since Paul. The chapter itself does not answer. It points us at the memory of Egypt and says: now act.

## The Thread

The ark of acacia wood in verse 3 is built for a book — the tablets go inside — and this is the first place in the Torah where scripture and cultic object converge. This non-obviously connects to 1 Kings 8:9, where Solomon's dedication prayer notes that the ark at the Temple still contained only those two tablets. The book Israel carries is the book that witnesses against Israel.

The itinerary in verses 6–7 names Moserah as Aaron's burial place; the root *mûsar* means "discipline" or "instruction." The parenthesis is grammatically about a location and theologically about a school. Aaron dies in the place of instruction, and from there the Levites are set apart to teach (cross-reference Deuteronomy 33:10, "they shall teach Jacob your rules").

The cosmic escalator in verse 17 — God of gods, Lord of lords, the great, the mighty, the awesome — is cited by Psalm 136:2–3 and picked up by Revelation 17:14 and 19:16 as a Christological title. The "high God" language of Deuteronomy grounds the apocalyptic Christology of the New Testament without any intervening step except the Psalter.

Verse 22's "seventy persons" into Egypt (citing Genesis 46:27) quietly preempts Exodus 1:5 and Acts 7:14's variant number (seventy-five, following the LXX). The chapter ends with a statistic that the New Testament will misremember — and the variance becomes itself a thread in the history of the text.
