# Psalm 71 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 71 is one of the few psalms in Book II without a superscription — no *le-David*, no *la-menatzeach*, no genre label. The Septuagint, sensing the gap, supplies *tō David, hyiōn Iōnadab kai tōn prōtōn aichmalōtisthentōn* ("of David, of the sons of Jonadab, and of the first ones taken captive") — a late attribution probably trying to read the psalm as a Maccabean or exilic composition, but no Hebrew witness preserves a heading. The Masoretic text begins simply with the petition: *be-cha Yahweh chasiti al-evosha le-olam* ("In you, O Lord, do I take refuge; let me never be put to shame," v. 1) — a line that is itself a near-quotation of Ps 31:1. The opening verses (71:1-3) reproduce Ps 31:1-3 with minor variation, and as the psalm proceeds, it threads in language from Pss 22, 35, 38, 40, and others. Hossfeld and Zenger (Hermeneia, *Psalms 2*) call Psalm 71 an "anthology psalm" or *Centonengebet* — a "patchwork prayer" composed of borrowed phrases.

This anthological quality has fed two converging hypotheses about the psalm's origin. First, Mitchell Dahood, Marvin Tate, and others have argued that Psalm 71 was composed late, by a worshipper deeply familiar with the existing Davidic Psalter, and built deliberately as a meditation woven from earlier psalmic threads. Second, John Goldingay and a number of older commentators (Delitzsch, Briggs) have noted that Psalm 71 reads as a continuation of Psalm 70 — that the two were originally a single composition, separated by editorial later. Some Hebrew manuscripts and the Septuagint indeed combine them, and the lack of a superscription on Psalm 71 (anomalous within the heavily-superscribed Davidic collection of Book II) is most easily explained if it was once joined to the preceding psalm. The textual evidence is thus consistent with a unit that once functioned as a single extended lament in two movements: the bare urgent cry of Ps 70, then the long autobiographical reflection of Ps 71.

The form-critical genre, on Gunkel's framework (*Einleitung in die Psalmen*, 1933), is again *Klagelied des Einzelnen* — individual lament — but with significant blending. Verses 1-13 unfold as classic lament (refuge appeal, petition, complaint, petition for enemies' confusion). Verses 14-24 turn toward thanksgiving and praise, anticipating deliverance with vows to declare God's *tzedaqah* (righteous acts). Westermann's modification of Gunkel — that lament psalms structurally move toward praise — fits Psalm 71 closely; some form critics classify it as a *Vertrauenspsalm* (psalm of trust) with strong lament features, while others call it a *gemischtes Klagelied* (mixed lament). What sets Psalm 71 apart from nearly every other lament in the Psalter is the explicit voice of old age. The psalmist names his life-stage three times: *al tashlikheni le-eit ziqnah* (אַל־תַּשְׁלִיכֵנִי לְעֵת זִקְנָה, "do not cast me off in the time of old age," v. 9); *kichloth kochi al ta'azveni* ("when my strength is spent, do not forsake me," v. 9b); and the climactic *gam-ad ziqnah ve-seivah Elohim al-ta'azveni* (גַּם־עַד־זִקְנָה וְשֵׂיבָה אֱלֹהִים אַל־תַּעַזְבֵנִי, "even to old age and gray hairs, O God, do not forsake me," v. 18). The complementary motif — the psalmist's reach back into earliest life — frames the prayer as a life-spanning testimony: *ki-atah tiqvati Adonay Yahweh mivtachi mi-ne'urai* ("for you, O Lord, are my hope, my trust from my youth," v. 5); *me-rechem ata gozri* (מֵרֶחֶם אַתָּה גֹזִי, "from my mother's womb you have been the one who took me out," v. 6); *Elohim limadtani mi-ne'urai* ("O God, you have taught me from my youth," v. 17). These womb-to-grave brackets are unique in the Psalter for their explicit autobiographical scope.

The Hebrew vocabulary is rich. *Ziqnah* (זִקְנָה) is the standard term for old age, paired here with *seivah* (שֵׂיבָה, "gray hair / hoary head"), the same word used of Abraham at burial (Gen 25:8) and Jacob's evocation of his approach to Sheol (Gen 42:38; 44:29). *Mofet* (מוֹפֵת, "portent, sign," v. 7) is striking — the psalmist describes himself as having become a *mofet* to many, the same word used of Isaiah's symbolic actions (Isa 8:18) and of the prophets as living signs to Israel. The image is dense: an aged sufferer whose life has become a public puzzle, watched, interpreted, perhaps mocked. *Tehom* (תְּהוֹם, "depths," v. 20) — *m'tehomot ha-aretz tashuv ta'aleni* ("from the depths of the earth you will bring me up again") — evokes the cosmological depths and connotes both Sheol and the primordial waters of Gen 1:2.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels for the aged-petitioner voice include some Egyptian instructional literature (the *Instruction of Ptahhotep* opens with the speaker's address from old age) and certain Mesopotamian dingir-šadibba prayers from elderly worshippers. But the womb-to-grave framing in covenant-relational terms is distinctively Israelite: the petitioner's history with God is the ground of his claim.

For text history: the Masoretic Text is well-preserved, with the Qumran Psalms scrolls (4QPsa, 11QPsa) preserving fragments. The Septuagint translation (Ps 70 LXX) generally follows the Hebrew, with the noted superscription expansion. There is little textual instability in the body of the psalm.

Book II of the Psalter (Pss 42-72) is the heart of the Elohistic Psalter — a sub-collection in which *Yahweh* has been editorially shifted to *Elohim*. Psalm 71 reflects this only partially; both names appear, with *Yahweh* and *Adonay Yahweh* (the latter at vv. 5, 16) actually somewhat prominent. This mixed pattern — and the borrowing of phrases from Yahwistic psalms in Book I — is part of why the editorial story is complex. The placement of Psalm 71, just before the great royal capstone of Psalm 72, has invited canonical readings (Wilson, Mitchell, Hossfeld-Zenger) in which the aged voice of 71 is set against the youthful kingly voice of 72: an aging speaker (perhaps figurally David at the end of his life) commits the future to a king (perhaps figurally Solomon). This generational succession is part of the editorial drama Book II builds toward its close.

## Lens 2: Literary Architecture

Robert Lowth's parallelism is the right starting frame, but Psalm 71 also rewards strophic and structural attention. The psalm divides into roughly four movements, each marked by changes in tone and address.

Verses 1-4 open with refuge language and petition. The synonymous parallelism is dense: *be-cha Yahweh chasiti* ("in you, O Lord, I take refuge") / *al-evosha le-olam* ("let me never be put to shame"). The refuge metaphors compound — *be-tzidqatcha tatzileni u-tefalteni* ("in your righteousness deliver me and rescue me," v. 2), *heyei li le-tzur ma'on* ("be to me a rock of refuge," v. 3), *ki sal'i u-metzudati atah* ("for you are my rock and my fortress," v. 3b). The Hebrew piles up the *tzur* (rock) imagery characteristic of Davidic refuge psalms (cf. Pss 18, 31, 62). Verbs in the imperative dominate: *natzal* (deliver), *palat* (rescue), *yashia* (save), *hatteh oznecha* (incline your ear).

Verses 5-13 are the heart of the lament's autobiographical movement. Here the psalm establishes its unique womb-to-old-age frame. Verse 5 is structurally pivotal: *ki-atah tiqvati* ("for you are my hope") — the *ki* opens grounds for the petition. Verses 5-9 weave a chiastic pattern of *from-youth* and *to-old-age* claims:

- A — *mi-ne'urai* ("from my youth," v. 5)
- B — *me-beten / mi-me'ei imi* ("from the womb / from my mother's bowels," v. 6)
- C — *mofet* ("portent, sign," v. 7)
- B' — *le-eit ziqnah / kichloth kochi* ("in old age / when my strength is spent," v. 9)
- A' — *ne'urai* implicit in the contrast structure

The complaint of vv. 10-11 introduces the enemies' speech in direct quotation: *Elohim azavo* ("God has forsaken him") — a taunt that picks up the same vocabulary the psalmist uses in petition (*al ta'azveni*, "do not forsake me"), creating an inclusio of dread. Verse 12 brings the petition to its highest pitch with a near-quotation of Ps 70:1: *Elohim al-tirchaq mimeni Elohai le-ezrati chushah* ("O God, be not far from me; O my God, make haste to help me") — a bridge that confirms the literary tie to Psalm 70.

Verses 14-21 turn dramatically. *Va-ani tamid ayachel ve-hosafti al-kol-tehilatecha* ("but I will hope continually, and I will praise you yet more and more," v. 14) — the *va-ani* ("but I") signals the speaker's pivot, and the verbs shift from imperative-petition to imperfect-of-confidence. The theme becomes proclamation across generations: *ad-aggid zero'acha le-dor le-khol yavo gevuratecha* ("until I proclaim your arm to the [next] generation, your might to all who are to come," v. 18). The aged speaker's mission becomes intergenerational testimony — his life, including its troubles, is data the next generation needs.

Verses 22-24 close with vow of praise, harp and lyre imagery, and a final note of enemies-shamed (an inclusio with v. 13). The instruments — *kinor* (כִּנּוֹר, lyre/harp) and *nevel* (נֵבֶל, harp) — are temple instruments (1 Chron 25). The closing *Qedosh Yisrael* ("Holy One of Israel," v. 22) is a divine title strongly associated with Isaiah and rare in the Psalter.

The full structural pattern resolves to: petition (1-4), autobiographical complaint (5-13), confidence and intergenerational vow (14-21), praise and conclusion (22-24) — a movement that exactly fits Westermann's description of the lament's progression from petition through confidence to praise.

There is no *selah* in Psalm 71. The psalm has no formal refrain but does have repeating motifs — *al-ta'azveni* / *al-tirchaq* / *al-tashlikheni* (do not forsake / be not far / do not cast off), and the *tzedaqah* (righteousness) thread that recurs at vv. 2, 15, 16, 19, 24. *Tzedaqah* is the structural keyword: the divine attribute on which the psalmist bases his claim and which his life is now commissioned to declare.

## Lens 3: Within the Larger Story — Reception

Jewish reception begins with the rabbis' attention to the absent superscription. *Midrash Tehillim* (Buber) and the Talmudic discussion in *Berakhot* note the unusual lack of attribution and consider whether Psalm 71 should be read as a continuation of Psalm 70. *Rashi* treats the psalm as Davidic by extension, reading the womb-to-old-age frame as autobiographical to David fleeing Absalom in his later years. *Ibn Ezra* is more cautious; he notes the anonymity and reads the psalm as a generic prayer for any aged sufferer. *Radak* (David Kimhi) attends to the literary craft, noting the careful interweaving of phrases from earlier psalms and reading this as the meditation of a mature worshipper. *Ramban* and the Spanish school tend to read it Davidically, fitting the autobiographical motifs to David's own movement from anointed youth to embattled aged king.

Modern Jewish reception finds in Psalm 71 a meditation on aging that has gathered fresh significance. Abraham Joshua Heschel's *The Insecurity of Freedom* contains a famous late essay, "To Grow in Wisdom," in which Heschel argues against the modern marginalization of the elderly and turns to the Hebrew tradition's veneration of age. Psalm 71's *al-tashlikheni le-eit ziqnah* becomes for Heschel a corrective to American culture's cult of youth. Jon Levenson and Nahum Sarna both note the psalm's distinctive contribution to the Psalter's voice: this is the prayer of someone who has lived long enough to have evidence to recite. Martin Buber finds in vv. 5-6 ("from my mother's womb you have been my strength") the I-Thou relationship's deep temporal foundation — the petitioner does not begin the relationship but discovers it. The psalm has not entered standard Jewish daily liturgy, but verses 9 and 18 are sometimes prayed in the *Bikkur Cholim* tradition and at the bedside of the elderly.

Christian reception begins with Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos*, where Psalm 71 is read as Christ speaking through his aging members, the Church praying its perduring fidelity through generations. Athanasius assigns the psalm to those persecuted in old age. Cassiodorus's *Expositio Psalmorum* dwells on the *tzedaqah* (rendered *iustitia*) thread, reading it as the divine righteousness that grounds the petitioner's claim and that the psalm exists to declare. Aquinas, in his commentary on the Psalms, takes the womb-to-old-age frame as the figural arc of the Christian's progress in grace from baptism to glory. Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* (1519-21) finds in v. 9 a meditation on the *theologia crucis* — the aged believer's confidence is not in his strength but in the God who has carried him from the womb. Calvin's 1557 commentary emphasizes the pedagogical function of v. 18: the elderly believer's task is to pass on the testimony of God's righteousness to the next generation.

Modern critical scholarship has converged on Psalm 71 as the Psalter's most explicit reflection on aging. Hermann Gunkel classified it as a mixed lament/trust composition. Sigmund Mowinckel, with his cult-functional approach, situated it in temple worship. Claus Westermann's structural analysis of lament's movement to praise fits Psalm 71 closely. Hans-Joachim Kraus (BKAT) emphasizes the *Anthologiecharakter* — the anthological nature — of its phrasing. Hossfeld and Zenger (Hermeneia) read it editorially against Psalm 72, the aged voice handing the kingdom to the young king. John Goldingay's *Baker Commentary on the Old Testament Wisdom and Psalms* treats Pss 70 and 71 as a paired unit and offers a sustained reflection on the pastoral significance of the aged voice. Marvin Tate (WBC), Beth Tanner (NICOT), and W. Dennis Tucker have followed similar lines. Walter Brueggemann's *The Message of the Psalms* and *Spirituality of the Psalms* names Psalm 71 a reorientation psalm — the suffering that has been processed and turned into intergenerational testimony. Patrick Miller's *They Cried to the Lord* highlights the "old age" verses as the Psalter's specific resource for the pastoral care of elderly believers. Bill Brown's *Seeing the Psalms* attends to the body imagery (womb, gray hairs, spent strength). Beth Tanner's *NICOT* commentary devotes special attention to the psalm's voice as a corrective to youth-dominant Psalter readings.

In Christian gerontology, Stanley Hauerwas's *Suffering Presence* and Kathleen Fischer's *Winter Grace: Spirituality and Aging* both turn to Psalm 71 as scripture's primary voice for the spiritual life of aging. The Catholic *anointing of the sick* (CCC §1499-1532) draws on the broader lament tradition; Psalm 71 is among the texts read at viaticum and during the *anointing*. The psalm has not entered standard daily Christian liturgy with the prominence of Psalm 70's first verse, but it is regularly used in pastoral care of the elderly and the dying across Catholic, Anglican, and Reformed traditions.

The New Testament does not directly cite Psalm 71, but several themes echo. Paul's "I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith" (2 Tim 4:7) breathes the same intergenerational-testimony spirit. Simeon's *Nunc dimittis* in Luke 2:29-32 — the aged man at the Temple commissioned to announce the next thing God is doing — is the closest New Testament analogue.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — What's at Stake

**Tension 1: The aging-and-prayer tradition vs. modern American age-marginalization.** Psalm 71's *al-tashlikheni le-eit ziqnah* — "do not cast me off in the time of old age" — is a petition that lands very differently in cultures that honor the elderly than in cultures that don't. *Position one — the psalm exposes a cultural failure:* Heschel, Hauerwas, Fischer, and others argue that the psalm names what modern Western (and especially American) culture does to its elderly: warehouses them, removes them from public life, treats their wisdom as obsolete. The psalmist's *al-tashlikheni* exposes a cultural failure mode that Christian and Jewish communities should resist. *Position two — the psalm names a universal anxiety:* Some readers (including parts of the existential and philosophical-theology tradition) read the psalm not as a critique of any particular culture but as the universal voice of a creature who must learn to age. Every culture struggles with aging, and the psalm's gift is the prayer-language for the universal human condition. *Position three — the psalm models intergenerational responsibility:* A third position, drawing on v. 18's *until I proclaim your might to another generation*, reads the psalm as making aging a *vocation* rather than a *complaint*. The aged believer's task is testimony; the community's task is to receive it. Steelmanning each: the cultural-critique view honors the empirical reality that modern American culture does treat the elderly badly, and reads scripture as a counter-practice; the universal-anxiety view honors that aging is hard everywhere, and resists turning the psalm into a culture-war proof text; the vocational view honors what the psalm itself does — refuses to leave the speaker in despair and turns him toward proclamation. *Where this leaves us:* the psalm holds all three. The *al-tashlikheni* names a real fear that real cultures (including ours) make worse. The fear is universal. And the answer the psalm models is not retirement but commission: the aged believer's life becomes intergenerational testimony. This means the contemporary American church has both a critique to receive (we have largely accepted secular age-marginalization) and a vocation to recover (the elderly are our living witnesses).

**Tension 2: Corporate vs. individual reading, and the Pss 70-71-72 editorial sequence.** Hossfeld and Zenger, Wilson, and Mitchell read Psalms 70-71-72 as an editorial unit: an aged voice (71) bracketed by an urgent cry (70) and a royal psalm (72), staging a generational succession from the dying David to the young Solomon and ultimately to the messianic king. *Position one — corporate-canonical:* The psalm is best read in its canonical setting as Israel's voice through the figural David in old age, handing the kingdom to the next king. The "I" is at once Davidic, exilic-community, and messianic-typological. *Position two — individual-original:* The psalm is best read as an individual's prayer in old age, possibly anonymous, possibly Davidic-extended, but functioning primarily as a personal lament whose canonical placement is editorial overlay on a more particular original meaning. *Position three — both/and (canonical-pluriform reading):* Brevard Childs and the canonical-criticism tradition argue that the canonical and individual readings are both legitimate; the canonical text invites both, and the church has historically prayed both at once. Steelmanning each: the corporate-canonical view fits the editorial evidence (the lack of superscription, the Pss 70-71-72 sequence, the Wilson hypothesis about Books I-III), and honors how the psalm has actually functioned in synagogue and church; the individual-original view fits the autobiographical specificity of vv. 5-9 and resists overreading editorial design into every detail; the both/and view honors the canonical text as it stands while preserving the original cry. *Where this leaves us:* the canonical-pluriform reading is the most honest. Psalm 71 was probably composed by an individual in old age, anthologically reusing earlier psalms, possibly attached to Psalm 70 as a single composition; was placed editorially before Psalm 72 to stage a generational handoff; and has been prayed in synagogue and church as the voice of the aged believer, of Israel, of David, and (in Christian reading) of Christ. The closing question is whether contemporary worshipping communities can hold all those layers — or whether we have flattened scripture's polyvocal depth into the single dimension of "what it meant to the original author."

**Closing question:** What does your community do with its elderly — and what does Psalm 71 reveal about whether you are receiving the testimony scripture says they are commissioned to give?

## The Thread

- **Psalm 22:9-10** — *for you took me from the womb; you made me trust in you at my mother's breasts.* The womb-language Psalm 71 picks up, here on the lips of the suffering servant.
- **Psalm 31:1-3** — *In you, O Lord, I take refuge.* Psalm 71's opening is a near-quotation of Psalm 31; the two psalms read together show how the Psalter's lament voices reuse one another.
- **Isaiah 46:3-4** — *Listen to me… you who have been borne by me from before your birth, carried from the womb; even to your old age I am he, and to gray hairs I will carry you.* The closest prophetic echo, with the same womb-to-gray-hairs frame.
- **Luke 2:29-32** — *Now you are letting your servant depart in peace.* Simeon's prayer in the Temple — the aged voice commissioned to announce the next generation, the New Testament's closest analogue to Psalm 71.
