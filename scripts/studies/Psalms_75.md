# Psalm 75 — The Cup in God's Hand

## Lens 1: Historical World

Psalm 75 carries the layered superscription *la-menatzeach al-tashchet, mizmor le-Asaph shir* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ אַל־תַּשְׁחֵת מִזְמוֹר לְאָסָף שִׁיר), "to the choirmaster, according to Do Not Destroy, a psalm of Asaph, a song." It is the third psalm of the Asaphite cluster (Pss 73-83) and remains within the Elohistic Psalter, addressing *Elohim* (v. 1) and *YHWH* (v. 8) in tension. The phrase *al-tashchet* ("do not destroy") appears as a tune-direction on four psalms (Pss 57, 58, 59, 75), perhaps drawing on the petition of Deuteronomy 9:26 ("do not destroy your people") or 1 Samuel 26:9 (David's restraint of Abishai); the consensus is that it names a known melody rather than a thematic instruction, though the irony of using a "do not destroy" tune for a psalm about cutting off the horns of the wicked has not escaped commentators.

Hermann Gunkel's form-critical taxonomy classifies Psalm 75 as a *Mischgattung* — a mixed-genre psalm — combining elements of communal thanksgiving, prophetic divine speech, and hymnic doxology. Sigmund Mowinckel placed it in the cult-functional category of the prophetic-cultic liturgy, where a temple prophet or Levitical singer would deliver a divine oracle in the first-person voice of God. Erhard Gerstenberger's *Psalms* commentary treats vv. 2-5 as a stylized prophetic insertion into a thanksgiving framework. The brevity and the abrupt voice-shifts — community thanksgiving (v. 1), divine first-person speech (vv. 2-5), wisdom-style declaration (vv. 6-8), and personal vow (vv. 9-10) — all point to a liturgical setting in which different voices alternate.

The dating is contested but tends to be placed in the late monarchic or post-exilic period. Hossfeld and Zenger tilt toward a post-exilic Asaphite editorial composition, drawing on older oracle material. Hans-Joachim Kraus reads the psalm as a Festival composition, possibly tied to the autumnal New Year cult. John Goldingay treats the dating as undeterminable but reads the psalm as functioning across post-exilic Second Temple worship. The references to cosmic order (v. 3, "I keep steady its pillars") and to the cup of wrath (v. 8) place the psalm within the prophetic-eschatological imagination shared by Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Habakkuk.

The Hebrew terms anchor the psalm's argument. The opening *qarov shimkha* (קָרוֹב שִׁמְךָ, v. 1) — "your name is near" — uses the adjective *qarov* (near), which carries strong covenantal force throughout the Hebrew Bible (cf. Deut 4:7, "what great nation is there that has a god so near to it as the LORD our God is to us whenever we call upon him?"). The nearness of the divine name is itself the ground of thanksgiving. Verse 2's *moed* (מוֹעֵד), "set time," is the same lexeme used in Habakkuk 2:3 of the appointed time for the vision's fulfillment, and is a key prophetic-eschatological term. The phrase *eshpot mesharim* (אֶשְׁפֹּט מֵישָׁרִים), "I will judge with equity," uses the noun *meshar* (uprightness, equity), the root of which is *yashar* (straight, upright) — judgment as the straightening of what has become crooked.

The horn imagery dominates the psalm. The Hebrew *qeren* (קֶרֶן, horn) appears at vv. 4, 5, and 10. *Qarnot resha'im* (קַרְנוֹת רְשָׁעִים, v. 10) — "the horns of the wicked" — uses the symbolic horn-of-power, a widespread ANE image for royal or military strength (cf. Deut 33:17, 1 Sam 2:10, Pss 89:17, 92:10, 132:17, Dan 7-8). Lifting up the horn signifies arrogant self-exaltation; cutting off the horn signifies the disempowering of pride. The phrase *be-tzavar atak* (בְּצַוָּאר עָתָק, v. 5), "with haughty neck" or "stiff neck," uses *atak* (insolent, defiant), the same root used in 1 Samuel 2:3 of "arrogant words."

The cup imagery of v. 8 is one of the most theologically charged in the Hebrew Bible. *Kos be-yad-YHWH* (כוֹס בְּיַד־יְהוָה) — "in the hand of the LORD there is a cup" — establishes the *cup of divine wrath* trope that runs through the prophets and into the New Testament. The cup contains *yayin chamar* (יַיִן חָמַר, "foaming wine"; the verb *chamar* may also mean "red" or "fermented"), and the wicked drink it *yimtzu yishtu kol-rish'ei-aretz* — they drain it to the dregs (*shemar*, the lees, the bitter sediment at the bottom). The image's ANE background includes the Mesopotamian *ḫamru*-festival cup and the Ugaritic mythology of the gods' banquet, but the prophetic appropriation in Israel makes the cup a symbol of the divine sentence executed in history.

ANE parallels for divine judgment-oracles include the prophetic letters from Mari, the Neo-Assyrian oracles to kings, and the Egyptian execration texts. None matches Psalm 75's combination of prophetic divine speech embedded within a thanksgiving liturgy; the form is distinctively Israelite. The cup-imagery has Mesopotamian and Canaanite analogs but reaches its theological depth specifically in Israelite prophecy, where it becomes the carrier of historical-covenantal judgment.

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of Psalm 75, and the text is largely consistent with the Masoretic Text. The Septuagint (LXX Ps 74) renders *al-tashchet* as *peri tou mē diaphtheirēs*, transliterating the Hebrew sense. The Targum gives the psalm a strong eschatological reading, identifying the *moed* of v. 2 with the messianic age.

## Lens 2: Literature

Lowth's parallelism patterns the psalm tightly. The opening — "we give thanks to you, O God; / we give thanks, for your name is near" — uses *staircase parallelism*, where the second line repeats and intensifies the first ("we give thanks... we give thanks"). The repetition is liturgical, perhaps antiphonal between leader and congregation.

The psalm divides into a clean four-strophe structure. Strophe I (v. 1): the corporate thanksgiving. Strophe II (vv. 2-5): the divine oracle in first-person voice. Strophe III (vv. 6-8): the wisdom-style commentary on the oracle, including the cup-image. Strophe IV (vv. 9-10): the personal vow and the divine-final-action declaration. The single *selah* falls at the end of v. 3 — pausing precisely at the moment when God declares "it is I who keep steady its pillars" — which is the structural foundation of the whole psalm. The placement of the *selah* is liturgically pointed: the *selah* invites contemplation of cosmic stability before the oracle proceeds to address the boastful.

The psalm's voice-shifts are its most striking literary feature. Verse 1 is corporate ("we give thanks"). Verses 2-5 are divine ("at the set time *that I appoint*..."). Verses 6-8 are sapiential third-person ("it is God who executes judgment"). Verses 9-10 are first-person individual ("I will declare... I will sing..."), though v. 10's "I will cut off" is sometimes read as continuation of the divine voice and sometimes as the worshipper's own oath in the role of judge-deputy. Most modern commentators (Goldingay, Tate, Hossfeld-Zenger) read v. 10 as resuming the divine voice, framing the psalm with divine first-person at vv. 2-5 and v. 10. Read this way, the psalm is a chiasm: thanksgiving (v. 1) → divine oracle (vv. 2-5) → wisdom commentary (vv. 6-8) → individual vow (v. 9) → divine final word (v. 10).

The horn-imagery and the lifting-up motif structure the psalm rhetorically. The verb *rum* (to be high, to lift up) recurs at vv. 4, 5, 6, 7, 10, threading the psalm together. The wicked are not to lift up their horns (v. 4); lifting up does not come from east or west or wilderness (v. 6); God lifts up one and puts down another (v. 7); the horns of the wicked are cut off, but the horns of the righteous are lifted up (v. 10). The structural inversion — the wicked who lift themselves are brought down, while the righteous who do not lift themselves are lifted by God — embodies the wisdom-tradition's central claim about the trajectory of pride and humility.

Inclusio and key-word work bind the psalm. The opening *qarov shimkha* ("your name is near," v. 1) is matched by the closing *Elohei Ya'akov* ("the God of Jacob," v. 9), framing the psalm with two references to the divine name. The verb *yadah* (to give thanks, praise) opens v. 1 and is reprised in v. 9's *azammerah* (I will sing praises). The cup-image at v. 8 stands as the central concrete metaphor, occupying the rhetorical center of the wisdom-strophe.

## Lens 3: Larger Story

Within Wilson's Psalter-editing thesis, Psalm 75 sits in Book III between the theodicy crisis of Ps 73 and the temple-destruction lament of Ps 74 on the one side, and the Songs of Zion (Pss 76, 84, 87) on the other. The Asaphite cluster's editorial logic places Psalm 75 as the liturgical-prophetic counterweight to Psalm 74's despair: Ps 74 asks "how long?" and Ps 75 answers "at the set time that I appoint." Whether the editorial sequence is chronologically deliberate or thematically ordered, the juxtaposition functions theologically. Wilson's *Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* (1985) traces Books I-III as the rise-and-crisis of Davidic kingship, with Pss 89's lament at the breaking-point. Psalm 75's confession of divine judgment at an appointed time fits the broader Book III structure of waiting for vindication when historical circumstances have collapsed.

In Jewish tradition, Psalm 75 has been read primarily as a meditation on divine providence and eschatological judgment. The *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 75 reads the *moed* (set time) of v. 2 as the messianic age, and the cup-image of v. 8 as the cup that the nations will drink in the eschatological judgment. Rashi glosses v. 6 — "not from the east or from the west" — as a denial that human geographic or political power can lift up; lifting up is a divine prerogative. Ibn Ezra reads the divine speech as an oracle delivered by a Levitical prophet in the Second Temple liturgy. Radak emphasizes the wisdom-tradition framing: the psalm teaches the temporariness of wicked exaltation and the certainty of righteous vindication. Nachmanides connects the psalm to the eschatological judgment imagery of the prophets, particularly Habakkuk 2 and Isaiah 51.

The cup-image has been a major locus for Jewish hermeneutical reflection. Levenson, in *Creation and the Persistence of Evil*, reads the cup as the carrier of partial-eschatological judgment, executed in history but not yet completed. Avivah Zornberg in her literary readings treats the cup as the dark complement to the Passover cups of redemption — judgment and redemption together as the structure of biblical eschatology. The Talmudic tradition reads the cup-imagery in dialogue with Isa 51 and Jer 25, recognizing a prophetic intertext.

In Christian tradition, Psalm 75 has been read primarily through a Christological lens, particularly because of the cup-image. Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 74 (LXX) reads the cup of v. 8 as the cup of divine judgment that Christ drinks in his passion (Mark 14:36, "let this cup pass from me") — Christ taking the wrath that the wicked deserve to drink. The *moed* of v. 2 is read as the appointed time of Christ's coming, both first and second. The horn-imagery is read as the eschatological reversal accomplished in the resurrection. Athanasius, Cassiodorus, and Aquinas follow similar patterns. Aquinas in particular reads the cup typologically — connecting Ps 75:8 with Mark 14:36, Matt 26:39, and Rev 14:10 — as the unified biblical witness to a cup of wrath that Christ drinks on behalf of those who would otherwise drain it to the dregs.

Calvin's 1557 commentary on Psalm 75 takes a more pastoral-political angle, reading the psalm as a model for Christian rulers and citizens who are tempted to seek lifting-up by political maneuvering. The psalm's "not from the east or from the west" (v. 6) is for Calvin a rebuke of all human striving for self-exaltation. Luther's psalm-lectures read the divine first-person speech of vv. 2-5 as a model for prophetic preaching: the preacher must learn to speak in God's voice, declaring God's judgment without flinching.

Modern scholarship on Psalm 75 has been slimmer than on its more famous neighbors. Hermann Gunkel's form-critical placement and Sigmund Mowinckel's cult-functional reading set the basic frames. Claus Westermann, Hans-Joachim Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, John Goldingay, Marvin Tate, J. Clinton McCann, Beth Tanner, and Gordon Wenham have all contributed. The cup-of-wrath tradition has received its own monograph treatment in James McKeown's work and in commentaries on Habakkuk and Jeremiah. Brueggemann places the psalm within the prophetic-cultic intersection that he sees as central to the Psalter's theological function.

The New Testament use of the cup-image is extensive. Mark 10:38-39 — Jesus' question to James and John, "are you able to drink the cup that I drink?" — draws on the prophetic cup-of-wrath tradition that Psalm 75 embodies. Mark 14:36's Gethsemane prayer — "remove this cup from me" — places Jesus in the position of the wicked of Ps 75:8, drinking the cup that should belong to others. Revelation 14:10 — "he also will drink the wine of God's wrath, poured full strength into the cup of his anger" — explicitly reprises the Ps 75 imagery in the eschatological judgment scene. The trajectory through the New Testament makes Psalm 75:8 one of the most theologically generative single verses in the Psalter for Christological-soteriological reflection.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

Two tensions press on every reader.

The first tension is the **Christological reading of the cup**. Psalm 75:8 is the locus classicus for the Christian doctrine of substitutionary atonement: Christ drinks the cup of divine wrath that should have been drained by the wicked.

A first position is the **classical Reformed substitutionary** reading: the cup of wrath represents the just judgment of God against sin; Christ drinks it on the cross in the place of the elect; the soteriological effect is propitiation. This reading is held by Calvin, the Reformed scholastic tradition, and contemporary defenders like John Stott (*The Cross of Christ*) and Steve Jeffery and Mike Ovey (*Pierced for Our Transgressions*).

A second position is the **Christus Victor / participatory** reading: Christ drinks the cup not primarily as a substitute but as a participant in the human condition, defeating the powers of sin and death from within. Gustaf Aulén's *Christus Victor*, and contemporary theologians like Fleming Rutledge in *The Crucifixion*, blend substitutionary and participatory dimensions. On this reading, Psalm 75:8's cup is fulfilled in the cross not as a transactional payment but as a victorious entry into the depth of divine judgment.

A third position is the **non-violent atonement** reading developed by J. Denny Weaver, Mark Heim, and others. On this reading, the cup-image must be detached from divine violence; Christ does not drink wrath because God does not pour wrath; instead, Christ exposes and absorbs human violence, showing the divine character of nonviolent love. This reading has costs — it must reread Psalm 75 (and Hab 2 and Isa 51 and Rev 14) against their plain sense — but it pursues a theological coherence that the violent-divine-judgment reading struggles with.

To steelman the non-violent atonement position: a God who pours wrath into a cup and forces creatures to drink it raises hard moral questions about the divine character. The biblical-prophetic tradition can be read as the inspired but partial articulation of the divine that Christ corrects rather than confirms. Christ's cry in Gethsemane — "let this cup pass" — can be read not as horror at the divine wrath but as horror at the cup of human violence and accumulated sin that he must enter. The cost of this reading is that it requires an explicit hermeneutical move against the surface of the prophetic-cultic texts; the gain is that it produces a more morally intelligible doctrine of God. Most of the Christian tradition has held the substitutionary reading, but the non-violent reading has become a serious theological alternative in the last fifty years and deserves engagement on its own terms.

The second tension is the **theology of judgment-at-appointed-time** versus the **expectation of immediate justice**. Psalm 75's *moed* (set time) is structurally important: judgment is not absent, but it is deferred to a divinely-determined moment.

A first position is the **traditional eschatological** reading: the prosperity of the wicked is real but temporary; final judgment is certain but not yet; the worshipper waits in patience and hope. This is the reading shared by Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin, and the broader classical tradition.

A second position is the **realized-eschatological** reading: the *moed* of v. 2 is not deferred but already inaugurated in Christ's first coming (or, in non-Christian readings, in particular historical moments of vindication). The cup is being drunk now; the horns are being cut off now; the worshipper's vow at v. 10 is to participate in that present-tense judgment. C. H. Dodd's realized eschatology, modified through Wright and others into "inaugurated eschatology," takes some form of this position.

A third position is the **process-eschatological** reading: judgment is not a single appointed moment but the cumulative weight of cosmic-moral consequence working its way through history. Process theologians (Whitehead, Hartshorne, Cobb, Griffin) read the *moed* not as an eschatological event but as the divine-aim shaping every actual occasion. On this reading, Psalm 75's confession is that judgment is built into the structure of reality, not awaited as a discrete future event.

The closing question — implicit in v. 9's vow ("I will declare it forever") — is whether the worshipper can wait for the *moed* without despair when the wicked still drain the cup of celebration rather than the cup of wrath. The psalm itself does not resolve this; it confesses that the cup is in God's hand and asserts that the worshipper will declare it "forever." The vow is liturgical-eschatological: declared in advance of full vindication, sustained by the confidence that the divine name is "near" (v. 1).

Where this leaves us: Psalm 75 is the Psalter's most concentrated meditation on the appointed time of divine judgment. Its central image — the cup in God's hand — has shaped Jewish eschatology and Christian Christology more decisively than its brevity might suggest. The psalm refuses to claim that judgment is fully present; refuses to claim that judgment is permanently absent; and stakes its theological position on the *moed*, the set time, when God will act.

The closing question for any reader: when you face arrogant power that will not accept the wisdom of "do not lift up your horn," what is your liturgical response — what cup, what vow, what nearness of the divine name, are you willing to declare while the *moed* remains future?

## The Thread

The cup waits in God's hand and the appointed time waits in God's calendar; in the meantime the worshipper declares the wisdom that lifting-up does not come from east or west but from the God who steadies the pillars of the world.
