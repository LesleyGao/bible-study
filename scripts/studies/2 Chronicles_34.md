# 2 Chronicles 34 — Josiah's Reform: The Book in the Temple, the Prophetess in the Second Quarter

## Lens 1 — The Historical World Behind the Text

Second Chronicles 34 narrates the reform of King Josiah of Judah (640-609 BC), the last great Davidic reformer before the descent to exile. The chapter parallels 2 Kings 22-23 but with the Chronicler's distinctive reframings: an earlier and more gradual reform timeline, a more elaborate Levitical involvement, and a more explicitly all-Israel reach. The chapter is anchored on three historical events: Josiah's reform begun in his youth, the discovery of the *sefer ha-torah* (סֵפֶר הַתּוֹרָה, "the book of the Law") in the temple in 622 BC during repair work, and the consultation of the prophetess *Chuldah* (חֻלְדָּה, Huldah).

The Chronicler is writing in the late fifth or early fourth century BC, working with Kings as primary source. The most striking divergence from Kings is the timeline. 2 Kings 22-23 collapses Josiah's reform into a single sequence beginning in his eighteenth year (when he was twenty-six) — the law-book discovery prompts the reform. The Chronicler stretches the reform across multiple stages: Josiah at eight begins to reign (34:1); at sixteen ("in the eighth year of his reign, while he was yet a boy") begins to seek the God of David (34:3a); at twenty (in the twelfth year) begins to purge Judah of the *bamot*, *asherim*, and idols (34:3b); at twenty-six (in the eighteenth year) repairs the temple, finds the law book, and consults Huldah (34:8-28).

This earlier-and-more-gradual timeline matters for two reasons. First, it fits the Chronicler's theological pattern: Josiah's heart turns toward YHWH before the law book is found, so the law book confirms a pre-existing direction rather than triggering a sudden conversion. Second, it has significant historical plausibility. The Assyrian empire weakened dramatically between 640 BC (Josiah's accession) and 612 BC (the fall of Nineveh). As Assyrian control over the Levant collapsed, Josiah had increasing latitude to act against the Assyrian-imposed astral cults (the "host of heaven" worship Manasseh installed and Amon continued). A reform that began cautiously around 632 BC (Josiah's sixteenth year) and accelerated by 622 BC (his twenty-sixth year) tracks with the geopolitical decline of Assyria better than a sudden reform in 622 BC alone.

The most significant historical-critical question concerns the **identity of the law book**. What was *sefer ha-torah* that Hilkiah found?

The standard mainstream-critical answer, going back to W.M.L. de Wette's 1805 dissertation, is that the book was **some form of Deuteronomy**, probably an early stratum of the Deuteronomic legal core (Deut 12-26 or a subset). The de Wette hypothesis has remained the dominant view for over two centuries because the evidence is unusually strong:

(1) Josiah's reforms — central-sanctuary worship, destruction of *bamot*, Passover at Jerusalem only — track precisely with the legislation of Deuteronomy 12-16, especially the centralization-of-worship demand of Deut 12:5-14 (*the place that the LORD your God will choose*). No other Pentateuchal book makes this centralization demand.

(2) The treaty-curse language Huldah cites ("all the curses that are written in the book," 34:24) matches the curse section of Deuteronomy 28 in genre and severity. The curses of Lev 26 are similar but less elaborately structured.

(3) The covenant-renewal ceremony Josiah leads (34:30-32) matches the structure of the Deuteronomic covenant ceremonies (cf. Deut 27-30).

(4) The book is small enough that Shaphan can read it aloud to the king and Josiah can hear it through and respond emotionally in a single session. This rules out the entire Pentateuch but fits a Deuteronomy-sized scroll.

Modern Pentateuchal scholarship (Eckart Otto, *Das Deuteronomium im Pentateuch und Hexateuch*, 2000; Bernard Levinson, *Deuteronomy and the Hermeneutics of Legal Innovation*, 1997; Bernard Levinson and Jeffrey Stackert; in earlier generations, Moshe Weinfeld, *Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School*, 1972) has refined the de Wette hypothesis substantially. The current critical consensus is that an *Urdeuteronomium* (proto-Deuteronomy), probably comprising something like Deut 12-26 plus a curse-blessing frame, was composed (or significantly redacted) in the seventh century BC, perhaps by reforming priests and scribes in Jerusalem who incorporated older northern traditions (some elements may go back to the Hezekian period or earlier). This proto-Deuteronomy was either deposited in the temple at some point (perhaps during Manasseh's reign, hidden during the apostate years) or — in a more skeptical version — *composed* during Josiah's reign and *presented* as recovered ancient law to give it authority. The first reading is more historically plausible; the second reading runs into the problem that fabrication of this scale would have been very difficult to maintain.

Conservative-evangelical scholars (Daniel Block, *The Gospel According to Moses*; J.G. McConville, *Deuteronomy*, AOTC) accept that Deuteronomy is the book in view but argue for an earlier (Mosaic-era) compositional date with later editorial framing, and they emphasize that the *finding* of the book in 622 BC means it had been lost (during Manasseh's apostasy) and now recovered, not composed.

The Chronicler is not interested in this question. His phrase *sefer torat YHWH be-yad Moshe* (סֵפֶר תּוֹרַת־יְהוָה בְּיַד־מֹשֶׁה, "the book of the Law of the LORD given through Moses," 34:14) treats it straightforwardly as Mosaic Torah. The historical-critical question is real, but the Chronicler's claim is a theological one: this book is the authoritative voice of YHWH through Moses, and Josiah's response to it — tearing his clothes, seeking prophetic word, leading covenant renewal — is the model.

The figure of **Huldah** is historically remarkable. She is one of only a small handful of named female prophets in the Hebrew Bible — alongside Miriam (Exod 15:20), Deborah (Judg 4:4), Noadiah (Neh 6:14, briefly), and Isaiah's wife (Isa 8:3). The chapter notes in passing that she "lived in Jerusalem in the Second Quarter" (*ba-mishneh*, בַּמִּשְׁנֶה, 34:22) — apparently a residential district added to Jerusalem in the late eighth or seventh century, perhaps the area expanded after the influx of refugees from the fall of Samaria. Huldah is married to Shallum, the keeper of the royal wardrobe — meaning she has both prophetic authority and access to royal-household networks. The text's casualness about her gender is itself striking: Hilkiah the high priest, Ahikam, Abdon, Shaphan the secretary, and Asaiah the king's servant — five high-ranking officials including the high priest — go to *her* to inquire of YHWH. Jeremiah is active in this same period; Zephaniah is probably active. They could have gone to either male prophet. They went to Huldah.

Phyllis Trible (*God and the Rhetoric of Sexuality*, 1978; *Texts of Terror*, 1984) and Wilda Gafney (*Womanist Midrash*, 2017; *Daughters of Miriam: Women Prophets in Ancient Israel*, 2008) have both written extensively on Huldah's significance. Gafney emphasizes that Huldah's oracle is decisive — what she says becomes scripture. The reform that follows is the reform Huldah authorized. Sara Japhet, Ralph Klein, and H.G.M. Williamson all note Huldah's prominence without making a large feminist point of it; the Chronicler simply treats her as authoritative.

## Lens 2 — The Literature of the Text

The chapter is a triptych: reform begins (34:1-7), reform deepens through book-discovery (34:8-21), reform consummates through covenant (34:22-33). At the literary center is the figure of Huldah and her oracle.

The opening section (34:1-7) deploys the Chronicler's signature timeline structure. *Be-shenat shemoneh le-malkho ve-hu na'ar* (בִּשְׁנַת שְׁמוֹנֶה לְמָלְכוֹ וְהוּא נַעַר, "in the eighth year of his reign, while he was a boy," 34:3) — the *na'ar* ("boy, youth") detail is theological: Josiah is *young* and already seeking. The reform reaches into "the cities of Manasseh, Ephraim, Simeon, and as far as Naphtali" (34:6) — the all-Israel reach the Chronicler insists on. The graphic detail "he burned the bones of the priests on their altars" (34:5) fulfills a prophecy from 1 Kings 13:2 (the unnamed man of God's word against Jeroboam's altar at Bethel: *a son shall be born to the house of David, Josiah by name*) — the Chronicler does not cite the prophecy explicitly but the reader of Kings will hear the echo.

The discovery sequence (34:8-21) is paced for narrative tension. Shaphan the secretary, Maaseiah the city governor, and Joah the recorder are sent to Hilkiah the high priest to disburse the temple-repair fund. The Levites (the Chronicler's distinctive emphasis — Kings just says "the keepers of the threshold") have been collecting money "from Manasseh and Ephraim … from all Judah and Benjamin and from the inhabitants of Jerusalem." During the work, *be-hotsi'am et ha-kesef* (בְּהוֹצִיאָם אֶת־הַכֶּסֶף, "while they were bringing out the money," 34:14), Hilkiah finds the *sefer torat-YHWH be-yad-Moshe*. The Chronicler is careful to keep the discovery incidental — they were not looking for it; they were doing the ordinary work; the book was waiting.

Hilkiah's report is laconic: "I have found the Book of the Law in the house of the LORD" (34:15). Shaphan reports first on the disbursement, almost as an afterthought adding "Hilkiah the priest has given me a book," then reads it before the king. The verbal economy here is deliberate. The book itself is the climax; the reader is meant to be paying attention to what Josiah will do when he hears it.

Josiah's response is to *tear his clothes* (*vayyiqra et begadav*, וַיִּקְרַע אֶת־בְּגָדָיו, 34:19) — the standard biblical gesture of grief and contrition. He sends a five-person delegation (Hilkiah, Ahikam ben Shaphan, Abdon ben Micah, Shaphan, Asaiah) to "inquire of the LORD" (*dirshu et-YHWH*, דִּרְשׁוּ אֶת־יְהוָה, 34:21). The verb *darash* — "seek, inquire" — is the Chronicler's signature verb for faithful posture toward God.

The Huldah oracle (34:22-28) is the literary peak. Huldah delivers two oracles: one for the people, one for the king. To the people she says: disaster is coming, the curses are coming, *my wrath will be poured out on this place and will not be quenched* (34:25). To the king she says: because your heart was *tender* (*rakh*, רַךְ — "soft," contrasted with hardened heart), because you *humbled yourself* (*hikkana'ta*, הִכָּנַעְתָּ — the Chronicler's signature verb for the kind of humbling that triggers mercy), I have heard you, you will be gathered to your fathers in peace, and your eyes will not see the disaster (34:27-28).

This double oracle — destruction for the nation, peace for the faithful king — sets up the chapter's deepest theological tension, which the next chapter will tighten: Josiah is promised burial in peace, but he dies in battle at Megiddo. We will return to this.

The chapter closes with the covenant ceremony (34:29-33). Josiah gathers all the elders, all the men of Judah, the inhabitants of Jerusalem, the priests, the Levites, *kol ha-am le-miggadol ve-ad-qatan* (כָּל־הָעָם לְמִגָּדוֹל וְעַד־קָטָן, "all the people both great and small"). He reads the *sefer ha-berit* ("the book of the covenant," 34:30 — the Chronicler shifts the name from *torah* to *berit* to mark the ceremonial moment) in their hearing. He stands and makes a covenant *be-kol levavo u-ve-kol nafsho* (בְּכָל־לְבָבוֹ וּבְכָל־נַפְשׁוֹ, "with all his heart and all his soul," 34:31) — a phrase drawn directly from Deut 6:5, which is itself excellent evidence that the book Josiah found included Deuteronomy. The chapter ends: "All his days they did not turn away from following the LORD" (34:33). All Josiah's days. The next chapter will tell us when those days ended.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Josiah's reform is one of the foundational events of the Hebrew Bible's literary and theological history. It is the moment when *book* becomes scripture in something like the modern sense. Before Josiah, we have *torah* as priestly oral tradition, royal decree, prophetic oracle. After Josiah, we have *sefer ha-torah* — a book that is read in public, that triggers covenant renewal, that creates a covenant community organized around its words. The Deuteronomistic History (Joshua-Kings) is shaped by the theology of this book; the prophetic books of Jeremiah and Zephaniah are shaped by the reform; the post-exilic Ezra-Nehemiah tradition takes the same pattern (Ezra 8 reads the law to the people in Jerusalem, weeping turns to celebration) and runs it again.

The **Jewish trajectory** holds Josiah's reform as the foundational moment of *torah*-centered Judaism. The annual Torah-reading cycle in synagogue worship descends, theologically, from Josiah's public reading. The Yom Kippur and *Shavuot* covenant-renewal traditions echo the structure of 34:30-32. Maimonides, in *Mishneh Torah, Hilkhot Hagigah* 3:1-3, codifies the *Hakhel* mitzvah (the public reading of Torah by the king every seven years at Sukkot, drawn from Deut 31:10-13, but executed paradigmatically by Josiah). The figure of Huldah is honored in rabbinic literature; the Talmud (*Megillah* 14b) lists her among the seven prophetesses of Israel (Sarah, Miriam, Deborah, Hannah, Abigail, Huldah, Esther — though the lists vary).

The **Christian trajectory** has read 2 Chronicles 34 in two main ways. First, as a model of *recovery* of scripture: the temple repair finds the book, the king responds, the people are restored. Reformers in particular — Wycliffe, Tyndale, Luther, Calvin — saw their own work as a Josiah-pattern recovery of the Bible from the dust of medieval neglect. The Reformation's *sola scriptura* drew direct typological energy from this chapter: the book had been there, hidden under temple repairs, and now was being found again. Second, as a model of *covenant renewal*: the Anglican Book of Common Prayer's Lent-and-Easter ceremonies, the Reformed renewal-of-covenant traditions (Scotland 1638), and contemporary evangelical altar-call practices all draw on the structure of 34:29-33.

The **Catholic** position on the reform pattern is broadly continuous with the rabbinic-Christian one: the reading of scripture in liturgy, the renewal of baptismal vows, the cycle of Lent-confession-Easter all participate in the structural logic of Josiah's reform. The post-Vatican-II liturgical renewal explicitly recovered the public reading of Old Testament scripture (which medieval Catholic liturgy had reduced) and saw itself, in part, as a Josiah-style recovery.

The **Council of Trent (1546)** debated the canon of scripture in part by reflecting on the recovery-of-Torah model. If the church received the canon from the Spirit through history, then *recovery* (in this case Trent's reaffirmation of the deuterocanonicals) was a legitimate ecclesial act analogous to Josiah's. Protestant reformers used the same model to argue *against* the deuterocanonicals — recovering "only" what was in the Hebrew canon. Both sides invoked Josiah; both sides cannot be right; the dispute remains.

The **Huldah** tradition has been particularly significant for women's prophetic agency. The very fact that the high priest and the secretary of state went to a woman in the Second Quarter to ask what God said about a recovered scripture is one of the strongest biblical warrants for women's authoritative speech in religious contexts. Phyllis Trible, Wilda Gafney, Carol Meyers, Tikva Frymer-Kensky have all developed this. Christian denominations that ordain women routinely cite Huldah; denominations that do not ordain women generally treat her as a special case or a non-priestly authority. The text itself does not solve the modern debate but it does establish that *some* women speak with prophetic authority that the highest officials of state recognize.

The NT does not cite 2 Chr 34 directly but the Josianic pattern — covenant renewal, public reading, the recovery of authoritative word — runs through Acts (especially Acts 2 and Acts 15) and through Hebrews (the new covenant inaugurated through Christ's blood, Heb 9-10).

## Lens 4 — The Chronicler's Theological Intent

The Chronicler is making several moves at once.

The agenda: *Josiah is the model king — early-seeking, all-Israel-reforming, scripture-receiving, covenant-renewing — and his pattern (faithful young king + recovered word + prophetic confirmation + public covenant) is what the post-exilic community should aspire to.*

The post-exilic community has its own *sefer ha-torah* now (in Ezra-Nehemiah, Ezra reads the Law to the people; the structure echoes Josiah). The Chronicler is showing them that this is not new — it is the pattern of every faithful renewal in Israel's history.

**Tension 1: what was the book?** The mainstream-critical answer is proto-Deuteronomy; the conservative answer is some form of Mosaic Torah; the Chronicler treats it as straightforwardly Mosaic. How seriously should the modern reader take the de Wette hypothesis?

The honest steelman of the critical position: the textual evidence for Deuteronomy as the book is unusually strong — the centralization, the curses, the covenant ceremony, the *be-kol levavo* echo all point to Deuteronomy. The honest steelman of the conservative position: the Chronicler and Kings both treat the book as Mosaic, and the early Jewish-Christian tradition received it as such; modern critical reconstructions are real but tentative, and the canonical claim that Moses is the source of the *torah* tradition is not lightly set aside.

A nuanced position (held by many evangelical Old Testament scholars, e.g., Block, McConville, J.B. Webster, Christopher Wright) accepts that *Deuteronomy* is the book Josiah found and that the book was *based on Mosaic tradition* but had been edited and updated over centuries by inspired tradents who understood themselves as faithful to Moses. The book is "Mosaic" in the sense of representing the Mosaic covenant tradition, but the literary form Hilkiah found in 622 BC was not necessarily identical to Moses's own writing. Both the Chronicler's claim and the de Wette hypothesis can be partially preserved.

**Tension 2: Huldah and women's prophetic agency.** Josiah's delegation goes to Huldah, and her oracle is decisive. What does this mean for women's prophetic, teaching, and ordained authority in the modern church and synagogue?

The egalitarian position (Trible, Gafney, Frymer-Kensky, Meyers; in evangelical circles, Cynthia Long Westfall, Lucy Peppiatt, Scot McKnight): Huldah is one of several biblical women whose authoritative speech is received without controversy by men of high status. This pattern — alongside Miriam, Deborah, Phoebe, Junia, the prophesying daughters of Philip — provides biblical warrant for women's authoritative speech and leadership in the contemporary church.

The complementarian position (Wayne Grudem, *Evangelical Feminism and Biblical Truth*; John Piper): Huldah's prophetic role does not establish a pattern for women's pastoral or priestly authority because (a) prophecy is occasional charismatic gift rather than ongoing office, (b) the Old Testament priesthood was male, (c) the New Testament apostolic and elder roles are male, and (d) prophecy and teaching/ruling are distinct in Pauline thought.

The honest engagement: the text does not directly answer the modern question. What it does establish is that the highest religious officials of Judah, including the high priest, sought oracle from a woman and acted on it. The chapter does not theorize about gender; it simply records that Huldah said it and Josiah did it. Modern readers must work out their own positions, but they should do so without minimizing the chapter's straightforward presentation.

**Tension 3: the recovery-of-Scripture model in modern reading.** Reformation rhetoric used 2 Chr 34 typologically — the church had buried the Bible, and the Reformers were finding it again. Is this typology helpful or distorting?

It is partially helpful — it captures the genuine experience that scripture had been functionally inaccessible to most lay people in the late medieval period, and that vernacular translation and printed accessibility transformed the church. It is partially distorting — the medieval church did not in fact "lose" the Bible; the Vulgate was studied throughout the Middle Ages by clergy, and Catholic theology never denied scripture's authority. The Reformation's "recovery" was more nearly a *redistribution* than a *recovery*. 2 Chr 34 is genuinely about a lost book being found; the Reformation analogy is loose.

**Where this leaves us:** Josiah's reform gives us the model of book-and-covenant. A community that gathers around a recovered, read, and received text — that hears it together, weeps when it convicts, makes covenant when it calls — is a community structured for renewal. Whether the book in question is Deuteronomy, the Bible, or a specific tradition's authoritative text, the pattern of public reading, contrite hearing, prophetic confirmation, and covenant response remains theologically generative.

**Closing question:** Where in your life is there a *sefer ha-torah* that has been waiting in the temple repairs to be brought out, read, and received? And whose Huldah do you trust to tell you what it means?

## The Thread

Chapter 34 is Josiah's reform from the inside: heart turning, book finding, prophetess consulting, covenant making. Chapter 35 will be Josiah's reform from the outside: the great Passover that is the largest since Samuel, and then — abruptly, tragically — Josiah's death at Megiddo. Huldah's promise of *peace at his grave* will collide with the reality of an arrow at Megiddo. The Chronicler will have to explain.
