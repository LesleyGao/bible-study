# Psalm 59 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 59 is a night-prayer of a man under surveillance. The superscription situates it precisely: "To the choirmaster: according to Do Not Destroy. A Miktam of David, when Saul sent men to watch his house in order to kill him." The historical reference is to 1 Samuel 19:11, where Saul dispatches men to David's house to kill him at dawn — an assassination plan thwarted only when Michal lowers David through a window in the night and stages a dummy in his bed. The melody-marker *al-tashchet* (אַל־תַּשְׁחֵת, "do not destroy") again names the tune, as in Psalms 57, 58, 75. The genre tag *miktam* again resists firm definition, but the LXX renders it *stēlographia* — an inscription worthy of stone — and the cluster of *miktam* psalms (16, 56–60) all have the quality of being polished, deliberate compositions.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 59 as an *Individualklagelied* (individual lament) with strong elements of imprecation and a concluding *Vertrauenslied* (psalm of trust). Sigmund Mowinckel saw the cult-functional element: the prayer of a man whose enemies are "the nations" (vv. 5, 8) sounds royal — a king's prayer against external threat — and Mowinckel argued that even if a Davidic incident lies behind the psalm, the canonical form has been adapted for royal-cultic use. Hans-Joachim Kraus locates the psalm in the "petitions of an accused person" (he reads vv. 3–4 as protests of innocence in a legal-cultic setting). Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger see two compositional layers — an original individual lament expanded by a redactor who added the "nations" language to make the personal threat into a universal one.

The historical setting in 1 Samuel 19 is plausibly preserved: David's enemies "come back each evening" (vv. 6, 14), they "prowl about the city," they "watch his house." This matches an assassination team posted overnight to ambush a target leaving at dawn. Whether the superscription was added by a post-exilic editor (Tate, Kraus) or reflects a genuine biographical memory (Wenham, Goldingay, with caution), the imagery fits the situation. The name *YHWH Tzeva'ot* (יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת — "LORD of hosts," v. 5) is a covenantal-military epithet that locates the prayer firmly within Israelite royal theology: God commands the heavenly armies and is asked to come to the aid of his anointed.

Psalm 59 sits inside Book II of the Psalter, the Elohistic Psalter (Psalms 42–72), where *YHWH* has been mostly replaced with *Elohim*. Psalm 59, however, preserves *YHWH* in vv. 3, 5, 8 — one of several psalms in Book II where the Elohistic redaction is incomplete, suggesting either an earlier compositional layer or a deliberate retention of the divine name in particular cultic contexts. The Dead Sea Scrolls (4QPsa, 11QPsd) preserve fragments of Psalm 59 with no major textual variants, and the Septuagint preserves the same lament-imprecation-trust shape.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels are sharp. The "prowling dog" imagery in vv. 6, 14 draws on the standard ANE view of dogs as unclean scavengers, not pets. In Mesopotamian and Egyptian texts, dogs roam city streets at night, eat refuse, and threaten travelers; the verb *yashuvu* (יָשׁוּבוּ — "they return") suggests the nightly rhythm of feral packs. *Ke-keleb yashuvu la-erev* (כְּכֶלֶב יָשׁוּבוּ לָעֶרֶב, "they return at evening like a dog," v. 6) makes the speaker's enemies into the city's despised scavengers. The same imagery is later picked up by Paul in Philippians 3:2 ("beware of the dogs") and Revelation 22:15 (the "dogs" excluded from the New Jerusalem).

## Lens 2: The Literature

Lowth's parallelism here works through a pronounced refrain structure. Two refrains anchor the psalm. The first (vv. 6 and 14) — the dog-refrain — is verbatim repeated: "Each evening they come back, howling like dogs and prowling about the city." The second (vv. 9 and 17) — the fortress-refrain — is variant repeated: "O my Strength, I will watch for you / I will sing praises to you, for you, O God, are my fortress." The psalm's architecture is thus a chiasm of menace and refuge. Outside menace (the dogs) is bracketed by inside refuge (the fortress). Twice the speaker hears the howling, twice he answers with the song.

The structure unfolds in three strophes. Strophe 1 (vv. 1–5) is petition: "Deliver me, protect me, save me." The verbs pile up — *natzal* (deliver), *sagav* (set on high), *natzal* again, *yasha* (save) — and the parallelism in v. 1 is synonymous, in v. 3 antithetical (the enemies act "for no transgression or sin of mine"). Strophe 2 (vv. 6–13) describes the threat and prays the imprecation. Strophe 3 (vv. 14–17) returns to the threat-image (the dogs) and resolves it into morning-praise.

The fortress imagery in v. 9 — *oz* (עֹז — "strength") and *misgav* (מִשְׂגָּב — "fortress, high stronghold") — locates the speaker on a height inaccessible to the prowling enemies below. The same root *sagav* appears in v. 1 ("set me on high"), creating a vertical poetic axis: enemies prowl below in the streets; God lifts the speaker to the high tower. In v. 16 the speaker promises *ashir uzkha* (אָשִׁיר עֻזֶּךָ — "I will sing of your strength") and *aranen ba-boqer chasdekha* (אֲרַנֵּן בַּבֹּקֶר חַסְדֶּךָ — "I will sing aloud of your steadfast love in the morning"). The morning is the surprise: the enemies came to kill him at dawn (1 Sam 19:11), but at dawn the speaker is still alive and singing.

Two *selahs* (vv. 5, 13) divide the petition-and-imprecation movement from the threat-and-praise movement. The strange imprecation in v. 11 — *al taharegem pen yishkechu ami* (אַל־תַּהַרְגֵם פֶּן יִשְׁכְּחוּ עַמִּי — "kill them not, lest my people forget") — is a unique twist on the standard imprecatory pattern. Most imprecations request swift destruction; this one asks for protracted disgrace, that the enemies be made to "totter" so that their fall becomes a public lesson. Some commentators (Calvin, more recently Goldingay) see this as evidence that the imprecation is pedagogical rather than vindictive: the wicked are spared death so that their visible humiliation becomes a witness to God's rule.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Psalm 59 is the fourth in the Davidic *miktam* cluster (Psalms 56–60). Read together, the cluster traces the prayer-life of David under threat: terror (56), refuge (57), imprecation against corrupt judges (58), surveillance and assassination (59), and military defeat (60). Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger argue the cluster forms a deliberate David-anthology curated for Book II of the Psalter, teaching the prayer-postures of the king through the full range of crisis.

Jewish reception has read Psalm 59 within the Davidic narrative. The *Midrash Tehillim* connects vv. 6, 14 to Saul's assassins lurking outside David's house. Rashi reads the dogs as both literal — Saul's spies — and metaphorical: the slanderers who circle the righteous. Ibn Ezra emphasizes the geographical-historical anchoring; Radak draws out the morning-deliverance theme as evidence of God's protection. Modern Jewish scholars take varied angles. Martin Buber heard in v. 16's "I will sing of your strength" a paradigm of the morning *amidah* — the prayer that begins the day in confident address to God. Abraham Joshua Heschel located the psalm within his theology of *the prophets* — the prophet's accusation against the nations in v. 5 ("rouse yourself to punish all the nations") expresses the divine *pathos* against systemic evil. Jon Levenson treats the dog-imagery within his work on covenantal theology — the prowling outsider versus the inside-the-walls covenant community. Nahum Sarna emphasizes the morning-deliverance motif as central to Israelite cultic experience. Liturgically, Psalm 59 has been associated in some traditions with prayers for protection against persecution and is recited in some Sephardic communities during times of communal danger.

Christian reception begins with Augustine, whose *Enarratio in Psalmum 58* (the Latin numbering) reads the prowling dogs as the heretics and persecutors of the church and the morning praise as the resurrection. Athanasius recommends Psalm 59 in his *Letter to Marcellinus* for those facing surveillance and false accusation. Cassiodorus emphasizes the threefold structure: lament, imprecation, trust. Aquinas, in his *Postilla super Psalmos*, treats the dog-imagery as pedagogically apt: dogs represent the loud, baseless accusation that cannot ultimately harm the one whom God protects. Luther in his *Operationes in Psalmos* treats the psalm as a paradigm of the *Anfechtung* — the spiritual assault that comes especially at night, and is dispelled by morning praise. Calvin's 1557 commentary draws out the providential reading: the speaker's deliverance is not coincidence but God's specific intervention, and v. 11 ("kill them not, lest my people forget") is for Calvin a central text on the pedagogical function of God's restraint of evil.

The modern critical conversation runs through Gunkel (form-critical individual lament with imprecatory features), Mowinckel (royal-cultic adaptation), Westermann (lament moves to praise as the deep grammar of biblical prayer), Kraus (the prayer of the falsely accused), Hossfeld and Zenger (compositional layers, redactional shaping into the *miktam* cluster), Goldingay (pastoral-theological reading), Tate (the *Word Biblical Commentary* on Psalms 51–100), and Wenham (canonical-ethical reading). Walter Brueggemann places Psalm 59 in his "psalms of disorientation" category and argues that the morning-praise resolution preserves a credible movement from terror to trust. Patrick Miller treats it as a paradigm of "prayer for and against enemies." William Brown reads the dog-imagery as part of the broader animal-iconography of the Psalter. Beth LaNeel Tanner and Wilda Gafney have both engaged the psalm from postcolonial and womanist angles, attending to whose voice is heard in the prayer and what kinds of threat it encodes.

The New Testament does not quote Psalm 59 directly, but the dog-imagery surfaces in Philippians 3:2 ("look out for the dogs, look out for the evildoers") and Revelation 22:15 ("outside are the dogs and sorcerers"). Paul's appropriation is striking: the "dogs" in Philippians 3 are Judaizers — those who threaten the gospel from inside the religious community — turning the trope on its head. The morning-praise pattern of v. 16 echoes faintly in Acts 16:25 (Paul and Silas singing in the Philippian jail at midnight) and in the Easter morning narratives.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

Two tensions deserve honest engagement.

The first is the imprecatory pattern in vv. 11–13. Like Psalm 58, this prayer asks God to act violently against enemies, though here the imprecation is unusual: "kill them not, lest my people forget." Three positions exist on how Christians should pray such verses. (1) The *Lewis position* (in *Reflections on the Psalms*) treats the imprecations as emotionally honest but morally sub-Christian — natural human reactions that the New Testament transcends. Steelman: Jesus says "love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44); the imprecation seems to belong to a different ethical universe. (2) The *Brueggemann / Zenger position* treats the imprecations as legitimate protests against impunity — refusals to be reconciled to evil. Steelman: the Psalter is the prayerbook Jesus prayed; to silence the imprecations is to silence the cry of the victims of history. (3) The *Augustinian-Aquinas position* reads the imprecations eschatologically and corporately — they ask God to judge the structures of evil, and they pray "thy kingdom come" in its hardest form. Steelman: this preserves the psalm for Christian prayer while integrating it into a Christological frame in which the wicked who must fall are first the powers of sin and death, including those within the worshiper. **Where this leaves us:** Psalm 59 specifically chooses *not* to ask for death — "kill them not" — which suggests the psalm itself is already wrestling with the violence-question and choosing a more nuanced imprecation. This is a psalm that prays for visible humiliation as pedagogy rather than annihilation, and that may be a clue to how the canonical voice intends imprecation to function.

The second tension is the dog-imagery and its modern Christian use. The psalm dehumanizes the enemies — they are scavenging dogs, prowling, growling, bellowing with swords in their lips. Modern readers, especially in cultures where dogs are companions, may find the metaphor difficult; readers attuned to the long history of dehumanizing rhetoric (where one ethnic or religious or national group calls another "dogs") may find it dangerous. C.S. Lewis warned that the imprecatory psalms can become a permission slip for our own contempt. Brueggemann counters that the psalm is the prayer of the *threatened* one against the *armed* one — the one being hunted naming the hunters in their cruelty. The closing question: when you find yourself reaching for the dog-image to describe an enemy, is your prayer the prayer of the hunted David in his besieged house, or has it become the prayer of the powerful contempt that the psalm itself would resist?
