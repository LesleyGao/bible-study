# Isaiah 25 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: Historical World

Isaiah 25 sits in the heart of the **Isaiah Apocalypse** (chs 24-27), the proto-apocalyptic block that mainstream-critical scholarship dates to the **late post-exilic period, between the 5th and 3rd centuries BC** (Hans Wildberger's *Jesaja* commentary, Otto Plöger's *Theocracy and Eschatology*, Joseph Blenkinsopp's Anchor Bible commentary, John J. Collins's *The Apocalyptic Imagination*, J. Todd Hibbard's *Intertextuality in Isaiah 24-27*). The composite-authorship hypothesis going back to Bernhard Duhm (*Das Buch Jesaia*, 1892) treats the section as one of the latest insertions into the Isaiah scroll — its vocabulary, dualistic moral structure, eschatological scope, and absence of named historical referents distinguish it sharply from the 8th-century oracles. The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran (1QIsaᵃ, c. 125 BC) preserves chs 24-27 in continuous form with the rest of the book, fixing the editorial integration well before Christ.

Conservative readings (Edward J. Young, J. Alec Motyer, John Oswalt) defend a substantially Isaianic origin for chs 24-27, treating the cosmic and eschatological language as the prophet's anticipatory reach beyond the immediate Assyrian crisis. Brevard Childs's canonical approach brackets the dating question and reads the Apocalypse as theologically integrated with the rest of the book.

What makes Isaiah 25 historically and theologically pivotal is its position within the **emergence of resurrection hope** in the Hebrew Bible. The biblical tradition's path to a fully articulated bodily-resurrection theology is famously slow. Most of the Hebrew Bible operates with **Sheol** (שְׁאוֹל) — the shadowy underworld where all the dead go, righteous and wicked alike, a place of dust and silence (Job 10:21-22; Ps 6:5; Ps 88:10-12; Eccl 9:5-10). Glimmers of something more begin to show up in select psalms (Pss 16, 17, 49, 73), in the Job 19:25-27 *gōʾēl* hope, and in a small handful of prophetic texts — and Isa 25:8 is the most striking and unambiguous of those texts. Daniel 12:2 — "many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt" — is usually cited as the clearest OT resurrection text, but it is also late (mid-2nd c. BC, during the Antiochene crisis). Isa 25:8 may be earlier and is in some ways even more theologically dense: it does not merely raise the dead, it announces that **death itself** will be undone.

Jon D. Levenson's *Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel* (Yale, 2006) is the foundational modern treatment of this slow emergence within Jewish tradition. Levenson argues — against the older Christian tendency to read resurrection as an alien Persian or Hellenistic import — that resurrection hope grew organically out of the Hebrew Bible's own logic of covenantal faithfulness, with Isa 25:8 and Isa 26:19 as key turning points within the Isaiah Apocalypse itself. The chapter, on this reading, is one of the moments where the slow theological seed begins to flower.

## Lens 2: Literary World

Chapter 25 splits into three units: a praise hymn (vv. 1-5), the eschatological banquet and death-swallowed-up oracle (vv. 6-9), and a closing oracle against Moab (vv. 10-12). The middle unit is the chapter's beating heart and one of the most theologically dense passages in the entire Hebrew Bible.

The hymn (vv. 1-5) opens in classical thanksgiving form: **"O LORD, you are my God; I will exalt you, I will praise your name"** — אֲרוֹמִמְךָ אוֹדֶה שִׁמְךָ (*ʾărômimkhā ʾôdeh šimkhā*). The reason for praise is the fall of "the city" (probably eschatological Babylon-typology, not a specific historical city) and Yahweh's role as **stronghold to the poor** (מָעוֹז לַדָּל, *māʿôz laddāl*) and **shelter from the storm** (מַחְסֶה מִזֶּרֶם, *maḥseh mizzerem*). The hymn binds the Apocalypse's cosmic judgment to a concrete care for the *dāl* and *ʾebyôn* — the poor and the needy.

Verses 6-9 are the chapter's apex. The opening line — **"On this mountain the LORD of hosts will make for all peoples a feast"** (וְעָשָׂה יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת לְכָל־הָעַמִּים בָּהָר הַזֶּה מִשְׁתֵּה, *wĕʿāśāh YHWH ṣĕvāʾôt lĕkhol-hāʿammîm bāhār hazzeh mišteh*) — sets up the eschatological banquet motif that will run from here through Mt 8:11 ("many will come from east and west and recline at table with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven"), Lk 14:15-24 (the parable of the great banquet), Mt 22:1-14 (the wedding feast), Lk 22:30 ("you may eat and drink at my table in my kingdom"), and Rev 19:9 ("blessed are those who are invited to the marriage supper of the Lamb").

Note the universalizing scope: **לְכָל־הָעַמִּים** (*lĕkhol-hāʿammîm*, "for all peoples") and again **כָּל־הָעַמִּים... כָּל־הַגּוֹיִם** (*kol-hāʿammîm... kol-haggôyim*, "all peoples... all nations") in v. 7. The Hebrew is unambiguous: this is not Israel's private banquet. The "veil that is cast over all peoples, the covering spread over all nations" of v. 7 is most plausibly read as the funeral shroud — the death-cloth — which Yahweh **swallows up** along with death itself in v. 8.

The climactic line is one of the densest verses in the Hebrew Bible:

**בִּלַּע הַמָּוֶת לָנֶצַח**
*billaʿ hammāwet lāneṣaḥ*
"He will swallow up death forever"

The verb **בָּלַע** (*bālaʿ*, "to swallow up, engulf") is striking. In the ancient Near Eastern background, **Mot** — the Canaanite god of death (Ugaritic *mt*) — is portrayed in the Baal Cycle as the great swallower, the one whose throat consumes all flesh. The Hebrew verse takes this mythic vocabulary and inverts it: the swallower is swallowed. The eschatological Yahweh consumes death itself, undoing the underworld's appetite. The line is therefore not merely a promise of individual immortality; it is the dismantling of the cosmic order in which death has the final word. **לָנֶצַח** (*lāneṣaḥ*, "forever / for the duration") seals the permanence.

Verse 8 continues: **"the Lord GOD will wipe away tears from all faces"** — וּמָחָה אֲדֹנָי יְהוִה דִּמְעָה מֵעַל כָּל־פָּנִים (*ûmāḥāh ʾădōnāy YHWH dimʿāh mēʿal kol-pānîm*). The verb *māḥāh* ("wipe") is tender and bodily. The "reproach of his people" (חֶרְפַּת עַמּוֹ, *ḥerpat ʿammô*) — the centuries of national humiliation — is taken away.

The closing oracle against Moab (vv. 10-12) is jarring in its violence, especially after the universalist banquet of vv. 6-9. Moab is "trampled down in his place, as straw is trampled down in a dunghill" (v. 10) and the swimmer-image of v. 11 is grotesque: Moab thrashes in the manure pile like someone trying to swim. The juxtaposition is one of the chapter's hardest interpretive puzzles — why this particular and ugly oracle right after the universal feast?

## Lens 3: Canonical and Theological World

The NT use of Isaiah 25 is among the most theologically weight-bearing in the entire Hebrew Bible.

**1 Corinthians 15:54-55** quotes Isa 25:8 verbatim at the climax of Paul's resurrection-defense:

*"When the perishable puts on the imperishable, and the mortal puts on immortality, then shall come to pass the saying that is written: 'Death is swallowed up in victory.' 'O death, where is your victory? O death, where is your sting?'"*

The Greek κατεπόθη ὁ θάνατος εἰς νῖκος (*katepothē ho thanatos eis nikos*, "death is swallowed up into victory") follows a textual tradition closer to Theodotion or a proto-Aquila reading than the LXX, but the verb **κατεπόθη** ("was swallowed down") catches the Hebrew *billaʿ* exactly. Paul's pairing with Hos 13:14 ("O death, where are your plagues?") creates one of the NT's most famous resurrection texts. Isaiah 25:8 is therefore not an obscure proof-text in Christian theology — it is one of the OT pillars of the entire Pauline resurrection vision.

**Revelation 7:17** and **Revelation 21:4** quote the second half of Isa 25:8 — "and the Lord GOD will wipe away tears from all faces" — applying the line to the New Jerusalem: "and God will wipe away every tear from their eyes." Rev 21:4 then continues: "and death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain anymore, for the former things have passed away" — fusing Isa 25:8's two clauses (death swallowed + tears wiped) with Isa 35:10. The eschatological vision of Revelation's closing chapters is in significant part a Christian reading of Isaiah 25.

The Jewish trajectory has been equally rich. Rabbinic literature (b. Berakhot 17a; b. Pesahim 119b; *Pesikta Rabbati* 36-37) reads Isa 25:6-9 as foundational for the *seudat Livyatan* — the eschatological feast on Leviathan that the righteous will share in the world to come (*Olam Ha-Ba*). The Talmudic and midrashic traditions develop the banquet motif in extraordinary detail, and the universalist scope of *lĕkhol-hāʿammîm* is preserved in some streams (e.g., Maimonides's *Mishneh Torah*, *Hilkhot Melakhim* 12, where the messianic age opens "for all the world to know the LORD"). Levenson's *Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel* traces how Isa 25:8 — alongside Isa 26:19, Dan 12:2, and the development of Pharisaic resurrection theology — becomes the Hebrew Bible's clearest seed for the rabbinic doctrine of *teḥiyyat hammētîm* (resurrection of the dead), one of the thirteen principles of Maimonidean Judaism.

The Christian theological trajectory is dense across traditions:

- **Catholic**: The Catechism of the Catholic Church §1027 cites Isa 25:8 in its treatment of beatitude and the Beatific Vision. The eschatological feast is foundational for Catholic Eucharistic theology, where every Mass is anticipation of the *cena Domini* on Mt. Zion.
- **Reformed**: The Heidelberg Catechism Q57-58 ("What comfort does the resurrection of the body offer you?... that not only my soul after this life shall immediately be taken up to Christ its Head, but also that this my body, raised by the power of Christ, shall be reunited with my soul, and made like the glorious body of Christ") rests substantially on the Pauline use of Isa 25:8 in 1 Cor 15.
- **Eastern Orthodox**: The cosmic transfiguration theology of Maximus the Confessor and the Paschal liturgy ("Christ is risen from the dead, trampling down death by death") draws directly on the *billaʿ hammāwet lāneṣaḥ* logic of Isa 25:8 as fulfilled in the Resurrection.
- **Anglican**: The *Book of Common Prayer*'s burial office uses Isa 25:8 alongside 1 Cor 15 as the central scriptural framing of Christian death.

## Lens 4: Tensions and Honest Reckoning

**Tension 1: The eschatological banquet's universalist scope versus supersessionist readings.** The Hebrew is unambiguous: the feast is *lĕkhol-hāʿammîm* — for all peoples — on Yahweh's mountain. Three positions on the scope: (a) The traditional Christian supersessionist reading takes the banquet to be open to the church (gathered from all peoples), with national Israel either replaced or absorbed — a reading Mt 8:11's threat to the "sons of the kingdom" has been used to support. (b) The Jewish reading preserves a particularist-then-universal structure: the banquet is on Mt. Zion (a specifically Jewish location) and the *ʿam* (people) at its center is Israel, with the nations included around but through Israel — the *Olam Ha-Ba* tradition, Maimonides's vision of all nations knowing Yahweh through the messianic age. (c) The post-supersessionist Christian reading (N. T. Wright, R. Kendall Soulen) recovers the Jewish-shaped frame: the banquet is on Mt. Zion, and Gentile inclusion happens *with* Israel, not in place of Israel. Steelman of the supersessionist concern: Mt 8:11's "sons of the kingdom thrown out" is a sharp NT text, and the universalist *lĕkhol-hāʿammîm* of Isa 25:6 cannot be read as Jewish-exclusive. Steelman of the post-supersessionist concern: the Hebrew text does not erase Israel; it places the banquet on Yahweh's specific mountain, with Israel as the host community and the nations as guests. Where this leaves us: Isa 25 holds Jewish particularity and universal welcome together. Christian readings that erase the Jewish frame distort the chapter; Jewish readings that exclude the nations distort it equally. The chapter resists both moves.

**Tension 2: The slow emergence of resurrection hope in the Hebrew Bible.** Three positions on how unique Isa 25:8 is in pre-exilic / early-post-exilic Hebrew literature: (a) The maximalist reading takes Isa 25:8 as fully developed resurrection theology, on a par with Dan 12 and 1 Cor 15 — "He will swallow up death forever" is read as cosmic-resurrection promise. (b) The minimalist reading (some critical scholars) treats Isa 25:8 as a metaphor for national restoration — "death" as the death of exile, "swallowing up" as the end of national humiliation, with bodily resurrection a later development. (c) The developmental reading (Levenson, Wright, John J. Collins) treats Isa 25:8 as one of the earliest unambiguous moments where the Hebrew text reaches beyond Sheol's permanence toward a hope that requires the undoing of death itself — not yet articulated as bodily resurrection but theologically pointing that direction, with Dan 12 and the rabbinic tradition flowering from this seed. Steelman of the minimalist reading: the immediate context (vv. 9-12, the national-deliverance language, the humiliation of Moab) does suggest national-restoration as the primary horizon; bodily resurrection as the explicit referent only develops fully later. Steelman of the developmental reading: the verb *bālaʿ* (swallow up) of *hammāwet* (death itself) is too cosmically loaded to reduce to national exile — it directly engages the ANE Mot mythology and proposes the dismantling of death as a cosmic principle. Where this leaves us: Isa 25:8 is genuinely transitional. It does more than the minimalist reading allows; it does not quite do what the maximalist reading wants. It is one of the clearest moments where the Hebrew Bible reaches toward resurrection hope, and its appropriation by 1 Cor 15 and Rev 21 is theologically continuous with — not a violent imposition on — the Hebrew text's own trajectory. Levenson's argument, that resurrection hope grew organically out of the OT's covenantal logic, finds in Isa 25:8 one of its strongest exegetical anchors.

**Tension 3: The Moab oracle (vv. 10-12) against the universalist banquet (vv. 6-9).** Why does the chapter close with Moab "trampled down in his place, as straw in a dunghill"? Three positions: (a) Editorial-seam reading: vv. 10-12 are a later addition that intrudes the older OAN logic into the new universalist vision. (b) Theological-tension reading: the chapter holds judgment and welcome together — the banquet is for all peoples *and* the proud who refuse the invitation are still judged. (c) Honest-with-violence reading: the imagery of Moab swimming in manure is genuinely ugly and should not be smoothed over; the prophet is unapologetic that some powers resist Yahweh's reign and are humiliated in the end. Steelman of the discomfort: the swimmer-in-the-dungheap image is hard to integrate with vv. 6-9, and Christian readings have often ignored it. Steelman of the theological-tension reading: prophetic literature regularly holds invitation and judgment together; the chapter is not incoherent, just demanding. Where this leaves us: the Moab oracle is jarring on purpose. Reading vv. 6-9 in isolation produces a sentimental universalism the text does not authorize; reading vv. 10-12 in isolation produces a triumphalist nationalism the text equally rejects. Both must be held.

**Closing question:** When the prophet sees death itself swallowed up forever, and tears wiped from all faces, what does it mean to live now — between the announcement and the banquet — as people whose bodies will not have the last word against them?
