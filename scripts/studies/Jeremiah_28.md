# Jeremiah 28: The Hananiah Confrontation

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jeremiah 28 is dated with unusual precision: "in that same year, at the beginning of the reign of Zedekiah king of Judah, in the fifth month of the fourth year" (28:1) — that is, **August 593 BC**, the immediate sequel to chapter 27. The "in that same year" links it to the preceding yoke-narrative; "the fifth month" specifies *Av*; "the fourth year" of Zedekiah locks the synchronism to the Babylonian Chronicle's ongoing record of Nebuchadnezzar's western affairs. By the time the chapter closes (28:17, "in the seventh month, the prophet Hananiah died"), only two months have passed — the narrative's compactness is deliberate.

The political context is the same anti-Babylon coalition gathering that frames chapter 27. The envoys are still in Jerusalem (or recently departed); Zedekiah is still on the throne; the temple establishment is still hoping that Nebuchadnezzar's troubles back in Babylon (the 595/594 BC uprisings recorded in Babylonian Chronicle entries) presage imminent collapse. Hananiah ben Azzur of **Gibeon** (28:1) — *Givon* (גִּבְעוֹן), one of the four cities allotted to the Levitical-priestly city of Aaronic descent (Josh 21:17) — speaks from a credentialed cultic location. Gibeon was an active priestly center in the late monarchy; archaeological work at el-Jib (the modern site) has recovered the famous Gibeonite jar handles (*gb'n* inscriptions) and a substantial Iron II winery, attesting to the town's economic and cultic significance. Hananiah is therefore not a fringe figure: he is a prophet from a priestly-Levitical city, and his social authority is comparable to Jeremiah's.

Material culture frames the chapter through the **yoke-bars** Jeremiah is still wearing from chapter 27. When Hananiah seizes them and breaks them (28:10), the act is performed in front of the priests and people in the temple court — a counter-sign-act of maximum public visibility. The wooden yoke shattering, replaced in YHWH's announcement by an *iron* yoke (28:13), draws on metallurgical realities of Late Iron II Levant, where iron implements were displacing bronze and where iron's hardness would have been the obvious ANE metaphor for an irrevocable burden. Babylonian bilateral treaty texts speak of "iron yokes" as the most absolute form of vassalage.

Authorship and dating: chapter 28 is part of the same Baruch-mediated narrative complex as chapters 26–29 and 36. Holladay, Lundbom, and Bright treat it as authentically Jeremianic; the named-personal-detail density (Hananiah ben Azzur of Gibeon, the precise dating to month-and-year, the two-month interval ending in Hananiah's death) is not the texture of late theological construction. Carroll reads more redaction. The MT/LXX divergence in this chapter is moderate: the LXX consistently calls Jeremiah simply *Hieremias* without "the prophet," whereas the MT pointedly uses "*the prophet Jeremiah*" alongside "*the prophet Hananiah*" — a literary device that drives the chapter's central irony, since the question is precisely *which* of the two is the prophet. Lundbom argues the MT's epithet-pattern is original and rhetorically essential.

Key terms shape the confrontation. *Navi* (נָבִיא, "prophet") is repeated nine times in the chapter — Jeremiah is called navi, Hananiah is called navi, and the question is what happens when two men with the same title in the same name say opposite things. *Sheker* (שֶׁקֶר, "lie / falsehood," 28:15) reappears from chapter 27 as the technical term for false-prophetic word. *Sarah* (סָרָה, "rebellion / apostasy," 28:16) is the rare term used in Deuteronomy 13:5 for the false prophet who *speaks rebellion against YHWH* — a deliberate Deuteronomic citation that grounds the death sentence in legal precedent. The verb *shalach* (שָׁלַח, "send," 28:9, 15) is the technical question of prophetic sending: Jeremiah was sent, Hananiah was not. *Amen* (אָמֵן, 28:6) is Jeremiah's stunning initial response — a humanizing word that the chapter places at the dramatic hinge.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 28 is one of the **most theologically loaded prophet-vs-prophet narratives in the Hebrew Bible**. Genre: biographical narrative (Mowinckel's Source B), with sign-act and counter-sign-act embedded. The chapter's structure is a five-act drama: Hananiah's prophecy (vv. 1–4), Jeremiah's reply (vv. 5–9), the counter-sign-act (vv. 10–11), the divine word against Hananiah (vv. 12–16), and the death of Hananiah (v. 17). The economy of the narrative is striking — fewer than three hundred Hebrew words, but containing nearly the entire Old Testament theology of true vs. false prophecy.

Several literary devices give the chapter its force. First, **identical formulae** — Hananiah uses "*Thus says the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel*" (28:2), the standard Jeremianic prophetic formula. There is no *external* mark distinguishing his speech from Jeremiah's; both speak in the name of YHWH, both invoke the same divine titles, both use the same prophetic syntax. Second, **Jeremiah's "Amen"** (28:6) — a word that reveals the prophet's inner life: he *hopes* Hananiah is right. The hope is genuine. Brueggemann calls this "the most humanizing verse in the prophet's career" — Jeremiah does not enjoy his vocation of doom-prophecy and would gladly be wrong.

Third, the **Deuteronomic test** of 28:8–9 is woven from Deut 18:21–22: "*when a prophet speaks in the name of the LORD, if the word does not come to pass… that is a word that the LORD has not spoken*." Jeremiah refines the test asymmetrically: prophets of *war* are the canonical pattern (28:8); prophets of *peace* are the suspect category (28:9), and only their fulfillment can vindicate them. Fourth, the **counter-sign-act** of 28:10–11 — Hananiah breaks the yoke off Jeremiah's neck. This is a public, performative reversal of Jeremiah's sign-act, and it lands in the chapter as a moment of apparent victory for Hananiah. Note that Jeremiah does *not* counter-respond on the spot; he simply "went his way" (28:11). The narrative withholds the divine word for "sometime after" (28:12), and the silence gives Hananiah's victory its full apparent weight before the reversal arrives.

Fifth, the **iron-yoke escalation** (28:13–14) is the divine response: not just the wooden yoke restored, but escalated. Hananiah's counter-sign-act has *worsened* the prophesied burden. Sixth, the **death sentence** (28:16) and its **two-month fulfillment** (28:17) function as the chapter's verdict on the entire confrontation. The text's terseness here is deliberate — Hananiah's death is reported in twelve Hebrew words, without elaboration, lament, or theological commentary.

The MT/LXX divergence in this chapter is moderate but interpretively interesting: the LXX simplifies several formulae and reduces some of the redundancy. The most notable variant is at 28:1, where the LXX simplifies the dating, possibly preserving an earlier text. The MT's heavier dating apparatus may reflect a later editorial concern to lock the synchronism.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish Trajectory.** Bavli Sanhedrin 89a–b is the locus classicus for rabbinic discussion of true and false prophecy and treats Jeremiah 28 directly. The Talmud asks: how is the test of prophecy operational *in real time*? The rabbinic answer in Sanhedrin 89a draws on Deut 18 and Jer 28 together: a prophet who prophesies in YHWH's name what YHWH has not said is liable to death by *strangulation* (the milder rabbinic capital mode, since direct stoning was reserved for idolatry); a prophet whose prophecy of *peace* fails is decisively false; a prophecy of *judgment* unfulfilled may simply mean the people repented (the Jonah principle). Targum Jonathan paraphrases Hananiah's death as the action of *the angel of the Lord*, smoothing the bare narrative. Pesikta Rabbati 26 reads the chapter as the paradigmatic confrontation. Rashi notes that Hananiah's prophecy was *plausibly true* — the political reading was that Babylon's troubles might indeed presage early collapse — and the chapter's point is that *plausibility is not prophecy*. Radak emphasizes the legal precedent of Deuteronomy 18:20. Ibn Ezra discusses the timing: why does Hananiah die two months later, not "this year" as Jeremiah said? The answer in some rabbinic readings is that the seventh month begins the new civil year and so the death falls within the prophesied year on a different reckoning. Abrabanel reads the chapter as warning to every generation's would-be peace-prophets.

**Christian Trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah* (Homily 16 especially) reads the chapter through the lens of Christian discernment of spirits (1 John 4:1, "test the spirits") — Hananiah is the type of every Christian false prophet who speaks in the Lord's name. **Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah*** is detailed on the chapter; he reads Hananiah's death as the fulfillment of Deuteronomic law and notes the rabbinic tradition that Hananiah died on Yom Kippur, an extra-textual gloss Jerome found in Jewish exegesis of his day. Augustine cites the chapter in *De Doctrina Christiana* on the proper interpretation of fulfilled and unfulfilled prophecy. Aquinas (*Summa* II-II.171–174) builds his treatise on prophecy partly on the basis of this chapter, distinguishing *true* prophecy (from the divine intellect) from *false* prophecy (from the imagination, the demons, or human invention). Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah* makes 28:9 the locus classicus for the *moral effect* test of prophecy: a true prophet calls people to repentance, a false prophet to false confidence.

In the modern critical period, Duhm read the chapter as relatively reliable Baruch material. Mowinckel placed it in his Source B. Holladay defends authenticity. Carroll reads more redaction-critically. Lundbom and Bright defend high historical reliability. **Stulman** treats chapter 28 as the climactic test in the chapters 26–29 *prophet-vs-prophet* unit. Brueggemann reads the chapter as the **theological core of the entire problem of prophetic authority**. **Kathleen O'Connor's *Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*** reads Hananiah as the voice of a community in denial — not deliberately deceptive but pastorally invested in not facing what is coming. Wayne Grudem's *The Gift of Prophecy in the New Testament and Today* uses the chapter as a counter-example for his argument that NT prophecy is *fallible* (which raises the question of whether Hananiah's mode of being-wrong is the kind of fallibility Grudem has in mind).

**Where they diverge.** Catholic *Magisterium* tradition reads the chapter as warrant for the church's authority to test prophetic claims — without an authoritative arbiter, real-time discernment between Jeremiah and Hananiah is impossible. Reformed *Word-and-Spirit* tradition (Westminster Confession 1.10) reads the test as scripturally derived: the canon, particularly Deut 13 and 18, is the norm by which prophecy is tested. Charismatic and Pentecostal traditions read 1 Cor 14:29 ("let two or three prophets speak, and let the others weigh what is said") as an ongoing real-time discernment ethic. Jewish tradition (Bavli Yoma 9b) closes prophecy with Malachi and reads the chapter as the closing of an era. Bonhoeffer's *Ethics* draws on the chapter for prophetic discernment under the Third Reich — the German Christian movement was the Hananiah-faction; the Confessing Church the Jeremiah-faction.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: the criterion of true prophecy.** What is the operational test for distinguishing true from false prophecy *in real time*? The text gives several candidate tests in dialectical relationship. Deuteronomy 18:21–22 offers the *fulfillment test*: if the word comes to pass, it is from YHWH. Jeremiah 23:16–22 offers the *moral effect* test: prophets who turn people from evil are true; prophets who fortify false confidence are false. 1 Kings 22 — Micaiah ben Imlah and the *lying spirit* — complicates everything: YHWH himself permits a deceiving spirit to fill the mouths of prophets. Ezekiel 14:9 — "if the prophet is deceived… I, the LORD, have deceived that prophet" — pushes the complication further: the deception is divinely-permitted.

Catholic *Magisterium* tradition resolves the dilemma by *ecclesial mediation*: the church is given authority to test prophetic claims, and individual believers are not on their own. Reformed *sola Scriptura* tradition resolves it by *canonical sufficiency*: the closed canon is the norm. Charismatic and Pentecostal traditions resolve it through *communal weighing* (1 Cor 14:29) and the ongoing fruit-of-the-Spirit test. Jewish rabbinic tradition resolves it by *closing prophecy* with Malachi and reading later "prophetic" claims as illegitimate.

Steelman the Calvin-against-Hananiah position. Calvin's reading is that the *moral effect* test is the operational one: Hananiah comforted the people in their sin; Jeremiah called them to repentance. The fulfillment test is retrospective and therefore useless in the moment of decision. The moral-effect test is forward-looking: does this word call people to repentance, or does it confirm them in their resistance? On this reading, Christians can in fact tell Hananiah from Jeremiah in real time — not by checking whether the prophecy comes true (one cannot know that yet), but by examining whether the message squares with the moral-covenantal trajectory of the canonical witness. Bonhoeffer's *Ethics* extends this to twentieth-century discernment: the German Christian movement preached *peace* with Nazi power; the Confessing Church preached repentance under coming judgment. The structural parallel is exact.

Where this leaves us: real-time discernment is harder than the fulfillment test pretends, but the moral-effect test gives genuine traction. Hananiah was wrong not because his prediction failed in two years but because his message let the people remain unrepentant.

**Tension 2: Hananiah's death.** The immediate divine execution of false prophecy in 28:16–17 raises the question of whether this still operates. The Christian patristic tradition (Augustine, Aquinas) read it as historically conditioned: the immediate-judgment regime of the Old Testament gives way to the longer-arc judgment of the New. Some charismatic traditions (notably Wayne Grudem's reading) treat NT prophecy as *fallible* and recoverable — a prophet who gets it wrong is corrected, not executed. The Reformed tradition closed prophecy with Scripture (Westminster Confession 1.1) and so treats Hananiah's case as canonically completed. Jewish closure of prophecy with Malachi places the question on the same rabbinic shelf.

Steelman the immediate-judgment reading. Hananiah's death is not arbitrary; it follows the Deuteronomic legal sequence (Deut 13, 18) precisely. The sentence ("you shall die because you have uttered *sarah* — rebellion — against YHWH," 28:16) is technical legal language. To declare this regime obsolete is to declare false prophecy a survivable offense — and the New Testament does not in fact treat it that way. Acts 5 (Ananias and Sapphira) shows that lying to the Holy Spirit retains capital consequence in the apostolic period. Galatians 1:8–9's "let him be *anathema*" if any preach a different gospel preserves the death-language theologically if not physically. Revelation's warnings to Jezebel (Rev 2:20–23) preserve the Hananiah-pattern.

Where this leaves us: the immediate execution of Hananiah is not an embarrassment to be domesticated. It is a textual witness that prophetic falsity has stakes — and that the church's softening of those stakes may itself be a Hananiah-move.

**Unresolved question.** Hananiah died two months after Jeremiah's announcement (28:17), but the text never tells us he repented. He simply died. What was the inner life of a man who prophesied with such confidence "Thus says the LORD" — and what does it mean that the canonical witness leaves him no last word? The question hovers over every modern figure who has prophesied in God's name and been proven wrong, from medieval apocalypticists to twentieth-century date-setters to twenty-first century political prophets. The chapter does not give us a script for how to die wrong.

## The Thread

The yoke broken in 28:10 echoes back to the wooden yoke of 27:2 (which Jeremiah was still wearing) and forward to the iron yoke of 28:13–14 — the same metaphor under three metallurgical forms. The Deuteronomic test of true and false prophecy in 28:8–9 is the prophetic application of Deut 18:21–22, and it sets up the New Testament test of 1 John 4:1 ("test the spirits, whether they are of God"). Hananiah's prophesying of *peace* without repentance prefigures Paul's warning in 1 Thessalonians 5:3 — "when people say *peace and safety*, then sudden destruction comes" — a deliberate echo of the false-prophet pattern. And Jeremiah's astonishing "Amen! May the LORD do so" (28:6) prefigures Christ's Gethsemane prayer (Mark 14:36, "if it is possible, let this cup pass") — both true prophets willing to be wrong if mercy could be wrought another way, and both yielding when it could not.
