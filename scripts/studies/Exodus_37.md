# Exodus 37: The Craft of the Holy

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Exodus 37 is pure workshop. Bezalel, named explicitly at v. 1, personally fashions the ark, the mercy seat, the table of showbread, the lampstand, and the incense altar. The chapter parallels the instruction of Exodus 25 and 30:1-10. If the historical framework is Ramesside (thirteenth century BCE), the technical vocabulary matches Late Bronze Age goldsmithing as attested across the Near East. If the P-source dating is post-exilic (so Wellhausen) or First Temple with later editing (so Milgrom, Knohl, Haran), the techniques described nonetheless reach back into metallurgical traditions whose continuity across centuries in the eastern Mediterranean is well documented.

Goldsmithing technique in the chapter is specific and accurate. The ark (vv. 1-5) is acacia wood overlaid with gold — a standard Bronze Age technique attested in Egyptian royal furniture. Tutankhamun's tomb (KV 62, excavated by Howard Carter from 1922) yielded gilded wooden chests whose construction directly parallels the ark's description: wooden core, gold foil applied by adhesion and hammer-smoothing. The mercy seat (*kapporet*, vv. 6-9) is solid gold, not overlay — more expensive, reserved for the most sacred element. The cherubim are "of hammered work" (*miqšâ*, v. 7) raised from the same piece as the mercy seat; this is *repoussé*, worked from the reverse and finished from the front.

The lampstand (*mĕnôrâ*, vv. 17-24) is the chapter's metallurgical tour de force. The text specifies "hammered work" — *miqšâ* again — with the constraint that base, stem, branches, cups, calyxes, and flowers are "of one piece" (*miqšâ ʾaḥat*, v. 22). Made from a single talent of pure gold (v. 24) — approximately 34 kg on the common ANE talent — the form had to emerge from that single mass without separate-and-weld construction. This is extreme repoussé. Rabbinic tradition (Menachot 29a; Rashi on Ex 25:31) holds that Bezalel found the menorah so difficult that Moses had to show him the heavenly pattern.

The menorah is also our clearest archaeological anchor to tabernacle cult objects. The Arch of Titus in Rome (81 CE), commemorating Vespasian and Titus' capture of Jerusalem in 70 CE, depicts in its south panel the Roman triumphal procession carrying the Second Temple's menorah — visibly shown with almond-blossom cups and a seven-branched form matching Exodus 37. The Arch image is not a first-hand depiction of Bezalel's original (destroyed with Solomon's Temple in 586 BCE and replaced multiple times) but indicates substantial continuity of the menorah's form across the tabernacle, First Temple, Second Temple, and later traditions. The modern Israeli state emblem uses this same iconography.

Other cult objects fit known ANE types. The table of showbread (*šulḥān*, vv. 10-16) with crown-molding and carrying rings has Egyptian and Mesopotamian parallels — offering tables before cult statues are standard ANE temple features. The incense altar (vv. 25-28) with horns at the four corners is attested architecturally: a limestone horned altar excavated at Tel Sheva (Beersheba, eighth century BCE), and smaller horned altars recovered at Megiddo and Gezer. Haran and Milgrom both argue the form is authentically Iron Age or earlier. The anointing oil and incense (v. 29) are described as "blended as by the perfumer" — *rōqēaḥ* denotes a specialized trade attested in Egyptian and Mesopotamian temple economies.

The critical source-critical picture: P-source execution narrative, parallel to the Exodus 25 and 30 instruction. The doubling between instruction and execution is characteristic of P literature. LXX ordering for chapters 35-40 continues to diverge from MT; some items attested in MT 37 appear in a different arrangement in the Old Greek, and some are condensed. Wevers' *Notes on the Greek Text of Exodus* and Aejmelaeus' work document this in detail.

Key Hebrew terms: *ʾărôn* (אֲרוֹן) — "chest, ark"; *kapporet* (כַּפֹּרֶת) — "mercy seat, cover, propitiatory" (v. 6), root *kpr* contested — "to cover" or "to atone"; *miqšâ* (מִקְשָׁה) — "hammered work" (v. 7, 17, 22); *mĕnôrâ* (מְנוֹרָה) — "lampstand" (v. 17); *šulḥān* (שֻׁלְחָן) — "table" (v. 10); *qeren* (קֶרֶן) — "horn" (here of the altar, v. 25), the same noun that appears as verb in 34:29 for Moses' face.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is near-pure execution narrative, structured by the "he made" (*wayyaʿaś*) refrain — the subject being Bezalel, singular, throughout. This is the chapter's literary distinctiveness. The broader construction narrative of chapters 36-39 uses a mixture of singular (Bezalel) and plural (all the craftsmen); chapter 37 stays singular. The rabbinic tradition reads this as the text's explicit acknowledgment that the most sacred furnishings were made by Bezalel personally — with his individual hand on each. Rashi (on v. 1) draws attention to the detail. The shift is deliberate.

The chapter's structure follows the sacred geography of the tabernacle, moving outward from center. The ark (vv. 1-9) is the innermost object — destined for the holy of holies. The table and the lampstand (vv. 10-16, 17-24) belong to the holy place outside the inner veil. The incense altar (vv. 25-28) stands just before the veil. The anointing oil and incense (v. 29) are the ritual substances. The literary ordering is theological: the text starts at the center and works outward, establishing the hierarchy of space. Jon Levenson's *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* and Samuel Balentine's *The Torah's Vision of Worship* both read this arrangement as encoded in sacred architecture's spatial theology.

The ark with its *kapporet* (vv. 6-9) is described with unusual textual care. The Hebrew *kapporet* is traditionally translated "mercy seat" (Tyndale's rendering picked up by the KJV), "propitiatory" (Vulgate's *propitiatorium*), or simply "cover" (some modern translations). The root *kpr* is contested — "to cover" on one reading, "to atone" or "to wipe away" on another (cf. Akkadian *kupparu*). Jacob Milgrom's *Leviticus 1-16* (Anchor Bible) favors "to wipe away" based on Mesopotamian cognate analysis. The cherubim's wings overshadow (*sōkĕkîm*, v. 9), with faces turned toward the *kapporet* — the arrangement becomes theologically freighted at Leviticus 16 and Numbers 7:89, where YHWH speaks "from between the cherubim."

The menorah's description (vv. 17-24) is the chapter's poetic peak. The almond-blossom (*mĕšuqqādîm*) motif — cups shaped like almond blossoms with calyx and flower — echoes botanically through the rest of Scripture. Jeremiah 1:11 uses the almond (*šāqēd*) as a wordplay on YHWH's "watching" (*šōqēd*) over his word. Numbers 17:8 has Aaron's almond-budding rod. Carol Meyers' *The Tabernacle Menorah: A Synthetic Study of a Symbol from the Biblical Cult* (her Ph.D. dissertation, published 1976) remains the standard study; she traces the botanical iconography across ANE and biblical material, arguing the menorah is a stylized almond tree of life.

The chapter's literary signature is its *restraint*. Compared to the evocative theophany of chapter 34 or the dramatic restraint-from-giving of chapter 36, chapter 37 is all specification. The prose is dense, technical, almost recipe-like. Childs reads this restraint as itself theologically purposeful: the text refuses to poeticize the making of the most sacred objects. The glory is in the fidelity of execution. Balentine and Fretheim concur.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** Second Temple: Philo (*Life of Moses* 2.95-108) allegorizes the furnishings — ark as wisdom, table as nourishment of souls, menorah as cosmic ordering by light, incense altar as the ascent of pure reason. Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.134ff) reads the twelve loaves as the twelve months, the seven lamps as the seven planets — standard Hellenistic Jewish cosmology.

Rabbinic tradition turns to Bezalel with intense interest. Bava Batra 10a: "Bezalel knew how to combine the letters with which heaven and earth were created." The rabbis read *bĕ-ṣēl ʾēl*, "in the shadow of God," and develop a Kabbalistic-adjacent reading in which Bezalel possesses creative knowledge exceeding Moses' in the domain of sacred construction. Berakhot 55a: Bezalel was twelve or thirteen years old — the Talmudic marker of prodigy.

Rashi (on vv. 1, 17) draws out the singular role of Bezalel. Ibn Ezra works technically on the menorah construction. Ramban's mystical theology of the tabernacle treats the ark and *kapporet* as the locus of continued divine speech (Num 7:89). Maimonides' *Hilchot Beit HaBechirah* and *Hilchot Klei HaMikdash* codify the tabernacle and Temple vessels into halakhic specification: the dimensions of the ark, the form of the menorah, the construction of the incense altar are all normatively binding for future sanctuary construction. Moses Maimonides' *Mishneh Torah* reads the chapter as permanently prescriptive halakha.

The menorah becomes the central Jewish symbol. The Arch of Titus, paradoxically a Roman trophy, makes the Second Temple menorah visually transmissible across the diaspora. Menorah representations appear on Second Temple coinage (Mattathias Antigonus, 40-37 BCE), in synagogue mosaics (Beit Alpha, Hammath Tiberias), and in the Jewish catacombs at Beit Shearim and Rome.

Moderns continue. Nahum Sarna's JPS commentary handles the technical specification. Carol Meyers' dissertation on the menorah is foundational. Jonathan Sacks reads Bezalel as the theological figure of the craftsman — the first biblical person described as filled with the Spirit of God (Ex 31:3, recalled in chapter 35), and the Spirit-filling is for artisanship. This is a striking biblical pattern: the Spirit comes first to the craftsman. Sacks builds a theology of sacred craft from this.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** Patristic readings typology-ize each object. Origen's *Homilies on Exodus* and his *Homilies on Numbers* treat the ark as Christ's body bearing the law, the mercy seat as the meeting place where propitiation occurs (explicitly connected to Rom 3:25, where Paul uses *hilastērion* — the LXX rendering of *kapporet* — for Christ). Gregory of Nyssa treats the menorah as the Word illumining the Church. Augustine in *Questions on the Heptateuch* and *City of God* works the typology, as does Cyril of Alexandria. John Chrysostom's homiletic use of the cult objects pulls the Hebrews 9-10 line forward.

The Epistle to the Hebrews is the chief New Testament reading of chapter 37. Hebrews 9:3-5 enumerates the tabernacle furnishings — "an altar of incense, and the ark of the covenant covered on all sides with gold, in which was a golden urn holding the manna, and Aaron's staff that budded, and the tablets of the covenant. Above it were the cherubim of glory overshadowing the mercy seat." The Hebrews author then typologizes Christ entering the true tabernacle not made with hands. Romans 3:25 uses *hilastērion* (LXX for *kapporet*) for Christ presented as the propitiation — one of the most theologically weighted lexical choices in the New Testament.

Bede's *On the Tabernacle* provides the definitive medieval reading: the ark as Christ, the cherubim as the two testaments reading one another, the menorah as the sevenfold Spirit, the incense altar as prayer. Aquinas (*ST* I-II qq. 101-103; III qq. 60-65) treats the furnishings as preparatory ceremonial law fulfilled in Christ and extended sacramentally. The showbread table is read eucharistically — bread of the Presence prefigures bread of the Altar.

The Reformation splits over iconographic implications. Luther treats the furnishings as abrogated; the body of Christ is the true tabernacle. Reformed iconoclasm appeals to the chapter for the regulative principle: *only* what is commanded may appear in worship. The cherubim were commanded, therefore permitted; other images, not commanded, are prohibited. Zwingli and Calvin both draw this line; Heidelberg Catechism Q. 98 and Westminster Larger Catechism Q. 109 work out the principle.

Critical scholarship: Wellhausen reads 35-40 as post-exilic P retrojection; Milgrom as preserving First Temple cult memory; Haran as pre-exilic priestly. Carol Meyers is foundational on the menorah. Childs reads the chapter as execution-fulfillment of chapter 25's instruction. Fretheim develops the theology of sacred craft. Balentine integrates chapter 37 into the Torah's comprehensive worship vision. Morales reads the furnishings within a canonical theology of sanctuary.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Convergence: both traditions treat the tabernacle furnishings as theologically significant beyond their material function. Both recognize Bezalel as a figure of sacred craftsmanship filled with the Spirit. Both read the menorah, the ark, the mercy seat, the incense altar as loaded with meaning. Divergence: Jewish tradition treats the furnishings as the permanently normative template for the Temple and for eschatological sanctuary. The menorah in particular is a central Jewish symbol — state emblem, synagogue iconography, Hanukkah tradition. Christian tradition treats the furnishings as typological of Christ and the Church, fulfilled and transformed but not reproduced. The iconoclasm-iconography debate within Christianity (Reformed vs. Catholic-Orthodox) further divides the chapter's reception — with both sides appealing to the cherubim of v. 7.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: sacred craft and iconoclasm.** The chapter specifies cherubim of hammered gold (v. 7), visual botanical motifs on the menorah (vv. 17-24), and elaborate metalwork throughout. This sits in tension with the second commandment's prohibition of images (Ex 20:4). How do the traditions handle this?

- *Jewish reading (Maimonides, Hilchot Avodah Zarah 3:10-11)*: the cherubim and menorah decorations were commanded, and commanded images are permitted because they are not made for worship. The prohibition is against worshiping images and against making three-dimensional human figures (with the tabernacle cherubim as explicit exception). Rabbinic halakha develops detailed rules distinguishing permitted decoration (flat or partial) from prohibited full three-dimensional human figures.
- *Orthodox reading (Lossky, John of Damascus, *Three Treatises on the Divine Images*)*: the cherubim of chapter 37 are the Old Testament warrant for Christian iconography. John of Damascus argued explicitly against Byzantine iconoclasm (eighth-ninth centuries) that if God commanded cherubim on the mercy seat, then He does not categorically prohibit images; the Incarnation further warrants images of Christ. The Seventh Ecumenical Council (Nicaea II, 787 CE) settles Orthodox iconography on this reasoning.
- *Catholic reading*: Aquinas (*ST* II-II q. 94) distinguishes images made for worship (prohibited as idolatry) from images used in worship (permitted as aids to devotion). The cherubim of chapter 37 are instances of the latter. The Council of Trent (1563) reaffirmed the legitimacy of sacred images with this line of reasoning.
- *Reformed reading (Calvin, *Institutes* 1.11)*: the cherubim are the exception that proves the rule. They were commanded by God specifically and uniquely; therefore they are permitted. All images not commanded are prohibited by the second commandment. Calvin in his *Commentary on Exodus* 37:7-9 emphasizes that the cherubim are hidden within the veil, not publicly displayed for worship. The Westminster Larger Catechism Q. 109 prohibits "making any representation of God, of all or of any of the three persons, either inwardly in our mind, or outwardly in any kind of image or likeness of any creature whatsoever."
- *Lutheran reading (Luther)*: images are adiaphora — neither commanded nor prohibited — and may be retained if they serve edification. Luther's relatively lenient position allowed much medieval church art to survive the Lutheran Reformation. Chapter 37 evidences that God is not image-phobic.
- *Critical reading (Childs, Meyers)*: the tension is genuine and canonically preserved. The chapter's rich visual specification coexists with the second commandment's prohibition in the same book of Exodus. The text does not resolve the tension; later traditions do, differently.

*Steelman the least intuitive — the Orthodox reading.* To Reformed ears the Orthodox appeal to chapter 37 as iconographic warrant sounds like special pleading — "one commanded image justifies centuries of churches full of icons." The charitable reading is that Orthodox iconography rests on a different theological claim: the Incarnation changes the conditions of image-making. God in Christ has become visible. The cherubim of chapter 37 did not yet instantiate that possibility, but they prepared the theological ground — they established that visibility and sacredness are not categorically opposed. John of Damascus is not arguing that the cherubim directly warrant icons of the saints; he is arguing that they falsify any *absolute* image-prohibition and open theological space that the Incarnation fills. One can disagree with the conclusion without dismissing the argument.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter contains genuine iconographic material commanded by God. All major traditions acknowledge this and draw different lines. Reformed: images are prohibited except when commanded, and the commanded exceptions are tightly bounded. Lutheran: images are indifferent, useful if edifying. Catholic: images are permitted as aids, with care against idolatry. Orthodox: images are enabled by Incarnation and retain sacred value. Jewish: images are permitted when commanded and not worshiped; three-dimensional human figures prohibited. The text does not close the question.

**Tension 2: Bezalel and priestly authority.** The chapter singles out Bezalel by name (v. 1) and ascribes to him the personal making of the most sacred objects. Yet Bezalel is neither priest nor Levite — he is of the tribe of Judah. The priestly office belongs to Aaron and his sons. How is the construction of the sanctuary accomplished by a non-priest?

- *Jewish reading (Bava Batra 10a, Berakhot 55a)*: the rabbinic tradition treats Bezalel's role as the paradigm of spirit-filled craft, distinct from priestly office but indispensable to it. The Spirit's filling for artisanship is a distinct category from the Spirit's filling for prophecy or for priestly service. Sacks in *Covenant and Conversation* reads Bezalel as the theological founding of sacred craft as a vocation equal to prophecy and priesthood in dignity, though distinct in function.
- *Catholic reading*: CCC 2501-2503 and papal teaching on art treat sacred craftsmanship as a legitimate charism. The artist-craftsman stands in a sanctified vocation, not as priest but as servant of priestly worship.
- *Reformed reading*: Calvin on vv. 1-2 treats Bezalel as the paradigm of the godly layman whose skilled work serves the church's worship under the ministry of the priesthood (later the presbyterate). The Spirit's filling for craft is the theological warrant for vocational craftsmanship — all honest skilled work is godly service.
- *Lutheran reading (Luther, *Treatise on Good Works*)*: Bezalel's sacred craft is an Old Testament instance of the priesthood of all believers applied to specific vocation. All work done in faith is sacred work.
- *Orthodox reading*: iconography is a hieratic craft, neither priestly in the narrow sense nor simply lay; iconographers traditionally work under ecclesial discipline, prayer, and fasting. The Bezalel pattern prefigures this discipline.
- *Critical reading (Brueggemann, Carol Meyers)*: the text's prominence given to Bezalel over the priestly line encodes a theology of community-wide participation in sacred construction. The priestly prerogative is preserved (Aaron's consecration in Lev 8-9 follows), but the *making* of the sanctuary is distributed across skilled members.

*Steelman the least intuitive — the critical reading.* To those suspicious of sociological readings, Brueggemann's emphasis on "distributed participation" can sound like retrojection of modern democratic categories. The charitable reading takes the textual prominence of Bezalel seriously: the chapter uses singular "he made" throughout for Bezalel, not for the priests. The text is not priest-centered. The sanctuary is *made* by the skilled among the whole people; it is *served* by the priests. This is a structural theological claim, not a modern political one.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter locates sacred craft in the whole people, with Spirit-filled skill as its warrant. Every major tradition finds in Bezalel the theological dignity of artisanship — craft as vocation, not just trade. Traditions differ on whether this warrants extended lay ministry or remains sharply distinct from priestly office.

*Unresolved:* If the menorah's "one piece of hammered gold" construction was so technically demanding that rabbinic tradition developed the legend of Moses needing to show Bezalel the heavenly pattern, what theology of sacred craft does that legend encode? Is the sacred object achievable by human skill alone, or does its making require a vision of the original that skill can approximate but not autonomously produce?

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references.

First: Romans 3:25. Paul presents Christ as *hilastērion* — the LXX word for *kapporet*, the mercy seat of Exodus 37:6-9. Paul is explicitly identifying Jesus with the place of atonement where cherubim overshadow and blood is sprinkled (Lev 16). This is one of the most typologically weighted single words in the New Testament, and its root runs through Exodus 37 back to the sacrificial vocabulary of Leviticus. The lexical thread is tight.

Second: 1 Kings 7:48-50. Solomon's Temple furnishings echo the tabernacle furnishings of chapter 37 but on a grander scale: the table of showbread, the menorahs (plural — ten of them, not one), the incense altar. The Chronicler (2 Chron 4) amplifies. The question for readers: is the tabernacle or the Temple the theological template? Jewish tradition tends toward Temple as fulfillment of tabernacle; Christian tradition treats both as preparatory for Christ's body. The expansion from one menorah to ten is itself a theological datum — the Solomonic project is richer, but is it more theologically mature?

Third: Revelation 11:19 — "the temple of God in heaven was opened, and the ark of his covenant was seen within his temple." The ark, lost after 587 BCE (2 Macc 2:4-8 has Jeremiah hiding it; rabbinic tradition varies), reappears in Johannine apocalyptic vision. The lost ark — untraceable archaeologically despite centuries of searching — has become the hinge of the biblical story's eschatological imagination. Chapter 37's description is what Revelation 11 is picturing. The ark made by Bezalel has not survived, but its theological afterlife is vast.
