# Revelation 17: The Woman on the Beast and the Seven Mountains

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The mainstream consensus places the Apocalypse in the last decade of Domitian's reign, c. AD 95, on the basis of Irenaeus's testimony in *Against Heresies* 5.30.3. David Aune's three-volume Word commentary, G.K. Beale's NIGTC volume, Craig Koester's Anchor Yale commentary, and Richard Bauckham's *Climax of Prophecy* all anchor here. The minority Neronian dating (J.A.T. Robinson, *Redating the New Testament*; Kenneth Gentry, *Before Jerusalem Fell*) finds its single strongest argument inside this chapter, at 17:10. We will return to that crux below; the historical-world stakes of chapter 17 cannot be separated from the dating debate, because the chapter is the most explicitly Roman-political vision in the book.

The provincial setting Steven Friesen and Adela Yarbro Collins reconstruct involves the imperial cult as the unifying civic religion of the seven cities of Asia. The cult of *Roma Aeterna* — the deified personification of the city of Rome — was not an antiquarian abstraction but a living provincial reality. Smyrna's temple to Roma was dedicated in 195 BC, the earliest such temple anywhere in the Mediterranean. The provincial *koinon* of Asia held annual festivals at provincial *neokoria* — Pergamum, Smyrna, Ephesus all bearing this title at various points. Friesen's *Imperial Cults and the Apocalypse of John* documents the civic-architectural saturation: temples, processions, coinage, athletic festivals all carried imperial-cult iconography. The "great prostitute, who is seated on many waters" (17:1) and "arrayed in purple and scarlet, and adorned with gold and jewels and pearls" (17:4) is a sustained inversion of Roman civic statuary depicting Roma as a goddess in imperial regalia. Bauckham reads the entire vision as a parodic counter-image to the official Roman self-presentation.

The equation Babylon = Rome was not Christian innovation. Sibylline Oracles 5.137-178 calls Rome "Babylon" explicitly. 4 Ezra (Latin: 2 Esdras) 3:1-2 begins, "In the thirtieth year after the destruction of our city, I, Salathiel, who am also called Ezra, was in Babylon" — written in the late first century after Rome's destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70, it makes the equation transparent. 2 Baruch 11:1 and 67:7 do the same. The shared post-70 Jewish-Christian apocalyptic vocabulary, as Loren Stuckenbruck, John Collins, James Charlesworth, and Daniel Boyarin have all argued, identifies Rome with the empire that destroyed Jerusalem in 587 BC. The destruction-by-Rome of the Second Temple in AD 70 made the typology unavoidable: as the first Babylon destroyed the first temple, the new Babylon destroyed the second.

The internal markers in chapter 17 force the Roman identification independently of these external parallels. The "seven mountains on which the woman is seated" (17:9) match Rome's famous seven hills: Aventine, Caelian, Capitoline, Esquiline, Palatine, Quirinal, and Viminal — a topographical commonplace celebrated on Vespasianic and Domitianic coins (the *Roma sedens* type, with the goddess seated on the seven hills). Roman writers from Varro to Cicero to Virgil to Pliny named the city *septicollis*, "seven-hilled." 17:18 is decisive: "the woman that you saw is the great city that has dominion over the kings of the earth." There was, in the Mediterranean of AD 95, exactly one city to which that description could refer.

The riddle of 17:10 — "five have fallen, one is, the other has not yet come" — is the central crux of New Testament dating. Counting from Augustus: 1 Augustus, 2 Tiberius, 3 Caligula, 4 Claudius, 5 Nero. The sixth ("one is") is then Vespasian (skipping the brief 69 AD trio as transitional); the seventh, Titus, "must remain only a little while" — and Titus reigned only June 79 to September 81. Robinson and Gentry use this to date the book to Vespasian's reign. The mainstream Domitianic readers (Aune, Bauckham, Koester) reply that the count is *symbolic* — seven is completeness, not literal succession — with the Domitianic horizon secured by Irenaeus's external testimony. The eighth king "of the seven" (17:11) is widely identified with the *Nero redivivus* legend, the rumor that Nero had not died but would return from the East with Parthian armies (Sibylline Oracles 4-5).

The cup of abominations (17:4) draws on Jeremiah 51:7 ("Babylon was a golden cup in the LORD's hand, making all the earth drunken; the nations drank of her wine; therefore the nations went mad"). The woman drunk with martyrs' blood (17:6) draws on the prophetic-martyr tradition (Jeremiah 2:34; 2 Kings 21:16; Matthew 23:35-37) and locates Rome's first-century persecution within that canonical pattern.

Key Greek terms: *pornē* (πόρνη — prostitute, used pejoratively for unfaithful covenant cities in Hosea, Ezekiel, Isaiah); *Babylōn hē megalē* (Βαβυλὼν ἡ μεγάλη — Babylon the great, 17:5); *thērion* (θηρίον — beast, continued from chapter 13); *epta orē* (ἑπτὰ ὄρη — seven mountains, 17:9, the Roman *septem montes*); *mystērion* (μυστήριον — mystery, 17:5, 17:7, marking the apocalyptic mode of disclosure-by-symbol).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre considerations: chapter 17 is an *ekphrasis* — an extended description of an image or figure, here the woman on the beast — followed by an apocalyptic *Deutung* (interpretive disclosure) in which an angelic interpreter explains the vision (17:7-18). The *Deutung* form is borrowed directly from Daniel 7-8, where Daniel sees a vision and a heavenly figure interprets it. Aune categorizes the chapter as an *epideictic* discourse with an interpretive coda. The structural parallel with Daniel 7's four beasts and ten horns is so close that Beale and Bauckham both treat chapter 17 as a Daniel-mediated unmasking of Rome.

Within the larger architecture of the book, 17-18 form an extended interlude. Strictly speaking, the seventh bowl has already announced "it is done" at 16:17, and the sequence-of-judgment is finished. Chapters 17-18 zoom in on what the bowls accomplished, expanding what 14:8 anticipated and what 16:19 compressed: the fall of Babylon. The literary function is *retrospective amplification* — the seer slows the narrative to reveal in detail what was telegraphed in shorthand. This pattern (interlude/expansion after a sequence-conclusion) is consistent with apocalyptic literature's non-linear temporal logic.

The chapter's ekphrastic image-work is precise. The woman is "arrayed in purple and scarlet" (17:4) — the imperial colors. She holds "a golden cup full of abominations" (17:4) — the inversion of the gold *phialai* of 5:8 and 16. The forehead-name "Babylon the great, mother of prostitutes" (17:5) recalls the inscription practice of Roman cult-statuary and inverts the mark of the saints (the name of the Lamb on their foreheads, 14:1). Drunkenness with blood (17:6) is itself an inversion of the eucharistic cup. The accumulation of inversions — purple/martyrs' robes, gold cup/wine of wrath, forehead-name/Lamb's name — makes the woman the comprehensive *anti-image* of the bride-city of 21:2.

The structural irony at 17:16 is sharp: "the ten horns that you saw, they and the beast will hate the prostitute. They will make her desolate and naked, and devour her flesh and burn her up with fire." The empire-system ultimately destroys what it sustained; the political-economic order rides until it doesn't, then turns on its rider. Bauckham reads this as the seer's claim that imperial idolatry contains its own self-destruction — a Pauline and prophetic insight (Romans 1:24-28; Jeremiah's image of Babylon undoing itself). The verse has been the basis for readings as different as Augustine's *City of God* (the *civitas terrena* destroys itself) and Marx's analyses of capital's internal contradictions.

The recapitulation school (Hendriksen, Beale) reads 17-18 as detailing a single fall narrated repeatedly. The dispensational reading (Walvoord, LaHaye) reads them as sequentially distinct future events.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical story Revelation 17 condenses runs from the prophetic anti-imperial oracles (Isaiah 13-14, 21, 47; Jeremiah 50-51; Ezekiel 16, 23, 26-28; Daniel 7) through the early Jewish apocalyptic literature (1 Enoch, 4 Ezra, 2 Baruch, the Sibyllines) into the Christian apocalyptic mode the seer of Patmos exemplifies. The image of the unfaithful city as a prostitute is canonical (Hosea 1-3; Ezekiel 16, 23; Isaiah 1:21 — "How the faithful city has become a whore"). The novel turn in Revelation is to apply this prophetic-Israel imagery to *Rome*, not Israel — the line of judgment that had run inward against Jerusalem now turns outward against the empire that destroyed Jerusalem.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

Patristic readers identified Babylon as Rome with little hesitation. Eusebius (*Hist. Eccl.* 3.18) reads the persecution under Nero and Domitian as the historical embodiment of the chapter. Tertullian (*Against Marcion* 3.13) and Jerome (*Letter 121*) explicitly identify Babylon-of-the-Apocalypse with Rome. Augustine's *City of God* XX expands the identification: Rome is one historical embodiment of the *civitas terrena*, but the city of man perdures across all history; Babylon will reappear in every empire-form. This ambiguity — first-century Rome but also every imperial structure — becomes the seedbed of all later interpretations.

Medieval reading was largely ecclesial. Joachim of Fiore in the twelfth century made the most consequential medieval move: he identified Babylon with the corrupt institutional church of his own day. The Joachite tradition fed into Spiritual Franciscan critique and prepared the ground for the Reformation's reading.

The Reformation reactivated chapter 17 as polemic. Luther's *On the Babylonian Captivity of the Church* (1520) takes its title directly from Revelation's Babylon. Luther's preface to Revelation names the seven hills, the cup, and the bloody woman as direct identifications of papal Rome. The "papal Antichrist" thesis — held by Luther, Tyndale, Calvin (more cautiously; Calvin wrote no commentary on Revelation), the Westminster divines, and the Puritan tradition — read chapter 17 as canonical authorization for separation from Rome. The Anabaptists (Denck, Marpeck) extended it: the corrupted church is not only Roman but *any* state-fused church, including the magisterial Reformation establishments.

The modern critical-scholarly shift, beginning with the historical-critical recovery of the first-century horizon, has tended to bracket the Reformation polemic in favor of the Domitianic-Roman reading. Bauckham, Aune, Koester, Yarbro Collins, Mounce, Caird, and Boring all anchor the historical referent in first-century Rome. Bauckham's distinctive contribution is to insist that this first-century specificity does not exhaust the meaning: the typological structure makes Rome the *paradigmatic* empire, of which any subsequent imperial structure is an instantiation.

Brian Blount, Clarice Martin, Demetrius Williams, Allan Callahan, and Stephen Moore from the African-American and postcolonial traditions read the chapter as canonical resource against any imperial system that traffics in human bodies. Catherine Keller (*Apocalypse Now and Then*; *Political Theology of the Earth*) reads Babylon as global late capitalism. Walter Wink's *Powers* trilogy reads the Beast and the Whore as the Domination System.

### Four Interpretive Schools

- **Preterist**: Babylon = Jerusalem under Roman judgment in 70 AD (David Chilton, Kenneth Gentry — the so-called "neo-preterist" or "covenantal-preterist" reading; the case is built on Babylon's "drunk with the blood of the saints," 17:6, and the consistent biblical trope of unfaithful Jerusalem as harlot, Isaiah 1:21, Ezekiel 16, Ezekiel 23). Or, in the more common partial-preterist reading (R.C. Sproul), Babylon = first-century Rome, with the chapter fulfilled in the events of 68-96.
- **Historicist**: Babylon = papal Rome through church history (the classic Reformation reading; Albert Barnes; many nineteenth-century English-speaking commentaries).
- **Futurist (dispensational)**: Babylon = a literal future rebuilt city of Babylon in Iraq (Tim LaHaye, *Left Behind*); or the Vatican (a popular dispensational variant); or a future global economic capital (sometimes identified with New York, Brussels, or Beijing depending on the commentator's geopolitical preoccupation).
- **Idealist (perennialist)**: Babylon = the perennial empire-system, the worldly city, the consumer civilization. Beale and Hendriksen represent this reading; Bauckham holds the typological version that maintains first-century specificity *and* trans-historical relevance.

### Jewish Scholarly Engagement

The contemporary Jewish parallels are indispensable. Sibylline Oracles 5 (especially 5.137-178) names Rome as Babylon, draws on the *Nero redivivus* tradition, and prophesies Rome's fall in language that Revelation 17 nearly echoes line for line. 4 Ezra and 2 Baruch, written within a generation of Revelation, share the same equation. James Charlesworth's two-volume *Old Testament Pseudepigrapha* makes these parallels accessible; John Collins's *The Apocalyptic Imagination* situates Revelation in the genre as a whole; Loren Stuckenbruck's work on the *Enoch* tradition shows the deeper apocalyptic vocabulary the seer inherits.

The prophetic-Israel-as-prostitute background (Ezekiel 16 and 23 most pointedly) is doing complex work in chapter 17. The seer is using the canonical anti-Israel idiom *against Rome* — a move that simultaneously borrows the prophetic line of judgment and shifts its target. Daniel Boyarin's argument that Revelation is what happens when a Jewish apocalyptic seer uses Christian frame to recast the canonical anti-imperial tradition for a new audience captures this precisely.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What God is doing in chapter 17: through the angelic *Deutung*, God exposes the actual structure of imperial power. The empire that appears as Roma the goddess — golden, regal, immortal — is shown to be a beast carrying a prostitute, drunk with the blood of saints, that ten horns will turn against and devour. The pastoral imperative is twofold: do not be deceived by imperial glamour; understand that empire's collapse is engineered (literarily, providentially) into empire's structure (17:16-17).

### Tension 1: Babylon's Identity

The mainstream historical-critical reading takes Babylon as first-century Rome. The internal markers (seven hills, dominion over kings, cup of abominations, drunk with martyrs' blood) and the contemporary Jewish parallels make this reading nearly compulsory historically. The pastoral function: encourage saints in Asia Minor to see through Roman propaganda.

The Reformation historicist reading — Babylon is papal Rome — was the dominant Protestant reading from 1520 to roughly 1900. Luther, Tyndale, Calvin (cautiously), the Westminster Confession (in its 1647 form identifying the Pope as Antichrist), the Puritans, and the Princeton tradition through Hodge held versions of this reading. Honest engagement requires neither rehabilitation nor dismissal: the polemic identified real corruptions of late-medieval ecclesial-political fusion that the chapter's image-grammar genuinely fits, while also extending the typology beyond what historical-critical reading can warrant.

The neo-preterist reading (Chilton, Gentry) takes Babylon as first-century Jerusalem under Roman judgment, citing the prophetic Israel-as-prostitute precedent (Ezekiel 16, 23) and Revelation 11:8's "where their Lord was crucified." Mainstream scholarship rejects this reading because of the seven hills and 17:18's "dominion over the kings of the earth," which Jerusalem of AD 70 manifestly did not have.

The futurist-dispensational reading takes Babylon as a literal future rebuilt Babylon — sometimes identified with the actual city in modern Iraq (Saddam Hussein's reconstruction of Babylon in the 1980s figured large in late-twentieth-century dispensational literature), sometimes with the Vatican, sometimes with a future global capital.

The idealist reading takes Babylon as the perennial empire-system. Beale's exegesis maintains the first-century anchor while reading the typological extension as authorized by the chapter's own image-grammar.

The least-intuitive position to steelman is the Reformation historicist reading — papal Rome as Babylon. Modern ecumenism makes this position embarrassing, but it must be steelmanned for the chapter's reception history. The steelman: the late-medieval papacy presented a sustained image-fit. It was institutionally located in Rome (the seven hills); it claimed dominion over kings (medieval papal supremacy in temporal matters); it was clothed in scarlet and purple (cardinalate vestments); it gathered immense wealth (the indulgence-trade; Renaissance Borgia and Medici excesses); it persecuted dissenters (the Inquisition; Hus, Tyndale); and it administered a sacramental cup the Reformers judged a cup of doctrinal abominations. Whether or not one grants the polemic, the image-fit was sufficiently strong to convince the magisterial Reformation, the Puritans, and the original Westminster divines.

Where this leaves us: how Christians identify Babylon shapes how Christians live politically. The first-century-only reading frees Christians from any contemporary political reading; the perennial reading enjoins critique of every imperial form, including one's own; the Reformation reading historicizes the polemic to a specific institutional opponent; the futurist reading defers political application to a future scenario.

### Tension 2: Religion's Relation to Empire

The image of the prostitute *riding* the beast (17:3) makes the chapter a site of perennial reflection on church-state relation. The woman is not the beast but is sustained by the beast; she rides until the beast turns. What does this image authorize?

The Anabaptist reading (Denck, Marpeck, the Schleitheim tradition; in modern theology John Howard Yoder, Stanley Hauerwas) takes the image as a critique of *all* church-state entanglement. The true church is the visible community of disciples gathered around the Lamb (14:1-5); any union with the beast — Constantinian, magisterial Reformation, modern Christian Nationalist — is the prostitute's posture. The American Anabaptist tradition (Hutterite, Amish, Mennonite) has lived this reading with unusual consistency.

The mainstream Catholic and magisterial Reformation reading takes the image as a critique only of *idolatrous* fusion. Legitimate cooperation between church and political authority is not the prostitute's posture; only when church seeks empire's protection by trading in the empire's idolatry — by participating in the imperial cult, by endorsing the beast's blasphemies — does the chapter's critique apply. Catholic social teaching has held some version of this through *Rerum Novarum*, *Quadragesimo Anno*, *Caritas in Veritate*, and *Laudato Si'*. The Reformed magisterial tradition similarly distinguishes legitimate civil cooperation from idolatrous fusion.

The preterist reading takes the chapter's critique as specific to the Roman imperial cult of the late first century — a historical particular without ongoing application to other church-state arrangements.

The contemporary American debate centers on Christian Nationalism — the integralist proposal (R.J. Rushdoony's Christian Reconstruction, more recent advocates like Stephen Wolfe, *The Case for Christian Nationalism*) versus the separationist response (Russell Moore's *Onward*, Tim Keller's late writings on the gospel and political pluralism, the broader evangelical-pluralist tradition). The chapter is invoked on both sides: separationists cite the prostitute-on-beast image as warning against any fusion; integralists cite the chapter as critique of *pagan* empire only, leaving open the possibility of a properly Christian polity.

The least-intuitive position to steelman is the integralist one. The mainstream evangelical and Catholic positions both reject Christian Nationalism in its strong forms; the steelman therefore: chapter 17 critiques empire that is *idolatrous* — that worships beast-power and demands worship as the price of belonging. A polity that explicitly subordinated political authority to the rule of Christ, on this reading, would not be the prostitute-on-beast but its inverse — the visible expression of the Lamb's lordship over the kings of the earth. Whether such a polity is historically possible or politically wise is a separate question; the integralist's claim is that the chapter does not rule it out, and that the Anabaptist's universal critique of church-state cooperation reads more into the image than the image warrants. The steelman force of this reading is that it takes seriously the chapter's distinction between *worshiping* the beast and *living in* a political order — a distinction the text does maintain.

Where this leaves us: every proposed political theology stands or falls in part on how it reads chapter 17. The chapter is not a manual but an image, and the image is doing harder work than any single position has yet exhausted.

### Closing Question

The ten horns turn on the prostitute (17:16-17) — the empire's own structure dissolves the religion that rode it. If empire eventually devours its sustaining religion, and if every age's church is, in some measure, riding some beast, then which beast is the church of your moment riding, and what does the inevitable turn look like for those who didn't see the riding for what it was?
