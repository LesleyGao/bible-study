# Jeremiah 10: Idols of Wood, the God of Storehouses, and the Aramaic Verse That Will Not Stay Put

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jeremiah 10 sits in the late Judean monarchy, between Josiah's death (609 BC) and the first Babylonian deportation (605), with vv. 17–22 likely reflecting an actual moment of besieged anticipation: "Gather up your bundle from the ground, O you who dwell under siege." The historical horizon is the same one chs. 7–9 occupy — the temple still standing, the court still calculating, the Babylonian shadow growing across the Levant. The Battle of Carchemish (605 BC) decisively ends Egyptian hegemony and establishes Babylonian power; the Babylonian Chronicle (BM 21946), excavated and published by D.J. Wiseman in 1956, dates that confrontation precisely. Jehoiakim's revolt against Nebuchadnezzar around 601 sets up the 597 siege, the deportation that takes Jehoiachin and the elite (including Ezekiel) into exile.

The astrological horizon of v. 2 — *al tehattu me'otot ha-shamayim*, "be not dismayed at the signs of the heavens" — points squarely at Babylonian celestial divination. The neo-Babylonian period was a high-water mark of *Enūma Anu Enlil* astrological omen-reading, and the *kalu*-priests and *bārû*-diviners constructed the world's most sophisticated astronomical observation tradition specifically in service to political prophecy. Solar and lunar eclipses, planetary conjunctions, and stellar phenomena were read as portents of imperial fate. Jeremiah's audience would have known this; the imperial culture pressing down on Judah considered celestial signs the language of divine government. Jeremiah's instruction *not to be dismayed* is a polemic against a specific imperial epistemology.

Material culture: idol manufacture is technologically specific. Wood from Lebanon or local forest, overlaid with beaten silver from Tarshish (probably Tartessos in southern Spain, or perhaps Sardinia per recent epigraphic argument) and gold from Uphaz (location debated; possibly Ophir, Arabian Peninsula). Violet and purple clothing — *tekhelet* and *argaman* — names the Murex-snail dyes whose production sites stretched along the Phoenician coast (Tyre, Sidon) and into Cyprus. The technology Jer 10:3–9 describes is real Iron Age IIc cult-image production: chryselephantine and woodcore-with-precious-metal-overlay statues, attested archaeologically across the Levant. The "scarecrows in a cucumber field" image (v. 5) likely refers to a *tomer*-trunk scarecrow (Hebrew *tomer miqsha*), a wooden pillar-figure used to frighten birds in melon and cucumber gardens — a bracingly rustic comparison to the elite craftsmanship just described.

The Khirbet el-Qom inscription (8th-c. tomb inscription mentioning "Yahweh and his asherah") and the Kuntillet Ajrud pithoi (early 8th-c. caravanserai inscriptions, also mentioning "Yahweh and his asherah") attest to syncretistic Yahwism where Asherah-worship operated alongside or within YHWH-cult. Jeremiah's polemic in chs. 7–10 against the "queen of heaven" (Jer 7:18, 44:17–19, almost certainly Astarte/Ishtar) and against idol-fashioning fits this archaeological context exactly. The Lachish Ostraca (1935–38, contemporary with Jer 34:7) preserve the texture of late-monarchy Judah; the Baruch ben Neriah, Gemariah ben Shaphan, Jehucal ben Shelemiah, and Pashhur ben Immer bullae from the City of David put Jeremiah's interlocutors in our hands.

Authorship and dating remain contested in a way that ch. 10 makes acute. The chapter contains the single Aramaic verse in Jeremiah (v. 11), an anomaly that has driven critical and traditional commentary alike to explain it as either later interpolation, scribal gloss, original message *to* Aramaic-speaking exiles, or pedagogical formula for Jews to deploy against pagan neighbors. **Jack R. Lundbom (Anchor Bible 1999–2004)** treats the verse as authentic to Jeremiah, intended for diaspora use. William Holladay (Hermeneia 1986/89) argues it was added later, possibly during or after the exile. William McKane (ICC 1986/96) treats it as a "rolling corpus" addition. Robert P. Carroll (OTL 1986) reads ch. 10 as substantially post-exilic, comparable to Second Isaiah. Walter Brueggemann reads the chapter theologically as a confrontation between two cosmologies. Bernhard Duhm (1901) and Sigmund Mowinckel (1914) located much of ch. 10 in their Source C (Deuteronomistic prose) or in still-later strata.

The MT–LXX divergence in ch. 10 is perhaps the most acute single textual problem in the entire book. Verses 6–8 and 10 are entirely missing from the LXX. The LXX places v. 9 inside v. 5. The Qumran evidence is decisive: 4QJer-b (mid-2nd c. BC) preserves a Hebrew Vorlage that matches the LXX, omitting vv. 6–8 and 10; 4QJer-a tracks the MT. Two Hebrew editions of Jer 10 circulated side by side in late Second Temple Judaism. This is not a minor textual variant; it is two structurally different chapters.

Key terms: *hevel* (הֶבֶל, "vanity, breath, futility" — the same word that runs through Ecclesiastes), used in v. 3 of idol-worship and again in v. 15; *helqat ya'aqov* (חֵלֶק יַעֲקֹב, "the portion of Jacob," v. 16), Yahweh as Israel's tribal allotment in inverse to Levi receiving Yahweh as inheritance (Deut 10:9); *yatzar* (יָצַר, "to form," v. 16), the Genesis 2:7 verb of divine craft contrasted with idol-craftsmanship; *otzarot* (אוֹצָרוֹת, "storehouses," v. 13), a meteorological metaphor with deep Mesopotamian parallels.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is structured as a chiastic confrontation between two cosmologies. Outer frame: oracle to Israel (v. 1) and to the gods who did not make heaven and earth (v. 11). Inner frame: idol-polemic poetry (vv. 2–5, 8–9, 14–15) interleaved with creator-doxology (vv. 6–7, 10, 12–13, 16). Then the abrupt shift to siege-distress (vv. 17–22) and the prophet's prayer (vv. 23–25). The structure performs the chapter's argument: idols are mocked from outside while Yahweh is praised from inside; then the praising voice itself collapses into lament as the Babylonian sword approaches — and the doxology of vv. 12–13 will return verbatim in 51:15–16 as the rationale for Babylon's fall, closing the book's largest theological loop.

The idol-polemic genre (vv. 2–5, 8–9, 14–15) belongs to a recognizable Hebrew Bible sub-genre: Isaiah 40:18–20, 41:6–7, 44:9–20, 46:1–7, Habakkuk 2:18–19, Psalms 115 and 135, Wisdom of Solomon 13–15, the Letter of Jeremiah (= Baruch 6, deuterocanonical). The literary parallels are dense; the genealogical question of who borrows from whom is contested. John Goldingay argues Isaiah 40s draw on Jer 10. Some critical commentators (Carroll, McKane) argue the reverse — Jer 10's polemic is a post-exilic insertion drawing on Deutero-Isaiah's mature monotheism. The chapter's idol-mockery is more concentrated and rhetorically sharper than in Isaiah, especially the cucumber-field scarecrow line (v. 5).

The single Aramaic verse (v. 11) is a literary anomaly. In a Hebrew prophetic book preserved in Hebrew throughout, one Aramaic sentence sits in the middle of ch. 10, syntactically free of its surroundings, theologically precise: *elaha di shemayya we'arqa la 'avadu yevadu me-ar'a u-min teḥot shemayya elleh*, "the gods who did not make the heavens and the earth shall perish from the earth and from under these heavens." The verse is in *imperial Aramaic*, not the dialect of Daniel's Aramaic chapters. Three theories: (1) Lundbom — Jeremiah dictated it deliberately as a formula for Jews to use against Aramaic-speaking pagan neighbors during exile; (2) Holladay, McKane — later scribal gloss inserted as a marginal pedagogical note that became canonical; (3) Carroll — evidence of post-exilic compositional layering, where the chapter's final form belongs to the exilic or post-exilic Jewish diaspora.

The doxological poetry (vv. 12–13, 16) is exquisite. *Otzarot*, "storehouses" of wind, anticipates the cosmology of Job 38:22 (storehouses of snow and hail) and reflects shared ANE meteorological imagery, including parallels in the *Enūma Eliš* tablets where Marduk controls winds and storms. But Jer 10:13's image converts cosmic-craft mythology into pure praise: Yahweh has *workshops* of weather. The line *yatzar ha-kol hu*, "he is the one who formed all things," uses the potter-verb of Gen 2:7, putting Yahweh the *yotzer* in pointed contrast to the idol-makers who *yatzaru* idols out of dead matter (v. 14). The pun is the chapter's theological hinge.

The prophet's lament (vv. 19–22) is *qina* meter. The siege-evacuation imagery (v. 17, *ispi me-eretz kin'atech, yoshevet ba-matzor*, "gather your bundle from the ground, O dweller under siege") is dense and physical; Bar-Efrat reads the verb cluster as evoking refugees lifting belongings into a cloth bundle on a stick. Verses 19–21 voice the personification of "Daughter Zion" who has lost children and tent — a metaphor that will be the structural backbone of Lamentations.

Canonical position: the second of Latter Prophets in the Hebrew Bible, after Isaiah. Within Jeremiah, ch. 10 sits in the prose-and-poetry temple-sermon block of chs. 7–10, which Stulman calls the book's "Scroll One" doom announcement.

Textual note: 4QJer-b (mid-2nd c. BC, Cave 4 at Qumran) preserves the shorter LXX-form Hebrew text of Jer 10. This is one of the strongest pieces of evidence in the entire Hebrew Bible textual tradition for plural canonical text-forms in late Second Temple Judaism.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the Hebrew canon, Jer 10 stands at the crossroads of monotheism, exile, and the rejection of imperial cosmology. Backward: Deut 4:15–20's prohibition on cult-images, Deut 32's Song of Moses on the gods of the nations, Isa 2:8 on idol-fashioning, Isa 40s' mature monotheistic polemic. Forward: the doxology returns verbatim in Jer 51:15–16 as the basis for Yahweh's judgment on Babylon (the chapter's theological loop closes only at the book's end). Lamentations' bereaved Mother Zion grows out of Jer 10:19–20. Daniel 2 and 4 will deploy the same Aramaic-prophetic mode against imperial Babylon. Wisdom of Solomon 13–15 elaborates the idol-polemic for a Hellenistic Jewish audience.

**Jewish trajectory.** Sirach 49:6–7 and Bavli Bava Batra 14b–15a place Jeremiah in the canon. The Aramaic verse (10:11) attracted intense rabbinic attention: the Talmud (Bavli Megillah 3a) considers whether the entire book might originally have been bilingual; Rashi and Radak both treat v. 11 as Jeremiah's instruction *to the exiles* on what to say to their pagan neighbors. Targum Jonathan paraphrases the Hebrew of the chapter and leaves the Aramaic verse untouched. Pesikta Rabbati and Yalkut Shimoni use Jer 10:7's "Who would not fear you, O King of the nations?" liturgically. Maimonides cites the chapter in the *Mishneh Torah* (Hilkhot Avodah Zarah) as foundational for the legal prohibition of idolatry. Abrabanel reads the chapter as Jeremiah's response to imperial astrology, making the political point that Babylonian *otot* (signs) are not the language Yahweh uses.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic: **Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah*** uses the idol-polemic against pagan philosophical theology; **Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah*** discusses the Aramaic verse's textual peculiarity at length, offering both his Latin translation of the LXX shorter form and his Hexaplaric work with the Hebrew. Athanasius cites Jer 10 against Arian subordination — the *living* and *true* God of v. 10 cannot be a creature. Augustine in *City of God* 7 deploys the chapter against Roman civic religion. Medieval: Aquinas in *Summa* II-II.94 (on idolatry) cites the chapter; Rupert of Deutz reads the *helqat ya'aqov* (portion of Jacob) Christologically. **Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah* (1563)** treats Jer 10 as the *locus classicus* of the Reformed image-prohibition: *Institutes* 1.11 builds an entire image-theology partly from this chapter. Geneva's image-stripping in 1535 was, doctrinally, an enactment of Jer 10:3–5. Luther's reading is more moderate; he allowed images for instruction and worried that Reformed iconoclasm overreached.

**Where they diverge.** The Catholic and Orthodox icon-defense (Council of Nicaea II, 787, against the Iconoclast Controversy) makes a doctrinal distinction between *idolon* (forbidden image of false god) and *eikon* (image of true God or saint, which receives *proskynesis* not *latreia*). The defense rests on the Incarnation: God has made an image of himself in Christ; therefore image-making per se is not idolatry. The Reformed reading, drawing heavily on Jer 10 and Deut 4, refuses this distinction and treats all cult-images as falling under the prohibition. The Anabaptist tradition is even stricter. Modern liberal theology (Tillich, Brueggemann) generalizes "idolatry" into ideology critique — any ultimate concern given to a non-ultimate (nation, market, race, self) is the modern form of Jer 10's tree-trunk gods.

**Jewish vs. Christian:** the Jewish tradition has read Jer 10 with continuous strictness on cult-images while remaining at home with non-cultic figural art (the synagogue murals at Dura-Europos, 3rd c., depict biblical scenes pictorially without controversy). The Christian tradition split itself in half over the chapter, and the Iconoclast Controversy of the 8th–9th c. and the Reformation of the 16th are both, in part, fights over how to read this chapter.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The image and the idol.** Jer 10:3–5 is the most concentrated idol-polemic in the prophets. Calvin made it foundational for Reformed iconoclasm. Catholic and Orthodox tradition responds with the Nicaea II distinction: an *icon* is not an *idol* because it points beyond itself to the prototype (the saint, Christ, Mary), and because the Incarnation has authorized the depiction of the divine through the human. John of Damascus, writing during the first iconoclast crisis (c. 730), argues precisely from incarnation: "I do not worship matter, but I worship the God of matter who became matter for my sake." The icon is venerated; only God is worshiped; the distinction is between *latreia* and *proskynesis*.

The Reformed counter-argument deserves a fair hearing in turn. The Decalogue's second commandment in its original Hebrew context prohibits *pesel* (carved image) and *temunah* (likeness) of Yahweh, full stop. Jer 10's polemic is not against pagan gods only but against the very *technique* of representing the divine in matter — "they fasten it with hammer and nails so that it cannot move." Calvin's argument is anthropological: human nature so reliably collapses image-veneration into image-worship that the prohibition has to be absolute. The historical record gives Calvin a hearing — Marian devotion in late-medieval Catholicism, popular Orthodox icon-veneration in some folk forms, Protestant nationalism's "Christian flag" practices, all show the human tendency to confuse image and prototype.

The Catholic-Orthodox steelman: the Incarnation is the *theological* fact that breaks the prohibition, and the prohibition was always provisional on the absence of an authorized divine image. When God became flesh, God authorized the depiction of flesh as the gateway to God. The Reformed answer: Christ is not a recoverable visual image; the New Testament gives no description of his physical appearance because the apostles knew the danger of fixing the divine in pigment. The unresolved question between traditions is whether the Incarnation actually did what Nicaea II says it did — authorize a new sacramental relationship between matter and the divine — or whether the Incarnation is *Word* primarily, not *image*, and the church's task is the preached and read Word, not the seen icon.

Where this leaves us: each tradition is reading Jer 10 honestly. The Catholic/Orthodox reading is the more theologically expansive; the Reformed reading is the more anthropologically vigilant. Modern liberal theology's generalization of idolatry into ideology critique (Tillich, Brueggemann) preserves the prophetic edge for traditions no longer fighting about painted boards.

**Tension 2: The undecidable text.** Jer 10 is the canonical case of plural Hebrew text-forms in late Second Temple Judaism. 4QJer-b matches LXX (shorter, missing vv. 6–8 and 10, reordered v. 9). 4QJer-a matches MT (longer, with the doxological vv. 6–8 and 10). What does it mean that the canonical *text* of this chapter is, at the level of Hebrew transmission, undecidable? Catholic doctrine of inspiration historically locates inspiration in the *canonical* text — the LXX-derived Vulgate for centuries, then the original-language texts since *Divino Afflante Spiritu* (1943) and Vatican II — and is comfortable with limited textual fluidity within the canonical edition. Protestant doctrine (especially in its higher forms) has often located inspiration in the *autographs*, the original prophetic dictation. If two Hebrew autographs circulated simultaneously in the late Second Temple period, the autograph theory faces a problem.

Brevard Childs's canonical reading (*Introduction to the Old Testament as Scripture*, 1979) offers one solution: the canonical text is the MT *as received* by the rabbinic community, and the LXX-Qumran text-form, while historically interesting, is canonically secondary. This works for Catholic and Protestant communities that received the Hebrew Bible through Masoretic transmission. It does not work as well for Orthodox communities who treat the Septuagint itself as canonically authoritative for the Old Testament.

Steelman the high-inspiration view: God preserves the canonical text through the canonical community, and the survival of the MT through rabbinic Judaism is providential preservation, not historical accident. The Qumran textual variety reflects local copying traditions, not parallel inspired editions. The autograph theory survives if "autograph" is understood textually rather than archaeologically — the *wording* the Spirit intended is recoverable through the Masoretic line.

Steelman the historical-critical view: there is no single autograph. Jeremiah preached oracles; Baruch and later scribes compiled, edited, expanded, and reorganized them; two Hebrew editions (the *Vorlage* of the LXX and the proto-MT) circulated in late Second Temple Judaism; both were used at Qumran; the MT prevailed because the rabbinic community standardized it. Inspiration is in the *process* and the *community*, not in a hypothetical original document.

Where this leaves us: the honest reader of Jer 10 has to know the textual history. The pious shortcut — "the Bible says" — is, in this chapter, ambiguous between two ancient Hebrew Bibles. This need not destabilize faith, but it should chasten the simpler views of mechanical inspiration that pretend the text is a single monolith descended from a single dictation.

The unresolved question that lands hard: if the Spirit preserved two Hebrew editions of Jer 10 simultaneously through the Qumran period, what does it mean to treat *one* of them as the canonical text and the other as a textual variant? Either both are canonical, or the doctrine of inspiration has to be more sophisticated than most popular accounts admit.

## The Thread

Deuteronomy 4:15–20 — the Mosaic foundation of the image-prohibition that Jer 10 activates. Isaiah 40:18–20 and 44:9–20 — the great Deutero-Isaianic idol-polemics that share genre and vocabulary with Jer 10 (the genealogical question of priority remains contested). Jeremiah 51:15–16 — the doxology of 10:12–13 returns verbatim as the basis for Babylon's fall, closing the book's largest theological loop: the God who made heaven and earth will unmake the imperial cosmology that built itself in defiance of the cosmos. Daniel 2 and 4 — the imperial Babylon Jer 10 confronts becomes the Aramaic-prophetic theater of Daniel, where the same God-of-storehouses brings down the same imperial astrologers. Lamentations 1–2, where Daughter Zion's torn tent (Jer 10:19–20) becomes the structural image of the entire book.
