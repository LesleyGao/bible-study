# Romans 6: Baptism, Slavery, and the Transfer of Dominion

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

If chapter 5 ended with grace abounding where sin increased, chapter 6 begins with the obvious objection: then why not sin more? The objection is not hypothetical. Paul has been hearing it, probably for years. By the time Tertius wrote down Romans in Corinth in the winter of 56–57 CE, the libertine reading of Pauline grace was already a recognizable distortion that opponents threw at Paul (3:8 — "as some people slanderously charge us with saying"). The Roman house churches receiving the letter were a mixed Jewish-Gentile community trying to figure out what life under grace actually looked like in a city where the household codes, the moral expectations, and the social rituals were Roman to the core.

Two Roman institutions sit underneath the chapter's metaphors. The first is **baptism**. Greco-Roman religious life knew ritual immersion — Jewish proselyte baptism, Qumran's daily ritual baths preserved in stepped *miqva'ot* still visible at Khirbet Qumran, the lustrations of Greek mystery cults like Eleusis and Isis. By 56 CE Christian baptism had been the single distinctive initiation rite for roughly twenty-five years. Paul takes the practice as known (6:3 — "do you not know?") and assumes the audience has been through it. Archaeology preserves the practice: the third-century Christian house church at Dura-Europos (excavated 1932–35, baptistery now reconstructed at Yale) shows that by then the baptismal pool was a fixed architectural feature. Under Paul, baptism likely happened in whatever water was available — a stream, a public bath, a household basin.

The second is **Roman slavery**. When Paul shifts metaphors at 6:15 from baptism to slavery, he is reaching for the dominant social institution of the empire. Estimates from Walter Scheidel and Keith Bradley put the slave population of Italy at perhaps 30–40 percent of the total in the first century. Slaves ran businesses, taught children, kept accounts, served as imperial bureaucrats. **Dale Martin's *Slavery as Salvation* (1990)** argues that for Greco-Roman audiences, "slave of Caesar" or "slave of Apollo" was not necessarily a degrading status — being a slave of a powerful master conferred derivative status. Paul's slave-of-righteousness language taps into this: the issue is never *whether* you serve someone but *whom*. Jewett's Hermeneia commentary draws on the same material to argue that Paul's call for the Roman believers to "present your members as slaves to righteousness" (6:19) was meant to recalibrate the actual lived experience of household members, many of whom were literally slaves.

Three Greek terms repay attention. ***Sunestaurōthē*** (συνεσταυρώθη, 6:6) — "was crucified with" — is one of Paul's *syn-* compounds (with-Christ words) that build the participation theology of the chapter: co-crucified, co-buried, co-living. ***Kainotēs zōēs*** (καινότητι ζωῆς, 6:4) — "newness of life" — uses *kainos* (qualitative newness, a different kind) rather than *neos* (chronological newness). Paul means a new *order* of life, not just a more recent edition. ***Mē genoito*** (μὴ γένοιto, 6:2, 15) — the diatribal "by no means!" or "God forbid!" — appears ten times in Romans (3:4, 3:6, 3:31, 6:2, 6:15, 7:7, 7:13, 9:14, 11:1, 11:11) and is one of Paul's signature rhetorical moves.

A textual note: the manuscripts of Romans 6 are remarkably clean. P46 preserves the chapter; Vaticanus, Sinaiticus, and the Western and Byzantine traditions agree on the substantive readings. The major question is at 6:11, where some manuscripts add "our Lord" after "Christ Jesus" — minor and theologically inconsequential.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Paul builds chapter 6 in two parallel movements answering two parallel objections. Verses 1–14 answer "shall we continue in sin?" with the baptismal argument: those baptized into Christ have died to sin, and the dead do not live in what they died to. Verses 15–23 answer "shall we sin because we are under grace?" with the slavery argument: there is no neutral ground; freedom from one master means slavery to another. The structure is diatribal and Stowers (*The Diatribe and Paul's Letter to the Romans*) reads the chapter as the most sustained example of Paul's diatribal method outside of Romans 9–11.

The literary devices stack:

The **rhetorical question + *mē genoito*** structure (6:1–2, 6:15) frames each section. The dialogical move (imagined interlocutor, refused conclusion, supporting argument) is Stoic-diatribal in form, scriptural-rabbinic in substance.

The ***ouk oidate*** ("do you not know," 6:3, 6:16) opens both halves. Paul appeals to what the audience already knows — the meaning of baptism, the logic of slavery — rather than introducing new doctrine. Cranfield (ICC) reads the *ouk oidate* as evidence that Paul knew the Roman believers had received baptismal catechesis even though he had not been their teacher.

The ***syn-* compound chain*** (συν-) is the hidden architecture: *sunetaphēmen* (buried with, 6:4), *symphytoi* (united with / grown together with, 6:5), *sunestaurōthē* (crucified with, 6:6), *suzēsomen* (will live with, 6:8). This is Pauline participation theology in its most concentrated form. **E.P. Sanders** (*Paul and Palestinian Judaism*, 1977) made participation rather than juridical imputation the heart of Pauline soteriology; chapter 6 is exhibit A.

The ***reign / dominion vocabulary*** carries over from chapter 5. *Basileuō* (βασιλεύω, "to reign," 6:12), *kurieuō* (κυριεύω, "to have dominion," 6:9, 6:14), *douleuō* (δουλεύω, "to serve as a slave," 6:6) — the chapter is built on the language of who rules whom. Käsemann's commentary makes this central: the gospel is the transfer of *dominion*, not the cancellation of a debt.

Genre is Pauline letter with embedded diatribe and embedded baptismal homily. Several scholars (Schnackenburg, Wedderburn, Tannehill *Dying and Rising with Christ*, 1967) have argued 6:3–11 reflects an underlying baptismal liturgical tradition Paul is reworking. The strongly antithetical, formula-like construction ("we have died with him… we shall live with him… he died to sin once for all… he lives to God") fits early Christian baptismal teaching as we glimpse it in Colossians 2:12 and 1 Peter.

Jewett's Hermeneia rhetorical analysis classifies the chapter as the second *probatio* in a deliberative argument, with 6:1–14 the first sub-proof (death) and 6:15–23 the second (slavery), each ending with a punchline (6:14 / 6:23).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The story Paul is telling here begins with Israel's exodus and ends with the eschatological liberation of the cosmos. The deep grammar is *redemption from slavery*. Israel was enslaved in Egypt; God brought them out by the blood of the Passover lamb and the waters of the sea; the Torah was given to a people who had been slaves and were now free. Paul reads Christian baptism as the analog: passing through water out of slavery to Sin into a new covenant. **Richard Hays** (*Echoes of Scripture in the Letters of Paul*) reads the *exodus typology* as the implicit script for chapter 6: the Red Sea is the waters of baptism, Pharaoh is Sin, the Promised Land is "newness of life."

The **Jewish interpretive tradition** had long developed the trope of the two inclinations — *yetzer hatov* (good inclination) and *yetzer hara* (evil inclination) — both real, both struggling within the same person, with Torah and the daily reciting of the Shema as the means by which one trains the *yetzer hatov* to dominate. The rabbinic tradition (Mishnah *Berakhot* 9:5; *b. Berakhot* 61b) sees the human as never *neutral* but always inclined; one chooses which inclination to obey. Paul's slave-to-sin / slave-to-righteousness logic is a Christian transposition of the rabbinic *two yetzers* anthropology — the same insight that humans are not autonomous but always under sway, given a Christological resolution.

The **Christian interpretive tradition** branches early on what baptism actually does. **Tertullian** (*De Baptismo*, c. 200) reads chapter 6 as ground for the realist claim that baptism *ex opere operato* (by the work performed) effects what it signifies — though the phrase is later. **Origen** (*Commentary on Romans*) reads it allegorically as well as sacramentally: baptism is the soul's death to the visible world and rebirth to the spiritual. **Chrysostom's *Homilies on Romans* (10–11)** stress moral participation: baptism is a real death-to-sin that demands the moral life consonant with it; the Antiochene reading is closer to Pelagius than to Augustine.

**Augustine** uses chapter 6 in two directions. In the anti-Donatist controversy (early 400s), he argues from "buried with him by baptism" that baptism's efficacy depends on Christ, not on the moral state of the minister. In the anti-Pelagian controversy (after 411), he argues from "our old self was crucified" that the believer's *flesh* is real but the dominion of sin is broken — *concupiscentia* (concupiscence, the disordered desire) remains, but it no longer rules. Augustine's reading became the Western mainstream.

**Medieval theology** systematized this through **Aquinas** (*Summa* III, qq. 66–71; *Lectura super Romanos*). Baptism is a sacrament that confers grace *ex opere operato*; it removes original sin (the guilt) and inaugurates the new life of grace, though concupiscence remains as *fomes peccati* (the kindling of sin) until glorification. Aquinas reads chapter 6 as foundational for the Catholic theology of sacramental incorporation.

The **Reformation** divided sharply. **Luther's *Lectures on Romans* (1515–16)** reads the chapter as teaching the *simul iustus et peccator* — the believer is simultaneously dead-to-sin (in Christ) and still struggling with sin (in the flesh) — and 6:11 ("consider yourselves dead to sin") is the existential summons to live by faith into the reality already accomplished. Luther preserves the realist reading of baptism but emphasizes the personal-existential appropriation. **Calvin's *Commentary* (1540)** reads the chapter as teaching the inseparability of justification and sanctification: those whom God justifies he also sanctifies, and chapter 6 spells out what sanctification means concretely. The Reformed tradition reads baptism as a sign-and-seal of a covenantal reality, not as the cause of regeneration. **Anabaptists** (Menno Simons, Hubmaier) read the chapter as requiring believer's baptism: the participation Paul describes presupposes faith, and faith presupposes the capacity to believe, which infants do not have. **Eastern Orthodoxy** holds the strongest realist reading: baptism is the actual incorporation into Christ's death and resurrection, the beginning of *theosis*; chrismation seals it; the Eucharist sustains it.

**Wesley's *Notes on the New Testament* (1755)** read chapter 6 as ground for the doctrine of *entire sanctification* / Christian perfection: if Paul says we are dead to sin and freed from its dominion, this must be a real and attainable condition in this life, not a juridical fiction. The Wesleyan-Holiness tradition (Adam Clarke, Phoebe Palmer) made chapter 6 a centerpiece. Wesley's reading is closer to Chrysostom's Antiochene reading than to Augustine's.

The four modern interpretive schools approach the chapter differently.

The **Old Perspective** (Cranfield ICC, Moo NICNT, Schreiner) reads chapter 6 as the answer to the Reformation worry: justification by faith alone does not lead to antinomianism because justification is inseparable from union with Christ, and union with Christ entails death-to-sin. Moo's commentary is the most thorough recent statement: the chapter is about the inevitable moral consequence of the gospel.

The **New Perspective** (Sanders, Dunn WBC, **N.T. Wright**) reads chapter 6 as Paul's argument against the antinomian misreading of justification by faith and as part of his account of how the *renewed covenant people* are formed. Wright (*Paul and the Faithfulness of God*) sees the chapter as the description of the new covenant community: the people of God are now those who have died and risen with Messiah, and their distinguishing mark is the Spirit's animation of a new way of life. The chapter is more about *ecclesial identity* than about *individual sanctification*.

The **Apocalyptic Paul** school (Käsemann, J.L. Martyn, **Beverly Gaventa**, **Douglas Campbell**, Susan Eastman) reads chapter 6 as the *transfer of dominion*. Sin (capitalized — the cosmic Power) had reign; in baptism, the believer is *transferred* from one cosmic regime to another. **Gaventa's *Our Mother Saint Paul*** emphasizes that the language is not primarily ethical exhortation but the announcement of a regime change. Campbell (*Deliverance of God*) makes this even stronger: the chapter is not "you have been justified, now live like it" but "Christ has invaded the cosmos and broken Sin's grip; the believer participates in that invasion." The *douleia* (slavery) of 6:15–23 is not metaphor for moral effort but the literal description of life under the cosmic Powers — the only freedom available is being enslaved to a different and life-giving Power.

The **Paul Within Judaism** school (Mark Nanos, **Pamela Eisenbaum**, **Matthew Thiessen**, **Paula Fredriksen**) reads chapter 6 as Paul's argument for *Gentile* incorporation into the eschatological people of God without becoming Jews. The chapter is for Gentile believers in Rome who have just been told (chapter 5) that they are reconciled and now ask: what shape does our life take? Paul's answer is not Torah-observance (that's for Jews) but a participatory *equivalent* of the Torah-shaped life: dead to the old aeon, alive in the Messiah, bearing the fruit Torah was meant to produce. Thiessen (*A Jewish Paul*) reads 6:14 ("you are not under law but under grace") as a statement specifically about Gentiles — Gentiles never were under Torah, and they don't need to be, because Christ has provided the eschatological equivalent.

Each school has texts it loves. Old Perspective loves 6:11 (the existential summons to faith). New Perspective loves 6:14 (the law/grace polarity). Apocalyptic loves 6:6 (the old self crucified, the body of Sin destroyed) and the entire dominion vocabulary. Paul Within Judaism loves 6:19 (Paul's apologetic note that he is "speaking in human terms because of your natural limitations" — the slavery metaphor as accommodation to a Gentile audience that had not been catechized in Torah categories).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions thread through this chapter, each with multiple serious historical positions.

**Tension One: What does baptism actually do?**

*Position One: Sacramental realism / *ex opere operato** (Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, high Lutheran). Baptism is the actual incorporation into Christ's death and resurrection; what 6:3–4 says happens, happens, by the operation of the sacrament received in faith (or, for infants, in the faith of the church). Texts: 6:3–5 ("baptized into his death… buried with him… united with him"). Strength: takes the language at face value; the verbs are indicative, not metaphorical.

*Position Two: Sign-and-seal* (Reformed). Baptism is a divinely ordained sign that signifies and seals the covenantal reality of union with Christ for those who receive it in faith. Texts: 6:11 ("consider yourselves dead to sin") read as the existential moment. Strength: preserves the priority of faith and the sovereignty of God's electing grace; avoids the magical reading of the rite. Weakness: the Greek is hard to fit into "sign-and-seal" without softening it.

*Position Three: Symbolic / believer's baptism* (Anabaptist, most Baptist traditions). Baptism is a symbolic profession of faith and a public identification with Christ's death and resurrection that has already happened to the believer at the moment of conversion. Texts: 6:11 (the call to *consider* yourselves dead) read as describing the state already true by faith. Strength: matches a tight believer's-baptism ecclesiology. Weakness: must demote the strongly causal language of 6:3–5.

*Position Four: Apocalyptic-incorporative* (Käsemann, Campbell, Gaventa). Baptism is the moment of cosmic transfer: the believer's body is incorporated into the dying and rising of the Messiah and into the regime of grace. Strength: takes the dominion language seriously and integrates baptism into the apocalyptic frame.

*Steelman the sacramental realist position*: 6:3 says "all of us who *have been baptized* into Christ Jesus *have been baptized* into his death." The verbs are passive aorists. The agent is implicit, but Paul is not saying baptism is a *sign* of incorporation — he is saying it *is* incorporation. The cleanest exegesis of 6:3–5 is the strongest sacramental reading. The question is whether one's broader theology of grace and faith requires modifying that.

*Where this leaves us:* The chapter does not directly settle the sacramental theology question, but its language pushes toward realism. Whether one parses that realism as Catholic-sacramental, Orthodox-theotic, Lutheran-realist, or Apocalyptic-incorporative, what the chapter resists is a thin symbolic reading. Paul thought baptism *did* something. The traditions that have pressed hardest on what it does (Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, Apocalyptic) are exegetically more secure than those that have softened it. But the chapter also resists *opus operatum* magic: the call to "consider yourselves dead" (6:11) and to "present your members" (6:13) refuses the conclusion that the rite makes ethical exhortation unnecessary.

**Tension Two: What is the relationship between the believer's death-to-sin and ongoing struggle?**

*Position One: Realist / sanctified-already* (Wesley, Holiness, some Pentecostal). 6:6, 6:11, 6:14 mean what they say: the believer is *actually* dead to sin and freed from its dominion. The expected condition is real and progressive sanctification toward perfection in this life. Strength: takes Paul's indicatives at full strength.

*Position Two: *Simul iustus et peccator** (Luther, much of Reformed). The believer is dead to sin *in Christ* and yet still battles sin *in the flesh*. The two realities are simultaneous, not sequential. Strength: matches the existential experience of every honest Christian.

*Position Three: Indicative-imperative / already-not-yet* (most modern critical scholars, Cranfield, Moo, Wright). Paul's logic is: *because* you have died to sin (indicative), *therefore* live as those who have died (imperative). The death-to-sin is real but its lived expression is progressive. 6:11 ("consider yourselves") is the bridge. Strength: integrates indicatives and imperatives without denying either. This is the modern consensus.

*Position Four: Apocalyptic-eschatological* (Käsemann, Martyn, Campbell). The death-to-sin is the cosmic event of regime change inaugurated at the cross and resurrection; the believer participates in that event but lives in the overlap of the ages, where the old aeon's death-throes are still felt. Strength: integrates the dominion vocabulary and the eschatological tension.

*Steelman the Wesleyan position*: The plain reading of 6:7 ("one who has died has been set free from sin"), 6:14 ("sin will have no dominion over you"), and 6:18 ("having been set free from sin") is that the believer is *actually* free. The Reformed move to subordinate these indicatives to the persistent reality of struggle has the unintended effect of making Paul mean less than he says. Wesley reads Paul at full strength. If Romans 6 is read on its own terms, Wesley wins; if it is read with Romans 7 importing the constraint, the Reformed reading wins.

*Where this leaves us:* Romans 6 alone teaches a remarkable thing — that the believer has been transferred from one regime to another and is no longer Sin's slave. Romans 7 will complicate this picture. The decision about whether to read 6 *as constrained by* 7 or 7 *as the pre-Christian backdrop to* 6 (Wesley's move) shapes the entire doctrine of sanctification. The indicative-imperative reading is a sober middle path, but it should not entirely flatten the radicality of the chapter's claims. Paul is announcing something genuinely new, not just exhorting harder.

**One unresolved question:** If 6:14 says "sin will have no dominion over you, since you are not under law but under grace," what is the *causal* relationship between not-being-under-law and sin's lost dominion? Is law itself part of what gave sin its grip? This is the question 7:1–13 will take up, and the answer one gives there reverberates through every reading of Paul on Torah, ethics, and grace.

## The Thread

Romans 6 should be read alongside Galatians 2:19–20 ("I have been crucified with Christ"), Galatians 3:27 ("as many of you as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ"), Colossians 2:11–13 (baptism as circumcision-of-Christ, made alive together with him), and 1 Corinthians 6:9–11 ("such were some of you, but you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified"). The chapter looks back to the Adam/Christ typology of chapter 5 (the dominion of death now broken in baptism) and forward to chapter 8 (the Spirit who animates the resurrection life chapter 6 promised). The slavery vocabulary of 6:15–23 is taken up again in 8:15 ("you did not receive the spirit of slavery to fall back into fear, but the spirit of adoption"). The "newness of life" (6:4) of this chapter is fulfilled in the freedom of the children of God (8:21). Flag forward: 7:7–25 will revisit the question of what Sin actually is and how the Law functions, and 8:1–17 will describe the lived experience of the regime-change announced here. Paul has named the new reality. Chapter 7 will explore what is left of the old.
