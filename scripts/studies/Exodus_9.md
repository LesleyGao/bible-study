# Exodus 9: What God Said, I Raised You Up For This

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 9 narrates plagues five, six, and seven — livestock pestilence, boils, and hail — and in doing so it provides one of the most precise historical markers in the plague cycle. The note in vv. 31–32 that "the flax and the barley were struck down, for the barley was in the ear and the flax was in bud; but the wheat and the emmer were not struck down, for they are late in coming up" dates the hail plague to January or February in the Egyptian agricultural calendar. Kenneth Kitchen (*On the Reliability of the Old Testament*), James Hoffmeier (*Israel in Egypt*), and John Currid (*Ancient Egypt and the Old Testament*) all treat this verse as evidence of close knowledge of Egyptian agriculture: barley and flax ripen in the Delta in January–February, while wheat and emmer ripen in March–April. The text knows this calendar, and the specificity is substantial data for dating the tradition.

The plagues of chapter 9 target three Egyptian religious domains. The livestock pestilence (*deber*, v. 3) targets Apis (the sacred bull of Memphis), Hathor (the cow-headed goddess of love, fertility, and music), and Mnevis (the sacred bull of Heliopolis). Sacred bull worship was a central feature of Egyptian state religion; the Apis bull was kept in its own temple, mummified at death, and interred with royal ceremony in the Serapeum at Saqqara, where archaeologists including Auguste Mariette in the nineteenth century excavated elaborate burial chambers. For YHWH to slaughter Egypt's cattle is to slaughter the Egyptian divine retinue. The boil plague (*šĕḥîn*, v. 9) targets Sekhmet (the lion-headed goddess of plague, but also of healing), Imhotep (the deified architect-turned-medical patron), and Serapis (a syncretic healing deity). Egyptian medicine was an elaborate craft — attested in the Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE) and the Edwin Smith Papyrus — and to afflict the land with incurable boils was to disable its healing gods. The soot Moses throws (v. 8) comes from a *kibšān* (כִּבְשָׁן, kiln) — almost certainly a brick-making kiln, the same industrial apparatus in which Hebrew slaves had labored. The symbolic reversal is heavy: the very ash of Israelite oppression becomes the medium of judgment.

The hail plague (*bārād*, v. 18) targets Nut (the sky-goddess whose body arches over the earth), Shu (god of air), and Tefnut (goddess of moisture). Hail in the Delta is rare and, when severe, catastrophic; the text's claim that such hail "has never been in Egypt from the day it was founded until now" (v. 18) takes aim at the sky-pantheon's monopoly on the heavens. The accompanying thunder and "fire running down to the earth" (v. 23) recall theophanic language elsewhere — Psalm 18, Sinai itself in Exodus 19 — and this is deliberate: the hail is the first plague explicitly described as theophany, with cosmic violence that will be repeated at Sinai.

The source-critical analysis of chapter 9 is one of the clearest test cases. Richard Elliott Friedman (*The Bible with Sources Revealed*), William Propp (*Anchor Bible Exodus 1–18*), Brevard Childs (*The Book of Exodus*), and Martin Noth all broadly agree: the livestock plague (vv. 1–7) is J, the boils plague (vv. 8–12) is P (tells: Moses and Aaron together, staff-free gesture, magicians afflicted), and the hail plague (vv. 13–35) is J with the famous theological speech in vv. 13–19 treated variously. Friedman assigns the whole speech to E or redaction; Propp treats much of it as J. Cassuto again contests the documentary partition. What is especially striking in chapter 9 is v. 12 — "the LORD hardened the heart of Pharaoh" — the first unambiguous active-causative use of the divine subject in the hardening vocabulary.

Hebrew terms worth examining: *deber* (דֶּבֶר), "pestilence," a word used for YHWH's plague-judgment throughout the prophets (Jeremiah 14:12, Habakkuk 3:5, which calls *deber* one of YHWH's martial companions alongside *rešep*, "fire-plague"). *Šĕḥîn* (שְׁחִין), "boils," will recur in Job 2:7 (Job's affliction) and Deuteronomy 28:27 (covenant curse: "boils of Egypt"). *Kibšān* (כִּבְשָׁן), "kiln" or "furnace," is the word used in Genesis 19:28 for the smoke rising from Sodom and in Deuteronomy 4:20 and 1 Kings 8:51 for Egypt itself — "the iron furnace" of Israel's oppression. *Bārād* (בָּרָד), "hail," recurs in Isaiah 28:17 and Joshua 10:11, always in contexts of divine judgment. And the pivotal phrase in v. 16, *baʿăbûr zōʾṯ heʿĕmadtîḵā* (בַּעֲבוּר זֹאת הֶעֱמַדְתִּיךָ), "for this purpose I have raised you up," uses the verb *ʿāmad* in its causative — literally, "I have caused you to stand." LXX translates with *eneka toutou dietērēthēs*, "for this reason you were preserved / made to continue," which differs in nuance and sets up the Pauline citation in Romans 9:17.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 9 contains plagues 5, 6, and 7 of the 3+3+3+1 arrangement. Plague 5 (livestock) is announced at the palace — matching the pattern for plagues 2, 5, 8. Plague 6 (boils) is unannounced — matching the pattern for plagues 3, 6, 9. Plague 7 (hail) is announced at the morning encounter — matching the pattern for plagues 1, 4, 7 and opening the third triad. Greenberg, Sarna, and Cassuto all treat this structural regularity as deliberate. Each triad escalates: plagues 1–3 are mere nuisance and disgust; plagues 4–6 attack property and bodies; plagues 7–9 attack the cosmic order itself (sky, sun, darkness). Fretheim in *Exodus* argues that the cycle is not merely escalating punishment but creation-undone theology — the plagues systematically unravel the orders of Genesis 1: water and its creatures (plague 1), fish and amphibians (plagues 1–2), insects and the earth (plagues 3–4), beasts of the land (plague 5), humans (plague 6), sky and weather (plague 7), vegetation (plague 8 — locusts), sun and stars (plague 9 — darkness), firstborn (plague 10).

The hardening vocabulary reaches a critical shift in chapter 9. Through chapters 7 and 8, the narrations of hardening use stative or self-reflexive forms — Pharaoh's heart *was* hard (*ḥāzaq* in 7:13, 22), or Pharaoh *hardened* his own heart (*hakbēd*, 8:15, 32). Verse 9:12 is the first explicit active-causative: *wayĕḥazzēq YHWH ʾeṯ-lēḇ par'ōh*, "YHWH strengthened Pharaoh's heart." After the sixth plague, the grammar shifts. The theological significance of this shift is contested but the grammatical fact is not. Propp in the *Anchor Bible* tracks the vocabulary in detail: *ḥāzaq* (to be firm, strong) and *kāḇēd* (to be heavy, weighty) are the primary verbs, with *qāšâ* (to be stiff) appearing occasionally; the subject shifts across the cycle, and 9:12 is the pivot.

The speech of vv. 13–19 before the hail plague is one of the most theologically charged texts in the cycle. "For this purpose I have raised you up, to show you my power, so that my name may be proclaimed in all the earth" (v. 16) will be quoted by Paul in Romans 9:17 at the peak of his argument about divine election. The speech also introduces the theme that will close the chapter: some of Pharaoh's servants fear the word of YHWH and bring their livestock in; others do not (vv. 20–21). For the first time, the Egyptian social body cracks — there are Egyptians who believe Moses. This foreshadows the mixed multitude that will leave Egypt at 12:38.

Pharaoh's confession in v. 27 — "This time I have sinned; the LORD is in the right, and I and my people are in the wrong" — uses the pair *ṣaddîq* (righteous) and *rĕšāʿîm* (wrong/wicked) in the rhetoric of formal legal admission. This is the only plague at which Pharaoh explicitly confesses guilt. The confession is narratively shallow — Pharaoh recants as soon as the hail stops (v. 34) — but it is the most developed verbal submission in the cycle. Moses's rejoinder in v. 30, "I know that you do not yet fear the LORD God," reads the confession as tactical.

Textually, the LXX in v. 16 shifts the agency subtly: where MT has "for this purpose I have raised you up" (active, YHWH doing), LXX has "for this reason you have been preserved" (passive, YHWH preserving). Paul in Romans 9:17 follows the LXX, which is theologically consequential: the Greek reading is slightly less strong on active divine causation, yet Paul uses it to build an argument for active divine sovereignty. 4QExod fragments confirm the MT reading. The Samaritan Pentateuch expands some speeches in the chapter as elsewhere.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Canonically, chapter 9 is the turning point of the plague cycle. Pharaoh's first verbal confession, the first explicit divine hardening, the first crack in Egyptian solidarity, the first theological declaration of the plagues' purpose ("that my name may be proclaimed in all the earth"), and the first theophanic plague all occur here.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory**. Josephus (*Antiquities* 2.300–305) treats chapter 9 with relative speed but pauses on the hail. Philo (*Life of Moses* 1.118–135) reads each plague as measure-for-measure: the livestock that Egyptian religion had deified are slaughtered; the healing priests are themselves sick; the sky-gods are conquered in their own domain. Wisdom of Solomon 16–17 elaborates. The *Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael* on Exodus 15 reflects on YHWH's mightiness through the cycle. *Exodus Rabbah* 11–12 develop measure-for-measure readings: the cattle Egyptians adored are dead; the boils erupt on the magicians who had worked with dust; the hail strikes the very fields Israel had been forced to till. Rashi on 9:27 reads Pharaoh's confession psychologically — he confesses while the hail falls, retracts when it stops, a paradigm of fear-driven religion. Ibn Ezra on 9:16 takes the Hebrew "I have raised you up" as divine appointment to the throne: Pharaoh was given his position by YHWH precisely for the purpose of this public confrontation. Ramban (Nachmanides) reads the hardening sequence theologically: Pharaoh hardens himself in plagues 1–5 (exercising free will in hardening); YHWH hardens him in plagues 6–10 (judicially sealing a disposition Pharaoh had chosen). Maimonides in *Hilchot Teshuvah* 6:3 is the locus classicus: "It is possible for a person to commit a great sin or many sins until the judgment given before the true Judge is to repay this person for these sins which he committed willfully and knowingly by preventing him from doing teshuvah and not allowing him the opportunity to turn back from his wickedness... Therefore it is written in the Torah, 'And I will harden Pharaoh's heart'" (Maimonides citing this very chapter as proof text). Maimonides's reading is preserved in most subsequent Jewish reflection on the hardening debate. Levenson (*The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*) reads the cycle as public theater; Sarna's JPS commentary is indispensable on the Egyptian realia; Zornberg's *Particulars of Rapture* reads Pharaoh's serial confessions-and-retractions as the pathology of the bound will. Greenberg and Cassuto both emphasize the structural climax of the third triad.

**Christian interpretive trajectory**. Origen's *Homilies on Exodus* 4–6 allegorizes boils as the inner corruption made visible, hail as the wrath of God against the reprobate, and the fire-in-hail as the mingled judgment and mercy of divine action. Augustine takes up chapter 9 as the biblical proof text for his doctrine of grace in multiple places — *On the Spirit and the Letter*, *Confessions*, letters against the Pelagians. The verse that drives his argument is 9:16, "for this purpose I have raised you up"; he reads it as evidence of sovereign divine disposal of human agents, and Paul's citation in Romans 9 makes the reading unavoidable. Augustine also emphasizes Pharaoh's confession (v. 27) as the model of counterfeit repentance — fear without love, regret without change. Gregory of Nyssa, *Life of Moses*, allegorizes the plagues as the progressive purgation of sin. Chrysostom uses the boils and the magicians' flight as images of the collapse of worldly wisdom. Aquinas (*ST* I-II q. 79 a. 3 on God's relation to sin; I-II q. 113 on grace and hardening; I q. 22 on providence) treats the hardening texts with care: God does not directly cause Pharaoh's sin but permits it and uses it for his own purposes. Aquinas distinguishes between God's active hardening (which would make God the author of sin) and God's permissive hardening (which allows the natural development of a corrupt will for good ends). The distinction is philosophically subtle and has been contested by Reformed readers. Bernard of Clairvaux develops the chapter's moral psychology. Calvin (*Institutes* 2.4, *Commentary on Exodus*) takes 9:12 and 9:16 as bedrock evidence for reprobation: God raises Pharaoh up for the specific purpose of displaying divine power in judgment, and God hardens Pharaoh's heart as the instrument of that display. Calvin is careful to distinguish divine causation from divine compulsion — Pharaoh acts freely according to his own corrupt nature — but he refuses Augustine's and Aquinas's permissive-only reading. Luther's *Bondage of the Will* 18 is the most explicit treatment: against Erasmus, Luther argues that "I have raised you up for this purpose" makes any synergistic reading impossible. Pharaoh is a chosen vessel of judgment, preserved precisely so that God's power may be publicly displayed. Jonathan Edwards in *Freedom of the Will* deepens the Reformed reading with an analysis of moral necessity: Pharaoh is both free (doing what he wants) and determined (his wants fixed by his nature and by divine governance). Modern critical: Childs's *Exodus* is balanced, reading the hardening passages as theological claims about divine sovereignty and human accountability held together without systematic resolution. Fretheim's *Exodus* offers the creation-undone reading and stresses the ecological dimension of the plagues — the cosmos itself participates in the judgment against oppression, with the hail as the climactic sky-plague before the darkening of the sun. Brueggemann (*Theology of the Old Testament*) reads the cycle as counter-imperial. Propp's *Anchor Bible* is the textual-philological gold standard. Houtman's three-volume commentary catalogs the history of interpretation. Goldingay's *Old Testament Theology* reads the chapter as the public assertion of YHWH against the ideological apparatus of imperial Egypt. John Walton and John Currid provide the ancient Near Eastern comparative material.

**Convergence and divergence**. Jewish and Christian traditions agree that chapter 9 is the climactic turn of the plague cycle, that v. 16 is theologically load-bearing, and that Pharaoh's confession is shallow. They diverge sharply on the hardening. Jewish tradition, from Maimonides onward, reads the sequence as judicial — Pharaoh's earlier sins produce the divine hardening as consequence, without compromising the justice of the initial accountability. Christian tradition has fractured on the issue: Augustinian/Thomistic readings favor permissive hardening (God permits the will's self-hardening); Reformed readings favor effective hardening (God causes the hardening directly); Wesleyan/Arminian readings favor responsive hardening (God confirms Pharaoh in the direction he has chosen). All three Christian positions have textual warrant; none of them fully resolves the tension the text sets up. On v. 16, both traditions recognize its force, but Jewish readings generally treat it as describing Pharaoh's divinely appointed role (kingship given for a purpose) while Christian readings, especially post-Paul, treat it as evidence of individual predestination to reprobation. The Pauline citation in Romans 9 is the fork in the interpretive road.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: "For this purpose I have raised you up" (9:16).** The verse is the focal point of the hardening debate. Paul quotes it in Romans 9:17 immediately after Jacob-and-Esau, and immediately before concluding that "God has mercy on whomever he wills, and he hardens whomever he wills." What does the verse mean, and what does the whole chapter mean?

- **Reformed (Calvin, Luther, Edwards, Piper, Horton)**: Calvin reads 9:16 as direct evidence that God raises up specific individuals as vessels of judgment, precisely so that divine power may be publicly displayed. Pharaoh is not accidentally in power; he is there by divine appointment for the specific purpose of being judged. Luther's *Bondage of the Will* takes the same line against Erasmus: the verse makes synergism impossible, and any attempt to soften it betrays the text. Edwards in *Freedom of the Will* argues that Pharaoh's freedom consists in acting according to his dominant affection, and his affections are shaped by divine governance. Steelmanned: this reading honors the verse's most natural grammar, takes Paul's citation in Romans 9 with full weight, and produces a high doctrine of divine sovereignty that does not flinch at the implications.
- **Catholic / Thomistic (Aquinas, CCC 1037, Maritain)**: Aquinas distinguishes between God's antecedent will (that all be saved) and his consequent will (which permits some to be lost as a consequence of their free rejection of grace). Pharaoh's "raising up" is not election to damnation but divine appointment to a specific public role; his hardening is the permissive consequence of his own prior free rejections. CCC 1037 teaches that "God predestines no one to go to hell." Steelmanned: this reading preserves divine goodness and human responsibility, tracks the grammatical fact that early plagues describe self-hardening before divine hardening in 9:12, and avoids the moral difficulties of double predestination without denying divine sovereignty.
- **Wesleyan / Arminian (Wesley, Oden, Walls, Olson)**: Wesley reads 9:16 as God's providential use of an agent's freely chosen disposition. Pharaoh was "raised up" — placed in the position of Egyptian kingship — and his choices produced the confrontation; God neither caused the choices nor left them undirected, but worked them into a plan Pharaoh himself had not foreseen. Jerry Walls (*Hell: The Logic of Damnation*, *Purgatory*) and Roger Olson (*Against Calvinism*) develop the Arminian reading: "raising up" is vocation, not unconditional election to reprobation. Steelmanned: this reading takes the plague cycle seriously as a sequence of real choices (why repeated warnings if outcome is fixed?), preserves the justice of God, and reads Paul in Romans 9 as describing the pattern of divine action without locking it into metaphysical determinism.
- **Jewish (Maimonides, Rashi, Ramban, Levenson)**: Maimonides (*Hilchot Teshuvah* 6:3) reads the chapter as judicial hardening: Pharaoh's prior exercise of free will created the conditions in which God justly withdrew the capacity for repentance. Rashi agrees. Ramban distinguishes the first five plagues (self-hardening, free choice) from the last five (divine hardening, judicial consequence). Levenson reads the cycle as public theater: the point is not to change Pharaoh but to display judgment. Steelmanned: this reading tracks the grammatical shift at 9:12, preserves human responsibility at the front end of the sequence, and avoids both unconditional election and strict libertarian autonomy by proposing a middle path — responsible freedom produces character, and character hardens into fate.
- **Critical (Fretheim, Brueggemann)**: The chapter is about counter-imperial theology, not individual soteriology. Pharaoh is a figure for imperial power; his hardening is the imperial ideology's inability to bend when confronted with a god who acts outside imperial categories. Steelmanned: this reading captures the political dimension the other readings risk neglecting and takes seriously the fact that the biblical text is interested in Egypt as a system, not merely in Pharaoh as an individual case of predestination.

**Where this leaves us**: the chapter generates a theological surplus that no single systematic framework fully contains; the most honest readings hold divine sovereignty and human responsibility in tension without claiming to resolve them.

**Tension Two: The Egyptians who feared YHWH (9:20).** For the first time in the cycle, some Egyptians respond to YHWH's word — they bring their livestock in from the field before the hail falls. What is the theological status of these Egyptians?

- **Catholic (Aquinas, contemporary: *Lumen Gentium*)**: The Egyptians who feared YHWH's word are an early biblical instance of extra-covenantal faith — recognition of divine authority by persons outside the formal covenant community. *Lumen Gentium* 16 discusses those who have not heard the gospel but respond to divine truth as it reaches them. Steelmanned: the reading connects the verse to the later canonical movement toward universality — Rahab, Ruth, Naaman, the Ninevites of Jonah, the magi — and honors what the text simply reports without theorizing it away.
- **Reformed (Calvin)**: Calvin reads the fearing Egyptians as evidence that God's common grace extends beyond the covenant community — the truth reaches where the covenant has not yet reached, and some respond. This does not make them saved in the full sense, but it does make them recipients of divine mercy in a lower register. Steelmanned: the reading preserves Reformed soteriological distinctions while accounting for the text's generous observation.
- **Jewish (Sarna, Levenson)**: These Egyptians foreshadow the mixed multitude of 12:38. The exodus is not only Israel's liberation; it is also an invitation to any who will follow the God whose power has been displayed. Steelmanned: the reading connects the verse to the later biblical tradition of universal blessing (Genesis 12:3) and the prophetic visions of Egypt worshiping YHWH (Isaiah 19).
- **Critical (Brueggemann, Fretheim)**: The verse is evidence that imperial ideology is not monolithic — even inside the empire, some respond to counter-imperial truth. The fearing Egyptians represent the possibility of defection from imperial logic. Steelmanned: the reading gives the verse a social-political weight that complements its theological meaning without displacing it.

**Where this leaves us**: the text registers, matter-of-factly, that the plague cycle produces differentiation not only between Egypt and Israel but also within Egypt — a narrative seed whose canonical growth is considerable.

**Unresolved question**: If divine hardening in 9:12 is genuinely judicial (a consequence of Pharaoh's prior self-hardening), why does YHWH announce the intention to harden already in 7:3, before Pharaoh's first refusal — and what does this announcement tell us about the direction of divine knowledge and agency in this narrative?

## The Thread

Three cross-references that do not usually appear together.

First, **Exodus 9:16 → Romans 9:17 → 2 Peter 3:9**. Paul's citation of 9:16 in Romans 9 is well known, but the verse's afterlife extends further. 2 Peter 3:9 insists that the Lord is "not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance," and early modern theological debates (especially between the Synod of Dort and the Remonstrants) marshaled these two texts against each other as the twin poles of the scriptural witness to divine will. The Exodus verse, read through Romans 9, anchors the sovereignty pole; 2 Peter 3:9 anchors the universal-desire pole. The biblical canon itself holds both and refuses to collapse them.

Second, **Exodus 9:18 → Joshua 10:11 → Revelation 16:21**. The hailstones that fall on Egypt recur in Joshua 10:11, where "the LORD threw down large stones from heaven on them" at Beth-horon, killing more Amorites than the Israelite sword. They recur again in Revelation 16:21 at the seventh bowl, where "great hailstones, about one hundred pounds each, fell from heaven on people." The plague hail inaugurates a biblical-theological typology of hail-as-cosmic-judgment that spans the Pentateuch, the historical books, and the Apocalypse.

Third, **Exodus 9:29 → Psalm 24:1 → 1 Corinthians 10:26**. Moses's declaration "that you may know that the earth is the LORD's" (*kî laYHWH hāʾāreṣ*) is the first appearance in Scripture of the formula that will become Psalm 24:1: "The earth is the LORD's and the fullness thereof." Paul quotes the psalm in 1 Corinthians 10:26 in his argument about food offered to idols. The theological claim Moses hurls at Pharaoh — that the earth is YHWH's, not Egypt's — becomes liturgical in the Psalter and ethical in Paul, tracing a line from the confrontation with imperial cosmology to the pastoral question of how the Corinthian church should eat.
