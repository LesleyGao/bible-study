# 1 Kings 9: The Second Appearance and the Cost of the Cedar

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

After the dedication's high theology, chapter 9 returns Solomon to the structures of empire — and to a divine warning that reads, in retrospect, like the chapter that frames the book's whole arc. The chapter has four loose units: (1) YHWH's second appearance to Solomon (9:1–9), (2) the disputed cession of Galilean cities to Hiram of Tyre (9:10–14), (3) the famous "Solomonic city-list" of forced-labor projects (9:15–24), and (4) maritime expedition to Ophir (9:25–28). The diversity of material reflects the dtr historian working with miscellaneous sources — likely the lost "Book of the Acts of Solomon" mentioned in 1 Kings 11:41.

The chronology in 9:10 — "at the end of twenty years, in which Solomon had built the two houses" — is the canonical sum: seven years for the Temple (chapter 6) plus thirteen for the palace (chapter 7). The narrator is reminding us, again, that the king's house got the larger share. The implied date for the second appearance is roughly 946 BCE. **Mordechai Cogan** in the Anchor Bible volume notes that Hiram's payment — 120 talents of gold (about 4 tons) — appears reciprocally in 9:14, suggesting the original passage may have detailed a more complex barter than the MT preserves; the Old Greek of "3 Reigns" 9 has variant orderings here.

The cession of "twenty cities in the land of Galilee" (9:11–13) has tantalizing archaeological correlates. Hiram's complaint — calling them "**Cabul**" (כָּבוּל), with a folk etymology punning on **כְּלֹא** ("good for nothing") or **כְּ-בוּל** ("as nothing") — names a real region in the Western Galilee just south of Tyre. The site **Khirbet Cabul** preserves the name. Excavations in the western Galilee and at sites like **Tel Keisan**, **Tell Abu Hawam**, and **Tel Akko** have shown a strong Phoenician material-culture signature in the early Iron II that fits the picture of Tyrian commercial and political reach into Israelite-claimed territory. Whether Solomon actually transferred sovereignty or only granted commercial concessions is debated; **Volkmar Fritz** and **Amihai Mazar** have read the archaeology cautiously. **Gary Knoppers** has written extensively on Israelite-Phoenician relations in this period.

The Solomonic city-list in 9:15 — **Hazor, Megiddo, Gezer**, plus Lower Beth-horon, Baalath, and Tamar — is the single most famous archaeological proof-text in the Hebrew Bible. **Yigael Yadin** in the 1950s–60s, excavating Hazor, identified a **six-chambered gate** there with close parallels at Megiddo and Gezer; he argued these were **Solomonic gates**, dating them to the tenth century on the basis of 1 Kings 9:15 itself. The argument was internally circular but was supported by ceramic typology and by the reasoning that uniform gate-architecture across three royal cities implied a single royal builder. **William Dever** has long defended a tenth-century Solomonic dating; **Amihai Mazar** has defended a "modified conventional chronology." **Israel Finkelstein**, beginning in the 1990s, mounted what is now called the **Low Chronology**, redating the gates and associated palaces to the **ninth-century Omride period** — Ahab's father Omri rather than Solomon as builder. The debate has shaped Iron Age Levantine archaeology for thirty years; the radiocarbon evidence is contested, and **Amihai Mazar** and **Christopher Bronk Ramsey** have argued for a date range that splits the difference. **William Dever** in *What Did the Biblical Writers Know and When Did They Know It?* offers the maximalist defense.

Three Hebrew terms anchor the chapter. **מִלֹּא** (*millôʾ*, "the Millo") in 9:15 and 9:24: a fortification or filled-earth platform in Jerusalem, built by David (2 Sam 5:9) and rebuilt by Solomon. The **Stepped Stone Structure** on the eastern slope of the City of David is the most plausible archaeological candidate; some scholars connect it with **Eilat Mazar's "Large Stone Structure"** identified above the steps and dated by Mazar to the tenth century (contested by Finkelstein and Singer-Avitz). **מַס** (*mas*, "forced labor, corvée") in 9:15, 21: the technical term for state-conscripted labor, with parallels in Ugaritic and Akkadian royal-administrative vocabulary. **כָּבוּל** (*kābûl*, "Cabul") with its derisive etymology — Hiram's quip is one of the rare moments of direct speech from a foreign king and gives the cities a memorial name. **אוֹפִיר** (*ʾôpîr*, "Ophir") in 9:28: the gold-source whose location is disputed (the Arabian peninsula? Somalia? India?). The Tell Qasile ostracon mentions "gold of Ophir" — physical evidence that *zhb ʾpr* circulated as a commercial term in tenth-century Israel.

The fleet at **Ezion-geber**, near Eloth (modern Eilat) on the Red Sea, is a real maritime infrastructure. Excavations at **Tell el-Kheleifeh** (identified by Nelson Glueck with Ezion-geber, though the identification is contested) found Iron Age remains; the more likely site may be **Jezirat Faraun** ("Coral Island") off the Sinai coast. The text's note that Hiram supplied "seamen who were familiar with the sea" is candid: Israel was not a maritime culture, and Phoenician expertise was indispensable. The 420 talents of gold (around 14 tons) is a large but not impossible figure for a period of significant Red Sea commerce.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is structurally pivotal, hinging on the divine speech at 9:6–9. The first half of 1 Kings 1–11 (Solomon's rise, wisdom, Temple) is celebratory; the second half (his administrative apparatus, foreign relations, ultimately his apostasy in chapter 11) is increasingly shadowed. Chapter 9 is the seam. **Robert Polzin** in *Samuel and the Deuteronomist* and **Jerome Walsh** in his commentary both read 9:6–9 as the dtr narrator's most explicit mid-book warning, with vocabulary so close to the curses of Deuteronomy 28–29 that the imitation is unmistakable. The phrase "Israel will become a proverb and a byword among all peoples... this house will become a heap of ruins" (9:7–8) is exilic prophecy retrojected: Cross, Nelson, and Cogan all read these verses as Dtr2 (exilic) composition that explicitly anticipates and explains the destruction of 587. The very form of the warning — "Why has the LORD done thus to this land and to this house?" — uses the Q&A structure of Deuteronomy 29:24–25, where exiles ask the same question.

The genre of the chapter is what **Burke Long** in his FOTL volume calls a **regnal summary** combined with a **theophanic warning**. The chapter does not advance plot so much as document the apparatus and articulate the conditions. The literary device most central is the **echo of chapter 3**. In 3:5–14 YHWH appeared to Solomon at Gibeon in a dream, offered him whatever he asked, and Solomon asked for wisdom. In 9:1–9 YHWH appears "a second time, as he had appeared to him at Gibeon" — but now the dream is replaced by a warning, the open offer by a closed conditional. The mirror is exact: where Gibeon expanded possibility, the second appearance contracts it. **Walsh** notes that the structural mirroring is the chapter's quiet judgment.

A second device is the **list as theological argument**. The forced-labor catalog in 9:15–22 piles up sites — Hazor, Megiddo, Gezer, Beth-horon, Baalath, Tamar, store cities, chariot cities, horsemen cities, "whatever Solomon desired to build" — and inserts a defensive gloss at 9:22: "But of the people of Israel Solomon made no slaves." This gloss directly contradicts 1 Kings 5:13–14, which says Solomon "drafted forced labor out of all Israel; and the levy numbered 30,000 men." **Mordechai Cogan**, **Marvin Sweeney**, and **Gary Knoppers** all read 9:22 as an apologetic gloss — the dtr historian (or a later editor) trying to soften the embarrassment of Solomon's conscription of his own people. The earlier text and the Chronicler's parallel (2 Chr 8:9) attempt to harmonize, but the seam is visible. Walsh argues that the contradiction is itself the chapter's theological argument: the narrator wants both to record imperial reality and to register imperial discomfort.

A third device is the **inclusio of foreigners**. The chapter opens with the YHWH-warning explicitly marking apostasy through "other gods" (9:6) and closes with maritime venture relying on Hiram's seamen (9:27) and gold from Ophir (9:28). In between, Pharaoh's daughter moves from the City of David to her separate house (9:24), and Hiram is partially-paid in disputed Galilean cities. Foreign presence is everywhere — and Solomon's apostasy in chapter 11 will be specifically through foreign wives. The narrator is laying the trip-wire.

Textually, **the LXX of 1 Kings 9** has substantial reorderings, especially around the city-list and the maritime venture. Cross, Trebolle Barrera, and Schenker have argued the OG sometimes preserves a different sequencing of these notices. The MT's placement of YHWH's warning at the chapter's head may itself be an editorial decision to give the second-appearance maximum theological weight at this hinge in the narrative.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish reading.** Josephus in *Antiquities* 8.130–162 narrates the chapter at length, especially the Galilee cession and the maritime venture; he treats Solomon's empire-building with admiration but also notes the foreign-wife problem with characteristic priestly conservatism. Rabbinic readings of the second appearance (*Sanhedrin* 21b; *Megillah* 11b) treat 9:6–9 as the moment Solomon should have understood his own vulnerability. **Rashi** at 9:13 explains the Cabul pun as Hiram's contemptuous rejection of the cities and notes that the **kāf-prefixed** name preserves the disgrace. **Radak** and **Ralbag** read the second appearance philosophically, with Ralbag treating the conditional structure of 9:4–7 as essential to a Maimonidean theology of providence — divine consequence follows human action lawfully. **Abarbanel** offers an extensive commentary on the chapter, treating the Galilee cession as Solomon's first political failure (giving away land of YHWH's inheritance) and the Cabul episode as a foreshadowing of the kingdom's fragmentation. **Avivah Zornberg** has written on the dream-and-warning structure linking chapters 3 and 9, the contraction of possibility. **Yair Zakovitch** and **Moshe Garsiel** attend to the Cabul pun and to the literary architecture.

**Christian reading.** Patristic exegesis took the second appearance with high seriousness. **Origen** reads 9:6–9 as a warning to the church not to rest in its sacred buildings. **Augustine** in *City of God* 17.20 treats the warning as already fulfilled in the Babylonian exile and as a typological warning to the visible church on earth. **Ambrose** treats Solomon's drift as a cautionary tale for Christian princes. **Chrysostom** dwells on the moral discipline required of a king who has been given everything. **Theodoret of Cyrus** in his *Questions on 3 Reigns* attends carefully to the Galilee cession and the forced-labor catalog, treating the chapter's tensions historically while noting their typological depth. **Gregory the Great** in the *Moralia* uses the second appearance as a model for the perpetual examination of the soul.

**Aquinas** in *Summa Theologiae* III, q. 102, a. 4 (on the ceremonial law) and elsewhere treats the conditional structure of the Davidic-Solomonic covenant as essential to a Christological reading: only Christ, the true Son of David, can fulfill the unconditional aspect of the promise; the Solomonic conditional necessarily fails, opening the trajectory toward the Messiah. **Calvin's sermons on 1 Kings** treat the second appearance with characteristic gravity — Solomon, despite the Temple, despite the wisdom, despite the dedication, is being warned. The lesson, for Calvin, is that no work, however holy, secures the soul. **Luther** in his lectures emphasizes that Pharaoh's daughter moving to her separate house (9:24) is the sign that Solomon's heart was already drifting before his official apostasy in chapter 11.

In modern critical scholarship, **Mordechai Cogan**'s Anchor Bible volume gives the most thorough treatment of the city-list and the Galilee cession; **Marvin Sweeney** in OTL reads the chapter as the dtr seam between celebration and critique. **Simon DeVries** in WBC and **Burke Long** in FOTL take more compositional approaches. **John Gray** in OTL is older but valuable. **Yigael Yadin** in his Hazor and Megiddo monographs and **Israel Finkelstein** in *The Bible Unearthed* (with Neil Asher Silberman) and *Has the Stable Been Found?* are the central voices in the Solomonic-vs.-Omride dating debate. **Amihai Mazar** offers the mediating position. **William Dever** defends the traditional dating most aggressively. **Gary Knoppers** in *Two Nations Under God* has written extensively on Israelite-Phoenician relations. **Walter Brueggemann** reads the chapter as the dtr historian's first explicit indictment of imperial theology. **Iain Provan**, **Ellen Davis**, and **John Goldingay** read pastorally and literarily; **Eugene Peterson** treats the second appearance as the moment Solomon should have repented.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — Conditional vs. unconditional covenant.** The chapter sharpens a tension implicit throughout 1 Kings: is the Davidic covenant unconditional (2 Sam 7:14–16: "if he commits iniquity, I will discipline him... but my steadfast love will not depart from him") or conditional (1 Kgs 9:4–7: "if you walk before me... if you turn aside... I will cut off Israel")? Three positions. (a) The unconditional reading: 2 Samuel 7 establishes a permanent dynastic promise; chapter 9's conditional only restricts the *individual* king's tenure, not the covenant itself. **Jon Levenson** distinguishes these in his work on covenant: the Davidic-Zion covenant is fundamentally a "covenant of grant," unconditional. (b) The conditional reading: the dtr historian, especially in the exilic redaction, makes the entire promise conditional on obedience, and the destruction of 587 is the application of 9:7. **Frank Cross**, **Richard Nelson**, and **Walter Brueggemann** read this way. (c) The dialectical reading: both vocabularies are present and held in tension; the conditional is real (and exile is its consequence) but the unconditional is also real (and the post-exilic restoration and, for Christians, the Davidic Messiah are its consequence). **Iain Provan** and **John Goldingay** lean here. The strongest version of (a) honors the trajectory through Psalm 89 and into messianic hope. The strongest version of (b) honors the exilic theological honesty — Israel did lose the land, and the dtr historian explains why. The strongest version of (c) honors the canonical layering. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter writes the conditional with painful clarity but does not say that conditional cancels promise. The Davidic line will be cut down to a stump; the question is whether the stump will sprout. The Hebrew Bible answers ambiguously, with Jeremiah's hope and Lamentations' grief held together. Christians answer: in the stump's branch.

**Tension 2 — Forced labor and the apologetic gloss.** 9:22's claim that "of the people of Israel Solomon made no slaves" directly contradicts 1 Kings 5:13–14. Three positions on what to do with this. (a) The harmonizing reading: the two texts describe different categories of labor — temporary corvée (chapter 5) vs. permanent slavery (chapter 9). Most older Jewish and Christian commentators harmonize. (b) The redactional reading: 9:22 is an apologetic gloss inserted to soften the embarrassment of Solomon's conscription of his own people; the contradiction is real, and the gloss is a sign of editorial discomfort. **Cogan, Sweeney, Knoppers, P. Kyle McCarter** all read this way. (c) The post-colonial reading: the chapter encodes a class structure in which "Israel" comes to mean the soldier-administrative class while indigenous peoples become permanent unfree labor — a structure with brutal real-world parallels. **Carol Meyers** and **Brueggemann** in his more critical mode read this way. The strongest version of (a) honors the canonical surface. The strongest version of (b) honors the textual evidence of editorial layering. The strongest version of (c) honors the human cost the text partially admits. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter records, partially conceals, and quietly judges the imperial labor regime that built the Temple. The dtr historian both writes the apologetic gloss and lets the contradiction stand. Honesty and embarrassment cohabit in the text.

**Unresolved question:** Does the second appearance imply that YHWH foresaw Solomon's apostasy at this point, with the warning therefore being a genuinely pastoral attempt to prevent it — or is the warning, in the dtr's exilic redaction, a *literary* device by which the historian can locate the cause of 587 in Solomon's later choices? The text does not say.

## The Thread

The chapter looks back to **1 Kings 3:5–14**: the first Gibeon dream, the open offer, the request for wisdom. The second appearance is structurally and verbally paired with the first; the contraction of possibility is the chapter's quiet judgment. It looks back to **2 Samuel 7**: the Davidic covenant whose conditional element 9:4–7 sharpens. It looks back to **Deuteronomy 28–29**: the curses for covenant-breaking, especially the Q&A about ruined houses (Deut 29:22–28), which 9:7–9 rewrites for the post-exilic reader.

It looks forward — and the cluster is dense — to **1 Kings 11:1–13**, where Solomon's foreign wives turn his heart and YHWH announces that the kingdom will be torn from him. Pharaoh's daughter moving to her own house (9:24) is the seed of 11:1's "many foreign women." It looks forward to **2 Kings 25** and to **Lamentations 1–5**, where the destruction of 9:6–9 is realized: the Temple becomes a heap of ruins, Israel a proverb among the nations, the conditional becoming the elegy. It looks forward to **Jeremiah 22:8–9**, which reuses 9:8–9's question almost verbatim ("And many nations will pass by this city, and every man will say to his neighbor, 'Why has the LORD dealt thus with this great city?'") — making chapter 9's rhetorical scenario the prophet's literal experience two centuries later.

For Christians, the chapter's conditional opens onto **Hebrews 3–4**'s reading of David and Solomon as figures whose disobedience left the promise's fulfillment to a greater Son. The trajectory from 1 Kings 9 — through exile, through Jeremiah's stump, through the silenced centuries — is the trajectory by which the unconditional element of 2 Samuel 7 had to outlive the conditional failures of David's sons and find its rest in the Son who walks before YHWH with integrity of heart and uprightness, doing all that is commanded.
