# Jeremiah 5: One Righteous Man, and the Lie Called Peace

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jeremiah son of Hilkiah preaches into the most compressed political crisis in Judah's history. His call comes in 627 BC under Josiah, the last serious reforming king (640–609), and his work reaches from the Deuteronomic reform through the deaths of Josiah at Megiddo (609), the brief reign of Jehoahaz (three months in 609), the long collaborationist reign of Jehoiakim (609–598), the three-month tenure of Jehoiachin (598/7), and finally Zedekiah (597–587) — through whose entire reign Jeremiah preaches the destruction Zedekiah refuses to believe in. Around all of this stand the geopolitical hinge points: the battle of Carchemish (605), where Nebuchadnezzar broke Egyptian power and inherited the Levant, and the three Babylonian deportations (605, 597, 587/6), the last of which destroys the Jerusalem temple. Jeremiah 5 belongs to the early-to-middle Jeremianic preaching, plausibly Josianic or early-Jehoiakim, when the disaster is still a sermon and not yet a smoking ruin.

Material culture confirms the chapter's social setting at almost every point. The Lachish ostraca, excavated by James L. Starkey between 1935 and 1938 and published by Harry Torczyner, give us soldiers' dispatches contemporary with Jeremiah's later ministry (cf. Jer 34:7); they reveal a Judah literate, militarized, and watching its perimeter forts collapse. The City of David excavations have produced a cluster of bullae that read like Jeremiah's table of contents: Berekhyahu son of Neriyahu the scribe (Baruch, Jer 36:4), Gemaryahu son of Shaphan (Jer 36:10), Yehukal son of Shelemyahu (Jer 37:3, 38:1), and Pashhur son of Immer (Jer 20:1) — alongside the Pashhur ben Immer and Gedalyahu ben Pashhur seal impressions found by Eilat Mazar in 2005 and 2008. No prophetic book is more thoroughly anchored in attested historical persons.

Religiously, this is the Judah of Yahwism layered with high places to Baal, Asherah pillars in family courtyards, and the Queen of Heaven cult that Jeremiah will detail in chapter 7. Anathoth — Jeremiah's priestly hometown three miles north of Jerusalem (modern Anata) — places him at the seam between rural Levitical piety and the urban temple establishment he indicts in chapter 5.

Authorship and dating run through Jeremiah and his scribe Baruch ben Neriah, with the canonical book preserving two divergent textual traditions. The Masoretic Text (MT) is roughly one-eighth longer than the Septuagint (LXX) and places the Oracles Against the Nations at the end (chs. 46–51); the LXX Vorlage relocates them after 25:13 in a different sequence. The Qumran finds — 4QJer-a, b, c, d — preserve fragments of both text types, ending centuries of debate about which is "original" by demonstrating that both circulated simultaneously in Second Temple Judaism. The standard modern commentaries are Jack R. Lundbom (Anchor Bible, three volumes 1999–2004), William Holladay (Hermeneia, 1986/89), Walter Brueggemann (two volumes, 1988/91), Robert P. Carroll (OTL, 1986, redaction-critical), William McKane (ICC, 1986/96), and John Bright (Anchor, 1965). Louis Stulman and Kathleen O'Connor have shaped the contemporary turn toward trauma-informed and literary readings.

Key Hebrew terms in chapter 5 carry the chapter. *Tsedaqah* (צְדָקָה, "justice") and *emunah* (אֱמוּנָה, "truth/faithfulness") in v.1 frame the Diogenes-search. *Shuv* (שׁוּב, "return/repent"), the Jeremianic leitwort that recurs more than a hundred times across the book, sits implicitly behind v.3's "they refused to take correction." *Shalom* (שָׁלוֹם, "peace, wholeness") drives v.12 — the false prophets' insistence that "no disaster will come upon us." *Davar YHWH* (דְּבַר־יְהוָה, "the word of the LORD") is what the false prophets are accused of lacking in v.13: "the word is not in them." And *to'evah*-adjacent terminology of *neuf* (נָאַף, "to commit adultery") in vv.7–8 governs the chapter's prostitution-and-stallion imagery.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Form-critically, Jeremiah 5 is a tightly stitched chain of judgment oracles in poetry (vv.1–17, 20–31) framing a Deuteronomistic prose insertion (vv.18–19). This pattern fits Sigmund Mowinckel's classic A/B/C three-source analysis (1914): A = poetic oracles attributed to Jeremiah; B = third-person biographical prose, traditionally Baruch's; C = Deuteronomistic prose sermons. Verses 18–19 — with their "as you have forsaken me and served foreign gods in your land, so you shall serve foreigners in a land that is not yours" — are textbook Mowinckel C, formally and ideologically of a piece with Deuteronomy 28's curses and the Deuteronomistic History.

The chapter opens with an imperative (v.1: "Run to and fro through the streets of Jerusalem"), pivots through a confessional inset (vv.4–5) in which the prophet himself voices a pretended naïveté, escalates to invasion-imagery (vv.15–17), and closes with the chapter's signature thunderclap: "an appalling and horrible thing has happened in the land: the prophets prophesy falsely, and the priests rule at their direction; my people love to have it so" (vv.30–31). The structural device is what Lundbom calls Jeremiah's "thus says the LORD" framing — v.14 inaugurates the announcement of judgment with the standard prophetic messenger formula after the people have spoken their false-shalom oracle in vv.12–13.

Devices accumulate. The lion-wolf-leopard triad of v.6 is a stock ANE judgment-imagery cluster — Hosea 13:7–8 deploys nearly the same predator chain — and the lions of Mesopotamian royal hunt reliefs (Ashurbanipal, North Palace, Nineveh, c. 645 BC) are the visual referent the audience would have known. The "lusty stallions, each neighing for his neighbor's wife" (v.8) is the most acidly satirical line in chapters 1–10, and may be the most graphic single image in the prophetic corpus. The sand-as-boundary-of-the-sea (v.22) is creation-theology pressed into ethical service: even the chaotic waters obey YHWH; this people will not.

Canonically, Jeremiah is the second of the Latter Prophets in the Hebrew ordering (after Isaiah, before Ezekiel and the Twelve). The Bavli's discussion at Bava Batra 14b acknowledges that Jeremiah's placement before Isaiah in some manuscript traditions reflected a thematic logic: "Kings ends with destruction, Jeremiah is all destruction, Ezekiel begins with destruction and ends with consolation, and Isaiah is all consolation."

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Chapter 5 sits at a hinge in the canonical arc. It belongs to the early Jeremianic indictment-cycle (chapters 2–10) that establishes the case for judgment that chapters 11–25 will make irrevocable, that chapters 26–45 will narrate as historical catastrophe, and that chapters 30–33 (the "Book of Consolation") and chapter 31's new covenant promise will eventually redeem. Without chapter 5's verdict — "you cannot find a man who does justice" — Jeremiah 31's "I will write it on their hearts" is unintelligible: the new covenant is necessary precisely because the search of 5:1 turned up nothing.

In Jewish reception, Jeremiah 5 has a quieter trajectory than chapters 7, 31, or the laments. Sirach 49:6–7 (early second century BC) singles out Jeremiah as the prophet who "was consecrated in the womb to be a prophet, to pluck up and ruin and destroy, and likewise to build and to plant." Bavli Megillah 14b lists Jeremiah among the seven prophets who prophesied to the gentile nations. Targum Jonathan paraphrases Jer 5:1 with characteristic intensification: "search her squares to see if a man can be found who does justice and seeks faithfulness — and I will pardon her *for his sake*." That last phrase — *bidil zakhuth*, "by the merit of" — is the Targumic seed of the rabbinic doctrine of *zekhut avot* (the merit of the righteous, often the Patriarchs) that runs through the Talmud and into the *zaddikim* tradition. The Bavli at Sukkah 45b famously says that "the world is never without thirty-six righteous men who receive the Shekhinah every day"; the *Lamed-Vav Tzaddikim* tradition extends this through medieval mysticism into modern Yiddish folklore. Rashi reads 5:1 plainly as an indictment of universal corruption; Radak (David Kimhi, 12th–13th c. Provence) softens slightly toward "if even one righteous person were found, judgment could be averted." Abrabanel (15th c. Spain/Italy) reads the Diogenes search as rhetorical exaggeration designed to provoke repentance, not literal head-count. Modern Jewish scholars — Yochanan Muffs, Moshe Greenberg, Benjamin D. Sommer — have read Jeremiah's intercessory framework against ancient Near Eastern parallels in which the prophet stands in the breach for the people.

In Christian reception, Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah* (c. 240, with fourteen Greek homilies surviving along with Jerome's Latin translation of others) reads Jeremiah allegorically and Christologically, treating the suffering prophet as a type of Christ. Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah* (begun late, incomplete at his death in 420; books 1–6 survive, taking him through chapter 32) is the major patristic resource and treats 5:1 as a prophecy fulfilled in the rejection of Christ — "they sought a righteous man and found none, until the Righteous One came." Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah* (1563, in five volumes, the longest of his Old Testament expositions) reads chapter 5 as a paradigmatic case of the doctrine of total depravity: the Diogenes search proves that "even among those who were thought to excel, there was no soundness." Bernhard Duhm's 1901 commentary inaugurated the modern source-critical reading of Jeremiah; Mowinckel's 1914 A/B/C analysis became the standard frame; Holladay, Carroll, and McKane refined and contested it through the late 20th century. Lundbom's Anchor commentary is the maximalist defense of substantial Jeremianic authorship; Carroll's OTL is the maximalist Deuteronomistic-redactional reading; the field has stabilized somewhere between them. Kathleen O'Connor's *Jeremiah: Pain and Promise* (2011) reads Jeremiah's rhetoric of judgment as trauma-disclosure rather than misogynistic invective — a reading that has reframed feminist engagement with the chapter's stallion-and-whore imagery (vv.7–8). J.A. Thompson's NICOT volume gives the steady evangelical-historical reading.

Where the traditions diverge most sharply on chapter 5: the Christian Christological frame turns the search of 5:1 into a Christological prophecy fulfilled (the Righteous One who alone could be found); the Reformed reading turns it into the proof-text for total depravity (no one was found, no one could have been); the Jewish *zaddikim* reading turns it into the doctrine that the world stands on the merit of hidden righteous individuals; the critical-Deuteronomistic reading reads it as theologically motivated judgment-rhetoric retroactively shaped by the actual destruction of 587. None of these readings can fully evacuate the others.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The one righteous person and the structure of mercy.** Jeremiah 5:1 is a deliberate echo of Genesis 18:23–32, where Abraham bargains YHWH down from fifty to ten righteous in Sodom. Jeremiah inverts the structure: not ten, not five — *one*. The Catholic intercessory tradition reads this trajectory through the saints and Mary as paradigmatic intercessors whose merit accumulates in the treasury of the Church (CCC §§946–962); the search of Jer 5:1 is fulfilled in Christ as the one righteous man, then extended through the communion of saints. The Reformed reading (Calvin, Owen, contemporary covenantal theology) takes the failure of the search as decisive: no one was found, no one could be found, the search itself proves the Reformed doctrine of total depravity and drives toward the necessity of imputed righteousness. The Lutheran reading sits adjacent — *simul iustus et peccator* — but emphasizes that the righteous person of 5:1 is found only extra nos, in Christ alone. The Eastern Orthodox tradition tends to read Jeremiah's search through the lens of synergy: the failure is real but the door of *metanoia* is never shut, and the Diogenes search is itself a divine condescension to seek what could repent. The Jewish tradition takes the steelman position that deserves the most careful hearing: the *zaddikim* doctrine — articulated at Sukkah 45b, developed in Hasidic thought, surviving in the *Lamed-Vav* folklore — holds that the world is sustained by a small number of hidden righteous individuals whose existence is itself the merit on which judgment is suspended. Read this way, Jer 5:1 is not a counsel of despair but a divine challenge: *find one*. The Targum's *bidil zakhuth* reading — pardoned *by the merit of* one — fits this trajectory exactly. The critical reading (Duhm, Carroll) reads the search as theological rhetoric retrojected onto the pre-587 situation by the Deuteronomistic redactor: the city was destroyed, therefore no one could have been found. **Where this leaves us:** Jeremiah 5:1 forces a choice about the structure of divine mercy — is it triggered by individual righteousness (Jewish *zaddikim*), by representative righteousness (Christ for the Church), by sheer grace independent of the search (Reformed sola gratia), or by the synergy of seeking and being found (Orthodox)?

**Tension 2: The political function of false peace.** The false prophets of 5:12–13 ("He will do nothing; no disaster will come upon us") and the priests-and-prophets pairing of 5:31 ("the prophets prophesy falsely, and the priests rule at their direction; my people love to have it so") raise a question Walter Brueggemann's *The Prophetic Imagination* (1978) makes the central political-theological problem of the prophetic corpus: what is the *function* of false prophecy? Brueggemann's answer — that the false prophets serve the "royal consciousness," the imperial-temple alliance whose maintenance requires the suppression of real grief and the manufacture of unreal hope — is the most influential modern reading. The Catholic social-teaching tradition (Rerum Novarum forward, especially Gaudium et Spes) reads false-peace prophecy as the religious face of structural injustice: the *pax* that requires the silencing of the orphan and the needy (Jer 5:28) is no peace. The liberation-theology critique (Gustavo Gutiérrez, Jon Sobrino) extends this: any peace that maintains the conditions of the oppressed is the false *shalom shalom v'ein shalom* of Jer 6:14. The Reformed prophetic-witness tradition reads 5:31 as a permanent indictment of state churches that trade prophetic word for political access; Bonhoeffer's *cheap grace* (Discipleship, 1937) is the 20th-century echo of "they have healed the wound of my people lightly," and his arrest and execution (1945) under the Third Reich for refusing the false peace of the German Christians is the modern test case. The Jewish tradition reads 5:31 through the figure of Hananiah (Jer 28) — the named false prophet who broke Jeremiah's yoke — and the rabbinic discussion at Sanhedrin 89a–90a of how to identify a false prophet. The contemporary critical reading (Carroll, McKane) tends to flatten the tension: false prophets are simply those whose oracles failed history's test. **Where this leaves us:** every prophetic-ish utterance — and every preaching, teaching, and political-theological statement made in religious voice — is exposed by 5:31 to a question Jeremiah will not let the reader avoid: *what is your prophecy doing for the powerful?* If the answer is "comforting them," the chapter's diagnosis applies.

The unresolved question that lands hardest: Jeremiah 5:31 ends with a phrase the Hebrew makes more disturbing than the English — *vé'ammi 'ahavu khen*, "my people love it so." Not "tolerate," not "accept" — *love*. What does it mean that the people themselves choose the lie, and that prophets who told the truth (Jeremiah, Uriah son of Shemaiah, Hananiah's victims, two and a half millennia of marginalized truth-tellers since) have been killed not by ignorant tyrants alone but by populations who *loved* the lie? Jeremiah will not answer that question. Neither will chapter 6, 7, or 8. The question runs through the remainder of the book and through every prophetic tradition that has read it.

## The Thread

The chain of cross-references most worth tracing forward and backward from chapter 5: First, Genesis 18:23–32 — Abraham's intercession for Sodom — which Jeremiah deliberately inverts in 5:1. Second, Deuteronomy 28:49–52 — "the LORD will bring against you a nation from afar… a nation whose language you do not understand" — which Jer 5:15 quotes almost verbatim, signaling the Deuteronomistic frame in which Jeremiah's invasion-warnings stand. Third, Jeremiah 6:14 and 8:11 — the famous *shalom shalom v'ein shalom* — extends the false-peace theme of 5:12 across chapters 5–8 and binds them into a single rhetorical movement. Fourth, the temple sermon of chapter 7 will pick up 5:31's "my people love to have it so" and aim it directly at the temple establishment whose three-fold *hekal YHWH* slogan (7:4) is the operationalized form of the false peace condemned here. And fifth, forward to Lamentations 4:13–15 — "this was for the sins of her prophets and the iniquities of her priests" — the post-587 elegy that names exactly what Jer 5:31 had warned and proves the indictment was not rhetorical exaggeration. Read 5:31 in light of Lamentations 4:13 and the chapter's question — *what will you do when the end comes?* — stops being rhetorical.
