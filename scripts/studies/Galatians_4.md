# Galatians 4: The Heir, the Slave, and the Spirit Who Cries Abba

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Whatever the precise destination of Paul's letter, his readers lived inside a Roman provincial order whose social grammar Paul both inhabits and subverts. The province of Galatia had been organized in 25 BC after the death of Amyntas, the last client king, when Augustus folded the territory into the imperial system. By Paul's day it stretched from the Phrygian uplands south through Pisidian Antioch, Iconium, Lystra, and Derbe — the southern route corresponding to Acts 13–14 — and northward to the Celtic-speaking regions around Ancyra, Pessinus, and Tavium. The North Galatian hypothesis (Lightfoot, Schlier, Martyn) places the addressees among ethnic Galatians evangelized on the second missionary journey (~AD 53–55); the South Galatian hypothesis (Ramsay, Bruce, Longenecker, Witherington) reads the recipients as the mixed-Gentile-and-Jewish congregations of Acts 13–14, which would put the letter as early as AD 48–49, possibly on the eve of the Jerusalem Council. Each dating reframes Galatians 2's encounter with Cephas at Antioch — either before or after the Council's compromise — and therefore reshapes our sense of how raw and unresolved the Judaizing controversy was when Paul wrote.

Roman provincial governance in this region was thin but real. The proconsul administered justice and tax; municipal elites ran assemblies; veterans had been settled at Pisidian Antioch under Augustus, where the imperial cult was unusually well attested in inscriptions and statuary, including the famous Res Gestae of Augustus inscribed bilingually on the Temple of Augustus and Roma. Paul's congregations would have been familiar with the visual rhetoric of imperial sonship — the *divi filius*, son of the deified Caesar — even as Paul deploys *huiothesia* (adoption) to claim a counter-sonship through the Spirit of God's own Son. The Roman household, anchored in *patria potestas*, was a legal universe in which the *paterfamilias* held life-and-death authority over freeborn children, slaves, freedmen, and clients alike. Slavery was ubiquitous and structurally indistinguishable in many tasks from the work of free dependents; manumission rates in urban Asia Minor were high; Roman adoption (the more typical *adoptio* of a child still under paternal authority, and the rarer *adrogatio* by which an independent adult was absorbed into another household, described carefully by Aulus Gellius in *Noctes Atticae* 5.19) was a public legal act creating full inheritance rights. Paul's argument in 4:1–7 — the heir who is "no different from a slave" until the father sets the date — is unintelligible without this material substrate.

The *paidagōgos* of 3:24, looming behind 4:1–2, was not a teacher but a household slave who escorted children to school and disciplined their conduct; Greco-Roman comedy and Plutarch alike depict the *paidagōgos* as a figure of necessary but humiliating supervision. Slaves carried *stigmata* — branded or tattooed marks of ownership; Herodotus, Aristophanes, and later legal sources describe the practice. Paul will pick this up at 6:17. Ancient agricultural metaphors saturate the imagination: the readers know what a barren woman's reproach looked like (4:27, citing Isaiah 54:1), and they know what it costs a household when the slave woman's son and the free woman's son share a tent (4:30, Genesis 21:10). Diaspora Judaism in Phrygia and Galatia is well attested archaeologically — synagogue inscriptions from Acmonia and elsewhere, the bones of long-settled Jewish communities — which means Paul's Gentile converts had access to Jewish observance through neighbors, godfearers, and competing missionaries. "You observe days and months and seasons and years" (4:10) most naturally points to Sabbaths, new moons, festal cycles, and sabbatical years, though some interpreters (Martyn, Witherington) hear in it the broader astral-calendrical religiosity of the Hellenistic world.

The textual base for Galatians is unusually strong. P46 (Chester Beatty II, ca. AD 200) preserves substantial portions of the letter, including chapter 4, and is our earliest witness; Codex Sinaiticus (ℵ) and Vaticanus (B) from the fourth century give the chapter in continuous form. The most discussed variant in the chapter is 4:25, where some manuscripts read *to gar Hagar Sina oros estin en tē Arabia* ("for Hagar is Mount Sinai in Arabia") and others *to de Sina oros estin en tē Arabia* (omitting the Hagar identification). The shorter reading is sometimes thought original, with scribes inserting Hagar to clarify Paul's allegory; Metzger's UBS committee leaves the longer reading but flags the difficulty. The variant matters because it determines whether Paul actually equates the slave woman with the mountain of the covenant.

Five Greek terms organize the chapter. *Huiothesia* (υἱοθεσία, 4:5) is "adoption as son," a Greco-Roman legal term Paul uses theologically, though scholars dispute whether his frame is Roman law (Lyall, Burke) or the Jewish hope of God's filial Israel (Scott). *Abba* (αββα, 4:6) is the Aramaic *abbā*, surviving untranslated alongside *ho patēr*; Joachim Jeremias famously over-claimed its intimacy as "Daddy" (a position now widely rejected — see James Barr, "Abba Isn't 'Daddy'"), but the word's preservation in Greek text remains a striking liturgical fossil of Aramaic-speaking prayer. *Stoicheia tou kosmou* (στοιχεῖα τοῦ κόσμου, 4:3, 4:9) is the chapter's most contested phrase — "elementary principles" or "elemental things of the world." *Allēgoroumena* (ἀλληγορούμενα, 4:24) is the present passive participle of *allēgoreō*, the only NT use of the verb, signaling Paul's awareness that he is performing an interpretive operation on the Genesis narrative. *Parousia* (παρουσία, 4:18) here denotes Paul's own bodily presence, not the eschatological coming — a usage that reminds us how much theological meaning the term acquired only later.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Hans Dieter Betz's 1979 Hermeneia commentary made the influential case that Galatians is a forensic apologetic letter built on Greco-Roman rhetorical conventions: *exordium* (1:6–11), *narratio* (1:12–2:14), *propositio* (2:15–21), *probatio* (3:1–4:31), *exhortatio* (5:1–6:10), and *peroratio* (6:11–18). On Betz's scheme, chapter 4 sits at the climax of the *probatio* — the proof section — closing with the Hagar/Sarah allegory as the rhetorical *coup de grâce* before the moral exhortation begins. Subsequent scholars (Longenecker, Witherington, George Kennedy) have argued for deliberative or epideictic-deliberative classification, observing that Paul is not defending himself against past charges so much as urging a future course of action. The dispute matters less than what it concedes: Paul writes inside a recognizable Hellenistic rhetorical world, even as he ruptures its conventions with a polemic too hot for any handbook.

The chapter is dense with literary devices. Verses 1–7 work as a sustained legal-domestic analogy — the heir-as-minor is no different from a slave under guardians, and only the father's *prothesmia* (set time, 4:2) marks the transition. The argument moves by tight parallel: "we also . . . were enslaved" (4:3) maps the human predicament onto the legal commonplace, then 4:4–5 ruptures the parallel with *to plērōma tou chronou* — the fullness of time — God's own *prothesmia*. The structure is chiastic in the verses on Spirit and sonship: sons → Spirit of the Son → cry Abba → no longer slave but son and heir. Verses 8–11 swing into diatribe, the Greek philosophical question-and-answer style descended from Bion of Borysthenes and adopted by Stoic and Cynic preachers; Paul's "how can you turn back again . . . ?" is classic *anakoinōsis*, deliberative interrogation.

Verses 12–20 are an emotional appeal — what classical rhetoricians called *pathos* — climaxing in maternal childbirth imagery so startling that commentators since Chrysostom have paused over it: "my little children, for whom I am again in the anguish of childbirth until Christ is formed in you" (4:19). Paul, the fierce apostle, here calls himself a mother in labor for his converts, an inversion of his usual paternal language (1 Cor 4:15). The pathos peaks in the irony of 4:15 ("you would have gouged out your eyes and given them to me"), which has fueled centuries of speculation that Paul's *astheneia* of 4:13 was an eye disease — though Lightfoot, Bruce, and others suggest malarial fever or epilepsy with equal plausibility.

The Hagar/Sarah passage of 4:21–31 is one of the New Testament's most distinctive literary moves. Paul announces explicitly that he is reading *allēgoroumena* — the only NT instance of "allegory" used as a verbal action — and then proceeds to align Hagar with Sinai, the present Jerusalem, slavery, and flesh, while aligning Sarah with the Jerusalem above, freedom, and Spirit. The structure is a sustained synkrisis (rhetorical comparison) culminating in the citation of Isaiah 54:1 (the barren one rejoicing) and Genesis 21:10 ("cast out the slave woman"). The interpretive method has Hellenistic-Jewish precedent — Philo of Alexandria allegorized Hagar as preliminary education and Sarah as virtue (*De Congressu* 11–12) — but Paul's identifications are not Philo's. Where Philo's Hagar is a positive station on the path to virtue, Paul's Hagar is the mountain of the covenant; the move is rhetorically scandalous and Paul appears to know it.

Two textual variants in the chapter affect meaning. The 4:25 problem (whether Paul originally identified Hagar with Sinai) was discussed above. A second variant cluster runs through 4:6–7, where some witnesses read "you" rather than "we" or vary the case of "heir," and the longer ending of 4:7 ("heir of God through Christ") in some manuscripts harmonizes with Romans 8:17. Locating chapter 4 in the larger argument: Paul has just argued in chapter 3 that Abrahamic promise precedes and outranks Sinai law, that Christ is the singular *sperma* in whom Gentiles inherit Abraham's blessing, and that the Law was a *paidagōgos* whose role has now ended. Chapter 4 turns the metaphor: not just *paidagōgos* but the whole condition of legal minority is over, replaced by adoption and the Spirit. The chapter prepares the ethical exhortation that begins in 5:1 with "for freedom Christ has set us free."

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** The patristic engagement with Galatians 4 was prolific. Marius Victorinus's late-fourth-century Latin commentary — the earliest surviving Latin commentary on a Pauline letter — read the Hagar/Sarah allegory as a figure of two peoples, with the church inheriting Sarah's promise. Jerome's commentary on Galatians (ca. AD 386), drawing heavily on Origen's lost work, deepened the allegorical reading, and Augustine's commentary (393–394) along with his much later treatment in *City of God* 15–16 fixed the supersessionist reading in Western Christian tradition: Hagar and Ishmael as the earthly city, Sarah and Isaac as the heavenly. Augustine's correspondence with Jerome (Letters 28, 40, 75, 82) wrestled mainly with Galatians 2 and the Antioch incident, but for chapter 4 it was his more allegorically inclined work that shaped a thousand years of preaching. Chrysostom, Theodore of Mopsuestia, and Ambrosiaster offered Antiochene-historical readings that were less allegorical but no less convinced of the chapter's ecclesial trajectory: the Sinai covenant as a temporary administration superseded by the new covenant of the Spirit.

Aquinas's *Lectura on Galatians*, delivered in Paris and Naples, treated 4:1–7 within his broader doctrine of grace and the New Law, reading the *stoicheia* as ceremonial precepts of the Old Law that no longer bind. Bernard of Clairvaux meditated on the freedom of the children of promise. The Reformation read chapter 4 with new urgency: Luther's 1535 *Lectures on Galatians* — his "Katherina von Bora," as he called the book — treated 4:21–31 as the foundational allegory of Law and Gospel, with Hagar's Sinai standing for the terrified conscience under Law and Sarah's Jerusalem for the free conscience under Gospel. Calvin's commentary (1548) was more restrained on the allegory but firm that the Sinai covenant, as administered to the unregenerate, bore "children of slavery." Anabaptist readings, from Menno Simons forward, lifted the chapter's freedom motif into a critique of state-church coercion.

Modern critical commentary begins with F.C. Baur's Tübingen reconstruction of Pauline-Petrine conflict (1845), continues through J.B. Lightfoot's masterful 1865 *Galatians* (still essential on the *paidagōgos* and the South Galatian theory), and reaches its twentieth-century apex in Hans Dieter Betz's 1979 Hermeneia commentary, the rhetorical analysis of which has shaped every subsequent treatment. Richard Longenecker's 1990 WBC, F.F. Bruce's 1982 NIGTC, and Ronald Fung's 1988 NICNT each represent strong evangelical-historical readings. J. Louis Martyn's 1997 Anchor Yale Bible commentary inaugurated the apocalyptic reading: the *stoicheia* are not religious training-wheels but cosmic spiritual powers, and Christ's coming is an invasion that breaks open a new creation. Douglas Moo's 2013 BECNT and Thomas Schreiner's 2010 ZECNT offer thorough Reformed-evangelical engagements with the apocalyptic school, while broadly retaining traditional Lutheran-Reformed soteriology.

The New Perspective on Paul, with roots in Krister Stendahl's 1963 essay "The Apostle Paul and the Introspective Conscience of the West," matured in E.P. Sanders's *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977) and James D.G. Dunn's commentary on Galatians (1993), reads the Sinai covenant in Galatians 4 not as a system of works-righteousness but as a national covenant whose boundary markers Paul refuses to import into Gentile communities. N.T. Wright's *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* (2013) treats the chapter as part of Paul's reworking of Israel's exile-and-return narrative around the Messiah and the Spirit. The apocalyptic Pauline school (Martyn, Beverly Gaventa, Douglas Campbell, Martinus de Boer) presses harder on the *stoicheia* and on 4:4's "fullness of time" as the rupture-event of God's invasion.

The Paul-Within-Judaism movement (Mark Nanos, Pamela Eisenbaum, Magnus Zetterholm, Caroline Johnson Hodge) has reread the chapter on the assumption that Paul never abandoned Judaism. Nanos in particular has argued that the agitators in Galatia were not Jewish-Christian missionaries demanding circumcision but Gentile influencers within local synagogues. Eisenbaum's *Paul Was Not a Christian* reads 4:21–31 as a Gentile-only argument: Hagar/Sinai stands for the path of Gentile proselyte conversion, not for Judaism per se, and Sarah for the Gentile inheritance of Abrahamic promise without Torah. On this reading, Paul is not telling Jewish believers to abandon Torah; he is telling Gentiles not to take it on as a means of inclusion.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** Joseph Klausner's *From Jesus to Paul* (1939) read Paul as a Hellenizer who broke from Jewish particularism into a universalism Jewish tradition could not accept. Hans Joachim Schoeps's *Paul* (1959) argued that Paul, reading Torah through a Hellenistic lens, fundamentally misunderstood its covenantal logic. The recovery of Paul as a Jewish thinker came with Daniel Boyarin's *A Radical Jew* (1994), which read the Hagar/Sarah allegory as a brilliant, if dangerous, Jewish midrashic move — Paul flipping the standard Jewish reading inside out so that the despised slave woman represents the mountain of the covenant. Boyarin admires the audacity even as he registers its costs. Paula Fredriksen's *Paul: The Pagans' Apostle* (2017) presses the case that Paul addresses Gentiles only and that misreading Galatians 4 as a verdict on Judaism was the church's later error, not Paul's argument. Pamela Eisenbaum agrees. Amy-Jill Levine has written extensively about the homiletic afterlife of the Hagar/Sarah allegory in Christian preaching, where it has functioned to delegitimize Judaism and, in extreme cases, to fuel anti-Judaism. Post-Holocaust Christian theology, the Second Vatican Council's *Nostra Aetate* (1965), and ongoing Catholic and Protestant ecumenical work have all had to reckon with how this chapter has been used.

The traditions converge in agreeing that Paul reads Genesis with rabbinic-style audacity, that he believes something epoch-shifting has happened in the sending of God's Son, and that the Spirit's cry of "Abba" marks a new mode of access to God. They diverge sharply on whether 4:21–31 authorizes a supersessionist reading of Judaism or whether it is a narrowly contextual rhetorical move against a specific Galatian crisis — and they diverge on what the *stoicheia* are. Lightfoot and Bruce read them as basic religious-pedagogical principles. Martyn and Wright read them as cosmic powers. The choice between these readings is partly a choice between modes of Pauline theology — pedagogical-historical vs. apocalyptic-cosmic.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did Paul believe God was doing in Galatians 4? Paul thought God had ended a long captivity. The fullness of time has come; God has sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under the law, to redeem those who were under the law; God has sent the Spirit of his Son into the hearts of Gentile believers, so that they pray in Aramaic the prayer Jesus prayed. The chapter reads the cosmos as having undergone a status change. What used to be observance of *stoicheia* is now reversion; what used to be the legal minority of an heir is now obsolete; what used to be slavery is now sonship. Paul is not preaching ethical improvement; he is announcing a transfer of jurisdiction.

**Tension One: What are the *stoicheia*, and what does it mean that the Galatians were "enslaved" to them?** Lightfoot, Bruce, and a tradition of philological-historical commentary read *stoicheia tou kosmou* as elementary religious teachings — the ABCs of religious knowledge to which both Gentile paganism and pre-Christian Judaism, in their unredeemed state, belonged. On this reading, Paul's argument is roughly pedagogical: humanity, like a child under guardians, was supervised by elementary religiosity until the Son came. Catholic teaching (CCC §§702–706, 1961–1964) has historically harmonized with this pedagogical reading, treating the Old Law as a divinely given preparatory tutor, with the New Law of grace as its fulfillment. The Reformed tradition similarly reads the Sinai administration as a typological-pedagogical phase. The apocalyptic school (Martyn, Gaventa, de Boer, Campbell) reads the *stoicheia* as cosmic spiritual powers — astral deities, calendar gods, the structures of an enslaved cosmos — to which both Gentile religion and even Torah observance, pressed into the service of separating Gentiles from God's promise, somehow belong. Wright reads them as the elemental powers of a creation in bondage, which Christ has overcome. The Orthodox tradition, with its strong cosmic-liberation soteriology (Lossky, Florovsky, Schmemann), is closer to the apocalyptic reading: Christ descends into a cosmos under the dominion of corruption and powers, and the Spirit rescues the church into theosis.

To steelman the apocalyptic reading — which is the one I find least intuitive on first encounter, because it seems to flatten the moral dignity of Torah by aligning it with pagan astral religion — the strongest case is exegetical: 4:8 ("you were enslaved to those that by nature are not gods") and 4:9 ("how can you turn back *again* to the weak and worthless *stoicheia*") form a tight parallel that treats Gentile paganism and prospective Torah observance as variations on a single condition. Paul is not saying Sinai is paganism; he is saying that to take on Torah as an entry-requirement for Gentile inclusion is to misuse it as another *stoicheion* — to import a power that the cross has dethroned. The apocalyptic reading takes seriously that 4:4 and Romans 8:3 describe an in-breaking, not a graduation. Where this leaves us: the *stoicheia* probably name the structures of an unredeemed age — including, in Galatians 4, the pre-Messianic administration of the Law as Gentile-excluding boundary marker — without thereby denigrating Torah as an artifact of pagan religion. Paul is making a sharper point about the structure of the present age than about Sinai itself.

**Tension Two: Does the Hagar/Sarah allegory authorize Christian supersessionism, or is it a contextual rhetorical move against Judaizing teachers?** The traditional Christian reading, deepened by Augustine and codified by Luther, takes 4:21–31 to teach that Sinai produces children of slavery and the Gospel produces children of promise — and therefore that Israel-according-to-the-flesh is theologically displaced. Lutheran two-uses-of-the-Law theology fits naturally here: the Law convicts and drives to Christ; the children of the promise live by faith. Reformed federal theology re-narrates the move as a covenantal succession: the old covenant administration gives way to the new, with the church now constituting the true Israel. Catholic teaching pre-Vatican II broadly inherited this displacement framework. After Vatican II, *Nostra Aetate* (1965) and subsequent magisterial documents (the 1985 *Notes on the Correct Way to Present the Jews and Judaism* and the 2015 "The Gifts and the Calling of God Are Irrevocable") have explicitly rejected supersessionism, affirming the ongoing covenantal status of Israel and acknowledging that Christian preaching of Galatians 4 has fueled centuries of anti-Judaism that needs repentance.

The Paul-Within-Judaism reading (Nanos, Eisenbaum, Fredriksen) and significant strands of the New Perspective (Stendahl, Wright in his irenic moments) argue that Paul's allegory is contextually narrow. It targets a specific Galatian crisis in which Gentile believers are being told they need circumcision and Torah-observance to inherit Abrahamic promise, and Paul replies — using a midrashic move available to him from his own Jewish formation — that the path of mandatory Gentile proselyte conversion produces "children of slavery" while the path of Spirit-given inheritance produces "children of promise." On this reading, the allegory says nothing about Jews who already live within the Sinai covenant; it says something about Gentiles considering whether to enter Israel through proselyte conversion. Boyarin reads Paul as a Jewish allegorist operating within rabbinic interpretive conventions — bold, even outrageous, but recognizably Jewish.

To steelman the traditional-supersessionist reading honestly: Paul does cite Genesis 21:10 — "cast out the slave woman and her son, for the son of the slave woman shall not inherit with the son of the free woman" — and applies it to a present opposition between two groups. He does identify Hagar with "the present Jerusalem." The text is not innocent of its later reception. The traditional reading is not a misreading invented in the fourth century; it is a possible reading of the text's apparent rhetorical thrust. Where this leaves us: post-Holocaust Christian honesty requires acknowledging that the chapter's homiletic afterlife has been catastrophic for Jews, that *Nostra Aetate* and parallel Protestant statements rule out the displacement reading as authoritative theological doctrine, and that the most defensible exegetical posture treats 4:21–31 as a rhetorical move within a specific Gentile-mission crisis rather than a verdict on Judaism. Whether Paul himself, writing under deadline in defense of his Gentile churches, knew exactly how his allegory would be used by readers no longer inside his Jewish-Christian community — that is an unanswerable question.

One unresolved question: if the *stoicheia* name the elemental powers of a not-yet-redeemed cosmos, and if even Torah pressed into Gentile-excluding service can become a *stoicheion*, then what counts as a *stoicheion* now? When Paul tells the Galatians they are reverting to slavery by observing days and months and seasons and years, what would he say to a contemporary Christian community whose calendar is set by the algorithmic feed, whose festivals are marketing cycles, and whose elemental forces are not astral but informational?

## The Thread

**Romans 8:14–17** — The same adoption-and-Spirit configuration appears in Romans, where the Spirit cries "Abba" and bears witness with our spirit that we are God's children. The Galatians 4 compression of inheritance, sonship, and Aramaic prayer becomes, in Romans, the basis for cosmic redemption (Rom 8:18–25), suggesting that for Paul the adoption motif always has cosmological extension.

**Genesis 21:8–14** — Paul's citation of Sarah's command to expel Hagar in 4:30 stands in deliberate tension with the Genesis narrator's tenderness toward Hagar (Gen 21:14–21, where God hears the boy and provides water in the wilderness). Reading Galatians 4 alongside Genesis 21 forces the question of whether Paul's allegory respects, or simply overrides, the source text's own moral grain.

**Isaiah 54:1** — Paul's citation of the barren one rejoicing connects his argument to Israel's exilic hope: the desolate woman whose children outnumber the married woman is, in Second Isaiah, restored Zion after Babylon. Paul's redirection of this verse to the "Jerusalem above" both honors and reroutes the prophetic original — a move that itself encodes the chapter's central interpretive question.

**Exodus 19** — The mountain of the covenant, which Paul aligns with Hagar and slavery, is the same mountain on which Israel's freedom from Egypt was constituted in Torah. The dissonance between Exodus 19's reading of Sinai as covenant-making liberation and Galatians 4:25's reading of Sinai as bearing children for slavery is not a contradiction Paul resolves; it is the productive friction his rhetoric creates, and it is the friction that makes the chapter both theologically generative and historically perilous.
