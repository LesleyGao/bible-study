# Isaiah 46 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Isaiah 46 belongs squarely inside Second Isaiah (chapters 40–55), which the mainstream-critical consensus since Bernhard Duhm's 1892 *Das Buch Jesaia* attributes to an anonymous prophet preaching in Babylon around 545–540 BC, in the run-up to Cyrus's 539 BC conquest. The unified-defense tradition (Motyer, Oswalt, Young) reads the chapter as the predictive work of the eighth-century Isaiah ben Amoz; the Childs canonical-criticism position holds the literary unity of the book without resolving the question of compositional history. What every position must reckon with is that chapter 46 reads as the work of someone with eyes on the Babylonian streetscape: it visualizes, in one of the most concrete images in the prophetic literature, what was about to happen to the gods of Babylon.

The historical context is the **akītu festival**, the Babylonian New Year procession in which the great cult-statues of the city — *Bēl-Marduk*, the chief god of Babylon, and *Nabu*, the scribal god of nearby Borsippa, son of Marduk — were carried in solemn ritual through the city. The statues were paraded on platforms borne by priests; this was not weakness but high ceremony. Isaiah 46 takes that processional image and inverts it. The gods are still being carried — but now they are being carried *off*, as plunder, on the backs of weary pack-animals fleeing Cyrus's advance.

The accuracy of the historical detail is striking. The **Nabonidus Chronicle** (British Museum BM 35382), an ancient cuneiform record of the events of 539 BC, reports that Nabonidus, in the years before Cyrus's invasion, had ordered the gods of the smaller Babylonian cities brought into Babylon for safekeeping. When Cyrus's army approached, the gods that had been gathered into Babylon, and even some of the city's own statues, were either captured or hastily evacuated. The Cyrus Cylinder presents this from Cyrus's side: *Marduk* is said to have looked on the disaster of the displaced gods with displeasure and to have raised up Cyrus to set things right by returning the gods to their home cities. Isaiah 46 sees the same scene from the opposite angle: the gods of Babylon are not majestic — they are dead weight, being hauled into captivity by exhausted animals. *They cannot save the burden, but themselves go into captivity* (46:2).

The textual witness is firm. The **Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa^a)** from Qumran Cave 1, paleographically dated to roughly 125 BC, preserves chapter 46 with only orthographic variation from the Masoretic Text. The chapter's anti-idolatry polemic was a fixed part of Isaiah's text well before the Christian era.

The Hebrew vocabulary is tightly controlled around the verb-pair *carry / bear*. Five times in the opening four verses some form of the root *nasa* (נשא) or *amas* (עמס, *to load, burden*) appears. The Babylonian gods *are carried* (*nasa*); Yahweh *carries* (*nasa*) Israel. The decisive line is 46:4 — *I have made, and I will bear; I will carry and will save* — in Hebrew *ani asiti va-ani esa, va-ani esbol va-amallet* (אֲנִי עָשִׂיתִי וַאֲנִי אֶשָּׂא וַאֲנִי אֶסְבֹּל וַאֲמַלֵּט). The contrast is exact: idols must be carried by their worshippers; Yahweh carries his worshippers. The radical-monotheism formula returns: *for I am God, and there is no other; I am God, and there is none like me* (46:9, *ki anokhi El ve-ein od, Elohim ve-efes kamoni*). And in 46:11 the prophet circles back to the Cyrus motif of chapter 45 with a fresh image: *calling a bird of prey from the east, the man of my counsel from a far country*. The "bird of prey" — Hebrew *ayit* (עַיִט) — is widely read by both critical (Blenkinsopp, Goldingay) and conservative commentators (Oswalt, Motyer) as Cyrus, whose royal standard was reported in classical sources to feature a golden eagle.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Chapter 46 is built as a tightly compressed three-part argument. **Verses 1–2** are a *taunt-song* against the Babylonian gods, in the form of a parade-scene reversal. **Verses 3–7** are a *disputation*: a direct contrast between Yahweh's care for Israel and the helplessness of idols, structured as a pair of mirror panels. **Verses 8–13** are a *trial-speech* and announcement of imminent salvation, in which Yahweh appeals to memory (*remember the former things of old*) and announces that his deliverer is on the way.

The poetic structure works through the *carrier-versus-carried* motif as its single load-bearing image. In verses 1–2 the gods are passively borne and cannot save what they were supposed to protect. In verses 3–4 Yahweh bears Israel — actively, personally, from womb to gray hair. In verses 6–7 the worshippers of idols make their gods, lift their gods, set them in place, and find that the gods *cannot move from their place*. The literary punchline is verse 7's flat indicative: *if one cries to it, it does not answer or save him from his trouble*. The contrast frames Yahweh not as the most powerful God in a divine pantheon but as the one God who acts where idols cannot.

The womb-to-old-age image of 46:3–4 is one of the most maternal-paternal pictures of God in the Hebrew Bible. The verbs *amas* (load up, bear), *nasa* (lift, carry), and *sabal* (carry a heavy load) are the language of a parent carrying an infant or an aging dependent. Yahweh is portrayed as the burden-bearer for Israel from before birth (*me-rechem*, "from the womb") through old age (*ad-zikna*, "to gray hair"). This is a rare and tender register inside a chapter whose surrounding verses are fierce polemic.

## Lens 3: Inside the Larger Story

**Within the prophetic canon**, Isaiah 46 belongs to a stream of anti-idolatry polemic that runs from Jeremiah 10 (*they are like scarecrows in a cucumber field; they cannot speak; they have to be carried, for they cannot walk*) through Habakkuk 2:18–20 to Psalm 115:4–8 (*they have mouths but do not speak, eyes but do not see*). What distinguishes Isaiah 46 from the others is its specificity: this is not a generic critique of idolatry; it is a named, time-stamped indictment of the cult-statues of *Bēl* and *Nabu* in the moment of their political defeat.

**Jewish trajectory**: The chapter became a foundational text for Jewish anti-idolatry theology and for the recognition of God as the *bearer* and *redeemer* of Israel. Rabbinic interpretation in *Sifrei Devarim* and the *Mekhilta* drew on the *carried-from-the-womb* motif to develop the theology of God's covenantal patience with Israel through every stage of national life. The radical-monotheism formula of 46:9 became, with the parallel verses in chapters 44 and 45, part of the ground for the daily *Shema* devotion and for the *Aleinu* prayer's confession of God as the only God. Maimonides in the *Mishneh Torah* (Hilkhot Avodah Zarah) cites Isaiah 46 as one of the central scriptural warrants for the prohibition of idolatry. The maternal Yahweh image of 46:3–4 was preserved in the rabbinic tradition without much theological anxiety: God carries Israel as a parent carries a child.

**Christian trajectory**: The chapter has been less heavily Christologized than its neighbors but has remained important for theological anthropology and for the doctrine of providence. Calvin treats 46:9–10 (*declaring the end from the beginning... my counsel shall stand*) as one of the central proof-texts for divine foreknowledge and the comprehensive sovereignty of God in his *Institutes* (I.16, I.18). Augustine in the *City of God* draws on the chapter's contrast between Yahweh and idols to argue for the difference between the *civitas Dei* and the *civitas terrena*. The *womb-to-old-age* passage of 46:3–4 has been read pastorally across Christian traditions as a counter to anxieties about aging, dependence, and death — God carries the believer not only at the beginning but at the end.

**NT echoes**: Direct quotation of Isaiah 46 in the New Testament is sparse, but the anti-idolatry pattern surfaces in Paul's polemic in Acts 17 (Athens) and 1 Corinthians 8–10, and the *declaring the end from the beginning* of 46:10 stands behind Revelation 22:13 (*I am the Alpha and the Omega, the first and the last, the beginning and the end*). The Mary-as-bearer language of Luke 1 and the *theotokos* tradition of patristic Christology stand in interesting analogical relation to the *I have borne you from the womb* of Isaiah 46:3, though the direct allusion is to other texts.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — Where the Tensions Sit

Three tensions deserve honest engagement.

**First, the carrier-versus-carried theology and modern reception.** The contrast in 46:1–7 is one of the cleanest in the Hebrew Bible: false gods must be carried; the true God carries his people. Modern theological reflection has run with this in several directions. The mainstream Christian-pastoral reading (across Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant traditions) has been to treat the *I will carry you to gray hairs* of 46:4 as a foundational text for the theology of dependence — that the proper posture of the human creature before God is not striving but receiving. The Reformed sovereignty tradition (Bavinck, Berkhof) has read the contrast as revealing the difference between religion-as-human-construction and religion-as-divine-initiative: idols are made by their worshippers (46:6); Yahweh makes his worshippers (46:4). The mainstream-critical reading (Westermann, Goldingay) reads the chapter more polemically: this is exilic theology built for an audience watching the Babylonian gods get carted off as plunder, and its claim is sharper than a general truth about religion — its claim is *only Yahweh saves, and the proof is what is happening this week to the gods of your captors.* The honest steelman of the comparativist critique — that ancient Babylonian theology was more sophisticated than Israelite polemic allowed, and that the cult-statues were never thought of as the *gods themselves* but as the gods' bodily presence for the sake of accessibility — has force. But Second Isaiah is not engaging Babylonian theology on its best academic terms. He is preaching to exiles whose grandparents watched Yahweh's temple be destroyed by people who were sure Marduk had won, and he is announcing that the verdict is being reversed in real time.

**Second, the maternal-paternal Yahweh image.** Verse 46:3–4 — *who have been borne by me from before your birth, carried from the womb; even to your old age I am he* — is one of a small but significant cluster of Hebrew Bible passages in which God is portrayed in what feminist biblical scholarship has called *maternal* terms (Phyllis Trible's *God and the Rhetoric of Sexuality*, 1978). The image is not strictly maternal — the verbs *amas* and *nasa* are used for both fathers and mothers carrying children — but the *womb* (*rechem*) language is unmistakably gestational and maternal. Modern Christian reception has run the spectrum: Catholic teaching (CCC §239) holds that God transcends the distinction of the sexes and that scriptural feminine images of God are genuinely revelatory while not changing the priority of the *Father* language given by Jesus; mainstream Protestant traditions have generally received the maternal images as enriching rather than overturning the trinitarian language of address; Jewish tradition has generally treated the maternal Yahweh imagery as theologically significant without making it the basis for a programmatic re-genderization of liturgy. The honest position is that the text means what it says: the God of Isaiah 46 is a God who carries Israel from the womb. The image is given. What is contested is what to do with it liturgically and dogmatically — and on that, traditions differ in good faith.

**Third, the *I will accomplish all my purpose* of 46:10.** *Declaring the end from the beginning... my counsel shall stand, and I will accomplish all my purpose.* This verse, with its companions in 45:7 and 48:3–5, is one of the strongest scriptural warrants for the doctrine of comprehensive divine sovereignty and foreknowledge. The Reformed sovereignty tradition (Calvin, Edwards, Bavinck) reads it as plain affirmation: God's counsel is exhaustive, his purposes encompass every contingency, and history unfolds along the pattern he has decreed. The Arminian / Wesleyan tradition holds that *my counsel shall stand* is consistent with genuine human freedom, distinguishing what God *foreknows* from what God *causes*. The open-theism tradition (Greg Boyd, John Sanders) reads the verse more narrowly — Yahweh's *purpose* in this passage is the specific purpose announced in context, the deliverance of Israel through Cyrus, and not a comprehensive metaphysical claim about every event. The mainstream-critical reading (Goldingay, Blenkinsopp) emphasizes the polemical contrast: in the disputation context, the point is that Yahweh, unlike the carried idols of Babylon, actually does what he says he will do. The honest position is that the verse will not be made to settle the metaphysics on its own. It is a confession of trust spoken by a prophet to exiles: the God who has carried us this far will finish what he started.

**Where this leaves us**: Isaiah 46 holds together two images that the modern theological imagination tends to keep apart. Yahweh is the cosmic sovereign who declares the end from the beginning, who calls a bird of prey from the east, whose counsel will stand. And Yahweh is the parent who bears the same people from the womb to gray hair, who will not put them down. The chapter does not divide these — it puts them on the same page, in the same speaker's mouth, in the same week of preaching. The God who is carrying Israel is also the God who is dismantling Babylon, and these are the same act.

**Closing question**: If the contrast between idols-that-must-be-carried and the God-who-carries is the chapter's argument, what are the things in our own lives that we keep lifting and setting back in place — and what would change if we let ourselves be carried instead?
