# Proverbs 6 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Proverbs 6 belongs to the long instructional sequence that opens the book (chapters 1–9), a cluster of parental discourses framed as a father addressing "my son" and exhorting him toward *chokhmah* (חָכְמָה, wisdom) and away from the snares of folly. The compositional history of Proverbs, as worked out by Bruce Waltke in his two-volume *NICOT* commentary, Michael V. Fox in his *Anchor Bible* volumes, Roland Murphy in *Hermeneia*, and Tremper Longman in his Baker commentary, is widely understood as composite. The sentence-proverbs that begin in chapter 10 carry a Solomonic-era core (tenth century BCE), while the extended instruction-poems of chapters 1–9 reflect a later editorial frame, most plausibly Persian-period (sixth–fourth century BCE), when the wisdom material was gathered, expanded, and given its present pedagogical shape. William P. Brown (*Wisdom's Wonder*) and Christine Roy Yoder (*Proverbs* in the Wisdom Commentary series) read the chapter-1–9 frame as a deliberate hermeneutical lens — readers are taught how to read the older proverbs that follow.

Chapter 6 contains four distinct units, possibly stitched together in successive editorial layers: the warning against rash *surety* or pledged guarantees (vv. 1–5), the famous sluggard-and-the-ant (vv. 6–11), the portrait of the *belial* — the worthless-man (vv. 12–15), the numerical *six-then-seven* abominations of YHWH (vv. 16–19), and a second extended adultery warning (vv. 20–35). Mark Boda and Will Kynes, in their work on wisdom-as-a-genre, note that chapter 6 disrupts the otherwise cleaner instructional flow of chapters 5 and 7, suggesting it gathers material that belonged to a related but distinct tradition.

The ANE backdrop is well established. The sluggard-and-the-ant has no exact parallel in extant ANE literature, but the figure of the lazy-pupil is a stock character in Egyptian instruction texts — the *Instruction of Amenemope* (likely 13th–11th c. BCE), which Fox and others have shown stands behind portions of Proverbs 22–24, repeatedly contrasts the diligent "silent man" with the "heated man" who fails. The numerical *x/x+1* parallelism of 6:16–19 ("six things… seven that are an abomination") is a recognized Northwest Semitic rhetorical device — Amos 1–2 uses the same form ("for three transgressions… and for four"), as does Job 5:19 ("six troubles… seven"). Yoder catalogues the form across Ugaritic poetry and the Sumerian *Instructions of Shuruppak*. The technique foregrounds the climactic final item: in Proverbs 6:19, "one who sows discord among brothers" is the rhetorical apex of the list.

Several Hebrew terms carry the chapter's weight. The opening unit on surety uses *‘arav* (עָרַב, to pledge for) and *taqa‘ kappekha* (תָּקַע כַּפֶּיךָ, "you have struck your hand," the gesture of contractual guarantee). The sluggard is *‘atzel* (עָצֵל), a term Waltke notes appears almost exclusively in Proverbs and connotes not merely physical laziness but a deep moral indolence — a refusal to engage the disciplined effort wisdom requires. The "worthless person" of v. 12 is *‘adam beliya‘al* (אָדָם בְּלִיַּעַל), where *beliya‘al* later becomes a name for cosmic evil (2 Cor 6:15: *Belial*). Most freighted is the phrase that frames vv. 16–19: *to‘avot Yahweh* (תּוֹעֲבוֹת יְהוָה), "abominations of YHWH." The singular *to‘evah* (תּוֹעֵבָה) is a category-term used elsewhere of idolatry (Deut 7:25), unclean food (Deut 14:3), forbidden sexual practices (Lev 18:22), unjust weights (Prov 11:1), and prayer that comes from a wicked heart (Prov 28:9). It marks something as ritually-and-morally repugnant within YHWH's covenant order.

## Lens 2 — Literature

Proverbs 6 is generative for understanding the literary architecture of the book. The chapter-1–9 instructions belong to the genre of *mashal* (מָשָׁל) in its long-discourse form — extended pedagogical poems addressed by a father (or Wisdom personified) to a son. Beginning in 10:1, the *mashal* compresses into the two-line sentence-proverb that dominates the central collection. Chapter 6 sits at the seam, and its four units demonstrate the range of forms wisdom literature deploys.

The surety unit (vv. 1–5) uses *staircase intensification*: the warning escalates through accumulating verbs — *go, hasten, plead, give your eyes no sleep, save yourself like a gazelle*. The piling of imperatives mimics the urgency of the situation. The sluggard pericope (vv. 6–11) is a *vignette* — a teaching scene in which the pupil is invited to observe a creature and draw the moral. *Lekh el-nemalah ‘atzel* (לֵךְ אֶל־נְמָלָה עָצֵל), "go to the ant, sluggard," is a command to look. The poem ends with a chillingly economical image: *me‘at shenot, me‘at tenumot, me‘at chibbuq yadayim lishkav* — "a little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to lie down" — and poverty arrives *kimhalek*, "like a vagrant," and want like an *ish magen*, "an armed man." Brown reads the cadence as deliberate lulling: the proverb's rhythm enacts the slide into ruin it warns against.

The portrait of the worthless-person (vv. 12–15) uses *bodily synecdoche* — the eyes that wink, the feet that signal, the finger that points, the heart that devises. The body-parts catalogue is mirrored, deliberately, in the *six/seven* abominations that follow: haughty *eyes*, lying *tongue*, *hands* that shed innocent blood, *heart* that devises, *feet* that hurry to evil, *false witness*. Fox observes that the rhetorical effect is to dismantle the wicked man limb-by-limb, then have YHWH name those same limbs as the targets of divine hatred. The composition is virtuosic.

The *six-then-seven* numerical proverb (vv. 16–19) is the form's textbook instance. The graduated count — "six things… *yea* seven" — marks the seventh as the climax. That climactic position holds *meshalleach medanim bein achim* (מְשַׁלֵּחַ מְדָנִים בֵּין אַחִים), "one who sends discord among brothers." The literary structure tells the reader: of all the things YHWH hates, the disruption of community-bonds tops the list.

The adultery warning (vv. 20–35) returns to the long-discourse form of chapter 5 and anticipates chapter 7. It opens with the parental commandment-as-lamp metaphor (*ki ner mitzvah ve-torah ‘or*, "for the commandment is a lamp and the teaching a light"), then deploys two *qal vachomer* (lesser-to-greater) arguments: if a thief caught stealing bread pays sevenfold, how much more the adulterer; if no man can carry fire in his bosom without being burned, how much less can the adulterer escape his own ruin. The chapter ends not with a redemptive turn but with the grim closure of a wronged husband whose jealousy *lo yisa pnei kol kofer*, "will accept no compensation."

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the Hebrew canon, Proverbs 6 contributes to the wisdom literature that Job, Ecclesiastes, and (in the broader Apocrypha) Sirach and Wisdom of Solomon will both extend and contest. Sirach 8:12–13 echoes Proverbs 6:1–5 directly: do not lend money to one stronger than yourself. Sirach 22:1–2 echoes the sluggard-pericope. The *to‘evah* category is foundational across the Pentateuch — Deuteronomy uses the term over 15 times, particularly to mark the Canaanite practices Israel must avoid (Deut 12:31, 18:9–14). When Proverbs 6 places interpersonal sins (haughty eyes, false witness, sowing discord) under *to‘evah*, it democratizes the category — what marks Canaan as cultically-foreign also marks the mundane vices of the everyday.

The Jewish reception is rich. *Mishlei Rabbah*, the medieval midrashic compilation, treats the seven abominations of 6:16–19 as a typology of the seven generations of the wicked (Cain, Enosh's generation, the Flood generation, Babel, Sodom, Pharaoh, Amalek). Rashi, working in 11th-century France, reads the surety warning as halakhic — a directive against the careless cosignature that would render one's household legally exposed. Ibn Ezra, in 12th-century Spain, emphasizes the rationalist register: the sluggard's poverty is not a divine curse but the natural consequence of un-disciplined labor. The Talmud (b. Pesachim 113b) cites Proverbs 6:6 in praise of the ant's modesty and industry.

The Christian trajectory has used the chapter heavily for moral catechesis. Augustine, in his *Enarrations on the Psalms* and various sermons, returns to 6:16–19 as a checklist for examined conscience. Aquinas, in *Summa Theologiae* II-II, draws on the catalogue to elaborate his treatment of the vices contrary to charity (q. 34–43), particularly *susurrus* and *detractio* under "sowing discord." Calvin's commentary on Proverbs reads the surety warning as a pastoral economics — Christian charity does not require imprudent self-destruction. Among modern critical commentators, Murphy's *Hermeneia* volume reads the chapter's unity as more thematic than compositional; Waltke argues for stronger structural coherence; Fox and Longman split the difference; Brown emphasizes the chapter's pedagogical hospitality.

The New Testament does not quote Proverbs 6 directly with frequency, but its categories pulse beneath the moral teaching. James 3:5–10 on the tongue draws on the same imaginative pool as 6:12–19. Romans 1:29–31's vice list, with its inclusion of "gossips" and "those who invent ways of doing evil," echoes the worthless-man portrait. The "abomination" category recurs in apocalyptic register (Mark 13:14; Rev 17:4–5), now reoriented toward eschatological idolatry.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

The chapter's theological work is both narrower and stranger than the moralizing reception has often allowed.

**Tension 1: The *to‘evah* category and modern reception.** The Hebrew *to‘evah*, translated *abomination*, has been weaponized in modern church-debates over sexuality, particularly through the appeal to Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13. Proverbs 6:16–19 is a critical text in any honest account of the term because it does not place sexual practices in the list at all. The seven *to‘avot* of YHWH here are: pride, lying, violence, scheming, evil-haste, false witness, sown-discord. Saul Olyan and Jacob Milgrom, in their respective work on Leviticus, have shown that *to‘evah* is a *category-term*, not a degree-marker — it identifies what falls outside YHWH's order, which can mean ritual purity (foods), cultic alterity (idolatry), economic injustice (false weights, Prov 11:1), or sexual practice. The Catholic Catechism (§2357–2359), in its careful treatment of homosexual acts, distinguishes between the act, the inclination, and the person, and is careful to avoid collapsing the *to‘evah* category into a single applied weapon.

The steelman case for the traditional Christian reading: Proverbs 6's catalogue does not exhaust the *to‘evah* category; Leviticus, Deuteronomy, and elsewhere extend it; the moral grammar of Scripture is canonical, not chapter-bound. The steelman case for the critical reading: Proverbs 6:16–19 is the most rhetorically prominent *to‘evah* list in the wisdom canon, and its content is interpersonal rather than sexual or cultic; any account of *what YHWH most hates* that ignores the chapter's own answer is selective. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter's seven *abominations* — pride, deception, violence, scheming, evil-haste, false witness, sown-discord — should sit at the center of the church's moral attention, not at the periphery. If a Christian community's "abomination" vocabulary attaches more readily to others' bedrooms than to its own gossip, its own pride, its own sown discord, the wisdom of Proverbs 6 has not yet been heard.

**Tension 2: Surety, sluggard, and the moral weight of prudence.** The chapter elevates *prudential* virtues — financial caution, work-discipline — to a level the modern reader may find startling. Is rash surety really worthy of a vignette as dramatic as the gazelle fleeing the hunter? Is sleep really an enemy worth four verses? Position one (Augustine, Aquinas): the practical is the moral; the household-economics of surety and labor are not below the dignity of divine instruction because YHWH governs the whole of life. Position two (modern existentialist or pietist readings): the prudential register is sub-spiritual; the chapter is "common-sense wisdom" and should not be conflated with theological depth. Position three (Brown, Yoder): the integration is itself the theological claim — wisdom collapses the secular/sacred binary; the form of life that honors YHWH is the form of life that pays its bills, keeps its hands working, and refuses to sign what it cannot redeem. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter's prudential register is theological precisely because it is prudential. The God of Proverbs is a God of mortgages and alarm clocks.

**Tension 3: The adultery warning and gendered audience.** The second adultery warning (vv. 20–35) directs the male disciple away from another man's wife and ends with the cuckolded husband's vengeance, not with any concern for the woman herself. Carol Newsom, Christine Roy Yoder, and Phyllis Trible have all worked the patriarchal grammar of the wisdom-instruction tradition. The text addresses men, warns them about women, and treats marriage primarily as a property-and-honor system in which jealousy is a husband's prerogative. The honest Christian reading must hold both that the wisdom is real (adultery does ruin lives, families, and souls) and that the framing is narrow (the woman's interiority and agency are not the chapter's concern). **Where this leaves us:** the contemporary reader hears a true warning in a partial register. The warning extends; the framing is to be questioned, not preserved.

The closing question: of the seven things YHWH most hates — pride, lying, violence, scheming, evil-haste, false witness, sown-discord — which would your community name first if asked, and which would it name last? The answer locates you on the map of Proverbs 6.

## The Thread

Proverbs 6 collects four warnings that look unrelated — surety, sloth, malice, adultery — and quietly insists they are one. Each is a failure to count the cost. The naive cosigner does not count what his pledge will cost his household. The sluggard does not count what his sleep will cost his harvest. The worthless man does not count what his discord-sowing will cost the brotherhood. The adulterer does not count what an hour will cost a life. Wisdom is the practiced art of seeing the bill before it arrives.
