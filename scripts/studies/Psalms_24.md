# Psalm 24: Lift Up Your Heads — The King of Glory at the Gate

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The superscription is *le-David mizmor* (לְדָוִד מִזְמוֹר), "Of David, a Psalm." Form-critically, Psalm 24 is best understood as a *liturgical entrance psalm* (Gunkel's *Liturgie* category, sometimes called a *Tor-Liturgie* or "gate-liturgy"). It is a ritual text — a script for an actual procession into the sanctuary — and its three sections each correspond to a moment in that procession.

- **vv. 1–2**: A cosmic confession. The earth and everything in it are YHWH's; he founded it on the seas. Likely sung as the procession is forming.
- **vv. 3–6**: The entrance question and answer (the *torah liturgy*). Who is qualified to ascend the holy hill? The one with clean hands and pure heart. Compare Psalm 15, which is the closest parallel — both psalms ask the entrance question and give an ethical answer. Both probably functioned at the temple gates as part of pilgrim screening.
- **vv. 7–10**: The antiphonal climax. "Lift up your heads, O gates!" sung by one choir; "Who is this King of glory?" asked by another (perhaps from inside the sanctuary); answered by the first.

The most influential cult-functional reconstruction is Sigmund Mowinckel's. In his *Psalmenstudien II* (1922) and later work, Mowinckel argued for an annual *autumn enthronement festival* in pre-exilic Jerusalem, paralleling the Babylonian Akitu festival, in which YHWH was symbolically re-enthroned as king. Psalm 24:7–10 was, on this reading, the climactic moment when the ark of the covenant — representing YHWH's presence — was processed into the temple, and the gates were ritually addressed as if they themselves were lifting their heads to receive the King of Glory.

The Mowinckel hypothesis is influential but contested. Goldingay and Kraus are more cautious: there is no direct evidence in the Hebrew Bible for an annual enthronement festival on the Babylonian model, and the proposed evidence is largely the psalms themselves, which makes the argument circular. What is widely accepted is more modest: Psalm 24:7–10 reflects an actual procession — perhaps the bringing of the ark into Jerusalem (2 Samuel 6) or a recurring liturgical re-enactment — and the gate-language is performative, not merely metaphorical. The gates are addressed; the King is announced; the people respond.

The opening confession (vv. 1–2) draws on ANE creation imagery. The cosmos is founded *al-yamim* (עַל־יַמִּים) and *al-neharot* (עַל־נְהָרוֹת), "upon the seas" and "upon the rivers." This is the *Chaoskampf* tradition: the ordered earth is established over the chaotic waters that YHWH has subdued. Compare Psalm 93, Psalm 29, the Baal Cycle from Ugarit, the Babylonian *Enuma Elish*. The creation language sets up the rest of the psalm: the King of Glory who comes through the gates is the same one who founded the world by mastering the chaos waters.

The *torah-liturgy* section (vv. 3–6) shows the ethical seriousness of temple access in ancient Israel. The qualifications — *neki kappayim* (נְקִי כַפַּיִם, "clean of hands"), *bar levav* (בַר לֵבָב, "pure of heart"), not lifting the soul to *shav* (שָׁוְא, "falsehood/vanity"), not swearing deceitfully — are not ritual purity rules in the Levitical sense but ethical conditions. Coming into the sanctuary required not merely washing but moral integrity. This is the same theology developed by the eighth-century prophets (Isa 1, Amos 5) — God's house is not for ritual entry alone.

The climactic section names the King of Glory three ways:

- *YHWH izzuz ve-gibbor* (יְהוָה עִזּוּז וְגִבּוֹר, v. 8) — "the LORD strong and mighty"
- *YHWH gibbor milchamah* (יְהוָה גִּבּוֹר מִלְחָמָה, v. 8) — "the LORD mighty in battle"
- *YHWH tzeva'ot* (יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת, v. 10) — "the LORD of hosts/armies"

The third title — *YHWH tzeva'ot* — is the climactic name. It is YHWH as commander of the heavenly armies, the divine warrior. The ark of the covenant in Israelite tradition was associated with this title (1 Sam 4:4); the cherubim above the ark were the throne of *YHWH tzeva'ot*. The procession of the ark, real or commemorated, was the procession of the divine warrior into his sanctuary.

Key Hebrew terms:

- *melekh ha-kavod* (מֶלֶךְ הַכָּבוֹד, vv. 7, 8, 9, 10) — "King of Glory"
- *YHWH tzeva'ot* (יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת, v. 10) — "the LORD of hosts/armies"
- *neki kappayim u-var levav* (נְקִי כַפַּיִם וּבַר־לֵבָב, v. 4) — "clean hands and pure heart"
- *pitchey olam* (פִּתְחֵי עוֹלָם, vv. 7, 9) — "ancient/everlasting doors"
- *har YHWH* (הַר־יְהוָה, v. 3) — "hill of the LORD," i.e., Mount Zion

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The structure is tripartite, with the three sections clearly demarcated by content and probably by ancient liturgical performance.

- **Cosmic frame** (vv. 1–2): hymnic-confessional
- **Entrance liturgy** (vv. 3–6): question-and-answer (the *torah liturgy* form), closing with *selah*
- **Procession dialogue** (vv. 7–10): antiphonal call-and-response, closing with *selah*

The two *selah* markers segment the psalm liturgically. The repetition between vv. 7–8 and vv. 9–10 — almost identical lines, with only minor variation — is not a copying error but the form of antiphonal performance: the call repeats; the answer escalates from "the LORD strong and mighty" to "the LORD of hosts." The doubling is the form *being* the function.

Parallelism is dense throughout. Berlin and Alter's "seconding" pattern controls vv. 1–2: "the earth is the LORD's and the fullness thereof / the world and those who dwell therein." First the spatial reality, then its inhabitants. V. 4: "clean hands and pure heart" — exterior and interior; "does not lift up his soul to what is false / does not swear deceitfully" — interior orientation and exterior speech. The four conditions of v. 4 cover act, motive, idolatry, and speech — a full ethical anthropology in two lines.

The literary device of *direct address to inanimate objects* — "Lift up your heads, O gates! Be lifted up, O ancient doors!" — is one of the psalm's most striking features. The gates are personified; they are commanded to receive the King. This is performative liturgy; the address makes the moment. The "ancient doors" (*pitchey olam*) are likely either the gates of the Jerusalem temple (which would have felt ancient to the worshipper) or, in some readings, the cosmic gates of the heavenly sanctuary that the earthly temple represents.

Westermann's structural analysis groups Psalm 24 with the *liturgical psalms* (rather than pure hymns or laments) — texts whose form is determined by their cultic use and which only make full sense when performed.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Book I placement

Psalm 24 closes a small triad with Psalms 22 and 23 in Book I. The editorial sequence — desolation (22), pastoral trust (23), temple entrance (24) — has been read by Christians as a Friday-Saturday-Sunday pattern, by others as a movement from suffering through care to arrival at the sanctuary. Whether the Book I editors intended this typological shape is debated; the texts produce the effect.

### Jewish Trajectory

Psalm 24 is preserved in Dead Sea Scroll fragments. Its consonantal text matches the MT.

The most important Jewish liturgical use is Psalm 24's designation as the *Shir shel Yom*, the Psalm of the Day, for **Sunday** (the first day of the week) in the daily synagogue liturgy. This designation goes back at least to the Mishnah, *Tamid* 7:4, which records the Levites' daily psalms in the Second Temple. Psalm 24 was sung on Sunday because, the rabbis reasoned, on the first day of creation God founded the earth — and Psalm 24 begins with the earth's foundation. The Sunday-Psalm assignment has remained constant in Jewish practice from the Mishnah through contemporary liturgy.

In Ashkenazi and Sephardi practice, Psalm 24 is also recited during the *Torah-removal liturgy* — when the Torah scroll is taken out of the ark to be read, on certain holidays. The connection is liturgical-theological: as the Torah is processed, the congregation sings the psalm whose own subject is processional entrance.

Rabbinic interpretation in *Midrash Tehillim* connects Psalm 24:7 ("Lift up your heads, O gates!") with several scenes — Solomon at the dedication of the temple (1 Kings 8), where the ark was brought in and the gates of the temple were said in midrashic tradition not to open until Solomon prayed; Moses at Sinai; the eschatological gates of the messianic age. The midrashic creativity around the "ancient doors" is rich.

Rashi reads the entrance liturgy historically (David's preparation for bringing the ark to Jerusalem) and eschatologically (the future temple). Ibn Ezra preserves the literal-historical frame. Radak holds both registers. Modern Jewish scholarship — Buber on the dialogical structure, Levenson on the temple as cosmic mountain, Sarna on the *Sitz im Leben* — has continued to develop the psalm.

### Christian Trajectory

Augustine's *Enarrationes* on Psalm 24 (Vulgate Ps 23) reads it Christologically and typologically. The "King of Glory" is Christ; the gates are sometimes the gates of heaven that lift their heads to receive the ascending Christ (an exegesis that became foundational for the Christian doctrine of Christ's ascension), sometimes the gates of hell that yield to the descending Christ in the harrowing of hell, sometimes the gates of the soul that are commanded to open to receive Christ. This multivalent typology became the patristic standard. Athanasius reads the gates as the gates of heaven at the ascension. Cassiodorus elaborates further.

Aquinas systematizes the Christological reading. Luther's lectures preserve the typology while attending to the literal-Davidic frame. Calvin in 1557 emphasizes the literal cultic-historical setting first, then allows the Christological extension.

Modern critical commentary — Gunkel, Mowinckel (with the contested enthronement-festival hypothesis), Westermann, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate, Patrick Miller, William Brown, Beth Tanner, Susan Gillingham — has largely focused on the cult-liturgical reading of the original setting. Brueggemann reads the psalm as a confession of YHWH's *kingship over creation*, with the entrance liturgy as the ethical correlate of that kingship.

In Christian liturgical use, Psalm 24 has been associated with several feasts:

- **Christmas Eve and the Christmas season**: "Lift up your heads, O gates!" as the announcement of Christ's coming into the world.
- **Palm Sunday**: Christ's entry into Jerusalem; the gates lifting their heads.
- **Ascension Day**: Christ ascending into heaven, the heavenly gates lifting their heads — the patristic-Augustinian reading.
- **Advent**: anticipatory use, the King who is coming.

The medieval iconography of Christ at the gates of hell or at the gates of heaven — the Anastasis icon in the Eastern tradition, the harrowing of hell in Western art — is shaped by Psalm 24 mediated through patristic exegesis.

### NT Use

Psalm 24:1 — "the earth is the LORD's, and the fullness thereof" — is quoted by Paul in **1 Corinthians 10:26** as the warrant for Christians eating food without scrupulous origin-checking: since the earth is the LORD's, food sold in the marketplace can be received with thanksgiving. This is the most explicit New Testament citation of Psalm 24.

The "King of Glory" language echoes through New Testament Christology. **1 Corinthians 2:8** calls Christ "the Lord of glory." **James 2:1** speaks of "our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory." The Christological reading of *melekh ha-kavod* as Christ is patristic but draws on these New Testament echoes.

The Acts uses of Psalm 24 are less direct — Acts 2 and 7 quote other psalms more explicitly — but the gates-of-the-temple imagery in Acts 3 (Peter and John at the gate called Beautiful) carries an echo.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions deserve careful naming.

**Tension 1: The Mowinckel enthronement-festival hypothesis.** Sigmund Mowinckel's reconstruction of an annual autumn festival in which YHWH was re-enthroned, with Psalm 24:7–10 as its climactic moment, has shaped twentieth-century psalm scholarship more than any other single proposal. But the evidence is contested.

Three positions:

1. **Strong Mowinckel**. The festival existed; the psalms reflect it; Israel's calendar paralleled the Babylonian Akitu in structure if not content. *Steelman*: the cult-liturgical features of Pss 24, 47, 93, 96–99 are real; some festival context is the most plausible setting; the parallels with Akitu are too suggestive to ignore.

2. **Weak Mowinckel**. The psalms reflect *some* cult-procession settings, but no specific autumn enthronement festival can be reconstructed; the proposal over-extends the evidence. Goldingay, Kraus, many recent commentators. *Steelman*: takes the cult-liturgical setting seriously but refuses to reconstruct beyond the evidence.

3. **Post-Mowinckel skepticism**. The psalms are best read theologically and literarily, with cult-historical reconstruction set aside as too speculative. *Steelman*: protects against circular argument; lets the psalms speak as literature.

**Where this leaves us**: Position 2 is the contemporary scholarly center of gravity. Some procession is presupposed by the psalm; whether it was an annual festival, a one-time event commemorated (the ark's coming to Zion), or both, is open. The Mowinckel hypothesis was a productive overreach.

**Tension 2: Original temple-procession reading vs. typological-Christological reading.** The patristic tradition read "lift up your heads, O gates" as Christ ascending into heaven. The historical-critical tradition reads it as the ark or the divine warrior entering the Jerusalem temple. These are not the same.

Three positions:

1. **Original-only**. The psalm means what it meant in pre-exilic Judah; Christological readings are eisegesis. *Steelman*: respects the integrity of the Hebrew Bible; refuses to flatten Israel's worship into Christian prefiguration.

2. **Typological-only**. The Christian church's reading is the truth toward which the original was groping; the original setting is interesting but secondary. *Steelman*: takes seriously the church's claim that Christ is the King of Glory; refuses to leave the psalm in a museum.

3. **Two readings, two integrities**. The original cult-liturgical reading is the psalm's first horizon. The Christological-typological reading is what the church has heard in it for two thousand years; that hearing is real but it does not erase the original. The two readings are in dialogue, not competition. Brueggemann, Hays, Levenson (from the Jewish side) all approach this synthesis. *Steelman*: keeps the historical and the theological in conversation.

**Where this leaves us**: Position 3, with honesty. The original setting is the cult; the Christological reading is the church's tradition; both can be true at different levels of the text's reception. What is wrong is to pretend the patristic reading is the original meaning, or to pretend the church's two-thousand-year hearing has no claim on the text.

**Closing question**: When the gates lift their heads, is it the temple gates of Jerusalem in 1000 B.C., the gates of heaven at the ascension, or the gates of your own life? The psalm gives liturgical permission for all three, but the order matters.

## The Thread

- **Psalm 15**: the parallel entrance liturgy. Pss 15 and 24 should be read together as twin gate-screening texts.
- **2 Samuel 6**: David bringing the ark up to Jerusalem — the most likely concrete event behind Ps 24:7–10.
- **1 Corinthians 10:26**: Paul citing Ps 24:1 to warrant freedom in food-marketplace ethics; the New Testament's most explicit Ps 24 quotation.
- **Revelation 21:25**: the New Jerusalem with gates that never shut — the eschatological transformation of the gate-imagery, where the King of Glory has entered and stays.
