# Matthew 1: The Genealogy and the Naming of Emmanuel

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Matthew 1 opens in the political shadow of two empires and one dynasty. The genealogy concludes with "the deportation to Babylon" (1:11–12, 17), the trauma of 587 BCE, and lands in the late Herodian world. **Herod the Great** ruled as Rome's client king from 37 BCE until his death in 4 BCE — the chronological window into which Matthew 1's narrative climax (Joseph's dream, Jesus's birth) must fit. Augustus had ruled the Roman world since the Battle of Actium in 31 BCE; the *Pax Augusta* was the global political condition under which Matthew 1's story unfolds. Judea had been incorporated into the Roman client-state network when Pompey took Jerusalem in 63 BCE, and Herod — an Idumean by ancestry, half-Jewish at best by the standards of his Pharisaic critics — held his throne by Antony's grant (40 BCE) and Augustus's confirmation (30 BCE). His paranoid execution of his Hasmonean wife Mariamne, three sons (Alexander, Aristobulus, Antipater), and brother-in-law Aristobulus III is recorded extensively by **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 15–17; *War* 1.431–673) and forms the psychological backdrop against which Matthew 2's massacre will be intelligible. In Matthew 1 itself, however, the political weight rests in the genealogy's structure: a Davidic dynastic claim made under a non-Davidic, non-Judean king.

The Jewish religious-political landscape was fragmented. **Pharisees** dominated synagogue life and lay piety; **Sadducees**, drawn from the priestly aristocracy, controlled the Temple and the Sanhedrin; **Essenes** (likely the Qumran community) lived in apocalyptic withdrawal; **Zealot**-style revolutionary currents were inchoate but present. **Messianic expectation** ran in multiple registers — Davidic-royal (*Pss. Sol.* 17), priestly (Qumran's two-messiah schema in 1QS 9:11), prophetic (the eschatological prophet of Deut 18), and apocalyptic son-of-man (1 Enoch's Similitudes). Matthew 1 enters this stream by asserting a precise, unambiguous claim: Davidic-royal, mediated through a non-biological adoption by Joseph, "son of David" (1:20).

**Archaeology** anchors several elements. **Herodium**, Herod's fortress-tomb 12 km south of Jerusalem, was excavated by **Ehud Netzer** from 1972 until his discovery of Herod's actual tomb in 2007 — a monumental mausoleum on the slope of the artificial mountain, with a sarcophagus deliberately smashed (likely by Jewish rebels during the First Revolt). The discovery confirms the scale and architectural ambition Josephus describes and concretizes the kind of king Matthew has in view. **Bethlehem** itself has yielded Iron Age remains (the 2012 First Temple-period bulla reading "Bethlehem" found in the City of David excavations) and the late-Herodian tomb of "Salome" near Beit Lehi. The traditional Bethlehem cave site under the Church of the Nativity was already venerated by the second century (Justin, *Dial.* 78). **Textual evidence** for Matthew is exceptionally early: **𝔓64/67** (the Magdalen / Barcelona papyrus, c. 175–225 CE) preserves Matthew 26 fragments; **Codex Vaticanus** (B, 4th c.) and **Codex Sinaiticus** (ℵ, 4th c.) furnish the foundational complete witnesses; **𝔓1** (3rd c.) preserves Matthew 1:1–9, 12, 14–20 — including the genealogy itself.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter: ***biblos geneseōs*** (βίβλος γενέσεως, 1:1) — "book of the genealogy," echoing LXX Gen 2:4 and 5:1 (a programmatic Genesis allusion); ***Christos*** (Χριστός, 1:1, 16, 17, 18) — "anointed one," the Greek equivalent of Hebrew *māšîaḥ*, which Matthew uses titularly here; ***genesis*** (γένεσις, 1:18) — "birth" or "origin," same root as 1:1, binding the genealogy to the birth narrative; ***Iēsous*** (Ἰησοῦς, 1:21) — "Jesus," Greek for *Yeshua*/*Yehoshua*, "YHWH saves," interpreted explicitly in 1:21 ("for he will save his people from their sins"); and ***Emmanouēl*** (Ἐμμανουήλ, 1:23) — "Immanuel," Hebrew *ʿimmānû ʾēl*, "God with us." The word ***parthenos*** (παρθένος) in 1:23, quoting LXX Isa 7:14, is the contested term to which we will return.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Matthew belongs to the genre **Greco-Roman bios** (per **Richard Burridge**, *What Are the Gospels?*, 1992) — a focused biographical narrative shaped around the protagonist's identity, deeds, sayings, and death — though some scholars (Adela Yarbro Collins, David Aune) emphasize Hellenistic-Jewish historiographical features. Most decisively, however, Matthew is a **Jewish-Christian Gospel reflecting a Matthean community** in conflict and self-definition: **Anthony Saldarini**'s *Matthew's Christian-Jewish Community* (1994) argues that Matthew's group is still inside Judaism, polemicizing against rival Jewish leadership rather than from the outside; **Daniel Harrington**'s *Sacra Pagina Matthew* (1991) treats Matthew as engaged with formative rabbinic Judaism at Yavneh post-70; **W.D. Davies and Dale Allison**'s *ICC Matthew* (3 vols., 1988–1997) weighs the evidence carefully and concludes for a Syrian (likely Antiochene) provenance with strong continuity to Jewish scribal practice. **Krister Stendahl**'s *The School of St. Matthew* (1954) proposed a Matthean "school" engaged in midrashic-pesher exegesis — controversial in detail but enduring in its insight that Matthew's OT use is technically sophisticated.

The chapter divides cleanly: the **genealogy** (1:1–17) and the **annunciation-and-naming** (1:18–25). The genealogy is a Hebraic *toledot* of the kind found in Gen 5, 11, 36, and 1 Chron 1–9, but with a programmatic title — "book of the *genesis*" — that signals canonical scope. The **three sets of fourteen** (1:17) is the literary structure: Abraham → David, David → Babylon, Babylon → Christ. The number 14 is widely interpreted as **gematria** for David (Hebrew DWD, ד-ו-ד = 4+6+4 = 14); some scholars (Goulder, Gundry) press this further to 6×7 = 42 generations leading to a seventh seven inaugurated by Christ. The genealogy is **artificially compressed** (omitting Ahaziah, Joash, and Amaziah between Joram and Uzziah; comparing 1 Chron 3) — a literary, not chronological, construction. **Brown** (*Birth of the Messiah*, 1977/1993) and **Davies/Allison** treat the omissions as intentional and theological.

Four named women appear: **Tamar** (1:3; Gen 38), **Rahab** (1:5; Josh 2, 6), **Ruth** (1:5; Ruth), and **the wife of Uriah** (1:6; 2 Sam 11–12) — Bathsheba named only by her husband. Mary is the fifth woman (1:16). The naming pattern is striking and contested. Older interpretation (Jerome, Aquinas) read all four as Gentiles foreshadowing Gentile inclusion; older Protestant interpretation read them as sinners foreshadowing the salvation of sinners (an awkward fit for Ruth and Bathsheba); **Brown** argues the common factor is **irregular sexual circumstance plus divine providential incorporation into the line** — a precise foreshadowing of Mary. Feminist readings (**Elaine Wainwright**, *Toward a Feminist Critical Reading of the Gospel of Matthew*, 1991; **Amy-Jill Levine**, *Social and Ethical Dimensions*; **Schüssler Fiorenza**, *In Memory of Her*) read these women as recovering subaltern agency; **Levine**'s *Jewish Annotated NT* note on 1:3–6 emphasizes their refusal to pass over Bathsheba's anonymity as an exegetical challenge.

The **annunciation to Joseph** (1:18–25) is structured as a **dream-visitation** type-scene paralleling Joseph the patriarch (Gen 37, 40–41), Daniel, and the rabbinic *bat qol*. The five-fold formula — angel announces, son named, mission stated, OT fulfilled, response — recurs in Matthew 2:13–15, 19–21, 22–23. The **first fulfillment formula citation** — *hina plērōthē to rhēthen hypo kyriou dia tou prophētou legontos* (ἵνα πληρωθῇ τὸ ῥηθὲν ὑπὸ κυρίου διὰ τοῦ προφήτου λέγοντος, 1:22) — establishes the pattern that will recur ten times in Matthew. **Richard Hays**'s *Echoes of Scripture in the Gospels* (2016) reads Matthew's citations as functioning more by *figural* than predictive logic; **Davies/Allison** parse the citation formulas philologically; **W.G. Kümmel**'s *Promise and Fulfillment* (1957) provides the older redemption-historical frame.

Textual variants of note: 1:16's "Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born" has variant readings in some Old Latin and Sinaitic Syriac witnesses (sys reads "Joseph, to whom was betrothed Mary the virgin, begot Jesus") that have provoked centuries of debate, but the critical text is secure.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish interpretive trajectories.** Rabbinic literature gives sparse but pointed engagement with the figure of Yeshu. The **Toledot Yeshu** — a medieval Jewish counter-gospel circulating from at least the 9th century but drawing on earlier traditions — narrates the genealogy of Jesus polemically as the illegitimate son of Mary and a Roman soldier named Pandera/Panthera (a name that echoes a possible misreading of *parthenos*). **Peter Schäfer**'s *Jesus in the Talmud* (2007) and **Daniel Boyarin**'s *The Jewish Gospels* (2012) trace the ways Jewish reception read Matthew's birth narrative as polemically disqualifying or alternatively recoverable. Modern Jewish-Jesus scholarship — **E.P. Sanders** (*Jesus and Judaism*, 1985), **Geza Vermes** (*Jesus the Jew*, 1973), **David Flusser** (*Jesus*, 1968/1997), **Amy-Jill Levine** (*The Misunderstood Jew*, 2006), and the *Jewish Annotated New Testament* (Levine/Brettler, 2nd ed. 2017) — generally treats the genealogy as a Jewish-Christian construction making Davidic-messianic claims legible to a Jewish audience, while bracketing the virginal conception as a separate (and to most modern Jewish readers, unrecoverable) Christological claim. **Boyarin**'s *Jewish Gospels* argues that the divine-Messiah expectation was not foreign to Second Temple Judaism, opening a path for re-reading Matthew 1's high Christology as *intra-Jewish* rather than *post-Jewish*.

**Christian interpretive trajectories.** Patristic engagement with Matthew 1 is dense. **Origen**'s *Commentary on Matthew* (preserved fragmentarily) treats the genealogy allegorically and the four women as types of Christ's union with sinners. **John Chrysostom**'s *90 Homilies on Matthew* (the most extensive patristic treatment of the Gospel, c. 390 CE) gives multiple homilies to chapter 1: Hom. 2–4 on the genealogy stress the women as foreshadowings of Christ's mercy, and Hom. 4–5 on Joseph's dream emphasize Joseph's "philosophy" (his self-restraint, *epieikeia*). **Jerome**'s *Commentary on Matthew* (398 CE) defends the perpetual virginity of Mary against Helvidius and reads the four women as Gentile-types (Tamar Canaanite, Rahab Canaanite, Ruth Moabite, Bathsheba "of the Hittites" via Uriah). **Hilary of Poitiers**'s *On Matthew* (c. 353 CE) reads the three fourteens as economy-of-salvation epochs. **Theodore of Mopsuestia**, **Theophylact**, and **Augustine** (*De Consensu Evangelistarum*, esp. Book 2) handle the harmonization with Luke's genealogy — which differs sharply — through various theories (Joseph's biological vs. legal father; Levirate marriage; Joseph's vs. Mary's line). **Aquinas**'s *Catena Aurea* on Matthew gathers the patristic stream comprehensively, and his *Lectura super Matthaeum* gives Thomistic systematic treatment.

The Reformation engaged the chapter sharply on the virginity question. **Calvin**'s *Harmony of the Gospels* (1555) defends *parthenos* as required by the angelic revelation but reads "until" in 1:25 as not necessarily implying subsequent marital relations (his Mariology is more open than later Reformed standards). **Luther**'s 1521 *Magnificat* and his lectures on Matthew defend the perpetual virginity and read 1:25 as Hebraistic idiom (cf. 2 Sam 6:23). 20th-century engagement: **Karl Barth**'s *Church Dogmatics* I/2 §15 ("The Miracle of Christmas") treats the virgin birth as a sign of God's free initiative in the incarnation — not a metaphysical proof but a confessional marker. **Bonhoeffer**'s Christology lectures emphasize the descent into hiddenness. **Bultmann** treats the birth narrative as mythological (in the technical *Religionsgeschichtliche* sense) and theologically dispensable. **Raymond Brown**'s *The Birth of the Messiah* (1977/1993) is the most balanced critical Catholic engagement — defending the substantive Christological claim while exegeting the narrative historically.

Contemporary commentary: **Davies/Allison** *ICC* (vol. 1, 1988); **Ulrich Luz** *Hermeneia Matthew* (vol. 1, 2007 ET) — uniquely strong on reception history (*Wirkungsgeschichte*); **R.T. France** *NICNT* (2007); **Craig Keener** *Commentary on Matthew* (1999, also in NICNT format 2009); **Daniel Harrington** *Sacra Pagina*; **Donald Hagner** *WBC*; **John Nolland** *NIGTC*; **Michael Wilkins** *NIV Application Commentary*; **N.T. Wright** *Matthew for Everyone*; **D.A. Carson** in *EBC*. Literary readings: **Jack Dean Kingsbury**'s *Matthew as Story* (1986) treats 1:1 as the Gospel's narrative title and the genealogy as the "exposition" of the plot. Postcolonial: **Warren Carter**'s *Matthew and the Margins* (2000) and *Matthew and Empire* (2001) read the genealogy as a counter-imperial Davidic claim subverting Augustan-Herodian authority. Feminist: **Wainwright** and **Schüssler Fiorenza** as cited.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Davidic legitimacy and irregular incorporation.** Matthew 1 makes a maximal Davidic claim ("son of David," 1:1, 20) while routing the line through four women whose stories involve sexual irregularity, Gentile origin, or both — and then through a fifth woman whose pregnancy threatens Joseph with public shame. The tension is acute because dynastic legitimacy in the ancient Mediterranean depended on patrilineal purity, and Davidic messianism in 4QFlor and *Pss. Sol.* 17 imagined a king of cleanly Israelite lineage.

The **Gentile-prefiguration position** (Jerome, Chrysostom, Aquinas, much patristic tradition) argues that the four women anticipate the inclusion of the nations — a programmatic announcement at the Gospel's very threshold that the Davidic king will be king over more than ethnic Israel. The reading is supported by Matthew's later universalizing trajectory (8:11; 28:19) but stumbles on Bathsheba, who is Israelite (the Hittite predicate attaches to Uriah, not to her).

The **sin-foreshadowing position** (older Protestant interpretation; some patristic strands) reads the women as "sinners" demonstrating that Christ's line passes through and redeems sin. This collapses the moral diversity of the four cases (Ruth is presented unambiguously positively in her own book; Tamar is vindicated by Judah; Rahab is praised in Heb 11) and projects later moral judgments onto narratives that resist them.

The **irregular-incorporation position** (Brown, Davies/Allison, Luz) reads the common factor as **divine providential override of social-legal irregularity** — each woman's story turns on an irregularity (Tamar's ruse, Rahab's profession, Ruth's foreignness and her threshing-floor approach to Boaz, Bathsheba's adultery and Uriah's death) that becomes the means by which the line continues. Mary's pregnancy is the climactic case: the Davidic line passes to Jesus through Joseph's *adoption* (his naming of the child, 1:25, is the legal act of paternity), an irregularity that Joseph is told by the angel to receive rather than reject. **The steelman of all three positions converges**: the genealogy is asserting that Davidic kingship is fulfilled not through the *purity* of the royal line but through God's freedom to work through and beyond it.

**Where this leaves us.** The genealogy is doing something more theologically aggressive than a dynastic credential. It is making the dynastic claim *while* exposing the dynasty's irregularity, and it is doing so in a way that prepares for a non-biological mode of Davidic sonship. Joseph, "son of David" (1:20), receives Jesus into the Davidic line by an act of obedient adoption. Davidic messiahship in Matthew is therefore not biological but legal-and-theological: God's choosing, named and ratified by a faithful man.

**Tension 2: The translation of Isaiah 7:14 — *almah* / *parthenos* / virgin birth.** Matthew 1:23 quotes LXX Isa 7:14: ἰδοὺ ἡ παρθένος ἐν γαστρὶ ἕξει, "behold, the virgin shall conceive." The Hebrew of Isa 7:14 reads ʿalmah (עַלְמָה), "young woman of marriageable age" — a term that does not lexically specify virginity (Hebrew has *bĕtûlâ* for that). The LXX translators (3rd–2nd c. BCE) chose *parthenos*, which in Greek does carry virginity connotations more strongly. Matthew quotes the LXX. The original Isaiah context is Ahaz's reign during the Syro-Ephraimite war (c. 735 BCE) — the sign-child is for Ahaz's day, his birth a chronological marker for Damascus's and Samaria's fall.

The **traditional Christian position** (patristic consensus through Aquinas; Catholic *de fide*; classical Reformed and most orthodox Protestant traditions) reads Matthew's quotation as recognizing in Mary the typological-prophetic fulfillment of a sign that operates on two levels: an immediate Ahaz-era sign and an eschatological messianic sign. The virgin birth itself is defended on Matthew 1:18, 20, 25 (the narrative claim, not just the citation) as a substantive historical-theological fact about how the incarnation occurred. **Karl Barth**'s formulation in *CD* I/2 — the virgin birth as a *sign* of God's free initiative, not a biological proof of divinity — is the most theologically careful modern defense.

The **historical-critical revisionist position** (Bultmann, Lüdemann, Vermes; *Jewish Annotated NT* on this passage) holds that the Hebrew *almah* does not mean "virgin," that Matthew's appeal to LXX Isa 7:14 is exegetically strained, and that the virgin-birth tradition is a Hellenistic-Christian theologoumenon (compare divine-conception myths around Augustus, Romulus, Plato). On this reading, Matthew 1's claim is religiously meaningful but historically constructed.

The **mediating position** (Brown, Davies/Allison, Hagner) holds that the historical question of Mary's biological state is not settleable by exegesis of *almah* alone — Matthew's claim in 1:18 ("found to be with child from the Holy Spirit") is a substantive narrative claim, not just a derivation from LXX Isa 7:14. The citation is *typological* (Hays's term: figural), not predictive in the wooden sense; the substantive claim about Mary stands or falls on the narrative tradition's reliability, not on the lexical scope of *almah*. Brown's careful conclusion in *The Birth of the Messiah* is that the virginal conception is inseparable from the canonical-confessional shape of the tradition while the historical-critical evidence is inconclusive in either direction.

**Where this leaves us.** Matthew is not arguing from Isaiah 7:14 that Mary must have been a virgin. He is reading Mary's already-given pregnancy *back into* Isaiah 7:14, finding in the LXX's word *parthenos* a textual confirmation of a narrative he has independently received. The hermeneutical logic is figural — *what God did at the Syro-Ephraimite crisis prefigures what God is doing now* — not predictive in the mechanical sense. This shifts the apologetic stakes: the credibility of the virginal conception rests on the credibility of Matthew's narrative tradition, not on the lexical range of *almah*.

**Unresolved question.** Why does Matthew name **Bathsheba** only as "the wife of Uriah" (1:6)? The other three women are named directly. Brown suggests the phrasing keeps Uriah's wronging in view; Levine reads it as a refusal to name the woman in her own right; Davies/Allison note the parallel to 2 Sam 12:10 ("the wife of Uriah") as a deliberate scriptural echo. The choice is theologically loaded and exegetically unresolved.

## The Thread

The genealogy reaches back to **Genesis 12:1–3** — the Abrahamic covenant, the promise that "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" — and to **2 Samuel 7:12–16** — the Davidic covenant, the promise of an everlasting throne. Matthew's title (1:1) names both. The genealogy reaches forward to **Matthew 28:18–20** ("**all authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me… and behold, I am with you always**") — the Davidic king claiming universal authority and the Emmanuel promise (1:23, "God with us") closed by the inclusio "I am with you always" (28:20). The Joseph-dream cycle anticipates **Matthew 2:13, 19–22** (more dreams) and resonates with **Genesis 37–50** (the patriarch Joseph, also a dreamer, also taking his family to Egypt). Flag forward: **Matthew 22:41–46** (Jesus's question about David's son and David's lord) will revisit the Davidic-genealogical claim and reframe it Christologically.
