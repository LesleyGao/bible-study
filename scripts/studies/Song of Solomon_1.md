# Song of Solomon 1 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The book opens with what is, by any reckoning, one of the most arresting titles in Hebrew scripture: *shir ha-shirim asher li-Shlomoh* (שִׁיר הַשִּׁירִים אֲשֶׁר לִשְׁלֹמֹה), "The Song of Songs, which is Solomon's." The Hebrew construction *shir ha-shirim* is a superlative — the song of songs, the supreme song, in the same grammatical mode as *qodesh ha-qodashim* (the holy of holies) or *melech ha-melachim* (king of kings). The book announces itself, with the very first breath, as superlative poetry. The phrase *asher li-Shlomoh* is grammatically slippery — it can mean "by Solomon," "for Solomon," "about Solomon," or "in the tradition of Solomon." This ambiguity is the seed of an authorship debate that has continued for two millennia.

The traditional view, following the surface reading, attributes the Song to Solomon himself, the tenth-century BC king to whom 1 Kings 4:32 ascribes 1,005 songs. But mainstream-critical scholarship has long abandoned strict Solomonic authorship. Marvin Pope's monumental Anchor Bible commentary, Roland Murphy's Hermeneia volume, Cheryl Exum's Old Testament Library commentary, and Robert Alter's poetic-literary readings all argue for an anonymous, possibly post-exilic compilation of Hebrew love poetry — likely edited into its final form during the Persian or early Hellenistic period. The evidence is largely linguistic: the Song contains a Persian loanword (*pardes*, "garden/orchard," in 4:13) and what some scholars identify as a Greek loanword (*appiryon*, "palanquin," in 3:9). These late lexical items would be impossible in a tenth-century document. Tremper Longman occupies a middle position, suggesting an early monarchic core with later editing. The Solomonic attribution, on the late-dating view, is honorific — a way of placing love-poetry under the patronage of Israel's most famously amorous king (Solomon's seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines, per 1 Kings 11:3).

The genre of the Song belongs to the broader ancient Near Eastern tradition of love poetry. The closest extant parallels are the **Egyptian love songs** preserved in the Chester Beatty Papyri (c. 1300 BC) and the Cairo Love Songs — collections of poems in which young lovers address each other directly, praise each other's bodies, and meet in gardens. Othmar Keel's Continental Commentary on the Song laid the modern groundwork for reading it against this Egyptian backdrop, and Michael Fox's *The Song of Songs and the Ancient Egyptian Love Songs* remains the standard comparative study. Mesopotamian sacred-marriage liturgies featuring Inanna and Dumuzi — where the divine couple's lovemaking blesses the land — provide a more cultic parallel, though most scholars now reject the older view (championed by T. J. Meek) that the Song itself originated as cultic liturgy. There is no temple, no priest, no sacrifice in the poem, and notably no musical notation (no *neginot*) — this is not, in its surface, a temple-cult song.

The Hebrew vocabulary of chapter 1 establishes the cast and the texture. *Dodi* (דּוֹדִי, "my beloved" — masculine) is the woman's recurring term for the man, appearing first in 1:13. *Ra'yati* (רַעְיָתִי, "my love," "my companion" — feminine) is the man's term for her, first in 1:9. *Yafah* (יָפָה, "beautiful") rings through the chapter — *hineni yafah* in 1:15, the man's twice-spoken declaration. The chapter's opening verb is the famous *yashqeni mi-neshiqot pihu* (יִשָּׁקֵנִי מִנְּשִׁיקוֹת פִּיהוּ), "let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth" — an emphatic Hebrew construction (cognate accusative) that Robert Alter renders as "let him smother me with kisses."

The material world of the chapter is a catalogue of the international luxury trade. Nard (*nerd*, 1:12) is spikenard from the Himalayas. Myrrh (*mor*, 1:13) comes from Arabia. Henna (*kofer*, 1:14) — used for body decoration — grows in oases like Engedi on the Dead Sea. The "tents of Kedar" (1:5) reference the black goat-hair tents of an Arabian tribe, and the curtains of Solomon evoke royal pavilions. Vineyards, gardens, fragrances, and the gazelle-and-dove iconography that runs through the entire book — these are the sensory lexicon of the ancient Near Eastern love poem.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The Song is dialogic poetry, and chapter 1 establishes its formal structure. Three voices speak: a woman (the dominant voice of the entire book, called the *Shulammite* in 6:13), a man (the *dod*, the beloved), and a chorus identified as "the daughters of Jerusalem" or simply "others." Some readers detect a third character, a King-Solomon figure distinct from the rural beloved — the basis of the so-called "shepherd hypothesis" in which the Song dramatizes a love triangle between a country girl, her shepherd lover, and Solomon as romantic rival. Most modern scholars (Pope, Murphy, Exum, Longman) reject the three-character reading as overly novelistic and read the Song as an anthology of love-poems with shifting voices.

What is striking — and distinctive in ANE poetry — is that the woman speaks first, speaks most, and speaks most boldly. The chapter opens not with the man addressing his beloved but with the woman demanding kisses. She is the grammatical and erotic subject. Phyllis Trible's *God and the Rhetoric of Sexuality* (1978) made this observation foundational for feminist readings of the Song: in a patriarchal canon, the Song gives extended voice to female desire. Cheryl Exum and Renita Weems have built on this. The chapter's first 4 verses are entirely the woman's speech.

The Song belongs, formally, to the broader ANE tradition of the *waṣf* — a descriptive poem (the term is borrowed from later Arabic love poetry) that catalogues the body of the beloved. Chapter 1 contains miniature *waṣf*-like moments (the man's "your eyes are doves" in 1:15; the woman's "my beloved is a sachet of myrrh between my breasts" in 1:13), but the full *waṣf* form will emerge in chapters 4, 5, and 7. The Song's most distinctive structural feature — the threefold *adjuration refrain* (2:7, 3:5, 8:4) — is not yet sounded in chapter 1; it begins in chapter 2.

The chapter's surface incoherence — voices alternating without narration, scenes shifting without transition — has driven much of the genre debate. Is it a single drama? An anthology? A song-cycle? Robert Alter's reading in *The Art of Biblical Poetry* and *The Hebrew Bible: A Translation with Commentary* treats it as a loose anthology unified by recurring imagery and verbal echo, not by plot.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

No book in the Hebrew Bible has had a more contested canonical history. The Mishnah (*Yadayim* 3:5) preserves a debate among the second-century rabbis: did the Song "defile the hands" (a technical idiom for canonical scripture)? Some objected — its eroticism unsettled them, and there are reports of the Song being sung in taverns. Rabbi Akiva's response is one of the most extraordinary statements in rabbinic literature: *"the whole world is not as worthy as the day on which the Song of Songs was given to Israel; for all the writings are holy, but the Song of Songs is the Holy of Holies."* It was Akiva's allegorical reading — the Song as the love between Yahweh and Israel — that secured the book's place in the canon.

This canonization-by-allegory shaped the entire Jewish reception. The *Targum Shir HaShirim* (the Aramaic paraphrase) reads the Song as a salvation-history allegory, walking from the Exodus through the giving of Torah at Sinai, the building of the temple, the exile, and the messianic age. *Midrash Shir HaShirim Rabbah* layers further allegorical readings. Rashi (eleventh-century France) gave the standard medieval Jewish reading: the Song is Israel-as-bride remembering the romance of her early covenant with Yahweh and longing for restoration. Maimonides, in the *Mishneh Torah*, treats the Song as the soul's love for God — a more philosophical, individualized allegory. The *Zohar* and the broader Kabbalistic tradition mine the Song for mystical readings of the divine union of the *Shekhinah* with the transcendent God. The Song became one of the **Five Megillot** (festival scrolls) and was assigned to **Passover**, where the love-poetry was read as the celebration of the covenantal romance between Yahweh and Israel born in the Exodus.

Modern Jewish reception has split. Robert Alter's translation reads the Song as exquisite literal love-poetry, refusing the allegorical overlay. Marcia Falk's feminist translation (*The Song of Songs: A New Translation*) and Chana Bloch and Ariel Bloch's translation-with-commentary recover the woman's voice and the literal eroticism. Avivah Zornberg's readings, in the tradition of literary midrash, hold the literal and allegorical in tension.

The Christian reception followed a parallel allegorical trajectory. Origen (third century) wrote both a commentary and a series of homilies on the Song that established the foundational Christian allegory: Christ as bridegroom, the Church as bride, with a secondary reading in which the bride is the individual soul. Origen wrote that no one should approach the Song who has not first put off the passions of the flesh — the very text that celebrates eros became, in Origen's hands, a text only the spiritually mature could read. Hippolytus of Rome wrote earlier still. Gregory of Nyssa's fifteen homilies developed a sophisticated mystical theology of the soul's progress toward God. The pinnacle of medieval Christian engagement with the Song is **Bernard of Clairvaux's eighty-six sermons on the Song of Songs** (twelfth century). Bernard preached on the Song for the last eighteen years of his life; he died in 1153 having reached only the opening of chapter 3. His sermons are, with Origen's, the foundational texts of Christian mystical theology. William of St. Thierry, Bernard's friend, wrote his own commentary in the same vein.

The Catholic mystical tradition continued to mine the Song. Teresa of Avila, John of the Cross — whose *Spiritual Canticle* is itself a poem written in the form and idiom of the Song with prose commentary — and the Beguine mystic Hadewijch all read the Song as the central scriptural witness to the soul's union with God.

The Reformation modulated, but did not abandon, the allegorical reading. Luther was suspicious of speculative allegory but kept a chastened version. Calvin emphasized the bridegroom-bride image of Christ and the Church. Modern Christian scholarship has seen a vigorous recovery of the *literal-erotic* reading, often in tension with the allegorical inheritance: Phyllis Trible, Ellen Davis, Tremper Longman, Renita Weems (whose *Just a Sister Away* and Song commentary read the book through Black women's experience), and Cheryl Exum have all argued that Christian theology is impoverished when it allegorizes away the Song's celebration of human sexuality. Catholic teaching since Vatican II holds both readings in tension. The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §1611–1620 treats marriage as sacrament and draws on the bridegroom-bride trajectory. Pope John Paul II's *Theology of the Body* lectures (1979–1984) drew extensively on the Song to articulate a Catholic vision of embodied love. Christopher West has popularized this work for a broader audience.

The New Testament rarely cites the Song directly, but the bridegroom-Christology that runs through Ephesians 5:22–33, Revelation 19:7–9 and 21:2, and John 3:29 draws unmistakably on the Hebrew love-poetry tradition the Song represents.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

What is the Song *for*? At least five readings have been defended, and chapter 1 — with its kisses, fragrances, and conspicuous Solomonic attribution — sets the question.

**Position 1 (literal/erotic):** The Song is a celebration of human erotic love within marriage, a canonical "yes" to embodied desire that pushes back against shame-traditions both ancient and modern. Phyllis Trible: the Song is the Hebrew Bible's deliberate counterweight to the shame imposed on Eve in Genesis 3 — a "garden" reopened. Ellen Davis: the Song teaches readers how to desire well. Tremper Longman: the literal reading is primary; the allegorical is secondary application.

**Position 2 (Yahweh-Israel allegory):** The Song is the covenantal love-poem of Yahweh and Israel — the position of Akiva, the Targum, Rashi, and the entire Passover-liturgical tradition. The literal eroticism is the *vehicle*; the covenant-romance is the *tenor*. Without this allegory, the Song would never have been canonized.

**Position 3 (Christ-Church allegory):** The Song is the love-poem of Christ for his Church — Origen, Bernard, and the dominant Christian reading for fifteen centuries. The bride is the gathered people of God; the bridegroom is the incarnate Word.

**Position 4 (Christ-soul mystical allegory):** The Song is the love-poem of Christ for the individual soul — Bernard, Teresa of Avila, John of the Cross. The most intimate, contemplative reading.

**Position 5 (both/and):** The Song is *fully* both literal and allegorical, and the integrity of the text depends on holding these together. Robert Alter, Ellen Davis, Mark Boda, the post-Vatican-II Catholic position. The literal celebrates human love; the allegorical recognizes that human love participates in, and points toward, divine love. To collapse either pole is to flatten the text.

The chapter's most contested moment for modern readers is 1:5–6: *shechorah ani ve-na'vah* (שְׁחוֹרָה אֲנִי וְנָאוָה), "I am very dark, but lovely" — or, as many modern translators (Alter, Bloch, Falk) prefer, "I am dark *and* lovely." The Hebrew *waw*-conjunction admits both readings, and the choice carries weight. The KJV's "I am black, but comely" baked an adversative into Christian English Bibles for four centuries; modern translations are recovering the conjunctive sense. The woman explains in 1:6 that she is sun-darkened from being made to keep her brothers' vineyards. Renita Weems's African-American hermeneutic reading insists this verse must be read as a celebration of dark beauty, not a confession of inadequacy; the woman is asserting her loveliness, not apologizing for her color. Cheryl Exum and Phyllis Trible have made similar observations from feminist angles. The history of the verse's translation is itself a history of how readers' sexual and racial assumptions have shaped the text.

The deeper tension chapter 1 raises is the **interpretive-paradigm choice itself**. To read the Song literally is to encounter a young woman demanding kisses, fragrant with myrrh, lying on a green couch with her lover. To read it allegorically is to encounter the soul's longing for God, the Church's love for Christ, Israel's covenantal romance. Both Jewish (Akiva) and Christian (Origen, Bernard) traditions chose the allegorical paradigm, and the modern critical recovery of the literal has been resisted by some traditional readers as a flattening, even a profanation. But the recovery has its own theological motive: a Christianity (or Judaism) that cannot speak honestly about the body, about desire, about a woman's voice demanding kisses, has lost something the canon itself preserved.

**Where this leaves us:** Chapter 1 forces a choice that the rest of the book will only intensify. To airbrush the eroticism is to mute Akiva's "Holy of Holies" by mistaking what made it holy. To absolutize the literal is to lose the trajectory that made canonization possible in the first place — the recognition that human love, named with this much specificity and joy, is the closest analog Israel's poets could find for the love between God and his people. The both/and reading is harder than either alternative, but it is the reading the canon's history seems to have arrived at.

**Closing question:** If the woman's first words in scripture's "Holy of Holies" are *let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth*, what does it mean that the canon places this voice — confident, desiring, dark, lovely — at the center of its meditation on love?
