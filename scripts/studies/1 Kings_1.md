# 1 Kings 1: The Senile King and the Manufactured Oath

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens in the bedchamber of a dying Iron Age monarch. David is "old and advanced in years" (zaqen ba ba-yamim, זָקֵן בָּא בַּיָּמִים), a stock biblical idiom for terminal senescence (cf. Joshua 13:1, Genesis 24:1). The detail that "they covered him with clothes, but he could not get warm" is medically suggestive — chronic hypothermia in the elderly, perhaps congestive heart failure or anemia. Josephus (Antiquities 7.343) elaborates with characteristic Hellenistic embarrassment, treating the warming-girl as a doctor's prescription rather than a courtier's expedient. The historical setting is c. 970 BCE on the conventional Thiele chronology — David's reign ends, Solomon begins. The Davidic kingdom at this moment is a chiefdom transitioning into a state, with disputed scope (the maximalist David of the biblical text vs. the minimalist David of Israel Finkelstein's "Low Chronology"). The Tel Dan Stele (Biran-Naveh, 1993, 1995), with its bytdwd ("House of David"), at minimum confirms a 9th-century BCE memory of a Davidic dynasty, settling the more aggressive form of the David-as-fiction position (Davies, Lemche, Thompson).

The political situation is a succession crisis. The eldest claimants — Amnon, Absalom, and possibly Chileab — are dead or vanished from the narrative. Adonijah, son of Haggith, is now the senior surviving son and the de facto crown prince by primogeniture (compare Mesopotamian and Egyptian normal practice, though dynastic succession in the ancient Near East was often contested rather than mechanical). His coalition is the old Davidic establishment: Joab, the warlord nephew who has run David's military since the Hebron years; Abiathar, the priest descended from Eli's house at Shiloh, who fled to David at Nob (1 Sam 22). Solomon's coalition is the prophetic and Jerusalemite-priestly establishment: Nathan the prophet (the dynasty's covenant theologian per 2 Sam 7); Zadok, whose genealogy in Chronicles is Aaronide but whose origins are obscure (Cross argues for an old Jebusite priesthood at the Jerusalem sanctuary co-opted by David); Benaiah son of Jehoiada, commander of David's foreign mercenaries.

The Cherethites and Pelethites (kereti u-feleti, כְּרֵתִי וּפְלֵתִי, v. 38) are almost certainly Aegean-origin mercenaries — "Cretans" and either "Philistines" or "Pelasgians." The ethnonym Caphtor/Crete and the Sea Peoples background converge here. McCarter, Cogan, and the standard commentaries note that David's bodyguard, like the Varangian Guard of Byzantium, is foreign by design — they have no tribal loyalties in Israel and answer only to the king. That a Solomon proclamation requires the foreign guard to enforce it tells us something material about the mood among Judahite royal officials, who were in the other camp at En-rogel.

Geography matters. En-rogel (literally "the spring of the fuller" or possibly "spying spring") sits south of the City of David, at the Kidron-Hinnom confluence, traditionally identified with Bir Ayyub. Gihon (gichon, גִּיחוֹן, "gushing") is the perennial spring on the eastern slope of the City of David, the city's water source — recently the focus of the Reich-Shukron excavations of the Spring Tower and Pool Tower (2005-2010), massive Middle Bronze fortifications still standing in the Iron Age. Both springs were sacrificial venues. But Gihon was the dynastic spring: it stood directly below the royal complex. Adonijah's choice of En-rogel keeps him just outside the palace's line of sight; David's choice of Gihon for Solomon's anointing is a literal acoustic counter-coronation — when the trumpet blows at Gihon, Joab can hear it from En-rogel ("What does this uproar in the city mean?" v. 41).

Hebrew terms worth tracking: hishtachavah (הִשְׁתַּחֲוָה), Bathsheba's prostration (v. 16, 31) — full proskynesis to a king, normal court protocol; nasa (נָשָׂא), Adonijah "exalted himself" (v. 5), a verb the dtr will use for Absalom-style hubris (cf. 1 Kgs 11:14, "the LORD raised up [hekim] an adversary"); ruach (רוּחַ) — not technically present here, but the absence of any "spirit of the LORD coming upon" Solomon at his anointing is notable when set against Saul (1 Sam 10:10) and David (1 Sam 16:13); melekh (מֶלֶךְ), the word repeated insistently — the chapter is monarchic vocabulary throughout, every actor positioned by relation to the throne.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: this chapter, with chapter 2, is the closing scene of the so-called Court History or Succession Narrative (2 Sam 9-20 + 1 Kgs 1-2), proposed by Leonhard Rost in 1926 as the oldest and most artistically achieved continuous narrative in the Hebrew Bible. Rost's hypothesis — that this was originally an apologetic court document defending Solomon's accession — has been refined (and partly contested) by McCarter, Whybray, Van Seters, Halpern (David's Secret Demons), Steven McKenzie. Whether or not the SN was a unified pre-dtr source, the chapter is conspicuously not in the dtr regnal-formula style. There is no "did right/evil in the eyes of the LORD" frame. There is no theological evaluation of any character. YHWH speaks not at all. The narrator is icily uncommitted.

The artistry is at the highest biblical level. Robert Alter (The David Story) and Meir Sternberg (The Poetics of Biblical Narrative) have made this chapter a showpiece of how biblical narrative withholds and discloses information. Note the gaps: did David ever actually swear that Solomon would succeed? The text never tells us. We only have Bathsheba and Nathan's claim that he did. McCarter, Brueggemann, and Halpern argue the oath is manufactured on the spot — Nathan coaches Bathsheba (vv. 11-14) precisely on what to say, then enters to "confirm" what she says, an explicit two-witness construction. The senile David, prompted, accepts the framing and re-swears it as if it had always been there ("As I swore to you... even so will I do this day," v. 30). Walter Brueggemann's reading (1 & 2 Kings, Smyth & Helwys) is that this is the Bible's most disturbing demonstration of how succession actually works — an exhausted king, two ambitious advisors, a clever mother, and a manufactured oath presented as a memory.

The structural symmetry is exquisite. Two feasts: Adonijah's at En-rogel (vv. 9-10, 25); Solomon's "uproar" at Gihon (vv. 39-40). Two messengers bring news to David: Bathsheba and Nathan (vv. 15-27). One messenger brings news to Adonijah: Jonathan ben Abiathar (vv. 42-48), and his report is a near-verbatim recapitulation of the actual coronation, retold for the doomed listeners — a virtuoso piece of dramatic irony. The chapter ends with Adonijah clinging to the horns of the altar (qarnot ha-mizbe'ach, קַרְנוֹת הַמִּזְבֵּחַ, v. 50), the asylum institution attested in Exodus 21:14 (premeditated murderers may be dragged from it; involuntary killers protected). The horns themselves are well-attested archaeologically — the four-horned altar from Tel Beersheba (Aharoni, c. 8th century BCE) is the canonical example.

Devices: dramatic irony saturates the chapter, particularly the Adonijah-Jonathan scene; type-scene reversal (David at the start of his career receives a successful anointing at Bethlehem, here at the end he authorizes one from his sickbed); chiasm in the parallel addresses of Bathsheba and Nathan, with Nathan's words structurally inverting Bathsheba's; characterization by speech (Adonijah never speaks until v. 51 — he is acted upon, never decisive; Solomon never speaks until v. 52 — pure receiver of action; Bathsheba and Nathan are the chapter's actors).

Place in the book argument: 1 Kgs 1-2 functions as both terminus to the Court History and prologue to the Solomon Source (1 Kgs 3-11). The dtr inherits this and uses it to set up Solomon's reign without himself having to evaluate the brutal politics of the accession. The dtr's voice will only fully kick in at 1 Kgs 3:3 ("Solomon loved the LORD, walking in the statutes of David his father, only he sacrificed and made offerings at the high places") and reach full editorial volume at 1 Kgs 11.

Textual variants: 1 Kings has substantial MT vs. Old Greek divergence — Adrian Schenker, Julio Trebolle Barrera, and Frank Cross have mapped the kaige-recension and proto-Lucianic strata. Chapter 1 is relatively stable, though the OG and the Antiochene text occasionally rearrange clauses. The most telling feature is that the OG retains some readings closer to a Hebrew Vorlage that preceded the proto-MT. 4QSama provides David-narrative variants but not for this chapter directly. The MT's ambiguity about the original oath is preserved in the LXX as well — neither tradition pins David down on whether the promise was real or fabricated, suggesting the gap is original.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Canonically, 1 Kings 1 stands at the seam of two arcs: the David story (concluding) and the Solomonic-Temple story (commencing). The chapter looks back to 2 Samuel 7 (the dynastic promise that Nathan now ostensibly delivers on) and forward to 1 Kings 8 (Solomon enthroned will dedicate a Temple) and 1 Kings 11 (Solomon will reproduce his father's polygamy and worse).

Jewish interpretive trajectory: Josephus (Antiquities 8.1-7) treats the chapter with strong pro-Solomon bias, smoothing Adonijah's claim and emphasizing Nathan and Bathsheba as faithful agents of David's intent. Rashi (11th century, Troyes) glosses the manufactured-oath problem by simply accepting Bathsheba's claim — David did swear, this is just the moment of activation. Radak (Kimhi) is more attentive to the political stagecraft and notes that Adonijah's failure was procedural: he held a feast presuming acclamation rather than seeking David's authorization. Ralbag (Gersonides, 14th c., Provence) reads the chapter as a study in royal counsel, with Nathan as the model courtier who works through the harem rather than against it.

Don Isaac Abarbanel (1437-1508), whose commentary on the Former Prophets is a sustained work of political philosophy under the pressure of the Iberian expulsions, takes 1 Kings 1 as a case study in succession theory. Abarbanel — who himself had served Portuguese, Spanish, and Italian kings — distinguishes designation by the previous monarch (the Solomon model) from primogeniture (the Adonijah model) and argues for the prophetic-designation model as theologically superior but politically fragile, since it requires either active prophecy or trusted prophetic counsel. His own historical situation, watching European monarchies with no prophets, makes the chapter an analysis of why Israel's politics could not survive transplantation into prophet-less contexts. Avivah Zornberg (more recent, in The Murmuring Deep and The Beginning of Desire) explores the senile-David sequence as a meditation on the body of the king failing while the office of the king must be transmitted — an early version of Kantorowicz's "two bodies." Yair Zakovitch and Moshe Garsiel work the chapter as Hebrew narrative virtuoso pieces, Zakovitch on the puns and inner-biblical exegesis, Garsiel on the name-symbolism (Adonijah, "YHWH is my Lord," vs. Solomon, "his peace" — the named Adonijah's claim YHWH does not in fact endorse).

Christian interpretive trajectory: Origen and Augustine read the chapter typologically — Solomon prefigures Christ (Augustine, City of God 17.8 makes the Davidic-Solomonic succession a figure for the eternal kingdom, since Solomon's earthly kingdom failed but pointed forward). Theodoret of Cyrus, in Quaestiones in Reges et Paralipomena, treats the chapter as moral instruction: Adonijah's hubris (he "exalted himself") becomes the type of pride that destroys claimants who do not wait on divine appointment. Ambrose (De Apologia David) and Chrysostom (Homilies on 1 Samuel) tend to focus on the Bathsheba arc rather than the succession scene. Gregory the Great's pastoral concerns — visible across the Moralia — are less relevant for this chapter. Aquinas (Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 102, on royal authority) draws on the dynastic-succession material more abstractly than text-specifically. Bernard does not engage 1 Kings 1 substantially.

Calvin's sermons on 1 Kings (delivered at Geneva 1561-62, partially extant) treat the chapter as a study in providence — God uses the human stagecraft of Bathsheba and Nathan to fulfill the dynastic promise. Calvin is unembarrassed about the politics; he is interested in how God's word stands even when human means are corrupt or compromised. Luther's lectures touch the chapter briefly, primarily for the Christological prefigurement.

Critical scholarship: Wellhausen treats Kings as a composite work, dtr-edited, with the SN as old material. Frank Cross's double-redaction model (Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic, 1973) — the Josianic Dtr1 and the exilic Dtr2 — undergirds most subsequent treatment of Kings; Richard Nelson's The Double Redaction of the Deuteronomistic History (1981) develops it. For 1 Kings 1 specifically, Mordechai Cogan (Anchor Bible, 2001) is the standard reference, with extensive engagement with cuneiform parallels for ANE succession; Marvin Sweeney (OTL, 2007) emphasizes the chapter's political theology in the dtr framework; Simon DeVries (WBC) tracks the lexical and form-critical features; Burke Long (FOTL) maps the rhetorical structures; Jerome Walsh (Berit Olam) is particularly strong on the literary architecture.

Contemporary: Walter Brueggemann's 1 & 2 Kings is the indispensable theological commentary, reading the chapter with characteristic suspicion of royal ideology. Robert Polzin's Samuel and the Deuteronomist treats the SN material as an exercise in compromised foundations. Eugene Peterson, characteristically pastoral, sees the chapter as a warning about how the kingdom of God is corrupted in transmission. Ellen Davis (Opening Israel's Scriptures) and John Goldingay (1 and 2 Kings for Everyone) provide accessible readings. Iain Provan (NIBC) offers a moderately conservative perspective. Feminist readings: Phyllis Trible's general project on biblical narrative; Tikva Frymer-Kensky on Bathsheba as agent rather than victim in this chapter (a notable inversion of her role in 2 Samuel 11); Athalya Brenner on the women of the David narrative; Cheryl Exum on the prostitution of female agency in service of patriarchal succession.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Tension One: Did David swear, and does it matter theologically? Position one (traditional Jewish and Christian, Rashi, Calvin, most pre-critical readers): David did swear; the chapter is a faithful execution of his prior covenant. Solomon's accession is divinely sanctioned because David sanctioned it. Position two (McCarter, Brueggemann, Halpern, much critical scholarship): the oath is manufactured by Nathan and Bathsheba in real time. The chapter is an apologetic narrative defending Solomon's irregular accession, but the apologetic shows its seams. Position three (Provan, Walsh, some literary readers): the question is irreducibly ambiguous and the ambiguity is theological — the Davidic dynasty is the instrument of God's purpose without itself being holy, and the ambiguity is meant to teach exactly that.

Steelman of the "manufactured" position: the structure of vv. 11-14 is too neat. Nathan tells Bathsheba precisely what to say, then enters to confirm it. The two never independently attest the oath; they orchestrate a duet. The senile David, prompted, ratifies it — and David has at this point shown no sign of remembering anything. Even his "as I swore to you" (v. 30) sounds like response to a prompt, not independent recall. The Court History's narrator never confirms the prior oath outside Bathsheba's and Nathan's mouths.

Where this leaves us: I think the text is deliberately holding both possibilities. The same narrative that gives us 2 Samuel 11 (David engineering Uriah's death) is not going to be naïve about how royal politics works. But the dtr editor lets the chapter stand because, true or fabricated, the resulting kingdom under Solomon will build the Temple — and the Temple is what the dtr cares about. The theological claim being made is darker and more honest than the pious reading: God works His promises through fully compromised political processes, and the church-and-synagogue line of inheriting Solomonic theology runs through a probably-fabricated oath. That should change how we read every dynastic-promise text.

Tension Two: Altar asylum and royal mercy (v. 50-53). Adonijah seizes the horns of the altar. Solomon — without consulting David, without consulting any priest — extends conditional clemency: "if he shows himself a worthy man... but if wickedness is found in him, he shall die." This is the new king's first recorded act, and it sets a pattern (cf. ch 2's much harsher resolution).

Catholic tradition (the right of asylum, traceable through canon law into modern church-state agreements), reads this affirmatively — even compromised mercy preserves space for repentance. Reformed Protestant readings (Calvin, Provan) treat Solomon's conditional clemency as a model of just kingship: mercy with accountability. Orthodox readings emphasize the altar as foreshadowing the Eucharistic altar where Christ's mercy meets divine justice. Jewish halakhic tradition (Rashi, Maimonides Hilchot Rotzeach) parses the Exodus 21:14 distinction — the altar protects the unintentional manslayer but not the premeditated killer. Adonijah is neither — he is a political claimant. The category-stretch is itself a comment on how royal politics outruns Torah categories. Critical scholarship (Cogan, Sweeney) reads the asylum as a political-narrative device: Adonijah's gesture acknowledges Solomon's authority, Solomon's response establishes the new king as the granter of life, not the priesthood. The cult's mercy has been absorbed into royal mercy.

Steelman of the critical position: every actor in the scene is operating on royal logic, not cultic logic. The altar functions as a political tool — Adonijah uses it to extract a public oath of safety, Solomon uses his response to perform mercy publicly. Nothing about YHWH's actual will or the cult's actual jurisdiction is engaged.

Where this leaves us: the chapter is teaching that under monarchy, even the cultic instruments of mercy become politically conditional. Solomon's "if... but if..." is the architecture of conditional grace — and it will return at chapter 2:36-46 when Shimei's conditional reprieve is revoked. The chapter's theology of mercy is brittle in a way the gospel will later have to repair.

Unresolved question: If Solomon's accession is built on a probably-fabricated oath and a deathbed coup, what does that say about every later text — including Christ's own designation as "Son of David" — that traces its legitimacy through this specific moment of transmission? Christian theology has rarely sat with this question as long as it deserves.

## The Thread

Cross-references: 2 Samuel 7 (the Davidic covenant, which Nathan now operationalizes — note that he does not in this chapter cite his own oracle, an interesting silence); 2 Samuel 11-12 (Bathsheba's first appearance; the chapter is her redemption arc, Frymer-Kensky); 1 Chronicles 22-29 (the Chronicler's wholly different telling, in which David openly designates Solomon and the politics are sanitized — the Chronicler is rewriting precisely the messiness this chapter preserves); Exodus 21:14 (altar asylum framework). Flag forward: 1 Kings 2:13-25 (Adonijah's request for Abishag, which reactivates the conflict and ends his life); 1 Kings 11 (Solomon's foreign wives and the unraveling of the kingdom whose accession the manufactured oath authorized); Matthew 1:6 (the genealogy of Christ runs through "David the king" and "Solomon by the wife of Uriah" — Matthew refuses to sanitize the line, naming Bathsheba obliquely but pointedly).
