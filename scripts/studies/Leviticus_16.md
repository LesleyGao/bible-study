# Leviticus 16: Once a Year Into the Holy of Holies

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Leviticus, *Wayyiqrāʾ* (וַיִּקְרָא) in its Hebrew title, continues directly from Exodus 40 where the glory cloud fills the tabernacle and Moses cannot enter. The book is a liturgical handbook at Sinai, and chapter 16 is its structural and theological summit. The critical dating of Leviticus remains contested: Wellhausen's 1878 *Prolegomena* placed the Priestly source (P) in the post-exilic period as a Second Temple retrojection; Menahem Haran's *Temples and Temple-Service in Ancient Israel* (1978) and Jacob Milgrom's three-volume Anchor Bible commentary (1991–2001) argue for a pre-exilic core, with chapter 16 in particular showing archaic features inconsistent with a purely post-exilic composition. Israel Knohl's *The Sanctuary of Silence* (1995) distinguishes P (cult-centered, hierarchical) from H (Holiness School, ethically inflected); Leviticus 17 opens the H stratum, which means 16 stands as P's final and grandest composition before the literary shift.

Structurally, chapter 16 is the theological pivot of the entire book: Leviticus 1–7 (sacrifices), 8–10 (ordination and the Nadab-Abihu disaster), 11–15 (purity laws on food and body), 16 (Day of Atonement), 17–26 (Holiness Code), 27 (appendix). Gordon Wenham, Michael Morales (*Who Shall Ascend the Mountain of the Lord?*, 2015), and Mary Douglas have argued that chapter 16 is the chiastic center not just of Leviticus but of the Pentateuch itself. The opening phrase — "after the death of the two sons of Aaron, when they drew near before the LORD and died" (v. 1) — explicitly links this chapter to Leviticus 10 and to the larger narrative problem: how can sinful humans approach a holy God without dying?

Material culture and ANE parallels: the Yom Kippur ritual draws on a shared Levantine ritual grammar. Hittite elimination rituals are the closest parallels. The Tunnawi Ritual (CTH 409), performed by the "Old Woman" priestess, transfers impurity to carrier animals subsequently released or destroyed. Pulisa's Ritual substitutes a live animal to carry plague or royal impurity outside settled territory. Ambazzi's Ritual uses small carrier animals (mice) for the same purpose. The Ritual Against Plague (CTH 410) similarly deploys a live animal sent away to carry collective contamination outside the community. The parallels to Israel's Azazel goat are precise: a live animal, identified as the bearer of communal impurity, is sent into uninhabited or demon-associated territory. David Wright's *The Disposal of Impurity* (1987) remains the foundational comparative study. Bernd Janowski and Hartmut Gese's *Sühne als Heilsgeschehen* (1982) treats the parallel with theological sophistication.

Mesopotamian *kuppuru* purgation rites are also important — the verb *kuppuru* ("to wipe, purge") is cognate with Hebrew *kippēr* (כִּפֵּר), the verb that governs the entire chapter. Milgrom argues that *kippēr* in Leviticus 16 is not primarily "ransom" or "cover" but "purge" — the high priest is purging the sanctuary from accumulated impurity, not primarily placating God's wrath. The theological stakes of this translation debate are considerable and will surface in Lens 4.

Egyptian purification rites and the general ANE practice of scapegoat-like elimination (Greek *pharmakos*, for instance, or Akkadian *namburbi* texts) supply the wider comparative horizon. What makes Leviticus 16 distinctive is not its ritual mechanics — they are thoroughly Levantine — but the monotheistic framing: one high priest, one day, one inaccessible inner sanctum, one living God.

Manuscript and archaeological evidence: 1Q4 (also called 1QLev-a) and 4QLeviticus fragments preserve portions of the chapter; the 11QpaleoLev scroll in paleo-Hebrew script attests the text's antiquity. The LXX renders *Azazel* in multiple ways — as "the averter" (*apopompaion*), as a sent-away goat (*eis aphesin*), and Jerome's Vulgate coined *caper emissarius* ("goat sent away"), the source of the English "scapegoat." The Temple Scroll (11Q19 xxvi–xxvii) expands Yom Kippur regulations at Qumran.

Three to five Hebrew terms worth naming: *kippēr* (כִּפֵּר, "atone/purge"); *kappōret* (כַּפֹּרֶת, "mercy seat," from the same root); *ʿăzāʾzēl* (עֲזָאזֵל, "Azazel" — the vexed term to be discussed); *ʿinnâ nepeš* (עִנָּה נֶפֶשׁ, "afflict oneself," v. 29, later read by tradition as fasting); *ḥaṭṭāʾt* (חַטָּאת, "sin offering," but more accurately "purification offering" per Milgrom).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: ritual instruction with narrative frame, of a distinct sub-type — the annual high-priestly purgation script. The chapter has a striking chiastic architecture. Wenham and Morales map it: (A) entrance warning and preparation vv. 1–5; (B) priest's own purification vv. 6, 11–14; (C) goats and lots vv. 7–10, 15–19; (B') Azazel goat and confession vv. 20–22; (A') egress, garments changed, closing statutes vv. 23–34. The two-goat rite at the center is the chapter's pulsing heart, flanked by sanctuary-blood manipulation on either side. This chiastic center-of-the-center argues for the theological weight Morales and others assign: the double-goat rite is the ritual and rhetorical climax of Leviticus.

Literary devices abound. The repeated triple formula — "uncleannesses and transgressions and sins" (*ṭumʾōt, pešaʿîm, ḥaṭṭāʾôt*, vv. 16, 21) — signals ritual completeness. The double-goat structure — one *la-YHWH* (for the LORD), one *la-ʿăzāʾzēl* (for Azazel) — is a ritual bifurcation with few parallels. The "once in the year" refrain (vv. 2, 29, 34) builds liturgical rhythm. The linen garments (*bigdê-bād*, vv. 4, 23) — not the ornate garments of glory described in Exodus 28 — mark Aaron's humility and priestly austerity on this day. This detail is theologically potent: on the day of greatest liturgical authority, the high priest wears the simplest garments.

The spatial movement is cinematic: Aaron moves from outside to altar to holy place to Holy of Holies — and back out again. He is the only Israelite permitted behind the veil, and only on this one day, and only if the preparatory rites are complete. The chapter enacts the answer to the Leviticus 10 question: Nadab and Abihu died because they drew near improperly; Aaron lives because he draws near rightly. The inclusio with Leviticus 10 makes chapter 16 a ritual response to a narrative trauma.

Textual variants: LXX handling of Azazel is the most visible variant — the translators appear uncertain and render the term variously, which some scholars read as evidence of a lost interpretive tradition. The Targum Pseudo-Jonathan expands with midrashic additions. The Samaritan Pentateuch aligns with MT. 4QLev fragments confirm the essentials.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** Second Temple reception is extensive and varied. Jubilees 34 associates the Day of Atonement with Joseph's brothers' deception of Jacob (the goat's blood on Joseph's coat), generating an etiology that links national sin with Yom Kippur. 1 Enoch 10:4–8 dramatically expands Azazel: he is the fallen angel who taught humanity forbidden arts (weapons, cosmetics, sorcery), bound in the wilderness by Raphael until judgment. Philo (*Life of Moses* 2.23–24, *Special Laws* 1.186–188) allegorizes the two goats as reason and passion. Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.240–243) summarizes the ritual. The Temple Scroll (11Q19 xxv–xxvii) expands the Yom Kippur legislation with stringencies.

Rabbinic literature devotes the entire Mishnah tractate *Yoma* (eight chapters) to Yom Kippur, with correspondingly massive gemara in the Bavli (Yoma 2a–88b). The service — the *Seder ha-Avodah* — is the choreographed sequence of the High Priest's acts, preserved liturgically in synagogue practice to this day. Sifra on Leviticus 16 is extensive. Rashi (on v. 8) says Azazel is "a hard mountain, a precipice in the wilderness" — rationalizing the name as a toponym. Ibn Ezra hints cryptically at a deeper (demonic) meaning but declines to elaborate: "the secret of Azazel I will disclose to you at the age of thirty-three" (commentary on v. 8) — an enigmatic allusion later unpacked by kabbalistic readers. Ramban (Nachmanides) takes the most provocative position: Azazel is indeed a demonic power in the wilderness, and the goat sent to it is a *token* — not worship, not sacrifice, but an acknowledgment by God's command that the wilderness has its own chaotic powers which must receive what is theirs. Maimonides (*Guide* 3.46) rationalizes: the goat sent into the wilderness is pedagogical, teaching Israel to relinquish sin by removing it from sight.

Medieval and modern Jewish readings retain Yom Kippur as the highest holy day of the liturgical year — a twenty-five-hour fast, full confession (*Vidui*), synagogue service centered on the *Avodah* liturgy that recites the High Priest's ritual as a kind of verbal re-enactment after the Temple's destruction. Abraham Joshua Heschel (*The Sabbath*) and Jonathan Sacks (*Ceremony and Celebration*) articulate Yom Kippur's enduring theology: repentance (*teshuvah*), atonement (*kapparah*), purification (*ṭohorah*). Milgrom's scholarly reading emphasizes the sanctuary-purgation logic: sin accumulates on the sanctuary's furniture (a kind of spiritual patina), and Yom Kippur is the annual cleansing of the space, not primarily the forgiveness of individual offenses (which have their own mechanisms). Knohl adds that the H school's understanding of atonement is more moralized than P's pure cultic logic. Levenson (*Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*, 1993) locates the chapter within a broader biblical pattern of the sacrificed-and-surviving son, connecting Isaac, Joseph, and the Azazel goat in a typology of vicarious deliverance.

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** Christian reception has been dense from the earliest stratum. The Epistle of Barnabas 7 (late first / early second century) is the first explicit double-goat Christology: both goats are Christ, one killed and one sent away, figuring Christ's passion and his resurrected life. Justin Martyr's *Dialogue with Trypho* 40 develops the typology. Tertullian (*Adversus Marcionem* 3.7.7) makes the two goats figure Christ's two advents — first in suffering, second in glory. Origen's *Homilies on Leviticus* 9 and 10 treat chapter 16 at length, reading the high priest's once-yearly entrance as a type of Christ's unique priestly work. Cyril of Alexandria and John Chrysostom follow.

Hebrews 9–10 supplies the decisive New Testament reading: Christ is the high priest who enters "once for all" (*ephapax*) into the heavenly Holy of Holies with his own blood. The author of Hebrews consciously echoes chapter 16 in its every motif — the veil, the blood, the annual pattern made redundant by a single perfect offering, the sanctifying effect that touches the conscience and not just the flesh. Patristic authors read this alongside the Gospel narrative of the Temple veil tearing at Christ's death (Matt 27:51).

Medieval theology (Aquinas, *ST* III q. 22) integrates Christ's priesthood into the atonement doctrine, drawing on Hebrews and chapter 16. Bernard of Clairvaux's devotional writings return repeatedly to the high priest motif. The Reformation put Leviticus 16 at the center of soteriology: Luther's lectures on Hebrews link the chapter to his doctrine of justification; Calvin's *Institutes* 2.15–16 develops Christ's threefold office of prophet, priest, and king, with the priestly office drawing heavily on the chapter. Calvin's commentary on Hebrews reads chapter 16 as the Old Testament's foundational atonement template.

Critical and contemporary Christian scholarship has wrestled with Azazel in particular. Milgrom defends a demonic reading on philological grounds (supported by 1 Enoch and Ramban): Azazel is a wilderness demon who receives the goat as a symbolic relegation of sin to its proper chaotic domain, but no worship is offered. Bernd Janowski has written the definitive monograph, *Sühne als Heilsgeschehen* (1982), treating the rite's theology with precision. Hartmut Gese's work on atonement (*Essays on Biblical Theology*, 1981) reads the chapter as the Old Testament's most developed articulation of vicarious atonement. Fleming Rutledge's *The Crucifixion* (2015) draws on chapter 16 to articulate a non-abstract atonement theology — substitution, yes, but also apocalyptic rescue. N. T. Wright (*The Day the Revolution Began*, 2016) reads Jesus' self-understanding through Yom Kippur categories. Hans Boersma's *Violence, Hospitality, and the Cross* (2004) engages the chapter in the context of atonement debates. Ephraim Radner's *Leviticus* (Brazos, 2008) offers a figural theological reading. Morales's *Who Shall Ascend the Mountain of the Lord?* (2015) argues chapter 16 is the theological climax of the entire Pentateuch.

**Where they converge and diverge.** The Christian reading of Leviticus 16 as the Old Testament's foundational atonement template — explicitly canonized in Hebrews — is central to Christian soteriology. The Jewish reading has preserved Yom Kippur as the living liturgical heart of the Jewish year, with the Temple's destruction in 70 CE rewriting atonement theology: no sacrifice possible, but *teshuvah* (repentance), *tefillah* (prayer), and *tzedakah* (charity) constitute atonement, with the Yom Kippur liturgy as calendrical anchor. Azazel interpretation splits along telling lines: patristic typology makes both goats Christological (one killed, one released = passion and resurrection); rabbinic tradition debates whether Azazel is place or demon, with Ramban's "token to the wilderness power" reading theologically audacious and often underappreciated. Tell the story honestly: both traditions read the chapter as the decisive Old Testament text on atonement, but they take it in radically different directions — one toward a single decisive event (Christ), the other toward an annually renewed communal practice (Yom Kippur). Both know that sin accumulates; both know that something must carry it away.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is the ritual primarily purgation of the sanctuary or expiation for the people?** Milgrom (Anchor Bible 3, 1991) argues decisively for sanctuary purgation: *kippēr* means "purge" (cognate to Akkadian *kuppuru*), and the blood is applied to the sanctuary furniture, not to the people. Sin is imagined as a kind of miasmic residue that adheres to holy space; if left uncleansed, it drives out the *Shekinah* (cf. Ezekiel 8–11). Knohl extends this with an H-level reading in which moral sin has cultic consequences. Against the purgation reading, classical Christian substitutionary atonement theology (Anselm, *Cur Deus Homo*; Calvin; Owen, *The Death of Death*) reads the chapter as vicarious expiation: the goats die or are sent in the people's place, bearing their sins. Catholic teaching (CCC 433, 613–618; Aquinas, *ST* III q. 22, q. 48) affirms both dimensions — the sacrifice cleanses and substitutes. Lutheran theology (Luther on Hebrews; Bonhoeffer, *Discipleship*) emphasizes substitution and the vicariousness of Christ's death under the chapter's figure. Reformed theology (Calvin, Horton, *Covenant and Salvation*) leans harder into penal substitution. Orthodox theology (Athanasius, *On the Incarnation*; Gregory of Nyssa; Gustaf Aulén's *Christus Victor*, 1931) reads the chapter in a *Christus Victor* frame — the goat sent to Azazel figures Christ's descent into and victory over the chaotic powers. Wesleyan theology emphasizes the transformative rather than merely forensic effect. Jewish rationalist tradition (Maimonides) reads the rite as pedagogical. Critical scholarship (Milgrom, Klawans, Levenson) tends toward the purgation reading.

The least intuitive position is Milgrom's, because it denies that the ritual's primary work is on the people at all. Steelman: the text itself applies blood to the mercy seat, the veil, the altar — not to people. The people "afflict themselves" but are not directly blood-sprinkled. The grammar of verse 16 is key: Aaron "shall make atonement [*wĕ-kipper*] for the Holy Place because of the uncleannesses of the people of Israel." Grammatically, the Holy Place is the direct object of the purging verb; the people's sins are the reason for the accumulated impurity. This is theologically sobering: on Milgrom's reading, holiness is fragile, sin is environmentally corrosive, and what the rite protects is the divine presence's habitability. The contemporary theological payoff is that atonement is not primarily about individual guilt removal but about maintaining the very conditions under which communion with God is possible.

**Where this leaves us:** purgation and substitution are not mutually exclusive, but Milgrom's insight disciplines substitutionary readings from becoming purely transactional. The goal is presence — God dwelling among Israel — and everything in the chapter serves that goal.

**Tension 2: What is Azazel, and what does the rite for Azazel mean theologically?** Three serious positions. (1) *Azazel is a place* — so Rashi, Ibn Ezra (in his surface comment), Targum Onkelos, and Jewish rationalist tradition. The goat is simply sent far away; the name is a toponym or a compressed description ("rugged place"). This reading protects Jewish monotheism from any demon-tribute reading. (2) *Azazel is a demonic being* — so Ramban, 1 Enoch 10, Milgrom's mature judgment, Janowski, Gese, and much contemporary scholarship. The goat is not sacrificed *to* Azazel (which would be idolatry) but rather sent to Azazel as symbolic relegation of sin to its proper chaotic domain; Israel gives the wilderness power what it is owed — the accumulated sin of the community — without worship. (3) *Azazel is a symbolic abstraction* — the "scapegoat" of Jerome's Vulgate, later developed by Christian typology and secular theory (René Girard, *Violence and the Sacred*, 1972). Azazel becomes a function rather than an entity: the place where collective guilt is deposited.

Reformed readings (Calvin on v. 8) tend toward position 1 or 3, wary of demonology. Catholic tradition (CCC 392 on the demonic; Ratzinger, *Spirit of the Liturgy*) is comfortable with position 2 as a serious pre-Christian acknowledgment of chaotic spiritual powers later defeated in Christ. Orthodox theology, with its robust angelology and demonology (Athanasius on Christ's descent; the *Christus Victor* tradition), reads position 2 naturally: Azazel is a real wilderness power over whom Christ triumphs. Jewish rationalism (Maimonides, Heschel) tends toward position 3. Patristic exegesis (Barnabas, Justin, Tertullian) uses the two-goat structure typologically — both are Christ, passion and resurrection — regardless of what Azazel originally denoted.

The least intuitive position is Ramban's — that the goat is a commanded, non-worshipful token to the wilderness power. Steelman: biblical monotheism does not require denying the reality of other spiritual powers; it requires refusing their worship. If the Hebrew Bible acknowledges chaotic powers (Tohu, Leviathan, *śĕʿîrîm*, the wilderness demons of Isaiah 13:21 and 34:14), and if those powers have domains and rights within the created order, then the Azazel rite is the ritual acknowledgment that sin — which comes from chaos — must be returned to chaos, at God's command, without worship. This reading is theologically serious because it refuses the rationalist reduction and the demonological panic alike; it treats the ancient cosmology as a resource rather than an embarrassment.

**Where this leaves us:** the Azazel debate matters because it exposes how we handle the biblical text's cosmological strangeness. A too-quick rationalism flattens the text; a too-quick demonology supernaturalizes it cheaply. The serious reading sits in between and takes the strangeness as theological data.

**Unresolved question:** If chapter 16 is, as Morales and Wenham argue, the literary and theological summit of the Pentateuch, and if Hebrews makes Christ's one-time entrance the fulfillment of what Aaron did annually, then what pastoral and liturgical work does the chapter still need to do in Christian communities whose worship has largely lost any ritual sense of sanctuary purgation — and has the post-Reformation tendency toward purely interior-psychological models of atonement cost Christians something that chapter 16 was trying to protect?

## The Thread

Four non-obvious cross-references. First, Hebrews 9:7 — "into the second [tent] only the high priest goes, and he but once a year, and not without taking blood, which he offers for himself and for the unintentional sins of the people." The Epistle to the Hebrews is the single most important reading of Leviticus 16 in Christian canon, and it works the chapter's every lever — veil, blood, priest, once-a-year, sanctuary — before declaring them all fulfilled in Christ's singular entrance. Second, Isaiah 53:4–6, "he has borne our griefs and carried our sorrows… the LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all." The verb for "laid" (*hipgiaʿ*) and the image of iniquity-bearing pick up the Azazel goat rite (where Aaron lays his hands and the goat *bears* [*nāśāʾ*] the iniquities, v. 22); Second Temple and Christian readings fuse the two figures into a single portrait of the Servant as Azazel-and-goat-of-YHWH combined. Third, Ezekiel 10 — the glory of YHWH departing the temple. Ezekiel's vision of the *Shekinah* abandoning a desecrated sanctuary confirms Milgrom's purgation theology from the negative side: when the sanctuary is not cleansed, the presence leaves. Yom Kippur exists to keep the presence present. Fourth, Matthew 27:51 / Mark 15:38 / Luke 23:45 — "the veil of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom." The synoptic tradition encodes Leviticus 16's veil — the barrier behind which Aaron alone entered — as the barrier Christ's death unbarred. The rending direction (top to bottom) signals divine initiative: the veil is not breached from below but opened from above. The annual ritual of chapter 16 becomes, in the Gospel narrative, a permanent structural change in the architecture of access.
