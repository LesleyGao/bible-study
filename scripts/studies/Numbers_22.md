# Numbers 22: The Seer and His Ass

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Numbers 22 opens on the plains of Moab, *ʿarvôt môʾāv* (עַרְבוֹת מוֹאָב), a flat alluvial plateau east of the Jordan across from Jericho. This is the staging ground where Israel will remain through the end of the book and into Deuteronomy. Archaeologically, the late Bronze/early Iron Age Transjordan was a patchwork of small kingdoms — Moab, Ammon, Edom — whose existence was long disputed by minimalist scholarship but is now firmly attested by the Mesha Stele (Moabite Stone, c. 840 BC, mentioning *kmš* Chemosh and the Omride dynasty), Iron Age fortresses at Dibon and Khirbet al-Mudayna, and Late Bronze continuity at sites like Khirbet Iskander. Kenneth Kitchen and James Hoffmeier have argued the political geography of Numbers 21-25 fits the Late Bronze/early Iron transition rather than a late Persian invention.

Balak son of Zippor (*bālāq ben-ṣippôr*, בָּלָק בֶּן־צִפּוֹר) is king of Moab. Chemosh is their chief deity; the Mesha Stele later records Chemosh's "anger" against his people in terms strikingly parallel to YHWH's anger against Israel in Deuteronomic theology. Moab's panic at "the horde" that will "lick up all that is around us, as the ox licks up the grass" (v. 4) is a characteristic ANE idiom — compare the Ugaritic language of devouring armies and Egyptian execration texts, which magically cursed foreign enemies by name inscribed on bowls and figurines then ritually smashed. The logic of Balak's commission to Balaam — "he whom you bless is blessed, and he whom you curse is cursed" (v. 6) — presumes an ANE metaphysics in which a skilled diviner could bend the divine council against an enemy by correct ritual.

The single most important datum for reading this chapter historically is the **Deir ʿAlla Inscription**, discovered in 1967 by Dutch archaeologist Henk Franken at Tell Deir ʿAlla in the Jordan Valley (ancient Succoth region), about 25 miles north of where Israel is camped in this narrative. The inscription is a plaster text, painted in red and black ink on a sanctuary wall, dated paleographically to c. 800-700 BC. Its opening line reads: *spr [bl]ʿm [br bʿ]r ʾš ḥzh ʾlhn*, "the book (or account) of Balaam son of Beor, a man who was a seer of the gods." Jo Ann Hackett's 1980 Harvard dissertation, *The Balaam Text from Deir ʿAlla*, remains the definitive critical edition; earlier editio princeps by Hoftijzer and van der Kooij (1976) established the readings. The inscription preserves a vision of cosmic reversal given to Balaam by the council of the *šdyn* — gods referred to by a name cognate to the biblical *shadday*. This extra-biblical text is as close as we get to archaeological confirmation of a biblical figure outside the royal/military record: an independent Transjordan religious tradition remembered Balaam son of Beor as a seer of international reputation. Critical scholars now broadly accept that Balaam was a genuine figure in the religious memory of the region, not a Hebrew literary invention.

Pethor (*pĕtôr*, פְּתוֹר) "near the River" (v. 5) — i.e., the Euphrates — places Balaam's home in the middle Euphrates region, plausibly identified with Pitru on the Sajur tributary, mentioned in Shalmaneser III's Assyrian annals. This is roughly 400 miles from Moab. That Balak sends a delegation that distance, with "fees for divination" (*qĕsāmîm bĕyādām*, v. 7), indicates Balaam's cross-regional renown. The technical term *qesem* (קֶסֶם) designates pagan divination generally proscribed in Deut 18:10-14; its use here, and Balaam's consultation of YHWH, situates him as a *bārû*-type diviner — the Mesopotamian extispicy specialists who read animal entrails, dream incubations, and astral signs — while simultaneously receiving genuine revelation from Israel's God.

The "angel of the LORD" (*malʾak YHWH*) standing in the way with drawn sword *kĕśîpô šĕlûpâ bĕyādô* (v. 23) employs execution imagery familiar from Joshua 5:13 and 1 Chronicles 21:16. The donkey's capacity to see what the seer does not — rabbinic and patristic readers both pounced on the reversal — draws on ANE beliefs that animals perceive the numinous; compare Aesop, but more directly the Mesopotamian omen series *Šumma ālu* which reads animal behavior as portent.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The Balaam cycle (Num 22-24) is arguably the most literarily sophisticated block in the Torah. Scholars from Wellhausen to George Buchanan Gray (ICC 1903) to Milgrom (JPS 1990) to Baruch Levine (Anchor Bible 2000) have long observed its distinctiveness. The narrative frame (ch. 22) is prose of a high comic register; the four oracles in chapters 23-24 are preserved in demonstrably archaic Hebrew poetry that Frank Moore Cross and William Albright (*Yahweh and the Gods of Canaan*, 1968) dated to pre-monarchic composition on linguistic grounds — retained case endings on nouns, the archaic energic *nun*, unusual vocabulary, the old *we-* conjunction, and prosodic features closer to Ugaritic than to classical Hebrew prose. This led to the proposal — Albright, Cross, Levine — that the oracles may derive from genuinely old Transjordan material embedded in the later narrative.

Chapter 22 itself divides cleanly into three scenes: (1) the two embassies from Balak (vv. 1-21), (2) the donkey episode (vv. 22-35), (3) the arrival at Bamoth-baal (vv. 36-41). Each scene uses threefold patterning: three requests, three blockings by the angel, three strikings of the donkey. The repetition is not authorial clumsiness but a deliberate folkloric device keyed to the triad of oracles that will follow.

The donkey pericope (vv. 22-35) deploys dramatic irony at its most pointed. Balaam, the "man whose eye is open" (as he will claim in 24:3), cannot see what his *ʾātôn* (אָתוֹן, she-donkey) perceives plainly. The Hebrew wordplay on "seeing" — *rāʾâ*, repeated seven times — and on the "way" (*derek*) the donkey keeps leaving — sets up the later oracles, where Balaam's eyes are finally "uncovered" (24:4, 16). Robert Alter's translation notes how the narrator plays Balaam against his donkey as a comic double act: the seer beats the beast who speaks truth; when his own eyes open, he falls on his face as the donkey had done. Everett Fox (Schocken Bible) renders the cadences to preserve the fairy-tale quality.

Textually, the Samaritan Pentateuch harmonizes minor details; 4QNum-b from Qumran preserves portions of this chapter with readings close to MT; the LXX of ch. 22 occasionally softens Balaam's ambiguity, a tendency that will intensify in chapters 23-24. A notable crux at v. 32 — "your way is perverse (*yāraṭ*) before me" — uses a rare verb which the LXX renders *ouk asteia* ("not fitting"); the Vulgate, *perversa est*. Milgrom and Levine both flag the ambiguity about why God's anger "burns" in v. 22 after God has just told Balaam to go in v. 20.

Genre-wise, the Balaam cycle mixes theophanic narrative, prophet-story (resembling Elijah and Elisha tales), and oracular poetry in a combination unique in the Pentateuch. Rolf Knierim and Dozeman have noted its formal affinities with later prophetic call narratives; Gordon Wenham (*Numbers*, TOTC) observed the way it functions as a diptych with the golden calf — in Exodus 32 Israel apostatizes from within, in Numbers 22-24 Israel is blessed from without.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Jewish readings

In Second Temple literature the Balaam traditions multiply almost beyond tracking. Philo's *Life of Moses* 1.263-300 treats Balaam as a wicked sorcerer whose unwilling blessings illustrate divine sovereignty over pagan magic. Josephus, *Antiquities* 4.100-158, gives the fullest ancient retelling, expanding Balak's messengers into noble delegations and treating Balaam as a tragic figure of impressive prophetic power corrupted by greed. Pseudo-Philo's *Biblical Antiquities* (LAB) 18 reworks the story as a meditation on providence. At Qumran, **4Q339 ("List of False Prophets")** names Balaam in a roster of prophetic antagonists, while — fascinatingly — **4Q175 (Testimonia)** cites Numbers 24:15-17 among its messianic proof texts. The tradition is already bifurcated in the Second Temple period: Balaam the false prophet in one stream, his oracle the messianic text *par excellence* in another.

Rabbinic literature sharpens the negative assessment. *Mishnah Sanhedrin* 10:2 lists Balaam among four commoners "who have no share in the world to come" (with Doeg, Ahithophel, Gehazi). The Babylonian Talmud (*Sanhedrin* 105a-106b) devotes extensive aggadic treatment to him: his donkey speaks in the ten things created at twilight on the eve of Sabbath; his wickedness is measured by contrast with Moses' humility; the rabbis proverb, "from the blessing of the wicked you can see what was in their heart" because the same Balaam who blessed Israel was the one who, per rabbinic tradition (based on Num 31:16), counseled the Moabite seduction at Peor in the following chapters. *Sifre Numbers* on this pericope builds an elaborate typology of prophetic greed. *Pirkei Avot* 5:19 famously contrasts "the disciples of Abraham" — "a good eye, a humble spirit, a lowly soul" — with "the disciples of Balaam" — "an evil eye, a proud spirit, a greedy soul."

Rashi (11th c.) reads the donkey's speech as a direct miracle meant to shame Balaam: the mouth of the beast opened while the mouth of the prophet was bound by divine purpose. Ibn Ezra more soberly probes the linguistics. Ramban (Nachmanides) addresses the theological puzzle of vv. 20-22 — why does God permit, then rage? — by distinguishing between bare divine permission and God's deeper desire. Maimonides, *Guide of the Perplexed* 2.45, treats Balaam in the hierarchy of prophetic ranks: the highest non-Israelite prophet, but still below the lowest degree of true Mosaic prophecy, because Balaam's visions come via dreams and sensory mediation rather than the unmediated clarity of Moses. Sforno contrasts Balaam's technique with Israelite prophecy's unsolicited character. In the modern period, Nehama Leibowitz's *Studies in Bemidbar* devotes seventy pages to Balaam as a case study in the moral economy of prophecy; Jacob Milgrom's JPS Numbers gives the most philologically careful treatment in English; Jonathan Sacks and Avivah Zornberg offer meditations on the uncanny inversion of prophet and ass.

### Christian readings

The New Testament canonizes the rabbinic negative verdict. **2 Peter 2:15-16** speaks of heretics who have "forsaken the right way and gone astray, following the way of Balaam son of Beor, who loved the wages of unrighteousness, but was rebuked for his transgression — the speechless donkey spoke with human voice and restrained the prophet's madness." **Jude 11** condemns those who "abandoned themselves for the sake of gain to Balaam's error." **Revelation 2:14** rebukes the church at Pergamum for tolerating those "who hold the teaching of Balaam, who taught Balak to put a stumbling block before the sons of Israel." The New Testament fixes three sins to Balaam: greed (*misthos adikias*), false teaching, and Peor-seduction by proxy.

Patristic exegesis ran with the donkey episode as an emblematic encounter. Origen's *Homilies on Numbers* 13-19 (preserved mainly in Rufinus's Latin) offer the most sustained early Christian treatment of Balaam, reading him as a reluctant but authentic prophet whose pagan origins show divine revelation exceeding Israel's borders while his corrupt heart illustrates the insufficiency of gift without virtue. Origen's move — taking Balaam's genuine oracles as proof of divine self-revelation to the nations — became standard. Augustine, *City of God* 10.18, treats the donkey as proof that the created order is subject to its Creator; in *Questions on the Heptateuch* he returns to the perversity of v. 22. Ambrose, *Letter* 50 and his treatise on Balaam, builds a sustained moral typology. Cyril of Alexandria's *Glaphyra* reads the blindness of Balaam against the seeing of the beast as Christological — the gentile seer groping, the humble bearer seeing.

Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 172 on prophecy, cites Balaam in distinguishing prophetic grace (*gratia gratis data*) from sanctifying grace: a true prophet can lack saving charity. Calvin's commentary on Numbers is sustained and vigorous — he treats Balaam as an archetype of the false-hearted clergy whose mouths utter truth while their hearts plot evil. Luther preached Balaam as the false prophet par excellence, warning that genuine doctrine can flow from corrupt teachers but the teacher remains under judgment.

Modern critical scholarship, as noted above, converges on Balaam's historicity after Deir ʿAlla. Contemporary commentators — Gordon Wenham (TOTC), R. Dennis Cole (NAC), Philip Budd (WBC), Ephraim Radner (Brazos), Walter Brueggemann, John Goldingay, Terence Fretheim — mostly work the seam between source-critical analysis and canonical reading, and they increasingly treat the cycle as evidence that Israelite tradition knew how to do comparative religion: it acknowledged the real revelatory capacity of non-Israelite prophets while subordinating it to YHWH's covenantal purposes.

### Convergences and divergences

Jewish and Christian traditions converge on Balaam as a morally bankrupt prophet whose words nonetheless tell truth. They converge on the comic reading of the donkey episode: animal sight shames human blindness. They diverge on the legitimacy of pagan prophecy in principle — patristic and medieval Christian exegesis more willingly affirms genuine revelation to the nations (for reasons tied to Gentile inclusion), while rabbinic thought more tightly quarantines Balaam as anomaly. And they diverge most sharply on the star of Jacob in chapter 24 — but that conversation is for the next chapter.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions.

**Tension 1: Why does God permit what God forbids?** In vv. 12-13 God tells Balaam flatly, "You shall not go with them. You shall not curse the people." In v. 20, God tells him, "If the men have come to call you, rise, go with them; but only do what I tell you." In v. 22, "God's anger was kindled because he went." The sequence appears contradictory.

*Rabbinic/classical Jewish position:* The rabbinic reading (Rashi most clearly) distinguishes permission from approval. God permits what God does not desire when the human will has hardened; this is a pedagogic permission that yields the person up to their own chosen path so that their character may be revealed. Balaam wanted to go for the gold. God said "no." Balaam asked again. God said "go." God was angry because the asking itself was evidence of greed.

*Augustinian/Reformed position:* Calvin reads the sequence as a single divine purpose executed through apparent contradiction. The "no" is the moral will; the "go" is the permissive will that bends Balaam into the instrument of a blessing Balak paid him to prevent. The anger is not divine wavering but a dramatic element in a story about how God uses the greedy to accomplish the gracious.

*Steelman — the modernist redactional position (Noth, Gray, sometimes Budd):* The apparent contradiction is a seam between two originally independent traditions — an older Elohist-type tale of Balaam as genuine prophet, and a later Priestly/Deuteronomic layer that demonizes pagan prophecy. The text's "contradiction" is real and historical; its preservation shows the redactor valued both traditions. To read the text is to read a palimpsest in which the tension is the meaning: Israel's scripture preserved honest ambivalence about foreign prophecy. Radner and Brueggemann, while not endorsing the diachronic reconstruction, treat the narrative's surface ambiguity as theologically productive — the text refuses to resolve whether Balaam is saint, sinner, or tool, because the real subject is not Balaam but YHWH.

**Where this leaves us:** The text shows a God whose sovereignty is not undermined by human willfulness and whose providence is stronger than the apparent contradiction between what is commanded and what is permitted. What we resist reading as coherent character psychology — Balaam wanting two things, God speaking two ways — the tradition reads as the terrain on which grace outruns greed. Whether the contradiction is redactional or theological or both, the canonical reader is asked to see the narrative as a picture of divine purpose working through and around human compromise.

**Tension 2: Is Balaam a true prophet or a false one?** Scripture gives contradictory signals within Numbers 22 itself. He consults YHWH ("the LORD my God," v. 18), is spoken to by YHWH, and later utters genuinely authoritative oracles. But the New Testament (2 Peter, Jude, Revelation) and the rabbinic tradition agree he is a villain.

*Maximalist-pagan position (some patristic, Origen, Aquinas):* Balaam is a genuine prophet. Revelation is not co-extensive with Israelite covenant. A prophet can receive true words and be morally corrupt, because prophecy is a *charisma* freely given, not a reward for virtue. Aquinas in II-II q. 172 formalizes this: prophetic grace is a *gratia gratis data*, distinct from sanctifying grace. On this reading, Balaam's oracles are real revelation, and his spiritual peril is the peril of every gifted but unconverted cleric.

*Minimalist-sorcerer position (Philo, most rabbinic, Luther):* Balaam is a pagan diviner whose "consultation of YHWH" is opportunistic — he speaks the divine name because the paying client is interested in the tribal god of the people he wants cursed. God overpowers his intended curses with blessings not because Balaam is a prophet but because God hijacks the ritual. His "words" in chapters 23-24 are not his; they are ventriloquy.

*Steelman — the morally ambiguous prophet (Milgrom, Wenham, Levine, Cole, and most contemporary critical readers):* Balaam is neither simply true nor simply false. He inhabits an intermediate category the Bible rarely theorizes directly: a genuine recipient of divine speech whose moral response to that speech is wrong. Numbers 22 is at pains to show both: God really does speak to him (v. 9, v. 20, v. 35), and he really is off-track morally (v. 22, v. 32). The Deir ʿAlla inscription reminds us that Balaam was, in historical memory, a genuine religious figure of the Transjordan — not a fiction invented to be condemned. The rabbinic and apostolic hostility crystallized later, partly for polemical purposes (Pergamum, the Nicolaitan controversy). To steelman this position is to refuse the either/or: revelation and corruption can coexist in a single human. That is in fact the theological bombshell Balaam delivers.

**Where this leaves us:** The text treats prophecy as a gift that does not sanctify the one who bears it. This is difficult for both Christian and Jewish traditions, which tend to want the messenger to match the message. The Balaam story insists they do not have to. A person can hear God, can speak God's words, and can still be on the way to destruction. That is a warning as much as a consolation, and the New Testament applies it inside the church: some who prophesy in Jesus' name will hear, "I never knew you."

**Unresolved question:** If the Deir ʿAlla inscription preserves a genuine independent tradition of Balaam son of Beor as a Transjordan seer, what is the relation between that tradition and the biblical one? Did Israelite scribes know the Transjordan Balaam texts and appropriate the figure? Did pagan and Israelite traditions independently remember a historical seer of international reputation? Is the biblical narrative polemically engaging an existing cult of Balaam that Israel knew was out there? The archaeological and literary evidence points to a real religious memory older than and independent of the biblical text, but its precise relation to Numbers 22-24 remains irrecoverable.

## The Thread

Four non-obvious cross-references.

1. **Genesis 22:3 and Numbers 22:21** — the saddled donkey. Rashi and the rabbinic tradition noted that Balaam saddles his own donkey with the identical phrase (*wayyaḥbōš ʾet-ʾătônô*) that Abraham uses saddling his on the way to sacrifice Isaac (*wayyaḥbōš ʾet-ḥămōrô*). The echo is deliberate. Abraham rides his donkey in obedience to a command he cannot understand; Balaam rides his in disobedience to a command he does understand. The saddled donkey is the image in which the two prophets — the father of the nation blessed and the seer paid to curse it — are set against each other.

2. **Joshua 5:13-15 and Numbers 22:23** — the angel with drawn sword. Only two figures in the Hebrew Bible meet the *malʾak YHWH* standing in the way with *ḥereb šĕlûpâ*: Balaam the pagan seer and Joshua the covenantal conqueror. The angel with drawn sword blocks the one from entering Moab to curse and meets the other entering Canaan to conquer. Joshua falls on his face and worships; Balaam falls on his face only after his eyes are opened. The two theophanies are bookends on Israel's conquest narrative.

3. **1 Kings 13 and Numbers 22** — the disobedient prophet and the obedient animal. The "man of God" from Judah in 1 Kings 13 is deceived, eats bread he was forbidden to eat, and is killed by a lion that does not eat his corpse or attack his donkey. The animals behave with decorum while the prophet does not. The narrative critique is structurally Balaam-shaped: the prophet rides a donkey that sees better than he does, or fails to see what the beasts see clearly. The Deuteronomistic Historian knows the Balaam story.

4. **Micah 6:5** — "O my people, remember what Balak king of Moab devised, and what Balaam answered him." In the prophetic covenant lawsuit, Balaam is named not as villain but as evidence. The blessing pronounced over Israel in Moab is a gift for which Israel is to be grateful. Micah treats the episode as canonical memory — load-bearing for Israel's self-understanding in a way the NT's condemnation can obscure.
