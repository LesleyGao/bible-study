# Psalm 135 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 135 has no superscription in the MT — it is anonymous, opening and closing with *halleluyah* (הַלְלוּיָהּ, "praise YHWH"). It sits in **Book V** (Pss 107–150), positioned immediately after the close of the Songs of Ascents (Pss 120–134) and immediately before the Great Hallel (Ps 136). Editorial logic places it as the **opening of the post-Ascents anthology** — Pss 135–136 form a hymnic pair that picks up where the pilgrim collection ends, expanding from the temple-threshold blessing of Psalm 134 into a fuller doxological recitation of Israel's history.

Hermann **Gunkel** classified Psalm 135 as a **hymn of praise** (*Hymnus*) — the formal hymnic genre, with its characteristic *halleluyah* opening, summons-to-praise structure, and recital of God's mighty deeds. **Mowinckel** read it as a cult-functional festival hymn, almost certainly used in temple liturgy (the explicit reference to "those who stand in the house of the LORD, in the courts of the house of our God," vv. 1–2, ties the psalm to Levitical-priestly service). **Hossfeld and Zenger** treat it as a deliberately composed **post-exilic anthology piece** — what is sometimes called a **cento** (Latin: "patchwork"), a psalm composed largely from quotations of other psalms and Pentateuchal passages, edited together into a new composition.

The **cento-form** is the psalm's most distinctive literary fact, and it has specific implications for dating. Psalm 135 verbatim or near-verbatim quotes:

- **Ps 115:4–8** in vv. 15–18 (the anti-idol polemic) — the parallel is so close that one is clearly the source of the other.
- **Pss 78, 105, 106** in vv. 8–12 (the Egypt and conquest recital) — the smiting of Egypt's firstborn, Sihon king of the Amorites, Og king of Bashan, and the gift of the land.
- **Ps 113** in vv. 1–4 (the opening summons to praise the LORD's name) and **Ps 134:1** echoes in vv. 1–2.
- **Exodus 3:15; Deut 32:36; 1 Kings 8:39** in vv. 13–14 (the *shem* and *zekher* couplet, and the LORD's vindication of his people).
- The closing **berakhah from Zion** of v. 21 echoes Ps 134:3.

This degree of intertextual borrowing places Psalm 135 firmly in the late-Persian or early-Hellenistic period, when the Psalter was being edited as a collection and individual psalmists could compose by quotation. It is one of the principal pieces of internal evidence for the Psalter's redactional formation.

The Hebrew opens with *halelu Yah, halelu et-shem Yahweh* (הַלְלוּ יָהּ הַלְלוּ אֶת־שֵׁם יְהוָה, "praise YH, praise the name of YHWH," v. 1). The *shem*-theology of the psalm is its opening claim: praise is directed not to YHWH abstractly but to *the Name*, which carries Deuteronomic associations (Deut 12:5 — "the place where the LORD will choose to put his name"). Other anchor terms: *segullah* (סְגֻלָּה, "treasured possession," v. 4) — the same word used in Exodus 19:5 and Deuteronomy 7:6 of Israel as YHWH's elect; *otot u-moftim* (אֹתוֹת וּמֹפְתִים, "signs and wonders," v. 9) — the technical Exodus-language for the plagues; *nachalah* (נַחֲלָה, "heritage," v. 12) — the conquest-allotment language.

ANE parallels: hymns to gods enumerating their cosmic power and historical deeds are widely attested in Mesopotamian, Egyptian, and Ugaritic literatures. The *Enuma Elish* recital of Marduk's deeds and the Egyptian hymn to Amun at Karnak both show structural similarity. What distinguishes Psalm 135 is the historical-conquest specificity (Sihon, Og, Canaan) and the explicit anti-idol polemic — these are Israel-particular contributions to the hymnic genre.

Textual transmission: 11QPs-a at Qumran preserves portions of Psalm 135. The LXX is essentially identical to MT. No significant text-critical issues.

## Lens 2: The Literature

The psalm divides into **five strophes**, with a clear architectural plan:

- **Strophe 1 (vv. 1–4)** — opening summons to praise (echoing Ps 113).
- **Strophe 2 (vv. 5–7)** — YHWH's cosmic supremacy (the creation/storm theophany).
- **Strophe 3 (vv. 8–12)** — YHWH's historical acts (Egypt and the conquest).
- **Strophe 4 (vv. 13–14)** — YHWH's eternal name and vindication.
- **Strophe 5 (vv. 15–21)** — the anti-idol polemic + the closing summons to bless YHWH.

The arc is: cult → cosmos → history → eternity → polemic and benediction.

**Lowth's parallelism** is dense throughout. Synonymous parallelism opens the psalm: *halelu Yah* // *halelu et-shem Yahweh* // *halelu avdei Yahweh* (vv. 1) — three near-identical summons in three lines. Antithetic parallelism dominates the anti-idol polemic: the idols' sensory-organs are listed and then negated — "they have mouths, but do not speak; eyes, but do not see; ears, but do not hear" (vv. 16–17). Synthetic / climactic parallelism builds the historical recital, each verse extending the previous (vv. 8–12).

**Inclusio** structures the psalm at multiple levels. Most obviously, *halleluyah* opens (v. 1) and closes (v. 21) the psalm. The summons to "servants of the LORD" who "stand in the house of the LORD" (vv. 1–2) is matched by the closing summons to "house of Israel," "house of Aaron," "house of Levi," and "you who fear the LORD" to bless YHWH (vv. 19–20). The opening *bless the LORD from his holy place* and the closing *blessed be the LORD from Zion* (v. 21) form a Zion-blessing inclusio.

**The cento technique.** What makes Psalm 135 literarily distinctive is its **composition by quotation**. The verbatim quotation of Ps 115:4–8 in vv. 15–18 is the clearest case: the anti-idol polemic is reproduced almost word-for-word, with a small variant (Ps 115 reads "they have noses, but do not smell," which Ps 135 omits). The historical recital (vv. 8–12) draws on the standard Pentateuchal-historical-Psalter language for the Exodus and conquest. The opening summons (vv. 1–4) overlaps with Ps 113, and the closing benediction (v. 21) overlaps with Ps 134.

This is not plagiarism in the modern sense; it is **anthological composition** — a recognized post-exilic literary mode in which the psalmist demonstrates the unity of the canonical witness by weaving its threads into a new tapestry. **John Goldingay**'s commentary calls it "the Psalter quoting itself in the act of becoming the Psalter." The form is itself theologically significant: it claims that Israel's history, cosmology, and anti-idol polemic are all of a piece, all praising the same LORD.

**Imagery.** The cosmic strophe (vv. 5–7) uses storm-theophany imagery — clouds, lightning, wind from divine "storehouses" (*otzarot*) — language that has West Semitic and ANE storm-god parallels (Baal of Ugarit), here transposed to YHWH. The historical strophe (vv. 8–12) uses concrete proper nouns: Egypt, Pharaoh, Sihon, Og, Canaan. The anti-idol strophe (vv. 15–18) uses sensory negation: mouths-eyes-ears that do not work, with the devastating closing line "those who make them become like them; so do all who trust in them" (v. 18) — a moral-psychological claim about idolatry's spiritual deadening.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Book V placement.** Psalm 135 sits at the seam between the closed Songs of Ascents and the Great Hallel. The pilgrim collection has ended; the editorial strategy now turns to extended anthology-hymnody. Pss 135 and 136 are best read as a paired diptych — Ps 135 as the anthological recital, Ps 136 as the antiphonal-responsion expansion of essentially the same material with the *ki le-olam chasdo* refrain. The two psalms cover overlapping historical content (Egypt, Sihon, Og, conquest) but use radically different forms: 135 is a single-voice cento; 136 is a call-and-response liturgy.

**Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 135 dwells on the *shem*-theology and the doctrine of Israel's election (*segullah*, v. 4). The Midrash treats vv. 15–18 (the anti-idol polemic) with characteristic edge: the rabbis read "those who make them become like them" as a warning that idolatry deforms the worshipper's own moral and intellectual faculties. **Rashi** glosses *otzarotav* ("his storehouses," v. 7) as the celestial chambers from which God dispenses wind and rain — a cosmological reading. **Ibn Ezra** treats the psalm as a comprehensive doxological recital, noting the cento structure with characteristic interpretive sobriety. **Radak** elaborates the historical reading.

Liturgically, Psalm 135 is recited in some Sephardic traditions on Sabbath evenings; portions are integrated into the Pesukei d'Zimra (the morning-prayer "Verses of Praise") in some rites. The psalm's closing summons to "house of Israel, house of Aaron, house of Levi, all you who fear the LORD" appears in modified form in Psalms 115:9–11 and 118:2–4 — and the parallel suggests a stable liturgical formula across the post-exilic community.

Modern Jewish scholarship has been productive on the cento phenomenon. **Levenson** treats Ps 135 as evidence of the late-Persian or early-Hellenistic anthological turn in psalmody. **Sarna** highlights the *shem*-theology and its Deuteronomic resonance. **Buber** read the psalm's anti-idol polemic as a still-relevant warning about modernity's own idolatries. **Heschel** in *God in Search of Man* draws on the psalm's claim that idols are merely "the work of human hands" as a meditation on what genuine religious encounter is not.

**Christian trajectory.** **Augustine**'s commentary on Psalm 135 develops a Trinitarian reading of the *shem*-theology: the Name of YHWH that endures forever (v. 13) is the Word made flesh, the Logos. The anti-idol polemic Augustine reads as a cosmic-spiritual diagnosis of pagan religion and as a warning about Christian temptations to idolatry. **Cassiodorus** systematizes the Augustinian reading. **Aquinas** treats the psalm as a comprehensive recital of God's perfections — cosmic, historical, eternal — and reads vv. 15–18 as a philosophical argument against the rationality of idolatry.

**Luther** preached Psalm 135 against late-medieval relic-veneration and image-worship; **Calvin** in his Psalms commentary read the anti-idol polemic as a Reformation-era warrant against Catholic image-cult, and the psalm became a touchstone for the Reformed iconoclastic tradition. The Westminster Confession's chapter on worship draws indirectly on the *they have mouths, but do not speak* polemic.

Modern critical scholarship: **Gunkel** classified the psalm a hymn. **Mowinckel** read it cult-functional. **Westermann** treated it as descriptive praise. **Kraus** worked out the strophic architecture. **Hossfeld and Zenger** read it as the post-exilic anthology piece par excellence — explicitly treating it as a *cento* and using it as a window into Psalter formation. **John Goldingay** dwells on the psalm's intertextual density and treats the cento form as a mode of canonical theology. **Gordon Wenham** notes the Levitical-priestly orientation of vv. 1–2 and the inclusio structure of the closing summons (vv. 19–21). **Walter Brueggemann** reads the psalm as a comprehensive doxological summary of YHWH's cosmic-historical-ethical reign. **Patrick Miller** and William **Brown** track the *shem* and *segullah* lexicon. **Beth Tanner** highlights the rhetorical force of the closing summons to the four named groups (Israel, Aaron, Levi, God-fearers) — the psalm widens its blessing-call across the whole community.

**The cento and Psalter formation.** In the last thirty years a substantial scholarly literature has developed around the editorial-canonical formation of the Psalter. **Gerald Wilson**'s *The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* (1985), and the subsequent work of **Hossfeld and Zenger**, **deClaissé-Walford**, and **Wenham**, has argued that the canonical Psalter shows deliberate editorial structuring at the seams between books. Psalm 135, sitting at the seam between the Songs of Ascents and the Great Hallel, is a key piece of evidence for this redactional view: a psalm composed by quotation that gathers up earlier psalmody and rebroadcasts it as a coherent post-exilic theological summary.

**NT use.** Psalm 135 is not directly cited in the New Testament, though its theology pervades early Christian preaching. The *shem*-theology of v. 13 is paralleled in Phil 2:9–11 (the "name above every name"). The anti-idol polemic of vv. 15–18 is directly quarried in Acts 14:15 (Paul at Lystra: "we bring you good news, that you should turn from these vain things to a living God") and Romans 1:21–25. The *those who make them become like them* (v. 18) is one of the most cited verses in patristic anti-idol literature.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Composition by quotation — original or derivative?** The cento form of Psalm 135 raises an unavoidable question: is this psalm a piece of inspired composition or a derivative anthology? Modern instincts about authorship tend to privilege original creation; the psalm appears to do something else entirely.

*Position A — the cento is derivative and second-rate.* If we measure psalms by originality, Psalm 135 fails: nearly every line is borrowed. The anti-idol polemic is Ps 115 verbatim; the conquest recital is Pentateuchal stock-language; the opening summons is Ps 113. The psalm contributes nothing new.

*Position B — the cento is the highest form of canonical composition.* The psalmist is not failing to be original; the psalmist is performing canonical theology — claiming that the inheritance of Torah, prophets, and earlier psalmody coheres as a single witness. By weaving the threads, the psalmist demonstrates that the Bible reads itself, that Israel's history and the anti-idol polemic and the cosmic doxology are not separate items but a single interlocking testimony. **Hossfeld and Zenger** and **Goldingay** read the cento this way.

*Position C — the cento is a Second-Temple liturgical practice.* Like the New Testament's reuse of OT scripture, the psalm's quotation-density reflects a community whose liturgical and intellectual life was saturated with prior text. Composition by quotation was not derivative but reverent — a way of praying in the inherited words. **Robert Alter** and **Jacob Milgrom** have both noted that this is broadly characteristic of Second-Temple Hebrew literature.

**Steelman.** Position A holds the modern literary instinct honestly. Position B reads canonically and treats the cento as theological achievement. Position C names what the cento actually is: a liturgical-communal practice rather than a literary experiment.

**Tension 2: The anti-idol polemic.** Vv. 15–18 are sharp: idols are "silver and gold, the work of human hands"; they cannot speak, see, hear, or breathe; "those who make them become like them; so do all who trust in them." The polemic is morally and theologically aggressive. What does it mean when a religious community sings this against its neighbors?

The traditional Jewish and Christian reading takes the polemic at face value: idolatry is spiritually deforming, and its critique is part of Israel's prophetic vocation. **Calvin** and the Reformed tradition extended the critique inward against Christian image-cult. **Buber** and **Heschel** modernized the polemic as a critique of all reductive substitutes for transcendent encounter — including, in the twentieth century, ideological idolatries (nationalism, Marxism, technological optimism).

The contemporary tension is the relationship between this polemic and **interfaith respect**. Modern liturgical communities sometimes abridge or soften vv. 15–18 in interfaith contexts. **Brueggemann** has argued that the polemic must be retained but interpreted as a critique of the *idolatrous form* of religion (whether one's own or another's), not as a license to demean other religious communities. The line between honest theological diagnosis and supremacist contempt has to be navigated with care.

**Where this leaves us.** Psalm 135 is the post-exilic anthology hymn — the psalmist's act of weaving together cult-summons, cosmic-praise, conquest-recital, eternal-name theology, and anti-idol polemic into a single doxological tapestry. Its compositional method (cento) is itself theological: the canon coheres, and the act of singing the inherited words is the act of confessing that coherence. Its anti-idol polemic is sharp and consequential; its summons-and-blessing structure widens to embrace Israel, the priesthood, the Levites, and "you who fear the LORD" (v. 20) — the latter phrase often read in Second-Temple usage as a designation for Gentile God-fearers, the proselyte fringe of the synagogue community. The psalm thus enacts what it sings: a comprehensive gathering of voices into a single blessing from Zion.

**Closing question.** What does it mean to praise God in someone else's words? Psalm 135 quotes Psalm 115, Psalm 113, Deuteronomy, Exodus — and through that quotation claims to speak truly. Whether liturgical composition by quotation can be the highest form of devotion or whether it is mere repetition is a question every worshipping community has to ask itself again, every generation.
