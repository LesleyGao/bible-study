# Psalm 137 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 137 is anonymous in the MT, with no superscription. It sits in **Book V** (Pss 107–150), among the most theologically and morally difficult texts in the entire Psalter. It is the **only** psalm in the canonical collection that is unambiguously and explicitly **exilic in setting** — the psalmist speaks from "the waters of Babylon" looking back toward Zion. The dating is consequently among the most secure in the Psalter: the psalm reflects the Babylonian exile (587–538 BCE) or its immediate aftermath, with most scholars favoring a composition either during the late exile or in the early post-exilic period when the trauma was still raw.

Hermann **Gunkel** classified Psalm 137 as a **communal lament** (*Volksklagelied*) with a closing **imprecation** (*Fluchpsalm*). **Mowinckel** treated it as a Zion-lament shaped for liturgical recitation in the post-exilic community. **Hossfeld and Zenger** read it as a **memorial-lament-with-curse**, structurally distinct from the standard communal-lament form because of its unusual narrative-recollection opening (vv. 1–4) and its closing imprecation (vv. 7–9). The psalm thus mixes lament, memorial, oath, and imprecation in a single composition.

The historical setting is concrete. After Nebuchadnezzar's siege of Jerusalem in 587 BCE, the Babylonians destroyed the temple, deported the elite, and effectively ended the Davidic state. The exile lasted until Cyrus's edict of 538 BCE permitted return. The Edomites, traditionally Israel's kin (descendants of Esau), are recorded in Obadiah, Lamentations 4:21–22, and Ezekiel 35 as having actively assisted the Babylonians by attacking fleeing Judeans and looting Jerusalem — explaining why v. 7 specifically calls down God's remembrance against Edom ("the day of Jerusalem... lay it bare, lay it bare, down to its foundations"). This concrete historical reference further anchors the psalm in the exilic moment.

The Hebrew opening is among the most famous lines in the Psalter: *al naharot Bavel sham yashavnu gam-bakhinu be-zakhrenu et-Tziyon* (עַל נַהֲרוֹת בָּבֶל שָׁם יָשַׁבְנוּ גַּם־בָּכִינוּ בְּזָכְרֵנוּ אֶת־צִיּוֹן, "by the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down and yes we wept when we remembered Zion," v. 1). The doubled *sham* ("there," vv. 1, 3) is structurally emphatic — *that place*, away from home, is the locus of the lament. The "rivers" likely refer to the Tigris-Euphrates river system and its irrigation canals, which are well-attested in Babylonian period records as gathering-places for displaced peoples.

Other anchor terms: *kinnoroteinu* (כִּנֹּרוֹתֵינוּ, "our lyres," v. 2) — the *kinnor* is the standard Israelite stringed instrument, the temple-music instrument *par excellence*. Hanging the *kinnor* on the willows is a powerful negative-symbol: the instruments of temple praise are silenced, suspended, withdrawn from use. *Aravim* (עֲרָבִים, "willows" or possibly "poplars," v. 2) — likely Euphrates poplars (*Populus euphratica*), abundant along Babylonian waterways. *Shir Tziyon* (שִׁיר־צִיּוֹן, "songs of Zion," v. 3) — almost certainly the Zion-pilgrimage hymns (Pss 46, 48, 76, 84, 87, 122) which the captors are demanding as entertainment, perversely.

The psalm's most contested phrase is v. 9, the closing imprecation: *ashrei she-yochez ve-nipetz et-olalayikh el-ha-sela* (אַשְׁרֵי שֶׁיֹּאחֵז וְנִפֵּץ אֶת־עֹלָלַיִךְ אֶל־הַסָּלַע, "blessed is he who takes and dashes your little ones against the rock"). *Olelim* (עֹלָלִים) means "small children, infants, sucklings"; *nipetz* is a violent verb meaning "to dash, smash, shatter." This is the most disturbing single verse in the entire Psalter, and any honest engagement with the text must reckon with it.

ANE parallels: lament-over-destroyed-cities is a well-attested ANE genre (the Sumerian *Lament for Ur*, the *Lament for Nippur*, the *Lament for Eridu*). Imprecations against enemies, including imprecations involving the killing of children, are also attested in ANE treaty-curse literature and in some extant prophetic material from Mesopotamia. Psalm 137's closing curse, while shocking to modern readers, is not without ancient context — though context does not blunt its moral edge.

Textual transmission: 11QPs-a at Qumran does not preserve Psalm 137 (the scroll is fragmentary). The LXX and Vulgate render the psalm with slight variants — most notably the Greek tradition adds an attribution to David (*Tō Dauid* — "of David") which the MT lacks; this is almost certainly a secondary expansion. No major text-critical issues affect the disturbing verses.

## Lens 2: The Literature

The psalm divides into **three strophes**, structurally distinct:

- **Strophe 1 (vv. 1–4)** — narrative recollection of exile (the captors' demand for entertainment).
- **Strophe 2 (vv. 5–6)** — the personal oath to remember Jerusalem.
- **Strophe 3 (vv. 7–9)** — the imprecation against Edom and Babylon.

The arc moves from collective memory ("we"), to personal vow ("I"), to imprecatory curse ("blessed is he..."). Each strophe shifts both grammatical person and emotional register.

**Lowth's parallelism** is dense throughout. Synonymous parallelism opens the psalm: "by the rivers of Babylon // there we sat down and wept" (v. 1); "if I forget you, Jerusalem // let my right hand forget" (v. 5). Antithetic parallelism appears in vv. 3–4: the captors demand "songs of Zion," but the captives ask "how shall we sing the LORD's song in a foreign land?" Synthetic / climactic parallelism builds the imprecation across vv. 8–9: the curse on Babylon is announced; then the *blessed-is-he-who* formula intensifies with each iteration.

**The doubled *sham*.** The Hebrew word *sham* ("there") appears twice in vv. 1–4 (once in v. 1, once in v. 3) and is structurally weighted — the geography of the exile is the geography of the lament. **Robert Alter** has noted that the doubled *sham* creates a deliberate spatial-emotional anchoring: *that place, far from here, is where this happened*.

**The oath structure of vv. 5–6.** The "if I forget... let my right hand forget" is a classical Hebrew oath-formula, with the implied apodosis (the punishment) made explicit: if the speaker forgets Jerusalem, the speaker's hand forgets its skill (perhaps the lyre-skill, fitting the v. 2 imagery; or the right hand more generally as instrument of capacity). The parallel "let my tongue stick to the roof of my mouth" is the oral-musical counterpart: if memory fails, both hand and tongue are paralyzed. This is the speaker's own self-imposed curse — a structural mirror to the imprecation that follows. The oath is what binds the speaker to memory; the imprecation is what binds Babylon to judgment.

**Inclusio and chiasmus.** The opening *zakhar* ("remember," v. 1 — *be-zakhrenu et-Tziyon*) is matched by the imperative *zakhor* ("remember," v. 7 — *zekhor Yahweh livnei Edom*): the captives remember Zion; the LORD is asked to remember Edom. This is a precise inclusio, with the same root in opening and closing strophes.

A subtle chiasmus runs through the spatial movement: rivers of Babylon → willows there → Jerusalem (vv. 5–6) → Edomites at Jerusalem (v. 7) → daughter of Babylon (vv. 8–9). The psalm moves from Babylon to Jerusalem and back, with Jerusalem at the structural center. The chiasmus argues that the lament's geography is theological: Babylon and Jerusalem are mirror-poles, with the speaker's heart bound to the latter even as the body sits beside the former.

**Imagery.** The hung-up lyres on the willows (v. 2) is one of the most enduring images in world literature — silent instruments, suspended over flowing water, with weeping captives below. The "right hand forgetting its skill" (v. 5) is bodily-vocational ruin. The "tongue sticking to the roof of the mouth" (v. 6) is muteness. The "bare foundations" of Jerusalem (v. 7) is architectural devastation. And the "little ones dashed against the rock" (v. 9) is the most violent image in the Psalter.

**Rhetorical structure of the imprecation.** Vv. 8–9 use the *ashrei* ("blessed is he who...") formula, the same blessing-formula that opens Psalms 1, 32, 41, 112, and many others — the formula of beatitude. The psalm thus turns the language of blessing into the language of curse: "blessed is he who repays you" (v. 8), "blessed is he who takes your little ones..." (v. 9). This is rhetorical perversion, deliberate: the form of beatitude is conscripted to declare a horror. The choice of formula makes the imprecation more, not less, disturbing.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Book V placement.** Psalm 137 sits in Book V immediately after the Great Hallel (Ps 136). The juxtaposition is editorial-theological: Psalm 136's twenty-six confessions of God's *chesed* are immediately followed by Psalm 137's brutal cry from exile. The collection refuses to let the *chesed*-confession stand alone without the witness from the rivers of Babylon. **Brueggemann** has argued that this editorial pairing is one of the most theologically courageous moments in the Psalter: the canon places the confession of God's enduring love next to the prayer for infants smashed on stone, and refuses to harmonize them.

**Jewish trajectory.** Psalm 137 is recited on the **Ninth of Av** (*Tisha B'Av*), the Jewish day of mourning commemorating the destruction of both temples. It is also recited at funerals in some Jewish traditions. The custom of breaking a glass at Jewish weddings is traditionally connected to v. 5 — "if I forget you, O Jerusalem" — the bride and groom remembering Jerusalem in their moment of greatest joy.

*Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 137 dwells on the captors' demand that the captives sing — the Midrash treats this as a refined torment, the demand to perform joyful Zion-hymns as entertainment for the destroyers of Zion. The Midrash also notes that v. 9 is among the harshest verses in Scripture and treats it as the inverse of God's promise to Babylon's destroyer in Isaiah 13.

**Rashi** glosses *yemini* ("my right hand," v. 5) as the lyre-playing hand and reads vv. 5–6 as the speaker's vow that the cantor's craft will fail if Zion is forgotten. **Ibn Ezra** treats the historical-Edomite reference of v. 7 in light of Obadiah's prophecy. **Radak** elaborates the messianic-eschatological reading: Babylon's overthrow anticipates the eschatological judgment of all powers that have oppressed Israel. None of the medieval Jewish commentators soften v. 9; all of them sit with its harshness.

In modern Jewish reception the psalm has had an extraordinary post-Holocaust career. **Emil Fackenheim**, in *To Mend the World* and elsewhere, treats Psalm 137 as the canonical text of authentic post-Holocaust Jewish theology — the refusal to forget, the demand for justice against the perpetrators, the unwillingness to let traumatic memory be smoothed over by religious sentimentality. **Irving Greenberg**, in his theological writings on covenant-after-Shoah, similarly invokes Psalm 137 as evidence that biblical faith has the resources to give voice to traumatic rage without being destroyed by it. **Yehuda Amichai**, the Israeli poet, has written multiple poems engaging Psalm 137's imagery from Jerusalem in the modern era.

**Christian trajectory.** The Christian reception of Psalm 137 — and especially of v. 9 — is one of the most fraught chapters in the history of biblical interpretation. **Augustine**, in his *Enarratio*, performs a famous **spiritualizing exegesis**: the "little ones" of Babylon are not literal infants but the *nascent thoughts* of sin and disorder in the believer's soul. To "dash them against the rock" (and the rock, Augustine notes following 1 Cor 10:4, is Christ) is to kill these inchoate sins in their cradle by bringing them to Christ. This allegorical reading became the dominant Christian-monastic reading for a thousand years and is preserved in **Cassiodorus**, **Bede**, and the medieval-monastic commentary tradition broadly.

**Aquinas** preserves the Augustinian reading but is more nuanced; he acknowledges that the literal-historical sense of the verse refers to actual Babylonian infants and that the spiritualizing sense is a secondary-typological extension. **Luther** worked hard with the psalm; in his lectures on the Psalms he engaged the literal sense more directly than the medieval tradition and treated the imprecation as the legitimate prayer of the wronged community for divine justice. **Calvin** in his Psalms commentary handled v. 9 with great care: he argued that the verse is the prayer of the Spirit-led believer for divine vengeance on the actual perpetrators, and that the "blessing" pronounced on the avenger is the recognition that God uses human agency for divine judgment. He resisted the Augustinian allegory and preferred a strict reading of the literal sense plus typological extension to the eschatological judgment of all evil.

Modern critical scholarship: **Gunkel** classified the psalm communal-lament-with-imprecation. **Mowinckel** read it cult-functional. **Westermann** treated it as collective memory shaped for liturgy. **Kraus** worked out the strophic architecture. **Hossfeld and Zenger** read it as a deliberate composition holding lament, oath, and curse in a single integrated form. **John Goldingay** dwells on the ethical and theological problems of v. 9 and rejects the Augustinian allegory as an evasion of the text's actual claim. **Gordon Wenham** has written extensively on the imprecation; in *The Psalter Reclaimed* he argues that Christian liturgical practice should retain Psalm 137 unabridged and grapple with its harshness rather than pretending it isn't there. **Walter Brueggemann**'s *The Costly Loss of Lament* (1986) and his subsequent essay specifically on Psalm 137 (in *The Message of the Psalms* and in *Praying the Psalms*) argues that the psalm's imprecation is not a moral failure but a theological achievement: it demonstrates that biblical faith can absorb even traumatic rage and bring it to God in prayer rather than externalizing it as actual violence. The prayer is the alternative to the deed.

**Erich Zenger**'s monograph *A God of Vengeance? Understanding the Psalms of Divine Wrath* (1996) is the modern critical-theological touchstone. Zenger argues — against centuries of Christian apologetic-allegorical evasion — that Psalm 137:9 must be read as **protest after genocide**: a community that has just witnessed the systematic destruction of its city, the murder of its non-combatants, and the dashing of its own little ones against rocks (an attested practice of ANE warfare; see 2 Kings 8:12; Isa 13:16; Hos 13:16; Nah 3:10) is praying that the same be done to its destroyers. The verse is the **mirror image of Babylon's actual atrocity**, prayed back to God as a demand for justice. Zenger's reading transforms the verse from a moral problem into a witness to the costs of genocide.

**James Cone**, in *God of the Oppressed* and elsewhere, reads Psalm 137 from the African-American slavery and post-emancipation tradition. The "songs of Zion" demanded by the captors (v. 3) Cone parallels with the slaveholders' demands that enslaved African-Americans perform "happy" songs and dances for their white owners. The psalm's refusal — "how shall we sing the LORD's song in a foreign land?" — Cone reads as the prototypical resistance to the cultural appropriation of the oppressed's own religious expression. The Black spiritual tradition has long drawn on Psalm 137; **By the Rivers of Babylon** (the Boney M. 1978 hit, drawing on the Melodians' 1970 reggae version) is itself a piece of this trajectory, mediated through the Caribbean Rastafarian appropriation of biblical exile-imagery.

**NT use.** Psalm 137 is not directly cited in the New Testament. But the **Babylon-trope of Revelation 17–18** ("fallen, fallen is Babylon the great") draws extensively on Psalm 137's imprecation: Revelation's prayer for Babylon's overthrow and the rejoicing of heaven at her fall (Rev 18:20; 19:1–4) parallels Psalm 137:8–9's "*blessed is he who repays you*." The early Christian apocalyptic tradition thus inherited the imprecation, transposing it onto Rome and onto the eschatological judgment of all oppressive powers.

**Christian liturgical practice today.** The Catholic Liturgy of the Hours, in its post-Vatican-II reform, **omits Psalm 137:7–9** from the cycle entirely; Psalm 137:1–6 is retained but the imprecation is excised. The Anglican Book of Common Prayer in its modern revisions has similarly bracketed or omitted the closing verses in many liturgical uses. Many Reformed and evangelical liturgies do the same. The pruning has been controversial: it spares congregations from singing infanticide-imprecation, but it also amounts to a canonical edit, removing the very verses that give the psalm its full theological witness.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: How to read v. 9.** The closing verse — "blessed is he who takes your little ones and dashes them against the rock" — is the most disturbing single verse in the Psalter. There is no single right way to read it; there are several positions, each defensible.

*Position A — Augustinian allegory.* The "little ones" are nascent sins in the believer's soul; "the rock" is Christ; to "dash" them against Christ is the spiritual-disciplinary work of mortification. This reading dominated Christian interpretation for over a millennium and is preserved in Augustine, Cassiodorus, Bede, and many medieval commentators. **Strength**: it allows the verse to be sung in Christian liturgy without endorsing infanticide. **Weakness**: it abandons the literal-historical sense entirely and reads into the verse a meaning the original cannot have carried. **Goldingay**, **Brueggemann**, and **Zenger** all reject this reading as evasion.

*Position B — protest after genocide.* The verse is the cry of a community that has just witnessed actual genocide, including the actual dashing of its own infants against rocks (Hos 13:16; 2 Kgs 8:12). It prays back at the perpetrator the same atrocity, as a demand for proportional justice. Zenger's reading; defended by Brueggemann, Goldingay, Wenham. **Strength**: it takes the historical and ethical context seriously and refuses to soften the trauma. **Weakness**: it still leaves the modern liturgical user with the question of how to *sing* the prayer.

*Position C — eschatological judgment, not personal vengeance.* The "blessing" is on the divine justice that overthrows Babylon, with the imprecation a request for God's judgment, not a personal undertaking. This is closer to Calvin's reading. **Strength**: it locates the violence with God's righteousness, not with the human pray-er. **Weakness**: it does not fully explain why the imprecation specifies infants.

*Position D — the verse is so toxic in Christian liturgy that pruning is responsible.* Modern Catholic and Anglican liturgical practice has substantially abridged or omitted vv. 7–9. **Strength**: it spares modern worshipers from mouthing infanticide. **Weakness**: it amounts to a canonical edit and removes the very verses that bear the strongest witness to the trauma of exile.

**Steelman both ends.** The case for **retention**: Psalm 137:9 is unflinching scriptural witness to what genocide does to the human heart, and the canon's preservation of this prayer rather than its suppression demonstrates that biblical faith has the spiritual depth to hold even traumatic rage in the presence of God. To prune the verse is to pretend that the church's fellowship cannot bear what the canon already bears. **Wenham** and **Brueggemann** make this case forcefully.

The case for **pruning**: the verse is so easily misread, so easily weaponized, and so liturgically devastating that responsible Christian use requires careful contextualization or abridgment. The post-Vatican-II Liturgy of the Hours' decision to remove vv. 7–9 reflects a pastoral judgment that the verses are too fraught for routine choral recitation. **Strength of this case**: the verse has been used historically to justify actual violence (anti-Jewish pogroms; colonial atrocities; sectarian wars). The cost of leaving it in liturgical rotation is real.

**Where this leaves us.** Psalm 137 is the canon's refusal to pretend that God's people have not been traumatized. It is the prayer of a community that has lost everything — temple, city, dynasty, children — and cannot bring itself to sing the songs of Zion in a foreign land. It is also the prayer of a community whose grief has turned to rage and whose rage demands divine justice in the most explicit and most terrible terms. **Brueggemann**'s key insight is that the prayer is the alternative to the deed: by bringing this rage to God, the psalmist is *not* taking it onto the streets of Babylon. The prayer is the discipline that saves the community from becoming what it hates. But the prayer is also unflinching — it does not soften or abstract or hide. It says what the rage actually says.

For modern Jewish readers, especially after the Holocaust, the psalm has become almost a charter-text for the legitimacy of unsoftened lament. **Fackenheim**'s post-*Shoah* theology depends on Psalm 137's witness that traumatic rage is a legitimate posture before God. For modern Christian readers, the psalm forces a choice between the long Augustinian-allegorical tradition and the more recent protest-and-witness reading; one cannot evade the choice. For African-American and other oppressed Christian traditions, the psalm has long been sung with full awareness of its imprecatory weight, as a prayer for divine justice against actual oppressors.

The psalm's enduring cultural reception — Don Quixote's setting; Verdi's Nabucco; Boney M.'s 1978 reggae-pop; Yehuda Amichai's poems; James Cone's theology — testifies that it speaks something the human community has not yet outgrown the need to say. Whether what it says can be sung honestly, today, in liturgical communities that have themselves been participants in atrocity (the Christian church's record on antisemitism, on slavery, on colonial violence), is the open question every singing community has to answer for itself.

**Closing question.** What do you do with a prayer that means what it says? Psalm 137 does not allow its readers the comfort of allegory or the cleanness of pruning without cost. It sits in the canon as a challenge: can the worshiping community bear what trauma actually says when given voice before God? The psalm's preservation in Scripture suggests that the answer must be yes — but the form of that yes, in any given liturgical generation, has to be worked out anew, with full awareness of what was lost, what is being prayed, and what kind of God is being addressed when these particular words are sung.
