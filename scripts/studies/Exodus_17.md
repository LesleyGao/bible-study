# Exodus 17: Is the LORD Among Us or Not?

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Exodus 17 lays two episodes side by side in a single chapter — water from rock at Massah and Meribah (vv. 1–7), and the battle with Amalek at Rephidim (vv. 8–16) — that feel distinct but share a single locale. Rephidim is generally located in the southern Sinai approaches to Horeb/Sinai, though as with all wilderness toponyms the identification is inferential. The traditional pilgrimage sites place Rephidim at Wadi Feiran, a substantial oasis area at the western approach to Jebel Musa. Emmanuel Anati's proposed Mount Karkom hypothesis repositions the whole wilderness geography northward toward the Negev-Sinai border. Kitchen's middle-route reconstruction takes a different path. I name the options without picking a side: the text is not cartographically precise enough to resolve the question.

The political and demographic horizon includes the Amalekites. Amalek in biblical tradition descends from Esau's grandson Amalek (Genesis 36:12), making them a collateral branch of the broader Edomite/Seirite family. They appear here as semi-nomadic raiders of the Sinai/Negev transition zone, the first foreign enemy Israel fights. They are not to be confused with the Amorites (a different ethnic category associated with the Transjordan and central Canaan) or the Ammonites (Transjordanian Lot-descendants). Amalekite raiding patterns in the Negev are historically plausible for Late Bronze or early Iron Age pastoral groups moving through the peripheries of settled kingdoms. Kitchen and Hoffmeier both take the Amalekite episode as fitting the regional picture of semi-nomadic raiders. The critical question of historicity — whether any specific battle lies behind the narrative or whether the Amalek tradition crystallizes around later conflicts (1 Samuel 15; 1 Samuel 30; Esther's Haman "the Agagite") — remains open.

Material culture: the staff (*maṭṭeh*, מַטֶּה) is the same shepherd's staff Moses struck the Nile with (v. 5 makes the connection explicit). This is the continuity marker between plagues, sea-crossing, and wilderness — the same instrument, now used differently. The rock (*ṣûr*, צוּר) at Horeb is specific: the narrator locates the miracle at "the rock at Horeb" (v. 6), already identifying Horeb with the region where Sinai will be. Whether Horeb and Sinai are strictly identical, nearby, or distinct in the sources is one of the source-critical tangles (E prefers Horeb; J and P prefer Sinai).

Authorship and dating: Exodus 17 is a J/E composite with later framing. The Massah/Meribah etiology in verses 1–7 is primarily J/E; a parallel doublet in Numbers 20:1–13 (Moses strikes the rock at Meribah-Kadesh, this time in disobedience) is attributed to P. Friedman treats the pair as one original tradition split into two episodes at different source layers. The Amalek battle narrative (vv. 8–16) is generally assigned to J/E with a Deuteronomistic tail (v. 14's "write this as a memorial in a book" and the "blot out the memory" language anticipate Deuteronomy 25:17–19). William Propp's philological analysis treats the Amalek episode as a distinct and possibly older unit attached here by the redactor.

Archaeological and textual evidence: the Merneptah Stele's late-thirteenth-century reference to "Israel" constrains the date window for the tradition's formation. Egyptian records of Sinai expeditions, the Serabit el-Khadim turquoise mines, and Proto-Sinaitic inscriptions attest to Late Bronze traffic through the region. No direct archaeological evidence confirms or denies the Rephidim episodes; the aridity of Sinai preserves little. The wilderness wadis do produce water from rock in the specific geological sense — a limestone crust can shatter under pressure and release water from a small aquifer behind it. This phenomenon has been reported by modern travelers (Burckhardt, Palmer). As with manna, the tradition holds the natural and supernatural together without reducing one to the other: the possibility that a staff-blow could release water does not neutralize the miracle of timing and quantity.

Joshua (*Yĕhôšûaʿ*, יְהוֹשׁוּעַ, "YHWH saves") makes his first named appearance in verse 9. He is Moses' *mĕšārēt* ("attendant," Exodus 24:13) and military lieutenant. He will reappear in the spy narrative of Numbers 13–14 under the name Hoshea (which Moses changes to Joshua, Numbers 13:16) and ultimately succeed Moses as leader (Deuteronomy 31). Hur is less well identified. He is not Ben Hur of later fiction. He appears again at Exodus 24:14 as one of the elders left in charge at Sinai, and rabbinic tradition (Exodus Rabbah 48.3) identifies him as the son of Miriam — which would make him Moses' nephew. Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.54) follows this tradition. The text itself does not specify.

Key Hebrew terms:

- *maṣṣâ* (מַסָּה) — testing (v. 7), from the root *nsh*, to test.
- *mĕrîbâ* (מְרִיבָה) — quarreling (v. 7), from the root *rîb*, legal dispute.
- *wayyārev* (וַיָּרֶב) — "he quarreled" (v. 2), the verb of legal dispute; the people are not merely complaining, they are initiating a forensic-style accusation.
- *ṣûr* (צוּר) — rock (v. 6); a key noun in theological usage (Deuteronomy 32:4 "the Rock, his work is perfect").
- *YHWH Nissî* (יְהוָה נִסִּי) — "the LORD is my banner" (v. 15); from *nēs*, a standard or ensign.
- *kābēd* (כָּבֵד) — heavy (v. 12, Moses' hands grew heavy); the same root used repeatedly of Pharaoh's heart in the plague cycle — a wry intertextual echo.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: two etiological narratives joined. The first (vv. 1–7) etymologizes Massah and Meribah. The second (vv. 8–16) narrates a battle with Amalek that establishes an ongoing theological claim (v. 16) and involves the erection of a named altar (v. 15). The combination is not accidental: both episodes concern Israel's capacity or incapacity to trust, and both introduce innovations — the striking of the rock, the named altar, Joshua's command, the "memorial book."

Literary devices: the chapter is rich in verbal parallels and wordplay. *maṣṣâ* (testing) and *mĕrîbâ* (quarreling) are doubled as place-names, forensic verbs, and accusations. The question of verse 7 — *hayēš YHWH bĕqirbēnû ʾim-ʾayin* ("Is the LORD among us or not?") — is the chapter's hinge. The people's testing is not arbitrary skepticism; it is a demand that YHWH's presence be vindicated, and verse 6 ("behold, I will stand before you there on the rock") is precisely the vindication requested.

The Amalek battle narrative has an almost liturgical structure. Moses on the hill, hands raised; Joshua in the valley, with the sword; Aaron and Hur supporting. The ascending and descending hand is the pivot: *wĕhāyâ kaʾăšer yārîm mōšeh yādô wĕgābar yiśrāʾēl wĕkaʾăšer yānîaḥ yādô wĕgābar ʿămālēq* — "when Moses raised his hand Israel prevailed, when he lowered it Amalek prevailed." The rhythm is epic. Robert Alter reads this as one of the most visually choreographed narratives in Exodus: three figures at the top of the hill, the battle below, the unbroken vertical axis of the raised hand. Brueggemann notes that the scene resists simple reduction to either "Moses' hands caused the victory" (magical) or "Moses' hands were symbolic" (merely pedagogical); the narrative leaves them productively ambiguous.

A striking intertextual echo: in verse 12 Moses' hands grow "heavy" — *kābēd*. This is the same root used of Pharaoh's heart throughout Exodus 7–10. The narrator, whether by accident or design, yokes Moses' ordinary human weariness to the language of Pharaoh's stubbornness. The burden of Israel's deliverance is that Moses too is subject to the gravity of the body; his hands, raised in intercession, grow heavy.

The altar is named *YHWH Nissî*, "the LORD is my banner" (v. 15). *Nēs* is the standard, ensign, or pole around which troops rally. Verse 16's Hebrew — *kî-yād ʿal-kēs Yāh* — is notoriously difficult. Literally "for a hand upon the throne (or: banner) of Yah." The consonantal text is *kēs* (throne) but many read it as *nēs* (banner), treating the initial *k* as a scribal confusion. LXX and Vulgate go different directions. Commentators from Rashi to Propp rehearse the options. The sense is something like: a hand is raised to (or upon) the throne/banner of Yah, sealing an oath of perpetual war against Amalek. What is clear is that this episode inaugurates a long theological thread about Amalek as paradigmatic enemy.

Textual variants: the LXX generally tracks the MT but softens some obscurities. 4QExod fragments preserve portions but not with significant variants. The verse 16 crux is the major textual puzzle.

The Amalek episode is referenced intertextually at Deuteronomy 25:17–19 (the commandment to remember and blot out Amalek), 1 Samuel 15 (Saul's war with Agag), 1 Samuel 30 (David's war with Amalek), Esther (Haman the Agagite as Amalekite descendant), and Psalm 83. This trail makes Exodus 17 generatively important for the later canon — it seeds a theme that recurs across the biblical narrative and into post-biblical Jewish liturgy (Purim).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Second Temple: Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.33–38 for water from rock; 3.40–62 for Amalek) narrates both episodes with rationalizing expansions. Philo allegorizes — the rock is the Logos; Amalek is the passions. Wisdom of Solomon 11:4 references water from rock briefly. Pseudo-Philo LAB treats Amalek expansively. Rabbinic: the Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael (Tractate Amalek) provides extensive running commentary on verses 8–16. A beloved rabbinic legend (Bavli Sukkah 11a and parallels) identifies the water-giving rock at Massah/Meribah as "Miriam's well" — a movable spring that accompanied Israel throughout the wilderness, ceasing at her death (Numbers 20:1–2 directly follows her death with the second rock episode). This legend becomes pervasive in midrash and carries into Paul's "rock that followed them" at 1 Corinthians 10:4 — a Christian reading overlaid on a Jewish legendary tradition. Bavli Rosh Hashanah 29a reflects on verses 11–12: "Did Moses' hands make war or break war? Rather, as long as Israel looked upward and subjected their hearts to their Father in heaven they prevailed, and when they did not, they fell." The rabbis refuse the magical reading and offer a pedagogical one.

Medieval: Rashi draws extensively from Mekhilta on both episodes. Ibn Ezra notes the geological possibility of water from rock. Ramban stresses the directed miracle — YHWH "standing before you on the rock" (v. 6) as theophanic presence. Maimonides (*Guide* III.50) treats the water miracle alongside manna as pedagogy. On Amalek: Maimonides (*Hilchot Melakhim* 5.4–5) codifies the enduring commandment to remember and to obliterate Amalek, though post-biblical identification of any specific ethnic group with Amalek is halakhically contested — most authorities hold that the nations are so thoroughly mixed that no living Amalekite is identifiable. Amalek becomes paradigmatic: the archetype of unprovoked evil.

Modern: Nahum Sarna in JPS surveys the geography and the rabbinic tradition. Levenson on Amalek as theological category. Avivah Zornberg in *The Particulars of Rapture* gives a psychoanalytically rich reading of the water episode as Israel's attack on their own origin — "is the LORD among us or not?" as the question the traumatized soul asks God. Ilana Pardes on Miriam's well as a feminist trace in the rabbinic tradition. Heschel on the prophet's task of lifting hands that grow heavy.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Paul, at 1 Corinthians 10:4, makes the most programmatic typological identification in the New Testament: "they drank from the spiritual Rock that followed them, and the Rock was Christ." Paul draws on the Jewish legend of Miriam's well (the "rock that followed") and identifies Christ as that rock. This is arguably the New Testament's most explicit case of Jewish midrashic tradition being adopted as Christian typology. Patristic: Origen (*Homilies on Exodus* 11) develops the rock-as-Christ typology systematically and the Amalek-as-vices allegory. Ambrose in *On the Mysteries* 8.48 and in his *On the Sacraments* ties the rock to baptismal water and to the wound in Christ's side from which blood and water flowed. Augustine (*City of God* 16.43; *Tractates on John* 28) follows Paul. Cyril of Alexandria works the same typology.

On Amalek: Origen reads Amalek allegorically as the soul's vices; the raised hands of Moses as the sign of the cross; Aaron and Hur as the law and the prophets supporting the cruciform prayer. Justin Martyr (*Dialogue with Trypho* 90–91) may have been the first to draw the cross-shape typology from Moses' raised hands; it becomes standard by the fourth century. Gregory of Nyssa (*Life of Moses* 2.148–153) reads the scene ascetically as the soul's perseverance in prayer against the passions.

Medieval: Aquinas (*ST* III q. 46 a. 4 ad 1) cites Moses' raised hands as a type of Christ's arms extended on the cross. Bernard preaches the rock as the pierced Christ. Reformation: Luther in his Lectures on Exodus reads the rock christologically, following Paul but rejecting allegorical excess. Calvin's commentary on Exodus 17 is more reserved — the rock typology is apostolic (Paul), so Calvin affirms it, but he resists extending allegory beyond what apostolic usage authorizes.

Critical: Martin Noth on the Massah/Meribah tradition as a cultic legend attached to a particular spring. Gerhard von Rad on the war narrative and holy war ideology. Brevard Childs on the composite unity of the chapter. Walter Moberly (*At the Mountain of God*) on the complex relation between Exodus 17 and Numbers 20. Propp's philology. Brueggemann on the political theology of Amalek as paradigm of the predator. Jon Levenson has written on the moral difficulty of the command to blot out Amalek.

**Convergence and divergence.** Jewish tradition reads water from rock as ongoing pedagogy of trust and (via Miriam's well legend) as a continuing gift accompanying Israel through the wilderness. Christian tradition reads it typologically, with Paul's 1 Corinthians 10:4 as scriptural warrant. The Amalek episode is read by both traditions as the emergence of evil as a recurring theological problem. Jewish tradition maintains the paradigmatic identification (Amalek = unprovoked wickedness, recurring in Haman) as liturgically alive; Christian tradition more often allegorizes Amalek into the vices or into the spiritual warfare typology. Both traditions register discomfort with the literal command to exterminate a people, and both handle it by pushing identification out of the ethnically concrete: for Jewish tradition through the halakhic dissolution of identifiable Amalekites after mass population admixture; for Christian tradition through allegorization. Modern critical scholarship, particularly from Brueggemann, Levenson, and ethical readers, has pressed the hard question of whether the canonical identification of a people-group for extermination is morally tolerable and what it does for subsequent biblical ethics.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The hermeneutics of Moses' raised hands.** When Moses lifts his hands, Israel prevails; when he lowers them, Amalek prevails (v. 11). Is this magical intercession (the hands as effective instrument), symbolic prayer (the hands as reminder), or Christological type (the hands as cross-shape)?

- Catholic (CCC 2577; Aquinas *ST* II-II q. 83 a. 12, *ST* III q. 46 a. 4 ad 1): intercessory prayer genuinely participates in God's action through the economy of grace; Moses' raised hands are typological of Christ's cross, which is the substantive reality the type participates in. The raised hands are not magical but they are effective, because the grace of Christ works backward through time through figural anticipation.
- Reformed (Calvin on Exodus 17; Vos *Biblical Theology*): Moses' raised hands are prayerful intercession; the efficacy is God's action in response to prayer, not the hand as instrument. Calvin rejects any quasi-magical construction.
- Lutheran (Luther's Lectures on Exodus): Moses' hands are a sign of the Word and prayer; Aaron and Hur's support teaches the need for mutual aid in the church's prayer life. Luther resists the cross-typology as speculative beyond Scripture's warrant.
- Wesleyan/Arminian: Moses' raised hands dramatize persistent intercession; the pattern is paradigmatic for the church's prayer life.
- Orthodox (John of Damascus; Schmemann): the raised hands are *orans* posture, the primordial Christian prayer position still used liturgically. The whole scene is proto-liturgical.
- Jewish (Mekhilta on Amalek; Bavli Rosh Hashanah 29a, "Did Moses' hands make war or break war?"): explicitly not magical. Moses' hands teach Israel to look upward; victory comes from submission of heart to the Father in heaven. Rashi follows this line. The rabbinic tradition is emphatic in rejecting the magical reading — perhaps precisely because the narrative could be read that way.
- Critical (Noth, Childs, Propp): the scene reflects archaic elements (the staff, the hill, the raised hand) perhaps drawn from older battle-rite traditions; the canonical narrator uses them to make a theological point about dependence.

The least intuitive position is the rabbinic one articulated most sharply in Rosh Hashanah 29a. The Gemara's "did Moses' hands make war or break war?" is a pointed polemic against a magical reading that seems natural from the surface of the text. Steelmanned: the rabbis read the text canonically within Torah — YHWH's victories are not accomplished by human ritual efficacy but by Israel's subjected heart; therefore the text must mean that Moses' hands function as the visible instrument by which Israel is reminded to trust, not as a mechanism generating divine action. The gain is theological consistency; the cost is accepting that a plain reading of the text has to be qualified by the larger canonical logic. The patristic cross-typology is the opposite instinct — take the visible sign and push it into maximal figural interpretation.

**Where this leaves us:** The text presents the raised hand as genuinely correlated with victory without explaining the mechanism. The traditions diverge on whether to read the correlation magically (no tradition does), pedagogically (the rabbis), intercessorily (the Reformers and Luther), or typologically (the patristic and Catholic tradition); they converge in denying mere magic.

**Tension 2: The perpetual war against Amalek.** Verse 14: "I will utterly blot out the memory of Amalek from under heaven." Verse 16: "The LORD will have war with Amalek from generation to generation." Deuteronomy 25:17–19 codifies this as commandment. 1 Samuel 15 applies it. How do the traditions hold the enduring theological claim together with the moral horror of commanded genocide?

- Catholic (CCC 2258, 2314 on protecting human life; Aquinas *ST* II-II q. 64 on killing; Ratzinger *Jesus of Nazareth*): the commandment belongs to the specific covenantal history of Israel and is not a standing norm for the church. The figural reading (Amalek as vice, as principality) becomes the operative Christian reading; the literal commandment is historically bound.
- Reformed (Calvin on Exodus 17 and 1 Samuel 15; John Frame *Doctrine of the Christian Life*): the Amalek commandment is part of the unique theocratic/covenantal economy of Israel, bound to the particular redemptive-historical moment. It is not transferable to the modern state or any contemporary people group.
- Lutheran (Luther's Lectures on Exodus; Bonhoeffer *Ethics*): distinguishes between God's left-handed work of judgment and the church's proper work. The Amalek commandment is God's work, not the church's mandate.
- Wesleyan/Arminian: emphasizes the progressive moral trajectory of revelation — the earlier severity gives way to fuller disclosure in Christ of enemy-love.
- Orthodox (Gregory of Nyssa, *Life of Moses* 2.91–95): hermeneutically allegorize. Amalek is the vices; the war is spiritual; the command is perpetual against the passions.
- Jewish (Maimonides *Hilchot Melakhim* 5–6; Meiri; later halakhic authorities): the commandment to blot out Amalek is codified but practically dissolved by the historical fact that no extant people group can be identified as Amalekite after the Assyrian and Babylonian population mixings (Sanhedrin 96b on Sennacherib's mixing of nations). Amalek remains as moral paradigm (evil that attacks the vulnerable from behind, Deuteronomy 25:18) and as liturgical memory (Purim, reading Deuteronomy 25:17–19 on Shabbat Zachor before Purim). Contemporary Jewish thinkers — Levenson, Walzer, Sagi — have engaged the question directly and with moral seriousness.
- Critical (Brueggemann; Phyllis Trible on "texts of terror"; Jon Levenson): the texts are morally difficult. Critical scholarship insists that the texts cannot be softened away but must be read with the moral question alive. Brueggemann's move is to read Amalek as the paradigmatic predator — the one who attacks the stragglers — and the commandment as a refusal to normalize predation, while acknowledging the hermeneutical danger.

The least intuitive position for most contemporary Western readers is the literal rabbinic halakhic one: that the commandment was a standing obligation until it was practically nullified by the demographic history of the ancient world, and that it continues as liturgical and moral memory. Steelmanned: this reading takes the text seriously as command, refuses to allegorize it away, and accepts the burden of living with texts that the tradition itself has labored over for two millennia. The gain is textual honesty; the cost is the continuing weight of morally difficult material in the liturgy.

**Where this leaves us:** The Amalek texts are the hardest cases in Exodus for moral reading. The traditions converge in refusing contemporary ethnic application (no one credibly identifies any modern people as Amalekite). They diverge on whether the primary move is allegorical (patristic, Orthodox), covenant-historical limitation (Reformed, Catholic), or liturgical-moral retention (Jewish).

*Unresolved question:* The chapter ends with "the LORD will have war with Amalek from generation to generation" (v. 16). Who is the subject of the war? If it is YHWH's own war — as the grammar allows — then the text claims God's ongoing judicial action against predatory evil, not Israel's commandment for perpetual combat. But then the Deuteronomy 25 commandment to Israel cannot be simply read off of Exodus 17:16. What is the exact relation between "YHWH's war" and "Israel's commanded war" — are they the same war differently described, or are they two things the tradition has conflated? The text does not resolve this, and the tradition has handled it variously.

## The Thread

- **Numbers 20:1–13, Moses strikes the rock at Meribah-Kadesh.** The doublet. Water from the rock again, but this time with disobedience and consequence: Moses strikes rather than speaks, and loses his entry to the land. The two episodes together form a frame across the wilderness narrative — water at the beginning, water at the end, both naming Meribah. Brevard Childs treats the pair as a theological inclusio on the wilderness generation's testing.
- **Psalm 95:7–11, "Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts, as at Meribah, as on the day of Massah."** The Psalter picks up the chapter directly, making Massah and Meribah paradigms of unbelief. Hebrews 3:7–4:11 cites Psalm 95 extensively as a warning to the church. The wilderness testing becomes the standing type of Christian perseverance.
- **Deuteronomy 33:8–11, the blessing of Levi.** "You tested [Levi] at Massah, with whom you quarreled at the waters of Meribah." The Levitical priesthood is tied to the Massah/Meribah testing — an obscure allusion that requires this chapter for context. The wilderness testing proves Levi and becomes part of the foundation of the priesthood.
- **1 Samuel 15 and Esther.** The Amalek thread is unusually long. Saul's failure to execute the ban on Amalek and Agag (1 Samuel 15) is the pivot on which his kingdom is lost. Haman "the Agagite" in Esther is read by the rabbinic tradition as the descendant of the Amalekite king Saul spared — Purim is a liturgical resolution of 1 Samuel 15's unfinished business, and both reach back to Exodus 17. The chapter seeds a narrative thread that runs six or seven hundred years through the canon.
