# 1 Samuel 20: The New Moon Feast, the Arrow Code, and the Stone of Ezel

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter takes place across two institutional settings: the **new moon feast** at Saul's court at Gibeah, and the **field at the stone of Ezel** outside the city. Each is precise in its cultural-material grounding.

**The new moon (rosh chodesh, רֹאשׁ חֹדֶשׁ).** Numbers 28:11–15 prescribes the new moon as a regular sacrificial day with two bulls, one ram, seven lambs, and a sin offering. The day was a public feast and a kind of monthly sabbath, attested across the Hebrew Bible (Amos 8:5, Hosea 2:11, Isa 1:13–14, 2 Kings 4:23) and well into Second Temple practice. At Saul's court, the new moon meal was a state-cultic event—the king and his cabinet dining together at the temple-adjacent hall, the meal itself a religious act requiring ritual purity (cf. Saul's reasoning in v. 26: "He is not clean"). The royal table was the locus where political loyalty was performed. Court officials ate with the king to demonstrate alignment; an empty seat was a political statement. The Mari archives, the Ugaritic banquet texts, and the Persian-period documentation of royal feasts (e.g., Ahasuerus's banquet in Esther 1) all show this convention. David's absence from the table is a defection in advance of declared exile.

**The seating arrangement** (v. 25) is described with surprising precision: Saul sat on his customary seat against the wall (*ha-yashav 'al moshavo*); Jonathan sat opposite (*lefi*, "before/facing him"); Abner sat at Saul's side. The arrangement places Jonathan and Abner as the two principal officers, flanking the king from the front and the side. **Lawrence Stager** has reconstructed Iron Age dining hall practice on the basis of architectural studies at Megiddo and Tel Dan; the wall-seat (a built bench against the longest wall) was the king's place, with subordinate seating arrayed in front. The arrangement is structural, not improvised, and the empty seat in such a configuration would be visible to every diner.

**The "stone heap" or "stone of Ezel."** The MT *etzel* (אֵצֶל) literally means "beside" or "the side of," not a place name; the verse reads "remain beside the stone *etzel*" or simply "remain beside the stone over there." The LXX reads *to ergab ekeino* ("that mound" or "that pile"), suggesting the LXX translator read the Hebrew as a place name *ha-argav* or *ha-egel*. Standing stones (*matzevot*) are well attested in Iron Age cultic and territorial contexts—the Gezer standing stones, the Tel Dan high place, the cult-stones at Arad. The stone may be a known landmark cultic feature outside Gibeah. Avi Ofer's surveys of the Judean hills have catalogued numerous standing-stone installations near Iron Age settlements that might preserve this kind of memory.

**The archery code.** Jonathan's signal mechanism (vv. 20–22) reflects realistic ancient practice for distance communication. Composite bows of the period had effective ranges of 150–200 meters; an arrow shot wide of a target and recovered by a runner was a normal training scenario. The code's plausible deniability is the point: a watching observer would see only ordinary archery practice, while the verbal signal to the boy ("on this side" / "beyond you") carried the prearranged meaning. Comparable signaling conventions appear in Greek warfare (Thucydides describes various concealed signals) and in the Lachish letters (where coded references appear).

**The "yearly clan sacrifice" (zevach ha-yamim, זֶבַח הַיָּמִים)** invoked as Jonathan's cover story (v. 6) is a real institutional category: the *zevach mishpachah*, the family/clan annual sacrifice, attested also at 1 Sam 1:21 (Elkanah's pilgrimage to Shiloh) and frequently in Deuteronomy. Each *mishpachah* (clan/extended family) maintained an annual sacrifice site, often at a local high place. The cover is plausible because Saul knows this institution exists and would not initially question David's clan obligation.

Three Hebrew terms repay attention. First, *chesed YHWH* (חֶסֶד יְהוָה, "the steadfast love of the LORD," v. 14)—the technical term for covenant faithfulness, the loyalty that obligates parties to a covenant relationship. **Nelson Glueck**'s 1927 dissertation *Das Wort hesed* and **Katharine Sakenfeld**'s *The Meaning of Hesed in the Hebrew Bible* (1978) are the foundational studies. *Chesed* is the bond-keeping virtue; Jonathan asks David to extend this *chesed* to Jonathan's house "forever" (a request David will fulfill in 2 Sam 9 with Mephibosheth). Second, *ben-na'avat ha-mardut* (בֶּן־נַעֲוַת הַמַּרְדּוּת, v. 30)—Saul's stunning insult to Jonathan, conventionally rendered "you son of a perverse, rebellious woman." The Hebrew is grammatically unusual; *na'avah* is from the root meaning "to be twisted, perverted," and *mardut* means "rebellion." The construction makes Jonathan's mother (Ahinoam) the target of the insult, but in honor-shame culture the wound lands on Jonathan: his existence as Jonathan is being delegitimized. Third, *higdil David* (הִגְדִּיל דָּוִד, v. 41), conventionally "David exceeded" or "David made [it] greater"—the verb form is enigmatic. The phrase has been read as "David wept the most" (so ESV) or "David made [the parting] great" or even "David made [his] [expression of grief] great." The Hebrew refuses to specify, leaving the parting opaque in a way the rest of the scene is not.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is one of the most carefully constructed in the Deuteronomistic History. Its structure pivots between three settings: the field for the covenant (vv. 1–23), the court for the new moon meal (vv. 24–34), and the field for the parting (vv. 35–42). The chiastic mirror is precise: the field is the locus of bond-making in the first movement and bond-loss in the third, with the court as the disclosing center.

**Robert Alter** in *The David Story* notes that Jonathan's protest (v. 2: "Far from it! You shall not die. Behold, my father does nothing... without disclosing it to me") is the chapter's most painful piece of denial. Jonathan's filial confidence has not yet absorbed what the chapter will teach him: his father will keep this from him precisely because he knows Jonathan loves David. The chapter is in part the story of Jonathan's loss of his father, parallel to David's loss of Saul as patron.

The arrow-code mechanism is a literary device of considerable elegance. The pre-arranged signal allows the narrative to display **the moment of communication** as well as the message itself. When Jonathan calls after the boy "Is not the arrow beyond you?" (v. 37), David hears the death sentence on his Israelite life. **Fokkelman** in his volume on chapter 20 maps the speech-economies: most of the chapter is dialogue, and the dialogue is between two characters who are speaking to each other and at the same time speaking past each other, because Jonathan persistently underestimates Saul's resolve and David persistently understates his own danger. The arrow-code is the only moment in the chapter where the language is precise, because the language has been pre-coded.

The **new moon dinner episode** (vv. 24–34) is a small masterpiece of dramatic irony. Saul sits and assumes ritual impurity; the reader knows it is flight. Saul asks gently on the second day; the reader knows the second day is the trigger. Jonathan answers with the prepared cover story; the reader knows it has been engineered. Saul's explosion (v. 30) is the moment when the king's actual political logic—usurpation by son-in-law and son-in-law's ally—surfaces unmistakably. The insult to Jonathan's mother is a deflection of shame about Jonathan's failure to inherit; Saul attacks Jonathan's lineage because he cannot bear what Jonathan's choice means about his own dynastic prospects. The spear-throw at Jonathan (v. 33) is the chapter's pivot: Saul is now willing to kill his own son. The narrative pattern of Saul-as-spear-thrower has crossed an unmistakable line.

**Polzin** treats the chapter as the formal conclusion of the History of David's Rise's first major movement. Up to chapter 19, David's flight could be understood as an emergency response to a momentary royal pathology; after chapter 20, the rupture is permanent. The arrow code operates as a literary marker of the irrevocable.

Textual variants: Chapter 20 is one of the more textually unstable chapters in 1 Samuel. The MT and LXX diverge in numerous small details, and the famous *higdil David* of v. 41 has at least three credible reconstructions. **4QSam-b** preserves fragments showing a shorter form in places. The Old Latin manuscripts preserve readings closer to the LXX. McCarter's Anchor Bible apparatus is the standard parallel-column treatment.

A literary device worth marking: **the broken oath of v. 6**. Jonathan's cover story for David requires Jonathan to lie to his father. The lie is structured around a real institution (the clan sacrifice) but its content is false. The narrator does not comment on Jonathan's lie any more than on Michal's lie in chapter 19. The Deuteronomistic narrator is depicting how the people who serve YHWH's anointed must operate against the surface of YHWH's currently-established (but rejected) authority—including by lying to it. The moral texture of this is left for the reader.

The **threefold prostration of v. 41** ("David rose from beside the stone heap and fell on his face to the ground and bowed three times") deploys the diplomatic prostration formula attested in the Amarna letters, where vassal kings prostrate themselves before the suzerain "seven and seven times" or three times. David's threefold bow before Jonathan implicitly recognizes Jonathan as his sovereign while the parting itself transfers sovereignty—a conflicted gesture that performs the chapter's central paradox. The royal heir formally bows out (Jonathan goes into the city, the place of Saul's failed kingship), and the new king bows three times to the old (David rises from the stone, and goes into the wilderness, the place of his own future).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical thread of this chapter is the **descendant clause**. Jonathan's request (vv. 14–15) and the renewed covenant (vv. 42) bind David to Jonathan's offspring "forever" (*'ad 'olam*, עַד עוֹלָם), and this provision will be honored when David takes the lame Mephibosheth (Jonathan's son) into his court in 2 Samuel 9. The descendant clause is dynastically significant: it defends the Saulide line from the customary post-coup elimination that ANE political practice would have demanded. David's *chesed* to Mephibosheth is the chapter's payoff a generation later. The covenant established at the stone of Ezel functions as the legal-ethical framework for the entire subsequent David-Saulide relationship.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory**: **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 6.225–243) retells the chapter at length, downplaying the affective intensity (the kissing, the weeping) and emphasizing the political structure (the covenant, the descendant clause). Josephus is uncomfortable with the corporeal display and Greco-Romanizes it into dignified parting. The rabbinic tradition treasures the parting scene. **Pirke Avot** (above) returns to the David-Jonathan covenant as the paradigm of unconditional love; **Sanhedrin** discusses the legal validity of Jonathan's covenant against the Saulide royal interest. **Rashi** glosses *higdil David* as "David wept more"—he wept more than Jonathan because David was leaving everything (court, wife, friend) while Jonathan was only losing one. Rashi's reading personalizes the asymmetry. **Radak** treats the new moon insult ("son of a perverse rebellious woman") as Saul's attack on Ahinoam, but reads Jonathan as taking the blow for his mother and bearing it. **Ralbag** philosophically: Saul's insult is the externalization of his own self-loathing about his lineage's coming end, displaced onto the woman who bore the heir. **Abarbanel** is sharpest on the political reading. He argues that the new moon scene reveals Saul's transition from private hostility to public state policy. By insulting Jonathan publicly at the royal table and throwing the spear at his own heir, Saul has lost the support of his court, and the cabinet now knows that Jonathan and Saul are at odds. Abarbanel reads this as the moment when the Saulide dynasty becomes politically untenable: a court that watches a king attempt fratricide of his heir cannot hold. He treats the field-covenant as a constitutional act—Jonathan and David are formally constituting the post-Saulide political order in advance, with Jonathan as the legitimate heir voluntarily submitting to David as the divinely chosen successor. **Zornberg** reads Saul's spear-throw at Jonathan as the moment when paternal devotion is exposed as conditional on filial conformity to the father's political project. **Zakovitch** maps the doublings between this covenant scene and the earlier covenant of 18:3–4. **Garsiel** catalogues the verbal echoes between Jonathan's *chesed YHWH* request and the later 2 Samuel 9 fulfillment.

**Christian interpretive trajectory**: **Origen** allegorizes Jonathan and David's parting as the soul's separation from temporal goods for eternal devotion to Christ. **Augustine** reads the descendant-clause covenant as the type of God's covenant with the elect, binding posterity to mercy. **Ambrose** in *De Officiis* book 3 makes the parting at Ezel the model for Christian friendship in adversity—the friend whose loyalty extends through political danger and personal cost is the Christian moral ideal. **Aelred of Rievaulx** in *Spiritual Friendship* makes Jonathan and David's parting his single most extended scriptural example: the kissing, the weeping, the covenant, all read as the form of *amicitia spiritualis* sealed before God. **Aquinas** treats the descendant clause as instance of *fidelitas*—the virtue of keeping promises across time. **Calvin**, in his *Sermons on 1 Samuel*, reads Jonathan's *chesed* request as the type of believers' intercession for their offspring before the throne of grace. Calvin returns repeatedly to the contrast between Saul's spear-throwing fatherhood and Jonathan's self-sacrificial sonship—the chapter shows fatherhood broken and friendship raised in its place. Luther's lectures emphasize the courage required of Jonathan in defending David before his father, treating it as the model for Christian witness against unjust authority.

**Critical**: Wellhausen identified the chapter as composite, with the field-covenant material possibly originating separately from the new moon material. McCarter (AB) provides the standard apparatus and notes that the chapter's textual instability is unusual even for Samuel. Klein (WBC) catalogues the Hebrew vocabulary. Halpern in *David's Secret Demons* reads the chapter as apologetic: David's exile is constructed to demonstrate that he was driven out by Saul's irrational hostility, not by his own ambition. The covenant with Jonathan is engineered as the witness that David did not seek the throne but had it pressed upon him by the legitimate heir himself. **McKenzie** in *King David: A Biography* reads the chapter as part of the apologia's construction.

**Contemporary**: Brueggemann (Interpretation) reads the chapter as the establishment of the covenantal relationship between the two main lines of Israelite kingship—the chosen line (David) and the rejected line (Saul-Jonathan), with the chosen line covenanted to preserve the rejected line. The chapter's theology is one of magnanimous succession. Polzin reads the chapter's new moon scene as the narrator's most concentrated piece of dramatic irony: every line of dialogue is inflected by what the reader knows and the characters do not. **Eugene Peterson** in *Leap over a Wall* reads the parting as a meditation on the cost of vocation: David must accept exile to receive his calling, and Jonathan must accept that his love costs him his family. **Davis** offers expository reading. **Goldingay** treats the chapter's covenant as a model for the ethical seriousness of friendship under God. **Feminist** readers note the absence of women in the chapter (Ahinoam is mentioned only as the target of Saul's insult; Michal does not appear; the women whose love defined chapter 18 are wholly silenced) and the all-male ritual economy of the new moon court. **Ackerman** in *When Heroes Love* makes chapter 20 her single most extended exhibit for the homoerotic surface reading: the hidden meeting in the field, the covenant ratified by oaths, the kiss, the weeping, the deliberately ambiguous *higdil David*—the accumulated texture, on Ackerman's reading, can no longer be read as merely covenantal-political. **Jennings** is similarly direct. The covenantal-political reading (McCarter, Halpern, Olyan) treats the kissing as the conventional Near Eastern parting kiss between covenant partners (cf. Gen 31:55, 33:4) and the *chesed* language as treaty-loyalty terminology. **Frymer-Kensky**'s synthetic reading remains influential: the registers are not exclusive.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**First tension: The ethics of deception in service of the LORD's anointed.** Jonathan lies to his father (v. 6) to protect David. Michal lied in the previous chapter. The narrative pattern of the LORD's people lying to the legitimately appointed (but rejected) king is conspicuous and uncommented.

*Position 1 (situational ethics, classical Christian):* Lying to protect innocent life from unjust authority is morally permissible (and possibly required) under the prudential application of moral law. The traditional examples include Rahab lying to the king of Jericho (Josh 2), the Hebrew midwives lying to Pharaoh (Exod 1), and Jonathan and Michal here. The narrator's silence is endorsement; the lies are defensible because the authority being deceived has forfeited its moral standing.

*Position 2 (strict moral law, Augustinian/Kantian):* Lying remains intrinsically wrong even in service of good ends. Augustine's *De Mendacio* and Aquinas (*Summa* II-II q. 110) hold that no end justifies a false statement. The narrator's silence is not endorsement; the lies are recorded as part of the moral cost of the political situation, not as model behavior. Jonathan and Michal sin (perhaps minor sin) in lying, even while their cause is just.

*Position 3 (descriptive-narrative, Brueggemann):* The narrator is not in the business of moral instruction at this level. The text is depicting how the providential transfer of kingship operates through morally compromised actions by all parties. The lies are part of the narrative texture; reading for direct moral application misframes the genre.

Steelman of 1: The text gives us multiple cases of YHWH-favored characters lying to dangerous authority (Rahab, the midwives, Michal, Jonathan, Abraham) without narratorial comment. The pattern is too broad to be coincidental. The implicit narrative ethics is that lying to lethal authority to protect innocent life is an acknowledged moral category, not a sin to be expiated.

*Where this leaves us:* All three positions illuminate. The strict reading (Position 2) preserves moral law's seriousness; the situational reading (Position 1) honors the narrative pattern; the descriptive reading (Position 3) keeps us from over-deriving general principles from depictions. The pastoral implication is that the narrative gives us cover for hard cases without giving us license. Jonathan's deception is at the limit of what the text will represent without comment, and the limit is not specified.

**Second tension: The theology of "the LORD has sent you away" (v. 22).** Jonathan's pre-arranged signal frames David's exile as YHWH's commissioning, not as flight from human enemies. What is the theology of providential exile?

*Position 1 (providentialist, classical Reformed):* David's exile is YHWH's instrument for forming the future king. The wilderness years are formative; David must learn dependence, leadership, and trust away from court. The exile is not punishment or accident but pedagogy. Jonathan correctly perceives the LORD's sending in what looks like Saul's persecution.

*Position 2 (theodical, mediating):* The exile is the convergent product of YHWH's overruling providence and Saul's free pathological hostility. YHWH does not cause Saul to throw spears, but YHWH does shape the resulting flight into the formative experience David needs. The theology is one of providential adaptation rather than direct sending.

*Position 3 (apocalyptic-tragic, Brueggemann/Gunn):* "The LORD has sent you away" is Jonathan's faith-statement, not a metaphysical claim the narrator is endorsing. Jonathan is naming the situation theologically because he has no other resources; the chapter does not in fact tell us that YHWH sent David out. The theology is one of human meaning-making in extremis rather than divine determinism.

Steelman of 3: The chapter never has YHWH speak. The Spirit does not appear, the prophet does not pronounce, the divine word does not come. Jonathan provides the theological frame because someone must, and Jonathan loves David. Reading "the LORD has sent you away" as objective theology rather than as devout interpretation may overload the text.

*Where this leaves us:* Position 3 is exegetically rigorous; Position 1 captures the canonical trajectory (the exile *will* turn out to be formative); Position 2 mediates. The reader is left with the question whether divine purpose is something the narrator pronounces or something the characters discern in faith. The chapter's reticence about direct divine action makes the discernment-question primary.

*Unresolved question:* Why does Jonathan stay? If David's cause is YHWH's cause, and Jonathan recognizes it (v. 13: "May the LORD be with you, as he has been with my father"), why does Jonathan return to the city of Saul rather than join David's flight? Jonathan's choice to remain with the rejected dynasty when he could join the chosen one is left without explanation. He will die at Gilboa with his father (1 Sam 31), faithful to the doomed kingdom. The chapter's theology of vocation includes Jonathan's choice to be faithful to a falling house, but does not explain or commend or condemn it.

## The Thread

The descendant clause (vv. 14–15, 42) is the chapter's longest forward arrow, finding its fulfillment in 2 Samuel 9 (David and Mephibosheth) and reverberating in 2 Samuel 21:7 (David spares Mephibosheth from Gibeonite execution "because of the oath of the LORD that was between them, between David and Jonathan the son of Saul"). The new moon institution will continue across the canon: Numbers 28:11–15 (priestly law), 2 Kings 4:23 (the Shunammite's husband expects new moon worship), Hosea 2:11 (prophetic critique of new moon ritualism), Colossians 2:16 (Paul on new moon observance in Christian freedom). Jonathan's *chesed* request becomes the David-Saulide template that will define David's relationship to surviving Saulides through 2 Samuel 21. The arrow-code's hidden communication will not recur, but its theological structure—God's people speaking in coded ways to one another while seeming to speak ordinarily—is the underlying form of much subsequent prophetic communication. Saul's spear-throwing at Jonathan formally extends the spear-motif beyond David to his own son, completing the ironic structure of Saul's weapon against his own house. Cross-references: Genesis 31:55 (Laban's parting kiss with Jacob, the prior covenantal-parting type-scene); Numbers 28:11–15 (the new moon legislation); 2 Samuel 1:26 (David's lament over Jonathan, the covenant's grief); 2 Samuel 9 (the Mephibosheth fulfillment); 2 Samuel 21:7 (the oath remembered). Flag forward: chapter 21 will trace David's first stop in the Nob priesthood, his deception of Ahimelech, and the consequences that will detonate in chapter 22's massacre of the priests—the unfolding of the political hostility that this chapter has now formalized.
