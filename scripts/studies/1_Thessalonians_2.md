# 1 Thessalonians 2: Nurses, Fathers, and the Letter's Hardest Verses

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 2 lives inside a specific argumentative situation: Paul has been forced out of Thessalonica, his converts are under social and possibly physical pressure, and someone — opponents in the synagogue, suspicious Gentiles, or pagan rivals — is apparently telling the Thessalonians that the missionary who blew through their city was a fraud. Chapter 2 is Paul's defense of the *original* mission. Reading it well requires reconstructing what the accusations might have looked like.

The political backdrop is the same as Chapter 1's: Thessalonica as the capital of Roman Macedonia, a *civitas libera* governed by *politarchēs* (the Vardar Gate inscription, IG X 2,1 no. 126, and parallel Macedonian inscriptions confirm the title), positioned on the Via Egnatia, dominated by imperial cult and civic religion. Acts 17:1–9 reports that opponents accused Paul before the politarchs of "acting against the decrees of Caesar" by proclaiming "another king, Jesus." Paul left under pressure; Jason posted bond. Months later, writing from Corinth, Paul addresses the social aftermath of that flight.

The accusations Paul anticipates and rebuts in 2:1–12 fit a recognizable Hellenistic profile. Abraham Malherbe (*Anchor Yale Bible*, 2000) showed at length that Paul's vocabulary in this passage — "error" (*planē*), "impurity" (*akatharsia*), "deceit" (*dolos*), "flattery" (*kolakia*), "pretext for greed" (*prophasis pleonexias*), "seeking glory" (*doxa*) — maps almost word-for-word onto the standard Cynic-Stoic list of accusations leveled against bad itinerant philosophers. Dio Chrysostom's *Oration 32*, addressed to the Alexandrians, reads as a near-mirror of Paul's defense: the *true* philosopher does not flatter, does not seek glory, does not aim at money, does not exploit his audience. Paul is using a familiar rhetorical genre — the apologia of a serious teacher against the suspicion of being a charlatan — and adapting it to apostolic ministry. He works *night and day* (2:9) precisely so the charge of greed has no traction.

Material culture matters here. Paul's tentmaking (*skēnopoios*, Acts 18:3) was the labor of an artisan, low-status by elite Greek standards but respectable within an artisanal household. Greek philosophical paraenesis often denigrated manual labor; Paul reverses the valence. The "nurse" (*trophos*, 2:7) and "father" (*patēr*, 2:11) metaphors draw on Greco-Roman household imagery: the nurse was often a slave or lower-status woman entrusted with the daily care of upper-class children, sometimes with deep affective bonds; the father was the authoritative figure responsible for moral formation. Paul uses both metaphors of himself, which is striking — they are not parallel; they are sequential, each capturing something different about apostolic work.

Then there is 2:14–16. The historical situation behind these verses is the most contested in the letter. Paul refers to "the Jews who killed both the Lord Jesus and the prophets," who "drove us out" and "hinder us from speaking to the Gentiles," and announces that "wrath has come upon them at last" (*eis telos*). The Greek phrase admits multiple temporal readings — "to the uttermost," "at last," "completely," or "finally." If the phrase refers to a concrete historical event, the leading candidate has often been the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70, which forces a late date for the letter (incompatible with the Gallio dating) or an interpolation hypothesis. Birger Pearson argued in 1971 (Harvard Theological Review) that 2:14–16 was a post-70 interpolation; that thesis once had wide support but is now a minority position. Most current scholars (Wanamaker, Malherbe, Bockmuehl, Still, Gilliard) accept Pauline authenticity and read *eis telos* and the wrath language proleptically, eschatologically, or in reference to events earlier than AD 70 — Caligula's threatened temple desecration in AD 40, the Claudian expulsion of Jews from Rome (AD 49), or the Tiberian banishment in AD 19.

Authorship is universally accepted as Pauline; co-authorship with Silvanus and Timothy. Dating remains c. AD 50–51 from Corinth. Manuscript witnesses — P46 (c. AD 200), Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Alexandrinus, Bezae — preserve the chapter; there is no manuscript evidence supporting interpolation, which is part of why Pearson's thesis has lost ground.

**Key Greek terms.** *Nēpioi / ēpioi* (νήπιοι / ἤπιοι, 2:7) — "infants" vs. "gentle"; the central textual variant of the chapter. *Parrēsia* (παρρησία, 2:2) — "boldness," the Greek civic virtue of frank speech, prized in the philosophical tradition. *Trophos* (τροφός, 2:7) — "nurse / nursing mother." *Patēr* (πατήρ, 2:11) — "father." *Orgē eis telos* (ὀργὴ εἰς τέλος, 2:16) — "wrath to the uttermost / at last." *Thlipsis* (θλῖψις, throughout) — "affliction, tribulation," the eschatological pressure of the present age.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: paraenetic apologia. Chapter 2 is Paul's defense of his ministry written in the vocabulary of Hellenistic moral exhortation. Malherbe's signal contribution was to show that Paul's apologia in 2:1–12 is not idiosyncratic but follows a recognizable convention: the serious itinerant teacher rebutting the charlatan's profile. Paul lists what he was *not* (deceitful, flattering, greedy, glory-seeking) and what he *was* (gentle, hardworking, holy, righteous, blameless, fatherly), and he does so in the cadence of antithetical pairs — the *peristasis* lists familiar from Stoic moral writing.

The single most-discussed literary feature of the chapter is the textual variant in 2:7. The manuscript evidence splits between *egenēthēmen nēpioi* ("we became infants / babes among you") and *egenēthēmen ēpioi* ("we became gentle among you"). The variant arises from a single letter — the *n* shared between *egenēthēmen* and the following word. The earliest and best witnesses (P65, Sinaiticus*, Vaticanus, Alexandrinus, Bezae*, Ephraemi) support *nēpioi*. The Byzantine tradition and many later witnesses support *ēpioi*. Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary* gives *nēpioi* a B rating. The NA28 and UBS5 critical editions print *nēpioi*. The ESV translates as "gentle" — implicitly preferring *ēpioi* on contextual grounds, since the surrounding metaphor is a nursing mother and "we became infants among you, like a nursing mother" is awkward.

Most modern commentators (Fee, Wanamaker, Gaventa, Malherbe accept *nēpioi* as the harder reading and read it as a deliberate paradox: Paul is claiming to be both *infant* and *nurse* — the apostle vulnerable as a child and tender as a mother. Gordon Fee in particular (NICNT, 2009) defends *nēpioi* and argues the mixed metaphor is intentional — Paul refuses both the lordly philosopher's pose and the patron's pose, claiming an unselfconscious tenderness. Other commentators (Bruce, Marshall) prefer *ēpioi* as fitting the maternal metaphor cleanly. The variant matters because it shapes Paul's self-presentation: vulnerable equal vs. caring guardian.

Literary devices: antithesis (2:3–6), maternal metaphor (2:7), paternal metaphor (2:11), recall formulas ("you yourselves know," "you remember," "you are witnesses" — 2:1, 2:5, 2:9, 2:10, 2:11), prophetic indictment (2:15–16), lament rhetoric (2:17–20). The chapter contains the letter's second thanksgiving (2:13), part of the unusual triple-thanksgiving structure that prompted Schmithals' partition theory and that most current scholars (Wanamaker, Malherbe, Fee, Green) read as evidence of unity rather than seam.

The chapter's position in the letter's argument: after Chapter 1's portrait of model converts, Chapter 2 turns backward to reconstruct the original mission. This serves both pastoral and rhetorical ends. Pastorally, it strengthens the converts against accusations being made about Paul. Rhetorically, it grounds Paul's later exhortations (Chapters 4–5) in his own established credibility. Chapter 2 ends (2:17–20) with the plaintive note that Paul has been "torn away" from them and that "Satan hindered" his return — language that sets up Chapter 3's account of Timothy's mission.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** John Chrysostom's *Homilies on 1 Thessalonians* devote considerable attention to Chapter 2; he reads Paul's nurse and father metaphors as the model of the bishop's pastoral office and treats 2:14–16 as a vindication of the gospel against Jewish opposition. Chrysostom's eight *Adversus Iudaeos* homilies (delivered separately in Antioch c. AD 386–387) are notorious for their anti-Jewish vehemence; their interpretive frame inflected the patristic reception of 2:14–16, often unfairly. Theodoret of Cyrus and Theodore of Mopsuestia followed Chrysostom in treating the passage straightforwardly as Pauline indictment of Jewish opposition to the gospel.

Augustine drew on the chapter primarily for ecclesiology — the church as imitating the Judean churches (2:14) — but inherited and transmitted the patristic frame on 2:14–16. Aquinas in *Super Epistolas* read the passage within scholastic categories of culpability and providence, distinguishing the proximate human agents from divine permission. Calvin's commentary (1551) interpreted 2:14–16 as Paul's straightforward diagnosis of Jewish unbelief and divine judgment, in line with his broader covenant theology in which historical Israel's rejection of Christ is part of the larger drama of election.

Luther had no full commentary on 1 Thessalonians, but his later anti-Jewish writings (especially *On the Jews and Their Lies*, 1543) cast a long shadow over Lutheran reception of 2:14–16. The passage was repeatedly conscripted, in pre-modern and modern Christian history, into supersessionist polemic — a use that the post-Holocaust church has spent eighty years explicitly repudiating.

Modern critical scholarship is more divided. Birger Pearson's 1971 article ("1 Thessalonians 2:13–16: A Deutero-Pauline Interpolation," *Harvard Theological Review*) argued that the passage was inserted after AD 70, citing the apparent reference to Jerusalem's destruction in *eis telos*, the supposedly un-Pauline tone, and the use of *imitating the churches in Judea* as inconsistent with Paul's own anti-imitation rhetoric elsewhere. Pearson's thesis was extended by other scholars in the 1970s–80s. It has since lost majority support. Charles Wanamaker (NIGTC, 1990) systematically rebuts each Pearson argument: the manuscript tradition shows no sign of interpolation, *eis telos* admits non-AD 70 readings, the inner-Jewish polemic vocabulary is at home in Pauline letters elsewhere (Rom 9–11, 2 Cor 11), and the imitation argument misreads Paul's pastoral logic. Markus Bockmuehl, Todd Still (*Conflict at Thessalonica*, 1999), Frank Gilliard (on the punctuation of 2:15), and Malherbe (*Anchor Yale Bible*, 2000) all defend Pauline authenticity while contextualizing the rhetoric.

The "apocalyptic Paul" school (Beker, Gaventa, Martyn, Campbell) reads 2:16 ("wrath has come upon them at last") within Paul's eschatological framework: divine wrath as the end-time judgment whose advance shadow falls on the present. Beverly Gaventa (Interpretation, 1998) frames 2:14–16 as proleptic apocalyptic announcement, not contemporary historical commentary on AD 70. Imperial-critical readers (Donfried, Koester, Horsley, Harrison) note that the same Roman authorities behind the Thessalonians' suffering are part of the cosmic powers Paul places under judgment.

**Jewish scholarly engagement.** 2:14–16 is the single most contested Pauline passage in Jewish-Christian dialogue. The post-Holocaust Catholic Church explicitly addressed it. Vatican II's *Nostra Aetate* (1965) declared that Christ's death "cannot be charged against all the Jews, without distinction, then alive, nor against the Jews of today." The Vatican's 1985 *Notes on the Correct Way to Present the Jews and Judaism in Preaching and Catechesis*, issued by the Commission for Religious Relations with the Jews, named 1 Thessalonians 2:14–16 as one of the texts requiring careful exegetical handling to avoid anti-Jewish reading. Joint Catholic-Jewish dialogue documents through the 1990s and 2000s have continued to address the passage directly.

The Paul Within Judaism school (Mark Nanos, Pamela Eisenbaum, Magnus Zetterholm, Paula Fredriksen) reads 2:14–16 as inner-Jewish prophetic polemic. Paul speaks *as a Jew* in the tradition of Jeremiah, Amos, and the Stephen speech in Acts 7 — denouncing some of his own people for opposing what Paul takes to be the divinely vindicated gospel, exactly as the prophets denounced their own people for opposing the divinely sent prophets. Krister Stendahl's earlier *Paul Among Jews and Gentiles* (1976) and Lloyd Gaston's *Paul and the Torah* (1987) prepared this reading. Daniel Boyarin (*A Radical Jew*) places 2:14–16 in Paul's apocalyptic framework where ethnic Israel and the church are not yet fully separate categories — Paul is in a family argument, not a religious other-ing. Amy-Jill Levine and Paula Fredriksen press the warning that even if Paul's rhetoric is intra-Jewish, its later Gentile-Christian reception has not been, and the church has to own that reception.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Christian and Jewish scholars now broadly converge on three points: (1) Pauline authenticity is more probable than not; (2) the rhetoric belongs within Jewish prophetic self-criticism rather than Christian-against-Jewish polemic in its original setting; (3) the *use* of the passage in Christian history has been catastrophic and needs explicit ecclesial and exegetical correction. They diverge on whether the rhetoric, even read inside Jewish prophetic convention, can be cleanly retrieved without continuing to wound — Levine and Boyarin are more skeptical, Bockmuehl and Wright more confident.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What is God up to in 1 Thessalonians 2? Paul's account is twofold: God has entrusted the gospel to particular human witnesses (2:4), and God's word — received as God's word and not human speech — is *energeitai*, "at work," in the Thessalonian believers (2:13). The chapter's theological grammar is one of agency: God's, the apostles', the converts', the opponents'. Paul reads his ministry as a divinely commissioned and humanly conducted public act, accountable to God who tests hearts.

**Tension One: 2:14–16 — interpolation, intra-Jewish prophecy, or anti-Jewish polemic?**

The Pearson interpolation reading (Pearson 1971; some critical scholars in the 1970s–80s; partial endorsement by Daryl Schmidt) accepts that the passage as it stands is awkwardly anti-Jewish for Paul and proposes that it was inserted after AD 70 by a scribe whose theology was harder than Paul's. The thesis preserves Pauline integrity by extracting the difficult verses. Its weakness is that no manuscript evidence supports the excision.

The intra-Jewish prophetic reading (Nanos, Stendahl, Gaston, Bockmuehl in part, Wright, Boyarin) accepts Pauline authenticity but reads the rhetoric in the prophetic tradition of self-criticism. Paul, a Jew, denounces some Jewish opposition to the gospel just as Jeremiah denounced the Jerusalem priests, just as Amos denounced the northern kingdom. The rhetoric is fierce but functionally familial. The strength of this reading is that it accounts for Paul's tone elsewhere in his letters (Romans 9–11) where his anguished love for "my kinsmen according to the flesh" coexists with sharp critique. Its weakness is that the *reception* of these verses in later Gentile Christianity has not been intra-Jewish at all.

The post-Holocaust Catholic and mainline Protestant reading (Nostra Aetate, the 1985 Vatican Notes, World Council of Churches statements, Christian-Jewish dialogue commissions) accepts Pauline authenticity, contextualizes the rhetoric, and *also* explicitly repudiates the supersessionist conclusions historically drawn from the passage. This position holds the text and the church's ethical responsibility for the text together.

**Steelmanning Pearson.** The interpolation thesis is the position I find least intuitive, because the manuscript evidence does not support it. But Pearson is not silly. He is taking seriously a real exegetical disquiet: "wrath has come upon them at last" reads, on the surface, as a *verdict* — and a final verdict, in Paul's mouth, on his own people, is hard to square with Romans 9–11's anguished open-ended hope. Pearson's intuition is that something is genuinely off, and rather than letting Paul author it, he extracts it. The strongest version of his case is not that the manuscripts support him but that the *theology* doesn't. That deserves to be sat with even after one rejects the interpolation thesis.

**Where this leaves us:** Most current scholarship reads 2:14–16 as authentic Pauline rhetoric within an inner-Jewish prophetic register, while the post-Holocaust church explicitly repudiates the supersessionist conclusions historically drawn from it. The rhetoric belongs to Paul; its *use* in later Christian history is the church's responsibility to disown.

**Tension Two: Apostolic ministry — nurse and father, equal and authority.**

The Catholic reading (CCC §§874–896 on apostolic succession, *Lumen Gentium*) finds in Paul's nurse / father language a theology of episcopal pastoral office: the bishop as gentle mother and authoritative father, in continuity with the apostles, is part of the ordained structure of the church. The Eastern Orthodox reading (Zizioulas, *Being as Communion*, 1985; Schmemann's liturgical theology) frames apostolic ministry within Eucharistic ecclesiology — the bishop as iconic of Christ in the gathered community, the liturgical assembly as the historical continuity of the apostolic faith.

Reformed and Lutheran readings emphasize the priesthood of all believers and read Paul's metaphors more functionally than ecclesiologically: the *gentleness* and *fatherly exhortation* are pastoral virtues incumbent on every Christian leader, not the marks of a sacerdotal office. Calvin in particular reads 2:7–12 as a model of pastoral conduct binding on every minister of the word. Anabaptist readings (the radical reformation, later free-church traditions) have used 2:1–12 as a critique of clerical hierarchy, finding in Paul's manual labor and refusal of "demands" a charter for non-stipendiary ministry.

Wesleyan and Pentecostal-charismatic readings emphasize the *power* dimension: Paul's gentleness coexists with the Spirit's energy, and the model apostolic minister is one whose tenderness is the visible side of Spirit-filled life.

**Steelmanning the Catholic-Orthodox sacramental reading.** Many Protestant readers, especially in low-church traditions, find the move from "Paul's pastoral metaphors" to "the structure of the episcopate" too quick. But the Catholic-Orthodox case is not arbitrary. Paul *does* claim authority "as apostles of Christ" (2:6); he *does* speak of being "approved by God to be entrusted with the gospel" (2:4); he *does* rest his exhortation on a recognized commission. The sacramental traditions read these as the seedbed of an ordered ministry that does not float free of historical continuity. The strongest version of their case is that *some* such ordered structure is needed to preserve apostolic teaching across generations, and that the New Testament itself — Paul, the Pastorals, Acts — leans this way.

**Where this leaves us:** Paul models authority and tenderness as a single posture. Whether that posture is best preserved by an ordained sacramental ministry, by Reformed pastoral office, by free-church plural eldership, or by charismatic recognition is a debate the New Testament does not adjudicate cleanly.

**The unresolved question.** If 2:14–16 is authentic and intra-Jewish prophetic, what is the church to do *now* with rhetoric whose later reception has done such damage — read it, redact it, retire it, or sit with it in lament?

## The Thread

**Acts 17:1–9 and Philippians 1:30.** The "shamefully treated at Philippi" of 2:2 maps to Acts 16's account of the Philippian beating and imprisonment, and the Philippian church remembers that suffering decades later (Phil 1:30, "having the same conflict"). Paul's apologia in Chapter 2 is the testimony of a man whose body remembers Macedonia.

**Romans 9–11.** Paul's most extended treatment of Jewish unbelief, written four or five years later, opens with "great sorrow and unceasing anguish in my heart" for "my kinsmen according to the flesh" (Rom 9:1–3) and ends with "all Israel will be saved" (Rom 11:26). 1 Thessalonians 2:14–16 must be read with Romans 9–11 in the same field of vision; the same author is responsible for both registers.

**Jeremiah 26 and Acts 7.** The prophetic tradition Paul echoes in 2:15 ("who killed both the Lord Jesus and the prophets") draws on Jeremiah's denunciation of those who killed the prophets and on Stephen's speech in Acts 7. These are intra-Jewish denunciations spoken by Jews in the prophetic register, the convention Paul stands inside.

**1 Corinthians 4:14–15 and Galatians 4:19.** Paul's parental metaphors recur. In 1 Corinthians 4 he is again "father" to converts in admonishment; in Galatians 4 he is "in the anguish of childbirth" until Christ is formed in them. The cluster suggests Paul reaches for parental imagery whenever the bond is under strain — a tic of the apostle's pastoral imagination.
