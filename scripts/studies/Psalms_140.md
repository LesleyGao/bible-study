# Psalm 140 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 140 carries the superscription *la-menatzeach mizmor le-David* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד, "to the choirmaster, a psalm of David") and stands as the third psalm in the **final Davidic cluster** (Pss 138-145, the second-to-last collection of Davidic psalms in the Psalter, gathered before the closing hallel-cascade of 146-150). After 138's thanksgiving and 139's omniscience-meditation, the cluster pivots into a sequence of laments and pleas (140, 141, 142, 143), and Psalm 140 is the first of them. The mood shifts abruptly: from "you have searched me and known me" to "deliver me, O LORD, from evil men."

In Gunkel's form-critical taxonomy, Psalm 140 is a clear **individual lament** (*Klagelied des Einzelnen*) with a strong **imprecatory** component. The standard lament structure is fully present: invocation (*Yahweh*), petition (vv. 1-5), complaint about enemies (vv. 2-5), expression of trust (vv. 6-7), imprecatory petition (vv. 8-11), and concluding affirmation of confidence (vv. 12-13). The threefold *Selah* punctuation (after vv. 3, 5, 8) gives the psalm a clear three-stanza meditative structure suggestive of liturgical chant with cantorial pauses.

Dating is uncertain. Some scholars (notably Hossfeld and Zenger) read 140 alongside 141-143 as a deliberately edited late-exilic or post-exilic compilation in which the lament voice of "David" is curated to model the persecuted righteous community's prayer. Goldingay and Wenham allow for older liturgical material reaching back into pre-exilic temple worship. The mention of "battle" (v. 7) and the enemy-language can fit either a royal-military setting or the metaphorical-theological idiom of post-exilic spiritual struggle.

Hebrew vocabulary is striking. The opening petition *chaltzeini Yahweh me-adam ra* (חַלְּצֵנִי יְהוָה מֵאָדָם רָע, v. 1) uses the verb *chalatz* — "to draw out / pull free / rescue" — the same verb used in Lev 14 for stripping plaster off a leprous house. The deliverance imagery is one of *extraction*, of being pulled out of an entanglement. *Mei-ish chamasim* (מֵאִישׁ חֲמָסִים, "from the man of violences," v. 1, plural-intensive) — *chamas* is the same noun that names the abundant violence which provoked the Flood (Gen 6:11), and which the prophets (Hab 1:2-4, Mic 6:12) repeatedly indict.

The most famous image of the psalm is in v. 3: *shanenu lashonam kemo-nachash chamat akhshuv tachat sefatemo* (שָׁנֲנוּ לְשׁוֹנָם כְּמוֹ־נָחָשׁ חֲמַת עַכְשׁוּב תַּחַת שְׂפָתֵימוֹ, "they sharpen their tongue like a serpent; the venom of an asp is under their lips"). The serpent imagery deliberately echoes the Eden serpent (Gen 3:1) and the Mosaic *nachash* of Numbers 21. *Akhshuv* — the asp / horned viper / Egyptian cobra — is a hapax legomenon, occurring only here in the entire Hebrew Bible. The hapax intensifies the rhetorical force: the slander of the wicked is so distinctively venomous that it requires a word that occurs nowhere else.

Verse 7 contains the title *Yahweh Adonai oz yeshu'ati* (יְהוָה אֲדֹנָי עֹז יְשׁוּעָתִי, "O LORD, my Lord, the strength of my salvation") — the Tetragrammaton joined to *adonai* in a doubled-divine-title rare in the Psalter and signaling solemn covenantal address. *Sakkota le-roshi be-yom nashek* (סַכֹּתָה לְרֹאשִׁי בְּיוֹם נָשֶׁק, "you have covered my head in the day of battle") — *nashek* is "weapon / armament," giving the line a martial concreteness; the *sakhakh* verb is the same "covering / sheltering / weaving" verb of Ps 139:13.

The imprecation block (vv. 9-11) is severe: *yamot rasha ra'ah* (יָמוֹת רָשָׁע רָעָה, "let evil hunt down the violent / wicked man," v. 11, with *yamot* read as polel of *yamut* or as cognate of "to bring down"); *gachalei esh yapeil aleihem* ("let burning coals fall upon them"), *be-mahamorot bal-yaqumu* ("into miry pits, no more to rise"). The burning-coals imagery echoes the Sodom-and-Gomorrah judgment (Gen 19:24), and the *mahamorot* (miry pits, deep waters) is a common Sheol-imagery for cosmic descent into death's domain.

ANE parallels include the Mesopotamian *šurpu* and *maqlû* incantation series, in which the petitioner asks the gods to redirect curses back upon the slanderer. The Egyptian "Letter to the Dead" tradition similarly invokes divine retribution against false accusers. What is distinctive in Psalm 140 is the explicit linkage of speech-violence (vv. 2-3) and physical violence (v. 1's *ish chamasim*) — the wicked are *both* violent men and venomous tongues, and the psalm refuses the modern bifurcation between speech and action.

Textually, Psalm 140 is reasonably well preserved. 11QPsa contains fragments. The LXX (139) follows the MT closely, though it occasionally smooths the more difficult Hebrew syntax. The Targum reads several phrases through the lens of Saul-and-David narratives, attaching the psalm to the historical David's persecution.

## Lens 2: The Literature in Front of Us

The psalm divides into four movements punctuated by the threefold *Selah*:

1. **Vv. 1-3**: complaint about violent men whose tongues are venomous (Selah)
2. **Vv. 4-5**: complaint about violent men who set traps (Selah)
3. **Vv. 6-8**: trust-statement and petition that the wicked not be exalted (Selah)
4. **Vv. 9-13**: imprecation followed by confidence-conclusion

The structure mirrors the threefold escalation common to lament: complaint, refined complaint, prayer-with-imprecation, resolution. The two opening complaint stanzas (vv. 1-3 and 4-5) are nearly parallel in structure — each opens with a deliverance-petition, names the *violent men*, and closes with an image of their evil device (venomous tongue / hidden trap). This is **structural anaphora** in service of intensification.

Lowth's parallelism varieties are richly present. **Synonymous**: "deliver me, O LORD, from evil men // preserve me from violent men" (v. 1). **Climactic**: vv. 4-5 build from "guard me from the hands" to the elaborated imagery of cords, nets, snares — three hunting-image variants stacked. **Antithetic**: vv. 12-13 oppose *afflicted/needy/righteous/upright* (the worshiper's class) to the wicked of the imprecation. The psalm consciously operates within the wisdom-Psalter's two-ways framework (cf. Pss 1, 37, 73).

The most rhetorically intense literary moment is the **chiasm of speech and violence** in vv. 2-3: "they plan evil things in their *heart* / they stir up *wars* / they sharpen their *tongue* / venom is under their *lips*" — heart > wars > tongue > lips. The chiasm moves from interior (heart) to physical-military (wars) and back to organs of speech (tongue, lips), enacting the very fusion of speech and violence the psalm protests.

The **imprecation-then-confidence** sequence (vv. 9-13) is one of the most emotionally violent juxtapositions in the Psalter. The petitioner moves from "let burning coals fall upon them" (v. 10) directly to "I know that the LORD will maintain the cause of the afflicted" (v. 12). The juxtaposition is not edited away. The Hebrew literary mind does not separate the indignation from the trust; both are facets of a single faithfulness.

## Lens 3: The Psalm Within the Larger Story

**Book V placement.** Psalm 140 stands as the third psalm in the final Davidic cluster (138-145), and it begins the lament-sequence (140-143) at the heart of the cluster. The flow is liturgically deliberate: thanksgiving (138) → omniscience-meditation (139) → laments (140-143) → war-prayer (144) → doxology (145). The cluster traces the full pastoral arc of one who has been delivered, who marvels at God's knowing presence, but who is now beset by enemies and must learn again to plead.

**Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 140 connects it explicitly to the historical David's persecution by Saul and by Doeg the Edomite (1 Sam 21-22), reading the *ish chamasim* as Doeg the slanderer. Rashi follows this Davidic-historical reading, identifying the venomous tongues with Doeg's false report to Saul. Ibn Ezra is more cautious about specific historical anchoring. Radak emphasizes the universal-typological reading: the psalm gives voice to all the persecuted righteous in every generation. In modern Jewish scholarship: Heschel reads the psalm's speech-violence theology as paradigmatic for prophetic indictment of *lashon hara* (the "evil tongue"); Levenson notes the imprecation's covenantal logic — the worshiper does not seek private revenge but invokes divine justice against those who have despised the God of justice. The psalm is recited in some Sephardic and Hasidic *seder ha-tachanunim* (penitential supplication) cycles, particularly in contexts of communal persecution.

**Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on 139 (Vulgate numbering of MT 140) reads the psalm as the prayer of the church-as-Christ's-body against the diabolical persecution that targets the head and members alike; the venom of the asp is read typologically against Genesis 3, with Christ as the new Adam whose heel is bruised by the serpent. Cassiodorus picks up the typology. Aquinas, in his Psalms commentary, treats the imprecation as pertaining properly to the eschatological judgment, not as a private wish for harm. Luther's *Operationes* attends to the verbal-violence dimension and reads the psalm pastorally against the false accusations Luther himself faced. Calvin reads the imprecation as the prayer of the Spirit-led believer against those whose violence is *primarily* anti-divine, not primarily personal.

Among modern critical scholars: Gunkel classifies it as *Klagelied des Einzelnen*; Mowinckel hypothesizes a cultic setting in which the falsely accused petitioner sought sanctuary at the temple and prayed this psalm; Westermann places it in his lament-typology; Hans-Joachim Kraus reads it in conjunction with 141-143 as an editorially shaped lament-sequence; Hossfeld and Zenger develop the social-justice reading — the *afflicted* and *needy* of v. 12 are the post-exilic poor whose voice the editor has lifted up; Goldingay's commentary attends to the speech-violence theology; Wenham reads the imprecation through his "ethical formation" frame; Brueggemann reads the psalm as one of the great "psalms of disorientation"; Patrick Miller emphasizes the prayer-as-resistance dimension; William P. Brown attends to the venom-imagery as poetic theology; Beth Tanner reads it as the cluster's first turn into petitionary lament.

**NT use.** Psalm 140 has one of the more famous NT citations: **Romans 3:13**, where Paul cites 140:3 ("the venom of asps is under their lips") in his catena of universal sinfulness, alongside Pss 5, 14, 36, 53, Isa 59, and others. The Pauline use is typological-anthropological: what the psalmist said about the wicked enemies, Paul applies to *all flesh* in their state apart from grace. This is one of the Psalter's most theologically generative cross-Testament citations, contributing to the Reformation's *total depravity* doctrine and to the universalist reach of Pauline soteriology. James 3:1-12, which thematizes the destructive power of the tongue ("a restless evil, full of deadly poison"), develops the same speech-violence theology that Psalm 140 voices.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent — Tensions, Positions, and Where This Leaves Us

**Tension 1: Speech-violence and contemporary social-media reception.** Psalm 140 makes no distinction between physical violence and verbal violence — *ish chamasim* (man of violences) is named as a synonym of the one whose tongue is *kemo nachash* (like a serpent). For modern readers, this raises a contemporary moral-ethical question: how seriously should we take the wounding-power of speech?

*Position A: Strong continuity (rabbinic tradition on *lashon hara*, Christian moral theology on the eighth commandment, contemporary advocates against online harassment).* Slander, mockery, and dehumanizing speech are real moral evils with real victims; Psalm 140 names this rightly. The contemporary social-media environment, where speech can mobilize crowds and destroy reputations at scale, vindicates the psalm's diagnosis. *Steelman:* this reading honors the long Jewish tradition that *lashon hara* kills three (the speaker, the listener, and the one spoken about); it grounds robust pastoral and ethical engagement with verbal abuse.

*Position B: Distinction-with-prudence.* While verbal violence is real and morally serious, the psalm's *response* — calling down burning coals — should not be conscripted to legitimize present-day verbal escalation. Imprecation belongs to God's eschatological judgment, not to the contemporary church's rhetorical arsenal. *Steelman:* this position takes seriously both the moral seriousness of speech and the New Testament's reframing of enemy-treatment (Matt 5:44, Rom 12:14).

*Position C: Critical of imprecation-as-pattern (some pacifist and progressive readings).* The psalm gives voice to a real human cry but should not be normalized as a model of Christian prayer; we read it descriptively, not prescriptively. *Steelman:* this preserves the integrity of the love-of-enemies command while allowing the Psalter to function as a faithful record of human anguish.

**Tension 2: The Pauline use and the Reformed *total depravity* doctrine.** Romans 3:13's citation of Ps 140:3 sits at the heart of Reformation soteriology.

*Position A: Reformed (Calvin, the Heidelberg Catechism, the Westminster Confession).* The Pauline citation establishes that what the psalm says of the wicked is in fact true of all human beings apart from grace; the speech-violence diagnosis is universal; this grounds the doctrine of total depravity and the necessity of grace.

*Position B: Catholic / Eastern Orthodox / Wesleyan-Arminian.* The Pauline citation establishes universal sinfulness but does not entail the more specific Reformed claims about the *non-posse non peccare* state of fallen humanity; the psalm's language about *the wicked* is partially universalized in Paul without being collapsed into a metaphysics of total inability.

*Position C: Newer Pauline studies (E. P. Sanders, N. T. Wright, James Dunn).* The Pauline catena, including the Ps 140 citation, functions in Romans 3 as part of an argument about *Israel's* failure under Torah, not as a universal anthropology in the later Reformed sense; the citation is in the service of a larger covenantal argument about what God has done in Christ Jesus. *Steelman:* this reading honors the Pauline rhetorical context, takes seriously the corporate and covenantal frame, and resists projecting later Reformation categories back onto the apostolic citation.

**Where this leaves us.** Psalm 140 names a reality the modern world re-discovers every news cycle: the violence of tongues, the planning-in-the-heart that escalates into wars, the snares set on the road. The psalm refuses two modern temptations. It refuses to dismiss verbal violence as merely-symbolic; the *ish chamasim* and the *nachash*-tongued slanderer are the same person. And it refuses to be optimistic about the possibility of self-help against such enemies; the response is *deliverance from* the LORD, not strategic counter-speech.

The closing affirmation — *yodati ki ya'aseh Yahweh* (יָדַעְתִּי כִּי־יַעֲשֶׂה יְהוָה דִּין־עָנִי, "I know that the LORD will maintain the cause of the afflicted," v. 12) — locates the worshiper's confidence not in his own innocence or in the eventual exposure of the wicked but in *Yahweh's character* as the one who executes justice for the needy. The psalm's resolution is not the wicked's defeat but the worshiper's renewed knowing of who Yahweh is.

**Closing question.** Psalm 140 puts violent speech and violent action on the same moral plane — both are *chamas*. In an information age that has industrialized speech-violence, what does it look like to pray Psalm 140 honestly, naming the venom without ourselves becoming venomous? Where does the petitioner's prayer for deliverance leave room for the worshiper to *not* become what he has been delivered from?
