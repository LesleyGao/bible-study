# 1 Samuel 16: The Anointing in Bethlehem and the Spirit Reversed

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter sits at a hinge in Israelite political history. Saul, the Benjaminite warlord whose kingship was extracted from a reluctant Samuel under popular pressure (chs. 8–12), has been juridically rejected at Gilgal (15:23) but remains de facto sovereign. The Philistine pentapolis on the coastal plain still presses inland through the Shephelah, the foothill region whose limestone valleys (Aijalon, Sorek, Elah) function as natural invasion corridors from the coast toward the central hill country. In this geography, Bethlehem—identified with modern Beit Lahm, six miles south of Jerusalem along the watershed road—is a small clan-based settlement of the tribe of Judah, sitting at roughly 2,500 feet above sea level on the eastern edge of the agricultural terrace zone. Iron Age I and IIA pottery scatters and the Wadi al-Khalil tomb evidence (Greenberg's surveys) suggest Bethlehem was a modest agricultural hamlet during the late eleventh century BCE, the period most scholars assign to the historical David (c. 1010–970 BCE on Albright's "low" chronology, slightly earlier on Kitchen's reconstruction). It is precisely this insignificance that makes it dramatically suitable: Samuel's covert mission to anoint a rival king cannot occur at Gibeah, Saul's tribal seat eight miles north.

The political danger of the chapter is real and material. Samuel's protest in v. 2—"How can I go? If Saul hears it, he will kill me"—reflects the actual mechanics of ancient Near Eastern court politics, where prophets functioned as officeholders within or adjacent to the royal cabinet. The Mari letters (eighteenth-century BCE Old Babylonian, but illustrative of the institutional category) show prophets reporting directly to the king and risking execution when they delivered unwelcome oracles. Saul's later massacre of the Nob priesthood (1 Sam 22) confirms that this anxiety was not paranoia. The "heifer" cover-story (v. 2) is plausible cultic camouflage: zebach (זֶבַח) sacrifices at local high places (bamot) were normal in the pre-temple period, predating the Deuteronomic centralization that would later define them as illegitimate.

The material culture of the anointing scene is precise. The "horn of oil" (qeren ha-shemen, קֶרֶן הַשֶּׁמֶן) refers to a hollowed animal horn, typically ram or ibex, used as a portable container for refined olive oil—archaeologically attested in Iron Age contexts at Tel Dan, Megiddo, and Lachish. Anointing oil was probably perfumed with myrrh and cinnamon (cf. Exod 30:23–25), expensive imports from Arabia and Sri Lanka respectively. The act of pouring oil on the head was the standard West Semitic ritual for transferring office, attested at Ugarit and in Hittite installation ceremonies; it physically marked a man as set apart and bound divine favor to him.

Three Hebrew terms repay attention. First, *mashach* (מָשַׁח, "to anoint") is the verbal root from which *mashiach* (מָשִׁיחַ, "anointed one"; transliterated into Greek as *christos*) derives—David is now, technically, the messiah of YHWH, though the eschatological freight that term will later carry has not yet attached. Second, *admoni* (אַדְמוֹנִי, v. 12, "ruddy") describes David's complexion. The same word appears in Genesis 25:25 to describe Esau, the rejected elder brother. The narrator is doing something deliberate with vocabulary: David is type-cast in Esau's color (red-haired, perhaps freckled, certainly outdoorsy) while occupying Jacob's structural role (younger brother chosen over older). Third, *tsalachah* (צָלְחָה, v. 13, "rushed upon"), used of the Spirit's descent on David, is the same verb earlier applied to the Spirit's descent on Saul (10:6, 10; 11:6); the narrator is signaling continuity and replacement simultaneously. The verb has connotations of forceful penetration; it is used elsewhere of an army crossing a river (2 Sam 19:18). The Spirit does not gently settle—it invades.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

This chapter inaugurates what Leonhard Rost in his foundational 1926 monograph *Die Überlieferung von der Thronnachfolge Davids* identified as the **History of David's Rise** (HDR), running from 1 Samuel 16 through 2 Samuel 5. Rost argued the HDR was an originally independent composition designed as apologia, defending David's accession against the obvious charge that he was a Saulide usurper. P. Kyle McCarter's 1980 Anchor Bible commentary refined this thesis by cataloguing the apologetic motifs: David is repeatedly chosen by YHWH, repeatedly refuses to harm Saul, repeatedly mourns Saul's house, and is repeatedly absent during the deaths of Saul's kin. Chapter 16 is the source's prologue, and every detail bears the weight of that argumentative function.

The genre is theological court narrative with strong folktale architecture. Robert Alter, in *The David Story* (1999), draws attention to the **type-scene of the youngest son chosen**: David inherits the structural slot of Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, and Gideon. The "seven sons pass; the eighth is summoned from the field" pattern is folkloric—seven is the conventional set, and the supernumerary eighth is the unexpected hero. Yet Alter notes that the narrator subverts the expected sympathy: we are told nothing of David's interiority, no dream, no struggle, no protest. He is simply produced from the pasture as the LORD's choice, and Samuel anoints him. The opacity is itself a literary device. Meir Sternberg's *Poetics of Biblical Narrative* (1985) treats this as the inauguration of David's characteristic narrative "depth"—we will spend the next sixty chapters trying to read him.

The pivotal verse is 16:7—"the LORD looks on the heart" (*ki ha-adam yir'eh la-einayim, va-YHWH yir'eh la-levav*). Polzin (*Samuel and the Deuteronomist*, 1989) calls this the programmatic theological key to the entire book of Samuel, the criterion against which both Saul and David will be measured throughout. Yet there is a deep literary irony embedded immediately: when David is finally produced (v. 12), the narrator describes him precisely in terms of his appearance—ruddy, beautiful eyes, handsome. We have just been told that the LORD does not look at appearances; we are then told what David looks like. Fokkelman, in volume two of his four-volume *Narrative Art and Poetry in the Books of Samuel* (1986), argues this tension is intentional: the narrator wants us to see what Samuel cannot see (the heart) and what Samuel does see (the body), and to feel the disjunction. David is chosen for his heart but presented to us through his face. The ambiguity will haunt the rest of the David narrative.

The chapter divides cleanly into three movements: vv. 1–13 (anointing), vv. 14–23 (Saul's torment and David's arrival at court). The architectural symmetry is striking. In the first movement, the Spirit comes upon David. In the second, the Spirit departs from Saul. The transfer is not explicitly causal—the text does not say Saul lost the Spirit *because* David received it—but the juxtaposition forces the reader to draw the connection. David Gunn (*The Fate of King Saul*, 1980) describes this as the hinge of Saul's tragedy: the same divine giving that elevates one man pulls another down. Gunn's reading, which treats Saul as a genuine tragic figure rather than a moral cautionary tale, depends heavily on this structural observation.

A literary device worth marking is what Sternberg calls the **gap of motivation** in v. 14b. The narrator says "a harmful spirit from the LORD tormented him." No explanation is given for why YHWH should send such a spirit; the reader is left to assemble theology from the bare juxtaposition with v. 13. Bar-Efrat (*Narrative Art in the Bible*, 1989) treats this as one of the most aggressive narrative gaps in the Hebrew Bible.

Textual variants here are real but modest by Samuel's standards. 4QSam-a, the Qumran scroll that frequently sides with the Old Greek against the MT, preserves only fragmentary text for chapter 16; what survives mostly agrees with MT. The LXX shows minor differences—it explicitly identifies the "harmful spirit" as *pneuma ponēron para kyriou* ("an evil spirit from the Lord"), preserving the theological scandal rather than softening it. Some Lucianic recension manuscripts ease the embarrassment by interposing language about divine permission, but these are clearly secondary. The major textual storm is reserved for chapter 17.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical arc launched here is the messianic line of Judah, traced from the dying Jacob's blessing (Gen 49:8–12: "the scepter shall not depart from Judah") through Ruth (which exists, on one reading, primarily to legitimate David's Moabite ancestry) and forward to the genealogies of Matthew 1 and Luke 3. The author of Chronicles, writing in the Persian period, will retell this anointing episode (1 Chron 11–12) with all of the embarrassing material about Saul scrubbed out. The Davidic covenant (2 Sam 7), the royal psalms (Pss 2, 89, 110, 132), the prophetic expectation of a Davidic shoot (Isa 11:1; Jer 23:5; Ezek 34:23), and ultimately the New Testament identification of Jesus as *Christos*—all flow from the act in 16:13.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory**: The rabbinic tradition is fascinated by the heifer ruse and what it implies about prophetic permissibility. Pesachim 8b discusses whether Samuel's statement in v. 2 was deception (and concluded permissible, since YHWH explicitly authorized the cover). Rashi reads v. 7 with characteristic restraint, glossing "the heart" as "the inward intention," and emphasizes that David's election is not arbitrary but reflects what YHWH knew of his future fidelity. Radak (David Kimhi, twelfth–thirteenth century Provence) is more interested in the harmonization problem: how did Samuel grieve so deeply over Saul (v. 1) when 15:35 already said he did so? Radak resolves the tension by treating the whole sequence as continuous mourning, broken only by divine command. Ralbag (Gersonides, fourteenth century) reads philosophically: the "harmful spirit" is a melancholic humor, a natural psychological condition that YHWH permits as the consequence of Saul's loss of providential support, not a literal demonic agent. **Abarbanel** (Don Isaac Abarbanel, fifteenth-century Iberian exile and statesman) reads the chapter with a politician's eye. His commentary on v. 2 dwells on the mechanics of regime change: he argues Samuel's fear is reasonable and that the heifer ruse should be understood as legitimate political tradecraft, not mere sleight of hand. Abarbanel notes that David's anointing happens "in the midst of his brothers" (v. 13)—he asks why the brothers, knowing what they have just witnessed, do not later honor or even acknowledge David's election (cf. their hostility in 17:28). His answer: they did not in fact know what the anointing meant, because Samuel did not announce its purpose. The anointing was a covert investiture, not a coronation. **Avivah Zornberg**, in her Jungian-inflected readings, reads the rejected sons (the seven who pass before Samuel) as figures of the unchosen self, the brothers David must internally surmount as much as outwardly displace. **Yair Zakovitch** treats the chapter as the seed of the David-Esau typology that the narrator will develop ironically across the entire Davidic corpus. **Moshe Garsiel** (*The First Book of Samuel: A Literary Study*, 1985) catalogues the chiastic patterns and the deliberate echoes between Saul's election (chs. 9–10) and David's anointing—both involve a prophet's mission, a search, a rejection of the obvious candidate, and the descent of the Spirit—structuring the comparison the reader is meant to draw.

**Christian interpretive trajectory**: **Origen**, in his *Homilies on 1 Samuel*, reads v. 7 christologically: the LORD's preference for the heart over the appearance prefigures the Pauline distinction between flesh and spirit (2 Cor 5:16). On the harmful spirit, Origen treats it as a permitted demonic affliction rather than a direct divine sending; YHWH's "from" (mi-, מִ-) is permissive, not causative. **Augustine**, treating Saul's torment in *City of God* book 17, distinguishes between the spirit of prophecy that left Saul and the foreign spirit that filled the resulting vacuum: the empty house gets occupied (cf. Matt 12:43–45). **Ambrose**, in *De Officiis Ministrorum*, treats David's election as the paradigm of meritorious humility: the shepherd boy is chosen because he was faithful in small things, and Ambrose makes David the model for Christian episcopal humility. **Chrysostom**, preaching on Saul, develops the theme of a king who lost his kingship by losing his self-mastery; the harmful spirit is the divine ratification of an interior collapse already accomplished. **Gregory the Great**, in the *Moralia*, reads Saul's case as the archetype of a soul abandoned to its own appetites, with the harmful spirit being God's withdrawal felt as torment.

**Reformation**: **Calvin**, in his *Sermons on 1 Samuel* (preached 1561–1562, recovered and edited in modern editions), wrestles directly with the theological scandal of v. 14. He rejects any reading that softens the divine sovereignty: God does send the harmful spirit, and the spirit does what God commands. But Calvin draws a careful distinction between sending (which is divine) and willing-as-good (which is not): the harmful spirit acts according to its own malignant nature, but its commission and limits are entirely set by YHWH. This is essentially the theological structure of Job 1–2. Luther, in his lectures, reads Saul's torment as an instance of Anfechtung—the spiritual assault from which no believer is exempt—but warns that Saul's failure to seek refuge in the word of God is what distinguished his case from David's later episodes of distress.

**Critical**: Wellhausen treated 1 Sam 16 as part of the late "prophetic" stratum, contrasting it with the older anointing-by-acclamation of chapters 9–11. Cross's "double redaction" model places it in the pre-exilic Dtr1 layer. McCarter's HDR thesis (above) is now the reigning critical framework. Klein (*1 Samuel*, WBC 1983) provides the most thorough source-critical mapping. Halpern (*David's Secret Demons*, 2001) treats the apologetic function aggressively: every detail of David's election is engineered to deflect the suspicion that he was a Philistine collaborator and Saulide rival, and chapter 16 is the apologia's foundation.

**Contemporary**: Brueggemann (*First and Second Samuel*, Interpretation, 1990) reads v. 7 as the theological pivot of the entire Deuteronomistic History—the LORD operates by criteria invisible to human politics, and this is why the LORD's chosen will repeatedly look implausible. Polzin treats the chapter as the narrator's invitation to readerly suspicion of all monarchic appearances. Eugene Peterson, in *Leap over a Wall* (1997), reads the anointing as a meditation on hiddenness: David is anointed in obscurity, returns to obscurity, and only emerges when summoned. Davis (*Reading Backwards*) and Goldingay treat the chapter as theology of vocation. **Feminist readers** have less material here than in surrounding chapters—the chapter is almost entirely a male world—but Phyllis Trible's broader project on the silenced women of biblical narrative notes the absence of David's mother (named only in the late tradition of Ps 86:16 and rabbinic Nitzevet bat Adael) and Jesse's wife from the family scene. Cheryl Exum (*Tragedy and Biblical Narrative*, 1992) reads Saul's possession as the inauguration of the tragic structure that will dominate the next fifteen chapters.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**First tension: Divine election versus human evaluation.** The programmatic statement "the LORD looks on the heart" raises the question of how YHWH's selection criteria relate to ordinary human moral and political judgment.

*Position 1 (classical Augustinian/Reformed):* Divine election is wholly disjunct from human criteria. YHWH chooses according to his sovereign purpose, not according to the qualifications visible to human eyes. This safeguards grace and disqualifies any merit-based account of vocation. The strongest case for this view is the entire trajectory of David's life: he will commit adultery and orchestrate murder (2 Sam 11), and yet remain "a man after God's own heart" (1 Sam 13:14)—a phrase the chapter requires us to interpret as a statement about election, not character.

*Position 2 (medieval scholastic, Thomistic):* Divine election respects but transcends human criteria. YHWH does see something real in David that humans cannot see—not arbitrary preference, but a deeper moral and spiritual capacity. The "heart" in v. 7 is a genuine evaluative locus, just one inaccessible to ordinary observation. This view preserves human moral seriousness: David is chosen because of who he is, not despite it.

*Position 3 (contemporary literary-theological, Brueggemann):* The verse is less a metaphysical claim about divine omniscience than a polemical disruption of monarchic ideology. It functions to delegitimize the politics of stature, the Saulide politics of "head and shoulders above." It is a programmatic critique of any kingship that derives authority from visible qualification.

Steelman of 3: The verse occurs precisely when Samuel is reproducing the criterion that produced Saul (height, stature). The narrator wants the reader to see Samuel making the same mistake again, and YHWH correcting it. The theological work being done is not metaphysical but political. Brueggemann is right that the verse functions polemically, not just descriptively.

*Where this leaves us:* Each position captures something. Position 1 reads the verse for its trajectory implications (David will fail morally and remain elect). Position 2 reads it for its anthropological implications (something real about David warranted his selection). Position 3 reads it for its rhetorical function (the verse subverts visible-greatness politics). The text does not clearly choose; perhaps the genius of v. 7 is that it generates all three readings precisely because it leaves the divine criterion underspecified. We are told what the LORD does not look at (appearance) without being told what the LORD does look at (the "heart" remains a black box). The negative is precise; the positive is suggestive.

**Second tension: The harmful spirit "from the LORD."** What does it mean theologically for YHWH to send a spirit that torments?

*Position 1 (classical theistic, monistic divine sovereignty):* YHWH is the immediate cause of all that occurs in creation, including the spirits that afflict Saul. The "from the LORD" is precise: this spirit is YHWH's commissioned agent. This view (held in different forms by Calvin, Aquinas, Edwards) preserves divine sovereignty at the cost of attributing moral evil's instrumentation to God.

*Position 2 (permissive/scholastic):* YHWH permits but does not directly cause the harmful spirit. The phrase "from the LORD" indicates divine sanction rather than direct agency. This is the dominant patristic move (Origen) and a major medieval move (Aquinas distinguishes God's permissive will from his preceptive will). It preserves divine goodness at the cost of complicating the text's straightforward "from."

*Position 3 (open theist / process):* YHWH genuinely sends the spirit, but the act is responsive to Saul's prior rejection rather than expressive of a fixed eternal decree. The harmful spirit is the relational consequence of broken covenant, not the execution of a script. This view preserves divine relationality at the cost of classical immutability.

Steelman of 1: The Hebrew is unambiguous—*ru'ach ra'ah me-im YHWH* (רוּחַ רָעָה מֵעִם יְהוָה), "an evil spirit from with YHWH"—and the parallel of 1 Kings 22:19–23 (where YHWH explicitly commissions a "lying spirit" in the divine council) makes it impossible to read this as mere permission. The Hebrew Bible is comfortable with a divine sovereignty that includes the commissioning of harmful agents. Theological discomfort is ours, not the text's.

*Where this leaves us:* The text resists the patristic softening. The harmful spirit really does come from YHWH, and Saul's torment is the theological correlate of his rejection. What we can do is observe that the canonical witness is not univocal: Job's prologue (where the *satan* must seek YHWH's permission) and 1 Chronicles 21:1 (which substitutes "Satan" for "YHWH" as the inciter of David's census, where 2 Sam 24:1 had said "YHWH") show the canonical tradition working through this problem internally, eventually distinguishing the locus of harmful agency more sharply. 1 Samuel 16 sits at the early end of that trajectory.

*Unresolved question:* If the harmful spirit's torment is the manifestation of YHWH's withdrawal, and David's lyre-playing soothes it, what theology of music is the narrator implying? Is music a counter-charisma that substitutes for the absent Spirit, a temporary pacification that does not heal, or a mediated form of the Spirit's continued presence through David? The text refuses to say.

## The Thread

David's anointing here is unconsummated—he will not become king de facto for fourteen years, and the chapter's secret election will be tested through every subsequent pursuit, exile, and near-death. The "Spirit rushed upon David" verb (*tsalachah*) connects this moment forward to the prophetic-ecstatic incident at Naioth in chapter 19, where the Spirit will rush upon Saul one final time, stripping him naked. Compare also: Genesis 25:25 (Esau's *admoni*, ironic typology); 1 Kings 22:19–23 (the divine council and the commissioned lying spirit, the canonical parallel to v. 14); 2 Samuel 7:8 ("I took you from the pasture, from following the sheep"—YHWH's later retrospective on this very moment); 1 Chronicles 11–12 (the Chronicler's sanitized retelling, with chapter 16's awkward material excised); Acts 13:22 (Paul's compressed citation of the Davidic election as "a man after my heart"); Matthew 1:1–17 (the Davidic genealogy whose first link is forged in Bethlehem here). Flag forward: the theme of the chosen-but-hidden king will define the David narrative through 2 Samuel 5 and will return as a structural pattern in the Gospels—Jesus, like David, is anointed in obscurity (the baptism), withdraws to wilderness, returns in power, and is rejected by his brothers (John 7:5). The Bethlehem location is not incidental; the prophet who points to it, Micah (5:2), reads this chapter as a topographical promise.
