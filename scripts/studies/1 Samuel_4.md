# 1 Samuel 4: The Day the Glory Walked Away

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens the **Ark Narrative** — Leonhard Rost's foundational 1926 source-critical proposal that 1 Sam 4:1b-7:1 (joined later with 2 Sam 6) constitutes a discrete pre-deuteronomistic source telling the story of YHWH's ark from Shiloh to Jerusalem. The hypothesis has held up remarkably well; modern critics (McCarter, Antony Campbell in his *The Ark Narrative*, P. D. Miller and J. J. M. Roberts in *The Hand of the Lord*) have refined Rost without overturning him. The narrative is set in the mid-eleventh century BCE, at the height of Philistine military pressure on the Israelite hill country — the period archaeologists associate with the destruction of Iron I Shiloh.

The geography is precise. Israel encamps at Ebenezer; the Philistines at Aphek. Aphek is identified with **Tel Aphek** at Ras el-Ain near the source of the Yarkon River, excavated by Moshe Kochavi from 1972 onward — a major Iron I-IIA site on the strategic Via Maris that controlled the coastal plain. Ebenezer has been more controversial; **Israel Finkelstein**, in collaboration with Bunimovitz, identified it with **Izbet Sartah**, a small Iron I Israelite settlement just east of Aphek, which they excavated in the 1970s. The location fits the narrative — a small Israelite settlement on the eastern edge of the coastal plain, just at the foothills, where the highland tribes confront the lowland Philistine cities. The fact that Israel takes thirty thousand casualties (v. 10) is hyperbolic by archaeological standards (the entire Israelite population of the highlands was probably under 50,000 in this period), but the broader fact of catastrophic Israelite defeat at the hands of Philistine professional infantry is consistent with the archaeological picture of Philistine ascendancy in this period.

The political theology is encoded in the title used for the ark: *aron berit YHWH tseva'ot yoshev ha-keruvim* (v. 4) — "the ark of the covenant of YHWH of hosts, who is enthroned on the cherubim." This is the full Shiloh cult-formula. **Frank Moore Cross** in *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* devoted extensive attention to the *yoshev ha-keruvim* (cherub-throne) theology: YHWH was understood as enthroned invisibly above the kapporet of the ark, with the two cherubim forming a throne and the ark itself functioning as a footstool. This is parallel to ancient Near Eastern royal iconography (Cross, Mettinger, and others have documented Phoenician and Syrian thrones flanked by composite winged creatures). The theological crisis of chapter 4 is therefore not just military: the throne-footstool of the divine king has been captured by his enemies. Either YHWH has been defeated, or YHWH has chosen to abandon his sanctuary.

The destruction of Shiloh — not narrated in 1 Samuel directly, but everywhere implied — is a hard archaeological fact. Finkelstein's excavations at Khirbet Seilun documented a violent destruction layer at Iron I Shiloh dated to the mid-eleventh century BCE, consistent with a Philistine sack in the immediate aftermath of the battle of Aphek. Jeremiah explicitly invokes this destruction (Jer. 7:12-14, 26:6-9): "Go now to my place that was in Shiloh, where I made my name dwell at the first, and see what I did to it because of the evil of my people Israel." Psalm 78:60-64 also: "He forsook his dwelling at Shiloh, the tent where he dwelt among mankind." Both texts treat the loss of Shiloh as YHWH's deliberate judgment — exactly the theological frame the dtr editor of 1 Samuel imposes on chapter 4.

A few Hebrew terms are worth lingering over. **אִיכָבוֹד** (*Ikhavod*, "Ichabod," v. 21) is composed of *i-* (a particle of negation, possibly meaning "where?" or "no") + *kavod* (glory). The traditional rendering "no glory" or "the glory is gone" is the sense the text gives in v. 22, but the etymology is contested: some (McCarter) prefer "alas, the glory!" reading *i* as an interjection. Either way, the name encodes the theological catastrophe of the chapter. **כָּבוֹד** (*kavod*) is the technical term for the divine presence — the same word translated "glory" throughout the Pentateuch (the *kevod YHWH* that fills the tabernacle in Exodus 40:34-35). The departure of the *kavod* is the worst possible thing that can happen to Israel. Ezekiel 10-11 will later describe the *kavod* departing from the Jerusalem temple in echo of this scene. **גָּלָה** (*galah*, "departed/exiled") is not used in v. 21 — the verb there is the more passive *gallah*, "is uncovered/gone." But it is the same root from which *galut* (exile) derives, and the Pentateuch and prophets routinely describe both the people and the divine presence as subject to *galah*. The naming of Ichabod is the ark-narrative equivalent of an exile-formula.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is the opening movement of the Ark Narrative, and its literary structure is built around three parallel reception scenes that each end in catastrophe. First Israel: the ark arrives in the camp, Israel shouts, the earth resounds, Israel is defeated. Second the Philistines: they hear the shout, recognize the ark, are afraid, take courage, fight, win. Third Eli and Phinehas's wife: news arrives, recognition, collapse. The narrator sets up a triple-receipt structure in which each receipt is a misjudgment — Israel thinks the ark guarantees victory, the Philistines think the ark guarantees defeat, only Eli and Phinehas's wife correctly grasp that the ark's capture is the catastrophe itself, regardless of who wins or loses the battle.

The narrative technique is what Bar-Efrat calls **scenic concentration**: the camera focuses on a small set of characters at each receipt, and the pacing slows dramatically as the news of the ark's capture moves from the battlefield to Shiloh. Verse 12 — "a man of Benjamin ran from the battle line and came to Shiloh the same day, with his clothes torn and with dirt on his head" — is one of the most cinematic single sentences in the Hebrew Bible. The runner is given no name, no patronymic; he exists only to bring news. (Some have noted the resonance with the runner in 2 Sam 18, the messenger who brings David word of Absalom's death — Polzin sees this as a deliberate Samuel-narrative motif.)

The chapter is also a study in **inverted formulas**. The phrase "a god has come into the camp" (v. 7) — *ba elohim el ha-maḥaneh* — is what the *Israelites* should be saying. The Philistines say it. The Philistine speech in vv. 7-9 is theologically sophisticated: they remember the Egyptian plague tradition, they know YHWH's reputation, they take the threat with full seriousness — and then they fight anyway. McCarter notes that the Philistine speech in v. 9 — "be men and fight" — uses the verbal form *hithqaslu* and *vihyitem la-anashim* in a self-exhortation that has parallels in ancient Near Eastern military oratory. The Philistine war-speech is more theologically literate than the Israelite war-cry. The narrator is making a sharp implicit indictment: Israel has reduced YHWH to a battle-talisman; the Philistines, who do not even worship YHWH, take him more seriously.

The death of Eli is structured as a literary inclusio with chapter 1. Eli first appears (1:9) "sitting on the seat beside the doorpost of the temple of the LORD" — *yoshev al ha-kisse al-mezuzat heikhal YHWH*. He dies (4:18) "fell over backward from his seat by the side of the gate" — the Hebrew echoes the same posture and the same verb-root (*yashav*, sit). Eli began the book sitting, judging, and misreading; he ends the book sitting, watching, and falling. The narrator notes his weight ("the man was old and heavy") with what Alter calls a "deceptively neutral" detail — the priest who fattened himself on the choicest parts of the offerings (cf. 2:29) is now too heavy to survive the news.

The death of Phinehas's wife is the chapter's most carefully crafted scene. She is unnamed (the narrator gives us her husband's name twice, but not hers), pregnant, going into labor at the moment of national catastrophe. The midwives' attempted comfort — "Do not be afraid, for you have borne a son" — is the formulaic reassurance offered to mothers in childbirth (compare Gen. 35:17 with Rachel's death). She refuses the comfort. She does not even acknowledge the son. She names him *Ichabod* and dies. This is one of the few times in the Hebrew Bible where naming is done by the dying mother; it is the female equivalent of the death-bed prophecy. **Phyllis Trible** in *Texts of Terror* gave this scene a brief but devastating reading: the unnamed woman is the chapter's true theological interpreter, the only character who correctly sees that the loss of the ark is greater than the loss of any individual — including herself, including her husband, including her newborn son.

The textual situation is again significant. The Hebrew text of v. 1 is notoriously short — "and the word of Samuel came to all Israel" hangs without a clear connection to the Ebenezer narrative — and the LXX preserves a longer reading: "And it came to pass in those days that the Philistines gathered for war against Israel." Most critical commentators (McCarter, Klein) accept the longer LXX reading as original; the MT here is haplographically defective.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish readings.** Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.11) tells the chapter with characteristic dramatic embellishment, expanding the Philistine speech and softening Eli's death. The rabbis (Sanhedrin 103b, Yoma 9b) treat the loss of the ark as the paradigmatic national catastrophe and read the chapter as proof that ritual access without ethical fidelity is worthless. Hophni and Phinehas, in rabbinic tradition, become the type of the corrupt clergy whose presence with the holy actually accelerates judgment. Pesikta de-Rav Kahana 8 develops the theme that the ark cannot be manipulated. Rashi works through the text with his usual literalism. Radak attends to the textual problems and notes the LXX variants. Ralbag treats the loss of the ark as a study in how providence works through military defeat. Abarbanel reads the chapter with full political seriousness: the Israelite elders' decision to bring the ark to the battlefield is treated as a category error — they thought they were dealing with a magical object when they were dealing with the throne of a sovereign who could not be commanded. For Abarbanel, the chapter is a critique of magical thinking in religion. Avivah Zornberg gives the chapter a psychoanalytic reading: the Israelites bring the ark because they cannot bear the silence of God, and the silence becomes catastrophe. Yair Zakovitch on the literary structure of the chapter is essential. Moshe Garsiel on the wordplay around the unnamed woman's death scene is illuminating.

**Christian readings.** The patristic tradition was preoccupied with the question of how YHWH could allow his ark to be captured. Origen treats the capture allegorically as the soul's loss of divine presence through unfaithfulness. Augustine in *City of God* 17.5 reads the chapter as the type of how God permits the apparent humiliation of his name in order to display his sovereignty more clearly later — the Philistine victory is the setup for the Philistine humiliation in chapter 5. Chrysostom's homilies on this material treat Eli's death as a warning to clergy and the unnamed woman's faithfulness as a model of true devotion. Gregory the Great's *Moralia* uses the chapter as a meditation on how God's apparent absence is sometimes the deeper form of his presence. Aquinas treats the ark theologically (*Summa* III.q.83) as a type of the eucharistic presence — though he notes that the type breaks down here because the ark can be captured while the Eucharist cannot be. Calvin's sermons read the chapter with characteristic rigor: the Israelite elders' decision to bring the ark to battle is treated as superstition, a mechanical use of divine things. Calvin uses the chapter to attack what he sees as Roman Catholic mechanical sacramentalism — a polemical use that does not exhaust the chapter's meaning but illustrates how it has functioned in Reformed homiletics. Luther on the chapter focuses on the failure of Eli and the inversion of expected outcomes. Eugene Peterson treats the chapter as a study in how religious institutions die. Ellen Davis on the ark as type of divine presence is helpful. John Goldingay reads the chapter as the central crisis of the book of Samuel: the question of whether YHWH's identity is bound to a sanctuary, an object, a place.

The critical tradition has produced detailed studies. **Rost's** original 1926 *Die Überlieferung von der Thronnachfolge Davids* identified the Ark Narrative as a discrete source. **Antony Campbell**'s *The Ark Narrative (1 Sam 4-6; 2 Sam 6): A Form-Critical and Traditio-Historical Study* (1975) is the standard English-language monograph; he refines Rost's hypothesis and argues for an early monarchic dating. **P. D. Miller and J. J. M. Roberts**, *The Hand of the Lord: A Reassessment of the "Ark Narrative" of 1 Samuel* (1977) is the other indispensable monograph: they argue the narrative was originally an apologetic for the Jerusalem cult, demonstrating that even when YHWH appears defeated he is not. **Brueggemann**'s commentary reads the chapter as the central crisis around which the book of Samuel is organized: can YHWH be YHWH apart from political-military success?

**Feminist readings.** Phyllis Trible's reading of Phinehas's wife in *Texts of Terror* is foundational. Cheryl Exum in *Fragmented Women* notes the unnamed status of the mother and treats her speech as a rare instance where a female character delivers the chapter's theological judgment. Tikva Frymer-Kensky reads her as the embodiment of correct theological perception in a chapter where every other character has misread.

Where do the traditions converge? On the loss of the ark as the central catastrophe of the chapter, and on the Israelite elders' instrumentalization of the ark as the underlying offense. Where do they diverge? On who exactly is being judged — the dtr editor frames it as a judgment on Eli's house specifically (cf. 2:34), but the narrative scope is broader, encompassing all Israel. Christian readings have tended to spiritualize; Jewish readings have tended to politicize; critical readings have tended to historicize. The text holds all three.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author believe God was doing? Two genuine tensions.

**Tension 1: Does YHWH's sovereignty depend on the ark?** The Israelite elders' theology in v. 3 is clear: bring the ark, it will save us. Their theology is wrong; the ark is captured and Israel slaughtered. But the corrective is itself unstable. Catholic theology has historically affirmed the legitimacy of sacramental presence — God really does act through physical objects (the ark, the Eucharist, relics) — while denying that the divine power is at the disposal of the user. Reformed theology (Calvin most sharply) has been suspicious of any account of localized divine presence, treating the chapter as a refutation of "mechanical sacramentalism." Orthodox theology, with its rich theology of icons and relics, has held a middle position: divine presence is real in physical objects, but the relationship is liturgical and doxological rather than instrumental. Jewish theology, especially after the destruction of the second temple, developed a robust account of how divine presence (*shekhinah*) survives the loss of any specific sanctuary — the ark was lost, the temple was lost, the *shekhinah* dwells now in the study of Torah. The position I find least intuitive — and the one that most rewards engagement — is the Catholic one: that the chapter does not refute sacramental presence but only its instrumentalization. The ark is genuinely the throne of YHWH; it is also genuinely capturable. Both are true. Where this leaves us: a doctrine of divine presence that is neither magical (the elders' error) nor purely spiritual (the Reformed temptation) but covenantal — the presence is real and conditional at once.

**Tension 2: The death of Phinehas's wife and the unnamed naming.** The chapter ends with a woman dying in childbirth, refusing the comfort of her newborn son, naming him with the worst name in the Hebrew Bible, and declaring the divine glory has departed. Catholic moral theology has tended to read her response as a model of theological priority — the loss of God matters more than the gain of family. Reformed readings (Calvin) celebrate her as a remarkable instance of female theological perception. Orthodox readings have sometimes been uncomfortable with the bleakness — a death-bed naming with no hope. Jewish readings (the rabbis in Berakhot 5b) treat her as a tragic figure whose theological clarity exceeds her personal capacity to survive. The position I find least intuitive — and which I think the text actually requires — is the Jewish one: that her clarity is real and her death is real, and the text neither promises nor denies divine vindication of her insight. The narrator just lets her speak and die. Where this leaves us: a portrait of theological perception under conditions of total catastrophe, given to a woman the text does not even name.

One unresolved question to leave with: if the glory has departed from Israel, where has it gone — and is the rest of the Ark Narrative the answer to that question?

## The Thread

When you get to **1 Samuel 5**, watch what happens to Dagon — the ark's "departure" from Israel turns out to be its arrival in enemy territory, and the answer to Phinehas's wife's question is not what she thought. When you get to **Jeremiah 7:12-14** ("Go now to my place that was in Shiloh, where I made my name dwell at the first, and see what I did to it because of the evil of my people Israel"), remember chapter 4 — Jeremiah is appealing to the destruction of Shiloh as the precedent for what YHWH may do to Jerusalem. The chapter you are reading is the unstated background of one of the most important warnings in the prophets. When you get to **Psalm 78:60-64** ("He forsook his dwelling at Shiloh, the tent where he dwelt among mankind"), notice the same theology. And when you get to **Ezekiel 10-11**, where the *kavod YHWH* is described as departing from the Jerusalem temple — pillar by pillar, gate by gate — remember the unnamed woman of 1 Sam 4 who first gave that theology its name. *Ichabod* is the prophetic vocabulary Ezekiel inherits.
