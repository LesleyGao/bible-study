# 1 Samuel 11: The King Who Cut the Oxen

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The world of 1 Samuel 11 is the politically fragmented Iron Age I–IIA transition in the southern Levant, roughly the late eleventh century BCE. Israel exists as a loose tribal confederation defined less by territorial sovereignty than by mutual obligation under YHWH's covenant, and that obligation is being tested in real time by external pressure on its peripheries. Three of those pressures are visible in this chapter: the **Ammonites** under **Nahash** in Transjordan, the more distant **Philistines** on the coastal plain, and the unstable internal politics of a people who have just acclaimed Saul as king at Mizpah (1 Sam 10) but who, the narrator tells us, do not all take the new arrangement seriously.

Ammon, centered around modern Amman (ancient Rabbath-Ammon), was a small kingdom in the Transjordanian highlands east of the Jordan River. Its political organization in this period is increasingly visible thanks to excavations at sites like Tall al-‘Umayri and Tall Jawa, and the **Amman Citadel Inscription** (ninth century but evidencing earlier political traditions) preserves the divine name **Milkom** known from the Hebrew Bible (1 Kgs 11:5). The Ammonite encroachment on **Jabesh-gilead** — a Manassite or Gadite town in the Transjordan, traditionally identified with Tell Abu Kharaz or, more probably, Tell el-Maqlub on the Wadi Yabis — is consistent with what we know of intermittent Ammonite expansionism in this period. Burton MacDonald and Larry Herr have argued from survey data that Ammonite settlement intensified in the late Iron I, exactly the moment 1 Samuel 11 depicts.

Nahash's threat — to **gouge out the right eye** (Hebrew *naqar 'eyn yamin*, נָקַר עֵין יָמִין) of every man in Jabesh — has good ANE parallels. Mutilation of conquered enemies is well attested in Assyrian royal annals: Ashurnasirpal II boasts of cutting off "the noses, the ears, and the fingers" of his enemies, and Tiglath-pileser III's annals describe similar disfigurements as standard treaty-imposition rituals. The right eye specifically would disable a man for warfare without making him useless as labor: a soldier sights along a spear or bow with his right eye while the left is shielded behind a buckler. So this is not gratuitous cruelty but calculated demilitarization, made permanent and visible — what the Hebrew calls *cherpah* (חֶרְפָּה, "disgrace, reproach"). The text says the goal is "to bring disgrace on all Israel," not just to subjugate Jabesh.

Saul comes "from the field behind the oxen" (11:5), a detail that has occupied historians since Albrecht Alt and Martin Noth, who saw in it evidence that the Saulide kingship was originally a charismatic, agrarian-tribal institution rather than a stabilized dynastic court. There is no royal residence at Gibeah yet — a fact corroborated by the modest fortifications uncovered at **Tell el-Ful** (commonly identified with Saul's Gibeah) by William F. Albright in the 1920s and revisited by Paul Lapp. The "palace" of Saul is a small, square fortress with casemate walls, no monumental architecture, no luxury imports. This is a chieftain's stronghold, not a king's residence in the sense Solomon would later understand the term.

Three more Hebrew terms shape the chapter. **Tsalachah** (צָלְחָה, "rushed upon"), used of the Spirit of God in 11:6, is the same verb used of the Spirit's empowerment of the judges Othniel, Samson, and Jephthah. Saul's kingship in this chapter is presented in the *charismatic-judge* mode, not yet the dynastic-bureaucratic mode that the institution will assume under David and Solomon. **Pachad-YHWH** (פַּחַד־יְהוָה, "the dread of YHWH") falling on the people in 11:7 is a holy-war formula (cf. Exod 15:16, Deut 11:25) that frames Saul's muster as a divine act, not a political one. And the place-name **Bezek** (בֶּזֶק, 11:8), the mustering point, has been plausibly identified with Khirbet Ibziq in the northern hill country — strategically placed to coordinate a rapid Transjordan crossing.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre matters here, and 1 Samuel 11 sits at a hinge. P. Kyle McCarter classifies it as a **deliverance narrative** in the mold of the book of Judges — Israel oppressed, a hero raised up, the Spirit rushes, victory, the people acclaim. Hertzberg called it "the last of the judge stories." Yet it is being deployed as a **kingship-confirmation narrative**: the chapter ends not with Saul returning to his oxen (as Gideon refuses kingship in Judg 8) but with the people going to **Gilgal** to "renew the kingdom" (11:14). The literary form has been recoded to do new institutional work.

Robert Alter, in *The David Story* and his subsequent commentary, points out the careful symmetries. Saul cuts oxen in pieces (*nittach*, נִתַּח) and sends them through Israel (11:7) — the verb and the action are an exact verbal echo of the Levite who cuts up his concubine and sends her pieces through Israel in **Judges 19:29**. Both acts produce the same response: the people gather "as one man" (*ke'ish echad*). The Judges echo is not accidental. The narrator is signaling: this is the same Israel, the same desperate, fragmented people; the cure for what produced the Judges 19 atrocity is what is now being attempted with kingship. Whether the cure works is the question the rest of 1 Samuel asks.

The literary force of the **Jabesh-gilead** setting deserves more attention than it usually gets. Polzin and Brettler have shown that the choice of Jabesh as the besieged city is loaded. **Judges 21** records that, after the war against Benjamin nearly extinguished the tribe, the surviving Benjaminites were given wives taken violently from Jabesh-gilead, the one Israelite town that had refused to join the war. Saul is a Benjaminite. The town he rescues in chapter 11 is, in a literal genealogical sense, the town of his grandmothers. The narrative is doing covenantal arithmetic: the tribe that took its women from Jabesh now sends its first king to save Jabesh. The structural symmetry is too tight to be accident; it is, as Polzin argues in *Samuel and the Deuteronomist*, the Deuteronomistic Historian's argument that kingship can either repair or perpetuate the cycle of Judges-era violence.

Then there is the **textual issue**, which is this chapter's most famous problem. **4QSam-a** (4Q51, the Cave 4 Samuel scroll edited by Frank Moore Cross and Eugene Ulrich) preserves a longer reading at the start of chapter 11 that is absent from the Masoretic Text. The Qumran reading, which **Josephus** independently knew (*Antiquities* 6.68–71), provides crucial backstory: Nahash had previously attacked the Reubenites and Gadites in Transjordan, gouging out the right eye of every man he could lay hands on; seven thousand men escaped to Jabesh-gilead, where they had been living in safety for one month before Nahash arrived. This makes the seven-day delay of 11:3 narratively coherent (otherwise, why would Nahash, whose policy is to mutilate immediately, suddenly grant a week's respite?). Cross argued in 1953 that the Qumran reading is original, lost from MT by parablepsis (a scribal eye-skip on a similar word). The **NRSV** adopts it; the **ESV** does not. McCarter, Klein, and Tov all consider the Qumran reading authentic. Engaging this honestly: the MT we read, in this chapter, is almost certainly defective.

Literary devices include the **threefold messengers** (Jabesh sends to Israel; messengers come to Saul; Saul sends back word), the **"three companies" tactical division** in 11:11 echoing Gideon's strategy in Judges 7 (and signaling to the reader that holy war is being conducted), and the **"morning watch"** timing (*ashmoret ha-boqer*, the last third of the night, ca. 2–6 a.m.) which is the same time the LORD threw Egypt into confusion at the Sea (Exod 14:24). The literary register is that of *yhwh-milchamah*, the wars of the LORD. Saul wins not by superior numbers but by divine timing.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical arc, 1 Samuel 11 functions as the **legitimating hinge** between Saul's secret anointing (1 Sam 10) and Samuel's kingship-renewal speech (1 Sam 12). The Deuteronomistic redaction, on Cross and Nelson's double-redaction theory, has braided together pro-monarchic sources (which 1 Sam 11 represents) with anti-monarchic sources (1 Sam 8, 10:17–27, 12) to produce a portrait of kingship that is institutionally ambivalent — necessary for survival, dangerous for covenant. Brueggemann reads 1 Samuel 11 as the *moment of greatest possibility* in the whole Saul story: a Spirit-empowered king saves a marginal Israelite town from a foreign tyrant, refuses to execute his domestic critics ("Not a man shall be put to death this day, for today the LORD has worked salvation in Israel," 11:13), and brings the people to renewed worship at Gilgal. Everything is right. From this peak, the trajectory is downward.

The **Jewish interpretive tradition** has read this chapter with deep attention to its ethical and political weight. **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 6.68–80) elaborates the Nahash episode using the longer 4QSam-a tradition, showing that the prior persecution of the Transjordanian tribes was known in Second Temple Judaism. The rabbis in **b. Yoma 22b** discuss Saul's mercy toward his political opponents in 11:13 favorably, contrasting it with his later harshness. **Rashi** glosses *tsalachah* on Saul as the Spirit of strength (*ruach gevurah*), distinguishing it from the prophetic Spirit. **Radak** (David Kimhi) emphasizes that Saul's cutting of the oxen, while echoing Judges 19, is meant to invoke the curse against any Israelite who fails to muster — the oxen are a self-imprecation (*"thus shall it be done to his oxen"*). **Ralbag** (Gersonides) reads Saul's restraint in 11:13 as the model of regal *chesed*. **Abarbanel**, the great late-medieval Iberian commentator, devotes significant attention to the political logic: Saul refuses to begin his kingship with a purge, and Abarbanel — himself a court Jew under multiple kings — sees in this a normative theory of constitutional monarchy. Modern Jewish readers like **Avivah Zornberg**, **Yair Zakovitch**, and **Moshe Garsiel** have all noted the Judges-19 verbal echoes and the disturbing ambiguity that Saul, the rescuer, also performs an act that recalls the worst horror of the pre-monarchic period.

The **Christian tradition** has read 1 Samuel 11 with a different set of preoccupations. **Origen** (*Homilies on 1 Samuel*) reads Saul's empowerment by the Spirit allegorically, as a type of the Christian's anointing in baptism — but ominously, since Saul will lose what he is given. **Augustine** in *City of God* references Saul as the first instance of a king whose Spirit-empowerment was real but whose election was not unto perseverance, a key building-block of his doctrine of grace. **Chrysostom** focuses on the moral example of Saul's mercy in 11:13, holding it up as a pattern of Christian magnanimity toward enemies. **Gregory the Great**, in his *Moralia in Job*, returns repeatedly to Saul as the figure of the pastor or ruler whose initial humility (the man behind the oxen) gives way to pride; the "behind the oxen" detail is treated as evidence that even the future apostate began in honest labor. **Aquinas** (*Summa* II-II, q.40) cites Saul's just war on behalf of Jabesh as a textbook case of legitimate war: just cause (rescue of the oppressed), legitimate authority (the Spirit of YHWH), right intention (mercy to the captives, not vengeance on critics).

The **Reformation** rereads this. **Calvin's sermons on 1 Samuel** — preached in Geneva in 1561–62 and now available in English translation by Douglas Kelly — treat chapter 11 as the demonstration that civil rulers are gifts of God and that resistance to lawful authority is sin. But Calvin is also alert to the cautionary edge: Saul is granted everything here and squanders it, which Calvin uses against any complacent Erastian view of magistracy. **Luther**, in his lectures on the historical books, reads Saul's restraint in 11:13 as a "mask of God" — God working through this man's mercy to give Israel a glimpse of what kingship should be, even as God already knows what Saul will become.

**Critical scholarship** has been more interested in source-critical and tradition-historical questions. **Wellhausen** and **Noth** assigned 1 Samuel 11 to the early, pro-monarchic source. **Cross** added the textual-critical insight from 4QSam-a. **McCarter** (Anchor Bible) treats the chapter as a relatively well-preserved tradition reflecting authentic Saulide memory, though the framing in 11:12–14 is Deuteronomistic. **Klein** (WBC) and **Hertzberg** (OTL) argue the chapter is the oldest stratum of the Saul tradition, the original "rise of Saul" narrative around which later anti-monarchic material was layered.

**Contemporary** readings include **Polzin's** structuralist work in *Samuel and the Deuteronomist*, which centers the Judges 19 verbal echoes and reads the chapter as the Dtr's argument about what kingship is for. **Eugene Peterson**, in *Leap Over a Wall*, writes pastorally of the moment of Saul's ascent as the rare and beautiful instance of public office and personal calling aligning. **Ellen Davis** highlights the agricultural realism — Saul, like David later, comes from the field, and biblical kingship begins as an interruption of ordinary work. **John Goldingay** stresses the coalitional theology: Israel is saved when the tribes act "as one man." **David Gunn**, in *The Fate of King Saul*, locates the tragic kernel of Saul's story precisely here, in the chapter where everything goes right and the ground is laid for everything to go wrong. **Feminist readers** — **Phyllis Trible**, **Cheryl Exum**, **Mieke Bal**, **Athalya Brenner**, **Alice Bach** — have asked who is missing from this scene: the women of Jabesh, descendants of the women carried off in Judges 21, are not mentioned. Their absence is itself a commentary on whose deliverance the male-narrated text is willing to celebrate.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: The Spirit and the Office.** What is the relationship between the charismatic Spirit-empowerment that lets Saul save Jabesh and the institutional kingship that is "renewed" at Gilgal? Three positions: (a) The **charismatic priority** view, defended by Albrecht Alt and continued by Brueggemann, holds that genuine biblical leadership is always Spirit-given and institution is at best a vehicle, at worst a corruption. The chapter, on this reading, shows Spirit doing what office cannot. (b) The **sacramental institution** view, classically Roman Catholic and articulated by Aquinas, holds that office and Spirit are not opposed: God uses institutional kingship as the regular means by which He governs Israel, and the Spirit's rushing is the divine confirmation that Saul really does hold the office. (c) The **dialectical** view, associated with Calvin and developed by Karl Barth, holds that Spirit and office stand in necessary tension: office without Spirit becomes tyranny; Spirit without office becomes anarchy; Israel — and the church — needs both, and the failure of Saul will be the failure to hold them together. Steelman: each of these reads the same Hebrew clause (*tsalach ruach 'elohim*, the Spirit rushed) as evidence for opposite conclusions, which suggests the text deliberately leaves the relationship underdetermined. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter is the high-water mark of the *fusion* of Spirit and office in Saul's life. We will not see them together again.

**Tension Two: The Justice and Mercy of New Power.** Saul refuses to execute his critics in 11:13 — "Not a man shall be put to death this day." Three positions on what this means. (a) **Magnanimous statesmanship**: Abarbanel, Calvin, and most modern political readers see Saul modeling the mature recognition that a new regime cannot consolidate by terror. (b) **Pious deflection**: a more skeptical reading (Polzin, some feminist critics) notes that Saul's stated reason — "today the LORD has worked salvation" — credits YHWH but conveniently spares Saul the political cost of bloodletting; the mercy is real but also strategic. (c) **Foreshadowing failure**: Gregory the Great and certain Christian readers see Saul's restraint here as the residue of his initial humility, a virtue he will catastrophically fail to display when he later spares **Agag** in chapter 15 — the same gesture, mercy toward a defeated enemy, will be obedient here and disobedient there, because the same act can be virtue or vice depending on whether YHWH commanded it. Steelman: position (c) is theologically the most demanding, because it requires us to hold that the moral character of an action depends not only on its intrinsic quality but on its conformity to specific divine command — a hard saying. **Where this leaves us:** Saul's mercy in 11:13 is genuinely good, and it becomes the precedent that destroys him, because he will replay it in chapter 15 against the express word of YHWH. The chapter that contains his finest hour also contains the seed of his ruin.

**One unresolved question:** If the longer 4QSam-a/Josephus reading is original, then the MT we have read for two thousand years is missing the moral and narrative key to Nahash's behavior. What does it mean for our doctrine of Scripture that the textually best reading of this chapter is the one most modern English Bibles do not print?

## The Thread

- **Judges 19:29** — The Levite cuts up his concubine and sends pieces through Israel, producing the same "as one man" gathering. Saul's act in 1 Sam 11:7 is the verbal twin. The Dtr is asking whether kingship can redeem the violence of the pre-monarchic age or only repeat it.
- **Judges 21:8–14** — The seizure of women from Jabesh-gilead to repopulate Benjamin. Saul, the Benjaminite king, rescues the city of his grandmothers. The genealogical and moral debt is being paid.
- **1 Samuel 31:11–13** — At the end of the book, when Saul's body is hung on the wall of Beth-shan, **the men of Jabesh-gilead** travel through the night to recover and bury him. The debt runs both ways.
- **1 Samuel 15:9** — Saul again spares a defeated enemy, this time **Agag**. The mercy that was virtue in chapter 11 becomes the disobedience that ends his dynasty. **Flag forward**: read these chapters together.
