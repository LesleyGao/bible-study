# 2 Kings 21: Manasseh, the Sin That Sealed Jerusalem's Fate

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Manasseh ben Hezekiah ruled Judah from approximately 697 to 642 BCE — fifty-five years, the longest reign in Judah's recorded history and longer than any monarch of the divided kingdom on either side of the border. The chronology is not in serious dispute: Edwin Thiele's regnal calculations, refined by Mordechai Cogan and Hayim Tadmor in the Anchor Bible commentary, anchor Manasseh's accession in the early 690s and his death around 642, with a likely co-regency overlap with the aging Hezekiah. The period he governed corresponds with the political height of the Neo-Assyrian Empire under Sennacherib, Esarhaddon, and Ashurbanipal — the three kings who pushed Assyrian armies as far as Memphis and Thebes and demanded vassal tribute from every petty kingdom in the Levant.

The political reality the Deuteronomistic narrator passes over almost in silence is Manasseh's status as an Assyrian vassal. The extrabiblical evidence is direct and humiliating. Esarhaddon's vassal list (ANET 291) names "Menasi king of the city of Judah" — *Menasi šar āl Yaudi* — among twenty-two kings of Hatti and the seacoast required to provide building materials for Esarhaddon's palace at Nineveh: timber from Lebanon, stone, gold, silver. Ashurbanipal's Cylinder C (the so-called "Prism C," a clay prism inscribed with the king's annals) repeats Manasseh's name in a parallel list of vassals supplying troops and tribute for Ashurbanipal's Egyptian campaigns of 667 and 663 BCE. This is the world Manasseh actually lived in — a world in which a Judahite king's sons might be hostages in Nineveh, in which Assyrian *qepu* officials moved through Judahite territory, and in which the astral cult of "the host of heaven" (*ṣəbāʾ haššāmayim*) the Deuteronomist condemns at 21:3 was not a free choice but the religious vocabulary of imperial diplomacy. The altars to the host of heaven in the two Temple courts (21:5) reflect Mesopotamian astral worship — Šamaš the sun, Sîn the moon, Ištar/Venus, the planetary deities — almost certainly imposed or invited as part of vassal religious obligation.

The material culture of seventh-century Judah confirms a partial economic recovery under Assyrian patronage. Israel Finkelstein and Lawrence Stager have argued that the Pax Assyriaca produced trade-network expansion: Judahite olive oil from the Shephelah and Negev settlements moved through the Assyrian-controlled coastal corridor; Ekron became the largest olive-oil production center in the ancient Near East; Beersheba and Aroer expanded. Manasseh's long reign, in pure material terms, was probably a period of provincial stability — a fact the dtr narrator does not engage. Lawrence Stager's *Forging an Identity* argues that the Iron IIC settlement pattern in the Negev highlands reflects Manasseh-era state-sponsored expansion into peripheral zones, possibly including caravan-route fortification.

Three Hebrew terms structure the chapter. *Bāmôt* (במות), "high places," appears in 21:3 — the local cult installations Hezekiah had centralized away. *ʾăšērâ* (אֲשֵׁרָה), the cult image of the goddess Asherah (or her wooden pole-symbol), appears in 21:3 and 21:7 — set, scandalously, *bə-bêt YHWH*, "in the house of YHWH." The shift from indefinite *ʾăšērâ* (21:3) to the definite *pesel hāʾăšērâ* (21:7), "the carved image of Asherah," is significant: Manasseh moves from generic Asherah veneration to installing a specific cultic statue inside the Temple itself. *Wayyaʿăbēr ʾet-bənô bāʾēš* (וַיַּעֲבֵר אֶת־בְּנוֹ בָּאֵשׁ), "he caused his son to pass through the fire" (21:6), is the technical formulation for child sacrifice — the same phrase used at 2 Kgs 16:3 of Ahaz and at 17:17 of the northern kingdom on the eve of its destruction. Susan Niditch has argued that the dtr's repetition of this phrase is part of a deliberate equation of Judah under Manasseh with the apostate north under Hoshea — preparing the reader for parallel judgment.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: regnal account in the Deuteronomistic History, with two unusual features. First, an extended prophetic indictment in the divine voice (21:10-15) — a near-unique rhetorical move within Kings, where prophetic speech usually arrives through a named prophet (Elijah, Elisha, Isaiah) rather than via the anonymous "his servants the prophets" formula. Second, the anomalous note about "innocent blood" at 21:16, which sits awkwardly outside the standard regnal frame and reads like an editorial supplement.

The structure pairs Manasseh (vv. 1-18) with his son Amon (vv. 19-26) in classic Deuteronomistic doublet form: same evil, same idolatry, same condemnation. But the chapter is dominated by Manasseh; Amon receives only the regnal frame plus assassination notice. The literary effect is to make Manasseh stand alone as the cause and Amon merely the continuation.

The most striking literary device is the **measuring-line metaphor** at 21:13: *wə-nāṭîtî ʿal-yərûšālaim ʾēt qaw šōmərôn wə-ʾet-mišqolet bêt ʾaḥʾāb* — "I will stretch over Jerusalem the measuring line of Samaria and the plummet of the house of Ahab." The construction equipment of the builder is repurposed as the instrument of demolition. Samaria — destroyed by Sargon II in 722 BCE — and the house of Ahab — exterminated by Jehu in 842 — become the architectural template for Jerusalem's coming destruction. Robert Alter notes the simile in 21:13b is even more shocking: *kaʾăšer-yimḥeh ʾet-haṣṣallaḥat māḥâ wə-hāpak ʿal-pānêhā*, "as one wipes a dish, wiping it and turning it upside down." The image is domestic, almost trivial — a housewife scraping out a bowl. The disproportion between the casual gesture and the cosmic devastation is the rhetorical point. Sternberg in *Poetics of Biblical Narrative* observes that the Deuteronomist deploys disproportion deliberately to mark divine judgment as both inevitable and almost careless — Jerusalem will be wiped not in fury but in finality.

Place in the book argument: 2 Kings 21 is the dtr's hinge. Frank Moore Cross's classic Dtr1/Dtr2 thesis (*Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*, 1973) reads vv. 10-15 as Dtr2's exilic insertion — the editor's retrojection of the cause of 587 onto Manasseh after Josiah's reform proved inadequate to reverse history's momentum. On Cross's reading, the pre-exilic edition (Dtr1) condemned Manasseh as a wicked king but left judgment open; the exilic editor (Dtr2), writing after the catastrophe, returned to chapter 21 and inserted the unconditional judgment oracle. Richard Nelson's *Double Redaction of the Deuteronomistic History* developed the argument: the language of "I will forsake the remnant of my heritage" (21:14) is exilic in vocabulary and theological register, drawing on Jeremiah-like idiom. Steven McKenzie in *The Trouble with Kings* has questioned how cleanly Dtr1 and Dtr2 can be separated, but the basic point — that Manasseh's role as the cause of the fall is editorial theology rather than a 7th-century prophetic word — has wide acceptance.

A textual note: the LXX preserves at 21:13 a slightly different verb form for "wipe" (ἐξαλείφω rather than the expected ἀπομάσσω), and 4QKgs (the Qumran Kings fragments) attests minor variants in the regnal frame but not in the judgment oracle. The chapter is textually stable.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Jewish interpretive trajectory on Manasseh is the most fascinating in the entire Hebrew Bible because the canonical sources themselves diverge. **2 Chronicles 33:11-13** preserves a tradition entirely absent from 2 Kings: Manasseh is captured by Assyrian commanders, taken to Babylon in fetters, repents in exile, is restored to Jerusalem, and removes the foreign gods and the Asherah from the Temple. The Chronicler's Manasseh is a paradigm of repentance. Critical scholars — Sara Japhet, Hugh Williamson — debate whether the Chronicler had access to an independent tradition or constructed the repentance theologically; the Esarhaddon vassal list confirms Manasseh did at minimum visit Nineveh as a tribute-bearer, which may be the historical kernel. **The Prayer of Manasseh** (2nd century BCE Greek text, in some Christian Old Testament canons as deuterocanonical, though preserved in Syriac and Greek and surviving among the Pseudepigrapha) elaborates the Chronicler's note into a full penitential prayer — read liturgically in Eastern Orthodox Compline.

Rabbinic tradition wrestles with the problem of Manasseh's salvation. **b. Sanhedrin 102b** records the famous discussion: Rabbi Yehudah says Manasseh has a portion in the world to come (citing Chronicles); Rabbi Yohanan says he does not (citing the irrevocability of the judgment in 2 Kings 21). The Talmud preserves both. Rashi on 21:13 explains the measuring-line as a *metzuyenet*, a precise standard — Jerusalem will be measured by the same yardstick that condemned Samaria. Radak (David Kimhi) develops the comparison: just as the line is straight, the judgment is unbending. Ralbag (Gersonides) argues that Manasseh's repentance in Chronicles is consistent with the Kings narrative if one assumes the judgment of 21:10-15 is contingent on no future king reversing it — and Josiah does not in fact succeed in reversing it (cf. 23:26). **Abarbanel** in his commentary engages the tension at length, arguing that the Kings author intentionally suppressed the repentance because his theological purpose was to identify the cause of the exile, not to celebrate individual repentance; the Chronicler's purpose was inverse. Yair Zakovitch and Avivah Zornberg in contemporary Jewish reading have explored the psychological dimensions — Manasseh as the son who must ruin his father's reform to define his own existence; Hephzibah, the queen mother, named at 21:1 (rare honor in Kings), as a cipher whose Isaian-sounding name (cf. Isa 62:4) raises questions the text declines to answer.

The Christian interpretive tradition has historically focused less on Manasseh than the Jewish; he appears mainly as a type of the worst kind of king who can nonetheless repent. **Origen** in his Homilies on Leviticus uses Manasseh as an example of grace exceeding sin. **Augustine** in *City of God* Book 18 mentions Manasseh briefly, primarily through the Chronicles tradition, as evidence that even the worst can be reclaimed. **Theodoret of Cyrus** in his *Quaestiones in Reges* (mid-5th century) addresses the apparent conflict between Kings and Chronicles directly: Theodoret argues the Kings author records what Manasseh deserved by his sins, the Chronicler what Manasseh received by his repentance — the divergence is theological emphasis rather than contradiction. **Chrysostom** uses Manasseh in his homilies on Romans as a paradigm of mercy. **Gregory the Great** in his Pastoral Rule cites Manasseh as evidence that no one is beyond reform. **Aquinas** treats the case briefly in the *Summa* IIa-IIae q. 25, on whether one can love an enemy.

The Reformation reading is more pointed because the chapter directly engages questions of cause and effect in divine judgment. **Calvin's sermons on 2 Kings** (preserved in the *Supplementa Calviniana* series) read 21:10-15 as definitive evidence that God may decree judgment for sins not yet committed by the present generation — a difficult doctrine Calvin defends through covenant theology: the people had broken the covenant repeatedly, and Manasseh was the last straw rather than the sole cause. **Luther's** scattered comments on Kings emphasize Manasseh as proof that human merit cannot reverse what divine wrath has decreed; only grace, in the Chronicles repentance, can do so.

Critical scholarship from the 19th century onward has been dominated by the source-critical question of vv. 10-15. **Cogan and Tadmor** in their 1988 Anchor Bible commentary provide the standard treatment, arguing for Dtr2 redaction and noting the parallels with Jeremiah's vocabulary. **Marvin Sweeney's** OTL commentary on 1 & 2 Kings argues the Manasseh-judgment retrojection is essential to the dtr's project: without it, Josiah's reform should have averted catastrophe, and the dtr cannot explain why it did not. **T. R. Hobbs** in the Word commentary takes a more conservative view, defending the unity of the Manasseh material. **Richard Nelson** in his Double Redaction argues for a layered redactional history. **Steven McKenzie's** *Trouble with Kings* questions whether Cross's two-edition model can be sustained, but accepts that 21:10-15 is at minimum a secondary expansion.

Contemporary engagement: **Walter Brueggemann** in his *1 & 2 Kings* (Smyth & Helwys) argues that the Manasseh chapter is the dtr's confession that the political and religious reality of the late seventh century was beyond reform — Josiah was too late, the apostasy too deep. **Iain Provan's** NIB commentary explores the theological problem of corporate guilt: a generation suffers for a king two generations dead. **Susan Niditch** in *War in the Hebrew Bible* notes that the closing of Kings repeatedly invokes Manasseh's "innocent blood" (21:16, 24:4) as the narrative's accumulating moral weight — judgment is not arbitrary but cumulative. **Susan Ackerman** in *Under Every Green Tree* has examined the Asherah-in-the-Temple datum (21:7) against the broader evidence for goddess worship in Iron Age Judah, arguing the dtr's horror reveals how pervasive Asherah veneration in fact was — the Khirbet el-Qom and Kuntillet 'Ajrud inscriptions referencing "YHWH and his Asherah" suggest popular religion had long included the consort the dtr disowns.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**First tension: cumulative versus immediate judgment.** The chapter insists Jerusalem will be destroyed because of Manasseh (21:11-15), and 2 Kgs 23:26-27 reaffirms this even after Josiah's reform. But Manasseh's grandchildren and great-grandchildren — three generations later — pay the price. Three positions. Position one (classical Reformed, Calvin, Brueggemann): the judgment is corporate-covenantal; the people sinned with Manasseh and continued to sin under his successors, and judgment is the cumulative weight of broken covenant rather than punishment for one king's deeds. The verses on Manasseh's "innocent blood" (21:16) and the people's complicity ("Manasseh seduced them," 21:9) anchor this. Steelman: the dtr is not arbitrary; he repeatedly notes that the people sinned alongside the king, and the judgment language ("they have done what is evil," 21:15, plural) supports the corporate reading. Position two (Ralbag, some critical readers): the judgment was contingent and could have been reversed by sufficient repentance; Josiah's reform was insufficient because it was political-cultic rather than personal-spiritual across the population. Steelman: this preserves moral agency but strains against the absolute language of 21:12-15. Position three (Cross, Nelson, the redaction-critical view): the Manasseh-judgment is exilic theological retrojection — the Dtr2 editor needed to explain the catastrophe, and Manasseh was the most plausible scapegoat. Steelman: this resolves the moral problem by relocating the judgment as the editor's interpretation rather than divine speech, but it relativizes the chapter's claim to be revealing divine purpose.

Where this leaves us: the dtr presents the catastrophe as overdetermined. Manasseh is the proximate cause, but the people's complicity, the cumulative covenant violations, and the editorial purpose of explaining 587 BCE all converge. The chapter resists a single causal account.

**Second tension: the Asherah inside the Temple.** The carved image of Asherah set in the house where YHWH's name dwells (21:7) is the narrative's central scandal. Three positions on what this means theologically. Position one (traditional, dtr-internal): YHWH's name *cannot* coexist with another deity's image; the installation forces an absolute contradiction that must be resolved by destroying either the image or the Temple. Steelman: this preserves divine holiness as ontologically incompatible with idol-coexistence, the dtr's central theological premise. Position two (Susan Ackerman, William Dever, popular-religion school): the Iron Age archaeological record (Kuntillet 'Ajrud, Khirbet el-Qom, female figurines throughout Judahite domestic contexts) suggests Asherah worship was widespread and possibly even part of official cult before the late-monarchic reforms; Manasseh is not introducing innovation but normalizing what had long existed at the periphery. Steelman: the material evidence for popular Asherah veneration is genuinely abundant, and the dtr's monolatrous picture is an idealized reform program rather than a historical norm. Position three (covenant-theological): the Temple was the location of divine presence by gracious choice (21:7's "in this house... I will put my name forever"); placing the Asherah there is not merely religious mixing but the deliberate occupation of sacred space by a competing claim, and YHWH's response — abandoning the place rather than coexisting — is consistent with covenant logic. Steelman: this honors the spatial-theological logic of the Deuteronomic name-theology and explains why the destruction of the Temple is the appropriate divine response.

Where this leaves us: the Asherah-in-the-Temple datum is the chapter's most theologically loaded image because it forces the question of whether the Temple is intrinsically holy (and thus profanable) or contingently holy (and thus abandonable). The dtr opts for the latter: YHWH leaves before the Babylonians arrive.

**Unresolved question:** What does the chapter ask us to do with Hephzibah, Manasseh's mother, named in 21:1? The naming of queen mothers in Kings is selective and freighted, and "Hephzibah" — meaning "my delight is in her" — is the same name Isaiah 62:4 gives to restored Jerusalem. Is the dtr inviting an irony the surface narrative does not acknowledge? Is Manasseh's mother, named for divine delight, the silent witness against her son? The chapter does not answer, and the centuries of commentary largely look past her.

## The Thread

Cross-references that matter. **Deut 28:36-37** anticipates exile to a foreign land as covenant curse — the architecture 21:10-15 activates. **1 Kgs 9:6-9** records Solomon's vision warning that if Israel forsakes YHWH the Temple will become a heap and a hissing — Manasseh's actions invoke the curse Solomon was warned against. **Jer 15:4** explicitly attributes the coming judgment to "what Manasseh son of Hezekiah did in Jerusalem" — Jeremiah and the dtr share a tradition. **2 Chr 33:11-13** preserves the repentance the Kings author omits — canonical witness that the historical Manasseh's story may be more complex than 2 Kings 21 allows.

Forward references this chapter sets up: the destruction of the Asherah and high places at **2 Kgs 23:4-20** reverses Manasseh point by point — Josiah is constructed in the narrative as the anti-Manasseh; **23:26-27** reaffirms that Josiah's reform did not avert the judgment 21:10-15 had decreed; and the actual fall narrated at **2 Kgs 25:8-12** is the working-out of the measuring line stretched over Jerusalem here. The Babylonian Chronicle ABC 5's record of 597 and the 587 destruction layers across Jerusalem (City of David, Burnt House, Bullae House) are the archaeological echoes of this chapter's prophetic word — the seventh-century king's choices producing sixth-century rubble.
