# 2 Samuel 21: Rizpah on the Rock

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 21 opens the so-called "Samuel Appendix" (chapters 21-24), a self-contained collection inserted by a later editor between the Court History (which ends at chapter 20 and resumes at 1 Kings 1) and the Solomonic accession narrative. The chapter contains two distinct historical episodes joined by the theme of *bloodguilt and reparation*: a famine resolved by the impalement of seven Saulides at Gibeon (vv. 1-14), and a list of four Philistine giant-killings (vv. 15-22).

The political backdrop of the Gibeonite episode is dense. The Gibeonites are identified as "of the remnant of the Amorites," which signals their pre-Israelite Hivite/Amorite origin and their special covenantal status. According to Joshua 9, they had secured a sworn treaty of protection from Joshua and the elders of Israel by means of a famous deception — appearing as travelers from a distant land to evade the *ḥerem* ban. Saul's violation of that oath, alluded to in our chapter but otherwise unrecorded in the Hebrew Bible, would have been understood in ancient Near Eastern terms as a fundamental breach of a sworn covenant, the kind of offense whose consequences extend through generations. Hittite, Mari, and Assyrian texts document parallel cases in which famine, plague, or military defeat are diagnosed as the consequence of unaddressed bloodguilt or covenant violation, requiring formal reparation to the wronged party.

The Gibeonites themselves were a real political community of the Iron Age. James Pritchard's excavations at Tell el-Jib in 1956-1962 documented a substantial Late Bronze and Iron Age city with the famous rock-cut pool (33 feet in diameter, 82 feet deep), an extensive wine industry (some 63 underground wine cellars, with handles inscribed *gb'n*), and continuous occupation through the period in question. That the chapter places the execution at "Gibeah of Saul" — Saul's home town, traditionally identified with Tell el-Ful three miles north of Jerusalem and partially excavated by W. F. Albright — adds a deliberate symbolic weight: the Saulides die at the political and ancestral center of Saul's own house.

Baruch Halpern (*David's Secret Demons*) and Steven McKenzie (*King David: A Biography*) read the chapter through a sharper political lens. They argue that the famine and the demand for Saulide blood may, in historical reality, have been the cover story for David's systematic elimination of the remaining Saulide line — a purge that the Court History takes pains to deflect onto divine necessity and Gibeonite demand. The text's careful insistence that David spared Mephibosheth "because of the oath of the LORD that was between them" can be read either as exculpatory (David honored covenant where he could) or as a tell (David chose which Saulide line to keep alive on his own terms).

Three Hebrew terms are central. The first is *kipper* (כִּפֵּר), "make atonement" (v. 3) — the verb that elsewhere governs the priestly atonement system but here describes a strictly horizontal, intra-human reparation between David's house and the Gibeonites. The second is *yaqa'* (יָקַע), traditionally "hang" or "expose" but more precisely "to dislocate" or "impale" — the same verb appears in Numbers 25:4, where Phinehas is told to *yaqa'* the Israelite leaders "before the LORD" in response to apostasy. The technical phrase "before YHWH" (*lifney YHWH*) marks this as a cultic act of bloodguilt expiation, not merely an execution. The third is *ḥesed* (חֶסֶד) — the covenant loyalty that David invokes to spare Mephibosheth, and which Rizpah enacts in the bone-vigil that follows, even though the text never names her loyalty by that word.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The Samuel Appendix is one of the Hebrew Bible's most discussed editorial structures. Brevard Childs in his *Introduction to the Old Testament as Scripture* and Cheryl Exum in *Tragedy and Biblical Narrative* both follow Klaus Koch and Walter Brueggemann in identifying a chiastic arrangement: A) Saulide bloodguilt (21:1-14), B) heroes against giants (21:15-22), C) David's Song (22), C') David's last words (23:1-7), B') list of mighty men (23:8-39), A') census-plague resolved at the future Temple site (24). The chiasm is not arbitrary: A and A' both treat divine wrath against Israel resolved by ritual reparation and burial/altar; B and B' both list named warriors; C and C' both contain Davidic poetry. Chapter 21, then, is the opening hinge of a deliberately composed appendix, not an editorial leftover.

Within chapter 21 itself, the Rizpah episode (vv. 10-14) is one of the most carefully shaped narrative units in Samuel. The narrator gives almost no dialogue and almost no interior life — Rizpah does not speak — and yet the scene generates extraordinary emotional density through repetition, time-marking, and physical detail. Rizpah is named by patronymic and tribal status three times in five verses ("Rizpah the daughter of Aiah," "Rizpah the daughter of Aiah," "Rizpah the daughter of Aiah, the concubine of Saul"). The temporal frame is exact: from the beginning of barley harvest (April) "until rain fell upon them from the heavens" — which in the Levantine climate means the early rains of October-November, so a vigil of perhaps six months. The threats are catalogued in formal cosmic pairs: birds by day, beasts by night. The image is iconic and has resonated in art and liturgy across Jewish and Christian traditions: the woman on the rock, the spread sackcloth, the corpses, the silent watch.

Robert Alter, in *The David Story*, calls the Rizpah scene "perhaps the most haunting set piece in the entire Samuel narrative." J. P. Fokkelman (*Narrative Art and Poetry in the Books of Samuel*, vol. 3) analyzes its rhetorical compression: the scene is built almost entirely from verbs of action and physical objects, refusing the kind of psychological access the Court History so often grants to David and Joab. The narrator forces us to watch what Rizpah does, day and night, season into season, without ever telling us what she thinks. Meir Sternberg's principle that the Bible's silences are as deliberate as its speeches finds here a paradigmatic example.

The textual variant in verse 8 is significant. The MT reads "the five sons of Michal the daughter of Saul, whom she bore to Adriel" — but Michal, per 2 Samuel 6:23, "had no child to the day of her death." The LXX (and 4QSam-a) read "Merab," consistent with 1 Samuel 18:19, which records Merab being given to Adriel the Meholathite. Most modern critics — including McCarter (Anchor Bible), A. A. Anderson (WBC), and Tsumura (NICOT) — accept Merab as original and treat the MT's "Michal" as an early scribal corruption. ESV's "Merab" reflects this textual judgment.

The four-giants list (vv. 15-22) is itself a literary form: a structured catalog with stereotyped openings ("there was again war with the Philistines"), each entry naming a Philistine champion, his anomalous attribute (giant size, monstrous spear, six fingers and toes), and the Israelite who killed him. Verse 19 contains the famous textual crux: "Elhanan the son of Jaare-oregim, the Bethlehemite, struck down Goliath the Gittite, the shaft of whose spear was like a weaver's beam." This directly contradicts 1 Samuel 17, where David kills Goliath. The Chronicler's harmonization at 1 Chronicles 20:5 reads "Elhanan… struck down Lahmi the brother of Goliath." Modern critical opinion, going back to Julius Wellhausen and continuing through McCarter and Halpern, generally treats 2 Samuel 21:19 as the older tradition: Elhanan killed Goliath, and the David-Goliath story of 1 Samuel 17 is a later transfer of the deed to the more famous king. The Chronicler harmonized the embarrassment.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical placement of chapter 21 invites readings in several directions. Backward, the chapter resolves a covenant breach that the Joshua narrative had carefully recorded (Joshua 9) and reaches all the way back to Saul's first battles (vaguely alluded to as the original violation). Forward, the bone-burial of Saul, Jonathan, and the seven impaled Saulides at Zela in Benjamin completes the long arc that began with the men of Jabesh-gilead retrieving Saul's body from Beth-shan in 1 Samuel 31. (Beth-shan, modern Tel Beit She'an, has been extensively excavated since the 1920s and most recently by Amihai Mazar; the tell preserves a Late Bronze and Iron I temple complex consistent with the biblical description of Saul's body being hung on the city wall.) Only after this reburial does "God respond to the plea for the land," closing the famine and closing the Saul story.

Within Jewish interpretation, Josephus (*Antiquities* 7.12) recounts the Gibeonite episode with apologetic care, emphasizing David's reluctance and the legitimacy of Gibeonite demand. The rabbinic tradition wrestles deeply with the chapter. Tractate Yevamot 78b-79a treats the Gibeonite episode as the foundational case for the doctrine that an oath sworn by Israel's leaders binds the nation across generations — and that breach of such an oath produces national consequences. Rashi reads the famine as direct divine vindication of Gibeonite right; Radak develops the theology of intergenerational guilt with theological caution; Ralbag (Gersonides) raises the moral question of why the sons should die for the father's sin and proposes that the seven Saulides themselves had personally collaborated in Saul's purge. Abarbanel, with his characteristic political acuity, reads the episode as a study in the inescapable demands of public reparation: a ruler who has inherited an unresolved wrong cannot defer it without cost.

The Rizpah scene, in particular, has generated rich rabbinic and modern Jewish commentary. The rabbis in Yevamot 79a actually attribute Israel's later admission of converts to the impression made by Rizpah's vigil: when the surrounding nations saw the king of Israel honor the daughter of a concubine who refused to abandon her dead, they concluded that this was a people worth joining. Avivah Zornberg (*The Murmuring Deep* and her broader oeuvre) has read Rizpah as a figure of unappeased mourning, a theological witness against the easy closure of public reparations. Yair Zakovitch reads the scene as preserving women's voice precisely through silent action — Rizpah does not argue with the king, she simply sits on the rock until the king cannot ignore her.

Christian interpretation has often passed too quickly over Rizpah. Patristic commentators (Origen on 2 Samuel; Gregory the Great in the *Moralia in Job*) tended to allegorize her vigil as the church guarding the bones of the martyrs. Augustine in *City of God* 17.20 treats the Gibeonite episode as a case study in the seriousness of sworn oaths and the consequences of violating them. Calvin's commentary, more pastorally acute, reads David's reburial as an act of belated kingly justice and Rizpah's vigil as a mute prophetic accusation that finally moves the king to do what he should have done years earlier.

The richest contemporary engagement comes from feminist scholarship in the tradition initiated by Phyllis Trible's *Texts of Terror*. Cheryl Exum, in *Tragedy and Biblical Narrative* and *Fragmented Women*, argues that Rizpah is the moral center of the chapter and arguably of the whole Samuel Appendix: she is the figure whose silent fidelity exposes the inadequacy of the ritual settlement, the woman who refuses to let the dead become political invisibilia. Mieke Bal, Athalya Brenner, and Tikva Frymer-Kensky (in *Reading the Women of the Bible*) develop variations on this reading. Frymer-Kensky in particular notes that Rizpah does what Antigone does in Sophocles — defies political authority to honor the bodies of the dead — but that the biblical text, unlike the Greek, reserves no punishment for her: instead, the king is moved to act, and the divine response follows. Ellen Davis in *Wondrous Depth* picks up this thread and argues that Rizpah is one of the great teachers in the Hebrew Bible of what it means to mourn truthfully in the face of state violence. The figure has had a steady afterlife in modern art and protest movements, from Toni Morrison's allusive use of maternal vigil to the Madres de la Plaza de Mayo holding photographs of the disappeared in Buenos Aires.

The four-giants list draws less interpretive attention but does generate one large issue: the Elhanan-Goliath problem (v. 19). The patristic harmonizations (Theodoret of Cyrus, Pseudo-Jerome) generally followed the Chronicler in treating Elhanan's victim as Goliath's brother. Reformation commentators (Calvin, then later Matthew Henry) accepted the harmonization with varying degrees of ease. Modern critical scholarship — McCarter, Halpern, Walter Dietrich, and most recently Jacob Wright (*David, King of Israel, and Caleb in Biblical Memory*) — generally treats 2 Samuel 21:19 as preserving an older tradition that the David-Goliath narrative later overwrote, raising large questions about how the Davidic legend grew over time.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions pulse through the chapter.

The first is *intergenerational guilt and individual justice*. The Gibeonites demand seven Saulides for Saul's sin, and YHWH appears to authorize the demand. This stands in apparent tension with Deuteronomy 24:16 ("fathers shall not be put to death because of their children, nor children put to death because of their fathers; each one shall be put to death for his own sin") and Ezekiel 18 ("the soul that sins, it shall die"). Three positions. First, the classical rabbinic and patristic harmonization (Rashi, Augustine): the seven Saulides shared in Saul's guilt by participation, so their deaths are not vicarious but individually deserved; the text simply does not bother to record their personal complicity. Second, the realist-political reading (McKenzie, Halpern, Brueggemann): the chapter preserves a more archaic conception of corporate identity in which the king's house bears the king's guilt, and the Deuteronomy/Ezekiel rule represents a later theological refinement that the Samuel author has not yet absorbed; we have here a window onto theological development within the canon itself. Third, the theological reading (Karl Barth, then Eugene Peterson and Ellen Davis): the chapter is descriptive of a real historical settlement, not prescriptive of divine justice; the narrator records what happened and what was understood to have happened, and the canonical voice in Deuteronomy 24 and Ezekiel 18 corrects rather than endorses the operative theology of the episode. Steelmanning the realist-political reading: the text genuinely does not flinch from corporate punishment, and the modern reader who insists on individual justice is reading later canonical voices back onto an earlier one. **Where this leaves us:** the canon itself contains a tension it does not resolve, and the reader is forced to decide whether scripture's authority lies in any single moment of its composition or in the dialogue among them.

The second tension is *the moral economy of ritual reparation versus enduring witness*. The chapter formally settles the Gibeonite grievance through the impalement, but the narrative will not let us treat the matter as closed: Rizpah's vigil exposes the inadequacy of any settlement that leaves the dead unburied. Three positions. First, the priestly-cultic reading (drawing on Jacob Milgrom's work on bloodguilt): the impalement "before YHWH" is the operative atonement, and the burial is supplementary closure; the chapter affirms that ritual reparation works. Second, the prophetic-protest reading (Brueggemann, Davis, Trible-tradition feminist scholarship): the chapter affirms that no ritual settlement is complete without the embodied honoring of the dead; Rizpah is the prophet of the chapter, and David's ritual was inadequate until her vigil forced its completion. Third, a synthesis (Childs, Goldingay): the chapter preserves both — the ritual settlement is necessary but not sufficient, and the divine response (rain, the lifting of famine) comes only after both the ritual and the vigil are complete. Steelmanning the prophetic-protest reading: the text gives Rizpah more narrative space than it gives the impalement itself, suggesting where the editorial weight lies. **Where this leaves us:** the chapter teaches that ritual and witness are both required; the polity that performs the rite without honoring the dead has not yet finished what it claimed to begin.

One unresolved question: why does YHWH speak only at the start of the chapter, identifying Saul's bloodguilt, and then go silent through the entire impalement and vigil? The text records no divine word approving the Gibeonite demand of seven specific Saulides; YHWH names the cause of the famine but does not prescribe the remedy. The remedy is negotiated between David and the Gibeonites. The reader is left to decide whether the impalement is what YHWH wanted, what the Gibeonites wanted, or what David was willing to give the Gibeonites in order to be done with the Saulide line.

## The Thread

Chapter 21 connects to several large biblical and historical movements. First, the Joshua 9 covenant with the Gibeonites is the explicit prior text — chapter 21 is the long-deferred consequence of an oath sworn under deception but binding nonetheless, and it raises the canonical question of how covenants made wrongly can still bind rightly. Second, Rizpah's vigil belongs to a small tradition of women whose silent fidelity to the dead exposes the limits of male political action — a tradition that includes the women at the cross in the Gospels, who follow Jesus to the tomb when the male disciples have fled, and the women of 1 Maccabees who mourn the desecrated temple. Third, the long arc of Saul's bones — from Gilboa (1 Sam 31), to Beth-shan, to Jabesh-gilead, to Zela in Benjamin — finds its closure here; the Saul story does not end at 1 Samuel 31 but at 2 Samuel 21:14, and only with the burial does the land receive its rain. Fourth, the four-giants list anticipates the larger David-as-warrior-king memory that flowers in Chronicles, the Psalms, and the messianic readings of Isaiah and the New Testament; the chapter is part of how Israel built its memory of David as the man who finished the Philistine wars Saul had only begun. Flag for later: the Elhanan-Goliath crux at v. 19 is one of the clearest examples in the Hebrew Bible of legendary growth — a seed for any serious conversation about how scripture handles its own historical layers.
