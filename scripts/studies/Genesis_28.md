# Genesis 28: The Fugitive's Stairway

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Genesis 28 drops us into the Middle Bronze IIA (c. 2000–1800 BCE), the world of the patriarchal narratives as scholars have reconstructed it from the Mari archives, the Execration Texts, and excavations across the Levant. Jacob leaves Beersheba in the Negev for Paddan-aram (northwest Mesopotamia, near modern Harran in southern Turkey) — a journey of roughly 400 miles along the trunk road through the hill country, across the Jezreel Valley, up through Damascus and out to the upper Euphrates. For a man traveling alone, that is weeks of exposure. He stops at what the narrator pointedly calls "a certain place" (v. 11) — a deliberately unspecified site that the story then transforms.

The political landscape is pre-imperial. No great power yet dominates the southern Levant. Egypt's Middle Kingdom (Twelfth Dynasty) exerts influence through the Execration Texts (figurines inscribed with the names of rebellious Asiatic princes and ritually smashed), but direct control is light. Canaan is a patchwork of city-states and tribal territories. The routes Jacob travels are controlled by local kings whose names appear at Mari and Ebla — Hazor, Shechem, Megiddo, Damascus. A wealthy semi-nomadic family like Isaac's moved through this patchwork with carefully negotiated safe-conduct agreements; Jacob, alone and carrying his father's blessing but none of his father's wealth, has none of that insulation.

The material culture of this encounter is striking. Jacob sleeps outdoors with a stone for a pillow. The Hebrew word translated "pillow" (*meraʾashotav* מְרַאֲשֹׁתָיו) literally means "at his head-places" — plural, suggesting he arranged several stones to cradle his head against the ground. Archaeologists at Tel Beitin (the likely site of ancient Bethel, excavated by W. F. Albright in 1934 and James Kelso in 1954, 1957, and 1960) found a Middle Bronze Age city gate and massive fortification walls, confirming Bethel was already a significant settlement when the patriarchal narratives are set. But the narrator is careful: Jacob sleeps *outside* the city, in the open — this is theophany in wilderness, not in temple.

The dream itself is saturated with Mesopotamian iconography. The *sullam* (סֻלָּם, v. 12) — traditionally translated "ladder" — is almost certainly something closer to a **ziggurat stairway**. The word appears only here in the Hebrew Bible. The Akkadian cognate *simmiltu* means "stairway" and is used for the temple stairways at ziggurats like the Etemenanki at Babylon and the great ziggurat at Ur (excavated by Leonard Woolley 1922–1934). Mesopotamian ziggurats were understood as *axis mundi* — points where heaven and earth touched, where messengers of the gods ascended and descended. Jacob, traveling toward the Mesopotamian heartland, dreams in Mesopotamian vocabulary.

**Authorship and dating**: Classical source criticism assigns most of this chapter to the Elohist (E) source — note the divine name *Elohim* dominates vv. 12, 17, 20-22, though *YHWH* appears at vv. 13, 16, suggesting a JE redaction where the Yahwist's covenant speech has been inserted into an Elohist dream narrative. Von Rad (*Genesis*, 1961) read v. 22's tithe promise as a cultic etiology for Bethel's later priesthood. Post-Wellhausen scholarship (Westermann, Blum) has complicated the neat source division, but most still see the Bethel etiology as shaped by the northern kingdom's effort to legitimate Bethel as a rival to Jerusalem after Jeroboam I's religious reforms (1 Kings 12:26-33).

**Key terms**:

- *sullam* (סֻלָּם, v. 12) — "stairway/ramp," not "ladder." The ziggurat connection transforms the image: Jacob sees the infrastructure of an ancient Near Eastern temple, projected across the sky at a site with no temple. English "ladder" suggests a rickety wooden contrivance; the Hebrew suggests monumental architecture.
- *massebah* (מַצֵּבָה, vv. 18, 22) — "standing stone," a cultic marker. Massebot have been excavated at Gezer, Hazor, Arad, and Tel Dan. Later Deuteronomic law (Deut 16:22) will condemn them as Canaanite; here the patriarch erects one without shame.
- *Beth-El* (בֵּית־אֵל, v. 19) — "House of God/El." Almost certainly the same *El* who is the Canaanite high god at Ugarit, here appropriated and identified with YHWH. The renaming from Luz to Bethel is an act of sacred cartography.
- *shaʿar ha-shamayim* (שַׁעַר הַשָּׁמָיִם, v. 17) — "gate of heaven." *Bab-ili* (Babel/Babylon) means exactly the same thing in Akkadian. Jacob is having a Babel-experience in reverse: at Babel humans tried to build up to God (Gen 11); at Bethel God's stairway comes down.
- *pachad* (פַּחַד, v. 17) — Jacob's "fear/dread." Rudolf Otto's *mysterium tremendum*. Not anxiety; numinous awe.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: **theophany narrative embedded in patriarchal saga**, with a formal **promise speech** at its center and an **etiological conclusion** (vv. 18-22) that explains why Bethel became a cultic site. The dream-vision form is a recognized Ancient Near Eastern type-scene (Robert Alter, *The Art of Biblical Narrative*), with parallels in the Egyptian *Dream Stele of Thutmose IV* and the Akkadian dream reports at Mari. In every case, a figure sleeps at a sacred site and receives divinely-mandated instructions.

The chapter divides cleanly in two halves that mirror each other:

**Part 1 (vv. 1-9): The sending.** Isaac blesses and dispatches Jacob; Esau, overhearing, tries to correct his own situation by marrying Ishmael's daughter Mahalath. This is tragicomedy: Esau keeps trying to please his parents by making the "right" marriage, and keeps missing the point. The narrator's flatness toward Esau here is devastating.

**Part 2 (vv. 10-22): The encounter.** Jacob dreams, wakes, names, vows.

The literary centerpiece is a precisely constructed **chiasm** at vv. 13-15:

> A. I am YHWH, the God of Abraham your father and the God of Isaac (v. 13a)
>  B. The land on which you lie I will give to you and to your offspring (v. 13b)
>   C. Your offspring shall be like the dust of the earth (v. 14a)
>    D. You shall spread abroad to the west and to the east and to the north and to the south (v. 14b)
>   C'. In you and your offspring all the families of the earth shall be blessed (v. 14c)
>  B'. I am with you and will keep you wherever you go and will bring you back to this land (v. 15a)
> A'. I will not leave you until I have done what I have promised (v. 15b)

The structure is not ornament. It is theology: at the center is *dispersion and return* — exactly the psychological and geographical crisis Jacob faces. The promise *encases* the dispersion. He goes out, but the word holds him.

The narrator also deploys rich **wordplay**. Jacob "happens upon" (*vayyifgaʿ*, וַיִּפְגַּע, v. 11) the place — the verb can mean "to encounter" or "to strike against." He stumbles into sacredness. He takes *from the stones* (plural, v. 11) and places them *at his head*, then wakes and takes *the stone* (singular, v. 18) and makes it a pillar — the plurality of the stones of exile gives way to the single stone of consecration. Ancient Near Eastern readers would have heard in this the pattern of **temple foundation rituals**, where kings would lay foundation stones with inscribed cylinders and anoint them with oil.

Textually, the chapter is stable. The Masoretic Text agrees closely with the Samaritan Pentateuch and the Septuagint. The Targum Onkelos expands v. 12 — angels "going up and coming down *upon him*" — an important interpretive move that we will meet again.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Place this in the OT arc: creation → covenant → kingdom → exile → hope. Genesis 28 sits squarely in the **covenant** movement, but it forecasts **exile and return**. Jacob is the covenant bearer (the Abrahamic promise has just been formally transmitted to him, vv. 3-4), but he is also the proto-exile — sent out of the land, preserved abroad, eventually returned. His whole life becomes a rehearsal of Israel's national story. This is why the rabbis read the chapter the way they did: they were reading themselves.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

**Second Temple** readers were fascinated by the ladder. *Jubilees* 27 retells the chapter and expands Jacob's vow. The *Testament of Levi* reworks the ladder into Levi's own ascent vision. Philo of Alexandria (*De Somniis* 1.133-156) allegorizes the *sullam* as the ascending and descending motions of the soul between body and intellect — the first systematic mystical reading of the passage. Philo's ladder is the one Neoplatonism later climbs.

**Rabbinic** interpretation exploded in creativity. *Bereshit Rabbah* 68-69 (compiled c. 5th c. CE, but preserving earlier traditions) offers multiple readings. One famous midrash sees the angels "ascending and descending" as the **four kingdoms** that would rule over Israel — Babylon, Media, Greece, Rome — each rising on the ladder and falling, with Rome climbing and then terrifyingly *not descending*, the foreshadowing of prolonged exile. Another reading asks why angels are **ascending first** (they are already on earth) and concludes that Jacob had been accompanied by angels in Canaan who now had to return because their jurisdiction was Eretz Yisrael, while new angels descended to accompany him in exile. The angels mark the boundary of the Land.

**Medieval** readings varied sharply. **Rashi** (1040–1105, Troyes) follows the midrashic four-kingdoms reading and adds that the stone Jacob slept on was the foundation stone (*even ha-shetiyah*) of the future Temple in Jerusalem — identifying Bethel with Mount Moriah. **Ibn Ezra** (1089–1167) resists allegorical excess: the dream is real; the ladder is the ladder; God speaks. **Nachmanides/Ramban** (1194–1270) develops an elaborate kabbalistic reading where the ladder is a theurgic conduit and the *Shechinah* stands at its head; Jacob's consecration of the stone is a channeling of divine energy into the material world. **Maimonides** (*Guide of the Perplexed* 1:15) reads the ladder as the philosopher-prophet's intellectual ascent through the orders of being to the Active Intellect — the Aristotelian Jacob.

**Modern** Jewish readers have reopened the text. **Martin Buber** (*Tales of the Hasidim*, *I and Thou*) read Bethel as the paradigmatic I-Thou encounter: the wayfarer in a strange place addressed by name. **Jon Levenson** (*Sinai and Zion*, 1985) emphasizes the **contested holiness** of Bethel — the text was a flashpoint between the northern kingdom's sanctuary theology and the Deuteronomist's centralization program. **Avivah Zornberg** (*The Beginning of Desire*, 1995) reads Jacob's dream as a psychological event: the fugitive who has just stolen his brother's blessing encounters, in the unconscious, the affirmation he has been trying to steal. **James Kugel** (*The Ladder of Jacob*, 2006) devotes an entire monograph to the chapter's reception history, tracing how ancient readers kept adding layers (Jacob's face on the throne, angels gazing at their counterpart on earth) to make sense of a fundamentally strange text.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

**Patristic** readers fastened on the ladder through one verse that changed everything: **John 1:51**, where Jesus tells Nathanael, "Truly, truly, I say to you, you will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man." This single intertext anchored centuries of Christian interpretation. **Origen** (*Homilies on Genesis* 27) reads the ladder as Christ and the angels as the church's preachers. **Augustine** (*Sermon* 79 on Genesis, and *Contra Faustum*) goes further: "the ladder is Christ, because by him we ascend from earth to heaven." **Chrysostom** (*Homilies on Genesis* 54) turns more pastoral — Jacob's piety in adversity, God's fidelity to the undeserving. **Irenaeus** (*Against Heresies* 3.24.2) makes the Christological typology structural: every OT theophany is proto-incarnational.

**Medieval** Christian readers extended the Christological reading into architecture. **Aquinas** (*Summa* II-II, q. 180) uses the ladder to structure the orders of contemplation; the Pseudo-Dionysian angelic hierarchies climb it. **Bernard of Clairvaux** (*The Steps of Humility and Pride*, c. 1125) inverts it: the ladder is humility, climbed downward — the soul descending through degrees of self-knowledge rather than ascending through degrees of merit. **Hildegard of Bingen** receives the ladder in her visions as a trembling cord between heaven and earth. The Glossa Ordinaria (the standard medieval commentary) preserves the Christological reading with added sacramental layers.

**Reformation** readings preserved the Christological core but accented different things. **Luther** (*Lectures on Genesis*, 1535-36) insisted the ladder is Christ and the promise is purely unilateral — Jacob did not merit this dream, the God of grace addresses a sinner on the run. **Calvin** (*Commentary on Genesis*, 1554) read the chapter covenantally: God's fidelity to Jacob despite Jacob's failures demonstrates the unconditional, sovereign character of election. Calvin is less interested in the ladder's iconography; he wants the *promise*.

**Historical-critical** scholarship (Wellhausen, Gunkel, von Rad, Noth, Westermann) largely set the Christological reading aside and reconstructed the chapter as a composite cultic etiology. Bethel, in this reading, was a northern sanctuary whose legitimacy the patriarchal narrative secured retroactively. Hermann Gunkel's *Genesis* commentary (1901) placed the dream in the comparative context of Mesopotamian temple-foundation rituals.

**Contemporary** Christian scholarship has returned, in a chastened way, to the earlier readings. **N. T. Wright** reads Bethel as a pre-echo of the incarnation — the "overlap of heaven and earth" that will be consummated in Christ. **Gordon Wenham** (*Genesis 16-50*, 1994) sees the chapter as establishing Bethel as a proto-temple, with all the cultic vocabulary (pillar, oil, vow, tithe) later formalized in Israel's worship. **Walter Brueggemann** (*Genesis*, Interpretation commentary, 1982) emphasizes the theology of **liminal space**: Jacob is encountered by God precisely in the no-place between homes, and that is where the covenant gets renewed — grace meets the fugitive on the road.

### Where the Traditions Converge and Diverge

Both traditions affirm: Jacob has a genuine theophany. God renews the Abrahamic covenant unconditionally. The place becomes sacred.

They divide on what the ladder *means*. For Jewish readers, the ladder is Israel's story — the angels are the kingdoms, the stone is the Temple, the dream is the covenantal architecture of history. For Christian readers, the ladder is Christ — the one who bridges heaven and earth — and the dream is incarnational typology. The stakes are Christology: Jewish readers see Israel fulfilling the vision; Christian readers see Jesus fulfilling (or replacing) the vision. After the Holocaust and *Nostra Aetate* (1965), Christian scholarship has become far more reluctant to frame this as replacement; readers like Wright increasingly speak of fulfillment-within rather than fulfillment-against.

**Where this leaves us**: The chapter is one of the most contested pieces of Scripture precisely because its imagery is so generative. Both trajectories are drawing out something real in the text. The text says *both* less and more than either tradition has claimed: less, because it doesn't name Christ or the four kingdoms; more, because its image of heaven's open gate over the sleeping fugitive has kept producing readings for 2,500 years.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What does the narrator believe God is doing in Genesis 28?

The text's theological vision is severe and consoling at once: **God renews the Abrahamic covenant with a man who has just committed grievous deception, without waiting for repentance.** Jacob has stolen his brother's blessing (Gen 27), broken his father's heart, and is fleeing for his life. He has not confessed. He has not changed. And at the first stop out of Canaan, God appears on a stairway and says, *"I am with you... I will not leave you until I have done what I have promised."*

This is the theological claim the narrator stakes: YHWH's covenant is **unconditional and pursuing**. The covenant comes to Jacob; Jacob does not rise to it.

### Tension 1 — Grace and Merit

If God renews the covenant with the deceiver, what does that say about divine justice?

- **Reformed** (Calvin, *Commentary on Genesis*; modern: Michael Horton): pure unconditional election. Jacob's moral status is irrelevant; the covenant is monergistic. The dream *is* the doctrine of grace in narrative form.
- **Wesleyan/Arminian** (John Wesley, *Notes on the Old Testament*; modern: Roger Olson): God foreknows Jacob's eventual character — the chapter anticipates Jacob's transformation at Peniel (Gen 32). The grace is still prevenient, not earned, but it is going somewhere.
- **Catholic** (CCC 2573; Augustine, *Contra Julianum*): Jacob's cooperation will come; the dream initiates a process of sanctification that the patriarch must complete. Grace justifies and transforms.
- **Eastern Orthodox** (John of Damascus, *Exposition of the Orthodox Faith*; modern: Lossky, *Mystical Theology*): synergy. The theophany offers itself; Jacob's vow (vv. 20-22) is his synergistic response. The ladder goes both ways.
- **Jewish** (Rashi following *Bereshit Rabbah*): *zekhut avot* — the merit of the fathers. Abraham's faithfulness covers Jacob. The covenant is not earned by Jacob but is honored in him because of the father's line.
- **Critical/academic** (Westermann, *Genesis 12-36*): the text preserves an ancient theology of favor that predates later moral systematization. The narrator is not embarrassed by divine partiality; embarrassment is ours.

**Steelman the reading I find least intuitive.** The Reformed reading seems harsh — the deceiver is simply chosen; the cheated brother is simply not. But consider: if the covenant depended on Jacob's moral worth, it would collapse *immediately*. The only way the Abrahamic promise survives contact with Jacob is if it rests entirely on God's fidelity and not on the patriarch's. The harsh reading is the only one that leaves the promise standing. The text may be less interested in Jacob's morality than in God's refusal to be contingent on it.

**Where this leaves us**: The chapter will not let grace and merit be separated cleanly. Jacob is both elected and will be transformed; the covenant is both unconditional and does something to him. Post-Reformation debate has often forced a choice the text refuses.

### Tension 2 — Sacred Space and the Contested Sanctuary

Jacob says, "This is the gate of heaven" (v. 17) and consecrates Bethel. But Bethel, later, becomes the site of Jeroboam's golden calves (1 Kings 12:28-29) and is denounced by Amos (Amos 4:4, "Come to Bethel, and transgress"). The same place that receives the patriarchal theophany becomes the site of the northern kingdom's apostasy. What is the theology of sacred space here?

- **Catholic** (Lumen Gentium; Joseph Ratzinger, *The Spirit of the Liturgy*): sacramental realism. Places really do become vessels of grace through consecration and repeated divine action. Bethel's corruption does not invalidate the original theophany; it testifies to human failure to honor holy ground.
- **Reformed** (Calvin; modern: John Frame, *Worship in Spirit and Truth*): the ladder is now Christ; after the incarnation, no geographical space is ontologically sacred. John 4:21-24 closes this chapter permanently.
- **Lutheran** (Luther, *Lectures on Genesis*): the Word makes any place sacred; Bethel's sanctity was always derivative of the promise, never intrinsic.
- **Eastern Orthodox** (Alexander Schmemann, *For the Life of the World*): sacramental cosmology — the whole creation is potentially transparent to the divine, and particular places become more transparent through liturgical use.
- **Jewish** (traditional): Bethel is identified midrashically with Mount Moriah = the Temple Mount. All sacred space converges on Jerusalem, and Bethel's theophany is displaced into the Temple.
- **Critical** (Wellhausen; Levenson, *Sinai and Zion*): the text preserves a specifically northern sanctuary theology that the later Deuteronomic editors tolerated but subordinated. The tension between Bethel and Jerusalem in the canon is not accidentally residual; it is doctrinally significant.

**Where this leaves us**: The chapter plants a theological question about sacred space that later biblical literature never fully resolves. The New Testament moves toward a Christological relocation of the sacred (John 2:19-21: the temple is Jesus's body), but the Old Testament, and the Jewish tradition reading out of it, resists the move. Whether God "dwells" in places or in persons is a choice each tradition makes differently.

---

**The unresolved question**: Jacob wakes from the dream and says, "Surely the LORD is in this place, and I did not know it" (v. 16). He has just committed fraud; he is running from his brother; he has nothing; and God has met him. What places in your life have you been too frightened or ashamed to recognize as ground that God might already be standing on?

## The Thread

- **John 1:51** — "You will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man." Jesus takes the Bethel vision and places himself at its center. For Nathanael — who moments earlier is called "an Israelite indeed, in whom there is no deceit" (v. 47, pointedly the opposite of Jacob) — this rewrites Genesis 28 as a promise about Christ.

- **Hosea 12:4-5** — The prophet reaches back to Bethel as a locus of authentic encounter with God, precisely to indict the northern kingdom for corrupting it. The Bethel-as-glory and Bethel-as-sin double-vision runs through the prophets.

- **1 Kings 12:26-33** — Jeroboam I installs golden calves at Bethel and Dan. The site of Jacob's theophany becomes the site of Israel's first major post-Davidic apostasy. The dissonance is theological: sacred places can be inherited and desecrated.

- **Revelation 21:2-3** — When you get to the last chapters of the canon, notice how the image reverses. In Genesis 28, the ladder bridges heaven and earth at one consecrated spot. In Revelation 21, the holy city *descends*, and "the dwelling place of God is with man." Bethel becomes geography; Bethel becomes person; Bethel becomes cosmos.
