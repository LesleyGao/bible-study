# Psalm 74 — Why Do You Cast Us Off Forever?

## Lens 1: Historical World

Psalm 74 carries the superscription *maskil le-Asaph* (מַשְׂכִּיל לְאָסָף), "a maskil of Asaph." It is the second psalm of the Asaphite cluster (Pss 73-83) and the second psalm in Book III. Like Psalm 73, it is part of the Elohistic Psalter (Pss 42-83), and across its 23 verses it consistently addresses *Elohim* rather than *YHWH* — though the divine name *YHWH* breaks through at v. 18, signaling the shift to direct covenantal address at the climactic appeal. The Asaphite attribution places the psalm within the Levitical singing guild traced to David's appointments in 1 Chronicles 16:5 and 25:1-9, but the content of the psalm forces the reading well past David — to a moment when the temple is in ruins.

The Hebrew term *maskil* (מַשְׂכִּיל), from the root *sakal* (to be wise, to teach, to make insightful), denotes a didactic or contemplative composition. Of the Psalter's thirteen *maskilim*, four are Asaphite (Pss 74, 78, 88, 89 in some readings). Whether *maskil* designates a musical genre, a wisdom subgenre, or a particular liturgical use is debated — Mowinckel preferred a musical reading, Gunkel a didactic one, and Goldingay treats it as a category that signals reflective depth.

Gunkel's form-critical taxonomy classifies Psalm 74 as a *Volksklagelied* — a community lament — and it stands as one of the clearest examples in the Psalter, alongside Pss 44, 60, 79, 80, 83, and 137. The classical structural elements of community lament are all present: opening invocation (vv. 1-3), description of distress (vv. 4-9), accusatory questions (vv. 10-11), confession of God's saving acts (vv. 12-17), petition (vv. 18-23). Sigmund Mowinckel and Erhard Gerstenberger placed the psalm in the cult-functional category of communal mourning liturgy, possibly recited at a fast-day commemorating the temple's destruction.

The dating question is the major issue. The psalm's vivid description of the sanctuary's destruction in vv. 3-8 has produced two main historical proposals.

The mainstream-critical position dates the psalm to the **Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 587/586 BC**, when Nebuchadnezzar's army burned the First Temple and razed the city (2 Kgs 25:8-10, Jer 52:12-14). This reading is held by Hossfeld and Zenger in the *Hermeneia Psalmen* commentary, by Hans-Joachim Kraus in his *Psalmen* commentary, by John Goldingay in his three-volume *Psalms*, and by the majority of modern critical scholarship. The arguments include: (1) the totality of destruction described — the sanctuary on fire (v. 7), all the meeting places of God burned in the land (v. 8); (2) the absence of a prophet (v. 9), which fits the early exilic period before Ezekiel's vocation matures and after the deportation of Jeremiah; (3) the perpetual-ruins language (*mashshu'ot netzach*, v. 3), which suggests a destruction not yet rebuilt.

The minority position dates the psalm to the **Maccabean crisis of 167-164 BC**, when Antiochus IV Epiphanes desecrated the Second Temple, set up the abomination of desolation (1 Macc 1:54), burned its gates (1 Macc 4:38: "they saw the sanctuary desolate, the altar profaned, and the gates burned"), and forbade the practice of Judaism. This reading was held by Theodore of Mopsuestia, by some medieval Jewish commentators, and was revived in the 19th and early 20th century critical literature. Arguments include: (1) the reference to setting up "their own signs for signs" (v. 4) fits the introduction of pagan cult-objects into the Temple; (2) the absence of a prophet fits 1 Macc 4:46 and 9:27 which lament prophetic absence; (3) the *moadei-El* (meeting places of God) burning fits the Seleucid attempt to suppress synagogue worship throughout Judea. The major problem with the Maccabean dating is that the Temple was not actually burned in 167 BC — it was desecrated but not razed — which has led most modern scholars back to the Babylonian dating, with the recognition that some details (the *moadei-El*, the absent prophet) carry their own complications regardless of dating.

The unique term *moadei-El* (מוֹעֲדֵי־אֵל, v. 8), "the meeting places of God," is one of the most debated phrases in the psalm. The plural construct is grammatically clear, but the referent is contested. Mowinckel and Albertz read it as evidence of a *plurality* of Yahweh-shrines across the land prior to Josiah's centralizing reform of 622 BC (or, on a later dating, the lingering memory of such shrines). Goldingay treats *moadei-El* as a poetic plural for the single sanctuary complex. Hossfeld-Zenger read it as referring to local synagogues or assembly-places that had developed in the Second Temple period. Each reading carries different implications for Israelite cult-history; Rainer Albertz's *A History of Israelite Religion in the Old Testament Period* uses Psalm 74:8 as one piece of evidence for the multi-shrine character of pre-Deuteronomistic Yahwism.

The cosmic combat language of vv. 13-15 reaches deep into ANE mythology. The verbs *parar* (you divided, v. 13) and *shibbar* (you broke, v. 13) recall the *Enuma Elish* — the Babylonian creation epic in which Marduk splits Tiamat — but the closer parallel is the Ugaritic Baal cycle, where Baal defeats Yam (the sea, *yam*) and Lotan (the seven-headed sea-dragon, cognate with biblical *Leviathan*). Frank Moore Cross's *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* (1973) traced Psalm 74:13-14 directly to West Semitic mythological substrate, arguing that the psalm has appropriated the Combat Myth of the storm-god's victory over chaos and applied it to YHWH's creative-redemptive work. Mark Smith's *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* and *The Early History of God* refined this reading, showing how Israelite tradition retained the mythic structure while reattributing the victory exclusively to YHWH. Jon Levenson, in *Creation and the Persistence of Evil*, drew the theological consequence: biblical creation is depicted not as creation *ex nihilo* but as the conquest of chaos, an ongoing victory whose finality is eschatological.

The Hebrew terms anchor the historical and mythological dimensions. The word *Liwyatan* (לִוְיָתָן, v. 14) is the same lexeme used in Job 41 and Isa 27:1, the cognate of the Ugaritic Lotan. *Tanninim* (תַּנִּינִים, v. 13), translated "sea monsters," is the same plural used in Gen 1:21. The verb *paratzta* (you split, v. 15) of opening springs recalls the wilderness traditions of Exodus 17:6 and Numbers 20:11. The closing inclusio *eretz halo' atta* (אֶרֶץ אַתָּה, v. 17) — "the earth is yours" — is implicit in the verses' frame: vv. 12-17 do not merely cite past acts; they make a claim of universal sovereignty.

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve fragments of Psalm 74 in 4QPs(a) and 4QPs(b). The text is largely consistent with the Masoretic Text. The Septuagint (LXX Ps 73) shows a few interpretive choices — it renders *moadei-El* as *tas heortas tou theou* ("the feasts of God" in some manuscript traditions) or *tas synagōgas tou theou* (the assemblies of God), reflecting the difficulty Greek translators had with the term.

## Lens 2: Literature

Lowth's parallelism patterns the psalm throughout. The opening bicolon — "O God, why do you cast us off forever? / Why does your anger smoke against the sheep of your pasture?" — uses *synonymous parallelism* with intensification: the second line gives the abandoned community a tender metaphor (sheep of God's pasture) that heightens the pathos of the first line's "cast off." This is a classic lament device — the speaker juxtaposes the divine fury with the imagery of God's own care, forcing the reader to feel the contradiction.

The psalm divides into a clear strophic structure. Strophe I (vv. 1-3): the invocation, with the initial "why?" and "remember" appeals. Strophe II (vv. 4-9): the description of the destruction, with the imagery of axes, hatchets, and fire. Strophe III (vv. 10-11): the central accusatory questions ("how long?" and "why do you hold back?"). Strophe IV (vv. 12-17): the central confession of God's primordial victory and ongoing creative sovereignty. Strophe V (vv. 18-23): the closing petitions, with renewed appeals to remember and not forget. The ESV preserves these stanza divisions roughly. There is no *selah* in Psalm 74; like Psalm 73 it presses through without liturgical pause, perhaps reinforcing the urgency of the lament.

The structural pivot at vv. 12-17 is theologically and rhetorically central. The psalm's first eleven verses descend into the destruction; the four-verse hymn of cosmic-creative remembrance at the center is the high point of the structure; the final six verses re-ascend with petitions that depend on the sovereignty just confessed. The chiasm is roughly: Lament → Confession of God's primordial victory → Petition. The center confesses what the petitioner has reason to claim: a God who has broken Leviathan in the past can break the present enemy.

Inclusio and key-word repetition do significant work. The verb *zakhar* (to remember) frames the petitions: v. 2 ("remember your congregation"), v. 18 ("remember this, O LORD"), v. 22 ("remember how the foolish scoff"). Counter-balanced is *shakhakh* (to forget), v. 19 ("do not forget the life of your poor") and v. 23 ("do not forget the clamor of your foes"). The pair *zakhar*/*shakhakh* is the lament's central rhetorical structure: the speaker calls God to *zakhar* the people and *not-shakhakh* the enemy. The verb *charaph* (to scoff, revile) recurs at vv. 10, 18, 22 — the enemy's continual reviling is the offense that demands divine response.

The metaphor cluster of vv. 4-7 is unusually graphic. The enemy's foes "roar" (*sha'agu*, the verb often used of lions and the divine voice) in the meeting place. They wield axes and hatchets and hammers. Imagery of woodcutting in a forest is reapplied to the cutting-down of the temple's carved wood — the irony is exact and devastating. The temple's ornamentation, which had been a sign of glory, becomes the target of destruction.

The cosmic combat hymn at vv. 13-17 uses a series of perfect-tense verbs (*partarta*, you divided; *shibbarta*, you broke; *ritzatzta*, you crushed; *baqa'ta*, you split; *hovashta*, you dried up; *hekhinota*, you established) that build a creedal recital. The pattern is dramatic: the enemy's perfect-tense verbs of destruction in vv. 4-8 (they roared, they set up, they swung, they broke down, they set on fire, they profaned, they burned) are answered by God's perfect-tense verbs of primordial victory. The literary structure embodies the theological argument: the God who has done these things is the God to whom the petition is addressed.

The closing verses use the imagery of the dove (*torekha*, v. 19) — the same word used of the dove in Genesis 8 and the beloved in the Song of Songs. The covenant community is named tenderly even as the petition is desperate.

## Lens 3: Larger Story

Within Wilson's reading of the Psalter, Psalm 74 sits early in Book III, immediately after Psalm 73's theodicy crisis. The placement is theologically deliberate: the wisdom-crisis of Ps 73 (the prosperity of the wicked) is followed by the historical-crisis of Ps 74 (the destruction of the temple). Wilson's *Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* (1985) traces a trajectory across Books I-III in which the Davidic covenant rises (Books I-II), peaks, and collapses, with Ps 89's lament closing Book III as the breaking-point. Psalm 74, with its catastrophic temple-loss, is one of the structural waystations on that trajectory. The Asaphite cluster as a whole carries this freight — Pss 74, 79, and 80 in particular all lament catastrophic loss. The editorial framing suggests that Book III is the Book of Israel's confrontation with covenantal failure on a national scale.

In Jewish tradition, Psalm 74 has been read primarily through the lens of *Tisha b'Av*, the ninth of Av, the fast day commemorating the destruction of both Temples (587 BC and 70 AD). The psalm is not a fixed component of the *kinnot* (lamentation poems) read on Tisha b'Av, but its imagery saturates the day. The *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 74 reads the destruction passages as referring jointly to both temples — the Babylonian and the Roman — with the perpetual-ruins of v. 3 understood as the Second Temple's still-unhealed wound. Rashi reads v. 9's "no longer any prophet" with the rabbinic tradition that prophecy ceased after Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi. Ibn Ezra grapples with the dating question and considers both Babylonian and Maccabean possibilities, ultimately leaning Babylonian. Radak emphasizes the moral structure: the lament is not despair but a covenantal pleading that depends on the people's prior election.

In modern Jewish thought, Psalm 74 has acquired a particular resonance after the Holocaust. Emil Fackenheim, in *To Mend the World* and *God's Presence in History*, treated the psalm as a paradigmatic case of how the biblical lament tradition handles catastrophic rupture: not by silencing the question but by addressing the question to God. Irving Greenberg's "no statement, theological or otherwise, should be made that would not be credible in the presence of burning children" finds an antecedent in Psalm 74's refusal to abstract from the destruction. Jon Levenson, in *Creation and the Persistence of Evil*, makes the psalm central to his argument that biblical theology does not assert the *total* defeat of evil but rather a *partial* and ongoing victory: the cosmic combat hymn at vv. 13-17 is not a creedal statement of accomplished fact but a confession that the God who has done these things must do them again.

In Christian tradition, Psalm 74 has been read primarily as a lament with eschatological resolution. Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 73 (LXX) reads the destroyed sanctuary spiritually — the church on earth always in some sense ruined by sin and persecution, awaiting heavenly restoration. The patristic tradition tended to read the temple-destruction allegorically rather than historically. Cassiodorus and Jerome held similar lines. Aquinas drew on the psalm in discussions of divine providence and the apparent abandonment of the church.

The Reformation tradition read the psalm with greater historical-literal force. Calvin's 1557 commentary on Psalm 74 treats the destruction as referring to the Babylonian event and reads the psalm as a model for the church under persecution: the church may legitimately ask "how long?" and may legitimately demand divine action while clinging to the confession of God's primordial victory. Luther's psalm-lectures emphasize the *anfechtung* dimension: the believer's experience of divine abandonment is not necessarily evidence of divine absence.

Modern Christian scholarship has produced significant treatments. Hermann Gunkel's classification of the psalm as community lament shaped the form-critical tradition. Sigmund Mowinckel placed it within the cult of communal mourning. Claus Westermann, in *Praise and Lament in the Psalms*, used Psalm 74 as a chief example of community lament that holds together accusation and confession, refusing to soften the question with premature resolution. Hans-Joachim Kraus, Frank-Lothar Hossfeld, Erich Zenger, John Goldingay, Marvin Tate, J. Clinton McCann, Beth Tanner, and Gordon Wenham have all contributed to recent commentary. Walter Brueggemann's treatments — particularly in *The Message of the Psalms* and *Theology of the Old Testament* — make Psalm 74 a chief witness for the lament tradition's role within biblical theology. Patrick Miller, in *They Cried to the Lord* and *Interpreting the Psalms*, treats the psalm as an exemplary corporate prayer that locates national catastrophe within covenant.

The cosmic combat tradition has produced its own scholarly literature. Frank Moore Cross's *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* (1973), John Day's *God's Conflict with the Dragon and the Sea* (1985), Jon Levenson's *Creation and the Persistence of Evil* (1988), Mark Smith's *The Early History of God* (1990, 2nd ed. 2002) and *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* (2001), Robert Miller's *The Dragon, the Mountain, and the Nations* (2018) have all treated Psalm 74:13-15 as a key text. The consensus is that Psalm 74 has appropriated the West Semitic Combat Myth — the storm-god's victory over chaos — and rewritten it as YHWH's victory, deploying it as a creedal foundation for present petition.

The New Testament does not directly cite Psalm 74, but the imagery threads through. The "how long?" of v. 10 is echoed in Revelation 6:10 — "how long, O Lord, holy and true, until you judge and avenge our blood?" The cosmic combat tradition reaches its New Testament expression in Revelation 12:9 (Satan as "the great dragon") and Revelation 20:2-3 (the binding of the dragon), drawing on the same Combat Myth substrate.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

Two tensions press on every reader.

The first tension is the **dating question and the multi-shrine question**. The psalm's reference to *moadei-El* (the meeting places of God, v. 8) has fueled a long debate about whether ancient Israelite worship was as centralized as the canonical narrative suggests.

A first position is the **canonical-Deuteronomistic** reading: from the time of Solomon, the Jerusalem temple was the legitimate single sanctuary, and Psalm 74's *moadei-El* is either a poetic plural for the temple complex or, on a Maccabean reading, a reference to local gathering-places (synagogues, assembly halls) that proliferated in the Second Temple period. This is roughly the position of Goldingay and the more conservative-evangelical commentary tradition.

A second position is the **historical-religion** reading: ancient Israelite worship was for centuries a multi-shrine system, with sanctuaries at Bethel, Dan, Shiloh, Mizpah, Gilgal, and elsewhere, and Psalm 74:8 is one of several biblical witnesses to this plurality (cf. 1 Sam 1, 1 Kgs 12, Amos 7-8). Rainer Albertz, Mark Smith, Karel van der Toorn, and the broader history-of-religions tradition incline this way. On this reading, the psalm preserves a memory of cult-multiplicity that the Deuteronomistic editors only partially erased.

A third position is the **Maccabean-revival** reading: the psalm dates to the Seleucid crisis, and *moadei-El* refers to the early synagogues being suppressed by Antiochus's edicts. This position has fewer modern defenders but is not without merit — the synagogue institution is attested by the third century BC, and Antiochus's persecutions did target Jewish worship in local assemblies as well as in the Temple.

Steelmanning the multi-shrine reading: the canonical narrative was edited from the perspective of the Jerusalem cult; texts like Judges 17-18, 1 Samuel 7:16-17, and 1 Kings 12 either grudgingly acknowledge or polemically describe the legitimate practice of worship at multiple locations. Archaeological evidence — the Arad temple, the Lachish shrine, the Tel Dan altar — shows actual worship complexes outside Jerusalem during the monarchic period. The Deuteronomistic centralization is a theological program more than a description of the actual lived religion of pre-exilic Israel. Psalm 74:8, on this reading, is a precious witness to that complexity. The cost of this reading is that it complicates the canonical narrative; the gain is that it allows the lived religion of ancient Israel to come through with greater historical depth.

The second tension is the **covenantal theology of suffering after temple-destruction**. Psalm 74 raises and never fully resolves the question of how to hold together divine sovereignty and historical catastrophe. The cosmic combat hymn at vv. 13-17 is a creedal anchor — but the speaker has already said that the enemy has burned the sanctuary. How does one confess the God who broke Leviathan while standing in the smoking ruins of God's house?

A first position takes the **traditional retributive** reading: the destruction is divine punishment for covenantal infidelity (cf. Deut 28, the curses; Lam 1:8, "Jerusalem sinned grievously, therefore she became filthy"). The psalm's lament is legitimate but should not obscure the underlying covenantal logic — the people are suffering the consequences of their own choices, and God's justice has not failed.

A second position is the **lament-as-protest** reading associated with Brueggemann and the post-Holocaust tradition. On this reading, Psalm 74 makes a different theological move: the speaker does not concede the retributive logic but presses against it, demanding that God act on the basis of prior covenant ("remember your congregation," v. 2) rather than on the basis of present desert. The lament does not silence the retributive theology but stands in dialectical tension with it. The legitimacy of the lament is itself a theological claim — that the worshipper has the right and the duty to address God when divine action has become opaque or absent.

A third position is the **post-Holocaust hermeneutical** reading developed by Fackenheim, Greenberg, and Levenson. This position pushes the lament-as-protest reading further: the catastrophic destruction of God's people requires a theological response that does not conscript the suffering into a justification system. Fackenheim's "614th commandment" — to remain Jewish, to refuse to grant Hitler a posthumous victory — is one form. Greenberg's principle of speech in the presence of burning children is another. Levenson's argument that biblical creation-theology is partial victory rather than total triumph is a third. Each reading takes Psalm 74 as a witness to a covenantal theology that does not collapse the question into an answer.

The closing question — implicit in the *zakhar*/*shakhakh* structure — is what the worshipper is to do when the petition does not bring obvious response. The psalm ends without resolution: God has not yet remembered, has not yet acted, has not yet defended his cause. The petitioner closes with "do not forget the clamor of your foes," and there it stops. There is no answering theophany, no liturgical reassurance.

Where this leaves us: Psalm 74 is the Psalter's most fully developed corporate lament for catastrophic historical loss. It refuses to silence the question, refuses to substitute pious resignation for faithful protest, and refuses to let the cosmic combat tradition slip into mere mythology — the God who broke Leviathan must break the present enemy too, or the confession is empty. The psalm models a theology that holds together creedal confession and unanswered petition, ancient victory and present ruins, the dove of the covenant and the wild beasts that threaten it.

The closing question for any reader: in your own moments of catastrophic loss — communal, personal, or both — what creedal confession are you willing to make in the smoking ruins, and what petition are you unwilling to abandon even when the answer does not come?

## The Thread

Standing in the temple's ashes, the lament confesses the God who has shattered Leviathan and demands he do it again, refusing both pious silence and despair.
