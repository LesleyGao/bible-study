# Ezekiel 11: The Sanctuary in Small Measure and the Heart of Flesh

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Political situation**: **Exilic period, dated to 592 BC** within the chapters-8–11 vision-cycle. Chapter 11 closes the cycle: the *kavod* completes its departure from Jerusalem to the mountain east of the city (11:23), and the prophet is returned in vision to Tel-abib by the Chebar canal to report what he has seen to the elders of the exilic community (11:24–25). The political content of the chapter centers on **the 25 leaders at the east gate of the temple** (11:1) — a council of *princes of the people* (*sarei ha-am*, שָׂרֵי הָעָם), among them **Jaazaniah son of Azzur and Pelatiah son of Benaiah**. These are not the same 25 men of 8:16 (those were sun-worshippers in the inner court); these are political-strategic counselors plotting either the city's defensive policy against Babylon or its religious self-understanding, or both. Their slogan — *the time is not near to build houses; this city is the cauldron, and we are the meat* (11:3) — is the chapter's interpretive key. The proverb has been variously read: (a) a confident-defensive reading: *we are the prized meat protected by the iron pot of Jerusalem's walls* (Greenberg, Block); (b) a nihilistic reading: *we are doomed flesh in the cooking-pot of judgment* (Zimmerli); (c) a political-realist reading rejecting the prophets: *don't bother building houses for an exilic future Jeremiah promises (Jer 29:5); the city itself is our protection*. Greenberg's reading has the strongest contextual fit: the leaders are denying the relevance of Jeremiah's *build houses in Babylon* counsel because they believe Jerusalem itself is the iron protective vessel.

**Material culture**: The east gate of the temple complex (11:1) and the broader Solomonic architecture remain the visionary locus. The *cauldron* (*sir*, סִיר) of 11:3, 7, 11 is the iron or bronze cooking pot of late-monarchic domestic and cult use — the same vessel Jeremiah sees boiling from the north in Jer 1:13, the same vessel deployed as image of judgment in Mic 3:3, the same cooking-vessel attested in Iron Age IIC archaeological strata at Lachish, Tel Megiddo, and the Jerusalem Ophel. The phrase *miqdash me'at* (מִקְדָּשׁ מְעַט, "a sanctuary in small measure," 11:16) is unique in the Hebrew Bible — *miqdash* is the standard temple-vocabulary term, *me'at* the diminishing modifier. The expression is one of the chapter's most theologically loaded coinages: the divine accommodation to exile-conditions takes the form of a *small-scale* sanctuary that travels with the people. The *new heart and new spirit* language of 11:19 (*lev ehad*, *ru'ah hadashah*, *lev basar* in place of *lev even*) employs Ezekiel's signature anatomical-theological vocabulary; the imagery has parallels in Mesopotamian medical-magical literature (the *libbu*-heart as the seat of will and intention) and in Egyptian wisdom (the heart as moral organ), but Ezekiel's deployment is distinctively Yahwistic-covenantal: the divine surgery replaces the stone-heart of covenant-rebellion with a flesh-heart of covenant-fidelity.

**Authorship & dating**: Ezekiel ben Buzi, exiled in 597 BC. **Greenberg (Anchor)** treats chapter 11 as the authentic conclusion of the chapters-8–11 vision-cycle, with possible secondary expansion in 11:14–21 (the *sanctuary in small measure* / *new heart* oracle). **Zimmerli (Hermeneia)** identifies 11:14–21 as a redactional insertion, attached to the vision-cycle by editorial framing; he reads it as *Ezekielian theology in Ezekielian style* but possibly a separate oracle gathered into this location. **Block (NICOT)** defends original Ezekielian provenance for the entire chapter. **Cooke (ICC 1936)** is more skeptical of unity. **Pelatiah's death** in 11:13 is a striking literary-historical datum: in the visionary frame, a named political figure dies while Ezekiel is prophesying; the prophet's word is *speech-act effective*. Greenberg defends the historicity of the death; Zimmerli regards it as visionary-dramatic without historical referent; Block stakes out a middle position.

**Archaeological & textual evidence**: **The Murashu archive** and **the Al-Yahudu tablets** (Pearce & Wunsch 2014) document the post-exilic Judean community in Babylonia in concrete administrative detail — the very community 11:16's *I will be a sanctuary in small measure to them in the countries* describes prospectively. **The Lachish letters** (early 6th c. BC) attest the late-monarchic political atmosphere — the false prophets Ezekiel denounces, the strategic confusion of Zedekiah's court. **The 25 men** of 11:1 are otherwise unknown to the historical record; **Jaazaniah son of Azzur** is to be distinguished from the Jaazaniah of Ezek 8:11 and from the Jaazaniah son of Shaphan named on the *Yzn yhqlb 'ašer 'al hbyt* seal-impression from Tell en-Nasbeh, though all are roughly contemporary. **4QEzek-a** preserves chapter 11 in fragmentary form; the LXX shows minor variants but the *miqdash me'at* and *new heart* texts are stable across textual witnesses.

**Key terms**: *miqdash me'at* (מִקְדָּשׁ מְעַט, "a sanctuary in small measure," 11:16 — unique to this verse, foundational for synagogue theology); *lev ehad* / *ru'ah hadashah* (לֵב אֶחָד / רוּחַ חֲדָשָׁה, "one heart / a new spirit," 11:19); *lev even* / *lev basar* (לֵב אֶבֶן / לֵב בָּשָׂר, "heart of stone / heart of flesh," 11:19); *kavod YHWH* (the chapter's final stage of departure, 11:22–23); *sir* (סִיר, "cauldron," 11:3, 7, 11); *peletah* (פְּלֵטָה, "remnant," 11:13 — Ezekiel's intercessory cry); *sarei ha-am* (שָׂרֵי הָעָם, "princes of the people," 11:1).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: **prophetic vision-report with embedded oracles, sign-event, and intercession**, the closing movement of the chapters-8–11 cycle. Chapter 11's literary architecture is bipartite: (a) **the oracle against the 25 leaders** (11:1–13), with Pelatiah's death and Ezekiel's intercession; and (b) **the oracle of restoration to the exiles** (11:14–21), with the *sanctuary in small measure* and *new heart* promises. The chapter closes with the *kavod*'s final departure to the mountain east of the city (11:22–23) and the prophet's return to Tel-abib (11:24–25).

The literary architecture is:

- A. The 25 leaders at the east gate (11:1)
- B. The cauldron-meat slogan; oracle of judgment (11:2–12)
- C. Pelatiah dies; prophet's intercession (11:13)
- D. The exiles' status: *I am a sanctuary in small measure* (11:14–17)
- E. The promised return and the new heart (11:18–21)
- A'. The *kavod* leaves the city for the mountain east (11:22–23)
- C'. The prophet returns to the exiles and tells them all (11:24–25)

The **leitwort** technique is concentrated. *Cauldron* and *meat* (5×) frame the judgment oracle; *sanctuary* (*miqdash*, 2×, with the *me'at* qualifier introducing the new theological category); *heart* (3×, with the stone-flesh contrast); *spirit* (*ru'ah*, 4×, both as the lifting-Spirit who transports the prophet and the new-spirit-given to the people); *kavod* (3×, completing the chapter-cycle's departure-arc); *abominations* (*to'evot* / *toevotehem*, the standard Ezekielian indictment-vocabulary).

Pelatiah's death in 11:13 is one of Ezekiel's most striking literary-theological moments. *Pelatiah* (פְּלַטְיָה) means *YHWH delivers* — the very name embodies the deliverance the leaders had counted on. The prophet's response is immediate: he falls on his face and cries out, *Will you make a full end of the remnant of Israel?* — the same intercessory pattern as 9:8, the same Mosaic-Abrahamic posture, again *not granted* in its surface form. But the answer here is *more theologically loaded* than in chapter 9: the apparent denial (*the guilt is great*) is followed by the *miqdash me'at* / *new heart* oracle, which is in some sense the *granted* form of the intercession — the remnant is preserved, but in the new mode.

The **oracle-formula** pattern is carefully structured: *Thus says the Lord GOD* introduces 11:5, 7, 16, 17 — four oracle-units, each marked. The closing *they shall be my people, and I will be their God* (11:20) is the **covenant-formula** that runs through Genesis 17, Exodus 6, Leviticus 26, Jeremiah 7, 30, 31, and reaches its New Testament reception at 2 Cor 6:16, Rev 21:3. Ezekiel deploys it here in the context of *new heart* — the formula is being re-grounded in the divine-surgical replacement of the heart of stone.

**MT/LXX variants**: 11:16 (*miqdash me'at*) is stable in MT, LXX (ἁγίασμα μικρόν), and 4QEzek-a; the textual unanimity matters because the verse is the foundation of post-Second-Temple Jewish synagogue theology. 11:19 has minor variants — the LXX reads *another spirit* (πνεῦμα ἕτερον) where the MT reads *new spirit* (*ruaḥ ḥadashah*); the Greek reading is occasionally preferred by critical commentators on the grounds of parallelism with *one heart*, but the MT is the broader textual witness. 11:23 (the *kavod* on the mountain east of the city) is stable.

The chapter's *miqdash me'at* and *new heart* oracles are **transitional in the prophetic corpus** — they bridge Jeremiah 31's *new covenant* and Ezekiel 36's developed *new heart* oracle. Greenberg, Levenson, and Block all read 11:19 and 36:26 as *the same oracle in two contexts*, with the chapter-11 instantiation being the inaugural and the chapter-36 instantiation the developed form.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Place in the canonical arc**: Ezekiel 11 closes the *kavod*-departure arc that began in 9:3. The departure is now complete: from cherub to threshold (9:3) to over the cherubim (10:18) to the east gate (10:19) to the mountain east of the city (11:23). Within the book's overall arc, chapter 11 also opens the *restoration* trajectory that runs through chapters 33–37 and culminates in chapter 43 (the *kavod*'s return). The chapter is thus structurally janus-faced: it closes the long departure and inaugurates the long return.

**Jewish trajectory**: **The Bavli Megillah 29a** preserves the foundational rabbinic reading of 11:16: *I will be a sanctuary in small measure to them in the countries where they have gone* is read as the synagogue. *R. Yitzhak said: this refers to the synagogues and the houses of study in Babylonia* (*ellu battei knesiyot u-vattei midrashot she-be-Bavel*). The reading is decisive for post-Temple Judaism. After 70 AD, when Judaism has to live without the Jerusalem temple, **Ezekiel 11:16 is the foundational biblical warrant** for the synagogue as a fully Yahwistic locus of worship. The synagogue is not a *replacement* for the temple but a *miqdash me'at* — a temple in small measure, where the divine presence is genuinely accessible. The reading shapes the entire architecture and theology of the synagogue: the *aron ha-kodesh* (the Torah ark, miniaturizing the temple's ark), the *bimah* (miniaturizing the altar), the eastern orientation toward Jerusalem, the *kedushah* prayer that is timed to the angelic Sanctus. **Targum Jonathan** glosses *miqdash me'at* as *batei knishta* (synagogues). **Rashi** follows: the synagogues and study houses are *miqdash me'at*. **Radak** elaborates on the *new heart* of 11:19 with explicit covenantal-theological weight: the divine surgical replacement of the heart of stone with the heart of flesh is the foundation of the *teshuvah* (repentance) doctrine. **Maimonides** in *Mishneh Torah, Hilkhot Tefillah* 4:5 builds the legal-theological structure of synagogue worship on Ezek 11:16, treating the synagogue as a real *miqdash* with associated *kedushah* requirements (ritual purity, behavioral reverence, architectural orientation). **Levenson** in *Theology of the Program of Restoration* treats Ezekiel 11 as the beginning of the *spatial-mobility* of the divine presence that the rest of the book elaborates. **Sommer** in *The Bodies of God* reads the *miqdash me'at* as a real-divine locality, not a metaphor.

**Christian trajectory**: Patristic — **Origen's *Homilies on Ezekiel*** reads 11:16 typologically: the *miqdash me'at* is the church-in-the-world, the assembly that bears the divine presence in the time between the temple's destruction and the eschatological New Jerusalem. **Jerome's Commentary on Ezekiel** (414) takes the same line, with the Christian *ecclesia* as the new *miqdash*. **Gregory the Great's *Homilies on Ezekiel*** elaborates the ecclesiology: the church is the *miqdash me'at* of the new economy, its altar the new altar, its sacraments the new locus of presence. **Augustine's *City of God* 17.7** cites the *new heart* oracle of 11:19 as Christological — the *circumcision of the heart* (Deut 30:6, Jer 4:4, Rom 2:29) finds its fulfillment in the Spirit-given new heart of the new covenant. Medieval — **Hugh of St. Victor**, **Aquinas (*ST* I-II.106 on the new law)** — the *new heart* oracle is foundational for the doctrine of grace as *infused virtue*. Reformation — **Calvin's *Lectures on Ezekiel* (1565, two volumes, incomplete; coverage extends through chapter 20)** is decisive on 11:19: the *heart of flesh* is the work of regeneration by the Holy Spirit, the foundation of the Reformed *monergism* of grace. **Luther's *Lectures on Galatians*** draws on the same oracle for the doctrine of justification. The Calvinist *Heidelberg Catechism* Q&A 8 (*Are we so corrupt that we are utterly incapable of doing any good?*) cites Ezek 11:19 directly as biblical warrant for the doctrine of total depravity and gracious regeneration. Critical — **Cooke (ICC)** treats *miqdash me'at* historically: the synagogue is an exilic-period adaptation, and Ezekiel 11:16 is its biblical authorization. **Zimmerli (Hermeneia)** elaborates the *new heart* oracle as an authentic Ezekielian theology of divine-initiative covenant-renewal, paralleling Jer 31:31–34. **Eichrodt (OTL)** on the *Heilsgeschichte* trajectory. Contemporary — **Greenberg (Anchor)**, **Block (NICOT)**, **Odell**, **Lapsley (*Can These Bones Live?*)** on Ezekiel's theology of moral agency, **Wright** on the new-heart and new-spirit theology in canonical perspective.

**Where they diverge**: the **Jewish synagogue tradition** reads 11:16 as the foundation of the synagogue — a real *miqdash* in small measure, the divine presence genuinely accessible in the place of gathered Torah-study and prayer. The **Christian ecclesiology tradition** reads 11:16 as the foundation of the church-as-temple (1 Cor 3:16; 6:19; 2 Cor 6:16; 1 Pet 2:5; Eph 2:21–22) — the gathered congregation as the locus of the Spirit's indwelling. Both readings agree that the divine presence is no longer architecturally bound to a single Jerusalem temple; both read 11:16 as the canonical warrant. They diverge on what *replaces* the temple's locus: synagogue (with the Jerusalem temple still anticipated as eschatological consummation) or church (with the eschatological New Jerusalem in which *no temple* is needed because *the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple*, Rev 21:22).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The synagogue / church as *miqdash me'at* — does Ezekiel 11:16 legitimize *non-temple* worship as fully Yahwistic, and what does that imply for the theology of *the holy place*?** The chapter's claim is striking: in the countries of exile, *I have been a sanctuary in small measure to them*. The Hebrew is unambiguous. The divine self-localization is real, even if scaled.

The **Jewish synagogue tradition** (Bavli Megillah 29a; Maimonides *Hilkhot Tefillah* 4:5) takes the *miqdash me'at* claim seriously and at face value: the synagogue is a real divine locus, not metaphor; the *aron ha-kodesh*, the eastern orientation, the *kedushah*, the architectural specifications, the ritual requirements — all are derived from the conviction that the synagogue genuinely participates in the divine presence the temple housed. The **Catholic parish-church and altar tradition** runs parallel: the parish church is a *real* locus of divine presence, supremely so in the Eucharistic *real presence* of Christ on the altar; the altar's consecration, the tabernacle's sanctity, the church's architectural orientation toward the East — all derive from the conviction that the church genuinely is *the house of God* (CCC 1180–1183). The **Reformed gathered-congregation tradition** locates the *miqdash me'at* differently — not in the building but in the gathering: where two or three are gathered in Christ's name (Matt 18:20), where the Word is preached and the sacraments rightly administered, there is the *miqdash*. The **Anabaptist house-church tradition** radicalizes the Reformed move: the gathered believers anywhere — kitchen, barn, prison cell — are the *miqdash me'at*, and architectural buildings are theologically indifferent. **Zwingli's symbolic-presence** view runs in this direction; the early Anabaptists radicalize it. The **Pentecostal indwelling** tradition reads the New Testament *temple-of-the-Holy-Spirit* (1 Cor 6:19) as the further interiorization: the *miqdash me'at* is now the believer's body.

The least intuitive position to a sacramental Catholic or Orthodox reader is the **radical Anabaptist / Pentecostal interiorization**: that the holy place is *not* architecturally locatable. The steelman: the chapter explicitly says *in the countries where they have gone* — the locus is *with the exiles*, wherever they are, not bound to a building they could not in fact build. The radicalization is exegetically defensible: if the *miqdash* is *me'at*, scaled to the exile-condition, then the limit-case of the scaling is the gathered believers themselves, with no architecture intervening. The least intuitive position to a non-sacramental Protestant reader is the **Catholic/Orthodox altar-tradition**: that the consecrated building, the consecrated altar, the reserved Eucharist genuinely participate in the temple's divine presence. The steelman: the chapter does not say *the gathered people are themselves the sanctuary* — it says *I have been a sanctuary*, with YHWH himself as subject; some real-divine localization is being asserted, and the sacramental reading takes that localization seriously rather than dissolving it.

Where this leaves us: 11:16 inaugurates a theology of *miqdash me'at* that the entire post-Second-Temple religious history has had to interpret. The unresolved question: between the Jerusalem temple (Solomon's, Zerubbabel's, Herod's) and the eschatological New Jerusalem (Rev 21:22 — *no temple*), what is the ontological status of the *miqdash me'at*? Synagogue, church, gathering, body — and is the difference between these one of degree or of kind? The chapter does not resolve. It sets the field.

**Tension 2: Pelatiah's death — the prophetic word as deadly speech-act, and what does it mean that *prophecy can kill*?** Pelatiah son of Benaiah dies *while Ezekiel is prophesying* (11:13). The prophet's word is speech-act effective; the named political figure dies on the spot. The OT phenomenon is broader than this verse — Elijah's fire-from-heaven on the captains of fifty (2 Kgs 1), Elisha's bears (2 Kgs 2:23–25), Hananiah's death after Jeremiah's word (Jer 28:15–17) — but Ezek 11:13 is the most compressed and theologically pointed instance.

The **Catholic / Orthodox sacramental word-theology** treats the divine *davar* (דָּבָר) and the divine *logos* as ontologically effective: when God speaks, reality is rearranged. The Eucharistic *verba* of consecration are the supreme instance: *this is my body* effects what it announces. The Reformation Catholic-Protestant divide on this point is famous. **Reformed Word-and-Spirit** tradition follows the same general pattern but more cautiously: the *Word preached* is genuinely effective, but the effectiveness is the Spirit's, not the word's autonomous power. **Karl Barth's *Church Dogmatics* I/1 §4** (the threefold Word of God) systematizes this. **Modern speech-act theory** (J. L. Austin's *How to Do Things with Words*; John Searle; Wittgenstein on language-games) provides a contemporary frame: the prophetic *davar* is a *performative* utterance, not merely a constative description. **Robert Alter** on biblical *davar* is essential here: the Hebrew word *davar* covers both *word* and *event* / *thing*, which is itself a theological datum. **Bonhoeffer's *Ethics*** on the relationship between word and deed in the Christian life draws on the same conceptual neighborhood.

Where this leaves us: Ezek 11:13 stands as one of the OT's strongest assertions of speech-act efficacy in prophecy, and its NT successor at Acts 5 (Ananias and Sapphira) makes the same point. The unresolved question: if the prophetic word can kill, what is the relation between *the speaker's intention*, *the divine sanction*, and *the effect in reality*? Ezekiel did not intend Pelatiah's death; he is *startled* by it. The text presupposes a divine effectiveness running *through* the prophetic word that exceeds the prophet's own willing. This is the territory where Christian doctrines of inspiration, sacrament, and absolution have always lived, and the chapter's puzzling brevity at 11:13 is part of why it has remained generative.

## The Thread

The *miqdash me'at* of 11:16 reappears explicitly in **1 Cor 3:16** (*do you not know that you are God's temple?*), **1 Pet 2:5** (*you yourselves like living stones are being built up as a spiritual house*), **Eph 2:21–22** (*in whom the whole structure, being joined together, grows into a holy temple in the Lord*), and reaches its eschatological consummation in **Rev 21:22** (*I saw no temple in the city, for the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple*). The *new heart* oracle of 11:19 is the inaugural form of the developed oracle in **Ezek 36:26**, which Paul cites in **2 Cor 3:3** (*written not on tablets of stone but on tablets of human hearts*) and **Heb 8:10–12; 10:16** integrates with Jer 31:31–34 as the foundational text of the new covenant. Pelatiah's death (11:13) finds its NT parallel in **Acts 5:1–11** (Ananias and Sapphira) — the prophetic-apostolic word as speech-act effective, the early church's recognition that the divine *davar* through the apostle does what it says.
