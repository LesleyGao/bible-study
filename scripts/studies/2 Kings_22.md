# 2 Kings 22: The Book Found, the Prophetess Consulted

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Josiah ben Amon ruled Judah from 640 to 609 BCE — coming to the throne at age eight after the assassination of his father Amon by palace officials and the counter-coup by *ʿam hāʾāreṣ* ("the people of the land") who killed the conspirators and installed the boy king (cf. 21:23-24). The political world he inherited was changing fast. The Neo-Assyrian Empire, dominant under Ashurbanipal until his death around 631, entered terminal decline within a decade: Babylon under Nabopolassar revolted in 626, the Medes under Cyaxares attacked the Assyrian heartland through the 620s, Asshur fell in 614, and Nineveh — Sennacherib's great capital — was destroyed in 612 BCE. The fall of Nineveh is celebrated in the book of Nahum and probably motivates the timing of Josiah's reform: Assyrian power was visibly retreating from the Levant, and the Assyrian-imposed astral cults (the host-of-heaven worship Manasseh had installed) were no longer politically necessary. The eighteenth year of Josiah — 622 BCE, the year of the temple repairs and the discovery of the book — falls precisely in the window between Ashurbanipal's death and Nineveh's fall, when Judah had room to assert religious autonomy.

The chapter focuses narrowly on a single bureaucratic event: routine temple maintenance produced an unexpected find. **Hilkiah the high priest** brings to Shaphan the secretary a *sefer hattôrâ*, "the book of the law," found in the house of YHWH. Shaphan reads it, brings it to the king, reads it again. Josiah tears his clothes and dispatches a delegation to inquire of YHWH. The delegation goes not to Jeremiah (whose call dates to Josiah's thirteenth year, 627 BCE — five years before the book's discovery; cf. Jer 1:2) but to **Huldah the prophetess** (*hannəbîʾâ*) in the *mishneh* district of Jerusalem.

The Mishneh — sometimes translated "Second Quarter" — is a real, archaeologically attested expansion of Jerusalem onto the western hill, built up during the eighth century after Sennacherib's 701 invasion drove refugees from the north and the Shephelah into the capital. Nahman Avigad's excavations in the Jewish Quarter exposed the **Broad Wall**, a roughly seven-meter-thick fortification of Hezekiah's expansion that defines the Mishneh's perimeter, and the **Israelite Tower** — both visible today. Huldah's residence in this newer western district places her in the seventh-century city's expansion zone, possibly near the institutional center but distinct from the Temple precinct.

The political bureaucracy named in the chapter — Shaphan the *sōfēr* (secretary), Hilkiah the high priest, Ahikam ben Shaphan, Achbor ben Micaiah, Asaiah *ʿebed hammelek* — is documented in **bullae** (clay sealings of papyrus documents) recovered from late-monarchic Jerusalem. Yigal Shiloh's City of David excavations in the 1980s recovered the **Bullae House** with several dozen seal impressions including one reading *lgmryhw bn špn* ("belonging to Gemaryahu son of Shaphan") — the same Gemariah who appears in Jeremiah 36:10 as Shaphan's son. **Eilat Mazar's** subsequent excavations on the Ophel exposed bullae of *yhwkl bn šlmyhw* (Jehucal son of Shelemiah, cf. Jer 37:3) and *gdlyhw bn pšḥwr* (Gedaliah son of Pashhur, cf. Jer 38:1), confirming the seventh-century scribal-administrative class around the Temple was a small named circle. The Shaphan family in particular emerges from these chapters as a politically influential reform-aligned scribal lineage; it is not accidental that Ahikam ben Shaphan later protects Jeremiah (Jer 26:24), or that Gedaliah ben Ahikam becomes the Babylonian-appointed governor in 587 (2 Kgs 25:22).

Three Hebrew terms organize the chapter. *Sēfer hattôrâ* (סֵפֶר הַתּוֹרָה), "the book of the law," appears in 22:8, 11 — its scope and identity is the chapter's largest interpretive question. *Wayyiqraʿ ʾet-bəgādāyw* (וַיִּקְרַע אֶת־בְּגָדָיו), "he tore his clothes" (22:11), is the formal mourning gesture: the king's garment-tearing is a cultic and political act announcing public repentance. *Dirʾšû ʾet-YHWH* (דִּרְשׁוּ אֶת־יְהוָה), "inquire of YHWH" (22:13, 18), is the technical formula for prophetic consultation, with parallels at 1 Kgs 22:5 (Jehoshaphat) and 2 Kgs 3:11 (Jehoshaphat again with Elisha) — Josiah is doing what kings do, but choosing a woman to consult.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: regnal opening (22:1-2) plus extended narrative episode of book-finding and prophetic consultation. The structure is precise. Verses 1-2: the standard regnal evaluation, with Josiah uniquely commended that "he did not turn aside to the right or to the left" — a citation of Deut 5:32 / 17:20. Verses 3-7: the bureaucratic frame of Temple repair. Verses 8-10: the discovery, with the doubled reading (Hilkiah to Shaphan, Shaphan to the king). Verses 11-13: the king's response, the dispatch of the delegation. Verses 14-20: Huldah's oracle, with two distinct movements (judgment on the place, mercy on the king).

The chapter's most striking literary feature is the way the book itself becomes a character. Walsh and Fokkelman in their narrative-critical readings have noted that the book is passed three times — Hilkiah hands it to Shaphan, Shaphan reads it himself, Shaphan reads it before the king — and that the verbal force of the text accumulates with each transmission. The king's tearing of his clothes is the climax of the book's own performative power: the words written generations earlier reach across time and break the king. Robert Alter in *The Hebrew Bible: A Translation with Commentary* observes that the chapter dramatizes the dtr's own theological project — a buried text restored to public reading is the metaphor for the dtr's own enterprise of recovering the Mosaic word for late-monarchic Judah.

Place in the book argument: chapter 22 sets up chapter 23. The discovery and consultation establish the legal-prophetic warrant for the reform that follows. **Frank Moore Cross** argued that 2 Kings 22-23 is the climactic moment of Dtr1, the pre-exilic edition: the original Deuteronomistic History was a Josianic propaganda document arguing that Josiah was the new David, the Davidic covenant was secure, and the dynasty would endure. The exilic editor (Dtr2) had to revise this confident narrative after 587 — and the revision is visible at 22:16-17 (the unrelenting judgment language) and 22:20 ("you shall be gathered to your grave in peace... your eyes shall not see all the disaster") which sits in tension with what actually happens to Josiah at Megiddo in 609. The fact that the oracle is preserved in this form despite the historical contradiction is itself evidence of pre-exilic composition: a Dtr2 editor writing after the fact would presumably not have left the awkward "in peace" language standing.

**Marvin Sweeney's** *King Josiah of Judah* (2001) is foundational on the chapter. Sweeney argues that Huldah's oracle is the dtr's central theological move: it preserves both the inevitability of the judgment (against the place) and the legitimacy of Josiah (against personal punishment). The contradiction at 22:20 — Josiah dies in battle at Megiddo, hardly "in peace" — has long puzzled commentators, and Sweeney's solution is that "peace" (*šālôm*) here means before the destruction rather than from natural causes; Josiah is gathered to his ancestors before the catastrophe, which technically fulfills the oracle.

A textual variant: 2 Chronicles 34 parallels and reorganizes this material, placing the reform's start in Josiah's *eighth* year (Chr 34:3) — when Josiah was sixteen — and the book's discovery in his eighteenth year (matching Kings). The Chronicler's reorganization places the reform before the discovery; the Kings author makes the discovery the catalyst for the reform. **William Schniedewind** in *How the Bible Became a Book* and **Bernard Levinson** in *Deuteronomy and the Hermeneutics of Legal Innovation* have argued the Kings sequence is the older and more historically credible version. 4QKgs preserves fragments of this chapter; the textual base is stable.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Jewish interpretive trajectory has two main concerns: the identity of the book, and the choice of Huldah over Jeremiah. **Josephus** in *Antiquities* 10.4.2 paraphrases the chapter and identifies the book as "the holy books of Moses" without further specification, echoing the rabbinic assumption that *the* Torah — the entire Pentateuch — was found. Rabbinic tradition takes this for granted: **Sifre Deuteronomy** and the **Tosefta** treat the book as the complete Torah lost during Manasseh's reign. **Rashi** on 22:8 follows this view, arguing that Manasseh hid or burned the Torah scrolls and Hilkiah found a surviving copy. **Radak** (David Kimhi) elaborates: the book was specifically the Torah scroll Moses placed beside the ark per Deuteronomy 31:26, recovered when the ark was moved during Temple repair.

**b. Megillah 14b** preserves the most-discussed rabbinic reflection on the chapter — why Huldah and not Jeremiah? The Talmud notes that Jeremiah was alive and prophesying. Three explanations: (1) Jeremiah was traveling on a mission to recall the ten lost tribes; (2) women are more compassionate, and the king needed compassion; (3) Huldah was a relative of Jeremiah (a tradition placing both as descendants of Rahab) and family had access. The discussion reflects rabbinic discomfort with the choice. Megillah 14b also lists Huldah among **seven prophetesses** of Israel (Sarah, Miriam, Deborah, Hannah, Abigail, Huldah, Esther) and notes that her name (related to *ḥōled*, "weasel") is criticized as inappropriately humble for a prophet — a midrashic reading of her presumption in transmitting an oracle of judgment.

**Abarbanel** in his commentary on Kings argues at length that Huldah's oracle is the dtr's deliberate signal that prophetic authority is not gendered — and that the king's delegation to a woman, in the late-monarchic period when the institution of prophecy was strongly male, was itself a sign of Josiah's reform-mindedness. **Ralbag** notes the structural symmetry: the book authored by Moses (a man) is interpreted by Huldah (a woman) — the text is open to both genders of reception. **Yair Zakovitch** in his Hebrew commentary engages the geography of the *mishneh* district as a deliberate signal of Huldah's outsider-status: she is a prophet of the city's expansion, not its religious-establishment core.

The Christian interpretive tradition focuses on Josiah's penitence. **Origen** in his Commentary on Romans cites Josiah's tearing of clothes (22:11) as a model of true repentance — the inner movement made visible. **Augustine** in *City of God* 18 mentions Josiah briefly as the last righteous Davidic king before the catastrophe; Augustine's larger argument requires Josiah as proof that even thorough reform cannot save a city whose collective sin has accumulated. **Theodoret of Cyrus** in his *Quaestiones in Reges* engages the Huldah question directly, arguing that her authority is straightforward — she is a prophet, prophets speak for God, gender is theologically irrelevant. **Chrysostom** in his Homilies on the Statues uses Josiah's response (tearing clothes, weeping) as a paradigm for Antiochene believers responding to imperial threat. **Gregory the Great** in the *Pastoral Rule* uses Josiah as the type of the ruler who responds rightly to prophetic correction.

**Aquinas** in the *Summa* (IIa-IIae q. 174 a. 4 ad 3) uses Huldah as evidence that prophecy is given without regard to gender, citing her with Deborah and Anna as women who exercised genuine prophetic authority. **Calvin's sermons on 2 Kings 22** (extant in the *Supplementa Calviniana*) read the chapter as a parable of how God's word, though long buried, retains its power to break the heart that hears it; Calvin uses Josiah's response as a model for Reformed reading practice — the book is read aloud, the heart breaks, the reform follows. **Luther** notes Josiah's response in his lectures on Deuteronomy as evidence that scripture's authority is self-attesting.

The single most important development in the modern interpretive history of this chapter is **W. M. L. de Wette's 1805 dissertation** at Jena (*Dissertatio critico-exegetica qua Deuteronomium a prioribus Pentateuchi libris diversum, alius cuiusdam recentioris auctoris opus esse monstratur*). De Wette identified the book of the law found in 622 BCE with **proto-Deuteronomy** — specifically Deuteronomy 12-26 plus the blessings and curses of chapter 28. His argument turned on the alignment of Josiah's reforms with Deuteronomy's distinctive prescriptions: centralization of worship at one place (Deut 12:5-14), the Passover at the central sanctuary (Deut 16:5-7) which 2 Kgs 23:21-23 records as the first such Passover since the judges, the prohibition of high places, the ban on Asherah and pillars. The de Wette identification became the anchor for the entire **Documentary Hypothesis**: if Deuteronomy was composed in the seventh century, then the JEDP source theory has its most secure dating. **Wellhausen** built on de Wette in his *Prolegomena*; **Eckart Otto** and **Bernard Levinson** are the contemporary scholars who have most extensively defended and refined the proto-Deuteronomy identification. **Otto's** multivolume *Deuteronomium* commentary in the HThKAT series is the standard. **Levinson's** *Deuteronomy and the Hermeneutics of Legal Innovation* argues the Deuteronomic legal code engages in deliberate revisionary engagement with earlier Pentateuchal law, which fits a seventh-century composition addressing the cultic situation Josiah confronted.

Critical scholarship today largely accepts some version of the de Wette thesis. **Cogan and Tadmor** in the AB commentary endorse it; **Marvin Sweeney** treats it as established; **Cross** built his Dtr1/Dtr2 model on it; **Nelson** assumes it. **Provan, Long, and Longman** in their *Biblical History of Israel* are more cautious, noting that the question of how much of Deuteronomy was found and how much was composed *for* the reform versus discovered as already-existing remains genuinely open.

**Susan Ackerman** in *Warrior, Dancer, Beloved, Queen* engages Huldah's role at length, arguing that her selection over Jeremiah reflects her established institutional standing — possibly as a Temple-affiliated prophet, possibly as a recognized authority in the *mishneh* district — rather than improvised consultation. **Phyllis Trible** has noted that Huldah's oracle becomes the first authoritative interpretation of written scripture in the canonical record, making her the founding figure of biblical hermeneutics. **Susan Niditch** observes the chapter's structural irony: the male prophet (Jeremiah) is silent, the female prophet (Huldah) authoritative, the male scribes (Shaphan and his sons) the vehicle of transmission — the late-monarchic prophetic-scribal network is more diverse than the dominant Jeremiah-centered narrative suggests.

**Iain Provan** in his 1 & 2 Kings commentary engages the theological structure. **Walter Brueggemann** in his Smyth & Helwys *1 & 2 Kings* reads chapter 22 as the dtr's portrayal of how a community responds to the recovery of its own foundational text — a model for the exilic community itself, who would be doing the same work with the dtr's history.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**First tension: the book's authority over the king.** Josiah, the absolute monarch of Judah, encounters a written text and tears his clothes in submission to it. Three positions on what this means. Position one (covenant-theological, classical Reformed): the book represents the covenantal word of YHWH, which has authority over kings — Deut 17:18-20 explicitly subordinates the Israelite monarch to "this Torah." Steelman: this is the chapter's plain rhetoric, and the legal architecture of Deuteronomy assumes royal subordination to written law. Position two (political-historical, Bernard Levinson, Eckart Otto): the book was *deliberately produced* to authorize Josiah's reform; the king is performing submission to a text whose composition his administration sponsored. The Shaphan family is the reform party; the discovery is staged or at minimum opportune. Steelman: this explains the text's uncanny alignment with the reform that follows and accounts for the political coherence of the seventh-century scribal-reform circle. Position three (epistemological, contemporary): the chapter dramatizes how scripture functions in covenant communities — the text both shapes and is shaped by the community that reads it; the question of whether the king made the book or the book made the king is anachronistic, since both are true simultaneously. Steelman: this resolves the historical question by reframing it as a feedback loop, but at the cost of leaving open whether anything in the chapter is genuinely revelatory.

Where this leaves us: Josiah's response to the book is the dtr's portrait of right relation to scripture. Whether that book was discovered, composed, or composed-as-discovery, the chapter's claim is that authoritative texts properly read produce political consequences. The Reformation's *sola scriptura* and the rabbinic centrality of Torah-study both descend from the model 2 Kings 22 establishes.

**Second tension: Huldah's divergent oracle.** The two halves of Huldah's response sit uneasily. Verses 16-17 announce unrelenting judgment on the place; verses 18-20 promise Josiah will be gathered in peace. Three positions. Position one (Sweeney, conservative): the oracle is internally consistent — the place is condemned, the king is spared its sight, and "peace" means *before* the catastrophe (Josiah dies in 609, before 587). Steelman: Sweeney's reading of *šālôm* as temporal-priority rather than mode-of-death is grammatically defensible and resolves the apparent contradiction with the Megiddo death. Position two (Cross, Nelson, redaction-critical): the second half reflects original Dtr1 confidence that Josiah and the reform would secure the dynasty; the first half is sharpened or expanded by Dtr2 after the catastrophe. The contradiction is editorial layering. Steelman: this acknowledges the textual history visible in the awkwardness and explains how the same author could write contradictory predictions. Position three (Brueggemann, theological): the contradiction is intentional — the dtr is showing that even the most righteous individual cannot reverse corporate judgment, but that individual righteousness is still recognized and rewarded within the larger judgment. Steelman: this preserves the chapter's moral seriousness about both individual response and corporate consequence.

Where this leaves us: Huldah's oracle is the dtr's clearest statement that righteousness mitigates judgment without averting it. Josiah is spared seeing the destruction. The reform delays the catastrophe but cannot reverse it. The dtr is making peace with a catastrophe his theology cannot fully explain.

**Unresolved question:** Why does Huldah speak the oracle in third person — "the man who sent you to me" (22:15) rather than addressing the king directly? Some commentators have read this as a status-marker (a prophetess does not directly address the king); others as a deliberate distancing that separates Josiah from the institutional kingship being condemned. The text does not say.

## The Thread

Cross-references that matter. **Deut 17:18-20** prescribes that the king copy the law and read in it all his days — Josiah's response in 22:11-13 enacts the Deuteronomic ideal of kingship. **Deut 31:9-13** records Moses giving the law to the priests with instructions to read it publicly every seven years; the tradition of public Torah-reading the dtr inherits is the institutional context for the discovery. **2 Chr 34** parallels the chapter with significant differences in chronology and scope. **Jer 11:1-13** preserves Jeremiah's covenant-renewal preaching from this period — Jeremiah and Josiah's reform are working in parallel, not in opposition.

Forward references this chapter sets up: the reform of **2 Kgs 23:1-25** is the implementation of the book's demands; the death of Josiah at Megiddo (**23:29-30**) is the working-out of Huldah's "peace" oracle in the form the text declines to elaborate; the eventual fall (**2 Kgs 25**) is the activation of the disaster the oracle promised. The Babylonian Chronicle ABC 3's record of Necho's 609 northern campaign — the political occasion of Josiah's death — and Nahum's prophecy of Nineveh's fall (612 BCE) are the contemporary historical-prophetic context for the reform's window of opportunity. The Shaphan family's later role protecting Jeremiah (Jer 26:24) and supplying Gedaliah for the post-587 governorship (2 Kgs 25:22) is the institutional continuity of the seventh-century scribal-reform circle that 2 Kings 22 first introduces.
