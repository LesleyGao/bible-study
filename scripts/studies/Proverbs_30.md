# Proverbs 30 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World — Agur ben Yakeh, the Non-Israelite Sage

Proverbs 30 begins with a superscription that marks an editorial transition every bit as decisive as 25:1's "These also are proverbs of Solomon": *divrei Agur bin Yakeh ha-massa* (דִּבְרֵי אָגוּר בִּן־יָקֶה הַמַּשָּׂא) — "the words of Agur son of Yakeh, the oracle." With these words the book of Proverbs leaves Solomon and the men of Hezekiah behind and turns to a *non-Israelite* voice.

The mainstream-critical consensus — Bruce Waltke (NICOT), Michael V. Fox (Anchor Bible), Roland Murphy, Tremper Longman, William P. Brown, Christine Roy Yoder — reads *ha-massa* as a place-name rather than as the common noun *massa* meaning "oracle / burden." Massa is named in Genesis 25:14 (and 1 Chr 1:30) as one of the twelve sons of Ishmael, an Arabian tribal-territorial group. Agur ben Yakeh, on this reading, is an Ishmaelite-Arabian sage whose wisdom — preserved by Israelite scribes — was deemed worthy of inclusion in the canonical book of Proverbs alongside Solomonic material. The same pattern will hold in 31:1, where King Lemuel is also identified as *melekh massa* — king of Massa.

This is theologically significant. The book of Proverbs, which opens with the foundational claim that "the fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge" (1:7), closes with an editorial willingness to incorporate wisdom from outside the covenant community. Christine Roy Yoder calls this "Proverbs's most explicit acknowledgment of *general revelation* — wisdom available beyond Israel because it is rooted in God's ordering of the world itself." The pattern is not unique in the Hebrew Bible: Job is a non-Israelite, possibly Edomite (cf. Job 1:1, the land of Uz; cf. Lam 4:21); Balaam in Numbers 22-24 is a non-Israelite seer who delivers genuine prophetic oracles; Melchizedek (Gen 14) is a non-Israelite priest of *El Elyon*. The Hebrew Bible has space for *chokhmah* spoken by Gentiles, and chapter 30 makes that space visible.

The opening verse's textual difficulty is famous. The Hebrew of 30:1b reads *ne'um ha-gever le-Iti'el le-Iti'el ve-Ukhal* (נְאֻם הַגֶּבֶר לְאִיתִיאֵל לְאִיתִיאֵל וְאֻכָל) — literally "the utterance of the man to Ithiel, to Ithiel and Ucal." The MT reads these as proper names (Agur addressing his disciples Ithiel and Ucal, parallel to a teacher-naming-students pattern). The ESV (and RSV/NRSV) follow an alternative consonantal repointing that yields *la'iti el la'iti el ve-akhal* — "I am weary, O God; I am weary, O God, and am worn out." Fox treats this repointing as exegetically superior; the Septuagint already shows confusion about the names, and the resulting confessional opening ("I am weary, O God") fits the chapter's tone. The honest critical reading is that both possibilities have textual support and that something theological is at stake either way: Agur is either naming his students (the conservative MT reading) or confessing his exhaustion before God (the modern critical repointing).

Verses 2-3 then announce Agur's confession of cognitive incapacity: *ki va'ar anokhi me-ish ve-lo binat adam li, ve-lo lamadti chokhmah ve-da'at qedoshim eda* (כִּי בַעַר אָנֹכִי מֵאִישׁ וְלֹא־בִינַת אָדָם לִי, וְלֹא־לָמַדְתִּי חָכְמָה וְדַעַת קְדֹשִׁים אֵדָע) — "Surely I am too stupid to be a man; I have not the understanding of a man. I have not learned wisdom, nor have I knowledge of the Holy One." The word *qedoshim* (קְדֹשִׁים, plural "Holy Ones") is unusual; it can refer to God in an intensive plural (the *qadosh*-of-qadoshim) or to the divine council (cf. Job 5:1, 15:15, where the *qedoshim* are heavenly beings). The plural form here is one of the chapter's signs that Agur is operating at the boundary of Israelite covenantal vocabulary, in the broader Semitic-religious lexicon.

The chapter then unfolds in distinct units: a wisdom-confessional opening (1-9), an ethical fragment (10), a four-fold "there are those" generation-portrait (11-14), and a long sequence of *numerical sayings* (15-31) — the famous "three-things-yes-four" form — closing with a brief instruction on restraint (32-33).

## Lens 2: Literature — Confession, Numerical Sayings, and the Mystery Verse

Agur's theological centerpiece is verse 4:

> מִי עָלָה־שָׁמַיִם וַיֵּרַד מִי אָסַף־רוּחַ בְּחָפְנָיו
> מִי צָרַר־מַיִם בַּשִּׂמְלָה מִי הֵקִים כָּל־אַפְסֵי־אָרֶץ
> מַה־שְּׁמוֹ וּמַה־שֶּׁם־בְּנוֹ כִּי תֵדָע
> *mi alah shamayim va-yerad? mi asaf-ruach be-chofnav? mi tzarar-mayim ba-simlah? mi heqim kol-afsei-aretz? mah-shemo u-mah-shem-beno ki teda?*
> "Who has ascended to heaven and come down? Who has gathered the wind in his fists?
> Who has wrapped up the waters in a garment? Who has established all the ends of the earth?
> What is his name, and what is his son's name? Surely you know!"

This is one of the most theologically loaded single verses in Hebrew wisdom literature. The five rhetorical questions move from cosmic agency (ascent and descent, mastery of wind, mastery of water, establishment of the earth's ends) to *naming* — and the final clause, *u-mah-shem-beno* ("and what is his son's name?"), is one of the genuinely strange features of the Hebrew Bible. Within the Old Testament's own register the question can be read as ironic ("who could possibly know?") or as gesturing toward divine relationality (the divine Son alongside the divine Father). Both readings have textual purchase.

The literary form of the chapter's middle section is the *graded numerical saying* — the *shalosh-arba* form (three-yes-four). This is a recognized ANE wisdom genre with parallels in the Aramaic *Words of Ahiqar* and earlier Egyptian instruction literature, but the Hebrew Bible deploys it with particular density in Proverbs 30 and in the prophetic *for three transgressions and for four* in Amos 1-2. Roland Murphy in his *Hermeneia* work on wisdom forms identified the numerical saying as a pedagogical mnemonic device: the teacher names a number, the student is invited into the puzzle of *which examples count*, and the resolution sharpens the underlying conceptual category.

Proverbs 30's numerical sayings are concentrated and varied:
- 30:15-16: "three never-satisfied things, yes four" — Sheol, the barren womb, parched earth, fire.
- 30:18-19: "three too-wonderful things, yes four I do not understand" — the way of an eagle in the sky, a serpent on a rock, a ship on the sea, a man with a virgin (*derekh gever be-almah*, דֶּרֶךְ גֶּבֶר בְּעַלְמָה).
- 30:21-23: "three earth-trembling things, yes four" — the slave become king, the fool filled with food, the unloved woman married, the maidservant displacing her mistress.
- 30:24-28: "four small-but-wise things" — ants, rock badgers (*shefanim*, שְׁפַנִּים, hyraxes), locusts, lizards.
- 30:29-31: "three stately-tread things, yes four" — the lion, the strutting rooster, the he-goat, and the king with his army.

The 30:18-19 mystery is the most famous of these. The four "ways" all share the feature of leaving no visible trace — the eagle's flight, the serpent's slither on smooth rock, the ship's wake erased by water, and (most provocatively) "the way of a man with a virgin." Bruce Waltke and Michael V. Fox both read this as a meditation on the *mystery of attraction and desire* — that something profoundly real has happened (in flight, in motion, in love-making) yet leaves no evidence the way other actions do. William P. Brown sees the four-fold image as a wonder-saying that culminates in the awe of human courtship and intimacy as participating in the same wonder-of-trace-less-motion as flight and seafaring. Verse 20 immediately darkens the picture: the adulteress "eats and wipes her mouth and says, 'I have done no wrong'" — the same trace-less mystery turned to deception. The juxtaposition is morally and literarily exquisite.

The 30:24-28 "small-but-wise" sequence is one of the chapter's most beloved units. Each animal embodies a wisdom that compensates for evident weakness: ants (no individual strength but collective foresight), rock badgers (no power but excellent dwelling-strategy), locusts (no king but coordinated movement), lizards (small enough to be caught yet found in royal palaces). The implicit lesson is that *chokhmah* is available across the spectrum of creaturely power, and that wise weakness can outperform foolish strength.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story — Jewish, Christian, and New Testament Reception

**John 3:13 and 30:4.** The clearest New Testament use of Proverbs 30 is Jesus' word to Nicodemus in John 3:13: *kai oudeis anabebēken eis ton ouranon ei mē ho ek tou ouranou katabas, ho huios tou anthrōpou* — "and no one has ascended into heaven except he who descended from heaven, the Son of Man." The verbal echo of Prov 30:4a (*mi alah shamayim va-yerad*, "who has ascended to heaven and come down") is unmistakable. Where Agur asks the question rhetorically, Jesus answers it Christologically. Ephesians 4:8-10 ("when he ascended on high... in saying 'he ascended,' what does it mean but that he had also descended?") plays the same theological move. Patristic writers from Irenaeus through Augustine to Aquinas all read Prov 30:4 as a wisdom-prefiguration of the incarnation and ascension; the Christological reading is one of the oldest in the Christian interpretive tradition.

**The "son's name" question.** Verse 4's *mah-shem beno* ("what is his son's name?") was read in patristic Christian tradition as a direct prophetic reference to the divine Son. The mainstream-critical reading is more cautious: Agur's question may be ironic ("who could possibly know any of this?") and the *son* may be a wisdom-poetic personification rather than a metaphysical claim. The honest middle position is that the verse is genuinely strange within the Old Testament's own language, that Christian Christological reading is not arbitrary, and that no responsible exegete should claim verse 4 *predicts* the incarnation — it is the kind of verse that, in retrospect, the New Testament authors and the patristic tradition found themselves drawn back to as resonant.

**Verses 7-9 and the prayer of moderation.** Agur's prayer — *shenayim sha'alti me-itakh, al-timna mimmenni be-terem amut: shav u-devar-kazav harcheq mimmeni, resh va-osher al-titen-li, hatrifeni lechem chuqqi* (שְׁנַיִם שָׁאַלְתִּי מֵאִתָּךְ, אַל־תִּמְנַע מִמֶּנִּי בְּטֶרֶם אָמוּת: שָׁוְא וּדְבַר־כָּזָב הַרְחֵק מִמֶּנִּי, רֵאשׁ וָעֹשֶׁר אַל־תִּתֶּן־לִי, הַטְרִיפֵנִי לֶחֶם חֻקִּי) — "Two things I ask of you; deny them not to me before I die: remove far from me falsehood and lying; give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with the food that is needful for me" — is one of the wisdom tradition's most famous prayers. The Hebrew *lechem chuqqi* (לֶחֶם חֻקִּי) means "the bread of my portion," "my appointed bread" — the bread that has been *fixed* or *measured* for me. Jesus' petition in the Lord's Prayer — *ton arton hēmōn ton epiousion* (Matt 6:11; Luke 11:3), "our daily / sufficient bread" — is in deep conceptual continuity with Agur's prayer. Augustine and the Reformers (Luther, Calvin) all draw the connection. Modern Christian and Jewish simplicity-traditions — from monastic vows of poverty through the Mennonite economic ethic through Wendell Berry's agrarian writing — root themselves partly in 30:7-9.

**Jewish reception.** *Mishlei Rabbah* and Rashi treat Agur's confession of incapacity (30:2-3) as a rhetorical posture of true wisdom — the wise person begins with the acknowledgment that wisdom transcends them. The medieval Jewish philosophical tradition (Maimonides, Saadia Gaon) reads verse 4 as confirming the *via negativa* — God's nature is known by what cannot be ascended to, grasped, wrapped, or established by human effort. The prayer of 30:7-9 has a particular place in the Jewish ethical tradition (*musar*) — the *Mishnah Avot* echoes its concern with the dangers of both wealth and poverty.

**The non-Israelite-wisdom reception in Christian theology.** Agur's inclusion in the canon has been one of the foundations for Christian theologies of *general revelation* and *common grace*. The Catholic tradition formalizes this in CCC §31-35 (the human capacity to know God through created things). The Reformed tradition develops it through Calvin's *sensus divinitatis* (the universal inward awareness of divinity, *Institutes* I.3) and through later Reformed writers' theology of common grace (Abraham Kuyper, *De Gemeene Gratie*). Agur, the Ishmaelite sage whose wisdom is canonical, becomes a textual warrant for both traditions' affirmation that genuine knowledge of God is available beyond the borders of explicit covenant.

**Verse 5 and the doctrine of Scripture.** "Every word of God proves true" (*kol-imrat Eloah tzerufah*, כָּל־אִמְרַת אֱלוֹהַּ צְרוּפָה) and verse 6's "Do not add to his words" have been foundational texts in both Jewish and Christian doctrines of Scripture's sufficiency and integrity. The Reformed *sola Scriptura* draws on this material; the rabbinic principle that one does not add or subtract from the Torah (Deut 4:2, 12:32) finds its wisdom-tradition echo here.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — Tensions, Steelman, and Where This Leaves Us

**Tension one: the John 3:13 / Eph 4 Christological appropriation of 30:4.** The position with the longest pedigree (patristic through medieval through Reformation) reads Prov 30:4 as messianic prefiguration — Agur's unanswered question ("who has ascended? what is his son's name?") is answered by Christ's identification of himself in John 3:13 as the one who descended and ascended. The steelman is that the verbal echo in John 3:13 is too precise to be coincidence and that the New Testament authors saw 30:4 as Christologically suggestive. The mainstream-critical pushback is that Agur's question is rhetorical and that responsible exegesis should not extract messianic prediction from material whose original force was the awe of divine transcendence beyond human reach. The honest middle position, held by Waltke and many evangelical scholars: the verse is *generative* of Christological reading without itself *making* a messianic prediction; the New Testament's typological move is legitimate without requiring the original Hebrew to bear the full weight.

**Tension two: the prayer for moderation (30:7-9) and Christian / Jewish economic ethics.** Agur's prayer is uncomfortable for both wealth-prosperity readings of the Bible and for poverty-as-virtue readings. The verse rejects both extremes: wealth produces self-deification ("Who is the LORD?"), and poverty produces theft and profanation. The steelman of the prosperity reading is that wealth is not condemned absolutely (cf. Abraham, Job, Solomon, the early Jerusalem church). The steelman of the poverty-renunciation reading is that Jesus' calls to discipleship in Luke 12 and elsewhere are radical. Agur sits between them — a reformed-ascetic moderation that asks for *enough* and rejects both extremes as theologically dangerous. Modern Christian voices like Tim Keller's *Counterfeit Gods*, Jewish ethical writing on *bechirah chofshit* and economic discipline, and the Catholic social teaching on the universal destination of goods all draw on this prayer.

**Tension three: non-Israelite wisdom in canonical Scripture.** Agur and Lemuel sit at the close of Proverbs as canonical voices of *non-Israelite* wisdom. The implications for theology of religions and theology of revelation are real. The steelman of the strong exclusivist reading is that Agur and Lemuel's inclusion is editorial — Israelite scribes recognized true wisdom and absorbed it into the covenantal frame, but the wisdom remains *true* only as so absorbed. The steelman of the inclusivist reading is that the canonical book itself stands as evidence that *chokhmah* is available beyond Israel and that the Spirit of God speaks through Gentile sages. The honest middle position: Proverbs 30 confirms both that wisdom is genuinely available outside the covenant and that its canonical force depends on its integration with *yir'at YHWH* (the fear of the LORD established at 1:7). Agur himself confesses this: his prayer is to *YHWH* (the divine name appears at 30:9), not to a generic deity. The Gentile sage has been drawn into the covenantal vocabulary.

**Where this leaves us:** Proverbs 30 is the canonical book's most explicit acknowledgment that wisdom can come from outside Israel, and Agur's voice — confessing exhaustion, asking the unanswerable cosmic questions of verse 4, praying for daily bread — is one of the most theologically resonant in the Hebrew Bible. The chapter sits in a unique place: a non-Israelite sage whose words have been received as Scripture, whose central question has been answered by Christ in John 3, and whose prayer for moderation has shaped two and a half millennia of Christian and Jewish economic ethics.

**Closing question:** Where in your own life are you reaching for the *more*-than-needful (30:8-9) — and what would the prayer of *lechem chuqqi*, the bread of *my portion*, ask you to release? And where in the world's voices that are *not* explicitly inside your tradition (Agur's lineage) is genuine wisdom waiting to be received with proper humility about its source?

## The Thread

Proverbs 30 belongs to Agur ben Yakeh, a non-Israelite sage from Massa (an Arabian-Ishmaelite tribal area). His chapter opens with a confession of cognitive incapacity, asks the great unanswerable questions of verse 4 (which John 3:13 will answer Christologically), prays the moderation-prayer of verses 7-9 (which echoes Jesus' "daily bread"), and unfolds in numerical sayings that reveal *chokhmah* in unexpected creaturely places. The chapter is the Bible's most explicit affirmation that genuine wisdom is available beyond Israel.
