# Job 28: The Shaft and the Place of Wisdom

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Job 28 is one of the great self-contained poems of the Hebrew Bible — a single twenty-eight-verse meditation on the whereabouts of wisdom, placed between the collapsing dialogues of chapters 22–27 and the resumption of Job's final monologue in chapter 29. Critical scholars have long noted its anomalous position. The poem is voiced without attribution (no "Job answered and said"), neither addresses the friends nor engages their arguments, and articulates a theological resolution — wisdom belongs to God alone, and the fear of the Lord is wisdom for human beings — that the surrounding narrative has not yet earned. Gerhard von Rad, in *Wisdom in Israel* (1970), treated the chapter as a theological intermezzo, possibly a later editorial insertion. Carol Newsom (NIB and *The Book of Job: A Contest of Moral Imaginations*) follows von Rad in treating it as a kind of choric interlude that the book stages as neither Job's nor the friends' but something the reader is meant to overhear. J. Gerald Janzen (Interpretation) argues similarly. Against this, Norman Habel (OTL), Francis Andersen (Tyndale), and Robert Fyall defend the chapter as Job's own speech and read it as integral to his developing thought. David Clines (WBC) takes a middle position — the poem is likely secondary to the dialogue but integrated early enough to function as a deliberate pivot.

The dating question tracks the book as a whole. Pope, Clines, and Newsom argue post-exilic composition, reading the book as a theological reckoning with the collapse of Deuteronomic retribution after 587 BC. Gordis and Andersen argue pre-exilic, perhaps seventh century, citing the archaic Hebrew and the absence of covenant vocabulary. Ezekiel 14:14 establishes Job as a legendary figure by the sixth century BC but does not date the book in its current form. Chapter 28 in particular shows signs of independent wisdom composition — its vocabulary and structure align closely with the hymnic wisdom of Proverbs 8 and Sirach 24, making many scholars suspect it originated as a stand-alone wisdom hymn later inserted into Job.

Archaeological evidence illuminates the chapter's extraordinary opening description of mining. Verses 1–11 offer the most detailed description of ancient mining operations in the Hebrew Bible: mine shafts opened in remote valleys, miners dangling on ropes, darkness mastered by lamps, rock hewn by hand, streams dammed, subterranean fire for smelting. Egyptian mining operations in the Sinai (Serabit el-Khadim) and at Timna in the Arabah — where archaeologist Beno Rothenberg's excavations (1959–90) exposed deep shaft mines for copper — provide material context. Ugaritic and Mesopotamian metallurgy supply parallels. The description of hanging miners swinging on ropes (v. 4) has been interpreted in light of documented Egyptian New Kingdom mining practice. The "dust of gold" (v. 6) and "gold of Ophir" (v. 16) locate the poem in a world of long-distance trade in precious metals, perhaps reflecting the period of Phoenician commerce. *Ludlul Bel Nemeqi*, the *Babylonian Theodicy*, and the so-called *Dialogue of Pessimism* furnish Mesopotamian wisdom parallels. The hymn-to-wisdom genre has partial antecedents in the Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemope* and the Sumerian *Instructions of Shuruppak*, but Job 28's sustained mining-analogy is unparalleled in the ancient Near East.

Textual witnesses include 11QTargum Job, which preserves portions of chapter 28 in Aramaic paraphrase, and the LXX Job, which compresses the chapter noticeably, perhaps because the translator found the mining vocabulary and the hymnic insertion difficult. Qumran's 4QpaleoJobᶜ preserves fragments in paleo-Hebrew script, testifying to the ancient sanctity of the book.

Hebrew terms carry the chapter's weight. חָכְמָה (*ḥokhmah*, vv. 12, 18, 20, 28) — "wisdom" — the governing noun of the poem, cognate with Akkadian *nemequ* (the same word in the title of *Ludlul Bel Nemeqi*, "I Will Praise the Lord of *Nemeqi*"). בִּינָה (*binah*, vv. 12, 20, 28) — "understanding," "discernment" — pairs with wisdom throughout. תְּהוֹם (*tehom*, v. 14) — "the deep" — the primordial abyss of Genesis 1:2, cognate with Ugaritic *thm* and Akkadian *tiamtu*/Tiamat, here personified as unable to locate wisdom. אֲבַדּוֹן (*ʾavaddon*, v. 22) — "Abaddon," "place of destruction" — paired with מָוֶת (*mavet*, "Death"), here speaking in first person to report only a "rumor" (שֵׁמַע, *shemaʿ*) of wisdom. יִרְאַת אֲדֹנָי (*yirʾat ʾadonai*, v. 28) — "the fear of the Lord" — strikingly, this is the only occurrence of the divine name אֲדֹנָי (*ʾadonai*) in Job (everywhere else the book uses ʾElohim, ʾEloah, Shaddai, or YHWH in the frame). Many scholars see this as decisive evidence of the chapter's editorial origin or later revision.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The poem is built in three unequal strophes demarcated by a refrain-question. Verses 1–11 describe the human mining of precious things from the earth. Verse 12 poses the first question: "But where shall wisdom be found?" Verses 13–19 assert that wisdom cannot be bought or mined. Verse 20 poses the refrain again: "From where, then, does wisdom come?" Verses 21–27 answer: God alone knows the way to wisdom. Verse 28 — arguably a fourth strophe, or arguably a coda — offers a gnomic conclusion: "the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom."

The structure is a masterpiece of poetic architecture. The opening strophe is a sustained hymn to human technical mastery — humanity lights the darkness, hangs in the air, overturns mountains, dams streams, brings hidden things to light. The middle strophe denies that this mastery extends to wisdom. The third strophe affirms that God, whose gaze reaches the ends of the earth and whose wisdom is coeval with creation, alone possesses it. The coda pivots anthropologically: wisdom for humans is not metaphysical knowledge but moral posture — fear of God and turning from evil.

Robert Alter, in *The Wisdom Books*, praises the opening strophe's descriptive genius: the miner's world is rendered in tight, vivid Hebrew that makes you feel the rock, the darkness, the suspension. The verb נִשְׁכָּחִים (*nishkaḥim*, v. 4) — "they are forgotten" — describing miners dangling in remote shafts suggests both physical remoteness and existential forgottenness, a small theological tremor in what otherwise seems a celebration of human ingenuity. Verse 5's "out of it comes bread, but underneath it is turned up as by fire" juxtaposes agriculture above and metallurgy below — the whole human economy mapped in a single couplet.

Hebrew parallelism is dense throughout. Verses 15–19 form a list-poem of precious substances (gold, silver, gold of Ophir, onyx, sapphire, gold-and-glass, jewels, coral, crystal, pearls, topaz of Ethiopia, pure gold) that wisdom outvalues. Alter notes that the cumulative effect is less informational than incantatory — the sheer pile of jewels staging wisdom's outstripping of them all. Verse 14's brief dialogue (*tehom* and *yam* each speaking) and v. 22's brief dialogue (*ʾavaddon* and *mavet* speaking) form a chiastic pair — deep and sea in the earlier verse, Abaddon and Death in the later — rhetorically stepping down into the darkest possible places where wisdom is still not found.

The refrain structure — vv. 12 and 20 asking nearly identically-phrased questions, with v. 20 slightly heightened ("from where, then") — creates the poem's rhythmic spine. Inclusio binds the opening (the miner's eye that "sees every precious thing," v. 10) with the closing (God who "looks to the ends of the earth and sees everything under the heavens," v. 24) — the human miner's sight stretched to divine scale.

The textual problem concerns v. 28. Clines and many critical commentators regard it as a later addition that resolves the poem's ambiguity in the direction of orthodox wisdom piety. The phrase יִרְאַת אֲדֹנָי (*yirʾat ʾadonai*) appears nowhere else in Job, and the conclusion closely echoes Proverbs 9:10 ("the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom"), which some read as evidence of editorial harmonization with Proverbs. Habel defends v. 28 as integral to the poem. Newsom and Janzen read it as a deliberate pivot that the poem builds toward — the surprise that wisdom's inaccessibility to human mining does not leave humans without any wisdom at all. LXX Job renders v. 28 but with theological adjustments.

Gerhard von Rad's treatment in *Wisdom in Israel* remains foundational: the chapter is a reflection on the limits of human knowledge, a wisdom-theological counterweight to wisdom's earlier confidence in Proverbs, and possibly a stage in Israel's intellectual history between Proverbs' optimism and Ecclesiastes' skepticism.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Job stands in the Ketuvim as the middle term between Proverbs' confident retributive wisdom and Ecclesiastes' skeptical reckoning with vanity. Within Job itself, chapter 28 sits at a hinge — after the dialogue has broken down, before Job's final monologue and Elihu's speeches, long before the whirlwind. Its placement makes it read almost as a commentary on everything that has preceded: neither Job's protest nor the friends' retributive theology has accessed wisdom, because wisdom is not the kind of thing that can be accessed by argument at all.

The Jewish reception tradition reads the chapter with intense seriousness. *Bava Batra* 15a–16b records rabbinic debate about Job's existence and era, and the rabbis understood the hymn to wisdom as one of the book's central theological moments. Rashi reads the chapter as Job's affirmation that true wisdom is known only to God, and that human wisdom consists in piety. Ibn Ezra attends to philological detail and reads the poem as a sustained contrast between the mining of the earth's hidden treasures and the inaccessibility of wisdom proper. Maimonides, in *Guide of the Perplexed* III.22–23, treats Job's speeches as a philosophical progression and v. 28's "fear of the Lord" as the book's teaching on practical rather than speculative knowledge. Nachmanides reads the chapter mystically, with attention to the divine names. In modern Jewish thought, Martin Buber reads Job 28 as a wisdom hymn that does not solve Job's problem but refracts it; Jon Levenson attends to the chapter's cosmological claims; James Kugel treats the poem as evidence for a late wisdom-theological strand in the book; Avivah Zornberg reads the poem as a meditation on the limits of human mastery. Robert Alter's translation and commentary in *The Wisdom Books* emphasize the chapter's literary autonomy and its role as an interlude of clarity between Joban voices of protest and counter-protest.

Christian reception is equally deep. Origen read chapter 28 Christologically — wisdom hidden from creation is Christ, the pre-existent Word. Gregory the Great's *Moralia in Job* devoted extensive allegorical attention to the chapter: the miner's shafts are the contemplative's descent into the interior life, the precious metals are virtues, and the "fear of the Lord" is the contemplative disposition. Augustine cited the chapter in discussions of divine providence. Thomas Aquinas's *Expositio super Iob* treated v. 28 as a statement of the formal relation between divine wisdom (inaccessible to humans) and practical wisdom (available through piety). Calvin preached on Job 28 at length in his 1554–55 sermon cycle, reading the chapter as a proof of human epistemic limitation and an argument for revelation. Luther found in the chapter an early statement of the hiddenness of God (*Deus absconditus*). The critical tradition — Wellhausen, Gunkel, von Rad — produced the hypothesis that chapter 28 is editorial, a view refined by Clines, Newsom, Janzen, and others. Gerhard von Rad in *Wisdom in Israel* made the chapter central to his argument for a wisdom-theological movement within Israelite thought. Gustavo Gutiérrez reads the chapter in *On Job* as a theological check on any certainty, especially the theological certainty that would enable oppression. Ellen Davis reads it as an ethical call to epistemic humility. Susannah Ticciati reads it as a speech of deconstruction of the self-mastering subject. Habel, Hartley (NICOT), Andersen, and Fyall defend the chapter's integrity to the book. Fyall in particular reads the chapter as preparing the reader for the whirlwind speeches by naming, in advance, that wisdom belongs to God alone.

A significant convergence runs across Jewish and Christian traditions: both read Job 28 as an affirmation that true wisdom is inaccessible to human technical mastery and accessible only through fear of God and moral turning. A sharp divergence appears over v. 28's status. The Jewish tradition generally reads v. 28 as integral and climactic. The Christian medieval and Reformation traditions read it as integral and doctrinally central. Modern critical scholarship, across both Jewish and Christian scholars, more often reads it as editorial — though critical scholars still affirm its theological value as a hermeneutical key to the whole book, whatever its compositional origin.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is Job 28 Job's speech, a narrator's interlude, or an editorial insertion?**

- *It is Job's.* Habel, Andersen, Hartley, and Fyall argue that the chapter belongs to Job, perhaps continuing from chapter 27 (where 27:1 marked "Job again took up his discourse"). On this reading, Job has moved from his oath of integrity to a mature reflection on the inaccessibility of wisdom — a pivot that prepares him for the direct encounter with God in the whirlwind. The chapter is Job's concession that even his protest has not located wisdom, which belongs to God alone. The sharp shift in tone and vocabulary reflects the depth of Job's theological development across the book.

- *It is an editorial intermezzo, a choric voice.* Von Rad, Newsom, Janzen, and a broad critical tradition treat the chapter as a narrative voice standing outside the dialogue — neither Job nor the friends, but a reflective commentary voiced by the book itself. The absence of speech-attribution, the hymnic style, the vocabulary distinctive to this chapter (especially *ʾadonai* in v. 28), and the theological closure all support this reading. The chapter does the work of slowing the reader down and offering a framework for what follows.

- *Steelman — the chapter is polyphonically integrated.* Newsom's subtler position (*Contest of Moral Imaginations*) is that the book is deliberately polyphonic, and chapter 28 is deliberately un-attributed. The reader is meant to hear the poem without being told whose it is — not because the text is damaged, but because the book wants the reader to wrestle directly with the theology of the poem rather than triangulating it through a speaker. On this reading, the attribution ambiguity is a feature, not a bug, and any attempt to resolve it misunderstands the book's design.

**Tension 2: What is the relationship between human mastery (vv. 1–11) and wisdom (vv. 12–28)?**

- *Disjunction — mining maps the limit.* The standard reading holds that the chapter establishes a sharp contrast: humans can master the earth's hidden treasures through technical skill, but wisdom is categorically different. Wisdom cannot be mined, bought, or located in the physical world. This reading emphasizes the epistemic gulf between what humans can achieve (extraordinary, as the opening strophe acknowledges) and what humans cannot access (wisdom proper).

- *Analogy — mining as template for wisdom-seeking.* A more subtle reading, traceable to Gregory the Great and modern readers attentive to the poem's imagery, sees the mining description as a positive analogy. Just as gold is hidden in the rock and must be sought with patience and skill, wisdom is hidden with God and must be sought with patience and the appropriate skill — which turns out to be fear of the Lord. The opening strophe is not irrelevant background but a preparatory parable. Humans know how to search; the question is how to transfer that searching disposition to the right object.

- *Steelman — the poem is about the limits of the subject that masters.* A reading drawing on Susannah Ticciati and von Rad suggests the chapter is neither pure disjunction nor pure analogy. The human miner, triumphant over rock and darkness, is the very figure whose mastery is being quietly relativized. The "forgotten" miners hanging in the air (v. 4) are the dark note that prepares the denial. The poem is about what happens to the technical, mastering self when it confronts what it cannot master. Wisdom is not a *harder* object of mastery; it is the object that dissolves the mastering self into fear and turning-from-evil. The chapter is a theological anthropology in miniature.

Where this leaves us: Job 28 is either the book's theological key or its theological distraction — depending on how one reads its attribution and its relation to what precedes and follows. The poem asserts that wisdom belongs to God and that human wisdom is fear of God — a claim so apparently orthodox that it raises the suspicion that the book is elsewhere saying something more radical. But the poem's strangeness — the miners' forgottenness, Abaddon's rumor, the divine name appearing only here — suggests that even its piety is more unsettled than it first appears.

One unresolved question: If wisdom belongs to God alone, and human wisdom is fear of God, what then is the book of Job itself offering us — is it wisdom, or is it the rigorous performance of not having wisdom?

## The Thread

- **Proverbs 8:22–31** — personified Wisdom declaring that she was with God at creation, "when he inscribed a circle on the face of the deep." The verbal and thematic overlap with Job 28:23–27 (God apportioning waters, making a decree for rain) is extensive, suggesting either direct dependence or shared wisdom-theological vocabulary.

- **Baruch 3:9–4:4** — a deuterocanonical hymn to wisdom that rewrites Job 28 directly, identifying wisdom with Torah. The continuation shows how later Jewish tradition resolved Job 28's open question by naming wisdom as covenantally given in the law.

- **Sirach 24** — Ben Sira's hymn to Wisdom, where Wisdom identifies herself as dwelling in Israel and being instantiated in "the book of the covenant of the Most High." Another later Jewish resolution of Job 28's unanswered question.

- **1 Corinthians 1:18–2:13** — Paul's Christological answer: "Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God," wisdom hidden from the wise of this age and revealed through the cross. The New Testament locates the Job 28 answer in a crucified Messiah.
