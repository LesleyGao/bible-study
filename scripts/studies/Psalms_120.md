# Psalm 120 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 120 opens the **Songs of Ascents** (*Shir HaMa'alot*, שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת), the fifteen psalms (Pss 120–134) bearing this superscription that form one of the most coherent sub-collections in the Psalter. The Hebrew phrase is most naturally rendered "song of the ascents" or "song of the steps." Three principal explanations have been proposed since antiquity. The first, dominant in modern scholarship from Hermann Gunkel forward, treats them as **pilgrim songs** sung on the way up to Jerusalem for the three pilgrimage festivals — Passover (*Pesach*), Pentecost (*Shavuot*), and Tabernacles (*Sukkot*) — fulfilling the Deuteronomic command (Deut 16:16) that all males appear before the LORD three times a year. Jerusalem sits in the Judean hills, and one literally *ascends* to it from any direction, a geographical fact that gave Hebrew the idiom *aliyah* (עֲלִיָּה) for pilgrimage. The second, preserved in *Mishnah Sukkah* 5:4, says that the Levites sang these fifteen psalms on the **fifteen semicircular steps** that led from the women's court to the men's court (Court of Israel) in the Second Temple — a literal "psalm per step." The third, advanced by Ibn Ezra and revived by some moderns, identifies the *ma'alot* with the post-exilic ascent from Babylon (cf. Ezra 7:9, where *ha-ma'alah* means the going-up from exile). These readings need not exclude one another: a post-exilic editor likely shaped pilgrim songs with the return-from-exile resonance audible underneath.

Gunkel's form-critical classification places Psalm 120 firmly in the genre of the **individual lament** (*Klagelied des Einzelnen*) — though it is the briefest and most concentrated specimen of the type. The opening verse, however, already steps into thanksgiving: *li-Yahweh ba-tzarah li qarati va-ya'aneni* (לַיהוָה בַּצָּרָה לִּי קָרָאתִי וַיַּעֲנֵנִי, "in my distress I called to the LORD, and he answered me," 120:1). The verb form is perfect — past completed action — so some scholars (Mowinckel, Kraus) classify the psalm as a **thanksgiving song** that retrospectively recounts the cry from distress. The petition then resumes in v. 2: *hatzilah nafshi Yahweh mi-sefat-sheqer mi-lashon remiyyah* (הַצִּילָה נַפְשִׁי יְהוָה מִשְּׂפַת־שֶׁקֶר מִלָּשׁוֹן רְמִיָּה, "deliver my soul, O LORD, from lying lips, from a deceitful tongue").

The dating is contested but probably **post-exilic**. The pilgrimage collection presupposes a functioning Jerusalem cult, and the diaspora-tinged geography of v. 5 — *Meshech* (מֶשֶׁךְ) and *Kedar* (קֵדָר) — fits a Persian-period audience scattered across the empire. Meshech, listed in Genesis 10:2 as a son of Japheth and known from Assyrian sources as the *Mušku* of central Anatolia (later associated with the Phrygians), lay in the **far north**; Kedar, the Arabian tribe descended from Ishmael (Gen 25:13) and dwelling in the desert tents east of Palestine (Isa 21:16–17), lay in the **far south**. No one lives in both at once. The pairing is **geographical merism** — a literary device that names two extremes to evoke totality, as in "from Dan to Beersheba." The speaker is figuratively saying: *I have lived as far from Zion as one can be, in every direction.*

The deceitful-tongue motif runs through Hebrew wisdom and lament literature (cf. Pss 12, 52, 64; Prov 6:16–19; Jer 9:3–9). The Hebrew *lashon remiyyah* (לָשׁוֹן רְמִיָּה) — "tongue of deceit" — names slander as a weapon. The rhetorical question of v. 3, *mah-yitten lekha u-mah-yosif lakh lashon remiyyah* ("what shall be given to you, and what more shall be done to you, you deceitful tongue?"), echoes the **oath formula** found in 1 Samuel 3:17 and 25:22 — the one who swears falsely invokes covenantal punishment on themselves. The answer in v. 4 is poetic *lex talionis*: *chitzei gibbor shenunim im gachalei retamim* (חִצֵּי גִבּוֹר שְׁנוּנִים עִם גַּחֲלֵי רְתָמִים) — "a warrior's sharp arrows, with glowing coals of the broom tree." The *retem* (broom tree, *Retama raetam*) of the Negev produces charcoal that burns at exceptionally high temperatures with little smoke and embers that retain heat far longer than ordinary wood — Bedouin tradition still prizes it for its slow, intense burn. The image is exact: the slanderer's tongue will be answered by arrows that strike sharp and embers that burn long.

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve the Songs of Ascents in **11QPs^a** (11Q5), the Great Psalms Scroll from Qumran Cave 11. The scroll arranges the psalms in a different order from the Masoretic Text and includes additional non-canonical compositions, but Psalm 120 appears with text substantially identical to the MT, confirming the stability of the Hebrew tradition. The Septuagint numbers it Psalm 119 (LXX numbering runs one behind MT here) and renders the superscription *ōdē tōn anabathmōn* (ᾠδὴ τῶν ἀναβαθμῶν, "song of the steps") — preserving the staircase imagery that informed the Mishnaic interpretation.

## Lens 2: The Literature Itself

The psalm is exceptionally compact — seven verses, organized in three short strophes with a clear movement: **answered cry** (v. 1) → **petition and curse** (vv. 2–4) → **lament of alienation** (vv. 5–7). The opening's perfect-tense report of past deliverance frames everything that follows: the speaker remembers being heard, then re-enters the still-present distress.

Robert Lowth's three categories of Hebrew parallelism are all present in miniature. **Synonymous parallelism** dominates v. 2 — "lying lips" (*sefat-sheqer*) parallels "deceitful tongue" (*lashon remiyyah*); the two body-part metaphors for speech reinforce one another. **Synthetic (or constructive) parallelism** governs v. 4: the second line ("glowing coals of the broom tree") doesn't repeat the first ("warrior's sharp arrows") but extends and intensifies it — sharp + slow-burning = double judgment. **Antithetic parallelism** structures v. 7: *ani-shalom ve-ki adabber hemmah la-milchamah* ("I am for peace, but when I speak, they are for war"). The contrast between *shalom* (שָׁלוֹם, peace, wholeness) and *milchamah* (מִלְחָמָה, war) is the psalm's final and bitterest pivot.

The psalm contains a striking **lexical inclusio** through repetition of speech-organs and tongue-imagery: *qarati* (I called, v. 1) → *sefat-sheqer / lashon remiyyah* (lying lips, deceitful tongue, v. 2) → *lashon remiyyah* (v. 3) → *adabber* (when I speak, v. 7). The whole psalm is **about speech under threat from speech**: the prayerful tongue that calls to God versus the deceitful tongues that surround the speaker. The body-part lexicon names the very organ being abused.

The geographical merism of v. 5 is a strophic centerpiece. The construction *oy-li ki-garti meshech shakhanti im-oholei qedar* ("woe is me that I sojourn in Meshech, that I dwell among the tents of Kedar") uses two different verbs of dwelling: *gur* (גּוּר) means temporary residence as a resident-alien (the same root behind *ger*, the sojourner that Israel was in Egypt and that Torah commands Israel to protect); *shakhan* (שָׁכַן) means more settled habitation (the verb behind *Shekhinah*, the divine indwelling). The speaker is at once temporary visitor and permanent resident in hostile territory — both descriptions of the diaspora condition.

The strophic shape mirrors the Songs of Ascents' broader pattern of **graded repetition** that gives the collection its incantatory rhythm: phrases cascade and tighten across these fifteen psalms (most famously in Ps 121's six-fold *shomer*). Psalm 120's contribution is the establishing shot — the place from which the journey to Zion *begins*.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story (Jewish and Christian Trajectories)

Within the Psalter's final shape, Psalm 120 stands at the threshold of **Book V** (Pss 107–150), the post-exilic-shaped book that begins with the call "let the redeemed of the LORD say so" (Ps 107:2) and ends with the *Hallel* doxology of Pss 146–150. Book V's editorial logic moves from the gathering of the diaspora (Ps 107) through the great Torah meditation (Ps 119) into the Songs of Ascents — a liturgical itinerary from exile to Zion. Psalm 120 is the first step on that staircase, and by beginning in Meshech and Kedar, the editor signals that the road to the Temple starts in alienation, not in proximity.

In **Jewish reception**, *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 120 reads the deceitful tongue through the rabbinic category of *lashon ha-ra* (לָשׁוֹן הָרַע, the evil tongue) — slander, gossip, defamation — which the rabbis treat as among the most destructive of sins, equivalent to murder, idolatry, and adultery combined (b. *Arakhin* 15b). **Rashi** (11th c. France) reads Meshech and Kedar literally as Gentile peoples among whom Israel suffered exile, while **Ibn Ezra** (12th c. Spain) treats them as symbolic of any wicked nation. **Radak** (David Kimchi, 12th–13th c. Provence) emphasizes the merism, noting that the speaker cannot literally dwell in both at once. In the Mishnah's account of Temple practice (*Sukkah* 5:4), the Levites stood on the fifteen steps and sang these psalms during the all-night water-drawing celebration of *Simchat Beit ha-Sho'evah* on Sukkot — embedding Psalm 120 in the most exuberant pilgrimage festival of the Jewish year. Modern Jewish readers — Martin **Buber**, Abraham Joshua **Heschel**, and Jon **Levenson** — locate the Songs of Ascents within the theology of *aliyah*: pilgrimage as the body's confession that one's true home is elsewhere. **Nahum Sarna** (*Songs of the Heart*) emphasizes the merism of Meshech and Kedar as the post-exilic poet's signature: the diaspora's longing distilled into geography.

In **Christian reception**, **Augustine**'s *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Ps 120 (in Latin numbering, Ps 119) reads Meshech and Kedar allegorically as the world of unbelief among which the pilgrim Christian dwells — an extended *peregrinatio* toward the heavenly Jerusalem. **Cassiodorus** (6th c.) systematizes this: the Songs of Ascents become the staircase of virtues by which the soul climbs to God. **Aquinas** reads v. 1 ("I called to the LORD, and he answered me") as the inaugural act of Christian prayer. **Luther**'s 1532 *Operationes in Psalmos* lectures highlight the deceit-versus-truth opposition as the inner-life shape of the Christian under spiritual attack. **Calvin**'s 1557 commentary stresses that Meshech and Kedar designate any place where God's people are a minority among the hostile — a reading that resonated with persecuted Reformed congregations across Europe. Among moderns, **Hermann Gunkel** classified the psalm as individual lament; **Sigmund Mowinckel** read the Songs of Ascents through cultic-festival reconstruction; **Claus Westermann** highlighted the lament-to-praise movement; **Hans-Joachim Kraus** (BKAT) and the **Hossfeld–Zenger** commentary develop the post-exilic pilgrim hypothesis with most rigor; **John Goldingay** (Baker, 2008) emphasizes the way each Song of Ascents builds on the previous; **Walter Brueggemann** treats Ps 120 as the lament without which the journey to Zion has no honesty; **Patrick Miller**, **Bill Brown**, and **Beth Tanner** locate it as the necessary first step. **Eugene Peterson**'s *A Long Obedience in the Same Direction: Discipleship in an Instant Society* (1980, IVP) is the contemporary popular treatment that has shaped a generation of evangelical readers; Peterson takes Ps 120's *Meshech and Kedar* opening as the spiritual fact that **Christian discipleship begins in exile** — the first step of the long obedience is the recognition that one is far from home, and the "instant society" he names is itself a kind of Meshech.

The **New Testament** does not directly cite Ps 120, but its ethic of speech under persecution (James 3:1–12 on the tongue; 1 Pet 2:11–12 on Christians as *paroikoi*, sojourners) carries the psalm's theology forward. The apostolic writers' framing of the church as a pilgrim people dwelling among hostile speech is recognizably Psalm 120's frame.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1 — Symbolic geography or literal exile?** When the speaker says "I sojourn in Meshech, I dwell among the tents of Kedar," is this a poetic merism for the diaspora condition broadly, or does it reflect a specific historical circumstance of literal exile in Anatolia or Arabia? The literal-historical position (Rashi; some 19th-century commentators) reads the verses as an actual diaspora address: Israelites scattered to the northern frontier (Meshech) or trading among Arabian Bedouin (Kedar) sing this psalm as they prepare for *aliyah*. The poetic-symbolic position (Ibn Ezra, Radak, most moderns including Goldingay and Hossfeld–Zenger) notes that one cannot dwell in both at once, and that the merism captures the universal diaspora experience without specifying location. A mediating position (Kraus, Brueggemann) holds that the merism is symbolic but the lament is rooted in a real historical Sitz-im-Leben of post-exilic dispersion. *The steelman for each:* The literal reading honors the specificity of the named places and the realism of post-exilic diaspora geography — these were not arbitrary symbols. The symbolic reading honors the impossibility of dwelling in both and the universality of the lament, which has allowed every subsequent diaspora generation (Babylonian, Hellenistic, Roman, medieval, modern) to read themselves into the verse. The mediating reading takes both seriously: the merism is symbolic *because* the historical situation made every location feel like Meshech-and-Kedar at once.

**Tension 2 — Where does the Songs of Ascents pilgrimage begin?** Eugene Peterson's *A Long Obedience* makes a strong claim: discipleship begins in *exile* (Ps 120's Meshech and Kedar), not in proximity to the Temple. The pilgrimage cannot start from a place of comfort because comfort needs no journey. Against this, a more cult-functional reading (Mowinckel, and in different register Kraus) treats the Songs of Ascents as actual pilgrim hymns sung en route or on arrival — the lament of Ps 120 is not the *start* of discipleship but a remembered prelude that the pilgrim has already moved beyond. *Steelman for Peterson's reading:* The collection's editorial shape — beginning in distress and merism, ending in priestly blessing in Ps 134 — argues for a journey-narrative reading in which Ps 120's exile is the necessary first step. Without lament, there is no road to praise. *Steelman for the cult-functional reading:* The psalms work as actual liturgy, and pilgrims who are physically *en route* to Jerusalem are not in Meshech in any meaningful sense — they are in motion toward the Temple. The lament is genre-formal, not biographical.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 120 establishes the **honesty of the journey**. Whatever the precise historical setting, the editorial placement at the head of the Songs of Ascents commits the entire pilgrimage collection to beginning with what is wrong before celebrating what is right. The pilgrim does not arrive at Zion by pretending Meshech and Kedar do not exist; the pilgrim arrives by naming them, calling on the LORD from inside them, and walking. This refuses two falsifications at once — the triumphalism that skips the lament, and the quietism that stops at the lament. The deceit-and-war world of vv. 2–7 is the world the pilgrim leaves behind by walking up; the call-and-answer of v. 1 is the road by which the leaving happens.

The closing verse — "I am for peace, but when I speak, they are for war" — leaves us with a question the rest of the Songs of Ascents will spend fourteen psalms answering: *what does it look like to keep speaking peace in a country at war with peace?*
