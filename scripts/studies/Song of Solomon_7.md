# Song of Solomon 7 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Song of Solomon 7 contains the man's most explicit *waṣf* (وصف, the descriptive love-poem genre) of the woman in the entire Hebrew Bible — a foot-to-head body-praise (7:1-9) that has tested the comfort of every interpretive tradition. The book's Hebrew title, *Shir HaShirim* (שִׁיר הַשִּׁירִים, "the Song of Songs"), uses a superlative construction (like *qodesh qodashim*, "Holy of Holies") meaning "the greatest of songs." The superscription *asher li-Shlomoh* (אֲשֶׁר לִשְׁלֹמֹה, 1:1) traditionally attributed authorship to Solomon. Modern critical scholarship — Marvin Pope (*Anchor Bible*), Roland Murphy (*Hermeneia*), Othmar Keel (*Continental Commentary*), Cheryl Exum (*Old Testament Library*), Tremper Longman (*NICOT*), Michael Fox (*The Song of Songs and the Ancient Egyptian Love Songs*), Robert Alter — generally dates the final form to the post-exilic Persian or Hellenistic period (fifth to third century BC), based on Persian loanwords (*pardes*, פַּרְדֵּס) and late Hebrew syntax. The poem may draw on older pre-exilic material.

The opening line, *mah-yafu fa'amayikh ba-ne'alim bat-nadiv* (מַה־יָּפוּ פְעָמַיִךְ בַּנְּעָלִים בַּת־נָדִיב, "how beautiful are your feet in sandals, O noble daughter," 7:1 in English; 7:2 in Hebrew), begins the *waṣf* at the feet — the inverse direction from the woman's head-to-feet praise of the man in 5:10-16. Othmar Keel's *Continental Commentary* documented the close affinity of this *waṣf* with Egyptian love poetry of the New Kingdom (Chester Beatty Papyri, c. 1300 BC) and with Mesopotamian wedding-poem fragments. Marvin Pope's *Anchor Bible* commentary controversially proposed cultic-funerary background (the *marzeah* feast) for some elements of the Song; most contemporary scholars treat the parallels as motif-borrowing rather than ritual borrowing.

The geographic references locate the body-praise in the northern Israelite landscape: *Heshbon* (חֶשְׁבּוֹן, 7:4), an Amorite-period city east of the Jordan; *Bat-Rabbim* (בַּת־רַבִּים, "Daughter of Many"), a gate at Heshbon; *Levanon* (לְבָנוֹן, the Lebanon range); *Damesek* (דַּמֶּשֶׂק, Damascus); *Karmel* (כַּרְמֶל, Mount Carmel). These are all northern, suggesting either a northern provenance for this poem-fragment or a pan-Israelite imaginative geography of the beloved as land.

The *duda'im* (דּוּדָאִים, "mandrakes," 7:13) are mandrake-plant fruits that feature in the Rachel-Leah exchange of Genesis 30:14-16. Mandrakes were widely understood in the ANE as fertility-aphrodisiacs — the Hebrew word may pun on *dod* (דּוֹד, "beloved"). The mention of *peraḥim* (פְּרָחִים, "blossoms") and *semadar* (סְמָדַר, "grape blossoms") locates the closing scene in spring, the canonical time of love (cf. 2:11-13).

Major scholars whose work intersects this chapter: Marvin Pope, Roland Murphy, Robert Alter, Cheryl Exum, Tremper Longman, Othmar Keel, Michael Fox, Phyllis Trible, Renita Weems, Ellen Davis, Ariel and Chana Bloch.

## Lens 2: The Literature

The chapter divides into three movements. First, the man's *waṣf* of the woman (7:1-9a), structured foot-to-head: feet, thighs, navel, belly, breasts, neck, eyes, nose, head, locks. This is the third *waṣf* in the book (cf. 4:1-7 head-to-breasts; 6:4-7 partial reprise) and the most explicit. The body imagery is architectural and agricultural at once: thighs as *ḥalla'im ma'aseh yedei aman* (חֲלָאִים מַעֲשֵׂה יְדֵי אָמָּן, "ornaments, the work of a master craftsman's hands"), navel as *aggan ha-sahar* (אַגַּן הַסַּהַר, "rounded bowl") of mixed wine, belly as *aremat ḥittim* (עֲרֵמַת חִטִּים, "heap of wheat") encircled with lilies, breasts as *shenei ofarim te'omei tzviyyah* (שְׁנֵי עֳפָרִים תָּאֳמֵי צְבִיָּה, "two fawns, twins of a gazelle"), neck as *migdal ha-shen* (מִגְדַּל הַשֵּׁן, "tower of ivory"), eyes as *brekhot be-Heshbon* (בְּרֵכוֹת בְּחֶשְׁבּוֹן, "pools in Heshbon"), nose as *migdal ha-Levanon* (מִגְדַּל הַלְּבָנוֹן, "tower of Lebanon"), head as *Karmel*, locks as purple held captive. Cheryl Exum reads the architectural-agricultural fusion as a poetic strategy that simultaneously eroticizes and dignifies the female body — the woman is land *and* fortress *and* harvest *and* royal subject.

The shift at 7:6-9 moves from third-person description to second-person address: *mah yafit u-mah na'amt ahavah ba-ta'anugim* (מַה־יָּפִית וּמַה־נָּעַמְתְּ אַהֲבָה בַּתַּעֲנוּגִים, "how beautiful and how pleasant you are, love, with delights!"). The intensifying *mah... u-mah* construction is rare. The man then declares his intent: *amarti e'eleh ve-tamar oḥazah be-sansinav* (אָמַרְתִּי אֶעֱלֶה בְתָמָר אֹחֲזָה בְּסַנְסִנָּיו, "I say I will climb the palm tree and lay hold of its fruit-stalks," 7:8). The palm-tree-climbing image is one of the most explicit erotic statements in scripture; it leaves little to allegorical evasion.

The woman interrupts at 7:9b with *holekh le-dodi le-meisharim dovev siftei yeshenim* (הוֹלֵךְ לְדוֹדִי לְמֵישָׁרִים דּוֹבֵב שִׂפְתֵי יְשֵׁנִים, "[the wine] goes down smoothly for my beloved, gliding over lips and teeth"). She completes his sentence — a literary marker of the lovers' increasing fusion of voice as the book progresses. Robert Alter notes this as a signature device of the Song.

The third movement (7:10-13) gives the third and final form of the possession refrain: *ani le-dodi ve-alai teshuqato* (אֲנִי לְדוֹדִי וְעָלַי תְּשׁוּקָתוֹ, "I am my beloved's, and his desire is for me," 7:10). This drops the reciprocal of 6:3 (*ani le-dodi ve-dodi li*) and instead foregrounds the man's *teshuqah* — *desire*. The word *teshuqah* (תְּשׁוּקָה) appears only three times in the Hebrew Bible: here, at Genesis 3:16 (*el ishekh teshuqatekh*, "your desire shall be for your husband"), and at Genesis 4:7 (sin's desire for Cain). Phyllis Trible's reading in *God and the Rhetoric of Sexuality* is decisive: the Song deliberately *reverses* Genesis 3:16. In the fall, the woman's desire is bent toward the man who rules over her; in the Song, the man's desire turns toward the woman. The Song is, on Trible's reading, a poetic restoration of Eden.

The closing scene (7:11-13) leaves the city for the countryside: vineyards, fields, villages, mandrakes, the doorway of their dwelling. The woman invites — *neṣe ha-sadeh nalinah ba-kfarim* (נֵצֵא הַשָּׂדֶה נָלִינָה בַּכְּפָרִים, "let us go out to the field, let us lodge in the villages") — and the active verbs are hers. *Sham etten et-dodai lakh* (שָׁם אֶתֵּן אֶת־דֹּדַי לָךְ, "there I will give you my loves," 7:12) places erotic gift in the woman's voice and agency.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

The Song's canonical status was secured against late first-century opposition by Rabbi Akiva. Mishnah *Yadayim* 3:5 records his ruling: *kol ha-ketuvim qodesh ve-Shir HaShirim qodesh qodashim* — "all the writings are holy, but the Song of Songs is the Holy of Holies." The same Mishnaic tradition warns: *kol ha-mena'ne'a qolo be-Shir ha-Shirim be-veit ha-mishteh ve-oseh oto ke-min zemer ein lo ḥeleq la-olam ha-ba* — "whoever trills the Song of Songs at a banquet and treats it as a kind of erotic ditty has no portion in the world to come" (Tosefta *Sanhedrin* 12:10). The very *vehemence* of the warning testifies to the chapter's erotic surface: the rabbis knew exactly what 7:1-9 sounds like, and they ruled that the Song must be read in liturgical and devotional context, not as drinking-song entertainment.

Akiva's allegorical reading was systematized in the *Targum Shir HaShirim* (probably seventh-eighth century AD), in *Shir HaShirim Rabbah*, and in Rashi's eleventh-century commentary. Rashi read 7:1-9 as the *praise of the synagogue* by God — the woman's body as the architectural and agricultural goodness of restored Israel. Maimonides cited the Song's longing-language as the highest model of the soul's love for God (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 10). The *Zohar* and later kabbalistic tradition read the lovers' embrace as the union of *Tiferet* and *Shekhinah*. Modern Jewish reception — Ariel and Chana Bloch's translation, Marcia Falk's translation, Robert Alter, Avivah Zornberg — has variously recovered the literal-erotic sense without abandoning the allegorical trajectory.

The Christian allegorical trajectory began with Hippolytus and reached systematic form in Origen's *Commentary on the Song of Songs*. Origen famously prefaced his commentary by warning that those *not yet free from the troublesome flesh* should not read the Song. Bernard of Clairvaux's eighty-six *Sermons on the Song* (1135-1153) treated each verse as the soul's union with Christ; Bernard never reached chapter 7 (he died at sermon 86 on chapter 3), but the William of St. Thierry continuation and the Cistercian tradition that followed read 7:1-9 as the soul's adornment by grace — feet as the soul's evangelical mission, breasts as nourishing doctrine, the palm-tree as the cross. The medieval *bridal mysticism* of Mechthild of Magdeburg, Hadewijch of Antwerp, Teresa of Avila, and especially John of the Cross's *Spiritual Canticle* (a Castilian poem and commentary built directly on the Song) used 7:1-9 in extraordinarily intimate ways.

The Reformers softened allegory. Luther read the Song politically. Calvin avoided it. The Puritan tradition (especially James Durham's *Clavis Cantici*, 1668) preserved Christ-soul allegory but tightened the erotic surface. The nineteenth century brought *typological* readings (Henry Mahan, Charles Spurgeon's occasional sermons) and *literal* recoveries (Franz Delitzsch's 1875 commentary).

The most striking modern Christian recovery is Pope John Paul II's *Theology of the Body*, 129 Wednesday catecheses delivered 1979-1984. John Paul II argued that the Song reveals the *spousal meaning of the body* — that human eros, including the body-praise of 7:1-9, is iconic of the trinitarian self-gift. Christopher West has popularized this reading. Modern critical Christian scholars — Phyllis Trible, Ellen Davis (*Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Songs*), Tremper Longman, Cheryl Exum, Renita Weems — have recovered the literal-poetic sense, with varying tolerance for allegorical use downstream.

NT echoes: Ephesians 5:22-33 (Christ and the Church as bridegroom and bride); Revelation 19:7-9 and 21:2 (the marriage supper of the Lamb); John 3:29 (John the Baptist as friend of the bridegroom). The reversal of Genesis 3:16's *teshuqah* in 7:10 is theologically loaded: if the Song is an unfallen Eden glimpse, then it stands behind every Pauline call to *love your wives as Christ loved the Church* (Eph 5:25).

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The principal tension in chapter 7 is the *explicitness* of the body-praise (7:1-9) and the canonical decision to keep it.

**Position one** — the dominant historical Jewish-Christian allegorical reading — treats the explicitness as a divine accommodation. The body-language is a teaching device: God uses the most intense human language to communicate the intensity of covenant-love. On this reading, the navel as wine-bowl, the breasts as twin-fawns, the palm-tree-climbing — all of these are *sacramental signs* whose true referents are the Church, the soul, Israel, the *Shekhinah*. Origen's warning against premature reading, the rabbinic ban on banquet-singing, and the medieval mystical tradition all converge on the view that this material is sacred precisely *because* it is so erotic — its danger and its power are the same.

**Position two** — modern critical and feminist scholarship — treats the explicitness as exactly what it appears to be: human love poetry, and the Song's place in canon as a profound theological affirmation of the goodness of the body. Phyllis Trible's reversal-of-Genesis-3:16 reading is the strongest version: the Song offers Eden restored, with mutual desire, female agency, and unembarrassed erotic celebration. Tremper Longman, Cheryl Exum, and Ellen Davis broadly converge on this.

**Position three** — the *both/and* reading — articulated most carefully by John Paul II's *Theology of the Body* and by Ellen Davis in different ways — argues the literal-erotic and the theological-covenantal are not rivals. Human love is sacramental — not in spite of being erotic, but precisely as erotic. The body is not a husk to be discarded for spiritual kernel; the body *is* the language. The Song is therefore *both* the most explicit love poetry in scripture *and* the deepest training-school for understanding God's covenant-love.

The Talmudic reading dilemma sharpens here. Tosefta *Sanhedrin* 12:10 banned banquet-singing of the Song, and *Shevuot* 35b cautions about misapplication. The Song's three adjuration refrains — *al ta'iru ve-al te'oreru et-ha-ahavah ad she-tehpatz* (אַל תָּעִירוּ וְאַל תְּעוֹרְרוּ אֶת־הָאַהֲבָה עַד שֶׁתֶּחְפָּץ, "do not stir up or awaken love until it pleases," 2:7, 3:5, 8:4) — were read by the rabbis as a hermeneutical instruction to readers as much as a refrain within the poem. Love, especially the love this Song uses to teach about God, must be approached at the right time, in the right state. This is itself a theological claim: that revelation through eros requires preparation in the reader.

Steelman position one: the canonical placement (Holy of Holies, *Megillah* read at Passover), the unbroken allegorical reception across Jewish and Christian classical traditions, and the rabbis' explicit warning about banquet-readings all suggest the text's intended canonical function is theological. The body-language is *vehicle*, not destination.

Steelman position two: to read 7:1-9 *only* allegorically is to flinch from the body, and the Song's place in canon is precisely a rebuke to that flinch. The reversal of Genesis 3:16's *teshuqah*, the woman's active erotic agency in 7:11-13, the unembarrassed celebration of mutual physical desire — all of this is *itself* the theology. The text does theological work *by* being erotic, not despite it.

**Where this leaves us**: chapter 7 forces every reader to choose how the most explicit body-praise in scripture should be received. The Christian reception has split between the medieval mystics who read with wide-open eyes (and disciplined hearts) and modern readers who often read with neither. The Jewish reception has held the eroticism and the covenant together with remarkable seriousness across two and a half millennia. The both/and reading — that human eros and divine love share grammar — is not a compromise but a recovery of what Akiva and Bernard knew: the body is not the problem; the body is the alphabet in which God spells *covenant*.

**Closing question**: when the rabbis warned against trilling the Song at a banquet, what kind of preparation were they asking for? And what would it mean, today, to read 7:1-9 with that preparation — not less explicitly, but more rightly?

## The Thread

The chapter's center is the man's foot-to-head body-praise (7:1-9) — the most explicit *waṣf* in scripture — followed by the woman's reversal of Genesis 3:16 (7:10) and her active invitation into the spring countryside (7:11-13). The thread is the restoration of unfallen mutuality: the man overwhelmed by the woman he praises, the woman desired and desiring, the body received and given. The Song does not blush.
