# 2 Samuel 22: The King Sings the Storm

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter is a **Davidic victory hymn** preserved in the Samuel appendix (chapters 21-24) and reproduced almost verbatim as **Psalm 18** in the Psalter. The double preservation is itself the chapter's most important historical-textual fact: the song is one of only a handful of texts in the Hebrew Bible that survives in two independent canonical versions. The differences between 2 Samuel 22 and Psalm 18 — there are roughly 75 minor variants, a handful substantive — are a foundational case study in biblical textual criticism. **Frank Moore Cross** and **David Noel Freedman** in their classic 1953 article "A Royal Song of Thanksgiving" treated the chapter as one of the oldest surviving Hebrew poems and used the textual variants between Samuel and Psalm 18 to reconstruct what they argued was an originally archaic Hebrew composition that the two transmitting texts had each updated differently.

The dating debate is consequential. **Cross-Freedman** dated the original composition to the **early monarchic period**, possibly to David's own reign (10th c. BCE), based on archaic morphological features: the **3rd masculine singular suffix** *-mo* (Hebrew מוֹ instead of standard *-am*), the use of the *yaqtul preterite* (an old Semitic perfective verb form attested in Ugaritic and early Hebrew but largely lost in classical Hebrew), the rare divine epithets, and the concentrated use of theophany imagery that finds its closest parallels in Ugaritic poetry and the earliest Hebrew poems (Exodus 15, Judges 5, Deuteronomy 33, Habakkuk 3). Cross-Freedman placed the song with these other "archaic Hebrew poetry" texts as evidence for a robust early Israelite poetic tradition. The dating has been challenged. Some scholars (David Robertson in *Linguistic Evidence in Dating Early Hebrew Poetry*, 1972) date the archaic features more conservatively to the 11th-10th c. BCE; others (Peter Machinist) date them more loosely; minimalist readings (Philip Davies) treat the archaisms as deliberate archaizing rather than authentic preservation. The mainstream position, with adjustments, holds that the song preserves authentically early Hebrew poetic language even if the final form has been updated by transmission.

The song is structured as a **royal thanksgiving** in two main movements. The first movement (vv. 2-20) is a personal cry-and-rescue narrative — distress, calling on YHWH, theophany, divine intervention, deliverance. The second movement (vv. 21-51) is a royal-victory narrative — divine reward of the king's righteousness, military empowerment, victory over enemies, foreign submission, the closing dynastic blessing. The form has clear ANE parallels in the **royal thanksgiving genre**: Mesopotamian royal hymns (especially Akkadian *šigû*-prayers and Sumerian royal liturgies), Egyptian royal victory stelae (Merneptah Stele, Ramesses II inscriptions), and the Ugaritic Baal Cycle's victory hymns. The form is distinctively ANE; the content is distinctively Israelite, recasting the divine warrior tradition around YHWH.

The **theophany imagery** at vv. 8-16 is the chapter's most visually striking sequence. The earth reels and rocks; the foundations of the heavens tremble; smoke from YHWH's nostrils; devouring fire from his mouth; YHWH bows the heavens and descends; thick darkness under his feet; he rides on a cherub and flies; the wings of the wind; thunder from heaven; arrows and lightning; the channels of the sea exposed; the foundations of the world laid bare. The sequence has direct parallels in the **Ugaritic Baal Cycle** (KTU 1.4, 1.5), where Baal as the storm god rides on cloud-chariots, hurls thunder and lightning, and parts waters. **Patrick Miller** in *The Divine Warrior in Early Israel* and **Frank Cross** in *Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic* developed the comparison: Israelite poetry inherits the Canaanite storm-god vocabulary and rewrites it around YHWH. The theophany at 2 Samuel 22:8-16 is the most extended single example. The **cherub** imagery at v. 11 — the divine warrior riding the cherub-throne — connects to the ark imagery (1 Samuel 4:4, 2 Samuel 6:2 — YHWH "enthroned on the cherubim") and to the broader ANE iconographic tradition of winged-throne deities (Ivory cherub-throne plaques from Megiddo, Samaria, and Arslan Tash document the visual vocabulary).

**Sheol** at v. 6 — "the cords of Sheol entangled me; the snares of death confronted me" — is the standard Hebrew underworld term, the realm of the dead beneath the earth. The vocabulary belongs to the broader ANE underworld imagery (Akkadian *erṣetu*, Ugaritic *arṣ*), and the cords/snares image presents death as a hunter setting traps. **Theodore Lewis** in *Cults of the Dead in Ancient Israel and Ugarit* and **Jon Levenson** in *Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel* both develop the chapter's underworld vocabulary in ANE context.

The chapter's military vocabulary is technically precise. ***Tsur*** (צוּר), "rock," is the song's signature divine epithet — repeated at vv. 2, 3, 32, 47. The vocabulary connects to the divine-warrior tradition (Deuteronomy 32:4, 32:18, 32:30) and to the broader tradition of YHWH as defensive stronghold. ***Misgav*** (מִשְׂגָּב), "high place, refuge," at v. 3 — the same word that names the fortress of Jerusalem in some Psalms. ***Geren-yishi*** (קֶרֶן-יִשְׁעִי), "horn of my salvation," at v. 3 — bull-horn imagery, the symbol of military strength in ANE iconography (Mesopotamian and Egyptian royal iconography both use bull horns as symbols of kingly power; cf. Hannah's song 1 Samuel 2:1, 2:10). ***Mashiach*** (מָשִׁיחַ), "anointed one," at v. 51 — the chapter's closing word, the messianic vocabulary that the prophets and the New Testament will pick up.

A few additional Hebrew terms transform the chapter. ***Qeshet nechushah*** (קֶשֶׁת נְחוּשָׁה), "bow of bronze," at v. 35 — military prowess so great that the king can bend a bronze bow (an exaggerated image; bronze is too rigid for an actual bow, the figure is rhetorical). ***Galim*** (גַּלִּים), "waves," at v. 5 — death imagined as overwhelming waters, the chaoskampf imagery shared with Mesopotamian Tiamat traditions and Ugaritic Yam-conflicts. ***Ed*** (עֵד) and related vocabulary of testimony are present in vv. 1, 21, 25 — the song is the king's testimony to YHWH's faithfulness. ***Berit-olam*** (בְּרִית-עוֹלָם), "everlasting covenant," is implicit in the closing v. 51 — David and his offspring forever, the Davidic covenant theology of 2 Samuel 7 returning here in poetic form.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter's genre is **royal thanksgiving hymn** in the style of the Hebrew Psalter. Its placement in the Samuel appendix (chs 21-24) is structurally significant. **Brevard Childs**, **Walter Brueggemann**, **J. Cheryl Exum**, and **Klaus Koch** have all argued that the appendix is **chiastically arranged**: A-Saulide bloodguilt and Rizpah's vigil (21:1-14), B-list of giant-killings (21:15-22), C-David's Song (22), C'-David's last words (23:1-7), B'-list of mighty men (23:8-39), A'-census plague and the threshing-floor altar (24). The two songs at the structural center (chapter 22 and 23:1-7) are the appendix's poetic core, framed by the heroic catalogues, framed in turn by the two narratives of bloodguilt and divine wrath. The chiastic placement gives the songs theological centrality.

The chapter is also bracketed with **Hannah's Song** at the start of 1 Samuel (1 Sam 2:1-10). The two songs form an **inclusio** around the entire Samuel narrative: a barren woman's victory hymn opens; a victorious king's victory hymn closes. Both are royal-eschatological in their final lines (Hannah's "his king, his anointed" at 1 Sam 2:10; David's "to David and his offspring forever" at 22:51). Both employ the divine-warrior tradition; both invert high and low (Hannah's "the bows of the mighty are broken, but the feeble bind on strength"; David's "you save a humble people, but your eyes are on the haughty to bring them down"). The structural parallel is widely recognized (Brueggemann, Polzin, Alter, Fokkelman). The books of Samuel are bracketed by two songs that name YHWH as the one who reverses social and political fortunes. The entire history between them — Saul's rise and fall, David's rise and complications — is read between the brackets.

The textual variants between **2 Samuel 22 and Psalm 18** are the chapter's most discussed literary problem. Roughly 75 differences exist; most are minor (orthography, suffix forms, divine epithets), but some are substantive. Examples: 22:11 has "He was seen on the wings of the wind" while Ps 18:10 has "He flew swiftly on the wings of the wind." 22:33 has "This God is my strong refuge" while Ps 18:32 has "The God who girded me with strength." 22:8 reads "the foundations of the heavens trembled" while Ps 18:7 reads "the foundations of the mountains trembled." Most critical scholars (McCarter, A.A. Anderson, Cross-Freedman) treat the two versions as developments from a common original. Some scholars argue Psalm 18 preserves the older form; others argue Samuel does. **Cross-Freedman** in their classic study reconstructed a hypothetical archaic original behind both. **4QSam-a** preserves fragments of 2 Samuel 22 with some variants; **11QPs-a** preserves Psalm 18 in the Qumran Psalter with fewer variants.

The chapter's literary craft is concentrated in three places. First, the **theophany sequence** (vv. 8-16) is one of the most concentrated single passages of theophany imagery in the HB, comparable only to Habakkuk 3 and parts of Psalms 29 and 68. Second, the **measure-for-measure section** (vv. 26-27) — "with the merciful you show yourself merciful, with the blameless man you show yourself blameless, with the purified you deal purely, and with the crooked (*ikkesh*, עִקֵּשׁ) you make yourself seem tortuous (*tappattal*, תִּתַּפָּל)" — is a striking theological formula about divine mirroring. The Hebrew wordplay between *ikkesh* (crooked, twisted) and *tappattal* (tortuous, devious) is famously difficult to translate; the verb *patal* shares a root with the standard word for wrestling (*niphtaltí*, "I wrestled," is what Rachel says at Gen 30:8). The theological implication is that YHWH meets each soul in its own posture — straight to the straight, twisted to the twisted. Third, the **closing dynastic blessing** at v. 51 ("great salvation he brings to his king, and shows *hesed* to his anointed, to David and his offspring forever") is the song's final line, and it knits the song into the Davidic covenant of 2 Samuel 7. The song ends as the Davidic theology of the canonical Samuel ends.

The narrator's framing at v. 1 — "**David spoke to YHWH the words of this song** on the day when YHWH had delivered him from the hand of all his enemies and from the hand of Saul" — is interpretively important. The framing locates the song as a Davidic composition (the canonical attribution of the Psalms to David has its anchor here and at Psalm 18's superscription) and dates it to a moment of comprehensive deliverance ("from all his enemies and from the hand of Saul"). The dating is interpretive: it places the song after the Saulide period but before the moral collapse of chapters 11-12 — a kind of canonical "apex" Davidic moment when the king can sing of righteousness and clean hands without irony. The placement in the appendix (after the Court History's narrative of moral failure) means the canonical reader hears the song's claims to clean hands knowing what came after. The juxtaposition is itself the canonical theological statement.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, 2 Samuel 22 is one of the most theologically dense single passages in the historical books. As the appendix's structural core (with 23:1-7), it provides the theological frame for reading the Davidic narrative. As the bracket with Hannah's Song (1 Sam 2:1-10), it provides the structural envelope for the entire Samuel narrative. As the parallel of Psalm 18, it links the historical books to the Psalter's Davidic tradition. As the source of the closing messianic vocabulary ("his king, his anointed, David and his offspring forever"), it underwrites the messianic theology that runs through the prophets and into the New Testament.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Josephus in *Antiquities* 7.305-307 retells the chapter in summary, treating it as evidence of David's piety. The Talmud (Sotah 10b, Megillah 10b, Sanhedrin 95b-96a) discusses the chapter at length, treating it as the foundational Davidic hymn and using it for liturgical and homiletical purposes. The rabbinic tradition treats David's claim to "righteousness" and "clean hands" (vv. 21-25) as referring to the period before his moral failure (or as eschatological righteousness of the messianic Davidic line). **Rashi** (11th c.) develops the rabbinic readings; he glosses the difficult theophany vocabulary and engages the Samuel/Psalm 18 variants. **Radak** (David Kimhi, 12th c.) provides the most extensive Jewish commentary, comparing line-by-line with Psalm 18 and treating the variants as interpretive opportunities. **Ralbag** (Gersonides) extracts moral *to'aliyot*. **Abarbanel** (15th c.) treats the song as a model royal thanksgiving and discusses its political-theological import. **Yair Zakovitch** in modern scholarship attends to the song's intertextual position. **Avivah Zornberg** has not written extensively on this chapter; **Tikva Frymer-Kensky** notes the song's masculinist royal-warrior frame and the absence of women's voices.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic Christianity read the chapter (and Psalm 18) extensively. **Origen's** commentary on Psalms (preserved in fragments) treats the chapter's theophany as figure of Christ's incarnation and resurrection. **Augustine's** *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 18 develops the chapter at length — David is figure of Christ; the deliverance is the Resurrection; the messianic line is the Church. Augustine's Christological reading shaped the medieval tradition. **Jerome's** translations and commentary engage the textual variants. **Ambrose's** *Enarratio in Psalmum 17* (Vulgate numbering for Psalm 18) develops the imperial-theological reading: the divinely-empowered king as figure of the Christ-empowered emperor (Ambrose's polemics with Theodosius gave the reading political edge). **Chrysostom's** homilies treat the chapter as a model of Christian thanksgiving. **Gregory the Great** uses the theophany imagery in his *Moralia*. **Aquinas** treats the chapter in his Old Testament commentary work and uses verses extensively in the *Summa* discussion of providence. The medieval tradition (Bernard of Clairvaux, mystical writers) developed the chapter's intimate vocabulary — "you are my lamp, O LORD" (v. 29) — for contemplative prayer.

The Reformation shifted register. **Luther's** lectures on the Psalms develop chapter-length engagements with Psalm 18 and 2 Samuel 22 — Luther reads the song christologically with new directness, treating David as the Old Testament's clearest type of Christ. **Calvin's** *Commentary on the Psalms* (Psalm 18) gives the chapter substantial attention; Calvin reads it as a model of how the believer relates to providence under affliction. The post-Reformation Pietist and Puritan tradition (Matthew Henry, Jonathan Edwards) develops the chapter at devotional length. **Charles Spurgeon's** *Treasury of David* on Psalm 18 is a 19th-century summa of the Christian devotional reading.

The critical period (Wellhausen, Gunkel, Mowinckel) treated the chapter through form-critical and historical-critical lenses. **Hermann Gunkel** identified the song as a royal thanksgiving hymn (*Königliches Danklied*). **Sigmund Mowinckel** placed it within his theory of cultic enthronement festival. **Frank Cross** and **David Noel Freedman**'s foundational 1953 article "A Royal Song of Thanksgiving" placed the chapter within the archaic Hebrew poetry corpus and dated the original composition to the early monarchic period. **Patrick Miller** in *The Divine Warrior in Early Israel* developed the chapter as one of the central Israelite divine-warrior texts. **Mitchell Dahood**'s Anchor Bible Psalms commentary develops the Ugaritic philology. **McCarter's** Anchor Bible commentary on 2 Samuel and **Hans Joachim Kraus's** commentary on the Psalms both develop the chapter at length. **Walter Brueggemann's *The Message of the Psalms*** reads the chapter as paradigm of royal thanksgiving. The contemporary reading attends to: the political-theological function of the chapter as imperial ideology (Brueggemann's later work, Norman Gottwald), the literary-theological function as Samuel's bracketing inclusio (Polzin, Brueggemann, Fokkelman), the textual-critical engagement of the Samuel/Psalm 18 variants (Cross, Tov), and the reception-historical study of the chapter's Christian and Jewish liturgical afterlife.

**Where the traditions converge and diverge.** Both traditions read the chapter as a Davidic theological pinnacle and use it liturgically. They diverge on the messianic interpretation of the closing "to David and his offspring forever" (v. 51). The Christian tradition reads the verse christologically — fulfilled in Jesus as Son of David. The Jewish tradition reads it as awaiting messianic fulfillment in a future Davidic descendant. They converge on the chapter's literary-theological function as inclusio with Hannah's Song. They diverge on the textual-critical question of the Samuel/Psalm 18 variants — Jewish rabbinic and medieval tradition has often preferred the MT readings of both as internally valid; Christian critical scholarship has often used the variants comparatively to reconstruct hypothetical originals.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author of the song believe God was doing? The theology is one of **divine warrior providence** — YHWH as the storm god who descends in fire and earthquake to rescue the king, who rewards righteousness with deliverance, who empowers the king to defeat enemies, who establishes the dynasty forever. The theology is operative throughout the song: every verse names YHWH as agent, and the song's structural movement is from distress (vv. 5-7) to theophany (vv. 8-16) to deliverance (vv. 17-20) to reward (vv. 21-25) to victory (vv. 35-46) to dynasty (v. 51).

**Tension One: David's claim to "righteousness and clean hands" (vv. 21-25) — sincere self-knowledge or canonical irony?** David's central claim in the middle of the song is that YHWH rewarded him "according to my righteousness; according to the cleanness of my hands he repaid me. For I have kept the ways of the LORD and have not wickedly departed from my God. For all his rules were before me, and from his statutes I did not turn aside. I was blameless before him, and I kept myself from guilt." The claim is striking. The reader of the canonical Samuel knows about Bathsheba, Uriah, Joab's murders that David benefited from, the family disasters of chapters 13-19. The song's claim to clean hands is hard to read straight after the Court History.

The traditional reading (rabbinic, patristic, Reformation) generally reads the claim as referring to David's righteousness *before* the Bathsheba episode — the song dates from the early period of David's reign when his hands were genuinely clean (or relatively so). The song's narrative framing at v. 1 ("the day YHWH delivered him from all his enemies and from the hand of Saul") supports this reading: the song belongs to the deliverance from the Saulide persecution, not to the post-Bathsheba period.

The Christian christological reading (Augustine, Aquinas) treats the claim as fulfilled not in the historical David (who was morally compromised) but in Christ, the messianic Son of David whose hands are truly clean. The claim is read as prophetic-typological rather than historically descriptive of the historical David.

The contemporary critical reading (Brueggemann, Polzin) reads the claim's juxtaposition with the Court History as **canonical irony**. The editor who placed the song in the appendix (after the Court History's narrative of moral failure) created a deliberate juxtaposition: the king sings of clean hands while the canonical reader knows what hands actually did. The irony is not necessarily unintentional; it may be the editor's theological move. The Davidic covenant (v. 51 "his king, his anointed, David and his offspring forever") holds despite the moral failures the canonical reader has just read about. The song's claim to clean hands and the dynastic blessing both stand because the covenant is unconditional in some respect.

A mediating position holds that the song belongs to a moment in David's life when its claims were genuinely true (or honestly experienced as true), and that the editor's placement creates a productive tension that the canonical reader is meant to hold rather than resolve. The historical David's clean hands and the Court History's complicated David are both real; both are preserved canonically; the tension is theologically generative.

The position to steelman is the canonical-irony reading. It is sometimes resisted as too clever, but it takes seriously that the editor of the appendix knew the Court History (and may have composed the appendix precisely as theological frame for reading the Court History). The song's claim to clean hands was not preserved despite what the reader knows; it was preserved as part of how the editor wanted the reader to hold what they know. The Davidic theology that survives the moral failures requires both the claim and the failure to be visible at once.

**Where this leaves us:** David's claim to clean hands is the chapter's most theologically loaded line. Whether one reads it as historical, christological, or canonically ironic, the chapter holds the claim alongside the Davidic covenant in a way that requires the reader to think about how covenant relates to moral failure.

**Tension Two: The divine-warrior theophany (vv. 8-16) — descriptive of YHWH's character or theological projection of royal ideology?** The chapter's theophany is one of the HB's most extended single sequences of divine-warrior imagery. YHWH descends in earthquake, fire, smoke, thunder, lightning, cherub-flight; he routes enemies; the foundations of the world are laid bare.

The traditional reading (rabbinic, patristic, Reformation) takes the theophany as descriptive of YHWH's actual character — a poetic intensification of YHWH's sovereign power and care for the king. The vocabulary is hymnic; the reality is YHWH's own.

The contemporary critical reading (Patrick Miller, Cross, Brueggemann's later work) holds the theophany within the broader divine-warrior tradition that Israel inherited from Canaanite religion and rewrote around YHWH. The vocabulary is real religious heritage, but it is also ideologically loaded: storm-god theology underwrites the king's military and political power. The same imagery that names YHWH's care for David also legitimates David's military campaigns. The theology is not separable from the politics.

The contemporary feminist and postcolonial readings (Brueggemann, Musa Dube) press further: the theophany imagery underwrites a violent royal ideology in which YHWH's power is on the king's side and against the enemies whom the king's army crushes ("I beat them fine as the dust of the earth; I crushed them and stamped them down like the mire of the streets" — v. 43). The hymn that praises YHWH for clean-handed righteousness also celebrates the violent crushing of those who rose against the king. The two sides of the theology are not separate.

The position to steelman is the contemporary critique. It is sometimes resisted because it treats the inherited theological vocabulary with critical distance. But the critique is asking the right question: the divine-warrior theology is the chapter's central engine, and that theology has done real work historically — from David's military expansion to medieval crusader theology to early modern colonial expansion. To celebrate the theology without recognizing its political afterlife is to receive only half the chapter.

**Where this leaves us:** The divine-warrior theophany is theologically dense and politically loaded simultaneously. The chapter offers no separation of the two. To read the song requires holding the praise of YHWH's care alongside the recognition of how that care has been mapped onto royal violence across centuries.

The unresolved question this chapter leaves us with: When the song that names YHWH's most intimate care for the king ("you are my lamp, O LORD; my God lightens my darkness") also names the king's most violent crushing of enemies as YHWH's gift, what does it mean to receive the intimacy without absorbing the violence — and what would it mean to refuse the violence without losing the intimacy?

## The Thread

**1 Samuel 2:1-10.** Hannah's Song opens the books of Samuel; David's Song closes them (chronologically, before the appendix's narrative material). The two hymns form an inclusio: a barren woman's victory hymn and a victorious king's victory hymn, both naming YHWH as the one who reverses social fortunes, both ending with "his king, his anointed" (Hannah) and "to David and his offspring forever" (David). When you reread Hannah's Song, hear it as the opening bracket of which 2 Samuel 22 is the closing bracket.

**Psalm 18.** The same song appears in the Psalter as Psalm 18 with roughly 75 minor variants. The double preservation is foundational for biblical textual criticism. When you read Psalm 18, compare it line-by-line with 2 Samuel 22 to feel the mechanics of textual transmission and the productive variability of the Hebrew Scriptures.

**Exodus 15, Judges 5, Habakkuk 3, Psalm 29, Psalm 68.** The other major divine-warrior hymns of the Hebrew Bible — the Song of the Sea, the Song of Deborah, Habakkuk's prayer, and the great storm psalms. Together with 2 Samuel 22, they form a coherent corpus of Israelite divine-warrior poetry, much of it dated by Cross-Freedman to the early monarchic period or earlier. When you read any of these, hear the others as siblings in a single poetic-theological tradition.

**Hebrews 1:5 and 2:13.** The Letter to the Hebrews quotes 2 Samuel 7 ("I will be his father and he shall be my son") and uses the broader Davidic-covenant theology that the closing verse of 2 Samuel 22 (v. 51) underwrites. The "to David and his offspring forever" of v. 51 is the Hebrew Bible's anchor for the New Testament's claim that Jesus as Son of David is the messianic heir of an everlasting covenant. When you read Hebrews's Christological argument from the Old Testament, the Davidic dynastic blessing of 2 Samuel 22:51 is one of its load-bearing texts.
