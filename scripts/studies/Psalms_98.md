# Psalm 98 — When the Sea Roars and the Rivers Clap

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 98 stands without superscription save for the bare Hebrew designation *mizmor* (מִזְמוֹר, "a psalm") — a one-word heading that plants the text in liturgical use without anchoring it to David, Asaph, or the sons of Korah. The anonymity is canonical.

Hermann Gunkel, in his foundational *Einleitung in die Psalmen* (1933), classified Psalm 98 as an enthronement hymn (*Thronbesteigungslied*), grouping it with Psalms 47, 93, 95–99 as the *Yahweh-malak* cluster — the "the LORD reigns" psalms that proclaim divine kingship. Sigmund Mowinckel, in *Psalmenstudien* II (1922) and later in *The Psalms in Israel's Worship* (1962), pressed the genre into a full cultic hypothesis: an annual Yahweh-enthronement festival (*Yahweh-Königsfest*) loosely modeled on the Babylonian *akitu*, at which these psalms were sung. Few today accept the full festival reconstruction, but the form-critical observation — that Psalms 93–99 share a hymnic shape oriented around Yahweh's reign — remains foundational. Hans-Joachim Kraus (*Psalmen*, 1978) trims Mowinckel's festival to a "royal Zion theology"; Frank-Lothar Hossfeld and Erich Zenger (*Psalmen 51–100*, 2000) read the cluster as a post-exilic theological program; John Goldingay (*Psalms*, vol. 3, 2008) treats Psalm 98 specifically as post-exilic and dependent on Second Isaiah.

The Isaianic dependence is the strongest clue to dating. *Zero'a qodsho* (זְרוֹעַ קָדְשׁוֹ, "his holy arm," v. 1) echoes Isaiah 52:10 nearly verbatim: "the LORD has bared his holy arm before the eyes of all the nations, and all the ends of the earth shall see the salvation of our God" — language Psalm 98:3 then mirrors. The new-song trope, the universal scope, the salvation-revealed-to-the-nations theme all belong to the theological vocabulary of Second Isaiah. Goldingay, Hossfeld–Zenger, and Patrick Miller (*Interpreting the Psalms*, 1986) accordingly date the psalm to the post-exilic period, late sixth to mid-fifth century BCE.

The Hebrew terms reward attention. *Shir chadash* (שִׁיר חָדָשׁ, "new song," v. 1) is a charged liturgical idiom appearing across Psalms 33, 40, 96, 144, 149 and reaching eschatological climax in Isaiah 42:10 and Revelation 5:9, 14:3. The "newness" is qualitative — a song commensurate with fresh salvific deeds. *Yam u-melo'o* (יָם וּמְלֹאוֹ, "the sea and its fullness," v. 7) and *neharot yimcha'u-kaf* (נְהָרוֹת יִמְחֲאוּ־כָף, "let the rivers clap their hands," v. 8) personify creation in a way that has deep ANE roots: in Canaanite myth the sea is Baal's chaos-rival; here the sea is jubilant subject. *Lifne ha-Melekh Yahweh* (לִפְנֵי הַמֶּלֶךְ יְהוָה, "before the King, the LORD," v. 6) deploys the royal title explicitly.

The placement matters. Psalm 98 sits in Book IV (Psalms 90–106), the section Gerald Wilson (*The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter*, 1985) identified as the redactional response to the failure of the Davidic monarchy lamented at the close of Book III in Psalm 89. Where Psalm 89 cries, "How long, O LORD?" Book IV answers: *Yahweh malakh*, the LORD reigns. Psalm 98 is a central voice in that answer. Textually, the MT is stable and the LXX renders faithfully; 4QPs-b and 4QPs-k preserve fragments without significant variants.

## Lens 2: The Literature in Front of Us

Psalm 98 unfolds in three strophes, each with a distinct mode of address.

- **Strophe 1 (vv. 1–3): Yahweh's saving deeds revealed to the nations.** Opens with the imperative *shiru* ("sing"), then turns declarative. The salvation is grounded in God's *zero'a qodsho* and is "made known" (*hodi'a*) and "revealed" (*gilah*) before the nations. Culminates in the covenantal grounding (*chesed* and *emunah*, recalling Exodus 34:6).
- **Strophe 2 (vv. 4–6): Universal call to praise with instruments.** Six imperatives stack: *hari'u, pitzchu, rannenu, zammeru, zammeru, hari'u*. The instrumentation is detailed: lyre (*kinnor*), trumpets (*chatzotzrot*), and the horn (*shofar*). Liturgical maximalism — every instrument before the King.
- **Strophe 3 (vv. 7–9): Cosmic creation joins the praise.** Imperatives shift from human worshipers to non-human creation: *yir'am ha-yam, neharot yimcha'u-kaf, harim yerannenu*. The strophe and the psalm end with the eschatological *ki ba* (כִּי בָא, "for he comes") to judge the earth with righteousness and equity.

Robert Lowth's eighteenth-century categories — synonymous, antithetic, synthetic parallelism — illuminate the texture, but James Kugel's "A, what's more B" model better captures the forward-driving quality. "His right hand AND his holy arm" is cumulative, not redundant. The *new-song* trope functions as a hinge: a *shir chadash* signals that something new has happened in salvation history, requiring fresh liturgical response. Hossfeld–Zenger track the trope as an inner-Psalter intertext linking the *Yahweh-malak* cluster to Second Isaiah's eschatological imagination.

The personification of rivers, hills, and sea is among the boldest in the Psalter. William P. Brown (*Seeing the Psalms*, 2002) reads it not as decorative metaphor but as theological claim: creation itself is a worshiping subject. The structural parallel with Psalm 96 is unmistakable — both open with *shiru la-Yahweh shir chadash*, both summon the nations, both end with the eschatological coming to judge the earth. Hossfeld–Zenger argue the two psalms function as bookends within the *Yahweh-malak* cluster.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story — Jewish and Christian Trajectories

Within Book IV's redactional logic, Psalm 98 occupies a central place. Wilson's canonical-shape thesis gives it weight: where Psalm 89:46 cries, "How long, O LORD?", Psalm 98 answers — Yahweh has not hidden; his right hand has worked deliverance in the sight of the nations. The post-exilic confession is that the failure of the human Davidic monarchy does not mean the failure of Yahweh's reign.

In the Jewish trajectory, *Midrash Tehillim* (Buber edition) reads Psalm 98 messianically — the new song is what will be sung at the final redemption. Rashi glosses *zero'a qodsho* with reference to future redemption and reads *ki ba* as messianic-age judgment. Ibn Ezra emphasizes the link to Isaiah 52; Radak (David Kimhi) develops the universal-missional reading. Modern Jewish reception is rich: Martin Buber's translation work (*Die Schrift*, with Rosenzweig) handles the psalm with attention to hymnic immediacy; Abraham Joshua Heschel (*Man's Quest for God*, 1954) cites vv. 7–9 as paradigmatic of biblical wonder; Jon Levenson (*Sinai and Zion*, 1985) reads the enthronement cluster as a theology of cosmic order against chaos; Nahum Sarna (*On the Book of Psalms*, 1993) emphasizes the Isaianic dependence. Liturgically, Psalm 98 is recited in *Kabbalat Shabbat* in many rites — the inauguration of Sabbath rest greeted with the proclamation of Yahweh's universal kingship.

Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 97 (LXX = MT 98) reads the psalm Christologically: the *new song* is the gospel; the salvation made known to the nations is the church's mission; the coming to judge is the second coming of Christ. Cassiodorus follows. Aquinas, in his *Postilla super Psalmos*, treats v. 9 as a key text for the doctrine of *iudicium futurum*.

The Reformation gave Psalm 98 a particular afterlife. Luther's psalm paraphrases set the early tone for Reformed metrical psalmody, and Calvin's 1557 commentary reads the psalm as a hymn of grateful covenantal acknowledgment. The Geneva Psalter, the Scottish Psalter (1650), and the Bay Psalm Book (1640) gave it prominent place. But the decisive moment for English-speaking reception came in 1719, when Isaac Watts published *The Psalms of David Imitated in the Language of the New Testament* and produced the metrical paraphrase of Psalm 98:4–9 that became "Joy to the World." Crucially, Watts wrote the hymn not as a Christmas hymn but as an Advent / second-coming hymn: the "Lord is come" refers to Christ's eschatological return, consistent with Watts's reading of v. 9. The hymn's transformation into a Christmas song is a nineteenth-century American development, accelerated when Lowell Mason adapted what became the tune *Antioch* (probably from Handel's *Messiah*) in 1839. By the late nineteenth century, "Joy to the World" had become firmly attached to Christmas in American Protestant practice — a placement that obscures Watts's eschatological intent and the psalm's own clear orientation toward *coming-to-judge*.

Modern critical scholarship is substantial. Westermann (*Praise and Lament in the Psalms*, 1981) classifies Psalm 98 as a "descriptive psalm of praise" with eschatological orientation. Hossfeld–Zenger emphasize the post-exilic redactional placement; Goldingay treats it as a "summary statement of post-exilic faith in Yahweh's universal kingship"; Walter Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*, 1984) places it among "psalms of new orientation"; Patrick Miller emphasizes its catechetical force; William P. Brown explores the cosmic personification as a sustained ecological theology; Beth Tanner (in deClaissé-Walford, Jacobson, and Tanner, *The Book of Psalms*, NICOT, 2014) treats it as the doxological response to the *Yahweh-malak* claim. NT use is allusive: no formal citation, but the *new song* of Revelation 5:9 and 14:3 extends the Psalter's *shir chadash* tradition into apocalyptic vision.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — Where the Tension Lives

The author's vision is unmistakable. Yahweh has acted decisively in history. His saving work is not hidden but publicly visible to the nations. Creation itself responds with cosmic praise to the King who comes to set the world right with righteousness and equity. The psalm holds together salvation-history (vv. 1–3), liturgical celebration (vv. 4–6), and eschatological-cosmic anticipation (vv. 7–9) in a single arc.

**Tension 1: The eschatological coming and the Christmas use.** Verse 9 — *ki ba lishpot ha-aretz* ("for he comes to judge the earth") — is the structural climax. Watts heard this as the second coming and wrote "Joy to the World" as an Advent hymn. The American liturgical reception attached the hymn to Christmas, making the most-sung paraphrase of Psalm 98 in English a celebration of the first coming rather than the second.

The first position, articulated by careful liturgical historians and by Watts himself, insists on the eschatological reading. The psalm is unambiguous: the coming is to judge; the *new song* responds to a salvation revealed to the nations and culminating in cosmic justice. To read the psalm Christmas-ward flattens its forward-leaning theology into a celebration of past nativity. The second position, articulated implicitly by centuries of liturgical practice and made explicit in Brevard Childs's canonical method (*Biblical Theology of the Old and New Testaments*, 1992), allows a *both/and*. The verse points beyond any single fulfillment; the first and second comings are theologically continuous moments in the one coming of Yahweh-in-Christ. N.T. Wright (*Surprised by Hope*, 2008) presses this: biblical eschatology is not "going to heaven when we die" but Christ's return to set the world right, and Psalm 98 is one of the great OT articulations of that hope. A third position, advanced by Brueggemann and by Reformed liturgical theologians critical of consumerist Christmas, argues the displacement is the problem: the psalm proclaims judgment; the hymn now proclaims sentimental joy.

Where this leaves us: Psalm 98 itself does not specify a single chronological referent for *ki ba*. The verse is participial in force — Yahweh is the one who comes, who is coming, who will come. But the *direction* is unambiguous: forward, toward judgment-as-setting-right, not backward toward a remembered moment. Whatever else "Joy to the World" does, the psalm itself sings forward.

**Tension 2: Cosmic creation as worshiping subject.** Verses 7–9 personify the sea, rivers, and hills as praise-makers. What is this — decorative metaphor, theological claim, or cosmological statement?

The first position, advanced by classical Reformed theology and articulated in Calvin's commentary, reads the personification as poetic anthropomorphism. The rivers do not literally clap; the hills do not literally sing. The poet calls humans to a praise commensurate with the cosmic scale of Yahweh's saving work. The second position, articulated by William P. Brown (*The Seven Pillars of Creation*, 2010), Pope Francis's encyclical *Laudato Si'* (2015), Catherine Keller, and Norman Wirzba's agrarian theology (*The Paradise of God*, 2003), insists the personification carries a cosmological claim. Creation is participant, not backdrop. *Laudato Si'* cites Psalm 98 (with Psalm 148) as foundational for an integral ecology in which praise is not anthropocentric but cosmic. Eastern Orthodox theology has held this for centuries — the doctrine of cosmic transfiguration teaches that creation itself is destined for glorification. A third position, mediating, is articulated by Reformed common-grace theology (Abraham Kuyper) and by recent biblical-theological work on creation (Richard Bauckham, *Bible and Ecology*, 2010): creation has its own God-given doxological vocation, but humans bear a particular responsibility within creation as those made in the divine image.

Where this leaves us: the personification in vv. 7–9 is doing more than ornament. Whether read as metaphor, cosmological claim, or something between, it commits the psalm — and any community that prays it — to a vision in which praise is not a uniquely human enterprise.

Closing question: If the rivers really do clap their hands and the hills really do sing — even in some real sense beyond figure — then what does it mean for human worship to fail to join them? And conversely: what does it mean for human worship to drown them out?

## The Thread

- **Isaiah 52:10** — "The LORD has bared his holy arm before the eyes of all the nations, and all the ends of the earth shall see the salvation of our God." The closest verbal parallel and almost certainly the literary source for Psalm 98:1–3. Reading the two together reveals the psalm as the liturgical-doxological response to Second Isaiah's announcement.
- **Psalm 96** — Sister enthronement psalm, sharing the *new-song* opening, the universal call to the nations, and the eschatological *for he comes to judge the earth* climax. Read in parallel, the two illuminate the *Yahweh-malak* cluster's redactional logic.
- **Revelation 5:9 and 14:3** — The eschatological *new song*, sung before the throne by the four living creatures and the elders and by the 144,000 redeemed. The Psalter's *shir chadash* tradition reaches its apocalyptic fulfillment here.
- **Romans 8:18–23** — Creation groaning toward freedom. Paul's vision of the cosmos itself awaiting the revealing of the children of God provides the NT theological framework for reading Psalm 98:7–9 as more than metaphor.
