# Exodus 6: The Name Revealed Again

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

If the exodus events cluster around the late thirteenth century BCE, the pharaoh whose stubbornness frames this chapter is most plausibly Ramses II (c. 1279–1213 BCE), the builder of Pi-Ramesses in the eastern Delta and the monarch whose very name is preserved in Exodus 1:11. Kenneth Kitchen, in *On the Reliability of the Old Testament*, has argued at length for this Ramesside setting on the basis of Egyptian construction toponyms, the absence of Israel from the Merneptah Stele's earlier horizon (the stele itself, c. 1208 BCE, already presumes Israel as a people in Canaan), and the plausibility of Semitic slave labor in nineteenth-dynasty corvée projects. James Hoffmeier (*Israel in Egypt*) adds the evidence of Semitic presence at sites like Tell el-Dabʿa and the structural verisimilitude of the Joseph and exodus narratives against a Ramesside backdrop. A minority, including Bryant Wood, still argue for a fifteenth-century Thutmoside exodus on the basis of 1 Kings 6:1, but the Ramesside reading dominates the field.

The material world of Exodus 6 is the world of Delta brickyards. The "harsh slavery" of v. 9 that has "broken the spirit" (*qōṣer rûaḥ*, literally "shortness of breath") of the people is not an abstraction. It is the world of mud-brick molding under the eastern-Delta sun, of Semitic *ʿApiru* and *Shasu* attested in Egyptian documents performing exactly such labor, of quotas, scribes, and the Egyptian religious apparatus that legitimated pharaoh as the earthly presence of Horus and son of Ra. The chapter itself does not yet contest Egyptian deities directly — that begins at the Nile in chapter 7 — but the stakes are already theological: YHWH is about to act in a way that discloses who he is against the backdrop of gods whose names the Israelites also knew.

The authorship of Exodus 6 is one of the most discussed questions in Pentateuchal criticism. The opening speech (vv. 2–8) is a textbook specimen of the Priestly source (P) by nearly every scholarly reckoning — Martin Noth, Brevard Childs (*The Book of Exodus*), William Propp (*Anchor Bible Exodus 1–18*), Richard Elliott Friedman (*The Bible with Sources Revealed*), and Cornelis Houtman all assign it there. The argument rests on vocabulary (*wāʾērāʾ*, "I appeared"; *hăqimōtî bĕrîtî*, "I established my covenant"; the specific formula "I am YHWH"), on the use of *El Shaddai* as the patriarchal name (P's signature), and on the apparent contradiction with J, which already has YHWH named and invoked in Genesis 4:26 and used throughout the patriarchal narratives. P's claim in v. 3 that the name YHWH was not known to the patriarchs thus becomes, on the documentary reading, a deliberate theological statement by the Priestly redactor rather than a historical observation. Umberto Cassuto (*A Commentary on the Book of Exodus*), arguing against the documentary hypothesis, proposed that "I did not make myself known" (*lōʾ nôdaʿtî*) refers not to the phonemes of the Name but to the experiential knowledge of what the Name means — a reading preserved in somewhat different form by Rashi, who had already said the patriarchs knew the Name but not "in its attribute of faithfulness to promises." The genealogical insertion in vv. 14–25 is also P, interrupting the narrative to establish the priestly lineage through Levi, Kohath, Amram — a feature Friedman flags as a P structural fingerprint.

A few Hebrew terms deserve a closer look. *Shaddai* (שַׁדַּי) remains contested — possibly from *šadû* (mountain) or *šadayim* (breasts, with fertility connotations), and translated by the LXX as *pantokratōr*, "Almighty." *Gāʾal* (גָּאַל), "redeem," is the kin-redeemer verb — the obligation of a near relative to buy back a relative from slavery (see Leviticus 25, Ruth 4). *Lāqaḥ* (לָקַח), "take," carries marriage overtones; to "take a people to myself" echoes covenantal betrothal language. *ʿĂrēl śĕpātāyim* (עֲרַל שְׂפָתַיִם), Moses's "uncircumcised lips," uses the cultic category of ritual incapacity — lips that are not fit for sacred speech. And the genealogical *rōʾš bêt-ʾāḇōt* (ראש בית אבות), "head of the father's house," is P's technical administrative term for the clan subunit that becomes the organizing grid of Israel in the wilderness census.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Exodus 6 is, generically, divine speech framed by two moments of Moses's inadequacy — vv. 9–12 and vv. 28–30 — with a priestly genealogy sitting awkwardly in the middle. The divine speech itself (vv. 2–8) is one of the most formally structured theophanies in the Pentateuch. It opens and closes with the self-revelation formula "I am YHWH" (vv. 2, 8), an inclusio that frames seven covenantal first-person promises. Four of those promises form a verbal quartet in vv. 6–7: *hôṣēʾtî* (I will bring out), *hiṣṣaltî* (I will deliver), *gāʾaltî* (I will redeem), *lāqaḥtî* (I will take). The Mishnah (Pesachim 10) famously treats these four verbs as the basis for the four cups of wine at the Passover seder, and Exodus Rabbah 6:4 develops this into a theology of the four stages of redemption. A rabbinic fifth verb, *wĕhēbēʾtî* ("I will bring," v. 8), is sometimes reserved for the messianic future — hence the unfilled cup of Elijah.

The literary structure around this speech is strikingly self-conscious. Moses's two objections — "uncircumcised lips" — bracket the genealogy and function as a *Wiederaufnahme* (resumptive repetition), a standard device by which a narrator returns to a narrative thread after a digression. Cassuto read this symmetry as evidence of unified composition. Source critics read the same symmetry as editorial suturing: after the P genealogy, the redactor resumes the P narrative in vv. 28–30 by repeating Moses's objection so that the plague cycle can begin at 7:1. Either way, the function is the same — to place Aaron and Moses's lineage between the divine promise and the plague narrative, as if to credential the mouthpieces before the miracles begin.

The chapter locates itself in the book's argument as a second commissioning. After chapter 3 (J/E, the burning bush) and the failed first audience with Pharaoh in chapter 5, the plot has reached an impasse: Israel refuses to listen, Pharaoh has intensified the quotas, and Moses is ready to resign. Exodus 6 is the relaunch — P's parallel commissioning narrative, with the Name disclosed, the covenantal promises restated, and the genealogy inserted as a kind of priestly warrant. From here, the narrative turns outward toward Pharaoh and the plagues.

Textually, Exodus 6 is reasonably stable. Fragments of 4QExod preserve parts of the chapter; the LXX translates *El Shaddai* as *theos ōn autōn* ("being their God") in v. 3, a translation choice that defuses the tension between Exodus 6 and Genesis by reading "El Shaddai" as a predicate rather than a proper name. The Samaritan Pentateuch generally agrees with the MT here. The name-theology of v. 3 is one of the passages where Greek and Hebrew traditions sit somewhat differently, and this feeds directly into the patristic reading, where the Greek opens interpretive possibilities the Hebrew does not.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Canonically, Exodus 6 is the hinge on which the Pentateuch's name-theology turns. Genesis repeatedly uses the Tetragrammaton, but Exodus 6 claims this usage was retrospective: the patriarchs knew God as El Shaddai. This sets up the logic of the plagues: the plagues are a public demonstration designed so that "the Egyptians shall know that I am YHWH" (7:5) and Israel "shall know that I am YHWH your God" (6:7). The Name is not merely a label; it is the thing being revealed through the plague cycle.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory**. Josephus (*Antiquities* 2.264–76) compresses this chapter and emphasizes the ancestry of Moses and Aaron, reading the genealogy as vindicating their credentials against later Korahite challenges. Philo (*Life of Moses* 1.74ff.) reads the divine name philosophically — YHWH as pure being, El Shaddai as the power of divine sufficiency. The *Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael* and *Exodus Rabbah* both focus on the four verbs of redemption, developing the four-cups tradition and reading the chapter as God's self-correction against Moses's despair at 5:22–23. Rashi harmonizes 6:3 with Genesis by reading "I was not known by my name YHWH" as "I was not recognized in the attribute of faithfulness embodied in that Name" — the patriarchs received promises but did not see them fulfilled; now YHWH will be known as the God who keeps faith. Ibn Ezra accepts a straightforward reading but distinguishes levels of revelation. Ramban (Nachmanides) pushes further: the patriarchs knew YHWH through private revelation, but only in Egypt does YHWH reveal himself through miracles in the public world — the plagues are the proof of the Name. Maimonides (*Guide* 1.61–63) reads *YHWH* as uniquely the non-predicative name, the only one pointing to essence rather than attribute. Buber and Rosenzweig, in their German translation of the Bible, rendered the name with the second-person pronouns ICH-BIN-DA, echoing Buber's reading of YHWH as the God of present relational presence. Jon Levenson (*Sinai and Zion*, *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son*) reads the chapter as covenant renewal in Priestly idiom. Nahum Sarna (*Exploring Exodus*, JPS Exodus) focuses on the juridical force of the four verbs, placing them in the conceptual world of ancient Near Eastern kinship-redemption law.

**Christian interpretive trajectory**. Origen's *Homilies on Exodus* 3 treats the name shift allegorically — the patriarchs knew God partially, as in a shadow, while Christ discloses the fullness. Augustine wrestled with the Genesis–Exodus contradiction, concluding in *City of God* and in sermons on Exodus that the patriarchs knew the Name orally but not experientially. Gregory of Nyssa, in the *Life of Moses*, takes chapter 6 as part of the contemplative ascent: each stage of Moses's commissioning corresponds to a deeper apprehension of divine reality. Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* I q. 13) takes up the philosophical reading of the Name as signifying *ipsum esse subsistens*, being itself. Calvin's *Commentary on Exodus* rejects the documentary reading before it was formalized — in his view the apparent contradiction is resolved by reading 6:3 as "I did not reveal the full glory of the Name in its fulfillment," an anticipation of Rashi's harmonization. Luther in *Lectures on Exodus* emphasizes the Name as promise, not as metaphysics: to know YHWH is to trust the promise that he will deliver. Modern critical scholars — Wellhausen, Gunkel, von Rad, Noth, Childs — take the documentary reading as the starting point. Childs's *The Book of Exodus* is particularly careful: he notes that while the sources are distinguishable, the final canonical form asks the reader to hold the Genesis Name-usage and Exodus 6:3 together as a theological claim about the incremental self-disclosure of God. Fretheim (*Exodus*) reads the chapter as God's renewed self-commitment in the face of Israel's despair; Brueggemann (*Theology of the Old Testament*) as an act of "counter-testimony" against the imperial reality of Egypt; Goldingay (*Old Testament Theology*) as an instance of God's accommodating self-translation into new historical conditions.

**Convergence and divergence**. Jewish and Christian trajectories converge remarkably on the pastoral point of the chapter: YHWH steps forward again, restates promises, and refuses to let Moses's despair terminate the mission. They diverge on the Name itself. Jewish commentary, from Rashi to Maimonides to Buber, reads YHWH as the personal, covenantal Name — a name tied to a people and a land. Christian commentary, especially after Augustine and Aquinas, reads YHWH through Exodus 3:14 and the LXX's *ho ōn* as the metaphysical Name — being itself — a reading that is theologically powerful but which most contemporary Jewish interpreters regard as importing Greek categories alien to the text's world. They also diverge on the genealogy: Jewish tradition takes it with full seriousness as the priestly warrant of Aaron (and thus of all later priesthood); Christian readers often skip over it or allegorize it.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Did the patriarchs know the Name?** Exodus 6:3 says they did not; Genesis says they did. The question is small in appearance but large in implication, because it bears on whether the Torah presents itself as a unified text or as a layered one, and on whether divine self-disclosure is cumulative or all-at-once.

- **Source-critical (Friedman, Propp, Childs)**: P represents a theological stream that reserved the Name for Moses. P's *El Shaddai → YHWH* sequence is a deliberate periodization of revelation: patriarchs get the promise, Moses and Israel get the Name. The apparent contradiction with J is a fossil of the compositional history, preserved because the redactors valued both strands. Steelmanned: this reading takes seriously the literary evidence that different vocabularies cluster in different passages, and it gives an account of why Exodus 6 reads like a second commissioning of Moses when chapter 3 already narrated one. The cost is that the unity of Scripture becomes a redactional achievement rather than an intrinsic property.
- **Traditional harmonization (Rashi, Cassuto, Calvin)**: "Making known" (*nôdaʿtî*) refers to experiential rather than nominal knowledge. The patriarchs heard the Name but did not see YHWH act in the attribute the Name embodies — covenant-keeping faithfulness made visible in deliverance. Steelmanned: the Hebrew verb *yādaʿ* (know) is famously experiential, not merely cognitive; the patriarchs "knew" YHWH as the one who gave promises, Moses's generation will "know" YHWH as the one who fulfills them. This reading preserves canonical unity without suppressing the real difference between patriarchal and Mosaic revelation.
- **Christian metaphysical (Aquinas, patristic)**: The Name signifies being itself, and the shift from *Shaddai* to YHWH marks a deepening philosophical disclosure of God's nature from power (Shaddai as "Almighty") to essence (YHWH as "I AM"). Steelmanned: this reading is continuous with Exodus 3:14 as read in the LXX and with the apostolic use of "the one who is" as a divine title; it gives the Name content beyond the merely historical. The cost is that it risks importing categories — essence, being — from Greek philosophy into a Hebrew text whose own concerns are covenantal and historical.

**Where this leaves us**: the chapter witnesses to a real discontinuity between the patriarchal and Mosaic experiences of God, whatever one makes of the compositional history; harmonizing readings that respect this discontinuity are textually stronger than ones that flatten it.

**Tension Two: Moses's incapacity as theological argument.** Twice Moses declares himself "uncircumcised of lips." The phrase is striking — Moses is already circumcised in the flesh (Exodus 4:24–26 has seen to that); now he disqualifies his own mouth in priestly idiom.

- **Catholic / ressourcement (Aquinas, de Lubac)**: Moses's self-description is genuine moral and physical incapacity, which God overcomes not by removing the incapacity but by compensating for it through Aaron. Grace perfects nature by working through ordinary means — the mouth of a brother. This prefigures the sacramental logic of the Church: God acts through limited human ministers.
- **Reformed (Calvin)**: Moses's incapacity is a genuine confession of inadequacy, and God's response is to act despite it — indeed, through it, so that the glory belongs to God alone. Calvin's reading anticipates Paul on the treasure in jars of clay. The function of the genealogy is to underline that Aaron, too, is an ordinary man of a specific lineage; the miracle is that God condescends to speak through such vessels.
- **Jewish (Rashi, Ramban, Zornberg)**: Moses's hesitation is real, not rhetorical. Avivah Zornberg (*The Particulars of Rapture*) reads "uncircumcised lips" as an image of Moses's deep psychological wound — his speech is blocked by trauma, by exile, by the gap between his Egyptian upbringing and his Hebrew identity. God does not cure him; God gives him Aaron. The chapter is honest about the permanent fractures in even God's chosen leaders.
- **Critical (Brueggemann, Fretheim)**: The double objection functions rhetorically to set up the pharaonic confrontation as asymmetric. God is speaking through a stammerer to the most powerful man in the world; the disproportion is the point. Fretheim in particular sees the frail-speaker motif as part of the book's sustained argument that power as Egypt understands it is not what determines history.

**Where this leaves us**: the text resists triumphalism about Moses. The man God uses remains unqualified in his own eyes and partially so in the text's; deliverance comes not through the hero's competence but through the Name that acts in spite of and through him.

**Unresolved question**: If the Name YHWH was genuinely undisclosed to the patriarchs, what was the content of Abraham's faith in Genesis 15 — faith in whom, precisely, if not in YHWH as YHWH?

## The Thread

Three cross-references that do not usually appear together.

First, **Exodus 6:7 → Revelation 21:3**. The formula "I will take you to be my people, and I will be your God" recurs in Leviticus 26:12, Jeremiah 31:33, Ezekiel 37:27, Zechariah 8:8, 2 Corinthians 6:16, and finally in Revelation 21:3, where the voice from the throne announces that "the dwelling place of God is with man." The covenant language coined in Exodus 6 becomes the scriptural signature of the God-with-us theme, climaxing in the new creation.

Second, **Exodus 6:20 → Numbers 26:59**. The note that Amram took Jochebed his father's sister as wife will become a legal problem after Leviticus 18:12 forbids precisely such a union. The rabbinic tradition (b. Sanhedrin 58b) reads this as evidence that the patriarchs and pre-Sinai generations were under a different legal regime; later Christian commentators from Augustine onward sometimes deployed this observation in arguments about the relationship of the Mosaic law to earlier and later moral orders.

Third, **Exodus 6:24 → Numbers 16 → Psalm 88**. Korah is listed here, matter-of-factly, as a Kohathite. He will lead the great priestly rebellion in Numbers 16 and be swallowed by the earth. Yet the sons of Korah become temple singers, and Psalm 88 — the darkest psalm in the Psalter, ending without resolution — is a Korahite composition. The name that appears without comment in Exodus 6 traces a long arc through Torah and Psalter, from rebellion to lament to liturgy: the genealogy already contains the seeds of both the priesthood's crises and its most haunting prayers.
