# Song of Solomon 3 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 3 falls into two sharply distinct scenes, and the seam between them is one of the Song's most discussed compositional questions. Verses 1–5 take place at night, in the woman's bed and in the streets of the city, and culminate in the second of the Song's three formal *adjuration refrains*. Verses 6–11 abruptly shift to a daylight royal procession — Solomon's wedding palanquin coming up from the wilderness, escorted by sixty armed warriors. The two scenes share no characters, no setting, no continuity — and yet the redactor (or composer) placed them adjacent. Most modern critical scholars (Pope, Murphy, Exum) treat them as separate poems anthologized side by side; some (Longman, Garrett) argue for a more deliberate juxtaposition.

The first scene's setting is *al-mishkavi ba-leilot* (עַל־מִשְׁכָּבִי בַּלֵּילוֹת), "on my bed in the nights" — a plural form (*ba-leilot*) that suggests not a single night but a recurring nighttime longing. The Hebrew construction is iterative; Robert Alter renders it "night after night on my bed." The verb *biqashtiv* (בִּקַּשְׁתִּיו) — "I sought him" — repeats four times in five verses, hammering the chapter's opening register into the reader. *Sheahavah nafshi* (שֶׁאָהֲבָה נַפְשִׁי), "whom my soul loves," is the woman's preferred designation for the man across the dream-scene; it appears nowhere else in the Song outside this chapter and its echo at 5:6.

The watchmen (*ha-shomrim*) of 3:3 are the city's nightwatch. In Iron Age Levantine and Persian-era cities, a nightwatch patrolled walls and streets — Nehemiah 4:9 references the practice, and ANE city-administration texts confirm it. The woman wandering the streets at night to find her lover is, by the standards of Persian-period civic life, a transgressive image: a respectable young woman did not roam the city alone after dark. The scene at 5:7, where the watchmen will beat her, makes the social risk explicit; in chapter 3 they are merely silent witnesses to her search.

The phrase *beit immi ve-el cheder horati* (בֵּית אִמִּי וְאֶל־חֶדֶר הוֹרָתִי), "my mother's house, the chamber of her who conceived me" (3:4), is striking. The Song mentions the woman's mother several times (1:6, 3:4, 6:9, 8:1, 8:2) and never her father. Marvin Pope and Cheryl Exum have observed that the Song imagines the female-line household — mother, brothers, the daughters of Jerusalem — far more than the patrilineal household standard in most of the Hebrew Bible. The "chamber of her who conceived me" is a specifically female space; the woman is bringing her lover home to the room in which she was conceived.

The second scene (3:6–11) shifts genre entirely. The Hebrew *appiryon* (אַפִּרְיוֹן) in 3:9, translated "carriage," is one of the loanwords that has anchored the case for late dating: it likely derives from the Greek *phoreion* (φορεῖον), a sedan-chair or palanquin. The presence of a probable Greek loanword pushes the redaction of the Song into the Hellenistic period (post-fourth century BC). The *appiryon* is constructed with cedar of Lebanon, silver posts, gold back, purple seat — the international luxury inventory of a Persian or Hellenistic court. The "sixty mighty men" (*shishim gibborim*) recall David's elite warriors (2 Sam. 23:8–39, where the cohort numbers thirty), perhaps a deliberate doubling. The fragrant cloud — myrrh, frankincense, "all the fragrant powders of a merchant" — evokes the spice caravans of the Arabian incense road.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter's literary architecture pivots on the second adjuration refrain at 3:5, which closes the night-scene with the same formula as 2:7: *al-ta'iru ve-al-te'oreru et ha-ahavah ad shetechpatz* (אַל־תָּעִירוּ וְאַל־תְּעוֹרְרוּ אֶת־הָאַהֲבָה עַד שֶׁתֶּחְפָּץ). The refrain functions as a structural hinge: it both closes the dream-search and creates the silence into which the royal procession irrupts.

Verses 1–5 are best read as a *dream-poem* or, in Cheryl Exum's terminology, a "lyrical fantasy." There is no narrative claim that the woman literally rose and walked the streets; the surreal compression — *I sought him; scarcely had I passed [the watchmen] when I found him* — has the texture of dream-logic. The same dream-form returns, more darkly, in 5:2–8. Roland Murphy reads the two scenes as a matched pair; Phyllis Trible, Cheryl Exum, and Ellen Davis have all written extensively on the active female agency in these passages.

What is striking about 3:1–4, in the larger landscape of biblical narrative, is the woman's grammatical subjectivity. *I will rise. I will go about. I will seek.* In a canon where female desire is most often narrated from the outside or filtered through male perspective, the Song gives the woman the active, first-person voice. She rises, walks the city, asks the watchmen, finds, holds, refuses to release, and brings her lover home to her own mother's house. Trible's *God and the Rhetoric of Sexuality* identifies this passage as one of the clearest counterweights in the canon to the traditional patriarchal pattern.

The wedding procession of 3:6–11 deploys a different literary register. The opening *mi zot olah min ha-midbar* (מִי זֹאת עֹלָה מִן הַמִּדְבָּר), "Who is this coming up from the wilderness?" — repeated at 8:5 — is a *spectator's* question, the voice of an onlooker watching a parade pass. The grammar is feminine ("who is *she*"), and there is real interpretive debate about whether the procession bears Solomon, his bride, or the bride being brought to Solomon. The ambiguity may be deliberate: the cloud of incense and the surrounding warriors leave the central figure visually unresolved, the way an actual procession's smoke would.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Jewish allegorical tradition has read both halves of chapter 3 as central salvation-history texts. The *Targum* and *Midrash Shir HaShirim Rabbah* read the night-search of 3:1–4 as Israel in exile, seeking the absent Yahweh — the watchmen as the prophets, the "mother's house" as the restored temple. The royal procession of 3:6–11 is read as the giving of Torah at Sinai (Solomon as Yahweh, the *appiryon* as the cloud of glory or the tabernacle, the sixty warriors as the angelic host or the elders of Israel) and, in some midrashic strands, as the eschatological wedding of Yahweh and Israel in the messianic age. Rashi gathered these readings into the standard medieval Jewish commentary.

In the rabbinic imagination, 3:11's "crown with which his mother crowned him on the day of his wedding" became a particular focus. The Mishnah (*Sotah* 9:14) records that after the Roman destruction of the Second Temple, the practice of bridegrooms wearing crowns at their weddings was banned in mourning; the verse was read both as a memory of pre-destruction joy and as a promise of restoration.

The Christian allegorical tradition gave chapter 3 some of its most extended treatments. Origen reads the night-search of 3:1–4 as the soul seeking Christ in the "night" of the present age — a reading that became foundational for the Christian mystical tradition's "dark night" theme. **Bernard of Clairvaux** preached on chapter 3 in the final years of his life; he died in 1153 having reached only the opening verses of the chapter. His Sermon 75 onward treats the bride's nighttime search as the soul's longing for the absent Christ — a longing that intensifies precisely because of his apparent absence. The "dark night of the soul" of John of the Cross, three centuries later, draws directly from this tradition; *Spiritual Canticle* uses the imagery of 3:1–4 explicitly. Teresa of Avila's *Interior Castle* echoes the same search-and-find pattern.

The Solomonic procession of 3:6–11 was the Christian liturgical text *par excellence* for **wedding theology**. Medieval Christian wedding rites drew on the verses to figure Christ as the royal bridegroom coming for the Church-bride. Reformation wedding orders retained the imagery; Calvin preaches on the procession as a figure of Christ's love for his Church. The "sixty mighty men against terror by night" became, in monastic and Reformed reception, the angelic protectors of the Christian community.

The modern critical recovery of chapter 3 has emphasized the **female agency** of 3:1–4. **Phyllis Trible** opened this conversation in *God and the Rhetoric of Sexuality* (1978): the woman's nighttime search is not a passive longing but an active pursuit that culminates in her physically grasping her lover and bringing him to *her* mother's house. **Cheryl Exum**, in her Old Testament Library commentary, develops the reading: the woman is the grammatical agent of every verb in the search-sequence. **Renita Weems** has written on the passage from a Black women's hermeneutical standpoint, noting how the active female desire of the text has often been muted in pulpit reception. **Ellen Davis** in her *Westminster Bible Companion* commentary on the Song reads the passage as a corrective to traditions, both Jewish and Christian, that have tried to constrain female religious agency.

The *erotic* texture of 3:4 — *I held him, and would not let him go* (Hebrew *achaztiv ve-lo arpennu*, אֲחַזְתִּיו וְלֹא אַרְפֶּנּוּ) — has discomfited some traditional Christian readers. Origen and Bernard sublimate the holding into spiritual grasping; modern critical readers (Pope, Exum) note the physicality of the verb and resist the sublimation. The line "until I had brought him into my mother's house, into the chamber of her who conceived me" implicates the lover's physical body in a private, female-marked space. Chana and Ariel Bloch's translation foregrounds the embodiment.

The Catholic and modern Reformed reception has tried to hold the literal and allegorical together. Pope John Paul II's *Theology of the Body* lectures dwell on the woman's active seeking as part of the integral reciprocity of love — a reading that treats female desire as theologically positive, not (as some older Catholic moral theology had treated it) as something to be regulated.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

Chapter 3 generates two distinct theological tensions, one for each scene.

**The night-search (3:1–5).** What does it mean that the canonical text gives the woman this much agency, this much physical urgency, this much initiative?

**Position 1 (literal-affirmative reading):** The text is exactly what it appears to be — an unembarrassed celebration of female desire in pursuit of its object. The Bible canonizes a young woman who rises from her bed at night, walks the city, refuses to be put off by the watchmen, and physically grasps her lover. This is the Bible's own corrective to traditions that would silence or domesticate female sexual agency. Trible, Exum, Weems, Davis converge on this reading. On this view, the modern feminist recovery is not imposing a foreign category on the text; it is hearing what the text was always saying.

**Position 2 (allegorical-mystical reading):** The night-search is the soul's pursuit of God, the Church's longing for the absent Christ, Israel's exile-longing for Yahweh. The "holding" of 3:4 is the contemplative grip of faith on a God whose presence has been sought through the long dark. Origen, Bernard, John of the Cross, Teresa converge on this reading. The eroticism is the *vehicle* of a deeper theological-mystical truth.

**Position 3 (traditional patriarchal reading, now widely critiqued):** Some pre-modern readings treated the woman's nighttime wandering as a *cautionary* example or as a strictly allegorical episode that should not be applied to literal female behavior. This reading is less defended in current scholarship; even Tremper Longman, who is conservative on the literal/allegorical question, refuses to read the woman's agency as negative.

**Position 4 (both/and):** The literal celebration of female desire and the allegorical reading of the soul's pursuit are not in competition. The honest interpretive move is to accept that the canon has placed an actively-desiring female voice at the center of its meditation on love, and that this voice can also serve as the figure for the soul's love for God. Phyllis Trible's later work, Ellen Davis, Mark Boda, and the post-Vatican-II Catholic reading converge here.

**The Solomonic procession (3:6–11).** The royal-wedding scene generates a different tension. Is it (a) a celebration of royal marriage that has been incorporated into the Song as a literal coronation/wedding-ode, perhaps composed for an actual royal wedding; (b) an idealized portrait of the male beloved imagined as Solomon, the lover-as-king; (c) a Yahweh-Israel allegorical procession — the giving of Torah, the wedding of Yahweh and his people; (d) a Christ-Church wedding allegory, picked up by Christian liturgy and devotional reading; or (e) all of the above, layered in the canonical text? Most modern commentators read it as (a) or (b) at the literal level; the rabbinic, patristic, and medieval traditions converge on (c) and (d).

A real interpretive worry, raised by feminist commentators including Exum, is that the Solomonic procession scene introduces the *king-as-bridegroom* motif precisely after the chapter has given us the woman's active agency. Does the royal pageantry overwhelm or subsume the woman's voice? Or is the canonical juxtaposition deliberate — the active female pursuit answered by the formal wedding, the woman's agency consummated rather than suppressed in the public marriage ceremony? Ellen Davis reads the juxtaposition as integrative; Cheryl Exum is more skeptical.

**Where this leaves us:** Chapter 3 holds together a fierce, urgent, female-led night-search and a formal, public, royal wedding procession. The two scenes seem at first incompatible — the private intensity of 3:1–5 against the public spectacle of 3:6–11. But the canon's own structure suggests that mature love is *both*: the urgent personal pursuit and the public covenanted union, the intimate grasp and the witnessed ceremony. Neither without the other; both required.

**Closing question:** If the canon has given us a woman who rises in the night and refuses to let her lover go, and if the same canon has given us a public wedding with sixty witnessing warriors, what do we make of religious traditions (or marital traditions) that have privileged one of these moments and erased the other?
