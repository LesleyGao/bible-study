# Exodus 27: A Fire That Must Not Go Out

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Exodus 27 moves from the sanctum outward: from the veil and frames of chapter 26 to the altar, the court, and the light that burns continually within. The historical matrix is the same — a late Bronze Age wilderness shrine if we follow Kitchen and Hoffmeier, or an exilic/post-exilic priestly retrojection if we follow Wellhausen — but the material culture evidence is thickest here, because horned altars are among the most distinctive artifacts of Levantine archaeology. The excavations at Dan (Avraham Biran), Beersheba (Yohanan Aharoni), and Arad (also Aharoni) have yielded monumental horned altars in stone and in dressed ashlar, and smaller household horned altars of limestone have been found across Judah and Israel. The horn typology is not a biblical peculiarity; it is a regional architectural idiom. Seymour Gitin's catalogue of Iron Age horned altars runs to dozens of exemplars.

The altar specifications in 27:1–8 describe a *portable* horned altar — five cubits square, three cubits high, acacia wood overlaid in bronze, hollow with boards, carrying poles. This is unusual. The large stone horned altars excavated at Dan and Beersheba were immobile; the biblical specification imagines an altar that, like the tabernacle it serves, travels. The archaeology of bronze sheeting over wooden cores is well attested in Egyptian royal furniture (Tutankhamun's tomb furniture is the clearest example), and the combination of portability and bronze cladding suggests a genuinely Late Bronze/early Iron Age technological context. Menahem Haran argued in *Temples and Temple-Service* that the chapter's altar design is too peculiar to be a late invention; a post-exilic scribe familiar with the massive stone altars of the Temple would not imagine a portable wooden core sheathed in bronze.

The four horns (*qarnôt*, 27:2) carried deep symbolic weight across the ANE. In Israelite culture they were also a zone of refuge: 1 Kings 1:50 shows Adonijah fleeing Solomon's wrath by grasping the horns of the altar, and 1 Kings 2:28 shows Joab doing the same — in both cases, the altar's horns marked a zone of sanctuary that could be legally contested but not violently breached without sacrilege. The horns also received sacrificial blood (Leviticus 4:7; Exodus 29:12) — a point Milgrom analyzes in his Leviticus commentary as marking the altar's four cardinal directions as places where atonement is enacted. Archaeologically, the four horns of the Arad altar are intact; the Beersheba altar's horns were found among dismantled stones, possibly reflecting Hezekiah's reform (2 Kings 18:4) or Josiah's (2 Kings 23).

The grating of bronze network (27:4) is one of the chapter's interpretive puzzles. The Hebrew *mikbār maʿăśēh rešet nĕḥōšet* ("grating, a work of a net of bronze") is specified as sitting *under the ledge* (*ṣad ha-mizbēaḥ* — the altar's rim or ledge, precise location debated) so that it "extends halfway down the altar" (27:5). Commentators divide: some see a mid-height shelf-grate that caught ash and cinders; others (Propp in the Anchor Bible) suggest an external decorative casing. The Samaritan Pentateuch's expansion at this point is revealing — the Samaritans, who built an alternate cult on Mount Gerizim, found the same passage unclear and harmonized with execution details from chapter 38. Rabbinic literature (Zevachim 61b) discusses the grate at length.

The court (*ḥāṣēr*, 27:9–19) is the outermost zone of the sanctuary and the only one accessible to the Israelite laity. Its dimensions — 100 cubits long by 50 cubits wide — yield a 2:1 ratio that recurs throughout the tabernacle's proportions. Fifty cubits on the east, the entrance side, with a 20-cubit screen of blue, purple, scarlet, and fine linen (27:16) — the same chromatic palette as the veil and inner curtain, but with an important difference: the screen is embroidered work (*maʿăśēh rōqēm*), not the "skillfully worked" (*maʿăśēh ḥōšēb*) cherubim weaving of the inner curtain and veil. Rashi on 26:1 vs. 26:36 elaborated this technical distinction: *rōqēm* is embroidery of a single figure visible on one side, while *ḥōšēb* is double-faced weaving with different figures on each side. The court screen has flowers and colors, but no cherubim — you meet the winged guardians only inside.

The bronze pillars with silver hooks and silver "fillets" (*ḥăšuqîm*, 27:10) — the term likely denotes silver bands encircling the tops of the pillars — describe a typical Iron Age architectural style. Carol Meyers and other archaeologists have catalogued comparable composite-metal hardware from Iron Age levels across the Levant.

The oil specifications in 27:20–21 shift register completely: from architecture to liturgy, from construction to ongoing practice. *Šemen zayit zāk kātît* — "pure beaten olive oil" — names the highest grade of oil. Mishnah Menachot 8:4–5 describes the process: olives were crushed in a mortar rather than pressed, producing a first oil of unmatched purity, suitable for the menorah. Egyptian and Phoenician olive presses are well attested archaeologically; oil production was central to every Iron Age Mediterranean economy. What matters theologically is *tāmîd* (27:20) — "regularly," "continually" — the lamp is never to be extinguished. This word becomes a technical term for continuous cultic observance across the rest of the Torah.

Key Hebrew terms: *mizbēaḥ* (מִזְבֵּחַ), altar, from *z-b-ḥ*, "to sacrifice"; *qarnôt* (קַרְנוֹת), horns; *nĕḥōšet* (נְחֹשֶׁת), bronze (the altar and court are bronze rather than gold — graded downward in value as one moves outward from the Holy of Holies); *ḥāṣēr* (חָצֵר), court or enclosed yard; *šemen* (שֶׁמֶן), oil; *tāmîd* (תָּמִיד), continually; *ʾōhel môʿēd* (אֹהֶל מוֹעֵד), tent of meeting.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Exodus 27 sits as the fourth unit in the first of the seven divine speeches that structure chapters 25–31. Jon Levenson and Moshe Weinfeld both noted that the speech-boundaries reveal a carefully ordered progression: from ark and cover (25:10–22) outward to table and menorah (25:23–40), then tabernacle structure (ch. 26), altar and court (ch. 27), priestly appointment (28:1–29:46), then golden altar and incense (30:1–10). The direction is centrifugal: sanctum outward. Chapter 27's position matters. It marks the transition from *building* to *burning* — the altar is a built structure, yes, but it exists only as the platform for fire, and the chapter ends (27:20–21) with the command to keep the lamp burning through the night. The order is: contain, enclose, then kindle.

Genre is priestly instruction with symbolic architectural load, but the register shifts within the chapter. Verses 1–19 are specification (cubit measurements, material counts, positional details); verses 20–21 are liturgical command (perpetual obligation, succession of generations, "statute forever"). The shift from one register to the other is common in P but notable here because it happens inside a single chapter: built things, then bound practices. The verbal forms shift accordingly: *taʿăśeh* (you shall make) dominates verses 1–19, then *tĕṣawweh* (you shall command) opens verse 20, shifting from construction to commissioning.

Literary devices: the recapitulating phrase "as it has been shown you on the mountain, so shall it be made" (27:8) repeats from 25:40 and anticipates 26:30. This is an inclusio structuring chapters 25–27 as a single architectural unit under the rubric of divinely revealed pattern. The chapter's arithmetic — 100+50 cubits for the court, 20+20+10 pillars, 50+15+15+20 cubits in the east wall — returns to the fifty/twenty/ten system that dominates chapter 26. As in chapter 26, the altar is a five-by-five square, a perfect square at three cubits height. The court's 2:1 ratio, the altar's 1:1 square, and the Holy of Holies' eventual cube all participate in a system of nested geometric perfection.

ANE parallels to the altar itself: the horned altars of Megiddo (Middle Bronze), Shechem, Tell el-Dab'a, and Ugarit supply the morphological type. Susan Ackerman and William Dever have both argued that the specifically Israelite innovation was not the horned altar but the *exclusion* of other cultic apparatuses from its use — no standing stones (*maṣṣēbôt*), no Asherah poles. The chapter's silence is itself a theological statement.

Textual variants: the LXX of Exodus 27 diverges from the MT especially in the ordering of the court specifications; 4QExod-Lev^f preserves a Qumran text type with several minor expansions. The Samaritan Pentateuch harmonizes with chapter 38's execution account. The LXX translates *mikbār* ("grating") as *eschara* — a Greek term for a sacrificial brazier — suggesting the Alexandrian translators understood the grating as a cooking element for the sacrifice rather than an ash collector. This is a live interpretive option.

An important micro-structural feature: the final verse (27:21) introduces Aaron and his sons *before* chapter 28's formal appointment. The lamp-tending command presupposes a priesthood not yet instituted in the narrative. Source critics have used this to argue for editorial sequencing: the lamp oil passage (27:20–21) is often paired with 30:7–8 (the incense command) as belonging to a secondary stratum. Propp in the Anchor Bible lays this out carefully. The effect of the present ordering is that the last word of the court/altar section announces the priesthood that chapter 28 will name.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The altar and the court form the theological spine of the rest of the Pentateuch's cultic legislation. Every sacrifice commanded in Leviticus 1–7 — burnt offering, grain offering, peace offering, sin offering, guilt offering — is performed on *this* altar. The blood manipulations described in Leviticus 4 (on the horns), Leviticus 8 (on the consecration), and Leviticus 16 (on the Day of Atonement) all presuppose the geography Exodus 27 establishes. The altar's bronze (as against the gold of the inner altar of incense in 30:1–10) marks the distinction between the people's offerings and the priestly liturgy: bronze outside, gold inside; blood outside, incense inside; consumption by fire outside, fragrance rising inside.

The court's boundary is itself a theology. Inside the court but outside the Holy Place is where the lay Israelite can go; beyond the screen, only priests. L. Michael Morales in *Who Shall Ascend to the Hill of the LORD?* traces how this graduated approach becomes the Bible's master pattern for the question of how sinful humans may come near a holy God. The answer in chapter 27 is architectural: through a gate, past an altar, through a screen. Every stage requires mediation.

The continually burning lamp (*tāmîd*) generates a long and rich trajectory. Leviticus 24:1–4 elaborates the command. First Samuel 3:3 situates the boy Samuel at Shiloh "before the lamp of God had gone out" — a detail scholars read both chronologically (dawn was approaching) and theologically (the lamp of divine presence was dimming in Eli's corrupt priesthood; it would re-ignite in the young prophet). Second Chronicles 13:11 invokes the perpetual lamp as a mark of Judah's faithfulness over against the north. Zechariah 4, the prophet's menorah vision, makes the lamp an image of the Spirit-empowered restoration. In rabbinic Judaism, *ner tamid* — the continuously burning lamp in every synagogue — derives directly from this passage. Every synagogue today has one.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Philo (*Special Laws* 1.66–72, 296–298) read the perpetual lamp as divine wisdom in the soul. Josephus (*Antiquities* 3.197–199) described the Second Temple's altar with Hellenistic embellishment. Mishnah Tamid is a whole tractate on the daily service; its opening dawn sequence at the altar is among the most evocative passages in rabbinic literature. Mishnah Middot catalogues the Second Temple's altar — larger, made of undressed stones per Exodus 20:25, but preserving the horns and fire. Bavli Zevachim analyzes the altar's sacrificial use in obsessive detail.

Medieval: Rashi explains the grating's position; Ibn Ezra argues for a genuine wilderness context; Ramban reads the altar as atoning substitution (what happens to the animal is what the sinner deserves). Maimonides (*Guide* 3.45–46) is strikingly deflationary — the altar is pedagogical, weaning Israel from Egyptian idolatrous sacrifice. *Hilchot Beit HaBechira* codifies the altar's halakhic dimensions.

Modern: Milgrom's *Leviticus*; Haran's *Temples and Temple-Service*; Levenson's cosmos-microcosm frame; Baruch Schwartz on priestly atonement; Carol Meyers on the menorah.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Origen (*Homilies on Exodus* 13) reads the bronze altar as the cross and the fire as the Spirit. Gregory of Nyssa (*Life of Moses* 2) moralizes the altar as self-offering. Augustine's *City of God* 10.5–6 reframes Christian sacrifice as self-giving of Christ and the Church. Chrysostom homilizes the altar as fulfilled in the eucharistic table. Aquinas (*Summa* I-II q. 102 a. 4) gives a rational account — the animal's destruction enacts the destruction of sin; the smoke signifies the heart's ascent.

Reformation: Luther's Hebrews commentary reads the altar as type of the cross, the perpetual lamp as Christ the light (John 8:12; 9:5). Calvin's *Commentary on the Four Last Books of Moses* treats the altar less allegorically — it is the place of atonement foreshadowing Christ's one-for-all sacrifice (Hebrews 9:26; 10:10). The Reformed regulative principle (Westminster 21) takes the altar's specificity as evidence that God alone prescribes his worship.

Contemporary: Fretheim reads the altar as site of divine vulnerability to human need; Brueggemann on the court as negotiated space; Morales on graded sanctity; N. T. Wright on sacrifice as narrative preparation for Christ's atonement; Alexander's *From Eden to the New Jerusalem* on the altar as restoring Edenic communion.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Jewish tradition preserves the altar's liturgical logic while acknowledging its inaccessibility without the Temple: prayer replaces sacrifice (Hosea 14:2 through the Amidah), and the *ner tamid* continues. Christian tradition reads the altar as superseded by the cross. Catholic sacramental theology (CCC 1382–1383; Trent) maintains an altar at the center of eucharistic celebration. Reformed theology removes the altar as theological category; the Lord's Table is memorial, not sacrificial. Orthodox liturgy retains the altar as the holy mountain descended into the sanctuary.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What is the altar *for*? Is sacrificial burning necessary, pedagogical, or metaphorical?**

*Catholic-patristic:* The altar is necessary for atonement because sin requires substitution, and substitution requires death and burning. Aquinas (ST I-II q. 102 a. 4) gives multiple reasons: the animal's destruction signifies the destruction of sin; the smoke signifies the heart's ascent; the continual fire signifies the ongoing need for atonement. The altar is fulfilled, not abolished, in Christ and the Mass.

*Reformed:* Calvin agrees the altar is necessary under the old covenant as type and shadow but insists that its fulfillment in Christ abolishes any continuing sacrificial category. There is no altar in a Reformed church, only a table. The altar's necessity was *for its time* (Hebrews 10:4: "the blood of bulls and goats cannot take away sins"); now the category is closed.

*Maimonidean:* The altar is pedagogical — a concession to a people formed by Egyptian religion. Maimonides (*Guide* 3.32, 3.45–46) argues that sacrificial worship was not God's preferred mode but an accommodation: God could not suddenly abolish sacrifice for a people who knew no other form of worship, so he redirected it from idols to the true God. Eventually, in Maimonides' view, the deeper intent of worship (prayer, meditation) will make sacrifice unnecessary.

*Christian Anabaptist-progressive:* The altar is metaphor from the start. Sacrifice reveals God's compassion for human fear, not God's need for blood. The category of atoning death is a human projection onto a God who desires mercy rather than sacrifice (Hosea 6:6, quoted twice by Jesus).

*Ramban:* The altar stages substitution. When the worshipper lays hands on the animal's head (Leviticus 1:4), he identifies with it; what happens to the animal is what, in justice, should happen to him. The altar makes this transfer visible. It is neither pedagogical (Maimonides is wrong) nor metaphorical (the Anabaptists are wrong); it is the ordered form of mercy.

*Steelman the least intuitive (Maimonides):* If sacrificial burning were intrinsically necessary, we would expect God to have instituted it from the beginning, and Cain's grain offering (Genesis 4:3–4) would have been categorically rejected for lack of blood. But the Genesis text does not say that; it says God "had regard for Abel and his offering" (4:4), without explaining *why*, and Cain's anger is at being dispreferred, not at lacking blood. The development of the sacrificial system over Genesis–Exodus looks more like accommodation than institution from eternity. Maimonides' pedagogical reading has real textual traction.

**Where this leaves us:** Hebrews 10 is canonical for Christians: "sacrifices and offerings you have not desired... then I said, Behold, I have come to do your will" (quoting Psalm 40). This rules out Christian Platonic insistence that sacrificial burning is intrinsically required. But Ramban is right that the altar enacts substitution, and that substitution is not an arbitrary pedagogy but a profound moral grammar. The altar is neither simply metaphor nor simply necessary physics; it is the choreographed form by which mercy and justice find each other.

**Tension 2: What is the theology of the *continually* burning lamp?** Is *tāmîd* descriptive (a liturgical detail) or prescriptive of something deeper about divine presence?

*Rabbinic-Jewish:* The *ner tamid* is the material sign that God has not abandoned his people. Every synagogue's perpetual lamp testifies that the *šĕkînâ* went into exile with Israel (Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael on 12:41; Megillah 29a). The lamp is the flame on the altar of the heart.

*Catholic-Orthodox:* The perpetual lamp before the tabernacle in every Catholic church (and the comparable Orthodox practice) derives directly from Exodus 27:20–21 read through the eucharistic theology of real presence. The lamp signifies Christ's abiding in the reserved Sacrament.

*Reformed-Anabaptist:* The lamp is a ceremonial detail of the old covenant, fulfilled in Christ as the light of the world (John 8:12) and extended in the church as light of the world (Matthew 5:14). There is no warrant for a perpetual lamp in a Reformed space; the Spirit dwells in believers, not in lamps.

*Zechariah-charismatic:* Zechariah 4 reads the menorah as the Spirit's ongoing empowerment; the oil flows from the two olive trees (the anointed ones) directly into the lamps. The lamp burns because the Spirit pours.

*Steelman the Reformed:* The rabbinic and Catholic readings risk tying God's presence to a physical lamp in a way that could slide into the very idolatry the tabernacle was meant to combat. The Reformed reading preserves the biblical logic that God dwells where he chooses, not where a flame happens to be burning. If every synagogue lamp went out tomorrow, the *šĕkînâ* would not be diminished; if every sanctuary lamp were extinguished, Christ would still be present in his word and sacrament.

**Where this leaves us:** The perpetual lamp is neither bare ceremony nor sacramental guarantee. It is a *sign* in the strong biblical sense — instituted by God, meaningful not because the flame has intrinsic presence-generating power but because the community, in tending it, enacts faithfulness across generations. The Reformed caution is well taken; the Jewish and Catholic practice is theologically defensible.

**Unresolved question:** If the altar's fire must never go out (Leviticus 6:12) and the lamp's flame must never go out (Exodus 27:20), what happens to the theology of continuous presence when, historically, both did go out — in 586 BCE when the Temple was destroyed, and again in 70 CE? The rabbinic answer is that the *šĕkînâ* migrated; the Christian answer is that the fire ignited anew at Pentecost; the question is whether these answers are compatible, and whether either adequately accounts for the long centuries when the fire was simply absent.

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references.

*First,* the altar's horns (*qarnôt*) recur in 1 Kings 1:50 (Adonijah) and 1 Kings 2:28 (Joab) as places of refuge — but with opposite outcomes. Adonijah grasps the horns and is spared; Joab grasps them and is killed at them by Solomon's order. The difference reveals a theological point: the altar provides asylum from personal vengeance but not from judicial execution. Horns are mercy, not immunity. When Amos later says "the horns of the altar shall be cut off" (Amos 3:14), he is announcing the withdrawal of even the asylum category.

*Second,* the perpetual lamp passage (27:20–21) and the manna passage (16:32–34) mirror each other verbally: both speak of something to be kept "throughout your generations" "before the LORD"; both institute a continuity of physical sign across time; both relate to a commodity (oil, manna) the people must provide or receive. The manna sustained the people's bodies in the wilderness; the lamp sustains their worship. Together they frame the wilderness period as a time when God's provision and the people's worship are perpetually visible.

*Third,* the court's screen at the east (27:13–16) is the only entrance. East-facing doors are common in ANE temples, but the biblical valence is specific: Eden faces east (Genesis 2:8), and the cherubim are placed east of the garden after the expulsion (Genesis 3:24). The tabernacle's east entrance, screened by embroidered cloth *without* cherubim (those appear inside), reverses the expulsion step by step — the worshipper approaches from the east, past gate and altar, into the Holy Place. Ezekiel 43:1–4 takes it further: YHWH returns to the future temple by the eastern gate. Revelation 21 abolishes the temple while multiplying gates. The east-facing entrance remembers what was lost and promises what is coming.
