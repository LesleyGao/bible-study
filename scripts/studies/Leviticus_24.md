# Leviticus 24: The Light That Must Not Go Out

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Leviticus 24 sits in the Holiness Code (Lev 17–26) — H material on Israel Knohl's analysis, writing in continuity and supplement to the older Priestly (P) stratum. Julius Wellhausen's post-exilic dating of P generally was answered by Menahem Haran and Avi Hurvitz's linguistic arguments for a pre-exilic core; Jacob Milgrom's three-volume Anchor Bible *Leviticus* (1991–2001) — whose third volume treats chs. 23–27 at greatest length — dates H to a late pre-exilic Judahite priestly circle, probably Hezekian, absorbing prophetic ethics. This chapter is unusual within the code because it *combines* legal material (the perpetual lamp and showbread, vv. 1–9; the casuistic lex talionis and associated laws, vv. 15–22) with a narrative (vv. 10–16, 23) — the only sustained narrative in Leviticus apart from the Nadab and Abihu account in ch. 10. That structural oddity — legal-narrative-legal sandwich — has been read variously: Milgrom treats it as H's deliberate *halakic-aggadic* pairing, case law framed by a precipitating incident; Baruch Levine reads it as an editorial insertion that reactivates Sinai-camp time in the middle of the Holiness Code's otherwise timeless framing; Mary Douglas (*Leviticus as Literature*) reads the chapter as a chiastic pivot at the book's center of gravity.

The material culture of the first nine verses is the tabernacle's interior furniture. The *mĕnôrāh* — the seven-branched lampstand of pure gold described in Ex 25:31–40 — required *šemen zayit zāḵ kātît* (v. 2), "pure oil, beaten olive." "Beaten" is technical: olives crushed in a stone mortar rather than pressed, yielding the first, clearest, highest-grade oil. Archaeologically, olive-oil production at Tel Miqne-Ekron (excavated by Seymour Gitin and Trude Dothan) has yielded the world's largest known concentration of ancient olive presses — more than 115 installations from the seventh century BC. The best oil was for the sanctuary; lower grades were for domestic use. The lamp was to burn "from evening to morning" *tāmîd* (v. 3), a keyword used four times in four verses. The *šulḥān happānîm* (table of the presence) held the *leḥem happānîm* (bread of the presence, vv. 5–9) — twelve loaves in two rows of six, two-tenths of an ephah per loaf, with frankincense as the *ʾazkārāh* (memorial portion) burned when the bread was replaced every Sabbath. The old loaves were eaten by the priests in a holy place. Mishnah Menachot 11 preserves extensive detail on the loaves' shape (rectangular, folded like a *qereš* according to Abba Saul in Menachot 94a), their arrangement, and the frankincense bowls.

The narrative of vv. 10–16, 23 is a rare named-episode in Leviticus. The blasphemer is identified precisely: "an Israelite woman's son, whose father was an Egyptian" (v. 10). His mother is named — *Shelomith bat-Dibri*, of the tribe of Dan (v. 11). This is extraordinary. The Torah names very few women outside genealogies and patriarchal narratives; to name her here, and to mark her Danite tribal affiliation, is a deliberate identifier. Rashi (drawing on Leviticus Rabbah 32:5) reads her name ironically — *Shelomith* from *šālôm* ("peace," she greeted everyone too freely) and *Dibri* from *dibrāh* ("speech," she talked too much) — a midrashic etymology that converts her into the cautionary-tale mother. But Milgrom, Baruch Levine, and contemporary feminist readers (Athalya Brenner, Tikva Frymer-Kensky, Judith Plaskow) emphasize the social reality: she was a Danite woman who had been in sexual/marital union with an Egyptian man, and her son is the text's test case for the legal standing of the *gēr* and of mixed-heritage Israelites. The fact that the son "went out among the people of Israel" (v. 10) — a phrase Rashi reads as a contested status claim — indicates genealogical liminality. That liminality is answered not by exclusion but by equalization: the same law binds native and sojourner (v. 22).

The political setting is a camp organized around a central sanctuary, with a young legal system still being formed through adjudication. The verb *yāṣāʾ* ("went out," v. 10) and the incident's placement just after the festival calendar of ch. 23 suggest (per Milgrom) that the precipitating quarrel may have involved the Sabbath or a festival privilege — some commentators propose the dispute was about encampment position (Dan's tribe had a fixed place; a mixed-heritage man's place was uncertain). The Torah preserves the dispute's outcome, not its specific cause.

ANE parallels. The perpetual lamp has analogues in Mesopotamian temple cultus, where lamps burned before divine statues (the *nūr-ilī* tradition; cf. James Hoffmeier's comparative work). The showbread has precise parallels: Egyptian temple offering-tables (at Karnak and Medinet Habu) bore rows of bread for the gods, refreshed daily; Mesopotamian temples offered *akal pāni ilī* ("bread of the gods' presence"), often twelve loaves, with tables in the cella. Blasphemy against the divine name was capital across the ancient Near East — Ugaritic, Hittite, Babylonian, and Egyptian texts all protect the divine name. Lex talionis (the principle of reciprocal justice) appears famously in Hammurabi's Code §§196–200 ("If a man has destroyed the eye of a free man, they shall destroy his eye… tooth for tooth") — strikingly close in wording to Lev 24:19–20, though with class-differentiated application (Hammurabi's code scales penalties by social class; Lev 24 explicitly flattens class, binding native and sojourner alike, v. 22).

Textual and manuscript evidence. 4QLev-b preserves portions of the lex talionis passage; 11QpaleoLev fragments include chs. 22–27 in paleo-Hebrew, signaling special priestly prestige. The LXX renders *hannāqēb ʾet-šēm YHWH* (v. 16) as *ho onomazōn to onoma kyriou* — "the one who names the name of the Lord" — a translation that unlocks the rabbinic development: the issue is not only cursing but naming.

Key Hebrew terms: *nēr tāmîd* / נֵר תָּמִיד (continual lamp); *leḥem happānîm* / לֶחֶם הַפָּנִים (bread of the presence, lit. "bread of the face"); *nāqab* / נָקַב (to pierce, puncture, explicitly pronounce — the root of the blasphemer's offense at v. 16); *ʾaššer-qillēl* / אֲשֶׁר-קִלֵּל (the one who cursed, v. 14); *šeber taḥat šeber* / שֶׁבֶר תַּחַת שֶׁבֶר (fracture for fracture, v. 20).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter's genre is genuinely mixed, which is the point. It opens with *legal* material (vv. 1–9) in technical sanctuary prose, continues with a *narrative* (vv. 10–14, 23) that precipitates a judicial ruling, and closes with *casuistic-apodictic* law (vv. 15–22). Brevard Childs, Milgrom, and Mary Douglas all recognize that this is a structural center of the book: legal material framed by narrative instantiation — a pattern familiar from Num 9:6–14 (Passover for the unclean), Num 15:32–36 (Sabbath-breaking man), Num 27:1–11 (daughters of Zelophehad). The Torah repeatedly creates law by *responding to a case*, and Lev 24 is the priestly-code's contribution to that jurisprudence.

Literary devices. The refrain *tāmîd* ("continually") binds vv. 2–4 into a single litany. The chapter's center is the pronouncing of the Name — and yet the Name is never pronounced in the text. The blasphemer "blasphemed the Name" (v. 11), is executed for blaspheming "the Name" (v. 16), and the Hebrew verb *nāqab* literally means "to pierce" or "to designate explicitly" — Milgrom argues the crime is not merely abusive cursing but *pronouncing* the Tetragrammaton in a cursing context. That philology launches the rabbinic and (later) modern Jewish practice of never pronouncing YHWH — by the Second Temple period the Name was spoken only by the high priest on Yom Kippur (Mishnah Yoma 6:2); in the diaspora *ʾădōnāy* replaced it; eventually even *ʾădōnāy* was replaced in casual speech by *hā-šēm* ("the Name"). The chapter is the source-text for that entire development.

The lex talionis formulation (v. 20) — "fracture for fracture, eye for eye, tooth for tooth" — is one of three such formulations in the Torah (Ex 21:23–25, Deut 19:21, and here). In each, the formula sits inside a context that qualifies its application. In Lev 24, the verses immediately preceding and following (vv. 18, 21) speak of animals: *nepeš taḥat nāpeš*, life for life, where the context is clearly compensation for damaged property (v. 18: "he shall make it good"; v. 21: same). The Mishnah (Bava Kamma 8:1) and the Bavli (Bava Kamma 83b–84a) use precisely this contextual reading to argue that the eye-for-eye formula refers to *monetary compensation calibrated to the injury's severity*, not to literal bodily mutilation. This reading is not a rabbinic invention contradicting the text; it is a close reading of how Lev 24:18–21 places the talionic formula inside a commercial-damages frame.

Textual variants. MT v. 11's *hannāqēb* is well-preserved in 4QLev-b. LXX's *onomazōn* (translating *nāqab* as "pronouncing") is the interpretive key for later Jewish tradition. The Samaritan Pentateuch preserves minor orthographic differences. The casuistic lex talionis at vv. 17–22 has particularly stable manuscript transmission — a sign of its jurisprudential centrality.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Philo's *Life of Moses* 2.193–208 recounts the blasphemer narrative and treats it as a sober test of legal egalitarianism — the mixed-heritage son faces the same law as the native. Josephus (*Antiquities* 4.202) summarizes the blasphemy law briefly. The Mishnah tractate Sanhedrin 7:5 gives the classical procedure for blasphemy trials: the principal witness is asked to repeat the exact words; upon hearing them, the judges rend their garments; the offender is stoned. Mishnah Yoma 6:2 preserves the high-priestly pronunciation of the Name on Yom Kippur, the only context in which the Name was spoken in the Second Temple. Mishnah Menachot 11 details the bread of the presence. Sifra on Leviticus 24 is the tannaitic midrash, unpacking each clause.

Bavli Sanhedrin 55b–56a develops the blasphemer's case into the foundational text for the law of *šēm hamĕpōrāš* — the explicit Name — and into one of the Seven Noahide Laws (blasphemy against God binds all humanity, not only Israel). Bavli Bava Kamma 83b–84a develops the monetary reading of the eye-for-eye law across several pages, with R. Dosetai ben Yehudah, R. Shimon bar Yohai, R. Eliezer, and others offering overlapping arguments. The rabbinic consensus is unambiguous: monetary compensation is what the Torah requires.

Medieval: Rashi reads the blasphemer narrative richly: the midrash on Shelomith's name (noted above), the blasphemer's actual words (classically held to be unpronounceable), and the legal principle that the congregation must lay hands on the offender's head to remove the curse's lingering force (v. 14). Ibn Ezra gives the philological reading of *nāqab*. Ramban reads the chapter as a meditation on the weight of divine speech and human reciprocation — the light that must burn *tāmîd*, the bread that must be set *tāmîd*, and the name that must never be profaned. Maimonides gives the chapter its most systematic treatment: *Hilchot Avodat Kochavim* (laws of idolatry and blasphemy), *Hilchot Qiddush Hashem* (laws of sanctifying the Name), *Hilchot Shabbat* on the showbread ritual's Sabbath timing, and *Hilchot Chovel u-Mazzik* on tort law — where the monetary reading of lex talionis is codified in the halakhic mainstream. Sforno reads the blasphemer narrative as a warning against the unmoored tongue.

Modern Jewish: Milgrom's commentary is, again, foundational. Baruch Levine emphasizes the chapter's jurisprudential centrality. Jacob Neusner on Sanhedrin traces the rabbinic procedural development. Nehama Leibowitz's *Studies in Leviticus* has a characteristically searching treatment of the blasphemer's mixed heritage. Jonathan Sacks reads the chapter as a defense of the sacred in a disenchanted world. Avivah Zornberg's psychological-literary readings and Tikva Frymer-Kensky's feminist engagements extend the analysis of Shelomith's role. Yeshayahu Leibowitz takes the chapter as emblematic of Judaism's refusal to subordinate divine *kavod* (glory/name) to any human consideration.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Leviticus* reads the perpetual lamp christologically (Christ as the light) and the showbread eucharistically (twelve loaves for the twelve tribes, fulfilled in the twelve apostles). Jerome in his Hebrew commentary debates the blasphemer's exact name. Augustine treats the blasphemy law in *Contra Faustum* and various sermons, arguing for its moral (if not ceremonial) continuity — blasphemy remains a capital spiritual offense even if not a capital civil one. Chrysostom in his Leviticus homilies reads the narrative as a warning about untrained tongues in a community.

Medieval: Aquinas in *ST* II-II q. 13 (on blasphemy) draws heavily on Lev 24, classifying blasphemy as the gravest sin against God under the First Commandment; *ST* II-II q. 64 a. 4 on lex talionis defends its deep justice while arguing that Christian societies, under Matt 5, are to transform it into proportionate civil justice rather than literal reciprocation. Bonaventure and Albert extend. The medieval canon law (*Decretum Gratiani*) absorbs the blasphemy laws into ecclesiastical discipline; secular law (*Corpus iuris civilis*'s treatment in Justinian Novellas 77 and 141, widely influential in medieval codes) makes blasphemy a capital civil offense. Beatus of Liébana and the Glossa Ordinaria hand on a stable medieval reading.

Reformation: Luther treats blasphemy as a grave sin but distinguishes — as in the Sabbath — between its moral core and its Levitical civil penalty. Calvin's approach at Geneva is the famous difficult case: his defense of the execution of Michael Servetus in 1553, for anti-Trinitarian writings judged blasphemous, cited Lev 24:14–16 directly (Calvin's *Defensio orthodoxae fidei* [1554] explicitly argues for civil capital punishment of blasphemers from this text). The episode is a dark chapter in the reception history. The English Blasphemy Act of 1697 and similar laws across Protestant Europe drew on Lev 24. Wesley in his *Explanatory Notes* treats the narrative as a warning but not as binding civil law.

Critical modern: Wellhausen, Noth, and Baentsch offered source-critical readings distinguishing narrative and legal strata; Childs's canonical approach reads them together; Gordon Wenham's NICOT provides the accessible Protestant commentary. John Hartley's WBC, Jay Sklar's ZECOT, and Jacob Milgrom's AB are the scholarly standards. L. Michael Morales (*Who Shall Ascend the Mountain of the Lord?*) reads the chapter as the structural center of the Torah's Sinai-to-tabernacle ascent. Contemporary legal-historical work (Bernard Jackson's *Wisdom-Laws*, Joseph Fleishman's studies of talionic law) places Lev 24 in comparative ANE frame. Contemporary debates over blasphemy laws in Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, Indonesia, Nigeria, and (until recently) parts of Europe inevitably return to this text; Joseph Lerner's and Paul Marshall's studies of modern blasphemy laws track both the continuities and discontinuities.

**Where they converge and diverge.** Jewish and Christian traditions both take the perpetual lamp and showbread as deep symbols of divine presence and covenantal sustenance — Jewish tradition in the ongoing halakhah of Shabbat candles and challah (which, through a complex trajectory, echoes the showbread), Christian tradition in the sanctuary lamp before the tabernacle in Catholic and Orthodox churches and in eucharistic theology. On blasphemy, both traditions affirm the gravity of the offense but disagree on civil consequences: Judaism held to a capital procedure until the temple's destruction ended the Sanhedrin's capital jurisdiction, after which blasphemy was ritually and socially sanctioned but not civilly executed; Christianity under Constantine and then medieval Christendom extended blasphemy law into civil capital punishment, a practice the Reformation inherited and that modern liberal democracy has largely abandoned. Jesus's Sermon on the Mount teaching "you have heard that it was said, 'An eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth'" (Matt 5:38–42) does not contradict the rabbinic monetary reading — he presupposes it and intensifies it toward non-retaliation. Modern Christian readers increasingly recognize this: Jesus is not overturning a literal biblical command; he is extending a jurisprudentially domesticated principle in the direction of love-of-enemy.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What kind of law is blasphemy law in a society where the state is not the covenant community?** Position A (Calvin at Geneva, medieval Christendom, traditional Islamic jurisprudence on *sabb*) says civil capital blasphemy laws remain appropriate in a society ordered under God. Position B (the American founding, modern liberal democracy, much contemporary Christian political theology including Oliver O'Donovan's qualified version in *Desire of the Nations*) says that civil blasphemy laws fail to respect the distinction between the covenant community and the civic community; blasphemy is a grave spiritual offense to be addressed within faith communities, not by the state's sword. Position C (Orthodox Judaism since the end of the Sanhedrin, some Reformed theology, much Catholic social teaching since *Dignitatis Humanae*) says the severity of the offense remains but its capital enforcement ceased with the covenantal political order that grounded it; the text's gravity is preserved by moral formation and community discipline rather than by execution. The least intuitive steelman is C's claim that the *severity* of Lev 24 is fully maintained when the *penalty structure* changes — that recognizing blasphemy as a grave spiritual harm can be more demanding, not less, than tolerating it as an acceptable civil liberty. *Where this leaves us:* modern liberal tolerance of blasphemy is not, on any honest reading, what Lev 24 commands. What we do with that recognition depends on whether we think the civic and covenant communities should be distinguished, and what we think the state's proper sphere is. Christians who take the Geneva line owe a reckoning with Servetus; Christians who take the tolerationist line owe an account of what it means for the gospel to claim the public square.

**Tension 2: Does the eye-for-eye formula (v. 20) authorize retaliation, require exact reciprocity, or prescribe proportionate monetary compensation?** Position A (early modern literalists, some Sadducean readings reconstructed from rabbinic opponents) reads the formula literally — the court is to inflict the same injury. Position B (the rabbinic mainline from tannaim forward, Maimonides, modern Orthodox Jewish jurisprudence) reads it as monetary compensation calibrated by the injury, on contextual grounds: vv. 18 and 21 clearly use the same *taḥat* formulation for animal-damages compensation, and the Mishnah and Talmud build elaborate valuation procedures (damage, pain, medical costs, loss of work, embarrassment — the five categories of Bava Kamma 8). Position C (Aquinas, many Reformation thinkers, much modern Christian ethics) reads the formula as a juridical principle of proportionate justice — neither literal mutilation nor simple monetary substitution, but the demand that punishment fit the crime, now mediated through civil legal systems. The least intuitive steelman is B: the Jewish reading is often caricatured by Christian interpreters as a rabbinic softening of a hard text, when in fact it is a careful reading of the text's own internal signals (vv. 18 and 21 explicitly use monetary language in identical constructions). *Where this leaves us:* Jesus's Sermon on the Mount intensifies the already-domesticated rabbinic reading toward non-retaliation; it does not overturn a barbaric literalism that never existed in practice. The Christian cliche of "Old Testament eye-for-eye vengeance vs. New Testament love-your-enemy mercy" misreads both testaments.

*Unresolved question:* Why does the Holiness Code locate its one sustained narrative — and its sole legal incorporation of a named woman (Shelomith bat-Dibri) — precisely at the intersection of mixed heritage and blasphemy? Is the text registering an anxiety about the theological consequences of genealogical mixing (an anxiety that would later feed into post-exilic Ezra-Nehemiah controversies), or is it making the opposite point — that the law applies equally to the sojourner and the native (v. 22), and that no mixed-heritage Israelite is beyond its reach or beyond its protection? The chapter seems to work against itself, and that tension is not obviously resolved.

## The Thread

1. The *leḥem happānîm* ("bread of the face/presence") of vv. 5–9 is the same bread David takes from the priest Ahimelech at Nob in 1 Sam 21:1–6 — a passage Jesus cites in Mark 2:25–28 to defend his disciples' Sabbath grain-plucking. Jesus's argument presupposes Lev 24:8–9 (the bread is holy, reserved for priests) and reads it against Davidic precedent. The Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath because the priestly table, and the bread on it, ultimately serve human life rather than ritual purity.

2. The perpetual lamp *nēr tāmîd* is echoed in the lampstand vision of Zechariah 4 (the two olive trees feeding the seven-lamp menorah) and then explicitly in Revelation 1:12–20, where the seven lampstands are the seven churches and Christ walks among them. The "light that must not go out" migrates from tabernacle to eschatological ecclesiology.

3. Shelomith bat-Dibri of the tribe of Dan (v. 11) is one of very few women named in Leviticus. The Danite identification is not incidental: Dan is the tribe curiously missing from the list of sealed tribes in Rev 7:4–8 (Manasseh replaces Dan), a gap patristic and medieval Christian exegesis famously read against the Antichrist tradition (Irenaeus, *Adversus Haereses* 5.30.2; Hippolytus, *De Antichristo*). Whether or not one accepts that trajectory, the textual marking of Shelomith as a Danite is a thread the tradition has pulled on for two thousand years.

4. The lex talionis closes with "you shall have the same rule for the sojourner and for the native, for I am the LORD your God" (v. 22). That refrain — one law for native and sojourner — runs through the Holiness Code (Lev 19:33–34, Lev 24:22, Num 15:15–16) and surfaces in the Gospels as the logic behind the Gentile mission: if one law binds native and sojourner, then the gospel's address to "every tribe and language and people and nation" (Rev 5:9) is not an innovation but a Leviticus-rooted principle finding its full scope.
