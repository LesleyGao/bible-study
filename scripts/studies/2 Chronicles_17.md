# 2 Chronicles 17 — Jehoshaphat's Reform and the Torah-Teaching Tour

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

With chapter 17 the Chronicler turns to one of his favored subjects: Jehoshaphat, son of Asa, fourth king of Judah after the division. The Chronicler — anonymous, post-exilic, writing in the Persian period for a community without a king and with a small Second Temple — gives Jehoshaphat four full chapters (17–20), more sustained narrative space than any king after Solomon and before Hezekiah. The expansion is theologically deliberate. Sara Japhet, Hugh Williamson, Gary Knoppers, Steven McKenzie, Ralph Klein, Mark Boda, and Louis Jonker all note the same pattern: the Chronicler treats Jehoshaphat as the post-exilic community's model southern king, with reform, teaching, military strength, and trust-in-Yahweh victory all concentrated in his reign.

The synoptic relationship to Kings is dramatic. The parallel material in **1 Kings 22** focuses primarily on Ahab and the doomed expedition to Ramoth-gilead, narrating Jehoshaphat through the northern lens. The Kings narrative gives Jehoshaphat himself only a brief summary in 1 Kings 22:41–50 — eight verses — and treats him largely as Ahab's southern ally. **Chapter 17 of Chronicles is almost entirely unique to the Chronicler**: the religious reforms (vv. 1–6, 10), the *Torah-teaching tour* of Judah by officials, Levites, and priests (vv. 7–9), the tribute from Philistines and Arabians (vv. 10–11), the building program (vv. 12–13), and the elaborate military muster (vv. 14–19) all derive from Chronicler tradition or composition. Williamson's commentary makes the case strongly: where Kings narrates Jehoshaphat as ally to a northern apostate, Chronicles narrates Jehoshaphat as a faithful southern reformer whose alliance with Ahab in chapter 18 will be presented as failure rather than the central frame.

The pivotal Hebrew vocabulary of *seeking* returns in modified form. Verses 3–4: *he did not seek* (לֹא דָרַשׁ, *lōʾ ḏāraš*) the **Baalim** (הַבְּעָלִים, plural of *Baʿal*, "lord"), *but sought* (כִּי דָרַשׁ, *kî ḏāraš*) the God of his father. The Chronicler is reusing the Asa-cycle vocabulary verbatim. The Baalim — local Canaanite manifestations of the storm god — were the recurring temptation of northern kings; Jehoshaphat's refusal is framed as not adopting the *practices of Israel* (i.e., the northern kingdom under the Omrides). The verb **דָּרַשׁ** (*dāraš*) is now applied not just to seeking versus forsaking Yahweh, but to seeking Yahweh *versus seeking the Baalim* — a specifying refinement that points the reader toward chapter 18, where Jehoshaphat will encounter Ahab and his Baal-influenced court.

The chapter also introduces the Hebrew **יָשָׁר** (*yāšār*, "upright, straight"). Verse 3's note that Jehoshaphat *walked in the earlier ways of his father David* uses the Chronicler's standard idiom of *Davidic uprightness* — the qualitative measure against which kings are evaluated. The textual witness here is interesting: the MT reads *the earlier ways of his father David*, which the ESV follows; some manuscripts and the LXX read simply *the ways of his father David*, omitting "earlier" (Hebrew **הָרִאשֹׁנִים**, *hāriʾšōnîm*). Williamson and Japhet read the longer text as deliberate Chronicler nuance: David's *earlier* ways, before the Bathsheba episode. The Chronicler is signaling that even David is held to a discriminating standard.

The most historically arresting feature of the chapter is the **Torah-teaching tour** of verses 7–9. *In the third year of his reign* — that is, after Jehoshaphat had consolidated his throne — he sends a delegation through *all the cities of Judah*, *having the Book of the Law of the LORD with them* (וְעִמָּהֶם סֵפֶר תּוֹרַת יְהוָה, *wəʿimmāhem sēp̄er tôraṯ Yahweh*). The delegation includes five royal officials (**שָׂרִים**, *śārîm*), nine Levites, and two priests. The structure is significant: the king's officials lead, the Levites supply the teaching corps, and the priests authorize. The Hebrew **לְלַמֵּד** (*ləlammēḏ*, "to teach") is the standard Deuteronomic verb for catechetical instruction (cf. Deut 4:1; 6:1; 11:19).

This *Torah-teaching tour* is **unique to Chronicles** — there is no parallel anywhere else in the Hebrew Bible. Mainstream-critical scholarship (Williamson, Japhet, Knoppers, McKenzie, Klein) has read this in two main ways. (1) **The retro-projection reading**: the Chronicler is projecting back onto Jehoshaphat's reign the post-exilic Levitical claim to teaching authority that we see institutionalized in Ezra-Nehemiah (Ezra 7:10; Neh 8:7–9, where Levites *teach the people the Law*). On this reading the tour is a Chronicler construction that legitimates the role Levites and officials played in his own day. (2) **The early-tradition reading**: Jehoshaphat's reign may have included real reforms whose echo the Chronicler preserved; the structural specifics (officials + Levites + priests) reflect a plausible early monarchic arrangement, and the tradition is older than its retelling. The two readings are not mutually exclusive — most current scholarship reads the passage as Chronicler retelling of plausible-but-recast tradition. Either way, what the post-exilic audience would have heard is unmistakable: lay officials, Levites, and priests can and should teach Torah throughout the land, with the king's authorization.

The numbers in the military muster (vv. 14–19) — over a million men in the standing forces alone — follow the Chronicler's usual pattern. Mainstream scholarship treats them as theological hyperbole representing comprehensive readiness; conservative *unit-translation* approaches (Kitchen, Merrill) propose lower realistic figures. The structural point is that Judah, Benjamin, and the priestly tribe are arrayed in proper order with named commanders — a portrait of organized faithfulness.

## Lens 2 — Literature

The chapter is built as a four-panel exposition of what makes a faithful reign: religious reform (vv. 1–6), Torah teaching (vv. 7–9), international respect with tribute (vv. 10–11), and military strength (vv. 12–19). The four panels are arranged so that each one is causally tied to the preceding: reform produces teaching; teaching produces respect from neighbors; respect produces tribute and prosperity; prosperity enables military strength. The literary geometry teaches that *every dimension of a king's flourishing* — spiritual, educational, diplomatic, military — *flows from the reform*.

Verse 6's *his heart was courageous in the ways of the LORD* (וַיִּגְבַּהּ לִבּוֹ בְּדַרְכֵי יְהוָה, *wayyiḡbah libbô bəḏarḵê Yahweh*) uses the verb **גָּבַהּ** (*gāḇah*, "to be high, exalted, lifted up"). In other contexts this verb is negative (Hezekiah's heart was *lifted up* in pride, 2 Chr 32:25); here the Chronicler reclaims the verb for positive *courageous loftiness in Yahweh's ways*. The literary note is careful: Jehoshaphat's elevation is *bəḏarḵê Yahweh*, *in the ways of Yahweh*, not in himself.

The Torah-teaching scene is set in the *third year* — a Chronicler signature for the moment a reign has stabilized and reform-of-substance can begin. The list of names in vv. 7–9 has a distinctive cadence: five officials (Ben-hail, Obadiah, Zechariah, Nethanel, Micaiah), then *with them* the Levites (nine names), then *with these Levites* the priests (Elishama and Jehoram). The literary recursion — *with them*, *with these* — performs the chain of authorization: officials with Levites, Levites with priests, all together with the *Book of the Law*. The structure stages a vision of integrated religious-civil teaching authority.

The geographic comprehensiveness — *they went about through all the cities of Judah and taught among the people* (וַיְלַמְּדוּ בִּיהוּדָה, *wayəlammədû bîhûḏâ*) — uses the *piel* of *lāmaḏ*, the intensive form for sustained, repeated teaching. The Chronicler is depicting saturation, not occasional instruction.

Verses 10–11's tribute scene answers the *fear-of-the-LORD* (פַּחַד יְהוָה, *paḥaḏ Yahweh*) that fell on neighboring kingdoms. **Philistines** bring presents and silver; **Arabians** bring 7,700 rams and 7,700 goats. The doubled seven is a Chronicler signature for theological completeness (cf. the seven-times-seven in 15:11). The international respect is presented as the natural consequence of internal faithfulness — the Chronicler is teaching that domestic spiritual order produces external geopolitical credibility.

The military muster in vv. 14–19 is one of the most detailed in the book. Three Judahite commanders (Adnah, Jehohanan, Amasiah) and two Benjaminite commanders (Eliada, Jehozabad) are named with their forces. **Amasiah** is described with a striking phrase — **הַמִּתְנַדֵּב לַיהוָה** (*hammiṯnaddēḇ laYahweh*, "the one who freely offered himself for the service of the LORD"). The verb **נָדַב** (*nāḏaḇ*, "to volunteer freely") is the same root that names the freewill offering in Levitical worship; Amasiah's volunteerism makes him a single named exemplar of the chapter's larger theology. Even in the military structure, the principle is *willing service to Yahweh*.

The chapter ends with the careful note that the Jerusalem-stationed forces were *besides* those in the fortified cities — a literary signal that everything described is supplementary to the underlying defense network. Jehoshaphat is fully prepared, fully equipped, fully arrayed, and the chapter's point is that all of this rests on the reform, the teaching, and the seeking that opened the chapter.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Within the Hebrew Bible**, the *Torah-teaching tour* tradition has a thin canonical footprint outside Chronicles but a dense post-exilic resonance. The closest parallel is **Nehemiah 8**, where Ezra and the Levites read and teach the Torah to the assembled people. The Chronicler's depiction of officials, Levites, and priests teaching together with the Book of the Law in hand reads as a precedent for the Ezra-Nehemiah event — the post-exilic reader would have recognized the pattern.

In **Jewish reception**, the verse-list in 17:7–9 became a touchstone for the rabbinic tradition of *Talmud Torah* (Torah study) and the institution of the **bet midrash** (study house). The medieval Tosafists (b. Sukkah 49a's discussion of the *seventy elders*) and the Rambam in his discussion of the obligations of teachers (*Mishneh Torah*, Hilkhot Talmud Torah) draw on the principle that lay teaching of Torah is not only permitted but commanded. The Chronicler's tableau of officials-with-Levites-with-priests teaching together helps shape the rabbinic vision of stratified but integrated teaching authority.

In **Christian reception**, the Torah-teaching tour has frequently been invoked as a precedent for Reformation-era catechetical programs. Calvin's *Institutes* and his church orders for Geneva drew explicit parallels with reform-era kings sending teachers throughout their realms; Bucer in Strasbourg and Zwingli in Zurich made similar moves. The Catholic Counter-Reformation, in the Catechism of the Council of Trent (1566) and the establishment of seminaries under Trent's reforms, draws implicitly on the same pattern — though without the lay-official component that distinguishes the Chronicler's vision. **Anabaptist and Reformed traditions** have made particular use of Jehoshaphat's lay-official teachers (the *śārîm* of v. 7) as scriptural warrant for the priesthood of all believers, the right and duty of laypeople to teach Scripture, and the catechetical responsibility of magistrates and parents.

The contemporary Christian application — adult catechesis, lay leadership in Bible study, ministerial education, missionary teaching — draws directly on the Chronicler's vision. **Three positions** on the contemporary application: (1) the *integrated-teaching* reading (mainstream Catholic, Lutheran, Anglican, Reformed) — official, ordained, and lay teachers together, each with proper authority, all anchored to the Scripture itself; (2) the *priesthood-of-all-believers* reading (Baptist, free-church, evangelical) — the pattern emphasizes lay teaching authority; (3) the *strict-clerical* reading (older Catholic and Orthodox tradition pre-Vatican II) — reserves Scripture interpretation primarily to the clergy. Steelman of position 1: the Chronicler's text deliberately includes officials, Levites, and priests together; no single authority claim exhausts the pattern. Steelman of position 2: the *officials* lead the list, suggesting the Chronicler is emphasizing the lay component as a deliberate move beyond a clerical-only model.

The New Testament does not quote 2 Chronicles 17 directly. But the apostolic teaching tradition (Acts 2:42, *they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching*; 1 Tim 3:2 and Tit 1:9 on the elder/overseer's teaching duty; Heb 5:12, on every believer's growth into teaching maturity) inhabits the same theological space the Chronicler opens here.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

The Chronicler's intent in this chapter is to model what comprehensive faithful kingship looks like. Religious reform alone is not enough; a faithful king must also institute Torah teaching across the land. Teaching alone is not enough; a faithful king must also build defenses and maintain readiness. Defense alone is not enough; a faithful king must rest the entire structure on *seeking the God of his father and not the Baalim*. The chapter is the Chronicler's most integrated portrait of a multi-dimensional reign.

But the chapter also raises a real question that has to be sat with.

**Tension: the Torah-teaching tour and post-exilic Levitical authority.** The chapter is the only Old Testament account of organized royal Torah-teaching in the pre-exilic period. The mainstream-critical reading is that the Chronicler has retro-projected post-exilic Levitical claims onto Jehoshaphat's reign. **Three positions on what to do with this.**

(1) The *reverent-historical* reading (older evangelical commentary, some conservative scholarship) takes the chapter at face value: a real Jehoshaphat ordered a real Torah-teaching tour, and the Chronicler is preserving genuine pre-exilic tradition that Kings happened not to record. The argument for this position: Chronicles often preserves details (geographical, genealogical, military) that prove correct against external evidence; arguing from absence in Kings is risky; Jehoshaphat's reform program in chapter 17 is consistent with what we know about the early ninth-century BCE Judahite court. Steelman: the burden of proof for retro-projection arguments is real, and the argument-from-silence (Kings doesn't mention it, so it didn't happen) is methodologically weak.

(2) The *retro-projection* reading (mainstream-critical: Williamson, Japhet, Knoppers, McKenzie) takes the chapter as Chronicler composition or substantial reshaping. The Chronicler is reading the Levitical-teaching role of his own day back into Jehoshaphat's reign because the role needed an early monarchic prototype. The argument: Chronicles shows clear patterns of Levitical-elevation across many narratives; Ezra-Nehemiah's organized Levitical teaching has no clear pre-exilic monarchic precedent; the precise composition of officials-Levites-priests in 17:7–9 fits post-exilic religious organization more cleanly than pre-exilic. Steelman: the Chronicler is doing what every theological historian does — reading the past through the lens of the present, with theological purpose. He is not lying; he is interpreting.

(3) The *both-and* reading (Boda, Jonker, more recent moderate scholarship) takes the chapter as containing real pre-exilic memory shaped by post-exilic theological concern. There may have been royal religious reform under Jehoshaphat — the Kings tradition itself preserves indirect hints — but the specific structure (officials-with-Levites-with-priests teaching together) reflects post-exilic ordering retro-applied to fit. Steelman: this reading honors both the Chronicler's interest in preserving older tradition and his obvious theological shaping; it does not require choosing between the two.

The contemporary Christian application is largely independent of which position one takes on the historical question. Whether the Torah-teaching tour was pre-exilic or post-exilic in form, the theological vision — integrated lay-Levitical-priestly teaching of Scripture, geographically saturating the community, anchored by *the Book of the Law* — has been read as instructive across two millennia of Jewish and Christian practice.

The contemporary Jewish application similarly transcends the historical question. The rabbinic and post-rabbinic emphasis on Torah study at every level of society, in every place where Jewish life is found, in every relationship (father to son, teacher to student, master to apprentice), inhabits the theological space the chapter opens.

**Where this leaves us.** The chapter portrays a king whose faithfulness is comprehensive: spiritual orientation, educational program, diplomatic posture, military readiness, all knit together. The integration is the chapter's theological argument. Reform without teaching becomes shallow. Teaching without reform becomes hypocrisy. Defense without spiritual orientation becomes paranoia. Spiritual orientation without practical structures becomes private piety. Jehoshaphat is the Chronicler's case study of how all four hold together. Chapter 18 will show how easily the structure can be tested by a single political alliance — but chapter 17 first builds the structure in full.

**Closing question.** If the chapter's vision is that *teaching the Book of the Law throughout the cities of Judah* was as central to faithful kingship as religious reform, military strength, and diplomatic respect, what does it ask of communities, families, and individuals today about the place of patient, geographically saturating, integrated teaching of Scripture — and what would it look like to send out *officials with Levites with priests* in one's own life and community?

## The Thread

The Torah-teaching tour — officials, Levites, and priests carrying the Book of the Law through every city of Judah — is the chapter's distinctive image and its lasting gift to Jewish and Christian tradition. The Chronicler's vision of integrated, geographically saturating, lay-and-clerical Scripture teaching has shaped two millennia of practice, whatever one decides about whether the tour was pre-exilic memory or post-exilic projection.
