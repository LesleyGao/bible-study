# Isaiah 36 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Isaiah 36 opens what scholars call the "historical appendix" of First Isaiah — chapters 36-39, four chapters of prose narrative that interrupt the prophetic poetry of chapters 1-35 and form a literary bridge to the exilic consolation of Second Isaiah (chapters 40-66). The shift from poetry to prose is unmistakable; readers move from the dense oracular Hebrew of *mashal* and visionary speech into the workmanlike narrative cadence of court history. These four chapters parallel **2 Kings 18:13-20:19** almost verbatim, raising one of the central source-critical questions in the prophetic books: which version came first?

The mainstream-critical consensus, articulated by Hans Wildberger, Joseph Blenkinsopp, and John Goldingay, is that the Isaianic version is **borrowed from the Deuteronomistic History** in 2 Kings, with redactional adjustments that fit the material into Isaiah's prophetic frame. Some scholars — Klaus Seitz prominent among them — have argued the opposite direction, that 2 Kings drew from an earlier Isaianic tradition. Brevard Childs's canonical reading, influential in evangelical and confessional circles, brackets the question of priority and instead reads the chapters as a deliberate hinge: chapters 36-37 recall the Assyrian crisis as Yahweh's deliverance, while chapters 38-39 prefigure the Babylonian exile that Second Isaiah will address from the other side. Either way, Isaiah 36-39 functions as a **historical bridge** between First Isaiah's 8th-century Assyrian-crisis poetry and Second Isaiah's 6th-century exilic consolation.

The chapter is dated "in the fourteenth year of King Hezekiah" — almost certainly **701 BCE**, the year of Sennacherib's third campaign in the west. The historical framework is one of the most archaeologically corroborated narratives in the Hebrew Bible. Three artifacts converge on this episode:

The **Sennacherib Prism**, also called the Taylor Prism (c. 691 BCE, now in the British Museum), is a hexagonal clay prism bearing Sennacherib's own annals. Of Hezekiah it boasts: "*As for Hezekiah, the Judahite, who did not submit to my yoke, 46 of his strong, walled cities... I besieged and took. He himself, like a caged bird, I shut up in Jerusalem, his royal city.*" The phrase *kīma iṣṣur quppi* — "like a caged bird" — has become one of the most cited lines in biblical archaeology.

The **Lachish Reliefs**, carved into the walls of Sennacherib's "Palace Without Rival" at Nineveh and now in the British Museum, depict in extraordinary detail the siege and fall of Lachish — the very city Isaiah 36:2 names as Sennacherib's command post when he sent the Rabshakeh up to Jerusalem. Battering rams, siege ramps, deportees with bundles on shoulders — the relief is essentially a war photograph from the 8th century.

The **Hezekiah Tunnel** beneath the City of David, with its **Siloam Inscription** (now housed in the Istanbul Archaeological Museum), records two teams of stonecutters meeting in the middle of a 533-meter rock-hewn aqueduct. This was Hezekiah's water project — moving the Gihon Spring's flow inside the walls to prepare for siege. The Rabshakeh stands "by the conduit of the upper pool on the highway to the Washer's Field" (36:2), almost certainly the Gihon Spring's exit, the same site where Isaiah confronted Hezekiah's father Ahaz in Isaiah 7:3. The geographic echo is deliberate: where Ahaz failed in faith, Hezekiah will succeed.

Hebrew names matter. **Sancheriv melech-Ashshur** (סַנְחֵרִיב מֶלֶךְ־אַשּׁוּר), "Sennacherib king of Assyria," opens the chapter. The **Rabshakeh** (רַב־שָׁקֵה, *rav-shaqeh*) is not a personal name but an Assyrian title — *rab šāqê*, "chief cupbearer," denoting a high palace official functioning as a diplomat-emissary. Likewise the speech is delivered "in the language of Judah" (*Yehudit*, יְהוּדִית, 36:11, 13), which Hezekiah's officials beg him to abandon for Aramaic, the diplomatic *lingua franca* of the age. The Rabshakeh's refusal — and his deliberate choice to speak past the officials to the people on the wall — is one of the earliest recorded instances of psychological warfare aimed at a civilian population.

The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran (1QIsaa, c. 125 BCE) preserves chapters 36-39 substantially intact, offering manuscript witness more than a thousand years older than the medieval Masoretic codices. The textual stability across 1QIsaa, the Septuagint, and the Masoretic Text is high, with only minor orthographic variation — testimony to the careful transmission of these chapters as a unit.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Isaiah 36 is **prose narrative** — a tight, 22-verse court scene with three movements. Verses 1-3 set the stage: army, emissary, embassy. Verses 4-10 are the Rabshakeh's first speech, addressed to Hezekiah's officials. Verses 11-20 are the second speech, deliberately shouted in Hebrew to the wall. Verses 21-22 close on silence: the officials say nothing and report back with torn clothes.

The narrative is built on three rhetorical pillars. First, **trust language**. The Rabshakeh's opening question — "On what do you rest this trust of yours?" (*mah ha-bittachon ha-zeh asher batachta*, מָה הַבִּטָּחוֹן הַזֶּה אֲשֶׁר בָּטָחְתָּ) — uses the Hebrew root *batach* (בטח), "to trust," seven times in this chapter. Trust is the theological lever Isaiah has been pressing since chapter 7 with Ahaz: "*If you will not believe, you shall not be established*" (Isa 7:9). The Rabshakeh systematically attacks every object of trust — Egypt (a "broken reed of a staff," 36:6), military strength ("two thousand horses, if you are able... to set riders on them," 36:8), and most audaciously, Yahweh himself.

Second, **the deconstruction of religious resources**. The Rabshakeh argues that Hezekiah has actually *offended* Yahweh by removing the high places: "*is it not he whose high places and altars Hezekiah has removed*" (36:7)? This is shrewd polemic — Hezekiah's centralizing reform (likely the same reform memorialized in 2 Kings 18:4) is recast as cultic vandalism. The Rabshakeh has done his intelligence homework, but he reads the data through Assyrian polytheistic assumptions, where a god deprived of altars would be a god deprived of power.

Third, **the blasphemous claim of divine commission**: "*Moreover, is it without the LORD that I have come up against this land to destroy it? The LORD said to me, 'Go up against this land and destroy it'*" (36:10). The Rabshakeh claims **Yahweh's own commission**. Theologically, this is the chapter's hinge. From one angle, the Rabshakeh is uncannily close to truth — Isaiah 10:5-15 had earlier called Assyria "*the rod of my anger*," sent by Yahweh against a faithless people. From another angle, the Rabshakeh weaponizes that providence into surrender propaganda, collapsing prophecy into imperial PR.

The Rabshakeh's second speech (verses 13-20) escalates with two devastating moves. The grotesque siege image — "*to eat their own dung and drink their own urine*" (36:12) — shocks the reader with the bodily reality of starvation under siege, an image the Lachish Reliefs render in visual form. Then the **inverted exodus offer** (36:16-17): make peace, and you shall eat your own vine and your own fig tree until I take you to "*a land like your own land, a land of grain and wine, a land of bread and vineyards*." The promise echoes Deuteronomic land-language — wine, grain, vines, fig trees — but it is exodus in reverse: deportation dressed as deliverance.

The chapter ends with a literary device of striking power: **silence**. "*But they were silent and answered him not a word, for the king's command was, 'Do not answer him'*" (36:21). Against the Rabshakeh's torrent of words — the chapter contains roughly 380 Hebrew words from his mouth — the response is *zero*. The narrator knows that prose, like poetry, can mean by what it withholds.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Within the **prophetic canon**, Isaiah 36 belongs to a small genre of prophet-against-empire narratives: Isaiah versus Sennacherib here, Jeremiah versus Nebuchadnezzar in Jeremiah 37-39, Daniel versus Nebuchadnezzar and Belshazzar in Daniel 1-5. Each functions as a courtroom drama where the empire's claims about itself meet the prophet's claim about Yahweh. The genre's signature move is to expose imperial speech as theology — usually bad theology — and to reveal Yahweh's lordship over the very kings who deny him.

The **Jewish trajectory** has read Isaiah 36-37 (the unit is rarely separated) primarily as a **deliverance narrative** — proof that Jerusalem and the temple are inviolable when the king prays rightly. Rabbinic tradition (b. Sanhedrin 94a, 95b) elaborates the Rabshakeh story extensively, identifying him by some legends as a Jewish apostate (his fluency in Hebrew taken as evidence) and treating his speech as the paradigm of the *meshumad* — the renegade who knows the tradition well enough to attack it. The Targum amplifies the theological framing: the Rabshakeh's blasphemy is recoded as direct affront to the *Memra* (Word) of the Lord. Medieval Jewish commentators — Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Radak — work carefully through the geography and the textual parallels with 2 Kings, treating the chapter as historiographic and didactic rather than apocalyptic.

The **Christian trajectory** has tended to read the chapter typologically and homiletically. The Rabshakeh becomes the figure of the accuser — Calvin in his *Commentary on Isaiah* draws explicit parallels to Satan's tempting of Christ in the wilderness, where each test pivots on whether to trust God's promise against worldly evidence. Augustine (*City of God* 17.8) reads the chapter as a microcosm of the two cities: imperial Babylon-language confronting the city of God. In the patristic and medieval homiletical tradition, the chapter served as a pastoral resource for souls under spiritual assault — the Rabshakeh's "do not let Hezekiah deceive you" became the template for the doubts that besiege the believer.

The **New Testament** does not directly cite Isaiah 36, but the chapter's logic is folded into the broader prophetic critique of empire that runs through Revelation 13, 17-18 (Babylon-Rome), and into Paul's argument in Romans 8:31-39 ("If God is for us, who can be against us?") — a passage that reads almost as a homiletical inversion of the Rabshakeh's "where are the gods of Hamath and Arpad?" Where the Rabshakeh argues from imperial victories that no god has delivered, Paul argues from the cross and resurrection that no power can separate from God's love.

In **liturgical history**, chapters 36-37 have served as a Lenten and crisis-time reading. The Eastern Orthodox tradition reads the deliverance narrative on the eve of major fasts; in the Anglican lectionary the passage appears in the Sundays after Trinity, framed as a witness to providential rescue. Hezekiah's prayer in the next chapter became a model for Christian prayer in extremis.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent — and Tensions

The chapter's theological core is a wager about **trust under empire**: when the Assyrian war machine has flattened forty-six fortified cities, when the diplomatic options have collapsed, when the religious resources have been mocked — what does it mean to trust Yahweh? Isaiah 36 puts the question in the mouth of an enemy and dares the reader to answer it.

But the chapter also raises real tensions modern readers cannot, in good faith, paper over.

**Tension 1: The biblical narrative versus the Sennacherib Prism.** The Bible reports that Sennacherib was repelled and Jerusalem was not taken. The Sennacherib Prism reports that Hezekiah paid heavy tribute — thirty talents of gold, eight hundred talents of silver, daughters and concubines and musicians sent to Nineveh. Critics from the 19th century onward seized on this as evidence that the biblical account whitewashes a defeat. The mainstream scholarly synthesis today (Mordechai Cogan in *The Raging Torrent*; Iain Provan, V. Philips Long, and Tremper Longman in *A Biblical History of Israel*) treats both records as **substantially compatible**: Hezekiah likely paid tribute (2 Kings 18:14-16 actually records the payment), and Sennacherib subsequently withdrew without taking Jerusalem. Each side reports the part flattering to itself. The Assyrian inscriptions never claim the capture of Jerusalem — a striking omission given Sennacherib's documentary boasting elsewhere.

**Steelman the skeptical reading**: Sennacherib's annals are an annual royal genre that systematically suppress losses; the absence of a "I took Jerusalem" claim is suggestive but not probative; and the biblical narrators have their own theology to vindicate. The cleanest minimalist reconstruction is: Sennacherib campaigned, devastated Judah, took tribute, withdrew for reasons (military, political, or providential) the Assyrian record prefers not to describe in detail.

**Tension 2: The theology of "empire's godly talk."** The Rabshakeh claims Yahweh sent him (36:10). And Isaiah himself, six chapters earlier, had said something close to this (Isa 10:5-7). How does the reader distinguish between true and false claims to divine commission, particularly when the false claim is, in some sense, partially correct? This is not a theoretical question. It is the question every generation of Jewish and Christian believers has had to ask when imperial power has draped itself in religious legitimation — Constantine's Rome, the Crusades, manifest destiny, Russian Orthodox war theology. The Rabshakeh shows that **the abuse of providence is older than the doctrine itself**. Walter Brueggemann's reading is sharp here: the test is not whether power claims theological legitimation (it always will) but whether the people of God can refuse the cooperative silence the empire requires. The wall officials' silence in 36:21 is not capitulation but discipline — a commanded silence that withholds legitimacy.

**Tension 3: The Rabshakeh's theological intelligence.** It is uncomfortable that the Rabshakeh knows the tradition. He knows about Hezekiah's reforms, about the language of trust, about the theology of deliverance. The chapter does not flatter the reader by making the enemy a fool. The enemy is a careful student of the very tradition he twists. For Christian and Jewish readers alike, the chapter's pastoral implication is severe: the most dangerous theological speech is not crude paganism but informed distortion.

**Where this leaves us:** Isaiah 36 stands at the threshold of the deliverance narrative without resolving it. The Rabshakeh's speech is the loudest sound in chapters 1-39, and the chapter ends not with God's reply but with torn clothes carrying the words back to Hezekiah. The chapter's theological achievement is to refuse the consolation it points toward. Before deliverance can be celebrated, the empire's claim must be heard in its full force — heard, not answered, not yet. The wall is still surrounded. The question is still hanging.

The closing question worth carrying into chapter 37: **when the Rabshakeh has had the last word, what does the king of Judah do with it?**
