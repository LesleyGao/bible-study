# Joshua 22: The Altar That Almost Started a Civil War

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Joshua 22 is set at the moment when a coalition army disbands. The Reubenites, Gadites, and half-tribe of Manasseh — roughly two and a half tribes whose allotments lay east of the Jordan (Num 32; Deut 3:12-20) — are dismissed from Shiloh, the cultic center in the central hill country, and head home to Gilead. The geography matters enormously. The Jordan Rift is not a gentle stream but a deep, scarred valley separating the Transjordanian plateau (roughly 600-900 meters above sea level) from the Cisjordanian highlands. In the dry season it can be waded; in the spring flood it is impassable without a ford. Any political community straddling that wound has to work very hard to remain one community. Joshua 22 is the first narrative stress-test of that arrangement.

The material culture implied is Iron I (roughly 1200-1000 BCE on the low chronology), when small hill-country villages of four-room houses, collar-rim storage jars, and terraced fields began multiplying east and west of the Jordan. Archaeological survey on the Transjordanian plateau — the Madaba Plains Project at Tell Hesban (biblical Heshbon), Tell Jalul, and Tell al-'Umayri, under Larry Herr and colleagues — has shown real Iron I settlement on the plateau, though the identification of these sites with specific biblical tribes remains disputed. The low-chronology dating (c. 1200-1150 BCE), tied to the Merneptah stele's 1208 BCE reference to "Israel" as a people-group in Canaan, sits more easily with this archaeological horizon than the high-chronology c. 1406 BCE Exodus-plus-forty. On either dating, the memory the chapter preserves — that a tribal league teetered on the edge of fratricide over a question of ritual jurisdiction — is consistent with the anthropology of segmentary lineage societies in the ancient Near East.

The central figure in the delegation is Phinehas ben Eleazar, grandson of Aaron. This is crucial for figure disambiguation: Phinehas ben Eleazar is the zealous priest of Numbers 25, who drove a spear through the Israelite and the Midianite woman at Baal-peor and stopped the plague. He is not Phinehas ben Eli, the scoundrel priest of 1 Samuel 4 who loses the Ark at Ebenezer. The Baal-peor reference in Joshua 22:17 is not incidental — the chapter is signaling that the same priest who once executed apostates is now leading a diplomatic mission, and the tribes know what he is capable of.

Five key terms carry the chapter's theological weight. First, *mizbēaḥ* (מִזְבֵּחַ), "altar," from the root *zbḥ*, "to slaughter for sacrifice." The trans-Jordanians build a *mizbēaḥ gādôl lĕmarʾê* — "an altar great in appearance" (22:10). The ambiguity is the whole problem: the structure *looks* like a sacrificial altar. Second, *maʿal* (מַעַל), "breach of faith, sacrilegious trespass" (22:16, 20, 22, 31) — the same term used of Achan's embezzlement of *ḥērem* goods in Joshua 7. Its repetition across the chapter binds the altar episode to Achan's story and insists the theological stakes are identical. Third, *ʿēd* (עֵד), "witness" — the climactic word and the name of the altar (22:27, 28, 34). The whole crisis hinges on whether the altar is a rival cult site or a forensic witness-object. Fourth, *dāvaq* (דָּבַק), "cling, cleave" (22:5), the same verb used in Genesis 2:24 of marriage and throughout Deuteronomy (4:4; 10:20; 11:22; 13:5; 30:20) of covenantal intimacy with YHWH. Joshua's charge to the departing tribes is not to *worship* YHWH but to *cling to* him — language of relational adhesion. Fifth, *marad* (מָרַד), "rebel" (22:16, 18, 19, 22, 29) — the political-theological verb for treason against a suzerain, used throughout Kings of vassal revolts.

The authorship and dating question sits inside the broader Deuteronomistic History debate. Martin Noth's 1943 *Überlieferungsgeschichtliche Studien* argued that Deuteronomy through Kings is a single exilic work. Frank Moore Cross in 1973 (*Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*) proposed dual redaction — a Josianic edition (Dtr1) with an exilic update (Dtr2). Joshua 22 is widely assigned to P-influenced material within DH because of its priestly vocabulary (*ʿēdâ*, congregation; *maʿal*; the prominence of Phinehas) and its concern for cultic centralization keyed to Deuteronomy 12 — the demand that sacrifices be offered only at "the place the LORD your God will choose." The altar at the Jordan, if sacrificial, would be a flagrant violation.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is a *covenantal crisis narrative* with embedded *juridical speech* — a trial scene without a formal court. The structure is symmetrical: accusation (22:16-20), defense (22:22-29), verdict (22:30-31), and dedication of the altar as a witness (22:34). Robert Alter would call this a "type-scene of misreading resolved" — comparable to Genesis 43's misreading of Joseph's steward, or Judges 20's misreading of Benjamin's intent. The wordplay is dense and deliberate. The altar is called *ʿĒd* (Witness) — but the Masoretic Text at 22:34 reads only "the people of Reuben and the people of Gad called the altar" followed by the explanatory clause, with the actual name supplied by inference. Several Hebrew manuscripts, the Syriac, and the Vulgate make the name explicit: "they called it *ʿĒd*." This is one of the chapter's few meaning-affecting textual variants — without the name, the pun is implicit; with it, the etiological formula is complete.

The chapter stands at a structural seam in the Book of Joshua. Chapters 13-21 have been the land-distribution ledger; chapters 23-24 will be Joshua's two farewell addresses. Joshua 22 is the pivot — the army disbands, the cultic geography fragments, and the question becomes: *now that the conquest is over, can this thing hold together?* Noth read the chapter as a late P-redactional interpolation designed to legitimate the Transjordanian tribes' place in the covenant community and, more sharply, to reassert Shiloh/Jerusalem-centered cultic control. Richard Nelson in his Old Testament Library commentary (1997) treats the chapter as a carefully crafted piece of political theology addressing the perennial question of how geographically dispersed communities remain religiously united.

Literary devices cluster thickly. The repetition of *hayyôm* ("this day," 22:16, 17, 18, 22, 29, 31) turns the chapter into a drumbeat of present-tense decision. The phrase *wĕʿattâ* ("and now," 22:4, 5) marks the hinge between military past and civilian future. The threefold invocation of divine names at 22:22 — *ʾĒl ʾĕlōhîm yhwh, ʾĒl ʾĕlōhîm yhwh* ("The Mighty One, God, the LORD! The Mighty One, God, the LORD!") — is one of the most intense oath-formulas in the Hebrew Bible, heaping up names to bind the speaker to truth under penalty of divine destruction. Trent Butler's Word Biblical Commentary notes that this doublet appears nowhere else in exactly this form and reads it as a self-imprecatory covenant lawsuit.

The narrator's own perspective is tellingly neutral until verse 33, when the report is "good in the eyes of the people of Israel" and they "blessed God." The chapter refuses to moralize in the voice of the narrator; it lets the speakers make the argument, and the reader has to decide along with Phinehas.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish trajectory.** Second Temple literature is quieter on Joshua 22 than on the conquest chapters, but the Qumran Joshua fragments (4Q378 and 4Q379, the *Apocryphon of Joshua*) do preserve expanded Joshua material that suggests the trans-Jordanian story was meaningful to the community. Philo's *De Vita Mosis* treats the Transjordanian allotment as an ethical problem — the tribes chose inferior land for cattle, and Philo allegorizes it as the soul's choice of lower goods. Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.1.26-27) retells Joshua 22 at length, emphasizing the near-war and the rhetorical skill of the trans-Jordanians' defense, and he frames the episode as proof that Israel was willing to fight even its own for the sake of the one altar.

The rabbinic tradition mines the chapter for two questions: the legitimacy of commemorative (non-sacrificial) altars, and the theology of corporate responsibility. Mishnah and the Babylonian Talmud's tractate *Zevachim* 119a debates the permissibility of altars outside the central sanctuary during various historical periods — before the tabernacle was erected, during the period of the wandering altars, and after Jerusalem's temple. Joshua 22 is the textual proof that a *commemorative* altar (one that does not receive sacrifice) is permissible even in a centralized-cult framework, so long as its function is clearly marked. *Bereshit Rabbah* and the *Tanhuma* midrashim develop the chapter as a lesson in *ʾahavat yisraʾel*, love of fellow Israelite — don't go to war over what you haven't investigated. Rashi (eleventh-century Troyes) glosses *mizbēaḥ gādôl lĕmarʾê* as "great in appearance only, not in function," catching the literary point exactly. Ramban (Nachmanides, thirteenth-century Spain) in his broader commentary on altars (Exod 20; Deut 12) cites Joshua 22 as evidence that geography can require ritual innovation. Abarbanel, writing after the expulsion from Spain in 1492, reads the chapter with special resonance — a dispersed people's need for visible markers of covenantal identity.

Modern Jewish readers have taken the chapter in several directions. Jon Levenson's work on covenant (*Sinai and Zion*, 1985) treats Joshua 22 as the test case for the possibility of *mediated* covenant — can a covenant community survive the absence of direct cultic participation? Martin Buber in *The Prophetic Faith* reads the altar as a "dialogical witness," a thing that stands between two communities and carries the word each would say to the other. Moshe Weinfeld in *Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School* (1972) places the chapter squarely in the Deuteronomic centralization program and notes the tension: the chapter affirms centralization while legitimating peripheral memory-objects. Baruch Halpern and Rachel Havrelock (*River Jordan*, 2011) have both written on the Jordan as a boundary-line that is constantly renegotiated; Havrelock's reading of Joshua 22 treats the altar as a hermeneutical object, a thing built to generate the question *whose river is this?*

**Christian trajectory.** Origen's *Homilies on Joshua* (twenty-six sermons, delivered in Caesarea in the 240s CE) do not devote a full homily to chapter 22, but he refers to the altar repeatedly as a figure of the apostolic witness — the church's task is to build not a second altar of sacrifice but an altar of *memorial* to the one sacrifice. Jerome's *Quaestiones Hebraicae* addresses the etymology of *ʿĒd*. Augustine in *City of God* 17.2-3 uses Joshua 22 briefly to argue that the visible Church can tolerate apparent but not real departures from the one sacrifice of Christ. Chrysostom preaches the chapter as a model of diplomatic Christian rebuke — go first and ask.

Aquinas treats the case in *Summa Theologiae* II-II (on the virtue of religion) as an example of the lawfulness of *sacred images and memorials* that do not themselves receive worship — a useful proof-text against iconoclastic objections. The *Glossa Ordinaria* reads the altar allegorically as the altar of the heart. Hugh of St. Victor folds the chapter into his meditation on the unity of the Church across distance.

Luther's lectures on Joshua treat the episode as a paradigm case of *adiaphora* — things neither commanded nor forbidden, which must be judged by their function, not their form. Luther is sharp on the near-disaster: the western tribes were zealous but ignorant, and zeal without knowledge is the mother of schism. Calvin's sermons on Joshua 22 (preserved in his sixteenth-century Geneva homilies, published in the Calvini Opera) press the corporate-responsibility theme hard — the western tribes were right to fear that *one rebellion poisons the whole body*, and the trans-Jordanians were right to submit to the investigation without taking offense. Menno Simons, among the Anabaptists, uses Joshua 22 to argue for the priority of *investigation before excommunication*.

Critical scholarship has been divided. Noth 1943 assigned the chapter to a P-influenced late stratum addressing post-exilic concerns about Diaspora Jews' relation to the Jerusalem cult. Richard Nelson (1997) argues it is integrated Deuteronomistic composition with priestly editorial touches. Thomas Dozeman's *Joshua 13-21* (2013, Anchor Yale) reads the chapter as a sustained meditation on the Deuteronomic centralization requirement and its geographical limits. Trent Butler treats the chapter as a carefully composed political theology. Klaus Koch on covenant formulary considers whether the altar's role as *ʿēd* echoes the witness-clauses of Hittite treaty form; Baltzer in *Das Bundesformular* (1964) sees the trans-Jordanians as essentially invoking a treaty-witness stipulation.

Contemporary readings are rich. Walter Brueggemann (*Theology of the Old Testament*, 1997) treats Joshua 22 as a case study in the tension between cultic centralization and geographical inclusion. Daniel Hawk's *Every Promise Fulfilled* (1991) and his later commentary read the chapter as meditation on "boundary-thinking" — how Israel's identity is negotiated at the edges. Lori Rowlett's *Joshua and the Rhetoric of Violence* (1996) argues that even the near-war of Joshua 22 reveals the book's rhetorical preoccupation with internal discipline and central authority. On the liberation and postcolonial side, Robert Allen Warrior's 1989 essay "A Native American Perspective: Canaanites, Cowboys, and Indians" asks a question Joshua 22 cannot ignore — where, in all this covenantal self-examination, is the voice of the Canaanite who was driven out so this altar-dispute could even arise? Musa Dube and Kwok Pui-lan extend the question: whose land is the "unclean land" of 22:19, and on what authority do the western tribes call it so?

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension one: Is orthodoxy portable?** The western tribes assume it is not — that holiness lives at the Shiloh sanctuary and that the Jordan is a spiritual, not merely geographic, divide. *Position one (strict centralization)*: Deuteronomy 12 is absolute. YHWH chooses one place, and any altar elsewhere is functionally idolatry, however sincerely intended. The western tribes are right to mobilize; Phinehas is right to investigate with force in reserve; the trans-Jordanians would have been better off building nothing. *Position two (mediated presence)*: the altar-as-witness shows that a geographically distant community can maintain covenantal identity through a symbolic object that points *back* to the central cult. Holiness is not bounded by the sanctuary walls; it is carried in the memory and practice of the community, and visible signs are aids to that memory. *Position three (the steelman — unpopular but coherent)*: the western tribes are exercising a zealous border-orthodoxy that is itself a symptom of insecurity. A truly secure covenant community does not need to march on its brothers at the first sign of ritual innovation; it asks, then asks again, then discerns. The trans-Jordanians' defense includes a quiet rebuke: "Did you think we did not know?" Orthodoxy carried at swordpoint tends to become its own kind of idolatry — worship of the sanctuary as talisman rather than of the God who inhabits it. *Where this leaves us*: Joshua 22 insists on both centralization and grace. It does not collapse the tension; it dramatizes it and shows the community surviving it by talking.

**Tension two: Does symbolism protect or endanger?** *Position one (symbols unite)*: the altar at the Jordan is a pedagogical object for children. It will sit there generation after generation and teach. The trans-Jordanians are right that their descendants might be told "you have no portion in the LORD" (22:25), and right to build against that day. *Position two (symbols mislead)*: the trans-Jordanians are naïve about how visible objects function. An altar-shaped thing by the Jordan will be read as an altar; no amount of explanatory naming will prevent misreading down the centuries. Better to invest in liturgical practices, not physical objects. *Position three (the steelman)*: both sides are right, and the chapter's genius is to show the near-miss as the *normal* condition of communities that communicate through symbols. Symbolic objects will *always* be misread; the only question is whether a community has developed the institutional capacity to investigate before it destroys. The chapter is less interested in the altar than in the delegation. The ten chiefs plus Phinehas are the real invention — a bilateral commission with authority to investigate, listen, and return a verdict. The altar will eventually be forgotten or buried or repurposed, but the method of investigation is meant to endure. *Where this leaves us*: the chapter is a quiet argument for institutions. Passion is not enough; symbols are not enough; centralization is not enough. A people needs a mechanism for the slow work of asking what the other tribe actually meant.

The ethical difficulty the chapter cannot escape is the Achan and Baal-peor logic the western tribes explicitly invoke. At Peor, the penalty was a plague and the execution of the offenders. With Achan, the penalty was the stoning of Achan and his entire household. In both cases, the covenant community is imagined as one body in which contagion spreads. Joshua 22 preserves this theology — "he did not perish alone for his iniquity" (22:20) — and it is not a theology modern readers accept comfortably. It is the theology that makes the delegation necessary in the first place. If the two and a half tribes had in fact been apostate, the western tribes would have been morally obligated to destroy them, and the chapter does not flinch from this. The happy ending works only because the accusation was wrong; the logic that would have governed a right accusation is left intact.

**One unresolved question**: What did the trans-Jordanians' descendants actually say when their children asked about the altar at the Jordan, and did any of them, in any generation, start offering sacrifices on it anyway?

## The Thread

Genesis 31:44-54, the Laban-Jacob covenant at Galeed/Mizpah, builds a pillar-and-heap between the two parties and names it "witness" — *ʿēd* in Hebrew, *yĕgar sāhădûtāʾ* in Aramaic. Joshua 22 is writing with Laban in mind: a boundary-object, a naming-as-witness, a refusal of violence. Isaiah 19:19-20, strikingly, envisions "an altar to the LORD in the midst of the land of Egypt, and a pillar to the LORD at its border" that will be "a sign and a witness to the LORD of hosts." The prophet imagines the Joshua 22 model extended to include the ancestral enemy. Hebrews 13:10 — "we have an altar from which those who serve the tent have no right to eat" — inverts the question: the Christian community is outside the camp, and its altar is not the visible one. And 1 Corinthians 10:14-22, on the table of the Lord and the table of demons, re-raises Joshua 22's exact question: when is an altar really an altar?
