# Matthew 5: The Mountain, the Beatitudes, and the Six Antitheses

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Matthew opens the first of his five major discourses by sending Jesus up onto "the mountain" (*to oros*, τὸ ὄρος, 5:1). The traditional site since the Byzantine period has been the gentle slope above Tabgha and Capernaum — the so-called Mount of Beatitudes — but the text is geographically vague. W.D. Davies and Dale Allison in their ICC commentary caution against pinning the site too precisely. The arthrous *to oros* may not designate a specific peak at all but signal a symbolic mountain: the new Sinai. Matthew's Jesus ascends as Moses ascended; sits as a rabbi sat to teach (*kathisantos autou*, καθίσαντος αὐτοῦ, 5:1); opens his mouth (*anoixas to stoma autou*, ἀνοίξας τὸ στόμα αὐτοῦ, 5:2 — a Septuagintal solemnity-formula common in wisdom literature). The pedagogy is unmistakably Jewish-Galilean: the teacher seated, disciples gathered around, crowds at a respectful distance. The Korazim plateau immediately north of Capernaum is plausible terrain, and 4th-century basalt synagogue ruins at Korazim itself testify to the village density of the upper Galilee Jesus inhabited.

The material world of the chapter is densely first-century Galilean. Salt (*halas*, ἅλας, 5:13) in the eastern Mediterranean was harvested impure from the Dead Sea evaporation pans and from inland deposits; Pliny the Elder (*Natural History* 31) describes such block-salt mixed with gypsum and other minerals. When the sodium chloride leached out, what remained was savorless residue fit only for the road — Jeremias and later F.F. Bruce noted that this was used to surface the flat roofs and pathways of villages, where it would be "trampled under foot" exactly as 5:13 describes. The "lamp under a basket" (*modios*, μόδιος, 5:15) is a Roman dry-measure of about nine liters used as a household grain container, a bushel-sized basket; oil lamps recovered by the hundreds from Galilean village excavations at Capernaum and Bethsaida are small enough to be eclipsed under one. The "city set on a hill" (5:14) likely echoes either Sepphoris four miles northwest of Nazareth or the literal hilltop villages of the upper Galilee whose lamp-light was visible across the lake at night.

Three further details earth the chapter. The "altar" of 5:23-24, where one is to leave the gift before reconciling with a brother, is the Jerusalem Temple altar — the chapter assumes the Second Temple is functioning, which is one of several reasons most scholars (Davies/Allison, Luz, Hagner) reconstruct an underlying Jesus-tradition prior to 70 CE even though Matthew himself writes ca. 80-90 from a community now negotiating life after the Temple's destruction. The "council" (*synedrion*, συνέδριον, 5:22) and "judgment" are the local *bet din* and the higher Sanhedrin — graded Jewish judicial bodies. And the "certificate of divorce" (*apostasion*, ἀποστάσιον, 5:31) refers to the *get* of Deuteronomy 24:1, the subject of a famous first-century rabbinic dispute between the schools of Hillel (permissive) and Shammai (restrictive), preserved in m. Gittin 9:10 and engaged extensively by E.P. Sanders in *Jewish Law from Jesus to the Mishnah*.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Benjamin W. Bacon's 1918 essay "The 'Five Books' of Matthew Against the Jews" remains the foundational structural thesis: Matthew has organized Jesus's teaching into five major discourses, each closing with the formula "and when Jesus had finished these sayings" (7:28; 11:1; 13:53; 19:1; 26:1), framing Jesus as the new Moses delivering a new Torah. The Sermon on the Mount is discourse one. Bacon's pentateuchal reading has critics — Dale Allison in *The New Moses: A Matthean Typology* (1993) defends it with Mosaic typology evidence; Ulrich Luz in his *Hermeneia* commentary thinks the five-discourse pattern real but the Pentateuch parallel forced — but no one denies that 5-7 functions as a deliberately constructed teaching block.

W.D. Davies's *The Setting of the Sermon on the Mount* (1964) remains the most influential single monograph on the discourse. Davies argued that Matthew composed the Sermon partly as a Jewish-Christian response to the Council of Jamnia (Yavneh) ca. 85-90 CE, where rabbinic Judaism was consolidating itself after the Temple's destruction; Matthew's community is staking its claim to be true Israel, with Jesus as authoritative interpreter of Torah. Anthony Saldarini's *Matthew's Christian-Jewish Community* (1994) extends the argument: Matthew's community is still inside Judaism, debating the Pharisaic-rabbinic stream from within, not yet a separate "Christianity."

Matthew 5 parallels Luke 6:17-49, the Sermon on the Plain, and the differences are programmatic for the Q-source debate. Luke has four beatitudes plus four woes; Matthew has eight or nine. Luke has "blessed are the poor"; Matthew has "blessed are the poor in spirit." Luke is shorter, sharper, more economically radical; Matthew is fuller, more spiritualized, more catechetical. The standard two-source hypothesis (Q + Mark) treats both as drawing on a shared sayings-source Q with redactional shaping; Matthean priority and Lukan priority hypotheses (Mark Goodacre, Michael Goulder) read the differences differently. Either way, Matthew's redaction has clearly built the Beatitudes into a deliberate eight-fold *makarismos* structure with the inclusio "for theirs is the kingdom of heaven" bracketing 5:3 and 5:10.

The literary architecture of Matthew 5 is a series of nested patterns. The Beatitudes (5:3-12) form an octave, with the ninth saying (5:11-12) shifting from third-person to second-person address as a hinge into the body of the sermon. Salt and light (5:13-16) functions as transitional ethical orientation. The programmatic statement on Law (5:17-20) opens the great central panel: the six antitheses (5:21-48), each introduced by the *ēkousate hoti errethē... egō de legō hymin* (ἠκούσατε ὅτι ἐρρέθη... ἐγὼ δὲ λέγω ὑμῖν) formula — "you have heard it said... but I say to you." The chapter closes with the imperative *teleios* (5:48) which is a literary hinge into chapter 6's teaching on the three pieties.

Textual variants in Matthew 5 are mostly minor, but two are noteworthy. In 5:22 the Byzantine majority reads "angry with his brother *without cause* (*eikē*, εἰκῇ)"; the earliest and best witnesses (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, P64) lack the qualifier. The harder, harsher reading is almost certainly original — anger itself is the moral problem. In 5:44 the Byzantine text adds "bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you," harmonizing with Luke 6:27-28; the shorter Matthean reading is original.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Matthew 5 sits at the most-interpreted juncture in the entire New Testament outside the Passion. Two interpretive trajectories — Jewish and Christian — must be tracked side by side.

On the **Jewish** side, the rabbinic parallels to nearly every saying in the chapter are dense. Strack-Billerbeck's *Kommentar zum Neuen Testament aus Talmud und Midrasch* assembled the parallels exhaustively in 1922-28, though the work was shaped by the supersessionism of its era and must be read with care. Pinchas Lapide's *The Sermon on the Mount: Utopia or Program for Action?* (1982) is an Orthodox Jewish theologian's appreciative reading: the Sermon is "the Magna Carta of every authentic Jewish-Christian dialogue." David Flusser's *The Sermon on the Mount and the Wisdom of Israel* and Daniel Boyarin's *The Jewish Gospels* read Matthew's Jesus as a thoroughly Jewish teacher whose moral demands stand inside, not against, the Jewish tradition. Géza Vermes's *The Religion of Jesus the Jew* and Amy-Jill Levine's *The Misunderstood Jew* and the Levine/Brettler *Jewish Annotated New Testament* are the contemporary corrective: the antithesis formula does not pit Jesus against "Judaism" but pits Jesus against particular contested interpretations within first-century Jewish halakhic debate. The Beatitudes themselves echo Psalm 1:1, Psalm 32:1, Psalm 119:1, and especially Isaiah 61:1-3 — Jesus is delivering a reading of Hebrew Scripture, not a replacement.

On the **Christian** side, the reception history is staggeringly long. Origen's homilies engage the chapter; John Chrysostom's *Ninety Homilies on Matthew* (late 4th century) gives the Beatitudes seven full sermons. But the foundational Christian text is Augustine's *De Sermone Domini in Monte* — the first systematic Christian commentary on the Sermon, written ca. 393-396 while Augustine was still a presbyter at Hippo. Augustine reads the eight Beatitudes as a Spirit-led ascent corresponding to the seven gifts of Isaiah 11:2-3; he reads "be perfect" as the soul's eschatological completion in vision of God; he reads the antitheses as Christ's deepening, not abrogation, of the moral law. Aquinas in the *Catena Aurea* assembles patristic comment and in the *Summa Theologiae* (II-II q. 184) develops the medieval framework that distinguishes the Sermon's "counsels of perfection" (*consilia evangelica*) from its "precepts" — the harder sayings (non-resistance, total non-swearing) bind those called to the religious life; precepts (the Decalogue, love of neighbor) bind all. Bernard of Clairvaux and Thomas à Kempis (*Imitation of Christ*) extend the monastic reading.

The Reformation re-frames everything. Luther's *Sermon on the Mount Sermons* (1530-32, on chapters 5-7) develops the **two-kingdoms** reading: the Sermon is for the Christian as Christian, in the kingdom of God; in the kingdom of the world the Christian as magistrate, soldier, parent, neighbor must use the sword, swear oaths, resist evil, because civil order requires it. Calvin's *Harmony of the Gospels* reads the antitheses as recovering the original intent of Mosaic law from Pharisaic accretion and develops what later Reformed theology calls the **third use of the law** — the Sermon as the law's positive moral guide for the regenerate. The **Anabaptist** tradition (Conrad Grebel, Felix Manz, Menno Simons; the Schleitheim Confession of 1527) reads the Sermon as a literal community ethic: pacifism, no oaths, no civil office, separation from the sword-bearing world. John Howard Yoder's *The Politics of Jesus* (1972) and Stanley Hauerwas's *The Peaceable Kingdom* (1983) are the modern Anabaptist-influenced restatements: the church is to be the visible community whose ethic is the Sermon, full stop.

Outside the magisterial-Anabaptist debate, three twentieth-century readings reshape the field. Albert Schweitzer's *Quest of the Historical Jesus* (1906) advances the **interim ethic** thesis: the Sermon was Jesus's emergency ethic for the imminent eschatological crisis he expected, not a workable ethic for ongoing history — Schweitzer's verdict is that the Sermon cannot be lived as a continuing program. Adolf von Harnack's *What Is Christianity?* (1900) and the liberal-Protestant tradition read the Sermon as the timeless ethical core: the kingdom of God as the ethical society. Dietrich Bonhoeffer's *Discipleship* (*Nachfolge*, 1937) is the great twentieth-century rebuttal of liberal moralism and Lutheran two-kingdoms quietism alike: the Sermon is the call of Christ to costly discipleship, "cheap grace" is the deadly enemy, and "when Christ calls a man, he bids him come and die." Bonhoeffer wrote in resistance to the German Christian movement; he was hanged at Flossenbürg in April 1945. Tolstoy's *The Kingdom of God Is Within You* (1894) reads the antitheses as a literal pacifism and influences Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr.; King's *Stride Toward Freedom* explicitly cites the Sermon as the theological ground of nonviolent direct action.

Modern critical commentaries to know: Hans Dieter Betz's *The Sermon on the Mount* (Hermeneia, 1995) is the comprehensive technical work; Davies/Allison's three-volume ICC *Matthew* (vol. 1, 1988) is the standard exegetical reference; Ulrich Luz's *Hermeneia Matthew* (vol. 1, 2007) is unmatched on reception history; R.T. France's NICNT and Craig Keener's NICNT and *The Sermon on the Mount* (2009) are the major evangelical scholarly engagements; Glen Stassen and David Gushee's *Kingdom Ethics* recovers the "transforming initiatives" structure of the antitheses (situation → vicious cycle → transforming initiative) and is the most useful pastoral-ethical reading of the chapter in print.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**First tension: how do the Beatitudes describe blessedness — character ethic, eschatological reversal, or social-political program?**

The Reformed-evangelical reading (Calvin; modern: D.A. Carson, John Stott) takes the Beatitudes as **character description** of the regenerate Christian: meekness, mercy, purity of heart, peacemaking are the dispositional fruit of grace. The eschatological-apocalyptic reading (Schweitzer; modern: Dale Allison, N.T. Wright) takes them as **promises of eschatological reversal** — the kingdom is breaking in, the world's standing order will be inverted, the meek really will inherit a renewed earth. The liberation reading (Gustavo Gutiérrez, Jon Sobrino, Leonardo Boff) takes them, especially in their Lukan form, as a **social-political program**: the poor are blessed because God is on the side of the materially oppressed; the kingdom is the just society. Pinchas Lapide's Jewish reading takes them as a **wisdom recital** in the *makarismos* tradition of Psalms — neither program nor prediction but pronouncement of what God already counts as blessed. Steel-manned: each catches something. The Beatitudes do describe character, do promise eschatological reversal, do critique social arrangements that crush the poor and meek, and do stand inside a Jewish wisdom tradition that pronounced God's verdict on what the world treated as worthless.

Where this leaves us: the choice between "spiritualized" Matthew ("poor in spirit") and "economic" Luke ("poor") is a false binary — the Hebrew *aniyyim* of Isaiah 61:1, which both evangelists are working from, carries both senses inseparably. To read 5:3 as purely interior piety is a Lutheran-pietist softening; to read it as purely material economics is a liberation reduction. The dispossession is real and total, and the spiritual posture and the economic condition are not separable in the first-century Galilean village context where Jesus was teaching peasants whose Roman taxation, Herodian double-taxation, and Temple tithes left them crushed.

**Second tension: how does the "I have not come to abolish but to fulfill" relate to the antitheses?**

The Lutheran reading: Christ fulfills the Law as its *telos* — its goal, end, completion — and the Sermon then drives the conscience to the cross by exposing the Law's impossible depth. The Reformed reading (Calvin, Westminster): Christ fulfills the Law by recovering its true intent, and the regenerate Christian is now empowered by the Spirit to obey the Sermon as the third use of the law. The Anabaptist reading (Yoder, Hauerwas): Christ fulfills the Law by founding a new community whose corporate life literally embodies the antitheses — pacifism, no oaths, enemy-love, total non-resistance. The Catholic reading (Aquinas, modified post-*Lumen Gentium*): the harder sayings are *consilia* — counsels of perfection for those called to the religious life — while the precepts of the Decalogue and love-command bind all; *Lumen Gentium* 5 reaffirms the universal call to holiness while preserving real diversity of state of life. The dispensationalist reading (Scofield, Ryrie): the Sermon is for the future millennial kingdom or the Tribulation saints, not directly applicable to the church age. The Jewish appreciative reading (Lapide, Flusser): the antitheses are intensifications of Torah within a recognizable rabbinic-halakhic register; Jesus's "but I say to you" claims authoritative interpretation, but does not abrogate Torah.

Steel-manned again: each preserves something the others lose. Lutheran two-kingdoms takes seriously that the Sermon's literal application to the magistrate's sword would dissolve civil order. Anabaptist literalism takes seriously that Jesus said it and meant it and founded a community to live it. The Catholic counsels-precepts distinction takes seriously that the church has always recognized different states of life have different forms of Sermon-obedience. The Jewish reading takes seriously that Jesus is a Jew teaching Jews inside the Torah, not against it.

Where this leaves us: the Anabaptist reading has the strongest grip on the plain sense of what Jesus said. Bonhoeffer's *Discipleship* has the strongest grip on what it costs to take that plain sense seriously. The two-kingdoms tradition has the strongest grip on the actual administrative requirements of running a society, but it is also the tradition that allowed German Lutheran Christianity to make peace with the Nazi state, and Bonhoeffer's witness from inside that tradition is a permanent indictment of any easy two-kingdoms quietism. The honest reading carries the tension forward without resolving it.

**Unresolved question:** Is *teleios* in 5:48 a realizable command (Wesleyan "entire sanctification") or an asymptotic standard that drives the believer to grace (Lutheran *simul iustus et peccator*)? The Greek *teleios* leans toward "complete, mature, full-grown" rather than moralistic perfection; the Lukan parallel "be merciful as your Father is merciful" (Luke 6:36) suggests the *teleios* is functionally specified as imitation of divine mercy. But the rhetorical force of the imperative, set as climax of the antitheses, refuses to be domesticated. The text holds open both readings.

## The Thread

Matthew 5 connects forward and back. Backward: the Beatitudes echo Psalm 1, Psalm 37 (the meek inheriting the land), Isaiah 61:1-3 (the poor, the mourners, those who hunger for righteousness), and the wisdom *makarismos* tradition of Sirach. The "you have heard it said" formula echoes Deuteronomy's repeated "you shall." Forward: the antitheses anchor the rest of Matthew's ethical program — 18:15-20 on community discipline, 19:3-12 on divorce (where 5:31-32 is reopened), 22:34-40 on the great commandment, 25:31-46 on the judgment of the nations by their treatment of the least.

The Lukan parallel must be tracked: Luke 6:17-49 (the Sermon on the Plain) is the primary New Testament cross-reference and runs in parallel through the Beatitudes, the love-of-enemies command, the golden rule, and the two-builders conclusion. The *Didache* 1-6 ("Two Ways") preserves a parallel early-Christian community-ethics tradition that absorbs Sermon material into a catechetical handbook for baptismal candidates — flag this for Matthew 7's two-ways material. James 2:5 ("has not God chosen the poor in this world to be rich in faith?") and James 5:12 ("let your yes be yes and your no be no") are the most Sermon-saturated passages in the rest of the New Testament canon.
