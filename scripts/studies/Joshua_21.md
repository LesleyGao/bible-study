# Joshua 21: Forty-Eight Cities and the Audacity of "All Came to Pass"

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Joshua 21 is a roster chapter — a land-grant record for the tribe of Levi. Forty-eight cities, distributed across the other twelve tribal territories, are set aside with their surrounding *migrāšîm* (pasturelands) for the priestly tribe that itself received no contiguous territorial inheritance. The structure is precise: Kohathites take twenty-three cities (thirteen for the Aaronid priestly line from Judah, Simeon, and Benjamin; ten for non-priestly Kohathite clans from Ephraim, Dan, and western Manasseh), Gershonites thirteen (from Issachar, Asher, Naphtali, and Bashan), Merarites twelve (from Reuben, Gad, and Zebulun). The six cities of refuge from Joshua 20 are explicitly folded into this list. The chapter then climaxes with one of the most consequential theological summaries in the Hebrew Bible: "Not one word of all the good promises that the LORD had made to the house of Israel had failed; all came to pass" (21:45).

The places named are geographically real and often excavated. In the Aaronid Judahite list, Hebron (Tel Hebron / er-Rumeide), Libnah (possibly Tel Burna), Debir (debated — Khirbet Rabud is the current leading candidate), Beth-shemesh (Tel Beit Shemesh, excavated since Mackenzie in 1911 and most recently by Bunimovitz and Lederman) are all concrete Iron Age sites with substantial stratigraphy. Anathoth, the home of Jeremiah a few centuries later (Jeremiah 1:1), is given to the Aaronid priests — a detail that ties the chapter's priestly geography to the later prophetic tradition. Gibeon (el-Jib) is excavated and famous for its Iron Age wine-jar industry and its enormous water system. Shechem, Gezer, and Beth-horon on the Ephraimite list are major Iron Age sites. Taanach on the Manassite list is excavated at Tell Taʿannek. Strikingly, no Levitical city is listed at Shiloh — although 21:2 locates the entire transaction at Shiloh. This silence is telling: by the time the list was compiled, Shiloh was destroyed (the Iron Age I destruction at Khirbet Seilun is usually dated around 1050 BCE, associated with Philistine pressure after the ark's capture, as reflected in 1 Samuel 4 and referenced in Jeremiah 7:12, 14; 26:6, 9; Psalm 78:60). The compiler remembers Shiloh as the venue of the assembly but does not list it among the active Levitical centers.

Dating is contested. The high view — that the list reflects a genuine pre-monarchic or early monarchic administrative system — was revived influentially by W. F. Albright in his 1945 *JBL* article "The List of Levitic Cities," which argued on geographical and epigraphic grounds for a tenth-century Davidic-Solomonic origin. Albright's reasoning was that the distribution pattern presupposes Israelite control over territories like Gezer (not fully Israelite until Solomon, per 1 Kings 9:16) and yet does not reflect the later eighth-century administrative structures. Martin Noth, by contrast, read the list as a late, even post-exilic, utopian scheme projecting priestly claims onto the conquest narrative. Albrecht Alt in his studies of the geographical system of Joshua saw it as a schematic template. Subsequent specialist geographers — Yohanan Aharoni, Zecharia Kallai in his magisterial *Historical Geography of the Bible* (1986), Nadav Naʾaman in multiple studies of Joshua's geographical lists, Menahem Haran's *Temples and Temple-Service in Ancient Israel* (1978) — generally accept an early monarchic core but recognize later redactional layers. The parallel list at 1 Chronicles 6:54–81, with minor but meaningful divergences (different names for several cities, omission of some), points to a shared but edited tradition.

Archaeologically, the concept of "pastureland" or *migrāš* corresponds to the belt of uncultivated grazing land around the walls of every Iron Age town, attested in both excavation and in the legal imagination of Leviticus 25:34 (which forbids the sale of Levitical pasturelands). Israel Finkelstein's surveys of highland settlement patterns from Iron Age I through II show the circumpolis pattern of pastureland-plus-village-plus-fields that the chapter presupposes.

Several Hebrew terms do the chapter's work. First, *migrāš* (מִגְרָשׁ), "pastureland" or "common land," in 21:2 and throughout. The root *grš* ("drive out," "expel") points to land from which the built structures have been cleared; it is the open common where livestock graze and where the walls' defensive sightlines are maintained. Numbers 35:4–5 specifies its dimensions: one thousand cubits in every direction from the wall. Second, *gôrāl* (גּוֹרָל), "lot," in 21:4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 40. The distribution proceeds by sacred lot, the same mechanism (likely involving the Urim and Thummim or sacred stones) by which the tribal territories were apportioned in Joshua 13–19. The theological claim is that Yahweh, not human negotiation, determined the placement. Third, *mōlādâ* and *naḥălâ* (מוֹלָדָה / נַחֲלָה), "ancestral possession / inheritance" — the Levites do not receive a *naḥălâ* in the tribal sense (Numbers 18:20, 23–24: "I am your portion"), but they receive cities and pasturelands, a qualified form of landed presence. Fourth, *mĕnûḥâ* (מְנוּחָה), "rest," in 21:44 — the state of secure dwelling that the summary claims Yahweh has given "on every side." And fifth, *nāpal* (נָפַל), "fall" — in the fierce formula of 21:45, "not one word has *fallen*": divine speech is imagined as something physically substantial that would collapse if unfulfilled.

Ancient Near Eastern parallels are relevant. Priestly land-grants to temples and temple personnel are well documented across Mesopotamia — the Babylonian *kudurru* stelae record royal grants of villages and fields to temples and priests; the Hittite *LÚsankunni* priests held allotments from the royal administration; Ugaritic palace administrative texts document similar priestly provisions. What distinguishes the Israelite scheme, as Haran emphasized, is the dispersion: instead of concentrating priestly land around a single temple complex, the system scatters priestly cities through every tribal territory. The priesthood is not a regional caste but a distributed function.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: an administrative register nested inside a narrative conclusion. The register (21:1–42) follows the formal pattern of a legal allocation document — petitioners (Levites, 21:1–3), authorization (Mosaic command, 21:2), distribution by lot with numerical tallies (21:4–40), summary total (21:41–42). The concluding coda (21:43–45) is a theological summary in the distinctive voice of the Deuteronomistic Historian.

The chapter exhibits a fourfold list structure: each of the three Levite clans receives a block of allocations, and each block specifies the donating tribe and the number of cities. The numbers have their own literary weight — thirteen for the Aaronid priests (matching the Gershonite total), ten for the rest of the Kohathites, twelve for the Merarites, summing to forty-eight. Forty-eight factors as twelve times four, recalling both the twelve tribes and the four corners of the land. The number is not arbitrary; it is the product of a symbolic arithmetic.

A significant literary device is the recurring notation "the city of refuge for the manslayer" (21:13, 21, 27, 32, 38), explicitly folding the six asylum cities of Joshua 20 into the Levitical catalog. The effect is structural: the asylum function is shown to be a subset of the Levitical function. The Levite is not only teacher and liturgist; he is also the keeper of the asylum. The city of refuge operates within a Levitical pastoral geography.

The most consequential literary moment is the coda. Joshua 21:43–45 is arguably the loudest theological sentence in the book. Noth famously argued that 21:43–45 is the original ending of the Deuteronomistic History's conquest section — that what follows (the tribal covenant renewals in 22–24) is a later addition, and that the book's original climax is this triumphant declaration. The rhetorical force of 21:43–45 creates a tension with Joshua 13:1 ("there remains yet very much land to possess") and with Judges 1 (which lists systematic failures to drive out the Canaanites). This tension is not incidental. It is a feature, not a bug, of the canonical shape: two moments of the same historical reality held in deliberate counterpoint, with the coda of Joshua 21 speaking the language of fulfilled promise and Judges 1 speaking the language of incomplete obedience.

Textual variants in the chapter are primarily in the city lists: the LXX and MT differ on a handful of names, and the 1 Chronicles 6 parallel includes both variants and additional names. Text-critical scholars including Auld and Nelson treat these differences as evidence of a shared base list with independent transmission histories rather than of meaning-affecting variants. More theologically interesting is that the summary in 21:43–45 is substantially identical in MT and LXX, which suggests it belonged to the core form of the book.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The chapter stands at the canonical hinge where promise and possession meet. It draws on and fulfills the prior tradition — Genesis 12:7, 15:18–21, 17:8 (the land promise to Abraham); Exodus 3:8, 33:1–3 (the land promise renewed through Moses); Numbers 18:20–24 and 35:1–8 (the specific Levitical allocation); Deuteronomy 18:1–8 (Levitical non-inheritance and support from tithes). It looks forward through Kings (where the Levitical cities are visible — Anathoth as the home of Abiathar, 1 Kings 2:26, and later Jeremiah; Gibeon as Solomon's sacrificial high place, 1 Kings 3:4) into Chronicles (1 Chronicles 6:54–81 as the parallel list, and the broader Levitical emphasis of Chronicles) and into the post-exilic restoration texts where the Levites become pedagogical and liturgical mediators.

**Jewish trajectory.** Second Temple sources reflect active interest. Philo treats the Levitical cities in *De Specialibus Legibus* 1.155ff as geographically distributed centers of priestly teaching, emphasizing the pedagogical function. Josephus (*Antiquities* 4.4.3–4) narrates the allocation as a Mosaic provision Joshua executes. Qumran texts, especially the *Temple Scroll* (11QT 44–45), imagine a reformed Levitical system. Rabbinic material treats Levitical cities primarily as halakhic cases: Mishnah *ʿArakhin* 9:8 specifies the non-transferability of Levitical urban property; Talmud Bavli *Makkot* 13a includes Levitical cities among those providing asylum to the manslayer. Medieval interpretation runs through Rashi on Numbers 35 and Joshua 21, and Ramban's extensive commentary on the priestly inheritance logic. Maimonides's *Mishneh Torah*, especially *Hilkhot Shemittah ve-Yovel* 13 and *Hilkhot Melakhim* 13:12, famously generalizes the Levitical calling: not only the tribe of Levi but "every single person from the inhabitants of the world whose spirit moves him and who understands to set himself apart to stand before Yahweh to serve him and know him" is counted in the Levitical category. Maimonides thereby reads Joshua 21 as an early figure for a universal priesthood of the discerning. Modern Jewish scholarship — Menahem Haran's *Temples and Temple-Service*, Yehezkel Kaufmann's arguments for pre-exilic priestly law, Moshe Weinfeld's *Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School*, Jacob Milgrom's priestly commentaries, Jon Levenson on the theology of divine dwelling — has built a rich literature that treats the Levitical city system as a genuine ancient institution theologically freighted with claims about distributed holiness.

**Christian trajectory.** Patristic interpretation reads the dispersed Levites typologically. Origen's *Homilies on Joshua* 25 treats the forty-eight cities as figures of apostolic distribution — the scattered Levite prefiguring the scattered apostle. Jerome follows Origen, and Augustine in *City of God* 17.4 connects Levitical landlessness to the church's status as sojourner. Bede's commentary on Joshua continues the apostolic-typological line. Aquinas (ST I-II q.101–104) treats the ceremonial law and Levitical provisions as pedagogical preparations for the incarnation. Reformation interpreters reconfigure the typology: Luther reads the Levitical distribution as a figure of the minister's dependence on the congregation; Calvin, in his *Commentary on the Four Last Books of Moses* and in passages of the *Institutes*, insists that the Levitical priesthood is abrogated in Christ but that the principle of the pastoral minister's embeddedness in the community endures. The Reformed tradition develops the analogical argument that congregations owe their ministers a living and a house, rooted in Levitical precedent. Critical scholarship — Noth reading the summary as the DtrH's original ending, Albright arguing for a tenth-century administrative list, Kaufmann defending pre-exilic priestly authorship, Benjamin Mazar's archaeological work on Levitical cities, A. Graeme Auld's *Joshua, Moses and the Land* (1980) on the relationship between Joshua and Deuteronomy, Richard Nelson's OTL commentary, Thomas Römer's more recent work on the DtrH — continues the geographical and redaction-critical investigation. Contemporary theological voices — Walter Brueggemann's *The Land* (1977, revised 2002) on the chapter's central theme, Christopher Wright's *Old Testament Ethics for the People of God*, Jeffrey Stackert's *Rewriting the Torah*, Richard Bauckham's *The Bible and Politics*, Jacqueline Lapsley's work on the ethical imagination of the Deuteronomistic corpus — integrate the chapter into discussions of social ethics, distributed leadership, and the theology of the land.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: "All came to pass" against "much land remains" — can the summary at 21:43–45 be true?**

Position one: the summary is straightforwardly historical. Yahweh has given Israel the land; the Canaanite pockets are within Israelite control but not yet subjugated, and the promise of 21:45 is a statement that the strategic conditions of the promise have been met. This is the traditional reading, held in various forms from the rabbinic midrashim through much of the Reformed tradition.

Position two: the summary is aspirational or eschatological. The Deuteronomistic Historian speaks in the voice of hope, not in the voice of empirical report. Brueggemann reads the summary as a liturgical acclamation that functions in Israel's worship regardless of its agreement with the political facts on the ground. On this reading, the tension with Judges 1 is not a contradiction but the difference between the language of liturgy and the language of historiography.

Position three (the steelman, least intuitive): the summary and Judges 1 are both true, because the promise operates at the level of divine commitment while fulfillment operates at the level of human reception. Yahweh has in fact given the land; Israel has in fact received it; Israel has also failed to take full possession of what has been given. The summary is not naive optimism but a rigorous theological claim that the divine side of the covenant is complete even when the human side is partial. Augustine develops this implicitly in his distinction between what God has done and what the pilgrim city is still doing. Where this leaves us: "all came to pass" is not contradicted by human failure; the divine promise can be fulfilled even while human reception is ongoing, and this asymmetry is itself the theological shape of covenant.

**Tension 2: Priestly landlessness against priestly urban presence — what does the Levite's dual status mean?**

Position one (the landlessness reading): the Levites have no inheritance because Yahweh is their portion (Numbers 18:20); the forty-eight cities are a provision for living but not a possession in the tribal-territorial sense. The Levite's calling is symbolically dependent; he lives among the tribes to teach and mediate, not to own. This reading emphasizes Maimonides's universalizing move — any person of discerning spirit can enter the Levitical vocation by forgoing the fiction of permanent possession.

Position two (the distributed-inheritance reading): the Levites have received a genuine, if non-contiguous, territorial allocation. Forty-eight cities plus pasturelands is a substantial holding, and the scattered geography ensures priestly presence in every tribe. The Levite is not less landed but differently landed — embedded, distributed, networked. Haran's *Temples and Temple-Service* develops this reading.

Position three (the sacred-diaspora reading, the steelman): the Levite is both landless and landed precisely in order to figure something new — a mode of belonging that is neither ownership nor exile. The Levite's city is his to live in but not his to sell; it is embedded within another tribe's *naḥălâ* but is Levitical in function. This third status prefigures the biblical trajectory toward a covenant community whose belonging is not primarily geographic. It is the same logical move that later generates the prophetic ethic of sojourning (Jeremiah 29:4–7 in Babylon), the Chronicler's priestly emphasis after 587 BCE, the Christian theology of the church as a pilgrim people, and the rabbinic reconstruction of Judaism after 70 CE around portable Torah rather than fixed temple. Where this leaves us: the Levite's peculiar status is not a lower form of belonging but a different and possibly more durable form — a belonging that survives the loss of territorial continuity.

One unresolved question: When promise and fulfillment are held apart — when the divine side is complete and the human side is partial — who gets to say whether the covenant is being kept?

## The Thread

Numbers 35:1–8 (the Mosaic command for Levitical cities, with the specific dimensions of the *migrāš*). 1 Chronicles 6:54–81 (the parallel list, differing in several names, pointing to shared tradition with independent transmission). Jeremiah 1:1 (Jeremiah of Anathoth — a Levitical city producing a prophetic voice centuries later). Hebrews 4:8–10 ("if Joshua had given them rest, God would not have spoken of another day later on" — the Christian reappropriation of Joshua 21:44's *mĕnûḥâ*, insisting that the promised rest remains open).
