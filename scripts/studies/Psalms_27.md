# Psalm 27 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 27 carries the superscription *le-david* (לְדָוִד), the standard Davidic attribution. In the Septuagint manuscript tradition, several witnesses add *pro tou christhēnai* ("before he was anointed") — a later interpretive expansion suggesting a setting early in David's career. Most contemporary scholars (Goldingay, Hossfeld-Zenger, Tate) treat the superscription as editorial; the LXX expansion shows how ancient readers tried to anchor the psalm in a Davidic biographical moment.

Form-critically, Psalm 27 has been one of the most contested compositions in the Psalter. **Hermann Gunkel** in his *Einleitung in die Psalmen* (1933) argued that vv. 1-6 and vv. 7-14 represent **two originally separate compositions** later joined: the first half is a confident **psalm of trust** (*Vertrauenspsalm*), the second half is an **individual lament** (*Klagelied des Einzelnen*) with the standard elements of complaint, petition, and expression of confidence. The differences in tone are striking: vv. 1-6 are buoyant, declarative, almost triumphant; vv. 7-12 are urgent, vulnerable, pleading; vv. 13-14 are climactic resolution. Gunkel's compositional split was influential through much of the twentieth century, picked up by Mowinckel and others.

The mainstream contemporary reading, however, has reversed this judgment. **Goldingay**, **Hossfeld-Zenger** (*Psalms 1*, Hermeneia, 2005), **Tate**, and **Patrick Miller** all argue that the unity of Psalm 27 is **deliberate and theologically significant** — the psalm dramatizes the dialectic of trust and distress that constitutes the actual life of faith. Hossfeld-Zenger calls it "a paradigmatic example of how confidence and crying-out belong together." The psalm refuses to allow trust and lament to be separated. The genre is best classified as a **hybrid trust-lament** within Gunkel's broad categories, sometimes called a **psalm of confidence with embedded lament** (Westermann's terminology).

The psalm's most famous opening line is *Yahweh ori veyish'i mi-mi ira* (יְהוָה אוֹרִי וְיִשְׁעִי מִמִּי אִירָא, "the LORD is my light and my salvation, whom shall I fear?", 27:1). The metaphor of God as *or* (אוֹר, "light") is unusual in the Hebrew Bible — explicitly applied to Yahweh only here and Micah 7:8. In the broader ancient Near Eastern context, the sun-god (Egyptian Ra/Re, Mesopotamian Shamash) was associated with light, justice, and revelation; the psalm's claim that *Yahweh* is light could be read as a polemical appropriation of solar-deity vocabulary. *Yish'i* (יִשְׁעִי, "my salvation/deliverance") is the noun form of the root *yasha* (the same root as Yeshua/Jesus). *Ma'oz chayyai* (מָעוֹז־חַיַּי, "stronghold of my life," 27:1b) draws on military fortification imagery.

The central petition in 27:4 is *achat sha'alti me-et Yahweh otah avaqqesh shivti beveit-Yahweh kol-yemei chayyai* (אַחַת שָׁאַלְתִּי מֵאֵת־יְהוָה אוֹתָהּ אֲבַקֵּשׁ שִׁבְתִּי בְּבֵית־יְהוָה כָּל־יְמֵי חַיָּי, "one thing have I asked of the LORD, that will I seek after: that I may dwell in the house of the LORD all the days of my life"). The verb *shivti* (from *yashav*, "to sit, dwell") with the temple as object is striking; in normal Israelite practice only priests had functional residence at the sanctuary. The phrase has occasioned much debate: literal residence, repeated pilgrimage, or an idealized longing? Most commentators (Mays, Goldingay, Tate) read it as the latter — a poetic intensification expressing the orientation of the speaker's whole life toward the divine presence.

The phrase *lachazot beno'am-Yahweh* (לַחֲזוֹת בְּנֹעַם־יְהוָה, "to gaze upon the beauty/pleasantness of the LORD," 27:4) uses the verb *chazah* (חָזָה, "to gaze, behold, see in vision") — the same verb used for prophetic vision (*chozeh* = "seer"). *No'am* (נֹעַם) means delight, pleasantness, or beauty. The phrase has been a major source-text for the **beatific vision** tradition in Christian theology and the *uncreated light* tradition in Eastern Orthodox spirituality.

The psalm's anguished line in 27:10 — *ki-avi ve-immi azavuni va-Yahweh ya'asfeni* (כִּי־אָבִי וְאִמִּי עֲזָבוּנִי וַיהוָה יַאַסְפֵנִי, "for my father and my mother have forsaken me, but the LORD will take me in") — has occasioned intense interpretive debate (see Lens 4).

Textually, the MT of Psalm 27 is reasonably stable; the Dead Sea Scrolls fragments containing portions (4QPs⁴, 11QPsᵃ does not include this psalm) align with the consonantal MT. The LXX translates the psalm without major divergence.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Psalm 27 is fourteen verses of carefully shaped Hebrew poetry that exhibits Lowth's parallelism in all three modes:

- **Synonymous**: "The LORD is my light and my salvation" / "the LORD is the stronghold of my life" (27:1) — with the rhetorical questions "whom shall I fear? / of whom shall I be afraid?"
- **Antithetic**: "My father and my mother have forsaken me, / but the LORD will take me in" (27:10).
- **Synthetic**: "I believe that I shall look upon the goodness of the LORD / in the land of the living" (27:13) — the second line specifies the realm of the seeing.

The strophic structure breaks naturally into:

- **Strophe 1 (27:1-3)**: Confidence in Yahweh as light, salvation, stronghold; enemies will stumble.
- **Strophe 2 (27:4-6)**: The "one thing" — life-orientation toward the sanctuary; promise of refuge in trouble.
- **Strophe 3 (27:7-10)**: Lament's pivot — urgent prayer, plea against divine hiding, trust transcending parental abandonment.
- **Strophe 4 (27:11-12)**: Petition for guidance, protection from false witnesses.
- **Strophe 5 (27:13-14)**: Confident climax and self-exhortation to wait.

The pivot at 27:7 (*shema-Yahweh qoli eqra*, "Hear, O LORD, when I cry aloud") is the dramatic turning point; the speaker who declared "I will not fear" in 27:1 now begs to be heard. This is the crux that Gunkel read as evidence of two separate psalms but that Hossfeld-Zenger reads as deliberate dialectic.

Several literary devices unify the whole:

- **Vocabulary inclusio**: "The LORD is my salvation" (*yish'i*, 27:1) and "God of my salvation" (*Elohei yish'i*, 27:9) and "wait for the LORD" (27:14) form a chain.
- **The "face" motif**: God's face (*panim*) appears in 27:8 (twice) and 27:9 — the central organizing image of the lament half.
- **The seeking-and-waiting frame**: "I seek" (27:4, 8) and "wait" (27:14) bracket the psalm's volitional posture.
- **Final self-address**: 27:14 is unusual — the psalmist exhorts himself (or addresses the worshipping community) with imperatives, the same rhetorical move as Psalm 42-43's refrain "Why are you cast down, O my soul?"

The psalm has no *selah* markers. Some commentators (Tate) see a chiastic embedding where the central panel (27:7-10) is the lament and the outer panels (27:1-6 and 27:11-14) are confidence — but the structure is supple rather than rigid.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

**Placement in Book I.** Psalm 27 completes the triplet Pss 25-26-27 (Davidic prayers of integrity and confidence) and stands in striking proximity to Pss 22 (the great cry of dereliction) and 23 (the shepherd psalm). The Psalter's editors placed Psalm 27 such that "the LORD is my light" answers the questions raised earlier in Book I about whether the suffering righteous will ultimately see the goodness of God.

**Jewish trajectory.** Psalm 27 is one of the most **liturgically central** psalms in Jewish life. It is recited **twice daily during the month of Elul** (the penitential preparation before the High Holy Days) and continues through *Hoshana Rabbah* (the seventh day of Sukkot). The *Sephardic* tradition extends its recitation through *Shemini Atzeret*. This places Psalm 27 at the center of the Jewish year's most introspective and spiritually intense weeks. The traditional reason given (cited in *Mateh Moshe* and other halakhic sources): the psalm's themes — light, refuge, divine seeking, fear and confidence — match the High Holy Day mood, and a midrash reads "my light" (*ori*) as a hint at *Rosh Hashanah*, "my salvation" (*yish'i*) as *Yom Kippur*, and "shelter/sukkah" (27:5) as *Sukkot*.

*Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 27 expands the "father and mother forsook me" line: David is presented as one whose conception was suspect (a midrashic tradition develops around 1 Sam 16's framing of David as the youngest, almost-forgotten son), so even his parents could not fully claim him. **Rashi** glosses 27:10 by referring to this midrash and reads the verse as a statement of David's trust that, where human kinship fails, divine adoption holds. **Ibn Ezra** is more sober, reading the verse as hyperbole expressing extreme distress. **Radak** notes that "though my father and mother forsake me" is conditional — even if such a thing happened, Yahweh would receive me. Modern Jewish thought (Buber, Heschel, Levenson, Sarna) has emphasized the psalm's dialectic of presence and hiddenness — Heschel's *I asked for wonder* tradition draws on 27:4's longing to "gaze upon the beauty" as a paradigm of religious awe.

**Christian trajectory.** **Augustine**'s *Enarratio in Psalmum 26* (LXX numbering = Ps 27 MT) is one of his most luminous psalm commentaries. He reads "the LORD is my light" as a Trinitarian text — the Father is light from whom the Son proceeds as Light from Light. He develops the *one thing necessary* (27:4) as the contemplative life, paralleling Mary's choice in Luke 10:42. **Athanasius** in his *Letter to Marcellinus* recommends Psalm 27 for those facing persecution. **Cassiodorus** treats the psalm as a christological prophecy: the speaker is Christ uniting head and members.

**Aquinas** in his *Postilla super Psalmos* uses 27:4 as a key text for the **beatific vision** doctrine — the final end of the rational creature is "to gaze upon the beauty of the Lord." **Catherine of Siena** and the medieval mystical tradition develop "to gaze upon the beauty" as the contemplative summit. **Eastern Orthodox theology**, especially Gregory Palamas and the Hesychast tradition, reads "your face, LORD, do I seek" (27:8) and "the beauty of the LORD" (27:4) as references to the *uncreated light* — the divine energies that the contemplative encounters in *theoria*. Psalm 27 is a major liturgical psalm in Orthodox practice, particularly during the Lenten triodion.

**Luther**'s *Operationes in Psalmos* read 27:1 as the gospel itself: Christ is the believer's light and salvation. **Calvin** (1557 commentary) emphasizes the psalm's pastoral application to believers under pressure: "though armies encamp against me" — the church's situation in every age. He warns against treating "to dwell in the house of the LORD" as monastic withdrawal; rather, it is the orientation of the heart that any believer can and must cultivate.

**Modern critical scholarship.** Gunkel's two-psalm hypothesis dominated mid-twentieth-century scholarship; Mowinckel followed him in seeing 27:7-14 as cultically distinct. **Westermann** noted the unity of mood across petition and confidence. **Kraus** (*Psalmen 1-59*) defends compositional unity on rhetorical grounds. **Hossfeld-Zenger** see the psalm as a deliberate, post-exilic theological composition. **Goldingay** (*Psalms*, 2006): "the unity of trust and crying-out is precisely the testimony — they are not alternatives." **Brueggemann** classifies the psalm as **disorientation passing through to new orientation**: 27:1-6 is reorientation language, 27:7-12 returns to disorientation, and 27:13-14 reaches a second reorientation that is hard-won, not innocent. **Patrick Miller** emphasizes the psalm's dialectical character. **William P. Brown** highlights the visual vocabulary — "see," "gaze," "look upon" — as central to the psalm's spirituality. **Beth Tanner** reads the psalm as a model for prayer that holds confidence and need together.

**New Testament use.** Psalm 27 is not directly cited in the NT, but its vocabulary echoes throughout. "The LORD is my light" anticipates John 8:12 ("I am the light of the world"). "Wait for the LORD" (27:14) reverberates in 1 Corinthians 1:7 and the Pauline language of waiting. The "false witnesses" of 27:12 echo the false witnesses at Jesus' trial (Matt 26:60).

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The unity question.** Did the psalm always sound like this, or did its editors create the dialectic by joining two compositions?

**Position 1a (Gunkel, early form-criticism).** Two originally separate psalms were joined: vv. 1-6 was an independent psalm of confidence, vv. 7-14 was a lament. The unity is editorial.

**Position 1b (Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, mainstream contemporary).** The psalm is a **deliberate single composition** that dramatizes the dialectic of trust and distress. The shift in tone is the point — confidence and crying-out belong together in actual lived faith.

**Position 1c (intermediate, Tate).** The psalm may have been composed by an editor who used existing material but shaped it into a new whole — neither fully unitary nor merely concatenated.

**Steelmanning Position 1a**: The tonal shift is real and abrupt. A reader encountering 27:1-6 alone could plausibly read it as a self-contained psalm of confidence. The form-critical instinct to notice generic seams is not wrong, even if the editorial-unity reading wins. We should not flatten the psalm's startling structural choice.

**Where this leaves us on tension 1**: The unity reading is now mainstream and persuasive — partly because we have learned that ancient compositions worked by juxtaposition more freely than nineteenth-century scholarship assumed, and partly because the dialectic between the halves is theologically too fertile to be accidental. But Gunkel's insight should not be lost: the psalm's two moods are genuinely different, and the second half is not a continuation of the first but a fresh starting-over.

**Tension 2: The vision of God (27:4).** What does it mean to "gaze upon the beauty of the LORD"?

**Position 2a (Aquinas, Catholic-scholastic).** The verse points to the **beatific vision** — the final intellectual and affective contemplation of the divine essence that constitutes heavenly blessedness. The psalm's "all the days of my life" anticipates eternal life with God.

**Position 2b (Eastern Orthodox: Palamas, Hesychasm).** The verse refers to encounter with the **uncreated light** — the divine energies that the saints behold in *theoria*. The "beauty" (*no'am*) is the radiance of divine glory that the heart, purified through prayer, perceives.

**Position 2c (Reformed: Calvin, Owen).** The psalm describes a **relational encounter** rather than a metaphysical vision — to gaze upon the LORD is to know God in the means of grace (word, sacrament, prayer), with the eschatological horizon of seeing God face to face (1 John 3:2). The Reformed reading is wary of contemplative-vision claims that bypass Christ as mediator.

**Position 2d (Pentecostal-Charismatic and contemporary devotional).** The verse describes an experiential encounter with God's presence available now in worship — not necessarily the beatific vision but a real foretaste of it.

**Steelmanning the contemplative tradition**: It is striking that the Hebrew uses *chazah* — the prophetic-vision verb. The text does suggest something more than ordinary seeing. Reformed reticence about mystical claims sometimes flattens the psalm's clearly visual vocabulary. The psalmist asks for one thing — and that one thing is to behold. We should not too quickly translate "gaze" into "trust."

**Tension 3: "My father and my mother have forsaken me" (27:10).**

**Position 3a (Historical-Davidic).** The verse reflects an actual experience in David's life — perhaps the obscure beginning hinted at in 1 Sam 16, or rejection during the Saulide persecution. Rashi and Ibn Ezra take this kind of reading.

**Position 3b (Hyperbolic).** The verse is rhetorical extremity: *even if* parents abandoned me, God would take me in. This is Radak's reading and is favored by many modern commentators.

**Position 3c (Paradigmatic).** The verse expresses the experience of any believer facing the deepest possible human abandonment — the kind of final loneliness that drives one entirely into divine care. Augustine and the pastoral tradition develop this reading.

**Where this leaves us.** The three positions on each tension layer rather than compete. Psalm 27 is best read as a deliberately unified dialectic of trust and lament (1b), which uses the prophetic-vision verb to claim something real and not merely metaphorical about encountering God (across 2a-d), and which holds out the most extreme possible human abandonment as the limit at which divine adoption holds (3c, while not denying the historical and rhetorical dimensions of 3a-b). The psalm refuses to choose between confidence and need, between vision and waiting, between divine presence and the ache of divine hiding. To pray Psalm 27 is to inhabit all of these at once.

**Closing question.** What would it mean to ask God for "one thing" — and could you name yours without immediately listing twelve other things you also need?

## The Thread

- **Exodus 33:18-23** — Moses' "Show me your glory" — the foundational text for "to gaze upon the beauty of the LORD" and the seeking-the-face vocabulary of 27:8.
- **John 8:12** — "I am the light of the world" — Christ's appropriation of the *Yahweh ori* opening.
- **Romans 8:31-39** — "If God is for us, who can be against us?" — the rhetorical questions of 27:1 echo into Paul's climactic confidence.
- **1 John 3:2** — "We shall see him as he is" — the eschatological completion of "gazing upon the beauty of the LORD."
