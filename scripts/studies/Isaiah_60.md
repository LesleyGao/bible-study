# Isaiah 60 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: Historical World

Isaiah 60 is one of the great soaring eschatological visions of the Hebrew Bible and the structural center of Third Isaiah's restoration-vision. On the standard mainstream-critical division (Bernhard Duhm's tripartite division, defended in Joseph Blenkinsopp's Anchor Bible commentary 2000-2003, Claus Westermann's *Isaiah 40-66* OTL, Paul Hanson's *The Dawn of Apocalyptic*, John Goldingay's ICC, Patricia Tull, Jacob Stromberg's *Isaiah After Exile* 2011), the chapter belongs to anonymous post-exilic prophecy of Trito-Isaiah (chapters 56-66), composed in Persian-period Yehud roughly between the late sixth and fifth centuries BC. Hanson's *The Dawn of Apocalyptic* identifies Isaiah 60-62 as the heart of Trito-Isaiah's visionary-prophetic program — three chapters of soaring restoration-vision that articulate the alternative future the visionary party held out against the realities of post-exilic decline.

The unified-authorship defense (Brevard Childs canonically; Alec Motyer; John Oswalt) reads Isaiah 60 as eighth-century material from Isaiah ben Amoz, with the eschatological-Zion vision as predictive prophecy. The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran (1QIsa-a, ca. 125 BC) preserves the chapter as part of the unified 66-chapter book. The chapter is included in the Jewish lectionary as the seventh haftarah of consolation (the climactic week of the *shiva d'nechemta* — the *Seven of Consolation* between Tisha B'Av and Rosh Hashanah).

The chapter opens with what may be the most famous imperative in Hebrew prophecy: *Arise, shine, for your light has come, and the glory of the LORD has risen upon you* (60:1). The Hebrew קוּמִי אוֹרִי כִּי בָא אוֹרֵךְ וּכְבוֹד יְהוָה עָלַיִךְ זָרָח (*qumi ori ki ba orekh u-khvod Yahweh alayikh zarach*) is feminine-singular throughout — the verbs *qumi* (arise) and *ori* (shine) are addressed to a feminine subject, identified through the chapter as Zion/Jerusalem personified as a woman. The verb זָרַח (*zarach*, has dawned/risen) is the same verb used for the rising sun (cf. Genesis 32:31, the sun rises on Jacob; Malachi 4:2, the sun of righteousness rises). The image is sunrise: Zion has been in night, and the dawn is breaking.

The contrast in 60:2 — *for behold, darkness shall cover the earth, and thick darkness the peoples; but the LORD will arise upon you* — establishes a cosmic-eschatological dualism. The earth is in darkness (חֹשֶׁךְ, *choshekh*) and *thick darkness* (עֲרָפֶל, *arafel*, the same word used for Sinai's storm-cloud in Exodus 20:21), but Zion is illumined. Yahweh's glory has risen specifically upon Zion. The asymmetry is theologically pointed: Zion's light is not its own; it is the reflection of divine glory.

The chapter unfolds the vision in concentric rings. Nations come to Zion's light (60:3). Sons and daughters return from afar (60:4). The wealth of the nations flows to Zion (60:5). Camels of Midian and Ephah, with gold and frankincense from Sheba (60:6). Flocks of Kedar and Nebaioth (60:7). Ships of Tarshish (60:9). Foreigners build the walls (60:10). The gates are open continually so wealth and kings can enter in procession (60:11). The cypress, plane, and pine trees of Lebanon come to beautify the sanctuary (60:13). Former enemies bow down (60:14). The community sucks the milk of nations and nurses at the breast of kings (60:16). Bronze is replaced by gold, iron by silver, wood by bronze, stones by iron (60:17) — material upgrade across the board. Violence is heard no more in the land (60:18).

The famous closing vision (60:19-20) declares that the sun and moon will no longer be Zion's light, *but the LORD will be your everlasting light, and your God will be your glory*. The Hebrew וְהָיָה־לָךְ יְהוָה לְאוֹר עוֹלָם (*ve-hayah-lakh Yahweh le-or olam*) — *and Yahweh shall be your everlasting light* — articulates an eschatological vision in which the cosmic luminaries themselves are surpassed. This will be picked up directly in Revelation 21:23 — *And the city has no need of sun or moon to shine on it, for the glory of God is its light, and its lamp is the Lamb*.

The chapter's specific commodities deserve historical attention. *Camels of Midian and Ephah... gold and frankincense from Sheba* (60:6) names the Arabian-trade-routes that converged on the Levant. Midian and Ephah were Arabian peoples (cf. Genesis 25:2-4); Sheba was a south-Arabian kingdom in modern Yemen. *Frankincense* (לְבוֹנָה, *levonah*) was a major luxury commodity from Arabia. *Kedar and Nebaioth* (60:7) were north-Arabian tribes. *Tarshish* (60:9) was probably Tartessos in Spain, the western terminus of Phoenician trade. The geographic scope encompasses east (Arabia) and west (Tarshish) — the totality of known international commerce flowing toward Zion.

## Lens 2: Literature

Isaiah 60 is poetry of the highest prophetic order, working in the genre of Zion-song and eschatological-restoration vision. The chapter is cast as direct address to Zion-as-female-person, with Yahweh as speaker throughout. The structural rhythm alternates between imperatives addressed to Zion (*arise, shine*; *lift up your eyes*) and declarations about what is coming (*nations shall come*; *foreigners shall build*).

The chapter's central literary device is the *light/darkness* contrast that frames the entire poem. The opening (60:1-3) establishes the contrast: light upon Zion, darkness over the peoples. The closing (60:19-20) returns to the contrast at a higher pitch: not just Yahweh's light upon Zion but Yahweh *as* Zion's light. The frame transforms across the chapter. At the start, Yahweh's glory has *risen upon* Zion (an external dawning); at the close, Yahweh *is* Zion's everlasting light (an indwelling). The literary movement parallels theological deepening: external dawn becomes constitutive presence.

The procession-imagery is one of the chapter's most distinctive literary features. Camels covering the land (60:6), doves flying to their windows (60:8), ships of Tarshish bringing children from afar (60:9), kings led in procession through open gates (60:11), the glory of Lebanon coming to beautify the sanctuary (60:13), former enemies bending low (60:14) — the cumulative effect is of all creation in motion toward Zion. The Hebrew verb בּוֹא (*bo*, come) recurs throughout, with all motion oriented inward. Zion does not go out; the world comes in.

The metallurgical imagery of 60:17 — *instead of bronze I will bring gold, and instead of iron I will bring silver; instead of wood, bronze, instead of stones, iron* — deploys what literary scholars call *grade-progression*. Each material is replaced by the next-higher grade. This is not merely material upgrade; it is an inversion of the *ages of metal* mythology common in the ancient world (cf. Hesiod's *Works and Days*, where humanity descends from gold age to silver to bronze to iron). Where the Greek mythological pattern moved downward, Isaiah 60 reverses: the eschatological age moves upward through the metals. The post-exilic community, which lives in iron-age conditions, is promised a new bronze age, then silver, then gold.

The closing imagery (60:19-22) deploys cosmic-luminary surpassing. The sun and moon — the great cosmic light-sources of Genesis 1:14-19 — are surpassed by Yahweh's direct presence. *Your sun shall no more go down, nor your moon withdraw itself; for the LORD will be your everlasting light* (60:20). This is eschatological cosmology: the structures of creation as currently constituted are transcended. The promise *your days of mourning shall be ended* (וְשָׁלְמוּ יְמֵי אֶבְלֵךְ, *ve-shalemu yemei evlekh*) closes the lament-trajectory that has run through Lamentations and the exilic-mourning tradition.

The closing line — *I am the LORD; in its time I will hasten it* (60:22) — combines patient timing (*in its time*) with urgency (*I will hasten it*). The Hebrew בְּעִתָּהּ אֲחִישֶׁנָּה (*be-ittah achishennah*, *in its time I will hasten it*) is paradoxical: God's timing is fixed, and yet the action will come with urgency when the time comes. The Talmud (*Bavli Sanhedrin* 98a) makes this paradox the basis for a famous discussion of the messianic timing — *if they are worthy, I will hasten it; if not, in its time*.

## Lens 3: Larger Story

In the prophetic canon, Isaiah 60 is the climactic Zion-vision of the Hebrew Bible. The chapter draws on and extends earlier traditions: Isaiah 2:2-4 (nations streaming to the mountain of Yahweh's house), Micah 4:1-5 (parallel vision), Zechariah 8:20-23 (peoples of every language seizing the robe of a Jew, saying *we have heard that God is with you*), Psalm 72 (kings bringing tribute to the messianic king), and Psalm 87 (Zion as the mother of all peoples). What earlier texts hint at, Isaiah 60 articulates in soaring detail.

In Jewish trajectory, Isaiah 60 is the climactic seventh haftarah of consolation, read in synagogue on the Sabbath before Rosh Hashanah. The seven Sabbaths between Tisha B'Av (the ninth of Av, commemorating the destruction of both temples) and the New Year are the *shiva d'nechemta* — the *Seven Consolations* — drawn from Second and Third Isaiah. Isaiah 60 closes the cycle with the highest-pitched restoration-vision: *arise, shine, for your light has come*. The lectionary placement is theologically masterful: after a year of mourning the destroyed temple, the community hears the eschatological promise that Zion will rise.

The medieval rabbinic commentators (Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Radak) read Isaiah 60 as messianic-eschatological — the vision applies to the *yemot ha-mashiach* (the messianic days). Maimonides (Rambam) in *Hilkhot Melakhim* draws on Isaiah 60 to articulate the messianic age as a historical-political restoration of Zion plus the gathering of the dispersed Jewish people. The *aliyah* movements of modern Zionism, both religious and secular, have drawn on Isaiah 60 imagery for the return of Jews to the land — *your sons shall come from afar, and your daughters shall be carried on the hip* (60:4). The chapter functions as one of the foundational prophetic texts for modern Jewish national-restoration consciousness.

In Christian trajectory, Isaiah 60 has had two especially load-bearing receptions. The first is the Magi tradition. Matthew 2:1-12 narrates the visit of the *magoi from the east* who bring gold and frankincense (and myrrh) to the infant Jesus. The Christian tradition — at least from the early church Fathers (Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 78; Origen, *Contra Celsum* 1.59-60) — has read this episode as a fulfillment of Isaiah 60:6 (*they shall bring gold and frankincense*) and 60:3 (*nations shall come to your light, and kings to the brightness of your rising*). Matthew himself does not quote Isaiah 60 directly, but he quotes the related Micah 5:2 and other Hebrew texts that situate the Magi-visit in the broader Hebrew prophetic-Zion tradition. The patristic and medieval Christian tradition developed the Magi-as-three-kings tradition (the names Caspar, Melchior, Balthasar appear in early medieval texts) on the basis of Isaiah 60. The Feast of Epiphany (January 6) celebrates this Magi-visit and is one of the major Christian feasts that draws explicitly on Isaiah 60 — *arise, shine, for your light has come* is a standard Epiphany text in Western Christianity.

The second major Christian reception is Revelation 21-22 — the New Jerusalem vision. Revelation 21:23-26 directly echoes Isaiah 60:19-20 and 60:11: *And the city has no need of sun or moon to shine on it, for the glory of God is its light, and its lamp is the Lamb. By its light will the nations walk, and the kings of the earth will bring their glory into it, and its gates will never be shut by day — and there will be no night there. They will bring into it the glory and the honor of the nations*. The structural correspondence is unmistakable. John of Patmos has read Isaiah 60 carefully, and the New Jerusalem vision of Revelation 21-22 is a Christological-eschatological extension of the Trito-Isaianic Zion-vision. What Isaiah 60 promised to the post-exilic Zion, Revelation realizes in the new Jerusalem of the eschaton.

Pauline theology draws on Isaiah 60 less directly but builds on its universalism. The *fullness of the Gentiles* (Romans 11:25) and the eschatological gathering-in of the nations into the people of God reflect the Hebrew Zion-vision of which Isaiah 60 is the climax.

In Catholic liturgy, Isaiah 60:1-6 is the first reading for the Solemnity of the Epiphany of the Lord (January 6 in the traditional calendar, transferred to the nearest Sunday in many modern observances). The lectionary placement makes Isaiah 60 the foundational Hebrew text for Christian Christmas-Epiphany theology — the rising light of Zion is read Christologically as the light that comes to all nations in Christ.

The *arise, shine* imperative has shaped Christian liturgical practice and Christian hymnody. The opening line is the basis for Charles Wesley's hymn *Arise, my soul, arise!* (1742), for the Anglican Epiphany hymn *Brightest and best of the sons of the morning* (Reginald Heber, 1811), for the African-American spiritual tradition's *Rise, shine, give God the glory*, and for countless modern worship songs. The Hebrew imperative *qumi ori* has become a foundational Christian liturgical-imperative.

In Reformed reception, Isaiah 60 has been a major text for missional theology — the gathering of the nations to Christ's light. John Calvin's commentary on Isaiah dwells at length on the chapter as foretelling the Christian-era gathering of Gentiles. Modern Reformed missiology (David Bosch, *Transforming Mission*; Lesslie Newbigin, *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society*) draws on the Isaiah 60 vision of nations streaming to Zion's light as the Hebrew-prophetic anticipation of the church's missional vocation.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

Three large tensions inhabit this chapter, and honest reading requires holding them.

The first tension concerns the *Magi-from-the-East* Christian-Christmas reception. The Matthean Magi-visit is read in dominant Christian tradition as the fulfillment of Isaiah 60:3, 60:6 — kings and nations bringing gold and frankincense to the rising light. The mainstream historical-critical reading of Matthew 2 (Raymond Brown, *The Birth of the Messiah* 1977/1993; Ulrich Luz, *Matthew 1-7*; Dale Allison, *The New Moses*) acknowledges Matthew's deep engagement with Hebrew prophetic texts but is more cautious about the historicity of the Magi-visit as narrated. The honest engagement preserves both the typological-theological move (Matthew is reading Isaiah 60 Christologically, and that reading has shaped two millennia of Christian devotion) and the historical-critical question (whether the narrative is historical-reportorial or theological-typological in genre).

The Catholic and Orthodox traditions celebrate Epiphany as one of the major Christian feasts, with Isaiah 60 as the foundational reading. The Protestant traditions vary in their observance of Epiphany but generally retain Isaiah 60 in their lectionaries. Whatever the historical question about the Magi, the theological-typological reading is canonical: the early Christian tradition read the gathering of the nations to Christ as the realization of Isaiah 60.

The second tension concerns the *eschatological-Zion* theology and competing readings. The Christian-typological reading sees Isaiah 60 as fulfilled in Revelation 21-22 — the new Jerusalem of the eschaton. The Jewish-eschatological reading preserves the messianic-age vision of historical-Zion restoration. The mainstream-critical reading sees in Isaiah 60 a post-exilic restoration-vision projected onto the eschatological future — the chapter began as a hopeful vision for the rebuilt Persian-period Zion and was gradually re-read as eschatological when historical realities did not match the soaring imagery.

These three readings are not incompatible if we recognize the canonical-historical layering. The text was probably composed as a post-exilic restoration-vision, was received in Jewish tradition as messianic-eschatological, and was read in Christian tradition as fulfilled christologically (in Christ's first coming) and consummated eschatologically (in the new Jerusalem of Christ's second coming). The text has functioned at all three levels in different communities.

The honest engagement asks about the dimensions of the vision that have proven uncomfortable. Isaiah 60:12 — *for the nation and kingdom that will not serve you shall perish; those nations shall be utterly laid waste* — strikes a sharp note that has been quietly elided in many liturgical receptions. The vision of nations streaming to Zion's light includes the implicit threat of judgment for those who refuse. Christian-supersessionist appropriations have sometimes weaponized this imagery against Jewish communities (a tragic inversion, given the chapter's Hebrew origin); modern Jewish-nationalist appropriations have sometimes used it for political-territorial claims. Both moves do violence to the text. The vision is theological-eschatological, not a political program; the *foreigners shall build up your walls* of 60:10 is an eschatological-doxological promise, not a current-political license. To read Isaiah 60 honestly is to preserve its soaring vision while resisting its instrumentalization.

The third tension concerns the *arise, shine* imperative as Jewish and Christian liturgical-imperative. The chapter has functioned across two-and-a-half millennia as a summons — to the post-exilic community, to the haftarah-listening synagogue, to the Christian Epiphany congregation, to the gospel-proclaiming missional church, to the modern worship-singer. The honest engagement asks what the imperative actually demands. The Hebrew is unambiguously feminine-singular: it is addressed to Zion-as-woman. The pluralizing-democratizing application to all listeners is a real interpretive move, with both Jewish and Christian warrant in the way the text has been received. But the original specificity matters: it is Zion that is summoned to arise; the light has *come* upon Zion; the nations are coming *to* Zion. The imperative is not merely a generic call to spiritual awakening; it is a specific summons to the people of God to take its place as the locus of divine glory in the world.

Where this leaves us: Isaiah 60 is the soaring eschatological-Zion vision of the Hebrew Bible. It articulates a future in which Yahweh's glory is so concentrated upon his people that the nations stream toward it, the cosmic luminaries are surpassed, and the structures of mourning are ended. The Jewish reading preserves the messianic-eschatological dimension; the Christian reading sees the vision realized in Christ and consummated in the new Jerusalem; the mainstream-critical reading insists on the post-exilic context and the layered receptions. The text has shaped Jewish synagogue liturgy, Christian Epiphany devotion, the Magi tradition, the Revelation 21-22 vision, modern Zionism, modern Christian missiology, and countless hymns and worship songs.

The closing question: when the prophet says *arise, shine, for your light has come*, does the imperative require waiting for the light or living as if the light had already come? Is the eschatological-vision a future-promise that grants present-permission to live differently, or is it a call to participate in something already underway? And what does it mean, for any community that claims this vision, to have its gates open continually — to refuse the closure that would protect it from those who might come?

## The Thread

Isaiah 60 is the structural center of Trito-Isaiah's restoration-vision. Chapter 56 opened the section with radical inclusion (foreigners and eunuchs welcomed); chapters 57-59 dealt with idolatry, true fasting, and the iniquities that separate the community from God; chapter 60 returns to the soaring eschatological-Zion vision; chapters 61-62 will continue with the prophetic-anointed proclamation (the text Jesus reads at Nazareth) and the new-name promise. The chapter's *arise, shine* is the Trito-Isaianic imperative that gathers the inclusion-vision of 56, the justice-demand of 58, and the diagnostic of 59 into a doxological summons. The light has come; the only question is whether the community will rise to meet it.
