# Psalm 25: The Acrostic of the Lonely Pupil

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The superscription is the spare *le-David* (לְדָוִד), "Of David." Form-critically, Psalm 25 is a hybrid — an *individual lament* (Gunkel's *Klagelied des Einzelnen*) interwoven with *wisdom-instructional* elements (the *Lehrgedicht* tradition). The crying voice asks God to teach him; the petitioner is also a pupil. This hybridity is part of what makes Ps 25 distinctive within the Psalter and difficult to slot cleanly into a single Gunkel category.

The most striking formal feature is the **alphabetic acrostic**. Each verse (with notable irregularities) begins with a successive letter of the Hebrew alphabet. A complete acrostic Psalter (Pss 9–10 are jointly acrostic, 25, 34, 37, 111, 112, 119, 145) shows the form was a beloved instrument of Hebrew religious poetry. The other clear acrostics in the Hebrew Bible include the four chapters of Lamentations (chapters 1, 2, 4 are simple alphabetic acrostics; chapter 3 is a triple acrostic; chapter 5 has 22 verses but is not acrostic) and the praise of the *eshet chayil* in Proverbs 31:10–31.

But Psalm 25's acrostic is *irregular*. It lacks one Hebrew letter (the *waw*) at the expected position (between v. 5's *he* and v. 7's *zayin* — the *waw* line is absent or merged), and it has the letter *resh* twice (vv. 18 and 19 both begin with *resh*) instead of the expected sequence. The final verse (v. 22) begins with *peh*, but it is sometimes counted as a closing addition rather than the acrostic's natural ending. The most common scholarly interpretations of these irregularities:

1. **Damaged transmission**: a copyist error early in the tradition, with the missing *waw* line lost. Possible but doesn't explain the doubled *resh*.

2. **Stylized completion**: the irregularity is intentional, perhaps to allow the psalm's first, middle, and last letters (in the MT, *aleph*, *lamed*, and *peh*) to spell out the consonants of *aleph* (which in turn forms the word *aluph*, "I will teach" or related teaching vocabulary; see Adele Berlin's analysis). On this reading, the acrostic encodes the wisdom theme.

3. **Liturgical adaptation**: v. 22 (the *peh* line, "Redeem Israel, O God, out of all his troubles") was added to the original acrostic to give the individual prayer a corporate-Israel ending suitable for synagogue use. This is the position of Mowinckel and many critical commentators.

The acrostic form itself has theological significance. Adele Berlin's *Biblical Poetry Through Medieval Jewish Eyes* and her *The Dynamics of Biblical Parallelism* both argue that the alphabetic acrostic is a form symbolizing *completeness* — A to Z (or *aleph* to *taw*) covers everything, the whole alphabet, the totality of language. To pray an acrostic is to pray with the whole alphabet, to leave nothing unsaid. This is especially important in a wisdom-instructional context: the teaching covers the whole.

The cult-functional setting (Mowinckel) is harder to fix. The wisdom-pedagogical features point to a setting in *learned piety* — perhaps the temple school, perhaps post-exilic synagogue-precursor settings, perhaps private devotion. The lament features point to a more concrete distress. The hybrid form may reflect a hybrid setting: a sufferer trained in wisdom traditions who prays in the forms he has learned.

ANE parallels for acrostic prayers are few but exist. The Babylonian *Acrostic Theodicy* is a notable parallel — a wisdom-poetic dialogue using acrostic structure to explore divine justice. Egyptian school texts use alphabetic-listing forms but without the same theological weight.

Three Hebrew terms control Psalm 25's theology:

- *derek* (דֶּרֶךְ, "way," vv. 4, 8, 9, 12) — the pedagogical *way*, the instructed path. The verb *yoreh* (יוֹרֶה, "he instructs/teaches") and the noun *torah* are cognate. To know the *derek* is to be discipled.
- *hesed* (חֶסֶד, "steadfast love" or "covenant loyalty," vv. 6, 7, 10) — appearing three times, the controlling theological term. *Hesed* is YHWH's covenant-faithfulness, his sworn loyalty to his people.
- *sod* (סוֹד, v. 14) — usually translated "friendship" or "secret counsel"; the intimate-fellowship word. *Sod YHWH* is the LORD's confidential council, shared with those who fear him.

Other key terms:

- *bagad* (בָּגַד, v. 3) — "deal treacherously," the verb of covenant betrayal
- *anavim* (עֲנָוִים, v. 9) — "the humble," a key term in poverty-piety theology
- *yacheed ve-ani* (יָחִיד וְעָנִי, v. 16) — "lonely and afflicted"

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The acrostic form imposes a sequence — verse by verse, alphabet-driven — but within that sequence the psalm has discernible movements.

- **vv. 1–7**: Address and petition — trust (vv. 1–3), request for instruction (vv. 4–5), appeal for mercy and pardon (vv. 6–7)
- **vv. 8–14**: Wisdom-instructional reflection — God's character toward sinners (vv. 8–10), petition for pardon (v. 11), generalizing reflection (vv. 12–14)
- **vv. 15–21**: Lament and petition return — eyes toward the LORD (v. 15), distress (vv. 16–18), enemies (v. 19), refuge (vv. 20–21)
- **v. 22**: Corporate petition for Israel

The shifting registers — petitionary lament, then wisdom reflection in the third person, then back to lament — make Psalm 25 difficult to read as a single emotional arc. Goldingay sees the wisdom-pedagogical sections (vv. 8–10, 12–14) as embedded teaching that *frames* the lament — the speaker is praying his lament *through* the wisdom he has learned. Westermann reads the structure as classic lament (address-complaint-petition-confession-vow), with the wisdom material as elaborated confession of trust.

The repetition of *hesed* three times (vv. 6, 7, 10) is theologically controlled. V. 6 asks God to *remember* his *hesed* "for it is from of old" (*me-olam*). V. 7 asks God to remember *me* "according to your *hesed*." V. 10 declares that all the LORD's paths *are* *hesed* and faithfulness toward those who keep his covenant. The triplet moves from God's eternal *hesed* (v. 6) to the speaker's claim on it (v. 7) to *hesed* as the very texture of God's ways (v. 10). This is wisdom-pedagogy through repetition: the psalm teaches the meaning of *hesed* by exposing it in three positions.

The parallelism (Berlin, Kugel, Alter) is *seconding* throughout. V. 4: "Make me to know your *ways*, O LORD / teach me your *paths*" — the second line specifying with a more concrete word (*archot*, "trodden tracks") what the first announced abstractly (*derakecha*, "your ways"). V. 14: "the *sod* of the LORD is for those who fear him / and his covenant he makes known to them" — the intimate friendship and the formal covenant in apposition; the *sod* is what *hesed* feels like from the inside.

The form-critical hybrid (lament + wisdom) is rare enough in the Psalter to be noteworthy. Pss 1, 19, 37, 73, 119 are the closest wisdom-Psalter relatives. Pss 32, 51 are penitential laments with wisdom edges. Psalm 25 sits at a specific intersection — the form allows the speaker both to plead and to teach himself, both to ask and to instruct.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Book I placement

Psalm 25 stands in Book I of the Psalter, after the entrance liturgy of Psalm 24 and before Psalm 26's protestation of innocence. The placement after Ps 24's "clean hands and pure heart" is suggestive: Ps 25 is, in part, the prayer of the worshipper who knows he does *not* have clean hands ("remember not the sins of my youth"). The two psalms make a self-aware pair: the entrance requires purity (24); the petitioner asks pardon for the lack of it (25).

### Jewish Trajectory

Psalm 25 is preserved in Dead Sea Scroll fragments. The acrostic structure is preserved in the manuscripts, with the same irregularities as the MT — confirming that the irregularities are old.

Rabbinic reception in *Midrash Tehillim* connects Ps 25 with Israel's penitential prayer tradition, especially its themes of pardon and being taught God's ways. Rashi reads the psalm at the literal-Davidic level — David in distress, asking forgiveness — with the corporate-Israel reading triggered especially by v. 22. Ibn Ezra preserves the literal frame and notes the acrostic structure carefully. Radak holds the literal-historical and corporate-Israel readings together. Nachmanides develops the *hesed*/*emet* pairing in v. 10 as foundational to Israel's covenantal anthropology.

Modern Jewish scholarship — Buber on the I-Thou structure of the prayer's address, Heschel on the prophetic concern with humility (v. 9's *anavim*), Levenson on the covenant-pedagogical horizon, Sarna on the acrostic-wisdom hybrid, Avivah Zornberg on the psychological depth of the speaker's self-examination — has continued to mine the psalm.

In synagogue practice, Psalm 25 is recited in some traditions as part of penitential liturgy and the *tachanun* (supplication) prayer. Verses 4–5 appear in the morning blessings in some siddurim ("teach me your paths"). The corporate ending (v. 22) is used as a kavvanah (intention-setting) in some communal prayer.

### Christian Trajectory

Augustine's *Enarrationes* on Psalm 25 (Vulgate Ps 24) reads the psalm as the prayer of the church in pilgrimage — instructed by Christ, asking pardon, waiting for redemption. The wisdom-pedagogical elements are read as Christ's pedagogy of the soul. Augustine attends to the acrostic structure but does not make as much of it as some later commentators do.

Aquinas reads the psalm as instructing on the relation between divine grace and human petition. Luther's *Operationes* picks up especially v. 11's "for your name's sake, O LORD, pardon my guilt, for it is great" — a verse that became central to Luther's understanding of forgiveness as *propter nomen tuum*, "for your name's sake," not for the worthiness of the petitioner. Calvin (1557) reads the psalm as a model of the *posture* of the believer: trust, instruction, pardon, waiting.

Modern critical commentary — Gunkel's foundational identification of the acrostic-lament-wisdom mix, Mowinckel, Westermann, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay's careful structural reading, Patrick Miller, William Brown, Beth Tanner, Susan Gillingham — has continued to develop the psalm's theological and literary features. Goldingay especially defends a wisdom-pedagogical reading: the psalm is a *learned prayer*, and the speaker's distress is the occasion for the wisdom to deepen, not for it to be set aside.

In Christian liturgical use, Psalm 25 is included in the lectionary for Advent and Lent (its themes of waiting and pardon are appropriate to both seasons). The Catholic Liturgy of the Hours assigns it to Tuesday of week one. Anglican and Lutheran daily offices use it regularly.

### NT Use

Psalm 25 is not directly quoted in the New Testament. Its echoes are diffuse — the *hesed*/*chesed* pairing with truth (v. 10) resonates with John 1:17 ("grace and truth came through Jesus Christ"); the petition for pardon for the sake of God's name resonates with 1 John 2:12 ("your sins are forgiven for his name's sake"); the pedagogical "teach me your paths" resonates with Jesus's "learn from me" (Matt 11:29). But these are echoes, not citations.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions deserve careful attention.

**Tension 1: Wisdom-pedagogical reading vs. lament-prayer reading.** Psalm 25's hybrid form has produced two interpretive traditions.

Three positions:

1. **Wisdom-pedagogical primary**. Goldingay's emphasis: the psalm is a *learned prayer*; the lament is the occasion, but the wisdom-instructional content is the substance; the speaker is teaching himself (and the reader) how to pray through trouble. The acrostic form supports this — completeness, A-to-Z, the whole curriculum. *Steelman*: takes the wisdom features seriously as features, not embellishments; honors the form's pedagogical signal; respects the speaker's competence.

2. **Lament-prayer primary**. Westermann's emphasis: the psalm is fundamentally an individual lament with embedded reflection; the wisdom material is the elaborated confession-of-trust standard in lament structure; the speaker is *suffering*, and his pain is the substance, with the wisdom as the form he prays it through. *Steelman*: takes the distress in vv. 16–21 with full seriousness; refuses to abstract the prayer into a teaching exercise; honors the lament tradition.

3. **Held together: prayer-as-pedagogy**. The psalm refuses the choice. The speaker is in distress *and* is teaching himself; the lament *is* the pedagogy. To pray through trouble is to learn what *hesed* means. This is closer to how the Jewish tradition has read the psalm — Torah includes prayer, prayer includes Torah. *Steelman*: respects both literary signals; resists the modern tendency to separate piety from pedagogy.

**Where this leaves us**: Position 3, almost certainly. The psalm's design — hybrid form, acrostic completeness, embedded wisdom — *enacts* the position that prayer is itself a learning. The speaker does not pause his prayer to teach a lesson; he prays the lesson.

**Tension 2: *Hesed* as covenantal mercy vs. unilateral grace.** The triple repetition of *hesed* (vv. 6, 7, 10), and the parallel *rachamim* (mercy, compassion) in v. 6, raise a question with Catholic-Protestant resonance.

Three positions:

1. **Covenantal-conditional reading**. *Hesed* is YHWH's covenant loyalty *to those who keep his covenant* (the qualification is explicit in v. 10: "all the paths of the LORD are *hesed* and faithfulness *for those who keep his covenant and his testimonies*"). The mercy is real but covenanted; the speaker can claim it because he is *in* covenant. *Steelman*: takes the v. 10 qualification seriously; respects the covenantal anthropology of the psalm; refuses cheap grace.

2. **Unilateral-grace reading**. The petition in v. 7 ("remember not the sins of my youth") and v. 11 ("pardon my guilt, for it is great") presupposes that the speaker's claim on *hesed* is *not* his covenant-keeping (his sins disqualify him); the *hesed* is what *gives* him standing in the covenant; grace precedes and constitutes covenant. Lutheran-Reformed reading. *Steelman*: takes v. 11 with full force; refuses to make the psalmist's pardon depend on his own merit; protects the gratuity of mercy.

3. **Both: covenantal mercy is unilateral grace inside the covenant**. The psalm holds these together. *Hesed* is YHWH's covenanted commitment to be merciful; the covenant is itself an act of grace; the qualification "for those who keep his covenant" is not a meritorious condition but a description of those who have been brought into the relationship. *Steelman*: respects both the covenantal frame (v. 10) and the unconditional petition (vv. 7, 11); avoids forced choice between Catholic and Protestant frames.

**Where this leaves us**: Position 3. The psalm itself does not draw the Catholic-Protestant distinction; it presupposes that mercy is both covenanted and unmerited. Reading later debates back into the text usually distorts it.

**Closing question**: When you ask God to teach you his ways, are you asking to be made smarter — or to be made humble enough that smartness no longer matters?

## The Thread

- **Psalm 24:3–6**: the entrance liturgy's "clean hands and pure heart" — the qualification Ps 25's speaker knows he lacks.
- **Psalm 51**: David's penitential prayer — the deeper development of the "remember not my transgressions" petition of Ps 25:7.
- **Lamentations 3**: the triple-acrostic counterpart to Ps 25's stylized acrostic — both prayers of suffering structured by the alphabet.
- **Proverbs 3:5–6**: "Trust in the LORD with all your heart… in all your ways acknowledge him, and he will make straight your paths" — the proverbial counterpart to Ps 25's pedagogical petition.
