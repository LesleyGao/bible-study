# Exodus 23: Do Not Follow a Crowd to Do Evil

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Exodus 23 closes the Book of the Covenant (Ex 20:22–23:33) and rounds its legal vision with judicial ethics, agricultural rhythms, the festival calendar, and the conquest promise. The chapter's world is still the Ramesside Late Bronze Age as preserved in early Israelite memory — a world of village-level adjudication at the gate, seasonal agriculture driven by the Levant's rainfall-dependent cycle, and the pressure of Canaanite and residual Egyptian cultic presence. Kenneth Kitchen's *On the Reliability of the Old Testament* argues the festival calendar of vv. 14–17, with its three pilgrim feasts keyed to the agricultural year, belongs to a pre-monarchic, village-centered phase of Israelite life before Temple centralization.

Judicial ethics in the ANE were conducted at the city gate — the scenes dramatized in Ruth 4, in the Code of Hammurabi's epilogue, and in the Mari letters. The prohibition against bribery (v. 8) echoes Hammurabi's prologue boast and the Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemope* (ch. 20), a roughly twelfth-century wisdom text whose parallels with Proverbs 22 have been studied since Adolf Erman's 1924 edition. The command to return an enemy's straying ox (vv. 4–5) has a near-parallel in Hittite Law §§71–75, where returning stray livestock is required but limited to fellow citizens; Israel's law extends the duty to enemies, a move Moshe Weinfeld in *Social Justice in Ancient Israel and in the Ancient Near East* identifies as a distinctive Israelite sharpening of a shared ANE concept.

The sabbath-year rule (vv. 10–11) and sabbath-day rule (v. 12) presume a mixed agricultural economy — grain, vines, olives — and an extended household that includes ox, donkey, male and female servants, and the resident alien. Carol Meyers' archaeological work on Iron Age households (*Households and Holiness*) documents exactly this configuration in the excavated four-room houses of Ai, Khirbet Raddana, and 'Izbet Sartah. The *šĕmiṭṭâ* year is unique in the ANE; no parallel institution is attested in Mesopotamian or Egyptian law, though Mesopotamian kings periodically proclaimed *mīšārum* (debt-release) decrees (studied by J.J. Finkelstein and Raymond Westbrook). The sabbath year binds the land itself into the covenantal rhythm and extends rest to the poor, wild beasts, and the soil.

The three pilgrim feasts (vv. 14–17) — Unleavened Bread (*Maṣṣôt*, later folded with Passover), Harvest/Firstfruits (later Shavuot/Pentecost), Ingathering (later Sukkot/Tabernacles) — track the Canaanite agrarian cycle: barley harvest in spring, wheat harvest in late spring, fruit/olive/grape harvest in autumn. Weinfeld and Jeffrey Tigay document the gradual historicization of these agricultural feasts into commemorations of exodus, Sinai, and wilderness — a theological overlay on an older agricultural base. The barnyard-festival archaeology of the southern Levant, especially at Shiloh (Finkelstein's excavations), suggests that the three-feast cycle was being observed at regional sanctuaries well before the Jerusalem Temple.

Verse 19's "you shall not boil a young goat in its mother's milk" has provoked extraordinary interpretive literature. The line appears three times in the Torah (Ex 23:19, Ex 34:26, Deut 14:21) and became the scriptural warrant for the entire rabbinic system of *bāśār bĕḥālāv* — separation of meat and dairy. The Ras Shamra Ugaritic tablet KTU 1.23 (sometimes cited as UT 52) was once read by Cassuto, Cyrus Gordon, and others as describing a Canaanite rite of boiling a kid in milk, making the biblical prohibition a repudiation of Canaanite fertility cult. This reading has been challenged — Ratner and Zuckerman's 1986 article in *HUCA* argued the Ugaritic text says no such thing (the word read as "milk" may mean "fat") — leaving the origin of the prohibition genuinely disputed. Maimonides in *Guide of the Perplexed* 3.48 attributed it to Canaanite cultic practice as a matter of probable reason, even without firm textual evidence.

The Angel of verses 20–23 — *malʾāk* — is one of the OT's strangest figures. "My name is in him" (v. 21) means the divine Name *YHWH* is somehow predicated of this mediating being; the Angel has the authority to forgive or not forgive transgression. Parallel figures appear in Genesis 16 (the *malʾāk* who speaks to Hagar and is addressed as YHWH), Genesis 22 (the angel at the Aqedah), Exodus 3 (the angel in the burning bush), and Joshua 5 (the commander of the army of YHWH). Comparative evidence: Mesopotamian *šukkallu* figures (divine viziers), Ugaritic Kothar-wa-Ḥasis, Egyptian messenger-deities. But none of the ANE parallels quite match the intimacy of "my name is in him."

Archaeological touchpoints: the Lachish ostraca and Samaria ostraca preserve Iron Age judicial formulas that resonate with vv. 1–8. The Gezer Calendar (tenth-century BCE) gives the agricultural cycle presupposed by vv. 10–17. The Khirbet el-Qom and Kuntillet 'Ajrud inscriptions document the pluralistic religious environment (YHWH alongside Asherah) that the conquest commands of vv. 23–33 sought to suppress.

Key Hebrew terms:

- *šĕmiṭṭâ* (שְׁמִטָּה) — release, fallow year; full institution developed in Lev 25 and Deut 15
- *maṣṣôt* (מַצּוֹת) — unleavened bread (the feast)
- *qāṣîr* (קָצִיר) — harvest (the feast of Harvest/Firstfruits)
- *ʾāsîp* (אָסִיף) — ingathering (the autumn harvest feast)
- *malʾāk* (מַלְאָךְ) — messenger, angel
- *ṣirʿâ* (צִרְעָה) — hornet or (metaphorically) "terror"; debated (v. 28)

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is stitched together from at least four distinct blocks: judicial ethics (vv. 1–9), agricultural/sabbatical rhythms (vv. 10–13), festival calendar (vv. 14–19), and the conquest promise with the Angel (vv. 20–33). Martin Noth and Frank Crüsemann argue the material was originally independent; the Covenant Code's editor has arranged it as a climactic summary, moving from human-to-human justice through temporal-sacred rhythms to the promise of land.

The judicial block (vv. 1–9) displays a remarkable two-sided balance that has drawn comment from Rashi through Brueggemann. Verse 3 forbids partiality to the poor in a lawsuit; verse 6 forbids perverting the justice due to the poor. The text refuses the easy sentimentalism of either siding with the poor as a class or siding against them as a class. Jonathan Sacks observed that this is among the clearest ancient statements of proceduralism — justice consists in the case, not in the category. Robert Alter's translation notes call vv. 2–3 "a cluster of clauses so lean they almost read as proverb": "You shall not fall in with the many to do evil" (*lō' tihyeh ʾaḥărê rabbîm lĕrāʿōt*) stands as one of the Hebrew Bible's most economical ethical statements.

The return-of-the-enemy's-ox law (vv. 4–5) is structurally placed between negative prohibitions (vv. 1–3 and vv. 6–9). Its positive, proactive requirement ("you shall bring it back," "you shall rescue it with him") interrupts the prohibitive rhythm. Nahum Sarna reads the placement as deliberate — the lawgiver embeds a concrete command of enemy-love inside a block of judicial integrity, as if to say that justice requires not only refusing partiality but actively overcoming enmity.

The festival calendar of vv. 14–17 forms a small ABA structure: the thrice-yearly pilgrim obligation (v. 14) is filled out by three named feasts (vv. 15–16), then summarized again (v. 17). This is the earliest attestation of the three-feast structure in the Torah; Deuteronomy 16 and Leviticus 23 develop and modify it.

The Angel passage (vv. 20–33) functions as the peroration of the entire Book of the Covenant — a rhetorical close that converts the legal code into a travelogue-promise. The grammar shifts from second-person plural ("you shall") to first-person divine ("I will send," "I will drive out," "I will set your border"). The cadence is elevated and almost credal. Brevard Childs (*Exodus*, OTL) read it as the editorial seal on the code; Dozeman reads it as pre-priestly framing.

The phrase "little by little" (*mĕʿaṭ mĕʿaṭ*) in v. 30 is noted by Ibn Ezra as a characteristic of divine pedagogy — the same phrase describes God's gradual work in the prophets, the rabbinic development of halakhah, and (in Christian reading) sanctification.

Textual variants: the LXX at v. 19 differs slightly in clause order. The Samaritan Pentateuch adds harmonizations from Deuteronomy 12 to vv. 24–25. The Peshitta renders *ṣirʿâ* (v. 28) differently (more like "wasp" than "hornet"). 4QExodc preserves the judicial block with minor orthographic variants.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Exodus 23 closes the legal core of the Sinai revelation. With its final verses, the Covenant Code hands off to the covenant-ratification scene of Ex 24. Canonically, the chapter projects forward into Deuteronomy (the reuse of the festival calendar in Deut 16, the sabbath year in Deut 15, the Angel's role in Deut 7 and Josh 5) and into the prophets (the ox-return enemy-love principle surfaces at Prov 25:21; the bribery prohibition at Isa 1:23, 5:23, and throughout Amos). The chapter's judicial ethic becomes the spine of prophetic social critique for five centuries.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory**:
- Second Temple: Philo's *Special Laws* 4.60–78 expounds vv. 1–8 as a treatise on judicial integrity. Josephus (*Antiquities* 4.212, 4.219) harmonizes the judicial block with Greco-Roman forensic ethics. Jubilees 49–50 expands the festival block and binds the sabbath year to the cosmic jubilee framework.
- Rabbinic: Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael on Mishpatim treats each verse; the "do not fall in with the many" (v. 2) becomes the scriptural basis for the majority-rule principle in Sanhedrin 2a and Bava Metzia 59b (Aharei rabbim lehatot, "incline after the many"), but with a crucial qualification: the principle applies to true judgment, not the mob. The sabbath-year laws are developed in Mishnah Shevi'it and the Yerushalmi tractate of the same name — Israel's observance of *šĕmiṭṭâ* in Mandate Palestine and modern Israel has required extensive responsa literature (the *heter mekhira* of Rav Kook, permitting sale of land to non-Jews during the seventh year). The three-feast structure is elaborated in Mishnah Hagigah. The *bāśār bĕḥālāv* prohibition becomes the object of Mishnah Hullin 8 and Maimonides' *Hilchot Ma'akhalot Asurot* 9 — the full separation of meat and dairy utensils and mealtimes. On the Angel, the Bavli's Sanhedrin 38b famously identifies the figure with Metatron, "whose name is like the name of his Master," a reading the later Hekhalot mystical literature developed.
- Medieval: Rashi on v. 2 develops the majority-rule reading carefully. Ibn Ezra on v. 19 (kid in milk) offers a philological caution — he refuses Maimonides' reconstruction of a Canaanite rite without evidence. Ramban on vv. 20–23 treats the Angel as a created intermediary, not a second deity, and draws the line sharply against later Christian Logos-Christology — this is one of the medieval Jewish-Christian disputation topics. Maimonides in *Guide* 3.48 gives the classic rationalist reading of the kid-in-milk rule as opposition to Canaanite practice.
- Modern: Sarna's JPS Exodus is the standard. Jonathan Sacks in *Covenant and Conversation: Exodus* treated v. 2 ("do not follow a crowd to do evil") as the Torah's foundational resistance to totalitarianism. Moshe Weinfeld showed how v. 8 (the bribery prohibition) became a touchstone of ANE royal virtue rhetoric that Israel democratized.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory**:
- Patristic: Origen's *Homilies on Exodus* reads vv. 4–5 (return of the enemy's ox) as a type of Christ's command to love enemies. Augustine in *De Doctrina Christiana* cites the bribery prohibition (v. 8) as foundational to Christian judicial ethics. Chrysostom repeatedly preached on v. 8 against the corruption of magistrates. Gregory of Nyssa in *Life of Moses* 2.151–170 allegorizes the three pilgrim feasts as stages of the soul's ascent.
- Medieval: Aquinas *ST* I-II q. 99–105 places the judicial block (vv. 1–9) within the "judicial precepts" of the Old Law and concludes that its moral substance (no bribery, no false witness, impartiality) is binding on Christians as instances of the natural law, while the specifically Israelite form is not. Bonaventure and later medieval exegetes read the festival calendar typologically — Passover as Christ's death, Firstfruits as resurrection, Ingathering as eschatological harvest.
- Reformation: Calvin's *Commentary on the Last Four Books* treats the judicial ethics of vv. 1–9 as a perpetual model for Christian magistracy; his *Institutes* 4.20 on civil government draws heavily here. Luther reads the Angel (vv. 20–23) Christologically — the pre-incarnate Son, whose name is in the Father — a reading he had already applied to Gen 16, 22, and Ex 3. The Reformed tradition after Calvin developed what is sometimes called the "general equity" principle: the judicial specifics are not binding, but their equity (what we today would call the underlying moral principle) is.
- Critical: Wellhausen assigned the chapter to the E source, with P additions. Martin Noth's *Exodus* commentary and Brevard Childs's *Exodus* (OTL) both treat the chapter as composite. William Propp's Anchor Bible *Exodus* (volume 2, 2006) provides the definitive critical treatment. Dozeman's *Exodus* (ECC) sees vv. 20–33 as a redactional peroration sealing the pre-priestly Covenant Code into the Sinai narrative.
- Contemporary: Walter Brueggemann reads the chapter as the OT's clearest specification of what the *imago Dei* requires in social practice. Terence Fretheim emphasizes the divine pathos behind the legal structure (compare the "I will hear" of Ex 22:23, 27). Christopher J.H. Wright's *Old Testament Ethics* uses vv. 1–9 as his model case for Christian appropriation of OT judicial material. N.T. Wright draws the Angel/Logos connection into NT theology (John 1:1–18, Col 1:15–20). L. Michael Morales's *Who Shall Ascend the Mountain of the Lord?* — though focused on Leviticus — treats the festival calendar as central to the OT's liturgical theology.

**Where they converge and diverge**: Jewish interpretation has maintained the binding force of the specifics — Israel still observes the three pilgrim feasts (though now at home or synagogue, not the Temple), the sabbath year is kept in the land of Israel, the kid-in-milk rule governs kosher kitchens worldwide. Christian interpretation has distinguished moral substance from ceremonial form, keeping the judicial ethics (no bribery, no partiality, enemy-love) and reading the feasts typologically in Christ. The Angel is the single figure where the traditions diverge most sharply: Jewish tradition reads him as a created agent (Maimonides) or, in mystical streams, as Metatron; Christian tradition from Justin Martyr forward reads him as the pre-incarnate Logos. The "my name is in him" clause (v. 21) is the textual anchor of a two-thousand-year Christological exegetical tradition that rabbinic literature largely rejected. Both traditions converge on vv. 1–9 as the foundation of scripture-based judicial integrity — the prohibition on bribery and on mob-justice is common ground.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Do the conquest commands of vv. 23–33 bind any contemporary ethic, or are they a historical artifact that modern readers must set aside?**

- Catholic: *Dei Verbum* 11–12 and the *Catechism* (CCC 101–119) distinguish the literal sense from the plenary sense; the conquest commands are read as historically contingent, fulfilled in Christ's victory over sin, and not prescriptive for the church. Joseph Ratzinger's *Jesus of Nazareth* treats the conquest as "spiritualized" in the NT.
- Reformed (Calvin): Calvin's *Commentary on Joshua* accepts the historicity and divine command of the conquest as a unique event of God's judicial action on the Canaanites' accumulated iniquity (citing Gen 15:16), but limits its prescriptive force to that event. Later Reformed theology (Herman Bavinck, Meredith Kline in *Kingdom Prologue*) develops the "intrusion" concept — the conquest represents an eschatological judgment "intruding" into ordinary history, which therefore cannot be generalized.
- Lutheran: Luther distinguished sharply between the "alien work" of God (judgment) and the "proper work" (mercy), reading the conquest as the alien work, a temporary and unrepeatable instrument.
- Orthodox: The conquest is read within a typological framework — the land of promise as heaven, the Canaanites as the passions of the flesh (Origen, whose reading entered Orthodox tradition through Cappadocian appropriation).
- Jewish (Maimonides): *Hilchot Melakhim* 5–6 treats the conquest commands as binding on the Israelite polity at the time of Joshua and on no one since. The laws of the seven Canaanite nations presume a historical situation that, as Maimonides explicitly notes, "has long since ceased to be."
- Critical: The conquest material is post-exilic theological reconstruction (Wellhausen, Noth, John Van Seters), not a report of events. The modern archaeological consensus (Finkelstein, Dever, Faust) is that Israel largely emerged from within Canaan rather than conquering it; the conquest narrative is founding myth, not history.

Steelmanning the least intuitive — Kline's intrusion reading: the OT conquest is neither prescriptive for later Israel nor a moral embarrassment to be explained away. It is the final judgment breaking into history ahead of time, on a people whose iniquity was "complete" (Gen 15:16), executed through a specific agent (Israel under Joshua). The ethical horror is not denied but relocated — it is the horror of final judgment itself, which Scripture everywhere affirms. The reading refuses both liberal dismissal and fundamentalist application.

**Where this leaves us:** No major tradition treats the conquest commands as prescriptive for any modern polity. The ethical weight of vv. 23–33 is directed, across traditions, into the rejection of idolatry (vv. 24, 32–33) rather than literal application.

**Tension 2: Is the sabbath year of vv. 10–11 a lost institution, a poetic ideal, or a continuing ethical demand?**

- Catholic: The sabbath-year principle is incorporated into Catholic social teaching (Leo XIII, John Paul II's *Centesimus Annus*) as the basis for periodic debt relief and land redistribution. Jubilee 2000 — the international debt-cancellation campaign led by Catholic and Protestant churches — drew explicitly on Ex 23, Lev 25, and Deut 15.
- Reformed: Abraham Kuyper's political theology treated the sabbath year as a permanent structural principle — an anti-accumulation mechanism woven into God's design for creation. Contemporary Reformed economists (Bob Goudzwaard, *Capitalism and Progress*) argue the sabbath-year framework should reshape capitalism from within.
- Lutheran: Luther's *To the Christian Nobility* (1520) invoked the jubilee principle against usury and accumulation; later Lutheranism has been more conservative on applying OT economic institutions.
- Wesleyan: John Wesley's sermons on money and stewardship draw on the sabbath-year logic implicitly; contemporary Wesleyan social ethics (Michael Cartwright, Ron Sider) have explicitly revived it.
- Jewish (Maimonides): *Hilchot Shemitta veYovel* codes the sabbath year as binding in the land of Israel; modern Israeli agricultural practice operates within this framework, with the *heter mekhira* of Rav Kook (temporary sale of land to non-Jews) permitting modern agriculture to function. The institution is literally observed, not metaphorically applied.
- Critical: The sabbath year is a utopian-priestly ideal of uncertain historical implementation (Wellhausen, Noth); extant evidence of ancient observance is thin (the Hellenistic-era remission attested in 1 Macc 6:49, 6:53 and by Josephus).

Steelmanning the least intuitive — the Jubilee 2000 reading: Scripture's sabbatical principle, though not literally applicable in a modern global economy, makes a structural demand on any economy that reads itself as standing under the God of Exodus. Periodic debt release is not charity; it is built into the theological architecture of creation. Debates about moral hazard, student-debt cancellation, and sovereign-debt restructuring suggest the theological question is not dead. Scripture offers a structural critique of debt accumulation that secular economics has no equivalent for.

**Where this leaves us:** Every tradition preserves some version of the sabbath-year principle but applies it differently: Jewish tradition literally in Israel, Catholic tradition through jubilee frameworks, Reformed tradition as structural-economic critique, critical scholarship as utopian-priestly ideology.

**An unresolved question**: The Angel of vv. 20–23 is either a created mediator in whom God's name dwells or is the pre-incarnate Son. Jewish and Christian traditions diverge fundamentally here, and the exegetical evidence of the text ("my name is in him," "he will not pardon your transgression") genuinely underdetermines the choice. The further question — how the divine identity relates to mediating figures in OT theophany (the *malʾāk YHWH*, the Angel of the LORD, the glory, the Name) — remains among the deepest unresolved questions in the theology of both Judaism and Christianity.

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references:

- The "do not follow a crowd to do evil" of v. 2 is quoted or echoed at Jeremiah 26:11–15 (when the priests and prophets demand Jeremiah's death and the "many" prevail), at the Gethsemane and trial narratives in the Gospels (Mark 14:55–64, where false witnesses conspire), and at Acts 23:12–15 (forty conspirators). The textual line from Ex 23:2 runs through the entire biblical literature of unjust judgment — Jesus' own trial is the paradigm violation.
- The enemy-donkey rescue of v. 5 reappears at Proverbs 25:21 ("if your enemy is hungry, give him bread to eat"), which Paul quotes in Romans 12:20 to ground his command "do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good." Jesus' Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37), read alongside Ex 23:5, makes clear that the priest and Levite are not merely callous but are violating an explicit command of the Covenant Code.
- The Angel of vv. 20–23 returns with startling specificity at Malachi 3:1 ("the messenger of the covenant, in whom you delight"), is identified by Matthew 11:10 with John the Baptist but also with a more-than-human precursor, and is invoked in Acts 7:38 (Stephen's speech: "the angel who spoke to him on Mount Sinai"). The trajectory traces the OT Angel through prophetic expectation into the NT's Christological reading.
