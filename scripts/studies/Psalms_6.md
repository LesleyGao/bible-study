# Psalm 6: The First Tear in the Penitent's Eye

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Psalm 6 carries a dense superscription that places it inside Israel's worship infrastructure: *lamenatzeach binginot al-ha-sheminith mizmor le-david* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ בִּנְגִינוֹת עַל־הַשְּׁמִינִית מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד), "to the choirmaster, with stringed instruments, according to *the Sheminith*, a Psalm of David." The word *sheminith* (שְׁמִינִית) literally means "the eighth," and its referent has been debated since antiquity. Three readings remain in serious play: an eight-stringed lyre or harp; a low octave (the bass register, contrasted with *al-alamoth*, "for soprano voices," in Ps 46); or a designation for a specific liturgical melody. Mitchell Dahood (Anchor Bible) leans toward the octave-register reading; Hans-Joachim Kraus (*Biblischer Kommentar*) treats the term as functionally opaque. The honest scholarly verdict is that the cultic meaning is recoverable in outline only.

Form-critically, Psalm 6 is a paradigm case of what Hermann Gunkel (*Einleitung in die Psalmen*, 1933) called the *Klagelied des Einzelnen* — the individual lament. Gunkel's structural typology fits the psalm almost perfectly: invocation (v. 1), complaint (vv. 2-3, 6-7), petition (vv. 2, 4), motivation appealing to God's character or self-interest (v. 5), and the abrupt turn to confidence (vv. 8-10). Sigmund Mowinckel pushed beyond Gunkel by asking after the *Sitz im Leben*, the cultic setting; in *The Psalms in Israel's Worship* he argued that many individual laments arose from temple rituals for the sick, where the speaker was attacked by enemies whose curses had induced the illness. The convergence of bodily suffering ("my bones are troubled," v. 2) and hostile foes ("all my foes," v. 7) in Ps 6 fits this hypothesis tightly.

Within the broader ANE world, the psalm has clear cousins. The Mesopotamian *šuilla* prayers ("hand-lifting" prayers to Marduk, Ishtar, Shamash) follow the same arc: a sufferer pleads for healing, names the deity's anger as the cause, and asks for restoration. Egyptian penitential hymns from Deir el-Medina ("the workmen's village" near the Valley of the Kings) likewise confess illness as divine discipline. What distinguishes Psalm 6 is the absence of any plea to a personal god mediating with a higher one; the speaker addresses YHWH directly, and the relationship is unmediated.

Three Hebrew terms anchor the psalm. *Refa'eni YHWH* (רְפָאֵנִי יְהוָה, "heal me, O LORD," v. 2) draws from the *rapha* root that gives Israel the divine name *YHWH-Rofeh* (Exod 15:26, "I am the LORD your healer"). *Behalu atzamai* (נִבְהֲלוּ עֲצָמָי, "my bones are terrified/shaken," v. 2) uses the verb *bahal*, suggesting trembling panic rather than mere ache; the same verb returns in v. 10 ("my enemies shall be greatly troubled") in a deliberate symmetry. And *Sheol* (שְׁאוֹל, v. 5) names the shadowy underworld of pre-exilic Israelite cosmology — not hell, not the grave alone, but the realm of the silent dead where YHWH's praise is not sounded.

Textually, Psalm 6 is preserved in 11Q5 (the Great Psalms Scroll from Qumran) only fragmentarily, but the wording aligns closely with the Masoretic Text. The Septuagint (LXX) numbers it Psalm 6 as well — the LXX/MT divergence does not start until Pss 9-10, where LXX combines what MT separates. The Old Greek translation renders *al-ha-sheminith* as *hyper tēs ogdoēs* ("concerning the eighth"), preserving the same uncertainty in Greek dress.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Psalm 6 is a tightly built ten-verse poem in three movements. The opening (vv. 1-3) is dominated by negation and questioning — *al-be'apekha* ("not in your anger"), *al-bachamatekha* ("nor in your wrath"), *ad-matai* ("how long?"). The middle section (vv. 4-7) shifts into imperative pleading and confessional weeping. The close (vv. 8-10) breaks with everything that came before: the speaker turns from prayer to enemies and announces vindication.

Hebrew parallelism organizes nearly every verse. Robert Lowth's classical taxonomy (*De Sacra Poesi Hebraeorum*, 1753) — synonymous, antithetic, synthetic — illuminates the structure, even if Adele Berlin (*The Dynamics of Biblical Parallelism*) and James Kugel (*The Idea of Biblical Poetry*) have rightly complicated Lowth's categories by showing that the second line almost always *advances* rather than merely echoes the first ("A, and what's more, B"). Verse 1 pairs *be'apekha* / *bachamatekha* in synonymous parallelism; verse 2 escalates from "be gracious" to "heal me," intensifying the petition; verse 5 sharpens *mavet* (death) into *Sheol*, narrowing the field. Robert Alter (*The Art of Biblical Poetry*) calls this "the small drama of intensification" — the second line tightens what the first opened.

The structural backbone follows Claus Westermann's lament template (*Praise and Lament in the Psalms*): address, complaint (against God, against enemies, against self), petition, motivation, and the *Stimmungsumschwung* — the abrupt change of mood. Westermann argued that this turn is the structural heart of the lament; in Psalm 6 it falls between verse 7 and verse 8 with almost no transition. The flooded bed and weak eye of v. 7 give way to "Depart from me, all you workers of evil." Why this turn? Some scholars (Begrich, "Das priesterliche Heilsorakel") posit a missing oracle — a priestly assurance of forgiveness or healing spoken between the petition and the confidence — that the text leaves implicit. The poem is silent at exactly the place where, ritually, something happened.

The poem uses repetition strategically. *YHWH* sounds eight times; *bahal* (trouble/terror) frames the psalm by appearing in v. 2 (the speaker's bones), v. 3 (the speaker's soul), and v. 10 (the enemies' fate) — an inclusio of sorts in which the speaker's terror migrates onto the foes. The threefold "the LORD has heard" / "the LORD has heard" / "the LORD accepts" in vv. 8-9 is a triple-strike confidence statement, the prosodic counterweight to the threefold lament of vv. 1-3.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the Psalter's five-book division (Book I = Pss 1-41, Book II = 42-72, Book III = 73-89, Book IV = 90-106, Book V = 107-150), Psalm 6 belongs to the heavily Davidic Book I and stands among a cluster of early laments (3, 4, 5, 6, 7) that follow the wisdom-Torah preface of Psalms 1-2. Gerald Wilson (*The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter*) argued that the placement is not accidental: after the king is introduced as God's son in Psalm 2, the speaker becomes a sufferer in Psalms 3-7, a posture that the rest of the Psalter will embrace.

The Jewish interpretive trajectory begins in the Dead Sea Scrolls, where Psalm 6 is included in the standard psalter ordering, and then runs through *Midrash Tehillim* — the great rabbinic homiletical commentary on Psalms — which connects v. 1's "rebuke me not in your anger" to David's plea for mercy after the Bathsheba episode. The medieval commentators sharpen distinct angles: Rashi (11th c., Troyes) reads the psalm as David facing illness in old age, with "my bones" understood somatically; Ibn Ezra (12th c., Spain) offers the lexically careful reading and notes the Sheminith uncertainty without resolving it; David Kimhi (Radak, 13th c., Provence) treats the enemies as the Davidic-era political opponents but allows a typological reading that extends to Israel under exile. In the liturgy, Psalm 6 has not entered the major daily cycles like the *Shir Shel Yom* or *Hallel*, but it appears in the *Tachanun* — the supplicatory prayers said weekday mornings and afternoons after the Amidah — fitting its plea-for-mercy character.

The Christian trajectory is where Psalm 6's afterlife becomes outsize. By the sixth century, Cassiodorus (*Expositio Psalmorum*) had codified the seven *Psalmi Poenitentiales* — the Penitential Psalms (6, 32, 38, 51, 102, 130, 143) — and this ordering became the central devotional sequence of medieval Catholic piety. The seven were prayed during Lent, on the deathbed, in monastic offices, and at any moment of acute moral crisis. Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 6 reads the speaker's sickness as the soul's sickness under sin and the *octave* of the superscription as eschatological — the eighth day, the day of resurrection — turning a cultic obscurity into a theological gateway. Athanasius (*Letter to Marcellinus*) recommends the psalm to anyone "harassed by demons or under the lash of conscience." Aquinas's *Postilla super Psalmos* aligns each penitential psalm with one of the seven deadly sins; Psalm 6 corresponds to *superbia* (pride). Luther translated all seven Penitential Psalms in 1517 — one of his very first publications, predating the Ninety-Five Theses by months — and the slim volume *Die sieben Bußpsalmen* shaped the early Reformation devotional imagination. Calvin (*Commentary on the Psalms*, 1557) calls the whole Psalter "the anatomy of all parts of the soul" and reads Psalm 6 as the soul under God's chastening hand, with the recovery in vv. 8-10 grounded in the assurance of the Spirit rather than any priestly oracle.

Modern critical scholarship — Gunkel, Mowinckel, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger (*Die Psalmen*), Goldingay (*Psalms*, BCOTWP), Allen (WBC), Tate, deClaissé-Walford-Jacobson-Tanner — converges on the form-critical reading and treats the historical David as the implied rather than identified author. Brueggemann's typology (*The Message of the Psalms*) places Ps 6 squarely in *disorientation* but with the late-verse turn anticipating *new orientation*. Patrick Miller (*They Cried to the Lord*) and William P. Brown (*Seeing the Psalms*) emphasize the embodied vocabulary — bones, eye, bed, couch — as evidence that biblical prayer is somatic before it is doctrinal.

The New Testament picks up Psalm 6 most clearly at Matthew 7:23, where Jesus tells the workers of lawlessness *apochōreite ap' emou* ("depart from me"), echoing the LXX of v. 8 (*apostēte ap' emou*). The shift is decisive: in the psalm the sufferer dismisses his enemies; in the Sermon on the Mount, the eschatological judge dismisses his. Jesus borrows the lament-speaker's dismissal and gives it Last Day weight.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author's theological vision is that the relationship between God and the suffering person is not severed by suffering but is its very medium. The psalm refuses to evade either pole: God's wrath is real (vv. 1-2), and so is God's *chesed* (steadfast love, v. 4). The speaker holds both. What God is being asked to do is to *turn* (*shuvah*, v. 4) — the same verb the prophets use for repentance, here applied to God Himself. In the psalm's grammar, divine repentance precedes human deliverance.

The first theological tension sits at verse 5: "for in death there is no remembrance of you; in Sheol who will give you praise?" This is a cosmology without resurrection. Jon Levenson (*Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel*) and James Barr (*The Garden of Eden and the Hope of Immortality*) have shown that pre-exilic and most pre-Hellenistic Israelite thought lacked a developed afterlife in the Christian or Pharisaic-Jewish sense; *Sheol* is a place of weakened existence, neither heaven nor hell. The tension: how should Christian readers, who confess resurrection, hear this verse?

- The classical Catholic reading (Augustine, Aquinas, Cassiodorus) allegorizes: *Sheol* is the spiritual death of sin, not bodily death, and the verse becomes a plea to be rescued from the soul's silence.
- The Reformation Protestant reading (Calvin, Luther) accepts the literal cosmology of the verse but treats it as an Old Covenant horizon that the New Covenant transcends; the psalm is true in its own register, fulfilled in Christ's resurrection.
- The Eastern Orthodox tradition (following Athanasius and the *Festal Letters*) reads the verse Christologically: Christ's descent into Hades reverses precisely this silence, so that praise now sounds where it could not before.
- Modern Jewish thought (Levenson, Heschel, Sarna, Zornberg) honors the verse on its own terms — the dead do not praise — and reads it as a profound argument-with-God: heal me *while I am still alive*, because the relationship is bound to embodied life.
- Critical scholarship (Kraus, Goldingay, Hossfeld-Zenger) treats the verse as straightforward evidence of pre-resurrection cosmology and warns against retrojecting later eschatology.

The least-intuitive position to steelman is the modern Jewish one. It says: the Christian rush to overlay resurrection on this verse loses something the psalm wants you to feel — that this life, this body, this voice, is where the relationship with God happens, and that God ought to care because the relationship cannot continue across the silence of death. The verse is not theological deficiency. It is theological pressure.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 6 functions for Christian readers without resolving its cosmology. It is a prayer of someone who does not yet know what we claim to know. Praying it as Christians is praying with the historical Israel — not above it, not past it, but with it.

A second tension is briefer but real: the abrupt turn at v. 8 from tears to enemy-dismissal. Is this triumphalism? Erich Zenger (*A God of Vengeance?*) reads the imprecation register in the Psalter as the protest of the powerless against actual oppressors; the psalm is not blessing violence but voicing the cry of the violated. Augustine reads the enemies allegorically as one's own sins. Both readings are honest; the literal reading should not be erased by the allegorical, and the allegorical should not be discarded as evasion.

**Closing question:** When the speaker says "the LORD has heard" (v. 8), what changed — God's posture, the speaker's perception, or the relation between them — and does the psalm permit us to know which?

## The Thread

- Exodus 15:26 — "I am the LORD your healer" (*YHWH-Rofeh*); the *rapha* root that v. 2 invokes.
- Psalm 38 — the second penitential psalm, with the same coupling of bodily suffering and divine wrath.
- Matthew 7:23 — Jesus's eschatological dismissal that echoes Ps 6:8.
- Hebrews 4:16 — the throne of grace, where the Christian reader brings what the psalmist brings: weeping and the request to be heard.
