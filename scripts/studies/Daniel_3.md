# Daniel 3: The Furnace, the Statue, and the Fourth Figure

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens on the plain of Dura with a golden image "sixty cubits high and six cubits wide" — about ninety feet tall and nine feet across, a wildly elongated proportion that has invited comment since the patristic period. **John J. Collins** (Hermeneia, 1993) reads the dimensions as deliberately hyperbolic — a literary device of the court tale rather than an engineering description; **Stephen Miller** (NAC) and **Joyce Baldwin** (TOTC, 1978) note that an obelisk-style image with a proportionally taller pedestal can be reconciled with the dimensions, and that hollow construction overlaid with gold leaf rather than solid casting is consonant with Mesopotamian practice. "Plain of Dura" is plausibly identifiable: a *Tulul Dura* southeast of Babylon was investigated by Julius Oppert in the nineteenth century and showed the base of a substantial monument; the identification is suggestive rather than certain.

The chapter's most striking historical-textual feature is its use of administrative-decree style. The **six-fold list of officials** (satraps, prefects, governors, counselors, treasurers, justices, magistrates, "all the officials") is repeated four times across vv. 2, 3, and 27 — the longest such list in the Old Testament. The **six-fold list of musical instruments** (horn, pipe, lyre, trigon, harp, bagpipe, "every kind of music") is repeated four times across vv. 5, 7, 10, and 15. The repetition is exact, ritualized, and verging on parody. **Carol Newsom** (OTL, 2014) reads the cumulative effect as comic hyperbole that lampoons the imperial-bureaucratic register; the chapter mocks the ponderous machinery of imperial worship by repeating its inventories until the reader can no longer take them seriously. The instrument list itself is the most-cited piece of evidence in the dating debate. Three of the instruments are Greek loanwords transliterated into Aramaic: *qitros* (κίθαρις, lyre/zither), *pesanterin* (ψαλτήριον, psaltery), and *sumponyah* (συμφωνία, "bagpipe" or harmony-instrument). Bertholdt (1806), S.R. Driver (1891), **R.H. Charles** (1929), **James Montgomery** (ICC, 1927), Collins, and Newsom argue that the cluster of Greek loanwords places the chapter's redaction in the post-Alexandrian, plausibly Maccabean, period; conservatives (Miller, Baldwin, **Gleason Archer**) counter that Greek loanwords could have entered Aramaic via trade contact in the seventh and sixth centuries (the Lydian-Greek connection, the presence of Greek mercenaries in Egyptian and possibly Babylonian service) and that *sumponyah* in particular need not require a Hellenistic dating. The argument is real and unsettled.

The names themselves carry theological weight. The Babylonian-given names of Daniel 1 are deliberate: Hananiah ("YHWH is gracious") becomes **Shadrach**, plausibly a deformation of the Sumerian sun-god *Marduk* or Akkadian *Aku* (Collins notes the proposed etymologies are speculative). Mishael ("Who is what God is?") becomes **Meshach**, possibly involving the Babylonian *Mishaaku* or a deformation invoking *Marduk* again. Azariah ("YHWH has helped") becomes **Abednego** — *Abdi-Nabu* or *Abed-Nego* — "servant of Nebo (Nabu)," the Babylonian god of writing and wisdom. Collins's reading of the philological theology: the Babylonian names invoke specifically *Marduk* and *Nabu*, the chief gods of the empire, and the chapter's narrative is the working-out of the names' apparent claim — that these men belong to those gods. The narrative reverses the names' force: Hananiah-now-Shadrach, the supposed servant of Marduk, refuses to bow to Marduk's image; Azariah-now-Abednego, the supposed servant of Nabu, refuses to honor him.

The Babylonian Chronicle (BM 21946), the Nabonidus Chronicle, and the Cyrus Cylinder are the standard external corroborations of the Neo-Babylonian setting. There is no specific Babylonian record of a golden image on the plain of Dura, but Mesopotamian royal ideology routinely included monumental cult images, and Nebuchadnezzar's building inscriptions (the *Nebuchadnezzar Cylinder* and others) attest his obsession with monumental religious architecture. Burning as a punishment is attested in Babylonian texts (the *Hammurabi Code* §157 prescribes burning for incest; cuneiform sources occasionally record burning of rebels), but the *furnace* form of execution is more characteristic of Iranian (Persian) practice — an additional small data point on the chapter's mixed period-features.

Key terms: *bar elahin* (בַּר אֱלָהִין, "son of the gods," v. 25 — the chapter's most-disputed phrase, taken in patristic-Christian exegesis as a christophany, in Jewish exegesis as an angelophany); *attun nura yaqidta* (אַתּוּן נוּרָא יָקִדְתָּא, "burning fiery furnace," repeated as a fixed formula seven times in the chapter); *malak* (מַלְאַךְ, "angel," v. 28 — Nebuchadnezzar's own retrospective identification of the fourth figure as an angel sent by God); *shumponyah* (סוּמְפֹּנְיָה, the Greek-loanword bagpipe/harmony-instrument).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is a court-conflict tale of a recognizable type — **Susan Niditch** (*Underdogs and Tricksters*) and **W. Lee Humphreys** (JBL 1973) defined the diaspora court-tale form as one in which the foreign hero's success provokes envy from the native courtiers, who plot a death-snare; the king is manipulated into endorsing it; the hero is delivered by divine intervention; the king is humbled and confesses. Daniel 3 is the textbook instance and Daniel 6 (the lions' den) is the chiastic pair within the Aramaic block. Both stories share the structure: royal decree designed to entrap a Jewish minister, faithful refusal, divine deliverance, royal confession, promotion of the faithful.

Daniel does not appear in chapter 3 — a long-standing puzzle. The chapter is a distinct novella centered on the three companions; it does not share narrative continuity with chapter 2's elevation of Daniel "to the king's gate" or with chapter 4's first-person royal decree. Critical scholars (Collins, Newsom, **Maurice Casey**) read the chapter as an originally independent court tale incorporated into the larger collection; conservatives note that Daniel's absence is consistent with the king's gate placing him at the central court while his three companions held provincial-administrative postings (Daniel 2:49 records exactly this division of labor). Either reading sustains the literary unity of chapters 1–6; the question is one of compositional history.

The chapter's literary devices are unusually rich. The repetition of the official lists and the music lists is the most obvious — the four-fold repetition of the official list (vv. 2, 3, 27) and the four-fold repetition of the music list (vv. 5, 7, 10, 15) generates a ritualized pseudo-liturgy that the chapter's narrative then dismantles. The verbal echo between Nebuchadnezzar's command "fall down and worship" (vv. 5, 6, 10, 11, 15) and the reversed verb "fell down" of v. 23 (the three men *fall* into the furnace) and the angelic figure who is *not* described as falling but as walking in the midst of the fire is a crafted visual joke. The chapter's most luminous image — four men walking unbound in the fire while the king's mighty men are killed by the heat at the door — is deliberately constructed: the executioners die; the executed live.

The Aramaic block's chiasm — chapter 3 paired with chapter 6, both deliverance stories framing chapters 4–5 (the humbled kings) — is one of the most secure literary observations on the book (A. Lenglet, 1972; Collins; Newsom). Chapter 3 paired with chapter 6 reveals the structural intent: deliverance of the faithful through impossible threats *brackets* the imperial humiliation narratives, suggesting that the empires fail not despite but precisely through the faithfulness of the persecuted minority.

The textual variant question is unusually consequential here. The Greek versions of Daniel — the Old Greek and Theodotion — both insert between MT 3:23 and 3:24 the **Greek Additions to Daniel**: the **Prayer of Azariah** (a confession-of-sin prayer in the names of the three young men inside the furnace) and the **Song of the Three Young Men** (a hymn of praise corresponding loosely to Psalm 148). These additions are present in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles, removed by **Jerome** in the late fourth century under his *hebraica veritas* principle (translating only from the Hebrew/Aramaic), and rejected by the Reformers as apocryphal. The Catholic and Orthodox liturgical traditions retain the Song of the Three as a canticle (the *Benedicite*), one of the most-sung pieces in the Christian liturgy. Whether the additions were original to the Aramaic and lost from the Masoretic text or whether they are Greek-period elaborations is debated; **John J. Collins** treats them as Hellenistic-period Greek additions; some recent scholars (**Lawrence Wills**, **Daniel Smith-Christopher**) leave the question open.

Canonical placement: Daniel sits in the *Ketuvim* (Writings) in the Hebrew Bible, in the prophets in Christian Bibles. The Catholic Daniel is meaningfully longer than the Protestant Daniel — chapter 3 alone gains 67 verses (Prayer of Azariah and Song of the Three) in the Catholic numbering.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc, Daniel 3 is the first sustained narrative of *non-violent civic resistance to imperial cult* in the Hebrew Bible. Earlier prophetic critique of foreign idolatry (Isaiah 44, Jeremiah 10) is theological-rhetorical; Daniel 3 stages the conflict in a courtroom. The chapter is, for that reason, the foundational text of biblical resistance theology.

**Jewish interpretive trajectory.** Second-Temple use of Daniel 3 is direct and political. **1 Maccabees 2:59–60** has the dying Mattathias instruct his sons: "Hananiah, Azariah, Mishael — believing, they were saved out of the flame; Daniel, in his integrity, was rescued from the mouth of the lions." The Maccabean use of Daniel 3 as authorization for armed resistance is among the earliest pieces of Daniel reception we have; the chapter's faithful three become the prototype of Jewish martyrdom and resistance to imperial idolatry. **4 Maccabees** 13–18 returns to the example, in a more philosophical register. **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 10.10.5) retells the story. Rabbinic engagement is dense: the Bavli (Pesahim 118a-b) reads the deliverance as a paradigm for Israel's deliverance from its persecutors; the Targum of Daniel paraphrases the Aramaic. Medieval Jewish commentary — **Saadia Gaon**, **Rashi**, **Ibn Ezra**, **Abrabanel** — treats the fourth figure as an angel and the deliverance as a prefiguration of messianic vindication. Modern Jewish reading (Aaron Koller, Daniel Boyarin) has reread the chapter as resistance literature against Antiochus IV's enforced Hellenization, with the golden image as an analogue to Antiochus's "abomination of desolation" placed in the temple in 167 BC (1 Macc 1:54; cf. Daniel 11:31, 12:11).

**Christian interpretive trajectory.** **Hippolytus of Rome's *Commentary on Daniel*** (c. AD 200, the earliest extant Christian biblical commentary) reads the fourth figure as the pre-incarnate Christ, a christophany analogous to the angel of the Lord in Genesis 16, 18, and Joshua 5. **Jerome** (*Commentary on Daniel*, 407) preserves and amplifies this reading. **Theodoret** continues it. Patristic preaching and martyrology lean heavily on Daniel 3: the three martyrs of the furnace become the iconographic model for early Christian martyrdom (frescoes in the catacombs of Priscilla in Rome, third-century sarcophagi). The Song of the Three becomes the *Benedicite* canticle in the Western and Eastern liturgies. The Anabaptist tradition — through the Schleitheim Confession, the Anabaptist martyr-tradition (the *Martyrs' Mirror*), and most modernly **John Howard Yoder** in *The Politics of Jesus* — has made Daniel 3 a charter document for non-violent resistance and gathered-church separatism. **Luther's *Preface to Daniel*** (1530) reads it as a model for confessing-church witness against imperial-religious overreach, with predictable application to the Holy Roman Empire of his day. **Calvin's *Lectures on Daniel*** (1561) treats the chapter as a model of pious civic disobedience while distinguishing the magistrate's office from idolatrous overreach; the *Vindiciae Contra Tyrannos* (1579) and the Knoxian Reformed political tradition more broadly draw on Daniel 3 as authorization for resistance to tyrannical magistrates.

The Lutheran two-kingdoms doctrine, articulated in Luther's *Temporal Authority* (1523) and developed in the Lutheran Confessions, is more cautious. The two-kingdoms framework distinguishes spiritual and temporal authority and has historically been used to *limit* armed resistance; the Daniel 3 reading in Lutheran exegesis (Brenz, Melanchthon's commentaries) emphasizes the *spiritual* refusal — they would not bow — without endorsing armed insurrection.

Modern Jewish and Christian readers have pushed both directions. The Maccabean reception line authorizes resistance up to and including armed revolt; the Anabaptist line authorizes non-violent resistance and martyrdom; the Lutheran line cautions against confusing the kingdoms; the contemporary critical reading (Collins, Newsom, **Walter Brueggemann** on Daniel) reads the chapter as resistance literature with broad political-theological application.

Where the trajectories converge: the chapter authorizes refusal to participate in imperial worship even at cost of life. Where they diverge: on whether that refusal extends to armed resistance, on whether the deliverance is normative or exceptional, and on whether the fourth figure is Christ, an angel, or a literary symbol.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Civil disobedience and the limits of resistance.** The Anabaptist reading (Yoder, *Politics of Jesus*; the Schleitheim tradition) takes Daniel 3 straight: the three companions refuse to bow, accept the death threat, and are vindicated; the chapter authorizes non-violent civil disobedience and martyrdom but not armed revolt — the three do not raise hands against Nebuchadnezzar. The Maccabean reading (1 Macc 2:59–60, 4 Macc 13–18) takes Daniel 3 as charter for armed resistance: the citation of the three immediately precedes Mattathias's call to revolt. The Reformed resistance-theory tradition (Knox, *First Blast of the Trumpet*, 1558; **Vindiciae Contra Tyrannos**, 1579; Calvin in his late *Sermons on 2 Samuel*) reads the chapter as authorization for resistance to tyrannical magistrates by lower magistrates and, eventually, the people. The Lutheran two-kingdoms tradition (Luther's *Temporal Authority*, 1523; the Augsburg Confession article 16) reads the chapter as authorizing spiritual refusal while preserving the temporal magistrate's authority — armed resistance is not encouraged. The Catholic Counter-Reformation reading (Suárez, Bellarmine in the *De Romano Pontifice*) developed a doctrine of legitimate resistance to tyranny via the principles of natural law, with Daniel 3 as supporting evidence. Modern readings (Bonhoeffer's reading of resistance in the Confessing Church, Yoder, **Stanley Hauerwas**) sit on the spectrum.

**Steelman the Lutheran position.** The two-kingdoms doctrine is the least intuitive option to many modern readers — it can look like quietism in the face of imperial idolatry. The Lutheran case is that Daniel 3 *itself* models the discipline: the three companions refuse to worship, openly, accepting the consequence, *but they do not attempt to overthrow Nebuchadnezzar*. The narrative has them die rather than depose. The Lutheran reading is that the kingdom of God is not advanced by the means of the kingdom of the world; armed revolt against pagan rulers tends to install new versions of the same kingdom. The chapter's deliverance is *God's*, not the resisters'. The discipline of the three — refuse, accept, die or be delivered — is the discipline the Lutheran tradition recommends, and the chapter does not authorize more than that.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter has been used to authorize both gathered-church non-violent resistance and Maccabean-style armed revolt. The narrative itself is closer to the Anabaptist-Lutheran reading than to the Maccabean one — there is no insurrection in Daniel 3, only refusal — but the Maccabean reception is also part of the chapter's canonical legacy and not simply a misreading. Christians and Jews in different polities have made different choices, and the choice is not relieved by the chapter alone.

**Tension 2: The fourth figure — pre-incarnate Christ or angel?** The patristic-Christian reading (Hippolytus, Jerome, Theodoret, Augustine in places, Calvin reluctantly) identifies the "fourth like a son of the gods" as the pre-incarnate Christ, drawing on the broader patristic tradition of christophanies in OT theophanies (Genesis 18, 32; Joshua 5; Judges 13). The reading has deep ecclesial consequences: it asserts that Christ acts in the Old Testament narrative, that the church's Christology is anticipated in the Jewish scriptures, and that the "son of the gods" phrase carries a Christological surplus.

The Jewish reading, sustained from the Targum through Saadia, Rashi, Abrabanel, and modern Jewish exegetes, treats the fourth figure as an angel. The chapter's own retrospective gloss in v. 28 — "his angel" (*malakeh*) — supplies internal warrant: Nebuchadnezzar himself, on coming out of the experience, identifies the figure as an angel. The Aramaic *bar elahin* ("son of the gods") in pagan-king idiom would naturally mean a divine being or angel — the phrase does not in Aramaic carry the technical Christological meaning it does in later Greek and Latin theology. The Jewish reading therefore is not skeptical of the supernatural; it identifies the agent more precisely.

The critical reading (Collins, Newsom, Casey) reads the figure as a narrative symbol and treats the question of metaphysical identity as orthogonal to what the chapter is doing — authorizing the audience that God's presence is with the persecuted in the fire. **Steelman the angelic reading.** The Christological reading is hermeneutically beautiful and has sustained Christian devotional life for two millennia, but the angelic reading has the text's own commentary on its side (v. 28), is consistent with the broader Hebrew Bible's angelology (the angel of the Lord, the watcher figures of chapter 4, the angelic intermediaries of Daniel 7–12), and does not require importing Christological categories anachronistically. A reader can hold to the Christological reading as canonical-typological reading-back from the New Testament without claiming that the chapter, in its original sense, requires it.

**Where this leaves us:** the chapter's most luminous image — four men walking in the fire, the executioners dead at the door — is bigger than the question of the fourth figure's identity. Christian readers can hold the Christological identification typologically without using it to dismiss the angelic reading. Jewish readers can hold the angelic identification without thereby denying that Christian christophany-readings have generated significant theology. The question is not which reading wins but what each reading requires of the reader.

**One unresolved question.** If the discipline of the three is not "fight back" but "refuse and let God decide," and if their famous "but if not" (v. 18) — "even if our God does not deliver us, we will not bow" — is the heart of the chapter, what does that discipline ask of a reader who lives in an empire whose idolatries are softer, more diffuse, harder to identify, and whose music plays continuously?

## The Thread

The Joseph parallel is less direct here than in chapters 1–2, but the **Esther** parallel deserves notice: Mordecai's refusal to bow to Haman (Esther 3:2–4) is the structural pair of the three companions' refusal to bow to the image, and Esther's eventual deliverance through hidden providence is the complementary form of Daniel 3's open deliverance through visible miracle. Read together, the two chapters define the spectrum of biblical responses to imperial idolatry.

Forward to the New Testament, three lines run from Daniel 3. First, **Hebrews 11:34** — "quenched the power of fire" — is a direct Daniel 3 allusion, included in the catalog of faith-acts; the three companions are part of the New Testament's canon of exemplary faith. Second, **Revelation 13** — the beast that compels worship under threat of death, with the false prophet performing signs to legitimate the worship — is Daniel 3 redeployed as eschatological frame; the imagery of the imperial cult under Domitian (or whichever empire one identifies with the beast) is read through Daniel 3's earlier instance. Third, the fourth-figure-in-the-fire image enters New Testament Christology through John 11:35 and Hebrews 13:5 — the Christ who is *with* his people in suffering — and through the Pauline "Christ in you" (Col 1:27) which the patristic reading then re-applies retrospectively to Daniel 3.
