# Deuteronomy 3: The Last of the Rephaim

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Deuteronomy 3 continues the Transjordan conquest narrative with Israel's northern campaign against Og of Bashan, followed by the allotment of the newly-taken territory to Reuben, Gad, and half-Manasseh, and closes with Moses's pleading prayer at Pisgah. Geographically, the chapter is precisely located. Bashan is the volcanic plateau east of the Sea of Galilee (Chinnereth, v. 17), bounded on the north by Mount Hermon (vv. 8–9), on the east by the Hauran lava fields, and on the south by the Yarmuk drainage. The region of Argob (vv. 4, 13–14), mentioned only here in the Torah, corresponds roughly to the Leja volcanic plateau — a naturally fortified landscape of basalt fields that has defined the area from antiquity to the present. Edrei (modern Der'a on the Syria–Jordan border) and Salecah (probably modern Salkhad in the Jebel Druze) mark the eastern reach of Og's kingdom. The Transjordan settlement described in vv. 12–17 allocates land from the Arnon River (Wadi Mujib) in the south up through Gilead to Mount Hermon in the far north.

The Late Bronze political landscape of Bashan is attested in Egyptian topographical lists. The name Bashan itself appears in New Kingdom Egyptian texts; Ashtaroth (Karnaim), Bashan's southern administrative center, is named in the Amarna correspondence (EA 197, 256), and in Egyptian execration texts from the Middle Kingdom. Ugaritic texts (KTU 1.108) use "Ashtaroth of Edrei" as a divine epithet, linking the region to chthonic Rephaim traditions long before Israel's arrival. Michael Astour and later John Day (*Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan*, 2000) have shown that Bashan and Edrei were remembered across the ANE as seats of Rephaim — the dead, divinized ancestors of Ugaritic ritual. Og as "last of the remnant of the Rephaim" (v. 11) is therefore culturally resonant, not merely legendary.

Authorship and dating debates play out here as they do in chapter 2. W.M.L. de Wette's 1805 identification of Deuteronomy with Josiah's reform scroll positions the Transjordan settlement as a retrospective reconstruction from the later monarchy. Julius Wellhausen's synthesis developed this. Moshe Weinfeld (*Deuteronomy 1–11* AB, 1991) emphasized parallels to neo-Assyrian royal campaign inscriptions — the Annals of Tiglath-pileser III and Shalmaneser III use remarkably similar "I captured all his cities" formulae. Against this, Kenneth Kitchen (*On the Reliability of the Old Testament*) points to the detailed topographical knowledge of a region that never featured prominently in later Israelite politics (Bashan's Israelite presence was brief and intermittent) as suggesting authentic second-millennium memory. Peter Craigie (NICOT 1976) and Daniel Block (NIVAC 2012) pressed the case for Mosaic substance with later editorial updating. Jeffrey Tigay (JPS 1996) and Richard Nelson (OTL 2002) take a middle position. Dennis Olson (*Deuteronomy and the Death of Moses*, 1994) and J. G. McConville (Apollos 2002) bracket the historical question to focus on the chapter's rhetorical function.

Og's "bed of iron" in Rabbah of the Ammonites (v. 11) is the chapter's most famous historical puzzle. Nine cubits long and four wide — roughly 13.5 feet by 6 feet on the common cubit — the object is preserved as if on display. *ʿereś barzel* (עֶרֶשׂ בַּרְזֶל) literally means "bed/couch of iron." Kitchen, following earlier German scholarship, argued it was a basalt sarcophagus — iron-colored stone, not metallic iron — since monumental basalt coffins are archaeologically attested in the Transjordan. Others (Allan Millard, John Walton) propose a cultic dolmen or funerary installation visible at Rabbah as a tourist curiosity in the author's day. Critical scholarship (Weinfeld, Nelson) treats it as legendary elaboration. Jewish midrashic tradition developed Og into a mythological figure — in *Niddah* 24b and later sources, Og becomes a survivor of the Flood who clung to Noah's ark.

Key Hebrew terms: *Rĕpāʾîm* (רְפָאִים) — the legendary giants also mentioned in 2:11, 20. *Argōb* (אַרְגֹּב, v. 4) — the volcanic tableland, possibly related to a root meaning "clod" or "stony ground." *Bāšān* (בָּשָׁן) — the northern Transjordan plateau, etymologically connected to a Semitic root for "smooth" or "fruitful." *Ḥērem* (חֵרֶם, v. 6) again for the ban. *ʿAśtārōt* (עַשְׁתָּרֹת) and *ʾEdreʿî* (אֶדְרֶעִי) — Og's twin capitals, named in Ugaritic cultic texts. *ʿĀnāh* (עָנָה, Hithpael stem) or more precisely *waʾetḥannan* (וָאֶתְחַנַּן, v. 23) — "I pleaded" — the intense form of supplication that gives the parasha its Jewish liturgical name, *Va'etchanan*.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Deuteronomy 3 completes the Transjordan section and pivots to Moses's personal tragedy. Structurally the chapter has three panels: the defeat of Og (vv. 1–11); the settlement of Reuben, Gad, and half-Manasseh (vv. 12–20); and the dual address to Joshua and to YHWH (vv. 21–29). The literary movement from triumphal conquest to Moses's refused prayer is deliberately jarring — the chapter's rhetorical power depends on the contrast.

The Og narrative (vv. 1–11) follows the same template as the Sihon narrative in chapter 2: divine encouragement ("do not fear him"), battle, total victory, ḥērem, enumeration of cities, parenthetical ethnographic note. Duane Christensen (WBC 2001) treats these as a doubled victory diptych, arguing that chapter 2 and chapter 3 together form a single poetic-narrative unit celebrating the Transjordan as a firstfruits of conquest. The sixty fortified cities of Argob (v. 4) — "fortified with high walls, gates, and bars, besides very many unwalled villages" — echoes the fearful report of the spies in Numbers 13, now inverted: these are the cities that were taken. Patrick Miller (Interpretation 1990) observes that Deuteronomy 3 systematically answers the anxieties of the spy narrative. What terrified the fathers, the sons conquer.

The parenthetical ethnographies — the "Sidonians call Hermon Sirion" gloss (v. 9), the Jair / Havvoth-jair note (v. 14), the Og-bed note (v. 11) — function as the same kind of editorial markers seen in chapter 2. "As it is to this day" (v. 14) is the signature phrase critical scholars treat as evidence of later redaction; Ibn Ezra in the twelfth century already noticed that such formulae sit awkwardly in a text purportedly spoken by Moses just before his death. The "Ibn Ezra problem" — sometimes called the problem of "post-Mosaica" — is an ancient Jewish acknowledgment that Deuteronomy bears signs of editorial supplement.

Moses's pleading prayer (*va'etchanan*, vv. 23–29) belongs to a distinct genre — personal lament within covenant address. The prayer's rhetoric moves from doxology ("O Lord GOD, you have only begun to show your servant your greatness and your mighty hand...") to petition ("Please let me go over and see the good land") to YHWH's striking refusal: "Enough from you; do not speak to me of this matter again" (v. 26). The Hebrew *rab-lāk* (רַב־לָךְ) is intensely colloquial — "too much for you," closer to "enough!" than to a formal prohibition. Tigay's JPS commentary notes this as one of the tonally severest moments in the Torah. The prayer structure anticipates Moses's final blessing in chapter 33 and the Song of Moses in chapter 32; McConville (Apollos 2002) treats *va'etchanan* as the emotional heart of Moses's personal narrative within the book.

Deuteronomic phraseology is dense: "the LORD your God has given you this land to possess" (v. 18); "the LORD your God who fights for you" (v. 22); "rest" (*mĕnûḥâ*, v. 20) — all characteristic of the Deuteronomic school. Miller notes the repetition of *nātan* ("gave") with YHWH as subject: the land is always given, never simply taken.

Textual witnesses for Deuteronomy 3 include 4QDeutj and 4QDeutn from Qumran, the Samaritan Pentateuch (which harmonizes with Num 21), and the Septuagint. The LXX preserves the Og-bed detail but translates *barzel* as *sidērā* (iron); it does not soften the curious datum. The Samaritan Pentateuch expands the divine command slightly in v. 2. Nash Papyrus (second–first century BC), the oldest known biblical manuscript, preserves the Decalogue and Shema but not this chapter. None of the extant variants significantly alter the meaning.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

Second Temple Jewish writing found Og especially generative. Josephus (*Antiquities* 4.5.3) narrates the battle in the gladiatorial idiom of Greco-Roman readers — Og is a terrifying king whose defeat proves Israelite courage. Pseudo-Philo's *Biblical Antiquities* and later midrashic expansions pile on legendary material: Og survives the Flood by clinging to Noah's ark; Og hurls a mountain at Israel but is tangled in it; his bed is sometimes understood as a cradle. These are not sober historiography, but they testify to Og's role as a storied figure — a theological marker for primeval giant-power subdued by YHWH.

The rabbinic tradition centers on *va'etchanan*. The parasha Va'etchanan (Deut 3:23–7:11) is read publicly on the Shabbat after Tisha b'Av, the day of communal mourning for the destruction of the Temples. The pairing is deliberate: Moses's grief at being denied the land aligns with Israel's grief at losing the Temple and land in later catastrophes. *Devarim Rabbah* (6:11) and *Midrash Petirat Moshe* develop Moses's pleading into an extended dramatic dialogue in which Moses offers 515 prayers (the gematria of *va'etchanan*), each more creative than the last, to overturn the decree. Rashi's comment on v. 23 is tender: Moses could have appealed to his merits, but he asked as a suppliant seeking undeserved grace (*ḥinnâm*, cognate with *ḥannan*). Ibn Ezra is characteristically sober — Moses's request was denied because it conflicted with YHWH's plan for Joshua's succession. Nachmanides (Ramban) reads the refusal against the backdrop of the Meribah incident (Num 20): Moses's sin was serious; its consequences were serious.

Maimonides in *Hilkhot Melakhim* and in the *Guide* 3.24 treats Moses's unfulfilled desire as part of the prophetic virtue — Moses desired not power but presence. Abarbanel saw political symbolism: Moses was fit to lead wilderness generation but not the settled land, which required a different kind of leader. Modern Jewish thought, especially Heschel (*God in Search of Man*) and Sacks (*Covenant and Conversation* on Devarim), treats Moses's unfulfilled desire as a template for the spiritual life — the one who leads toward a goal is not always permitted to reach it. Nehama Leibowitz's *Studies in Devarim* offers a sustained meditation on *va'etchanan* as Moses's deepest prayer. Aviva Zornberg's *The Particulars of Rapture* (on Exodus) and her later work treats Moses's refused request as foundational to Jewish mysticism's understanding of incompletion.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

The patristic reception of Moses's refusal was remarkably unified: Moses represents the Law, and the Law cannot bring Israel into the land; only Joshua (Greek *Iēsous*, the same name as Jesus) can. Origen's *Homilies on Joshua* 1.3 makes this typology explicit: "Jesus led the people into the promised land." Augustine (*City of God* 17.2) and Jerome (*Letter* 53) adopt the same reading. The typology is so durable that it shapes Christian reading of the entire Pentateuch — Moses up the mountain but not across the Jordan, seeing but not entering.

Medieval Christian exegesis — especially the *Glossa Ordinaria* and Aquinas — used this typology to explain why the old covenant could not accomplish what the new could. Aquinas (ST I-II q. 98 a. 1) treats Moses as faithful but limited, a servant in Christ's house (Heb 3:5–6). Hebrews 3–4 had already established this theological framework: the "rest" (*menûḥâ* here in v. 20) promised through Joshua was not the final rest; a sabbath-rest remains for the people of God. Deuteronomy 3's rest-theology becomes the deep background for Hebrews.

Reformation commentators read the chapter with fresh intensity. Luther preached on Deuteronomy in 1529; his sermon on chapter 3 treats Moses's refused prayer as a foundational example of prayer under God's "no." God's refusal is not rejection but discipline — Moses is shown the land, which is better than not seeing it, and is given Joshua as the fulfillment of his work. Calvin's *Commentary on the Last Four Books of Moses* (on 3:26) insists that even the greatest servants of God are subject to discipline, and that the refusal should teach believers not to presume. Calvin rejects sentimental readings: Moses sinned at Meribah, and the consequence stands.

The Reformed political-theological tradition drew extensively on the Transjordan settlement (vv. 12–20) for theorizing inter-tribal obligation. Samuel Rutherford's *Lex Rex* (1644) invoked the requirement that the Transjordan tribes fight for their brothers as a foundational text for mutual national obligation within a federation. Eric Nelson's *Hebrew Republic* (2010) traces this line through seventeenth-century political thought.

Critical scholarship, from de Wette through Wellhausen to Martin Noth's *Deuteronomistic History* (1943), has treated the chapter primarily as the closing frame of the Transjordan narrative in Deuteronomy's historical prologue, redacted in light of later monarchic and exilic concerns. Weinfeld's AB commentary analyzes the treaty-form function of Moses's succession of Joshua (vv. 21–22, 28) as analogous to royal succession oaths in neo-Assyrian and Hittite treaties. Thomas Römer (*The So-Called Deuteronomistic History*) distinguishes Assyrian-era and exilic editorial layers in the Joshua commissioning material.

Contemporary commentators follow varied paths. Walter Brueggemann (AOTC 2001) reads Moses's prayer as a paradigm of faith that accepts divine "no" without ceasing to be faith. Patrick Miller (Interpretation 1990) emphasizes the pedagogical intent: Moses's refusal teaches Israel about the gift-character of the land and the limits of even prophetic authority. Christopher Wright (NIBC 1996) and McConville (Apollos 2002) develop the Joshua-typology without flattening Moses's tragedy. Daniel Block (NIVAC 2012) argues for a pastoral reading: Moses's grief is a model for leaders who labor toward goals they will not personally achieve. Ellen Davis (*Scripture, Culture, and Agriculture*, 2009) notes the agricultural anxiety in the "good land" of v. 25 — Moses is denied what every peasant would most want.

### Convergence and Divergence

Both traditions treat Moses's refused prayer as theologically central. Jewish tradition reads it primarily through the register of Moses's personal stature, and through the liturgical pairing with Tisha b'Av reads the refusal as a paradigm of Jewish communal longing for unfulfilled restoration. Christian tradition reads it typologically — Moses as Law, Joshua/Jesus as fulfillment — and treats the refusal as structural, pointing beyond itself.

The divergence is sharp: for Jewish readers, Moses's incompletion is itself meaningful — prophetic leadership does not require arrival. For Christian readers, the incompletion is *supersessible* — Joshua does what Moses cannot. Richard Hays's *Echoes of Scripture in the Gospels* (2016) has argued for a more chastened Christian typology that respects the Jewish reading's weight; the fact is that Moses's request is denied *because of Israel* ("the LORD was angry with me because of you," v. 26), which complicates any simple Moses-as-Law reading.

The Og-as-Rephaim material has had entirely different trajectories. Jewish tradition developed it into rich legend. Christian tradition allegorized the giants as demonic powers defeated by Christ. Modern critical scholarship reads the Rephaim material as vestigial Canaanite cultic memory, which neither tradition had the comparative ANE tools to see.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

### Tension 1: Moses's Refused Prayer and the Justice of God

Why does YHWH refuse Moses's request to enter the land? The text offers a reason: YHWH's anger "because of you" (v. 26), alluding to Meribah (Num 20:10–13). But the reason raises its own questions. If Moses's sin at Meribah was serious enough to bar him from the land, why does the most faithful servant in the Hebrew Bible die outside the promise while an entire wicked generation passes through the land after him? The tension is not new; it is the tension the text itself raises.

**Reformed**: Calvin (on 3:26) and later Reformed commentators take the refusal as a real consequence of real sin — Moses's action at Meribah constituted a failure to sanctify YHWH before the people, and the penalty, severe though it was, testifies to the holiness of God. The text emphasizes that Moses is not spared because of his greatness; grace is unearned and consequences are real. Jonathan Edwards developed this in his treatment of God's moral government.

**Catholic**: The Catholic tradition, particularly through Aquinas and the Fathers, sees Moses's death outside the land as part of the imperfection of the old covenant. The *Catechism* (#708) places Moses in a line of mediators whose limitation prepares for Christ. Benedict XVI in *Jesus of Nazareth* (2007) treats Moses's incompletion as pointing to the ultimate mediator whose work does not leave believers outside the land.

**Jewish**: Jewish tradition holds multiple positions in tension. Rashi treats the refusal as strict justice; Maimonides sees it as divine pedagogy; later Hasidic readings understand Moses's "failure" as itself a kind of perfection — Moses remains on the side of desire, never crossing over into completion, and this is his glory. Levinas developed this into a philosophical ethic: the prophet does not enter the promised land.

**Wesleyan**: Wesley in his *Notes* treats the refusal as evidence that even the holiest are imperfect, and that grace does not erase natural consequence. The Wesleyan reading accommodates the sharpness of v. 26 without despair.

**Critical**: Weinfeld, Nelson, and Dennis Olson treat the refusal as theologically motivated narrative construction — the Deuteronomic historians needed to explain why Moses did not complete the conquest, and the Meribah story became the explanation. The refusal is structurally necessary: Moses must die outside the land so that the book can end where it ends and the Deuteronomistic history can begin where it begins with Joshua.

**Steelman (critical reading, least intuitive for traditionalists)**: If the refusal is narrative construction, its function is not to report a real-time injustice but to constitute Moses as the liminal figure par excellence — prophet who sees but does not enter, speaks but does not possess. This is not a cheapening of the text; it is the theological claim the narrative was designed to make. The Deuteronomic school placed Moses where Israel needed him: at the threshold, showing both what YHWH requires and what YHWH gives, but never crossing into the space where the people must make their own decisions about faithfulness.

**Where this leaves us**: The tension cannot be dissolved without losing something. Traditional readings keep the moral stakes high — even Moses is not above discipline. Critical readings keep the literary stakes high — Moses at the threshold is theologically indispensable. Both agree that Moses's refusal is not incidental but central.

### Tension 2: Og, the Rephaim, and the Reality of Giants

Deuteronomy 3 presents Og as a literal king with a literal bed whose dimensions can be checked in Rabbah of the Ammonites. He is also "last of the remnant of the Rephaim" — a term with deep mythological resonance across the ANE, referring to pre-historic giant-beings and to deified dead. The text treats Og as both historical opponent and mythological survivor. How should readers handle this?

**Reformed**: Calvin and most traditional Protestant commentators treat Og as a historical king of exceptional size, using "Rephaim" as an ethnographic term for a taller-than-average people. The bed is taken at face value or with minor adjustments. The mythological resonance is either ignored or deflected.

**Catholic**: Catholic tradition has been more comfortable with the legendary material. The giants of Gen 6, the Anakim of Num 13, and the Rephaim of Deut 2–3 fit into a broader picture of primeval powers — what Augustine called the "ancient men of renown" — displaced as human history unfolds. The bed is a relic of that displacement, not a challenge to faith.

**Orthodox**: Eastern Orthodox readings have preserved legendary material in liturgical and hagiographic tradition. The giants are real, were defeated, and their defeat is part of the cosmic drama of salvation.

**Jewish**: Rabbinic Judaism cheerfully develops Og into the mythic survivor of the Flood (Niddah 61a, Zevachim 113b). Rashi repeats the legend. The text's mythological resonance is not suppressed but amplified.

**Critical**: Modern comparative scholarship (Ugaritic studies, especially KTU 1.161; John Day, *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan*; Nicolas Wyatt) reads Og and the Rephaim material against the background of ANE cults of divinized royal ancestors. Bashan and Edrei were Ugaritic cultic centers associated with the Rephaim-dead. Og as "last of the Rephaim" at Bashan is a theological claim: YHWH has defeated the primordial powers in their own cultic homeland.

**Steelman (comparative-ANE reading, least intuitive for modernist skeptics)**: The critical-comparative reading is not a demythologization but a re-theologization. Og is not just a king; he represents what ANE religion worshipped — the deified dead, the primeval powers, the Rephaim cults that dominated pre-Israelite Bashan. Deuteronomy's polemic is that YHWH defeats not only human kings but the cosmic powers they embody. The bed in Rabbah, whatever its material reality, functions as a trophy of YHWH's victory over the chthonic deities of the Transjordan. Modern scientific scruples about literal giants miss the point; the text is making a theological claim about YHWH's power over forces ANE religion took seriously.

**Where this leaves us**: The Og material is neither simple history nor simple legend. It functions as a theological marker that YHWH defeats the powers of other cults in their own homeland. Traditions differ on how literally to read the specifics; all traditions agree on the theological claim.

**One unresolved question**: What does it mean theologically that the Transjordan — including Bashan, Gilead, and the territory Reuben and Gad settled — was always marginal to Israelite history, lost early to Assyria in 733 BC (2 Kings 15:29), and never featured prominently in later Israelite self-understanding? The land won here was the first lost. Does this mean that the giving of land is conditional in a way the text does not fully acknowledge, or that YHWH's gifts operate on longer time-scales than territorial possession?

## The Thread

Three cross-references that open the chapter outward:

First, Moses's view from Pisgah (vv. 27–29) connects directly to Deuteronomy 34:1–4, where Moses ascends Mount Nebo (the peak of Pisgah) and YHWH shows him "all the land" before he dies. The narrative frame Deuteronomy 3 opens is closed only at the end of the book. The chapter's emotional weight resolves only thirty-one chapters later.

Second, Joshua 11:21–22 tells of Joshua's destruction of the Anakim — the only survivors being those in Gaza, Gath, and Ashdod. This connects forward to 1 Samuel 17, where Goliath of Gath (a descendant of these survivors, 2 Sam 21:22) is slain by David. The Og-Anakim-Goliath chain is not a coincidence; the narrative of YHWH's victory over giant-power runs through Israel's history.

Third, the Transjordan tribes' commitment to fight alongside their brothers (vv. 18–20) is tested in Joshua 22, when the altar at the Jordan is built and nearly precipitates civil war, and again in Judges 5:15–17, where Gilead and Reuben are rebuked for failing to join Deborah's battle. The vow of inter-tribal obligation made in Deuteronomy 3 is a recurring pressure point through the tribal period. The "Hebrew Republic" of early Protestant political theory (Rutherford, Selden, Grotius) built explicitly on this material.

Finally — a fourth thread — the word *rab-lāk* ("enough from you," v. 26) is picked up and reversed in Deuteronomy 1:6 ("you have dwelt long enough at this mountain") — suggesting a deliberate echo. And in the Jewish liturgy, the root *rab* ("great, much") comes to characterize YHWH's greatness. The same word that silences Moses in v. 26 celebrates YHWH in the Kaddish (*yitgadal veyitkadash shemei rabba* — "magnified and sanctified be His great Name"). The silencing of Moses and the magnification of God use the same linguistic root.
