# Psalm 37 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 37 carries the simplest of superscriptions — *le-David* (לְדָוִד, "of David") — but its content is the longest, most sustained piece of wisdom-pedagogy in the Psalter. Form-critically Hermann Gunkel classified it as a *Weisheitspsalm* (wisdom psalm), tightly paralleled to the book of Proverbs. Its didactic mood (imperatives addressed to a younger student), its retributive logic (the wicked perish, the righteous endure), and its experiential authority — *na'ar hayiti gam-zaqanti* ("I have been young, and now am old," v. 25) — all place it in the wisdom tradition that Gerhard von Rad analyzed in *Wisdom in Israel* (1972) as Israel's distinctive way of theologizing through observation of created order.

The dating is contested but the sapiential register is uncontested. Mitchell Dahood (Anchor Bible) sees post-exilic shaping; Hossfeld-Zenger and Goldingay (BCOTWP) note that the wisdom *Sitz im Leben* may have been schools and scribal academies rather than the temple cult. Sigmund Mowinckel did not deny that some psalms belonged to wisdom circles outside the festival. The vocabulary — *tzaddiq* (צַדִּיק) and *rasha* (רָשָׁע), *ashrei* and *hesed*, the imagery of *eretz* (אֶרֶץ, "land/earth") as inheritance — situates the psalm near the world of Proverbs 2-3 and 10-15, the older sentence-wisdom collections. Job and Ecclesiastes will eventually contest exactly the retributive theology Psalm 37 confidently asserts.

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve substantial portions of Psalm 37 in 4QPsa and especially in **4Q171 — the Pesher on Psalm 37 (4QpPsa)** — one of the great Qumran finds. The pesher reads the psalm as prophecy of the eschatological community: the *tzaddiqim* are the Yahad of the Teacher of Righteousness; the *resha'im* are the Wicked Priest and the Man of the Lie. The "meek who inherit the land" (v. 11) become the Qumran covenanters who will inherit the eschatological inheritance. This is one of the earliest interpretive moves to read the psalm's *eretz* as eschatological land.

The Septuagint (Ps 36 in LXX numbering) translates *eretz* as *gē* (γῆ) throughout — preserving the ambiguity: it can mean "land" (Israel) or "earth." That LXX rendering matters enormously for the New Testament reception, especially Matthew 5:5.

The most distinctive feature historically is the **acrostic structure**. Each pair of verses begins with a successive letter of the Hebrew alphabet: *aleph* (v. 1), *bet* (v. 3), *gimel* (v. 5), and so on. The psalm is one of nine fully or partially alphabetic acrostics in the Psalter (Pss 9-10, 25, 34, 37, 111, 112, 119, 145). Some letters are doubled (the *daleth* extends across vv. 7-8) and the *ayin* appears only by emendation in v. 28a — a textual difficulty the LXX preserves with a longer reading that completes the *ayin*. This structural device signals a deliberate didactic intention: comprehensiveness, memorability, *aleph-tav* completeness.

Hebrew terms are densely loaded. *Al-tit'char* (אַל־תִּתְחַר, vv. 1, 7, 8), from the root *ḥ-r-h*, denotes the heat of envy or fretting indignation — three repetitions structure the psalm's opening movement. The *dal* (poor) and *evyon* (needy) of v. 14 are the canonical pair for the economically vulnerable. *Anavim* (עֲנָוִים, v. 11), translated "meek," denotes those bowed-low in social and spiritual humility — neither resignation nor weakness but a covenantal stance of dependence on YHWH.

ANE parallels are real but secondary. The Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemope* (c. 1300 BC) shares with both Proverbs and Psalm 37 the genre of paternal wisdom-instruction, and Amenemope's counsel against contention with the wicked has formal similarities. But Psalm 37 grounds its advice not in worldly prudence alone but in covenantal *hesed*: the *eretz* is YHWH's gift, not merely a horizon of survival.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Robert Lowth's *parallelismus membrorum* operates at high density throughout the psalm. Antithetical parallelism dominates, with the *tzaddiq* / *rasha* contrast structuring nearly every couplet:

- v. 16: "Better is the little that the righteous (*tzaddiq*) has / than the abundance of many wicked (*resha'im*)."
- v. 17: "For the arms of the wicked shall be broken / but the LORD upholds the righteous."
- v. 21: "The wicked borrows but does not pay back / but the righteous is generous and gives."

Synonymous parallelism reinforces (v. 30: "the mouth of the righteous utters wisdom / and his tongue speaks justice"). Synthetic parallelism (where the second line completes or develops the first) carries the imperatives of vv. 3-7.

The acrostic is not merely formal. Patrick Skehan and others have argued that the alphabetic structure functions as a wisdom-poetic claim: the psalm covers the *whole alphabet* of the moral life, an *aleph-tav* of righteousness. The deliberate doubling and irregularities suggest that the form is held loosely — content presses through structure when it must.

Strophic divisions are debated. Most modern commentators (Kraus, Goldingay, Tate, Hossfeld-Zenger) propose roughly:

- **vv. 1-11:** opening exhortation — three "fret not" imperatives, three "trust / delight / commit" imperatives, climaxing in v. 11's promise of the meek inheriting.
- **vv. 12-22:** contrast of fates — the wicked plot, the LORD laughs, swords turn back, *eretz* inheritance recurs.
- **vv. 23-31:** the established steps — providential preservation, the elder's testimony of v. 25, the inner Torah of v. 31.
- **vv. 32-40:** conflict and salvation — the watching enemy, the unfound wicked of vv. 35-36, and the closing *machseh* (refuge, v. 40).

The verb *yarash* (יָרַשׁ, "inherit") and the phrase *yir'shu eretz* ("they will inherit the land/earth") form a structural *Leitmotif*, recurring in vv. 9, 11, 22, 29, 34. Five iterations build a theological insistence: *eretz* is the destination of the psalm's wisdom, the eschatological horizon toward which fretting, envy, and patient trust all point.

Inclusio binds the whole. The opening *al-tit'char* / "fret not" (v. 1) and the closing *machseh / yoshi'em* / "they take refuge in him / he saves them" (v. 40) bracket the psalm: do not fret because there is a refuge. There is no *selah* — the psalm is meant for sustained didactic reading, not liturgical breathing.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Psalm 37 sits in Book I (Pss 1-41) as the Psalter's most extensive wisdom meditation. Patrick Miller and Gerald Wilson, drawing on the canonical-criticism approach Wilson laid out in *The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* (1985), see Psalm 37 as deliberately positioned: its retributive confidence answers the laments around it (Ps 35, 38, 39) with sapiential patience, and its *eretz*-vocabulary echoes Psalm 1's tree planted by streams of water.

**Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 37 develops v. 25 — "I have not seen the righteous forsaken" — at length, with rabbinic narrators expanding the testimony into instances of providential rescue. The Qumran *Pesher on Psalm 37* (4Q171), already mentioned, is foundational: the meek who inherit the land become the Yahad. Rashi reads the *eretz* of v. 11 as the literal Land of Israel and the inheritance as the eschatological return; he glosses *anavim* as those who endure suffering for Torah. Ibn Ezra emphasizes the wisdom-genre and treats v. 25's testimony as the experiential authority of the elder, not as a universal claim immune to apparent counterexamples. Radak harmonizes Psalm 37 with Job by distinguishing apparent forsakenness from ultimate forsakenness. Nachmanides reads the psalm's promises eschatologically, locating their fulfillment in the world-to-come.

In modern Jewish scholarship: Nahum Sarna treats *eretz* as inheritance of the Land within Israel's covenantal history. Jon Levenson, in *Resurrection and the Restoration of Israel*, situates Psalm 37 within the broader biblical witness to ultimate vindication. Avivah Zornberg's literary-psychological reading would attend to the elder's voice in v. 25 — the testimony that has *seen* and the strange confidence that comes of seeing. Buber, in *Right and Wrong*, reads Psalm 37 as a wisdom-meditation on the apparent prosperity of the wicked and the patient *Bewegung* (movement) of trust. Heschel emphasizes the psalm's pedagogical insistence on inner stillness — *dom la-Yahweh ve-hitcholel lo* (v. 7).

Liturgically, Psalm 37 features in some traditions for the day of Yom HaZikaron and on the Sabbath of consolation; verses 23-24 are quoted at funerals.

**Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* (Latin Ps 36) treats Psalm 37 as a portrait of the patient endurance of the *civitas Dei* amid the prospering *civitas terrena*. He reads v. 25 not as denial of the suffering of the righteous but as testimony to ultimate divine fidelity. Athanasius recommends the psalm to the soul tempted to envy the wicked. Cassiodorus structures his commentary around the alphabetic letters, seeing pedagogical comprehensiveness.

Aquinas (*Postilla*) develops the connection between v. 4 — *delight yourself in the LORD, and he will give you the desires of your heart* — and his theology of the rectified will: when the will is ordered to God, what it desires is God, and God gives himself.

Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* and Calvin's 1557 commentary both engage Psalm 37 at length. Calvin emphasizes the experiential pedagogy: the psalm teaches the believer to *interpret time differently*, to read history under the long view of providence rather than the short view of envy. Calvin is unusually firm that v. 25 must be read with discriminating care — the elder's testimony is true within the covenant of providence but does not preclude that the righteous suffer in this life.

Modern critical readers — Gunkel, Mowinckel, Westermann (*Praise and Lament*), Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate, Wenham (*The Psalter Reclaimed*), Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*), Patrick Miller, William P. Brown (*Seeing the Psalms*), Beth Tanner, Susan Gillingham (*Psalms Through the Centuries*) — generally agree on the wisdom genre and the retributive theology, while differing on how that theology relates to the lament tradition (Ps 73, Ps 88, Job).

**NT use.** Matthew 5:5 — *makarioi hoi praeis, hoti autoi klēronomēsousin tēn gēn* ("blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth") — quotes Ps 37:11 directly, with *praeis* rendering *anavim* and *klēronomēsousin tēn gēn* rendering *yir'shu eretz* (LXX *klēronomēsousin gēn*). This is the only direct quotation of an Old Testament verse in the Beatitudes (Matt 5:3-12). Romans 4:13 echoes the inheritance promise to Abraham. Hebrews 11:13-16 reframes inheritance eschatologically. 1 Peter 3:10-12 quotes Ps 34, but its spirit of patient endurance breathes Ps 37.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Retributive wisdom vs. lived experience.** Psalm 37 confidently asserts that the wicked perish and the righteous endure, that *I have not seen the righteous forsaken* (v. 25). Job, Ecclesiastes, and Psalm 73 will protest exactly this confidence. (a) **Naive retributivism (read flat):** the psalm is simply wrong about how the world works, and its testimony is sociologically narrow — the experience of an elder in a relatively stable community. (b) **Eschatological retributivism (Augustine, Aquinas, Nachmanides, much of the tradition):** the psalm is true on the long horizon but not necessarily on the daily one — *I have not seen the righteous forsaken forever*. (c) **Pedagogical retributivism (Calvin, Brueggemann, von Rad):** the psalm teaches a way of *interpreting* what one sees, training the worshipper to read providence amid apparent counterevidence; its truth is performative, not predictive. (d) **Dialogical retributivism (canonical reading — Childs, Wilson, Wenham):** Psalm 37 stands in the canon *with* Job and Psalm 73, not over them; the Psalter holds the tension rather than resolving it.

The strongest steelman is the dialogical reading: the canon does not retract Psalm 37 when it adds Psalm 73. Rather, Psalm 37 articulates the *normal* shape of a faithful life within providence, and Psalm 73 articulates the crisis when the normal shape seems to fail. Both are scripture; neither is the whole. To take Psalm 37 alone is to flatten the canon. To take Psalm 73 alone is to lose the everyday faithfulness Psalm 37 trains.

**Tension 2: The Beatitude — what is the *eretz*?** Matthew 5:5's quotation of v. 11 has produced sharply different readings. (a) **Land of Israel reading (Sarna, much rabbinic tradition):** the *eretz* is *the* Land — the promised inheritance restored to a chastened, meek Israel. (b) **Cosmic-eschatological reading (N. T. Wright, *The Climax of the Covenant*; Brant Pitre):** Matthew universalizes — the meek inherit *the earth*, the renewed creation, fulfilling Israel's promise on a cosmic scale. (c) **Wesleyan / Catholic Beatitudes-as-virtue reading:** *praeis* names a Christian virtue (gentleness, non-grasping); the inheritance is its own consequence in the kingdom of God. (d) **Reformed eschatological-promise reading:** the Beatitudes are not virtue-instructions but kingdom-promises; the meek inherit because the kingdom belongs to such, by grace not by attainment. (e) **Hauerwasian practice-reading (Stanley Hauerwas, *Matthew*):** the Beatitudes describe the *practices* of the church in the world; the meek are those who refuse the violence of the *resha'im*, and they inherit because the church *is* the inheritance.

The strongest steelman is the cosmic-eschatological reading: Wright argues that Matthew, writing in a post-AD-70 context, deliberately universalizes Israel's land-promise to fit Jesus' kingdom-of-heaven vision. *Tēn gēn* in Matthew is what *eretz* always *anticipated* — the renewed earth in which YHWH dwells with his people (Rev 21-22). Psalm 37's *yir'shu eretz* is not abandoned by Matthew but expanded; the meek of the new covenant inherit what the meek of the old covenant longed for in microcosm.

Where this leaves us: Psalm 37 is a wisdom-meditation under pressure. The pressure is not merely intellectual but pastoral — it is hard to wait while the *resha'im* prosper. The psalm does not deny the pressure; it teaches a posture under it. *Al-tit'char* — do not let the heat of envy in. *Bonu kashor ezarekha* — be still and wait. *Yir'shu eretz* — the inheritance is coming. The wisdom is not that the world is fair but that *YHWH is a refuge* (v. 40) and the long view is real. Whether that long view is Israel's land, the church's practice, or the renewed cosmos depends on where one stands in the canonical drama. The psalm itself stands at the threshold and points.

**Closing question:** When the elder of v. 25 says "I have not seen the righteous forsaken," do we take this as autobiographical testimony, eschatological promise, or pedagogical formation — and how would changing the answer change how we live in the actual middle of envy and patience?

## The Thread

- **Psalm 1:** the *tzaddiq* / *rasha* contrast and the tree planted by water; Ps 37 elaborates the wisdom Ps 1 announces.
- **Psalm 73:** Asaph's confession of nearly stumbling at the prosperity of the wicked — Ps 37's confidence under crisis.
- **Job 21:** Job's protest that the wicked *do* prosper, untroubled, rebutting the friends' Ps-37-style retribution.
- **Matthew 5:5:** the Beatitude that takes *yir'shu eretz* into the kingdom of heaven and reframes the inheritance cosmically.
