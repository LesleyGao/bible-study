# Matthew 6: The Three Pieties, the Lord's Prayer, and the Lilies

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Matthew 6 takes the listener inside the three pillars of first-century Jewish piety: almsgiving (*tsedakah*), prayer (*tefillah*), and fasting (*tsom*). The triad is not Matthew's invention — Tobit 12:8 says "prayer is good with fasting and almsgiving," and m. Avot 1:2 lists the three things on which the world stands: Torah, worship, and deeds of loving-kindness. Jesus is teaching inside a known catechetical structure, not displacing it. What he attacks is the *theatricality* of public piety — the sounding of trumpets, the standing on street corners, the disfigured fasting face — practices for which there is genuine first-century evidence and rabbinic critique. The "trumpet" of 6:2 is debated: Jeremias suggested a literal shofar-blast announcing fast-day collections; more likely it is a metaphor for self-advertisement. The street-corner prayer is the *Amidah* (the eighteen benedictions), recited three times daily standing, which a person caught outdoors at the appointed hour would simply pause to pray — but could be timed deliberately for maximum visibility. The "disfigured face" of 6:16 reflects the practice of going unwashed and ash-streaked on fast days; the standard public fast was Yom Kippur, but supplementary fasts (Mondays and Thursdays in Pharisaic practice; the four fasts of Zechariah 8:19) were widespread.

The material world is densely Galilean. The "synagogues" (*synagōgais*, συναγωγαῖς, 6:2, 5) are first-century village assembly buildings — the Magdala synagogue excavated in 2009 is the best-preserved Galilean exemplar, with its limestone-carved Magdala stone showing the Temple menorah. The "inner room" (*tameion*, ταμεῖον, 6:6) is the storeroom of a Galilean four-room house, often the only room with a door that could be shut — the village houses excavated at Capernaum and Nazareth typically have one or two rooms with no door at all. The "moth and rust" (*sēs kai brōsis*, σὴς καὶ βρῶσις, 6:19) refers literally to clothes-moth larvae in stored fine wool and to the corrosion of bronze and iron implements; "rust" can also be glossed "consumption" or "eating away." The "lilies of the field" (*krina tou agrou*, κρίνα τοῦ ἀγροῦ, 6:28) are not the cultivated white lily but the riot of wildflowers — anemones, poppies, gladioli — that briefly carpet the Galilean hillsides in early spring before the summer drought withers them. They are then cut with the "grass of the field" (6:30) for fuel in the bread-oven (*klibanon*, κλίβανον) — the small clay bread-oven of the village courtyard burnt grass and thorns to fire-up briefly to baking heat. The Galilean peasant economy of the chapter is real: subsistence-margin, anxiety-about-tomorrow, a single tunic, a single cloak, daily bread.

The "birds of the air" (6:26) are most plausibly the sparrows that Matthew 10:29 will describe being sold "two for a penny" in the marketplace — small, ubiquitous, fed by God in the sense that they live on what they find rather than what they store. "Mammon" (*mamōnas*, μαμωνᾶς, 6:24) is an Aramaic loanword (*mamona*) appearing in the Targums and at Qumran (1QS 6:2); it means accumulated wealth, often with a connotation of trust placed in wealth.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 6 unfolds in a tightly chiastic structure. The programmatic 6:1 announces the theme: "beware of practicing your righteousness (*dikaiosynē*, δικαιοσύνη) before others to be seen by them." Then three parallel panels on the three pieties (almsgiving 6:2-4, prayer 6:5-15, fasting 6:16-18), each constructed on the same threefold pattern: "when you do X, do not Y as the hypocrites do — truly they have received their reward — but do Z, and your Father who sees in secret will reward you." The Greek *misthos* (μισθός, "reward") appears nine times in the chapter; *kryptō* (κρυπτῷ, "secret") frames each piety. The pattern is so symmetrical that scholars (Davies/Allison, Luz, Betz) treat 6:1-18 as a single compositional unit with the Lord's Prayer (6:9-13) inserted into the middle panel as expansion.

The Lord's Prayer itself is the most-prayed text in Christian history. It exists in two New Testament forms: Matthew 6:9-13 (longer; seven petitions) and Luke 11:2-4 (shorter; five petitions, in a different setting). The form-critical question is which is older. Joachim Jeremias in *The Prayers of Jesus* argued for Lukan priority — Matthew has expanded a shorter original — and most contemporary scholars agree (Davies/Allison; Luz; Betz; Raymond Brown's classic essay "The Pater Noster as an Eschatological Prayer"). The *Didache* 8:2-3, dated by most scholars to ca. 90-110 CE, gives a form essentially identical to Matthew's plus the doxology "for thine is the power and the glory forever," and instructs the community to pray it three times daily — replacing or supplementing the Jewish *Amidah*. The Byzantine textual tradition picked up the doxology back into Matthew 6:13 ("for thine is the kingdom and the power and the glory forever, amen"), which is why the King James Version has it; the earliest Greek manuscripts (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Bezae) do not.

The chiastic structure of the prayer itself is recognized in nearly all the major commentaries: an opening address ("Our Father in heaven") followed by three God-ward petitions in synonymous parallelism (hallowed name / kingdom come / will done) and three human-ward petitions (daily bread / debt forgiveness / deliverance from temptation/evil). The transition from God-ward to human-ward sits at "on earth as it is in heaven" (6:10) — the prayer's hinge.

After the Prayer the chapter pivots to economic ethics: treasures (6:19-21), the eye as lamp (6:22-23), two masters (6:24), and the great extended argument against anxiety with its agrarian images (6:25-34). The structure is associative rather than tightly logical — each saying linked to the next by catchword (treasure / heart / eye / light / darkness / serve / mammon / anxious / tomorrow) — which is a hallmark of oral teaching crystallized in writing.

Textual variants in chapter 6 are minor except for the doxology issue (6:13b). One translation crux dwarfs all others: *epiousion* (ἐπιούσιον) in 6:11. The word appears in Luke 11:3, in Matthew 6:11, in *Didache* 8:2, and in exactly one fragmentary 5th-century papyrus shopping list (now lost; reported by F.G. Kenyon) and nowhere else in Greek literature. Origen in *On Prayer* 27 says he could not find it in any other Greek author and suggests the evangelists coined it. The lexical possibilities: from *epi* + *ousia* ("substance," hence Vulgate *supersubstantialem* in Matthew, kept by Aquinas and read eucharistically by Augustine); from *epi* + *eimi* ("for the existing day, daily"); from *epi* + *ienai* ("for the coming day, for tomorrow," supported by Jeremias from a possible Aramaic *lehem yomenu d'ane le-mehar*). The translation choice carries theological weight: "daily" naturalizes the petition as everyday subsistence; "supersubstantial" sacramentalizes it; "for the coming day" eschatologizes it.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Lord's Prayer alone has a reception history sufficient for a doctorate. Tertullian's *On Prayer* (ca. 200), Cyprian of Carthage's *On the Lord's Prayer* (ca. 252), and Origen's *On Prayer* (ca. 233) are the foundational patristic treatments — the three of them set the pattern for Christian commentary on every petition. Augustine devoted Letter 130 (to Proba) and substantial sections of *De Sermone Domini in Monte* to the Prayer; Cyril of Jerusalem's *Catechetical Lectures* uses it as the catechumen's basic instruction; Gregory of Nyssa preached five sermons on it. Aquinas's sermons on the Pater Noster develop the medieval scholastic reading, treating each petition as ordering desire toward God and neighbor. Luther's Small and Large Catechisms (1529) make the Prayer one of the three pillars of Christian instruction (with the Decalogue and Creed); Calvin's *Institutes* 3.20 gives it sustained exposition; the Westminster Larger Catechism (1647) Q. 186-196 treats each petition catechetically. N.T. Wright's *The Lord and His Prayer* (1996) reads the petitions through the lens of Jewish kingdom-eschatology: "your kingdom come" is concrete prayer for God's reign to break in on earth, drawn directly from the Aramaic *qaddish* prayer ("magnified and sanctified be his great name in the world which he created according to his will, and may he establish his kingdom in your lifetime"). The continuities with synagogue prayer language are deep — *Avinu Malkenu* ("our Father, our King") and the *qaddish* and the *Amidah* all sit behind the Lord's Prayer.

Pope Francis in 2017 sparked an international controversy by approving an Italian missal revision changing "lead us not into temptation" to "do not let us fall into temptation," on the theological ground that God does not lead humans into temptation (cf. James 1:13). The Greek *eisenenkēs hēmas eis peirasmon* (μὴ εἰσενέγκῃς ἡμᾶς εἰς πειρασμόν) is grammatically unambiguous — "do not bring us into" — and *peirasmos* can mean ordinary moral temptation, eschatological tribulation (the Great Tribulation, the testing of the last days), or both. Jeremias and Wright read it eschatologically: do not bring us into the final testing.

The economic teaching of 6:19-34 has its own deep reception. The Patristic tradition (Clement of Alexandria's *Quis dives salvetur*, Basil's *To the Rich*, John Chrysostom's homilies, Ambrose's *De Nabuthe*) read the chapter as a sustained critique of accumulated wealth and a call to almsgiving. The medieval mendicant orders (Francis of Assisi, Dominic) take the lilies-of-the-field passage as warrant for radical poverty. The Reformation tradition tends to spiritualize: anxiety as inner sin, mammon as idolatrous trust rather than wealth as such. Wendell Berry's essays in *Sex, Economy, Freedom and Community* recover an agrarian reading of the chapter, treating "consider the lilies" as a meditation on creaturely belonging within ecological limits. Walter Brueggemann's *Sabbath as Resistance* reads "do not be anxious" as Sabbath theology: the rest of trust against the anxiety of acquisition. Liberation theology (Gutiérrez, Sobrino) reads "you cannot serve God and mammon" as the theological ground for the preferential option for the poor.

The Jewish appreciative readings remain crucial. Pinchas Lapide's *The Sermon on the Mount* points out how thoroughly Jewish the chapter is — the three pieties are Jewish, the prayer language is Jewish, the trust-in-providence theme echoes Psalms 37 and 127, and the warning against ostentatious righteousness has direct rabbinic parallels (b. Berakhot 17b on the prayer "may my actions please you and may I not be among those who do their works for show"). David Flusser's *Sermon on the Mount and the Wisdom of Israel* notes that "your Father who sees in secret" closely echoes the Qumran *Hodayot* and second-temple Jewish piety. Amy-Jill Levine's *The Misunderstood Jew* and the Levine/Brettler *Jewish Annotated New Testament* stress that the polemic against "hypocrites" is intra-Jewish moral critique, not anti-Jewish polemic — Jesus is criticizing certain practices in his own community, the way Amos and Isaiah criticized theirs.

Two twentieth-century readings carry particular weight on chapter 6. Bonhoeffer's *Discipleship* treats the three pieties as the test of "extraordinary" (*perissos*) Christian existence: piety done in secret, before God alone, is the form discipleship takes when it has shed the need for human approval. Bonhoeffer's reading of "you cannot serve two masters" is uncompromising — the Christian must choose, and any attempt to compromise is already idolatry. Jacques Ellul's *Money and Power* (1954) reads "mammon" as a *spiritual power*, not merely a metaphor for greed — money becomes a quasi-personal claimant on human worship.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**First tension: how should the Lord's Prayer be prayed and understood — eschatological prayer for the coming kingdom, daily devotional prayer for ordinary needs, or liturgical formula?**

The eschatological reading (Jeremias, Wright, Raymond Brown): every petition is colored by the imminent kingdom. "Hallowed be your name" is the eschatological vindication of God's name (Ezek 36:23). "Your kingdom come" is the concrete prayer for God's reign to arrive on earth. "Daily bread" with *epiousion* read as "for the coming day" can be read as prayer for the bread of the messianic banquet. "Forgive us our debts" is prayer for the eschatological Jubilee. "Lead us not into *peirasmos*" is prayer not to enter the final tribulation. The whole prayer is the disciple's plea: *come, Lord, soon*.

The catechetical-devotional reading (Augustine; Luther's Small Catechism; the broad pastoral tradition): the prayer is the believer's daily ordering of life — God's name, kingdom, will above all; bread, forgiveness, deliverance from sin in the ordinary day. Each petition addresses concrete spiritual and material need.

The liturgical-sacramental reading (Augustine on *epiousion* as supersubstantial; the Eastern and Roman traditions of praying the Pater immediately before communion): the prayer is the church's corporate speech, gathering all believers into one address to the Father, with the bread petition partly Eucharistic.

Steel-manned: each catches something the others lose. The eschatological reading recovers what was probably the original force; the catechetical reading captures how the prayer has actually formed Christian devotional life across two millennia; the liturgical reading captures the prayer as the church's primary act of corporate identity.

Where this leaves us: the prayer is not a single thing. It was likely first prayed eschatologically in Jesus's earliest community; it became liturgical in the *Didache* community within a generation; it has functioned catechetically in every century since. The choice between readings is a choice about which moment of its long life to privilege. The honest reading prays it knowing all three.

**Second tension: how literally must "do not be anxious" be obeyed — and how does that interact with the duty to provide for one's family, plan for the future, save, insure, prepare?**

The Anabaptist-mendicant reading (Francis; Hutterites; some Mennonite traditions; Tolstoy): take it literally. Live without storehouses. Trust day by day. Hold goods in common. The text means what it says.

The Lutheran-Reformed reading (Luther; Calvin; the broad magisterial Protestant tradition): the prohibition is on *anxiety* (*merimnaō*, μεριμνάω) as a posture of unbelieving worry, not on prudence as such. The Christian provides for the household (1 Tim 5:8), saves, plans, but does not let acquisition become a master. Two-kingdoms theology distinguishes the Christian as Christian (free of anxiety) from the Christian as householder (responsible for provision).

The wisdom-tradition reading (Stassen/Gushee; Brueggemann; Berry): the chapter is teaching a *practice* of trust set within an ecological-creaturely framework. "Consider the lilies" is a meditation on belonging — the human stands inside creation and is fed as creation is fed, not outside it as anxious manager.

The liberation reading (Gutiérrez; Sobrino): the command is to a community that practices economic sharing such that the anxiety-question changes shape — what the individual cannot trust God to provide alone, the community of mutual provision can trust God to provide together.

The dispensationalist reading (Scofield; Ryrie): the literal prohibition belongs to the kingdom dispensation, not the church age, and is not directly binding on present Christian conduct.

Steel-manned: each reading is responding to a real pastoral question — the radical to the affluent church's domestication of Jesus, the magisterial Protestant to the dissolution of ordinary household duty, the wisdom tradition to the disconnect of modern life from creaturely belonging, the liberationist to individualistic readings that ignore communal practice, the dispensationalist to the puzzle of why most Christians clearly do not literally live this way.

Where this leaves us: the prohibition is on the *posture of anxious accumulation*, and the test is whether the Christian's actual relationship to wealth — money in the bank, the retirement account, the insurance policy, the inheritance — feels like trust or feels like the second master. Bonhoeffer's reading still cuts: "you cannot serve two masters" makes the choice unavoidable, and most middle-class Christian compromise is precisely the attempt to evade the choice.

**Unresolved question:** What does *epiousion* mean? After two thousand years of Christian commentary, the word remains the great untranslated puzzle of the New Testament. The translation choice rebalances the prayer's center of gravity each time.

## The Thread

Matthew 6 connects to the Hebrew Bible at every joint. The three pieties echo Tobit 12:8 and the Pharisaic-rabbinic threefold framing. The Lord's Prayer's address echoes Avinu Malkenu and the Qaddish. "Hallowed be your name" echoes Ezekiel 36:23. "Your will be done on earth as in heaven" echoes Psalm 103:20-21. "Forgive us our debts" echoes the Jubilee legislation of Leviticus 25 and the prophetic concern for debt-release in Isaiah 58. "Lilies of the field" echoes Solomon's glory in 1 Kings 10. "Seek first the kingdom" echoes Proverbs 3:5-6 and Psalm 37:4-5.

Forward: the Lord's Prayer is taken up in the *Didache* 8 and becomes the church's daily prayer; the Lukan parallel at Luke 11:1-13 expands the prayer with the parable of the friend at midnight and "ask, seek, knock" (which Matthew places in 7:7-11). The economic teaching is reopened in Matthew 19:16-30 (the rich young ruler), Matthew 25:14-30 (the talents), and Matthew 25:31-46 (the judgment of the nations on care for the poor). James 1:9-11 and James 5:1-6 are the New Testament's most direct echoes of Matthew 6's economic ethic.
