# John 5: The Healing at Bethesda and the Father-Son Discourse

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter opens with Jesus going up to Jerusalem for an unnamed "feast of the Jews" (5:1). The vagueness has spawned an entire scholarly literature: Brown (Anchor Bible) thinks Pentecost likeliest; Carson and Köstenberger lean toward Tabernacles; some manuscripts insert the definite article ("the feast"), pulling toward Passover. The ambiguity matters less than the geography it triggers — Jesus, a Galilean teacher, returns repeatedly to the holy city, and John frames his ministry as a series of festival confrontations rather than (as in the Synoptics) a single climactic Passover.

The decisive historical detail is the pool itself. John locates it ἐπὶ τῇ προβατικῇ — "by the Sheep Gate" — and gives its Aramaic name *Bēthzatha* / *Bēthesda* (בית חסדא, "house of mercy" or "house of grace"; some manuscripts read *Bēthsaida*, "house of the fisher"). Critically, John specifies five roofed colonnades (πέντε στοὰς ἔχουσα). For most of the modern critical era, this number was treated as theological symbolism — five porticoes for the five books of Moses (so Augustine), or simply a literary flourish, since pools known from the Greco-Roman world were typically rectangular and four-sided. Bultmann took the number as evidence the evangelist was working from a Signs Source rather than firsthand topographical knowledge.

Then archaeology reversed the verdict. Beginning with explorations near St. Anne's Church in the 19th century and culminating in the systematic Dominican excavations from 1956 onward (under the École Biblique, with Pierre Benoit and later Jean-Baptiste Humbert), excavators uncovered a twin-pool complex separated by a central rock partition — four colonnades around the perimeter and a fifth running across the dividing wall. Five porticoes, exactly as John describes. Urban C. von Wahlde has made this the textbook case for the Fourth Gospel's topographical reliability; John Wilkinson's work on Jerusalem holy sites confirmed the alignment. The site also yielded votive offerings to Asclepius and Serapis from the Roman period, suggesting that by the second century the pool had been adapted as a healing shrine for the pagan god — which raises the uncomfortable possibility that even in Jesus's day the "stirring of the water" (5:7) reflected popular folk-medical beliefs at the edges of Jewish practice.

The 38-year duration of the man's illness is striking but probably not coincidental. Numbers carry weight in Johannine narrative. Israel wandered 38 years between Kadesh-barnea and the crossing of the Zered (Deut 2:14), and Augustine, Calvin, and a long medieval tradition read the paralytic as a figure for Israel under the law, healed when Christ commands what the law could not.

The Sabbath conflict (5:9b-18) lands the narrative inside a sharp halakhic dispute. Carrying a load on the Sabbath was already controversial in Second-Temple Jewish practice; the Mishnah's tractate *Shabbat* (eventually codified ca. 200 CE but reflecting earlier oral tradition) lists thirty-nine categories of forbidden labor (*avot melachot*, *Shabbat* 7:2), and "carrying from one domain to another" is one of them. Jeremiah 17:21-22 already prohibited bearing burdens through the city gates on Sabbath. Jesus's command "take up your bed and walk" thus triggers a known and codified objection. What escalates the conflict is not the healing per se — there is rabbinic precedent for healing on Sabbath when life is endangered — but the carrying.

Three Greek terms anchor the discourse:

**ἐργάζομαι** (*ergazomai*, "to work") — Jesus's defense, "My Father is working until now (ἕως ἄρτι ἐργάζεται), and I am working" (5:17), engages a known rabbinic problem: God rested on the seventh day, yet still sustains the world, and rabbinic discussion (Genesis Rabbah 11:10; Philo *Legum Allegoriae* 1.5-6) had already wrestled with whether divine providence counts as "work." Jesus's claim aligns himself with that ongoing divine activity.

**ἴσος τῷ θεῷ** (*isos tō theō*, "equal to God") — the bystanders' interpretation of his Father-claim (5:18). The phrase is technical; it appears in Philippians 2:6 and in Greco-Roman discourse about deification. John's narrator does not contest the inference.

**μαρτυρία** (*martyria*, "testimony, witness") — the legal-rhetorical key to 5:31-47. Jewish law required two or three witnesses for capital matters (Deut 19:15), and Jesus organizes his self-defense around five: John the Baptist, his works, the Father, the Scriptures, and Moses.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Structurally, John 5 is a hinge. The healing at Bethesda is the **third of seven signs** (after Cana, 2:1-11, and the official's son, 4:46-54), and unlike the first two — which produce belief — this one produces escalating hostility. Bultmann argued the *Sēmeia Quelle* (Signs Source) once contained the miracles in a more triumphant sequence and that John has deliberately subverted the form by appending a hostile reception. Whether or not one accepts a Signs Source, the pattern is real: from chapter 5 onward, signs increasingly polarize rather than persuade.

The chapter splits cleanly into a narrative half (5:1-18) and a discursive half (5:19-47), the longest sustained Christological monologue in John before the Bread of Life Discourse. C.H. Dodd, in *The Interpretation of the Fourth Gospel*, treated this pattern — sign followed by interpretive discourse — as the structural signature of the gospel's middle section. Brown's Anchor Bible commentary calls 5:19-30 the "miniature gospel within the gospel," noting that it contains realized eschatology ("an hour is coming, and is now here," 5:25) and final eschatology ("an hour is coming when all who are in the tombs will hear his voice," 5:28-29) within a few verses — a juxtaposition that has fueled the long debate, joined by Bultmann (who argued the future-eschatology lines were a later ecclesiastical redactor's gloss) and Käsemann (who in *The Testament of Jesus* read the Johannine Christ as a "naively docetic" figure walking the earth as God).

A textual-critical issue presents itself early. Most modern critical editions (NA28, UBS5) omit verses 5:3b-4 — the passage about the angel troubling the water — because the earliest and best manuscripts (𝔓⁶⁶, 𝔓⁷⁵, Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Codex Bezae in part) lack it. Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary* notes the verse uses non-Johannine vocabulary and offers a "miraculous" mechanical-folkloric explanation alien to the gospel's theology. Bart Ehrman has argued the addition reflects later scribal harmonization with popular healing-shrine traditions. Without 5:4, the man's reference to the water being "stirred up" in 5:7 hangs without explanation — possibly the original intent: a folk belief Jesus does not endorse.

The discourse itself develops two of John's signature literary patterns. First, the **double-amen formula**: "Truly, truly (ἀμὴν ἀμήν), I say to you" appears at 5:19, 5:24, and 5:25 — the only place in any gospel where Jesus says "amen, amen" three times in a single passage. Second, the **Father-Son symmetry**: a long series of paired statements ("as the Father… so also the Son") that creates what Marianne Meye Thompson (NTL) calls a "grammar of mutual indwelling." The literary effect is hypnotic; the theological effect is to claim equality of operation while preserving derivation of authority.

The witness section (5:31-47) deploys forensic vocabulary — *martyria*, *krisis* (judgment), *alētheia* (truth) — that Brant (Paideia) has shown borrows from Greco-Roman juridical rhetoric. John structures Jesus's defense as a courtroom apologia. The climax, "you search the Scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life; and it is they that bear witness about me" (5:39), has been read as either a *programmatic Johannine canonical Christology* (so Carson, Köstenberger) or a polemical move against Jewish covenantal reading of Torah (Reinhartz reads it more critically).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the Christian interpretive trajectory, John 5 is foundational for Trinitarian-Christological development. The Father-Son discourse generated some of the longest patristic engagement of any chapter in the New Testament. Origen's *Commentary on John* devotes extensive attention to "the Son can do nothing of his own accord" (5:19), reading it as evidence of derived but co-equal divine activity. Augustine's *Tractates on the Gospel of John* (especially Tractates 17-23) makes 5:19 the locus classicus for distinguishing the Son's eternal generation from temporal subordination — the Son receives all things from the Father eternally, not in time.

Cyril of Alexandria, in his commentary, fights Arianism on this very ground: the Arian reading of 5:19 ("the Son can do nothing") as ontological subordination misses, Cyril insists, the immediate completion ("but only what he sees the Father doing") which establishes equality of activity and therefore of nature. Aquinas in the *Lectura super Joannem* synthesizes the patristic reading: the Son's "seeing" the Father is not temporal observation but eternal procession; what the Son does, he does as one who eternally receives his being from the Father.

The Reformation engagement is dominated by Calvin, whose *Commentary on John* on chapter 5 is foundational for Reformed Christology. Calvin reads 5:17 ("My Father is working until now") as direct claim to participation in providential divine activity, and 5:19 as preserving both equality and order in the Trinity — a careful balance that becomes Reformed orthodoxy. Luther's sermons on John 5 emphasize Jesus's challenge to externalized religion (the man at the pool waiting for water-magic) as a parable of works-righteousness.

Twentieth-century engagement is bracketed by Bultmann and Barth. Bultmann's *Gospel of John* commentary reads the discourse as the evangelist's existentialized Christology — the "hour" that "is now here" (5:25) is the hour of decision in which the hearer is confronted with revelation. Karl Barth in *Church Dogmatics* II/1 takes 5:26 ("as the Father has life in himself, so he has granted the Son also to have life in himself") as the dogmatic anchor of his doctrine of God's aseity shared with the Son. Käsemann's *Testament of Jesus* makes John 5 the prime exhibit for his charge that Johannine Christology is "naively docetic." Catherine Mowry LaCugna in *God for Us* uses the chapter to argue for a relational, economic Trinitarianism that resists the speculative excess of patristic ontology.

Contemporary Johannine commentary continues to mine the chapter. Brown (Anchor Bible) treats 5:19-30 as a parable of son-and-father craft apprenticeship later expanded into Christological discourse — a fascinating sociological reading. Köstenberger (BECNT) emphasizes the chapter as the inauguration of John's "trial motif" that culminates in chapters 18-19. Keener brings massive Greco-Roman parallel material to the witness section. Beasley-Murray (WBC) and Schnackenburg (HthKNT) provide the standard German-language critical readings. Marianne Meye Thompson (NTL) attends carefully to the Father-Son relation as the gospel's theological grammar. Moloney (Sacra Pagina) and O'Day (NIB) hold the literary-narrative readings against the redaction-critical ones. Newbigin (*The Light Has Come*) offers the missiological reading: Jesus's authority claim is authority *for the world*, not merely intra-Jewish polemic.

The Jewish interpretive trajectory cannot be ignored on this chapter. Daniel Boyarin's *Jewish Gospels* and *Border Lines* argue that the "two powers in heaven" theology John attributes to Jesus was not yet heretical in late-Second-Temple Judaism — Boyarin documents Jewish traditions about a divine vice-regent (the "lesser YHWH" of 3 Enoch, the Son of Man of the Parables of Enoch, Metatron traditions) that made claims like Jesus's intelligible within a Jewish frame. The rabbinic crackdown on "two-powers" theology (in tractate *Hagigah*) came later, partly in response to Christian readings of texts like John 5. Adele Reinhartz, in *Cast Out of the Covenant* and *Befriending the Beloved Disciple*, attends to how Johannine "the Jews" language — already escalating in this chapter (5:10, 5:15-16, 5:18) — produced the supersessionist trajectory. Amy-Jill Levine's *The Misunderstood Jew* and Charlesworth's *John and Qumran* (situating the Father-Son theology against Qumran's "Spirit of Truth" passages in 1QS 3:13-4:26) round out the Jewish-context reading. John Ashton's *Understanding the Fourth Gospel* synthesizes much of this into a portrait of John as "Jewish apocalyptic" rather than Hellenistic philosophy.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: What does Jesus mean by "do you want to be healed?" (5:6) — and why does the man not answer the question?**

*Position 1: The pastoral reading.* Augustine, Chrysostom (in his *88 Homilies on John*), and a long devotional tradition take the question as searching the man's interior — 38 years of paralysis can produce a settled identity in which healing is feared, not desired. Jesus's question forces the man to confront his own willingness. The pastoral payoff: many of us prefer the familiar paralysis to the demanding freedom of healing. Steelman: the question's plain sense is interrogative, the man's evasive non-answer ("I have no one to put me into the pool") suggests psychological complexity, and Jesus heals despite, not because of, his answer.

*Position 2: The polemical reading.* The man is presented unsympathetically throughout the chapter — he doesn't know who healed him, he reports Jesus to the authorities (5:15), he never confesses. Brown notes this is the only Johannine healing recipient who turns adversarial. On this reading, Jesus's question exposes the man's unbelief from the start. Steelman: the narrative trajectory does sour, the contrast with the man born blind in chapter 9 (who progresses to confession) is striking, and 5:14 ("sin no more") implies moral compromise.

*Position 3: The sovereign-grace reading.* Calvin and the Reformed tradition read Jesus's question as deliberately exposing the man's helplessness — he cannot get into the pool, he has no one, he is utterly without resource. Jesus heals not because the man wants it well enough but because Jesus wills it. Steelman: the sequence reinforces this — the man does not ask for healing, does not have faith, does not even know who Jesus is. The healing is unilateral grace.

*Where this leaves us:* The three readings are not mutually exclusive. The question searches the man, the man's response reveals limited religious imagination (he can only conceive of healing through the pool's mechanism), and Jesus heals sovereignly without waiting for adequate response. What stays uncomfortable is the warning in 5:14 ("nothing worse may happen to you") — Johannine Christology generally resists tying physical illness to specific sin (cf. 9:3), so the warning here is anomalous and probably better read as eschatological warning than as causal etiology.

**Tension Two: What does "the Son can do nothing of his own accord" (5:19) say about the Trinity?**

*Position 1: Eternal subordination of order, not of essence.* The patristic-Reformed mainstream — Augustine, Cyril, Aquinas, Calvin, Bavinck, contemporary Trinitarian theology — reads 5:19 as preserving both equality of nature and order of relations. The Son receives his being eternally from the Father (eternal generation); therefore the Son acts always in dependence on the Father, but this dependence is itself the eternal mode of the Son's divine being, not a temporal subordination. Steelman: the immediate completion ("only what he sees the Father doing") and the equality claim of 5:23 ("that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father") foreclose a unitarian or Arian reading.

*Position 2: Functional/economic subordination.* A growing twentieth- and twenty-first-century tradition — including evangelical "eternal functional subordination" (Wayne Grudem, Bruce Ware) and some readings of Karl Barth — argues the Son's submission to the Father is eternal but functional, not ontological. Steelman: Jesus's statements of dependence are not bracketed temporally; they describe how Father and Son eternally relate. Counter-steelman: critics (Kevin Giles, Millard Erickson) argue this collapses into Arianism in disguise.

*Position 3: Kenotic / relational Christology.* From Bultmann's existential reading through Moltmann's *Crucified God* through LaCugna's *God for Us* runs a different impulse: the Son's "doing nothing of his own accord" is the divine self-emptying, the structure of love itself, not metaphysical hierarchy. Steelman: the gospel's love-grammar (Father loves Son, Son loves Father, both love the world) suggests the dependence is mutual indwelling rather than asymmetric subordination.

*Where this leaves us:* John 5 will not settle Trinitarian debate; it constrains it. The chapter forecloses Arianism (the Son does what the Father does, receives equal honor, has life in himself) and forecloses modalism (Father and Son are distinct, the Son is sent by the Father). It leaves room for the long-running argument between hierarchical-of-order and mutual-of-relation construals.

**One unresolved question:** If Jesus's witnesses include "the Scriptures" (5:39) — "they bear witness about me" — what is the hermeneutical weight of this for Christian reading of the Hebrew Bible after the Holocaust? Reinhartz, Boyarin, and Levine push back against the supersessionist trajectory this verse can authorize. Yet the verse is canonical and Jesus stakes his identity on it. Christians have not yet found a stable settlement between confessing Christ as the one to whom Scripture bears witness and refusing to read Israel's Scripture as merely Christian prelude. *Nostra Aetate* (1965) and the PCBC's *The Jewish People and Their Sacred Scriptures* (2001) opened the question; it remains open.

## The Thread

5:39 ("you search the Scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life") points back to the prologue's claim that "no one has ever seen God; the only God, who is at the Father's side, he has made him known" (1:18) — reading is always a derivative knowing. The Father-Son discourse foreshadows the high-priestly prayer of chapter 17, where the same vocabulary of mutual indwelling reaches its climax. The "hour is coming, and is now here" (5:25) anticipates the realized-final eschatology debate that runs through chapters 6, 11, and 14. And the witness theme — the five witnesses to Jesus — sets up the trial-narrative architecture that culminates in the Roman trial of chapters 18-19, where Pilate's "what is truth?" answers (and refuses) the question Jesus's witnesses have been raising since this chapter.
