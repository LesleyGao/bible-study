# Psalm 62 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 62 carries one of the more precise superscriptions in the Psalter: *lamnatzeach al-yedutun mizmor le-David* (לַמְנַצֵּחַ עַל־יְדוּתוּן מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד), "to the choirmaster, according to Jeduthun, a Psalm of David." Jeduthun appears in 1 Chronicles 16:41-42 and 25:1-6 as one of the three Levitical chief musicians (alongside Asaph and Heman) appointed by David for Tabernacle and later Temple service. The same superscription appears on Psalms 39 and 77, suggesting either a guild of musicians descended from Jeduthun, a particular musical mode, or a melody associated with his line. The preposition *al* (עַל) in liturgical superscriptions is notoriously ambiguous — it can mean "upon," "according to," or "for the leader of." Most modern commentators (Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate) read it as a performance instruction rather than a genre or historical marker.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 62 as a *Vertrauenspsalm* — a psalm of trust or confidence — distinct from the lament proper because the speaker has already moved through complaint into settled assurance. Sigmund Mowinckel placed such psalms in the cultic context of the individual worshipper bringing thanksgiving after deliverance, while Claus Westermann located them at the hinge between petition and praise. The genre is closely related to Pss 4, 11, 16, 23, 27, and 131, but Psalm 62's repeated structural marker — the particle *akh* (אַךְ) — gives it a distinct rhetorical signature.

That particle is the linguistic spine of the psalm. *Akh* opens verses 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, and 9 — half of the psalm's twelve verses. Hebraists (Waltke-O'Connor §39.3.5d) classify it as an emphatic-restrictive adverb: it can mean "only" (exclusive), "surely" (asseverative), or "yet/nevertheless" (adversative). The translation crux is theologically loaded. If the opening is rendered "*Only* toward God is my soul silent" (NJPS, JPS 1985), the psalm is exclusive — God alone is the legitimate object of trust. If "*Surely* toward God my soul waits in silence" (ESV, NRSV), it is asseverative — the speaker is reassuring himself. Mitchell Dahood's *Anchor Bible* commentary (1968) argued for the asseverative throughout; Hossfeld and Zenger (Hermeneia, 2005) read the opening *akh* as exclusive and the *akh* of v. 9 ("*only* a breath") as restrictive. The two readings yield different psalms: one a polemic against false objects of trust, the other a self-address moving through doubt to confidence.

The opening line — *akh el-Elohim dumiyya nafshi* (אַךְ אֶל־אֱלֹהִים דּוּמִיָּה נַפְשִׁי) — pairs the noun *dumiyya* (דּוּמִיָּה, "silence, stillness, quiet waiting") with *nafshi* (נַפְשִׁי, "my soul / my whole being / my life-breath"). *Dumiyya* is rare; the cognate verb *damam* covers everything from the silence of mourning (Lev 10:3) to the silence of the wicked cut off (1 Sam 2:9) to the still of the night. Its semantic field is closer to "stillness in expectation" than to mere absence of speech. The refuge cluster that follows — *tzur* (צוּר, "rock"), *yeshu'ati* (יְשׁוּעָתִי, "my salvation"), *misgabbi* (מִשְׂגַּבִּי, "my high tower / fortress"), *machsi* (מַחְסִי, "my refuge") — is stock vocabulary of the trust psalms but here piled in unusual density.

Psalm 62 stands in Book II of the Psalter (Pss 42-72), the *Elohistic Psalter*, where the divine name YHWH is systematically replaced by *Elohim*. The phenomenon is well-attested: where Psalm 14 (Book I) uses YHWH four times, the parallel Psalm 53 (Book II) uses Elohim. Psalm 62 uses *Elohim* seven times and *YHWH* (rendered "Lord" / *Adonai*) only once — at the very end (v. 12), where it functions as a kind of theological signature breaking through the Elohistic redaction. Critical scholars (Joel Burnett, *A Reassessment of Biblical Elohim*, 2001) debate whether the substitution reflects a Northern provenance, a post-exilic editorial layer, or a deliberate theological move toward divine transcendence. The Dead Sea Scrolls (especially 11QPsa) preserve a partial text of Psalm 62 substantially identical to the Masoretic. The Septuagint translates *dumiyya* as ὑποταγήσεται ("shall be subject"), reading the noun as a verb — an interpretive move toward submission rather than waiting that would echo through the Christian reception.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Robert Lowth's parallelism — synonymous, antithetic, synthetic — is on display throughout. The opening bicolon ("for God alone my soul waits in silence; / from him comes my salvation," v. 1) is synthetic: the second line completes the first by giving its ground. The famous v. 2 — "He alone is my rock and my salvation, / my fortress; I shall not be greatly shaken" — is climactic, building from metaphor (*tzur*, *yeshu'ah*) to assertion (*lo' emmot rabbah*, "I shall not be greatly shaken").

The strophic structure pivots on two near-identical refrains. Verses 1-2 and 5-6 form a paired confession, with the small but telling shift: in v. 2, "I shall not be *greatly* shaken" (*lo' emmot rabbah*); in v. 6, "I shall not be shaken" (*lo' emmot*). The qualifier disappears. The speaker has moved from a tempered confidence ("not greatly") to an unqualified one ("not at all"). Some translators flatten this; the Hebrew is doing real psychological work. The same shift recurs in v. 5: where v. 1 says "my soul *waits* in silence," v. 5 commands "my soul, *wait* in silence" — the indicative becomes imperative, description becomes self-address.

The *selah* markers (vv. 4 and 8) divide the psalm into three movements: (1) confession of trust + accusation against enemies (vv. 1-4); (2) renewed confession + invitation to community (vv. 5-8); (3) wisdom reflection on human and divine power (vv. 9-12). The first two movements mirror each other; the third opens outward to a general truth. This is closer to a Wisdom-trust hybrid than a pure lament — the kind of structure Erhard Gerstenberger has called "instructional psalmody."

The chiasmic and inclusio elements knit the psalm tight. The verb *batach* (בָּטַח, "trust") appears in the imperative at v. 8 ("*bitchu vo bekhol-et 'am*," "trust in him at all times, O people") and the negated jussive at v. 10 ("*al-tibtechu be-'osheq*," "put no trust in extortion"). The polarity is structural: trust *in* God versus trust *in* extortion and riches. The *akh* particle is itself an inclusio of sorts — opening the first major movement and reappearing in v. 9 ("*akh hevel beney-adam*," "only a breath are the sons of man") to disqualify the false objects of trust the psalm has been polemicizing against.

The famous numerical formula of v. 11 — *achat dibber Elohim, shtayim-zu shama'ti* (אַחַת דִּבֶּר אֱלֹהִים שְׁתַּיִם־זוּ שָׁמָעְתִּי), "once God has spoken, twice have I heard this" — is a graded numerical saying (the *x // x+1* pattern familiar from Proverbs 30 and Amos 1-2). The two things the speaker has heard are *oz* (עֹז, "power / strength") belonging to God and *chesed* (חֶסֶד, "steadfast love / covenant loyalty") belonging to God. That these two — power and covenant love — should rest in the same divine subject is the psalm's deepest theological claim. Hossfeld-Zenger calls it "the dogmatic core of the Psalter."

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

In Book II of the Psalter, Psalm 62 sits in a small cluster of trust and complaint psalms (61-64) attributed to David, immediately preceding the great hymns of Pss 65-68. Gerald Wilson's canonical reading (*The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter*, 1985) sees Books I-II as a "Davidic covenant" collection establishing the theological foundation that Books III-V will then test through exile and restoration. Psalm 62's combination of personal trust and wisdom-style universalism makes it a hinge: the individual's settled confidence becomes a teaching for the assembled people (v. 8).

**The Jewish trajectory.** *Midrash Tehillim* (Shocher Tov) on Psalm 62 amplifies the *akh* into a polemic against idolatry: "only" toward God, never toward the works of human hands. Rashi (11th c.) reads the psalm as Davidic, set against Absalom's rebellion, with the "leaning wall" (v. 3) as the king under siege. Ibn Ezra (12th c.) is more philosophical, treating the *dumiyya* as the soul's quieting of its passions — an early move toward what would become the Spanish-Jewish mystical reading. Radak (David Kimchi, 13th c.) splits the difference: Davidic in occasion, universal in application. The medieval *piyyut* tradition picked up v. 12's "to you, Lord, belongs *chesed*" as a key liturgical phrase, and the Yom Kippur *Avinu Malkenu* sequence echoes its theology of judgment-tempered-by-mercy. Martin Buber, in *Right and Wrong* (1952), read Psalm 62 as the model of "religious realism" — a faith that does not deny human treachery but locates its hope elsewhere. Abraham Joshua Heschel cited the psalm in *Man's Quest for God* (1954) as the locus classicus of Jewish silent prayer: "in spiritual life, words are like guards posted before the holy of holies; they are not the holy of holies itself." Jon Levenson and Nahum Sarna both note the psalm's striking integration of wisdom and trust genres. The psalm is recited in some traditions as part of weekday morning prayer; v. 12's pairing of *oz* and *chesed* is a recurring motif in *Selichot*.

**The Christian trajectory.** Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 62 (his numbering 61) is among his most penetrating: he reads *nonne Deo subjecta erit anima mea* — following the LXX's "shall not my soul be subject to God?" — and develops a theology of the soul's restless subjection. "My soul, be subject to God; do not seek another." Athanasius in his *Letter to Marcellinus* recommended Psalm 62 for use "when you would learn how the just man waits." Cassiodorus (6th c.) developed the threefold movement (confession, exhortation, doctrine) that became standard medieval division. Aquinas's *Postilla super Psalmos* lectured on the psalm during his Paris years and stressed the *akh* as exclusive: God alone, not the affections of the soul, is the proper resting place. Luther's *Operationes in Psalmos* (1519-21) worked Psalm 62 hard during his Reformation breakthrough — the psalm's polemic against trust in riches and power dovetailed with his attack on indulgences. Calvin's 1557 *Commentary on the Psalms* read the psalm as a model of how faith argues with itself: "the saints, even when they have laid hold of the protection of God, are still molested by various distrusts." 

The 19th-20th-century critical tradition — Gunkel, Mowinckel, Westermann, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate, Wenham — has converged on a reading that emphasizes Psalm 62's hybrid trust-wisdom genre and its rhetorical use of the *akh* particle. Walter Brueggemann (*The Message of the Psalms*, 1984) places Psalm 62 in his "psalms of new orientation" — psalms spoken not from within disorientation but from the far side of it. Patrick Miller (*Interpreting the Psalms*, 1986) emphasizes the move from individual confession to communal exhortation in v. 8 as a key feature of how the Psalter teaches. William P. Brown's work on metaphor (*Seeing the Psalms*, 2002) traces the rock-fortress-refuge cluster as a coherent imaginative world. Beth Tanner's contribution to the *NICOT* volume (2014) underscores the psalm's integration of trust and ethics: the speaker who trusts in God will also refuse to trust in extortion.

The New Testament does not directly cite Psalm 62, but Romans 2:6 — "He will render to each one according to his works" — is widely recognized as drawing on Psalm 62:12 (along with Prov 24:12). Hebrews 13:5-6 ("I will never leave you nor forsake you... so we can confidently say, 'The Lord is my helper'") echoes the trust-language of the psalm, and 2 Timothy 2:19's "the Lord knows those who are his" stands in the same tradition.

The silence-before-God reading — *dumiyya* as contemplative stillness — became a foundational text for the apophatic tradition. The *Cloud of Unknowing* (14th c. anonymous English) and John of the Cross's *Dark Night* both echo the psalm's *dumiyya* as the soul's silence before what cannot be spoken. The Quaker silent meeting and Eastern Orthodox hesychasm (the Jesus Prayer, the practice of *hesychia* / stillness) draw deeply on this verse. Reformed exegesis (Calvin, then later Geneva and Westminster traditions) has tended to read *dumiyya* as active waiting rather than contemplative emptying — a difference that maps onto deeper Catholic-Protestant disagreements about the nature of contemplation.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

Two tensions stand at the center of Psalm 62.

**Tension 1: silence as emptying or silence as expectation.** The contemplative tradition — Catholic apophatic mysticism (*Cloud of Unknowing*, John of the Cross, Thomas Merton), Eastern Orthodox hesychasm, Quaker silent worship — reads *dumiyya* as the soul's emptying, the laying aside of words and concepts so that God may fill the cleared space. The Reformed and broadly Protestant tradition reads *dumiyya* as active waiting, the silence of one who has finished arguing and now listens for the covenant word. The Jewish tradition (Heschel, Buber) often holds these together: the silence is both empty and expectant, both quieting and listening. A fourth position, found in Hossfeld-Zenger and developed by Tanner, sees *dumiyya* as the silence of one who has stopped trying to defend himself — the trust that lets the case rest with God.

The steelman of the apophatic position: the psalm itself moves from speech (the accusation against enemies) to silence (the settled confession), and the climactic v. 11 says "*once* God has spoken, *twice* have I heard" — the speaker is in receptive mode, not generative. The steelman of the active-trust reading: the psalm is full of speech — the speaker calls the people to trust (v. 8), instructs against trusting in riches (v. 10), declares what he has heard (v. 11). The silence is not a refusal of language but its right ordering. The steelman of the rest-the-case reading: in legal contexts (think Job), *dumiyya* is the silence of one who has finished his complaint — the silence of trust, not of mysticism or exhortation.

**Tension 2: the *akh* particle and exclusive trust.** If the *akh* is exclusive ("only toward God"), the psalm is a polemic against any rival object of trust — riches, power, princes, even the self. This reading aligns the psalm with the First Commandment and with the prophetic critique of idolatry. If the *akh* is asseverative ("surely toward God"), the psalm is the speaker's self-reassurance, his way of arguing himself out of doubt. If the *akh* is adversative ("yet toward God, despite everything"), the psalm is a confession spoken into the teeth of the speaker's distress. All three are grammatically defensible. The exclusive reading (Hossfeld-Zenger, Tanner) makes the psalm a teaching; the asseverative (Goldingay) makes it a meditation; the adversative (Brueggemann) makes it a defiance.

A third position worth surfacing: the *akh* may be doing all three at once. Hebrew particles are not Greek logical operators; they hold semantic ranges. The psalm may be exclusive *and* asseverative *and* adversative — a single word that simultaneously says "only," "surely," and "yet." That ambiguity is not a weakness but a feature of the genre.

**Where this leaves us:** Psalm 62 is a master class in how trust speaks. It does not pretend that enemies do not exist (vv. 3-4) or that human power is not seductive (v. 10) or that the speaker's own soul does not need to be commanded (v. 5). It does not collapse silence into mysticism or trust into resignation. It pairs *oz* (power) and *chesed* (steadfast love) in the same divine subject — and refuses to choose between them. The pastoral and theological force of the psalm is that it lets the speaker (and us) hold contradiction without resolution: enemies real, riches seductive, the soul argumentative, God alone trustworthy. The repetition is not redundancy but the work of trust being done in real time.

**Closing question:** When the *akh* of Psalm 62 disqualifies every rival object of trust — princes, riches, power, even the speaker's own striving — what is left for the soul actually to do, and is "wait in silence" enough to live on?

## The Thread

- Psalm 39 — the other Jeduthun psalm of silence, where *dumiyya* is the silence of submission to suffering
- Psalm 73 — the wisdom-trust hybrid that, like Psalm 62, contrasts the prosperity of the wicked with the durability of God
- Romans 2:6 — Paul's appropriation of Psalm 62:12's "render to each according to his works"
- Hebrews 13:5-6 — the New Testament echo of the rock-refuge confession in Christian assurance
