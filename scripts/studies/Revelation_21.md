# Revelation 21: The Garden Becomes a City

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Authorship and dating.** John of Patmos, almost certainly not the apostle John of the Fourth Gospel — a Jewish-Christian prophet writing self-consciously Semitic-flavored Greek, exiled (1:9) on a small Aegean island. The mainstream dating, anchored by Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 5.30.3, c. AD 180), places the vision toward the end of Domitian's reign, around AD 95 — the consensus across Aune's WBC, Bauckham, Beale, and Koester. A respectable minority (J.A.T. Robinson in *Redating the New Testament*, Kenneth Gentry in *Before Jerusalem Fell*) argues for a Neronian date in the late 60s. The Domitianic reading does not require a systematic empire-wide persecution; it requires only the local pressure of imperial-cult enforcement in the Asian cities Revelation addresses.

**The chapter as canonical *telos*.** Whichever date one accepts, Revelation 21 functions as the canonical hinge. Genesis 1's "in the beginning" finds its symmetrical echo here in 21:5 — *idou kaina poiō panta* (ἰδοὺ καινὰ ποιῶ πάντα), "Behold, I am making all things new." The verb is *kainos* (καινός), connoting qualitative novelty, transfigured kind, not merely chronologically subsequent (*neos*, νέος). The choice is deliberate and theologically loaded: this is not a replacement model that throws out the old like obsolete inventory but a renewal model that makes the old kind new.

**"The first heaven and the first earth had passed away" (21:1)** is the single most contested phrase in the chapter for systematic theology. The annihilationist reading — adopted in some dispensational expositors (John Walvoord, Tim LaHaye), in some older Lutheran dogmatics (the early Helmut Thielicke leaned this way before softening), and not without warrant in 2 Peter 3:10's "the elements will be dissolved with fire" — reads this as cosmic replacement: the old creation is destroyed, a new creation is made *ex nihilo*. The renewal-and-transformation reading — now the prevailing modern view across Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and most Reformed and Anglican exegetes — reads the passing-away as the passing-away of corruption, decay, sin, and death, not of created matter itself. N.T. Wright's *Surprised by Hope* is the most influential popular articulation; Bauckham's *Theology of Revelation* and Beale (more cautiously) come down here too. The Catholic Catechism §1047 explicitly calls "the visible universe... destined to be transformed." The controlling Pauline parallel is Romans 8:18-23, where the *whole creation* (πᾶσα ἡ κτίσις) groans not for annihilation but for liberation from *douleia tēs phthoras* (δουλεία τῆς φθορᾶς), the slavery of decay. The Eastern Orthodox tradition, drawing on Maximus the Confessor and refracted through Vladimir Lossky and Sergei Bulgakov, frames this as cosmic *theōsis*: matter itself is to be transfigured, not discarded — Mt. Tabor's uncreated light (the Transfiguration) extended to the entire cosmos.

**"And the sea was no more" (21:1).** Not a marine zoology claim. The sea in the Hebrew Bible is the residual chaos of pre-creation — *tehom* (תְּהוֹם) in Genesis 1:2, the Leviathan-and-Rahab waters of Job 41 and Psalm 74:13-14, the source of the four beasts in Daniel 7:2-3. Its abolition signals the final eradication of chaos. (The book's own beast in 13:1 rises from the sea.) Bauckham reads this as the symbolic completion of creation: the work begun in Genesis 1, where God *separates* but does not yet *eliminate* the chaotic waters, is here brought to completion.

**The descent of the new Jerusalem (21:2, 21:10).** *Katabainousan ek tou ouranou apo tou theou* (καταβαίνουσαν ἐκ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ ἀπὸ τοῦ θεοῦ) — coming down out of heaven from God. The directionality is theologically decisive and is what Bauckham, Beale, and Wright stress most emphatically. The Christian eschaton is not the soul's escape upward from earth into heaven — that is Platonism, not the Apocalypse — but God's descent into a renewed earth. The bridal imagery (πόλιν τὴν ἁγίαν Ἰερουσαλὴμ καινὴν... ὡς νύμφην, "the holy city, new Jerusalem... as a bride") extends chapter 19's wedding of the Lamb. Heaven and earth are not collapsed into one, exactly; they are joined.

**"The dwelling place (*skēnē*, σκηνή) of God is with man" (21:3)** is the chapter's covenantal core. *Skēnē* is the tabernacle — the wilderness tent of Exodus 25-40 where the Shekinah glory dwelt. The verbal echo is precise to Leviticus 26:11-12 ("I will make my dwelling among you... I will walk among you and will be your God, and you shall be my people"), Ezekiel 37:27, and Zechariah 2:10-11. The entire tabernacle-temple-Shekinah trajectory of the Hebrew Bible — the slow approach of God to dwell with humanity, repeatedly delayed by sin and exile — culminates here.

**The cube (21:16).** The city measures 12,000 stadia in length, breadth, and height — a perfect cube of roughly 1,500 miles per side, larger than the Roman Mediterranean. The single Old Testament structure that was a perfect cube was the Holy of Holies in Solomon's temple: 20 cubits by 20 cubits by 20 cubits (1 Kings 6:20). This is the chapter's most dazzling architectural pun. The new Jerusalem is not a city with a Holy of Holies; it is a Holy of Holies expanded to civic scale. The entire city is *sanctum sanctorum*. The wall of 144 cubits (12², roughly 216 feet) is comically out of proportion to a 1,500-mile-tall city — the dimensions are theologically symbolic, not architecturally functional. Twelve gates with the twelve tribes (21:12), twelve foundations with the twelve apostles (21:14): Israel and the church, Old and New Covenants, fused into one structure. The twelve foundation stones (21:19-20) recall the twelve stones of the high priest's breastpiece in Exodus 28:17-21, on which the names of the tribes were engraved. The whole city is priestly.

**"I saw no temple in the city" (21:22)** is the Apocalypse's most radical break with Ezekiel 40-48. Ezekiel's eschatological vision had measured the new temple stone by stone. John has just measured the new Jerusalem stone by stone — and pointedly omitted the temple. *Naos* (ναός), the inner sanctuary itself, has been replaced: *ho gar kyrios ho theos ho pantokratōr naos autēs estin, kai to arnion* — the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb *are* its temple. The Eastern Orthodox and Catholic theological tradition reads this not as anti-sacramental but as sacramental-cosmic culmination: the sacramental mediation that the church now practices points forward to a state in which the mediation is no longer required because the mediated reality is fully present.

**The nations and the kings (21:24-26).** *Peripatēsousin ta ethnē dia tou phōtos autēs* — "the nations will walk by its light" — and the kings of the earth bring their *doxa* (δόξα, glory) into it. Bauckham (in his "Conversion of the Nations" chapter in *Theology of Revelation*) calls this the most surprising note in the book: after twenty chapters of judgment, the new Jerusalem somehow contains nations and kings beyond the resurrected saints. The full universalist reading (Origen distantly, David Bentley Hart and Robin Parry contemporarily) takes 21:24-26 as evidence that the lake-of-fire judgments of chapter 20 are penultimate, not final. The restrictivist reading (Beale, Robert Mounce) reads "nations" as the saved among the nations — the redeemed taken from every tribe and tongue (5:9, 7:9). Bauckham's mediating "conversion of the nations" reading takes the survivors of the judgment chapters as those who, like the Ninevites at Jonah's preaching, repent and are gathered in.

**Key Greek terms across the chapter.** *Kainos* (καινός, qualitative new — 21:1, 21:2, 21:5); *skēnē* (σκηνή, tabernacle — 21:3); *naos* (ναός, sanctuary — 21:22, *absent*); *ethnē* (ἔθνη, nations — 21:24, 26); *prosōpon* (πρόσωπον, face — anticipated, fulfilled in 22:4).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

**Genre.** The chapter is sustained apocalyptic *ekphrasis* — visionary description on the Greco-Roman model of Homeric shield-of-Achilles or Virgilian temple-description, transposed into prophetic key. But its deepest formal ancestor is not Greek; it is the Isaianic and Ezekielian visionary inheritance.

**The Old Testament intertext.** Bauckham's *The Climax of Prophecy* makes the case at exhaustive length that Revelation does not merely allude to the Hebrew prophets; it *recombines* them. Chapter 21 is the sustained Christian-apocalyptic reworking of three texts in particular:

- Isaiah 65:17-25 — the most direct verbal source. "Behold, I create new heavens and a new earth... no more shall be heard the sound of weeping and the cry of distress." The *kainos ouranos kai kainē gē* of 21:1 is a direct quotation.
- Ezekiel 40-48 — the structural source. The angelic guide with the measuring rod (40:3, echoed in 21:15), the measured city, the river from the temple (Ezek 47:1-12, deferred to Rev 22:1-2), the gates with tribal names (Ezek 48:30-34, echoed in 21:12).
- Isaiah 60 — the source for the nations and kings bringing tribute. "Nations shall come to your light, and kings to the brightness of your rising... they shall bring gold and frankincense" (Isa 60:3, 6) is reworked at 21:24-26.

**The chiastic shape of the canon.** The single most important literary observation about Revelation 21 is that it closes a chiasm that runs the length of the Christian Bible. Genesis 1-3 opens with creation, Eden, river, tree of life, *imago Dei*, presence of God. The narrative falls into curse, exile, covenant, prophets, exile-and-return, and finally Christ. Revelation 21-22 reverses every move: new creation, new Jerusalem, river (22:1), tree of life (22:2), unmediated presence (22:4), no curse (22:3). This is not coincidental ornament; it is the editorial-canonical signature of Revelation's place at the end of the Christian scriptures.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Patristic.** Irenaeus, in *Against Heresies* 5.32-36, mounts the earliest sustained defense of the new earth's continuity with present creation against Marcionite and Gnostic dualism. The fleshly resurrection and the renewed bodily existence are, for Irenaeus, the whole point: the same God who made matter redeems matter. Augustine in *City of God* XX-XXII reads the new Jerusalem allegorically as the heavenly *civitas Dei* breaking into history through the church and consummated in the eternal *visio Dei* of Book XXII. Origen's *De Principiis* 3.6 develops his famous *apokatastasis* — universal restoration — which finds tentative warrant in passages like 21:24-26, though his hypothesis was condemned (in a controversial reading of the relevant anathemas) at the Second Council of Constantinople in 553.

**Medieval.** Aquinas treats the chapter at length in *Summa* III, Suppl. q. 91-99 and across the *Compendium* 168-184: the resurrection body, the new heavens and new earth, the qualities of the glorified state. Dante's *Paradiso* draws on Revelation 21-22 throughout — the celestial rose of cantos 30-32 is the new Jerusalem reimagined. Bonaventure and Hildegard of Bingen develop the *visio Dei* in mystical-contemplative key; Catherine of Siena's *Dialogue* takes it into the affective register.

**Reformation.** Luther holds the bodily resurrection and new creation but cools the speculative temperature relative to the medievals. Calvin's *Institutes* III.25 affirms the heavenly inheritance with deliberate reserve about speculative detail, warning against the "labyrinth" of unanswerable curiosity. The Anabaptist trajectory — Hubmaier, Pilgram Marpeck — pushes the new Jerusalem into the church's anticipatory communal life now: the eschaton inaugurated, partially, in the gathered congregation.

**Modern critical.** Bauckham's *The Theology of the Book of Revelation* and *The Climax of Prophecy* are the indispensable scholarly anchors; his "conversion of the nations" reading has become almost the default among critical evangelicals. N.T. Wright's *Surprised by Hope* is the most influential popular reorientation of evangelical eschatology back toward bodily resurrection in renewed creation. Adela Yarbro Collins, Christopher Rowland, Brian Blount, Wilfrid Harrington, George Caird, William Hendriksen, and Eugene Boring across various commentary traditions; Jürgen Moltmann's *The Coming of God* gives the most expansive systematic-theological treatment. Catherine Keller's *Apocalypse Now and Then* offers a feminist-ecological re-reading.

**Contemporary Catholic and Orthodox.** *Lumen Gentium* 48-51 and *Gaudium et Spes* 39 frame the chapter's eschatology as continuity-and-renewal of creation. CCC §§1042-1050 collects the doctrine. Pope John Paul II in *Dominum et Vivificantem* and Pope Benedict XVI in *Spe Salvi* both read 21:1-5 as the cosmic horizon of Christian hope. Eastern Orthodox: Vladimir Lossky's *Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church* and Sergei Bulgakov's *The Bride of the Lamb* read Revelation 21 as the cosmic culmination of *theōsis*; Gregory Palamas's essence-energies distinction undergirds the Orthodox account of how creatures can participate in the divine life without metaphysical absorption.

**Four interpretive schools.** *Preterist:* the new Jerusalem is symbolic of the post-AD-70 church / new covenant community in present existence (David Chilton, Kenneth Gentry — "this is what we live in now"). *Historicist:* the new Jerusalem is a future actual culmination, with present church anticipating it across history. *Futurist (dispensational):* the new Jerusalem is a literal future descended city, sometimes located in the millennial reign (Walvoord), sometimes only post-millennial; the dimensions are debated as literal or symbolic. *Idealist:* the new Jerusalem is eschatological reality breaking into the present church, anticipated and consummated.

**Jewish scholarly engagement.** The proximate Jewish sources — Isaiah 65-66, Ezekiel 40-48, Zechariah 14 — are also the proximate sources of the rabbinic tradition's *Olam Ha-Ba* (the world to come; *Mishnah Sanhedrin* 10:1ff, *Pirke Avot* 4:21), the renewed *gan eden* (*Bereshit Rabbah* 21:9, *Pesikta Rabbati* 41), and the messianic banquet on Leviathan (*B. Bava Batra* 75a). Daniel Boyarin in *Border Lines* and *The Jewish Gospels* has argued that the new-Jerusalem and messianic-age framework is shared Jewish-Christian eschatological grammar; the differences (Lamb-as-temple, no third temple) are sharp but the framework is shared. The single sharpest divergence is at 21:22: Christian eschatology has no third temple. This is the chapter's most direct disagreement with contemporary Jewish eschatological hope, both ancient and modern. Jon Levenson and John Goldingay, in different idioms, model how Christian-Jewish dialogue can honor both the shared rootage and the genuine divergence without flattening either side. Sarah Coakley's work on theological method provides one Christian frame for that dialogue.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What God is doing in Revelation 21 is consummating the work begun in Genesis 1. The Garden — wild, unbuilt, cultivable — becomes a Garden-City: cultivated, civilized, urbanized into the new Jerusalem, but a city in which the river and tree of life still run through the streets. The Holy of Holies, restricted in the wilderness tabernacle to one priest one day a year behind a curtain, expands into a 12,000-stadia cube where everyone lives in the unmediated presence of God. The bride is married to the Lamb. The covenant formula spoken to Abraham, repeated to Moses, repeated to David, repeated to the prophets — *I will be their God and they shall be my people* — is finally, completely, consummated.

### Tension 1: New Creation — Annihilation or Renewal?

**The annihilationist new-creation reading.** Some dispensational expositors (Walvoord) and some older Lutheran dogmaticians read 21:1 as cosmic replacement. The first heaven and first earth are *destroyed* — 2 Peter 3:10's dissolution of the elements — and the new is created *ex nihilo*. Steelmanned: this reading takes seriously the depth of the Fall. If the corruption goes all the way down — if every quark and electron of the present cosmos is in some sense fallen — then redemption may require not transformation of *this* matter but the bringing-into-being of a wholly new substrate. There is also a Pauline echo: "if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation; the old has passed away" (2 Cor 5:17) uses the same *parēlthen* verb as 21:4. Helmut Thielicke's earlier work flirted with this reading.

**The renewal-and-transformation reading.** Catholic teaching (CCC §1047), Eastern Orthodox theology, Reformed mainstream (the *Heidelberg Catechism* Q57-58 reads the bodily resurrection as continuity of *this* body), and the now-dominant Anglican-evangelical reading articulated by N.T. Wright. The key verb in 21:5 is *kainos* not *neos* — qualitative not chronological newness. Romans 8:18-23 is the controlling parallel: the creation groans for *liberation*, not for *destruction*. Bauckham, Moltmann, and Wright converge here.

**The spiritualist / Gnostic-leaning reading** (a non-Christian foil worth naming). The present material world has no eschatological future; salvation is the soul's escape. This was the Marcionite and Valentinian view Irenaeus opposed in *Against Heresies* 5. It is the view N.T. Wright argues, polemically, much of contemporary evangelicalism has *de facto* slipped into when it speaks of "going to heaven when we die" as the final state.

The stakes are not academic. They run through ecological theology, Christology, and the spirituality of bodily life. *Where this leaves us:* the renewal reading has the better exegetical and patristic warrant, but the annihilationist reading should not be caricatured — it is trying to honor the depth of cosmic corruption.

### Tension 2: The Nations and the Kings (21:24-26)

**The universalist reading.** Origen in seed form; David Bentley Hart's *That All Shall Be Saved* and Robin Parry's *The Evangelical Universalist* in contemporary form. Revelation 21:24-26 says the nations walk by the city's light and the kings bring their glory in — *after* chapter 20's lake of fire. The natural reading, on this view, is that the lake of fire is purgative-penultimate, not final-eternal; the kings of the earth who fought against the Lamb in 19:19 end up in 21:24 bringing their glory in. Steelmanned: this reading honors the consistent New Testament testimony to the breadth of redemption (Col 1:20's "reconciling all things"; 1 Tim 2:4's "God desires all to be saved"; Rom 11:32's "mercy upon all"), and it reads Revelation as *internally* coherent rather than as two contradictory pictures stapled together. Hart's case in particular is rigorous and theologically serious; it deserves to be engaged, not dismissed.

**The restrictivist reading.** Beale and Mounce read "nations" in 21:24 as the redeemed-from-among-the-nations of 5:9 and 7:9 — "every tribe and language and people and nation." The kings are the redeemed kings. The lake of fire of 20:14-15 is final. The Christian universalist tradition is ruled out by Revelation 14:9-11 and 20:10-15.

**The inclusivist mediating reading.** Catholic teaching at *Lumen Gentium* 16 and CCC §847-848: those who through no fault of their own do not know Christ but seek God may yet be saved. Bauckham's "conversion of the nations" reading: 21:24-26's nations and kings are those who, surviving the judgment chapters, have repented in response to the witness of the Lamb's church (the two witnesses of chapter 11). This reading takes seriously both the warning passages and the universal-scope passages without collapsing one into the other.

The historic Christian default has been Eternal Conscious Torment, but it should be acknowledged that the New Testament evidence is more ambiguous than that default suggests. Annihilationism (the lake of fire as final destruction, not eternal torment — Edward Fudge, John Stott in his later thought) and universalism are both serious Christian positions with patristic warrant. *Where this leaves us:* Revelation 21 is internally pulled toward universalism in 21:24-26 and toward restrictivism in 21:8 and 21:27 ("nothing unclean shall enter it"). The book does not resolve the tension; the careful reader should not pretend it does either.

**Closing question.** If the new Jerusalem has gates that are never shut (21:25), and outside those gates (22:15) are the dogs and sorcerers and idolaters — then who, exactly, is doing the keeping-out? And what would it mean for someone outside an open gate to enter, and for someone inside to walk out?

## The Thread

- **Genesis 1:1** — "In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth." The great inclusio that 21:1 closes: "I saw a new heaven and a new earth."
- **Isaiah 65:17-25** — the new-creation prophetic source. Read in full alongside Revelation 21; the verbal dependence is direct.
- **Ezekiel 40-48** — the measured-city prophetic source. The angelic guide, the measuring rod, the gates with tribal names. Note where John follows Ezekiel and where he pointedly diverges (no temple).
- **Romans 8:18-23** — Paul's parallel cosmic-redemption hope. The whole creation groaning for liberation from decay is the Pauline correlate of the renewal reading of Revelation 21.
