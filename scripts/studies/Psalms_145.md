# Psalm 145 — Scholarly Analysis

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Psalm 145 is the only psalm in the entire Psalter that bears the superscription *Tehillah le-David* (תְּהִלָּה לְדָוִד), "A Praise. Of David." This is a remarkable label. The Hebrew word *tehillah* — "praise" — is the singular form from which the rabbis derived the Hebrew name of the entire book of Psalms: *Sefer Tehillim* (סֵפֶר תְּהִלִּים), "The Book of Praises." The plural *tehillim* takes its name from this single superscription. Psalm 145 is, in a real sense, the namesake of the Psalter. Hermann Gunkel classified it as a **hymn** (*Hymnus*) of pure praise, with no lament, petition, or imprecation — a form-critical pure type. Westermann placed it among the *descriptive psalms of praise* that name God's character and works as such, distinct from *declarative* praise that recounts a specific deliverance. Mowinckel saw it as a temple hymn. Most modern critical scholars (Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, deClaisse-Walford) date it late — Persian or early Hellenistic — based on its language, its acrostic form, and its placement at the hinge between the final Davidic cluster and the closing Hallel.

The most important formal feature is the **alphabetic acrostic**. Each verse begins with a successive letter of the Hebrew alphabet — *aleph* (v. 1, *aromimcha*), *bet* (v. 2, *be-khol yom*), *gimel* (v. 3, *gadol Yahweh*), *dalet* (v. 4, *dor le-dor*), and so on through *tav* (v. 21, *tehillat Yahweh*). The acrostic is not merely a mnemonic device. It is a theological claim: praise of Yahweh extends from *aleph* to *tav* — from A to Z — and embraces all of language. The form itself enacts totality.

But the MT acrostic is missing one letter: **the *nun* line is absent**. Between *mem* (v. 13) and *samekh* (v. 14) the MT skips the *nun*. This has been one of the most debated text-critical questions in the Psalter. The Great Psalms Scroll from Qumran (11Q5/11QPs-a) supplies a *nun* line: *ne'eman Elohim bi-dvarav ve-chasid be-khol ma'asav* (נֶאֱמָן אֱלֹהִים בִּדְבָרָיו וְחָסִיד בְּכָל מַעֲשָׂיו), "God is faithful in his words and kind in all his works." The Septuagint reads: *pistos kyrios en tois logois autou kai hosios en pasi tois ergois autou* — "the Lord is faithful in his words and holy in all his works." The Syriac Peshitta also has the *nun* line. The convergence of 11Q5 + LXX + Peshitta against the MT has persuaded mainstream-critical commentators (Goldingay, Hossfeld-Zenger, Kraus) and modern translations (NRSV, ESV in brackets, NIV) that the *nun* line was original and the MT lost it through scribal accident — a *parablepsis* (eye-skip) caused by the similar shape of *nun* and the surrounding letters or by the parallel of v. 17 ("the Lord is righteous in all his ways and kind in all his works"). The Talmud (b. *Berakhot* 4b) was already aware of the missing *nun* and offered a homiletical explanation: the *nun* was suppressed because it would have begun a verse about the *fall* (*nofelet*) of Israel, and the next verse (v. 14) reverses it — "the Lord upholds (*somekh*) all who are falling (*ha-nofelim*)." This is the rabbinic theological reading: the absence is intentional, the upholding-verse comes immediately to negate any falling-verse. The text-critical and the rabbinic readings stand in productive tension.

The Hebrew theological vocabulary is dense. *Aromimcha* (אֲרוֹמִמְךָ, "I will exalt you," v. 1) is the rare *polel* form of *rum*, used almost exclusively in praise contexts. *Gadol Yahweh u-mehullal me'od* (גָּדוֹל יְהוָה וּמְהֻלָּל מְאֹד, "great is the Lord and greatly to be praised," v. 3) echoes Psalm 48:1 and 96:4 — a hymnic refrain. *Dor le-dor* (דּוֹר לְדוֹר, "generation to generation," v. 4) is the covenantal-transmission formula. The most theologically loaded verse is 145:8: *channun ve-rachum Yahweh erekh apayim u-gdol-chased* (חַנּוּן וְרַחוּם יְהוָה אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם וּגְדָל־חָסֶד), "Yahweh is gracious and merciful, slow to anger and great in steadfast love." This is the **divine-name formula** of Exodus 34:6, the self-revelation of God to Moses on Sinai, here repeated as the heart of Psalm 145. The psalm is, in a way, a meditation on Exodus 34:6.

The DSS evidence is significant beyond the *nun* line. 11Q5 contains Psalm 145 with a refrain — *barukh Yahweh u-varukh shemo le-olam va'ed* (blessed be Yahweh and blessed be his name forever) — inserted after each verse. This is liturgical-responsorial form, evidence that Psalm 145 was already being used as a congregational antiphon at Qumran in the second century BC. The LXX, as usual, numbers it as Psalm 144.

## Lens 2: The Literature Itself

Lowth's parallelism is on full display. Synonymous parallelism saturates the psalm: "I will extol you, my God and King / and bless your name forever and ever" (v. 1). "Great is the Lord and greatly to be praised / and his greatness is unsearchable" (v. 3). The repetition of *l'olam va'ed* (לְעוֹלָם וָעֶד, "forever and ever") in vv. 1, 2, 21 forms an **inclusio** that frames the whole psalm with eternity. The *kol* (כֹּל, "all") that pervades the psalm — "the Lord is good to *all*" (v. 9), "*all* your works" (v. 10), "*all* his ways" (v. 17), "*all* who call on him" (v. 18), "*all* who love him" (v. 20), "*all* flesh" (v. 21) — appears seventeen times in twenty-one verses, more than any other single word. The acrostic structure is matched by a *kol*-saturation that makes universality the thematic heart of the psalm.

The strophic structure (following Hossfeld-Zenger and Goldingay) has been analyzed several ways. One common division:
1. **Vv. 1-3:** Opening declaration — "I will extol."
2. **Vv. 4-7:** Generational transmission — "one generation to another."
3. **Vv. 8-9:** Central confession — the Exodus 34:6 formula extended by *good to all*.
4. **Vv. 10-13:** Kingdom declaration — "your kingdom is everlasting."
5. **Vv. 14-20:** God's care for the falling, the hungry, the calling, the loving.
6. **V. 21:** Closing — "let all flesh bless his holy name."

The central confession in vv. 8-9 is the literary-theological hinge. The Exodus 34:6 formula — *channun ve-rachum* — is followed in v. 9 by an expansion that goes beyond Exodus: *tov-Yahweh la-kol ve-rachamav al kol-ma'asav* (טוֹב־יְהוָה לַכֹּל וְרַחֲמָיו עַל־כָּל־מַעֲשָׂיו), "the Lord is good to *all*, and his mercies are over *all* his works." The *all* here is creation-wide, not covenant-restricted. Verse 17 returns to the same theme: *tzaddiq Yahweh be-khol-derakhav ve-chasid be-khol-ma'asav* — "the Lord is righteous in all his ways and kind in all his works." Verses 9 and 17 frame the second half of the psalm with the same *kol-ma'asav* (all his works) refrain.

The acrostic and the *kol*-saturation work together. The form says: praise extends from *aleph* to *tav*. The vocabulary says: God's goodness extends to *all*. The form and the vocabulary are mutually reinforcing — totality of language matched by totality of mercy.

The psalm has no lament, no enemy-imprecation, no petition. It is pure descriptive praise. The only shadow is v. 20b: "but all the wicked he will destroy" — and even that is held within the *all* and the *love him* of the same verse. The literary effect is a praise that has passed through and left behind the lament-protest-petition movements that dominate the rest of the Psalter.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Psalm 145 closes the final Davidic cluster (Pss 138-145) and stands at the hinge before the closing Hallel (Pss 146-150). Gerald H. Wilson's canonical-shape thesis treats this position as redactionally deliberate: the Davidic voice gives its last hymn — pure praise, alphabetic, generational — and then yields to the anonymous, all-creation Hallel. J. Clinton McCann calls Psalm 145 "the climax of the praise of God in the Psalter as a whole." James L. Mays reads it as the theological summary of the Psalter's view of Yahweh's kingship.

**Jewish trajectory.** Psalm 145 is **the Ashrei**, the most-recited psalm in synagogue liturgy. The *Ashrei* prayer is Psalm 145 prefixed with two verses (Ps 84:5 and Ps 144:15, both beginning with *ashrei*) and followed by Ps 115:18. It is recited **three times every day**: twice in the morning service (*Shacharit*) and once in the afternoon service (*Mincha*). The Talmud, *Berakhot* 4b, makes the famous claim: "Rabbi Eleazar said in the name of Rabbi Avina: Whoever recites *Tehillah le-David* (Ps 145) three times a day is assured of being a son of the world to come (*olam ha-ba*)." The Talmud gives two reasons: first, the alphabetic acrostic — praise from *aleph* to *tav*; second, the verse "you open your hand and satisfy the desire of every living thing" (v. 16), which captures God's daily providence. Together — totality of language and totality of providence — make the psalm uniquely fit for daily prayer.

The Talmud explicitly addresses the missing *nun*. Rav Nachman bar Yitzchak (b. *Berakhot* 4b) explains: "Because it contains an allusion to Israel's downfall, as it is written 'Fallen, never to rise again, is the virgin Israel' (Amos 5:2). The *nun*-verse was suppressed; immediately after, David reverses it — *somekh Yahweh le-khol ha-nofelim*, the Lord upholds all the falling (v. 14)." This rabbinic reading turns the missing *nun* into a theological feature: God's upholding-grace immediately answers any falling-verse before it can be uttered.

**Midrash Tehillim** on Ps 145 is one of the longer and richer expositions in the collection, with extensive material on *one generation to another* and on the *all* of God's mercy. **Rashi** glosses each verse plainly and emphasizes the universal scope of v. 9 — "good to *all*, even to the wicked, even to the gentiles." **Ibn Ezra** notes the acrostic and discusses the missing *nun*, treating it as an early loss. **Radak** is again the most expansive: he reads the psalm as a hymn for the messianic age, when God's kingdom will be *everlasting* (v. 13) in the fullest sense, and when *all flesh* (v. 21) will praise. **Nachmanides** emphasizes the link to Exodus 34:6 and reads the psalm as the song of the redeemed.

Modern Jewish thinkers extend the trajectory. **Buber** read v. 18 — "the Lord is near to all who call on him, to all who call on him in truth" — as the I-Thou nearness that authorizes prayer. **Heschel** treated the *channun ve-rachum* of v. 8 as the heart of biblical theology. **Levenson** emphasizes that the universalist *good to all* of v. 9 is not a denial of Israel's particular election but the cosmic frame within which election makes sense. **Sarna** notes the psalm's role in shaping the *Ashrei* prayer and the rabbinic confidence that praise is the surest path to *olam ha-ba*. **Zornberg** reads the missing *nun* and the *somekh ha-nofelim* (upholding the falling) of v. 14 as the structural grace of the psalm — the suppressed letter answered by the upholding hand.

**Christian trajectory.** **Augustine**'s *Enarratio* on Psalm 145 is one of his most extended psalm-treatments. He reads the alphabetic acrostic as the totality of the Word — Christ, who is *aleph* and *tav*, *alpha* and *omega*. He reads v. 9 — *good to all* — as the universal scope of grace, with the Donatist-anti-rigorist polemical edge that God's mercy reaches further than any human boundary. **Cassiodorus** follows Augustine and emphasizes the kingdom-language of v. 13. **Aquinas** treats the psalm as the song of the praising soul. **Luther**, in his *Operationes in Psalmos*, reads v. 8 (*channun ve-rachum*) as the heart of the gospel — the God who is gracious and slow to anger is the God of the cross. **Calvin**'s 1557 commentary is sober and pastoral: he reads v. 16 (*you open your hand*) as the doctrine of providence and v. 18 (*near to all who call*) as the doctrine of prayer.

Modern critical scholars converge. **Gunkel** treats it as a hymn-form pure type. **Mowinckel** placed it in the temple cult. **Westermann**'s descriptive-praise category fits exactly. **Kraus** reads it as a meditation on Yahweh's kingship. **Hossfeld-Zenger** emphasize the universalist *kol-ma'asav* theology and the post-monarchic recasting of kingship-language. **Goldingay** writes that Psalm 145 is the Psalter's most complete answer to the question "what is praise?" **Brueggemann** treats it as descriptive doxology that "answers idolatry" by naming God's true character. **Patrick D. Miller** reads it as the prayer that summarizes the Psalter's theology of God. **William P. Brown** notes the cosmic scope. **Beth Tanner** emphasizes the generational-transmission language. **Mitchell**'s eschatological reading treats it as the hymn of the messianic age.

**NT use.** Psalm 145 is not directly quoted in the NT, but its language saturates Christian liturgy. The "your kingdom come, your will be done" of the Lord's Prayer echoes the kingdom-language of Ps 145:11-13. The Magnificat (Luke 1:46-55) shares vocabulary with Ps 145 (the lifting of the lowly, the satisfying of the hungry). The doxology of 1 Tim 1:17 echoes Ps 145:13.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: The missing *nun* and textual criticism.** Was the *nun* line original? Three positions.

*Position A: The *nun* was original; MT lost it.* Mainstream-critical view (Goldingay, Hossfeld-Zenger, Kraus, NRSV/ESV/NIV translation committees). The convergence of 11Q5 + LXX + Peshitta is decisive. The acrostic form requires it. The MT scribe's eye skipped from *mem* (v. 13) to *samekh* (v. 14), or harmonized v. 13b with v. 17 and lost a line. The *nun* line — "the Lord is faithful in all his words and kind in all his works" — fits the rhythm and vocabulary of the psalm and parallels v. 17.

*Position B: The MT preserves the original; the *nun*-line is a secondary harmonization.* A minority of scholars (Yehuda Kil, some MT-priority commentators) argue that 11Q5 was a liturgical anthology that supplied a *nun* line for completeness, and that the LXX may have followed a similar harmonizing tradition. On this reading the missing *nun* is original — perhaps the psalmist deliberately omitted it.

*Position C: The Talmudic theological reading.* The missing *nun* is intentional and meaningful: it points to Israel's unfallable status. The *nun*-verse (Amos's *nofelet* — "fallen, never to rise") is precisely what the next verse cancels — "the Lord upholds all who are falling" (v. 14, *somekh*). On this reading the textual lacuna is theological: God's upholding-grace cancels the falling-verse before it can be uttered.

**Steelman of Position C.** Even if Position A is text-critically correct, Position C is theologically generative. The Talmudic tradition reads the gap not as defect but as sign. Whether or not the *nun* was original, the rabbinic reading turns the missing letter into a meditation on grace: where there should have been a verse about Israel's fall, there is silence; and the silence is filled by the next verse's upholding hand. This is a way of reading Scripture in which form and absence both signify.

**Tension 2: Universalism — the *all* and the *kol-ma'asav* theology.** Verse 9 says *the Lord is good to all, and his mercies are over all his works*. Verse 17 repeats: *the Lord is righteous in all his ways and kind in all his works*. But verse 20 says *all the wicked he will destroy*. How do these *alls* relate?

*Position A: Universalist reading.* Levenson, Brueggemann, Goldingay: the *good to all* of v. 9 is the cosmic-creation frame; the *destroys the wicked* of v. 20 is the eschatological-judgment moment within that frame. The two are not contradictory: God's mercy precedes judgment and shapes its purpose. The psalm's overwhelming weight is on the universal mercy, not on the destruction.

*Position B: Particularist-covenantal reading.* A more covenantal reading (some Reformed commentators, Luther in places) emphasizes that the *all who love him* of v. 20a is the proper object of preservation, and the destruction of the wicked is the necessary obverse. On this reading the universalism of v. 9 is real but not unconditional — it is universal mercy with covenantal shape.

*Position C: Liturgical-doxological reading.* The synagogue tradition (and Augustine in places) treats the psalm as praise rather than systematic theology. The *all* of v. 9 and the destruction of v. 20 are both held within the praise-frame; the worshipper does not resolve them but offers them.

**Tension 3: Christian under-recognition.** Psalm 145 is the daily prayer of synagogue Israel — three times a day, every day, for two thousand years. It has no equivalent place in most Christian liturgical traditions. The Catholic Liturgy of the Hours assigns it weekly; Anglican and Reformed lectionaries cycle it less frequently. Is this an asymmetry that Christians should address? **Marc Zvi Brettler** and others have noted that Christian theology has tended to read the Psalter through Pss 22 and 51 (lament and confession) and to give less attention to descriptive praise. **William P. Brown** has called for Christians to take the *Ashrei* tradition seriously — to learn from the Jewish practice of praying Ps 145 thrice daily.

**Where this leaves us.** Psalm 145 is the Psalter's namesake, the *tehillah* from which *Sefer Tehillim* takes its name. Its alphabetic acrostic enacts totality of language; its *kol*-saturation enacts totality of mercy. The Exodus 34:6 formula at its heart anchors the whole psalm in the Sinai self-revelation of God. The missing *nun* — whether original or lost — has become a theological sign: where there might have been a verse about falling, the next verse upholds. The psalm has been the daily prayer of synagogue Israel for two millennia and has shaped Christian liturgy more than Christians often recognize. It is the prayer that answers the question, *what is praise?*

**Closing question.** If Psalm 145 — alphabetic, universalist, kingdom-declaring, Exodus 34:6-grounded — is the namesake of the Psalter, then what does it mean to call the whole book *Tehillim*, "Praises"? Does the singular *tehillah* at the head of Psalm 145 reach back through the whole Psalter and reframe even the laments and the imprecations as participating in praise? Or does the Psalter remain a polyphony — lament and praise, protest and trust, vengeance and mercy — that Psalm 145 names rather than absorbs?

## The Thread

Psalm 145 is the *Ashrei*, the alphabetic *tehillah* from which the entire Psalter takes its name. Its acrostic enacts totality of language; its *kol*-saturation enacts totality of mercy; its central Exodus 34:6 formula anchors it in Sinai. The synagogue has prayed it three times a day for two thousand years. The missing *nun* — whether scribal accident or theological sign — points to the upholding hand of v. 14. After Psalm 145 the Davidic voice falls silent, and the closing Hallel begins.
