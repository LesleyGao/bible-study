# Leviticus 19: You Shall Be Holy

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Leviticus 19 is the rhetorical and theological center of the Holiness Code (Lev 17–26), the block that Israel Knohl, in *The Sanctuary of Silence* (1995), assigned to a distinct "Holiness School" (H) that edited and supplemented the earlier Priestly material (P). For Knohl, H was a reform movement inside the priesthood, probably active in the late eighth century during Assyria's westward pressure, that pushed P's ritual holiness out of the sanctuary and into the fields, the marketplace, and the courtroom. Lev 19 is his keystone: the programmatic "You shall be holy" of v. 2 is H's signature, and the chapter's fusion of cultic, ethical, and social law is exactly the kind of boundary-crossing integration H makes its project. Julius Wellhausen had earlier placed all of P (including what Knohl would call H) in the Persian period and read the Holiness Code as post-exilic priestly lawgiving. Jacob Milgrom (Anchor Bible *Leviticus 17–22*) and Menahem Haran (*Temples and Temple-Service in Ancient Israel*) rehabilitated a pre-exilic origin.

The political world the chapter inhabits is small-scale agrarian Israel. Verses 9–10 presuppose the family farm — the *śādeh* (field) and *kerem* (vineyard) worked by an owner-household whose harvest must intentionally leave margin for the landless. The laborer of v. 13 is the *śākîr*, a day-wage worker vulnerable to same-day cash-flow; the command that wages not remain "until morning" maps onto a hand-to-mouth economy. The court of v. 15 is the village gate, where elders adjudicate. The *gēr* of v. 34 is the resident non-Israelite without land, dependent on local patronage. This is not the world of the Persian imperial bureaucracy Wellhausen imagined; it is the world of Iron Age Judah as Lawrence Stager, David Schloen, and others have reconstructed it through archaeology of the four-room house and the village pattern.

The gleaning law (vv. 9–10) has striking cultural resonance. The Hittite Laws and the Code of Hammurabi contain no equivalent — their agricultural provisions address property transfer, tenancy, and liability, not structural provision for the poor. Mesopotamian royal ideology produced *mīšarum* edicts (debt-amnesty proclamations) issued by the king at his discretion, and Egyptian wisdom (the Instruction of Amenemope) urged generosity to widows. Leviticus 19's innovation is to embed the poor's claim into the normal annual harvest cycle rather than leaving it to royal mercy. The book of Ruth dramatizes this law in action: Boaz's field, Ruth's gleaning, and the transformation of a landless Moabite widow into the ancestress of David. Christopher Wright (*Old Testament Ethics for the People of God*) has emphasized this as Israel's distinctive economic-theological innovation.

The mixed-seed, mixed-fabric, mixed-livestock prohibitions of v. 19 use the term *kilʾayim*. Mary Douglas, in *Purity and Danger* (1966) and more pointedly in *Leviticus as Literature* (1999), argued that the boundary-maintenance logic of these laws matches the cosmological ordering of Genesis 1: creation works by separation, and Israel's holiness works by honoring those separations. The prohibition on "cuts for the dead" and tattoos (v. 28) corresponds to Canaanite and Syrian mourning practices documented in the Ugaritic epic of Aqhat and in Mesopotamian lament texts. The prohibition on rounding the hair of the temples (v. 27) has been linked (since Herodotus 3.8) to Arabian cultic haircuts honoring the god Orotalt.

The mediums and necromancers of v. 31 (*ʾōbôt wĕyiddĕʿōnîm*) appear again in Saul's visit to the witch of Endor (1 Sam 28). The Hebrew terms have debated etymologies — *ʾōb* may derive from a word for a pit or a leather bag used in necromantic ritual (cf. the Akkadian *āpil*); *yiddĕʿōnî* comes from the root *yādaʿ*, "to know," designating a spirit-familiar.

Archaeological evidence for the chapter's world includes the Ketef Hinnom silver scrolls (late seventh century, excavated by Gabriel Barkay in 1979), which preserve a version of the priestly blessing and witness to a priestly-liturgical culture contemporaneous with H; the Khirbet Qeiyafa ostracon (ca. tenth century), which preserves Hebrew legal-ethical terms including *yātôm* (orphan) and *gēr* (sojourner), suggesting the social vocabulary of Lev 19 was ancient; and the Arad and Lachish ostraca, which testify to the scribal and administrative culture that could produce and transmit such a text.

Key Hebrew terms: *qādōš* (קָדוֹשׁ), "holy"; *leqeṭ* (לֶקֶט), "gleanings," vv. 9–10; *śākîr* (שָׂכִיר), "hired worker," v. 13; *rēaʿ* (רֵעַ), "neighbor" or "fellow," v. 18; *gēr* (גֵּר), "sojourner/resident alien," v. 34; *kilʾayim* (כִּלְאַיִם), "two kinds/mixed," v. 19; *ʾāhab* (אָהֵב), "love," in v. 18's *wĕʾāhabtā lĕrēʿăḵā kāmôḵā*.

Manuscript evidence is solid: MT, Samaritan Pentateuch, 4QLevᵈ, 4QLevⁿ, and the Septuagint (which renders v. 18 as *agapēseis ton plēsion sou hōs seauton*, the exact phrase Jesus and Paul later cite).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is organized less by topical groupings than by cadence. *ʾănî YHWH*, "I am YHWH," punctuates the text sixteen times (more than any other chapter in the Torah), sometimes in the short form, sometimes expanded to *ʾănî YHWH ʾĕlōhêkem*. Knohl identified this refrain as H's diagnostic feature — it converts discrete commands into covenant speech, grounding each imperative in the speaker's identity. The refrain creates a poetic structure inside prose law.

Mary Douglas proposed a ring composition: the chapter moves from Sabbath and parents (v. 3) through cult, ethics, and social provision to Sabbath and sojourners (vv. 30, 34), with the Decalogue-like ethical core in the middle. James W. Watts has argued more cautiously for a series of smaller structural units — the peace-offering regulation (vv. 5–8), the gleaning-theft-court triptych (vv. 9–16), the vengeance-mixed-kinds-slave block (vv. 17–22), the agricultural-cultic block (vv. 23–31), the elders-strangers-weights block (vv. 32–36).

The literary relationship to the Decalogue is obvious and has been studied since Calvin. Verse 3 pairs parents and Sabbath (Commandments 4 and 5); v. 4 forbids idols (1 and 2); v. 11 prohibits theft, deceit, lying (8 and 9); v. 12 prohibits false oaths (3); v. 16 prohibits slander and standing against a neighbor's life (6); v. 20 addresses adultery (7). Moshe Weinfeld argued that Leviticus 19 is H's own Decalogue exposition — the Holiness School's way of saying that holiness is what the Ten Words look like when they go to work.

The genre is apodictic: "You shall not," "You shall." Unlike the casuistic "If a man… then…" formulations that dominate Exodus 21–23 and much of Deuteronomy 22, Leviticus 19 issues direct commands. The single casuistic intrusion is vv. 20–22 (the slave-woman case), which has always struck commentators as stylistically anomalous — Milgrom treats it as an older P-style fragment embedded in H material.

The rhetorical climax is v. 18's *wĕʾāhabtā lĕrēʿăḵā kāmôḵā*, "and you shall love your neighbor as yourself." The syntactic parsing is debated. *Kāmôḵā* can be read as an adverbial predicate of the verb ("love your neighbor as-yourself," i.e., with the same intensity you love yourself) or as an adjectival predicate of the object ("love your neighbor, who is like yourself" — another Israelite in covenant). Martin Buber's translation leaned toward the second reading. Milgrom and most modern translations follow the first. The rabbinic tradition held both simultaneously.

The second *kāmôḵā* (v. 34), applied to the sojourner, extends the ethic: "you shall love him as yourself, for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt." The grammatical parallel with v. 18 forms an inclusio: neighbor-love and stranger-love are the same love with different addressees.

Textual variants are minor. The Septuagint's rendering of v. 18 uses the future *agapēseis*, a form Jesus and Paul quote verbatim (Matt 22:39; Rom 13:9). 11QpaleoLev preserves fragments of the chapter matching MT. The rabbinic tradition occasionally reads v. 17's "reason frankly" (*hôḵeaḥ tôḵîaḥ*) as "you shall surely rebuke," producing a textually grounded halakhic obligation of fraternal correction.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Second Temple Jewish literature treated Leviticus 19 as a moral core. The book of Jubilees echoes its language in retelling patriarchal history. Philo's *Special Laws* 2 treats the chapter's commands as the "unwritten laws" inscribed on nature and engraved in revelation. Josephus (*Against Apion* 2.190–219) presents Israel's law in summary as essentially Leviticus 19's social ethic. The Damascus Document and the Community Rule from Qumran apply the rebuke command of v. 17 as internal sectarian discipline.

Rabbinic interpretation crystallized around v. 18. Hillel's famous encounter with the Gentile (Bavli Shabbat 31a): "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor — that is the whole Torah, and the rest is commentary; go and learn." Rabbi Akiva declared in the Sifra (Kedoshim 4:12): *zeh kĕlāl gādôl ba-tôrâ*, "this is the great principle of the Torah." Ben Azzai countered that Genesis 5:1 ("this is the book of the generations of Adam") is an even greater principle, because it grounds neighbor-love in shared humanity and so extends past the covenant community; the dispute is a foundational moment in Jewish ethics. The Sifra on Kedoshim is the tannaitic midrash on the chapter and remains indispensable. Mishnah Peah develops the gleaning laws; Mishnah Baba Metzia addresses the wage law of v. 13; Mishnah Sanhedrin deals with judicial impartiality (v. 15).

Medieval commentary is dense. Rashi (eleventh-century Champagne) quotes the Sifra constantly and reads v. 18 as requiring love of any Jewish neighbor. Ibn Ezra (twelfth-century Spain/Italy) attends to syntactic nuance — his note on *kāmôḵā* preserves both the adverbial and adjectival readings. Ramban (Nahmanides, thirteenth-century Spain) argues that *kāmôḵā* cannot mean literal equal affection (which would be unattainable) but rather "desire for the neighbor the good you desire for yourself." Maimonides codifies the commandment's content in *Hilchot Deʿot* 6:3 as including specific obligations of honor, compassion, and material help; in *Hilchot Melakhim* 10 he addresses how neighbor-love reaches Gentiles. Sforno and Hirsch develop the ethical psychology — Hirsch, the nineteenth-century neo-Orthodox rabbi of Frankfurt, produced an extraordinary commentary that reads Leviticus 19 as the blueprint for a just society.

Modern Jewish thought has made v. 18 foundational. Nahum Sarna's JPS Commentary, Baruch Levine's Leviticus, and Jacob Milgrom's Anchor Bible volume all treat the chapter extensively. Milgrom's unit-by-unit exposition of Lev 19 runs over 100 pages. Nehama Leibowitz's *New Studies in Leviticus* devotes careful attention to Hillel, Akiva, and Ben Azzai. Rabbi Jonathan Sacks's *Covenant and Conversation: Leviticus* develops the chapter as Israel's gift to civilization. Emmanuel Levinas, writing philosophically rather than as an exegete, treats neighbor-love as the primary ethical event that precedes ontology. Aviva Zornberg's hermeneutic work places the chapter in conversation with psychoanalytic reading.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Jesus cites Leviticus 19:18 at the climax of his teaching on the greatest commandment (Matt 22:37–40; Mark 12:31; Luke 10:27), pairing it with Deut 6:5. In Luke 10 the parable of the Good Samaritan follows as an expansion of the scope of "neighbor." In Matt 5:43–48 he cites v. 18 and reframes it to include enemy-love. Paul treats v. 18 as the summation of the Mosaic ethic (Rom 13:9; Gal 5:14). James calls it "the royal law" (James 2:8).

Patristic exegesis ran accordingly. The Epistle of Barnabas, the Didache, and the Shepherd of Hermas all echo the verse. Origen's *Homilies on Leviticus* develops the chapter allegorically but insists on its literal ethical force. Augustine, in *On Christian Doctrine* 1.22–30 and *Sermon 355*, made love of God and neighbor the interpretive key to all Scripture — any reading that does not produce love is a misreading. John Chrysostom's homilies on Matthew return again and again to Lev 19:18 as the meeting point of Law and Gospel.

Medieval theology systematized. Thomas Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* II-II qq. 23–46 treats charity — love of God and neighbor — as the "form of all the virtues." His analysis of v. 18 distinguishes the material object (the neighbor) from the formal object (God, in whom the neighbor is loved), producing the classical Catholic account of agape. Meister Eckhart pushed the mysticism of neighbor-love further: the love commanded is the same love by which God loves himself.

Reformation theology reread the verse within justification-by-faith. Luther's *Freedom of a Christian* (1520) argued that love of neighbor is the necessary expression of faith's freedom — the believer, freed from the need to earn, is now freed *for* the neighbor. Calvin's *Institutes* 2.8.51–55 treats the Second Table of the Decalogue as summed up in Lev 19:18 and insists that neighbor extends to anyone in whom God's image is found. Zwingli and Bullinger similarly. The Anabaptist tradition (Menno Simons) pressed neighbor-love into a more radical communalism and nonviolence.

Modern critical scholarship reopened the source questions. Wellhausen treated the chapter as late. Noth and von Rad engaged its Decalogue shape. Milgrom gave it extensive exposition. Walter Brueggemann's *Theology of the Old Testament* makes Lev 19 central to his account of Israel's "core testimony" — Yahweh is a God of justice and provision, and neighbor-love is the human shape of that character. Chris Wright's *Old Testament Ethics* frames the chapter as Israel's paradigmatic social vision. Richard B. Hays's *The Moral Vision of the New Testament* treats Jesus's and Paul's use of v. 18 as canonical confirmation of the chapter's binding authority. N. T. Wright emphasizes the new-creation telos — neighbor-love is the anticipatory form of the age to come.

The gleaning law has drawn special attention. Walter Brueggemann has written extensively on vv. 9–10 as a prophetic economics; John Howard Yoder engaged the chapter in *The Politics of Jesus*; Liberation theology (Gustavo Gutiérrez) reads it as structural provision for the poor. Ellen Davis's *Scripture, Culture, and Agriculture* takes the chapter as a template for a theologically grounded land ethic.

**Where they converge and diverge.** On Leviticus 19 — unlike 18 and 20 — Jewish and Christian traditions converge more than they diverge. Hillel, Akiva, Jesus, Paul, James, Augustine, Aquinas, Maimonides, Luther, Calvin, Hirsch, and Sacks agree that v. 18 carries exceptional weight. The disagreements are finer: Does neighbor-love extend beyond the covenant community (Ben Azzai's side; Jesus's Samaritan; Maimonides's carefully graded treatment of Gentiles)? Is love primarily affective or primarily practical (the Christian tradition has oscillated; the Jewish tradition has tended toward practical)? Does the command require loving self-regard as its premise (modern psychological readings) or assume it (medieval and rabbinic readings)? The deepest divergence is structural: for Jewish tradition, v. 18 sits inside a thick halakhic specification that tells you exactly what neighbor-love looks like in property, labor, speech, and judicial life; for much Christian tradition, the verse floats more freely as a principle the Spirit applies case by case. Both sides are poorer without the other.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: What does "holy" mean, and how does it relate to ethics?**

Verse 2 issues the program: "You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy." The traditional Christian understanding (going back to Rudolf Otto's *Das Heilige*, 1917) treated holiness as the numinous — the wholly other, the mysterium tremendum. But the chapter that follows unpacks holiness as honest weights, timely wages, honest speech, care for the deaf and blind, and love of neighbor. How do these fit?

- **Catholic teaching** (CCC 2012–2016 on the universal call to holiness; *Lumen Gentium* 5) holds that holiness is participation in God's own life (theosis, grace), and that moral excellence flows from this ontological participation. Leviticus 19's ethics are the outward form of a life drawn into God.
- **Reformed teaching** (Calvin *Institutes* 3.7–10; John Owen *On the Mortification of Sin*) holds that holiness is conformity to God's revealed will, empowered by the Spirit. Leviticus 19 prescribes that conformity's content.
- **Lutheran teaching** emphasizes that holiness is passive — received through the imputed righteousness of Christ — and that the ethics of Leviticus 19 are the third use of the law (Formula of Concord VI), guiding the sanctified but not making them holy.
- **Wesleyan teaching** (John Wesley, *A Plain Account of Christian Perfection*) reads the chapter as evidence that God really does expect and enable practical holiness, not merely imputed righteousness. Lev 19:2 is a performative possibility.
- **Orthodox teaching** (Paul Evdokimov; Vigen Guroian; the long tradition of the *Philokalia*) reads holiness as theosis, and reads the chapter's ethics as the created shape of the deified life.
- **Jewish teaching** (Maimonides *Hilchot Deʿot*; Sacks) resists the ontological reading in favor of an imitative-covenantal one: God is holy (separate, morally exalted), and Israel is commanded to imitate — to be *qādōš* in the same mode. The chapter's ethics are not the fruit of a metaphysical transformation; they are the transformation.
- **Critical scholarship** (Milgrom, Knohl, Douglas) reads holiness as both separation and moral weight — a boundary marker that also specifies content.

The least intuitive position is the Wesleyan one: that Leviticus 19:2 really does envision a present-tense possibility of practical holiness, not a deferred eschatological hope. Steelmanned: Wesley reads the imperative as performative; God does not command what his grace cannot empower; the entire tradition that reads "holy" as unattainable in this life has softened the text under the pressure of introspective pessimism. Lev 19, on this reading, is exactly what God expects, and the Spirit makes it possible.

**Where this leaves us:** Jewish and Christian traditions agree that Lev 19:2 is programmatic but disagree about what kind of transformation it presupposes. The metaphysics of holiness remain contested.

**Tension 2: How universal is Lev 19:18?**

The verse says "love your neighbor as yourself" — but the Hebrew *rēaʿ* is often restrictive, and the context ("the sons of your own people") narrows it. The later addition at v. 34 extends the love to the *gēr*. Does the verse bind intra-Israel, or does it reach outside?

- **Rabbi Akiva** read *rēaʿ* as the fellow Jew and treated the verse as the "great principle" of *internal* Jewish solidarity.
- **Ben Azzai** countered that Genesis 5:1 is greater because it grounds love in shared humanity, reaching beyond Israel.
- **Maimonides** (*Hilchot Melakhim* 10) worked a graded system: strict covenantal obligations to fellow Jews, Noahide-framed obligations to Gentiles, and within them the *ḥăsîdê ʾummôt hāʿôlām* (righteous Gentiles) who have a share in the world to come.
- **Jesus** (Luke 10:25–37) expanded neighbor to the despised Samaritan; the parable deliberately scandalizes Akiva's narrower reading.
- **Paul** (Gal 3:28; Rom 13:9) universalized further.
- **Catholic teaching** (CCC 1822–1829; *Deus Caritas Est*) treats neighbor-love as universal, grounded in the image of God in every person.
- **Reformed teaching** similarly universalizes, though with varying accounts of how covenant particularism and universal ethics relate.
- **Liberation theology** (Gutiérrez, Sobrino) reads the verse as demanding preferential option for the poor — the neighbor in greatest need has prior claim.
- **Critical scholarship** (Milgrom, Knohl) reads the original sense as intra-Israel with v. 34 as the extension to the *gēr*, and notes that Jewish and Christian traditions legitimately develop the verse further.

Steelmanned, Akiva's narrower reading has force: covenant bonds have specific content that general humanism lacks, and flattening all love into universal benevolence dissolves the particular claims Jews have on one another (and Christians on one another within the body of Christ). Ben Azzai and Jesus are not refuting Akiva so much as pressing a second principle alongside his — humanity-love and covenant-love both pull on the same heart.

**Where this leaves us:** The verse has always generated plural readings. Both Jewish and Christian traditions have held the tension, extending neighbor-love universally while preserving thick covenantal loves inside.

**Unresolved question:** If Lev 19:34 extends love to the *gēr*, the resident alien bound to Israel — and if the NT extends further to enemies (Matt 5:44) — what are the remaining moral obligations of particular love (to family, to nation, to religious community) once neighbor-love reaches everyone? The tradition has not settled the calculus of graded loves; Augustine's *ordo amoris* and Aquinas's *ordinate caritas* are contested even among those who accept the basic framework.

## The Thread

First: Ruth. The book of Ruth is a narrative meditation on Leviticus 19:9–10 and 19:34. Ruth the Moabite is exactly the *gēr* whom Israel is commanded to love as itself, and Boaz's gleaning field is the working infrastructure the chapter prescribes. The story is the Holiness Code in action — and the child born from it becomes the grandfather of David. The Levitical ethic of gleaning and stranger-love reaches into the genealogy of the Messiah.

Second: the Good Samaritan. Luke 10 is organized around two Levitical texts — Deut 6:5 and Lev 19:18. The lawyer's question — "who is my neighbor?" — is a question about the reach of *rēaʿ*. Jesus's answer refuses the abstract question and redirects: the neighbor is the one whose need confronts you, and the command is to become a neighbor, not to identify one. The parable deliberately places the Samaritan — the ethnic and religious outsider, the impure figure — in the role of Levitical neighbor-lover, while the priest and Levite who know Lev 19 fail it.

Third: James on the royal law. James 2:1–13 quotes Lev 19:18 as "the royal law" and reads it against partiality toward the rich. What is striking is that James grounds his argument in exactly the judicial-impartiality command of Lev 19:15. The whole paragraph is an exposition of the Holiness Code — showing that the NT's practical ethics are substantially a midrash on Leviticus 19.

Fourth: Paul and the fulfillment of the Law. Romans 13:8–10 declares that the Mosaic commandments are "summed up" (*anakephalaioutai*) in Lev 19:18. Galatians 5:14 says the whole law is "fulfilled" (*peplērōtai*) in it. The vocabulary is precise: Paul is not discarding Leviticus but compressing it. The Christian ethic is not less than Leviticus 19 but its gathered form. This is why Christian tradition cannot escape the Holiness Code even while it claims freedom from the Law.
