# 2 Chronicles 3 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Chapter 3 begins the actual construction of the temple. The parallel is 1 Kings 6, but the Chronicler's version is dramatically compressed and theologically reweighted. Where Kings devotes thirty-eight verses to construction details, the Chronicler covers the same ground in seventeen, but he opens with one of the most theologically loaded verses in all of Chronicles.

*Then Solomon began to build the house of the LORD in Jerusalem on Mount Moriah, where the LORD had appeared to David his father, at the place that David had appointed, on the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite* (verse 1). The verse identifies the temple site by *three* historical-theological coordinates: Mount Moriah, the place of Yahweh's appearance to David, and the threshing floor of Ornan. Hebrew *be-har ha-Moriyyah asher nir'ah le-David aviv asher hekhin bi-mqom David be-goren Ornan ha-Yevusi* (בְּהַר הַמּוֹרִיָּה אֲשֶׁר נִרְאָה לְדָוִיד אָבִיו אֲשֶׁר הֵכִין בִּמְקוֹם דָּוִיד בְּגֹרֶן אָרְנָן הַיְבוּסִי).

The first coordinate — *har ha-Moriyyah*, "Mount Moriah" — is unique to Chronicles. The Books of Kings never call the temple mount Moriah. The only other occurrence of "Moriah" in the Hebrew Bible is Genesis 22:2, where Yahweh tells Abraham, *Take your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love, and go to the land of Moriah, and offer him there as a burnt offering on one of the mountains of which I shall tell you*. Hebrew *eretz ha-Moriyyah* (אֶרֶץ הַמֹּרִיָּה). The Chronicler is identifying the temple-mount with the place of the *Aqedah* — the binding of Isaac. This identification is *unique to the Chronicler* in the canonical Hebrew Bible. No earlier biblical text makes this connection.

Sara Japhet, in her *Old Testament Library* commentary, treats the identification as one of the Chronicler's most consequential theological moves. Gary Knoppers calls it "the Chronicler's most distinctive geographical-theological claim about the temple." Hugh Williamson notes that the identification is unique to him among the biblical writers. Whether this represents an old tradition the Chronicler is preserving or his own innovation is debated. Jon Levenson, in his landmark study *The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son: The Transformation of Child Sacrifice in Judaism and Christianity* (1993), argues that the Aqedah-temple connection is plausibly ancient — the temple cult would naturally seek to ground itself in patriarchal foundation-narratives, and the connection between the place of Isaac's near-sacrifice and the place of Israel's later sacrificial system is theologically too tight to be late invention. Either way, the Chronicler is the canonical witness to the connection, and his identification shaped everything that came after.

The second coordinate — *asher nir'ah le-David aviv* ("where Yahweh appeared to David his father") — refers to the theophany of 1 Chronicles 21:18–22:1, when David, after the census-plague, was directed by the angel of Yahweh to build an altar on Ornan's threshing floor, and where Yahweh answered David's offering with fire from heaven (1 Chronicles 21:26). David then concluded: *This is the house of the LORD God, and here shall be the altar of burnt offering for Israel* (1 Chronicles 22:1). The Chronicler's verse 1 is therefore identifying the temple-mount as the site of *three* foundational events: the Aqedah, David's atonement-altar, and now Solomon's temple.

The third coordinate — *be-goren Ornan ha-Yevusi* ("on the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite") — preserves the urban-Canaanite history of the site. Ornan (called Araunah in 2 Samuel 24) was a Jebusite, the pre-Israelite inhabitant of Jerusalem. The temple is built on land purchased from a Jebusite, on a threshing floor — a working agricultural surface. The site is therefore not pristine sacred land but acquired, contested, sanctified by purchase and by theophany.

Verse 2 dates the construction: *the second month of the fourth year of his reign*. The standard chronology places this around 967 BC, though the precise date depends on which dating system is used (Williamson and Knoppers note the difficulties). The Chronicler omits Kings's elaborate dating ("the four hundred and eightieth year after the people of Israel came out of the land of Egypt," 1 Kings 6:1), simplifying to regnal year alone.

Verses 3–7 describe the main building. The dimensions in *cubits of the old standard* — 60 cubits long, 20 cubits wide, with a vestibule 20 cubits long and *120 cubits high* — give the building its proportions. The 120-cubit vestibule height is a problem. 1 Kings 6:2 says the temple as a whole was 30 cubits high, and Kings does not give a separate vestibule height. A 120-cubit (roughly 180-foot) vestibule on a 30-cubit-tall building is architecturally implausible. Sara Japhet and Ralph Klein both treat this as either a textual corruption (the LXX of Chronicles reads "20 cubits" here, matching the building height) or as the Chronicler's deliberate exaggeration to emphasize the temple's grandeur. Knoppers favors textual corruption. Williamson is open to either.

The materials are gold, gold, and more gold: pure gold overlay on the vestibule (verse 4), fine gold on the nave with palms and chains (verse 5), settings of precious stones (verse 6), gold of Parvaim — a place-name otherwise unknown, possibly Persian-period (Hebrew *zahav Parvayim*, זְהַב פַּרְוָיִם, verse 6) — and gold throughout: beams, thresholds, walls, doors (verse 7).

Verses 8–9 describe the Most Holy Place: 20 by 20 cubits, overlaid with 600 talents of fine gold, plus 50 shekels of gold for the nails alone. Six hundred talents is roughly 20 tons. The figure is theological-rhetorical, not auditable.

Verses 10–13 describe the cherubim of the Most Holy Place. This is one of the chapter's most theologically distinctive sections. The two cherubim are 10 cubits tall each, made of wood and overlaid with gold. Each cherub has two wings, each five cubits long. The total wingspan stretches across the entire 20-cubit width of the Most Holy Place: *one wing of the one, of five cubits, touched the wall of the house, and its other wing, of five cubits, touched the wing of the other cherub* (verse 11). The cherubim are positioned so that their wings together span the entire room, with the inner wings meeting in the middle and the outer wings touching the side walls. *The cherubim stood on their feet, facing the nave* (verse 13) — a detail unique to Chronicles. The cherubim face *outward*, toward the nave (the holy place), not inward toward the ark. Knoppers and Williamson both note this: in Chronicles, the cherubim are oriented toward the worshipers, mediating divine presence outward, rather than focused inward on the ark itself.

Verse 14 describes the *parokhet* — the veil — of blue, purple, crimson, and fine linen, with cherubim worked into it. Hebrew *parokhet* (פָּרֹכֶת). This is a Chronicles-specific addition. 1 Kings 6 does not mention a veil; Kings has olive-wood doors separating the Most Holy Place. The Chronicler is *adding* the tabernacle's veil (compare Exodus 26:31–35) into the temple's furnishings, drawing the same tabernacle-temple typology he drew in chapter 2 with Huram-abi. For the Chronicler, the temple is the tabernacle continued and amplified.

Verses 15–17 describe the two pillars in front of the temple, named *Jachin* and *Boaz*. Each pillar is 35 cubits high (Kings says 18 cubits, 1 Kings 7:15 — another numerical discrepancy that commentators treat as either textual corruption or Chronicler stylization), with a 5-cubit capital on top, decorated with 100 pomegranates on a chain. The names *Yakhin* (יָכִין, "he will establish") and *Bo'az* (בֹּעַז, possibly "in him is strength" or a proper name) are unexplained in the text. Williamson and Knoppers offer competing interpretations: Yakhin and Boaz may be the opening words of dynastic-theological inscriptions (e.g., "Yahweh will establish your throne forever" and "in Yahweh's strength the king rejoices"), with the names serving as mnemonics for longer affirmations. Their precise meaning remains debated.

## Lens 2: The Literature

The chapter is structured as a guided tour of the temple's main building, moving from outer to inner: site identification (verse 1), date (verse 2), main building dimensions and gold (verses 3–7), Most Holy Place dimensions and gold (verses 8–9), cherubim (verses 10–13), veil (verse 14), pillars (verses 15–17). The literary form is *temple-description* — a recognized ancient Near Eastern genre (the Cyrus Cylinder, the Esarhaddon temple inscriptions, and various Egyptian temple texts use similar conventions).

The Chronicler's compression compared to Kings is theologically pointed. He omits extensive details of the side chambers, the windows, the cedar paneling specifications, the elaborate woodworking. What he keeps and amplifies: the site identification (with the Aqedah-Moriah connection), the gold, the cherubim, and the pillars. The selection prioritizes theological resonance over architectural completeness. He is writing for a post-exilic audience who would never see the original temple and who needed the temple's *meaning* more than its blueprints.

The numerical inflation (120-cubit vestibule, 35-cubit pillars, 600 talents of gold) functions rhetorically rather than architecturally. Boda calls this "theological hyperbole" — the temple's grandeur is being conveyed through numerical excess rather than literal measurement.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

The Aqedah-Moriah-Temple identification is the chapter's most consequential afterlife. Both Jewish and Christian traditions inherited and transformed this typology in distinctive ways.

The Jewish trajectory begins with the Chronicler himself but extends through the rabbinic tradition. The Talmud (*Berakhot* 62b, *Zevachim* 62a) accepts the Moriah-Aqedah identification and treats the temple-mount as the place of Isaac's binding. Rashi (eleventh century) makes the connection explicit in his commentary on Genesis 22:2 and 2 Chronicles 3:1, treating the two passages as identifying the same mountain. Targum Onkelos translates *Moriah* as "the land of worship," and Targum Pseudo-Jonathan elaborates further, treating the Aqedah-mountain as the eternal location of Yahweh's chosen worship-site. Radak (David Kimchi) follows Rashi.

The deeper Jewish theological development is the doctrine of *zekhut Avot* — the merit of the patriarchs — which holds that the Aqedah established a permanent atoning merit that benefits Israel forever. The Aqedah is read in the synagogue every Rosh Hashanah morning service; the *Zikhronot* prayer asks Yahweh to remember the Aqedah and grant Israel mercy on its account. The temple, built on the very mountain of the Aqedah, is the institutional continuation of this atoning logic. Even after the temple's destruction, Jewish prayer continued to face the temple-mount-Moriah, and the daily service liturgically reenacts the temple cult that the Aqedah-merit established. Modern Jewish scholarship — Jon Levenson, Isaac Kalimi, Sara Japhet — has emphasized how the Chronicler's identification is foundational to the entire Jewish liturgical-theological framework around the temple-mount.

The Christian trajectory is parallel but transformed. Early Christian writers — beginning with the New Testament, with Paul's reading in Romans 4 and Galatians 3 of Abraham's faith, and Hebrews 11:17–19's treatment of the Aqedah as a type of resurrection — read the binding of Isaac christologically. Isaac becomes a type of Christ: the only beloved son, carrying the wood of his sacrifice up the mountain, willingly submitting to the father's will. The Aqedah-mountain becomes the type of Calvary. Origen, Tertullian, Augustine, and the entire patristic tradition develop this typology. The medieval Latin tradition, codified in Aquinas, treats the Aqedah as one of the primary Old Testament foreshadowings of Christ's sacrifice. The temple-mount, in Christian typology, is the place where the type was set and where the antitype (Calvary, just outside Jerusalem) was fulfilled.

Levenson's *Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son* is essential reading on this typological inheritance. Levenson argues that *both* Judaism and Christianity inherited the Chronicler's Aqedah-temple identification but moved it in different directions: Judaism developed the doctrine of Aqedah-merit-atoning-forever-through-the-temple-cult; Christianity transferred the typology to Christ and made the Aqedah a foreshadowing of the cross. Both readings have a common root in the Chronicler's verse, and both have a logic that follows from the original identification. Levenson treats the two trajectories as theologically respectable rivals, neither one a corruption of the other, both legitimate developments from a shared post-exilic insight.

The New Testament does not quote 2 Chronicles 3:1 directly, but the Aqedah-typology runs through Hebrews 11:17–19 (where Abraham's near-sacrifice of Isaac is treated as faith in resurrection), John 3:16 (where the Father's giving of his only Son echoes Abraham's giving of his only son), and Romans 8:32 (where God's "not sparing his own Son" deliberately echoes the Aqedah's *withholding* language from Genesis 22:12, 16). The temple itself is reframed in Christian theology as Christ's body (John 2:19–22), and the place of the sacrifice is moved from the mountain to the cross — but the Chronicler's foundational geography remains in the background.

The modern critical commentaries — Williamson, Knoppers, Klein, Boda — converge on the importance of 2 Chronicles 3:1 as a theological hinge. The Chronicler is doing more than locating a building; he is theologically integrating Israel's foundational stories. The temple is on the mountain of the Aqedah, on the threshing floor of David's atonement, on land purchased from a Jebusite. It is a site of patriarchal sacrifice, royal atonement, and urban acquisition — and the temple sanctifies all three histories.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The Chronicler's agenda in chapter 3 is to place the temple at the geographical-theological center of Israel's foundational stories — Aqedah, David's atonement, the Jebusite city's incorporation into Israel — and to describe the temple's most sacred features (the cherubim, the veil, the pillars) in terms that echo the tabernacle. Two tensions deserve careful naming.

**The first tension: the Moriah-Aqedah-Temple typology and its trajectories.** The Chronicler's identification of the temple-mount with Mount Moriah is the foundation for two parallel and irreducible religious developments — the Jewish Aqedah-merit liturgical tradition, and the Christian Christ-as-Isaac sacrificial typology. How should the modern reader hold these?

**Position 1: Christian-supersessionist reading.** The Aqedah-Moriah connection is a foreshadowing of Christ's sacrifice. Once the antitype has come, the type is fulfilled. The Jewish liturgical use of Aqedah-merit is therefore obsolete. This was the long-dominant Christian reading, from the Church Fathers through medieval theology and well into the twentieth century.

**Position 2: Jewish-particularist reading.** The Aqedah is the foundational atoning event of Israel, and the temple is its institutional continuation. Christian christological readings impose categories foreign to the text and to Jewish liturgical-theological tradition. The Chronicler is not foreshadowing anything; he is establishing Israel's sanctuary on Israel's foundational mountain.

**Position 3: Levensonian-respectful-rivalry reading.** Both readings are legitimate developments from a shared post-exilic insight. Judaism develops the Aqedah-temple connection in the direction of communal atoning merit and liturgical reenactment. Christianity develops it in the direction of christological fulfillment. Neither reading is a corruption of the other; both have textual warrant; both have produced enormous spiritual fruit. The honest reader holds them as parallel theological trajectories that share a common origin in the Chronicler's verse.

**Steelman of Position 3.** Levenson's argument is that the typological move *from Aqedah to Christ* is structurally similar to the rabbinic move *from Aqedah to liturgical merit*. Both take the same text — Abraham almost sacrificing his beloved son on Moriah — and develop its atoning logic in their own theological idioms. Christianity's Christ-as-Isaac typology is a recognizable development from the Aqedah, just as the rabbinic *zekhut Avot* doctrine is. Calling either reading a corruption is to deny the legitimate theological imagination of one tradition while privileging the other. The Chronicler himself is doing typological work in chapter 3 — identifying Solomon's temple-mount with Abraham's near-sacrifice-mount — and the two later traditions are extending the same kind of work in different directions. Levenson's stance is not a relativism; it is a recognition that the same biblical event can sustain multiple theologically serious readings.

**Where this leaves us on this tension.** The honest Christian reader does not need to dismiss the Jewish liturgical-Aqedah tradition as obsolete; it is a legitimate development from the Chronicler's verse, with its own internal coherence. The honest Jewish reader does not need to deny that Christian christological readings have textual warrant in the Chronicler's identification; they too are extensions of the same logic. What both traditions share is gratitude to the Chronicler for the identification — without his verse, neither development would have its primary biblical anchor. The Christian reader who takes Levenson seriously also recognizes that the cross is not a *replacement* for the Aqedah-temple-mountain logic but its christological extension. Christ is the new Isaac; Calvary is the antitype of Moriah; and the Jewish liturgical tradition that continues to hear the Aqedah read each Rosh Hashanah is keeping alive the same theological grammar in its own valid idiom.

**The second tension: the cherubim's outward orientation.** In Chronicles, the cherubim *stand on their feet, facing the nave* (verse 13) — looking outward, toward the worshipers in the holy place, rather than inward toward the ark. This is unique to Chronicles. In Exodus 25:20, the tabernacle's cherubim face one another over the mercy seat, with their faces toward the cover. In Kings's description of the temple's cherubim (1 Kings 6:23–28), the orientation is not specified. The Chronicler's outward-facing cherubim represent a theological adjustment.

**Position 1: Detail-only reading.** The orientation is an architectural detail without major theological weight. The Chronicler is filling in a gap Kings left silent.

**Position 2: Mediation-oriented reading.** The Chronicler is making a deliberate theological move. The cherubim, as guardians of the divine presence (Genesis 3:24, Ezekiel 1, 10), face outward to mediate that presence to the worshiping community. The temple is not a hidden divine residence but an outward-radiating sanctuary. This is consistent with the Chronicler's overall theology — the temple as meeting place, the cult as community-oriented, prayer as the means by which Yahweh's presence reaches the people. Williamson and Boda take this view.

**Position 3: Liturgical-procession reading.** The cherubim face the nave because the priestly liturgy moves through the nave. The cherubim's gaze accompanies the priestly action, witnessing it from the inner sanctuary. This is a more functional reading — the orientation reflects the ritual choreography, not a theological abstraction.

**Where this leaves us.** The outward orientation, even as a small detail, fits the Chronicler's broader vision: the temple as a place where Yahweh's presence is mediated outward to the community, not contained inward as a divine secret. The cherubim, in this reading, are not just guards of the holy of holies but witnesses-and-mediators of the divine presence to the worshiping people. For the post-exilic reader, this would be theologically meaningful — even with the ark gone (the Second Temple had no ark), the cherubim's outward gaze suggests that the temple is still oriented toward the people, still a place where Yahweh's presence reaches beyond the empty sanctuary.

**Where this leaves us.** The chapter is theologically dense and architecturally compressed. It locates the temple at the intersection of Israel's foundational stories — Abraham, David, the Jebusite city — and describes its features (cherubim, veil, pillars, gold) in terms that echo the tabernacle while expanding it. The Chronicler is not just reporting construction; he is integrating the temple into Israel's sacred geography in a way that no other biblical writer attempts.

**Closing question.** The Chronicler placed the temple on Mount Moriah, knowing that this identification would shape every later reading of Israel's foundational story. Was he preserving an old tradition, or creating one? Either way, his single verse generated two thousand years of Jewish liturgical practice and two thousand years of Christian christological typology, and the contemporary reader stands at the intersection of both inheritances. What does it mean to read a single verse that is the silent center of so much religious imagination — and to read it knowing that two of the world's deepest theological traditions both consider the verse foundational, but build different houses on it?

## The Thread

Solomon begins to build the temple on Mount Moriah, the place of Abraham's near-sacrifice of Isaac, of David's atonement-altar after the plague, of Ornan the Jebusite's threshing floor. The Chronicler's single identifying verse — *har ha-Moriyyah* — sets the temple at the geographical-theological center of Israel's foundational stories. The cherubim of the Most Holy Place stand outward-facing, mediating Yahweh's presence to the worshipers. The veil of blue, purple, crimson, and fine linen draws the tabernacle into the temple. The pillars Yakhin and Boaz frame the entry. The Aqedah-temple identification will give Judaism its liturgical-merit theology and Christianity its christological-typology, both legitimate developments from the same single verse.
