# Daniel 8: The Ram, the Goat, and the Maccabean Crisis

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Daniel 8 is the chapter where the dating debate becomes unavoidable. Where chapter 7's apocalyptic vision speaks in symbolic beasts requiring decoding, chapter 8 names its referents directly: the ram with two horns is Media and Persia (8:20), the male goat from the west is the king of Greece (8:21), the great horn between his eyes is the first king of Greece (8:21 — historically Alexander the Great), the four horns that arise after the great horn is broken are four kingdoms from his nation but not with his power (8:22 — historically the four Diadochi: Ptolemy, Seleucus, Cassander, Lysimachus), and the little horn that emerges from one of the four is "a king of bold face, one who understands riddles" who attacks "the Prince of the host" and removes "the regular burnt offering" and overthrows "the place of his sanctuary" (8:9–14, 23–25 — historically Antiochus IV Epiphanes). The precision is what makes the chapter the strongest single piece of evidence in the dating debate.

The chapter is dated in the narrative to "the third year of the reign of King Belshazzar" (8:1) — chronologically following 7:1's "first year of Belshazzar." The vision is set in Susa, the Elamite-Persian capital that became Darius I's primary residence, on the bank of the Ulai canal. Susa was not under Babylonian control during Belshazzar's co-regency; the location is therefore visionary rather than physical (Daniel is at Susa "in the vision"). The chapter shifts back to Hebrew — the Aramaic block ended at 7:28, and Hebrew resumes at 8:1 and continues through 12:13. The bilingual structure is deliberate: the Aramaic portion addressed gentile empires in their administrative language; the Hebrew portion addresses Israel about her future under those empires.

The political situation behind the chapter is the Hellenistic crisis. **Alexander the Great** (356–323 BC) destroyed the Persian Empire in a four-year campaign (334–330 BC), defeating Darius III at Issus (333), Gaugamela (331), and pursuing the king's death in Bactria. Alexander died in Babylon in 323 at age thirty-two — "broken" at the height of his power (8:8). His generals, the *Diadochi*, fought a forty-year war over the succession; by the Battle of Ipsus in 301 BC the empire had stabilized into four kingdoms: Ptolemaic Egypt, Seleucid Syria-Mesopotamia-Iran, Cassander's Macedonia, and Lysimachus's Thrace-western Anatolia. By the late third century, only Ptolemaic and Seleucid rule persisted in the regions concerning Israel.

**Antiochus IV Epiphanes** (reigned 175–164 BC) is the chapter's "little horn." He came to the Seleucid throne after the assassination of his brother Seleucus IV by the regent Heliodorus, displacing his nephew Demetrius (held hostage in Rome) and a brief Heliodorus-Antigonus puppet regime. His self-designation "Epiphanes" — "God Manifest" — was a theological provocation; his enemies punned the title to "Epimanes" — "the Madman." His Egyptian campaigns of 170 and 168 BC ended in humiliation when the Roman legate Gaius Popilius Laenas drew a circle in the sand around him at Eleusis and demanded an answer before he stepped out (the *dies Eleusinius*, recorded by Polybius 29.27 and Livy 45.12). Returning enraged, Antiochus turned on Jerusalem. In December 167 BC he installed a statue of Olympian Zeus on the altar of burnt offering, sacrificed swine on it, and outlawed Torah observance, circumcision, and Sabbath keeping. The "abomination that makes desolate" — *šiqqūṣ mešōmēm* in chapter 11, *peša šomem* / "transgression that makes desolate" in 8:13 — entered Jewish memory as the worst sacrilege in the Second Temple period. The Maccabean revolt (167–164 BC) led by Mattathias and his sons (Judas, Simon, Jonathan) restored the Temple in December 164 BC, the festival of Hanukkah. Antiochus died in Persia in late 164, "broken — but by no human hand" (8:25).

The "2,300 evenings and mornings" of 8:14 has provoked the longest interpretive controversy in the chapter. Read as 2,300 actual days, the figure corresponds roughly to six years and four months — a period that has been variously placed (Antiochus's first interference around 171 to the Temple's rededication in 164; or an earlier reckoning). Read as 2,300 *tamid* sacrifices (the daily morning and evening burnt offering), the figure corresponds to 1,150 days, roughly three years and two months — which matches more closely the period from the desecration (December 167) to the rededication (December 164). The 1,150-day reading is preferred by **John J. Collins** (Hermeneia), **Carol Newsom** (Old Testament Library), **James Montgomery** (ICC), and most contemporary scholars; the 2,300-day reading was preferred by **William Miller** in nineteenth-century American adventism (his calculation of 1844 from this verse founded the Seventh-day Adventist tradition's "Investigative Judgment" doctrine). The Hebrew is genuinely ambiguous; the chapter does not settle the question.

The dating debate that runs through Daniel as a whole becomes acute here. The conservative position, defended by **Stephen Miller** (NAC), **Joyce Baldwin** (TOTC), **Gleason Archer**, and **John Walvoord**, holds that the chapter is genuine sixth-century BC predictive prophecy. The position is theologically intelligible and exegetically defensible if one's prior commitments allow miraculous predictive prophecy on a four-century scale. The critical position, dominant since **Porphyry of Tyre** in the third century AD (preserved in Jerome) and consolidated through **S. R. Driver**, **R. H. Charles**, **James Montgomery**, **H. H. Rowley**, and Collins, Newsom, Casey, Grabbe, and Davies, holds that the chapter is *vaticinium ex eventu* — prophecy after the fact — composed by a Maccabean redactor who already knew Antiochus IV's career and projected it back into Daniel's mouth. The strongest critical argument runs: (a) the precision of the prophecies extends with high accuracy through Antiochus IV's career and stops at his death; (b) chapter 11's predictive timeline of 11:40–45 (Antiochus's death campaign in the south) does not match what historically happened (Antiochus died in Persia, not in a southern Egyptian campaign); (c) the precision-then-vagueness pattern is exactly what one would expect from *vaticinium ex eventu* by a writer who knew the history up to roughly 165–164 BC and projected the future from there.

Conservative responses to this argument acknowledge the difficulty and offer two main paths: (1) the prophecies of 11:40–45 refer to a future Antichrist figure whose career typologically resembles Antiochus's, with the precision-then-shift marking the prophet's perspective rather than his ignorance (the dispensationalist reading favored by Walvoord); (2) the prophecies of 11:40–45 refer to a more complex Antiochus campaign whose details are not well-preserved in our extant sources (the historicist reading favored by Miller and Archer). Critical scholarship finds neither response convincing; conservative scholarship finds the *vaticinium* hypothesis theologically inadmissible. There is no comfortable middle.

The textual situation includes the Old Greek (LXX) version, which differs from Theodotion's revision in several details for chapter 8 but agrees on the basic visionary content. Eight Daniel manuscripts at Qumran preserve portions of chapter 8 with consistency to the Masoretic text. The chapter's reception in the **War Scroll (1QM)** at Qumran shows the Maccabean-or-later Jewish community reading the chapter eschatologically, with the "kittim" (Romans, in the Qumran reading) as the chapter's ultimate referent.

Key terms anchor the chapter. *Sar* (שַׂר, "prince," in the phrases "Prince of the host" 8:11 and "Prince of princes" 8:25) is the chapter's most disputed term — the figure may refer to God himself, to the high priest (Onias III, murdered in 171 BC, a likely candidate), to the archangel Michael (who returns explicitly in 10:13, 12:1), or to a celestial-divine figure of the Most High. *Tamid* (תָּמִיד, "regular / continual," referring to the *tamid* burnt offering of Numbers 28:3–8) is the technical term for the daily morning-and-evening sacrifice that Antiochus suspended. *Peša šomem* (פֶּשַׁע שֹׁמֵם, "transgression that makes desolate," 8:13) is the chapter's version of the phrase that becomes *šiqqūṣ mešōmēm* (שִׁקּוּץ מְשֹׁמֵם, "abomination of desolation," 11:31, 12:11), the phrase that Mark 13:14 and Matthew 24:15 cite. *Erev boqer* (עֶרֶב בֹּקֶר, "evening morning," 8:14) is the unusual phrasing of the 2,300 figure. *Gabriel* (גַּבְרִיאֵל, "man of God," 8:16) is named for the first time in canonical Scripture here.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter is apocalyptic in the same technical sense as chapter 7 (per Collins's *Semeia 14* definition) but with a different mode. Chapter 7 deployed surreal beasts requiring symbolic decoding; chapter 8 deploys ordinary apocalyptic animals (ram, goat) with explicit decoding ("the ram = Media and Persia," "the goat = Greece") and a specificity of historical reference that earlier prophecy avoided. **Klaus Koch** in *Das Buch Daniel* (1980) and **John Collins** in *The Apocalyptic Imagination* (1984) note that chapter 8 represents a turning point in the genre: the move from symbolic generality to historical specificity that characterizes mature Second Temple apocalyptic.

The literary structure is tight. Verses 1–14 are the vision proper, in three movements: the ram (vv. 3–4), the goat and his transformations (vv. 5–8), and the little horn's career (vv. 9–14). Verses 15–26 are the angelic interpretation, with **Gabriel** named (8:16) — the first explicit naming of an angel by personal name in the canonical Hebrew Bible. Verse 27 closes with Daniel's distress: "I lay sick for some days" — a notation that anticipates chapter 10's even more extended visionary distress. The vision-then-interpretation structure parallels chapter 7's literary architecture, but with the interpretation expanded and the vision compressed.

The genre signal of the chapter — alongside the language shift from Aramaic to Hebrew at 8:1 — is the move from foreign-court setting (chapters 2–7 take place in Babylonian and Persian courts) to interior visionary setting (Daniel "in the vision" at Susa, then on the Ulai canal). The mode of revelation has shifted. In chapters 2 and 4, kings have dreams that Daniel interprets; in chapter 5, fingers write on a wall. In chapter 7, Daniel himself dreams, and the heavenly courtroom is shown. In chapter 8, Daniel is taken visionarily to a specific geographical location and confronts a specific historical sequence with a named angelic interpreter. The genre's mature form has crystallized.

The use of explicit identification — the angel naming the ram as Media and Persia (8:20) and the goat as Greece (8:21) — is the chapter's distinctive feature. Earlier prophetic traditions (Isaiah 13–14 against Babylon, Jeremiah 50–51 against Babylon, Ezekiel 26–28 against Tyre) named their referents directly. Apocalyptic traditions (Daniel 7, 1 Enoch's Animal Apocalypse) typically did not. Chapter 8 sits between the two: an apocalyptic vision with prophetic-style explicit identification. The hybridization is deliberate, and it is what gives the chapter its peculiar interpretive force — and its peculiar problem for the dating debate.

The chiastic structure of the Aramaic block (chapters 2–7) ended at 7:28; chapter 8 begins a new structure. Most contemporary commentators (Collins, Newsom, Goldingay) read chapters 8–12 as a series of four progressive visions: chapter 8 (ram and goat, focused on the Greek period), chapter 9 (the seventy weeks, focused on Jerusalem's restoration), chapters 10–12 (the long apocalypse, comprising chapter 10's preamble, chapter 11's history-as-prophecy, and chapter 12's eschatological close). Each vision is increasingly specific about the Antiochene crisis. Chapter 8 introduces the figure; chapter 11 dilates him at length; chapter 12 frames his end.

Canonically, Daniel sits in the *Ketuvim* in the Hebrew Bible, alongside Esther and Ezra-Nehemiah. The Christian arrangement places it in the prophets, after Ezekiel. The placement determines the genre framing: as wisdom-and-vision literature (Hebrew arrangement) or as prophecy looking forward to fulfillment (Christian arrangement).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The chapter sits in the canonical arc as the moment when prophetic specificity becomes apocalyptic specificity. The Hellenistic crisis under Antiochus IV was the most severe assault on Jewish religious life in the Second Temple period; chapter 8 (along with 11) is the canonical Scripture that interprets it.

The Jewish trajectory is rich. **1 Maccabees 1:54–64** narrates Antiochus's desecration of the Temple in language that explicitly evokes Daniel ("the abomination of desolation"); **2 Maccabees 5–7** narrates the same events with emphasis on martyrdom. The Maccabean books read Daniel and Daniel reads the Maccabean events — the relationship is bidirectional and probably contemporaneous in the redactor's perspective. **Josephus**, *Antiquities* 10.11 and 12.5–7, paraphrases Daniel 8 and reads it as fulfilled in Antiochus IV's career. Josephus is the earliest extra-biblical witness to identify the chapter's little horn explicitly with Antiochus.

The Babylonian Talmud, *Yoma* 9a, *Megillah* 11b, and *Sotah* 9a discuss the chapter; the rabbinic tradition generally identifies the little horn with Antiochus IV but extends the chapter's eschatological reach forward to Rome (which the Maccabean victors had partnered with against the Seleucids and which became Israel's later oppressor). **Saadia Gaon**'s tenth-century commentary, **Rashi**, **Ibn Ezra**, and **Don Isaac Abrabanel** read the chapter as fulfilled in Antiochus, with eschatological extensions toward "Edom" (a coded designation for Rome / Christianity in some medieval Jewish reading). Modern Jewish scholarship — **Robert Gordis**, Boyarin (focused on chapter 7 but with implications for chapter 8), **Aaron Koller** — emphasizes the Maccabean context.

The Christian trajectory is split at the chapter's identification. **Hippolytus of Rome's *Commentary on Daniel*** (c. 200 AD), the earliest extant Christian biblical commentary, reads the little horn as Antiochus IV but sees in him a *type* of the eschatological Antichrist; the typological reading becomes the patristic standard. **Jerome's *Commentary on Daniel*** (407 AD), composed against Porphyry, accepts the historical identification (the little horn is Antiochus IV) but maintains the typological extension (he is also a figure of the future Antichrist). **Theodoret of Cyrus** in the fifth century follows Jerome.

The medieval reception holds the typological reading steady. **Aquinas**'s *Commentary on Daniel*, the *Glossa Ordinaria*, and **Joachim of Fiore**'s twelfth-century apocalyptic schema all read Antiochus as type and Antichrist as antitype. **Luther's *Preface to Daniel*** (1530) extends the typology to include the Pope as a fulfillment of the Antichrist figure; **Calvin's *Lectures on Daniel*** (1561, two volumes) is exegetically careful, identifies the little horn historically as Antiochus IV, and is more measured about further typological extensions.

The critical trajectory begins with **Porphyry of Tyre** (preserved in Jerome) and runs through Bertholdt, Driver, Charles, Montgomery, Rowley, and into the contemporary consensus represented by Collins, Newsom, Casey, Davies, and Grabbe. The contemporary critical reading: chapter 8 is *vaticinium ex eventu* by a Maccabean redactor; the little horn is Antiochus IV; the chapter's theological work was done in 167–164 BC, and any further eschatological extension is interpretive rather than authorial.

The Reformation-period adventist tradition (Miller's 1844 calculation, the early Seventh-day Adventists under Ellen White) reads the 2,300 days as 2,300 years (using the day-for-a-year hermeneutic from Numbers 14:34 and Ezekiel 4:6) and extends the chapter's timeline to 1844, when Miller predicted Christ's return; the failure of that prediction and the subsequent re-interpretation by Hiram Edson and others as an "investigative judgment" beginning in 1844 founded the SDA tradition's distinctive sanctuary doctrine. The reading is not standard in mainstream Christian or Jewish interpretation but is significant for understanding the chapter's nineteenth-century reception.

Where the trajectories diverge most sharply is on the dating. The conservative reading (Miller, Archer, Walvoord, Baldwin) takes the chapter as genuine predictive prophecy and reads the little horn as a future Antichrist figure with Antiochus as a historical foreshadowing. The patristic-typological reading (Hippolytus, Jerome, Aquinas) treats the historical Antiochus as type and the future Antichrist as antitype — both real, both intended. The critical reading (Collins, Newsom, the academic consensus) treats the chapter as Maccabean *vaticinium* with no future referent. The Jewish reading is generally Maccabean with eschatological extensions. There is genuinely no comfortable middle.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The first tension is the dating problem made unavoidable. Chapter 8's specificity is the strongest single piece of evidence in the dating debate: the explicit identification of Persia and Greece (8:20–21), the description of Alexander's career and death (8:5–8), the four Diadochi (8:8, 22), and the precise career of Antiochus IV (8:9–14, 23–25). The chapter's author either knew this history and wrote about it as past (Maccabean *vaticinium*), or knew this history four centuries in advance through divine revelation (sixth-century predictive prophecy). There is no third option that respects the text.

The Catholic position, articulated in Vatican II's *Dei Verbum* (1965) and the Pontifical Biblical Commission's *Interpretation of the Bible in the Church* (1993), holds that historical-critical analysis is essential and that the canonical-theological reading is the church's authoritative interpretation — both can be true at different levels. Catholic scholars including **Joseph Fitzmyer**, **Raymond Brown**, and **John J. Collins** himself (a Catholic priest before his shift to academic-critical work) have generally accepted Maccabean composition. The Eastern Orthodox tradition, through **Father John Behr** and **Andrew Louth**, similarly holds that historical *peshat* and spiritual *theoria* are both legitimate readings; Orthodox scholarship has been more circumspect about Maccabean composition but does not require the conservative position. Protestant traditions split: conservative evangelicals (Miller, Archer, the Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy 1978) treat the predictive-prophecy reading as required by inerrancy; mainline Protestant scholarship (**Brevard Childs**'s canonical approach, the Yale school) treats the historical-critical reading as compatible with canonical-theological reading. Jewish traditions have not had to resolve the question because they do not require Christological fulfillment and are comfortable with Second Temple composition.

Steelman the conservative position. Miraculous predictive prophecy is not impossible if Yahweh is the God Daniel claims; the precision is exactly what one would expect from such a God; the New Testament's confidence that the chapter speaks of historical and eschatological events is the deepest reading; and the *vaticinium* hypothesis requires assuming what it tries to prove (that miraculous prediction is impossible). Where this leaves us: the chapter forces a theological decision about the kind of book one reads — and that decision is downstream of one's theology of revelation rather than a neutral exegetical matter. The conservative reading is theologically intelligible if its prior commitments are accepted; the critical reading is exegetically tight if its prior commitments are accepted. Neither is reachable from the other by argument alone.

Steelman Collins's critical reading. The precision-then-vagueness pattern across chapters 8 and 11 is genuinely a literary phenomenon. Chapter 11's predictive timeline of 11:40–45 does not match what historically happened to Antiochus (he died in Persia, not in a southern campaign); the simplest explanation is that the redactor knew the history up to roughly 165–164 BC and projected from there. The 1,150-day reading of the 2,300 evenings and mornings (Collins, Newsom, Montgomery) corresponds to the actual desecration period (December 167 to December 164) with high precision. The Maccabean redactor hypothesis is not a hostile reading; it is a charitable one that accounts for the data.

The second tension is the *abomination that makes desolate* — the trope that crosses Jewish and Christian readings. The historical referent in 8:13 is the Olympian Zeus statue installed on the Temple altar by Antiochus IV in 167 BC. The phrase is amplified in 11:31 and 12:11 (*šiqqūṣ mešōmēm*) and is cited explicitly by Jesus in **Mark 13:14** ("the abomination of desolation standing where it should not be") and **Matthew 24:15** ("the abomination of desolation, spoken of by Daniel the prophet, standing in the holy place"). Jesus applies the Daniel 8 / 11 phrase to the Temple's then-future fall in AD 70 — a typological extension that takes the chapter's Maccabean referent and reads it forward through Roman desecration. Some interpretive traditions (dispensationalist Christian, parts of the patristic tradition) extend the typology further to a future eschatological Antichrist desecration of a rebuilt Temple.

The Catholic position, expressed in the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* §675 and in commentary by **Joseph Ratzinger** / Benedict XVI in his *Eschatology* (1977 / 1988 English), holds that the typological extension is legitimate but should not require a literalistic Antichrist scheme; the chapter's eschatological force is the church's perennial trial under power that opposes God. The Eastern Orthodox tradition similarly holds the typology as legitimate without requiring rigid futurism. The Reformed tradition has historically applied the typology to the Pope (Luther, in his 1530 *Preface to Daniel*); the conservative evangelical tradition has historically applied it to a future Antichrist; the mainline Protestant tradition (under Childs's canonical approach) treats the typology as canonically meaningful without requiring a single referent. Jewish readings retain the Maccabean referent with eschatological extensions toward Rome. Critical scholarship treats the typological extensions as later interpretive moves rather than authorial intent.

**N. T. Wright's** "already / not yet" reading (*Jesus and the Victory of God*, 1996) offers a useful structuring: the chapter's vision was inaugurally fulfilled in Antiochus's defeat (167–164 BC), typologically fulfilled in the Roman destruction of the Temple (AD 70), Christologically inaugurated in Jesus's death and resurrection (the true sanctuary cleansed), is currently being worked out in the church's witness against blasphemous powers, and will be consummated in the parousia. The reading does not require choosing between Maccabean and eschatological referents; it locates the fulfillment in a structure that includes all of them.

The unresolved question. The chapter ends with Daniel "appalled by the vision and did not understand it" (8:27). The angelic interpretation has named the kingdoms, the king of bold face, the broken-without-human-hand end. The reader has been told what the text means, in detail, more directly than in any other chapter of Daniel. And yet Daniel — the seer — does not understand. Why does the chapter close on incomprehension after offering its most explicit identifications? Either because the visionary form requires more than information to transfer (the chapter is showing the reader that knowing the identifications is not the same as understanding what they mean), or because the redactor is preserving Daniel's epistemic distance from a Maccabean future the redactor himself already knew. Both readings remain in the text.

## The Thread

The first cross-reference is **Mark 13:14** / **Matthew 24:15** — Jesus's Olivet Discourse, which cites Daniel's *abomination of desolation* explicitly and applies it to the Temple's then-future fall. The intertext is the chapter's typological-historical pattern as Jesus himself reads it: the abomination that desolated the Temple under Antiochus is the type of the desolation that came under Rome, which the gospels treat as the climactic vindication of Jesus's prophetic ministry. The Daniel 8 phrase is one of Jesus's most direct citations of the Hebrew Scriptures, and it shapes early Christian reading of the AD 70 destruction.

The second is **1 Maccabees 1:54–64** — "they erected the abomination of desolation upon the altar … they have profaned the sanctuary." The Maccabean books read Daniel 8 and Daniel 8 reads the Maccabean events; the relationship is the closest case in the canonical-deuterocanonical relationship of one book interpreting another in real time. For Catholic and Orthodox readers (whose canon includes 1 and 2 Maccabees), the intertext is direct; for Protestant readers (whose canon excludes them), it is historically critical but textually external.

The third is **Revelation 13** — the beast from the sea, who blasphemes the Most High, makes war on the saints, and rules for "forty-two months" (Rev 13:5 — three and a half years, the *time, times, and half a time* of Daniel 7:25 and the 1,150 days / 2,300 evenings and mornings of Daniel 8:14). John of Patmos has read Daniel 7 and 8 together and has taken the chapter's vocabulary and timing into Christian apocalyptic. The intertext is the chapter's afterlife as the seedbed of New Testament apocalyptic.

The fourth is **2 Thessalonians 2:3–4** — Paul's "man of lawlessness, the son of destruction, who opposes and exalts himself against every so-called god or object of worship, so that he takes his seat in the temple of God, proclaiming himself to be God." This is the chapter's clearest Pauline echo: the figure who exalts himself "as great as the Prince of the host" (Daniel 8:11) becomes, in 2 Thessalonians, the figure who "exalts himself against every so-called god." Paul has read Daniel 8 typologically and applied it to a figure who exceeds the historical Antiochus while sharing his theological signature. The chapter's reach extends from 167 BC into the church's eschatological self-understanding through this Pauline link.
