# Numbers 24: A Star Shall Come Out of Jacob

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Numbers 24 completes the Balaam cycle on the top of Peor, looking out across the wilderness at Israel encamped "tribe by tribe" (*lišbāṭāyw*, v. 2). The chapter opens with a shift in Balaam's technique: "he did not go, as at other times, to look for omens (*liqraʾt nĕḥāšîm*)" (v. 1). The Hebrew *neḥāšîm* (נְחָשִׁים, from *nḥš*) denotes serpentine/omen divination — one of the classical ANE forms alongside *qesem* (general divination) and *bārû*-type extispicy. Balaam has abandoned his standard procedural toolkit. Instead, "the Spirit of God came upon him" (*rûaḥ ʾĕlōhîm*, v. 2), and what follows are two more oracles and three brief utterances of more archaic, more ecstatic, more strikingly messianic shape.

The political and ethnic geography of the fourth oracle (vv. 15-19) and the subsequent short oracles (vv. 20-24) gives us dense ANE topographical data. Moab, Edom/Seir, Amalek, the Kenites, Asshur, Kittim, and Eber are all mentioned. Moab is Balak's kingdom. Edom and Seir are the southern Transjordan kingdom (Esau's lineage, Gen 36). Amalek is the desert tribe first encountered in Exodus 17. The Kenites (*qayin*, Kain) are a small semi-nomadic group associated with Moses' father-in-law Jethro/Hobab; they range from Midian through the Negev. Asshur is Assyria — its mention in vv. 22 and 24 has been read either as prophetic anticipation (older critical argument for post-exilic dating) or, given the early mentions of Asshur in 18th-century BC Middle Bronze inscriptions, as plausibly pre-monarchic (so Albright, Kitchen). Kittim (*kittîm*, כִּתִּים) originally names Kition on Cyprus (Phoenician colonization in the 13th-12th centuries BC gives a terminus a quo) and by extension Mediterranean maritime powers; in later Second Temple usage it shifts to mean the Greeks (Maccabees) or Romans (Qumran). "Eber" (*ʿēber*) is Genesis 10's eponymous ancestor of the Hebrews and northwest Semitic peoples generally.

The presence of "Agag" (*ʾăgāg*, v. 7) — "his king shall be higher than Agag" — has been debated. The Amalekite king defeated by Saul in 1 Samuel 15 bears this name; some read Numbers 24:7 as a *vaticinium ex eventu* glorifying the Davidic monarchy over the Amalekites. Others (Kitchen, Hoffmeier, Cole) note that "Agag" may be a dynastic title — compare "Pharaoh," "Abimelech" — attached to a sequence of Amalekite rulers, so the reference could be genuine and early rather than retrojected.

The cultic site of Peor is the most important archaeological/religious datum for the chapter. Peor (*pĕʿôr*) is the same location where Israel will apostatize in chapter 25. Balaam looks down from the "high place of Baal" toward Israel's camp and speaks words that bless; just below him, Israel will shortly practice the rites of the local Baal and call down destruction on themselves. The spatial irony of the oracles being delivered over the same ground where Israel will fail is a deliberate compositional touch.

Three Hebrew terms carry weight in this chapter. First, *šĕtûm hāʿāyin* (שְׁתֻם הָעָיִן, v. 3, v. 15), "the man whose eye is opened," uses a rare verb (*šātam*) that may mean "opened" or, paradoxically, "closed" (cf. Aramaic and some Arabic cognates); Levine and Milgrom both note the ambiguity, which fits the uncanny mystical register. Second, *nōpēl ûgĕlûy ʿênāyim* (v. 4, v. 16), "falling down with eyes uncovered" — the language of ecstatic prophetic experience, comparable to the trance states of ANE seers. Third, *kôkāb miyyaʿăqōb* (כּוֹכָב מִיַּעֲקֹב, v. 17), "a star from Jacob" — the single most famous phrase in the entire Balaam corpus, which will reverberate through Second Temple apocalypticism, rabbinic messianism, the Bar Kokhba revolt, and Christian Christology.

The archaeological framing remains the Deir ʿAlla inscription: Balaam as a figure of Transjordan religious memory, a seer whose ecstatic visions are preserved in an independent non-Israelite plaster text. Jo Ann Hackett's analysis demonstrates that the Deir ʿAlla Balaam delivers his vision from an ecstatic state comparable to the one described in Numbers 24:3-4 and 15-16. Whatever the precise relationship between the two corpora, the type — Balaam the ecstatic seer — is attested outside the biblical text.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Numbers 24 contains the third and fourth oracles, plus three brief *mĕšālîm* (oracular sayings) against Amalek, the Kenites, and the Mediterranean powers. The chapter's literary structure is an ascending climax: each oracle rises in poetic density, in messianic focus, and in eschatological distance.

The third oracle (vv. 3-9) opens with the formal self-introduction, "The oracle of Balaam son of Beor, the oracle of the man whose eye is opened," a four-line induction that matches verbatim the opening of the fourth oracle. This repetition creates a formal parallel that marks these two oracles as the climactic pair. The body of the third oracle praises Israel's encampment in lush botanical imagery — palm groves, gardens, aloes, cedars beside waters — drawn from the ANE royal-garden topos. The move toward kingship begins here ("his king shall be higher than Agag," v. 7), and the lion imagery returns from oracle two, now coupled with the echo of Genesis 12:3, "Blessed are those who bless you, and cursed are those who curse you" (v. 9b). The Balaam cycle closes a literary arc by quoting the Abrahamic blessing back to itself.

The fourth oracle (vv. 15-19) is the chapter's high point. After the same four-line induction, Balaam says: "I see him, but not now; I behold him, but not near" — the temporal marker that separates this oracle from the others. Balaam now speaks prophetically about a future figure. "A star shall come out of Jacob, and a scepter shall rise out of Israel" (v. 17). The Hebrew is emphatic: *dāraḵ kôkāb miyyaʿăqōb wĕqām šēbeṭ miyyiśrāʾēl*. *Dāraḵ* ("shall tread, march forth, emerge") is a rare verbal choice, evoking the astral motion of a rising star. *Šēbeṭ* is the scepter or staff of rule — Genesis 49:10's "the scepter shall not depart from Judah" uses the same word. The pairing of star and scepter combines astral omen (a royal birth heralded by a celestial sign, a common ANE motif) with royal regalia. "It shall crush the forehead of Moab and break down all the sons of Sheth" (v. 17b) — *qarqar* ("crush") echoes Genesis 49:10's violence, and "sons of Sheth" is obscure (possibly "sons of tumult," possibly a reference to a specific Transjordan people attested in the Egyptian execration texts as *Šutu*).

The linguistic case for the antiquity of these oracles is even stronger than for chapters 22-23. Albright's *Yahweh and the Gods of Canaan* devotes a chapter to the oracles' archaic features: the retained *we-* conjunction; rare vocabulary (*nōpēl*, *šĕtûm*, *qarqar*, *dāraḵ* in its archaic sense); the 3ms suffix *-yw* spelled in old orthography; prosodic patterns close to Ugaritic epic poetry. Cross, Freedman, and Levine all agree the fourth oracle in particular preserves pre-monarchic Hebrew. This is critical for the question of whether the messianic reading developed later: the linguistic evidence supports the oracle's antiquity, meaning the Bar Kokhba reading and the Christian reading both engage a genuinely ancient text.

The three short oracles (vv. 20-24) are compressed and enigmatic. Amalek "was first among the nations" — possibly first to attack Israel (Exod 17), possibly first among desert peoples — "but its end is utter destruction." The Kenites are praised for their enduring dwelling but warned of Assyrian captivity. The final oracle invokes Kittim (Cyprus and Mediterranean maritime powers) afflicting "Asshur and Eber," ending "and he too shall come to utter destruction." This coda has a distinctly apocalyptic flavor — "Alas, who shall live when God does this?" (v. 23) — and has attracted much Second Temple and patristic attention as a cipher for eschatological judgment on world empires.

Textually, the key variant appears in the LXX of v. 7, which reads "a man (*anthrōpos*) shall come out of his seed" — an interpretation the LXX translator read messianically already in the 3rd-2nd centuries BC. The Samaritan Pentateuch preserves MT readings closely; 4QNum-b contains fragments of the oracles with high MT agreement. The Targums (Onkelos, Pseudo-Jonathan, Neofiti) translate the "star" of v. 17 as *malkāʾ* ("king") and frequently add "Messiah" explicitly — Targum Onkelos is relatively restrained, but Pseudo-Jonathan and Fragment Targum render v. 17 with full messianic interpretation.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Jewish readings

The messianic reading of Numbers 24:17 is attested remarkably early. At Qumran, **4Q175 (Testimonia)** gathers four scriptural texts read as messianic promises: Deut 5:28-29 + 18:18-19 (the prophet like Moses), Numbers 24:15-17 (Balaam's star), Deut 33:8-11 (the blessing of Levi), and a passage about the "evil one" (4QTestim). The placement of Numbers 24 in this sequence shows that by the 2nd-1st century BC, the Qumran community read the star of Jacob as a promise of the Davidic messiah (paired with priestly and prophetic figures in their two- or three-messiah expectation). The *Damascus Document* (CD 7:18-21) cites the star-and-scepter verse and distinguishes the "star" as the "Interpreter of the Law" and the "scepter" as the "Prince of the Congregation" — Qumran's dual messianic reading. **4Q339 (List of False Prophets)** names Balaam, showing the simultaneous and entirely compatible tradition of Balaam as villain whose words are true.

The Targums consistently render "star" as "king" and "scepter" as "anointed/messiah." Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Numbers 24:17 expands: "A king shall arise from Jacob, and an anointed one shall be raised from Israel, and he shall kill the princes of Moab and rule over all the sons of men."

The **Bar Kokhba Revolt (132-135 AD)** is the pivotal moment in the Jewish reception of this verse. The revolt's leader, Simon ben Kosiba, was renamed by his partisans *Bar Kokhba*, "son of the star," in explicit application of Numbers 24:17. Rabbi Akiva — the greatest rabbinic authority of the generation — proclaimed him the messiah, citing this verse. The Jerusalem Talmud (*Taʿanit* 4:8) preserves the tradition: "Rabbi Akiva, when he saw Bar Koziba, would say, 'This is the king, the Messiah'." When Rabbi Yohanan ben Torta retorted, "Akiva, grass will grow up from your cheeks and the son of David will still not have come," he spoke as a dissenter. After the revolt's catastrophic failure (perhaps 600,000 Jewish dead by Cassius Dio's count, the effective end of Judean Jewry for a millennium), the movement's leader was relabeled *Bar Koziba*, "son of the lie." Rabbinic literature is ambivalent thereafter about Numbers 24:17 — it remained on everyone's list of messianic texts, but the hermeneutical trauma of Bar Kokhba's failure weighed heavily.

Maimonides, in *Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Melachim U'Milchamoteihem* (Laws of Kings) 11:1, cites Numbers 24:17 as a foundational proof text for the messianic hope: "From the words of Balaam can be learned — the star shall come forth from Jacob — this refers to David; there shall arise a scepter from Israel — this refers to the Messiah." Maimonides folds the verse into his naturalistic messianism: the messiah will be a human king who restores Torah and rebuilds the Temple, no cosmic upheaval required. Rashi and Ibn Ezra treat the star and scepter together as messianic, following Targum.

In modern Jewish scholarship, Levine's Anchor Bible volume traces the reception history in detail. Milgrom is more measured, noting the verse's ambiguity and the danger of over-reading. Sacks's *Covenant & Conversation* on Balak addresses the Bar Kokhba trauma candidly: the messianic reading is ancient and real, and its premature application cost Jewry everything.

### Christian readings

**Matthew 2:1-12** — the Magi from the East following the star to Bethlehem — is the foundational Christian reception of Numbers 24:17. The early church read the convergence as explicit: pagan astrologer-priests (*magoi*, a term overlapping Balaam's profile) follow a star to a Judean king. Multiple patristic writers articulate the typology: Balaam-pagan-seer prefigures Magi-pagan-seers; star of Jacob becomes star of Bethlehem. Origen in *Contra Celsum* 1.60 and *Homilies on Numbers* 13-19 develops the parallel extensively; Justin Martyr in *Dialogue with Trypho* 106 cites Numbers 24:17 as Christological; Irenaeus in *Against Heresies* 3.9.2 and Eusebius in *Demonstration of the Gospel* 9.1 follow. The convergence was taken as evidence that Christian messianism was not a novel invention but a fulfillment of a text the Jewish tradition itself treated as messianic.

Cyril of Alexandria's *Glaphyra* on Numbers reads the oracle as explicitly Trinitarian: the "Most High" (v. 16) is the Father, the "Almighty" the Son, the "Spirit of God" upon Balaam the Holy Spirit. Augustine in *City of God* 18.45 uses Balaam as evidence that God revealed Christ to the nations in advance. Ambrose's *De Balaam* develops the typological parallel between Balaam's star and the Magi's star.

Aquinas in the *Summa* (III q. 36 a. 5) cites Numbers 24:17 in treating the star of Bethlehem; he notes that it belongs to God's providence to confirm through pagan expectation what the prophets had promised in Israel. Calvin's commentary on Numbers and on Matthew make the connection explicit: "The testimony of Balaam, a pagan diviner, makes the fulfillment at Bethlehem more convincing precisely because no Christian apologist could have invented it."

Critical scholarship on the verse from Wellhausen through Cross, Albright, Hoftijzer, Levine, and most recent Numbers commentators (Wenham, Cole, Budd, Dozeman, Radner) accepts the ancient date of the oracle on linguistic grounds, debates whether the "star" originally referred to David, the Davidic dynasty generically, or a more open eschatological figure, and generally treats the later Qumran, Bar Kokhba, and Christian readings as legitimate developments of an inherently open text.

### Convergences and divergences

Jewish and Christian traditions converge on the messianic reading of Numbers 24:17 and have done so since at least the 2nd century BC (Qumran). They converge on the paradox of the pagan diviner delivering the central messianic oracle. They diverge on the identity of the one the star names. Jewish readings have ranged from David (Maimonides) to the eschatological son of David (most rabbinic) to Bar Kokhba (Akiva, tragically). Christian readings crystallized around Jesus of Nazareth, with the Matthean Magi narrative as the hermeneutical bridge. The Bar Kokhba failure and the Matthean success both turn on the same verse. What Judaism and Christianity share is the conviction that a pagan seer four hundred years before Israel had a king, eight hundred years before the exile, twelve hundred years before the New Testament, was given a vision of something distant that would be, when it came, decisive. What they dispute is who has come, and who is still to come.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions.

**Tension 1: Does "I see him, but not now; I behold him, but not near" (v. 17) refer to the historical David, a dynastic Davidic line, the eschatological messiah, or all of the above?**

*Historical-Davidic position (some critical scholarship; Noth; Gray):* The oracle was composed under the united monarchy as a legitimation of David's rule, particularly his conquest of Moab (2 Samuel 8:2). The "star" is David; the "scepter" his royal house. The verse's apparent distance ("not now, not near") is the narrative voice placing David in Balaam's future; the composition is retrospective. Agag in v. 7 is the Amalekite king Saul and David defeated; the Edom/Seir conquests in v. 18 match the Davidic campaigns.

*Dynastic-continuing-royal-line position (Ibn Ezra, many rabbinic, most recent critical commentators who accept the pre-monarchic dating):* The oracle promises not a single figure but a dynasty. David is the first fulfillment; the Davidic line continues the pattern; the ultimate figure is the eschatological messiah who consummates what David began. This is the dominant Jewish reading and the basis of the rabbinic expectation of a Davidic messiah.

*Exclusively eschatological/messianic position (much patristic, Christian tradition, Bar Kokhba readings):* The oracle does not meaningfully refer to David or the historical dynasty at all. It refers to a single figure who comes at the end of days. The Bar Kokhba application tried to locate him in the second century AD; the Christian application locates him in Jesus of Nazareth; Maimonidean and later rabbinic expectation continues to await him.

*Steelman — typological multi-fulfillment (Aquinas, Calvin, Radner, N. T. Wright):* Refuse the either/or. The oracle's open, distant language ("not now, not near") invites multiple fulfillments that do not cancel each other. The oracle meant something about David and the Davidic dynasty; it meant something about messianic expectation; it meant something about Jesus, if one reads Christologically; it meant something about Bar Kokhba, tragically, if one read him as messiah. The text is not ambiguous because the redactor was confused but because prophetic oracles of this sort are intentionally open to iterated fulfillment. This is the theological logic that sustained both Jewish and Christian messianic reading simultaneously for two millennia. Aquinas makes this typological logic explicit; Radner and Wright develop it for contemporary audiences.

**Where this leaves us:** The oracle has been heard by two great traditions as the promise of one who is coming. Both traditions have read it faithfully by its own internal grammar. The text does not adjudicate between them. What it does is refuse to be domesticated to a single historical moment: the "not now, not near" is a permanent structural feature of the verse, not a riddle the oracle pretends to answer.

**Tension 2: The pagan seer as vehicle of messianic revelation — does this vindicate natural religion, or is it ironic?**

*Natural-theology/preparatio evangelica position (Justin, Clement of Alexandria, some patristic, later Vatican II):* The Balaam oracles, along with the Sibylline Oracles and whatever was truly prophetic in pagan religion, constitute a *praeparatio evangelica* — a preparation for the gospel among the nations. God did not leave the Gentiles without witness. A genuine revelation was given to the nations through their own seers, and Balaam is the exemplar. This grounds a generous Christian theology of religions.

*Anti-natural-religion/ironic position (Luther, much Reformation, Barth):* No. The point of Balaam is not that pagan religion can produce true prophecy but that God overrides pagan religion by hijacking its vehicles. Balaam speaks truth *despite* his paganism, not *because* of it. His religion — divination, omens, ritual purchase of divine favor — is thoroughly rejected in the text (v. 1, v. 23). To read Balaam as endorsing "natural religion" is to miss the polemic: the master diviner confesses before his pagan client that divination is null and the God of Israel is sovereign. Karl Barth's rejection of natural theology explicitly uses Balaam: the pagan speaks God's word only as captive to it.

*Steelman — the ironic witness position (Milgrom, Levine, Wenham, Radner, Brueggemann):* The Balaam cycle rejects pagan religion as a system but affirms that God can speak through anyone, including a morally compromised gentile seer. This is not natural theology; nor is it pure supersessionism. It is the claim that divine revelation exceeds Israel's covenantal boundary without ceasing to be covenantal revelation. The pagan seer speaks Israel's blessing and Israel's messianic hope because God chose to speak it through him, not because Balaam's religious apparatus had the resources to produce it. But the choice to speak through him matters theologically: God's willingness to use a pagan voice signals something about how the blessing will ultimately reach the nations.

**Where this leaves us:** The text is not a manifesto for natural religion and is not a Barthian repudiation of it. It is a story in which God speaks through a pagan, subverting his paganism without abandoning him, and through that subversion delivers the central messianic promise to Israel. The implication — worked out in Matthew 2, in Acts 17, in Romans 1-2, in the Christian theology of the religions — is that the nations are not religiously sealed off from the God of Israel, even though their religions are not the means by which they are brought in. This is a careful and specific theological claim the Balaam cycle makes, and neither tradition should collapse it.

**Unresolved question:** If the fourth oracle genuinely dates to the 12th-10th centuries as the linguistic evidence suggests, what kind of pre-monarchic Israelite messianism does it presuppose? Did early Israel already have a developed hope for a coming king — not merely a leader like Moses or Joshua — whose star would rise at the end of days? Or is the "messianism" we see in the verse a retrojection of later reading into an oracle whose original sense was more modest? The stakes of this question are considerable. If pre-monarchic Israel already carried a messianic expectation, then the David-tradition and the prophetic-tradition and the apocalyptic-tradition are all elaborations of something older than any of them. If the messianism is later, then Numbers 24:17 is a remarkable case of a text whose meaning expanded as the tradition ran forward. The linguistic evidence is suggestive but not decisive. This is one of the live scholarly and theological questions.

## The Thread

Three non-obvious cross-references.

1. **Matthew 2 and Numbers 24:17** — the Magi narrative. Pagan astrologer-priests from the East see a star, follow it to a Judean king, and offer royal gifts. The convergence with Balaam is structurally exact: pagan seers (*magoi* in Matthew 2:1, compare Balaam) from the East (Matthew 2:1 says *apo anatolōn*, from the East, echoing Balaam's origin from Aram / the Euphrates), led by a star (the astral imagery of Balaam's oracle), arriving to bless and worship a king (as Balaam blessed, not cursed, Israel). Matthew does not cite Numbers 24:17 explicitly — but the parallel is dense. The early church saw it; the rabbinic tradition saw it; the structural echo is one of the New Testament's most sophisticated pieces of intertextuality.

2. **Revelation 22:16 and Numbers 24:17** — Jesus identifies himself: "I am the root and the descendant of David, the bright morning star (*ho astēr ho lampros ho prōinos*)." The image combines Isaiah 11's "root of Jesse" with Numbers 24:17's star from Jacob. Revelation's final self-identification of the risen Christ reaches back to Balaam's oracle. The book that begins with Christ dictating to seven churches ends with him naming himself the star of Jacob. In 2 Peter 1:19 and Revelation 2:28, the "morning star" appears to members of the church — a participation in what the oracle promised.

3. **Daniel 7:13-14 and Numbers 24:17** — "one like a son of man" approaching the Ancient of Days and receiving dominion is a later apocalyptic development of the Balaam trajectory. The "scepter" of Numbers 24:17 becomes the "dominion" (*šolṭān*) of Daniel 7. The language of a future figure who exercises rule over the nations — already present in Balaam's "one from Jacob shall exercise dominion" (v. 19) — develops in Daniel into the Son of Man tradition that will feed both the Enochic literature and the Synoptic Christology. The prophetic thread from Balaam to Daniel to Jesus is not a straight line, but it is traceable: each generation of Israelite apocalyptic hope returned to the star of Jacob and found something new.
