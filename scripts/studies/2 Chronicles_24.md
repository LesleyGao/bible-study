# 2 Chronicles 24 — Going Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World Behind the Text

Second Chronicles 24 is one of the chapters where the Chronicler's distinctive theological program shows most plainly. Joash reigns forty years (c. 835–796 BCE), divided sharply by Jehoiada the priest's death: faithful while Jehoiada lives, apostate after he dies. The chapter has substantial Kings parallel material — 2 Kings 12 covers Joash's reign with the temple-repair and Hazael episodes — but the Chronicler adds three theologically decisive blocks: (1) an expanded temple-repair narrative with explicit reference to the Mosaic temple-tax (vv. 5-14); (2) the apostasy after Jehoiada's death (vv. 17-19), unique to Chronicles; and (3) *the murder of Zechariah son of Jehoiada* in the temple court (vv. 20-22) — also unique to Chronicles, and the load-bearing scene of the chapter for both Hebrew-canonical and New Testament reception. The Chronicler also alters the ending: in Kings, Joash is assassinated for unstated reasons; in Chronicles, the assassins explicitly avenge Zechariah's blood (v. 25).

The Chronicler — anonymous, post-exilic, fifth- or fourth-century BCE Yehud — is constructing one of his clearest *ethical-theological diagrams*. Sara Japhet (*The Ideology of the Book of Chronicles*; *I & II Chronicles*, OTL) reads chapter 24 as the Chronicler's most explicit demonstration of his *immediate-retribution* schema: every faithful act yields blessing, every apostate act yields wrath, and the ratio is precisely calibrated. Joash is faithful — he repairs the temple. Joash apostatizes — Aram invades, his servants kill him. Joash murders the priest's son in the temple court — Joash is murdered on his bed. The chiastic moral architecture is intentional and unsubtle. Ralph Klein (*2 Chronicles*, Hermeneia) calls chapter 24 "the most starkly bilateral chapter in Chronicles" — two halves with reversed signs. H. G. M. Williamson (*1 and 2 Chronicles*, NCBC) emphasizes the Chronicler's distinctive willingness to *dethrone* a king whom Kings treats more sympathetically: where 2 Kings 12 has Joash mostly as a faithful temple-restorer with a problematic ending, the Chronicler develops the second-half apostasy in full.

Gary Knoppers (Anchor Yale) and Steven McKenzie (*1–2 Chronicles*, AOTC) note the temple-tax detail as a particular Chronicler concern. The chapter twice references "the tax that Moses the servant of the LORD" laid on Israel (vv. 6, 9), tying the project to Exodus 30:11-16's half-shekel temple-maintenance tax. Mark Boda (*1–2 Chronicles*, CBC) and Louis Jonker (Eerdmans Critical) read this as the Chronicler legitimating post-exilic Yehud's temple-tax practice (visible in Neh 10:32-33) by retrojecting it onto Joash.

The vocabulary signals the theological grammar. *Bĕnê ʿĂtalyāhū hā-mirshaʿat* ("the sons of Athaliah, that wicked woman," v. 7) is the Chronicler's harshest naming of his bad-queen figure. *Lābshāh ʾet-Zĕkharyāh* ("[the Spirit] clothed Zechariah," v. 20) — the verb *lābash* in the Spirit-comes-upon constructions of Chronicles (1 Chr 12:18; 2 Chr 24:20) is unusual; the Spirit *clothes* a prophet as a garment, an embodied prophetic empowerment. *Yēreʾ YHWH wĕ-yidrōsh* ("may the LORD see and avenge," v. 22) is Zechariah's dying cry — the verb *dārash* here means "seek out, require," the same root used elsewhere in Chronicles for properly seeking Yahweh, but here turned to a request for divine prosecution.

## Lens 2: The Literature Itself

The chapter is structured as a six-movement bilateral drama with Jehoiada's death (vv. 15-16) as the precise pivot.

Movement one (vv. 1-3): *the king's youth*. Joash is seven, reigns forty years, his mother is Zibiah of Beersheba, Jehoiada arranges his marriages. The repeated phrase *kol-yĕmê Yĕhōyādāʿ ha-kōhēn* ("all the days of Jehoiada the priest," v. 2) is the load-bearing temporal qualifier — Joash is faithful *while* Jehoiada lives.

Movement two (vv. 4-14): *temple repair*. The Chronicler develops the project in deliberate stages. Verse 4: Joash *had it in his heart* (*hāyāh ʿim-lēbab*) to restore the temple — the same heart-language used of David's temple project in 1 Chr 22 and 28. Verses 5-7: the Levites do not move quickly; Joash summons Jehoiada and asks pointedly, *why have you not required* the Levites to bring in *the tax of Moses the servant of the LORD?* This is the Chronicler's polite version of the king reprimanding the high priest — a remarkable inversion of the chapter 23 power dynamic, where Jehoiada was effectively regent. Verse 7's parenthetical justification — Athaliah's sons had broken into the temple and used the dedicated things for Baal-worship — explains why the temple needs repair: it has been physically and theologically violated. Verses 8-14: the chest, the proclamation, the daily emptying, the masons and carpenters and metalworkers, the strengthening of the house, the new utensils, the regular burnt offerings *all the days of Jehoiada*. The Chronicler's repeated time-marker (vv. 2, 14) is doing structural work.

Movement three — the pivot (vv. 15-16): *Jehoiada's death*. He dies at 130 years old (one of the highest ages recorded for a non-patriarchal figure in the Hebrew Bible — Williamson notes this is roughly the lifespan of Aaron and Moses, deliberately invoked). He is buried *bĕ-ʿīr Dāwīd ʿim ha-mĕlākīm* ("in the city of David among the kings"), with the explicit justification *kī ʿāsāh ṭōbāh bĕ-yiśrāʾēl wĕ-ʿim hāʾĕlōhīm ū-bētō* ("because he had done good in Israel and toward God and his house"). Compare 21:20 — Jehoram was buried in the city of David but *not in the tombs of the kings*; the Chronicler's burial-honors are precisely calibrated. The high priest is given the king's burial; the apostate king (Joash) will not be (v. 25).

Movement four (vv. 17-19): *post-Jehoiada apostasy*. The princes of Judah come and pay homage to Joash; he listens to them; they all abandon the temple and serve *the Asherim and the idols*. Wrath comes. Yahweh *sends prophets* to bring them back, but they do not listen. The narrative is moving deliberately toward the Zechariah scene.

Movement five (vv. 20-22): *the murder of Zechariah*. This is the chapter's theological and literary climax. The Spirit *clothes* Zechariah son of Jehoiada the priest — the same Jehoiada who saved Joash's life as an infant. He stands *mēʿal lā-ʿām* ("above the people," v. 20) and delivers a brief, devastating oracle: *kōh ʾāmar hāʾĕlōhīm lāmmāh ʾattem ʿōbĕrīm ʾet-miṣwōt YHWH wĕ-lōʾ taṣlīḥū kī-ʿăzabtem ʾet-YHWH wa-yyaʿăzōb ʾetkem* ("thus says God, why do you transgress the commandments of the LORD, so that you cannot prosper? Because you have forsaken the LORD, he has forsaken you"). The wordplay — *ʿăzabtem... wa-yyaʿăzōb* — is exact: you have forsaken him, he has forsaken you. The conspiracy against Zechariah is *bĕ-miṣwat ha-melekh* ("by command of the king," v. 21) — explicitly Joash's responsibility. He is stoned *bĕ-ḥăṣar bēyt YHWH* ("in the court of the house of the LORD") — the same temple court that hid him as an infant in 22:12. The Chronicler closes the scene with Zechariah's dying words: *yēreʾ YHWH wĕ-yidrōsh* ("may the LORD see and avenge," v. 22). Verse 22's narrator-comment is striking: *wa-lōʾ-zākar Yōʾāsh ha-melekh ha-ḥesed ʾăsher ʿāsāh Yĕhōyādāʿ ʾābīw ʿimmō* — "Joash did not remember the *ḥesed* (covenant kindness) that Jehoiada his father had done with him, but killed his son." The verbs *zākar* (remember) and *ḥesed* (steadfast love) are loaded covenant terms; the king's failure is, at root, a failure of covenant memory.

Movement six (vv. 23-27): *the retribution*. Aram comes up *with few men* (a Chronicler signature: small forces defeat large ones when Yahweh fights, large defeat small when Yahweh withdraws), destroys the princes, sends spoil to Damascus. Joash is left severely wounded; his servants conspire against him *biděmê bĕnê Yĕhōyādāʿ ha-kōhēn* ("because of the blood of the sons of Jehoiada the priest"); they kill him on his bed. The two named conspirators — Zabad son of Shimeath *the Ammonite*, and Jehozabad son of Shimrith *the Moabite* — are foreigners, their mothers' nationalities specified in a final note of irony: a king whose temple-renewal began the chapter is killed by foreign-born servants while in his bed. He is buried in the city of David but *not in the tombs of the kings* (v. 25), the same dishonor given to Jehoram — and pointedly *not* the honor given to Jehoiada. The Chronicler closes the chapter with reference to "the Story of the Book of the Kings" — one of his cited sources.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

In the Hebrew canonical order Chronicles closes the *Tanakh* — Genesis is the first book, 2 Chronicles the last — and this canonical placement gives chapter 24 a position of extraordinary weight. The murder of Zechariah son of Jehoiada is the last named act of murderous violence in the Hebrew Bible. From *Abel*, the first murdered righteous man in Genesis 4, to *Zechariah*, the last named murdered righteous man in 2 Chronicles 24, the Hebrew canon brackets all human history within a frame of *blood crying for justice*. This canonical bracket is the single most important interpretive datum for understanding Jesus's words in Matthew 23:35 and Luke 11:51. Brevard Childs (*Introduction to the Old Testament as Scripture*) and Richard Bauckham (*The Bible in the Contemporary World*) have argued that Jesus's *from the blood of righteous Abel to the blood of Zechariah* spans the entire Hebrew Bible — every righteous martyr from the first book to the last — and that this *canonical-bracket* effect is lost in the Christian-canonical order, where the prophets and writings rearrange the sequence and 2 Chronicles is no longer last.

The New Testament use is extensive and theologically loaded. Matthew 23:35: *hopōs elthē eph' hymas pan haima dikaion ekchynnomenon epi tēs gēs apo tou haimatos Habel tou dikaiou heōs tou haimatos Zachariou huiou Barachiou hon ephoneusate metaxy tou naou kai tou thysiastēriou* — "that on you may come all the righteous blood shed on the earth, from the blood of righteous Abel to the blood of Zechariah son of Berechiah, whom you murdered between the sanctuary and the altar." Luke 11:51 has the parallel without "son of Berechiah." The phrase *between the sanctuary and the altar* (*metaxy tou naou kai tou thysiastēriou*) precisely matches the Chronicler's *bĕ-ḥăṣar bēyt YHWH* (the temple court, where the altar of burnt offering stood between the sanctuary entrance and the outer court).

The famous difficulty: Matthew specifies "Zechariah son of *Berechiah*," but the Zechariah of 2 Chr 24 is son of *Jehoiada*. Zechariah son of Berechiah is the post-exilic prophet of Zech 1:1. This is one of the most discussed exegetical puzzles in the Synoptic Gospels. Davies and Allison (*Matthew*, ICC) catalog the major positions. (1) *Textual confusion*: Matthew or his source confused the two Zechariahs; Luke preserves the tradition without the patronymic, suggesting the *son of Berechiah* may be a Matthean redactional addition. (2) *Deliberate conflation*: Matthew is intentionally fusing the two, perhaps because both faced violent traditions (Zech 1:1's prophet has rabbinic legends of martyrdom). (3) *Late tradition*: a later scribe added "son of Berechiah" to harmonize with the prophet Zechariah. (4) *Different Zechariah*: Josephus (*Jewish War* 4.5.4) records a Zechariah son of Baris/Baruch killed in the temple in 67–68 CE during the First Jewish Revolt; some scholars (going back to early Christian commentators) wondered whether Jesus's words were prophetic of this event, but the chronology is awkward. John Goldingay and most contemporary commentators settle on a combination of (1) and (2): the Synoptic tradition variously preserved the Zechariah-of-Chronicles reference, with the patronymic later either added in error or in conflation. The honest engagement is that there is real difficulty, and the canonical-bracket reading from Abel to Zechariah of 2 Chronicles works whether or not we resolve the patronymic problem.

In Jewish reception the chapter's gravity has been recognized for two thousand years. The Talmud (*b. Gittin* 57b; *b. Sanhedrin* 96b) preserves a tradition that Zechariah's blood continued to boil on the temple floor for centuries until Nebuzaradan, the Babylonian general, finally pacified it through massacre — a haggadic tradition expressing the depth of the bloodguilt. Rashi (eleventh century) reads the chapter as the paradigmatic case of *kafui tov* — ingratitude — in which Joash forgets the *ḥesed* of the priest who saved his life. Radak (twelfth–thirteenth century) emphasizes the canonical placement: the last murder narrated in the Hebrew Bible is fitting for the last book. Abarbanel (fifteenth century) takes the chapter as a meditation on the dependence of kings on their advisers — Joash without Jehoiada is no king at all. Sara Japhet reads chapter 24 as the Chronicler's clearest statement that *covenant memory* (*zākar*) is the load-bearing virtue of any leadership. Isaac Kalimi catalogs the chapter as one of the most carefully bilateral structures in Chronicles.

Christian reception has been substantial. Jerome's Vulgate, Cassiodorus, and the medieval lectionary all treated Zechariah's martyrdom as a foundational text for the *blood of the righteous* tradition. Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* II-II q. 124 on martyrdom) cites Zechariah son of Jehoiada as one of the foundational martyrs whose witness anticipates Christian martyr-theology. Calvin's 1561 lectures on Chronicles read the chapter as a hard demonstration that institutional memory dies fast — Joash cannot remember the priest who saved him as soon as the priest is in the ground. Modern Christian commentary (Williamson, Klein, Selman, Pratt, Hill) reads chapter 24 as a meditation on the danger of borrowed faith — Joash's righteousness was Jehoiada's; once Jehoiada was gone, Joash had no faith of his own. The contemporary preaching tradition (in both Catholic and evangelical settings) often pairs 2 Chr 24 with Hebrews 11:32-38 (the unnamed martyrs "of whom the world was not worthy") as a meditation on the long history of righteous blood.

## Lens 4: Theological Intent

The Chronicler's agenda in chapter 24 is the *fragility of borrowed faith* and the *gravity of righteous blood*. The chapter is the Chronicler's clearest demonstration that personal covenant fidelity cannot be inherited — Joash's righteousness was held in trust by Jehoiada, and when the trustee died the deposit vanished. The chapter is also the Hebrew Bible's most pointed claim that the blood of the righteous shed in temple-space is the gravest possible covenantal offense, with consequences that ripple through subsequent biblical theology and into the New Testament's *Abel-to-Zechariah* canonical bracket.

Three tensions deserve careful engagement.

First: *the Hebrew-canonical Abel-to-Zechariah bracket*. This is one of the most productive interpretive frames in contemporary biblical scholarship. Three positions: (1) *Strong canonical-bracket reading* (Childs, Bauckham, Beale): Jesus's words in Matt 23:35 and Luke 11:51 are deliberately citing the Hebrew canonical bracket — the first and last named righteous-blood murders in the Tanakh — and the canonical placement of 2 Chronicles as the last book is theologically load-bearing. The reading reveals an underlying claim: *all righteous blood from Genesis to 2 Chronicles*, the entire Hebrew canon, is being summed up in Jesus's accusation. (2) *Weak canonical-bracket reading* (more critical scholars, partly Davies-Allison): Jesus or the Synoptic tradition picked Zechariah-of-2-Chronicles because it was *a* prominent murdered prophet, not because of the canonical placement; the bracket-effect is a happy accident rather than a deliberate construction. (3) *Mixed reading*: the bracket-effect is real and theologically meaningful, but Jesus may not have constructed it consciously; the Chronicler's positioning at the end of the canon and Jesus's choice of Zechariah jointly produce an interpretive frame that the church recognizes retrospectively. The steelman of position 1: the Hebrew-canonical order was already largely fixed by the first century (Josephus and 4 Ezra both group Chronicles with the Writings); Jesus would have known this; the choice of Abel-and-Zechariah is theologically too apt to be accidental. Where this leaves us: the canonical-bracket reading is the dominant framework in contemporary scholarship, and it offers a reading of New Testament use of the Hebrew Bible that takes canon-shape seriously. The Christian-canonical order with its different sequence loses this effect, which is one reason the Hebrew-canonical order is theologically valuable to recover.

Second: *the son-of-Berechiah problem*. Three positions, fairly summarized: (1) *Matthean redactional error or scribal harmonization*: the patronymic conflicts with 2 Chr 24 and most likely entered the textual tradition by accident or harmonization with Zech 1:1. (2) *Deliberate conflation*: Matthew or his source intentionally merged the two Zechariahs to make a theological point about the long history of murdered prophecy. (3) *Different Zechariah entirely*: the Josephan Zechariah son of Baris is a distinct possibility, but chronology makes it awkward as a referent for Jesus's words in his earthly ministry. The honest position is that we do not know for certain. The canonical-bracket reading does not depend on resolving the patronymic — even if Matthew's "son of Berechiah" is a textual anomaly, the underlying Zechariah-of-2-Chronicles reference holds in Luke 11:51 and in the substantive content of "between the sanctuary and the altar."

Third: *the temple-violence theme*. The chapter's most disturbing fact is that the murder happens *in the temple court*. The same court that hid Joash in chapter 22; the same temple Joash spent the first half of chapter 24 repairing; the same sacred space whose purity Jehoiada protected even during the coup of chapter 23. Three positions on what to do with this: (1) *Theological intensification* (most confessional commentary): the violence in the holy place compounds the offense; the Chronicler is showing how far apostasy can reach when leadership memory fails. (2) *Honest reckoning with sacred-space violence* (Walter Brueggemann; some pastoral commentary): the chapter forces us to acknowledge that holy spaces themselves do not protect against human violence; the church and synagogue have known this through centuries of murder in sanctuaries. (3) *Eschatological-prophetic* reading (linking to Christ's death "outside the gate," Heb 13:12, and to martyr traditions): the murder of the prophet in the temple court is the proper home of all violence against righteous witness — the holy precincts cannot contain it, and the cross is in some sense already adumbrated here. Where this leaves us: the chapter does not resolve the difficulty of sacred-space violence; it lets the bloodguilt stand. Zechariah's dying words — *yēreʾ YHWH wĕ-yidrōsh*, "may the LORD see and avenge" — are not a prayer the Chronicler softens, and the chapter ends with that prayer answered through Aramean invasion and the king's bedside murder. The vengeance is granted, but not in a way that gives anyone comfort.

The deeper question the chapter poses: the Chronicler's *immediate-retribution* schema fits Joash's murder neatly enough — kill the prophet, get killed yourself — but the canonical-bracket reading suggests that righteous blood does not stop crying out in one generation. From Abel through Zechariah and onward to the New Testament's invocation of these names, the cry of the righteous shed on holy ground accumulates. The Chronicler ends his book three chapters later with the exile and the rebuilding decree of Cyrus. The reader is left to wonder whether the *cry of Zechariah* is finally answered in the post-exilic restoration, or whether it remains, like Abel's blood, *crying from the ground* through every age that fails its prophets.

Closing question: when our institutions have a Jehoiada and a Joash and a Zechariah — when borrowed faith, institutional memory, and the prophets who tell us the truth all coexist within the same building — would we recognize ourselves as the king or as the king's princes, and what *ḥesed* are we in danger of forgetting the moment our last Jehoiada is buried?

## The Thread

— *The blood of Abel* (Gen 4:10) crying from the ground reaches its Hebrew-canonical bracket-mate at 2 Chr 24:21-22 — *Zechariah's blood*.
— Matt 23:35 / Luke 11:51 cite the canonical bracket as the sum of all righteous blood.
— The temple-tax of Moses (Exod 30:11-16) is invoked at 2 Chr 24:6, 9, foreshadowing post-exilic Neh 10:32-33.
— *Ḥesed* and *zākar* (covenant memory) connect the chapter's failure (Joash forgets Jehoiada's *ḥesed*, v. 22) to the broader Davidic-covenant tradition.
