# 2 Kings 10: The Heaps of Heads, the Latrine, and the Calves That Stayed

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter completes the Jehu coup begun in chapter 9, narrating the systematic extermination of the Omride dynasty (vv. 1-17), the slaughter of Ahaziah's Judahite relatives (vv. 12-14), the temple-massacre of Baal worshipers at Samaria (vv. 18-28), the dtr's evaluation of Jehu's incomplete reform (vv. 29-31), and the chapter's grim coda — Hazael's territorial seizures from Israel (vv. 32-33), Jehu's death-and-succession formula (vv. 34-36). The chronology lands the coup and its aftermath in c. 842-841 BCE, with Jehu's twenty-eight-year reign extending to c. 814 BCE.

The most consequential extrabiblical witness to this chapter is the **Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III** (~825 BCE), excavated by Henry Layard at Calah (modern Nimrud) in 1846 and now in the British Museum (BM 118885). On its second register from the top, the relief depicts a figure in a fringed robe prostrating before Shalmaneser and presenting tribute; the cuneiform caption reads *ma-da-tu sa Ia-ú-a mār Hu-um-ri-i*: "Tribute of Jehu son of Omri." The image is the only specific Israelite king visually depicted in any Assyrian or other foreign monument. Its dating (~825 BCE) places it within Jehu's twenty-eight-year reign — and the "son of Omri" designation is one of the most ironic details in the entire ancient Near Eastern epigraphic corpus: Jehu had wiped out Omri's house in 842 BCE, but the Assyrians continued to call the polity *bit Humri* ("House of Omri") and Jehu its current "son of Omri" — the dynastic-political category Assyria established in the 880s for Israel proved more durable than the dynasty itself. **Mordechai Cogan and Hayim Tadmor** (Anchor Bible *2 Kings*) treat the Obelisk as the chapter's external anchor — Jehu's tribute to Shalmaneser is the geopolitical price of the territorial losses to Hazael (vv. 32-33), with Israel switching its alignment from Aramean partner to Assyrian client in a generation.

The chapter's archaeological geography is dense. **Samaria** — the Omride capital — was excavated by **George Reisner** (1908-1910), **John W. Crowfoot** (1931-1935), and restudied by **Ron Tappy** (*The Archaeology of Israelite Samaria*, 1992, 2001). Tappy's Building Periods I-II preserve the Omride palace platform, casemate fortification, and elite residential quarter; the **Samaria Ostraca** and the **ivory house material** (cf. Amos 3:15, "houses of ivory") show the cultural sophistication of the city Jehu inherited. The "house of Baal" (*bet ha-ba'al*, vv. 21, 23, 25-27) is not yet definitively located archaeologically.

**Beth-eked of the Shepherds** (*Bet-eked ha-ro'im*, vv. 12, 14) is a small site between Jezreel and Samaria — possibly Beit Qad, c. 5 km east of Jenin (so the *Anchor Bible Dictionary* identification). The "pit" (*bor*, בּוֹר) where the forty-two Judahite relatives are slaughtered is a cistern or perhaps a sheep-shearing tank attached to the way-station. The narrative detail of forty-two persons (a precise count, not a round number) is preserved as historical-circumstantial; these are not random Judahite travelers but, per their own statement, family members of the murdered Ahaziah on a courtesy visit to the Israelite court — the Davidic-Omride intermarriage's collateral damage.

**Bethel and Dan** (v. 29) — the two royal sanctuaries founded by Jeroboam I (1 Kgs 12:26-33) — are the cult centers Jehu does *not* dismantle. **Tel Dan** has been excavated by Avraham Biran (1966-1999) and recently by Daniel Master. The Iron II high-place complex (Areas T and B), with its monumental staircase, *bamah*, and a horned altar fragment recovered in 1979, gives material form to the cult Jehu preserves. The Tel Dan inscription (the stele relevant to chs. 8-9) was found in secondary use near the gate complex — Hazael probably erected it after defeating Joram; it was later broken up and reused.

**Jehonadab son of Rechab** (vv. 15, 23) is the founder of the Rechabites, a Yahwistic ascetic sectarian group whose later witness Jeremiah will record (Jer 35) — they refused wine, permanent housing, agriculture; Jehonadab's alliance with Jehu links the purge to ninth-century Israel's most uncompromisingly Yahwistic community.

Three Hebrew terms shape the chapter. **Qin'ah** (קִנְאָה, "zeal," v. 16) — Jehu's "see my zeal for the LORD" — is the same root that describes God's "jealousy" (Ex 20:5) and Phinehas's covenant-zeal (Num 25:13). Phinehas's zeal averts plague; Jehu's zeal extinguishes a dynasty; the chapter invokes the term knowingly. **'Oqvah** (בְּעָקְבָה, "with cunning," v. 19) shares its root with Jacob's name (*ya'aqov*), narratively marking Jehu's deception as Jacob-style trickery. **Moḥera'ot** (מוֹצָאוֹת, "latrine," v. 27) — the temple of Baal becomes a public toilet "to this day"; the vocabulary is deliberately scatological-polemical and matches broader prophetic anti-Baal rhetoric (Hos 9:10, Jer 11:13).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre-precisely, ch. 10 is a continuation of the regicide/coup narrative of ch. 9 with the addition of dtr regnal-summary materials at the end. Robert Alter and Walsh both treat the chapter as the second half of a single literary unit (chs. 9-10) — the anointing, the killing of the kings, the killing of the queen, the extermination of the dynasty, the temple-purge. The chapter's distinct subgenre is the *administrative-violence narrative*: Jehu rules by letter (vv. 1-7), by ambush (vv. 12-14), by alliance (vv. 15-17), by ruse (vv. 18-28). The chapter is procedural in its violence — the narrator's eye is on the mechanics of dynastic extinction.

The chapter's literary architecture is built on a triple-purge structure: (a) the seventy sons of Ahab in Samaria (vv. 1-11), (b) the forty-two Judahite relatives at Beth-eked (vv. 12-14), (c) the Baal-worshipers in the Samaria temple (vv. 18-28). Each purge is differently structured. The first uses the threat of the elders' own complicity to make them do the killing — Jehu writes a letter, the elders respond with the heads in baskets, Jehu disclaims responsibility while owning the prophetic authorization (vv. 9-10). The second is direct ambush — Jehu meets the Judahites on the road and orders their slaughter at the pit. The third is ritual deception — Jehu announces a great Baal-sacrifice, gathers all the worshipers, seals the temple, and orders the eighty soldiers to slaughter them inside.

Devices: dramatic irony saturates the temple-purge. The reader knows Jehu's "I will serve Baal much" (v. 18) is a ruse (the narrator tells us at v. 19, "but Jehu did it with cunning"); the Baal-worshipers hear the same line as a religious commitment from the new king and respond with full attendance. The "vestments for the worshipers of Baal" (v. 22) — special cultic garments brought from a wardrobe — serve a double narrative function: they signal to the reader that the temple-massacre is total (no Yahwist will be killed by mistake because the vestment-distinction is made explicit at v. 23) and they also create a visual moment in which the worshipers are dressed for slaughter. The narrator does not pause to evaluate; the chapter moves at the pace of execution.

The "two heaps at the entrance of the gate" (v. 8) is the chapter's most arresting visual detail. Cogan-Tadmor and the broader scholarly tradition note the Assyrian parallel: Neo-Assyrian royal annals frequently describe the public display of severed heads as a mode of political signaling (cf. Ashurnasirpal II's annals, where heaps of heads are listed by city). The biblical narrator deploys a familiar Assyrian visual idiom against the Omride dynasty's own elite. The "you are innocent" speech (v. 9) — Jehu standing at the gate the next morning, telling the gathered population that they are not guilty of the killings he himself orchestrated through the elders — is one of the most morally textured speeches in the historical books. **Robert Alter** (*The Hebrew Bible*, vol. 2) reads it as a deliberate rhetorical move: Jehu owns his killing of Joram (the public coup) and disclaims his orchestration of the seventy sons (which the elders technically executed). The dtr historian preserves the speech without explicit comment.

Sternberg (*The Poetics of Biblical Narrative*) and Polzin both note the chapter's structural shift at v. 29 — after the relentless violence of vv. 1-28, the dtr historian abruptly inserts a verbatim reuse of the Jeroboam-judgment formula ("but Jehu did not turn aside from the sins of Jeroboam"). The chapter's first-order narrative voice celebrates the purge; the dtr's evaluative frame condemns its incompleteness. This is the chapter's most important interpretive seam: the same chapter that describes the Baal-temple becoming a latrine ("to this day") also notes that the golden calves of Bethel and Dan remain. The reform is partial; the dtr's verdict is mixed.

Vv. 32-33 (Hazael's territorial seizures) is the chapter's grim political coda — Israel loses Transjordan (Gilead, Bashan, the territories of Reuben, Gad, eastern Manasseh from the Arnon north) within Jehu's reign. The dtr is signaling that the geopolitical price of the Aramean realignment under Jehu is the loss of the Trans-Jordan, fulfilling Elisha's weeping-prophecy at 8:12. The chapter ends with the standard regnal summary formula (vv. 34-36) — the source-citation of the *Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Israel*, the burial-and-succession note, the regnal length — placing the coup-narrative within the dtr's standard administrative framework.

Textual variants: 4QKgs preserves only minor fragments. The LXX (Vaticanus) has standard variants — particularly in v. 22 (the wardrobe-keeper detail), v. 27 (*moḥera'ot*, the latrine, where the LXX renders with an explicit Greek term for public toilet, indicating the translator caught the polemical force). Susanne Otto and Robert Coote both identify multiple compositional layers — a pre-dtr Jehuite apologetic core (vv. 1-28), a dtr evaluative overlay (vv. 29-31), a separate political-historical note on Hazael's seizures (vv. 32-33), and the standard regnal-summary closure (vv. 34-36).

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The Jewish trajectory: **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 9.124-139) retells the chapter at length with characteristic political smoothing. He preserves the seventy heads, the Beth-eked massacre, the Baal-temple ruse — but emphasizes the prophetic-divine warrant throughout, treating Jehu as a justly-zealous Yahwistic king. **Rashi** addresses the chapter's moral complexity — he notes the dtr's verdict at v. 31 ("Jehu was not careful to walk in the law") and reads the partial reform as the seed of the Jehuite dynasty's eventual fall. **Radak (David Kimhi)** treats the seventy sons' deaths in detail — he notes that the elders, not Jehu directly, executed the killings, and reads Jehu's "you are innocent" speech (v. 9) as moral sleight-of-hand. **Ralbag (Gersonides)** treats Jehu's preservation of the Bethel-Dan calves as the chapter's central theological problem: the king who eradicates Baal cannot bring himself to dismantle the Yahwistic-but-iconographic state cult, because the latter underwrites his own dynastic legitimacy in the north. The political-religious limits of reform are the chapter's deeper subject. **Abarbanel** writes at length on the chapter's political theology — he reads the Jehu coup as a paradigmatic case of providential overthrow that nevertheless fails to attain its full theological potential because the executor's own political interests intervene; the dtr's mixed verdict is the canonical recognition of this. **Avivah Zornberg's** psychological readings treat the chapter as a study in the moral degradation that accompanies sustained instrumentalized violence — Jehu begins as the prophetically-anointed instrument and ends as the dynastically-secured monarch whose own theology has become limited by the political settlement his violence purchased. **Yair Zakovitch** treats the chapter as the closing panel of the literary masterpiece begun in ch. 9, with deliberate symmetries between the killings of the kings (ch. 9) and the killings of the dynasty's extended family (ch. 10).

The Christian trajectory: **Origen** (catena fragments) reads the temple-purge typologically — the Baal-temple becoming a latrine as the figure of the soul's idolatries reduced to filth in Christian conversion. **Augustine** (*City of God* 18 implicitly; *Contra Faustum* on biblical violence more directly) addresses the chapter's moral problem head-on: how can the same God who commands "thou shalt not kill" warrant Jehu's mass killings? Augustine's answer — that prophetic warrant in particular cases does not abrogate the general moral law but operates as a divinely-authorized exception — has shaped every subsequent Christian engagement with the chapter. **Theodoret of Cyrus** (*Quaestiones in Reges*) treats the chapter at length, defending Jehu's commission as proper prophetic warrant but addressing Hosea 1:4's eventual indictment as the canonical recognition that even rightly-warranted violence carries moral debt. **Chrysostom** uses the Baal-temple passage homiletically as the standard illustration of the proper destruction of idolatry. **Gregory the Great** (*Moralia in Job*) cites Jehu's incomplete reform (vv. 29-31) as the type of the soul that abolishes one besetting sin while cherishing another. **Aquinas** (*Summa* II-II q. 64, on killing; q. 42, on sedition; q. 94, on idolatry) treats the chapter as a paradigm for the question of divinely-warranted violence against idolatry. **Calvin's sermons on 2 Kings** (1561-1562, partially preserved) are extensive on this chapter — Calvin defends the Baal-temple massacre as proper execution of the first commandment but is unsparing about Jehu's preservation of the Bethel-Dan calves, reading it as the clearest possible evidence that political-religious reform is always incomplete in its actual instantiations and requires the eschatological completion only the gospel can effect. **Luther** in his marginalia treats the chapter as a sermon on the difficulty of complete reform. The critical-historical commentaries — **Cogan & Tadmor (AB 2 Kings)**, **Hobbs (WBC)**, **Sweeney (OTL)**, **John Gray (OTL)**, **Mark S. Smith** on the Baal polemics, **Susanne Otto** on the Jehuite cycle, **Robert Coote** (*In Defense of Revolution*) on the chapter as Jehuite court apologetic — give the dense scholarly base. **Frank Moore Cross**, **Richard Nelson**, **Gary Knoppers** on the dtr framework. **Brueggemann** (*1 & 2 Kings*) reads the chapter as the OT's most direct meditation on the moral cost of revolutionary violence and the structural impossibility of pure religious-political reform. **Eugene Peterson** (Westminster Bible Companion) reads the chapter pastorally as a study in the theology of zeal — Jehu's zeal both achieves and corrupts. **Iain Provan** (NIBC), **John Goldingay** (*1 & 2 Kings for Everyone*), **Ellen Davis** (*Opening Israel's Scriptures*) all emphasize the chapter's mixed verdict. **Robert Coote** reads the entire chapter as Jehuite court apologetic preserved by the dtr but eventually overruled by the prophetic canon, with **Mark S. Smith**'s work on the Baal cult illuminating the religious-historical depth of the temple-purge. Feminist scholars — **Janet Howe Gaines**, **Tina Pippin**, **Cheryl Exum** — give less attention to ch. 10 than to ch. 9 (the chapter has no women narrators), but Gaines treats Jezebel's seventy sons' deaths as the structural completion of the violence against the Phoenician royal house begun with Jezebel herself.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Hosea 1:4 versus the dtr's celebration of Jehu's prophetic fulfillment.**

Position A (dtr-apologetic, the chapter's first-order voice): Jehu does what Elijah's prophecy required (1 Kgs 21:21-24); the chapter is a sustained celebration of prophetic word fulfilled; the LORD himself confirms the work at v. 30 ("because you have done well in carrying out what is right in my eyes... your sons of the fourth generation shall sit on the throne of Israel"). Position B (Hosea 1:4-incorporated, canonical-prophetic): Hosea (c. 750 BCE) names "the blood of Jezreel" — the specific geography of Jehu's killings — as something God will *avenge on the house of Jehu*, directly indicting the chapter's violence. The canon contains its own self-correction. Position C (synthetic, Calvin and the Reformed tradition): the prophetic word can be rightly executed and the executor still be morally culpable for the manner of execution; the dtr's celebration and Hosea's indictment are both correct; God can use a Jehu and still hold him accountable.

Steelman of position B: Hosea 1:4 is not metaphorical — the "blood of Jezreel" is the specific blood Jehu spilled at Jezreel in 2 Kings 9 (Joram, Jezebel) and at Jezreel-area in 2 Kings 10 (the seventy heads delivered to Jezreel). Hosea is naming the chapter's specific violence as itself bloodguilt. The fact that the dtr can preserve Jehu's prophetic warrant *and* the canon can preserve Hosea's indictment is one of the most striking cases of internal canonical argument in the OT — the historical and the prophetic books are not in agreement on this question, and neither is suppressed in favor of the other.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter's first-order voice is dtr celebration of prophecy fulfilled with a critical evaluative overlay (vv. 29-31) noting incomplete reform; the canon's larger voice (read with Hosea) is moral indictment of the violence by which the prophecy was fulfilled. The reader is asked to hold both — to see the chapter's theological satisfaction at Baal's destruction and the prophetic indictment of the violence' excess as both true and both belonging to the canon. The political-theological argument the chapter makes (Yahwistic state-religion is achievable through coup violence) is precisely what Hosea will reject.

**Tension 2: Jehu's preservation of the Bethel-Dan calves (v. 29) as either pragmatic statecraft or theological compromise.**

Position A (theological-pure, Calvin and the prophetic tradition): the calves were idolatrous from Jeroboam's first installation (1 Kgs 12:28-30); Jehu's preservation of them is straightforwardly idolatrous; his "zeal for the LORD" (v. 16) is exposed as selective zeal, and the dtr's verdict at v. 31 is precisely correct. Position B (political-pragmatic, sociologically realistic): the Bethel-Dan calves were the religious legitimization of the northern kingdom as a separate polity from the Davidic south; dismantling them would have required Jehu to either reunify with the south (politically impossible after centuries of separation) or invent an entirely new northern Yahwistic state-cult; he chose the politically-feasible reform (eradicate Baal) over the politically-impossible one (eradicate the calves), and the chapter is honest about the trade-off. Position C (canonical-eschatological, Christian and some Jewish): the chapter's mixed verdict points forward — neither Jehu nor any subsequent northern king will succeed in completing the reform; the canonical hope for true Yahwistic restoration shifts to a future agency that the historical books cannot provide.

Steelman of position B: the calves had stood for 140 years by the time of Jehu's coup; they were embedded in the northern political-religious infrastructure; Jehu inherited them as an institutional reality, not as a new innovation. The chapter does not pretend otherwise — it states the fact directly at v. 29 with the dtr's standard formula. The political-pragmatic reading does not exonerate Jehu; it explains him.

**Where this leaves us:** The chapter holds both the religious purity standard (v. 31's verdict) and the political reality (vv. 29-30's preservation of the calves and divine endorsement of Jehu's dynasty for four generations) without resolution. The canon presents the partial reform as both inadequate (by the standard of Yahwistic exclusivity) and as the maximum politically feasible at the moment (by the standard of what Jehu could actually do). Both judgments are true.

**Unresolved question:** What does it mean that the same divine voice that endorses Jehu's reform with a four-generation dynastic promise (v. 30) also presides — through Hosea — over the eventual extinction of that dynasty for the violence of the very killings the dynastic promise rewards? The chapter does not say. The canon's two voices are preserved without harmonization.

## The Thread

Cross-references: 1 Kgs 21:21-24 (Elijah's prophecy of dynastic extinction) is the prophetic word the chapter is fulfilling, and Jehu cites it explicitly at vv. 10, 17. 1 Kgs 12:26-33 (Jeroboam's calves at Bethel and Dan) is the cultic background to v. 29's note that Jehu preserves them. Jeremiah 35 (the Rechabites under Jeremiah) preserves the long-term witness of the Jehonadab tradition introduced at vv. 15-16; the Rechabite sectarian commitment to nomadic-Yahwistic life will outlast both the Jehuite dynasty and the northern kingdom by centuries.

Forward flag: **Hosea 1:4-5** ("I will avenge the blood of Jezreel on the house of Jehu, and I will put an end to the kingdom of the house of Israel") is the prophetic indictment that will overturn the chapter's apparent endorsement; Hosea writes c. 750 BCE, two generations into the Jehuite dynasty's decline, and his prophecy will be fulfilled in 722 BCE with Samaria's fall to Sargon II. The **Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III** (~825 BCE, British Museum) — visually attesting "Jehu son of Omri" prostrating before the Assyrian king — is the chapter's external geopolitical witness; the territorial losses to Hazael (vv. 32-33) drove Jehu into the Assyrian tribute system within his own lifetime. Israel's eventual destruction at the hands of Assyria's expansion (chs. 17, 18) is the structural consequence of the geopolitical realignment this chapter inaugurates. The Bethel-Dan calves Jehu preserved (v. 29) will be cited again and again in the dtr's continuing condemnation of the northern kingdom and will eventually be carried off when the Assyrians destroy Samaria (ch. 17). The chapter's mixed verdict — Yahwistic in its destruction of Baal, idolatrous in its preservation of the calves, blood-guilty in its violence — is the dtr's structural template for the entire Jehuite era and for the prophetic indictment that will eventually consume it. Two extrabiblical inscriptions (the Tel Dan Stele for chs. 8-9; the Black Obelisk for ch. 10), one prophetic indictment (Hosea), and one cult-archaeological witness (Tel Dan's bamah complex) frame the chapter's afterlife in Israel's longer history.
