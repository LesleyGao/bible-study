# Proverbs 25 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World — Hezekiah's Scribes and a Window into OT Literary Production

Proverbs 25 opens with one of the most historically remarkable superscriptions in the entire Old Testament: *"These also are proverbs of Solomon which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied"* (Hebrew *gam-eleh mishlê shelomoh, asher he'tiqu anshê chizqiyyah melekh-yehudah*, גַּם־אֵלֶּה מִשְׁלֵי שְׁלֹמֹה אֲשֶׁר הֶעְתִּיקוּ אַנְשֵׁי חִזְקִיָּה מֶלֶךְ־יְהוּדָה). This single line is one of the **clearest historical witnesses to royal-scribal literary production in the Hebrew Bible** — a named king, a named scribal guild, a named compilation activity, attached to a definite editorial section (chs. 25–29).

**Hezekiah** ruled Judah ca. 715–686 BC (chronologies vary slightly). His reign is well documented in 2 Kings 18–20, 2 Chronicles 29–32, Isaiah 36–39, and remarkably in Assyrian records — the Sennacherib Prism (Taylor Prism, 691 BC) describes Sennacherib's siege of Jerusalem in 701 BC and Hezekiah being shut up "like a bird in a cage." His reign is the historical horizon in which Israel's prophets (First Isaiah) and sages were active. The verb *he'tiqu* (העתיקו) literally means "removed / transferred / copied" — Bruce Waltke (NICOT) argues it means "transcribed and edited," Michael V. Fox (AB) reads it as "redacted," Roland Murphy (WBC) as "transmitted." The semantic range covers anything from straight copying of older Solomonic-era materials to active editorial gathering and compilation.

**Mainstream-critical position** (Fox, Murphy, William P. Brown, Christine Roy Yoder, Will Kynes): the Hezekiah notice is taken as **historically reliable evidence** of late-8th-century scribal-cultural literary production. This is not a matter of theological commitment but of historical-archaeological convergence. The same period saw:
- The compilation of much of the early prophetic corpus (First Isaiah).
- The emergence of substantial Judahite scribal infrastructure (the *bullae* and *seals* archaeologists have recovered from late 8th-c. Jerusalem strata, including the famous *l'melekh* jar handles).
- Significant alphabetic literacy expansion across Judah (Christopher Rollston's epigraphic studies).
- The compilation activity that produced what would become Hezekiah's editorial layer of the Deuteronomistic History (per some critical theories).

Yoder's social-historical work on Proverbs places this scribal-school activity in the temple- and palace-adjacent context of late-8th-c. Jerusalem — sages serving as royal counselors and tutors, gathering older proverbs for use in court training. The chapter's prominent **king-themes** (25:2–7) make sense in exactly this Sitz im Leben: instruction for those who will serve in the royal court.

**Conservative-evangelical readings** (Waltke, Longman, Kidner) accept the same basic historical picture, with stronger emphasis on Solomonic-era authorship of the underlying materials. Both readings agree: Pr 25 is a literary-archaeological artifact of late-8th-c. Judahite scribal culture transmitting older Solomonic wisdom-stock.

The collection continues through ch. 29 and is sometimes called the **Hezekiah collection** or the **Second Solomonic Collection** to distinguish it from chs. 10–22:16. It contains some of the most famous proverbs in the entire book — *apples of gold in settings of silver* (25:11), *if your enemy is hungry, give him bread to eat* (25:21–22, quoted by Paul in Romans 12:20), *iron sharpens iron* (27:17), *where there is no vision, the people perish* (29:18).

## Lens 2: The Literature — Comparison-Proverbs and Royal-Court Rhetoric

Pr 25 returns to **sentence-proverb** form after the longer instructional discourses of 22:17–24:34. But the sentence-proverbs of chs. 25–27 differ stylistically from those of chs. 10–22:16: where the earlier collection favored **antithetic parallelism** ("the righteous… but the wicked…"), the Hezekiah collection favors **comparison-proverbs** — extended similes and metaphors that draw analogies from nature, agriculture, and craft. This stylistic shift is one of the strongest internal markers of distinct collection-origin.

The chapter is dense with simile-proverbs:
- *"like apples of gold in a setting of silver"* (25:11) — *tappuchê zahav be-maskiyyot kasef* (תַּפּוּחֵי זָהָב בְּמַשְׂכִּיּוֹת כָּסֶף). The Hebrew *tappuach* may not refer to apples specifically but to a round golden ornament; *maskit* is a carved or filigreed silver setting. The image is an exquisite craft-object: fine words in fine craftsmanship.
- *"like the cold of snow in the time of harvest"* (25:13) — refreshment in heat; the snow is presumably from Mount Hermon, brought down for cooling drinks.
- *"like clouds and wind without rain"* (25:14) — promise without delivery, devastating in an agrarian economy.
- *"like a war club, or a sword, or a sharp arrow"* (25:18) — false witness as a weapon.
- *"like a bad tooth or a foot that slips"* (25:19) — unreliable allies as physical betrayals.
- *"like one who takes off a garment on a cold day, and like vinegar on soda"* (25:20) — songs to a heavy heart (the *vinegar on soda* image is chemically precise; the reaction destroys both).
- *"like cold water to a thirsty soul"* (25:25) — good news from far away.
- *"like a muddied spring or a polluted fountain"* (25:26) — a righteous man giving way to the wicked.
- *"like a city broken into and left without walls"* (25:28) — the man without self-control.

**Key Hebrew terms:**
- **כָּבוֹד** (*kavod*, "glory") — 25:2. *Kevod elohim hester davar, ukevod melakhim chaqor davar* (כְּבֹד אֱלֹהִים הַסְתֵּר דָּבָר וּכְבֹד מְלָכִים חֲקֹר דָּבָר), "the glory of God is to conceal a matter; the glory of kings is to search it out." A profound theological epigram on the asymmetry of divine and royal knowledge.
- **דָּבָר עַל־אָפְנָיו** (*davar 'al-ofnav*, "a word on its proper occasions") — 25:11. *Ofnav* may relate to *ofan*, a wheel — words that turn properly in their setting. The phrase has produced extensive Jewish and Christian midrash on the timing of speech.
- **גַּחֲלֵי־אֵשׁ** (*gachalê-esh*, "burning coals") — 25:22. *"You will heap burning coals on his head."* The most disputed image in the chapter (treated below).
- **רֶגֶל מוּעָדֶת** (*regel mu'adet*, "a foot that slips") — 25:19. The verb *ma'ad* describes a foot that gives way; the image of friend-betrayal as physical un-footing.
- **חוֹמָה** (*chomah*, "wall") — 25:28. The defensive city-wall; its absence renders the city defenseless. Self-control (*motser le-rucho*, מֹצֵר לְרוּחוֹ, "restraining his spirit") is the inner-city wall.

The chapter's **opening cluster** (25:2–7) on royal court behavior is unified by setting: *court of the king*. The remainder is more loosely arranged, though scholars detect catchword links (the *honey* of 25:16 and 25:27; the *quarrelsome wife* of 25:24, doublet of 21:9).

## Lens 3: The Larger Story — Wisdom Canon, Jewish Trajectory, Christian Trajectory

**Within the wisdom canon**, Pr 25 develops three threads with extensive downstream reception:

1. **The glory of concealment and the glory of searching** (25:2) — anticipates Eccl 3:11 (*"He has put eternity into man's heart, yet so that he cannot find out what God has done from the beginning to the end"*) and Job 28's hymn on wisdom's hiddenness. The asymmetry is theological: God's glory is mysterious; human (and royal) glory is investigative. This is the closest the OT comes to a *theology of inquiry*.

2. **A word fitly spoken** (25:11) — joined by Pr 15:23, 16:24, and especially Eccl 3:7 (*"a time to keep silence, and a time to speak"*) and Eccl 12:11 (*"the words of the wise are like goads"*). The cumulative wisdom-tradition emphasis on **timing of speech** as a moral discipline.

3. **Enemy-hospitality** (25:21–22) — joined by Ex 23:4–5 (helping your enemy's animal), Pr 24:17–18 (do not rejoice when your enemy falls), Lev 19:18 (do not take vengeance) — the OT non-retaliation thread already noted in Pr 24, brought here to its sharpest formulation: not just *do not retaliate*, but *actively feed*.

**Jewish trajectory.** Pr 25:2 became foundational to Jewish epistemological humility — the rabbis cite it as warrant for both **mystical concealment** (*kabbalah* literature on the hidden depths of Torah) and **investigative inquiry** (the Talmudic culture of *pilpul*, dialectical analysis). Maimonides (*Guide for the Perplexed* I.59) cites the verse on the limits of human knowledge of God. **25:11** (*apples of gold*) became a Jewish proverb in its own right for the ideal of beautiful, well-timed teaching; medieval Jewish poetry uses the image. **25:21–22** (feeding the enemy) is treated by Maimonides (*Hilkhot De'ot* 6:5) and the *Sefer Mitzvot Gadol* as one of the supreme expressions of *gemilut chasadim* — kindness even to the enemy. The Talmud (*Megillah* 16a, *Berakhot* 17a) develops the ethic. **25:28** (the city without walls) became a foundational text in Jewish *mussar* (ethical-discipline) literature on self-control.

**Christian trajectory.** The chapter's reception is dominated by three texts:

- **25:21–22** (enemy-hospitality and burning coals). **Paul quotes this verse verbatim in Romans 12:20**, making it the OT proof-text for the Christian non-retaliation ethic. *"Beloved, never avenge yourselves… 'if your enemy is hungry, feed him; if he is thirsty, give him something to drink; for by so doing you will heap burning coals on his head.' Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good."* The verse passed through Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin, Luther, the Anabaptists, MLK, Bonhoeffer — every major Christian tradition that has reckoned with the ethics of conflict has had to engage Pr 25:21–22 in its Romans 12 frame.

- **25:11** (a word fitly spoken). Quoted across Christian preaching and pastoral literature; central to Jonathan Edwards's writings on holy conversation and Charles Spurgeon's homiletical theory.

- **25:2** (concealment and searching). Francis Bacon famously inscribed this verse on the title page of *The Advancement of Learning* (1605), arguing that it gives biblical warrant for empirical scientific inquiry — kings (and by extension, scientists) glorify God by searching out what God has hidden in nature. The verse became foundational in the Protestant theological argument for natural science as a sacred calling, shaping the early Royal Society and the broader scientific-revolution-era theology of inquiry.

**NT use.** Pr 25:6–7 is closely paralleled in Lk 14:8–11 — Jesus's teaching on taking the lower seat at a wedding feast. *"When you are invited by someone to a wedding feast, do not sit down in a place of honor… But when you are invited, go and sit in the lowest place, so that when your host comes he may say to you, 'Friend, move up higher.'"* The verbal and structural parallels are direct enough that Luke is almost certainly drawing on Pr 25:6–7. Pr 25:21–22 is quoted in Romans 12:20. Pr 25:14 (*clouds and wind without rain*) is echoed in Jude 12 (*"waterless clouds, swept along by winds"*) describing false teachers. Pr 25:28 (the city without walls / the man without self-control) is echoed in 2 Pet 1:6 and Gal 5:23 in the lists of the fruit of the Spirit including *enkrateia* (self-control).

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent — Tensions, Positions, Steelman

Two tensions in Pr 25 deserve honest reckoning.

**Tension 1: Hezekiah's scribes and the historicity of OT literary production.** The 25:1 superscription is a window into how the Old Testament was actually produced. How should we hold it?

- *Mainstream-critical literary-archaeology reading.* The notice is historically reliable evidence of late-8th-c. royal-scribal compilation. We can locate this activity in time, place, and institutional setting (Jerusalem, the temple-and-palace-adjacent scribal school, under Hezekiah's reform program). The OT is the product of real human scribal communities working in real historical settings, transmitting and editing older traditions. Steelman: this reading aligns with the broader epigraphic and archaeological data on Iron Age Judah; it is the honest historical position.
- *Conservative-evangelical reading* (Waltke, Longman). The notice testifies to the **continuous transmission** of Solomonic-era wisdom across two and a half centuries through royal-scribal infrastructure. The text we have was edited and arranged but not invented at the Hezekiah stage. Steelman: the doctrine of inspiration accommodates editorial activity through human scribal communities; this is how the Holy Spirit has chosen to give us Scripture.
- *Critical-skeptical minimalist reading.* The 25:1 notice may itself be a later editorial fiction, projecting Hezekian compilation onto materials that are wholly post-exilic. Steelman: the position is internally consistent but unnecessary; the simplest reading of the data — internal evidence + external archaeology + the absence of any motive for inventing such a notice — is that the notice records something real.

**Where this leaves us:** Pr 25:1 is one of the friendliest texts in the OT for anyone who wants to take both Scripture's authority and its human history seriously. It tells us, on the face of the text, that what we are reading is the product of editorial work — Hezekiah's scribes copying, gathering, arranging. There is no embarrassment in this for Christian or Jewish readers; the same thing has happened with every text in the canon, and the inspired character of the result is not undermined by the human scribal labor that produced it. **The OT is what it appears to be: divine speech through human community across centuries**, and the Hezekiah notice is one of the clearest acknowledgments of this within the text itself.

**Tension 2: The "burning coals on his head" image (25:21–22) and what it actually means.** This is one of the most disputed metaphors in the OT. Three serious positions:

- *Self-condemnation reading* (Augustine, Calvin, traditional Christian). The burning coals are the **shame and remorse** the enemy feels when met with kindness instead of vengeance. Kindness to an enemy makes them aware of their own wrongdoing; their conscience burns. Pauline use in Rom 12:20 supports this — the goal is *overcome evil with good*, which fits a reading where the kindness softens or convicts the enemy. Steelman: this reading has dominated Christian interpretation for fifteen centuries; it is internally coherent and matches the immediate context's emphasis on God's reward (25:22b).
- *Egyptian ritual reading* (William Klassen 1962, Siegfried Morenz; widely accepted in modern critical scholarship). There is evidence in Egyptian texts — including a funeral ritual described in the Aramaic *Onomasticon* tradition and possibly reflected in the *Instruction of Amenemope* — of a **repentance ritual** in which a person publicly carried a brazier of coals on their head as a sign of contrition. If Pr 25:21–22 alludes to this, the meaning is that kindness leads the enemy to **publicly repent** — to "carry coals" as a sign of changed mind. Steelman: this reading has the advantage of explaining why the image is one of *reward* rather than punishment (25:22) — repentance is the goal, and reconciliation, not torment, is the outcome. It also fits the broader OT-Egyptian wisdom-borrowing pattern already evident in Pr 22–24.
- *Divine-judgment reading* (some older commentators; Roman parallels in Ovid, Seneca). The burning coals are **judgment** that God will inflict on the enemy if they do not repent — your kindness leaves their refusal naked before God. Steelman: this reading has some textual warrants (Ps 140:10, *"let burning coals fall upon them"*), but it sits uneasily with the ethic of love-of-enemy that Paul builds on the verse. Most modern readers reject it.

**Where this leaves us:** The Egyptian ritual reading has gained ground in critical scholarship, but the **Pauline use in Rom 12:20** orients Christian interpretation. Whether the original Hebrew imagery referenced an Egyptian repentance ritual or a metaphor for conscience-burning, the canonical-shape meaning is clear: **kindness to enemies is not surrender but spiritual warfare** — fighting evil with the most powerful weapon available, undeserved good. The reward is from the LORD, and the enemy's softening is the hoped-for fruit. The verse does not authorize using kindness as a weapon for punishment; it authorizes using kindness as the weapon that overcomes evil.

**Closing question:** Hezekiah's scribes copied this collection two and a half centuries after Solomon. What we read in Pr 25 is the result of a scribal community choosing what to preserve, what to arrange, what to pass forward to readers two and a half millennia later. We sit at the far end of that scribal labor. **What are you copying forward — to the next generation, the next decade, the next person who will inherit your wisdom-stock — and have you submitted it to the test of "kindness even to enemies" before you transmit it?**
