# 1 Samuel 26: The Spear in the Ground

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The chapter is set in the **Wilderness of Ziph**, a stretch of arid limestone hill country south of Hebron, dropping eastward toward the Dead Sea escarpment. The toponyms — Ziph, Hachilah, **Jeshimon** (יְשִׁימוֹן, *yeshimon*, "wasteland" or "desolation") — point to the rugged shelf country between the Hebron highlands and the Judean desert. Survey work by Yohanan Aharoni in the 1950s and the more recent regional surveys directed by Avi Ofer have located Tel Zif (Khirbet Zif), about six kilometers southeast of Hebron, with Iron I/II occupation that fits the chapter's chronological horizon (late eleventh century BCE). The "hill of Hachilah" has not been securely identified, but its description as overlooking Jeshimon "on the road" suggests a position above one of the wadis (likely Wadi el-Khalil or Wadi Malaki) that descend toward the En-Gedi escarpment. The scene presupposes a topography Saul's three thousand "chosen men" (*baḥurim*, בַּחוּרִים) can occupy in formation, with David able to view the camp from a parallel ridge across "a great space between them" (26:13) — exactly the kind of staggered ridge-and-wadi terrain the central Judean wilderness affords.

The political world is one of late tribal-monarchic Israel, with Saul ruling from **Gibeah** (Tell el-Ful, excavated by W.F. Albright in the 1920s and revisited by Paul Lapp; recent reassessments by Israel Finkelstein and others question the dating but accept eleventh-century use). The Ziphites' tip-off (26:1) reflects the political reality that David's "Hapiru-like" warband — to use the analogy advanced by Frank Moore Cross and pressed by Baruch Halpern — operated within Judahite territory but lacked unanimous local support. Some southerners protected him; others, like the Ziphites and earlier the Keilahites (1 Sam 23), preferred not to be caught between the king and the outlaw.

Material culture surfaces in three details. First, **Saul's spear stuck in the ground at his head** (26:7) is not a casual camping arrangement. Royal spears (*ḥanit*, חֲנִית) functioned as portable insignia of office in the southern Levant; the same spear appears as Saul's identifier in 18:10–11, 19:9–10, 20:33, and 22:6. Comparative iconography from Late Bronze and Early Iron Age cylinder seals and Phoenician ivories shows seated rulers with vertical spears or staffs, and the Ugaritic Keret epic (KTU 1.14–16) similarly associates the king with a planted weapon. Joel S. Burnett and earlier Roland de Vaux noted that the planted spear functioned almost as a mobile throne. Abishai's request "let me pin him to the earth with one stroke" (26:8) is therefore not just a wish to kill but a wish to use the king's own emblem against him — a savage symbolic act David refuses.

Second, the **water jar** (*ṣappaḥat ha-mayim*, צַפַּחַת הַמַּיִם) is a small ceramic juglet, the kind familiar from Iron I assemblages at Tel Masos, Tel Beersheba, and Tel Halif — typically a one-handled vessel of a few liters' capacity, suited for personal use beside a sleeping mat. Together the spear and jar are functioning realia and narrative tokens: Saul's authority and Saul's life-water, both lifted while he slept.

Third, the **"deep sleep from the LORD"** (*tardemat YHWH*, תַּרְדֵּמַת יְהוָה, 26:12) deploys the rare term *tardemah*, used elsewhere of the divinely induced sleep of Adam (Gen 2:21) and Abraham (Gen 15:12). The vocabulary is theological commentary disguised as etiology: the camp's failure of vigilance is not chance but YHWH's hand.

Linguistically, four further terms repay attention. **Mashiaḥ** (מָשִׁיחַ, "anointed") appears in the chapter's most important refrain (26:9, 11, 16, 23) — the ideological center of David's restraint. **Naval** (נָבָל, related to the verb in Saul's confession "I have acted foolishly," *hiskalti*, הִשְׂכַּלְתִּי, in 26:21) connects the chapter to the Nabal episode of chapter 25, where the same root "fool" surfaces. **Ner** (נֵר, the patronym of Abner, "lamp") quietly carries the book's recurring lamp-and-darkness imagery. And **Tsuriyah** (צְרוּיָה), Zeruiah, David's sister and mother of Joab and Abishai, marks a kinship politics that will dominate the rest of the David narrative — the sons of Zeruiah are loyal but bloodthirsty, the foil to David's restraint.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre is heroic prose narrative within the larger **History of David's Rise** (1 Sam 16 – 2 Sam 5), the literary unit isolated by Leonhard Rost in 1926 and refined by P. Kyle McCarter (who treated it as an apologetic justifying David's accession). Within this unit the chapter belongs to a tightly constructed sequence — the wilderness chase narratives of chapters 23–26 — that frames David's outlaw years as a moral test he passes and Saul fails.

The chapter's structure is symmetrical: Ziphite betrayal (vv. 1–4), reconnaissance (v. 5), descent into the camp (vv. 6–7), the Abishai exchange (vv. 8–11), the taking of the tokens (v. 12), the address from across the valley (vv. 13–16), Saul's recognition and David's defense (vv. 17–20), Saul's confession (vv. 21–25). Robert Alter, in *The David Story*, points to the **"deep sleep" verse (12)** as the literal and structural pivot — every clause radiates outward from the moment YHWH's hand falls on the camp.

The most discussed literary feature is the chapter's relation to **1 Samuel 24**, the cave at En-Gedi, where David also spares Saul. Source-critical scholarship since **Wellhausen** has read these as **doublets** — two parallel renderings of a single tradition. McCarter (Anchor Bible) and Halpern (*David's Secret Demons*) treat them as separate strands harmonized by the Deuteronomistic redactor. The literary case is strong: both begin with a tip-off, both place Saul "with three thousand chosen men," both feature Saul entering a vulnerable space, both end with Saul's emotional confession and a benediction over David. Walter Dietrich and others note characteristic doublet markers — the second telling intensifies (corner of robe → spear and water jar; cave → infiltrated camp; David's solo speech → speech with Abishai as foil).

The opposite reading, championed by **Robert Polzin**, Jan Fokkelman (*Narrative Art and Poetry in the Books of Samuel*, vol. 2), and Alter, treats the two episodes as a **deliberate literary diptych**. On this reading the redactor (or single author) has crafted parallels not by accident but by design — the second sparing surpasses the first because the moral test has escalated. In chapter 24 David is reactive (Saul stumbles into the cave); in chapter 26 David is proactive (he infiltrates the camp). In chapter 24 he is alone with conscience; in chapter 26 he must restrain a companion. Both readings can be defended, and the choice between them is not finally decidable on the texts alone — it depends on prior commitments about Deuteronomistic composition. What is incontestable is that the redactor wanted both, and the cumulative effect is to insist that David's restraint is policy, not accident.

Devices abound. The chapter is unusually rich in **wordplay**. Abishai means "my father is a gift" — the gift the father (Jesse) sends is restrained from the gift the brother offers (regicide). The verb **shamar** (שָׁמַר, "keep watch") in David's mocking address to Abner (vv. 15–16) is the same verb used elsewhere of Saul's failure to "keep" YHWH's word (1 Sam 13:13–14, 15:11). The taunt is theological. **Polzin** also notes the inversion of Saul's own oath formulas back at him.

The textual situation is mostly stable. **4QSam-a** (where preserved for this chapter) shows minor variants — a longer reading at v. 22 and a slight variant in v. 20 ("a single flea" vs. LXX's "my soul") — but no meaning-shifting differences of the kind that affect chapters 17 or 28. The Old Greek follows MT closely here, suggesting the chapter circulated in a stable form by the late Persian period.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The chapter sits inside the canonical arc of the rise of Israel's first dynastic king. In Christian canon it is part of the prelude to the Davidic covenant (2 Sam 7); in Jewish canon (Nevi'im Rishonim) it belongs to the Former Prophets, where the editorial frame is the question of whether kingship can be reconciled with covenant.

**Jewish interpretation.** **Josephus** (*Antiquities* 6.13.7–9) recasts the episode in moralizing Hellenistic register: David's restraint exemplifies *philanthropia* and *eusebeia*, virtues that legitimate his future rule. **Pseudo-Philo** (*Liber Antiquitatum Biblicarum*) embellishes with a heavenly voice rebuking Abishai. The classical rabbis used the chapter sparingly; **b. Berakhot 62b** treats David's taking of the spear and jug as a symbolic demonstration that he could have killed Saul, with rabbinic commentary on the protective sleep as miracle. **Rashi** focuses on the *tardemah* as supernatural and reads David's speech to Abner as legal indictment — Abner has failed his duty as captain of the guard. **Radak (Kimhi)** is more political: David's address aims to expose to the army that Saul's pursuit is private vendetta, not public duty. **Ralbag (Gersonides)** characteristically extracts ethical *to'aliyot* (lessons), foregrounding the principle that one must not use private vengeance against a legitimately appointed authority even when wronged. **Abarbanel**, writing in late-fifteenth-century Iberia and Italy with court politics fresh in mind, treats the chapter as a manual of monarchic ethics: he asks why God did not simply remove Saul, and answers that the legitimacy of succession had to be visible — David's hands had to be clean when the kingdom passed. **Avivah Zornberg**, in her psychological-midrashic mode, reads David's restraint as the disciplined refusal of the satisfying narrative — he will not "complete" the story Abishai offers him. **Yair Zakovitch** and **Moshe Garsiel** belong to the Israeli school that reads chapters 24 and 26 together; Garsiel's *The First Book of Samuel: A Literary Study of Comparative Structures, Analogies and Parallels* (1985) treats the doublet as authorial parallelism for theological emphasis.

**Christian interpretation.** Patristic readers gravitated to the chapter's typology of restraint. **Origen** (homilies on 1 Samuel, only fragmentarily preserved for this chapter) reads David as a type of Christ who refuses to claim by force what will be given by gift. **Ambrose** (*De Officiis* 1.36) cites the chapter to argue that magnanimity toward enemies is the highest virtue of the ruler. **Augustine** (*Contra Faustum* 22; cf. *De Civitate Dei* 17) uses the sparing as a counter-example to Manichaean charges that the Hebrew Bible glorifies vengeance — David is the Old Testament's most luminous restrainer. **Gregory the Great** (*Moralia in Job*) likens the spear in the ground to the temptations of power that surround the contemplative. **Aquinas** (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 64, a. 2 ad 3) cites the chapter on the question of when private persons may slay rulers — answer: not for personal cause, even when the ruler is unjust.

In the Reformation, **Calvin's sermons on 1 Samuel** (preached in Geneva in the late 1550s, edited by Wulfert de Greef and now available in the Calvini Opera Database) press the chapter against political resistance theory. Calvin, alert to French Huguenot and Marian-exile temptations, insists David shows that even an anointed but apostate king must not be lifted against by private hand — the magistrate's restraint is the model for the godly subject. **Luther**'s comments are briefer but characteristically blunt: the chapter shows that the office of king is from God even when its bearer is wicked, and the Christian's vocation is patience.

Critical scholarship from Wellhausen onward has, as noted, focused on source-critical questions. **Frank Moore Cross**'s reconstruction of the Deuteronomistic History places the doublet within the pro-Davidic redactional layer (Dtr1). **P. Kyle McCarter**'s Anchor Bible volume treats chapter 26 as the older of the two sparing accounts on linguistic grounds. **Ralph W. Klein** (Word Biblical Commentary) and **Hans Wilhelm Hertzberg** (Old Testament Library) take more synthesizing positions.

Contemporary readings have widened. **Walter Brueggemann** (*First and Second Samuel*, Interpretation) treats the chapter as a meditation on the competing claims of *vocation* (David's anointing) and *recognition* (David's refusal to take what is not yet given). **Polzin**'s deconstructive reading (*Samuel and the Deuteronomist*) sees the doublet as the narrator's strategy of forcing the reader to weigh two versions of the same moral act and so participate in the construction of David's character. **Eugene Peterson** (*Leap Over a Wall*) frames David's restraint as the discipline of waiting on God in the wilderness years. **David Gunn** (*The Fate of King Saul*) reads the chapter from Saul's side: Saul's tearful confession is not duplicity but the genuine, unbearable self-knowledge of a man who cannot stop hunting the one he loves. Feminist criticism on this chapter is sparser than on neighboring ones (Bal and Exum focus more on chs 25 and 28), but **Athalya Brenner** has noted the chapter's exclusively male economy of honor and how the Zeruiah-line men frame David's politics.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions structure the chapter's theology, and each opens onto a contested set of positions worth steelmanning.

**First tension: providence versus human agency in the protective sleep (v. 12).** If YHWH causes the camp's *tardemah*, has David really exercised restraint? Or is the moral act diluted into divine puppetry?

*Position 1 (classical providentialist):* The deep sleep does not diminish David's restraint; it makes it possible. **Aquinas** would say grace perfects nature: God supplies the conditions in which David's choice can be free. The sleep is the open door; David still has to walk through and walk back out without striking. The moral weight remains with David. *Position 2 (narrative-ironic):* The narrator inserts the *tardemah* clause precisely to take the credit off David and put it on YHWH. Polzin and Brueggemann read v. 12 as the editorial finger pressing the scales — David is great but YHWH is greater. The point is not human heroism but divine sovereignty. *Position 3 (compatibilist-theological):* Both are true at once. David's restraint and YHWH's enabling are the same event seen from different sides. **Calvin** reads exactly this way: secondary causation does not compete with primary causation. Steelman of the most uncomfortable position (the ironic): if the narrator did not include v. 12, David would be a hero in his own strength — the verse exists because the editor wanted to prevent that misreading. The moral lesson is not "be brave like David" but "trust YHWH like David."

*Where this leaves us:* The chapter refuses the easy heroism that would either glorify David alone or reduce him to instrument. The harder reading is that providence and agency cohabit, and that the test of a vocation is whether one acts *as if* one were the only cause while knowing one is not.

**Second tension: the sanctity of the anointed even when the anointed is unfit.** David's twice-repeated principle — "who can put out his hand against YHWH's anointed and be guiltless?" (vv. 9, 11) — became one of the most consequential political-theological claims in the Christian West. It underwrote divine-right monarchy and constrained centuries of debate about resistance to tyrants.

*Position 1 (high-monarchic):* The anointing creates an inviolable office; the office's bearer cannot be touched by private hand regardless of conduct. **Luther**, the Stuart-era English royalists, and later French legitimists drew this inference. *Position 2 (covenantal-conditional):* The anointing is conditional on covenant fidelity; Saul has already been rejected (1 Sam 15), and David's restraint is not theological necessity but personal piety — a refusal to act as Saul has acted, not a denial that Saul could in principle be removed. **Calvin's** more careful position pushes here: God may use the lower magistrate to depose a tyrant, but the private subject must not. **Theodore Beza** and the later monarchomachs developed the implication. *Position 3 (apologetic-political):* The whole chapter is a Davidic apologia — David's hands are clean because David has political need for clean hands. **Halpern** and **McKenzie** treat the principle of inviolable anointing as functional ideology written backward to legitimate David's later succession. Steelman of the apologetic reading: the chapter would not need to make the point so insistently (four times in seventeen verses) if the point were uncontroversial. The repetition is the tell.

*Where this leaves us:* The chapter establishes a category — the anointed — that the rest of the canon will both exalt and undercut. Nathan will rebuke David's house (2 Sam 12); Elijah and Elisha will help orchestrate the toppling of dynasties (1 Kgs 19, 2 Kgs 9); the prophets will assail anointed kings without restraint. The principle of restraint here is not absolute but situational — the question for every reader since has been which situation is theirs.

**One unresolved question:** Saul says "I have sinned… I have acted foolishly" (v. 21) and blesses David (v. 25), then within months consults the medium at Endor and dies on Gilboa. Is the confession sincere and Saul simply incapable of sustaining it, or is it performance under duress? The chapter does not tell us — and the silence may be the most theologically serious thing in it.

## The Thread

The chapter looks backward and forward through Israel's canon. **Backward:** the Spirit's "deep sleep" (*tardemah*) reaches across to **Genesis 2:21** and **15:12** — sleep as the medium of YHWH's decisive act. **Forward:** David's principle of not striking the anointed becomes the explicit basis for his execution of the Amalekite who claims to have killed Saul (**2 Samuel 1:14–16**) and for his lament in **2 Samuel 1**; it shapes his refusal to act against Absalom even in revolt (2 Sam 15–18) and his deathbed instructions about Joab and Shimei (1 Kings 2). **Outward:** the formula "the LORD's anointed" enters the Psalter (**Psalm 105:15**, **Psalm 2**) and becomes the seed of messianic vocabulary. **Saul's confession** ("I have sinned… I have played the fool") echoes the unfulfilled trajectory of **1 Samuel 15** and anticipates the silence of **1 Samuel 28**, where YHWH no longer answers. The spear stuck in the ground at Saul's head will reappear, in tragic inversion, on the slopes of Gilboa, where Saul falls on his own sword (1 Sam 31:4) — the weapon David refused to use is the one Saul finally turns on himself.
