# Acts 13: Antioch Sends, Cyprus Believes, Pisidian Antioch Hears the Long Story of Israel

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Acts 13 inaugurates what scholars conventionally call the **First Missionary Journey**, and the chapter places its events within a precisely-drawn first-century Mediterranean. The opening scene is the Antiochene church in the late 40s CE — most likely 46–48 CE, after the famine relief of 11:30 / 12:25 and before the Jerusalem Council of 49 CE. The chapter's geography traces a careful arc: from **Antioch on the Orontes** down to its port at **Seleucia Pieria** (sixteen miles distant, archaeologically attested with extant harbor moles), across the eastern Mediterranean to **Cyprus** at **Salamis** on the eastern coast and **Paphos** on the western, then northward to the southern Anatolian coast at **Perga in Pamphylia**, and finally inland and uphill some 100 miles to **Pisidian Antioch** (modern Yalvaç, Turkey) at roughly 1,100 meters elevation in the Anatolian plateau.

The leadership list of 13:1 deserves close attention: Barnabas (the Cypriot Levite of 4:36), **Simeon called Niger** ("the black"), **Lucius of Cyrene** (North African), **Manaen** (the *syntrophos*, "lifelong friend" or "foster-brother" of Herod Antipas the tetrarch — possibly raised at the Herodian court), and Saul. The demographic sweep — Cypriot, North African, Latin-named (Lucius), Herodian-court connected, and Tarsian — illustrates the cosmopolitan character of the Antiochene church and confirms the Antiochene tradition that **Magnus Zetterholm** and **Glanville Downey** independently emphasize.

**Cyprus** had been a Roman senatorial province since 22 BCE, governed by a **proconsul** (*anthypatos*). Sergius Paulus is the named proconsul (13:7), and the historical confirmation is one of the most cited triumphs of late-nineteenth-century New Testament archaeology. **Sir William Mitchell Ramsay**, working in the 1880s and 1890s, drew attention to a Latin inscription at Soli, Cyprus mentioning "Paulus the proconsul," and to several inscriptions from Pisidian Antioch and Rome mentioning a **Lucius Sergius Paullus** of senatorial rank — most plausibly the same family. The CIL VI 31545 inscription from Rome honors a Lucius Sergius Paullus as one of the *curatores riparum et alvei Tiberis* (caretakers of the Tiber's banks) under Claudius. **Stephen Mitchell**'s *Anatolia: Land, Men, and Gods in Asia Minor* (Oxford, 1993, 2 vols.) treats the Sergius Paulus connection as anchoring Pisidian Antioch in the network of senatorial Italian colonies.

**Pisidian Antioch** itself is one of the most thoroughly excavated of the Pauline cities. The site (Yalvaç, near Lake Eğirdir) was excavated by **David M. Robinson** in 1924 and extensively re-excavated by the Pisidian Antioch Project under **Stephen Mitchell**, **Marc Waelkens**, and others from the 1980s. The sanctuary of Augustus, the *via Sebaste* connecting the city to Pamphylia, the cardo, and the cult-statue inscriptions are all archaeologically documented. The Augustus Temple's *Res Gestae Divi Augusti* inscription — a copy of Augustus's autobiographical political testament, also found at Ankara (the Monumentum Ancyranum) — anchors the city's imperial-cult identity. Pisidian Antioch was a **Roman colonia** (founded by Augustus in 25 BCE for Italian veterans of the Battle of Actium), a fact that gives Paul's synagogue speech in 13:16–41 its specific social texture: he is preaching to a mixed Jewish-Gentile audience in a Latin-administrative town with strong imperial-cult overlay.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter. First, **προφῆται καὶ διδάσκαλοι** (*prophētai kai didaskaloi*, v. 1) — "prophets and teachers" — Antioch's twofold ministerial structure, distinct from the apostolic-elder structure of Jerusalem. Second, **λειτουργούντων** (*leitourgountōn*, v. 2) — "while they were worshiping/ministering" — the verb has cultic resonance, drawing the Antiochene gathering into priestly-liturgical language. Third, **ἀνθύπατος** (*anthypatos*, v. 7) — "proconsul" — the precise Roman administrative term for the governor of a senatorial province like Cyprus, distinguishing it from the *praeses* or *legatus* of an imperial province. Luke's terminological precision is a hallmark of his historiography. Fourth, **σεβομένων προσηλύτων** (*sebomenōn prosēlytōn*, v. 43) — "devout converts" — the term *proselyte* designates fully-circumcised Gentile converts to Judaism, distinguished from the broader category of *phoboumenoi ton theon* / *sebomenoi* (God-fearers) in v. 16, 26. Fifth, **διασπαρῇ** (compare v. 49 *diaphereto*) — "spreading/being carried through" — the language of dispersion-and-diffusion that Luke uses programmatically for the gospel's geographical advance.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 13 is the structural pivot of Acts. Luke has carefully prepared the transition through chapters 9 (Saul's conversion), 11 (Saul brought to Antioch), and 12 (Peter's exit). Now, at 13:1, Luke moves the narrative center from Jerusalem to Antioch and from Peter to Paul. **Robert Tannehill**'s *Narrative Unity of Luke-Acts* identifies this as the second great structural division of the book, paralleling the move from chapter 1 (the Jerusalem ascension) to chapter 2 (the Pentecostal beginning). Just as Pentecost inaugurates the apostolic mission, the Antiochene laying-on-of-hands inaugurates the Gentile mission. The naming-shift from "Saul" to "Paul" at 13:9 is the literary marker of the new phase: from this point forward, the Lukan narrator uses "Paul" almost exclusively, and the protagonist's Roman name signals the protagonist's Roman-imperial mission field.

The **Bar-Jesus / Elymas** scene (13:6–12) is intricately constructed. The pairing of Saul-Paul versus Bar-Jesus-Elymas works on multiple levels: a former Saul confronts a "son of Jesus" whose other name *Elymas* may derive from Aramaic *halamā* (interpreter, magician); Paul's denunciation as "son of the devil" (*hyie diabolou*, 13:10) reverses the Bar-Jesus name (*bar-Iēsou*, "son of Jesus/Yeshua") in pointed inversion. The blinding of Elymas mirrors and inverts the blinding of Saul on the Damascus road in chapter 9, except that here the blindness leads not to conversion but to judgment. **Daniel Marguerat** in *The First Christian Historian* treats the scene as Lukan literary art at its most concentrated — a typological economy that uses one event to recall and re-key another.

Paul's Pisidian Antioch synagogue speech (13:16–41) is the longest speech of Paul in Acts and the structural counterpart to Stephen's speech in chapter 7 and Peter's Pentecost speech in chapter 2. **Hans Conzelmann** in *Die Mitte der Zeit* (English: *The Theology of St. Luke*, Faber & Faber, 1960) and his Hermeneia commentary identify the speech's tripartite structure: Israel-history-summary (vv. 17–25), kerygmatic core on Jesus's death and resurrection (vv. 26–37), and pneumatic-prophetic warning (vv. 38–41). The speech compresses the entire Old Testament narrative — Egypt, wilderness, Conquest, judges, Samuel, Saul, David — into eight verses, then pivots through John the Baptist into the Christ-event. The Davidic emphasis is dense: the speech cites Psalm 2:7, Isaiah 55:3, and Psalm 16:10 in close succession (the same Psalm 16:10 Peter cited at Pentecost in 2:25–28), all converging on the resurrection-as-Davidic-promise. **Marion Soards**'s *The Speeches in Acts: Their Content, Context, and Concerns* (Westminster John Knox, 1994) is the standard treatment of the Lukan speeches as theological compositions.

The closing turn (13:46) — "we are turning to the Gentiles" — is one of three programmatic turn-statements in Acts (the others at 18:6 in Corinth and 28:28 in Rome). **F. F. Bruce** and **C. K. Barrett** both treat these as Lukan structural markers organizing the narrative's eastward-then-westward Gentile-mission arc. The citation of **Isaiah 49:6** ("I have made you a light for the Gentiles") echoes Simeon's Nunc Dimittis at Luke 2:32 — the same Isaianic text frames the entire Lukan double-work as a mission-to-the-nations theology.

The **Lukan composition vs. Pauline witness** question is acute in this chapter. **Pervo**'s Hermeneia commentary takes the speech as Lukan composition with no claim to Pauline historicity. **F. F. Bruce**'s NICNT defends substantial Pauline kernels — the Davidic typology, the Habakkuk citation, the structural turn-to-the-Gentiles — as recognizable from the Pauline letters (Rom 1:3; Rom 11; Gal). **Daniel Marguerat** takes the middle position: Luke composes the speech in his own idiom while preserving authentic Pauline kerygmatic emphases. **Stanley Porter** in *Paul in Acts* (Hendrickson, 2001) argues for higher continuity than Pervo allows.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The **Antiochene sending** of 13:1–3 has functioned as foundational for Christian mission theology across all major traditions. **Chrysostom**'s *Homilies on Acts* (Homilies 27–28) read it as paradigmatic of the church's missionary calling — the Spirit speaks to the worshiping community, the community fasts and prays, the community lays hands and sends. The scene became the basis for the Catholic theology of **missio canonica** — that authentic mission proceeds from ecclesial authorization. **Bede**'s *Expositio* extended the typology: the church gathered in worship is the Spirit's chosen instrument, and the laying-on-of-hands is sacramental. **Calvin**'s *Commentary on Acts* drew the Reformed conclusion that mission is by prayer, fasting, and the church's call — no individual self-commissioning, but communal sending.

**Twentieth-century Protestant missiology** rebuilt itself around this passage. **Roland Allen**'s *Missionary Methods: St. Paul's or Ours?* (1912) treated the chapter as the model for indigenous-church missionary practice over against denominational missionary-society models. **Lesslie Newbigin**'s *The Open Secret* and the entire ecumenical *missio Dei* tradition read 13:2 — "the Holy Spirit said" — as evidence that mission is God's work in which the church participates rather than the church's project that God blesses. **David Bosch**'s *Transforming Mission* (Orbis, 1991) traced the Antiochene paradigm through subsequent missionary history.

The **Pentecostal tradition** (Stronstad's *Charismatic Theology of St. Luke*; Menzies's *Empowered for Witness*) reads 13:2–4 as the recurring paradigm: Spirit speaks, community discerns, mission proceeds. **Justo González**'s *Acts: The Gospel of the Spirit* draws out the social implications: an Antiochene church that already includes North African, Cypriot, Latin, and Herodian-court members sends a mission that crosses further cultural lines, with the Spirit driving the movement from cultural diversity into cultural diversity.

The **Pauline scholarship** of recent decades has reshaped the reading of Paul's Pisidian Antioch speech. **Krister Stendahl**'s *Paul Among Jews and Gentiles* (Fortress, 1976) reframed Paul as called to the Gentiles rather than converted from Judaism — a reading that rereads 13:46 as Paul's vocational logic rather than a rejection of Israel. **E. P. Sanders**'s *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (Fortress, 1977) and *Paul, the Law, and the Jewish People* (Fortress, 1983) reshaped the broader question of how Paul read the Mosaic law in relation to the gospel — a question that v. 39 ("freed from everything from which you could not be freed by the law of Moses") raises in compressed form. **N. T. Wright**'s *Paul and the Faithfulness of God* (Fortress, 2013) treats the speech as a compressed version of Paul's larger story-of-Israel theology developed at length in Romans 9–11. **Pamela Eisenbaum**'s *Paul Was Not a Christian* (HarperOne, 2009) and **Matthew Thiessen**'s *A Jewish Paul* (Baker Academic, 2023) read the speech as fully Jewish — a Jewish messianic kerygma to fellow Jews and God-fearers. **Paula Fredriksen**'s *Paul: The Pagan's Apostle* (Yale, 2017) places Paul's mission within the Jewish eschatological expectation of the gathering of the Gentiles in the last days, of which 13:47 (Isaiah 49:6) is the programmatic citation.

The **"as many as were appointed to eternal life believed"** of 13:48 has been a contested text in the broader theological tradition. **Augustine**'s anti-Pelagian writings cite the verse as evidence of divine election. **Calvin**'s *Institutes* and the Synod of Dort develop the verse into a foundational predestinarian text. **John Wesley**'s *Predestination Calmly Considered* and the broader Wesleyan-Arminian tradition read the verse as evidence of God's foreknowledge rather than determinative election. **F. F. Bruce**'s NICNT and **C. K. Barrett**'s ICC treat the verse as Lukan rather than developed Pauline-doctrinal: Luke is recording the providential ordering of the Antiochene mission's success without entering the systematic question.

The contemporary commentary tradition — **Bruce NICNT**, **Barrett ICC**, **Fitzmyer Anchor Bible**, **Gaventa Abingdon**, **Johnson Sacra Pagina**, **Keener** four-volume Hendrickson, **Witherington Socio-Rhetorical**, **Schnabel ZECNT**, **Pervo Hermeneia**, **Conzelmann Hermeneia**, **Peterson Pillar**, **Marguerat**, **Marshall TNTC** — collectively gives the chapter the most thorough exegetical attention of any in the second half of Acts. **Schnabel**'s two-volume *Early Christian Mission* (IVP, 2004) is the most ambitious recent reconstruction of the Pauline missionary practice.

The **Jewish interpretive trajectory** has approached the chapter with fresh eyes in the post-Holocaust era. **Adele Reinhartz**, **Daniel Boyarin** (*Border Lines*, 2004), **Amy-Jill Levine**, **Pamela Eisenbaum**, and **Matthew Thiessen** have all worked to recover Paul as a fully-Jewish figure whose mission to the Gentiles was a recognizable Jewish-eschatological vocation rather than a break from Judaism. The "we turn to the Gentiles" of 13:46 is, on this reading, a tactical-vocational pivot within an intra-Jewish messianic mission, not a supersessionist rejection of Israel. **Lawrence Schiffman** has emphasized the synagogue setting of Paul's preaching: the speech is delivered within a fully Jewish liturgical context (after the reading from Law and Prophets, vv. 14–15), and Paul speaks as a Jew to Jews and God-fearers.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension 1: Is the Antiochene sending model normative for all subsequent Christian mission?** The chapter narrates a particular sequence — gathered worship, fasting, prophetic word, fasting and prayer, laying-on-of-hands, sending — and the question is whether this sequence is descriptive or prescriptive. Three positions have shaped the tradition.

The **Catholic-sacramental position** (developed in canon law from medieval through Vatican II, with **Yves Congar**'s *I Believe in the Holy Spirit* and the *Decree on Missionary Activity* "Ad Gentes" giving the modern formulation) reads the sequence as constitutive: legitimate mission requires ecclesial discernment and authorized sending, which is why the laying-on-of-hands by the Antiochene church confers something that mere individual call could not. The **Protestant-evangelical position** (from the Great Awakenings through the modern missionary movement, with **William Carey**'s *Enquiry* and **Hudson Taylor**'s autobiography as touchstones) reads the sequence as illustrative: the Spirit's call is primary, and the sending church's role is recognition and support rather than authorization. The **Pentecostal-charismatic position** reads the sequence as paradigmatic of Spirit-driven mission generally — the Spirit speaks, the community confirms, the mission proceeds — without resolving the question of authorization in either Catholic or Protestant directions.

Steelmanned: the Catholic reading captures Luke's clear emphasis that the Antiochene sending was a corporate ecclesial act with sacramental weight (laying-on-of-hands, fasting and prayer). The Protestant reading captures the Spirit's priority — the Spirit speaks first, the community acts in response. The Pentecostal reading captures the dynamism of the chapter's narrative without imposing later constitutional categories. **Where this leaves us**: Acts 13 narrates a sending that is both Spirit-initiated and ecclesially-mediated, and any missiology that severs the two — whether by claiming sacramental authority over the Spirit or by claiming Spirit-call independent of ecclesial discernment — is reading against the chapter.

**Tension 2: What does "we are turning to the Gentiles" mean for Israel?** The pivot of 13:46 has functioned across the tradition as either supersessionist or missiological-tactical, with substantial consequences. Three positions.

The **classical Christian-supersessionist reading** (developed from **Justin Martyr**'s *Dialogue with Trypho* through medieval and Reformation theology, present in **Calvin** and in much subsequent Reformed literature) reads the verse as evidence that Israel's rejection of the gospel transfers the covenant promises to the Gentile church — Israel having "judged itself unworthy of eternal life" (v. 46), the inheritance passes elsewhere. The **dispensationalist reading** (J. N. Darby, the Scofield Bible, **Charles Ryrie**) reads the same verse as a parenthetical pause: Israel's national rejection initiates the "church age" during which God works primarily with Gentiles, but the promises to ethnic Israel remain and will be fulfilled at the eschaton. The **post-Holocaust Pauline-within-Judaism reading** (**Stendahl**, **Eisenbaum**, **Thiessen**, **Fredriksen**, **N. T. Wright** in *Paul and the Faithfulness of God*) reads the verse as a tactical-vocational pivot within Paul's continuing mission to Jews, with the broader Romans 9–11 framework affirming God's irrevocable covenant with Israel.

Steelmanned: the supersessionist reading takes seriously the chapter's stark language ("you thrust it aside and judge yourselves unworthy"). The dispensationalist reading takes seriously the parenthetical structure of the Gentile mission within a larger God-and-Israel narrative. The Pauline-within-Judaism reading takes seriously the post-Holocaust theological recognition that supersessionism cannot stand and that Paul's own mission practice (synagogue first, repeatedly, in city after city) belies the supersessionist reading. **Where this leaves us**: the chapter creates a tension that Romans 9–11 will eventually address, and the Gentile mission must be read alongside Paul's continuing engagement with Israel, not as its replacement. *Unresolved question*: When Luke writes "as many as were appointed to eternal life believed" (13:48), does the appointment language commit the chapter to a particular doctrine of election, or is this Lukan providential-narrative language that does not reach systematic conclusions one way or the other?

## The Thread

Acts 13 reaches across the canon in multiple directions. The **Antiochene leadership list** of v. 1 echoes the Pentecost crowd of **Acts 2:9–11** — both lists demonstrate the global-cosmopolitan reach of the Spirit's gathering. The **Bar-Jesus / Elymas episode** echoes **Simon Magus** of Acts 8 and prefigures **the sons of Sceva** at **Acts 19:13–17**, with Luke developing a recurring polemic against magical-religious power. **Paul's speech** echoes **Stephen's speech** of Acts 7 in its use of the long Israel-history summary, while the Davidic-resurrection christology of vv. 32–37 prefigures and parallels **Romans 1:3–4** ("descended from David according to the flesh, declared Son of God in power... by his resurrection from the dead"). The **Isaiah 49:6 citation** of v. 47 gathers up **Luke 2:32** (Simeon's Nunc Dimittis) and projects forward to the **Acts 28:28** climax — the Lukan double-work is bound by this single text. And the **John Mark abandonment** at v. 13 plants the seed for the **Paul-Barnabas split** of **Acts 15:36–41**, with Mark eventually reappearing in the Pauline correspondence (**Colossians 4:10**, **Philemon 24**, **2 Timothy 4:11**) as reconciled.
