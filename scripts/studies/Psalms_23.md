# Psalm 23: The Shepherd Who Becomes the Host

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The superscription is the spare *mizmor le-David* (מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד), "A Psalm of David" — six verses, one of the shortest psalms, and possibly the most beloved religious poem ever written. Form-critically, Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 23 as a *Vertrauenspsalm* (psalm of trust or confidence), a sub-genre that develops out of the lament tradition but stands on its own. The lament typically moves from complaint to confession of trust to vow of praise; the psalm of trust takes the trust-confession and expands it into the entire poem, omitting the complaint. Westermann saw this as a literary refinement: the psalmist who has learned the lament's grammar can now speak its conclusion as the whole song.

The cult-functional setting (Mowinckel) is harder to pin down for Ps 23 than for the corporate-procession psalms. The likely *Sitz im Leben* is the temple-thanksgiving meal — the *todah* (תּוֹדָה) sacrifice and shared feast — with the speaker as a delivered individual celebrating God's faithfulness. The reference to a table prepared in the presence of enemies (v. 5) and dwelling in the house of the LORD (v. 6) points to the temple precincts and the thanksgiving meal eaten there. Sarna and Goldingay both lean this direction, though without the dogmatism of Mowinckel's broader cultic reconstructions. Hossfeld-Zenger note that the trust-genre is at home in the post-exilic period as much as the pre-exilic, complicating dating.

The shepherd metaphor was never original to Israel. In the ancient Near East, "shepherd" was a stock royal-divine epithet. Hammurabi's prologue to his law code calls him "the shepherd called by Enlil." Egyptian pharaohs are routinely styled as shepherds of the people. Marduk is hailed as a shepherd in Babylonian hymns. The Sumerian king Lugalzagesi described himself as the shepherd of his land. Within Israel's own scriptural tradition, the shepherd-image for God is older than this psalm: Jacob blesses Joseph with reference to "the God who has been my shepherd all my life long" (Gen 48:15) and "the Mighty One of Jacob, by the name of the Shepherd, the Stone of Israel" (Gen 49:24). Isaiah 40:11 — "He will tend his flock like a shepherd; he will gather the lambs in his arms" — is a more developed expression. Ezekiel 34 turns the shepherd metaphor into a sustained polemic: the false shepherds of Israel have failed; YHWH himself will shepherd his people. Micah 7:14 prays for YHWH to shepherd the flock of his heritage.

What Psalm 23 does is take a metaphor that was royal-political-divine and make it *first-person-personal*: "the LORD is *my* shepherd." This is unusual. The shepherd-of-the-people image is corporate; the shepherd-of-me image is intimate. Some commentators (Levenson) caution against an over-individualized reading — the speaker is still in covenant with the flock, even when the address is "I" — but the personal note is real and theologically generative.

The shepherd's tools in v. 4 are *shevet umishanet* (שִׁבְטְךָ וּמִשְׁעַנְתֶּךָ, "your rod and your staff"). These are two distinct objects. The *shevet* is a club or short rod, primarily defensive — used against predators, against threats to the flock. The *mishanet* is the longer staff, supportive and guiding — used to steady the shepherd over rough ground, to pull a sheep out of a hole, to nudge it back to the path. The two objects together cover defense and direction. The Hebrew phrase tells us, with two compact words, what the shepherd actually carries.

The shift in metaphor at v. 5 is one of the psalm's literary marvels. The shepherd image, sustained for four verses, gives way to the host image: "you prepare a table before me" — a banquet, an anointing, a cup overflowing. The two metaphors — shepherd and host — are not collapsed. The speaker has been led through the valley by the shepherd; in the next breath, he is at table with the host. Whether this is a single referent with two roles or a deliberate shift is debated. The simplest reading is that both are YHWH and the metaphors complement: the shepherd-God and the host-God are one God, and the speaker has experienced both kinds of care.

The closing phrase *beyt YHWH* (בֵּית יְהוָה, "house of the LORD," v. 6) almost certainly means the temple precincts. The phrase *le-orekh yamim* (לְאֹרֶךְ יָמִים), traditionally translated "forever," more literally is "for length of days." Whether this means the speaker's lifetime or eschatological eternity is one of the psalm's open questions, taken up under Lens 4.

Key Hebrew terms:

- *YHWH ro'i* (יְהוָה רֹעִי, v. 1) — "the LORD is my shepherd"
- *na'ot deshe* (נְאוֹת דֶּשֶׁא, v. 2) — "pastures of tender grass"
- *mey menuchot* (מֵי מְנֻחוֹת, v. 2) — "waters of rest/quiet places"
- *gey tzalmavet* (גֵּיא צַלְמָוֶת, v. 4) — "valley of deep darkness" (or "shadow of death," depending on whether *tzalmavet* is parsed as *tzel*+*mavet* "shadow of death" or *tzalmut* "deep darkness")
- *shevet umishanet* (שֵׁבֶט וּמִשְׁעֶנֶת, v. 4) — "rod and staff"
- *kosi revayah* (כּוֹסִי רְוָיָה, v. 5) — "my cup is saturated/overflowing"
- *beyt YHWH* (בֵּית יְהוָה, v. 6) — "house of the LORD"

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The structure is bipartite, with v. 4 as the hinge.

- **vv. 1–3**: Shepherd metaphor, third-person address ("*he* makes me lie down")
- **v. 4**: Transition through the valley of the shadow, address shifts to second person ("*you* are with me")
- **vv. 5–6**: Host metaphor, second-person address sustained ("*you* prepare a table")

The address-shift at v. 4 is the psalm's central literary move. As long as things are sunny, God can be spoken of in the third person — *he leads, he restores*. When the speaker enters the valley, the grammar changes: now God is *you*. The grammar enacts the theology. Trouble does not push God further away; it pulls God closer. Robert Alter's translation preserves the shift carefully.

Parallelism in Ps 23 is mostly *seconding* rather than synonymous (Berlin, Kugel) — the second half of each line sharpens the first. V. 2: "He makes me lie down in green pastures / he leads me beside still waters" — first the rest, then the source of the rest; first the lying, then the journey. V. 6: "Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me / and I shall dwell in the house of the LORD" — the active pursuit *(yirdephuni*, "shall pursue me"; the verb is unexpectedly aggressive, as if mercy is hunting the speaker down) and then the destination.

The form-critical structure (Westermann) is that of a confession of trust expanded into a complete song, with a doxological ending. There is no lament here — only the trust that lament works toward.

The poetic compression is extraordinary. Psalm 23 in Hebrew is approximately fifty-five words. Almost every word does multiple work: *deshe* (tender grass) implies both nourishment and beauty; *menuchot* (rest, quiet) implies both physical safety and spiritual peace; *tzalmavet* (deep darkness / shadow of death) holds together mortality, fear, and any extreme darkness; *shulchan* (table) implies both fellowship and provision. This is poetry in the strict sense — language compressed to maximum density.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

### Book I placement

Psalm 23 sits between Psalm 22 (extreme lament) and Psalm 24 (temple entrance) in Book I of the Psalter. The editorial sequence — desolation, then trust, then arrival — has been read as deliberate. Brueggemann's reading places these three together as Friday-Saturday-Sunday or as the typological pattern of Christian initiation. Whether the editors of Book I intended this shape is debated, but the texts as we have them produce the effect.

### Jewish Trajectory

Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, Psalm 23 is preserved in the Cave 11 Psalms scroll (11QPs-a) and other fragments, with the consonantal text largely matching the MT.

Rabbinic reception in *Midrash Tehillim* connects the psalm with Israel's wilderness journey — the green pastures and still waters become the manna and the rock that gave water; the table in the presence of enemies becomes the manna eaten in sight of the surrounding hostile nations. This corporate-historical reading is important. The "I" of Psalm 23 is read in Jewish tradition as Israel as well as the individual, and the wilderness-Sinai reading prevents the psalm from collapsing into private sentimentality.

Rashi reads the psalm at the literal level (David in difficulty, trusting God) with corporate-Israel implications. Ibn Ezra preserves the literal-Davidic frame. Radak holds the Davidic and corporate readings together. Modern Jewish scholarship — Buber on the I-Thou immediacy of the second-person address, Heschel on the prophetic emphasis on God's presence with the suffering, Levenson on the temple-pilgrimage frame, Sarna's *Songs of the Heart*, Avivah Zornberg's hermeneutic readings — has continued to deepen the reading.

In synagogue practice, Psalm 23 is recited in Sephardi tradition at the third Sabbath meal (*seudah shlishit*), in some Ashkenazi traditions at funerals (a relatively modern adoption), and is included in some prayer books for various occasions. Its centrality in Jewish liturgy is, however, far less than in Christian devotion. This asymmetry is itself worth noting — the psalm has had its largest cultural career in Christian and post-Christian contexts.

### Christian Trajectory

The Christian tradition has read Psalm 23 with extraordinary creativity. Augustine's *Enarrationes* on Psalm 23 (Vulgate Ps 22) reads it as the prayer of the Christian who has been baptized — the green pastures are the sacraments, the still waters are baptismal waters, the anointing is chrism, the table is the Eucharist, the house of the Lord is heaven. This sacramental reading became the patristic standard. Athanasius and Cassiodorus follow it. Aquinas systematizes it.

Luther's reading in his lectures on the psalms holds the literal-trust dimension and the Christological extension together; for Luther, the psalm is the prayer of the Christian who has been brought into Christ the Good Shepherd's flock. Calvin (1557) reads David first as the speaker, then Christ as the deeper referent, and emphasizes the covenantal frame: the speaker can claim "the LORD is my shepherd" only because he is in covenant with that LORD.

Modern critical commentary has largely set aside the patristic sacramental reading and asked what the psalm meant in its first-millennium context. Gunkel, Mowinckel, Westermann, Kraus, Hossfeld-Zenger, Goldingay, Tate, Wenham, Anderson, Brueggemann, Miller, Brown, Tanner, Gillingham — all treat the psalm as an expression of personal trust within ancient Israelite cultic and theological frames. Brueggemann's *The Message of the Psalms* reads it as a statement of "orientation" — the world as gift, before disorientation and reorientation. Patrick Miller in *They Cried to the Lord* takes the second-person address as central. William Brown's *Seeing the Psalms* attends to the visual imagery. Susan Gillingham's *Psalms Through the Centuries* traces the reception across millennia of liturgical and devotional use.

The metrical Scottish Psalter setting "The Lord's My Shepherd, I'll Not Want," set to the tune CRIMOND, became one of the most beloved English-language sacred songs. The Reformed psalm-singing tradition gave Psalm 23 a public-musical career that Catholic and Anglican traditions complemented through chant, anthem, and organ setting. The Anglican Daily Office uses Ps 23 regularly; the Catholic Lectionary includes it on Good Shepherd Sunday and at funerals. In the late twentieth century, Psalm 23 became a staple of Western Christian funeral practice almost to the exclusion of other contexts. This funereal-comfort tradition is real but represents a narrowing of a psalm whose original setting was thanksgiving in the temple, not death.

### NT Use

Psalm 23 is not directly quoted in the New Testament. Its echoes are everywhere:

- **John 10:11, 14**: Jesus as *ho poimēn ho kalos*, "the Good Shepherd" — the most explicit pickup of the shepherd-of-Israel tradition, with Ps 23 in the background (along with Ezek 34).
- **Hebrews 13:20**: God as "the great shepherd of the sheep" — applied to the resurrected Christ.
- **1 Peter 2:25**: "the Shepherd and Overseer of your souls" — addressed to scattered Christian communities, drawing on the same tradition.
- **Revelation 7:17**: "the Lamb in the midst of the throne will be their shepherd, and he will guide them to springs of living water" — a striking inversion (the Lamb as Shepherd) with Ps 23's still waters in the background.

The New Testament does not cite Psalm 23 as prooftext but inhabits its imagery — which is, perhaps, a higher form of use.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two tensions deserve careful attention.

**Tension 1: Personal-individual reading vs. corporate-Israel reading.** Western Christian devotion has read Psalm 23 as the prayer of the individual believer — me and my shepherd, my journey, my table. This is not wrong, but it is partial. The Jewish tradition has more often heard the "I" as Israel: the wilderness shepherd who led the flock through the desert is leading the people through history. The ANE shepherd metaphor was always corporate before it was personal.

Three positions:

1. **Personal-individual primacy**. The psalm's first-person grammar is intentional; the speaker is an individual, and the psalm authorizes individual trust. Augustinian and modern devotional readings. *Steelman*: takes the grammar seriously; refuses to dissolve the speaker into the collective; honors lived experience of God's care.

2. **Corporate-Israel primacy**. Levenson's emphasis: the "I" is always within the "we"; the wilderness-Sinai allusions in vv. 2–4 are the psalm's controlling frame; the temple in v. 6 is the corporate destination. *Steelman*: refuses to privatize Scripture; insists that even the most intimate prayer is voiced from within covenant; protects against bourgeois sentimentality.

3. **Both held together**. Brueggemann, Miller, most modern commentators. The psalm authorizes individual trust *because* of covenantal belonging; the "I" speaks because the "we" makes the "I" possible. *Steelman*: avoids forcing a choice the text does not make.

**Where this leaves us**: probably Position 3, with Position 2 as a needed corrective whenever the psalm is being privatized. Devotional individualism without covenantal grounding turns the psalm into a sentimental object.

**Tension 2: "House of the LORD forever" — eschatological eternity or temple-pilgrimage continuity?** The phrase *le-orekh yamim* is ambiguous. The patristic tradition (Augustine, Aquinas) read it eschatologically: the speaker will dwell in heaven, in the Father's house, eternally. The historical-critical reading (Sarna, Goldingay): the speaker hopes to continue making pilgrimage to the temple all his life, to remain within the cult's living reality.

Three positions:

1. **Eschatological**. The "forever" means eternal life; the house of the LORD is heaven. Augustinian and Aquinas's reading; the Christian funereal tradition presupposes this.

2. **Temple-cultic**. The "forever" means as long as the speaker lives; the house of the LORD is the Jerusalem temple; the prayer is for sustained access to YHWH's presence in the cult.

3. **Eschatological-cultic continuity**. The temple presence and the eternal life are not opposed but continuous; the cult anticipates eternity, eternity completes the cult. This is closer to the synthesis Levenson proposes in *Sinai and Zion*.

**Where this leaves us**: the historical-critical reading is almost certainly closer to what the original psalmist meant; the eschatological reading is what the tradition has done with it; the synthesis is what allows the psalm to remain alive across its many uses. The Christian use at funerals is not a misreading — it is an extension that the psalm's own ambiguity invites.

**Closing question**: When you pray "the LORD is my shepherd," is the *my* a possession claim, a covenant claim, or a confession that you are the one being possessed?

## The Thread

- **Ezekiel 34**: YHWH against the false shepherds, promising to shepherd Israel himself — the prophetic context for the metaphor in Ps 23.
- **John 10:11**: Jesus's "I am the good shepherd" — the New Testament's most explicit identification with the Shepherd of Ps 23 and Ezek 34.
- **Genesis 48:15**: Jacob blessing Joseph with "the God who has been my shepherd all my life long" — possibly the earliest *personal* shepherd-confession in the Hebrew Bible.
- **Revelation 7:17**: the Lamb who is the Shepherd, leading to springs of living water — Ps 23's still waters in apocalyptic transformation.
