# Psalm 106 — Go Deeper

## Lens 1: The Historical World

Psalm 106 carries no superscription in the Masoretic Text, but it bears the weight of a position no other psalm in Book IV holds: it closes the book. The final verse — *bārûkh-Yahweh 'ĕlōhê yiśrā'ēl min-hā‛ôlām wə‛ad-hā‛ôlām wə'āmar kol-hā‛ām 'āmēn hallə-Yāh* (בָּרוּךְ יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל מִן-הָעוֹלָם וְעַד-הָעוֹלָם וְאָמַר כָּל-הָעָם אָמֵן הַלְלוּ-יָהּ, "Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, from everlasting to everlasting! And let all the people say, 'Amen!' Praise the LORD!", v. 48) is not part of the psalm proper but the **editorial doxology that closes Book IV** of the Psalter, parallel to the closing doxologies at Psalms 41:13 (Book I), 72:18–19 (Book II), and 89:52 (Book III). The doxologies are the architectural seams of the Psalter, marking the joins between its five books, and they are demonstrably editorial because the same text in 1 Chronicles 16:36 includes verse 48 as part of a Davidic liturgical performance — meaning the doxology was already attached to the psalm by the Chronicler's time but is structurally distinct from the historical recital that precedes it.

Hermann Gunkel classified Psalm 106 as a *Geschichtspsalm* paired with a communal lament, sometimes called a "historical confession" or *Bußliturgie* — penitential liturgy. The form is rarer than pure lament: it tells Israel's story not for praise (as Psalm 105 does) but for confession of failure. Gunkel grouped it with Nehemiah 9:6–37 and Daniel 9:4–19 as the canonical examples of the historical-confessional form. Sigmund Mowinckel located Psalm 106 in the cultic context of penitential days, perhaps connected to fast days or the New Year cycle. Hans-Joachim Kraus, Erhard Gerstenberger, John Goldingay, and Frank-Lothar Hossfeld with Erich Zenger all retain some version of this classification while emphasizing the post-exilic editorial work that shaped the psalm into its current form.

Dating leans clearly post-exilic. Verse 47's plea — "Save us, O LORD our God, and gather us from among the nations" — places the speaker among scattered Israel, asking for the regathering that the post-exilic prophetic literature anticipates (Isaiah 11:11–12, Ezekiel 36:24, Zechariah 8:7–8). The exile is treated as a present condition. The historical recital reaches all the way through to verse 41–46's "he gave them into the hand of the nations" — narrating the exile as the climax of the cycle of failure. This puts the psalm's composition (or at least its current form) firmly in the sixth century or later, with most scholars settling on the early Persian period (late sixth or fifth century BC).

Among the **Dead Sea Scrolls**, Psalm 106 appears in 4QPsd and 11QPsa with text largely consistent with the Masoretic. The 11QPsa scroll's distinctive arrangement places Psalm 106 near other historical-confessional material, and the doxology is preserved.

The Hebrew vocabulary is dense with covenant-rupture language. Verse 12's *vayya'ămînû vidvārāyw yāšîrû təhillātô* (וַיַּאֲמִינוּ בִדְבָרָיו יָשִׁירוּ תְּהִלָּתוֹ, "Then they believed his words; they sang his praise") and the immediate reversal in verse 13's *miharû šākhəḥû ma‛ăśāyw lō'-ḥikkû la‛ăṣātô* (מִהֲרוּ שָׁכְחוּ מַעֲשָׂיו לֹא-חִכּוּ לַעֲצָתוֹ, "But they soon forgot his works; they did not wait for his counsel") form the pivot of the entire psalm — belief and forgetting in two adjacent verses. Verse 31's *vatēḥāšev lô liṣədāqâ lədōr wādōr ‛ad-‛ôlām* (וַתֵּחָשֶׁב לוֹ לִצְדָקָה לְדֹר וָדֹר עַד-עוֹלָם, "and that was counted to him as righteousness from generation to generation forever") about Phinehas's intervention uses the same verb-formula — *ḥāšav liṣədāqâ* — that Genesis 15:6 uses of Abraham. The deliberate echo is theologically loaded.

The **ancient Near Eastern** context for the historical-confessional form includes Mesopotamian penitential prayers (the *šigû* and *eršaḥunga* texts), where the worshipper confesses failure and pleads for restoration. The Hebrew form differs in scale: while Mesopotamian penitential prayers are typically individual, Psalm 106 is corporate-historical, confessing the failures of the entire national history rather than personal sin. The closest formal parallel is the Hittite "plague prayer" of Mursili II, where the king confesses inherited sin (his father's broken oath) and asks the gods for relief from the plague — but even this is narrower than Psalm 106's sweeping historical confession. The full corporate-historical form of penitential prayer appears most clearly in the post-exilic Jewish literature (Nehemiah 9, Daniel 9, the Prayer of Azariah, Baruch 1:15–3:8), and Psalm 106 is one of the earliest surviving examples.

## Lens 2: The Literature

Robert Lowth's parallelism is everywhere; the psalm uses both synonymous and antithetic forms heavily. The most theologically loaded antithetic parallel is between verses 12 and 13: "Then they believed his words; they sang his praise / But they soon forgot his works; they did not wait for his counsel." The pivot is the temporal adverb *miharû* — "they hastened, they soon" — which captures the entire theological argument of the psalm: belief is real but not durable; praise is real but not retained; the cycle of failure is not a single fall but a habitual amnesia.

The **strophic structure** organizes the recital around discrete episodes of failure, each narrated as a unit:

- Opening doxology and personal plea (vv. 1–5)
- General confession (vv. 6)
- Egypt and Red Sea: rebellion before the sea, salvation by God's name (vv. 7–12)
- Wilderness craving and disease (vv. 13–15)
- Korah's rebellion (Dathan and Abiram, vv. 16–18)
- Golden calf at Horeb, Moses's intercession (vv. 19–23)
- Refusal to enter the land, swearing of wilderness death (vv. 24–27)
- Baal of Peor, Phinehas's intervention (vv. 28–31)
- Meribah, Moses's failure (vv. 32–33)
- Conquest failure: not driving out the Canaanites, idolatry, child sacrifice (vv. 34–39)
- Cycle of judgment, repentance, deliverance, relapse (vv. 40–46)
- Exilic plea (v. 47)
- Editorial doxology (v. 48)

Notice the symmetrical tension with Psalm 105's structure. Psalm 105 narrates Joseph in seven verses and gets through Egypt and into the land. Psalm 106 spends most of its verses on failure *between* Egypt and the land, and on failure *after* the land is given. The two psalms pair as the front and back of the same coin: the same history, told from opposite angles.

The psalm has no *selah*, like Psalms 104 and 105 — Book IV's closing trio moves continuously without pause. The literary intensity is unbroken; the recital does not give the worshipper room to step out.

The **opening and closing inclusio** is the *halləlû-Yāh* — "Praise the LORD!" — which opens v. 1 and closes the editorial doxology in v. 48. Whether the opening *halləlû-Yāh* belonged to Psalm 106 originally or was placed there by the editors who arranged the doxological seam is debated, but in the canonical text it forms a frame that holds the entire recital of failure within the bracket of praise. This is theologically significant: the confession of national failure is not despair; it is a form of praise.

The Phinehas episode (vv. 30–31) deserves particular attention. The Hebrew *vayya‛ămōd pînḥās vayəfallēl vattē‛āṣar hammaggēfâ* (וַיַּעֲמֹד פִּינְחָס וַיְפַלֵּל וַתֵּעָצַר הַמַּגֵּפָה, "Then Phinehas stood up and intervened, and the plague was stayed") uses the verb *pālal* — "to mediate, to intervene, to pray" — but the Numbers 25 narrative makes clear that the "intervention" was an act of zealous violence: Phinehas drove a spear through an Israelite man and a Midianite woman in the act of cohabitation. The psalm's language *softens* the violence ("he intervened") while the Genesis 15:6 echo ("counted to him as righteousness") *intensifies* the theological claim. The combination — softened verb, intensified theological frame — is exegetically and ethically loaded, and we will return to it.

## Lens 3: The Larger Story

Psalm 106 sits in **Book IV of the Psalter (Psalms 90–106)** at the structural climax: it is the last psalm before Book V opens with the great hallel-collection of Psalm 107. Gerald Wilson's canonical-shape thesis, refined by David Mitchell, James Mays, J. Clinton McCann, and Hossfeld-Zenger, reads the seam between Books IV and V as the answer to the Davidic-collapse question of Books I–III. The collapse was real (Psalm 89's "How long?"); the answer is twofold: God's ancient kingship over creation (the *Yahweh-mālāk* psalms of Book IV) and Israel's honest historical confession (Psalms 105–106). Psalm 106 in particular delivers the *negative* recital that explains why exile happened — Israel's repeated covenant infidelity — and *also* the plea (v. 47) that God remember and gather. The plea is then answered in Book V's opening proclamation of restoration (Psalm 107:1–3: "Let the redeemed of the LORD say so, whom he has redeemed from trouble and gathered in from the lands").

The structural logic is precise. Books I–III tell the rise, reign, and ruin of David's house. Book IV answers with: the LORD reigns, he has been our dwelling place from before David, the covenant with Abraham endures, and Israel's failure is real but not final. Psalm 106 is the deepest articulation of that "failure is real": it walks through every major failure tradition in the Pentateuch and the Conquest, names them, and confesses them. Then verse 47's plea — "Save us, gather us" — turns the recital of failure into a request for restoration. Book V opens with the answer.

The **Jewish trajectory** treats Psalm 106 as a foundational penitential text. The *Midrash Tehillim* on Psalm 106 reads the recital of failures as the model for honest national repentance. Rashi reads each episode straightforwardly, drawing on the Pentateuchal narratives. Ibn Ezra is more lexically precise and notes the Hebrew variations between the psalm's recitation and the Pentateuchal source. Radak (David Kimchi) emphasizes the psalm's use as a guide for exilic prayer: every Jew in exile prays this psalm not as a relic of the past but as a confession of the cycles continuing in his own generation. Nachmanides reads the Phinehas episode in particular through his Kabbalistic system, where Phinehas's zeal is the channeling of *gevurah* (judgment) into *tiferet* (harmony) — a reading that some later interpreters extended in troubling directions.

Liturgically, Psalm 106 is associated with fast days and penitential seasons. The opening verse — "Oh give thanks to the LORD, for he is good, for his steadfast love endures forever" — is one of the most quoted formulas in Jewish liturgy (it recurs in Psalms 107, 118, 136 and is woven into the *Hodu* of the daily service). The psalm's confessional structure has shaped the *Selichot* tradition.

Among modern Jewish thinkers, Martin Buber treats the psalm as evidence of the prophetic-confessional honesty that distinguishes biblical religion: only a tradition that can confess its own failures has a future. Abraham Joshua Heschel's reading of the prophetic tradition treats Psalm 106 as a key text for "prophetic memory" — the memory that holds failure honestly so that repentance is possible. Jon Levenson and Nahum Sarna both attend to the psalm's relationship to the Pentateuchal narrative tradition. The Phinehas episode has been a site of difficult reckoning in modern Jewish thought: while traditional readings honor Phinehas as the model of *qin'ah* (zeal for God), modern Jewish thinkers have raised serious questions about the celebration of religious violence. Israeli scholar Moshe Halbertal has written carefully about the troubled Phinehas legacy in Jewish tradition, particularly as it has been invoked in modern political contexts.

The **Christian trajectory** is dense and complicated. Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos* on Psalm 105 (LXX numbering) reads the recital of failures as a description of sin's universality: the failures of Israel are the failures of every Christian, and the psalm teaches honest confession. Cassiodorus and the medieval commentators follow this Augustinian line. Aquinas treats the Phinehas verse 31 in the *Summa* (II-II, q. 108) as evidence for the legitimacy of zealous defense of the faith, a reading that has been historically consequential and ethically problematic. Luther's exposition emphasizes the psalm as a sermon on *simul iustus et peccator* — the believer is simultaneously righteous and sinner — and finds in the cycle of failure-and-deliverance the gospel pattern of grace overcoming sin. Calvin's commentary on Psalm 106 is one of his sharpest pastoral treatments: he reads the psalm as the confessional school where the believer learns to see his own face in Israel's history and to ask for the same mercy.

Modern Christian scholarship has paid increasing attention to Psalm 106 as the *honest* counterpoint to triumphalist historical readings. Walter Brueggemann's *Israel's Praise* and *The Message of the Psalms* treat Psalm 106 as the paradigmatic "psalm of disorientation" within the historical recital tradition — the psalm that refuses to clean up the story for cultic purposes. Patrick Miller, James Mays, Beth Tanner, J. Clinton McCann, William P. Brown, and Hossfeld-Zenger all converge on the psalm's importance for the canonical shape of Book IV. Erhard Gerstenberger reads the psalm as Second Temple penitential liturgy. John Goldingay's commentary attends carefully to the psalm's relationship to its Pentateuchal sources and to Psalm 105. David Mitchell treats Psalms 105–106 as the historical-confessional foundation for Book V's restoration theology. Gerald Wilson, in his original canonical-shape work, identified the Books IV–V seam as the structural climax of the entire Psalter.

The **New Testament** echoes Psalm 106 in several places. 1 Corinthians 10:1–13 — Paul's recital of Israel's wilderness failures as warnings to the Corinthian church — is essentially Psalm 106's structure applied to a new community: the failures of Israel are typological warnings for those who think they stand. Romans 1:23's "exchanged the glory of the immortal God for images" alludes to Psalm 106:20 ("they exchanged the glory of God for the image of an ox that eats grass"). Stephen's speech in Acts 7 recites Israel's failures in the Psalm 106 manner. Hebrews 3–4's wilderness warnings draw on the same tradition. The Phinehas verse 31 ("counted to him as righteousness") shadows Paul's argument in Romans 4 about Abraham, where the same verb is applied to faith rather than zealous violence — a deliberate Pauline reframing.

## Lens 4: The Theological Intent

The first hard tension is **the Phinehas episode and the "righteousness through zealous violence" tradition**. Numbers 25 narrates that during Israel's encampment at Shittim, Israelite men engaged in sexual relations with Moabite and Midianite women and joined in the worship of Baal of Peor. A plague broke out. Phinehas, grandson of Aaron, took a spear and drove it through an Israelite man and a Midianite woman in flagrante delicto. The plague stopped. God declared his "covenant of perpetual priesthood" with Phinehas because of his *qin'ah* — zeal. Psalm 106 picks up this episode and adds the Genesis 15:6 echo: *vatēḥāšev lô liṣədāqâ* — "and that was counted to him as righteousness." The same verb-formula used of Abraham's faith is now used of Phinehas's spear-thrust.

Three positions stake out the territory.

The first is the **traditional reading**: Phinehas's zeal is genuinely righteous, and the psalm is right to honor him. The killing was theologically justified because it stopped the plague and ended the apostasy. 1 Maccabees 2:24–26 invokes Phinehas as the model for Mattathias's zealous killing at the start of the Maccabean revolt; the Zealot tradition in late Second Temple Judaism honors Phinehas as patron; Christian crusader theology has repeatedly invoked him. The strength of this reading is that it takes the biblical text at face value. The weakness — and it is severe — is that it has been used to justify religious violence across Christian and Jewish history with consequences that the text itself, reread, would not endorse.

The second is the **chastened reading**: Phinehas's act was theologically effective in the immediate context but is not a general template for religious zeal. The narrative singularizes the act ("Phinehas stood up") rather than universalizing it; the psalm's word *vayəfallēl* — "he intervened/mediated/prayed" — softens the killing into something closer to priestly intervention than to violent zeal. The Genesis 15:6 echo elevates the *theological* significance without endorsing the *means*. On this reading, the episode is one of many instances in the Hebrew Bible where the text records something it does not necessarily commend as a model. James Mays, John Goldingay, and Patrick Miller hold versions of this position. The strength is its hermeneutical care; the weakness is that it can sound like reading against the grain of the text.

The third is the **prophetic-critical reading**: the Phinehas tradition is honored by *one* stream of biblical literature (Numbers, Psalm 106, 1 Maccabees, parts of Sirach) but is *implicitly critiqued* by another stream — the prophetic tradition that condemns violent zeal in God's name (Hosea 1:4 explicitly condemns the "blood of Jezreel" that Jehu spilled in zealous purge of Baal worship; Isaiah 65 redefines the messianic age as one without violence; Jesus's "you have heard it said... but I say to you" of Matthew 5 takes the *opposite* trajectory from violent zeal). On this reading, the canon contains the Phinehas tradition *and* the prophetic correction of that tradition, and the church's task is to read both together. James Crossley, Walter Brueggemann (in his more polemical mode), and a number of modern New Testament scholars hold versions of this view. The strength is its recognition of intra-canonical tension; the weakness is that it can flatten the text's complexity into a too-easy progressive narrative.

**Where this leaves us**: the Phinehas verse cannot be excised, and it cannot be triumphally celebrated. It must be read with full awareness of how it has been weaponized — from Mattathias to the Zealots, from medieval crusaders to modern religious militants — and with full awareness that the canonical tradition itself contains corrective resources. The verse 31 echo of Genesis 15:6 is not a license for religious violence; it is a theologically loaded claim that should be read alongside Romans 4's careful redirection of the same formula to Abraham's *faith*. The honest response is neither to celebrate the verse nor to excise it but to read it within the canonical and ethical conversation that surrounds it.

The second hard tension is **the cycle of failure pattern and what it makes of Israel's covenantal-survival theology**. The psalm narrates eight or nine episodes of failure spanning from Egypt through wilderness through conquest through judges through exile. The pattern is: Israel forgets, Israel rebels, God judges, Israel cries out, God delivers, Israel forgets again. Verse 13's *miharû šākhəḥû* — "they soon forgot" — captures the entire pattern. Verses 43–46 explicitly state the cycle: "Many times he delivered them, but they were rebellious in their purposes... Nevertheless, he looked upon their distress when he heard their cry. For their sake he remembered his covenant."

Three positions on what to make of this cycle.

The first reading sees the cycle as **the structure of grace**. God's faithfulness operates *within* and *despite* Israel's failure; the cycle is not evidence against the covenant but evidence of its depth. Augustine, Luther, Calvin, and most of the Christian tradition read the cycle this way: the gospel is precisely that God's mercy meets repeated failure with repeated mercy. The strength is its theological depth. The weakness is that it can soften the seriousness of the failure.

The second reading sees the cycle as **the structure of judgment**. The cycle is real and the failures are real, and the cumulative weight of failure is what brings about exile. The psalm's plea in v. 47 — "save us, gather us" — is asked from the position of one who has earned the exile and knows it. Ezekiel's theology, where the exile is the just consequence of generations of covenant violation, fits here. The strength is its honesty about consequence. The weakness is that it can flatten God's role into mere reactor to Israel's actions.

The third reading sees the cycle as **the dialectic of covenantal life**. The cycle is neither pure grace nor pure judgment but the ongoing tension between divine fidelity and human infidelity that *is* the covenant in time. The covenant is not a one-time arrangement but a continuing conversation in which both parties act, fail, repent, return. Brueggemann's "psalms of orientation, disorientation, new orientation" framework applies here: the cycle is the structure of covenantal life, not a problem to be solved. Hossfeld-Zenger and most modern continental scholars hold versions of this view. The strength is its dynamism; the weakness is that it can underplay the asymmetry between God and Israel.

**Where this leaves us**: the cycle is real, the asymmetry is real (God's faithfulness does not equal Israel's faithfulness), and the psalm's plea in v. 47 acknowledges that the cycle has not ended. The post-exilic community prays this psalm not as a memorial of past failure but as a confession that the cycle continues in the present. That confession — *both we and our fathers have sinned* (v. 6) — collapses the temporal distance between past and present and makes the psalm a permanent prayer of God's people. The Davidic-failure of Books I–III is not the only failure; it is the visible apex of a longer pattern, and Psalm 106 traces that pattern back to Egypt.

The third tension is **the relationship between the historical confession and the editorial doxology**. Verse 48 — "Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, from everlasting to everlasting" — is the editorial seam closing Book IV. It is structurally distinct from the psalm proper; it is the kind of liturgical formula that closes each book of the Psalter. But it is *also* the canonical conclusion to the historical confession. The juxtaposition is theologically loaded: a recital of failure ending in eternal blessing.

Three readings.

The first sees the doxology as **liturgical formality** — a conventional closing without deep connection to the preceding psalm. On this reading, the doxology is editorial furniture, like the page-numbers in a book. The strength is its honesty about editorial work. The weakness is that it underestimates how much theological work editorial juxtaposition does.

The second sees the doxology as **the answer to the confession**. The recital of failure ends, structurally, with a blessing of the eternal God. The doxology says: even as we confess failure, even as we plead for gathering from exile, even as the cycle of failure continues, God is to be blessed forever. The juxtaposition is the editorial argument that Israel's failure is not the last word about God. McCann, Wilson, Mitchell, and Hossfeld-Zenger lean toward versions of this reading. The strength is its theological seriousness about the editorial work.

The third sees the doxology as **the bridge to Book V**. The "blessed be the LORD, from everlasting to everlasting" echoes forward into Book V's restoration theology, where Psalm 107's "Oh give thanks to the LORD, for he is good, for his steadfast love endures forever" picks up the same vocabulary that opened Psalm 106. The doxology is not just the closing of Book IV but the hinge between the historical confession of Book IV and the restoration of Book V. The strength is its attention to canonical flow; the weakness is that it can over-read the editorial intentionality.

**Where this leaves us**: the doxology is editorial *and* theologically loaded *and* canonically pivotal. It is the place where the Psalter's editors say, after the longest and bleakest historical confession in the Psalter, *blessed be the LORD forever*. That juxtaposition is not naive; it is the considered claim of a community that has lost the temple, the throne, and the land, and is praying its way home through honest confession into doxological hope.

**Closing question**: If the canonical sequence of Book IV teaches that the LORD's kingship outlasts David's, that creation outlasts collapse, that fidelity outlasts failure — and if all of this is asked from inside the exile, with the plea "save us, gather us" still hanging unresolved at the seam — then what does it mean to live as a community whose primary act of faith is to sing the eternal blessing of God *while still in the middle of the cycle*, neither pretending the cycle has ended nor letting its persistence dissolve the song?

## The Thread

Psalm 106 closes Book IV by completing what Psalm 105 began. Psalm 105 sang God's covenant fidelity through Abraham, Joseph, the Exodus, and the Conquest; Psalm 106 confesses Israel's covenant infidelity through the same history and into exile. The pair holds both claims without dissolving either. The Davidic crisis of Books I–III turns out not to be a mystery but a chapter in a longer pattern — the cycle of forgetting and remembering, rebellion and rescue, that runs from the Red Sea forward. The exile is the cycle's latest expression, not its conclusion. The editorial doxology at v. 48 puts the entire confession within the bracket of eternal blessing, and Book V opens immediately with Psalm 107's announcement that the redeemed have been gathered from the lands. Book IV ends in the middle of the cycle; Book V claims the cycle's turning.
