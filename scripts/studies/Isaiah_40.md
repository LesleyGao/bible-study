# Isaiah 40 — Go Deeper

Isaiah 40 is the hinge on which the entire book turns. After thirty-nine chapters of judgment, indictment, and threatened catastrophe, the voice changes. The Babylonian conquest that loomed in chapters 1–39 has now happened; Jerusalem lies in ruins; the Davidic dynasty is gone; the temple is destroyed; the people are in exile. And into that night comes a voice that says, simply, *Comfort, comfort my people*. Few chapters in the Hebrew Bible have shaped Jewish liturgy, Christian theology, and Western imagination more deeply. Handel set its lines to music; the Gospels read it back onto John the Baptist; rabbinic tradition reads it forward into messianic hope; the prophet's question *to whom then will you compare me?* became the Hebrew Bible's most concentrated articulation of monotheism.

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

**Second Isaiah begins here.** Since Johann Christoph Döderlein's 1775 commentary and especially Bernhard Duhm's 1892 *Das Buch Jesaia*, mainstream-critical scholarship has divided the book of Isaiah into at least two and usually three sections: First Isaiah (chs. 1–39), the work of the eighth-century Jerusalem prophet (active c. 740–700 BC); Second Isaiah or Deutero-Isaiah (chs. 40–55), an anonymous exilic prophet writing in Babylon around 545–540 BC, just before Cyrus the Persian conquered Babylon in 539 BC; and Third Isaiah (chs. 56–66), a postexilic voice or voices working in restored Judah after 538 BC. The evidence for this composite-authorship view is cumulative: Cyrus is named explicitly in 44:28 and 45:1 as Yahweh's anointed shepherd, which would require the eighth-century Isaiah to have named a Persian king some 150 years before he ruled; the audience throughout chapters 40–55 is presupposed to be in exile (40:1–2, 49:14–21, 51:11) rather than threatened with future exile; and the literary style — long flowing strophes, repeated *fear-not* oracles, hymnic creator-doxologies, courtroom-trial speeches against the gods of the nations — differs sharply from the staccato judgment oracles of chapters 1–39. Duhm further isolated four "Servant Songs" within chapters 40–55 (42:1–9, 49:1–13, 50:4–11, 52:13–53:12), a literary judgment that has shaped scholarship for over a century.

The major modern critical commentaries on Second Isaiah include Claus Westermann's *Isaiah 40–66* in the Old Testament Library (1969), Joseph Blenkinsopp's three-volume Anchor Bible commentary (2000–2003), John Goldingay's massive ICC *Isaiah 40–55* (2006, with David Payne), Roy Melugin's *The Formation of Isaiah 40–55* (1976), Patricia Tull Willey's *Remember the Former Things* (1997), and James Muilenburg's classic Interpreter's Bible essay (1956). Conservative-evangelical scholars — J. Alec Motyer in *The Prophecy of Isaiah*, John Oswalt in the NICOT, Edward J. Young in his three-volume commentary — defend Isaianic unity by appeal to prophetic foreknowledge and to literary echoes between the sections, though even most evangelical scholars now grant some role for later editorial expansion. Brevard Childs's canonical-criticism approach, articulated in *Isaiah* (OTL, 2001), brackets the historical question and reads the book as a unified canonical witness, since whatever its compositional history, the synagogue and church received it as one book. For the chapter-by-chapter reading that follows, both questions matter: the historical setting in Babylonian exile illuminates the rhetorical force of *comfort* spoken to a defeated people, while the canonical shape connects Second Isaiah's promises back to First Isaiah's threats and forward to Third Isaiah's struggles.

The ancient Near Eastern parallel that anchors Second Isaiah's historical setting is the **Cyrus Cylinder**, a clay barrel inscription discovered at Babylon in 1879 and now in the British Museum. The Cylinder, dating to 539–538 BC, records Cyrus's conquest of Babylon and his policy of returning displaced peoples and restoring their gods to their home temples — precisely the policy that allowed the Judean exiles to return and rebuild the temple under Zerubbabel. Cyrus presents himself in the Cylinder as chosen by Marduk, the chief Babylonian god, to set things right; Second Isaiah, with stunning theological audacity, claims that the same Cyrus is in fact the anointed of Yahweh (Isaiah 45:1), unwittingly serving the God of Israel.

Several Hebrew terms in Isaiah 40 anchor the chapter's theology. *Naḥămû naḥămû ʿammî* (נַחֲמוּ נַחֲמוּ עַמִּי), *comfort, comfort my people* (40:1), uses the doubled imperative for emphatic intensity; the verb *nāḥam* carries both the sense of consoling and of changing one's mind, and here Yahweh's relenting from judgment becomes consolation for the wounded. *Qôl qôrēʾ* (קוֹל קוֹרֵא), *a voice cries* (40:3), names the speaker only as a voice, anonymizing the herald in the way Second Isaiah's own author remains anonymous. *Kol-bāśār ḥāṣîr* (כָּל־בָּשָׂר חָצִיר), *all flesh is grass* (40:6), uses the noun *bāśār* for human flesh in its mortality and *ḥāṣîr* for the wild grass that springs up after rain in the Judean wilderness and withers within weeks. *Dəbar-ʾĕlōhênû yāqûm ləʿôlām* (דְּבַר־אֱלֹהֵינוּ יָקוּם לְעוֹלָם), *the word of our God will stand forever* (40:8), opposes the *dābār* of God to the perishing flesh. And the climactic *qôyê YHWH yaḥălîpû kōaḥ yaʿălû ʾēber kannəšārîm* (קוֹוֵי יְהוָה יַחֲלִיפוּ כֹחַ יַעֲלוּ אֵבֶר כַּנְּשָׁרִים), *those who wait for Yahweh shall renew strength, they shall mount up with wings like eagles* (40:31), uses the verb *qāwâ*, to wait expectantly, as the posture of exilic faith.

## Lens 2 — Literature

Isaiah 40 displays the characteristic literary forms that will run through chapters 40–55: prophetic poetry of consolation, the courtroom-trial-speech genre against false gods, hymnic creator-material, and the disputation. The chapter opens (vv. 1–11) with what Westermann called the *prologue* to Second Isaiah, a heavenly-council scene in which divine voices commission a herald to announce return from exile. This is followed by a creator-hymn (vv. 12–26) built on rhetorical questions — *who has measured the waters in the hollow of his hand?* — that overwhelm the imagination by piling cosmic scale on cosmic scale. The hymn turns into a disputation against idols (vv. 18–20), where the prophet pictures with deadpan precision the absurdity of a craftsman casting a god, overlaying it with gold, and bolting it down so it will not topple. The chapter closes (vv. 27–31) with a direct address to Jacob/Israel that gathers the chapter's themes into pastoral encouragement: the Creator who never faints renews the strength of those who wait.

The poetry uses Hebrew parallelism with unusual flexibility. Synonymous parallelism dominates (*The grass withers, the flower fades*), but Second Isaiah also deploys staircase-parallelism, where a line builds on the previous through repetition and elaboration (*lift up your voice with strength, O Jerusalem, herald of good news; lift it up, fear not*). The famous strophe of 40:30–31 uses contrast-parallelism: youths faint and fall exhausted, *but* those who wait mount up. Alliteration, assonance, and word-play intensify particular lines (*ḥāṣîr* / *ʾāḏām*, grass / humanity), though much is lost in English translation.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

For Jewish tradition, Isaiah 40 is one of the great consolation passages of the Hebrew Bible. In the synagogue lectionary cycle, the chapter is read as the *haftarah* for Parashat Va'etchanan, the first of the seven *haftarot* of consolation following the fast of Tisha B'Av, which mourns the destruction of the temples. The opening *naḥămû naḥămû* gives the Sabbath its name — *Shabbat Naḥamu*, the Sabbath of consolation. Rashi (eleventh century) reads the doubled comfort as addressed to Israel after their exile, with prophets enjoined to console the people. Ibn Ezra (twelfth century) was among the first medieval Jewish commentators to suggest that Isaiah 40 onward might be the work of a later prophet, anticipating modern critical scholarship by six centuries; the suggestion was rejected by most later Jewish exegetes, including Radak (Kimhi, twelfth–thirteenth century) and Abarbanel (fifteenth century), but its existence within the rabbinic tradition is significant. The Targum Jonathan paraphrases the chapter to emphasize repentance and the promised return. Modern Jewish thinkers — Jon Levenson at Harvard, Daniel Boyarin at Berkeley, the late Nahum Sarna — have read Second Isaiah as the high-water mark of biblical monotheism, the moment when the Hebrew tradition makes the universal claim that Yahweh is not merely Israel's god but the only God.

For Christian tradition, Isaiah 40 is foundational for the New Testament's self-understanding. All four Gospels apply 40:3 to John the Baptist (Mark 1:3, Matthew 3:3, Luke 3:4–6, John 1:23), making the Baptist's prophetic identity rest on Second Isaiah's *voice in the wilderness*. The Septuagint's parsing of the verse — *a voice crying in the wilderness, prepare the way of the Lord* — slightly shifts the emphasis from the Hebrew, where the wilderness is the location of the highway being prepared rather than the location of the voice; the Gospel writers follow the Greek. First Peter 1:24–25 quotes 40:6–8 directly: *all flesh is grass… but the word of the Lord remains forever* — and immediately glosses *the word of the Lord* as the gospel preached. Justin Martyr in his second-century *Dialogue with Trypho* uses Isaiah 40 to argue Christ's pre-existence; Origen reads the *highway* allegorically as the soul's preparation; Jerome translates the chapter into the Vulgate's lapidary Latin (*consolamini consolamini populus meus*). Aquinas in his *Lectura super Isaiam* connects 40:3 both to John the Baptist and to the soul's contemplative ascent. Calvin in his *Commentary on Isaiah* reads the chapter as comfort for the church under persecution. Modern critical Christian scholarship — Westermann, Blenkinsopp, Walter Brueggemann — reads the chapter primarily in its sixth-century BC setting, while granting the legitimacy of canonical and Christological re-readings. Brueggemann's *Theology of the Old Testament* devotes substantial attention to what he calls Second Isaiah's *comfort tradition*, the prophetic voice that refuses to let suffering have the last word.

Handel's *Messiah* (1741) opens with three movements drawn from Isaiah 40: the tenor recitative *Comfort ye my people* (40:1–3), the air *Every valley shall be exalted* (40:4), and the chorus *And the glory of the Lord* (40:5). The chapter has shaped how the English-speaking world hears the Christmas season more than any other passage in the Hebrew Bible.

## Lens 4 — Theological Intent

Two tensions run through Isaiah 40 and must be named honestly.

The first is the **composite-authorship question** as it bears on the doctrine of inspiration. If Second Isaiah is in fact an anonymous exilic prophet of the sixth century, not the eighth-century Isaiah of Jerusalem, what does this do to the church's traditional reading of Isaiah as a single inspired book? Three positions deserve to be heard. The conservative-evangelical position (Motyer, Oswalt, Young) defends Isaianic unity and treats prophetic foreknowledge as an integral part of inspiration; the chapter's prediction-of-Cyrus material in 44–45 is precisely what makes it powerful as inspired prophecy. The mainstream-critical position (Westermann, Blenkinsopp, Goldingay) treats the composite authorship as established and reads inspiration not as authorial but as the spirit's work in the canonical process; the anonymous exilic prophet was no less inspired for being anonymous, and the editors who joined his work to First Isaiah's were doing the spirit's work too. The canonical-criticism position (Childs) brackets the historical question altogether: whatever the book's compositional history, the synagogue and church received it as Isaiah, and the canonical shape is itself the locus of inspiration. The Catholic *Catechism* §107–108 and the Reformed *sola scriptura* tradition can both, in fact, accommodate the critical position, because both root inspiration in the church's reception of the canonical text rather than in any specific theory of authorship. Steelmanned: a thoughtful evangelical can grant that Second Isaiah is exilic without losing inspiration; a thoughtful critical scholar can grant that the canonical shape matters without surrendering historical method.

The second tension is the **John the Baptist appropriation**. Christian readers from the Gospels onward have read 40:3 as fulfilled in John, but the prophet himself was speaking to and about the sixth-century BC return from Babylon. Is the New Testament reading therefore a misreading? Three positions. The traditional Christian position holds that the prophets spoke better than they knew, and that the New Testament's Christological readings do not erase the historical referent but extend it; the historical exodus from Babylon and the eschatological exodus accomplished in Christ are linked by typology, not by erasure. The mainstream-critical position grants the historical referent priority and treats the New Testament reading as theological appropriation — legitimate within the church's hermeneutic, but distinct from the prophet's intent. The Jewish position, articulated in modern dialogue by figures like Levenson and Boyarin, asks Christians to recognize the priority of Israel's reading and to be cautious about supersessionism; Second Isaiah's *comfort* was spoken to exiled Israel, and any Christian appropriation must hold that reading sacred even as it adds its own. Where this leaves us: Christological readings of Isaiah 40 are part of the Christian inheritance and need not be surrendered, but they should be received with awareness that the prophet first spoke to and for a defeated, exiled people whose hope was the rebuilding of Jerusalem, not the birth of Jesus seven centuries later.

The chapter's theology of **the word of God that stands forever** (40:8) is what holds these tensions together. Whether spoken first by an eighth-century prophet or a sixth-century one, whether read in the synagogue at Tisha B'Av or in the cathedral at Advent, whether glossed by the rabbis or by the Gospel writers, the *dābār* outlasts every empire that thought it had silenced God's people.

Where this leaves us: a chapter that comforts not by denying the loss but by re-anchoring the universe — the same God who measures the waters in the hollow of his hand also gathers the lambs in his arms.

Closing question: When Second Isaiah heard the voice say *cry*, his first response was *what shall I cry? all flesh is grass*. What does it cost a prophet — or any of us — to speak comfort to a people whose lives have just been incinerated, knowing that we ourselves are grass?
