# Lamentations 5: The Alphabet Runs Out

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The book ends without ending. Twenty-two verses — the alphabetic count is preserved — but the acrostic itself is gone. Where chapters 1, 2, and 4 had walked the reader letter by letter from *aleph* to *taw*, and chapter 3 had tripled the discipline into sixty-six rungs of alphabetical scaffolding, chapter 5 simply lays the verses down without the framework. Norman Gottwald reads this as the moment the formal containment of grief has collapsed. Adele Berlin reads it as a final restraint exercised by leaving the verse-count intact while abandoning the acrostic — the form is wounded but recognizable. F.W. Dobbs-Allsopp argues the broken acrostic is theological argument: the alphabet of grief has been spoken; what remains is petition without ornament.

The historical setting is the aftermath of 586 BCE — but now significantly removed in time. Verses 7-8 imply the speaker is a generation past the destruction. The fathers have sinned and "are no more"; the children inherit consequences. The phrase "slaves rule over us" (v. 8) reflects the post-destruction administration: the Neo-Babylonian Empire stationed regional governors and provincial officials over conquered territories, often promoted Babylonian functionaries who had themselves been slaves or low-born administrators. The land of Judah has been incorporated into the province of Yehud; native rule has ended. Verse 18's "jackals prowl over [Mount Zion]" suggests a Jerusalem that has stood ruined long enough for desert fauna to colonize it — the city had not yet been resettled in any organized way.

Material culture saturates the catalogue. Verse 4's "we must pay for the water we drink; the wood we get must be bought" is concrete economic detail. In a functioning Judahite agrarian economy, water from common cisterns and wood from common groves were communal goods, regulated by clan and village custom. Under Babylonian administration, these became taxable resources or were privatized into the hands of foreign settlers. The verse names the indignity of paying for what had been ancestrally free. Verse 13's "young men are compelled to grind at the mill" refers to the rotary millstone — work normally done by slave women (Exodus 11:5, Matthew 24:41) or pack animals; for free Judahite young men to be assigned millstone labor was a degradation of social order. Samson, blinded, was forced to grind at the mill in Judges 16:21 — Lam 5:13 echoes the Samson humiliation. Verse 14's "old men have left the city gate" describes the collapse of legal-civic life: the gate was the place where elders judged disputes (Ruth 4:1-12 records Boaz's negotiation at the Bethlehem gate; Deuteronomy 21:19, 22:15 prescribe gate-justice). When the elders leave the gate, the legal community has dissolved. Verse 14's "young men their music" is paired with verse 15's "our dancing has been turned to mourning" — music and dance, which had structured Israelite festival life (Exodus 15:20, Psalm 149:3, 150:4), are absent.

Verse 11 names what most ancient siege literature glosses over: rape. "Women are raped in Zion, young women in the towns of Judah." The Hebrew verb is *'innah* (עִנָּה) — the same verb used in Genesis 34:2 (Dinah), Deuteronomy 22:24, 2 Samuel 13:14 (Tamar). It denotes specifically sexual violation. The verse acknowledges what the women of conquered cities suffered when foreign armies entered. Phyllis Trible's *Texts of Terror* and Cheryl Exum's work have insisted that this verse cannot be read past — the chapter's catalog of communal suffering must include the specifically gendered violence the conquerors inflicted, recorded by the lament without euphemism.

Verse 6's "we have given the hand to Egypt, and to Assyria, to get bread enough" reflects the diaspora reality: some Judahites fled to Egypt after the destruction (Jeremiah 41-44 records the flight under Johanan after Gedaliah's assassination), and others ended up in the territory of the former Assyrian Empire (now part of Babylonia). The "hand" (*yad*, יָד) given is the hand of submission or alliance; to "give the hand" is the gesture of dependent fealty (2 Kings 10:15, 1 Chronicles 29:24). Survival required submission to the very empires from whom the prophets had warned Judah to keep distance.

Five Hebrew terms organize the chapter. *Zekhor* (זְכֹר, v. 1), "remember," is the imperative addressed to the LORD — the chapter opens by asking God to do what God had failed to do in 2:1 ("he has not remembered his footstool"). The verb *zakhar* is theologically loaded: divine remembering is what initiates rescue throughout the Hebrew Bible (Genesis 8:1's flood, 19:29's Sodom, Exodus 2:24's Egypt). To ask God to remember is to ask for the next exodus. *Nachalah* (נַחֲלָה, v. 2), "inheritance," is the covenant land-grant vocabulary — Joshua's distribution of the land to the tribes was *nachalah*; its loss to "strangers" is covenant reversal. *Ketem* (כֶּתֶם, v. 16), "crown," signifies royal-Davidic dignity; "the crown has fallen from our head" can be read literally (Zedekiah's deposition) or communally (Israel's loss of national status). *Hashivenu* (הֲשִׁיבֵנוּ, v. 21), "restore us," is the petitioner's verb — the Hebrew sense is closer to "turn us back" or "make us return," with the implicit recognition that the people cannot return on their own. *Ki im* (כִּי אִם, v. 22), "unless / but / for surely," is the chapter's most contested phrase, governing what some read as an unbearable conditional and others as a stark assertion.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The chapter's most striking literary feature is what is missing. Twenty-two verses preserves the alphabetic count of the previous four chapters, but the *aleph-bet* sequence is gone. The form announces its own incompletion. F.W. Dobbs-Allsopp argues this is the book's structural climax: the discipline of the acrostic was the aesthetic argument that grief could be contained; chapter 5 confesses the containment has not held. Tod Linafelt reads it differently: the alphabet has been *spoken*, and what remains is plain prayer. Either reading attends to the form's deliberate erosion.

The chapter is the only one in the book that is entirely first-person plural ("we, our, us") from beginning to end. Where chapter 1 had been Zion personified, chapter 2 the narrator-poet, chapter 3 the *geber* and the community, chapter 4 the third-person observer with first-person plural intrusions — chapter 5 is the unbroken communal voice. The pronoun shift performs the chapter's theological move: the lament has finally become liturgy. The community speaks, not the soloist.

The verses are also the shortest in the book. Where chapters 1 and 2 had three-line verses, chapter 3 had one-line verses but in tight triplets per letter, and chapter 4 had two-line verses, chapter 5 has single-line verses — twenty-two of them. The form has compressed. Adele Berlin notes the contraction is itself a kind of exhaustion: the speaker has run out of breath for elaborated lament and now offers naked petition.

The chapter is structurally a *prayer*, not a poem about a prayer. The Latin Vulgate adds the superscription *Oratio Hieremiae prophetae* — "The Prayer of Jeremiah the Prophet" — preserved in Catholic and Eastern Orthodox liturgies. The Greek Septuagint similarly heads the chapter as a prayer. The genre shift from lament to petition is the book's final formal move: after four chapters of lament, the survivors finally turn directly to God and ask.

Verse 22's *ki im* is the chapter's textual crux. The phrase has multiple possible renderings:

- *Conditional*: "unless you have utterly rejected us" (NRSV, ESV) — a despairing possibility introduced as a condition that may obtain.
- *Concessive*: "though you have utterly rejected us, though you remain exceedingly angry with us" — affirming the petition despite the rejection.
- *Causal-emphatic*: "for indeed you have rejected us" (LXX, Vulgate's *sed proiciens reppulisti nos*) — a flat assertion that the rejection is the case.
- *Interrogative-rhetorical*: "have you utterly rejected us?" (Targum's reading) — the rejection as question, not assertion.

Linafelt's *Surviving Lamentations* (2000) builds an entire argument around the verse's irresolution: the Hebrew refuses to commit to any of the readings, and the canon has preserved the irresolution. The book ends not in restoration but in the unresolved possibility that the petition may not be heard. The Masoretic tradition, conscious of how unbearable v. 22 sounds as a final word, instituted the liturgical practice (still observed in synagogue Tisha B'Av reading) of repeating verse 21 ("Restore us to yourself, O LORD, that we may be restored! Renew our days as of old") *after* verse 22, so the book's last spoken word is the petition for restoration. The same convention applies to the ends of Isaiah, the Twelve Prophets (Malachi), and Ecclesiastes — books that end on uncomfortable notes have v. 21 (or its equivalent) ritually re-spoken so the listener does not exit on rejection.

The chapter draws on the language of communal lament psalms (Psalms 44, 74, 79, 80, 89). Compare especially Psalm 80:3, 7, 19's threefold "Restore us, O God; let your face shine, that we may be saved" — the same imperative *hashivenu* that governs Lam 5:21. Westermann argues Lamentations 5 is Lamentations' deliberate move into the form of a communal lament psalm, completing the book's formal arc: from the Mesopotamian-style city lament of chapter 1 to the wisdom-influenced psalm-form of chapter 5.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Lamentations 5 sits at one of the canon's most ethically demanding moments. The chapter ends a book preserved in Scripture that does not resolve — that may, depending on how one reads v. 22, end on rejection. The chapter's reception history is a long meditation on what to do with a Scripture that does not close.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

The Talmudic and Masoretic instruction to repeat v. 21 after v. 22 is one of the most theologically significant liturgical decisions in Jewish tradition. The instruction does not erase v. 22 — the verse remains in the text, read aloud, given its full unbearable weight — but the community refuses to *exit* on it. The verse is heard, then the petition is reasserted. This is interpretation as liturgical practice rather than commentary. Masoretes preserved the instruction as a *seder* (ordering rubric) at the end of Lamentations.

Eikhah Rabbah on chapter 5 develops v. 7 ("our fathers sinned, and are no more; and we bear their iniquities") at length. The midrash sets the verse against Ezekiel 18:20's "the son shall not bear the iniquity of the father" and against Deuteronomy 24:16 — and refuses to flatten the contradiction. The rabbinic position: both verses are true. Sometimes children inherit consequences (Lam 5:7) without inheriting culpability; sometimes generations are theologically connected even when each is morally distinct. The midrash has the patriarchs themselves complain about v. 7 in heavenly court.

Rashi (11th c.) reads v. 22 as a question, not an assertion: "Surely you have not utterly rejected us?" The reading softens the verse without removing it. Ibn Ezra reads *ki im* as concessive — the petition is made *even though* God's rejection appears to remain. Maimonides treats the chapter's communal prayer-form as the proper communal response to catastrophe; Lam 5 becomes a model for the *Selichot* prayers of penitential seasons.

The Hasidic masters made v. 21's *hashivenu* a touchstone. Rabbi Nachman of Breslov (1772-1810) preached repeatedly on the verse: the soul cannot return to God on its own; the petition is to be turned. The Sefat Emet (Yehudah Aryeh Leib Alter, 19th c.) read v. 19 ("you, O LORD, reign forever") as the liturgical key — even within the lament, the kingship is affirmed; this affirmation is what makes v. 21's petition possible.

The post-Holocaust Jewish reading reorients the chapter. Eliezer Berkovits, Yitzhak Greenberg, and David Hartman all read v. 22 as the verse the twentieth century made unavoidable. *Ki im* — "unless" — is no longer hypothetical for survivors. The question is whether prayer remains possible in the genuine possibility of rejection. The answer the survivors gave (Berkovits: *Faith After the Holocaust*) was yes, but not as confidence; as commitment. Tod Linafelt's reading depends on this Jewish theological tradition: Lamentations ends in irresolution because the people who recited it knew the irresolution was real.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

The Vulgate's *Oratio Hieremiae prophetae* superscription embedded chapter 5 into Christian liturgy as a single prayer-unit. In the medieval Latin liturgy, chapter 5 was the final chant of the Maundy Thursday Tenebrae service: as the candles were extinguished one by one and the church sank into darkness, the *Oratio* was sung. The choice of chapter 5 as the climax of the Triduum's grief was theologically deliberate — the church was placing itself in Jerusalem's place, awaiting Easter. The great Tenebrae settings (Tallis, Palestrina, Lassus, Couperin, Charpentier) gave chapter 5 some of the most extraordinary polyphonic music ever written.

Origen's surviving fragments treat v. 7 ("our fathers sinned... we bear their iniquities") through original sin — Augustine's later doctrine of inherited guilt finds part of its scriptural basis here. Augustine in *De Civitate Dei* and his anti-Pelagian writings cites Lam 5:7 as evidence that Scripture itself attests to inherited consequence of ancestral sin. Eastern Orthodox theology has been more reserved about the original-sin reading; Lossky and Florovsky read v. 7 as describing inherited *consequences* (mortality, weakness, vulnerability) rather than inherited guilt.

Calvin's *Commentary on Lamentations* gives v. 22 a remarkable reading. Calvin treats *ki im* as marking a question or doubt the speaker raises against the petition — the verse is the doubt that prayer must overcome, not the doubt's victory. Calvin: "The faithful pray even when they cannot be sure their prayer will be heard." The reading is consonant with his theology of faith persisting through divine hiddenness. Luther's reading is similar; he treats v. 22 as the *Anfechtung* that must be confessed in prayer.

Modern Christian commentary has increasingly read v. 22 as Linafelt does — as genuine irresolution. Walter Brueggemann's *The Costly Loss of Lament* and *Theology of the Old Testament* both insist that the church has lost something essential by skipping past Lamentations' refusal of closure. Kathleen O'Connor reads chapter 5 through trauma theory: the inability to close is the trauma victim's authentic voice. Soong-Chan Rah reads the chapter as a model for communities under prolonged systemic violence: the unfinished prayer is the genuine genre of suffering that has not yet ended.

Bonhoeffer cited Lamentations 5 from Tegel prison: "Renew our days as of old" was not a request for restoration of comfort; it was the prayer of those who had no return path available. Bonhoeffer's writings on the *cantus firmus* of Christ in suffering integrate Lam 5:19-21 into a theology of waiting that does not know whether the waiting will be honored.

### Where the Traditions Converge and Diverge

Both traditions have made chapter 5 a central liturgical text — Tisha B'Av in Judaism, Tenebrae in Catholicism, Holy Saturday in Eastern Orthodoxy and much of Protestantism. Both recognize that the chapter is a prayer that does not know whether it will be heard. Both have developed liturgical practices to handle v. 22's bare possibility of rejection.

They diverge on whether the book has a sequel. Christian readings have often treated Lamentations as a stage in a story whose resolution comes in the New Testament — Jeremiah's tears anticipate Christ's tears at Jerusalem (Luke 19:41), and the cross is the answer to Lamentations' unanswered cry. Jewish readings refuse this resolution. Tisha B'Av does not lead to Easter. The book is preserved as the prayer of the unredeemed moment, repeatable in any subsequent unredeemed moment. Daniel Boyarin and Marc Saperstein have argued this difference is not minor; it is constitutive of how the two traditions inhabit time.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author's vision in chapter 5 is the survival of prayer in the absence of evidence. The chapter does not argue for hope; it does not argue against it. It enacts petition as the practice of those who have nothing else.

Two genuine tensions emerge.

**Tension 1: How does v. 22 work?**

- *Catholic* (CCC 2738-2741, on Christ's prayer of supplication): The verse's irresolution is taken up into Christ's "let this cup pass from me" at Gethsemane. Christ's prayer was answered in resurrection; Lam 5:22's prayer is answered in Christ. The verse is a stage in a longer arc.
- *Reformed* (Calvin): The verse marks the doubt that faith must outlast. The petition stands; the doubt is preserved as honest. Faith does not deny the doubt; it prays anyway.
- *Lutheran*: The verse names the *Deus absconditus* — God's hiddenness under what looks like rejection. Faith is the practice of speaking to the hidden God.
- *Process / Open theology*: The verse's irresolution reflects a real openness in the God-world relationship. The future is not pre-decided; the prayer matters because the outcome depends in part on the praying.
- *Jewish (Linafelt's reading building on rabbinic tradition)*: The verse is irresolution, and the canon preserves the irresolution. The liturgical repetition of v. 21 after v. 22 is the community's practice for dwelling with — not resolving — the irresolution.
- *Critical*: The verse refuses systematic reading. Its theological achievement is the refusal.

**Steelmanning the position I find least intuitive**: the Catholic reading that the verse's irresolution is taken up in Christ. The temptation is to dismiss this as supersessionist — leaping past the Hebrew Bible's own honesty to a Christian resolution. But the more careful Catholic readings (Joseph Ratzinger's *Jesus of Nazareth*, Hans Urs von Balthasar's *Mysterium Paschale*) do not collapse the verse into resolution. They argue that Holy Saturday — the day Christ lies in the tomb, between Good Friday and Easter Sunday — is the Christian liturgy's way of preserving Lam 5:22's irresolution. Holy Saturday is the day there is no answer. Easter does not erase Holy Saturday; the church returns to it every year. There is a real Christian reading that hears the verse without closing it.

**Where this leaves us**: Whether read with the synagogue's repetition of v. 21, or with Calvin's faith-against-doubt, or with Linafelt's preserved irresolution, or with Holy Saturday's annual re-encounter — the verse demands a posture, not a doctrine. The posture is to keep praying after the prayer has been refused, or has not yet been answered.

**Tension 2: What does v. 7 ("our fathers sinned... we bear their iniquities") do to individual moral responsibility?**

- *Catholic / Augustinian*: The verse undergirds the doctrine of original sin. Inherited guilt is real; baptism is necessary for its remediation.
- *Eastern Orthodox*: The verse describes inherited consequences (mortality, weakness, ancestral disorder) rather than inherited guilt. The distinction is doctrinally important.
- *Reformed* (Westminster Confession 6.3): The verse is integrated into federal headship and the imputation of Adam's sin. Calvin and Edwards develop the theology.
- *Anabaptist / Wesleyan*: More cautious about reading inherited guilt from the verse; it describes inherited consequences, not inherited culpability.
- *Jewish* (Eikhah Rabbah, Maimonides): Inherited *consequences*, yes. Inherited *culpability*, no. Ezekiel 18:20 stands alongside Lam 5:7; both must be held. Children may bear what their fathers brought about without bearing the guilt of what their fathers chose.
- *Critical*: The verse reflects ancient Israelite collective identity, in which the boundary between individual and community was much more porous than modern Western individualism allows. The verse is not contradicting Ezekiel 18:20; it is operating in a different register.

**Where this leaves us**: The verse is uncomfortable for any modern reader steeped in individualism. It insists on solidarity through time — that what was done before us shapes what we now suffer. The theological challenge is to take the solidarity seriously without using it to evade present moral responsibility.

The unresolved question the author leaves the reader: *What does it mean to pray "Renew our days as of old" when the prayer may not be heard?* The chapter does not promise it will be heard. The chapter does not promise it will not be heard. The chapter shows what people who have lost everything do when they have one practice left. The unresolved question is whether such prayer is faith or its remnant.

The book ends. The prayer remains.

## The Thread

- **Psalm 80** — "Restore us, O God of hosts; let your face shine, that we may be saved." The threefold *hashivenu* refrain of Psalm 80 (vv. 3, 7, 19) is the same imperative that governs Lam 5:21. Psalm 80 and Lam 5 belong to the same prayer-tradition: the communal petition for the LORD to turn his people back. Read together to feel how the lament of the city becomes the lament of the dispersed people.
- **Ezekiel 18:20** — "The son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father, nor the father suffer for the iniquity of the son." Lam 5:7 ("our fathers sinned... we bear their iniquities") sits in canonical tension with this verse. The Hebrew Bible's preservation of both forces the reader to hold inherited *consequence* without inherited *culpability* — a distinction the rabbinic tradition has long worked.
- **Luke 19:41-44 / Luke 23:28** — Jesus weeping over Jerusalem at the Mount of Olives, and at the cross telling the daughters of Jerusalem to weep for themselves. The Lukan Jesus has Lamentations open in his speech. Mount Zion lies desolate (Lam 5:18); jackals prowl over it; Jesus stands above it and weeps in the same idiom.
- **Revelation 21:1-5** — "And I saw a new heaven and a new earth... and God will wipe away every tear from their eyes." The prayer of Lam 5:21 — "Restore us, O LORD, that we may be restored" — is the prayer Revelation 21 answers in one Christian reading. The unresolved question is whether the answer is sufficient, or whether (as much Jewish tradition holds) the prayer must continue to be prayed even after Revelation. When you reach Revelation 21, ask whether Lam 5:22's *ki im* has been spoken to or has been spoken over.
