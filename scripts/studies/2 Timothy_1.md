# 2 Timothy 1: The Grandmother's Faith and the Guarded Deposit

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

The opening of 2 Timothy is letter-writing under shadow of execution. If the letter is genuinely Pauline — and 2 Timothy has the strongest claim to authenticity among the three Pastoral Epistles — the historical setting is Rome in the mid-60s AD, in the aftermath of the Great Fire of July 64. Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44, records that Nero, suspected by the populace of having ordered the fire that consumed ten of Rome's fourteen districts, "fastened the guilt and inflicted the most exquisite tortures on a class hated for their abominations, called Christians." Tacitus's reference, written around 116 AD, is the earliest pagan Roman source acknowledging that the *Christiani* were a distinct, despised group in the imperial capital. Paul writes either during or just after the wave of persecutions Tacitus describes. Suetonius's *Nero* 16 corroborates the picture: the emperor's punishments fell on "Christians, a class of men given to a new and mischievous superstition." The chains Paul references in 1:16 are almost certainly literal, and the prison conditions almost certainly brutal — Roman *carceres* near the Forum, possibly the Mamertine *Tullianum* (a tradition the *Liber Pontificalis* preserves but cannot verify archaeologically), were unventilated stone chambers, often subterranean, where prisoners awaiting execution were held with minimal provision.

If the letter is pseudonymous, as Hans Conzelmann, Norbert Brox, Lewis Donelson, and Raymond Collins have argued for the Pastorals as a corpus, the setting shifts to the late first or early second century — perhaps the era of Pliny the Younger's famous letter to Trajan (*Epistles* 10.96, c. 112 AD) inquiring how to handle Christians who refused to sacrifice to the imperial cult. The chains and the abandonment by "all who are in Asia" then function as literary symbols of a generalized post-apostolic experience, not eyewitness reportage. But 2 Timothy presses harder against pseudonymity than its companion letters. The biographical concreteness — Lois the grandmother, Eunice the mother, Phygelus and Hermogenes named as deserters, Onesiphorus named as the one who searched Rome to find Paul's cell — is the kind of detail that pseudepigraphers tend not to invent. Hans Conzelmann, despite his commitment to the corpus's pseudonymity, conceded that personal fragments here look genuinely Pauline. P.N. Harrison's 1921 *Problem of the Pastoral Epistles* and James Miller's *The Pastoral Letters as Composite Documents* (1997) propose a middle position: a later redactor incorporated authentic Pauline notes into the letter framework. William Mounce, I. Howard Marshall, Philip Towner, George Knight, and Luke Timothy Johnson defend full Pauline authorship and read the letter as Paul's last words after a possible release from the Acts 28 imprisonment, a re-arrest, and a final detention awaiting beheading on the Ostian Way — the "second imprisonment" tradition Eusebius preserves in *Ecclesiastical History* 2.22, citing earlier sources.

Material culture matters. Travel between Asia Minor and Italy in the mid-60s ran along well-mapped routes — the Via Egnatia from Macedonia, Brundisium-to-Rome by the Appian Way — but it was costly and slow, especially in winter when sea-lanes closed (compare 4:21's "do your best to come before winter"). Onesiphorus's search of Rome to find Paul (1:17) reflects the practical reality that imperial bureaucracy did not maintain accessible prisoner registries. Paul's status as a Roman citizen (Acts 22:25-29) would have spared him crucifixion but not arbitrary detention. Among textual witnesses, Codex Sinaiticus א (4th c.) preserves the full Pastorals; Codex Alexandrinus A (5th c.) does as well; Codex Vaticanus B (4th c.), one of the great uncials, conspicuously breaks off in Hebrews and lacks the Pastorals — a significant absence that Bart Ehrman cites in *Forged* and Bruce Metzger discusses in his *Textual Commentary*. Daniel Wallace and Larry Hurtado have argued the absence is best explained by physical loss rather than deliberate exclusion; nonetheless, B's silence remains a textual data point in the authorship debate.

Five Greek terms anchor the chapter: παραθήκη *parathēkē* (deposit, sacred trust, 1:12, 14); χάρισμα *charisma* (gift, 1:6); ἀναζωπυρεῖν *anazōpyrein* (to fan into flame, 1:6); δειλία *deilia* (cowardice, fear, 1:7); σωφρονισμός *sōphronismos* (self-control, sound mind, 1:7).

## Lens 2 — The Literature

The genre of 2 Timothy is testamentary. It belongs to a recognizable Greco-Roman and Jewish form — the farewell discourse delivered by a dying figure to chosen successors. Margaret Mitchell, Loveday Alexander, Benjamin Fiore, and Abraham Malherbe have all argued that the Pastorals, and especially 2 Timothy, draw on this *suggraphē* tradition. Greco-Roman parallels include the philosophical wills of Epicurus and the moral exhortations of dying Stoics; Jewish parallels include Moses' farewell in Deuteronomy 31-33, Jacob's blessing in Genesis 49, the *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs*, and (if one accepts the typology) the Johannine Farewell Discourse in John 13-17 and Paul's own Miletus address in Acts 20:17-38. The conventions of the form include personal anamnesis, exhortation, prediction of trouble, designation of successors, and self-vindication. Chapter 1 sets these gears in motion: the recollection of Timothy's tears at parting (1:4), the recovery of his familial faith heritage (1:5), the charge to rekindle the gift (1:6), the warning against shame (1:8), the self-defense ("I am not ashamed," 1:12), and the imperative to guard what has been entrusted (1:14).

Within this frame the chapter deploys two distinctive literary moves. The first is the elevated quasi-creedal passage in 1:9-10 — "who saved us and called us to a holy calling, not because of our works but because of his own purpose and grace, which he gave us in Christ Jesus before the ages began, and which now has been manifested through the appearing of our Savior Christ Jesus, who abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel." Mounce, Marshall, and Knight all identify this as a probable hymnic or creedal fragment incorporated by the author, marked by participial relative clauses, the rare term ἀφθαρσία *aphtharsia* ("immortality," "incorruption"), and the antithesis of "before the ages" / "now manifested" that mirrors the structure of 1 Timothy 3:16 and Titus 3:4-7. The presence of such fragments is one of the structural features Dibelius and Conzelmann identified as distinctive of the Pastorals.

The second is the *parathēkē* motif. In Greco-Roman law, a *parathēkē* was a deposit entrusted to a banker or trusted friend — Plato (*Republic* 331e), Demosthenes, and the papyri use the term technically. The deposit must be returned intact. Paul (or "Paul") deploys the term twice in close succession with two different polarities: 1:12, what Paul has entrusted to God ("he is able to guard until that day what has been entrusted to me"), and 1:14, what Paul has entrusted to Timothy ("guard the good deposit entrusted to you"). The reciprocity is theologically loaded: God preserves the apostle, and the apostle preserves the gospel through his successor. This is *the* keyword of the Pastorals. It recurs at 1 Timothy 6:20 ("guard the deposit"). The literary force of the term is contractual and almost legal — and that legal-custodial register is exactly what later Catholic theology will mine for the doctrine of the *depositum fidei*.

Textually, 1:1 has a minor variant where some witnesses read "promise of life in Christ Jesus" without the article; 1:11 in some manuscripts adds "of the Gentiles" after "teacher" (ἐθνῶν), which Sinaiticus א and Alexandrinus A omit but which appears in later witnesses, an assimilation to 1 Timothy 2:7. None of these variants are doctrinally load-bearing.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

In the canonical arc — Israel called, Messiah revealed, Spirit poured out, church sent — 2 Timothy 1 sits at the moment when the apostolic generation begins to die and the question of transmission becomes acute. The Old Testament had Moses charging Joshua, Elijah throwing his mantle on Elisha. Acts had Paul telling the Ephesian elders at Miletus to feed the flock because savage wolves would come (Acts 20:29). Now the wolves have come, the apostle is in chains, and the next generation must hold what it has received. The chapter's three motifs — the matrilineal faith of Lois and Eunice, the rekindled charisma through laying-on-of-hands, and the guarded deposit — name three different mechanisms of transmission: family, ordination, doctrine.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** Patristic readers found in this chapter a manual for ecclesiastical succession. John Chrysostom's *Homilies on 2 Timothy* (Homily 1) emphasizes that Timothy's faith descended through women and praises Eunice as a model catechist; Ambrosiaster reads 1:6 as the basis for sacramental ordination. Theodore of Mopsuestia treats the *parathēkē* as the rule of faith inherited from the apostles. The apocryphal *Acts of Paul* (mid-2nd c.) leans into the biographical concreteness of 2 Timothy to embellish the apostle's final days, even narrating his beheading on the Ostian Way. Eusebius's *Ecclesiastical History* 2.22, drawing on earlier traditions, uses 2 Timothy 4:16-17 as evidence that Paul was released after Acts 28 and arrested a second time — making the letter our window into a "post-Acts" Pauline ministry. Vincent of Lérins's *Commonitorium* (434 AD) takes the *parathēkē* as the foundation for his famous canon: *quod ubique, quod semper, quod ab omnibus creditum est* — "what has been believed everywhere, always, by all." Vincent essentially turns 1:14 into the architecture of catholicity.

Medieval readers extended Vincent's move. Thomas Aquinas's *Lectura* on 2 Timothy treats the deposit as the complete *fides quae creditur* — the content of faith — handed on through ordained teachers. Bonaventure links the rekindling of charisma to the sevenfold gift of the Spirit conferred at episcopal consecration. Reformation readers split. Luther's lectures on the Pastorals embraced the testamentary genre and read 1:9-10 as a proto-justification statement ("not because of our works"). Calvin's commentary on 2 Timothy treated the *parathēkē* as identical with Scripture itself, not with an oral tradition supplementary to it — a polemical move that anchored Reformed *sola scriptura* against Tridentine claims of equally authoritative tradition. The Magdeburg Centuries (1559-1574) deployed 2 Timothy 3 against Rome but found the *parathēkē* of chapter 1 harder to control.

Critical scholarship from F.C. Baur (1835) onward denied Pauline authorship of the entire Pastoral corpus on grounds of vocabulary, ecclesiology, and situation. Schleiermacher had earlier flagged 1 Timothy as suspicious; Eichhorn extended this; H.J. Holtzmann formalized the case in 1880; Adolf von Harnack saw a second-century church-order document; Martin Dibelius (1913) and Hans Conzelmann (1955, Hermeneia) institutionalized the consensus. The Pastoral Epistles became the textbook example of NT pseudepigraphy. Bart Ehrman's *Forged* (2011) makes the case popularly. But the line has softened. James Miller and earlier P.N. Harrison proposed the composite-fragment hypothesis. Jerome Quinn, Luke Timothy Johnson, William Mounce, I. Howard Marshall, Philip Towner, and George Knight have mounted serious defenses of authenticity, with 2 Timothy as the strongest case. Daniel Wallace and Bruce Metzger note that the textual transmission of the Pastorals — outside Vaticanus's lacuna — is otherwise stable and ancient.

**Jewish Scholarly Critique and Its Evolution.** The broader Jewish-Christian conversation about pseudepigraphy and inspired-text claims is long, and the Pastorals sit inside it. Rabbinic tradition treats the Tanakh's threefold authority — Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim — as a closed canon by the late first or second century, the Mishnah and Tosefta showing the boundaries already firm. The *parathēkē* of 2 Timothy 1, framed as transmitted tradition guarded by appointed successors, has a structural Jewish parallel in *Pirkei Avot* 1:1 — "Moses received Torah from Sinai and handed it down to Joshua, and Joshua to the elders, and the elders to the prophets, and the prophets to the Men of the Great Assembly." Both communities ground authority in chains of reception. The point of divergence is what counts as the deposit: for the rabbis, Torah (written and oral); for the Pastorals' author, the apostolic gospel about Jesus. Modern Paul-within-Judaism scholars — Krister Stendahl, E.P. Sanders, Daniel Boyarin, Mark Nanos, Paula Fredriksen — have argued that Paul's own self-understanding remained inside Israel's covenantal story and that later supersessionist readings of 2 Timothy and the Pastorals (especially the rejection of "myths and genealogies" in 1 Tim 1:4 and Titus 1:14) have to be carefully unpicked from their post-Constantinian afterlife. Where Jewish scholarship continues to push back is on Christian claims that the apostolic *parathēkē* simply replaces or supersedes Torah-observant communal life. The Lois-Eunice matriline complicates the picture: Acts 16:1 calls Eunice a "Jewish woman" married to a Greek; the Mishnah (m. Qiddushin 3:12) and later codifications make matrilineal descent the marker of Jewishness, with Shaye Cohen's *The Beginnings of Jewishness* tracing the rule's emergence to roughly this period. In rabbinic terms, Timothy was Jewish through his mother — a fact Paul implicitly recognizes by circumcising him (Acts 16:3). Carolyn Osiek, Margaret MacDonald, and other feminist scholars have recovered the chapter as evidence of women's domestic catechetical authority in the Jesus movement, partially overlapping with synagogue household instruction.

**Where this leaves us.** Christian and Jewish readers can agree that the Pastorals are written from inside a tradition-handing community and disagree about whether the deposit is the apostolic gospel or the Torah. The Lois-Eunice detail, far from being decorative, is the most concrete piece of evidence we have for what early Jewish-Christian household religion looked like.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Tradition and Scripture.** The *parathēkē* of 1:14 is the conceptual seed of the Catholic *depositum fidei* (CCC 84). Vatican II's *Dei Verbum* 8-10 develops the doctrine: revelation is given once-for-all in Christ; Scripture and Tradition flow from a single source; the Church's Magisterium authentically interprets both. Newman's 1845 *Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine* showed how the deposit can develop without changing — the seed becomes a tree without becoming an oak instead of an apple. *Lumen Gentium* 25 ties this to the teaching authority of bishops in succession. The Reformed answer, articulated in the Westminster Confession 1.6 ("the whole counsel of God concerning all things necessary for his own glory, man's salvation, faith, and life, is either expressly set down in Scripture, or by good and necessary consequence may be deduced from Scripture"), insists that Scripture alone is the *parathēkē* and tradition is interpretive servant rather than co-equal source. Herman Bavinck's *Reformed Dogmatics* and John Webster's *Holy Scripture: A Dogmatic Sketch* refine this: tradition is the church's hearing of Scripture, not a second deposit. Eastern Orthodox theology, following Vladimir Lossky's *The Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church* and Georges Florovsky's "neo-patristic synthesis," speaks of the one Tradition (capital T) that includes Scripture as its inmost text and the liturgy, conciliar definitions, iconography, and lived life of the Church as its full body — explicitly resisting the Western Tradition-vs-Scripture binary. The steelman of the Catholic position: the New Testament canon itself is a tradition before it is a Scripture. The early Church identified which writings were apostolic by the same custodial logic 2 Timothy 1:14 prescribes; *sola scriptura* is therefore parasitic on a non-Scriptural act of judgment about what counts as Scripture. **Where this leaves us:** the verse refuses both naked traditionalism and naked biblicism — it locates authority in a continuity of faithful custodians, and the question is who the custodians are and what tools they use.

**Tension Two: Spirit and ordination.** "Fan into flame the gift of God, which is in you through the laying on of my hands" (1:6) ties the χάρισμα *charisma* to a specific apostolic act. The Catholic Catechism 1581-1584 reads this as the foundation of the indelible sacramental character of Holy Orders — the gift conferred is permanent, objective, ontological. Aquinas in *Summa Theologiae* III.35 develops the metaphysics: ordination imprints a *character* on the soul. Reformed theology (Calvin, *Institutes* IV.3-4; the Westminster Form of Presbyterial Church-Government, 1645) preserves the laying-on-of-hands but reinterprets it as recognition of a calling already conferred by Christ through congregational and presbyterial discernment, not as a sacramental conferral of grace. Lutheran theology in the Augsburg Confession Article 14 occupies a middle space — ordination is divinely instituted, the *Amt* is a real office, but its grace is the grace of the gospel preached, not a separate sacramental infusion. Wesleyan tradition, following John Wesley's own irregular ordinations for America, treats laying-on-of-hands as connectional and apostolic in spirit but pragmatically flexible. Pentecostal and charismatic theology presses a fourth direction: the *charisma* of 1:6 is the Spirit's own gifting, available to any believer through prayer and faith, with or without episcopal succession (Gordon Fee's *Gospel and Spirit* makes this case from inside the academy). The steelman of the Pentecostal reading: 1:7's "spirit of power and love and self-control" reads like a description of a Spirit-baptism experience, not a sacramental theology, and the burden is on cessationists and sacramentalists to show that the text means anything more. **Where this leaves us:** ordination matters in 2 Timothy, but the chapter refuses to disentangle the institutional and the charismatic — Paul treats them as one event.

**Unresolved question.** If the *parathēkē* must be guarded unchanged across generations (1:14) and yet must be fanned into new flame in every successor (1:6), what exactly is the difference between faithful transmission and creative reinvention — and who, in any given generation, gets to draw that line?

## The Thread

- **Acts 16:1-3** — The biographical anchor for Lois and Eunice. Paul finds Timothy in Lystra; Eunice is identified as a Jewish believer married to a Greek; Paul circumcises Timothy "because of the Jews who were in those places." Read against 2 Tim 1:5, this places Timothy's faith inside a multigenerational Jewish-Christian household. The detail makes pseudonymity harder to maintain.

- **Numbers 27:18-23** — Moses lays his hands on Joshua and confers leadership "before Eleazar the priest and all the congregation." 2 Tim 1:6's "laying on of my hands" is the same Hebrew-Bible gesture (סמיכה *semikhah*), the same logic of transmitted authority. The continuity from Moses-Joshua to Paul-Timothy is the canonical thread.

- **2 Peter 1:13-15** (later in reading order if the canonical order is followed) — Peter, also writing on the eve of his death ("I know that the putting off of my body will be soon"), uses the same testamentary form. Read as a literary echo: the apostolic generation knew it was passing on a deposit and shaped its final letters as wills.

- **John 14:26 / 16:13** — Jesus' farewell promise of the Spirit "who will teach you all things and bring to remembrance all that I have said to you." The Spirit is the deep guarantor of the *parathēkē*; 2 Tim 1:14 says the deposit is guarded "by the Holy Spirit who dwells within us." The two passages together form a Trinitarian theology of tradition: the Father gives, the Son commits, the Spirit guards.
