# Jeremiah 37: The Prophet in the Dungeon

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Jeremiah 37 opens in the final, claustrophobic phase of the kingdom of Judah. The political situation is precise and dateable: Zedekiah, the third son of Josiah, was installed as a vassal king by Nebuchadnezzar in 597 BC after the first deportation, in place of his nephew Jehoiachin (called Coniah in 37:1). The chapter's narrative window falls in 588–587 BC, during the eighteen-month Babylonian siege of Jerusalem. The Babylonian Chronicle (BM 21946) dates the campaign from the seventh year of Nebuchadnezzar's reign, and the brief Egyptian intervention noted in 37:5 and 37:11 is historically anchored by Egyptian records of Pharaoh Hophra (Apries, 589–570 BC) marching from the Nile delta in roughly 588 BC. Hophra's intervention forced a temporary Babylonian withdrawal — the kind of false dawn that made the siege survivors believe Jerusalem might yet be spared. Ezekiel, prophesying among the exiles in Babylon, watches this same political maneuver from the other end and condemns it (Ezek 17:11–21; 29–32).

The material culture of the chapter is exactly that of late Iron II Judah on the eve of destruction. The Benjamin Gate (37:13) was the northern gate of Jerusalem, opening toward Jeremiah's hometown of Anathoth roughly three miles away in Benjaminite territory. The "house of Jonathan the secretary" (37:15), repurposed as a dungeon, reflects how administrative residences were converted under siege conditions. The "court of the guard" (חֲצַר הַמַּטָּרָה, *chatzar ha-mattarah*, 37:21) was a courtyard within the royal palace complex where prisoners could be held under house-arrest conditions — Jeremiah is detained here from chapter 32 through the fall of the city. The "bakers' street" (37:21) names a quarter of Jerusalem whose existence is corroborated by other ANE city plans where guilds clustered along named streets. The note that bread was rationed "until all the bread of the city was gone" matches the famine sequence described in Lamentations 2:11–12, 4:4–10 and the archaeologically attested late-siege food crisis at Lachish.

On authorship, this chapter belongs to what Sigmund Mowinckel famously classified as the "B" source of the book of Jeremiah — biographical narrative, third-person, almost certainly preserved by Baruch ben Neriah, Jeremiah's scribe (Jer 36:4, 32; 45:1). Lundbom (Anchor Bible) treats chs. 37–44 as a coherent biographical unit, the most extensive prose narrative cluster in the Latter Prophets. Holladay (Hermeneia) regards 37:1–2 as a redactional bridge linking the prior literary block to the Baruch narrative proper, which begins with a strict 588 BC dating in 37:3. Carroll (OTL) and McKane (ICC) emphasize that the MT here runs roughly one-eighth longer than the LXX — the LXX (probably translating an earlier Hebrew Vorlage from Qumran-style traditions) lacks several phrases. The MT vs. LXX divergence is structural, not just stylistic, and chapters 37–40 are one of the places it matters most.

Archaeologically and textually, this chapter is a goldmine. Jehucal son of Shelemiah (37:3) — sent by Zedekiah to ask Jeremiah for prayer — is one of two royal officials in this chapter whose seal-impressions have been recovered from the destruction layer of the City of David. Eilat Mazar's excavations in Area G unearthed in 2005 a clay bulla reading *l-yhwkl bn šlmyhw bn šbʿy* ("Belonging to Yehuchal son of Shelemiah son of Shovi"), published in 2007. In 2008 Mazar's team uncovered a second bulla naming Gedaliah son of Pashhur (the same Gedaliah named in Jer 38:1 — distinct from the post-fall governor in ch. 40). Both bullae were found in the same stratum, dated to the early sixth century BC, in what Mazar identifies as a royal administrative building destroyed in the 587 BC burn layer. The Lachish Letters, ostraca discovered by James Starkey in 1935 in the gate guardroom of Lachish Level III, are dated to roughly the same months as Jeremiah 34–38; Letter IV famously notes "we are watching for the fire-signals of Lachish… for we cannot see Azekah," matching Jeremiah 34:7's note that Lachish and Azekah were the last two fortified cities still holding out. Few prophetic narratives in the Hebrew Bible can be located with such precision in the archaeological and epigraphic record.

Key Hebrew terms: *bor* (בּוֹר, "pit/cistern/dungeon" — the "dungeon cells" of 37:16); *chatzar ha-mattarah* (חֲצַר הַמַּטָּרָה, "court of the guard"); *paqid* (פָּקִיד, "officer/sentry," used of Irijah in 37:13); and *malkhei Yehudah* (מַלְכֵי יְהוּדָה, "kings of Judah") — the running theme of royal failure that structures chs. 21–24 and resurfaces here.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre: third-person biographical narrative — what Mowinckel labels the "B" source and most commentators since have treated as Baruch's eyewitness memoir or memoir-tradition. The narrator stands outside the action and reports it, but the reportorial voice is intimate with palace and prison details (the names of secret messengers, the layout of the dungeon, the rationing of bread) in a way that suggests insider testimony. Stulman (AOTC) reads this material as "trauma narrative" — a literature produced by a community processing catastrophe, where the prophet's body becomes the symbolic site of collective suffering.

The chapter is built around three structural moves: first, a compressed introduction (37:1–2) that condemns Zedekiah, his servants, and the people for not listening; second, the Egyptian-intervention episode and Jeremiah's oracle that this reprieve is illusory (37:3–10); third, the arrest, beating, and dungeon-imprisonment of the prophet (37:11–21). Each move tightens the irony. In the first, the king and people refuse to listen. In the second, Jeremiah tells them their hope is false. In the third, they punish him for telling them. Carroll calls this the "rejection-spiral" structure of the Baruch material — every prophetic word produces escalating resistance.

Devices: dramatic dialogue (37:13–14, 17–20), scene-shifting between palace and prison, and what Brueggemann calls "ironic role inversion." The prophet who has been preaching surrender (Jer 21:9; 38:2) is accused by a sentry of literal desertion (37:13) — Jeremiah says, "It is a lie" (שֶׁקֶר, *sheker*), the very word he has used throughout his ministry to describe the false prophets (Jer 14:14, 23:25–32, 27:10). The accusation against him uses the same noun he has used against his accusers. This is not coincidence; the Baruch narrative is structured to expose the recursive logic of false speech in a society at war with truth.

The MT vs. LXX divergence in this chapter is moderate but theologically loaded: the LXX is consistently shorter and lacks the explicit "Thus says the LORD, God of Israel" formulae that the MT inserts (37:7, 9). Lundbom treats the LXX as preserving an earlier, less rhetorically embellished Vorlage; Tov (Qumran textual scholarship) connects the LXX-Vorlage to 4QJer^b. The longer MT shape will matter again in ch. 39, where 39:4–13 is missing from the LXX altogether.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

The canonical placement of Jeremiah 37 is at the hinge of the Latter Prophets. The fall of Jerusalem in 587 BC is the single most consequential event in Old Testament history — it ends the Davidic monarchy as a functioning institution, ends the First Temple cult, and generates the diaspora condition that shapes every subsequent layer of biblical and Jewish reflection. Jeremiah is the prophet who watches it happen from inside the city, and chapters 37–39 are the up-close record of the watching. Lamentations is traditionally attributed to him (Bavli Bava Batra 15a; LXX superscription) precisely because his ministry produced the only extended pre-exilic / fall-period prophetic body that does not flinch from the catastrophe.

In the Jewish trajectory, Jeremiah 37 sits inside a larger memory tradition. Sirach 49:6–7 (early second century BC) praises Jeremiah for his suffering at the hands of his own people. The Babylonian Talmud (Megillah 14b) lists Jeremiah among the seven prophets who prophesied to the gentiles, and explores why a prophet who told truth was treated as a traitor. Pesikta Rabbati (chs. 26–29) develops a haunting midrashic cycle in which Jeremiah weeps over Jerusalem and the patriarchs intercede in heaven. Targum Jonathan glosses the chapter to make the Babylonian withdrawal explicitly an act of YHWH "in his anger." Rashi reads 37:13 ("you are deserting") as a direct echo of Jer 21:9 — the people accuse him of doing what he was telling them to do. Radak emphasizes the secrecy of Zedekiah's audience (37:17) as evidence that the king knew Jeremiah's word was true but lacked the courage to act publicly. Ibn Ezra notes the textual oddity of "Coniah" (37:1) instead of "Jehoiachin" — a deliberate diminutive marking the *displaced* king. Abrabanel, writing as a Spanish exile after 1492, reads the chapter with personal force: a prophet rejected by his own people, persecuted for telling them to surrender to a foreign power for their own good. Tisha B'Av — the ninth of Av, commemorating the destruction of both temples — reads Lamentations and selections of Jeremiah; chapters 37–39 form the historical scaffold of that mourning.

In the Christian trajectory, the patristic reception read Jeremiah 37 as a passion-narrative-in-advance. Origen's *Homilies on Jeremiah* (mid-third century) treats the prophet's secret audiences with Zedekiah and the dungeon imprisonment as figural anticipations of Christ's trial before Pilate and Christ's tomb. Jerome's *Commentary on Jeremiah* (late fourth century) notes the irony of Jeremiah being charged with desertion for the very position he urged others to take, and reads this as paradigmatic of the Christian's relation to the world. Aquinas (in his Old Testament commentaries) cites Jeremiah's response to the king ("Where are your prophets?", 37:19) as a model of legitimate prophetic confrontation of political power. Calvin's *Lectures on Jeremiah* (1563) — the most extensive Reformation engagement with this material — devotes nearly thirty pages to ch. 37 alone, focusing on the "secret colloquies" between the prophet and the king and using them to argue that civil rulers must hear the word of God privately and publicly.

Among critical scholars, Bernhard Duhm's 1901 commentary detected in this chapter the hand of a prose biographer distinct from the prophet's own poetic voice; Mowinckel formalized this as the "B" source. Holladay (Hermeneia, 1989) treats chs. 37–44 as preserving a relatively reliable historical core. Carroll (OTL, 1986) is more skeptical and reads the material as theological reflection by a Deuteronomistic redactor. Brueggemann (Theology of the Old Testament, 1997) emphasizes the *vulnerability* of the prophet's body. Stulman (AOTC, 2005) introduces the trauma-theological frame. Kathleen O'Connor (*Jeremiah: Pain and Promise*, 2011) — the indispensable contemporary reading of these chapters — argues that the Baruch narrative is a sustained literary act of testimony to disaster, that the prophet's repeated descent into pits and prisons is the book's way of saying *this is what truth-telling cost in a society collapsing under its own lies*.

Where they diverge: the Christian patristic reading sees in Jeremiah's body the figural shape of Christ's body, the dungeon prefigures the tomb, the false-charge prefigures the trial. The Jewish *churban* tradition reads the chapter as the historical scaffolding of an ongoing communal mourning — Tisha B'Av is not metaphor, it is calendar. The critical Deuteronomistic reading sees the chapter as theological vindication: the prophet's word was true, the people did not listen, the city fell, the redactor has organized the material to prove it. Each reading captures something the others miss.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

Two genuine tensions run through Jeremiah 37, and both deserve careful steelmanning.

The first tension is whether the prophet should hold political power at arm's length or engage it directly. Jeremiah does both, and it costs him. He refuses public legitimation of Zedekiah — refuses to pray a saving prayer (37:7–10), refuses to prophesy success — yet he meets with the king secretly (37:17), speaks the truth in private, and accepts royal protection in the court of the guard (37:21). The Catholic *prophetic ministry within church-state* tradition (encapsulated in Vatican II's *Gaudium et Spes* 76) holds that prophetic truth-telling can and must operate inside political structures, that engagement is itself a form of witness; cite also Aquinas (*ST* II-II.42) on resisting tyrannical authority through legitimate channels. Reformed two-kingdoms theology (Luther's *Temporal Authority*, 1523; Kuyper's sphere sovereignty) treats civil and prophetic spheres as distinct but mutually accountable, allowing the prophet to address the king without becoming the king. The Anabaptist position (Schleitheim Confession 1527; Yoder, *The Politics of Jesus*) refuses any negotiation with political power on principle — the prophet who consults secretly with the king has already compromised. Steelmanning the Anabaptist reading: Jeremiah's secret audiences end with Zedekiah unwilling to act publicly on what he privately knows is true (37:17 vs. 37:21; cf. 38:24–27); private truth that cannot become public action is a peculiar form of complicity, and the prophet who accepts it has accepted a role within a system designed to suppress his message. The modern parallel — Bonhoeffer's struggle over how to engage the Nazi regime, his refusal of mere "private piety," his eventual conspiracy — works either direction. **Where this leaves us:** prophetic ministry inside power is not the same as captivity to power, but it is dangerously adjacent to it, and the line is drawn not by who you talk to but by what you refuse to soften when you talk.

The second tension is the experience of being falsely accused as constitutive of prophetic vocation. Irijah the sentry calls Jeremiah a deserter (37:13); the officials beat him (37:15); the city's elite punish him for the very surrender-counsel that would have saved them. This is not incidental suffering; the Baruch narrative treats the false accusation as the form prophetic witness takes inside a society that has chosen self-deception. The early Christian reception (Justin Martyr's *Second Apology* 1.1–4; Tertullian's *Apology* 1) explicitly read the persecution of the church as recapitulating the prophets' false-charge tradition — the charge of "atheism" against Christians (because they refused civic gods) was itself a Roman analogue of "desertion to the Chaldeans." The Bonhoeffer reception in the twentieth century — his *Letters and Papers from Prison*, his trial, his execution at Flossenbürg in 1945 — explicitly cited Jeremiah 37 in his prison correspondence. The Jewish post-Holocaust reception, especially Emil Fackenheim and Elie Wiesel, has taken the false-accusation theme as paradigmatic of Jewish historical experience. Critical scholarship (O'Connor, Stulman) treats the false-accusation motif as the book of Jeremiah's structural insight that truth-telling in a society organized around denial is *necessarily* heard as betrayal. **Where this leaves us:** the prophet's reputation in his own time is not a reliable indicator of his fidelity; the question is not whether he is loved but whether what he says is true.

The chapter does not resolve. It ends with Jeremiah in the court of the guard, eating rationed bread until the bread runs out. The unresolved question: when the truth-teller is reduced to bread-rationing in the courtyard of a king who knows he is right but cannot act, what is faithfulness — to keep speaking, to keep eating, to wait?

## The Thread

The pit and dungeon imagery (37:15–16, anticipating the cistern of ch. 38) connects to Joseph in Genesis 37 — also lowered into a *bor* by his own brothers, also accused unjustly, also rising from the pit to bear witness in a foreign court. The theme is recursive in the prophetic literature: Daniel in the lion's pit (Dan 6), the psalmist in the miry pit (Ps 40:2; 88:6), and ultimately Christ in the tomb — patristic readers (Origen, Jerome) drew the line straight. Jeremiah's secret audience with Zedekiah (37:17) parallels Pilate's private question to Jesus, "What is truth?" (Jn 18:38) — both rulers know they are face-to-face with truth and lack the courage to act on what they know. The Egyptian false-rescue (37:5, 11) connects to the recurrent "do not return to Egypt" motif (Deut 17:16; Hos 7:11; Isa 30:1–7) — the temptation to look south for salvation when one has already been told to look elsewhere. And the condition of being "in the court of the guard" (37:21) becomes, by chapter's end, the place from which the rest of the prophet's surviving messages will be spoken — a new genre of speech: prophecy from the prison courtyard, which will produce some of the book's most luminous oracles in chs. 32–33.
