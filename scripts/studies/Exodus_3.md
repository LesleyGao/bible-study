# Exodus 3: Ehyeh Asher Ehyeh

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Exodus 3 opens with a shepherd and a mountain. The shepherd is a fugitive from the Egyptian court; the mountain is identified both as Horeb and, in later chapters, as Sinai — two names for one place, or two places conflated by different traditions, a question that has never fully settled. Moses leads his father-in-law Jethro's flock to the "west side" or "back side" (Hebrew *ʾaḥar hammidbār*, behind the wilderness), terrain marginal to the settled Midianite encampments and penetrating the arid volcanic zone of the Sinai peninsula or, if one follows a different geographical tradition, northwestern Arabia.

The question of where this mountain is has real stakes. The traditional identification is Jebel Musa in the southern Sinai peninsula, the site of St. Catherine's monastery, a tradition stabilized by Helena in the fourth century and given monastic institutional form under Justinian in the sixth. The inscriptions in Wadi Mukattab and the Bedouin tradition at Jebel Musa reflect longstanding veneration. A minority scholarly tradition, however, identifies Sinai with Jebel al-Lawz in northwestern Saudi Arabia, following Paul's remark in Galatians 4:25 that Sinai is "in Arabia," and pointing to volcanic-looking rock at Jebel al-Lawz and what some read as altar-like structures. Kenneth Kitchen, James Hoffmeier in *Ancient Israel in Sinai*, and most serious geographers dismiss the Jebel al-Lawz thesis as archaeologically thin — no datable Israelite material, no Egyptian presence, and the geological "burn marks" are metamorphic rock, not scorching. Jebel Musa remains the most defensible identification, though the text itself is maddeningly indifferent to geographic precision.

The political world is Midianite: a loose confederation of tribes in the arid belt southeast of Canaan, attested in Egyptian records of the thirteenth and twelfth centuries BC. Midianite pottery — a distinctive painted ware found at Tell el-Kheleifeh, Timna, and across the Hejaz — marks the archaeological horizon. The Timna copper-smelting shrine, excavated by Beno Rothenberg in the 1970s, showed Midianite ritual activity in a zone previously presumed purely Egyptian. A Midianite tent-shrine with a copper serpent, dating to the late Bronze Age, raises provocative questions about Exodus 3's serpent imagery (developed in chapter 4) and Numbers 21's bronze serpent. The Midianite priesthood, then, is not a literary invention — there was a functional religious infrastructure in this region, and Moses' marriage into Jethro's household locates him inside it.

The Egyptian political situation is implicit but foundational. The "taskmasters" (*nōgĕśîm*) whose cry rises to God imply corvée labor under royal oversight. Papyrus Anastasi V and Papyrus Leiden 348 describe the administration of forced labor in the eastern Delta, with complaints about insufficient straw, mobile populations, and the use of *ʿapiru* — a term that in Egyptian administrative documents designates rootless laborers, fugitives, and marginal peoples. The etymological relationship between *ʿapiru* and "Hebrew" (*ʿibrî*) remains debated (Kitchen and Hoffmeier cautious; Nadav Na'aman more skeptical), but the social category is real.

Material culture details are dense. The burning bush (*sĕneh*, סְנֶה) is a rare word used almost uniquely in this passage — a thornbush or bramble, possibly *Rubus sanctus*, possibly *Acacia*. The philological rarity is itself a clue: *sĕneh* wordplays with *Sinai*, and the mountain of God is named for the humble plant that burned on its slope. Moses is told to remove his sandals — a gesture whose ancient Near Eastern parallels are extensive. Plutarch (*De Iside et Osiride* 5) records that Egyptian priests went barefoot in the temple; Islamic practice inherits and continues this; and Japanese Shinto retains a related practice. The body posture signals the rupture between profane and sacred ground — a rupture the Hebrew word *qōdeš* (קֹדֶשׁ), holy, denotes: not moral purity but ontological separation.

Authorship and source layers: classical Documentary Hypothesis assigned most of chapter 3 to E (the Elohist), noting the divine designation *Elōhîm* through much of the dialogue, with J overlaying the divine name YHWH and the "Land flowing with milk and honey" formula. The doublet between the call scene in chapter 3 and the call scene in chapter 6 (where God also reveals the name) has been the engine of source-critical theorizing since Wellhausen. Rolf Rendtorff's *The Problem of the Process of Transmission in the Pentateuch* (1977) dismantled the neat J/E partition here and argued that the Moses story was a self-contained tradition complex edited late. Erhard Blum's compositional model sees two major editorial layers (D and P). Konrad Schmid, in *Genesis and the Moses Story*, argues the chapter is a post-exilic theological composition bridging patriarchal and Mosaic strands that were originally separate. Thomas Römer's *The Invention of God* places the divine-name revelation in a Persian-period theological crystallization. The critical scholarly consensus since the 1990s is less confident about source partition than Wellhausen was, but more confident that the final form is a deliberate theological composition.

Five Hebrew terms carry meaning English flattens. *Sĕneh* (סְנֶה), bush, I have noted. *Ehyeh asher ehyeh* (אֶהְיֶה אֲשֶׁר אֶהְיֶה) in verse 14 — I'll treat this at length in Lens 3. *Malʾāk YHWH* (מַלְאַךְ יְהוָה), "angel of the LORD," is not a separate being but, in many OT theophanies, a veiled form of YHWH's own presence — the construction "the angel of the LORD appeared... then the LORD called" in verses 2 and 4 moves without seam between messenger and sender. *Qōdeš* (קֹדֶשׁ), holy, denotes separation before moral purity. *ʿĀlîṯî lĕhaṣṣîlô* (עָלִיתִי לְהַצִּילוֹ), "I have come down to deliver" in verse 8 — the paradox of divine descent; the infinite-above bends downward, and the language is spatial and physical, not abstract.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Genre-wise, Exodus 3 is a call narrative, and call narratives have a recognizable structure: encounter, commission, objection, reassurance, sign. Norman Habel's classic 1965 article "The Form and Significance of the Call Narratives" catalogued the form across Judges 6 (Gideon), Jeremiah 1, Isaiah 6, and Ezekiel 1–3. Moses' call at the bush is the archetype. The structure runs: (1) theophanic encounter (vv. 1–6), (2) commission ("I will send you," v. 10), (3) objection ("Who am I?" v. 11), (4) reassurance ("I will be with you," v. 12), (5) sign (return to this mountain, v. 12). The structure will extend through chapter 4, where objections proliferate.

Literary devices are dense. The verb *rāʾâ* (see) patterns: Moses turns aside to see, God sees that Moses turns aside, God has seen Israel's affliction. The chapter insists that seeing is mutual and that seeing precedes acting. Wordplay on *sĕneh* / Sinai anchors the theology of a humble bush and a holy mountain. The call "Moses, Moses!" is the characteristic doubled vocative of theophany — the same doubling in "Abraham, Abraham" at the binding of Isaac (Genesis 22:11), "Jacob, Jacob" (Genesis 46:2), "Samuel, Samuel" (1 Samuel 3:10), and Jesus' "Saul, Saul" (Acts 9:4). The form is a signature of the divine address.

The chapter sits at the book's hinge. Exodus 1–2 has narrated oppression and the preservation of the deliverer. Chapter 3 turns the narrative inside out by revealing what has been happening behind the visible story: God has been listening, remembering, and now appears to act. Chapter 4 will complete the call (further objections, Aaron as partner, the return to Egypt). Chapters 5–12 will execute the plagues that verse 20 already promises. The sign-of-the-sign in verse 12 — "when you have brought the people out of Egypt, you shall serve God on this mountain" — is a prospective inclusio that will close at Exodus 19, when Israel indeed returns to Sinai.

Textual variants. The Dead Sea Scrolls fragment 4QExodᵇ preserves parts of Exodus 3 with generally Masoretic alignment. The Septuagint's rendering of *ehyeh asher ehyeh* as *egō eimi ho ōn* ("I am the one who is") in verse 14 made a decisive theological choice — ontological rather than temporal/presential — that shaped Greek patristic and scholastic metaphysics (see Lens 3). The Samaritan Pentateuch harmonizes some chronological details but preserves the divine-name revelation. Targum Onkelos renders *ehyeh* as "I will be what I will be," leaning toward the presential reading. These translation choices carry the whole weight of subsequent theological development.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Within the canonical arc, Exodus 3 is a pivot. It reaches backward to Genesis 15 (the covenant of the pieces, where God promised Abraham his descendants would come up from enslavement) and verse 15's "God of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob" explicitly names the continuity. It reaches forward to every subsequent theophany and to the New Testament's appropriation of the divine name (John 8:58; Revelation 1:8). The chapter is where the God of the patriarchs becomes the God of the nation, and where the unnamed deity of Genesis acquires the tetragrammaton as the name under which he will be worshiped.

**Jewish Interpretive Trajectory.** Second Temple readings are already intensely theological. Philo, in *Life of Moses* 1.75 and *De Mutatione Nominum* 11–12, interprets *ehyeh asher ehyeh* as revealing God's being — "He who is" — and Philo's Platonizing lens will shape Alexandrian Christianity and Augustine. Josephus (*Antiquities* 2.12) retells the bush and call with narrative expansion. The *Wisdom of Solomon* sees God's self-revelation in creation and deliverance as continuous.

Rabbinic readings in *Exodus Rabbah* 2–3 and the Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael elaborate. The lowliness of the bush becomes a theological point — God appears in the humblest plant to teach that divinity is accessible from below. R. Eliezer says God appeared in a thornbush to signify that God suffers when Israel suffers: the bush is wounded by thorns, burned but not consumed, like Israel in exile. The divine name *ehyeh asher ehyeh* receives patient philological and theological attention. The rabbis note that the verb is imperfect, not present — "I will be" or "I am becoming" — and refuse to collapse it into static ontology.

Rashi (eleventh-century France) reads verse 14 as "I will be with them in this trouble as I will be with them in subsequent troubles" — a radically historical and promissory reading. Rashi refuses the metaphysical interpretation and keeps the name tethered to God's fidelity in specific deliverances. Ibn Ezra applies grammar: *ehyeh* is the first-person imperfect of *hāyâ* (to be), and its doubling creates a tautology that functions as a refusal to be pinned down. Ramban (Nachmanides) harmonizes: the name is both metaphysical and promissory.

Maimonides, in the *Guide of the Perplexed* 1.63, reads *ehyeh asher ehyeh* as the doctrine of necessary existence: God's essence is his existence, and the name names this uniqueness. Maimonides here aligns with Philonic and later Thomistic traditions. But Maimonides also insists (*Guide* 1.61–64) that the tetragrammaton names God as he is, unqualified, while other names describe his actions — the name at the bush is the name of divine being, not divine deeds.

Modern Jewish readings reopen the name. Martin Buber, with Franz Rosenzweig, in their 1925 German translation project, rendered *ehyeh asher ehyeh* as "Ich werde dasein, als der ich dasein werde" — "I will be there as the one I will be there." Buber and Rosenzweig refused the ontological reading on philological and theological grounds: the verb is about *being-there*, presence, not abstract being. This is presence-theology, not metaphysics. Abraham Joshua Heschel, in *The Prophets* and *God in Search of Man*, extended the reading — God is the God of pathos, of presence in history, not the Unmoved Mover. Jon Levenson (in *Sinai and Zion* and *Creation and the Persistence of Evil*) reads the revelation as the continuity of covenantal commitment: the name promises God's fidelity, not God's substance. Avivah Zornberg, in *Particulars of Rapture*, reads the dialogue psychoanalytically: Moses asks for a name that would authorize him; God gives a name that refuses authorization and insists on relational becoming.

**Christian Interpretive Trajectory.** The patristic tradition sometimes ran hard into the ontological reading. Origen (*Homilies on Exodus* 3) treats the bush as a type of the Incarnation — the divine nature dwelling in human nature without consuming it. Gregory of Nyssa's *Life of Moses* allegorizes: the bush is the virginal flesh of Mary, the fire is divinity, the non-consumption is the Incarnation's wonder. Athanasius uses the bush against the Arians: divinity dwells in humanity without compromising it. Augustine (*City of God* 8.11; *De Trinitate* 5.2) reads *ehyeh asher ehyeh* via the LXX's *ho ōn* as God's self-identification as being itself — *ipsum esse* — a reading that structures the whole Latin theological tradition.

Aquinas (*Summa Theologica* I q. 13 a. 11) makes the identification systematic: God's most proper name is *qui est*, "He who is," because it names God's essence, which is his existence — *esse ipsum subsistens*, subsistent existence itself. For Aquinas, the bush reveals the metaphysical truth that only God exists essentially; all else exists participatively. This Thomistic reading dominates Catholic metaphysics.

Luther, in his lectures on Exodus (1524–27), reads the bush pastorally: God appears in the humble to show that God comes down. The divine name is less a metaphysical puzzle for Luther than a word of address and promise — "I will be" is a promise of presence in Christ. Calvin's *Harmony* treats verse 14 philologically and soberly: the name denotes God's eternal self-existence and his fidelity, both; Calvin refuses to force the name into a single metaphysical mold.

Critical scholarship from Wellhausen forward treated the chapter as a composite of J and E with the name-revelation as a late theological development. Gerhard von Rad's *Old Testament Theology* I read the name-revelation as the heart of the Yahwistic confession — YHWH is the God who acts in history. Martin Noth's *Exodus* commentary was more fragmentary. Brevard Childs's *The Book of Exodus* (1974) made the decisive canonical move: he read the final form as theologically coherent and took *ehyeh asher ehyeh* as a deliberate refusal of metaphysical capture — God's freedom to be who God will be. Terence Fretheim's *Exodus* reads the name relationally: it names the God who commits to be present. William Propp, in the Anchor Bible *Exodus 1–18*, offers dense philological analysis — he considers whether the verb is a preterite ("I have been") or imperfect ("I will be" or "I am") and leans toward the imperfect. Walter Brueggemann reads the name as liberative: it cannot be weaponized by empire because it refuses definition. Göran Larsson stresses Jewish continuity. Ellen Davis reads the theophany ecologically: the holy ground and the burning plant.

**Where the trajectories converge and diverge.** Both traditions treat the chapter as decisive for divine self-revelation. Both elaborate the lowliness of the bush. The decisive divergence is on the divine name. The Greek/Latin Christian tradition, following the LXX, read *ehyeh asher ehyeh* metaphysically — God as being itself, the ground of all existence, the one whose essence is existence. This reading generated the entire Thomistic ontological tradition and shaped Western philosophy from Augustine to Heidegger. The Jewish tradition (with notable exceptions like Maimonides, who partly agreed) has tended to read the name presentially and historically — God as the one who will be present in history, whose name promises fidelity rather than defining essence. Buber and Rosenzweig's translation was an explicit attempt to recover the presential reading against what they saw as Hellenistic distortion.

The second divergence is the angel of the LORD. Christians from the patristic period read *malʾāk YHWH* Christologically — the pre-incarnate Son appearing to the patriarchs and Moses. Justin Martyr, Irenaeus, and Augustine all developed this. Jewish tradition reads the angel as a manifestation of YHWH's own agency without hypostatic distinction, refusing the Christological move.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

What did the author (or final editor) believe God was doing in Exodus 3? The chapter's claim is that the God of the patriarchs is about to act decisively in history, that this God has heard the cry, and that this God will reveal himself by a name that binds him to deliverance while refusing to reduce him to it. The author's theological vision is that the God of Abraham is not a local deity but a God who acts universally (creation through the nations of verse 8) while choosing covenantally. The name given at the bush is the name under which Israel will worship, fight, lament, and hope — and the name is both a gift and a refusal, both presence and mystery.

**Tension 1: The nature of the divine name.** Is *ehyeh asher ehyeh* a metaphysical self-identification (I am Being itself) or a covenantal-presential promise (I will be with you as I will be)?

- **Catholic teaching** (Aquinas *ST* I q. 13; Catechism §§203–213) holds the ontological reading: God's most proper name is *qui est*, and Exodus 3:14 reveals that God's essence is his existence. This generates the whole analogical ontology of Thomistic metaphysics and underwrites natural theology.
- **Reformed Protestant readings** (Calvin; Kevin Vanhoozer) hold a mediating position: the name reveals both God's self-existence and his covenantal fidelity. Calvin refuses to pit metaphysics against relationality.
- **Lutheran readings** (Luther; later Jüngel, Jenson) stress the promissory dimension — the name is a word of address, a promise of presence in Christ. The name is for faith, not speculation.
- **Orthodox readings** (Lossky, Schmemann, Gregory of Nyssa) preserve the ontological reading but balance it with divine incomprehensibility — the essence of God is unknowable, and the name reveals the energies by which God acts. The name is not a handle but an icon.
- **Jewish readings** (Buber, Rosenzweig, Heschel, Sarna) resist metaphysical capture. The name is promise of presence, covenantal fidelity, refusal of idolatry. Maimonides partly agrees with the ontological reading but insists that any positive predicate of God must be negatively qualified.
- **Critical readings** (Childs, Brueggemann) read the name as deliberate ambiguity — God's freedom to be who God will be. The name refuses domestication.

Steelman the least intuitive: the Buber-Rosenzweig presential reading refuses what has been the dominant Christian metaphysical reading for 1700 years. The philological case is strong. The verb is imperfect, and the Hebrew mind does not think in terms of essence and existence in the Greek philosophical sense. To read the name as *ipsum esse subsistens* is to read it through the Septuagint's Greek translation rather than through its Hebrew morphology. The presential reading is not a modern softening but possibly the original sense.

**Where this leaves us:** The name is probably both — covenantal presence first in Hebrew context, ontological self-identification as a legitimate later reflection. The tradition's generative power has come from refusing to choose.

**Tension 2: Divine foreknowledge and human freedom in the plague cycle.** In verses 19–20, God tells Moses that Pharaoh will not let Israel go "unless compelled by a mighty hand." This prefigures the hardening-of-heart theology that will dominate chapters 4–14. Is Pharaoh free, and if not, how is God's judgment just?

- **Catholic teaching** (Aquinas *ST* I q. 19 a. 9 on antecedent and consequent will; Catechism §311) distinguishes between God's antecedent will (that all be saved) and God's consequent will (that justice be done, including judgment on those who refuse). Pharaoh is free to repent but will not, and God works with that refusal.
- **Reformed readings** (Calvin *Institutes* III.23–24) hold predestination and double agency: Pharaoh's hardening is both Pharaoh's sin and God's sovereign use. Calvin argues God's justice is not compromised because Pharaoh's refusal is genuinely his own even as it is decreed.
- **Lutheran readings** (Luther *On the Bondage of the Will*) hold that all human wills are bound and only God can liberate them; Pharaoh's hardening is God withholding grace, not imposing sin.
- **Wesleyan/Arminian readings** stress prevenient grace and genuine freedom: Pharaoh could repent; God's foreknowledge is not causation. The hardening is Pharaoh's own work, with God's forbearance.
- **Orthodox readings** (Gregory of Nyssa; John of Damascus) preserve human freedom strongly. God's foreknowledge does not cause human action. The mystery is real.
- **Jewish readings** (Maimonides *Hilchot Teshuvah* 5–6) insist on free will while acknowledging that repeated sin can close the doors of repentance. Pharaoh's hardening after his own refusals is a natural consequence, not divine coercion.
- **Critical readings** (Fretheim) read the hardening as divine response to human intransigence and as theological claim that judgment is not arbitrary but responsive.

Steelman the least intuitive: the Calvinist double-agency reading. It seems ethically monstrous at first glance, but it is doing theological work — refusing to let God's sovereignty be compromised by apparent human resistance, and refusing to reduce Pharaoh's sin to external force. On Calvinist terms, Pharaoh's refusal is most fully Pharaoh's own precisely because it is sovereignly embraced; the human is not a puppet but a fully actualized rebel.

**Where this leaves us:** The text presents both: Pharaoh's will is real, and God's sovereignty is real, and the narrative holds them without systematic resolution.

One unresolved question: If God "came down" (v. 8) to deliver Israel, why did it take four hundred years to come? What did the God who sees and hears do during the centuries of silence?

## The Thread

**Genesis 15:13–16 — The four-hundred-year prophecy.** God told Abraham exactly what would happen. Exodus 3 activates this timetable. The "descent" of verse 8 has been planned all along.

**1 Kings 19:8–18 — Elijah at Horeb.** The only other prophet who meets God at this mountain. Elijah collapses, God feeds him, Elijah travels forty days to the very spot where Moses stood, and God passes by — not in wind, earthquake, or fire, but in the still small voice. The parallel is deliberate: when you get to 1 Kings 19, remember that the God who burns in the bush can also speak in silence.

**John 8:58 — "Before Abraham was, I am."** Jesus' *egō eimi* deliberately echoes the LXX of Exodus 3:14. When you reach John's seven *egō eimi* statements, remember this bush. John is making a Christological claim rooted in the divine name.

**Revelation 1:8 — "I am the Alpha and the Omega, the one who is, who was, and who is to come."** The closing of the canon picks up the name of Exodus 3 and expands it temporally in all three directions. The God of Moses is the God of the end.
