# Romans 15: One Voice, the Collection, and the Road to Spain

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 15 is the most autobiographical and concretely geographical chapter in Romans, and the historical world it discloses is exact. Paul writes from Corinth (most likely from the household of Gaius, Rom 16:23 — corroborated by 1 Cor 1:14 and Acts 19:21–20:3) in the late winter of 56–57 CE, on the eve of his departure for Jerusalem with the collection. His itinerary is laid out: Corinth to Jerusalem first, then back through Rome on the way to Spain (15:24–28). Acts 20–21 shows that the Jerusalem leg unfolded much as Paul anticipated, except that his arrival was followed by riot, arrest, two years' detention in Caesarea under Felix and Festus, and finally a Roman shipwreck-voyage that brought him to the capital not as a free missionary apostle but as an imperial prisoner. The plan in Romans 15 is not the plan that came to pass.

The geographical sweep of 15:19 — "from Jerusalem and all the way around to Illyricum I have fulfilled the ministry of the gospel of Christ" — is breathtaking and not entirely literal. Paul claims a quarter-arc of the eastern Mediterranean as the scope of his work: Syria, Asia, Macedonia, Achaia, with the western terminus at Illyricum (modern Albania and the Dalmatian coast, never explicitly named as a Pauline destination in Acts though possibly in view at the end of Acts 20:1–2 when Paul moves from Macedonia "in those parts"). The verb *peplērōkenai* ("I have fulfilled / completed") raises a question: in what sense had Paul "fulfilled" the gospel ministry across this vast area, when entire cities had not yet heard? Robert Jewett's Hermeneia argues that Paul deploys a "missionary representational" theology — establishing apostolic foundations in key urban centers from which the gospel will radiate, rather than evangelizing every village. Once representative ekklesia are planted in the major cities (Antioch, Ephesus, Corinth, Thessalonica, Philippi), the regional task is "filled up." The geography of fulfillment is symbolic and imperial — the urban network of Roman Asia Minor and Greece — not exhaustive.

The Spain plan (15:24, 28) is historically intriguing. Spain — *Hispania*, Roman provinces of *Tarraconensis*, *Baetica*, *Lusitania* — was the western limit of the Roman empire and the linguistic boundary where Greek gave way to Latin and the indigenous Celtiberian and Iberian tongues. Whether Paul actually reached Spain has been debated since antiquity. The earliest witness is *1 Clement* 5:7 (c. 96 CE), written from Rome to Corinth, which says of Paul that "having taught righteousness to the whole world, and having reached the limits of the West (*to terma tēs duseōs*), and having borne his testimony before the rulers, he departed from this world." "The limits of the west" is most naturally read as Spain in a Roman context where Italy is the center. The Muratorian Canon (late 2nd c.?) refers to Paul's "departure from the city to Spain." The Pastoral Epistles, if Pauline, presuppose a release from a first Roman imprisonment, further travel, and a second arrest leading to martyrdom under Nero (most likely 64–67 CE). Eusebius and Jerome accept the Spanish journey. The historical question is open, but Romans 15 is the textual rock on which the entire later tradition stands.

The Jerusalem collection (15:25–28) is the second great historical reality of the chapter and the most extensive single project of Paul's mission, attested across Galatians (2:10), 1 Corinthians (16:1–4), 2 Corinthians (8–9), Acts (24:17), and Romans 15. Paul has been gathering money from his gentile churches across Macedonia (Philippi, Thessalonica, Berea) and Achaia (Corinth, Cenchreae) for the *ptōchoi tōn hagiōn tōn en Ierousalēm*, "the poor among the saints in Jerusalem" (15:26). The Jerusalem community's poverty is multiply attested — recurring famines under Claudius (Acts 11:28; Josephus *Antiquities* 20.51, 101 records the great famine of 46–48 CE under Tiberius Alexander); the experiment in shared property recorded in Acts 4:32–37 likely produced cash-flow strain; and the tense relationship between Jewish and Christian communities meant the Christ-believing minority operated outside many traditional Jewish charitable networks. The collection has multiple layers of meaning: practical poor-relief; symbolic ratification of the gentile churches' debt to Jerusalem (15:27, "they owe it to them"); embodied unity of Jew and gentile in Christ; and likely an allusion to the eschatological pilgrimage of nations to Zion bringing tribute (Isa 60:5–7; Mic 4:1–2; Tob 13:11) — Paul as the apostle of the gentiles bringing the firstfruits to the holy city in fulfillment of prophetic vision. Stephan Joubert (*Paul as Benefactor*, 2000) and David Downs (*The Offering of the Gentiles*, 2008) explore the Greco-Roman benefaction (*euergetism*) framework alongside the Jewish-eschatological frame.

Three Greek terms shape the chapter's argument. ***Diakonos*** (διάκονος, 15:8), "servant," is now applied to Christ himself ("Christ became a *diakonos* to the circumcision"), echoing the magistrate of 13:4 and anticipating Phoebe of 16:1 — the same word organizes the whole concluding parenetic-personal section. ***Leitourgos*** (λειτουργός, 15:16), "minister / priestly officer," casts Paul's mission in cultic terms: Paul is the *leitourgos* of Christ Jesus offering up the gentiles as an acceptable *prosphora* (offering, 15:16). This is sacrificial-priestly language transposed onto missionary apostleship — a Pauline move with major consequences for later Christian theology of ministry. ***Plērōma*** (πλήρωμα) appears at 15:29 ("I will come in the *plērōma* of the blessing of Christ"), the same word used at 11:12, 25 of the *plērōma* of Israel and the gentiles — an eschatological-fullness term. ***Diakonia*** (διακονία, 15:31) names the Jerusalem collection, again knitting service-of-the-poor to the broader vocabulary of Christian ministry.

Material culture: Roman roads (the Via Egnatia from Dyrrhachium to Thessalonica; the Via Appia from Brundisium to Rome) made the Spain project geographically conceivable. Sea travel from Corinth to Caesarea, then back across the Mediterranean to Rome and beyond to Tarragona on the Spanish coast was the standard imperial route. A Pauline missionary plan of this scope presupposed the *Pax Romana* and the imperial road and shipping network that the gospel had quietly co-opted.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Chapter 15 has two distinct halves stitched at verse 14, with three compositional units before that hinge. The first unit (15:1–6) extends the weak-strong argument of chapter 14 into Christological grounding: "Christ did not please himself" (15:3), citing Psalm 69:9. The second unit (15:7–13) is a programmatic statement of Jew-gentile unity in Christ, built on a four-fold catena of OT citations. The third unit (15:14–33) is the apostolic peroratio — Paul's account of his ministry, his travel plans, and his prayer request — closing with a benediction (15:33) that some manuscript witnesses treat as the original end of the letter.

The catena of 15:9–12 is one of the densest scriptural-citation passages in Paul, four OT texts in a row each rooting gentile inclusion in Israel's scriptures: Psalm 18:49 (David among the Gentiles, "I will praise you among the Gentiles, and sing to your name"); Deuteronomy 32:43 LXX (the Song of Moses calling the Gentiles to rejoice with God's people — note the LXX expansion over the MT, which Paul follows); Psalm 117:1 (the universal-praise psalm); and Isaiah 11:10 (the Davidic root of Jesse, "even he who arises to rule the Gentiles, in him will the Gentiles hope"). The four texts are drawn from the three sections of the Hebrew canon — Torah (Deut 32), Prophets (Isa 11), and Writings (Pss 18, 117) — a rabbinic *gezerah shawah* technique designed to demonstrate the unanimity of scripture on the gentile mission. Richard Hays (*Echoes of Scripture in the Letters of Paul*, 1989; *The Conversion of the Imagination*, 2005) treats this passage as the climactic statement of Pauline scriptural hermeneutics: every OT citation is selected to display gentile inclusion as the scripture's own inner logic. The Davidic note in two of the four texts (Pss 18 and Isa 11) reinforces the messianic-Davidic frame Paul opened the letter with at 1:3 ("descended from David according to the flesh") — Romans is structured by an inclusio of Davidic gentile-rule.

Rhetorically the chapter modulates from parenesis (15:1–13) to epistolary self-disclosure (15:14–33). The shift in 15:14 ("I myself am satisfied about you, my brothers") is a classical *captatio benevolentiae* — the rhetorical stroke of credit-extension that softens the boldness Paul has shown in writing to a community he did not found. The travel-plan narrative of 15:22–29 follows ancient epistolary conventions for *philophronetic* (friendship-affirming) closing material. The prayer request of 15:30–32 is a *parakaleō*-introduced exhortation — "I appeal to you" — with three substantive petitions: deliverance from Judean unbelievers, acceptance of the collection by the Jerusalem saints, and a joyful arrival in Rome.

Three benedictions punctuate the chapter (15:5–6, 13, 33), and a fourth follows in 16:20. The accumulation of benedictions is unusual for Paul and may indicate that the chapter passed through editorial refinement — perhaps with the fourteen-chapter and fifteen-chapter recensions both circulating and benedictions being attached at different terminal points. The doxology of 16:25–27 (which P46 places at the end of chapter 15) compounds the textual question: did some early form of the letter end at 15:33? The most influential modern reconstruction is Harry Gamble's, who argues that the earliest form of the letter ended at 16:23 (or 16:24, with the textual "grace of our Lord" benediction); the doxology of 16:25–27 is a later addition; the fourteen-chapter form is a still later liturgical abridgment. But the manuscript evidence is genuinely tangled, and any reconstruction is provisional.

There is no major textual variant within chapter 15 itself, but the chapter's place in the textual transmission of Romans is at the heart of the doxology problem.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

**Canonical arc.** The chapter's first half (15:1–13) culminates the letter's theological argument. The Davidic Messiah confirms God's promises to the patriarchs (15:8) and brings the Gentiles to glorify God for his mercy (15:9). This is the inner logic of the entire letter from 1:1–7 onward: the *euangelion* of God concerning his Son, descended from David, declared Son of God in power. The four OT citations of 15:9–12 close the canonical loop opened at 1:3: David, Moses, the Psalter, Isaiah — all converge on a single eschatological fulfillment, gentile praise of the God of Israel through Israel's Messiah. The chapter thus stands as the doctrinal climax of Romans, with chapters 1–14 as the path and 15:1–13 as the destination.

The chapter's second half (15:14–33) opens onto the canonical arc of mission. The Spain plan situates Paul's apostolate within the prophetic vision of the gospel's reach to "the ends of the earth" (Isa 49:6, the Servant Song; Acts 1:8, "to the ends of the earth"). The Jerusalem collection brings forward the Isaianic vision of nations bringing tribute to Zion (Isa 60:5–11). Paul's apostleship is imagined as the historical execution of the prophetic eschatological pattern.

**Jewish trajectory.** The eschatological pilgrimage of nations to Zion is a major prophetic theme: Isa 2:2–4 / Mic 4:1–2 (nations stream to the mountain of the Lord); Isa 60 (gentiles bringing gold and frankincense); Isa 66:18–21 (gentiles bringing brothers as an offering); Tobit 13–14 (Jerusalem rebuilt with sapphires and emeralds, all nations praising); Zech 8:20–23 (ten men from every nation taking hold of the robe of a Jew, "let us go with you, for we have heard that God is with you"). The Jewish hope was that at the eschaton, gentile nations would acknowledge Israel's God and bring tribute. Paul reads his collection as the partial firstfruits of this expectation — and more strikingly, reads the gentile churches' generosity as their own pilgrimage, with him as their representative bringing the offering to Jerusalem in their place.

The Jewish reception of the chapter has historically been suspicious of the catena strategy — a pulling of OT proof-texts out of context to demonstrate gentile inclusion in ways the original texts do not always require. The eschatological pilgrimage tradition in rabbinic and post-rabbinic Jewish thought continued into the medieval and modern periods (preserved liturgically in the *Aleinu* prayer's universalist hope) but understood the gentile inclusion as an end-time event subordinated to Israel's election, whereas Paul reads the eschatological inclusion as having already begun in the gentile mission. The dispute is the heart of the Jewish-Christian theological conversation.

**Patristic.** The Fathers read Romans 15 as the charter of catholic mission. Irenaeus, Tertullian, and Origen all cite the four-fold catena of 15:9–12 to demonstrate that the gentile mission is not Pauline innovation but scriptural fulfillment. Augustine treats 15:14–33 in the context of his own missiological reflection (the Donatist controversy about the universality of the church) and the Jerusalem collection as a paradigm of inter-ecclesial communion. Eastern fathers (Chrysostom, Theodoret) emphasize the priestly-cultic language of 15:16 — Paul as the *leitourgos* offering the gentiles — as the apostolic foundation of the church's eucharistic-sacrificial theology.

**Medieval.** Aquinas's commentary on Romans treats 15:8 ("Christ became a servant to the circumcision") as a major Christological-economic text: Christ's earthly ministry was directed primarily to the Jews ("the circumcision") to confirm the patriarchal promises, while the gentile mission unfolded through the apostolic church. The exegetical tradition produced refined accounts of the relationship between the *adventus Christi* to Israel and the *missio* to the gentiles.

**Reformation.** The chapter received less direct dogmatic attention than chapters 1–11, but Calvin's commentary (1540) treats 15:14–33 as the model of apostolic ministry — bold but not domineering, scripture-saturated, motivated by priestly offering of converts to God. The Reformed tradition has been particularly attentive to 15:4 ("for whatever was written in former days was written for our instruction, that through endurance and through the encouragement of the Scriptures we might have hope"), reading the verse as a foundational charter of OT canonical-instructional value.

**Modern missions.** Romans 15 became a charter text of the modern Protestant missionary movement. William Carey's *An Enquiry into the Obligations of Christians to Use Means for the Conversion of the Heathens* (1792) cites Paul's "ambition to preach the gospel where Christ has already not been named" (15:20) as an apostolic-missionary principle. The "frontier missions" theology of the late twentieth century (Ralph Winter's *unreached peoples* movement) is a direct extension of Paul's strategy in 15:20–21.

**The four modern interpretive schools.**

The **Old Perspective** reads chapter 15 as the practical-pastoral coda to the dogmatic argument of 1–11. The Christological grounding of the weak-strong accommodation (15:3, 7) and the OT catena of 15:9–12 demonstrate that the gospel of justification has always been intended for both Jew and Gentile. The chapter's missionary self-disclosure (15:14–33) is taken at face value: Paul is the authentic apostle of the gentiles, and his Spain plan is the natural extension of his commission. Schreiner, Moo, and Murray all read in this register.

The **New Perspective** (Sanders, Dunn, Wright) reads the chapter as the climactic statement of Paul's covenantal-ecclesiological vision. Wright in particular treats 15:7–13 as the doctrinal capstone of Romans: God's faithfulness to the patriarchs is fulfilled when Jews and Gentiles together glorify God with one voice through the Davidic Messiah. The catena of 15:9–12 is the scriptural demonstration that Israel's election was always unto the gentile mission. Wright reads the Jerusalem collection in 15:25–28 not just as poor-relief but as the embodied performance of the theology of Romans: the gentile churches' material gift to Jerusalem is the love-debt of which 13:8 spoke and the ratification of the Jew-Gentile *ekklēsia* of which 11:25–32 prophesied.

**Apocalyptic Paul** (Käsemann, Martyn, Gaventa, Campbell) emphasizes the cosmological-missionary framework of 15:14–21. Paul's apostolate is not an institutional career but an apocalyptic operation, the unveiling of the gospel of God's act in Christ across the *oikoumenē* in advance of the consummation. Gaventa's *Our Mother Saint Paul* (2007) and her Romans commentary stress the maternal-priestly-sacrificial imagery of 15:16: Paul as the *leitourgos* offering up the gentiles in apocalyptic fulfillment of the prophetic pilgrimage. Käsemann reads the collection as the pre-eschatological embodiment of the eschatological people of God.

**Paul Within Judaism** (Nanos, Eisenbaum, Thiessen, Fredriksen) reads the chapter as a thoroughly Jewish text from a Jewish apostle to gentiles understanding themselves as engrafted into the Jewish covenantal story. Fredriksen's *Paul: The Pagan's Apostle* (2017) emphasizes the eschatological-pilgrimage frame: Paul's collection is the firstfruits of the gentile influx of the eschaton, and his Spain mission is the apocalyptic extension of the prophetic pilgrimage to the western edge of the world. The chapter is read not as charter-text of "gentile Christianity" against Judaism but as one Jewish apostle's prophetic apocalyptic execution of the prophetic vision.

The contemporary commentary tradition is extensive. Cranfield, Fitzmyer, Moo, Dunn, Schreiner, Wright, and Jewett (Hermeneia, with extensive material on the Jerusalem collection and the Spanish mission). For the collection specifically: Stephan Joubert, David Downs, John Barclay (*Paul and the Gift*, 2015) on the gift-economy frame. For the missionary-strategy material: Eckhard Schnabel (*Paul the Missionary*, 2008) and Roland Allen (*Missionary Methods: St. Paul's or Ours?*, 1912 — a foundational missiological text built largely on Romans 15).

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

**Tension One: Is gentile inclusion in Christ a *fulfillment* of Israel's covenant or a *replacement* of Israel by a new people?**

Position A — *Fulfillment.* The patristic-medieval-Reformation mainstream: the gospel fulfills Israel's covenantal vocation, the Gentiles are incorporated into the people of God, and the church is the ongoing reality of Israel's covenant — but the people-of-God is now Jew-and-Gentile in Christ, with no remainder Israel-after-the-flesh that has independent covenantal standing. The steelman: the four-fold catena of 15:9–12 reads gentile inclusion as the inner logic of Israel's scriptures themselves, not a sideways break from them.

Position B — *Inclusion alongside Israel's continuing election.* The Reformed and post-Holocaust mainline reading, supported by Romans 11:25–32: Israel-after-the-flesh has not been cast away, and gentile believers are engrafted into a covenant whose Jewish branches retain their election. The steelman: Paul has just spent chapters 9–11 establishing precisely that God has not rejected his people, and the climactic ekphonēsis "all Israel will be saved" (11:26) cannot be erased by a supersessionist reading of chapter 15.

Position C — *Two parallel covenantal economies.* Some Paul-Within-Judaism scholars (Stowers's *A Rereading of Romans*, 1994; Fredriksen) move toward a sharper distinction: Jewish believers continue under Torah; gentile believers are saved as gentiles through Christ without becoming Jews; the *ekklēsia* is the eschatological gathering of two covenantally distinct peoples. The steelman: this preserves the integrity of Israel's election while making intelligible Paul's refusal to require gentile circumcision.

Where this leaves us: the chapter's plain language is Position A in form (one people, Jew and Gentile together, with one voice glorifying God) but its argument depends on Position B's logic (the patriarchal promises are ratified, not abrogated, and the gentile inclusion is *added to*, not *replacing*, Israel's covenantal standing). The text resists a clean supersessionism. Whether Position C is required to do justice to chapters 9–11 is one of the live questions in contemporary Pauline scholarship.

**Tension Two: What is the proper relationship between the church's spiritual and material economies?**

Position A — *Material support follows from spiritual debt* (Paul's plain reading at 15:27). The gentile churches owe Jerusalem material help because they have shared in Jerusalem's spiritual blessings. The economy of grace creates an economy of debt. The steelman: this is the explicit logic of the chapter and is the foundational text for an entire Christian tradition of inter-ecclesial financial responsibility.

Position B — *Spiritual gift flows freely; material exchange is auxiliary.* The strict gift-economy reading (Barclay's *Paul and the Gift*): the spiritual blessing of Christ is freely given without expectation of return; material support is fitting but not strictly owed. The steelman: any insistence on material reciprocation risks compromising the unconditioned character of grace.

Position C — *Material gift is the *visible* form of spiritual unity.* The sacramental-ecclesiological reading (Eastern Orthodox, Anglo-Catholic, much of contemporary mission theology): the collection is not a transaction but a sign — the embodied visibility of the *koinōnia* between the gentile and Jewish churches. Material exchange is the body of which spiritual unity is the soul. The steelman: this avoids both the legalism of A and the disembodiment of B.

Where this leaves us: Paul's language is closest to A but his theology is closest to C. The collection is *owed* (15:27), but the owing is not contractual — it is the natural debt of a body to itself. Material exchange is the embodiment of spiritual *koinōnia*. The chapter's vision is not transactional but sacramental — and that vision is the foundation of every later Christian theology of inter-church partnership and the diaconal economy.

One unresolved question: Paul writes "I will leave for Spain by way of you" (15:28). He never recorded reaching Spain in any extant text. The earliest extra-biblical witness (1 Clement 5:7, c. 96 CE) suggests he did. The Pastoral Epistles imply a release from a first Roman imprisonment. But we have no *epistula ad Hispaniēs*, no Pauline letter from Tarragona, no mention of a Spanish church in any first-century source. The historical question — did Paul reach Spain? — bears on how we read this chapter. If yes, Romans 15 is the prelude to a missionary expansion the canon does not record. If no, Romans 15 is the testament of a missionary-eschatological vision that the historical events derailed. Either reading transforms the chapter's tone.

## The Thread

- Romans 1:3 — "descended from David according to the flesh." The Davidic root motif of 15:12 closes a canonical inclusio.
- Romans 11:25–32 — the *plērōma* of the Gentiles and the salvation of all Israel. The collection of 15:25–28 is the embodied performance of this eschatological vision.
- Isaiah 11:10 — the root of Jesse, the rallying-banner for the nations. Cited verbatim at 15:12 as the doctrinal climax of the OT catena.
- Acts 21–28 — the Jerusalem arrival, the arrest, the Caesarean detention, the voyage to Rome. The historical fulfillment of 15:25–32, with the prayer for deliverance partially answered (Paul lived) and partially unanswered (the *diakonia* was not received without trouble).

Flag forward: chapter 16 closes the letter with 26 named greetings, embodying in personal-relational form what chapter 15 has argued in doctrinal-missiological form. Phoebe of Cenchreae is the *diakonos* who carries this letter to Rome — she is the historical link between Paul's writing in Corinth and the Roman audience's hearing of the catena and the collection. The Jerusalem collection eclipses Paul's biography; the Spain plan haunts the canonical record; and the chapter's three benedictions (5–6, 13, 33) prepare the listener for the personal greetings that follow.
