# Lamentations 3: The Steadfast Love That Does Not End the Lament

## Lens 1 — The Historical World

Chapter 3 is the structural center of the book and the place where Western Christianity has been most tempted to stop reading. Verses 22-24 — "The steadfast love of the LORD never ceases; his mercies never come to an end; they are new every morning; great is your faithfulness" — supply the title and chorus of Thomas Chisholm's 1923 hymn that still sits in nearly every Protestant hymnal. The temptation, having found this verse, is to treat the rest of the chapter as preface or aftermath. The chapter itself does not let the reader rest there. It begins in despair (vv. 1-18), turns suddenly to hope (vv. 19-39), descends back into communal confession that God has not forgiven (vv. 40-47), and ends — sixty-six verses later — with a curse on the enemies (vv. 64-66). The text refuses to let v. 22 carry more weight than the ruin around it.

The historical setting remains 586 BCE Jerusalem, but the chapter's perspective shifts dramatically. Where chapter 1 had been Jerusalem personified and chapter 2 had been the narrator-poet, chapter 3 introduces a single first-person speaker — *geber* (גֶּבֶר), "the man," in v. 1, with the definite force of a strong, militarily-fit male, the same root from which "Gabriel" derives. Jewish tradition (Talmud Bava Bathra 14b-15a) identifies the *geber* as Jeremiah, reading the chapter autobiographically: vv. 52-55 ("they flung me alive into the pit and cast stones on me; water closed over my head") echo the historical episode of Jeremiah's confinement in Malchiah's cistern (Jeremiah 38:6) — sunk in mud, lifted out by the Cushite Ebed-Melech. The Septuagint's preface and the Vulgate's placement reinforce the Jeremianic identification. Modern critical scholarship reads the *geber* differently: William Lanahan and Iain Provan read him as the personified communal voice; Claus Westermann reads him as a Job-like representative sufferer; Tod Linafelt argues the figure is deliberately ambiguous so that the individual speaker can become "we" at v. 40 ("Let us test and examine our ways") without rupture.

Material culture saturates the chapter's imagery. The "rod" of v. 1 (*shevet*, שֵׁבֶט) is the same word as the parental disciplinary rod of Proverbs 13:24, and the same word as the king's scepter (Genesis 49:10) — the chapter's framing image is paternal-judicial discipline. The "wormwood" (*la'anah*, לַעֲנָה) of vv. 15, 19 is *Artemisia herba-alba*, a bitter desert shrub still abundant in the Judean wilderness; it appears in Deuteronomy 29:18, Jeremiah 9:15, Amos 5:7, 6:12 always as the taste of judgment. The "gall" (*rosh*, רֹאשׁ) of v. 19 is a poisonous plant — the same vocabulary later appearing in the cup offered Christ at the cross (Matthew 27:34). The "yoke" of v. 27 (*'ol*, עֹל) is the wooden agricultural ox-yoke, used metaphorically of foreign domination (1 Kings 12:4-14, Jeremiah 27:2-12) — to "bear the yoke in his youth" is to internalize submission early. The "pit" (*bor*, בּוֹר) of vv. 53-55 is the cistern — a stone-lined water reservoir, often empty in summer, used as a makeshift prison; archaeological excavation at sites like Lachish has recovered cisterns matching the description.

Five Hebrew terms govern the theological argument. *Chesed* (חֶסֶד, v. 22), often translated "steadfast love" or "loving-kindness," is the covenant-loyalty term that has been conspicuously *absent* in chapters 1-2 — its appearance here, after seventy verses of silence on the subject, is the chapter's hinge. Norman Snaith's *The Distinctive Ideas of the Old Testament* (1944) and Katherine Sakenfeld's *The Meaning of Hesed in the Hebrew Bible* (1978) trace its semantic field: not romantic love, not generic kindness, but loyalty within a covenant relationship that persists despite the partner's failure. *Rachamim* (רַחֲמִים, v. 22) is from the root *rechem* (רֶחֶם, "womb") — "womb-love," a maternal compassion. The same root governs Isaiah 49:15's "Can a woman forget her nursing child?" *Emunah* (אֱמוּנָה, v. 23) is "faithfulness" or "trustworthiness," the Hebrew root of "amen." *Cheleq* (חֵלֶק, v. 24), "portion," is the priestly inheritance language — the Levites had no tribal land allotment because "the LORD is their portion" (Numbers 18:20, Deuteronomy 10:9). To say "the LORD is my portion" is to claim Levitical landlessness as theological possession. *Geber* (גֶּבֶר, v. 1, 27, 35, 39), "man," distinguishes the warrior-figure from the generic *adam* — its repeated use marks a specific theology of male sufferer-as-representative.

## Lens 2 — The Literature

Lamentations 3 is the most elaborate acrostic in the Hebrew Bible. Its sixty-six verses are organized into twenty-two triplets, each triplet sharing an initial Hebrew letter — verses 1, 2, and 3 all begin with *aleph*; verses 4, 5, and 6 all begin with *bet*; and so on through *taw*. The same *pe-'ayin* inversion observed in chapters 2 and 4 (*pe* before *'ayin*, departing from the standard alphabet) is preserved in chapter 3, in vv. 46-51. The triple acrostic represents a fivefold intensification beyond ordinary alphabetic poetry: where a single acrostic asserts the alphabet of grief, the triple acrostic insists on it. Adele Berlin notes that the form turns the poem into virtuoso liturgical performance — only a master could sustain a triple acrostic over sixty-six verses without lapsing into filler. The aesthetic discipline is itself argument: the form refuses to break even when the content is breaking.

The chapter has an unusual structural arc that interpreters describe in different ways. Westermann reads a five-part structure: lament (1-18), reflection (19-24), wisdom teaching (25-39), confession and petition (40-51), thanksgiving for past deliverance (52-66). Hillers reads a U-shape: descent into despair, ascent to hope at the center, return to lament. Linafelt rejects both readings, arguing the chapter does not arc upward but oscillates — hope is one of several voices, not the dominant one. The decisive observation for Linafelt's reading is that the chapter ends in vv. 64-66 with an imprecation against the enemies, not a benediction. Whatever vv. 22-24 are doing, they are not closing the book.

The voice shifts are the chapter's structural signature. Verses 1-21 are "I" (the *geber*); verses 22-39 generalize to "the soul who seeks him," "a man," "us"; verses 40-47 become explicitly "we" ("Let us test and examine"); verses 48-51 return to "I" ("my eyes flow"); verses 52-66 are "I" addressing "you" (the LORD). The grammar performs the chapter's theological argument: the individual sufferer's voice can become the community's voice, and the community's voice can return to address God in petition.

Two intertextual fields converge here. First, the chapter is saturated with Job vocabulary — God as bear/lion (3:10; cf. Job 10:16), being walled in (3:7-9; cf. Job 19:8), being made a target (3:12; cf. Job 16:12), shutting out prayer (3:8; cf. Job 30:20). Westermann argues Lamentations 3 belongs alongside Job in a wisdom-lament tradition. Second, the chapter draws on Jeremiah's "confessions" (Jer 11-20) and the laments of the Psalter, especially Psalms 22 and 88. The pit imagery of vv. 53-55 echoes Jonah 2 ("the waters closed in over me") and Psalm 69:1-2.

The text's most discussed ambiguity is v. 33: "for he does not afflict from his heart (*lo' 'innah millibo*, לֹא עִנָּה מִלִּבּוֹ) or grieve the children of men." The Hebrew is unusual. Some translate "he does not afflict willingly"; others "he does not afflict from the heart" (i.e., it is not his deepest disposition); the Targum reads "he does not afflict from himself" (the affliction is response to sin, not divine spontaneity). Robert Alter's translation captures the ambiguity: "for not from His heart did He afflict." This single line has been the textual basis for centuries of theological debate about whether divine wrath is essential or accidental to God.

## Lens 3 — The Larger Story

Chapter 3 is the place where Lamentations enters the Christian liturgical and devotional tradition most deeply. It is also the place where Jewish and Christian readings most decisively diverge.

### Jewish Interpretive Trajectory

Talmud Bava Bathra 14b-15a names Jeremiah as the *geber* and reads the chapter as autobiographical. Eikhah Rabbah develops vv. 22-23 into a foundational text for Jewish morning prayer — the "new every morning" of the LORD's mercies becomes the basis for *Modeh Ani*, the prayer recited upon waking ("I give thanks before You, living and eternal King, for returning my soul to me with compassion; great is Your faithfulness"). The Hebrew prayer cites Lam 3:23 directly. This is liturgically momentous: the chapter that emerges from the destruction of the Temple becomes the first prayer the observant Jew speaks each morning of life.

Rashi (11th c.) reads v. 1's *geber* as Jeremiah and the chapter's pit-language as memoir. His comments on v. 22 emphasize the *chesed* as renewable precisely because it had been exhausted — the hope is not in continuity but in recreation. Ibn Ezra is more philological; he treats the *geber* as exemplary rather than autobiographical. Maimonides (*Guide* 3.51) integrates the chapter's wisdom passages (vv. 25-39) into his account of providence: the soul who waits, who bears the yoke, who sits silently is the soul aligned with the divine intellect.

The Hasidic masters made the chapter central. Levi Yitzhak of Berditchev (18th c.) preached on v. 22 every year on the Sabbath after Tisha B'Av — the *chesed* is what makes survival possible. The Sefat Emet (Yehudah Aryeh Leib Alter, 19th c.) read v. 23's "new every morning" as the kabbalistic doctrine of *hithadshut* — God's continuous re-creation of the world each day. Each morning is a fresh act of divine fidelity, not a continuation but a renewal.

The post-Holocaust readings sharpen the chapter's edge. Eliezer Berkovits, Emil Fackenheim, and especially David Hartman (*A Heart of Many Rooms*) have insisted that v. 22 cannot be quoted without v. 42 ("we have transgressed and rebelled, *and you have not forgiven*") immediately following. The chapter holds *chesed* and unforgiveness in the same breath. Tod Linafelt argues this is the chapter's ethical rigor: it refuses to use *chesed* as a salve.

### Christian Interpretive Trajectory

Patristic readings developed v. 30 ("let him give his cheek to the one who strikes") as a Christological prophecy. Origen, Cyprian (*Testimonies Against the Jews* 2.13), Ambrose, and Augustine all cited the verse as fulfilled in Christ's silent suffering before the soldiers (Matthew 26:67, Mark 14:65, Luke 22:64). The chapter's *geber*, on this reading, is a type of Christ — the strong man who absorbs blows. The same exegesis read vv. 7-9 (walled in, prayer shut out) as anticipating Gethsemane and the cross.

Jerome's commentary on Lamentations is largely lost; his Vulgate translation embedded "great is your faithfulness" into Western Christian devotion as *magna est fides tua*. Bernard of Clairvaux's sermons on Lamentations treated the chapter as a school for the contemplative soul — the descent into the pit and the discovery of *chesed* in the depths becomes the pattern of mystical theology.

John Calvin's *Commentary on Lamentations* (1563) gave the chapter its great Reformed reading. Calvin would not allow vv. 22-24 to be detached from the surrounding lament. He treated the verses as the moment when faith asserts itself *despite* the evidence — not because the situation has changed but because *chesed* is what God *is*, regardless of present appearance. Calvin's reading of v. 33 ("he does not afflict from his heart") became central to Reformed theology of divine wrath: God's justice is essential to him, but his joy is in mercy. Edwards and Owen extend this line.

Thomas Chisholm's hymn "Great Is Thy Faithfulness" (1923, music by William Runyan) brought vv. 22-24 into Protestant congregational worship. The hymn has had enormous pastoral effect — it was sung at the Billy Graham Crusades, became a global lingua franca of Protestant devotion, and remains a staple of funerals. Its strength is its weakness: its sweetness can soften the chapter from which it draws. Soong-Chan Rah's *Prophetic Lament* explicitly critiques the way the hymn isolates vv. 22-24 from their context.

Walter Brueggemann's *Theology of the Old Testament* and Kathleen O'Connor's commentary push back against any reading that lets the chapter's "hope" close the book's wound. O'Connor reads vv. 22-24 as a "fragile possibility" the speaker is *trying out*, not a certainty the speaker has achieved. Bonhoeffer's *Letters and Papers from Prison* (especially the December 1943 reflections) reads the chapter through the cross: the *chesed* that is "new every morning" is a discipline, not an emotion — a practice of receiving each day as gift even when nothing in the day looks like gift.

### Where the Traditions Converge and Diverge

Both traditions read vv. 22-24 as the chapter's most theologically loaded verses. Both connect *chesed* (Christian "steadfast love"; Jewish *chesed*-as-covenant-loyalty) to the renewability of mercy. Both have built liturgical practices around the verses (Modeh Ani in Judaism; "Great Is Thy Faithfulness" in Protestantism; Tenebrae chants in Catholicism).

They diverge on the chapter's center of gravity. Christian readings, especially from the Reformation forward, tend to make vv. 22-24 the chapter's resolution and to read everything before and after as either preparation or footnote. Jewish readings — and now an increasing number of Christian critical readings (O'Connor, Linafelt, Rah) — refuse this. The chapter ends not at v. 24 but at v. 66 ("you will pursue them in anger and destroy them"). The hope is asserted in the middle of the lament, not after it. Daniel Boyarin has argued that this difference about where to locate the chapter's center is, in the end, a difference about whether suffering is *redemptive* — a question that shapes Christian and Jewish identity differently.

## Lens 4 — The Theological Intent

The author's vision is the disciplined coexistence of confession and accusation. Verses 22-24 are real assertions of *chesed*. Verse 42 is a real charge that "you have not forgiven." The chapter does not choose. Its theological achievement is the simultaneity.

Two genuine tensions emerge.

**Tension 1: Are vv. 22-24 the chapter's resolution or one voice among others?**

- *Reformed evangelical* (Calvin, Owen, Chisholm, contemporary Tim Keller readings): Verses 22-24 are the theological center. The lament is real but the *chesed* is more real; it is the structural pivot. To read the chapter without making vv. 22-24 the resolution is to misread the author's intent.
- *Catholic*: The chapter is read in the Holy Saturday liturgy and in the Liturgy of the Hours; vv. 22-24 are central but the chapter's full sweep — including v. 66 — is preserved. The integration is liturgical rather than systematic.
- *Lutheran*: The hope of vv. 22-24 is *fides* — faith asserting itself against experience. It is not resolution but resistance.
- *Critical (Linafelt, O'Connor)*: Verses 22-24 are one voice — the voice of fragile hope — among multiple voices in the chapter. The chapter does not resolve; it preserves the possibility of hope without claiming it has arrived. Reading vv. 22-24 as resolution is reading against the structure (which ends in imprecation at v. 66).
- *Liberation (Soong-Chan Rah)*: When vv. 22-24 are isolated from the rest, they are weaponized — the "great faithfulness" hymn becomes a way of silencing communal lament. The chapter only works when read whole.
- *Jewish (post-Holocaust)*: The chapter teaches that *chesed* and unforgiveness can be confessed in the same breath. Anyone who reads vv. 22-24 without v. 42 has not yet read Lamentations.

**Steelmanning the position I find least intuitive**: the Reformed evangelical reading. The temptation is to dismiss it as devotional flattening — a refusal to dwell in the wound. But Calvin's actual reading is more rigorous than the hymn that descends from it. Calvin insists on vv. 22-24 as theological center *because* he has read the rest of the chapter; the *chesed* is the answer to the lament, not the avoidance of it. The hymn's softness is not Calvin's softness. There is a serious case that the chapter's structural placement of *chesed* at the alphabetical center (the *chet*-triplet, vv. 19-21, is followed immediately by the *teth*-triplet of vv. 22-24, the eighth and ninth letters of the twenty-two-letter alphabet — close to the literal middle) is the author's own indication of theological priority. The acrostic itself argues for the position.

**Where this leaves us**: The reader has to decide whether to read with the chapter's middle or with its end. Both readings are textually defensible; neither is harmless.

**Tension 2: Does God afflict willingly?**

Verse 33 says no: "he does not afflict from his heart." Verse 38 says all things — good and bad — come from his mouth. Verse 42 says "we have transgressed and rebelled, and you have not forgiven." How are these compatible?

- *Catholic / Thomist*: Aquinas distinguishes God's antecedent will (what he wills as creator and end) from his consequent will (what he wills in response to creaturely choice). Affliction is consequent, not antecedent — true of him in this circumstance, but not what he is in himself.
- *Reformed*: God ordains all things including affliction, but his disposition toward his people is *chesed*. The affliction is his foreign work (*opus alienum*); his proper work is mercy.
- *Lutheran*: God hides under his opposite. The affliction is real, the hiddenness is real, and the hidden God is the same as the revealed God of *chesed* — known finally only at the cross.
- *Process theology*: God does not afflict at all in the strong sense; v. 33 is the truest statement. The lament's other voices are the human experience of contingency mistaken for divine action.
- *Jewish (Maimonides, Heschel)*: God's pathos is real; his suffering with Israel is the deeper truth than his apparent affliction of Israel. Heschel: "There is darkness in the world, but it is not God."
- *Critical*: The verses do not cohere systematically; they hold competing intuitions in tension. The lament does not have to be a treatise.

**Where this leaves us**: The chapter does not provide a doctrine of providence; it provides a posture toward providence. The posture is that God can be charged and trusted in the same prayer.

The unresolved question the author leaves: *What does it mean to say "great is your faithfulness" while saying "you have not forgiven"?* The chapter is built so that one cannot say either alone. The deeper question: is faith the synthesis of these two claims, or is faith the survival of holding them unsynthesized?

## The Thread

- **Numbers 18:20 / Deuteronomy 10:9** — "The LORD is your portion." The Levites had no land allotment because YHWH was their inheritance. Lamentations 3:24's "the LORD is my portion" reaches back into this priestly economy and turns it into the survivor's claim: when everything else is taken, the LORD remains as portion. Read together to see how landlessness becomes possession.
- **Job 19:7-12; 30:20-21** — Job's God-as-besieger imagery (he has walled up my way, he hunts me like a lion, he has made me a target for his arrows) is almost word-for-word the *geber*'s complaint in Lam 3:7-12. Lamentations 3 reads as Job's voice in a communal key. The two books illuminate each other.
- **Jeremiah 38:6-13** — Jeremiah cast into the cistern of Malchiah, sinking in mud, rescued by Ebed-Melech. The "pit" of Lam 3:53-55 echoes this episode and is the textual basis for the traditional Jeremianic reading of the *geber*.
- **Matthew 5:39 / Matthew 26:67** — "If anyone slaps you on the right cheek, turn to him the other also" / "they spit in his face and struck him." Lam 3:30's "let him give his cheek to the one who strikes" was read by the church fathers as both ethical instruction (Sermon on the Mount) and Christological prophecy (the Passion). When you reach the trial scenes in the Gospels, remember Lam 3:30 — Christ is reading his own script.
